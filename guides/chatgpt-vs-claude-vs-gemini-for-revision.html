ChatGPT vs Claude vs Gemini for Revision: An Honest Comparison

Everyone has an opinion on the 'best' AI. The honest answer: for revision, how you use it matters far more than which one you pick. Here's the plain comparison.

JM
James Martin · AI Study Method
Updated 24 July 2026 · 8 min read

Ask five people which AI is best for studying and you'll get five confident, contradictory answers. The honest truth is less exciting than the arguments: for revision, the differences between the main tools matter far less than how you use them. A student using ChatGPT well will out-revise a student using “the best” tool badly, every time.

So this isn't a scores-out-of-ten shoot-out. It's a plain guide to what the three big tools have in common, where each has a slight edge, and how to actually choose — without falling for the hype (or the affiliate links).

What all three are genuinely good at

ChatGPT (OpenAI), Claude (Anthropic) and Gemini (Google) are all extremely capable, and for the core jobs of revision they overlap almost completely. Any of them will happily:

  • explain a tricky concept at exactly the level you ask for — “explain like I'm in Year 10”;
  • quiz you with exam-style questions and mark your answers;
  • turn your notes into flashcards, a summary, or a mind-map outline;
  • build analogies, worked examples and memory hooks;
  • help you plan a revision timetable.

If you only ever learn to do those five things well, in any one of the three, you have most of the value AI can offer a student. Which brings us to the uncomfortable point for a “versus” article: you could stop reading here, pick one, and be fine.

The rule that beats every comparison

The quality of what you get out is set by the quality of what you put in. A sharp prompt in the “worst” tool beats a lazy prompt in the “best” one. Method first, tool second — always.

Where each one has an edge

With that said, there are mild, real differences in personality and strengths. These shift with every update, so treat them as tendencies, not laws.

ChatGPT — the versatile all-rounder

The most widely used and the one most students have already heard of. It's a strong generalist, has a polished app with voice mode (handy for out-loud quizzing on the bus), and there's a huge amount of shared knowledge online about how to use it. If you want one tool that does a bit of everything and plenty of people around you already use, it's the safe default.

Claude — careful writing and longer documents

Tends to be strong at thoughtful, well-structured writing and at working through reasoning carefully, and it's comfortable handling long pieces of text — useful if you want to paste in a full set of notes or a whole essay for feedback. A good pick if a lot of your revision is essay-based subjects.

Gemini — built into Google

Google's tool, and its advantage is that it lives where a lot of students already work — Docs, Gmail, Android. If your notes are in Google Docs and your school uses Google, that integration is genuinely convenient, and it handles images and diagrams well.

The part that actually decides your grade

Notice that none of those edges is really about “intelligence.” What decides whether AI lifts your grade is the shape of how you use it — and that's identical across all three. The Velvet Method calls the highest-value move “Verify”: making the AI test you rather than tell you.

Paste this into any of the threeAct as my examiner for [subject/topic]. Ask me one exam-style question at a time. After each answer, mark it, show me the mark scheme points I missed, and explain why. Keep going until I've had ten questions, then tell me my weakest area.

That single prompt is worth more than choosing the “right” brand, because it turns a chatbot into retrieval practice — among the study techniques with the strongest evidence behind them (Dunlosky et al., 2013).

How to actually choose (a one-week test)

Don't overthink it. Run this experiment: take one real revision task — say, being quizzed on a topic you find hard — and do it in each tool for a couple of days. Keep whichever one you found yourself wanting to open again. Fit and habit matter more than any feature list, because the best tool is the one you'll actually use.

Do

  • Start with the free version — all three are plenty for revision.
  • Get fluent in one before adding another.
  • Judge them on being quizzed, not just being told things.
  • Check every factual answer against your notes or textbook.

Don't

  • Don't pay for a subscription until you've hit a real limit on the free tier.
  • Don't switch tools every week chasing the “best” one.
  • Don't trust a confident answer just because it's well written.
  • Don't paste in personal details — check each tool's age and privacy terms.

Questions students and parents ask

Is the paid version worth it for revision?

Usually not, at least to start. The free tiers of all three are more than good enough for explaining, quizzing and planning. Paid plans mainly add speed, higher usage limits and the newest models — none of which is required for sound study technique. Hit a wall on the free version first, then decide.

Can my child use more than one?

Yes, and some students like to — for example one for essay feedback and one that's tied to their Google Docs. But there's no need. Fluency in one beats dabbling in three.

Which is best for maths, essays or languages?

For essays, a tool that's strong on careful writing and long documents helps. For languages and maths, all three are capable, but always verify — AI can slip on arithmetic and on precise grammar. Whichever you use, the rules on acknowledging AI in coursework still apply.

They all keep changing — how do I keep up?

You don't need to. This is exactly why we argue for a method over a tool: learn a way of working and it transfers to whatever's newest.
