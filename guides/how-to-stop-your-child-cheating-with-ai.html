Guide for Parents

How to Stop Your Child Cheating with AI (Without Banning It)

You've seen the ChatGPT tab open next to their homework and felt that flicker of worry — is it helping them, or doing it for them? Here's how to tell the difference, and how to steer them toward the kind of AI use that actually builds grades and understanding.

By Clare Saunders & James Martin · AI Study Method · Updated 3 July 2026 · 7 min read

Almost every parent of a secondary-school child is now having some version of the same quiet worry. The homework comes back suspiciously polished. The essay reads a little too smoothly. And somewhere in the background there's a chatbot that can, in ten seconds, produce something that looks like a night's work.

The instinct is to reach for a ban. It's understandable — but as two educators who spend our days with students and exam specifications, we'd gently suggest it's the wrong first move. Not because rules don't matter, but because a ban tackles the symptom and misses the actual skill your child needs: knowing where the line is, and how to stay on the right side of it.

The reframe: The goal isn't a house with no AI in it. That's neither realistic nor, honestly, in your child's interest — AI is now part of how people study and work. The goal is a child who can use AI to learn faster without ever handing over their own thinking. That's a teachable skill, and it's a far better outcome than a rule they'll route around by Tuesday.

First, what actually counts as cheating with AI?

This is the part most "just ban it" advice skips, and it's the part that matters most — because your child genuinely may not know. The line isn't the tool; it's what the tool is asked to do.

✕ This is cheating

  • Asking AI to write the essay and handing it in as their own
  • Copying AI answers into coursework or a marked assignment
  • Getting AI to do the maths and only writing down the result
  • Pasting a question in and submitting whatever comes back, unread

✓ This is revising

  • Asking AI to quiz them on a topic and mark their answers
  • Getting it to explain a concept a different way when the textbook didn't land
  • Having it check their own attempt against the mark scheme
  • Using it to build practice questions, then closing it to do them

The single question that settles almost every case: does the thinking, and the final piece of work, stay your child's own? If AI produced the answer, that's cheating. If AI helped them understand so they could produce the answer, that's exactly what good revision has always looked like — it just used to be a tutor, a textbook, or you at the kitchen table.

Why banning it usually backfires

We say this from experience rather than ideology. Three reasons a ban tends to fail:

1. It's unenforceable. It's on their phone, their friends' phones, and the school computers. A ban you can't enforce quietly teaches a child that your rules are optional — the opposite of what you want.

2. AI literacy is now a real skill. Universities and employers increasingly expect young people to use these tools well and honestly. A child who never learns to use AI with judgement isn't protected; they're behind.

3. It teaches nothing. The thing that keeps a child honest in an exam hall, where you're not there, is internal judgement — not a rule from home. Every conversation you have about how to use AI is building that judgement. A ban skips the lesson entirely.

Signs your child may be leaning on it too hard

These aren't reasons to go through their phone — they're prompts for a conversation. Over-reliance tends to show up as a gap between what a child can produce and what they can explain:

  • Homework is polished, but they can't talk you through how they got there.
  • Class-test or mock results don't match the quality of work done at home.
  • They can hand in a correct answer but can't reproduce the method a day later.
  • Revision has quietly become "reading what the AI said" rather than retrieving anything from memory.

That last one is the real danger, and it's subtle. Learning happens through effortful recall — the mild struggle of pulling something out of your own head. If AI removes the struggle, it removes the learning, however productive the session felt. The work looks done; the memory never formed.

What to do instead: turn the answer-machine into a study tool

You don't need to be technical to steer this. You just need to help your child swap a few habits. The prompts on the left get answers handed over; the ones on the right build understanding:

"Write me an essay on the causes of WW1.""Ask me five exam questions on the causes of WW1, then mark my answers against the mark scheme."
"Solve this quadratic for me.""I got x = 3 and x = −5. Check my working and show me exactly where I went wrong."
"Summarise this chapter.""Explain osmosis simply, then close — I'll write it back from memory and you tell me what I missed."
"Give me the answer to question 4.""Give me a hint for question 4, not the answer."

Same tool, completely different outcome. In the right-hand column the AI behaves like a tutor who quizzes, explains and checks — and your child still does the thinking, which is the only part that counts in an exam.

The conversation worth having

Lead with curiosity, not accusation. Something like: "I'm not going to take it away — I actually want you using it. I just want to make sure you're using it in the way that helps you, not the way that leaves you stuck in the exam when it's not there."

Then make the stakes concrete and honest: in the exam hall there is no chatbot. Everything they've outsourced is a gap that shows up under timed conditions. Framing it as "AI is your practice partner, not your ghostwriter" lands far better than a rule, because it's on their side — it's about them doing well, not about them being caught.

Questions parents ask

Is using ChatGPT for homework cheating?

It depends entirely on how it's used. Getting AI to write an answer and submitting it is cheating; getting it to explain, quiz or check your child's own attempt is legitimate revision. The test is whether the thinking and the final work stay theirs.

Can schools and exam boards detect AI writing?

Not reliably. AI-detection tools are widely known to be inaccurate and can flag genuine work as AI-written, so schools are advised not to lean on them alone. That unreliability is precisely why teaching honest, defensible use matters more than trying to police it.

Should I ban my child from using ChatGPT?

A ban is hard to enforce and teaches nothing about judgement. Since AI is now part of study and work, the more useful goal is teaching your child where the line is and how to use AI to learn rather than copy.

What if my child was wrongly accused of using AI?

Because detectors are unreliable, false accusations do happen. Keep drafts and version history as evidence of the working, ask the school which specific tool or judgement was used, and request the work be assessed on its merits. Teaching your child to show their working — plans, sources, rough drafts — is the best protection.

Is AI bad for my child's learning?

Only when it removes the effort that causes learning. Used to hand over answers, it short-circuits memory. Used to generate practice, explain mistakes and test recall, it can work like a patient tutor. The method matters more than the tool.
