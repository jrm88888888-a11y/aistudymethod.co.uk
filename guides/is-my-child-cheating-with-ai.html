Is My Child Cheating with AI? What UK Parents Need to Know

You've spotted ChatGPT open beside their homework and felt that jolt of worry. Take a breath. As two educators, here's the honest answer to the question you're really asking — is my child cheating, or just using a tool everyone now uses? — and what to do about it.

By Clare Saunders & James Martin · AI Study Method · Updated 3 July 2026 · 7 min read

The honest reality

Let's start with the thing that will actually calm the panic: nearly every secondary-school student is now using AI in some form. If your child has a phone and a homework deadline, the odds that they've never once asked a chatbot a question are vanishingly small. That's the world they're growing up in, and no household rule changes the wider tide.

But here is the distinction that matters, and it's the one the alarming headlines skip: using AI is not the same as cheating with it. A child who asks a chatbot to explain photosynthesis a different way is doing exactly what students have always done with a tutor, a textbook or a patient parent — they've just found a new door to the same room. A child who has AI write the essay and hands it in unread has crossed a line. Same tool; completely different act.

The reframe: The question isn't really "is my child using AI?" — they almost certainly are, and that alone isn't a problem. The question is "is my child using it to learn, or to avoid learning?" That's the line worth watching, and the good news is it's a teachable one.

So what actually counts as cheating?

The shortest honest version: if AI produced the work, that's cheating. If AI helped your child understand so that they could produce the work, that's revision. The tool is the same in both cases — what changes is where the thinking happened.

Getting a chatbot to explain, quiz, or check an answer leaves your child's own understanding at the centre. Getting it to generate a finished piece and passing that off as their own removes them from it entirely. That single test settles the vast majority of cases. (If you want the fuller version — worked examples, the conversation to have, and how to steer habits — we go deep on it in our companion guide, How to Stop Your Child Cheating with AI.)

"Is it cheating if…"

Parents rarely need a philosophy lecture — they need quick rulings on the situations they're actually seeing. Here's the plain-English version:

✓ Almost always fine

  • Homework help & explanations — "explain this a simpler way" when the textbook didn't land
  • Grammar & spellcheck — tidying their own writing, much like Word's checker
  • Generating ideas — brainstorming angles, then writing the piece themselves
  • Quizzing & checking — asking AI to test them or mark their own attempt

✕ Not fine

  • Submitting AI text as their own — the essay wrote itself; they just pasted it in
  • Coursework & NEA — passing off AI-generated work in formal assessment (see below — be careful)
  • Handing in answers they can't explain or reproduce
  • Copying a chatbot response unread into a marked assignment

Notice the pattern: the "fine" column keeps your child doing the thinking, while the tool supports it. The "not fine" column hands the thinking over. When you're unsure about a specific case, that's the question to ask.

Coursework & the exam-board line

This is the one area where we'd urge genuine care, because the stakes are higher. There's a real difference between private revision and formal assessment.

For private revision at home — using AI to understand, practise and test — there's no issue at all. It's just a modern study tool. But for formal non-exam assessment (NEA) and coursework, the rules are stricter: passing off AI-generated work as your own can count as malpractice and be penalised, sometimes seriously. That work is meant to be a record of what your child can genuinely do.

We're deliberately not going to quote specific board rules here, because they change and they differ between subjects and awarding bodies. What we can say confidently: if any of your child's grade depends on work produced outside the exam hall, check the school's and exam board's own policy on AI use, and make sure your child knows it. When in doubt, the safest habit is simple — do the work themselves, and keep evidence of the working.

Practical rule of thumb: Revision folder at home? Use AI freely. Anything that will be marked as your child's own assessed work? Assume AI-generated content is off-limits unless the school explicitly says otherwise — and keep the drafts either way.

When schools get it wrong: AI detectors are unreliable

Here's the part almost no one tells worried parents, and it deserves saying plainly: AI-detection tools are widely known to be inaccurate. They can flag a child's completely genuine work as "AI-written" — and they do. A pupil who simply writes in a clear, tidy style, or who is neurodivergent, or who learned a formal register, can trip these tools with no wrongdoing at all.

This means false accusations happen. It's one of the most stressful situations a family can face, because it's hard to prove a negative. So if your child is wrongly accused, here's what to do:

  • Stay calm and don't concede. An accusation is not a verdict, and detector output is not proof.
  • Gather the evidence of the working. Drafts, version history in Google Docs or Word, browser history, handwritten plans, notes, rough workings — anything that shows the piece was built over time by a human.
  • Ask which tool or judgement was used. Politely ask the school what the accusation is actually based on — a specific detector, a teacher's impression, or something else — and whether they know that detectors are unreliable.
  • Request assessment on merit. Ask that the work be judged on its content and on your child's demonstrable knowledge, and offer for them to talk through it or reproduce similar work under supervision.

And the best long-term protection? Teach your child to show their working as a matter of habit — keep drafts, jot plans, save version history. A child who can always demonstrate how a piece came together is a child who's almost impossible to wrongly convict. It's the same instinct as showing your working in maths: the answer matters, but the trail proves it's yours.

How to tell if YOUR child is over-relying on it

Separate from cheating is a quieter risk: leaning on AI so much that the learning never sticks. It shows up as a gap between what a child can produce and what they can explain. If the homework is polished but they can't talk you through how they got there, or the mock results don't match the work done at home, that's the signal — not a reason to raid their phone, but a reason for a conversation.

We cover the full checklist and the fix in the companion guide, How to Stop Your Child Cheating with AI, so we won't repeat it all here. The short version: over-reliance is about effort quietly disappearing, and it's fixable by changing how the tool is used, not by banning it.

The constructive response

So — is your child cheating with AI? Most likely they're using it, some of it helpfully and some of it in ways worth redirecting, and almost none of it beyond fixing with a calm conversation. The panic-driven answer is a ban. The educator's answer is to teach the right use, because that's the skill that protects them in the exam hall, in coursework, and in the years after school when these tools are everywhere.

That's the whole idea behind what we do: showing students how to use AI to test, explain and stretch their thinking — never to replace it. Get that right, and the cheating question mostly answers itself.

Questions parents ask

Is my child cheating if they use ChatGPT for homework?

Not necessarily. Using AI to explain a tricky concept, quiz themselves, or check their own attempt is legitimate revision. It only becomes cheating when AI produces the work and your child hands it in as their own. The test is simple: does the thinking, and the final piece of work, stay theirs?

Can teachers tell if my child used AI?

Not reliably. AI-detection tools are widely known to be inaccurate and can flag genuine work as AI-written, so schools are advised not to rely on them alone. Teachers often lean more on knowing a pupil's usual standard than on any detector — which is exactly why teaching honest, defensible use matters more than trying to police it.

What if my child is falsely accused of using AI?

Because detectors are unreliable, false accusations do happen. Stay calm and keep drafts, version history and notes as evidence of the working. Ask the school which specific tool or judgement was used, and request that the work be assessed on its merits. Teaching your child to show their working — plans, sources, rough drafts — is the best protection against a wrong accusation.

Is it against the rules to use AI for GCSE coursework?

For private revision, AI is fine. For formal non-exam assessment or coursework, passing off AI-generated work as your own can count as malpractice and be penalised. Rules vary, so check your child's school and exam-board policy rather than assuming — and if in doubt, have them keep evidence of their own working.
