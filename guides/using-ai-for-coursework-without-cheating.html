How to Use AI for Coursework Sensibly (Without Cheating)

AI can make coursework genuinely better — or get you disqualified. The difference is where you point it. Here's how to use it well and stay safe.

JM
James Martin · AI Study Method
Updated 24 July 2026 · 9 min read

AI can genuinely make coursework better — sharper arguments, better structure, fewer silly errors, deeper understanding of the brief. It can also get a student disqualified. The difference isn't the tool; it's where you point it. Point it at the thinking and it's a brilliant tutor. Point it at the writing you hand in and it's misconduct.

This guide is the practical companion to our overview of the exam-board rules on AI. Here we focus on how to actually use AI well on a piece of coursework, and stay firmly on the right side of the line.

The whole guide in one line

Use AI on the process, never the product. If it helps you think, plan, understand and improve your own work, good. If it produces the words, data or ideas you submit as yours, stop.

Six ways to use AI that make coursework better — legitimately

1. Understand the brief and mark scheme

Paste in the task and the assessment criteria and ask AI to explain what a top-band answer actually needs. You're clarifying the target, not answering it.

2. Plan and structure

Talk through your own argument and ask for feedback on the structure — is the logic in the right order, what's missing? The ideas stay yours; AI is a sounding board.

3. Find leads to verify yourself

Ask what topics, theories or sources are worth investigating — then go and read the real sources and cite those, never the AI's summary. (AI can invent citations, so this step is non-negotiable.)

4. Get feedback on your draft

This is the highest-value use. Write the paragraph yourself, then ask AI to critique it against the mark scheme — what's weak, what's unsupported, what a examiner would push on. Then you rewrite it.

A safe, powerful promptHere is a paragraph I wrote for my [subject] coursework, and the mark scheme. Don't rewrite it. Instead, tell me where it's weak against the criteria, what evidence is missing, and what questions an examiner would ask — so I can improve it myself.

5. Check your understanding

Have AI quiz you on the topic, or ask it to explain the bit you found confusing a different way. Pure revision — always allowed.

6. Suggest proofreading fixes

Ask it to point out spelling, punctuation and clarity issues for you to fix — not to silently rewrite your prose into its own voice.

Four things that cross the line

Don't

  • Generate text with AI and submit it as your own — even if you reword it.
  • Paraphrase AI output to disguise where it came from.
  • Use AI-invented facts, quotes or data (it fabricates convincingly).
  • Use AI as a source without acknowledging it where the rules require.

Do

  • Keep every draft and your version history — it proves the work grew from you.
  • Acknowledge AI use as your board requires (tool, date, how used).
  • Verify every fact against a real, citable source.
  • Make sure you can explain and defend every line you submit.

A worked example: improving a paragraph the right way

Say you've written a history paragraph and it's landing at a middle grade. The wrong move is “rewrite this to get top marks” — now the words aren't yours. The right move keeps you as the author:

YouDon't rewrite it. My paragraph argues the main cause was economic. Against this mark scheme, what's the strongest counter-argument I've ignored, and where do I need specific evidence?
AIYou haven't addressed political factors, and your economic claim needs a specific statistic or source to support it. An examiner would ask how you weigh the two causes…
You[You go away, research the political angle, find a real source, and rewrite the paragraph yourself — stronger, and entirely your own work.]

Same tool, completely different outcome: your understanding deepened, your words stayed yours, and you could explain every sentence to a teacher. That's the whole game.

How to keep yourself safe

Beyond using AI the right way, protect yourself with a simple habit: keep your workings. Save drafts, keep your document's version history on, and note where you used AI and how. If your work is ever questioned, the single best defence is being able to show it grew, messily and genuinely, from you — and being able to talk about it fluently, because you actually did it.

Questions students and parents ask

If I write it myself and only use AI for feedback, do I still have to declare it?

It depends on the assessment and board — some require you to acknowledge any AI use, even feedback. The safe habit is to note how you used it and check your school's and board's specific rules. Declaring honest, legitimate use never gets you in trouble; hiding use does.

Is using AI to fix my grammar cheating?

Light spelling and grammar checking is generally fine and long-standing. The line is crossed when AI substantially rewrites your text into its own words — then it's no longer your writing. Keep the voice yours.

Where's the official line on all this?

The exam boards' rules are set out by JCQ and summarised in our guide to what's actually allowed. Your school may add its own policy on top, so check both.

References & further reading

This guide is general information, not a substitute for your school’s policy or the current official JCQ guidance, which is periodically updated. Always check both before you act.
