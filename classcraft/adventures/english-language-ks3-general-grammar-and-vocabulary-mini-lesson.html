Word classes

Every word has a job

A word class tells you the job a word does in a sentence. The same word can even change class depending on how it is used. Here are the eight you need at KS3:

  • Noun — a person, place, thing or idea: teacher, London, courage.
  • Verb — an action or a state of being: sprint, think, was.
  • Adjective — describes a noun: a rusty gate.
  • Adverb — describes a verb, often ending -ly: she sang softly.
  • Pronoun — replaces a noun: they, him, it.
  • Preposition — shows position or time: before lunch, on the roof.
  • Conjunction — joins words or clauses: and, but, because.
  • Determiner — comes before a noun to pin it down: the, a, some, my.

The core: nouns & verbs

Nouns and verbs do the heavy lifting

A sentence needs a subject (usually a noun or pronoun) and a verb. Nouns come in useful types:

  • Common noun — a general thing: city, river.
  • Proper noun — a specific name, always capitalised: Sheffield, Amara.
  • Abstract noun — an idea you cannot touch: freedom, fear.

Verbs can be action verbs (leap, whisper) or state verbs (be, seem, know). Swapping a dull verb for a sharp one is one of the fastest ways to improve writing:

Upgrade the verb

❌ She went down the corridor.

✅ She crept down the corridor.  → now we feel the tension.

Tip: to test whether a word is a noun, try putting the or a in front of it. To test for a verb, try changing the tense (run → ran).

Sentence types

Simple, compound and complex

A clause is a group of words with its own verb. Sentences are built from clauses:

  • Simple — one main clause: The storm arrived.
  • Compound — two main clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction (and, but, or, so): The storm arrived and the lights went out.
  • Complex — a main clause plus a subordinate clause (using because, although, when, if): Although the storm arrived, we stayed calm.

Why it matters: short simple sentences build tension. Longer complex sentences let you pack in detail. Mixing lengths keeps writing lively.

Punctuation · apostrophes

The apostrophe has two jobs

Common mistake — its vs it's: it's only ever means it is or it has. its (no apostrophe) means belonging to it. Test: if you can swap in "it is", use the apostrophe. The cat licked its paw.

Punctuation · commas

Commas — and the comma splice

Commas separate items in a list, mark off extra information, and follow an opening subordinate clause:

  • Lists: We packed apples, cheese, bread and water.
  • Extra information: My brother, who is twelve, plays chess.
  • After an opener: Because it was late, we left.

The comma splice (a very common error): you cannot join two complete sentences with only a comma.
❌ It was raining, we stayed inside.
✅ Fix it with a full stop, a conjunction, or a semicolon: It was raining, so we stayed inside.

Punctuation · colons & semicolons

Colons and semicolons

These two are the marks that lift writing from KS3 to top-band:

clause : list / explanationa colon introduces something the first clause has promised
  • Colon (:) introduces a list or an explanation. The part before it must be a full clause: She had one goal: to win.
  • Semicolon (;) links two closely related complete sentences: The rain stopped; the sun broke through.
Semicolon vs comma splice

❌ The rain stopped, the sun broke through.  (comma splice)

✅ The rain stopped; the sun broke through.  (semicolon joins two sentences)

Punctuation · speech marks

Punctuating speech

Speech marks (also called inverted commas) go around the exact words spoken. The rules:

  • Open speech marks before the words, close them after.
  • Punctuation goes inside the closing speech marks.
  • Start a new line for each new speaker.
Correct vs incorrect

❌ "Where are we going" asked Priya.

✅ "Where are we going?" asked Priya.

✅ Priya asked, "Where are we going?"

Watch out: the comma or question mark belongs inside the speech marks, and you need a comma before the speech begins when the sentence opens with the speaker.

Standard English

Standard English vs dialect & slang

Standard English is the form used in formal writing, exams and the news. It is not "better" than dialect — dialect and slang are perfect with friends — but you must be able to switch into Standard English when the situation calls for it.

Correcting common non-standard errors

❌ We was late.  → ✅ We were late. (agreement)

❌ I done my homework.  → ✅ I did my homework. (verb form)

❌ I ain't got none.  → ✅ I haven't got any. (double negative)

❌ He should of gone.  → ✅ He should have gone.

there / their / they're: there = a place; their = belonging to them; they're = they are. They're putting their bags over there.

Tense & agreement

Keep tense and agreement steady

Tense tells us when something happens: past, present or future. Keep it consistent unless you mean to shift time.

Consistent tense

❌ She walked in and sits down.  (past → present, by accident)

✅ She walked in and sat down.

Subject–verb agreement: a singular subject takes a singular verb; a plural subject takes a plural verb.

Agreement

❌ The list of names are long.  → ✅ The list of names is long. (the subject is list, which is singular)

Building vocabulary

Prefixes, suffixes & word families

You can unlock new words by breaking them into parts. A prefix goes on the front; a suffix goes on the end; the root is the core meaning.

  • Prefixes: un- (not), re- (again), pre- (before), sub- (under), mis- (wrongly).
  • Suffixes: -ful (full of), -less (without), -ness (a state), -tion (an action or result).
  • Word family: help → helper, helpful, unhelpful, helplessly.

Etymology basics: many roots come from Latin and Greek. port (Latin: carry) gives transport, portable, export. Knowing the root helps you decode words you've never met.

Grammar for effect

Choices create effect

Grammar is not just about being "correct" — writers choose structures to shape how a reader feels.

  • Short sentence after long ones = impact: The house was silent. Then it moved.
  • Precise verbs & adjectives paint a clearer picture than vague ones.
  • Fronting an adverbial shifts emphasis: Slowly, the door opened.
  • Repetition of a conjunction can build a breathless rhythm: and up, and over, and down.
Same event, different effect

Flat: A man walked in and said something.

Crafted: A stranger strode in. He said nothing.  → tense and controlled.

Recap

The toolkit to remember

Word classes: noun, verb, adjective, adverb, pronoun, preposition, conjunction, determiner.

Sentence types: simple (1 main clause), compound (2 main clauses + and/but/so), complex (main + subordinate).

Apostrophes: possession (dog's) & contraction (don't); its ≠ it's.

Comma splice: never join two sentences with just a comma.

Colon = introduces; semicolon = links two sentences.

Standard English: were (not was), did (not done), no double negatives; there/their/they're.

Vocabulary: prefix + root + suffix; use roots to decode new words.

Grammar for effect: vary sentence length and choose precise words.
