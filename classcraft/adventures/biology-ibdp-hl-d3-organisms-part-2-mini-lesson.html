Mission 1

Mission 1: Why do some inherited conditions turn up far more often in sons than in daughters?

Make a guess: Why do some inherited conditions turn up far more often in sons than in daughters?

Mission 1

Sex chromosomes

  • Human body cells: 22 pairs of autosomes + one pair of sex chromosomes.
  • Female: XX. Male: XY.
  • Every egg carries an X. A sperm carries X or Y. So the sperm decides the sex of the zygote.
  • The X carries far more genes than the much smaller Y.
  • A gene on a sex chromosome is sex-linked. Most are on the X: X-linked.
  • A male has one copy of each X-linked gene. A female has two.
Mission 1

Writing an X-linked cross

  • Write the allele as a superscript on the X: Xᴴ (normal clotting), Xʰ (haemophilia). The Y carries no allele.
  • Haemophilia: a recessive X-linked allele; a clotting factor is missing, so blood clots slowly.
  • Carrier mother × unaffected father: XᴴXʰ × XᴴY.
  • Gametes: Xᴴ or Xʰ · Xᴴ or Y.
  • Offspring: XᴴXᴴ, XᴴXʰ (carrier), XᴴY, XʰY (affected).
  • Daughters: none affected, ½ carriers. Sons: ½ affected.
Quick check
?A woman who is a carrier of haemophilia (XᴴXʰ) has children with a man who does not have haemophilia (XᴴY). Their first child is a boy. What is the probability that he has haemophilia?
Mission 1

Why sons are affected more often

  • A male's one X comes from his mother. One recessive allele is enough: XʰY is affected.
  • A female needs two: XʰXʰ. That needs an affected father and a carrier or affected mother.
  • An affected father passes his X to every daughter (all carriers) and to no son.
  • So X-linked recessive traits never pass father → son.
  • Say: "a son inherits his X chromosome from his mother" — not: "haemophilia passes from father to son".
Mission 1

Reading a pedigree

  • Square = male; circle = female; shaded = affected. Generations I, II, III; individuals numbered left to right.
  • Two unaffected parents with an affected child → the allele is recessive; both parents carry it.
  • Two affected parents with an unaffected child → the allele is dominant.
  • Recessive allele + affected daughter of an unaffected father → not X-linked, so autosomal.
  • A father passes his X to no son. If a son can only have got the allele from his father, the gene is not X-linked.
  • Close relatives are more likely to carry the same recessive allele from a shared ancestor. That is the genetic basis for prohibiting marriage between close relatives in many societies.
  • Inferring the pattern from many cases is inductive; applying it to deduce one person's genotype is deductive.
Quick check
?A man and a woman who are both unaffected by a genetic disorder have three children: an affected daughter, an unaffected son and an unaffected daughter. The affected daughter later has four children with an unaffected man, and all four are unaffected. What can be deduced about the disorder?

Mission 1

Predicting from a pedigree

  • Step 1: dominant or recessive? Look for unaffected parents with an affected child.
  • Step 2: autosomal or X-linked? Check affected daughters' fathers and affected fathers' sons.
  • Step 3: write the genotypes you know (affected recessives; carriers revealed by children).
  • Step 4: use a cross to give a probability for the next child.
Mission check

?In birds, the sex chromosomes are Z and W: males are ZZ and females are ZW. In a hypothetical species of bird, a feather trait is caused by a recessive allele of a gene on the Z chromosome; the W chromosome carries no allele of this gene. In a population in which this allele is present, which sex is expected to show the trait more often, and why?

It is not about being male. It is about having only one copy of the chromosome that carries the gene.

Mission 2

Mission 2: If two genes sit on the same chromosome, can they ever be separated?

Make a guess: If two genes sit on the same chromosome, can they ever be separated?

Mission 2

Two genes on different chromosomes

  • A plant is RrYy. R and Y are on different chromosomes.
  • In meiosis I, each homologous pair lines up independently of the other pair.
  • So R is just as likely to go with Y as with y.
  • Gametes: RY, Ry, rY, ry in equal numbers.
  • This is Mendel's second law: the law of independent assortment.
  • Each gamete still gets one allele of each gene (segregation).
Mission 2

Worked example: 9 : 3 : 3 : 1

Peas: R round > r wrinkled; Y yellow > y green. F1 RrYy × RrYy.

  • Each parent makes 4 gamete types: a 4 × 4 Punnett grid, 16 cells.
  • Group the cells by phenotype: 9 round yellow : 3 round green : 3 wrinkled yellow : 1 wrinkled green.
  • Short cut: each gene gives 3 : 1. P(round and yellow) = ¾ × ¾ = 9/16.
  • 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 is a phenotype ratio. The 16 cells hold 9 different genotypes.
  • Mendel's real F2: 315 : 108 : 101 : 32. Close to 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 (tested in Mission 3).
Mission 2

The dihybrid test cross: 1 : 1 : 1 : 1

  • Cross the double heterozygote with a double homozygous recessive: RrYy × rryy.
  • rryy gives only ry gametes. So each offspring shows the gamete from the heterozygote.
  • Four gamete types, equally likely → 1 : 1 : 1 : 1.
  • Round yellow : round green : wrinkled yellow : wrinkled green.
Quick check
?Two pea plants heterozygous for two unlinked genes (RrYy) are crossed. Why is the expected phenotypic ratio among their offspring 9:3:3:1?
Mission 2

Linked genes

  • Genes whose loci are on the same chromosome are linked.
  • Their alleles travel together into gametes. They do not assort independently.
  • But in prophase I, crossing over between non-sister chromatids can swap them.
  • A crossover between the two loci gives recombinant chromatids: new combinations of alleles.
  • The closer the loci, the less often a crossover falls between them.
  • Show linked alleles beside two vertical lines, one per homologous chromosome.
  • Say: "autosomal linkage" — not "sex linkage". Linkage means the same chromosome, any chromosome.
  • So the answer to the mission's question: yes. Crossing over separates linked alleles, but only in some meioses.
Quick check
?In a fruit fly the loci for body colour and wing shape are on the same autosome. A fly inherited one chromosome carrying the alleles G and W and the homologous chromosome carrying g and w, shown as G W and g w beside two vertical lines. Why does this fly not produce GW, Gw, gW and gw gametes in equal numbers?

  • Parental classes are the large ones. They need not be "both dominant" and "both recessive".
Quick check

Mission 2

Finding gene loci in a database

  • A genome database gives each gene's locus (chromosome and position) and its polypeptide product.
  • HBB (β-globin of haemoglobin) and HBD (δ-globin): neighbouring loci on chromosome 11. Linked.
  • CFTR (a chloride channel protein): chromosome 7. Not linked to HBB.
  • F8 (clotting factor VIII): on the X chromosome. Sex-linked.
  • The locus is the same in every person; people differ in the alleles at it.
Quick check
?In a genome database, a student finds that a human gene has its locus on chromosome 11 and that a second gene has its locus a few thousand base pairs away on the same chromosome; each record names a different polypeptide product. Which conclusion is correct?
Mission check

Linked genes can be separated, but only when a crossover falls between them. The rarer the recombinants, the closer the genes.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Mendel's peas came out at almost three to one, never exactly. How close is close enough?

Make a guess: Mendel's peas came out at almost three to one, never exactly. How close is close enough?

Mission 3

Chance or a real difference?

  • Which gametes fuse is random. So observed counts scatter around the expected ones.
  • The χ² test asks: is the difference small enough to be due to chance?
  • Null hypothesis: there is no significant difference between observed and expected results.
  • Alternative hypothesis: there is a significant difference (e.g. the genes are linked).
  • The test uses counts in categories (phenotype classes). Never percentages or ratios.
  • The counted offspring are a sample of all the offspring the cross could produce.
Mission 3

The formula

  • χ² = Σ (O − E)² ÷ E. O = observed, E = expected, Σ = sum over all classes.
  • E = total counted × expected fraction for that class.
  • Degrees of freedom = number of classes − 1.
  • Big differences → big χ². A perfect fit → χ² = 0.
Mission 3

  • χ² below it: the difference is not significant. A difference this large would arise by chance more than 5% of the time.

Mission 3

When the null hypothesis is rejected

  • A significant difference means the expected ratio is probably wrong for this cross.
  • Then look for a biological reason:
    • two genes linked (too many parental types);
    • a sex-linked gene;
    • unequal survival of one phenotype.
  • χ² tells you whether the difference is significant, not why.
Mission 3

Common slips

  • Use raw counts, not percentages.
  • Scale E to your total. Never use 9, 3, 3, 1 as E.
  • Degrees of freedom = classes − 1.
  • Big χ² = big difference. Above the critical value → significant.
  • Not significant ≠ proved.
Mission check

?In tomatoes, purple stems (A) are dominant to green stems (a). An Aa plant is crossed with an aa plant. Of 200 seedlings, 115 are purple and 85 are green (illustrative data). Critical values at p = 0.05: 3.84 for 1 degree of freedom, 5.99 for 2. Which conclusion is correct?

Unlike Mendel's seed counts, this gap is too large to be chance. Next step: look for a reason, such as fewer green seedlings surviving.

Mission 4

Mission 4: A kangaroo rat can live its whole life without drinking. How do its kidneys hold on to water?

Make a guess: A kangaroo rat can live its whole life without drinking. How do its kidneys hold on to water?

Mission 4

Keeping blood water in balance

  • Osmoregulation: control of the osmotic concentration of the blood (osmol L⁻¹).
  • Sweating, salty food, not drinking: the blood is short of water.
  • Drinking a lot: the blood has surplus water.
  • The kidney responds by changing how much water it reabsorbs: a small volume of concentrated urine, or a large volume of dilute urine.
  • The loop of Henle makes concentrated urine possible. ADH controls how much water is kept.
Mission 4

The loop of Henle builds a salty medulla

  • 1: Ascending limb: impermeable to water. It actively transports Na⁺ (with Cl⁻) out of the filtrate into the medulla tissue.
  • This keeps the osmotic concentration of the medulla high: up to about 1.2 osmol L⁻¹ deep in the medulla.
  • 2: The collecting duct runs down through this salty medulla, so water can be drawn out of it by osmosis.
  • Longer loops → saltier deep medulla → more concentrated urine possible.
  • Say: "the ascending limb pumps sodium ions out" — not: "the loop pumps water out".
Quick check
?How does the loop of Henle make it possible for the kidney to produce concentrated urine?
Mission 4

The adjustable part: DCT and collecting duct

  • Filtrate leaving the loop is dilute.
  • In the DCT and collecting duct, water can leave by osmosis into the salty medulla, then into the blood.
  • How much leaves depends on how permeable their walls are to water.
  • That permeability is set by ADH (antidiuretic hormone).
Mission 4

ADH: the control loop

  • 1: Osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus detect a rise in blood osmotic concentration.
  • 2: ADH is made in the hypothalamus, then stored in and released from the posterior pituitary into the blood. More ADH is released.
  • 3: ADH binds receptors on collecting duct cells. Vesicles containing aquaporins (water channels) fuse with the membrane facing the filtrate.
  • 4: More water leaves the duct by osmosis → small volume of concentrated urine. As blood water is restored, less ADH is released: negative feedback.
  • Surplus water: less ADH → aquaporins taken back into vesicles → large volume of dilute urine.
  • Say: "ADH makes the duct walls more permeable to water" — not: "ADH pumps water back into the blood".
Quick check
?A person has drunk very little water for a day and their blood osmotic concentration has risen. Which sequence of events follows?

Mission check

?In a rare inherited condition, the collecting duct cells cannot respond to ADH, although the hypothalamus and pituitary work normally. Which prediction is correct?

The loop builds the gradient; ADH decides how much water uses it. Break the response and the feedback loop keeps shouting.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • X-linked recessive alleles show in any male who carries one (one X), but in females only when homozygous; sons get their X from their mother.
  • Pedigrees: unaffected parents with an affected child → recessive; then an affected daughter with an unaffected father → autosomal.

Mission 2

  • Unlinked genes assort independently: 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 from two double heterozygotes, 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 from a test cross.
  • Linked genes travel together; crossing over between the loci makes a minority of recombinants.

Mission 3

  • χ² = Σ (O − E)²/E with degrees of freedom = classes − 1; compare with the critical value at p = 0.05.
  • Below it: do not reject the null hypothesis. Above it: reject it (significant difference).

Mission 4

  • The ascending limb actively transports Na⁺ out, making the medulla salty; the collecting duct loses water to it by osmosis when ADH has inserted aquaporins.
  • Osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus → ADH from the posterior pituitary → aquaporins → more water reabsorbed; negative feedback switches it off.
