Mission 1

Mission 1: The UK is famous for rain. So why is its most crowded corner officially classed as water-stressed?

Make a guess: The UK is famous for rain. So why is its most crowded corner officially classed as water-stressed?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR GCSE Geography B (J384)

  • 7.1a overview of UK human and physical characteristics (population density, land use, rainfall, relief) and issues linked to them: water stress, housing shortage
Mission 1

Population density

  • Population density = people ÷ area (people per km²).
  • In 2021, England and Wales had 394.6 people per km²; London 5,597.6; Wales 149.9.
  • The densest district, Tower Hamlets (London), had 15,702.9 per km²; the sparsest, Eden (Cumbria), had 25.5: over 600 times fewer (our calculation).
  • Pattern: dense in London, the south-east and big cities; sparse in uplands of the north and west.
Mission 1

Land use

  • Farmland covers 69% of the UK's land; agriculture covers 63.1% of England's.
  • Woodland covers 14% of the UK; 20% of Scotland, 10% of England.
  • Only 8.7% of England's land is developed (homes, roads, industry).
  • Even in the Green Belt around cities, just 6.8% is developed.
Quick check
?Which statement about land in England is supported by the data?
Mission 1

Relief and rainfall

  • Relief: uplands mainly in the north and west on hard rocks; lowlands mainly in the south and east.
  • Rainfall: moist Atlantic air rises over hills, so rain maps "look very similar to a topographic map".
  • Over 3,500 mm a year falls on the high west Highlands; around 500 mm in the driest parts of eastern England.
Mission 1

Put the maps together

  • Most rain falls in the north and west, where few people live.
  • Most people live in the south and east, where less rain falls.
  • So water and people are in different places, and land for homes is under most pressure where people already crowd.
Quick check
?A place has steep mountains, over 2,500 mm of rain a year and fewer than 30 people per km². Where is it most likely to be?
Mission 1

Issue 1: water stress

  • Serious water stress: household demand for water is "a high proportion" of the effective rainfall available to meet it.
  • In 2021 these company areas were confirmed as seriously stressed: Thames Water, Southern Water, South East Water, Affinity Water, Essex and Suffolk Water, SES Water, Anglian Water (East Anglia): London, the south-east and the east.
  • 2021 added some Midlands and south-west areas (e.g. Severn Trent, Wessex Water).
  • Not seriously stressed: e.g. United Utilities (North West), Northumbrian Water.
  • It allows companies to consider compulsory water metering; it "does not indicate that there will not be enough water".
Mission 1

Issue 2: housing shortage

  • Affordability ratio = average house price ÷ average yearly earnings.
  • In 2025, homes in London sold for 10.6 times average earnings; in the North East, 5.0 times.
  • In 19 of London's 33 local authorities the ratio was above 12.
  • Too few homes where people want to live pushes prices up.
Mission 1

The government response

  • In July 2024 the government set councils in England mandatory housing targets to deliver 1.5 million homes over five years.
  • Councils must review Green Belt land if needed, prioritising "grey belt" land.
  • This creates a land-use conflict: homes v. farmland, Green Belt and countryside.
Quick check
?Why is the housing shortage most severe in London?
Mission check

?A plan proposes 10,000 new homes on farmland in Kent, in the dry south-east, near commuter links to London. Which issue does the plan ease and which could it make worse?

The UK's people are concentrated in the dry, lowland south and east; its rain falls mostly on the sparse, upland north and west. That mismatch makes water stress and housing shortage two sides of the same pressure.

Mission 2

Mission 2: In one English district a third of people are 65 or over; in another, one in eighteen. What makes a place old?

Make a guess: In one English district a third of people are 65 or over; in another, one in eighteen. What makes a place old?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR GCSE Geography B (J384)

  • 7.1b UK population trends since 2001 from pyramids and migration data; the UK's DTM stage
  • 7.1b UK ageing population: causes, effects, spatial distribution, responses
  • 7.1b how the population structure and ethnic diversity of a named UK place have changed since 2001
Mission 2

The UK keeps growing

  • Mid-2001: 59.1 million people. Mid-2025: 69.5 million.
  • That is about 10.4 million more, a rise of 17.5% (our calculation).
  • Growth in the year to mid-2025 was 0.3%, the slowest since mid-2020.
Mission 2

Fewer births

  • Year to mid-2025: 652,400 births, the lowest for at least 43 years; 647,500 deaths.
  • So natural change (births − deaths) added only 4,900 people.
  • In England and Wales the total fertility rate was 1.39 children per woman in 2025.
  • Social factor: "people are having fewer children and are having children later in life".
Mission 2

Migration into the UK

  • Year to mid-2025: 886,200 people arrived to live for a year or more; 663,400 left.
  • Net international migration = 222,900, down from 651,700 the year before.
  • Net migration has been the largest driver of UK growth in every year since mid-1999 except mid-2012.
  • Political factor: in December 2023 the government raised the earnings threshold for overseas workers by "nearly 50%".
Quick check
?In the year to mid-2025, what added most to the UK's population?
Mission 2

Pyramids and the DTM

  • In England and Wales the share aged 65+ rose from 16.0% (2001) to 18.6% (2021) (our calculation from census counts).
  • The UK's median age was 40.9 in mid-2025.
  • The pyramid is narrowing at the base and widening at the top.
  • DTM: low birth rate and low death rate = stage 4. With natural change near zero, and negative in Wales and Scotland, the UK shows signs of stage 5.
Quick check
?Which evidence best supports placing the UK at stage 4 moving towards stage 5 of the DTM?
Mission 2

Why is the UK ageing?

  • Living longer: improvements in life expectancy mean more people reach older ages.
  • Fewer children: fertility has fallen since the 1960s.
  • Baby boomers: people born in the 1960s are now moving into older age.
  • Ageing varies by place: in 2021, North Norfolk had the highest share aged 65+ (33.5%, about a third); Tower Hamlets in London had the lowest (5.6%, about one in eighteen).
Mission 2

Where are older people?

  • Older people make up higher proportions of rural and coastal areas than urban areas.
  • Highest share of 65+ in 2021: North Norfolk 33.5%, Rother 32.4%, East Lindsey 30.5%, all coastal.
  • Lowest: Tower Hamlets 5.6%, then Newham 7.2% (both London).
  • Young adults move to cities for study and work; many coastal and rural places keep or attract older residents.
Mission 2

Effects of an ageing population

  • Old-age dependency ratio: 285 people of pensionable age per 1,000 of working age in mid-2025.
  • Pressure on pensions, social care, housing and health services.
  • Older people in rural areas face "longer journeys" to reach services.
  • Opportunities too: volunteering, community activism, longer working lives, new markets.
Mission 2

Responses

  • Raise State Pension age: the Pensions Act 2014 brings in an "increase in pensionable age to 67", phased in from 2026.
  • Keep it under review: the Act requires a review of State Pension age every 6 years.
  • Save more: auto-enrolment in workplace pensions began in 2012.
  • Work longer: the "Default retirement age" (forced retirement at 65) "no longer exists".
Quick check
?The old-age dependency ratio rises. What does this mean?
Mission 2

A named place: Tower Hamlets, 2001–2021

  • Population: 196,106 (2001) → 310,306 (2021), +58.2% (our calculation).
  • 2011–2021: the largest percentage growth of any local authority in England (22.1%).
  • Aged 65+: 9.4% → 5.6%; aged 0–14: 21.5% → 17.4% (our calculations).
  • Aged 20–29: about one in four in both years (24.1% → 24.6%): a young, working-age borough.
Mission 2

Tower Hamlets: ethnic diversity

  • White British: 42.9% (2001) → 22.9% (2021).
  • Other White (e.g. other European): 6.5% → 14.6%.
  • Bangladeshi: 33.4% → 34.6%, now the largest single group as the White British share fell.
  • Black African 3.4% → 5.0%; Mixed 2.5% → 5.0%. The borough became more diverse.
Quick check
?Which statement about Tower Hamlets between 2001 and 2021 is supported by the census data?
Mission check

?A city borough has a university and many office jobs. Only 6% of its residents are 65 or over, and about a quarter are in their twenties. What best explains why it is so young?

Where people move decides how old a place is: young adults move to cities for study and work, while many rural and coastal places keep or attract older residents. Nationally, longer lives and fewer births make the whole UK older.

Mission 3

Mission 3: In 1978 a quarter of UK jobs were in manufacturing. Today it is about one in fifteen. What replaced them?

Make a guess: In 1978 a quarter of UK jobs were in manufacturing. Today it is about one in fifteen. What replaced them?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR GCSE Geography B (J384)

  • 7.1c major UK economic changes since 2001: political priorities, employment sectors, working hours
  • 7.1c pattern of the UK's core economic hubs
Mission 3

From making things to services

  • UK workforce jobs in manufacturing (June each year):
  • 1978: 6.73 million of 26.99 million = 24.9% (our calculation)
  • 2001: 3.83 million of 30.14 million = 12.7%
  • 2026: 2.46 million of 36.73 million = 6.7%
  • Services rose to 84.8% of all jobs in 2026 (31.16 million).
  • This is deindustrialisation: the decline of the traditional industrial base.
Mission 3

A post-industrial economy

  • A post-industrial economy earns most of its income from services and knowledge, not factories.
  • Tertiary jobs provide a service (shops, health, transport); quaternary jobs are based on knowledge: research, IT, consultancy.
  • Professional, scientific and technical jobs: 1.85 million (2001) → 3.50 million (2026), up 90% (our calculation).
  • Information and communication (IT, media, telecoms): 1.21 million → 1.61 million.
  • Finance: UK financial services exports were worth £98.7 billion in 2025.
Quick check
?Which job is in the quaternary sector?
Mission 3

Why the shift happened

  • Manufacturing employment "declined drastically from the 1960s onwards".
  • Globalisation: many goods are now made more cheaply abroad and imported.
  • Technology: machines do more of the work in the factories that remain.
  • Rising incomes: people spend more on services such as health, travel and finance; tertiary jobs reached 83.6% of employment by 2016.
Mission 3

Working hours

  • Average actual weekly hours worked, all workers, UK (April–June):
  • 2001: 32.8 hours → 2026: 32.1 hours
  • Full-time workers: 38.0 → 36.7 hours; part-time: 15.7 → 16.7 hours.
  • A slightly shorter working week for full-time workers; part-time workers work slightly longer.
Mission 3

Political priorities since 2001

  • 2011–12: Enterprise Zones to help "the parts of Britain that had missed out", with business-rate discounts.
  • 2019: the UK set in law a net zero target for 2050 (the 2050 target raised "from 80% to 100%").
  • 2020: the UK left the EU at 11.00 p.m. on 31 January.
  • 2025: a 10-year Industrial Strategy backs 8 growth-driving sectors: advanced manufacturing, clean energy, creative industries, defence, digital and technologies, financial services, life sciences, professional and business services.
Quick check
?Which of these is a political priority that has shaped the UK economy since 2001?
Mission 3

The UK's core economic hubs

  • An economic hub is "a focal point for the economy of its area".
  • London produced 22.8% of UK GDP in 2024; London, the South East and the East together 45.6% (our calculations).
  • Outside London, Greater Manchester had the largest GDP of the combined authorities (£114.5 billion).
  • Hubs also include specialist clusters, such as the Cambridge science and technology cluster.
Mission check

?A northern town loses its steelworks. Ten years later most new jobs are in a hospital, a call centre and a software firm. Which description fits best?

In the UK, manufacturing fell from 24.9% of jobs in 1978 to 6.7% in 2026, and services rose to 84.8%.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Greater Manchester's economy has grown faster than the UK's this century. What changed in this northern hub?

Make a guess: Greater Manchester's economy has grown faster than the UK's this century. What changed in this northern hub?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR GCSE Geography B (J384)

  • 7.1c pattern of the UK's core economic hubs
  • 7.1c changes in one economic hub and its significance to its region and the UK
Mission 4

What is an economic hub?

  • An economic hub is "a focal point for the economy of its area".
  • It can be part of a city (London's financial district), a whole city (Manchester) or a region (Silicon Fen, around Cambridge).
  • Hubs pull in firms, workers and investment, and serve the area around them.
Mission 4

The pattern: London and the South East

  • London produced 22.8% of UK GDP in 2024 (our calculation).
  • London, the South East and the East of England together produced 45.6% (our calculation).
  • GDP per person: £72,201 in London, £29,957 in the North East, the lowest.
Mission 4

Other core hubs

  • Among England's combined authorities in 2024:
  • Greater Manchester had the largest economy: £114.5 billion GDP.
  • West of England (around Bristol) had the highest GDP per person, £50,353; then Cambridgeshire and Peterborough, £45,769.
  • So the pattern: one dominant capital, then a few large city-regions and smaller high-tech hubs.
Quick check
?Which statement best describes the pattern of the UK's core economic hubs?
Mission 4

Greater Manchester: the hub

  • A city-region in north-west England, centred on the city of Manchester.
  • £114.5 billion GDP in 2024: 40.1% of the North West's and 4.0% of the UK's (our calculations).
  • The city of Manchester's population grew 9.7% from 2011 to 2021, to about 552,000, faster than England's 6.6%.
Mission 4

Change 1: from making things to services

  • Production (mainly manufacturing) fell from 18.3% of Greater Manchester's output in 2001 to 10.0% in 2024 (our calculation).
  • Services rose from 75.3% to 84.3% (our calculation).
  • Textiles now make up just 0.5% of its output.
Mission 4

Change 2: the growth sectors

  • Legal and accounting: from 2.3% to 5.0% of output, 2001–2024.
  • Computer programming: from 1.5% to 2.8%.
  • Broadcasting: output rose from £47 million to £527 million: over 11 times (current prices).
  • Health: from 5.2% to 7.2%.
  • Exam move: name the change, give the figure, then explain one cause (e.g. new office jobs need graduates from the city's universities).
Quick check
?In real terms, Greater Manchester's GDP rose from £69,463 million in 2001 to £110,510 million in 2024. What was the percentage increase?
Mission 4

Faster than the UK

  • Real GDP growth, 2001–2024 (our calculations):
  • UK: +40.1%.
  • Greater Manchester: +59.1%.
  • City of Manchester (the core): +113.1%.
  • The core grew fastest: the city of Manchester more than doubled its real output.
Mission 4

Significance, and who benefits

  • To the region: 40% of the North West's output; people commute in from across the region for work.
  • To the UK: the largest economy outside London among the combined authorities; a counterweight to London's dominance.
  • Uneven gains: GDP per person in 2024 was £67,635 in Manchester but £23,932 in north-east Greater Manchester.
  • Careful: GDP per person is counted where people work, so commuting into the city inflates its figure.
Quick check
?GDP per person was £67,635 in the city of Manchester but £23,932 in north-east Greater Manchester. Why should you be cautious about this gap?
Mission check

?A northern city lost most of its factory jobs, yet its economy now grows faster than the national average. What is most likely to explain this?

Greater Manchester's production share fell from 18% to 10% while services rose to 84%. Legal, digital, broadcasting and health activity grew fastest, and real GDP rose 59% from 2001 to 2024, against 40% for the UK.

Mission 5

Mission 5: One veto can block the UN Security Council. How else can Britain act over the war in Ukraine?

Make a guess: One veto can block the UN Security Council. How else can Britain act over the war in Ukraine?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR GCSE Geography B (J384)

  • 7.2a the UK's political role in one global conflict through its participation in international organisations
Mission 5

The conflict

  • In 2014 Russia annexed Crimea, part of Ukraine.
  • In February 2022 Russia launched a full-scale invasion of Ukraine.
  • Countries cannot simply act alone: they work through international organisations with rules and members.
  • This mission follows the UK's role in four of them.
Mission 5

The UK's seats at the table

  • United Nations (UN): the UK is one of five permanent members of the Security Council, with China, France, Russia and the USA.
  • NATO: a military alliance of North American and European countries; the UK is a founding member.
  • International Criminal Court (ICC): tries individuals for war crimes, crimes against humanity and genocide. The UK is a member state.
Mission 5

The UN Security Council and the veto

  • 15 members. A "no" from any one of the five permanent members stops action: a veto.
  • 25 February 2022: a draft resolution demanding Russia stop its attack and withdraw. 11 voted for; China, India and the UAE abstained; Russia voted against, so it failed.
  • The UK voted for it. Its ambassador said: "Make no mistake, Russia is isolated".
Quick check
?Eleven of the fifteen Security Council members voted for the draft on 25 February 2022. Why did it fail?
Mission 5

The UN General Assembly

  • All 193 UN member states have a vote. No country has a veto here.
  • 2 March 2022: the Assembly demanded that Russia withdraw all its forces from Ukraine. 141 for, 5 against, 35 abstentions.
  • The UK voted for it.
  • Assembly resolutions show world opinion, but they cannot force a country to act.
Mission 5

NATO

  • After Russia annexed Crimea in 2014, NATO placed multinational battlegroups in its eastern member states.
  • The UK is the framework nation (the leading country) for the battlegroup in Estonia.
  • In March 2022 NATO leaders agreed four more battlegroups, in Bulgaria, Hungary, Romania and Slovakia.
  • Ukraine is not a NATO member, so NATO's role is to defend its own members and deter attack.
Mission 5

The International Criminal Court

  • On 2 March 2022 the ICC Prosecutor received referrals of the situation in Ukraine from 39 member states, including the UK.
  • This let the Prosecutor open an investigation into alleged war crimes and crimes against humanity.
  • The ICC tries individuals, not countries.
Quick check
?Which action did the UK take through NATO?
Mission 5

How much influence?

  • Strengths: a permanent seat gives the UK a voice and a vote on every Security Council decision; it leads a NATO battlegroup; it helped start an ICC investigation.
  • Limits: Russia's veto blocks Security Council action; Assembly votes cannot be enforced; in each body the UK is one member among many.
  • Exam move: "Examine" means weigh both sides. Name the organisation, the date and the UK's action, then judge how far it changed events.
Mission check

?A Security Council draft on a different conflict is vetoed by a permanent member. What could the UK still do through international organisations?

Over Ukraine, the UK used its Security Council seat, voted in the General Assembly, led a NATO battlegroup and referred the situation to the ICC. The veto blocked one route, not all of them.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Britain sells its films and TV to the world. What has the world brought to Britain's own culture?

Make a guess: Britain sells its films and TV to the world. What has the world brought to Britain's own culture?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR GCSE Geography B (J384)

  • 7.2b UK media exports (television, film) and their global influence
  • 7.2b contribution of ethnic groups to UK cultural life through one of food, media or fashion (food chosen: South Asian food and the curry house)
Mission 6

Media exports and soft power

  • A media export is a film, TV programme, TV format or music sold to or watched in other countries.
  • Exports earn money and spread British stories, places and accents.
  • This builds soft power: influence through attraction, not force.
  • The UK government's 2026 review says audiences trust the BBC World Service because it is independent of government.
Mission 6

UK films worldwide

  • 2023: UK-qualifying films took 22.5% of box office across 23 major film markets: $6.1 billion of $27.2 billion.
  • Top title in those markets: Barbie (UK/USA), $1,234.1 million.
  • UK independent films took only 0.9%. Most of the share comes from UK/US studio films made in the UK.
Mission 6

UK TV worldwide

  • Doctor Who first aired on the BBC in 1963.
  • In 2013 its 50th-anniversary episode was screened at the same time on TV and in cinemas around the world.
  • A Peruvian fan club drew over 700 members.
  • UK TV also sells formats: the idea and rules of a show, remade with local presenters in other countries.
Mission 6

Global influence

  • Stories: films based on UK writers' stories took huge sums, e.g. The Jungle Book (Kipling), $967 million; Spectre (Fleming), $882 million.
  • Tourism: film and TV draw visitors to the UK to see filming locations.
  • Investment: film and TV attracted £4.8 billion of inward investment.
Quick check
?In 2023 UK-qualifying films took 22.5% of box office in 23 markets, but UK independent films took only 0.9%. What does this suggest?
Mission 6

Ethnic groups and UK culture

  • 2021 Census: 9.3% of people in England and Wales (5.5 million) were in the Asian, Asian British or Asian Welsh group, up from 7.5% (4.2 million) in 2011.
  • Migrants and their families have shaped UK food, media and fashion.
  • This mission uses food: South Asian food and the British curry house.
Mission 6

The curry house

  • In 2009 an MP told Parliament there were "more than 12,000" curry houses, employing about 80,000 people and worth an estimated £3.5 billion a year.
  • In 2016 a member of the House of Lords said "large numbers" are owned by Bangladeshis, and put their value at an estimated £4 billion.
  • These are estimates quoted in Parliament, not official statistics, and they differ.
Mission 6

Cultural hybridity

  • Cultural hybridity: cultures mix and create something new.
  • In 2001 the politician Robin Cook called chicken tikka masala a British national dish, as members of both Houses of Parliament later recalled.
  • In 2009 a government minister described Birmingham as "the birthplace of balti".
  • South Asian cooking, adapted for British tastes, became part of everyday British food.
Quick check
?Which is the best example of cultural hybridity?
Mission check

?In 2017 an MP told Parliament that Chinese and Indian restaurants together contribute £5.5 billion to the UK economy and employ 250,000 people. Which conclusion is best supported?

The UK exports films, TV and stories that shape how the world sees it. In return, migrants and their families have added to UK culture: in food, the curry house and hybrid dishes such as balti and chicken tikka masala.

Exam move: name a group, a place and a specific contribution (e.g. Bangladeshi-owned curry houses; balti in Birmingham), then explain why it matters to UK cultural life.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Density, land use, relief and rainfall do not line up: people in the south-east, rain in the north-west.
  • Water stress: seriously stressed company areas cluster in London, the south-east and the east.
  • Housing shortage: least affordable in London (10.6× earnings); target of 1.5 million homes.

Mission 2

  • Growth since 2001: +17.5%; mostly net migration; natural change near zero.
  • Ageing: living longer + fewer children + baby boomers; effects on pensions, care and housing.
  • DTM stage 4, edging to 5; oldest districts coastal (North Norfolk 33.5%); responses: pension age to 67, auto-enrolment.
  • Tower Hamlets 2001–2021: +58.2%, younger, more diverse.

Mission 3

  • Manufacturing jobs: 24.9% → 6.7% (1978–2026); services 84.8%.
  • Hours slightly shorter (32.8 → 32.1); policies: Enterprise Zones, net zero, EU exit, Industrial Strategy; hubs concentrated in London and the south-east.

Mission 4

  • UK hubs: London dominates (22.8% of GDP); large city-regions (Greater Manchester, £114.5 bn) and high-tech hubs (West of England, Cambridgeshire) follow.
  • Greater Manchester changed from production to services (84% of output in 2024) and grew faster than the UK, but gains are uneven between the core and outer boroughs.

Mission 5

  • The UK acts through international organisations: the UN Security Council (permanent member), the General Assembly, NATO and the ICC.
  • Over Ukraine, Russia's veto blocked the Security Council, so the UK used other bodies; their power is real but limited.

Mission 6

  • UK media exports (films, TV, formats) reach global audiences, build soft power, and draw tourists and investment.
  • Ethnic groups have shaped UK culture; in food, South Asian cooking became part of everyday British life through cultural hybridity.
