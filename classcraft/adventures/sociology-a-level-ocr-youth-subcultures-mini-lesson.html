Youth subcultures

This lesson covers the two OCR key questions for this option: How and why are youth culture and subcultures formed? and Why do young people participate in deviant subcultures?

KQ1 · functionalism

Functionalism: youth culture as a bridge

  • Talcott Parsons saw youth culture as a bridge between the family and adult society. The family judges you by particularistic, ascribed standards ('you are my child'); the adult world judges you by universalistic, achieved ones ('you are an employee'). Youth culture, with its own norms and peer solidarity, cushions that jolt — a period of licensed irresponsibility before adult roles begin.
  • Shmuel Eisenstadt (From Generation to Generation, 1956) developed the same argument: age groups provide young people with status and belonging during the transition, and so integrate them into society rather than threatening it.
  • The functionalist verdict: youth culture is functional — it manages a potentially destabilising transition, and most young people emerge as conforming adults. The evidence for that is real: the great majority of young people never join a spectacular subculture and never offend.

Evaluation. Functionalism assumes one youth culture shared by all young people, and it ignores class, gender and ethnicity — which is precisely what the next fifty years of research went on to show mattered most. It also cannot explain oppositional subcultures, which do not smooth the transition to adulthood but attack the value consensus itself.

KQ1 · subcultural theory

Status frustration and opportunity structures

  • Albert Cohen (Delinquent Boys, 1955) built on Merton's strain theory but asked a question Merton could not answer: why is much delinquency non-utilitarian — vandalism, fighting, truancy — with no financial gain? His answer: working-class boys internalise mainstream success goals but are denied the means to achieve them, and so suffer status frustration. They respond by inverting mainstream values, forming a delinquent subculture with an alternative status hierarchy in which the very things that get you condemned at school earn you respect on the street.
  • Cloward and Ohlin (Delinquency and Opportunity, 1960) added the decisive refinement: blocked legitimate opportunity is not enough — the illegitimate opportunities available differ by neighbourhood. They identified three subcultures: criminal (a stable local criminal hierarchy offers an apprenticeship in crime), conflict (no such structure exists, so status is won through violence and territory) and retreatist (those who fail in both worlds — 'double failures' — retreat into drug use).

Evaluation: both assume the boys shared mainstream goals in the first place; both are overwhelmingly about boys (McRobbie's charge); and both risk making delinquency sound like an automatic reaction rather than a choice. But the concept of an alternative status hierarchy remains one of the most useful ideas in the whole option.

KQ1 · neo-Marxism and the CCCS

The CCCS: style as resistance

The Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies at Birmingham — the CCCS — transformed the field in the 1970s by reading spectacular youth subcultures as class phenomena.

  • Stuart Hall and Tony Jefferson (Resistance Through Rituals, 1976) argued that working-class youth subcultures are a form of symbolic resistance to their class subordination. They cannot change their material position, so they contest it culturally — through style, ritual and territory.
  • Phil Cohen (1972) analysed mods and skinheads as attempts to work out, symbolically, the contradictions of their parent culture as traditional working-class communities were broken up. The mod's smart suit and scooter magically resolved the aspiration to affluence; the skinhead's boots and braces magically reasserted a lost, hard, communal working-class masculinity. Both are what he called a 'magical' solution: they resolve the contradiction in imagination, not in fact.
  • Dick Hebdige (Subculture: The Meaning of Style, 1979) read style semiotically. Punks practised bricolage — taking ordinary objects (a safety pin, a bin liner, a swastika) and violently re-signifying them to disrupt the natural order of meanings. But Hebdige is not a romantic: he showed that resistance is always followed by incorporation — the market converts the style into a commodity (punk on the high street) and the media convert the punk into either a folk devil or a harmless eccentric. Resistance gets sold back to you.

Evaluation of the CCCS: it reads enormous political meaning into style choices that participants themselves might not recognise — a serious problem of validity, since the researchers largely interpreted rather than asked. It focuses on the spectacular minority and ignores the conforming majority. And, as McRobbie showed, it was written almost entirely about boys.

KQ1 · feminism

Where were the girls?

  • Angela McRobbie and Jenny Garber ('Girls and Subcultures', 1976) made the decisive feminist intervention: girls were absent from CCCS research not because they were absent from youth culture, but because male researchers were looking in male spaces — the street, the football terrace, the gig.
  • Their answer was bedroom culture: girls' subcultural life was organised in the private sphere — friendship groups, magazines, music, dancing, talk — because girls were far more closely supervised by parents, and because the street was dangerous and reputationally risky for them.
  • Reputation as control. Sue Lees' work showed that girls' behaviour was policed by sexual name-calling, which had no male equivalent. The double standard is itself a mechanism of subcultural exclusion — a boy's street reputation is an asset, a girl's is a liability.
  • Later feminist work insists that girls are present in public subcultures — in punk, rave and grime scenes — and that they must be studied on their own terms rather than as girlfriends of the boys.

This is a methods point as well as a theory point (synoptic!). The absence of girls in the classic studies is partly an artefact of where male ethnographers chose to stand. Who you can access determines what you can find.

KQ1 · postmodernism

Is 'subculture' still a useful word?

  • David Muggleton (Inside Subculture, 2000) interviewed participants themselves — and found they did not describe their style as class resistance. They valued individuality, fluidity and authenticity, moved between styles, and rejected being categorised at all. His conclusion: the CCCS was projecting a political meaning onto people who did not hold it.
  • Andy Bennett (1999) proposed replacing 'subculture' with neo-tribes: loose, temporary, fluid groupings organised around taste and consumption, which people drift in and out of rather than belonging to.
  • Sarah Thornton (Club Cultures, 1995) offered the sharpest bridge between the two camps with subcultural capital — Bourdieu applied to youth: knowing the right music, the right club, the right slang is a resource that confers status within the scene. And crucially, she showed that the media are not external to subcultures reporting on them: media coverage is part of how a subculture constitutes itself, and being condemned by the tabloids is good for your subcultural capital.

The balanced judgement: postmodernists are right that today's styles are more fluid, commercialised and consumer-driven, and that participants' own meanings must be taken seriously. But they overstate freedom — access to scenes still costs money, is still shaped by class and place, and the young people most heavily policed as 'gangs' do not get to fluidly opt out of that label. Structure has not vanished simply because style has diversified.

KQ1 · class, gender, ethnicity, hybridity

Subcultures and social divisions

  • Class. The CCCS tradition reads spectacular subcultures as working-class; middle-class youth cultures (hippies, indie scenes) were read by the CCCS as counter-cultures — more explicitly political, longer-lasting, and drawing on the resources of a class that could afford to drop out.
  • Ethnicity. Black and Asian British youth cultures have been sites of powerful cultural creativity — sound systems, reggae, jungle, grime, drill — and also of intense policing. Claire Alexander's The Asian Gang (2000) is essential: her ethnography found that the 'Asian gang' widely feared in the 1990s was substantially a media and policy construction, projected onto ordinary friendship groups of young Bangladeshi men. The 'gang' existed more securely in the newspapers than on the street.
  • Hybridity. Les Back's South London research found young people creating genuinely new, mixed cultural forms across ethnic lines — sharing music, language and style. Youth culture is one of the main engines of cultural hybridity in modern Britain, which links this option straight back to Section A.

Do not treat ethnic-minority youth culture as automatically deviant. That slippage — from cultural difference to presumed criminality — is itself the thing Alexander and Hall are analysing.

KQ2 · deviant subcultures

Delinquent, criminal, spectacular, anti-school — and gangs

  • Delinquent subcultures — Cohen's status-frustrated boys; largely non-utilitarian rule-breaking.
  • Criminal subcultures — Cloward and Ohlin's stable, organised route into acquisitive crime.
  • Spectacular youth subcultures — teds, mods, rockers, skinheads, punks: highly visible style subcultures, deviant chiefly in appearance and attitude rather than in serious criminality.
  • Anti-school subcultures — Paul Willis's Learning to Labour (1977) followed twelve working-class 'lads' who rejected school, celebrated 'having a laff', and saw manual work as masculine and mental work as effeminate. The devastating irony Willis identifies: their resistance guaranteed the very future they were rebelling against. They saw through the meritocratic myth — and that clear-sightedness delivered them to the factory floor.
  • Gangs. Handle this term with extreme care. John Pitts' Reluctant Gangsters (2008) argued that gang involvement in deprived areas is often not a free choice but something young people are drawn into by neighbourhood, debt and threat. And Alexander shows how readily the label 'gang' is applied to groups of minority-ethnic young men who are simply friends.

KQ2 · patterns and trends

Patterns of youth deviance — and why the statistics lie

OCR asks you to consider patterns and trends in youth deviance by class, gender and ethnicity, based on official statistics. So use them — and then interrogate them.

  • Class. Official statistics show offending concentrated among working-class young people. But self-report studies consistently show far more widespread offending across classes than the official figures capture. What differs is not necessarily the offending; it is who gets caught, charged and recorded.
  • Gender. Recorded offending is overwhelmingly male. Explanations: differential socialisation and the greater control exercised over girls (Heidensohn's 'control theory'); Connell's hegemonic masculinity; and the chivalry thesis — the argument that the criminal justice system treats women more leniently, so their offending is under-recorded. The counter-argument is that women who breach gender norms are treated more harshly, not less.
  • Ethnicity. Black young people are heavily over-represented in stop-and-search and in the criminal justice system. Two readings: either differential offending, or — far better supported — differential policing and institutional racism (Macpherson, 1999). Once you accept that police discretion and public reporting shape who enters the statistics, the statistics stop being a measure of offending and become a measure of the criminal justice system itself.

Synoptic gold: whenever OCR gives you 'evidence presented by official statistics', your first move is to ask how those statistics were made. That is Component 02 doing work in a Component 01 answer.

KQ2 · the media and youth deviance

Folk devils, moral panics and deviance amplification

  • Stanley Cohen (Folk Devils and Moral Panics, 1972) studied the media reaction to disturbances between mods and rockers at English seaside resorts in 1964. He found the actual events were minor and the reporting exaggerated, distorted, predicted and symbolised them. The young people were cast as folk devils — visible embodiments of what is wrong with society — and the reaction became a deviance amplification spiral: sensational coverage produced public outrage, which produced more policing, which produced more arrests, which produced more coverage. Crucially, the coverage also created the two groups as opposed identities and drew more young people into them. The reaction manufactured the deviance it claimed to be reporting.
  • Jock Young (The Drugtakers, 1971) found the same spiral among marijuana users in Notting Hill: intensified policing pushed drug use to the centre of a formerly marginal identity, and turned casual users into a self-conscious deviant subculture.
  • Stuart Hall and colleagues (Policing the Crisis, 1978) push this much further and much darker. Analysing the 1970s panic over 'mugging', they argued the panic was not a simple over-reaction: it functioned ideologically. At a moment of economic crisis and rising class conflict, the black 'mugger' provided a folk devil onto whom public anxiety could be displaced, legitimating a drift towards a more authoritarian state. The moral panic did political work — it manufactured consent for the crackdown.

Know the difference between the two Cohens and the two theories. Stanley Cohen = the media over-react and amplify. Stuart Hall = the panic serves the interests of the powerful in a crisis of hegemony. One is interactionist; one is neo-Marxist. Getting this contrast right is a top-band discriminator.

KQ2 · explanations

Why do young people join deviant subcultures?

OCR names four families of explanation. Run them as a debate.

  • Functionalism / New Right. Deviant subcultures arise where socialisation and social control have failed. Charles Murray's underclass thesis blames absent fathers, welfare dependency and the collapse of the two-parent family for producing young men without discipline or role models. Critique: this blames the victim and ignores the structural collapse of stable work in the areas concerned.
  • Marxism / neo-Marxism. Subcultures are a response to class inequality: either symbolic resistance (CCCS) or a rational response to blocked material opportunity. The 'problem' is not the young people but the system that offers them nothing. Critique: over-politicises style; ignores those who conform.
  • Interactionism. Howard Becker: deviance is not a quality of the act but of the label applied to it. Once labelled, a young person may face a master status that overrides everything else about them, be excluded from conventional opportunities, and fulfil the self-fulfilling prophecy by joining others similarly labelled. Cohen's amplification spiral is this argument at the level of a whole society.
  • Culture and identity. Young people join subcultures because they offer belonging, status and a way of being someone — particularly where ethnic or gender identity is under pressure. This is why subcultures flourish precisely where mainstream routes to respect are closed.

Youth subcultures — the big ideas

Functionalism: Parsons and Eisenstadt — youth culture as a bridge to adult roles

Subcultural theory: Albert Cohen (status frustration, alternative status hierarchy) · Cloward and Ohlin (criminal, conflict, retreatist)

CCCS / neo-Marxism: Hall and Jefferson (resistance through rituals) · Phil Cohen (the magical solution) · Hebdige (bricolage, style and incorporation)
