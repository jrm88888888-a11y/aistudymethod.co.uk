3.1 What is an ecosystem?

Ecosystems, biomes & biotic vs abiotic

An ecosystem is a community of living things (plants and animals) together with the non-living environment they live in, and the way they all interact.

  • Biotic parts — the living components: plants, animals, insects, fungi and micro-organisms.
  • Abiotic parts — the non-living components: climate (temperature, rainfall, sunlight), soil, water and rock.
  • A biome is a very large, global-scale ecosystem (e.g. tropical rainforest, savanna, hot desert) defined mainly by its climate.

Key idea: ecosystems come in all sizes — from a single pond up to a whole biome. The living (biotic) and non-living (abiotic) parts are all linked, so a change to one affects the others.

Biomes & climate zones

Why biomes sit in latitude belts

Biomes are arranged in rough belts around the globe because climate changes with latitude — and climate is driven by global patterns of air pressure and rainfall.

  • At the equator, intense sun heats the air, which rises — this is a belt of low pressure giving heavy rain all year, so tropical rainforest grows.
  • Near 30° N and S, that air has cooled and sinks as dry high pressure, giving hot deserts and, at their edges, hot semi-arid grassland (savanna).

Contrasting biome 1

Tropical rainforest

The tropical rainforest is hot and wet all year (typically around 26–28°C with over 2,000 mm of rain a year). This constant warmth and moisture supports the most biodiverse biome on Earth, arranged in distinct layers:

Key idea: because it is hot and wet all year, dead leaves rot very quickly and nutrients are taken straight back up by the trees — so nutrient cycling is rapid and most nutrients are stored in the biomass, leaving the soil surprisingly poor.

Contrasting biome 2

Hot semi-arid grassland (savanna)

The savanna, or hot semi-arid grassland, lies between the rainforest and the hot deserts. Its climate has a distinct wet season and dry season, so the plants and animals must survive long periods of drought:

  • Vegetation — mostly grasses with scattered, drought-resistant trees such as the acacia (small waxy leaves, long roots to reach deep water).
  • Animals — large grazing herds (e.g. zebra, wildebeest) that migrate to follow the rains, and predators that follow them.
  • Adaptations — grasses grow fast in the wet season then die back and survive underground in the dry season; many plants can regrow after fire, which is common on the dry grassland.

Contrast: the rainforest is hot and wet all year and stores nutrients in its biomass; the savanna has seasonal drought, so life is adapted to survive the dry season and to bounce back with the rains.

Your turn — rainforest nutrients

✎The tropical rainforest is lush, yet its soil is surprisingly poor in nutrients. Explain why. Use the words hot and wet, decompose, biomass and uptake.
Model answer
  • Because the climate is hot and wet all year, dead leaves that fall to the floor decompose (rot) very quickly.
  • The released nutrients are almost immediately taken back up by plant roots (rapid uptake), so they do not build up in the soil.
  • This means most nutrients are stored in the living biomass (the plants and trees), leaving the soil itself thin and nutrient-poor.
Interdependence

Everything is interdependent

In an ecosystem the climate, soils, vegetation, animals and humans all depend on one another. Change one part and there are knock-on effects right through the system:

Worked example: if the rainforest trees are cleared, the soil is exposed to heavy rain and nutrients are washed away (leached); with no leaves falling the nutrient cycle breaks; animals lose their food and shelter; and the local climate becomes drier. One change ripples through the whole ecosystem.

Biodiversity

Biodiversity: the variety of life

Biodiversity means the range of different species living in an ecosystem. Warm, wet, stable environments tend to have the highest biodiversity:

Why it matters: high biodiversity makes an ecosystem more resilient — if one species is lost, others can fill its role. Losing biodiversity (e.g. through deforestation) makes the whole system less stable.

Your turn — surviving the savanna

✎Explain two ways plants and animals are adapted to survive the savanna's climate. Use the words dry season, roots, migrate and fire.
Model answer
  • To survive the long dry season, trees like the acacia have long roots to reach deep water and small waxy leaves to cut water loss; grasses die back above ground but survive from their roots.
  • Many grasses and trees can regrow quickly after fire, which is common on the dry grassland.
  • Grazing animals such as wildebeest and zebra migrate to follow the rains and fresh grass when their area dries out.

Using & managing the pond

How the UK pond is used & managed

People use and value a pond ecosystem — for wildlife, education and recreation — but it faces threats that have to be managed:

  • Pollution & nutrient run-off — fertiliser or rubbish washing in can trigger algal blooms that use up the oxygen and kill fish.
  • Silting & overgrowth — a pond naturally fills with sediment and vegetation, which can eventually turn it into dry land.
  • Invasive species — non-native plants can crowd out the native pondweeds and reduce biodiversity.

Management: conservation groups (e.g. Wildlife Trusts) manage ponds by clearing excess vegetation, occasionally de-silting, keeping fertiliser buffer strips around the edge, and removing invasive species — keeping the ecosystem healthy and biodiverse while people still enjoy it.

Recap

The key ideas to know

Ecosystem: living (biotic) + non-living (abiotic) parts, all interacting. A biome is a global-scale ecosystem set by climate.

Biomes & climate: equator = rising air, low pressure, wet → rainforest; ~30° = sinking air, high pressure, dry → hot desert/savanna.

Tropical rainforest: hot & wet all year, layered structure, very high biodiversity, rapid nutrient cycling, nutrients stored in biomass (poor soil).

Savanna (hot semi-arid grassland): wet & dry seasons, grasses + scattered acacia, grazing herds that migrate, adapted to drought & fire.

Interdependence: climate, soil, plants, animals & humans are linked — one change has knock-on effects.

Nutrient cycle: stores = biomass, litter, soil; flows = uptake, fall, decompose (Gersmehl).

Food webs: producers → primary → secondary → tertiary consumers; energy lost at each trophic level.

Biodiversity: variety of species; highest in the rainforest; makes ecosystems more resilient.

UK ecosystem: a freshwater pond — its own food web; managed by clearing vegetation, de-silting & reducing run-off.
