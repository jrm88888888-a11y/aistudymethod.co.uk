Mission 1

Mission 1: How do scientists know what Earth's climate was like 800,000 years ago, long before thermometers?

Make a guess: How do scientists know what Earth's climate was like 800,000 years ago, long before thermometers?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 5.1.1 overview of Quaternary climate change: cyclical glacial and inter-glacial periods
  • 5.1.1 validity of evidence (e.g. tree rings, historical accounts, temperature records), including ice cores and CO₂ levels
Mission 1

The Quaternary period

  • The Quaternary is the most recent period of geological time. It began 2.58 million years ago.
  • We live in its latest part, the Holocene, which began about 11,700 years ago (0.0117 million years).
  • Through the Quaternary the climate has switched again and again between cold and warm.
Mission 1

Glacials and interglacials

  • Glacials: long cold periods when ice sheets grew. Interglacials: shorter warm periods, like today.
  • In the last 800,000 years, warm interglacials came "roughly every 100,000 years".
  • At the peak of the last glacial, glaciers covered about 32% of Earth's land; today about 10%.
  • In England, ice sheets dumped the boulder clay (till) that now forms the Holderness cliffs: tills about 18,000 to 13,000 years old.
  • Smaller swings happen too: the Little Ice Age (about the early 1300s to the mid-1800s; sources date its start differently), when glaciers advanced in many areas.
Quick check
?Which statement best describes climate in the Quaternary?
Mission 1

Ice cores: how they work

  • Snow builds up in layers on ice sheets and is pressed into ice.
  • As it does, "the ice encloses small bubbles of air that contain a sample of the atmosphere". Scientists measure the CO₂ in the bubbles.
  • The ratios of different water isotopes in the ice show past temperature.
  • The oldest continuous records reach 800,000 years in Antarctica and 123,000 years in Greenland.
Mission 1

What ice cores show

  • In the ice-age cycles of the last million years or so, CO₂ "didn't get any higher than 300 ppm". Before the Industrial Revolution it was 280 ppm or less.
  • CO₂ and temperature rose and fell together through glacials and interglacials.
  • Today CO₂ is "nearly 50% higher than it was before the industrial revolution".
Quick check
?In ice cores, the fastest natural CO₂ rise is about 15 ppm in 200 years. Today CO₂ rises 15 ppm every 6 years. About how many times faster is today's rise?
Mission 1

Temperature records

  • Central England Temperature: monthly data since 1659, "the longest instrumental record of temperature in the world". It covers a triangle from Lancashire to London to Bristol.
  • Global records using thermometers start around 1850. In 2024 the world was about 1.55 °C warmer than in 1850–1900.
  • Reliability: accurate and direct, but short; early records cover few places. Since 1974, Central England data "have been adjusted to allow for urban warming".
Mission 1

Tree rings and pollen

  • Tree rings: trees grow one ring a year. Wider rings usually mean warmer or wetter growing seasons.
  • Pollen: pollen grains in lake mud and peat show which plants grew there. Warmth-loving trees mean a warmer climate.
  • Reliability: tree rings give exact years but only for the tree's lifetime and place, and growth also depends on rain, soil and disease. Pollen covers thousands of years but is dated less precisely.
Mission 1

Historical sources: diaries and paintings

  • Diaries: in Edo (Tokyo), records of cherry blossom dates and blossom-viewing parties from 1601–1905 were used to estimate March temperatures.
  • They show two cold spells (late 1600s and early 1800s), matching cold periods found in Kyoto records.
  • Paintings: show frozen rivers, snowy winters or glacier fronts at a place and time.
  • Reliability: rich local detail, but one place, not measured, and artists may exaggerate. They must be checked against other evidence.
Mission 1

Weighing the evidence

  • Ice cores: longest and continuous; a direct sample of old air. Only polar ice, and "small artefacts can arise" where the ice holds many impurities.
  • Temperature records: most accurate; only about 175 to 370 years long.
  • Sea ice: satellite-accurate since 1979; patchy before.
  • Tree rings, pollen: long or yearly; local, and affected by more than temperature.
  • Diaries, paintings: local and descriptive; need checking against measurements.
  • Scientists trust a finding most when several types agree.
Quick check
?Which source is least reliable for working out the global average temperature of the 1600s?
Mission check

?A new Antarctic ice core is drilled. Ice from one layer, about 450,000 years old, has air bubbles with about 190 ppm of CO₂, and its water isotopes point to cold. What can scientists conclude?

Ice cores trap old air (CO₂) and record temperature in their isotopes, back 800,000 years. Scientists trust the record because ice cores, pollen, tree rings, temperature records and historical sources agree.

Mission 2: Most of Earth's carbon is locked away in rock. So why does burning a little of it matter so much?

Make a guess: Most of Earth's carbon is locked away in rock. So why does burning a little of it matter so much?

Specification: what this mission covers

Mission 2

Stores and flows

  • A carbon store (or sink) is where carbon is held: rocks, oceans, the atmosphere, plants, soils, fossil fuels.
  • A carbon flow (or transfer) moves carbon from one store to another.
  • Processes cause the flows: photosynthesis, respiration, decomposition, combustion, and exchange between the ocean and the air.
Mission 2

How big are the stores?

  • Rocks: "Most of Earth's carbon — about 65,500 billion metric tons — is stored in rocks".
  • Oceans: hold "about 50 times more carbon than the atmosphere".
  • Frozen soils (permafrost): Arctic and boreal permafrost hold 1,460–1,600 billion tonnes of organic carbon, "almost twice the carbon in the atmosphere".
  • The atmosphere is one of the smallest stores, so small changes to it matter.
Quick check
?Which of these is a flow, not a store?
Mission 2

The fast carbon cycle

  • Photosynthesis: plants take in CO₂ and water and use sunlight to make sugar, giving out oxygen.
  • Respiration and decomposition: living things and decomposers break sugar down, releasing CO₂ back to the air.
  • These flows move 1,000 to 100,000 million tonnes of carbon every year.
  • Each northern summer, growing plants draw about 2 parts per million of CO₂ out of the air, peaking in August.
Mission 2

Ocean exchange

  • CO₂ dissolves into seawater where the air has more of it than the water.
  • It is "more than twice as soluble in cold polar waters than in warm equatorial waters", so cold seas take up most.
  • Tiny ocean plants (phytoplankton) photosynthesise; some of their carbon sinks to the deep ocean.
  • Warm tropical upwelling waters give some CO₂ back to the air.
Mission 2

The slow carbon cycle

  • Carbon takes "between 100–200 million years" to move between rocks, soil, ocean and atmosphere.
  • Only 10–100 million tonnes of carbon a year move through this slow cycle.
  • Volcanoes return carbon from rocks to the air; weathering of rocks and shells sinking to the sea floor lock it away again.
Quick check
?In 2025, burning fossil fuels was projected to release 38.1 billion tonnes of CO₂, which is about 10.4 billion tonnes of carbon (CO₂ is 12/44 carbon by mass). The slow cycle moves 10–100 million tonnes of carbon a year. About how many times faster is burning?
Mission 2

Human activity 1: combustion

  • Combustion of coal, oil and gas takes carbon from the slow, rock store and puts it into the air in years, not millions of years.
  • 2025 projection: 38.1 billion tonnes of CO₂ from fossil fuels.
  • CO₂ in the air reached 423.9 ppm in 2024: 52% above pre-industrial levels (before 1750).
Mission 2

Human activity 2: land-use change

  • Deforestation: cutting and burning forests releases the carbon in trees and soils, and removes future photosynthesis.
  • Land-use change was projected to add 4.1 billion tonnes of CO₂ in 2025.
  • Farming and draining peat speed up decomposition of soil carbon.
  • Planting trees and restoring wetlands can move carbon back into the land store.
Mission 2

Where do our emissions go?

  • Over 2015–2024, of all the CO₂ people emitted:
    • 50% stayed in the atmosphere
    • 29% was taken up by the ocean
    • 21% was taken up by land (plants and soils).
  • The ocean store is changing too: surface pH has fallen by 0.1, about a 30% increase in acidity.
Quick check
?Over 2015–2024, about half of the CO₂ people emitted stayed in the atmosphere; the ocean took 29% and land 21%. If people emit 10 billion tonnes of CO₂, about how much stays in the atmosphere?
Mission 2

Can the sinks keep up?

  • As total emissions grow, the share taken up by land and ocean is projected to decrease.
  • Warming may turn some stores into sources: "Thawing terrestrial permafrost will lead to carbon release".
  • More carbon in the air → more warming → more carbon released: a positive feedback.
Mission check

?A peat bog is drained, then used for farming. Which change to the carbon cycle is most likely?

Humans speed up flows. Combustion and land-use change move carbon from stores that took millions of years to fill into the atmosphere, one of the smallest stores. About half stays there, which is why a "little" carbon matters so much.

Mission 3: In 1991 one volcano in the Philippines cooled the whole planet. How?

Mission 3

Volcanic eruptions: Pinatubo, 1991

  • In June 1991 Mount Pinatubo "injected about 15 million tons of sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere".
  • The gas formed tiny droplets (aerosols) that "scatter and absorb incoming sunlight".
  • High winds spread the haze around the world, so less sunlight reached the surface.
  • This is global dimming: the cooling Eduqas names as a natural cause.
Mission 3

How big, and how long?

  • Global average temperature fell by about 0.6 °C over the next 15 months.
  • There was "a measurable cooling of the Earth's surface for a period of almost two years".
  • The droplets slowly fall out of the stratosphere, so the cooling fades.
  • Ash falls out within days; it is the sulfur haze that cools the whole planet.
Quick check
?Why did Pinatubo's cooling last only about two years?
Mission 3

The natural greenhouse effect

  • Sunlight (short-wave radiation) passes through the atmosphere and warms the ground.
  • The ground gives off heat (long-wave, infrared radiation).
  • Greenhouse gases absorb this heat and send some back down, warming the lower atmosphere.
  • "Water vapour is the most important greenhouse gas, and carbon dioxide (CO2) is the second-most important one".
  • Without it, the surface would be about −19 °C; the global mean is about 14 °C: a difference of 33 °C. It makes Earth habitable.
Quick check
?Which statement describes the natural greenhouse effect?
Mission check

?Imagine a huge eruption injects sulfur gas high into the stratosphere. What would happen to global temperature?

Pinatubo "injected about 15 million tons of sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere". The haze scattered sunlight (global dimming) and cooled the planet by about 0.6 °C for almost two years.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why are the world's rainforests and hottest deserts found in bands either side of the equator?

Make a guess: Why are the world's rainforests and hottest deserts found in bands either side of the equator?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 5.2.1 overview of global atmospheric circulation

Mission 4

Uneven heating

  • The Sun heats the Earth unevenly. Near the equator sunlight is more direct; near the poles it is spread out.
  • This sets up a temperature difference between the equator and the poles.
  • Global atmospheric circulation: the large-scale movement of air that redistributes heat and moisture around the planet.
  • Warm air moves towards the poles; cold air moves towards the equator.
Mission 4

Rising air, sinking air

  • Rising air → low pressure at the surface. It cools, clouds form, rain falls.
  • Sinking air → high pressure at the surface. It warms, clouds clear, the weather is dry.
  • At the surface, wind blows from high pressure towards low pressure.
Mission 4

The three-cell model

  • In each hemisphere, air circulates in three cells in the lowest layer of the atmosphere (up to 10–15 km high):
    • Hadley cell: equator to about 30–40°
    • Ferrel cell: about 30–40° to 60–70°
    • Polar cell: about 60–70° to the pole
  • Together they work like a "global conveyor belt" carrying energy from the tropics to the poles.
Mission 4

The pressure belts

  • Equator: low pressure. Trade winds meet and air rises in the Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ), forming thunderstorms.
  • About 30°: high pressure. Air from the Hadley cell sinks over the subtropics, over oceans and deserts such as the Sahara.
  • About 60°: low pressure. Warm air meets cold polar air at the polar front, "a hotspot for unsettled weather", including over the UK.
  • Poles: high pressure. Cold air sinks at the poles.
Quick check
?Why is the Sahara, at about 20–30°N, so dry?
Mission 4

Surface winds

  • Surface winds blow from the high-pressure belts to the low-pressure belts.
  • Trade winds: blow from about 30° towards the equator.
  • Westerlies: in the Ferrel cell, from the west or south-west in the northern hemisphere. They bring Atlantic weather systems to the UK.
  • Polar easterlies: blow out from the polar highs towards about 60°.
  • Coriolis effect: Earth's rotation deflects moving air to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern hemisphere. So winds do not blow straight north or south.
Mission 4

Climatic zones

  • Equator (low pressure): hot, wet all year → tropical rainforests.
  • About 30° (high pressure): hot, dry → hot deserts.
  • About 60° (low pressure): mild, wet, changeable → e.g. the UK.
  • Poles (high pressure): cold and dry.
  • The circulation "shapes prevailing winds and the distribution of deserts and rainforests around the world".
Quick check
?A ship sails from the Tropic of Cancer (about 23°N) towards the equator. Which surface wind is it most likely to meet?
Mission 4

Tropical storms and the circulation

  • Tropical storms form over warm oceans: NOAA gives a sea surface of about 27 °C (80 °F) or more.
  • They need to be at least about 480 km (300 miles) from the equator, where the Coriolis effect is strong enough to make them spin.
  • They occur at about 5°–30° north and south: the zone of the ITCZ, rising air and warm seas.
  • They first move westward with the trade winds, then many turn eastward when they reach the westerlies.
Mission 4

Pressure belts bring hazards

  • Low pressure (ITCZ, polar front): rising air → heavy rain, floods, thunderstorms, tropical storms and mid-latitude storms.
  • In winter the jet stream shifts south over the UK, "increasing the risk of storms and even snow".
  • High pressure (about 30°): sinking air → little rain → drought and heatwaves.
  • Arica, Chile, on the edge of the subtropical high-pressure belt, went 172 months without rain (October 1903 to January 1918), a world record.
Mission check

?The Amazon and Congo rainforests lie close to the equator. The Kalahari and the great Australian deserts lie close to the Tropic of Capricorn. Which explanation fits this pattern best?

Uneven heating drives the three-cell circulation. Air rises at the equator (low pressure, rain, rainforest) and sinks at about 30° (high pressure, dry, desert), so both form bands that circle the Earth.

Mission 5: Hurricane Ian hit Florida with 130-knot winds in 2022. Why was water, not wind, its deadliest hazard?

Make a guess: Hurricane Ian hit Florida with 130-knot winds in 2022. Why was water, not wind, its deadliest hazard?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 5.2.1 global distribution of hurricanes and cyclones; seasonality (e.g. monsoon) and long-term changes in frequency and magnitude
Mission 5

One storm, three names

  • A tropical cyclone is "a rapidly rotating storm that begins over tropical oceans".
  • It is called a hurricane in the Atlantic and north-east Pacific, a typhoon in the north-west Pacific, and a cyclone in the Indian Ocean and South Pacific.
Mission 5

Where and when

  • They form over tropical oceans, between about 25°S and 25°N.
  • Sources draw the belt slightly differently. NOAA's 25°S–25°N is where storms form; the Met Office gives about 5°–30° north and south for where they occur.
  • They do not form within about 480 km (300 miles) of the equator: there is too little spin there.
  • Main seasons: July–October in the Northern Hemisphere, December–March in the Southern; globally, September is the most active month.
  • The north-west Pacific has them all year round. Over cool water or land, they weaken.
Mission 5

Seasons and the monsoon

  • In the North Indian Ocean (Bay of Bengal and Arabian Sea), cyclones have two seasons a year.
  • The main season is after the monsoon (October–December); a second, smaller one comes before it (March–May).
  • So the cyclone seasons there are timed by the monsoon, not just by the calendar summer.
Mission 5

Linked to global circulation

  • Near the equator, the trade winds of the two Hadley cells meet. Warm air rises: a belt of low pressure and thunderstorms.
  • Tropical storms grow from disturbances in this rising air over warm oceans: the source areas.
  • Trade winds then steer most storms westwards; many later curve towards the poles.
  • Low pressure brings hurricanes; high pressure (sinking air) brings heatwaves and drought.
Quick check
?Why do tropical storms almost never form right on the equator, even where the sea is warm?
Mission 5

Hurricane Ian, 2022: Florida

  • Ian intensified rapidly over water of 30 °C and above, with low wind shear (winds at different heights were similar).
  • 28 September: it peaked at 140 kt (category 5), then hit Cayo Costa, Florida, at 130 kt (category 4).
  • Storm surge (sea water piled up by onshore winds) flooded Fort Myers Beach to 3–4.6 m (10–15 feet) above ground.
  • In Lee County, at least 52,514 buildings were damaged, 5,369 of them destroyed.
  • At least 156 people died in the US, 66 directly from the storm: surge killed 41, freshwater flooding 12 and wind 4.
Quick check
?Of Ian's 66 direct deaths in the US, 41 were caused by storm surge. What percentage is that, to the nearest whole number?
Mission 5

Are tropical storms changing?

  • Intensity: the share of storms reaching the strongest categories (3–5) has likely risen over the last four decades.
  • Distribution: in the north-west Pacific, the place where storms reach their peak has very likely moved further north.
  • Frequency: scientists have "low confidence" in long-term trends in the total number of storms.
  • Rain: human-caused warming increases the heavy rain they bring (high confidence).
  • Future: the share of the most intense storms (categories 4–5) and their top wind speeds are projected to rise as the world warms (high confidence).
Quick check
?Which statement best matches the IPCC's findings on tropical storms?
Mission check

?A category 3 hurricane is heading for a low-lying coast with a wide, gently sloping sea floor and a bay that narrows inland. Which hazard is likely to kill most people?

Ian's onshore winds drove a storm surge 3–4.6 m (10–15 feet) above ground at Fort Myers Beach. Surge caused 41 of the 66 direct deaths in the US (62%); wind caused 4.

Mission 6

Hurricane Ian, 2022

  • 27 Sept: hit western Cuba (Pinar del Río) as a category 3 hurricane, 110 kt.
  • It strengthened again over the sea, then hit south-west Florida on 28 Sept as a category 4, 130 kt.
  • Income per person (GNI per capita, 2021 PPP $): USA about $73,650; Cuba about $8,415, a UN estimate.
Mission 6

Causes: a deep low-pressure system

  • A tropical cyclone is a low-pressure system: warm, moist air rises over the ocean, and air rushes in to replace it.
  • Ian rapidly intensified over sea of 30 °C and higher, with little wind shear to tear it apart.
  • After crossing Cuba, it strengthened again over the Gulf of Mexico, to a peak of 140 kt.
Mission 6

Primary effects: Florida

  • Primary effects are caused directly by the wind, rain and sea.
  • Storm surge flooded Fort Myers Beach 3–4.6 m (10–15 ft) above ground.
  • Lee County: at least 52,514 buildings damaged; 5,369 destroyed.
  • Roads and bridges to Sanibel and Pine Islands were washed away; they could be reached only by boat or helicopter.
  • 66 direct deaths in the US, all in Florida; storm surge killed 41.
Mission 6

Secondary effects: Florida

  • Secondary effects follow on from the primary ones.
  • About 4.45 million customers (about 9.62 million people) lost power in the US.
  • 90 more deaths were indirect: lack of timely medical care (18), falls and other accidents during power cuts (16), heart attacks (16).
  • Floodwater carried debris, fertiliser and pollutants into the sea; scientists then tracked a harmful algal bloom.
  • Crop and farm losses in Florida: $1.1–1.8 billion. Total US damage: $112.9 billion.
Mission 6

Effects in Cuba

  • Storm surge pushed the sea 300 m inland at La Coloma.
  • More than 100,000 homes damaged or destroyed; the whole island lost power for a few days.
  • 5 deaths, 3 of them direct.
  • Tobacco, a key export (10.4% of goods exports) and a main livelihood in Pinar del Río, suffered "disastrous" losses, putting the next harvest at risk.
  • More than 70 schools in Pinar del Río were damaged.
  • No dollar estimate of Cuba's losses exists, so the two cannot be compared in money.
Quick check
?Which of these was a secondary effect of Hurricane Ian?
Mission 6

Shorter-term and longer-term impacts

  • Shorter term (days to weeks): deaths, flooding, power cuts (Cuba's two worst-hit provinces were still without power two days later), water rescues: over 250 in central and eastern Florida.
  • Longer term (months to years): rebuilding homes; lost tobacco harvest; Florida's federal recovery support passing $10.2 billion two years on.
  • People, economy, environment: sort each effect into one of these.
Quick check
?Which impact of Ian on Cuba was longer term?
Mission 6

Immediate responses

  • Warnings: the US National Hurricane Center briefed emergency managers from 23 Sept, as Ian formed.
  • Florida: a storm surge watch, then a warning, were issued for Fort Myers Beach 48 h and 30 h before tropical-storm winds arrived.
  • Cuba: Civil Defence moved 76,221 people out of danger, to government shelters or to friends and family.
  • Rescue: over 250 water rescues in Florida; the US Coast Guard received 16 live briefings from the NHC to support its rescues.
  • An early warning system gives people time to evacuate before the hazard arrives.
Mission 6

Long-term responses

  • USA: federal support passed $10.2 billion within two years.
  • $1.15 billion went to 387,139 households; flood insurance paid $4.67 billion to about 47,000 policyholders.
  • Cuba: the Red Cross appealed for 9 million Swiss francs to help 25,000 people, but received only 43% of it.
  • Many Cuban families rebuilt with salvaged materials; the Red Cross called for sturdier, longer-lasting shelter.
  • Emergency aid: money, food and shelter from outside the country, often through agencies like the Red Cross.
Mission 6

Who responded?

  • Individuals: left their homes when told to; Cuban families sheltered relatives and rebuilt their own homes.
  • Organisations: the Cuban Red Cross with the IFRC; UN agencies in Cuba; the US Coast Guard.
  • Governments: Cuba's Civil Defence ran the evacuation; the US federal government (FEMA) paid for recovery.
Quick check
?Which response to Ian was long term?
Mission check

?Two coasts are hit by equally strong cyclones. Coast X is wealthy, with good forecasts, but many people choose to stay in low-lying homes. Coast Y is poorer, but its civil defence moves everyone from low-lying areas to shelters before the storm. Which statement is most likely?

Cuba evacuated 76,221 people and lost 5 lives, but recovery money was short (43% of the Red Cross appeal). Florida had $10.2 billion of federal support, yet Ian hit it harder, at 130 kt, and 41 people drowned in the storm surge.

Mission 7

High pressure: sinking air

  • In an anticyclone (high pressure), air sinks and warms as it falls.
  • Sinking air stops clouds forming: clear skies, strong sunshine, little rain.
  • If a high stalls for days (a blocking high), heat builds day after day: a heatwave.
  • If highs keep returning for months, rain fails: drought.
Mission 7

Why some places are more at risk

  • At the equator, air rises: low pressure and heavy rain.
  • Around 30° north and south, that air sinks: belts of high pressure. Many of the world's hot deserts lie here.
  • The rain belt moves north and south with the seasons. Places at its edge, such as the Sahel, depend on a short rainy season that can fail.
  • Heatwaves can strike much further from the equator too, wherever a blocking high stalls in summer.
Mission 7

Changing over time: the Sahel

  • Sahel summer rainfall was high in the 1950s and 1960s, fell sharply in the 1970s and 1980s (driest year in the index: 1984), and has partly recovered since 1990.
  • The IPCC links a cooler North Atlantic to a drier Sahel; air pollution (aerosols) has also been suggested as a cause of the droughts.
  • Worldwide, dry (arid) climate zones have expanded. A farming drought that came once in 10 years in drying regions now comes 1.7 times per decade.
Quick check
?Why is the Sahel more vulnerable to drought than places on the equator?
Mission 7

Heatwaves are changing

  • IPCC: it is "virtually certain" that heatwaves have become more frequent and more intense over most land regions since the 1950s.
  • A heat extreme that used to come once in 10 years now comes 2.8 times per decade.
  • At 2 °C of warming it is projected to come 5.6 times per decade.
Mission 7

Case study: the Pacific Northwest heatwave, June 2021

  • Where: western Canada (British Columbia) and the north-west USA.
  • Cause: "a slow-moving strong high pressure system", sometimes called a heat dome, which reached record strength.
  • Sinking air and winds blowing down the mountainsides heated the air further.
  • In British Columbia temperatures rose from 24 June to a peak on 28–29 June, passing 40 °C in many places.
  • Lytton set Canada's all-time record: 49.6 °C.
Mission 7

Consequences

  • Deaths: 619 deaths in British Columbia between 25 June and 1 July were identified as heat-related.
  • 98% died indoors, 67% were aged 70 or over, and 56% lived alone. Most were in homes without adequate cooling.
  • Services: 911 emergency calls doubled at the peak; some callers waited or were told no ambulance was available.
  • Property and environment: shortly after its record, Lytton was largely destroyed in a wildfire.
Quick check
?619 deaths in British Columbia were heat-related, and 67% of those who died were aged 70 or over. About how many people aged 70 or over died?
Mission 7

Responses

  • Short term: heat alerts were issued, but there was a lag before people responded.
  • Long term: a coroners' review panel recommended:
    • a co-ordinated provincial heat alert and response system
    • identifying and supporting the people most at risk
    • prevention and long-term risk reduction.
  • Exam move: for a case study, give the place-specific cause (the heat dome), then consequences, then responses, each with a figure.
Mission 7

Could it happen without climate change?

  • Attribution science compares how likely an event is today with a modelled world without human-caused warming.
  • World Weather Attribution: the heatwave was "virtually impossible without human-caused climate change".
  • An event this rare would have been at least 150 times rarer without it, and the heat was about 2 °C hotter.
  • The heat dome itself is weather. Climate change made its heat more extreme.
Mission check

?A city has its hottest day ever, during a stalled high-pressure system. Which statement could an attribution study support?

For Lytton, World Weather Attribution found the heat "virtually impossible without human-caused climate change": about 2 °C hotter, and at least 150 times more likely. The heat dome was weather; warming made it deadlier.

Mission 8: The wettest Scottish mountains get about eight times the rain of the driest parts of eastern England. Why?

Mission 8

Weather or climate?

  • Weather: the state of the atmosphere at one place and time: today's rain, wind, temperature.
  • Climate: "the general weather over a long period", usually averaged over 30 years.
  • One cold week is weather. Thirty years of average temperatures is climate.
Quick check
?Which of these describes climate rather than weather?
Mission 8

The UK's climate today

  • Temperate maritime: mild, wet, changeable, with no dry season.
  • Mean annual temperature: about 7 °C in Shetland to over 11 °C in the far south-west of England.
  • Annual rainfall: over 4,000 mm in the western Scottish mountains; around 500 mm in the driest parts of eastern England.
  • "Places in the east and south of the UK tend to be drier, warmer, sunnier and less windy than those further west and north".
Mission 8

Latitude

  • Further north, the Sun is lower in the sky, so its energy is spread over a larger area: colder.
  • So mean annual temperatures fall from over 11 °C in the far south-west to about 7 °C in Shetland.
Mission 8

Altitude

  • Temperature falls by about 0.5 °C for every 100 m of height.
  • Moist air forced up hills cools, condenses and makes cloud and rain: relief rainfall.
  • So a map of rainfall "looks very similar to a topographic map".
Mission 8

Prevailing wind and the Atlantic

  • The UK's prevailing winds are west to south-westerly, blowing in off the Atlantic.
  • They bring moist air and Atlantic depressions, which give most of the autumn and winter rain.
  • The air meets the western mountains first and drops its rain there. The east lies in a rain shadow.
Quick check
?Why does the west of the UK get much more rain than the east?
Mission 8

Ocean currents: the North Atlantic Drift

  • The Gulf Stream and its extension, the North Atlantic Drift, carry warm water from the tropics towards north-west Europe.
  • The Met Office notes that the warm Gulf Stream has "a strong influence" on western Scotland.
  • Winds blowing off this warm ocean keep UK winters mild for their latitude. Most of that warmth arrives through the winds; the ocean current adds "a few °C" in winter.
Mission 8

Maritime and continental climates

  • The sea heats up and cools down slowly; land does both quickly.
  • Maritime: near the sea, small yearly temperature range. About 9 °C in the Hebrides, the gap between the warmest and coldest month.
  • More continental: further from the sea, bigger range. About 14 °C in the English Midlands.
  • Winds from mainland Europe bring continental extremes: hot summer days on south-easterly winds, and the coldest winter spells when "a strong, cold easterly wind ousts the normal south westerlies".
Quick check
?In February, cold dry air arrives over England on an easterly wind. Where has this air most likely come from?
Mission 8

Micro-climate

  • A micro-climate is the climate of a small area that differs from its surroundings.
  • Urban heat: central Glasgow reaches the same annual mean temperature as the mild coasts, "due to the urban heat island effect".
  • Relief: cold air sinks into hollows; inland low-lying spots in Somerset have over 50 air frosts a year, against fewer than 2 on Scilly.
  • Aspect: south-facing slopes get more sun and are warmer.
  • Shelter and water: walls and trees cut the wind; lakes and the sea keep nearby air milder in winter.
Quick check
?A gardener in a valley bottom finds frost on her plants on clear nights, while gardens on the slopes above are frost-free. Why?
Mission check

?Town A is on a west coast at sea level, facing prevailing winds off a warm ocean. Town B is at the same latitude and height, but far inland to the east, beyond a range of mountains. Compared with Town B, Town A is most likely to be:

In the UK, westerly winds off the Atlantic drop their rain on the western mountains (over 4,000 mm a year) and leave eastern England in a rain shadow (around 500 mm). The sea also keeps the west's temperature range small: about 9 °C in the Hebrides, 14 °C in the Midlands.

Mission 9

Mission 9: Why is British weather so changeable, yet sometimes settled for weeks on end?

Make a guess: Why is British weather so changeable, yet sometimes settled for weeks on end?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 5.2.2 UK weather linked to low pressure (e.g. winter depressions) and high pressure (e.g. summer drought)
Mission 9

High and low pressure

  • Air pressure is measured in millibars (mb) or hectopascals; standard sea-level pressure is 1013.
  • Isobars join places of equal pressure on a weather chart.
  • Low pressure (depression): air rises, cools and forms cloud and rain → unsettled weather.
  • High pressure (anticyclone): air sinks, so few clouds form → settled weather.
  • Winds blow anticlockwise around a low and clockwise around a high in the northern hemisphere. Close isobars mean strong winds.
Mission 9

Weather fronts

  • A front is the boundary between two air masses with different temperature and moisture.
  • Warm front: red line with semicircles. Warm air rises over colder air.
  • Cold front: blue line with triangles. Cold air pushes under warmer air.
  • Occluded front: purple line; forms when the cold front catches up with the warm front.
  • Most depressions reaching the UK form over the Atlantic and move west to east.
Quick check
?On a weather chart, a blue line with triangles is moving east across Northern Ireland. What is happening?
Mission 9

Inside a frontal depression

  • Low pressure at the centre; air spirals in anticlockwise and rises.
  • Warm front in front; cold front behind.
  • Between them is the warm sector: a wedge of warm, moist air.
  • The whole system moves from west to east, so a place in the UK gets the warm front first, then the warm sector, then the cold front.
Mission 9

1. The warm front arrives

  • High, wispy cirrus cloud "often form[s] in advance of a warm front".
  • Cloud thickens and lowers: cirrostratus, then altostratus, which "deepens and bulks out to become nimbostratus".
  • Steady, persistent rain for several hours "until the associated front passes over": the warm air rises gradually over a wide area.
  • Pressure falls as the depression approaches.
  • After the front passes, it turns milder: "warm air follows a warm front".
Mission 9

2. The warm sector

  • Mild air and often low cloud.
  • "The area between these fronts, called the 'warm sector', often brings low cloud and patchy light rain".
  • Rain may ease to drizzle; winds are steady.
Mission 9

3. The cold front, then clearer

  • Warm air is forced up quickly over the incoming cold air, forming tall cumulonimbus clouds.
  • Heavy, short-lived showers, strong gusty winds, sometimes thunder.
  • After the front: colder air, as "cold air follows a cold front"; skies clear, with scattered showers.
  • Pressure rises again after the cold front.
Quick check
?Which order of weather would someone in Belfast most likely see as a depression passes?
Mission 9

Winter depressions: 2013/14

  • In winter the jet stream is further south, so "unsettled conditions affect more of the UK".
  • Winter 2013/14: a "large number of low pressure centres" reached the UK, often "in rapid succession".
  • It was the wettest winter in the records: 165% of average precipitation for the UK.
  • It was mild (about 1.5 °C above average) and possibly the stormiest on record for the British Isles.
  • Flooding included the Somerset Levels.
  • Low pressure hazards: heavy rain, floods, storms and, in winter, snow.
Mission 9

Anticyclones

  • Sinking air → little cloud → dry, settled weather.
  • Light winds, blowing clockwise; isobars are far apart.
  • A high can become stationary and "keep weather fronts at bay": a block.
  • So high pressure can last for days or weeks.
Mission 9

Summer anticyclones

  • "Fine, warm weather with long sunny days and dry spells".
  • Hazards: heatwaves, droughts, poor air quality, high pollen counts.
  • Summer 2003: a "long-lasting high pressure system" reduced rainfall across Europe.
  • In the UK there were about 2,000 heat-related deaths, and hosepipe bans were brought in.
  • Exam move: link "summer drought" to sinking air, few clouds and a blocking high.
Mission 9

Winter anticyclones

  • "Cold, dry days with light winds" and "severe nighttime frosts".
  • Frost and fog are common hazards: clear skies let heat escape at night.
  • Sometimes a layer of stratocumulus cloud traps the gloom and keeps days cold.
  • December 1952, London: an anticyclone trapped smoke near the ground under an inversion. About 4,000 people were known to have died.
Quick check
?In January, a high-pressure system sits over Ireland for a week. Which weather is most likely?
Mission check

?A weather station in Belfast records this over one day: high thin cloud that thickens; steady rain for six hours; mild air with drizzle; then a heavy shower with gusty wind; then colder, clearer air. The next week a high settles over the UK. Which explains both parts?

British weather is changeable because Atlantic depressions, with their warm and cold fronts, cross the country from west to east. It turns settled when an anticyclone (sinking air) sits over the UK and blocks the fronts, sometimes for weeks.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The Quaternary (2.58 million years to today) has cycled between glacials and interglacials; CO₂ now is about 50% above pre-industrial levels.
  • Evidence: ice cores (CO₂ and temperature, 800,000 years), temperature records (since 1659 in England), sea ice (satellites since 1979), tree rings, pollen, diaries and paintings. Each has limits; agreement between them gives confidence.

Mission 2

  • Stores: rocks (largest), oceans, soils and permafrost, plants, atmosphere. Flows: photosynthesis, respiration, decomposition, ocean exchange, combustion.
  • Human activity (combustion, deforestation, draining peat) moves carbon into the atmosphere far faster than the slow cycle; about half stays there.

Mission 3

  • Greenhouse gases keep Earth habitable; adding more enhances the effect.

Mission 4

  • Uneven heating → three cells (Hadley, Ferrel, Polar) → belts of low pressure (equator, about 60°) and high pressure (about 30°, poles) → surface winds (trade winds, westerlies) bent by the Coriolis effect.
  • Low pressure brings rain, storms and floods; high pressure brings drought and heatwaves.

Mission 5

  • Tropical storms form over warm tropical oceans between about 25°S and 25°N, but not near the equator, where there is too little spin.
  • A larger share now reach the strongest categories; their total number shows no clear trend.
  • On low coasts, storm surge is usually the deadliest hazard: in Ian, 41 of 66 direct US deaths.

Mission 6

  • Primary effects come from the storm itself (surge, wind, rain); secondary effects follow (power cuts, lost harvests, indirect deaths).
  • Immediate responses save lives (warnings, evacuation, rescue); long-term responses rebuild (aid, insurance, reconstruction). Wealth speeds recovery more than it prevents deaths.

Mission 7

  • Sinking air in high pressure brings clear skies and little rain: heatwaves and droughts.
  • High-pressure belts near 30° and the edges of the seasonal rain belt (the Sahel) are most at risk; the Sahel dried in the 1970s–80s and has partly recovered.
  • Pacific Northwest, June 2021: a heat dome; 619 deaths in British Columbia. Warming made it about 2 °C hotter. A heat alert and response system was recommended.

Mission 8

  • Weather is day to day; climate is the 30-year average. The UK's climate is temperate maritime.
  • Latitude makes the north cooler; altitude makes hills cooler and wetter; south-westerly winds off the Atlantic and the North Atlantic Drift make the west wet and mild; continental air from Europe brings hot summer days and cold winter spells.
  • Micro-climates come from towns, hollows, aspect, shelter and water.

Mission 9

  • Low pressure (rising air) = cloud, rain, wind; high pressure (sinking air) = settled, few clouds.
  • A frontal depression brings: warm front (thickening cloud, steady rain) → warm sector (mild, low cloud, drizzle) → cold front (heavy showers, gusts, then colder).
  • Summer highs: heat, sunshine, drought (2003). Winter highs: cold, frost, fog (1952 smog).
  • Winter depressions (2013/14) bring floods; blocking highs bring summer drought.
