Mission 1

Mission 1: How can a government raise the most its economy can produce, and why does it take so long?

Make a guess: How can a government raise the most its economy can produce, and why does it take so long?

Mission 1

What supply-side policies aim to do

  • Supply-side policies: government policies that aim to raise the economy's productive capacity.
  • They work on the quantity and quality of factors of production and on how efficiently markets work.
  • Goals:
    • long-term growth: LRAS shifts right
    • a lower natural rate of unemployment
    • growth with less inflationary pressure
    • better international competitiveness
Mission 1

On the AD/AS diagram

  • Capacity rises: LRAS shifts right.
  • Productive capacity rises from Yfe1 to Yfe2.
  • With AD unchanged: real output rises, the price level falls.
  • Lower costs per unit often shift SRAS right too.
  • On the Keynesian AS, the vertical section shifts right.
  • Employment can rise at a lower natural rate of unemployment.
  • Agents: consumers gain from lower prices and more output; workers from more jobs; firms from lower unit costs.
Quick check
?Which policy is most clearly aimed at the supply side of the economy?
Mission 1

Worked example: per person, what can policy change?

  • Illustrative data. Real GDP per person = output per hour × hours per worker × workers ÷ population.
  • Population 10 million; workers 5 million; 1,600 hours each a year; output per hour 50.
  • Real GDP = 50 × 1,600 × 5 million = 400 billion. Per person: 40,000.
  • Policy 1: childcare and job support bring 0.5 million more people into work. Real GDP = 440 billion; per person 44,000 (+10%).
  • Policy 2: training and better equipment raise output per hour to 55. Per person also 44,000 (+10%).
  • Both together: 48,400 per person: +21%, not +20%, because the effects multiply.
  • Labour productivity (output per worker or per hour) depends on capital per worker, skills, health, technology and management.
  • Why it matters: higher productivity lowers unit costs, supports higher real wages and makes exports more competitive.

  • Market-based: free markets and incentives work better with fewer barriers.
  • Interventionist: markets under-provide some things (positive externalities, public goods), so the government steps in.
Mission 1

Market-based policies: how they work

  • Lower income tax raises the reward for work: some work more hours or join the labour force.
  • Lower profit tax raises the return on investment: more capital, more productivity.
  • Benefit reform: a bigger gap between income in and out of work makes taking a job more attractive. Voluntary unemployment falls: a lower natural rate.
  • Deregulation and privatisation: fewer rules and a profit motive push firms to cut costs and innovate.
  • Competition policy and trade liberalisation: rivals force firms to be efficient.
  • Labour market reform: weaker union power and more flexible contracts let wages and jobs adjust faster.
  • But: some of these may widen inequality or weaken job security.
Mission 1

Labour market diagrams

  • Minimum wage: set above the equilibrium wage, labour supplied exceeds labour demanded: unemployment (Ls − Ld).
  • Removing or lowering it: employment moves towards Le. Evidence on how many jobs this adds is mixed.
  • Benefit reform and flexibility: more people seek work at each wage, so labour supply shifts right: employment rises.
  • Childcare and back-to-work support reduce economic inactivity: more people join the labour force.
Mission 1

Interventionist policies: how they work

  • Education and training raise human capital: more output per worker. Fewer skill gaps: lower structural unemployment.
  • Health care: fewer days lost to illness; longer working lives.
  • R&D: new ideas spill over to other firms (a positive externality), so markets under-invest. Subsidies or tax credits raise R&D.
  • Infrastructure: roads, rail and broadband cut firms' costs and link workers to jobs.
  • Industrial policy: support for strategic industries.
  • But: costly, slow, and the government may "pick losers" (government failure).
Quick check
?A government sells its state-owned rail company to private investors and lets rival firms bid to run routes. How is this policy meant to raise LRAS?
Mission 1

Two effects at different speeds

  • Many supply-side policies also change spending:
    • building roads or schools is government spending: AD shifts right at once
    • a tax cut raises disposable income: AD shifts right
  • The supply-side effect comes later, once the road is built or the trained workers arrive: LRAS shifts right.
  • So fiscal policy can have supply-side effects (e.g. tax incentives), and supply-side policies can have demand-side effects.

Mission 1

Real world: cutting taxes on company profits

  • The US cut its main federal corporate tax rate from 35% to 21% from 2018.
  • Aim: more investment in capital, so higher productivity and pay.
  • One 2024 study estimated investment rose by about 11% at firms given the average tax cut, compared with firms given none, but wages and growth rose by less than supporters had claimed.
  • The UK cut its main corporation tax rate from 28% (2010) to 19% (2017), then raised it to 25% in 2023 for profits over £250,000.
  • Question to weigh: how would you judge whether a tax cut on profits raised capacity, and over how many years?
Mission 1Evaluate

How well do supply-side policies work?

It depends on:

  • Time lags. Education, health and infrastructure take years, even decades, to raise capacity.
  • Response to incentives. Tax cuts may change effort or investment only a little; profits may go to shareholders instead.
  • Cost and trade-offs. Interventionist policies need spending (opportunity cost); market-based ones may widen inequality.
  • The output gap. With weak AD, extra capacity sits unused; Keynesians would boost demand first.
  • Government failure. Poor information can lead to wasted subsidies or badly designed rules.
  • Strength: if they work, growth with lower inflation and a lower natural rate of unemployment.
Mission check

?A government expands free childcare and job training, paid for by cutting other spending, so AD does not change. Over several years, more parents take jobs and workers become more skilled. According to the AD/AS model, what happens in the long run?

Supply-side policies work on capacity, not spending, and capacity takes years to build.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Can an economy keep growing just by people spending more?

Make a guess: Can an economy keep growing just by people spending more?

Mission 2

What counts as growth

  • Economic growth: an increase in real GDP (real output) over time.
  • Rate of growth = % change in real GDP from one year to the next.
  • Nominal GDP is measured at current prices, so it rises with inflation. Real GDP removes price changes.
  • Total real GDP shows the size of the economy. Real GDP per capita (per person) is closer to average living standards.
  • Say: "real GDP rose by 2%" — not: "GDP rose by 2%", when you mean output.
Mission 2

Worked example: growth rates

  • Illustrative data.
Year 1Year 2
Nominal GDP500 bn540 bn
Real GDP (year 1 prices)500 bn515 bn
Population50 m51 m
  • Real growth = (515 − 500) / 500 × 100 = 3.0%.
  • Nominal growth = (540 − 500) / 500 × 100 = 8.0%. Most of that is higher prices.
  • Real GDP per capita: 500 bn / 50 m = 10,000 → 515 bn / 51 m = 10,098.
  • Growth per capita = (10,098 − 10,000) / 10,000 × 100 = 0.98%.
  • Population grew too, so output per person grew much less than total output.
  • Small rates compound. Real GDP per capita doubles in about 35 years at 2% a year, about 10 years at 7%, about 8 years at 9%.
  • After 35 years, 2% a year makes output per person about 2 times as big; 7% a year makes it about 10.7 times as big.

?Illustrative data: a country's real GDP rises from 800 bn to 820 bn in a year. Its population rises from 40 million to 41 million. What is the growth rate of real GDP per capita?
Mission 2

Actual and potential growth on a PPC

  • Actual growth: a rise in the output the economy actually produces (real GDP).
  • Potential growth: a rise in the economy's productive capacity: the most it could produce with all resources fully and efficiently used.
  • On a PPC:
    • point A inside the curve: unemployed resources (spare capacity)
    • A → B, onto the curve: actual growth, using spare capacity
    • PPC1 → PPC2, shifting outwards: potential growth
  • Once the economy is on its PPC, output can only keep rising if the PPC shifts out.
  • Say: "potential growth: productive capacity increases" — not: "the economy produces more".
Mission 2

Short-run growth: AD rises

  • Start with spare capacity: real output Y1 is below full-employment output Yf.
  • AD rises (more consumption, investment, government spending or net exports): AD1 → AD2.
  • Real output rises Y1 → Y2; firms use idle workers and machines. The price level rises a little.
  • This is short-run (actual) growth: the economy moves towards its capacity. LRAS has not moved.
  • Demand-side policies that raise AD: lower interest rates, higher government spending, lower taxes.
  • Close to Yf, more AD raises the price level more and real output less.
Mission 2

Long-run growth: LRAS shifts right

  • Long-run growth: productive capacity rises, so LRAS shifts right: LRAS1 → LRAS2.
  • Full-employment output rises: Yf1 → Yf2. This is the same event as the PPC shifting out.
  • With AD unchanged, real output rises and the price level falls.
  • On a Keynesian AS curve, the vertical section moves right: the economy's maximum output rises.
  • Long-run growth is what lets output keep rising year after year without rising inflation.

Mission 2

Causes of growth

  • Short run (actual growth): a rise in AD, when there is spare capacity: C, I, G or (X − M).
  • Long run (potential growth): more or better factors of production.
    • Quantity: a bigger labour force (migration, more people in work); more capital (investment).
    • Quality: education and training (human capital); new technology.
    • Institutions: property rights, rule of law, stable government; good infrastructure.
  • Investment does both: it raises AD now and capacity later.
  • Supply-side policies that shift LRAS: education and training, infrastructure, support for research, tax incentives for investment, policies that bring more people into work.
Mission 2

  • Real case: since 1978, China's GDP growth averaged over 9% a year, lifting almost 800 million people out of extreme poverty (World Bank).
  • In LDCs: growth can fund clean water, health care and schools. Costs can include urban overcrowding, environmental damage and gains going mostly to a few.
Mission 2

Sustainable and inclusive growth

  • Sustainable growth: growth that meets today's needs without reducing future generations' ability to meet theirs (idea from the Brundtland Report, 1987).
  • It needs growth that does not use up natural resources faster than they renew or degrade the environment for the future.
  • Say: "sustainable growth" — not: "slow growth". It is about whether growth can continue, not how fast it is.
Quick check
?Illustrative data: over ten years a country's real GDP grows by 6% a year. Over the same years the real incomes of its poorest 40% of households hardly change. Which description fits this best?
Mission 2Evaluate

Is economic growth always good?

It depends on:

  • The source: growth from productivity and investment can last; growth from AD alone with no spare capacity mainly raises prices.
  • Who gains: if most gains go to a few, average figures overstate how much most people's living standards rise.
  • The environment: growth with high emissions can lower future living standards; cleaner technology and carbon pricing can reduce the conflict.
  • The level of development: an extra 2% matters more for health and poverty in a low-income country than in a rich one.
Mission check

?An economy is at full capacity: unemployment is at its natural rate and firms are working at full capacity. The government wants output to keep rising over the next ten years. Which policy is most likely to achieve this?

Spending can only use the capacity an economy has. Lasting growth needs that capacity to grow.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why does an economy grow in bursts and slumps instead of at a steady pace?

Make a guess: Why does an economy grow in bursts and slumps instead of at a steady pace?

Mission 3

The trend and the cycle

  • Plot real GDP against time.
  • The trend line shows potential output: it rises steadily as productive capacity grows (long-term growth).
  • Actual real GDP wobbles above and below the trend: short-term fluctuations.
  • These ups and downs are the business cycle.
  • Also called the economic cycle or trade cycle: the same thing.
  • Say: "actual output fell below potential" — not: "potential output fell", when the economy has spare capacity.
Mission 3

The phases of the cycle

  • Boom: fast growth, actual output above trend. Peak: the top.
  • Downturn (slowdown): growth falls. Recession: real GDP falls.
  • Trough: the bottom. Recovery (upturn): growth returns.
  • Slump: the low phase of the cycle, opposite to a boom: output falling or very low, unemployment high.
  • Common definition of a recession: real GDP falls for two consecutive quarters.
  • Say: "a slowdown: growth falls but stays positive" — not: "a recession".

Mission 3

Output gaps

  • Output gap = actual output − potential output.
  • Negative output gap: actual below potential.
  • Spare capacity, idle machines, cyclical unemployment. Downward pressure on inflation.
  • Positive output gap: actual above potential.
  • Overtime, shortages of workers, capacity stretched. Upward pressure on wages and prices. It cannot last.
  • Significance: the gap shows whether inflation is likely to rise or fall, and how much room the economy has to grow without inflation.
Mission 3

Output gaps on AD/AS and the PPC

  • AD/AS: LRAS is vertical at full-employment output (Yf).
  • AD1 meets SRAS left of Yf: output Y1 < Yf.
  • That is a negative output gap.
  • AD2 meets SRAS right of Yf: output Y2 > Yf.
  • That is a positive output gap.
  • On a PPC, a negative gap is a point inside the curve: resources are unemployed.
  • A positive gap is output beyond its sustainable level. Resources are over-used (overtime, machines run without maintenance).
Mission 3

Worked example: the size of the gap

  • Illustrative data.
  • Potential output = 2,000 bn. Actual real GDP = 1,950 bn.
  • Gap = 1,950 − 2,000 = −50 bn.
  • As % of potential: −50 / 2,000 × 100 = −2.5%.
  • A negative output gap of 2.5% of potential output.
  • Two years later, actual = 2,030 bn and potential = 2,000 bn: gap = +30 bn = +1.5% of potential.

Mission 3

Why gaps are hard to measure

  • Actual output is measured; potential output is not. It has to be estimated.
  • Estimates use past trends, business surveys of spare capacity, and unemployment data.
  • Different institutions get different answers, and revise them later.
  • GDP itself is revised. UK example: the fall in real GDP in the 2008–09 recession was estimated at 5.8% in earlier data, then revised to 6.3% (OBR, 2012).
  • So policymakers may not know in real time whether the gap is positive or negative.
Mission 3

Why output swings: shocks and instability

  • Demand shocks shift AD: a collapse in confidence, a financial crisis, falling export demand, a big change in government spending.
  • Supply shocks shift SRAS: a sharp rise in oil prices, a pandemic, a harvest failure.
  • Real case: UK real GDP fell for five quarters in a row and shrank by more than 6% between early 2008 and mid-2009. It took five years to regain its pre-recession size (ONS, 2018).
Quick check
?Illustrative data: potential output is estimated at 800 bn. Actual real GDP is 776 bn. Which statement is correct?
Mission 3Evaluate

Should governments try to close every output gap?

It depends on:

  • Measurement: potential output is estimated, so the size and even the sign of the gap may be wrong.
  • Time lags: policy can take a year or more to work, so it may arrive when the gap has closed.
  • The cause: a demand shock suits demand-side policy; after a supply shock, boosting AD can add to inflation.
  • Size and length: a small, short gap may close on its own; a deep recession can leave lasting damage.
Mission check

?A surge in confidence and borrowing has kept a country's actual output above its potential output for two years. Unemployment is below its natural rate. Which is most likely to happen next?

When demand pushes output above what the economy can sustain, inflation tends to rise. When it falls below, workers and machines sit idle.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Who counts as unemployed, and who slips through the count?

Make a guess: Who counts as unemployed, and who slips through the count?

Mission 4

Three groups, not two

  • Start with the working-age population.
  • UK: everyone aged 16 and over.
  • Split it three ways:
    • Employed: did any paid work in the survey week (even 1 hour), or has a job but is temporarily away.
    • Unemployed: no job, available to start, and actively looking for work.
    • Economically inactive: everyone else.
  • ILO definition of unemployed: without a job, looked for work in the last 4 weeks, can start in the next 2 weeks (or has found a job and is waiting to start it).
  • Labour force = employed + unemployed.
  • The labour force is also called the economically active population.
Mission 4

Worked example: the unemployment rate

  • Illustrative data. Working-age population 50 m: employed 28.5 m, unemployed 1.5 m, inactive 20 m.
  • Labour force = 28.5 + 1.5 = 30 m.
  • Unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ labour force × 100.
  • = 1.5 ÷ 30 × 100 = 5%.
  • Not 1.5 ÷ 50 (= 3%): the inactive are not in the labour force.
  • Economic inactivity rate = inactive ÷ working-age population × 100 = 20 ÷ 50 × 100 = 40%.
  • UK practice: ONS quotes employment and inactivity rates for ages 16–64; the unemployment rate uses everyone 16 and over.
  • Say: "5% of the labour force" — not: "5% of the population".
Mission 4

Inactive is not unemployed

  • Economically inactive: not working and not both looking and available.
  • Who? Full-time students, retired people, people looking after family, the long-term sick or disabled.
  • And discouraged workers: they want a job but have stopped looking, because they think none is available.
  • Discouraged workers are not counted as unemployed. Joblessness is understated.
  • Inactivity matters too: inactive people produce no market output and many rely on benefits.
  • Say: "economically inactive" — not: "unemployed", for someone who is not looking for work.
Quick check
?Illustrative data for a country: working-age population 8.0 m, employed 4.6 m, unemployed 0.4 m, inactive 3.0 m. What is the unemployment rate?
Mission 4

Two ways to count

  • Labour force survey (ask people):
    • interviewers question a large sample of households about the week before
    • anyone who meets the ILO definition is counted as unemployed
    • UK: the Labour Force Survey (LFS), run by the ONS, gives the ILO unemployment figure
  • Claimant count (count benefit claims):
    • counts people claiming benefits because they are unemployed
    • comes straight from benefit records, so no survey is needed
    • UK: people claiming Jobseeker's Allowance, plus Universal Credit claimants who are required to look for work

Mission 4

The UK's two measures in practice

  • ONS publishes both every month.
  • Universal Credit (introduced from 2013) changed who must look for work to keep receiving benefit.
    • Some people in low-paid work must now look for more work and are included in the claimant count.
    • From spring 2020 until mid-2025 the claimant count was higher than LFS unemployment (ONS). Before 2020 it was lower.
  • The LFS has its own problem: fewer households answer.
    • Response fell sharply by 2023. ONS paused its usual LFS estimates in October 2023 and published adjusted figures instead.
    • Reweighted LFS estimates returned in February 2024, labelled "official statistics in development".
  • Lesson: every measure depends on its method. Check how the data were collected before comparing years.
Mission 4

What the rate misses

  • Hidden unemployment: discouraged workers count as inactive, not unemployed.
  • Underemployment:
    • part-time workers who want full-time work count as employed
    • people in jobs well below their skills count as employed too
  • Real case: in 2013 Spain's unemployment rate for under-25s was above 50% (Eurostat: 55.5%). Many under-25s are full-time students, so the youth labour force is small: this is a share of the young people in the labour force, not of all young people.
  • So the unemployment rate understates how many people want work, or more work.
Mission 4Evaluate

How well does the unemployment rate measure joblessness?

It depends on:

  • Hidden unemployment: in a long slump, more people give up looking. The rate can fall with no new jobs.
  • Underemployment: when firms cut hours instead of jobs, the rate rises less than lost work.
  • The method: survey response, benefit rules and the size of the informal economy all change what is counted.
  • So use it alongside other data: the employment rate, inactivity, hours worked and vacancies.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Why are some people out of work even when the economy is booming?

Make a guess: Why are some people out of work even when the economy is booming?

Mission 5

Between jobs

  • Frictional unemployment: people between jobs, searching for a suitable one.
  • Examples: a school leaver's first search, someone who quit to find better work, a worker who moved town.
  • Cause: imperfect information. Matching workers to vacancies takes time.
  • Usually short-term. Some is unavoidable, even useful: a good match raises productivity.
  • Seasonal unemployment: jobs that disappear at certain times of year (farm harvests, beach resorts, ski resorts, winter construction).
  • Seasonal unemployment is predictable and recurs every year.
Mission 5

The wrong skills, the wrong place

  • Structural unemployment: a mismatch between the skills or locations of the jobless and the jobs on offer.
  • Cause: the pattern of demand and production changes: industries decline, new ones grow.
  • Occupational immobility: workers lack the skills for new jobs (a laid-off steelworker cannot become a nurse overnight).
  • Geographical immobility: jobs are elsewhere, but moving is costly (house prices, family ties).
  • Technological unemployment: machines or software replace a skill, a type of structural unemployment.
  • Often long-term, and concentrated in particular regions.
Mission 5

Not enough demand

  • Cyclical unemployment: caused by too little aggregate demand, in a downturn or recession.
  • Also called demand-deficient unemployment.
  • AD falls → firms sell less → they need fewer workers (labour is a derived demand).
  • Real national output falls below full-employment output: a negative output gap.
  • It affects many industries and regions at once.
  • Real case: UK unemployment rose from about 5% in early 2008 to 8.4% in late 2011 (ONS, Labour Force Survey).
Quick check

Mission 5

Wages stuck above equilibrium

  • If wages are held above the market-clearing level, more people want to work than firms want to hire.
  • The excess supply of labour is unemployment.
  • This is real-wage unemployment (also called classical unemployment).
  • Causes: a minimum wage set above equilibrium; trade union bargaining; wages that do not fall when demand for labour falls.
  • Debate: whether minimum wages cost jobs in practice depends on the level set and the market. Studies disagree; the evidence is in an earlier lesson.
  • Say: "an excess supply of labour at the minimum wage" — not: "a shortage of workers".

Mission 5

The natural rate

  • Even at full-employment output, some people are unemployed. Full employment does not mean zero unemployment.
  • Natural rate of unemployment: the unemployment rate when output is at its full-employment level.
  • It is the unemployment that remains when the labour market is in equilibrium: frictional, structural and seasonal.
  • So: cyclical unemployment = actual rate − natural rate.
  • The natural rate changes slowly: with skills, mobility, benefit rules, demographics and technology.
  • Voluntary unemployment: people who choose not to take a job at the going wage (e.g. still searching for a better offer). Involuntary: willing to work at the going wage but unable to find a job (e.g. cyclical).
Mission 5

Worked example: splitting the rate

  • Illustrative data. Actual unemployment rate 7%. Frictional 1.5%, structural 3.5%.
  • Seasonal 0.5%.
  • Natural rate = 1.5 + 3.5 + 0.5 = 5.5%.
  • Cyclical = 7 − 5.5 = 1.5 percentage points.
  • Cyclical unemployment above zero means output is below full employment.
  • Say: "1.5 percentage points above the natural rate" — not: "1.5% above".
Quick check
?Illustrative data: a country's unemployment rate is 6.5%. Its natural rate is estimated at 4.5%. Which statement is correct?
Mission 5

The costs of unemployment

  • Personal: lost income; skills fade; stress and worse mental and physical health.
  • Social: links with crime and family strain; whole communities decline when a big employer closes; inequality rises.
  • Economic:
    • lost output that can never be recovered: the economy is inside its PPC
    • lower tax revenue and higher benefit spending, so a larger budget deficit
    • long spells reduce skills, so potential output may fall too
  • Causes of economic inactivity: full-time study, early retirement, caring for family, long-term sickness or disability, and discouragement (giving up the search).
  • Economic inactivity has similar costs: lost output and tax, and often benefit spending.
  • Say: "benefits are a transfer" — not: "benefits are lost output". The lost output is what the unemployed could have produced.
Quick check
?A deep recession keeps unemployment high for three years. Which effect could reduce the economy's potential output, not just its actual output?
Mission 5Evaluate

How serious is a rise in unemployment?

It depends on:

  • The type and length: short frictional spells can improve job matches; long spells cut skills and can raise the natural rate.
  • Who and where: losses concentrated in one region or group cause deeper social costs than the same rise spread thinly.
  • Support and policy response: benefits, retraining and quick action on demand reduce the lasting damage.
  • What else is happening: rising unemployment with falling inflation leaves room for demand-side policy; with high inflation, the choice is harder.
Mission check

?Four people are unemployed in the same country. Aggregate demand then rises, and output returns to its full-employment level. Whose unemployment is most likely to end because of the rise in demand?

Extra demand cures cyclical unemployment. Frictional and structural unemployment remain even at full employment: they are part of the natural rate.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Supply-side policies aim to raise capacity: LRAS shifts right, raising real output without adding to inflation.
  • Market-based policies use incentives and competition; interventionist policies fix under-provision of skills, health, R&D and infrastructure.
  • Effects take years and are uncertain in size.

Mission 2

  • Economic growth is an increase in real GDP.
  • Actual growth uses spare capacity (AD); potential growth raises capacity (PPC shifts out, LRAS shifts right).
  • Long-run growth comes from more or better factors: labour, capital, human capital, technology, institutions.
  • Growth raises living standards but can harm the environment, widen inequality and add to inflation.

Mission 3

  • Actual real GDP fluctuates around a rising trend (potential output): the business cycle.
  • Phases: boom, peak, downturn, recession, trough, recovery.
  • Output gap = actual − potential. Below potential: spare capacity. Above potential: inflationary pressure.
  • Demand and supply shocks cause the swings.

Mission 4

  • Working-age population = employed + unemployed + inactive. Labour force = employed + unemployed.
  • Unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ labour force × 100.
  • Survey (ILO) and claimant count measure different things and often differ.
  • Discouraged and underemployed workers are not counted as unemployed, so the rate understates joblessness.

Mission 5

  • Frictional: between jobs. Structural: skills or location mismatch. Cyclical: too little aggregate demand.
  • Seasonal: jobs that vanish at certain times of year.
  • Real-wage: wages held above equilibrium.
  • Costs are personal, social and economic; lost output is never recovered.
