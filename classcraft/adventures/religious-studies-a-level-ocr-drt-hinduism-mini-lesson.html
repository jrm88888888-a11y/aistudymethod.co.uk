Mission 1

A religion without a starting point

  • Hinduism has no founder, no single moment of origin and no single founding text.
  • Many Hindus call it sanatana dharma: the "eternal" way, without beginning.
  • Historians look at two kinds of evidence:
  • Archaeology: the cities of the Indus Valley civilisation.
  • Texts: the Rig Veda, the oldest Hindu scripture, composed orally in an early form of Sanskrit.
  • The hard question: how, if at all, do the two connect?
Mission 1

The Indus Valley civilisation

  • Flourished along the Indus and the Ghaggar-Hakra rivers (today Pakistan and north-west India); mature phase c. 2600–1900 BCE.
  • Rediscovered in the 1920s: Harappa (excavated 1921) and Mohenjo-daro (1922); surveys led by John Marshall of the Archaeological Survey of India.
  • Other sites: Dholavira, Lothal, Kalibangan, Rakhigarhi.
  • Features: planned streets on a grid, covered drains, standard-sized bricks and weights, granaries or large halls, trade with Mesopotamia.
  • A script on thousands of small stamp seals: still undeciphered.
  • No palaces or obvious temples have been found.
Mission 1

Evidence of religion

Each find is real; each interpretation is debated.

  • The Great Bath (Mohenjo-daro): a watertight brick pool. Perhaps ritual bathing for purity, as at later temple tanks.
  • The "Pashupati" seal: a horned figure seated cross-legged, surrounded by animals. Marshall called it a "proto-Shiva", as lord of animals and a yogi.
  • Female figurines: terracotta figures, often read as mother goddesses, linked to later Devi worship.
  • Fire altars (Kalibangan, Lothal): pits with ash, perhaps for offerings.
  • Other symbols: the svastika, pipal leaves, conical stones once read as lingams.
Mission 1

Decline

  • From c. 1900 BCE the great cities shrank; people moved to smaller villages east and south.
  • Likely causes, debated: rivers shifting or drying (the Ghaggar-Hakra), a weaker monsoon, declining trade.
  • Mortimer Wheeler (1890–1976) once blamed invading Aryans for a massacre at Mohenjo-daro.
  • Later study showed the skeletons were buried at different times, not killed in one attack. Most archaeologists now reject the massacre idea.
  • Decline was gradual: much village culture, crafts and perhaps beliefs continued.

Mission 1

Who were the "Aryans"?

  • Arya ("noble") is what the people of the Rig Veda called themselves.
  • It names a language and culture, not a race. They spoke Vedic Sanskrit, related to Old Persian, Greek and Latin (the Indo-European family).
  • Their culture, as the hymns describe it: cattle herding, horses and chariots, fire sacrifice (yajna), the sacred drink soma, gods such as Agni and Indra.
  • The Rig Veda is usually dated c. 1500–1200 BCE: after the Indus cities declined.
  • 19th- and 20th-century writers misused "Aryan" as a racial label; scholars reject that use.

A river in the hymns

Rig Veda 7.95.1–2 (paraphrase): the Sarasvati surpasses all other waters in greatness, flowing pure from the mountains to the sea.

Rig Veda 10.75.5 (paraphrase): a hymn to the rivers names, in order, the Ganga, Yamuna, Sarasvati and others.

Q1 Why do continuity supporters stress these verses?

Show answer

If the Sarasvati is the Ghaggar-Hakra, which dried up after c. 1900 BCE, the hymns may describe a time when Indus sites still flourished.

Q2 What is the main objection?

Show answer

The identification is disputed: "Sarasvati" may name a different river, a smaller later stream, or a goddess as much as a place.

Mission 1

Problems with the evidence

  • Undeciphered script: we have Indus writing but cannot read it.
  • Reading backwards: seeing a "Shiva" or a "goddess" may project later Hinduism onto older objects.
  • Different kinds of evidence: archaeology without texts (Indus) versus texts without much archaeology (the Vedas).
  • Dating oral texts: the Rig Veda was memorised for centuries before being written; its date is estimated from language.
  • Genes and language: ancient DNA shows people moving, not what they believed or spoke.
  • Politics: national and religious identity is at stake, so evidence is read with strong motives.

Mission 1Evaluate

Did Hinduism begin in the Indus Valley?

  • Claim: Modern Hinduism has its roots in the Indus Valley civilisation.
  • For: Possible links: ritual bathing, a yogi-like figure, goddess figurines, fire altars, the svastika.
  • For: Hinduism absorbed many local traditions; Indus village culture continued after the cities fell.
  • Against: Without the script, every link is a guess; similar objects need not mean similar beliefs.
  • Against: The Vedas, which most Hindus treat as the highest authority, come from Indo-Aryan culture.
  • Judgement criteria: What counts as evidence of belief? Does "roots" mean one source or many? How far does inconclusive evidence matter to the many Hindus whose faith rests on the Vedas, not on archaeology?

Mission 2

The four Vedas

  • Veda means "knowledge". There are four:
  • Rig Veda: 1,028 hymns of praise in 10 books (mandalas); the oldest, c. 1500–1200 BCE.
  • Sama Veda: verses set to chant. Yajur Veda: formulas for performing sacrifice. Atharva Veda: hymns and spells for healing, protection and daily life.
  • Each Veda grew in layers: hymns (Samhitas), ritual texts (Brahmanas), "forest" texts (Aranyakas), and the philosophical Upanishads.
  • Written texts: for centuries the Vedas were passed on orally, memorised syllable by syllable by Brahmin families. They were written down much later; recitation still matters more than reading.
Mission 2

Yajna: the sacrifice

  • Yajna: offerings (ghee, grain, milk, soma, and in some rites animals) poured into a sacred fire.
  • Specialist priests: one recites hymns, one performs the actions, one chants, one oversees.
  • Purpose: to praise and feed the gods (devas), who give in return: rain, cattle, sons, long life, victory.
  • Sacrifice keeps rta, the cosmic order that holds nature and society together.
  • Vedic society: semi-nomadic, cattle-herding tribes led by a chief (rajan), with tribal assemblies. Priests and warriors held high status; the four-fold varna scheme appears only in a late hymn (Rig Veda 10.90; see a later lesson).
  • Household rites (daily fire offerings) and great public rites (lasting days) both existed.
Mission 2

Agni: the fire

  • Agni is fire, and the god in the fire. About 200 hymns are addressed to him; the Rig Veda opens with a hymn to Agni (1.1).
  • Messenger: Agni carries offerings up to the gods and brings the gods down to the sacrifice.
  • Priest of the gods: he is called the household priest and the "mouth" of the gods.
  • Present in three places: the hearth on earth, lightning in the air, the sun in the sky.
  • Every yajna depends on Agni: without fire, there is no offering.
Mission 2

Indra: king of the gods

  • Indra: the warrior god of storm and thunder, armed with the vajra (thunderbolt). About a quarter of the Rig Veda's hymns are to him.
  • Great deed (Rig Veda 1.32, paraphrase): Indra kills the serpent Vritra, who had held back the waters, and releases the rivers.
  • He leads the Aryas in battle and is strengthened by drinking soma.
  • Worshippers ask Indra for victory, rain and cattle.
  • He is a model of the Vedic warrior-chief: generous, powerful, sometimes reckless.
Mission 2

Soma: the drink and the god

  • Soma is a plant, the drink pressed from it, and a god. Book 9 of the Rig Veda (114 hymns) is wholly to Soma.
  • In the rite, stalks are pressed, the juice is filtered through wool, mixed with milk and offered; priests drink it too.
  • Effects described: inspiration, strength, visions, a sense of immortality (Rig Veda 8.48.3, paraphrase: the worshippers have drunk soma, become immortal and reached the light).
  • The plant is unknown today: suggestions include ephedra and a mushroom; no theory is agreed.
  • Later, Soma is identified with the moon, and substitute plants are used in rites.

Mission 2

Other gods of the Rig Veda

  • Varuna: guardian of rta and of oaths; he sees wrongdoing and can forgive.
  • Mitra: god of agreements and friendship, often paired with Varuna.
  • Surya and Savitr: the sun. The Gayatri mantra (Rig Veda 3.62.10) is addressed to Savitr.
  • Ushas: goddess of the dawn; one of several goddesses, with Aditi and Vac (speech).
  • Rudra: a fierce god of storms and healing, later linked with Shiva.
  • Vishnu: a minor god famed for his three strides across the universe (Rig Veda 1.154).
  • Tradition speaks of 33 gods.
Mission 2Read the source

One being, many names

Rig Veda 1.164.46 (paraphrase): They call it Indra, Mitra, Varuna, Agni, and the heavenly bird. The One Being the wise call by many names. (Sanskrit: ekam sad vipra bahudha vadanti.)

Rig Veda 10.129, the Hymn of Creation (paraphrase): Who really knows how creation came about? The gods came after it. Perhaps the one who watches over it in the highest heaven knows, or perhaps he does not know.

Q1 What does 1.164.46 suggest about the many gods?

Show answer

They may be names for one reality.

Q2 How is 10.129 different from hymns praising Agni or Indra?

Show answer

It asks questions rather than praising; it doubts that anyone, even a god, knows the origin.

Mission 2

Different ideas of God in the Rig Veda

  • Polytheism: many gods, each with a role (Agni, Indra, Varuna…).
  • Henotheism: Max Müller (1823–1900) noticed that each hymn praises its god as if supreme, though others are praised elsewhere.
  • Monism: one reality behind all names (1.164.46), developed later in the Upanishads as Brahman.
  • Cosmic person: the universe as the body of Purusha (10.90).
  • Questioning: the Hymn of Creation (10.129) leaves the origin open.
Mission 2

Vedic and modern deities

  • Agni: still central. In weddings the couple walk around the fire with Agni as witness; the havan (fire offering) is common, and the Arya Samaj makes it its main worship.
  • Indra: now a lesser king of heaven. In the Puranas, Krishna lifts Mount Govardhan to shelter villagers from Indra's storm.
  • Soma: no longer drunk; Soma is the moon god.
  • Vishnu and Rudra-Shiva: minor in the Rig Veda, now supreme for Vaishnavas and Shaivas.
  • Goddesses: Ushas and Aditi were minor; Devi (Shakti) is supreme for Shaktas.
  • Vedic mantras are still recited in samskaras (life-cycle rites), and the Gayatri mantra daily by many Hindus.

Mission 2Evaluate

Do the Vedic gods still matter?

  • Claim: The gods of the Rig Veda have little importance for Hindus today.
  • For: Indra and Varuna are rarely worshipped; daily puja centres on Vishnu, Shiva, Devi, Ganesha and avatars.
  • For: Many Hindus know the Vedic hymns mainly as recited sound, not for their content.
  • Against: Agni is present at weddings, havan and cremation; the Gayatri mantra is recited daily.
  • Against: The Vedic idea of one reality with many names (1.164.46) shapes how Hindus understand all their deities.
  • Judgement criteria: Importance as objects of worship, or as ideas? For which Hindus (Arya Samaj, Vaishnava, Smarta)? Ritual presence or personal devotion?

Mission 3

Three theistic traditions

  • Theistic: centred on a personal Supreme God (saguna Brahman).
  • Vaishnavism: Vishnu (and his avatars) supreme. Shaivism: Shiva supreme. Shaktism: the Goddess, Devi or Shakti, supreme.
  • Each has its own scriptures (beside the Vedas), temples, festivals, marks, saints and schools.
  • They grew over many centuries, especially from the time of the epics and Puranas (c. 300 BCE–1000 CE).
  • Smartas worship several deities as equal forms of one Brahman.
  • Many Hindus respect all the deities while devoting themselves to one.
Mission 3

Vaishnavism

  • Supreme deity: Vishnu, with Lakshmi; worshipped especially as his avatars Krishna and Rama.
  • Bhakti: loving devotion and surrender (prapatti) to God, who saves by grace; chanting the divine names.
  • Ritual worship: temple and home puja to the murti; offering food that becomes prasad; the tulsi plant; great temples such as Tirupati, Srirangam and Puri.
  • Moral and ethical values: dharma as Vishnu's order; many Vaishnavas are strict vegetarians; humility and service of other devotees.
  • Schools: Ramanuja's Sri Vaishnavism; Madhva; Gaudiya (Chaitanya, ISKCON); Pushti Marg; Swaminarayan.
  • Mark: a vertical U- or V-shaped tilak on the forehead.
Mission 3

Shaivism

  • Supreme deity: Shiva, with Parvati; worshipped in the lingam, a smooth, rounded stone pillar, symbol of formless divine presence.
  • Bhakti: Tamil poet-saints, the 63 Nayanars; the five-syllable mantra Om Namah Shivaya; festival Maha Shivaratri.
  • Ritual worship: pouring water and milk over the lingam and offering bilva leaves; temples such as Varanasi's Kashi Vishwanath and the twelve Jyotirlingas.
  • Asceticism: Shiva is the great yogi; many sadhus are Shaiva, with ash (vibhuti), matted hair and rudraksha beads.
  • Schools: Shaiva Siddhanta (Tamil, based on the Agamas); Kashmir Shaivism (non-dual; Abhinavagupta, c. 950–1016); Lingayats or Virashaivas (Basava, 12th century), who wear a small lingam on the body.
  • Mark: three horizontal lines of ash (tripundra).

Mission 3

Shakti: power as Goddess

  • Shakti: "power", "energy": the creative power of the divine.
  • In many traditions every god has a shakti, pictured as his consort: Lakshmi with Vishnu, Parvati with Shiva, Saraswati with Brahma.
  • A common saying (from the Saundarya Lahari, paraphrase): Shiva united with Shakti can create; without her he cannot even stir.
  • In Shaktism, the Goddess, Devi or Mahadevi, is not only a consort but the Supreme reality: she brings forth Brahma, Vishnu and Shiva.
  • She is worshipped as the Divine Mother: both gentle and fierce.
Mission 3Read the source

Uma teaches the gods

Kena Upanishad (Swami Paramananda's translation, 1919). The gods cannot recognise a mysterious being. Indra runs towards it, but it disappears:

> "Then he saw in that very space a woman beautifully adorned, Uma of golden hue, daughter of Haimavat (Himalaya)." (3:12)

> "She (Uma) said: 'It is Brahman. It is through the victory of Brahman that ye are victorious.'" (4:1)

  • Uma is a name of Parvati, Shiva's consort.

Q1 Who knows what the gods do not?

Show answer

Uma, the Goddess: she reveals Brahman to Indra.

Q2 Why might Shaktas value this passage?

Show answer

The Goddess appears as the one who knows and reveals the ultimate reality.

Mission 3

Forms of the Goddess

  • Durga: the warrior, riding a lion. In the Devi Mahatmya (c. 5th–6th century; part of the Markandeya Purana) the gods' powers join to form her, and she slays the buffalo-demon Mahishasura.
  • Kali: dark, fierce, garlanded with skulls; destroys evil and ego; loved as Mother (e.g. by Ramakrishna, 1836–1886).
  • Lakshmi (wealth, good fortune), Saraswati (learning, music), Parvati (devoted wife and mother).
  • Village goddesses (grama-devatas) protect local communities.
  • Festivals: Navaratri (nine nights), Durga Puja (Bengal), Kali Puja. Shakti Peethas: shrines where, the story says, parts of the goddess Sati's body fell.
Mission 3

Right-hand and left-hand Shaktism

  • Right-hand (dakshinachara): devotion and ritual worship within ordinary dharma: puja, mantras, fasting at Navaratri, the Sri Yantra in Sri Vidya worship; vegetarian offerings; moral values kept.
  • Left-hand (vamachara): Tantric ritual that deliberately uses substances and acts normally forbidden (the "five Ms": wine, meat, fish, parched grain or gestures, and sexual union).
  • Aim: to transcend purity and impurity, see Shakti in everything and awaken inner power (kundalini) towards liberation.
  • Usually secret, under a guru, after initiation; many practitioners use symbolic substitutes.
  • Many Hindus regard left-hand practice as dangerous or wrong; Tantric practitioners see it as a fast but demanding path.
  • Some Shakta temples offer animal sacrifice (e.g. at Kamakhya, Assam); many Shaktas reject it.

Mission 3Evaluate

Separate religions?

  • Claim: Vaishnavism, Shaivism and Shaktism are best seen as separate religions.
  • For: Each has its own Supreme God, scriptures, temples, saints, theology and initiation.
  • For: In history they sometimes competed; some devotees worship only their own deity.
  • Against: They share the Vedas, dharma, karma, samsara and moksha, and the idea of one Brahman.
  • Against: Many Hindus move between them: a Vaishnava family may visit a Shiva temple at Shivaratri.
  • Against: "Religion" is a Western category that may not fit.
  • Judgement criteria: What makes a religion: beliefs, practices, institutions, self-understanding? Do devotees see themselves as Hindus first?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Hinduism has no founder or start date; historians use Indus archaeology and the Rig Veda.
  • Indus finds (Great Bath, "Pashupati" seal, figurines, fire altars) suggest religion, but the script is unread and every link is an interpretation.
  • Invasion is largely abandoned; migration is the majority academic view; continuity is held by some Indian scholars and many Hindus.

Mission 2

  • The four Vedas (Rig, Sama, Yajur, Atharva) were memorised and recited long before they were written.
  • Yajna (fire sacrifice) kept rta; Agni carried offerings, Indra won battles and released the waters, soma inspired.
  • The Rig Veda holds many ideas of God: many gods, each praised as supreme, one being with many names, and open questioning.
  • Agni and Vedic mantras remain central to ritual; Vishnu and Shiva rose from minor Vedic gods to supreme deities.

Mission 3

  • Vaishnavism (Vishnu supreme; bhakti, puja, ethical life), Shaivism (Shiva supreme; lingam, bhakti, asceticism) and Shaktism (Devi supreme) are the main theistic traditions.
  • Shakti is the creative power of the divine, pictured as Goddess; for Shaktas she is supreme, worshipped as Durga, Kali and others; right-hand devotion and left-hand Tantra.
  • Whether they are separate religions or one family is debated.
