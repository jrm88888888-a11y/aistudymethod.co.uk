Mission 1

Mission 1: London is more than 600 times as crowded as the Highlands. Why do people cluster where they do?

Make a guess: London is more than 600 times as crowded as the Highlands. Why do people cluster where they do?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 2.1.1 overview of the location of significant areas of population in the UK
Mission 1

The UK's people

  • UK population: about 69.5 million (mid-2025).
  • Latest censuses: England 56.5 million and Wales 3.1 million (2021); Scotland 5.4 million (2022); Northern Ireland 1.9 million (2021).
  • London alone had 8.8 million: about 1 in 8 of the people counted (our calculation).
Mission 1

Dense and sparse

  • Population density = people per square kilometre (km²).
  • London: 5,598 per km², the most densely populated region.
  • England 434; Wales 150; South West England 239, the least dense English region.
  • Glasgow City: 3,555. Highland 9.2; Na h-Eileanan Siar (Outer Hebrides) 8.6, the lowest in Scotland.
  • Pattern: dense in the south-east, Midlands, north-west England and Scotland's Central Belt; sparse in upland Scotland, Wales and the Pennines.

Quick check

Mission 1

An urban country

  • In England, an area is rural if it is outside settlements of more than 10,000 people.
  • In mid-2024, 17% of England's people lived in rural settlements.
  • So about 83% lived in urban areas (our calculation).
Mission 1

Physical factors

  • Uplands are often steep, wet and windy, with poor soils: few people live there.
  • The North Pennines has "a severe climate, with high rainfall, low temperatures and strong winds"; its population "is low, with only a few small market towns".
  • Lowlands, coasts and river estuaries are easier to farm, build on and trade from: most big cities grew there.
Mission 1

Human factors

  • Jobs and money: London had the highest GDP per head of any UK region in 2024, £72,201. Jobs draw people.
  • History: England and Wales grew fastest from 1801 to 1911, by 13.6% a decade on average, as industrial cities grew. Industry around the Tees estuary, for example, grew from "the abundance of mineral deposits" nearby.
  • Transport: ports, railways and motorways link the biggest cities.
Quick check
?Why is the North Pennines one of England's emptiest areas?
Mission check

?In Country Z, areas P and Q are both flat, low-lying and have the same mild climate. P is a port that has been a big city since the 1800s; Q is farmland far from main roads. Why is P far more densely populated?

The UK fits the same pattern: London is over 600 times as dense as the Highlands, because people cluster where jobs, transport and history meet on easy lowland, and stay sparse on harsh uplands.

Mission 2

Mission 2: In one year, about 339,000 people moved into England's mostly rural council areas. What does that do to villages?

Make a guess: In one year, about 339,000 people moved into England's mostly rural council areas. What does that do to villages?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 2.1.1 urban-rural continuum; sphere of influence for retailing and urban services (e.g. rural access to health care)
  • 2.1.1 counter-urbanisation: reasons and impact on rural settlements
  • 2.1.1 commuting patterns and transport issues arising from counter-urbanisation in the UK
Mission 2

The urban-rural continuum

  • Settlements are not just "town" or "country". They form a continuum: city centre → suburbs → towns → villages → hamlets → isolated farms.
  • Official line in England: rural = outside settlements of more than 10,000 people.
  • Rural places are also split by access: nearer to a major town or city (within a 30-minute drive) or further from one.
  • In 2024, 17% of England's people lived in rural settlements; two-thirds of them lived nearer to a major town or city.
Mission 2

Spheres of influence

  • A settlement's sphere of influence is the area whose people use its shops and services.
  • High-order services (hospital, big shopping centre) need many customers: large sphere, found in big places.
  • Low-order services (village shop, primary school): small sphere, found everywhere.
  • Small hub towns of 10,000–30,000 people act as centres "for services and businesses for a wider rural hinterland".
Mission 2

Shopping from the countryside

  • Rural residents' average shopping trip: 6.2 miles. Urban residents': 3.2 miles.
  • Rural people travel "nearly twice as far" a year to shop as urban people.
  • Why? Bigger shops are in towns; villages sit on the edge of towns' spheres of influence.
Mission 2

Health care in rural areas

  • Health care destinations: pharmacies, GPs, dentists, hospitals.
  • Without a car, 46% of people in remote rural areas live in places with very low connectivity to health care; for rural people nearer a major town or city, 18%.
  • Driving "inevitably improves" access, but 10% of rural households have no car or van.
Quick check
?Which service would you expect to have the largest sphere of influence?
Mission 2

Counter-urbanisation

  • Counter-urbanisation: people moving out of cities to smaller towns, villages and the countryside.
  • Year to June 2023: 339,000 moved into England's mostly rural council areas; 278,400 moved out. Net gain: about 61,000.
  • Largest net gain: people aged 30–39 (15,600). The only net loss: ages 15–19 (13,700), mostly young people leaving for education.
  • Moves spiked in 2020–21 during the COVID-19 pandemic.
Mission 2

Why people move

  • Push from cities: high house prices. In London, homes sold for 10.6 times average earnings in 2025, the least affordable region.
  • Pull of the countryside: more space, quieter, greener; popular with families and retirees.
  • Technology and cars make it possible: 35% of rural workers worked from home at least half the week in 2025 (urban 30%); 51% of rural households have two or more cars.
Quick check
?Which statistic best shows that working from home helps people live in the countryside?
Mission 2

Impacts on rural settlements

  • Growth: rural settlements nearer a major town or city grew 9% in 2014–2024; those further away, 5%. South Cambridgeshire, near Cambridge, grew 8.9% in 2011–2021.
  • New housing on village edges.
  • Age mix changes: teenagers leave; incomers are mostly aged 30–39. Median age in smaller rural settlements is 50.5; in London 34.7.
  • Second homes: more than 2 in 100 homes in mostly rural council areas, twice the national average.
  • Services may gain customers, or empty in the day as residents work and shop in towns: a dormitory village.
Mission 2

Commuting patterns

  • Commuters live in one place and work in another; flows run into towns in the morning and out in the evening.
  • Rural residents' average commuting trip: 12.1 miles; urban residents': 8.6 miles. That is about 40% longer (our calculation).
  • Yet rural residents spent less time commuting a year (41 hours vs 60), possibly because of slower speeds and congestion in towns.
  • 88% of the distance rural people travel is by car, van or other private transport.
Mission 2

Transport issues

  • Car dependence: public transport is only 4% of rural residents' trips (urban 10%).
  • Poor access without a car: 54% of rural residents live in areas with low connectivity by walking, cycling or public transport.
  • Congestion and pollution where commuter traffic meets town roads at peak times.
  • School runs: 41% of rural children's school journeys are by car or van.
Quick check
?Which is a transport issue made worse by counter-urbanisation?
Mission check

?A village 15 minutes' drive from a large city gains 300 new homes. Most buyers are couples in their thirties who work in the city two or three days a week. Which pair of changes is most likely?

Villages near cities grow as people move out for space and cheaper homes; because they still work, shop and see doctors in towns, they drive further, and the roads in between carry the pressure.

Mission 3: Why are some UK villages growing fast while others are losing people?

Make a guess: Why are some UK villages growing fast while others are losing people?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 2.1.2 urban spheres of influence and technological change on rural services (post office, bank closures)
  • 2.1.2 causes and effects of rural poverty and deprivation; depopulation of remote rural areas

Mission 3

Rural England in numbers

  • In 2024, 17% of England's people lived in rural settlements; 27% of them were aged 65+.
  • Farming makes up 14% of rural businesses but only 8% of rural employment.
  • Rural people travel further, mostly by car, and have poorer access to services.
Mission 3

Two kinds of rural change

  • Growth near cities: people move out of towns into villages (counter-urbanisation); towns spread outwards (suburbanisation).
  • Decline in remote areas: more people leave or die than arrive or are born (rural depopulation).
  • In England, people on average move into rural areas, except 15–19-year-olds, who move out.
Mission 3

Growth: South Cambridgeshire

  • A rural district around the city of Cambridge.
  • Population: 148,800 (2011) → 162,000 (2021), up 8.9%; England grew 6.6%.
  • People aged 65+ rose by 28.7%; children under 15 by 8.5%.
Mission 3

Northstowe: a new town in the fields

  • Northstowe, South Cambridgeshire, is planned to have 10,000 homes and about 25,000 people.
  • New schools, roads, public transport routes, cycleways and green space are part of the plan.
  • Social change: new families and commuters; pressure on services until new ones are built.
  • Economic change: building jobs; more customers for local shops.
Quick check
?A family moves from Cambridge to a nearby village and the parents still work in the city. What process is this?
Mission 3

Decline: the Outer Hebrides

  • Na h-Eileanan Siar (Outer Hebrides): islands off north-west Scotland.
  • Population fell 5.5% between the 2011 and 2022 censuses: the biggest fall of any Scottish council area.
  • It has Scotland's lowest population density.
  • In Scotland's shrinking areas, mostly in the west and south-west, more people died than were born.
Mission 3

What changes when people leave?

  • 13.7% of people are aged 55+ and living alone: the highest share in Scotland.
  • Only 19.7% of households have dependent children, among the lowest in Scotland.
  • Social change: an ageing population; fewer children for schools; more need for care.
  • Economic change: fewer workers and customers, so shops, buses and services are harder to keep open.
Quick check
?South Cambridgeshire grew from about 148,800 to 162,000 people (2011–2021). How many extra people is that?
Mission 3

The negative multiplier

  • Negative multiplier effect: young people leave → fewer customers and pupils → shop, school or bus closes → fewer jobs and services → more people leave.
  • Each loss makes the next more likely: a downward spiral.
  • It explains why remote areas such as the Outer Hebrides find decline hard to reverse.
Mission 3

Bigger towns, new technology

  • A town's sphere of influence is the area whose people use its shops and services. Better roads and cars widen it, so village shops lose trade.
  • Technology: banks report a shift to remote (online) banking, so branch networks shrink.
  • Example: when Chepstow's last bank branch closed, the nearest was 8 miles away: about 30 minutes and £4 by public transport.
Mission 3

Hidden rural deprivation

  • Rural areas are, on average, less deprived than urban areas, but face problems of access and housing quality.
  • Rural incomes are higher, but higher spending, especially on transport, more than cancels that out.
  • Rural fuel poverty is deeper on average, especially in old homes off the gas grid.
  • The most rural areas have poorer broadband.
Quick check
?A village bank closes and services move online. Who is likely to lose out most?
Mission 3

Building sustainable rural communities

  • Community shops: in 2009, 124 residents of Great Ryburgh, Norfolk, bought shares to help raise £130,000 and save their shop, which also has a post office and café.
  • Banking hubs: shared counters run by a not-for-profit company; the 250th opened in 2026.
  • Broadband: Project Gigabit, the government's £5 billion plan, is bringing fast broadband to 4,000+ rural premises in Teesdale alone.
  • Affordable homes let young people stay. Outside London, majority-rural areas built the most new affordable housing relative to their housing stock.
Mission check

?Village X is 10 km from a growing city and has a railway station. Village Y is on a remote peninsula, two hours from any city. What is most likely?

Accessibility to city jobs decides it. South Cambridgeshire, around Cambridge, grew 8.9% (2011–21); the remote Outer Hebrides fell 5.5% (2011–22).

Remote decline can become a negative multiplier: services close, and more people leave.

Mission 4

Mission 4: In one English district a third of people are 65 or over; in another, one in eighteen. What makes a place old?

Make a guess: In one English district a third of people are 65 or over; in another, one in eighteen. What makes a place old?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 2.2.1 economic, political and social factors in UK population change: birth rates, ageing, migration into and within the UK
  • 2.2.1 consequences of the ageing UK population
  • 2.2.1 the need for new housing in the UK
Mission 4

The UK keeps growing

  • Mid-2001: 59.1 million people. Mid-2025: 69.5 million.
  • That is about 10.4 million more, a rise of 17.5% (our calculation).
  • Growth in the year to mid-2025 was 0.3%, the slowest since mid-2020.
Mission 4

Fewer births

  • Year to mid-2025: 652,400 births, the lowest for at least 43 years; 647,500 deaths.
  • So natural change (births − deaths) added only 4,900 people.
  • In England and Wales the total fertility rate was 1.39 children per woman in 2025.
  • Social factor: "people are having fewer children and are having children later in life".
  • Economic and social factors often linked to this: the cost of housing and childcare, and more years in education and work before starting a family.
Mission 4

Migration into the UK

  • Year to mid-2025: 886,200 people arrived to live for a year or more; 663,400 left.
  • Net international migration = 222,900, down from 651,700 the year before.
  • Net migration has been the largest driver of UK growth in every year since mid-1999 except mid-2012.
  • Political factor: in December 2023 the government raised the earnings threshold for overseas workers by "nearly 50%".
Quick check
?In the year to mid-2025, what added most to the UK's population?
Mission 4

Why is the UK ageing?

  • Living longer: improvements in life expectancy mean more people reach older ages.
  • Fewer children: fertility has fallen since the 1960s.
  • Baby boomers: people born in the 1960s are now moving into older age.
  • Ageing varies by place: in 2021, North Norfolk had the highest share aged 65+ (33.5%, about a third); Tower Hamlets in London had the lowest (5.6%, about one in eighteen).
Mission 4

Effects of an ageing population

  • Old-age dependency ratio: 285 people of pensionable age per 1,000 of working age in mid-2025.
  • Pressure on pensions, social care, housing and health services.
  • Older people in rural areas face "longer journeys" to reach services.
  • Opportunities too: volunteering, community activism, longer working lives, new markets.
Quick check
?The old-age dependency ratio rises. What does this mean?
Mission 4

Migration within the UK

  • Internal migration = moving home within the UK.
  • In the year to mid-2025, London's population fell 0.29%: births and international arrivals were "more than offset by net outward migration to other areas of the UK".
  • England and Northern Ireland both had a small net outflow to the rest of the UK.
  • Economic factor: first-time buyers bought homes further from London than ten years earlier.
  • Young adults often move to cities for study and work; older people more often move out to smaller towns, the coast and the countryside.
Mission 4

The need for new housing

  • More people: +10.4 million since 2001 (our calculation).
  • An ageing population means older people stay in their homes for longer, so fewer homes come free.
  • Affordability: homes in London cost 10.6 times average earnings in 2025; in the North East 5.0 times.
  • Response: mandatory council targets to deliver 1.5 million homes in England over five years (2024 plan).
Mission check

?A city borough has a university and many office jobs. Only 6% of its residents are 65 or over, and about a quarter are in their twenties. What best explains why it is so young?

Where people move decides how old a place is: young adults move to cities for study and work, while many rural and coastal places keep or attract older residents. Nationally, longer lives and fewer births make the whole UK older.

Mission 5

Two kinds of building land

  • Brownfield site: land that has been built on before, e.g. old factories, gasworks, docks. It is often derelict (abandoned and decaying).
  • Greenfield site: land never built on, e.g. farmland or countryside, often at the edge of a city.
  • Dereliction: empty, run-down buildings and land, often left when industries close.
Mission 5

London builds on brownfield

  • In England in 2021–22, 54% of new residential addresses were on previously developed land.
  • London had the highest share: 88%.
  • Only 2% of new addresses in England were in the Green Belt.
Mission 5

The Green Belt

  • A Green Belt is a ring of open land kept free of most building. Its aim: "to prevent urban sprawl by keeping land permanently open".
  • England's Green Belt covers about 12.5% of its land (31 March 2024).
  • London's Metropolitan Green Belt is the largest: 31.1% of England's Green Belt area.
  • One purpose is to "assist urban regeneration by encouraging the recycling of derelict and other urban land".
Quick check
?Why does London build most new homes on brownfield land?
Mission 5

Brownfield: for and against

  • For: brings derelict land back into use and regenerates run-down areas.
  • For: roads, pipes, schools and transport are already nearby.
  • For: protects the countryside from sprawl.
  • Against: old industry may leave contaminated ground that is costly to clean.
  • Against: sites can be small and cramped, so homes are built densely.
Mission 5

Greenfield: for and against

  • For: open land is easier and quicker to build on: no demolition or clean-up.
  • For: room for larger homes, gardens and new roads.
  • Against: loses farmland and habitats. Worldwide, about 60% of land turned urban since 1970 was productive farmland.
  • Against: new estates far from jobs mean more car journeys, adding to sprawl.
Quick check
?Which is an advantage of a greenfield site for a developer?
Mission 5

What makes a community sustainable?

  • A sustainable community meets people's needs now without harming the future.
  • Egan's wheel is a model of the parts such a community needs. Use it as a checklist for any scheme:
  • Good housing and design · jobs and a thriving economy · transport links · services (schools, health, shops) · a cared-for environment · a say in decisions · fair for everyone, with a strong community life.
Mission 5

A brownfield context: Ebbsfleet

  • Ebbsfleet Garden City is being built "across 2,500 acres of previously used, brownfield land".
  • Up to 15,000 homes; 40% green and blue space; £3.9 billion of investment planned by 2035 (figures updated August 2023).
  • Issues: preparing old industrial land is slow and costly; schools, health centres and transport must arrive with the homes; many residents may commute to London.
Mission 5

A greenfield context: building on the Green Belt

  • England's Green Belt shrank by 3,540 hectares in the year to March 2024, as councils changed boundaries in new local plans.
  • National policy now lets some "grey belt" land be built on, under "Golden Rules": more affordable housing, better infrastructure, and new or improved public green space.
  • Issues: homes are needed, but open countryside is lost, and new estates may depend on cars.
Quick check
?A new estate has 2,000 homes but no school, no shop and one bus a day. Which two parts of a sustainable community are weakest?
Mission check

?A city with a green belt needs 2,000 new homes. It could use a derelict gasworks near the centre or farmland at its edge. What is the strongest argument for the gasworks?

London put 88% of new homes on previously developed land in 2021–22, because the Green Belt holds back sprawl. Brownfield reuse regenerates derelict land but can be costly; greenfield building is easier but loses countryside.

Mission 6

The shift online

  • Internet sales as a share of all retail sales in Great Britain:
  • 2007: 3.4% → 2019: 19.2% → 2025: 27.5%
  • The peak was 37.8% in January 2021, during the coronavirus pandemic.
  • Today about £1 in every £4 spent in shops is spent online (our rounding of 27.5%).
Mission 6

Why retail is changing

  • Technological: fast internet, smartphones and home delivery make buying online easy; online sales rose eightfold as a share of retail from 2007 to 2025 (our calculation).
  • Economic: high-street rents and business rates are high; out-of-town sites offer cheaper land and free parking.
  • Cultural: shopping has become a leisure activity; on GB high streets, jobs in accommodation and food rose by over 20% in every region from 2012 to 2017.
Quick check
?"More people meet friends for brunch and browse the shops afterwards." Which kind of factor is this?
Mission 6

Pressure on the CBD and high street

  • Across Great Britain, retail businesses on high streets fell 2% from 2012 to 2017, while those off the high street grew 6%.
  • High-street retail jobs fell in every country and region except London (+6%).
  • Even London's main shopping street, Oxford Street in the West End CBD, "has faced significant challenges in recent years due to the impact of the pandemic".
Mission 6

Out-of-centre shopping: Westfield Stratford City

  • Opened in September 2011 in Stratford, east London, well away from the West End CBD.
  • Built for £1.8 billion on a site that was "unsightly landfill", next to the Olympic Park.
  • Over 300 shops and over 70 places to eat, a 17-screen cinema, hotels and offices; direct access to Stratford International station.
  • The minister opening it called it "Europe's biggest shopping centre".
Quick check
?Why is Westfield Stratford City described as out-of-centre shopping?
Mission 6

Out-of-town shopping: costs and benefits

  • Benefits: many shops in one place; parking and transport links; jobs and regeneration (the minister said Westfield "brings welcome investment and jobs").
  • Costs: can draw shoppers and trade away from older town centres; car-based sites add traffic; out-of-town sites can take greenfield land.
  • Westfield Stratford City avoided greenfield land and is served by trains, which reduces some costs.
Mission 6

Internet shopping: costs and benefits

  • Benefits: shop any time; compare prices; easier for people far from shops or with limited mobility.
  • Costs: fewer shop jobs and empty units on high streets; more delivery van traffic; harder for people who are not online.
  • Online's share rose to 27.5% of retail in 2025, so these effects are now large.
Quick check
?Which is a cost of the growth of internet shopping for a city?
Mission 6

A high street changing: Oxford Street, London

  • From 28 February to 2 May 2025, the Mayor of London consulted on Oxford Street's future; there were over 6,000 responses.
  • Almost 70% of Londoners who expressed a view supported a Mayoral Development Corporation; two-thirds favoured pedestrianisation.
  • The Oxford Street Development Corporation was set up on 1 January 2026 to "lead the transformation of Oxford Street".
  • Transport for London was asked to develop proposals to pedestrianise the street.
Mission 6

High streets fight back

  • New uses: more cafés, restaurants, gyms and services; high-street jobs in accommodation and food rose by over 20% in every region (2012–2017).
  • Events and space: car-free streets and public space give people reasons to visit that online shopping cannot.
  • Government: the Hope on the High Street strategy (2026) aims at "reducing vacancy" and is backed by a £210 million Community Assets Package.
Mission check

?In a market town, two clothes shops close. In the same year a café, a gym and a parcel-collection point open in their place. What does this show?

Internet shopping grew from 3.4% to 27.5% of retail sales (2007–2025), and out-of-centre malls like Westfield Stratford City drew shoppers from older centres. High streets such as Oxford Street are responding by changing function: more leisure, food, services and public space.

Make a guess: In 1979 Shenzhen was what its government calls "a small backward border town". By 2024 it had 17.98 million residents. Where did they all come from?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 2.3.1 overview of global patterns of urbanisation
  • 2.3.1 distribution of global cities and how that pattern has changed over time
Mission 7

Urbanisation

  • Urbanisation: a rising share of a country's people living in towns and cities.
  • Urban growth: a rising number of urban people.
  • Cities grow in three ways:
    • natural increase: more births than deaths;
    • migration into the city;
    • reclassification: villages and towns grow big enough to be counted as part of a city.
Mission 7

The world since 1950

  • 1950: 20% of the world's 2.5 billion people lived in cities.
  • 2025: 45% of 8.2 billion lived in cities and 36% in towns.
  • Using each country's own definition of "urban", the world's urban share rose from 37.4% in 1974 to 57.6% in 2024.
Mission 7

Contrasting trends, 1974 to 2024

Urban share of the population, by income group:

  • High income: 69.2% → 80.1%
  • Upper-middle income: 28.8% → 67.8%
  • Lower-middle income: 22.9% → 41.6%
  • Low income: 18.6% → 34.7%
Mission 7

Different times, different speeds

  • High-income countries urbanised first, with industrialisation. On the UN's new city definition, their city populations passed their rural populations by 1957.
  • Low-income countries started later: on the UN definition they reached urban–rural parity only in 2017. (On national definitions, they are still mostly rural.)
  • Urban population growth in 2024:
    • low income 3.99% a year · lower-middle 2.27% · upper-middle 0.90% · high income 0.84%.
  • Fast growth comes from high birth rates and migration; slow growth where most people already live in cities.
Quick check
?From 1974 to 2024, the urban share rose by 10.9 percentage points in high-income countries but by 39.0 in upper-middle-income countries. Why the difference?
Mission 7

Why people move to cities

  • Push (rural): poverty, few jobs, mechanised farming, poor schools and clinics.
  • Pull (city): jobs, higher wages, education, health care, bright lights.
  • Natural increase: young people are the most likely to move to cities, so cities gain births as well as migrants.
Mission 7

Shenzhen

  • Established in March 1979; China's first special economic zone from August 1980.
  • 17.98 million permanent residents at the end of 2024.
  • Its government calls it "a young immigrant city": average age 32.5 at the seventh national census.
Mission 7

China: what drove city growth?

  • China's urban share (national definition): 20.6% in 1982 → 63.8% in 2020.
  • Of China's urban growth between the 2010 and 2020 censuses:
    • 56% rural-to-urban migration
    • 26% administrative changes (reclassification)
    • 18% natural increase.
  • Worldwide, natural increase and reclassification drive most city growth; migration matters most in Eastern and South-Eastern Asia and sub-Saharan Africa.
Quick check
?Use the figures earlier in this mission. What share of China's urban growth in 2010–2020 did not come from rural-to-urban migration?
Mission 7

Global cities

  • Global (world) cities "play an important role in the global economic system of finance and trade".
  • GaWC (Loughborough University) ranks cities by the offices of 175 leading business-service firms across 785 cities (2024).
  • London and New York are top (Alpha++) in both 2000 and 2024.
  • Changing pattern:
    • mainland China: 1 Alpha city in 2000 (Shanghai) → 4 in 2024 (Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Shenzhen);
    • Dubai: Beta (2000) → Alpha+ (2024);
    • Mumbai: Alpha− (2000) → Alpha (2024).
Quick check
?How did the distribution of top-level (Alpha) world cities change between 2000 and 2024?
Mission check

?Between 1975 and 2025, Ghana's total population grew 3.5 times, but its city population grew 13.5 times, from just under 2 million to 27 million. Where did most of the extra city dwellers come from?

When cities grow much faster than the whole country, people must be moving in from rural areas and towns. In China, 56% of urban growth in 2010–2020 came from rural-to-urban migration; Shenzhen calls itself "a young immigrant city".

Mission 8

Mission 8: Mumbai keeps drawing people in from across India. Who really gains from its growth?

Make a guess: Mumbai keeps drawing people in from across India. Who really gains from its growth?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 2.3.2 global city in an NIC set in its regional, national and global context
  • 2.3.2 growth: natural change and migration; push/pull; city-region links; international migration
  • 2.3.2 ways of life: social and cultural patterns; informal economy
  • 2.3.2 current challenges: reducing poverty and deprivation, providing housing
Mission 8

Where is Mumbai?

  • Site: on India's west coast, on the Arabian Sea; built on a group of former islands, with a sheltered natural harbour.
  • Situation: capital of Maharashtra state; India's main gateway to the Arabian Sea and the Gulf.
  • India is lower-middle income.
  • Mumbai is on Eduqas's list of global cities, in an NIC.
Mission 8

Why Mumbai matters

  • Global: a world city at the Alpha level, linked to firms worldwide (GaWC, 2024). In the 19th century it became "a global trading centre".
  • National: home of India's central bank; the Reserve Bank's central office "permanently moved to Mumbai in 1937".
  • Regional: the main centre for jobs, ports, trade and services in Maharashtra and the wider city-region.
  • Connectivity: international airport, container port, and a suburban rail network linking the city-region.
Mission 8

How big, and how fast?

  • UN, same boundary method each year:
    • 2000: 16.0 million · 2025: 20.2 million · 2050 (projected): 23.1 million.
    • 12th-largest city in the world in 2025; growth 0.94% a year (2000–25).
  • Census 2011, Greater Mumbai's municipal area: 12,442,373 people.
  • Different boundaries, different totals: always name the source.
  • It is the world's densest city of a million or more: 27,000 people per km².
Mission 8

Why it grows

  • Natural increase: births exceed deaths in a young population.
  • Rural–urban migration from across India:
    • Push: low farm incomes, few jobs, poor services in rural areas.
    • Pull: jobs, wages, schools, hospitals in the city.
  • Since the 1960s M/East ward "has given shelter to migrants from different parts of India".
Mission 8

A clue in the census

  • Greater Mumbai, 2011: 6,715,931 males and 5,726,442 females.
  • That is about 853 females per 1,000 males (our calculation).
  • Many migrants are young men who come alone to work, sending money home.
  • International links: Indians working abroad sent home US$150.7 billion in 2025 (all of India).
  • City-region: commuters travel in daily from satellite towns such as Navi Mumbai and Thane.
Quick check
?A young man leaves his village in Bihar because farm work pays too little, and takes a job on a Mumbai building site. Which factors are at work?
Mission 8

Opportunities

  • Economic: jobs in finance, trade, ports, film, manufacturing and services; urban industry stimulates growth across Maharashtra.
  • Social: more schools and hospitals than in most rural areas. In 2011, about 90% of Greater Mumbai's people aged 7+ could read and write (calculated from census).
  • Resources: piped water and electricity reach far more homes than in villages.
  • For migrants, wages sent home help rural families.
Mission 8

Ways of life

  • Culture and ethnicity: migrants from every region bring many languages, religions and festivals.
  • Housing: luxury towers, older tenements and slums side by side.
  • Leisure and consumption: Art Deco cinemas, open spaces such as the Oval Maidan, beaches and malls.
  • Informal economy: work without a contract, sick pay or tax records: street selling, recycling, small workshops. In lower-middle-income countries like India, 81.5% of jobs are informal.
Mission 8

Challenge: housing

  • Greater Mumbai, Census 2011 (municipal tables): 6,534,460 of 12,442,373 people lived in slums: 52.5% (our calculation).
  • By the census definitions used by Praja Foundation the figure is 5,206,473, or 41.84%.
  • Shares differ hugely by ward: D ward 10.0%; M/East 84.9% (our calculation).
  • Slums lack secure tenure, space, toilets and reliable water.
Quick check
?In MCGM's tables, M/East ward had 685,994 slum residents and Greater Mumbai had 6,534,460. What share of the city's slum residents lived in this one ward?
Mission 8

Challenge: services, jobs, health and waste

  • In M/East ward (studies cited by Praja Foundation, 2018):
    • Waste: the Deonar landfill "still receives around 4500 metric tons of garbage every day".
    • Health: infant mortality 66 per 1,000 births, against India's 41 at the time.
    • Jobs: "Every second male and six out of seven females are unemployed."
    • Water: M/East logged the city's highest number of water-supply complaints (2014–16).
Mission 8

Managing growth: housing and services

  • Upgrading slums: the UN calls for slums to be upgraded "by ensuring their access to basic services", "preventing displacement".
  • Bottom-up: in M/East, citizen-led action helped families get "legal water connections, electricity connections, cooking gas".
  • Top-down redevelopment can replace slums with flats, but may move people far from their work.
Mission check

?Which evidence best shows that the costs of Mumbai's growth fall unevenly?

Many migrants gain jobs and wages; the city gains as a national and global hub. But wards such as M/East carry the costs: slums, a landfill, high infant mortality and joblessness.

Mission 9

Mission 9: London produces more per person than any other UK region. So why can so many Londoners not afford a home?

Make a guess: London produces more per person than any other UK region. So why can so many Londoners not afford a home?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 2.3.2 global city in an HIC set in its regional, national and global context
  • 2.3.2 growth through natural change and migration, links to the city-region, international migration
  • 2.3.2 social and cultural patterns (ways of life)
  • 2.3.2 current challenges: reducing poverty and deprivation, providing housing
Mission 9

London: where and why it matters

  • The UK's capital, on the River Thames in south-east England. Population 8.80 million in 2021.
  • UK: the highest GDP per person of any UK region in 2024, £72,201: 2.4 times the North East's (our calculation).
  • World: one of only two Alpha++ world cities in the GaWC ranking, with New York; Heathrow is "Britain's aviation hub".
  • Region: the centre of a wider city-region of commuter towns in south-east England.
Mission 9

Site, situation and connectivity

  • Site: where the city was built. London grew where the Thames could be bridged and sea-going ships could reach.
  • Situation: its location relative to other places: south-east England, close to mainland Europe.
  • Connectivity: how well it is linked. Heathrow, the UK's hub airport; the Elizabeth line carries about 660,000 journeys a day.
Mission 9

Growth: natural change and migration

  • London shrank to 6.61 million in 1981, then grew to 8.80 million by 2021. 2011–2021: up 7.7% (our calculation).
  • International migration: in 2021, 40.6% of Londoners were born outside the UK.
  • National migration: young adults move in for work and study; many families move out. 47.4% of Tower Hamlets residents were aged 20–39, against 26.3% across England (Census 2021).
  • In the year to mid-2025, London's population fell 0.29%.
Mission 9

How migration changed London's character

  • Ethnicity: 53.8% of Londoners were White in 2021, against 81.0% across England; 20.7% were Asian and 13.5% Black.
  • Neighbourhoods differ: in Tower Hamlets, 44.4% of residents were Asian; in Richmond upon Thames, 80.5% were White.
  • Language: London had the lowest share of people with English as their main language: 78.4%.
  • Migration brings new food, faith buildings, festivals, music and businesses to the city.
Quick check
?London's population fell 0.29% in the year to mid-2025, even though it had more births than deaths and more people arriving from abroad than leaving. What must explain the fall?
Mission 9

Ways of life

  • Housing: 30.0% of London households rent privately, against 20.5% in England; 45.2% own their home, against 61.3%.
  • Culture and ethnicity: many communities, with neighbourhoods that differ sharply, e.g. Tower Hamlets and Richmond upon Thames.
  • Leisure: Queen Elizabeth Olympic Park, created for the 2012 Games, is 560 acres and free to visit every day.
  • Consumption: most of the waste London's councils collect is burned, not recycled.
Mission 9

Challenges: inequality and deprivation

  • Housing cost: an average London home cost 10.6 times average earnings in 2025, the highest of any region; the North East's ratio was 5.0.
  • In 19 of London's 33 boroughs, the ratio was above 12.
  • Deprivation is concentrated in "parts of east London".
  • Gaps between boroughs: in Tower Hamlets, 35.9% of households rent from a council or housing association; in Richmond upon Thames, 62.4% own their home.
Mission 9

Challenges: dereliction, brownfield and greenfield

  • Brownfield land has been built on before; greenfield land has not.
  • The Heygate estate (Southwark), completed in 1974 and housing about 3,000 people, was judged "structurally sound, but in need of complete refurbishment" and was demolished.
  • On its site, Elephant Park was approved in 2013 with 3,000 new homes; after renegotiation, the affordable share rose from 11% to 25%.
  • Building on brownfield land protects the green belt, but can push out the people who lived there.
Quick check
?London has the UK's highest GDP per person, yet many Londoners struggle. Which statistic best shows why?
Mission check

?A fast-growing city has high wages and many new jobs, but homes cost more than ten times average earnings and some old estates are being demolished. Which outcome is most likely?

London produces £72,201 per person, the UK's highest, but an average home costs 10.6 times average earnings. High output does not reach everyone. Estates like the Heygate were replaced by new homes, only a quarter of them affordable. Many Londoners rent privately (30%).

Mission 10

Mission 10: London and Mumbai are on different continents. What ties each of them so tightly to the rest of the world?

Make a guess: London and Mumbai are on different continents. What ties each of them so tightly to the rest of the world?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 2.3.3 concepts of global cities and globalisation
  • 2.3.3 how global cities connect via transport hubs (ports, airports), trade/tourism and media/communications
  • 2.3.3 how each of the two studied cities is connected to the rest of the world
Mission 10

Globalisation

  • Globalisation: places becoming more connected and dependent on each other.
  • Links of goods, money, people, ideas and media flow between countries.
  • Cheaper flights, container shipping and the internet speed it up.
Mission 10

Global cities

  • Global cities "play an important role in the global economic system of finance and trade".
  • They are nodes (meeting points) in a network of flows.
  • Researchers at Loughborough University (GaWC) rank cities by the offices of 175 leading business-service firms across 785 cities.
  • 2024: London is in the top level, Alpha++, with New York. Mumbai is Alpha, the third level.
Quick check
?What makes a city a global city?
Mission 10

Transport hubs

  • A transport hub is a place where many routes meet and people or goods change routes.
  • Airports link cities by passengers and air freight. Heathrow calls itself "Britain's aviation hub".
  • Ports handle container ships carrying traded goods.
  • Hubs attract firms, workers and tourists, which makes the city more connected still.
Mission 10

Trade and tourism

  • Global cities hold headquarters and regional offices of large companies, banks and stock exchanges.
  • Money and decisions flow out from them to other countries.
  • Tourism brings visitors and spending: overseas residents made about 40.8 million visits to the UK in 2025.
Mission 10

Media and communications

  • TV channels, film studios, news agencies and internet firms send media worldwide.
  • Undersea internet cables and data centres connect the city's offices to the world in a fraction of a second.
  • Media spreads the city's image, which attracts more tourists, students and investment.
Quick check
?A film made in Mumbai is streamed by viewers in 50 countries. Which type of connection is this?
Mission 10

How London connects

  • Transport: Heathrow, Gatwick and other airports; Eurostar trains to Paris and Brussels; container ports on the Thames estuary.
  • Trade and finance: the City of London and Canary Wharf hold banks, insurers and the London Stock Exchange.
  • Media: the BBC and many global news, film and advertising firms.
  • People: in 2021, 40.6% of London's residents were born outside the UK.
Mission 10

How Mumbai connects

  • Transport: Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj International Airport; Jawaharlal Nehru Port, a major container port across the harbour in Navi Mumbai.
  • Trade and finance: India's main financial centre: the Bombay Stock Exchange, the National Stock Exchange and the Reserve Bank of India.
  • Media: the Hindi film industry, known as Bollywood, with audiences across Asia, Africa, the Gulf and the diaspora.
  • Link back to the Mumbai and London missions: migrants also tie each city to the places they came from.
Quick check
?GaWC ranks London Alpha++ and Mumbai Alpha. What does that mainly tell you?
Mission check

?In 2000 GaWC placed Dubai at "Beta" level. By 2024 it was "Alpha+". Which change best explains its rise?

A city becomes "global" through its connections: transport hubs, trade and finance, tourism and media. London and Mumbai are both tied in by all four.

Exam move: for "How is [city] connected…?", name a connection of each type for your city, with a named place (e.g. Jawaharlal Nehru Port, Heathrow).

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • About 69.5 million people; densest in London (5,598 per km²), the Midlands, northern English cities and Scotland's Central Belt; emptiest in the uplands (Highland 9.2 per km²).

Mission 2

  • The urban-rural continuum runs from city to isolated farm; bigger places offer higher-order services with larger spheres of influence, so rural people travel further for shops and health care.
  • Counter-urbanisation (net 61,000 into rural council areas, 2022–23) grows commuter villages near cities; it brings longer car commutes, congestion and car dependence.

Mission 3

  • Growth area (South Cambridgeshire, +8.9%): counter-urbanisation and new towns such as Northstowe; more homes, schools and traffic. Decline area (Outer Hebrides, −5.5%): an ageing population, more deaths than births, fewer families.
  • Services close as spheres of influence widen and banking moves online; sustainable rural communities need community shops, banking hubs, broadband and affordable homes.

Mission 4

  • Growth since 2001: +17.5%; mostly net migration; natural change near zero.
  • Ageing: living longer + fewer children + baby boomers; effects on pensions, care and housing.
  • Internal migration out of London; housing need → 1.5 million homes target.

Mission 5

  • Brownfield = previously developed land; greenfield = never built on. Brownfield regenerates and protects countryside but can be costly; greenfield is easier but causes sprawl.
  • Test any scheme against the parts of a sustainable community (Egan's wheel).

Mission 6

  • Retail is changing for technological, economic and cultural reasons; online sales are now 27.5% of GB retail.
  • Out-of-town and internet shopping have costs and benefits; high streets are changing function (Oxford Street's development corporation, 2026).

Mission 7

  • The world's urban share rose from 37% to 58% in 50 years.
  • High-income countries urbanised early and are now growing slowly; low-income and lower-middle-income countries are urbanising fastest, through migration and natural increase.
  • Top world cities are spreading towards Asia and the Gulf.

Mission 8

  • Mumbai: west-coast port city, India's financial centre, an Alpha world city; 20.2 million (UN, 2025).
  • Growth: natural increase + rural–urban migration. Gains: jobs, services. Costs: slums (52.5% in MCGM's tables), waste, health, unemployment, unevenly spread.

Mission 9

  • London: the UK's capital and an Alpha++ world city; 8.80 million people (2021), 40.6% born abroad, with neighbourhoods that differ sharply.
  • Opportunities (jobs, culture, transport, parks) sit beside challenges (housing costs, deprivation in east London).

Mission 10

  • Globalisation connects places; global cities are the main nodes, ranked by GaWC (London Alpha++, Mumbai Alpha, 2024).
  • Connections: transport hubs (ports, airports), trade and tourism, media and communications.
