Rural-Urban Links

This mini-lesson covers Eduqas Theme 2 — Rural-Urban Links: the urban-rural continuum from remote countryside to city centre, how settlements are interdependent, counterurbanisation and pressure on the rural-urban fringe, population and urban change in the UK, regeneration, and the challenges of contrasting global cities in lower- and higher-income countries.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go (multiple-choice, short-answer and games) and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you're ready.

The continuum

The urban-rural continuum

There is no single dividing line between "town" and "country". Instead, settlements sit on a continuum — a sliding scale. Eduqas asks you to place places along it:

  • Remote rural — isolated hamlets and farms, few services, low population density.
  • Accessible rural — villages within commuting distance of a town.
  • Rural-urban fringe / suburbs — the edge of the built-up area where town meets country.
  • Inner city — older, densely built housing close to the centre.
  • City centre (CBD) — the Central Business District: shops, offices, tallest buildings.

Key idea: the continuum is a gradual change in population density, land use and services from open countryside to the heart of a city — not two separate categories.

Interdependence

Sphere of influence & the commuter belt

Rural and urban places are interdependent — they rely on each other. A town's sphere of influence is the area it serves and draws people from.

Key term — sphere of influence: the area served by a settlement. Larger settlements (with more services) have a larger sphere of influence.

Population movement

Three ways people move

Eduqas expects you to tell apart three processes that change where people live:

  • Urbanisation — a rising proportion of people living in towns and cities. This is happening fastest today in lower-income countries.
  • Suburbanisation — people (and shops/offices) moving out from the inner city to the suburbs on the edge of the built-up area.
  • Counterurbanisation — people moving out of cities altogether to live in rural villages and small towns, often while still commuting or working remotely.

Misconception: counterurbanisation is movement out of the city into the countryside — the opposite of urbanisation. Don't confuse it with suburbanisation, which stays within the wider built-up area (just moving to the edge).

The fringe

Pressure on the rural-urban fringe

The rural-urban fringe is the zone where the edge of the town meets the countryside. It faces strong pressure for development:

  • Greenfield development — building on land never previously built on; cheaper and easier, but destroys countryside and wildlife habitats.
  • Commuting increases traffic on rural roads, while some villages become dormitory settlements.
  • The Green Belt is protected open land around some cities designed to stop urban sprawl.

Your turn — why cities attract migrants

✎Explain two pull factors that draw people from the countryside into cities in lower-income countries. Use full sentences.
Model answer
  • More jobs — cities have more factories, construction and service work, so people move there hoping for regular, better-paid employment than farming.
  • Better services — cities usually offer better access to healthcare, schools and clean water/electricity than remote rural areas.
  • (Other valid pulls: the chance of a higher standard of living, entertainment, and joining family already in the city.)
UK population change

Population & urban change in the UK

The UK's population and its cities are changing for several reasons:

  • Internal migration — movement within the UK, e.g. young people moving to cities for university and work; families counterurbanising to the countryside.
  • International migration — people moving to the UK from abroad, often settling in larger cities where jobs and communities are found.
  • An ageing population — people are living longer and birth rates are lower, so the average age is rising. This raises demand for healthcare and pensions.
  • Urban decline — when industry closes, inner-city areas can suffer unemployment, empty buildings and out-migration.

Exam link: be able to explain causes (migration, changing economy) and effects (pressure on housing and services, an ageing population) of population change.

Regeneration

Regeneration: Cardiff Bay

When inner-city or old industrial areas decline, they can be regenerated — improved and given a new use. Cardiff Bay is a well-known example.

  • Before: Cardiff's old docklands had declined as the coal-export trade collapsed, leaving derelict, mudflat waterfront and unemployment.
  • The scheme: a barrage was built across the bay to create a freshwater lake, and the waterfront was redeveloped with housing, offices, shops, the Wales Millennium Centre and leisure attractions.
  • Brownfield land: this reused already-developed, run-down land rather than building on countryside.

Key contrast — brownfield vs greenfield: brownfield sites are previously-built-on land (cheaper for the environment, but often need clearing/decontaminating); greenfield sites are undeveloped countryside (easier to build on, but destroy open land).

Global pattern

Global urbanisation & megacities

Across the world the share of people living in cities is rising. A megacity is a city with over 10 million people, and most new megacities are in lower- and middle-income countries.

Rural-urban migration

Push and pull factors

Rapid urban growth in LICs/NICs is driven by rural-urban migration. People are pushed away from the countryside and pulled towards the city.

Push = negatives that make people leave the countryside. Pull = attractions that draw people to the city. Many pull factors are hopes that may not come true for everyone.

Contrasting city (LIC/NIC)

A city in an NIC: Mumbai, India

Mumbai is one of the world's largest cities and India's financial centre. Rapid rural-urban migration and natural increase mean it has grown faster than housing and services can keep up.

  • Informal settlements: many arrivals cannot afford or find formal housing, so they build homes in squatter settlements (slums). Dharavi is one of the largest informal settlements in Asia.
  • Challenges: overcrowding, limited clean water and sanitation, poor waste disposal and disease risk, and few secure jobs (much work is in the informal sector).
  • But also: Dharavi has a huge, busy informal economy — recycling, pottery and leather workshops employ many people.

Key term — informal settlement: unplanned housing, often self-built on land the residents do not own, usually lacking proper services. Also called squatter settlements or slums.

Solutions & the HIC contrast

Managing urban challenges

Both LIC/NIC and HIC cities face problems of housing, traffic, inequality and waste — but tackle them differently.

  • Slum upgrading: instead of demolishing informal areas, authorities improve them in place — laying on clean water, sanitation, electricity and secure land rights.
  • Site-and-service schemes: the city provides a plot with basic services (water, sewerage, a road) and residents build their own homes gradually.
  • Sustainable transport: HIC cities like London tackle congestion and pollution with a congestion charge, cycle hire and public transport; many cities invest in metro/bus rapid transit.

Exam tip: a strong 'contrasting cities' answer names the cities, describes a real challenge (e.g. informal settlements in Mumbai; congestion in London) and gives a matching solution (e.g. slum upgrading; congestion charging).

Model answer

  • Solution: slum upgrading or site-and-service schemes — the authorities provide clean water, sanitation, electricity and secure land, and help residents improve their homes rather than demolishing them.

Recap

The key ideas to know

Continuum: a sliding scale from remote rural → accessible rural → suburbs → inner city → city centre.

Interdependence: the sphere of influence & commuter belt link town and country.

Processes: urbanisation (into cities), suburbanisation (out to the edge), counterurbanisation (out to the countryside).

Rural-urban fringe: pressure from greenfield development, commuting; protected by the Green Belt.

UK change: internal & international migration, an ageing population, urban decline & regeneration.

Brownfield vs greenfield: Cardiff Bay reused brownfield docklands; greenfield builds on countryside.

Global cities: urbanisation fastest in LICs/NICs; megacities (10 m+) driven by rural-urban migration.

LIC/NIC challenges: informal settlements (e.g. Dharavi, Mumbai) — fixed by slum upgrading & site-and-service; HICs tackle congestion with sustainable transport.
