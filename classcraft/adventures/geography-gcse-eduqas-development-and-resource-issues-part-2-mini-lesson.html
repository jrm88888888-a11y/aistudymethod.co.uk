Mission 1: Producing 1 kg of beef takes about 15,400 litres of water, on a world average. Is beef draining the world's rivers?

Make a guess: Producing 1 kg of beef takes about 15,400 litres of water, on a world average. Is beef draining the world's rivers?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 6.3.1 past (last 50–100 years) and present global trends in water consumption
  • 6.3.1 concepts of water footprints and water security
  • 6.3.1 links between population growth, agricultural change, consumerism and rising water demand
Mission 1

The water footprint

  • The water footprint measures the fresh water used, or polluted, to make what people consume:
    • blue: water taken from rivers, lakes and groundwater
    • green: rainwater used by crops and pasture
    • grey: water needed to dilute pollution.
  • Per person, 1996–2005: world 1,385 m³ a year; United States 2,842; India 1,089; China 1,071.
Mission 1

Water hidden in food

  • Embedded (virtual) water: the water used to make a product.
  • World averages, litres per kg: beef 15,400; sheep meat 10,400; pig meat 6,000; chicken 4,300; pulses 4,000.
  • Agriculture makes up 92% of the world's water footprint.
Quick check
?World averages: beef 15,400, sheep meat 10,400 and chicken 4,300 litres of water per kg. How many times as much water does 1 kg of beef take as 1 kg of chicken? Give 1 decimal place.
Mission 1

Where beef's water comes from

  • Beef's footprint: 93.5% green, 3.6% blue, 2.9% grey.
  • For farm animals, 98% of the water footprint is in their feed.
  • So most of it is rain on pasture and feed crops, not water pumped from rivers.
  • But place matters: blue water from a stressed aquifer does more harm than rain on wet pasture.
Mission 1

Water use over 100 years

  • Global freshwater use has risen about six-fold since 1900.
  • It rose sharply from the 1950s; since 2000 it appears to be levelling off, or at least slowing.
  • Farming takes 72% of freshwater withdrawals.
Mission 1

Why demand has grown

  • Population growth: 3.06 billion people in 1961, 8.06 billion in 2023.
  • Agricultural change: more irrigation and more meat. In China, meat supply per person rose from 3.32 kg a year in 1961 to 44.00 kg in 2000.
  • Consumerism: goods carry hidden water. One pair of jeans: about 10,850 litres (global average).
  • More people × more meat × more goods = more water.
Mission 1

Water security

  • Water security: "the capacity of a population to safeguard sustainable access to adequate quantities of and acceptable quality water".
  • In 2024, about 2.1 billion people (one in four) lacked safely managed drinking water.
  • About 10% of the world's people live in countries with high or critical water stress.
Quick check
?What is the difference between a water footprint and water security?
Mission check

?A 250 g cotton T-shirt has a water footprint of: blue 1,230 litres, green 1,110 litres, grey 380 litres (global average). Compared with beef, how much of the T-shirt's water comes from rivers and aquifers?

Beef is not mainly draining rivers: 93.5% of its 15,400 litres per kg is green water (rain on pasture and feed), and only 3.6% is blue. Irrigated crops such as cotton draw far more on rivers and aquifers.

Mission 2: The Lower Mekong's fisheries land an estimated 2.3 million tonnes of fish a year, worth about US$11 billion. Its hydropower capacity could more than double. Can one river be both a great fishery and a giant power station?

Make a guess: The Lower Mekong's fisheries land an estimated 2.3 million tonnes of fish a year, worth about US$11 billion. Its hydropower capacity could more than double. Can one river be both a great fishery and a giant power station?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 6.3.2 how and why water supply is managed: reservoirs for supply/irrigation, transfer schemes, groundwater abstraction

Mission 2

Ways to increase water supply

  • Dams and reservoirs: store water from wet seasons for use in dry ones.
  • Water transfers: move water from an area of surplus to an area of shortage.
  • Diversion: send a river's water along a new course, e.g. into canals.
  • Desalination: remove salt from sea water.
  • Groundwater abstraction: pump water from aquifers.
  • Reservoirs also store water for irrigation: Egypt's High Aswan Dam releases water to meet Egypt's "agricultural, municipal, and industrial water requirements".
Mission 2

Dams and reservoirs: the Mekong

  • The Mekong flows nearly 5,000 km through six countries.
  • Lower Mekong: 88 hydropower projects already operate, with over 13,257 MW of capacity.
  • 20 more are being built and 14 planned. If all are built, capacity could reach 27,302 MW.
  • Benefits: electricity, income from selling power to neighbours, and flood and drought mitigation.
Quick check
?Use the capacity figures on the last screen. By what factor could the Lower Mekong's hydropower capacity grow?
Mission 2

Who gains, who loses?

  • "Dams trap sediment" that keeps the Mekong Delta fertile.
  • Dams block fish migration, which "threatens the food security and income of millions of people".
  • About 80% of the nearly 65 million people in the lower basin depend on the river.
  • Gains go mostly to power users and governments; losses fall mostly on fishers and delta farmers downstream.
Mission 2

Water transfer: Kielder, north-east England

  • Kielder Water, Northumberland, holds 200 billion litres; the scheme was finished in 1982.
  • It was planned for "a predicted rise in demand for water" from industry and homes.
  • Water is "released into the Rivers Tyne, Derwent, Wear and Tees" to be taken out downstream.
  • It also keeps up "minimum flow levels at times of low natural rainfall".
  • Costs: reservoirs flood land, and transfer schemes are costly to build and run.
Mission 2

Groundwater abstraction

  • Water is pumped from aquifers. In southern England, chalk aquifers supply public water; e.g. it is pumped in the headwaters of the River Lambourn, in the Berkshire Downs.
  • If water is pumped faster than rain refills the aquifer, levels fall and rivers fed by groundwater shrink.
Quick check
?Water stored in a reservoir in one river basin is released into rivers in other basins so that it can be used there. Which strategy is this?
Mission 2

Small scale: rainwater harvesting

  • Appropriate technology: simple, cheap kit that local people can build and repair, e.g. roof gutters feeding a storage tank, or small ponds and check dams.
  • India's "Catch the Rain" campaign (launched 22 March 2021) aims to "catch the rain, where it falls, when it falls", with "people's active participation".
  • Social: water close to home; less time spent fetching it.
  • Economic: low cost; local people build and maintain it.
  • Environmental: tops up groundwater; little damage. But it fails in long droughts and can only store small amounts.
Mission check

?A country plans a large dam on a river that flows on into a neighbouring country with big river fisheries and a farmed delta. What is the most likely outcome?

Lower Mekong capacity could grow about 2.06 times, but dams trap sediment and block fish migration in a fishery worth about US$11 billion a year. One river can do both only with trade-offs.

Make a guess: About 85% of the Nile's water starts in Ethiopia, yet a 1959 agreement shared it between Egypt and Sudan. Who decides how it is used now, and what would make a deal work?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 6.3.2 social, economic and environmental consequences of managing water at an international scale, where rivers cross national boundaries
Mission 3

Why shared rivers cause conflict

  • Many rivers cross national borders. Countries upstream control how much water flows on, and when.
  • Countries downstream depend on what arrives.
  • Pressure grows with population, irrigation, hydropower and drought.
  • Stakeholders: governments, organisations (basin groups, companies, researchers) and individuals (farmers, households).
Mission 3

The Nile basin

  • Flow at Aswan, Egypt, averaged 86.5 billion m³ a year (1900–2018).
  • The Blue Nile gives about 55% of it, the White Nile 32% and the Atbara 13%.
  • "85% of Nile waters originate in Ethiopia", yet "nearly all consumptive use occurs downstream in Egypt and Sudan".
Mission 3

The 1959 agreement

  • Signed by Egypt and Sudan in Cairo on 8 November 1959.
  • It shared an expected 84 billion m³ a year: 55.5 billion for Egypt, 18.5 billion for Sudan, and about 10 billion lost from storage.
  • "Not being signatories, Ethiopia and other upstream states do not recognize this agreement".
Quick check
?Use the 1959 figures. What percentage of the 84 billion m³ in the 1959 agreement went to Egypt?
Mission 3

Ethiopia's dam: the GERD

  • Ethiopia began the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam (GERD) on the Blue Nile in 2011.
  • Capacity 5,150 MW, expected to generate about 16 TWh of electricity a year.
  • Its reservoir can hold 74 billion m³.
  • Ethiopia's prime minister inaugurated it on 9 September 2025.
Mission 3

Governments' views

  • Ethiopia (upstream): needs electricity. Demand is projected to rise from 11.1 TWh (2020) to 27.3 TWh (2030).
  • Egypt (downstream): its foreign minister told the UN Security Council that Egypt is "entirely dependent on the Nile River for its survival".
  • Sudan (between them): smoother Blue Nile flows could end flood losses and add hydropower and irrigation water.
  • Each view depends on where the country sits in the basin.
Mission 3

Organisations' views

  • The Nile Basin Initiative (1999) links 10 basin countries.
  • Its Cooperative Framework Agreement (CFA) is based on "equitable and reasonable utilization" and "the obligation not to cause significant harm".
  • The CFA came into force on 13 October 2024. Egypt and Sudan rejected opening it for signature in 2010 and are not parties.
  • Researchers modelled the GERD: it should help Ethiopia and Sudan "without significantly affecting water users in Egypt", but "multi-year droughts will require careful coordination".
Quick check
?Why do Ethiopia and Egypt hold such different views on the GERD?
Mission 3

Consequences of managing a shared river

  • Economic: Ethiopia gains electricity to use and sell; Sudan could avoid flood losses.
  • Social: Egypt says its water per person is "approaching the absolute water scarcity threshold of 500 cubic metres"; that is Egypt's own statement.
  • Environmental: dams trap sediment; Egypt's High Aswan Dam has lost about 7 billion m³ of storage to sediment. Big reservoirs also lose water to evaporation.
  • Political: the CFA is in force, but without Egypt and Sudan.
Quick check
?Which is an environmental consequence of building large dams on a shared river?
Mission check

?On the Mekong, China has 12 dams on the upper river but is not one of the four member countries of the Mekong River Commission. What would most help the basin's countries share the river?

Ethiopia supplies about 85% of the Nile's water but was not part of the 1959 deal. A deal that works needs all the basin countries, with agreed rules for droughts.

Mission 4: Farms on the US High Plains pump water from underground. What happens when they take more than rain puts back?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 6.3.2 one location where groundwater over-abstraction is an issue, with alternative geographical futures
Mission 4

Water under your feet

  • An aquifer is permeable rock (or sand and gravel) that stores water and lets it flow.
  • The top of the saturated layer is the water table.
  • Recharge: rain and rivers soak down and refill the aquifer, often very slowly.
  • People reach the water with wells and pumps.
Mission 4

Over-abstraction

  • Over-abstraction: pumping water out faster than recharge puts it back.
  • The water table falls, so wells must be deepened and pumping costs more.
  • Springs, streams and wetlands fed by groundwater can dry up.
  • The ground can sink: since 1935, parts of the Las Vegas Valley, USA, have sunk about 1.8 m (6 feet) as pumping compacted the aquifer.
Quick check
?Which situation is over-abstraction?
Mission 4

The High Plains aquifer, USA

  • It covers about 453,000 km² (175,000 square miles) under parts of eight states, from South Dakota and Wyoming to Texas.
  • About 95% of the groundwater pumped from it in 2015 was for irrigation.
  • Large-scale irrigation began around 1950.
Mission 4

How much has gone?

  • From before about 1950 to 2019, water levels fell by an average of about 5 m (16.5 feet).
  • Recoverable water in storage fell by about 10%.
  • The fall is uneven: one well in Texas dropped about 81 m (265 feet), while one in Nebraska rose about 26 m (86 feet).
Quick check
?The average fall is 16.5 feet, but one Texas well fell 265 feet and one Nebraska well rose 86 feet. What is the best conclusion?
Mission 4

Why it matters

  • A University of Kansas report on Kansas Geological Survey research says the declines "will continue unless pumping is reduced".
  • Even if pumping stopped completely, refilling the aquifer in Kansas would take "hundreds of years".
  • Stakeholders: irrigating farmers, towns that drink the groundwater, food companies, state water agencies, and wildlife that depends on springs and streams.
Mission 4

Future 1: carry on as now

  • Pumping continues at today's rates.
  • Short term: harvests and farm incomes stay high.
  • Long term: where the water layer becomes too thin to pump, farms would have to stop irrigating and switch to rain-fed (dryland) crops, with lower yields.
Mission 4

Future 2: farmers cut pumping together

  • In Sheridan County, Kansas, local irrigators set up a voluntary Local Enhanced Management Area (LEMA) to cut pumping.
  • They cut pumping by 35% over four years.
  • The water-level fall slowed from about 2 feet a year to about 5 inches a year.
  • A survey scientist said this happened "without affecting the bottom line" of the farmers.
  • The survey estimates that levels could be steadied with 25–45% cuts in most areas.
Mission 4

Future 3: change what is grown, or bring water in

  • Switch crops: grow crops that need less water, or more dryland farming. Saves water; may earn less.
  • Use water more efficiently: better sprinklers, soil-moisture sensors. Saves water if the savings are not used to irrigate more land.
  • Bring water in: pipelines from rivers elsewhere. Costly, and the source region may object.
Quick check
?Which future best protects the aquifer while keeping farms in business?
Mission check

?In a dry valley, a town pumps 15 million m³ a year from an aquifer that rain refills by 10 million m³ a year. What happens, and what would stop it?

When pumping is greater than recharge, the store shrinks and the water table falls: on the High Plains, by 16.5 feet on average since about 1950. Agreed cuts in pumping, as in Sheridan County, can slow the fall.

Mission 5

What is development?

  • Development: improving people's lives: income, health, education and choice.
  • Economic measures: GNI per capita, the country's income divided by its population (US$ a year).
  • Social measures: life expectancy, years of schooling, infant mortality.
  • The Human Development Index (HDI) combines health, education and income in one number.
Mission 5

Income groups

  • The World Bank sorts countries by GNI per capita. For 2026–27:
    • low income: $1,175 or less
    • lower-middle income: $1,176–4,635
    • upper-middle income: $4,636–14,375
    • high income: more than $14,375
  • 25 low, 47 lower-middle, 59 upper-middle and 87 high-income economies.
  • GNI per capita is a national average: the country's income divided by its population. One label covers the whole country, so its regions can be far richer or poorer than the label suggests.
Mission 5

The global pattern

  • Low income: 21 of the 25 are in sub-Saharan Africa (e.g. Niger, Rwanda).
  • High income: Europe and Central Asia has the most (40); others are in the Americas, East Asia and the Pacific, and the Gulf (e.g. the UK, Japan, Qatar).
  • Middle income covers most of Asia and Latin America: India is lower-middle, China upper-middle.
  • Pattern: a rich "North" and poorer "South", with many exceptions (Australia, Singapore).
Quick check
?A country's GNI per capita is $3,200. Which World Bank group is it in for 2026–27?
Quick check
?Which statement best describes the global pattern in 2026–27?
Mission 5

Uneven within countries: core and periphery

  • A national average hides big differences between regions.
  • The core: the richest region, with most jobs, investment, transport links and power.
  • The periphery: poorer, remote regions with fewer jobs and services.
  • Young workers and money flow from periphery to core, which can widen the gap.
Mission 5

Uneven within India

  • India is lower-middle income, but its states differ hugely.
  • Share of people in multidimensional poverty, 2019–21:
    • Bihar 33.76% (highest)
    • Maharashtra (home of Mumbai) 7.81%
    • Kerala 0.55% (lowest)
    • India 14.96%.
  • India is the NIC in your country study; this is its regional pattern.
Mission 5

Why development is uneven

  • Within India:
    • Economic: industry, services and ports cluster in core states such as Maharashtra and Gujarat; investment follows.
    • Social: schooling and health care are stronger in some states (Kerala) than others.
    • Historic: early colonial ports and industry, such as Mumbai's, gave Maharashtra a head start.
    • Political: state governments differ in how well they deliver services.
    • Cultural: e.g. attitudes to girls' schooling and marriage age vary between regions.
    • Environmental: Bihar lies on the flood-prone Ganges plain and is landlocked.
Mission 5

What uneven development does to welfare

  • Poverty: 135 million Indians left multidimensional poverty (2015–16 to 2019–21), but the poorest states still lag.
  • Housing and infrastructure: 41.37% of Indians lived in homes deprived in housing quality, and 30.13% lacked adequate sanitation (2019–21).
  • Jobs: fewer formal jobs in poorer states push young adults to migrate to core cities such as Mumbai and Delhi.
  • Economic consequence: core cities grow fast; periphery states lose workers.
Quick check
?Which is a social consequence of India's regional inequality?
Mission check

?Country X is upper-middle income. Its capital region is far richer than its rural north. What does its income label tell you?

A label is an average. Inside one country, core regions can be far richer than the periphery. In India, core Maharashtra is far richer than peripheral Bihar.

Mission 6: A baby boy in Hart, Hampshire can expect to live ten years longer than one in Blackpool. Why?

Mission 6

What is the north–south divide?

  • The north–south divide is the gap in wealth, jobs and health between the south of England (especially London and the South East) and the north (and much of Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland).
  • GDP per head in 2024: London £72,201; South East £43,178; UK £41,627; North East £29,957.
  • London's figure is 2.4 times the North East's (our calculation).
Mission 6

The divide in health and deprivation

  • The 10 local areas with the highest life expectancy were all in the south of England; the 10 lowest were concentrated in the north of England and Scotland (2022–2024).
  • Male life expectancy at birth was highest in Hart, Hampshire (83.7 years), and lowest in Blackpool (73.7 years): a gap of 10 years.
  • Male life expectancy was 3.0 years higher in the South East than in the North East.
  • Deprivation is concentrated in big conurbations, former heavy-industry and mining areas, coastal towns and parts of east London.
  • So the divide is a broad pattern, not a neat line: rich and poor places exist in both north and south.
Quick check
?Which statement about the north–south divide is the most accurate?
Mission 6

Economic causes

  • Deindustrialisation: coal, steel, shipbuilding and textiles were concentrated in the north, Wales and Scotland; manufacturing jobs "declined drastically from the 1960s onwards".
  • Closures hit whole towns: after the Redcar steelworks closed in 2015, 2,150 workers and supply-chain staff claimed benefits within a year.
  • Growth sectors (finance, business services, tech) cluster in London and the south-east: London alone produced 22.8% of UK GDP in 2024 (our calculation).
Mission 6

Political and social causes

  • Political: where government invests matters. HS2's chairman wrote in 2014 of "historic underinvestment in infrastructure and our economic over-reliance on London".
  • Political: London is the seat of government and of most national institutions and company headquarters.
  • Social: poorer health and lower life expectancy reduce people's ability to work.
  • Social: young, qualified people often move to where the jobs are, which widens the gap.
Mission 6

Consequences

  • Economic: the gap is widening: London's GDP per head was 2.2 times the North East's in 2001 and 2.4 times in 2024 (our calculation).
  • Economic: homes in London cost 10.6 times average earnings; in the North East 5.0 times. The south overheats; the north has cheaper homes but fewer well-paid jobs.
  • Social: people in the poorest areas live shorter lives (Blackpool v. Hart: 10 years for males).
  • Social: pressure on housing, roads and water in the crowded south-east.
Quick check
?Which of these is a cause, not a consequence, of regional inequality in the UK?
Mission check

?A northern city gets an Enterprise Zone and a faster rail link to Leeds. Ten years later, which result would best show the north–south divide is narrowing?

The north–south divide shows in wealth (£72,201 v. £29,957 GDP per head), health (10 years of life expectancy) and housing. Its causes are economic (deindustrialisation), political (where investment goes) and social, and the gap has widened since 2001.

Mission 7

Why invest in a deprived region?

  • A deprived region has fewer jobs, lower pay and weaker services than the national average.
  • Investment = money spent on new factories, offices, roads or railways.
  • Investment creates jobs. Workers spend their wages locally. That spending supports more jobs.
Mission 7

The positive multiplier

  • Positive multiplier: one investment sets off a chain of further growth.
  • New firm → jobs → wages spent in local shops → suppliers get orders → more jobs → more taxes → better services → more firms attracted.
  • The final gain is bigger than the first investment.
Mission 7

Sunderland: Nissan

  • Nissan's Sunderland plant opened in 1986 and is Britain's largest car factory.
  • It supports a UK workforce of about 7,000, plus about 30,000 staff in the wider supply chain.
  • In 2023 Nissan announced up to £2 billion of new investment for electric vehicles there.
Quick check
?Which job is part of Nissan's positive multiplier, not a job at Nissan itself?
Mission 7

The negative multiplier

  • Negative multiplier: a closure sets off a downward spiral.
  • Plant closes → jobs lost → less spending → local shops and suppliers lose trade → more job losses → less tax → services cut → young people leave.
  • The final loss is bigger than the first closure.
Mission 7

Redcar, 2015

  • The SSI steelworks at Redcar, Teesside, went into liquidation on 2 October 2015.
  • By 31 August 2016, 1,550 former SSI employees had claimed benefits.
  • So had 610 people who said they worked in SSI's supply chain.
  • The government called the closure "a huge blow to local people".
Quick check
?In total 2,150 people claimed benefits after the Redcar closure; 610 of them were supply-chain workers. About what share were supply-chain workers?
Mission 7

Stopping the spiral

  • 2015: a government package worth up to £80 million to retrain workers and help local firms grow.
  • Lord Heseltine was appointed to attract foreign investment to the Tees Valley.
  • A devolution deal gave the area £15 million a year for 30 years and an elected mayor from 2017.
  • Aim: start a new positive multiplier before the negative one runs on.
Mission 7

National policy: big infrastructure

  • Governments also try to narrow the gap with major infrastructure: roads, railways, broadband.
  • HS2's own 2014 report spoke of the UK's "economic over-reliance on London" and aimed at "rebalancing Britain".
  • The idea: faster links let northern and Midlands cities attract firms and workers.
Mission 7

HS2: what happened

  • HS2 was planned from London to Birmingham, then on to Manchester and Leeds.
  • The legs north of Birmingham, to Manchester and Leeds, were cancelled.
  • In 2026 the expected cost was £87.7–£102.7 billion (2025 prices).
  • First trains (Old Oak Common to Birmingham) are now expected between May 2036 and October 2039.
Quick check
?Why can HS2 not yet have narrowed the gap between regions through faster travel?
Mission 7

Other national policies

  • Northern Powerhouse Rail: in January 2026 the government pledged up to a further £45 billion for faster rail between Liverpool, Manchester, Bradford, Leeds, Sheffield and York.
  • Investment Zones: e.g. North East England, with £160 million of support including tax incentives, expected to create 4,000+ jobs in five years.
  • Devolution: elected mayors and local funds, as in the Tees Valley.
  • Exam move: to evaluate, weigh benefits (jobs, links) against limits (cost, delay, benefits may flow to the bigger city), then judge.
Mission check

?A government builds a new motorway to a deprived coastal region. Ten years later, which result would best show it reduced regional inequality?

Investment helps a poorer region catch up only if it starts a positive multiplier that lasts: local jobs, suppliers and spending. Nissan's plant supports about 30,000 supply-chain jobs; Redcar's closure hit hundreds of supply-chain workers too. HS2's trains are not expected until 2036–39.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Water footprint: blue (rivers, aquifers), green (rain), grey (dilution). Beef is mostly green water.
  • Water use rose about six-fold since 1900, driven by population, farming change and consumerism; water security is reliable access to enough clean water.

Mission 2

  • Dams and reservoirs store water and make power, but trap sediment and block fish.
  • Transfers (Kielder) move water to areas of shortage; groundwater is pumped from aquifers; rainwater harvesting is cheap, small-scale appropriate technology.

Mission 3

  • Upstream and downstream governments see the same river differently.
  • Managing a shared river has economic, social, environmental and political consequences.

Mission 4

  • Over-abstraction means pumping faster than recharge; the water table falls, wells deepen and land can sink.
  • The High Plains aquifer has three possible futures: carry on, cut pumping together, or change crops and supply. Each has winners and losers.

Mission 5

  • Global pattern: low income mostly in sub-Saharan Africa; high income mostly in Europe, the Americas, East Asia and the Gulf.
  • Within countries: a national label is an average that hides richer and poorer regions. Core regions pull ahead of the periphery. India: Bihar 33.76% vs Kerala 0.55% in poverty (2019–21).

Mission 6

  • The divide: the south (especially London) is richer and healthier on average, with exceptions on both sides.
  • Causes: deindustrialisation, investment patterns, health and migration of young workers. Consequences: widening GDP gap, housing pressure in the south, shorter lives in poorer areas.

Mission 7

  • Positive multiplier: investment → jobs → spending → more jobs (Nissan, Sunderland). Negative multiplier: closure → job losses spread to suppliers and shops (SSI, Redcar, 2015).
  • National policies include major infrastructure (HS2, Northern Powerhouse Rail), Investment Zones and devolution; their success depends on starting lasting local growth.
