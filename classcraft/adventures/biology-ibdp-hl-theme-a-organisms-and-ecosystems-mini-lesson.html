Mission 1: Cuttings from one plant share every gene. So why don't they all grow to the same height?

Make a guess: Cuttings from one plant share every gene. So why don't they all grow to the same height?

Mission 1

Genes plus surroundings

  • Genotype: the alleles an organism has.
  • Phenotype: its observable traits.
  • Phenotype = genotype interacting with the environment.
  • Same genotype, different surroundings → different phenotypes. That's the cuttings.
  • Due to genotype only: ABO blood group. Due to environment only: a scar, the language you speak. Interaction: height, skin colour.
Quick check
?Identical twins have the same genotype. Two identical twins were raised apart, one in a sunny country on a good diet and the other in a cloudy country on a poor diet. Which of these traits are expected to be the same in both twins as adults: ABO blood group, adult height and skin colour?
Mission 1

Where variation comes from

  • No two individuals are identical in all their traits. Variation is a defining feature of life.
  • Patterns of variation are the basis for naming and classifying organisms.
Mission 1

One genotype, many phenotypes

  • Phenotypic plasticity: one genotype can produce different phenotypes in different environments.
  • It works by changing gene expression, not the genotype.
  • The base sequences stay as inherited. Many such changes can reverse in the organism's lifetime; some are fixed during development.
  • Example: skin darkens in strong sunlight (more melanin made) and fades in winter.
  • Say: "the environment changed gene expression" — not: "the environment changed the genes".
Quick check
?A person's skin darkens after several weeks in strong sunlight and returns to its previous colour over the winter. How is this change best explained?

  • Skin colour: several genes add melanin, and sunlight adds more. A continuous variable.

Mission 1

Many genes, one smooth curve

  • Polygenic inheritance: many genes, at different loci, affect one trait.
  • Worked example: three genes (A/a, B/b, C/c). Each capital allele adds one unit of height.
  • Cross AaBbCc × AaBbCc. Offspring carry 0–6 capital alleles.
  • Step 1: one gene alone → 3 classes, 1 : 2 : 1.
  • Step 2: two genes → 5 classes, 1 : 4 : 6 : 4 : 1.
  • Step 3: three genes → 7 classes, 1 : 6 : 15 : 20 : 15 : 6 : 1 (out of 64).
  • Step 4: add the environment. Each class spreads out; the gaps fill in → a smooth bell curve.
  • More genes → more classes → closer to continuous.
Quick check
?Human skin colour varies continuously across a population, whereas ABO blood group falls into four separate classes. Which explanation of this difference is correct?
Mission 1

Read the data: summarise heights

Heights of 11 students, cm (illustrative data): 152, 161, 161, 161, 163, 165, 166, 170, 170, 174, 189

  • Mean = 1832 ÷ 11 = 166.5 cm.
  • Median (middle, 6th value) = 165 cm.
  • Mode (most frequent) = 161 cm.
  • Quartiles: Q1 = 161 cm (median of lower half), Q3 = 170 cm (median of upper half).
  • IQR = Q3 − Q1 = 9 cm. 1.5 × IQR = 13.5 cm.
  • Outlier if above Q3 + 13.5 = 183.5 cm, or below Q1 − 13.5 = 147.5 cm. So 189 cm is an outlier.
  • A box-and-whisker plot shows six things: minimum, Q1, median, Q3, maximum, outliers.
  • Whiskers stop at the furthest values that are not outliers: 152 cm and 174 cm.
  • The one tall student pulls the mean above the median.
Quick check
?For a class's heights, the first quartile is 150 cm, the median is 158 cm, the third quartile is 166 cm, and the minimum and maximum are 130 cm and 192 cm. Above what height would a data point be categorised as an outlier on a box-and-whisker plot?
Mission 1

Twin studies

  • Monozygotic (identical) twins come from one zygote: same genotype.
  • So differences between them arise mainly from the environment.
  • Real data: in 40 pairs of identical twins, epigenetic tags (DNA methylation) were almost identical in young twins and differed more in older twins.
  • Differences were largest in twins who had lived apart longest (Fraga and others, 2005).
  • Conclusion: the environment changes gene expression without changing the genes.
Quick check
?In a study, the DNA methylation patterns of pairs of monozygotic twins were compared. Three-year-old twins had almost identical patterns. Fifty-year-old twins showed large differences, and the differences were greatest in pairs who had spent the most years living apart. What is the best interpretation?
Mission check

?Two true-breeding varieties of a crop plant differ in seed mass. Crossing them gives F1 plants with seeds of intermediate mass, all very similar. The F1 are interbred. Grown in the same field as the F1, the F2 show a wide, bell-shaped spread of seed mass, with very few at either parental extreme. Which explanation fits best?

Many genes plus the environment: that is why genetically identical cuttings still differ, and why a whole population varies even more.

Mission 2

What counts as a species?

  • Morphological concept (Linnaeus): a species is a group of organisms that share a set of traits.
  • Biological species concept: a group of organisms that can interbreed and produce fertile offspring.
  • Horse × donkey → mule. Offspring, yes; fertile, no. So horse and donkey are two species.
  • Say: "can produce fertile offspring" — not: "can produce offspring".
Quick check
?According to the biological species concept, what is a species?
Mission 2

Where the definition struggles

  • Often it cannot be tested:
    • fossils cannot be crossed;
    • populations that never meet cannot show it in the wild.
  • It does not fit organisms that reproduce asexually.
  • Some pairs of species still produce some fertile hybrids (common in plants).
  • So competing species concepts exist: shared traits, DNA, ancestry. None fits every case.
Mission 2

Speciation means splitting

  • Speciation: one species splits into two or more.
  • It is the only way new species have appeared. Speciation adds species; extinction removes them.
  • The key step: populations become reproductively isolated. Gene flow between them stops.
  • Their gene pools then accumulate differences, until members can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring.
  • Usually gradual. Part-way through, "one species or two?" can be an arbitrary call.
  • A lineage that changes over time without splitting has not speciated.
Mission 2

Allopatric speciation, step by step

  • (1) One population. Gene flow keeps its gene pool mixed.
  • (2) A geographical barrier (river, sea, mountains) splits it. Gene flow stops. This is allopatric speciation.
  • (3) Each gene pool now changes on its own: new mutations, different selection pressures, and genetic drift in small populations. Allele frequencies diverge.
  • (4) In time, the two can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring: two species.
  • Named example: the Congo River separates bonobos (south) from common chimpanzees (north). Neither swims. Differential selection on each side drove divergence.
  • Say: "isolation stopped gene flow; selection changed allele frequencies" — not: "the river caused mutations" or "they changed in order to adapt".

Mission 2

What keeps species apart

  • Isolating mechanisms stop two gene pools mixing.
  • Before fertilisation (pre-zygotic):
    • geographical: they live in different places;
    • seasonal (temporal): they breed at different times;
    • behavioural: courtship signals differ;
    • morphological (mechanical): reproductive parts or flowers do not fit;
    • gametic: sperm cannot fertilise the other species' eggs.
  • After fertilisation (post-zygotic):
    • the hybrid dies before it can breed;
    • hybrid sterility: the hybrid lives but cannot breed. Mule: 32 horse + 31 donkey chromosomes = 63; the unmatched sets cannot pair properly in meiosis.
Mission 2

Courtship: the right reply or no mating

  • Courtship behaviour is a necessary step before successful mating.
  • It lets an animal:
    • recognise its own species;
    • recognise a mate of the opposite sex that is ready to breed;
    • synchronise mating, so gametes meet;
    • choose a strong, healthy mate.
  • Signals are species-specific: songs, displays, flashes, scents.
  • Example: male fireflies of each Photinus species flash their own pattern; females answer only their own.
  • Wrong signal, no reply, no mating: behavioural isolation.
  • Courtship often prevents hybridisation between animal species.
Quick check
?Two closely related bird species live in the same forest. The males of each species sing a different song, and females respond only to the song of their own species. What is the significance of this for the two species?
Mission check

?A population of lizards was split when rising sea level cut off an island from the mainland. Many generations later, island and mainland lizards can no longer produce fertile offspring together. Which statement about this speciation is correct?

The barrier only stops gene flow. Mutation, selection and drift do the rest, over many generations.

Mission 3

Too many to know one by one

  • About 1.2 million species are catalogued; an estimated 8.7 million species of eukaryotes exist.
  • No two individuals are identical in every trait. The patterns of variation are the basis for naming and classifying.
  • Classification: sorting organisms into groups.
  • Why classify?
    • identify an organism and name it without confusion;
    • predict features of a group's members;
    • show evolutionary relationships;
    • make wider study possible (ecology, medicine, conservation).
Mission 3

The species: the unit everything rests on

  • Every classification is built on the species.
  • Linnaeus grouped species by shared traits (morphological concept).
Mission 3

Groups within groups

  • Each group is a taxon (plural taxa).
  • Traditional hierarchy: kingdom → phylum → class → order → family → genus → species (domains, added above kingdoms, come later).
  • Smaller groups sit inside larger ones, with no overlap.
  • Lower taxa: fewer species, more features shared.
  • Humans: Eukarya · Animalia · Chordata · Mammalia · Primates · Hominidae · Homo · Homo sapiens.
Quick check
?The red fox, Vulpes vulpes, and the grey wolf, Canis lupus, are both placed in the family Canidae. Which statement must be true?
Mission 3

Two-part names

  • Binomial system (Linnaeus): every species has a two-part name.
  • 1st word: genus, with a capital letter.
  • 2nd word: species, all lower case. It means nothing on its own.
  • Italics in print; underline when handwritten.
  • Same genus = similar traits, usually close relatives: Panthera leo (lion), Panthera tigris (tiger).
  • After first use, shorten the genus: P. leo.
  • Say: "Homo sapiens" — not: "Homo Sapiens", and not "sapiens" alone.
Quick check
?Which statement about the scientific name of the tiger, Panthera tigris, is correct?
Mission 3

Classify by ancestry

  • Phylogenetic classification: groups reflect evolutionary origins and relationships.
  • Ideal: every member of a group descends from one common ancestor.
  • Advantage: features of a group can be predicted. A newly found member probably shares them.
  • Evidence: anatomy, plus DNA and protein sequences.
  • Classification is tentative: it changes as new evidence arrives.
  • Ranks (family, order …) are arbitrary levels laid on continuous variation, and do not always match evolution.
  • Example: traditional class Reptilia leaves out birds, although birds share a more recent ancestor with crocodiles than lizards do.
  • Cladistics uses unranked clades instead: a paradigm shift in classification.
Quick check
?What is the main advantage of a classification in which every group corresponds to evolutionary relationships?
Mission 3

Build a dichotomous key

  • A dichotomous key: numbered steps, each with two contrasting statements.
  • Each choice leads to another step or to a name.
  • Use fixed traits every individual shows: leaf arrangement, number of legs, wing veins.
  • Avoid size, age, colour that changes with season, and where it was found.
  • Skill: build a key for local plants or animals; then test it on new specimens.
Quick check
?A student is constructing a dichotomous key to identify five species of tree growing in a local park. Which first step is the best design?
Mission check

?The raven is Corvus corax, the rook is Corvus frugilegus and the magpie is Pica pica. What can be concluded from these names alone?

Two words: the genus places it among its closest relatives; the species word picks it out from them.

Mission 4

Clues to who is related

  • Relatives share features inherited from a common ancestor.
  • Evidence:
    • anatomy: homologous structures, in living species and fossils;
    • DNA and mRNA base sequences;
    • amino acid sequences of proteins.
  • After two lineages split, mutations build up separately in each.
  • So: fewer differences → more recent common ancestor → closer relatives.
  • Sequences are the most objective evidence for placing organisms in a clade: differences are counted, not judged.
  • Say: "share a more recent common ancestor" — not: "one evolved from the other".

Mission 4

Reading a cladogram

  • A cladogram shows the order in which lineages branched.
  • Root: the base; the common ancestor of every group shown.
  • Node: a branch point; a hypothetical common ancestor, where one lineage split in two.
  • Terminal branch: ends at one group.
  • Clade: an ancestor and all of its descendants.
  • How related? Find the most recent node they share. Not which tips sit side by side.
  • No living group at a tip is the ancestor of another.
Quick check
?On a cladogram, the root leads to node 1, where species P branches off. Node 2 separates species Q from the remaining species. Node 3 separates species R from species S and T, which are joined at node 4. Which statement is correct?
Mission 4

Worked example: fewest changes wins

  • Same 5 bases of one gene, four species (illustrative):
    • J: A C G T A · K: A C G T T · L: G T G C T · M: G T A C T
  • Step 1: draw each possible pairing.
  • Step 2: count the fewest base changes each tree needs.
    • J with K, L with M: 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 5.
    • J with L, K with M: 8. J with M, K with L: 8.
  • Step 3: choose the tree needing fewest changes: parsimony. J + K and L + M is most probable.
  • Different genes or criteria can give different trees. Each is a hypothesis.
Quick check
?Six bases of the same gene were sequenced in four species: W is ACTGCG, X is ACTATG, Y is GTAGCG and Z is GTAATG. Using parsimony analysis, which cladogram is the most probable?
Mission 4

Adding time: the molecular clock

  • Sequence differences build up at a roughly steady rate: a molecular clock.
  • Calibrate the rate with fossils of known age; then estimate when two clades diverged.
  • Worked example (illustrative):
    • A protein differs at 12 positions between two species.
    • Rate: about 1 change per 4 million years in each lineage.
    • Both lineages changed: 12 ÷ 2 = 6 changes each.
    • 6 × 4 = about 24 million years since they split.
  • Only an estimate: rates vary with generation time, population size and selection pressure.
Quick check
?The same protein in two living species differs at 8 amino acid positions. Substitutions in this protein occur at about 1 per 5 million years in each lineage. Which is the best estimate of the time since the two species diverged from their common ancestor?
Mission 4

Every tree is a hypothesis

  • Trees and cladograms are testable hypotheses. New evidence can revise them.
  • Anatomy can mislead: convergent evolution gives similar traits to unrelated groups.
  • The figwort family (Scrophulariaceae) was built on similar flowers. DNA showed some genera (e.g. snapdragons, foxgloves) belong with other families. They were moved: the old grouping was falsified.

Mission 5

One chemistry, many cell plans

  • Every living thing contains the same four classes of biochemicals: carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, nucleic acids.
  • All store genetic information in DNA and use ATP.
  • The big differences are in cell structure and in how organisms get energy and carbon.
  • These differences sort life into its largest groups.
Mission 5

Three domains

  • 1977: Carl Woese and George Fox compared ribosomal RNA (rRNA) base sequences from many organisms.
  • Prokaryotes fell into two groups, as different from each other as either is from eukaryotes.
  • So life has three domains, a level above kingdoms: Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya.
  • You and a mushroom: both Eukarya, one domain.
  • Archaea look like bacteria: no nucleus. But their key molecules differ, and some are closer to ours.
  • Say: "archaea are a separate domain" — not: "archaea are unusual bacteria".
Quick check
?Which evidence was used in 1977 to propose that all organisms should be classified into three domains?
Mission 5

Archaea: many ways to power a cell

  • Archaea are one of the three domains of life.
  • Metabolically very diverse. Energy for making ATP comes from:
    • light;
    • oxidising inorganic chemicals;
    • oxidising carbon compounds.
  • Some live in hot springs and salt lakes, but many live in ordinary soils and oceans.
  • Say: "archaea are a diverse domain" — not: "archaea are all extremophiles".
Quick check
?Three species of archaea were studied. Species 1 obtains energy by oxidising ammonia. Species 2 obtains energy by oxidising carbon compounds absorbed from decaying matter. Species 3 obtains energy from light. What do these observations illustrate?
Mission check

?A single-celled organism is found in ordinary garden soil. It has no nucleus. Its key molecules, including its ribosomal RNA, match those of known archaea, not bacteria. How should it be classified?

Looks put it with the bacteria; its molecules put it in a domain of its own.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Phenotype = genotype interacting with the environment; genetically identical organisms still differ.
  • Continuous variation comes from many genes with additive effects plus the environment; discontinuous variation from one or a few genes.
  • Phenotypic plasticity: the environment changes gene expression, not the genotype; many such changes can reverse, and none alters the alleles passed on.

Mission 2

  • A species can interbreed to produce fertile offspring; speciation is one species splitting into two once gene flow between populations stops and their gene pools diverge.
  • Isolating mechanisms (geographical, seasonal, behavioural, morphological, hybrid sterility) keep species' gene pools apart.

Mission 3

  • A binomial names the genus (capital) and the species (lower case); together they name one species, used worldwide.
  • Taxa are nested with no overlap, from domain (or kingdom) down to species, and the ideal grouping follows common ancestry.

Mission 4

  • Differences in DNA, mRNA and protein sequences build up after lineages split, so fewer differences mean a more recent common ancestor.
  • On a cladogram, relatedness is the most recent shared node; every tree is a hypothesis open to new evidence.

Mission 5

  • Archaea look like bacteria but are a separate domain of life, and are metabolically very diverse.
