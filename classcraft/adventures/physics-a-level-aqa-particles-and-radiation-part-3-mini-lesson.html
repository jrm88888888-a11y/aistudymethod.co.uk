Mission 1

Mission 1: Charge and energy would both balance if a proton turned into a positron and light. So what forbids it?

Make a guess: Charge and energy would both balance if a proton turned into a positron and light. So what forbids it?

Mission 1

The bookkeeping rules

  • Some quantities are the same before and after every interaction and decay.
  • Charge (in units of e): always conserved.
  • Energy (including rest energy) and momentum: always conserved.
  • Also baryon number and lepton number.
  • And strangeness, but only in strong interactions.
  • Check each quantity separately: add up the left side, add up the right side.
Mission 1

Charge on quarks

  • Quark charges: u +⅔e, d −⅓e, s −⅓e. Antiquarks: opposite sign.
  • β⁻ decay, one quark changes: d → u + e⁻ + ν̄ₑ
  • Charge: −⅓ = +⅔ + (−1) + 0 ✓
  • β⁺ decay: u → d + e⁺ + νₑ
  • Charge: +⅔ = −⅓ + (+1) + 0 ✓
  • In a decay, quarks change type; they do not appear or vanish one at a time.
Quick check
?In a decay, an up quark changes into a down quark. Which of these must also be emitted for charge to be conserved?
Mission 1

Baryon number, B

  • Quark +⅓, antiquark −⅓.
  • Baryons (qqq), e.g. p, n: B = +1. Antibaryons: B = −1.
  • Mesons (q q̄): B = 0. Leptons and photons: B = 0.
  • Total B is the same before and after.
  • The proton is the only stable baryon. Other baryons decay, eventually to a proton: it is the lightest baryon, so B has nowhere else to go.
Mission 1

Lepton number, L

  • Leptons (e⁻, νₑ …): L = +1. Antileptons (e⁺, ν̄ₑ …): L = −1. Everything else: L = 0.
  • Total L is the same before and after.
  • Two separate counts, each conserved: Lₑ (e⁻, νₑ) and Lμ (μ⁻, νμ).
  • The muon decays into an electron: μ⁻ → e⁻ + ν̄ₑ + νμ
  • Lμ: 1 = 0 + 0 + 1 ✓ · Lₑ: 0 = 1 + (−1) + 0 ✓
Quick check
?A student proposes that a free neutron decays into a proton and an electron and nothing else: n → p + e⁻. Which conservation law that holds in all nuclear decays would this proposal break?
Mission 1

Worked example: allowed or forbidden?

  • Free neutron decay: n → p + e⁻ + ν̄ₑ
  • Q: 0 = (+1) + (−1) + 0 ✓
  • B: 1 = 1 + 0 + 0 ✓
  • L: 0 = 0 + 1 + (−1) ✓ → allowed.
  • Proposed: p → e⁺ + γ
  • Q: +1 = +1 + 0 ✓
  • B: 1 → 0 ✗ · L: 0 → −1 ✗ → forbidden.
  • One broken law is enough to rule an interaction out.
Mission 1

Strangeness, S

  • s quark: S = −1. s̄: S = +1. u and d: S = 0.
  • Strange particles are produced by the strong interaction (in pairs, when made from non-strange particles) and decay by the weak interaction.
  • Strong: S conserved. Weak: S changes by 0, +1 or −1.
  • Production (strong): π⁻ + p → K⁰ + Λ⁰. S: 0 + 0 = (+1) + (−1) ✓
  • Decay (weak): K⁺ → μ⁺ + νμ. S: +1 → 0, so ΔS = −1.
Quick check
?The Λ⁰ (quarks u, d, s) decays: Λ⁰ → p + π⁻. Which statement is correct?
Mission 1

Energy and momentum

  • Total energy (rest energy + kinetic energy + photons) is conserved.
  • Free neutron decay: rest energies 939.55 MeV → 938.26 + 0.511 MeV.
  • The missing 0.78 MeV becomes kinetic energy of the products.
  • A free proton cannot decay into a neutron: the products would have more rest energy.
  • Momentum: a particle at rest decays into products whose momenta add to zero.
Mission check

?Which of these proposed interactions is forbidden by a conservation law?

A proton turning into a positron and light keeps charge and energy balanced, but breaks baryon number (1 → 0) and lepton number (0 → −1). No one has ever seen it.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why can a few minutes of weak ultraviolet damage skin cells' DNA when hours of bright red light can't?

Make a guess: Why can a few minutes of weak ultraviolet damage skin cells' DNA when hours of bright red light can't?

Mission 2

Light comes in packets: photons

  • Electromagnetic radiation is emitted and absorbed in discrete packets of energy: photons.
  • A photon is a quantum of electromagnetic energy.
  • For any EM wave c = fλ, with c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹, so f = c/λ.
  • Energy of one photon: E = hf = hc/λ.
  • h = Planck constant = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s.
  • Higher frequency (shorter wavelength) → more energy per photon.
  • A brighter beam of the same colour has more photons, not bigger ones.
Mission 2

The electronvolt

  • Photon energies in joules are tiny. Use the electronvolt.
  • 1 eV: the energy gained by an electron accelerated through a potential difference of 1 V.
  • 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. J → eV: divide by 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹.
  • Example, blue light, λ = 450 nm:
    • E = hc/λ = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸)/(450 × 10⁻⁹) = 4.42 × 10⁻¹⁹ J
    • = 4.42 × 10⁻¹⁹/1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 2.76 eV.
Quick check
?A laser pointer emits light of wavelength 633 nm with a power of 5.0 mW. How many photons does it emit each second? Use h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s and c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.
Quick check
?Predict first. Which observation from photoelectric experiments cannot be explained by the classical wave theory of light?
Mission 2

The photoelectric effect

Shine electromagnetic radiation on a clean metal surface. Electrons may be emitted: photoelectrons.

  • Below a threshold frequency f0: no emission, however intense the light.
  • Above f0: emission immediately, even in very dim light.
  • Maximum kinetic energy of the electrons rises with frequency; it does not depend on intensity.
  • Number emitted per second is proportional to intensity (above f0).
  • f0 depends on the metal (e.g. zinc needs ultraviolet).
Mission 2

Einstein's explanation

  • One photon gives all its energy to one electron: a one-to-one interaction.
  • Work function φ: the minimum energy needed to remove an electron from the metal's surface.
  • If hf < φ: no electron can escape. Threshold: hf0 = φ.
  • If hf is bigger than the work function, the extra energy becomes kinetic energy. Electrons from just below the surface lose some on the way out, so this is a maximum.
  • hf = φ + Ek(max)
Mission 2

Why waves can't explain it

Wave theory: energy arrives spread over the wavefront and builds up with intensity and time. It predicts:

  • Any frequency should work, given bright enough light. ✗ Observed: a sharp threshold f0.
  • Dim light should cause a delay while energy builds up. ✗ Observed: instant emission.
  • Brighter light should give faster electrons. ✗ Observed: the maximum kinetic energy is set by f alone.
  • Photon model explains all three: energy comes in lumps of hf, one lump per electron.
  • So the photoelectric effect is evidence for the particle (photon) nature of light.
Mission 2

Worked example

Light of wavelength 400 nm falls on potassium, work function 2.30 eV.

  • Photon energy: hf = hc/λ = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸)/(400 × 10⁻⁹) = 4.97 × 10⁻¹⁹ J = 3.11 eV.
  • Maximum kinetic energy = 3.11 − 2.30 = 0.81 eV = 1.29 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
  • Threshold frequency: f0 = (work function)/h = (2.30 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹)/(6.63 × 10⁻³⁴) = 5.55 × 10¹⁴ Hz.
Mission 2

Stopping potential

  • Emitting plate and collecting plate in a vacuum tube; monochromatic light on the emitter.
  • Make the collector negative relative to the emitter: electrons are slowed as they cross.
  • Increase this reverse p.d. until the current just falls to zero: the stopping potential Vs.
  • Work done on a charge Q moved through a p.d. V: E = QV. For an electron, eV.
  • Then even the fastest electrons are turned back: eVs = maximum kinetic energy.
  • In the worked example: maximum kinetic energy = 0.81 eV, so Vs = 0.81 V.
  • You won't be asked to describe the apparatus: use eVs = Ek(max).
Quick check
?In a photoelectric experiment, monochromatic light whose photons each have energy 4.1 eV illuminates a metal plate in a vacuum tube. The graph shows the current I as a function of the potential difference V of the collecting plate relative to the illuminated plate. What is the work function of the metal?
Quick check
?Light whose photons each have energy 4.0 eV shines on three metals: X, with work function 2.0 eV; Y, with work function 3.0 eV; and Z, with work function 4.5 eV. Which statement about the maximum kinetic energy of the electrons emitted from each metal is correct?
Mission check

?Ultraviolet light above the threshold frequency shines on a metal plate and electrons are emitted. The ultraviolet is replaced by ultraviolet of a shorter wavelength, with the same intensity (the same power per square metre). What happens?

Energy per photon is set by the frequency; the number of photons by the power. The same holds in skin: each ultraviolet photon can damage a molecule on its own; red photons cannot, however many arrive.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Experiments in the early 1800s convinced physicists light is a wave. Which experiment made them think again?

Make a guess: Experiments in the early 1800s convinced physicists light is a wave. Which experiment made them think again?

Mission 3

Demonstration: electroscope and zinc plate

  • Clean zinc plate on a gold-leaf electroscope, charged negatively: the leaf rises (repelled).
  • Shine ultraviolet on the zinc: the leaf falls. Electrons are leaving the zinc.
  • Shine a very bright visible lamp instead: no change. Below the threshold frequency.
  • Charge it positively and shine UV: no change. Emitted electrons are pulled back.
  • Put a sheet of glass (absorbs UV) in the beam: the leaf stops falling.
Quick check
?A clean zinc plate is isolated and initially uncharged, and it is not connected to any circuit or battery. In a vacuum, ultraviolet light with a frequency above the threshold frequency for zinc shines on the plate. Which claim is correct?
Mission check

?The zinc on a negatively charged electroscope is replaced by a clean plate of a metal that emits electrons only for light of wavelength shorter than 540 nm. A bright red lamp (650 nm) shines on it, then a dim blue lamp (450 nm). What does the leaf do?

Whether electrons leave depends on the energy of each photon, set by its frequency; brightness changes only how many leave. A sharp threshold like this fits photons, not waves.

Mission 4

Mission 4: An electron meets its antimatter twin and both vanish. What comes out, and why never just one thing?

Make a guess: An electron meets its antimatter twin and both vanish. What comes out, and why never just one thing?

Mission 4

Annihilation

  • Every particle has an antiparticle: same mass, opposite charge.
  • When a particle meets its antiparticle, they annihilate.
  • Their rest energy (mass) becomes the energy of photons: usually two gamma-ray photons.
  • e⁻ + e⁺ → γ + γ
  • Charge: −1 + 1 = 0 ✓ (photons are neutral).
  • Momentum: almost at rest, total momentum ≈ 0. One photon would carry momentum away, so there must be two, travelling in opposite directions.
  • Energy: total energy before = total photon energy after.
Quick check
?An electron and a positron annihilate, producing two photons. Which quantity is different after the annihilation from before it?
Mission 4

How much energy?

  • Slow electron and positron: each photon carries the rest energy of one electron: 0.511 MeV.
  • Rest energies are given in MeV (data booklet: electron 0.510999 MeV).
  • Each photon: 8.2 × 10⁻¹⁴ J ≈ 0.51 MeV. Wavelength λ = hc/E ≈ 2.4 × 10⁻¹² m: a gamma ray.
  • If the particles were moving, their kinetic energy adds to the photon energy.
Quick check
?A muon and an antimuon, both almost at rest, annihilate to produce two photons. The rest energy of a muon is 105.7 MeV. What is the wavelength of each photon?
Mission 4

Pair production

  • The reverse: a photon's energy becomes a particle and its antiparticle.
  • γ → e⁻ + e⁺
  • Minimum photon energy = rest energy of the pair = 2 × rest energy of one particle.
  • Electron–positron: at least 2 × 0.511 = 1.022 MeV.
  • Extra photon energy becomes the particles' kinetic energy.
  • It happens near a nucleus, which takes some momentum: a lone photon cannot conserve both energy and momentum.
Mission 4

Worked example: minimum photon energy

  • Pair production of an electron and a positron.
  • Emin = 2 × 0.511 MeV = 1.022 MeV = 1.022 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ J = 1.64 × 10⁻¹³ J.
  • Minimum frequency: f = E/h = 1.64 × 10⁻¹³ ÷ 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ = 2.5 × 10²⁰ Hz.
Quick check
?A photon creates a proton–antiproton pair. The rest energy of a proton is 938 MeV. What is the minimum frequency of the photon?
Mission check

?An electron and a positron, each with 0.30 MeV of kinetic energy, collide head-on and annihilate into two photons. The rest energy of an electron is 0.511 MeV. What is the energy of each photon?

An electron and a positron vanish into two gamma photons that fly apart in opposite directions. One photon alone could not conserve momentum. Any kinetic energy the pair had goes into the photons too.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Check each conserved quantity separately; one failure forbids the interaction.
  • Charge, baryon number and lepton number are conserved in every interaction.
  • Strangeness: conserved in strong interactions; changes by 0 or ±1 in weak ones. Lₑ and Lμ are conserved separately.

Mission 2

  • Photon energy E = hf = hc/λ; 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Intensity sets the number of photons, frequency the energy of each.
  • One photon frees at most one electron; threshold hf0 = φ; maximum kinetic energy = hf − φ, independent of intensity. Evidence for photons.
  • Stopping potential: eVs = maximum kinetic energy.

Mission 3

  • Negatively charged zinc on an electroscope discharges under UV, not under bright visible light: a threshold frequency.

Mission 4

  • Annihilation: particle + antiparticle → two photons; charge, energy and momentum are conserved.
  • Pair production needs a photon of at least 2 × the rest energy of one particle; extra energy becomes kinetic energy.
