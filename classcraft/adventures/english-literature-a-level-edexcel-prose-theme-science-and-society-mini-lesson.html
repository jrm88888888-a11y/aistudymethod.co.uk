Science and society · what writers fearScreen 7

Three fears to compare

  • Science without responsibility: Victor creates, then abandons. The Creature: "I was benevolent and good; misery made me a fiend." (Chapter X). Anne K. Mellor reads the novel as a feminist critique of science that tries to master a "female" nature.
  • Humans displaced: the Martians are what evolution might make of us, "practically mere brains" (Book Two, Chapter II).
  • Empire reversed: the narrator likens the invasion to Europeans' "war of extermination" on Aboriginal Tasmanians (Book One, Chapter I).

Mission checkScreen 9

?Frankenstein and the Martians: what do writers fear science will do?

Neither novel is simply "anti-science". Both fear what happens when knowledge outruns responsibility and humility: Victor abandons what he creates, and Wells's humans learn that they don't rule nature. Comparing how each text tests human pride is the core of the theme.

A-level skills · connections and comparison (AO4)Screen 1

What AO4 asks

  • Every board's AO4: explore connections across literary texts.
  • Connect through theme (what the texts are about), method (voice, imagery, structure), genre (Gothic, science fiction, tragedy) or context (the ideas each text answers).
  • Difference counts as much as likeness: the strongest connections show two texts treating one idea in different ways.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Two novels, 1818 and 1898, both about science. What connection scores?

A connection scores when it goes beyond the shared topic. Compare how each text treats the idea, using precise evidence and methods from both, and explain the differences, often through context.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Frankenstein (1818; 1831 text quoted here) answers the galvanic experiments of its day; The War of the Worlds (1897–98) answers evolutionary science after Darwin.
  • Victor's "torrent of light" and Wells's human "empire over matter" are confident images that each novel then tests.
  • Component 2 is one open-book comparative essay, 40 marks: keep both novels in view throughout.
  • AO4 rewards connections of theme, method, genre and context, and difference counts as much as likeness.
  • Move from topic ("both about science") to a connection that compares how each text works and why they differ.
  • Shelley's scientist gazes down at his creation; Wells turns the microscope on humanity: one idea, opposite ends of the lens.
  • Component 2 is one comparative essay on your two prose texts: compare throughout.

Key quotations to learn

  • Life and death appeared to me ideal bounds, which I should first break through (Frankenstein, Chapter IV (Victor)) The scientist's ambition to cross natural limits.
  • how dangerous is the acquirement of knowledge (Frankenstein, Chapter IV (Victor to Walton)) The warning the frame narrative delivers.
  • I was benevolent and good; misery made me a fiend. (Frankenstein, Chapter X (the Creature)) Rejection, not science alone, makes the "monster".
  • serene in their assurance of their empire over matter (The War of the Worlds, Book One Chapter I) Human confidence in mastering nature, about to break.
  • intellects vast and cool and unsympathetic (The War of the Worlds, Book One Chapter I) Intelligence without feeling: evolution's possible end.
  • slain, after all man’s devices had failed, by the humblest things that God, in his wisdom, has put upon this earth (The War of the Worlds, Book Two Chapter VIII) Bacteria, not human science, end the invasion.
