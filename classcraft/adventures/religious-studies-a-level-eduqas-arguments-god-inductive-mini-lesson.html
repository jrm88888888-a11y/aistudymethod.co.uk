Make a guess: Can anyone prove that God exists, or is the most an argument can do to make it likely?

Mission 1

What is an argument for God trying to do?

  • An argument: premises (reasons) leading to a conclusion.
  • Arguments for God's existence can aim at different things:
  • Prove: show God must exist.
  • Make probable: show God's existence is more likely than not.
  • Show belief is reasonable: a rational person may believe.
  • Deepen faith: help believers understand what they already believe.
  • Ask of every argument: which of these is it trying to do, and does it succeed?
Mission 1

Observation or thought?

  • A posteriori: based on observation and experience of the world. ("After" experience.)
  • Cosmological (the universe exists and changes), teleological (design), religious experience and moral arguments start from what we observe.
  • A priori: based on thought alone, before or independent of experience.
  • The ontological argument starts only from the concept of God.
Mission 1

Deductive and inductive

  • Deductive: if the premises are true and the logic is valid, the conclusion must be true. A valid argument with true premises is sound.
  • Inductive: the premises support the conclusion, making it probable; true premises could still lead to a false conclusion.
  • Usual pairing: a priori arguments are deductive (ontological); a posteriori arguments are inductive (design, cosmological).
  • Your spec calls the cosmological and design arguments inductive, and the ontological argument deductive.
  • But not always: Aquinas' Ways and the Kalam argument start from observation yet are often set out as deductive syllogisms.
Mission 1

Analytic and synthetic

  • Analytic proposition: true by the meaning of its words. "A triangle has three sides."
  • Synthetic proposition: true or false because of how the world is. "This triangle is red."
  • The ontological argument treats "God exists" as analytic: existence is part of what "God" means.
  • Immanuel Kant (1724–1804), Critique of Pure Reason (1781): all statements that something exists are synthetic; existence is not a predicate (it adds nothing to the concept of a thing).
  • Whether existence can be a predicate: the key test for the ontological argument.

Mission 1Read the source

Aquinas on demonstrating God

Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I, q. 2, a. 2 (English Dominican translation, 1920s):

> "Hence the existence of God, in so far as it is not self-evident to us, can be demonstrated from those of His effects which are known to us."

> "faith presupposes natural knowledge, even as grace presupposes nature"

He supports this with Romans 1:20: "the invisible things of him since the creation of the world are clearly seen, being perceived through the things that are made" (WEBBE).

Q1 Does Aquinas think God's existence is known a priori or a posteriori?

Show answer

A posteriori: from God's effects, which we know.

Q2 How does he see reason and faith?

Show answer

Reason can reach some truths about God; faith builds on them, as grace builds on nature.

Mission 1

Proof, probability and the cumulative case

  • A proof would compel agreement from anyone who accepts the premises. Very few philosophers think any argument for (or against) God does this.
  • So many defenders aim at probability.
  • Cumulative case: several arguments, each weak alone, together make God's existence more probable. Like evidence in a court case.
  • Richard Swinburne (b. 1934), The Existence of God (1979): arguments that each raise the probability of God combine to make it more probable than not.
  • Basil Mitchell (1917–2011), The Justification of Religious Belief (1973): belief is justified as the best overall interpretation of the evidence.
  • Critique: ten leaky buckets hold no more water than one (Antony Flew's image): weak arguments may not add up.
Mission 1

Where arguments can go wrong

  • Fallacy of composition: what is true of each part need not be true of the whole. Bertrand Russell (1948 radio debate, paraphrase): every human has a mother, but the human race does not.
  • The leap: even if the universe has a cause or a designer, why must it be the transcendent, all-powerful, loving God of classical theism? (Hume's objection.)
  • Chance: order may result from chance plus vast time, or from natural selection, not design.
  • Defining into existence: can any concept, however perfect, guarantee that something exists? (Gaunilo; Kant.)
  • Each objection is met by replies (–).

Mission 1

Reason and faith: four positions

  • Natural theology: reason can show that God exists; faith adds what reason cannot reach (the Trinity, the incarnation). Aquinas: reason supplies "preambles" of faith.
  • Faith seeking understanding (fides quaerens intellectum): Anselm (1033–1109), Proslogion 1 (paraphrase): he believes in order to understand. Argument expresses and deepens faith; it does not create it.
  • Fideism: faith does not rest on argument and may go beyond reason. Søren Kierkegaard (1813–1855): a "leap" of faith; Blaise Pascal (1623–1662), Pensées (paraphrase): the heart has reasons that reason does not know.
  • Evidentialism: belief should match the evidence. W. K. Clifford (1845–1879), "The Ethics of Belief" (1877, paraphrase): it is always wrong to believe anything on insufficient evidence.
  • Reformed epistemology: Alvin Plantinga (b. 1932): belief in God can be properly basic, rational without argument, like belief in other minds.
Mission 1Diversity

Reason and faith across traditions and ages

  • Catholic: the First Vatican Council (1870) taught that God can be known with certainty by natural reason from created things; faith and reason cannot truly conflict.
  • Protestant: many follow the Reformers in stressing faith and scripture; Karl Barth (1886–1968) rejected natural theology as a human attempt to reach God; many evangelicals defend it.
  • Eastern Orthodox: God is known above all in prayer and worship; reason cannot grasp God's essence. (Swinburne, a leading natural theologian, is Orthodox.)
  • Islam: the kalam tradition (e.g. al-Ghazali, 1058–1111) argued that what begins to exist needs a cause: the root of today's Kalam argument.
  • Many atheists and agnostics: the arguments fail, so belief is not justified (Clifford); or the evidence is balanced.
Mission 1Evaluate

Is a priori or a posteriori more persuasive?

  • Claim: A posteriori arguments are more persuasive than a priori arguments.
  • For: They start from facts everyone shares: the universe exists, shows order, people report experiences.
  • For: They match how we reason in science and daily life.
  • Against: At best they give probability, and face the leap, chance and fallacy objections.
  • Against: A sound a priori argument would give certainty, and needs no disputed evidence.
  • But: many find a priori arguments feel like a word trick (Gaunilo, Kant); and persuasiveness differs for believers and non-believers.
  • Judgement criteria: Persuasive to whom? Proof or probability: which is needed? Which style survives its strongest objections?

Mission 2

Arguing from the world we see

  • A cosmological argument starts from a fact about the cosmos (the universe): things move, are caused, or exist but need not.
  • It is a posteriori: it begins from experience (Latin "from what comes after").
  • It is usually classed as inductive: the premises support the conclusion but do not make it logically certain. (Aquinas presented the Ways as demonstrations; some philosophers read them as deductive.)
  • Conclusion: there is a first or necessary being, which believers identify as God.
  • Your spec: "inductive proofs; the concept of 'a posteriori'".
Mission 2

A very old question

  • Plato (c. 427–347 BCE), Laws Book X: all motion traces back to a soul that moves itself.
  • Aristotle (384–322 BCE), Physics VIII and Metaphysics XII: an Unmoved Mover that moves all things without being moved.
  • Al-Kindi (c. 801–873) and al-Ghazali (1058–1111): Muslim thinkers who argued the universe began and so needs a cause. This is the root of the Kalam argument.
  • Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274), Summa Theologica I, question 2, article 3 (written 1265–74): the Five Ways; the first three are cosmological.
  • G. W. Leibniz (1646–1716): the principle of sufficient reason.
Mission 2Read the source

The First Way: motion

Aquinas, Summa Theologica I.2.3 (English Dominican translation, 1920s):

> "For motion is nothing else than the reduction of something from potentiality to actuality."

> "Therefore it is necessary to arrive at a first mover, put in motion by no other; and this everyone understands to be God."

  • Motion means any change, not just movement from place to place.
  • Fire (actually hot) makes wood (potentially hot) actually hot. Nothing moves itself from potential to actual in the same respect.
  • Aquinas' image: a staff moves only because the hand moves it.

Q1 Why can't wood make itself hot?

Show answer

It is only potentially hot; something already actually hot must bring about the change.

Q2 What does Aquinas mean by "motion"?

Show answer

Any change from potentiality to actuality.

Mission 2

The Second Way: causes

  • We observe an order of efficient causes: things are brought about by other things.
  • Nothing can be the cause of itself: it would have to exist before itself.
  • Take away the first cause and you take away every later cause and effect.
  • So there must be a first efficient cause "to which everyone gives the name of God" (ST I.2.3).
Mission 2Read the source

The Third Way: contingency and necessity

Aquinas, Summa Theologica I.2.3:

> "We find in nature things that are possible to be and not to be, since they are found to be generated, and to corrupt …"

> "Therefore we cannot but postulate the existence of some being having of itself its own necessity, and not receiving it from another …"

  • Contingent ("possible to be and not to be"): things that come into being and pass away.
  • If everything were contingent, at some time there would have been nothing; and from nothing, nothing comes. But things exist now.
  • So something exists necessarily.
  • A necessary being might owe its necessity to another; that series cannot go on forever. So there is a being necessary of itself: "This all men speak of as God."

Q1 Why does Aquinas think contingent things alone cannot explain why anything exists now?

Show answer

Contingent things need something already existing to bring them into being.

Q2 What is the difference between "necessary" and "necessary of itself"?

Show answer

Some necessary things depend on another for their necessity; one does not.

Mission 2

Two kinds of series

  • Aquinas rejects an infinite regress: a series of causes with no first member.
  • But he means a series where each member acts now, through the one above it (Aquinas' term in ST I.46.2: per se; later writers say "essentially ordered"): the hand moves the staff, which moves the stone.
  • He allows that a series in time (per accidens, "accidentally": a man begets a son, who begets a son …) could, as far as reason shows, go back forever (ST I.46.2). That the world began he holds "by faith alone".
  • So the Ways ask what keeps things existing and changing now, not what happened first.
Mission 2

The Kalam argument

  • Kalam (Arabic kalām, "speech"): Islamic theology, the tradition of al-Ghazali.
  • Revived by William Lane Craig (b. 1949), The Kalām Cosmological Argument (1979):
  • 1. Whatever begins to exist has a cause.
  • 2. The universe began to exist.
  • 3. Therefore the universe has a cause.
  • For premise 2, Craig argues that an actual infinite cannot exist in reality. Example: Hilbert's Hotel, a hotel with infinitely many full rooms that can still take infinitely many new guests: absurd if real.
  • Also, an infinite past could never be crossed one event at a time to reach today.
  • Craig adds scientific support: Big Bang cosmology.
  • Your spec: "rejection of actual infinities".
Quick check
?In the Third Way, Aquinas allows that a necessary being might have "its necessity caused by another". Why does he still conclude there is a being necessary of itself?
Mission 2

Kalam: why a personal creator?

  • The cause of the universe must be uncaused, timeless, changeless, immaterial and enormously powerful (Craig's reasoning, paraphrased).
  • Why personal? If the cause were a set of timeless conditions, the universe would exist as long as those conditions: eternally.
  • Only a free agent can choose, timelessly, to bring about an effect with a beginning.
  • Craig: this matches the God of classical theism (in Islam, Judaism and Christianity).
  • Your spec: "concept of personal creator".

Mission 3

Hume: do we know everything has a cause?

  • David Hume (1711–1776), Scottish empiricist: all knowledge of matters of fact comes from experience.
  • We never observe causation itself, only one event regularly followed by another (A Treatise of Human Nature, 1739–40, Book 1, Part 3).
  • We can imagine something beginning to exist without a cause; so "everything has a cause" is not known by logic alone (paraphrase).
  • Hume did not say things happen without causes. He said the principle rests on experience, and so cannot be stretched beyond experience.
  • And the universe is single: we have no experience "of the origin of worlds" (Dialogues concerning Natural Religion, Part II), so we cannot know what kind of cause, if any, it has.
  • Your spec: Hume's "empirical objections and critique of causes".
Mission 3Read the source

Hume, Dialogues Part IX

David Hume, Dialogues concerning Natural Religion (published 1779), Part IX. Cleanthes answers an argument for a necessary being:

> "Did I show you the particular causes of each individual in a collection of twenty particles of matter, I should think it very unreasonable, should you afterwards ask me, what was the cause of the whole twenty."

> "… why may not the material universe be the necessarily existent Being …?"

  • Explain each member of a series and you have explained the series; the "whole" is something the mind adds.
  • If something must be necessary, why not the universe itself, rather than God?
  • Hume also argues (same Part) that whatever we can conceive as existing we can conceive as not existing, so no being exists of logical necessity (paraphrase).

Q1 What does the twenty-particles example say about asking for a cause of the whole universe?

Show answer

Once each part is explained, there is no further whole to explain.

Q2 What alternative to God does the second sentence offer?

Show answer

The material universe as the necessary being.

Mission 3

Russell: the universe as brute fact

  • Bertrand Russell (1872–1970), BBC radio debate with Frederick Copleston SJ (1907–1994), 1948.
  • Brute fact: something that exists with no further explanation. Russell: "the universe is just there, and that's all."
  • Fallacy of composition: what is true of each part need not be true of the whole. Russell's example (paraphrase): every human being has a mother, but the human race does not have a mother.
  • So "each thing has a cause" does not show "the universe has a cause".
  • "Necessary" applies only to propositions (true by definition), not to beings; "necessary being" makes no sense (paraphrase).
Quick check

Mission 3

Science: the Big Bang

  • Big Bang model: the universe expanded from a hot, dense state about 13.8 billion years ago. Proposed by Georges Lemaître (1894–1966), physicist and Catholic priest (1927–31).
  • For the argument: Craig uses it to support Kalam premise 2: the universe began.
  • Against: Physics describes the universe from its earliest moments; for many, no further cause is needed. Some models (e.g. Hartle and Hawking's "no-boundary" proposal, 1983) remove a first moment.
  • Reply: physics starts with laws and a state; it does not explain why anything exists (Aquinas' Third Way does not need a beginning).
  • Lemaître himself kept his physics and his faith as separate questions.
  • Your spec: Big Bang theory as an alternative scientific explanation (Darwin in a later lesson).
Mission 3

Is the first cause God?

  • Even if there is a first cause or necessary being, critics ask why it must be the God of classical theism: one, personal, all-powerful, all-good.
  • It might be the universe (Hume), many causes, or an impersonal cause.
  • Replies: Aquinas spends the next questions of the Summa (ST I.3–11) arguing that a first, necessary being must be simple, perfect, infinite, unchanging, eternal and one; Craig argues the cause is personal (see Mission 2).
Mission 3

Proof, faith and the atheist view

  • Status as proof: a posteriori arguments give probability, not certainty. Richard Swinburne (b. 1934) treats them as parts of a cumulative case (The Existence of God, 1979).
  • Value for faith: Aquinas: such arguments are preambles to faith: "faith presupposes natural knowledge" (ST I.2.2, reply 1). The Catholic Church teaches that God can be known with certainty by natural reason from created things (First Vatican Council, Dei Filius, 1870).
  • Many Protestants differ: Karl Barth (1886–1968) rejected natural theology; faith rests on God's self-revelation in Christ.
  • Atheist view: the universe may need no explanation (Russell); "Who caused God?" (Richard Dawkins, The God Delusion, 2006); the argument at most shows a cause, not a God to worship.

Mission 3Evaluate

Is the cosmological argument persuasive?

  • Claim: The cosmological argument gives good reason to believe in God.
  • For: It starts from undeniable facts: things change, are caused, and might not have existed.
  • For: The principle of sufficient reason (everything has an explanation) seems built into reason and science: we expect explanations.
  • Against: Hume and Russell: the whole needs no cause beyond its parts; the universe may be a brute fact.
  • Against: At best a first cause, not the God of classical theism.
  • Judgement criteria: Is the principle of sufficient reason true or only a habit of mind? Is a brute fact a real answer? Is an infinite regress possible? Can an inductive argument be enough for faith?

Mission 4

Order and purpose

  • Teleological from Greek telos, "end" or "purpose".
  • The design argument reasons from order in the world to an intelligent designer.
  • Two kinds of order:
  • Design qua purpose: parts fitted together to serve an end (an eye for seeing).
  • Design qua regularity: things behave in regular, law-like ways (planets in orbit).
  • Like the cosmological argument, it is a posteriori and inductive: at best it makes a designer probable.
Mission 4Read the source

Aquinas' Fifth Way

Aquinas, Summa Theologica I.2.3 (English Dominican translation):

> "We see that things which lack intelligence, such as natural bodies, act for an end, and this is evident from their acting always, or nearly always, in the same way, so as to obtain the best result."

> "… as the arrow is shot to its mark by the archer."

  • Governance: natural things without minds regularly move towards goals (an acorn grows into an oak).
  • Something without a mind cannot aim itself. So "some intelligent being exists by whom all natural things are directed to their end; and this being we call God."
  • An argument from regularity directed to an end, not from complex machines.

Q1 What does the arrow stand for?

Show answer

Natural things that reach goals without knowing it.

Q2 What evidence does Aquinas give that natural bodies act for an end?

Show answer

They act always, or nearly always, in the same way to get the best result.

  • Your spec: "concept of governance; archer and arrow analogy".
Mission 4

Paley's watch

  • William Paley (1743–1805), Anglican clergyman, Natural Theology (1802), chapter 1 (paraphrase):
  • Crossing a heath, I strike my foot on a stone. It might have lain there forever.
  • But if I find a watch, I cannot say the same. Its parts are framed and put together for a purpose: to mark the hours.
  • If the parts were shaped or arranged differently, it would not work.
  • So the watch must have had a maker who understood and designed it.
  • The world, and above all living things, shows the same complex purpose, on a far greater scale: so it too has a designer.
  • This is an analogy: watch : watchmaker :: world : world-maker.
Mission 4

Paley's "even ifs"

  • In chapter 1 Paley says the inference still holds even if (paraphrase):
  • we have never seen a watch made;
  • the watch sometimes goes wrong;
  • we do not know what some parts are for.
  • Not "chance", not a "principle of order", not "laws": laws need an agent to act by them.
  • Chapter 2: a watch that could produce another watch would make us admire its maker more; an endless line of such watches would still need a designer.
  • Chapter 3: the eye is built like a telescope, and more finely.

Mission 4

Tennant: the anthropic argument

  • F. R. Tennant (1866–1957), Philosophical Theology, volume II (1930).
  • He accepted evolution; he argued from the order of the world as a whole ("wider teleology"), not from single organs.
  • Anthropic (Greek anthropos, "human"): the world is suited to produce and sustain intelligent human life.
  • Matter, laws and environment fit together so that minds that think, understand the world and make moral choices can appear.
  • No single fact proves design; the fit of many facts together does: a cumulative argument.
  • Your spec: "the universe specifically designed for intelligent human life".
Mission 4

Tennant: the aesthetic argument

  • The world is full of beauty: sunsets, music, mathematics, landscapes (Tennant, paraphrase).
  • Beauty seems to have no survival value: evolution does not need us to enjoy a sunset.
  • So beauty looks like a gift: evidence of a designer who wanted us to enjoy the world.
  • It adds to the cumulative case: it does not prove God alone.
  • Critics: beauty is in the eye of the beholder; or our sense of beauty may itself have evolved.
Mission 4

The anthropic principle and fine-tuning

  • Anthropic principle: term introduced by physicist Brandon Carter (1973).
  • Weak form: we can only observe a universe that allows observers like us.
  • Fine-tuning: many physical constants (e.g. the strength of gravity, the strong nuclear force) seem to lie in a narrow range that allows stars, chemistry and life.
  • Design reading: fine-tuning is best explained by a designer (Swinburne; John Polkinghorne, 1930–2021, physicist and Anglican priest).
  • Other readings: many universes (a multiverse), so one like ours was likely somewhere; or we should not be surprised to find what we must find to be here.
  • Reply (John Leslie, Universes, 1989, paraphrase): if fifty marksmen all miss you, you should still ask why.

Mission 4Worked answer

Explaining Paley

Question: Explain Paley's design argument.

  • Point: Paley argues by analogy from a watch to the world.
  • Develop: A watch's parts are framed and put together for a purpose; if they were arranged differently it would not tell the time; so it had a designer.
  • Develop: Living things show the same, and greater, complex purpose (the eye works like a telescope).
  • Conclude: As like effects have like causes, the world has a designer, God.
  • Precision: the inference holds even if we never saw a watch made, or it goes wrong (Natural Theology, 1802, ch. 1).

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A posteriori arguments start from observation; a priori arguments from thought alone. Deductive arguments aim to prove; inductive arguments to make probable.
  • The ontological argument treats "God exists" as analytic; Kant holds existence claims are synthetic and existence is not a predicate.
  • Few think any argument proves God; many defend a cumulative case for probability.
  • Reason and faith: natural theology, faith seeking understanding, fideism, evidentialism, reformed epistemology.

Mission 2

  • Cosmological arguments are a posteriori and inductive: from motion, causation or contingency in the world to a first or necessary being.
  • Aquinas' Third Way: contingent beings cannot explain existence; there must be a being necessary of itself.
  • The First and Second Ways reject an infinite regress of movers and causes acting now; the Ways do not depend on a beginning in time.
  • Kalam (Craig): whatever begins to exist has a cause; the universe began; its cause is personal.

Mission 3

  • Hume: the causal principle rests on experience; the whole needs no cause beyond its parts; the universe itself might be necessary.
  • Russell: the universe is a brute fact; "each thing has a cause" does not give "the universe has a cause" (fallacy of composition).
  • The Big Bang is used both to support Kalam and to replace the need for a cause.

Mission 4

  • The design argument is a posteriori and inductive: from order in the world to a designer.
  • Paley (1802): a watch's parts are fitted together for a purpose; so is the world; so the world has a designer. The inference holds even if the watch goes wrong.
  • Aquinas' Fifth Way: mindless things act regularly for ends, like an arrow shot by an archer.
  • Tennant's anthropic argument and modern fine-tuning argue from the fit of the whole universe to intelligent life.
