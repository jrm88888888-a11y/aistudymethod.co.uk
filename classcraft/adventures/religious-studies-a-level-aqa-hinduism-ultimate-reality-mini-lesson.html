Mission 1

The four Vedas

  • Veda means "knowledge". There are four:
  • Rig Veda: 1,028 hymns of praise in 10 books (mandalas); the oldest, c. 1500–1200 BCE.
  • Sama Veda: verses set to chant. Yajur Veda: formulas for performing sacrifice. Atharva Veda: hymns and spells for healing, protection and daily life.
  • Each Veda grew in layers: hymns (Samhitas), ritual texts (Brahmanas), "forest" texts (Aranyakas), and the philosophical Upanishads.
Mission 1

Yajna: the sacrifice

  • Yajna: offerings (ghee, grain, milk, soma, and in some rites animals) poured into a sacred fire.
  • Specialist priests: one recites hymns, one performs the actions, one chants, one oversees.
  • Purpose: to praise and feed the gods (devas), who give in return: rain, cattle, sons, long life, victory.
  • Sacrifice keeps rta, the cosmic order that holds nature and society together.
  • Vedic society: semi-nomadic, cattle-herding tribes led by a chief (rajan), with tribal assemblies. Priests and warriors held high status; the four-fold varna scheme appears only in a late hymn (Rig Veda 10.90; see an earlier lesson).
  • Household rites (daily fire offerings) and great public rites (lasting days) both existed.
Mission 1

Agni: the fire

  • Agni is fire, and the god in the fire. About 200 hymns are addressed to him; the Rig Veda opens with a hymn to Agni (1.1).
  • Messenger: Agni carries offerings up to the gods and brings the gods down to the sacrifice.
  • Priest of the gods: he is called the household priest and the "mouth" of the gods.
  • Present in three places: the hearth on earth, lightning in the air, the sun in the sky.
  • Every yajna depends on Agni: without fire, there is no offering.
Mission 1

Indra: king of the gods

  • Indra: the warrior god of storm and thunder, armed with the vajra (thunderbolt). About a quarter of the Rig Veda's hymns are to him.
  • Great deed (Rig Veda 1.32, paraphrase): Indra kills the serpent Vritra, who had held back the waters, and releases the rivers.
  • He leads the Aryas in battle and is strengthened by drinking soma.
  • Worshippers ask Indra for victory, rain and cattle.
  • He is a model of the Vedic warrior-chief: generous, powerful, sometimes reckless.
Mission 1

Soma: the drink and the god

  • Soma is a plant, the drink pressed from it, and a god. Book 9 of the Rig Veda (114 hymns) is wholly to Soma.
  • In the rite, stalks are pressed, the juice is filtered through wool, mixed with milk and offered; priests drink it too.
  • Effects described: inspiration, strength, visions, a sense of immortality (Rig Veda 8.48.3, paraphrase: the worshippers have drunk soma, become immortal and reached the light).
  • The plant is unknown today: suggestions include ephedra and a mushroom; no theory is agreed.
  • Later, Soma is identified with the moon, and substitute plants are used in rites.

Mission 1

Other gods of the Rig Veda

  • Varuna: guardian of rta and of oaths; he sees wrongdoing and can forgive.
  • Mitra: god of agreements and friendship, often paired with Varuna.
  • Surya and Savitr: the sun. The Gayatri mantra (Rig Veda 3.62.10) is addressed to Savitr.
  • Ushas: goddess of the dawn; one of several goddesses, with Aditi and Vac (speech).
  • Rudra: a fierce god of storms and healing, later linked with Shiva.
  • Vishnu: a minor god famed for his three strides across the universe (Rig Veda 1.154).
  • Tradition speaks of 33 gods.
Mission 1Read the source

One being, many names

Rig Veda 1.164.46 (paraphrase): They call it Indra, Mitra, Varuna, Agni, and the heavenly bird. The One Being the wise call by many names. (Sanskrit: ekam sad vipra bahudha vadanti.)

Rig Veda 10.129, the Hymn of Creation (paraphrase): Who really knows how creation came about? The gods came after it. Perhaps the one who watches over it in the highest heaven knows, or perhaps he does not know.

Q1 What does 1.164.46 suggest about the many gods?

Show answer

They may be names for one reality.

Q2 How is 10.129 different from hymns praising Agni or Indra?

Show answer

It asks questions rather than praising; it doubts that anyone, even a god, knows the origin.

Mission 1

Different ideas of God in the Rig Veda

  • Polytheism: many gods, each with a role (Agni, Indra, Varuna…).
  • Henotheism: Max Müller (1823–1900) noticed that each hymn praises its god as if supreme, though others are praised elsewhere.
  • Monism: one reality behind all names (1.164.46), developed later in the Upanishads as Brahman.
  • Cosmic person: the universe as the body of Purusha (10.90).
  • Questioning: the Hymn of Creation (10.129) leaves the origin open.
  • So the Rig Veda already holds differing ideas about God and gods: one reason Hindus today can be both polytheist and monist.
Mission 1

Vedic and modern deities

  • Agni: still central. In weddings the couple walk around the fire with Agni as witness; the havan (fire offering) is common, and the Arya Samaj makes it its main worship.
  • Indra: now a lesser king of heaven. In the Puranas, Krishna lifts Mount Govardhan to shelter villagers from Indra's storm.
  • Soma: no longer drunk; Soma is the moon god.
  • Vishnu and Rudra-Shiva: minor in the Rig Veda, now supreme for Vaishnavas and Shaivas.
  • Goddesses: Ushas and Aditi were minor; Devi (Shakti) is supreme for Shaktas.
  • Vedic mantras are still recited in samskaras (life-cycle rites), and the Gayatri mantra daily by many Hindus.

Mission 1Evaluate

Do the Vedic gods still matter?

  • Claim: The gods of the Rig Veda have little importance for Hindus today.
  • For: Indra and Varuna are rarely worshipped; daily puja centres on Vishnu, Shiva, Devi, Ganesha and avatars.
  • For: Many Hindus know the Vedic hymns mainly as recited sound, not for their content.
  • Against: Agni is present at weddings, havan and cremation; the Gayatri mantra is recited daily.
  • Against: The Vedic idea of one reality with many names (1.164.46) shapes how Hindus understand all their deities.
  • Judgement criteria: Importance as objects of worship, or as ideas? For which Hindus (Arya Samaj, Vaishnava, Smarta)? Ritual presence or personal devotion?

Mission 2: How can Hindus say God is beyond every word and also worship God with flowers in a temple?

Make a guess: How can Hindus say God is beyond every word and also worship God with flowers in a temple?

Mission 2

Brahman

  • Brahman: the one ultimate reality, the source and ground of everything. From a root meaning "to grow, expand": the "great" or "vast".
  • Not a god among gods: all gods, people and things depend on Brahman.
  • Both transcendent (beyond the world) and immanent (within it).
  • Isha Upanishad 1 (Paramananda translation, 1919): "All this, whatsoever exists in the universe, should be covered by the Lord."
  • Hindus hold two ways of seeing Brahman, often together: nirguna and saguna.
  • Not to be confused with Brahma (the creator god) or a Brahmin (priestly varna).
Mission 2

Nirguna and saguna

  • Guna: quality. Nir: without. Sa: with.
  • Nirguna Brahman: Brahman without qualities: beyond form, name, description and thought. Impersonal.
  • Saguna Brahman: Brahman with qualities: a personal God who loves, creates, protects and can be worshipped, e.g. as Vishnu, Shiva or the Goddess.
  • Many Hindus say these are not two Brahmans but one reality seen in two ways.
  • Schools differ: Advaita (non-dualist Vedanta, following Shankara) treats nirguna Brahman as the highest truth; Ramanuja, Madhva and many Vaishnavas hold that Brahman is always a personal God with qualities (for them "nirguna" means free of any bad quality).
  • An image often used: water and ice. The same substance, formless or in form.
  • Your spec calls nirguna Brahman "nothingness": not that Brahman does not exist, but that it is no thing: nothing that can be named or pictured.
Mission 2Read the source

Kena Upanishad 1:3–8

Kena Upanishad (Swami Paramananda's translation, 1919):

> "There the eye does not go, nor speech, nor mind. We do not know That; we do not understand how It can be taught." (1:3)

> "That which cannot be thought by mind, but by which, they say, mind is able to think: know that alone to be the Brahman, not this which people worship here." (1:5; 1:6 in most editions)

  • In this translation, verses 1:4–8 repeat the pattern for speech, mind, eye, ear and breath (1:5–9 in most editions).

Q1 Why can't the eye see Brahman?

Show answer

Brahman is what makes seeing possible; it is the "eye of the eye", not an object.

Q2 What does "not this which people worship here" mean?

Show answer

Any form people worship is not the whole of Brahman, which lies beyond every form.

Mission 2

Neti neti: not this, not this

  • The Brihadaranyaka Upanishad (2.3.6, paraphrase) describes Brahman as "not this, not this" (neti neti); the sage Yajnavalkya repeats it (e.g. 4.4.22).
  • The via negativa: say what Brahman is not, since any positive word would limit it.
  • Not a body, not the senses, not the mind, not any object: every description falls short.
  • The same idea as Kena 1:3: "There the eye does not go, nor speech, nor mind."
  • Criticism: if nothing can be said, how can anyone know or love Brahman? Saguna answers this.

Mission 2

Why both matter: Brahman in deities, world and scripture

  • Deities: for most Hindus, Vishnu, Shiva, the Goddess and others are forms of saguna Brahman. A worshipper may treat one as supreme (an ishta-devata, chosen deity).
  • Images: in a murti, many Hindus believe God is truly present, so it is bathed, dressed and offered food. Some Hindus (e.g. the Arya Samaj) reject image worship.
  • World: Brahman pervades all things (Isha 1), so rivers, cows and people can be honoured as sacred.
  • Scripture: the Vedas are seen as Brahman in sound (shabda-brahman); the syllable Om stands for Brahman: "This Word is indeed Brahman" (Katha 2:16, Paramananda).
  • Importance: saguna Brahman makes bhakti possible; nirguna Brahman is the goal of jnana and meditation. Many Hindus worship forms while believing Brahman is beyond them.
Mission 2Evaluate

Which matters more for Hindus?

  • Claim: Saguna Brahman is more important for most Hindus than nirguna Brahman.
  • For: Daily puja, festivals and temples centre on personal forms of God; bhakti is very widely followed.
  • For: A God with qualities can be loved, prayed to and trusted.
  • For: Vishishtadvaita (Ramanuja) and Dvaita (Madhva) hold that Brahman is in truth personal, not nirguna.
  • Against: Kena 1:3–8 says the forms worshipped "here" are not Brahman itself; for Advaita, the final truth is nirguna.
  • Against: Advaita Hindus and many sannyasis aim at knowledge of nirguna Brahman.
  • Against: Many Hindus see the two as inseparable: worship of forms leads to the formless.
  • Judgement criteria: Important for belief or practice? For which Hindus? Can either be understood without the other?
  • 15-mark (AO2) tip: reach a judgement that follows from the reasons you have weighed.

Mission 3

One reality, three functions

  • Trimurti: "three forms". Three functions of saguna Brahman (God with qualities):
  • Brahma: creator. Vishnu: preserver (sustainer). Shiva: destroyer (and transformer).
  • The three work as a cycle: what is created is sustained, then dissolved, then created again.
  • For many Hindus they are not three separate gods competing but three faces of the one divine power behind the universe.
  • In Hindu belief time is a wheel, not a straight line: the cycle repeats over vast ages. One "day of Brahma" is traditionally 4.32 billion years, and an equal night follows.
  • The idea appears in later texts such as the Maitri Upanishad and the Puranas, more than in the Vedas.
Mission 3

Brahma, the creator

  • Hindus believe Brahma creates the universe at the start of each cycle and is the source of the Vedas.
  • Often shown with four heads (facing the four directions, linked with the four Vedas), sitting on a lotus.
  • Holds a water pot, prayer beads and a book; his partner is Saraswati, goddess of learning; his vehicle a swan (hamsa).
  • In Vaishnava stories he is born from a lotus growing from Vishnu's navel.
  • Today he is rarely worshipped: very few temples (the best known at Pushkar, Rajasthan).
  • Puranic stories explain this; another reason often given: creation is complete for this cycle, so devotion turns to the God who sustains and saves.
Mission 3

Vishnu, the preserver

  • In Hindu belief Vishnu sustains the universe and protects dharma (right order).
  • Shown with four arms holding a conch (shankha), a discus (chakra), a mace (gada) and a lotus (padma); often blue-skinned.
  • Rests on the serpent Ananta (Shesha) on the cosmic ocean between cycles.
  • Partner: Lakshmi, goddess of good fortune; vehicle: the eagle Garuda.
  • Comes down as an avatar when dharma declines: e.g. Rama and Krishna (see Mission 4).
  • For Vaishnavas, Vishnu is the Supreme God, not only one of three.
Mission 3

Shiva, the destroyer

  • In Hindu belief Shiva dissolves the universe at the end of each cycle, so that it can be renewed: destruction as transformation.
  • Also destroys ignorance and ego: a great yogi, meditating on Mount Kailash.
  • Nataraja, "lord of the dance": dancing in a ring of fire; drum (creation), flame (destruction), raised hand (protection), foot on a dwarf (ignorance).
  • Other symbols: trident, third eye, crescent moon, the River Ganga in his hair; worshipped often as the lingam.
  • Partner: Parvati; vehicle: the bull Nandi.
  • For Shaivas, Shiva is the Supreme God who performs all cosmic acts.

Brahma's day and night

Bhagavad Gita 8:17–18 (Edwin Arnold's verse translation, 1885):

> "If ye know Brahma's Day

> Which is a thousand Yugas; if ye know

> The thousand Yugas making Brahma's Night,"

> "When that vast Dawn doth break, th' Invisible

> Is brought anew into the Visible;"

  • Verses 8:18–19 go on: at Brahma's night all "Fades back again to Him Who sent it forth"; at his dawn, living things rise again.

Q1 How long is one day of Brahma, according to the Gita?

Show answer

A thousand yugas.

Q2 What happens at Brahma's dawn and night?

Show answer

At dawn the universe comes forth; at night it is dissolved: the cycle repeats.

Mission 3

Many deities, one Brahman

  • Hindus worship many deities: Ganesha, Hanuman, Lakshmi, Saraswati, Murugan, Durga and more.
  • For most Hindus these are forms or aspects of the one Brahman, not rival gods.
  • In the Gita (11:15), Arjuna sees within Krishna's universal form "Brahma, sitting lone / Upon His lotus-throne" (Arnold): the gods are all contained in the one.
  • Ishta-devata: a person's chosen deity, worshipped as supreme for them.
  • Smarta Hindus worship five deities (Vishnu, Shiva, the Goddess, Surya, Ganesha) as equal forms of Brahman.

Mission 3Diversity

The Trimurti seen from each tradition

  • Vaishnavas: Vishnu is the Supreme; Brahma is born from his navel; Shiva acts under his authority, or is his devotee.
  • Shaivas: Shiva is the Supreme, who performs all cosmic acts (in Shaiva Siddhanta, five: creation, preservation, destruction, concealment and grace); Brahma and Vishnu are his agents.
  • Shaktas: the Goddess (Devi) is the Supreme power who brings forth Brahma, Vishnu and Shiva.
  • So for many Hindus the Trimurti is one way of picturing the divine, not the centre of their faith.
  • Art reflects this: the famous three-headed figure in the Elephanta Caves (6th century) shows Shiva in three aspects, not Brahma, Vishnu and Shiva.
Mission 3Evaluate

How important is the Trimurti?

  • Claim: The Trimurti is central to Hindu belief and practice.
  • For: It explains the cycle of creation, preservation and destruction in one picture of saguna Brahman.
  • For: It links cosmic time with samsara and the hope of liberation; it teaches that all deities are one.
  • Against: Hardly anyone worships "the Trimurti"; Brahma has very few temples.
  • Against: Vaishnavas, Shaivas and Shaktas each place one deity above the three; the concept is late and appears little in the Vedas.
  • Against: Other concepts, such as Brahman, atman, karma and dharma, shape daily life more.
  • Judgement criteria: Central to belief, or to worship? For which tradition? Compared with which concepts?

Mission 4

Avatar: a descent

  • Avatar (Sanskrit avatara): "descent", a "crossing down".
  • God, especially Vishnu, the preserver (see Mission 3), comes into the world in a form.
  • Why: to restore dharma when it declines, protect the good and remove evil.
  • Not a reincarnation: an avatar is not driven by karma; God descends freely, out of compassion.
  • Most Vaishnavas believe the avatar is fully divine, not a human who became holy.
Mission 4Read the source

"from age to age"

Bhagavad Gita 4:7–8 (Edwin Arnold's verse translation, 1885), Krishna speaks:

> "When Righteousness

> Declines, O Bharata! when Wickedness

> Is strong, I rise, from age to age, and take

> Visible shape, and move a man with men,"

  • 4:8 continues: "Succouring the good, thrusting the evil back, / And setting Virtue on her seat again."

Q1 What does "from age to age" suggest about how often avatars come?

Show answer

Again and again, in each cosmic age, whenever dharma declines.

Q2 What does "move a man with men" tell us?

Show answer

God lives among people in a human form: close enough to be known and loved.

Mission 4

Rama: the account

  • Prince Rama of Ayodhya, eldest son of King Dasharatha; seventh avatar of Vishnu (Valmiki's Ramayana;).
  • Exiled for 14 years to keep his father's promise to Queen Kaikeyi; Sita and Lakshmana go with him.
  • The demon-king Ravana abducts Sita; with Hanuman and the monkey army, Rama defeats Ravana in Lanka.
  • Rama returns to rule: Rama-rajya, the ideal just kingdom; celebrated at Diwali and Rama Navami.
  • Retold in Tulsidas's Ramcharitmanas (16th century), where Rama is clearly God, and in Ramlila plays.
  • Devotees repeat his name, Ram, as a mantra.
Mission 4

Rama: the ideal man and many dharmas

  • Maryada purushottama: "the best of men, who keeps the limits".
  • Son (putra dharma): accepts exile at once to keep his father's word.
  • Brother: loyalty to Lakshmana and Bharata, who rules only as his regent.
  • Husband (pati dharma): crosses the ocean and fights a war to rescue Sita.
  • King (raja dharma): rules for the people's good, even at personal cost.
  • Clash of dharmas: hearing his people doubt Sita's purity, Rama, as king, sends her away. Many Hindus find this deeply painful; some say the later book (Uttara Kanda) is an addition; others see the sacrifice of a king's private happiness.
  • Other debated episodes: killing Vali from hiding; the punishment of the Shudra ascetic Shambuka (Uttara Kanda).
Mission 4

Krishna

  • Eighth avatar of Vishnu; for many Vaishnavas the Supreme God himself.
  • Born in Mathura in a prison; raised among cowherds in Vrindavan by Yashoda and Nanda.
  • Stories of the child: stealing butter, defeating the serpent Kaliya, lifting Mount Govardhan to shelter the villagers.
  • The youth: playing the flute; dancing with the gopis (cowherd women), especially Radha: a picture of the soul's love for God.
  • The adult: king of Dwarka; guide of the Pandavas; teacher of the Gita to Arjuna.
  • Sources: the Mahabharata, the Harivamsha, and the Bhagavata Purana (book 10). Festival: Janmashtami.
Mission check

?A newspaper calls a firefighter who rescued children "an avatar of courage". A Vaishnava Hindu says the word has lost its religious meaning. What is missing from the newspaper's use?

In Hindu belief an avatar is not a hero who rises: it is God who comes down, to restore dharma and to be loved.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The four Vedas (Rig, Sama, Yajur, Atharva) were memorised and recited long before they were written.
  • Yajna (fire sacrifice) kept rta; Agni carried offerings, Indra won battles and released the waters, soma inspired.
  • The Rig Veda holds many ideas of God: many gods, each praised as supreme, one being with many names, and open questioning.
  • Agni and Vedic mantras remain central to ritual; Vishnu and Shiva rose from minor Vedic gods to supreme deities.

Mission 2

  • Brahman is the one ultimate reality: nirguna (without qualities, beyond words) and saguna (with qualities, a personal God).
  • Kena 1:3–8: Brahman is what makes speech, mind and senses work, "not this which people worship here"; neti neti: not this, not this.

Mission 3

  • In Hindu belief the Trimurti is three functions of saguna Brahman: Brahma creates, Vishnu preserves, Shiva destroys (and renews).
  • Many deities, one Brahman; Vaishnavas, Shaivas and Shaktas each see their deity as supreme over the three.

Mission 4

  • An avatar is a free descent of God, especially Vishnu, to restore dharma "from age to age" (Gita 4:7–8).
  • Rama is the ideal man, keeping dharma as son, brother, husband and king, though his choices, such as sending Sita away, are debated.
  • Krishna is loved as child, lover and teacher; for many he is God himself.
