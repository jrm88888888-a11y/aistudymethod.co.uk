Mission 1

What the Kingdom of God is

  • Greek basileia tou theou: God's reign or rule, more than a place.
  • Jewish background: God is king of all (e.g. Psalm 145:13); prophets hoped God would one day reign openly, putting the world right.
  • Luke uses the phrase more than 30 times: it is the heart of Jesus' preaching (4:43; 8:1; 9:2).
  • In Luke the Kingdom brings reversal: the poor, the sick, sinners and outsiders are welcomed (6:20; 14:21).
  • The key question: when does this reign arrive?
Mission 1

Parables in Luke

  • A parable (Greek parabolē, comparison): a short story or image from everyday life that points to the Kingdom.
  • Characteristics: ordinary settings (farming, feasts, money, family); a twist that surprises; an open ending that demands a response.
  • Purpose: to teach, to provoke decision, and to reveal or conceal: "To you it is given to know the mysteries of God's Kingdom, but to the rest it is given in parables" (8:10).
  • Interpretation has changed over time:
  • Early and medieval Church: allegory: every detail has a hidden meaning.
  • Adolf Jülicher (1857–1938), Die Gleichnisreden Jesu (1888–99): one main point per parable.
  • C. H. Dodd (1884–1973), The Parables of the Kingdom (1935): parables announce a crisis: the Kingdom is here now.
Mission 1

The Sower (8:4–15)

  • Seed falls on four soils: path (eaten by birds), rock (no root), thorns (choked), good soil (fruit "a hundred times").
  • Luke gives the explanation: "The seed is the word of God." (8:11)
  • Path: the devil takes the word away. Rock: faith fails in testing. Thorns: "choked with cares, riches, and pleasures of life" (8:14). Good soil: those who "hold it tightly, and produce fruit with perseverance" (8:15).
  • Luke's emphasis: perseverance, and the danger of riches (a Lukan theme).
  • Kingdom meaning: the Kingdom is already being sown in Jesus' preaching, with a great harvest to come. Small beginnings, sure end.

The Great Banquet (14:12–24)

> "Blessed is he who will feast in God's Kingdom!" (Luke 14:15, WEBBE)

> "Go out quickly into the streets and lanes of the city, and bring in the poor, maimed, blind, and lame." (Luke 14:21, WEBBE)

  • A guest at a Pharisee's meal speaks of the future feast of the Kingdom (Isaiah 25:6). Jesus replies with a story.
  • The invited guests make excuses (field, oxen, marriage). The host fills his house with the poor, then with people from "the highways and hedges" (14:23).
  • The servant was first sent to say "Come, for everything is ready now" (14:17); after the refusals the host is angry (14:21) and the servant reports "there is still room" (14:22).
  • Just before (14:12–14): invite "the poor, the maimed, the lame, or the blind" to your own feasts.

Mission 1

Already here

  • "If I by God's finger cast out demons, then God's Kingdom has come to you." (11:20)
  • "God's Kingdom is within you." (17:21) The Greek entos hymōn can mean "within you" or "among you", in Jesus himself.
  • "The law and the prophets were until John. From that time the Good News of God's Kingdom is preached" (16:16): a new era has begun.
  • The disciples are sent to say "God's Kingdom has come near to you" (10:9).
  • Present-tense texts point to the Kingdom arriving in Jesus' ministry: healings, exorcisms, welcome of sinners.
Mission 1

Not yet

  • "May your Kingdom come." (11:2) The disciples still pray for it.
  • "There are some of those who stand here who will in no way taste of death until they see God's Kingdom." (9:27)
  • "This generation will not pass away until all things are accomplished." (21:32)
  • At the Last Supper Jesus will not drink wine again "until God's Kingdom comes" (22:18).
  • People "from the east, west, north, and south" will feast in it (13:29): a future gathering.
  • Future-tense texts point to a final coming, with judgement and the Son of Man (17:24).

Mission 2

The Sermon on the Plain

  • Luke 6:17–49. Jesus "stood on a level place" (6:17) with disciples and crowds, including people from Gentile Tyre and Sidon.
  • Matthew's longer Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5–7) shares much of the material.
  • Shape of Luke's sermon:
  • 4 blessings and 4 woes (6:20–26).
  • Love your enemies (6:27–36).
  • Do not judge (6:37–42).
  • A tree and its fruit (6:43–45); house on the rock (6:46–49): hearing must become doing.
Mission 2Read the source

Blessings and woes

> "Blessed are you who are poor, for God's Kingdom is yours." (Luke 6:20, WEBBE)

> "But woe to you who are rich! For you have received your consolation." (Luke 6:24, WEBBE)

  • Matthew: "Blessed are the poor in spirit" (Matthew 5:3). Luke has material poverty, and addresses the hearers directly ("you").
  • Only Luke has the woes: each blessing is matched by a reversal (poor/rich, hungry/full, weeping/laughing, rejected/praised).

Q1 What does the woe say the rich have already received?

Show answer

Their "consolation": comfort now, not later.

Q2 Why might Luke's version be called a reversal?

Show answer

Those at the bottom now are lifted up in the Kingdom; those at the top now are brought down: compare Mary's song, 1:52–53.

Mission 2

Love your enemies (Luke 6:27–36)

  • "Love your enemies, do good to those who hate you, bless those who curse you, and pray for those who mistreat you." (6:27–28)
  • Offer the other cheek; give to everyone who asks (6:29–30).
  • Golden Rule: "As you would like people to do to you, do exactly so to them." (6:31)
  • Beyond reciprocity: "If you love those who love you, what credit is that to you?" (6:32). Lend "expecting nothing back" (6:35).
  • Reason: imitate God, who "is kind towards the unthankful and evil" (6:35).
  • Climax: "Therefore be merciful, even as your Father is also merciful." (6:36)
  • Matthew 5:38–48 has the same core (love your enemies, 5:44; the other cheek, 5:39) but ends: "be perfect, just as your Father in heaven is perfect" (5:48); Luke ends with mercy.

Mission 2

Jesus and the Judaism of his time

  • Jesus' ethics grow from the Hebrew Scriptures; many scholars stress continuity.
  • Neighbour and stranger: "you shall love your neighbour as yourself" (Leviticus 19:18); love the foreigner "as yourself" (Leviticus 19:34).
  • Enemies: return your enemy's stray ox (Exodus 23:4); "If your enemy is hungry, give him food to eat" (Proverbs 25:21).
  • The poor: "open your hand… to your needy, and to your poor" (Deuteronomy 15:11); the prophets condemn oppression of the poor (e.g. Amos 2:6–7).
  • Golden Rule: "what you yourself hate, do to no man" (Tobit 4:15); Rabbi Hillel taught the same in negative form (Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 31a, paraphrase). Jesus states it positively.
  • Distinctive emphases: love of enemies as a command for all; mercy modelled on God; reversal of rich and poor.
  • Note: "hate your enemy" (Matthew 5:43) is not a command in the Torah; it describes a popular attitude.
Mission 2

The Rich Man: three versions

  • A man asks how to inherit eternal life; he has kept the commandments. Jesus: sell, give to the poor, follow me. He leaves sad.
  • Mark 10:17–31: "Jesus looking at him loved him" (10:21): only Mark.
  • Matthew 19:16–30: a "young man"; "If you want to be perfect, go, sell what you have" (19:21).
  • Luke 18:18–30: a "ruler"; "Sell all that you have and distribute it to the poor" (18:22).
  • All three: "easier for a camel to enter in through a needle's eye" (Luke 18:25); "The things which are impossible with men are possible with God." (18:27)
  • Peter: "we have left everything" (18:28): followers receive "many times more" now and eternal life (18:30).
Mission 2

The Rich Fool and riches in Heaven

  • Rich Fool (Luke 12:13–21, only Luke): a dispute over inheritance prompts: "a man's life doesn't consist of the abundance of the things which he possesses" (12:15).
  • A farmer builds bigger barns; God says: "You foolish one, tonight your soul is required of you." (12:20)
  • Point: "So is he who lays up treasure for himself, and is not rich towards God." (12:21) His fault is self-centred security, not farming.
  • Riches in Heaven (Luke 12:32–34; Matthew 6:19–21): "Sell what you have and give gifts to the needy" (Luke 12:33).
  • Both versions: "where your treasure is, there will your heart be also" (Luke 12:34).
Mission 2

The poor, the oppressed and the blind

  • Warning and the Widow's Offering (Luke 20:45–21:4; Mark 12:38–44): scribes "who devour widows' houses" (20:47); then a poor widow gives two small coins: "out of her poverty, put in all that she had to live on" (21:4).
  • Two readings: praise of total generosity; or (read with 20:47) a lament that the system takes a widow's last coins.
  • Blind beggar: Mark names Bartimaeus, leaving Jericho (10:46); Matthew has two blind men (20:30); Luke an unnamed man as Jesus approaches Jericho (18:35). He cries "son of David, have mercy on me!"; healed, he "followed him, glorifying God" (18:43).
  • The poor (Luke 14:12–14): invite "the poor, the maimed, the lame, or the blind", because "they don't have the resources to repay you" (14:14).

Mission 2Diversity

How Christians read the wealth texts

  • Literal renunciation: e.g. St Antony of Egypt and St Francis of Assisi gave all away after hearing these texts; monks and nuns take vows of poverty; Hutterite and Bruderhof communities share goods (Acts 2:44–45).
  • Counsel, not command: Catholic teaching (following Aquinas) treats "sell all" as a counsel of perfection for some; all are bound to detachment and care for the poor.
  • Stewardship: many Protestants see wealth as entrusted by God: John Wesley, "Gain all you can, save all you can, give all you can" ("The Use of Money", 1760).
  • Liberation theology: God's preferential option for the poor; the texts call for changing unjust structures.
  • Prosperity teaching (some Pentecostal and charismatic churches) sees wealth as God's blessing; critics within Christianity say it ignores Luke's woes (6:24).
Mission 2Evaluate

Is Jesus' ethic too demanding to live by?

  • Claim: Jesus' teaching on enemies and wealth in Luke is too demanding to be a practical guide.
  • For: Literal non-retaliation could leave the vulnerable unprotected; no state could govern by it.
  • For: Schweitzer called it an interim ethic for a short time before the end (see Mission 1).
  • For: Reinhold Niebuhr (1892–1971), An Interpretation of Christian Ethics (1935): an "impossible" ideal that judges and inspires, but cannot be applied directly.
  • Against: People live it: e.g. the Amish community at Nickel Mines (2006) forgave the man who killed their children; many Christians fund food banks and give sacrificially.
  • Against: Matera and Marshall: it is a response to grace, not a law to earn salvation; God's mercy makes it possible ("possible with God", 18:27).
  • Against: Secular ethics echo it: Peter Singer (b. 1946), "Famine, Affluence, and Morality" (1972), argues the comfortable have strong duties to the poor.
  • Judgement criteria: Is it a rule or an ideal? For individuals or for states? Does it assume an imminent end?

Mission 3

Parables of mercy

  • Three of Luke's best-known parables are found only in Luke: the Good Samaritan, the Forgiving Father (often called the Prodigal Son), and the Rich Man and Lazarus.
  • Each turns on mercy (compassion acted out) and each reverses expectations.
  • Key word: the Samaritan and the father are both "moved with compassion" (10:33; 15:20): the same Greek verb.
  • Luke's setting matters: the parables answer questions or complaints (10:29; 15:2; 16:14).
Mission 3

The command to love

  • A lawyer asks: "what shall I do to inherit eternal life?" (10:25).
  • In Luke, the lawyer answers, joining two Torah texts: love God (Deuteronomy 6:5) and "your neighbour as yourself" (Leviticus 19:18). Jesus: "Do this, and you will live." (10:28)
  • In Mark 12:28–34 and Matthew 22:34–40, Jesus gives the two commands, as the greatest commandments.
  • Mark: the scribe agrees and is "not far from God's Kingdom" (Mark 12:34). Matthew: "The whole law and the prophets depend on these two commandments." (Matthew 22:40)
  • Luke's twist: the lawyer then asks, "Who is my neighbour?" (10:29). The parable answers.

The Good Samaritan (10:30–37)

> "But a certain Samaritan, as he travelled, came where he was. When he saw him, he was moved with compassion," (Luke 10:33, WEBBE)

> "He said, 'He who showed mercy on him.' Then Jesus said to him, 'Go and do likewise.'" (Luke 10:37, WEBBE)

  • A man is beaten on the Jerusalem–Jericho road. A priest and a Levite pass by. A Samaritan binds his wounds, takes him to an inn, and pays.

Mission 3

Why a Samaritan?

  • Samaritans worshipped the God of Israel at Mount Gerizim and accepted only the Torah. Jews and Samaritans had centuries of hostility (cf. 9:52–53).
  • The priest and Levite may have feared ritual impurity from a corpse (Leviticus 21:1); the text gives no reason. Many scholars note the Law also commands saving life.
  • The surprise: the despised outsider fulfils the Law's command to love.
  • The question shifts from "whom must I love?" (a boundary) to "how do I become a neighbour?" (an action with no boundary).
  • For Christians: love has no ethnic or religious limit.
Mission 3

The Lost (Luke 15)

  • Setting: tax collectors and sinners come to listen; Pharisees and scribes murmur: "This man welcomes sinners, and eats with them." (15:2)
  • Three parables with the same pattern: lost → found → joy.
  • Lost sheep: one of a hundred; the shepherd searches.
  • Lost coin: one of ten; a woman searches the house (often read as an image of God searching).
  • Lost son: one of two (15:11–32).
  • "There will be more joy in heaven over one sinner who repents, than over ninety-nine righteous people" (15:7).
Mission 3

The Forgiving Father and the elder brother

  • Setting (15:1–2): Pharisees and scribes grumble that Jesus welcomes sinners and eats with them.
  • The younger son takes his inheritance, wastes it, feeds pigs (unclean for a Jew), and "came to himself" (15:17).
  • "While he was still far off, his father saw him and was moved with compassion, and ran, fell on his neck, and kissed him." (15:20) Many commentators note that running was undignified for an older man in that culture: mercy before any speech.
  • Robe, ring, sandals, fattened calf: restored as son, not servant.
  • The elder brother refuses to come in: "I never disobeyed a commandment of yours" (15:29).
  • The father goes out to him too: "Son, you are always with me" (15:31).
  • The story ends open: does the elder brother join the feast? Luke's setting (15:2) suggests the grumblers are invited to decide.

Mission 3

Mercy to sinners

  • The woman who was a sinner (Luke 7:36–50): at a Pharisee's meal she weeps on Jesus' feet and anoints them. The host, Simon, thinks a prophet would know "what kind of woman this is" (7:39).
  • Jesus tells a short parable of two debtors: "her sins, which are many, are forgiven, for she loved much" (7:47). "Your faith has saved you. Go in peace." (7:50)
  • Zacchaeus (19:1–10): "a chief tax collector, and he was rich" (19:2). Jesus invites himself to stay; the crowd murmur.
  • Zacchaeus gives half his goods to the poor and repays fourfold (19:8). "Today, salvation has come to this house… the Son of Man came to seek and to save that which was lost." (19:9–10)
  • Pattern: welcome first, change follows.
Mission 3

The Rich Man and Lazarus (16:19–31)

  • A rich man in purple and fine linen; at his gate a beggar, Lazarus (apart from Abraham, the only character in Jesus' parables given a name; it means "God helps").
  • Both die. Lazarus is carried "to Abraham's bosom"; the rich man is "in anguish in this flame" (16:22, 24).
  • Reversal: "you, in your lifetime, received your good things, and Lazarus… bad things" (16:25): the woes of 6:24 told as a story.
  • A "great gulf" is fixed (16:26). His brothers have "Moses and the prophets" (16:29).
  • "If they don't listen to Moses and the prophets, neither will they be persuaded if one rises from the dead." (16:31) Christians hear a hint of the resurrection.
  • Most scholars read it as a warning about wealth and neglect, not a map of the afterlife.

Mission 3Diversity

How the parables have been read

  • Allegory: Augustine (354–430), Questions on the Gospels II.19: the wounded man is Adam; the robbers, the devil; the Samaritan, Christ; the inn, the Church.
  • One main point: Jülicher (see Mission 1) and later historical critics: read each parable in Jesus' setting for its central challenge.
  • Social and liberation readings: the parables expose who is excluded and call for structural change (e.g. Luke 16 and global poverty).
  • Martin Luther King Jr (1929–1968), in his last speech (Memphis, 3 April 1968), turned the priest's question round: not "what will happen to me if I stop?" but "what will happen to him if I don't?" (paraphrase).
  • Jewish scholarship: Amy-Jill Levine (b. 1956), Short Stories by Jesus (2014), warns against reading the priest, Levite or elder brother as symbols of a "legalistic" Judaism: the parables are Jewish stories told to Jews.
Mission 3

Judaism, and the parables today

  • Jewish roots: God is "merciful and gracious" (Exodus 34:6); God has no pleasure in the death of the wicked but wants them to "return… and live" (Ezekiel 18:23). Repentance (teshuvah) and welcome are central Jewish themes.
  • What stood out: Jesus' table fellowship with sinners before repentance, and outsiders as heroes.
  • Codes of living: hospices, food banks and charities rooted in these texts (e.g. Christian Aid; the Trussell Trust); Catholic social teaching's option for the poor.
  • Equality: worth does not depend on ethnicity, status or wealth (Samaritan, Lazarus, the sinful woman).
  • Pluralism: the Samaritan held different beliefs, yet acted rightly; some Christians use this to support interfaith cooperation.
  • Secular views: humanists accept the ethic of the Good Samaritan without its religious basis; "Good Samaritan" laws in many countries protect those who help.
Mission 3Evaluate

Is mercy the heart of the believer's life?

  • Claim: Mercy is the most important quality in the life of a religious believer.
  • For: "Be merciful, even as your Father is also merciful" (Luke 6:36); the parables of mercy; "I desire mercy, and not sacrifice" (Hosea 6:6).
  • For: Christians are themselves forgiven, so must forgive (11:4). Pope Francis' Jubilee of Mercy (2015–16) made mercy the Church's focus.
  • For (outreach): Jesus sought "that which was lost" (19:10); outreach to the marginalised follows from this.
  • Against: Justice matters too: mercy without justice can excuse wrongdoers or ignore victims (the elder brother's complaint has some force).
  • Against: Some stress faith or holiness as primary; others love of God (the first commandment) over love of neighbour.
  • Against (outreach): Outreach can be paternalistic or used mainly to win converts; some argue change must come from the marginalised themselves.
  • Judgement criteria: Can mercy and justice be separated? Is mercy a quality, or the sum of the others? Who decides who is "marginalised"?

Mission 4

What Luke calls a miracle

  • Luke's main word is dunameis: acts of power (e.g. Luke 10:13; 19:37).
  • In Luke the power comes from God through the Spirit: "The power of the Lord was with him to heal" (5:17).
  • A common modern definition treats healing and raising to life as contrary to the laws of nature. Luke himself speaks of God's power, not of natural laws.
  • But the Gospel's interest is less "how?" than "what does it show?"
  • Four kinds in this unit: healing, raising to life, nature miracle, feeding.
Mission 4

Purpose and theology

  • Signs of the Kingdom: "if I by God's finger cast out demons, then God's Kingdom has come to you" (11:20).
  • Fulfilment: Jesus answers John's question with a list from Isaiah: "the blind receive their sight, the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed…" (7:22). The Nazareth programme (4:18) in action.
  • Faith: miracles respond to faith and call for it ("Your faith has healed you", 17:19).
  • Compassion: Jesus acts from compassion (7:13); Luke stresses his care for widows, outsiders and the unclean.
  • Identity: the crowd asks "Who is this?" (8:25). Miracles point to who Jesus is.
Mission 4

The Centurion's Servant (7:1–10)

  • A centurion (an army officer, probably serving Herod Antipas in Capernaum), a Gentile, whose servant is "at the point of death" (7:2).
  • Unique to Luke: Jewish elders plead for him ("he loves our nation, and he built our synagogue for us", 7:5). He sends friends rather than coming himself.
  • "I am not worthy for you to come under my roof… say the word" (7:6–7): he trusts Jesus' word at a distance.
  • Jesus: "I have not found such great faith, no, not in Israel." (7:9)
  • Theology: for Luke, faith, not ancestry, opens the way to salvation; a preview of the Gentile mission in Acts (Acts 10, another centurion).
  • Catholic Mass echoes his words before Communion: "Lord, I am not worthy that you should enter under my roof…"
Mission 4

The Ten Lepers (17:11–19)

  • Ten men with leprosy stand at a distance (Leviticus 13:46: those with the disease lived outside the camp) and cry "Jesus, Master, have mercy on us!" (17:13).
  • "Go and show yourselves to the priests" (17:14): the Law's procedure for a declared cure (Leviticus 14). They are cleansed as they go.
  • One returns, praising God: "and he was a Samaritan" (17:16).
  • "Where are the nine?" (17:17). "Your faith has healed you" (17:19): the Greek can also mean "saved you".
  • Theology: gratitude and worship; a double outsider (leper and Samaritan) receives more than physical healing.
Mission 4

The Widow's Son at Nain (7:11–17)

  • An only son, carried out dead; his mother a widow: without him she has no protector or income.
  • "When the Lord saw her, he had compassion on her" (7:13). No one asks: Jesus acts from compassion alone.
  • He touches the coffin (risking ritual impurity) and says "Young man, I tell you, arise!" (7:14).
  • "Then he gave him to his mother" (7:15): almost the words used of Elijah raising a widow's son ("delivered him to his mother", 1 Kings 17:23).
  • The crowd: "A great prophet has arisen amongst us!" and "God has visited his people!" (7:16).
  • Theology: Jesus as prophet like Elijah; God's care for the vulnerable; a sign of the future resurrection.

Mission 4

The Calming of the Storm (8:22–25)

  • A nature miracle: Jesus sleeps; the storm threatens; the disciples wake him: "Master, Master, we are dying!" (8:24).
  • He "rebuked the wind": the same verb Luke uses for driving out demons. Chaos is treated as an enemy.
  • "Where is your faith?" (8:25): Luke's version is gentler than Mark's ("How is it that you have no faith?", Mark 4:40).
  • "Who is this then, that he commands even the winds and the water, and they obey him?" (8:25)
  • In the Hebrew Bible only God rules the sea (Psalm 107:29). The question points to Jesus' identity.

The Feeding of the Five Thousand (9:10–17)

> "He took the five loaves and the two fish, and looking up to the sky, he blessed them, broke them, and gave them to the disciples to set before the multitude." (Luke 9:16, WEBBE)

> "They ate and were all filled. They gathered up twelve baskets of broken pieces that were left over." (Luke 9:17, WEBBE)

  • Four verbs: took, blessed, broke, gave: almost the same as the Last Supper (took, gave thanks, broke, gave: 22:19).
  • Echoes of Israel in the wilderness (manna, Exodus 16) and of Elisha feeding a hundred (2 Kings 4:42–44).
  • Twelve baskets: often linked to the twelve tribes and twelve apostles.

Mission 4

Credibility: the challenge

  • David Hume (1711–1776), An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding (1748), Section X: "no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous, than the fact, which it endeavours to establish".
  • Rationalist explanations: H. E. G. Paulus (1761–1851) read the feeding as people sharing food they had hidden; the storm as a natural lull.
  • Rudolf Bultmann (1884–1976), "New Testament and Mythology" (1941): miracle stories are myth expressing faith; the modern reader should demythologise them to find their message.
  • Form critics: miracle stories took shape in early preaching; their form tells us about the church's faith, not necessarily about events.
Mission 4

Credibility: responses

  • Traditional view (most Catholic, Orthodox and evangelical Christians): God can act beyond nature; the Gospels preserve eyewitness tradition (Luke 1:2).
  • Miracle stories are found in every layer of the Gospel tradition; many historians accept that Jesus was known as a healer and exorcist, whatever the explanation.
  • Symbolic reading (many liberal Christians): the meaning is true whether or not the event happened as told; e.g. Jesus' power to feed people spiritually.
  • Psychosomatic explanation for some healings: real change, natural mechanism. It struggles with the raising and nature miracles.
  • Luke's own warning: signs do not force belief (16:31). Faith shapes how a miracle is seen.
Mission 4Evaluate

Are miracles valid and relevant today?

  • Claim: The miracles in Luke remain valid and relevant today.
  • For (validity): If God exists and is creator, acting in creation is possible (realist view).
  • For (validity): Christians report healing today (e.g. claims examined at Lourdes; Pentecostal healing ministry).
  • For (relevance): The meaning survives any doubt: compassion for outsiders, faith across boundaries, care for the hungry inspire Christian action.
  • Against (validity): Hume's test of testimony; no repeatable evidence; accounts written decades later by believers.
  • Against (validity): Selective miracles raise the problem of evil: why heal one servant and not millions?
  • Against (relevance): A demythologised miracle may lose what Luke claims: that God really acted.
  • Judgement criteria: What counts as evidence for a unique event? Can meaning be separated from history? Does the answer depend on prior belief in God?
  • Skill tip (part b, AO2): weigh validity and relevance separately; a strong answer can find one more convincing than the other.

Mission 5

What is a disciple?

  • Greek mathētēs: a learner who follows a teacher, in life as well as in lessons.
  • In Luke there are rings of followers: the Twelve (apostles); the seventy sent on mission (10:1); a wider group including women who travel with Jesus and support him "from their possessions" (8:1–3).
  • Discipleship in Luke is a journey: much teaching comes on the road to Jerusalem (9:51–19:27).
  • Five strands in this mission: call, mission, demands, teaching, and Peter.
Mission 5

The call of the fishermen (5:1–11)

  • Only in Luke: after a night with no catch, Simon obeys: "at your word I will let down the net" (5:5). The nets almost break.
  • Simon's response: "Depart from me, for I am a sinful man, Lord." (5:8) An encounter with holiness reveals unworthiness.
  • Jesus: "Don't be afraid. From now on you will be catching people alive." (5:10)
  • "They left everything, and followed him." (5:11)
  • Compare Mark 1:16–20: a simple call by the lake; Luke adds a miracle and Simon's confession.
Mission 5

Levi and the Twelve (5:27–32; 6:12–16)

  • Jesus calls Levi, a tax collector (in Galilee, collecting for Herod Antipas' regime), widely seen as a collaborator and a sinner: "Follow me!" He "left everything" (5:27–28).
  • Levi holds a great feast for tax collectors; Pharisees and scribes object. Jesus: "I have not come to call the righteous, but sinners, to repentance." (5:32)
  • The Twelve: Jesus "continued all night in prayer to God" (6:12), then chose twelve and "named apostles" (6:13): those sent.
  • Twelve recalls the twelve tribes: a renewed Israel.
  • The list ends: "Judas Iscariot, who also became a traitor" (6:16). Even the chosen can fail.

Mission 5

The nature of mission (9:1–6; 10:1–16)

  • The Twelve (9:1–6): given "power and authority" over demons and disease; sent "to preach God's Kingdom and to heal the sick" (9:2).
  • Travel light: "Take nothing for your journey" (9:3): total dependence on God and on hospitality.
  • Rejection is expected: "shake off even the dust from your feet" (9:5).
  • The seventy (10:1–16, only Luke): sent "two by two"; "The harvest is indeed plentiful, but the labourers are few" (10:2); "as lambs amongst wolves" (10:3).
  • Seventy (some manuscripts seventy-two) may recall the seventy nations of Genesis 10: a sign of the worldwide mission in Acts.
  • "Whoever listens to you listens to me" (10:16): disciples carry Jesus' authority.

Mission 5

Counting the cost (9:57–62; 14:25–33)

  • Three would-be followers (9:57–62): no home ("the Son of Man has no place to lay his head", 9:58); no delay to bury a father; no going back to say goodbye.
  • Family: whoever "doesn't disregard his own father, mother, wife, children… he can't be my disciple" (14:26). The Greek word is literally "hate": a Semitic idiom for "love less" (compare Matthew 10:37: loving family "more than me").
  • Carry the cross (14:27): readiness for rejection and even death.
  • Two short parables: a tower builder and a king going to war both "sit down first" and count the cost (14:28–32).
  • "Whoever of you who doesn't renounce all that he has, he can't be my disciple." (14:33)

Mission 5

Teachings to disciples

  • Prayer (11:1–13): Luke's shorter Lord's Prayer ("Give us day by day our daily bread", 11:3); the friend at midnight who gets bread through persistence (11:5–8); "keep asking… keep seeking… keep knocking" (11:9).
  • Luke 11:13: the Father will give "the Holy Spirit to those who ask him" (11:13). Matthew 7:11 has good things instead. Luke stresses the Holy Spirit.
  • Courage (12:8–12): confess Jesus openly; when on trial, "the Holy Spirit will teach you in that same hour what you must say" (12:12). Fulfilled in Acts.
  • Trust, not anxiety (12:22–34): look at the ravens and lilies; "seek God's Kingdom, and all these things will be added to you" (12:31); "Don't be afraid, little flock" (12:32).
Mission 5

Peter: failure and restoration

  • Warning (22:31–34): "Satan asked to have all of you, that he might sift you as wheat, but I prayed for you, that your faith wouldn't fail. You, when once you have turned again, establish your brothers." (22:31–32)
  • Peter boasts he is ready for prison and death; Jesus predicts three denials before the rooster crows.
  • Denial (22:54–62): Peter follows "from a distance" (22:54); three times denies knowing him.
  • Only Luke: "The Lord turned and looked at Peter." (22:61) Peter "went out, and wept bitterly." (22:62)
  • Luke is gentle with Peter: no "Get behind me, Satan!" after his confession (contrast Mark 8:33); Jesus prays for him; the risen Lord "has appeared to Simon" (24:34); Peter leads the Church in Acts 1–12.

Mission 5Evaluate

Can Luke's demands be lived in any age?

  • Claim: The demands of discipleship in Luke are too radical for Christians today.
  • For: "Renounce all that he has" (14:33) and "no place to lay his head" (9:58) fit wandering missionaries, not families with jobs.
  • For: Luke's urgency may reflect expectation of an imminent end (see Mission 1).
  • Against: Some live them literally: monks, nuns and friars take vows of poverty, chastity and obedience; missionaries leave home.
  • Against: Christians in many countries face persecution: "take up the cross" is literal for some.
  • Against: Dietrich Bonhoeffer (1906–1945), The Cost of Discipleship (1937): grace that asks nothing is "cheap grace"; real grace is costly because it calls us to follow. He was executed by the Nazis in 1945.
  • Against (interpretation): Luke's own community included supporters with homes and money (8:3; Acts 2:46); the demand is total priority, lived in different ways. The Catholic Church teaches a universal call to holiness for lay people too (Vatican II, Lumen Gentium, 1964).
  • Judgement criteria: Are the demands rules or priorities? For all, or for some? Does Peter's restoration show that failure is part of discipleship?
  • Skill tip (part b, AO2): evaluate, don't just list; weigh a literal and a priority reading, and reach a supported conclusion.

Mission 6

What is Salvation History?

  • German Heilsgeschichte: the belief that God acts in history to save, following a plan with a goal.
  • The term became widely used through 19th-century German theology (e.g. J. C. K. von Hofmann, 1810–1877).
  • Roots in the Hebrew Bible: God saves in events: the call of Abraham, the Exodus, the return from exile.
  • History is linear, not cyclical: promise → fulfilment → completion.
  • Applied to Luke: his two volumes (Luke–Acts) tell the story as one plan, from Adam (3:38) to Paul preaching in Rome (Acts 28:31).
Mission 6

Luke's portrayal (1): fulfilment and necessity

  • Fulfilment: Luke writes about "those matters which have been fulfilled amongst us" (1:1). The Infancy Narrative is full of prophecy fulfilled.
  • Divine necessity: the Greek word dei, "must": "I must be in my Father's house" (2:49); "I must preach the good news of God's Kingdom" (4:43); "The Son of Man must suffer" (9:22).
  • "Today": salvation arrives now: "there is born to you today… a Saviour" (2:11); "Today, this Scripture has been fulfilled" (4:21); "Today, salvation has come to this house" (19:9); "today you will be with me in Paradise" (23:43).
  • Dated in world history: "the fifteenth year of the reign of Tiberius Caesar" (3:1). God's plan runs through real politics.
Mission 6

Luke's portrayal (2): for all peoples

  • Simeon: "a light for revelation to the nations, and the glory of your people Israel" (2:32).
  • Luke extends the Isaiah quotation about John beyond Mark's version, to end: "All flesh will see God's salvation." (3:6)
  • Genealogy back to Adam (3:38), not only to Abraham (contrast Matthew 1:1–2): Jesus belongs to all humanity.
  • Geography as theology: Galilee → the journey to Jerusalem (9:51) → "Jerusalem… Judea and Samaria… the uttermost parts of the earth" (Acts 1:8).
  • Gentiles praised for faith (the centurion, 7:9); Samaritans as examples (10:33; 17:16).

  • The risen Jesus explains the whole plan: Scripture → suffering and resurrection (24:46) → preaching to the nations.

Mission 6

Conzelmann: three epochs

  • Hans Conzelmann (1915–1989), Die Mitte der Zeit (1954); English The Theology of St Luke (1960). ("The Middle of Time".)
  • Luke divides history into three epochs, using 16:16 ("The law and the prophets were until John…"):
  • 1. Israel: law and prophets, up to and including John the Baptist.
  • 2. Jesus: the middle of time, a unique period of salvation. Satan leaves "until another time" (4:13) and returns at 22:3: a "Satan-free" period.
  • 3. The Church: from the Ascension; a long time of witness under pressure, guided by the Spirit.
  • Conzelmann's explanation: Luke faced the delay of the parousia (Jesus' return). He replaced the hope of an imminent end with a history of salvation stretching forward.
Mission 6

Cullmann: Christ the midpoint

  • Oscar Cullmann (1902–1999), Christ and Time (1946; English 1950); Salvation in History (1965; English 1967).
  • Salvation History is the core of the whole New Testament, not a late Lukan invention.
  • Time is a line; Christ is its midpoint.
  • His analogy: the decisive battle of a war (D-Day) can be won before the war ends (V-Day). Christ's death and resurrection are the decisive victory; the final victory is still to come.
  • So "already" and "not yet" belong together, and the delay of the end is not a crisis.
Mission 6

Critique of Conzelmann

  • The birth stories: Paul Minear (1906–2007), "Luke's Use of the Birth Stories" (1966): Conzelmann's scheme starts at chapter 3 and ignores chapters 1–2, where John and Jesus belong together to the time of fulfilment.
  • John's place: Luke 16:16 is ambiguous ("until John… from that time"); John also announces the good news (3:18).
  • "Satan-free"? Jesus casts out demons throughout his ministry; a woman "whom Satan had bound" is freed (13:16).
  • Future hope survives: Luke keeps sayings of a near end (9:27; 21:32).
  • I. Howard Marshall (1934–2015), Luke: Historian and Theologian (1970): the key to Luke is salvation itself, not a scheme of periods; Luke is a historian and a theologian.
  • Many still credit Conzelmann with showing that Luke is a theologian, not just a chronicler.

Mission 6

Resurrection in the plan

  • In Luke the resurrection is necessary: "Didn't the Christ have to suffer these things and to enter into his glory?" (24:26).
  • In Acts, God "raised up" Jesus "because it was not possible that he should be held by" death (Acts 2:24).
  • It is the turning point of Salvation History: from Jesus' ministry to the Church's mission (24:47–49).
  • It is God's vindication of Jesus and the guarantee of judgement and hope: God "has given assurance to all men, in that he has raised him from the dead" (Acts 17:31).
  • Christians across the traditions confess it in the creeds; some liberal theologians understand it as a spiritual rather than bodily event.
Mission 6Diversity

Salvation claims in a multi-religious world

  • Exclusivism: salvation only through explicit faith in Christ: "There is salvation in no one else" (Acts 4:12). Held by many evangelical Christians.
  • Inclusivism: salvation is through Christ, but may reach those who do not know him: Karl Rahner (1904–1984), "anonymous Christians"; Catholic teaching since Vatican II (Lumen Gentium 16; Nostra Aetate, 1965). Luke–Acts: "in every nation he who fears him and works righteousness is acceptable to him" (Acts 10:35).
  • Pluralism: John Hick (1922–2012), God and the Universe of Faiths (1973): the great religions are different responses to one ultimate Reality; Christ is not the only way.
  • Other faiths on resurrection: traditional Jewish teaching hopes for the resurrection of the dead (Daniel 12:2) but does not accept Jesus as Messiah; most Muslims believe Jesus was not crucified but raised to God (Qur'an 4:157–158); Buddhists and Hindus frame liberation without a bodily resurrection.
  • Non-religious views: the resurrection is not historical; "salvation" language describes human hopes.
Mission 6Evaluate

Are Luke's claims still relevant in a multi-religious world?

  • Claim: The Gospel's claims about salvation and resurrection remain relevant in a multi-religious world.
  • For: If true, a universal claim matters for everyone: Luke presents salvation as offered to "all flesh" (3:6).
  • For: Luke's own vision is inclusive in tone: outsiders, Samaritans and Gentiles welcomed; Acts 10:34–35.
  • For: The resurrection gives hope beyond death and a basis for Christian ethics and mission.
  • Against: Exclusive claims can seem arrogant or divisive and have been linked to coercion in history.
  • Against: Pluralists (Hick) argue that a loving God would not limit salvation to one tradition.
  • Against: Historical doubts about the resurrection, and different beliefs in other faiths about Jesus' death, weaken a single claim's authority.
  • Judgement criteria: Can a claim be both universal and respectful? Does relevance depend on truth? Does Luke's inclusive tone fit exclusive or inclusive readings better?
  • Skill tip (part b, AO2): use named views (exclusivist, inclusivist, pluralist) as positions to weigh, then reach a judgement of your own.
Mission check

?A historian writes: "The writer of this chronicle dates every event by the reigns of kings, sees each disaster as part of a divine purpose, and ends the story with the faith reaching the capital of the empire." Which scholarly view of Luke does this most resemble?

Luke writes real history and reads it as God's plan, from Adam to Rome; scholars still debate how he divided that plan and why. Its claims about salvation and resurrection raise sharp questions in a world of many faiths.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The Kingdom of God is God's reign; in Luke it brings reversal, welcoming the poor and outsiders.
  • Parables such as the Sower and the Great Banquet show it sown now, harvested and feasted later.

Mission 2

  • The Sermon on the Plain pairs blessings for the poor with woes for the rich, and calls for love of enemies modelled on God's mercy (6:36).
  • The wealth texts (Rich Man, Rich Fool, riches in Heaven, the widow, the poor at table) warn against trusting possessions; Christians read them literally, as counsels, as stewardship or as a call to justice.
  • Whether the ethic is practical or an ideal is debated (Schweitzer, Niebuhr, lived examples).

Mission 3

  • The Good Samaritan, the Lost (including the Forgiving Father) and the Rich Man and Lazarus turn on mercy and reversal: outsiders show it, sinners receive it, the comfortable are warned.
  • Jesus' teaching grows from Jewish teaching on love and mercy; the parables have been read as allegory, as single-point challenges, and as social critique.
  • Whether mercy is the heart of a believer's life, and how outreach should work, is debated.

Mission 4

  • In Luke miracles are acts of power that show God's Kingdom arriving, fulfil the Nazareth programme, and reveal who Jesus is.
  • The five set miracles stress faith across boundaries (centurion, Samaritan leper), compassion (widow), Jesus' authority over nature, and God feeding his people.
  • Credibility is debated (Hume, rationalists, Bultmann; traditional and symbolic responses); validity and relevance can be judged separately.

Mission 5

  • Luke's disciples are called (fishermen, a tax collector, the Twelve after prayer), sent with Jesus' authority, and told to count the cost: self-denial, the cross, priority over family and possessions.
  • Teachings stress persistent prayer, the Holy Spirit, courage and trust.
  • Peter fails and is restored; whether Luke's demands can be lived today is debated (literal vows, Bonhoeffer, priority readings).

Mission 6

  • Salvation History: God saves through events in history, following a plan with a goal; Luke shows it by fulfilment, "must", "today", dating, and a mission to all peoples.
  • Conzelmann: three epochs (Israel, Jesus as the middle of time, the Church), prompted by the delay of the parousia; Cullmann: Christ the midpoint of all history; critics (Minear, Marshall) stress the birth stories and salvation itself.
  • Exclusivist, inclusivist and pluralist views answer differently whether Luke's claims about salvation and resurrection are relevant in a multi-religious world.
