Mission 1

Mission 1: Charge and energy would both balance if a proton turned into a positron and light. So what forbids it?

Make a guess: Charge and energy would both balance if a proton turned into a positron and light. So what forbids it?

Mission 1

The bookkeeping rules

  • Some quantities are the same before and after every interaction and decay.
  • Charge (in units of e): always conserved.
  • Energy (including rest energy) and momentum: always conserved.
  • Also baryon number and lepton number.
  • Check each quantity separately: add up the left side, add up the right side.
Mission 1

Charge on quarks

  • Quark charges: u +⅔e, d −⅓e, s −⅓e. Antiquarks: opposite sign.
  • β⁻ decay, one quark changes: d → u + e⁻ + ν̄ₑ
  • Charge: −⅓ = +⅔ + (−1) + 0 ✓
  • β⁺ decay: u → d + e⁺ + νₑ
  • Charge: +⅔ = −⅓ + (+1) + 0 ✓
  • In a decay, quarks change type; they do not appear or vanish one at a time.
Quick check
?In a decay, an up quark changes into a down quark. Which of these must also be emitted for charge to be conserved?
Mission 1

Baryon number, B

  • Quark +⅓, antiquark −⅓.
  • Baryons (qqq), e.g. p, n: B = +1. Antibaryons: B = −1.
  • Mesons (q q̄): B = 0. Leptons and photons: B = 0.
  • Total B is the same before and after.
Mission 1

Lepton number, L

  • Leptons (e⁻, νₑ …): L = +1. Antileptons (e⁺, ν̄ₑ …): L = −1. Everything else: L = 0.
  • Total L is the same before and after.
Quick check
?A student proposes that a free neutron decays into a proton and an electron and nothing else: n → p + e⁻. Which conservation law that holds in all nuclear decays would this proposal break?
Mission 1

Worked example: allowed or forbidden?

  • Free neutron decay: n → p + e⁻ + ν̄ₑ
  • Q: 0 = (+1) + (−1) + 0 ✓
  • B: 1 = 1 + 0 + 0 ✓
  • L: 0 = 0 + 1 + (−1) ✓ → allowed.
  • Proposed: p → e⁺ + γ
  • Q: +1 = +1 + 0 ✓
  • B: 1 → 0 ✗ · L: 0 → −1 ✗ → forbidden.
  • One broken law is enough to rule an interaction out.
Mission 1

Energy and momentum

  • Total energy (rest energy + kinetic energy + photons) is conserved.
  • Free neutron decay: rest energies 939.55 MeV → 938.26 + 0.511 MeV.
  • The missing 0.78 MeV becomes kinetic energy of the products.
  • A free proton cannot decay into a neutron: the products would have more rest energy.
  • Momentum: a particle at rest decays into products whose momenta add to zero.
Mission check

?Which of these proposed interactions is forbidden by a conservation law?

A proton turning into a positron and light keeps charge and energy balanced, but breaks baryon number (1 → 0) and lepton number (0 → −1). No one has ever seen it.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why are the most powerful particle accelerators built as rings many kilometres around?

Make a guess: Why are the most powerful particle accelerators built as rings many kilometres around?

Mission 2

Electric fields push, magnetic fields steer

  • Electric field: force along the field, so it does work on a charge. Energy gained crossing a pd V: charge × V.
  • Magnetic field: force perpendicular to the velocity. No work done, so the speed stays the same; only the direction changes.
  • A uniform B field perpendicular to the motion bends the path into a circle.
  • Radius: r = p/BQ. More momentum, bigger circle.
Mission 2

The linear accelerator (linac)

  • A line of drift tubes in a vacuum, joined alternately to an alternating pd.
  • Inside a tube: no electric field. The particle drifts at constant speed.
  • In each gap: a field pushes the particle forward. It gains charge × V at every gap.
  • The pd reverses while the particle is inside a tube, so the next gap is ready to push it again.
  • Tubes get longer along the line: the particle is faster, but must spend the same time (half a cycle) in each tube.
Quick check
?A proton crosses 20 gaps in a linac. The pd across each gap is 50 kV as the proton crosses it. How much kinetic energy does the proton gain?
Mission 2

The cyclotron

  • Two hollow D-shaped electrodes ("dees") in a vacuum, between the poles of a magnet.
  • B field (perpendicular to the plane of the dees): bends the particle into a semicircle inside each dee.
  • Alternating pd across the gap: accelerates the particle each time it crosses.
  • Faster particle → bigger radius: it spirals outwards, then leaves at the edge.
  • Time for a half-turn: πr/v = πm/(BQ): it does not depend on the speed.
  • So a pd of fixed frequency stays in step: f = BQ/(2πm). (For B = 1.5 T, protons: about 23 MHz.)
Quick check
?A cyclotron accelerates protons with a fixed magnetic field. It is now used for alpha particles: twice the charge, about four times the mass. What must happen to the frequency of the alternating pd?
Mission 2

The synchrotron

  • A ring of fixed radius: an evacuated beam pipe.
  • Bending magnets keep the particles on the ring. Focusing magnets keep the beam narrow.
  • Accelerating cavities: an alternating electric field gives the particles energy each lap.
  • As momentum grows, the B field is increased in step, so the radius stays the same.
  • The frequency of the accelerating field is matched to the particles' lap time.
  • Two beams can travel in opposite directions and collide head-on.
Mission 2

Detectors

  • A charged particle ionises atoms along its path: this leaves a track (e.g. droplets in a cloud chamber, bubbles in a bubble chamber, signals in wire chambers).
  • Neutral particles do not ionise: no track.
  • A B field across the detector deflects charged particles.
  • Curve direction → sign of charge. Radius → momentum (r = p/BQ).
  • A particle losing energy spirals inwards: p falls, so r falls.
Mission 2

Reading tracks with conservation laws

  • A "V" of two opposite curves starting from nowhere: a neutral particle decayed. Charge: 0 = (+1) + (−1).
  • A track that suddenly kinks: a charged particle decayed into a charged particle and something neutral (unseen).
  • The momenta of the products must add up to the momentum of the particle that decayed.
  • Missing momentum or energy → an unseen neutral particle (e.g. a neutrino).
Quick check
?In a detector with a uniform magnetic field, a V of two tracks starts from one point. Both products have the same size of charge. One track has a much larger radius of curvature than the other. What does this show?
Mission check

?A ring's bending magnets already run at their maximum field. Engineers want protons with three times the momentum. What must they do?

Magnets have a maximum field. At the highest energies, the only way to keep the bending radius manageable is a bigger ring: the Large Hadron Collider's ring is about 27 km round.

Mission 3

Mission 3: An electron meets its antimatter twin and both vanish. What comes out, and why never just one thing?

Make a guess: An electron meets its antimatter twin and both vanish. What comes out, and why never just one thing?

Mission 3

Annihilation

  • Every particle has an antiparticle: same mass, opposite charge.
  • When a particle meets its antiparticle, they annihilate.
  • Their rest energy (mass) becomes the energy of photons: usually two gamma-ray photons.
  • e⁻ + e⁺ → γ + γ
  • Charge: −1 + 1 = 0 ✓ (photons are neutral).
  • Momentum: almost at rest, total momentum ≈ 0. One photon would carry momentum away, so there must be two, travelling in opposite directions.
  • Energy: total energy before = total photon energy after.
Quick check
?An electron and a positron annihilate, producing two photons. Which quantity is different after the annihilation from before it?
Mission 3

How much energy?

  • Slow electron and positron: each photon carries the rest energy of one electron: 0.511 MeV.
  • ΔE = c²Δm: mass and energy are equivalent; 1 kg ↔ 9.0 × 10¹⁶ J.
  • From ΔE = c²Δm: 2 × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × (3.00 × 10⁸)² = 1.64 × 10⁻¹³ J, shared between two photons.
  • Each photon: 8.2 × 10⁻¹⁴ J ≈ 0.51 MeV. Wavelength λ = hc/E ≈ 2.4 × 10⁻¹² m: a gamma ray.
  • If the particles were moving, their kinetic energy adds to the photon energy.
Quick check
?A muon and an antimuon, both almost at rest, annihilate to produce two photons. The rest energy of a muon is 105.7 MeV. What is the wavelength of each photon?
Mission 3

Pair production

  • The reverse: a photon's energy becomes a particle and its antiparticle.
  • γ → e⁻ + e⁺
  • Minimum photon energy = rest energy of the pair = 2 × rest energy of one particle.
  • Electron–positron: at least 2 × 0.511 = 1.022 MeV.
  • Extra photon energy becomes the particles' kinetic energy.
  • It happens near a nucleus, which takes some momentum: a lone photon cannot conserve both energy and momentum.
Mission 3

Making antimatter in accelerators

  • High-energy particles from accelerators collide; some of their kinetic energy becomes mass.
  • p + p → p + p + p + p̄: an antiproton is created along with an extra proton.
  • At least 2 × 938 MeV must become new rest energy. With a stationary target, more is needed: the products must also carry the incoming momentum. Head-on collisions avoid this waste.
  • Antiparticles are created in pairs with particles: charge and baryon number stay balanced.
  • Antimatter is stored in vacuum, held by magnetic and electric fields, so it never touches matter.
Mission 3

Worked example: minimum photon energy

  • Pair production of an electron and a positron.
  • ΔE = c²Δm: 2 × 0.511 MeV/c² × c² = 1.022 MeV = 1.64 × 10⁻¹³ J.
  • Minimum frequency: f = E/h = 1.64 × 10⁻¹³ ÷ 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ = 2.5 × 10²⁰ Hz.
Mission 3

Energy and mass units

  • 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. 1 MeV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ J. 1 GeV = 1000 MeV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁰ J.
  • Mass written as energy ÷ c²: MeV/c² and GeV/c².
  • 1 MeV/c² = 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ J ÷ (3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹)² = 1.78 × 10⁻³⁰ kg.
  • Electron: 0.511 MeV/c² = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg. Proton: 938 MeV/c² = 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg.
  • In MeV/c², the rest energy in MeV is simply the same number.
Quick check
?The tau lepton has a mass of 1.78 GeV/c². What is its mass in kilograms?
Quick check
?A photon creates a proton–antiproton pair. The rest energy of a proton is 938 MeV. What is the minimum frequency of the photon?
Mission check

?An electron and a positron, each with 0.30 MeV of kinetic energy, collide head-on and annihilate into two photons. The rest energy of an electron is 0.511 MeV. What is the energy of each photon?

An electron and a positron vanish into two gamma photons that fly apart in opposite directions. One photon alone could not conserve momentum. Any kinetic energy the pair had goes into the photons too.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Muons made high in the sky should decay long before they reach the ground. Why do so many arrive?

Make a guess: Muons made high in the sky should decay long before they reach the ground. Why do so many arrive?

Mission 4

Moving clocks run slow

  • A clock moving relative to you is measured to tick slower than an identical clock at rest beside you.
  • An unstable particle is a clock: moving fast, its lifetime measured in the lab is longer than at rest.
  • The factor depends only on the speed as a fraction of c:
  • 0.10c: × 1.005 · 0.50c: × 1.15 · 0.90c: × 2.3 · 0.99c: × 7.1 · 0.999c: × 22
  • Everyday speeds: far too small to notice. Close to c: large.
Mission 4

The muon evidence

  • Cosmic rays hit the upper atmosphere and make muons, which fall at about 0.995c.
  • A muon at rest has a half-life of 1.5 μs: in 1.5 μs, half of a large group of muons decays. In 1.5 μs at nearly c it covers only about 450 m.
  • Without relativity, almost none should survive a few kilometres. Yet detectors at sea level count many.
  • Measured from the ground, their lifetime is about 10 times longer than at rest. They survive much further.
Mission 4

When does it matter?

  • Significant when particles move at a large fraction of c:
  • particles in accelerator beams and colliders; cosmic-ray particles such as muons.
  • Particle physicists rely on it: unstable particles from a collision travel far enough to reach the detectors.
  • Negligible at everyday speeds: an airliner at 250 m s⁻¹ changes the lifetime by about 3 parts in 10¹³.
  • Rule of thumb: below about 0.1c the change is under 1%.
Quick check
?In which situation is the relativistic increase in the lifetime of an unstable particle significant?
Mission check

?In a storage ring, muons circulate at 0.9994c. Timed with clocks in the laboratory, they live about 29 times longer than muons at rest. Which statement explains this?

Fast-moving muons reach the ground for the same reason: measured from the Earth, their lifetime is about 10 times longer than at rest.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Check each conserved quantity separately; one failure forbids the interaction.
  • Charge, baryon number and lepton number are conserved in every interaction.

Mission 2

  • Electric fields give charged particles energy; magnetic fields bend them in circles of radius set by momentum.
  • Linac: drift tubes lengthen. Cyclotron: spiral outwards at a fixed frequency.
  • Synchrotron: fixed radius, so B rises as the momentum rises.
  • Tracks: curvature gives charge sign and momentum.

Mission 3

  • Annihilation: particle + antiparticle → two photons; charge, energy and momentum are conserved.
  • Pair production needs a photon of at least 2 × the rest energy of one particle; extra energy becomes kinetic energy.
  • 1 MeV/c² = 1.78 × 10⁻³⁰ kg; m = E/c².

Mission 4

  • A fast particle's lifetime measured in the lab is longer than at rest; significant only near c (accelerators, cosmic-ray muons).
