Mission 1

Mission 1: What is the pH of boiling pure water?

Make a guess: What is the pH of boiling pure water?

Mission 1

pOH, and the four-way switch

  • pOH = −log[OH⁻], just as pH = −log[H⁺].
  • At 298 K, pH + pOH = 14.00, because Kw = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴.
  • Worked: [OH⁻] = 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ → pOH 3.60 → pH 10.40 → [H⁺] = 4.0 × 10⁻¹¹ mol dm⁻³.
  • Background: pOH is not in your specification. You can always go via [H⁺] = Kw ÷ [OH⁻] instead.
Mission 1

Kw changes with temperature

  • H2O ⇌ H⁺ + OH⁻ is endothermic. Heat it: equilibrium shifts right, so Kw rises.
  • Kw: 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² dm⁻⁶ at 298 K; about 5.4 × 10⁻¹³ at 373 K.
  • Boiling pure water: [H⁺] = [OH⁻], so [H⁺] = √Kw = 7.35 × 10⁻⁷. pH = 6.13.
  • Still neutral: neutral means [H⁺] = [OH⁻]. It means pH 7.00 only at 298 K.
Calculate
?At 273 K, Kw = 1.14 × 10⁻¹⁵ mol² dm⁻⁶. Calculate the pH of pure water at 273 K.
Mission 1

Ka and pKa

  • Weak acid: HA(aq) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + A⁻(aq). Ka = [H⁺][A⁻] / [HA], units mol dm⁻³.
  • Ka is just the equilibrium constant for the dissociation. Water, the solvent, is left out.
  • pKa = −log10Ka, and Ka = 10⁻ᵖᴷᵃ. pKa has no units.
  • Larger Ka → smaller pKa → stronger weak acid (more dissociated).
  • Ethanoic acid: Ka = 1.74 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³, so pKa = 4.76.
  • pKw = −log10Kw; 14.00 at 298 K.
Quick check
?Benzoic acid has pKa = 4.20. What is its Ka?
Quick check
?At 298 K the pKa of methanoic acid, HCOOH, is 3.75 and the pKa of ethanoic acid, CH3COOH, is 4.76.
Which statement about 0.10 mol dm⁻³ solutions of the two acids is correct?
Mission 1

Ka from a measured pH

  • 0.740 g of propanoic acid (M = 74.0 g mol⁻¹) is made up to 250.0 cm³. Measured pH: 3.13 (example result).
  • n = 0.740 ÷ 74.0 = 0.0100 mol; [HA] = 0.0100 ÷ 0.2500 = 0.0400 mol dm⁻³.
  • [H⁺] = 10⁻³·¹³ = 7.41 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³.
  • Assume [A⁻] = [H⁺] and [HA] at equilibrium ≈ 0.0400 (very little dissociates).
  • Ka = (7.41 × 10⁻⁴)² ÷ 0.0400 = 1.37 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³.
  • Core practical 9 also finds Ka another way: pH at half-neutralisation = pKa (in the pH-curves mission).
Mission 1

Weak bases: Kb and pKb

  • Background: Kb and pKb are not in your specification. For bases you use Kw.
  • Weak base: B + H2O ⇌ BH⁺ + OH⁻. Kb = [BH⁺][OH⁻] / [B].
  • pKb = −log10Kb. Larger Kb, smaller pKb: stronger base.
  • Ammonia: pKb 4.75. Methylamine: pKb 3.34, a stronger base.
  • Also background: for a conjugate pair, Ka × Kb = Kw. The stronger the acid, the weaker its conjugate base.
Mission check

?What is the pH of pure water at this temperature, and is it neutral?
  • At 323 K, Kw = 5.5 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² dm⁻⁶.

Boiling pure water is about pH 6.1, and it is still neutral. Kw rises with temperature, so [H⁺] and [OH⁻] rise together.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why is a solution of sodium ethanoate alkaline when no hydroxide was put in?

Make a guess: Why is a solution of sodium ethanoate alkaline when no hydroxide was put in?

Mission 2

pH of a weak acid: two approximations

  • HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻, with Ka = [H⁺][A⁻]/[HA].
  • Approximation 1: so little HA dissociates that [HA]eqm ≈ [HA]initial.
  • Approximation 2: each HA gives one H⁺ and one A⁻, and water adds almost none, so [H⁺] ≈ [A⁻].
  • So Ka ≈ [H⁺]²/[HA], giving [H⁺] = √(Ka × [HA]). No quadratic needed.
Mission 2

Worked example: methanoic acid

  • 0.050 mol dm⁻³ methanoic acid, pKa 3.75, at 298 K.
  • Ka = 10⁻³·⁷⁵ = 1.78 × 10⁻⁴.
  • [H⁺] = √(1.78 × 10⁻⁴ × 0.050) = 2.98 × 10⁻³.
  • pH = 2.53.
  • A strong acid at the same concentration: pH 1.30. Weak means fewer H⁺ ions.
Quick check
?What is the pH of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ ethanoic acid, CH3COOH(aq), at 298 K? Ka = 1.74 × 10⁻⁵. Assume that the equilibrium concentration of CH3COOH equals its initial concentration.
Quick check
?A 0.20 mol dm⁻³ solution of a weak acid, HA, has pH 2.70 at 298 K. What is Ka?
Mission 2

Diluting strong and weak acids

  • 0.100 mol dm⁻³ acids diluted 10, 100 and 1000 times:
  • HCl: pH 1.00 → 2.00 → 3.00 → 4.00. +1 per tenfold: [H⁺] falls tenfold.
  • Methanoic acid (pKa 3.75): pH 2.38 → 2.90 → 3.47 → 4.15. About +0.5 per tenfold at first.
  • Why: [H⁺] = √(Ka × [HA]), so [H⁺] falls by only √10 ≈ 3.2 each time.
  • Dilution shifts HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻ right: a larger fraction dissociates. So at high dilution the rise creeps above 0.5.
Quick check
?A 0.100 mol dm⁻³ solution of a weak acid has pH 3.40 at 298 K. It is diluted 100 times with water. Which is the best estimate of the new pH?
Mission 2

pH of a weak base

  • Background: your exams won't ask you to calculate this, but it shows the same method working for bases.
  • B + H2O ⇌ BH⁺ + OH⁻. Same two approximations: [OH⁻] = √(Kb × [B]).
  • Then [H⁺] = Kw ÷ [OH⁻], and pH = −log10[H⁺].
  • Worked: 0.10 mol dm⁻³ methylamine, pKb 3.34. Kb = 4.57 × 10⁻⁴.
  • [OH⁻] = √(4.57 × 10⁻⁵) = 6.76 × 10⁻³; [H⁺] = Kw ÷ [OH⁻] = 1.48 × 10⁻¹², so pH = 11.83.
Mission 2

Salt hydrolysis: the answer

  • Sodium ethanoate dissolves: CH3COONa(s) → Na⁺(aq) + CH3COO⁻(aq).
  • CH3COO⁻ is the conjugate base of a weak acid, so it takes H⁺ from water:
  • CH3COO⁻ + H2O ⇌ CH3COOH + OH⁻
  • OH⁻ is left behind, so [OH⁻] > [H⁺]: alkaline. No hydroxide was added; water supplied it.
  • Na⁺ comes from a strong base and does not react with water.
  • This reaction of an ion with water is hydrolysis.
Mission 2

Predicting the pH of a salt

  • Strong acid + strong base (NaCl, KNO3): neither ion hydrolyses → pH 7.
  • Weak acid + strong base (CH3COONa, Na2CO3): anion takes H⁺ from water → pH > 7.
  • Strong acid + weak base (NH4Cl): NH4⁺ + H2O ⇌ NH3 + H3O⁺ → pH < 7.
  • Typical data, 0.10 mol dm⁻³ at 298 K: NaCl 7.0 · CH3COONa 8.9 · NH4Cl 5.1.
Quick check
?Which 0.10 mol dm⁻³ salt solution has a pH above 7 at 298 K?
Mission check

?Sodium benzoate, C6H5COONa, is a food preservative. Benzoic acid is a weak acid. A solution of sodium benzoate in pure water is alkaline. Which equation explains why?

Same as sodium ethanoate. The anion of a weak acid takes H⁺ from water, so OH⁻ builds up without any hydroxide being added.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Two acids at the same concentration need the same volume of alkali. How can a titration show which one is weak?

Make a guess: Two acids at the same concentration need the same volume of alkali. How can a titration show which one is weak?

Mission 3

Four curves, four shapes

  • Base added to 25.0 cm³ of acid, all 0.100 mol dm⁻³:
  • Strong acid + strong base: starts near pH 1; long steep section; equivalence at pH 7.
  • Weak acid + strong base: starts higher; gentle buffer region; equivalence above 7.
  • Strong acid + weak base: starts near pH 1; equivalence below 7; levels off well below the strong-base curve.
  • Weak acid + weak base: no sharp steep section.
  • Acid added to base: the same features, with the curve falling.
Mission 3

Measuring a pH curve

  • Calibrate the pH meter with buffer solutions of known pH (e.g. 4.00 and 7.00).
  • Acid in a beaker with the probe; stir throughout.
  • Add base from a burette: 1 cm³ at a time, then 0.1–0.2 cm³ near the steep section.
  • Record the pH once steady after each addition. Plot pH (y) against volume added (x).
Mission 3

Reading the key points

  • Intercept: pH before any titrant is added.
  • Equivalence point: middle of the steep section; amounts match the equation.
  • Buffer region: gentle slope before equivalence; HA and A⁻ both present.
  • Half-equivalence: [HA] = [A⁻], so Ka = [H⁺] and pH = pKa.
Quick check
?25.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ ethanoic acid (pKa = 4.76) is titrated with 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH(aq) at 298 K. What is the pH after 12.5 cm³ of NaOH(aq) has been added?
Mission 3

Same volume, different shape

  • Equal amounts of acid need equal volumes of base, strong or weak. As OH⁻ removes H⁺, more HA dissociates until all has reacted.
  • But a weak acid's curve starts higher: it is only partly dissociated.
  • It has a buffer region: HA and A⁻ together resist pH change.
  • Its equivalence point is above 7: the flask holds A⁻, and A⁻ + H2O ⇌ HA + OH⁻.
  • Strong acid + weak base: the flask holds BH⁺, which makes equivalence below 7.
Mission 3

Ka from half-neutralisation

  • Find the equivalence volume, then read the pH at half that volume. pH = pKa.
  • Core practical 9: titrate the acid to the end point, then add an equal volume of the same acid. Now half is neutralised: measure the pH.
  • Example: half-neutralisation pH = 4.20.
  • Ka = 10⁻⁴·²⁰ = 6.3 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³.
  • Lower pH at half-neutralisation → larger Ka → stronger acid.
Quick check
?A pH curve starts at pH 1.0. It rises slowly, then steeply from about pH 3 to pH 7, with the equivalence point at about pH 5.5. It then levels off below pH 10. Which titration does it show?
Mission 3

The curve picks the indicator

  • The indicator must change colour within the steep section, at the equivalence point.
  • Weak acid + strong base: phenolphthalein (changes about pH 8–10).
  • Strong acid + weak base: methyl orange (changes about pH 3–4.5).
  • Strong + strong: either works. Weak + weak: no indicator gives a sharp end point.
  • Why indicators work: an earlier lesson.
Mission check

?Separate 20.0 cm³ portions of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH(aq) and of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ base B(aq) are each titrated with 0.100 mol dm⁻³ HCl(aq). Both need 20.0 cm³ of acid. Which feature of the curves shows that B is a weak base?

Same volume, different shape. A weak partner raises (or lowers) the start, adds a buffer region, and moves equivalence away from pH 7.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why does your blood stay at about pH 7.4, even after a sprint?

Make a guess: Why does your blood stay at about pH 7.4, even after a sprint?

Mission 4

What a buffer is

  • A buffer resists changes in pH when small amounts of acid or alkali are added.
  • Acidic buffer: a weak acid + its salt, e.g. CH3COOH with CH3COONa.
  • Basic buffer: a weak base + its salt, e.g. NH3 with NH4Cl.
Mission 4

Two ways to make one

  • Mix a weak acid with its salt.
  • Or part-neutralise: excess weak acid + some NaOH. CH3COOH + OH⁻ → CH3COO⁻ + H2O makes the salt; leftover acid stays.
  • That is the buffer region of a weak acid–strong base pH curve: HA and A⁻ together, so the pH rises slowly.
  • Basic buffer: excess weak base + some strong acid, e.g. NH3 + HCl.
Mission 4

How a buffer works

  • Acidic buffer: big reservoirs of HA and A⁻.
  • Add H⁺: the base of the pair removes it. CH3COO⁻ + H⁺ → CH3COOH.
  • Add OH⁻: the acid of the pair removes it. CH3COOH + OH⁻ → CH3COO⁻ + H2O.
  • The ratio [HA]/[A⁻] changes only a little, so the pH changes only slightly.
  • Basic buffer: NH3 + H⁺ → NH4⁺, and NH4⁺ + OH⁻ → NH3 + H2O.
  • A buffer reduces a pH change; it can't stop it completely.
Quick check
?Which mixture, made up to 1.00 dm³ with water, is an acidic buffer solution?
Mission 4

Buffer pH

  • Rearrange Ka: [H⁺] = Ka × [HA] ÷ [A⁻].
  • Assume [HA] = acid added and [A⁻] = salt added: very little of either reacts with water.
  • [HA] = [A⁻] gives pH = pKa.
  • Worked: 0.10 mol dm⁻³ HCOOH + 0.25 mol dm⁻³ HCOONa, Ka = 1.78 × 10⁻⁴. [H⁺] = 1.78 × 10⁻⁴ × 0.10 ÷ 0.25 = 7.1 × 10⁻⁵. pH = 4.15.
  • More A⁻ than HA → pH above pKa.
Quick check
?A buffer solution at 298 K contains 0.20 mol dm⁻³ CH3COOH and 0.10 mol dm⁻³ CH3COONa. Ka(CH3COOH) = 1.74 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³. What is the pH of the buffer?
Calculate

A buffer from part-neutralisation

?50.0 cm³ of 0.200 mol dm⁻³ propanoic acid (Ka = 1.35 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³) is mixed with 25.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH(aq) at 298 K. Calculate the pH.
Mission 4

Adding a little acid: the numbers

  • 1.00 dm³ buffer: 0.100 mol CH3COOH + 0.100 mol CH3COO⁻. pH = pKa = 4.76.
  • Add 0.010 mol HCl. CH3COO⁻ + H⁺ → CH3COOH.
  • Now 0.110 mol CH3COOH and 0.090 mol CH3COO⁻.
  • [H⁺] = 1.74 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.110 ÷ 0.090 = 2.13 × 10⁻⁵. pH = 4.67: down only 0.09.
  • The same HCl in 1.00 dm³ of pure water: pH 7.00 → 2.00.
Mission 4

Making a buffer of a chosen pH

  • Pick a weak acid with pKa close to the target pH.
  • Target pH 5.00 with ethanoic acid (Ka 1.74 × 10⁻⁵): [A⁻]/[HA] = Ka ÷ [H⁺] = 1.74 × 10⁻⁵ ÷ 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ = 1.74.
  • With 0.100 mol dm⁻³ CH3COOH you need 0.174 mol dm⁻³ CH3COONa: 14.3 g per dm³ (Mr 82.0).
Mission 4

Diluting a buffer

  • Dilution lowers [HA] and [A⁻] by the same factor: the ratio, and so the pH, stays about the same.
  • Compare: diluting a strong acid tenfold raises its pH by 1.
  • But each portion now holds less HA and A⁻, so the buffer capacity falls.
Quick check
?A buffer containing 0.20 mol dm⁻³ CH3COOH and 0.10 mol dm⁻³ CH3COO⁻ has pH 4.46. It is diluted with an equal volume of water. To a first approximation, what happens to its pH?
Mission 4

Your blood after a sprint

  • Blood plasma holds carbonic acid, H2CO3, and hydrogencarbonate ions, HCO3⁻.
  • Muscles add H⁺: HCO3⁻ + H⁺ → H2CO3. Most of the H⁺ is removed.
  • H2CO3 → CO2 + H2O, and you breathe out the extra CO2.
  • Result: pH stays 7.35–7.45.
Mission check

?Inside cells, a buffer of dihydrogenphosphate ions, H2PO4⁻, and hydrogenphosphate ions, HPO4²⁻, helps keep the pH close to 7. A process in the cell releases some OH⁻ ions. Which reaction removes most of the added OH⁻?

Same idea as in blood. The acid of the pair removes OH⁻, the base removes H⁺, and the ratio barely changes.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Ka = [H⁺][A⁻]/[HA]; pKa = −log10Ka. Smaller pKa: stronger acid.
  • Kw rises with temperature: pure water is always neutral, but pH 7.00 only at 298 K.

Mission 2

  • Weak acid: [H⁺] = √(Ka × [HA]), assuming little dissociation and [H⁺] = [A⁻].
  • Salt of a weak acid + strong base → pH > 7; strong acid + weak base → pH < 7; strong + strong → pH 7.

Mission 3

  • Equal amounts need equal volumes; strength shows in the shape: intercept, buffer region, equivalence pH.
  • Half-equivalence: pH = pKa. Equivalence: above 7 for a weak acid, below 7 for a weak base.

Mission 4

  • A buffer holds a weak acid and its conjugate base (or a weak base and its conjugate acid).
  • [H⁺] = Ka × [HA] ÷ [A⁻]; dilution keeps the ratio, so the pH stays about the same.
