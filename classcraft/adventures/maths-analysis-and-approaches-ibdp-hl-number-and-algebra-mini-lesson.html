Number and algebra · standard form and approximation

Standard form

  • A number is in standard form when it is written a×10ka × 10^k with 1≤a<101 ≤ a < 10 and kk an integer.
  • Large numbers have k>0k > 0: 305000=3.05×105305 000 = 3.05 × 10⁵.
  • Small numbers have k<0k < 0: 0.00072=7.2×10−40.000 72 = 7.2 × 10⁻⁴.
  • Calculator notation such as 5.2E30 earns no credit in IB exams. Write 5.2×10305.2 × 10³⁰.
Quick check

Number and algebra · standard form and approximation

Calculating in standard form

  • Multiply: multiply the aa parts and add the indices. (3×104)×(5×106)=15×1010(3 × 10⁴) × (5 × 10⁶) = 15 × 10¹⁰.
  • Divide: divide the aa parts and subtract the indices: 10n10m​=10m−n(10^m)/10ⁿ = 10^(m−n).
  • Then adjust so 1≤a<101 ≤ a < 10: 15×1010=1.5×101115 × 10¹⁰ = 1.5 × 10¹¹.
  • Add or subtract: first rewrite both numbers with the same power of 10.
  • Give final answers exact or to 3 significant figures, the IB default.

Number and algebra · arithmetic sequences and series

Arithmetic sequences

  • An arithmetic sequence adds the same amount each time: the common difference d=un+1​−un​d = uₙ₊₁ − uₙ.
  • With first term u1​u₁, the nnth term is
un​=u1​+(n−1)duₙ = u₁ + (n−1)d
  • It is (n−1)d(n−1)d, not ndnd: from u1​u₁ to un​uₙ there are n−1n − 1 steps.
  • Plotted against nn, the terms lie on a straight line with gradient dd.

Arithmetic series and sigma notation

  • An arithmetic series is the sum of the terms. The sum of the first nn terms is
Sn​=2n​(2u1​+(n−1)d)=2n​(u1​+un​)Sₙ = n/2(2u₁ + (n−1)d) = n/2(u₁ + uₙ)
  • The second form pairs first and last terms: each pair has the same total.
  • Sigma notation: r=1∑n​(3r+2)Σ_(r=1)ⁿ (3r + 2) means "substitute r=1,2,…,nr = 1, 2, …, n and add".
  • A GDC can generate terms and sums, but you must still state u1​u₁ and dd.

Number and algebra · arithmetic sequences and series

When data are nearly arithmetic

  • Real data rarely go up by exactly the same amount. If the differences are roughly equal, an arithmetic model is reasonable.
  • Approximate the common difference, for example by the mean difference: d≈number of stepslast value−first value​d ≈ (last value − first value)/(number of steps).
  • Use the model to predict, then comment: predictions far beyond the data are less reliable.

Number and algebra · geometric sequences and series

Geometric sequences

  • A geometric sequence multiplies by the same number each time: the common ratio r=un​un+1​​r = (uₙ₊₁)/uₙ.
  • With first term u1​u₁, the nnth term is
un​=u1​rn−1uₙ = u₁ rⁿ⁻¹
  • Again n−1n − 1 steps: u1​u₁ is multiplied by rr a total of n−1n − 1 times.
  • r>1r > 1: growth. 0<r<10 < r < 1: decay. r<0r < 0: the signs alternate.

Geometric series

  • The sum of the first nn terms, for r=1r ≠ 1:
Sn​=r−1u1​(rn−1)​=1−ru1​(1−rn)​Sₙ = (u₁(rⁿ − 1))/(r − 1) = (u₁(1 − rⁿ))/(1 − r)

Number and algebra · geometric sequences and series

Sum to infinity

  • If ∣r∣<1|r| < 1, the terms shrink towards 0 and Sn​Sₙ gets closer and closer to a limit. The series converges.
  • The sum to infinity is
S∞​=1−ru1​​,∣r∣<1S_(∞) = u₁/(1 − r), |r| < 1
  • If ∣r∣≥1|r| ≥ 1 the series has no sum to infinity: it diverges.
  • Example: 1+21​+41​+⋯=1−0.51​=21 + 1/2 + 1/4 + ⋯ = 1/(1 − 0.5) = 2.

Number and algebra · financial mathematics

Compound interest

  • Simple interest adds the same amount every year (an arithmetic sequence). Compound interest earns interest on the interest (a geometric sequence).
  • With present value PVPV, nominal annual rate r%r%, compounded kk times a year for nn years, the future value is
FV=PV×(1+100kr​)knFV = PV × (1 + r/100k)^kn
  • Yearly: k=1k = 1. Half-yearly: k=2k = 2. Quarterly: k=4k = 4. Monthly: k=12k = 12.
  • A GDC finance solver gives the same answers.

Number and algebra · financial mathematics

Depreciation, inflation and time

  • Depreciation: a value falls by r%r% each year, so FV=PV×(1−100r​)nFV = PV × (1 − r/100)ⁿ.
  • Real value: inflation reduces what money can buy. IB markschemes use the real interest rate ≈ interest rate − inflation rate in the compound interest formula. This is an approximation: dividing by the inflation factor gives slightly less.
  • To find how long, solve for nn with a table of values, the finance solver or logarithms, then round up to a whole period if the question asks when a value is first reached.

Laws of exponents

Number and algebra · proof and the binomial theorem

Identities and LHS to RHS proof

  • An identity is true for every value of the variable; write it with ≡≡. An equation, with ==, is true only for some values.
  • To show that LHS ≡≡ RHS, start with one side and transform it, one valid algebraic step per line, until you reach the other side. Never work on both sides at once.
  • Check your result by substituting a value. If m+11​+m2+m1​≡m1​1/(m+1) + 1/(m²+m) ≡ 1/m, then m=3m = 3 must give 41​+121​=31​1/4 + 1/12 = 1/3, which it does.
Worked example Number and algebra · proof and the binomial theorem

A deductive proof from left to right

  2. 2

  4. 4

⚠️ Watch out
writing the whole identity on every line and "doing the same to both sides" assumes what you are trying to prove. Start from one side only.

Number and algebra · proof and the binomial theorem

The binomial theorem and Pascal's triangle

  • Each row of Pascal's triangle starts and ends with 1; every other entry is the sum of the two above it. Row nn gives the coefficients of (a+b)n(a+b)ⁿ.
  • The entries are (rn​)=r!(n−r)!n!​C(n, r) = (n!)/(r!(n−r)!), also written nCr​ⁿCᵣ. Find them by the formula or with your GDC.
  • Binomial theorem for n∈Nn ∈ ℕ:
(a+b)n=an+(1n​)an−1b+⋯+(rn​)an−rbr+⋯+bn(a+b)ⁿ = aⁿ + C(n, 1)aⁿ⁻¹b + ⋯ + C(n, r)a^(n−r)b^r + ⋯ + bⁿ
  • The general term is (rn​)an−rbrC(n, r)a^(n−r)b^r. The powers of aa and bb always add to nn.

Counting principles: permutations and combinations

  • Multiplication principle: if one choice can be made in pp ways and the next in qq ways, together they can be made in p×qp × q ways. 5 runners on a podium: 5×4×3=605 × 4 × 3 = 60 ways.
  • nn objects in a row: n!=n(n−1)⋯2×1n! = n(n−1)⋯ 2 × 1 ways.
  • Permutation (order matters): nPr​=(n−r)!n!​ⁿPᵣ = (n!)/((n−r)!) ways to choose and arrange rr of nn.
  • Combination (order does not matter): nCr​=(rn​)=r!(n−r)!n!​ⁿCᵣ = C(n, r) = (n!)/(r!(n−r)!). Each choice of rr objects can be ordered in r!r! ways, so nCr​=nPr​÷r!ⁿCᵣ =ⁿPᵣ ÷ r!.

Number and algebra · partial fractions and linear systems

Partial fractions

  • A fraction with two distinct linear factors in the denominator can be split into partial fractions:
(x−a)(x−b)px+q​≡x−aA​+x−bB​(px+q)/((x−a)(x−b)) ≡ A/(x−a) + B/(x−b)
  • This works when the degree of the numerator is less than the degree of the denominator. Factorise the denominator first.
  • Multiply through by (x−a)(x−b)(x−a)(x−b) to get px+q≡A(x−b)+B(x−a)px + q ≡ A(x−b) + B(x−a). It is an identity, so substitute x=ax = a and x=bx = b, or equate coefficients.

Number and algebra · partial fractions and linear systems

Systems of three linear equations

  • Each equation ax+by+cz=dax + by + cz = d is a plane. A solution is a point on all three planes.
  • Solve by elimination (row reduction): use one equation to remove a variable from the other two, then solve the pair. Or use your GDC's solver or matrices.
  • Three outcomes: a unique solution (one common point); infinitely many solutions, when elimination gives 0=00 = 0 (the planes share a line); no solution, when elimination gives a false statement such as 0=50 = 5. A system with no solution is inconsistent.

Question

Mission check

?Three equations, three unknowns: how can there be no solution?

Each equation is a plane. If the equations contradict each other, elimination ends in a false statement like 0=30 = 3 and the system is inconsistent: the planes have no point in common, for example two parallel planes, or three planes that meet in pairs like the sides of a tent.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Standard form is a×10ka × 10^k with 1≤a<101 ≤ a < 10 and kk an integer; never write calculator notation like 5.2E30.
  • To multiply, add the indices; to divide, subtract them; then adjust so 1≤a<101 ≤ a < 10.
  • Arithmetic sequence: un​=u1​+(n−1)duₙ = u₁ + (n−1)d, where dd is the common difference.
  • Arithmetic series: Sn​=2n​(2u1​+(n−1)d)=2n​(u1​+un​)Sₙ = n/2(2u₁ + (n−1)d) = n/2(u₁ + uₙ); sigma notation lists the terms to add.
  • Simple interest grows arithmetically; for real data, approximate dd and judge how far a prediction can be trusted.
  • Geometric sequence: un​=u1​rn−1uₙ = u₁ rⁿ⁻¹, where rr is the common ratio (next term ÷ previous term).
  • Geometric series: Sn​=r−1u1​(rn−1)​=1−ru1​(1−rn)​Sₙ = (u₁(rⁿ − 1))/(r − 1) = (u₁(1 − rⁿ))/(1 − r), r=1r ≠ 1.
  • If ∣r∣<1|r| < 1 the series converges and S∞​=1−ru1​​S_(∞) = u₁/(1 − r).
  • Compound interest: FV=PV(1+100kr​)knFV = PV(1 + r/100k)^kn, with kk compounding periods a year; depreciation multiplies by 1−100r​1 − r/100 each year.
  • For real value, use the interest rate minus the inflation rate (the approximation IB uses); to find a time, solve for nn and round up to a whole period.
  • Laws of exponents: add indices to multiply, subtract to divide, multiply for a power; a−n=an1​a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ.
  • ax=b⟺loga​b=xa^x = b ⇔ log_a b = x; loglog means base 10 and lnln means base e. On a base-10 log scale, +1 means ×10.
  • Laws of logarithms turn products into sums and powers into multipliers; take logs to solve af(x)=ba^(f(x)) = b.
  • Show that LHS ≡≡ RHS by starting from one side and transforming it step by step; check with a value.
  • (a+b)n=∑r=0n​(rn​)an−rbr(a+b)ⁿ = Σ_(r=0)ⁿC(n, r)a^(n−r)b^r for n∈Nn ∈ ℕ, with (rn​)=r!(n−r)!n!​C(n, r) = (n!)/(r!(n−r)!) from Pascal's triangle, the formula or a GDC.
  • To find one coefficient, use the general term, choose rr from the power you want, and keep brackets round each part.
  • Multiply the choices at each stage; order matters gives nPr​=(n−r)!n!​ⁿPᵣ = (n!)/((n−r)!), order does not matter gives (rn​)=r!(n−r)!n!​C(n, r) = (n!)/(r!(n−r)!).
  • With conditions, split into cases and add, or glue items together into a block.
  • For n∈Qn ∈ ℚ, (1+x)n=1+nx+2!n(n−1)​x2+⋯(1+x)ⁿ = 1 + nx + (n(n−1))/(2!)x² + ⋯ is infinite and valid for ∣x∣<1|x| < 1; write (a+b)n=an(1+ab​)n(a+b)ⁿ = aⁿ(1+b/a)ⁿ, valid for ​ab​​<1|b/a| < 1.
  • (x−a)(x−b)px+q​≡x−aA​+x−bB​(px+q)/((x−a)(x−b)) ≡ A/(x−a) + B/(x−b): clear fractions, then substitute x=ax = a and x=bx = b.
  • Solve three linear equations by elimination or with a GDC: one common point gives a unique solution.
  • Elimination giving 0=00 = 0 means infinitely many solutions (write the general solution with a parameter); a false statement like 0=30 = 3 means no solution (inconsistent).
