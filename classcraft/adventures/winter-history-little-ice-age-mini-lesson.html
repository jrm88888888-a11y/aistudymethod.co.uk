The Little Ice Age

3 missions and a finale. Frost fairs, a quiet Sun and two historians who disagree. Guess, learn, then check.

Mission 1

Mission 1: Why did the River Thames freeze — and then stop freezing?

Make a guess: Why did the River Thames freeze — and then stop freezing?

Mission 1

A fair on the ice

  • Between the early 1600s and the early 1800s the Thames at London froze over about 24 times.
  • When it did, Londoners set up stalls on the ice: a frost fair. The first recorded one was in the winter of 1607–08.
  • The last was in 1814. None since.
  • The cold years come in pulses across 1300–1850, not one solid block.
Mission 1

Evidence: a diary, January 1684

  • John Evelyn kept a diary. On 9 January 1684 he wrote that the ice had "become so thick as to bear not only streets of booths, in which they roasted meat, and had divers shops of wares, quite across as in a town, but coaches, carts, and horses passed over."
  • Written at the time by someone who was there: a primary source.
  • A week earlier, on 1 January, he had noted air so cold "as of many years there had not been the like": unusual even then.
Quick check
?Evelyn says coaches and carts crossed the ice in January 1684. What can we safely conclude from this entry alone?
Mission 1

What makes a river freeze

  • Still, shallow water freezes far more easily than deep, fast water.
  • The Thames at London was then wider and shallower than today, and slow-moving.
  • Cold alone is not the whole story. The shape of the river matters too.
Mission 1

Old London Bridge

  • The medieval stone bridge (1209–1832) stood on 19 closely spaced arches.
  • Ice and debris jammed the arches, damming the river and slowing it further.
  • The slowed water froze more easily.
Quick check
?Which change would make the Thames less likely to freeze over?
Mission 1

The river is rebuilt

  • 1831: a new London Bridge opened with only 5 arches. The old bridge came down in 1832.
  • Later in the 1800s the river was dredged and lined with embankments.
  • The Thames became too deep and too fast to freeze over fully again.
  • Fewer very cold winters after the mid-1800s were part of it. The rebuilt river was the other part.
Mission 1

Test the explanation: 1963

  • Winter 1962–63 was one of the coldest on the UK instrumental record.
  • The Thames froze over upstream at Windsor and Reading. In central London, no frost fair.
  • A good explanation survives a test: the cold came back; the slow, shallow river did not.
Mission check

?Why did the River Thames freeze — and then stop freezing?

Cold froze a slow, shallow river; the rebuilt river runs too deep and fast to freeze.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Did the Sun go quiet?

Make a guess: Did the Sun go quiet?

Mission 2

The missing sunspots

  • Astronomers have counted sunspots through telescopes since 1610.
  • Normally they rise and fall in a cycle of about 11 years.
  • From 1645 to 1715 they almost vanished. This gap is called the Maunder Minimum.
  • Named after Edward Maunder, a Greenwich astronomer who, using Gustav Spörer's work, traced the gap in old records in the 1890s.
Mission 2

Two things at once

  • The quiet Sun overlaps the 1600s, most likely north-west Europe's coldest century of the last thousand years.
  • Two things happening together is a correlation.
  • A correlation is a clue. On its own it does not show which caused which, or whether something else caused both.
Quick check
?Sunspots were rare from 1645 to 1715, and north-west Europe's coldest century was the 1600s. On its own, what does this show?
Mission 2

A volcano and a lost summer

  • April 1815: Tambora, on Sumbawa in Indonesia, erupted. Rated VEI 7, the largest eruption in recorded history.
  • Tens of thousands died nearby. Sulfur gases spread high around the globe and dimmed sunlight.
  • 1816 was cold across Europe and North America: frosts all summer in New England, failed harvests, hunger. People called it the year without a summer.
Mission 2

Switzerland, summer 1816

  • Mary Shelley spent that summer in Switzerland, by the lake, as Lord Byron's neighbour. She later wrote: "it proved a wet, ungenial summer, and incessant rain often confined us for days to the house."
  • Indoors, they read ghost stories and agreed each to write one. She began Frankenstein.
  • Her account is evidence of the weather. Linking that weather to Tambora is scientists' interpretation.
Quick check
?Which of these is an interpretation, not a piece of evidence?
Mission 2

Weighing the causes

  • Sun: in 1976 the astronomer John Eddy argued the sunspot gap matched the coldest part of the period.
  • Volcanoes and oceans: in 2012 Gifford Miller's team argued clusters of eruptions started the cold, and sea ice and ocean feedbacks kept it going. They concluded that big changes in the Sun were not needed to explain it.
  • Same evidence, different weighting of causes. The debate continues.
Mission check

?Did the Sun go quiet?

Yes, 1645–1715. Scientists disagree how much it mattered next to volcanoes and oceans.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Was the cold a disaster — or did people adapt?

Make a guess: Was the cold a disaster — or did people adapt?

Mission 3

1315: rain and hunger

  • From 1315 northern Europe had years of heavy rain and hard winters. Harvests failed.
  • The Great Famine (1315–17, in places to 1322) killed on a scale remembered for centuries.
  • The historian William Chester Jordan argues the famine came from weather plus animal disease, prices, war and social conflict. Not weather alone.
Quick check
?Jordan argues the Great Famine came from a mix of causes, not bad weather alone. Which statement best fits his view?
Mission 3

One diary entry, two pictures

  • Evelyn, 24 January 1684, on the fair: "a bacchanalian triumph, or carnival on the water".
  • Same entry, same sentence: "while it was a severe judgment on the land", with trees splitting and cattle dying.
  • Fuel was so dear "that there were great contributions to preserve the poor alive".
  • One source can show celebration and hardship at once.
Quick check
?What does Evelyn's entry for 24 January 1684 show?
Mission 3

Painted winters

  • Dutch artists painted frozen canals. Hendrick Avercamp's Winter Landscape with Ice Skaters (c. 1608, Rijksmuseum) shows hundreds on the ice.
  • Most are skating for pleasure; a few, the museum notes, are there to work, not play. In one corner lies a horse that froze to death.
  • A painting is evidence of what the painter chose to show.
Mission 3

Two historians, one period

  • Interpretation 1: Geoffrey Parker (Global Crisis, 2013) argues cold weather ruined harvests, and hunger fed the revolts and wars of the 1600s.
  • Interpretation 2: Dagomar Degroot (The Frigid Golden Age, 2018) argues the Dutch Republic prospered in the same cold, turning it to advantage in trade and war.
  • Both are built from evidence. They ask different questions about different places.
Quick check
?Why can two historians study the same cold period and reach different conclusions?
Mission check

?Was the cold a disaster — or did people adapt?

Both, depending where and when you look; historians weigh evidence differently.

Finale

Was it even one "Little Ice Age"?

  • In 2019 a team led by Raphael Neukom tested the label against records from across the world.
  • The coldest century differed by region: the 1400s in the central and eastern Pacific, the 1600s in north-west Europe and south-east North America, the mid-1800s in most other regions.
  • They found no sign of a globally coherent cold period. The label is a convenient generalisation, not one event.
  • By contrast, the warmest period of the last 2,000 years fell in the 1900s for more than 98% of the globe.
Finale

A snowflake, 1611

  • In the winter of 1610–11 Johannes Kepler, in Prague, asked why snowflakes always have six corners.
  • He wrote it up as a New Year's gift for a friend: Strena seu de Nive Sexangula (1611).
  • He could not answer it. The science lesson on this hub picks up where he left off.
Quick check
?A textbook says "the Little Ice Age caused the frost fairs". What is the problem with this sentence?
Recap

The big ideas

  • Europe's cold years c. 1300–1850 came in pulses, and the coldest century differed from region to region.
  • A diary shows what one person saw. Causes need more evidence than one source.
  • The Thames fairs ended for two reasons: fewer very cold winters, and a river rebuilt to run deep and fast.
  • Correlation is a clue, not a cause. Scientists still weigh Sun, volcanoes and oceans differently.
  • Same evidence, different questions: historians can disagree and both be rigorous.
  • "The Little Ice Age caused…" is a sentence to be suspicious of.
