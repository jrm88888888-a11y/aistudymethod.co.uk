Mission 1

What makes something a resource?

  • A resource is a concentration of material "in such form and amount that economic extraction … is currently or potentially feasible".
  • So a resource needs three things: the material, a use (demand), and the technology and price to extract it.
  • Change the technology or the price and the size of the resource changes.
Mission 1

Stock and flow resources

  • Stock (non-renewable): formed over geological time, used far faster than it forms: coal, oil, gas, metal ores.
  • Flow (renewable): renewed by natural cycles: solar, wind, river water.
  • Some flows are critical: they renew only if use stays within limits (fish stocks, groundwater, soils).
  • Using a stock depletes it; overusing a critical flow can turn it into a stock in practice.
Quick check
?Which is a stock resource?
Mission 1

Stock evaluation: how sure are we?

  • Measured: grade and size "well established" from closely spaced outcrops, trenches and drill holes.
  • Indicated: similar data, but sample points "farther apart"; continuity can still be assumed.
  • Inferred: "based on an assumed continuity beyond measured and(or) indicated resources"; may not be sampled.
  • Mining codes use the same ladder: an inferred resource rests on "limited geological evidence and sampling".
Mission 1

Reserves, resources and "possible"

  • A reserve is the part that could be "economically extracted or produced at the time of determination".
  • Mining codes: an ore reserve is "the economically mineable part of a Measured and/or Indicated Mineral Resource". There is "no direct link" from an inferred resource to a reserve.
  • Industry often uses proved, probable, possible; USGS notes these have been "loosely interchanged" with measured, indicated, inferred.
  • Possible resources: the least certain, by geology or economics.
  • Reserves grow with higher prices, new technology and more drilling, and shrink as they are used.
Quick check
?Drill holes are farther apart than for a measured resource, but close enough to assume the ore body is continuous between them. Which category fits best?
Mission 1

Resource development over time

  • Exploration: survey, drill and sample to find and classify the resource.
  • Exploitation: build the mine or field and begin extraction.
  • Development: expand output, infrastructure and processing; reinvest or decline.
  • UK North Sea: oil and gas production "peaked in 1999"; total production is now 68% below that peak.
Mission 1

The resource frontier

  • A resource frontier is a new area opened up for extraction, usually by new technology, higher prices or new access.
  • Example: polymetallic nodules on the deep sea floor, mostly at 4,000–6,000 m, with nickel, copper, cobalt and manganese.
  • The International Seabed Authority holds 17 nodule exploration contracts in the Clarion-Clipperton Zone (Pacific).
  • Exploitation rules have been in development "Since 2014": the frontier is still at the exploration stage.
Mission 1

Resource peak

  • Output often follows a curve: rise, peak, decline, as the easiest deposits are used up and costs rise.
  • UK North Sea: peak in 1999, then long decline.
  • A peak can also come from demand: the IEA's Stated Policies Scenario has world oil demand levelling off "around 2030 at 102 mb/d".
  • Peak ≠ running out: much remains, but extraction becomes slower, costlier or unwanted.
Quick check
?UK oil and gas production is now 68% lower than its 1999 peak. If 1999 output is set at 100, what is output now?
Mission 1

Sustainable resource development

  • Brundtland (1987): "meeting the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs".
  • For stocks: use efficiently, recycle, substitute, and invest the income for the future.
  • For flows: keep use within the rate of renewal.
  • Weigh environmental, social and economic effects before a project starts: that is the job of EIA.
Mission 1

Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA)

  • EIA aims "to protect the environment" by making sure decisions are taken "in the full knowledge of the likely significant effects".
  • Stages: screening (is EIA needed?), scoping (which effects?), the Environmental Statement, consultation, then the decision.
  • The public get "early and effective opportunities to participate".
  • EIA does not ban harmful projects; it requires a reasoned conclusion that informs the decision.
Mission 1

EIA in court: Horse Hill, Surrey

  • Plan: extract oil from six wells over 20 years near Horley.
  • The council assessed only emissions on site, not from burning the oil.
  • 20 June 2024: the UK Supreme Court ruled 3–2 that combustion emissions are within the scope of the EIA, so the permission was unlawful.
  • Lesson: an EIA's scope decides what counts as an "impact".
  • Exam move ("Assess …"): judge how far EIA makes development sustainable: it informs but does not decide.
Mission check

?A company drills a few holes into lithium ore in a remote upland and assumes the ore continues far beyond them. It announces "20 million tonnes of lithium reserves" and asks for a fast-track permit. Which response best uses resource concepts?

The amount "in the ground" depends on how well it is known (measured, indicated, inferred, possible) and whether it pays to extract (reserve or not). Both change over time; EIA tests the project's wider effects.

Mission 2

Primary and secondary energy

  • Primary energy: energy as found in nature: coal, crude oil, natural gas, uranium, sunlight, wind, flowing water, biomass.
  • Secondary energy: energy converted from primary sources before use: electricity, petrol and diesel refined from crude oil.
  • Renewable (flows: sun, wind, water) vs non-renewable (stocks: fossil fuels, uranium).
Mission 2

Components of demand

  • Demand comes from industry, transport, homes (domestic) and commerce and services.
  • Electricity is only part of demand: renewables supplied over 30% of world electricity in 2023, but only 4.3% of transport energy.
  • Transport and much heating still run mainly on fuels, so a fast-changing electricity mix moves total energy use slowly.
Mission 2

The world mix, 2025

  • Total energy supply passed 600 exajoules in 2025, up 1.7% on 2024.
  • Fossil fuels "continued to expand in absolute terms" and supplied 86% of total energy supply.
  • All major energy sources reached "all-time highs for the second consecutive year".
  • Renewables were "the largest source of TES growth for the first time outside of a recession".
Quick check
?World solar generation was 2,143 TWh in 2024 and 2,779 TWh in 2025. What was the percentage growth?
Mission 2

Unequal consumption

  • Primary energy per person, 2024 (kWh): USA 76,800; France 37,866; China 34,514; world 21,654; India 7,813.
  • Energy intensity (primary energy per unit of GDP), 2022, kWh per international-$: USA 1.36; world 1.30; France 0.86.
  • Per person shows how much each person uses; intensity shows how much energy each $ of output takes: France needs about a third less than the USA (our calculation).
Quick check
?In 2024, primary energy use per person was 76,800 kWh in the USA and 37,866 kWh in France. How many times as much did the USA use?
Mission 2

Contrasting mixes: USA and France

  • Share of primary energy, 2024:
    • USA: fossil fuels 80.3% (oil 37.8, gas 34.2, coal 8.3); nuclear 7.6%; renewables 12.1%
    • France: fossil fuels 46.1% (oil 31.3, gas 12.9, coal 2.0); nuclear 37.4%; renewables 16.5%.
  • Electricity: nuclear generated 67.7% of France's, 17.8% of the USA's.
  • Method: these shares use the substitution method, which counts non-fossil electricity as the fossil fuel it replaces.
Mission 2

Supply and physical geography

  • Geology: coal, oil and gas lie in sedimentary basins; uranium in particular rocks. China, with large coal reserves, produced 26,245 TWh of coal in 2024 and generated 57.8% of its electricity from coal.
  • Climate: sunshine and wind speeds set solar and wind potential; cold climates raise heating demand.
  • Drainage: hydropower needs high rainfall, large rivers and steep relief. Norway generated 88.8% of its electricity from hydro in 2024.
  • Quality matters too: coal grade, crude oil type and how reliable a river's flow is through the year.
Quick check
?Norway generates almost 89% of its electricity from hydropower. Which physical factors best explain this?
Mission 2

Where fossil fuels are produced and used

  • 2024 oil (our calculation):
    • the Middle East produced 31.0% of the world's oil but used 9.5%
    • Asia Pacific used 38.1% but produced 7.6%.
  • Europe used about 4.9 times as much oil as it produced, and produced 42% of the gas it used.
  • The mismatch comes from geology: oil and gas fields are where they are; demand is where people and industry are. Trade bridges the gap.
Mission 2

Ore minerals: iron ore

  • 2025 mine production (estimated), usable ore: Australia 980 Mt and Brazil 420 Mt, of 2,600 Mt worldwide.
  • That is 37.7% and 16.2% (our calculation).
  • China mined 290 Mt but in 2024 imported 742.5 Mt from Australia and 273.0 Mt from Brazil.
  • Australia supplied 60.1% of the value of China's iron ore imports in 2024.
Quick check
?Asia Pacific uses about 38% of the world's oil but produces under 8%. What most directly follows?
Mission check

?Nuclear's share of world electricity fell from 16.6% (2000) to 8.9% (2025), yet nuclear generation rose from about 2,540 to 2,812 TWh. How can both be true?

The same logic answers the hook. Total energy supply grew 1.7% in 2025; renewables were the largest source of growth, but fossil fuels still grew and supplied 86%. Solar is growing fast from a small base, so it is adding to fossil fuels, not yet replacing them.

Mission 3

Water availability: a very uneven map

  • Renewable internal freshwater per person, 2022 (m³ a year):
    • Canada 73,170; Brazil 26,918; world 5,393
    • India 1,014; Singapore 106; Saudi Arabia 75; Jordan 61; Egypt 9.
  • "Internal" counts only water generated inside the country. Egypt depends on the Nile, which rises upstream in other countries.
  • Availability follows climate (rainfall minus evaporation), river inflows and geology (aquifers), divided by population.
Mission 3

Demand against supply: water stress

  • Water stress: freshwater withdrawn as a share of available supply.
  • World, 2023: 18%.
  • Highest: Northern Africa 101%; Western Asia 68%; Central and Southern Asia 68%.
  • Lowest: Latin America and the Caribbean 5%; sub-Saharan Africa 10%.
  • About 10% of the world's people live in countries with high or critical water stress.
Quick check
?Egypt has only 9 m³ of internal renewable freshwater per person a year, yet it irrigates farmland and supplies large cities. What does the "internal" figure leave out?
Mission 3

Who demands the water?

  • Freshwater withdrawals: agriculture 72%, industry 16%, municipal (homes and services) 12%.
  • Agriculture is the least efficient user: about $0.7 of output per m³, against $38 in industry and $115.5 in services.
  • "90% of global power generation is water-intensive": energy supply is also a water demand.
Mission 3

Why demand keeps rising

  • Population growth: more people to feed and supply.
  • Living standards: richer diets and more goods embed more water. The average consumer's water footprint was 2,842 m³ a year in the USA and 1,071 m³ in China (1996–2005).
  • Industrialisation and energy: factories and power stations withdraw and pollute water.
  • Projections: "Global water demand is projected to increase by 20 to 30% by 2050"; "A 50% increase in food demand is expected by 2050".
Mission 3

Virtual water and the water footprint

  • Virtual (embedded) water: the water used to produce a product.
  • The water footprint has three parts:
    • blue: surface water and groundwater consumed
    • green: rainwater consumed, mainly by crops and pasture
    • grey: freshwater needed to dilute pollution to water-quality standards.
  • World averages (litres per kg): beef 15,400; sheep meat 10,400; pig meat 6,000; chicken 4,300; pulses 4,000.
Mission 3

Virtual water trade

  • Trade in crops and goods moved about 2,320 billion m³ of virtual water a year between countries (1996–2005).
  • The biggest net exporters are in North and South America, southern Asia and Australia.
  • Water-scarce importers lean on water used abroad: 92% of Malta's water footprint, 90% of Kuwait's and 86% of Jordan's lie outside the country.
  • Sustainability issues: imports ease pressure on scarce local water, but create dependence on trade, and an exporter may be draining its own rivers or aquifers.
Quick check
?86% of Jordan's water footprint lies outside Jordan. What does this most directly show?
Mission 3

Conservation, recycling and greywater

  • Conservation cuts demand. Singapore prices water "to recover the full cost of its supply and production" and runs a Mandatory Water Efficiency Labelling Scheme.
  • Household use there fell from 165 litres per person per day (2003) to 141 (2019).
  • Recycling: treating used water for reuse. Singapore's NEWater is "high-grade reclaimed water" produced from used water.
  • Greywater: lightly used water from basins, showers and washing machines, reused for toilet flushing or watering gardens, not for drinking.
  • Issues: treatment uses energy and money; public acceptance can be low.
Mission 3

Groundwater management

  • Groundwater pumping underpins food security in China, India and Pakistan, but "has accelerated the depletion of water resources".
  • In Jordan, 10 of 12 main aquifers are "being used faster than they can naturally recharge"; some fall by more than 10 m a year.
  • Management: licences and metering for wells, pricing, limits on abstraction, and recharging aquifers with surplus or treated water.
  • The issue: groundwater is shared and hidden, so each user gains from pumping while the cost falls on all.
Quick check
?Which of these is an example of greywater reuse?
Mission 3

Weighing beef's water

  • Beef's footprint per tonne: 14,414 m³ green, 550 blue, 451 grey: 93.5% green, 3.6% blue, 2.9% grey (our calculation).
  • For all farm animals, feed makes up 98% of the footprint.
  • An earlier study put beef at 43,000 m³ per tonne; Mekonnen and Hoekstra call such values "rough estimates" whose assumptions "have not been spelled out".
  • Place matters: blue water from a stressed aquifer costs more than rain on wet pasture.
  • Exam move: separate how much water from which kind and where.
Mission check

?Two countries produce the same amount of beef. Country P feeds cattle on rain-fed grassland. Country Q, with high water stress and falling aquifers, irrigates feed crops with groundwater. Which conclusion fits the evidence best?

Not mostly. On a world average, 93.5% of beef's water is rain on pasture and feed crops; only 3.6% is blue water from rivers and aquifers. That small blue share matters most where water is already stressed, and virtual water trade moves the demand to wherever the beef is produced.

Mission 4

Energy pathways and chokepoints

  • An energy pathway is the route energy takes from producer to consumer: a pipeline, shipping lane or power line.
  • A chokepoint is a narrow point on a pathway with few alternatives.
  • In 2025, an average of 20 million barrels a day of crude oil and oil products passed through the Strait of Hormuz.
  • Asia is "normally the destination for the vast majority of the oil and gas that flows through the Strait".
Mission 4

Suez and the Red Sea

  • The Suez Canal "normally passes about 15 percent of global maritime trade volume".
  • After attacks on shipping in the Red Sea, Suez trade volume "dropped by 50 percent year-over-year in the first two months of 2024".
  • Ships diverted around the Cape of Good Hope, adding "10 days or more on average" to deliveries.
  • A chokepoint does not have to close to cause harm: delay and extra cost spread along supply chains.
Mission 4

The 2026 Hormuz disruption

  • "The conflict in the Middle East that began on 28 February 2026 has impeded oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz".
  • Flows averaged 2.7 million barrels a day in March, April and May.
  • The North Sea Dated crude price "reached an all-time high of $144 per barrel – more than double its pre-war levels".
  • Responses: IEA members released 400 million barrels of emergency stocks; Saudi Arabia and the UAE used pipelines that bypass the strait.
Quick check
?Oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz fell from about 20 million barrels a day (2025) to 2.7 million (March to May 2026). What was the percentage fall?
Mission 4

From energy shock to food shock

  • The UN's SDG Report 2026: disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz "affected the movement of key inputs for food production – including fertilizers, sulphur, natural gas and fuels".
  • This drove up "production and transportation costs across food systems".
  • "Import-dependent countries are especially vulnerable".
  • The link is the water–food–energy nexus: farming runs on fuel and fertiliser.
Mission 4

Russian gas to Europe

  • Gas reaches Europe by pipeline and as liquefied natural gas (LNG) by ship.
  • Russia's share of EU pipeline gas imports dropped "from around 40% in 2021 to around 6% in 2025".
  • Counting pipeline gas and LNG together, Russia supplied around 12% of EU gas imports in 2025.
  • Russian LNG is harder to replace: it still made up 17.3% of EU LNG imports in the second quarter of 2026, down from 21.2% in the first quarter of 2021.
  • A pathway built for decades of trade was largely closed by politics within four years.
Mission 4

Energy players

  • Producer governments and OPEC: OPEC was formed in 1960 to "coordinate and unify petroleum policies among Member Countries". Including the UAE, it produced 35% of world crude oil in 2025; the UAE then left OPEC on 1 May 2026.
  • Consumer governments: the IEA "was founded in 1974 to ensure the security of oil supplies"; its members hold and release emergency stocks.
  • TNCs: Nord Stream AG, which built gas pipelines from Russia to Germany, is 51% owned by a Gazprom Group company, with stakes held by Wintershall Dea (15.5%), ENGIE (9.0%), E.ON and Gasunie.
  • Consumers: their demand, and how much they cut it when prices rise, decides how much must flow.
Quick check
?Which player's main role is to coordinate oil production policy among exporting countries?
Mission 4

Minerals and water

  • Ore minerals: China is the dominant refiner for 19 of 20 energy minerals studied by the IEA, with an average market share of around 70%.
  • The top three refining nations' average share rose from around 82% (2020) to 86% (2024); more than half of energy-related minerals face some form of export controls.
  • Water: Singapore may draw up to 250 million gallons a day from Malaysia's Johor River under a 1962 agreement that expires in 2061. A vital supply depends on a neighbour.
Mission 4

Competing national interests

  • Exporters gain from high prices and use supply as leverage; importers seek diversity of suppliers, stocks and routes.
  • The EU cut its dependence on Russian pipeline gas; Gulf exporters built bypass pipelines; Singapore diversifies its food imports and stockpiles.
  • TNCs and state companies build pathways that tie countries together, which makes those links costly to break.
  • Exam move: name the player, its interest and the pathway; then judge who holds power, and how that changed.
Mission check

?A country imports most of its diesel and fertiliser by sea through one strait. The strait is blocked for three months. What is the most likely first effect on its farmers?

In 2026 Hormuz flows fell about 86.5% (our calculation from IEA figures). The UN found that fertilisers, sulfur, natural gas and fuels were disrupted, raising production and transport costs across food systems. Import-dependent countries were most exposed: an energy shock became a food shock.

Mission 5

Where water comes from

  • Surface water: rivers, lakes and reservoirs. Groundwater: water stored in aquifers.
  • Only about 2.5% of Earth's water is fresh; of that, 68.7% is ice and snow and 30.1% groundwater.
  • Other sources: desalination, recycled wastewater, imported water.
  • Singapore's "Four National Taps": local catchment, water imported from Malaysia, recycled NEWater and desalinated seawater.
Mission 5

Components of demand

  • Agriculture (mostly irrigation): 72% of the world's freshwater withdrawals.
  • Industry (including cooling power stations) and domestic/municipal use make up the rest.
  • Output per m³: agriculture about $0.7, industry $38, services $115.5. Farming uses most water and earns least from it.
  • Demand rises with population, living standards, industrialisation and irrigation.
Mission 5

Measuring water stress

  • Water stress (withdrawal measure): freshwater withdrawn as a share of what is available. World, 2023: 18%.
  • Water stress (per-person measure): renewable water per person per year. Below 1,700 m³ = water stress; below 1,000 m³ = water scarcity.
  • The per-person measure is the Falkenmark indicator.
Mission 5

The global pattern

  • Renewable internal freshwater per person, 2022 (m³): Canada 73,170; world 5,393; UK 2,144; India 1,014; Ethiopia 973; South Africa 718; Jordan 61; Egypt 9.
  • Withdrawals vs supply, 2023: Northern Africa 101%, Western Asia 68%, Central and Southern Asia 68%, but sub-Saharan Africa 10%.
  • Caution: "internal" water leaves out rivers flowing in from abroad. Egypt's 9 m³ ignores the Nile.
Quick check
?South Africa had about 718 m³ of renewable internal freshwater per person in 2022. How is it classified by the per-person thresholds?
Mission 5

Physical and economic scarcity

  • Physical scarcity: more than 75% of river flows are withdrawn.
  • Economic scarcity: less than 25% of river water is withdrawn, yet "human, institutional, and financial capital limit access to water even though water in nature is available locally".
  • Pattern: arid regions are most often physically scarce; "much of Sub-Saharan Africa" is economically scarce.
  • So sub-Saharan Africa's low withdrawals do not mean water security.
Quick check
?A region withdraws less than 25% of its river flow, yet many villages have no piped water or treatment. Which term fits?
Mission 5

Climate and supply: Cape Town

  • Climate variability is a physical cause of insecurity: dry years empty reservoirs.
  • 2015–2017: "three consecutive years of below average rainfall", with "a return time of more than a hundred years".
  • Climate change made such a drought about three times as likely (95% range 1.5–6).
  • Storage in the six largest dams fell from 100% in 2014 to 71%, 60% and 38% in the following years (each 1 November).
Mission 5

Geology and supply

  • Permeable rocks (chalk, sandstone, limestone) store groundwater and release it slowly; impermeable rocks (clay, granite) send rain quickly to rivers.
  • River Lambourn, England: a chalk catchment, 97% high-permeability bedrock, base flow index 0.97. Most of its flow comes from groundwater.
  • Public water is pumped from the chalk in its headwaters.
  • Geology also affects quality: limestone gives hard water; some rocks release arsenic or salts.
Mission 5

Drainage and supply

  • Large basins with many tributaries gather more water; dense drainage on impermeable rock floods fast but stores little.
  • Over-abstraction is a human cause: High Plains aquifer, USA: an average fall of 15.8 feet in water level from before development to 2015.
  • Saltwater encroachment (physical, made worse by pumping): near the coast, falling fresh groundwater levels let denser seawater move inland into the aquifer.
Mission 5

Water quality

  • A supply is only useful if its quality is fit for use.
  • Agrochemicals: in Chesapeake Bay, USA, agriculture supplied about 46% of nitrogen loads (2024 modelling). Nutrients feed algae; their decay creates low-oxygen "dead zones".
  • Salinisation: more than 424 million ha of topsoil is salt-affected worldwide.
  • Industrial pollution and untreated sewage add metals and pathogens.
Quick check
?Fertiliser washes off farmland into a bay. A few weeks later, fish die in deep water. What links the two?
Quick check
?Which is a human cause of water insecurity?
Mission 5

How Cape Town pulled back

  • Farms: irrigation cuts rose to 60% (December 2017).
  • Homes: from 1 February 2018, a limit of 50 litres per person per day.
  • Daily use fell from over 1,000 Ml in early 2016 to "just below 500Ml a few times".
  • Day Zero was deferred on 7 March 2018; winter rain then filled the dams to above 75%.
Mission check

?A city's reservoirs fall for three dry winters in a row while its population keeps growing. Using Cape Town as evidence, which explanation and response fit best?

Climate variability (three dry years, about three times as likely with climate change) met rising demand. Restrictions and farm cuts brought use from over 1,000 Ml to just below 500 Ml a day, and Day Zero was deferred until rain returned.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Hong Kong's own reservoirs supply only 20–30% of the water it uses. So how does it have water 24 hours a day?

Make a guess: Hong Kong's own reservoirs supply only 20–30% of the water it uses. So how does it have water 24 hours a day?

Specification: what this mission covers

  • 3.2.5.3 strategies to increase water supply: catchment, diversion, storage, water transfers, desalination
Mission 6

Five ways to increase supply

  • Catchment: protect land so rain runs off into reservoirs clean.
  • Diversion: take water out of a river's natural course by channel, pipe or pump.
  • Storage: hold water from wet periods for dry ones, in reservoirs or aquifers.
  • Water transfer: move water from a surplus area to a deficit area, often between river basins (inter-basin transfer).
  • Desalination: remove salt from seawater to make fresh water.
Mission 6

Hong Kong's problem

  • Local reservoirs supply only 20–30% of consumption.
  • The local yield can differ between years by up to 200 million m³.
  • "Undulating terrain" makes rainwater hard to collect and store.
Mission 6

Catchment and storage

  • About one third of Hong Kong's land is water gathering grounds, where runoff is collected for storage.
  • Most of it lies in Country Parks, "protected by the Country Parks Ordinance": protecting the catchment protects water quality.
  • Storage carries water from wet years to dry ones, but it cannot store more than the rain delivers.
Quick check
?Why could Hong Kong not solve its shortage just by building more reservoirs?
Mission 6

Diversion and transfer: the Dongjiang

  • 15 November 1960: an agreement to import 22.7 million m³ a year from the Shenzhen Reservoir.
  • Since 1965: water from the Dongjiang (East River) in Guangdong.
  • Diversion: the water is taken from the river at Taiyuan Pumping Station, Dongguan.
  • Transfer: since June 2003 a dedicated aqueduct keeps it apart from pollution along the Shima River.
  • Annual supply ceiling: 820 million m³. Hong Kong has had 24-hour supply since 1982.
Mission 6

The risk of one big source

  • The Dongjiang basin has only about 1,100 m³ of water per person a year, and is "regarded as an area of water scarcity".
  • "When our local yield reduces during drought years, the quantity of DJ water available for distribution will also dwindle".
  • Neighbours share a climate: a regional drought hits both sources at once.
Mission 6

Desalination

  • Tseung Kwan O desalination plant has supplied drinking water since 22 December 2023.
  • It is planned to meet about 5–10% of Hong Kong's fresh water demand. In 2024, desalinated water was 1% of consumption.
  • Strength: it does not depend on rain. Limits: high energy use, cost, and salty brine to dispose of.
Mission 6

Different water for different uses

  • Seawater for toilet flushing: 22% of all water used.
  • Reclaimed water: treated sewage effluent for non-potable uses; pilot schemes since 2006.
  • Since March 2024, recycled water has supplied flushing in Sheung Shui and Fanling. It is expected to save about 22 million m³ of fresh water a year.

Mission 6

Supply, demand and sustainability

  • Since 2008, Hong Kong's Total Water Management Strategy has aimed to contain demand and develop new sources.
  • Increasing supply is one half; managing demand (leak repair, efficient fittings, pricing) is the other.
  • Exam move: judge each strategy on reliability, cost, environmental impact and dependence on others.
Mission check

?A coastal city has steep land, little room for reservoirs, and a neighbouring region whose river basin is already short of water. Which plan gives the most secure supply in a long drought?

Hong Kong has water 24 hours a day by diversifying: Dongjiang water (60% in 2024), seawater for flushing (22%), local rainwater (17%), and desalination since 2023.

Mission 7

Mission 7: The Lower Mekong's fisheries land an estimated 2.3 million tonnes of fish a year, worth about US$11 billion. Its hydropower capacity could more than double. Can one river be both a great fishery and a giant power station?

Make a guess: The Lower Mekong's fisheries land an estimated 2.3 million tonnes of fish a year, worth about US$11 billion. Its hydropower capacity could more than double. Can one river be both a great fishery and a giant power station?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.2.5.3 environmental impacts of a major water supply scheme with a major dam and/or barrage and its distribution networks
Mission 7

Hard engineering: the techno-fix

  • To increase supply, engineers store water (dams and reservoirs), move it (transfers by canal and pipeline) or make it (desalination).
  • These large schemes are a techno-fix: a technical solution to a water shortage.
  • They bring big benefits, but also costs that fall on other places, people and ecosystems.
Mission 7

Mega-dams: costs and benefits

  • Benefits: hydropower, flood control, storage for irrigation and supply, navigation.
  • Environmental costs: flooded land and habitats, blocked fish migration, trapped sediment and changed flows downstream.
  • Social costs: displaced communities; lost farmland and fisheries.
  • Benefits and costs often fall on different places and people.
Mission 7

The Mekong: power or fish?

  • Lower Mekong fisheries: a total catch estimated at 2.3 million tonnes, worth 11 billion US dollars a year.
  • 88 hydropower projects operate, with over 13,257 MW installed; 20 more are under construction and 14 planned. If all are built, capacity could reach 27,302 MW.
  • "Dams trap sediment" that maintains the fertility of the Mekong Delta; disrupted fish migration "threatens the food security and income of millions".
  • About 80% of the nearly 65 million people in the lower basin depend on the river. Yet information on dams' sediment impact in the lower basin "is limited".
Quick check
?Using the figures just taught, by what factor could the Lower Mekong's installed hydropower capacity grow?
Mission 7

Case study: China's South–North Water Transfer

  • Launched in 2002, it plans to move about 45 km³ of water a year from the Yangtze basin to the dry north through three canal and pipeline systems.
  • The central route starts at the Danjiangkou Reservoir (capacity 29 km³), behind a dam on the Han River, the Yangtze's largest tributary.
  • It is planned to divert 9.5 km³ a year (13 km³ by 2030) to more than 20 major cities, including Beijing and Tianjin.
  • The central route began operating in December 2014.
Mission 7

Benefits in the receiving region

  • Beijing's groundwater had been falling about 0.7 m a year, from 15 m below the surface in 2000 to 26 m in 2014.
  • With transferred water, pumping fell. The mean groundwater level rose 3 m in five years (25.7 m in December 2014 to 22.7 m in December 2019).
  • Transferred water accounted for about 40% of Beijing's groundwater recovery in 2006–2018; more rain and less irrigation gave the rest.
Mission 7

Environmental impacts in the source region

  • The planned 9.5 km³ a year is about 30% of the mean annual flow into the Danjiangkou Reservoir.
  • Navigation and environmental flows downstream of the reservoir "were negatively impacted".
  • In the reservoir's catchment, pesticide and fertiliser use has been limited and polluting industries are not allowed, restricting development there.
  • Researchers also flag a risk of seawater intrusion into the Yangtze estuary at low flows, when water is abstracted upstream.
Mission 7

Social and economic costs

  • More than 300,000 people were resettled from the Danjiangkou Reservoir catchment.
  • Users in the receiving cities pay higher prices for diverted water, to cover construction and maintenance.
  • The authors conclude that more reliable supply must be weighed against costs in the source region.
Quick check
?Which impact of the South–North transfer falls on the Han River below the Danjiangkou Dam?
Mission check

?The Danjiangkou Reservoir on the Han River now supplies Beijing as well as the Han valley below the dam. Can it serve both fully?

Like the Mekong, the Han River cannot give everything to everyone. A dam and transfer scheme moves benefits to one place (power, or Beijing's water) and environmental costs to another (lost sediment and fish, or lower flows downstream).

Mission 8

Mission 8: The Aral Sea was once the fourth-largest lake in the world. In 2014 the eastern part of its southern basin disappeared completely. What drained it?

Make a guess: The Aral Sea was once the fourth-largest lake in the world. In 2014 the eastern part of its southern basin disappeared completely. What drained it?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.2.5.3 strategies to manage water consumption, including reducing demand
  • 3.2.5.3 sustainability issues in water management: virtual water trade, conservation, recycling, greywater, groundwater management
Mission 8

Two ways to close a water gap

  • Supply-side management: find more water (dams, transfers, desalination, new boreholes).
  • Demand-side management: use less water for the same needs (pricing, restrictions, efficiency, reuse).
  • Supply-side schemes are often costly and can move the problem elsewhere (as the Aral shows).
  • Sustainable management balances use with what the system can renew.
Mission 8

The Aral: demand nobody managed

  • In the 1960s the Soviet Union diverted the Amu Darya and Syr Darya to turn desert into farms "for cotton and other crops".
  • The lake, once "the fourth largest in the world", shrank; in 2014 the eastern lobe of the South Aral completely disappeared.
  • Salty dust from the dry bed degraded fields, so croplands "had to be flushed with larger and larger volumes of river water": demand fed on itself.
  • Kazakhstan's Kok-Aral dam (2005) let the North Aral rebound, while flow to the South Aral was limited.
Mission 8

Reducing demand: rules and prices

  • Restrictions: Cape Town limited households to 50 litres per person per day from 1 February 2018.
  • Pricing: non-domestic tariffs "more than doubled"; commercial and industrial use fell about 20%.
  • Cuts to farms: irrigation restrictions reached 60% in December 2017.
  • Result: daily use fell from over 1,000 Ml in early 2016 to "just below 500Ml a few times".
Mission 8

Conservation: same job, less water

  • Conservation: cutting waste and using efficient technology.
  • USA: each person uses an average of 82 gallons of water a day at home; efficient fixtures can cut that by "at least 20 percent".
  • A typical family can waste 9,400 gallons a year through household leaks.
  • A labelled irrigation controller can cut a home's irrigation water use "by up to 30 percent".
Quick check
?Which of these is a demand-side strategy?
Mission 8

Recycling: NEWater

  • Recycling: treating used water (sewage effluent) to a high standard so it can be used again.
  • Singapore's NEWater is made in three stages: microfiltration or ultrafiltration, reverse osmosis, and ultraviolet disinfection.
  • Mainly used for industry and air-conditioning cooling; in dry periods it is added to reservoirs and treated again before reaching taps.
  • It is one of Singapore's "Four National Taps".
Mission 8

Greywater

  • Greywater: lightly used water from showers, sinks and laundry. (Toilet waste is blackwater.)
  • It can be treated simply and reused for toilet flushing or garden watering.
  • Example: a nature centre in Florida recycles its own wastewater for toilet flushing.
  • It saves mains water at the scale of one building, but needs separate pipes.
Mission 8

Virtual water trade

  • Virtual water: the water used to make a product. Beef: about 15,400 litres per kg, world average.
  • Trade moved about 2,320 billion m³ of virtual water a year between countries (1996–2005).
  • Water-short countries import it: 86% of Jordan's water footprint is external. Jordan has about 61 m³ of renewable internal water per person.
  • Importing food saves local water.
Mission 8

Virtual water: is it sustainable?

  • For beef, 93.5% of the water footprint is green water (rain on pasture and feed); only 3.6% is blue water from rivers and aquifers.
  • So beef from rain-fed pasture uses little scarce blue water; beef from irrigated dry land uses far more.
  • Risks: dependence on trade and prices, and the exporter's aquifers may themselves be depleted.
Mission 8

Groundwater management: the High Plains

  • The High Plains aquifer underlies parts of eight US states.
  • Water levels began to fall soon after large-scale irrigation began, around 1950.
  • From before development to 2015: an average fall of 15.8 feet and about 273.2 million acre-feet less recoverable water.
  • The core rule of groundwater management: over the long term, pumping must not exceed recharge.
Mission 8

Groundwater management: Las Vegas

  • By about 1955, wells pumped nearly 40,000 acre-feet a year, more than the estimated natural recharge of 25,000–35,000.
  • By 1990, water levels had fallen more than 300 feet in places; the land had subsided nearly 6 feet since 1935.
  • Responses: importing water from Lake Mead, and from 1988 injecting treated water into the aquifer: managed recharge.
Quick check
?Using the figures just taught, what was the smallest possible yearly overdraft (pumping minus recharge) at Las Vegas around 1955?
Mission 8

Judging sustainability

  • Restrictions and pricing act fast, but need public trust; Cape Town officials said the campaign and "the panic it caused, came at a high price".
  • Recycling by reverse osmosis is reliable in drought but needs energy and investment.
  • Greywater is cheap per building but small in scale.
  • Virtual water helps the importer; sustainability depends on the exporter's water.
  • Groundwater management works only if pumping is held at or below recharge.
Mission check

?A desert lake fed by two rivers keeps shrinking. Rainfall in the mountains is normal, but the irrigated area along the rivers has tripled. What is draining the lake, and which response targets the cause?

Irrigation drained the Aral: Soviet planners diverted the Amu Darya and Syr Darya to grow cotton. Demand-side tools (pricing, restrictions, efficiency, recycling, greywater, virtual water) and groundwater rules that keep pumping below recharge tackle that kind of cause.

Mission 9: About 85% of the Nile's water starts in Ethiopia, yet a 1959 agreement shared it between Egypt and Sudan. Who decides how it is used now, and what would make a deal work?

Make a guess: About 85% of the Nile's water starts in Ethiopia, yet a 1959 agreement shared it between Egypt and Sudan. Who decides how it is used now, and what would make a deal work?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.2.5.3 water conflicts at local, national and international scales
Mission 9

Why water causes conflict, at every scale

  • Demand grows (people, irrigation, industry, energy) while supply varies with drought.
  • Local: users in one place compete, such as farms and a city.
  • National: regions or states within a country share one river.
  • International: a trans-boundary river crosses borders; upstream states control flow and timing, downstream states depend on what arrives.
  • Players: governments, farmers, cities, power companies, basin organisations and mediators.
Mission 9

Local: Cape Town's drought, 2015–18

  • The Western Cape water supply system is managed by the national Department of Water and Sanitation "in cooperation with the City".
  • In the drought, water restrictions for agriculture rose to 30%, then 50% in October 2017 and 60% in December 2017.
  • The cuts were expected to "lead to a loss of agricultural work, particularly for seasonal labourers".
  • Farms and the city drew on the same dams, so every litre saved for taps was a litre lost to farms.
Mission 9

National: the Colorado River, USA

  • The 1922 Colorado River Compact, signed by seven states, gave the Upper Basin and the Lower Basin each "7,500,000 acre-feet of water per annum".
  • In August 2021, the US Bureau of Reclamation declared Lake Mead's "first-ever Level 1 Shortage Condition", for 2022.
  • Required cuts: Arizona 512,000 acre-feet (about 18% of its apportionment), Nevada 21,000 (7%), and Mexico 80,000 (about 5%).
  • So a national share-out also has an international edge: Mexico is downstream.
Quick check
?Which is an example of a national-scale water conflict?
Mission 9

International: the Nile basin

  • Natural flow at Aswan averaged 86.5 billion m³ a year (1900–2018), ranging from 45.6 to 120 billion.
  • The Blue Nile supplies about 55%, the White Nile 32% and the Atbara 13%.
  • "85% of Nile waters originate in Ethiopia", but "nearly all consumptive use occurs downstream in Egypt and Sudan".
Mission 9

The 1959 agreement

  • Signed by Egypt and Sudan in Cairo on 8 November 1959, it assumed a mean flow at Aswan of about 84 billion m³ a year.
  • It gave 55.5 billion m³ a year to Egypt and 18.5 billion to Sudan, with storage losses at the High Aswan Dam estimated at 10 billion.
  • "Not being signatories, Ethiopia and other upstream states do not recognize this agreement".
Quick check
?Using the figures just taught, what share of the water accounted for in the 1959 agreement went to Egypt?
Mission 9

The GERD and the players' interests

  • Ethiopia began the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam (GERD) on the Blue Nile in 2011: 5,150 MW, about 16 TWh a year; reservoir 74 billion m³, 59 billion active. It was inaugurated on 9 September 2025.
  • Ethiopia: electricity demand projected to rise from 11.1 TWh (2020) to 27.3 TWh (2030).
  • Egypt: its foreign minister wrote to the UN Security Council that Egypt is "entirely dependent on the Nile River for its survival".
  • Sudan: smoother Blue Nile flows could eliminate flood losses and raise hydropower.
Mission 9

Basin-wide management: the Nile Basin Initiative

  • The Nile Basin Initiative, founded on 22 February 1999, links 10 basin countries.
  • Its Cooperative Framework Agreement (CFA) sets out "the principle of equitable and reasonable utilization" and "the obligation not to cause significant harm".
  • The CFA entered into force on 13 October 2024. Egypt and Sudan rejected opening it for signature in 2010 and are not parties.
Mission 9

What the evidence suggests

  • Modelling: once filled, the GERD "will benefit Ethiopia and Sudan without significantly affecting water users in Egypt".
  • But "management of multi-year droughts will require careful coordination".
  • So the key issue is less the dam itself than agreed rules for droughts and data sharing.
Quick check
?Which conclusion is best supported by the evidence on the Nile?
Mission check

?For 2022, Lake Mead's first-ever shortage triggered required cuts: Arizona lost about 18% of its share, Nevada 7% and Mexico about 5%. What does this suggest would make a Nile deal work?

The Colorado cuts followed rules agreed years earlier: the 2007 Interim Guidelines, the 2019 Lower Basin Drought Contingency Plan and Minute 323 with Mexico. Ethiopia supplies about 85% of the Nile's water but was not part of the 1959 deal. The GERD and the CFA shift the balance upstream; modelling suggests that agreed rules for multi-year droughts, involving all three states, will decide the risk.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Resources are cultural and economic as well as geological: stock vs flow; measured, indicated, inferred and possible; reserves are the economic, well-known part.
  • Resources move through exploration, exploitation and development; frontiers open, peaks pass; sustainable development and EIA weigh present use against future needs.

Mission 2

  • Primary energy is used as found; secondary energy (electricity, refined fuels) is converted. Mixes differ: France leans on nuclear; the USA on its own oil and gas.
  • Energy use per person and per unit of GDP vary widely; oil and gas are produced in a few regions and used in others, so trade fills the gap.
  • Geology, climate and drainage set what each place can produce; iron ore shows the same mismatch, from Australia and Brazil to China.

Mission 3

  • Water availability per person ranges from tens of thousands of m³ (Canada) to almost none (Egypt's internal supply); stress is highest in Northern Africa and Western and Southern Asia.
  • Agriculture withdraws 72% of freshwater; rising population, living standards, industry and energy push demand up.
  • Virtual water trade, conservation, recycling, greywater and groundwater management each ease or shift pressure, with costs.

Mission 4

  • Energy pathways (pipelines, shipping lanes, power lines) pass through chokepoints such as Hormuz and Suez; disruption raises costs far away.
  • Producers, consumers, TNCs and governments compete over energy, minerals and water; the Russia–EU gas link shows how quickly pathways can close.

Mission 5

  • Water stress: withdrawals vs supply (world 18%), or water per person (<1,700 m³ stress, <1,000 m³ scarcity). Physical scarcity is too little water; economic scarcity is too little access.
  • Supply depends on climate, geology and drainage, and on quality.

Mission 6

  • Five supply strategies: catchment, diversion, storage, transfer and desalination.
  • Hong Kong combines all five, plus seawater and reclaimed water for non-drinking uses.
  • Security comes from diversity: one transferred source shares the region's droughts.

Mission 7

  • Mega-dams, transfers and desalination increase supply, but shift environmental and social costs to other places and people.
  • The South–North Water Transfer (Danjiangkou Dam plus canals and pipelines) helped Beijing's groundwater recover while reducing flows on the Han River below the dam.

Mission 8

  • Managing consumption means reducing demand: pricing, restrictions, efficient fixtures, recycling and greywater.
  • Sustainability depends on scale and source: virtual water helps importers; aquifers last only if pumping stays at or below recharge.

Mission 9

  • Water conflicts occur locally (farms vs city, Cape Town), nationally (Colorado states) and internationally (the Nile).
