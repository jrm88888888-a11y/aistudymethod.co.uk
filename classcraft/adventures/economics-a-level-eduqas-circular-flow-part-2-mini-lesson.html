Mission 1

Mission 1: How can a government raise the most its economy can produce, and why does it take so long?

Make a guess: How can a government raise the most its economy can produce, and why does it take so long?

Mission 1

What supply-side policies aim to do

  • Supply-side policies: government policies that aim to raise the economy's productive capacity.
  • They work on the quantity and quality of factors of production and on how efficiently markets work.
  • Goals:
    • long-term growth: LRAS shifts right
    • a higher trend rate of growth (the long-run average growth of capacity)
    • a lower natural rate of unemployment
    • growth with less inflationary pressure
    • better international competitiveness
Mission 1

On the AD/AS diagram

  • Capacity rises: LRAS shifts right.
  • Productive capacity rises from Yfe1 to Yfe2.
  • With AD unchanged: real output rises, the price level falls.
  • Lower costs per unit often shift SRAS right too.
  • On the Keynesian AS, the vertical section shifts right.
  • Employment can rise at a lower natural rate of unemployment.
Quick check
?Which policy is most clearly aimed at the supply side of the economy?
Mission 1

Worked example: per person, what can policy change?

  • Illustrative data. Real GDP per person = output per hour × hours per worker × workers ÷ population.
  • Population 10 million; workers 5 million; 1,600 hours each a year; output per hour 50.
  • Real GDP = 50 × 1,600 × 5 million = 400 billion. Per person: 40,000.
  • Policy 1: childcare and job support bring 0.5 million more people into work. Real GDP = 440 billion; per person 44,000 (+10%).
  • Policy 2: training and better equipment raise output per hour to 55. Per person also 44,000 (+10%).
  • Both together: 48,400 per person: +21%, not +20%, because the effects multiply.
  • Labour productivity (output per worker or per hour) depends on capital per worker, skills, health, technology and management.

  • Interventionist: markets under-provide some things (positive externalities, public goods), so the government steps in.
  • Both aim at product and factor market flexibility and higher trend growth.
Mission 1

Market-based policies: how they work

  • Lower income tax raises the reward for work: some work more hours or join the labour force.
  • Lower profit tax raises the return on investment: more capital, more productivity.
  • Benefit reform: a bigger gap between income in and out of work makes taking a job more attractive. Voluntary unemployment falls: a lower natural rate.
  • Deregulation and privatisation: fewer rules and a profit motive push firms to cut costs and innovate.
  • Competition policy and trade liberalisation: rivals force firms to be efficient.
  • Labour market reform: weaker union power and more flexible contracts let wages and jobs adjust faster.
  • But: some of these may widen inequality or weaken job security.
Mission 1

Labour market diagrams

  • Minimum wage: set above the equilibrium wage, labour supplied exceeds labour demanded: unemployment (Ls − Ld).
  • Removing or lowering it: employment moves towards Le. Evidence on how many jobs this adds is mixed.
  • Benefit reform and flexibility: more people seek work at each wage, so labour supply shifts right: employment rises.
Mission 1

Interventionist policies: how they work

  • Education and training raise human capital: more output per worker. Fewer skill gaps: lower structural unemployment.
  • Health care: fewer days lost to illness; longer working lives.
  • R&D: new ideas spill over to other firms (a positive externality), so markets under-invest. Subsidies or tax credits raise R&D.
  • Infrastructure: roads, rail and broadband cut firms' costs and link workers to jobs.
  • Industrial policy: support for strategic industries.
  • But: costly, slow, and the government may "pick losers" (government failure).
Quick check
?A government sells its state-owned rail company to private investors and lets rival firms bid to run routes. How is this policy meant to raise LRAS?
Mission 1

Two effects at different speeds

  • Many supply-side policies also change spending:
    • building roads or schools is government spending: AD shifts right at once
    • a tax cut raises disposable income: AD shifts right
  • The supply-side effect comes later, once the road is built or the trained workers arrive: LRAS shifts right.
  • So fiscal policy can have supply-side effects (e.g. tax incentives), and supply-side policies can have demand-side effects.
  • Demand-side fiscal policy aims at AD; supply-side fiscal policy aims at incentives and capacity.

Mission 1

Real world: cutting taxes on company profits

  • The US cut its main federal corporate tax rate from 35% to 21% from 2018.
  • Aim: more investment in capital, so higher productivity and pay.
  • One 2024 study estimated investment rose by about 11% at firms given the average tax cut, compared with firms given none, but wages and growth rose by less than supporters had claimed.
  • The UK cut its main corporation tax rate from 28% (2010) to 19% (2017), then raised it to 25% in 2023 for profits over £250,000.
  • Question to weigh: how would you judge whether a tax cut on profits raised capacity, and over how many years?
Mission 1Evaluate

How well do supply-side policies work?

It depends on:

  • Time lags. Education, health and infrastructure take years, even decades, to raise capacity.
  • Response to incentives. Tax cuts may change effort or investment only a little; profits may go to shareholders instead.
  • Cost and trade-offs. Interventionist policies need spending (opportunity cost); market-based ones may widen inequality.
  • The output gap. With weak AD, extra capacity sits unused; Keynesians would boost demand first.
  • Government failure. Poor information can lead to wasted subsidies or badly designed rules.
  • Strength: if they work, growth with lower inflation and a lower natural rate of unemployment.
Mission check

?A government expands free childcare and job training, paid for by cutting other spending, so AD does not change. Over several years, more parents take jobs and workers become more skilled. According to the AD/AS model, what happens in the long run?

Supply-side policies work on capacity, not spending, and capacity takes years to build.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why can a jump in oil prices make a whole economy produce less and charge more at once?

Make a guess: Why can a jump in oil prices make a whole economy produce less and charge more at once?

Mission 2

Aggregate supply in the short run

  • Aggregate supply (AS): the total real output that all firms in an economy plan to produce at each price level, in a given period.
  • Axes: Price level (vertical), Real national output (Y) (horizontal).
  • Short run: some costs, especially money wages (wages in cash terms, also called nominal wages), are fixed. Firms cannot change every cost straight away.
  • The SRAS curve slopes upwards: a higher price level goes with more real output.
  • Long run: all costs can adjust, so the long-run curve (LRAS) is drawn differently.
Mission 2

Why SRAS slopes upwards

  • Sticky wages: money wages are set in contracts and pay deals, often for a year. They do not rise straight away when prices rise.
  • So when the price level rises:
    • firms get more for each unit they sell
    • many costs per unit stay the same
    • profit per unit rises, so firms supply more
  • Rising costs near capacity: to produce more, firms pay overtime and use older, less efficient machines. Extra output costs more, so it needs a higher price level.
  • The reverse holds too: a lower price level squeezes profit per unit, so firms cut output.
  • Say: "a higher price level", "real output" — not: "a higher price", "quantity". This is the whole economy, not one market.
Mission 2

Worked example: a firm with a fixed wage

  • Illustrative data. A firm sells each unit for 20.
  • Costs per unit: wages 12 (fixed by contract for a year), materials 4.
  • Profit per unit: 20 − 12 − 4 = 4.
  • The price level rises by 10%. The firm's price and its materials both rise by 10%:
    • price: 20 × 1.1 = 22
    • materials: 4 × 1.1 = 4.4
    • wages: still 12
  • New profit per unit: 22 − 12 − 4.4 = 5.6, up 40%.
  • Producing more now pays, so output rises: a movement up along SRAS.
  • When wages are renegotiated up 10% (to 13.2), profit per unit is 22 − 13.2 − 4.4 = 4.4: the same as 4 once prices are 10% higher. The extra reason to produce disappears in the long run.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A firm sells its product for 50. Per unit, it pays 30 in wages, fixed by contract for the year, and 10 for materials. The price level then rises by 10%, and the firm's price and materials cost rise by 10% too. What is the firm's new profit per unit?
Mission 2

Movement along SRAS or shift?

  • A change in the price level → a movement along SRAS.
    • Price level rises → output rises: an extension of SRAS.
    • Price level falls → output falls: a contraction of SRAS.
  • A change in firms' costs (anything other than the price level) → a shift of SRAS.
    • Costs rise → SRAS shifts left (up): less output at each price level.
    • Costs fall → SRAS shifts right (down).
  • Say: "SRAS shifts left" — not: "SRAS falls" or "SRAS moves up".

Mission 2

Real world: the 1973–74 oil shock

  • In October 1973, Arab oil producers began an embargo on oil exports to the United States and other countries.
  • The price of oil rose from 2.90 dollars a barrel before the embargo to 11.65 dollars a barrel in January 1974: about four times as much.
  • Oil is an input to transport, power, plastics and fertiliser, so costs rose across the economy: SRAS shifted left.
  • The US was in recession from November 1973 to March 1975, while prices rose fast.
  • Rising prices and falling output together: stagflation.
  • Caution: US inflation was already rising before the embargo. The oil shock added to it; it was not the only cause.
Mission 2Evaluate

How much damage does a cost shock do?

It depends on:

  • Size and duration. A brief price spike may barely move SRAS; a lasting rise shifts it for years.
  • How much firms use the input. Energy-heavy industries (steel, chemicals, transport) are hit far harder than services.
  • Second-round effects. If workers win higher money wages to keep up with prices, SRAS shifts left again.
  • Firms' margins. Firms with high profits may absorb some of the cost rather than raise prices.
  • Policy. Subsidies or tax cuts can offset some of the rise in costs, at a cost to public finances.
Mission check

?New software lets workers in most firms produce 10% more each hour. Money wages and AD do not change. What is the most likely short-run effect?

Anything that changes firms' costs per unit shifts SRAS; only a change in the price level moves the economy along it. Higher productivity also raises capacity in the long run: a separate, long-run effect.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why can prices and unemployment rise at the same time?

Make a guess: Why can prices and unemployment rise at the same time?

Mission 3

Where AD meets AS

  • Macroeconomic equilibrium (short run): where AD = SRAS.
  • It sets two things at once: the price level and real output (real GDP).
  • Above it: planned output > planned spending. Unsold stock piles up, so firms cut output and prices.
  • Below it: spending > output. Stocks run down, so firms raise output and prices.
  • A change in the price level → a movement along AD or SRAS.
  • A change in anything else (confidence, interest rates, costs) → a shift of the curve.
  • Say: "an extension of SRAS" for a movement up the curve — not: "SRAS increases".
Mission 3

Long-run equilibrium

  • LRAS is vertical at full-employment (potential) output, Yfe.
  • Long-run equilibrium: AD and SRAS cross on LRAS.
  • There, unemployment is at its natural rate: no cyclical unemployment, but some frictional and structural unemployment.
  • Inflation is stable: no pressure from too much or too little spending.
  • In the short run, equilibrium can sit left or right of LRAS. Then the economy has an output gap.
Mission 3

The Keynesian picture

  • Keynesian AS is reverse-L shaped. Three sections:
    • flat: lots of spare capacity; more output with no rise in the price level
    • rising: bottlenecks appear; output and prices both rise
    • vertical at full-employment output: full capacity; only prices can rise
  • Label full-employment output Yfe.
  • Equilibrium is where AD crosses AS, and it can sit on the flat section, far below full-employment output.
  • Same idea: an equilibrium with spare capacity and high unemployment that need not correct itself (see Mission 4).
Quick check
?A rise in consumer confidence shifts AD to the right. In the diagram, the price level rises from P1 to P2. In the short run, what happens to aggregate supply?
Mission 3

Demand shocks

  • Demand shock: a sudden, unexpected shift of AD.
  • Negative: AD shifts left. Examples: a collapse in confidence, a credit crunch, a world recession cutting exports.
    • Price level falls (or rises more slowly), real output falls, unemployment rises.
  • Positive: AD shifts right. Examples: an export boom, a burst of confidence, a sudden rise in government spending.
    • Price level rises, real output rises, unemployment falls.
  • Key pattern: after a demand shock, prices and output move in the same direction.
  • Real world: in 2008–09 banks cut lending and confidence collapsed. Real GDP fell in 2009 in the US, the UK, the euro area and Japan.

Mission 3

Supply shocks

  • Supply shock: a sudden, unexpected shift of SRAS, usually from a change in costs.
  • Negative: SRAS shifts left. Examples: a jump in oil or gas prices, a poor harvest, a jump in import prices.
    • Price level rises, real output falls, unemployment rises.
    • Rising prices and rising unemployment: stagflation.
  • Positive: SRAS shifts right (e.g. a fall in world commodity prices).
    • Price level falls, real output rises, unemployment falls.
  • Key pattern: after a supply shock, prices and output move in opposite directions.
  • Some shocks hit capacity itself: war or a natural disaster destroys capital, so LRAS shifts left too.
  • Real world: in 1973–74 oil exporters cut supply and the oil price roughly quadrupled. Many rich countries had rising inflation and rising unemployment.
  • In 2022 energy prices jumped after Russia's invasion of Ukraine. UK CPI inflation reached 11.1% in October 2022 (ONS).
Mission 3Graph lab

A supply shock

Illustrative data. The economy starts in long-run equilibrium.

  • Takeaway: a negative supply shock brings stagflation: higher prices and lower output at the same time.
Mission 3

Output gaps

  • Output gap: the difference between actual real output and potential (full-employment) output.
  • Negative output gap: output below Yfe. Spare capacity; unemployment above the natural rate.
  • Positive output gap: output above Yfe. Firms run beyond normal capacity (overtime, deferred maintenance); unemployment below the natural rate; inflation pressure.
  • The unemployment caused by a shortfall in AD is cyclical (demand-deficient) unemployment.
  • Output gap (%) = (actual − potential) ÷ potential × 100.
  • Say: "output below potential is a negative gap", even if output is still growing.

Mission 3

AD/AS, growth and the PPC

  • Short-run growth: AD shifts right into spare capacity. Output rises towards Yfe; little upward pressure on prices.
  • Once there is no spare capacity, more AD mainly raises the price level.
  • Long-run growth: LRAS shifts right (more or better workers and capital, new technology). Output can rise without inflation pressure.
  • On a PPC (production possibility curve):
    • a negative output gap is a point inside the curve
    • long-run growth shifts the PPC outward, just as it shifts LRAS right
  • A positive output gap means resources are used faster than is sustainable: it cannot last.
Mission 3Evaluate

How big is the effect of a shock?

It depends on:

  • Where the economy starts. With spare capacity, a rise in AD mostly raises output. Near full capacity, it mostly raises prices.
  • Size and length of the shock. A short, one-off oil price spike differs from a lasting rise.
  • Time. Short-run effects can fade as wages and prices adjust (see Mission 4).
  • Policy response. Central banks and governments may offset a demand shock; a supply shock is harder.
Mission check

?An economy starts in long-run equilibrium. The world prices of the raw materials it imports fall sharply and stay low; nothing else changes at first. Using AD, SRAS and LRAS, what happens in the short run?

Supply shocks move prices and output in opposite directions, the good way as well as the bad.

Mission 4

Mission 4: If a slump ends by itself, why do some economists say we should not wait?

Make a guess: If a slump ends by itself, why do some economists say we should not wait?

Mission 4

Two views of aggregate supply

  • Neo-classical view: LRAS is vertical at full-employment output, Yfe.
  • Keynesian view: AS is reverse-L shaped.
    • flat with large spare capacity, rising as bottlenecks appear, vertical only at full capacity
  • The two views agree at full capacity: more AD there only raises prices.
  • They disagree about below full capacity: does the economy get back to full-employment output on its own, and how fast?
Mission 4

A recessionary gap closes itself (in theory)

  • Start: AD has fallen. Output is below full employment; unemployment is above its natural rate.
  • With many people looking for work, nominal wages fall (and other input prices fall).
  • Lower costs → SRAS shifts right.
  • The price level falls, so more real output is bought (a movement down along AD).
  • This continues until SRAS meets AD on LRAS.
  • Long run: output back at full employment, unemployment back at its natural rate, price level lower.
  • Adjustment can be slow. Until it happens, output is lost and unemployment stays high, so governments and central banks may act instead.
Mission 4

An inflationary gap closes itself

  • Start: AD has risen. Output is above full employment; unemployment is below its natural rate.
  • Labour is scarce, and workers expect prices to keep rising: they win higher nominal wages.
  • Higher costs → SRAS shifts left.
  • The price level rises again; real output falls back.
  • Long run: output back at full employment, unemployment at its natural rate, price level higher than before the boom.
  • So a lasting rise in AD has no lasting effect on real output: only the price level is higher.
  • Say: "SRAS shifts", not "LRAS shifts": capacity has not changed.
Quick check
?An economy has an inflationary (positive output) gap. There is no change in policy, and wages and prices are flexible. Which describes how the economy returns to full employment?

Mission 4

Why a slump can last

  • Why nominal wages may not fall:
    • wage contracts last a year or more
    • trade unions resist pay cuts
    • minimum wage laws set a floor
    • firms prefer to lay off some workers than cut everyone's pay and damage morale
  • Prices can be slow to fall too: changing them has costs.
  • Keynes's second point: even if wages fall, workers' incomes fall. Spending falls, so AD may shift left again.
  • Falling prices raise the real burden of debts: households and firms may cut spending further.
  • Result: the economy can sit on the flat part of Keynesian AS, below full-employment output, for years.
  • A negative output gap with spare capacity that persists.
  • Keynes (1923): "In the long run we are all dead."
Quick check
?In the Keynesian view, why might a negative (recessionary) output gap last for years without government action?
Mission 4

Real world: the Great Depression

  • US, 1929–33: real GDP fell by about a quarter; consumer prices fell by about a quarter.
  • Unemployment reached about 25% in 1933.
  • Wages and prices did fall, but output stayed far below capacity for years.
  • Keynes (The General Theory, 1936): an economy can be stuck with too little demand; government spending can lift it.
  • Friedman and Schwartz (1963): the US money supply fell by about a third from 1929 to 1933. The central bank let it happen: a policy mistake, not proof that markets cannot adjust.
  • Both views agree the slump was deep and long. They disagree on why, and so on the cure.
Mission 4

What each view implies for policy

  • Keynesian: in a deep slump, use expansionary fiscal policy (more government spending, lower taxes).
    • AD shifts right on the flat part of AS: real output and jobs rise, with little rise in the price level.
  • Neo-classical: expansionary policy at full employment raises output only for a while.
  • Contractionary fiscal policy (less spending, higher taxes) closes an inflationary gap in both models: AD shifts left.
  • Today most economists accept parts of both. The real argument: how slowly does the economy adjust, and how well can policy be timed?

Mission 4Evaluate

How long is the long run?

It depends on:

  • Wage and price flexibility. Contracts, unions, minimum wages and labour-market rules decide how fast SRAS shifts back towards LRAS.
  • Expectations. If people expect low, stable inflation, wages may adjust faster; if confidence collapses, spending may keep falling.
  • Size of the gap. A small gap may close in a few quarters; a deep slump with falling prices and heavy debts can take years.
  • Policy timing. Policy acts with lags; badly timed stimulus can add to inflation once recovery has begun.
Mission check

?An economy is in a recessionary gap after a fall in AD. Wages and prices are flexible, but take three years to adjust fully. Path 1: no policy. Path 2: an expansionary policy shifts AD back to the right straight away. How do the two paths compare?

The slump does end by itself. The case for acting is the output and jobs lost while wages and prices adjust.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Supply-side policies aim to raise capacity: LRAS shifts right, raising real output without adding to inflation.
  • Market-based policies use incentives and competition; interventionist policies fix under-provision of skills, health, R&D and infrastructure.
  • Effects take years and are uncertain in size.

Mission 2

  • SRAS slopes upwards because money wages and some other costs are sticky in the short run.
  • A change in the price level → a movement along SRAS. A change in costs (wages, raw materials, energy, exchange rate, taxes, subsidies, productivity) → a shift.

Mission 3

  • Short-run equilibrium: AD = SRAS. Long-run equilibrium: on LRAS, at full employment and the natural rate of unemployment.
  • Demand shocks move the price level and real output the same way; supply shocks move them opposite ways (stagflation).
  • Output below Yfe is a negative output gap; above it, a positive output gap.

Mission 4

  • Without policy, flexible wages shift SRAS until the economy is back on LRAS: output at full employment, unemployment at its natural rate.
  • Keynesians argue wages are sticky downwards and falling incomes cut spending, so a negative gap can persist; the vertical-LRAS view expects fairly quick self-correction.
  • Policy follows: Keynesians favour fiscal stimulus in a slump; the vertical-LRAS view favours supply-side policy and stable inflation.
