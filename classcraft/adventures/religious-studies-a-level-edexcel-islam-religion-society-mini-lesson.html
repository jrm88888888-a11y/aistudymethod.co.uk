Mission 1

Mission 1: The Qur'an says only Islam is accepted by God. Can a Muslim still believe others may be saved?

Make a guess: The Qur'an says only Islam is accepted by God. Can a Muslim still believe others may be saved?

Mission 1

One religion, many prophets

  • Islam means "submission" to God. Muslims believe it is the religion of every prophet, from Adam to Muhammad.
  • Abraham, Moses and Jesus are prophets of Islam in this sense: they submitted to the one God.
  • Muslims believe God sent earlier scriptures: Tawrat (to Moses), Zabur (to David), Injil (to Jesus).
  • The Qur'an is the final revelation; Muhammad is the seal of the prophets (33:40).
  • So Islam does not see itself as a new religion, but as the original one restored.
Mission 1Read the source

The one religion

> "The Religion before Allah is Islam (submission to His Will)" (Qur'an 3:19, Yusuf Ali)

> "The same religion has He established for you as that which He enjoined on Noah … and that which We enjoined on Abraham, Moses, and Jesus" (Qur'an 42:13, Yusuf Ali)

> "We make no difference between one and another of them: And we bow to Allah (in Islam)." (Qur'an 2:136, Yusuf Ali)

Q1 Does 3:19 mean the religion founded in 7th-century Arabia, or submission to God in any age?

Show answer

Both readings exist: this is the key debate.

Q2 What does 2:136 say about the earlier prophets?

Show answer

Muslims believe in all of them and make no distinction among them.

Mission 1

People of the Book

  • Ahl al-kitab: Jews and Christians, who received earlier scripture. Sabians are named too (2:62). Classical law treated Zoroastrians in a similar way.
  • Shared ground: their food and marriage to their chaste women are lawful for Muslims (5:5). "Our Allah and your Allah is one" (Qur'an 29:46, Yusuf Ali).
  • Call to common terms: "come to common terms as between us and you: That we worship none but Allah" (Qur'an 3:64, Yusuf Ali).
  • Mixed verdicts: some of them "stand (For the right)" (Qur'an 3:113, Yusuf Ali); others are criticised for changing God's word (2:75).
  • Tahrif (alteration): did they change the text of their scriptures, or only its meaning? Muslim scholars differ.
  • Believers (mu'minun), disbelievers (kafirun) and People of the Book are three distinct Qur'anic categories.

Mission 1

Exclusivism

  • Exclusivism: salvation only through Islam as brought by Muhammad.
  • > "If anyone desires a religion other than Islam (submission to Allah), never will it be accepted of him" (Qur'an 3:85, Yusuf Ali)
  • Many classical scholars: once the message has properly reached someone, salvation requires accepting it.
  • Some reported that 3:85 abrogated the gentler 2:62 (a report from Ibn Abbas in al-Tabari's commentary).
  • Hadith: any Jew or Christian who hears of the Prophet and dies without believing in his message is among the people of the Fire (Muslim; paraphrase).
  • Exclusivism is about salvation, not about how to treat others: many exclusivists also defend kindness and justice to non-Muslims (60:8).
Mission 1

Inclusivism

  • Inclusivism: Islam is the fullest truth, but sincere followers of other faiths may also be saved.
  • Abrahamic: "Those who believe … and those who follow the Jewish (scriptures), and the Christians and the Sabians,- any who believe in Allah and the Last Day, and work righteousness, shall have their reward with their Lord" (Qur'an 2:62, Yusuf Ali). Repeated at 5:69.
  • Different paths: "To each among you have we prescribed a law and an open way" (Qur'an 5:48, Yusuf Ali).
  • Non-Abrahamic: "there never was a people, without a warner having lived among them" (Qur'an 35:24, Yusuf Ali). Some Muslims ask whether figures in other traditions were such warners.
  • Example: the Mughal prince Dara Shikoh (1615–1659), Majma' al-Bahrayn (The Meeting of the Two Seas, 1655), compared Sufi and Hindu Upanishadic ideas.
  • Pluralism goes further: all religions are equally valid paths. Most Muslim inclusivists stop short of this.
Mission 1

Good deeds without faith?

  • No reward: the deeds of those who reject faith become "floating dust scattered about" (Qur'an 25:23, Yusuf Ali).
  • Hadith: Aisha asked about Ibn Jud'an, a generous man of the pre-Islamic era; the Prophet said his deeds would not benefit him, because he never once asked God to forgive his sins on the Day of Judgement, that is, he never believed in it (Muslim; paraphrase).
  • Some reward: "anyone who has done an atom's weight of good, see it!" (Qur'an 99:7, Yusuf Ali).
  • The Prophet's uncle Abu Talib, who protected him but did not accept Islam, receives the lightest punishment (Bukhari, Muslim; paraphrase).
  • Mercy: "My mercy extendeth to all things" (Qur'an 7:156, Yusuf Ali). Many argue God judges each person by what reached them.

Mission 1Read the source

Hadith on other faiths

  • When a funeral passed, the Prophet stood up. Told it was a Jew's funeral, he said: "Is it not a soul?" (Bukhari; paraphrase).
  • Whoever kills a non-Muslim living under a treaty will not smell the fragrance of Paradise (Bukhari; paraphrase).
  • The exclusive hadith in Muslim is read in different ways: as every non-Muslim, or only those who heard the message truly and rejected it.
  • Non-religious worldviews: the Qur'an describes people who say only "time" destroys us (45:24); classical Muslims called such materialists dahriyya.

Q1 What does the funeral hadith show?

Show answer

Respect for every human soul, whatever its faith.

Q2 Why does the second hadith matter for tolerance?

Show answer

It protects the lives of non-Muslims under agreement.

Mission 1

Dialogue today

  • Nurcholish Madjid (1939–2005), Indonesian scholar: reads al-islam in 3:19 as the attitude of submission to God, which others can share. Religious diversity is part of God's plan (5:48) (paraphrase).
  • Tariq Ramadan (b. 1962), Swiss scholar, Western Muslims and the Future of Islam (2004): move beyond tolerance to mutual respect and knowledge; European Muslims as full citizens bearing witness by their lives (paraphrase).
  • Scriptural Reasoning (from the 1990s): Jews, Christians and Muslims read passages from their scriptures side by side; they explore, without needing to agree.
  • A Common Word Between Us and You (2007): a letter from 138 Muslim scholars to Christian leaders, based on 3:64: love of God and love of neighbour.
  • The Christian Muslim Forum (England, 2006) brings leaders together.
Quick check

Mission 1Evaluate

"Islam is an exclusivist religion."

  • For: 3:85 and the hadith in Muslim; the finality of the Prophet (33:40); classical majority views on salvation after the message has reached someone.
  • Against: 2:62, 5:69 and 5:48; every people had a warner (35:24); the word islam means submission (Madjid); mercy "extendeth to all things" (7:156).
  • Another view: Islam is exclusive about truth but inclusive about mercy: God alone judges who received the message properly.
  • Judge it by: Which verses are read in light of which? Is abrogation accepted? Does "Islam" mean a community or an attitude? Is the question salvation, or how Muslims treat others?

Muslims share the same verses but weigh them differently. Freedom of religion and minority rights come next.

Mission 2

Mission 2: The Qur'an says "no compulsion in religion". So why do some laws punish leaving Islam?

Make a guess: The Qur'an says "no compulsion in religion". So why do some laws punish leaving Islam?

Mission 2Read the source

No compulsion

> "Let there be no compulsion in religion: Truth stands out clear from Error" (Qur'an 2:256, Yusuf Ali)

> "There is no compulsion in religion. The right direction is henceforth distinct from error." (Qur'an 2:256, Pickthall)

  • Setting (reported from Ibn Abbas, Abu Dawud): some Madinan Muslims had sons raised as Jews and wanted to force them into Islam. The verse forbade it.
  • Related: "wilt thou then compel mankind, against their will, to believe!" (Qur'an 10:99, Yusuf Ali).

Q1 Why can there be no compulsion, according to the verse?

Show answer

Truth is already clear from error; faith must be a free response.

Q2 Why does the setting matter?

Show answer

It shows the verse applied even to Muslims' own children.

Mission 2

How far does 2:256 reach?

  • All agree: forced conversion is invalid, because faith is in the heart.
  • Some classical scholars: 2:256 was abrogated by later verses of war (9:5, 9:29).
  • Others: it was never abrogated, but applies to People of the Book, who may keep their religion under protection.
  • Most modern scholars: a universal principle of freedom of belief, backed by 10:99 and 18:29 ("Let him who will believe, and let him who will, reject (it)" (Qur'an 18:29, Yusuf Ali)).
  • The hard question: does 2:256 also protect a Muslim who leaves Islam?

Mission 2

The dhimma contract

  • Dhimma: "protection" or "covenant". Dhimmis: non-Muslims living under Muslim rule by agreement.
  • Rights: life, property, freedom of worship, their own courts for family law.
  • Duties: pay the jizya (poll tax; 9:29); loyalty to the state. Exempt: women, children, the poor, monks; no army service.
  • Restrictions in the "Pact of Umar" (attributed to Caliph Umar, 634–644; probably compiled later): no new churches, no public crosses or processions, distinct dress, no riding horses.
  • Yohanan Friedmann, Tolerance and Coercion in Islam (2003): protection was real, but within a hierarchy: tolerance, not equality (paraphrase).
  • Your spec: "religious tolerance … alongside other approaches that might reject or limit other forms of religious expression".
Quick check

Mission 2

From dhimmi to citizen

  • Ottoman reforms: the edicts of 1839 and 1856 declared all subjects equal, whatever their religion; the jizya was abolished.
  • Modern states: most Muslim-majority constitutions give equal citizenship; Egypt's Coptic Christians are citizens, not dhimmis.
  • Tensions: limits on church building in some states; blasphemy laws (e.g. Pakistan) used against Christians and Ahmadis; ISIS re-imposed jizya (2014), condemned by most Muslim scholars.
  • The Marrakesh Declaration (2016): hundreds of Muslim scholars and leaders called for full citizenship rights for religious minorities, citing the Charter of Madinah.
Mission 2

Apostasy in classical law

  • Ridda: apostasy, leaving Islam. Murtadd: an apostate.
  • Qur'an: condemns apostasy and warns of punishment in the Hereafter (2:217, 4:137). It names no worldly penalty.
  • Hadith: whoever changes his religion is to be killed (Bukhari; paraphrase). Another hadith lists leaving religion and separating from the community among the three cases where life may be taken (Bukhari, Muslim).
  • Classical rulings (the four Sunni schools): an adult, sane, willing male apostate is invited to repent; if he refuses, death. Hanafis: a woman is imprisoned, not executed. Ja'fari law is similar, but stricter for a man born Muslim.
  • Other effects: marriage dissolved; no inheritance from Muslim relatives.
  • Context: early apostasy often meant revolt: the Ridda wars under Abu Bakr (632–633).
Mission 2

Apostasy laws today

  • Death penalty possible: in a few states, e.g. Iran and Saudi Arabia, courts may apply it, though executions for apostasy alone are rare.
  • Civil effects only: e.g. Egypt has no apostasy crime, but in 1995 a court declared the scholar Nasr Hamid Abu Zayd an apostate and dissolved his marriage; he moved to the Netherlands.
  • Traditional view: the classical ruling stands, because the hadith are sound.
  • Reformist view: the penalty was for treason and armed revolt, not change of belief; 2:256 and 18:29 protect belief (e.g. Abdullah Saeed and Hassan Saeed, Freedom of Religion, Apostasy and Islam, 2004).
  • Hadd debate: some scholars hold a fixed penalty cannot rest on a single-chain hadith alone.
Mission 2

Freedom of expression and offence

  • The Satanic Verses (1988): protests in Britain, a book burned in Bradford (January 1989); Ayatollah Khomeini's fatwa (February 1989).
  • Cartoons of the Prophet: Denmark (2005); the Charlie Hebdo attack in Paris (2015), condemned by the Muslim Council of Britain and most Muslim bodies.
  • Responses from sources: "Revile not ye those whom they call upon besides Allah" (Qur'an 6:108, Yusuf Ali): respect for others' faith; "when the ignorant address them, they say, 'Peace!'" (Qur'an 25:63, Yusuf Ali).
  • Legal routes: England's blasphemy law protected only Christianity, and was abolished in 2008; the Racial and Religious Hatred Act (2006) covers incitement to hatred, not insult.
  • Muslims' own freedom: Switzerland banned new minarets by referendum (2009); bans on face-coverings.
Mission 2Evaluate

"Islam supports freedom of belief."

  • For: 2:256, 18:29; no worldly penalty for apostasy in the Qur'an.
  • For: dhimma protected worship for centuries; the Marrakesh Declaration.
  • Against: classical law punished apostasy by death; some states today restrict conversion, blasphemy and minority worship.
  • Against: dhimmis were tolerated but unequal.
  • Another view: distinguish the Qur'an, classical law (fiqh) and state practice: they may answer differently.
  • Judge it by: freedom to hold, to practise, or to change a belief? Which sources carry most weight? Does historical context limit a ruling?
Mission check

?A modern Muslim scholar notes: "The people punished for leaving Islam in the Prophet's time had also joined armies fighting the Muslims. And 2:256 forbids forcing belief." Which conclusion is she most likely drawing?

The same verse, 2:256, sits beside classical rulings on apostasy and dhimma. How Muslims read their context decides the answer.

Mission 3: Equal before God but different in law: what does Islam teach about men, women and the family?

Make a guess: Equal before God but different in law: what does Islam teach about men, women and the family?

Mission 3Read the source

Spiritual equality

> "O mankind! Be careful of your duty to your Lord Who created you from a single soul and from it created its mate" (Qur'an 4:1, Pickthall)

> "For Muslim men and women,- for believing men and women, for devout men and women, for true men and women … for them has Allah prepared forgiveness and great reward." (Qur'an 33:35, Yusuf Ali)

> "Whosoever doeth right, whether male or female, and is a believer, him verily we shall quicken with good life" (Qur'an 16:97, Pickthall)

Q1 What does 4:1 say about the origin of men and women?

Show answer

Both come from a single soul.

Q2 What does 33:35 suggest about reward from God?

Show answer

Men and women who share the same virtues receive the same forgiveness and reward.

Mission 3

Qur'an and Hadith on men and women

  • Same worth: both are God's servants and trustees (khalifa); in the Qur'an, Adam and his wife both disobey and both repent: "Our Lord! We have wronged ourselves." (Qur'an 7:23, Pickthall). Eve is not blamed alone.
  • Hadith: women are the "twin halves" of men (paraphrase; Sunan Abu Dawud, no. 236); the best of you are those best to their families (paraphrase; Jami' al-Tirmidhi, no. 3895); "your mother" is named three times before "your father" as most deserving of kindness (paraphrase; Sahih al-Bukhari, no. 5971).
  • Different roles (traditional view): men must maintain (nafaqa) wife and family; so in law, shares and duties differ. "they (women) have rights similar to those (of men) over them in kindness, and men are a degree above them" (Qur'an 2:228, Pickthall).
  • Contested texts: a hadith calls women "deficient in intellect and religion" (paraphrase; Sahih al-Bukhari, no. 304). Traditional scholars link it to rules on witness and exemption from prayer during menstruation; many modern Muslims read it as context-bound or question how it is used.
  • So most Muslims hold equal worth before God; they disagree on how far legal differences follow (feminist readings).
Mission 3

Women in early Islam

  • Khadijah (d. 619): a wealthy merchant who employed Muhammad, proposed marriage, and was the first person to accept Islam.
  • 'A'isha (d. 678): one of the main sources of hadith (over two thousand reports), consulted on law and corrected leading Companions.
  • Umm Salama: tradition says her question why the Qur'an spoke of men led to the revelation of 33:35.
  • Nusayba bint Ka'b: fought to defend the Prophet at Uhud (625).
  • Al-Shifa' bint 'Abdullah: taught reading and writing; reported to have supervised the Madinah market under 'Umar.
  • Umm Waraqa: the Prophet let her lead the people of her household in prayer (Sunan Abu Dawud, no. 592; its scope is debated).

Mission 3

Inheritance

  • Before Islam, many Arab women inherited nothing; the Qur'an forbids inheriting women "against their will" (Qur'an 4:19, Yusuf Ali) and gives them fixed shares.
  • "to the male the equivalent of the portion of two females" (Qur'an 4:11, Pickthall), for sons and daughters.
  • Reason given: the son must maintain a family and pay mahr; the daughter's share is hers alone.
  • Not always double: each parent of a deceased with children receives a sixth (4:11); maternal siblings share equally (4:12).
  • Debate: most scholars hold the shares fixed by revelation. Some modern Muslims argue that where women share the duty of maintenance, the reason for the difference no longer applies.
Mission 3Evaluate

Are women and men equal in Islam?

  • Claim: Islam teaches full equality between men and women.
  • For: equal creation and reward (4:1, 33:35, 16:97); the Qur'an gave women property, inheritance, mahr and consent in the 7th century; early women's leadership (Khadijah, 'A'isha).
  • For: many differences in practice come from culture, not revelation.
  • Against: legal texts give different shares and rights (4:11; men "a degree above", 2:228; talaq mostly in the husband's hand).
  • Against: "equal but different" may still mean unequal in power: critics within and outside Islam argue this.
  • Judgement criteria: Does equality mean identical rights, or equal worth with different roles? Are legal rules fixed or tied to 7th-century conditions? Which counts more: text, history or today's practice?

Mission 4: One verse seems to put husbands in charge. How have Muslim women scholars read it differently?

Make a guess: One verse seems to put husbands in charge. How have Muslim women scholars read it differently?

Mission 4

Equal before God

  • Muslims believe men and women come from one soul: "created you from a single person, created, of like nature, His mate" (Qur'an 4:1, Yusuf Ali).
  • Same duties of faith, same reward: "If any do deeds of righteousness,- be they male or female - and have faith, they will enter Heaven" (Qur'an 4:124, Yusuf Ali).
  • Qur'an 33:35 names believing men and women, side by side, ten times over, with one promise: forgiveness and great reward.
  • So most Muslims agree on spiritual equality. The debate is about roles in family and society.
Mission 4

Women in early Islam

  • Khadijah: a trader who employed Muhammad, proposed marriage, and was the first to accept his message.
  • Aisha: a leading teacher of the first generation; she narrated over two thousand hadith, and senior Companions brought her questions of law.
  • Umm Salamah: hadith collections report she asked why the Qur'an spoke of men and not women; 33:35 followed (al-Nasa'i, Ahmad; Tirmidhi reports the question from Umm 'Umara).
  • Nusaybah bint Ka'b defended the Prophet at Uhud (625).
  • Women prayed in the Prophet's mosque. Hadith: do not stop the women servants of God from the mosques of God (Bukhari, Muslim; paraphrase).

Mission 4

Surah 4: family law

  • Surah 4 is called al-Nisa', "Women". Much of it is family law.
  • Marriage: a contract; the bride's consent; the mahr (dower) is hers (4:4). Up to four wives, but "if ye fear that ye shall not be able to deal justly (with them), then only one" (Qur'an 4:3, Yusuf Ali).
  • Kindness: "live with them on a footing of kindness and equity" (Qur'an 4:19, Yusuf Ali).
  • Inheritance: women inherit in their own right; a son's share is twice a daughter's (4:11). Classical reason: the man must support the family; her share is hers alone.
  • Conflict and divorce: arbiters from both families (4:35); a settlement if the husband is cruel (4:128); divorce rules in 2:228–232.
Mission 4Read the source

Qur'an 4:34

> "Men are the protectors and maintainers of women, because Allah has given the one more (strength) than the other, and because they support them from their means." (Qur'an 4:34, Yusuf Ali)

  • The verse goes on: if a husband fears nushuz (Yusuf Ali: "disloyalty and ill-conduct") from his wife: admonish her; then separate beds; then a word Yusuf Ali renders "beat them (lightly)" (paraphrase).
  • Pickthall renders the opening "Men are in charge of women".
  • Key Arabic words: qawwamun (maintainers; in charge), faddala (has given more, or preferred), daraba (to strike; it has many other senses).

Q1 What two reasons does the verse give for men's role?

Show answer

God has given one more than the other; men spend of their means.

Q2 Why does the translation matter here?

Show answer

Each English version already chooses a reading of qawwamun and daraba.

Mission 4

Classical readings

  • Most classical scholars read qawwamun as the husband's authority and duty to provide and protect, within the household.
  • They read the three steps as a ladder of last resort, and limited the third:
  • Not on the face, not to injure. Ibn Abbas is reported as saying: with a tooth-stick or the like (al-Tabari's commentary).
  • Aisha reported that the Prophet never struck a woman or a servant (Muslim).
  • A hadith: the best of you are those best to their wives (Tirmidhi; paraphrase).
  • Many jurists (e.g. al-Shafi'i) said that not striking at all is better.
  • So even traditional readings restricted the verse. The modern question is whether it gives permission at all.

Mission 4

Amina Wadud

  • Amina Wadud (b. 1952), American Muslim scholar: Qur'an and Woman: Rereading the Sacred Text from a Woman's Perspective (1992); Inside the Gender Jihad (2006).
  • Method: read each verse in its first context, alongside the whole Qur'an, guided by its universal principles (justice, the oneness of God).
  • Qawwamun is not built-in superiority: it depends on the husband actually providing ("because they support them").
  • Nushuz is also used of husbands (4:128), so it means a breakdown of marital harmony, not a wife's disobedience alone.
  • The striking clause restricted a violent practice in 7th-century Arabia; the Qur'an's direction is towards ending it.
  • In 2006 she wrote that she now says "no" to applying the clause literally at all (paraphrase).
Mission 4Diversity

Four ways of reading 4:34

  • Traditionalist: husband's leadership is a duty of care; the striking clause stands but is hedged by the Prophet's example.
  • S. H. Nasr (b. 1933), Ideals and Realities of Islam (1966): men and women are complementary, equal before God but not identical in role; he sees Western pressure for sameness as a modern error (paraphrase).
  • Reformist (feminist exegesis): Wadud; Asma Barlas, Believing Women in Islam (2002): the Qur'an is not patriarchal; patriarchal readings came from its interpreters.
  • Re-translation: Laleh Bakhtiar, The Sublime Quran (2007), renders daraba "go away from them".
  • Critical: some Muslim scholars (e.g. Ayesha Chaudhry, Domestic Violence and the Islamic Tradition, 2013) argue that the pre-modern tradition did permit striking, and modern readers must face that honestly (paraphrase).
Quick check
?A scholar argues: "4:34 gives husbands responsibility only because, and only when, they provide for the family." Which point from the verse is she building on?
Mission 4Evaluate

"Feminist readings of the Qur'an are faithful to the text."

  • For: they read the Qur'an through itself, like much classical tafsir. They follow its own principles of justice (4:135) and mercy between spouses (30:21). They recover women of early Islam.
  • Against: classical scholars across all schools read the verse otherwise for a thousand years. Re-defining daraba is linguistically disputed. Saying "no" to a verse (Wadud, 2006) goes beyond interpretation.
  • Another view: all reading is shaped by its age; the classical readings were themselves interpretations by men of their time.
  • Judge it by: Who has authority to interpret? Does the reading fit the Arabic? Does it fit the whole Qur'an? Does it serve justice in practice?
Mission check

?A student meets the word zawj (spouse, pair) in a verse about marriage. She lists every place the Qur'an uses it, notices it is used of both partners equally, and reads the verse in that light. Which approach is she using?

The words of the text are fixed for Muslims; how to read them is the debate. Next: the veil.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Muslims see Islam (submission) as the religion of all prophets (3:19, 42:13, 2:136); Jews and Christians are People of the Book.
  • Exclusivists stress 3:85; inclusivists stress 2:62, 5:48 and 35:24; pluralism goes further than most Muslims accept.
  • Madjid, Ramadan and Scriptural Reasoning model dialogue.

Mission 2

  • 2:256: no compulsion in religion; all agree forced conversion is invalid; its reach to apostasy is debated.
  • Dhimma protected non-Muslims' life, property and worship for jizya, within a hierarchy; modern states moved to citizenship.
  • Classical law punished apostasy; reformists link the penalty to treason.

Mission 3

  • The Qur'an teaches spiritual equality: one soul (4:1), the same reward (33:35, 16:97); Adam and his wife sin and repent together.
  • Traditional law gives different duties: men maintain; inheritance shares differ (4:11); divorce routes differ (talaq, khul', judicial). Consent and mahr belong to the woman.

Mission 4

  • Most Muslims agree men and women are spiritually equal (4:1, 4:124, 33:35); debate is about family roles.
  • Classical scholars read 4:34 as household authority with striking as a limited last step; feminist readers (Wadud) read the role as conditional and the striking clause as a restriction on its way to ending.
