Mission 1

Mission 1: What can you say about God in about twenty words, and why do Sikhs begin every day with them?

Make a guess: What can you say about God in about twenty words, and why do Sikhs begin every day with them?

Mission 1

The root of the scripture

  • Mool = root; Mantar = sacred words, a formula.
  • Your spec spells it Mool Mantra.
  • The Mool Mantar is the first passage of the Guru Granth Sahib (page 1).
  • Sikhs believe Guru Nanak (1469–1539) first spoke it, after his experience of God in the river at Sultanpur.
  • It opens Japji Sahib, recited every morning by many Sikhs.
  • It is a summary, the "root", of Sikh belief about God.
Mission 1Read the source

The Mool Mantar

Guru Granth Sahib 1, word by word (paraphrase):

  • Ik Onkar: there is one God (One Creator, all that is).
  • Satnam: Truth is God's name.
  • Karta Purakh: the Creator, present in all.
  • Nirbhau: without fear.
  • Nirvair: without hate.
  • Akal Murat: beyond time; never dying.
  • Ajuni: beyond birth; never born.
  • Saibhang: self-existent.
  • Gurprasad: known by the Guru's grace.
  • In Macauliffe's 1909 translation, in summary: there is one God, whose name is true, the Creator, without fear or enmity, immortal, unborn, self-existent, known by the Guru's grace (Guru Granth Sahib 1, paraphrase).

Q1 Which two words say what God is not affected by?

Show answer

Nirbhau, Nirvair: fear and hate.

Q2 Which word explains how people come to know God?

Show answer

Gurprasad: by the Guru's grace.

Mission 1

Ik Onkar: God is one

  • The numeral 1 (ੴ begins with it) stresses that God is one.
  • Onkar points to God as the whole of reality, present in everything.
  • One God for all people: not a Sikh God, a Hindu God or a Muslim God.
  • No rival gods, no partner of God.
  • The Ik Onkar symbol appears on gurdwaras, books and jewellery.
  • Importance: if one God made and lives in all, all people are equal.
Mission 1

True, Creator, without fear or hate

  • Satnam: God alone is eternal truth, real for ever. Sikhs remember God's name in nam simran (meditation on the Name), often repeating Waheguru.
  • Karta Purakh: God made everything and is present in creation.
  • Nirbhau: God fears nothing, because nothing is greater than God.
  • Nirvair: God hates no one and is the enemy of none.
  • Importance: Sikhs try to live truthfully, care for creation, act without fear (the Khalsa's courage) and without hatred of anyone.

Mission 1

Beyond time, beyond birth

  • Akal Murat: God is timeless; God's being does not age or die.
  • Ajuni: God is never born, so God does not take human form.
  • So Sikhs do not believe in avatars (God born on earth), unlike many Hindus.
  • Saibhang: self-existent: nothing made God; God depends on nothing.
  • Importance: worldly things pass away; only God lasts, so Sikhs keep God at the centre of life.
Mission 1

Gurprasad: known by grace

  • Gurprasad: God is known only by the Guru's grace (nadar, God's gift), not by cleverness or ritual alone.
  • Sikhs believe God's word came through the ten human Gurus and is now in the Guru Granth Sahib.
  • Sikhs respond with humility, prayer and nam simran, trusting God's grace.
  • Importance: for Sikhs, no one can earn God; everyone can receive grace.

Mission 1Diversity

Where does the Mool Mantar end?

  • Most Sikhs end it at Gurprasad.
  • Some include the lines that follow: Jap ("meditate"), then a verse that God was true in the beginning, true through the ages, true now, and, Nanak says, will be true for ever (paraphrase).
  • The short form "Ik Onkar Satgur Prasad" (one God, known by the True Guru's grace) opens many sections of the Guru Granth Sahib.
  • All agree on its meaning: one, true, creating, timeless God, known by grace.
Mission 1

Why the Mool Mantar is significant

  • First words of the Guru Granth Sahib, the living Guru: everything else unfolds from them.
  • Guru Nanak's own summary of God.
  • Daily: recited at the start of Japji Sahib in morning prayers; many repeat it through the day.
  • Ceremonies: Japji Sahib, which opens with it, is one of the prayers recited when amrit is prepared at the Khalsa initiation.
  • Shapes life: equality (one God); truthfulness (Satnam); courage and peace (Nirbhau, Nirvair); humility (Gurprasad).
  • Identity: it marks out Sikh belief: one God, no avatars, no images of God.
Mission 1Evaluate

"Ik Onkar is the most important idea in the Mool Mantar."

  • For: it comes first; everything else describes the one God it names.
  • For: it grounds equality, the heart of Sikh life (langar, sangat).
  • Against: without Gurprasad, no one could know the one God at all.
  • Against: Satnam (Truth) shapes daily life most directly through nam simran and truthful living.
  • Another view: the Mool Mantar is one statement; its words explain each other and cannot be ranked.
  • Judge it by: Which idea is most needed for the others to make sense? Which most changes how Sikhs live? Can parts of one statement be ranked?
Mission 1Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain two Sikh beliefs about the nature of God. Refer to sacred writings in your answer.

  • Belief 1: God is one (Ik Onkar). Source: the Mool Mantar, Guru Granth Sahib 1, whose very first words are Ik Onkar, so the scripture puts God's oneness before everything else. Development: one God made and lives in all, so Sikhs treat everyone as equal, e.g. sitting together in langar.
  • Belief 2: God is timeless and never born (Akal Murat, Ajuni). Source: the Mool Mantar names God Akal Murat and Ajuni, so the scripture itself rules out God being born or dying. Development: God does not take human form, so Sikhs worship God through the Guru's word, not through images or avatars.

Mission 2: If God made everything, is God inside the world, outside it, or both?

Make a guess: If God made everything, is God inside the world, outside it, or both?

Mission 2

Karta Purakh: the Creator Being

  • The Mool Mantra opens the Guru Granth Sahib. Its third phrase is Karta Purakh: the Creator Being.
  • Sikhs believe one God made everything that exists, seen and unseen.
  • Sikhs believe that before creation only God existed, so nothing comes from anywhere else.
  • So everything depends on God, and nothing exists apart from God.
  • Sikhs often call God Waheguru ("Wonderful Lord") in worship.
Mission 2

How did it begin?

  • Sikhism has no step-by-step creation story like Genesis.
  • Before the universe, for countless ages, there was only darkness and stillness, with God alone (GGS 1035, paraphrase).
  • God made the vast universe with a single word or command (Japji Sahib, pauri 16, GGS 3, paraphrase).
  • Everything happens within God's hukam: God's will and order.
  • There are countless worlds below and above; no one can count them (Japji Sahib, pauri 22, GGS 5, paraphrase).
  • Guru Nanak teaches that only the Creator knows when and how creation began (Japji Sahib, pauri 21, GGS 4, paraphrase).
Quick check
?In the Mool Mantar, what does the phrase Karta Purakh mean?
Mission 2

In the universe, and beyond it

  • Immanent / sargun: God is present in and through everything created.
  • Guru Tegh Bahadur: as fragrance lives in a flower and a reflection in a mirror, God lives within all, so search within (GGS 684, paraphrase).
  • Transcendent / nirgun: God is also beyond creation: without form, beyond time, beyond all description.
  • Sikhs hold both. God is in the world, but God is not the same thing as the world.
  • If every star vanished, God would still be God.
Mission 2Read the source

Sukhmani and Anand

GGS 294, from Sukhmani Sahib by Guru Arjan (paraphrase):

  • God pervades the elements, the directions, the sun, moon and stars and every living being, and watches through every eye.
  • God is inside and outside every person; yet God stays unattached and cannot be measured.

GGS 921, from Anand Sahib by Guru Amar Das (paraphrase):

  • God placed His light in the body; only then did a person come into the world. Like mother and father, God gives each being its birth.
  • Maya (illusion: attachment to the world as if it were all there is) makes people forget God; remembering God, by the Guru's grace, brings joy.

Q1 Which words on GGS 294 show God as transcendent?

Show answer

Q2 According to GGS 921, what did God place in the body before a person was born?

Show answer

Mission 2

Why it matters to Sikhs

  • Respect for nature: the world is God's work and God is present in it.
  • Respect for people: God's light is in every person, so no one is worthless.
  • The world is real and good, not a trap to escape. Japji Sahib calls the earth a place to practise dharam (right living) (pauri 34, GGS 7, paraphrase).
  • So Sikhs live in the world, as working householders, rather than withdrawing from it.
  • Trust: everything is within hukam, so Sikhs try to accept what happens without fear.
Mission 2Diversity

Creation and science

  • Most Sikhs see no clash between God as Creator and the Big Bang or evolution: they answer different questions (how vs who and why).
  • They point out the Guru Granth Sahib already speaks of countless worlds and ages, and says only God knows when it began.
  • Some Sikhs read creation "by one word" as a sudden beginning, which they link to the Big Bang.
  • Some stress God within creation (sargun) in devotion, such as singing hymns; others stress God beyond it (nirgun) in silent meditation. Most combine both.
  • Non-religious view (e.g. humanist): the universe needs no Creator; science can explain it without God.
Mission 2Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain two Sikh beliefs about God as Creator. Refer to Sikh scripture.

  • Belief 1: God is Karta Purakh, the Creator Being. Source: the Mool Mantra. Development: everything depends on God, so Sikhs are grateful and respect all of creation.
  • Belief 2: God is present in creation and also beyond it. Source: God lives in all as fragrance lives in a flower (GGS 684). Development: Sikhs look for God within themselves and others, while knowing God is greater than anything made.

Mission 3

A rare chance

  • Sikhs believe the soul passes through many lives: Sikh teaching speaks of 8.4 million life forms (84 lakh joon).
  • Being born human is rare and precious.
  • Only humans can choose to remember God, live well, and unite with God.
  • So human life is not a test to pass and forget. It is an opportunity that can be used or wasted.
  • (Karma, rebirth and mukti in detail: a later mission.)
Mission 3Read the source

"This is your chance"

Guru Granth Sahib 12, a hymn of Guru Arjan (Raag Asa), recited daily in Rehras Sahib (paraphrase):

  • You have been given this human body. This is your chance to meet the Lord of the Universe.
  • Other tasks are of no use to you here. Join the company of the holy and remember only the Name.
  • GGS 921, from Anand Sahib by Guru Amar Das (paraphrase): God placed His light in your body before you came into the world; forgetting God for maya wastes the gift.

Q1 What is the human body given for, in this hymn?

Show answer

To meet God: to unite with God.

Q2 Does "other tasks are of no use" mean Sikhs should stop working?

Show answer

No: work and family matter; the point is that they do not last unless joined with remembering God.

Mission 3

Body, mind and soul

  • Body (sarir): given by God, temporary; a home for the soul. Cared for, kept free of intoxicants; never worshipped.
  • Man (mind): thoughts, desires, will. It can be ruled by selfish desire or turned towards God.
  • Guru Nanak: conquer your mind and you conquer the world (Japji Sahib, pauri 28, GGS 6, paraphrase).
  • Atma (soul): a spark of God's light (jot). It is not born and does not die; it longs to return to its source.
  • Guru Amar Das: O my mind, you are a form of divine light; recognise your origin (GGS 441, paraphrase).

Mission 3

Virtues: becoming like God

  • To unite with God, a Sikh grows in God's qualities: the virtues.
  • Five often taught together: sat (truth), santokh (contentment), daya (compassion), nimrata (humility), pyar (love).
  • Guru Nanak: truth is above everything, but higher still is truthful living (GGS 62, paraphrase).
  • Virtues are not just feelings. They show in honest work, fair dealing and courage to defend others.
Mission 3

Why unite with God, and what changes?

  • Why: the soul is separated from its source. Union ends the separation, ends rebirth (mukti) and brings bliss.
  • How it changes life (the three pillars of Guru Nanak):
  • Nam japna: remembering God's Name, e.g. daily prayers (Nitnem).
  • Kirat karni: earning an honest living.
  • Vand chhakna: sharing with others, e.g. langar and giving.
  • Also: sewa (service), joining the sangat, and resisting selfish desires.
  • Union is not only after death: many Sikhs believe it can begin in this life.
Mission 3Diversity

One goal, different paths

  • All Sikhs agree: human life is the chance to unite with God.
  • Amritdhari (initiated Khalsa) Sikhs: initiation, the Five Ks and the daily discipline of the Rahit Maryada are the fullest way to live this out.
  • Sahajdhari Sikhs: not (yet) initiated. Many believe a sincere, devoted, honest life also leads towards God.
  • Some Sikhs stress union after death; others stress living as one united with God now (jivan mukti).
  • Some stress personal prayer most; others stress serving others most.

Question: Explain why Sikhs see human life as an opportunity to unite with God.

  • Point 1: Human birth is rare among millions of life forms. Source: "this human body is your chance to meet God" (GGS 12, paraphrase). Development: so Sikhs try not to waste time on things that do not last.
  • Point 2: The atma is a spark of God's light. Development: it longs to return to God, so Sikhs build virtues such as truth and humility to become more like God.

Mission 4

One light in everyone

  • Sikhs believe in the oneness of humanity: God's light (jot) is in every person.
  • So all people are equal: whatever their caste, gender, religion, race or wealth.
  • After his experience at the river, Guru Nanak is remembered as saying: "There is no Hindu, there is no Muslim": God sees people, not labels.
  • In 15th-century Punjab, caste decided who you could eat with, marry or pray beside. The Gurus rejected it.
  • Equality includes complete equality of women with men.
Mission 4Read the source

"Do not ask about caste"

Guru Nanak, GGS 349 (Raag Asa) (paraphrase):

  • Recognise God's light within everyone. Do not ask about a person's caste or social class: in the world beyond, there is no caste.

Guru Nanak, GGS 473 (Asa di Var) (paraphrase):

  • Why call woman inferior, when kings are born from her? Without woman there would be no one at all.

Q1 What reason does GGS 349 give for ignoring caste?

Show answer

God's light is in everyone; caste counts for nothing in the next world.

Q2 Which argument does GGS 473 use for respecting women?

Show answer

Everyone, even kings, is born from a woman.

Mission 4

Guru Nanak: two meals

  • Sikh tradition tells how Guru Nanak stayed with Bhai Lalo, a poor carpenter of low caste.
  • A rich official, Malik Bhago, invited the Guru to a feast. The Guru refused.
  • Challenged, he squeezed Lalo's plain bread: milk came out. From Bhago's rich food: blood.
  • Meaning: Lalo's bread was earned honestly; Bhago's wealth came from exploiting the poor.
  • Guru Nanak also began the langar (free kitchen) at Kartarpur: all sit together in rows (pangat) and eat the same food.

Mission 4

Guru Amar Das: langar first

  • Guru Amar Das (third Guru) made the langar central: "first the pangat, then the sangat" (first eat together, then meet the Guru).
  • Sikh tradition says even the Mughal emperor Akbar sat on the floor and ate in the langar before meeting him.
  • He spoke out against sati (widows burning on a husband's funeral pyre) (GGS 787) and is remembered for opposing purdah (veiling and secluding women).
  • He organised Sikh communities into regions, and appointed women as well as men as preachers and teachers to lead them.
  • Your spec: "Guru Amar Das appoints women preachers".
Mission 4

Guru Gobind Singh and the Khalsa

  • At Vaisakhi 1699, Guru Gobind Singh (tenth Guru) founded the Khalsa.
  • The first five to offer their lives, the Panj Pyare (Five Beloved Ones), came from different castes and regions.
  • All drank amrit from one bowl, which broke caste rules about sharing food and drink.
  • Tradition says Mata Sahib Kaur added sugar sweets to the amrit: a woman's part in the first initiation.
  • All men take the name Singh (lion), all women Kaur (princess): one family, no caste surnames.
  • Women and men take amrit and join the Khalsa on equal terms.
Mission 4

Mai Bhago

  • During the long siege of Anandpur (1704), forty Sikhs left Guru Gobind Singh, signing a letter disowning him.
  • Mai Bhago, a Sikh woman from Jhabal near Amritsar, shamed them for deserting the Guru.
  • In 1705 she led them back to fight beside the Guru at the Battle of Muktsar (Khidrana).
  • The forty died in battle. The Guru tore up their letter and blessed them as the Chali Mukte ("forty liberated ones").
  • Mai Bhago was wounded but survived; Sikh tradition says she then served as one of the Guru's bodyguards.
  • She shows that a woman can lead men and fight with courage equal to any man.

Mission 4

Equality in Sikh life today

  • Langar: everyone, Sikh or not, sits and eats the same free food together.
  • Worship: any Sikh, man or woman, may read from the Guru Granth Sahib, lead prayers, sing kirtan or serve as granthi.
  • The Sikh Rahit Maryada (code of conduct) allows women to take part in all religious ceremonies.
  • Names: Singh and Kaur still replace caste names for many Sikhs.
  • Service: Sikh charities (e.g. Khalsa Aid, founded 1999) help people of every faith and none.
  • Why complete equality matters: it follows from one light in all; denying it would be haumai.
Mission 4Diversity

Ideal and practice

  • Most Sikhs: women and men are equal and may take any role.
  • Some Sikhs: equal in worth, but often with different roles in family life.
  • Caste has not fully disappeared: some families still consider it in marriage, and some UK gurdwaras grew from caste-based communities (e.g. Ramgarhia and Bhatra).
  • Women are often under-represented on gurdwara committees and are rarely chosen as Panj Pyare in ceremonies.
  • Some Sikhs campaign for change, e.g. for women to perform kirtan inside the Harmandir Sahib (Golden Temple); others defend existing customs there.
  • All agree the Gurus' teaching is equality; the debate is about practice.
Mission 4Evaluate

"Sikhs today fully live out the Gurus' teaching on equality."

  • Reasons for: the langar is open to all; any Sikh may lead worship; Singh and Kaur; the Rahit Maryada allows women in every ceremony; Sikh charities serve everyone.
  • Reasons against: caste still affects some marriages and gurdwaras; women are under-represented in leadership and as Panj Pyare; customs at some historic gurdwaras are debated.
  • Another view: no community lives its ideals perfectly; what matters is that GGS 349 gives Sikhs the standard to challenge themselves.
  • Judge it by: Should we judge by teaching or by practice? Is the gap closing? Whose experience counts: leaders' or ordinary Sikhs'?

Mission 5

What is langar?

  • Langar: the free meal served in every gurdwara, and the kitchen where it is made.
  • Anyone may eat: any faith or none, any caste, rich or poor, man or woman.
  • People sit side by side in rows (pangat) and eat the same simple food.
  • It is cooked, served and cleared away by volunteers.
  • Two Sikh ideas meet here: equality of all people, and sewa (selfless service).
Mission 5

Beginnings: Guru Nanak

  • The true bargain (Sacha Sauda): sent to trade, the young Nanak used the money to feed hungry holy men. Sikhs see the spirit of langar here.
  • At Kartarpur (c. 1522), Nanak's community farmed together, worshipped together and ate together.
  • Sharing food from honest work became part of the Sikh way: vand chhakna (sharing with others).
  • In a society divided by caste, where many would not eat with "lower" castes, eating together was a radical act.
Mission 5

Mata Khivi and Guru Angad

  • Guru Angad (2nd Guru) kept the langar going at Khadur.
  • His wife, Mata Khivi, ran the langar: she organised the kitchen and served everyone who came.
  • She is remembered for her generous care and for the rich rice pudding (kheer) she served.
  • Many Sikhs see her as a model of women's leadership in sewa from the earliest days.
Mission 5

Guru Amar Das: first langar, then the Guru

  • Guru Amar Das (3rd Guru, at Goindwal) made a rule: "first pangat, then sangat" (first eat together in the rows, then join the congregation).
  • Everyone who came to see him had to eat langar first, sitting on the floor with everyone else.
  • Sikh tradition says even the Mughal Emperor Akbar sat in the rows and ate langar before meeting the Guru.
  • The message: before the Guru, an emperor and a beggar are equal.
  • Guru Amar Das also appointed women as well as men to lead and teach Sikh communities, another sign of equality (more in Mission 4).

Mission 5

How langar works

  • Food: simple vegetarian dishes (e.g. dal, vegetables, chapatis, rice pudding), so people of any faith or diet can eat.
  • Cooking: volunteers prepare it with heads covered and hands washed; many recite "Waheguru" or Gurbani as they work.
  • Serving: people sit in rows on the floor; volunteers walk the rows serving; all receive the same.
  • Manners: take what you will eat, waste nothing, receive with thanks.
  • Funding: donations of food and money, including daswandh (giving a tenth of income).
  • Clearing up: washing dishes and floors is honoured sewa, done by doctors and teachers alongside everyone else.
Mission 5

Langar as sewa: tan, man, dhan

  • Sikhs serve with body, mind and wealth (tan, man, dhan).
  • Tan (body): chopping, cooking, serving, washing up.
  • Man (mind): organising, planning the rota, remembering God while working, welcoming guests.
  • Dhan (wealth): giving money, flour, vegetables, gas and equipment.
  • Sewa is done without seeking reward or praise. Sikhs believe it helps to defeat haumai (ego) and to become gurmukh (God-centred).
  • Purposes of langar: equality, humility, sharing, hospitality, and serving God by serving people.
Quick check

Mission 5

Langar today

  • Every gurdwara serves langar; the largest, at the Harmandir Sahib in Amritsar, feeds very large numbers every day.
  • Langar for the homeless: Sikh groups in many British cities serve free hot food on the streets.
  • Disaster relief: Sikh charities set up langar after floods, earthquakes and wars, and for refugees.
  • During the Covid-19 pandemic, many gurdwaras cooked and delivered meals to people self-isolating and to health workers.
  • Significance: langar takes the gurdwara's equality and sewa into the wider world.
Mission 5Diversity

Different practices and views

  • Floor or chairs? Many Sikhs insist on sitting on the floor, as the Gurus taught, a sign of equality. Some gurdwaras provide tables and chairs, especially for elderly and disabled people. The Akal Takht has ruled that langar should be eaten on the floor; practice still varies.
  • Simple or rich food? Some say langar should stay simple, so that no one competes to impress; others serve festive dishes on special days.
  • Inside or outside? Some stress langar in the gurdwara with the sangat; others see street langar for the homeless as the truest form.
  • All agree: langar is free, open to everyone, and given as sewa.
Mission 5Evaluate

"Langar is the most important form of sewa."

  • Reasons for: it involves tan, man and dhan at once; it goes back to Guru Nanak and was made central by Guru Amar Das; it feeds people and teaches equality every day.
  • Reasons against: sewa takes many forms: caring for the sick, teaching, cleaning shoes at the gurdwara, giving daswandh. Bhai Kanhaiya's care for the wounded is also held up as sewa. Some sewa outside the gurdwara (e.g. disaster relief) reaches people langar in a gurdwara cannot.
  • Another view: the attitude matters more than the task: any service done humbly, without wanting praise, is true sewa.
  • Judge it by: Who benefits, and how many? Does the server's attitude matter more than the act? What did the Gurus themselves practise?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The Mool Mantar (Guru Granth Sahib 1) opens the scripture: Ik Onkar, Satnam, Karta Purakh, Nirbhau, Nirvair, Akal Murat, Ajuni, Saibhang, Gurprasad.
  • God is one, true, creator, without fear or hate, timeless, never born, self-existent, known by grace; so no avatars and no images of God.

Mission 2

  • For Sikhs, God is Karta Purakh, the Creator Being: one God made everything by hukam, with no step-by-step creation story.
  • God is sargun (in and through creation) and nirgun (beyond it); Sikhs hold both.
  • So Sikhs respect nature and every person, and live in the world rather than withdrawing from it.

Mission 3

  • Human birth is rare: only humans can choose to unite with God (GGS 12). A human is body, man (mind) and atma (soul, a spark of God's light).
  • Virtues, nam japna, honest work and sharing change daily life.

Mission 4

  • God's light is in everyone, so caste, gender and wealth do not make anyone better (GGS 349; women honoured in GGS 473).
  • The Gurus lived it: Guru Nanak and Lalo; Guru Amar Das's langar and women preachers; Guru Gobind Singh's Khalsa; Mai Bhago at Muktsar.
  • Today: langar, women may lead all worship, Singh and Kaur; debate continues about caste and women's roles in practice.

Mission 5

  • Langar began with Guru Nanak's sharing, grew with Mata Khivi and Guru Angad, and was made a rule of equality by Guru Amar Das ("first pangat, then sangat").
  • Langar is sewa in action (tan, man, dhan), done humbly; today it reaches far beyond the gurdwara.
