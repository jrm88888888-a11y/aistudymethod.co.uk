Mission 1

Phagocytosis

  • Phagocytes squeeze out of capillaries (amoeboid movement) and move to the infection site.
  • A phagocyte recognises foreign molecules on the pathogen.
  • It engulfs the pathogen by endocytosis, enclosing it in a vesicle: a phagosome.
  • Lysosomes fuse with the phagosome and release hydrolytic enzymes, which digest the pathogen.
  • Your spec words these lysosomal enzymes as "lysozymes". Lysozyme is only one of the many hydrolases in a lysosome.
  • Soluble products are absorbed.
  • Some phagocytes then display the pathogen's antigens.
  • Say: "digested inside a phagosome by lysosomal enzymes" — not: "enzymes are released onto the pathogen".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly describes how a phagocyte destroys a bacterium?
Mission check

?In a phagocyte, the lysosomes cannot fuse with phagosomes, but every other step works normally. What happens to a bacterium that meets this phagocyte?

Barriers keep most pathogens out. For those that get in, a phagocyte must both engulf and digest.

Mission 2

What an antibody is

  • An antibody is a protein made by plasma cells.
  • It binds specifically to one antigen, because its binding site is complementary in shape.
  • Binding forms an antigen–antibody complex, which leads to the antigen's destruction.
Mission 2

Antibody structure

  • Four polypeptide chains: two heavy and two light, so a quaternary structure.
  • Chains held together by disulfide bridges. Overall: a Y shape.
  • Variable region at each tip: the antigen-binding site. Its tertiary structure differs between antibodies.
  • Constant region: the same in all antibodies of one class. Phagocytes bind here.
  • Two binding sites per antibody: it can bind two antigens at once.
Quick check
?Antibodies against measles and antibodies against tetanus toxin are compared. Which part of the molecule differs between them?
Mission 2

How antibodies disable pathogens

  • Agglutination: each antibody has two binding sites, so it links pathogens together. Bacteria clump.
  • Clumped bacteria cannot spread, and phagocytes engulf many at once: phagocytosis of the complex.
  • Say: "antibodies label and clump pathogens" — not: "antibodies kill or digest bacteria".
Quick check
?Why does an antibody cause bacteria to clump together?
Mission 2

First time slow, second time fast

  • Primary response (first exposure): few specific B cells must be selected, activated and cloned. Days pass; antibody peaks low. You may become ill.
  • Memory cells form and survive for years.
  • Secondary response (same antigen again): memory cells divide at once into plasma cells. Antibody rises sooner, much higher, and stays high longer.
  • The pathogen is usually destroyed before symptoms: you are immune.
  • Say: "memory cells persist" — not: "antibodies stay in the blood for life".

Mission 2

Active and passive immunity

  • Active: your own lymphocytes make antibodies and memory cells. Takes days. Long-lasting.
  • Passive: you receive ready-made antibodies. Works at once. Short-term: the antibodies are broken down and no memory cells form.
  • Examples: vaccination (active); antibodies from the mother, or injected antibodies (passive).
Quick check
?After a bite from a possibly rabid dog, a traveller is injected with rabies antibodies and also starts a course of rabies vaccine. Why are both given?
Mission check

?Illustrative data: a vaccine is given in two doses, one month apart. After dose 1, antibody rose slowly over two weeks to a low level. After dose 2, the same antibody rose within a few days to about ten times that level. What best explains the response to dose 2?

The second meeting is faster because memory cells are waiting, not antibodies. That is how a second infection, or a second vaccine dose, works.

Mission 3

The HIV particle

  • HIV (human immunodeficiency virus) is a retrovirus.
  • Genome: two copies of single-stranded RNA.
  • Enzymes packed inside: reverse transcriptase (and others).
  • Capsid: a protein coat around the RNA.
  • Matrix: a protein layer under the envelope.
  • Envelope: a lipid bilayer, taken from the host cell membrane.
  • Attachment proteins (glycoproteins) stick out of the envelope.
Mission 3

Inside a helper T cell

  • (1) The attachment protein binds a CD4 receptor on a helper T cell.
  • (2) The envelope fuses with the cell membrane. Capsid enters, then releases RNA and enzymes.
  • (3) Reverse transcriptase copies the viral RNA into DNA.
  • (4) The viral DNA enters the nucleus and is inserted into the host cell's DNA.
  • (5) The host cell transcribes and translates it: viral RNA and proteins.
  • (6) New particles assemble and bud out, wrapped in host membrane.
  • The inserted DNA can stay inactive for years, then switch on.
Quick check
?HIV enters some white blood cells but not others. Which statement identifies the cells it mainly enters, and why?
Mission 3

Read the data: from HIV to AIDS

  • The graph: a typical untreated infection (schematic).
  • Weeks 2–6: virus multiplies fast. Helper T cells fall. Often flu-like illness.
  • Years: few or no symptoms. Helper T cells fall slowly.
  • AIDS: helper T cells below about 200 per mm³, or an AIDS-defining illness.
  • Few helper T cells: B cells are not activated (few antibodies); killer T cells are not stimulated.
  • So opportunistic infections and some cancers take hold: e.g. TB, pneumonia, Kaposi's sarcoma.
  • These, not HIV itself, cause most symptoms and deaths.
  • Say: "HIV destroys helper T cells" — not: "HIV destroys the immune system".
Quick check
?A person with untreated HIV infection eventually becomes unable to produce antibodies against new pathogens, even though tests show that B-lymphocytes are still present in their blood in substantial numbers. Which statement explains this?
Mission check

?Two people infected with HIV were compared. Person X had 900 helper T-lymphocytes per mm³ of blood and no other infections. Person Y had 150 helper T-lymphocytes per mm³ and had developed pneumonia caused by a fungus that rarely causes disease in healthy people. Which conclusion is best supported?

HIV turns helper T cells into virus factories and destroys them. Without them, other pathogens do the killing.

Mission 4

Mission 4: How can a drug that once cured an infection stop working against the very same germ?

Make a guess: How can a drug that once cured an infection stop working against the very same germ?

Mission 4

Hit what only bacteria have

  • An antibiotic: a chemical that kills bacteria or stops them growing.
  • It blocks a process found in bacteria but not in eukaryotic cells.
  • Penicillin: blocks the enzymes that cross-link peptidoglycan in the bacterial cell wall.
  • Tetracycline: binds bacterial 70S ribosomes; protein synthesis stops.
  • Human cells: no peptidoglycan wall; 80S ribosomes in the cytoplasm.
  • So the drug has a target in the bacterium and none in our cells: selective toxicity.
Mission 4

Why viruses escape antibiotics

  • A virus has no cell wall, no ribosomes, no metabolism of its own.
  • It replicates using the host cell's processes.
  • Antibiotics block none of these, at any dose.
  • Antibiotics for a cold or flu: no benefit, but still selection for resistant bacteria.
  • Say: "viruses have no bacterial processes for the drug to block" — not: "viruses are too small for the drug".
Quick check
?Why do antibiotics fail to control infections caused by viruses?
Mission 4

How resistance spreads

  • A random mutation, or a gene gained from another bacterium on a plasmid, gives resistance before any drug is used.
  • The antibiotic kills susceptible bacteria. Resistant ones survive, reproduce, pass the allele on.
  • Resistance to each drug usually needs its own gene. Strains can collect several: multiresistant.
  • More antibiotic use, more selection: careful use slows resistance.
  • Say: "resistant bacteria were selected" — not: "the bacteria got used to the drug".
Quick check
?In a weed population of a hypothetical plant species, an allele gives resistance to herbicide H. Where the herbicide is not used, resistant plants produce fewer seeds than susceptible plants. After years of spraying with H, most plants in a field carry the allele. The farmer then stops using H. Which prediction about the resistance allele over the following generations is best supported?
Mission 4Practical

Antibiotics on a bacterial lawn

  • Required practical 6: use of aseptic techniques to investigate the effect of antimicrobial substances on microbial growth.
  • Spread a bacterial culture evenly over agar: a lawn.
  • Add paper discs soaked in different antibiotics. Control: a disc soaked in sterile water.
  • Tape the lid (not sealed all round). Incubate upside down at 25 °C for 24–48 h.
  • Measure the clear zone (zone of inhibition). Larger zone, more effective antibiotic, if concentration and disc size are the same (and the drugs diffuse through agar alike).
Mission 4

Aseptic technique

  • Aim: keep unwanted microbes out of the culture, and the culture away from people.
  • Disinfect the bench. Work beside a lit Bunsen burner: the updraft carries microbes away.
  • Flame the inoculating loop until red hot; let it cool.
  • Flame the neck of the culture bottle when opening and closing it.
  • Lift the Petri dish lid only slightly, at an angle.
  • Sterile pipettes, spreaders and forceps; dispose of cultures by autoclaving.

Mission check

?Tuberculosis is treated with four antibiotics at once for the first two months. Each drug acts on a different target in the bacterium. Why is a combination used rather than a single drug?

The drug never changed. The population of bacteria did, because the drug selected the few that could already survive it.

Mission 5: How can a paper strip pick out one hormone from the thousands of chemicals in a urine sample?

Make a guess: How can a paper strip pick out one hormone from the thousands of chemicals in a urine sample?

Mission 5

One clone, one target

  • An antibody binds an antigen with a complementary shape.
  • Monoclonal antibodies: identical antibodies made by one clone of cells.
  • So every molecule binds the same antigen, and nothing else.
  • Made in the laboratory, in large amounts.
  • They can carry a dye, an enzyme or a drug to one target.
Mission 5

The pregnancy test

  • After implantation, the embryo, then the placenta, secretes hCG (human chorionic gonadotropin). Some reaches the urine.
  • (1) Urine soaks along the strip. Mobile monoclonal antibodies, complementary to hCG and carrying coloured particles, bind any hCG.
  • (2) Test line (T): fixed antibodies to hCG catch the hCG–antibody complexes. A coloured line forms.
  • (3) Control line (C): fixed antibodies that bind the mobile antibodies themselves. A line forms whether or not hCG is present.
  • C and T: pregnant. C only: not pregnant. No C line: the test has failed.
  • Say: "antibodies complementary to hCG" — not: "antibodies that detect pregnancy".
Quick check
?A home pregnancy test strip contains monoclonal antibodies. Which statement explains how the test indicates pregnancy?
Mission 5

Delivering a drug to one cell type

  • Some cancer cells carry many copies of an antigen that normal cells carry few of, e.g. the receptor HER2.
  • A drug is attached to a monoclonal antibody complementary to that antigen.
  • The antibody binds only cells with the antigen: the drug is delivered there.
  • Fewer healthy cells are harmed: fewer side effects; a lower dose is effective.
  • E.g. trastuzumab binds HER2 on some breast cancers; one form carries a cell-killing drug.
Mission 5

ELISA

  • ELISA: enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. Detects, and measures, a protein in a sample.
  • (1) Capture antibody fixed to the well.
  • (2) Sample added. The target protein binds. Wash.
  • (3) Detection antibody, with an enzyme attached, binds the protein. Wash.
  • (4) Substrate added. The enzyme turns it coloured. More protein, more colour.
  • Washing removes unbound antibody. Otherwise the enzyme gives colour even with no target present.
  • Control well with no target: stays colourless.
  • Variant: antigen fixed to the well tests a patient's blood for antibodies, e.g. an HIV test.
Quick check

Mission 5

Ethics: the arguments

  • Vaccines. For: protect individuals and, through herd immunity, others. Against or concern: rare side effects; compulsion versus choice; who gets scarce doses first.
  • Monoclonal antibodies. For: targeted treatment, fast diagnosis. Concerns: production has used mice; very high cost limits access.
  • Trials of both: volunteers face unknown risks; informed consent is essential.
  • E.g. 2006, TGN1412: six healthy volunteers became critically ill in a first-in-human monoclonal antibody trial. Trial rules were tightened.
  • Exam answers weigh both sides and reach a reasoned conclusion.
Mission 5

Evaluating the evidence

  • Sample size: large enough to detect rare effects?
  • Control group with a placebo? Patients randomly allocated?
  • Double-blind: neither patients nor doctors know who had the drug.
  • Repeated by other groups? Peer reviewed?
  • Who funded it? Were all results published?
Quick check
?A company tests a new monoclonal antibody treatment. Which trial design gives the most reliable evidence that the treatment works?
Mission check

?A rapid test for virus X has monoclonal antibodies complementary to protein N of virus X, on mobile coloured particles and fixed at the test line. The control line works as in a pregnancy test. A person infected with virus Y, whose protein N has a different shape, uses the test correctly. What result is expected?

One clone, one shape, one target. That is how a strip finds a single hormone in a sample full of other molecules.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Innate defences respond the same way to any pathogen; phagocytes engulf by endocytosis and digest with lysosomal enzymes in a phagosome.

Mission 2

  • Antibodies are Y-shaped proteins with two complementary binding sites; they clump pathogens for phagocytes. Active immunity makes memory cells and lasts; passive immunity is borrowed antibody and fades.
  • Memory cells make the secondary response sooner, larger and longer: that is immunity.

Mission 3

  • HIV binds helper T cells, copies its RNA into DNA, inserts it into host DNA and is made by the cell; losing helper T cells leads to AIDS and opportunistic infections.

Mission 4

  • Antibiotics block processes found only in bacteria, so they do not harm our cells and cannot touch viruses.
  • Resistance evolves by selection: random variants that survive the drug reproduce, so every use of an antibiotic must be justified.

Mission 5

  • Monoclonal antibodies are identical and bind one complementary antigen, so they can find, report or deliver to a single target.
  • ELISA: antibody captures the target; an enzyme-linked antibody and substrate make colour in proportion to the amount; washing prevents false positives.
