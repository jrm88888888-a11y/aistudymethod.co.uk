Mission 1: Why do Sikhs start each day with a poem that says God cannot be grasped by thinking?

Make a guess: Why do Sikhs start each day with a poem that says God cannot be grasped by thinking?

Mission 1

Waheguru: one, wonderful

  • Waheguru: the name for God most used by Sikhs; roughly "Wonderful Lord" or "Wondrous Enlightener".
  • Sikhs repeat it in meditation; it is the Gurmantar given at Khalsa initiation.
  • Oneness: God is one, the only one, with no second and no rival.
  • The scripture begins with the numeral 1: Ik Onkar, "One God" (Guru Granth Sahib 1).
  • One God for all people, not a tribal God of Sikhs.
Mission 1Read the source

The Mool Mantar

Guru Granth Sahib 1, word by word (paraphrase):

  • Ik Onkar: one God, the one reality.
  • Satnam: Truth is God's name.
  • Karta Purakh: the Creator, present in creation.
  • Nirbhau, Nirvair: without fear, without hate.
  • Akal Murat: beyond time, undying.
  • Ajuni, Saibhang: never born; self-existent.
  • Gurprasad: known by the Guru's grace.
  • Sikhs believe Guru Nanak (1469–1539) first spoke it. Mool = root: the root of Sikh belief.

Mission 1

Sat: God is truth

  • Sat = truth, reality. Satnam: God's very name is Truth.
  • After the Mool Mantar, Guru Nanak adds a verse: God was true before time, true through every age, true now, and will be true for ever (Guru Granth Sahib 1, paraphrase).
  • So God alone is ultimately real; everything else changes and passes.
  • Truth is also a way of living: truth is higher than everything, but higher still is truthful living (Guru Granth Sahib 62, paraphrase).
  • Belief in Sat shapes honesty in speech, work and dealings with others.
Mission 1

Shabad: God as Word

  • Shabad = word; also each hymn of the Guru Granth Sahib.
  • Sikhs believe God is revealed through the Word, not through images or a human incarnation.
  • In Guru Nanak's dialogue with the Nath yogis (Siddh Gosht), the Word is the Guru and the attentive mind is the disciple (Guru Granth Sahib 943, paraphrase).
  • So the ten Gurus taught one Word; since 1708 that Word is Guru Granth Sahib.
  • K. E. Nayar and J. S. Sandhu, The Socially Involved Renunciate (2007), stress that for Guru Nanak union comes through the shabad heard and lived, not through yogic withdrawal (paraphrase).

Mission 1

The Japji: shape and use

  • Japji Sahib, by Guru Nanak, opens the Guru Granth Sahib (pages 1–8).
  • Shape: Mool Mantar → opening verse → 38 pauris (stanzas) → closing verse (salok).
  • Personal meditation: the first of the daily prayers (Nitnem), recited in the early morning (amrit vela).
  • Initiation: Japji is the first of the five prayers recited as amrit is prepared; the Panj Piare recite the Mool Mantar and the initiates repeat it.
  • Many Sikhs know it by heart.
Mission 1Read the source

Japji, pauris 1, 2 and 20

Guru Granth Sahib 1–4 (paraphrase):

  • Pauri 1: God is not reached by thinking, even a hundred thousand times; nor by silence; nor by piling up the world's goods. The way is to walk in God's hukam (will).
  • Many translations say "by thinking"; others read the word as ritual washing. Either way: no human technique reaches God.
  • Pauri 2: all things come to be by God's command; no one is outside it. Whoever understands it does not speak in haumai (ego).
  • Pauri 20: water washes dirty hands; but a mind stained by wrongdoing is cleansed only by love of the Name (nam).

Q1 According to pauri 20, what cleanses a mind stained by wrongdoing?

Show answer

Love of God's Name.

Q2 On the "by thinking" reading, why does pauri 1 reject thinking alone?

Show answer

God is beyond human reasoning; God is reached by living in God's will. On either reading, no human technique reaches God.

Mission 1

An awesome, liberating presence

  • Awe: Japji piles up the countless (asankh): countless worlds, devotees, scholars; even the time of creation is known only to the Creator (pauris 17–22, paraphrase).
  • Wonder at God's greatness, not argument, is the fitting response.
  • Closing verse: air is the Guru, water the father, earth the great mother; deeds are read out before Dharam, the righteous judge; those who meditated on the Name are freed, and many are freed with them (Guru Granth Sahib 8, paraphrase).
  • Liberating: release comes by grace and the Name, not by ritual (pilgrimage and austerity earn only "a sesame seed" of merit: pauri 21, paraphrase).
  • (Nam, hukam, grace and the five realms of pauris 34–37: next missions.)

Mission 1

A summary of Sikh belief

  • Mool Mantar: compact, creed-like; says who God is.
  • Japji: longer, poetic; says how to live towards God: hukam, nam, grace, the five khands, truthful living.
  • Together they are often called the summary of Sikh belief about God.
  • Sikh traditions differ on where the Mool Mantar ends: at Gurprasad (Sikh Rehat Maryada) or after the following verse "true… for ever" (some traditions, e.g. Damdami Taksal).
  • The Mool Mantar is singled out in your spec as the key text on oneness, sat and nam.
Mission 1Evaluate

"The Mool Mantar is more important than the Japji as a statement of Sikh faith."

  • For: it opens the scripture and almost every Sikh text and service; it is learned first and repeated at initiation.
  • For: short and memorable: a creed any Sikh can recite.
  • Against: the Mool Mantar is itself part of the Japji; they are not rivals.
  • Against: the Japji gives what a creed lacks: how to live (hukam, nam, truthful living) and the path to liberation.
  • Against: Japji itself warns that no human technique, whether thinking (or ritual washing), silence or cleverness, reaches God (pauri 1).
  • Judge it by: what "statement of faith" means (a definition or a way of life?); use in worship; content; Guru Nanak's own ordering.

Mission 2

Ik Onkar: one without a second

  • Ik Onkar: God is one; nothing exists beside God or outside God's power.
  • Before creation, Guru Nanak says, there was only darkness for countless ages: no earth, no sky, only God's hukam (will) (Guru Granth Sahib 1035, paraphrase).
  • Creation came from God's single word, and the whole expanse appeared (Japji pauri 16, paraphrase).
  • Symbol ੴ: the numeral 1 joined to Onkar, with an arc reaching outwards: one God, without end.
Mission 2

Nirgun and sargun

  • Nirgun: God without qualities: beyond form, beyond anything the mind can grasp.
  • Sargun: God with qualities: present and knowable in creation, the Word and the Guru.
  • One God, two ways of being known: God is nirgun and is also sargun (Sukhmani, Guru Granth Sahib 287, paraphrase).
  • Sargun does not mean God is born as an avatar (Ajuni): God is present in creation, never incarnate in one body.
Mission 2

Beyond the world, within it

  • Transcendent: beyond creation, time and space (Akal, Nirankar: timeless, formless).
  • Immanent: present in everything: the light within all is God's light (Guru Granth Sahib 13, paraphrase).
  • Creator and sustainer: God makes, supports and watches over all life.
  • Omnipotent and omniscient: all-powerful (everything happens within hukam) and all-knowing.
Mission 2

A personal God

  • Sikh scripture speaks to God as you: you are my father, my mother, my kin and my brother (Guru Granth Sahib 103, paraphrase).
  • God is also friend, beloved husband (the soul as bride), True King (Sacha Patshah).
  • So the formless God can be loved and spoken to.
  • These images are ways of relating, not claims that God has a body or gender.

Mission 2

Explaining the divine: propositional and analytic

  • Propositional: explain God in statements that can be listed and defined: "God is one", "God is never born".
  • Analytic: break the idea of God into concepts and study each in turn.
  • Examples: the Mool Mantar read as a list of attributes; the Sikh Rehat Maryada (1950) defines a Sikh as one who believes in One Immortal Being, the ten Gurus and the Guru Granth Sahib (paraphrase).
  • Academic accounts often work this way: Cole and Sambhi's chapter on Sikh religious thought (Anthology extract 1) explains God, nam, hukam and grace concept by concept (paraphrase).
  • Strengths: clarity, comparison with other religions, teaching and law. Risk: God becomes a list.
Mission 2

Explaining the divine: unitive and holistic

  • Unitive: God is known by union and experience, not by definition.
  • Holistic: the whole person knows God: mind, heart, senses, music, community.
  • Japji: God is not reached by thinking, even a hundred thousand times (pauri 1, paraphrase, on the common English reading; gives the other reading, ritual washing).
  • Vismad (wonder): Guru Nanak's response to creation is wonder upon wonder (Guru Granth Sahib 463, paraphrase).
  • The scripture is poetry set to raga: it is sung (kirtan), not only analysed.
  • Nikky-Guninder Kaur Singh, The Feminine Principle in the Sikh Vision of the Transcendent (1993): the Gurus' poetry uses rich, often feminine imagery to evoke a God beyond all categories (paraphrase).
Mission 2Diversity

How the explanation shapes attitudes

  • Gender: if God is nirgun and beyond gender, scripture gives no ground for ranking men above women. Guru Nanak: why call woman inferior, when kings are born of her? (Guru Granth Sahib 473, paraphrase).
  • N K Singh argues that English translations calling God "He" and "Lord" made the tradition sound more male than the Gurus' language (paraphrase).
  • Doris Jakobsh, Relocating Gender in Sikh History (2003): ideals of equality have often not been matched in Sikh practice and history (paraphrase).
  • Social: one light in all → rejection of caste ranking; langar for everyone.
  • Political: God as the True King → no earthly ruler is absolute; the Gurus and the Khalsa resisted tyranny (miri-piri).
  • Some Sikhs stress inward union and devotion; others stress the political and social demands of the same God.

Mission 2Evaluate

"Sikh understanding of the divine is better explained in unitive and holistic ways than in propositional ones."

  • For: Japji, on the common English reading, says God cannot be reached by thought; the scripture is sung poetry; the goal is union, not a correct definition.
  • For: holistic readings (N K Singh) recover imagery that analytic, male-centred translations lost.
  • Against: the Mool Mantar itself is a set of propositions; the Gurus did make clear claims (one God, never born).
  • Against: without propositions there is no shared identity, no Rehat Maryada and no way to compare or teach.
  • Judge it by: what the scripture itself does; whether the two methods compete or complete each other; what each does to attitudes on gender and society.

Mission 3

Nam: the Name

  • Nam (EDQ: Naam) = the Name of God; more than a word: God's presence and revealed nature in all creation.
  • All creatures, worlds and realms are upheld by the Name (Sukhmani, Guru Granth Sahib 284, paraphrase).
  • To remember the Name is to become aware of God everywhere.
  • Forgetting the Name is the root of the human problem.
Mission 3

Hukam: God's will and order

  • Hukam = command, order, will.
  • Everything comes to be by hukam; no one is outside it (Japji pauri 2, paraphrase).
  • The way to God is to walk in hukam (Japji pauri 1, paraphrase).
  • Understanding hukam ends haumai: you stop seeing yourself as the centre.
  • Accepting God's will (bhana) is not fatalism: Sikhs still work, choose and resist injustice.
Mission 3

Nadar: grace

  • Nadar = God's gracious glance; also called kirpa and, in the Mool Mantar, Gurprasad.
  • By karma comes the body (this birth); by grace comes the gate of liberation (Japji pauri 4, paraphrase).
  • Grace is a gift: it cannot be earned or bought by ritual.
  • But Sikhs are not passive: they meditate, serve and live honestly, and pray for grace.
  • So the way to unite with Waheguru: hukam accepted, nam remembered, nadar received.

Mission 4

The soul: a spark of the divine

  • Sikhs believe each person has a soul (atma, or jot: light).
  • The soul is a spark of Waheguru's light: O my mind, you are the embodiment of divine light; know your origin (Guru Granth Sahib 441, paraphrase).
  • Every person, of any religion, caste or sex, carries the same light.
  • The soul's goal: to return to and unite with its source.
Mission 4

Haumai: the self at the centre

  • Haumai = "I, I": ego, self-centredness.
  • It makes the self feel separate from God and others, and hides the divine light.
  • Scripture calls haumai a chronic disease, but says its cure lies within it too (Guru Granth Sahib 466, paraphrase): the same self that turns inward can turn to God.
  • Haumai is the root of the five thieves.
Mission 4

Maya: the passing show

  • Maya = attachment to the world as if it were the final reality.
  • Mainstream Sikh teaching does not call the world unreal or evil: it is God's creation and good.
  • When scripture calls the world "false" or a dream, it means passing, not non-existent.
  • The problem is clinging: to wealth, status, family, pleasure, as though they last.
  • Maya feeds haumai; haumai clings to maya.
  • Sikh image: the world is like a dream or a passing shade; enjoy it, but do not be owned by it.
Mission 4

Manmukh: facing the self

  • Manmukh = "facing one's own mind": self-willed, led by haumai.
  • Marked by the five thieves (panj chor): kaam (lust), krodh (anger), lobh (greed), moh (attachment), ahankar (pride).
  • The manmukh forgets the Name and stays in the cycle of rebirth (see Mission 5).
  • Not a fixed class of people: anyone can live as a manmukh, and anyone can turn.
Mission 4

Gurmukh: facing the Guru

  • Gurmukh = "facing the Guru": God-centred, led by the Guru's word.
  • Marked by the five virtues: sat (truth), santokh (contentment), daya (compassion), nimrata (humility), pyaar (love).
  • Lives in the world: family, work, service, remembering the Name.
  • The goal of Sikh life is to move from manmukh to gurmukh, by effort and grace.

Mission 4

Guru: from darkness to light

  • Guru = one who leads from darkness (gu) to light (ru) (traditional explanation).
  • For Sikhs, the true Guru (Satguru) is God, speaking through the ten Gurus and now the Guru Granth Sahib.
  • Without the Guru, the self cannot see past haumai; the Guru's word turns the face (mukh) from self to God.
  • So "gurmukh" literally names the person whose face is turned to the Guru.
Mission 4

The socially involved renunciate

  • K. E. Nayar and J. S. Sandhu, The Socially Involved Renunciate (2007), study Guru Nanak's dialogue with the Nath yogis (Siddh Gosht, Guru Granth Sahib 938–946).
  • The yogis renounced the world; Guru Nanak taught renouncing haumai, not the world.
  • Image: like a lotus untouched by the water it floats on, the gurmukh lives in the world unstained (Guru Granth Sahib 938, paraphrase).
  • Their reading: the gurmukh is a householder who is inwardly detached and outwardly socially engaged: family, honest work, service (paraphrase).
Mission 4

Values against manmukh

  • Your spec (1.3d): Sikh values express the reverse of manmukh qualities.
  • Each virtue answers a thief: santokh ↔ lobh; daya ↔ krodh; nimrata ↔ ahankar; pyaar (selfless love) ↔ kaam and moh; sat ↔ the falsehood of haumai.
  • Practices train the virtues: nam simran (remembrance), seva (selfless service), kirt (honest work), vand (sharing).
  • N K Singh, Sikhism: An Introduction (2011): Sikh ethics flows from experience of the divine, not from rules alone (paraphrase).
Mission 4Evaluate

"Haumai is the greatest obstacle to mukti."

  • For: haumai creates separation from God; scripture calls it a chronic disease.
  • For: the five thieves all grow from self-centredness.
  • Against: maya is just as strong: attachment feeds haumai.
  • Against: liberation depends finally on nadar (grace), so no human obstacle is decisive.
  • Judge it by: whether haumai and maya can be separated; the role of grace; Nayar and Sandhu's stress on renouncing ego, not the world.

Mission 5

The journey of the soul

  • Samsara: the cycle of birth, death and rebirth (Sikh phrase: aavan jaan, coming and going).
  • The soul passes through many life forms: traditionally 8.4 million (chaurasi lakh joon).
  • Human birth is rare and precious: the one chance to meet God.
  • You have been given this human body; this is your turn to meet the Lord of the universe (Guru Granth Sahib 12, paraphrase).
Mission 5

Karma

  • Karma = action and its consequences, carried from life to life.
  • By karma comes the body, this birth (Japji pauri 4, paraphrase).
  • Deeds, good and bad, are "read out" before Dharam, the righteous judge (Japji closing verse, paraphrase).
  • Karma is not fate: present choices matter, and a person can turn from manmukh to gurmukh.
  • Karma works within hukam, God's order.
Mission 5

Grace beyond karma

  • By karma comes the body; by grace (nadar), the gate of liberation (Japji pauri 4, paraphrase).
  • Karma explains where a soul is; it cannot by itself set the soul free.
  • Liberation is God's gift: Gurprasad.
  • So good deeds are not a payment for liberation; they are the life of someone open to grace.
Quick check
?A Sikh is born into a poor family. A classmate says: "That is his karma, so he must accept that his life is fixed." Which response best reflects Sikh teaching?
Mission 5

Mukti: liberation

  • Mukti = liberation from the cycle of rebirth.
  • For Sikhs it is union with God: the soul's light merging with the divine light, or dwelling in Sach Khand.
  • Jivan mukti: liberation while still alive: the jivan mukt lives in the world, free of haumai, absorbed in the Name.
  • Heaven and hell are not the goal: many Sikhs see them as states of separation or nearness to God.
Mission check

?Two Sikh brothers, Harpreet and Jasvir, argue about a teacher who is honest, humble and devoted to the Name. Harpreet says: "He has earned liberation by his good karma." Jasvir says: "He may already be liberated, by God's grace, even while he lives among us." Which is closer to Sikh teaching?

Karma explains where the soul is; grace sets it free, and Sikhs believe that freedom can begin in this life.

Mission 6

Punjab around 1500

  • Guru Nanak: born 1469 at Talwandi (now Nankana Sahib, Pakistan); died 1539 at Kartarpur.
  • Punjab was ruled by the Lodi sultans of Delhi: Muslim rulers, a mostly Hindu population, Persian as the language of government.
  • His father was a Khatri (a trading caste) and village accountant: Nanak grew up literate, among Hindus and Muslims.
  • Two religious worlds met in his Punjab: Hindu (caste, Brahmin ritual, pilgrimage, temples) and Muslim (mosques, qazis, Sufi orders).
  • Page numbers below are from the standard 1,430-page Guru Granth Sahib (GGS).
Mission 6

Islam and the Sufis

  • Islam taught strict monotheism: one God, no images. Nanak's teaching also begins with one God.
  • Sufis (Muslim mystics) sought God through love, longing, poetry and music, guided by a pir (master).
  • Sheikh Farid (1173–1266), a Chishti Sufi of Punjab: his verses are in the GGS.
  • Nanak's lifelong companion Mardana was a Muslim musician who played the rebab.
  • Nanak called Muslims to inner truth: make mercy your mosque, faith your prayer mat, honest living your Qur'an (GGS 140, paraphrase).
Mission 6

The Hindu world, and Lalla

  • Caste: birth decided status, work and marriage; Brahmins controlled ritual and Sanskrit scripture.
  • Twice-born boys received a sacred thread (janeu); women and low castes were excluded.
  • Popular practice: temple images, pilgrimage, fasting, ritual bathing.
Mission 6

The sant tradition

  • Sants: north Indian poet-saints, about the 13th–16th centuries: Namdev, Kabir (a weaver of Banaras), Ravidas (a leatherworker).
  • Nirgun bhakti: loving devotion to God without form (nirgun), not to an image.
  • Repeating God's Name; the Guru as an inner voice or Word; songs in everyday languages, not Sanskrit.
  • Rejected caste, idols, Brahmin authority and empty ritual; many sants were of low caste.
  • Shared Indic ideas: karma, samsara (rebirth), maya, mukti (liberation).
  • The GGS includes hymns of Kabir, Namdev and Ravidas.

Mission 6

Old words, new meanings

  • Maya: in Advaita Vedanta, the world as illusion. For Nanak, the world is real, God's creation; maya is the attachment that turns people from God.
  • Mukti: often after death, through knowledge, ritual or withdrawal. For Nanak, liberation can come in this life (jivan mukti), by God's grace (nadar) and the Name.
  • Karma and samsara: shared; but God's grace can end the cycle.
  • Guru: a human teacher. For Nanak, God is the true Guru, speaking through the Word (shabad).
  • Renunciation: Naths left society. Nanak taught detachment within family and work.
Mission 6

Nayar and Sandhu: the socially involved renunciate

  • Kamala Elizabeth Nayar and Jaswinder Singh Sandhu, The Socially Involved Renunciate: Guru Nanak's Discourse to the Nath Yogis (2007).
  • They study the Siddh Gosht (GGS 938–946), Nanak's dialogue with the Nath yogis (Siddhs).
  • Nanak uses yogic vocabulary (shabad, sahaj, the Name) but redefines it: live untouched by the world, like a lotus in water (GGS 938, paraphrase).
  • His ideal is the socially involved renunciate: inwardly detached, outwardly engaged in family, work and service.
  • Their point: Sikh thought grows in dialogue with Indic thought: inherited terms, transformed meanings.
Mission 6Diversity

How new was Nanak's message?

  • Revelation (most Sikhs): Nanak's message came from God in God's court; likenesses to others are because truth is one, not borrowing.
  • W. H. McLeod (1932–2009), Guru Nanak and the Sikh Religion (1968): Nanak's ideas belong to the sant tradition, but he expressed them with new coherence. McLeod rejected the idea that Nanak blended Hinduism and Islam.
  • Syncretism (an older outsider view): Sikhism as a mix of Hinduism and Islam. Most scholars and Sikhs reject it: Guru Arjan wrote that he is neither Hindu nor Muslim (GGS 1136, paraphrase).
  • Nayar and Sandhu: neither copy nor clean break; Nanak transforms the terms he inherits.
  • Many Sikh scholars object that "influence" language treats the Guru as a product of his time.

Mission 6Evaluate

"Guru Nanak was a religious innovator."

  • For: rejected caste, Brahmin ritual, idols and ascetic withdrawal; founded a community at Kartarpur with shared kitchen and worship.
  • For: began a line of Gurus and a collection of hymns that became scripture; the sants left no lasting institution like this.
  • Against: much vocabulary is shared with sants and Indic thought (nam, karma, samsara, mukti, nirgun God).
  • Against: Kabir also rejected caste and ritual; Sufis also taught love of one God through song.
  • Another view: many Sikhs say "innovator" misdescribes a Guru: the message is revealed, whatever words carry it.
  • Judge it by: Is newness about ideas or institutions? Do shared words mean shared meanings? Which sources count: the hymns, or later stories?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Waheguru is one, true (Sat) and revealed through the Word (shabad); the Mool Mantar is the root statement of these beliefs.
  • The Japji (Guru Nanak) moves from who God is to how to reach God: hukam, the Name, grace and awe.

Mission 2

  • Ik Onkar: one God without a second, both nirgun (beyond qualities) and sargun (present in creation), transcendent and immanent, personal, creator and sustainer.
  • The divine can be explained propositionally (statements, analysis) or in unitive, holistic ways (union, poetry, music); each shapes attitudes to gender, society and politics.

Mission 3

  • Nam is God's Name and presence; hukam is God's will and order; nadar is grace, God's gift.

Mission 4

  • The soul is a spark of Waheguru's light; haumai (ego) and maya (clinging) hide it.
  • Manmukh: self-facing, ruled by the five thieves. Gurmukh: Guru-facing, shaped by the five virtues, living in the world.
  • Nayar and Sandhu: renounce ego, not the world.

Mission 5

  • The soul passes through many lives (samsara); human birth is the precious chance to meet God.
  • Karma shapes birth, but liberation (mukti), union with God, comes by grace (nadar), and can begin in this life (jivan mukti).

Mission 6

  • Nanak (1469–1539) grew up in Lodi-ruled Punjab, among Hindu caste practice, Islam's monotheism, Sufi devotion and the sant poets.
  • He shared much vocabulary (Name, Guru, karma, maya, mukti) but changed meanings: a real world, liberation in this life, detachment within family and work.
  • Nayar and Sandhu: the socially involved renunciate, transforming Indic thought in dialogue with the Nath yogis.
