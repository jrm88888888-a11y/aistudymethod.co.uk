Statistics · charts and diagrams

Tally charts, frequency tables and pictograms

  • A tally chart records data as you collect it, in groups of five strokes.
  • A frequency table lists each category or value with its frequency (how many). A two-way table sorts by two features at once.
  • A pictogram uses a symbol for a fixed number of items, shown in a key: if one car symbol = 4 cars, then 321​31/2 symbols = 14 cars.
  • Part-symbols must be easy to read: halves or quarters.
Statistics · charts and diagrams

Bar charts: single, dual and composite

  • A bar chart shows categorical or discrete data: equal widths, gaps between bars, and an even frequency scale from 0.
  • A dual bar chart puts bars for two groups side by side, with a key.
  • A composite (stacked) bar chart stacks the groups in one bar: its total height is the total frequency.
  • Check what each small square is worth first.
Calculate
?The dual bar chart shows the number of library books borrowed by Year 7 and Year 8 from Monday to Thursday. How many more books did Year 8 borrow than Year 7 altogether over the four days?

Statistics · charts and diagrams

How graphs can mislead

  • Read the vertical axis before the picture. If it does not start at 0 (and has no break mark), small differences look huge.
  • In chart B the April bar looks 7 times as tall as January's, yet April's 1,030 members are only 3% more than January's 1,000.
  • Other tricks: unequal steps on a scale, missing labels, pictogram symbols of different sizes, tilted 3-D pie charts.

Statistics · averages and spread

Mean, median, mode and range

  • Mean: add all the values and divide by how many there are.
  • Median: put the values in order; the median is the middle one. With an even number of values it is halfway between the middle two. Its position is the 2n+1​(n+1)/2th value.
  • Mode: the value that occurs most often (there can be more than one, or none).
  • Range == largest value −− smallest value. It measures spread: how varied the data are.

Four measures from a list

Question

  3. 3

  4. 4
    Mode: 30 minutes (three times). Range: 110−20=90110 − 20 = 90 minutes
Answer
mean 40 min, median 30 min, mode 30 min, range 90 min

110 is an outlier. It lifts the mean to 40, above 6 of the 8 values. Without it the mean is 30. Finding the "middle" of the unordered list gives 230+35​=32.5(30+35)/2 = 32.5, which is wrong.
Statistics · averages and spread

Averages from a frequency table

  • In a frequency table each value xx occurs ff times, so each row totals fxfx.
mean=∑f∑fx​=number of valuestotal of all values​mean = (Σ fx)/(Σ f) = (total of all values)/(number of values)
  • Mode: the value with the highest frequency. Median: find its position, 2n+1​(n+1)/2, then use running totals.
  • Pets owned by 25 people (0 pets: 7, 1: 9, 2: 6, 3: 2, 4: 1): the median is the 13th person, and running totals 7, 16, … show they own 1 pet. The mode is also 1.
  • Divide by ∑fΣ f, not by the number of rows.

Statistics · averages and spread

Estimated mean from grouped data

  • In grouped data you do not know the exact values, so use the midpoint of each class to stand for every value in it.
  • Estimated mean =∑f∑(midpoint×f)​= (Σ (midpoint × f))/(Σ f). It is an estimate because the values are not all at the midpoint.
  • The modal class is the class with the highest frequency.
  • The range can only be estimated: largest possible value minus smallest possible value.

Statistics · averages and spread

Choosing an average and comparing data

  • Mean: uses every value, but an outlier (far from the rest) can drag it a long way.
  • Median: not affected by a few extreme values; best for data like pay or house prices.
  • Mode: the only average for categorical data, such as favourite colour.
  • To compare two data sets, compare an average and the range, in context: "P spent less time on average, and their times were more consistent."
Quick check

Mission check

?A firm says average pay is £60,000, yet 9 of its 10 staff earn £25,000. How?

The owner earns £375,000. Total pay is 9×25,000+375,000=£600,0009 × 25,000 + 375,000 = £600,000, and 600,000÷10=£60,000600,000 ÷ 10 = £60,000: a true mean, but higher than 9 of the 10 salaries. The median and mode, both £25,000, describe a typical worker far better.

Statistics · cumulative frequency and histograms

Quartiles and the interquartile range (Higher)

  • In ordered data the median splits it in half; the lower and upper quartiles (LQ, UQ) split it into quarters.
  • For nn listed values: LQ is the 4n+1​(n+1)/4th value, median the 2n+1​(n+1)/2th, UQ the 43(n+1)​(3(n+1))/4th.
  • Interquartile range (IQR) == UQ −− LQ: the spread of the middle half, unaffected by outliers.
  • For 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 9, 12: LQ is the 2nd value (4), median the 4th (7), UQ the 6th (9): IQR =5= 5.
Calculate
?The marks of 11 students are 23, 15, 30, 18, 12, 28, 20, 35, 17, 25, 22. Find the interquartile range.
Statistics · cumulative frequency and histograms

Cumulative frequency graphs (Higher)

  • Cumulative frequency is a running total: how many values are up to each upper class boundary.
  • Plot each total at the upper class boundary, start with 0 at the lowest boundary, and join with a smooth curve.
  • For nn values, read across from 2n​n/2 (median), 4n​n/4 (LQ) and 43n​3n/4 (UQ), then down.
  • For how many are more than a value, read up from it and subtract from nn.

Statistics · cumulative frequency and histograms

Comparing distributions (Higher)

  • Two data sets can share a median yet be very different. Compare an average (the median) and a spread (the IQR).
  • A smaller IQR means the middle half of the data is more consistent (closer together).
  • Write each comparison in context: "The median time for Group A was lower, so on average Group A was quicker. Group A's IQR was smaller, so their times were more consistent."
  • Use like for like: median with IQR, or mean with range.

Statistics · cumulative frequency and histograms

Histograms and frequency density (Higher)

  • A histogram shows continuous data with no gaps between bars. When class widths are unequal, the vertical axis is frequency density.
frequency density=class widthfrequency​frequency density = frequency/(class width)
  • The area of each bar represents the frequency: frequency=frequency density×class widthfrequency = frequency density × class width.
  • Example: the class 20<h≤3020 < h ≤ 30 has frequency 15 and width 10, so its bar has height 15÷10=1.515 ÷ 10 = 1.5.
Calculate

Mission check

?Class X: median 62, LQ 55, UQ 68. Class Y: median 62, LQ 44, UQ 76. Were their results the same?

The same median only tells you the middle marks match. Class X's IQR is 68−55=1368 − 55 = 13 and Class Y's is 76−44=3276 − 44 = 32, so X's marks were much more consistent, while Y had both weaker and stronger students. Always compare an average and a spread.

Probability · basics and relative frequency

The probability scale

  • Probability measures how likely an event is, from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). Evens is 0.5.
  • Write probabilities as fractions, decimals or percentages, never as "1 in 4" or "1 : 4" in an answer.
  • Words along the scale: impossible, unlikely, evens, likely, certain.
  • A probability can never be below 0 or above 1. If you get 1.2, something has gone wrong.
Probability · basics and relative frequency

Equally likely outcomes

  • When outcomes are equally likely: P(event)=total number of outcomesnumber of outcomes in the event​P(event) = (number of outcomes in the event)/(total number of outcomes)
  • A fair dice: P(5 or 6)=62​=31​P(5 or 6) = 2/6 = 1/3.
  • P(not A)=1−P(A)P(not A) = 1 − P(A). A fair dice: P(not 6)=1−61​=65​P(not 6) = 1 − 1/6 = 5/6.
  • The probabilities of all the possible outcomes, when they cannot happen together (mutually exclusive), add up to 1.
  • "Not A" is written A′A', so P(A′)=1−P(A)P(A') = 1 − P(A).

A missing probability

Question

  2. 2

  3. 3

Probability · basics and relative frequency

Relative frequency and expected outcomes

  • From an experiment: relative frequency =number of trialsnumber of times the event happened​= (number of times the event happened)/(number of trials). Use it as an estimate of the probability.
  • The more trials, the closer relative frequency tends to get to the true probability. A few trials can wobble a lot. Each flip or spin is unaffected by earlier ones: a coin has no memory.
  • Expected number of outcomes =probability×number of trials= probability × number of trials. It is an estimate, not a promise.
  • A fair (unbiased) coin, dice or spinner has equally likely outcomes. A large experiment whose relative frequencies are far from these suggests bias.

Probability · sample spaces, two-way tables and Venn diagrams

Sample spaces

  • A sample space is the list of every possible outcome. List it systematically so you miss none and repeat none.
  • Two coins: HH, HT, TH, TT. HT and TH are different outcomes (first coin, second coin).
  • For two dice use a sample space diagram: a 6 by 6 grid with one cell for each of the 36 equally likely outcomes.
  • When outcomes are equally likely:
P(event)=total number of outcomesnumber of outcomes in the event​P(event) = (number of outcomes in the event)/(total number of outcomes)

Probability · sample spaces, two-way tables and Venn diagrams

Venn diagrams

  • A Venn diagram sorts items into overlapping circles inside a rectangle. The overlap is in both sets; outside the circles is in neither.
  • Fill in the overlap first, then the "only" parts, then outside. All regions sum to the total.
  • Probability = (number in your region) ÷ (total in the rectangle).
  • The universal set (rectangle) is ξxi or Eℰ; A∩BA ∩ B is the overlap; A∪BA ∪ B is A or B or both; A′A' is not A.

Probability · combined events and tree diagrams

The AND rule and the OR rule

  • Events are independent if one happening does not change the probability of the other (two dice, a coin and a spinner, repeated spins).
  • AND for independent events: multiply. P(A and B)=P(A)×P(B)P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B)
  • OR for mutually exclusive events (they cannot both happen): add. P(A or B)=P(A)+P(B)P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B)
  • P(not A)=1−P(A)P(not A) = 1 − P(A), so P(at least one) =1−= 1 − P(none).
  • Higher: the AND rule and tree diagrams are Higher tier; adding mutually exclusive probabilities is Foundation.
Quick check

Probability · combined events and tree diagrams

Tree diagrams (Higher)

  • A tree diagram shows each stage as a set of branches. Write the outcome at the end of each branch and its probability beside it.
  • The branches from any one point add to 1.
  • Multiply along a path to get the probability of that combined outcome.
  • Add the paths that give the event you want (the paths are mutually exclusive).
  • Check: all the end probabilities together add to 1.

Probability · combined events and tree diagrams

Without replacement (Higher)

  • With replacement, the item goes back: the second-stage probabilities are the same as the first.
  • Without replacement, the events are dependent: the second branches change because there is one fewer item.
  • Take one away from the total on every second branch, and from the colour already taken on its own branch.
  • Then multiply along and add the paths as before.

Probability · conditional probability

"Given that" shrinks the total (Higher)

  • A conditional probability is a probability given that something has already happened.
  • "Given B" means you only look at the outcomes in B. B's total becomes the new denominator.
P(A given B)=number in Bnumber in both A and B​P(A given B) = (number in both A and B)/(number in B)

  3. 3
    Divide by the spin total, not by 120: 7230​30/72
  4. 4

Conditional probability from a Venn diagram (Higher)

  • "Given B": cover everything outside circle B. Circle B's total is the new denominator.
  • The numerator is the overlap (the part of B that is also in A).
  • If you are given set sizes and "neither", find the overlap first: overlap = A + B − (total − neither).
Calculate
?In a group of 50 students, 21 study history, 28 study geography and 8 study neither. A student who studies geography is chosen at random. Find the probability that this student also studies history. Give your answer as a decimal.
Probability · conditional probability

Reversing a tree with expected frequencies (Higher)

  • The second branches of a tree are already conditional: "late, given it rained".
  • To reverse the condition ("rained, given late"), imagine a round number, such as 100 or 1,000, going through the tree. These are expected frequencies.
  • Add up every path that ends in the given outcome: that is the new total.
  • Divide the path you want by that total.

Mission check

?1 in 100 people are ill. A 90% accurate test says you are. What is the chance you are ill?

Imagine 1,000 people. 10 are ill and 9 of them test positive; 990 are well and 10% of them, 99, also test positive. Given a positive result you are one of 9+99=1089 + 99 = 108 people, and only 9 are ill: 1089​=121​9/108 = 1/12, about 8%. The false positives from the large healthy group swamp the true ones.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Choose the chart for the data: pictogram, bar chart (dual or composite) or pie chart for categories; read every value against the scale and key.
  • Pie chart angle = frequency ÷ total × 360°; reverse it to find a frequency.
  • A vertical axis that does not start at 0 can make a tiny change look dramatic.
  • Mean =number of valuestotal​= total/(number of values); median = middle value of the ordered list; mode = most common; range = largest −− smallest.
  • From a table, mean =∑f∑fx​= (Σ fx)/(Σ f); for grouped data use midpoints, and the answer is an estimate.
  • Outliers drag the mean, not the median: choose the average that fits the data, and compare an average and a spread in context.
  • Quartiles split ordered data into quarters; IQR = UQ − LQ measures the spread of the middle half.
  • Cumulative frequency: plot running totals at upper class boundaries; read across from 2n​n/2, 4n​n/4, 43n​3n/4.
  • Histograms: frequency density = frequency ÷ class width, and bar area gives frequency.
  • Probability runs from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain); P(not A)=1−P(A)P(not A) = 1 − P(A); all mutually exclusive outcomes sum to 1.
  • Relative frequency estimates probability, and gets more reliable with more trials. Expected number = probability × trials.
  • A fair coin has no memory: past results never make an outcome "due".
  • List outcomes systematically; for two events use a grid. P(event) = favourable ÷ total, only when the outcomes are equally likely.
  • In a two-way table or Venn diagram, find the missing values from the totals first, then divide by the total you are choosing from.
  • A∩BA ∩ B is the overlap, A∪BA ∪ B is either or both, and A′A' is everything outside AA.
  • AND (independent events): multiply. OR (mutually exclusive events): add. P(at least one) =1−= 1 − P(none).
  • On a tree diagram, multiply along the branches and add the paths you want; each set of branches adds to 1.
  • Without replacement, the second-stage probabilities change: one fewer in the total and in the group already taken.
  • "Given B" means choose only from B: B's total is the denominator.
  • From a two-way table or Venn diagram, divide the overlap by the total of the given group; to reverse a tree, use expected frequencies of a round number.
