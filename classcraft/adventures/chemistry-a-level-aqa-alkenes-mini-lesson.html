Mission 1

Mission 1: Why does an alkene take bromine's colour away in the dark, when an alkane needs light?

Make a guess: Why does an alkene take bromine's colour away in the dark, when an alkane needs light?

Mission 1

Electrophiles

  • An electrophile is an electron pair acceptor.
  • It is electron-deficient: H⁺, or the δ+ atom of a polar molecule.
  • No charge needed: HBr (through Hδ+) and Br2 act as electrophiles.
  • Heterolytic fission: a bond breaks and both electrons go to one atom, forming two ions.
  • Radical reactions use homolytic fission instead: one electron to each atom.
Quick check
?Which set contains only species that can act as electrophiles?
Mission 1

Why alkenes attract electrophiles

  • Alkenes are unsaturated: they contain a C=C bond, so they can add more atoms.
  • The C=C bond is a region of high electron density.
  • It attracts electrophiles and donates an electron pair to them.
  • Alkanes have only C–C and C–H σ bonds: no electron-rich site. They react with bromine only when light makes Br• radicals.
  • The reagent adds across C=C. Double becomes single; nothing is lost: electrophilic addition.
Mission 1

The test for C=C

  • Shake with bromine water, at room temperature, in the dark.
  • Alkene: orange → colourless. Alkane: stays orange.
  • With Br2 in an organic solvent: CH2=CH2 + Br2 → CH2BrCH2Br.
  • In bromine water, water also adds and much of the product is BrCH2CH2OH. The colour change is the same.
Quick check
?Ethene is bubbled through a solution of bromine in an organic solvent at room temperature, in the dark. Which statement is correct?
Mission 1

Adding halogens and hydrogen halides

  • Room temperature. Each reagent adds one part to each C=C carbon.
  • CH2=CH2 + Br2 → CH2BrCH2Br, 1,2-dibromoethane.
  • CH2=CH2 + HBr → CH3CH2Br, bromoethane.
  • Cyclohexene + HBr → bromocyclohexane.
  • Unsymmetrical alkenes, such as propene, can give two products: Mission 2.
Mission 1

Adding water

  • Steam + an acid catalyst (e.g. H3PO4) adds H to one carbon and OH to the other.
  • CH2=CH2 + H2O → CH3CH2OH, ethanol. This is how ethanol is made industrially.
  • Cyclohexene + H2O → cyclohexanol.
  • Also: concentrated H2SO4 adds to ethene; warming with water then gives ethanol.
Quick check
?2,3-dimethylbut-2-ene, (CH3)2C=C(CH3)2, reacts with hydrogen bromide. What is the product?
Mission 1

Mechanism: alkene + HBr

  • H–Br is already polar: Hδ+–Brδ−.
  • Arrow 1: from the C=C bond to the Hδ+.
  • Arrow 2: from the H–Br bond to Br. Heterolytic fission: Br leaves as Br⁻.
  • A carbocation forms: a C⁺ with only three bonds.
  • Arrow 3: from a lone pair on Br⁻ to the C⁺. Product: bromoethane.
Mission 1

Mechanism: alkene + bromine

  • The electron-rich C=C repels the Br–Br electrons: an induced dipole, Brδ+–Brδ−.
  • Arrow 1: from the C=C bond to Brδ+.
  • Arrow 2: from the Br–Br bond to Brδ−, which leaves as Br⁻.
  • A carbocation forms. Arrow 3: from a lone pair on Br⁻ to the C⁺. Product: CH2BrCH2Br.
  • No light and no radicals: the C=C itself splits Br2.
Quick check
?Ethene reacts with hydrogen bromide by electrophilic addition. Which describes the curly arrows in the first step of the mechanism?
Mission 1

Ethene + sulfuric acid

  • Cold concentrated H2SO4 adds across C=C, through its Hδ+–O bond.
  • Arrow 1: from the C=C bond to the H of H–OSO3H.
  • Arrow 2: from the H–O bond to O: HSO4⁻ forms. A carbocation forms.
  • Arrow 3: from a lone pair on an O⁻ of HSO4⁻ to C⁺.
  • Product: CH3CH2OSO2OH, ethyl hydrogensulfate.
  • Warm with water: CH3CH2OSO2OH + H2O → CH3CH2OH + H2SO4. The acid is regenerated.
Mission check

?In the dark, at room temperature, but-2-ene quickly decolourises a solution of iodine monochloride, ICl. Butane does not react with it. The product is CH3CHICHClCH3. Which statement explains why but-2-ene reacts without light?

The electron-rich C=C pulls an electrophile towards it and splits it into ions. So bromine (or ICl) adds in the dark. An alkane has no such site: it waits for light to make Br• radicals.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Hydrogen bromide can add to propene two ways round, so why does one product win almost every time?

Make a guess: Hydrogen bromide can add to propene two ways round, so why does one product win almost every time?

Mission 2

Two ways round

  • Propene, CH3CH=CH2, is unsymmetrical: its two C=C carbons carry different groups.
  • H–Br can add two ways:
  • H to C1, Br to C2 → CH3CHBrCH3, 2-bromopropane.
  • H to C2, Br to C1 → CH3CH2CH2Br, 1-bromopropane.
  • The H⁺ adds first. Where it goes decides where the C⁺ ends up, and so where Br goes.
Mission 2

Carbocations: primary, secondary, tertiary

  • Count the carbon (alkyl) groups bonded to the C⁺.
  • Primary: one, e.g. CH3CH2CH2⁺. Secondary: two, e.g. CH3CH⁺CH3. Tertiary: three, e.g. (CH3)3C⁺.
  • Stability: tertiary > secondary > primary.
  • Why: alkyl groups are electron-releasing. They push electron density towards C⁺.
  • More alkyl groups spread the positive charge more, so the ion is more stable.
Quick check
?Which carbocation is the most stable?
Mission 2

The more stable carbocation wins

  • Route 1: H⁺ to C1 (the CH2 end) → secondary CH3CH⁺CH3.
  • Route 2: H⁺ to C2 → primary CH3CH2CH2⁺.
  • The route via the more stable carbocation has the lower activation energy, so it is much faster.
  • So 2-bromopropane is the major product; 1-bromopropane is the minor product.
  • The pattern is often called Markovnikov's rule. Explain it by carbocation stability.
Mission 2

Drawing it

  • Draw the mechanism through the more stable carbocation for the major product.
  • Arrow 1: from the C=C bond to the Hδ+ of HBr. Arrow 2: from the H–Br bond to Br.
  • H bonds to the CH2 end; C⁺ is on the middle carbon (secondary).
  • Arrow 3: from a lone pair on Br⁻ to that C⁺ → 2-bromopropane.
  • The minor product forms by the same arrows, through the primary carbocation.
Quick check
?2-methylbut-1-ene, CH2=C(CH3)CH2CH3, reacts with hydrogen bromide. What is the major product, and why?
Mission 2

Same rule, other reagents

  • Water with an acid catalyst: propene → mainly propan-2-ol; minor propan-1-ol.
  • H2SO4: propene → mainly propan-2-yl hydrogensulfate, which water turns into propan-2-ol.
  • The electrophile (H⁺ or Hδ+) adds first. The nucleophile goes to the more substituted carbon.
  • Symmetrical alkenes (e.g. but-2-ene) give one product: no major or minor.
Quick check
?2-methylpropene, (CH3)2C=CH2, reacts with water in the presence of an acid catalyst. What is the major organic product?
Mission check

?1-methylcyclohexene is a six-membered ring with one C=C. One C=C carbon carries a CH3 group and no H; the other carries one H. It reacts with hydrogen bromide. What is the major product, and why?

The two directions give different carbocations. The route via the more stable one is faster, so its product is the major one. For propene + HBr, that is 2-bromopropane.

Mission 3

Mission 3: When a plastic softens in heat, what is breaking?

Make a guess: When a plastic softens in heat, what is breaking?

Mission 3

Polymers

  • A polymer is a very long molecule made of many small monomers joined by covalent bonds.
  • Neighbouring chains are held together only by intermolecular forces.
  • Heat a plastic such as poly(ethene): the chains gain energy, overcome the forces between them and slide past each other. It softens.
  • The covalent bonds along each chain stay intact.
  • In poly(ethene), the forces between chains are van der Waals forces. Longer chains → more contact → stronger forces.
Mission 3

Addition polymerisation

  • One bond of each monomer's C=C breaks. The carbons join neighbouring monomers by single bonds.
  • Nothing else is made: the polymer contains every atom of the monomers.
  • Ethene → poly(ethene): [–CH2–CH2–]n.
  • Propene → poly(propene): [–CH2–CH(CH3)–]n. The CH3 is a side group.
  • The repeating unit has the two former C=C carbons in the chain, with extension bonds at each end.
  • Substituted alkenes too: chloroethene, CH2=CHCl → poly(chloroethene), PVC.
  • Name = "poly" + the monomer's IUPAC name in brackets.
Quick check
?Propene, CH2=CHCH3, forms the addition polymer poly(propene), written as [repeating unit]n. Which option gives the repeating unit and describes it correctly?
Mission 3

Back to the monomer

  • In a section of chain, find the repeating unit: two main-chain carbons for an addition polymer.
  • Keep each side group on the carbon it is attached to.
  • Remove the extension bonds and put a C=C between the two carbons.
  • –CH2–CHCl–CH2–CHCl– → repeating unit –CH2–CHCl– → monomer CH2=CHCl.
  • Check: the monomer has the same atoms as the repeating unit.
Quick check
?A section of an addition polymer is –CH2–C(CH3)2–CH2–C(CH3)2–CH2–C(CH3)2–. Which is the monomer?
Mission 3

Why polyalkenes last

  • A polyalkene is saturated: its chains have only strong C–C and C–H bonds.
  • These bonds are non-polar, so nucleophiles and electrophiles are not attracted.
  • So polyalkenes are unreactive: ideal for containers and pipes.
  • They are also non-biodegradable: microorganisms cannot break them down, so they persist for many years.
  • Polyesters and polyamides are different: their links can be hydrolysed.
Mission 3

PVC and plasticisers

  • Poly(chloroethene), PVC, has polar C–Cl bonds: dipole–dipole forces add to van der Waals forces between chains.
  • So pure PVC is rigid: window frames, drainpipes, water pipes.
  • A plasticiser is a small molecule mixed in. It gets between the chains and pushes them apart.
  • The intermolecular forces weaken, chains slide more easily: flexible PVC.
  • Uses: electrical cable insulation, flooring, waterproof clothing.
Quick check
?Why does adding a plasticiser make PVC flexible?
Mission 3

Landfill or incineration?

  • Landfill: cheap and simple, but polyalkenes persist, use land, and the material is lost.
  • Incineration (combustion): releases energy to generate heat or electricity, and cuts waste volume.
  • But it releases CO2, and can form toxic gases.
  • Burning PVC releases HCl, a toxic, acidic gas.
  • Remove it by passing the waste gases through a base (e.g. calcium oxide or carbonate): a scrubber.
Mission 3

Recycling

  • First, sort waste polymers into specific types.
  • Mechanical recycling: wash, shred, melt and remould into new products.
  • Saves crude oil and much of the energy used to make new polymer.
  • But: sorting and cleaning cost money; quality falls each time the polymer is reprocessed.
  • Feedstock recycling: heat or crack the polymer into small molecules, used to make new plastics and other chemicals.
Mission 3

Polymers that break down

  • Biodegradable polymers are broken down by microorganisms, e.g. poly(lactic acid), made from plant starch.
  • Often they need the right conditions, such as an industrial composter.
  • Benefits: less persistent plastic waste and, for plant-based polymers, less use of finite crude oil.
Mission 3

Making plastics more sustainable

  • Ethene can be made by dehydrating ethanol from fermentation.
  • Then poly(ethene) needs no monomer from crude oil.
Quick check
?A council burns mixed plastic waste, including PVC, to generate electricity. Which extra step deals with the main problem caused by burning the PVC?
Mission check

?Poly(propene) can be heated until soft, moulded into a new shape, and cooled. The same plastic can be remoulded many times, and its chemical properties do not change. Which statement explains this?

When a plastic softens, nothing covalent breaks. The forces between the chains are overcome, and the chains slide past each other.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • C=C is electron-rich, so alkenes undergo electrophilic addition with Br2, HX and H2O (acid catalyst).
  • Mechanism: C=C → δ+ atom; bond → δ− atom; lone pair of the anion → C⁺.
  • Bromine water: orange → colourless with C=C.

Mission 2

  • Unsymmetrical alkene + HX: two carbocations possible. Stability: tertiary > secondary > primary.
  • The more stable carbocation forms faster, giving the major product; the other route gives the minor product.
  • Alkyl groups release electron density towards C⁺, spreading its charge.

Mission 3

  • Addition polymer: C=C opens; repeating unit = two main-chain carbons with their side groups; monomer has the same atoms.
  • Softening overcomes intermolecular forces between chains; covalent bonds stay intact.
  • Polyalkenes are unreactive and persist: landfill, incineration (remove HCl), recycling, or degradable alternatives.
