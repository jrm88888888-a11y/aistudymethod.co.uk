Role of marketing

Meeting customer needs profitably

Marketing identifies, anticipates and satisfies customer needs profitably. A market-oriented firm researches customers first; a product-oriented firm leads with the product. Key measures: market share and market growth. Firms choose mass marketing (whole market) or niche marketing (a small specialist segment).

Concept — ethics and sustainability: marketing must avoid misleading claims and can promote sustainable consumption.

Marketing planning

Segmentation, targeting, positioning

A marketing plan sets objectives and a strategy to reach them:

  • Segmentation — splitting a market into groups: demographic, geographic, psychographic, behavioural.
  • Targeting — choosing which segment(s) to serve.
  • Positioning — the image a product holds in customers minds (shown on a position/perception map).
  • USP — the unique selling point that sets the product apart.

Market research

Primary and secondary; qual and quant

Primary research gathers new data first-hand (surveys, interviews, focus groups, observation). Secondary research uses existing data (reports, government statistics, internal records).

  • Quantitative data — numerical (how many, how often).
  • Qualitative data — opinions and reasons (why).
  • Sampling: random, quota and stratified — watch for bias and small samples.

The seven Ps

The extended marketing mix

The mix is the controllable tools a firm blends to meet customer needs. Services extend the classic 4Ps to seven:

  • Product · Price · Promotion · Place
  • People · Processes · Physical evidence

All seven must be consistent with the target market and positioning.

Product

Life cycle and the Boston Matrix

The product life cycle runs introduction → growth → maturity → decline; extension strategies prolong maturity. The Boston Matrix plots products by market share and market growth:

  • Star — high share, high growth.
  • Cash cow — high share, low growth (funds the rest).
  • Question mark — low share, high growth.
  • Dog — low share, low growth.

Price

Pricing methods

Common methods: cost-plus (mark-up), penetration (low price to enter), skimming (high launch price), competitive, psychological, loss leader, premium, price discrimination and dynamic pricing.

Sales forecasting

Time-series analysis (HL only)

Sales forecasting predicts future sales from past data. A time series contains a trend, plus seasonal, cyclical and random variations. A moving average smooths the data to reveal the underlying trend, which can then be extrapolated forward.

Forecasts aid planning (stock, staffing, cash) but assume the past predicts the future — risky when the market changes suddenly.

International marketing

Going global (HL only)

International marketing sells across borders. Firms choose between standardisation (one global mix, cheaper via economies of scale) and adaptation (tailoring the mix to local tastes, language and culture). They weigh opportunities (larger markets, growth) against threats (cultural mistakes, exchange-rate and legal risk).

E-commerce

Selling online

E-commerce trades goods and services online. Models include B2B (business to business), B2C (business to consumer) and C2C (consumer to consumer). Benefits: wider reach, lower fixed costs, 24/7 trading and data on customers. Costs: delivery and returns, cyber-security, and intense price competition. It reshapes every element of the mix, especially place and promotion.
