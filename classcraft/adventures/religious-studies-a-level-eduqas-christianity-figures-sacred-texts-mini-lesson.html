Mission 1

Mission 1: Matthew says Jesus' birth "fulfilled" five old prophecies. What did he mean by fulfilled?

Make a guess: Matthew says Jesus' birth "fulfilled" five old prophecies. What did he mean by fulfilled?

Mission 1

Matthew's story in five scenes

  • Matthew opens with a genealogy (1:1–17): Jesus is "the son of David, the son of Abraham".
  • Then five scenes (1:18–2:23), told from Joseph's side:
  • 1. Joseph's dream: Mary's child is "of the Holy Spirit"; name him Jesus (1:18–25).
  • 2. Magi from the east follow a star, visit Herod, worship the child in a house in Bethlehem (2:1–12).
  • 3. Flight to Egypt (2:13–15).
  • 4. Herod kills the boys of Bethlehem (2:16–18).
  • 5. Return after Herod's death; the family settles in Nazareth (2:19–23).
  • Each scene is tied to an Old Testament text.
Mission 1

The fulfilment formula

  • Matthew uses a set phrase: "that it might be fulfilled which was spoken by the Lord through the prophet".
  • Scholars call these formula quotations (or fulfilment citations); in textbooks, proof texts.
  • About ten in the Gospel; five in chapters 1–2.
  • They are the narrator's comment, not words of Jesus or of the characters (except 2:6, spoken by the chief priests and scribes).
  • Purpose: to show that Jesus' life completes Israel's scripture.
Mission 1Read the source

"God with us"

> "Now all this has happened that it might be fulfilled which was spoken by the Lord through the prophet, saying," (Matthew 1:22, WEBBE)

> "Behold, the virgin shall be with child, and shall give birth to a son. They shall call his name Immanuel" (Matthew 1:23, WEBBE), "which is, being interpreted, God with us."

> "Therefore the Lord himself will give you a sign. Behold, the virgin will conceive, and bear a son, and shall call his name Immanuel." (Isaiah 7:14, WEBBE)

> "behold, the young woman shall conceive, and bear a son, and shall call his name Immanuel." (Isaiah 7:14, JPS 1917, translated from the Hebrew)

  • Isaiah spoke to King Ahaz (c. 735 BC), as a sign that his enemies would soon fall.
  • The Hebrew word is 'almah (young woman); the Greek Septuagint has parthenos (virgin). Matthew quotes the Greek. Christian translations such as WEBBE keep "virgin" in Isaiah; the Jewish JPS translation follows the Hebrew.

Q2 Why do scholars say Isaiah 7:14 had a meaning of its own before Matthew?

Mission 1

The five proof texts

  • 1:23 ← Isaiah 7:14: born of a virgin; Immanuel.
  • 2:6 ← Micah 5:2: a ruler from Bethlehem, David's town.
  • 2:15 ← Hosea 11:1: "Out of Egypt I called my son". In Hosea, the son is Israel at the Exodus.
  • 2:18 ← Jeremiah 31:15: Rachel weeping for her children; in Jeremiah, Israel's exiles.
  • 2:23 ← "he will be called a Nazarene": no Old Testament verse says this. Suggested sources: netser ("branch", Isaiah 11:1) or nazir (Nazirite, Judges 13:5).
  • Pattern: most texts in chapter 2 are about places (Bethlehem, Egypt, Ramah, Nazareth).

Mission 1

Why Matthew uses them

  • Audience: most scholars think Matthew wrote for a largely Jewish-Christian community, debating with the synagogue: scripture was the shared authority.
  • Jesus as son of David: genealogy, Bethlehem (Micah 5:2).
  • Jesus as a new Moses / new Israel: a wicked king kills baby boys (Pharaoh, Exodus 1:22); the child escapes and returns "for those who sought the young child's life are dead" (Matthew 2:20), echoing Exodus 4:19.
  • Gentiles worship: the magi look ahead to "make disciples of all nations" (Matthew 28:19).
Mission 1Evaluate

Do the proof texts show prophecy fulfilled?

  • Claim: Matthew's proof texts show that Jesus' birth fulfilled Old Testament prophecy.
  • For: "Fulfil" can mean completing a pattern, not only predicting: Israel's story (Egypt, exile) is relived in Jesus. On this reading Hosea and Jeremiah fit.
  • For: Christians read all scripture as pointing to Christ (Luke 24:27); Jewish interpreters of the time also applied texts to their own day (e.g. the pesher commentaries at Qumran).
  • Against: Read in context, the texts had other first meanings (Ahaz's sign; the Exodus; the exile).
  • Against: 2:23 has no clear source; Isaiah 7:14 depends on the Greek parthenos.
  • Against: Some argue texts may have shaped the stories; Brown thinks the fit is often too awkward for that, so the stories came first.
  • Judgement criteria: What does "fulfil" mean (prediction or pattern)? Should Matthew be judged by modern or first-century methods of reading scripture? Does the theology depend on the history?
Mission 1

Matthew beside Luke

  • Agree: Mary and Joseph engaged; Joseph of David's line; an angel announces; conception by the Holy Spirit, not by Joseph; the name Jesus; born in Bethlehem in Herod's reign; raised in Nazareth. (Brown lists about eleven shared points.)
  • Differ:
  • Viewpoint: Joseph (Matthew) / Mary (Luke).
  • Home: Bethlehem, in a house (Matthew 2:11) / Nazareth; travel for a census (Luke 2:1–7).
  • Visitors: magi and a star / shepherds and angels.
  • After the birth: Egypt, then Nazareth / Temple in Jerusalem, then Nazareth.
  • Genealogies: through Solomon, from Abraham (Matthew 1) / through Nathan, back to Adam (Luke 3:23–38).
  • Dating: Herod died c. 4 BC; the census under Quirinius is dated AD 6 by Josephus (see Mission 2).
Mission 1

Harmonisation and redaction

  • Harmonisation: fitting the accounts into one story, assuming both are historical.
  • Examples: magi arrive later, perhaps up to two years (house, not stable; Herod's "two years old and under", Matthew 2:16). Matthew's genealogy is Joseph's line, Luke's Mary's (an old suggestion; others propose a levirate marriage). The Christmas crib scene is a harmonisation.
  • Redaction criticism: each evangelist edited his material for his own theology and readers; differences are clues to purpose, not only problems.
  • Matthew: son of David, new Moses, fulfilment, Gentiles. Luke: the poor, the Holy Spirit, the Temple, salvation for all (see Mission 2).
  • Different Christians weigh these: many evangelical and traditional Catholic readers harmonise; many critical scholars stress redaction.
  • Spec issue: "the relative importance of redaction criticism for understanding the biblical birth narratives".

Mission 1

Incarnation: two models

  • Incarnation: God the Son "became flesh" (John 1:14) in Jesus: fully God, fully human (Council of Chalcedon, 451).
  • Substantial presence model: God is present in Jesus in his very being (substance), not only by inspiration as in a prophet. "In him all the fullness of the Deity dwells bodily" (Colossians 2:9). Birth link: "Immanuel", "God with us"; the magi worship the child.
  • Kenotic model (Greek kenōsis, "emptying"): the Son set aside, or chose not to use, divine powers such as omniscience to live a truly human life.
  • Developed by Gottfried Thomasius (1802–1875) and in England by Charles Gore (1853–1932), The Incarnation of the Son of God (1891).
  • Birth link: a helpless baby, a manger, a refugee; Jesus "increased in wisdom" (Luke 2:52).
Mission 1Read the source

"He emptied himself"

> "who, existing in the form of God, didn't consider equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, taking the form of a servant, being made in the likeness of men." (Philippians 2:6–7, WEBBE)

Q1 Which phrase gives the kenotic model its name?

Show answer

"emptied himself": Greek ekenōsen .

Q2 What does Paul say Christ took on?

Show answer

"the form of a servant"; human likeness.

  • Critics ask: if the Son gave up divine powers, was he still fully God? William Temple (1881–1944), Christus Veritas (1924), asked who was running the universe meanwhile (paraphrase).
Mission 1Evaluate

Do the birth narratives show the incarnation?

  • Claim: The birth narratives provide real insight into the doctrine of the incarnation.
  • For: "Immanuel, God with us" (1:23); conception by the Holy Spirit; worship by the magi: divine origin.
  • For: Vulnerability, flight and growth fit the kenotic model's truly human Jesus.
  • Against: Neither Matthew nor Luke speaks of a pre-existent Son (one who existed before his human birth); Brown argues that for them the conception is when God's Son comes to be (a "conception christology"), unlike John 1 or Philippians 2.
  • Against: If the narratives are largely theological composition, doctrine rests on uncertain history; some (e.g. many liberal Protestants) hold the incarnation without a literal virginal conception.
  • Reply (for): Catholic, Orthodox and most Protestant teaching affirms the virginal conception as historical and as a sign of the incarnation (Apostles' Creed).
  • Judgement criteria: Is insight theological or historical? Must the narratives teach the doctrine fully, or only point to it? Which model (substantial presence or kenotic) do they support better?

Mission 2: Why does Luke's Gospel begin, and end, in the Temple, with shepherds and old prophets as witnesses?

Mission 2

Why chapters 1–2 matter

  • After a polished Greek preface (1:1–4), Luke switches to a style like the Greek Old Testament (Septuagint): the story picks up where Israel's scriptures left off.
  • Two parallel panels: John and Jesus, each announced, born, named, praised. Jesus is always the greater.
  • Temple frame: the story opens in the Temple (1:9) and the Gospel ends there (24:53).
  • Themes the whole Gospel will develop: the Holy Spirit, prayer and praise (four songs), joy, the poor and lowly, women (Elizabeth, Mary, Anna), salvation for all peoples.
  • Titles: Son of the Most High, Son of God, Saviour, Christ, Lord.
Mission 2

Two annunciations

  • An annunciation is an angel's announcement of a birth. Both follow an Old Testament pattern: appearance → fear → "do not be afraid" → message → question → sign.
  • Jesus (1:26–38): Gabriel comes to Mary in Nazareth, a virgin engaged to Joseph of David's house. Her son will be "the Son of the Most High" and reign on David's throne (1:32–33).

> "Behold, the servant of the Lord; let it be done to me according to your word." (Luke 1:38, WEBBE)

Q2 What does Mary's reply show about her as a model for Luke's readers?

Show answer

Trusting obedience: a disciple who hears God's word and accepts it.

  • Catholic and Orthodox tradition see Mary's "yes" (the fiat) as central to salvation; many Protestants stress it as a model of faith.
Mission 2

Two births

  • John (1:57–80): neighbours expect him to be named after his father; Elizabeth and Zechariah insist on John. Zechariah speaks again and sings the Benedictus ("Blessed be the Lord, the God of Israel", 1:68).
  • Between the annunciations and the births, Mary visits Elizabeth and sings the Magnificat (1:46–55): God "has put down princes from their thrones, and has exalted the lowly" (Luke 1:52). It echoes Hannah's song (1 Samuel 2:1–10).
  • Jesus (2:1–7): Caesar Augustus orders a census; Joseph and Mary travel from Nazareth to Bethlehem, "David's city".
  • Mary lays her firstborn in a feeding trough "because there was no room for them in the inn" (Luke 2:7). The Greek katalyma may mean a guest room.
  • Luke's contrast: the child he calls Saviour and Lord is born in lowliness, while the emperor issues decrees.
Mission 2

The shepherds

  • An angel appears to shepherds at night near Bethlehem (2:8–20).
  • "I bring you good news of great joy which will be to all the people. For there is born to you today, in David's city, a Saviour, who is Christ the Lord." (Luke 2:10–11)
  • A heavenly host sings "Glory to God in the highest" (2:14).
  • Why shepherds? Ordinary working people, often poor: the first to hear are the lowly. Shepherds also recall David, the shepherd king of Bethlehem. (Some scholars add that shepherds had low status; others think this is overstated.)
  • Counter-imperial echo: Augustus was hailed as "saviour" and his birthday as "good news" (Priene inscription, 9 BC). Luke gives these words to Jesus.
  • "Mary kept all these sayings, pondering them in her heart" (Luke 2:19).

Mission 2

The Presentation in the Temple

  • Day 8: circumcision and naming (2:21): the family keeps the Law.
  • Day 40: Mary's purification (Leviticus 12) and the presentation of the firstborn to God (Exodus 13:2). Their offering, "a pair of turtledoves, or two young pigeons" (Luke 2:24), is the offering of those who cannot afford a lamb (Leviticus 12:8).
  • Simeon: righteous, waiting for "the consolation of Israel"; promised by the Spirit he would see "the Lord's Christ" (2:25–26). He sings the Nunc Dimittis.
  • He warns: the child is "for a sign which is spoken against" (Luke 2:34), and "a sword will pierce through your own soul" (Luke 2:35), often read as pointing to the cross.
  • Anna: an elderly prophetess of the tribe of Asher, who worships in the Temple night and day and speaks of the child "to all those who were looking for redemption in Jerusalem" (Luke 2:38).
  • A man and a woman, both faithful Jews, witness together: a Lukan pairing.

Mission 2

Luke's redaction and the history question

  • Redaction: Luke shapes the birth to show his themes: the lowly raised, the Spirit at work, faithful Israel welcoming its Messiah, salvation for Gentiles, Jesus greater than John.
  • Problem: Luke dates the birth to the census "when Quirinius was governor of Syria" (2:2). Josephus dates Quirinius' census to AD 6; Matthew and Luke both place the birth under Herod, who died c. 4 BC.
  • No other source records an empire-wide census under Augustus, or a rule to register in one's ancestral town.
  • Defences: Greek prōtē could mean "before Quirinius governed"; Quirinius may have held earlier office in the region; Luke claims careful research (1:3); Mary as an eyewitness source ("pondering", 2:19, 2:51).
  • Scholarly views: Raymond Brown (The Birth of the Messiah, 1977) judges the census a confusion and the narrative largely theological; conservative scholars such as I. Howard Marshall (The Gospel of Luke, 1978) keep the history open.
Mission 2Evaluate

History, theology, or both?

  • Claim: Luke's Infancy Narrative is valuable mainly for its theology, not as history.
  • For: Septuagint style, songs and angelic speeches suggest careful composition.
  • For: The census date conflicts with Herod's reign; the accounts of Matthew and Luke are hard to harmonise.
  • Against: Luke claims to write "in order" after careful research (1:3); the shared core with Matthew suggests older tradition.
  • Against: "Mainly theology" is a false choice for many believers: Catholic, Orthodox and most evangelical Christians hold that the events happened and carry meaning.
  • Judgement criteria: What did ancient readers expect of history? How much weight can the census problem bear? Does the faith claim depend on each detail?
  • Spec issue: "the relative importance of redaction criticism for understanding the biblical birth narratives".

Mission 3

What is at stake

  • Paul makes the resurrection the hinge of Christian faith:
  • "If Christ has not been raised, then our preaching is in vain and your faith also is in vain." (1 Corinthians 15:14, WEBBE)
  • Three separate questions:
  • Did it happen? (history)
  • Can a historian show it? (method)
  • What kind of event was it? (theology: a body revived, a body transformed, or a change in the disciples)
  • Christians answer these differently. This mission maps the answers.
Mission 3

The scientific challenge

  • Modern science studies a world of regular natural laws. A dead man rising looks like the clearest violation of them.
  • David Hume (1711–1776), An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding (1748), Section X: "it is a miracle, that a dead man should come to life; because that has never been observed in any age or country."
  • So, for Hume, uniform experience always outweighs testimony to such an event.
  • Ernst Troeltsch (1865–1923), "On Historical and Dogmatic Method" (1898): historians judge the past by analogy with the present. Dead people do not rise now, so a historian cannot conclude that one did then (paraphrase).
  • Reply from many believers: the resurrection claims to be unique, an act of God, so analogy cannot rule it out in advance.
Mission 3

Experience: Bultmann

  • Rudolf Bultmann (1884–1976), "New Testament and Mythology" (1941).
  • The New Testament world-view is mythical (heaven above, demons, miracles). Modern people cannot share it, so the message must be demythologised: interpreted for what it says about human existence.
  • The resurrection is not an event of past history that a historian could check (paraphrase). Easter is the rise of faith in the disciples: they came to see the cross as God's saving act.
  • The risen Christ meets people now in the kerygma (the preached message), which calls for a decision of faith.
  • Related: Willi Marxsen (1919–1993), The Resurrection of Jesus of Nazareth (1968): "the cause of Jesus continues" (paraphrase).
  • Your spec: Bultmann on "the relation of the resurrection event to history".

Mission 3

N. T. Wright: the best explanation

  • N. T. Wright (b. 1948), The Resurrection of the Son of God (2003).
  • In Jewish and pagan usage, "resurrection" meant a new bodily life after death, not a spirit surviving. Wright's phrase: "life after life after death".
  • Early Christians held a remarkably consistent, changed form of Jewish hope: one man raised before the end of the age, in a transformed body (Wright: "transphysical").
  • Historian's question: what explains this belief?
  • An empty tomb alone suggests theft; visions alone would be read as a ghost. Together, the empty tomb and the meetings with Jesus are, in his view, the best historical explanation (paraphrase).
  • Wright's method is critical realism: real events, known through critically tested texts.

Mission 3

Resuscitation or resurrection

  • Resuscitation: a dead body returns to the same mortal life. The person will die again: the widow's son at Nain (Luke 7:15), Jairus' daughter, Lazarus (John 11:44).
  • Resurrection: raised to a new, transformed, imperishable life beyond death.
  • "Christ, being raised from the dead, dies no more. Death no longer has dominion over him!" (Romans 6:9, WEBBE)
  • Catholic teaching (Catechism of the Catholic Church, 646): Christ's resurrection was not a return to earthly life like the raisings he worked (paraphrase).
  • Contemporary critique: some read Luke's fish and flesh as a crude "resuscitation"; others (Rudolf Bultmann, 1884–1976, who read Easter as the rise of the disciples' faith; John Shelby Spong, 1931–2021, Resurrection: Myth or Reality?, 1994) reject a bodily event altogether and read resurrection as transformed faith.

Mission 3Evaluate

Can history decide the resurrection?

  • Claim: Historical study can show whether Jesus rose from the dead.
  • For (Wright): historians explain events by their best causes; early belief, the empty tomb and the meetings need explaining, and natural theories fail.
  • For: Paul's early list (1 Corinthians 15:3–8) names witnesses, some still living: a claim open to checking.
  • Against (Bultmann): faith built on historical proof is no longer faith; the resurrection's meaning is met in decision, not evidence.
  • Against (Troeltsch, Hume): history works by analogy and probability; a unique act of God lies outside its method.
  • Weaknesses each way: Bultmann seems to cut loose from what Paul meant (1 Corinthians 15:14); Wright's "best explanation" depends on whether God's action is allowed as an explanation.
  • Judgement criteria: What can historians prove? What does faith need? What did the first witnesses claim?
Mission 3Evaluate

Which reading fits the narratives?

  • Claim: The Gospels describe a resuscitation, not a resurrection.
  • For: Luke and John stress touch, wounds and eating (Luke 24:39–43; John 20:27): the same body back.
  • For: a physical reading answers the charge that the disciples saw a ghost.
  • Against: the same accounts show a body that passes locked doors (John 20:19), vanishes and is not recognised at first (Luke 24:16, 31).
  • Against: Paul's "spiritual body" (1 Corinthians 15:44) and "dies no more" (Romans 6:9) describe transformation.
  • Against (from another side): Bultmann and Spong say both readings miss the point: the texts express faith, not biology.
  • Judgement criteria: continuity and change: which do the texts keep together? Does the word "body" need to be physical?
  • Links to the nature of the resurrected body (see Mission 4).

Mission 4

What is the soul?

  • Hebrew Bible: a human is a "living soul" (Hebrew nephesh): dust given God's breath (Genesis 2:7). The soul is the whole living person, not a separate part.
  • Greek thought (Plato, Phaedo): the soul is immortal and is freed from the body at death.
  • Christian dualism: the soul is created by God, spiritual, and survives death; Jesus distinguishes those who "kill the body" from the soul they cannot kill (Matthew 10:28).
  • Aquinas (1225–1274): the soul is "the form of the body" (Summa Theologiae I, q. 76, a. 1): it makes the body a living human. A soul without its body is not a complete person.
  • Some modern Christians are physicalists: no separate soul; God re-creates the whole person (e.g. Nancey Murphy, Bodies and Souls, or Spirited Bodies?, 2006).
  • Philippians 1:21–24: Paul's "desire to depart and be with Christ" suggests the person is with Christ between death and resurrection.
Mission 4

Resurrection, not just immortality

  • Christian hope is the resurrection of the body, not only the survival of a soul.
  • The Apostles' Creed in Latin says carnis resurrectionem: "the resurrection of the flesh".
  • Roots: "Many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth will awake" (Daniel 12:2).
  • Jesus' resurrection is the "firstfruits": the pattern of what will happen to believers (1 Corinthians 15:20).
  • Traditional timeline: at death the soul goes to God; at the last day the dead are raised and soul and body are reunited.
  • The key question: what will the raised body be like?
Mission 4

Augustine: the resurrection of the flesh

  • Augustine (354–430), City of God (413–426), Book XXII; also the Enchiridion.
  • The same flesh will rise: whatever was lost from the body, in life or after death, will be restored (XXII.21).
  • Even a body burnt, eaten or scattered is not beyond the Creator's power: "not a hair of its head shall perish" (XXII.20–21; Luke 21:18).
  • Bodies will be perfected: deformities and blemishes removed (XXII.19); everyone raised at the age of full maturity, about thirty, Christ's age (XXII.15).
  • Sex remains: women rise as women, though there is no marriage (XXII.17; Matthew 22:30).

Mission 4

Spiritual resurrection

  • Many read Paul as teaching a spiritual resurrection: the person is raised in a new, non-physical form fitted for God's presence.
  • Evidence: "flesh and blood can't inherit" (15:50); "a spiritual body" (15:44); the seed is not the plant (15:37).
  • Origen (c. 185–c. 254): the risen body keeps the form (pattern) of the earthly body, not its matter.
  • On this view identity lies in the person (the pattern or soul), not in the same particles.
  • Others (e.g. N. T. Wright, The Resurrection of the Son of God, 2003) read "spiritual body" (sōma pneumatikon) as a body animated by God's Spirit: physical, but transformed. Augustine read it similarly.
  • Bultmann reads resurrection language as existential: about new life and faith now, not a raised corpse (see Mission 3).
Mission 4

The model: Jesus' risen body

  • Physical: "Touch me and see, for a spirit doesn't have flesh and bones" (Luke 24:39); he eats fish (Luke 24:42–43).
  • Wounds remain: Thomas is invited to touch them (John 20:27).
  • Different: he appears in a room with locked doors (John 20:19, 26); disciples do not at first recognise him (John 20:14; 21:4).
  • Christians see this as the pattern: continuity (same person, marks of his life) and transformation (no longer limited as before).
  • Augustine's readers stress the physical; spiritual-resurrection readers stress the change.
  • John 21: the risen Jesus cooks breakfast on the shore (21:9–13): bodily, yet mysterious.
Mission 4Evaluate

Must the resurrected body be physical?

  • Claim: The resurrected body must be physical, the same flesh restored.
  • For: Jesus' risen body could be touched and ate food (Luke 24:39–43); the empty tomb implies the same body.
  • For: Personal identity: without the same body, is it really me (or a copy)?
  • For: The goodness of the body: God made matter good; the creeds say "resurrection of the flesh".
  • Against: 1 Corinthians 15:50: flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom; the seed is not the plant.
  • Against: Practical puzzles (bodies decomposed, atoms reused) led Origen and others to locate identity in form, not matter.
  • Against: Jesus' body passed through locked doors: not ordinary physicality.
  • Judge it by: What makes a person the same person? How literally should Paul's and the Gospels' language be read? What did the early Church mean by "flesh"?
  • Spec issue: "the nature of the resurrected body".
Quick check
?A critic asks: "If the atoms of a dead body later become part of other living things, how can the same body rise?" Which response would Augustine give?
Mission check

?A Christian woman whose husband was lost at sea says: "His body is gone, but I believe God will raise him, changed but still him." Which belief best fits her words?

Christians hold together continuity (the same person) and transformation (a glorified body). Augustine and spiritual-resurrection readings differ on how much of the old body is kept.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Matthew 1:18–2:23 is told from Joseph's side; each scene is tied to an Old Testament text, four of them by Matthew's fulfilment formula.
  • The five proof texts (Isaiah 7:14, Micah 5:2, Hosea 11:1, Jeremiah 31:15, "Nazarene") show Jesus as Immanuel, son of David and new Israel; "fulfil" can mean completing a pattern, not only predicting.
  • Matthew and Luke share a core but differ widely: harmonisation reconciles them, redaction criticism explains them; the narratives are read as support for substantial-presence and kenotic models of the incarnation.

Mission 2

  • Luke 1–2 sets two births side by side (John and Jesus), in Septuagint style, framed by the Temple, and introduces Luke's themes: the Spirit, prayer and praise, joy, the lowly, women, salvation for all.
  • Redaction explains Luke's shaping; the Quirinius census raises the history question, answered differently by different scholars and believers.

Mission 3

  • Bultmann: the resurrection is the rise of faith, met now in the kerygma; Wright: the empty tomb and the meetings together best explain early belief in a transformed bodily resurrection.
  • Resuscitation returns a person to mortal life; resurrection, as Christians mean it, is a transformed life that cannot die.

Mission 4

  • Soul: Hebrew whole-person view; Greek immortal soul; Christian dualism (soul survives); Aquinas: soul as form of the body.
  • 1 Corinthians 15:42–44, 50–54: sown perishable, raised imperishable; "spiritual body"; flesh and blood cannot inherit; all will be changed.
