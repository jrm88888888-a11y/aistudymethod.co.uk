Why do accents survive TV, radio and the internet?

Make a guess: Why do accents survive TV, radio and the internet?

Language diversity · accent and dialectScreen 1

Accent, dialect and prestige

TermWhat it coversInvented example
Accentpronunciation only'bath' with /æ/ (most of the North) or /ɑ:/ (much of the South)
Dialectvocabulary and grammar of a region or group'I were sat there' (parts of northern England); 'bairn' for child (North East, Scotland)
Standard Englishthe dialect of print and schooling; it can be spoken in any accent'I was sitting there'
Received Pronunciation (RP)a non-regional accent with high social status, used by only a small minority/bɑ:θ/, /t/ fully pronounced in 'water'
  • Overt prestige: open, public status, linked to education and power (Standard English, RP).
  • Covert prestige: the hidden value of local forms, which signal loyalty, toughness and belonging.
Language diversity · accent and dialectScreen 2

Study: Trudgill, Norwich (1968 fieldwork)

Method60 Norwich residents sampled at random; ranked in five social classes; recorded in four styles: word list, reading passage, formal speech, casual speech. One variable: the -ing ending, [ɪŋ] or [ɪn] ('walking' or 'walkin'')
Findings[ɪn] rose down the class scale and from formal to casual style; men used more [ɪn] than women in most class and style groups
Self-evaluationAsked what they said, men often claimed more non-standard forms than they used; women claimed more standard forms
ConclusionWorking-class speech has covert prestige, especially for men
  • Evaluate: a 1968 city may not represent today; a recorded interview makes speech more careful (the observer's paradox); speakers are treated as reflecting class rather than choosing an identity.
Language diversity · accent and dialectScreen 3

Study: Labov, identity and status

StudyWhat Labov didWhat he found
Martha's Vineyard (fieldwork 1961; published 1963)Interviewed islanders on an island filling with summer visitors; measured the first part of the diphthongs in 'right' and 'house'Centralised (older island) vowels were strongest among speakers who identified with the island, such as fishermen and young people planning to stay
New York department stores (published 1966)Asked staff in Saks (expensive), Macy's (middle) and S. Klein (cheap) where something was, to get 'fourth floor'; then asked againMost /r/ after vowels at Saks, least at S. Klein; more /r/ in the careful repeat
  • Accent features act as identity markers: islanders used vowels to sound like Vineyarders, not visitors.
  • Evaluate: store staff were anonymous, so their own class and background are unknown.

Moving towards and away: accommodation and levelling

IdeaWho and whenKey point
Convergence and divergenceGiles, accommodation theory (1973 onwards)We shift our speech towards someone (convergence) to gain approval, or away (divergence) to stress difference; upward = towards higher status, downward = towards lower
Dialect levellingKerswill and Williams, Milton Keynes (2000)In a new town (designated 1967), 48 locally born children aged 4, 8 and 12 were forming a new shared variety, unlike their parents' mixed accents

?Why do accents survive TV, radio and the internet?

We mostly learn and keep our accents from the people we talk to and identify with, not from people on screens. Accents carry covert prestige and mark belonging, so they persist; they still change, but mainly through contact and levelling with neighbours, with TV at most speeding up changes already under way.

Mission 2

London teens say 'innit' and 'wagwan'. Where did that come from?

Make a guess: London teens say 'innit' and 'wagwan'. Where did that come from?

Language diversity · sociolect, age and ethnicityScreen 1

Sociolect and the social variables

  • A sociolect is the variety used by a social group; an idiolect is one person's own mix.
  • Social variables: class, age, gender, ethnicity, occupation and friendship networks all shape speech.
  • Age-grading: teenagers use most slang and non-standard forms, then often move towards the standard at work.
  • Change in progress: if young people keep a new form as they age, the language changes. Adolescents are often its leaders.
Language diversity · sociolect, age and ethnicityScreen 2

Study: Eckert, jocks and burnouts (fieldwork 1980–82)

MethodTwo years of ethnography in a Detroit suburban high school ('Belten High'), plus shorter visits to four other schools
GroupsJocks: school-oriented, linked to middle-class culture. Burnouts: locally and urban-oriented, linked to working-class culture
FindingsBurnouts used more advanced forms of a vowel change spreading in the region's cities; what students did and who they did it with mattered more than family background
Key ideaCommunity of practice: a group that meets around a shared activity and shares ways of speaking (Eckert and McConnell-Ginet, 1992)
  • Evaluate: one US school, 40 years ago; but it shows speakers actively use variation to build identity.
Language diversity · sociolect, age and ethnicityScreen 3

Study: the Linguistic Innovators project (2004–07)

WhoKerswill, Cheshire, Fox and Torgersen; funded by the ESRC
MethodRecorded 16–19-year-olds and over-70s in Hackney (inner London) and Havering (outer London); a follow-up project (2007–10) added speakers aged 4 to 50
FindingsA new variety, Multicultural London English (MLE), was strongest among inner-London teenagers; it was not tied to one ethnicity, and speakers used more MLE features the more friends they had from different language backgrounds
ExplanationA feature pool: children learn English from each other, drawing on Cockney, Caribbean Creoles, African and South Asian Englishes, learners' English and Standard English
  • MLE is a multiethnolect: a variety shaped by many languages and used across ethnic groups.

Do men interrupt more? How would you even test it?

Language diversity · language and genderScreen 1

Four ways of looking at gender and talk

ApproachKey namesBig claimA criticism
DeficitLakoff (1975)Women's speech is weaker and less assertiveTreats men's speech as the norm
DominanceZimmerman and West (1975); Spender (1980)Talk reflects male power: men interrupt and control topics; the language itself treats the male as the normAssumes all men dominate all women
DifferenceTannen (1990); Coates (1996)Boys and girls grow up in different talk cultures: 'report' versus 'rapport'; women friends build talk togetherIgnores power; overstates the gap
DiversityButler (1990); Cameron (2007)Gender is something we perform, and it varies with context, group and identityMakes general claims harder to test
Language diversity · language and genderScreen 2

Lakoff and her critics

  • Language and Woman's Place (1973 article, 1975 book) listed features of 'women's language': hedges ('sort of'), tag questions ('isn't it?'), empty adjectives ('divine'), super-polite forms, rising intonation on statements.
  • Her method was mainly introspection: her own and acquaintances' speech, and TV.
  • Holmes (1984): women used more facilitative tags (inviting others in); men more modal tags (checking facts).
  • O'Barr and Atkins (1980): in US trials these features appeared in low-status witnesses of both sexes: powerless language.
Language diversity · language and genderScreen 3

Testing the interruption claim

Zimmerman and West (1975)Recorded 31 conversations around a US university campus; in the 11 between a man and a woman, men made almost all of the 48 interruptions (46)
Their conclusionMen deny women equal status as speakers: dominance
ProblemsSmall campus sample; and does every cut-in count? A supportive overlap that agrees is not a grab for the floor
Later evidenceJames and Clarke's (1993) review found little consistent difference between the sexes; power and context matter more
  • To test the claim you must define an interruption, choose comparable contexts and code every cut-in the same way.

?Do men interrupt more? How would you even test it?

The answer depends on what you count. Zimmerman and West found men made almost all the interruptions in their 1975 sample, but later studies found little consistent difference once supportive overlaps were separated from floor grabs and contexts were compared. A fair test defines an interruption, compares like settings and looks at power as well as gender.

Language diversity · language and powerScreen 1

What kind of power?

ConceptWhoMeaningInvented example
Instrumental powerWareing (1999)Power held through a role or authority, backed by rulesA head: 'Phones away, please.'
Influential powerWareing (1999)Power to persuade, without the authority to commandAn advert: 'Join 10,000 happy members.'
Power in discourseFairclough (1989)Power exercised inside an interaction: topics, turns, terms of addressA teacher deciding who speaks next
Power behind discourseFairclough (1989)The social structures that make that control seem normalThe school system behind the teacher
  • Fairclough's critical discourse analysis shows how language makes unequal power look natural.
Language diversity · language and powerScreen 2

Face, politeness and indirectness

  • Face (Goffman, 1967): the public self-image we want to protect. Brown and Levinson (1978; 1987): positive face = the wish to be liked; negative face = the wish not to be imposed on.
  • Orders, requests and criticism are face-threatening acts (FTAs). Choice of strategy depends on power, social distance and size of imposition.
StrategyInvented example
Bald on record'Sit down.'
Positive politeness (stress closeness)'Shall we all grab a seat, guys?'
Negative politeness (avoid imposing)'Could you possibly sit down?'
Off record (hint)'Plenty of chairs free.'
  • Grice (1975): hearers assume we cooperate, so a hint creates an implicature they must work out.
Language diversity · language and powerScreen 3

Powerful and powerless speech

  • In unequal encounters the powerful speaker opens and closes the talk, sets topics, asks the questions, can phrase orders as questions (indirect directives), may interrupt, and chooses terms of address (first name downwards, title upwards).
  • O'Barr and Atkins (1980): low-status witnesses in US trials, of both sexes, used more hedges and intensifiers: powerless language.
  • Synthetic personalisation (Fairclough, 1989): mass texts address everyone as one friendly 'you'.

Mission checkScreen 9

?How does a headteacher's 'Shall we sit down?' differ from a friend's?

From a friend, 'Shall we sit down?' is a real suggestion between equals that can be turned down. From a headteacher it is a directive: the question form and inclusive 'we' are politeness strategies that soften the face threat, but the head's instrumental power means the student can't really say no.

Why do pilots say 'affirm', not 'yes' or 'affirmative'?

Make a guess: Why do pilots say 'affirm', not 'yes' or 'affirmative'?

Language diversity · occupational languageScreen 1

Talk shaped by the job

  • Occupational language is the language of a job: a sociolect shared by people who do the same work.
  • Jargon: specialist lexis (triage, bail, read back); initialisms (ECG, ETA) and acronyms, said as words (AWOL).
  • Everyday words with specialist semantics: in a hospital, theatre and discharge have narrow meanings.
  • Main functions: precision and efficiency, a shared group identity, and sometimes the power to include or exclude.
Radio wordFixed meaning in UK and European rules
AFFIRMYes
NEGATIVENo; or permission not granted; or that is not correct; or not capable
ROGERI have received all of your last transmission
WILCOI understand your message and will comply with it
Language diversity · occupational languageScreen 2

Discourse communities: Swales (1990)

John Swales (Genre Analysis, 1990) defined a discourse community by six features:

FeatureAir traffic example
Shared public goalssafe, efficient flights
Ways to communicate with each otherradio, written procedures
Using them to share information and feedbackread-backs, reports
Its own genresthe clearance, the weather report
Specialist lexisaffirm, wilco, squawk
Enough expert members to train newcomersinstructors, examiners
  • Jargon is only one feature: a community also has goals and genres.
  • Its lexis serves its goals: pilots say affirm, not affirmative, which a crackly radio can blur into negative.

Language diversity · occupational languageScreen 4

Institutional talk: Drew and Heritage (1992)

Paul Drew and John Heritage (Talk at Work, 1992) set out three features of talk in institutions:

  • Goal orientation: talk is aimed at the institution's task (a diagnosis, a verdict, a sale), tied to roles such as doctor and patient.
  • Constraints: there are limits on what counts as an allowable contribution, and who may ask the questions.
  • Inferential frameworks: words are interpreted in ways particular to that setting.
  • These often make the talk asymmetrical: the professional controls topics and turns.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Why do pilots say 'affirm', not 'yes' or 'affirmative'?

Because in air traffic control, a misheard word can be dangerous. 'Yes' is short and easily lost, and 'affirmative' shares its ending with 'negative', so the rules fix one clear word, AFFIRM. Like other occupational lexis, it serves the community's goals first; it also marks who belongs.

Mission 6

Most English speakers aren't native speakers. Whose English is it?

Make a guess: Most English speakers aren't native speakers. Whose English is it?

Language diversity · global EnglishScreen 1

How English went global

  • Settlement: from the 1600s, speakers moved to North America, then Australia and New Zealand; English became the main first language there.
  • Colonial rule: in India, Nigeria and Singapore, English became a language of government, law and education alongside local languages.
  • Globalisation: since 1945, business, science, aviation, film and the internet made it the main international lingua franca.
  • David Crystal (English as a Global Language, 2nd ed. 2003) argues a language goes global through the power of its speakers, not because it is simpler.
Language diversity · global EnglishScreen 2

Language diversity · global EnglishScreen 4

Pidgins, creoles and new Englishes

  • A pidgin is a contact language with no native speakers: simple grammar, vocabulary mostly from one language.
  • A creole develops when children learn a pidgin as their first language; its grammar and vocabulary expand.
  • Tok Pisin, an official language of Papua New Guinea, is now a creole: gras (hair, from 'grass'), haus sik (hospital), bilong (of, from 'belong').
  • New Englishes coin forms: Indian English prepone (bring forward), the Singapore English particle lah.

Language diversity · global EnglishScreen 6

Whose English? Attitudes and debates

  • English as a lingua franca (ELF): English between speakers with different first languages; success means being understood, not sounding native (Jennifer Jenkins, 2000).
  • Robert Phillipson (Linguistic Imperialism, 1992) argues English's dominance is kept up by structural and cultural inequalities with other languages.
  • Crystal (2003) sees a global language as useful for international contact while warning about threats to other languages.
  • Prescriptive attitudes persist: Singapore's government launched the Speak Good English Movement (2000) to steer speakers from Singlish to standard English.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Most English speakers aren't native speakers. Whose English is it?

English now has many owners. It spread through settlement, colonial rule and globalisation, and today most speakers use it as an additional language, developing their own norms. But ownership is also a question of power: attitudes still rank some varieties above others, which is why linguists debate it.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Accent is pronunciation; dialect is vocabulary and grammar. Standard English and RP carry overt prestige; local forms carry covert prestige.
  • Trudgill (Norwich) and Labov (Martha's Vineyard, New York) link accent to class, style and identity; Giles explains short-term shifts (convergence, divergence).
  • Regional speech changes mainly through contact: dialect levelling (Milton Keynes), Estuary English; TV may speed change but doesn't replace contact.
  • A sociolect is a group's variety; age, class, ethnicity, gender and friendship networks all shape it, and teenagers often lead change.
  • Eckert's jocks and burnouts show speakers using variation to build identity within communities of practice.
  • MLE (Linguistic Innovators, 2004–07) is a multiethnolect formed from a feature pool in multiethnic friendship groups; labels like 'Jafaican' misdescribe it.
  • Four approaches: deficit (Lakoff), dominance (Zimmerman and West, Spender), difference (Tannen, Coates), diversity (Cameron, Butler). Each has evidence and critics.
  • Lakoff's features were challenged by Holmes (functions of tags) and O'Barr and Atkins (powerless language, linked to status not sex).
  • Claims about interruption depend on definitions and context; later reviews found little consistent sex difference.
  • Power can be instrumental (from a role) or influential (from persuasion) (Wareing); Fairclough separates power in discourse from power behind it.
  • Brown and Levinson's face and politeness strategies, and Grice's implicature, explain how indirect forms can carry commands.
  • In unequal encounters the powerful control topics, turns and address; O'Barr and Atkins linked 'powerless' features to status.
  • Occupational language is a sociolect: jargon, initialisms and specialist meanings serve precision, efficiency and group identity, and can include or exclude.
  • Swales (1990): a discourse community has shared goals, ways to communicate, feedback, genres, specialist lexis and expert members.
  • Drew and Heritage (1992): institutional talk is goal-oriented, constrained and read through the setting's own frameworks, so it is often asymmetrical.
  • In the Paper 2 essay, evaluate: weigh functions against each other and support claims with data and research.
  • English spread by settlement, colonial rule and globalisation; Crystal (2003) links a language's global spread to its speakers' power.
  • Kachru (1985): Inner (norm-providing), Outer (norm-developing) and Expanding (norm-dependent) circles; criticised for sorting nations, not speakers.
  • Pidgins have no native speakers; creoles do (Tok Pisin). New Englishes create their own forms.
  • Evaluate attitudes: ELF (Jenkins), linguistic imperialism (Phillipson), prescriptive campaigns.
