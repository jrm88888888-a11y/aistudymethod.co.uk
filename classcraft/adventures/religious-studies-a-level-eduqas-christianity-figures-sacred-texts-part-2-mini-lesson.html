Mission 1

A library, not one book

  • Biblia (Greek): "the books". The Bible is a library written over many centuries by many authors.
  • Old Testament: built on the Jewish scriptures. New Testament: 27 books about Jesus and the first Christians.
  • Genres: law, history, poetry, prophecy, wisdom, gospel, letters, apocalyptic.
  • Protestant Bibles: 66 books. Catholic Bibles: 73, adding the deuterocanonical books (e.g. Tobit, Sirach, 1–2 Maccabees). Orthodox Bibles include a few more.
  • Most Protestants call those extra books the Apocrypha: useful reading, but not Scripture.
  • The canon (Greek kanon, "measuring rod"): the agreed list of books that count as Scripture.
Mission 1

How the Old Testament canon formed

  • The Hebrew Bible has three parts: Torah (Law), Prophets, Writings. Jewish communities accepted them in stages; the Writings were settled by about the 2nd century CE.
  • The older idea of a single "Council of Jamnia" (c. 90 CE) fixing the list is now widely doubted.
  • The Septuagint (Greek translation, 3rd–1st centuries BCE) included extra books. The early Church mostly read the Old Testament in Greek.
  • Jerome (c. 347–420) preferred the Hebrew list, but his Latin Vulgate came to include the extra books.
  • Reformation: Luther's Bible (1534) placed them in a separate section. Catholic Council of Trent (1546) declared them fully canonical.
  • Order matters too: the Hebrew Bible ends with Chronicles; Protestant and most modern Catholic Old Testaments end with the prophets (Malachi), pointing forward to Christ.
Mission 1

How the New Testament canon formed

  • Earliest texts: Paul's letters (c. 50s–60s CE); the Gospels followed (c. 65–100 CE, in most scholars' dating).
  • Marcion (c. 140) accepted only an edited Luke and ten of Paul's letters; the Church's rejection of him pushed it to define its list.
  • Irenaeus (c. 180) defended exactly four Gospels.
  • Tests Christians used: apostolic origin (by or close to an apostle); orthodoxy (agreeing with the rule of faith); catholicity (widely used); use in worship.
  • Some books were disputed for a long time (e.g. Hebrews, James, 2 Peter, Revelation).
  • Athanasius, Festal Letter 39 (367), is the first known list of exactly our 27 books; North African councils (Hippo 393, Carthage 397) confirmed it for the West.
  • So the canon was recognised over time, not decreed in one moment.

Mission 1

Three views of the Bible's nature

  • The literal word of God: God guided every word; the Bible is inerrant (without error) in all it says, including history and science. Used as a direct rulebook. Held by many fundamentalist and some conservative evangelical Christians.
  • Inspired by God, written by humans: God worked through writers' language, culture and limits. Inerrant (or infallible) in matters of faith and salvation; genre and context matter. Held by most Catholic, Orthodox and mainstream Protestant Christians.
  • A human record of experiences of God: written by people of faith, inspired as great writing is inspired; open to reinterpretation. Held by many liberal Christians.
  • Impact on use: the first view reads teachings as timeless commands; the others ask what a passage meant then and how its principle applies now.
Mission 1

Objective inspiration

  • Objective view: inspiration lies in the text itself. God is its true author, so the words carry God's truth whether or not a reader feels it.
  • Strong forms: verbal, plenary inspiration (every word, in every part); at the extreme, a dictation model (rarely held by scholars).
  • Linked to inerrancy, e.g. the Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy (1978).
  • Catholic teaching (Dei Verbum 11, 1965): God is the author, and the human writers are true authors too; Scripture teaches without error the truth God willed for our salvation (paraphrase).
  • Strength: a fixed, public authority. Challenge: tensions between texts, and the obvious human features of the writing.
Mission 1

Subjective inspiration

  • Subjective view: inspiration lies in people: the writers' experience of God, and the Spirit at work in the reader.
  • The writers were inspired as people of faith; the text is their human response, shaped by their culture.
  • The Bible becomes God's word when God speaks through it to the reader. Karl Barth (1886–1968, Church Dogmatics I) called Scripture a witness to the Word of God, Jesus Christ (paraphrase).
  • Friedrich Schleiermacher (1768–1834) located inspiration in the writers' religious consciousness.
  • Strength: fits the human features of the text and believers' experience of being addressed by it. Challenge: who decides which parts "speak"? Authority may become private.
Mission 1

Calvin's accommodation

  • John Calvin (1509–1564), Institutes of the Christian Religion (final Latin edition 1559).
  • God is infinite; humans are limited. So God accommodates (adapts) revelation to our capacity.
  • Calvin's image (Institutes I.13.1, paraphrase): as nurses speak simply to infants, God "lisps" to us.
  • His example (Commentary on Genesis, 1554, on Genesis 1:16): Moses calls the sun and moon "the two great lights" as they appear to ordinary people, though astronomers know Saturn is larger than the moon. Moses wrote in a popular style for everyone.
  • So anthropomorphic language (God's arm, God "repenting") and ancient science are God speaking at our level, not errors.
  • Calvin also held that the Spirit's inner testimony convinces believers that Scripture is God's word (Institutes I.7.4–5).

Mission 1Evaluate

Is the Bible the inspired word of God?

  • Claim: The Bible can be regarded as the inspired word of God.
  • For: Its own witness (2 Timothy 3:16; 2 Peter 1:21); its unity across centuries centred on Christ; the Church's long recognition; believers' experience of the Spirit speaking through it.
  • Against: Its human features (research, style, varied accounts); tensions between texts; ancient science; the canon was chosen by people over centuries.
  • Replies: The "inspired but human" view explains human features without denying inspiration.
  • Calvin's accommodation does the same: God speaks at our level.
  • The subjective view moves authority to the reader's encounter with God.
  • Judgement criteria: What does "word of God" mean: dictated words, or witness to Christ? Does inspiration require inerrancy? Can a human selection process be guided by God?
  • Spec issue: whether the canonical orders (the order in which the books are arranged, the earlier screen) are inspired, or only the texts they contain.

Mission 2

The Bible in Christian life

  • Worship: readings at most services; many churches follow a lectionary, a set cycle of readings (often over three years). Preaching explains them.
  • Sacraments and prayer: the words of the Eucharist and the Lord's Prayer come from Scripture; the Psalms are sung and prayed daily in many traditions.
  • Personal devotion: daily reading; Bible-study groups; lectio divina ("divine reading": read, meditate, pray, contemplate).
  • Decisions and ethics: Christians look to the Bible for moral advice and guidance.
  • Meaning and comfort: in illness, grief or fear, many turn to passages they know by heart.

Mission 2

Models of interpretation

  • Literal / fundamentalist: the text means what it plainly says; every statement is true as written.
  • Conservative (evangelical): inerrant or infallible, but read by genre and context (poetry as poetry, history as history).
  • Catholic: the literal sense (what the human author meant) and spiritual senses (allegorical, moral, anagogical), read in Tradition and guided by the Magisterium.
  • Liberal / historical-critical: a human document; study sources, authors, editing and context; apply its principles, not every rule.
  • Existential: what matters is how the text addresses me now (e.g. Bultmann, 1884–1976, "demythologising").
  • Across these models, most Christians look for principles in Scripture (love of God and neighbour, justice, forgiveness) and apply them with tradition, reason, prayer and the Church's guidance.

How far are the Psalms a guide to living?

  • Claim: The Psalms studied (Psalm 119) are still a good guide to living for Christians.
  • For: They form habits (memorising, meditating, daily prayer); centuries of use in worship; they cover all of life, including fear and failure.
  • Against: Psalm 119 praises the law of Moses, much of which Christians do not keep; poetry gives attitudes, not specific answers to modern questions.
  • Replies: Christians read "your word" in the light of Christ; the Psalms guide the heart rather than giving rules.
  • Judgement criteria: What counts as a "guide": rules, principles or formation? Compare the relative value of Genesis 1 and Ecclesiastes 9 as teaching on meaning and purpose.

Mission 3

Kerygma and didache

  • Kerygma (Greek kērygma): the proclamation a herald (kēryx) cries aloud: the public announcement of what God has done in Jesus.
  • Didache (didachē): teaching for those already believers: ethics, instruction, worship (Acts 2:42).
  • The kerygma is aimed at outsiders and calls for a response: repent, believe, be baptised.
  • Your spec says kerygmata: the plural, the proclamations.
Mission 3

Dodd's six points

  • C. H. Dodd (1884–1973), The Apostolic Preaching and its Developments (1936), reconstructed the "Jerusalem kerygma" from Peter's speeches in Acts 2–4 and 10 (paraphrase):
  • 1. Fulfilment: the age foretold by the prophets has dawned.
  • 2. Jesus: it has come through the ministry, death and resurrection of Jesus, descended from David.
  • 3. Exaltation: by the resurrection, Jesus is exalted at God's right hand as messianic head of the new Israel.
  • 4. Spirit: the Holy Spirit in the Church is the sign of Christ's present power and glory.
  • 5. Return: the messianic age will soon be completed when Christ returns.
  • 6. Appeal: repent; receive forgiveness and the Holy Spirit; the promise of salvation.
Mission 3Read the source

> "But the things which God announced by the mouth of all his prophets, that Christ should suffer, he thus fulfilled." (Acts 3:18, WEBBE)

> "Repent therefore, and turn again, that your sins may be blotted out, so that there may come times of refreshing from the presence of the Lord" (Acts 3:19, WEBBE)

  • 3:13–15: God glorified his Servant; you killed the Prince of life; God raised him.

Mission 3

Dodd's wider claims

  • One message: the same pattern underlies Paul's earliest tradition: "Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures… he was buried… he was raised on the third day according to the Scriptures" (1 Corinthians 15:3–4).
  • The Gospels grew from it: Mark's outline (John the Baptist, Galilee ministry, journey, Jerusalem, death, resurrection) follows the kerygma (Dodd, 1932).
  • Early material in Acts: some phrases sound primitive, not like Luke's later theology:
  • Jesus as God's Servant (3:13, 26); "God has made him both Lord and Christ" (2:36), as if at the resurrection; Semitic turns of phrase.
  • So, for Dodd, the speeches preserve the preaching of the Jerusalem church, not just Luke's ideas.
Mission 3

Do the speeches record what was said?

  • Ancient practice: the Greek historian Thucydides (c. 460–c. 400 BC), History of the Peloponnesian War 1.22, says he could not recall speeches word for word, so he had speakers say what the occasion required, keeping as close as possible to the general sense (paraphrase).
  • Martin Dibelius (1883–1947; essays on Acts, 1949): the speeches are Luke's compositions, written as a historian of his day would; Peter and Paul preach in the same pattern and style.
  • F. F. Bruce (1910–1990), The Speeches in the Acts of the Apostles (1942): Luke gives faithful summaries that keep early tradition, as their primitive features show.
  • Length: Peter's Pentecost speech takes only a few minutes to read aloud: clearly a summary.
  • Accuracy test: in Acts 5:36–37 the Pharisee Gamaliel names a rebel Theudas before Judas the Galilean (AD 6), but the historian Josephus dates a Theudas to c. AD 44–46, after this scene: a possible slip by Luke, or another Theudas.
Mission 3

Bultmann's challenge

  • Rudolf Bultmann (1884–1976), Theology of the New Testament (1948–53).
  • The kerygma is not mainly a summary of past facts: it is God's word addressing the hearer now and calling for a decision of faith (paraphrase).
  • Faith needs only that Jesus came and was crucified (German Dass), not a detailed story of what he did: against Dodd's link between the kerygma and a historical outline.
  • The kerygma comes wrapped in myth: exaltation to a heaven above, a quick return on the clouds. It must be demythologised to speak to modern people.
  • Dodd's point 5 (an imminent return) did not happen as expected: for Bultmann, a sign that the framework is mythical, while the call to decision remains.
  • Bultmann also doubted how much the Gospels and Acts tell us about the historical Jesus and the earliest preaching.

Mission 3

Adapting the message

  • To Jews in Jerusalem (Acts 2; 3): Scripture fulfilled; David, Moses, Abraham; Jesus as Messiah; "you… we".
  • To country people at Lystra (14:15–17): turn "to the living God, who made the sky, the earth, the sea"; he gave "rains from the sky and fruitful seasons". No Scripture quoted.
  • To philosophers at Athens (17:22–31): an altar "TO AN UNKNOWN GOD"; Greek poets: "For in him we live, move, and have our being" (17:28); then judgement and resurrection.
  • What stays constant: turn to the living God who acts; in the fuller speeches (Jerusalem, Athens) this leads to Jesus raised and a call to repent. At Lystra the speech, as Luke gives it, ends before Jesus is named (the crowd has to be stopped from offering sacrifice, 14:18).
  • Paul: "I have become all things to all men, that I may by all means save some." (1 Corinthians 9:22, WEBBE)
  • Your spec: "the adapting of the Christian message to suit the audience".
Mission 3Evaluate

Do the speeches have historical value?

  • Claim: The speeches in Acts are reliable evidence of the earliest Christian preaching.
  • For (Dodd, Bruce): primitive titles and Christology (Servant; "made him Lord and Christ"); agreement with Paul's early tradition (1 Corinthians 15:3–5).
  • For: Luke claims careful research (Luke 1:1–4); some first-hand sources likely.
  • Against (Dibelius): ancient convention let historians compose speeches; Peter and Paul sound alike; Luke's own vocabulary and themes run through them.
  • Against: written perhaps 50 years later; possible slips (Theudas).
  • Middle view: Luke's words, shaped for his book, carrying early content.
  • Judgement criteria: What did "history" mean to an ancient writer? Can early material survive in a later style? How much would summaries change?
Mission 3Evaluate

Is the kerygma of value for Christians today?

  • Claim: The kerygmata in Acts are of little value for Christians today.
  • For: the expected quick return did not come; the language of exaltation "above" reflects an ancient picture of the universe (Bultmann).
  • For: they were tailored to 1st-century Jews; modern hearers do not share those scriptures or hopes.
  • Against: they give the core message many churches still preach (creeds, evangelism): Jesus died, rose, is Lord; repent and receive forgiveness.
  • Against: Bultmann himself kept the kerygma central, only reinterpreting it; the call to decision still speaks.
  • Against: Acts itself models adapting the message, which churches still follow.
  • Judgement criteria: Is value in the wording, the structure or the core claims? Who decides what is myth?

Mission 4

The question of the historical Jesus

  • The Christ of faith: Jesus as Christians worship him (Son of God, risen Lord).
  • The historical Jesus: what a historian can show about the man from Nazareth, using ordinary historical methods.
  • The Gospels were written by believers, c. 30–60 years after his death. Historians must ask which material goes back to Jesus.
  • Method decides a great deal: which sources count, what is assumed possible, and what question is asked.
  • Eduqas compares two scholars whose methods and conclusions differ sharply.
Mission 4

Crossan: who he is and how he works

  • John Dominic Crossan (b. 1934), Irish-American New Testament scholar, co-founder of the Jesus Seminar (1985).
  • Key work: The Historical Jesus: The Life of a Mediterranean Jewish Peasant (1991); shorter: Jesus: A Revolutionary Biography (1994).
  • His method works on three levels (his "triple triad"). First he combines social anthropology (how peasant societies under empire work), Graeco-Roman and Jewish history, and literary study of the texts.
  • Then he inventories the sources, sorts the sayings of Jesus into layers by date, and gives most weight to the earliest material and to sayings independently attested more than once.
Mission 4

Crossan: using apocryphal gospels

  • Apocryphal gospels: early Christian gospels outside the New Testament canon.
  • Crossan treats some as early, independent sources, alongside Mark and the sayings source Q:
  • The Gospel of Thomas: 114 sayings of Jesus with no birth, death or resurrection story (Coptic text found at Nag Hammadi, 1945).
  • A "Cross Gospel", which he reconstructs from the Gospel of Peter (fragment found at Akhmim, Egypt, 1886–87).
  • Result: the earliest layer, for Crossan, is mostly sayings about the Kingdom, not claims about Jesus' divinity.
  • Most scholars date Thomas, in the form we have it, to the 2nd century, and few accept the Cross Gospel as earlier than the canonical Gospels.
Mission 4

Crossan's Jesus: the social revolutionary

  • Jesus as a product of his time: a Jewish peasant in Roman-occupied Galilee, where land and wealth were being drawn to the cities and elites.
  • What his words meant then: to peasants under Caesar, "the Kingdom of God" meant a different way of living now, not the end of the world.
  • Open commensality: eating with anyone, outcasts included (Mark 2:16), against the social map of honour and shame.
  • Free healing: offered outside the Temple and its fees.
  • Together: a "brokerless kingdom" (Crossan's phrase): direct access to God and to one another, with no patrons or priests as go-betweens.
  • His Jesus is like a Jewish Cynic sage: a radical, non-violent social revolutionary. Easter, for Crossan, is the continuing experience of Jesus' presence, not a bodily return from death.

Mission 4

Wright: who he is and critical realism

  • N. T. (Tom) Wright (b. 1948), Anglican New Testament scholar, Bishop of Durham 2003–2010.
  • Key works: The New Testament and the People of God (1992), Jesus and the Victory of God (1996), The Resurrection of the Son of God (2003).
  • Critical realism (paraphrase of Wright, 1992): there is a real past, other than the historian, that can be known (realism); but we only reach it through a continuing, self-correcting conversation between the historian and the evidence (critical).
  • So: reject naive "the text simply tells us what happened"; reject too the view that we only ever meet our own ideas.
  • Texts are "the articulation of worldviews": the Gospels express the stories, symbols, questions and practices of first-century Jews and Christians.
Mission 4Read the source

One saying, two readings

> "The time is fulfilled, and God's Kingdom is at hand! Repent, and believe in the Good News." (Mark 1:15, WEBBE)

  • Crossan's reading: the Kingdom is God's rule practised now, by peasants sharing meals and healing, in place of Caesar's rule.
  • Wright's reading: "the time is fulfilled" fits a Jewish worldview story: Israel still in a kind of exile, waiting for God to return to Zion and defeat evil. Jesus announces that the story's climax is now.

Q1 Which phrase in the verse does Wright's reading depend on most?

Show answer

"The time is fulfilled": it places Jesus within Israel's long story.

Q2 Why might the two readings differ, though both use the same verse?

Show answer

They frame it differently: Crossan through peasant society under empire; Wright through the Jewish story of exile and return.

Mission 4

Wright's Jesus: the true Messiah

  • Jesus was a prophet announcing the Kingdom, who came to see himself as Israel's Messiah.
  • His actions are symbols: choosing twelve (Israel's twelve tribes), the Temple protest (Mark 11:17), the Last Supper.
  • He saw his death as the way God's Kingdom would come; he acted out the return of Israel's God to Zion.
  • Best explanation: Wright asks what best explains the Gospel traditions and the rise of the Church. The early belief that Jesus was raised (1 Corinthians 15:3–5), with an empty tomb and appearances, is best explained, he argues, by a bodily resurrection (2003).
  • He uses mainly the canonical Gospels, read within their Jewish setting, and dates Thomas later.

Mission 4Evaluate

Which method brings us closer to Jesus?

  • Validity of apocryphal gospels: for — they may preserve early, independent sayings; using them avoids relying only on the Church's chosen texts.
  • Validity of apocryphal gospels: against — most date Thomas to the 2nd century and see Gnostic influence; the Cross Gospel is a reconstruction few accept. Building on them may tilt the result.
  • Validity of critical realism: for — it avoids both naive acceptance and total scepticism; it tests hypotheses against all the evidence.
  • Validity of critical realism: against — critics say it can become apologetics: the "best explanation" may be decided by what a historian already believes (e.g. whether resurrection is possible).
  • Both: critics say each scholar finds a Jesus who fits his own outlook (a charge first made famous by Albert Schweitzer, 1906, about earlier lives of Jesus).
  • Judgement criteria: Are the sources early and independent? Are assumptions stated? Does the picture explain Jesus' Jewish setting and the rise of the Church?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The canon is the agreed list of books: Catholic Bibles have 73, Protestant Bibles 66.
  • It was recognised over centuries: the Old Testament from Jewish and Greek traditions, the New Testament by tests of apostolic origin, orthodoxy and use (Athanasius, 367).
  • Views of the Bible range from the literal, inerrant word of God, through "inspired by God but written by humans", to a human record of experiences of God.
  • Objective inspiration places inspiration in the text; subjective inspiration in the writers' and readers' experience.
  • Calvin's accommodation: God speaks at our level, so human features of the text are not errors.

Mission 2

  • Christians use the Bible in worship, devotion, ethics and times of need.
  • Moral advice (Eccl 12:13–14; Luke 6:36–37), guide (Ps 119), meaning (Gen 1:26–28; Eccl 9:5–9), comfort (Ps 46:1–3; Matt 6:25).
  • Churches turn Scripture into ethical teaching through principles, tradition and reason; models of interpretation (literal, conservative, Catholic, liberal, existential) lead to different guidance.

Mission 3

  • Kerygma is the public proclamation; Dodd's six points: fulfilment, Jesus' death and resurrection, exaltation, Spirit, return, appeal (Acts 2:14–39; 3:12–26).
  • Historical value: Dibelius sees Luke's compositions on ancient models; Dodd and Bruce see summaries with early content.
  • Bultmann: the kerygma is a present call to decision needing only "that" Jesus came, and its mythical framework must be demythologised; Acts shows the message adapted to each audience.

Mission 4

  • Crossan (b. 1934): triple triad, early layers including apocryphal gospels (Thomas, the Cross Gospel), Jesus a product of his time: a peasant social revolutionary with a brokerless kingdom.
  • Wright (b. 1948): critical realism, texts as the articulation of worldviews, the best explanation of the traditions: Jesus the true Messiah, bodily raised.
  • Evaluate the methods: early dating of apocryphal gospels; whether critical realism is neutral or apologetic.
