Mission 1

Mission 1: Astronauts float on the space station. How much weaker is Earth's gravity up there?

Make a guess: Astronauts float on the space station. How much weaker is Earth's gravity up there?

Mission 1

Newton's law of gravitation

  • Every pair of masses attracts. Gravity never repels.
  • F = −GMm/r² (minus sign: the force is attractive, opposite to r measured outwards)
  • r: distance between the centres. G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻².
  • Acts along the line joining the centres. Each mass pulls the other equally hard (third law).
  • Inverse-square law: double r → force ÷ 4.
Mission 1

Spheres act as point masses

  • Outside a uniform sphere (or one made of uniform shells), its field is exactly that of a point mass at its centre.
  • So for planets and stars, r is measured from the centre, never from the surface.
Quick check
?Two objects exert gravitational forces of magnitude F on each other. The mass of each object is doubled, and the distance between their centres is tripled. What is the new magnitude of the gravitational force that each object exerts on the other?
Mission 1

Gravitational field strength

  • Gravitational field: a region where a mass experiences a force.
  • Field strength g = F/m: force per unit mass on a small test mass. Unit N kg⁻¹. Towards the mass.
  • Divide by m: g = −GM/r² (the minus sign: g points towards the mass; its size is GM/r²).
  • Free fall: g in N kg⁻¹ equals the acceleration in m s⁻².
  • Weight W = mg.
Quick check
?A student sketches the gravitational field lines around an isolated planet that is a uniform sphere. Which description of a correct sketch is right?
Mission 1

Field lines and the uniform field near the surface

  • Field lines: the direction of the force on a small mass. Around a planet: radial, pointing inwards.
  • Closer lines = stronger field. Radial lines spread out: g falls with distance.
  • Near the surface, over a few kilometres, g barely changes: treat the field as uniform.
  • Uniform field: parallel, equally spaced lines.
Mission 1

Worked example: the space station

  • Earth's radius R = 6.37 × 10⁶ m. Station about 4.0 × 10⁵ m up: r = 6.77 × 10⁶ m from the centre.
  • g ∝ 1/r²: gstation/gsurface = (6.37/6.77)² = 0.89.
  • gstation = 0.89 × 9.81 ≈ 8.7 N kg⁻¹.
  • Gravity there is only about 11% weaker. Astronauts float because they are in free fall with the station.
  • Turn it round to find a mass: M = gR²/G = 9.81 × (6.37 × 10⁶)² ÷ 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ = 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg (Earth).
Quick check
?The Earth exerts a gravitational force of 2.0 × 10²⁰ N on the Moon. The mass of the Moon is about 1.2% of the mass of the Earth. Which statement about the gravitational force that the Moon exerts on the Earth is correct?
Mission check

?The diagram shows a planet of radius R and two points, P and Q, on a line through the planet's centre. The arrow at P represents the planet's gravitational field at P, of magnitude 4.5 N kg⁻¹. What is the magnitude of the planet's gravitational field at Q?

g ∝ 1/r², with r from the centre. At 400 km up, r is only 6% larger than Earth's radius, so g is about 89% of its surface value. Astronauts float because they are falling freely, not because gravity has gone.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Double a satellite's orbital radius. How many times longer does one orbit then take?

Make a guess: Double a satellite's orbital radius. How many times longer does one orbit then take?

Mission 2

Kepler's three laws

  • First law: each planet moves in an ellipse, with the Sun at one focus.
  • Second law: the line from the Sun to the planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times.
  • Third law: T² ∝ r³ for all planets of the same star (r = mean distance; for a circle, the radius).
Quick check
?A comet moves around the Sun in a highly elliptical orbit. Which statement about the comet agrees with Kepler's laws?
Mission 2

Gravity is the centripetal force

  • In a circular orbit, gravity is the only force on the satellite. It points to the centre of the planet.
  • So gravity provides the centripetal force: GMm/r² = mv²/r.
  • There is no outward "centrifugal" force. The satellite is in free fall all the way around.
  • m cancels: the orbit does not depend on the satellite's mass.
  • Orbital speed: v = √(GM/r). Smaller orbit → faster.
Mission 2

Deriving T² ∝ r³

  • One orbit: distance 2πr in time T, so v = 2πr/T.
  • Substitute into GMm/r² = mv²/r:
  • GM/r² = 4π²r/T²
  • T² = (4π²/GM)r³
  • T² ∝ r³. The constant 4π²/GM depends only on the central mass.
  • Same law for moons of Jupiter or planets of another star; only the constant changes.
  • Double r: T² rises 2³ = 8 times, so T rises √8 ≈ 2.8 times.
Quick check
?Tethys and Rhea are moons of Saturn, and their orbits may be taken as circular. Tethys has an orbital radius of 2.95 × 10⁸ m and a period of 1.89 days. Rhea has an orbital radius of 5.27 × 10⁸ m. What is the period of Rhea?
Mission 2

Worked example: weighing the Earth

  • The Moon: r = 3.84 × 10⁸ m, T = 27.3 days.
  • T = 27.3 × 86 400 = 2.36 × 10⁶ s.
  • Rearrange: M = 4π²r³/(GT²).
  • M = 4π² × (3.84 × 10⁸)³ ÷ (6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × (2.36 × 10⁶)²) = 6.0 × 10²⁴ kg.
  • Any orbiting body works: measure r and T (or v), get the central mass.
  • A graph of T² against r³ is a straight line through the origin; gradient = 4π²/GM.
Quick check
?Astronomers measure the orbital period T and orbital radius R of several moons that move in circular orbits around a planet. The graph shows T² plotted against R³, with a best-fit line through the origin. What is the mass of the planet? Use G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻².
Quick check
?Which set of conditions must a satellite meet to be geostationary?
Mission 2

Geostationary and low orbits

  • A geostationary satellite stays above one point on the equator. It needs:
  • period = Earth's rotation period, about 24 h;
  • orbit in the equatorial plane;
  • moving west to east, the same way as Earth spins.
  • From T² = (4π²/GM)r³: r ≈ 4.2 × 10⁷ m, height ≈ 3.6 × 10⁷ m (36 000 km).
  • Uses: communications and TV, weather images. Ground dishes stay fixed.
Mission check

?Two planets, P and Q, move in circular orbits around the same distant star. The orbital period of Q is 8.0 times the orbital period of P. What is the ratio (orbital radius of Q)/(orbital radius of P)?

T² ∝ r³ for anything orbiting the same central mass. Doubling the radius makes the period √8 ≈ 2.8 times longer.

Mission 3

Mission 3: How much energy does it take to lift a probe far from a planet, or to free it completely?

Make a guess: How much energy does it take to lift a probe far from a planet, or to free it completely?

Mission 3

Zero at infinity

  • Choose zero gravitational potential energy when the masses are infinitely far apart.
  • Gravity attracts. As two masses come together from infinity, gravity does positive work: energy is released.
  • So at any finite separation the potential energy is below zero: negative.
  • Closer → more negative. Moving apart → rises towards zero, and that needs work done against gravity.
Mission 3

Potential energy of two masses

  • E = −GMm/r
  • r: centre to centre. The energy belongs to the pair, not to one of the masses.
Mission 3

Gravitational potential

  • Gravitational potential at a point: the work done per unit mass to bring a small mass from infinity to that point. Unit J kg⁻¹.
  • Vg = −GM/r
  • Negative everywhere, rising to zero at infinity.
  • Potential energy of a mass m there: mVg.
  • Moving a mass m: change in potential energy = mΔVg.
Quick check
?Mars has mass 6.42 × 10²³ kg and radius 3.39 × 10⁶ m. Take G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻². What is the gravitational potential due to Mars at a point 2.00 × 10⁶ m above its surface?
Mission 3

Worked example: when is constant g good enough?

  • Lift a 1000 kg probe from Earth's surface (R = 6.37 × 10⁶ m) to a height R, so r goes from R to 2R. M = 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg.
  • Gain = GMm/R − GMm/2R = GMm/2R = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 5.97 × 10²⁴ × 1000 ÷ (2 × 6.37 × 10⁶) = 3.1 × 10¹⁰ J.
  • Using mgΔh = 1000 × 9.81 × 6.37 × 10⁶ = 6.2 × 10¹⁰ J: twice too big.
  • mgΔh assumes g stays constant. Here g falls to a quarter. Use mgΔh only when Δh ≪ R.
Quick check
?A satellite of mass 500 kg is moved from a circular orbit of radius 6.80 × 10⁶ m to a circular orbit of radius 2.04 × 10⁷ m around the Earth, of mass 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg. Take G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻². The gravitational field strength at the Earth's surface is 9.81 N kg⁻¹. What is the change in the gravitational potential energy of the Earth–satellite system?
Mission 3

Escape speed

  • To escape, an object must reach infinity, where potential energy is zero, with kinetic energy ≥ 0.
  • So it needs total energy ≥ 0. The minimum launch speed gives total energy exactly 0.
  • ½mv² − GMm/r = 0 → vesc = √(2GM/r).
  • The object's own mass cancels. It holds at any distance r from the centre, not only at the surface.
  • Earth's surface: √(2 × 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 5.97 × 10²⁴ ÷ 6.37 × 10⁶) = 11.2 km s⁻¹ (ignoring air resistance and Earth's rotation).
  • Gas molecules: the fastest in a hot atmosphere can exceed vesc and leak away, light gases first. The Moon (vesc ≈ 2.4 km s⁻¹) has kept almost no atmosphere.
Quick check
?A probe is launched straight up from the surface of a planet with exactly the escape speed. The planet has no atmosphere, its motion is negligible, and the only force exerted on the probe after launch is the planet's gravitational force. Which statement describes the probe's motion after launch?
Mission check

?The Moon has mass 7.35 × 10²² kg and radius 1.74 × 10⁶ m. Take G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻². What is the escape speed from a point 2.00 × 10⁶ m above the Moon's surface?

Escape means total energy ≥ 0: ½mv² = GMm/r, so vesc = √(2GM/r). From Earth's surface that is about 11 km s⁻¹, whatever the mass launched.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Gravitational potential is negative everywhere around a planet. How does it change as you move away?

Make a guess: Gravitational potential is negative everywhere around a planet. How does it change as you move away?

Mission 4

V and g against r

  • Vg = −GM/r
  • Outside a planet of radius R: V starts at its (negative) surface value and rises towards zero as r increases. It never becomes positive.
  • V ∝ −1/r: at 2R, V is half its surface value.
  • g ∝ 1/r² falls faster: at 2R it is a quarter of its surface value.
  • The V–r graph is steepest near the planet, where the field is strongest.
Mission 4

Field and work from the graphs

  • Force–distance graph for a mass m near a planet: F = GMm/r². Work done moving m from r1 to r2 = area under the graph between them = gain in potential energy.
Quick check
?A 200 kg probe moves away from the Earth, from r = 7.0 × 10⁶ m to r = 9.0 × 10⁶ m from its centre. The gravitational force on it falls from 1.6 × 10³ N to 9.8 × 10² N. Using the force–distance graph, which is the best estimate of the gain in the probe's gravitational potential energy?
Mission check

?The gravitational potential at the surface of a planet of radius R is −6.0 MJ kg⁻¹. A probe is at a height of 3R above the surface. What is the gravitational potential at the probe?

Moving away, potential rises towards zero, as −1/r. It is highest (zero) at infinity and never becomes positive.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • F = −GMm/r²: always attractive (the minus sign), inverse square, r between centres.
  • g = F/m = −GM/r²; outside a sphere, treat it as a point mass at its centre.

Mission 2

  • Gravity alone provides the centripetal force: GMm/r² = mv²/r, so T² = (4π²/GM)r³; the satellite's mass cancels.
  • v = √(GM/r): lower orbits are faster.
  • Kepler: ellipses, equal areas in equal times, T² ∝ r³.
  • Geostationary: 24 h period, equatorial plane, west to east, r ≈ 4.2 × 10⁷ m.

Mission 3

  • Gravitational potential energy is zero at infinity and negative at any finite separation.
  • E = −GMm/r; use mgΔh only when Δh ≪ R.
  • Potential Vg = −GM/r (J kg⁻¹); work to move a mass m = mΔVg.
  • Escape: total energy = 0, vesc = √(2GM/r), independent of the launched mass.

Mission 4

  • V ∝ −1/r rises towards zero with r (half at 2R); g ∝ 1/r² falls faster (a quarter at 2R).
