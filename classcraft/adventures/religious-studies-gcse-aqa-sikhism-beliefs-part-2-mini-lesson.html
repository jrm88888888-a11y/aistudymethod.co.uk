Mission 1

One light in everyone

  • Sikhs believe in the oneness of humanity: God's light (jot) is in every person.
  • So all people are equal: whatever their caste, gender, religion, race or wealth.
  • After his experience at the river, Guru Nanak is remembered as saying: "There is no Hindu, there is no Muslim": God sees people, not labels.
  • In 15th-century Punjab, caste decided who you could eat with, marry or pray beside. The Gurus rejected it.
  • Equality includes complete equality of women with men.
Mission 1Read the source

"Do not ask about caste"

Guru Nanak, GGS 349 (Raag Asa) (paraphrase):

  • Recognise God's light within everyone. Do not ask about a person's caste or social class: in the world beyond, there is no caste.

Guru Nanak, GGS 473 (Asa di Var) (paraphrase):

  • Why call woman inferior, when kings are born from her? Without woman there would be no one at all.

Q1 What reason does GGS 349 give for ignoring caste?

Show answer

God's light is in everyone; caste counts for nothing in the next world.

Q2 Which argument does GGS 473 use for respecting women?

Show answer

Everyone, even kings, is born from a woman.

Mission 1

Guru Nanak: two meals

  • Sikh tradition tells how Guru Nanak stayed with Bhai Lalo, a poor carpenter of low caste.
  • A rich official, Malik Bhago, invited the Guru to a feast. The Guru refused.
  • Challenged, he squeezed Lalo's plain bread: milk came out. From Bhago's rich food: blood.
  • Meaning: Lalo's bread was earned honestly; Bhago's wealth came from exploiting the poor.
  • Guru Nanak also began the langar (free kitchen) at Kartarpur: all sit together in rows (pangat) and eat the same food.

Mission 1

Guru Amar Das: langar first

  • Guru Amar Das (third Guru) made the langar central: "first the pangat, then the sangat" (first eat together, then meet the Guru).
  • Sikh tradition says even the Mughal emperor Akbar sat on the floor and ate in the langar before meeting him.
  • He spoke out against sati (widows burning on a husband's funeral pyre) (GGS 787) and is remembered for opposing purdah (veiling and secluding women).
  • He organised Sikh communities into regions, and appointed women as well as men as preachers and teachers to lead them.
Mission 1

Guru Gobind Singh and the Khalsa

  • At Vaisakhi 1699, Guru Gobind Singh (tenth Guru) founded the Khalsa.
  • The first five to offer their lives, the Panj Pyare (Five Beloved Ones), came from different castes and regions.
  • All drank amrit from one bowl, which broke caste rules about sharing food and drink.
  • Tradition says Mata Sahib Kaur added sugar sweets to the amrit: a woman's part in the first initiation.
  • All men take the name Singh (lion), all women Kaur (princess): one family, no caste surnames.
  • Women and men take amrit and join the Khalsa on equal terms.
Mission 1

Mai Bhago

  • During the long siege of Anandpur (1704), forty Sikhs left Guru Gobind Singh, signing a letter disowning him.
  • Mai Bhago, a Sikh woman from Jhabal near Amritsar, shamed them for deserting the Guru.
  • In 1705 she led them back to fight beside the Guru at the Battle of Muktsar (Khidrana).
  • The forty died in battle. The Guru tore up their letter and blessed them as the Chali Mukte ("forty liberated ones").
  • Mai Bhago was wounded but survived; Sikh tradition says she then served as one of the Guru's bodyguards.
  • She shows that a woman can lead men and fight with courage equal to any man.

Mission 1

Equality in Sikh life today

  • Langar: everyone, Sikh or not, sits and eats the same free food together.
  • Worship: any Sikh, man or woman, may read from the Guru Granth Sahib, lead prayers, sing kirtan or serve as granthi.
  • The Sikh Rahit Maryada (code of conduct) allows women to take part in all religious ceremonies.
  • Names: Singh and Kaur still replace caste names for many Sikhs.
  • Service: Sikh charities (e.g. Khalsa Aid, founded 1999) help people of every faith and none.
  • Why complete equality matters: it follows from one light in all; denying it would be haumai.
Mission 1Diversity

Ideal and practice

  • Most Sikhs: women and men are equal and may take any role.
  • Some Sikhs: equal in worth, but often with different roles in family life.
  • Caste has not fully disappeared: some families still consider it in marriage, and some UK gurdwaras grew from caste-based communities (e.g. Ramgarhia and Bhatra).
  • Women are often under-represented on gurdwara committees and are rarely chosen as Panj Pyare in ceremonies.
  • Some Sikhs campaign for change, e.g. for women to perform kirtan inside the Harmandir Sahib (Golden Temple); others defend existing customs there.
  • All agree the Gurus' teaching is equality; the debate is about practice.
Mission 1Evaluate

"Sikhs today fully live out the Gurus' teaching on equality."

  • Reasons for: the langar is open to all; any Sikh may lead worship; Singh and Kaur; the Rahit Maryada allows women in every ceremony; Sikh charities serve everyone.
  • Reasons against: caste still affects some marriages and gurdwaras; women are under-represented in leadership and as Panj Pyare; customs at some historic gurdwaras are debated.
  • Another view: no community lives its ideals perfectly; what matters is that GGS 349 gives Sikhs the standard to challenge themselves.
  • Judge it by: Should we judge by teaching or by practice? Is the gap closing? Whose experience counts: leaders' or ordinary Sikhs'?
Mission check

?A gurdwara committee is choosing who will read from the Guru Granth Sahib at a festival. One member says it should be a man from a "high-caste" family. Which reply best reflects Sikh teaching?

One light in all: the Gurus' stories, from Lalo's bread to Mai Bhago's courage, turn that belief into practice.

Mission 2

A boy who asked questions

  • Guru Nanak was born in 1469 at Talwandi, Punjab (now Nankana Sahib, Pakistan), into a Hindu family.
  • The janamsakhis (life stories written down after his death) show him questioning empty ritual from childhood.
  • The true bargain (Sacha Sauda): his father gave him money to make a good deal. He spent it feeding hungry holy men, and called that the only true bargain.
  • Sikhs see in this story the seed of langar (free shared meal) and sewa (selfless service).
Mission 2

The river experience

  • About 1499, at Sultanpur Lodhi, Nanak went to bathe in the River Bein and disappeared for three days.
  • Sikhs believe he was taken into God's presence, given the Name of God, and told to share it.
  • When he came back, his first words are remembered as: "There is no Hindu, there is no Muslim."
  • Meaning: God sees no religious labels; all people stand equal before the One God.
  • Sikh tradition says he then spoke the Mool Mantar, the opening words of the Sikh scripture.
Mission 2

Travels, Kartarpur and death

  • Nanak made long journeys (udasis) across India and beyond, with Bhai Mardana, a Muslim musician who played the rabab while Nanak sang.
  • He visited Hindu and Muslim holy places, challenging ritual done without devotion.
  • Bhai Lalo and Malik Bhago: Nanak chose to eat the plain bread of Lalo, a poor carpenter, not the feast of rich Bhago. Squeezed, Lalo's bread gave milk; Bhago's gave blood, earned by exploiting the poor.
  • About 1522 he settled at Kartarpur (now in Pakistan): a community that farmed, worshipped together and ate together in langar.
  • He died in 1539, having chosen his disciple Lehna (renamed Angad) as Guru instead of his own sons.
  • Sikhs believe the same divine jot (light) in Nanak passed to Angad, and on through all ten Gurus: ten men, one Guru.

?After his experience at the River Bein, Guru Nanak said, "There is no Hindu, there is no Muslim." What do Sikhs take this to mean?
Mission 2

Guru Gobind Singh

  • Born 1666; became the 10th Guru aged nine, after his father, Guru Tegh Bahadur, was executed (1675).
  • Vaisakhi 1699, Anandpur: he founded the Khalsa, a community of initiated Sikhs ready to give everything for their faith.
  • 1708, Nanded: before his death he declared that the Guru Granth Sahib would be the Guru from then on.
Mission 2

Equality in the Gurus' lives

  • Guru Nanak: "no Hindu, no Muslim"; Lalo's honest bread over Bhago's feast; one langar where all sat together.
  • Guru Gobind Singh: the first five Khalsa members (the panj pyare) came from different castes and regions.
  • All drank amrit from one iron bowl: a deliberate break with caste rules about sharing food and drink.
  • Every Khalsa man took the name Singh (lion), every woman Kaur (princess), replacing caste surnames.
  • For equality in the Guru Granth Sahib and in Sikhism today (including the equality of women), see Mission 1.
Quick check
?At Vaisakhi 1699 the first five Khalsa members all drank amrit from one bowl and took the same new name. Why do Sikhs see this as a sign of equality?
Mission check

?A new Sikh charity uses a picture of one lamp lighting nine others in a row. Its leaflet says: "Different people, one message." Which Sikh belief does the picture fit best?

Ten Gurus, one light: from Guru Nanak's river experience to Guru Gobind Singh's Khalsa, Sikhs see one message passed on.

Mission 3

What is sewa?

  • Sewa = selfless service to others, with no wish for reward, praise or payment.
  • Sikhs believe serving people is serving God, because God's light is in everyone.
  • Sewa is a mark of a gurmukh and a cure for haumai: it puts others before self.
  • It is for everyone: Sikh or not, friend or stranger.
  • Sikh teaching gives priority to service: helping others comes before one's own comfort.
Mission 3

Tan, man, dhan

  • Sikhs serve with body, mind and wealth (tan, man, dhan).
  • Your spec calls these physical (tan), mental (man) and material (dhan) service.
  • Tan (body): cooking and serving langar, washing dishes, cleaning the gurdwara, looking after shoes.
  • Man (mind): teaching Punjabi or gurbani, singing kirtan, giving advice, using your skills for others.
  • Dhan (wealth): giving money, food or goods; daswandh.
  • Many Sikhs see tan sewa as the humblest, and so the most powerful against ego: e.g. cleaning others' shoes.
  • All three are needed; what matters most is the attitude.
Quick check
?Amrit, a Sikh teenager, spends every Sunday teaching younger children to read Gurmukhi at the gurdwara. Which kind of sewa is this mainly?
Mission 3

Where sewa comes from

  • Guru Nanak set up the first langar at Kartarpur, where everyone ate together and shared the work.
  • Mata Khivi, wife of Guru Angad, ran the langar so generously it is praised in the Guru Granth Sahib.
  • Guru Amar Das served Guru Angad for years before becoming Guru, tradition says fetching water for him daily.
  • Guru Arjan guided the building of the Harmandir Sahib by the voluntary labour of the sangat.
  • Bhai Ghanaiya (also spelled Kanhaiya; Guru Gobind Singh's time) gave water to wounded soldiers on both sides. Questioned, he said he saw only the Guru's light in each face. The Guru blessed him and gave him ointment for their wounds too.
  • So sewa built the community, its gurdwaras and its identity: it shaped how Sikhism developed.
Mission 3Read the source

Service in the world

Guru Nanak, GGS 26 (Sri Raag) (paraphrase):

  • Do sewa here, in the middle of this world; then you will be given a place of honour in God's court.

Q1 Where does GGS 26 say sewa should be done?

Show answer

In the world, in everyday life, not by withdrawing from it.

Q2 What does it promise?

Show answer

Honour in God's court: acceptance by God.

Mission 3

Daswandh: the tenth part

  • Daswandh = giving one tenth of your income for others: the gurdwara, the langar, the poor, education, disasters.
  • It is dhan sewa made regular, so giving is not left to mood.
  • Some Sikhs give exactly a tenth; others give what they can, or give a tenth of their time.
  • It shows the priority of service: others' needs are built into a Sikh's own budget.
Quick check
?Why do Sikhs believe sewa must be done without wanting a reward?
Mission 3Diversity

Sewa today

  • Gurdwara sewa: langar, cleaning, kirtan; often the first sewa a young Sikh learns.
  • Sewa in society: food for homeless people, food banks, blood drives, disaster relief (e.g. Khalsa Aid).
  • Most Sikhs do both. Some say gurdwara sewa trains the heart; others say the real test is serving strangers.
  • Family sewa: caring for elderly parents or a sick relative counts too.
  • Priority: some Sikhs say sewa matters even more than private prayer; others say nam simran and sewa cannot be separated, since prayer keeps sewa selfless.
Mission 3Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain two reasons why sewa is important to Sikhs. Refer to Sikh teaching.

  • Reason 1: Serving others is serving God, because God's light is in everyone. Development: so Sikhs serve all people equally, like Bhai Ghanaiya giving water to both sides.
  • Reason 2: Sewa removes haumai. Source: doing sewa in this world brings honour in God's court (GGS 26). Development: so humble tan sewa, like cleaning shoes, is valued as a way to become gurmukh.
  • 6-mark tip: explain two beliefs and develop each; refer to sacred writings or another source of religious belief and teaching, and show how it supports your point, not just its name.
Mission check

?After a flood, Sikh volunteers set up a langar and give hot food to everyone affected, including a group who had recently shouted insults at Sikhs. A reporter asks why. Which Sikh teaching best explains their action?

Sewa is service to God through service to everyone, done with body, mind and wealth, and without wanting anything back.

Mission 4

The sangat

  • Sangat = congregation: Sikhs gathered together to remember God, usually in the presence of the Guru Granth Sahib.
  • It usually meets at the gurdwara, but a sangat is people, not a building.
  • Anyone may join: men and women, all castes, Sikhs and non-Sikhs.
  • In the sangat Sikhs sing kirtan, hear the Guru's word, pray (Ardas), share langar and do sewa.
  • The worldwide community of Sikhs is the Panth.
Mission 4

Where the sangat came from

  • Guru Nanak gathered followers into sangats on his travels, in places later called dharamsals.
  • At Kartarpur he built a community that prayed, worked and ate together.
  • Later Gurus organised sangats across a growing area, linking them to the Guru.
  • Guru Arjan compiled the Adi Granth (1604) and installed it at the Harmandir Sahib for the sangat.
  • Before he died (1708), Guru Gobind Singh named the scripture as the Guru: Guru Granth. Many Sikhs add that authority also rests with the Guru Panth: the Khalsa community, deciding together.
Mission 4

Sat sangat: the true congregation

  • Sat Sangat = "true congregation": the company of people who remember God.
  • Guru Nanak asks how the sat sangat can be recognised; it is where the Name of the One is spoken (GGS 72, paraphrase).
  • So not every crowd is sat sangat: it is defined by what it is centred on.
  • Different views: many Sikhs say it is the gurdwara sangat before the Guru Granth Sahib; others say any gathering where God's Name is sung, even online; some also speak of the inner company of gurmukhs.
Mission 4Read the source

The Guru's school

Guru Ram Das, GGS 1316 (Kanara ki Var) (paraphrase):

  • The sat sangat is the True Guru's school: there the soul learns God's virtues.

Q1 What image does GGS 1316 use for the sat sangat?

Show answer

A school, where the soul learns God's virtues.

Q2 Why might a school be a good image?

Show answer

People learn together, with guidance, step by step; no one is born already knowing.

Mission 4

What the sangat does for a Sikh

  • Spiritual progress: being with others who remember God helps a person overcome haumai and stay gurmukh.
  • Training: the sangat teaches gurbani, Punjabi, kirtan and right conduct, to children and adults.
  • Support: in births, marriages, illness and death, the sangat stands with each family.
  • Sewa: the langar and every other sewa is organised and done by the sangat.
  • Decisions: the sangat decides together, e.g. electing a gurdwara committee.
Mission 4

Living without a sangat

Problems for Sikhs living far from a sangat:

  • No shared kirtan, langar or Ardas with others.
  • Harder to learn gurbani, Punjabi and the Sikh way, especially for children.
  • No gurdwara for ceremonies: naming, Anand Karaj (marriage), funerals.
  • Isolation: harder to resist haumai alone, and harder to keep Sikh identity among people who do not understand it.
  • Responses: online kirtan and the daily hukamnama from Amritsar; prayer at home with a gutka (prayer book); travelling to gurdwaras; small home sangats; university Sikh societies.

Mission 4Diversity

How important is the sangat?

  • Essential: many Sikhs, citing the Gurus, say ego cannot be overcome without the company of the holy.
  • Very helpful, but not the only way: others stress each person's own nam simran; sangat supports, but cannot replace, it.
  • Online sangat: some welcome live-streamed kirtan as true sangat; others say being physically present, sharing langar and sewa, cannot be replaced.
  • Amritdhari / sahajdhari: both belong to the sangat equally; some Khalsa Sikhs place special weight on the Panj Pyare and gatherings of the initiated.
  • Local or global: some stress the local congregation; others the whole Panth, guided by the Akal Takht.
Mission check

?At a university far from any gurdwara, a group of Sikh students meets every week. They read from a prayer book, sing kirtan, then cook a free meal for any student who comes. In Sikh terms, which description fits the group best?

A sangat is people centred on God: it trains, supports and serves, and Sikhs feel its absence when they are alone.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • God's light is in everyone, so caste, gender and wealth do not make anyone better (GGS 349; women honoured in GGS 473).
  • The Gurus lived it: Guru Nanak and Lalo; Guru Amar Das's langar and women preachers; Guru Gobind Singh's Khalsa; Mai Bhago at Muktsar.
  • Today: langar, women may lead all worship, Singh and Kaur; debate continues about caste and women's roles in practice.

Mission 2

  • Guru Nanak (1469–1539): the river experience, travels with Mardana, Kartarpur and langar; one God, and all people equal.
  • Guru Gobind Singh founded the Khalsa (1699) and made the Guru Granth Sahib the Guru (1708).

Mission 3

  • Sewa is selfless service to all, because God's light is in everyone; it shrinks haumai.
  • Three kinds: body, mind and wealth (tan, man, dhan, including daswandh); GGS 26: sewa in this world brings honour in God's court.
  • From Guru Nanak's langar to Bhai Ghanaiya, sewa shaped Sikhism; today it ranges from the gurdwara to disaster relief.

Mission 4

  • The sangat is the community gathered to remember God, usually before the Guru Granth Sahib; sat sangat is the company of those who remember God (GGS 72; the Guru's school, GGS 1316).
  • It helps Sikhs overcome haumai, learn, support one another and do sewa; living without one is hard.
