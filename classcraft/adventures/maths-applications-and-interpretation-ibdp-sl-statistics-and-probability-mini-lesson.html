Statistics and probability · collecting and presenting data

Population, sample and types of data

  • The population is the whole group you want to know about. A sample is the part you collect data from.
  • In a random sample every member of the population has an equal chance of being chosen.
  • A sample that over- or under-represents part of the population is biased.
  • Discrete data are counted (goals, siblings). Continuous data are measured and can take any value in a range (time, mass).
  • Check reliability: deal with missing values and recording errors first.
Statistics and probability · collecting and presenting data

Sampling techniques

TechniqueHow it worksRandom?
Simple randomEvery member has an equal chance (number everyone, use random numbers)Yes
SystematicPick a random start, then every kkth member from a listYes, if the list has no pattern
StratifiedSplit into groups (strata); sample each group in proportion to its size, at randomYes
QuotaFill a fixed number from each group, choosing whoever is availableNo
ConvenienceAsk whoever is easiest to reachNo
  • Non-random methods are quicker but more likely to be biased.

Statistics and probability · collecting and presenting data

Cumulative frequency, box plots and outliers

  • Classes are inequalities with no gaps (10≤t<2010 ≤ t < 20). A histogram uses equal class widths, frequency upwards.
  • Cumulative frequency: plot running totals at upper class ends. Read the median at 2n​n/2, Q1​Q₁ at 4n​n/4, Q3​Q₃ at 43n​3n/4, the ppth percentile at p%p% of nn. IQR=Q3​−Q1​IQR = Q₃ − Q₁.
  • An outlier is over 1.5×IQR1.5 × IQR from the nearest quartile: mark it with a cross. A symmetric box plot suggests possible normality.
  • Compare box plots by median (average) and IQR or range (spread).

Mission check

?A survey at a gym finds 90% of the people asked exercise daily. Why can't you conclude that 90% of all adults do?

The population is all adults, but the sample came only from people at a gym. That convenience sample is biased: it over-represents people who exercise, and asking more people at the same gym would not remove the bias. A random or stratified sample of all adults would.

Mean, median and mode

xˉ=n∑x​or, from a frequency table,xˉ=∑f∑fx​x̄ = (Σ x)/n or, from a frequency table, x̄ = (Σ fx)/(Σ f)

Statistics and probability · averages and spread

Grouped data

  • When data are grouped you don't know the exact values, so use each class's mid-interval value as xx.
  • Estimated mean =∑f∑fx​= (Σ fx)/(Σ f) with xx the mid-interval values. It is an estimate, not exact.
  • The modal class is the class with the highest frequency (equal class widths only).

Statistics and probability · averages and spread

Measuring spread

  • Range = largest − smallest. IQR =Q3​−Q1​= Q₃ − Q₁, the spread of the middle half; outliers do not affect it.
  • Standard deviation σσ measures the typical distance of the values from the mean. Variance =σ2= σ².
  • Find σσ with your GDC's one-variable statistics. Treat the data as the population and use σx​σ_x, not the sample value sx​s_x.
  • Find quartiles of discrete data on your GDC too. Methods differ, so a hand answer may not match exactly.

Statistics and probability · averages and spread

Changing every value

  • Add or subtract a constant cc: the mean, median and quartiles move by cc. The standard deviation, IQR and range do not change, because every gap stays the same.
  • Multiply by a positive constant kk: the mean and the standard deviation are both multiplied by kk. The variance is multiplied by k2k².
  • Do multiplication and addition in the order the question gives them.

Mission check

?Salaries at a firm have mean £2,400 and standard deviation £300. Everyone gets a £100 rise. What are the new mean and standard deviation?

Adding a constant shifts every value by the same amount. The mean rises by £100 to £2,500, but every distance from the mean stays the same, so the standard deviation stays £300. Only multiplying (for example a 5% rise for everyone) would change the standard deviation.

Statistics and probability · correlation and regression

The regression line of yy on xx

  • Your GDC gives the regression line of yy on xx, y=ax+by = ax + b. It always passes through (xˉ,yˉ​)(x̄, ȳ).
  • aa is the gradient: the change in yy for each increase of 1 in xx. bb is the value of yy when x=0x = 0, which may have no meaning in context.
  • Use it to predict yy from a given xx inside the data range. Predicting outside it is extrapolation and may be unreliable.
  • Don't rearrange a yy on xx line to predict xx from yy.

Statistics and probability · correlation and regression

Spearman's rank correlation

  • Spearman's rank correlation coefficient rs​r_s: rank each variable separately, then find Pearson's rr for the ranks. Use your GDC.
  • Tied values share the average of the ranks they occupy: two values in 2nd and 3rd place both get 2.5.
  • rs​=1r_s = 1 or −1−1 for any monotonic relationship (always increasing or always decreasing), even a curved one. Pearson's rr only measures linearity.
  • rs​r_s is less sensitive to outliers than rr.

Statistics and probability · probability

Sample spaces and events

  • A trial has outcomes; the set of all outcomes is the sample space UU; an event is a set of outcomes.
  • For equally likely outcomes:
P(A)=n(U)n(A)​P(A) = (n(A))/(n(U))
  • The complement A′A' means "not AA": P(A′)=1−P(A)P(A') = 1 − P(A).
  • Relative frequency (successes ÷ trials) estimates a probability from an experiment.
  • Expected number of occurrences in nn trials =n×P(A)= n × P(A).

Statistics and probability · probability

Combined and conditional probability

  • "AA or BB" includes both, so subtract the overlap once:
P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B)P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)
  • Mutually exclusive events cannot happen together: P(A∩B)=0P(A ∩ B) = 0.
  • Conditional probability ("AA given BB") restricts you to BB: P(A∣B)=P(B)P(A∩B)​P(A | B) = (P(A ∩ B))/(P(B)).
  • Independent events don't affect each other: P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B)P(A ∩ B) = P(A) P(B).

Statistics and probability · probability

Tree diagrams and "without replacement"

  • On a tree diagram, multiply along the branches for "this and then that", and add the end results for "either path".
  • Each set of branches from one point adds to 1.
  • Without replacement, the second-stage probabilities change: there is one fewer item, and one fewer of the kind you took.
  • For "at least one", it is often quicker to use 1−P(none)1 − P(none).

Statistics and probability · discrete random variables

Expected value and fair games

  • The expected value (mean) is the long-run average value of XX:
E(X)=∑xP(X=x)E(X) = Σ x P(X=x)
  • E(X)E(X) need not be a value XX can take. The expected number of occurrences in nn trials is npnp: 128 students each absent with probability 0.1 gives 12.8 absent on average.
  • In a game, let XX be the player's gain (winnings minus the cost to play). The game is fair when E(X)=0E(X) = 0.

  • Use X∼B(n,p)X ~ B(n, p) when there is a fixed number nn of trials, each with two outcomes (success or failure), the trials are independent, and the probability of success pp is constant. XX counts the successes.

Statistics and probability · the normal distribution

The normal curve

  • X∼N(μ,σ2)X ~ N(μ, σ²) has a bell-shaped curve, symmetric about the mean μμ, so mean, median and mode are equal. The second parameter is the variance σ2σ².
  • Probabilities are areas under the curve; the total area is 1. Many measurements (lengths, masses, times) are close to normal.
  • About 68% of values lie within μ±σμ ± σ, 95% within μ±2σμ ± 2σ and 99.7% within μ±3σμ ± 3σ.

Statistics and probability · the normal distribution

Probabilities and inverse normal on your GDC

  • Sketch first: draw the curve, mark μμ and shade the area you want.
  • Normal cdf needs a lower bound, an upper bound, μμ and σσ. With no lower limit enter a huge negative number such as −1099−10⁹⁹; with no upper limit, 109910⁹⁹.
  • Inverse normal takes an area and returns the value xx with that area to its left (unless you set the tail). For the top 5%, enter 0.95.
  • P(X=a)=0P(X = a) = 0 for a continuous variable, so P(X<a)=P(X≤a)P(X < a) = P(X ≤ a).

Statistics and probability · hypothesis tests

Hypotheses, significance and pp-values

  • The null hypothesis H0​H₀ is the "no effect" claim: no association, or equal means. The alternative hypothesis H1​H₁ is what you are looking for evidence of.
  • Fix the significance level (1%, 5% or 10%) before testing.
  • The pp-value is the probability of a result at least as extreme as yours, assuming H0​H₀ is true.
  • If pp-value << significance level, reject H0​H₀. Otherwise there is insufficient evidence to reject H0​H₀. Conclude in context; a test never proves H0​H₀.

Statistics and probability · hypothesis tests

The tt-test for two means

  • Compares the means of two populations using two independent samples. The variables must be normally distributed; assume equal variances and use the pooled two-sample tt-test.
  • H0​H₀: μ1​=μ2​μ₁ = μ₂. Two-tailed: H1​H₁: μ1​=μ2​μ₁ ≠ μ₂. One-tailed: H1​H₁: μ1​>μ2​μ₁ > μ₂ (or <<), when the claim has a direction.
  • Choose the tail from the claim before looking at the data. Your GDC gives tt and the pp-value; compare pp with the significance level.

Recap

Big ideas

  • A sample should represent its population: random and stratified samples reduce bias; convenience and quota samples risk it.
  • On a cumulative frequency graph read the median at 2n​n/2 and the quartiles at 4n​n/4 and 43n​3n/4; IQR=Q3​−Q1​IQR = Q₃ − Q₁.
  • An outlier is more than 1.5×IQR1.5 × IQR from the nearest quartile; mark it with a cross on a box plot and decide in context whether it is genuine.
  • Mean xˉ=∑f∑fx​x̄ = (Σ fx)/(Σ f); for grouped data use mid-interval values to estimate it.
  • Standard deviation measures typical distance from the mean; find it on your GDC using the population value σx​σ_x; variance =σ2= σ².
  • Adding a constant shifts the mean but leaves the standard deviation alone; multiplying by kk multiplies both by kk.
  • Pearson's rr measures linear correlation, −1≤r≤1−1 ≤ r ≤ 1; correlation does not imply causation.
  • The regression line of yy on xx passes through (xˉ,yˉ​)(x̄, ȳ); use it to predict yy from xx within the data range, and interpret aa as the change in yy per unit of xx.
  • Spearman's rs​r_s ranks the data (averaging ties), measures any monotonic relationship and is less affected by outliers.
  • P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B)P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B) and P(A∣B)=P(B)P(A∩B)​P(A | B) = (P(A ∩ B))/(P(B)); Venn diagrams and trees do the bookkeeping.
  • For "at least one", find 1−P(none)1 − P(none). Without replacement, later probabilities change: multiply along branches, add between paths.
  • A discrete random variable has a distribution whose probabilities sum to 1; E(X)=∑xP(X=x)E(X) = Σ x P(X=x) is its long-run mean.
  • With XX as the player's gain (prize minus cost), a game is fair when E(X)=0E(X) = 0.
