CO2 / ppm316.9338.8369.7414.2
  • Every year it peaks in May and is lowest in September–October. In 2019: 414.9 ppm in May, 408.7 ppm in October. A swing of about 6 ppm.
  • 1960 to 2020: up 97 ppm, about 1.6 ppm per year on average.
  • The rise is speeding up: about 1.1 ppm per year in 1960–1980; about 2.2 ppm per year in 2000–2020.
Mission 1

Two patterns, two causes

  • The yearly swing: most land, and most plants, are in the Northern Hemisphere.
  • Northern summer: photosynthesis > respiration, so CO2 falls.
  • Northern autumn and winter: respiration and decomposition > photosynthesis, so CO2 rises.
  • The long-term rise: combustion of fossil fuels, and burning and clearing of forests, add CO2 faster than photosynthesis and the oceans remove it.
  • Carbon locked away for millions of years is returned to the air in decades.
  • Say: "the rise comes from combustion" — not: "from more people breathing out".
Quick check
?At Mauna Loa, atmospheric carbon dioxide concentration rises to a peak each May and falls to a minimum each September or October, a swing of several parts per million. What causes this annual fluctuation?
Mission 1

The greenhouse effect

  • Sunlight passes through the atmosphere and warms the Earth's surface.
  • The warm surface gives off infrared radiation.
  • Greenhouse gases absorb infrared and re-emit it, partly back towards the surface. This keeps Earth warm enough for life.
  • More greenhouse gas → more infrared retained → the enhanced greenhouse effect → global warming.
  • CO2 rises from combustion of fossil fuels and from deforestation.
  • Methane rises from ruminant livestock, rice paddies, landfill and leaks from gas and coal extraction.
  • Say: "greenhouse gases absorb infrared" — not: "the ozone hole lets in more heat".
Quick check
?Which is an anthropogenic cause of the rise in the atmospheric concentration of methane since pre-industrial times?
Mission check

?At a monitoring station in Tasmania, in the Southern Hemisphere, carbon dioxide shows a much smaller yearly swing than at Mauna Loa, but a similar long-term rise. Which explanation fits both observations?

Two clocks run in the same curve: the yearly breath of the northern forests, and the steady climb from burning carbon stored for millions of years.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why can a warming of just a degree or two force species to move, or vanish from a place?

Make a guess: Why can a warming of just a degree or two force species to move, or vanish from a place?

Mission 2

Species on the move

  • Each species survives only within a band of conditions: temperature, rainfall, season length.
  • As the climate warms, that band shifts towards the poles and up mountains.
  • New populations establish at the cool edge; old ones die out at the warm edge. The range shifts.
  • This happens over generations, through dispersal and the survival of young, not by individuals migrating.
  • No cooler place to go (mountain tops, poles), or barriers in the way? Local extinction, or extinction of the species.
  • Say: "the range shifts" — not: "the range grows" or "the animals move house".
Quick check
?The bird species of a mountain in New Guinea were surveyed in 1965 and again in 2012. For most species both the upper and the lower limits of the altitudinal range had moved upslope, by about 100 m on average, over a period in which the region warmed. What is the best interpretation?
Mission 2

Losers, winners and biodiversity

  • Likely losers: species with narrow tolerances, slow dispersal or specialist diets; mountain-top and polar species.
  • Roads, farms and towns can block movement, trapping species in shrinking ranges.
  • Local extinctions lower local biodiversity; some species may become extinct altogether.
  • Likely winners: species that spread easily, including some pests, pathogens and invasive species.
  • Species shift at different rates, so communities reassemble and interactions (predator–prey, pollinator–flower) change.
Quick check
?In a survey of forests in the eastern United States, seedlings of several northern tree species were found on average further north than mature trees of the same species, while at the southern edge of the range there were mature trees but few seedlings. What does this indicate?
Mission check

?A hypothetical small mammal lives only in cool meadows near the top of an isolated mountain. Over 40 years the region warms by 1.5 °C. Which outcome is most likely?

A degree or two moves the conditions a species needs. Species that can follow survive in new places; those with nowhere to go are lost.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Species have always gone extinct. So why are biologists so alarmed by the extinctions happening now?

Mission 3

Extinction, then and now

  • Species have always gone extinct, at a slow background rate.
  • Today the rate is far higher. Vertebrate species lost since 1900 would have taken 800–10,000 years to disappear at the background rate (depending on the group).

Mission 3

What is driving the losses?

  • Habitat loss: deforestation, clearance for farming, urbanisation, drainage of wetlands.
  • Over-exploitation: hunting, overfishing, collecting.
  • Pollution: pesticides, fertiliser run-off, plastics.
  • Invasive species, pests and diseases, spread by global transport.
  • Climate change: species' ranges shift; some cannot move.
  • Species most at risk: small or isolated populations, slow breeding, specialised needs, small ranges.
Mission 3

Climate change moves species

  • As climate warms, many species shift their ranges: towards the poles and uphill.
  • Real data (Chen et al. 2011, many species and regions combined): median shift 16.9 km per decade to higher latitude and 11.0 m per decade uphill.
  • Species that cannot move fast enough, or have nowhere to go (mountain tops, islands), suffer local extinctions.
  • Arriving species change communities; timing mismatches (e.g. flowering and pollinators) can follow.
Quick check
?Illustrative mountain. A plant grows between 1800 m and 2000 m altitude. The summit is at 2100 m. Assume its range moves uphill at 11.0 m per decade (the median rate above). About how long until the top of its range reaches the summit?
Mission 3

Small populations spiral down

  • A small population loses alleles by chance (genetic drift) and by inbreeding.
  • Low genetic diversity: fewer individuals can withstand a new disease or change.
  • Lower survival and reproduction make the population smaller still.
  • Each step strengthens the next: the population can spiral towards extinction.
Quick check
?The model shows how the size and the genetic diversity of a population can affect each other: Small population → Loss of genetic diversity by drift → Fewer individuals able to withstand new pressures → Lower survival and reproduction → back to Small population. Which statement best describes the relationship shown by the model?
Mission check

?In the early 1800s the passenger pigeon was probably the most numerous bird in North America, with flocks of billions. Through the 1800s it was hunted on a commercial scale, and the eastern forests where it fed and nested were cleared for farming. The last known bird died in a zoo in 1914. Which explanation is best supported?

Extinction is natural; this pace is not. Human activity is removing species far faster than new ones arise.

Mission 4

Mission 4: If the last of a species survives safely in a zoo, has that species really been saved?

Make a guess: If the last of a species survives safely in a zoo, has that species really been saved?

Mission 4

Why conserve biodiversity?

  • Ecological: species depend on each other. Losing a keystone species changes the whole community.
  • Genetic resource: wild relatives of crops carry alleles for disease resistance and drought tolerance.
  • Economic: food, timber, medicines, tourism; healthy soils (continuous monoculture depletes them).
  • Aesthetic: landscapes and wildlife people value and enjoy.
Mission 4

In situ conservation

  • In situ: protecting a species in its natural habitat.
  • Nature reserves and wildlife reserves.
  • Reserves need active management: control invasive species and poaching; keep habitats suitable.
  • Conserving gene pools in the wild: large protected areas and wildlife corridors link populations, so alleles move between them and inbreeding falls.
  • Advantage: the species keeps its habitat, its interactions and its natural selection pressures.
Mission 4

Ex situ conservation

  • Ex situ: conserving a species outside its natural habitat.
  • Zoos: captive breeding; studbooks pair unrelated animals to limit inbreeding; reintroduction.
  • Botanic gardens: living collections of rare plants; propagation; research.
  • Seed banks: seeds dried and frozen, then germinated and tested at intervals. Kew's Millennium Seed Bank holds seeds of over 40,000 species.
  • Gene pools in captivity: seed banks, frozen sperm, eggs and embryos; exchanging animals between zoos.
Quick check
?Seeds of many tropical rainforest trees are killed by the drying and freezing used in seed banks. A conservation programme wants to conserve one such tree species, whose natural forest is being logged. Which approach to conserving this species is appropriate?

Mission check

?A rare orchid survives in one wild meadow. Its seeds are now stored in a seed bank, and plants grow in a botanic garden. The meadow is due to be ploughed. Orchid seeds need a particular soil fungus to germinate. Which conclusion is best supported?

A species kept only in a zoo or a seed bank survives, but it is not truly saved. Conservation needs the habitat as well.

Mission 5

Mission 5: How can people keep taking food and materials from nature without wrecking it?

Make a guess: How can people keep taking food and materials from nature without wrecking it?

Mission 5

Take less than grows back

  • Sustainable: meets today's needs without reducing what future generations can take.
  • Harvesting is sustainable only if the rate of harvesting is below the rate of replacement.
  • Net replacement = growth + new young (recruitment) − natural deaths.
  • Renewable does not mean it cannot run out.
Quick check
?A stock of Atlantic cod has a biomass of 200 000 tonnes. Each year, growth of the fish and recruitment of young fish add 15% to the biomass, while natural mortality removes 5% of it. What is the annual rate of replacement of the stock, below which the catch must stay for harvesting to be sustainable?
Mission 5

Fishing without emptying the sea

  • Quotas: a maximum catch, set from estimates of the stock.
  • Minimum mesh size: young fish escape and live to breed.
  • Closed seasons and no-take zones: protect spawning fish and nursery areas.
  • Limits on fleet size or days at sea.
  • Warning signs: more effort for the same catch; the fish caught get younger and smaller.
  • Real case: Newfoundland's northern cod. Catches peaked at over 800 000 tonnes in 1968. By 1992 the spawning stock had fallen by about 99%, and the fishery was closed.
Quick checkRead the data
?Illustrative data.
Records for a cod fishery show that the annual catch stayed close to 250 000 tonnes for 20 years. Over the same period the number of fishing days needed to land that catch doubled, and the mean age of the cod caught fell from 8 years to 4 years. What do these data indicate?
Mission 5

Timber and forest products

  • Coppicing: cut a tree near the ground; many new stems regrow; cut again years later.
  • Rotational felling: fell one area at a time; replant; return only when it has regrown.
  • Selective felling: take only some mature trees; the forest structure and habitats remain.
  • Large-scale clear-felling exposes soil to erosion and leaching and destroys habitats.
  • Certification (e.g. FSC timber) lets buyers choose sustainably managed wood.
Mission 5

Production versus conservation

  • Deforestation: timber, farmland, cattle, palm oil vs habitats, soil and carbon stores.
  • Overfishing: food and jobs now vs fish stocks for the future.
  • Resolutions: quotas, protected areas, replanting, certification, agroforestry, ecotourism.
  • Attitudes and informed choices: eco-labels (e.g. MSC fish, FSC wood), less waste, sustainably sourced products.
  • Decisions based on sound science: the Montreal Protocol (1987) phased out CFCs after scientists showed they destroy ozone; the ozone layer is recovering.
Mission 5

Planetary boundaries

  • Nine Earth-system processes, each with a safe limit for humanity (Rockström et al., 2009).
  • Climate change · biosphere integrity · land-system change · freshwater change · biogeochemical flows (nitrogen, phosphorus) · ocean acidification · atmospheric aerosols · stratospheric ozone · novel entities (e.g. plastics, synthetic chemicals).
  • Beyond a boundary, the risk of large, possibly irreversible change rises.
  • 2025 assessment: seven of nine boundaries crossed.
  • Scientists set the boundaries and test them by peer review; societies decide what to do with the evidence.
Mission check

?Illustrative data.
A seaweed bed with a biomass of 5000 tonnes is harvested for food. Each year, growth adds 30% to the biomass and natural losses remove 10%. The current harvest is 1400 tonnes per year. Which conclusion is correct?

Fish, timber or seaweed: take no more than the net amount that grows back, and nature can keep supplying.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The yearly swing in CO2 is photosynthesis against respiration in the northern growing season; the long-term rise is combustion of fossil fuels and forests.
  • CO2 and methane absorb infrared; more of them enhances the greenhouse effect.

Mission 2

  • Warming shifts the conditions species need poleward and upslope; ranges follow over generations, and species with nowhere to go face local extinction.

Mission 3

  • Today's extinction rate is far above the background rate, driven by human activity: habitat loss, over-exploitation, pollution, invasive species and climate change.
  • Small populations with low genetic diversity can spiral towards extinction.

Mission 4

  • In situ conservation keeps species and their interactions in the wild; ex situ (zoos, botanic gardens, seed and tissue banks) is insurance. No single approach is enough.

Mission 5

  • Harvesting is sustainable only if it stays below net replacement (growth + recruitment − natural deaths).
