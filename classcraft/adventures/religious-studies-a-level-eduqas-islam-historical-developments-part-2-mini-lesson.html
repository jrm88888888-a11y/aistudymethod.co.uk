Mission 1

Mission 1: One verse seems to put husbands in charge. How have Muslim women scholars read it differently?

Make a guess: One verse seems to put husbands in charge. How have Muslim women scholars read it differently?

Mission 1

Equal before God

  • Muslims believe men and women come from one soul: "created you from a single person, created, of like nature, His mate" (Qur'an 4:1, Yusuf Ali).
  • Same duties of faith, same reward: "If any do deeds of righteousness,- be they male or female - and have faith, they will enter Heaven" (Qur'an 4:124, Yusuf Ali).
  • Qur'an 33:35 names believing men and women, side by side, ten times over, with one promise: forgiveness and great reward.
  • So most Muslims agree on spiritual equality. The debate is about roles in family and society.
Mission 1

Surah 4: family law

  • Surah 4 is called al-Nisa', "Women". Much of it is family law.
  • Marriage: a contract; the bride's consent; the mahr (dower) is hers (4:4). Up to four wives, but "if ye fear that ye shall not be able to deal justly (with them), then only one" (Qur'an 4:3, Yusuf Ali).
  • Kindness: "live with them on a footing of kindness and equity" (Qur'an 4:19, Yusuf Ali).
  • Inheritance: women inherit in their own right; a son's share is twice a daughter's (4:11). Classical reason: the man must support the family; her share is hers alone.
  • Conflict and divorce: arbiters from both families (4:35); a settlement if the husband is cruel (4:128); divorce rules in 2:228–232.
Mission 1Read the source

Qur'an 4:34

> "Men are the protectors and maintainers of women, because Allah has given the one more (strength) than the other, and because they support them from their means." (Qur'an 4:34, Yusuf Ali)

  • The verse goes on: if a husband fears nushuz (Yusuf Ali: "disloyalty and ill-conduct") from his wife: admonish her; then separate beds; then a word Yusuf Ali renders "beat them (lightly)" (paraphrase).
  • Pickthall renders the opening "Men are in charge of women".
  • Key Arabic words: qawwamun (maintainers; in charge), faddala (has given more, or preferred), daraba (to strike; it has many other senses).

Q1 What two reasons does the verse give for men's role?

Show answer

God has given one more than the other; men spend of their means.

Q2 Why does the translation matter here?

Show answer

Each English version already chooses a reading of qawwamun and daraba.

Mission 1

Classical readings

  • Most classical scholars read qawwamun as the husband's authority and duty to provide and protect, within the household.
  • They read the three steps as a ladder of last resort, and limited the third:
  • Not on the face, not to injure. Ibn Abbas is reported as saying: with a tooth-stick or the like (al-Tabari's commentary).
  • Aisha reported that the Prophet never struck a woman or a servant (Muslim).
  • A hadith: the best of you are those best to their wives (Tirmidhi; paraphrase).
  • Many jurists (e.g. al-Shafi'i) said that not striking at all is better.
  • So even traditional readings restricted the verse. The modern question is whether it gives permission at all.
Mission 1

Amina Wadud

  • Amina Wadud (b. 1952), American Muslim scholar: Qur'an and Woman: Rereading the Sacred Text from a Woman's Perspective (1992); Inside the Gender Jihad (2006).
  • Method: read each verse in its first context, alongside the whole Qur'an, guided by its universal principles (justice, the oneness of God).
  • Qawwamun is not built-in superiority: it depends on the husband actually providing ("because they support them").
  • Nushuz is also used of husbands (4:128), so it means a breakdown of marital harmony, not a wife's disobedience alone.
  • The striking clause restricted a violent practice in 7th-century Arabia; the Qur'an's direction is towards ending it.
  • In 2006 she wrote that she now says "no" to applying the clause literally at all (paraphrase).
Mission 1

Bint al-Shati'

  • Aisha Abd al-Rahman (1913–1998), pen-name Bint al-Shati' ("daughter of the shore"), Egyptian professor of Arabic literature and Qur'anic studies.
  • Your spec writes "Bint ash shati".
  • One of the first modern women to publish a commentary (tafsir) on the Qur'an: al-Tafsir al-Bayani (The Literary Explanation of the Qur'an, 1962–69).
  • Method: collect every use of a word in the Qur'an; let the Qur'an explain itself; reject later legends and forced "scientific" readings.
  • She wrote lives of the women of the Prophet's household, bringing them into the story of Islam.
  • She held that the Qur'an gave women rights to education, property and consent, but criticised Western feminism, keeping distinct family roles.
  • Pioneering, because a woman now interpreted the text itself. Wadud's method shares this habit of reading the Qur'an through itself.
Mission check

?A student meets the word zawj (spouse, pair) in a verse about marriage. She lists every place the Qur'an uses it, notices it is used of both partners equally, and reads the verse in that light. Which approach is she using?

The words of the text are fixed for Muslims; how to read them is the debate. Next: the veil.

Mission 2

Words for the veil

  • Hijab: literally "screen" or "curtain". Today usually a headscarf covering hair and neck.
  • Khimar: head-covering (24:31). Jilbab: outer garment or cloak (33:59).
  • Niqab: face-veil, eyes visible. Burqa: full covering with a mesh over the eyes. Chador: Iranian full-length cloak.
  • Awra: the parts of the body to be covered in public.
  • Hanafi and Maliki scholars, the Ja'fari Shi'a school and many others: a woman covers all but face and hands. Many Hanbali and some Shafi'i scholars require the face to be covered too.
  • A small number of modern scholars argue the headscarf itself is not obligatory.
Mission 2Read the source

Modesty for men and women

> "Say to the believing men that they should lower their gaze and guard their modesty: that will make for greater purity for them" (Qur'an 24:30, Yusuf Ali)

> "And say to the believing women that they should lower their gaze and guard their modesty; that they should not display their beauty and ornaments except what (must ordinarily) appear thereof; that they should draw their veils over their bosoms" (Qur'an 24:31, Yusuf Ali)

  • 33:59 asks the Prophet's wives, daughters and the believing women to draw their outer garments around them, "that they should be known (as such) and not molested" (Qur'an 33:59, Yusuf Ali).
  • A hadith: modesty (haya') is a branch of faith (Bukhari, Muslim; paraphrase).

Q1 Who is told to be modest first?

Show answer

Men: 24:30 comes before 24:31.

Q2 What phrase do scholars argue over?

Show answer

"Except what must ordinarily appear": face and hands, or less.

Mission 2

Haleh Afshar

  • Haleh Afshar (1944–2022), Iranian-born British professor of politics and women's studies, University of York; Baroness Afshar from 2007.
  • Islam and Feminisms: An Iranian Case-Study (1998): women in the Islamic Republic used Islamic arguments to win back rights in education, work and family law.
  • Islamic feminism: women can claim equality from within their faith, not only from secular feminism.
  • Her research with young British Muslim women who cover: many chose it themselves, sometimes against family wishes, as faith and as identity (paraphrase).
  • She opposed compulsion both ways: forced veiling in Iran and forced unveiling by bans.
  • Your spec: "the contemporary feminist view of Haleh Afshar".
Quick check
?Which claim fits Haleh Afshar's view of Muslim women and the veil?
Mission 2Diversity

Why Muslim women cover, or do not

  • Obedience: a duty from God (24:31, 33:59), held by most scholars.
  • Identity: being visibly Muslim, especially as a minority.
  • Freedom: some say covering means being judged by mind, not body.
  • Protest: against Western consumer culture, or against a regime (Iran before 1979).
  • Not covering: some Muslim women hold that modesty is in conduct, not in a scarf; others face pressure or danger if they cover.
  • Culture or religion? Burqa, chador and niqab differ by region: culture shapes how Islam is lived.
Mission 2Evaluate

"The veil is a symbol of oppression."

  • For: some Muslim reformers saw veiling and seclusion as barriers to education and public life. Where the law forces it (Iran), it is imposed by the state. Family pressure is real for some women.
  • Against: many women choose it freely (Afshar). Bans also take away choice.
  • Another view: the cloth has no single meaning: oppression or resistance depends on choice and context.
  • Judge it by: Who chose it? Who is speaking for the women? Is the meaning religious, cultural or political? Does a ban or a law help women?
Mission check

?In one country the law forces women to cover their hair in public. In another, the law fines women who cover their faces in public. Muslim women protest in both. Which idea, defended by Muslim feminists such as Afshar, links the two protests?

The same cloth can be duty, identity, freedom or a weapon of empire. Context and choice decide its meaning.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Can British Muslims keep their faith whole and still belong fully in Britain?

Make a guess: Can British Muslims keep their faith whole and still belong fully in Britain?

Mission 3

Coming to Britain

  • Small Muslim communities existed earlier: Yemeni and Somali seamen in Cardiff, South Shields and Liverpool; the Shah Jahan Mosque, Woking (1889).
  • 1950s–60s: men from Pakistan (many from Mirpur, Kashmir) and India (Gujarat) came to work in mills and foundries in Lancashire, Yorkshire and the Midlands.
  • Many planned to return. After the Commonwealth Immigrants Act 1962 tightened entry, wives and children joined them: sojourners became settlers.
  • 1970s–80s: families from Bangladesh (Sylhet); East African Asians expelled from Uganda (1972).
  • 1990s onwards: refugees and migrants from Somalia, Bosnia, Iraq, Afghanistan, Syria and elsewhere; also British converts.
  • 2021 Census, England and Wales: about 3.9 million Muslims (6.5% of the population).
Mission 3

Not one community but many

  • Ethnic diversity: South Asian heritage is the largest group; also Arab, Somali, Turkish, Iranian, Malaysian, Nigerian, white British and others.
  • Sunni traditions (most British Muslims): Deobandi (scholarly, reformist; many seminaries), Barelwi (devotional, Sufi-influenced), Salafi, the Tablighi Jamaat (preaching movement).
  • Shi'a: mainly Twelvers (Ithna 'Ashari); also Isma'ilis (the Nizari Isma'ilis are led by the Aga Khan).
  • Mosques often began along lines of village, language and school of thought.
  • Generations differ: many young British-born Muslims separate "culture" from "religion" and choose their own path.

Diversity as a sign

> "O mankind! Lo! We have created you male and female, and have made you nations and tribes that ye may know one another. Lo! the noblest of you, in the sight of Allah, is the best in conduct." (Qur'an 49:13, Pickthall)

> "There is no compulsion in religion." (Qur'an 2:256, Pickthall)

Q1 According to 49:13, why do nations and tribes exist?

Show answer

So that people may know one another.

Q2 Which verse could a British Muslim use to support freedom of religion for all?

Show answer

2:256: faith cannot be forced.

Mission 3

Segregation, assimilation, integration

  • Segregation: communities live separate lives: different streets, schools, workplaces.
  • Assimilation: a minority is absorbed into the majority culture and loses what makes it distinct.
  • Integration: full part in shared life (work, law, politics) while keeping a distinct faith.
  • After riots in Oldham, Burnley and Bradford (2001), the Cantle Report (2001) found communities living "parallel lives".
  • Causes debated: cheap housing near the mills, discrimination, choice to live near family and mosque, and others moving away.
  • Most British Muslim bodies reject both segregation and assimilation and argue for integration.
Mission 3

Food, dress, practice, education

  • Food: halal meat; school meals and workplace canteens; disputes about whether halal meat must be unstunned.
  • Dress: hijab worn widely; the face veil (niqab) debated in schools, courts and work. Some women choose no headscarf.
  • Practice: Friday prayer (Jumu'ah) in work or school time; prayer rooms; fasting in Ramadan during exams.
  • Education: evening Qur'an classes (madrasas); state-funded Muslim schools since 1998 (Islamia Primary, Brent); RE and sex education debates.
  • The law: UK law protects practising a religion (Article 9 of the European Convention on Human Rights; Equality Act 2010), within limits for safety and others' rights.
  • A society where many faiths and none live side by side is pluralist (religious pluralism).
  • Spec spellings: salah, masjid, shari'a.
Quick check
?A Muslim teenager in Bradford wears hijab, prays at school, plays in a mixed football team and plans to study law. Which term best describes her approach?
Mission 3

The Muslim Council of Britain

  • Founded 1997: an umbrella body of affiliated mosques, schools, charities and professional groups (it reports over 500).
  • Aims: a united voice for British Muslims; the common good; fighting discrimination.
  • Work: guidance to mosques (e.g. during Covid-19); Visit My Mosque days (from 2015); voter guides; advocacy on Islamophobia.
  • First woman Secretary-General: Zara Mohammed (2021).
  • Criticisms: not elected by ordinary Muslims; weighted to some Sunni South Asian groups; Shi'a, Sufi and other bodies (e.g. the British Muslim Forum, 2005) formed their own voices.
  • UK governments have largely avoided formal engagement with it since 2009; the MCB calls this unjust.
Mission 3Evaluate

Can Muslims keep their identity in secular Britain?

  • Claim: Muslims in Britain can fully belong without losing their religious identity.
  • For: strong legal protection (Article 9, Equality Act); thriving mosques, schools, halal economy; many British-born Muslims are both confident Muslims and active citizens.
  • For: 49:13 gives religious grounds for taking part.
  • Against: secular values (privacy of religion, alcohol-based social life, sexual ethics) pull young people away; some lose practice.
  • Against: Islamophobia and discrimination push some towards separate lives; dress codes and halal rules still cause conflict.
  • Judge it by: What counts as "identity": belief, practice, or community? Is assimilation ever possible without loss? Who decides?
  • Spec issue: "the possibility of assimilation into a secular society".
Quick check

Mission 4: Can a Muslim live faithfully as a minority, and why do so many Europeans fear Islam?

Make a guess: Can a Muslim live faithfully as a minority, and why do so many Europeans fear Islam?

Mission 4

After 9/11: multiculturalism under fire

  • Events: 11 September 2001; Madrid (2004) and London (7 July 2005) bombings; the Danish cartoons (2005–06); Charlie Hebdo (2015).
  • Laws: France banned conspicuous religious symbols in state schools (2004) and face coverings in public (2010); Swiss voters banned new minarets (2009).
  • Opponents of multiculturalism: Angela Merkel (2010) and David Cameron (Munich, 2011) said state multiculturalism had failed and called for shared national values.
  • Supporters reply: multiculturalism is not the cause of extremism; exclusion and discrimination push young people away.
  • Muslims across Europe condemned the attacks, yet often felt treated as suspects.
  • Many Muslim scholars teach that Muslims can live faithfully as a minority where they are free to practise, keeping the law of the land as a promise ("keep the covenant", Qur'an 17:34, Pickthall).
Mission 4

What is Islamophobia?

  • Term made widely known by the Runnymede Trust report Islamophobia: A Challenge for Us All (1997).
  • It contrasted closed views of Islam (one unchanging bloc; separate from other cultures; inferior; an enemy; manipulative) with open views (diverse; interacting; open to fair criticism).
  • APPG on British Muslims (2018) definition: Islamophobia is rooted in racism and targets "expressions of Muslimness or perceived Muslimness".
  • Debate: critics say "Islamophobia" may chill legitimate criticism of religion and prefer "anti-Muslim hatred"; supporters say it names a real pattern of prejudice.
  • Todd Green (The Fear of Islam, 2015) traces such fears back through European history, long before 9/11.
  • Spec words: bias, misrepresentation, inaccuracy, Islamophobia.
Quick check
?A commentator writes: "Muslims everywhere think the same way and can never really accept Western values." In Runnymede's terms, which closed view is this mainly?
Mission 4

Bias, misrepresentation, inaccuracy

  • Bias: choosing which stories to tell. Studies of UK newspapers (e.g. Cardiff University, 2008) found most stories about British Muslims framed them as a threat or a problem.
  • Misrepresentation: the actions of a few presented as typical; "Islamist" and "Muslim" blurred.
  • Inaccuracy: false claims, sometimes later corrected by the press regulator (IPSO).
  • Politics: politicians' language about veils, migration or "no-go areas" can make prejudice seem respectable.
  • Online: algorithms reward outrage; organised hate campaigns; groups such as Tell MAMA record anti-Muslim incidents.
  • Edward Said (1935–2003), Covering Islam (1981): Western media present "Islam" as one threatening thing.

Verify before you judge

> "O ye who believe! If an evil-liver bring you tidings, verify it, lest ye smite some folk in ignorance and afterward repent of what ye did." (Qur'an 49:6, Pickthall)

> "let not hatred of any people seduce you that ye deal not justly." (Qur'an 5:8, Pickthall)

Q1 What does 49:6 tell Muslims to do with news from an untrustworthy source?

Show answer

Q2 How might a Muslim journalist use 5:8 when reporting on people who are hostile to Islam?

Show answer

Mission 4Evaluate

How far does the media shape perceptions of Islam?

  • Claim: The media is the main cause of negative Western perceptions of Islam.
  • For: most people meet Islam mainly through news; studies show threat-framing; online outrage spreads fast; Said's argument.
  • Against: real events (terror attacks) shape fear whatever the coverage; older history (crusades, empire) shaped attitudes before modern media (Green).
  • Against: personal contact with Muslim neighbours and colleagues changes views; media also produces positive portrayals.
  • Judge it by: What evidence links coverage to attitudes? Which media: press, TV, online? Is Islam represented accurately today?
Mission check

?After a terrorist attack abroad, a local mosque in Leeds is sprayed with graffiti. A councillor says: "What do they expect? Muslims are all the same, and they answer for what their people do." The mosque holds an open day with neighbours. Which description is most accurate?

Living as a minority raises questions for Muslims themselves, and for how the majority sees them. Both are still being worked out.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Most Muslims agree men and women are spiritually equal (4:1, 4:124, 33:35); debate is about family roles.
  • Classical scholars read 4:34 as household authority with striking as a limited last step; feminist readers (Wadud) read the role as conditional and the striking clause as a restriction on its way to ending.
  • Bint al-Shati' pioneered women's tafsir and a method of reading the Qur'an through itself.

Mission 2

  • Qur'an 24:30–31 and 33:59 ask modesty of men and women; most scholars require women to cover all but face and hands.
  • Afshar: Islamic feminism and women's own choice, against compulsion in either direction.

Mission 3

  • Muslim migration to Britain grew from the 1950s; family settlement after 1962 built mosques, halal food and schools; British Muslims are diverse in ethnicity and school of thought.
  • Segregation, assimilation and integration are different responses; most Muslim bodies seek integration, protected by freedom of religion (Article 9).
  • The Muslim Council of Britain (1997) aims to speak for British Muslims; its representativeness is debated.

Mission 4

  • Islamophobia (Runnymede, 1997) involves closed views of Islam; bias, misrepresentation and inaccuracy in politics, media and online shape perceptions.
