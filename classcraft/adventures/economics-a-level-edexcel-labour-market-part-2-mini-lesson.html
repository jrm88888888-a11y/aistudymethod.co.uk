Mission 1

Mission 1: Can a group of workers win higher pay without anyone losing their job?

Make a guess: Can a union win higher pay without costing any jobs?

Mission 1

What a trade union does

  • A trade union: an organisation of workers that acts for its members.
  • Collective bargaining: the union negotiates pay and conditions for all its members at once, instead of each worker alone.
  • Aims: higher pay · better conditions and safety · job security · training · a voice in how work changes.
  • Tools: negotiation; if talks fail, industrial action (strikes, overtime bans, working to rule).
  • A union acts as a single seller of labour: some of the market power of a monopoly.
Mission 1

Real data: unions in the UK

  • UK, 2025 (Department for Business and Trade):
  • 6.6 million employees were union members.
  • Union density (members as % of employees): 22.4%, down from 32.4% in 1995.
  • Public sector 48.5%; private sector 12.1%.
  • Union membership peaked at 13.2 million in 1979 (unions listed in Great Britain).
  • Union wage premium: members' average hourly pay was 5.3% above non-members' in 2025 (before allowing for other differences between the two groups).

Mission 1

A union in a competitive labour market

  • Without a union: wage We, employment Le.
  • The union negotiates Wu above We. Firms will not pay less, so the supply of labour to firms is horizontal at Wu, then follows S.
  • At Wu firms move up their demand curve: employment falls to Lu.
  • At Wu more people want these jobs (Ls). The gap Ls − Lu is excess supply of labour.
  • Illustrative data: Ld = 1000 − 40W, Ls = 60W − 200. We = £12, Le = 520.
  • Union wage £14: Ld = 1000 − 560 = 440; Ls = 840 − 200 = 640.
  • Employment falls by 80; excess supply 200.
  • Wage +16.7%, employment −15.4%: the wage elasticity of demand for labour is about −0.92.
Mission 1

Raising pay without losing jobs (competitive market)

  • Inelastic demand for labour: if firms cannot easily cut jobs, a higher wage costs few jobs.
  • Productivity deals: the union agrees new working methods or training. Higher productivity shifts demand for labour right.
  • Shifting demand in other ways: campaigns for the industry's product, health and safety that cuts sick days.
  • High profits: a firm earning supernormal profit can pay more without closing.
  • Otherwise, in a competitive market, a wage above We means fewer jobs: union members gain, but others (outsiders) lose.
  • Say: "a contraction of demand for labour" — not: "demand for labour falls" (the curve has not moved).
Mission 1

A union facing a monopsony

  • Recall: a monopsonist hires where MRP = MCL and pays the low wage Wm on the supply curve.
  • The union sets a wage Wu between Wm and Wc. Now the firm can hire up to the supply curve at Wu, with no need to raise anyone's pay.
  • So the MCL becomes horizontal at Wu (MCL = ACL = Wu) up to the supply curve.
  • The firm hires more workers, because each extra worker now costs only Wu.
  • Illustrative data: ACL = S: W = 5 + 0.5L; MCL = 5 + L; MRP = 20 − 0.5L (L = workers).
  • Monopsony: 5 + L = 20 − 0.5L → L = 10, W = £10.
Mission 1Graph lab

Set the union wage

  • Takeaway: between the monopsony wage and the MRP at the monopsony level of employment, a union wage can raise employment; push higher and jobs fall. At a union wage of £12: workers willing = (12 − 5) ÷ 0.5 = 14; the firm wants (20 − 12) ÷ 0.5 = 16. Employment = 14, so pay and employment rise: £10 → £12, 10 → 14 workers.

Mission 1

What gives a union power?

  • Membership: a high share of the workforce in the union, so a strike stops production.
  • Inelastic demand for labour, which is more likely when:
  • wages are a small share of total costs · workers are hard to replace with machines or other workers · demand for the product is price inelastic.
  • The firm's profits: profitable firms can afford to pay more.
  • The state of the economy: low unemployment makes strikers harder to replace.
  • The law: rules on ballots, strikes and picketing.
  • Information: unions can tell members what similar workers earn, which reduces employers' power from imperfect information.
Mission 1

Unions and labour market flexibility

  • Labour market flexibility: how quickly wages, hours and jobs adjust to changes in demand.
  • Unions may reduce flexibility:
  • Wage rigidity: wages slow to fall when demand falls, so more jobs are lost.
  • Resisting job cuts, new methods or changes to hours.
  • Unions may also help:
  • Voice: workers raise problems instead of quitting, cutting staff turnover and training costs.
  • Agreeing change: productivity deals can be easier with one negotiating partner.
  • Countering monopsony power.
Quick check
?Unions in four industries each win a 10% pay rise. Other things equal, in which industry are job losses likely to be smallest?
Mission 1

Evaluate: do unions help or harm?

It depends on…

  • Market structure: in a monopsony, unions can raise pay and jobs; in a competitive market, higher pay usually costs some jobs.
  • Elasticity of demand for labour: the more inelastic, the smaller the job loss.
  • Productivity: deals that raise output per worker can pay for higher wages.
  • Who is counted: insiders (members in work) may gain while outsiders lose jobs or chances.
  • Scale and timing: strikes cost output; wage rises above productivity growth across the economy can push up costs and prices.
Mission check

?On a remote island, one fish-processing plant employs almost all workers and pays the monopsony wage. A new union agrees a wage above the current wage but below the wage a competitive market would set. The plant can hire anyone willing to work at that wage. What happens to employment at the plant?

Facing a single employer, a union wage can raise both pay and jobs.

Mission 2

Mission 2: If a law lifts the lowest pay, do some workers lose their jobs?

Make a guess: A government raises the minimum wage. What happens to the number of jobs?

Mission 2

A legal wage floor

  • A minimum wage: the lowest hourly wage an employer may legally pay.
  • It is a price floor in the labour market. It only has an effect if set above the equilibrium wage.
  • Aims: raise low pay · reduce in-work poverty and inequality · make work pay more than benefits.
  • UK example: a National Minimum Wage since 1999. The rate for workers aged 21 and over is called the National Living Wage.
  • From 1 April 2026: £12.71 an hour for 21 and over (it was £12.21 in 2025); £10.85 for ages 18–20; £8.00 for ages 16–17 and apprentices.
  • Rates are set by the government on the advice of the Low Pay Commission.
  • Some employers also pay a voluntary "living wage", based on the cost of living rather than set by law.
Mission 2

The diagram: a competitive labour market

  • Step 1: labour market clears at We, Le.
  • Step 2: a minimum wage Wmin above We.
  • At Wmin, firms move up their demand curve: employment contracts to Ld.
  • More people want to work at Wmin: supply of labour extends to Ls.
  • Step 3: Ls − Ld is excess supply of labour: unemployment caused by the minimum wage.
  • Workers who keep their jobs earn more; those who lose jobs or cannot find one lose.
  • Say: "a contraction of demand for labour" — not: "demand for labour falls" (the curve has not moved).
Mission 2

Worked example

  • Illustrative data. Ld = 900 − 50W; Ls = 100W − 300 (W in £ per hour, L = workers).
  • Equilibrium: 900 − 50W = 100W − 300 → We = £8, Le = 500.
  • Minimum wage £9: Ld = 900 − 450 = 450; Ls = 900 − 300 = 600.
  • Employment falls by 50. Unemployment (excess supply) = 600 − 450 = 150.
  • Wage +12.5%; employment −10%.
  • Wage elasticity of demand for labour ≈ −10 ÷ 12.5 = −0.8 (inelastic): the wage bill rises from £4000 to £4050 an hour.
Quick check
?Use the market in the previous screen. The minimum wage is set at £10 instead. How much unemployment does it create?
Mission 2

How big is the effect?

  • The bite: the minimum wage as a % of the median wage. A higher bite affects more jobs.
  • UK bite: 45.6% in 1999, 52.5% in 2015; the government then set a target of two-thirds of median hourly pay, to be reached in 2024.
  • Elasticity of demand for labour: job losses are bigger where machines or other workers can replace low-paid staff, wages are a big share of costs, or customers can switch.
  • Elasticity of supply of labour: the more new job-seekers a higher wage draws in, the bigger the excess supply.
  • Firms can also respond in other ways: cut hours, training or perks · raise prices · accept lower profit · reorganise to raise productivity.
Mission 2Graph lab

Set the minimum wage

  • Takeaway: a floor below We does nothing; above We, the job loss grows with the bite and with the elasticity of demand for labour.
Mission 2

A minimum wage facing a monopsony

  • Recall: a monopsony employer hires Lm at wage Wm, below the competitive Wc, Lc.
  • A minimum wage between Wm and Wc makes the MCL horizontal at Wmin: an extra worker no longer raises everyone's pay.
  • The firm hires more, up to the supply curve: wages and employment both rise.
  • Set above the MRP at Lm, it would cut employment below Lm.
  • The same logic as a union wage (see Mission 1).
Mission 2

Evidence: what happened?

  • New Jersey, 1992: the state minimum wage rose from $4.25 to $5.05 an hour. Neighbouring Pennsylvania's did not change.
  • Card and Krueger (1994) surveyed fast-food restaurants in both states. They found no fall in New Jersey's fast-food employment relative to Pennsylvania's.
  • Critics (Neumark and Wascher, 2000), using payroll records, found a small fall. The debate on methods continues.
  • UK: the Low Pay Commission (2024) said it had "not found strong evidence" that the National Living Wage reduced employment or hours.
  • Effects depend on the bite: evidence from moderate rises may not apply to much larger ones.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. Country X has a minimum wage of 6 dollars an hour and a median wage of 10 dollars. Country Y has a minimum wage of 9 dollars and a median wage of 18 dollars. Other things equal, which minimum wage is more likely to reduce employment?
Mission 2

Maximum wages and public sector pay

  • A maximum wage is a legal ceiling on pay. Binding below We, it creates a shortage of labour (excess demand).
  • Example: EU rules limited bankers' bonuses to 100% of fixed pay (200% with shareholder approval). UK regulators removed the cap from 31 October 2023.
  • Firms can respond by raising fixed salaries instead, so total pay may change less than the cap suggests.
  • Public sector pay is set by the government, often on advice from pay review bodies.
  • UK: pay was frozen in 2011–12 and 2012–13 for all but the lowest earners, then rose about 1% a year. By 2017 some pay review bodies reported recruitment and retention problems.
  • National pay scales ignore local labour markets: shortages are worst where private pay is high (London and the South East).
Mission 2

Evaluate: does a minimum wage help?

It depends on…

  • The bite: moderate floors show small job effects; very high ones risk more.
  • Elasticity of demand for labour: easy substitution by machines means larger losses.
  • Market structure: with monopsony power, a floor can raise jobs and pay.
  • The economy: in a recession, firms are less able to absorb higher costs.
  • Who gains: many minimum-wage earners are not in the poorest households; benefits and taxes may target poverty better.
  • Other effects: higher productivity and lower staff turnover can offset the cost; fewer hours or perks can offset the gain.
Mission check

?A minimum wage is raised by the same amount above the market wage in two industries. In industry A, firms can easily replace staff with self-service machines. In industry B, there is no practical substitute for the workers, and wages are a small share of costs. Other things equal, where is the fall in employment likely to be larger?

The same rise in the minimum wage costs more jobs where workers are easy to replace.

Mission 3

Mission 3: When should a government stop big firms from merging, fixing prices or growing too powerful?

Make a guess: When should a government stop big firms from merging, fixing prices or growing too powerful?

Mission 3

Why have competition policy?

  • Market power can mean higher prices, lower output, less choice and a deadweight loss.
  • Firms with little competition may also let costs drift up (X-inefficiency) and innovate less.
  • Competition policy: laws and authorities that promote competition and stop firms abusing market power.
Mission 3

Three targets

1. Anti-competitive agreements (restrictive practices): cartels, price fixing, sharing out markets, bid rigging.

2. Abuse of a dominant position: e.g. predatory pricing (pricing below cost to drive out a rival), refusing to supply rivals, forcing buyers to take one product to get another.

3. Mergers that would substantially lessen competition.

  • Being large is not illegal. Abusing that position is.
  • The key question: would buyers face higher prices, lower quality or less choice?
  • A firm facing cheap imports or easy entry by rivals has little power to raise prices, even with a large share.
Mission 3

UK and EU rules

  • Competition and Markets Authority (CMA), since 2014: investigates cartels, abuse of dominance and mergers.
  • Competition Act 1998: bans anti-competitive agreements and abuse of a dominant position. Fines up to 10% of worldwide turnover.
  • Enterprise Act 2002: merger control; taking part in a cartel can be a criminal offence.
  • Leniency: the first cartel member to report can avoid a fine. This makes cartels unstable.
  • Regulators (e.g. Ofgem, Ofwat, Ofcom) also have competition powers in their industries.
  • Real case: in 2019 the CMA blocked the merger of Sainsbury's and Asda, two of the largest UK supermarket chains.
  • EU (awareness only): the European Commission enforces bans on cartels and abuse of dominance. In 2016 it fined truck makers €2.93 billion for a price-fixing cartel, and in 2017 Google €2.42 billion for abusing its dominance in online search.
  • Since the end of 2020 the Commission no longer rules on UK markets. The CMA does.

?Which of these is most likely to break competition law?
Mission 3

Worked example: a merger

  • Illustrative data. Market shares (%): A 30, B 20, C 15, D 10, others 25 (each under 5).
  • Three-firm concentration ratio (CR3) before: 30 + 20 + 15 = 65%.
  • B and C propose to merge: share 35%.
  • CR3 after: 35 + 30 + 10 = 75%.
  • Merger types: horizontal (same stage, rivals: B + C), vertical (supplier and buyer), conglomerate (unrelated markets).
  • Horizontal mergers get the closest scrutiny: they remove a rival directly.
  • The authority can clear the merger, clear it with conditions (e.g. sell some stores), or block it.
  • Against the loss of a rival, it weighs any cost savings passed on to buyers.
Mission 3Graph lab

What does a cartel cost buyers?

Illustrative data. Market demand: P = 50 − Q. MC = AC = 10. A cartel acts like a monopoly: P = 30, Q = 20. Competition would give P = 10, Q = 40.

  • Takeaway: ending a cartel returns its profit to buyers and removes the deadweight loss.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. Market demand is P = 80 − 2Q and MC = AC = 20. A cartel sets Q = 15 and P = 50, earning a profit of 450. Under competition, P = 20 and Q = 30. If the cartel is broken up, by how much does consumer surplus rise?
Mission 3

Other tools to promote competition

  • Break up a monopoly into competing firms.
  • Price controls and regulation: price caps (RPI − X), quality targets.
  • Ban unfair trading practices: e.g. misleading pricing, forcing suppliers into unfair terms.
  • Deregulation: remove legal barriers to entry (see Mission 4).
  • Competitive tendering: firms bid for the right to supply a public service.
  • Privatisation: sell state-owned firms, often opening the market to rivals (see Mission 4).
  • Help for small businesses: grants, simpler rules, access to finance.
  • Make markets more contestable: lower barriers to entry so the threat of entry disciplines incumbents.
Mission 3

Impact on the economy

  • Prices: fall as cartels end and rivals enter.
  • Output and employment: output rises towards the competitive level. Jobs may rise across the market, though a merger blocked or a firm broken up can also cost jobs at the firm itself.
  • Profit: supernormal profit is squeezed.
  • Efficiency: allocative (P nearer MC), productive and X-efficiency improve. Dynamic efficiency may rise or fall.
  • Quality and choice: rivals compete to attract buyers.
  • Economic welfare: deadweight loss falls.
Mission 3Evaluate

How well does competition policy work?

  • It depends on information: proving a cartel, or telling predation from fierce competition, is hard and slow.
  • It depends on the size of the market: blocking a merger may stop a firm reaching the scale it needs to compete with global rivals.
  • It depends on dynamic efficiency: large firms' supernormal profit may fund R&D.
  • It depends on fines: too small to deter, or passed on to buyers?
  • It depends on the authority: regulatory capture (it comes to side with the firms it oversees) and the cost of investigations.
  • It depends on contestability: where entry is easy, the threat of entry may protect buyers without intervention.
Mission check

?Illustrative case: four firms supply cement in a country, with market shares of 35%, 25%, 20% and 20%. The two smallest firms propose to merge, saying it will cut their costs. Which finding gives the strongest economic case for the competition authority to allow the merger?

The test is not size or profit but whether buyers would lose: higher prices, less choice or lower quality.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Should a country's trains, water pipes and power lines be run by the state or by private companies?

Make a guess: Should a country's trains, water pipes and power lines be run by the state or by private companies?

Mission 4

Four tools, four meanings

  • Nationalisation: the state takes a firm or industry into public ownership.
  • Privatisation: transfer of a firm or activity from the public sector to the private sector.
  • Regulation: rules set by government or an appointed regulator, e.g. on prices, quality, safety or entry.
  • Deregulation: removing rules that restrict entry, prices or how firms operate.
  • Ownership (who owns it) and market structure (how many rivals) are separate questions.
  • Say: "privatisation changes the owner" — not: "privatisation creates competition".
Mission 4

Forms of privatisation

  • Sale of a state-owned firm: shares sold to the public or a buyer (denationalisation). E.g. British Telecom (1984).
  • Contracting out (competitive tendering): the state still pays for a service but invites private firms to bid to provide it, e.g. bin collection.
  • Liberalisation: opening a state monopoly to private rivals (a form of deregulation).
  • Public–private partnership (PPP): a private firm finances, builds and often runs a public project (e.g. a hospital); the state pays over many years.
  • Sale of state assets, e.g. land or council houses.
Mission 4

The case for privatisation

  • Profit motive: private owners gain from cutting costs, so less X-inefficiency (waste when a firm faces little pressure to keep costs down).
  • Shareholder pressure: a poorly run firm's share price falls, and it risks a takeover.
  • Competition, where the industry allows rivals: lower prices, more choice.
  • Access to private capital for investment, without adding to government borrowing.
  • One-off revenue for the government from the sale.
  • Less political interference in pricing and investment decisions.
  • Example: the UK sold telecoms (1984), gas (1986), water in England and Wales (1989), electricity (1990–91) and rail (1994–97).
Mission 4

The case against, and renationalisation

  • A natural monopoly (one network serves the whole market most cheaply, e.g. a power grid) sold whole stays a monopoly: a private monopoly still needs regulation.
  • Short-termism: dividends may come before long-term investment.
  • Social objectives may be dropped, e.g. loss-making rural routes.
  • Sale price: if shares are sold too cheaply, taxpayers lose; the one-off revenue replaces a flow of future profits.
  • Fragmentation: splitting a network among many firms can raise coordination costs.
  • Renationalisation arguments: run for the public interest, cheaper public borrowing, no dividends to pay out, one integrated network.
  • Example: Railtrack (privatised rail infrastructure) was replaced in 2002 by Network Rail, now a public body. A 2024 law lets passenger train services in Great Britain return to public ownership as contracts end.
Quick check
?A city council stops using its own staff to clean public parks. It invites private firms to bid, and pays the winning firm to do the work for five years. The parks stay free to use. Which policy is this?
Mission 4

Nationalisation: evaluate

  • For:
    • Can price nearer MC and serve social aims (e.g. universal service), not just profit.
    • Long-term investment in networks; account for externalities.
    • Rescue of a failing firm that the economy relies on, e.g. the bank Northern Rock (UK, 2008).
  • Against:
    • Weak profit incentive: X-inefficiency; losses may be covered by taxpayers (a "soft budget").
    • Political interference, e.g. prices held down before elections.
    • Investment competes with schools and hospitals for public money.
    • Cost of compensating the previous owners.
Mission 4

Deregulation, licences and prohibitions

  • A licence limits who may operate (e.g. taxis, mobile spectrum). A prohibition bans an activity outright.
  • Deregulation removes barriers, so new firms can enter: supply rises, prices fall, choice widens.
  • Example: the US Airline Deregulation Act (1978) ended government control of fares and routes.
  • Example: UK local buses outside London were deregulated in 1986: any licensed operator could run a route.
  • Risks: firms "cherry-pick" profitable routes; safety and quality may suffer; financial deregulation can raise risk-taking.
  • Say: "deregulation lowers barriers to entry" — not: "deregulation means no rules at all".
Mission 4Graph lab

Lift the licence cap

Illustrative data. A city caps taxi licences, limiting rides to 5,000 a day. Fare: 20. Cost of the last ride (MC): 11.

  • Takeaway: removing an entry limit shifts the market to where price = MC. The gap was a reward for holding a licence.
Mission 4

Worked example: competitive tendering

  • Illustrative data. A council's in-house refuse service costs 1.20 million a year.
  • Bids: Firm X 0.96 million; Firm Y 1.02 million.
  • Saving with X = 1.20 − 0.96 = 0.24 million. As a %: 0.24 ÷ 1.20 × 100 = 20%.
  • Saving with Y = 0.18 million = 15%.
  • But the cheapest bid is not always best value: check quality standards, penalties for missed collections, and staff terms.
  • Example: UK councils had to tender services such as refuse collection after the Local Government Act 1988.
  • PPP example: the UK's Private Finance Initiative (from 1992) built hospitals and schools. In 2018 the UK government stopped signing new PFI deals, after the National Audit Office questioned their value for money. Supporters point to projects delivered on time and risk passed to private firms.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A hospital spends 2.50 million a year on its own cleaning staff. After tendering, it accepts a bid of 2.00 million a year. What is the percentage saving?
Mission 4

What regulators do

  • Where competition is weak, a regulator acts for consumers in its place.
  • Role: set price caps, quality and service standards; grant licences; give rivals fair access to networks; fine firms that break the rules.
  • UK examples: Ofwat (water), Ofgem (energy), Ofcom (communications), the Office of Rail and Road.
  • Limits on monopsony power: e.g. the Groceries Code Adjudicator (2013) protects suppliers from unfair treatment by large supermarkets.
  • Promoting competition also includes help for small businesses, e.g. simpler rules or easier access to finance.
Mission 4

When regulators fail

  • Asymmetric information: the firm knows its costs better than the regulator. It may overstate costs to win a looser price cap.
  • Regulatory capture: a regulator comes to act in the interests of the firms it regulates, not consumers.
  • Why capture happens:
    • The regulator relies on the industry for information and expert staff.
    • A revolving door: staff move between regulator and industry.
    • A few firms gain a lot and lobby hard; millions of consumers each lose a little.
  • Example: after the 2010 Deepwater Horizon oil spill, the US Minerals Management Service, criticised as too close to the industry it oversaw, was broken up (2010–11).
  • Say: "regulatory capture" — not: "the regulator was bribed". Capture can happen without any law being broken.
Quick check
?Illustrative case: a country's rail regulator hires most of its senior staff from the train companies. Over ten years it approves every fare rise the companies ask for, using cost figures that only the companies collect. Which problem does this best show?
Mission 4Evaluate

Does it work?

  • It depends on competition: privatising a firm that faces rivals differs from selling a natural monopoly.
  • It depends on regulation: information, capture and the regulator's powers.
  • It depends on time: cost savings may come fast; under-investment may show only years later.
  • It depends on objectives: efficiency and low prices, or universal service and equity?
  • Weigh the effects on prices, profit, efficiency, quality and choice.
  • Evidence on ownership is mixed: many studies find that competition and regulation matter at least as much as who owns the firm.
Mission check

?Illustrative case: a government sells its state-owned national electricity grid as a single company. There is still only one grid, and the government sets up no regulator. What is the most likely result for the price of using the grid?

Privatising a monopoly without competition or regulation swaps a public monopoly for a private one.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A union bargains collectively and acts as a single seller of labour.
  • In a competitive labour market, a wage above equilibrium usually cuts employment unless demand for labour is inelastic or shifts right.
  • Facing a monopsony, a union wage between Wm and Wc can raise both pay and employment.

Mission 2

  • A minimum wage above equilibrium creates excess supply of labour in a competitive market.
  • How many jobs it costs depends on the bite and on the elasticity of demand for labour.
  • In a monopsony, a minimum wage between Wm and Wc can raise both pay and employment.

Mission 3

  • Competition policy targets anti-competitive agreements, abuse of dominance and mergers that would substantially lessen competition.
  • UK: the CMA enforces the Competition Act 1998 and the Enterprise Act 2002; fines up to 10% of worldwide turnover. The EU has parallel rules.

Mission 4

  • Nationalisation, privatisation, regulation and deregulation answer two different questions: who owns the firm, and what rules it faces.
  • Privatisation and deregulation can cut costs and prices where rivals can enter; natural monopolies still need regulation.
  • Regulators face asymmetric information and the risk of regulatory capture.
