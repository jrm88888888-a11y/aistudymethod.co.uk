Mission 1

Why Islam met philosophy

  • From the 9th century, Greek works (Aristotle, Plato, Plotinus) were translated into Arabic in Baghdad.
  • Three groups engaged with them:
  • Falasifa (philosophers), e.g. al-Kindi, Ibn Sina (Avicenna), Ibn Rushd (Averroes): reason can demonstrate truths about God.
  • Mutakallimun (theologians of kalam): Mu'tazili and Ash'ari schools, using argument to defend revealed belief.
  • Sufis: knowledge of God comes by experience as well as argument (see Mission 2).
  • Their debates fed into Jewish and Christian thought: Aquinas read Ibn Sina and Ibn Rushd.

> "Naught is as His likeness; and He is the Hearer, the Seer." (Qur'an 42:11, Pickthall)

  • Tawhid: God is one, unique and transcendent.

Mission 1

Arguing for God: kalam and contingency

  • Al-Kindi (c. 801–c. 873): the universe cannot have an infinite past, so it began.
  • Al-Ghazali (1058–1111), al-Iqtisad fi'l-I'tiqad: whatever begins to exist has a cause; the universe began; so it has a cause (paraphrase). Revived as the kalam cosmological argument by William Lane Craig (1979).
  • Ibn Sina (980–1037): every being is contingent (could not-exist) or necessary; contingent beings need a cause; the series ends in a Necessary Existent (wajib al-wujud). Compare Aquinas' Third Way.
  • Criticisms: Hume (Dialogues, 1779): why not treat the universe itself as necessary? Russell (1948): the universe is "just there".
  • Impact: al-Ghazali argued that Ibn Sina's God, who causes an eternal world by necessity, is not the Qur'an's free Creator.
Mission 1

Evil and coherence

  • Problem of evil: if God is all-powerful and all-merciful (al-Rahman, al-Rahim), why suffering?
  • Muslim response: life is a test: "Who hath created life and death that He may try you which of you is best in conduct" (Qur'an 67:2, Pickthall). Compare Hick's soul-making theodicy.
  • Coherence problem: if God decrees all things (al-Qadr), how are humans responsible?
  • Mu'tazila: humans create their own acts; God's justice requires it.
  • Ash'ari: God creates every act; humans acquire (kasb) it and are responsible. Critics ask whether "acquisition" is coherent.
  • Shi'a thinkers stress God's justice too, and the concept of bada'.

Mission 1

Speaking of a God with no likeness

  • The Qur'an speaks of God's hand (38:75), face (55:27) and being "established on the Throne" (20:5).
  • Mu'tazila: such words are metaphors (ta'wil): the hand means power or favour.
  • Hanbali: affirm the words as revealed, without likening God to creatures.
  • Ash'ari: al-Ash'ari affirmed them bila kayfa, "without asking how"; many later Ash'aris interpreted them.
  • Dialogue with Component 1:
  • Via negativa: 42:11 "naught is as His likeness" fits saying what God is not.
  • Analogy (Aquinas): "Hearer" applies to God in a higher, related sense.
  • Symbol (Tillich): the Throne points beyond itself to God's sovereignty.
Mission 1

Soul, death and resurrection

  • Soul: "The Spirit is by command of my Lord, and of knowledge ye have been vouchsafed but little" (Qur'an 17:85, Pickthall).
  • Muslims believe that at death the soul is taken (39:42), waits in Barzakh (a barrier, 23:100), and that the dead will be raised bodily for judgement (36:78–79).
  • Ibn Sina's "floating man": a person created in mid-air, senses cut off, would still know he exists; so the self is not the body. Compare Descartes' argument.
  • Al-Ghazali, The Incoherence of the Philosophers (1095): charged the philosophers with unbelief on three points: an eternal world, God not knowing particulars, and denying bodily resurrection.
  • Identity problem: is the raised person the same person, or a copy? Compare Hick's replica theory. A hadith says one bone (the base of the spine) remains, from which a person is re-created (Bukhari; paraphrase).

Mission 1

Revelation and reason

  • Sources: Qur'an, Sunnah (in hadith), ijma' (consensus), qiyas (analogy), and 'aql (reason).
  • Mu'tazila: reason can know good and evil before revelation; the Qur'an is created. Enforced by the caliphs in the mihna (833–c. 848).
  • Ash'ari: the Qur'an is God's uncreated speech (the view of most Sunnis); good is what God commands (compare divine command theory).
  • Ibn Rushd (1126–1198), Decisive Treatise (c. 1179): the Qur'an commands reflection ("So learn a lesson, O ye who have eyes!", 59:2, Pickthall); truth does not contradict truth; where they seem to clash, scripture is interpreted.
  • Hadith depend on chains of testimony (isnad), so Hume's questions about testimony apply to them too.
  • Impact: al-Ghazali's critique and the Ash'ari mainstream limited philosophy's authority in Sunni theology; Ibn Rushd's influence was greater in Latin Europe.
Mission 1

Is talk of God meaningful, and for whom?

  • Verification principle (A. J. Ayer, Language, Truth and Logic, 1936): "God is One" cannot be checked by sense experience, so it is meaningless.
  • Falsification (Antony Flew, 1950): if nothing could count against "God is merciful", it asserts nothing.
  • Muslim responses:
  • Eschatological verification (Hick): the Day of Judgement will verify or falsify belief; this fits Muslim teaching on the Akhirah.
  • The Qur'an invites testing: "If it had been from other than Allah they would have found therein much incongruity" (Qur'an 4:82, Pickthall).
  • Language games (Wittgenstein): "Allahu akbar" in salah has its meaning within a form of life; meaningful for believers.
  • For whom?: a statement may be meaningful to a believer and opaque to an outsider. Critics ask whether that makes it true or only coherent within a game.
Mission 1

Belief that, belief in

  • H. H. Price (1899–1984), "Belief 'In' and Belief 'That'" (1965): belief that accepts a proposition; belief in is trust and commitment to a person.
  • Belief that: in the Hadith of Jibril, iman means believing in God, angels, books, messengers, the Last Day and God's decree: six claims to be accepted.
  • Belief in: tawakkul (trust in God) and love: "in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find rest!" (Qur'an 13:28, Pickthall).
  • Iman in classical definitions: assent in the heart, declaration with the tongue, and, for many, action with the limbs.
  • Relevance of philosophy: if iman is mainly belief in, arguments matter less; if it rests on belief that, failed arguments matter more.
Mission 1Evaluate

Is Muslim belief about God reasonable?

  • Claim: Muslim belief about God is reasonable: based on, or consistent with, reason.
  • For: a long tradition of rational argument (Ibn Sina, al-Ghazali, Ibn Rushd); the Qur'an invites reflection on signs (3:190–191).
  • For: kalam and contingency arguments are still defended today.
  • Against: Hume and Russell challenge the arguments; evil and al-Qadr raise coherence problems.
  • Against: many Muslims ground belief in revelation and trust, not proof; reason may be secondary.
  • Coherence: can the Mu'tazili, Ash'ari or Hanbali reading of God's attributes hold tawhid and the Qur'an's descriptions together?
  • Judgement criteria: Does "reasonable" mean proven, or consistent with reason? Is belief in more basic than belief that? For whom must belief be meaningful?

Mission 2

Revelation as religious experience

  • Wahy (revelation): Muslims believe God's word came to Muhammad through the angel Jibril, from 610 CE, over 23 years.
  • It began in the cave of Hira near Makkah; hadith describe his fear and his wife Khadijah's comfort.
  • Wahy is unique to prophets; Muhammad is "the Seal of the Prophets" (Qur'an 33:40, Pickthall), so prophetic revelation has ended.
  • Others may receive ilham (inspiration) or true dreams, but these carry no authority over the Qur'an.
  • In Component 1 terms, wahy is like an auditory and intellectual vision; but Muslims hold the words are God's, not Muhammad's.

Mission 2

Sufi experience

  • Sufis seek direct knowledge (ma'rifa) of God by dhikr (remembrance) and discipline under a shaykh.
  • Fana': the passing away of the self in God; for most Sufis, followed by baqa' (remaining in God, back in daily life).
  • Al-Ghazali, Deliverance from Error (c. 1108): after a crisis of doubt, he found certainty not in argument but in dhawq, "tasting", like the difference between knowing the definition of drunkenness and being drunk (paraphrase).
  • Dialogue with Component 1: James's ineffability and noetic quality; Stace's introvertive union; Otto's numinous awe before God's majesty.
  • Al-Hallaj (executed 922) cried "I am the Real"; many Sufis (e.g. al-Ghazali, Mishkat al-Anwar) read such words as speaking from fana', not as claiming to be God.
Mission 2

Testing religious experience

  • Swinburne's principle of credulity: if it seems to someone that God is present, probably God is, unless there are special reasons for doubt. Testimony: believe others' reports unless there is reason not to.
  • Challenges: experiences cannot be checked publicly; neuroscience links them to brain states; different religions report conflicting experiences.
  • Muslim tests: an experience is judged by its fit with Qur'an and Sunnah; the Sufi tradition prized sober Sufism and suspected ecstatic claims.
  • For the Qur'an itself: Muslims point not to Muhammad's inner experience but to the text, open to anyone to examine.
  • Impact: some Muslims (e.g. many Salafis) reject much Sufi practice as innovation; others see Sufism as Islam's inner heart.

Mission 2

Miracles in Islam

  • Mu'jiza: a miracle given to a prophet to confirm his message; the Qur'an usually calls such events ayat (signs).
  • The Qur'an as the chief miracle (i'jaz, inimitability): "produce a surah of the like thereof" (Qur'an 2:23, Pickthall).
  • Other accounts: the moon "rent in twain" (54:1); the Night Journey (17:1). Most Muslims hold it was bodily; some early reports describe it as a vision.
  • Karamat: wonders of saints; accepted by most Sunnis (Ash'ari), rejected by the Mu'tazila; many Salafis are cautious.
  • Modern readings: some Muslims (e.g. reformers influenced by science) read some accounts symbolically; most hold that God can and does act.
Mission 2

Al-Ghazali, Hume and Wiles on causes

  • Al-Ghazali, Incoherence, 17th discussion: when fire touches cotton, we see burning with the contact, not through it; God creates the burning. Nature's regularity is God's habit ('ada), not necessity (paraphrase). So miracles are possible.
  • Hume: we see only constant conjunction; our expectation is custom or habit. But he concludes that uniform experience makes reports of miracles less credible than their falsehood.
  • Same analysis of causation; opposite conclusions.
  • Ibn Rushd replied that denying natural causes destroys knowledge of nature.
  • Maurice Wiles (1923–2005), God's Action in the World (1986): God acts through creation as a whole, not by particular interventions; otherwise God would be arbitrary (curing some, not others).
  • Muslim response to Wiles: God's acts are wise even when humans cannot see why; God's sovereignty means every event is God's act.
Mission 2Read the source

David Hume, An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding (1748), Section X, Part II:

> "in matters of religion, whatever is different is contrary"

  • Hume argues that the miracles of each religion are meant to prove that religion, so they count against every other; he names the miracles "of Mahomet" in the same passage.

Mission 2

Scientific and religious discourses

  • Early Islam: study of nature as a religious duty: reflect on the "signs" of creation (3:190). Scholars advanced astronomy, optics and medicine.
  • Concordism: "the heavens and the earth were of one piece, then We parted them" (Qur'an 21:30, Pickthall) is read by some as the Big Bang. Maurice Bucaille (1920–1998), The Bible, the Qur'an and Science (1976), argued that the Qur'an anticipated modern science.
  • Critics (Muslim and non-Muslim): reading science into scripture makes faith hostage to changing theories.
  • Evolution: responses range from rejection, to accepting evolution for all life except Adam, to full acceptance with God working through it.
  • Two discourses? Science asks how, testing by experiment; the Qur'an speaks of why, as signs pointing to God. Some Muslims accept this division; others say both describe one reality.
Mission 2

Truth claims of other religions

  • Exclusivism: "whoso seeketh as religion other than the Surrender (to Allah) it will not be accepted from him" (Qur'an 3:85, Pickthall).
  • Inclusivism: Jews, Christians and Sabaeans who believe in God and the Last Day and do right have their reward (2:62); "For each We have appointed a divine law and a traced-out way" (Qur'an 5:48, Pickthall).
  • Conflicting claims: the Qur'an says of Jesus, "they slew him not nor crucified him" (Qur'an 4:157, Pickthall), a direct contradiction of Christian belief.
  • John Hick (1922–2012), An Interpretation of Religion (1989): all great religions are responses to one Real, beyond every tradition's description.
  • Muslim responses: some welcome pluralism (e.g. perennialists such as Frithjof Schuon); many reject it, because tawhid and the finality of the Qur'an are truth claims, not just perspectives.
Mission 2Evaluate

Can Muslims treat other religions as true?

  • Claim: Islam's truth claims are coherent with accepting other religions as true.
  • For: the Qur'an affirms earlier prophets and scriptures (2:136) and gives each people "a traced-out way" (5:48).
  • For: 2:62 promises reward to some outside Islam; "no compulsion in religion" (2:256).
  • Against: 3:85 and the finality of Muhammad imply that later revelation corrects earlier ones.
  • Against: factual claims clash (4:157 on the crucifixion; Trinity vs tawhid); Hume: "whatever is different is contrary".
  • Against Hick: an indescribable Real seems to empty tawhid of content.
  • Judgement criteria: Does "true" mean saving, or factually correct? Can respect for others require agreeing with them? Is coherence within Islam more important than agreement with Hick?

Mission 3

The question behind this dialogue

  • Component 1 gave you three ways to make moral decisions:
  • Deontological (duty and rules; Kant): some acts are right or wrong in themselves.
  • Teleological / consequential (ends and results; Bentham): right acts produce the best outcomes.
  • Character-based (virtue ethics; Aristotle): ask what a good person would do and become.
  • This dialogue asks two things: how do Muslims respond to each theory, and how far does Muslim ethics itself fit each box?
  • Influence runs both ways: Muslim thinkers used Greek ethics; Muslim ethics challenges modern theories.
Mission 3

Key Muslim moral principles

  • Shari'ah ("the path"): God's guidance for all of life. Main sources: Qur'an, Sunnah (the Prophet's example, known through hadiths), ijma' (scholarly consensus), qiyas (analogy). Shi'a jurists add the teaching of the Imams and give reason ('aql) a named place.
  • Fiqh: the human, fallible work of understanding the Shari'ah. Schools differ.
  • The five-fold classification of actions:
  • Fard / wajib (obligatory) · mustahabb / mandub (recommended) · mubah (permitted) · makruh (disliked) · haram (forbidden).
  • Most of life is mubah: personal freedom and judgement work inside the limits.
  • Niyyah (intention) matters: "actions are judged by intentions" (hadith, Bukhari 1, paraphrase).

Mission 3

Command or reason? A Muslim Euthyphro debate

  • Is an act good because God commands it, or does God command it because it is good? (Divine Command Theory, Component 1.)
  • Mu'tazila (8th–10th centuries): reason can know that justice is good and oppression evil, even before revelation. God commands the good because God is just.
  • Ash'ari school (al-Ash'ari, 874–936): good is what God commands; human reason cannot set a standard over God. Revelation is needed to know what is obligatory.
  • Maturidi school: a middle way: reason can recognise good and evil; on most accounts, obligation comes through revelation.
  • Most Sunni theology followed the Ash'ari and Maturidi schools; Shi'a (Twelver) theology kept a Mu'tazili-style place for reason.
Mission 3

Responding to Kant

  • Immanuel Kant (1724–1804), Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785).
  • Common ground: duty for its own sake; the good will and right intention (compare niyyah); some acts (lying, murder) wrong in themselves; universal rules for all people; human dignity ("We have honoured the Children of Adam", Qur'an 17:70).
  • Tension: Kant demands autonomy: reason gives itself the moral law. A law received from outside the will is heteronomy, which Kant says yields only hypothetical imperatives. Obeying God for reward in the Akhirah looks heteronomous.
  • Muslim replies: submission (islam) is the free choice of a rational person; Mu'tazili thinkers held that reason and revelation agree; Ash'ari thinkers reply that human reason is not the final judge of God.
  • Read the source: Kant wrote that if the will "goes out of itself" to find its law, "there always results heteronomy" (Groundwork, Abbott translation, 1895).
Mission 3

Responding to Bentham

  • Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832), An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789): right acts produce the greatest happiness; pleasure and pain measured by the hedonic calculus.
  • Common ground: maqasid al-shari'ah (the purposes of the law). Al-Ghazali (1058–1111) said the law protects five necessities: religion, life, intellect, lineage, property. Maslahah (public benefit) guides new rulings; al-Shatibi (d. 1388) developed the theory.
  • Necessity (darura) can lift a prohibition: one driven by necessity may eat forbidden food (Qur'an 2:173).
  • Tension: the goals are set by God, not by pleasure; reward and punishment in the Akhirah count, which no calculus can measure; some acts stay haram even if they would please the majority.
  • Many scholars: maqasid explain the law's wisdom; they do not replace its texts.
Mission 3

Responding to virtue ethics

  • Character (akhlaq) is central. The Qur'an praises the Prophet: "And lo! thou art of a tremendous nature." (Qur'an 68:4, Pickthall)
  • A widely cited hadith: the Prophet was sent to perfect good character (Muwatta of Malik, paraphrase).
  • Ihsan (excellence): worshipping God as if you see Him (hadith of Jibril, Muslim 8, paraphrase). Goodness as a way of being, not only rule-keeping.
  • The mean: the Muslims are "a middle nation" (Qur'an 2:143, Pickthall). Miskawayh (c. 932–1030), Tahdhib al-Akhlaq (The Refinement of Character), took Aristotle's virtues as means between extremes and his idea of habituation. Al-Ghazali, Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din, taught that character can be trained by practice.
  • Tension: for Aristotle the goal is eudaimonia in this life; for Muslims the goal is God's pleasure and the Akhirah, and the virtues are defined by revelation, not by human reason alone.

Mission 3

Test case 1: theft

  • "As for the thief, both male and female, cut off their hands." (Qur'an 5:38, Pickthall). A hadd (fixed) punishment: looks strictly deontological.
  • But classical jurists set strict conditions: a minimum value (nisab); taken from a secure place; no shubha (doubt), including need. Most cases fall to lesser, discretionary punishment (ta'zir) instead.
  • Caliph 'Umar is reported to have suspended the hadd during the famine of the "Year of Ashes" (c. 639 CE): hunger created doubt. This reads as attention to ends and circumstances.
  • Property is one of the five necessities: theft harms the victim and society (teleological reason too).
  • Today: many Muslim-majority states do not apply the hadd; some do. In Britain Muslims follow UK law.
Mission 3

Test case 2: lying

  • Truthfulness is a core virtue; lying is generally haram. Hadith: truthfulness leads to righteousness, and lying to wickedness (Bukhari 6094, paraphrase).
  • Kant: you may not lie even to a murderer at the door ("On a Supposed Right to Lie from Philanthropy", 1797).
  • Hadith exceptions: "He is not a liar who reconciles between people" (Bukhari 2692, paraphrase); a version in Muslim (2605) names three cases: war, making peace between people, and words between husband and wife.
  • Saving an innocent life (hifz al-nafs) is widely held to permit concealment or untruth.
  • So lying is not judged by consequences alone: the exceptions are limited and textually grounded, not a general "lie when it helps".
Mission 3Diversity

Different ways Muslims reason

  • Textualist / traditional: start from Qur'an and Sunnah; follow the established schools (taqlid); rules first, purposes as their wisdom.
  • Maqasid-based / reformist: read the texts in the light of their purposes; renewed ijtihad (independent reasoning) on new issues. E.g. Tariq Ramadan (b. 1962), Radical Reform (2009).
  • Sufi: purify the nafs (self) and cultivate inner virtues: love, humility, sincerity. Ethics as transformation of character.
  • Shi'a: living mujtahids (qualified jurists) issue rulings; reason is one of the four sources of law.
  • All agree that God is the source of morality; they differ on how reason, purposes and character relate to the texts.
Mission 3Evaluate

"Muslim ethics is essentially deontological."

  • For: God's commands are absolute; the five-fold classification is a scheme of duties; hadd penalties are fixed; obedience to God and the Prophet is a duty (Qur'an 33:36); Ash'ari theology grounds good in command.
  • Against (teleological): maqasid and maslahah; darura lifts prohibitions; 'Umar's famine ruling; the reward and punishment of the Akhirah are consequences.
  • Against (character): akhlaq, ihsan, the Sufi path, niyyah: the inner state matters, not just the act.
  • Another view: Muslim ethics is a hybrid: a revealed framework (deontological) whose purposes (teleological) are lived through good character (virtue). The Western boxes may not fit a theocentric ethic at all.
  • Judge it by: Which comes first when they clash: text, purpose or character? Does reward in the Akhirah make obedience consequentialist? Do Western categories distort the tradition?
Mission 3Worked answer

Building the dialogue

Question: "Muslim ethics cannot be reconciled with Kant." Evaluate this view.

  • Point: Both treat some acts as wrong in themselves: lying is generally haram, and Kant forbids lying even to a murderer.
  • Development: But the hadith exceptions (reconciliation, war, spouses) show Muslim ethics allows limited untruth; Kant does not.
  • Influence both ways: Mu'tazili rationalism anticipates Kant's claim that reason knows the good; Kant's autonomy challenges divine command ethics; Ash'ari thinkers reply that reason is not the final judge.
  • Judgement: decide how far, and say which Muslim school your judgement depends on.
  • 25-mark dialogue tip: show influence in both directions, and name the school or scholar, not "Muslims" in general.

Mission 4

Three principles behind every answer

  • Sanctity of life: life is God's gift and trust (amanah). "Slay not the life which Allah hath made sacred, save in the course of justice" (Qur'an 6:151, Pickthall).
  • "Whoso saveth the life of one, it shall be as if he had saved the life of all mankind" (Qur'an 5:32, Pickthall). Saving life is a duty, not only a permission.
  • Khalifah (steward): humans are God's trustees on earth (Qur'an 2:30), answerable for creatures and creation.
  • Darura (necessity): "he who is driven by necessity, neither craving nor transgressing, it is no sin for him" (Qur'an 2:173, Pickthall). Jurists' maxim: necessity makes the forbidden permissible, but only as far as needed.
  • These three pull in different directions; much Muslim bioethics is about balancing them.
Mission 4

When does a person begin?

  • A hadith (Ibn Mas'ud; Bukhari 3208, Muslim 2643, paraphrase) describes the embryo developing in stages of forty days, after which an angel breathes in the soul.
  • Most jurists read this as ensoulment at 120 days; some read it as 40 days.
  • Before ensoulment the embryo is potential life: protected and respected, but not yet a full person.
  • After ensoulment it has the full rights of a person.
  • This gives a developmental view of the embryo's status, unlike Catholic teaching that full protection begins at conception.
Mission 4

Embryos, IVF, cloning, "designer" babies

  • IVF: widely permitted within marriage, using the husband's sperm and the wife's eggs. Most Sunni jurists forbid donor sperm or eggs (they confuse lineage, nasab). Some Shi'a authorities have permitted donor eggs.
  • Embryo research: many Sunni scholars allow research on surplus IVF embryos before ensoulment, for serious medical benefit; others refuse any destruction of embryos.
  • Reproductive cloning: forbidden by the International Islamic Fiqh Academy (1997): it breaks the marriage bond and lineage. Therapeutic research and cloning of animals and plants for real benefit: permitted with safeguards.
  • "Designer" babies: selecting embryos to avoid serious disease: widely allowed. Choosing traits for preference: most scholars forbid, as "changing Allah's creation". Sex selection for non-medical reasons: many forbid; some allow it in limited cases.
Mission 4Diversity

Abortion

  • All schools: after ensoulment, abortion is haram. Most modern scholars allow one exception: to save the mother's life (her life is established; the fetus's is dependent).
  • Before 120 days, views differ:
  • Hanafi: many permit for a valid reason (e.g. the mother's health), though it is makruh.
  • Shafi'i and Hanbali: many permit only in the first 40 days, or for serious reasons.
  • Maliki: most forbid from conception, as the embryo is already developing life.
  • The Islamic Fiqh Council of the Muslim World League (1990) permitted abortion before 120 days for a confirmed severe abnormality.
  • Never for fear of poverty: "Slay not your children, fearing a fall to poverty, We shall provide for them and for you." (Qur'an 17:31, Pickthall)

Mission 4

Euthanasia and assisted suicide

  • Life belongs to God; only God sets its term. "Kill not one another" (Qur'an 4:29, Pickthall), read by many as forbidding suicide too.
  • Voluntary euthanasia and assisted suicide: haram in all major schools. Suffering may have meaning: patience (sabr) is rewarded.
  • But: doctors need not prolong a dying life by futile treatment. Withholding or withdrawing treatment that cannot help is widely permitted (Islamic Code of Medical Ethics, Kuwait, 1981, paraphrase). Pain relief that may shorten life is accepted if the aim is relief.
  • This is close to double effect (Aquinas, Component 1): the intention (niyyah) decides.
  • Challenge (Bentham): if suffering outweighs pleasure, ending it maximises utility. Challenge (Kant): Kant also forbids suicide (it uses one's own person as a mere means), so here Kant supports the Muslim view.
Mission 4

Capital punishment

  • The Qur'an permits the death penalty for murder (qisas, retaliation) and some hadd crimes: "And there is life for you in retaliation" (Qur'an 2:179, Pickthall).
  • But mercy is preferred: the victim's family may forgive and accept compensation (diyah) instead (Qur'an 2:178).
  • Sheikh Ahmad Ash-Sharabasi (Al-Azhar scholar, 20th century): capital punishment is God's law, protects society and deters.
  • Tariq Ramadan (b. 1962): called in 2005 for an international moratorium on capital and corporal punishment and stoning in Muslim-majority countries, while scholars debate the conditions, which are rarely met fairly today.
  • Dialogue: Kant supports death for murder (retributive justice); Bentham's calculus allows it only if deterrence outweighs harm (Bentham himself came to oppose it). Both arguments appear in Muslim debate.

Mission 4

Animals: trust, not ownership

  • "There is not an animal in the earth, nor a flying creature flying on two wings, but they are peoples like unto you." (Qur'an 6:38, Pickthall)
  • Hadiths (paraphrase): a woman was punished for starving a cat (Bukhari 3318); a man was forgiven for giving water to a thirsty dog (Bukhari 2363).
  • Food: eating meat is permitted; slaughter (dhabihah) with God's name, a sharp knife, quickly. Debate today about pre-stunning: some accept reversible stunning; others refuse.
  • Intensive farming: many Muslim scholars and groups argue that cruel confinement breaks the duty of mercy, even if the meat is halal (some call this "tayyib": wholesome as well as lawful).
  • Blood sports: hunting for food is permitted (Qur'an 5:4); hunting purely for sport and making animals fight are forbidden by most scholars. The Prophet forbade using a living creature as a target (Muslim 1957, paraphrase).
Mission 4

Animals in science and transplants

  • Scientific procedures: permitted by most scholars for real human benefit (medicine), with minimal suffering and no alternative. Cosmetic testing: widely rejected.
  • Cloning animals: permitted for real benefit (Fiqh Academy, 1997), with welfare safeguards.
  • Animal organs for transplant (xenotransplantation): pig organs and valves raise the question of swine. Many scholars permit them under darura when no alternative exists, since the aim is to save life (Qur'an 5:32; 2:173). Others prefer non-pig sources where possible.
  • Dialogue: Bentham (and Peter Singer after him) counts animal suffering ("can they suffer?"); the Muslim view agrees suffering matters but keeps a hierarchy: humans as khalifah may use animals with mercy. Kant gives animals only indirect duties; Islam gives animals rights of their own (Prophetic hadiths).
Mission 4

Genetic engineering

  • Concern: "they will change Allah's creation" (Qur'an 4:119, Pickthall) are Satan's words: deliberate alteration of creation for vanity is condemned.
  • Gene therapy to cure disease: widely permitted, as healing is commanded ("God has not sent down a disease without sending down a cure", hadith, Bukhari 5678, paraphrase).
  • Germline editing (changes passed to children) and enhancement: most scholars cautious or opposed: unknown harms, lineage, pride.
  • GM crops: permitted by many if safe; questioned by others on stewardship grounds.
  • The line most scholars draw: restoring health is healing; re-designing humans for preference is changing creation.

Mission 4Evaluate

"Muslim views on life and death are more deontological than teleological."

  • For: absolute bans on euthanasia, suicide, donor gametes and reproductive cloning; qisas and hadd rest on revealed texts; Kant agrees with Islam on suicide.
  • Against: ensoulment gives a developmental view with graded rules; darura and maslahah permit xenotransplants and embryo research; withdrawal of futile treatment turns on intention and outcome; Ramadan's moratorium looks to justice in practice.
  • Character: mercy (rahmah), patience (sabr) and stewardship shape how rules are lived.
  • Judge it by: Do the exceptions (darura) come from the rules themselves or from outcomes? Are there real differences between schools, or only in detail? Does saving life (5:32) work as a duty or as a goal to maximise?

Mission 5

Marriage: a contract and a sign

  • "He created for you helpmeets from yourselves that ye might find rest in them, and He ordained between you love and mercy." (Qur'an 30:21, Pickthall)
  • Nikah is a contract: both partners must consent (a woman may not be married without her permission: hadith, Bukhari 5136, paraphrase); the husband gives mahr (a gift that belongs to the wife); witnesses.
  • Marriage is Sunnah and strongly encouraged; celibacy as a religious ideal is not.
  • Divorce is permitted (talaq by the husband; khul' at the wife's request), after attempts at reconciliation.
  • Polygyny: up to four wives only if treated with justice (4:3); "Ye will not be able to deal equally between (your) wives" (Qur'an 4:129, Pickthall). Many modern Muslims read these together as favouring monogamy; others see a limited permission. UK law recognises only one marriage.
Mission 5

Dialogue on marriage

  • Kant: marriage as a lifelong contract of equals that protects each from being used as a mere means: a parallel with nikah as a contract with rights on both sides.
  • J. S. Mill (1806–1873), The Subjection of Women (1869): unequal marriage law harms happiness and wastes women's talents. Challenges unequal rights in classical fiqh (divorce, inheritance, guardianship).
  • Muslim responses: some defend complementary roles as just and fitting; Muslim feminists (e.g. Amina Wadud, b. 1952, Qur'an and Woman, 1992) argue the Qur'an's principle of justice requires equality, and that classical rulings reflected their times.
  • Virtue: the Qur'an grounds marriage in tranquillity, love and mercy: character, not only contract.
Mission 5

Same-sex relationships

  • Traditional view (all four Sunni schools, Shi'a jurists): sexual acts belong only within marriage between a man and a woman; same-sex acts are haram. Main source: the story of Lut: "Lo! ye come with lust unto men instead of women." (Qur'an 7:81, Pickthall)
  • Many contemporary scholars distinguish orientation (not chosen, not sinful in itself) from acts (forbidden); they urge compassion and reject violence and abuse.
  • Progressive minority: some Muslim scholars and groups (e.g. Scott Siraj al-Haqq Kugle, Homosexuality in Islam, 2010) argue that the Lut story condemns rape and inhospitality, not loving relationships. Mainstream scholars reject this reading.
  • Dialogue: Bentham and Mill (harm principle): private acts between consenting adults that harm no one are not wrong. Muslim reply: morality comes from God, and harm is not the only measure.
  • In Britain same-sex marriage is legal (2013 Act); religious bodies are not compelled to conduct it.
Mission 5

Transgender matters

  • Classical fiqh discussed the khuntha (person of ambiguous sex, intersex): surgery to clarify their sex was permitted.
  • Egypt's Grand Mufti (Sheikh Sayyid Tantawi, 1988): permitted surgery where a medical condition existed.
  • Ayatollah Khomeini (Iran, 1980s): a fatwa permitted gender reassignment; Iran's state now recognises legal change of sex after surgery.
  • Most Sunni scholars: reassignment without a physical condition is forbidden, as "changing Allah's creation" (4:119); a hadith condemns men who imitate women and women who imitate men (Bukhari 5885, paraphrase).
  • Some Muslims today argue for acceptance on grounds of mercy and the reality of gender dysphoria; this is a minority view.

Mission 5

Wealth: a trust from God

  • Wealth belongs to God; humans are trustees: "spend of that whereof He hath made you trustees" (Qur'an 57:7, Pickthall).
  • Zakah (fard): about 2.5% of savings above the nisab each year, for those named in 9:60 (the poor, the needy, debtors, travellers…). Sadaqah: voluntary charity.
  • Riba (interest, usury) is forbidden: "Allah permitteth trading and forbiddeth usury" (Qur'an 2:275, Pickthall). Islamic banking uses profit-sharing and leasing instead.
  • Wealth is not evil: trade is praised; the Prophet's wife Khadijah was a merchant. Hoarding and extravagance are condemned (9:34; 7:31).
  • Dialogue: a Benthamite case for redistribution (wealth gives more happiness to the poor) fits zakah's aims; but zakah is a duty to God, fixed in amount, not a calculation.
Mission 5

Tolerance

  • "There is no compulsion in religion." (Qur'an 2:256, Pickthall) · "Unto you your religion, and unto me my religion." (Qur'an 109:6, Pickthall)
  • "We have... made you nations and tribes that ye may know one another." (Qur'an 49:13, Pickthall): diversity as God's plan.
  • History: the Constitution of Medina (c. 622) included Jewish tribes in one community; under Muslim rule, Jews and Christians (dhimmi) kept their worship and law, paying the jizyah. Defenders: tolerant for its time; critics: protected but unequal.
  • Exclusivist (3:85) and inclusivist (2:62) readings shape attitudes to other faiths.
  • Dialogue: Mill's On Liberty (1859): tolerance as a condition of truth and progress. Many Muslims agree on tolerance but ground it in God's will, not in individual autonomy.
Mission 5

Freedom of religious expression

  • Muslims' own freedom: in Britain, the Human Rights Act 1998 (Article 9 of the European Convention) protects belief and its expression: mosques, hijab, halal food, prayer at work. Some European states restrict face veils or headscarves in some settings.
  • Apostasy (riddah): classical law prescribed death for a man who left Islam. Many modern scholars reject this: 2:256 and 18:29 ("whosoever will, let him believe, and whosoever will, let him disbelieve", Pickthall); the classical penalty is read as for treason in war. Others keep the classical view.
  • Blasphemy and offence: the Rushdie affair (The Satanic Verses, 1988; Khomeini's fatwa, 1989); the Danish cartoons (2005). Most British Muslim bodies called for lawful protest only.
  • Dialogue: Mill's harm principle protects offensive speech; many Muslims argue that deliberate insult to the Prophet harms communities and is not real freedom.
Mission 5

Free will and al-Qadr

  • Al-Qadr: God's decree. God knows and wills all things; yet humans are judged for their deeds: "Every soul is a pledge for its own deeds" (Qur'an 74:38, Pickthall).
  • Mu'tazila: humans create their own acts; God's justice requires real freedom, or judgement would be unfair. Close to libertarianism.
  • Ash'ari: God creates every act; humans acquire it (kasb) by choosing. Freedom and responsibility are real, but God's power is total. Often compared to compatibilism.
  • Shi'a: "neither compulsion nor total delegation, but a matter between the two" (saying of Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq, paraphrase); bada': God may change a decree in response to human action.
  • Conditions of responsibility: a hadith says "the pen is lifted" from the sleeper until he wakes, the child until maturity and the insane until sane (Abu Dawud 4403, paraphrase): no free, informed choice, no blame.
  • Hard determinism challenges all three: if God (or causes) fixes every act, reward and punishment look unjust.

Mission 5

Conscience

  • Fitrah: humans are created with a natural inclination to the good and to God (Qur'an 30:30).
  • The soul is "inspired... (with conscience of) what is wrong for it and (what is) right for it" (Qur'an 91:8, Pickthall).
  • The nafs in three states: the soul that urges evil (12:53); "the accusing soul" (Qur'an 75:2, Pickthall), close to a guilty conscience; the soul at peace (89:27).
  • Hadiths (paraphrase): "consult your heart": righteousness is what the soul is at ease with (Wabisah, Ahmad); sin is what wavers in the soul and you dislike people to know (Muslim 2553).
  • Value and limits: the heart can be clouded by desire, so conscience must be informed by Qur'an and Sunnah; it cannot overrule clear texts.
  • Dialogue: Aquinas: synderesis (inborn grasp of first principles) is close to fitrah; Butler (1692–1752): conscience as a God-given "principle of reflection" with authority; Freud: conscience as the internalised super-ego, a challenge to all religious accounts.

Mission 5Evaluate

"Muslim ethics leaves little room for individual freedom."

  • For: obedience to God and the Prophet (33:36); fixed rulings on sexuality and riba; classical apostasy law; conscience must yield to clear texts.
  • Against: most acts are mubah (permitted, neither required nor forbidden); "no compulsion in religion" (2:256); responsibility presupposes free choice (74:38; the pen hadith); ijtihad and consultation (shura, 42:38); modern scholars defend religious freedom.
  • Other perspectives: Mill and Kant locate freedom in individual autonomy; many Muslims see true freedom as freedom from the lower self (nafs) through submission to God.
  • Judge it by: What kind of freedom is meant: political, moral or spiritual? Are restrictions in the texts, or in later fiqh? Does diversity among Muslims weaken the claim?
Mission check

For Muslims, freedom, responsibility and conscience all rest on the fitrah God gave, and are tested by revelation. Freedom here means freedom to do the good.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Al-Kindi and al-Ghazali (kalam) and Ibn Sina (contingency) argued for God; Hume and Russell critique such arguments; evil is met with life as a test, and al-Qadr raises coherence questions (Mu'tazila vs Ash'ari).
  • God "has no likeness" (42:11): Mu'tazili metaphor, Ash'ari bila kayfa, Hanbali affirmation; compare via negativa, analogy and symbol. Soul, Barzakh and bodily resurrection: al-Ghazali vs Ibn Sina; the identity problem.
  • Revelation and reason (Mu'tazila, Ash'ari, Ibn Rushd); meaningfulness (verification, falsification, eschatological verification, language games); iman as belief that and belief in.

Mission 2

  • Wahy is unique and ended with the Seal of the Prophets (33:40); Sufi experience (fana', al-Ghazali's dhawq) parallels James, Stace and Otto, and is tested against Qur'an and Sunnah.
  • Miracles: mu'jiza, karamat and the Qur'an's i'jaz (2:23); al-Ghazali and Hume share a view of causation but reach opposite conclusions; Wiles' critique and Muslim replies.
  • Science: early scientific duty, concordism (Bucaille) and its critics, evolution; other religions: exclusivism (3:85), inclusivism (2:62, 5:48), conflict (4:157), Hick's pluralism and Muslim responses.

Mission 3

  • Muslim ethics rests on the Shari'ah (Qur'an, Sunnah, consensus, analogy; reason in Shi'a law) and classifies acts five ways: fard, mustahabb, mubah, makruh, haram.
  • Kant: shared stress on duty, intention and dignity, but tension over autonomy; Bentham: echoes in maqasid and maslahah, but God sets the goals; virtue ethics: akhlaq, ihsan and Miskawayh's use of Aristotle's mean.
  • Theft and lying show a hybrid: fixed rules, with conditions and exceptions that look to purposes.

Mission 4

  • Sanctity of life (6:151, 5:32), stewardship (khalifah) and necessity (darura) frame Muslim answers on life and death.
  • Ensoulment (120 days for most jurists) gives graded views on embryos and abortion; euthanasia is forbidden, but futile treatment may be withdrawn; capital punishment is permitted, with forgiveness preferred and its use today debated.
  • Animals have claims as "peoples like unto you"; use for food and medicine is allowed with mercy; healing genetics is permitted, re-designing humans is not.

Mission 5

  • Marriage is a contract and a sign of love and mercy; traditional teaching forbids same-sex acts, with a progressive minority dissenting; Muslim views on transgender matters range from Iran's acceptance of reassignment to Sunni prohibition.
  • Wealth is a trust (zakah, no riba); tolerance rests on "no compulsion in religion", with live debates on apostasy and offence.
  • Al-Qadr is held with human responsibility (Mu'tazili, Ash'ari kasb, Shi'a bada'); conscience (fitrah, the accusing soul) guides, informed by revelation.
