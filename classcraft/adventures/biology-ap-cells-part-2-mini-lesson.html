Mission 1

Mission 1: A cell's outer membrane is just two molecules thick. Why do its molecules form that sheet by themselves?

Make a guess: A cell's outer membrane is just two molecules thick. Why do its molecules form that sheet by themselves?

Mission 1

A molecule with two ends

  • A phospholipid has a head and two tails.
  • Head: a charged phosphate group. It is polar: hydrophilic ("water-loving").
  • Tails: two fatty acid chains. They are non-polar: hydrophobic.
  • Polar phosphate region: hydrophilic. Nonpolar fatty acid region: hydrophobic.
Mission 1

The bilayer builds itself

  • In water, heads face the water. Tails are kept away from it.
  • A cell has water on both sides: cytoplasm inside, tissue fluid or surroundings outside.
  • So phospholipids form two layers. Heads form both surfaces; tails meet in the middle.
  • The sheet forms spontaneously: no enzyme or ATP is needed to arrange it. (Enzymes do make the phospholipids.)
  • No covalent bonds join the phospholipids. Each stays a separate molecule.
  • Say: "water molecules bond to each other and exclude the tails" — not: "the tails repel water".
Quick check
?In a plasma membrane, the phosphate heads of the phospholipids face the cytosol and the extracellular fluid, and the fatty acid tails face each other in the interior of the membrane. Which statement best explains this arrangement?
Mission 1

The middle is a barrier

  • The core of the bilayer is the hydrophobic tails.
  • Small non-polar molecules pass between the phospholipids: O2, CO2.
  • Also N2.
  • Ions and polar molecules (such as glucose) barely enter the core. Charge matters more than size.
  • Water is polar but very small: it crosses slowly.
  • The hydrophobic interior makes the membrane selectively permeable. It separates the cell's internal environment from the external one.
  • How substances cross: Mission 3.
Mission 1

Proteins: the mosaic

  • Proteins are scattered irregularly among the phospholipids, like the pieces of a mosaic.
  • Integral proteins are embedded in one or both layers. Some span the whole membrane.
  • Peripheral proteins sit on one surface.
  • Roles vary: channels and carriers (transport), enzymes, receptors, cell recognition.
  • Say: "integral: embedded in the bilayer" — not: "integral: always spans it".
Mission 1

Sugar chains on the outside

  • Glycoprotein: a protein with a carbohydrate chain attached.
  • Glycolipid: a lipid with a carbohydrate chain attached.
  • The carbohydrate is only on the outer (extracellular) face.
  • It works in cell recognition and cell adhesion.
Mission 1

Cholesterol, and why "fluid"

  • Cholesterol, a steroid, sits between the phospholipids in vertebrate animal cells.
  • Fluid: phospholipids and proteins move sideways within the layer. The sheet stays continuous.
  • Say: "fluid: parts move sideways within the membrane" — not: "fluid: it is leaky, with gaps".
Mission 1

Draw it

A two-dimensional drawing needs:

  • Two rows of phospholipids, heads out, tails in.
  • An integral protein spanning the bilayer; a peripheral protein on one surface.
  • A glycoprotein, carbohydrate chain on the outside.
  • A glycolipid, carbohydrate chain on the outside.
  • Cholesterol among the tails, not on the surface.
  • Labels: hydrophilic (both surfaces), hydrophobic (the core).
  • Proteins scattered unevenly, never in a regular row.
Quick check
?According to the fluid mosaic model, which statement correctly describes the plasma membrane of a vertebrate animal cell?
Mission check

?Liposomes are tiny hollow spheres made by shaking phospholipids in water; some are used to carry medicines in the blood. Each one is a single bilayer enclosing a droplet of water. A water-soluble drug is dissolved in the water before the liposomes form. Where will the drug be held, and why?

Like a cell membrane, a liposome assembles itself in water, and its hydrophobic core keeps water-soluble substances on one side.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How can you show that the molecules in a membrane never stop moving?

Make a guess: How can you show that the molecules in a membrane never stop moving?

Mission 2

The cell-fusion test

  • Label the surface proteins of a mouse cell with one fluorescent dye, and a human cell's with another.
  • Fuse the two cells into one.
  • At first, each color covers its own half.
  • At 37 °C, within about 40 minutes the colors are mixed all over.
  • The proteins moved sideways through the bilayer. The membrane is fluid.
  • This was Frye and Edidin's experiment (1970).
  • When they cooled the cells to about 15 °C or below, the colors did not mix within 40 minutes: in a cold membrane, molecules move far more slowly.
Mission 2Read the data

Temperature and mixing

  • At 15 °C, hardly any cells mix in 40 minutes: molecules move far more slowly in a colder membrane.
Quick check
?In an investigation, the proteins on the surface of one type of cell were labeled with a green fluorescent dye, and those of a second type of cell with a red dye. Pairs of cells were then fused to form single cells, and the fused cells were kept at 37 °C or at 15 °C. The table shows the percentage of fused cells in which the green and red labels were fully mixed over the whole cell surface. Which statement is supported by the data?
Mission 2

How a protein sits in a membrane

  • A protein's surface depends on its amino acid R groups.
  • AP calls these side groups.
  • Part touching the fatty acid tails: non-polar (hydrophobic) R groups.
  • Parts in the cytoplasm or the extracellular fluid: polar or charged (hydrophilic) R groups.
  • A channel through the protein is lined with hydrophilic R groups.
Quick check
?In a hypothetical cell, a membrane protein crosses the plasma membrane once. The segment that crosses the membrane is made mainly of amino acids with nonpolar side groups. A mutation replaces several of these amino acids with amino acids that have charged side groups of similar size. Which prediction about the mutant protein is best supported?
Mission check

?A student claims that proteins in the plasma membrane can move sideways within the membrane. Which observation would best support this claim?

Mixing after fusion and a bleached patch filling in point the same way: membrane proteins drift sideways through a fluid bilayer.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Oxygen slips straight through a cell membrane, yet a smaller sodium ion is stopped. Why?

Make a guess: Oxygen slips straight through a cell membrane, yet a smaller sodium ion is stopped. Why?

Mission 3

Diffusion

  • Particles move randomly, all the time.
  • More particles cross from the crowded side than from the other: net movement from higher to lower concentration.
  • This is movement down a concentration gradient.
  • It is passive: no energy from ATP.
  • AP calls it passive transport: no direct input of metabolic energy.
  • At equal concentrations, particles still cross both ways. Net movement is zero.
  • Say: "net movement stops" — not: "the molecules stop moving".
Mission 3

Straight through the bilayer

  • Simple diffusion: particles pass between the phospholipids.
  • Only small non-polar molecules do this readily: O2, CO2.
  • Also N2. Small polar, uncharged molecules (H2O, NH3) cross in small amounts.
  • Permeability of the bilayer depends only on size and polarity or charge. It is not selective in any other way.

  • Na⁺ is smaller than O2, yet the slowest. Its charge, and the shell of water molecules it holds, keep it out of the core.
Quick check
?The table shows approximate relative rates at which five substances cross an artificial membrane made only of phospholipids, with no proteins. Each step of 10⁻³ is a thousandfold change. Which statement is supported by the data?
Mission 3

Channel proteins

  • Facilitated diffusion: diffusion through a protein. Still passive; still down the gradient.
  • A channel protein spans the membrane with a water-filled, hydrophilic pore. Ions pass through it, avoiding the hydrophobic core.
  • Selective: the pore's width and the charges lining it let only certain ions through. A K⁺ channel passes K⁺ but not the smaller Na⁺.
  • Many are gated: open or closed. No ions pass when closed.
  • Charged ions such as Na⁺ and K⁺ need channel proteins. Ions moving through channels can leave the membrane polarized (charge separated across it).
  • Aquaporins are channel proteins for water. They let large amounts of water cross quickly.
  • Say: "a channel is specific" — not: "a channel is a hole any small particle can use".
Mission 3

Carrier proteins

  • A transport protein (carrier) binds a specific molecule, such as glucose.
  • Binding causes a change in shape. The molecule is released on the other side.
  • Large polar molecules cross this way.
  • Net movement is still down the gradient. No ATP is used.
  • (Carriers that use ATP to pump against a gradient.)
Quick check
?Cells of a hypothetical animal take up Na⁺ from the fluid around them by facilitated diffusion. Which statement best describes how the Na⁺ ions cross the plasma membrane?
Mission 3

Which route?

  • O2, CO2 → between the phospholipids.
  • Water → between the phospholipids, slowly.
  • Water → aquaporins, fast.
  • Ions (Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻) → channel proteins.
  • Glucose and other large polar molecules → transport proteins.
  • The bilayer sorts only by size and charge. Proteins make a membrane selective: each lets through particular substances.
  • Selective permeability lets cells keep concentration gradients across their membranes.
  • Constant movement of molecules across membranes keeps cells growing and in homeostasis.
Quick check
?Artificial vesicles were made from a pure phospholipid bilayer. Oxygen crossed the bilayer rapidly, glucose very slowly and potassium ions hardly at all. When a potassium channel protein was inserted into the bilayer, potassium crossed rapidly but permeability to sodium ions and to glucose did not change. Which conclusion is supported?
Mission check

?A hypothetical toxin binds to and blocks all of the channel and transport proteins in the plasma membrane of a cell. Which prediction about the cell, immediately after exposure to the toxin, is best supported?

Two routes, two rules: the bilayer lets through what is small and non-polar; proteins let through specific substances, ions included.

Mission 4

Mission 4: A bacterium has no nucleus. So where does it keep its genes, and what else does it manage without?

Make a guess: A bacterium has no nucleus. So where does it keep its genes, and what else does it manage without?

Mission 4

What every cell has

  • Every cell has: a plasma membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes and DNA.
  • Genetic information is stored in DNA; in some viruses, in RNA.
  • Two kinds of cell:
    • Prokaryotic (bacteria, archaea): no nucleus.
Mission 4

Inside a bacterium

  • No nucleus. One circular DNA molecule lies free in the cytoplasm, in the nucleoid region.
  • The nucleoid is a specialized region with no membrane around it.
  • Ribosomes are smaller than those in eukaryotic cytoplasm.
  • No membrane-bound organelles: no mitochondria, no ER, no Golgi.
  • Cell wall of peptidoglycan, outside the plasma membrane.
  • Often present: plasmids, small extra circles of DNA.
  • Say: "the DNA is in the nucleoid" — not: "the DNA is in a small nucleus".
Quick check
?Which statement best describes how the DNA of a typical prokaryotic cell is organized?
Mission 4

Prokaryote or eukaryote?

FeatureProkaryotic cellEukaryotic cell
Typical sizeabout 1–5 µmabout 10–100 µm
Nucleusnone: nucleoidyes: double membrane
DNAone circular molecule; no histonesseveral linear chromosomes, with histones
Membrane-bound organellesnonemany
Ribosomessmallerlarger, in cytoplasm

  • Shared by both: plasma membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes, DNA.
  • Eukaryotic internal membranes partition the cell into specialized compartments (nucleus, ER, Golgi, mitochondria).
  • Plasmids occur in both kinds of cell; for example, in yeast.
  • A cell wall does not tell you the cell type. Look for a nucleus.
Quick check
?Cells of baker's yeast often contain many copies of a small circular DNA molecule that is located in the nucleus but is not part of any of the yeast's linear chromosomes. Which statement about this molecule is correct?
Mission 4

Why a bacterium needs its wall

  • Bacterial cytoplasm is rich in solutes, so in dilute surroundings water enters by osmosis.
  • The wall is strong: it resists swelling and stops the cell bursting.
  • This is protection from osmotic lysis. The wall is also a structural boundary and a permeability barrier to some substances.
  • Bacteria, archaea, fungi and plants all have walls; their materials differ.
  • Strip the wall away (e.g. with the enzyme lysozyme) and a bacterium in water bursts.
Quick check
?Lysozyme is an enzyme that breaks down the cell walls of some bacteria. Cells of a hypothetical bacterial species, whose walls are completely removed by lysozyme, are treated with lysozyme in two solutions. Solution 1 is distilled water. Solution 2 contains sucrose at a concentration that makes its total solute concentration equal to that of the bacterial cytoplasm; sucrose does not cross the plasma membrane of these bacteria. Which prediction is best supported?
Mission check

?The diagram shows the structures in a cell of a newly described photosynthetic microorganism. Which statement best describes the cell?

No nucleus, but not disorganized: the genes sit in the nucleoid, a specialized region with no membrane.

Mission 5

Mission 5: A virus cannot feed, grow or divide. So how does one virus become millions?

Make a guess: A virus cannot feed, grow or divide. So how does one virus become millions?

Mission 5

A virus is not a cell

  • A virus particle is acellular: no cytoplasm, no ribosomes, few or no enzymes.
  • It has genetic material, DNA or RNA, never both.
  • The genetic material sits inside a capsid: a coat of protein.
  • Some viruses store their genetic information in RNA, not DNA.
  • Viruses do not divide. The host cell's ribosomes, enzymes and ATP make new virus particles.
  • Say: "virus particle" — not: "virus cell".
Quick check
?Tobacco mosaic virus consists of a protein coat surrounding a single RNA molecule; it contains no DNA. When it infects tobacco cells, new virus particles are made, each with the same genetic information as the infecting virus. Which statement best explains how this virus stores and passes on its genetic information?
Mission 5

Two very different viruses

  • Bacteriophage (phage): infects bacteria.
    • DNA inside a protein head; a protein tail injects the DNA.
  • HIV: infects human helper T cells.
    • RNA (two single strands) inside a protein capsid.
    • An envelope: a lipid bilayer taken from the host cell, studded with glycoproteins.
    • Carries the enzyme reverse transcriptase: HIV is a retrovirus.
Mission 5

Retroviruses: from RNA back to DNA

  • HIV is a retrovirus: its genome is RNA.
  • (1) Reverse transcriptase copies the viral RNA into DNA.
  • (2) The viral DNA integrates into the host genome.
  • (3) The host transcribes and translates it: new viral RNA and proteins.
  • (4) New particles assemble and leave the cell.
  • An alternate flow of information: RNA → DNA → RNA → protein.
Quick check
?A hypothetical drug is proposed to block reverse transcriptase. Researchers add the drug to human cells in culture and then expose the cells to a retrovirus. If the drug works as proposed, which result is predicted?
Mission 5

Viruses evolve fast

  • Two related viruses in the same host cell can recombine: new mixes of genes.
  • Mutations are random; drugs and antibodies select variants that already exist.
  • Say: "resistant variants are selected" — not: "the virus mutates to escape the drug".
Mission 5

Bacteria swap genes sideways

  • Prokaryotes gain genes horizontally, not only from a parent cell:
  • Transformation: a cell takes up DNA from its surroundings.
  • Transduction: a virus (phage) carries bacterial DNA from one cell to another.
  • Conjugation: DNA (often a plasmid) passes cell to cell through a pilus.
  • Transposition: DNA segments move within and between DNA molecules.
  • Each one increases genetic variation, which natural selection can act on (e.g. antibiotic resistance).
Quick check
?A population of a hypothetical soil bacterium contains no cells resistant to antibiotic Z. A few of its cells receive, by conjugation with cells of another bacterial species, a plasmid carrying a gene for resistance to Z. Which statement best describes the effect of this transfer on the population?
Mission check

?The antibiotic tetracycline blocks bacterial ribosomes but does not block the ribosomes in the cytoplasm of human cells. A doctor is asked whether tetracycline would stop influenza viruses multiplying in the cells lining a patient's airways. Which answer is correct?

One virus becomes millions because each infected cell becomes a factory, using its own ribosomes, enzymes and ATP to build new particles.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Phospholipids have hydrophilic heads and hydrophobic tails, so in water they form a bilayer on their own; the hydrophobic core blocks ions and polar molecules.
  • Proteins, glycoproteins, glycolipids and cholesterol sit in or on this fluid sheet, and its parts move sideways.

Mission 2

  • Labeled proteins mix after cell fusion and refill a bleached patch: membrane components move sideways, faster when warmer.

Mission 3

  • Simple diffusion: small non-polar molecules pass between the phospholipids, down their gradient; ions and polar molecules are kept out by the hydrophobic core.
  • Facilitated diffusion: specific channel or carrier proteins carry them across, still passively and down the gradient.

Mission 4

  • A prokaryote keeps one circular DNA molecule in the nucleoid, with no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles; it still has a membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes and (usually) a wall.

Mission 5

  • A virus is an acellular particle: DNA or RNA in a protein capsid, with no metabolism; host cells make and assemble new particles, often hundreds per cell.
  • Retroviruses such as HIV use reverse transcriptase to copy RNA into DNA that joins the host genome.
  • Prokaryotes gain genes horizontally (transformation, transduction, conjugation, transposition); related viruses recombine in a shared host cell.
