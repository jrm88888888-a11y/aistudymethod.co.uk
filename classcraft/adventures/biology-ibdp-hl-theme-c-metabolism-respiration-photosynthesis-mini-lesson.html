Mission 1

Enzymes are catalysts

  • An enzyme is a protein that acts as a biological catalyst.
  • A catalyst increases the rate of a reaction.
  • It is not used up: it leaves the reaction unchanged, so one molecule catalyses the same reaction again and again.
  • The reaction can happen without the enzyme, but at body temperature it would be far too slow.
  • Benefit: reactions in cells run fast enough to sustain life.
  • Say: "speeds up a reaction" — not: "makes a reaction possible".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly describes how an enzyme acts as a catalyst in a cell?
Mission 1

Metabolism: a web of reactions

  • Metabolism: all the chemical reactions in an organism — a network of linked pathways.
  • Almost every step is catalysed by its own enzyme. So an organism needs thousands of different enzymes.
  • Control the enzyme → control the step. That is how cells regulate metabolism.
  • Two ways to control an enzyme: how much of it is made, and how active each molecule is. So a cell can slow one pathway and leave the rest running.
  • Anabolism builds larger molecules from smaller ones by condensation: protein synthesis, glycogen formation, photosynthesis. Needs energy, usually from ATP.
  • Catabolism breaks larger molecules into smaller ones: hydrolysis in digestion, oxidation of substrates in respiration. Releases energy.
Quick check
?Which process is an example of anabolism?
Mission 1

Lowering the energy barrier

  • To react, bonds in the substrate must first be broken. That needs energy: the activation energy.
  • Energy is taken in to break bonds in the substrate; energy is released when the bonds of the products form.
  • An enzyme gives the reaction a route with a lower activation energy.
  • At the same temperature, more substrate molecules now have enough energy to react. So the rate rises.
  • The energy of the substrate and of the products is unchanged. So is the energy released overall.
  • The enzyme supplies no energy.
  • Say: "the enzyme lowers the activation energy" — not: "the enzyme provides the activation energy".

Mission 1

The active site

  • Enzymes are globular proteins: one or more polypeptide chains folded into a compact 3D shape (tertiary structure).
  • The active site is a small pocket or groove where the substrate binds.
  • It is made of only a few amino acids. But bonds between R groups across the whole molecule hold it in its exact shape.
  • The substrate's shape is complementary to the active site; its charged groups are attracted to opposite charges there.
  • Substrate + active site → enzyme–substrate complex → products released; enzyme free again.
  • Say: "complementary shape" — not: "the same shape".
Mission 1

Specificity

  • Each enzyme catalyses one reaction, or a few very similar ones: specificity.
  • Reason: only a molecule whose shape and charges suit the active site can bind.
  • Starch and cellulose are both chains of glucose. Amylase hydrolyses starch but not cellulose.
  • The glucose units are linked differently (α-glucose in starch, β-glucose in cellulose), so cellulose does not fit amylase's active site.
  • Size alone does not decide: many small molecules do not bind.
  • Say: "the active site is complementary to one substrate" — not: "any molecule small enough fits".
Quick check
?Lipase catalyses the hydrolysis of triglycerides but has no effect on starch. What is the reason?

Mission 2

Meeting by chance

  • Molecules in solution are in constant, random motion.
  • A reaction can only happen when a substrate collides with the active site, the right way round.
  • Enzymes do not seek, pull or grab their substrates.
  • More collisions per second → more enzyme–substrate complexes → faster rate (used again in Mission 3).
  • Only one partner needs to move. Some enzymes are fixed in membranes (e.g. ATP synthase); some large substrates are fixed while enzymes move to them.
  • Say: "substrate and active site collide by random motion" — not: "the enzyme attracts its substrate".
Quick check
?ATP synthase is embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane and cannot move freely. How do its substrates, ADP and phosphate, reach its active site?
Mission 2

Model 1: lock and key

  • Proposed by Emil Fischer, 1894.
  • The active site is a rigid shape. Only a substrate of exactly complementary shape fits, like one key in one lock.
  • Strength: explains specificity.
  • Limits: a rigid site does not explain how bound substrate is strained into reacting; and later evidence showed many enzymes change shape when they bind.
Mission 2

Model 2: induced fit

  • Proposed by Daniel Koshland, 1958.
  • The active site is flexible. As the substrate binds, the active site changes shape and moulds closely around it.
  • Both the enzyme and the substrate change shape as they bind.
  • The tight fit strains bonds in the substrate, which lowers the activation energy.
  • Enzyme–substrate complex → products released; enzyme returns to its original shape.
  • Say: "the active site changes shape as the substrate binds" — not: "the active site is a rigid exact match".
Quick check
?According to the induced-fit model, what happens when a substrate binds to the active site of an enzyme?
Mission 2

Inside or outside the cell?

  • Intracellular enzymes act inside the cell that made them.
  • e.g. enzymes of glycolysis (cytoplasm) and the Krebs cycle (mitochondria).
  • Extracellular enzymes are made inside cells, secreted, and act outside them.
  • e.g. chemical digestion in the gut: enzymes act in the lumen; only the products are absorbed.
  • Say: "extracellular enzymes are made inside cells and secreted" — not: "made outside cells".
Quick check
?Which statement about intracellular and extracellular enzyme-catalysed reactions is correct?
Mission check

?Hexokinase is the enzyme that adds a phosphate group to glucose. X-ray structures show that the two halves of the enzyme are further apart when no glucose is bound. When glucose binds, the two halves close around it. What does this evidence show?

Not a rigid lock: the enzyme and its substrate adjust to each other, and that close, strained fit is part of what speeds the reaction.

Mission 3

Measuring the rate

  • Rate = amount of product formed (or substrate used) per unit time.
  • Measure product appearing (e.g. oxygen from catalase) or substrate disappearing (e.g. starch, with iodine).
  • A product–time curve is steep at first, then flattens as substrate is used up.
  • Initial rate: the gradient at time zero, before the substrate runs down. The fairest way to compare conditions.
  • Timing a fixed end point (a colour change, a disc rising)? Rate is proportional to 1 ÷ time.
  • Rates can come from your own experiments or from secondary data.
Mission 3

Worked example: initial rate from a tangent

Illustrative data: oxygen released by catalase, plotted against time.

1. Draw a straight line touching the curve at t = 0: the tangent.

2. Pick two points far apart on the tangent: (0 s, 0) and (40 s, 36).

3. Gradient = change in y ÷ change in x = 36 ÷ 40.

  • Initial rate = 0.90 cm³ s⁻¹.
  • Same method at any point on a curve: tangent there, then gradient.
  • Fixed end point: a disc took 25 s to rise. Rate = 1 ÷ 25 = 0.040 s⁻¹.
Quick check
?Catalase was added to hydrogen peroxide and the volume of oxygen collected was recorded: 0 s, 0 cm³; 20 s, 12 cm³; 40 s, 20 cm³; 60 s, 24 cm³ (illustrative data). What is the best estimate of the initial rate of reaction from these data?
Mission 3

Temperature

  • Warmer → molecules have more kinetic energy → they move faster.
  • More frequent collisions between substrate and active sites, and more with enough energy → more enzyme–substrate complexes per second → faster rate.
  • Above the optimum, vibrations break hydrogen and ionic bonds that hold the tertiary structure.
  • The active site changes shape; the substrate no longer fits. The enzyme is denatured.
  • Denaturation is usually permanent: cooling does not restore the shape.
  • In the cold, enzymes are slow, not denatured.
  • Optima differ: many human enzymes ≈ 37 °C; DNA polymerase from a hot-spring bacterium ≈ 75 °C.
  • The textbook rise-and-fall sketch is a model; real results can be used to test and refine it.
  • Say: "the enzyme is denatured" — not: "the enzyme is killed".
Quick check
?A student measured the rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction at six temperatures. The rates, in arbitrary units, were: 10 °C, 2; 20 °C, 4; 30 °C, 8; 40 °C, 15; 50 °C, 9; 60 °C, 1 (illustrative data). Which conclusion is supported by these data?
Mission 3

pH

  • pH measures the concentration of hydrogen ions (H⁺).
  • H⁺ ions change the charges on R groups (e.g. –COO⁻ + H⁺ → –COOH).
  • Ionic and hydrogen bonds change → the active site's shape and charges change → substrate binds less well.
  • Far from the optimum the enzyme is denatured.
  • Optima differ: pepsin (stomach) ≈ pH 2; salivary amylase ≈ pH 7; trypsin (small intestine) ≈ pH 8.
  • Say: "pH changes the charges on R groups" — not: "pH slows the molecules down".
Quick check
?Pepsin has an optimum pH of about 2. When it passes with food into the small intestine, where the pH is about 8, its activity falls almost to zero. Which explanation is correct?
Mission 3

Substrate and enzyme concentration

  • More substrate: more collisions with active sites → faster rate.
  • At high substrate concentration, every active site is occupied almost all the time: the enzyme is saturated. The rate levels off; enzyme concentration now limits it.
  • More enzyme: more active sites → rate rises in proportion, as long as substrate is in excess.
  • A rate–concentration plateau (saturation) is not the same as a product–time curve flattening (substrate used up).
  • Say: "the active sites are saturated" — not: "the enzyme is used up".
Quick check
?At high substrate concentrations, the rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction levels off even when more substrate is added. Why?
Mission 3Practical

Rate of an enzyme-controlled reaction

  • Determine reaction rates by experiment.
  • Method (catalase and hydrogen peroxide): put substrate and enzyme in separate tubes in a water bath to reach the set temperature.
  • Mix; collect oxygen in a gas syringe; read the volume every 10 s.
  • Repeat at five or more values of the independent variable, three times each.
  • Plot volume against time; tangent at t = 0 gives the initial rate.
  • Control: temperature (water bath), pH (buffer), volumes and concentrations not being tested. Control tube: boiled enzyme.
Mission check

?An enzyme from a cold-water fish has an optimum temperature of about 15 °C. A sample was kept at 45 °C for 10 minutes and then cooled back to 15 °C. Its activity at 15 °C was only 5% of that of an unheated sample tested at the same time (illustrative data). Which explanation is correct?

Heat speeds enzymes by speeding collisions, until it shakes apart the bonds that shape the active site. Then the rate falls, and usually stays down.

Mission 4

Metabolic pathways

  • A metabolic pathway is a chain of enzyme-catalysed steps.
  • The product of one step is the substrate of the next: A → B → C → D, with enzymes 1, 2 and 3.
  • Linear pathway: glycolysis, glucose → pyruvate.
  • Cyclical pathway: Krebs cycle, Calvin cycle. One intermediate is regenerated each turn (oxaloacetate; RuBP), while substrates enter and products leave.
Quick check
?In a hypothetical bacterium, compound S is made from compound P by a pathway of three enzyme-catalysed steps: P → Q → R → S. Enzyme 1 converts P to Q, enzyme 2 converts Q to R, and enzyme 3 converts R to S. A mutation makes enzyme 2 non-functional. Which prediction about the mutant bacterium is best supported?
Mission 4

Control through enzymes

  • Each step has its own specific enzyme.
  • So a cell can slow one pathway by acting on one enzyme, and leave the rest of metabolism running.
  • Two ways to control an enzyme: how much of it is made, and how active each molecule is.
  • Activity is changed by molecules that bind the enzyme: inhibitors (next screens).
Mission 4

Competitive inhibitors

  • Similar in shape (and charge) to the substrate.
  • Binds reversibly to the active site. While it is there, the substrate cannot bind.
  • Inhibitor and substrate compete: whichever collides with a free active site binds.
  • More substrate → substrate wins more often → rate climbs back towards the uninhibited maximum.
  • More inhibitor → more active sites blocked → lower rate.
Mission 4

Non-competitive inhibitors

  • Bind at a site away from the active site.
  • This is an allosteric site.
  • Binding changes the enzyme's tertiary structure → the active site changes shape → substrate cannot bind, or is not converted.
  • No competition: extra substrate does not restore the rate. The maximum rate is lowered.
  • Only specific substances bind an allosteric site, and binding is reversible.
  • Say: "changes the shape of the active site" — not: "blocks the active site".
  • Say: "binds away from the active site" — not: "non-competitive means irreversible".

Mission 4

Statins: a competitive drug

  • Liver cells make cholesterol by a pathway. One step is catalysed by HMG-CoA reductase.
  • Statins resemble its substrate and bind reversibly to its active site: competitive inhibition.
  • Less cholesterol is made → blood LDL cholesterol falls.
  • Binding is reversible: as the body clears the drug, the enzyme works again. So statins are taken every day.
Mission 4

Inhibitors that never let go

  • Some inhibitors form covalent bonds with the enzyme: non-reversible.
  • Mechanism-based inhibition: the inhibitor binds irreversibly and chemically changes the active site.
  • Penicillin binds the active site of transpeptidases, bacterial enzymes that cross-link peptidoglycan in the cell wall. It becomes covalently attached there.
  • No cross-links → weak wall → growing bacteria burst.
  • Resistance: a changed transpeptidase whose active site still cross-links peptidoglycan but binds penicillin poorly. The change arises by chance in the bacteria's DNA; penicillin then selects those bacteria.
Mission 4

Feedback inhibition

  • The end product of a pathway inhibits an enzyme early in it, usually the first.
  • It binds at an allosteric site: reversible, non-competitive.
  • Plenty of product → pathway slows. Product used up → inhibitor leaves → pathway restarts. Negative feedback.
  • Saves resources; intermediates do not pile up.
  • In bacteria: threonine → (5 steps) → isoleucine. Isoleucine inhibits threonine deaminase, the first enzyme.
Quick check
?In bacteria, threonine is converted into isoleucine by a pathway of five enzyme-catalysed steps. What happens when isoleucine is added to a growing culture?
Mission check

?Methanol is not very toxic itself, but the enzyme alcohol dehydrogenase converts it into toxic products. Ethanol is also a substrate of alcohol dehydrogenase. Doctors have treated methanol poisoning by giving ethanol. Why does this reduce the harm?

One enzyme, one step: a molecule that occupies or reshapes that enzyme's active site slows that reaction and leaves the rest of metabolism running.

Mission 5

Three kinds of variable

  • Independent variable: the one you change on purpose.
  • Dependent variable: the one you measure. It may depend on the independent variable.
  • Controlled variables: everything else that could affect the result. Keep them constant.
  • Example: lamp distance (independent) → bubbles of gas per minute from pondweed (dependent). Temperature, CO2 supply and the piece of pondweed stay the same.
  • Change one variable at a time. If two change together, you cannot tell which caused the effect.
  • The hook: fertiliser and light changed together. The result is confounded.
Quick check
?A student places a lamp at different distances from a beaker of pondweed in sodium hydrogencarbonate solution kept at 25 °C, and counts the bubbles of gas released per minute at each distance. Which correctly identifies the variables?
Mission 5

Controls

  • A control is a set-up identical to the others except that the factor being tested is absent (or at a standard level).
  • It shows what happens without the factor, so any difference can be put down to that factor.
  • Negative control: no effect expected (e.g. boiled enzyme, or water in place of an extract).
  • Positive control: an effect is known to occur. It shows the method can detect one.
  • A control need not show "nothing". It gives the baseline for comparison.
  • Say: "temperature was a controlled variable" — not: "temperature was the control".
Quick check
?A student tests the claim that an extract from a hypothetical plant contains an enzyme that speeds up the breakdown of compound P. When P breaks down, the solution turns yellow. Tube 1 contains P, buffer and the extract. Tube 2 contains P and buffer, with water in place of the extract. Both tubes are kept at 25 °C, and the time each takes to turn yellow is recorded. Which statement best justifies including tube 2?
Mission 5

Questions and hypotheses

  • A testable question names what you will change and what you will measure: "Does light intensity affect the rate of photosynthesis in pondweed?"
  • A hypothesis is a provisional explanation that can be tested: "Rate rises with light intensity, because light drives the light-dependent reactions."
  • A prediction says what you expect to measure if the hypothesis is right.
  • The null hypothesis says the independent variable has no effect (or there is no difference). Statistical tests check whether the data let you reject it.
  • Hypotheses can come from theory before an experiment, or from evidence after one. Either way they need repeated testing.
  • Data can support a hypothesis. They never prove it.
Quick check
?A student plans to test whether the number of double bonds in a fatty acid affects its melting point, using fatty acids with 0, 1, 2 and 3 double bonds that all have the same chain length. Which statement is an appropriate null hypothesis for this investigation?
Mission 5

Measuring the dependent variable

  • Biologists often measure a rate: change ÷ time.
  • Product formed over time: change in amount ÷ time taken.
  • e.g. 15 cm³ of oxygen in 30 s → 15 ÷ 30 = 0.50 cm³ s⁻¹.
  • Time for a fixed change (a disc rises, a colour appears): rate ∝ 1 ÷ time. A disc that rises in 25 s → 1 ÷ 25 = 0.040 s⁻¹.
  • A shorter time means a faster rate.
  • Measure the initial rate where you can: the reaction slows as substrate is used up.
  • Choose apparatus that suits the size of the change: a gas syringe or a colorimeter beats judging by eye.
Quick check
?A filter-paper disc soaked in catalase solution sinks in hydrogen peroxide and rises when enough oxygen has formed on it. In 1% hydrogen peroxide the disc took 50 s to rise; in 2% it took 20 s. Which statement about the reaction rate is correct?
Mission 5

Experiment, observation or model?

  • Experiment: you manipulate the independent variable and control the rest. Best evidence for cause and effect.
  • Observation (survey, field sampling): you record what is already there. Shows patterns and correlations, not cause.
  • Example: barnacle A grows only where barnacle B is absent (observation). Scrape B off marked plots and A moves in (field experiment).
  • Even a good experiment only indicates a cause; other differences may remain.
  • A model stands in for a system too big to manipulate, e.g. a mesocosm: ideally a sealed glass vessel of pond water, algae and small invertebrates. Light enters; matter cannot.
  • A model is simplified. Its results apply to real ecosystems only with caution.
Quick check
?Ecologists want to test the hypothesis that two limpet species compete for algae on a shore. Which approach tests the hypothesis by experiment rather than by observation?
Mission 5

Repeats, samples and validity

  • Living things vary. One measurement may be unusual, so repeat and use the mean.
  • Repeats also expose anomalies: results far from the others.
  • Sample size: more individuals make the mean more likely to be near the true population mean.
  • Sample selection: choose at random, so the sample represents the population and no one picks "good" specimens.
  • Valid: the method answers the question asked. It needs controlled variables and a suitable dependent variable.
  • Results that come out the same when repeated are repeatable; when someone else repeats them with other equipment, reproducible.
  • "Reliable" is used for results that stay consistent when repeated.
  • Repeats cannot rescue an invalid design: the hook's plants could be measured 100 times and still be confounded.
Mission 5

Tables and graphs

  • Table: independent variable in the first column; repeats, then mean. Units in the headings, never in the cells. Same number of decimal places down each column.
  • Graph: independent variable on the x-axis, dependent on the y-axis.
  • Label each axis with the quantity and its unit.
  • e.g. "Rate / cm³ s⁻¹".
  • Choose a scale that uses over half the grid, in even steps.
  • Line graph: continuous independent variable. Bar chart: categories.
  • Scatter graph: looking for a correlation between two measured variables.
  • Do not extend a line beyond your data.

Mission 6

Accurate or precise?

  • Accurate: close to the true value.
  • Precise: repeats agree closely, with little spread about the mean.
  • Random error: unpredictable scatter (judging an end point, natural variation). It lowers precision. Repeats and a mean reduce its effect.
  • Systematic error: shifts every reading the same way (a balance not zeroed, a thermometer reading 2 °C high). It lowers accuracy. Repeats do not remove it; calibrating does.
  • Resolution: the smallest change an instrument can show.
  • Quantitative observations are measured values with units (an angle of 32°). Qualitative observations are drawings or descriptions ("bent towards the light").
  • Say: "precise but not accurate" — not: "precise, so accurate".
Quick check
?Two students each measured the angle of curvature of the same seedling shoot five times with a protractor. Student A recorded 28°, 29°, 28°, 29°, 28°. Student B recorded 22°, 35°, 30°, 26°, 37°. A later check showed that the true angle was 35° and that Student A had aligned the protractor with the edge of the pot instead of the vertical part of the stem. Which conclusion is justified?
Quick check
?Students investigating phototropism recorded three observations of seedlings lit from one side: (1) a labelled drawing showing the shoot curved towards the lamp; (2) "the shoots bent much more than those of the control"; (3) an angle of curvature of 32° measured with a protractor. Mission 6

Anomalies, limitations, improvements

  • Anomaly: a result that does not fit the pattern. Look for a cause; repeat it; leave it out of the mean only with a stated reason.
  • Limitation: a feature of the method that reduces validity, accuracy or precision.
  • Match the improvement to the problem:
    • random scatter → more repeats, then a mean;
    • systematic error → zero or calibrate the instrument;
    • low resolution → an instrument with finer divisions;
    • an uncontrolled variable → control it (water bath, buffer).
  • Tropism measurements: align the protractor with the stem; photograph seedlings and measure on screen; use more seedlings per treatment.
Mission check

?A student uses a water bath to keep enzyme tubes at 30 °C. Her thermometer gives readings that agree closely: 30.0, 30.0, 30.5, 30.0 °C. A calibrated electronic probe in the same water bath reads 28.0 °C. Which improvement would make her temperature measurements more accurate?

Agreement shows precision, not truth. A systematic error moves every reading together, and only calibration finds it.

Mission 7

Make heat or borrow it

  • Endotherms (mammals, birds) use heat released by their own metabolism to keep core temperature nearly constant.
  • Ectotherms (e.g. lizards, fish, insects) gain heat mainly from their surroundings; they have little internal control.
  • Cost: an endotherm needs far more food than an ectotherm of the same mass.
  • Benefit: an endotherm stays active in the cold; an ectotherm slows down.
  • Birds and mammals regulate body temperature by physiological and behavioural means. Details here are for humans.
Mission 7

Heat: the unavoidable by-product

  • No metabolic reaction transfers energy with 100% efficiency.
  • Part of the energy of every reaction is released as heat.
  • So every organism makes some heat. Endotherms depend on it to hold body temperature.
  • In colder surroundings heat is lost faster, so an endotherm must raise its metabolic rate.

Mission 7

The control centre

  • Peripheral thermoreceptors in the skin detect skin and outside temperature: an early warning.
  • Thermoreceptors in the hypothalamus monitor the temperature of the blood: core temperature.
  • The hypothalamus compares this with the set point (about 37 °C) and sends nerve impulses to effectors.
  • Effectors: skin arterioles, sweat glands, hair erector muscles, skeletal muscles.
  • Slow route: hypothalamus → pituitary (TSH) → thyroid → thyroxin, which raises metabolic rate over days.
  • Another effector: brown adipose tissue.
  • This is negative feedback: each response opposes the change.
Mission 7

Too hot

  • Vasodilation: smooth muscle in skin arterioles relaxes. More blood flows through surface capillaries; more heat is lost to the air.
  • Sweating: water in sweat evaporates, taking latent heat from the skin.
  • Hairs lie flat: erector muscles relax; less air is trapped.
  • Say: "arterioles dilate, so more blood flows near the surface" — not: "blood vessels move closer to the skin".
Mission 7

Too cold

  • Vasoconstriction: skin arterioles constrict. Less blood reaches the surface; less heat is lost.
  • Shivering: rapid, involuntary contractions of skeletal muscle. More respiration, more heat.
  • Hair erection: erector muscles contract, trapping air. Effective in furry mammals; little effect in humans.
  • Sweating falls.
  • Brown adipose tissue (important in babies): uncoupled respiration. Protons flow back into the mitochondrial matrix without passing through ATP synthase, so the energy is released as heat, not stored in ATP.
  • More thyroxin in long cold spells: higher metabolic rate.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Enzymes are protein catalysts: they lower the activation energy, leave the energy released unchanged, and are not used up.
  • The active site, held in shape by the whole tertiary structure, is complementary in shape and charge to one substrate: hence the enzyme–substrate complex and specificity.

Mission 2

  • Substrate and active site meet by random collisions; in induced fit the active site changes shape as the substrate binds, straining its bonds.
  • Intracellular enzymes act inside cells; extracellular enzymes are made inside cells and secreted to act outside.

Mission 3

  • Temperature and substrate concentration raise the rate by increasing collisions, until denaturation (temperature) or saturation (substrate) takes over; pH acts on the charges that hold the active site's shape.
  • Compare initial rates, found from the tangent at time zero.

Mission 4

  • In a pathway the product of one step is the substrate of the next; acting on one enzyme controls one pathway.
  • Competitive inhibitors bind the active site and are outcompeted by more substrate; non-competitive inhibitors bind elsewhere, change the active site and lower the maximum rate.
  • End products inhibit early enzymes of their own pathway; penicillin inhibits irreversibly.

Mission 5

  • Change one variable, measure one, keep the rest constant; a control gives the baseline for comparison.
  • A null hypothesis says "no effect"; data can support a hypothesis but never prove it.
  • Experiments manipulate a variable; observations record patterns. Random, repeated samples make a mean trustworthy.

Mission 6

  • Precise = repeats agree; accurate = close to the true value. Repeats reduce random error; calibration removes systematic error.

Mission 7

  • The hypothalamus compares blood temperature with the set point and drives effectors: vasodilation and sweating to cool; vasoconstriction and shivering to warm.
  • Endotherms make their own heat by metabolism at a high energy cost; ectotherms rely on surroundings and behaviour.
