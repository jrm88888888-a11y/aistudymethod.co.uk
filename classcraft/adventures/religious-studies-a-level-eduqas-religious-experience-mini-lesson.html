Mission 1

What counts as a religious experience?

  • William James (1842–1910), The Varieties of Religious Experience (1902), Lecture II: religion is "the feelings, acts, and experiences of individual men in their solitude, so far as they apprehend themselves to stand in relation to whatever they may consider the divine".
  • A religious experience is an experience the person takes to be an encounter with, or awareness of, God or ultimate reality.
  • Types in this mission: visions, conversion, prayer, numinous; mystical experience in Mission 2.
Mission 1

Visions

  • A vision is something "seen" that the person believes comes from God.
  • Eduqas wording: sensory (seen with the senses), intellectual (no image, direct knowing), dreams: "He dreamt and saw a stairway set upon the earth, and its top reached to heaven." (Genesis 28:12); Joseph's dream (Matthew 1:20).
  • Many Christian writers, following Augustine and Aquinas, rank intellectual visions highest, because they depend least on the senses, which can deceive (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II q. 174 a. 2).

Mission 1

Conversion

  • James, Lecture IX: conversion is "the process, gradual or sudden, by which a self hitherto divided, and consciously wrong inferior and unhappy, becomes unified and consciously right superior and happy".
  • James describes this whether or not God directly causes it: he studies the change in the person.
  • Sudden: Paul on the road to Damascus (Acts 9). Gradual: Augustine's years of searching, ending in the garden in Milan, "take up and read" (Confessions VIII.12).
  • Individual (one person) or communal (a group together): after Peter's sermon at Pentecost "about three thousand souls" were added (Acts 2:41).
  • Conversion can be to a faith, between faiths, or within one (a nominal believer becoming committed).

Mission 1

Teresa: the four waters of prayer

  • Teresa of Avila (1515–1582), Carmelite reformer; The Life of Teresa of Jesus (c. 1565), chs. 11–21; The Interior Castle (1577).
  • Types: vocal prayer (spoken), mental prayer (meditating on Christ), recollection, quiet, union.
  • The soul is a garden; prayer is how it is watered. Four ways, from much effort to none:
  • 1. Well and bucket: beginners' meditation; hard work, little water.
  • 2. Water wheel: the prayer of quiet; less effort, the will is held by God.
  • 3. Stream: the faculties "sleep"; the gardener only directs the flow.
  • 4. Rain: the prayer of union; God does everything; the soul is passive. Raptures may follow.

Mission 1

Otto: the numinous

  • Rudolf Otto (1869–1937), Das Heilige (1917); English The Idea of the Holy (1923).
  • "Holy" usually means morally good. Otto strips this away to find the raw experience underneath: the numinous (Latin numen, divine power).
  • Mysterium: the wholly other (das ganz Andere): beyond all understanding; the response is blank wonder.
  • Tremendum: awe-filled dread before overwhelming power and majesty; creature-feeling: "I am nothing before this".
  • Fascinans: yet the person is drawn towards it, attracted as well as afraid.
  • Non-rational, not irrational: it cannot be put into concepts, but it is not nonsense.
  • Human predisposition: Otto holds the sense of the holy is an a priori category of the mind, a built-in capacity, not learned from elsewhere.

  • Between these verses the seraphim cry "Holy, holy, holy" and the house shakes and fills with smoke (6:3–4).

Revelation and shared experience

  • Individual experience happens to one person; corporate experience to a group at once: Pentecost (Acts 2); the "Toronto Blessing" (Toronto Airport Vineyard church, from 1994).
  • Influence on individuals: conversions, vocations, renewed commitment. John Wesley: "I felt my heart strangely warmed" (Journal, 24 May 1738), a turning point for Methodism.
  • Influence on communities: shared experiences can found or renew movements.

Mission 1Evaluate

"Conversion is the most important religious experience in a believer's life."

  • For: it changes the whole direction of a life, not one moment (James: a divided self unified).
  • For: Paul's conversion shaped his mission and much of the New Testament; many evangelical Christians see being "born again" (John 3:3; WEBBE "born anew") as the start of real faith.
  • Against: many believers grow into faith gradually through family, sacraments and worship, with no single turning point; Catholic and Orthodox Christians stress baptism and lifelong growth.
  • Against: ongoing prayer, numinous awe and mystical union sustain faith after conversion.
  • Against: Teresa's deepest experiences came decades into religious life.
  • Against: James notes that some conversions fade; the lasting fruits matter more than the moment.
  • Judge it by: Which experience changes behaviour most lastingly? Does the answer differ between traditions? Is conversion one event or a lifelong process?

Mission 2

What makes an experience mystical?

  • A mystical experience is a sense of union or oneness with God or ultimate reality, beyond ordinary perception and thought.
  • Contrast with Otto's numinous (see Mission 1): there the self stands apart before the wholly other; here the gap closes.
  • Christian: Julian of Norwich (Revelations of Divine Love, 1373 visions); Teresa of Avila.
  • Islam: Sufi fana (passing away of the self in God).
  • Judaism: Kabbalistic devekut (cleaving to God).
  • Hinduism: samadhi; for Advaita, realising atman is Brahman.
  • Buddhism: meditative absorption and awakening, with no creator God to unite with.
Mission 2Read the source

James' four marks

William James (1842–1910), The Varieties of Religious Experience (1902), Lectures XVI–XVII:

  • Ineffability: "it defies expression, that no adequate report of its contents can be given in words." So mystical states "are more like states of feeling than like states of intellect"
  • Noetic quality: "states of insight into depths of truth unplumbed by the discursive intellect."
  • Transiency: "Mystical states cannot be sustained for long."
  • Passivity: the mystic feels "as if he were grasped and held by a superior power."
  • James: the first two are enough to call a state mystical; the last two are "less sharply marked, but are usually found".

Q1 Why does James compare mystical states to feelings?

Show answer

Like music or love, they must be experienced to be understood: they cannot be passed on in words.

Q2 Which mark makes mystical states more than feelings?

Show answer

Noetic quality: they seem to give knowledge.

Mission 2

James' conclusions

James asks (Lecture XVII) whether mystical states give truth. His answer, in three parts:

  • (1) Well-developed mystical states "usually are, and have the right to be, absolutely authoritative over the individuals to whom they come."
  • (2) They give no duty to outsiders to accept them uncritically.
  • (3) They break down the claim that reason and the senses are the only kind of consciousness: other orders of truth are possible.
  • Lecture XX: the religious life includes the beliefs "That the visible world is part of a more spiritual universe" and "That union or harmonious relation with that higher universe is our true end".
Mission 2

Stace: the common core

  • W. T. Stace (1886–1967), Mysticism and Philosophy (1960).
  • Extrovertive mysticism: looking outward through the senses, the mystic sees the many things of the world as one.
  • Introvertive mysticism: senses and thoughts shut down; what remains is pure consciousness, an undifferentiated unity.
  • Stace calls the introvertive type the higher; he argues it is the common core of mysticism in every tradition.
  • The differences between traditions come from interpretation after the experience: a Christian says "union with God", an Advaitin "atman is Brahman".
Mission 2

Theistic and monistic mysticism

  • R. C. Zaehner (1913–1974), Mysticism Sacred and Profane (1957), rejects the idea that all mysticism is one (he was answering Aldous Huxley; Stace later replied to him). He distinguishes:
  • Nature mysticism: oneness with the natural world.
  • Monistic: the self realised as identical with the Absolute; no distinction remains (Advaita Vedanta).
  • Theistic: loving union with a personal God in which God and soul stay distinct (Teresa; much Sufism; Ramanuja's Vaishnava devotion).
  • Eduqas types: transcendent (awareness of a reality beyond self and world), ecstatic (rapture: the self "standing outside" itself), unitive (union with the divine).
  • Paul's "caught up into the third heaven" (2 Corinthians 12:2) is a classic ecstatic case; he "heard unspeakable words" (12:4).
Mission 2

Teresa: union without losing the self

  • The Interior Castle (1577): the soul is a castle of seven sets of rooms (mansions), with God at the centre.
  • Mansions 1–3: ordinary prayer and effort. 4: the prayer of quiet.
  • 5: the prayer of union: brief, but the soul is certain afterwards that it was in God.
  • 6: "spiritual betrothal": raptures, visions, locutions, intense longing (ecstatic).
  • 7: "spiritual marriage": a lasting union, with peace and active service of others (unitive).
  • Her image (Mansion VII): like rain falling into a river, the waters can no longer be separated, yet God and the soul are not simply identical: a theistic union.

Mission 2

Union, illusion or physiological effect?

Three ways of understanding the same report:

  • Union with a greater power: the mystics' own account; James is open to it ("the visible world is part of a more spiritual universe").
  • Psychological effect, such as illusion: Sigmund Freud (1856–1939), Civilization and Its Discontents (1930), traces the "oceanic feeling" to the infant's early sense of being one with its surroundings.
  • Physiological effect: produced by the body or brain: fasting, sleep loss, temporal-lobe epilepsy, drugs. James himself studied nitrous oxide states (Lecture XVI).
  • Walter Pahnke's "Good Friday experiment" (1962): students given psilocybin in a chapel reported mystical-type states.
  • James' reply (Lecture I): the origin of a state does not settle its value. Judged by fruits, a state may be valuable whatever brain process accompanies it.
  • Full assessment of these challenges.
Mission 2

The credentials of mystical experience

How do believers and philosophers test whether an experience is genuine?

  • James (Lecture I): "_Immediate luminousness_, in short, _philosophical reasonableness_, and _moral helpfulness_ are the only available criteria."
  • James on Teresa: "Saint Teresa might have had the nervous system of the placidest cow, and it would not now save her theology" if it failed these tests.
  • Teresa: genuine experiences leave humility, peace and love; they agree with Scripture and the Church; a visionary should submit to a wise confessor. False ones leave disturbance and pride.
  • Scripture: "Therefore by their fruits you will know them." (Matthew 7:20)
  • Traditions: Sufi shaykhs and Christian spiritual directors test experiences against the teaching of the community.

Mission 2Evaluate

"James' four characteristics adequately define mystical experience."

  • For: drawn from reports across many traditions; descriptive, not tied to one creed.
  • For: ineffability and noetic quality capture the mystics' paradox: they claim knowledge they cannot express.
  • Against: the marks leave out the content: union. Stace adds undifferentiated unity; Zaehner adds the theistic/monistic difference.
  • Against: passivity sits awkwardly with traditions where states are reached by long deliberate practice (Buddhist and yogic meditation), though James allows preparation beforehand.
  • Against: transiency does not fit Teresa's seventh mansion, a lasting union.
  • Against: Steven Katz ("Language, Epistemology, and Mysticism", 1978): there is no unmediated experience; each tradition's concepts shape the experience itself, so no single list fits all.
  • Judge it by: Should a definition describe experiences or their content? Can drug states that fit the marks be mystical? Is a common core real?

Mission 3

From experience to God

  • The argument from religious experience is a posteriori and inductive: from experiences, it argues that God probably exists.
  • Ordinary perception works this way: how things seem is usually good evidence of how they are.
  • People in every age and culture report experiences of God or the divine.
  • So, the argument says, the best explanation is that some of these experiences are of something real.
  • Often used in a cumulative case: with other arguments, it raises the probability of God.
Mission 3

Swinburne: credulity and testimony

  • Richard Swinburne (b. 1934), The Existence of God (1979; 2nd ed. 2004), ch. 13.
  • Principle of credulity: if it seems to someone that X is present, then probably X is present, unless there are special considerations against it.
  • Principle of testimony: without special considerations, we should believe that others' experiences are probably as they report.
  • Special considerations that defeat a claim: the person or conditions are unreliable (drugs, history of lying); similar perceptions have been shown false; strong evidence that X was absent; X, even if present, probably did not cause the experience.
  • So the burden of proof falls on the sceptic: without these principles, Swinburne argues, we could trust no perception at all ("sceptical bog").
  • Critics: God is not like a chair: we know what makes ordinary perceptions reliable, but have no agreed test for perceiving God.

Mission 3

Franks Davis: three kinds of challenge

  • Caroline Franks Davis, The Evidential Force of Religious Experience (1989).
  • Description-related: the report is inconsistent or incoherent ("I saw the invisible"), or contradicts other reports.
  • Subject-related: the person is unreliable: drugs, illness, suggestibility, strong expectation.
  • Object-related: the object is not the kind of thing that could be perceived, or is unlikely to be there (a transcendent, infinite God).
  • She argues these challenges defeat some claims, not all; in a cumulative case religious experience still has evidential force.
  • Further challenges: misunderstanding; delusion, possibly linked to substance misuse; fantastical claims against everyday experience.
  • Responses: an experience can be valid though not verifiable; the person's integrity counts; a one-off experience can be valid though never repeated.
Mission 3

Can a religious experience be verified?

  • Private: no one else can see what I saw "inwardly".
  • Unrepeatable: no experiment can recreate it on demand.
  • No agreed test: we know how to check eyesight, not "God-sight".
  • Conflicting claims: Christians report Christ, Hindus Krishna, Buddhists no God at all.
Mission 3

Challenges from science

  • Michael Persinger (1945–2018): weak magnetic fields applied to the temporal lobes (the "God helmet") led many subjects to report a sensed presence.
  • A 2005 double-blind replication (Granqvist and colleagues, Uppsala) found no effect of the fields; reports tracked personality and suggestibility.
  • Richard Dawkins (b. 1941), The God Delusion (2006): the brain constructs models of reality and can produce vivid hallucinations; a private experience is no evidence to anyone else.
  • Physiological triggers: fasting, sleep loss, epilepsy, drugs (Pahnke).
  • Psychological: wish-fulfilment (Freud); expectation shaped by religious upbringing.
Mission 3

Religious responses

  • Correlation is not causation: finding the brain activity linked to an experience does not show what caused it. Seeing a tree also involves brain activity.
  • If God made human beings, a capacity to experience God would work through the brain.
  • Swinburne: a special consideration must be shown, not assumed; the possibility of a natural cause is not enough.
  • James: origin does not settle value; judge by fruits (see Mission 2).
  • Many believers do not rest faith on experience alone: scripture, tradition and reason test experiences.
  • Critics reply: if a natural explanation is complete, God becomes an unnecessary extra (Ockham's razor).

Mission 3

Why experience matters to faith

  • Even if it proves nothing to others, experience is often what makes faith personal.
  • Value for the community: affirms the belief system; promotes the faith's values; strengthens cohesion.
  • Value for the individual: restores faith; strengthens it in the face of opposition; renews commitment.
  • Founding experiences: Muhammad's first revelation at Mount Hira (610 CE) for Muslims; Guru Nanak's experience at the river Bein (c. 1499) for Sikhs; Pentecost for Christians.
  • Renewal: Wesley's "heart strangely warmed" (1738) energised Methodism; charismatic renewal in many churches.

Mission 3Evaluate

"Religious experience provides a sound basis for belief in God."

  • For: credulity and testimony are principles we rely on everywhere; refusing them only here looks like special pleading (Swinburne).
  • For: reports are widespread, often from sane, sincere people whose lives change for the better.
  • Against: experiences cannot be checked; conflicting reports across religions suggest interpretation, not perception.
  • Against: natural explanations (brain, expectation, suggestion) are available; Dawkins: private conviction is not public evidence.
  • Against: the argument may assume what it tries to prove: only someone already open to God reads an experience as God.
  • Judge it by: Basis for whom: the experiencer or others? Proof or probability? Do natural causes rule out a divine one?

Mission 4

Two questions behind every definition

  • Every definition of miracle answers two separate questions.
  • What kind of event? A violation of natural law, or a natural event that happens at a striking moment?
  • What makes it a miracle? A real act of God in the world (realist), or the meaning the event has within a community of faith (anti-realist)?
  • Keep the two questions apart: a natural event can still be understood realistically as God's act.
Mission 4

Aquinas: beyond the usual order

  • Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274), Summa Contra Gentiles III, chapters 100–101 (c. 1259–65).
  • A miracle is done by God outside the order usually followed in nature (paraphrase). God, who made that order, is not bound by it.
  • Three degrees, ranked by how far the event exceeds nature:
  • 1st: God does what nature could never do (his examples: the sun going backwards; the sea opening a path).
  • 2nd: God does what nature can do, but not in this order (someone living after death; seeing after blindness).
  • 3rd: God does what nature does, but without natural causes (a fever cured instantly by God, though medicine could cure it).
  • A realist account: miracles are real divine acts.
Mission 4Read the source

Hume's definition

David Hume (1711–1776), An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding (1748), Section X, "Of Miracles", Part I:

> "A miracle is a violation of the laws of nature; …"

And in Hume's footnote to the same passage:

> "A miracle may be accurately defined, a transgression of a law of nature by a particular volition of the Deity, or by the interposition of some invisible agent."

Q1 What two things must be true for an event to count as a miracle on Hume's footnote definition?

Show answer

A law of nature is broken; the breaking is caused by God's will or by an invisible agent.

Q2 Would Aquinas' third degree count as a miracle on Hume's definition?

Show answer

Arguably not: the cure is something nature does anyway, so no law is clearly broken.

  • Eduqas wording: Hume, "transgression of a law of nature".

Mission 4

Holland: the contingency miracle

  • R. F. Holland (1923–2013), "The Miraculous", American Philosophical Quarterly (1965).
  • His example (paraphrase): a child's toy car is stuck on a level crossing. A train comes round a bend. The driver faints, for medical reasons; the brakes apply automatically; the train stops a few feet from the child. The mother thanks God.
  • Every step has a natural explanation. No law is broken.
  • Yet the mother calls it a miracle, and Holland says she may: a contingency miracle is a remarkable, beneficial coincidence that is taken religiously as a sign.
  • So a miracle can be a natural event: what matters is its timing and what it means.
  • Eduqas wording: Holland, "contingency miracle".
Mission 4

Swinburne: religious significance

  • Richard Swinburne (b. 1934), The Concept of Miracle (1970).
  • A miracle is an event of an extraordinary kind, "brought about by a god, and of religious significance".
  • Religious significance: an event that God causes for no purpose would not be a miracle. It must show something about God or answer prayer.
  • He keeps a violation model, but re-defines it: a non-repeatable counter-instance to a law of nature (an event that breaks a law but gives no reason to rewrite it). Defended against Hume in Mission 5.
  • Eduqas wording: Swinburne, "religious significance".
  • A realist account: miracles are real acts of God, and they mean something.
Mission 4Read the source

Miracles in the Bible

  • The Bible has no single word for "miracle". The New Testament uses three Greek words: sēmeia (signs), terata (wonders), dunameis (mighty works, acts of power).

> "Jesus of Nazareth, a man approved by God to you by mighty works and wonders and signs which God did by him amongst you" (Acts 2:22, WEBBE)

> "This beginning of his signs Jesus did in Cana of Galilee, and revealed his glory; and his disciples believed in him." (John 2:11, WEBBE)

  • The point is meaning, not law-breaking: a sign points to who Jesus is and calls for faith.
  • In the Hebrew Bible, God's acts in the Exodus are "signs and wonders" that show his power and his covenant with Israel.

Q1 What does the word "signs" suggest a miracle is for?

Show answer

To reveal something: here, Jesus' glory, leading to faith.

Q2 Which modern definition is closest to John's idea of a sign?

Show answer

Swinburne's "religious significance"; Holland too stresses meaning.

Mission 4

Realist and anti-realist

  • Realist: "A miracle happened" is true if God really caused the event, whether or not anyone believes it. Aquinas, Hume's definition and Swinburne are realist accounts.
  • Anti-realist: "miracle" expresses how a community of faith sees an event; its truth lies in that way of seeing and living, not in a hidden extra cause.
  • Holland's account is often read as a step towards anti-realism: the event is natural; its being a miracle depends on its being taken religiously. But a believer who takes it so may still hold that God really acted.
  • Critics of anti-realism: most believers mean that God really acted; a miracle that is only a way of seeing may not be what they claim.
  • Anti-realism does not call miracle stories lies: it says miracle-talk is not a claim about an extra cause in the world.

Mission 4

Why believers accept that miracles occur

  • Sacred writings: miracle accounts in scripture (e.g. the Exodus, Jesus' healings, the resurrection) are taken as reliable testimony.
  • Faith traditions affirm them: e.g. the Catholic Church investigates and recognises some healings, for example at Lourdes, and requires verified miracles in most canonisations. Many Pentecostal churches expect healing today.
  • Personal experience: answered prayer and religious experience make miracles seem credible from the inside.
  • Different Christians weigh these differently: many Catholic, Orthodox and evangelical Christians accept physical miracles; some liberal Protestants read many accounts as symbolic.
Mission 4Evaluate

Do the definitions contradict each other?

  • Claim: The definitions of miracle contradict each other, so the word has no single meaning.
  • For: Hume requires a broken law; Holland says no law need be broken. Both cannot be the necessary condition.
  • For: Realist and anti-realist views disagree about whether a miracle-claim says anything about the world.
  • Against: They answer different questions: Hume and Aquinas define the event; Holland and Swinburne stress its meaning.
  • Against: Swinburne combines both: an extraordinary event and religious significance.
  • Judgement criteria: Does a definition fit how believers use the word? Does it fit the biblical idea of a sign? Can it be tested? Does it make God's action credible?
  • Spec issues: "the adequacy of different definitions"; "how far … contradictory".

Mission 5

Hume: weigh the evidence

  • David Hume (1711–1776), Scottish empiricist: knowledge comes from experience.
  • An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding (1748), Section X, Part I.
  • A wise person proportions belief to the evidence.
  • A law of nature rests on uniform experience: every observed case, without exception.
  • A miracle report sets testimony against that uniform experience.
  • So the evidence against any miracle is as strong as evidence can be.
  • Hume does not claim miracles are logically impossible. He claims it is never reasonable to believe a report of one.
Mission 5Read the source

Hume's maxim

Hume, Enquiry (1748), Section X, Part I:

> "A wise man, therefore, proportions his belief to the evidence."

> "no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous, than the fact, which it endeavours to establish"

Q1 A friend says she saw a dead man walk. What two possibilities does Hume tell you to compare?

Show answer

That she is mistaken or lying; that the dead man walked.

Q2 Which does Hume think will always be the less miraculous?

Show answer

That the witness is mistaken or lying: people often are; the dead never rise in uniform experience.

Mission 5

Hume: four practical arguments

Part II adds four points about real miracle reports:

  • Witnesses: no miracle in history is attested by enough people of good sense, education and reputation to rule out error or deceit.
  • Susceptibility: people love surprise and wonder, and enjoy passing on marvellous stories; religious zeal can make even honest people exaggerate.
  • Origins: miracle stories abound chiefly among "ignorant and barbarous nations", and fade as learning grows (Hume's phrase).
  • Contrary claims: "in matters of religion, whatever is different is contrary". Miracles of rival religions are used to prove rival systems, so their testimonies cancel out.
  • Eduqas headings: testimony-based belief; credibility of witnesses; susceptibility of belief; contradictory nature of faith claims.

Mission 5

Swinburne replies to Hume

  • Richard Swinburne (b. 1934), The Concept of Miracle (1970); The Existence of God (1979).
  • Laws of nature: a law describes what normally happens. A non-repeatable counter-instance breaks a law without giving any reason to revise it. So a violation is coherent.
  • More kinds of evidence than testimony: our own memories, others' testimony, physical traces (e.g. medical records), and our understanding of what is physically possible.
  • Principle of testimony: believe what others report unless there is good reason not to.
  • Witnesses: Hume's standards (a "sufficient number" of witnesses of "education"; "barbarous nations") are vague or prejudiced.
  • Contrary claims: reports cancel out only if each miracle supports a doctrine that contradicts the other's. Many miracle reports support no rival doctrine at all.
Mission 5Evaluate

Hume or Swinburne?

  • Claim: Swinburne's responses defeat Hume's challenge.
  • For: Hume's Part II standards are vague and culturally biased; physical traces and memories add evidence Hume ignores.
  • For: Hume's "uniform experience" assumes no miracle has ever happened: arguably circular.
  • Against: Hume weighs probabilities: even good testimony is far more often wrong than nature is irregular.
  • Against: "Non-repeatable" events cannot be re-tested, so how could anyone show a law was broken rather than just not yet understood?
  • Judgement criteria: Is belief about the past ever certain? Who carries the burden of proof? Does background belief in God change the probabilities?
  • Spec issue: "the extent to which Swinburne's responses to Hume can be accepted as valid".

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A religious experience is taken by the person to be an encounter with God or ultimate reality; types include visions, conversion, prayer, numinous and mystical experience.
  • Visions: corporeal/sensory, imaginative, intellectual; dreams. Teresa's four waters move from effort to union.
  • Otto's numinous: mysterium tremendum et fascinans before the wholly other.

Mission 2

  • James' four marks: ineffable, noetic (the two essentials), transient, passive. Mystical states are authoritative for the subject, not binding on others.
  • Stace: extrovertive and introvertive types; a common core, interpreted differently. Zaehner: nature, monistic and theistic mysticism.
  • Union, illusion or physiological effect; tested by fruits (James, Teresa, Matthew 7:20).

Mission 3

  • The argument from religious experience is inductive: how things seem is usually how they are (Swinburne's credulity and testimony).
  • Challenges: unverifiable, conflicting, naturally explicable (Persinger, Dawkins). Responses: correlation is not causation; judge by fruits.
  • Franks Davis: description-, subject- and object-related challenges defeat some claims, not all.

Mission 4

  • Definitions answer two questions: what kind of event (violation or natural event) and what makes it a miracle (a real act of God, or a community's way of seeing).
  • Aquinas (three degrees beyond the usual order), Hume (transgression of a law of nature) and Swinburne (extraordinary, caused by God, religiously significant) are realist; Holland's contingency miracle is a natural coincidence taken religiously.
  • The Bible speaks of signs, wonders and mighty works: events that point to God and call for faith.

Mission 5

  • Hume: a wise person proportions belief to evidence; uniform experience always outweighs testimony to a miracle; Part II adds witnesses, susceptibility, origins and contrary claims.
  • Swinburne: violations are coherent (non-repeatable counter-instances); more evidence than testimony; contrary claims need not cancel.
