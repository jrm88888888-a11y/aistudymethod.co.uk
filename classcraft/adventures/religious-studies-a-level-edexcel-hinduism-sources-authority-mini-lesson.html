Mission 1

A religion without a starting point

  • Hinduism has no founder, no single moment of origin and no single founding text.
  • Many Hindus call it sanatana dharma: the "eternal" way, without beginning.
  • Historians look at two kinds of evidence:
  • Archaeology: the cities of the Indus Valley civilisation.
  • Texts: the Rig Veda, the oldest Hindu scripture, composed orally in an early form of Sanskrit.
  • The hard question: how, if at all, do the two connect?
Mission 1

The Indus Valley civilisation

  • Flourished along the Indus and the Ghaggar-Hakra rivers (today Pakistan and north-west India); mature phase c. 2600–1900 BCE.
  • Rediscovered in the 1920s: Harappa (excavated 1921) and Mohenjo-daro (1922); surveys led by John Marshall of the Archaeological Survey of India.
  • Other sites: Dholavira, Lothal, Kalibangan, Rakhigarhi.
  • Features: planned streets on a grid, covered drains, standard-sized bricks and weights, granaries or large halls, trade with Mesopotamia.
  • A script on thousands of small stamp seals: still undeciphered.
  • No palaces or obvious temples have been found.
Mission 1

Evidence of religion

Each find is real; each interpretation is debated.

  • The Great Bath (Mohenjo-daro): a watertight brick pool. Perhaps ritual bathing for purity, as at later temple tanks.
  • The "Pashupati" seal: a horned figure seated cross-legged, surrounded by animals. Marshall called it a "proto-Shiva", as lord of animals and a yogi.
  • Female figurines: terracotta figures, often read as mother goddesses, linked to later Devi worship.
  • Fire altars (Kalibangan, Lothal): pits with ash, perhaps for offerings.
  • Other symbols: the svastika, pipal leaves, conical stones once read as lingams.
Mission 1

Decline

  • From c. 1900 BCE the great cities shrank; people moved to smaller villages east and south.
  • Likely causes, debated: rivers shifting or drying (the Ghaggar-Hakra), a weaker monsoon, declining trade.
  • Mortimer Wheeler (1890–1976) once blamed invading Aryans for a massacre at Mohenjo-daro.
  • Later study showed the skeletons were buried at different times, not killed in one attack. Most archaeologists now reject the massacre idea.
  • Decline was gradual: much village culture, crafts and perhaps beliefs continued.

Mission 1

Who were the "Aryans"?

  • Arya ("noble") is what the people of the Rig Veda called themselves.
  • It names a language and culture, not a race. They spoke Vedic Sanskrit, related to Old Persian, Greek and Latin (the Indo-European family).
  • Their culture, as the hymns describe it: cattle herding, horses and chariots, fire sacrifice (yajna), the sacred drink soma, gods such as Agni and Indra.
  • The Rig Veda is usually dated c. 1500–1200 BCE: after the Indus cities declined.
  • 19th- and 20th-century writers misused "Aryan" as a racial label; scholars reject that use.

Mission 1

What the named scholars say

  • J Brockington (John Brockington, The Sacred Thread, 1981), paraphrase: Hinduism is best seen as a continuous development that absorbed many sources: Vedic religion and also older, non-Vedic and local traditions, perhaps including Indus ones. Continuity and change go together.
  • W O'Flaherty (Wendy Doniger O'Flaherty, b. 1940, The Rig Veda: An Anthology, 1981), paraphrase: the hymns reflect Indo-Aryan migrants who met and mingled with peoples already in India; much later Hinduism arose from that meeting.
  • Both treat later Hinduism as a blend, not a straight line from one origin.
  • Ask of each view: what follows for later Hinduism, and what follows if the evidence stays inconclusive?

Show answer

Show answer

Mission 1

Problems with the evidence

  • Undeciphered script: we have Indus writing but cannot read it.
  • Reading backwards: seeing a "Shiva" or a "goddess" may project later Hinduism onto older objects.
  • Different kinds of evidence: archaeology without texts (Indus) versus texts without much archaeology (the Vedas).
  • Dating oral texts: the Rig Veda was memorised for centuries before being written; its date is estimated from language.
  • Genes and language: ancient DNA shows people moving, not what they believed or spoke.
  • Politics: national and religious identity is at stake, so evidence is read with strong motives.

Mission 1Evaluate

Did Hinduism begin in the Indus Valley?

  • Claim: Modern Hinduism has its roots in the Indus Valley civilisation.
  • For: Possible links: ritual bathing, a yogi-like figure, goddess figurines, fire altars, the svastika.
  • For: Hinduism absorbed many local traditions; Indus village culture continued after the cities fell.
  • Against: Without the script, every link is a guess; similar objects need not mean similar beliefs.
  • Against: The Vedas, which most Hindus treat as the highest authority, come from Indo-Aryan culture.
  • Judgement criteria: What counts as evidence of belief? Does "roots" mean one source or many? How far does inconclusive evidence matter to the many Hindus whose faith rests on the Vedas, not on archaeology?

Mission 2

The four Vedas

  • Veda means "knowledge". There are four:
  • Rig Veda: 1,028 hymns of praise in 10 books (mandalas); the oldest, c. 1500–1200 BCE.
  • Sama Veda: verses set to chant. Yajur Veda: formulas for performing sacrifice. Atharva Veda: hymns and spells for healing, protection and daily life.
  • Each Veda grew in layers: hymns (Samhitas), ritual texts (Brahmanas), "forest" texts (Aranyakas), and the philosophical Upanishads.
Mission 2

Yajna: the sacrifice

  • Yajna: offerings (ghee, grain, milk, soma, and in some rites animals) poured into a sacred fire.
  • Specialist priests: one recites hymns, one performs the actions, one chants, one oversees.
  • Purpose: to praise and feed the gods (devas), who give in return: rain, cattle, sons, long life, victory.
  • Sacrifice keeps rta, the cosmic order that holds nature and society together.
  • Vedic society: semi-nomadic, cattle-herding tribes led by a chief (rajan), with tribal assemblies. Priests and warriors held high status; the four-fold varna scheme appears only in a late hymn (Rig Veda 10.90; see Mission 3).
  • Household rites (daily fire offerings) and great public rites (lasting days) both existed.
Mission 2

Agni: the fire

  • Agni is fire, and the god in the fire. About 200 hymns are addressed to him; the Rig Veda opens with a hymn to Agni (1.1).
  • Messenger: Agni carries offerings up to the gods and brings the gods down to the sacrifice.
  • Priest of the gods: he is called the household priest and the "mouth" of the gods.
  • Present in three places: the hearth on earth, lightning in the air, the sun in the sky.
  • Every yajna depends on Agni: without fire, there is no offering.
Mission 2

Indra: king of the gods

  • Indra: the warrior god of storm and thunder, armed with the vajra (thunderbolt). About a quarter of the Rig Veda's hymns are to him.
  • Great deed (Rig Veda 1.32, paraphrase): Indra kills the serpent Vritra, who had held back the waters, and releases the rivers.
  • He leads the Aryas in battle and is strengthened by drinking soma.
  • Worshippers ask Indra for victory, rain and cattle.
  • He is a model of the Vedic warrior-chief: generous, powerful, sometimes reckless.
Mission 2

Soma: the drink and the god

  • Soma is a plant, the drink pressed from it, and a god. Book 9 of the Rig Veda (114 hymns) is wholly to Soma.
  • In the rite, stalks are pressed, the juice is filtered through wool, mixed with milk and offered; priests drink it too.
  • Effects described: inspiration, strength, visions, a sense of immortality (Rig Veda 8.48.3, paraphrase: the worshippers have drunk soma, become immortal and reached the light).
  • The plant is unknown today: suggestions include ephedra and a mushroom; no theory is agreed.
  • Later, Soma is identified with the moon, and substitute plants are used in rites.

Mission 2

Vedic and modern deities

  • Agni: still central. In weddings the couple walk around the fire with Agni as witness; the havan (fire offering) is common, and the Arya Samaj makes it its main worship.
  • Indra: now a lesser king of heaven. In the Puranas, Krishna lifts Mount Govardhan to shelter villagers from Indra's storm.
  • Soma: no longer drunk; Soma is the moon god.
  • Vishnu and Rudra-Shiva: minor in the Rig Veda, now supreme for Vaishnavas and Shaivas.
  • Goddesses: Ushas and Aditi were minor; Devi (Shakti) is supreme for Shaktas.
  • Vedic mantras are still recited in samskaras (life-cycle rites), and the Gayatri mantra daily by many Hindus.

Mission 2Evaluate

Is Vedic sacrifice still significant?

  • Claim: Vedic sacrifice is no longer significant in Hinduism.
  • For: Great public sacrifices are now rare; animal sacrifice and soma drinking have largely ended.
  • For: Later texts (the Upanishads, the Bhagavad Gita) value knowledge and devotion over ritual.
  • Against: Fire offerings (havan, homa) continue in homes, temples and samskaras; Agni witnesses every wedding.
  • Against: The idea of sacrifice is reinterpreted, not dropped: offering actions, food and devotion to God.
  • Judgement criteria: Is significance about the rite itself, or its meaning? How far does reinterpretation count as continuity?

Mission 3

Heard and remembered

  • Shruti ("that which is heard"): texts Hindus believe were heard or seen by ancient sages (rishis) in deep meditation, not composed by them.
  • Shruti = the Vedas, each with its hymns, ritual texts and Upanishads.
  • Smriti ("that which is remembered"): texts composed and handed down by humans: the epics (Ramayana, Mahabharata, including the Bhagavad Gita), the Puranas, and law books such as the Manusmriti.
  • Many Hindus believe shruti is eternal and without a human author (apaurusheya).
Mission 3

Which has more authority?

  • Traditionally, shruti has the highest authority: it is revealed, timeless knowledge.
  • Smriti is authoritative because it explains and applies shruti.
  • Classical rule (Mimamsa school): if a smriti text conflicts with shruti, shruti wins.
  • Manusmriti 2.6 (paraphrase) lists the sources of dharma in order: the Veda, then smriti, then the conduct of good people, then one's own conscience.
  • Hindu schools that accept Vedic authority are called astika ("orthodox"); Buddhists and Jains, who reject it, are nastika.
Mission 3

The Vedas in worship

  • The Vedas are mostly heard in ritual, not read: priests recite them in Sanskrit with exact sound and accent.
  • Samskaras (life-cycle rites): Vedic mantras are chanted at the sacred-thread ceremony, weddings and funerals.
  • The Gayatri mantra (Rig Veda 3.62.10) is recited daily by many Hindus.
  • Havan and temple rites use Vedic verses alongside later ritual texts.
  • Traditionally only "twice-born" men learned the Vedas; reform movements such as the Arya Samaj teach them to everyone, including women.
  • So shruti's authority is seen above all in worship ceremonies.
Mission 3

The Upanishads: the end of the Veda

  • The Upanishads are the last part of the Vedas, c. 800–200 BCE.
  • Distinctive: philosophical dialogues between teacher and pupil, not hymns or ritual rules.
  • Key teaching: Brahman (ultimate reality) and atman (the true self) and how they relate.
  • They are called Vedanta, "end of the Veda", and are the basis of the Vedanta schools (Shankara's Advaita, Ramanuja's Vishishtadvaita, Madhva's Dvaita).
  • Detail in a later mission.

Mission 3

The great smriti texts

  • Ramayana (attributed to Valmiki): Prince Rama's exile, the rescue of Sita and his rule. Rama is the ideal of dharma.
  • Mahabharata (attributed to Vyasa): the war between cousins, the Pandavas and Kauravas; duty in impossible situations.
  • Bhagavad Gita: Krishna's teaching to the warrior Arjuna, within the Mahabharata. Often treated almost as shruti.
  • Puranas: stories of the gods and avatars; the Manusmriti: rules for varna, stage of life, family and kingship.
  • Smriti is known and loved: through festivals, storytelling, temple art, drama and television.
Mission 3Read the source

The Gita on the Vedas

Bhagavad Gita 2:46–47 (Edwin Arnold's verse translation, 1885):

> "Look! like as when a tank pours water forth

> To suit all needs, so do these Brahmans draw

> Text for all wants from tank of Holy Writ.

> But thou, want not! ask not! Find full reward

> Of doing right in right! Let right deeds be

> Thy motive, not the fruit which comes from them."

  • Arnold translates freely. Most translations of 2:46 read: for one who knows, the Vedas are as much use as a well in a flood. The criticism of using the Vedas for rewards is in 2:42–45.

Q1 In Arnold's version, what do some Brahmins do with the Vedas ("Holy Writ")?

Show answer

Draw texts from them for every wish, using ritual to gain rewards.

Q2 What does he ask of Arjuna instead?

Show answer

To act rightly for its own sake, not for the fruits.

  • So a smriti text reinterprets how to use shruti: one reason the Gita is so influential.
Mission 3

Which texts are shruti? Relative authority

  • General agreement: the Vedas, including the Upanishads, are shruti.
  • Disputed: Vaishnavas treat the Pancharatra Samhitas, and Shaivas the Shaiva Agamas, as revealed; others do not. Tamil Shrivaishnavas call their hymn collection a "Tamil Veda".
  • J Brockington (The Sacred Thread, 1981), paraphrase: in practice the epics and Puranas shape Hindu life far more than the Vedas, whose authority is acknowledged more than studied.
  • W O'Flaherty (Hindu Myths, 1975), paraphrase: the living tradition is carried mainly by Puranic myths and stories, which are re-told and adapted freely.
  • So "highest authority" in theory and "greatest influence" in practice can differ.

Mission 3Evaluate

Does smriti matter more than shruti?

  • Claim: For Hindus today, smriti is more important than shruti.
  • For: The epics and Gita are widely known among Hindus; they shape festivals (Diwali, Ramnavami), ethics and devotion.
  • For: Stories teach dharma in situations people recognise.
  • Against: Shruti defines what counts as Hindu (astika) and is present in every samskara.
  • Against: Smriti's own authority rests on agreeing with shruti; the Gita itself is called an Upanishad by tradition.

Mission 4

Sitting near the teacher

  • Upanishad is often explained as "sitting down near" a teacher to receive hidden teaching.
  • The final part of the Vedas (shruti), so also called Vedanta, "end of the Veda".
  • The oldest (Brihadaranyaka, Chandogya) date from c. 700–500 BCE; others are later. Tradition counts 108; about 13 are "principal".
  • Setting: teachers (gurus) with pupils, often in forest retreats. Teachers include Yajnavalkya and Uddalaka; women such as Gargi and Maitreyi question and debate.
  • A shift: from performing outer sacrifice to gaining inner knowledge (jnana) of reality.
Mission 4

Nachiketas and Yama

  • Katha Upanishad: Vajashravas gives away worn-out cows at a great sacrifice (yajna). His son Nachiketas asks to whom he himself will be given; in anger his father replies that he gives him to Death.
  • Nachiketas waits three days at the house of Yama, god of death. As amends Yama grants three boons.
  • (1) His father's peace of mind. (2) Knowledge of the fire-sacrifice that leads to heaven. (3) What happens to a person after death.
  • Yama offers wealth, long life and pleasures instead; Nachiketas refuses, because such things are fleeting.
  • Yama's answer to the third boon: "This Self is never born, nor does It die. ... It is not slain even though the body is slain." (Katha 2:18, Paramananda)
  • Yajna: the story moves from ritual (the second boon) to knowledge of the self (the third), which ritual cannot give.
Mission 4

Brahman and atman

  • Brahman: the ultimate reality, the ground of everything, beyond all names and forms.
  • Atman: the true self in every living being, beneath body, senses and mind.
  • The Upanishads' central teaching: atman is Brahman, or at least inseparable from it. To know this is liberation (moksha).
Mission 4Read the source

The chariot

Katha Upanishad 3:3–4 (paraphrase of Paramananda): the atman is the lord of the chariot; the body is the chariot; the intellect is the driver; the mind is the reins; the senses are the horses; the objects of sense are the roads.

> "But he who is full of discrimination and whose mind is always controlled, his senses are manageable, like the good horses of a driver." (Katha 3:6)

> "This Atman (Self), hidden in all beings, does not shine forth; but It is seen by subtle seers through keen and subtle understanding." (Katha 3:12)

  • Katha 3:15 (paraphrase): knowing the soundless, formless, eternal reality, a person escapes "from the mouth of death".
  • Q: Who is the driver, and what does a good driver do? (The intellect; it uses discrimination to keep the senses under control.)
Mission 4Read the source

Salt in water

Chandogya Upanishad 6:13:1–3 (paraphrase): The teacher Uddalaka tells his son Shvetaketu to put salt in water overnight. In the morning the salt cannot be seen, yet every sip tastes salty. "In the same way," says Uddalaka, "you do not see the Real here, but it is here. That finest essence is the self of all this world. That is the Real. That is the atman. You are that (tat tvam asi), Shvetaketu."

Q1 What does the salt stand for?

Show answer

Brahman: unseen but present everywhere.

Q2 What does "you are that" claim?

Show answer

Shvetaketu's true self is one with that essence.

Mission 4

Jnana and the Vedanta schools

  • Jnana (knowledge) is realisation, not information: "This Self cannot be attained by study of the Scriptures, nor by intellectual perception" (Katha 2:23, Paramananda).
  • The path: a guru, ethical life, control of the senses, meditation (e.g. on Om).
  • The Upanishads begin the philosophical traditions of India, especially Vedanta. Its schools read the same texts differently:
  • Advaita (Shankara, c. 8th century): atman and Brahman are identical; the world of difference is maya.
  • Vishishtadvaita (Ramanuja, 11th–12th century): selves are real and distinct, yet part of Brahman, a personal God.
  • Dvaita (Madhva, 13th century): God and selves are eternally different.

Mission 5

Smriti in story form

  • Much Hindu smriti is narrative:
  • Itihasa ("so it was"): the two great epics, the Ramayana and the Mahabharata.
  • Puranas ("ancient lore"): stories of creation, the gods and the avatars.
  • They are known through recitation, festivals, temple carvings, dance and drama, comics, film and television (Ramanand Sagar's Ramayan, 1987–88; B. R. Chopra's Mahabharat, 1988–90).
  • Their aim: to show dharma (right conduct and duty) in action.
Mission 5

The Ramayana: the story

  • Attributed to the sage Valmiki: about 24,000 verses in seven books; composed over several centuries before c. 200 CE.
  • Prince Rama of Ayodhya is exiled for 14 years because of a promise his father made to a queen. His wife Sita and brother Lakshmana go with him.
  • The demon-king Ravana abducts Sita to Lanka. With the monkey Hanuman and an army, Rama defeats Ravana and rescues her.
  • Rama returns to rule Ayodhya: Rama-rajya, the ideal kingdom. Many Hindus light lamps at Diwali to welcome him home.
  • Many versions: Tulsidas's Hindi Ramcharitmanas (16th century) is the best known in north India; Tamil, Thai and other tellings differ.
Mission 5

What the Ramayana teaches

  • Rama: the ideal man (maryada purushottama), keeping dharma as son, brother, husband and king. Vaishnavas worship him as an avatar of Vishnu.
  • Sita: faithfulness, courage and strength in captivity; for many Hindus the ideal wife.
  • Lakshmana: loyalty and selfless caring for his brother.
  • Hanuman: devotion (bhakti), courage and service; a model of friendship with Rama.
  • Debates: Sita's trial by fire and later banishment are criticised by many, including feminist readers; some versions retell or omit them.

Mission 5

The Mahabharata

  • Attributed to the sage Vyasa: about 100,000 verses in 18 books, among the longest poems in the world. It calls itself a "fifth Veda".
  • Two sets of cousins: the five Pandavas and the hundred Kauravas, rivals for one kingdom.
  • The Pandavas lose everything in a rigged game of dice; their wife Draupadi is humiliated. After 13 years of exile, war follows at Kurukshetra (18 days).
  • Krishna guides the Pandavas; before the battle he teaches Arjuna: the Bhagavad Gita (book 6).
  • Key theme: doing one's duty (svadharma) when duties clash; "dharma is subtle". Good people make hard choices; victory brings grief.

Why the avatar comes

Bhagavad Gita 4:7–8 (Edwin Arnold's verse translation, 1885), Krishna speaks:

> "When Righteousness

> Declines, O Bharata! when Wickedness

> Is strong, I rise, from age to age, and take

> Visible shape, and move a man with men,

> Succouring the good, thrusting the evil back,

> And setting Virtue on her seat again."

Q1 When does the avatar come?

Show answer

Whenever righteousness, dharma, declines and wickedness grows strong.

Q2 What three things does the avatar do?

Show answer

Protects the good, pushes back evil, re-establishes dharma.

Mission 5

Avatars in the epics and Puranas

  • Avatar: a "descent" of God, especially Vishnu, into the world to restore dharma.
  • A common list of ten (dashavatara): Matsya (fish), Kurma (tortoise), Varaha (boar), Narasimha (man-lion), Vamana (dwarf), Parashurama, Rama, Krishna, the Buddha or Balarama, and Kalki (still to come).
  • The epics give the two most loved: Rama (Ramayana) and Krishna (Mahabharata, and his childhood in the Puranas).
  • Lists vary between texts and regions; Vaishnavas often see Krishna as the source of all avatars, not one of them.
Mission 5

The Puranas

  • Eighteen major (Maha) Puranas, plus many minor ones, composed c. 300–1000 CE and later.
  • Traditionally said to cover five topics: creation, re-creation, genealogies of gods and sages, cosmic ages, and royal dynasties.
  • Each tends to honour one deity: e.g. the Bhagavata Purana (Krishna, bhakti), the Shiva Purana, the Devi Mahatmya within the Markandeya Purana (the Goddess).
  • Ethical teaching through story: the child Prahlada stays devoted to Vishnu despite his father; Narasimha protects him. The lesson: faith and dharma win over tyranny.
  • The Bhagavata Purana (paraphrase, 1.4.25) says the Mahabharata was composed for women, Shudras and others who could not hear the Vedas: stories open dharma to everyone.
Mission 5

Why narrative?

  • Accessible: anyone can hear a story; the Vedas were traditionally restricted.
  • Realistic: stories show dharma in conflict (Arjuna's duty as warrior vs. love of family), which rules alone cannot.
  • Memorable and shared: performed at festivals (Ramlila at Dussehra), retold in every language.
  • Open to interpretation: many versions let each community find meaning; but stories can also be used to support caste or gender roles.
  • J Brockington (The Sanskrit Epics, 1998), paraphrase: the epics grew over centuries, with layers that show changing religious ideas, especially the growth of devotion to Vishnu as Rama and Krishna.
  • W O'Flaherty (Hindu Myths, 1975), paraphrase: Hindu myths are many-versioned and often paradoxical; their power lies in holding tensions, not in a single moral.

Mission 5Evaluate

Stories or rules?

  • Claim: The epics and Puranas are a better guide to dharma than law books such as the Manusmriti.
  • For: They are widely known among Hindus; they show dharma in real dilemmas; they inspire devotion.
  • For: Their many versions allow adaptation to new times.
  • Against: Stories can be ambiguous: the same episode can justify opposite actions.
  • Against: Some episodes reflect ancient attitudes to women and caste that many Hindus now question; rules can be reformed more openly.
  • Judgement criteria: Is the aim to know the rule, or to become a good person? Who interprets the story (gurus, families, the community of believers)?

Mission 6: "Hinduism" is a word outsiders coined. Is it one religion, many religions, or a way of life?

Make a guess: "Hinduism" is a word outsiders coined. Is it one religion, many religions, or a way of life?

Mission 6

Where the word came from

  • Sindhu is the Sanskrit name of the Indus river.
  • Persians said Hindu: the land and people beyond the Indus. It was a geographical name, not a religious one.
  • Muslim rulers in India later used "Hindu" for Indians who were not Muslim.
  • "Hinduism" (early spelling "Hindooism") came into English around 1800, used by British writers and also by Indian reformers.
  • Before that, people named their own traditions: devotees of Vishnu or Shiva, followers of a teacher, the Vedic way.
  • So the word groups together traditions that did not always see themselves as one.
Mission 6

Sanatana and sadharana dharma

  • Dharma: the order that upholds the universe, and the duty and right conduct that follow from it.
  • Sanatana dharma: the eternal dharma. Many Hindus prefer it to "Hinduism": it has no founder and no beginning.
  • Sadharana dharma: universal duties for everyone: non-violence, truthfulness, not stealing, purity, self-control (e.g. Manusmriti 10.63, paraphrase).
  • These contrast with duties that depend on one's place in society and stage of life (varnashrama dharma).
Mission 6

The Hindu renaissance and neo-Hinduism

  • 19th-century reformers responded to British rule and Christian missions by presenting Hinduism as one rational, universal religion.
  • Ram Mohan Roy (1772–1833), Brahmo Samaj (1828): one formless God, from the Upanishads; against image worship and sati.
  • Dayananda Saraswati (1824–1883), Arya Samaj (1875): back to the Vedas alone.
  • Swami Vivekananda (1863–1902): Chicago Parliament of Religions (1893): Hinduism as Vedanta, tolerant, accepting all religions as true paths.
  • Neo-Hinduism (term used by Paul Hacker, 1913–1979): the modern, unified, Vedanta-centred Hinduism of these reformers.
  • Critics: it put Advaita Vedanta at the centre and shaped "Hinduism" for Western audiences. Supporters: it renewed genuine Hindu sources.

Mission 6Diversity

One religion or many?

What differs:

  • Deities: Vaishnavas (Vishnu and his avatars), Shaivas (Shiva), Shaktas (the Goddess), Smartas (several deities as forms of one Brahman).
  • Philosophy: non-dualist (Advaita), qualified non-dualist, dualist (Dvaita).
  • Practice: regional festivals, caste customs, temple or home worship, ascetic or family life.
  • No single founder, creed, scripture or authority.

What is shared (by most):

  • Respect for the Vedas; ideas of karma, samsara, moksha, dharma; puja, pilgrimage, samskaras.
  • Images used by scholars: a family of traditions; a banyan tree with many trunks (Julius Lipner).
Mission 6

Is "religion" the right word?

  • The modern idea of "religion" grew from Christian (and Jewish) history: a creed, a founder, a scripture, a membership, and a line between sacred and secular.
  • Wilfred Cantwell Smith (1916–2000), The Meaning and End of Religion (1962), paraphrase: "religion" turns living faith into a thing; "Hinduism" is an especially misleading example, since Hindus did not see themselves as one system.
  • Dharma is wider than "religion": it covers law, ethics, ritual, social duty and cosmic order.
  • Defenders reply: every concept is imperfect; "Hinduism" still names real, shared features.
Mission 6

So many scriptures

  • Hindu scriptures range from the Vedas and Upanishads to the epics, Puranas, Agamas, Tantras and vernacular devotional poetry.
  • No single text is read by all Hindus or binding on all.
  • J Brockington (John Brockington, The Sacred Thread, 1981), paraphrase: Hinduism has changed and grown continuously, adding new texts without discarding old ones; its unity is that of a developing tradition.
  • W O'Flaherty (Wendy Doniger O'Flaherty, Hindu Myths, 1975), paraphrase: Hindu myths exist in many versions that sometimes contradict each other; the tradition tolerates multiple tellings rather than one orthodox account.
  • Implication: understanding Hinduism means studying many sources and communities, not one canon.

Mission 6

Hinduism as a world religion

  • World religion model: Hinduism listed alongside Christianity, Islam and Buddhism in textbooks, censuses and interfaith events.
  • Encouraged by Vivekananda's Chicago speech (1893), migration (Hindu communities in the UK, USA, Caribbean, East Africa), and movements such as ISKCON and the Ramakrishna Mission.
  • For the model: gives Hindus a shared identity, voice in interfaith dialogue and legal recognition.
  • Against: imposes a Christian-shaped template; favours the Vedanta and Brahmin version over local and lower-caste practice.
  • A political edge: Hindutva (V. D. Savarkar, Essentials of Hindutva, 1923) treats Hindu identity as national and cultural; neo-Vedanta treats it as universal and spiritual.
Mission 6Evaluate

Is Hinduism one religion?

  • Claim: "Hinduism" names a single religion.
  • For: Shared ideas (karma, samsara, dharma, moksha) and respect for the Vedas link almost all Hindus.
  • For: Hindus themselves use the name; the idea of sanatana dharma gives an inner unity.
  • Against: The word came from outsiders; deities, philosophies and goals differ greatly.
  • Against: "Religion" assumes a founder, creed and canon that Hinduism lacks.
  • Judgement criteria: What makes a religion "one": a founder, a creed, shared practices, or self-identity? Who decides: scholars, reformers or ordinary Hindus?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Hinduism has no founder or start date; historians use Indus archaeology and the Rig Veda.
  • Indus finds (Great Bath, "Pashupati" seal, figurines, fire altars) suggest religion, but the script is unread and every link is an interpretation.
  • Invasion is largely abandoned; migration is the majority academic view; continuity is held by some Indian scholars and many Hindus.
  • Brockington and O'Flaherty see later Hinduism as a blend of Vedic and older traditions.

Mission 2

  • The four Vedas (Rig, Sama, Yajur, Atharva) were memorised and recited long before they were written.
  • Yajna (fire sacrifice) kept rta; Agni carried offerings, Indra won battles and released the waters, soma inspired.
  • Agni and Vedic mantras remain central to ritual; Vishnu and Shiva rose from minor Vedic gods to supreme deities.

Mission 3

  • Shruti ("heard": the Vedas, including the Upanishads) has the highest authority; smriti ("remembered": epics, Gita, Puranas, Manusmriti) applies it.
  • The Vedas live mainly in ritual recitation; smriti is better known and shapes festivals and ethics.
  • Which texts are shruti is disputed (Agamas, Pancharatra); influence and authority can differ.

Mission 4

  • The Upanishads are the end of the Vedas (Vedanta): teachings of gurus on Brahman and atman, moving from outer sacrifice to inner knowledge.
  • The Katha Upanishad: Nachiketas learns from Yama; the chariot image (Katha 3) shows the intellect controlling the senses.
  • "You are that" (Chandogya 6:13) is read as identity (Advaita), part and whole (Vishishtadvaita), or difference (Dvaita).

Mission 5

  • The epics (itihasa) and Puranas are smriti in story form, widely known among Hindus.
  • The Ramayana shows ideal dharma in Rama, Sita, Lakshmana and Hanuman; the Mahabharata shows duty in conflict, and contains the Gita.
  • The avatar descends "from age to age" when dharma declines (Gita 4:7–8).
  • The Puranas teach through stories of the gods and avatars; Brockington and O'Flaherty stress the epics' growth and the many versions of myth.

Mission 6

  • "Hindu" began as a Persian geographical name; "Hinduism" entered English around 1800. Many Hindus prefer sanatana dharma.
  • Hinduism has no single founder, creed or scripture; scholars speak of a family of traditions with shared ideas.
  • 19th-century reformers (Roy, Dayananda, Vivekananda) presented a unified "neo-Hinduism"; critics question whether "religion" fits.
  • Brockington and O'Flaherty show a growing, many-versioned scriptural tradition.
