6.1 · Defining urbanisation

What "urbanisation" means

Urbanisation is the increase in the proportion of people living in towns and cities rather than in the countryside. In 2007 the world passed a milestone — for the first time, more than half of all people lived in urban areas.

  • An urban area is a built-up town or city; a rural area is countryside.
  • Urbanisation happens through rural–urban migration (people moving in) and natural increase (more births than deaths in the city).

Key idea: urbanisation is about the share of people who are urban, not just the total number. A country can be highly urbanised even if its cities are not the biggest in the world.

6.1 · Global trends

Where urbanisation is fastest now

Rates of urbanisation are very different in richer and poorer parts of the world:

  • Developed countries (e.g. the UK, USA, Japan) urbanised early, during the Industrial Revolution. They are already highly urban (often 80%+), so today they grow slowly.
  • Developing & emerging countries (e.g. Nigeria, India, Brazil) are urbanising rapidly right now — this is where the fastest growth is happening.

Common mistake: people think Europe and North America are urbanising fastest. In fact their rates are now low — the fastest urbanisation today is in Africa and Asia.

6.1 · Megacities & world cities

Megacities and world cities

  • A megacity is a city with a population of 10 million or more people. In 1975 there were only a few; today there are 30+, and most new ones are in developing and emerging countries.
  • A world city (or global city) has worldwide influence — a hub for finance, trade, culture and decision-making, e.g. London, New York, Tokyo.

6.1 · Land use

What shapes urban land use

Different parts of a city are used for different things. Three factors decide who ends up where:

  • Accessibility — the centre is easiest to reach, so shops and offices cluster there.
  • Land value — central land is scarce and expensive, so it is used intensively (high-rise). Land gets cheaper towards the edge.
  • Locational needs — factories want cheaper, flatter land and transport links; housing wants space and quiet.

Key idea: the bid-rent effect — activities that can afford the most (offices, shops) outbid others for the accessible, high-value centre.

6.1 · Urban structure

The Burgess concentric model

The Burgess model describes a city as a set of rings growing outwards from the centre. Buildings get newer and lower-density towards the edge.

Remember the zones: the CBD is the shops-and-offices core; the inner city is older, denser housing near the centre; suburbs are newer, lower-density housing; the fringe is the countryside edge.

6.2 · Challenges

Problems of rapid urban growth

When cities grow faster than housing, jobs and services can be provided, serious problems appear — especially in developing-country cities:

  • Squatter settlements (slums / shanty towns) — self-built homes on land people don't own, often lacking clean water, sewers and electricity.
  • Informal economy — unregulated, insecure jobs (street selling, waste-picking) with no contract or tax.
  • Under-provision of services — not enough schools, clinics, clean water or waste collection.
  • Traffic congestion and air & water pollution from too many vehicles and untreated waste.

Watch out: a squatter settlement is not the same as planned housing. It is built informally, without permission or proper infrastructure — that's exactly why services are missing.

6.2 · Developed-city challenges

Challenges in developed-world cities

Rich cities face problems too, though they are often different in kind:

  • Inner-city decline — older industrial areas lose factories and jobs (de-industrialisation).
  • Urban sprawl — the city spreads onto the countryside at the rural–urban fringe.
  • Traffic congestion & air pollution from commuting.
  • Housing shortages and high prices; inequality between rich and poor districts.

Contrast: developing-city problems centre on rapid growth outpacing services (slums, informal work); developed-city problems centre on decline, sprawl and cost.

6.3 · Management

Building a sustainable city

A sustainable city meets the needs of people today without harming the ability of future generations to meet theirs. Planners aim to cut resource use, waste and pollution:

  • Reduce car use — invest in buses, trams, cycle lanes and congestion charges.
  • Manage waste & water — recycling schemes and clean water for all.
  • Upgrade slums — "site-and-service" schemes give residents legal land, water and sanitation instead of demolishing homes.

Case study · developing country

Mumbai & Dharavi, India

Mumbai (India) is a fast-growing megacity of around 20 million people and India's financial capital. Rapid rural–urban migration has driven huge growth.

  • Dharavi is one of Asia's largest squatter settlements (slums) — roughly 1 million people packed into about 2 km².
  • It has a huge informal economy — recycling, pottery, leather and textiles — turning over hundreds of millions of dollars a year.
  • Problems: overcrowding, poor sanitation, shared toilets, and limited clean water.
  • Management: slum-upgrading and redevelopment schemes aim to add water, sanitation and better housing.

Use it as evidence: Dharavi shows rapid urbanisation producing a slum and a thriving informal economy at the same time.

Case study · developed country

London, United Kingdom

London (UK) is a world city of around 9 million people — a global hub for finance, government, culture and transport.

  • Challenges: traffic congestion, air pollution, high house prices, and inequality between wealthy and deprived boroughs.
  • Management & sustainability: the Congestion Charge (2003) and Ultra Low Emission Zone (ULEZ) cut traffic and pollution.
  • Cleaner transport — the Underground, buses, cycle "superhighways" and hire bikes reduce car use.
  • Regeneration — schemes such as the 2012 Olympic Park in Stratford renewed run-down inner-city land.

Compare the two: Mumbai battles slums and informal work from rapid growth; London tackles congestion, pollution and cost — the classic developing-vs-developed contrast the exam wants.

6.1 · Later stages

What happens after urbanisation

Once a country is highly urban, movement can reverse or spread out:

  • Suburbanisation — people move from the crowded inner city to lower-density suburbs on the edge, often as car use and rail links grow.
  • Counter-urbanisation — people move out of the city entirely to towns and villages in the countryside, seeking space and a quieter life (common in developed countries).
  • Re-urbanisation — later, regeneration draws people back into renewed inner-city areas.

Direction check: urbanisation and suburbanisation still add to the urban share; counter-urbanisation moves people the other way, back to rural areas.
