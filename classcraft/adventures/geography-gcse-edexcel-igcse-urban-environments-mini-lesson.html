Make a guess: In 1979 Shenzhen was what its government calls "a small backward border town". By 2024 it had 17.98 million residents. Where did they all come from?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 6.1a contrasting urbanisation trends over 50 years worldwide, incl. suburbanisation and counter-urbanisation
  • 6.1b factors affecting the rate of urbanisation; emergence of megacities
Mission 1

Urbanisation

  • Urbanisation: a rising share of a country's people living in towns and cities.
  • Urban growth: a rising number of urban people.
  • Cities grow in three ways:
    • natural increase: more births than deaths;
    • migration into the city;
    • reclassification: villages and towns grow big enough to be counted as part of a city.
Mission 1

The world since 1950

  • 1950: 20% of the world's 2.5 billion people lived in cities.
  • 2025: 45% of 8.2 billion lived in cities and 36% in towns.
  • Using each country's own definition of "urban", the world's urban share rose from 37.4% in 1974 to 57.6% in 2024.
Mission 1

Contrasting trends, 1974 to 2024

Urban share of the population, by income group:

  • High income: 69.2% → 80.1%
  • Upper-middle income: 28.8% → 67.8%
  • Lower-middle income: 22.9% → 41.6%
  • Low income: 18.6% → 34.7%
Mission 1

Different times, different speeds

  • High-income countries urbanised first, with industrialisation. On the UN's new city definition, their city populations passed their rural populations by 1957.
  • Low-income countries started later: on the UN definition they reached urban–rural parity only in 2017. (On national definitions, they are still mostly rural.)
  • Urban population growth in 2024:
    • low income 3.99% a year · lower-middle 2.27% · upper-middle 0.90% · high income 0.84%.
  • Fast growth comes from high birth rates and migration; slow growth where most people already live in cities.
  • In some high-income countries people later moved out of cities, to suburbs (suburbanisation) or the countryside (counter-urbanisation); parts of Europe saw city populations fall.
Quick check
?From 1974 to 2024, the urban share rose by 10.9 percentage points in high-income countries but by 39.0 in upper-middle-income countries. Why the difference?
Mission 1

Why people move to cities

  • Push (rural): poverty, few jobs, mechanised farming, poor schools and clinics.
  • Pull (city): jobs, higher wages, education, health care, bright lights.
  • Natural increase: young people are the most likely to move to cities, so cities gain births as well as migrants.
Mission 1

Shenzhen

  • Established in March 1979; China's first special economic zone from August 1980.
  • 17.98 million permanent residents at the end of 2024.
  • Its government calls it "a young immigrant city": average age 32.5 at the seventh national census.
Mission 1

China: what drove city growth?

  • China's urban share (national definition): 20.6% in 1982 → 63.8% in 2020.
  • Of China's urban growth between the 2010 and 2020 censuses:
    • 56% rural-to-urban migration
    • 26% administrative changes (reclassification)
    • 18% natural increase.
  • Worldwide, natural increase and reclassification drive most city growth; migration matters most in Eastern and South-Eastern Asia and sub-Saharan Africa.
Quick check
?Use the figures earlier in this mission. What share of China's urban growth in 2010–2020 did not come from rural-to-urban migration?
Mission 1

Megacities

  • Megacity: a city of 10 million people or more.
  • 8 megacities in 1975 → 33 in 2025 → 37 projected by 2050.
  • 2025: 19 in Asia (India 5, China 4), 5 in Latin America, 4 in Africa, 3 in Europe, 2 in Northern America.
  • Largest in 2025: Jakarta (41.9 million), Dhaka (36.6 million), Tokyo (33.4 million).
  • They emerge where big countries urbanise fast: "Most megacities are located in the global South".
Mission check

?Between 1975 and 2025, Ghana's total population grew 3.5 times, but its city population grew 13.5 times, from just under 2 million to 27 million. Where did most of the extra city dwellers come from?

When cities grow much faster than the whole country, people must be moving in from rural areas and towns. In China, 56% of urban growth in 2010–2020 came from rural-to-urban migration; Shenzhen calls itself "a young immigrant city".

Make a guess: Detroit had 1,849,568 people in 1950 and 639,111 in 2020. What emptied the Motor City?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 6.1a contrasting urbanisation trends over 50 years worldwide, incl. suburbanisation and counter-urbanisation
Mission 2

The Motor City empties

  • Detroit, USA, the home of the American car industry, had 1,849,568 people in 1950 and 639,111 in 2020.
  • Historian Thomas Sugrue told the Associated Press that Detroit lost 140,000 jobs between 1947 and 1963.
  • Factories closed or moved out; people who could, often the better-off, moved to the suburbs and beyond.
  • Jobs left the city, and people followed.
Mission 2

Four stages of urban change

  • Urbanisation: people move into the city; it grows.
  • Suburbanisation: people move from the inner city to the suburbs at its edge.
  • Counter-urbanisation: people move beyond the city to small towns and villages.
  • Re-urbanisation: people move back into the inner city.
Mission 2

London's population story

Census counts and estimates for London:

  • Urbanisation: 1.1 million (1801) → 8.6 million (1939).
  • Inner and outer London: inner London fell from 4.90 million (1901) to 2.43 million (1981), while outer London grew from 1.61 million (1901) to 4.52 million (1951).
  • Decline and counter-urbanisation: Greater London fell from 8.62 million (1939) to 6.61 million (1981): down 23% (our calculation).
  • Re-urbanisation: back up to 8.80 million (2021); inner London grew 40% from 1981 (our calculation).
Quick check
?Between 1901 and 1951, inner London's population fell while outer London's nearly tripled. Which process does this show?
Mission 2

Why people moved out, and back

  • Suburbanisation: trams, railways and then cars; cheaper land; space and gardens; clearance of crowded inner-city housing.
  • Counter-urbanisation: quieter, cleaner places; retirement; better roads and remote working let people commute or work from home.
  • Re-urbanisation: regeneration of old docks and factories; service jobs in the centre; students and young professionals; international migrants.
Mission 2

Deindustrialisation's impact on London

  • Jobs: manufacturing jobs fell nationally from the 1960s, including in London's factories and docks.
  • People: as factory and dock jobs went, more people left; Greater London's population kept falling until 1981. (The fall began in 1939, so war and planned moves to new towns played a part too.)
  • Deprivation: England's 2025 deprivation index still finds concentrations in areas with a history of heavy industry and in "parts of east London".
  • Land: closed docks and factories left derelict land, later reused for offices and housing (re-urbanisation).
Mission check

?Greater London lost about 2 million residents between 1939 and 1981, then gained about 2.2 million by 2021. What best explains the loss?

In Detroit, as in London, jobs left the city (deindustrialisation) and people followed them outwards (suburbanisation and beyond). Detroit lost about 65% of its 1950 population by 2020; London lost about 23% by 1981, then grew again as it re-urbanised.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Mumbai keeps drawing people in from across India. Who really gains from its growth?

Make a guess: Mumbai keeps drawing people in from across India. Who really gains from its growth?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 6.1c problems of rapid urbanisation: congestion, transport, employment, crime, environment
Mission 3

Where is Mumbai?

  • Site: on India's west coast, on the Arabian Sea; built on a group of former islands, with a sheltered natural harbour.
  • Situation: capital of Maharashtra state; India's main gateway to the Arabian Sea and the Gulf.
  • India is lower-middle income.
Mission 3

Why Mumbai matters

  • Global: a world city at the Alpha level, linked to firms worldwide (GaWC, 2024). In the 19th century it became "a global trading centre".
  • National: home of India's central bank; the Reserve Bank's central office "permanently moved to Mumbai in 1937".
  • Regional: the main centre for jobs, ports, trade and services in Maharashtra and the wider city-region.
Mission 3

How big, and how fast?

  • UN, same boundary method each year:
    • 2000: 16.0 million · 2025: 20.2 million · 2050 (projected): 23.1 million.
    • 12th-largest city in the world in 2025; growth 0.94% a year (2000–25).
  • Census 2011, Greater Mumbai's municipal area: 12,442,373 people.
  • Different boundaries, different totals: always name the source.
  • It is the world's densest city of a million or more: 27,000 people per km².
Mission 3

Why it grows

  • Natural increase: births exceed deaths in a young population.
  • Rural–urban migration from across India:
    • Push: low farm incomes, few jobs, poor services in rural areas.
    • Pull: jobs, wages, schools, hospitals in the city.
  • Since the 1960s M/East ward "has given shelter to migrants from different parts of India".
Mission 3

A clue in the census

  • Greater Mumbai, 2011: 6,715,931 males and 5,726,442 females.
  • That is about 853 females per 1,000 males (our calculation).
  • Many migrants are young men who come alone to work, sending money home.
Quick check
?A young man leaves his village in Bihar because farm work pays too little, and takes a job on a Mumbai building site. Which factors are at work?
Mission 3

Opportunities

  • Economic: jobs in finance, trade, ports, film, manufacturing and services; urban industry stimulates growth across Maharashtra.
  • Social: more schools and hospitals than in most rural areas. In 2011, about 90% of Greater Mumbai's people aged 7+ could read and write (calculated from census).
  • Resources: piped water and electricity reach far more homes than in villages.
  • For migrants, wages sent home help rural families.
Mission 3

Challenge: housing

  • Greater Mumbai, Census 2011 (municipal tables): 6,534,460 of 12,442,373 people lived in slums: 52.5% (our calculation).
  • By the census definitions used by Praja Foundation the figure is 5,206,473, or 41.84%.
  • Shares differ hugely by ward: D ward 10.0%; M/East 84.9% (our calculation).
  • Slums lack secure tenure, space, toilets and reliable water.
Quick check
?In MCGM's tables, M/East ward had 685,994 slum residents and Greater Mumbai had 6,534,460. What share of the city's slum residents lived in this one ward?
Mission 3

Challenge: services, jobs, health and waste

  • In M/East ward (studies cited by Praja Foundation, 2018):
    • Waste: the Deonar landfill "still receives around 4500 metric tons of garbage every day".
    • Health: infant mortality 66 per 1,000 births, against India's 41 at the time.
    • Jobs: "Every second male and six out of seven females are unemployed."
    • Water: M/East logged the city's highest number of water-supply complaints (2014–16).
  • Crime and congestion also rise where jobs are scarce and roads and trains are overcrowded.
Mission 3

Managing growth: transport

  • Mumbai Urban Transport Project (World Bank finance of US$542 million, loan and credit, 2002–2011): aimed at "an efficient and sustainable urban transport system".
  • Phase 2A added 864 suburban rail cars, taking the fleet to about 3,124.
  • Metro Line 3: a 33.5 km, "fully underground" line, Colaba–Bandra–SEEPZ.
  • Costs: families living beside the tracks had to be resettled; the project paid for a resettlement plan.
Mission check

?Which evidence best shows that the costs of Mumbai's growth fall unevenly?

Many migrants gain jobs and wages; the city gains as a national and global hub. But wards such as M/East carry the costs: slums, a landfill, high infant mortality and joblessness.

Mission 4

Mission 4: A square metre of land in Ginza, central Tokyo, is valued at ¥67.1 million. A square metre of housing land in Ome, also in Tokyo, averages ¥98,600. Why the gap?

Make a guess: A square metre of land in Ginza, central Tokyo, is valued at ¥67.1 million. A square metre of housing land in Ome, also in Tokyo, averages ¥98,600. Why the gap?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 6.2a factors shaping urban land use: locational needs, accessibility, land values
Mission 4

Four zones of a city

  • CBD (central business district): the city centre.
  • Inner city: the ring around the CBD, usually the oldest housing and industry.
  • Suburbs: newer housing further out.
  • Rural–urban fringe: the edge, where town meets countryside.
  • These zones form because land users compete for space.
Mission 4

What each zone looks like

  • CBD: shops, offices, banks; tall buildings; little housing; busy roads, main stations.
  • Inner city: terraced houses and flats, old factories and warehouses; some redeveloped.
  • Suburbs: semi-detached and detached houses with gardens; curving roads, cul-de-sacs; parks.
  • Rural–urban fringe: retail parks, business parks, golf courses, sewage works, farmland.
Quick check
?A zone has semi-detached houses with gardens, curving streets and a small parade of shops. Which zone is it?
Mission 4

Accessibility and land value

  • Accessibility: how easy a place is to reach. It is highest in the CBD, where roads and railways meet.
  • Many land users want accessible sites, so they compete for them, and land values rise.
  • Shops and offices gain most from being central, so they can pay the most.
  • Values fall with distance from the centre, with smaller peaks at stations and main roads.
  • This pattern is called bid rent.
Mission 4

Tokyo's land values

  • Japan's highest commercial land value on 1 January 2026: Ginza 4-chome, central Tokyo, ¥67.1 million per m².
  • Average housing land values per m², 2026:
    • Chiyoda ward (central): ¥3,631,400
    • Setagaya ward: ¥779,900
    • Hachioji (west Tokyo): ¥131,300
    • Ome (far west Tokyo): ¥98,600
Quick check
?About how many times greater is Ginza's value (¥67,100,000 per m²) than Ome's (¥98,600 per m²)?
Mission 4

Locational needs

  • Shops need many passing shoppers, so central or busy sites.
  • Offices need access for workers and clients.
  • Industry needs large, cheap, flat sites with road links, so it moves to the edge.
  • Housing needs space and quiet; most people can afford only cheaper land further out.
  • Planning (zoning) also decides what may be built where.
Quick check
?A firm wants to build a large warehouse that needs lorry access. Where will it most likely locate?
Mission check

?In a made-up city, a plot beside the main station in the city centre costs far more per square metre than a plot of the same size near the city edge. Why?

Land values fall with distance from the most accessible point, the CBD. Shops and offices outbid housing and industry there, which is why each zone of a city has its own land use.

Mission 5

London: where and why it matters

  • The UK's capital, on the River Thames in south-east England. Population 8.80 million in 2021.
  • UK: the highest GDP per person of any UK region in 2024, £72,201: 2.4 times the North East's (our calculation).
  • World: one of only two Alpha++ world cities in the GaWC ranking, with New York; Heathrow is "Britain's aviation hub".
Mission 5

Growth: natural change and migration

  • London shrank to 6.61 million in 1981, then grew to 8.80 million by 2021. 2011–2021: up 7.7% (our calculation).
  • International migration: in 2021, 40.6% of Londoners were born outside the UK.
  • National migration: young adults move in for work and study; many families move out. 47.4% of Tower Hamlets residents were aged 20–39, against 26.3% across England (Census 2021).
  • In the year to mid-2025, London's population fell 0.29%.
Mission 5

How migration changed London's character

  • Ethnicity: 53.8% of Londoners were White in 2021, against 81.0% across England; 20.7% were Asian and 13.5% Black.
  • Neighbourhoods differ: in Tower Hamlets, 44.4% of residents were Asian; in Richmond upon Thames, 80.5% were White.
  • Language: London had the lowest share of people with English as their main language: 78.4%.
  • Migration brings new food, faith buildings, festivals, music and businesses to the city.
Quick check
?London's population fell 0.29% in the year to mid-2025, even though it had more births than deaths and more people arriving from abroad than leaving. What must explain the fall?
Mission 5

Ways of life

  • Housing: 30.0% of London households rent privately, against 20.5% in England; 45.2% own their home, against 61.3%.
  • Culture and ethnicity: many communities, with neighbourhoods that differ sharply, e.g. Tower Hamlets and Richmond upon Thames.
  • Leisure: Queen Elizabeth Olympic Park, created for the 2012 Games, is 560 acres and free to visit every day.
  • Consumption: most of the waste London's councils collect is burned, not recycled.
Mission 5

Challenges: inequality and deprivation

  • Housing cost: an average London home cost 10.6 times average earnings in 2025, the highest of any region; the North East's ratio was 5.0.
  • In 19 of London's 33 boroughs, the ratio was above 12.
  • Deprivation is concentrated in "parts of east London".
  • Gaps between boroughs: in Tower Hamlets, 35.9% of households rent from a council or housing association; in Richmond upon Thames, 62.4% own their home.
Mission 5

Challenges: dereliction, brownfield and greenfield

  • Brownfield land has been built on before; greenfield land has not.
  • The Heygate estate (Southwark), completed in 1974 and housing about 3,000 people, was judged "structurally sound, but in need of complete refurbishment" and was demolished.
  • On its site, Elephant Park was approved in 2013 with 3,000 new homes; after renegotiation, the affordable share rose from 11% to 25%.
  • Building on brownfield land protects the green belt, but can push out the people who lived there.
Mission 5

Challenges: waste

  • In 2024/25 London sent only 28.8% of its council-collected waste to recycling: the lowest of any English region.
  • 65.2% was burned (incinerated), the highest share; only 0.1% went to landfill.
  • Recycling varies with "how heavily populated an area is, the kind of housing present": Tower Hamlets recycled 17.0% of household waste, Bromley 49.9%.
Mission 5

Challenges: transport and access to services

  • Congestion: London's congestion charge (2003) cut traffic, but by 2007 congestion in the zone was back to its 2002 level.
  • Air quality: traffic pollutes the air; in 2023 the Ultra Low Emission Zone (ULEZ) was extended to every borough to tackle it.
  • Services: schools, GPs and housing must keep up with a fast-growing, young population in boroughs like Tower Hamlets (+22.1% in 2011–2021, our calculation).
Quick check
?London has the UK's highest GDP per person, yet many Londoners struggle. Which statistic best shows why?
Mission 5

Resource demands and segregation

  • Concentrated consumption: 8.8 million people living at about 5,598 people per km² need food, energy and water brought in, and create waste that must be taken away.
  • Transport demand: the Elizabeth line alone carries about 660,000 journeys a day.
  • Segregation: people with different incomes, tenures and ethnic groups cluster in different areas: e.g. Tower Hamlets (36% social rented homes) and Richmond (62% owner-occupied).
Mission 5

Who shapes London?

  • Planners draw up land-use plans and assess applications; at Elephant Park, Southwark's planning committee approved the scheme in 2013.
  • Politicians: the Mayor of London expanded the ULEZ to all boroughs in 2023.
  • Property developers build and sell homes; at Elephant Park, the developer opened a construction skills centre in 2016.
  • Industrialists and businesses bring jobs: technology firms at the Olympic Park's innovation district.
Mission 5

A sustainability initiative: the ULEZ

  • On 29 August 2023, the Mayor expanded the Ultra Low Emission Zone to all London boroughs.
  • Older, more polluting vehicles pay a daily charge to drive in the zone.
  • Harmful roadside NO₂ in 2024 was estimated to be 27% lower across London than without the ULEZ.
Quick check
?Why does the ULEZ count as a sustainability initiative?
Mission check

?A fast-growing city has high wages and many new jobs, but homes cost more than ten times average earnings and some old estates are being demolished. Which outcome is most likely?

London produces £72,201 per person, the UK's highest, but an average home costs 10.6 times average earnings. High output does not reach everyone. Estates like the Heygate were replaced by new homes, only a quarter of them affordable. Many Londoners rent privately (30%).

Mission 6

Mumbai: a megacity under pressure

  • About 20.2 million people in 2025.
  • One of the densest cities on Earth: about 27,000 people per km².
  • About 42% to 52% of residents lived in slums in 2011, depending on which census table is used.
  • India's financial capital: home of the Reserve Bank of India since 1937.
Mission 6

Urban challenges

  • Squatter settlements: homes built without permission on land people do not own; often lacking water and toilets.
  • Informal economy: in lower-middle-income countries like India, 81.5% of jobs were informal in 2024.
  • Pollution: the Deonar landfill took about 4,500 tonnes of rubbish a day (2018).
  • Low quality of life: in M/East ward, infant mortality was 66 per 1,000 births against a national average of 41.
Quick check
?Which is an example of Mumbai's informal economy?
Mission 6

Top-down and bottom-up

  • Top-down: governments or big organisations plan and pay; large and fast, but local people may have little say.
  • Bottom-up: residents and community groups decide and act; cheap and trusted, but small.
  • Mumbai uses both.
Mission 6

Top-down 1: transport

  • Mumbai Urban Transport Project: World Bank loan of US$542 million (2002–2011) for "an efficient and sustainable urban transport system".
  • A later phase added 864 suburban rail cars, taking the fleet to about 3,124.
  • Metro Line 3: 33.5 km, "fully underground", Colaba–Bandra–SEEPZ.
  • For: faster journeys to jobs; electric trains instead of cars.
  • Against: people affected by the project had to be resettled; huge cost.
Mission 6

Top-down 2: slum rehabilitation

  • The Maharashtra state government set up the Slum Rehabilitation Authority (SRA) in December 1995.
  • The deal: developers build free flats for slum dwellers, and in return may build extra floor space (incentive FSI) to sell on the open market.
  • Residents who move out during building can receive rent.
  • For: new, permanent homes at no cost to residents or the state.
  • Against: depends on developer profit; the SRA can take "punitive action on non-participating slum-dwellers".
Mission 6

Bottom-up: community action

  • SPARC began in 1984 in Byculla, Mumbai; it works in an alliance with the National Slum Dwellers Federation (NSDF) and Mahila Milan (women's groups).
  • Women's savings groups manage loans and help families get identity documents.
  • The alliance has built "many hundreds of community toilets" with city governments.
  • In M/East, citizen-led work with the charity Apnalaya helped families get "legal water connections, electricity connections, cooking gas".
  • For: fits local needs; residents stay in place. Against: slow; still needs government land and permission.
Quick check
?Which Mumbai project is bottom-up?
Mission 6

Who manages the city?

  • Politicians: the state government created the SRA and its scheme.
  • Planners: the SRA is the "Planning Authority for all Slum areas" in Greater Mumbai.
  • Property developers: build rehab flats in return for flats they can sell.
  • Industrialists: big companies build and run infrastructure, e.g. Mumbai Metro One, whose site lists Reliance Infrastructure and the regional development authority (MMRDA) as partners.
  • Conflict: developers want profit; residents want to stay near work; planners must balance both.
Mission 6

Is it sustainable?

  • Sustainable urban living: meets needs now (homes, jobs, clean air) without harming the future.
  • Metro and rail: + low-carbon transport; − cost and resettlement.
  • SRA flats: + permanent homes; − may move people from their work and community.
  • Community toilets: + health and dignity; − small scale.
  • Most sustainable: upgrading in place, as the UN advises, linked to good public transport.
Quick check
?Which strategy best meets social, economic and environmental goals at once?
Mission check

?City Z's government, without consulting residents, plans to clear an informal settlement near the city centre and rehouse everyone in free flats 25 km away, beside a new metro station. Which is the strongest criticism of this top-down plan?

Mumbai combines top-down projects (railways, metro, SRA flats) with bottom-up action (SPARC, Mahila Milan, Apnalaya). Top-down gives scale; bottom-up gives residents a say in where and how they live.

Mission 7

Two kinds of building land

  • Brownfield site: land that has been built on before, e.g. old factories, gasworks, docks. It is often derelict (abandoned and decaying).
  • Greenfield site: land never built on, e.g. farmland or countryside, often at the edge of a city.
  • Dereliction: empty, run-down buildings and land, often left when industries close.
Mission 7

London builds on brownfield

  • In England in 2021–22, 54% of new residential addresses were on previously developed land.
  • London had the highest share: 88%.
  • Only 2% of new addresses in England were in the Green Belt.
Mission 7

The Green Belt

  • A Green Belt is a ring of open land kept free of most building. Its aim: "to prevent urban sprawl by keeping land permanently open".
  • England's Green Belt covers about 12.5% of its land (31 March 2024).
  • London's Metropolitan Green Belt is the largest: 31.1% of England's Green Belt area.
  • One purpose is to "assist urban regeneration by encouraging the recycling of derelict and other urban land".
Quick check
?Why does London build most new homes on brownfield land?
Mission 7

Brownfield: for and against

  • For: brings derelict land back into use and regenerates run-down areas.
  • For: roads, pipes, schools and transport are already nearby.
  • For: protects the countryside from sprawl.
  • Against: old industry may leave contaminated ground that is costly to clean.
  • Against: sites can be small and cramped, so homes are built densely.
Mission 7

Greenfield: for and against

  • For: open land is easier and quicker to build on: no demolition or clean-up.
  • For: room for larger homes, gardens and new roads.
  • Against: loses farmland and habitats. Worldwide, about 60% of land turned urban since 1970 was productive farmland.
  • Against: new estates far from jobs mean more car journeys, adding to sprawl.
Quick check
?Which is an advantage of a greenfield site for a developer?
Mission 7

The rural–urban fringe

  • The rural–urban fringe is where the city meets the countryside.
  • It attracts land uses that need space and road access: housing estates, retail parks, business and science parks, industrial estates.
  • Example: in 1970 Trinity College turned farmland outside Cambridge into the UK's first science park. It now covers 152 acres.
Mission 7

Sprawl and commuter settlements

  • Urban sprawl: the city spreading outwards onto greenfield land.
  • Commuter settlements: towns and villages beyond the city where people live but travel in to work.
  • Growth beyond the city edge is not always on fields: Ebbsfleet Garden City in north Kent is planned for up to 15,000 homes on previously used, brownfield land.
  • Impacts: busier roads and stations, rising house prices for local people, and quiet villages during the working day.

London put 88% of new homes on previously developed land in 2021–22, because the Green Belt holds back sprawl. Brownfield reuse regenerates derelict land but can be costly; greenfield building is easier but loses countryside.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The world's urban share rose from 37% to 58% in 50 years.
  • High-income countries urbanised early and are now growing slowly; low-income and lower-middle-income countries are urbanising fastest, through migration and natural increase.
  • Megacities rose from 8 (1975) to 33 (2025), mostly in Asia.

Mission 2

  • London shows the whole sequence: urbanisation to 1939, suburbanisation, decline to 1981, then re-urbanisation.

Mission 3

  • Mumbai: west-coast port city, India's financial centre, an Alpha world city; 20.2 million (UN, 2025).
  • Growth: natural increase + rural–urban migration. Gains: jobs, services. Costs: slums (52.5% in MCGM's tables), waste, health, unemployment, unevenly spread.

Mission 4

  • Cities have four broad zones: CBD, inner city, suburbs and rural–urban fringe. Accessibility, land values and locational needs explain where each land use goes.

Mission 5

  • London: the UK's capital and an Alpha++ world city; 8.80 million people (2021), 40.6% born abroad, with neighbourhoods that differ sharply.
  • Opportunities (jobs, culture, transport, parks) sit beside challenges (housing costs, deprivation in east London). London has the lowest recycling rate of any English region.
  • The ULEZ is one sustainability initiative: roadside NO₂ an estimated 27% lower.

Mission 6

  • Mumbai: about 20 million people; 42–52% in slums (2011, census tables differ); informal work, waste and health gaps.
  • Top-down: World Bank-funded railways, Metro Line 3, SRA flats: big and fast, but displacement and developer-led.
  • Bottom-up: SPARC, NSDF, Mahila Milan, Apnalaya: local and trusted, but small and slow.
  • Roles: politicians set policy; planners (SRA) approve; developers build; industrialists run infrastructure.

Mission 7

  • Brownfield = previously developed land; greenfield = never built on. Brownfield regenerates and protects countryside but can be costly; greenfield is easier but causes sprawl.
  • The rural–urban fringe attracts estates, retail parks and science parks; commuter settlements grow beyond the Green Belt.
