Mission 1

Ethics about ethics

  • Normative ethics asks: what should I do? (Kant, utilitarianism, natural law.)
  • Meta-ethics asks about moral language and its basis: what does "good" mean? Can "murder is wrong" be true? What would make it true?
  • Spec issue: is the meaning of "good" the defining question of ethics? Moore (1903) thought so: until we know what "good" means, we cannot tell what is good.
Mission 1

Two questions, four positions

  • Cognitivism: moral statements express beliefs; they can be true or false (factual language).
  • Non-cognitivism: they express attitudes or commands, not beliefs; not true or false (see Mission 3).
  • Realism: moral facts exist independently of human minds.
  • Anti-realism: there are no mind-independent moral facts; morality depends on human attitudes, choices or cultures.
  • Naturalism and intuitionism are both cognitivist and realist. They disagree about what kind of fact a moral fact is.

Mission 1

Ethical naturalism

  • Naturalism: moral terms can be defined by a natural property: something in the world we can observe or study.
  • So moral statements are cognitivist (they express beliefs that can be true or false): they can be verified or falsified like other facts.
  • Verified moral statements are objective truths and universal.
  • Moral facts exist independently of human beings: we discover them, as we discover facts in science.
  • Candidates for "good": pleasure (Bentham); happiness; what satisfies human needs or flourishing; what a well-ordered society requires (F. H. Bradley).
  • Spec definition: "the belief that values can be defined in terms of some natural property in the world".
Mission 1Read the source

Utilitarianism as naturalism

Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832), An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789), ch. I:

> "Nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure."

John Stuart Mill (1806–1873), Utilitarianism (1863), ch. IV:

> "the sole evidence it is possible to produce that anything is desirable, is that people do actually desire it."

  • Right = what produces pleasure (or happiness); wrong = what produces pain. Both are natural, observable states.
  • So "this act is right" becomes a factual claim about consequences, checkable in principle.

Q1 Where does Bentham locate the standard of right and wrong?

Show answer

In nature: pleasure and pain.

Q2 Which step in Mill's sentence would a critic say slides from "is" to "ought"?

Show answer

From what people do desire to what is desirable, i.e. worth desiring.

Mission 1

Naturalism and absolutism

  • If "good" names a natural property, moral truths are facts, the same for everyone. That supports absolutism: some acts are right or wrong always and everywhere.
  • Example: if good = what meets basic human needs, then torturing for fun is wrong in every culture, because it never meets them.
  • But naturalism need not be absolutist: if good = what this society approves, or what gives this person pleasure, the facts vary, and naturalism supports relativism.
  • Spec link: naturalism "and its application to absolutism". Compare emotivism and relativism (see Mission 3).
Mission 1Read the source

Hume's is–ought gap

David Hume (1711–1776), A Treatise of Human Nature (1739–40), Book III, Part I, Section I:

> "… instead of the usual copulations of propositions, is, and is not, I meet with no proposition that is not connected with an ought, or an ought not."

  • Hume noticed moral writers move from facts (what is) to duties (what ought to be) without explanation.
  • Hume's Law (the is–ought gap): you cannot validly derive an "ought" from premises that contain only "is".
  • Against naturalism: facts about pleasure or needs do not by themselves give a duty.

Q1 What change in the writers' sentences surprised Hume?

Show answer

From "is / is not" to "ought / ought not".

Q2 What would a naturalist need to bridge the gap?

Show answer

A premise linking fact to value, e.g. "we ought to promote pleasure", which is itself an "ought".

Mission 1

Moore: the naturalistic fallacy and the open question

  • G. E. Moore (1873–1958), Principia Ethica (1903).
  • Good is indefinable: it is a simple notion, like yellow. You can point to it, not break it into parts.
  • The naturalistic fallacy: identifying "good" with any natural property (pleasure, what is desired, what is more evolved).
  • The open question argument: for any definition, "X is pleasant, but is it good?" is still a sensible, open question. "X is pleasant, but is it pleasant?" is not.
  • So "good" does not mean "pleasant". Moral facts cannot be reduced to natural ones.
  • Reply: William Frankena (1908–1994), "The Naturalistic Fallacy" (1939): calling it a "fallacy" assumes what it sets out to prove.
  • Later naturalists' reply: "water is H₂O" was once an open question, yet true: two terms can pick out the same property.
  • Moore's own view: good is a real, non-natural property known by intuition (see Mission 2).

Mission 1Evaluate

Can "good" be defined in natural terms?

  • Claim: Moral terms can be defined by natural properties.
  • For: makes morality objective and knowable by evidence; fits a scientific view of the world; explains why pleasure, pain and needs matter morally; makes moral disagreement resolvable in principle.
  • Against: Hume's is–ought gap; Moore's open question; naturalists disagree on which property (pleasure? needs? a well-ordered society?); some pleasures seem bad (cruelty enjoyed).
  • Replies to the critics: Frankena; "water = H₂O"; Mill offers more than a dictionary definition.
  • Judgement criteria: Does the theory explain why moral facts bind us? Can it survive the open question? Does it fit how people actually argue about morals?

Mission 2

Real but not natural

  • Non-naturalism: moral properties are real and objective, but not natural properties. You cannot see, measure or test them.
  • Like naturalism, it is cognitivist and realist: "torturing for fun is wrong" is true, whatever anyone thinks.
  • Unlike naturalism: "good" cannot be defined by pleasure, happiness or any other natural property (see Mission 1).
  • So how do we know moral truths? Not by the senses, but by intuition: a direct, rational grasp.
  • Intuitionism: basic moral truths are indefinable but self-evident.
Mission 2Read the source

Moore on "good"

G. E. Moore (1873–1958), Principia Ethica (1903), ch. I:

> "If I am asked 'What is good?' my answer is that good is good, and that is the end of the matter." (§6)

> "My point is that good is a simple notion, just as yellow is a simple notion" (§7)

  • You can explain "horse" by its parts. You cannot explain "yellow" to someone who has never seen it: you can only point.
  • "Good" is like yellow: simple, with no parts to define it by, yet perfectly real.
  • Moore: we know by intuition which things are good in themselves; he named personal affection and the enjoyment of beauty as the greatest (ch. VI).

Q1 Why is "good is good" not an empty answer, for Moore?

Show answer

Because good is simple: there is nothing more basic to define it by.

Q2 What does the yellow comparison suggest about how we know good?

Show answer

Directly, by acquaintance, not by definition or proof.

Mission 2

Self-evident is not the same as obvious

  • Self-evident: known by reflecting on the claim itself, without proof from other truths. Compare: "a whole is greater than its part".
  • Not "obvious to everyone at once": it may take a mature mind and careful thought.
  • So moral intuition is like the grasp of mathematical axioms, not like a hunch or gut feeling.
  • Examples intuitionists give: we ought to keep promises; we ought not harm the innocent; gratitude is owed to benefactors.
  • Spec issue: do people "just know within themselves" what is good, bad, right and wrong? Intuitionism says yes, for basic truths.

Mission 2

Prichard: we see what we ought to do

  • H. A. Prichard (1871–1947), "Does Moral Philosophy Rest on a Mistake?", Mind (1912).
  • Moore intuits good; Prichard intuits obligation: "ought to do" has no definition.
  • The "mistake": trying to prove our duties, by showing they lead to good results or to our happiness.
  • Our sense of obligation is "absolutely underivative or immediate" (1912).
  • Two ways of thinking: general thinking (reasoning about facts and consequences) and moral thinking (directly apprehending what we ought to do in a situation).
  • If we doubt a duty, "the remedy lies not in any process of general thinking": we must face the actual situation and let moral thinking see the obligation.
Mission 2

Ross: duties seen, then weighed

  • W. D. Ross (1877–1971), The Right and the Good (1930).
  • Prima facie duties (fidelity, reparation, gratitude, justice, beneficence, self-improvement, non-maleficence) are self-evident, like axioms.
  • When they conflict, intuition cannot read off the answer: we need judgement about this case.
  • Ross: the considered moral convictions of thoughtful people are the data of ethics, as sense-perceptions are of science (paraphrase).
  • So intuitionism can be pluralist: several basic duties, not one master principle.

Mission 2

What intuitionism claims

  • Objective moral laws exist independently of human beings.
  • Moral truths are discovered by using our minds intuitively.
  • Intuitive ability is innate and the same for all moral agents.
  • But it needs a mature mind: children and careless thinkers can get it wrong. So intuition is not infallible.
  • It allows objective moral values without defining them by natural facts or by God's commands.
Mission 2

Challenges

  • No proof: there is no evidence that a moral "intuition" faculty exists; the claim cannot be tested.
  • Intuitions differ widely: across cultures and between thoughtful people (e.g. on eating animals, on war).
  • No way to resolve conflicts: if two intuitions clash, intuitionism has no further test to settle which is right.
  • Queerness: J. L. Mackie (1917–1981), Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong (1977): non-natural moral properties, and a special faculty to perceive them, would be utterly unlike anything else we know (paraphrase).
  • Origins: intuitions may come from upbringing, culture or evolution, not insight into moral truth.
  • Replies: many disputes are about the facts of a case or the weighting of duties (how much each counts when they clash), not about basic duties; disagreement in maths or science does not prove there is no truth; the mature-mind condition explains some differences.
Quick check
?Two thoughtful, well-informed people have opposite, firmly held intuitions about whether eating meat is wrong. Which challenge to intuitionism does this case illustrate most directly?
Mission 2Evaluate

Are moral terms intuitive?

  • Claim: We know basic moral truths by intuition.
  • For: avoids the naturalistic fallacy and Hume's gap; explains moral certainty (torturing for fun is wrong, whatever the argument); fits common sense: people do "just know"; allows objective values.
  • Against: no proof of the faculty; intuitions differ; no way to settle conflicts; queerness; intuitions may be products of culture.
  • Compare: naturalism (see Mission 1) makes morality testable but faces the open question.
  • Judgement criteria: Is disagreement at the level of basic principles or of application? Can a theory without a test still give knowledge? Does it explain why morality binds us?

An evaluation paragraph

Question: "Moral truths are self-evident." Evaluate this view.

  • Support: Moore: good is simple and indefinable, so it can only be known directly.
  • Support: Prichard: obligation is "absolutely underivative or immediate".
  • Counter: But intuitions differ widely, and intuitionism offers no test to decide between them.
  • Response: Ross: many disagreements concern facts or the weighting of duties, not basic duties themselves.
  • Mini-judgement: The view is strongest for a small core of basic duties, weakest where intuitions conflict.

Mission 3

Logical positivism: the test for meaning

  • Logical positivism: a movement of the 1920s–30s, centred on the Vienna Circle.
  • The verification principle: a statement is meaningful only if it is either
  • analytic: true by definition ("all bachelors are unmarried"), or
  • empirically verifiable: checkable, at least in principle, by sense experience ("there is milk in the fridge").
  • Anything else is, literally, meaningless as a statement of fact.
  • A. J. Ayer (1910–1989) brought it to Britain in Language, Truth and Logic (1936).
Mission 3

Ayer: ethics fails the test

  • Language, Truth and Logic, ch. 6, "Critique of Ethics and Theology".
  • "Stealing is wrong" is not analytic (not true by definition) and not verifiable (no observation shows "wrongness").
  • So it states no fact: ethical words are pseudo-concepts. It is neither true nor false: non-cognitivism.
  • What it does: expresses the speaker's feeling (approval or disapproval) and aims to arouse feeling in others and stimulate action.
  • Spec definition: ethical terms "evince approval or disapproval".
Mission 3Read the source

Ayer on stealing

Paraphrase of Ayer, Language, Truth and Logic (1936), ch. 6:

  • If I tell someone, "You acted wrongly in stealing that money", I state nothing more than "You stole that money".
  • Adding "wrongly" adds no fact. It is as if I had said "You stole that money" "in a peculiar tone of horror", or with exclamation marks.
  • If I go on to say "Stealing money is wrong", my sentence has no factual meaning: it expresses moral disapproval.

Q1 What does the word "wrongly" add to the sentence, on Ayer's view?

Show answer

No fact: only the speaker's feeling, like a tone of voice.

Q2 Can two people contradict each other about whether stealing is wrong, on this view?

Show answer

Not about a fact: they express different feelings. They can still disagree about the facts of the case.

Mission 3

Boo, hurrah, and not subjectivism

  • Critics nicknamed emotivism the "boo-hurrah" theory: "Stealing is wrong" ≈ "Stealing: boo!"; "Kindness is good" ≈ "Kindness: hurrah!"
  • Emotivism is not subjectivism:
  • Subjectivism: "Stealing is wrong" reports a feeling: it means "I disapprove of stealing", which is true or false (am I really disapproving?).
  • Emotivism: it expresses the feeling, as a groan does. It reports nothing, so it is neither true nor false.
  • Ayer: on subjectivism, "X is wrong" and "I disapprove of X" would mean the same, but someone can disapprove of X and deny that X is wrong. So subjectivism fails; emotivism does not face this.
  • Emotivism explains disagreement: people's attitudes really differ.

Mission 3

Emotivism and relativism

  • If moral statements only express attitudes, there is no objective standard to judge whose attitude is correct.
  • So emotivism fits a relativist picture: moral views vary with persons and cultures, and none is "the" truth.
  • But emotivism is not the same as cultural relativism: a relativist says "slavery is wrong" is true in some cultures. An emotivist says it is not true or false anywhere.
  • Emotivism cannot say tolerance is objectively right either: "We ought to tolerate other views" is also an expressed attitude.
  • Spec link: emotivism "and its application to relativism".
Mission 3

Mackie: error theory

  • J. L. Mackie (1917–1981), Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong (1977), ch. 1, opens: "There are no objective values."
  • Moral statements aim to state objective facts (so Mackie is a cognitivist), but there are none, so they are all false: an error theory (anti-realist).
  • Argument from relativity: moral codes vary widely; this is better explained by different ways of life than by everyone perceiving objective values badly.
  • Argument from queerness: objective values would be strange entities, and knowing them would need a strange faculty (see Mission 2).
  • So we invent right and wrong. Mackie still thought morality useful and worth building.
Mission 3

Challenges and replies

  • No basic moral principles can be established: nothing is really right or wrong.
  • Ethical debate becomes pointless: if facts are agreed, all that is left is a clash of feelings.
  • No universal agreement that some actions are wrong: emotivism cannot say genocide is wrong for everyone, only that people express disapproval.
  • The verification principle fails its own test: it is neither analytic nor verifiable.
  • Moral progress (e.g. the abolition of slavery) seems to be more than a change of feelings.
  • Replies: most moral arguments are about facts, which can be settled (Ayer); attitudes can be changed by reasons (Stevenson).
Quick check

Mission 3Evaluate

Objective, subjective or meaningless?

  • Objective factual basis: naturalism and intuitionism: moral claims are true or false, made true by facts.
  • Only in the mind of the user: subjectivism (reports of feeling); emotivism (expressions of feeling).
  • Meaningless? Ayer: no factual meaning, but they have emotive meaning and a use.
  • Meaningful but false: Mackie's error theory.
  • For emotivism: explains deep moral disagreement; fits a scientific view of facts; explains why moral words move us to act.
  • Against: debate seems real; some acts seem wrong whatever anyone feels; the verification principle undermines itself.
  • Judgement criteria: Does the theory fit how people actually argue? Can it explain moral progress? Does it rest on a sound theory of meaning?
Mission check

?A historian says: "The abolition of the slave trade was moral progress." Which reply could an emotivist like Ayer consistently give?

For Ayer, moral words do things rather than describe things. The debate is whether that can do justice to moral reasoning and moral progress.

Mission 4

What is conscience?

  • Conscience (Latin con-scientia, "knowing with"): our awareness of right and wrong in our own actions.
  • It works before an act (warning, urging) and after it (approval, or guilt and shame).
  • Three big questions:
  • Where does it come from? God, reason, upbringing, society, evolution?
  • Is it reliable? Can it be wrong?
  • Must I obey it? Even against the law, or a religious authority?
  • Religious and non-religious thinkers answer differently.
Mission 4

Aquinas: ratio

  • Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274), Summa Theologica (1265–1274), I, question 79, articles 12–13.
  • Ratio (reason): placed in every person as a result of being created in the image of God.

> "God created man in his own image. In God's image he created him; male and female he created them." (Genesis 1:27, WEBBE)

  • For Aquinas the image of God lies above all in our reason: we can know what is good (link: natural law).
  • So, for Aquinas, conscience is not a feeling or a voice: it is reason at work on moral questions.
Mission 4

Synderesis and conscientia

  • Synderesis: an inner principle directing a person towards good and away from evil.
  • It holds the first principle of morality: "good is to be done and pursued, and evil is to be avoided" (ST I-II q.94 a.2).
  • Aquinas: "'Synderesis' is not a power but a habit" (ST I q.79 a.12): a natural, built-in disposition.
  • Conscientia: a person's reason making moral judgements about particular acts: applying synderesis to "this, here, now".
  • Synderesis does not err about the first principle; conscientia can err, through faulty reasoning or wrong information.
Mission 4Read the source

Conscience as an act

Aquinas, Summa Theologica I q.79 a.13 (English Dominican translation, 1920):

> "Properly speaking, conscience is not a power, but an act."

> "For conscience is said to witness, to bind, or incite, and also to accuse, torment, or rebuke."

And the Bible text Aquinas and other Christians draw on:

> "they show the work of the law written in their hearts, their conscience testifying with them, and their thoughts amongst themselves accusing or else excusing them" (Romans 2:15, WEBBE)

Q1 Name one thing conscience does before an act and one after.

Show answer

Before: binds or incites; after: witnesses, accuses or excuses.

Q2 Why does Romans 2:15 matter for the claim that conscience is universal?

Show answer

Paul says even Gentiles without the law have it "written in their hearts".

Mission 4

When conscience gets it wrong

  • Erring conscience still binds (ST I-II q.19 a.5): acting against your conscience is always wrong, even when conscience is mistaken.
  • But does a mistaken conscience excuse? It depends on the ignorance (q.19 a.6):
  • Vincible ignorance: lack of knowledge for which a person is responsible: they could and should have known (e.g. a doctor who did not read the patient's notes).
  • Invincible ignorance: lack of knowledge for which a person is not responsible: they could not have known (Aquinas' example: a man deceived, without any negligence, into thinking another woman is his wife).
  • Vincible ignorance does not excuse; invincible ignorance does.
  • Aquinas notes whole cultures can err: Julius Caesar reported that the ancient Germans did not think robbery wrong (ST I-II q.94 a.4).

Mission 4Diversity

Other religious views

  • Joseph Butler (1692–1752), Fifteen Sermons (1726): conscience is the God-given supreme principle of reflection in human nature:

> "Had it strength, as it had right; had it power, as it had manifest authority, it would absolutely govern the world." (Sermon II)

  • John Henry Newman (1801–1890), Letter to the Duke of Norfolk (1875): "Conscience is the aboriginal Vicar of Christ": the voice of God within.
  • Catholic teaching today: conscience is a law written by God in the heart, where a person is alone with God; it must be informed by reason and the Church's teaching (Vatican II, Gaudium et Spes 16, 1965; Catechism 1776–1785, paraphrase).
  • Protestant emphasis: conscience bound by scripture; Luther at Worms (1521) said his conscience was "captive to the Word of God".
  • Joseph Fletcher: conscience is a verb: the activity of deciding, not a store of rules.
Mission 4

Non-religious ideas

  • Sigmund Freud (1856–1939): conscience is part of the super-ego: rules of parents and society taken in during childhood.
  • Erich Fromm (1900–1980), Man for Himself (1947): authoritarian conscience is the inner voice of an outside authority we fear; humanistic conscience is our own voice calling us back to grow as persons.
  • Jean Piaget (1896–1980), The Moral Judgment of the Child (1932): children move from heteronomous morality (rules from adults, judged by consequences) to autonomous morality (rules agreed, judged by intentions).
  • Charles Darwin, The Descent of Man (1871): a moral sense arises from social instincts plus developed intelligence (paraphrase).
  • Humanists often see conscience as reason and empathy, shaped by experience, with no need for God.
Mission 4

Conscience and moral duty

  • Is there a duty to follow conscience? Aquinas: yes, even when it errs; but also a duty to inform it.
  • Newman, on whether he would put the Pope before conscience:

> "I shall drink—to the Pope, if you please,—still, to Conscience first, and to the Pope afterwards." (Letter to the Duke of Norfolk, §5)

  • Duty runs both ways: a duty to follow conscience, and a duty to form it well (study, advice, prayer, honesty).
  • Following conscience against the law: conscientious objectors refusing to fight; prisoners of conscience, jailed for peaceful belief.

Mission 4Evaluate

Is conscience God-given?

  • Claim: Conscience is a God-given guide to right and wrong.
  • For: It seems universal: every culture has a moral sense (Romans 2:15).
  • For: It has authority beyond self-interest or fear (Butler, Newman).
  • For: Aquinas: reason in the image of God explains both its reliability and its errors.
  • Against: Consciences differ across cultures and eras (Aquinas' own example of the ancient Germans).
  • Against: Natural explanations are available: upbringing (Freud, Piaget), evolution (Darwin), society.
  • Against: If it is God's voice, why does it so often err?
  • Judgement criteria: Does the view explain both agreement and disagreement in conscience? Is a natural explanation enough? Does it make conscience trustworthy?
Mission check

For Aquinas, conscience must be followed and formed: the duty lies in both. A later mission: Freud's very different account, and whether conscience is a good guide at all.

Mission 5

Freud: the hidden mind

  • Sigmund Freud (1856–1939), Austrian founder of psychoanalysis; The Ego and the Id (1923); Civilization and Its Discontents (1930).
  • Much of mental life is unconscious: desires and memories we cannot see directly, which still shape what we feel and do.
  • For Freud, conscience is not reason, and not God's voice: it is part of a structure built in early childhood.
  • Freud was an atheist: in The Future of an Illusion (1927) he called religion an illusion rooted in the wish for a protecting father.
  • His method: explain moral feeling, especially guilt, by its psychological origins.
Mission 5

Psychosexual development

  • Libido: sexual or life energy, present from infancy (Three Essays on the Theory of Sexuality, 1905).
  • Children pass through stages, each focused on a different zone: oral (infancy), anal (toddler), phallic (about 3–6), latency, genital (puberty onward).
  • Phallic stage: the Oedipus complex: the child desires the opposite-sex parent and sees the same-sex parent as a rival, while fearing that parent's punishment.
  • Resolution: the child identifies with the same-sex parent and takes in that parent's prohibitions.
  • These taken-in rules become the super-ego, "the heir of the Oedipus complex" (The Ego and the Id, ch. 3).
  • Freud's account of girls' development differs and was criticised in his own time and since.
Mission 5

Id, ego and super-ego

  • Id: instinctive impulses that seek satisfaction in pleasure; present from birth; wholly unconscious.
  • Ego: mediates between the id and the demands of social interaction and reality; mostly conscious.
  • Super-ego: contradicts the id and, working on internalised ideals from parents and society, tries to make the ego behave morally.
  • The super-ego has two sides: the ego-ideal (who I should be) and conscience (what I must not do, punished by guilt).
  • Moral life is a struggle among the three, much of it unconscious.
Mission 5Read the source

The rider and the judge

Freud, The Ego and the Id (1923), chapters 2, 3 and 5 (paraphrase):

  • The ego is like a rider on a horse: it has to hold in check the greater strength of the id; often the rider can only guide the horse where it wants to go anyway.
  • The super-ego keeps the character of the father; the stronger the early authority, the more severely the super-ego later rules the ego as conscience or as an unconscious sense of guilt.
  • The sense of guilt is the tension between the ego and the super-ego's demands; it can be fierce even when the person has done nothing wrong.

Q1 Why is the super-ego called "heir" of childhood authority?

Show answer

It takes over the parents' commands and punishments inside the mind.

Q2 According to Freud, does strong guilt prove a real wrong was done?

Show answer

No: guilt measures super-ego tension, not wrongdoing.

Mission 5

Guilt: Aquinas and Freud

  • Aquinas: guilt follows when conscientia accuses after a wrong act (ST I q.79 a.13). It is a rational verdict: it can be checked, corrected and resolved by repentance and forgiveness.
  • Freud: guilt is the super-ego's aggression turned on the ego. It may be unconscious, irrational and out of proportion; resolved by understanding its origins in analysis.
  • Healthy and unhealthy guilt: Aquinas would say guilt over a real wrong is fitting; Freud warns that guilt is often excessive (as in obsessional neurosis and melancholia).
  • Both agree guilt can mislead: for Aquinas through an erring conscience; for Freud through a harsh super-ego.
Mission 5Diversity

Aquinas and Freud compared

  • Source: Aquinas: reason, placed in us as the image of God · Freud: parents and society, taken in during childhood.
  • God: Aquinas: present: synderesis is part of God's design · Freud: absent: God is itself a projection of the father; the super-ego explains the "voice".
  • Moral decision-making: Aquinas: conscious reasoning from first principles to a case · Freud: the ego negotiating between id, super-ego and reality, largely unconscious.
  • Can it err? Aquinas: yes (conscientia, through ignorance or bad reasoning) · Freud: yes (super-ego can be harsh, outdated or neurotic).
  • Common ground: conscience is formed over time; guilt is not always a reliable guide.
  • Spec issue: whether conscience is linked to, or separate from, reason and the unconscious mind.

Mission 5

Does conscience exist at all?

  • Some argue "conscience" is an umbrella term: a name for many factors involved in moral decisions, not one faculty.
  • Factors: culture (moral norms differ), environment (family, peers), genetic predisposition (twin studies of empathy and self-control), education.
  • On this view, asking "what does my conscience say?" means "what do my upbringing, culture and reasoning together incline me to?"
  • Replies: Aquinas would say these factors shape conscientia, but synderesis, the shared first principle, is universal. Butler: conscience has authority, not just influence.
  • A middle view: conscience is real as an activity (judging one's own acts), even if it has many sources (compare Fletcher).
  • Spec issue: whether conscience exists or is an umbrella term.
Mission 5Evaluate

Is conscience a good moral guide?

  • Claim: Conscience is a reliable guide to moral decisions.
  • For: It is personal and immediate, and has authority over self-interest (Butler).
  • For: It can resist unjust orders: e.g. Franz Jägerstätter (1907–1943), an Austrian Catholic farmer executed for refusing to fight for Hitler's army; beatified 2007.
  • For: An informed conscience joins principle and situation (Aquinas).
  • Against: Conscience can be shaped by prejudice. In Mark Twain's Huckleberry Finn (1884, ch. 31), Huck's conscience tells him to turn in Jim, an enslaved man; he decides to help Jim instead: "All right, then, I'll go to hell."
  • Against: Freud: the super-ego can be harsh, irrational or outdated.
  • Against: Consciences conflict; sincerity does not settle who is right.
  • Judgement criteria: Is conscience informed? Is it checked against reason, others and (for believers) scripture and tradition? Is it a source or a tool?
Mission 5Worked answer

A comparison paragraph

Question: "Aquinas' view of conscience is more convincing than Freud's." Discuss. (AO2 paragraph on guilt)

  • Point: On guilt, Freud explains more of the evidence than Aquinas.
  • Develop: Freud accounts for guilt that is out of proportion or felt over no real wrong: the super-ego's tension with the ego, formed in childhood.
  • Counter: Aquinas also explains error: conscientia can misjudge through vincible or invincible ignorance, and guilt over a real wrong is a fitting verdict of reason.
  • Weigh: Freud explains where guilt comes from; Aquinas explains when guilt is justified. The two may answer different questions.
  • Mini-judgement: On this criterion, Freud is stronger as explanation, Aquinas as guidance; a full answer must test other criteria (God, decision-making).

Mission 6

The traditional Christian framework

  • Traditional Christian teaching: sex belongs within marriage, between one man and one woman, for life.
  • Two purposes of sex in marriage: unitive (binding the couple in love) and procreative (open to children).
  • So the tradition counts as wrong: sex before marriage (fornication), sex with someone other than your spouse (adultery), and same-sex sexual acts.
  • Catholic teaching sets this out in the Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992): e.g. §2353 (fornication), §2380–2381 (adultery), §2357–2359 (homosexuality).
  • Many Protestant and Orthodox Christians have held the same framework, from different sources (scripture first, or scripture with tradition).
  • The question for this mission: what happens when Christians re-read that framework?
Mission 6Read the source

Marriage and faithfulness

> "Therefore a man will leave his father and his mother, and will join with his wife, and they will be one flesh." (Genesis 2:24, WEBBE)

> "You shall not commit adultery." (Exodus 20:14, WEBBE)

> "Let marriage be held in honour amongst all, and let the bed be undefiled; but God will judge the sexually immoral and adulterers." (Hebrews 13:4, WEBBE)

  • Jesus quotes Genesis 2:24 on marriage (Mark 10:7–9) and widens adultery to lustful looking (Matthew 5:28).

Q1 Which verse is the root of the idea that sex makes a couple a union?

Show answer

Genesis 2:24: "one flesh".

Q2 Why would a traditional Christian read Hebrews 13:4 as ruling out sex before marriage, not only adultery?

Show answer

It honours the marriage bed and judges "the sexually immoral" as a separate group from adulterers.

Mission 6

Premarital sex, adultery and promiscuity

  • Premarital sex: traditional teaching forbids it; Paul: "because of sexual immoralities, let each man have his own wife" (1 Corinthians 7:2, WEBBE).
  • Many liberal Christians now distinguish casual sex from sex in a committed relationship heading towards marriage, and judge the second less harshly or not wrong.
  • Adultery (extramarital sex) is condemned almost universally: religious and secular ethics both see a broken promise, deception and harm to a spouse and children.
  • Mercy matters too: Jesus refuses to condemn a woman caught in adultery, yet tells her "sin no more" (John 8:11).
  • Spec terms: "premarital" and "extramarital" sex.
Quick check

Mission 6Diversity

Same-sex relationships: a spectrum

  • Catholic: same-sex attraction is not itself sinful, though the Catechism calls the inclination "objectively disordered" (§2358); same-sex acts cannot be approved; people must be treated with "respect, compassion, and sensitivity" (Catechism §2358). Fiducia Supplicans (2023) allows short, non-liturgical blessings of couples, including same-sex couples, without approving the union.
  • Many evangelicals and the Orthodox: same-sex acts are against scripture (Leviticus 18:22; Romans 1:26–27); some stress pastoral care and celibacy.
  • Church of England: does not marry same-sex couples; Prayers of Love and Faith (commended 2023) can be used to pray for same-sex couples.
  • Churches that marry same-sex couples: Quakers in Britain (decision 2009), Scottish Episcopal Church (2017), Methodist Church in Britain (2021), Church of Scotland (2022, for ministers who choose to).
  • Secular perspectives: humanists and most liberal secular ethics: consenting adult relationships are a private good; equal treatment is a matter of justice and rights.
Mission 6

Contraception and childlessness

  • Lambeth Conference 1930 (Resolution 15): Anglican bishops accept contraception where there is a morally sound reason. Most Protestant churches now leave it to a couple's conscience.
  • Pope Paul VI, Humanae Vitae (1968): every marital act must stay open to life (§11); artificial contraception is excluded (§14); natural family planning, using infertile periods, is allowed (§16).
  • Many Catholics in Britain and elsewhere do not follow §14: a gap between official teaching and practice.
  • Secular view: contraception gives control over fertility, protects health, and was central to women's equality.
  • Humanae Vitae is one of the board's suggested sources.
Mission 6Diversity

Beyond Christianity

  • Islam: sex only within marriage (nikah): "And come not near unto adultery. Lo! it is an abomination and an evil way." (Qur'an 17:32, Pickthall). Mainstream Sunni and Shi'a scholars forbid same-sex acts, citing the story of Lut (e.g. 7:80–81); some progressive Muslim groups dissent. Many scholars permit contraception, citing hadith on withdrawal ('azl).
  • Judaism: sex is good and a duty within marriage; the first command is "Be fruitful, and multiply" (Genesis 1:28, JPS 1917). Orthodox: Leviticus 18:22 binding; contraception often allowed for women with rabbinic advice. Reform and Liberal: many rabbis officiate at same-sex weddings; Liberal Judaism in Britain was an early supporter.
  • Pattern: in each tradition the split falls between those who treat the law as fixed and those who re-read it through ethical principle.
Mission 6

How teachings changed

  • Re-reading scripture: e.g. Leviticus as part of a holiness code for ancient Israel; Romans 1 about idolatry and excess, not loving partnerships (contested by traditionalists).
  • New knowledge: psychology treats sexual orientation as a stable part of identity, not a chosen vice.
  • Experience: Christians knowing gay and lesbian Christians in faithful partnerships.
  • Institutions decide: Church of England House of Bishops, Issues in Human Sexuality (1991): same-sex partnerships fall short of the ideal, but lay people who enter them in conscience may be welcomed; clergy may not.
  • Then: Lambeth 1998 Resolution I.10 (homosexual practice "incompatible with Scripture"); Living in Love and Faith (2020); Prayers of Love and Faith (2023).
  • Spec: "the ideas of religious figures and institutions".

Mission 6

Secularism and the law

  • Secularism: religion's influence on law and public life declines; law rests on consent, harm and equality.
  • Sexual Offences Act 1967: male homosexual acts partly decriminalised in England and Wales (Scotland 1980; Northern Ireland 1982).
  • Civil Partnership Act 2004; Marriage (Same Sex Couples) Act 2013 (England and Wales; first weddings 2014); Scotland 2014; Northern Ireland 2020.
  • The 2013 Act protects religious freedom: no religious body must conduct same-sex weddings.
  • The contraceptive pill on the NHS from 1961 (at first for married women); no-fault divorce in England and Wales from 2022, so adultery need no longer be alleged.
  • Debate: did secular change drive church change, or did Christians help drive the law (e.g. some church leaders backed the 1957 Wolfenden Report)?
Mission 6Evaluate

Do religious teachings still have a role?

  • Claim: Religious teachings on sex have a continuing role in sexual ethics.
  • For: They guard values secular ethics also prizes: commitment, faithfulness, care for children.
  • For: Thinkers such as Dominian show traditions can renew themselves without losing their core.
  • Against: In secular Britain many people do not accept religious authority; the law now rests on consent and equality.
  • Against: Some teachings (e.g. on same-sex relationships, contraception) are rejected even by many believers.
  • Judgement criteria: Is the teaching about rules or about values? Does it address believers only, or society? Can it change and still be the same tradition?
  • Spec issue: "whether or not religious beliefs and practices … have a continuing role".

Mission 7

Natural law applied

  • Aquinas: the natural purpose (telos) of sex is reproduction and the education of children (primary precepts), within a stable society.
  • So sex belongs in marriage, which gives children a secure home. Premarital sex and adultery fail that purpose; adultery also harms ordered living in society.
  • Same-sex acts and contraception separate sex from reproduction, so traditional natural law judges them wrong (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II, q. 154).
  • Catholic teaching today adds the unitive purpose (Humanae Vitae, 1968).
  • Issues raised: infertile couples marry and have sex: so is reproduction really necessary? Same-sex behaviour occurs in nature: what counts as "natural"? Proportionalists may allow exceptions for a proportionate reason.
Mission 7

Situation ethics applied

  • Fletcher: only agape is always good; rules about sex are guides, not absolutes.
  • Personalism: put people first. Ask: is anyone used, deceived or harmed? Is this the most loving act?
  • Premarital sex: may be right in a loving, responsible relationship; wrong if it exploits.
  • Adultery: almost always betrays someone and harms them, so usually wrong, but not by rule.
  • Same-sex relationships: a faithful, loving relationship can be right.
  • Polyamorous relationships: right only if everyone knows and consents, and no partner or child is neglected; love and justice must be shared fairly.
Quick check
?Why might a situationist approve a faithful, loving same-sex relationship that traditional natural law condemns?
Mission 7

Kant applied

  • Humanity formula: never treat a person merely as a means. Kant thought sexual desire on its own does exactly that: it wants the other's body, not the person (Lectures on Ethics, paraphrase).
  • His answer: marriage, a lifelong, equal and reciprocal union, in which each gives themselves and receives the other (Metaphysics of Morals, 1797).
  • Adultery breaks a promise: a maxim of breaking promises cannot be universalised.
  • Kant himself condemned same-sex acts as against nature.
  • Issues raised: many modern Kantians argue that honest, consenting, committed relationships, same-sex included, can treat each partner as an end. Is Kant's conclusion his principle, or his culture?
Mission 7

Utilitarianism applied

  • Bentham (act): weigh pleasure and pain. Consensual sex that harms no one adds to happiness.
  • Bentham wrote an essay (c. 1785, published only in 1978) arguing that same-sex acts should not be punished, because they cause no harm.
  • Adultery: the pain of betrayal to a spouse and children usually outweighs the pleasure.
  • Mill (often read as a rule utilitarian): rules of fidelity and honesty, generally followed, maximise happiness; higher pleasures of mind and feeling outrank merely bodily ones.
  • Contraception: reduces unwanted pregnancies and the spread of infection, so utilitarians usually support it.
  • Issues raised: can a calculus measure love or betrayal? Could secret adultery that is never found out be "right" for an act utilitarian?
Quick check
?Why did Kant think sexual relations are morally acceptable only within marriage?
Mission 7Read the source

Mill's harm principle

John Stuart Mill (1806–1873), On Liberty (1859), Chapter 1:

> "…the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilised community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others."

> "Over himself, over his own body and mind, the individual is sovereign."

Q1 On this principle, may the law forbid sex between consenting adults in private?

Show answer

No: no harm to others, so the law has no right to interfere.

Q2 Which laws on sex would still pass Mill's test?

Show answer

Laws protecting others from harm: consent, protection of children, laws against coercion and exploitation.

  • Mill, On Liberty, Chapter 1 is a suggested source.
Mission 7

Private choice or society's business?

  • Wolfenden Report (1957): the law should not punish private, consensual homosexual acts between adults; there is a realm of private morality that is not the law's business (paraphrase). Led to the Sexual Offences Act 1967.
  • Patrick Devlin (1905–1992), The Enforcement of Morals (1965): a society is held together by shared morality; the law may defend it against conduct that ordinary people find deeply offensive.
  • H. L. A. Hart (1907–1992), Law, Liberty and Morality (1963): following Mill, only harm, not offence or disapproval, justifies criminal law.
  • Today's law still regulates sex: consent (Sexual Offences Act 2003), age of consent, bigamy, image-based abuse.
  • Religious voices disagree: some agree with Devlin that law teaches values; many accept a secular law while keeping stricter standards for believers.
  • Spec issue: "entirely private and personal, or … subject to societal norms and legislation".

Mission 7Evaluate

Are normative theories useful in sexual ethics?

  • Claim: Normative theories are useful for deciding sexual ethics.
  • For: They give reasons, not just reactions: a utilitarian or Kantian argument can be tested.
  • For: Situation ethics and utilitarianism attend to real people and harms; Kant's "never merely as a means" names the wrong in exploitation.
  • Against: Natural law and Kant reached conclusions on homosexuality that many now see as products of their age; theories may just rationalise prior views.
  • Against: Sex involves love, trust and vulnerability: hard to calculate (utilitarianism) or universalise (Kant).
  • Judgement criteria: Does the theory give clear guidance? Does it respect persons? Can it handle new kinds of relationship? Does it depend on religious belief?
  • Spec issue: "whether normative theories are useful".
Mission 7

Changing social attitudes: strengths and weaknesses

  • Strengths: less shame and secrecy; protection from discrimination; marriage open to same-sex couples; contraception has given people, especially women, more control over their lives.
  • Weaknesses (argued by critics, religious and secular): sex treated as a consumer product; pornography and casual sex linked to objectification; fewer stable relationships for children.
  • Supporters answer: stability is better served by consent, honesty and equal rights than by stigma.
  • Ethical theories cut both ways: Kant's ban on objectification is used by conservatives (against casual sex) and liberals (against coercion).
Mission 7Worked answer

Applying and evaluating one theory

Task: "Kantian ethics is the most useful approach to premarital sex." Discuss. One paragraph:

  • Point: Kant's humanity formula is useful because it names what is wrong in exploitative sex.
  • Develop: sex that treats a partner as a means (e.g. deception, pressure) fails respect for persons, whatever pleasure it produces.
  • Counter: but Kant's own conclusion (sex only in marriage) seems to go beyond the formula; consenting, honest partners can respect each other outside marriage.
  • Judge (for this paragraph): useful as a test of how people treat each other; less useful as a rule about where sex belongs.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Cognitivism (true or false) vs non-cognitivism; realism (mind-independent facts) vs anti-realism.
  • Naturalism defines "good" by a natural property, e.g. pleasure (Bentham, Mill).
  • Challenges: Hume's is–ought gap; Moore's naturalistic fallacy and open question argument.

Mission 2

  • Non-naturalism: "good" is real and objective but not a natural property; Moore: good is simple and indefinable, like yellow.
  • Intuitionism: basic moral truths are self-evident to a mature, reflective mind; intuition is not infallible.
  • Prichard: "ought" is indefinable and known immediately by moral thinking. Challenges: no proof, differing intuitions, no way to resolve conflicts, queerness.

Mission 3

  • Logical positivism: meaningful statements are analytic or verifiable; Ayer: moral statements are neither, so they express feelings and aim to persuade (non-cognitivism).
  • Emotivism is not subjectivism: it expresses feeling ("boo!"), it does not report it; challenges: no basic principles, pointless debate, no universal wrongs.
  • Relativism; Mackie's error theory: moral claims aim at facts but are all false.

Mission 4

  • Aquinas: ratio (reason, from the image of God); synderesis (habit directing to good, away from evil); conscientia (reason judging particular acts). Erring conscience binds; vincible ignorance does not excuse, invincible does.
  • Religious views: Butler (supreme principle), Newman (voice of God), Catholic teaching (informed conscience), Fletcher (a verb). Non-religious: Freud, Fromm, Piaget, Darwin, humanists.

Mission 5

  • Freud: psychosexual stages; the super-ego, heir of the Oedipus complex, holds internalised parental and social ideals; guilt is tension between super-ego and ego, often unconscious.
  • Aquinas and Freud compared: reason v the unconscious; God present v God explained; guilt as verdict v guilt as tension. Some argue "conscience" is an umbrella term.

Mission 6

  • Traditional Christian teaching: sex within lifelong marriage of a man and a woman, unitive and procreative; churches now range from that view to celebrating same-sex marriage.
  • Change came through re-reading scripture, new knowledge, experience, institutions (Humanae Vitae 1968; Issues in Human Sexuality 1991) and a more secular law.

Mission 7

  • Natural law asks about purpose; Kant about treating people as ends; situation ethics about agape in the situation; utilitarianism about happiness and harm.
  • Mill and Hart: law only to prevent harm; Devlin: law may defend a shared morality.
  • Evaluate theories by clarity, respect for persons, ability to handle new cases and dependence on belief.
