Mission 1

Mission 1: How can just two words tell you where any living thing fits among millions?

Make a guess: How can just two words tell you where any living thing fits among millions?

Mission 1

Too many to know one by one

  • About 1.2 million species are catalogued; an estimated 8.7 million species of eukaryotes exist.
  • Classification: sorting organisms into groups.
  • Why classify?
    • identify an organism and name it without confusion;
    • predict features of a group's members;
    • show evolutionary relationships;
Mission 1

The species: the unit everything rests on

  • Every classification is built on the species.
  • Two organisms are the same species if they can interbreed to produce fertile offspring.
Mission 1

Groups within groups

  • Each group is a taxon (plural taxa).
  • Domain → kingdom → phylum → class → order → family → genus → species.
  • Smaller groups sit inside larger ones, with no overlap.
  • Lower taxa: fewer species, more features shared.
  • Humans: Eukarya · Animalia · Chordata · Mammalia · Primates · Hominidae · Homo · Homo sapiens.
Quick check
?The red fox, Vulpes vulpes, and the grey wolf, Canis lupus, are both placed in the family Canidae. Which statement must be true?
Mission 1

Two-part names

  • Binomial system (Linnaeus): every species has a two-part name.
  • 1st word: genus, with a capital letter.
  • 2nd word: species, all lower case. It means nothing on its own.
  • Italics in print; underline when handwritten.
  • Same genus = similar traits, usually close relatives: Panthera leo (lion), Panthera tigris (tiger).
  • After first use, shorten the genus: P. leo.
  • Say: "Homo sapiens" — not: "Homo Sapiens", and not "sapiens" alone.
Mission 1

Why not just use common names?

  • A binomial is universal: the same in every country and language.
  • Common names mislead. "Robin" means Erithacus rubecula in Britain but Turdus migratorius in North America.
  • One species can have many local names; a "starfish" is not a fish.
  • The genus shows at once which species are close relatives.
Quick check
?Which statement about the scientific name of the tiger, Panthera tigris, is correct?
Mission 1

Classify by ancestry

  • Phylogenetic classification: groups reflect evolutionary origins and relationships.
  • Ideal: every member of a group descends from one common ancestor.
  • Advantage: features of a group can be predicted. A newly found member probably shares them.
  • Evidence: anatomy, plus DNA and protein sequences.
  • Classification is tentative: it changes as new evidence arrives.
Quick check
?What is the main advantage of a classification in which every group corresponds to evolutionary relationships?
Mission check

?The raven is Corvus corax, the rook is Corvus frugilegus and the magpie is Pica pica. What can be concluded from these names alone?

Two words: the genus places it among its closest relatives; the species word picks it out from them.

Mission 2: How can one protein from your cells show you are closer to a dog than to a chicken?

Make a guess: How can one protein from your cells show you are closer to a dog than to a chicken?

Mission 2

Clues to who is related

  • Relatives share features inherited from a common ancestor.
  • Evidence:
    • anatomy: homologous structures, in living species and fossils;
    • DNA and mRNA base sequences;
    • amino acid sequences of proteins.
  • After two lineages split, mutations build up separately in each.
  • So: fewer differences → more recent common ancestor → closer relatives.
  • Gene technology changed the method: from inferring DNA differences from observable characteristics to reading base sequences directly.
  • Say: "share a more recent common ancestor" — not: "one evolved from the other".

Mission 2

More ways to measure similarity

  • Immunology: inject a protein (e.g. serum albumin) from species A into a rabbit; it makes antibodies against it.
  • Mix those antibodies with the same protein from species B. More precipitate = more similar proteins = closer relatives.
  • Genome sequencing: compare whole sets of DNA base sequences directly.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Moving costs energy. So what makes it worth an animal's while to move at all?

Make a guess: Moving costs energy. So what makes it worth an animal's while to move at all?

Mission 3

Responding pays

  • Environments change: light, humidity, temperature, food, predators.
  • Organisms that respond to change increase their chance of survival and of reproducing.
  • Locomotion: moving the whole body from place to place. It costs energy.
  • It pays when the move brings a benefit: food, safety, a mate or better conditions.
Mission 3

Taxes and kineses

  • Innate behaviour: inherited; performed correctly the first time; no learning needed.
  • Taxis: a directional movement in response to the direction of a stimulus.
  • Towards the stimulus: positive. Away from it: negative.
  • Woodlice move away from light: negative phototaxis. Euglena swims towards light: positive phototaxis.
  • Kinesis: a non-directional response. The rate of movement, or of turning, changes with the intensity of a stimulus.
  • Both are simple responses that keep a mobile organism in a favourable environment.
Mission 3

How a kinesis gathers woodlice in the damp

  • Dry air: a woodlouse moves fast and turns rarely. It soon leaves the dry area.
  • Damp air: it slows down and turns more often. It stays.
  • Over time, most woodlice end up in the damp, without ever steering towards it.
  • The advantage: woodlice lose water quickly through their exoskeleton. Damp places cut water loss; dark, damp places also hide them from predators.
Quick check
?Woodlice are placed in a dish that is damp on one half and dry on the other, under even, dim light. On the dry half they walk faster and turn less often; on the damp half they slow down and turn more. After 15 minutes most are on the damp half. Which term describes this response?
Mission 3Practical

Choice chamber

  • Required practical 10: investigation into the effect of an environmental variable on the movement of an animal using either a choice chamber or a maze.
  • Damp side: water under the gauze. Dry side: a drying agent, such as silica gel.
  • Cover the chamber so light is even. Add 10 woodlice through the centre; wait 10 minutes; count each side.
  • Repeat with fresh woodlice, swapping the damp and dry sides between runs.
  • Keep constant: light, temperature, number and type of animal, time.
  • A control differs from the experimental set-up only in the variable being tested.
  • Handle woodlice gently with a soft paintbrush; return them to where they were found.
  • Maze option: a T-maze; record which arm each animal turns into.
  • Analysis: a χ² test on the counts (next screen).
Mission 3

Worked example: is the preference real?

Illustrative data: 40 woodlice; after 10 minutes, 29 on the damp side and 11 on the dry side.

  • Null hypothesis: no preference. Expected: 20 on each side.
  • χ² = Σ (O − E)²/E
  • Damp: (29 − 20)²/20 = 4.05. Dry: (11 − 20)²/20 = 4.05. χ² = 8.10.
  • Degrees of freedom = number of categories − 1 = 1. Critical value at p = 0.05: 3.84.
  • 8.10 > 3.84: reject the null hypothesis. The difference is significant: unlikely to be due to chance.
  • Say: "the difference is significant, so we reject the null hypothesis" — not: "this proves woodlice prefer damp".

Mission check

?Larvae of a hypothetical beetle lose water quickly in dry air. Forty larvae are placed in a dark choice chamber with a damp side and a dry side. After 15 minutes, 34 are on the damp side and 6 on the dry side. A chi-squared test gives χ² = 19.6; the critical value at p = 0.05 is 3.84. Which conclusion is best supported?

Moving costs energy, but a response that keeps an animal in damp, dark or food-rich places raises its chance of surviving.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why would an animal spend energy singing, displaying or fighting instead of just feeding?

Make a guess: Why would an animal spend energy singing, displaying or fighting instead of just feeding?

Mission 4

Courtship: a chain of signals

  • Courtship: behaviour before mating; usually specific to one species.
  • Three-spined stickleback (Tinbergen):
  • The male, with a red belly, swims a zigzag dance. A female swollen with eggs swims towards him, head up.
  • He leads her to his nest; she enters.
  • He nudges the base of her tail; she lays her eggs. He follows and fertilises them.
  • Each action is the stimulus for the partner's next action. If one step fails, the chain stops.
Mission 4

Why court?

  • Courtship is a necessary precursor to successful mating.
  • Species recognition: only partners of the same species give and answer the right signals. No gametes wasted on matings that cannot give fertile offspring.
  • Recognise a partner of the opposite sex.
  • Show that the partner is sexually mature and ready: mating is synchronised. Vital when fertilisation is external.
  • Choose a high-quality mate.
Quick check
?Female three-spined sticklebacks were given a choice of males. They chose males with a brighter red belly more often. Males infected with parasites had duller red bellies. Under green light, which hides the red, the preference disappeared. Which conclusion is best supported?
Mission check

?Males of two related fiddler crab species live on the same mudflat. Each male waves one enlarged claw in a rhythm typical of his own species. Waving uses energy and draws the attention of birds that eat crabs. Females approach only males waving in their own species' rhythm. Which explanation best accounts for waving persisting?

Singing, displaying and fighting cost energy and risk, but they win mates, territory or rank. An individual that leaves more offspring passes on the alleles for the behaviour.

Mission 5

Mission 5: The first human genome took years and a fortune to read. What can we do now that it is quick and cheap?

Make a guess: The first human genome took years and a fortune to read. What can we do now that it is quick and cheap?

Mission 5

Reading whole genomes

  • Genome sequencing finds the order of nucleotides in all of an organism's DNA.
  • Human Genome Project (1990–2003): about 3 billion base pairs; about 20 000–21 000 protein-coding genes.
  • Thousands of genomes are now sequenced: bacteria, crops, the malaria mosquito, and extinct Neanderthals, from bone.
Mission 5

Faster and cheaper

  • High-throughput ("next-generation") sequencing:
    • DNA is cut into millions of short fragments.
    • All are sequenced at the same time.
    • Computers assemble overlapping reads into the genome.
  • Methods are continuously updated and automated.
  • A human genome now takes about a day.

Mission 5

From genome to proteome

  • Using the genetic code, a gene's base sequence predicts the amino acid sequence of its polypeptide.
  • Proteome: the full range of proteins a cell can produce.
  • Simple organisms (e.g. bacteria): little non-coding DNA, no introns. So the proteome can be worked out from the genome.
    • Use: find surface proteins to try as vaccine antigens. A meningitis B vaccine was found this way.
  • Complex organisms: introns, other non-coding DNA and regulatory genes. The proteome cannot easily be read from the genome.
Quick check
?Why is it easier to predict a bacterium's proteome from its genome than to predict a human's?
Mission 5

Comparing genomes: bioinformatics

  • Bioinformatics: storing and analysing sequence data in databases, using computer software.
  • Between individuals: single-base differences (SNPs) are linked to traits and disease risk.
  • Between species: more similar sequences mean a more recent common ancestor. This tests evolutionary relationships.
Mission 5

Genomes in medicine

  • Personalised medicine: choose a treatment or dose to suit a person's genotype.
  • Cancer: sequence a tumour; choose drugs that target its particular mutations.
  • Rare diseases: a genome can reveal the cause, ending years of uncertain diagnosis.
  • A genome shows risks and likely responses, not a fixed future.
Quick check

Mission 6

Extinction, then and now

  • Species have always gone extinct, at a slow background rate.
  • Today the rate is far higher. Vertebrate species lost since 1900 would have taken 800–10,000 years to disappear at the background rate (depending on the group).

Mission 6

What is driving the losses?

  • Habitat loss: deforestation, clearance for farming, urbanisation, drainage of wetlands.
  • Over-exploitation: hunting, overfishing, collecting.
  • Pollution: pesticides, fertiliser run-off, plastics.
  • Invasive species, pests and diseases, spread by global transport.
Mission 6

Farming and biodiversity

  • Farming techniques that reduce biodiversity:
  • Monoculture: one crop over large areas; few habitats, few food sources.
  • Removing hedgerows, ponds and field margins; draining wetlands.
  • Pesticides and herbicides kill non-target species; fertilisers let a few fast-growing plants dominate.
  • Intensive farming gives more food and income, at a cost to biodiversity.
  • The balance: payments to farmers for conservation (agri-environment schemes), hedgerows and wild margins kept.
Quick check
?A farmer removes the hedgerows between three small fields to make one large field of wheat, sprayed with herbicide. Which statement best explains the effect on biodiversity?
Mission check

?In the early 1800s the passenger pigeon was probably the most numerous bird in North America, with flocks of billions. Through the 1800s it was hunted on a commercial scale, and the eastern forests where it fed and nested were cleared for farming. The last known bird died in a zoo in 1914. Which explanation is best supported?

Extinction is natural; this pace is not. Human activity is removing species far faster than new ones arise.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A binomial names the genus (capital) and the species (lower case); together they name one species, used worldwide.
  • Taxa are nested with no overlap, from domain (or kingdom) down to species, and the ideal grouping follows common ancestry.

Mission 2

  • Differences in DNA, mRNA and protein sequences build up after lineages split, so fewer differences mean a more recent common ancestor.

Mission 3

  • Responses that keep an animal in favourable conditions increase its chance of survival: a taxis is directional; a kinesis changes rate, not direction.
  • A choice chamber plus a chi-squared test shows whether a preference is significant.

Mission 4

  • Courtship lets animals recognise their own species and a ready, high-quality mate: costly signals persist because they raise reproductive success.

Mission 5

  • Sequencing reads nucleotide order; it has become automated, faster and vastly cheaper, so whole genomes are compared routinely.
  • Uses: relationships between species, outbreaks, personalised medicine; a genome gives risks, not a fixed future.
  • The genome predicts polypeptides, but non-coding DNA, regulation and cell-specific expression mean it does not simply give the proteome.

Mission 6

  • Today's extinction rate is far above the background rate, driven by human activity: habitat loss, over-exploitation, pollution, invasive species and climate change.
  • Intensive farming raises food production but lowers biodiversity.
