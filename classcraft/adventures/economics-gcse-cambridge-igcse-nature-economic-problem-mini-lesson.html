Mission 1

Mission 1: Even the richest person on Earth cannot have everything. Why not?

Make a guess: Even the richest person on Earth cannot have everything. Why not?

Mission 1

Needs, wants and why we produce

  • Needs: things we must have to survive: food, water, shelter, clothing.
  • Wants: things we would like beyond our needs: a games console, a holiday.
  • Needs and wants change over time. A phone was a luxury in the 1990s. Today many people need one to study, work and bank.
  • As incomes rise, people want more and better things. Wants never run out.
  • Purpose of economic activity: to produce goods and services that satisfy needs and wants.
Mission 1

The basic economic problem

  • Scarcity: resources are limited, but wants are unlimited.
  • So everyone must choose. Choosing one thing means going without another.
  • It hits every group:
    • Consumers: a limited income, many things to buy.
    • Workers: limited hours; more overtime means less free time.
    • Firms: limited money and staff; a new factory or a new shop?
    • Governments: limited tax revenue; hospitals, schools or roads?
  • Even the richest person has only 24 hours a day.
  • Say: "scarcity" (limited resources, always) — not: "shortage" (a short-term lack of one product in the shops).
Quick check
?Which statement best describes the basic economic problem?
Mission 1

Is anything not scarce?

  • Economic good: made with scarce resources, so it has an opportunity cost. Almost everything.
  • Free good: needs no scarce resources. There is more than enough for everyone, even at a zero price. Its opportunity cost is zero.
  • Examples: air on a remote mountain, sunlight.
  • Free of charge is not the same as a free good. A state school is paid for by taxes and uses teachers and buildings. It is an economic good.
  • A free good can become scarce: clean air in a polluted city.
Quick check
?Which of these is a free good?
Mission 1

Opportunity cost

  • Opportunity cost: the next best alternative given up when a choice is made.
  • Only the next best alternative counts, not all of them added together.
  • Consumer: spending on concert tickets instead of new trainers.
  • Worker: a year at college instead of a year's wages.
  • Producer: a new delivery van instead of new shop tills.
  • Government: a new hospital instead of a new school.
  • Opportunity cost shapes decisions: if the next best option becomes more valuable, people switch. A worker offered higher pay elsewhere may quit their job.
Mission 1

Worked example: what does Saturday cost?

  • Illustrative data. Amounts in any one currency.
  • Jo has one free Saturday. Her options, from best to worst:

1. Go to a friend's party (free entry)

2. Work a shop shift that pays 50

3. Babysit for 35

  • Jo chooses the party.
  • Step 1: find the next best alternative: the shop shift.
  • Step 2: its value is the opportunity cost: 50.
  • Not 50 + 35 = 85: Jo could only have done one of the jobs.
  • Not 0: free entry does not mean nothing was given up.
Quick check

Mission 1

Costs and benefits: the cod collapse

  • Choices have benefits and costs: economic, social and environmental.
  • Renewable resources (fish, forests) can regrow. But renewable is not unlimited: used faster than they regrow, they run out.
  • Real case: Canada banned fishing for northern cod off Newfoundland on 2 July 1992, after the stock collapsed.
  • Economic cost: nearly 30,000 people in Newfoundland and Labrador lost their jobs.
  • Social cost: fishing towns lost their main source of work.
  • Environmental cost: a wild fish stock was badly damaged. A commercial cod fishery returned only in 2024.
  • Sustainability: meeting today's needs without harming future generations' ability to meet theirs. Big catches today had an opportunity cost: fish for the future.
Mission 1

Three questions every economy must answer

  • What to produce? (food or computers; hospitals or roads)
  • How to produce? (more workers or more machines)
  • For whom to produce? (who gets the output: by income, need or another rule)
Quick check
?A government decides that hospital treatment will be given according to medical need, not ability to pay. Which basic economic question is it mainly answering?
Mission check

?A city council turns an empty plot of land into a park with free entry. If it had not built the park, the council would have built a car park and earned parking fees. Its other option, ranked below the car park, was selling the land for housing. What is the opportunity cost of the park?

Scarce resources always have another use, so even a free park costs the next best thing that land could have been.

Make a guess: What four ingredients does every product in the world need before it can be made?

Mission 2

The four factors of production

  • An economic resource (a factor of production) is anything used to produce goods and services.
  • Land: all natural resources: soil, water, oil, fish, minerals.
  • Labour: human effort, physical and mental, used in production.
  • Capital: man-made goods used to make other goods: machines, tools, factories, delivery vans.
  • Enterprise: the entrepreneur who organises the other three and takes the risk of the business.
  • Every product needs a combination of factors. A bakery: land (a site, wheat), labour (bakers), capital (ovens), enterprise (the owner).
  • Say: "capital = machines, tools, buildings" — not: "capital = money". Money buys capital, but money cannot make anything.
Mission 2

What each factor earns

FactorReward
LandRent
LabourWages
CapitalInterest
EnterpriseProfit
  • The reward is the payment for using the factor in production.
  • Profit is what is left for the entrepreneur after all other costs are paid. It can be negative: a loss. That is the risk.
Mission 2

Worked example: a café's month

  • Illustrative data. Amounts in any one currency.
  • A café takes 10,000 in revenue in one month.
  • Step 1: pay for land: rent 2,000.
  • Step 2: pay for labour: wages 5,000.
  • Step 3: pay for capital: interest 500 on the loan for the coffee machines.
  • Step 4: what is left rewards enterprise: 10,000 − 2,000 − 5,000 − 500 = 2,500 profit.
  • If revenue fell to 7,000, profit would be 7,000 − 7,500 = −500: a loss for the owner.
Quick check
?A fishing firm uses a boat, a crew, the fish in the sea, and an owner who borrowed to buy the boat. Which is classed as capital?
Quick check
?A landowner rents a field to a farmer. A bank lends the farmer money to buy a tractor. Which rewards do the landowner and the bank receive?
Mission 2

More factors, better factors

  • Quantity of factors can change:
    • Land: reclamation turns sea or marsh into farmland. The Netherlands drained part of an inland sea to create the province of Flevoland.
    • Labour: population growth and migration add workers; emigration removes them.
    • Capital: firms invest in more machines and buildings.
  • Quality can change too:
    • Land: fertiliser and irrigation raise crop yields; over-farming can wear soil out.
    • Labour: education and training build human capital (skills and knowledge).
    • Capital: new technology makes machines more productive.
  • More or better factors → the economy can produce more.
Mission 2

Worked example: productivity

  • Productivity: output per unit of a factor (per worker, per hectare, per machine).
  • Illustrative data: a farm has 200 hectares of land.
  • Before irrigation: 3 tonnes of wheat per hectare → 200 × 3 = 600 tonnes.
  • After irrigation: 4.5 tonnes per hectare → 200 × 4.5 = 900 tonnes.
  • Change: (900 − 600) ÷ 600 × 100 = +50%.
  • Same quantity of land, better quality: higher land productivity.
  • Say: "productivity rose" (output per unit) — not: "production rose", which could just mean more land or workers.
Quick check
?Which change raises the quality of a country's labour rather than its quantity?
Mission check

?A family runs a strawberry farm. Which list correctly matches each item to its factor of production?

Ask "natural, human, man-made, or risk-taking?" and every input finds its factor.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why does each extra unit of one thing cost a country more and more of another?

Make a guess: A country moves workers from farms to factories. Each extra batch of machines costs it more food than the last. What is the most likely reason?

Mission 3

The curve of the possible

  • A production possibility curve (PPC) shows the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can make with its resources.
  • It assumes:
    • only two goods
    • a fixed amount of resources and fixed technology
    • on the curve, all resources are used.
  • Resources are limited, so the curve has an edge: scarcity.
  • Picking one point means giving up others: choice.
Mission 3

Worked example: opportunity cost on the curve

  • Illustrative data: an economy makes food and machines.
PointMachinesFood (tonnes)
A0150
B10140
C20120
D3090
E4050
F500
  • Step 1: B → C adds 10 machines and loses 140 − 120 = 20 tonnes of food.
  • Step 2: per machine: 20 ÷ 10 = 2 tonnes of food.
  • Step 3: repeat along the curve: 1, 2, 3, 4, then 5 tonnes per machine (E → F).
  • Moving along the curve, the opportunity cost of each machine rises.
  • Say: "the opportunity cost of 1 machine is 2 tonnes of food" — not: a money price.
Quick check
?Use the table on the previous screen (illustrative data). The economy moves from D (30 machines, 90 tonnes of food) to E (40 machines, 50 tonnes of food). What is the opportunity cost of each extra machine?
Mission 3

Why the curve bows outward

  • Rising opportunity cost → the curve is bowed outward.
  • Why: resources are not equally good at making both goods.
  • The first workers moved to machines are the ones best at machines and worst at farming. Little food is lost.
  • The last ones moved are expert farmers. A lot of food is lost for each machine.
  • If resources were equally good at both, the cost would stay the same: a straight-line PPC.
Mission 3Graph lab

Slide back along the curve

  • Takeaway: rising opportunity cost works in both directions. On a straight line the cost stays the same.
Mission 3

Inside, on and outside the curve

  • On the curve (A–F): all resources are used. The economy is at its potential.
  • Inside the curve (point U): some resources are unemployed, e.g. in a recession: jobless workers, idle factories.
  • Outside the curve (point X): unattainable with today's resources and technology.
  • Points on or inside the curve are attainable.
  • Also said: a point under the curve (inside) and a point beyond the curve (outside).
  • From U, the economy can make more of both goods without giving anything up: the resources were idle.
Quick check
?An economy is at a point inside its PPC. Which statement is correct?
Mission 3

Movements along and shifts of the curve

  • Movement along the curve: resources are moved between the two goods. The curve stays put.
  • Outward shift: more or better resources or technology: more workers, investment in machines, education and training, new discoveries.
  • Inward shift: resources lost or damaged: war, natural disaster, emigration, resources running out.
  • If only one good is affected, the curve pivots: only that end moves.
  • Real case: the 2011 earthquake and tsunami in Japan destroyed buildings, ports, roads and equipment. Japan's government put the damage at about 16.9 trillion yen. Lost capital → an inward shift.
  • Say: "a movement along the PPC" for a change of mix — not: "a shift".
Mission 3Graph lab

Better machines only

  • Takeaway: only the machines end moves out. Each machine now costs half as much food.
Mission 3

Economic growth on the PPC

  • Economic growth: an increase in the economy's output.
  • Using unemployed resources: move from U (inside) to the curve. Output rises; the curve does not move.
  • Positive growth in what the economy can produce: the curve shifts outward. Causes: more workers, more capital, better technology, better education and training, new resources found.
  • Negative growth: the curve shifts inward. Causes: natural disasters, war, emigration, resources used up.
Quick check
?A country's farms and factories are unchanged, but thousands of skilled workers leave to work abroad. At the same time, some workers move from farming into making machines. How should this be shown on a PPC for food and machines?

Mission 4

Mission 4: Can an economy keep growing just by people spending more?

Make a guess: Can an economy keep growing just by people spending more?

Mission 4

Measuring output: GDP

  • GDP (gross domestic product): the total value of all goods and services produced in a country in a year.
  • Economic growth: an increase in real GDP over time.
  • Nominal GDP uses the prices of that year, so it rises when prices rise.
  • Real GDP removes the effect of rising prices. It shows whether more is really being produced.
  • GDP per capita (per person) = GDP ÷ population. It is a better guide to average living standards.
  • Say: "real GDP rose" — not: "GDP rose", when you mean the economy produced more.
Mission 4

Worked example: growth rates

  • Illustrative data.
Year 1Year 2
Nominal GDP800 bn856 bn
Real GDP (year 1 prices)800 bn824 bn
Population40 m40.4 m
  • Nominal growth = (856 − 800) ÷ 800 × 100 = 7%. Part of that is just higher prices.
  • Real growth = (824 − 800) ÷ 800 × 100 = 3%.
  • Real GDP per capita: 800 bn ÷ 40 m = 20,000 → 824 bn ÷ 40.4 m = 20,396.
  • Growth per capita = (20,396 − 20,000) ÷ 20,000 × 100 = about 2%.
  • The population grew, so output per person grew less than total output.
Mission 4

Small rates add up

  • Growth builds on growth: each year's rise is on top of last year's.
  • Output per person doubles in about 35 years at 2% a year.
  • At 9% a year, output per person would double in about 8 years.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. Country X: real GDP 1,200 bn, population 300 million. Country Y: real GDP 400 bn, population 50 million. Which statement is correct?
Mission 4

Two kinds of growth on a PPC

  • A PPC shows the most an economy can produce with all its resources fully used.
  • Point A inside the curve: some workers and machines are unemployed (spare capacity).
  • A → B, onto the curve: actual growth. The economy produces more by using spare capacity.
  • PPC1 → PPC2, shifting outwards: potential growth. The economy can produce more.
  • Once the economy is on its PPC, output can only keep rising if the PPC shifts out.
  • An inward shift (PPC1 → PPC0) shows that capacity has fallen: negative growth in what the economy can produce.

  • Takeaway: extra spending raises output only while there is spare capacity. After that, it mostly pushes up prices. Lasting growth needs capacity to grow too.
Quick check

Mission 4

Causes of growth

  • Demand: more spending by households, firms, government or foreign buyers raises output if there is spare capacity.
  • Investment: more and better capital (machines, factories, roads).
  • Technology: new methods and inventions let each worker produce more.
  • Workforce: more workers, e.g. migration or a higher retirement age.
  • Education and training: more skilled workers produce more (human capital).
  • Natural resources: new discoveries, such as oil or minerals.
  • Government policy: stable rules, good infrastructure, support for research.
  • So, growth comes from demand, and from the quantity and quality of resources.
Mission 4

When growth turns negative

  • A recession: a period when real GDP falls. Firms cut jobs, and incomes fall.
  • The usual test: real GDP falls for two quarters (6 months) in a row.
  • Historical data: in the 2008 recession, the UK economy shrank by more than 6%. It took five years to get back to its pre-recession size (ONS).
  • Most years real GDP rises, but not every year. Growth is not guaranteed.
Mission 4

Policies to promote growth

  • Lower interest rates (central bank): cheaper borrowing raises spending and investment.
  • Fiscal policy: more government spending (e.g. on roads, broadband) or lower taxes.
  • Supply-side policies: education and training, help for research, lower taxes on investment.
  • Also: privatisation and deregulation to make firms more efficient (results are debated).
  • Spending policies work in the short run if there is spare capacity. Supply-side policies raise capacity in the long run.
Mission 4

Benefits and costs of growth

BenefitsCosts
Higher incomes and living standardsPollution and emissions
More jobsUsing up resources such as oil and forests
More tax revenue for schools and hospitalsGains can go mostly to some people

  • Real case: since 1978, China's GDP growth averaged over 9% a year. This helped lift almost 800 million people out of extreme poverty (World Bank).
  • Sustainable growth: growth that meets today's needs without harming future generations' ability to meet theirs.
Quick check
?A country's real GDP grows fast for ten years. Over the same years, its rivers become badly polluted and its forests are cut down. Which statement is best?
Mission 4Evaluate

Is growth always good?

It depends on:

  • Who gains: if most of the extra income goes to a few, most people gain little.
  • The environment: cleaner technology can let output grow with less damage.
  • How rich the country is: extra growth matters more for health and poverty in a low-income country.
Mission check

?A small country has no spare capacity: its workers and factories are all fully used. Its government wants output to keep rising for the next ten years. Which plan is most likely to work?

Spending can only use the capacity an economy has. Lasting growth needs that capacity to grow.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Scarcity: limited resources, unlimited wants. Everyone must choose.
  • Opportunity cost: the next best alternative given up, even when the price is zero.
  • Every economy must decide what, how and for whom to produce.

Mission 2

  • Four factors: land (natural), labour (human effort), capital (man-made aids to production), enterprise (organising and risk-taking).
  • Rewards: rent, wages, interest, profit.
  • Quantity and quality of factors can change: reclamation, migration, investment, irrigation, training, technology.

Mission 3

  • A PPC shows the maximum combinations of two goods. On it: all resources used. Inside: unemployed resources. Outside: unattainable.
  • Bowed outward = rising opportunity cost, because resources are not equally good at both goods.
  • Moving resources between goods = movement along. More or better resources or technology = outward shift; losing resources = inward shift.

Mission 4

  • Economic growth is a rise in real GDP. GDP per capita = GDP ÷ population.
  • Actual growth uses spare capacity; potential growth shifts the PPC out.
  • Growth comes from investment, technology, more and better-skilled workers, resources and good policy.
  • Growth raises living standards but can harm the environment and may not be shared fairly.
