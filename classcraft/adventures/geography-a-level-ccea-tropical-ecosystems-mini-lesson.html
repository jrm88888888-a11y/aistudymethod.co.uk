Mission 1

Mission 1: Rainforest, savanna and desert all lie in the hot tropics. What decides which one grows where?

Make a guess: Rainforest, savanna and desert all lie in the hot tropics. What decides which one grows where?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • A2-1.B1.i distribution, climate and biomass (flora, fauna) of tropical forest, tropical grassland and desert ecosystems
  • A2-1.B1.ii role of the Hadley cell and ITCZ in the location and climate of tropical forest, grassland and desert
Mission 1

Three tropical biomes

  • Tropical (moist) forest: near the equator: Amazon, Congo basin, Southeast Asia.
  • Tropical grassland (savanna): either side of the forest belt: grass with scattered trees.
  • Hot desert: towards the tropics of Cancer and Capricorn: Sahara, Arabia, central Australia.
  • Shares of Earth's land: tropical moist forest 14.4%; tropical grasslands and savannas 15.8%; deserts and xeric shrublands 19.3% (this last includes cold deserts).
  • The pattern is roughly in bands parallel to the equator. Why?
Mission 1

The Hadley cell

  • The Sun heats the equator most. Hot air rises, cools, and its water vapour condenses: towering clouds and heavy rain.
  • High up, the air flows poleward. Around 30° N and S it descends into subtropical high pressure.
  • "The sinking air is relatively dry because its moisture has already been released near the Equator".
  • Surface trade winds blow back towards the equator.
  • This loop is the Hadley cell: rising, wet air at the equator; sinking, dry air in the subtropics.
Mission 1

The ITCZ

  • Trade winds from both hemispheres meet in the Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ): a band of low pressure near the equator.
  • There, air rises, giving "frequent thunderstorms and heavy rain".
  • The ITCZ is the rising limb of the Hadley cell; the subtropical highs are its sinking limbs.
Mission 1

The ITCZ moves with the Sun

  • The ITCZ "follows the migration of the Sun's overhead position", about one to two months behind.
  • July–August: well north of the equator over Africa, Asia and Central America.
  • January–February: south over South America, central Africa and Australia.
  • It swings further over land than over sea, as oceans heat more slowly.
  • So each place's rain depends on how long the ITCZ sits over it each year.
Mission 1

Rainforest climate: under the ITCZ all year

  • Singapore, near the equator, 1991–2020:
  • Rain 2,113 mm a year; even the driest month (February) gets 105 mm.
  • 24-hour mean temperature 26.8–28.6 °C all year: an annual range under 2 °C.
  • "There is no distinct wet or dry season in Singapore".
  • The ITCZ never moves far away, so rising air and rain come all year.
Mission 1

Rainforest biomass

  • Flora: tall evergreen trees in layers (emergents, canopy, understorey); lianas and epiphytes; buttress roots; drip-tip leaves shed heavy rain.
  • Constant warmth and water allow growth all year, so the forest holds a very large biomass, mostly in the living trees.
  • Fauna: very high species diversity; many animals live in the canopy (primates, birds, insects).
  • Fast decay in warm, wet conditions recycles nutrients quickly; the soil itself is poor.
Mission 1

Savanna climate: the ITCZ visits

  • Darwin, northern Australia (Bureau of Meteorology, 1941–2026):
  • Rain 1,732 mm a year, but 1,373 mm falls in December–March.
  • May–September gets only 43.9 mm in total: a long dry season.
  • Monthly mean temperature 25.0–29.4 °C (our calculation from mean maxima and minima).
  • In January–February the ITCZ lies over northern Australia: wet season. When it moves north, subtropical high pressure brings the dry season.
Mission 1

Savanna biomass

  • Flora: grasses dominate, with scattered trees adapted to drought and fire (e.g. acacia and baobab in Africa; eucalypts in Australia).
  • Many trees shed leaves or grasses die back in the dry season; growth surges when the rains arrive.
  • Fire in the dry season helps keep trees sparse.
  • Fauna (East African savanna): large herds of grazers such as wildebeest and zebra, which migrate with the rains, and predators such as lions.
  • Biomass is lower than in rainforest because growth stops in the dry season.
Mission 1

Desert climate: under sinking air

  • Alice Springs, central Australia (BOM, 1941–2026):
  • Rain 283 mm a year, spread thinly; no month averages over 43 mm.
  • Monthly mean temperature from 11.9 °C (July) to 29.1 °C (January) (our calculation).
  • Daily range about 15–16 °C (mean max minus mean min, January and July): clear skies let heat escape at night.
  • The ITCZ rarely reaches this far south; sinking, dry air of the subtropical high dominates.
Mission 1

Desert biomass

  • Flora: sparse and widely spaced: succulents that store water, shrubs with deep or wide roots, small leaves or spines that cut water loss.
  • Ephemerals: seeds lie dormant, then germinate, flower and seed quickly after rare rain.
  • Fauna: many animals are nocturnal or burrow to avoid the heat; some get most of their water from food.
  • Low, patchy water means low biomass and low productivity.

Quick check

Mission check

?A town at about 10° N in West Africa gets heavy rain from June to September and almost none for the rest of the year. Which biome would you expect, and why?

The Hadley cell sets the pattern. Under the ITCZ all year: rainforest. Visited by the ITCZ for part of the year: savanna, with a wet and a dry season. Under the sinking limb most of the year: hot desert.

Mission 2

Mission 2: From the 1960s, irrigation turned Central Asian desert into cotton fields. Later, the Aral Sea largely dried up and fields turned salty. How can watering a desert damage it?

Make a guess: From the 1960s, irrigation turned Central Asian desert into cotton fields. Later, the Aral Sea largely dried up and fields turned salty. How can watering a desert damage it?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • A2-1.B2.i use of irrigation in arid/semi-arid tropical environments (regional case study)
  • A2-1.B2.ii environmental and socio-economic benefits and problems of irrigation
  • A2-1.B2.iii possible solutions to the problems of irrigation
Mission 2

Why irrigate a dryland?

  • Aridity (too little water) is not infertility (too few nutrients): given water, many dryland soils grow crops well.
  • In Central Asia, NASA notes that "irrigation made the desert bloom", but it devastated the Aral Sea.
  • Irrigation brings water from rivers or aquifers to fields; the question is what else it brings, and where the water stops.
Mission 2

Case study: the Indus Basin, Pakistan

  • Pakistan's Indus Basin Irrigation System is "the largest contiguous surface water irrigation system in the world": barrages and thousands of kilometres of canals.
  • Twenty-three barrages can divert up to 69,500 m³/s; the Sukkur Barrage (1932) alone can divert 1,642 m³/s.
  • About 129 km³ of Indus water a year is abstracted for irrigation and other uses.
  • The lower basin, Sindh, is hot and arid: crops there depend on canal water.
Mission 2

Benefits

  • Socio-economic: the system's water is "central to the country's water and food security".
  • Canal leakage recharges aquifers: "fresh groundwater exists primarily due to widespread leakage" from canals.
  • Groundwater now supplies over 50% of agricultural water and 90% of domestic water in rural areas.
  • Environmental: recharged aquifers and green farmland where there would otherwise be dry plain.
Mission 2

Problem: waterlogging

  • Seepage from canals into the aquifer raised the water table.
  • Waterlogging still affects 35% of the canal command area, mostly in Sindh.
  • Roots in saturated soil lack oxygen, so yields fall.
  • The World Bank links it to "excess irrigation in areas where groundwater levels are high".
Mission 2

Problem: salinisation

  • All irrigation water carries dissolved salt. The World Bank: irrigation adds "around 16 million tons of salt" to the Indus basin each year.
  • Where the water table is near the surface, water rises through the soil and evaporates, leaving salt behind.
  • Salinity threatens soil health and production in up to 43% of the irrigated area.
  • The Aral basin shows the same process: half of Central Asia's irrigated lands were affected by secondary salinisation (cited by the IPCC).
Quick check
?Why does irrigation make soil salty more often in hot drylands than in humid regions?
Mission 2

Problems: depletion and low returns

  • Over-pumping affects about 18% of the irrigated area; wells have dried up in parts of Punjab.
  • Rice, wheat, sugarcane and cotton use 80% of the water but contribute only 5% of GDP.
  • Poor water management is conservatively estimated to cost 4% of GDP, about $12 billion a year.
  • So the same scheme can be too wet in Sindh and too dry underground in Punjab.
Mission 2

Possible solutions

  • Lower the water table: drains and pumping; the government encouraged groundwater pumping for this.
  • Manage surface and groundwater together ("conjunctively"), with an accountable agency in each province and a modern groundwater database.
  • Modernise irrigation and reform subsidies so prices reflect the real value of water.
  • Field scale: line canals, irrigate less but more precisely (drip, levelled fields), flush salts where drainage exists, grow salt-tolerant crops, which FAO notes may not be "high income cash crops".
Quick check
?A district in Sindh has waterlogged, salty fields. Which response treats the cause rather than a symptom?
Mission check

?A new canal scheme in a hot, semi-arid valley floods fields each season. No drains are built. Ten years later, white crusts cover low-lying fields and yields are falling. What happened?

Watering a desert can damage it when water has nowhere to go: the water table rises, evaporation leaves salt.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Canals made a dry plain between two Niles into one of Africa's largest irrigation projects. What went wrong next?

Make a guess: Canals made a dry plain between two Niles into one of Africa's largest irrigation projects. What went wrong next?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • A2-1.B2.i use of irrigation in arid/semi-arid tropical environments (regional case study)
  • A2-1.B2.ii environmental and socio-economic benefits and problems of irrigation
  • A2-1.B2.iii possible solutions to the problems of irrigation in arid/semi-arid tropics
Mission 3

Why irrigate the arid tropics?

  • Irrigation: supplying water to crops by artificial means (canals, pumps, pipes) where rain is too little or unreliable.
  • Gezira lies in "a hot and semi-arid region".
  • Long-term rainfall: Khartoum 156 mm, Wad Medani 354 mm, Sennar 472 mm a year.
  • Reference evapotranspiration at the scheme is about 5.4–8.6 mm a day: at least about 1,970 mm a year (5.4 × 365; our lower-bound calculation).
  • So crops would lose far more water than rain supplies. A river that rises far away (the Blue Nile) fills the gap.
Mission 3

Case study: the Gezira Scheme, Sudan

  • Between the Blue Nile and White Nile, south of Khartoum.
  • One of "the largest irrigation projects in Africa": 880,000 ha (over 2.1 million feddans).
  • Water comes through two main canals from the Sennar Dam on the Blue Nile.
  • Began in 1911 with an experimental farm at Tayba village.
  • About 100,000 tenant households farm it with the government and the Gezira Board; an average farm is 8.4 ha in four plots.
  • Main crops: cotton, wheat, sorghum, groundnuts and vegetables.
Quick check
?At 5.4 mm a day, about how many days would evapotranspiration take to equal Wad Medani's 354 mm of annual rainfall?
Mission 3

Benefits

  • Socio-economic: a livelihood for about 100,000 tenant households.
  • Food: in 1996/97 Gezira produced roughly 50% of Sudan's wheat.
  • Cash crop: cotton, the scheme's long-standing main crop, earns income.
  • Reliability: river water allows crops in a dry season and in drought years, when rain-fed farms fail.
  • Environmental: on irrigated land, plant cover and productivity are far higher than the semi-arid rain would support.
Mission 3

Problem: silt and weeds

  • Blue Nile water carries sediment. In slow-flowing canals, silt settles out and chokes them.
  • Researchers have modelled how canal operation affects sedimentation in Gezira's canals.
  • An "inappropriate program of silt removal" damaged the canals' hydraulics: water no longer flowed as designed.
  • Aquatic weeds grow in canals and slow the flow further; snails and weeds in Gezira's canals have long been studied.
  • Result: water reaches some fields late or not at all.
Mission 3

Problem: water-related disease

  • Schistosomiasis: larvae "released by freshwater snails" enter the skin in infested water.
  • WHO lists farmers and irrigation workers among people whose work brings contact with such water.
  • Irrigation "creates new habitats for vectors that have aquatic life-cycle stages": snails and malaria mosquitoes.
  • Malaria control has been studied in "the Gezira and Sennar irrigation schemes".
  • A social and economic cost: sick workers, lost labour, health spending.
Quick check
?Why can an irrigation scheme raise schistosomiasis in a dry region?
Mission 3

Problem: managing water and money

  • 1999–2005: "the system experienced the most serious deterioration throughout its long history".
  • The cultivated area fell significantly while water supply kept rising, often well above crop requirements.
  • Responsibilities clashed between the Sudan Gezira Board and the irrigation ministry, and budgets were insufficient.
  • The 2005 Gezira Scheme Act gave minor canals to Water User Associations (WUAs); by late 2014 the Act was changed and the WUAs dissolved.
  • Since mid-April 2023, armed conflict "has significantly impacted agricultural cultivation" across Sudan.
Mission 3

Problem: salt and waterlogging

  • Too much water on poorly drained land raises the water table: roots waterlog.
  • In hot, dry air, water evaporates at the surface and leaves its salts behind: salinisation.
  • Worldwide, FAO reported 45 million ha of 230 million ha of irrigated land (19.5%) as salt-affected.
  • Farmers may be "forced to cultivate salt-tolerant crops which might not always be high income cash crops".
  • In Gezira, water supply often exceeded requirements, which raises this risk where drainage is poor.
Mission 3

Possible solutions

  • Sediment: regular, well-planned desilting; operate canals to keep silt moving.
  • Water use: deliver water to match crop needs; drip irrigation wets only the root zone, cutting losses and standing water; drainage lowers the water table and flushes salts.
  • Health: praziquantel treatment, safe water, sanitation, hygiene education and snail control; mollusciciding with mass drug treatment; removing mosquito breeding sites such as leaking canals.
  • People and institutions: farmers' participation "requires efficient training to raise their awareness and skills"; pilot new set-ups before full adoption.
  • Exam move ("Evaluate"): weigh cost, scale and who must act. Drip is efficient but costly for 100,000 smallholders; desilting must be repeated as new silt keeps arriving.
Quick check
?Which measure tackles schistosomiasis transmission in the canals, rather than only treating people already infected?
Mission check

?Planners design a new canal scheme fed by a silt-laden river in a semi-arid tropical region. Which plan best avoids Gezira's problems?

Irrigation brings water, food and income to the arid tropics, but it also brings silt, weeds, snails and salt, and it needs strong institutions. Solutions work best in combination: engineering, health and people.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Clearing tropical forest and savanna brings farms, jobs and food. Who gains, and what is lost?

Make a guess: Clearing tropical forest and savanna brings farms, jobs and food. Who gains, and what is lost?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • A2-1.B3.i evaluate the threat of large-scale development to tropical forest trophic structure, nutrient cycle and zonal soil (regional case study: Amazon Basin)
Mission 4

Why tropical land is cleared

  • Across the tropics, 90–99% of deforestation in 2011–2015 was driven by agriculture.
  • Amazon: cattle pasture first; soy often follows. "Most soybean expansion occurred on pastures originally converted from natural vegetation for cattle production".
  • Brazil's satellite survey (PRODES): 3,335 km² cleared in the Legal Amazon, Aug 2025–Jul 2026 (preliminary estimate), the lowest since 1988; the previous low was 4,571 km² (2012).
  • Pará cleared the most: 1,130 km².
Mission 4

What the Amazon loses

  • Biodiversity: about 16,000 tree species.
  • Rain: air that has passed over extensive forest produces "at least twice as much rain" as air that has passed over little vegetation.
  • Carbon: "Southeastern Amazonia, in particular, acts as a net carbon source".
  • Medicines: quinine from the Andean Cinchona tree was for centuries "the primary treatment of malaria".
Mission 4

Nutrient cycle and soil after clearance

  • The zonal soil is the latosol (oxisol): "very highly weathered", with "extremely low native fertility".
  • "Most nutrients … are contained in the standing vegetation and decomposing plant material": the Gersmehl biomass store.
  • Clear and burn: the biomass store is removed; ash gives a brief flush of nutrients.
  • Heavy rain then leaches them; bare soil erodes; no litter returns.
  • Oxisols "can be quite productive with inputs of lime and fertilizers": without them, yields fall and new land is cleared.
Mission 4

Trophic structure

  • Forest: many producers (thousands of tree species), many herbivores, several carnivore levels up to top predators.
  • Pasture or soy: one or two producer species; few consumers (cattle, people).
  • Fragmented forest is too small for wide-ranging top predators, so food webs lose their top levels.
  • Fewer animals disperse seeds and pollinate, so regrowth is slower and poorer.
Quick check
?Why do crop yields on newly cleared Amazon land fall unless fertiliser and lime are added?
Mission 4

Evaluating the threat at regional scale

  • Scale: large-scale ranching, soy, dams and mining clear thousands of km² a year; small clearings can regrow, large ones break the regional rainfall and nutrient systems.
  • Reversibility: food webs and soils recover slowly; the biomass nutrient store takes decades to rebuild.
  • Trend: Amazon clearing in 2026 was the lowest since 1988: governance can reduce the threat.
  • Exam move (CCEA "Evaluate"): judge each part (trophic structure, nutrient cycle, zonal soil) separately, say which is most threatened and why, and use regional data.
Mission check

?In a tropical lowland, a company plans to clear 10,000 ha of rainforest on oxisols for plantations, creating 3,000 jobs. Which evaluation is best supported?

Clearing brings income, jobs and food to some, mostly locally and soon. The losses (nutrients locked in the biomass, complex food webs, rainfall recycling, carbon storage and biodiversity) are wider, longer-lasting and shared.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Disease wiped out pepper farms in the Amazon in the 1960s. Could farmers rebuild by copying the forest?

Make a guess: Disease wiped out pepper farms in the Amazon in the 1960s. Could farmers rebuild by copying the forest?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • A2-1.B3.ii evaluate attempts at sustainable development in the tropical forest (small-scale case study: agroforestry in Tomé-Açu)
Mission 5

What counts as sustainable development?

  • Meets people's needs now without destroying the resource for the future.
  • Environmental: keeps tree cover, soil, nutrients, water and biodiversity.
  • Economic: stable incomes that survive price falls, pests and bad years.
  • Social: better quality of life, fair shares, skills that last.
  • Small-scale: a community or district, judged on what it can and cannot do.
Mission 5

Tomé-Açu: the setting

  • A municipality in Pará, eastern Amazon.
  • Pará cleared more Amazon forest than any other state in Aug 2025–Jul 2026: 1,130 km².
  • 1929: Japanese immigrants founded the cooperative (CAMTA) and grew cacao, vegetables and rice.
  • Early crops failed; "extreme poverty" and tropical diseases drove many families away.
Mission 5

Boom and bust: pepper monoculture

  • Black pepper, introduced in 1930, prospered after the Second World War: the "Black Diamond".
  • Brazil became the world's largest exporter of pepper at that time.
  • From the late 1960s, the monocultures were "decimated" by a disease, fusariosis.
  • The lesson the settlers drew: diversify production.
Quick check
?Why did a single disease destroy so much of Tomé-Açu's pepper?
Mission 5

SAFTA: farming like a forest

  • From the 1970s: the Tomé-Açu Agroforestry System (SAFTA) mixed fruit and forest trees on the declining pepper land.
  • Inspired by riverside peoples' home gardens, which "imitate the forest" with mixed fruit and forest trees.
  • More than 200 combinations tested, including banana, passion fruit, acerola, açaí, cupuaçu, rubber, Brazil nut, andiroba and mahogany.
  • The best were repeated with set spacings: a "mosaic" of agroforestry plots.
Mission 5

Why it suits the rainforest

  • Continuous cover: several layers shade and protect the soil from heavy rain.
  • Nutrients: in latosols, "most nutrients … are contained in the standing vegetation and decomposing plant material". Agroforestry keeps that biomass store and a litter layer: the stores that hold most nutrients in Gersmehl's model.
  • Diversity: many species spread the risk of pests, diseases and price falls.
  • Climate: the IPCC lists agroforestry among effective adaptation options and among land-based mitigation options.
Quick check
?Which SAFTA feature does most to protect soil fertility on latosols?
Mission 5

Economic sustainability

  • 1987: a fruit-pulp factory, built with finance from the Japanese government.
  • 172 cooperative members, plus more than 1,800 registered family farmers who supply produce.
  • About 10,000 direct and indirect jobs, by CAMTA's own figure.
  • Sells pepper, cocoa beans, plant oils (from cupuaçu, passion fruit and andiroba seeds) and 15 fruit pulps, in Brazil and abroad.
  • Cocoa is "the economic base of the region".
Mission 5

Social benefits and spread

  • CAMTA says SAFTA has improved quality of life and kept local food culture.
  • Workers labour "under the shade" of trees rather than in open fields.
  • SAFTA methods have been spread to other parts of Brazil, to Bolivia and to Ghana.
  • CAMTA encourages members to adopt SAFTA to help reduce clearance of primary forest.
Mission 5

Limits

  • Evidence: most figures come from CAMTA itself; independent measures of incomes, yields and forest cover are needed.
  • Scale: a few thousand farmers in one municipality, set against 1,130 km² cleared in Pará in one year.
  • Inputs: it needed decades of trial (200+ models), a strong cooperative and outside finance (Japan).
  • Markets: incomes still depend on prices for cocoa, pepper and fruit.
  • Land: it restored already-cleared pepper land; it does not stop clearing on the frontier elsewhere.
Quick check
?Which is the strongest limitation when judging Tomé-Açu a success?

Mission check

?A rainforest village replaces single-crop cassava on degraded land with mixed fruit and timber trees. An NGO pays for seedlings for three years, and all fruit is sold to one buyer. Which evaluation is best supported?

After fusariosis destroyed the pepper monocultures, Tomé-Açu's farmers rebuilt by copying the forest: layered mixtures of fruit and forest trees that protect soil and spread risk, backed by a cooperative and a processing plant. It is a strong small-scale example, but much of the evidence is self-reported, it relied on outside finance, and it is tiny next to regional clearance.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Rising air at the ITCZ (Hadley cell) gives rain; sinking air near 30° gives dry high pressure. The ITCZ follows the overhead Sun, one to two months behind.
  • Rainforest: always under the ITCZ (Singapore: 2,113 mm, no dry season) → high biomass. Savanna: ITCZ part of the year (Darwin: 79% of rain in four months) → grass and scattered trees. Desert: sinking air (Alice Springs: 283 mm) → sparse, drought-adapted plants.

Mission 2

  • Irrigation turns aridity into productivity, but without drainage it causes waterlogging and salinisation (Indus: 35% waterlogged command area; salt threatens up to 43% of irrigated land).
  • Solutions treat causes (drainage, less water, conjunctive management) or symptoms (salt-tolerant crops).

Mission 3

  • Gezira (Sudan): 880,000 ha between the Blue and White Niles, fed from Sennar Dam; ~100,000 tenant households; cotton, wheat, sorghum, groundnuts.
  • Benefits: livelihoods, food (≈50% of Sudan's wheat in 1996/97), cash crops. Problems: silted, weedy canals; schistosomiasis and malaria; oversupply and decline; salt and waterlogging risk; conflict since 2023. Solutions: desilting, matched supply, drip, drainage, health measures, trained water users.

Mission 4

  • Impacts: agriculture drives 90–99% of tropical deforestation; clearance removes the biomass nutrient store, so latosols are leached and food webs simplified.

Mission 5

  • Tomé-Açu (Pará): 1929 Japanese settlement → pepper boom → fusariosis in the late 1960s → SAFTA from the 1970s: mixed fruit and forest trees (cocoa, açaí, cupuaçu, Brazil nut, mahogany…) on degraded pepper land; fruit-pulp factory 1987.
  • Evaluation: environmentally suited to latosols (biomass and litter kept, diversity); economically diversified; socially beneficial by CAMTA's account; limited by scale, outside finance, market prices and self-reported evidence.
