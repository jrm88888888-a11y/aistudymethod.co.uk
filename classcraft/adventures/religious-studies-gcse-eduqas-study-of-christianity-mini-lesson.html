Mission 1

Mission 1: Christians call God all-powerful, all-loving and a fair judge. How can one God be all three?

Make a guess: Christians call God all-powerful, all-loving and a fair judge. How can one God be all three?

Mission 1

One God

  • Christianity is monotheistic: there is only one God.
  • It inherits this from Judaism: "Hear, Israel: The LORD is our God. The LORD is one." (Deuteronomy 6:4)
  • Christians believe God is greater than any human idea of God.
  • So they use several words, each naming one attribute (quality) of the one God.
  • (How one God is Father, Son and Holy Spirit: a later mission.)
Mission 1

Power and knowledge

  • Omnipotent: all-powerful. Nothing that can be done is beyond God.
  • The creeds call God Almighty.
  • Exodus shows God's power over nature and rulers: the plagues on Egypt (Exodus 7–11) and the parting of the sea (Exodus 14:21).
  • Omniscient: all-knowing. God knows every thought, past, present and future.
  • Significance: Christians trust God in prayer, because God both knows the need and can act.
Mission 1

Loving, just, forgiving

  • Benevolent (omni-benevolent): all-loving; God wills only good for creation.
  • Just: God is fair. Wrongdoing matters to God, and God judges rightly.
  • Christians hold these together: God's justice means wrong is real; God's love offers a way back.

Mission 1Read the source

God so loved the world

"For God so loved the world, that he gave his only born Son, that whoever believes in him should not perish, but have eternal life." (John 3:16)

  • "But you, Lord, are a merciful and gracious God, slow to anger, and abundant in loving kindness and truth." (Psalm 86:15)
  • Paul: nothing at all, not death, not any power, can separate believers from the love of God in Christ Jesus (Romans 8:37–39, paraphrase).

Q1 Which quality of God does John 3:16 show most clearly?

Show answer

Love: God gives what is most precious.

Q2 What does God's love lead to, for those who believe?

Show answer

Eternal life.

Mission 1Diversity

Same God, different emphases

  • All mainstream Christians affirm every attribute. Traditions differ in which they stress.
  • Orthodox: God's transcendence (God beyond and outside the world) and mystery; worship is full of awe; some theology speaks of God mainly by saying what God is not.
  • Catholic: all attributes held together; God can be known through creation and reason as well as through revelation.
  • Reformed (Calvinist) Protestants: God's sovereignty: God's power and control over all things.
  • Evangelical and Pentecostal: God as personal (someone who can be known and spoken to) and immanent (present and active within the world): a living relationship with Jesus; the Holy Spirit at work today.
  • Quakers: God's immanence (God within the world and people): "that of God in every one".
Mission 1Evaluate

"Love is the most important quality of God."

  • Reasons for: "God is love" (1 John 4:8). John 3:16: God's love is the reason for the Incarnation and the cross. Jesus names love of God and neighbour as the greatest commandments (Mark 12:29–31).
  • Reasons against: love without power could not create or save (omnipotence). Love that ignores wrong would fail victims, so justice matters too (Matthew 25). Worship in many traditions, especially Orthodox and Catholic, stresses God's holiness and transcendence (God beyond the world): "Holy, holy, holy" (Isaiah 6:3).
  • Another view: many theologians say God's qualities cannot be ranked: each describes the whole of God.
  • Judge it by: Do the qualities depend on each other? Which most shapes how Christians live? Can God's qualities be ranked at all?
Mission 1Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain two Christian beliefs about the nature of God. Refer to a source of Christian belief.

  • Belief 1: God is omnipotent: the creeds call God "Almighty". Development: nothing that can be done is beyond God, so Christians trust that God can act when they pray.
  • Belief 2: God is loving. Source: "God so loved the world, that he gave his only born Son" (John 3:16). Development: God's love is shown in sending Jesus, so Christians try to show the same self-giving love (agape) to others.
Mission check

?A head teacher catches a pupil cheating. She records it and gives a fair consequence, then tells him she will help him make a fresh start. Most Christians (Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant) would say this pictures God in which way?

One God, many attributes: power, love and justice are not rivals but different sides of the same God.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why do earthquakes and wars make some people doubt that an all-powerful, loving God exists?

Make a guess: Why do earthquakes and wars make some people doubt that an all-powerful, loving God exists?

Mission 2

Two kinds of evil

  • Evil: what is morally wrong or causes harm. Suffering: the pain or distress that results.
  • Moral evil: caused by human choices: murder, war, abuse, bullying.
  • Natural evil: caused by nature, not human choice: earthquakes, floods, disease.
  • Some suffering mixes both: a famine made worse by war; a flood made worse by poor building.
Quick check
?A dam bursts after its builders cut corners to save money, and a village is flooded. Which kind of evil is this?
Mission 2

The inconsistent triad

  • Christians hold three beliefs:
  • 1. God is omnipotent: able to stop evil.
  • 2. God is benevolent: wants to stop evil.
  • 3. Evil and suffering exist.
  • Together they seem to clash: if 1 and 2 are true, why is 3 true? This is the problem of evil.
Mission 2Read the source

Epicurus' questions

Epicurus (341–270 BCE), a Greek philosopher, is credited with these questions. David Hume (1711–1776) gives them in Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion (1779):

"Is he willing to prevent evil, but not able? then is he impotent. Is he able, but not willing? then is he malevolent. Is he both able and willing? whence then is evil?"

  • impotent = powerless · malevolent = wishing harm · whence = from where

Show answer

The first.

Q2 Which challenges God's benevolence?

Show answer

The second.

Q3 What does the third question ask?

Show answer

If God is both, where does evil come from?

Mission 2

When suffering shakes faith

  • Believers ask: Why me? Why do the innocent suffer? Why doesn't God act?
  • The Bible itself asks: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Psalm 22:1). Jesus prays these words on the cross (Mark 15:34).
  • Some believers doubt or lose faith; some become agnostic or atheist.
  • Many people see the problem of evil as the strongest argument against God's existence.
  • Other believers say their faith grew: they found God with them in suffering, and point to Jesus, who suffered too.
Quick check
?A flood kills thousands of people. Why might this lead a Christian to question God?
Mission 2Evaluate

"Evil and suffering prove that God does not exist."

  • Reasons for: the inconsistent triad; Epicurus' questions seem unanswered. Natural evil is not caused by human choice. There is so much suffering of the innocent, including children.
  • Reasons against: "prove" is a strong word: God may have reasons humans cannot see, as the book of Job suggests (Job, a blameless man, suffers without being told why). Free will may need the possibility of evil (a later mission). Believers experience God's compassion even in suffering (psalms such as Psalm 103 praise God's compassion). Christians believe God shares suffering in Jesus.
  • Judge it by: Is it a contradiction (a proof) or a challenge (evidence against)? Could God have reasons we cannot know? Do natural and moral evil need different answers?
Mission 2Worked answer

Why the problem troubles believers

Question: Explain two reasons why evil and suffering may cause some Christians to question their faith.

  • Reason 1: Natural suffering, such as an earthquake, is not caused by human choice. Development: as Creator of nature, God seems responsible, which is hard to square with a benevolent God.
  • Reason 2: Christians believe God is omnipotent and loving: able to stop suffering and wanting to. Development: when prayers for a suffering loved one seem unanswered, God's power and love can feel absent.
Mission check

?A newborn baby has a serious, painful illness. Why do many people find this kind of suffering especially hard to square with belief in an omnipotent, benevolent God?

Power, love and suffering together form the problem; a later mission looks at how Christians respond to it.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Christians still trust God when disaster strikes. What answers do they give, and do any of them work?

Make a guess: Christians still trust God when disaster strikes. What answers do they give, and do any of them work?

Mission 3

Three kinds of answer

  • Christians do not usually deny the problem. They respond in three ways:
  • Theoretical: reasons why a good God might allow evil (free will, soul-making).
  • Biblical: what scripture says about suffering (Job, a good man who suffers terribly without being told why; the Psalms; the cross).
  • Practical: what to do about suffering (prayer, charity, care for the sufferer).
  • A reasoned defence of God's goodness in the face of evil is a theodicy.
Mission 3

Free will (Augustine)

  • St Augustine of Hippo (354–430), Confessions (c. 400) and City of God (413–426).
  • God made everything good (Genesis 1:31). Evil is not a thing God made: it is a lack or loss of good (a privation).
  • God gave angels and humans free will. They turned from God: the Fall (Genesis 3).
  • Moral evil comes from misused free will. Augustine links natural evil to a creation disordered by the Fall.
  • Why allow it? Love and goodness must be freely chosen; a world of robots could not love.
Mission 3

The vale of soul-making (Irenaeus, Hick)

  • St Irenaeus (c. 130–c. 202): humans are made in God's image but must grow into his likeness.
  • John Hick (1922–2012), Evil and the God of Love (1966), develops this idea.
  • Hick borrows the poet John Keats' phrase (1819): the world is a "vale of soul-making".
  • A world with real dangers and pain lets people develop courage, compassion, patience and trust.
  • In a world with no risk, nothing would be at stake: no one could be brave or kind.
  • Hick believed all people will finally reach that goal (heaven), so suffering is not wasted.
Quick check
?After a flood, neighbours who had never spoken risk their lives to rescue strangers. Which response to the problem of evil uses events like this as evidence?
Mission 3Read the source

Job's answer

  • Job is a good man who loses his wealth, his children and his health (Job 1–2).
  • Job's friends insist he must have sinned. Job, who is innocent, demands an answer from God.
  • God answers out of a whirlwind: "Where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth? Declare, if you have understanding." (Job 38:4)
  • Job replies: "I know that you can do all things, and that no purpose of yours can be restrained." (Job 42:2)
  • "I had heard of you by the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees you. Therefore I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes." (Job 42:5–6)
  • God says the friends were wrong about him (42:7, paraphrase) and restores Job (42:10).

Show answer

No: God shows how far his wisdom exceeds Job's.

Q2 What changes for Job?

Show answer

Mission 3

Practical responses: prayer and charity

  • Prayer: Christians bring suffering to God, for others and for themselves. It brings comfort, strength and a sense of God's presence.
  • Charity (from Latin caritas, love): Christians act to reduce suffering: Jesus healed the sick and taught care for the hungry and sick (Matthew 25:31–46).
  • Examples: Christian Aid and CAFOD (disaster relief); hospices; foodbanks run by churches.
  • Many Christians say the practical answer matters most: evil is something to fight, not just explain.
Mission 3

The God who suffers with us

  • Christians believe that in the Incarnation God became human in Jesus, and that Jesus suffered and died on the cross.
  • So God does not watch suffering from far away: God has entered it.
  • Christians also hope that suffering is not the end: the resurrection points to a future without it (Revelation 21:4).
Mission 3Diversity

Different emphases

  • Catholic: suffering joined to Christ's can be redemptive; many Catholics "offer up" pain in prayer.
  • Orthodox: the Fall disordered all creation; Christ's resurrection begins its healing.
  • Reformed Protestants: God's providence: "all things work together for good for those who love God" (Romans 8:28).
  • Liberal Protestants: often stress practical action and God's suffering with people over a full explanation.
  • Some Christians see suffering as punishment or a test of faith (see Mission 2); many reject the punishment idea (John 9:1–3).
Quick check
?How does belief in the Incarnation shape many Christians' response to suffering?
Mission 3Evaluate

"Christian responses solve the problem of evil."

  • Reasons for: free will explains moral evil and makes real love possible. Soul-making gives natural evil a purpose. Job shows that limited humans may not see God's reasons. Prayer and charity actually reduce suffering.
  • Reasons against: natural evil (disease, earthquakes) is hard to blame on human choice. Could God not make free beings who always choose good? (J. L. Mackie, "Evil and Omnipotence", 1955.) The amount of suffering, of children especially, seems more than soul-making needs. Non-religious thinkers, including humanists, say no theodicy works.
  • Another view: many Christians say the problem is not "solved" but lived with: trust, as Job learned, and action, as Jesus showed.
  • Judge it by: Does it explain natural as well as moral evil? Does it fit the amount of suffering? Is a practical answer enough without a theoretical one?
Mission 3Worked answer

Two solutions, one source

Question: Explain two ways Christians respond to the problem of evil and suffering. Refer to a source of wisdom and authority.

  • Way 1: Free will. Development: God gave humans the freedom to choose, so love can be real; moral evil comes from misusing that freedom, not from God.
  • Way 2: Trust, as Job did. Source: "I know that you can do all things" (Job 42:2). Development: Christians accept that God's purposes are greater than they can see, so they keep faith without a full answer.
Mission check

?A volcano erupts millions of years before the first humans exist. A Christian wants to explain why God would allow natural destruction like this. Which explanation is hardest to use here?

Different evils fit different answers: free will suits moral evil; soul-making and Job's trust reach further; prayer and charity act on what remains.

Mission 4

Mission 4: How can Christians believe in only one God, yet pray to the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit?

Make a guess: How can Christians believe in only one God, yet pray to the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit?

Mission 4

One God, not three

  • Christians are monotheists, like Jews and Muslims.
  • They hold to the Jewish Shema, which Jesus himself quoted (Mark 12:29): "Hear, Israel: The LORD is our God. The LORD is one." (Deuteronomy 6:4)
  • So the Trinity (Latin trinitas, "threeness": God as three-in-one) never means three gods.
  • The word "Trinity" is not in the Bible. It names a belief Christians find in the Bible.
  • This is the oneness of God: one being, one will, one God.
Mission 4

Three Persons, one God

  • Most Christians believe in one God in three Persons: Father, Son and Holy Spirit.
  • Each Person is fully God, not a third of God.
  • The three are distinct (not the same as each other) but not separate (not three beings).
  • All three are equal and eternal. None was made.
  • Not three gods. Not one God wearing three masks in turn.
Mission 4

Each Person

  • Trinitarian Christians describe each Person like this:
  • Father: Creator and source of all; loves and cares for people.
  • Son: Jesus Christ, God become human (the Incarnation); saves humanity.
  • Holy Spirit: God present and active in the world now; guides, strengthens and gives life.
  • Jesus promises the Father will send "another Counsellor… the Spirit of truth" (John 14:16–17).
Mission 4Read the source

The baptism of Jesus

"Jesus, when he was baptised, went up directly from the water: and behold, the heavens were opened to him. He saw the Spirit of God descending as a dove, and coming on him. Behold, a voice out of the heavens said, 'This is my beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased.'" (Matthew 3:16–17)

  • Q1: Find each Person: who is baptised, what descends, who speaks?

Q1 Why do Christians see this passage as evidence for the Trinity?

Show answer

Mission 4

More roots in scripture

  • Jesus sends his followers to baptise "in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 28:19): one name, three Persons.
  • "I and the Father are one." (John 10:30)
  • "He who has seen me has seen the Father." (John 14:9). Jesus is "the way, the truth, and the life" to the Father (John 14:6).
  • So the doctrine grew from texts like these, then was defined by the Church.
Mission 4

The Nicene Creed

  • The Nicene Creed is built in three parts: Father, Son, Holy Spirit.
  • The Apostles' Creed has the same three-part shape (a later mission).
Mission 4

The Trinity in worship and life

  • Baptism is "in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit".
  • Sign of the cross (Catholic, Orthodox, many Anglicans and Lutherans) traces the same words.
  • Prayer is often to the Father, through the Son, in the Spirit. Many hymns and blessings end with all three ("Glory be to the Father…").
  • Daily life: God is a communion of love, so Christians aim to live in loving community; they rely on the Spirit's help.
Mission 4Evaluate

"The Trinity is a contradiction."

  • Reasons for: one cannot equal three. The word "Trinity" is not in the Bible. Deuteronomy 6:4 stresses that God is one. Unitarians and Christadelphians reject it. Every everyday analogy seems to fail.
  • Reasons against: God is one in being and three in Persons: "one" and "three" count different things, so there is no logical contradiction. Scripture treats Father, Son and Spirit as God (Matthew 28:19; John 10:30; Galatians 4:6). Christians call it a mystery: beyond full understanding, but not against reason.
  • Experience: Christians say they meet God as above them (Father), with them (Son) and within them (Spirit).
  • Judge it by: Do "one" and "three" count the same thing? Must a belief be fully understood to be reasonable? Should belief rest on the Bible alone or also on the Church's councils?
Mission check

?A student describes the Trinity as "like one actor playing three roles in a play, one after another". How would most Christians (Catholic, Orthodox and most Protestants) respond?

One being, three Persons, all at once: that is what keeps the Trinity from being three gods or one God in disguise.

Mission 5

Mission 5: If Genesis says the world was made in six days, how can many Christians also accept the Big Bang?

Make a guess: If Genesis says the world was made in six days, how can many Christians also accept the Big Bang?

Mission 5

God the Creator

  • Christians believe God made everything that exists: the whole universe.
  • God made it out of nothing (Latin ex nihilo): no material existed before.
  • God made it freely, by speaking: "God said… and there was" (Genesis 1).
  • God saw it was "very good" (Genesis 1:31): creation is good.

Mission 5

Father, Word and Spirit at work

  • Father: the source; the one who creates (Genesis 1:1).
  • Word (the Son): the one through whom all things were made (John 1:3), heard in "God said" (Genesis 1:3).
  • Spirit: God's life-giving power, "hovering" over the waters (Genesis 1:2).
Quick check

Mission 5

Two accounts, side by side

  • First account (Genesis 1:1–2:3): six days, then God rests on the seventh. God speaks; humans, male and female together, come last, in God's image. God is transcendent.
  • Second account (from Genesis 2:4): no days. "The LORD God" forms the man from dust and breathes life into him, plants a garden, brings the animals, then makes the woman. God is close and personal.
  • Different order, different style, different picture of God.
  • Most Christians read them as two complementary accounts, each teaching something true about God and humans.
Quick check
?Which feature belongs to the second creation account (from Genesis 2:4) but not to the first?
Mission 5Diversity

Literal or metaphorical?

  • Literal: some Christians, often evangelical or fundamentalist, read Genesis 1 as six 24-hour days; the earth is thousands of years old (young-earth creationism).
  • Day-age: others read each "day" as a long age; the order still matters.
  • Metaphorical (non-literal): Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican and many Protestant Christians read Genesis as teaching truths about God and humans, not a scientific timetable.
  • Literary type: Genesis 1 reads like a hymn: repeated refrains, days 1–3 forming spaces, days 4–6 filling them.
Quick check
?A Christian says: "The six days of Genesis 1 are not 24-hour days. They are a poetic picture showing that God made everything in good order." Which approach is this?
Mission 5Evaluate

"Christian beliefs about creation conflict with science."

  • Reasons for: a literal reading gives six days and a young earth; science gives an age of billions of years. In Genesis 1, plants appear before the sun. Some creationists reject evolution.
  • Reasons against: most Christians read Genesis metaphorically: science asks how, Genesis asks who and why. The Big Bang theory was first proposed by Georges Lemaître (1894–1966), a Catholic priest and physicist. A universe with a beginning fits belief in creation out of nothing.
  • Judge it by: Does it depend on how Genesis is read? Can science answer "why is there anything at all?" Do literal readers speak for all Christians?
Mission 5Worked answer

Word and Spirit in creation

Question: Explain two Christian beliefs about the role of the Word and the Spirit in creation. Refer to a source of wisdom and authority.

  • Belief 1: The Word, the Son, was the agent of creation. Source: "All things were made through him." (John 1:3) Development: so Christians believe Jesus existed before creation, and creation bears his mark.
  • Belief 2: The Spirit gave life and order. Source: "God's Spirit was hovering over the surface of the waters" (Genesis 1:2). Development: so Christians believe the same Spirit still gives life and guides believers today.
Mission check

?A Christian astronomer studies light from galaxies billions of years old. She says, "God created all of this." Which belief, held by many Christians, lets her hold both views?

Read as a picture of who made the world and why, the six days need not clash with a universe billions of years old.

Mission 6

Made in the image of God

"God created man in his own image. In God's image he created him; male and female he created them." (Genesis 1:27)

  • Latin: imago Dei, "image of God".
  • Not a physical likeness: Christians believe God has no body.
  • It means humans share, in a small way, some of God's qualities: reason, free will, conscience, creativity, and the ability to love and to know God.
  • Male and female are made in God's image equally.
Mission 6Read the source

Rule, breathe, keep

"God said to them, 'Be fruitful, multiply, fill the earth, and subdue it. Have dominion over the fish of the sea, over the birds of the sky, and over every living thing that moves on the earth.'" (Genesis 1:28)

"The LORD God formed man from the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul." (Genesis 2:7)

"The LORD God took the man, and put him into the garden of Eden to cultivate and keep it." (Genesis 2:15)

1:28; 2:15.

Mission 6

Humans in Genesis 2: made for a purpose

  • Dust: humans belong to the earth. (Hebrew adam, "human", sounds like adamah, "ground".)
  • Breath of God: humans have a spiritual side and a special relationship with God.
  • Work: to "cultivate and keep" the garden. Work and care are good, part of God's plan.
  • Naming the animals (Genesis 2:19–20): understanding and responsibility for other creatures.
  • Partnership: God says it is not good for the man to be alone, and makes the woman (Genesis 2:18–23, paraphrase). Humans are made for relationship.
  • Purpose: to know and love God, to love one another, and to care for creation.
  • Some Christians read Adam and Eve as the first two people; many read them as standing for all humanity.

Mission 6

Dominion or stewardship?

  • Dominion: rule or authority over nature (Genesis 1:26, 28).
  • Historian Lynn White (1907–1987) argued in 1967 that reading dominion as a licence to exploit helped cause the ecological crisis.
  • Stewardship: looking after something that belongs to someone else, and answering to them for it.
  • "The earth is the LORD's" (Psalm 24:1): humans are not the owners.
  • Most Christians today read dominion as stewardship: rule as God would, like a good shepherd, not a tyrant.
Quick check
?Which statement best describes the Christian idea of stewardship?
Mission 6Diversity

How Christians see our place in creation

  • Stewardship (Catholic, Anglican, Methodist, many evangelicals): humans care for creation on God's behalf.
  • Human needs first: some conservative Christians stress dominion and human development; environmental policy must not make the poor poorer.
  • Orthodox: humans as "priests of creation", offering the world back to God in thanksgiving. Ecumenical Patriarch Bartholomew is called the "Green Patriarch".
  • Kinship with creation (Franciscan): St Francis of Assisi (c. 1181–1226) praised "Brother Sun" and "Sister Moon".
  • All agree that creation is good and humans are accountable to God for it.
Mission 6Evaluate

"Being made in God's image means humans matter more than the rest of creation."

  • Reasons for: only humans are made in God's image (Genesis 1:27) and given dominion (1:28). Jesus says people are worth more than many sparrows (Matthew 10:31). Many Christians believe human life is sacred in a way other life is not (the sanctity of life).
  • Reasons against: God called each part of creation "good" before humans existed (Genesis 1:10–25). God's covenant after the Flood is with "every living creature" (Genesis 9:10). Stewardship means more responsibility, not more importance.
  • Judge it by: Does "special" mean "more important" or "more responsible"? Can something matter to God without being in God's image? Who speaks for future generations?

Mission 7

What "Incarnation" means

  • Incarnation comes from Latin in carne: "in flesh".
  • Christians believe God the Son, the second Person of the Trinity, became a human being: Jesus of Nazareth.
  • Jesus is fully God and fully human: not half and half, not God in disguise, not a man who became God.
  • One person, two natures (divine and human): agreed at the Council of Chalcedon (451).
  • The Nicene Creed: he "was incarnate by the Holy Ghost of the Virgin Mary, and was made man".

Mission 7

The virgin birth

  • Christians believe Jesus was conceived by the Holy Spirit, without a human father, and born of the Virgin Mary (Apostles' Creed).
  • It shows Jesus is from God (divine) and born of a woman (human).
  • Matthew and Luke both tell it; Christians see it as God's initiative, not human achievement.
  • Catholic and Orthodox Christians honour Mary as Theotokos, "God-bearer" (Council of Ephesus, 431).
  • Most Christians accept the virgin birth as a historical event; some liberal Protestants read it as a symbol of Jesus' unique link with God.

Mission 7Diversity

Why it matters, and how Christians differ

  • For all mainstream Christians: God is not distant; Jesus shows what God is like; God understands human life from the inside.
  • Catholic: Mary as Mother of God; the Angelus prayer recalls the Incarnation three times a day; Christmas Midnight Mass.
  • Orthodox: God became human so humans can share God's life (theosis). Athanasius: "He was made man that we might be made God" (On the Incarnation 54).
  • Protestant: often stress the cross more; the Incarnation makes the saving death possible.
  • Impact: worship at Christmas; respect for human bodies and lives; serving the poor, in whom Jesus is met (Matthew 25:40).
Mission 7Evaluate

"The Incarnation is the most important Christian belief."

  • Reasons for: without it, Jesus' death is the death of a good man, not God's saving act. It shows God's love (John 3:16). Only God become human can reveal God fully (John 1:18).
  • Reasons against: Paul says faith depends on the resurrection (1 Corinthians 15:14). Many Protestants see the cross as the centre. The Trinity is the foundation all else rests on.
  • Another view: the beliefs depend on each other: Incarnation, cross and resurrection form one story.
  • Judge it by: Which belief do the others depend on? What do Christians celebrate most (Christmas or Easter)? Which makes most difference to daily life?
Mission 7Worked answer

Influence on Christians today

Question: Explain two ways belief in the Incarnation influences Christians today.

  • Way 1: Christians celebrate it at Christmas. Development: worship centres on God coming to Earth as a baby, "the Word became flesh" (John 1:14), so many attend Midnight Mass or carol services.
  • Way 2: Christians follow Jesus' example. Development: if God showed himself in a human life, then Jesus' way of loving and serving is how God wants people to live.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Christians believe in one God (monotheism), described by many attributes: omnipotent, loving (benevolent), just.

Mission 2

  • Moral evil comes from human choices; natural evil does not.
  • The inconsistent triad: an omnipotent, benevolent God and the existence of evil seem to clash (Epicurus' questions).
  • Suffering can make believers question their faith or God's existence.
  • Job shows an innocent man whose faith is tested.

Mission 3

  • Christians respond to evil with theoretical answers (free will, soul-making), biblical answers (Job) and practical action (prayer, charity).
  • Many add that in Jesus God shares human suffering.

Mission 4

  • Christians believe in one God in three Persons: Father, Son and Holy Spirit, each fully God, distinct but not separate.
  • At Jesus' baptism (Matthew 3:16–17) all three Persons are present.

Mission 5

  • Christians believe God created everything out of nothing: the Father creates, through the Word (the Son, John 1:1–3), with the Spirit giving life (Genesis 1:1–3).
  • Genesis has two accounts (1:1–2:3 and from 2:4) with different pictures of God; some Christians read them literally, many metaphorically.

Mission 6

  • Humans are made in God's image (imago Dei, Genesis 1:27): this gives every person equal dignity, free will and responsibility.
  • Genesis 1:28 gives dominion; Genesis 2:15 asks humans to "cultivate and keep" the garden. Most Christians read dominion as stewardship.

Mission 7

  • Christians believe in the Incarnation: God the Son became human in Jesus, fully God and fully human (John 1:14).
  • He was conceived by the Holy Spirit and born of the Virgin Mary (Luke 1:35).
