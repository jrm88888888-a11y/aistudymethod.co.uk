Mission 1

What is a market?

  • A market: any place or system where buyers and sellers meet to exchange goods or services.
  • It can be a street market, a shop, a website, an app or a phone call.
  • Buyers want low prices; sellers want high prices. Trade happens at a price both accept.
  • Markets help answer the basic questions: prices tell firms what to make, and decide who gets it (those willing and able to pay).
Mission 1

Two kinds of market

  • Product market: goods and services are bought and sold. Households buy; firms sell. Example: buying trainers.
  • Factor market: factors of production are bought and sold (or hired). Firms buy; households sell. Example: a firm hiring workers (the labour market).
  • Other factor markets: renting land, hiring or buying machines and equipment (capital).
  • The same household is a buyer in product markets and a seller in factor markets.
Quick check
?Which of these is an example of a factor market?
Mission 1

Three sectors of the economy

  • Goods: physical things you can touch (bread, cars). Services: things done for you (a haircut, a bus ride, banking).
  • Primary sector: takes natural resources from the earth or sea: farming, fishing, forestry, mining, oil extraction.
  • Secondary sector: makes or builds goods from raw materials: manufacturing and construction.
  • Tertiary sector: provides services: shops, transport, banking, health care, education, tourism.
  • A chain of production: wheat (primary) → flour and bread made in a bakery (secondary) → sold in a shop and delivered (tertiary).
  • Say: "delivery and transport are tertiary" (a service) — not: secondary, even when they carry goods.

Mission 2

What is specialisation?

  • Specialisation: concentrating on a limited range of tasks or products.
  • It happens at every level:
    • Workers: a dentist, an electrician, a web designer.
    • Firms: a company that only makes bicycle brakes.
    • Regions: a city known for finance, or for films.
    • Countries: one exports coffee, another aircraft parts.
  • Division of labour: splitting the making of one product into separate tasks, each done by a different worker.
  • Division of labour is specialisation inside a firm.
Mission 2

Adam Smith's pin workshop

  • In 1776, Adam Smith described a small pin workshop in The Wealth of Nations.
  • Pin-making was split into about 18 separate operations: one worker draws out the wire, another straightens it, a third cuts it, a fourth sharpens the point…
  • 10 workers made upwards of 48,000 pins a day: about 4,800 each.
  • Working alone and untrained, Smith wrote, each "certainly could not" have made twenty pins a day.
  • Smith's three reasons:
    • Skill: repeating one task makes a worker faster and better at it.
    • Time saved: no switching between tasks and tools.
    • Machines: a small, simple task is easier to do with a machine, so machines get invented.
Mission 2

Worked example: output and cost per unit

A sandwich bar has 4 workers, each paid 12 per hour (illustrative data).

  • Before: each worker makes whole sandwiches: 30 per hour each, 120 per hour in total.
  • After: one slices, one fills, one wraps, one serves: 200 per hour in total.
  • Step 1: output per worker = 200 ÷ 4 = 50 per hour.
  • Step 2: labour productivity (output per worker) rises from 30 to 50: (50 − 30) ÷ 30 × 100 = +66.7%.
  • Step 3: labour cost per sandwich before = 12 ÷ 30 = 0.40.
  • Step 4: labour cost per sandwich after = 12 ÷ 50 = 0.24: down 40%.
  • Same workers, same pay, same hours: more output and a lower cost per unit.
Quick check
?A bakery has 5 workers, each paid 80 per day. When each worker bakes whole loaves, the bakery makes 100 loaves a day. After it divides the work into separate tasks, the same 5 workers make 160 loaves a day (illustrative data). What is the labour cost per loaf after the change?
Mission 2

Who gains?

  • Firms: higher labour productivity, lower cost per unit, more output from the same workers. Lower costs can mean more profit.
  • Workers: quicker to train for one task; can become expert; may earn more as their productivity rises.
  • Consumers: lower prices and more products, often of better quality.
  • The economy: more output from the same scarce resources.
Mission 2

The downsides

  • Boredom: doing the same task all day can lower motivation and quality, and raise absence and staff turnover.
  • Narrow skills: if a machine takes over the task, or demand changes, a worker may struggle to find another job.
  • Interdependence: each stage depends on the others. If one stage stops, the whole line stops.
  • Real case, 2021: a global shortage of computer chips forced car makers around the world to cut production.
  • Less variety: products made in large, standard batches can all look the same.
  • Smith himself warned that a lifetime of "a few simple operations" can dull a worker's mind.
Quick check
?A car factory buys one key electronic part from a single supplier abroad. A fire stops that supplier's production, and within a week the car factory has to stop too. Which disadvantage of specialisation does this show?
Mission 2

Regions and countries specialise too

  • Regions specialise where they have the right resources or skills.
  • Example: Stoke-on-Trent in England grew as "the Potteries", making pottery from local clay and coal.
  • Countries specialise in what they can produce well, then trade.
  • Example: copper is Chile's biggest export.
  • Benefits: more output in total, lower prices, a wider choice of imports.
  • Risks: if world demand for the product falls, the whole region or country suffers. Relying on one product is risky.
Mission 2

Specialise, then exchange

  • A specialist makes far more of one thing than they need, and none of most other things.
  • So specialisation only works with exchange: selling what you make to buy what you need.
  • Barter: swapping goods directly, with no money.
  • Barter needs a double coincidence of wants: each side must want exactly what the other has, at the same time.
  • Money fixes this as a medium of exchange: sell to anyone for money, then buy from anyone.
  • So money makes specialisation and exchange quicker and easier. A later lesson looks at money in depth.
Quick check
?In a village with no money, a farmer has eggs and wants shoes. The shoemaker has shoes but wants a haircut, not eggs. What problem does this show?
Mission check

?A hospital laundry keeps the same 6 workers, the same hours and the same machines. Instead of each worker washing, drying and folding whole batches, each now does one stage only. Sheets cleaned per worker rise by a quarter. Which explanation fits?

Same workers, same hours, more output: the division of labour raises productivity.

Mission 3

Specialisation everywhere

  • Specialisation: concentrating on making a narrow range of goods or services.
  • Producers: a bakery makes bread, not cars.
  • Workers: a nurse, an electrician, a coder: each gets very good at one job.
  • Regions: some areas are known for finance, cars or farming.
  • Countries: each country focuses on what it makes best and trades for the rest.
  • Specialising only works with exchange: you sell what you make and buy what you need.
  • Benefits: more output, more skill, lower costs. Risks: if demand for that one product collapses, jobs and income fall with it.
Mission 3

Why countries trade

  • Export: a good or service sold to another country. Import: one bought from another country.
  • Countries import because:
    • they cannot make it at all: no oil, the wrong climate for coffee or bananas
    • it is cheaper to make abroad: lower costs, better technology, larger scale
    • foreign goods offer choice, variety or better quality
  • Countries export to earn the money that pays for imports, and to reach a much bigger market.
  • Free trade: trade between countries with no barriers such as tariffs (taxes on imports) or quotas (limits on the amount imported).
Mission 3

Worked example: gains from specialising

  • Illustrative data. Each country has 10 workers. One worker can make in a day:

    • A: 50 rice, 10 shirts. B: 20 rice, 40 shirts.
    • Total: 70 rice, 50 shirts.
  • Specialise: A makes only rice, B only shirts.
    • A: 10 × 10 = 100 rice. B: 10 × 8 = 80 shirts.
  • The world gains 30 rice and 30 shirts from the same workers. Trade lets both countries share the gain.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. Each country has 10 workers. One worker in X makes 8 bikes or 2 tables a day; one worker in Y makes 2 bikes or 6 tables. With no trade, each country puts half its workers on each good. If X makes only bikes and Y only tables, how much extra output is produced?
Mission 3

What the UK trades

  • UK, 12 months to August 2026: exports £949 billion, imports £998 billion (goods and services).
  • Services were about 57% of UK exports: business services, financial services, travel, telecoms and computer services.
  • Top goods exports: mechanical power generators (such as engines), medicines and pharmaceuticals, cars, crude oil, aircraft.
  • Top goods imports: cars, mechanical power generators, refined oil, medicines and pharmaceuticals, gold.
  • The UK imports far more goods than it exports, and exports more services than it imports.
  • Interdependence: the EU bought 42% of UK exports in 2023. Problems in one country (a recession, a factory shutdown, a blocked shipping route) spread to its trading partners.
Quick check
?Which statement about UK trade in the 12 months to August 2026 is correct?
Mission 3

Free trade: the case for

  • Lower prices and more choice for consumers.
  • Firms reach bigger markets: more sales, economies of scale, lower costs.
  • Competition from abroad pushes home firms to be more efficient.
  • Countries can specialise: total output rises.
  • Access to raw materials, parts and technology from abroad.
  • Exports create jobs in successful industries.
Mission 3

Free trade: the case against

  • Home firms that cannot compete may close: job losses, often in particular towns and regions.
  • New industries (infant industries) may struggle to grow against established foreign rivals.
  • Dumping: foreign firms may sell below cost to win the market.
  • Over-specialisation: a country depending on one or two exports is hit hard if their prices fall.
  • More transport means more emissions; production may move to countries with weaker environmental or labour rules.
  • The gains are often spread thinly (slightly lower prices for many), while the losses are concentrated (whole factories close).
Quick check
?A country removes all barriers to imports of cheap steel. Which group is most likely to lose?
Mission 3

Weigh it up: does free trade leave everyone better off?

It depends on:

  • Who you are: consumers and exporters usually gain; workers in industries facing imports may lose.
  • The time scale: job losses come fast; gains from lower prices and new industries build up over years.
  • Help for those who lose: retraining and support can share the gains more widely.
  • The country's stage of development: some argue young industries need time and protection to grow; others say protection keeps them inefficient.
  • Most economists agree trade raises total output. Whether it is fair depends on how the gains are shared.
Quick check
?Which is an advantage of specialisation by a country?
Mission check

?A country removes its tariff on imported shoes. Shoes from abroad become cheaper. What is the most likely result?

Free trade usually makes a country better off in total, but not everyone in it.

Mission 4

What demand means

  • Demand: the amount of a good buyers are willing and able to buy at each price, over a period of time.
  • Wanting is not enough: you must be able to pay.
  • Law of demand: as price rises, quantity demanded falls (other things staying the same).

Worked example: draw the curve from the data

  • Step 2: choose even scales: price 0 to 3 in steps of 0.50; quantity 0 to 12 in steps of 2.

  • Demand is the whole curve, not one point on it.

Mission 4

Movement or shift?

  • A change in the good's own price → a movement along the demand curve.
  • Step 1: price falls → slide down the curve = extension of demand. Price rises → slide up = contraction of demand.
  • Step 2: a change in anything else → the whole curve shifts. Right (D1 → D2) = increase in demand. Left = decrease in demand.
  • Increase in demand: more is bought at every price.
  • Say: "the price cut caused an extension of demand" — not: "the price cut increased demand".

Mission 5

Mission 5: Why do some buyers barely react when a price jumps, while others switch away at once?

Make a guess: Why do some buyers barely react when a price jumps, while others switch away at once?

Mission 5

One ratio for "how strongly buyers react"

  • Price elasticity of demand (PED): how much quantity demanded responds to a change in price.
  • PED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price
  • % change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100.
  • Price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions, so PED is negative.
  • Judge the size (ignore the minus sign): bigger than 1 → elastic; smaller than 1 → inelastic.
Mission 5

Worked example: cinema tickets

  • Illustrative data: ticket price rises from 8 to 10. Tickets sold fall from 500 to 450 a week.
  • % change in price = (10 − 8) ÷ 8 × 100 = +25%
  • % change in quantity = (450 − 500) ÷ 500 × 100 = −10%
  • PED = −10 ÷ 25 = −0.4
  • Size 0.4 is less than 1 → demand is inelastic: quantity changes by a smaller % than price.
Quick check
?Illustrative data: a café cuts the price of a smoothie from 5 to 4. Daily sales rise from 200 to 260. What is the PED?

Mission 5

What makes demand elastic or inelastic?

  • Substitutes: many close substitutes → elastic. Buyers can switch.
  • Necessity or luxury: necessities (basic food, medicine) → inelastic.
  • Share of income: a small share of income (salt, matches) → inelastic.
  • Habit or addiction (cigarettes) → inelastic.
  • Time: buyers react more over a long period than straight away.
  • Brand or whole market: one brand of crisps has many close substitutes (other brands), so its demand is very elastic. If one brand puts up its price, its buyers can switch at once. Crisps as a whole have fewer close substitutes, so demand is less elastic.
Mission 5

Real evidence: US petrol

  • Real data (Hughes, Knittel and Sperling, 2008). Economists measured how US drivers reacted to petrol (gasoline) prices.
  • Short-run PED for petrol, 2001–2006: −0.034 to −0.077.
  • In 2001–2006, a 10% price rise cut petrol use by less than 1% in the short run.
  • For 1975–1980 they found −0.21 to −0.34. Inelastic in both periods, but even less elastic later.
  • Why so inelastic? For most journeys there is no close substitute for a car, and petrol is a necessity for many drivers.

Mission 5

PED and total revenue

  • Total revenue (TR) = price × quantity sold. For buyers, the same amount is their total spending.
  • Inelastic demand: price rise → TR rises. Price fall → TR falls.
  • Elastic demand: price rise → TR falls. Price fall → TR rises.
  • Unitary elastic: TR stays the same.
  • Illustrative data: cinema tickets, price 8 → 10.

Mission 5

Why PED matters

  • Producers (firms): with inelastic demand, a firm can raise its price and raise revenue. With elastic demand, a price cut may win more revenue.
  • Consumers: when demand is inelastic (fuel, medicines), price rises are hard to avoid: buyers keep paying.
  • Government – taxes: a tax on a good with inelastic demand (tobacco, fuel) raises a lot of revenue, because sales fall little. It does less to cut use.
  • Government – subsidies: a subsidy that lowers the price raises consumption most when demand is elastic.
Quick check
?A government wants a new tax to raise as much revenue as possible with the smallest fall in sales. On which good should it put the tax?
Mission check

?Illustrative data: a bus company raises its fare from 2.00 to 2.20. Daily passengers fall from 10,000 to 9,700. Which statement is correct?

First turn both changes into percentages from the original values. Then the size of PED tells you which way total revenue moves.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A market brings buyers and sellers together.
  • Product markets sell goods and services; factor markets sell or hire factors of production.
  • Primary: natural resources. Secondary: making and building. Tertiary: services.
  • In rich economies most jobs are in services.

Mission 2

  • Specialisation and the division of labour raise labour productivity (skill, time saved, machines) and cut cost per unit.
  • Downsides: boredom, narrow skills, interdependence, less variety.
  • Specialists must exchange; money as a medium of exchange removes barter's need for a double coincidence of wants.

Mission 3

  • Countries trade to get goods they cannot make, goods that are cheaper abroad, and more choice; exports pay for imports.
  • Specialising in what each country does best raises total output.
  • Free trade brings lower prices, bigger markets and competition, but also job losses in some industries and risks from over-specialisation.
  • The UK exports mainly services and high-value manufactures, and imports more goods than it exports.

Mission 4

  • Demand is the whole schedule or curve: price on the vertical axis, quantity on the horizontal.
  • Own price changes → extension or contraction (movement along). Any other factor → increase or decrease (shift).
  • Market demand = the total of all buyers' demand at each price.

Mission 5

  • PED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price; size above 1 = elastic, below 1 = inelastic.
  • Close substitutes make demand elastic; necessities, habits and small shares of income make it inelastic.
  • Inelastic: a price rise raises total revenue. Elastic: a price rise cuts it.
