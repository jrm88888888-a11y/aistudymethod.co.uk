Mission 1

Mission 1: How can one protein from your cells show you are closer to a dog than to a chicken?

Make a guess: How can one protein from your cells show you are closer to a dog than to a chicken?

Mission 1

Clues to who is related

  • Relatives share features inherited from a common ancestor.
  • Evidence:
    • anatomy: homologous structures, in living species and fossils;
    • DNA and mRNA base sequences;
    • amino acid sequences of proteins.
  • After two lineages split, mutations build up separately in each.
  • So: fewer differences → more recent common ancestor → closer relatives.
  • Say: "share a more recent common ancestor" — not: "one evolved from the other".

Mission 1

Reading a cladogram

  • A cladogram shows the order in which lineages branched.
  • Root: the base; the common ancestor of every group shown.
  • Node: a branch point; a hypothetical common ancestor, where one lineage split in two.
  • Each node is the most recent common ancestor of the lineages beyond it; each split shows a speciation.
  • Terminal branch: ends at one group.
  • Clade: an ancestor and all of its descendants.
  • How related? Find the most recent node they share. Not which tips sit side by side.
  • No living group at a tip is the ancestor of another.
Quick check

Mission 1

Adding time: the molecular clock

  • Sequence differences build up at a roughly steady rate: a molecular clock.
  • Calibrate the rate with fossils of known age; then estimate when two clades diverged.
  • Worked example (illustrative):
    • A protein differs at 12 positions between two species.
    • Rate: about 1 change per 4 million years in each lineage.
    • Both lineages changed: 12 ÷ 2 = 6 changes each.
    • 6 × 4 = about 24 million years since they split.
  • Only an estimate: rates vary with generation time, population size and selection pressure.
  • Phylogenetic tree: branch lengths can show time or amount of change. Cladogram: branching order only.
Quick check
?A student draws a phylogenetic tree and a cladogram for the same four species. Which statement correctly describes a difference between the two kinds of diagram?
Mission 1

Every tree is a hypothesis

  • Trees and cladograms are testable hypotheses. New evidence can revise them.
  • Anatomy can mislead: convergent evolution gives similar traits to unrelated groups.
  • Whales were once classified apart from the even-toed hoofed mammals (pigs, cattle, hippos). DNA evidence showed hippos are their closest living relatives; trees were redrawn with whales inside that group.

Mission 2

How resistance spreads

  • A random mutation, or a gene gained from another bacterium on a plasmid, gives resistance before any drug is used.
  • The antibiotic kills susceptible bacteria. Resistant ones survive, reproduce, pass the allele on.
  • Resistance to each drug usually needs its own gene. Strains can collect several: multiresistant.
  • Resistance to antibiotics, pesticides, herbicides and chemotherapy drugs shows that populations continue to evolve today.
  • Say: "resistant bacteria were selected" — not: "the bacteria got used to the drug".
Quick check
?In a weed population of a hypothetical plant species, an allele gives resistance to herbicide H. Where the herbicide is not used, resistant plants produce fewer seeds than susceptible plants. After years of spraying with H, most plants in a field carry the allele. The farmer then stops using H. Which prediction about the resistance allele over the following generations is best supported?

Mission check

?Tuberculosis is treated with four antibiotics at once for the first two months. Each drug acts on a different target in the bacterium. Why is a combination used rather than a single drug?

The drug never changed. The population of bacteria did, because the drug selected the few that could already survive it.

Mission 3

Three levels of biodiversity

  • Biodiversity: the variety of life, at three levels.
  • Ecosystem (habitat) diversity: the range of habitats or ecosystems, e.g. sand dune, woodland, meadow, stream.
  • Species diversity: the number of species and how evenly the individuals are spread among them.
  • Genetic diversity: the variety of alleles within a species.
  • A community's structure is described by its species composition (which species) and species diversity.
Mission 3

Richness and evenness

Illustrative data: two meadows, same four species, 40 plants each.

SpeciesMeadow AMeadow B
Buttercup1031
Clover103
Daisy103
Plantain103
  • Species richness: the number of species. Both meadows: 4.
  • Species evenness: how evenly individuals are spread among the species. A is even; B is dominated by buttercup.
  • Species diversity needs both. Meadow A is more diverse.
  • Say: "diversity depends on richness and evenness" — not: "diversity is the number of species".
Mission 3

Simpson's Index of Diversity

  • Simpson's Diversity Index: Diversity Index = 1 − Σ(n/N)² (equation sheet).
  • N = total number of organisms of all species; n = number of organisms of each species.
  • Values from 0 to just under 1. Closer to 1 = more diverse; near 0 = one species dominates.
  • Meadow A: 0.75. Meadow B: 0.38.
Mission 3

Worked example

Illustrative data: pitfall traps in a wood. Species P 12, Q 6, R 2.

  • N = 12 + 6 + 2 = 20.
  • n/N: 0.60, 0.30, 0.10.
  • Square each: 0.36, 0.09, 0.01. Sum: Σ(n/N)² = 0.46.
  • D = 1 − 0.46 = 0.54.
  • Square before you add, and do not stop at 0.46.

Mission 3

Sampling for an index

  • You cannot count every organism. Take samples that represent the habitat.
  • Random sampling: random-number coordinates on a grid; quadrats of equal size. Avoids bias.
  • Take many samples. One quadrat misses species and shows nothing about variation.
  • Compare two sites only with the same method and effort.
Quick check
?Ecologists want to compare the species diversity of the plant communities in two meadows by calculating Simpson's Diversity Index for each. Which procedure is most appropriate?
Mission check

?Illustrative data. Two hedgerows were sampled for insects with the same method and effort. Each had the same 6 species and 120 insects in total. In hedgerow X, one species made up 100 of the 120 insects. In hedgerow Y, each species had 20 insects. Which statement is correct?

Counting species is only half the story. How evenly the individuals are shared out decides which habitat is more diverse.

Mission 4

Genetic diversity

  • Genetic diversity: the variety of alleles within a species or population.
  • Every member of a species has the same genes. They differ in which alleles they carry.
  • Small, isolated or captive populations often have low genetic diversity.
  • Say: "different alleles of the same genes" — not: "different genes".
Mission 4

Why genetic diversity matters

  • Environments change: new diseases, new climates, new chemicals.
  • High genetic diversity: some individuals are more likely to carry alleles that let them survive. The population persists.
  • Low genetic diversity: if one is susceptible, nearly all are. The population can crash.
  • Example: California condors fell to 22 birds in 1982. All living condors descend from the 27 taken into captivity by 1987.
  • An allele adaptive in one environment can be harmful in another: selection depends on current conditions.
  • Say: "the population is more likely to contain resistant individuals" — not: "each individual is more resistant".
Quick checkRead the data

Which conclusion is best supported?

  • Yield of the susceptible varieties: 89% greater in mixtures.

Mission 4

Diverse ecosystems are more resilient

  • Ecosystems with few species, and little diversity among them, are often less resilient to change. This holds for farms too.
  • More species in a food web: if one is lost, consumers can switch to others.
  • Producers capture the energy for every trophic level. Lose them and the whole web shrinks.
  • Keystone species have effects far larger than their abundance. Sea otters eat sea urchins; without otters, urchins overgraze kelp forests.
  • Abiotic factors (light, water, nutrients) and biotic factors (food, predators) must also be maintained.
  • Predict effects by tracing every link in the web, not only direct ones.
Quick check
?Soil eroded from nearby farms makes the water of a lake cloudy for several years, so much less light reaches the plants and algae growing in the lake. The number of animal species in the lake then decreases. Which explanation best accounts for the decrease?
Mission check

?Almost all bananas in international trade are one variety, Cavendish. Every plant is grown from a piece of another plant, so all are genetically identical. A fungal wilt disease that kills Cavendish plants is spreading between plantations. Why are biologists concerned for the whole crop?

Numbers do not protect a population; variety does. Genetic diversity is a population's insurance against change.

Mission 5

Extinction, then and now

  • Species have always gone extinct, at a slow background rate.
  • Today the rate is far higher. Vertebrate species lost since 1900 would have taken 800–10,000 years to disappear at the background rate (depending on the group).

Mission 5

What is driving the losses?

  • Habitat loss: deforestation, clearance for farming, urbanization, drainage of wetlands.
  • Over-exploitation: hunting, overfishing, collecting.
  • Pollution: pesticides, fertilizer run-off, plastics.
  • Invasive species, pests and diseases, spread by global transport.
Mission 5

Invasive species

  • An invasive species is introduced by humans (deliberately or by accident) and spreads, harming native species.
  • In its new range it often has no predators, parasites or competitors that held it in check.
  • It can outcompete native species for food, light or space.
Quick check
?Zebra mussels, small filter-feeding mussels native to Eurasia, were unintentionally introduced into North American lakes, where they had few predators. They filter large volumes of water, removing much of the plankton, and attach in dense clusters to hard surfaces, including the shells of native mussels. Predict the most likely effect on populations of native mussels in these lakes.
Mission 5

Small populations spiral down

  • A small population loses alleles by chance (genetic drift) and by inbreeding.
  • Low genetic diversity: fewer individuals can withstand a new disease or change.
  • Lower survival and reproduction make the population smaller still.
  • Each step strengthens the next: the population can spiral toward extinction.
  • The same holds for ecosystems: those with few species, and little diversity among them, are less resilient to change.
Quick check
?The model shows how the size and the genetic diversity of a population can affect each other: Small population → Loss of genetic diversity by drift → Fewer individuals able to withstand new pressures → Lower survival and reproduction → back to Small population. Which statement best describes the relationship shown by the model?
Mission check

?In the early 1800s the passenger pigeon was probably the most numerous bird in North America, with flocks of billions. Through the 1800s it was hunted on a commercial scale, and the eastern forests where it fed and nested were cleared for farming. The last known bird died in a zoo in 1914. Which explanation is best supported?

Extinction is natural; this pace is not. Human activity is removing species far faster than new ones arise.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Differences in DNA, mRNA and protein sequences build up after lineages split, so fewer differences mean a more recent common ancestor.
  • On a cladogram, relatedness is the most recent shared node; every tree is a hypothesis open to new evidence.

Mission 2

  • Resistance evolves by selection: random variants that survive the drug reproduce, so every use of an antibiotic must be justified.

Mission 3

  • Species diversity = richness (number of species) + evenness (how individuals are spread).
  • 1 − Σ(n/N)²: closer to 1 = more diverse.

Mission 4

  • Genetic diversity is the variety of alleles in a population; high diversity makes it more likely that some individuals survive a change.
  • Less diverse populations and ecosystems are less resilient; keystone species and producers maintain diversity.

Mission 5

  • Today's extinction rate is far above the background rate, driven by human activity: habitat loss, over-exploitation, pollution, invasive species and climate change.
  • Small populations with low genetic diversity can spiral toward extinction.
