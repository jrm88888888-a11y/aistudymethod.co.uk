Mission 1: Between 1951 and 2004 the cliffs at Aldbrough, on England's Holderness coast, retreated about 2 m a year. Why do they disappear so fast?

Make a guess: Between 1951 and 2004 the cliffs at Aldbrough, on England's Holderness coast, retreated about 2 m a year. Why do they disappear so fast?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 2.1a wave action, erosion, transport incl. longshore drift, deposition; mechanical/chemical/biological weathering; sliding and slumping
  • 2.1b influence of geology, vegetation, people and sea-level change
Mission 1

Where waves get their energy

  • Waves are made by wind blowing over the sea.
  • Bigger waves come from stronger winds, winds that blow for longer, and a longer fetch (the distance of open water the wind blows over).
  • When a wave breaks, water rushes up the beach (swash) and drains back (backwash).
Mission 1

Constructive and destructive waves

  • Constructive waves: low and gentle. Strong swash, weak backwash, so they add sediment and build beaches.
  • Destructive waves: high and steep, often in storms. Weak swash, strong backwash, so they remove sediment and attack cliffs.
Quick check
?A beach is getting wider and higher over a calm summer. Which waves are most likely to be doing this?
Mission 1

Four ways the sea erodes

  • Hydraulic action: the force of the water, and air forced into cracks, breaks rock apart.
  • Abrasion: waves throw sand and pebbles at the cliff, wearing it away.
  • Attrition: pebbles knock together, becoming smaller and rounder.
  • Solution: sea water dissolves some rocks, such as chalk and limestone.
Mission 1

Transport, longshore drift and deposition

  • Four ways sediment moves: traction (big pebbles roll), saltation (small pebbles and sand bounce), suspension (fine particles carried in the water), solution (dissolved).
  • Longshore drift: waves arrive at an angle, so the swash pushes sediment up the beach at that angle, and the backwash drags it straight down. Sediment zig-zags along the coast.
  • Deposition: sediment is dropped where waves and currents lose energy, such as in sheltered bays.
Quick check
?A beach faces south. The prevailing waves arrive from the south-west. Which way does longshore drift move the sand?
Mission 1

Weathering: breaking rock where it stands

  • Weathering breaks rock down in place; erosion carries it away.
  • Mechanical: freeze-thaw (water in a crack freezes, expands and widens it) and salt weathering (salt crystals grow in tiny gaps and split the rock).
  • Chemical: rainwater is a weak acid. It slowly dissolves chalk and limestone (carbonation).
  • Biological: plant roots widen cracks, and animals burrow into the cliff.
Mission 1

Mass movement: cliffs that fall and slump

  • Mass movement: rock and soil move downhill under gravity.
  • Rock fall: blocks break off a steep cliff, often after waves undercut it or freeze-thaw loosens them.
  • Sliding: material slides down a straight slip surface.
  • Slumping: soft, wet rock such as clay slips along a curved surface, rotating as it goes.
Mission 1

Holderness: soft clay against hard chalk

  • Holderness cliffs are glacial till (boulder clay), mostly 18,000–13,000 years old, and about 20 m high at Aldbrough.
  • At high tide, waves erode their lower and sometimes middle sections.
  • Above the waves, the till fails mainly by rotational slumping, with smaller topples and falls. More rainfall could make the till more likely to fail.
  • To the north, Flamborough Head is chalk, which "is more resistant to erosion than the overlying till".
  • Aldbrough retreated 2.16 m a year in 1951–2004: about 114 m in total (2.16 × 53, calculated).
Mission 1

Where the eroded clay goes

  • The eroded cliffs are a sediment supply: up to 2 million m³ a year was removed from the cliffs, according to data going back to 1786.
  • Longshore drift carries it south. Spurn, at the southern end, formed "as a long, narrow spit from deposits moving along the coast".
  • One stretch's erosion is the next stretch's deposition: link the processes in your answer.
Mission 1

Other influences on coasts

  • Geology: weak till retreats fast; resistant chalk forms headlands.
  • Vegetation: saltmarsh "helps to attenuate the wave action, forming a natural defence against the sea"; marram grass binds dunes.
  • People: groynes trap drifting sediment. On the Sussex coast, groynes at one point "have contributed to erosion" of the spit beyond them.
  • Sea-level change: global sea level rose 0.20 m between 1901 and 2018, and by 3.7 mm a year in 2006–2018, letting waves reach higher up the cliffs.
Quick check
?Why does Holderness retreat so much faster than Flamborough Head, just to the north?
Mission check

?Two cliffs face the same North Sea storms. Cliff X is resistant chalk. Cliff Y is soft glacial clay, soaked after a wet winter. Which retreats faster, and why?

Aldbrough's cliffs are soft till. Waves attack the foot at high tide, the wet clay slumps from above, and longshore drift carries the debris south. That is why they retreated 2.16 m a year from 1951 to 2004, while chalk at Flamborough Head resists erosion.

Mission 2

Headlands and bays

  • Where bands of hard and soft rock meet the coast at right angles, the sea erodes the soft rock faster.
  • Soft rock is worn back into bays; hard rock is left sticking out as headlands.
  • This is a discordant coast: rock bands run across the coastline.
  • Bays are sheltered, so waves there lose energy and drop sand: bay-head beaches.
Mission 2

When the rocks run along the coast

  • On a concordant coast the rock bands run parallel to the coastline.
  • At Lulworth, Dorset, hard Portland Stone lies on the seaward side, with softer rocks behind it.
  • Lulworth Cove formed "after a break in the Portland and Purbeck rocks" let the sea erode the softer rocks behind: a small, round cove.
Mission 2

Cliffs, notches and platforms

  • Waves attack the foot of a cliff between high and low tide, cutting a wave-cut notch by hydraulic action and abrasion.
  • The rock above is left overhanging; it collapses and the cliff retreats.
  • A gently sloping wave-cut platform is left, exposed at low tide.
Mission 2

Cave, arch, stack, stump: Old Harry Rocks

  • At Old Harry Rocks, Dorset, the sea exploits "a crack in the chalk joints", slowly creating a cave.
  • Erosion along joints and bedding planes turns the cave into an arch; when the arch collapses, a stack is left.
  • Old Harry's Wife "was still a stack until her eventual collapse in 1896 to render her a stump": a stump is a stack worn down until it is covered at high tide.
  • Exam move: name the process at each stage (hydraulic action, abrasion) and the weakness it exploits (joints, faults, bedding planes).
Quick check
?What is the correct order as a headland is eroded?
Mission 2

Why the sea drops sediment: beaches

  • The sea deposits sediment where it loses energy: in sheltered bays, in shallow water, behind obstacles, and where constructive waves dominate.
  • Deposition also needs a supply of sediment, from eroding cliffs, rivers and the seabed.
  • A beach builds up where more sediment arrives than is removed.
  • Sandy beaches have gentle slopes; shingle beaches (pebbles) are steeper. Slapton Sands, Devon, is shingle: "flint, chert and quartz pebbles".
Mission 2

Spits

  • Where the coast changes direction, sediment carried along the coast keeps going and is dropped in the calmer water beyond: a spit grows out from the land.
  • The end often curves round as waves bend into the calmer water behind it: a hooked (recurved) spit. Saltmarsh forms in the shelter behind.
  • Spurn formed "as a long, narrow spit from deposits moving along the coast carried by currents". It is over three miles long but as little as 50 m wide.
Mission 2

Bars

  • A bar is a ridge of sediment that joins two headlands, cutting off the water behind as a lagoon.
  • Slapton Sands, Devon, is a shingle bar linking Strete Gate with Torcross. It dammed an old estuary and formed a freshwater lagoon, Slapton Ley.
  • Its main formation probably began about 5,000 years ago, as the rising sea pushed shingle landward. Longshore drift still replenishes it.
Quick check
?What is the key difference between a spit and a bar?
Quick check
?Why do beaches often form in bays rather than on headlands?
Mission check

?Shingle drifts east along a straight coast. Then the coastline turns sharply north into a river estuary. What will most likely form, and why?

Spurn is a spit. Sediment carried south along the Holderness coast by longshore drift keeps going where the coast turns at the Humber mouth, and is dropped in the calmer water. Its end hooks into the estuary, with saltmarsh behind.

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

Mission 3

Warm-water coastal ecosystems

  • Coral reefs: in "tropical and semi-tropical waters". Reef-building corals need water of at least 18 °C, clear water for light, and salinity of 32–42 parts per thousand.
  • Mangroves: salt-tolerant trees on muddy tropical and subtropical coasts; they "cannot withstand freezing temperatures".
  • World mangroves: 14.8 million hectares in 2020; nearly 44% in South and Southeast Asia.
Mission 3

Salt marshes and sand dunes

  • Salt marshes: grasses and herbs on sheltered muddy coasts and estuaries, flooded by high tides.
  • The most complete map so far shows about 5.5 million hectares of salt marsh in 43 countries; the largest areas are outside the tropics, especially around the North Atlantic.
  • Sand dunes: form behind wide sandy beaches where onshore winds blow dry sand inland. Plants such as marram grass trap the sand.
  • Sand dunes are found on sandy coasts at all latitudes, from the tropics to the Arctic, wherever there is plenty of sand and a strong onshore wind.
  • UK examples: the Sefton Coast (Merseyside) and Studland (Dorset).
Quick check
?Why do salt marshes, not mangroves, grow on the muddy estuaries of north-west Europe?
Mission 3

Abiotic and biotic

  • An ecosystem is a community of living things and the non-living environment they interact with.
  • Abiotic (non-living): climate, water, salt, tides, sediment, soil, light.
  • Biotic (living): plants, animals, fungi, bacteria.
  • Abiotic factors decide what can live in a place; living things then change the abiotic environment.
Mission 3

The Sundarbans

  • The Sundarbans lie on the delta of the Ganges, Brahmaputra and Meghna rivers, on the Bay of Bengal.
  • It is "the largest contiguous mangrove forest in the world".
  • About 60% lies in Bangladesh and the rest in India.
  • Bangladesh's World Heritage site covers about 140,000 hectares.
Mission 3

Abiotic characteristics

  • Tides: "a complex network of tidal waterways, mudflats and small islands"; the land "has been moulded by tidal action".
  • Water: "brackish" — a mix of river fresh water and seawater.
  • Sediment: the land was "formed from sediments deposited by three great rivers".
  • Soil: mud low in oxygen, where slow water lets fine sediment settle.
  • Climate: hot and wet, with monsoon rains and floods; cyclones and surges "up to 7.5 m high".
Mission 3

Biotic characteristics

  • Plants: 334 plant species; the forest trees are salt-tolerant mangroves.
  • Animals: 49 mammal, 59 reptile, 210 fish (white fish), 24 shrimp and 14 crab species.
  • Top predators include the Bengal tiger and the estuarine crocodile; Ganges and Irrawaddy dolphins live in the channels.
  • Adaptations: a tangle of roots lets trees "handle the daily rise and fall of tides"; mangroves tolerate salt.
Quick check
?Which of these is an abiotic characteristic of the Sundarbans?
Mission 3

The food web

  • Producers: mangrove trees, algae on the mud, and phytoplankton in the water.
  • Many mangrove leaves are not eaten fresh: they fall, rot, and become detritus (tiny bits of dead matter).
  • Primary consumers: crabs, shrimps, snails and small fish eat detritus, algae and plankton.
  • Secondary consumers: larger fish, birds and dolphins eat them.
  • Top predators: crocodiles and tigers.
  • Remove one link (e.g. crabs) and others above it go short of food.
Mission 3

The nutrient cycle

  • Nutrients move between three stores: biomass (living things), litter (fallen leaves) and soil (mud).
  • Inputs: river sediment and tides bring nutrients in.
  • Leaf fall moves nutrients from biomass to litter; heat and moisture make decomposition fast, moving them into the mud; roots take them back up.
  • Outputs: tides also wash leaves and nutrients out to sea, where they feed coastal fisheries.
Quick check
?New embankments stop the tide reaching part of a mangrove forest. What is the most likely first effect on its nutrient cycle?
Mission 3

How abiotic and biotic are linked

  • Abiotic → biotic: salt and daily flooding mean only salt-tolerant, tide-adapted plants grow.
  • Biotic → abiotic: roots slow tidal water so sediment settles, building up the mud.
  • The forest also "acts as a shelter belt to protect the people from storms, cyclones, tidal surges".
  • Exam move: describe a characteristic, then explain how it links to another (e.g. "brackish water → only salt-tolerant trees → leaf litter feeds crabs").
Mission check

?On a salt marsh in north-west Europe, plants nearest the sea are flooded by every high tide; plants higher up are flooded only by the highest tides. Fewer species grow nearest the sea. What best explains this?

In coastal ecosystems, abiotic factors (tides, salt, sediment, climate) decide which biotic life can survive. In the Sundarbans, salt-tolerant mangroves turn river mud and tides into a food web from detritus to tigers.

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

Mission 4

Where coral reefs grow

  • Reef-building corals live in tropical and semi-tropical seas.
  • They cannot survive water below 18 °C.
  • The water must be clear, so sunlight reaches the algae living in the coral, and salty (32–42 parts per thousand).
  • Reefs cover only about 1% of the ocean, yet give habitat to at least 25% of marine life.
Mission 4

Where mangroves grow

  • Mangroves: salt-tolerant trees on muddy tropical and subtropical coasts, between low and high tide.
  • The world had 14.8 million hectares of mangroves in 2020.
  • Nearly 44% is in South and South-east Asia; Indonesia alone has 21%.
Mission 4

Why they matter

  • Coral reefs protect shorelines "by reducing the impact from waves, storms, and floods". They support fishing and tourism, and many medicines are being developed from reef animals and plants.
  • Mangroves store large amounts of carbon in their soils, protect coasts from storms and erosion, and support rich wildlife.
  • They also shelter young fish that later live on reefs.
Quick check
?Which is a benefit of mangroves to people on the coast?
Mission 4

Threats to reefs: heat

  • Coral bleaching: under stress, corals expel the algae (zooxanthellae) living in their tissues and turn white.
  • The leading cause is warmer water from climate change; runoff and pollution, strong sunlight and very low tides add stress.
  • From early 2023 to mid-2025, bleaching-level heat stress hit 84% of the world's coral reef area.
  • Bleached coral is not dead, but many corals die if the stress lasts.
Mission 4

Threats from people

  • Farming: fertiliser and soil wash into the sea, clouding the water and feeding algae. Poor water quality is a named threat to the Great Barrier Reef.
  • Deforestation: mangroves are cleared for fish and shrimp ponds, rice and oil palm. Forest cleared inland sends mud down rivers onto reefs.
  • Industrialisation: ports, dredging, factories and coastal building pollute water and bury habitat.
  • Tourism: hotels on the shore, sewage, boat anchors, and divers touching coral.
Quick check
?A new resort dredges a channel for boats, and its sewage is piped into the lagoon. Which threat is this?
Mission 4

Mangroves: losses and gains

  • 2000–2020: 677,000 ha of mangroves were lost, and 393,000 ha grew back, a net loss of 284,000 ha.
  • The biggest drivers of loss: aquaculture (fish and shrimp ponds) 27%, natural retreat 26%, other farming 12%, oil palm 8%, rice 8%.
  • The rate of net loss fell by 44% between 2000–10 and 2010–20.
Mission 4

Gazi Bay, Kenya: Mikoko Pamoja

  • "The world's first community-led blue carbon project": local people protect and replant mangroves.
  • The carbon the trees store is sold as carbon credits to buyers who want to balance out their own emissions.
  • 1,081 households take part.
  • The money "has enabled community development such as provision of schoolbooks, construction of school buildings and the provision of clean drinking water".
Mission check

?A fishing village on another tropical coast wants to protect its mangroves but needs income. Which scheme follows the Gazi Bay model?

In Gazi Bay, buyers of carbon credits pay for the carbon that protected mangroves store. The village gains income for schools and clean water, so it has a reason to keep the trees.

Mission 5

Many users, one coast

  • Coasts are wanted for homes, tourism, ports and industry, energy, farming and fishing.
  • They are also home to wildlife that needs space and quiet.
  • Conflict happens when one stretch of coast is wanted for several uses at once.
Mission 5

Conservation or development?

  • Development: building or using the coast for jobs, energy, housing and income.
  • Conservation: protecting habitats, wildlife and landscapes, often through legal designations.
  • UK designations include Sites of Special Scientific Interest (SSSIs), Special Protection Areas (for birds), Ramsar sites (wetlands) and Areas of Outstanding Natural Beauty (AONBs).
Quick check
?Which view is a conservation view of coastal management?
Mission 5

Case study: Sizewell C, Suffolk

  • A planned nuclear power station: two reactors of about 1,670 MW each, about 3,340 MW in total.
  • The plan also included a workers' accommodation campus and land set aside to make up for lost wildlife habitat.
  • Next door: RSPB Minsmere nature reserve, part of a Special Protection Area and Ramsar site, and the Sizewell Marshes SSSI.
Mission 5

Who had a say?

  • 1,282 formal comments (relevant representations) were received.
  • Those taking part included the developer, East Suffolk Council, Natural England, the RSPB, Suffolk Wildlife Trust, the National Trust, the water company, local action groups and residents.
  • Planning inspectors examined the plan from April to October 2021 and reported to the government in February 2022.
Mission 5

The conservation case

  • The inspectors concluded that harm to the Minsmere SSSI "is likely".
  • The project takes land permanently from the Sizewell Marshes SSSI.
  • The developer accepted there would be significant harm to the recreational benefits of the AONB, Minsmere and Dunwich.
Mission 5

The development case, and its pressures

  • Economy: the councils and other stakeholders acknowledged that "substantial economic opportunities would arise".
  • Housing: a 2,400-bed campus and a 600-berth caravan site for workers, plus a Housing Fund.
  • Tourism: effects likely to be negative during construction and neutral once operating; a Tourism Fund was the response.

Mission 5

The decision

  • The inspectors recommended against consent unless the water supply plan was resolved and enough information was provided on protected habitats.
  • On 20 July 2022 the Secretary of State granted consent: "the very substantial and urgent need for the proposal outweighs the harms".
  • The government took its final investment decision on 22 July 2025.
Mission 5

Who won, who lost?

  • Gains: the developer, the government (national energy need) and local businesses.
  • Losses: wildlife sites, and quiet recreation in the AONB; tourism during construction.
  • Reduced, not removed: harms were mitigated with funds and compensation land.
  • Exam move: name each user, say whether they want conservation or development, and explain who decides.

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

Mission 6

Coastal flooding

  • Coastal flooding: the sea covers land that is normally dry.
  • Three causes in this mission: storm surges, tsunamis and climate change (rising sea level).
  • Floods are worst when a surge or wave arrives at high tide.
Mission 6

Storm surges

  • Storm surge: "an abnormal water level rise generated by a storm, over and above the predicted astronomical tide".
  • Strong onshore winds pile water against the coast. A "wide, gently sloping continental shelf will allow for a higher storm surge".
  • UK example: in December 2013, "a huge tidal surge" breached Spurn, a spit in East Yorkshire.
Mission 6

Tsunamis

  • A large undersea earthquake moves the seabed, shifting the whole depth of water above it. A series of waves spreads out.
  • Near the source, waves arrive in minutes: after New Zealand's 2016 Kaikōura earthquake, "the first wave arrived within 10 minutes".
  • The 2004 Indian Ocean earthquake "generated a tsunami that killed over 230,000 people".
Quick check
?Which conditions would give the highest storm surge?
Mission 6

Climate change: a higher sea

  • Global mean sea level rose 0.20 m between 1901 and 2018.
  • The rate is rising: 1.3 mm a year (1901–1971), 3.7 mm a year (2006–2018).
  • Projection for 2100: a likely rise of 0.28–0.55 m with very low emissions, and 0.63–1.01 m with very high emissions.
  • A higher sea lets every surge and wave reach further inland.
Mission 6

Prediction: forecasting and warnings

  • Forecasting: satellites and computer models track storms days ahead.
  • Hurricane Ian (Florida, USA, 2022): the storm surge watch and warning for Fort Myers Beach were issued 48 and 30 hours before tropical-storm-force winds arrived.
  • Forecasts change: the peak surge forecast there rose from 1.2–2.1 m (4–7 ft) to a final 3.7–5.5 m (12–18 ft); the surge reached 3–4.6 m (10–15 ft) above ground.
  • Tsunamis give far less warning: minutes near the source.
Mission 6

Prevention: building design, planning and education

  • Building design: raise homes on stilts or high foundations above the expected flood level; keep wiring and boilers upstairs; use materials that survive soaking.
  • Defences: in Japan in 2011, a tsunami "overtopped coastal defenses, primarily 5–10 m seawalls, causing more than 15,000 deaths". Replacement walls planned are about 12 m high.
  • Planning: Japan now plans for "two levels of tsunamis": frequent ones that defences stop, and the largest possible ones.
  • Education: for the largest tsunamis, Japan relies on "non-structural measures centering on evacuation, such as preparation of hazard maps": people must know where to go.
Quick check
?Every year, schools on a coast practise walking to high ground after a tsunami alarm. Which kind of response is this?
Mission check

?On a low coast, the sea floods two towns overnight. There is no rain and no earthquake, but strong onshore winds blow over a shallow sea at high tide. What pushed the sea inland?

The sea is pushed inland by storm surges (wind-driven), tsunamis (earthquake-driven) and, slowly, by rising sea level, which makes both reach further.

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 2.3 coastal management in a developed country
  • 2.3c soft engineering, hard engineering and shoreline management plans
Mission 7

Four policies for each stretch of coast

  • Hold the line: keep the defences where they are.
  • Advance the line: build new defences further out to sea.
  • Managed retreat (managed realignment): let the sea in, behind a new line of defence inland.
  • No active intervention: "Let nature take its course – no work will be carried out to repair defences".
  • A shoreline management plan (SMP) sets one of these policies for each stretch of a coast, so that neighbouring schemes do not clash.
Mission 7

Hard engineering

  • Sea walls: concrete walls that reflect waves. Strong, but very costly, and waves can scour the beach in front.
  • Rock armour (rip rap): big boulders that break up waves. Cheaper than a sea wall, but can look out of place and make the beach harder to reach.
  • Gabions: wire cages filled with rocks; cheaper than walls, but the wire rusts, so they do not last as long.
  • Groynes: barriers across the beach that trap longshore drift and keep the beach wide. Down-drift, the beach can be starved: groynes at one point on the Sussex coast "have contributed to erosion" of the spit beyond them.
  • Revetments: sloping walls or fences that absorb wave energy.
  • Ventnor, Isle of Wight, sits on "the largest urbanised landslide complex in north-west Europe"; without its sea wall, erosion "would create instability".
Mission 7

Soft engineering

  • Beach nourishment (replenishment): add sand or shingle to the beach, which absorbs wave energy. It looks natural, but must be repeated.
  • Cliff regrading and drainage: make a cliff less steep and drain water from it. Revegetation and ecosystem rehabilitation: replant dunes and saltmarsh.
  • Managed retreat: give low land to the sea; it becomes saltmarsh, which absorbs wave energy.
Quick check
?Groynes keep one town's beach wide. What is their most likely cost?
Mission 7

Medmerry: the problem

  • Medmerry lies west of Selsey. A shingle bank there protected 300 properties, large caravan sites and 650 hectares of land.
  • The flood risk was 1 in 1 in any given year, and the bank had about 1 year of life left.
  • The B2145 and the utilities that serve Selsey cross the low land behind the bank.
Mission 7

The options and their costs (2007)

  • No active intervention: £8–10 million. Without winter repairs, the bank was likely to breach within a year.
  • Hold the line: £80 million; judged "not practical".
  • Managed realignment: £10 million, for benefits valued at £40 million.
  • Costs are present values: today's value of the spending over the strategy's time span.
Quick check
?Managed realignment at Medmerry was estimated to cost £10 million and to bring benefits worth £40 million. How many pounds of benefit for each £1 spent?
Mission 7

Hold the line or let the sea in?

  • For holding the line: all property and land keep some protection, and losses are delayed.
  • Against: rising flood risk, more future funding, and "increasingly out of step with natural processes".
  • For managed realignment: it works with natural processes, cuts long-term spending, manages flood risk and may create a large area of wildlife habitat.
  • Against: flooding of land and property, possible loss of beach access, and a possible cut in shingle supply to the Witterings, along the coast.
Mission 7

What happened: effects and conflicts

  • Completed in 2014, with 7 km of new embankments inland, protecting key infrastructure for Selsey.
  • The £28 million scheme was then "the largest open-coast scheme in Europe"; it created a new wetland, with the RSPB's support.
  • The government also had a duty to create new intertidal habitat, to replace losses elsewhere.
  • Conflict: realignment meant "the flooding of a relatively large area of land".
  • Evaluate: £28 million spent is not directly comparable with the 2007 present-value estimate of £10 million.
Mission check

?On a low farmland coast, an old sea wall is failing. Rebuilding it would cost far more than the land behind it is worth. A town sits on higher ground nearby, but its only road crosses the low land. Which policy fits best?

At Medmerry, holding a failing bank was costed at £80 million against £10 million to realign (2007 estimates). New banks inland protect Selsey's road and services, and the flooded land became wetland. The price was a "relatively large area of land" given to the sea.

Mission 8

Mission 8: Bangladesh guards its coast with 6,000 km of earth banks. Why is it now planting forests in front of them?

Make a guess: Bangladesh guards its coast with 6,000 km of earth banks. Why is it now planting forests in front of them?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 2.3 case study of coastal management in a developing or emerging country
  • 2.3c coastal management: soft engineering, hard engineering and shoreline management plans
Mission 8

A coast at risk

  • Bangladesh is a low-lying delta country on the Bay of Bengal: a developing country.
  • Threats: cyclones, storm surges, river and coastal erosion, sea-level rise and saline intrusion (salt water getting into farmland).
  • A World Bank study found a 10-year cyclone would flood 8 million people to depths over 3 m; by 2050, 22.5 million (projection).
Mission 8

Hard engineering: the polders

  • A polder is low land enclosed by an embankment (earth bank) to keep out the sea and tides.
  • Bangladesh has 6,000 km of embankments enclosing 139 polders, built in the 1960s–1970s.
  • They raised farm output by 200–300% at some polders.
  • But they "were not designed to withstand coastal surges", and poor maintenance led to tidal flooding and lower yields.
Quick check
?Why is an embankment around a polder an example of hard engineering?
Mission 8

Hard, soft and ecosystem-based

  • Hard engineering: built structures (sea walls, embankments, groynes). Strong, but costly and need maintenance.
  • Soft engineering: works with natural processes (beach nourishment, dune or marsh restoration).
  • Ecosystem-based: restoring living defences, such as mangrove belts, that absorb wave energy.
  • Shoreline management plans (as in the UK) decide the approach for each stretch of coast over the long term. Bangladesh plans its polders as a long-term programme, using a storm-surge model of 19 major cyclones from 1960 to 2009.
Mission 8

The Coastal Embankment Improvement Project

  • Supported by the World Bank, approved in 2013, closed at the end of 2023.
  • Aims: protect polders from tidal flooding and storm surges, and improve farming by reducing saline intrusion.
  • It upgraded 405.67 km of embankment around 10 polders: 17 were planned, but the scope was cut "due to the financing constraints".
  • Result: 65,657 hectares protected; 720,573 people with increased resilience.
Mission 8

Planting a living defence

  • The project afforested 700 hectares on embankment slopes and foreshores, as a "nature-based solution".
  • 142 social forestry groups with 8,278 members, including 2,351 women, care for the trees and share plantation income.
  • The trees store carbon: about 117,814 tonnes of CO₂e.
Mission 8

Why forests in front of walls?

  • A World Bank model found that mangrove belts 100–1,000 m wide could cut wave heights by 7–55%.
  • Smaller waves mean embankments could be 0.09–0.30 m lower, with 13–46% thinner slope protection.
  • Limits: mangroves likely have only a small effect on surge height; narrow belts can be damaged by pests and storms, and trees older than 10–20 years "might collapse during storms".
Quick check
?A 2.0 m wave reaches a mangrove belt that cuts wave height by 55%. How high is the wave when it reaches the embankment?
Mission 8

Who gained?

  • Farmers: cropping intensity rose from 140% to 192%: more crops grown on the same fields each year.
  • Communities: 10 water management associations now oversee polder upkeep, such as closing gates during cyclones.
  • Women: 36% of the 72,329 members of water management groups.
Mission 8

Who lost out?

  • Building on embankments needed land. Some households lost land, homes or businesses.
  • 291 and 478 female-headed households were affected by resettlement under the two works packages; they received relocation benefits and a grant.
  • The report's "major lesson": delays in land acquisition and in payment to affected people.
  • Works ran 36 months late; in some areas farmers had no irrigation for a whole year while gates were rebuilt.
Quick check
?Which group gained least from the project?
Mission 8

Was it a success?

  • Successes: 65,657 ha protected; more crops; 700 ha of new forest; local groups running maintenance.
  • Problems: only 10 of 17 polders; long delays; resettlement and late payments.
  • The remaining 7 polders were moved to a possible second phase.
  • The report's lesson: a "critical need to complement infrastructure interventions in polders with nature-based solutions".
Mission 8

Exam move: evaluating management

  • Name both parts: hard (upgraded embankments) and ecosystem-based (afforestation, mangroves).
  • Give evidence for and against, with figures.
  • Judge for whom: farmers and communities gained; some resettled households lost out.
  • Compare with the developed-country case: richer countries can afford to choose between holding the line and managed realignment.
Mission check

?A low-income country plans a concrete sea wall along an eroding, muddy tropical coast. It has little money for maintenance. Based on Bangladesh's experience, which plan is most likely to work best?

Bangladesh is planting forests in front of its embankments because mangrove belts cut wave heights (by up to 55% in models), so the banks last longer and can be smaller. Banks still hold back the surge; together they work better than either alone.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Destructive waves erode and constructive waves build. Erosion and slumping wear cliffs back; longshore drift moves the sediment along the coast.
  • Rock type sets the pace: weak till retreats fast; chalk resists.

Mission 2

  • Hard rock makes headlands; soft rock makes bays. Headlands are eroded along joints: cave, arch, stack, stump.
  • Where waves lose energy, sediment builds beaches; where the coast turns, it builds spits.
  • A spit that crosses a bay becomes a bar.

Mission 3

  • Distribution: coral reefs and mangroves in warm tropical waters; salt marshes mostly in cooler mid-latitudes (e.g. north-west Europe); sand dunes on sandy, windy coasts at all latitudes.
  • Sundarbans: abiotic (tides, brackish water, river sediment, monsoon, cyclones) and biotic (334 plant species, tiger, crocodile, crabs, fish) linked by a detritus food web and a tidal nutrient cycle.

Mission 4

  • Coral reefs (clear, warm, shallow tropical seas) and mangroves (tropical muddy coasts) protect coasts and support fish, but face warming, farming, deforestation, industry and tourism.
  • Management works best when local people gain (Mikoko Pamoja), but no local plan can stop global warming.

Mission 5

  • Coastal conflict is conservation versus development: list the users, their views and who decides.
  • At Sizewell C, development was approved, with funds and compensation land to reduce the harm.

Mission 6

  • Causes: storm surge, tsunami, sea-level rise; worst at high tide.
  • Prediction and prevention: forecasting, building design, planning, education.

Mission 7

  • Hard engineering holds the line but is costly and can harm the coast down-drift; soft engineering works with natural processes.
  • Medmerry chose managed retreat: cheaper and lower-risk than holding a failing bank, with habitat gained and land lost.

Mission 8

  • Bangladesh: 6,000 km of 1960s–70s embankments (139 polders) are being upgraded (hard) and fronted with planted forest (ecosystem-based).
  • Outcomes (CEIP-I, 2013–2023): 65,657 ha protected and 720,573 people more resilient, more crops; but only 10 of 17 polders, delays and resettlement.
