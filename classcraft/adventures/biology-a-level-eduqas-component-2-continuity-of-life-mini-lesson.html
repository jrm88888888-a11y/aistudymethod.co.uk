Mission 1

Mission 1: Two animals look almost identical. How can biologists tell whether they are truly close relatives?

Make a guess: Two animals look almost identical. How can biologists tell whether they are truly close relatives?

Mission 1

Same bones, different jobs

  • Homologous structures: the same basic structure, used for different functions.
  • Pentadactyl limb: one upper bone, two lower bones, a group of wrist bones, five digits.
  • Found in a human arm, a bat wing, a whale flipper.
  • No single job needs this layout. It was inherited from a common ancestor and modified.
  • Eduqas calls this physical relatedness.
Mission 1

Same job, different origins

  • Analogous structures: the same function, different evolutionary origins.
  • They arise by convergent evolution: similar selection pressures favour similar forms in unrelated lineages.
  • Examples: streamlined body and fins of a shark (fish) and a dolphin (mammal); wings of birds and insects.
  • Say: "convergent: features became alike" — not: "the species are merging".
Quick check
?Sharks, which are fish, and dolphins, which are mammals, both have streamlined bodies, a dorsal fin and paddle-shaped front fins. Which statement about these features is correct?

  • Fewer differences → more recent common ancestor. P and Q are the closest relatives here.
  • DNA differs more than protein: some base changes do not change the amino acid.
  • Genetic fingerprinting: more similar DNA profiles, closer relationship. Enzyme studies: closer relatives have more similar enzymes.
  • At the widest scale, almost all organisms share one genetic code: best explained by a common ancestor of all life.

Quick check
?The amino acid sequence of the same protein was compared in four species. Compared with species W, species X differs at 2 positions, species Y at 15 positions and species Z at 31 positions. Which conclusion is supported by the data?
Mission check

?The marsupial mole of Australia and the golden moles of southern Africa look remarkably alike: tiny or hidden eyes, no external ears and spade-like front claws for digging. DNA comparisons place the marsupial mole closest to other Australian marsupials, and the golden moles closest to other African placental mammals. Which conclusion is best supported?

Looks can converge. Inherited structure and molecular sequences show who is really related.

Mission 2

Mission 2: If two groups of the same animal never meet again, how do they end up as different species?

Make a guess: If two groups of the same animal never meet again, how do they end up as different species?

Mission 2

What counts as a species?

  • Biological species concept: a group of organisms that can interbreed and produce fertile offspring.
  • Horse × donkey → mule. Offspring, yes; fertile, no. So horse and donkey are two species.
  • Say: "can produce fertile offspring" — not: "can produce offspring".
Quick check
?According to the biological species concept, what is a species?
Mission 2

Where the definition struggles

  • Often it cannot be tested:
    • fossils cannot be crossed;
    • populations that never meet cannot show it in the wild.
  • It does not fit organisms that reproduce asexually.
  • Some pairs of species still produce some fertile hybrids (common in plants).
  • So competing species concepts exist: shared traits, DNA, ancestry. None fits every case.
Mission 2

Speciation means splitting

  • Speciation: one species splits into two or more.
  • The key step: populations become reproductively isolated. Gene flow between them stops.
  • Their gene pools then accumulate differences, until members can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring.
  • Usually gradual. Part-way through, "one species or two?" can be an arbitrary call.
  • A lineage that changes over time without splitting has not speciated.
Mission 2

Allopatric speciation, step by step

  • (1) One population. Gene flow keeps its gene pool mixed.
  • (2) A geographical barrier (river, sea, mountains) splits it. Gene flow stops. This is allopatric speciation.
  • (3) Each gene pool now changes on its own: new mutations, different selection pressures, and genetic drift in small populations. Allele frequencies diverge.
  • (4) In time, the two can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring: two species.
  • Say: "isolation stopped gene flow; selection changed allele frequencies" — not: "the river caused mutations" or "they changed in order to adapt".

Mission 2

What keeps species apart

  • Isolating mechanisms stop two gene pools mixing.
  • Before fertilisation (pre-zygotic):
    • geographical: they live in different places;
    • seasonal (temporal): they breed at different times;
    • behavioural: courtship signals differ;
    • morphological (mechanical): reproductive parts or flowers do not fit;
    • gametic: sperm cannot fertilise the other species' eggs.
  • After fertilisation (post-zygotic):
    • the hybrid dies before it can breed;
    • hybrid sterility: the hybrid lives but cannot breed. Mule: 32 horse + 31 donkey chromosomes = 63; the unmatched sets cannot pair properly in meiosis.
  • Eduqas names: geographical, behavioural, morphological and seasonal isolation, and hybrid sterility.
Mission 2

Courtship: the right reply or no mating

  • Courtship behaviour is a necessary step before successful mating.
  • It lets an animal:
    • recognise its own species;
    • recognise a mate of the opposite sex that is ready to breed;
    • synchronise mating, so gametes meet;
    • choose a strong, healthy mate.
  • Signals are species-specific: songs, displays, flashes, scents.
  • Example: male fireflies of each Photinus species flash their own pattern; females answer only their own.
  • Wrong signal, no reply, no mating: behavioural isolation.
Quick check
?Two closely related bird species live in the same forest. The males of each species sing a different song, and females respond only to the song of their own species. What is the significance of this for the two species?
Mission check

?A population of lizards was split when rising sea level cut off an island from the mainland. Many generations later, island and mainland lizards can no longer produce fertile offspring together. Which statement about this speciation is correct?

The barrier only stops gene flow. Mutation, selection and drift do the rest, over many generations.

Mission 3

Mission 3: How can just two words tell you where any living thing fits among millions?

Mission 3

Too many to know one by one

  • About 1.2 million species are catalogued; an estimated 8.7 million species of eukaryotes exist.
  • Classification: sorting organisms into groups.
  • Why classify?
    • identify an organism and name it without confusion;
    • predict features of a group's members;
    • show evolutionary relationships;
    • make wider study possible (ecology, medicine, conservation).
Mission 3

The species: the unit everything rests on

  • Every classification is built on the species.
  • Two organisms are the same species if they can interbreed to produce fertile offspring.
Mission 3

Groups within groups

  • Each group is a taxon (plural taxa).
  • Domain → kingdom → phylum → class → order → family → genus → species.
  • Smaller groups sit inside larger ones, with no overlap.
  • Lower taxa: fewer species, more features shared.
  • Humans: Eukarya · Animalia · Chordata · Mammalia · Primates · Hominidae · Homo · Homo sapiens.
Quick check
?The red fox, Vulpes vulpes, and the grey wolf, Canis lupus, are both placed in the family Canidae. Which statement must be true?
Mission 3

Two-part names

  • Binomial system (Linnaeus): every species has a two-part name.
  • 1st word: genus, with a capital letter.
  • 2nd word: species, all lower case. It means nothing on its own.
  • Italics in print; underline when handwritten.
  • Same genus = similar traits, usually close relatives: Panthera leo (lion), Panthera tigris (tiger).
  • After first use, shorten the genus: P. leo.
  • Say: "Homo sapiens" — not: "Homo Sapiens", and not "sapiens" alone.
Mission 3

Why not just use common names?

  • A binomial is universal: the same in every country and language.
  • Common names mislead. "Robin" means Erithacus rubecula in Britain but Turdus migratorius in North America.
  • One species can have many local names; a "starfish" is not a fish.
  • The genus shows at once which species are close relatives.
Quick check
?Which statement about the scientific name of the tiger, Panthera tigris, is correct?
Mission 3

Classify by ancestry

  • Phylogenetic classification: groups reflect evolutionary origins and relationships.
  • Ideal: every member of a group descends from one common ancestor.
  • Advantage: features of a group can be predicted. A newly found member probably shares them.
  • Evidence: anatomy, plus DNA and protein sequences.
  • Classification is tentative: it changes as new evidence arrives.
Quick check
?What is the main advantage of a classification in which every group corresponds to evolutionary relationships?
Mission check

?The raven is Corvus corax, the rook is Corvus frugilegus and the magpie is Pica pica. What can be concluded from these names alone?

Two words: the genus places it among its closest relatives; the species word picks it out from them.

Mission 4

Clues to who is related

  • Relatives share features inherited from a common ancestor.
  • Evidence:
    • anatomy: homologous structures, in living species and fossils;
    • DNA and mRNA base sequences;
    • amino acid sequences of proteins.
  • After two lineages split, mutations build up separately in each.
  • So: fewer differences → more recent common ancestor → closer relatives.
  • Say: "share a more recent common ancestor" — not: "one evolved from the other".

Mission 4

More ways to measure similarity

  • Genetic fingerprinting: compare DNA band patterns. More shared bands = closer relatives.
  • Enzyme studies: compare the structure of the same enzyme across species.
  • Molecules are not fooled by convergence: unrelated organisms that look alike.
Mission 4

Every tree is a hypothesis

  • Trees and cladograms are testable hypotheses. New evidence can revise them.
  • Anatomy can mislead: convergent evolution gives similar traits to unrelated groups.
  • Biochemical methods show relatedness without the problem of morphological convergence.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Why are some microbes that look just like bacteria placed further from them than you are from a mushroom?

Make a guess: Why are some microbes that look just like bacteria placed further from them than you are from a mushroom?

Mission 5

One chemistry, many cell plans

  • Every living thing contains the same four classes of biochemicals: carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, nucleic acids.
  • All store genetic information in DNA and use ATP.
  • The big differences are in cell structure and in how organisms get energy and carbon.
  • These differences sort life into its largest groups.
Mission 5

Five kingdoms: the small and simple

  • The five-kingdom system was built on observable features: cell type, nucleus, cell wall, body form, nutrition.
  • Prokaryotae:
    • no nucleus, no membrane-bound organelles; circular DNA;
    • small (70S) ribosomes; cell wall usually present (in bacteria, made of peptidoglycan);
    • mostly single cells; reproduce by division (binary fission).
  • Protoctista:
    • eukaryotic;
    • mostly single cells, or little cell differentiation;
    • some autotrophs, some heterotrophs.
Mission 5

Five kingdoms: fungi, plants, animals

  • Fungi:
    • eukaryotic; threads called hyphae, with chitin cell walls;
    • heterotrophs: release enzymes onto food, digest it outside the body, absorb the products (many are saprotrophs).
  • Plantae: eukaryotic, multicellular; autotrophs (producers); chlorophyll in chloroplasts; cellulose cell walls.
  • Animalia: eukaryotic, multicellular; heterotrophs; no cell walls; capable of locomotion.
  • Say: "fungi are heterotrophs with chitin walls" — not: "fungi are plants".
Quick check
?A newly described organism grows as a mass of branching threads through dead leaves. Its cells have nuclei and walls containing chitin. It releases enzymes onto the leaves and absorbs the digested products. In which kingdom should it be placed?
Mission 5

Three domains

  • 1977: Carl Woese and George Fox compared ribosomal RNA (rRNA) base sequences from many organisms.
  • Prokaryotes fell into two groups, as different from each other as either is from eukaryotes.
  • So life has three domains, a level above kingdoms: Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya.
  • You and a mushroom: both Eukarya, one domain.
  • Archaea look like bacteria: no nucleus. But their key molecules differ, and some are closer to ours.
  • Say: "archaea are a separate domain" — not: "archaea are unusual bacteria".

Mission 5

Kingdoms or domains? New data on trial

  • Five kingdoms: based on observable features. All prokaryotes: one kingdom.
  • Three domains: based on molecular evidence. Prokaryotes: two domains. Protoctista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia all sit inside Eukarya.
  • Domains add a level above kingdoms. The eukaryote kingdoms stay; kingdom Prokaryotae is split into two domains.
  • It shows the tentative nature of classification.
Mission check

?A single-celled organism from a salt lake has no nucleus. Its cell wall contains no peptidoglycan, and its rRNA base sequence is closest to those of known archaea. How is it classified in the two systems?

Looks put it with the bacteria; its molecules put it in a domain of its own.

Mission 6

Three levels of biodiversity

  • Biodiversity: the variety of life, at three levels.
  • Ecosystem (habitat) diversity: the range of habitats or ecosystems, e.g. sand dune, woodland, meadow, stream.
  • Species diversity: the number of species and how evenly the individuals are spread among them.
  • Genetic diversity: the variety of alleles within a species.
  • Eduqas: the number and variety of organisms in a specified geographic region.
Quick check
?A survey of an island records three findings: the island contains six distinct ecosystems; 420 species have been identified; and its population of one lizard species carries 18 alleles at a studied gene, compared with 4 alleles in the same species on a neighbouring island. Which of these findings describe the island's biodiversity?
Mission 6

Richness and evenness

Illustrative data: two meadows, same four species, 40 plants each.

SpeciesMeadow AMeadow B
Buttercup1031
Clover103
Daisy103
Plantain103
  • Species richness: the number of species. Both meadows: 4.
  • Species evenness: how evenly individuals are spread among the species. A is even; B is dominated by buttercup.
  • Species diversity needs both. Meadow A is more diverse.
  • Say: "diversity depends on richness and evenness" — not: "diversity is the number of species".
Mission 6

Simpson's Index of Diversity

  • Use the form the question gives. One common form: D = 1 − Σ(n/N)².
  • N = total number of organisms of all species; n = number of organisms of each species.
  • Values from 0 to just under 1. Closer to 1 = more diverse; near 0 = one species dominates.
  • Meadow A: 0.75. Meadow B: 0.38.
  • Another form, N(N − 1) ÷ Σn(n − 1), gives values of 1 or more: always check which form you are given.
Mission 6

Worked example

Illustrative data: pitfall traps in a wood. Species P 12, Q 6, R 2.

  • N = 12 + 6 + 2 = 20.
  • n/N: 0.60, 0.30, 0.10.
  • Square each: 0.36, 0.09, 0.01. Sum: Σ(n/N)² = 0.46.
  • D = 1 − 0.46 = 0.54.
  • Square before you add, and do not stop at 0.46.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A rock pool holds limpets 8, barnacles 6, periwinkles 4 and sea anemones 2. Using D = 1 − Σ(n/N)², what is Simpson's Index of Diversity?
Mission 6

Sampling for an index

  • You cannot count every organism. Take samples that represent the habitat.
  • Random sampling: random-number coordinates on a grid; quadrats of equal size. Avoids bias.
  • Take many samples. One quadrat misses species and shows nothing about variation.
  • Non-random sampling: systematic (at regular intervals, e.g. along a transect across a gradient); stratified (sample each zone in proportion to its area); opportunistic (where organisms happen to be: quick, but biased).
  • Compare two sites only with the same method and effort.
Mission 6

Practical: collecting samples

  • Specified practical: investigation into biodiversity in a habitat.
  • Sweep net: swept through long grass; catches flying and plant-dwelling insects.
  • Pitfall trap: a cup sunk level with the soil; catches ground-dwelling invertebrates.
  • Pooter: suck small insects from a net or tray into a tube.
  • Tullgren funnel: lamp heat and light drive soil and leaf-litter invertebrates down into a collecting tube.
  • Kick-sampling: disturb a stream bed for a set time; the current carries invertebrates into a net held downstream.
  • Calculation: the index of diversity for each site, then compare.
Quick check
?Ecologists want to compare the species diversity of the plant communities in two meadows by calculating Simpson's Diversity Index for each. Which procedure is most appropriate?
Mission 6

One survey is not enough

  • Biodiversity varies from place to place and over time.
  • One survey is a snapshot. It cannot show a decline.
  • Repeat the survey with the same method and effort to show a change in richness or evenness.
  • Richness can stay the same while evenness falls.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A woodland was surveyed for ground beetles in 2000 and again in 2020 using the same pitfall-trap method and the same trapping effort. In 2000, 8 species were caught and the most abundant species made up 20% of the individuals. In 2020, 8 species were caught and the most abundant species made up 75% of the individuals. What do these results show?
Mission check

?Illustrative data. Two hedgerows were sampled for insects with the same method and effort. Each had the same 6 species and 120 insects in total. In hedgerow X, one species made up 100 of the 120 insects. In hedgerow Y, each species had 20 insects. Which statement is correct?

Counting species is only half the story. How evenly the individuals are shared out decides which habitat is more diverse.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Homologous structures share an origin but differ in function; analogous structures share a function but arose separately by convergent evolution.
  • Fewer differences in DNA or protein sequences mean a more recent common ancestor.

Mission 2

  • A species can interbreed to produce fertile offspring; speciation is one species splitting into two once gene flow between populations stops and their gene pools diverge.
  • Isolating mechanisms (geographical, seasonal, behavioural, morphological, hybrid sterility) keep species' gene pools apart.

Mission 3

  • A binomial names the genus (capital) and the species (lower case); together they name one species, used worldwide.
  • Taxa are nested with no overlap, from domain (or kingdom) down to species, and the ideal grouping follows common ancestry.

Mission 4

  • Differences in DNA, mRNA and protein sequences build up after lineages split, so fewer differences mean a more recent common ancestor.

Mission 5

  • Five kingdoms (Prokaryotae, Protoctista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia) rest on observable features; three domains (Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya) rest on rRNA sequences and sit above them.

Mission 6

  • Species diversity = richness (number of species) + evenness (how individuals are spread).
  • 1 − Σ(n/N)²: closer to 1 = more diverse.
