Mission 1

Mission 1: Can burning 1 kg of charcoal really make more than 3 kg of carbon dioxide?

Make a guess: Can burning 1 kg of charcoal really make more than 3 kg of carbon dioxide?

Mission 1

What an equation tells you

  • Atoms are rearranged, never made or destroyed: each element balances.
  • Coefficients give the ratio of amounts in moles, not masses.
  • CH4(g) + 2O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2O(l): 1 mol : 2 mol : 1 mol : 2 mol.
  • A coefficient multiplies the whole formula: 2O2 is four O atoms.
  • Total mass is conserved. Total moles need not be.
Mission 1

Formulae of ionic compounds

  • Charges must total zero: Na⁺ and SO4²⁻ → Na2SO4.
  • Charge from the group: groups 1, 2 → 1+, 2+; Al → 3+; groups 16, 17 → 2−, 1−.
  • Learn the common ions: NO3⁻, CO3²⁻, SO4²⁻, OH⁻, NH4⁺.
  • Also recall Zn²⁺ and Ag⁺.
  • Brackets for two or more of a polyatomic ion: Ca(OH)2, (NH4)2SO4, Al2(SO4)3.
Mission 1

Balancing, with state symbols

  • Write correct formulae first. H2, N2, O2 and Cl2 are molecules.
  • Balance by changing coefficients only, never subscripts.
  • State symbols: (s), (l), (g), (aq). A precipitate is (s); water formed at room temperature is (l); a gas given off is (g).
  • Unfamiliar reaction? Turn the names you're given into formulae, then balance.
  • "Sodium reacts with water to give sodium hydroxide solution and hydrogen": 2Na(s) + 2H2O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H2(g).
Quick check
?Aluminium burns in oxygen to form aluminium oxide. Which equation for this reaction is correctly balanced?
Mission 1

Ionic equations

  • In solution, soluble ionic compounds and strong acids are present as separate ions. Precipitates, water and gases keep their full formulae.
  • Ions unchanged on both sides are spectator ions. Leave them out.
  • Precipitation: Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s).
  • Neutralisation: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H2O(l).
  • Displacement: Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s).
  • Check that atoms and charges balance.
  • Your board calls these the full equation and the ionic equation.
Quick check
?Magnesium ribbon fizzes and dissolves in dilute hydrochloric acid. Which is the ionic equation for the reaction?
Mission 1

Three steps for any reacting quantity

  • Step 1: given quantity → amount in moles.
  • Step 2: × mole ratio (wanted coefficient ÷ given coefficient).
  • Step 3: moles → the quantity asked for.
  • Mass: n = m/M. M is for one formula unit: the coefficient is used only in step 2.
  • Gas: n = V ÷ 24.0 (V in dm³, at RTP) (see Mission 4).
  • Solution: n = c × V (V in dm³).
Mission 1

Worked example: reacting masses

  • CaCO3(s) → CaO(s) + CO2(g). What mass of CaO forms from 25.0 g of CaCO3?
  • Step 1: M(CaCO3) = 40.1 + 12.0 + 3(16.0) = 100.1 g mol⁻¹; n = 25.0 ÷ 100.1 = 0.2498 mol.
  • Step 2: ratio 1 : 1, so n(CaO) = 0.2498 mol.
  • Step 3: m = 0.2498 × 56.1 = 14.0 g.
  • Mass check: the other 11.0 g leaves as CO2.
Quick check
?Iron is produced by reducing iron(III) oxide with carbon monoxide: Fe2O3(s) + 3CO(g) → 2Fe(s) + 3CO2(g). What is the maximum mass of iron that can be produced from 40.0 g of iron(III) oxide? (Ar: O 16.0, Fe 55.8)
Mission check

?A strip of magnesium burns brightly in air and leaves white magnesium oxide: 2Mg(s) + O2(g) → 2MgO(s). What mass of MgO forms from 2.43 g of Mg?
  • (Ar: O 16.0, Mg 24.3)

Same with charcoal. 1.00 kg C is 83 mol; C : CO2 is 1 : 1, so about 3.7 kg of CO2 forms. The extra 2.7 kg is oxygen taken from the air.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Methanal, ethanoic acid and glucose all boil down to CH2O. How can three different substances share one formula?

Make a guess: Methanal, ethanoic acid and glucose all boil down to CH2O. How can three different substances share one formula?

Mission 2

Empirical and molecular formulae

  • Empirical formula: the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound.
  • Molecular formula: the actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule.
  • Molecular formula = (empirical formula)x, x a whole number. C2H4 → CH2; H2O2 → HO.
  • Neither shows which atoms are bonded to which.
Mission 2

Percentage by mass

  • % by mass of an element = (number of its atoms × Ar) ÷ Mr × 100.
  • NH4NO3: Mr = 2(14.0) + 4(1.0) + 3(16.0) = 80.0. % N = 28.0 ÷ 80.0 × 100 = 35.0%.
Quick check
?A compound contains 40.0% carbon, 6.7% hydrogen and 53.3% oxygen by mass.
Its Mr is 60.0. What is its empirical formula?
Mission 2

From percentages to empirical formula

  • Take a 100 g sample: percentages become grams. Divide each by its Ar.
  • Divide every result by the smallest.
  • Scale to whole numbers: × 2 for .50; × 3 for .33 or .67; × 4 for .25 or .75. Never round 1.50.
  • Example: 43.7% P, 56.3% O.
  • P: 43.7 ÷ 31.0 = 1.41. O: 56.3 ÷ 16.0 = 3.52. Ratio 1 : 2.50.
  • × 2 = 2 : 5. Empirical formula P2O5.
Quick check
?A compound of aluminium and sulfur contains 35.93% aluminium and 64.07% sulfur by mass. What is its empirical formula?
  • Data: Ar Al 27.0, S 32.1.
Mission 2

From empirical to molecular formula

  • You also need the Mr of the compound, from another measurement.
  • x = Mr ÷ empirical formula mass.
  • P2O5: 2(31.0) + 5(16.0) = 142.0. Mr = 284.0, so x = 2: P4O10.
  • The hook: methanal CH2O (x = 1), ethanoic acid C2H4O2 (x = 2), glucose C6H12O6 (x = 6). Same ratio, different molecules.
Quick check
?A hydrocarbon contains 88.80% carbon and 11.20% hydrogen by mass. Its Mr is 54.0. What is its molecular formula? (Ar: H 1.0, C 12.0)
Mission 2

Water of crystallisation

  • Hydrated salt: crystals contain water of crystallisation, e.g. MgSO4·7H2O. Anhydrous: no water.
  • Heat a weighed sample in a crucible, cool, reweigh. Repeat until the mass stops changing: heat to constant mass.
  • Example: 2.46 g hydrated MgSO4 → 1.20 g anhydrous. Water lost = 1.26 g.
  • n(MgSO4) = 1.20 ÷ 120.4 = 0.00997 mol. n(H2O) = 1.26 ÷ 18.0 = 0.0700 mol.
  • Ratio 1 : 7.02, so x = 7: MgSO4·7H2O.
  • Not heated to constant mass → water left behind → x too low.
Calculate
?Washing soda is hydrated sodium carbonate, Na2CO3·xH2O. It contains 62.9% water by mass. Find x. (Ar: H 1.0, C 12.0, O 16.0, Na 23.0)
Mission check

?Ethyne, a welding gas, and benzene, a liquid, both contain 92.3% carbon and 7.7% hydrogen by mass. What is the molecular formula of benzene?
  • Data: Ar H 1.0, C 12.0. Mr of benzene = 78.0.

Ethyne is C2H2 and benzene C6H6: one empirical formula, CH, two molecules. Methanal, ethanoic acid and glucose share CH2O the same way: an empirical formula is only a ratio.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why should drivers check tyre pressure before a long drive, not after?

Make a guess: Why should drivers check tyre pressure before a long drive, not after?

Mission 3

Pressure and volume

  • Fixed amount of gas, constant temperature: p ∝ 1/V.
  • Halve the volume: particles hit each bit of wall twice as often, so p doubles.
  • p against V: a falling curve. p against 1/V: a straight line through the origin.
Mission 3

Why kelvin?

  • Constant pressure: V ∝ T. Constant volume: p ∝ T. Only with T in kelvin.
  • T/K = θ/°C + 273.
  • Hotter particles move faster: they hit the walls harder and more often. They don't expand.
  • Against T in K: straight lines through the origin. Against °C: they reach zero at −273 °C (0 K).
  • So 10 °C → 20 °C does not double p: 283 K → 293 K is only 3.5% more.
Quick check
?A fixed mass of an ideal gas is kept at constant pressure. Its volume is measured at several temperatures and plotted against temperature in °C. Which description of the graph is correct?
Mission 3

The ideal gas equation

  • pV = nRT: p in Pa, V in m³, n in mol, T in K.
  • R = 8.31 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ (8.314 on the Data Sheet: either gives the same answers to 3 s.f.).
  • Convert before you substitute:
  • 1 kPa = 10³ Pa · 1 dm³ = 10⁻³ m³ · 1 cm³ = 10⁻⁶ m³.
  • Rearrange first: n = pV/RT · V = nRT/p · p = nRT/V · T = pV/nR.
Mission 3

Worked example: amount of gas

  • 250 cm³ of gas at 98.0 kPa and 22 °C. What amount is present?
  • p = 9.80 × 10⁴ Pa · V = 2.50 × 10⁻⁴ m³ · T = 295 K.
  • n = pV/RT = (9.80 × 10⁴ × 2.50 × 10⁻⁴) ÷ (8.31 × 295)
  • = 24.5 ÷ 2451.5 = 9.99 × 10⁻³ mol.
  • Units check: Pa × m³ = J, so J ÷ (J mol⁻¹) = mol.
Quick check
?A gas syringe contains 2.20 cm³ of an ideal gas at 20 °C and 1.20 × 10⁵ Pa. What amount of gas does it contain? (R = 8.31 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹)
Mission 3

The combined gas law

  • Fixed amount of gas: n and R are constant, so pV/T is constant.
  • p1V1/T1 = p2V2/T2. T in kelvin; p and V in the same units on both sides.
  • Leave out anything that stays constant.
  • 0.0150 m³ at 1.01 × 10⁵ Pa and 290 K goes to 5.05 × 10⁴ Pa and 250 K.
  • V2 = V1 × (p1/p2) × (T2/T1) = 0.0150 × 2 × (250/290) = 0.0259 m³.
  • Sense-check: lower pressure → bigger; cooler → smaller.
Quick check
?A fixed mass of an ideal gas occupies 2.00 × 10⁻³ m³ at 1.00 × 10⁵ Pa and 27 °C. It is compressed to 5.00 × 10⁻⁴ m³ and at the same time its temperature rises to 87 °C. What is its new pressure?
Mission check

?A sealed aerosol can is left in a car on a sunny day.
What happens to the pressure inside the can?
  • At 20 °C the gas inside is at 3.00 × 10⁵ Pa. The can warms to 70 °C; its volume stays constant.

Same with tyres. Driving warms the air from about 15 °C to perhaps 45 °C: 318 ÷ 288 = 1.10, about 10% higher. Check when cold, or a warm tyre reads high and you'll under-inflate it.

Mission 4

Avogadro's law

  • Avogadro's law: equal volumes of gases at the same T and p contain equal numbers of molecules.
  • Why: gas particles are tiny compared with the gaps. Their size doesn't change how many fit.
  • It counts molecules (or atoms, for noble gases), not atoms in molecules.
  • Equal volumes → equal amounts, so masses are in the ratio of molar masses.
Quick check
?Two identical sealed flasks are at the same temperature and pressure. One is filled with methane, CH4, and the other with chlorine, Cl2. The methane flask contains 5.00 × 10²² atoms in total. How many atoms does the chlorine flask contain? (Ar: H 1.0, C 12.0, Cl 35.5)
Mission 4

Reacting gas volumes

  • Same T and p: gas volume ∝ amount in moles.
  • So gases react in the volume ratio of their coefficients.
  • 2CO(g) + O2(g) → 2CO2(g).
  • 40 cm³ CO reacts with 20 cm³ O2 to make 40 cm³ CO2. Total: 60 cm³ in → 40 cm³ out.
  • Only gases count. A liquid or solid has almost no volume.
Quick check
?Sulfur dioxide reacts with oxygen: 2SO2(g) + O2(g) → 2SO3(g). What volume of oxygen reacts completely with 30.0 cm³ of sulfur dioxide? All volumes are measured at the same temperature and pressure.
Mission 4

Molar gas volume

  • Molar gas volume, Vm: the volume occupied by one mole of any gas, at a stated T and p. Units: dm³ mol⁻¹.
  • Room temperature and pressure (RTP): Vm = 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹ (given).
  • n = V ÷ 24.0, with V in dm³. For cm³: n = V ÷ 24 000.
  • Gases only, and only at the stated conditions.
Mission 4

Worked example: mass to gas volume

  • CaCO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g). 0.500 g of CaCO3 reacts with excess acid. What volume of CO2 forms?
  • n(CaCO3) = 0.500 ÷ 100.1 = 4.995 × 10⁻³ mol.
  • Ratio CaCO3 : CO2 = 1 : 1, so n(CO2) = 4.995 × 10⁻³ mol.
  • V = 4.995 × 10⁻³ × 24 000 = 120 cm³ at RTP.
Quick check
?Magnesium reacts with excess dilute hydrochloric acid: Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2(g). What volume of hydrogen, in cm³ at RTP, forms from 0.120 g of magnesium? (Ar: Mg 24.3; molar gas volume 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹ at RTP)
Mission 4

Measuring molar gas volume

  • Weigh a strip of magnesium ribbon (about 0.1 g). Clean off oxide first.
  • React it with excess dilute HCl in a conical flask; collect H2 in a gas syringe (or an inverted burette or measuring cylinder over water).
  • Example results: 0.0950 g Mg → 94.0 cm³ H2 at room conditions.
  • n(H2) = n(Mg) = 0.0950 ÷ 24.3 = 3.91 × 10⁻³ mol.
  • Vm = 0.0940 dm³ ÷ 3.91 × 10⁻³ mol = 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹.
  • Errors: gas escaping before the bung is in; gas syringe sticking; oxide on the ribbon.
Quick check
?In the molar gas volume experiment, a student uses magnesium ribbon with a thin oxide coating that has not been cleaned off. How does this affect the calculated molar gas volume?
Mission check

?Two identical flasks at the same temperature and pressure are filled, one with neon, Ne, and one with sulfur hexafluoride, SF6, a dense gas used in electrical switchgear. The SF6 flask holds 7.2 times the mass of gas. Which statement is correct? (Ar: F 19.0, Ne 20.2, S 32.1)

Same for the balloons. Equal volumes, same T and p: equal numbers of H2 and CO2 molecules. The CO2 balloon is about 22 times heavier.

Mission 5

Limiting and excess

  • The limiting reactant is used up first, and the reaction stops.
  • So it fixes the maximum amount of product.
  • The other reactant is in excess: some is left over.
  • 2CO + O2 → 2CO2, starting with 6 CO and 4 O2: 6 CO2 form, and 1 O2 is left over.
Mission 5

Finding the limiting reactant

  • Convert every reactant to moles. Masses alone tell you nothing.
  • Divide each amount by its coefficient in the equation. The smallest result is the limiting reactant.
  • Calculate all products from the limiting reactant only.
  • Excess left over = amount present − amount that reacted.
Mission 5

Worked example: burning methane

  • CH4(g) + 2O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2O(l), with 8.00 g of CH4 and 24.0 g of O2.
  • n(CH4) = 8.00 ÷ 16.0 = 0.500 mol; ÷ 1 = 0.500.
  • n(O2) = 24.0 ÷ 32.0 = 0.750 mol; ÷ 2 = 0.375. Smaller, so O2 limits.
  • n(CO2) = 0.750 ÷ 2 = 0.375 mol; mass = 0.375 × 44.0 = 16.5 g at most.
  • CH4 left = 0.500 − 0.375 = 0.125 mol = 2.00 g.
  • The smaller mass (methane) was the one in excess.
Quick check
?Nitrogen and hydrogen react: N2(g) + 3H2(g) → 2NH3(g). A mixture contains 5.60 g of N2 and 1.50 g of H2. Which statement is correct? (Aᵣ: H 1.0, N 14.0)
Mission 5

Theoretical and experimental yield

  • Theoretical yield: the maximum mass of product, calculated from the limiting reactant.
  • Experimental (actual) yield: the mass of product actually obtained. It is normally less.
  • Percentage yield = experimental yield ÷ theoretical yield × 100.
  • Why less: incomplete or reversible reaction, side reactions, product lost in transfer and purification.
  • Above 100%? The product is impure, for example still wet.
Quick check
?Which statement describes the theoretical yield of a product in a reaction?
Mission 5

Worked example: percentage yield

  • CuO(s) + H2SO4(aq) → CuSO4(aq) + H2O(l); crystallising gives CuSO4·5H2O.
  • 4.00 g CuO: n = 4.00 ÷ 79.5 = 0.0503 mol. 40.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ acid: n = 0.0400 mol.
  • Ratio 1 : 1, so H2SO4 limits. The excess CuO is filtered off.
  • Theoretical yield: 0.0400 mol × 249.6 g mol⁻¹ = 9.98 g of CuSO4·5H2O.
  • 7.50 g collected: percentage yield = 7.50 ÷ 9.984 × 100 = 75.1%.
Mission 5

Working backwards from a yield

  • To make a target mass at a known yield, find the theoretical mass needed first.
  • Theoretical mass = target ÷ (percentage yield ÷ 100). It is always larger than the target.
  • CaCO3 → CaO + CO2 at 80.0% yield. To make 5.00 g of CaO: theoretical = 5.00 ÷ 0.800 = 6.25 g.
  • n(CaO) = 6.25 ÷ 56.1 = 0.1114 mol = n(CaCO3). Mass = 0.1114 × 100.1 = 11.2 g of CaCO3.
Calculate
?Copper(II) oxide is reduced by hydrogen: CuO(s) + H2(g) → Cu(s) + H2O(g). With hydrogen in excess, 3.98 g of CuO gives 2.85 g of copper. Calculate the percentage yield. (Aᵣ: O 16.0, Cu 63.5)
Mission check

?Thermite is a mixture of aluminium and iron(III) oxide. 2Al(s) + Fe2O3(s) → Al2O3(s) + 2Fe(l). A mixture of 10.0 g of Al and 10.0 g of Fe2O3 reacts, and 5.60 g of iron is collected. What is the percentage yield of iron? (Molar masses: Al 27.0, Fe2O3 159.6, Fe 55.8 g mol⁻¹)

Now the hydrogen. 10 g of H2 is about 5 mol; 10 g of O2 is only about 0.31 mol, which needs just 0.63 mol of H2. Oxygen runs out first, and most of the hydrogen is left over.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Can a reaction with a 100% yield still turn most of its reactants into waste?

Make a guess: Can a reaction with a 100% yield still turn most of its reactants into waste?

Mission 6

Atom economy

  • Atom economy: the percentage of the reactants' total mass that ends up in the desired product.
  • Atom economy = M of desired product ÷ sum of M of all reactants × 100%.
  • Mass is conserved, so the reactant total equals the product total: either gives the same answer.
  • Multiply each M by its coefficient in the balanced equation.
  • It is fixed by the equation. The masses used and the yield do not change it.
Mission 6

Worked example: limestone to lime

  • CaCO3(s) → CaO(s) + CO2(g). Desired product: CaO (lime).
  • M: CaCO3 100.1; CaO 56.1; CO2 44.0.
  • Atom economy = 56.1 ÷ 100.1 × 100 = 56.0%.
  • So 44% of the mass leaves as CO2, even at 100% yield.
  • An addition reaction with a single product has 100% atom economy.
Quick check
?Which statement about atom economy and waste in an industrial process is correct?
Calculate
?In an older industrial process, titanium was made by reducing titanium(IV) chloride with sodium: TiCl4 + 4Na → Ti + 4NaCl. Calculate the atom economy for producing titanium, as a percentage. (Aᵣ: Na 23.0, Cl 35.5, Ti 47.9)
Quick check
?Which reaction has an atom economy of 100% for the product named?
Mission 6

Why high atom economy matters

  • Economic: less raw material bought per tonne of product; less waste to treat or dispose of.
  • Environmental: fewer finite resources used; less waste; often fewer steps and less energy.
  • Ethical / social: resources are kept for future generations; less waste dumped on communities.
  • This is sustainability: meeting today's needs without harming the future's.
  • If a by-product can be sold or reused, the process wastes less than its atom economy suggests.
Mission check

?Hydrogen is manufactured by reacting methane with steam: CH4(g) + H2O(g) → CO(g) + 3H2(g). In one batch, 16.0 kg of methane gave 4.80 kg of hydrogen. What is the atom economy for producing hydrogen by this reaction? (Aᵣ: H 1.0, C 12.0, O 16.0)

Yes. Even at 100% yield, only 17.6% of the reactant mass becomes hydrogen; 82.4% leaves as carbon monoxide. Yield measures losses; atom economy measures by-products.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Coefficients are mole ratios. Balance with coefficients; ionic equations leave out spectator ions; check atoms and charge.
  • Three steps: given → moles; × mole ratio; moles → what's asked.

Mission 2

  • Empirical formula: % → ÷ Ar → ÷ smallest → whole numbers. Molecular formula = (empirical)x, x = Mr ÷ empirical formula mass.
  • Hydrates: heat to constant mass; moles of water ÷ moles of anhydrous salt = x.

Mission 3

  • p ∝ 1/V (T fixed); V ∝ T and p ∝ T, with T in kelvin. Fixed amount: p1V1/T1 = p2V2/T2.
  • pV = nRT in Pa, m³, K. Convert first: cm³ × 10⁻⁶, dm³ × 10⁻³, kPa × 10³.

Mission 4

  • Same T and p: equal gas volumes hold equal numbers of molecules, so gases react in the volume ratio of their coefficients.
  • n = V ÷ 24.0 (dm³) at RTP.

Mission 5

  • Moles ÷ coefficient: the smallest is the limiting reactant. It fixes the theoretical yield.
  • Excess left = amount present − amount reacted.
  • Percentage yield = experimental ÷ theoretical × 100.

Mission 6

  • Atom economy = M(desired product) ÷ total M of reactants (= total M of products) × 100, using coefficients.
  • It is set by the equation: a 100% yield can still waste most of the mass as by-product.
  • High atom economy saves resources, money and waste: a more sustainable process.
