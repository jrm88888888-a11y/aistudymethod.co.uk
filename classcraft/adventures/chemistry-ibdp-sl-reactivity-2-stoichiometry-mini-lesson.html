Mission 1: Can burning 1 kg of charcoal really make more than 3 kg of carbon dioxide?

Make a guess: Can burning 1 kg of charcoal really make more than 3 kg of carbon dioxide?

Mission 1

What an equation tells you

  • Atoms are rearranged, never made or destroyed: each element balances.
  • Coefficients give the ratio of amounts in moles, not masses.
  • CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l): 1 mol : 2 mol : 1 mol : 2 mol.
  • A coefficient multiplies the whole formula: 2O₂ is four O atoms.
  • Total mass is conserved; the total amount in moles need not be.
Mission 1

Deducing an equation

  • Write correct formulae first: from ion charges, with H₂, O₂, N₂ and Cl₂ as molecules.
  • Balance by changing coefficients only, never subscripts.
  • Add state symbols for the reaction conditions: (s), (l), (g), (aq).
  • (aq) = dissolved in water. A precipitate is (s); water formed at room temperature is (l); a gas given off is (g).
  • Sodium in water: 2Na(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H₂(g).

Mission 1

Three steps for any reacting quantity

  • Step 1: given quantity → amount in moles.
  • Step 2: multiply by the mole ratio (wanted coefficient ÷ given coefficient).
  • Step 3: moles → the quantity asked for: mass, gas volume or concentration.
  • For masses, n = m/M, with Ar from the data booklet to two decimal places.
  • M is for one formula unit. The coefficient is used only in step 2.
Mission 1

Worked example: reacting masses

  • CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g). What mass of CaO forms from 25.0 g of CaCO₃?
  • M(CaCO₃) = 40.08 + 12.01 + 3(16.00) = 100.09 g mol⁻¹.
  • n(CaCO₃) = 25.0 ÷ 100.09 = 0.2498 mol.
  • Ratio 1 : 1, so n(CaO) = 0.2498 mol.
  • m(CaO) = 0.2498 × 56.08 = 14.0 g.

Mission 2

Three ways to moles (from Structure 1.4 and 1.5)

  • From a mass: n = m/M.
  • From a solution: n = CV, with V in dm³ (cm³ ÷ 1000).
  • From a gas at STP: n = V ÷ Vm, with Vm = 22.7 dm³ mol⁻¹ (data booklet).
  • From a gas at other conditions: PV = nRT.
  • After the mole ratio, go back the same way.
Mission 2

Worked example: mass to gas volume

  • Zn(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + H₂(g), with the acid in excess.
  • What volume of H₂ at STP forms from 1.30 g of Zn?
  • n(Zn) = 1.30 ÷ 65.38 = 0.01988 mol. Ratio 1 : 1, so n(H₂) = 0.01988 mol.
  • V = 0.01988 × 22.7 = 0.451 dm³ (451 cm³).
  • Only gases have this molar volume; never apply 22.7 to a solid or liquid.
  • Gas to gas at the same T and p: volume ratio = mole ratio, with no Vm step.
Calculate
1Hydrogen peroxide decomposes with a catalyst: 2H₂O₂(aq) → 2H₂O(l) + O₂(g). Calculate the volume of oxygen, in cm³, formed at STP from 1.70 g of H₂O₂. (Ar: H 1.01, O 16.00; Vm = 22.7 dm³ mol⁻¹ at STP)
Quick check
?Nitrogen reacts with hydrogen: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) → 2NH₃(g). What volume of hydrogen reacts completely with 45.0 cm³ of nitrogen? Both volumes are measured at the same temperature and pressure.
Mission 2

Titration

  • A pipette delivers a fixed volume of one solution into a conical flask.
  • A burette adds the other until the indicator changes colour: the end-point.
  • The volume added from the burette is the titre.
  • Repeat until titres are concordant (differ by 0.10 cm³ or less).
  • Use the mean of the concordant titres.
  • One solution is a standard solution: its concentration is known accurately.
Mission 2

Worked example: titration calculation

  • 25.0 cm³ of NaOH(aq) needs a mean titre of 21.40 cm³ of 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ H₂SO₄.
  • 2NaOH(aq) + H₂SO₄(aq) → Na₂SO₄(aq) + 2H₂O(l).
  • n(H₂SO₄) = 0.0500 × 0.02140 = 1.070 × 10⁻³ mol.
  • n(NaOH) = 2 × 1.070 × 10⁻³ = 2.140 × 10⁻³ mol.
  • [NaOH] = 2.140 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.0250 = 0.0856 mol dm⁻³.
Quick check

Calculate
125.0 cm³ of sulfuric acid is neutralised by a mean titre of 18.60 cm³ of 0.120 mol dm⁻³ NaOH: 2NaOH(aq) + H₂SO₄(aq) → Na₂SO₄(aq) + 2H₂O(l). Calculate [H₂SO₄] in mol dm⁻³.
Mission check

?10.0 cm³ of vinegar is titrated with 0.500 mol dm⁻³ NaOH, using an indicator. The mean titre is 16.60 cm³. CH₃COOH(aq) + NaOH(aq) → CH₃COONa(aq) + H₂O(l). What is the concentration of ethanoic acid in the vinegar?

The indicator changes colour at the end-point. With a suitable indicator, this is when exactly enough NaOH has been added to react with all the acid. The titre gives n(NaOH); the mole ratio gives n(acid). That is about 50 g of ethanoic acid per dm³.

Mission 3

Limiting and excess

  • The limiting reactant is used up first, and the reaction stops.
  • It fixes the maximum amount of product.
  • The other reactant is in excess: some is left over.
  • 2CO + O₂ → 2CO₂, starting with 6 CO and 4 O₂: 6 CO₂ form and 1 O₂ is left.
Mission 3

Finding the limiting reactant

  • Convert every reactant to moles. Masses alone tell you nothing.
  • Divide each amount by its coefficient. The smallest result is the limiting reactant.
  • Calculate all products from the limiting reactant only.
  • Excess left = amount present − amount that reacted.
Mission 3

Worked example

  • CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l), with 8.00 g of CH₄ and 24.0 g of O₂.
  • n(CH₄) = 8.00 ÷ 16.05 = 0.4984 mol; ÷ 1 = 0.4984.
  • n(O₂) = 24.0 ÷ 32.00 = 0.750 mol; ÷ 2 = 0.375. Smaller, so O₂ limits.
  • n(CO₂) = 0.750 ÷ 2 = 0.375 mol; m = 0.375 × 44.01 = 16.5 g at most.
  • CH₄ left = 0.4984 − 0.375 = 0.1234 mol = 1.98 g. The smaller mass was in excess.
Quick check
?Nitrogen and hydrogen react: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) → 2NH₃(g). A mixture contains 5.60 g of N₂ and 1.50 g of H₂. Which statement is correct? (Ar: H 1.01, N 14.01)
Mission 3

Theoretical and experimental yield

  • Theoretical yield: the maximum mass of product, calculated from the limiting reactant.
  • It assumes complete reaction, no side reactions and no losses.
  • Experimental yield: the mass of product actually obtained.
  • The experimental yield is normally less than the theoretical yield.

Mission 4

Mission 4: If the equation promises 10 g of product, why do chemists often collect only 7 g?

Make a guess: If the equation promises 10 g of product, why do chemists often collect only 7 g?

Mission 4

Percentage yield

  • Percentage yield = experimental yield ÷ theoretical yield × 100%.
  • Use the same units for both yields, usually grams.
  • Product is lost through incomplete reaction, side reactions, transfer and purification.
  • A catalyst speeds a reaction up; it does not raise the theoretical yield.
  • Above 100% signals an impure product (such as a wet one) or an error.
Mission 4

Worked example: a combined problem

  • CuO(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → CuSO₄(aq) + H₂O(l); crystallizing gives CuSO₄·5H₂O.
  • 4.00 g CuO: n = 4.00 ÷ 79.55 = 0.0503 mol. 40.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ acid: n = 0.0400 mol.
  • Ratio 1 : 1, so H₂SO₄ limits. Excess CuO is filtered off.
  • Theoretical: 0.0400 mol × 249.72 g mol⁻¹ = 9.99 g of CuSO₄·5H₂O.
  • 7.50 g collected: yield = 7.50 ÷ 9.99 × 100 = 75.1%.
Quick check
?Which statement about percentage yield is correct?
Calculate
1Copper(II) oxide is reduced by hydrogen: CuO(s) + H₂(g) → Cu(s) + H₂O(g). With hydrogen in excess, 3.98 g of CuO gives 2.85 g of copper. Calculate the percentage yield. (Ar: O 16.00, Cu 63.55)
Mission 4

Working backwards from a yield

  • To make a target mass at a known yield, first find the theoretical mass needed.
  • Theoretical = target ÷ (percentage yield ÷ 100). It is always larger than the target.
  • Example: 5.00 g of CaO at 80.0% yield needs a theoretical 6.25 g.
  • n(CaO) = 6.25 ÷ 56.08 = 0.1114 mol = n(CaCO₃). Mass = 0.1114 × 100.09 = 11.2 g CaCO₃.
Calculate
1Zinc is extracted from zinc oxide: ZnO(s) + C(s) → Zn(g) + CO(g). The yield of zinc is 90.0%. What mass of ZnO, in g, is needed to make 10.0 g of zinc? (Ar: O 16.00, Zn 65.38)
Mission check

?A student heats 2.70 g of aluminium in 8.51 g of chlorine and collects 7.60 g of aluminium chloride: 2Al(s) + 3Cl₂(g) → 2AlCl₃(s). What is the percentage yield of aluminium chloride? Ar: Al 26.98; Cl 35.45

The equation gives the theoretical yield, 10.67 g here. Real yields are lower: some reactant doesn't react, side reactions make other products, and product is lost in transfer and purification. No mass is destroyed; it just doesn't all end up as the product you weigh.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Can a reaction with a 100% yield still turn most of its reactants into waste?

Make a guess: Can a reaction with a 100% yield still turn most of its reactants into waste?

Mission 5

Atom economy

  • Atom economy = M of desired product ÷ total M of all reactants × 100% (data booklet).
  • Multiply each M by its coefficient in the balanced equation.
  • Every reactant counts, even water.
  • It is fixed by the equation: neither the masses used nor the yield change it.
  • Low atom economy means more reactant mass ends as by-products: more waste.
Mission 5

Worked example

  • CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g), where CaO is the desired product.
  • Atom economy = 56.08 ÷ 100.09 × 100 = 56.0%.
  • 44% of the reactant mass leaves as CO₂, even at 100% yield.
  • An addition reaction with a single product has 100% atom economy.
  • Waste can still come from unreacted reactants, solvents and separation.
Quick check
?Which statement about atom economy and waste in an industrial process is correct?
Calculate
1In an older industrial process, titanium was made by reducing titanium(IV) chloride with sodium: TiCl₄ + 4Na → Ti + 4NaCl. Calculate the atom economy for producing titanium, as a percentage. (Ar: Na 22.99, Cl 35.45, Ti 47.87)
Quick check
?Which reaction has an atom economy of 100% for the product named?
Mission check

?Hydrogen is manufactured by reacting methane with steam: CH₄(g) + H₂O(g) → CO(g) + 3H₂(g). In one batch, 16.0 kg of methane gave 4.80 kg of hydrogen. What is the atom economy for producing hydrogen by this reaction? Ar: H 1.01; C 12.01; O 16.00

Yes. Even at 100% yield, only 17.8% of the reactant mass becomes hydrogen; the other 82.2% leaves as carbon monoxide. Yield measures losses; atom economy measures by-products.

Recap

The big ideas

  • Coefficients give mole ratios. Balance with coefficients only, and add state symbols.
  • Any quantity → moles → mole ratio → moles → the quantity wanted.
  • Titration: n = CV for the known solution, then the mole ratio, then C = n ÷ V.
  • Limiting reactant: divide moles by coefficients; the smallest limits and fixes the theoretical yield.
  • Percentage yield = experimental ÷ theoretical × 100%.
  • Atom economy is set by the equation; the lower it is, the more waste.
