Mission 1: Could right and wrong be worked out like a sum of pleasures and pains?

Make a guess: Could right and wrong be worked out like a sum of pleasures and pains?

Mission 1

Judging by results

  • Teleological (from telos, end or goal): an act is judged by the goal it achieves.
  • Consequentialist: what makes an act right or wrong is its consequences, not the act in itself or the motive.
  • Utilitarianism: the right act is the one with the best consequences for happiness: the most pleasure and least pain overall.
  • Founded in its classic form by Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832), An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (printed 1780, published 1789).
  • Contrast: deontological ethics (Kant, natural law) judge the act itself, by duty or rule.
Mission 1Read the source

Two sovereign masters

Bentham, An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789), chapter I, paragraph 1:

> "Nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure. It is for them alone to point out what we ought to do, as well as to determine what we shall do."

  • Two claims: pleasure and pain determine what we do (a claim about human psychology), and point out what we ought to do (a moral claim).

Q1 Which words make the moral claim?

Show answer

"point out what we ought to do"

Q2 Which words make the psychological claim?

Show answer

"determine what we shall do"

Mission 1

The principle of utility

  • Utility: the tendency of an act to produce pleasure, benefit or happiness, or to prevent pain.
  • Principle of utility: approve an act if it tends to increase the happiness of those affected; disapprove if it tends to decrease it.
  • "The greatest happiness of the greatest number" (A Fragment on Government, 1776): the measure of right and wrong.
  • Hedonism: pleasure is the only thing good in itself; pain the only thing bad in itself. Happiness = pleasure and the absence of pain.
  • Equality: "everybody to count for one, nobody for more than one" (Bentham's rule, as Mill reports it).
  • Quantity only: kinds of pleasure do not differ in value. Bentham: push-pin (a children's game) is as valuable as poetry, if it gives as much pleasure.
Quick check
?Bentham's opening sentence combines two kinds of hedonism. Which pairing is correct?
Mission 1

The hedonic calculus

Bentham's way of measuring the pleasure or pain an act will cause (IPML, chapter IV), seven factors:

  • Intensity: how strong? · Duration: how long?
  • Certainty: how likely? · Propinquity (remoteness): how soon?
  • Fecundity: will it lead to more pleasure? · Purity: is it free from pain afterwards?
  • Extent: how many people are affected?
  • Bentham's own memory verse: "Intense, long, certain, speedy, fruitful, pure— / Such marks in pleasures and in pains endure."
Quick check
?Reading for pleasure tends to make further pleasures possible: new interests, friendships and skills. Which factor of the hedonic calculus does this describe?
Mission 1

Act utilitarianism

  • Act utilitarianism: judge each act, in its own situation, by its consequences. No act is right or wrong in itself.
  • So lying, or breaking a promise, can be right if it produces the greatest balance of pleasure over pain here.
  • Relativist: the right act is relative to the situation and its consequences; only the principle of utility is fixed.
  • Bentham applied it above all to law and government: a law is good if it increases the community's happiness.
Mission 1

Utilitarianism as naturalism

  • Ethical naturalism: moral words refer to natural facts that can be observed.
  • Bentham: "right" means what tends to produce pleasure; "wrong" what tends to produce pain. Both are measurable facts about the world.
  • So moral claims are true or false, like scientific claims, and can be checked by evidence.
  • Strength: it explains morality without needing God or mysterious properties.
  • Weakness: G. E. Moore (Principia Ethica, 1903) argued that "good" cannot be defined as any natural property (the naturalistic fallacy); Hume's is–ought gap.
  • Comparison with Kant, and with religious moral decision-making.
Mission 1Evaluate

Is Bentham's act utilitarianism a good guide?

  • Claim: Bentham's act utilitarianism is a good way to decide what is right.
  • For: Simple and practical; based on something everyone recognises (pleasure and pain); every person counts equally.
  • For: Looks at real consequences; secular, so usable by anyone; inspired real reforms (and includes animals).
  • Against: Can justify injustice: harming an innocent minority if the majority gain enough pleasure.
  • Against: Consequences are hard to predict; pleasures are hard to measure and compare; motives and rights seem to count for nothing.
  • Judgement criteria: Can pleasure be measured? Does justice for individuals matter more than totals? Is pleasure really the only good?
  • Spec: strengths and weaknesses of naturalism; comparison with Kant in Mission 3.
Mission check

?A city council can afford either a big fireworks festival or a free vaccination clinic. Which reasoning is Bentham's act utilitarianism?

Bentham's answer might well be the clinic. But he would reach it by adding up pleasure and pain, not by ranking kinds of pleasure.

Mission 2

Ethics from duty

  • Immanuel Kant (1724–1804), Groundwork of the Metaphysic of Morals (1785).
  • Deontological: from Greek deon, "duty". Right and wrong lie in the act and its motive, not in the consequences.
  • Absolutist: a moral duty holds in every case, for every rational being, whatever the outcome.
  • Morality comes from reason, not from feelings, custom or results. Any rational being can work out what is right.
  • Autonomy: the rational will gives itself the moral law. Obeying a rule only because someone (even God) commands it is heteronomy.
Mission 2

The good will and duty

  • "Nothing can possibly be conceived in the world, or even out of it, which can be called good, without qualification, except a good will." (Groundwork I, Abbott trans. 1873)
  • Talents, courage and even happiness can be used badly. Only a will set on doing right is good in itself.
  • A good will acts from duty: out of respect for the moral law.
  • Kant's shopkeeper does not overcharge a child. He acts in accordance with duty, but if he does it only because honesty is good for business, it has no moral worth.
  • An act done from inclination (because you like doing it) may be "amiable", but its moral worth comes only from the motive of duty.
Quick check
?Four volunteers serve meals at a homeless shelter. According to Kant in the Groundwork, whose action has moral worth?
Mission 2

Two kinds of command

  • A maxim is the personal rule behind an action: "When I need money, I will borrow it and promise to repay."
  • A hypothetical imperative: "If you want X, do Y." "If you want to pass, revise." It binds only those who want X.
  • So it cannot be morality: drop the desire and the command loses its force.
  • A categorical imperative: "Do Y." It commands unconditionally, whatever you want, because the act is good in itself.
  • Morality must be categorical: it binds every rational being, whatever their desires.
Mission 2Read the source

The formula of universal law

  • "Act only on that maxim whereby thou canst at the same time will that it should become a universal law." (Groundwork II, Abbott trans. 1873)
  • Kant's "law of nature" variant: "Act as if the maxim of thy action were to become by thy will a universal law of nature."
  • Contradiction in conception: Kant's lying promise. If everyone made false promises when in need, no one would believe a promise; the maxim destroys itself. Result: a perfect duty (never do this).
  • Contradiction in will: a world where no one helps others can exist, but no rational being could will it, since they will one day need help. Result: an imperfect duty (do this sometimes, as you judge).

Q1 Why can the lying promise not even be thought of as a universal law?

Show answer

Promising would cease to exist, so the lie could not work.

Q2 Is the test about whether universal lying would have bad results?

Show answer

No: it is about whether the maxim contradicts itself.

Mission 2

The formula of the end in itself

  • "So act as to treat humanity, whether in thine own person or in that of any other, in every case as an end withal, never as means only." (Groundwork II, Abbott trans. 1873)
  • Rational beings set their own goals, so they have intrinsic worth.
  • Using a bus driver to get to school is fine: he has agreed and is paid. Lying to someone to get their money is not: they cannot agree to what they do not know.
  • Applies to yourself too: Kant thought suicide and neglect of your own talents fail this test.
  • Say: "never merely as a means" — not: "never as a means". Everyday cooperation uses people as means, with their consent.
Quick check
?Ella asks a friend to help her move house, explaining that she will buy pizza in return; he agrees. Fran pretends to be ill so a classmate will do her homework for her. What would Kant say?
Mission 2Worked answer

Testing a maxim

Task: show how Kant's categorical imperative applies to handing in a friend's essay as your own.

  • Maxim: "When I am short of time, I will submit others' work as mine."
  • Universal law: if everyone did this, no submitted work would be taken as the author's own; the practice of assessment collapses, so the maxim cannot be conceived as law: a perfect duty not to do it.
  • End in itself: the teacher is deceived and used merely as a means to a grade.
  • Link: the act is wrong in itself, whatever the grade, whatever the risk of being caught.
Mission check

?Leo wants to know whether he may lie on a job application. Four classmates explain how Kant's first formulation would settle it. Which explanation is accurate to Kant?

Kant's test is logical, not a forecast: a maxim fails because it cannot be willed for everyone, not because of what happens next. Next: Kant's postulates, his critics and his defenders.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Pleasure or duty: which fits a religious believer's way of deciding what is right?

Make a guess: Pleasure or duty: which fits a religious believer's way of deciding what is right?

Mission 3

Two different questions

  • Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832), An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789). Teleological: an act is right if it produces the greatest balance of pleasure over pain.
  • Immanuel Kant (1724–1804), Groundwork of the Metaphysic of Morals (1785). Deontological: an act is right if its maxim could be a universal law and treats people as ends.
  • Bentham asks: "What will happen?"
  • Kant asks: "What am I willing, and could everyone will it?"
  • Same situation, different question: so they can reach different answers.
Mission 3Read the source

The two openings

  • Bentham: "Nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure. It is for them alone to point out what we ought to do, as well as to determine what we shall do." (Principles, ch. I, §1, 1789)
  • Kant: "Nothing can possibly be conceived in the world, or even out of it, which can be called good, without qualification, except a good will." (Groundwork I, Abbott trans. 1873)

Q1 Where does each thinker locate goodness?

Show answer

Bentham: in pleasure and the absence of pain, an outcome; Kant: in the good will, a motive.

Q2 Which sentence links what we ought to do with what we will in fact do?

Show answer

Bentham's: pleasure and pain both guide and motivate us.

Mission 3

Side by side

  • Source of morality: Bentham: experience of pleasure and pain. Kant: pure practical reason.
  • What is judged: Bentham: consequences. Kant: the maxim and motive.
  • Method: Bentham: the hedonic calculus (intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity, extent). Kant: the categorical imperative (universal law; end in itself).
  • Rules: Bentham: none absolute; act on the case. Kant: perfect duties hold without exception.
  • Feelings: Bentham: pleasure is the good itself. Kant: inclination gives no moral worth.
  • The individual: Bentham: each person's pleasure counts equally in the sum; natural rights are "nonsense upon stilts". Kant: each person has dignity that cannot be traded.
Quick check
?A hospital could save five patients by secretly taking the organs of one healthy visitor. Which statement compares Bentham and Kant correctly?
Mission 3

Common ground

  • Both are Enlightenment theories: morality worked out by human reason, not taken on authority.
  • Both are impartial: no one counts for more than another.
  • Both offer a decision procedure: a test anyone can apply.
  • Both are secular in method: neither needs God to tell us what is right.
  • Bentham rejected the "principle of asceticism" (that self-denial is good in itself) and moral judgements based on mere feeling. Kant rejected morality based on reward, fear or divine command (heteronomy).
  • So a religious believer may agree with their conclusions while disagreeing about where morality comes from.
Mission 3Diversity

How do religious believers decide?

  • Christianity: scripture, Church teaching, reason (natural law), conscience, agape. Catholics stress the Magisterium; many Protestants stress the Bible and conscience; Fletcher's situation ethics makes love the only rule.
  • Islam: the Qur'an and Sunnah, worked out through Shari'ah and fiqh; maslaha (public interest) lets scholars weigh benefits and harms.
  • Judaism: Torah and halakhah; pikuach nefesh: saving a life overrides almost every commandment (Talmud, Yoma 85b).
  • Buddhism: the Precepts; karma depends on intention (cetana); the aim is to reduce suffering.
  • Hinduism: dharma (duty, including one's own duty); the Bhagavad Gita teaches action without attachment to its fruits (2:47, paraphrase).
  • Most traditions combine rules from revelation with reasoning about consequences and a concern for motive.
Mission 3

Kant and religious decision-making

  • Consistent: absolute duties resemble divine commandments ("You shall not steal", Exodus 20:15).
  • Consistent: humanity as an end echoes the image of God (Genesis 1:27); motive matters, as in Jesus' teaching on the heart (Matthew 5:27–28) and Buddhist intention.
  • Consistent: Kant argued (Critique of Practical Reason, 1788) that the moral law tells us to aim at the highest good: virtue rewarded with happiness in proportion. This only makes sense if we "postulate" (assume) freedom, immortality and God: so his postulates give God a place in ethics.
  • Inconsistent: for Kant, obeying a command because God commands it is heteronomy; many believers see obedience to God as the heart of morality.
  • Inconsistent: Kant said Abraham should have doubted a voice commanding him to kill his son (Conflict of the Faculties, 1798): reason judges revelation.
  • Inconsistent: no moral worth in love as a feeling; much religious ethics centres on love and grace.
Mission 3

Bentham and religious decision-making

  • Consistent: concern for the welfare of all echoes love of neighbour, compassion and mercy.
  • Consistent: Fletcher's situation ethics adapts the calculus: love seeks the greatest neighbour-welfare for the most neighbours (his "agapeic calculus").
  • Consistent: maslaha and pikuach nefesh weigh harms and benefits within religious law.
  • Inconsistent: pleasure is not the highest good for most believers: holiness, salvation, nirvana or moksha are.
  • Inconsistent: no act is wrong in itself; most traditions hold some acts always forbidden (e.g. killing the innocent).
  • Inconsistent: Bentham was a critic of religion and wrote against it (under a pseudonym, 1822–23).

Mission 3Evaluate

Which theory is more consistent with religious moral decision-making?

  • Claim: Kant's ethics is more consistent with religious moral decision-making than Bentham's.
  • For: absolute duties, human dignity and motive all have close religious parallels.
  • For: Kant gives God a place (the postulates); Bentham gives none.
  • Against: Kant's autonomy puts reason above revelation (Abraham); Bentham's attention to suffering fits compassion and mercy.
  • Against: many religious traditions weigh consequences: pikuach nefesh, maslaha, situation ethics.
  • Another view: both are equally distant, because both make human reason the final judge; or both are partly consistent, depending on the tradition.
  • Judgement criteria: Which religion or denomination? Consistent in conclusions or in method? Does the source of authority matter more than the answer?
Mission 3Worked answer

Building an evaluation

Issue: How far is Bentham's theory consistent with religious moral decision-making?

  • Point: It is partly consistent: many believers weigh outcomes.
  • Evidence: pikuach nefesh lets almost any commandment be broken to save a life; Fletcher's agapeic calculus.
  • Counter: but Bentham makes pleasure the only good, and no act wrong in itself; most traditions disagree.
  • Judgement: consistent in some methods, inconsistent in its goal; the degree depends on the tradition.
  • AO2 tip: name the tradition you mean; "religious" is too broad for a precise judgement.

Mission check

?A Jewish doctor is told that a patient will die unless she breaks the Shabbat rules to treat him. She treats him, citing pikuach nefesh. How would a careful comparison classify her reasoning?

Religious decision-making often mixes rule, motive and outcome: neither Bentham nor Kant fits it whole.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Utilitarianism is teleological and consequentialist: the right act produces the greatest happiness of the greatest number. For Bentham, pleasure is the only good, measured by quantity.
  • The hedonic calculus: intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity, extent. Act utilitarianism judges each act afresh (relativist).
  • As naturalism: "right" means what causes pleasure, a checkable fact; challenged by Moore and the is–ought gap.

Mission 2

  • Kant: only a good will is good without qualification; moral worth comes from acting from duty, not from inclination or self-interest.
  • Morality is a categorical imperative ("Do Y"), not a hypothetical one ("If you want X, do Y").
  • Formulations: universal law (contradictions in conception and will, perfect and imperfect duties); humanity as an end, never merely a means.

Mission 3

  • Bentham: right = greatest balance of pleasure over pain (consequences). Kant: right = a maxim that could be universal and treats people as ends (duty and motive).
  • Kant fits religious absolutes, dignity and motive, but his autonomy puts reason above revelation; Bentham fits concern for welfare and outcome-weighing, but not pleasure as the highest good.
  • How far each is consistent depends on the tradition and on whether you compare methods or conclusions.
