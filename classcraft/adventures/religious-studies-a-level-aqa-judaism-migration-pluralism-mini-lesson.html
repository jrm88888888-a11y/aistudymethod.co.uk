Mission 1

Mission 1: How have Jews in Britain stayed Jewish for 350 years without either hiding or shutting themselves away?

Make a guess: How have Jews in Britain stayed Jewish for 350 years without either hiding or shutting themselves away?

Mission 1

Diaspora

  • Diaspora (Greek, "scattering"): Jewish communities living outside the Land of Israel.
  • Two Hebrew words: galut ("exile", a punishment and a loss) and tefutzot ("dispersion", a neutral fact).
  • Began with the Babylonian exile (586 BCE); grew after the Romans destroyed the Second Temple (70 CE).
  • Today the two largest Jewish communities are in Israel and the USA; Britain has one of Europe's larger communities.
  • Every diaspora community faces one question: how to belong to the wider society and stay Jewish.
Mission 1Read the source

"Seek the peace of the city"

> "And seek the peace of the city whither I have caused you to be carried away captive, and pray unto the LORD for it; for in the peace thereof shall ye have peace." (Jeremiah 29:7, JPS 1917)

  • Jeremiah's letter to the exiles in Babylon: build houses, plant gardens, raise families there.
  • The Talmud's principle dina de-malkhuta dina ("the law of the kingdom is the law"): Jews obey the civil law of the land they live in, unless it forces them to break the Torah.
  • British synagogues pray each Shabbat for the Royal Family and the country.

Q1 What does Jeremiah tell exiles to do about the city they live in?

Show answer

Pray for it and work for its peace.

Q2 Why?

Show answer

Their own peace depends on the peace of the place where they live.

Mission 1

Migration and Jews in Britain

  • 1070s: Jews come from Normandy after the Norman Conquest.
  • 1190: massacre at Clifford's Tower, York. 1290: Edward I expels all Jews from England.
  • 1656: Jews resettle under Cromwell, after petitions by Menasseh ben Israel; Sephardi Jews build Bevis Marks synagogue (1701), still in use.
  • 18th century: Ashkenazi Jews from Germany and Holland; the Board of Deputies (1760).
  • 1858: Lionel de Rothschild is the first practising Jew to sit in Parliament.
  • 1881–1914: Jews fleeing Russian pogroms settle in London's East End, Manchester, Leeds, Glasgow; the Aliens Act (1905) restricts entry.
  • 1930s: refugees from Nazism; the Kindertransport (1938–39) brings nearly 10,000 children.
  • After 1945: Jews from Iraq, Egypt, Iran, South Africa, Israel and, recently, France.
Mission 1

Diversity today

  • Census 2021: about 271,000 people in England and Wales gave their religion as Jewish (0.5%); many more are Jewish by family but not by religion.
  • Main centres: London (e.g. Barnet, Hackney's Stamford Hill), Greater Manchester, Gateshead, Leeds, Glasgow.
  • Orthodox (central): the United Synagogue (Chief Rabbi of the United Hebrew Congregations of the Commonwealth); the Federation of Synagogues; the Sephardi community.
  • Haredi: e.g. the Union of Orthodox Hebrew Congregations; Hasidic groups in Stamford Hill and Salford; the fastest-growing part of the community.
  • Progressive: Reform and Liberal (recently joined as Progressive Judaism); Masorti.
  • Many Jews are secular or "just Jewish": identity by culture, family and Israel, not synagogue.

Mission 1

Freedom of religion and pluralism

  • European Convention on Human Rights, Article 9: freedom of thought, conscience and religion, including the right to manifest religion in worship, teaching, practice and observance; limits only where "necessary in a democratic society".
  • UK: the Human Rights Act 1998 makes Article 9 enforceable; the Equality Act 2010 protects religion and race.
  • In law, Jews are both a religious and an ethnic group: in the JFS case (2009) the Supreme Court held that admitting pupils by matrilineal descent was discrimination by ethnic origin.
  • Contested freedoms: shechitah (permitted in the UK; some European regions have banned slaughter without stunning) and brit milah.
  • Pluralism: secular states protect many religions equally and favour none (in practice the UK has an established Church of England).
Mission 1

How this context shapes Jewish thought

  • Integration models: Rabbi Jonathan Sacks (1948–2020), Chief Rabbi 1991–2013, The Home We Build Together (2007): society as a home all groups build together, each keeping its own identity (paraphrase).
  • The Dignity of Difference (2002): God is found in human difference, not only in sameness (paraphrase).
  • Enclave models: many Haredi communities limit contact with secular culture to protect Torah life.
  • Pluralistic Judaism: respect between Orthodox, Progressive and secular Jews, and with other faiths (own mission).
  • Freedom also allows assimilation: no law keeps anyone Jewish, so identity must be chosen.
Mission 1Evaluate

Does assimilation mean loss of identity?

  • Claim: For Jews in Britain, assimilation equals loss of Jewish identity.
  • For: intermarriage and dropping Shabbat and kashrut often lead, within a generation or two, to no Jewish practice at all.
  • For: Nehemiah's covenant shows separation as the historic defence of identity.
  • Against: many Jews integrate: full citizens at work and in public life, while keeping Shabbat, kashrut and Jewish schooling.
  • Against: identity can be cultural, ethnic or tied to Israel as well as religious; secular Jews often still identify strongly.
  • Judgement criteria: what counts as "Jewish identity": practice, belief, descent or belonging? Is integration different from assimilation?

Mission 2: Jews are called God's "chosen people". Chosen for what, and what does it mean for everyone else?

Make a guess: Jews are called God's "chosen people". Chosen for what, and what does it mean for everyone else?

Mission 2

What "chosen" means

  • The Torah says God chose (Hebrew bachar) Israel: the descendants of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob.
  • Israel is God's am segulah, God's treasured people (see Exodus 19:5; Deuteronomy 7:6).
  • The choice is rooted in the covenants with Abraham and at Sinai.
  • Before reading the Torah in synagogue, Jews bless God "who has chosen us from all peoples and given us his Torah": chosen for the Torah.
  • Anyone can join: a sincere convert (ger) becomes fully part of the chosen people. Chosenness is not a matter of race.
Mission 2Read the source

Why Israel, and what follows

> "the LORD thy God hath chosen thee to be His own treasure, out of all peoples that are upon the face of the earth." (Deuteronomy 7:6, JPS 1917)

> "The LORD did not set His love upon you, nor choose you, because ye were more in number than any people — for ye were the fewest of all peoples —" (Deuteronomy 7:7, JPS 1917)

> "You only have I known of all the families of the earth; therefore I will visit upon you all your iniquities." (Amos 3:2, JPS 1917)

Q1 According to Deuteronomy 7:7, was Israel chosen for its size or power?

Show answer

No: "the fewest of all peoples"; 7:8 gives God's love and his oath to the patriarchs.

Q2 What does Amos say follows from being "known" by God?

Show answer

Being held to account: "therefore I will visit upon you all your iniquities".

Mission 2

Chosen for a task

  • Obligation more than privilege (as many Jews put it): Jews accept 613 mitzvot; other peoples are not asked to.
  • A kingdom of priests (Exodus 19:6): to serve God and bring others closer to him.
  • "A light of the nations" (Isaiah 42:6; 49:6): to show by example what living by God's will looks like.
  • Kiddush ha-Shem ("sanctifying God's name"): a Jew's good conduct honours God; bad conduct (chillul ha-Shem) shames God's name.
  • Chosenness has brought suffering as well as honour: in the musical Fiddler on the Roof (1964), Tevye asks God to choose someone else once in a while.

Mission 2

Everyone else in God's plan

  • In the Torah God made a covenant with Noah and all humanity first (Genesis 9).
  • The rabbis drew from it seven Noahide laws for all people: no idolatry, blasphemy, murder, theft, sexual immorality or eating flesh from a live animal; and set up courts of justice.
  • "The righteous of the nations have a share in the world to come" (Tosefta Sanhedrin 13:2, paraphrase). Non-Jews do not need to become Jews to have a share in the world to come.
  • So Judaism does not seek converts, though it accepts sincere ones.
  • The prophets imagine all peoples worshipping God: "My house shall be called a house of prayer for all peoples" (Isaiah 56:7, JPS 1917).
  • God cares for other nations too: "Are ye not as the children of the Ethiopians unto Me, O children of Israel?" (Amos 9:7, JPS 1917).

Mission 2Diversity

Chosenness across Jewish movements

  • Orthodox: chosenness is real and lasting; Jews have extra duties. Some thinkers (Judah Halevi, Kuzari, 12th century) spoke of a special spiritual capacity in Israel; others (Maimonides) based it on the Torah alone, open to any convert.
  • Reform: the "mission of Israel": to spread ethical monotheism by example among the nations (classical Reform: Pittsburgh Platform, 1885; Columbus Platform, 1937).
  • Reconstructionist: Mordecai Kaplan (1881–1983) rejected chosenness as implying superiority; Reconstructionist prayer books changed the Torah blessing to "who has drawn us to his service".
  • Humanistic and many secular Jews reject divine choice, but keep a strong sense of shared history and peoplehood.
  • Pluralistic Judaism: recognising different Jewish movements as valid affects how chosenness is understood within Judaism too.
Mission 2

Chosenness and other faiths

  • Dialogue: Jews take part in the Council of Christians and Jews (founded in Britain, 1942) and Jewish–Muslim forums.
  • Jonathan Sacks (1948–2020), The Dignity of Difference (2002), paraphrase: one God made a diverse world; the covenant with Noah is for all; difference between faiths can be a blessing, not a threat.
  • Joseph B. Soloveitchik (1903–1993), "Confrontation" (1964), paraphrase: Jews should cooperate with other faiths on shared moral and social concerns, but not debate the inner truths of each faith, which cannot be translated.
  • Critics of chosenness: Baruch Spinoza (1632–1677), Theological-Political Treatise (1670), argued that Israel's election concerned only its state and prosperity at that time. Others say any idea of chosenness risks seeming exclusive.
  • Antisemites have twisted "chosen" into claims of Jewish superiority or conspiracy; Jewish teachers reply that the idea is about duty, not domination.

Mission 2Evaluate

Does chosenness hinder relations with other faiths?

  • Claim: Belief in being chosen makes good relations with other faiths harder.
  • For: "Chosen" can sound exclusive; outsiders may hear superiority, and it has been misused by enemies of Jews.
  • For: A distinct people with its own laws may limit social mixing (e.g. kashrut, marriage within the faith).
  • Against: Chosenness is for service: "a light of the nations". Noahide teaching gives other peoples a real place with God.
  • Against: Judaism does not seek converts, so it has no reason to compete with other faiths for followers.
  • Against: Sacks and many others argue that a confident identity makes respectful dialogue easier.
  • Judgement criteria: What do Jews themselves mean by "chosen"? Which movement's view is weighed? Is the obstacle the belief or its misuse by others?
Mission check

In Jewish teaching "chosen" points outwards: a responsibility lived among other peoples. A later mission: who is the God who chooses?

Mission 3: Judaism doesn't ask the world to convert. So what does it think about other faiths?

Make a guess: Judaism doesn't ask the world to convert. So what does it think about other faiths?

Mission 3

Pluralistic Judaism

  • Migration made Britain multi-faith: Jews arrived from 1656 (mostly Sephardi), then in large numbers from Eastern Europe (1881–1914) and as refugees from Nazism (1930s).
  • Jewish Britain is itself diverse: Orthodox (United Synagogue; Haredi communities; Sephardi), Masorti, Reform, Liberal, and many secular Jews.
  • Pluralistic Judaism: accepting that these different Jewish movements are each legitimate ways of being Jewish.
  • Implications within Judaism: shared bodies (e.g. the Board of Deputies, the Jewish Leadership Council) work across movements, but most Orthodox authorities do not recognise non-Orthodox rabbis or conversions.
  • Implications beyond Judaism: a community used to living with internal difference may find it easier to respect other faiths.
Mission 3

Inclusivism inside Judaism: Sacks and Persoff

  • Rabbi Jonathan Sacks (1948–2020), Chief Rabbi 1991–2013, One People? (1993): inclusivism: all Jews belong to the one covenant people, even if Orthodox halakhah alone is authoritative (paraphrase).
  • So: love and respect for every Jew, without recognising every movement's theology.
  • Tensions: in 1996 Sacks did not attend the funeral of the Reform rabbi and Auschwitz survivor Hugo Gryn; a leaked letter criticising Gryn caused a public row (1997).
  • Meir Persoff, Another Way, Another Time: Religious Inclusivism and the Sacks Chief Rabbinate (2010): a critical history; he argues inclusivism often gave way to pressure from the religious right, leaving Progressive Jews excluded (paraphrase).
  • Question for evaluation: is inclusivism genuine pluralism, or polite exclusivism?

Mission 3

Three attitudes to other faiths

  • Exclusivism: only one religion is true or leads to God.
  • Inclusivism: one religion holds the fullest truth, but others contain truth and their followers can be "saved" or righteous.
  • Pluralism: many religions are equally valid responses to God.
  • Rabbinic Judaism is often described as unusual: it is exclusive about the Torah's truth for Jews, but inclusive about salvation: the righteous of all nations have a share in the world to come (Tosefta Sanhedrin 13:2).
  • Many Orthodox Jews: inclusivist. Many Reform and Liberal Jews: closer to pluralism. Some Haredi Jews: exclusivist about truth, wary of any religious contact.
Mission 3

The Noahide Code and universal values

  • The rabbis teach seven Noahide laws for all humanity (Sanhedrin 56a): courts of justice; no blasphemy, idolatry, sexual immorality, murder, theft, or eating flesh from a living animal (full study).
  • Non-Jews who keep them are righteous; they need not convert. So Judaism does not seek converts.
  • Maimonides: Islam is strict monotheism; he counted Christian worship as idolatry for Jews (paraphrase). Menachem Meiri (1249–c. 1315): Christians and Muslims are nations "bound by the ways of religion", not idolaters (paraphrase).
Mission 3Read the source

Each in the name of its god

> "For let all the peoples walk each one in the name of its god, but we will walk in the name of the LORD our God for ever and ever." (Micah 4:5, JPS 1917)

> "Have we not all one father? Hath not one God created us?" (Malachi 2:10, JPS 1917)

  • Micah's vision of peace (4:1–4) is followed by this verse about the nations' own ways.

Q1 How could a pluralist read Micah 4:5?

Show answer

The peoples rightly walk in their own ways for now; Israel keeps its own.

Q2 How could an inclusivist or exclusivist read it?

Show answer

It describes the nations' present practice without approving it; Israel alone walks with the LORD.

  • Malachi's verse was originally about Israelites; it is now often quoted in interfaith settings for the shared humanity of all people.

Mission 3Diversity

Interfaith dialogue

  • Reform: the Pittsburgh Platform (1885) saw Christianity and Islam as helping spread monotheism; strong support for dialogue and joint action for tikkun olam.
  • Modern Orthodox: Rabbi Joseph B. Soloveitchik (1903–1993), "Confrontation" (1964): cooperate on shared moral and social concerns, but no debate about the content of faith (paraphrase).
  • Sacks, The Dignity of Difference (2002): one God made a diverse world; difference is a blessing (paraphrase). Some Haredi rabbis objected, and he revised passages in a 2003 edition.
  • Haredi: many avoid interfaith dialogue altogether.
  • Examples: the Council of Christians and Jews (founded 1942); Jewish–Muslim projects; Catholic Nostra Aetate (1965) and the Jewish statement Dabru Emet (2000).
Mission 3

Freedom of religious expression

  • Freedom of religion is a human right in European law: European Convention on Human Rights, Article 9 (1950); UK Human Rights Act 1998. Israel's Declaration of Independence (1948) promises freedom of religion.
  • Jews, long a persecuted minority, strongly support it: for themselves and for others.
  • Live issues: shechitah (kosher slaughter) banned in Denmark (2014) and parts of Belgium (2019); a German court ruled against infant brit milah (Cologne, 2012) before Germany passed a law protecting it; wearing a kippah safely in public.
  • Jewish and Muslim groups often defend shechitah and halal, and circumcision, together.
  • Limits: most Jews support free speech but want protection from incitement and antisemitism.
  • In Israel: debates about the Orthodox Chief Rabbinate's control of marriage and about non-Orthodox prayer at the Western Wall.
Quick check
?Why do many Jewish organisations in Britain campaign alongside Muslim groups on religious slaughter?
Mission 3Evaluate

Can Judaism be truly pluralist?

  • Claim: Judaism can accept other faiths as equally valid without losing its own identity.
  • For: the teaching that the righteous of all nations have a share in the world to come means non-Jews need not become Jews; Micah 4:5 can be read pluralistically.
  • For: Reform and Liberal Judaism practise pluralism and keep a strong Jewish identity.
  • Against: chosenness and Sinai claim a unique revelation (see Mission 2); full pluralism seems to dissolve it.
  • Against: even within Judaism, most Orthodox bodies do not recognise other movements' rabbis or conversions.
  • Persoff: Sacks' inclusivism often gave way to pressure from the religious right.
  • Judgement criteria: pluralism about salvation or about truth? Does respect require equal validity? Which movement?
Mission check

?A Jewish speaker at an interfaith event says: "The Torah is God's unique gift to Israel. But I believe a righteous Muslim or Christian is fully loved by God and needs no conversion." Which attitude does this best show?

Judaism has a long-standing way of being sure of its own revelation while honouring the righteous of every nation. Whether that is pluralism is still debated.

Mission 4

The Shoah

  • Shoah ("catastrophe"): the murder of about six million Jews by Nazi Germany and its collaborators, 1941–45.
  • Centuries of Christian anti-Judaism and modern racial antisemitism prepared the ground.
  • 1933 Hitler in power · 1935 Nuremberg Laws strip Jews of citizenship · 1938 Kristallnacht, a nationwide pogrom (9–10 November) · 1941 mass shootings in the east · 1942 Wannsee Conference coordinates the "Final Solution" · death camps such as Auschwitz-Birkenau and Treblinka.
  • About one third of the world's Jews were killed; whole communities of Eastern Europe vanished.
  • Jews also use churban ("destruction"), the word for the destruction of the Temples.
Mission 4

Why it shakes belief

  • Classical Jewish belief: God is all-powerful, just and acts in history: God brought Israel out of Egypt.
  • The covenant: God chose Israel and promised to protect it; Israel keeps the mitzvot.
  • So where was God at Auschwitz? Four possible answers:
  • Punishment: a traditional prayer says "because of our sins we were exiled" (festival Musaf). A few Haredi leaders applied this to the Holocaust; most Jews find it unbearable.
  • Loss of faith: many survivors stopped believing.
  • God hidden or limited.
  • A purpose within God's plan (Maybaum, later in this mission).
Mission 4Read the source

Covenant: curse and promise

> "But it shall come to pass, if thou wilt not hearken unto the voice of the LORD thy God, … that all these curses shall come upon thee, and overtake thee." (Deuteronomy 28:15, JPS 1917)

> "And yet for all that, when they are in the land of their enemies, I will not reject them, neither will I abhor them, to destroy them utterly, and to break My covenant with them" (Leviticus 26:44, JPS 1917)

Q1 How does Deuteronomy 28:15 explain suffering?

Show answer

As punishment for breaking the covenant.

Q2 What does Leviticus 26:44 promise even in exile?

Show answer

God will not destroy Israel or break the covenant.

  • Rubenstein rejects the first idea as applied to Auschwitz; Maybaum keeps the second, but not as punishment.

Mission 4

Rubenstein: the "death of God"

  • Richard L. Rubenstein (1924–2021), American rabbi, After Auschwitz (1966; 2nd edition 1992).
  • In 1961 a German Protestant dean, Heinrich Grüber, told him the Holocaust was part of God's will for the Jews. Rubenstein concluded: if God controls history, then Hitler was God's instrument, and he could not worship such a God.
  • So the God of history who chose Israel and acts in events is "dead" (paraphrase): belief in that God is no longer possible.
  • The covenant and chosenness die with it: being "chosen" made Jews a target.
  • "Death of God" describes a cultural event in human belief, not a claim that God once lived and then died.
  • Yet he kept Jewish community, ritual and festivals: they help people face life and death together.
  • He later spoke of God as the Holy Nothingness: the ground of being, not a God who intervenes (paraphrase).

Mission 4

Maybaum: the third churban

  • Ignaz Maybaum (1897–1976), Reform rabbi from Vienna who led communities in Germany, came to London in 1939; his mother and two sisters were murdered. The Face of God after Auschwitz (1965).
  • Churban: a destruction that ends an old era and makes way for a new one.
  • 1st churban (586 BCE): Temple destroyed; exile spread Jews and their God among the nations.
  • 2nd churban (70 CE): sacrifice ended; the synagogue and rabbinic Judaism grew.
  • 3rd churban: the Holocaust ended the medieval world of East European Jewry and moved Jews into the modern world.
  • God's providential plan: God is still Lord of history, bringing progress through catastrophe.
  • AQA spells his name Ignatz.
Mission 4Read the source

The suffering servant

> "Surely our diseases he did bear, and our pains he carried; whereas we did esteem him stricken, smitten of God, and afflicted. But he was wounded because of our transgressions, he was crushed because of our iniquities" (Isaiah 53:4–5, JPS 1917)

  • Jewish tradition (e.g. Rashi) reads the servant as the people of Israel, suffering among the nations; Christians read it as Jesus.
  • Maybaum: Israel is the suffering servant; the six million died as an innocent sacrifice (vicarious atonement), not for their own sins.
  • Like the crucifixion for Christians, he said, Auschwitz should shock the Western world into turning from evil (paraphrase).

Q1 In verse 5, whose wrongdoing causes the servant's suffering?

Show answer

Others': "our transgressions".

Q2 Why does this suit Maybaum but not the punishment view?

Show answer

The servant is innocent; he suffers for others, not for his own sins.

Mission 4

"Hitler, my servant" and the remnant

> "Nebuchadrezzar the king of Babylon, My servant" (Jeremiah 25:9, JPS 1917)

  • Role of Hitler: as God used Babylon, Maybaum said, God used Hitler as an instrument, without making him any less guilty (paraphrase).
  • Not punishment: Maybaum explicitly rejects the idea that the victims were punished for sin.

> "A remnant shall return, even the remnant of Jacob, unto God the Mighty." (Isaiah 10:21, JPS 1917)

  • Remnant: the survivors, especially Jews in the West, carry Judaism into the new era.
  • Critics: making God use genocide for "progress" seems monstrous; the Christian imagery troubles many Jews; Rubenstein said the God of history could not be worshipped.

Mission 4Evaluate

Maybaum or Rubenstein?

  • Claim: Rubenstein's response takes the Holocaust more seriously than Maybaum's.
  • For: Rubenstein refuses to make Auschwitz part of God's plan; he faces the evidence.
  • For: Maybaum's "Hitler, my servant" and sacrifice for progress seem to justify the unjustifiable.
  • Against: Maybaum keeps the covenant and gives survivors hope and purpose, as the prophets did after 586 BCE.
  • Against: Rubenstein lets the Holocaust destroy Judaism's core belief; critics call this a posthumous victory for Hitler.
  • Judgement criteria: faithful to the victims? to Jewish tradition? morally acceptable? does it help Jews live after it?
Mission 4Worked answer

Comparing two responses

Question: "Compare Maybaum's and Rubenstein's responses to the Holocaust."

  • Shared starting point: both accept that the traditional God of history is challenged by Auschwitz.
  • Difference 1 (God): Rubenstein: the God of history is "dead"; God as Holy Nothingness. Maybaum: God still directs history (Jeremiah 25:9).
  • Difference 2 (covenant): Rubenstein: the covenant and chosenness end. Maybaum: Israel remains God's servant (Isaiah 53).
  • Difference 3 (meaning): Rubenstein: no purpose. Maybaum: churban as painful progress; the remnant goes on.
  • Skill tip: in a comparison, write each point as a pair ("whereas…") rather than two separate summaries.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The diaspora began with the Babylonian exile and grew after 70 CE; a portable Judaism of Torah, prayer and synagogue let Jews live anywhere (Jeremiah 29:7; dina de-malkhuta dina).
  • Jews in Britain: medieval community, expulsion (1290), resettlement (1656), migration waves; diverse today (Orthodox, Haredi, Progressive, secular); freedom of religion under Article 9 and the Equality Act.

Mission 2

  • Israel was chosen out of God's love and covenant, not for size or merit (Deuteronomy 7:6–8); chosenness brings obligations (Amos 3:2) and the task of being "a light of the nations".
  • Non-Jews have their own covenant through Noah: the righteous of all nations have a share in the world to come, so Judaism does not seek converts.
  • Views differ across Orthodox, Reform, Reconstructionist and secular Jews; they shape interfaith relations (Sacks, Soloveitchik).

Mission 3

  • Judaism usually combines unique truth (the Torah for Israel) with open salvation (the righteous of the nations, through the Noahide laws).
  • Orthodox views range from Haredi caution to Soloveitchik's limited cooperation and Sacks' "dignity of difference"; Reform and Liberal Jews lean to pluralism.
  • Pluralistic Judaism and freedom of religious expression: internal diversity, Sacks' inclusivism and its critics, and shared campaigns for religious freedom in a secular society.

Mission 4

  • The Shoah challenges belief in a just, all-powerful God who acts in history and keeps covenant with Israel.
  • Rubenstein (After Auschwitz, 1966): the God of history and the covenant are "dead"; God as Holy Nothingness; Jewish community still matters.
  • Maybaum (The Face of God after Auschwitz, 1965): the third churban; Hitler as God's instrument; Israel as suffering servant (vicarious atonement); the remnant carries Judaism into a new age.
