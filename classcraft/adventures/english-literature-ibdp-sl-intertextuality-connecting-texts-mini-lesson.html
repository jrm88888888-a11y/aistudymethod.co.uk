IB areas of exploration · intertextualityScreen 1

Ways texts talk to each other

  • Allusion and quotation: a text names or echoes another.
  • Epigraph: a borrowed line placed before a text, framing how we read it.
  • Rewriting and parody: a new text retells or mocks an old one.
  • Conventions: every text follows or breaks the habits of its form or genre.
  • The area asks how texts follow and break conventions and how comparing them can change our reading.

IB areas of exploration · intertextualityScreen 4

Following and breaking conventions

  • Renaissance love sonnets, after Petrarch, praised a beloved with fixed comparisons: eyes like the sun, lips like coral, cheeks like roses.
  • Shakespeare's Sonnet 130 uses that set of comparisons to refuse it, one by one.
  • Its final couplet turns the joke into a claim about true love.
  • Intertextuality here is a conversation with a whole tradition, not one source.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Every story borrows from an older one. Is that theft or conversation?

Conversation. Writers borrow to answer: Shelley gives her creature Adam's question to God, and Shakespeare quotes the love-poem tradition in order to refuse it. In an IB essay, the connection earns credit when you show what the new text does with the old.

Mission 2

Seven concepts: how do you actually use one in an essay?

IB concepts · using a conceptScreen 1

Seven concepts, seven questions

ConceptA question it helps you ask
IdentityHow does the text build or challenge who someone is?
CultureHow does it show or question shared values and customs?
CreativityHow does the writer make something new?
CommunicationHow does meaning pass between writer, text and reader?
PerspectiveWhose point of view shapes what we see?
TransformationWhat changes: a character, a reader, an older text?
RepresentationHow does the text depict people, groups or ideas?
IB concepts · using a conceptScreen 2

A lens, not a topic

  • A topic says what: Jane's independence in Jane Eyre.
  • A concept asks how and why: through identity, how does Brontë's first-person narration let Jane claim an identity others deny her?
  • Use one concept as the thread of an argument; don't tour all seven.

Where this counts

  • No criterion marks a concept: concepts help you build an argument.
  • Paper 2 (25 marks): its four general questions may not name a concept, but many turn on ideas like representation or perspective; one concept can link your two works.
  • Individual Oral: a concept can sharpen how both texts present your global issue.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Seven concepts: how do you actually use one in an essay?

Use it as a lens. Pick the one concept that best opens your text, turn it into a how-question (how does Brontë's narration build Jane's identity?), and let it guide which methods and evidence you analyse. A concept named but not used earns nothing.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Intertextuality covers allusion, quotation, epigraphs, rewriting, parody and the conventions of a form.
  • A connection earns credit when you say what the borrowing changes in how we read.
  • Paper 2 rewards comparison.
  • The IO links an original work and a work in translation.
  • The seven concepts: identity, culture, creativity, communication, perspective, transformation, representation.
  • Use one as a lens: it turns a topic into a how-question and an arguable thesis.
  • No criterion marks a concept; it helps Paper 2 and the IO.

Key quotations to learn

  • Did I request thee, Maker, from my clay / To mould me man? (Frankenstein (1818), epigraph; Paradise Lost X) Adam's protest becomes the creature's question.
  • Like Adam, I was created apparently united by no link to any other being (Frankenstein, Vol. II Ch. VII) The creature reads himself through Milton.
  • Satan as the fitter emblem of my condition (Frankenstein, Vol. II Ch. VII) He turns the comparison: wronged, and dangerous.
  • My mistress’ eyes are nothing like the sun (Sonnet 130, line 1) A convention refused in the first line.
  • As any she belied with false compare (Sonnet 130, line 14) Names the tradition being answered.
  • I am no bird; and no net ensnares me (Jane Eyre, Ch. 23) Identity: Jane rejects Rochester's image of her.
  • I am a free human being with an independent will (Jane Eyre, Ch. 23) She names the identity she claims.
  • because I am poor, obscure, plain, and little (Jane Eyre, Ch. 23) Representation: the labels others use for her.
  • I have as much soul as you (Jane Eyre, Ch. 23) Equality claimed across class.
