Mission 1

Mission 1: If the last of a species survives safely in a zoo, has that species really been saved?

Make a guess: If the last of a species survives safely in a zoo, has that species really been saved?

Mission 1

Why conserve biodiversity?

  • Ecological: species depend on each other. Losing a keystone species changes the whole community.
  • Genetic resource: wild relatives of crops carry alleles for disease resistance and drought tolerance.
  • Economic: food, timber, medicines, tourism; healthy soils (continuous monoculture depletes them).
  • Aesthetic: landscapes and wildlife people value and enjoy.
Mission 1

In situ conservation

  • In situ: protecting a species in its natural habitat.
  • Nature reserves and wildlife reserves.
  • Reserves need active management: control invasive species and poaching; keep habitats suitable.
  • Many habitats are held at one stage of succession: grazing, mowing, coppicing or controlled burning stop heathland or grassland turning into woodland.
  • Advantage: the species keeps its habitat, its interactions and its natural selection pressures.
  • The alternative is ex situ: keeping a species outside its natural habitat, in zoos, botanic gardens or seed banks. It can keep the species alive, but not its habitat or the species it depends on.
Mission 1

Conservation versus human needs

  • People need land for food, homes and income. Conservation that ignores this rarely lasts.
  • Compromises: sustainable timber and fishing quotas; payments to farmers; ecotourism income for local people.
Mission 1

Evaluating conservation evidence

  • Before accepting a claim that a conservation measure works, ask:
  • Is there a comparison: before and after, or treated and untreated sites?
  • Were sites similar at the start, and sampled by the same method and effort?
  • How large are the samples? Are differences bigger than the variation within groups?
  • A correlation does not show the measure caused the change.
  • Evidence can conflict: weigh the size, design and source of each study.

?A rare orchid survives in one wild meadow. Its seeds are now stored in a seed bank, and plants grow in a botanic garden. The meadow is due to be ploughed. Orchid seeds need a particular soil fungus to germinate. Which conclusion is best supported?

Mission 2

Mission 2: How can people keep taking food and materials from nature without wrecking it?

Make a guess: How can people keep taking food and materials from nature without wrecking it?

Mission 2

Take less than grows back

  • Sustainable: meets today's needs without reducing what future generations can take.
  • Harvesting is sustainable only if the rate of harvesting is below the rate of replacement.
  • Net replacement = growth + new young (recruitment) − natural deaths.
  • Renewable does not mean it cannot run out.
Quick check
?A stock of Atlantic cod has a biomass of 200 000 tonnes. Each year, growth of the fish and recruitment of young fish add 15% to the biomass, while natural mortality removes 5% of it. What is the annual rate of replacement of the stock, below which the catch must stay for harvesting to be sustainable?
Mission 2

Fishing without emptying the sea

  • Quotas: a maximum catch, set from estimates of the stock.
  • Minimum mesh size: young fish escape and live to breed.
  • Closed seasons and no-take zones: protect spawning fish and nursery areas.
  • Limits on fleet size or days at sea.
  • Warning signs: more effort for the same catch; the fish caught get younger and smaller.
  • Real case: Newfoundland's northern cod. Catches peaked at over 800 000 tonnes in 1968. By 1992 the spawning stock had fallen by about 99%, and the fishery was closed.
Quick checkRead the data
?Illustrative data.
Records for a cod fishery show that the annual catch stayed close to 250 000 tonnes for 20 years. Over the same period the number of fishing days needed to land that catch doubled, and the mean age of the cod caught fell from 8 years to 4 years. What do these data indicate?
Mission 2

Timber and forest products

  • Coppicing: cut a tree near the ground; many new stems regrow; cut again years later.
  • Rotational felling: fell one area at a time; replant; return only when it has regrown.
  • Selective felling: take only some mature trees; the forest structure and habitats remain.
  • Large-scale clear-felling exposes soil to erosion and leaching and destroys habitats.
Mission 2

Farming for yield

  • Simplify food webs: pesticides, herbicides and fungicides cut losses to pests, weeds and disease.
  • Reduce respiratory losses: animals kept indoors, warm, with movement restricted, so less energy is used in respiration and more becomes meat.
  • Higher yields from less land, but at a cost: fewer habitats, fewer species, polluted water.
  • Balance: keep hedgerows and field margins; agri-environment schemes pay farmers to manage land for wildlife.
Mission check

?Illustrative data.
A seaweed bed with a biomass of 5000 tonnes is harvested for food. Each year, growth adds 30% to the biomass and natural losses remove 10%. The current harvest is 1400 tonnes per year. Which conclusion is correct?

Fish, timber or seaweed: take no more than the net amount that grows back, and nature can keep supplying.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Two sets of results share the same average but look nothing alike. What number captures the difference?

Mission 3

Three averages and a range

  • Mean = sum of values ÷ number of values.
  • Median = the middle value when the values are in order (for an even number, halfway between the middle two).
  • Mode = the most frequent value.
  • Range = largest − smallest.
  • One extreme value pulls the mean towards it. The median hardly moves.
Quick check
?The heights in centimetres of nine students are: 150, 152, 152, 155, 158, 160, 163, 170 and 175. What is the median height?
Mission 3

Standard deviation: the typical spread

  • Standard deviation (SD): how far values typically lie from the mean.
  • Small SD: values cluster tightly. Large SD: values are widely spread.
  • Two samples can share a mean and differ hugely in SD: the hook.
  • For data with a normal (bell-shaped) distribution, about 68% of values lie within ±1 SD of the mean, and about 95% within ±2 SD.
  • SD has the same unit as the data.
  • A large SD is not a "mistake". In biology it often reflects real variation between individuals.
Mission 3

Worked example: calculating SD

Stomata counted in five fields of view on one leaf: 22, 26, 19, 27, 21.

  • Mean, x̄ = 115 ÷ 5 = 23.
  • Σ(x − x̄)² = 46.
  • SD, s = √[Σ(x − x̄)² ÷ (n − 1)] = √(46 ÷ 4) = √11.5 = 3.4.
  • Divide by n − 1 (here 4), not n: this estimates the SD of the whole population from a sample.
Quick check
?Limpets were counted in twenty random quadrats on each of two shores. On both shores the mean was 8 limpets per quadrat. The standard deviation was 1.2 on shore A and 6.5 on shore B. What can be concluded?
Mission 3

SD or range?

  • The range uses only two values: the largest and the smallest.
  • One outlying result sets the range on its own.
  • The SD uses every value, so it describes the spread of the whole data set.
  • So SD is usually the more useful measure of dispersion, especially where there is an outlying result.
  • Still check an outlier: is it an anomaly (a mistake) or real variation?
  • Say: "SD shows the spread of the data about the mean" — not: "SD shows how many errors were made".
Quick check
?Two samples of seven leaf lengths, in mm:
Both have a mean of 20 mm and a range of 20 mm. The standard deviation of P is 5.8 mm and of Q is 10.0 mm. Which statement is correct?
  • P: 10, 20, 20, 20, 20, 20, 30
  • Q: 10, 10, 10, 20, 30, 30, 30
Mission 3

Random samples and replicates

  • A mean is only as good as the sample behind it.
  • Random sampling: place quadrats at random coordinates (random number tables or a generator), never by choice.
  • Replicates: count stomata in several fields of view, not one field several times.
  • Biological material varies, so replicate counts differ. That spread is information, not failure.
  • Report the mean ± SD.
Quick check
?Three students estimated the stomatal density of leaves from the same plant. Student A counted one field of view at high power and reported 90 per mm². Student B counted ten different fields, obtained values between 60 and 130 per mm², and reported the mean, 95 per mm². Student C counted the same single field as A five times, getting 90 per mm² each time. Which evaluation of these results is best?
Mission check

?The lengths of five seedling roots, in mm, are 14, 16, 15, 18 and 17. Which gives the mean and standard deviation correctly?

Same mean, different spread: the SD is the number that tells two such data sets apart.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Two bars on a graph have different heights. When does that difference actually mean something?

Make a guess: Two bars on a graph have different heights. When does that difference actually mean something?

Mission 4

What an error bar can show

  • A bar or point shows a mean. The error bar shows uncertainty or spread.
  • Error bars can show different things. Read the legend first.
  • ±1 SD: the spread of the individual values.
  • Bars from the range (min to max) are sometimes drawn too. They depend on only two values.
Mission 4

Reading SD bars

  • Compare two means, each with ±1 SD bars.
  • Bars overlap widely: many individuals in the two groups are alike. The difference in means may well be due to chance.
  • Bars do not overlap: the difference may be real.
  • SD bars ignore sample size, so they cannot settle it. A statistical test (e.g. a t-test) decides.
  • A small SD means repeats agree closely: a more precise, more repeatable measurement.
Quick check
?Five potato cylinders were bathed in 0.4 mol dm⁻³ sucrose for 24 hours. The mean percentage change in mass was −3.0% with a standard deviation of 1.0%; the mean percentage change in length was −2.0% with a standard deviation of 2.5%. What do these data indicate?
Mission check

?A student grew bean seedlings with fertiliser X or fertiliser Y, 10 plants each. Mean root length ± 1 standard deviation: X, 42 mm (bar from 33 to 51 mm); Y, 47 mm (bar from 39 to 55 mm). Which conclusion is best supported?

Bar heights alone mean little. The spread around each mean decides whether a difference could just be chance.

Mission 5

Genes plus surroundings

  • Genotype: the alleles an organism has.
  • Phenotype: its observable traits.
  • Phenotype = genotype interacting with the environment.
  • Same genotype, different surroundings → different phenotypes. That's the cuttings.
Quick check
?Identical twins have the same genotype. Two identical twins were raised apart, one in a sunny country on a good diet and the other in a cloudy country on a poor diet. Which of these traits are expected to be the same in both twins as adults: ABO blood group, adult height and skin colour?
Mission 5

Where variation comes from

  • Genetic sources: mutation (the primary source of new alleles), then meiosis and random fertilisation (new combinations).
  • Environmental sources: in animals, diet; in plants, light and minerals.
  • So a population varies widely: many allele combinations × many environments.
Quick check
?Seeds of one genetically uniform variety of bean were sown in two trays. Tray X was kept in darkness; tray Y in light. After two weeks, the seedlings in X had long, thin, pale stems and small yellow leaves. The seedlings in Y were short and green. Which statement is correct?

Mission 5

Many genes, one smooth curve

  • Polygenic inheritance: many genes, at different loci, affect one trait.
  • Worked example: three genes (A/a, B/b, C/c). Each capital allele adds one unit of height.
  • Cross AaBbCc × AaBbCc. Offspring carry 0–6 capital alleles.
  • Step 1: one gene alone → 3 classes, 1 : 2 : 1.
  • Step 2: two genes → 5 classes, 1 : 4 : 6 : 4 : 1.
  • Step 3: three genes → 7 classes, 1 : 6 : 15 : 20 : 15 : 6 : 1 (out of 64).
  • Step 4: add the environment. Each class spreads out; the gaps fill in → a smooth bell curve.
  • More genes → more classes → closer to continuous.
Quick check
?Human skin colour varies continuously across a population, whereas ABO blood group falls into four separate classes. Which explanation of this difference is correct?
Mission 5Practical

Investigating continuous variation

  • Question: do leaves in shade differ in length from leaves in sun, on the same kind of plant?
  • Take random samples: e.g. pick plants and leaves using random numbers, not by eye.
  • Measure at least 20 leaves per sample (e.g. length, mm, with a ruler).
  • Calculate the mean and standard deviation of each sample.
  • Control: same species, leaves of the same age (e.g. the 4th leaf from the tip).
Mission 5

Twin studies

  • Monozygotic (identical) twins come from one zygote: same genotype.
  • So differences between them arise mainly from the environment.
  • To weigh genes against environment: compare how often both twins share a trait in identical twins (same genotype) and in non-identical twins (on average, half of their variable alleles shared).
  • Much higher agreement in identical twins → a large genetic influence. Similar agreement → mainly environmental.
Quick check
?In a study, the DNA methylation patterns of pairs of monozygotic twins were compared. Three-year-old twins had almost identical patterns. Fifty-year-old twins showed large differences, and the differences were greatest in pairs who had spent the most years living apart. What is the best interpretation?
Mission check

?Two true-breeding varieties of a crop plant differ in seed mass. Crossing them gives F1 plants with seeds of intermediate mass, all very similar. The F1 are interbred. Grown in the same field as the F1, the F2 show a wide, bell-shaped spread of seed mass, with very few at either parental extreme. Which explanation fits best?

Many genes plus the environment: that is why genetically identical cuttings still differ, and why a whole population varies even more.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • In situ conservation keeps species and their interactions in the wild; ex situ (zoos, botanic gardens, seed and tissue banks) is insurance. No single approach is enough.

Mission 2

  • Harvesting is sustainable only if it stays below net replacement (growth + recruitment − natural deaths).

Mission 3

  • Mean, median and mode describe the centre; SD describes the typical spread about the mean, using every value.
  • SD is usually more useful than the range, which depends only on the two extreme values.

Mission 4

  • Read the legend: SD bars show the spread of individuals; SE and confidence bars show how precisely each mean is known.
  • Overlapping SD bars: the difference may be due to chance; a statistical test decides.

Mission 5

  • Phenotype = genotype interacting with the environment; genetically identical organisms still differ.
  • Continuous variation comes from many genes with additive effects plus the environment; discontinuous variation from one or a few genes.
