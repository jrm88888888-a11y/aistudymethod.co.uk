Mission 1

What globalisation means

  • Globalisation: the growing integration and interdependence of the world's economies.
  • Its main characteristics:
    • more trade in goods and services, faster than world output grows
    • more capital flows across borders: foreign direct investment (FDI) and portfolio investment
    • more movement of people: migration for work and study
    • global supply chains: one product made in stages in many countries
    • the spread of multinational corporations (MNCs), technology and ideas
  • Interdependence cuts both ways: shocks in one economy now spread quickly to others.

Mission 1

Why globalisation sped up

  • Trade liberalisation: tariffs cut through GATT rounds (from 1947) and the WTO (from 1995); more trading blocs and agreements.
  • New economies joined world markets: China's reforms (from 1978) and WTO entry (2001); the former USSR and Eastern Europe after 1991. Hundreds of millions more workers became part of world trade.
  • Lower transport costs: containers, larger ships, cheaper air freight.
  • Lower communication costs: the internet and mobile networks let a firm run a supply chain across continents.
  • Capital market liberalisation: exchange controls removed, so money moves freely; global financial markets.
  • Growth of MNCs: firms spreading production and sales across many countries.
Mission 1

Supply chains, capital and people

  • A global supply chain: design in one country, parts from several, assembly in another, sales worldwide.
  • Much trade is now in parts and components, not finished goods.
  • Capital flows: firms build or buy factories abroad (FDI); investors buy foreign shares and bonds (portfolio).
  • Labour mobility: migrants fill skill gaps and send home remittances; students and skilled workers move between countries.
  • Result: specialisation by country and by stage of production.
  • More competition from abroad makes international competitiveness (cost, quality, productivity) vital for firms and countries.
Quick check
?A clothing firm designs in Sweden, buys cotton from India and has its garments sewn in Bangladesh. It tracks every order online. Which development made this pattern most practical?
Mission 1

Consequences for developed economies

Possible gains:

  • Cheaper imports: higher real incomes; lower inflation.
  • Bigger export markets, especially for high-value services.
  • Access to foreign capital and skills.

Possible costs:

  • Deindustrialisation: import-competing manufacturing shrinks.
  • Structural unemployment in regions that relied on those industries; pressure on low-skill wages; wider inequality.
  • Real case: US local areas more exposed to Chinese imports saw larger falls in manufacturing jobs in the 1990s and 2000s (Autor, Dorn and Hanson, 2013).
  • Exposure to global shocks, and pressure to compete on tax and regulation.
Mission 1

Consequences for emerging and developing economies

  • Emerging economies (e.g. China, India, Vietnam) grew fast through manufacturing exports and inward FDI.

Possible gains:

  • Export-led growth, jobs, new technology and skills from MNCs.
  • World poverty: the share of people living on under $3.00 a day (2021 prices, PPP) fell from 43.4% in 1990 to 10.1% in 2024.
  • That fall happened while trade grew; how much trade caused it is debated.

Possible costs:

  • Dependence on a few primary commodities with volatile prices.
  • Poor pay and working conditions; weak environmental rules.
  • Widening inequality inside countries; some low-income countries are left out.
  • Brain drain: skilled workers emigrate.

  • A world commodity price surge raises firms' costs → SRAS falls → higher prices, lower output; importers' current account worsens.

Mission 1

Evaluate: does globalisation benefit a country?

It depends on…

  • Which group. Consumers and exporters gain; workers in import-competing industries may lose unless they retrain, move or are compensated.
  • What the country sells. Diversified manufactures and services are less exposed than one or two primary commodities.
  • Institutions. Strong education, infrastructure and rules on pay and pollution help a country capture gains and limit harm.
  • Time. Job losses come fast; gains in productivity and real incomes build up slowly.
  • Direction of travel. World trade relative to GDP has grown little since 2008 (60.3% then, 56.7% in 2024): some call this "slowbalisation".
  • Say: "globalisation increases interdependence" — not: "globalisation means everyone is better off".
Mission check

?A large emerging economy cuts its export costs and its furniture exports to a developed country surge. Which outcome in the developed country is most consistent with economic analysis?

Globalisation's gains are real but uneven: who gains and who loses decides the politics as well as the economics.

Mission 2

Why trade patterns change

  • Pattern of trade: what a country exports and imports, and with whom.
  • It changes because of:
    • Comparative advantage shifts: skills, technology and investment change relative costs.
    • Emerging economies grow: new low-cost producers and fast-growing markets (China, India, Vietnam).
    • Trading blocs and bilateral agreements: trade grows inside a bloc; leaving one raises barriers.
    • Exchange rates: a depreciation makes exports cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home.
    • Income growth abroad: richer buyers want more services and high-value goods.
    • Transport and communication costs: lower costs make distant partners worthwhile.
Mission 2

The UK's changing trade

  • Partners: the EU bought 47% of UK exports in 2010 and 2011, and 42% in 2023. It is still the UK's largest partner (52% of UK imports in 2023).
  • Products: services rose from 34% of UK exports in 2000 to 52% in 2023.
  • Reasons:
    • faster growth in markets outside Europe (US, Asia)
    • leaving the EU single market and customs union (2021) raised barriers to EU trade
    • comparative advantage in services: finance, business services, education, creative industries
    • decline of manufacturing under competition from emerging economies

Mission 2

The terms of trade

  • Terms of trade: the rate at which a country's exports exchange for its imports.
  • Measured as an index:
  • Terms of trade = (index of average export prices ÷ index of average import prices) × 100
  • Base year = 100.
  • Index rises: an improvement (favourable movement): each unit of exports buys more imports.
  • Index falls: a deterioration (unfavourable movement): each unit of exports buys fewer imports.
  • Say: "the terms of trade improved" — not: "the trade balance improved". They are different measures.

Mission 2

What changes the terms of trade

Short run:

  • Exchange rate: an appreciation raises export prices in foreign currency and lowers import prices at home: the terms of trade improve. A depreciation worsens them.
  • Relative inflation: faster inflation at home raises export prices relative to import prices.
  • World commodity prices: changes in world demand or supply for oil, metals, food.

Long run:

  • Relative productivity: faster productivity growth cuts costs and export prices.
  • Incomes: as world incomes rise, demand for food and raw materials (low YED) grows more slowly than for manufactures and services.
  • Competition: low-cost producers in emerging economies push down world prices of many manufactures.

Mission 2

Impact: it depends on why

An improvement in the terms of trade:

  • Demand-led (world demand for the exports rose): higher export revenue, incomes and jobs; the current account improves; each export buys more imports, so living standards rise.
  • Cost-led (appreciation, high domestic inflation): exports lose price competitiveness. If demand is price elastic: revenue falls, jobs go, the current account worsens.

A deterioration:

  • Imports cost more: imported inflation and lower real incomes.
  • If caused by a depreciation, exports become cheaper abroad: export volumes rise and the current account can improve.
  • Whether it does depends on the price elasticities of demand for exports and imports: the Marshall–Lerner condition.
  • Primary exporters face a long-run risk: the Prebisch–Singer hypothesis says their terms of trade tend to fall against manufactures. Evidence is debated.
Quick check
?Country K's currency appreciates by 10%. Its export prices in foreign currency rise, and its import prices in its own currency fall. Demand for its exports is price elastic. Which outcome is most likely?
Mission 2

Evaluate: is an improvement good news?

It depends on…

  • Why it happened. Rising world demand for exports usually helps; higher domestic costs or an appreciation may hurt.
  • Price elasticities of demand for exports and imports.
  • Time. Trade volumes respond slowly, so short-run and long-run effects differ.
  • Openness. Where trade is a large share of GDP, the effects are larger.
  • What is imported. Dearer energy and food raise firms' costs and hit poorer households hardest.
  • Real case: Japan's index fell from 106.5 to 79.3 between 2020 and 2022: each unit of exports bought about 26% fewer imports.
Mission check

?Country C exports coffee (illustrative data). In the base year its export and import price indices are both 100. A year later its export price index is 90, its import price index is 108, and the volume of its coffee exports has risen by 30%. Which statement is correct?

The terms of trade compare prices, not earnings: a deterioration can come with higher export revenue, and an improvement with lower.

Mission 3

An exchange rate is a price

  • Exchange rate: the price of one currency in terms of another.
  • Example: £1 = $1.25. So $1 = 1 ÷ 1.25 = £0.80.
  • Appreciation: the currency buys more of another currency.
  • Depreciation: the currency buys less of another currency.
  • Floating exchange rate: set by demand and supply in the foreign exchange market, with no target set by the government or central bank.
  • In a fixed system, the government or central bank sets the rate; a change is a devaluation or revaluation (see Mission 4).
  • Say: "the pound appreciated against the dollar" — not: "the exchange rate went up", which depends on which way the rate is quoted.
Mission 3

Worked example: one price, two currencies

  • Rule, when the rate is quoted as foreign currency per 1 unit of home currency: from home to foreign, multiply by the rate; from foreign to home, divide.
  • Rate £1 = $1.30.
  • A £500 phone sells in the US for 500 × 1.30 = $650.
  • $65 jeans cost a UK buyer 65 ÷ 1.30 = £50.
  • The pound appreciates to $1.50. Phone: 500 × 1.50 = $750. Jeans: 65 ÷ 1.50 = £43.33.
  • Appreciation: exports cost foreigners more; imports cost home buyers less.
Mission 3

Worked example: how much did it change?

  • % change = (new rate − old rate) ÷ old rate × 100.
  • Real data: the euro averaged $1.1830 in 2021 and $1.0534 in 2022 (about $1.18 and $1.05).
  • Euro: (1.0534 − 1.1830) ÷ 1.1830 × 100 = −11.0%. The euro depreciated against the dollar.
  • Flip the rate: the dollar averaged 1 ÷ 1.1830 = €0.8453 in 2021 and 1 ÷ 1.0534 = €0.9493 in 2022.
  • Dollar: (0.9493 − 0.8453) ÷ 0.8453 × 100 = +12.3%. The dollar appreciated against the euro.
  • Not the same size: each % change uses a different starting value.
  • A €100 hotel room cost a US visitor $118.30 at the 2021 average rate and $105.34 at the 2022 average.

Mission 3

The foreign exchange market

  • Currencies are traded worldwide, around the clock, mainly through banks. Buyers and sellers include firms, investors, speculators, tourists and central banks.
  • Much of the trading is for financial investment and speculation, not to pay for goods.
  • Demand for a currency comes from foreigners who need it: to buy its exports and to make inflows of investment.
  • Supply of a currency comes from its own residents selling it: to buy imports and to invest abroad (outflows).
  • Demand slopes down: a cheaper currency makes the country's goods and assets cheaper to foreigners, so more of it is demanded.
  • Supply slopes up: a dearer currency makes imports cheaper to residents, so they supply more of it.
  • Equilibrium where demand = supply. Above it, a surplus of the currency pushes the rate down; below it, a shortage pushes it up.

Mission 3

Real case: the euro and the dollar in 2022

  • US central bank (the Federal Reserve): top of its target range for interest rates 0.25% at the start of 2022, 4.50% at the end.
  • ECB deposit facility rate: −0.50% until July 2022, 2.00% at the end of 2022.
  • US interest rates rose further than euro-area rates.
  • Theory: demand for dollars rises as savers buy US assets, so the dollar should appreciate against the euro.
  • Data: the dollar rose 12.3% against the euro, comparing 2021 and 2022 averages.
  • The data fit the theory. But other factors were also changing, so one year's data cannot prove the cause.
Mission 3Evaluate

How far can you predict a currency?

  • It depends on:
    • several factors at once: interest rates may rise while growth slows; the effects can offset
    • expectations: speculators act on what they expect, so a rate can move before anything else changes
    • relative, not absolute: what matters is a country's interest rates, inflation and growth compared with other countries'
    • time horizon: day to day, financial flows dominate; over years, trade and competitiveness matter more
  • A central bank may also intervene (see Mission 4).

Mission 4

Three exchange rate systems

  • Floating: demand and supply in the currency market set the rate (see Mission 3). A rise is an appreciation, a fall a depreciation.
  • Fixed (a peg): the central bank commits to one rate against another currency or a basket. A deliberate cut in the official rate is a devaluation; a deliberate rise, a revaluation.
  • Managed float: the rate floats, but the central bank steps in to limit or steer its movements.
  • Real examples: the Hong Kong dollar has been pegged to the US dollar at about 7.80 since 1983; the euro floats; Singapore manages its currency against a basket of its trading partners' currencies.
  • Say: "the pound depreciated" for a floating currency — not: "the pound was devalued". Devaluation is a policy decision under a fixed rate.
Quick check
?A country pegs its currency to the US dollar. Its government announces that the official rate will be cut from 2.0 to 1.6 US dollars per unit of its currency. Which term describes this?
Mission 4

Holding a fixed rate

  • Step 1: the market for the currency clears at the peg.
  • Step 2: demand for the currency falls (D1 → D2). At the peg, supply now exceeds demand: the market rate would fall below the peg.
  • Step 3: the central bank closes the gap. It buys its own currency, paying with foreign currency reserves. This adds to demand: D2 → D3, back to the peg.
  • Other tools: raise interest rates (attracts financial inflows, so more demand for the currency); exchange controls (limits on buying foreign currency); borrowing reserves from abroad.
  • Pressure the other way (demand rises): the central bank sells its own currency and builds up reserves, or cuts interest rates.

  • Where shocks come from: a floating rate absorbs shocks from abroad; a peg imports the anchor country's interest rates, which may not suit.
  • Openness and trade links: a small, open economy trading mainly with one partner (Hong Kong, with the US dollar) gains more from a peg.
  • Inflation history: a peg can import credibility, but if inflation stays higher than the anchor's, the currency becomes overvalued.

Mission 5

What competitiveness means

  • International competitiveness: the ability of a country's firms to sell goods and services, at home and abroad, against foreign rivals.
  • Price competitiveness: selling at lower prices than rivals for similar quality.
  • Non-price competitiveness: quality, design, reliability, innovation, brand, after-sales service, delivery times.
  • Two price measures: relative unit labour costs and relative export prices.
Mission 5

Unit labour costs

  • Unit labour cost (ULC) = total labour costs ÷ output.
  • Or: labour cost per worker ÷ output per worker (productivity).
  • Illustrative data, same currency:
Labour cost per workerOutput per workerULC
Country A50,0002,000 units50,000 ÷ 2,000 = 25
Country B20,000500 units20,000 ÷ 500 = 40
  • A pays 2.5 times the wage, but its ULC is lower: each A worker produces 4 times as much.
  • Productivity is the key: it lets firms pay higher wages without raising ULC.
  • Say: "unit labour costs rose" — not: "wages rose", unless productivity stood still.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. In a year, labour cost per worker rises by 4% and output per worker rises by 4%. What happens to unit labour costs?
Mission 5

Worked example: relative unit labour costs

  • Relative ULC = home ULC ÷ trading partners' ULC (in a common currency) × 100.
  • Rising: less competitive than in the base year. Falling: more competitive.
  • Illustrative data. Year 1: home ULC = partners' ULC = 40. Index = 100.
  • Year 2: home pay per worker +5%, productivity +1%.
    • Home ULC = 40 × 1.05 ÷ 1.01 = 41.58 (about +4%: 5% − 1%).
    • Partners' ULC +2% = 40.80.
    • Relative ULC = 41.58 ÷ 40.80 × 100 = 101.9: less competitive.
  • Now the home currency depreciates 5% against partners' currencies.
    • Home ULC in partners' currency falls by 5%: 101.9 × 0.95 = 96.8: more competitive.
  • So the exchange rate can outweigh wage and productivity changes.
Mission 5

Relative export prices

  • Relative export prices = index of home export prices ÷ index of rivals' export prices (in a common currency) × 100.
  • Illustrative data: home export price index 104, rivals' 102. Relative export prices = 104 ÷ 102 × 100 = 102.0: less price competitive.
  • Home currency depreciates 3%: 104 × 0.97 ÷ 102 × 100 = 98.9: more price competitive.
  • Export prices depend on ULC, but also on other costs (energy, materials), profit margins and the exchange rate.
  • Say: "relative export prices" compare our export prices with rivals' export prices — not: with our import prices (that is the terms of trade).
Quick check
?Which change would make a country's relative export price index fall, so it becomes more price competitive?
Mission 5

What drives competitiveness

  • Price factors:
    • wage costs relative to productivity (investment, skills, technology, management)
    • the exchange rate
    • inflation relative to trading partners
    • taxes, regulation, energy costs, infrastructure
  • Non-price factors: quality, design, innovation (research and development), reliability, branding.
  • Globalisation brings competition from emerging economies with lower labour costs. Higher-cost countries compete through high productivity, skills and non-price factors.

Mission 5

Benefits and problems

  • Benefits of being competitive:
    • export-led growth and more jobs
    • a stronger current account
    • more inward FDI
    • economies of scale from bigger markets
  • Problems of being uncompetitive:
    • current account deficits and falling shares of world markets
    • deindustrialisation and structural unemployment in exposed industries
    • slower growth; pressure on the currency, so dearer imports and inflation
    • less inward investment
  • Competing on low wages keeps costs down but also keeps living standards down. Competing on productivity raises both.
Mission 5Evaluate

How useful are the measures?

It depends on:

  • Non-price factors: a country with high relative ULC can still win on quality and brand.
  • Sector mix: ULC averages over the whole economy; exporters may differ from the average.
  • The exchange rate: it can swing relative costs fast, and reverse just as fast.
  • The route: lower relative ULC from productivity growth is lasting; from wage cuts it lowers living standards.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. In a year, Country K's labour cost per worker rises 6% and its output per worker rises 6%. Its trading partners' unit labour costs, in their own currencies, do not change. K's currency appreciates 10% against theirs. What happens to K's relative unit labour costs?

Competitiveness is relative and measured in a common currency. Pay, productivity and the exchange rate all count.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Globalisation: growing integration and interdependence through trade, capital flows, migration, supply chains and MNCs.
  • Causes: liberalisation, new economies joining world markets, cheaper transport and communication, free capital movement, MNCs.
  • Gains are large in total but uneven between groups and countries; global shocks spread faster.

Mission 2

  • Trade patterns change with comparative advantage, emerging economies, trading blocs, exchange rates, incomes and transport costs.
  • UK trade has shifted towards services and towards markets outside the EU.
  • Terms of trade = (export price index ÷ import price index) × 100; a rise is an improvement.
  • Whether an improvement is good news depends on its cause and on price elasticities.

Mission 3

  • A floating exchange rate is set by demand for and supply of a currency.
  • Demand comes from foreigners buying exports and investing in; supply from residents buying imports and investing abroad.
  • % change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100; the two currencies' changes are not the same size.

Mission 4

  • Floating: market sets the rate (appreciation/depreciation). Fixed: the central bank holds it (devaluation/revaluation). Managed: it floats, with intervention.
  • A peg is held by buying or selling the currency with reserves, and by interest rates.

Mission 5

  • ULC = labour cost per worker ÷ output per worker. High wages with high productivity can still mean low ULC.
  • Relative ULC and relative export prices compare a country with its rivals in a common currency. Rising = less competitive.
  • Productivity, inflation, the exchange rate and non-price factors drive competitiveness.
