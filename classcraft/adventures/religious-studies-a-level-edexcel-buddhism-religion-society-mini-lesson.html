Mission 1

What ahimsa means

  • Ahimsa: non-harming, non-violence towards all living beings. The word is shared with Hindu and Jain traditions.
  • In Buddhism it is expressed in the first precept: to abstain from killing or harming living beings.
  • It covers all sentient beings: humans, animals, insects.

> "All men tremble at punishment, all men fear death; remember that you are like unto them, and do not kill, nor cause slaughter." (Dhammapada 129, Müller 1881)

  • The reason given is empathy: put yourself in the other's place.
  • "Nor cause slaughter": ahimsa covers causing others to kill, not only killing yourself.
Mission 1

Why harm matters: four reasons

  • Karma: harming from hatred or greed is akusala and brings suffering to the one who harms.
  • Rebirth: all beings circle in samsara; an animal may have been a human, even one's mother, in a past life (Samyutta Nikaya 15.14–19, paraphrase).
  • Compassion (karuna) and loving-kindness (metta): the wish that all beings be well.
  • Interdependence: harming others damages the web of conditions one also depends on.
  • Ahimsa is applied with intention in view: deliberate harm weighs most.
Mission 1

Animals as fellow beings

  • The animal realm is one of the six realms of rebirth; animals suffer, fear and seek happiness.
  • The Jataka tales tell of the Buddha's past lives, many as animals, often acting with great compassion.
  • The Buddha rejected animal sacrifice: in the Kutadanta Sutta (Digha Nikaya 5) he describes an ideal sacrifice in which no animals are killed (paraphrase).
  • Practices today: releasing animals (life release, common in East Asia and Tibet); caring for temple animals; avoiding pesticides where possible.
  • Hard cases: pests, animal experiments for medicine, farming. Buddhists weigh intention, need and degree of harm, and come to different conclusions.

Mission 1Diversity

Meat-eating

  • Theravada: monks eat what they are given on almsround. The Buddha allows meat that is pure in three respects: the monk has not seen, heard or suspected that the animal was killed for him (Jivaka Sutta, Majjhima Nikaya 55, paraphrase).
  • Pali texts show the Buddha himself accepting meat offered as alms, e.g. pork from the householder Ugga (Anguttara Nikaya 5.44, paraphrase).
  • When Devadatta demanded that monks be made vegetarian, the Buddha refused to make it a rule (Vinaya, Cullavagga VII, paraphrase). Many Theravada lay people eat meat; some are vegetarian.
  • East Asian Mahayana: monastics are vegetarian. The Lankavatara Sutra (ch. 8) and the bodhisattva precepts of the Brahmajala Sutra forbid eating meat, since all beings have been one's relatives.
  • Tibetan: meat is widely eaten, partly because of climate. The 14th Dalai Lama (b. 1935) encourages vegetarianism and tried it himself, but health problems led doctors to advise some meat.
  • Your named scholar for ahimsa: the 14th Dalai Lama.

Right livelihood and the Lotus Sutra

  • Pali Canon: a lay follower should not trade in weapons, living beings, meat, intoxicants or poison (Vanijja Sutta, Anguttara Nikaya 5.177, paraphrase).
  • Lotus Sutra, chapter 14 ("Peaceful Practices"): a bodhisattva should not keep close company with those who hunt, fish, butcher, or raise animals for slaughter (paraphrase).

Mission 1Evaluate

Does ahimsa require vegetarianism?

  • Claim: A Buddhist who takes ahimsa seriously must be vegetarian.
  • For: Buying meat causes slaughter through demand: "nor cause slaughter" (Dhammapada 129).
  • For: Mahayana sutras (Lankavatara, Brahmajala) and leading teachers (the Dalai Lama) urge it; compassion for all beings points that way.
  • Against: The Buddha himself refused to make vegetarianism a rule and allowed meat pure in three respects (MN 55).
  • Against: Monks must accept what they are given; refusing food can be attachment to purity; climate and health may make it hard (Tibet).
  • Judgement criteria: Is intention or result the key to karma? Does modern industrial farming change the "not killed for me" test? Should the Buddha's rule or later Mahayana teaching decide?

Mission 2

The ideal: non-violence

  • Ahimsa (see Mission 1) rules out deliberate killing of humans most of all.

> "For hatred does not cease by hatred at any time: hatred ceases by love, this is an old rule." (Dhammapada 5, Müller 1881)

> "Victory breeds hatred, for the conquered is unhappy." (Dhammapada 201, Müller 1881)

  • War is driven by the three poisons: greed (land, wealth), hatred, delusion (us and them).
  • The greatest victory is over oneself: one who conquers himself is "the greatest of conquerors" (Dhammapada 103, Müller 1881).
  • Monks may not go to watch an army in the field, stay with it more than three nights, or visit its drills and parades (Vinaya, Pacittiya 48–50, paraphrase); serving royal soldiers may not be ordained without release (Mahavagga I.40, paraphrase).
Mission 2Read the source

The Yodhajiva Sutta

Samyutta Nikaya 42.3 (paraphrase):

  • Yodhajiva ("warrior"), the headman of a village of professional soldiers, tells the Buddha his teachers taught that a warrior who dies fighting is reborn among the "devas (gods) of those slain in battle". What does the Buddha say?
  • Twice the Buddha declines to answer and tells him to leave the question.
  • Asked a third time, he answers: in battle the warrior's mind is already low and misdirected, set on the thought that these beings should be struck down and destroyed.
  • If he dies like that, he is reborn not in heaven but in hell, in the hell "of those slain in battle"; if he holds the wrong view, hell or the animal womb.
  • Yodhajiva weeps: not at the Buddha's words, but because he was deceived by his teachers for so long. He takes refuge.

Q1 Why is the warrior's rebirth bad, on the Buddha's account?

Show answer

His intention at death is to kill: karma follows intention.

Q2 Why might the Buddha refuse at first?

Show answer

The truth was painful; he answers only when the soldier sincerely insists.

  • Anthology extract (4): Samyutta Nikaya 42.3 (Thanissaro translation): paraphrase only.

Mission 2

The Buddha's example

  • The Rohini river: the Sakyas (the Buddha's own clan) and the Koliyas are about to fight over water in a drought. The Buddha asks which is worth more, water or the warriors' blood; they make peace (Jataka and Dhammapada commentaries, paraphrase).
  • Vidudabha's army: three times the Buddha sits in thin shade on the road where the king will march against the Sakyas; three times the king turns back. The fourth time, the Buddha does not intervene: the Sakyas' karma ripens (Dhammapada commentary, paraphrase).
  • War between kings: told of a victory, the Buddha says that the killer begets a killer and the conqueror a conqueror (Samyutta Nikaya 3.15, paraphrase).
  • Lessons drawn: mediate where possible; see conflict's causes; but violence has karmic consequences on all sides.
Mission 2

Kings, protection and Ashoka

  • Buddhist texts describe an ideal righteous king (cakkavatti) who rules by Dhamma, not the sword, but who gives protection to his people (Cakkavatti-sihanada Sutta, Digha Nikaya 26, paraphrase).
  • Ashoka (Mauryan emperor, ruled c. 268–232 BCE): after conquering Kalinga (c. 260 BCE) he records deep remorse for the slaughter and turns to "conquest by Dhamma" (Rock Edict XIII).
  • But Ashoka kept an army and warned the forest peoples that he still had power (same edict).
  • So the tradition holds two things in tension: non-violence as the ideal, and rulers' duty to protect.
Mission 2Diversity

Buddhists who have justified war

  • Sri Lanka: the Mahavamsa (5th–6th century CE chronicle) tells how King Dutthagamani (2nd century BCE) fought the Tamil king Elara with a relic in his spear "for the Dhamma". When he grieves over the dead, arahants tell him only "one and a half" true people were killed: one who had taken refuge and one who kept the precepts (paraphrase).
  • Tessa J. Bartholomeusz (d. 2001), In Defense of Dharma: Just-War Ideology in Buddhist Sri Lanka (2002): during the civil war (1983–2009) many Sinhalese Buddhists, including monks, argued that defending the island and the Dhamma could justify war. She argues Buddhist ethics in practice is shaped by context, not one pacifist rule.
  • Japan: many Zen and other leaders supported militarism up to 1945 (documented by Brian Victoria, Zen at War, 1997); several sects later apologised.
  • Most Buddhists see these as departures from the Buddha's teaching; the debate is how far they show a real strand of Buddhist thought.
Mission 2

Mahayana: compassion and skilful means

  • Upayakausalya Sutra (Skill in Means; Mahayana, early centuries CE) (paraphrase): a ship's captain (the Buddha in a past life) learns that a robber plans to kill 500 merchants. Out of compassion for the merchants, and for the robber, who would suffer terribly for the crime, he kills the robber and accepts the karmic cost himself.
  • Some Mahayana thinkers draw from this that killing with pure compassion, to prevent greater harm, may be justified in extreme cases.
  • Lotus Sutra, chapter 20: the bodhisattva Never Disparaging bows to everyone, saying they will become Buddhas; when people beat him with sticks and stones, he steps back and bows again (paraphrase): non-retaliation rooted in respect for every being's Buddha nature.
  • So Mahayana sources point both ways: radical non-violence, and exceptional compassionate force.

Mission 2

Buddhists in the armed forces

  • Most Buddhist-majority countries (e.g. Thailand, Sri Lanka, Myanmar) have armies, many with conscription and Buddhist chaplains.
  • In the UK, Buddhists serve in the armed forces, which appoint a Buddhist chaplain.
  • Arguments for serving: protecting the vulnerable is compassionate; intention can be defensive; roles include peacekeeping, medicine, disaster relief.
  • Arguments against: right livelihood excludes trading in weapons (AN 5.177); a soldier may be ordered to kill; the Yodhajiva Sutta warns about the mind of the killer.
  • Some Buddhists take non-combat roles or are conscientious objectors.
Mission 2

Weapons of mass destruction

  • WMD (nuclear, chemical, biological) kill indiscriminately: civilians, children, animals, and future generations through radiation and pollution.
  • So almost all Buddhist voices oppose them: they breach ahimsa on a vast scale, and no intention can be precise enough to justify them.
  • Josei Toda, second president of Soka Gakkai (Japanese Nichiren lay movement), issued a Declaration Against Nuclear Weapons in 1957; Soka Gakkai International still campaigns for abolition.
  • The 14th Dalai Lama (Nobel Peace Prize 1989) calls for the elimination of nuclear weapons and for "inner disarmament" as the root of outer disarmament; in 1987 he proposed Tibet as a zone of ahimsa.
  • A debated question: can possessing WMD as a deterrent be justified if the intention is to prevent war? Most Buddhist leaders say no; some argue it reduces suffering.
Mission 2Evaluate

Can a Buddhist ever fight?

  • Claim: Ahimsa means a Buddhist should never take part in war.
  • For: Dhammapada 5 and 201; the Yodhajiva Sutta; the Vinaya's distance from armies; the Buddha's example of mediation.
  • For: Hatred and fear in battle make akusala intention almost unavoidable.
  • Against: Rulers have a duty to protect (DN 26); Ashoka kept an army after Kalinga.
  • Against: Mahayana skilful means (Upayakausalya Sutra) allows compassionate force in extreme cases; Bartholomeusz shows Buddhists reasoning from context.
  • Judgement criteria: Is the ideal for monks or for everyone? Can intention in war be compassionate? Do the Mahavamsa and Japan show Buddhism or its distortion? Is defence different from attack?

Buddhist teaching on war centres on intention: hatred in battle is akusala, and even compassionate force carries karmic weight. Buddhists differ on whether that ever permits fighting.

Mission 3

One name, many forms

  • "Buddhism" covers Theravada (Sri Lanka, Thailand, Myanmar), Mahayana (e.g. Zen, Pure Land, Nichiren in East Asia) and Vajrayana (Tibet), plus newer Western forms (e.g. Triratna, secular Buddhism).
  • They differ in scriptures, goals, practices, languages and forms of monastic life.
  • The word "Buddhism" itself was popularised by 19th-century Western scholars, who grouped these traditions under one label. Asian Buddhists spoke of the Dharma (dhamma) or the Buddha's teaching.
  • The question: are these one religion with branches, or several religions sharing a name?
Mission 3

A shared core?

  • The Three Jewels (Buddha, Dharma, Sangha): every tradition takes refuge in them.
  • The Four Noble Truths and the Eightfold Path.
  • The three marks of existence: Dukkha, Anicca (impermanence), anatta (not-self).
  • Dependent origination and karma and rebirth.
  • The five precepts for lay people.
  • 1967: the World Buddhist Sangha Council (Colombo) agreed nine basic points uniting Theravada and Mahayana, drafted by Walpola Rahula (1907–1997): e.g. the Buddha as the only master; refuge in the Triple Gem; no creator God; the Four Noble Truths; and that differing customs in different countries do not touch the essence.

Mission 3

Upaya: skilful means

  • Upaya (skilful means): the Buddha adapts his teaching to each listener's capacity, like a doctor prescribing different medicine.
  • Lotus Sutra, chapter 2 ("Skilful Means") and chapter 3: the parable of the burning house. A father's children will not leave a burning house; he promises them goat-, deer- and ox-carts. Once they are safe, he gives each the same, far finer great carriage (paraphrase).
  • The three carts are the "three vehicles"; the great carriage is the One Vehicle (ekayana) to Buddhahood.
  • So, for many Mahayana Buddhists, different teachings are provisional: true as means, not final statements.
  • This can explain diversity positively: many forms because there are many kinds of people.
Mission 3Read the source

The raft

Majjhima Nikaya 22, Alagaddupama Sutta (paraphrase): A traveller reaches a great river. This bank is dangerous; the far bank is safe. With no ferry, he builds a raft from grass and branches and crosses. Should he carry the raft on his head for the rest of his journey? No: having crossed, he leaves it. In the same way, the Dhamma is for crossing over, not for holding on to.

Q1 What is the raft in the parable?

Show answer

The teaching, Dhamma: a means to cross to nirvana.

Q2 How might a Buddhist use this parable when meeting a very different Buddhist tradition?

Show answer

Forms of teaching are tools; disputes over the raft matter less than reaching the far shore.

  • Note: this is a Pali (Theravada) text: the idea that teachings are means is not only Mahayana.
Mission 3

How Buddhists respond to diversity within Buddhism

  • Inclusive / upaya view: all forms are skilful means suited to different people (common in Mahayana; Lotus Sutra).
  • Graded view: traditions are steps on one path. Tibetan lamrim ("stages of the path") places shravaka, Mahayana and Vajrayana practice in order. Older Mahayana texts called earlier schools Hinayana ("lesser vehicle"), a term now avoided.
  • Authenticity view: many Theravadins hold that the Pali Canon is the closest record of the Buddha's words and treat later Mahayana sutras with caution.
  • Exclusive view: Nichiren (1222–1282) taught that only the Lotus Sutra leads to Buddhahood in this age.
  • Co-operative view: shared bodies such as the World Buddhist Sangha Council (1966) or the UK's Network of Buddhist Organisations.
  • Blind men and the elephant (Udana 6.4, paraphrase): blind men each touch one part of an elephant and quarrel over what it is; the Buddha compares this to teachers who cling to one-sided views.

Mission 3Evaluate

Many traditions with little in common?

  • Claim: Buddhism is a collection of different traditions with little in common.
  • For: goals differ (arhat, bodhisattva, Pure Land); powers differ (self-power vs other-power); scriptures differ (Pali Canon vs Mahayana sutras).
  • For: "Buddhism" as one religion is partly a Western scholarly label.
  • Against: all take refuge in the Three Jewels and teach the Four Noble Truths, impermanence and not-self; the 1967 nine points show agreement.
  • Against: upaya and the raft explain diversity as different means to one end.
  • Criteria: What counts as "in common": beliefs, practices, goals or history? Is it enough to share a founder and refuge? Who decides: insiders or scholars?

Mission 4

Test it, even the Buddha

  • Kalama Sutta (Anguttara Nikaya 3.65; see Mission 7): do not accept a teaching merely from tradition, scripture or a teacher's reputation; test it by whether it leads to harm or well-being.
  • Vimamsaka Sutta (Majjhima Nikaya 47): monks should investigate even the Buddha to see whether he is truly awakened (paraphrase).
  • So loyalty rests on experience, not on belonging to a group.
  • This leaves room for respect: a person's honest testing may lead them along another path.
Mission 4Read the source

The Buddha and Upali

Majjhima Nikaya 56, Upali Sutta (paraphrase). Upali, a wealthy lay supporter of the Jain teacher Nigantha Nataputta, comes to defeat the Buddha in debate. Instead he is convinced and asks to become the Buddha's lay follower.

  • The Buddha replies (section 16): investigate carefully first; careful investigation is right for a well-known man like you.
  • Upali is even more impressed: other teachers would have paraded through the town announcing their new convert.
  • The Buddha adds (section 17): your family has long supported the Jain monks; you should still give them alms when they come.

Q1 What two things does the Buddha ask of Upali?

Show answer

Investigate before changing allegiance; keep supporting his former teachers.

Q2 What does this suggest about Buddhist attitudes to other faiths?

Show answer

Respect and generosity continue across religious lines; conversion is not a trophy.

Mission 4

Ashoka's edicts

  • Ashoka (reigned c. 268–232 BCE), Mauryan emperor of most of India. After the bloody Kalinga war he turns to the Dharma (Rock Edict 13). His edicts are carved on rocks and pillars across his empire.
  • Rock Edict 7 (paraphrase): the king wishes that all sects may live everywhere, for all of them seek self-control and purity of heart.
  • Rock Edict 12 (paraphrase):
  • The king honours all sects, ascetics and householders, with gifts.
  • What matters most is the growth of the essentials in all of them.
  • Its root is restraint in speech: do not praise your own sect or condemn others without good reason.
  • Whoever praises his own sect and condemns others out of devotion harms his own sect.
  • Concord is good: listen to and respect one another's teachings.

Mission 4

Nichiren: one teaching for this age

  • Nichiren (1222–1282), Japanese monk: in this degenerate age (mappo), only the Lotus Sutra leads to Buddhahood; chant Nam(u) Myoho Renge Kyo (the daimoku).
  • Rissho Ankoku Ron (1260): disasters strike Japan because rulers support false teachings, above all Honen's Pure Land; later he also attacks Zen, Shingon and Ritsu.
  • Two methods of spreading the Dharma: shakubuku (forcefully refuting error) and shoju (gentle persuasion).
  • Modern Nichiren groups differ:
  • Nichiren Shoshu: strongly exclusive; members avoid other religions' rites.
  • Soka Gakkai International (Daisaku Ikeda, 1928–2023): still holds the Lotus Sutra supreme, but stresses dialogue, peace and humanism with other faiths.
  • Rissho Kosei-kai (founded 1938; Nikkyo Niwano): co-founded the interfaith World Conference of Religions for Peace (1970).
Mission 4Diversity

Other religions and worldviews: truth claims

  • Real differences in truth claims: most Buddhists reject a creator God (the Brahmajala Sutta, Digha Nikaya 1, tells of a god who wrongly thinks he made the world); anatta contrasts with an eternal soul; liberation is by insight, not by grace (except in Pure Land's other-power).
  • Exclusivist: only the Buddha's path (or, for Nichiren, the Lotus Sutra) fully liberates.
  • Inclusivist: other religions contain real goodness and partial truth, useful as upaya, but the full path is the Dharma.
  • Pluralist: many paths can lead to the good; the 14th Dalai Lama (b. 1935): for an individual, "one truth, one religion"; for society, "several truths, several religions" (Toward a True Kinship of Faiths, 2010; short phrases).
  • Non-religious worldviews: the Buddha rejected materialism (nothing survives, actions have no fruit) as a wrong view, as well as eternalism; yet many Buddhists share humanists' reliance on reason and ethics, and secular Buddhists drop rebirth.
  • K. Sri Dhammananda (1919–2006): Buddhists should respect other religions and never convert by force or bribery; but the Buddhist goal of nibbana by one's own effort differs from salvation by a god (paraphrase).
Mission 4

Interfaith dialogue: benefits, extent, barriers

  • Examples: the Dalai Lama with Thomas Merton (1968) and with Christian, Jewish and Muslim leaders; Thich Nhat Hanh, Living Buddha, Living Christ (1995); Buddhist and Christian monastics at the Gethsemani Encounter (Kentucky, 1996).
  • Benefits: peace and mutual understanding in multicultural societies; shared action on poverty, peace and climate; learning from one another's contemplative practice.
  • Barriers: different truth claims (God, soul, grace); Buddhism tied to national identity in some countries (Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Thailand), with fear of conversion; exclusivist schools; no single Buddhist authority to speak for all.
  • Extent varies: strong in Western convert communities and among some Mahayana groups (SGI, Rissho Kosei-kai); weaker where Buddhism is a majority faith under pressure.
  • Your spec: the different importance of interfaith dialogue across traditions and countries; named scholars the Dalai Lama and K. Sri Dhammananda.
Mission 4

Freedom of religious expression

  • Context: freedom of thought, conscience and religion is a human right in European law (European Convention on Human Rights, Article 9); modern secular states treat pluralism as normal.
  • Buddhist support: Ashoka's edicts; the Kalama Sutta's free testing; Thich Nhat Hanh's third precept (never force views on others).
  • Buddhists also need it: e.g. state control over the recognition of reincarnate lamas in Tibet.
  • Tensions: offence at the use of Buddha images as décor, tattoos or fashion (in 2014 Sri Lanka deported a British tourist for a Buddha tattoo); some majority-Buddhist states protect Buddhism's status (Sri Lanka's constitution gives Buddhism "the foremost place" while guaranteeing other religions); Myanmar's 2015 laws on conversion and interfaith marriage were backed by nationalist monks and opposed by others.
  • Range of responses: defend full freedom, including criticism; ask for respect for the Three Jewels without legal force; or support legal protection of Buddhism.

Mission 4Evaluate

Does openness put Buddhist identity at risk?

  • Claim: Buddhism's openness to other paths puts its own identity at risk.
  • For: if testing may lead elsewhere, and other faiths contain good, why remain Buddhist? Blending (e.g. "dual belonging") can blur the Dharma.
  • For: in majority-Buddhist countries, fear of conversion shows openness can feel like a threat.
  • Against: openness is part of the identity: Upali, Ashoka and the Kalama Sutta are themselves Buddhist sources.
  • Against: respect is not relativism: Buddhists still hold that the Dharma ends dukkha; Nichiren and others draw firm lines.
  • Criteria: Is identity defined by doctrine, practice, community or ethics? Does the answer differ by tradition or country?

Mission 5

What "Engaged Buddhism" means

  • Engaged Buddhism: applying Buddhist teaching and practice to the social, political and environmental causes of suffering, not only to the individual mind.
  • It works on two levels at once: inner change (mindfulness, compassion) and outer change (service, protest, campaigning).
  • Liberationist: aims to free people from suffering caused by structures: war, poverty, caste, oppression, environmental damage.
  • It is a movement across many traditions (Theravada, Mahayana, Vajrayana, Western convert groups), not a separate school.
Mission 5

Why a Buddhist might engage

  • Dukkha is the starting point of the Four Noble Truths: the Buddha taught to end suffering, and much suffering has social causes.
  • Karuna (compassion) and metta (loving-kindness): wishing beings free from suffering leads to action.
  • Dependent origination: nothing exists alone; my peace depends on others' peace.
  • The precepts: the first precept (not harming) and right livelihood concern how society treats life.
  • These teachings underpin liberationist approaches: compassion (karuna), interdependence (paticcasamuppada), non-harm (ahimsa) and the goal of nibbana for all beings.
Mission 5Read the source

Two verses

Dhammapada (Müller translation, 1881):

> "All men tremble at punishment, all men fear death; remember that you are like unto them, and do not kill, nor cause slaughter." (Dhammapada 129)

> "For hatred does not cease by hatred at any time: hatred ceases by love, this is an old rule." (Dhammapada 5)

Q1 What reason does verse 129 give for not killing?

Show answer

Others fear death as you do: put yourself in their place.

Q2 How might an engaged Buddhist use verse 5 in a conflict?

Show answer

To argue for non-violent action and reconciliation, not revenge.

  • Note: "nor cause slaughter" goes beyond personal conduct: it also rules out making others kill.

Mission 6

From mindfulness to activism

  • Social activism: organised action to change society: campaigns, protest, service, building alternatives.
  • Why a Buddhist may see it as practice, not a distraction:
  • Compassion (karuna): wishing beings free from suffering is incomplete without action.
  • Interdependence: harm to forests, the poor or the enemy is not separate from me.
  • Right action and right livelihood: the path includes how I act and earn in the world.
  • Non-violence (ahimsa): it shapes the method as much as the goal.
  • These are the teachings your spec means by support "in and for liberationist approaches" (spellings: dhamma, nibbana).
Mission 6Evaluate

Should engagement be central to the Buddhist path?

  • Claim: Social engagement is central to the Buddhist path, not a distraction from it.
  • For: the path aims to end dukkha, and much dukkha is social; karuna without action is empty.
  • For: interbeing / dependent origination: there is no private awakening apart from others; the bodhisattva ideal (a vow to work for the awakening of all beings).
  • Against: the Buddha left home; nirvana is reached by insight, not by changing society; time in politics is time not spent on practice.
  • Against: activism can bring anger and attachment to results; monks in politics have also backed nationalism and violence (e.g. Japanese Zen support for militarism before 1945).
  • Criteria: Which goal comes first: awakening or relief of suffering? Can the two be separated? Does it matter who acts (lay or monastic)? Does engagement undo the stereotype of Buddhism as inward-looking, or misread the tradition?

Mission 7

Why Buddhism and science keep meeting

  • From the late 19th century, Asian and Western writers present Buddhism as a "scientific religion": no creator God, no demand for blind faith, cause and effect at its centre.
  • Buddhist responses range from "science confirms the Dharma", to "science and Dharma answer different questions", to "drop what science disproves".
  • Your spec asks for responses in the historical and contemporary world; Pali forms on your lines: kamma, dhamma, nibbana.
Mission 7Read the source

The Kalama Sutta

Anguttara Nikaya 3.65 (paraphrase). The Kalamas are confused: visiting teachers each praise their own doctrine and attack others'. The Buddha replies:

  • Section 9: do not accept a teaching merely because of: repeated report; tradition; rumour; scripture; logic or inference; how things appear; agreement with a view you already hold; the speaker's apparent ability; or because "this monk is our teacher".
  • When you yourselves know that something is unwholesome, blameworthy, criticised by the wise, and leads to harm and suffering when done, abandon it.
  • Section 10: when you yourselves know that something is wholesome, blameless, praised by the wise, and leads to benefit and happiness, take it up and stay with it.
  • Earlier in the sutta (sections 5–8), he asks them: does greed (then hatred, then delusion) arise in a person for their benefit or harm? They answer: for harm.

Q1 What is the test the Buddha gives?

Show answer

Your own experience of whether something leads to harm or to well-being, checked against the wise.

Q2 Is this the same as a laboratory test?

Show answer

Not quite: it is a test of ethical and mental results in one's own life.

Mission 7

Two readings of the Kalama Sutta

  • "Charter of free inquiry" (popular, especially in the West): accept nothing without evidence, as in science.
  • Bhikkhu Bodhi ("A Look at the Kalama Sutta", 1988): a warning against that reading.
  • The Kalamas were not yet followers; the Buddha starts from what they can see now (greed, hatred and delusion cause harm).
  • Faith (saddha) still has a place: the Buddha later teaches what cannot be checked at once (e.g. rebirth).
  • The same sutta offers four assurances: if there is rebirth and karma, a good life leads to a good rebirth; if not, a good life is still happy here and now. Belief in rebirth is not demanded first.
  • So the sutta supports testing in experience, but it is not a claim that Buddhism is a laboratory science.
Mission 7

The challenge to karma and rebirth

  • Challenge: science finds no mechanism by which moral actions shape future lives; most neuroscientists see consciousness as produced by the brain, ending at death.
  • Response 1, karma as intention: the Buddha says that intention is karma (Anguttara Nikaya 6.63, paraphrase). Intentions shape habits and character; psychology can observe this in one life.
  • Response 2, outside science's reach: the Dalai Lama: science has not disproved rebirth; it has not investigated it. Not finding something is different from showing it is false.
  • Response 3, evidence offered: some Buddhists cite the psychiatrist Ian Stevenson (1918–2007) and his studies of children who report past lives; critics dispute the method.
  • Response 4, agnostic / secular: Stephen Batchelor, Buddhism Without Beliefs (1997): practise without needing to believe in rebirth.
  • Karma is not fate: present intention matters. And the Buddha listed the exact results of karma among four things not to be speculated about (Anguttara Nikaya 4.77).
  • Spelling on your papers: kamma.
Mission 7

Miracles

  • Pali texts report iddhi (psychic powers) from deep meditation: e.g. the Buddha's Twin Miracle at Savatthi (fire and water from his body).
  • Challenge: such events break known laws of nature; science treats the reports as legend.
  • The Buddha's own caution: in the Kevaddha Sutta (Digha Nikaya 11) he names three "wonders": psychic power, reading minds, and instruction. He says he dislikes and is ashamed of the first two, because a sceptic could explain them away as a mere charm; only instruction truly transforms people (paraphrase).
  • Vinaya: monks are forbidden to display psychic powers to lay people (Cullavagga V.8, the sandalwood bowl).
  • Responses: powers are real but irrelevant to liberation; or the stories are symbolic; or, like rebirth, outside current science. All agree they are not the basis of faith.

Mission 7

Three Buddhist voices on science

  • 14th Dalai Lama (b. 1935), The Universe in a Single Atom (2005); dialogues with scientists since 1987 (Mind and Life Institute).
  • Buddhism and science share investigation; if science conclusively disproves a Buddhist claim, Buddhists should give that claim up (paraphrase).
  • But science has limits: it cannot settle questions of value, and it is not the whole of knowledge (first-person experience matters too).
  • Science needs compassion to guide its use.
  • K. Sri Dhammananda (1919–2006), Sri Lankan-born Theravada monk based in Malaysia, What Buddhists Believe (1964): the Buddha's teaching is rational and open to testing like science, and agrees with much of it; but it goes further, into mind and morality, where science alone cannot bring peace (paraphrase).
Mission 7

Genetic engineering

  • Science as a stimulus: new technologies force fresh Buddhist ethical thinking.
  • Key principles: the first precept (ahimsa); intention (cetana); compassion (karuna); the Middle Way; avoiding craving.
  • Gene therapy for disease: widely supported if motivated by compassion and not harming others.
  • Embryo research: many Buddhists hold that a new life (rebirth consciousness) begins at conception, so destroying embryos breaks the first precept (Damien Keown, Buddhism and Bioethics, 1995).
  • "Designer babies" / enhancement: often criticised as driven by craving and attachment to self.
  • GM crops: some support them to reduce hunger (compassion); others fear harm to ecosystems and farmers.
  • The Dalai Lama supports research that relieves suffering, but warns that ethics must keep pace with genetic power and that enhancement needs great caution.

Mission 7Evaluate

How close are Buddhism and science?

  • Claim: Buddhism and science are closely compatible.
  • For: no creator God; cause and effect; testing in experience (Kalama Sutta); meditation research; the Dalai Lama's readiness to revise.
  • For: interdependence and impermanence fit ecology, evolution and physics.
  • Against: karma, rebirth, devas and realms are not scientific claims; miracle stories remain.
  • Against: different aims: liberation from dukkha vs explanation of nature; the "scientific Buddhism" image is partly a modern construction (Lopez).
  • Criteria: Compatible with which Buddhism (modernist, traditional Asian, secular)? Does compatibility need agreement on facts, or only on method? Must Buddhists change beliefs, or can they set limits to science?
Mission check

?A neuroscientist tells a meditation group: "Brain scans show meditation changes attention, so Buddhism is proved true." Which reply would the Dalai Lama's approach most support?

For many Buddhists, science is a partner in investigation, not the judge of everything. Which claims it can test is the real debate.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Ahimsa (non-harming) is the principle of the first precept: do not kill, nor cause killing (Dhammapada 129).
  • Theravada allows meat that is not seen, heard or suspected to be killed for oneself; East Asian Mahayana monastics are vegetarian; Tibetans often eat meat; the Dalai Lama encourages vegetarianism.

Mission 2

  • The ideal is non-violence: hatred ceases by love (Dhammapada 5); the Yodhajiva Sutta denies heaven to warriors who die with killing in mind.
  • The Buddha's example: mediation (Rohini river), but no protection from karma; rulers' duty to protect sits in tension with ahimsa (DN 26, Ashoka).
  • Views differ: just-war reasoning in Sri Lanka (Mahavamsa; Bartholomeusz) and wartime Japan; Mahayana skilful means; near-universal opposition to WMD (Toda; the Dalai Lama).

Mission 3

  • Buddhist traditions differ in goal, power, practice, clergy and scripture, yet share the Three Jewels, Four Noble Truths and three marks.
  • Upaya (Lotus Sutra) and the raft (Majjhima Nikaya 22) present teachings as means, explaining diversity positively.
  • Buddhists respond to internal diversity with inclusive, graded, authenticity-based, exclusive and co-operative views.

Mission 4

  • Upali (Majjhima Nikaya 56) and the Kalama Sutta: investigate, and keep honouring other teachers; Ashoka's Rock Edicts 7 and 12: all sects seek self-control; restraint in speech; concord is good.
  • Nichiren is the sharpest exclusivist strand; modern Nichiren groups range from exclusive (Nichiren Shoshu) to interfaith (SGI, Rissho Kosei-kai). Buddhists respond to freedom of expression with support, requests for respect, or calls for legal protection.
  • Interfaith dialogue: real benefits, real barriers; the Dalai Lama and K. Sri Dhammananda model respect without merging.

Mission 5

  • Engaged Buddhism applies Buddhist teaching to the social and structural causes of suffering, alongside inner practice.
  • Teachings behind liberationist approaches: compassion, interdependence, non-harm.

Mission 6

  • Buddhist activism spans the environment (tree ordination, climate declaration), oppression and poverty (Ambedkar, Sarvodaya, Tibet, Myanmar) and peace (Thich Nhat Hanh, Ghosananda, BPF).
  • Buddhists disagree over whether engagement is central or a distraction, and over whether war could ever be accepted.

Mission 7

  • The Kalama Sutta encourages testing teachings in experience, though not as a laboratory science; Buddhists respond to challenges to karma, rebirth, realms and miracles by reinterpretation, by setting limits to science, or by revising.
  • The Dalai Lama and K. Sri Dhammananda: Buddhism is open to science but goes beyond it in mind and ethics.
