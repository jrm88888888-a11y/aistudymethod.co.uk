Mission 1

Mission 1: Moving costs energy. So what makes it worth an animal's while to move at all?

Make a guess: Moving costs energy. So what makes it worth an animal's while to move at all?

Mission 1

Responding pays

  • Environments change: light, humidity, temperature, food, predators.
  • Organisms that respond to change increase their chance of survival and of reproducing.
  • Locomotion: moving the whole body from place to place. It costs energy.
  • It pays when the move brings a benefit: food, safety, a mate or better conditions.
  • Responses can be behavioral (such as moving) or physiological (such as a change in heart rate).
Mission 1

Moving toward better conditions

  • Many animals respond to environmental change by moving.
  • Taxis: movement directed toward or away from a stimulus. Example: some small invertebrates move away from light.
  • Kinesis: a change in the rate of movement, not its direction. Example: woodlice move faster in dry air, so they end up in damp places.
  • Either way, the response keeps the animal where it is more likely to survive.
Mission 1Practical

Choice chamber

  • Damp side: water under the gauze. Dry side: a drying agent, such as silica gel.
  • Cover the chamber so light is even. Add 10 woodlice through the center; wait 10 minutes; count each side.
  • Repeat with fresh woodlice, swapping the damp and dry sides between runs.
  • Keep constant: light, temperature, number and type of animal, time.
  • A control differs from the experimental set-up only in the variable being tested.
  • Handle woodlice gently with a soft paintbrush; return them to where they were found.
  • Analysis: a χ² test on the counts (next screen).
Mission 1

Worked example: is the preference real?

Illustrative data: 40 woodlice; after 10 minutes, 29 on the damp side and 11 on the dry side.

  • Null hypothesis: no preference. Expected: 20 on each side.
  • χ² = Σ (o − e)²/e
  • Damp: (29 − 20)²/20 = 4.05. Dry: (11 − 20)²/20 = 4.05. χ² = 8.10.
  • Degrees of freedom = number of categories − 1 = 1. Critical value at p = 0.05: 3.84.
  • 8.10 > 3.84: reject the null hypothesis. The difference is significant: unlikely to be due to chance.
  • Say: "the difference is significant, so we reject the null hypothesis" — not: "this proves woodlice prefer damp".

Mission check

?Larvae of a hypothetical beetle lose water quickly in dry air. Forty larvae are placed in a dark choice chamber with a damp side and a dry side. After 15 minutes, 34 are on the damp side and 6 on the dry side. A chi-squared test gives χ² = 19.6; the critical value at p = 0.05 is 3.84. Which conclusion is best supported?

Moving costs energy, but a response that keeps an animal in damp, dark or food-rich places raises its chance of surviving.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why does an animal stop reacting to a harmless touch that keeps coming back?

Make a guess: Why does an animal stop reacting to a harmless touch that keeps coming back?

Mission 2

Innate or learned?

  • Innate behavior: inherited; much the same in every member of the species; done correctly the first time.
  • Learned behavior: changes with experience.
  • Learning changes the individual, not its alleles. Offspring must learn the behavior again.
Mission 2

Habituation

  • Habituation: a reduced response to a repeated stimulus that brings neither harm nor reward.
  • A snail touched between its tentacles pulls them in. Touch it again and again: it pulls them in less, and comes out sooner.
  • It is stimulus-specific: a different stimulus still gives a full response. So it is not tired muscles.
  • Advantage: no time or energy wasted on harmless stimuli; more time to feed, while staying alert to new ones.
  • Humans habituate too: you stop noticing a ticking clock.
  • Say: "a reduced response to a repeated stimulus" — not: "the animal is too tired to respond".
Mission 2Practical

Habituation in snails

  • Let a snail settle on a clean, damp surface until its tentacles are fully out.
  • Touch it between the tentacles with a moist cotton bud. Time how long until the tentacles are fully out again.
  • Repeat the touch each time it re-emerges, 10 or more times. Use several snails.
  • Independent variable: number of times touched. Dependent variable: time to re-emerge.
  • Keep constant: force and place of touch, interval, temperature, light, surface.
  • Handle gently; keep snails damp; return them afterward.

Mission 2

Learned or innate, selection favors what works

  • Fitness: the number of offspring an individual contributes to the next generation.
  • Survival counts only through its effect on reproduction.
  • Natural selection favors innate and learned behaviors that increase survival and reproductive success.
  • The learning is not inherited. What can be inherited is the ability to learn.
  • Habituation is one example: ignoring harmless stimuli leaves more time and energy for feeding.
  • Say: "fitness is reproductive success" — not: "fitness is survival or strength".
Quick check

Mission check

The snail and the birds both stop reacting to a stimulus that keeps coming and never hurts. Ignoring it saves time and energy; responding to anything new keeps them safe.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why would an animal spend energy singing, displaying or fighting instead of just feeding?

Make a guess: Why would an animal spend energy singing, displaying or fighting instead of just feeding?

Mission 3

Signals change behavior

  • A signal changes the behavior of another individual.
  • Visual: a peacock's train. Audible: bird song. Tactile: grooming.
  • Chemical: a female moth's scent. Electrical: pulses from weakly electric fish.
  • Triggers can be internal (readiness to breed) or external (a predator approaching).
  • Animals use signals to show dominance, find food, hold territory and win mates.
  • Plants exchange information too: a plant eaten by caterpillars releases airborne chemicals, and nearby plants raise their defenses.
  • Say: "the signal spreads because it increases survival or reproduction" — not: "the animal signals because it wants to help".
Mission 3

Why court?

  • Species recognition: only partners of the same species give and answer the right signals. No gametes wasted on matings that cannot give fertile offspring.
  • Recognize a partner of the opposite sex.
  • Show that the partner is sexually mature and ready: mating is synchronized. Vital when fertilization is external.
  • Choose a high-quality mate.
  • Signals that win mates give some individuals more offspring than others: differential reproductive success.
Quick check
?Female three-spined sticklebacks were given a choice of males. They chose males with a brighter red belly more often. Males infected with parasites had duller red bellies. Under green light, which hides the red, the preference disappeared. Which conclusion is best supported?
Mission 3

Holding a territory

  • Territory: an area an animal defends, usually against members of its own species.
  • It holds resources: food, nest sites, a place to attract mates.
  • Defended mostly by signals: song, scent marks, displays. Threats are far commoner than fights.
  • Costs: energy, time, risk of injury. It pays when the resources are worth more.
  • Great tits: when territory owners were removed and their song played from loudspeakers, new males took longer to move in (Krebs 1977).
Mission 3Read the data

Long tails win mates

Andersson (1982), long-tailed widowbirds, Kenya. Males have tails about half a meter long.

  • Four groups of 9 territory-holding males:
  • (1) tails shortened; (2) tails cut and glued back at the same length; (3) tails untouched; (4) tails lengthened with the pieces cut from group 1.
  • Before the change: the groups had similar numbers of nests (females) on their territories.
  • After: males with lengthened tails gained significantly more new nests than males in the other groups.
  • Conclusion: females choose males with longer tails. That is sexual selection by female choice.
Quick check
?In Andersson's experiment, why was a group included whose tails were cut and glued back at the same length?
Mission 3

Living in groups

  • Advantages: more eyes to spot predators; each animal less likely to be the one caught; group defense.
  • Advantages: hunting together catches bigger prey; shared care of young; information about food.
  • Disadvantages: competition for food and mates; faster spread of disease and parasites.
  • Disadvantages: large groups are easier for predators to find.
  • Herds, flocks, schools and packs: cooperation tends to increase the fitness of the individual and the survival of the population.

Mission 3

Helping relatives

  • Some behaviors cost the individual but help others: alarm calls, sharing food, helping to raise young.
  • Kin selection: relatives share many alleles. If helping relatives survive and reproduce, alleles for helping are passed on, even when the helper pays a cost.
  • Cooperation can also raise an individual's own fitness: more food per hunter, earlier warning of predators.
  • Say: "a behavior spreads when it increases the passing on of alleles for it" — not: "it is done for the good of the species".
Quick check
?In a hypothetical species of social rodent, an individual that gives an alarm call when a predator approaches is more likely to be attacked than individuals that stay silent. Individuals give alarm calls mostly when close relatives are nearby. Which explanation best accounts for how alarm calling can persist in the population?
Mission check

?Males of two related fiddler crab species live on the same mudflat. Each male waves one enlarged claw in a rhythm typical of his own species. Waving uses energy and draws the attention of birds that eat crabs. Females approach only males waving in their own species' rhythm. Which explanation best accounts for waving persisting?

Singing, displaying and fighting cost energy and risk, but they win mates, territory or rank. An individual that leaves more offspring passes on the alleles for the behavior.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Your core temperature barely moves in snow or in sun. How does your body manage that, and at what cost?

Make a guess: Your core temperature barely moves in snow or in sun. How does your body manage that, and at what cost?

Mission 4

Make heat or borrow it

  • Endotherms (mammals, birds) use heat released by their own metabolism to keep core temperature nearly constant.
  • Ectotherms (e.g. lizards, fish, insects) gain heat mainly from their surroundings; they have little internal control.
  • Cost: an endotherm needs far more food than an ectotherm of the same mass.
  • Benefit: an endotherm stays active in the cold; an ectotherm slows down.
Mission 4

Heat: the unavoidable by-product

  • No metabolic reaction transfers energy with 100% efficiency.
  • Part of the energy of every reaction is released as heat.
  • So every organism makes some heat. Endotherms depend on it to hold body temperature.
  • In colder surroundings heat is lost faster, so an endotherm must raise its metabolic rate.
Mission 4

Behavior does the work

  • Ectotherms control body temperature mainly by behavior.
  • To warm: bask in the sun; turn the body side-on to it; press against warm rock.
  • To cool: move into shade or a burrow; face the sun head-on; be active at cooler times of day.
  • Some aggregate with others.

Mission 4

The energy budget

  • Organisms use energy to stay organized, grow, reproduce and maintain homeostasis.
  • Even at rest, cells use energy all the time, e.g. for active transport.
  • Net gain of energy → energy stored, growth, more offspring.
  • Net loss of energy → loss of mass, fewer offspring and, eventually, death.
  • Endotherms spend much of their budget on heat, so a food shortage hits them fast.
Quick check
?An adult lizard of a hypothetical species is kept alone in a cage at a constant 25 °C. It is not growing or reproducing and moves very little, yet it slowly loses mass if it is not fed. Which statement best explains why the lizard continues to use energy?
Mission check

?In a population of a hypothetical songbird, a cold spring reduces the number of insects the birds eat for six weeks, so that most adults take in less energy each day than they use. Which prediction about the adults is best supported?

Keeping warm is paid for in food; when the food runs short, growth and offspring are cut first.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Energy flows along every food chain but never comes back. So where does it start, and where does it go?

Make a guess: Energy flows along every food chain but never comes back. So where does it start, and where does it go?

Mission 5

Energy in, energy out

  • Sunlight is the energy source for most ecosystems.
  • Producers (plants, algae) capture light and fix carbon dioxide from the air or water into organic compounds.
  • That energy is now chemical energy in carbon compounds.
  • It passes along food chains when one organism eats another.
  • At every step, respiration releases energy that leaves as heat. Heat cannot be captured again.
  • Decomposers feed on dead matter and feces, and respire too.
  • So energy flows through an ecosystem, one way. It must be resupplied all the time.
  • Say: "energy is transferred, and lost as heat" — not: "energy is used up".
Mission 5

Food chains and food webs

  • Food chain: one feeding pathway, e.g. grass → rabbit → fox.
  • Each arrow points from the food to the feeder: the direction energy and biomass move.
  • Food web: many linked chains in one community.
  • Say: "the arrow means 'is eaten by'" — not: "the arrow means 'eats'".
Mission 5

Trophic levels

  • A trophic level is a feeding position in a food chain.
  • Level 1: producers. Level 2: primary consumers (herbivores). Level 3: secondary consumers. Level 4: tertiary consumers.
  • Level 5: quaternary consumers. Decomposers feed on dead matter from every level.
  • An animal with a varied diet sits at different levels in different chains.
  • Count from the producer: the producer is not a consumer.
Quick check
?In a garden, blackbirds eat berries from shrubs and also eat snails, which feed on the leaves of the same shrubs. Which trophic level or levels does the blackbird occupy?
Mission 5

Autotrophs and heterotrophs

  • Autotrophs make carbon compounds from inorganic substances, using an external energy source.
  • Photoautotrophs: energy from light (photosynthesis).
  • Chemoautotrophs: energy from oxidizing inorganic substances (chemosynthesis).
  • Example: vent microorganisms oxidize H2S. Some chemosynthesis can occur without O2.
  • Exceptions to sunlight: some ecosystems in caves and in the deep ocean (e.g. around vents), beyond the reach of light, where chemoautotrophs are the producers.
  • Heterotrophs take in carbon compounds made by other organisms.
  • Heterotrophs include herbivores, carnivores, omnivores, scavengers and decomposers. They metabolize carbohydrates, lipids and proteins for energy.
Quick check
?At a deep-sea hydrothermal vent, where no sunlight reaches, microorganisms obtain energy by oxidizing hydrogen sulfide (H2S) released from the vent and use it to make organic molecules from CO2. Tube worms and clams at the vent depend on these microorganisms. Which statement best describes the microorganisms?
Mission 5

Feeding on the dead

  • Dead leaves, dead bodies and feces still hold chemical energy in carbon compounds.
  • Decomposers (many fungi and bacteria) are saprotrophs: they secrete enzymes onto dead matter, digest it outside their cells, then absorb the products.
  • Detritivores (e.g. earthworms, woodlice) ingest dead matter and digest it inside.
  • Decomposers respire the carbon compounds: CO2 returns to the air; energy leaves as heat.
  • Say: "feces still contain energy" — not: "feces are energy-free waste".
Quick check
?A bracket fungus grows on a fallen tree trunk. It releases enzymes that digest the wood, absorbs the products, and uses them to grow. Which statement correctly classifies the fungus?
Mission 5

Energy flows; matter cycles

  • Energy: in as light, out as heat. One way.
  • Matter (carbon, nitrogen, water …): recycled. Decomposers return elements to the soil, water and air.
  • Matter is conserved: atoms are rearranged, never made or destroyed.
  • Energy is not recycled: the system needs light entering all the time.
Quick check
?A student seals soil, water, a small plant, and some soil animals in a clear glass container and places it where it receives light. After three months the plant has grown, and the mass of the sealed container and its contents is unchanged. Which reasoning best accounts for both observations?
Mission check

?In autumn, an oak tree drops its leaves. Over the next year, fungi and bacteria break the leaves down. What happens to the carbon and to the energy that were in the leaves?

Matter goes round; energy goes through. Sunlight in, heat out: that is why every food chain needs the sun, or another outside source, all the time.

Mission 6

Mission 6: A caterpillar eats a whole leaf. Why does so little of that leaf end up as caterpillar?

Make a guess: A caterpillar eats a whole leaf. Why does so little of that leaf end up as caterpillar?

Mission 6

Gross and net primary production

  • Producers use light energy to fix CO2 into sugars.
  • Primary productivity: the rate at which producers capture energy and store it in organic matter, per area per time.
  • Say: "producers capture and store energy" — not: "producers make energy".
Mission 6

Where a consumer's food goes

  • Energy in the food eaten (ingested): I.
  • Part is never absorbed: lost in feces (egestion).
  • Most of the absorbed energy is respired (R) and lost as heat.
  • Only the rest becomes new biomass: the consumer's net production.
  • Only energy stored in new biomass can pass to the next trophic level.
  • Example (illustrative): a bullock eats grass holding 100 kJ; 64 kJ is lost in feces and urine; R = 32 kJ. Net production = 100 − (64 + 32) = 4 kJ.

Mission 6

Why so much is lost

  • Not eaten: roots, wood, bones. Dead remains go to decomposers.
  • Not digested: lost in feces. Cellulose is hard to digest, so herbivores lose much more this way than carnivores.
  • Respired: every organism, plants included, respires; most absorbed energy ends up as heat.
  • Endotherms (mammals, birds) respire far more to keep warm, so less of their food becomes biomass.
  • Say: "energy is lost to the surroundings as heat" — not: "energy is used up".
Mission 6

An animal's energy budget

  • Every cell uses energy all the time to stay organized, even at rest.
  • Endotherms (birds, mammals) use heat from metabolism to hold a steady body temperature: a high energy cost.
  • Ectotherms lack efficient internal mechanisms. They regulate temperature by behavior: moving into sun or shade, or huddling.
  • Net gain of energy → storage, growth, more offspring.
  • Net loss of energy → loss of mass, fewer offspring and, eventually, death.
  • Reproductive strategy can track energy: some organisms switch between asexual and sexual reproduction as food supply changes.
Quick check
?A hypothetical lizard and a hypothetical mouse of the same body mass are kept at 20 °C. Over a week, the mouse eats several times as much food as the lizard. Which statement best explains this?
Quick check
?The model shows the life cycle of a hypothetical species of water flea in which reproduction changes with food availability. According to the model, how would the genetic variation among the offspring produced in a pond change when food becomes scarce?
  • Model, food plentiful: adult female → eggs made by mitosis develop unfertilized → female offspring → adult female.
  • Model, food scarce: adult female → males produced; eggs made by meiosis → eggs fertilized by sperm → resting eggs hatch when food returns → adult female.
Mission check

?A wood mouse eats seeds containing 500 kJ of energy. 100 kJ is lost in feces and urine, and 380 kJ is released in respiration. What percentage of the energy it ate is available to an owl that eats the mouse?

Like the caterpillar, the mouse loses most of its food in waste and as heat from respiration. Only the small remainder becomes body, and only that can feed the next level.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Responses that keep an animal in favorable conditions increase its chance of survival: a taxis is directional; a kinesis changes rate, not direction.
  • A choice chamber plus a chi-squared test shows whether a preference is significant.

Mission 2

  • Habituation: a learned, stimulus-specific drop in response to a repeated, harmless stimulus; it saves time and energy.
  • Selection favors innate and learned behaviors that raise reproductive success; learning itself is not inherited.

Mission 3

  • Courtship lets animals recognize their own species and a ready, high-quality mate: costly signals persist because they raise reproductive success.
  • Signals can be visual, audible, tactile, electrical or chemical; cooperation and kin selection can increase fitness.

Mission 4

  • Endotherms make their own heat by metabolism at a high energy cost; ectotherms rely on surroundings and behavior.

Mission 5

  • Energy enters as light (or, for chemoautotrophs, from oxidizing inorganic substances), passes along food chains in carbon compounds, and leaves as heat; it is never recycled.
  • Trophic level is a feeding position; one species can occupy several. Decomposers feed on dead matter from every level.

Mission 6

  • Consumers lose energy in feces, urine and, above all, as heat from respiration; only new biomass passes on.
  • A net energy gain allows storage, growth and reproduction; a net loss leads to lost mass, fewer offspring and death.
