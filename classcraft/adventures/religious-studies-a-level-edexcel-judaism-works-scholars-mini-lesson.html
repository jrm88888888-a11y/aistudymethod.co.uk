Mission 1

Maimonides writes a list

  • Moses ben Maimon, called Maimonides or Rambam (1138–1204): rabbi, legal scholar, philosopher, physician.
  • Born in Córdoba, Spain; his family fled persecution; he settled in Fustat (Old Cairo), Egypt.
  • In his Commentary on the Mishnah (Arabic, completed c. 1168) he reached Mishnah Sanhedrin 10:1: "All Israel have a share in the world to come" (paraphrase).
  • Who counts as "Israel" here? His answer: anyone who holds thirteen foundations (Hebrew yesodot) of faith.
  • They are known as the Thirteen Principles of Faith.
Mission 1

Thirteen principles, three groups

  • About God (1–5): God exists and causes all · is one · has no body · is eternal · alone is to be worshipped.
  • About revelation (6–9): prophecy · Moses the greatest prophet · the Torah from heaven · the Torah unchanging.
  • About God and humanity (10–13): God knows our deeds · reward and punishment · the Messiah · resurrection of the dead.
  • Principles 6–13: a later mission (see Mission 2).
  • Robinson (Anthology extract 3) places Maimonides in a line of Jewish philosophers who sought to bring Torah and reason together (paraphrase).
Mission 1

Principles 1 and 2: God exists; God is one

  • 1. Existence: God exists, is perfect, and is the cause of everything else that exists. Everything depends on God; God depends on nothing.
  • Root: "I am the LORD thy God" (Exodus 20:2), which Maimonides counts as the first commandment: to know that God exists.
  • 2. Unity: God is one, but not one as a pair has one member, nor one as a species, nor one as a body made of parts (paraphrase).
  • God's unity is unique: nothing else is one in this way.
  • Root: "HEAR, O ISRAEL: THE LORD OUR GOD, THE LORD IS ONE." (Deuteronomy 6:4, JPS 1917)
Mission 1Read the source

Principle 3: no body

> "for ye saw no manner of form on the day that the LORD spoke unto you in Horeb out of the midst of the fire" (Deuteronomy 4:15, JPS 1917)

  • 3. Incorporeality: God has no body, no shape, and nothing that happens to bodies (moving, resting, being in a place).
  • So what about God's "hand" or "eyes"? Maimonides uses the Talmud's rule: "The Torah speaks in the language of human beings" (paraphrase).
  • These words are figurative: "hand" means power; "eyes" means knowledge.

Q1 What did the Israelites not see at Horeb (Sinai)?

Show answer

Any form: they heard a voice but saw no shape.

Q2 Why does the Torah still use body words for God?

Show answer

To speak in a way ordinary people understand.

Mission 1

Principles 4 and 5: eternal; worship God alone

  • 4. Eternity: God is first: before everything, without beginning. Later versions add "and last".
  • Roots: "The eternal God is a dwelling-place" (Deuteronomy 33:27, JPS 1917); "I am the first, and I am the last" (Isaiah 44:6, JPS 1917).
  • 5. Worship God alone: prayer and praise go to God only.
  • Not to angels, stars or forces of nature, and not through them as go-betweens.
  • Root: "Thou shalt have no other gods before Me." (Exodus 20:3, JPS 1917)
  • Principle 5 is about practice: what a Jew may do in prayer, not only what a Jew thinks.
Mission 1

Speaking about God: negative attributes

  • In the Guide for the Perplexed (Arabic, c. 1190), Part I, chapters 50–60, Maimonides asks what words can truly say about God.
  • Positive attributes ("God is wise", "God is powerful") would divide God into parts, against principle 2.
  • So we can say only what God is not: "God exists" means God is not non-existent; "God is powerful" means God is not weak (paraphrase).
  • Each negation removes a wrong idea, and brings us a little closer.
  • Attributes of action: "merciful" and "gracious" (Exodus 34:6) describe what God does, not what God is.
  • The highest praise is silence: he reads Psalm 65 as "to Thee silence is praise" (Psalm 65:2 in Hebrew numbering, paraphrase).

Mission 1

Roots, development, living Judaism

  • Biblical roots: Exodus 20:2–3; Deuteronomy 4:15, 6:4, 33:27; Isaiah 44:6.
  • Maimonides' development: turns these into defined beliefs that mark who is inside Judaism; adds philosophical precision (unique unity; no body at all; negative attributes).
  • Living Judaism: the hymn Yigdal (c. 14th century) sings all thirteen at the start or end of services in many synagogues.
  • Ani Ma'amin ("I believe with perfect faith…"), printed in many prayer books after the morning service, turns each principle into a sentence of faith.
  • Principles 1–5 are the least disputed today: almost all Jewish movements affirm one God, beyond the world, to be worshipped alone.
Mission 1Diversity

Who disagreed about principles 1–5?

  • Raavad (Abraham ben David of Posquières, c. 1125–1198) glossed Maimonides' code: people "greater and better" than Maimonides had read verses literally and thought God has a body; why call them heretics? (paraphrase). Raavad himself did not hold that God has a body.
  • So the dispute was about the status of the belief: a wrong idea, or heresy?
  • Kabbalah (from the late 12th and 13th centuries) speaks of the sefirot, ten emanations of God; critics said this risks dividing God. Kabbalists answered that God (Ein Sof) stays one.
  • Progressive thinkers: Mordecai Kaplan (1881–1983) re-described God as the power in the world that makes for human fulfilment, not a supernatural person.
  • Cohn-Sherbok (Judaism: History, Belief and Practice, 2003) presents the principles as the best-known summary of Jewish belief, while noting that later thinkers disputed them (paraphrase).

Mission 2

Principle 6: prophecy

  • 6. Prophecy: God communicates with chosen human beings, the prophets.
  • Root: "the Lord GOD will do nothing, but He revealeth His counsel unto His servants the prophets" (Amos 3:7, JPS 1917).
  • In the Guide (II.32–48) Maimonides explains prophecy as the highest human perfection: God's influence reaches a person's intellect and then their imagination.
  • A prophet must be wise and morally perfected; even then, God may withhold prophecy.
  • Your spec stresses the importance of prophecy: it is how the Torah, and so Jewish law, reached Israel.
Mission 2Read the source

Principle 7: Moses

> "if there be a prophet among you, I the LORD do make Myself known unto him in a vision, I do speak with him in a dream. My servant Moses is not so; … with him do I speak mouth to mouth" (Numbers 12:6–8, JPS 1917)

  • 7. Moses is the greatest of all prophets, before or after him.
  • Maimonides lists four differences (paraphrase): Moses received prophecy awake, not in a dream; directly, not through an angel; without terror; and whenever he chose.
  • "And there hath not arisen a prophet since in Israel like unto Moses" (Deuteronomy 34:10, JPS 1917).

Q1 How does God speak to other prophets, according to Numbers 12?

Show answer

In a vision or a dream.

Q2 Why does principle 7 matter for principle 8?

Show answer

If Moses' prophecy is unique, no later prophet can overrule the Torah he received.

Mission 2

Principles 8 and 9: the Torah from heaven, unchanging

  • 8. Torah from heaven (Torah min ha-shamayim): the whole Torah we now have was given by God to Moses.
  • Every verse is equally divine: a list of Esau's descendants (Genesis 36) as much as "Hear, O Israel" (Maimonides' own example, paraphrase).
  • The Oral Torah, the explanation of the commandments, also comes from God.
  • 9. Unchanging: the Torah will not be replaced, and nothing will be added or taken away. Root: Deuteronomy 12:32 (13:1 in Hebrew numbering).
  • Context: many Christian and Muslim teachers of his time held that a later revelation completed or replaced the Torah; principle 9 denies this.

Mission 2

Principles 10 and 11: God knows; reward and punishment

  • 10. God knows human deeds and thoughts: "He that fashioneth the hearts of them all, that considereth all their doings" (Psalm 33:15, JPS 1917).
  • 11. Reward and punishment: God rewards those who keep the commandments and punishes those who break them.
  • The greatest reward is the world to come (olam ha-ba); the greatest punishment is for the soul to be cut off (karet).
  • But Maimonides adds: serve God out of love, not for reward. He cites Antigonus: be not like servants who serve their master for a reward (Pirkei Avot 1:3, paraphrase).
  • His image: a child is given sweets to learn; an adult learns for the sake of truth (paraphrase).
Mission 2

Principles 12 and 13: the Messiah and resurrection

  • 12. The Messiah will come: "though it tarry, wait for it" (Habakkuk 2:3, JPS 1917). Do not calculate the date. (Full picture in a later lesson.)
  • 13. Resurrection of the dead (techiyat ha-metim): "many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake" (Daniel 12:2, JPS 1917).
  • Maimonides stressed a spiritual world to come for souls, so some accused him of denying bodily resurrection.
  • His reply, the Treatise on Resurrection (1191): resurrection is real, a miracle, a foundation of the Torah; the risen will later die, and the final reward is for the soul (paraphrase).

Mission 2

How his contemporaries received the principles

  • Raavad (c. 1125–1198) criticised Maimonides' code for calling people heretics over beliefs (e.g. a bodily God) held by sincere, learned Jews (see Mission 1).
  • Critics such as Samuel ben Ali, head of the academy in Baghdad, and Meir Abulafia of Toledo questioned his view of resurrection.
  • After his death, the Maimonidean controversy (1230s): some rabbis in Provence banned the Guide and the first book of his code; copies were burned in Montpellier (c. 1233).
  • Others defended him fiercely; Nachmanides (1194–1270) tried to calm the dispute.
  • So the principles were admired and contested from the start.
Mission 2

Later critics: Crescas, Albo, Abarbanel

  • Hasdai Crescas (c. 1340–1411), Or Adonai (1410): you cannot be commanded to believe that God exists; it is the presupposition of every command. He also sorted beliefs into different kinds.
  • Joseph Albo (c. 1380–1444), Sefer ha-Ikkarim (1425): only three roots — God's existence, revelation, reward and punishment. The Messiah is a belief, not a root.
  • Isaac Abarbanel (1437–1508), Rosh Amanah: defended Maimonides, but concluded that every teaching of the Torah is equally binding, so none is a "principle" above the rest (paraphrase).
  • The debate: is Judaism defined by beliefs, or by a Torah to be lived?
Mission 2Diversity

The principles today

  • Orthodox: the principles mark the boundary of Orthodox belief; Yigdal and Ani Ma'amin are in Orthodox prayer books. Some Orthodox scholars note that respected authorities disputed individual principles in the past.
  • Masorti / Conservative: God, Torah and Israel affirmed; revelation includes a human element, so principle 8 is reinterpreted.
  • Reform: the Pittsburgh Platform (1885) rejected bodily resurrection and a personal Messiah (a Messianic Age instead), and saw the Torah as inspired but written by people. Reform versions of Yigdal change the resurrection line.
  • Reconstructionist: Kaplan rejected a supernatural revelation altogether.
  • Principles 1–5 unite most Jews; 8, 12 and 13 divide them most.
Mission 2Evaluate

Does Judaism need a creed?

  • Claim: Maimonides was right to define Judaism by a list of beliefs.
  • For: A clear list protects Jews from error (e.g. a bodily God) and gives them unity.
  • For: Mishnah Sanhedrin 10:1 already names beliefs (resurrection, Torah from heaven); Maimonides only made them explicit.
  • Against: Biblical and rabbinic Judaism stresses practice and covenant; emunah means trust more than assent (a case argued by Menachem Kellner, Must a Jew Believe Anything?, 1999, paraphrase).
  • Against: Crescas, Albo and Abarbanel disagreed about the list; Moses Mendelssohn (1729–1786) held that Judaism reveals laws, not dogmas.
  • Judgement criteria: Does it fit the Tanakh and the rabbis? Does it unite or divide Jews today? Does it explain why Jews keep the mitzvot?

Mission 3

Three great works

  • Maimonides (Rambam, 1138–1204): rabbi, philosopher and court physician in Fustat (Old Cairo).
  • Commentary on the Mishnah (Arabic, c. 1168): explains each Mishnah; includes the Thirteen Principles (see Mission 1).
  • Mishneh Torah (Hebrew, c. 1180): a complete code of Jewish law, in 14 books.
  • Guide for the Perplexed (Arabic, c. 1190): philosophy for a Jew torn between Torah and reason.
  • One aim runs through all three: to know God truly, and to live by the Torah with understanding.
Mission 3

The Mishneh Torah

  • Means "Repetition of the Torah"; also called Yad ha-Chazakah, "the Strong Hand" (yad = 14 in Hebrew letters: 14 books).
  • Covers all of Jewish law, including Temple laws not practised since 70 CE.
  • Clear Mishnaic Hebrew; gives rulings without sources or debates.
  • His aim (paraphrase): read the Written Torah, then this book, and you know the whole Oral Torah.
  • Book 1, the Book of Knowledge, opens with God, the universe and how to love God.
  • Critics: Raavad and others feared readers would skip the Talmud and could not check his rulings.
  • Influence: a foundation of Joseph Karo's Shulchan Arukh (1565).
Mission 3

The Guide for the Perplexed

  • Written as letters to a student, Joseph ben Judah, perplexed because Torah and philosophy seemed to clash.
  • Arabic, Hebrew letters; quickly translated into Hebrew (Samuel ibn Tibbon, 1204) and Latin.
  • Method: reason and Torah cannot truly conflict, since both come from God.
  • Where a verse seems to clash with what is proved, read the verse figuratively (e.g. God's "hand").
  • Written deliberately in hints: deep matters are for the prepared, not for everyone.
Mission 3

Proving that God exists

  • Guide II.1: four proofs, built on Aristotle's physics (paraphrase):
  • Motion: every movement needs a mover; the chain cannot go back for ever; so there is a first mover, not a body.
  • Necessary existence: things that can cease to exist depend on something that cannot: a necessary being.
  • Potential to actual: what is only potential needs something actual to change it; ultimately something wholly actual.
  • A fourth proof, from things made of parts, reaches the same end.
  • His strategy: he grants, for argument's sake, that the world is eternal, so the proofs work whether or not it was created.
  • The God proved is one and without body: principles 1–3 of.
Mission 3

Creation: where Maimonides rejects Aristotle

  • Guide II.13: three views of the world's origin:
  • Torah of Moses: God brought the world, and time itself, into being from nothing.
  • Plato: God shaped the world from eternal matter.
  • Aristotle: the world is eternal and flows from God by necessity.
  • Maimonides: Aristotle never proved eternity; the arguments on both sides are not conclusive.
  • If eternity had been proved, he would read Genesis figuratively, as he does God's body (Guide II.25, paraphrase).
  • Since it has not, he accepts creation: a God who wills can give a Law and work miracles; an eternal, necessary world leaves no room for either.
  • This is the rejection of Greek philosophy your spec names: not of reason, but of Aristotle where he goes beyond proof.

Mission 3Read the source

Nature reveals God's glory

Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Foundations of the Torah 2:2 (paraphrase):

  • How does a person come to love and fear God?
  • By contemplating God's great and wonderful works and creatures, and seeing in them God's wisdom, which has no limit.
  • At once the person loves, praises and longs to know God, as David said:

> "My soul thirsteth for God, for the living God" (Psalm 42:2, JPS 1917)

  • Then, reflecting more, the person draws back in awe, knowing how small and dark they are before God's knowledge:

> "When I behold Thy heavens, the work of Thy fingers, the moon and the stars, which Thou hast established; What is man, that Thou art mindful of him?" (Psalm 8:3–4, JPS 1917)

Q1 Which response comes first, love or awe?

Show answer

Love: wonder at God's wisdom.

Q2 What makes the person feel small?

Show answer

Comparing their own knowledge with the wisdom shown in creation.

Mission 3

Science as a path to God

  • For Maimonides, studying nature (physics, astronomy, biology) is not a distraction from religion: it is a commandment, part of loving God.
  • The rabbis' "Account of Creation" (Ma'aseh Bereshit) he identifies with physics; the "Account of the Chariot" (Ma'aseh Merkavah) with metaphysics.
  • He used the best science of his day (Aristotle's, Ptolemy's astronomy) and wrote medical works himself.
  • Glory of God: the order of nature shows God's wisdom: "How manifold are Thy works, O LORD! In wisdom hast Thou made them all" (Psalm 104:24, JPS 1917).
  • Today: many Jews see scientific study as a way to wonder at creation; some worry that science replaces wonder with explanation.

Mission 3

Genesis 1: Rashi and Maimonides compared

  • Rashi: asks what the words plainly mean. "In the beginning" is "in the beginning of God's creating"; the verse does not teach the order of creation. The Torah starts with creation to show the earth is God's to give.
  • Maimonides: Genesis 1 is the Account of Creation: physics in the form of a parable, for the many to read and the few to understand (Guide II.29–30, paraphrase).
  • "Beginning" cannot be the first moment in time, since time itself was created.
  • The rabbis taught that heaven and earth were created together, then separated: the days give an order of nature, not only a sequence of 24-hour periods (paraphrase).
  • Shared: God is the creator; the world is not eternal by necessity; the Torah is true.
  • Different: Rashi aims at clarity for every reader; Maimonides at hidden philosophical truth for the prepared.
Mission 3

Strengths and weaknesses within Judaism

  • Strengths: codified the whole of Jewish law; showed that faith and reason can stand together; defended Judaism in the language of the philosophers.
  • Ethics: the middle way between extremes (Mishneh Torah, Laws of Character 1:4); his Epistle to Yemen (1172) comforted persecuted Jews and warned against false messiahs.
  • Weaknesses: philosophy for the few seemed elitist; rulings without sources; his Aristotelian science (heavenly spheres) is now outdated; he sparked bitter controversy (see Mission 2).
  • Prophecy (see Mission 2): his rational account made Moses unique and the Torah unchangeable.
  • Cohn-Sherbok and Robinson (Anthology extract 3) both survey his achievements and the controversies his work caused (paraphrase). Use also extract 4 (Goodman) on reason and faith in Jewish philosophy.
  • "From Moses to Moses there was none like Moses": a traditional saying in his praise.
Mission 3Evaluate

Is Maimonides the most complete Jewish scholar?

  • Claim: Maimonides is the most complete Jewish scholar.
  • For: He mastered every field: Mishnah, Talmudic law, philosophy, science, medicine.
  • For: His code still guides halakhah; his principles are in the prayer book.
  • Against: Rashi is more widely studied: every child meets Rashi first.
  • Against: He neglected mysticism (Kabbalah) and poetry; Halevi argued that experience matters more than proof.
  • Against: His philosophy depends on Aristotle's science, now abandoned.
  • Judgement criteria: "Complete" in breadth or in influence? Complete for which Jews (Orthodox, Progressive, Hasidic)? Then or now?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Maimonides (1138–1204) set out Thirteen Principles in his Commentary on the Mishnah; principles 1–5 are about God: exists, one, without body, eternal, alone to be worshipped.
  • Body words for God are figurative: "the Torah speaks in the language of human beings".
  • In the Guide, positive words about God are read as negations or as attributes of action.

Mission 2

  • Principles 6–9 are about revelation (prophecy, Moses, Torah from heaven, Torah unchanging); 10–13 about God and humanity (knowledge, reward and punishment, Messiah, resurrection).
  • Contemporaries and later thinkers (Raavad, Crescas, Albo, Abarbanel) disputed the list; the debate is about belief versus practice.
  • Today Orthodox Jews affirm all thirteen; Progressive Jews reinterpret especially 8, 12 and 13.

Mission 3

  • Maimonides wrote the Commentary on the Mishnah, the Mishneh Torah (a code without sources) and the Guide (philosophy); reason and Torah cannot truly conflict.
  • He proved God on Aristotle's premises but rejected Aristotle's eternal world as unproved; Genesis 1 for him is physics in a parable, unlike Rashi's plain reading.
  • Contemplating nature reveals God's wisdom and glory, leading to love and then awe.
