Mission 1

Radicals

  • A radical is a species with an unpaired electron.
  • A dot marks the unpaired electron: Cl•, •CH₃.
  • The dot is not a charge. These radicals are neutral.
  • Odd number of outer electrons → one is unpaired. •CH₃: 4 + 3 = 7.
  • Radicals are highly reactive: the unpaired electron pairs up by forming a new bond.

Mission 1

Homolytic fission and fish-hook arrows

  • Homolytic fission: a bond breaks with one electron to each atom.
  • Two radicals form, and no ions.
  • A fish-hook arrow (one barb) moves one electron.
  • A curly arrow with two barbs moves an electron pair.
  • For Cl₂: two fish-hooks, each from the Cl–Cl bond to a different Cl atom.

Mission 1

Initiation

  • Initiation: UV light or heat splits the halogen: Br₂ → 2Br•.
  • The halogen–halogen bond breaks because it is the weakest bond present.
  • Data booklet: Br–Br 193 kJ mol⁻¹, C–C 346 kJ mol⁻¹. C–H is stronger still.
  • So initiation makes no H• and no alkyl radical.

Mission 1

Propagation: the chain

  • Propagation: radical + molecule → new molecule + new radical.
  • Br• removes an H atom from cyclohexane: C₆H₁₂ + Br• → •C₆H₁₁ + HBr.
  • Fish-hooks: one electron from Br• and one from the C–H bond form the H–Br bond.
  • The other C–H electron stays on carbon, giving the cyclohexyl radical, •C₆H₁₁.
  • Then •C₆H₁₁ + Br₂ → C₆H₁₁Br + Br•. Br• is regenerated, so the chain repeats.
  • Br• never swaps directly for H, and no H• forms.
Mission 1

Termination and the product mixture

  • Termination: two radicals combine. Radicals are removed; none are made.
  • Br• + Br• → Br₂ · •C₆H₁₁ + Br• → C₆H₁₁Br · •C₆H₁₁ + •C₆H₁₁ → C₁₂H₂₂.
  • Radicals are scarce, so termination is rare: most product forms in propagation.
  • Br• also attacks C₆H₁₁Br, so further substitution gives C₆H₁₀Br₂ and beyond.
  • The result is a mixture of products.

Mission 1

Why alkanes are so unreactive

  • C–C and C–H bonds are strong: breaking them needs a large energy input.
  • C and H have similar electronegativities: the bonds are essentially non-polar.
  • There are no significant δ+ or δ− sites, so polar reagents are not attracted.
  • Only very reactive species, such as radicals, attack alkanes.

Mission 2

Nucleophiles

  • A nucleophile donates both electrons of a new bond to its partner.
  • It needs an available electron pair, usually a lone pair: OH⁻, CN⁻, Cl⁻, Br⁻.
  • No charge needed: H₂O and NH₃ are neutral nucleophiles, with lone pairs on O and N.
  • Its partner, the electrophile, accepts the pair.

Mission 2

Heterolytic fission and curly arrows

  • Heterolytic fission: both bonding electrons go to one fragment, so ions form.
  • H–Br → H⁺ + Br⁻. The pair goes to Br, the more electronegative atom.
  • The fragment that takes the pair becomes negative.
  • A curly arrow (two barbs) moves an electron pair.
  • Start it at the pair's source, a lone pair or a bond. End it where the pair goes.

Mission 2

Nucleophilic substitution

  • In a halogenoalkane the C–X bond is polar: Cδ+–Xδ−.
  • The nucleophile donates a lone pair to the δ+ carbon.
  • The C–X pair moves onto X, which leaves as X⁻: the leaving group.
  • CH₃Cl + OH⁻ → CH₃OH + Cl⁻.
  • Two curly arrows: lone pair on O → C; C–Cl bond → Cl.

Mission 3

Electrophiles

  • An electrophile accepts both electrons of a new bond from a nucleophile.
  • It is electron-deficient: H⁺, NO₂⁺, or the δ+ atom of a polar molecule.
  • No charge needed: HBr (through Hδ+) and Br₂ act as electrophiles.
  • Pair donor = nucleophile. Pair acceptor = electrophile.

Mission 3

Why alkenes attract electrophiles

  • The C=C double bond is a region of high electron density.
  • It attracts electrophiles and donates an electron pair to them.
  • C=C is stronger than C–C. Alkenes react because of electron density, not weakness.
  • The reagent adds across C=C. The double bond becomes single; nothing is lost.
  • This is electrophilic addition.
Mission 3

Three addition reactions

  • Halogen: CH₂=CH₂ + Br₂ → CH₂BrCH₂Br. Fast in the dark, at room temperature.
  • Bromine turns orange-brown → colourless: the test for C=C.
  • Hydrogen halide: C₆H₁₀ + HBr → C₆H₁₁Br (cyclohexene → bromocyclohexane).
  • Water, with an acid catalyst (e.g. H₃PO₄): C₆H₁₀ + H₂O → C₆H₁₁OH (cyclohexanol).
  • With HX or H₂O, H goes to one carbon; X or OH to the other.

Mission 3AHL

Mechanism: alkene + halogen

  • Approaching C=C repels the Br–Br electrons: an induced dipole, Brδ+–Brδ−.
  • Arrow 1: from the C=C bond to Brδ+.
  • Arrow 2: from the Br–Br bond to Brδ−, which leaves as Br⁻.
  • A carbocation forms: a C⁺ with only three bonds.
  • Arrow 3: from a lone pair on Br⁻ to C⁺. Product: CH₂BrCH₂Br.
  • Some books show a three-membered ring, the bromonium ion, in place of C⁺.
Quick checkAHL

Mission 3AHL

Mechanism: alkene + hydrogen halide

  • H–Br is already polar: Hδ+–Brδ−.
  • Arrow 1: from the C=C bond to Hδ+.
  • Arrow 2: from the H–Br bond to Br, which leaves as Br⁻.
  • Arrow 3: from a lone pair on Br⁻ to the carbocation.
  • Cyclohexene + HBr → bromocyclohexane. HCl and HI react the same way.
Quick checkAHL
?Ethene reacts with hydrogen bromide by electrophilic addition. Which describes the curly arrows in the first step of the mechanism?
Mission 3AHL

Mechanism: alkene + water

  • Step 1: the C=C bond donates a pair to H⁺ from the acid. A carbocation forms.
  • Step 2: H₂O donates a lone pair from O to C⁺, giving R–OH₂⁺.
  • Step 3: an O–H bonding pair moves onto O. H⁺ is released; the alcohol forms.
  • H⁺ is used in step 1 and returned in step 3: a catalyst.
  • The nucleophile is neutral H₂O, not OH⁻.
Quick checkAHL
?Hex-3-ene reacts with water in the presence of an acid catalyst. Which sequence describes the mechanism?
Mission 3AHL

Carbocation stability

  • A carbocation has a C⁺ with three bonds and six outer electrons. It is planar.
  • Primary: C⁺ bonded to one alkyl group. Secondary: two. Tertiary: three.
  • Alkyl groups release electron density towards C⁺, spreading the charge.
  • Stability: tertiary > secondary > primary.
Quick checkAHL
?Which carbocation is the most stable?
Mission 3AHL

Which product wins

  • An unsymmetrical alkene can form two carbocations, so two products.
  • H adds to the C=C carbon that already has more H atoms.
  • That gives the more stable carbocation, so X or OH lands on the more substituted carbon.
  • (CH₃)₂C=CHCH₃ + HBr → mainly (CH₃)₂CBrCH₂CH₃, via a tertiary carbocation.
  • This pattern is Markovnikov's rule. Explain it with carbocation stability.

Quick checkAHL

Mission check

?Why are alkenes susceptible to attack by electrophiles?

The electron-rich C=C attracts Br₂, which accepts a pair from it, so addition happens in the dark. An alkane has no such site: it waits for light to make Br• radicals.

Mission 4AHL

Mission 4: Benzene does react with bromine, given a catalyst. Why does a hydrogen get swapped rather than bromine adding?

Make a guess: Benzene does react with bromine, given a catalyst. Why does a hydrogen get swapped rather than bromine adding?

Mission 4AHL

Why benzene substitutes

  • Benzene's six π electrons are delocalized round the ring: extra stability.
  • The ring is electron-rich, so it attracts electrophiles, E⁺.
  • Addition would destroy the delocalized ring. Substitution keeps it.
  • Overall: C₆H₆ + E⁺ → C₆H₅E + H⁺.
Mission 4AHL

The substitution mechanism

  • Arrow 1: from the delocalized ring to E⁺. A C–E bond forms.
  • The intermediate is positive: delocalization over five carbons only, a horseshoe with + inside.
  • The attacked carbon now carries both H and E.
  • Arrow 2: from that C–H bond into the ring. H⁺ leaves; the full ring is restored.
  • Example: E⁺ = NO₂⁺ gives nitrobenzene. How E⁺ forms is not assessed.

Mission 5AHL

Mission 5: Two bromoalkanes share the formula C₄H₉Br. Why do they swap Br for OH by different routes?

Make a guess: Two bromoalkanes share the formula C₄H₉Br. Why do they swap Br for OH by different routes?

Mission 5AHL

SN2: one step

  • SN2: substitution, nucleophilic, bimolecular: two species in the rate-determining step.
  • Typical of primary halogenoalkanes. One concerted step: bonds form and break together.
  • The nucleophile attacks the δ+ carbon from the side opposite the halogen.
  • Transition state [HO···C···Br]⁻: an energy maximum, not an intermediate.
  • Rate = k[RX][OH⁻].
Mission 5AHL

SN2 turns the molecule inside out

  • As the nucleophile bonds, the other three groups flip, like an umbrella in a gale.
  • This is inversion of configuration.
  • One enantiomer of a chiral halogenoalkane gives one enantiomer of product.
  • So SN2 is stereospecific.
Quick checkAHL
?A single enantiomer of 2-bromobutane, CH₃CHBrCH₂CH₃, reacts with aqueous hydroxide ions by the SN2 mechanism. Which statement about this reaction is correct?
Quick checkAHL
?1-bromopropane reacts with OH⁻ by SN2. Which description of the transition state is correct?
Mission 5AHL

SN1: two steps

  • SN1: unimolecular: only RX in the rate-determining step. Rate = k[RX].
  • Typical of tertiary halogenoalkanes.
  • Step 1 (slow): C–X breaks heterolytically, giving a tertiary carbocation and X⁻.
  • Step 2 (fast): the nucleophile donates a lone pair to the planar C⁺.
  • (CH₃)₃CCl + OH⁻ → (CH₃)₃COH + Cl⁻.
  • If that carbon is chiral, attack on either face gives a racemic mixture.
Mission 5AHL

Why the routes differ

  • Tertiary: three alkyl groups stabilize the carbocation and block attack from the back → SN1.
  • Primary: the carbocation is too unstable; the back is open → SN2.
  • Secondary: both mechanisms occur.
  • The 1 and 2 are not step counts: SN1 has two steps, SN2 one.
Mission 5AHL

Energy profiles

  • SN2: one hump, the transition state. There is no intermediate.
  • SN1: two humps, with the carbocation intermediate in the dip between them.
  • The dip lies above the reactants and below both humps.
  • The first hump, the slow step, is the higher one.
Quick checkAHL
?2-bromo-2-methylpropane, (CH₃)₃CBr, reacts with aqueous hydroxide ions by the SN1 mechanism. Which describes the first step?
Quick checkAHL
?Which statement about the mechanisms of nucleophilic substitution of halogenoalkanes is correct?
Mission 5AHL

The leaving group

  • In both mechanisms the C–X bond must break, so its strength matters.
  • Data booklet: C–Cl 324, C–Br 285, C–I 228 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Rate, same R and conditions: RCl < RBr < RI.
  • Bond strength decides, not polarity: C–Cl is the most polar, yet the slowest.

?2-chloro-, 2-bromo- and 2-iodo-2-methylbutane, (CH₃)₂CXCH₂CH₃, each react with aqueous hydroxide ions by SN1, under identical conditions. Data booklet bond enthalpies: C–Cl 324, C–Br 285, C–I 228 kJ mol⁻¹. Which is the order of rate, slowest first?
Mission checkAHL

?1-bromobutane, CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂Br, and 2-bromo-2-methylpropane, (CH₃)₃CBr, are each warmed with aqueous hydroxide ions. Which statement about their mechanisms is correct?

(CH₃)₃CBr forms a stable tertiary carbocation, and its back is crowded, so it reacts by SN1. 1-bromobutane's primary carbocation is too unstable, and its back is open, so it reacts by SN2.

Mission 6: Aluminium chloride contains no hydrogen at all. How can it be an acid?

Make a guess: Aluminium chloride contains no hydrogen at all. How can it be an acid?

Mission 6AHL

Lewis acids and bases

  • Lewis acid: electron-pair acceptor. Lewis base: electron-pair donor.
  • No H needed: in AlCl₃, Al has only six outer electrons, so it accepts a pair.
  • H⁺ is a Lewis acid too: it accepts a lone pair from H₂O, giving H₃O⁺.
  • Every nucleophile is a Lewis base. Every electrophile is a Lewis acid.
  • A bonding pair can be donated: a C=C bond or benzene's ring can act as the base.
Mission 6AHL

Coordination bonds

  • A Lewis base donating a pair to a Lewis acid makes a coordination bond.
  • Both shared electrons came from one atom.
  • Once formed, it is an ordinary covalent bond: all three O–H bonds in H₃O⁺ are identical.
  • In a Lewis formula the donor's lone pair becomes the bond. Show the overall charge.
  • Some books draw it as an arrow, donor → acceptor.

Mission 6AHL

Complex ions

  • Transition element cations accept lone pairs from ligands, forming coordination bonds.
  • Ligands can be neutral (H₂O, NH₃) or negative (Cl⁻, CN⁻).
  • Write the complex in square brackets, with its charge outside.
  • Charge = metal ion charge + sum of ligand charges.
  • [CuCl₄]²⁻: (+2) + 4(−1) = −2. [Ni(NH₃)₆]²⁺: (+2) + 6(0) = +2.

Recap

The big ideas

  • Radicals have an unpaired electron. UV light or heat splits a halogen homolytically: fish-hook arrows.
  • Radical substitution: initiation, propagation (radical regenerated), termination. The product is a mixture.
  • Nucleophiles donate an electron pair; electrophiles accept one. A curly arrow runs from the pair to where it goes.
  • Halogenoalkanes undergo nucleophilic substitution. Alkenes add halogens, hydrogen halides and water.
  • AHLH adds to give the more stable carbocation. Benzene substitutes to keep its ring.
  • AHLSN2: one step, inversion. SN1: via a carbocation. RI reacts fastest.
  • AHLLewis acid = pair acceptor. Complex charge = metal charge + ligand charges.
