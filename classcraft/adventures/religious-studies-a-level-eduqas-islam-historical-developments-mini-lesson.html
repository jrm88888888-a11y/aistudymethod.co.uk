Mission 1

Madinah: the model

  • In Madinah (622–632) the Prophet was prophet, judge, law-giver and commander.
  • Constitution of Madinah: one community (ummah) above tribe; Jewish tribes kept their religion; disputes referred to God and His Messenger.
  • Religious: prayer, zakah and fasting set the rhythm of society. Moral: justice, care for the poor. Political: treaties, defence, consultation.
  • So most Muslims hold that Islam began with no split between religion and state. A minority disagree: Ali Abd al-Raziq (Islam and the Foundations of Governance, 1925) argued the Prophet's mission was spiritual, not the founding of a state.
  • The debate: is Madinah a blueprint to copy, or a set of principles (justice, consultation, accountability) to apply in new forms?
  • Your spec writes shari'a.

Authority and consultation

> "who (conduct) their affairs by mutual Consultation" (Qur'an 42:38, Yusuf Ali)

Mission 1

Sunni theory: a contract

  • After the Prophet, Abu Bakr was chosen by companions and given the bay'ah (pledge of allegiance).
  • Al-Mawardi (974–1058), al-Ahkam al-Sultaniyya (The Ordinances of Government): the imamate (caliphate) is a contract ('aqd) between the caliph and "those who loose and bind" (qualified electors).
  • Caliph's duties: protect the faith, enforce the law, judge, defend the borders, collect zakah, appoint honest officials.
  • Complementary roles: the ruler holds power and enforces; the ulama interpret Shari'a and advise. Neither replaces the other.
  • Obedience: most Sunni scholars counselled obeying even a flawed ruler who keeps the prayer, to avoid civil strife (fitnah). Ibn Taymiyya (1263–1328): government must serve Shari'a (al-Siyasa al-Shar'iyya).
Mission 1

Shi'a theory: the Imam and the ulama

  • Imam: chosen by God, designated by the previous Imam, infallible (ma'sum). The supreme authority in religion and politics.
  • For Twelvers, the twelfth Imam went into Occultation (from 874; the Greater Occultation from 941).
  • In his absence, the ulama act as his general deputies: they judge, teach, receive khums, guide believers.
  • Quietist view: no state is fully legitimate before the Imam returns; scholars guide but do not rule (e.g. Ayatollah Sistani, b. 1930, Najaf).
  • Wilayat al-faqih (guardianship of the jurist): Ayatollah Khomeini (1902–1989), lectures of 1970: a just senior jurist should govern on the Imam's behalf. Basis of Iran's 1979 constitution.

Mission 1

Western codes and secularism

  • In the 19th–20th centuries many reformers identified secularism with modernisation and progress.
  • Ottoman Empire: French-based commercial and penal codes (1850s); the Mecelle (1869–76) codified Hanafi civil law.
  • Turkey: Atatürk abolished the sultanate (1922) and the caliphate (1924), adopted the Swiss civil code (1926), and made the state secular.
  • Egypt: European-style courts (1875, 1883); the civil code of al-Sanhuri (1949) blended French law with Shari'a principles.
  • Iran: Reza Shah (ruled 1925–41) moved law and education away from the clergy.
  • Family law usually stayed Shari'a-based: marriage, divorce, inheritance.
Mission 1

Opposition: God's law, not the West's

  • Critics linked secularism with Western imperialism and moral decline.
  • Hasan al-Banna (1906–1949) founded the Muslim Brotherhood (Egypt, 1928): Islam as a complete way of life, including the state.
  • Abul A'la Mawdudi (1903–1979), Jamaat-e-Islami (1941): sovereignty (hakimiyyah) belongs to God alone; an Islamic state is a "theo-democracy": elected, but bound by Shari'a.
  • Sayyid Qutb (1906–1966), Milestones (1964): societies that replace God's law with human law live in jahiliyyah (ignorance).
  • The Iranian Revolution (1979) put opposition to the secular Shah into power.
  • Many Muslims oppose secularism without endorsing these movements.
Mission 1

Shari'a in modern Muslim states

  • Saudi Arabia: the Qur'an and Sunnah are the constitution (Basic Law, 1992); Hanbali law applied by judges.
  • Iran: Islamic Republic; elected president and parliament under the Supreme Leader and the Guardian Council.
  • Egypt: "principles of Islamic Shari'a are the principal source of legislation" (constitution, from 1980).
  • Pakistan: Islamic Republic (1956); a Federal Shariat Court reviews laws (1980).
  • Turkey: secular constitution. Indonesia: largest Muslim-majority democracy; not an Islamic state (Pancasila), though Aceh applies Shari'a by-laws.

Mission 1

Shari'a in a secular state: Britain

  • Most of Shari'a concerns personal life: prayer, fasting, diet, giving, honest trade. British law already permits these.
  • Shari'a councils (e.g. the Islamic Sharia Council, 1982) issue religious divorces; they have no power in criminal or civil law.
  • Muslim Arbitration Tribunal (2007): binding arbitration in civil disputes, under the Arbitration Act 1996.
  • Archbishop Rowan Williams' lecture (2008) on accommodating some religious law caused fierce debate.
  • Independent review (2018), chaired by Mona Siddiqui: concerns for women's rights; recommended that Islamic marriages also be registered civilly.
  • Minority jurisprudence (fiqh al-aqalliyyat): keep contracts, including the law of the land.
Mission 1

Law schools and democracy

  • Incompatible: law-making belongs to God alone; majority votes cannot permit what God forbids (e.g. Hizb ut-Tahrir, banned in the UK since 2024; Qutb).
  • Compatible: shura (42:38), the bay'ah as consent, and the accountability of rulers make Islam friendly to democracy (e.g. Khaled Abou El Fadl, Islam and the Challenge of Democracy, 2004; Rached Ghannouchi, Tunisia).
  • In between: Mawdudi's theo-democracy; Iran's elected bodies checked by jurists.
Mission 1Evaluate

"Islam is compatible with democracy."

  • For: shura (42:38, 3:159); the first caliphs chosen and pledged to; elections in Indonesia, Pakistan and Tunisia (from 2011); British Muslims vote and stand for office.
  • For: the contract theory of al-Mawardi.
  • Against: sovereignty belongs to God (Mawdudi, Qutb); a majority could vote against Shari'a; the Prophet's authority came from revelation, not votes.
  • Another view: it depends on what "democracy" means: majority rule within fixed moral limits is found in many democracies.
  • Judge it by: Who makes law? Who interprets Shari'a? Can minorities be equal citizens? Is Madinah a blueprint or a set of principles?
  • Your spec issue: "the compatibility of Islam with democracy".

Mission 2

Modern science meets Islam

  • Modern European science reached Muslim lands in the 19th century, often with colonial power (Napoleon in Egypt, 1798; British India).
  • It stressed evidence and reason, natural laws, deep time and evolution.
  • Muslim reformers welcomed it as their own heritage returning:
  • Sayyid Ahmad Khan (1817–1898), founder of the Aligarh college (1875): the Word of God (Qur'an) and the Work of God (nature) cannot contradict.
  • Jamal al-Din al-Afghani (1838/9–1897): answering Ernest Renan (1883), he argued Muslims must master modern science.
  • Others feared science came bundled with materialism and Western dominance.
Mission 2Read the source

Creation in the Qur'an

> "Have not those who disbelieve known that the heavens and the earth were of one piece, then We parted them, and we made every living thing of water?" (Qur'an 21:30, Pickthall)

> "Then turned He to the heaven when it was smoke" (Qur'an 41:11, Pickthall)

> "We have built the heaven with might, and We it is Who make the vast extent (thereof)." (Qur'an 51:47, Pickthall)

  • "Say: O Allah! Owner of Sovereignty! … Thou bringest forth the living from the dead, and Thou bringest forth the dead from the living." (Qur'an 3:26–27, Pickthall)
  • Creation took "six Days" (7:54), but "a Day with Allah is as a thousand years of what ye reckon" (Qur'an 22:47, Pickthall): many Muslims read the days as ages.

Q1 Which verse suggests the heavens and earth began as one thing?

Show answer

21:30: "of one piece, then We parted them".

Q2 Why do some modern translators render 51:47 as "We are expanding it"?

Show answer

Mission 2

Big Bang, Steady State, oscillating universe

  • Big Bang: the universe began about 13.8 billion years ago from a hot, dense state and is expanding.
  • Many Muslims welcome it: a beginning fits God as Originator (2:117); 21:30 and 41:11 seem to describe a single mass and "smoke".
  • Steady State (Hoyle, Bondi and Gold, 1948): an eternal universe with no beginning, matter created continually. Most Muslims see a universe without a beginning as clashing with creation, though some link continual creation to God's constant sustaining (50:15 "a new creation").
  • Oscillating universe: endless cycles of expansion and collapse. Some cite "As We began the first creation, We shall repeat it" (21:104); but endless cycles with no first beginning would clash with creation.
  • 3:26–27: whatever the mechanism, God is Owner of Sovereignty, bringing life from death and night into day.
  • Some Muslims warn against tying the Qur'an to any theory, since theories change.

Mission 2Diversity

Science in Islam today

  • Science is neutral; adopt it: follow evidence; the Qur'an gives values, science gives facts (in the line of Sayyid Ahmad Khan; many Muslim scientists).
  • Scientific miracles: modern discoveries confirm the Qur'an (Bucaille; popular preaching).
  • Islamise knowledge: rebuild the sciences on Islamic principles (Isma'il al-Faruqi, Islamization of Knowledge, 1982).
  • Reject parts: accept technology but reject theories seen as contradicting scripture (e.g. human evolution).
  • Maurice Bucaille (1920–1998), a French doctor, argued in The Bible, the Qur'an and Science (1976) that the Qur'an contains facts no 7th-century person could have known, so it must be from God; critics reply that this reads modern meanings into old words and ties faith to theories that may change.
  • The same range appears in responses to cosmology: from "the Qur'an foretold the Big Bang" to "the Qur'an teaches that God created, not how".

Mission 2Evaluate

Is Islam compatible with modern science?

  • Claim: Islam and modern science are fully compatible.
  • For: the Qur'an commands study of creation (3:190); a beginning fits the Big Bang; many Muslim scientists see no conflict; a rich history.
  • For: God works through causes; evolution or expansion can be God's method.
  • Against: a literal Adam created from clay clashes with common descent; Steady State-type eternity clashes with creation.
  • Against: modern science's naturalism (only natural causes count) can clash with belief in God's constant action and miracles.
  • Judgement criteria: Compatible with the Qur'an, or with a reading of it? With scientific results, or with naturalism as a worldview? Does reading verses as predictions help or harm faith?

Mission 3

One religion, many prophets

  • Islam means "submission" to God. Muslims believe it is the religion of every prophet, from Adam to Muhammad.
  • Abraham, Moses and Jesus are prophets of Islam in this sense: they submitted to the one God.
  • Muslims believe God sent earlier scriptures: Tawrat (to Moses), Zabur (to David), Injil (to Jesus).
  • The Qur'an is the final revelation; Muhammad is the seal of the prophets (33:40).
  • So Islam does not see itself as a new religion, but as the original one restored.
Mission 3Read the source

The one religion

> "The Religion before Allah is Islam (submission to His Will)" (Qur'an 3:19, Yusuf Ali)

> "The same religion has He established for you as that which He enjoined on Noah … and that which We enjoined on Abraham, Moses, and Jesus" (Qur'an 42:13, Yusuf Ali)

> "We make no difference between one and another of them: And we bow to Allah (in Islam)." (Qur'an 2:136, Yusuf Ali)

Q1 Does 3:19 mean the religion founded in 7th-century Arabia, or submission to God in any age?

Show answer

Q2 What does 2:136 say about the earlier prophets?

Mission 3

People of the Book

  • Ahl al-kitab: Jews and Christians, who received earlier scripture. Sabians are named too (2:62). Classical law treated Zoroastrians in a similar way.
  • Shared ground: their food and marriage to their chaste women are lawful for Muslims (5:5). "Our Allah and your Allah is one" (Qur'an 29:46, Yusuf Ali).
  • Call to common terms: "come to common terms as between us and you: That we worship none but Allah" (Qur'an 3:64, Yusuf Ali).
  • Mixed verdicts: some of them "stand (For the right)" (Qur'an 3:113, Yusuf Ali); others are criticised for changing God's word (2:75).
  • Tahrif (alteration): did they change the text of their scriptures, or only its meaning? Muslim scholars differ.

Mission 3

Exclusivism

  • Exclusivism: salvation only through Islam as brought by Muhammad.
  • > "If anyone desires a religion other than Islam (submission to Allah), never will it be accepted of him" (Qur'an 3:85, Yusuf Ali)
  • Many classical scholars: once the message has properly reached someone, salvation requires accepting it.
  • Some reported that 3:85 abrogated the gentler 2:62 (a report from Ibn Abbas in al-Tabari's commentary).
  • Hadith: any Jew or Christian who hears of the Prophet and dies without believing in his message is among the people of the Fire (Muslim; paraphrase).
  • Exclusivism is about salvation, not about how to treat others: many exclusivists also defend kindness and justice to non-Muslims (60:8).
Mission 3

Inclusivism

  • Inclusivism: Islam is the fullest truth, but sincere followers of other faiths may also be saved.
  • Abrahamic: "Those who believe … and those who follow the Jewish (scriptures), and the Christians and the Sabians,- any who believe in Allah and the Last Day, and work righteousness, shall have their reward with their Lord" (Qur'an 2:62, Yusuf Ali). Repeated at 5:69.
  • Different paths: "To each among you have we prescribed a law and an open way" (Qur'an 5:48, Yusuf Ali).
  • Non-Abrahamic: "there never was a people, without a warner having lived among them" (Qur'an 35:24, Yusuf Ali). Some Muslims ask whether figures in other traditions were such warners.
  • Example: the Mughal prince Dara Shikoh (1615–1659), Majma' al-Bahrayn (The Meeting of the Two Seas, 1655), compared Sufi and Hindu Upanishadic ideas.
  • Pluralism goes further: all religions are equally valid paths. Most Muslim inclusivists stop short of this.

Mission 3

Dialogue today

  • Nurcholish Madjid (1939–2005), Indonesian scholar: reads al-islam in 3:19 as the attitude of submission to God, which others can share. Religious diversity is part of God's plan (5:48) (paraphrase).
  • Tariq Ramadan (b. 1962), Swiss scholar, Western Muslims and the Future of Islam (2004): move beyond tolerance to mutual respect and knowledge; European Muslims as full citizens bearing witness by their lives (paraphrase).
Mission 3

Conversion in Islam

  • Becoming Muslim: saying the shahadah sincerely before witnesses. No baptism-like rite.
  • Many converts call themselves reverts: a hadith teaches every child is born in the natural state, fitrah (Bukhari; paraphrase).
  • Da'wah (invitation): "Invite (all) to the Way of thy Lord with wisdom and beautiful preaching" (Qur'an 16:125, Yusuf Ali).
  • No compulsion: forced conversion is invalid (2:256;).
  • Leaving Islam (apostasy): the debate is in Mission 4.
  • Your spec issue: "whether or not there is a coherent approach to conversion in Islam".

Mission 3Evaluate

"Islam is an exclusivist religion."

  • For: 3:85 and the hadith in Muslim; the finality of the Prophet (33:40); classical majority views on salvation after the message has reached someone.
  • Against: 2:62, 5:69 and 5:48; every people had a warner (35:24); the word islam means submission (Madjid); mercy "extendeth to all things" (7:156).
  • Another view: Islam is exclusive about truth but inclusive about mercy: God alone judges who received the message properly.

Mission 4Read the source

No compulsion

> "Let there be no compulsion in religion: Truth stands out clear from Error" (Qur'an 2:256, Yusuf Ali)

> "There is no compulsion in religion. The right direction is henceforth distinct from error." (Qur'an 2:256, Pickthall)

  • Setting (reported from Ibn Abbas, Abu Dawud): some Madinan Muslims had sons raised as Jews and wanted to force them into Islam. The verse forbade it.
  • Related: "wilt thou then compel mankind, against their will, to believe!" (Qur'an 10:99, Yusuf Ali).

Q1 Why can there be no compulsion, according to the verse?

Show answer

Truth is already clear from error; faith must be a free response.

Q2 Why does the setting matter?

Show answer

It shows the verse applied even to Muslims' own children.

Mission 4

How far does 2:256 reach?

  • All agree: forced conversion is invalid, because faith is in the heart.
  • Some classical scholars: 2:256 was abrogated by later verses of war (9:5, 9:29).
  • Others: it was never abrogated, but applies to People of the Book, who may keep their religion under protection.
  • Most modern scholars: a universal principle of freedom of belief, backed by 10:99 and 18:29 ("Let him who will believe, and let him who will, reject (it)" (Qur'an 18:29, Yusuf Ali)).
  • The hard question: does 2:256 also protect a Muslim who leaves Islam?
  • Your spec: "the importance of religious freedom in the history of the Islamic religion".

Mission 4

Apostasy in classical law

  • Ridda: apostasy, leaving Islam. Murtadd: an apostate.
  • Qur'an: condemns apostasy and warns of punishment in the Hereafter (2:217, 4:137). It names no worldly penalty.
  • Hadith: whoever changes his religion is to be killed (Bukhari; paraphrase). Another hadith lists leaving religion and separating from the community among the three cases where life may be taken (Bukhari, Muslim).
  • Classical rulings (the four Sunni schools): an adult, sane, willing male apostate is invited to repent; if he refuses, death. Hanafis: a woman is imprisoned, not executed. Ja'fari law is similar, but stricter for a man born Muslim.
  • Other effects: marriage dissolved; no inheritance from Muslim relatives.
  • Context: early apostasy often meant revolt: the Ridda wars under Abu Bakr (632–633).
Mission 4

Apostasy laws today

  • Death penalty possible: in a few states, e.g. Iran and Saudi Arabia, courts may apply it, though executions for apostasy alone are rare.
  • Civil effects only: e.g. Egypt has no apostasy crime, but in 1995 a court declared the scholar Nasr Hamid Abu Zayd an apostate and dissolved his marriage; he moved to the Netherlands.
  • Traditional view: the classical ruling stands, because the hadith are sound.
  • Reformist view: the penalty was for treason and armed revolt, not change of belief; 2:256 and 18:29 protect belief (e.g. Abdullah Saeed and Hassan Saeed, Freedom of Religion, Apostasy and Islam, 2004).
  • Hadd debate: some scholars hold a fixed penalty cannot rest on a single-chain hadith alone.
Mission 4Evaluate

"Islam supports freedom of belief."

  • For: 2:256, 18:29; no worldly penalty for apostasy in the Qur'an.
  • Against: classical law punished apostasy by death; some states today restrict conversion, blasphemy and minority worship.
  • Another view: distinguish the Qur'an, classical law (fiqh) and state practice: they may answer differently.
  • Judge it by: freedom to hold, to practise, or to change a belief? Which sources carry most weight? Does historical context limit a ruling?

Mission 5

Qur'an and Hadith on men and women

  • Same worth: both are God's servants and trustees (khalifa); in the Qur'an, Adam and his wife both disobey and both repent: "Our Lord! We have wronged ourselves." (Qur'an 7:23, Pickthall). Eve is not blamed alone.
  • Hadith: women are the "twin halves" of men (paraphrase; Sunan Abu Dawud, no. 236); the best of you are those best to their families (paraphrase; Jami' al-Tirmidhi, no. 3895); "your mother" is named three times before "your father" as most deserving of kindness (paraphrase; Sahih al-Bukhari, no. 5971).
  • Different roles (traditional view): men must maintain (nafaqa) wife and family; so in law, shares and duties differ. "they (women) have rights similar to those (of men) over them in kindness, and men are a degree above them" (Qur'an 2:228, Pickthall).
  • Contested texts: a hadith calls women "deficient in intellect and religion" (paraphrase; Sahih al-Bukhari, no. 304). Traditional scholars link it to rules on witness and exemption from prayer during menstruation; many modern Muslims read it as context-bound or question how it is used.
  • So most Muslims hold equal worth before God; they disagree on how far legal differences follow (feminist readings).
Mission 5

Women in early Islam

  • Khadijah (d. 619): a wealthy merchant who employed Muhammad, proposed marriage, and was the first person to accept Islam.
  • 'A'isha (d. 678): one of the main sources of hadith (over two thousand reports), consulted on law and corrected leading Companions.
  • Umm Salama: tradition says her question why the Qur'an spoke of men led to the revelation of 33:35.
  • Nusayba bint Ka'b: fought to defend the Prophet at Uhud (625).
  • Al-Shifa' bint 'Abdullah: taught reading and writing; reported to have supervised the Madinah market under 'Umar.
  • Umm Waraqa: the Prophet let her lead the people of her household in prayer (Sunan Abu Dawud, no. 592; its scope is debated).

Mission 5

The family as foundation

  • Marriage is a sunnah: the Prophet's way, strongly encouraged.
  • "He ordained between you love and mercy." (Qur'an 30:21, Pickthall); spouses are "raiment" (clothing, protection) for each other (2:187).
  • The home is the first school of faith: children learn prayer, the Qur'an, halal food and good character there.
  • Duties: parents raise and educate children; children honour parents, especially in old age (17:23); the extended family supports its members.
  • Traditional roles: the husband provides; the wife is the heart of the home. A wife's own earnings and property remain hers (4:32).
  • Changing roles: many Muslim women now study and work; many couples share earning and childcare; scholars debate whether the duty of maintenance changes when both earn.
Mission 5

Marriage and divorce in law (Surah 4)

  • Nikah is a contract: offer and acceptance, witnesses (required by most Sunni schools), and the bride's consent (a hadith requires it: Sahih al-Bukhari, no. 5136). Most scholars today hold that a forced marriage is invalid; some classical schools let a father marry off a daughter who had never married.
  • Wali (guardian): most schools require the bride's guardian; the Hanafi school allows an adult woman to contract her own marriage.
  • Mahr: "give unto the women (whom ye marry) free gift of their marriage portions" (Qur'an 4:4, Pickthall). It belongs to the wife.
  • Polygyny: up to four wives, on condition of justice (4:3); yet "Ye will not be able to deal equally between (your) wives" (Qur'an 4:129, Pickthall). Many modern scholars and states treat monogamy as the norm; some states ban or restrict polygyny.
  • Divorce: permitted but discouraged. The husband's talaq with a waiting period ('idda, 2:228), revocable twice (2:229); the wife's khul' (she returns the mahr); or a judge's dissolution for harm. First, arbiters from both families should try to reconcile (4:35).

Mission 5

Women in worship

  • Women pray, fast, give zakah and perform Hajj as equal duties; women are excused prayer and fasting during menstruation (fasts made up later).
  • Mosques: "Do not prevent the female servants of Allah from the mosques of Allah" (paraphrase; Sahih al-Bukhari, no. 900). Friday prayer is obligatory for men, optional for women; other hadith praise prayer at home.
  • Rows: men in front, women behind or in a separate area. Some British mosques have had no women's space; campaigns have pushed for change.
  • Leading prayer: a woman may lead women (Shafi'i and Hanbali view; reported of 'A'isha and Umm Salama). Most scholars of every school forbid women leading mixed congregations; a few reformers dissent.
  • Women serve as scholars, Qur'an teachers and preachers to women; female hadith scholars were common in the medieval period.
Mission 5

Religion, culture and change

  • Culture vs religion: forced marriage, "honour"-based violence, denying daughters their inheritance, and lavish dowry from the bride's family (South Asian jahez, unlike the Qur'anic mahr) come from local custom, and most scholars condemn them as against Islamic law.
  • Responses differ: some see this as a return to the rights Islam first gave; others fear secular norms will erode the family.
Mission 5Evaluate

Are women and men equal in Islam?

  • Claim: Islam teaches full equality between men and women.
  • For: equal creation and reward (4:1, 33:35, 16:97); the Qur'an gave women property, inheritance, mahr and consent in the 7th century; early women's leadership (Khadijah, 'A'isha).
  • For: many differences in practice come from culture, not revelation.
  • Against: legal texts give different shares and rights (4:11; men "a degree above", 2:228; talaq mostly in the husband's hand).
  • Against: "equal but different" may still mean unequal in power: critics within and outside Islam argue this.
  • Judgement criteria: Does equality mean identical rights, or equal worth with different roles? Are legal rules fixed or tied to 7th-century conditions? Which counts more: text, history or today's practice?
  • Spec issue: "Whether or not women are equal to men in Islam."

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Madinah united religion and state; Muslims debate whether it is a blueprint or a set of principles (4:59, 42:38).
  • Sunni theory: a contract (al-Mawardi), ruler and ulama in complementary roles; Shi'a theory: the Imam, then the ulama in his absence (quietism or wilayat al-faqih).
  • Modern states range from secular to Islamic; Muslims disagree whether Islam and democracy fit together.

Mission 2

  • Modern science arrived with colonial power; reformers (Sayyid Ahmad Khan, al-Afghani) welcomed it as compatible with Islam.
  • Big Bang: many welcome a beginning (21:30, 41:11, 51:47); most Muslims see Steady State and endless cycles as clashing with creation.
  • Bucaille: the Qur'an anticipated science; critics: reading in, changing theories.

Mission 3

  • Muslims see Islam (submission) as the religion of all prophets (3:19, 42:13, 2:136); Jews and Christians are People of the Book.
  • Exclusivists stress 3:85; inclusivists stress 2:62, 5:48 and 35:24; pluralism goes further than most Muslims accept.

Mission 4

  • 2:256: no compulsion in religion; all agree forced conversion is invalid; its reach to apostasy is debated.
  • Classical law punished apostasy; reformists link the penalty to treason.

Mission 5

  • The Qur'an teaches spiritual equality: one soul (4:1), the same reward (33:35, 16:97); Adam and his wife sin and repent together.
  • Traditional law gives different duties: men maintain; inheritance shares differ (4:11); divorce routes differ (talaq, khul', judicial). Consent and mahr belong to the woman.
