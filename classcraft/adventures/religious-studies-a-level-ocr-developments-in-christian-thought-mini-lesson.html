Mission 1

Augustine and his questions

  • Augustine of Hippo (354–430), North African bishop; the most influential theologian of the Western Church.
  • Confessions (c. 397–400): his own story, including his struggle with desire and his conversion in Milan (386).
  • City of God (413–426): written after the sack of Rome (410); Book 14 is his reading of Genesis 3 (OCR's set reference: XIV.16–26).
  • His question: why does the human will turn from the good it knows? His answers were shaped by his own experience and his debate with Pelagius.
Mission 1Read the source

The divided will (Confessions VIII)

> "Thus did my two wills, one new, and the other old, one carnal, the other spiritual, struggle within me; and by their discord, undid my soul." (Augustine, Confessions VIII.5, trans. Pusey)

> "Give me chastity and continency, only not yet." (Augustine, Confessions VIII.7, recalling a prayer of his youth)

  • Augustine wanted to serve God, but habit and desire held him back.
  • He found his experience in Paul: "For the good which I desire, I don't do; but the evil which I don't desire, that I practise." (Romans 7:19)

Q1 What does Augustine mean by "two wills"?

Show answer

A will turned to God and an older will bound by habit and desire, pulling against each other.

Q2 What does the prayer "not yet" reveal about his will?

Show answer

He wanted to want chastity, but did not yet wholly want it: the will was divided.

Mission 1

Before the Fall: harmony and friendship

  • Augustine taught that in Eden, Adam and Eve lived in perfect harmony with God, each other and their own bodies.
  • Their bond was friendship and love: "their mutual affection was that of faithful and sincere marriage" (City of God XIV.10).
  • Reason ruled desire: the body obeyed the will, as a hand does.
  • Augustine held that had they not sinned, they would have had children without lust, by calm choice (XIV.24, 26).
  • They were able not to sin (posse non peccare): free to choose good, with God's help.
  • Even Adam's sin, he says, came partly from friendship: he "could not bear to be severed from his only companion" (XIV.11).
Mission 1

The Fall: pride before the act

  • Genesis 3: Eve, then Adam, eat the forbidden fruit (Genesis 3:6).
  • For Augustine, the evil act came from an evil will already turned: "the evil act had never been done had not an evil will preceded it" (XIV.13).
  • The root is pride: "the craving for undue exaltation" (XIV.13): the soul makes itself its end, not God.
  • Sin is disobedience: turning from the highest good (God) to a lower good (self).
Mission 1Read the source

After the Fall: shame and lust

> "The man and his wife were both naked, and they were not ashamed." (Genesis 2:25, WEBBE)

> "Their eyes were opened, and they both knew that they were naked." (Genesis 3:7, WEBBE)

  • Augustine: having disobeyed God, they found their own bodies disobeying them: "their disobedience might be punished by fit retribution" (XIV.17).
  • Concupiscence (lust, disordered desire), especially sexual desire, now resists reason and will: the sign and punishment of the Fall (XIV.16–19).
  • Relationships are damaged: shame, blame, selfish desire and domination (Genesis 3:16).

Q1 What changes between Genesis 2:25 and 3:7?

Show answer

Shame enters: they are aware of their nakedness and cover themselves.

Q2 Why, for Augustine, is lust a "fit" punishment?

Show answer

They disobeyed God; now their bodies' desires disobey their own wills.

Mission 1

Original sin: passed on to all

  • Augustine taught original sin: the sinful state every human inherits from Adam: both guilt and a damaged nature.
  • How it is passed on: through sexual intercourse, which since the Fall always involves concupiscence (lust).
  • Biblical basis: "sin entered into the world through one man" (Romans 5:12). Augustine read the Latin as "in whom all sinned": all humanity was "in Adam" (see Points).
  • Also: "I was born in iniquity" (Psalm 51:5).
  • So even infants need baptism, which removes the guilt (though not concupiscence itself).
  • Augustine held that Jesus, conceived without concupiscence, was free of original sin.
Mission 1

Effects on the will

  • Augustine taught that after the Fall humans are not able not to sin (non posse non peccare).
  • The will is not destroyed but bound: it chooses, but it chooses what it loves, and its loves are now selfish (cupiditas).
  • So humans lack true free will: the freedom to choose and do the good.
  • This explains Romans 7 and Augustine's own "two wills".
  • Even good deeds may spring from pride or self-interest.
Mission 1

Effects on societies

> "Accordingly, two cities have been formed by two loves: the earthly by the love of self, even to the contempt of God; the heavenly by the love of God, even to the contempt of self." (City of God XIV.28)

  • For Augustine, the earthly city is ruled by the lust of rule (libido dominandi): desire to dominate others.
  • Every human society is unstable and corrupt: "Justice being taken away, then, what are kingdoms but great robberies?" (IV.4).
  • Government is still needed (to keep order and restrain sin) but can never create a perfect society.
  • The two cities are mixed together in history: the Church on earth is not simply the heavenly city.

Mission 1

Grace and the summum bonum

  • Augustine taught that humans cannot heal themselves. Only grace (God's free, generous, undeserved love) can turn and heal the will.
  • Grace comes first: God moves the will before it can turn to God. Augustine's conversion: "Take up and read" (Confessions VIII.12), then Romans 13:13–14.
  • Grace restores love of God (caritas) in place of love of self.
  • The summum bonum (supreme good): "life eternal is the supreme good" (City of God XIX.4): the enjoyment of God for ever.
  • Final freedom: the saved will be not able to sin (non posse peccare), a greater freedom than Adam's (XXII.30).
  • "Thou madest us for Thyself, and our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee." (Confessions I.1)
Mission 1Diversity

Responses to Augustine

  • Pelagius (c. 354–c. 418): humans can choose good by their own free will; sin spreads by bad example. Condemned at the Council of Carthage (418).
  • Catholic teaching keeps original sin but as a state "contracted", not a personal fault committed (Catechism 404–405); concupiscence remains after baptism.
  • Eastern Orthodox: ancestral sin: humans inherit mortality and a tendency to sin, but not Adam's guilt.
  • Calvin (Institutes II): total depravity: sin affects every part of human nature; strongly Augustinian.
  • Modern critics: evolution makes a single historical first couple hard to accept; many read Genesis 3 as a myth about every human being; feminists criticise the link between sin, sex and women.
Mission 1Evaluate

Pessimistic or optimistic?

  • Pessimistic: humans cannot be morally good unaided; sex is tainted by lust; all societies are corrupt; many are not saved (Augustine's view of predestination).
  • Realistic: explains the divided will, cruelty and corruption better than a rosy view; modern politics and psychology echo his insight into self-interest.
  • Optimistic: humans were created good; sin is a corruption of a good nature, not the nature itself; grace can restore the will to a freedom greater than Adam's.
  • Is a historical Fall needed? Some say the doctrine stands without a literal Adam (sin is universal); others say inherited guilt needs a real first sin.
  • A distinctive human nature? Augustine says yes: made for God and restless without God. Critics: human nature may be shaped mainly by evolution and society.
  • Judgement criteria: Does the view fit human experience? Is inheriting guilt fair? Does grace leave room for moral effort?

Mission 2

Three ways to read the last things

  • Eschatology: Christian teaching on the "last things": death, judgement, heaven, hell (and, for Catholics, purgatory).
  • Christians agree that God judges and that life with God is the goal. They differ on what kind of reality these words name.
  • Three readings, from most literal to least:
  • Places: real locations a person goes to after death, with physical and emotional joy, punishment or cleansing.
  • Spiritual states: not locations, but conditions of the person after death: with God, cut off from God, or being purified.
  • Symbols: pictures of a person's moral and spiritual life on Earth, not of anything after death.
Mission 2

Judgement: at death, or at the end of time?

  • Particular judgement: each person is judged at death. "It is appointed for men to die once, and after this, judgement" (Hebrews 9:27, WEBBE). Jesus to the thief: "today you will be with me in Paradise" (Luke 23:43, WEBBE).
  • Last (general) judgement: all people judged together when Christ returns (Matthew 25:31–32; Revelation 20:12).
  • Catholic teaching holds both: the soul's destiny is fixed at death; the final judgement makes it public and includes the risen body. Orthodox teaching also holds both, while praying that the departed may still be helped before the last judgement.
  • Some Protestants (e.g. Seventh-day Adventists) hold that the dead "sleep" until the last judgement: no judgement at death.
  • Realised reading: judgement happens now, in how people respond to Christ. "He who doesn't believe has been judged already" (John 3:18, WEBBE).
Mission 2

Heaven and hell as places

  • Heaven: Jesus speaks of his Father's house with "many homes" (John 14:2). Revelation pictures a new heaven and a new earth (Revelation 21:1).
  • With the resurrection of the body, heaven is often understood as a real, bodily life in a renewed creation.
  • Augustine (354–430), City of God Book XXII: the saints rise with real, perfected bodies and see God.
  • Hell: Jesus warns of Gehenna, the valley of Hinnom outside Jerusalem, remembered for child sacrifice (Jeremiah 7:31), used as a picture of punishment (Mark 9:47–48).
  • The rich man and Lazarus (Luke 16:19–31): two places after death, with "a great gulf fixed" between them (16:26).
  • Many Christians through history read these literally: actual places, actual fire.

Mission 2

Heaven and hell as spiritual states

  • Heaven as the beatific vision: seeing God directly. "Now we see in a mirror, dimly, but then face to face" (1 Corinthians 13:12, WEBBE). Aquinas made this the final happiness of the soul.
  • Pope John Paul II, general audiences (July 1999): heaven is not a place in the clouds but a living relationship with the Trinity; hell is not so much a place as a state (paraphrase).
  • Hell as self-exclusion: the Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992) calls it "definitive self-exclusion from communion with God" (CCC 1033).
  • So the pain of hell is above all loss of God, chosen freely; fire is an image of that loss.
  • Suggested source: CCC 1020–1050 (judgement, heaven, purgatory, hell).
  • These readings still treat heaven and hell as real after death; they deny only that they are locations.

Mission 2

Purgatory

  • Catholic teaching: those who die in God's grace but are not yet fully purified undergo purification after death, so as to enter heaven (CCC 1030–1031, paraphrase).
  • Defined at the Councils of Florence (1439) and Trent (1563). The living may help by prayer and the Mass offered for the dead.
  • Earlier roots: Augustine accepted that some punishments after death may purify (City of God XXI.13, XXI.24).
  • Place or state? Medieval art and Dante's Purgatorio (early 1300s) show a mountain; recent popes describe a process: Benedict XVI, Spe Salvi (2007, §47), speaks of the encounter with Christ as the fire that burns and heals (paraphrase).
  • Protestants reject purgatory: Luther attacked indulgences in 1517 and by about 1530 rejected purgatory itself as unscriptural; the Church of England's Article XXII calls the "Romish Doctrine concerning Purgatory" "a fond thing, vainly invented".
  • Orthodox Christians pray for the dead but do not accept the Western doctrine of purgatory.
  • Issue: does everyone pass through purgatory? Catholic teaching: no, saints and martyrs go straight to God. Some modern thinkers (e.g. John Hick) see further growth after death as needed by all.
Mission 2Read the source

Saved "as through fire"

> "For if he wasn't expecting that those who had fallen would rise again, it would be superfluous and idle to pray for the dead." (2 Maccabees 12:44, WEBBE)

> "… the fire itself will test what sort of work each man's work is. … he himself will be saved, but as through fire." (1 Corinthians 3:13, 15, WEBBE)

Q1 Why do Catholics cite 2 Maccabees but most Protestants do not?

Show answer

Catholics accept it as scripture; most Protestants place it in the Apocrypha, not the canon.

Q2 Does 1 Corinthians 3 have to mean purgatory?

Show answer

No: Protestants read the fire as the testing of a Christian's work at the final judgement, not a state of purification.

Mission 2

Symbols of life here and now

  • Realised eschatology: C. H. Dodd (1884–1973), The Parables of the Kingdom (1935): the kingdom has already come in Jesus.
  • "This is eternal life, that they should know you, the only true God" (John 17:3, WEBBE): eternal life as a quality of life now.
  • Rudolf Bultmann (1884–1976), "New Testament and Mythology" (1941): heaven above and hell below belong to an ancient picture of the world; demythologise them to find their meaning for how a person lives now.
  • D. Z. Phillips (1934–2006), Death and Immortality (1970): eternal life is not more life after death but a way of living: self-giving love now.
  • On this reading: heaven = wholeness and love; hell = selfishness and alienation; purgatory = moral growth.
  • Critics: most Christians, and the creeds, mean a real life after death; symbols alone may empty the hope of the resurrection.

Mission 2Evaluate

Are heaven, hell and purgatory best read as symbols?

  • Claim: Heaven, hell and purgatory are best understood as symbols of life on Earth.
  • For: The Bible's images (fire, worm, gates, a city of gold) are pictures; reading them literally creates puzzles (where is heaven?).
  • For: Jesus says the kingdom "is within you" (Luke 17:21, WEBBE); John speaks of eternal life now (John 17:3).
  • Against: Jesus, Paul and the creeds speak of real resurrection and real judgement; a symbol-only view changes the faith, not just its language.
  • Against: The spiritual-state reading avoids the literal puzzles without denying life after death.
  • Judgement criteria: Faithfulness to scripture and creeds · coherence with a modern picture of the universe · justice for those who suffer in this life · whether hope must reach beyond death.

Mission 3

Election: who will be saved?

  • Election: God's choosing of people for salvation (from Latin eligere, to choose).
  • Salvation: being rescued from sin and death into eternal life with God.
  • The question: does God choose some, call all, or save all?
  • Limited election: only some will be saved (OCR's wording: "only a few Christians").
  • Unlimited election: all are called to salvation, but not all are saved.
  • Universalism: all people will, in the end, be saved.
  • All three appeal to the Bible, and to God's love and justice.
Mission 3

Limited election

  • Augustine (354–430): after the Fall, humanity is a "mass of the damned" (massa damnata). God in mercy chooses some for grace; the rest receive justice (City of God XXI.12).
  • John Calvin (1509–1564), Institutes of the Christian Religion (final edition 1559), III.21: double predestination: God decrees eternal life for some, and eternal death for others.
  • Synod of Dort (1618–19): Christ died effectively for the elect (limited atonement).
  • Sources: Romans 8:29–30; Ephesians 1:4–5 ("he chose us in him before the foundation of the world"); "many are called, but few chosen" (Matthew 22:14, WEBBE); John 6:44.
  • Why hold it? Salvation is entirely God's gift ("not of him who wills … but of God who has mercy", Romans 9:16, WEBBE); no one can boast.
  • Note: Calvinists do not say how many are elect; "few" is Matthew's word and OCR's.
Mission 3Read the source

A Reformed confession

> "By the decree of God, for the manifestation of His glory, some men and angels are predestinated unto everlasting life; and others foreordained to everlasting death." (Westminster Confession of Faith, 1646, III.3)

> "Whom he predestined, those he also called. Whom he called, those he also justified. Whom he justified, those he also glorified." (Romans 8:30, WEBBE)

Q1 What does the Confession say is God's purpose in election?

Show answer

The manifestation of God's glory.

Q2 How could a Calvinist use Romans 8:30 to support limited election?

Show answer

The chain is unbroken: those God predestines are the ones called and glorified, so salvation depends on God's choice from start to finish.

Mission 3

Unlimited election

  • God "desires all people to be saved" (1 Timothy 2:4, WEBBE); "whoever believes in him should not perish" (John 3:16, WEBBE).
  • So God calls everyone; but love cannot be forced, so people may refuse. Hell is the result of that free refusal.
  • Jacob Arminius (1560–1609) and the Remonstrants (1610): election is conditional on God's foreknowledge of faith.
  • John Wesley (1703–1791): grace is offered to all ("free grace"); Methodist teaching follows him.
  • Catholic teaching: God wills all to be saved; Lumen Gentium 16 (1964): those who, through no fault of their own, do not know the gospel but seek God sincerely may be saved (paraphrase).
  • Most Christians today, Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant, hold some form of this view.

Mission 3

Universalism

  • Origen (c. 185–c. 253): the restoration of all things (apokatastasis): in the end all rational beings return to God.
  • Gregory of Nyssa (c. 335–c. 395) hoped for the same.
  • Augustine rejected it: City of God XXI.17 argues against those who think no one will be punished eternally, naming Origen.
  • John Hick (1922–2012), Death and Eternal Life (1976): God's love will win every person freely, through further lives and growth after death (paraphrase).
  • Sources: "as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive" (1 Corinthians 15:22, WEBBE); God reconciles "all things" (Colossians 1:20); "every knee should bow" (Philippians 2:10).
  • Hopeful universalism: some (e.g. Hans Urs von Balthasar, 1905–1988) hope all will be saved without claiming to know it.

> "Before him all the nations will be gathered, and he will separate them one from another, as a shepherd separates the sheep from the goats." (Matthew 25:32, WEBBE)

Q1 Who is gathered for judgement?

Show answer

All the nations, not only Christians or Israel.

Q2 What are people judged on?

Show answer

Mission 3

Key ideas in the parable

  • The judge: the Son of Man, enthroned in glory (25:31): a final, public judgement.
  • The criteria: six acts of mercy (feeding, giving drink, welcoming, clothing, visiting the sick and prisoners).
  • Christ is present in the needy: what is done to them is done to him.
  • Surprise: both groups ask, "Lord, when did we see you …?" Neither knew they were serving or refusing Christ.
  • Two outcomes: the kingdom "prepared for you from the foundation of the world" (25:34); the fire "prepared for the devil and his angels" (25:41), not for humans.
  • Eternal: Greek aiōnios, used for both the punishment and the life (25:46).
Mission 3Diversity

One parable, three readings

  • Limited election: the sheep's kingdom was prepared "from the foundation of the world": their good works are the fruit of God's choice, not its cause.
  • Unlimited election: all nations are judged by their free response; the surprised sheep show that people may serve Christ without knowing him. The goats chose their fate.
  • Universalism: a parable is a warning, not a timetable; aiōnios can mean "of the age to come", and kolasis (punishment) can mean correction; so the punishment need not be endless.
  • Who are "the least of these"? Most readers: anyone in need. Some scholars: Jesus' own disciples (compare Matthew 10:42; 12:50), so the nations are judged by how they treated his messengers.
  • Critics of the universalist reading: the same word, aiōnios, describes the life; if the life is endless, so is the punishment (Augustine's argument, City of God XXI.23).

Mission 3

Is hell eternal?

  • Eternal conscious punishment: the traditional view of Augustine, Aquinas and most Reformers; based on Matthew 25:46.
  • Annihilationism (conditional immortality): the lost are destroyed, not tormented forever. Held by Seventh-day Adventists; tentatively by the Anglican evangelical John Stott (1921–2011) in Essentials (1988).
  • Universalism: hell is real but remedial and so not endless.
  • Heaven as new creation: "the creation itself also will be delivered from the bondage of decay" (Romans 8:21, WEBBE); Revelation 21:1. Many theologians today stress a renewed whole creation, not souls leaving the world.
  • Suggested source: Revelation 20:7–15 and 21:1–8; Hick, Death and Eternal Life, Part III.
Mission 3Evaluate

Would a loving God save everyone?

  • Claim: A God of perfect love would, in the end, save every person.
  • For: God "desires all people to be saved" (1 Timothy 2:4); if God is omnipotent, that desire will not fail.
  • For: Endless punishment for sins in a finite life seems out of proportion; parents do not give up on a child for ever.
  • Against: Love respects freedom: if no one can finally say no, the yes is not free (unlimited election).
  • Against: Jesus' strongest warnings (Matthew 25:46; Mark 9:48) seem pointless if all are saved; justice requires that choices matter.
  • Judgement criteria: Weight of the biblical texts on each side · the nature of human freedom · whether love and justice can conflict in God · pastoral effects (assurance or complacency?).

Mission 4

Two routes to knowing God

  • Natural knowledge: what anyone can know of God through reason, conscience and the created world.
  • Revealed knowledge: what God makes known of himself, received by faith: in Jesus Christ, the Bible, the Church, the Spirit.
  • The debate is not "reason or faith?" but: how much can reason reach, and what needs revelation?
  • And: can natural knowledge alone save anyone, or lead them to trust God?
Mission 4

An innate sense of the divine

  • Christians believe humans are made "in God's image" (Genesis 1:27), with an inbuilt capacity and desire to know God.
  • Openness to beauty and goodness as glimpses of God; intellect able to reason towards God.
  • Augustine: "our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee" (Confessions I.1).
  • John Calvin (1509–1564), Institutes of the Christian Religion (final edition 1559):
  • I.i: almost all wisdom is knowing God and knowing ourselves, and the two are bound together (paraphrase).
  • I.ii: true knowledge of God is piety: reverence and love, not mere information (paraphrase).
  • I.iii: every human has a sensus divinitatis, a natural sense of the divine (paraphrase).

Mission 4

The order of creation

  • "The heavens declare the glory of God. The expanse shows his handiwork." (Psalm 19:1)
  • Many Christians hold that something of God can be seen in the apparent design and purpose of nature.
  • Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274): from God's effects we can know "whether He exists", but not God's essence (Summa Theologiae I, q. 12, a. 12).
  • Aquinas: "faith presupposes natural knowledge, even as grace presupposes nature" (ST I, q. 2, a. 2).
  • Catholic teaching (First Vatican Council, Dei Filius, 1870): God can be known with certainty by the natural light of reason from created things (paraphrase).

Mission 4

Why natural knowledge is not enough

  • Sin: the "senseless heart was darkened" (Romans 1:21); humans suppress or twist what they know.
  • Finite minds: God's essence exceeds any creature's grasp.
  • Aquinas: "It is impossible to attain to the knowledge of the Trinity by natural reason." (ST I, q. 32, a. 1)
  • Calvin: the sense of the divine is real but corrupted into idolatry and superstition; Scripture is like spectacles that bring the blurred knowledge into focus (Institutes I.vi.1, paraphrase).
  • So: natural knowledge can leave people "without excuse", but cannot bring full knowledge or trust.
Mission 4

Faith, grace and the Spirit

  • Faith: trusting response to God's self-revelation.
  • Grace: God's free gift of knowledge of himself: "by grace you have been saved through faith … it is the gift of God" (Ephesians 2:8).
  • Through the Holy Spirit: "God revealed them through the Spirit. For the Spirit searches all things" (1 Corinthians 2:10).
Mission 4

Revealed in Christ, the Church and the Bible

  • Jesus Christ: for Christians, the full and perfect revelation of God. "He who has seen me has seen the Father." (John 14:9)
  • God "has at the end of these days spoken to us by his Son" (Hebrews 1:2).
  • The Bible: the written witness to God's revelation.
  • The Church: the community where Scripture is read, the sacraments given and the faith handed on; in Catholic and Orthodox teaching it also interprets revelation with authority.
  • Revelation does not cancel reason: it tells reason what it could not find alone.
Mission 4Diversity

Barth versus Brunner, 1934

  • Emil Brunner (1889–1966), Nature and Grace (1934): sin damaged but did not destroy the image of God; a point of contact remains for revelation to address.
  • Karl Barth (1886–1968), No! (Nein!, 1934): there is no point of contact; God is known only where God reveals himself, in Jesus Christ.
  • Context: some "German Christians" used natural theology (nation, race, history) to back Nazism; Barth's Barmen Declaration (1934) rejected any source of revelation besides Jesus Christ.
  • Catholic: natural knowledge is real and grace perfects it (Aquinas; Vatican I).
  • Reformed (Calvin): natural knowledge is real but fruitless without Scripture and the Spirit.

Mission 5

Whose authority?

  • Authority: the right to be believed and obeyed.
  • The Gospels say people were amazed that Jesus taught "as having authority, and not as the scribes" (Mark 1:22).
  • Christians ask where that authority comes from. Two broad answers:
  • God's own authority: Jesus is God the Son, so his word is God's word.
  • Human authority: Jesus is a uniquely wise and holy human being, close to God, but not God (see Mission 6).
  • The answer changes how his teaching is followed: as a command of God, or as wise human guidance.
Mission 5

"Son of God": what the title meant

  • In the Hebrew Bible, "son of God" could mean Israel, or the king God chose: "You are my son" (Psalm 2:7). A close, chosen relationship, not divinity.
  • Mark opens: "the Good News of Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (Mark 1:1); at the cross a Roman centurion says it (Mark 15:39).
  • Asked "Are you the Christ, the Son of the Blessed?", Jesus answers "I am" (Mark 14:61–62).
  • John 10:30: "I and the Father are one."
  • Mainstream Christian teaching (Nicaea 325, Chalcedon 451;): Jesus is the Son, true God and true man, not a son by adoption.
Mission 5

Knowledge of God

  • OCR's first sign of divinity: Jesus' knowledge of God.
  • He calls God Abba (Aramaic, "Father") in prayer (Mark 14:36): a sign, for many Christians, of a unique closeness.
  • "No one knows the Son, except the Father; neither does anyone know the Father, except the Son" (Matthew 11:27).
  • He claims to forgive sins (Mark 2:5). Critics ask: "Who can forgive sins but God alone?" (Mark 2:7).
  • He reinterprets the Law on his own word: "But I tell you…" (Matthew 5;).
  • A Christian reading: he knows God from the inside, as the Son. A sceptical reading: these are the words of later believers, or of a prophet with a deep sense of God.

Mission 5

The resurrection

  • For Paul, the resurrection is God's own verdict on Jesus: he "was declared to be the Son of God with power… by the resurrection from the dead" (Romans 1:4).
  • "If Christ has not been raised, your faith is vain" (1 Corinthians 15:17): for most Christians the whole faith rests on it.
  • Bodily resurrection: the tomb was empty and Jesus was raised in a transformed body (Catholic, Orthodox and most Protestant teaching).
  • Spiritual or existential readings: e.g. Rudolf Bultmann (1884–1976) held that Jesus "rose into the kerygma": the event is the rise of faith in the risen Christ in the preaching.
  • On a bodily view, the resurrection is the strongest sign of divinity; on Bultmann's view, it is not a historical proof at all.

Mission 5Diversity

Did Jesus think he was divine?

  • John's Gospel: open claims ("I and the Father are one", 10:30; "before Abraham came into existence, I AM", 8:58). Many scholars think John shows later reflection; many Christians take it as Jesus' own words.
  • Mark: Jesus often tells people to keep silent. William Wrede (1859–1906), The Messianic Secret (1901), argued this "secret" was Mark's device, not Jesus' own practice.
  • Geza Vermes (1924–2013), Jesus the Jew (1973): Jesus was a Galilean charismatic holy man, like Honi and Hanina ben Dosa; "son of God" meant a holy man close to God.
  • Traditional view: claims to forgive sins and to judge (Mark 2:7; 14:62) only make sense if Jesus saw himself as more than a prophet.
  • OCR issue: was Jesus' relationship with God very special (different in degree from ours) or truly unique (different in kind)?
Mission 5

If his authority is God's: what follows

  • His teaching is binding: not advice to weigh, but God's will to obey.
  • Hard sayings (love your enemies, turn the other cheek;) cannot be set aside as unrealistic. Christians may still debate how to apply them.
  • As a role model, he is perfect: "without sin" (Hebrews 4:15). Some ask whether a sinless, divine model can be followed by ordinary humans.
  • His words carry more weight than any later Church teaching or tradition.
Mission 5Evaluate

Do the miracles and the resurrection show that Jesus was divine?

  • Claim: The Gospels' miracles and resurrection show that Jesus had God's own authority.
  • For: The allusions in Mark 6 point to God himself, not only a prophet.
  • For: John 9 ends with the healed man worshipping Jesus; many readers see worship due to God alone.
  • For: The resurrection is presented as God's verdict on Jesus (Romans 1:4).
  • Against: Prophets also worked wonders (Elijah and Elisha raise the dead in 1 Kings 17 and 2 Kings 4), so miracles alone show a link to God, not divinity.
  • Against: The texts are written from faith, decades later. Historians cannot easily reach the events behind them.
  • Against: Some Christians accept Jesus' authority on the basis of his life and teaching, not of miracles.
  • Judgement criteria: What would count as evidence of divinity? How far can the texts be read as history? Is authority shown by power, by teaching, or by both?

Mission 6

Teacher of wisdom

  • Jesus is called "Teacher" (Rabbi) throughout the Gospels.
  • Like the Jewish wisdom tradition (Proverbs, Sirach), he teaches how to live well before God, in sayings and parables.
  • His focus: the inner life: motive, desire, the heart, not only outward acts.
  • A question for every reader: is he a teacher of God's wisdom, or only a teacher of wisdom?
Mission 6

Fulfilling the Law (Matthew 5:17–37)

  • "I didn't come to destroy, but to fulfil" (Matthew 5:17). Jesus does not abolish the Torah; he goes to its root.
  • Six antitheses: "You have heard that it was said… But I tell you…"
  • Murder → anger and contempt (5:21–22). Adultery → lustful looking, "adultery… in his heart" (5:27–28).
  • So: inner purity (a clean heart) and right moral motivation, not only right action.
  • "Unless your righteousness exceeds that of the scribes and Pharisees…" (5:20): a deeper, not a looser, righteousness.

Retaliation and enemies (Matthew 5:38–48)

> "But I tell you, don't resist him who is evil; but whoever strikes you on your right cheek, turn to him the other also." (Matthew 5:39, WEBBE)

> "But I tell you, love your enemies, bless those who curse you, do good to those who hate you, and pray for those who mistreat you and persecute you," (Matthew 5:44, WEBBE)

Mission 6

Liberator: the woman healed (Mark 5:24–34)

  • A woman with bleeding for twelve years has spent all she had on doctors (5:25–26).
  • Under the purity law she counts as unclean (Leviticus 15:25): barred from the Temple, and many readers think kept at a distance by others.
  • Some scholars (e.g. Amy-Jill Levine) note that Mark never mentions impurity, and warn against picturing Judaism as cruel to women; the purity reading is one interpretation.
  • She touches Jesus' clothes; she is healed. Jesus does not rebuke her. He calls her "Daughter" and says "your faith has made you well. Go in peace" (5:34).
  • Challenge to religious authority (on the purity reading): Jesus puts the woman's restoration above purity concerns.
  • Liberation: healing, plus a return to community and dignity for someone poor and excluded.
Mission 6

Liberator: the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25–37)

  • A lawyer asks, "Who is my neighbour?" (10:29).
  • A priest and a Levite pass by; a Samaritan, from a despised group, stops and cares for the wounded man.
  • Jesus reverses the question: who acted as neighbour? "He who showed mercy on him." "Go and do likewise." (10:37)
  • Religious authority challenged: the religious officials fail; the outsider fulfils the Law of love (10:27).
  • Political and social challenge: Jesus elsewhere tells his followers not to "lord it over" others as rulers do (Mark 10:42–43). Liberation theologians (e.g. Gustavo Gutiérrez) read him as siding with the poor against unjust power.

Mission 6

Authority as only human: what follows

  • Some Christians and others see Jesus as a uniquely wise and holy human, not God incarnate: e.g. Unitarians; some liberal Protestants; John Hick (1922–2012) read incarnation as a metaphor (The Metaphor of God Incarnate, 1993).
  • Many non-Christians respect him as a teacher too: Gandhi drew on the Sermon on the Mount for non-violence.
  • Implications for his teaching: its authority rests on its wisdom; it can be weighed by reason and conscience, like other great teaching.
  • As a role model: a fully human Jesus shows what a human being can do; his example is reachable, not superhuman.
  • Criticism: if he is only human, why should his hardest teachings bind anyone? And the Gospels present him as claiming more (see Mission 5).
Mission 6Diversity

Turning the other cheek today

  • Pacifist reading: taken as binding for all of life. Mennonites, Quakers and other historic peace churches refuse to fight. Leo Tolstoy (1828–1910) made it the heart of The Kingdom of God Is Within You (1894).
  • Non-violent resistance: Martin Luther King Jr. (1929–1968) read loving enemies as active resistance without hatred or violence.
  • Personal, not political: Augustine and Luther held that the teaching governs personal revenge, while rulers may still use force justly.
  • An ideal: "be perfect" (5:48) sets a goal of God's love; failure calls for repentance, not despair.
  • The reading often links to authority: if Jesus' words are God's, some argue they cannot be softened; if human, some argue they can be weighed. Both links are disputed.
Mission 6Evaluate

Was Jesus only a teacher of wisdom?

  • Claim: Jesus was only a human teacher of wisdom.
  • For: His teaching (antitheses, parables) fits the Jewish wisdom tradition and can be judged on its own merits.
  • For: Historians can reach his teaching more easily than claims about his divine nature.
  • Against: He teaches with personal authority ("But I tell you"), and claims to forgive sins (see Mission 5).
  • Against: C. S. Lewis (1898–1963), Mere Christianity (1952): a mere man who said what Jesus said would not be a great moral teacher; either he was what he claimed, or he was not good (paraphrase). Critics reply that the claims may come from the Gospel writers.
  • Was he more than a political liberator? His liberation of the poor and excluded (Mark 5, Luke 10) is linked in the Gospels to repentance and the Kingdom of God, not only to social change.
  • Judgement criteria: Is the Gospel picture reliable? Can teaching carry authority without divinity? Is liberation spiritual, social, or both?

Mission 7

Three answers to one question

  • Question: where do Christians find out what is right?
  • The Bible alone: God's will is revealed in scripture; follow its commands.
  • Bible, Church and reason: scripture, read within the Church's teaching, and applied with human reason.
  • Love (agape) alone: Jesus' one command is to love; reason works out how.
  • Agape (Greek): self-giving love that seeks the other's good, modelled on God's love in Christ.
  • Most Christians use more than one source; the three answers differ in which has the final say.
Mission 7

The Bible as the only authority

  • Sola scriptura ("scripture alone"): Martin Luther (1483–1546) and the Reformers held scripture to be the supreme authority over Church tradition.
  • OCR's version: as the Bible reveals God's will, only biblical commands must be followed.
  • Held most strongly by many conservative evangelical and fundamentalist Protestants.
  • Model: the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20:1–17) as God's direct commands.
  • Strengths: God's authority, not human opinion; the same for everyone; does not change with fashion.
  • Note: the Reformers themselves used reason and the early creeds; "scripture alone with nothing else" is a stricter later view.

Mission 7

Problems for "Bible alone"

  • Interpretation: which commands still apply? Most Christians do not keep Old Testament food or clothing laws.
  • Silence: the Bible says nothing directly about IVF, nuclear weapons or the internet.
  • History: some Christians once quoted texts such as Ephesians 6:5 ("Servants, be obedient to … your masters") to defend slavery; others used the Bible to oppose it.
  • Hidden sources: every reader brings reason and a church community; "Bible alone" already uses them.
  • Issue: is the Bible a comprehensive moral guide? Supporters: its principles cover every case. Critics: principles still need reason and community to apply.
Mission 7

Bible, Church and reason

  • Catholic teaching: Scripture and Tradition, interpreted by the Magisterium (the teaching authority of the Pope and bishops), stand together; none stands without the others (Dei Verbum 10, 1965, paraphrase).
  • Reason: natural law: reason can discover God's moral order in human nature (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I-II q.94;).
  • Anglican: Richard Hooker (1554–1600), Of the Laws of Ecclesiastical Polity (1594–97): Scripture first, then reason, then the Church's tradition.
  • Methodist: the "Wesleyan quadrilateral" adds experience (a name coined in the 1960s for John Wesley's method).
  • Strength: applies faith to new questions; keeps continuity with 2,000 years of Christians.
  • Criticism (from "Bible alone"): Church and reason may override God's word; churches have erred.

Mission 7

Love (agape) as the only principle

  • Jesus sums up the law in two commands: love God; "love your neighbour as yourself" (Mark 12:29–31, WEBBE).
  • "A new commandment I give to you, that you love one another" (John 13:34, WEBBE). "Love therefore is the fulfilment of the law" (Romans 13:10, WEBBE).
  • Augustine (354–430): "Love, and do what thou wilt" (Homilies on the First Epistle of John 7.8): if love is the root, good actions follow.
  • Joseph Fletcher (1905–1991), Situation Ethics (1966): love is the only thing good in itself; rules are guidelines; reason decides what love requires in each case.
  • OCR's version: Jesus' only command was to love; human reason decides how best to apply it.
  • Criticism: Augustine meant love formed by God's grace and the Church, not love without rules; Fletcher's critics say "love" alone is too vague to decide hard cases.

One dilemma, three approaches

A person is dying. The family ask the doctor, a Christian, to lie and say there is hope.

  • Bible alone: lying breaks God's command ("you shall not give false testimony", Exodus 20:16; Ephesians 4:25); tell the truth, gently.
  • Bible, Church and reason: lying is wrong (Catechism, natural law); but truth may be told gradually and with compassion; not every truth must be said at once.
  • Agape alone: ask what love requires here; for Fletcher, a loving lie could be right.
  • Bonhoeffer, who deceived the Nazi state, wrote that telling the truth means saying what is real in that relationship, not merely matching words to facts (Ethics, paraphrase).

Mission 7

Distinctive? Personal or communal?

  • Distinctive? Yes: love of enemies (Matthew 5:44), forgiveness, motivated by grace and by imitating Christ. No: Paul says Gentiles "show the work of the law written in their hearts" (Romans 2:15, WEBBE); much Christian morality overlaps with natural morality.

Mission 7Evaluate

Is love enough?

  • Claim: The principle of love is sufficient to live a good Christian life.
  • For: Jesus and Paul both make love the summary of the law (Mark 12:31; Romans 13:10).
  • For: Rules cannot foresee every case; love adapts to new situations.
  • Against: Love needs content: without commands and tradition, people call selfish choices "loving".
  • Against: The Bible and the Church give love its shape (1 Corinthians 13 ties love to truth).

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • For Augustine, before the Fall: harmony, friendship, reason ruling desire (able not to sin). The Fall came from pride; after it, concupiscence and selfishness disorder every relationship.
  • Augustine taught that original sin passes to all through procreation: humans are not able not to sin, and all societies are ruled by love of self and the lust of rule.
  • For Augustine, only grace can heal the will and lead to the summum bonum, eternal life with God; critics (Pelagius, the Orthodox, modern thinkers) dispute inherited guilt and a historical Fall.

Mission 2

  • Christians read heaven, hell and purgatory as places, spiritual states after death, or symbols of life now.
  • Judgement: at death (particular), at the end of time (final), or both; some see it as already happening (realised).

Mission 3

  • Limited (God chooses some: Augustine, Calvin), unlimited (all called, not all saved: Arminius, Wesley, Catholic teaching), universalist (all saved: Origen, Hick).
  • The Sheep and the Goats: all nations judged on acts of mercy to "the least of these"; read by each view in its own way.
  • Is hell eternal? Eternal punishment, annihilation or remedial punishment.

Mission 4

  • Natural knowledge: an innate sense of the divine (image of God; Calvin's sensus divinitatis) and the order of creation (Psalm 19; Romans 1; Acts 17; Aquinas).
  • Revealed knowledge: for Christians, it comes by faith and grace through the Spirit, fully in Jesus Christ, through the Bible and the Church.
  • Catholic, Reformed and Barthian views differ on how much natural knowledge survives sin.

Mission 5

  • Christians debate whether Jesus' authority is God's own (he is God the Son) or human (a uniquely holy man close to God); the answer shapes how his teaching is followed.
  • Signs of divinity: knowledge of God (Abba, forgiving sins), miracles (Mark 6:47–52 echoes God treading the sea; John 9 leads from sight to worship) and resurrection (Romans 1:4).
  • Scholars differ on whether Jesus thought he was divine (Wrede's messianic secret, Vermes' charismatic holy man).

Mission 6

  • Jesus teaches love even of enemies, without revenge (Matthew 5:38–48).
  • He also teaches inner purity and right motive (Matthew 5:17–37).
  • Agape is love that seeks the good of all, even enemies.
  • Luke 15 shows repentance met by free forgiveness; Mark 5 and Luke 10 show him liberating the excluded and challenging religious and social power.
  • If Jesus' authority is only human, his teaching is wise guidance and his life a reachable model; if it is God's, the teaching is command. Christians apply Matthew 5:38–48 in pacifist, non-violent and personal-only ways.

Mission 7

  • Bible alone (sola scriptura, strongest among conservative Protestants); Bible, Church and reason (Catholic, Anglican, Methodist); agape alone (Augustine's "Love, and do what thou wilt" as read by Fletcher).
  • Issues: distinctive? personal or communal? love sufficient? Bible comprehensive?
