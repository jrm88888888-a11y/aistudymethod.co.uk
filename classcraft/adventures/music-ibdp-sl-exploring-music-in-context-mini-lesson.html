Exploring music in context

In IB Music you take on three roles — researcher, creator and performer. Exploring music in context is the researching process: you listen deeply to a wide, diverse range of music and place it in its context.

Work through each screen, answer the questions and mini-games as you go, and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you are ready.

The researcher role

Diverse, contextual listening

Exploring music in context means engaging with a diverse range of music to broaden your horizons and gather stimuli for your own music-making. You show breadth by exploring music from all four areas of inquiry across three kinds of context:

  • Personal — music connected to your own life and experience.
  • Local — music from your immediate culture or community.
  • Global — traditions from beyond your own culture.

No fixed set-work list. You and your teacher choose the repertoire; what matters is genuine breadth and depth of inquiry, evidenced in a portfolio.

The elements of music

Your analytical toolkit

When you analyse, describe what you hear using the musical elements:

  • Melody — the tune: pitch, contour (rising or falling), conjunct (stepwise) versus disjunct (leaps), motif and phrase.
  • Harmony — how chords combine: consonance and dissonance, progressions, cadences.
  • Tonality — major, minor, modal or atonal; the key and any modulation.
  • Rhythm and metre — beat, tempo, time signature (duple, triple, quadruple; simple or compound), syncopation.
  • Timbre (tone colour) — the specific sound of a voice or instrument; the instrumentation.
  • Dynamics and articulation — loud or soft (forte, piano), crescendo; legato or staccato.

Texture

Monophonic, homophonic, polyphonic, heterophonic

Texture is how many musical lines sound together and how they relate:

  • Monophonic — a single line, no harmony (plainchant, a solo folk melody).
  • Homophonic — one clear melody supported by chords (most pop songs, hymns).
  • Polyphonic (contrapuntal) — two or more independent melodic lines woven together (a Bach fugue).
  • Heterophonic — one melody performed in slightly varied versions at the same time (common in gamelan and much East-Asian and Middle-Eastern music).

The four areas of inquiry

Four lenses for listening

The guide organises listening through four areas of inquiry:

  • Music for sociocultural and political expression.
  • Music for listening and performance.
  • Music for dramatic impact, movement and entertainment.
  • Music technology in the electronic and digital age.

These are lenses, not genres. The same piece can be explored through more than one. You demonstrate breadth by exploring across all four, and across local and global contexts.

How a researcher works

Aural and score analysis

  • Aural analysis — listen repeatedly, identify the elements, and describe how they create effect and meaning.
  • Score analysis (when notation exists) — follow the score to see harmony, form and instrumentation.
  • Context — research the who, where, when and why, and connect it to what you hear.

You gather this in a portfolio of exploration and use it as stimulus for your own creating and performing.

Comparison

Linking contrasting musics

A key research skill is comparison: finding meaningful links between contrasting musics — for example, how a Baroque ground bass and a hip-hop loop both repeat a bass pattern, or how call and response appears in West African drumming, gospel and the blues.

Good comparison uses precise elemental vocabulary and respects each tradition on its own terms — avoid judging one tradition by the rules of another.

From exploration to ideas

Turning listening into stimulus

The point of exploring is not just to describe but to be inspired. A texture, scale, rhythm or timbre you discover can seed your own creating and performing.

Keep a record of the features that struck you and how you might borrow or transform them — with understanding of their context.

Skills

Writing about music well

  • Always link feature → effect → meaning (for example, the sudden forte and rising melody build tension that matches a song protest message).
  • Use correct terminology and support claims with specific moments (timings or bars).
  • Acknowledge context and avoid stereotyping unfamiliar traditions.
  • Show breadth across the four areas and across personal, local and global.
