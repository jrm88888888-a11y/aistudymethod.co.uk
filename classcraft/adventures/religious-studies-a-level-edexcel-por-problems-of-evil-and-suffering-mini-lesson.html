The Problem of Evil and Suffering

If God is omnipotent and wholly good, why is there evil? This lesson sets out the logical and evidential problems and the main theodicies that try to answer them.

You will distinguish moral from natural evil, study the Augustinian and Irenaean/Hick theodicies and the free will defence, and weigh their success. Answer the questions and collect ⭐.

The problem

The problem stated

Moral evil is caused by human choices (cruelty, war); natural evil is suffering from nature (disease, earthquakes).

  • Epicurus / Hume: the "inconsistent triad" — God is omnipotent, God is wholly good, yet evil exists; the three seem incompatible.
  • J. L. Mackie ("Evil and Omnipotence", 1955): this is a logical problem — the beliefs are contradictory, so one must be given up.
  • The evidential problem: even if not a strict contradiction, the sheer quantity of suffering counts as evidence against God.

Augustine

The Augustinian theodicy

Augustine argues:

  • God's creation was wholly good; evil is not a substance but a privation of good (privatio boni) — a falling-short.
  • Evil entered through the free choice of angels and of humans at the Fall; we were "seminally present" in Adam, so all share the consequences.
  • Natural evil is a consequence of this disorder; God is just to permit it, and the whole retains an aesthetic harmony.

Irenaeus / Hick

The Irenaean (soul-making) theodicy

Building on Irenaeus, John Hick (Evil and the God of Love, 1966) defends a soul-making theodicy:

  • Humans are created immature and grow towards God's likeness through freely meeting challenges.
  • God maintains an epistemic distance so that faith and virtue are genuinely free, not compelled.
  • The world is a vale of soul-making; suffering has a purpose in developing character. Hick adds universal salvation as the final justification.

Free will

The free will defence and alternatives

  • Alvin Plantinga: a world with free creatures who freely choose good is more valuable than one of programmed goodness; God cannot guarantee that free beings never choose evil, so moral evil is compatible with omnipotence.
  • Process theology (e.g. Griffin, after Whitehead): God is not omnipotent in the classical sense but works persuasively; this dissolves the triad but revises the concept of God.

Challenges

Evaluating the theodicies

  • The free will defence explains moral evil but struggles with natural evil.
  • Critics (e.g. Mackie) ask why God could not create free beings who always freely choose good.
  • The scale and distribution of suffering — including animal and childhood suffering — is a powerful evidential objection; Dostoevsky's Ivan Karamazov protests that no future harmony justifies a child's torment.

The big ideas to know

The problem: Logical (Mackie, inconsistent triad) vs evidential; moral vs natural evil

Augustinian: Evil = privation; the Fall; free will; aesthetic harmony

Irenaean/Hick: Soul-making; epistemic distance; suffering develops virtue

Free will defence: Plantinga: free good outweighs risk of evil; process theology revises omnipotence
