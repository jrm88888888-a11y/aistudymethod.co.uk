Problems of evil and suffering

Mission 1

Mission 1: If God is all-powerful and all-loving, why is there malaria? Is that a proof or just a problem?

Make a guess: If God is all-powerful and all-loving, why is there malaria? Is that a proof or just a problem?

Mission 1

Moral and natural evil

  • Moral evil: suffering caused by free agents: murder, cruelty, war, neglect.
  • Natural evil: suffering caused by nature: earthquakes, disease, drought, predators.
  • Edexcel wording: moral and non-moral evil.
  • Boundary cases: a famine made worse by corruption; a flood worsened by building on flood plains.
  • Evil names what is bad; suffering names what is felt: pain, fear, grief.
  • Why it matters: theodicies often explain moral evil by free will, but natural evil needs another answer.
Mission 1

One problem, many traditions

  • The problem is sharpest for classical theism: one God, all-powerful, all-knowing, all-good (Judaism, Christianity, Islam; Sikhism's one Creator).
  • Judaism: Job's suffering and protest; after the Holocaust, some speak of God's "hidden face" (Deuteronomy 31:18).
  • Islam: suffering as a test of faith: "And surely We shall try you with something of fear and hunger, and loss of wealth and lives and crops; but give glad tidings to the steadfast" (Qur'an 2:155, Pickthall).
  • Hinduism: karma explains suffering across lifetimes, so the question shifts from "why does God allow it?" to how the law of karma is just.
  • Buddhism: dukkha is a mark of all conditioned existence; with no creator God, there is no one to justify; the question is how to end suffering.
  • So the "problem of evil" is a problem for particular beliefs about God.
Mission 1Read the source

Epicurus' old questions

David Hume (1711–1776), Dialogues concerning Natural Religion (1779), Part X; Philo speaks:

> "Epicurus's old questions are yet unanswered. Is he willing to prevent evil, but not able? then is he impotent. Is he able, but not willing? then is he malevolent. Is he both able and willing? whence then is evil?"

  • Epicurus (341–270 BCE): the argument is attributed to him by the Christian writer Lactantius (On the Anger of God, ch. 13, c. 314).

Q1 Which two attributes does each question test?

Show answer

Power, "able"; goodness, "willing".

Q2 Why is the third question the hardest?

Show answer

If God has both, evil should not exist; yet it does.

Mission 1

Logical and evidential

  • Logical problem: God's attributes and the existence of evil are inconsistent: both cannot be true. Deductive: if it works, God is impossible.
  • Evidential problem: the amount, intensity and distribution of evil make God's existence improbable. Inductive: it does not claim impossibility.
  • A response to one may not answer the other: showing consistency is not showing likelihood.
Mission 1

Mackie: the inconsistent triad

  • J. L. Mackie (1917–1981), "Evil and Omnipotence", Mind (1955).
  • Edexcel Anthology Extract 1 (paraphrased here).
  • Three propositions central to theism: God is omnipotent; God is wholly good; evil exists. If any two are true, the third seems false.
  • The contradiction appears once two further rules are added (paraphrase):
  • A good being always eliminates evil as far as it can.
  • There are no limits to what an omnipotent being can do.
  • So a wholly good, omnipotent being would eliminate evil completely; yet evil exists.
  • Mackie: theists must give up one premise or admit their beliefs are inconsistent: religious belief would then be not merely unsupported but irrational.
Quick check
?Why does Mackie add two extra rules to his three propositions?
Mission 1

Mackie against the easy answers

Mackie rejects four "fallacious solutions" (paraphrase):

  • "Good cannot exist without evil": this limits omnipotence, and at most needs a tiny amount of evil as a contrast.
  • "Evil is necessary as a means to good": only a limited being needs means; an omnipotent God could produce the good directly.
  • "The universe is better with some evil": pain (first-order evil) allows sympathy and courage (second-order goods); but then cruelty and cowardice (second-order evils) arise, needing higher goods, and the problem returns.
  • "Evil is due to human free will": if God could make people who sometimes freely choose good, why not people who always freely choose good?
  • Paradox of omnipotence: can an omnipotent being make things it cannot control? Either answer limits its power.
  • Replies to Mackie (Plantinga's free will defence).
Mission 1

Rowe: the fawn in the forest

  • William Rowe (1931–2015), "The Problem of Evil and Some Varieties of Atheism", American Philosophical Quarterly (1979).
  • Example: lightning strikes a forest; a fawn is trapped, badly burned, and lies in terrible agony for several days before dying. No one sees it; no one learns from it.
  • (1) There exist instances of intense suffering that an all-powerful, all-knowing being could have prevented without losing a greater good or allowing something as bad or worse.
  • (2) A wholly good, all-knowing being would prevent such suffering.
  • (3) So there is no such being. Premise (1) is supported by how such cases appear: pointless.
  • Rowe calls himself a "friendly atheist": a theist may still be rational in believing.
  • Response: sceptical theism (Stephen Wykstra, 1984): our failure to see a reason is weak evidence that there is none.

Mission 1

Living with evil

  • The problem is not only philosophical: it is lived. Suffering can destroy or deepen faith.
  • Job curses the day he was born (Job 3), protests to God, and is answered from the whirlwind: "Where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth?" (Job 38:4)
  • Elie Wiesel (1928–2016), Night (1958): a Holocaust survivor's account of faith shaken by the camps.
  • Some Jewish thinkers keep faith through protest, arguing with God; others speak of God hiding.
  • Muslims are taught sabr (patient endurance): "Lo! we are Allah's and lo! unto Him we are returning" (Qur'an 2:156, Pickthall).
  • Christians point to a God who suffers on the cross, and to hope: "He will wipe away every tear from their eyes" (Revelation 21:4).
Mission 1Evaluate

"The evidential problem is a greater challenge to belief than the logical problem."

  • For: many philosophers judge the logical form answered: Plantinga only needs to show that God and evil are possibly consistent (see Mission 4).
  • For: the evidential form needs only probability; cases such as Rowe's fawn and the early deaths of children from disease are hard to explain away.
  • Against: the logical form, if sound, disproves God outright; Mackie argues free-will replies fail.
  • Against: "this suffering seems pointless" may reflect our limited view (sceptical theism).
  • Against: probability must be judged on all the evidence, including arguments for God and religious experience.
  • Judge it by: What does each argument claim to show? Which responses are stronger? Does a believer need certainty or only reasonableness?
Mission check

?Malaria still kills many people every year, most of them young children. A doctor says: "I can't prove God doesn't exist, but this much suffering, with no purpose I can see, makes a loving, all-powerful God hard to believe in." How is her argument best classified?

The problem has two forms: logical (inconsistency) and evidential (improbability). The next three missions test the main responses.

Mission 2

Mission 2: If God made everything and everything God made was good, where did evil come from?

Make a guess: If God made everything and everything God made was good, where did evil come from?

Mission 2

A good creation

  • Augustine of Hippo (354–430), bishop in North Africa; Confessions (c. 397–400); City of God (413–426).
  • As a young man he followed the Manichees, who taught two equal powers: Good and Evil, Light and Darkness.
  • He came to reject this dualism: there is one God, and everything God made is good.
  • "God saw everything that he had made, and, behold, it was very good." (Genesis 1:31)
  • So evil cannot be a created thing. Then what is it?
Mission 2Read the source

Evil as privation

> "…evil was nothing but a privation of good, until at last a thing ceases altogether to be…" (Confessions III.7, Pusey translation)

> "Let no one, therefore, look for an efficient cause of the evil will; for it is not efficient, but deficient…" (City of God XII.7, Dods translation)

  • Privation (Latin privatio): a lack of a good that should be there. Blindness is not a thing; it is missing sight. Rot is a good apple losing its goodness.
  • An evil will has no positive cause: it is a turning away from God, the highest good, to lesser goods.

Q1 Why can't God be the author of evil, on this view?

Show answer

God creates only things; evil is not a thing but a lack.

Q2 What does "deficient" cause mean?

Show answer

A falling short, a failure, rather than something that produces.

Mission 2

Augustine: the Fall of angels and humans

  • In Augustine's account, God gave angels and humans free will.
  • Some angels, led by Satan, turned in pride from God to themselves: the first evil will (City of God XI–XII).
  • Humans, tempted, disobeyed in Eden (Genesis 3): the Fall.
  • "Therefore, as sin entered into the world through one man, and death through sin, so death passed to all men because all sinned." (Romans 5:12)
  • Augustine: "For we all were in that one man" (City of God XIII.14): all humanity was seminally present in Adam.
  • So all inherit original sin: a damaged nature, inclined to sin, and liable to just punishment.
Mission 2

Augustine on natural evil

  • For Augustine, after the Fall creation itself is disordered: "the ground is cursed for your sake" (Genesis 3:17); "the whole creation groans and travails in pain together until now" (Romans 8:22).
  • Augustine: natural evil is the just consequence of sin: humans lost their harmony with God, so nature lost its harmony with humans.
  • Some Augustinians add the fallen angels as a cause of disorder in nature.
  • Aesthetic principle: the universe is like a poem "set off with antitheses"; "the beauty of the course of this world is achieved by the opposition of contraries" (City of God XI.18).
  • Plenitude: a full creation, with every level of being, is better than one with only the highest things.
Quick check
?On Augustine's account, what is the best description of evil?
Mission 2

Augustine: soul-deciding, the Cross and reconciliation

  • John Hick called Augustine's theodicy soul-deciding: this life decides each person's eternal destiny, heaven or hell.
  • On this view Hell is just punishment for freely chosen sin; God's grace saves those who are saved.
  • The Cross overcomes evil: Christians believe that through Christ sin and death are defeated: "For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive." (1 Corinthians 15:22)
  • Reconciliation: "God was in Christ reconciling the world to himself" (2 Corinthians 5:19).
  • God "deeming it to be more befitting His power and goodness to bring good out of evil than to prevent the evil from coming into existence" (City of God XXII.1).
  • The Easter Vigil hymn (Exsultet) even sings of Adam's sin as "O happy fault", which gained so great a Redeemer.
Quick check
?Why did Hick call Augustine's theodicy "soul-deciding"?
Mission 2

Challenges: logic and science

  • Friedrich Schleiermacher (1768–1834), The Christian Faith (1821–22): a perfect creation that goes wrong is a contradiction. If it was flawless, it would not fall; if it fell, the flaw was there from the start, and God made it.
  • Where did the first evil will come from in perfect angels? Hick: Augustine needs evil to create itself out of nothing.
  • Evolution: the fossil record shows death, disease and predation for hundreds of millions of years before humans. Order did not become chaos at a human Fall.
  • Single pair: population genetics indicates humans descend from a population, not one couple, which undermines inheriting Adam's sin.
  • Genesis 2–3: many scholars and believers read the Eden story as myth or theology, not history.
Mission 2

Challenges: God's goodness

  • Hell: eternal punishment for finite sins seems inconsistent with an all-loving God.
  • Augustine thought most of humanity a "mass of perdition" (massa damnata), saved only by grace: critics ask how that is good.
  • Inherited guilt: why should anyone be punished for Adam's sin?
  • Innocent suffering: infants and animals suffer natural evil, though they have not sinned.
  • Foreknowledge: God knew angels and humans would fall, yet created them: does responsibility return to God?
  • Life after death: the theodicy depends on an afterlife; if none exists, much suffering is never made good.
Mission 2

Augustinian replies today

  • Many Christians read the Fall theologically: a truth about the human condition (we turn from God) rather than a dated event.
  • Catholic teaching: Genesis 3 uses figurative language but affirms a real primeval event at the start of human history (Catechism 390, paraphrase).
  • Privation is still widely defended (Aquinas; Herbert McCabe): evil as lack, not thing, keeps God the creator of good only.
  • Alvin Plantinga's free will defence builds on Augustine's free-will core (see Mission 4).
  • Some suggest fallen angels could explain natural evil before humans: critics call this unfalsifiable.
  • Strengths: takes sin and human responsibility seriously; fits Christian story of Fall and redemption; consistent with one good God.
Quick check
?Which criticism of Augustine is Schleiermacher's?
Mission 2Evaluate

"Augustine's theodicy spares God from blame for evil."

  • For: evil is not a created thing, so God creates only good (privation).
  • For: free creatures, not God, chose evil; free will is a great good worth its risk.
  • For: evil is overcome by the Cross; God brings good out of evil.
  • Against: a perfect creation could not fall unless flawed (Schleiermacher); God foreknew the Fall.
  • Against: natural evil long predates humans (evolution); single-pair descent is biologically unlikely.
  • Against: inherited guilt and eternal Hell sit badly with omnibenevolence.
  • Judge it by: Must the Fall be historical for the theodicy to work? Is privation a real answer or a play on words? Does responsibility pass back to the creator?
Mission check

?A doctor explains that cancer cells are ordinary cells that have lost the controls that normally regulate their growth. A critic says: "So God created cancer: God created evil." What reply would an Augustinian give?

Augustine places evil in creatures' misused freedom and calls it a lack of good. Critics ask whether the Fall makes sense after Darwin. Next: a theodicy that looks forward, not back.

Make a guess: Would a world with no pain at all be a better place to grow up in?

Mission 3

Irenaeus: made in the image, growing into the likeness

  • Irenaeus (c. 130–c. 202), bishop of Lyons; Against Heresies (c. 180), Book IV, chs. 37–39.
  • "God said, 'Let's make man in our image, after our likeness.'" (Genesis 1:26)
  • Irenaeus reads two stages: the image (reason and freedom) is given at creation; the likeness (moral and spiritual maturity, like God) must be grown into.
  • Humans were created immature, like children, not perfect: they could not receive perfection all at once.
  • We learn good by experiencing its opposite, as the tongue learns sweet by tasting bitter (IV.39, paraphrase).
  • So a world with evil and suffering is a place to grow, not a ruined paradise.
Mission 3

Hick: the vale of soul-making

  • John Hick (1922–2012), Evil and the God of Love (1966), Part IV.
  • He borrows the poet John Keats' phrase (letter of 1819): "Call the world if you Please 'The vale of Soul-making'."
  • The world is not a hedonistic paradise or a pet's cage designed for comfort. It is an environment for making persons.
  • Goodness that grows through free choices in a hard world is worth more than goodness made ready-made.
  • Hick accepts evolution: the first, biological stage of creation; the second, spiritual stage is our free growth.
Mission 3

Epistemic distance

  • If God's presence were overwhelming, humans could not freely choose to love God: they would be compelled.
  • So God creates humans at an epistemic distance: a distance in knowledge, not space.
  • The world looks "as if there were no God": it is religiously ambiguous.
  • Faith and love of God can then be a free response.
  • The story of the Fall becomes, for Hick, a myth of this distance, not a historical event.
Mission 3Read the source

Suffering that shapes

> "Not only this, but we also rejoice in our sufferings, knowing that suffering produces perseverance; and perseverance, proven character; and proven character, hope" (Romans 5:3–4, WEBBE)

> "Count it all joy, my brothers, when you fall into various temptations, knowing that the testing of your faith produces endurance." (James 1:2–3, WEBBE)

  • Hebrews 2:10 even speaks of the Son made "perfect through sufferings".

Q1 What chain does Paul describe?

Show answer

Suffering → perseverance → character → hope.

Q2 How does this support Hick?

Show answer

Suffering can be part of how character and faith are formed.

Quick check
?Why, according to Hick, does God create humans at an epistemic distance?
Mission 3

A world without pain?

Hick's thought experiment (paraphrase): imagine God prevented every harm.

  • A falling climber floats; a knife turns soft when used to hurt; fire never burns flesh.
  • There would be no danger, so no courage; no need, so no generosity or compassion; no wrong to resist.
  • Science would be impossible: nature would have no reliable laws.
  • So a world fit for soul-making needs regular natural laws that can cause pain.
  • Critics: some of these goods could exist with far less suffering than there is.
Mission 3

Beyond this life

  • Most people do not complete soul-making in this life.
  • Eschatological justification: the process continues after death, until all reach the likeness of God.
  • "For I consider that the sufferings of this present time are not worthy to be compared with the glory which will be revealed towards us." (Romans 8:18)
  • Universal salvation: Hick holds that all will finally be saved; if any were lost, their suffering would never be justified.
  • Mystery: Hick admits some suffering crushes rather than builds. Even this has a role: if suffering always visibly fitted what people deserved, compassion and goodness would become calculation.
Mission 3

Challenges to soul-making

  • Universal salvation unjust? If the unrepentant torturer and his victim share the same end, justice seems ignored; it also conflicts with texts on judgement (Matthew 25:46).
  • Suffering as a tool? D. Z. Phillips (1934–2006), The Problem of Evil and the Problem of God (2004): love is never expressed by inflicting suffering as a means; a God who did so would not be loving.
  • Immensity and unequal distribution: the Holocaust; some lives crushed, others easy. Does soul-making need so much, so unevenly?
  • Innocent suffering: children who die before any soul-making; animals, who have no souls to make on many views.
  • Life after death: the theodicy depends on an afterlife; without one, most suffering remains unjustified.
Mission 3

Strengths, and Augustine compared

  • Strengths: fits evolution and a developing world; gives natural evil a purpose; preserves free will; a loving God saves all.
  • Biblical roots: Genesis 1:26; Romans 5:3–4; Hebrews 2:10.
  • Augustine: looks back to a perfect creation and a Fall; evil a privation; soul-deciding; hell.
  • Irenaeus–Hick: looks forward to a perfection still to come; evil part of growth; soul-making; all saved.
  • Both: free will is central; both rely on an afterlife to complete the account.
Mission 3Evaluate

"The need for a vale of soul-making justifies the existence and extent of evil."

  • For: virtues freely developed are worth more than ready-made goodness; a world with real danger is needed for them.
  • For: regular natural laws, which can harm, are needed for science, planning and responsible choice.
  • For: an infinite future good may outweigh finite suffering (eschatological justification).
  • Against: the extent of suffering far exceeds what soul-making seems to need (Holocaust; the early deaths of children).
  • Against: using suffering as a means is incompatible with love (Phillips); universal salvation sits uneasily with justice.
  • Against: animal suffering over millions of years serves no soul-making.
  • Judge it by: existence or extent? Does an afterlife make the case, or assume it? Is a God who uses suffering still good?
Mission check

?A six-year-old dies in an earthquake, before she has had any chance to grow in courage or kindness. Which part of Hick's theodicy is designed to answer the objection that she never had the chance to grow?

Hick's theodicy looks forward to a future good; its critics ask whether any future good could justify this much suffering. Next: free will, the best possible world, and a God who cannot coerce.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Could God make people who are free but never choose evil? And if not, was freedom worth it?

Make a guess: Could God make people who are free but never choose evil? And if not, was freedom worth it?

Mission 4

The free will defence

  • Alvin Plantinga (b. 1932), God, Freedom, and Evil (1974).
  • A defence, not a theodicy: it does not claim to know God's actual reasons; it shows God and evil are logically consistent (answering Mackie).
  • A world with significantly free creatures, who do more good than evil, is more valuable than a world without them.
  • If God causes creatures to do only right, they are not free. So God cannot create moral good without allowing the possibility of moral evil.
  • Moral evil comes from creatures' misuse of freedom, not from any lack of God's power or goodness.
  • Natural evil: Plantinga notes it is possible that it too results from free non-human persons (fallen angels), which is enough for consistency.
Mission 4

Couldn't God make people who always choose good?

  • Mackie (1955) and Antony Flew (1923–2010): there is no contradiction in people who are free yet always choose good; so an omnipotent God could have made them.
  • This assumes compatibilism: freedom fits with being determined by one's nature.
  • Plantinga assumes libertarian freedom: a free choice is not determined by anything, even by God.
  • Transworld depravity: it is possible that every person God could create would freely go wrong at least once, in any world God could make actual.
  • If so, a world with moral good but no moral evil was not God's to create. Omnipotence means doing whatever is logically possible (Aquinas).
  • Many philosophers judge this enough to answer the logical problem; the evidential problem remains.
Quick check
?Why does Plantinga call his argument a defence rather than a theodicy?
Mission 4

Strengths and weaknesses of the free will defence

  • Strengths: takes human responsibility seriously; fits Christian, Jewish and Muslim teaching on moral choice; widely judged to answer the logical problem.
  • Weakness: scale. Could God not intervene sometimes, to stop a Holocaust, without abolishing freedom?
  • Weakness: victims. The abuser's freedom is respected; the victim's freedom is destroyed.
  • Weakness: natural evil. Blaming fallen angels for earthquakes looks ad hoc to critics.
  • Weakness: heaven. If the saved in heaven are free yet never sin, why could God not create that state from the start?
  • Edexcel criteria: fits origins of life (evolution)? fits God's nature? answers innocent suffering? needs life after death?
Mission 4

The best of all possible worlds

  • G. W. Leibniz (1646–1716), Théodicée (1710): he coined the word theodicy (God + justice).
  • A perfectly wise, good and powerful God would create the best possible world, not a perfect one: the best overall balance of good over evil.
  • Evils are parts of the best whole, as dissonance is part of the finest music; to remove them would make the whole worse.
  • Voltaire (1694–1778), Candide (1759), mocks this after the Lisbon earthquake (1755): Dr Pangloss insists all is for the best amid disaster.
  • Critics: for any world, a better one can be imagined, so there may be no "best possible"; and the claim cannot be tested.
Mission 4

Process theodicy: a God who persuades

  • A. N. Whitehead (1861–1947), Process and Reality (1929); David Ray Griffin (1939–2022), God, Power, and Evil: A Process Theodicy (1976).
  • God did not create from nothing (ex nihilo): God shapes a world from a pre-existing chaos.
  • Reality is made of events, each with some power to determine itself.
  • God persuades ("lures") each event towards the good, but cannot coerce it.
  • Evil arises when creatures do not follow God's lure.
  • God has all the power a being could have in such a world, but not unlimited power: so God is not responsible for evil.
Mission 4

Why risk it? The co-suffering God

  • Griffin: greater complexity brings greater capacity for enjoyment (rich experience) and for suffering: the two cannot be separated.
  • God judged a world capable of great value worth the risk of great suffering.
  • God is not a distant judge but feels every suffering with creatures: in Whitehead's words, "the great companion—the fellow-sufferer who understands".
  • Edexcel wording: God is a co-sufferer who cannot coerce the free will of human agents.
  • Some Christians link this to the cross: God sharing suffering, not standing outside it.
Quick check
?According to Griffin, why is God not to blame for evil?
Mission 4

Criticisms of process theodicy

  • Not the God of classical theism: not omnipotent, not creator from nothing. Is such a God worthy of worship?
  • Elitism: the rich experiences of some are said to justify the suffering of many. Would the victims agree?
  • No guaranteed victory: God cannot ensure evil will be overcome; there is no promise that every tear will be wiped away.
  • Unbiblical? It conflicts with creation ex nihilo and with miracles and resurrection as acts of power.
  • Strengths: God is not to blame; fits evolution; God's solidarity with sufferers is a real comfort.
  • Mackie had already called limiting God's power an "adequate" solution, but not one most theists accept.
Mission 4Evaluate

"Monotheism can be successfully defended in the face of evil."

  • For: the free will defence shows God and evil are logically consistent (Plantinga).
  • For: soul-making gives evil a purpose; eschatological justification answers scale (Hick).
  • For: process theodicy removes God's blame entirely (Griffin); privation keeps God the author of good only (Augustine).
  • Against: each defence pays a price: Augustine (science, Hell), Hick (suffering as a tool), Griffin (a limited God), free will (natural evil, scale).
  • Against: the evidential problem remains: Rowe's fawn, the early deaths of children from disease (see Mission 1).
  • Judge it by: "defended" as consistent, as probable, or as worthy of worship? Which God: classical or process? Does a defence need to convince the sufferer?
Mission check

?After a mine collapse, a chaplain says: "God was not absent. God was urging every rescuer on and grieving with every family, but God could not simply override the rock or the owners' choices." Which response to evil does this express?

The responses differ most on God's power: classical theists keep omnipotence and give reasons; process thinkers limit it and keep God beside the sufferer.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Moral evil comes from free agents; natural (non-moral) evil from nature.
  • Logical problem: Epicurus via Hume; Mackie's inconsistent triad plus two rules. Evidential problem: Rowe's fawn.

Mission 2

  • Creation was good; evil is a privation of good with a deficient, not efficient, cause.
  • Angels and humans misused free will; the Fall brought sin and natural disorder (Romans 5:12; seminal presence in Adam).
  • Soul-deciding: this life leads to heaven or just punishment; the Cross overcomes evil. Challenges: Schleiermacher, evolution, single pair, Hell, innocent suffering.

Mission 3

  • Irenaeus: humans made immature, in God's image, growing into God's likeness. Hick: the world is a vale of soul-making; epistemic distance allows free faith; regular laws and second-order goods; eschatological justification and universal salvation.
  • Challenges: universal salvation unjust, suffering as a tool (Phillips), immensity and unequal distribution.

Mission 4

  • Free will defence (Plantinga): moral good requires free creatures able to choose evil; answers the logical problem; critics: scale, victims, natural evil.
  • Process theodicy (Whitehead, Griffin): God persuades, cannot coerce, and co-suffers; critics: a God not worth worshipping, elitism, no final victory.
  • Leibniz: the best of all possible worlds.
