Mission 1

Earth's structure and the plates

  • Crust: oceanic about 5 km thick; continental about 30 km on average, up to 100 km under mountains.
  • Mantle about 2,900 km thick; core: liquid outer (about 2,200 km) and solid inner (about 1,250 km).
  • Plates are pieces of rigid lithosphere (crust plus the rigid uppermost mantle, at least 80 km thick over much of Earth) moving over the hot asthenosphere: solid rock that can flow very slowly.
Mission 1

The global pattern

  • Plate boundaries form a connected network: divergent ridges through the oceans, convergent margins around much of the Pacific and along the Alpine–Himalayan belt, and transform faults offsetting ridges.
  • About 81% of the world's largest earthquakes occur around the Pacific, where oceanic plates subduct; about 17% in the Alpide belt; a third belt follows the Mid-Atlantic Ridge.
  • There are about 1,350 potentially active volcanoes, not counting ocean-floor spreading centres; many ring the Pacific.
Mission 1

Causes of earthquakes, eruptions and tsunamis

  • Earthquakes: stress builds where plates lock, then releases suddenly along faults, at all three margin types.
  • Eruptions: magma forms where plates part, where slabs sink, or above plumes.
  • Tsunamis: mostly from large undersea earthquakes at subduction zones that lift the sea floor. The 2004 magnitude 9.1 Indonesia earthquake generated a tsunami that killed over 230,000 people.
Mission 1

What moves the plates?

  • Earth's heat comes from radioactive decay and residual heat; hot material rises and cold, dense material sinks: mantle convection.
  • Ridge push: hot, elevated new lithosphere slides "downhill" away from a mid-ocean ridge.
  • Slab pull: a cold, dense slab sinking at a subduction zone drags the plate behind it. Plates attached to slabs (Pacific, Australian, Nazca) move fastest.
  • USGS: no mechanism "can explain all the facets of plate movement".
Quick check

Mission 1

Sea-floor spreading and palaeomagnetism

  • From 1961, scientists proposed that ocean floor spreads from mid-ocean ridges; Hess described new crust moving away "in a conveyor belt-like motion".
  • Palaeomagnetism: cooling lava records Earth's magnetic field. Stripes of normal and reversed magnetism lie in rows on either side of the ridge, showing the field "has flip-flopped many times".
  • Rocks are youngest at the crest and older away from it: evidence that new crust forms at ridges and moves outwards.
Quick check
?A survey finds magnetic stripes that are mirror images on either side of a mid-ocean ridge. What does this show?
Mission 1

Divergent (constructive) margins

  • Plates move apart; magma rises and builds an ocean ridge. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge spreads about 2.5 cm a year; the East Pacific Rise more than 15 cm a year.
  • Iceland sits on the ridge; rifting at Krafla opened about 7 m in 1975–84.
  • On continents, stretching forms rift valleys: a new spreading centre may be developing along the East African Rift; spreading opened the Red Sea.
  • Shallow earthquakes; basaltic, mostly effusive eruptions.
Mission 1

Convergent: oceanic–continental

  • The oceanic Nazca Plate is subducted under the South American Plate, forming a deep-sea trench and lifting the Andes.
  • This convergence "sustains many of the Earth's active volcanoes"; strong earthquakes are common, some very deep (636 km under Bolivia).
  • Water released from the sinking slab helps the mantle above it melt, feeding viscous, explosive eruptions.
Mission 1

Convergent: oceanic–oceanic

  • One oceanic plate sinks beneath the other, forming a trench and a curved chain of volcanic islands: an island arc (the Marianas; the Aleutians).
  • Challenger Deep, in the Mariana Trench, is about 10,935 m deep.
  • Frequent moderate to strong earthquakes along the descending plate.
Mission 1

Convergent: continental–continental (collision)

  • When two continents meet, "neither is subducted because the continental rocks are relatively light".
  • India's collision with Asia, about 50 million years ago, crumpled the crust into the Himalaya: fold mountains.
  • Edexcel names it a collision margin, separate from destructive.
  • Strong shallow earthquakes; little or no volcanic activity.
Quick check
?Which landform pair is matched to the correct convergent margin?
Mission 1

Conservative (transform) margins

  • Plates slide horizontally past each other; most transform faults are on the ocean floor, offsetting ridges.
  • The San Andreas fault zone is about 1,300 km long; the Pacific Plate has moved past the North American Plate at about 5 cm a year for 10 million years.
  • Shallow earthquakes; little or no vulcanicity, because crust is neither made nor destroyed.
Mission 1

Hot spots and mantle plumes

  • A mantle plume is a column of exceptionally hot material feeding a hotspot at the base of a plate.
  • Hawaii formed more than 3,200 km from the nearest plate boundary; islands get older away from the hotspot (Big Island under 0.7 million years; Kauai about 5.5 million) as the Pacific Plate moves.
  • Some hotspots, such as Iceland's, sit near ridges; some studies suggest hotspots are neither deep nor fixed.
Mission 1

Intra-plate earthquakes

  • New Madrid, central USA, far inside the North American Plate: three earthquakes of magnitude 7.0 or more in 1811–12.
  • Seismicity there may come from movement on old faults of the Reelfoot rift, in response to compressive stress from plate motions.
  • USGS: "No one knows what causes New Madrid earthquakes".
  • Exam move (Edexcel): intra-plate quakes are often explained by reactivated ancient faults; say how certain the evidence is.
Mission check

?The Pacific Plate is almost ringed by subduction zones and is one of the fastest-moving plates. What does this suggest moves plates?

Internal heat (radioactive decay and residual heat) drives convection; gravity turns it into motion through slab pull and ridge push. The Atlantic widens as plates move apart at the Mid-Atlantic Ridge.

Mission 2

Focus, epicentre and depth

  • The focus (hypocentre) is where rupture starts underground; the epicentre is the point on the surface above it.
  • Depth classes: shallow 0–70 km, intermediate 70–300 km, deep 300–700 km.
  • Shallow-focus earthquakes occur at every type of margin. Deep-focus ones occur only inside slabs sinking at subduction zones.
Mission 2

The Benioff zone

  • Plot the foci beneath a subduction zone and they form a sloping band that deepens away from the trench: the (Wadati–)Benioff zone.
  • It traces the sinking slab: every earthquake deeper than 70 km lies within "great slabs of lithosphere".
  • Example: the Sea of Okhotsk earthquake (24 May 2013) was Mw 8.3 at about 600 km depth, "deep within the subducting Pacific lithosphere".
  • Deep-focus earthquakes "cause less damage on the ground surface" than similar shallow ones.
Quick check

Mission 2

P, S and L waves

  • Rupture on a fault fractures the crust and releases energy as seismic waves.
  • P waves (primary): compressional, fastest, 5–7 km/s in typical crust.
  • S waves (secondary): shear waves, about 60% of P-wave speed; they cannot travel through liquids.
  • L waves (surface: Love and Rayleigh): slowest, but with the largest ground motions; they "cause buildings to fail".

Mission 2

Ground shaking and displacement

  • Ground shaking is the main primary hazard: surface waves cause buildings to fail and fall.
  • Ground displacement: the fault itself moves. Kaikōura (New Zealand), 2016: the rupture "unzipped" about 180 km in nearly 2 minutes, displacing the ground by up to 12 m.
  • More than 20 faults ruptured, a world record; parts of the coast rose by up to 6.5 m.
Mission 2

Liquefaction

  • Liquefaction: loosely packed, saturated sediments lose strength in strong shaking.
  • Buildings sink or tilt, and buried pipes can float up.
  • Christchurch (New Zealand): in March 2012 the government rezoned 251 more properties red, citing "widespread liquefaction".
Mission 2

Landslides

  • Shaking triggers landslides on steep slopes: at least 25,000 in Nepal in 2015; over 10,000 at Kaikōura in 2016.
Quick check

Mission 2

How a tsunami forms

  • At a subduction zone the plates lock, then the fault slips in a great earthquake.
  • The sea bed is thrust up (or drops) and lifts the whole water column above it: a tsunami.
  • In deep water a tsunami travels at 800–1,000 km/h with a wavelength of 500–1,000 km; near shore it slows to 30–50 km/h, and its height and steepness grow.
Mission 2

Tsunami impacts

  • 2004, Indian Ocean (Mw 9.1, Sumatra–Andaman): the tsunami killed over 230,000 people. The USGS records deaths in 10 countries.
  • 2011, Tōhoku (Japan): the tsunami overtopped seawalls of mainly 5–10 m; more than 15,000 people died. Inundation was much wider than hazard maps predicted.
  • Near the source there is little time: at Kaikōura the first wave arrived within 10 minutes, with a 6.9 m run-up at Goose Bay.
Quick check

Mission check

In 2004 the sea bed moved along the Sumatra–Andaman subduction zone and lifted the whole water column. The tsunami crossed the Indian Ocean at deep-water speeds of 800–1,000 km/h and killed people in 10 countries.

Mission 3

What makes an eruption explosive

  • Magma composition, viscosity and gas content largely decide whether an eruption is explosive.
  • Silica has the greatest effect on viscosity; most igneous rocks hold about 45–78% silica by weight.
  • Low-silica basalt is runny: at Kīlauea, lava flow fronts move up to 10 km/h. Gas escapes easily, so eruptions are effusive.
  • Silica-rich magma is sticky and traps gas, so pressure builds until it erupts explosively.
Mission 3

Eruption style follows the setting

  • Constructive (divergent) margins: basalt from the mantle; effusive fissure eruptions. Reykjanes, Iceland, is where the Mid-Atlantic Ridge rises above sea level.
  • Destructive (convergent) margins: the sinking plate feeds more silica-rich, gas-rich magma; explosive stratovolcanoes. Fuego sits where the Cocos Plate subducts beneath the Caribbean Plate.
  • Hot spots: plumes away from margins. Hawaii is more than 3,200 km from the nearest plate boundary; its eruptions are effusive.
Mission 3

Measuring eruption size: the VEI

  • The Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI) runs from 0 (non-explosive); 8 is the top of the usual scale, though USGS calls it open-ended.
  • Each step is about 10 times the volume of ash, except VEI 1. Mount St Helens (1980) was VEI 5: about 1 km³ of ash.
  • Most Hawaiian eruptions rate VEI 0 or 1.
Quick check

Mission 3

Primary hazards: lava and pyroclastic flows

  • Lava flows are usually slow enough to walk away from, so they mostly destroy property: at Grindavík in January 2024, lava burned three homes.
  • Pyroclastic flows: avalanches of hot rock fragments and gas, typically faster than 80 km/h and 200–700 °C. They destroy nearly everything in their path.
  • Their speed means evacuation before they start is the only reliable protection.
Mission 3

Primary hazards: tephra, ash and gases

  • Tephra: any rock fragments blasted into the air, from fine ash to large bombs.
  • Ash is "hard, does not dissolve in water, is extremely abrasive and mildly corrosive". It can collapse roofs, and wet ash can weigh twice as much as dry. Jet engines have failed after flying through thin ash.
  • Gases: water vapour is the most abundant; carbon dioxide and sulfur dioxide (SO₂) matter most.
  • SO₂ "can cause acid rain and air pollution downwind". CO₂ is dense and collects in hollows: three ski patrol members died at Mammoth Mountain, California, after falling into a CO₂-filled snow depression.
Quick check
?Why does ash fall kill more people when heavy rain falls at the same time?
Mission 3

Secondary hazard: lahars

  • Lahars (volcanic mudflows) are slurries of debris and water that look like wet concrete.
  • Water comes from melting snow and ice, heavy rain, or crater lakes and rivers.
  • They often grow to more than 10 times their starting size, and can exceed 200 km/h on steep slopes.
  • They follow river valleys far from the volcano, and outlast the eruption: rain after Fuego's 2018 eruption produced many lahars.
Mission 3

Ice-melt floods: jökulhlaups

  • A jökulhlaup (Icelandic: glacier burst) is a flood released when an eruption under a glacier melts ice and the meltwater escapes.
  • 1996, Vatnajökull: meltwater from the Gjálp eruption (late September to early October) collected in the Grímsvötn caldera. The flood came weeks later, on 5 November.
  • Peak flow was about 33,000 m³/s in the Gígjukvísl river. The flood breached the road across the outwash plain and removed the Gígjukvísl bridge.
  • Lahar or jökulhlaup? Both need meltwater; a jökulhlaup is mostly water, a lahar mostly debris.
Quick check
?In 1996 the Gjálp eruption began under Vatnajökull in late September, but the flood that destroyed the Gígjukvísl bridge came on 5 November. Why the delay?
Mission 3

Armero, 1985

  • Nevado del Ruiz (Colombia) is a 5,389 m stratovolcano topped by glaciers.
  • On 13 November 1985 an explosive eruption melted ice. Lahars raced down river valleys and reached Armero, 45 km away, about two hours later.
  • Within minutes about 23,000 people, most of the town, were killed.
  • Why no escape: scientists lacked the equipment and government support to monitor the volcano, the government was focused on political unrest, and residents had been told they were safe. USGS: two hours was "plenty of time" to evacuate.
Mission check

?A town of 20,000 lies in a river valley 50 km from an ice-capped stratovolcano. No lava could reach it. How could an eruption still kill most of its people hours later?

At Armero, melted ice made lahars that followed the valleys 45 km to the town in about two hours. Weak monitoring and governance meant no warning reached people who had time to climb to higher ground.

Mission 4

Natural hazard or disaster?

  • A natural hazard is a natural process or event that could cause loss of life, injury or damage: shaking, a lahar, a tsunami.
  • A disaster is "a serious disruption of the functioning of a community or a society at any scale", caused when hazardous events meet exposed, vulnerable people and lead to human, material, economic or environmental losses.
  • So an earthquake in an empty desert is a hazard event, not a disaster.
Mission 4

Vulnerability, capacity and resilience

  • Vulnerability: conditions "which increase the susceptibility" of people or assets to a hazard.
  • Capacity: "the strengths, attributes and resources" available to manage and reduce risk.
  • Resilience: the ability to "resist, absorb, accommodate, adapt to, transform and recover".
  • The resilience threshold: the point beyond which a community can no longer cope with its own resources. Push past it and a hazard becomes a disaster.
Mission 4

The hazard risk equation

  • Risk = (hazard × vulnerability) ÷ capacity to cope.
  • Risk rises with a more severe or more frequent hazard, and with vulnerability; it falls as capacity grows.
  • UNDRR adds exposure (who and what is in harm's way): risk is "a function of hazard, exposure, vulnerability and capacity".
  • It is a way of thinking, not a calculation.

Mission 4

The Pressure and Release (PAR) model

  • PAR (Blaikie, Cannon, Davis and Wisner, 1994): a disaster is where two "pressures" meet: the hazard on one side, vulnerability on the other.
  • Vulnerability builds in three stages: root causes (limited access to power, structures and resources) → dynamic pressures (lack of training, local markets or press freedom; rapid urbanisation) → unsafe conditions (fragile housing on dangerous land; low incomes).
  • Release: reduce the pressures at each stage, and the disaster shrinks.
  • It shows the hazard's wider context: a disaster's causes can lie far from the fault, and long before the event.
Mission 4

PAR applied: Nepal, 2015

  • Unsafe conditions: over half a million houses were destroyed; many "did not have any seismic-resistant features".
  • Root causes and pressures: "poorer rural areas" were hit hardest; the six lowest-HDI districts with losses above NPR 130,000 per person included Dolakha, Sindhupalchowk and Gorkha.
  • Hazard: Mw 7.8, focus about 8 km deep.
  • Exam move: PAR is a model, not a measurement. Name which evidence sits at which stage, and say what is hard to measure.

Mission 4

Inequality: income

  • UNDRR (all disaster types, 2000–2019): low-income countries had 23% of disaster deaths but under 10% of the world's population.
  • They also had the most deaths per event (284).
  • High-income countries had 67% of economic losses, US$1.99 trillion.
  • Pattern: richer places lose more money; poorer places lose more lives.
Mission 4

Inequality: education, housing and healthcare

  • Education: a review of eleven studies found "consistent and robust evidence" that formal education reduces vulnerability. Countries with more women educated to secondary level had lower disaster mortality.
  • Housing: in Nepal, damage fell most on houses without seismic-resistant features, mostly in poorer rural areas.
  • Healthcare: access to care decides whether the injured survive the hours and days after an event; remote, poorer areas wait longest.
Mission 4

Aceh 2004: who was exposed

  • Oxfam (2005): in Kuala Cangkoy, four females died for every male. In Cuddalore (India), 391 women died, against 146 men.
  • The wave struck on a Sunday morning, when women were at home near the shore and men were away.
  • Many women stayed to look for their children; men more often than women could swim or climb trees.
  • The hazard was the same for everyone; exposure and access to skills were not.
Quick check

Mission 4

Governance: national and local

  • National: Armero, 1985. Scientists lacked the equipment and government support to monitor Nevado del Ruiz, and the government saw political unrest as "more pressing".
  • Local (city): Freetown (Sierra Leone), 2017 landslide. The assessment found "highly fragmented governance in the urban area"; land and building permits were "split between two ministries", so no single body controlled building on the slopes.
  • Strong governance: New Zealand's Building Code requires buildings to "withstand likely loads, including wind, earthquake".
Mission 4

Geographical factors

  • Population density and urbanisation: people living in seismic areas rose from about 1.41 billion (1975) to 2.72 billion (2015); the JRC expects exposure to rise "with a growing population and urbanizing area".
  • Isolation and accessibility: road damage further isolated rural Nepal; landslides cut Kaikōura's road and rail links.
  • Time: Nepal's earthquake struck at 11:56 on a Saturday, when schools were closed. A night-time quake would "certainly" have killed more.

Mission check

?In Japan's 2011 tsunami, people over 60 were about 31% of the area's population but about 65% of the dead. What best explains this?

A wave does not choose. Exposure (who is where, when) and vulnerability (what people can do) decide who dies. In Aceh it was gender roles and skills; in Japan, age and mobility. Risk = (hazard × vulnerability) ÷ capacity.

Mission 5

Magnitude: how big the earthquake is

  • Magnitude measures the energy released at the source; it does not change with distance.
  • The Richter scale (1935) is now used mainly for small, local earthquakes. USGS uses the moment magnitude scale (Mw) for the rest.
  • Each whole number up means 10 times the measured amplitude and about 32 times the energy.
  • Agencies can differ: Nepal's seismological centre gave the 2015 earthquake 7.6; USGS gives Mw 7.8. Name your source.
Mission 5

Intensity: what people feel

  • Intensity is the effect of an earthquake at a place. The US uses the Modified Mercalli Intensity (MMI) scale, in Roman numerals.
  • It has no mathematical basis: it is "an arbitrary ranking based on observed effects".
  • Low numbers describe how people feel the shaking; engineers help assign VIII and above.
  • One earthquake has one magnitude but many intensities, usually falling with distance.
Quick check
?Using about 32 times the energy for each whole step, how many times as much energy does an Mw 7.8 release as an Mw 5.8?
Mission 5

Volcanic magnitude: the VEI

  • The Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI) runs from 0; each step is about 10 times the ash volume (except VEI 1).
  • VEI 5, such as Mount St Helens in 1980, is about 1 km³ of ash; most Hawaiian eruptions are VEI 0–1.
  • A VEI 8 eruption puts out more than 1,000 km³. Pinatubo in 1991 ejected more than 5 km³.
  • The VEI measures explosivity only: a huge effusive lava eruption can score low.
Mission 5

Magnitude and frequency

  • Small events are far more frequent than large ones. Averages from USGS counts, 2000–2021: about 1,613 earthquakes of M5–5.9 a year, 137 of M6–6.9, 14 of M7–7.9 and 1.2 of M8 or more.
  • Over the long term USGS expects about 15 M7 and one M8+ a year.
  • Volcanoes follow the same pattern: each VEI step is rarer.
Mission 5

Regular or random?

  • Parkfield, on the San Andreas Fault, had magnitude-6 earthquakes in 1857, 1881, 1901, 1922, 1934 and 1966. Gaps: 12–32 years, average about 22.
  • In 1985 USGS forecast another "within the next several years". It came on 28 September 2004 (Mw 6.0), 38 years after 1966.
  • So even Parkfield, with an unusually regular record, is only roughly regular: the timing of the next earthquake is close to random.
  • Volcanoes are no more regular: in the Cascades, eruptions averaged about 2 per century, in clusters.
Quick check

Mission 5

Predictability

  • Earthquakes: USGS: "Neither the USGS nor any other scientists have ever predicted a major earthquake". Where is predictable (plate margins); when is not.
  • Instead, hazard maps give probabilities: the shaking with a 10% or 2% chance of being exceeded in 50 years.
  • Volcanoes give warning signs. At Svartsengi, GPS showed 35 cm of uplift by mid-December 2023; forecasts at Pinatubo saved at least 5,000 lives.

The hazard management cycle

  • Mitigation: reduce the hazard's effects or people's vulnerability before it strikes.
  • Preparedness: warnings, plans, drills and stockpiles.
  • Response: rescue and relief in the hours and days after.
  • Recovery: rehabilitation (weeks to months: shelter, water, services), then reconstruction (years).
  • How a place recovers sets its vulnerability for the next event, so the cycle repeats.
Mission 10

Park's model

  • Park's model (Park, 1991) plots quality of life against time: normal → a sharp fall at impact → relief → rehabilitation → reconstruction.
  • A well-prepared place falls less and recovers faster; reconstruction can end above the old level (built back better) or below it.
  • Limitation: it is a generalised curve, not a measurement, and recovery is uneven between groups.

  • Land-use zoning: California's Alquist-Priolo Act bars buildings for people from active fault traces, with a set-back of 15 m (50 feet). After 2011, Christchurch's 602-hectare red zone became a river corridor.
  • Engineering defences: in 2011 Japan's tsunami overtopped seawalls of mainly 5–10 m; more than 15,000 people died.
  • Japan now plans for two levels: defences for frequent tsunamis, and evacuation-centred measures for the largest. Walls of about 12 m are planned; critics argue relocating shrinking communities may be more efficient.
Mission 10

Modify the event: hazard-resistant design

  • Braced frames and shear walls resist sideways forces; base isolation bearings change how a building moves and add damping.
  • Codes matter: in Mashiki (Kumamoto, Japan, 2016) wooden houses built before 1981 collapsed far more often than newer ones, so retrofitting older buildings is a priority.
  • New Zealand's Building Code requires buildings to withstand earthquake loads.
Mission 10

Modify the event: lava diversion

  • Earth barriers at Fagradalsfjall (2021) delayed lava by up to 16 days.
  • At Heimaey (1973), seawater sprayed on a lava flow cooled and slowed it, saving the harbour.
  • Limit: at Grindavík on 14 January 2024 a fissure opened outside the barrier, and its lava burned three homes.
  • Diversion works for slow lava, not for pyroclastic flows or lahars.

Mission 10

Hong Kong: preventing slope failure

  • Shaking and rain both trigger landslides, so slope engineering is tectonic mitigation too.
  • Methods: drain surface and groundwater, cover slopes, build retaining walls or toe berms, remove weight from the crest.
  • Hong Kong set up a slope-safety system in 1977, after the Sau Mau Ping disaster of 1976. In 2013 the government said the risk from man-made slopes had fallen by 75%; its engineers now say "less than 20% of the 1977 level".
  • Note: the government is measuring its own programme.
Mission 10

Modify the loss: emergency aid and community action

  • Community action comes first: in Nepal in 2015, neighbours, especially young people, dug others out of the rubble.
  • Emergency aid: Nepal asked for international help within hours; 134 search-and-rescue teams from 34 countries came. Helicopters made 4,236 flights.
  • Short-term aid: transit shelters opened in Kathmandu, but tarpaulins ran short.
  • NGOs: the Red Cross used volunteer maps; in Iceland the IFRC's Disaster Response Emergency Fund (DREF) supported the response to Grindavík's evacuation.
Mission 10

Modify the loss: long-term aid and technology

  • Long-term aid: a World Bank-supported project funded over 300,000 of Nepal's more than 700,000 rebuilt homes.
  • Grants of NPR 300,000 came in three parts, paid only after engineers certified safe building; mobile masons were recruited to help.
  • Volunteer mapping: over 6,000 volunteers added data to OpenStreetMap, used by Nepal's army and the Red Cross.
  • Phones: anonymised data from 12 million phones showed an estimated 390,000 more people than normal leaving the Kathmandu Valley.
Mission 10

Modify the loss: insurance

  • New Zealand: about 40,000 claims after Kaikōura went to the state Earthquake Commission; insured homeowners get cover generally up to NZ$300,000 plus GST.
  • Iceland: NTÍ covers buildings damaged by eruptions and earthquakes.
  • California: only 15.24% of homeowners' policies included earthquake cover in 2025.
  • Insurance spreads the loss and speeds rebuilding; it does not save lives.

Mission 10

Evaluating management

  • Mitigation can pay: a US study found federal mitigation grants "save $6 per $1 spent", and adopting model building codes $11.
  • Pre-event measures can be exceeded by rarer events (Tōhoku's walls).
  • Rescue saves lives in hours; reconstruction decides how vulnerable people are next time.
  • Scale: local (barriers, slope drains), national (codes, insurance), global (volunteer mapping, phone alerts).
Mission check

Hong Kong's slope stabilisation cut the risk from man-made slopes by about 75% (2013), and US studies find mitigation repays its cost several times over. But defences can be exceeded, so rescue, rehabilitation and reconstruction still matter, and Nepal's inspected rebuilding turned recovery into lower future vulnerability.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Heat drives convection; gravity drives slab pull and ridge push. Margins: divergent (ridges, rift valleys), convergent (trenches, island arcs, volcanoes, fold mountains), conservative (transform faults, shallow quakes).
  • Sea-floor spreading is shown by symmetric palaeomagnetic stripes; plumes make hotspots; intra-plate quakes such as New Madrid may come from old faults.

Mission 2

  • P, S and L waves bring shaking; secondary hazards include liquefaction, landslides.
  • Subduction earthquakes lift the water column to make tsunamis, which give distant coasts hours of warning but nearby coasts minutes.

Mission 3

  • Silica and gas decide eruption style; setting decides the magma: effusive at constructive margins and hot spots, explosive at destructive margins.

Mission 4

  • A hazard becomes a disaster when it meets exposed, vulnerable people and passes their resilience threshold.
  • PAR traces vulnerability from root causes through dynamic pressures to unsafe conditions.
  • Inequality (income, education, housing, healthcare), governance and geography (density, isolation, urbanisation) explain why the same hazard harms some more than others.

Mission 5

  • Magnitude (Richter, Mw; VEI) measures the event; intensity (Modified Mercalli) measures its effects at a place.
  • Small events are frequent, large ones rare; timing is close to random, so we forecast probabilities rather than predict dates.
  • Hazard profiles compare onset, extent, duration, frequency and predictability.

Mission 6

  • Nepal 2015 (developing) and Kaikōura 2016 (developed): same magnitude, very different impacts, because of buildings, poverty, exposure and timing.
  • Short-term responses rescue; long-term responses rebuild, and decide future vulnerability.
  • Türkiye 2023 (emerging) shows that severity must be judged relative to population and economy.

Mission 7

  • Plate setting sets the hazard: explosive pyroclastic flows at Fuego, effusive lava at Reykjanes.
  • Evacuation practice, communication and capacity explain who survived; insurance and buy-outs shape recovery.
  • Pinatubo shows an emerging economy's forecast saving lives, while ash and typhoon rain still killed hundreds.

Mission 8

  • Mega-disasters: 2004 tsunami (regional, over 230,000 killed); Eyjafjallajökull 2010 (VEI 3–4, global aviation disruption: interdependence); Japan 2011 (US$309 billion loss; nuclear policy change in Japan and Germany).
  • Development: richer countries suffer larger economic losses, poorer ones larger death tolls. Multiple-hazard zones such as the Philippines (about 20 tropical cyclones a year, active volcanoes, large earthquakes) suffer compounding disasters, e.g. Pinatubo with Typhoon Yunya and later lahars.

Mission 9

  • Large earthquakes are not more frequent; recorded disasters and losses rise with exposure and reporting.
  • Trend data since 1960 need care: reporting, inflation and disputed tolls.
  • Prediction is not possible for earthquakes; forecasts, monitoring, preparedness, zoning and insurance reduce losses.

Mission 10

  • The management cycle runs mitigation → preparedness → response → recovery; Park's model shows how preparation and wealth shape the fall and recovery.
  • Modify the event (zoning, design, defences, diversion), the vulnerability (warnings, preparedness) and the loss (aid, insurance, community action).
