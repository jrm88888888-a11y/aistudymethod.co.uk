Jekyll and Hyde · structure and narratorsScreen 5

Holding back the answer

  • Delayed revelation: the explanation of Hyde comes only in the last two chapters.
  • Clues arrive as documents: a cheque (ch 1), Jekyll's will (ch 2), a letter signed by Hyde (ch 5), Lanyon's sealed envelope (ch 6), Jekyll's confession (ch 10).
  • Like a detective story, we follow Utterson, who knows little more than we do.
  • Genre (Gothic mystery) is one of the literary contexts your spec lists.

The narrative stays with Utterson for eight chapters, so we only know what a respectable lawyer can find out. Clues come as documents that are sealed or misread, and the full explanation comes last, in Lanyon's and Jekyll's own written accounts.

Mission 2

Is Hyde a monster the potion made, or Jekyll all along?

Make a guess: Is Hyde a monster the potion made, or Jekyll all along?

Jekyll and Hyde · dualityScreen 1

A double life before the potion

  • Jekyll hid his "pleasures" to keep "a more than commonly grave countenance before the public".
  • He calls this "a profound duplicity of life".
  • He concludes "man is not truly one, but truly two" and dreams of housing good and evil "in separate identities".
  • Duality: two opposed sides in one person. The potion is Jekyll's attempt to split them.
  • All of this comes from chapter 10, "Henry Jekyll's Full Statement of the Case".

Jekyll and Hyde · dualityScreen 6

Two ways to read Hyde

  • Many readers see Hyde as Jekyll's own hidden desires set free: the drug "but shook the doors of the prisonhouse of my disposition".
  • Others stress Jekyll's excuses: he treats Hyde as a separate "familiar", so "Jekyll was no worse".
  • Either way, the split fails: Hyde's body "had grown in stature", and the changes start to happen on their own.
  • Remember who narrates chapter 10: Jekyll himself.

Mission 3

Utterson is a lawyer. Why can't he solve the case?

Make a guess: Utterson is a lawyer. Why can't he solve the case?

Jekyll and Hyde · Utterson and the gentlemenScreen 1

Meet Utterson

  • A lawyer, "cold, scanty and embarrassed in discourse", yet "somehow lovable" (ch 1).
  • Strict with himself, tolerant of others: "I let my brother go to the devil in his own way."
  • Often "the last reputable acquaintance" of men going downhill.
  • Chapters 1–8 stay close to him, so his blind spots become the reader's.

Jekyll and Hyde · Utterson and the gentlemenScreen 6

Detective, or keeper of secrets?

  • Some readers see Utterson as a detective: "If he be Mr. Hyde," he thinks, "I shall be Mr. Seek."
  • Others note how often he chooses silence: he locks Guest's comparison of the handwriting into his safe, and in chapter 8 hopes to "save his credit".
  • The critic Elaine Showalter reads this world of bachelors as one where men could not speak of their private lives.
  • Characters are constructs: write Stevenson presents Utterson as…

Mission checkScreen 8

?Utterson is a lawyer. Why can't he solve the case?

Utterson tries hard, but he reads every clue the way a respectable gentleman would: as blackmail (ch 1–2), forgery (ch 5) or a disfiguring illness (ch 8). His code of silence and loyalty keeps him from asking the questions that would expose his friend, so the truth only comes from the documents in chapters 9 and 10.

Jekyll and Hyde · secrecy and reputationScreen 5

Doors, safes and seals

  • Secrets live in objects: a door "equipped with neither bell nor knocker" (ch 1), Utterson's safe (ch 2, 5), Lanyon's envelope marked "PRIVATE" (ch 6), Jekyll's sealed confession (ch 8).
  • A repeated image like this is a motif.
  • Each one has to be opened, or broken down, before the truth comes out.
Jekyll and Hyde · secrecy and reputationScreen 6

Where the double life began

  • Jekyll "concealed my pleasures" to look "grave" in public, hiding them "with an almost morbid sense of shame" (ch 10).
  • He never says what the pleasures were, only "undignified".
  • Many readers link this to Victorian repression: desires hidden, not removed.
  • Elaine Showalter connects the novel's secrecy with an 1885 law against sexual acts between men, in force from January 1886. Others read drink or gambling. The novel leaves it open.

Mission checkScreen 8

?A man is murdered. Why do his friends worry about a good name?

In this world a good name was a gentleman's livelihood, and scandal could drag down anyone near it. So Utterson worries that Jekyll's name will be "sucked down in the eddy of the scandal", and Jekyll himself admits "I was thinking of my own character". Stevenson suggests respectability can matter more to these men than the truth.

Jekyll and Hyde · science, religion and the supernaturalScreen 5

Sin and the Bible

  • Jekyll writes "If I am the chief of sinners", echoing St Paul: "Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners; of whom I am chief" (1 Timothy 1:15, King James Version).
  • Utterson reads "Satan's signature upon a face" in Hyde's (ch 2).
  • In the cabinet, a pious book Jekyll admired is "annotated, in his own hand with startling blasphemies" (ch 8).
  • These are biblical allusions and religious imagery: they frame Jekyll's experiment as sin, not only science.
Jekyll and Hyde · science, religion and the supernaturalScreen 6

Science, sin or the supernatural?

  • The novel offers chemistry: a white salt, "a blood-red liquor", an impure first supply.
  • Yet Jekyll won't explain the science ("my discoveries were incomplete") and says the drug "was neither diabolical nor divine": what came out depended on him.
  • Some readers see a warning about science going too far; others a story about sin, with science only the means. Strong answers show how Stevenson blurs them.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Is Jekyll's potion science, sin or magic?

All three, and the blur is the point. The potion is chemistry (a salt and a tincture), but Stevenson surrounds it with the language of sin and the supernatural: Satan, blasphemy, a "prodigy". The novel suggests science alone can't explain or control the evil it releases.

Mission 6

Why do so many characters describe Hyde like an animal?

Make a guess: Why do so many characters describe Hyde like an animal?

Jekyll and Hyde · Darwin, degeneration and LondonScreen 1

Darwin and the fear of going backwards

  • Darwin's On the Origin of Species (1859) argued that species evolve; The Descent of Man (1871) applied this to humans.
  • Some Victorians feared evolution could run backwards: degeneration. E. Ray Lankester's Degeneration: A Chapter in Darwinism (1880) argued species could decline; Cesare Lombroso (1876) claimed some criminals were primitive throwbacks, atavism.
  • The novel was published in January 1886.

Jekyll and Hyde · Gothic setting and languageScreen 5

A building with a face

  • The block with the door shows "a blind forehead of discoloured wall" and "the marks of prolonged and sordid negligence" (ch 1).
  • Personification: the building has a forehead but no eyes, like a face hiding something.
  • The street beside it shines "like a fire in a forest".
  • Jekyll's front door "wore a great air of wealth and comfort" (ch 2): one property, two faces.
Jekyll and Hyde · Gothic setting and languageScreen 6

Weather that feels

  • Pathetic fallacy: setting and weather reflect mood.
  • "The Last Night" opens on "a wild, cold, seasonable night of March"; "the thin trees in the garden were lashing themselves along the railing".
  • In ch 5 "the lamps glimmered like carbuncles" through the fog (carbuncles: red jewels, or boils).
  • Fog returns at moments of secrecy (chs 4 and 5); firelit rooms offer brief safety.

Write itScreen 8
?A short practice paragraph.

In 2–4 sentences, explain how Stevenson uses setting to create a Gothic atmosphere in the extract on screen 2. Use one quotation and name one method.

Model answer

Stevenson calls the fog "a great chocolate-coloured pall", a metaphor that turns the sky into the cloth over a coffin. On the morning after Carew's murder, London itself seems to be in mourning, and the fog hides as much as it shows. When Soho appears "like a district of some city in a nightmare", the Gothic has moved from castles into Victorian streets.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Why does the fog never seem to lift on this London?

The fog is real London weather, but Stevenson uses it with purpose. It returns at moments of secrecy (the morning after the murder, the evening Utterson locks Jekyll's note in his safe), hiding and distorting the city just as the gentlemen hide and distort the truth. It is the urban Gothic's version of the haunted castle's darkness.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Chapters 1–8 follow Utterson through a third-person narrator; chapters 9 and 10 are Lanyon's and Jekyll's written accounts.
  • Delayed revelation and documents (cheque, will, letters, sealed envelopes) keep the mystery going until the end.
  • You choose an extract-based or a discursive question; either way, know the plot chapter by chapter.
  • Jekyll's double life began before the potion: he hid his pleasures behind a "grave countenance".
  • He welcomes Hyde as "myself", then blames him and finally calls him "he": track the pronouns.
  • Hyde can be read as Jekyll's released desires or as the separate being Jekyll wants him to be; argue with evidence.
  • Stevenson presents Utterson as reserved, strict with himself, tolerant of others and loyal: the novel's main viewpoint.
  • The gentlemen share a code of silence ("the less I ask") that protects reputations and also hides the truth.
  • Utterson can be read as a detective or as a keeper of secrets; strong answers weigh both.
  • Reputation runs the novel: Enfield threatens it, Utterson fears "disgrace", Jekyll protects his "character".
  • Secrets are kept in a motif of doors, safes and sealed papers that must be broken open.
  • Jekyll's double life began with hiding "undignified" pleasures out of shame; the novel never names them.
  • Lanyon stands for material, practical science; Jekyll for "transcendental" science aimed at the soul.
  • Religious language (Satan, sin, blasphemy, biblical allusion) surrounds the experiment.
  • The potion is chemistry described as something supernatural; strong answers show the blur.
  • Darwin (1859, 1871) and theories of degeneration and atavism help explain why Hyde is "ape-like" and "troglodytic".
  • The animal imagery is a motif across many narrators, and Jekyll places the animal inside himself.
  • Soho and the respectable squares sit side by side, and one property joins Hyde's door to Jekyll's front.
  • Stevenson moves Gothic conventions (secret buildings, darkness, the double, transgression) into modern London: urban Gothic.
  • Fog, personified buildings and pathetic fallacy make setting reflect secrecy and fear.
  • Learn short quotations that serve several themes at once.

Key quotations to learn

  • "Did you ever remark that door?" (Ch 1, "Story of the Door") The first clue is a place, not a person.
  • "He is not easy to describe" (Ch 1, "Story of the Door") Witnesses can't put Hyde into words.
  • "If he be Mr. Hyde … I shall be Mr. Seek." (Ch 2, "Search for Mr. Hyde") Utterson casts himself as the detective.
  • "not to be opened till the death or disappearance of Dr. Henry Jekyll" (Ch 6, "Incident of Dr. Lanyon" (1886 first edition: "Remarkable Incident of Dr. Lanyon")) A sealed document delays the truth.
  • "man is not truly one, but truly two" (Ch 10, "Henry Jekyll's Full Statement of the Case") The explanation, given last.
  • "I bring the life of that unhappy Henry Jekyll to an end" (Ch 10, "Henry Jekyll's Full Statement of the Case") The novel ends with Jekyll's own words.
