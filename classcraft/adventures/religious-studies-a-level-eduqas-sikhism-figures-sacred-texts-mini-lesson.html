Mission 1: Guru Nanak grew up between Hindu and Muslim worlds. How much of his message was new?

Make a guess: Guru Nanak grew up between Hindu and Muslim worlds. How much of his message was new?

Mission 1

Punjab around 1500

  • Guru Nanak: born 1469 at Talwandi (now Nankana Sahib, Pakistan); died 1539 at Kartarpur.
  • Punjab was ruled by the Lodi sultans of Delhi: Muslim rulers, a mostly Hindu population, Persian as the language of government.
  • His father was a Khatri (a trading caste) and village accountant: Nanak grew up literate, among Hindus and Muslims.
  • Two religious worlds met in his Punjab: Hindu (caste, Brahmin ritual, pilgrimage, temples) and Muslim (mosques, qazis, Sufi orders).
  • Page numbers below are from the standard 1,430-page Guru Granth Sahib (GGS).
  • Your spec calls it the Adi Granth: same page numbers.
Mission 1

Invasion: Babur's armies

  • From about 1520, Babur raided Punjab; in 1526 he won at Panipat and founded the Mughal empire.
  • Sikh tradition says Nanak saw the sack of Saidpur (Eminabad) and was briefly taken prisoner.
  • His Babarvani hymns (GGS 360, 417–418, 722) lament the killing, and the suffering of women, and ask the Creator how such pain could be allowed (paraphrase).
  • He blames rulers, not only invaders: the age is a knife, kings are butchers, and righteousness has flown away (GGS 145, paraphrase).
  • Teaching drawn from this: God is the only true king; rulers will answer for injustice.
Mission 1

Islam and the Sufis

  • Islam taught strict monotheism: one God, no images. Nanak's teaching also begins with one God.
  • Sufis (Muslim mystics) sought God through love, longing, poetry and music, guided by a pir (master).
  • Sheikh Farid (1173–1266), a Chishti Sufi of Punjab: his verses are in the GGS (see Mission 5).
  • Nanak's lifelong companion Mardana was a Muslim musician who played the rebab.
  • Nanak called Muslims to inner truth: make mercy your mosque, faith your prayer mat, honest living your Qur'an (GGS 140, paraphrase).
  • Women in Islam: the Qur'an gives women a share of inheritance (Qur'an 4:7) and rights in marriage. Historians note that in practice, in Punjab as across north India, many women's lives were bounded by family honour and male guardianship, and wealthy families of both faiths kept women in seclusion (purdah).
  • Devotional songs: like the Sufis and sants, Nanak taught through hymns sung to music (kirtan).
Mission 1

The Hindu world, and Lalla

  • Caste: birth decided status, work and marriage; Brahmins controlled ritual and Sanskrit scripture.
  • Twice-born boys received a sacred thread (janeu); women and low castes were excluded.
  • Popular practice: temple images, pilgrimage, fasting, ritual bathing.
  • Lalla (Lal Ded, c. 1320–1392): a Kashmiri Shaiva woman poet. Her short verses (vakhs) mock outward ritual and idol worship and seek God within.
  • She lived a century before Nanak, so any influence came through her widely sung verses; there is no evidence they met.
Mission 1

The sant tradition

  • Sants: north Indian poet-saints, about the 13th–16th centuries: Namdev, Kabir (a weaver of Banaras), Ravidas (a leatherworker).
  • Nirgun bhakti: loving devotion to God without form (nirgun), not to an image.
  • Repeating God's Name; the Guru as an inner voice or Word; songs in everyday languages, not Sanskrit.
  • Rejected caste, idols, Brahmin authority and empty ritual; many sants were of low caste.
  • Shared Indic ideas: karma, samsara (rebirth), maya, mukti (liberation).
  • The GGS includes hymns of Kabir, Namdev and Ravidas.

Caste and women

  • On caste: recognise God's light in everyone, and do not ask about caste; in the next world there is no caste (GGS 349, paraphrase).
  • On women: why call her inferior, from whom kings are born? We are born of woman, betrothed to woman, and befriend woman (GGS 473, paraphrase).
  • Your spec also cites Adi Granth 91 (Sri Rag ki Var, Guru Nanak): the real "outcasts" within are evil-mindedness, cruelty, slander and anger; drawing purity lines round the kitchen is useless while they sit inside (paraphrase).
  • And Adi Granth 150 (Majh ki Var, salok of Guru Angad): honour comes from God, not from social status; what can caste do to air and water? (paraphrase).

Mission 1

Old words, new meanings

  • Maya: in Advaita Vedanta, the world as illusion. For Nanak, the world is real, God's creation; maya is the attachment that turns people from God.
  • Mukti: often after death, through knowledge, ritual or withdrawal. For Nanak, liberation can come in this life (jivan mukti), by God's grace (nadar) and the Name.
  • Karma and samsara: shared; but God's grace can end the cycle.
  • Guru: a human teacher. For Nanak, God is the true Guru, speaking through the Word (shabad).
  • Renunciation: Naths left society. Nanak taught detachment within family and work.
Mission 1Diversity

How new was Nanak's message?

  • Revelation (most Sikhs): Nanak's message came from God in God's court (see Mission 2); likenesses to others are because truth is one, not borrowing.
  • W. H. McLeod (1932–2009), Guru Nanak and the Sikh Religion (1968): Nanak's ideas belong to the sant tradition, but he expressed them with new coherence. McLeod rejected the idea that Nanak blended Hinduism and Islam.
  • Syncretism (an older outsider view): Sikhism as a mix of Hinduism and Islam. Most scholars and Sikhs reject it: Guru Arjan wrote that he is neither Hindu nor Muslim (GGS 1136, paraphrase).
  • Many Sikh scholars object that "influence" language treats the Guru as a product of his time.

Mission 1Evaluate

"Guru Nanak was a religious innovator."

  • For: rejected caste, Brahmin ritual, idols and ascetic withdrawal; founded a community at Kartarpur with shared kitchen and worship.
  • For: began a line of Gurus and a collection of hymns that became scripture; the sants left no lasting institution like this.
  • Against: much vocabulary is shared with sants and Indic thought (nam, karma, samsara, mukti, nirgun God).
  • Against: Kabir also rejected caste and ritual; Sufis also taught love of one God through song.
  • Another view: many Sikhs say "innovator" misdescribes a Guru: the message is revealed, whatever words carry it.
  • Judge it by: Is newness about ideas or institutions? Do shared words mean shared meanings? Which sources count: the hymns, or later stories?

Mission 2

How we know about Guru Nanak

  • His hymns in the Guru Granth Sahib (GGS): his own words, but not a biography.
  • Bhai Gurdas (c. 1551–1636), Var 1: a short early account, written about 80 years after Nanak's death.
  • Janamsakhis ("birth stories"): collections of episodes from the 17th century on (Puratan, Bala, Miharban traditions).
  • Janamsakhis teach through story: each episode carries a teaching. Their status as sources.
Mission 2

Birth and the One

  • Born 1469 at Talwandi; father Mehta Kalu, a Khatri village accountant; mother Tripta; sister Nanaki.
  • Janamsakhi: the family astrologer, casting the baby's horoscope, foretold that Hindus and Muslims alike would honour him (paraphrase; versions differ).
  • As a schoolboy, he is said to have written verses on the One God rather than his lessons.
  • Teaching: Ik Oankar: there is one God, Nirankar (formless), creator of all.
  • Your spec: "the acknowledgement of One Formless Lord".
Mission 2Read the source

The sacred thread

At about age eleven, Nanak refused the janeu (sacred thread) at his initiation. In Asa di Var he describes the thread he would accept:

  • Make compassion the cotton, contentment the thread, self-control the knot and truth the twist. Such a thread does not break, get dirty, burn or get lost (GGS 471, paraphrase).

Q1 What does Nanak put in place of the outward thread?

Show answer

Inner virtues: compassion, contentment, self-control, truth.

Q2 Why does refusing the thread challenge caste?

Show answer

It was given only to high-caste boys; women and low castes were excluded.

Mission 2

God's court

  • About 1499, at Sultanpur Lodhi, Nanak worked as storekeeper for the governor Daulat Khan Lodi.
  • Bathing in the Bein river, he disappeared for three days.
  • Janamsakhis: he was taken to God's court (dargah), given a cup of nectar (amrit) and sent to praise God's Name and teach others to do the same.
  • He returned saying: "Na koi Hindu, na koi Musalman": "There is no Hindu, there is no Muslim" (janamsakhi tradition).
  • Story: he joined the governor and the qazi at prayer but did not bow: their minds were on a horse purchase and a foal near a well. True prayer is attention to God.
  • Sikhs see this as Nanak's call: his mission to reveal the Name to the world.

Mission 2

Journeys with Mardana

  • About 1500–1520s: long journeys (udasis), usually counted as four, with the Muslim musician Mardana.
  • East (Banaras, Puri, Assam) · South (Sri Lanka) · North (the Himalayas, the Nath yogis) · West (Mecca, Baghdad).
  • At Haridwar he threw water west, "to my fields in Punjab": if water reaches ancestors, it can reach my fields.
  • At Mecca, asked to move his feet from the Kaaba, he asked to be pointed where God is not (janamsakhi).
  • At Eminabad he ate the honest bread of Lalo, a low-caste carpenter, not the feast of the rich official Malik Bhago: honest work over wealth from exploitation.
Mission 2

Sajjan the thug

  • Sajjan kept an inn with a Hindu temple and a mosque for travellers. At night he robbed and killed rich guests.
  • Nanak sang to him (GGS 729, Suhi): bronze is bright and shiny, but rub it and the black shows; washing cannot remove that dirt. Only those who walk with you to the end are friends (paraphrase).
  • Sajjan saw himself in the hymn, confessed, gave his wealth to the poor and became a follower; tradition says his inn became the first dharamsal (place of worship).
  • Teaching: true religion is inner purity and honest deeds, not outward show. Anyone can turn.

Mission 2

Kartarpur

  • In the 1520s Nanak founded Kartarpur ("the Creator's town") on the Ravi, and lived there as a farmer and householder until 1539.
  • Bhai Gurdas (Var 1) describes Japji recited before dawn and the hymns Sodar and Arti sung in the evening.
  • A community of women and men of all castes eating together (langar), working and singing together (sangat).
  • It models three principles: remember God, earn honestly, share.
  • Your spec's terms: Naam Japo, Kirat Karo, Vand Chhako.
  • Liberation is lived in ordinary life, not in retreat.
Mission 2

The Guru line begins

  • Before he died (1539), Nanak chose Bhai Lehna, a devoted disciple, not his own sons. Sri Chand, his elder son, was an ascetic.
  • He renamed him Angad ("my own limb") and bowed to him.
  • Janamsakhis tell of tests of obedience, e.g. carrying a bundle of wet grass that soiled his fine clothes.
  • Sikhs believe the same light (jot) passed from Guru to Guru: only the body changed (Satta and Balwand, GGS 966, paraphrase).
  • So Guru-ship depends on worth, not birth.

Mission 2Evaluate

Background or key events: which shaped his teaching more?

  • Background: sant ideas (the Name, nirgun God), Sufi devotion, caste practice and Mughal violence (see Mission 1) shaped his themes and vocabulary.
  • Key events: God's court is, for Sikhs, the source of the message; Sajjan, Lalo and Mecca show teaching applied to real people.
  • Key events: Kartarpur turned teaching into a way of life and an institution; choosing Angad created the Guru line.
  • Problem of evidence: most "events" come from janamsakhis written long after; the hymns are the surest guide.
  • Judge it by: Which source do you trust more? Does background explain content, or only the words used? Can revelation and context both be true?

Mission 3: How did one Guru turn followers into the Khalsa, then end the line of human Gurus?

Make a guess: How did one Guru turn followers into the Khalsa, then end the line of human Gurus?

Mission 3

The boy Guru

  • Guru Gobind Singh (1666–1708), born at Patna; Guru from 1675, aged nine, after his father Guru Tegh Bahadur's martyrdom.
  • Grew up at Anandpur in the Punjab hills; learned Persian, Sanskrit and Braj, and the use of arms.
  • Kept poets at his court and wrote poetry himself.
  • Pressed by the hill rajas and by Mughal governors under Emperor Aurangzeb; fought at Bhangani (1688).
  • The question he faced: how could the Panth survive persecution and stay true to Nanak?
Mission 3

Vaisakhi 1699

  • At the spring festival of Vaisakhi, the Guru gathered Sikhs at Anandpur and asked who would give his head.
  • Five men came forward, one by one, from different castes and regions: the Panj Piare (the Five Beloved Ones).
  • He prepared amrit (sweetened water stirred with a double-edged sword, the khanda, while hymns were recited) and initiated them as the Khalsa.
  • Then he asked them to initiate him.
  • New names: Singh ("lion") for men, Kaur ("princess") for women (Sikh tradition; some historians date the general use of Kaur later).
  • A discipline including the 5Ks (kesh, kangha, kara, kachera, kirpan): detail in Mission 4. Accounts of the day differ in detail.
Mission 3

Why the Khalsa?

  • Protector: a community ready to defend faith and the weak against persecution.
  • Restorer: renewing Nanak's equality: all castes drank from one bowl; caste surnames replaced by Singh and Kaur.
  • Direct belonging: "Khalsa" can mean land held directly by the ruler; the corrupt masands (agents) were removed, so Sikhs belonged directly to the Guru.
  • Visible identity: the 5Ks and uncut hair mean a Sikh cannot hide when faith is under threat.
  • Sikh tradition: women were initiated too (Kaur).
  • Zafarnama, his letter to Aurangzeb: when every other means has failed, it is right to take up the sword (paraphrase).

Mission 3

War and loss, 1700–1708

  • Sieges of Anandpur (1704–05); promises of safe passage were broken when the Sikhs left.
  • At Chamkaur his elder sons, Ajit Singh and Jujhar Singh, died fighting.
  • At Sirhind his younger sons, Zorawar Singh and Fateh Singh (aged about nine and six), were executed for refusing to convert; his mother Mata Gujri died there.
  • At Damdama Sahib (1706) the final text of the scripture was prepared, adding Guru Tegh Bahadur's hymns (see Mission 5).
  • In 1708 at Nanded (in the Deccan) he was stabbed by assassins and died of the wound.
Mission 3

1708: Guru Granth and Guru Panth

  • Before his death, he named no human successor.
  • Guru Granth: the scripture is the Guru: the Gurus' Word carries the Gurus' authority.
  • Guru Panth: the Khalsa community, gathered in the scripture's presence, also carries the Guru's authority.
  • A couplet sung after Ardas (later tradition): by the command of the Timeless One the Panth was created; all Sikhs are commanded to accept the Granth as Guru (paraphrase).
  • In practice: the Panj Piare lead initiations; a gurmatta (resolution) is passed before the Guru Granth Sahib; the Akal Takht issues edicts (hukamnamas).
  • Your spec: "interpretation and new concept of Guru – the Guru Panth".
Mission 3Read the source

The Dasam Granth

  • The Dasam Granth ("book of the tenth Guru"): writings attributed to him, gathered after his death (traditionally by Bhai Mani Singh).
  • Includes Jaap Sahib (daily prayer; recited at amrit), Akal Ustat, Bachittar Natak (his account of his mission), Zafarnama (Persian letter).
  • Akal Ustat (paraphrase): temple and mosque are the same; Hindu worship and Muslim prayer are the same; all humans are one, though they look different. Recognise the whole human race as one.

Show answer

Mission 3Diversity

Did he change Nanak's Sikhism?

  • Continuity (most Sikhs): one light (jot) in all ten Gurus; the Khalsa protects what Nanak taught: one God, the Name, equality, honest work, service.
  • Development: Guru Hargobind had already taken up arms (miri-piri); Gobind Singh gave the Panth a form fit for survival.
  • Change (some historians): W. H. McLeod argued that much Khalsa identity took shape in the 18th century, recorded in later conduct codes (rahitnamas); the 1699 accounts are later.
Mission 3Evaluate

"Guru Gobind Singh changed the Sikhism of Guru Nanak."

  • For: a militarised Khalsa with a visible uniform; the end of human Gurus; new initiation by the sword.
  • For: a strong, separate identity, where Nanak's community was open and loosely defined.
  • Against: the beliefs stay the same: one God, the Name, grace, equality. Jaap Sahib praises the formless One.
  • Against: Nanak too opposed tyranny (Babarvani); the Word was already Guru (Bani Guru).
  • Judge it by: change of belief or of practice? Which sources: his own writings or later rahitnamas? Is development a kind of change?
Mission 3Worked answer

Linking an action to Nanak's vision

Question: Examine Guru Gobind Singh's role as restorer of Guru Nanak's vision. (AO1)

  • Action: in 1699 all five initiates, of different castes, drank amrit from one bowl and took the name Singh; Sikh tradition adds Kaur for women initiates.
  • Link: this restores Nanak's rejection of caste (GGS 349: no caste in God's court).
  • Development: a shared name removed the caste surname, so equality became visible and daily, not just taught.
  • Scholar or view: some historians see the identity as forming over the next century: the vision restored, the form developing.
Mission 3

His legacy today

  • Vaisakhi marks the Khalsa's birth; amrit ceremonies are often held then.
  • Singh and Kaur are used by most Sikhs, initiated or not.
  • Amritdhari, keshdhari and sahajdhari Sikhs relate differently to the Khalsa discipline (see Mission 4).
  • Readiness to defend the oppressed: modern Sikh relief work often cites the saint-soldier ideal (sant-sipahi).
  • Gurdwaras marking his life: Takht Sri Patna Sahib (birth), Anandpur Sahib (Khalsa), Hazur Sahib, Nanded (death).

Mission 4

Vaisakhi 1699: the Khalsa

  • Vaisakhi 1699, Anandpur: Guru Gobind Singh asks the gathered Sikhs who will give their head for the faith.
  • Five men, from different castes and regions, step forward one by one: the Panj Piare ("Five Beloved Ones").
  • The Guru initiates them with amrit ("nectar") and founds the Khalsa ("the pure"): a community of initiated Sikhs.
  • Then the Guru asks the Five to initiate him: Guru and disciple become equals.
  • (Story and its sources in Mission 3.)
Mission 4

The amrit ceremony

  • Also called amrit sanskar or khande di pahul.
  • Held in private before the Guru Granth Sahib; five amritdhari Sikhs take the place of the Panj Piare.
  • Water and sugar (patashe) are stirred in an iron bowl with a khanda (double-edged sword) while five banis (prayers) are recited.
  • Each initiate drinks amrit five times; it is sprinkled five times on the eyes and five times on the hair; all drink from the same bowl, a sign that caste is abolished.
  • The Mool Mantar is recited; initiates, who come wearing the 5Ks, are taught the Rehat (code of conduct).
  • Open to men and women of any caste or background who are ready to keep the discipline.
Mission 4

Singh, Kaur and the discipline

  • Men take the name Singh ("lion"); women take Kaur ("princess").
  • These replace caste surnames: all Khalsa Sikhs are one family.
  • Initiates vow to avoid the four kurahits (cardinal prohibitions): cutting hair; eating ritually slaughtered (kutha) meat; sexual relations outside marriage; tobacco.
  • Daily discipline: the set banis morning and evening, nam simran, honest work, sharing, sewa.
  • Spec link: the Guru welcomed women and all castes into the community.
Mission 4

The 5Ks

  • Kesh: uncut hair, kept covered (men wear a turban, many women too): accepting God's will and the natural form God gave.
  • Kangha: a small wooden comb: cleanliness and order, in body and mind.
  • Kara: a steel bangle on the wrist: one God without beginning or end; a reminder to do only good with one's hands.
  • Kachera: cotton undershorts: modesty, self-control, readiness for action.
  • Kirpan: a sword: defending justice and the weak.
  • Together: an outward uniform of the Khalsa, and reminders of an inner discipline.

Mission 4

Guru Panth

  • 1708: before his death, Guru Gobind Singh ends the line of human Gurus.
  • Authority passes to the Guru Granth Sahib (the scripture) and the Guru Panth (the Khalsa community).
  • The Panth acts as Guru when Sikhs meet in the presence of the Guru Granth Sahib and decide together, through five chosen representatives in some decisions.
  • So the Khalsa is not only a set of individuals: it is a community with collective authority.
  • (Detail in Mission 3.)
Mission 4Read the source

Who may take amrit?

Sikh Rehat Maryada, on the amrit ceremony (paraphrase): any man or woman, of any country, religion or caste, who embraces the Sikh faith and promises to live by its principles may receive amrit. They should be of an age to understand, and should come with hair uncut and the 5Ks, ready to keep the Rehat.

Q1 Which barriers does this remove?

Show answer

Gender, nationality, religion of birth and caste.

Q2 Why must initiates be old enough to understand?

Show answer

Amrit is a lifelong commitment chosen freely, not a ceremony done to a child.

Mission 5

What the Guru Granth Sahib is

  • The Sikh scripture: 1,430 pages (angs) in the standard printing; poetry meant to be sung.
  • Arranged mainly by 31 raags (musical modes). Within each raag: the Gurus in order, then other saints.
  • It opens with the Japji of Guru Nanak, beginning with the Mool Mantar.
  • Your spec spells it Mul Mantra.
  • Authors: six Gurus (the first five and the ninth), bhagats (Hindu and Muslim saints), court poets (bhatts) and a few Sikhs of the Gurus.
  • Each hymn is headed by its Guru's number: "Mahala 1" is Guru Nanak, "Mahala 5" Guru Arjan.
Mission 5

Gurmukhi

  • Gurmukhi: "from the mouth of the Guru". Tradition credits Guru Angad with shaping the alphabet from scripts already used in Punjab.
  • It wrote the spoken language of the people, not Sanskrit (Brahmins' scripture) or Persian (the court).
  • Effect: ordinary Sikhs, of any caste, could read the hymns for themselves.
  • It also gave the Panth its own visible written identity.
  • The Guru Granth Sahib is installed only in Gurmukhi; translations are for study.
Mission 5

From pothis to the Guru

  • Guru Nanak kept his hymns in a book (pothi), passed to his successors (tradition).
  • Goindval Pothis (1570s, Guru Amar Das): the earliest surviving collection.
  • 1604, Adi Granth: Guru Arjan, with Bhai Gurdas as scribe; often identified with the Kartarpur Pothi.
  • 1706, Damdama Sahib: Guru Gobind Singh, with Bhai Mani Singh as scribe, adds Guru Tegh Bahadur's hymns.
  • 1708, Nanded: this text is declared the Guru Granth Sahib.
  • Adi Granth ("first book") is the 1604 compilation; Guru Granth Sahib is the final text, as Guru. Many Sikhs use both names for the scripture.
  • Scholars argue over this history. The historian Gurinder Singh Mann studies early manuscripts and sees the text growing in stages; many Sikh scholars, such as the Institute of Sikh Studies, hold that this approach undermines a scripture they regard as revealed.

Mission 5

The bhagat bani

  • Bhagat bani: hymns of 15 bhagats (saints), Hindu and Muslim, from the 12th to the 16th centuries.
  • Many of low caste: Kabir (weaver), Ravidas (leatherworker), Namdev (cloth-printer), Sain (barber), Dhanna (farmer).
  • A Muslim Sufi: Sheikh Farid. A Brahmin teacher: Ramanand.
  • Included because their teaching agreed with the Gurus': one formless God, the Name, against caste and empty ritual.
  • Placed after the Gurus in each raag, but given the same reverence. The Gurus sometimes add verses responding to them.

Farid and Ramanand

  • Ramanand (GGS 1195, paraphrase): Where should I go? My home is full of joy. I set out with sandalwood and incense to worship God in a temple, but the Guru showed me God within my own heart.
  • Farid (Saloks of Farid, GGS 1377–1384, paraphrase): do not strike back at someone who strikes you; go home with humility. Elsewhere: answer evil with good, and do not let anger fill your mind.
  • Your spec cites Farid at Guru Granth Sahib 448 and 1384, and Ramanand at 1195.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Nanak (1469–1539) grew up in Lodi-ruled Punjab, among Hindu caste practice, Islam's monotheism, Sufi devotion and the sant poets.
  • He shared much vocabulary (Name, Guru, karma, maya, mukti) but changed meanings: a real world, liberation in this life, detachment within family and work.

Mission 2

  • Sources: the Guru's hymns, Bhai Gurdas' Var 1, and the later janamsakhis, which teach through story.
  • Key events carry teachings: the One Formless God, the sacred thread refused, God's court and "no Hindu, no Muslim", Sajjan's turning, Lalo's bread, Kartarpur's way of life.
  • Nanak chose Angad for worth, not birth: Sikhs believe one light passed through ten Gurus.

Mission 3

  • 1699: the Khalsa founded through the Panj Piare and amrit; one bowl, Singh and Kaur, the 5Ks, the Guru as disciple too.
  • 1708: no human successor: Guru Granth (the Word) and Guru Panth (the community).
  • Sikhs see continuity of one light from Nanak; historians debate how much the form developed later.

Mission 4

  • 1699: Guru Gobind Singh founded the Khalsa; amrit (khande di pahul) initiates men and women of any caste; names Singh and Kaur; four kurahits.
  • The 5Ks: kesh, kangha, kara, kachera, kirpan: an outward uniform and inner discipline.

Mission 5

  • The Guru Granth Sahib: 1,430 pages, in Gurmukhi, arranged by raag; hymns of six Gurus, the bhagats and others.
  • Stages: Goindval Pothis → Adi Granth (1604) → Damdama (1706) → declared Guru (1708).
