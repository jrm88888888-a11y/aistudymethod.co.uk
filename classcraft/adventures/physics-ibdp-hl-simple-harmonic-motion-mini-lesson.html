Mission 1

Mission 1: Why does an oscillating mass on a spring move fastest at the one point where no overall force acts on it?

Make a guess: Why does an oscillating mass on a spring move fastest at the one point where no overall force acts on it?

Mission 1

What makes motion simple harmonic

  • Periodic motion: repeats in equal time intervals. SHM is a special case. Every SHM is periodic; not every periodic motion is SHM.
  • Equilibrium position: where the object would rest; the restoring force is zero there.
  • Restoring force: always points back towards equilibrium, opposite to the displacement.
  • SHM: the restoring force is proportional to the displacement from equilibrium.
  • Condition: a ∝ −x
Mission 1

Describing an oscillation

  • Displacement x: distance from equilibrium, with a sign (+ one side, − the other).
  • Amplitude x0: maximum displacement.
  • Period T: time for one complete cycle. Frequency f: cycles per second (Hz). T = 1/f.
  • Angular frequency ω = 2π/T = 2πf, in rad s⁻¹. One cycle = 2π rad.
Quick check
?Each object below is displaced and released, and then moves back and forth. Which one moves with simple harmonic motion?
Mission 1

The defining equation

  • a = −ω²x
  • The minus sign: acceleration always points opposite to the displacement, towards equilibrium.
  • It does not mean "slowing down": moving towards equilibrium, the object speeds up.
  • a against x: a straight line through the origin with a negative gradient.
  • Gradient of the a–x graph = −ω².
Mission 1

Worked example

  • f = 2.5 Hz → ω = 2π × 2.5 = 15.7 rad s⁻¹.
  • At x = +0.030 m: a = −ω²x = −(15.7)² × 0.030 = −7.4 m s⁻² (towards equilibrium).
  • At x = 0: a = 0.
Mission 1

x, v and a against time

  • Released from rest at x = +x0 at t = 0: x–t is a cosine curve.
  • v = gradient of x–t. v–t is a negative sine curve, a quarter cycle ahead of x.
  • a = gradient of v–t. a–t is x–t upside down, because a = −(constant)x.
  • At x = ±x0: v = 0, |a| maximum (pointing inwards).
  • At x = 0: |v| maximum, a = 0.
  • Speeding up when moving towards equilibrium; slowing down when moving away.
Quick check
?The graph shows the position x of an object in simple harmonic motion as a function of time t. Points P, Q and R are marked on the graph. Let aP, aQ and aR be the magnitudes of the object's acceleration at P, Q and R. Which of the following ranks them?
Mission check

?A pendulum bob swings to and fro in a small arc. At the lowest point of its swing, what are its acceleration along its path and its speed?

All the way in from a turning point, the acceleration points along the motion, so the object keeps speeding up. It stops speeding up only where the acceleration (and the restoring force) is zero: at equilibrium. That is where it is fastest.

Mission 2

Mission 2: If you know where an oscillating particle is, but not when, can you work out how fast it is moving?

Make a guess: If you know where an oscillating particle is, but not when, can you work out how fast it is moving?

Mission 2

Phase angle

  • In x = x0 sin(ωt + φ), the angle (ωt + φ) is the phase, in radians.
  • φ = phase constant: the phase at t = 0. It fixes where the cycle starts.
  • One cycle = 2π rad. Two oscillators with the same T, Δt apart: phase difference = 2πΔt/T.
  • In phase: 0. Antiphase: π rad.
Mission 2

Choosing φ

  • x = x0 sin(ωt + φ). Read the start (t = 0) and pick φ:
  • At equilibrium, moving in the + direction: φ = 0.
  • At +x0, momentarily at rest: φ = π/2 (x = x0 cos ωt).
  • At equilibrium, moving in the − direction: φ = π.
  • At −x0: φ = −π/2 (or 3π/2).
  • Check: put t = 0 into x and into v. Both must match the start.
Quick check
?A particle moves with simple harmonic motion, x = x0 sin(ωt + φ). At t = 0 it is at x = +x0/2, moving towards its equilibrium position. What is the phase constant φ?
Mission 2

Velocity against time

  • v = ωx0 cos(ωt + φ): the gradient of the x–t graph.
  • v is a quarter cycle (π/2) ahead of x.
  • Maximum speed ωx0, at x = 0. Zero speed at x = ±x0.
  • From a = −ω²x: maximum |a| = ω²x0, at x = ±x0.
Mission 2

Speed from position

  • v = ±ω√(x0² − x²): speed at displacement x. No time needed.
  • ±: at each x the particle passes both ways (once going out, once coming back).
  • x = 0 → v = ±ωx0 (maximum). x = ±x0 → v = 0.
  • Close to the ends the speed changes fast; near equilibrium it changes slowly.
Mission 2

Worked example

  • x0 = 0.060 m, T = 1.5 s. ω = 2π/T = 4.19 rad s⁻¹.
  • Speed at x = 0.030 m: v = 4.19 × √(0.060² − 0.030²) = 4.19 × 0.0520 = 0.22 m s⁻¹.
  • Starts at equilibrium moving +, so φ = 0. At t = 0.20 s: ωt = 0.838 rad.
  • x = 0.060 sin(0.838) = 0.045 m. v = 4.19 × 0.060 × cos(0.838) = 0.17 m s⁻¹.
  • Check with v = ω√(x0² − x²) = 4.19 × √(0.060² − 0.0446²) = 0.17 m s⁻¹. ✓
Quick check
?A particle moves with simple harmonic motion with a time period of 1.2 s and an amplitude of 0.030 m. At t = 0 it is at +x0, so x = x0 cos ωt. What is its displacement at t = 0.50 s?
Quick check
?A particle moves with simple harmonic motion with an amplitude of 0.080 m and a time period of 0.50 s. What is its speed when its displacement is 0.050 m?
Mission check

?A particle moves with simple harmonic motion of amplitude x0. At what displacement is its speed half of its maximum speed?

Yes: v = ±ω√(x0² − x²) gives the speed from the displacement alone. The ± is the honest part: position fixes the speed, not the direction.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Take a pendulum clock to the Moon. Does it run fast, run slow, or keep time?

Make a guess: Take a pendulum clock to the Moon. Does it run fast, run slow, or keep time?

Mission 3

Mass on a spring

  • T = 2π√(m/k)
  • m = oscillating mass; k = spring constant.
  • Bigger mass → longer T. Stiffer spring → shorter T.
  • Not on g: hung vertically, gravity only shifts the equilibrium position. Same T as horizontal.
  • Not on amplitude.
Mission 3

Simple pendulum

  • T = 2π√(l/g)
  • Length from the pivot to the centre of mass of the bob.
  • Small angles only. Then the restoring force is (nearly) proportional to the displacement.
  • Not on the bob's mass. Not on the (small) amplitude.
Mission 3

Worked examples

  • Spring: m = 0.25 kg, k = 40 N m⁻¹. T = 2π√(0.25/40) = 0.50 s.
  • Pendulum, length 0.80 m:
  • T = 2π√(0.80/9.8) = 1.8 s (g = 9.8 m s⁻²).
  • To make the pendulum's period three times as long: nine times the length.
Quick check
?A trolley hung from a newton meter gives a reading of 14.7 N. The trolley is then attached to a spring system on a horizontal track and oscillates with negligible friction. The spring constant of the system is 7.50 N m⁻¹. Take g = 9.8 m s⁻². What is the time period of the oscillation?
Mission 3

What sets the period?

  • Mass on a spring: longer with more mass; shorter with a stiffer spring. Unchanged by g and by amplitude.
  • Pendulum: longer with a longer string; shorter where g is larger. Unchanged by the bob's mass and by small changes of amplitude.
  • Large swings (beyond about 10°): a pendulum's period grows slightly. The motion is no longer quite SHM.
Quick check
?A long simple pendulum hangs in the stairwell of a school. Swinging with a small amplitude, its bob takes 2.10 s to move from one extreme position to the other. Take g = 9.8 m s⁻². What is the length of the pendulum?
Mission check

?A simple pendulum and a mass–spring oscillator each have a period of 1.0 s on Earth. Both are taken to a planet where the gravitational field strength is four times as large as on Earth, and both oscillate with small amplitude. What are their new periods?

A pendulum's period depends on its length and on g. On the Moon, g is about one-sixth of Earth's, so each swing takes longer: the clock runs slow. A watch regulated by a balance wheel and hairspring would keep time.

Mission 4

Mission 4: A mass on a spring keeps stopping and starting again. Where is its energy at the instant it stops?

Make a guess: A mass on a spring keeps stopping and starting again. Where is its energy at the instant it stops?

Quick check
?A mass on a horizontal spring moves with simple harmonic motion on a frictionless surface; air resistance is negligible. Which statement about the energy of the oscillator is correct?
Mission 4

Energy swaps back and forth

  • Total energy = Ek + Ep
  • At ±x0 (turning points): at rest. Kinetic energy zero; potential energy maximum = the total.
  • At equilibrium: fastest. Kinetic energy maximum; potential energy minimum.
  • Potential energy: in the spring (mass–spring) or gravitational (pendulum).
  • Undamped: the total stays constant. Energy is only exchanged.
Mission 4

Energy against displacement

  • Potential energy: a U-shaped parabola, zero at x = 0, maximum at ±x0.
  • Kinetic energy: an upside-down parabola, maximum at x = 0, zero at ±x0.
  • Total: a horizontal line. At every x, kinetic + potential = total.
  • Ep = ½mω²x².
Mission 4

Energy against time

  • Kinetic and potential energy each rise and fall twice per period: two peaks per cycle.
  • Both are never negative: each is 0 at its minimum.
  • When one is at a peak, the other is zero. Their sum is constant.
  • (x = x0 sin ωt gives Ep ∝ sin²ωt and Ek ∝ cos²ωt.)
Quick check
?A block attached to an ideal spring oscillates on a horizontal surface with negligible friction. The graph shows the elastic potential energy Ep of the block–spring system as a function of the block's position x, measured from equilibrium; the curve is a parabola, and the dashed line shows the system's total energy. What is the kinetic energy of the block when it is at x = 0.050 m?
Mission 4

Total energy and amplitude

  • At x = ±x0 all the energy is potential.
  • ET = ½mω²x0² (k = mω² for a mass–spring).
  • ET ∝ x0²: double the amplitude → four times the energy, twice the maximum speed.
  • Halfway out (x = x0/2): Ep = ¼ of the total. Ek = ¾, not ½.
Mission 4

Speed from energy

  • Energy at any x: ½mv² = ½mω²(x0² − x²):
  • v = ±ω√(x0² − x²). At x = 0: vmax = ωx0.
  • ± because the mass passes each x moving both ways.
Mission 4

Worked example

  • m = 0.50 kg on a spring, k = 200 N m⁻¹, amplitude 0.060 m.
  • ET = ½mω²x0² = ½kx0² = ½ × 200 × 0.060² = 0.36 J.
  • At x = 0.030 m: Ep = ½ × 200 × 0.030² = 0.090 J → Ek = 0.36 − 0.090 = 0.27 J.
  • Speed: ½ × 0.50 × v² = 0.27 → v = 1.0 m s⁻¹.
  • Check: ω = 20 rad s⁻¹ (= √(k/m)). v = 20 × √(0.060² − 0.030²) = 1.0 m s⁻¹. ✓
Quick check
?A mass of 0.25 kg on a spring of spring constant 16 N m⁻¹ oscillates with simple harmonic motion of amplitude 0.050 m. What is its speed when its displacement is 0.030 m?
Mission check

?A glider on an air track is attached between two springs and oscillates with simple harmonic motion of amplitude 0.10 m. Friction is negligible. At what distance from the equilibrium position are the glider's kinetic energy and the elastic potential energy of the system equal?

At each stop, all of the energy is potential (in the spring, or gravitational for a pendulum). It flows back into kinetic energy on the way in, and the total stays the same throughout.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • SHM: restoring force ∝ displacement, always towards equilibrium.
  • a = −ω²x, with ω = 2π/T = 2πf.
  • Fastest at equilibrium (a = 0); at rest at ±x0 (|a| largest). v = gradient of x–t; a = gradient of v–t.

Mission 2

  • x = x0 sin(ωt + φ); φ fixes the start. Phase difference = 2πΔt/T.
  • v = ωx0 cos(ωt + φ); v = ±ω√(x0² − x²); vmax = ωx0.

Mission 3

  • T = 2π√(m/k); T = 2π√(l/g), for small angles.
  • Spring: no g, no amplitude. Pendulum: no mass, no (small) amplitude.

Mission 4

  • Ek and Ep swap twice per cycle; the total is constant if there is no damping. At ±x0 all Ep; at equilibrium all Ek.
  • ET = ½mω²x0²; Ep = ½mω²x²; v = ±ω√(x0² − x²).
