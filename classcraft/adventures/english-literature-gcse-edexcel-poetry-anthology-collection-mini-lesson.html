Mission 1

Hidden anger grows into a tree. Who ends up poisoned?

Make a guess: Hidden anger grows into a tree. Who ends up poisoned?

Blake · A Poison TreeScreen 1

The poem in brief

  • William Blake (1757–1827) published it in Songs of Experience (1794), the darker partner to his Songs of Innocence.
  • In Blake's notebook the draft was called "Christian Forbearance". Forbearance means patient self-control, not hitting back.
  • That title is ironic: the speaker holds his anger in, as if being patient, but it grows into a deadly trap.
  • Many readers link the garden and apple to the Fall in Genesis.

Blake · A Poison TreeScreen 5

Form: a song with a sting

  • Four quatrains of rhyming couplets (AABB): friend / end, foe / grow.
  • Short lines and a steady beat sound like a nursery rhyme. The childlike form clashes with a murder.
  • Stanza 1 is two balanced halves: friend and "end", foe and "grow".
  • Seven lines begin "And": a chain in which each step leads to the next.
  • The ending moves into the present tense: "In the morning glad I see".

?Hidden anger grows into a tree. Who ends up poisoned?

Anger spoken ends; anger hidden grows. Fed by fear and false smiles, it bears a fruit that kills the foe, and the speaker's "glad" shows it has poisoned him too. The nursery-rhyme form makes the lesson more chilling.

Mission 2

An army dies in its sleep. Why does Byron make the poem gallop?

Make a guess: An army dies in its sleep. Why does Byron make the poem gallop?

Conflict poetry · "The Destruction of Sennacherib"Screen 1

The story behind the poem

  • The poem retells a Bible story (2 Kings 18–19). In 701 BC the Assyrian king Sennacherib besieges Jerusalem.
  • Overnight, "the angel of the LORD" strikes the Assyrian camp (2 Kings 19:35, King James Version); the Bible gives 185,000 dead.
  • Byron wrote it in February 1815 for Hebrew Melodies, a book of songs on Old Testament subjects set to music by Isaac Nathan.
  • No battle is fought: the poem's war is between an empire and God.

Conflict poetry · "The Destruction of Sennacherib"Screen 4

The galloping beat

  • Each line has four beats in the pattern da-da-DUM: anapaestic tetrameter.
  • the as-SYR | -ian came DOWN | like the WOLF | on the FOLD
  • It sounds like horses at the charge, and it never stops, even after the army dies.
  • Rhyming couplets (fold/gold, sea/Galilee) push each pair of lines forward.
  • Stanza 2 is built on two matching similes: "summer" leaves, then "autumn" leaves. The repeated line pattern makes the reversal sudden.

Mission checkScreen 9

?An army dies in its sleep. Why does Byron make the poem gallop?

The galloping anapaests carry the Assyrian army in all its pride, and the beat doesn't stop when the army dies in one night without a battle. Byron uses the speed of the rhythm to make the empire's fall sudden and complete, a victory for God rather than for any soldier. Some readers find it unsettling that a song makes mass death sound so exciting: that is worth arguing about.

Mission 3

A boy steals a boat for fun. Why does a mountain haunt him?

Make a guess: A boy steals a boat for fun. Why does a mountain haunt him?

Poetry · The PreludeScreen 1

A boy, a boat, a mountain

  • The Prelude is Wordsworth's long autobiographical poem in blank verse (unrhymed iambic pentameter).
  • He began it in 1798 and revised it for the rest of his life. The final version was published in 1850, three months after his death.
  • In this episode, from Book I, he remembers a childhood summer evening in the Lake District: he takes a boat that isn't his and rows out across a lake.
  • Your extract is from the 1850 text.
Poetry · The PreludeScreen 2

Three moods in one episode

  • Pleasure and guilt: the boy pushes off proudly, but knows it is theft. "It was an act of stealth / And troubled pleasure"
  • Terror: as he rows, a huge peak seems to rise and stride after him, as if alive. He turns back.
  • Haunting: for days afterwards, strange, huge shapes fill his thoughts and dreams.
  • The turning point is the moment the peak appears: confidence becomes fear.

Mission checkScreen 8

?A boy steals a boat for fun. Why does a mountain haunt him?

The theft begins as a thrill, but when the peak seems to rise and follow him, his pride turns to fear. Nature appears to judge his guilty act, and for days afterwards he senses a strange power in the world, bigger than himself. Many readers see this as a lesson in awe and conscience; others focus on the boy's own guilty imagination.

Mission 4

He shot a man he'd have shared a drink with. Why can't he say why?

Make a guess: He shot a man he'd have shared a drink with. Why can't he say why?

Conflict poetry · "The Man He Killed"Screen 1

Who is speaking

  • Hardy wrote the poem in 1902, during the Second Boer War (1899–1902) in South Africa, and collected it in 1909.
  • The whole poem sits inside speech marks: a dramatic monologue by an ordinary ex-soldier, talking in a pub.
  • He remembers shooting an enemy soldier "face to face".
  • Words to know: nipperkin, a small measure of beer; 'list, enlist; traps, belongings; half-a-crown, a coin (2s 6d), a loan to a friend in need.
Extract Conflict poetry · "The Man He Killed"Screen 2

The whole poem

Conflict poetry · "The Man He Killed"Screen 3

A simple song for a hard thought

  • Five quatrains, rhymed ABAB, with short lines of three beats; each third line stretches to four.
  • The tune is plain and regular, like a ballad or a pub song, which fits a working man's voice.
  • Hardy uses colloquial speech: "Off-hand like", "Just so", "you'd treat".
  • The calm form clashes with the subject: killing a man is told in a jingle.

Conflict poetry · "The Man He Killed"Screen 5

How the reason falls apart

  • Repetition with a dash: "because— / Because" shows him hunting for words.
  • Caesuras in stanza 3 ("Just so:", "That's clear enough;") sound like someone trying to convince himself.
  • Enjambment: stanza 3 runs into stanza 4, the poem's only run-on between stanzas.
  • Stanza 4 finds the real reason: like the speaker, the enemy was poor and "out of work".
  • "Quaint and curious" is understatement: mild words for a killing.

Mission checkScreen 9

?He shot a man he'd have shared a drink with. Why can't he say why?

The speaker reaches for the official answer, "he was my foe", but stumbles over it, because the man he killed was a working man like himself who might have been a friend. Hardy uses an ordinary voice and a simple song form to question why ordinary people are sent to kill each other. How bitter or how gentle the ending is ("quaint and curious") is open to debate.

Mission 5

Cast off for her cousin, she ends with a 'gift'. Who has won?

Make a guess: Cast off for her cousin, she ends with a 'gift'. Who has won?

Conflict and anthology poetry · "Cousin Kate"Screen 1

Rossetti and the "fallen woman"

  • Christina Rossetti (1830–1894) published "Cousin Kate" in Goblin Market and Other Poems (1862).
  • Victorian society called a woman who had sex outside marriage "fallen": shamed and shut out, while the man often kept his good name. This is a double standard.
  • From 1859 to 1870 Rossetti volunteered at a church home for women who had been prostitutes.
  • Class matters too: the speaker is a "cottage maiden"; the man is "a great lord".

Conflict and anthology poetry · "Cousin Kate"Screen 5

Form, voice and the turn

  • Six eight-line stanzas. Lines alternate four and three beats, like a ballad, a traditional form for stories of love and betrayal.
  • In each stanza the even lines share one rhyme (air / fair / hair / care).
  • A dramatic monologue: one speaker addresses a silent Kate ("O Lady Kate, my cousin Kate"), then her son.
  • Antithesis: "I sit and howl in dust, / You sit in gold and sing".
  • The turn: "Yet I've a gift you have not got," and the poem ends with the speaker holding the power.

?Cast off for her cousin, she ends with a 'gift'. Who has won?

The last stanza gives the speaker power: her "fair-haired son" is the heir the lord "would give lands" for. But it is a bitter win ("my shame, my pride"), and both women's lives were decided by the lord and judged by the neighbours. Strong answers weigh the triumph against what it cost.

Mission 6

No battle, no attack. So what is killing these soldiers?

Make a guess: No battle, no attack. So what is killing these soldiers?

Poetry · "Exposure"Screen 1

Owen's winter at the front

  • Wilfred Owen served in France with the Manchester Regiment from December 1916.
  • In the winter of 1917 he and his men lay out in the snow for two days and nights, unable to move. The final draft of "Exposure" is dated September 1918.
  • Owen was killed on 4 November 1918; his Poems came out in 1920.
  • His draft Preface says: "The subject of it is War, and the pity of War."
Extract Poetry · "Exposure"Screen 2

Stanzas 1 and 2

Poetry · "Exposure"Screen 3

A poem that goes nowhere

  • Eight stanzas, each of four long, slow lines and a short last line. Most last lines are the refrain "But nothing happens", or a question.
  • Half-rhyme (pararhyme): words share consonants but not vowels, as in "silent / salient" and "wire / war". The sounds almost match, never quite: like the men's hopes.
  • Ellipses (". . .") trail off: time drags.
  • The first person plural ("we", "our") speaks for all the men.

Mission checkScreen 9

?No battle, no attack. So what is killing these soldiers?

The weather kills them, slowly, while they wait. Owen makes the wind, snow and frost the attackers and shows the enemy's bullets as "Less deadly than the air". The refrain "But nothing happens" turns their deaths into something routine and pointless, which is the pity of war.

Mission 7

600 men ride into a deadly blunder. Why does Tennyson say 'Honour'?

Make a guess: 600 men ride into a deadly blunder. Why does Tennyson say 'Honour'?

Poetry · "The Charge of the Light Brigade"Screen 1

An order goes wrong, 1854

When25 October 1854, the Battle of Balaclava, Crimean War
WhatA confused order sent the Light Brigade (cavalry, about 670 men) charging down a valley lined with Russian guns; 118 were killed and 127 wounded
The poemTennyson, Poet Laureate since 1850, read reports in The Times, one calling it "some hideous blunder". His poem appeared in The Examiner on 9 December 1854
  • In stanza 2 the soldiers know someone "blunder'd", yet they obey: "Their's not to reason why, / Their's but to do and die,".
Extract Poetry · "The Charge of the Light Brigade"Screen 2

Stanza 1

Poetry · "The Charge of the Light Brigade"Screen 3

A galloping beat

  • Dactylic dimeter: two beats a line, each a dactyl (DUM-da-da): HALF a league | HALF a league. Hooves at full gallop.
  • Short lines and repetition ("Cannon to right of them, / Cannon to left of them") drive the men on.
  • The refrain changes as men die: "Rode the six hundred" becomes "Not the six hundred", then "Left of six hundred". The last stanza commands: "Honour the Light Brigade,".
  • The order is direct speech, but the commander is never named.

Mission checkScreen 9

?600 men ride into a deadly blunder. Why does Tennyson say 'Honour'?

Tennyson honours the soldiers' courage and their duty to obey, even into "the valley of Death". But by telling us that "Some one" had blundered, he lets readers see the waste too. Many readers hear celebration; others hear a quiet criticism of those in command.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Songs of Experience (1794); draft title "Christian Forbearance": holding anger in, as if patient, turns it into murder.
  • An extended metaphor: hidden anger is a plant fed by fears, tears and fake smiles until its apple kills the foe.
  • Simple rhyming couplets and a chain of "And" clash with the shock of "glad" at the end.
  • If this poem is printed, choose a second Conflict poem that lets you compare causes of conflict and the speaker's voice.
  • Byron retells 2 Kings 18–19: the Assyrian army besieging Jerusalem (701 BC) dies in one night, struck by "the Angel of Death", not by soldiers. Published in Hebrew Melodies, 1815.
  • Anapaestic tetrameter in rhyming couplets gives a galloping beat that carries both the attack and the ruin; the paired leaf similes in stanza 2 put pride and fall side by side.
  • In Section B Part 1, context counts (AO3): link the poem's religious source and its idea of pride destroyed to the poem you choose.
  • The stolen-boat episode moves through three moods: guilty pleasure, terror as the peak "rises", and a haunting that lasts for days.
  • Personification ("Upreared its head", "Strode after me") makes the mountain seem alive; the ending replaces familiar shapes with "huge and mighty forms".
  • A dramatic monologue by an ex-soldier, written in 1902 during the Boer War: he killed a man he would have treated to a drink, and he can't find a reason that holds.
  • Mirror-image lines, the stumbling "because—", the one run-on stanza and the understatement "quaint and curious" carry Hardy's doubt about war.
  • In Section B Part 1, context counts (AO3): use the Boer War and the speaker's poverty to explain an idea, not as free-standing facts.
  • A cottage girl, seduced and discarded by a lord who then marries her cousin, speaks to Kate in a ballad-like dramatic monologue (1862).
  • Similes ("like a silken knot", "like a glove") and antithesis ("dust" / "gold") expose the lord's power and society's double standard.
  • The turn ("Yet I've a gift") gives her a bitter victory: a son and heir, "my shame, my pride".
  • Part 1 rewards language, form and structure (15) and context (5): link the "fallen woman" double standard to specific lines.
  • "Exposure" draws on Owen's own winter at the front in 1917: soldiers die from the cold while waiting for an attack that never comes.
  • Slow stanzas, half-rhyme, ellipses and the refrain "But nothing happens" make time drag and hope fail.
  • The weather is personified as the enemy ("knife us", the frost that will "fasten" on them); the poem shows the pity of war, not its glory.
  • The poem (1854) responds to news of the Light Brigade's charge at Balaclava, caused by a confused order.
  • Galloping dactyls, repetition and a changing refrain carry the men forward; personification makes the valley a devouring monster.
  • It honours the soldiers' courage while showing the blunder: strong answers weigh both.

Key quotations to learn

  • I told my wrath, my wrath did end. (Line 2) Spoken anger ends.
  • I told it not, my wrath did grow. (Line 4) Hidden anger grows: the poem's idea.
  • And I sunned it with smiles, (Stanza 2) Fake friendliness feeds the hatred.
  • And with soft deceitful wiles. (Stanza 2) The speaker's deliberate deceit.
  • Till it bore an apple bright; (Stanza 3) The tempting fruit: links to Genesis.
  • In the morning glad I see (Stanza 4) No guilt: the speaker is poisoned too.
  • The Assyrian came down like the wolf on the fold, (Stanza 1, line 1) The simile: a predator falling on a helpless flock.
  • And his cohorts were gleaming in purple and gold; (Stanza 1, line 2) Royal colours: the army's wealth and pride.
  • That host on the morrow lay withered and strown. (Stanza 2, line 4) The reversal in one night: summer leaves become dead leaves.
  • For the Angel of Death spread his wings on the blast, (Stanza 3, line 1) The destroyer is supernatural, not human.
  • The lances unlifted—the trumpet unblown. (Stanza 5, line 4) Negative "un-" words: a silent, motionless army.
  • And the might of the Gentile, unsmote by the sword, (Stanza 6, line 3) No human fought: God's power is the point.
  • It was an act of stealth / And troubled pleasure (1850, lines 361–362) Pleasure mixed with guilt from the start (AQA, Edexcel text).
  • a huge peak, black and huge, (1850, line 378) The turning point: the threat appears.
  • Upreared its head. (1850, line 380) Personification: the mountain seems alive.
  • Strode after me. (1850, line 385) The peak seems to pursue him.
  • But huge and mighty forms, that do not live (1850, line 398) Nature's strange power stays in his mind.
  • By day, and were a trouble to my dreams. (1850, line 400) The lasting effect: the extract's last line.
  • Right many a nipperkin! (Stanza 1, line 4) The friendship they might have had, in pub talk.
  • I shot at him as he at me, (Stanza 2, line 3) Parallelism: the two men are mirror images.
  • I shot him dead because— / Because he was my foe, (Stanza 3, lines 1–2) The faltering reason: repetition across a dash.
  • Was out of work—had sold his traps— (Stanza 4, line 3) The real reason men enlist: poverty, not hatred.
  • Yes; quaint and curious war is! (Stanza 5, line 1) Understatement: mild words for killing.
  • He wore me like a silken knot, (Stanza 2) Simile: she is an ornament to be worn.
  • He changed me like a glove; (Stanza 2) Simile: used and replaced.
  • The neighbours call you good and pure, / Call me an outcast thing. (Stanza 4) Society's double standard.
  • Even so I sit and howl in dust, / You sit in gold and sing: (Stanza 4) Antithesis: two fates, one man.
  • Yet I've a gift you have not got, (Stanza 6) The turn: the speaker's power.
  • My fair-haired son, my shame, my pride, (Stanza 6) A bitter, mixed victory.
  • merciless iced east winds that knife us (stanza 1, line 1) The weather as attacker (1920 text; some anthologies print "knive").
  • But nothing happens. (refrain (stanzas 1, 3, 4, 8)) Endless waiting; death becomes routine.
  • What are we doing here? (stanza 2) The soldiers question the war itself.
  • Less deadly than the air that shudders black with snow, (stanza 4) Nature is deadlier than the enemy.
  • frost will fasten on this mud and us, (stanza 8) The frost grips men and mud alike (1920 text "His frost"; most anthologies "this frost").
  • All their eyes are ice, (stanza 8) The dead frozen; the living numbed.
  • Half a league, half a league, (stanza 1) Galloping dactylic rhythm.
  • Into the valley of Death (stanza 1) The outcome is clear from the start.
  • Their's not to reason why, (stanza 2) Duty without question.
  • Their's but to do and die, (stanza 2) Obedience, even to death.
  • Into the jaws of Death, (stanza 3) Personification: a valley that devours.
  • Honour the Light Brigade, (stanza 6) The final command to the reader.
