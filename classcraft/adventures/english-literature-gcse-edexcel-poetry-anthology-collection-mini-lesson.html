Poetry Anthology: Comparing Poems

This mini-lesson teaches the skills and structure of the Edexcel GCSE English Literature (1ET0) Pearson Poetry Anthology question (Paper 2, Section B, Part 1): the three thematic collections, how to compare one named poem with a second from the same collection, and how to hit the comparison Assessment Objectives AO2 and AO3. It drills collections, terminology and comparative technique — not the finished essay.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go (some ask you to pick the strongest comparison, some to name a method) and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you're ready.

The shape of the anthology

Three collections — you study one

The Pearson Edexcel Poetry Anthology is split into three thematic collections of 15 poems each. Your class studies one of them, and in the exam you compare poems only within your own collection:

  • Relationships — romantic love, family and loss. E.g. La Belle Dame sans Merci (Keats), My Last Duchess (Robert Browning), Sonnet 43 (Elizabeth Barrett Browning), Valentine (Carol Ann Duffy), The Manhunt (Simon Armitage).
  • Conflict — war, inner conflict, identity and prejudice. E.g. A Poison Tree (Blake), The Charge of the Light Brigade (Tennyson), Exposure (Owen), War Photographer (Carol Ann Duffy), Poppies (Jane Weir).
  • Time and Place — nature, memory, belonging and home. E.g. To Autumn (Keats), London (Blake), Nothing's Changed (Tatamkhulu Afrika), Presents from my Aunts in Pakistan (Moniza Alvi), Hurricane Hits England (Grace Nichols).

The skills are the same for every collection: compare two poems across theme, language, form & structure and tone, always linking method to effect and weaving in context.

The Assessment Objectives

What the examiner rewards

The anthology comparison is worth 20 marks, and the two objectives it actually rewards are AO2 (methods, 15 marks) and AO3 (comparison + context, 5 marks) — AO1 is not separately marked on this question:

Key difference from the novel section: here AO2 and AO3 are the main drivers. AO3 rewards genuine comparison between the two poems (their similarities and differences) as well as context. So you must keep comparing methods and effects, not analyse each poem in isolation.

The heart of AO2

Method → effect, every time

Naming a technique earns almost nothing. The marks come from linking the method to the effect it creates on the reader — in both poems you compare:

method + evidence → effect on readere.g. "the enjambment in the opening line (method) makes the speaker's longing spill over uncontrolled (effect)"

Useful methods to spot in poetry: metaphor, simile, personification, imagery, symbolism, pathetic fallacy, semantic field, sibilance, enjambment, caesura, volta, first-person speaker, tone and form (sonnet, ballad, dramatic monologue).

Golden rule: never stop at "this is a metaphor." Always finish the sentence: "…which suggests / creates / emphasises / makes the reader feel …" — then compare it to how the other poem does the same idea.

Worked model · annotation

Annotating an anthology poem

Here is a short, made-up stanza (not a real anthology poem) annotated the way you'd mark up a printed poem in the exam:

The autumn road runs silver to the sea,
and every gate I pass was once a home;
the wind still whispers names it will not keep.
  • "autumn" — seasonal imagery / semantic field of endings: hints at loss and passing time.
  • "silver" — colour imagery: makes the memory feel cold and beautiful at once.
  • "the wind still whispers" — personification: nature seems to remember what the speaker cannot hold on to.
  • "gate I pass" — enjambment runs the line on, mirroring a journey that will not stop.

Notice the pattern: each note is quote → method (with terminology) → effect. That is exactly the sentence shape your comparison paragraphs need for both poems.

The core skill · comparison

Compare — don't just describe two poems

The anthology question is a comparison. Weak answers write about poem A, then poem B. Strong answers knit the two together using comparative connectives:

  • Similarities: similarly, likewise, both, in the same way.
  • Differences: whereas, however, by contrast, unlike, while.

Compare across four angles: theme (what each poem says about love / conflict / place), language, form & structure and tone.

Integrated vs block: an integrated comparison moves between the two poems within each paragraph ("Whereas Duffy uses… Armitage instead…"). This scores higher than a block answer (all of poem A, then all of poem B).

Form matters

Name the form — it earns AO2

The anthology poems use very different forms, and naming the form (then its effect) is strong AO2. Look out for:

  • Dramatic monologue — a single speaker unknowingly reveals their own character, as in Robert Browning's My Last Duchess (the Duke exposes his controlling menace).
  • Sonnet — a 14-line form, often about love, as in Barrett Browning's Sonnet 43 ("How do I love thee").
  • Ballad — a story-telling poem with regular rhythm, as in Keats's La Belle Dame sans Merci.

Effect, not label: don't just write "this is a sonnet." Say why — e.g. the tight sonnet form contains and controls the speaker's overflowing love, making its intensity feel disciplined and sincere.

Argument skill

Open with a comparative thesis

Open with a thesis that already compares — a clear line of argument about how the two poems treat the theme. Everything after it is proof.

Model opening (generic)

Question: "Compare how the poets present love in the named poem and one other."

Thesis: "Both poets present love as powerful, yet whereas the first celebrates it as tender devotion, the second exposes it as possessive and destructive — a contrast driven by their very different tones and forms."

Why it works: it takes a comparative position (both powerful, but tender vs destructive), signals methods (tone, form) and sets up the comparison the mark scheme rewards. Now every paragraph proves one part of it.

Evidence done well

Embed quotes — keep them short

Learn a bank of short, flexible quotations from both a named poem and your chosen comparison poem. Weave them into your own sentence rather than dropping in whole lines:

Clumsy vs embedded

❌ "Duffy shows love is not romantic. The quote is: 'Not a red rose or a satin heart. I give you an onion.'"

✔ "Duffy's blunt refusal — 'Not a red rose' — rejects cliché, while the surprising gift of an 'onion' insists love is raw and honest."

Misconception: long quotations don't earn more marks — they waste time and bury your analysis. Quote the few words you'll actually analyse in each poem.

Context & AO3

Context serves the comparison

AO3 also rewards context — but only where it explains a choice in the poem. It must be woven in, not bolted on as a history lecture. Useful anthology contexts include:

  • Romanticism (Keats, Wordsworth) — nature and intense feeling.
  • The First World War (Owen's Exposure) — the horror and futility of the trenches.
  • Post-colonial identity — Alvi's mixed heritage, Zephaniah's protest, and apartheid behind Afrika's Nothing's Changed.

Bolted-on vs woven-in: ❌ "Owen fought in WW1, which was terrible." · ✔ "Owen's icy imagery, drawn from his own trench experience, makes the true enemy the merciless weather — sharpening his protest against war's futility."

Exam technique

Planning under the clock

The anthology comparison is worth 20 marks. Give it roughly 35–40 minutes, and spend the first 5 minutes planning:

  • Read the named printed poem twice, annotating 3–4 methods and their effects.
  • Choose your second poem from the SAME collection — one you can compare well.
  • Jot 3 comparison points (theme / language / form & structure / tone), each linking both poems.
  • Write a one-line comparative thesis, then write, comparing in every paragraph.

Choose wisely: pick a second poem that gives you real similarities and differences to discuss — not just one that shares the topic.

Worked model · comparison

A comparison paragraph in action

Watch how one paragraph compares two poems, with method, effect and a touch of context — this is a generic model (poems named for teaching only):

Model paragraph (generic)

"Both Duffy's Valentine and Barrett Browning's Sonnet 43 present love as powerful and sincere. Whereas Barrett Browning's tightly controlled sonnet form makes her devotion feel ordered and timeless — its repeated 'I love thee' building like a vow — Duffy, by contrast, rejects such tradition, offering an 'onion' whose metaphor insists love can also 'blind you with tears'. Written in a Victorian age of romantic idealism, Barrett Browning elevates love; Duffy, a modern poet, strips it back to something raw and honest, so both celebrate love yet define it very differently."

Spot the moves: both poems compared in each sentence (integrated), methods named (form, metaphor), effects explained, context woven in, and connectives (whereas, by contrast, both) driving the comparison — AO2 and AO3 together (the objectives marked here).

Avoid these traps

The five marks-killers

  • Block answers: all of poem A, then all of poem B, with no comparison.
  • Feature-spotting: naming techniques without explaining their effect.
  • Wrong comparison poem: choosing one from a different collection, or one you can't compare.
  • Bolted-on context: a history block that doesn't serve a point.
  • Fat quotes: long copied stanzas instead of short, embedded ones.

Fix them all with one habit: in every paragraph, compare both poems with a connective, name a method, explain its effect, and — where it helps — weave in context.

Recap

The skills to take into the exam

The anthology: three collections (Relationships / Conflict / Time & Place) of 15 poems — you study one

The question: compare one named printed poem with a second you choose from the SAME collection · 20 marks

AO2: method + terminology + evidence → effect on the reader, in both poems

AO3: genuine comparison (similarities & differences) + context that explains a choice

Connectives: similarly, likewise, both · whereas, however, by contrast, unlike, while

Structure: integrated comparison > block; ~35–40 min; plan 5 min

Before you finish

Your comparison checklist

Run this checklist over every anthology answer you write:

  • ✅ Did I compare in every paragraph, not describe two poems separately?
  • ✅ Did I use comparative connectives (whereas, similarly, however…)?
  • ✅ For each poem, did I go method → effect using terminology?
  • ✅ Did I name form where it matters (sonnet, ballad, dramatic monologue)?
  • ✅ Did I weave in context only where it explains a choice?
  • ✅ Are my quotes short and embedded in my own sentences?

One line to remember: the anthology question rewards comparison — keep both poems talking to each other from your very first sentence.
