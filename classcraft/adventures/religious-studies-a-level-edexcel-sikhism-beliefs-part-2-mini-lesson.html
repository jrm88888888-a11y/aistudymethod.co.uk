Mission 1: Who has authority among Sikhs now there is no living human Guru?

Make a guess: Who has authority among Sikhs now there is no living human Guru?

Mission 1

Sangat: the holy company

  • Sangat = congregation, the company of Sikhs gathered around the Guru's word.
  • How is the true congregation known? Where the one Name is praised (Guru Granth Sahib 72, paraphrase).
  • Sat sangat / sadh sangat: the company of the truthful, which helps a person become gurmukh.
  • Sangat sits together, eats together (langar) and sings together (kirtan): no caste or rank.
  • A Sikh is never meant to walk the path alone.
Mission 1

The Gurus build the sangat

  • Guru Nanak: a community at Kartarpur in his last years: work, kirtan, shared meals.
  • Guru Angad: langar at Khadur; the Gurmukhi script.
  • Guru Amar Das: Goindwal; langar before meeting the Guru; manjis (regional leaders); gatherings at Vaisakhi and Diwali.
  • Guru Ram Das: founds Ramdaspur (later Amritsar) and its pool.
  • Guru Arjan: builds the Harmandir Sahib; installs the Adi Granth there (1604); masands collect dasvandh.
  • Guru Hargobind: the Akal Takht (1606), facing the Harmandir.
Mission 1

Amritsar: spiritual and political centres

  • Harmandir Sahib (Golden Temple): spiritual centre: four doors (open to all), set lower than the city (humility), Guru Granth Sahib at the centre, kirtan through the day.
  • Akal Takht ("throne of the Timeless"): seat of temporal authority: miri beside piri.
  • In the 18th century the Sarbat Khalsa met there and passed gurmattas (decisions in the Guru's name).
  • Today the Jathedar of the Akal Takht issues hukamnamas (edicts) to Sikhs worldwide.
  • One site, two kinds of authority: the sangat is spiritual and political.

Mission 1

1699 and 1708: Guru Panth

  • 1699, Vaisakhi at Anandpur: Guru Gobind Singh creates the Khalsa; the Panj Piare (five beloved) are initiated, then initiate the Guru.
  • 1708, Nanded: before his death he ends the line of human Gurus.
  • Guruship passes to the Guru Granth (the scripture: spiritual authority) and to the Guru Panth (the Khalsa community: corporate authority).
  • So decisions are made collectively, in the scripture's presence: gurmatta.
  • Detail of Guru Gobind Singh's decisions.
Mission 1

The Panth as a qaum

  • Panth = path; the whole Sikh community. Qaum = a people or nation (Persian/Arabic).
  • 18th century: the Khalsa acts as a sovereign people (misls, Dal Khalsa).
  • 1898: Kahn Singh Nabha's Ham Hindu Nahin ("We are not Hindus"): the Sikhs a distinct people.
  • 20th century: the SGPC (from 1920; Sikh Gurdwaras Act 1925) and Akali politics give the qaum institutions.
  • Debate: a religious community, a nation, or both? Many diaspora Sikhs stress a worldwide faith community; some stress political self-rule.
Mission 1

Sangat today: local, global, virtual

  • Local: the gurdwara diwan, langar, Punjabi and kirtan classes, sewa rotas.
  • Global: the Panth worldwide: Vaisakhi, Akal Takht edicts, relief groups such as Khalsa Aid (founded 1999).
  • Virtual: live kirtan streamed daily from the Harmandir Sahib; online paths and diwans, especially in the 2020 lockdowns; social media debate.
  • K. E. Nayar, The Sikh Diaspora in Vancouver (2004): three generations negotiate tradition, language and identity differently; the young may relate to sangat in new ways (paraphrase).
  • N K Singh, Sikhism: An Introduction (2011): the community is formed by the Guru's word heard and shared (paraphrase).
Mission 1Evaluate

"An online sangat is a true sangat."

  • For: sangat is where the Name is praised (GGS 72), not a building; streamed kirtan unites the global Panth.
  • For: it includes the housebound, the isolated and Sikhs far from a gurdwara.
  • Against: langar, seva and sitting together on the floor are embodied equality; a screen cannot share food.
  • Against: the Guru Granth Sahib's physical presence and respect (maryada) are central to gathering.
  • Judge it by: what makes sangat (Name, presence, service); local vs global; whether virtual sangat supplements or replaces the physical.

Mission 1

Sants in Sikh life today

  • Sant = holy person; in Sikh use, a respected spiritual teacher (also the "sant" of sant-sipahi, saint-soldier).
  • Many contemporary sants lead deras (centres): kirtan, langar, schools, amrit campaigns, charity.
  • Supporters: sants inspire devotion and model the gurmukh life, especially in rural Punjab and the diaspora.
  • Critics (often reformist, Singh Sabha tradition): personal followings can rival the Guru Granth and Guru Panth; 1708 ended human Guruship.
  • Groups that keep living human gurus (e.g. Namdharis) are regarded by the mainstream as outside the Guru Granth principle.

Mission 2

Gurus 2 to 4 build a community

  • Guru Angad (Guru 1539–1552): tradition credits him with shaping the Gurmukhi script used for the hymns; with his wife Mata Khivi, the free kitchen (langar).
  • Guru Amar Das (1552–1574): at Goindval, "first langar, then the Guru": everyone, even (tradition says) Emperor Akbar, ate sitting together.
  • He set up manjis (preaching districts), spoke against sati and the veiling of women, and wrote Anand Sahib.
  • Guru Ram Das (1574–1581): founded Ramdaspur, later Amritsar, and began its pool; wrote the Lavan (wedding hymns).
  • Each Guru was chosen for service, not birth; from Ram Das on, from one family (the Sodhis).
Mission 2

Sangat and Amritsar

  • Sangat: the congregation of Sikhs who gather with the Guru, and now around the Guru Granth Sahib.
  • It grew from Kartarpur (Nanak) to Goindval's langar and the manjis (Amar Das), which spread sangats across Punjab.
  • Amritsar became a spiritual centre: the Harmandir in the pool, with the scripture at its heart (1604).
  • And a political centre: Guru Hargobind's Akal Takht ("throne of the Timeless One", 1606) faces it.
  • Together they express miri (temporal) and piri (spiritual) authority (see Mission 5). Sangat as Panth.
Mission 2

The Harmandir

  • Built from 1588 in the pool at Amritsar; tradition says the Sufi Mian Mir of Lahore laid the foundation stone.
  • Lower than the surrounding ground: worshippers step down, showing humility before God.
  • Four doors, one on each side: open to all four directions, all castes, everyone.
  • Reached by a causeway across the sarovar (pool); the scripture installed at the centre in 1604: the Word, not an image.
  • The gold covering came later (early 19th century, Maharaja Ranjit Singh): hence "Golden Temple".

Mission 2

The martyrdom of Guru Arjan, 1606

  • Your spec spells his name Guru Arjun.
  • Emperor Jahangir (ruled 1605–1627). Arjan was said to have blessed Jahangir's rebel son Khusrau.
  • Jahangir's memoirs: the Guru at Goindval had drawn many Hindus and even Muslims; Jahangir had long wished to end this "shop", and ordered his death and the seizure of his property (paraphrase).
  • Sikh tradition: in Lahore he was seated on a red-hot iron plate and burning sand was poured over him; then taken to the river Ravi, where he died.
  • He accepted God's will: Your will is sweet to me (GGS 394, paraphrase).
  • Sikhs remember him as the first Sikh martyr (shaheed).
Mission 2Diversity

Why was Guru Arjan killed?

  • Religious persecution (Sikh tradition): he refused to change his scripture or convert; his death shows the cost of faith under tyranny.
  • Political (Jahangir's own words): the Khusrau blessing and a growing following the emperor saw as a threat.
  • Personal enmities (Sikh tradition): Chandu Shah, a Lahore official, and the rival Prithi Chand.
  • Some historians also cite a Naqshbandi Sufi leader, Ahmad Sirhindi, whose letter welcomed the execution.
  • Most historians combine causes; many Sikhs see in all of them the same meaning: standing firm in truth.
Mission 2

Hargobind to Har Krishan

  • Guru Hargobind (Guru 1606–1644), aged 11 when his father died: wore two swords, miri (worldly power) and piri (spiritual authority).
  • Built the Akal Takht, kept horsemen, and fought Mughal forces in the 1620s–30s; imprisoned at Gwalior and released.
  • Guru Har Rai (1644–1661): kept the army but avoided war; ran a herbal dispensary.
  • Guru Har Krishan (1661–1664): Guru at five; died of smallpox in Delhi, caring for the sick.
  • Arjan's death is often seen as the turning point: the Panth learns to defend itself.
Mission 2

The martyrdom of Guru Tegh Bahadur, 1675

  • Guru Tegh Bahadur (1621–1675; Guru 1664–1675): found at Bakala by the trader Makhan Shah; founded Anandpur; his hymns are in the GGS.
  • Sikh tradition (the Bachittar Natak in the Dasam Granth, and later Sikh sources): Kashmiri Pandits (Brahmins), facing forced conversion under Emperor Aurangzeb, asked his help, and he told them: if the Guru converts, they will.
  • He was arrested, refused to convert and was beheaded in Delhi (Gurdwara Sis Ganj marks the place).
  • Companions Bhai Mati Das, Bhai Sati Das and Bhai Dayal Das were killed before him.
  • Sikhs remember him as dying for the freedom of a faith not his own, and call him Hind di Chadar, "shield of India".
  • Mughal court sources present him as a political threat; historians debate the causes.
  • Significance today: martyrdom (shaheedi) as witness to truth and defence of others' rights.

Mission 3

Three pillars

  • Nam japna: remember God's Name.
  • Kirat karni: earn an honest living by your own work.
  • Vand chhakna: share what you have with others.
  • Rooted in Guru Nanak's community at Kartarpur: prayer, farming and shared meals.
  • Your spec spells them nam japna, kirt karna, vand chakna.
Mission 3

Nam japna: remembering the Name

  • Keeping God in mind all the time, not only at set prayers.
  • Forms: simran (repeating "Waheguru"), Nitnem (daily prayers), kirtan (sung hymns), amrit vela (early-morning meditation).
  • Purpose: to overcome haumai and become gurmukh.
  • The other two pillars come from this remembrance: work and sharing done with God in mind.
Mission 3

Kirat karni: honest work

  • Earn by honest effort: no theft, cheating, bribery or exploiting others.
  • Guru Nanak rejected begging and renunciation as a path: the Sikh is a householder (grihasti).
  • Janamsakhi story: Guru Nanak eats with Bhai Lalo, a poor carpenter, rather than at the feast of Malik Bhago, a rich official. Squeezed, Lalo's bread gives milk; Bhago's gives blood: wealth gained by oppression.
  • Work is not a distraction from God; done honestly, it is worship.
Mission 3Read the source

Earn, give, remember

Guru Granth Sahib (paraphrase):

  • One who works for what he eats, and gives some of what he has: Nanak says, he knows the path (GGS 1245, Guru Nanak).
  • Make the effort and earn, and enjoy it; meditate, and meet God, and anxiety ends (GGS 522, Guru Arjan).

Mission 3

Vand chhakna: sharing

  • Share wealth, food, time and skill, especially with those in need.
  • Dasvandh: giving a tenth of income (Guru Arjan organised it; the Rehat Maryada commends it).
  • Langar: free food for all, of any faith or caste: sharing made visible.
  • Sarbat da bhala: "the welfare of all", prayed for at the end of every Ardas.
  • Sharing cuts lobh (greed) and moh (attachment), two of the five thieves.

Mission 3

How the three fit together

  • Kirat in relation to Naam: work is done while remembering God, so it is free of greed and dishonesty.
  • Kirat in relation to Vand: only what is honestly earned can truly be shared.
  • Karma: honest work and sharing are good actions; but liberation is not bought by them.
  • Grace: the three pillars are a life open to nadar, not a wage for it.
  • Sangat: shared earnings fund the gurdwara and langar; the community lives from its members' kirat.
Mission 3

Gurmukh lifestyles today

  • K. E. Nayar and J. S. Sandhu (2007): Guru Nanak's ideal is the socially involved householder, detached from ego, engaged with society (paraphrase).
  • Work: many Sikhs avoid trades they see as harmful (e.g. selling alcohol or tobacco, gambling); ethical business and fair wages.
  • Sharing: langar for people in need, e.g. Sikh groups feeding stranded lorry drivers in Kent (December 2020); global relief by groups such as Khalsa Aid.
  • Remembrance: Nitnem before work; simran on the commute; kirtan apps.
  • N K Singh (2011): Sikh ethics is lived in ordinary life, family and work, not withdrawn from it (paraphrase).
Mission 3Diversity

Different emphases

  • Some Sikhs, often in devotional dera traditions, put nam japna first: long hours of meditation and kirtan.
  • Others put social action first: campaigns for justice, relief work, langar on the streets.
  • Diaspora pressures: long working hours, secular workplaces, debt and consumer culture make balance harder.
  • Debate: is giving a tenth still realistic? Many give time and skill as well as money.
  • All agree the three belong together; they differ on balance, not on the principle.

Mission 3Evaluate

"Nam japna is more important than kirat karni and vand chhakna."

  • For: remembrance of the Name is the root: without it, work and sharing can feed haumai (pride in giving).
  • For: the five khands and mukti centre on the Name and grace.
  • Against: Guru Nanak's own life at Kartarpur joined all three; scripture links them (GGS 1245, 522).
  • Against: remembrance that never reaches work or sharing is the yogis' withdrawal Guru Nanak rejected.
  • Judge it by: whether the three can be ranked or are one way of life; the relation of spiritual and material values; what Sikhs today actually do.

Mission 4

What sewa means

  • Sewa (also spelled seva): selfless service, to God and to others.
  • Your spec spells it seva.
  • Sikhs see no line between the two: God (Waheguru) is present in every person, so serving people is serving God.
  • Sewa is done freely, with no wish for reward, praise or return.
  • It belongs with the three pillars (see Mission 3): remember God, work honestly, share.
  • Your spec: nam japna, kirt karna, vand chakna.
Mission 4

Sewa in the Guru Granth Sahib

  • Guru Nanak: serve in this world, and you will be given a seat of honour in God's court (Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 26, Sri Rag; paraphrase).
  • Guru Arjan, in the Sukhmani: the one who serves without desire for reward (nishkam) reaches the Lord (Ang 286; paraphrase).
  • So sewa is not a side-activity: it is part of the path to mukti (liberation).
  • But Sikhs hold that liberation comes finally by God's grace (nadar): sewa prepares the self; it does not buy salvation.
Mission 4

Three forms of sewa

  • Tan (body): physical service. Cooking and serving langar, washing dishes, cleaning the gurdwara, caring for the sick, wiping pilgrims' shoes.
  • Man (mind): mental service. Teaching, sharing knowledge, reciting and explaining gurbani, counselling, offering skills.
  • Dhan (wealth): material service. Giving money and goods; many Sikhs aim to give dasvandh, a tenth of their income.
  • Sikh tradition often ranks tan sewa highly: it is personal, cannot be paid for by someone else, and is the most humbling.
Mission 4

The state of mind: sewa against haumai

  • Haumai: the ego, "I-am-ness"; self-centredness that keeps the soul apart from God.
  • A manmukh (self-willed person) serves to be seen. A gurmukh (God-centred person) serves because self has given way to God.
  • So the intention matters more than the act: selfless and desire-less action.
  • Sewa done for status or reputation feeds haumai; done humbly, it loosens it.

Mission 4

Bhai Ghanaiya: service without barriers

  • Bhai Ghanaiya (d. 1718), a Sikh of Guru Gobind Singh, carried water to the wounded at Anandpur, Sikh and Mughal alike.
  • Accused of helping the enemy, he said he saw no friend or foe: he saw the Guru in every face (tradition).
  • The Guru praised him and gave him ointment to dress wounds as well.
  • Sewa recognises no barriers of religion, caste or race.
  • Ghanaiya is remembered as founder of the Sewapanthi order; Sikh first-aid and relief groups today often name him.
  • Modern examples: langar open to all; Sikh relief charities serving disaster areas regardless of faith.
Mission 4

Sarbat da bhala: sewa for everyone

  • Every Ardas (the congregational prayer) ends asking for sarbat da bhala: "the welfare of all".
  • Sewa is the practical side of that prayer: not only for Sikhs.
  • Langar shows it: anyone may eat, sitting together, whatever their religion, caste, gender or wealth.

Mission 4

The socially involved renunciate

  • K. E. Nayar and J. S. Sandhu, The Socially Involved Renunciate (2007), on Guru Nanak's dialogue with the Nath yogis (Siddh Gosht).
  • Their reading (paraphrase): Guru Nanak rejects leaving the world to find God. The ideal Sikh is detached inside but engaged in society: householder, worker, servant of others.
  • Seva fits this ideal exactly: spiritual discipline lived out in the world, not in a cave.
  • Nikky-Guninder Kaur Singh (Sikhism: An Introduction, 2011) presents Sikh ethics as flowing from love of the One: the person who knows the divine in all serves all (paraphrase of her approach).
  • Spec names N K Singh and J Sandhu for 1.3; use them on why seva is a principle, not just an activity.
Mission 4Evaluate

"Sewa is the most important Sikh teaching."

  • For: it puts every other belief into action: one God in all, equality, sarbat da bhala.
  • For: it is the visible mark of Sikh identity: langar and Sikh relief work are what many non-Sikhs know of Sikhism.
  • For: it attacks haumai directly, the main obstacle to mukti.
  • Against: without nam simran (remembering God's Name in meditation), sewa becomes social work; Gurbani puts remembering the Name at the centre.
  • Against: liberation is by grace (nadar); no practice, sewa included, earns it.
  • Against: sewa depends on kirat karni (honest work): you can only share what you have honestly earned.
  • Practicality today: busy working lives and distance from a gurdwara limit tan sewa; but man and dhan sewa, online teaching and relief charities widen it.
  • Judgement criteria: what Gurbani stresses most; whether one teaching makes the others possible; what shapes Sikh identity in practice.

Mission 5

Miri and piri

  • Piri: spiritual authority, from pir (a holy teacher).
  • Miri: temporal (worldly, political) authority, from mir (a ruler).
  • Miri-piri: the two belong together. Faith is not only private prayer; it must also act in the world, defending justice.
  • Your spec calls miri-piri the unique, original and distinctive feature of Sikhism: be ready to test that claim.
Mission 5

Guru Hargobind's two swords

  • 1606: Guru Arjan, the fifth Guru, dies in Mughal custody in Lahore.
  • His son Guru Hargobind (1595–1644) becomes the sixth Guru.
  • At his installation he wears two swords: one of miri, one of piri.
  • He builds the Akal Takht ("Throne of the Timeless One") facing the Harmandir Sahib: a seat of temporal authority beside the seat of worship.
  • He keeps horses and armed followers; the Sikhs fight defensive battles against Mughal forces.
  • Many Sikhs see this as Arjan's own wish: the community must be able to protect itself.
Mission 5

Original or new?

  • Continuity view: miri-piri was present from Guru Nanak, who criticised unjust rulers and taught a faith lived in society. Hargobind made visible what was there.
  • Nayar and Sandhu's "socially involved renunciate" (see Mission 4) can support the continuity view.
  • Development view: Nanak's path was peaceful and devotional; armed resistance arose from historical pressure after Arjan's death.
  • Both views agree the Gurus never taught a purely private religion.
  • Your spec calls miri-piri "unique, original and distinctive": weigh that phrase against both views.
Mission 5

The saint-soldier: Sant Sipahi

  • Guru Gobind Singh (1666–1708), tenth Guru, founds the Khalsa in 1699.
  • Ideal: the sant sipahi, the saint-soldier.
  • Sant duties: nam simran, sewa, honest work, self-discipline, humility.
  • Sipahi duties: courage, readiness to defend the weak and the faith, discipline in arms.
  • The saint comes first: the soldier is only legitimate when controlled by a saintly mind free of anger, greed and ego.

The Zafarnama

  • 1705: Guru Gobind Singh writes the Zafarnama ("Letter of Victory"), in Persian, to the emperor Aurangzeb, after Mughal commanders broke sworn oaths of safe passage at Anandpur.
  • Verse 22 (paraphrase): when every other means has failed, it is right to take up the sword.

Mission 5

Teg and deg

  • Teg: the sword: protecting the poor and oppressed.
  • Deg: the cooking pot: feeding the hungry (langar).
  • Together: Sikhs meet material and spiritual needs: food for the body, protection for the vulnerable, the Name for the soul.
  • The phrase "Deg Teg Fateh" ("victory to the pot and sword") appears on the seal of Banda Singh Bahadur (from 1710) and on Sikh coins from 1764.
  • Today: langar and Sikh relief work are deg; standing up for others' rights is teg.
Mission 5

The kirpan

  • One of the 5Ks worn by amritdhari (initiated) Sikhs.
  • A common explanation of the word: kirpa (mercy, grace) + aan (honour, dignity).
  • It is a religious article of faith, not a weapon for attack: a reminder of the duty to defend justice and the weak.
  • Sizes vary: from a full sword in ceremonies to a small blade worn under clothes.
  • Many Sikhs treat it with great respect and do not draw it in anger.

Mission 5Evaluate

"It is impossible to combine spirituality with the use of force."

  • For: force tends to feed anger and ego (haumai), the very thing spiritual life overcomes.
  • For: many spiritual traditions (e.g. Jain ahimsa, Christian pacifists, Gandhi) hold that true spirituality renounces force.
  • For: in many traditions, "righteous war" claims have been made for causes others reject.
  • Against: the sant sipahi ideal subordinates force to the saint: only to protect others, as a last resort.
  • Against: refusing to defend the oppressed may itself be a moral failure; Guru Tegh Bahadur died defending others' freedom of religion.
  • Against: the Khalsa's rules and Dharam Yudh's criteria limit force in a way pure "warrior" cultures do not.
  • Judgement criteria: is force truly a last resort? who decides when dharam is threatened? can inner detachment really survive battle?
  • Apply the same map to the claim that miri-piri is the unique, original and distinctive feature of Sikhism.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Sangat grew through the Gurus' lives, with Amritsar as spiritual (Harmandir) and political (Akal Takht) centre.
  • In 1708 guruship passed to the Guru Granth and the Guru Panth; the Panth is also understood as a qaum (people or nation).
  • Sangat today is local, global and virtual; sants inspire many Sikhs, but some worry they rival the Guru Granth and Panth.

Mission 2

  • Gurus 2–4 built the community: Gurmukhi, langar, manjis, Amritsar; sangat grew with each Guru.
  • Guru Arjan: Harmandir (humility, openness), Adi Granth (1604), Sukhmani, dasvandh; martyred in 1606 under Jahangir.
  • Hargobind's miri-piri answered that death; Guru Tegh Bahadur was executed in 1675; Sikhs remember him as defending others' freedom of faith.

Mission 3

  • Nam japna (remember the Name), kirat karni (earn honestly) and vand chhakna (share) form one gurmukh way of life, rooted in Guru Nanak's Kartarpur.
  • The pillars are good karma and a life open to grace, but grace is not earned; they sustain the sangat.
  • Today they shape work choices, giving (dasvandh, langar) and daily remembrance; Sikhs differ on balance.

Mission 4

  • Sewa (seva) is selfless service to God and to others; Sikhs see no line between the two.
  • Three forms: tan (physical), man (mental), dhan (material); intention matters most: desire-less action that loosens haumai.
  • Sewa knows no barriers of religion or caste (Bhai Ghanaiya; sarbat da bhala) and is central to Sikh identity.

Mission 5

  • Miri (temporal) and piri (spiritual) belong together: Guru Hargobind's two swords and the Akal Takht facing the Harmandir Sahib.
  • Guru Gobind Singh's Khalsa lives the ideal of the sant sipahi (saint-soldier): force only for dharam, as a last resort, under saintly control.
  • Teg and deg: protect the oppressed and feed the hungry; the kirpan is an article of faith reminding Sikhs of that duty.
