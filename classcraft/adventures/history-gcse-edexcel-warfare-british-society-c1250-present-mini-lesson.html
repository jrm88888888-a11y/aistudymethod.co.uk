Warfare & British Society c1250–present

This mini-lesson is a through-time thematic study of how warfare changed in Britain from the medieval world to today. We track three big threads: changing weapons (longbow → gunpowder → machine gun → nuclear), changing recruitment (feudal levy → volunteers → conscription → professionals), and the changing role of civilians (from the sidelines to the front line of total war).

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go (some are multiple choice, some ask for a date or a key term) and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you're ready.

How this topic works

A "through time" study

This is a thematic study: instead of one short period, it sweeps across roughly 750 years. The exam wants you to trace continuity and change — what stayed the same and what transformed.

Three threads run through the whole story:

  • Weapons — how armies fought, from bows and swords to nuclear missiles;
  • Recruitment — how soldiers were raised, from feudal duty to professional forces;
  • Civilians — how ordinary people went from bystanders to being in the front line.

Exam tip: the biggest marks come from spotting turning points — moments when warfare changed sharply, such as gunpowder, the Industrial Revolution and the arrival of total war.

Medieval · c1250–1500

Knights, castles and the longbow

Medieval war was dominated by the mounted knight (heavy cavalry) and the castle. Armies were small and raised through the feudal levy — lords owed the king soldiers in return for their land.

  • Knights fought on horseback in armour; expensive and highly trained.
  • Castles were vital for defence and controlling territory (sieges were common).
  • The longbow became a war-winning weapon — decisive at Crécy (1346) and Agincourt (1415) in the Hundred Years' War.
  • Late in the period, gunpowder and cannon began to appear, hinting at change to come.

The English longbow could fire fast and pierce armour at range — allowing outnumbered English armies to defeat larger forces of French knights.

Early modern · c1500–1700

Gunpowder transforms war

The spread of gunpowder was a huge turning point. Guns and cannon changed how battles and sieges were fought:

  • Muskets and cannon came to dominate the battlefield; armies used "pike and shot" tactics.
  • The armoured knight and the tall stone castle both declined — cannon could smash castle walls.
  • Armies grew larger and needed more money and organisation.

Turning point: gunpowder ended the age of the knight. A trained gunner with a musket could now bring down a knight who had spent a lifetime learning to fight.

Civil War · 1642–51

The New Model Army

During the English Civil War (1642–51), Parliament created the New Model Army in 1645 — Britain's first professional standing army.

  • Soldiers were full-time and paid, promoted on merit rather than birth.
  • It was well drilled and disciplined — a modern-style fighting force.
  • This marked a shift away from armies raised only for a single campaign and then disbanded.

Recruitment thread: the New Model Army is a key step from the medieval feudal levy towards the professional, standing armies of later centuries.

18th–19th century

Industry changes war

By the 1700s and 1800s Britain fielded professional armies. The Duke of Wellington's victory over Napoleon at the Battle of Waterloo (1815) ended the Napoleonic Wars. Then the Industrial Revolution transformed warfare:

  • Railways moved troops and supplies quickly over long distances;
  • Rifled muskets were far more accurate than old smoothbore guns;
  • The telegraph allowed rapid communication and command.
  • The Crimean War (1853–56) exposed poor army organisation — Florence Nightingale highlighted the terrible medical care.

Recruitment thread: soldiers were still mostly volunteers in this era — mass compulsory conscription had not yet arrived.

20th century · WW1 1914–18

WW1 and total war

The First World War (1914–18) brought total war — whole nations, economies and populations were thrown into the conflict. Industrial weapons made it appallingly deadly:

  • Machine guns and heavy artillery created deadlock — the war of the trenches.
  • New weapons: tanks (from 1916), poison gas and aircraft.
  • Conscription (compulsory military service) was introduced in 1916 to fill the ranks.
  • The Home Front mattered as never before — civilians made munitions, and could now be targeted.

20th century · WW2 1939–45

WW2 and the Home Front

The Second World War (1939–45) was total war on an even greater scale. Warfare became faster and more mobile — and British civilians were directly in the firing line:

  • Blitzkrieg ("lightning war"): fast-moving tanks and aircraft working together.
  • New technology: radar and secret code-breaking gave a vital edge.
  • The Blitz — German bombing of British cities put civilians in the front line.
  • Civilian life changed: rationing, evacuation of children, and conscription of men and women into war work.

Civilians thread: in WW2 the difference between "soldier" and "civilian" blurred — bombing meant ordinary families faced the war directly at home.

The nuclear age · 1945→

The atomic bomb and after

In 1945 the USA dropped atomic bombs on Japan, ending WW2 and opening the nuclear age. Warfare would never be the same:

  • Nuclear weapons could destroy whole cities in seconds — and Britain built its own.
  • During the Cold War, the threat of nuclear war (deterrence) shaped defence policy.
  • Modern forces use hi-tech weapons: drones, precision missiles and cyber warfare, with small, highly trained professional forces.
  • Today the media and public opinion strongly shape how wars are fought.

Recruitment thread: Britain ended conscription in the 1960s. Today it relies on a small, well-equipped professional army — a striking contrast with WW1 and WW2.

Exam skill

Writing about change through time

The exam rewards you for explaining how and why warfare changed. Two habits win marks:

  • Link cause to change. New technology (gunpowder, railways, the aircraft) and big events (the Civil War, the World Wars) drove the biggest changes.
  • Note continuity too. Not everything changed — for example, good leadership, training and supply mattered in every era, from Agincourt to today.

Turning points to remember: gunpowder (c1500), the Industrial Revolution (1800s), and the arrival of total war in 1914 — each transformed weapons, recruitment or the role of civilians.

Recap

The essentials to remember

Medieval (c1250–1500): knights, castles, feudal levy; the longbow decisive at Crécy 1346 & Agincourt 1415.

Early modern (c1500–1700): gunpowder ends the knight/castle; New Model Army 1645 = first professional standing army.

18th–19th century: professional armies; Waterloo 1815; Industrial Revolution (railways, rifles, telegraph); Crimea 1853–56.

20th century: total war; WW1 (machine guns, tanks 1916, gas, aircraft), conscription 1916; WW2 (Blitzkrieg, radar, the Blitz, rationing).

The nuclear age: atomic bomb 1945; deterrence; today hi-tech, drones, cyber and professional forces.

Threads: weapons deadlier; recruitment feudal→conscription→professional; civilians moved into the front line.
