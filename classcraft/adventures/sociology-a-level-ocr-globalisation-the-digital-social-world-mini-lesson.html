Globalisation and the digital social world

This mini-lesson covers Component 03, Section A of OCR A Level Sociology (H580) — the compulsory half of the Debates in contemporary society paper. Component 03 is synoptic: 105 marks, 2 hours 15 minutes.

Balance is the examiner's test. Every claim in this topic has a serious counter-claim: the internet liberates and surveils; it connects and isolates; it flattens culture and revives it. Answers that pick one side and shout it do not reach the top band.

Answer the questions as you go and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you are ready.

KQ1 · defining globalisation

What is globalisation — and why is it hard to define?

Anthony Giddens defines globalisation as the intensification of worldwide social relations linking distant localities in such a way that local happenings are shaped by events occurring many miles away. His key concept is time-space distanciation: technology stretches social relations across space and compresses time.

  • Economic globalisation — transnational corporations, global supply chains, 24-hour financial markets, outsourcing.
  • Political globalisation — the EU, UN, WTO, NATO, international law and NGOs. The nation state's sovereignty is shared, pooled or bypassed.
  • Cultural globalisation — the worldwide circulation of media, brands, food, music, sport and religious movements.

OCR explicitly asks you to be 'aware of the problems with defining globalisation'. Four to deploy: (1) It is contested — hyperglobalists say the nation state is finished, sceptics reply that trade was proportionally comparable before 1914 and that most economic activity remains regional, while transformationalists (Giddens' camp) take a middle path. (2) It is uneven — vast populations are barely connected, so 'global' is an overstatement. (3) It is ideological — the word can be used to make particular economic policies sound like an unstoppable natural force. (4) It is not new — empire, missionary religion and the slave trade were all global.

KQ1 · the digital revolution

The digital revolution and the networked society

  • The digital revolution — the shift from analogue to digital, and from broadcast media (few-to-many) to networked media (many-to-many). Ordinary users become producers as well as consumers.
  • The global village. Marshall McLuhan (1964) predicted that electronic media would contract the world so that we experience distant events as immediately as village gossip — 'the medium is the message'.
  • The networked global society. Manuel Castells (The Rise of the Network Society, 1996) argues the network has replaced the hierarchy as the basic form of social organisation. Power now lies with those who can programme the networks and switch between them — and being excluded from the network is the new poverty.
  • Media convergence. Henry Jenkins (Convergence Culture, 2006) shows separate media functions collapsing into single devices and platforms, producing a participatory culture in which audiences remix, share and talk back.
  • Social media, digital social networks and virtual communities — communities of interest, not of place. You can be closer to a stranger who shares your rare condition than to your next-door neighbour.

Social capital. Robert Putnam (Bowling Alone, 2000) argued that American civic life was hollowing out, and distinguished bonding capital (ties within a group — thick, supportive, potentially insular) from bridging capital (ties across groups — thinner, but the source of new opportunities). The exam question is whether digital networks rebuild social capital, or merely provide a thin, bridging substitute for the thick bonds Putnam mourned.

KQ1 · applying theories

Marxism, feminism and postmodernism online

OCR names three theories to apply to digital communication. Learn them as three arguments, not three labels.

  • Marxism. The platforms are owned — a small number of transnational corporations control the infrastructure of global communication. Christian Fuchs (Digital Labour and Karl Marx, 2014) argues that users perform unpaid digital labour: our attention, content and data are the commodity that is sold, so the 'free' platform is in fact an extraordinarily efficient extraction of surplus value. Add Gramsci: the platform doesn't need to censor you if it can shape what feels normal.
  • Feminism. Technology is not neutral — it is designed and governed in a male-dominated industry. Judy Wajcman (TechnoFeminism, 2004) argues gender and technology shape each other. Online spaces reproduce patriarchy through harassment, abuse and the policing of women's appearance; conversely, Donna Haraway's 'A Cyborg Manifesto' (1985) offered an optimistic cyberfeminism in which technology could dissolve the boundaries that fix gender. Both halves are worth marks.
  • Postmodernism. Jean Baudrillard's hyperreality: signs and simulations detach from reality and become more real than the real — the curated feed is more vivid than the life. Lyotard: the fragmentation of metanarratives. Bauman: identity becomes a fluid consumer project. The digital world is postmodernism's best evidence — and its greatest temptation to exaggerate.

KQ2 · impact on identity

Digital communication and identity

  • Identity as performance. Erving Goffman's dramaturgy fits social media almost too well: the profile is front stage, endlessly managed; the drafts, the deleted posts and the group chat are backstage. What is new is that the performance is now archived, measurable and quantified — likes, followers, views become a running audit of the self.
  • The reflexive project intensified. Giddens' self, continuously narrated and revised, now has a public timeline.
  • Postmodern fluidity. Online, identity can be chosen, multiplied and remade — a case for Bauman's liquid identity.
  • The structural reply. Online identity is not weightless. Racism, misogyny and homophobia follow people onto platforms; algorithms sort and target by inferred class, gender and ethnicity; and the freedom to 'be anyone' is not evenly distributed.

Age matters here — OCR asks you to consider class, gender and age. The idea of 'digital natives' (a term popularised by Marc Prensky in 2001) is useful and contested: researchers have repeatedly shown that young people's digital skills vary enormously by class and education, so age alone does not confer competence. Use the term, then criticise it — that is exactly the AO3 move.

KQ2 · impact on relationships

Digital communication and relationships

  • The optimistic case. Digital media sustain relationships across distance — migrant families keep daily contact across continents; isolated people find others like themselves; support communities form around rare illnesses, sexualities and interests that no local neighbourhood could supply. Castells (Networks of Outrage and Hope, 2012) shows networks enabling rapid collective mobilisation.
  • The pessimistic case. Sherry Turkle (Alone Together, 2011) argues we have come to 'expect more from technology and less from each other': constant connection substitutes for the harder work of presence, and we sacrifice conversation for mere connection. Bauman's Liquid Love (2003) describes relationships as increasingly easy to enter and easy to delete — 'connections' rather than bonds.
  • Putnam's question again. Do online ties generate real social capital, or a thin simulation of it? The honest answer sociologists give is: it depends on whether the online tie supplements face-to-face relationships (it usually strengthens them) or replaces them (it usually does not compensate).

Evaluation to remember: most claims in this area rest on survey correlations, and correlation is not cause. Do lonely people use social media more, or does social media make them lonely? The methods point is a free AO3 mark in a synoptic paper.

KQ2 · impact on social inequalities

The digital divide

The digital divide is the gap between those who can access and use digital technology and those who cannot. It has three levels, and the higher ones matter more:

  • First level — access. Devices, broadband, data. This gap is narrowing in rich countries but remains stark globally, and within countries it tracks income and region.
  • Second level — skills and use. Having a phone is not the same as being able to complete a benefits application, compare energy tariffs or spot a scam. Sociologists such as Jan van Dijk argue this usage gap is the deeper divide, and it maps closely onto education and class.
  • Third level — outcomes. Who actually converts internet use into better jobs, health, education and political voice? Unsurprisingly, those already advantaged.

By class: digital exclusion compounds poverty — public services, cheap tariffs and job applications have moved online, so the offline poor pay a poverty premium. By age: older people are disproportionately excluded, and 'just go online' is a policy that quietly abandons them. By gender: women face disproportionate online abuse, which is itself a form of exclusion from public digital space. Globally: whole regions remain thinly connected, so the 'global village' is a village with locked gates.

The killer line: the digital divide does not merely reflect existing inequality — it amplifies it, because everything that was already unequal now has a digital multiplier.

Stretch · surveillance

Surveillance in the digital social world

  • Michel Foucault (Discipline and Punish, 1975) used Bentham's panopticon — a prison where inmates can always be seen but never know when they are being watched — as a model of modern power. Because you might be observed, you police yourself. Surveillance produces self-discipline, which is far cheaper than force.
  • David Lyon and the field of surveillance studies extend this: we live in a surveillance society in which routine data collection sorts populations into categories that determine what they are offered — credit, insurance, a job interview, police attention. Lyon calls this social sorting: surveillance is not just watching, it is classifying.
  • Shoshana Zuboff (The Age of Surveillance Capitalism, 2019) argues that platforms harvest behavioural data not merely to serve you but to predict and modify future behaviour, selling those predictions. Marxists point out that this is exploitation of a new kind: the raw material is you.
  • Sousveillance — the watching runs both ways. Citizens filming police, and the viral documentation of abuses, show that digital surveillance can also be turned upwards.

Balance: surveillance is not simply sinister. The same data infrastructure enables public health, missing-person searches and fraud detection. The sociological question is not 'is surveillance bad?' but who is watched, by whom, with what consequences — and who is exempt?

KQ2 · impact on culture

Homogenisation, defence and glocalisation

OCR names three cultural consequences. They are three competing answers to one question: does global digital culture flatten local difference?

  • Cultural homogenisation. The same platforms, formats, brands and celebrities everywhere. George Ritzer's McDonaldization (1993) argues that the principles of the fast-food restaurant — efficiency, calculability, predictability and control — are spreading through every sector of global society, producing a standardised, disenchanted sameness. Herbert Schiller's cultural imperialism thesis adds the power dimension: the flow is not symmetrical, it runs from the powerful West outwards.
  • Cultural defence and conflict/change. Global culture provokes resistance. Religious revival, nationalist movements and fundamentalism can all be read as defensive reactions against globalisation, using its own tools — the very platforms that spread global culture are used to organise against it.
  • Glocalisation. Roland Robertson (1992) coined the term: the global is always received locally and adapted. Global formats are re-made in local idioms; global music genres are hybridised into local ones. Culture is not being erased, it is being recombined — which links straight back to the hybridity you met in Component 01.

The strongest position is the third one. Both homogenisation and pure local resistance are too simple. Actual evidence shows global forms adapted, resisted and hybridised at once — often by the same people, on the same phone, in the same hour.

Stretch · the political argument

Liberation or manipulation?

  • The liberation case. Digital networks lower the cost of organising. Castells documents how networked movements can mobilise at speed without formal leadership, bypassing state-controlled media. Marginalised groups can publish without permission.
  • The manipulation case. Eli Pariser's filter bubble (2011) argues that personalisation algorithms feed us more of what we already agree with, quietly narrowing the world. Combine that with echo chambers, disinformation, and the concentration of platform ownership, and the 'democratising' internet starts to look like a machine for fragmenting the public and selling its attention.
  • The sober middle. Both are true, and which dominates depends on who owns the platform, who regulates it, and who has the skills to use it — which returns you, neatly, to Marxism and to the digital divide.

Synoptic link: everything in this topic can be tested against Component 02. How would you research a filter bubble? Content analysis of feeds is possible but the algorithm is a corporate secret — a genuine practical and ethical barrier. Saying so is AO3.

Exam craft

Writing the Section A essay

  • Define, then problematise. Open by defining globalisation — and immediately note that the definition is contested. That is AO3 in your first paragraph.
  • Use the three named theories (Marxism, feminism, postmodernism) as an argumentative spine, not a shopping list. Each should answer the previous one.
  • Always split the impact by group. OCR names class, gender and age. An impact that is liberating for a middle-class London teenager may be exclusionary for a poor pensioner in a rural area — say so.
  • Finish with a judgement. Something like: digital globalisation has genuinely expanded connection, voice and cultural hybridity — but it has done so on privately owned infrastructure, and has amplified existing inequalities rather than dissolving them.
