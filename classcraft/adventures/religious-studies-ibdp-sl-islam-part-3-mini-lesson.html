Mission 1: Why would a Muslim who already prays five times a day choose a teacher and a path of training?

Make a guess: Why would a Muslim who already prays five times a day choose a teacher and a path of training?

Mission 1

Ihsan: worship as if you see God

  • The Gabriel hadith (Sahih Muslim, Book of Faith, from 'Umar; also Sahih al-Bukhari, Book of Faith): a stranger in white questions the Prophet; the Prophet later says it was the angel Gabriel, come to teach the religion.
  • Three levels (paraphrase):
  • Islam: the five pillars (outward practice).
  • Iman: faith in God, angels, books, messengers, the Last Day and God's decree.
  • Ihsan: "to worship God as though you see Him; and if you do not see Him, He sees you."
  • Sufis take ihsan (doing what is beautiful; spiritual excellence) as their goal: the same religion, lived with full awareness of God.
Mission 1

Purifying the self

  • Tazkiyat al-nafs: purifying (and growing) the self (nafs), the part of us that wants and grasps.
  • Sufis map three states of the nafs from Qur'anic phrases:
  • The soul that commands evil (al-nafs al-ammara, 12:53).
  • The self-reproaching soul (al-nafs al-lawwama, 75:2): conscience awake.
  • The soul at peace (al-nafs al-mutma'inna, 89:27): "But ah! thou soul at peace!" (Qur'an 89:27, Pickthall)
  • The struggle against the ego is often called the greater jihad (a saying attributed to the Prophet; many hadith scholars judge its chain weak).

Remembrance

> "O ye who believe! Remember Allah with much remembrance." (Qur'an 33:41, Pickthall)

> "Therefore remember Me, I will remember you." (Qur'an 2:152, Pickthall)

> "And do thou (O Muhammad) remember thy Lord within thyself humbly and with awe, below thy breath, at morn and evening." (Qur'an 7:205, Pickthall)

Q1 Which verse could support silent remembrance?

Show answer

7:205: "within thyself … below thy breath".

Q2 What does 2:152 promise in return for remembering God?

Show answer

That God will remember the believer.

Mission 1

Dhikr and muraqaba

  • Dhikr (remembrance): repeating God's names or short formulas, e.g. la ilaha illa'llah (no god but God) or Allah, often counted on prayer beads.
  • Aloud (jahri), often in a group circle (Qadiriyya); or silent (khafi), in the heart (Naqshbandiyya).
  • A litany (wird) is a daily set of prayers and formulas given by the shaykh.
  • Goal: to move remembrance from the tongue to the heart, until the whole person is aware of God.
  • For many Sufis, dhikr is the main doorway to religious experience: states of nearness, love or awe.

Mission 1

Sama': spiritual listening

  • Sama' ("listening"): hearing poetry, chant or music to stir love and longing for God.
  • Mevlevi ceremony (the "whirling dervishes", Konya): the reed flute (ney) plays; dervishes turn, right palm up to receive from God, left palm down to pass it to the earth.
  • Their tall hat stands for a tombstone, the white skirt for a shroud: turning is dying to self.
  • Chishti gatherings in South Asia use qawwali (sung devotional poetry).
  • Listening can bring wajd (ecstasy), a state in which the listener is overcome.
  • Debate: many jurists forbid musical instruments; the Naqshbandiyya avoid music; al-Ghazali allowed sama' with conditions of time, place and company (Ihya', Book of Listening).

Rumi's Song of the Reed

Jalal al-Din Rumi (1207–1273), Masnavi, Book I, opening lines (paraphrase):

  • Listen to the reed flute, telling of separation.
  • Since I was cut from the reed-bed, my cry has made men and women weep.
  • Whoever is far from their source longs to return to the time of union.
  • Its sound is fire, not wind: the fire of love.
  • Meaning (as Sufis read it): the reed is the soul; the reed-bed is its origin with God; the lament is longing; the path is the journey home.

Show answer

It has been cut off from the reed-bed: separation from its source.

Fire, not wind: the fire of love.

Mission 2

Mission 2: A Sufi cried out "I am the Real" and was executed. What did he mean, and what do Sufis say of God?

Make a guess: A Sufi cried out "I am the Real" and was executed. What did he mean, and what do Sufis say of God?

Mission 2

Fana: passing away in God

  • Fana' ("passing away", annihilation): the self's awareness of itself dissolves, until only God is in view.
  • Qur'anic root: "Everyone that is thereon will pass away; There remaineth but the Countenance of thy Lord" (Qur'an 55:26–27, Pickthall).
  • A hadith qudsi (Sahih al-Bukhari, Book of Softening of Hearts, no. 6502): God draws near to the servant who draws near through extra worship, until God becomes "the hearing with which he hears, the sight with which he sees" (paraphrase).
  • What fana is not: the soul does not become God (ittihad), and God does not move into a body (hulul). Mainstream Sufis rejected both.
  • Sober Sufis such as al-Junayd of Baghdad (d. 910) stress what follows: after fana comes baqa' ("remaining"): the self returns, transformed, to live in God and keep the law.
  • Fana is the goal of Sufi mystical experience, and is often compared with union or absorption in other traditions.
Mission 2

Drunken Sufism: ecstatic sayings

  • Drunken (sukr, intoxication) Sufism stresses being overwhelmed by God in fana.
  • In that state some spoke shatahat (ecstatic utterances) that sound like claims to divinity:
  • Abu Yazid al-Bistami (d. c. 874): "Glory be to me! How great is my majesty!" (paraphrase of the Arabic subhani).
  • Al-Hallaj (c. 858–922): "I am the Real" (Ana al-Haqq; al-Haqq is a name of God). Executed in Baghdad in 922: in Sufi tradition, for these words; historians also point to political charges.
  • Readings: (1) blasphemy, as his accusers and later critics held; (2) the human "I" had passed away, so God was speaking: as glowing iron in a fire might say "I am fire" (a classic Sufi image); (3) words of the drunk: excused, but not to be repeated (al-Junayd, al-Ghazali).
Mission 2

Sober Sufism: baqa, states and stations

  • Al-Junayd of Baghdad (d. 910): after fana comes baqa' ("persistence", remaining): the self returns, transformed, to live in God and keep the law.
  • Sobriety (sahw) ranks above intoxication: the perfect Sufi lives in the world as the Prophet did.
  • Sober Sufis value the return to ordinary life as much as the experience itself.

Mission 3

Shari'a and fiqh

  • Shari'a: literally "the path to the water": God's way for human life.
  • "Then We put thee on the (right) Way of Religion: so follow thou that (Way), and follow not the desires of those who know not." (Qur'an 45:18, Yusuf Ali) The Arabic word is shari'ah.
  • It covers worship (prayer, fasting) and dealings (family, trade, food, crime): much more than criminal law.
  • Fiqh ("deep understanding"): the human effort to understand and apply the shari'a. Scholars can err; the shari'a, as God's will, cannot.
  • So Muslims distinguish the ideal (God's shari'a) from law in practice (the jurists' fiqh).
Mission 3

Scriptural sources: Qur'an and Sunnah

  • Qur'an: the first source, God's word. Some rulings are clear (no pork, 5:3; fasting in Ramadan, 2:185); many verses give principles (justice, 4:58; consultation, 42:38).
  • Sunnah: the Prophet's example, known through hadith. It explains and adds detail: the Qur'an commands prayer; the Sunnah shows how to pray.
  • "So take what the Messenger assigns to you, and deny yourselves that which he withholds from you." (Qur'an 59:7, Yusuf Ali)
  • Hadith are graded by chain and text: sahih (sound), hasan (good), da'if (weak).
  • Shi'a Muslims include the teachings of the Imams within the Sunnah.
Mission 3

Non-scriptural sources

  • Ijma (consensus): agreement of the qualified scholars of an age on a ruling. Once reached, Sunnis treat it as binding ("my community will not agree on error", Ibn Majah; paraphrase).
  • Qiyas (analogy): extending a ruling from a case in the texts to a new case that shares its cause ('illah).
  • 'Urf (custom): local practice accepted where it does not contradict the texts (e.g. customary amounts of dowry). Malik also gave weight to the practice ('amal) of Madinah.
  • Other tools: istihsan (juristic preference, Hanafi), maslahah (public interest, Maliki).
  • Twelver Shi'a jurists: Qur'an, Sunnah (with the Imams), ijma (valid if it reflects the Imam's view) and 'aql (reason); they reject qiyas.
Mission 3Read the source

From wine to every intoxicant

> "They ask thee concerning wine and gambling. Say: 'In them is great sin, and some profit, for men; but the sin is greater than the profit.'" (Qur'an 2:219, Yusuf Ali)

> "O ye who believe! Intoxicants and gambling, (dedication of) stones, and (divination by) arrows, are an abomination,- of Satan's handwork: eschew such (abomination), that ye may prosper." (Qur'an 5:90, Yusuf Ali)

  • Many scholars see stages: 2:219 (more harm than benefit), 4:43 (do not pray intoxicated), 5:90 (avoid completely).
  • A hadith: "Every intoxicant is khamr, and every khamr is forbidden" (Muslim; paraphrase).
  • Qiyas: original case: wine · cause ('illah): intoxication · new case: cannabis, cocaine, other drugs · ruling: haram.

Q1 Which verse gives the final ruling to avoid intoxicants completely?

Show answer

5:90.

Q2 What shared cause allows the ruling on wine to cover drugs?

Show answer

They intoxicate: they cloud the mind.

Mission 3

The five rulings (ahkam)

  • Fard / wajib (obligatory): rewarded if done, sin if left: salah, fasting in Ramadan.
  • Mustahabb / mandub (recommended): rewarded if done, no sin if left: extra prayers, sadaqah.
  • Mubah (permitted): neutral: most foods, ordinary work.
  • Makruh (disliked): better avoided, no sin if done: e.g. wasting water in wudu; coming to the mosque after eating raw garlic.
  • Haram (forbidden): sin if done: alcohol and gambling (5:90), pork (5:3).
  • Halal: lawful, everything not haram. Modesty: lower the gaze and guard modesty (24:30–31); dress rules derive from these verses and the Sunnah.
  • Killing and eating animals: halal meat is slaughtered with God's name (6:121), a swift cut and draining of blood; scholars differ on pre-stunning.
  • Makruh applied: smoking was long called makruh; as its harm became known, many scholars ruled it haram (harm to self is forbidden).

Mission 4

Mission 4: Islam has five verdicts for any action, not two. Why would a religion need a "disliked" category?

Make a guess: Islam has five verdicts for any action, not two. Why would a religion need a "disliked" category?

Mission 4

Why five categories?

  • Muslims believe the shari'a covers all of life: worship, family, trade, food, manners.
  • As fiqh developed, jurists needed a clear way to say how strongly God wants or forbids an act.
  • The answer: al-ahkam al-khamsa, the five rulings, a scale from "must" to "must not".
  • The scale guides intention and reward, not only law courts.
Mission 4

The scale of five

  • Fard (also wajib): obligatory. Doing it is rewarded; leaving it is a sin.
  • Mustahab (also mandub, sunna): recommended. Doing it is rewarded; leaving it is no sin.
  • Mubah: permitted and neutral. No reward, no sin.
  • Makruh: disliked. Avoiding it is rewarded; doing it is no sin, but it is discouraged.
  • Haram: forbidden. Doing it is a sin; avoiding it for God's sake is rewarded.
  • Halal means "permitted": in a broad sense everything not haram; Eduqas uses halal for the middle category.
Mission 4Read the source

Do not invent the haram

> "O ye who believe! make not unlawful the good things which Allah hath made lawful for you, but commit no excess" (Qur'an 5:87, Yusuf Ali)

> "But say not - for any false thing that your tongues may put forth,- 'This is lawful, and this is forbidden,' so as to ascribe false things to Allah." (Qur'an 16:116, Yusuf Ali)

  • Jurists drew a principle: in everyday matters, the default is permission; a ban needs evidence.

Q1 What two errors does 5:87 warn against?

Show answer

Forbidding what God allowed; and excess.

Q2 Why might 16:116 make jurists slow to call something haram?

Show answer

Declaring a ban without proof is attributing a lie to God.

Mission 4

Fard and mustahab: examples

  • Fard: the five daily salah; fasting in Ramadan; zakah; Hajj if able; honesty; caring for parents.
  • Fard 'ayn: on each person (salah). Fard kifaya: on the community; if some do it, the rest are free (funeral prayer, training doctors).
  • Mustahab: extra (sunna) prayers; fasting on Mondays and Thursdays; sadaqah; saying bismillah before eating; using the miswak (tooth-stick).
  • Greeting with salam is mustahab; replying to it is obligatory.
  • These categories show a Muslim where the floor is and how to go above it.
Mission 4

Mubah, makruh, haram: examples

  • Mubah: most daily choices: which halal food, which job, what colour to wear.
  • Makruh: wasting water in wudu; eating raw garlic or onion before going to the mosque (Bukhari; paraphrase); many classical jurists put smoking here.
  • Divorce is lawful but discouraged: reported as the most hated of permitted things to God (Abu Dawud; paraphrase; chain debated).
  • Haram: alcohol and gambling (5:90); pork, blood and carrion (2:173); theft; riba; lying; murder.
Mission 4Diversity

Same scale, different placements

  • Hanafi jurists split the top: fard (proved by certain evidence) and wajib (by less certain evidence), e.g. the witr prayer is wajib for Hanafis, strongly recommended for other schools.
  • Hanafis also split makruh tahrimi (close to haram) and makruh tanzihi (mildly disliked).
  • Twelver Shi'a jurists use the same five: wajib, mustahabb, mubah, makruh, haram.
  • The same act may sit in different places: e.g. prawns are halal for most schools, makruh or forbidden for many Hanafis.
  • Each believer follows their school or, for Twelver Shi'a, a senior scholar (marja') (taqlid: following a qualified scholar or school).

Mission 5

Mission 5: Why do Muslims call the fight against your own selfishness the "greater" jihad?

Make a guess: Why do Muslims call the fight against your own selfishness the "greater" jihad?

Mission 5

Jihad means striving

  • Jihad: from j-h-d, "to strive, to make an effort". Not the word for "holy war".
  • "And those who strive in Our (cause),- We will certainly guide them to our Paths" (Qur'an 29:69, Yusuf Ali).
  • Forms of jihad: of the self (nafs); of the tongue and pen (speaking truth, teaching); of wealth (giving); and armed struggle.
  • "Strive in His cause as ye ought to strive" (Qur'an 22:78, Yusuf Ali).
  • Tradition: greater jihad (al-jihad al-akbar): struggle with the self; lesser jihad (al-jihad al-asghar): armed struggle.
Mission 5

Greater jihad: the nafs

  • Nafs: the self, the ego with its desires.
  • Three states named from the Qur'an: the soul inciting to evil (12:53); the self-reproaching soul, conscience (75:2); the soul at peace (89:27).
  • Greater jihad: lifelong effort to move the self towards peace with God: prayer, fasting, honesty, controlling anger and greed.
  • The Prophet is reported to have said that the true striver (mujahid) is the one who strives against his own self for God (Tirmidhi; paraphrase).
  • A popular report calls returning from battle a return "from the lesser jihad to the greater" (al-Bayhaqi; paraphrase; many scholars grade it weak).
Mission 5Read the source

> "Truly he succeeds that purifies it, And he fails that corrupts it!" (Qur'an 91:9–10, Yusuf Ali)

> "And for such as had entertained the fear of standing before their Lord's (tribunal) and had restrained (their) soul from lower desires" (Qur'an 79:40, Yusuf Ali)

Show answer

Mission 5

Lesser jihad in the Prophet's time

  • Makkah (610–622): persecution, but no fighting; patience and preaching.
  • 622: the hijrah to Madinah. Permission to fight comes because the Muslims "are wronged" (22:39).
  • Battles: Badr (624), Uhud (625), the Trench (627).
  • 628: Treaty of Hudaybiyyah: a peace accepted on hard terms.
  • 630: Makkah taken almost without fighting; a general amnesty.
  • Jurists made lesser jihad a communal duty of the ummah (fard kifaya), led by legitimate authority; it becomes an individual duty if the land is invaded.
Mission 5

Conditions of lesser jihad

  • Just cause: defence, or ending oppression of the weak (4:75), never forcing faith: "no compulsion in religion" (2:256).
  • Legitimate authority: declared by a recognised leader, not by individuals.
  • Right intention: for God and justice, not gain or revenge.
  • Last resort and proportion: "do not transgress limits" (2:190).
  • Protect non-combatants: the Prophet forbade killing women and children (Bukhari, Muslim; paraphrase); Abu Bakr told his commanders not to kill the old or children, cut fruit trees or destroy buildings (Malik, Muwatta; paraphrase).
  • Stop when the enemy seeks peace (8:61); treat prisoners humanely.

Mission 5Diversity

Defensive, expansionist, suspended

  • Classical Sunni jurists (c. 8th–12th centuries) divided the world into dar al-Islam and dar al-harb and allowed wars of expansion led by the caliph, alongside truces.
  • Many modern Sunni scholars read the Qur'an as permitting defensive war only; expansion reflected the empires of its time.
  • Twelver Shi'a: most jurists hold that offensive jihad needs the presence of the infallible Imam, so it is suspended during the Occultation; defensive jihad remains.
  • Sufi teachers stress the inner jihad above all.
  • Ahmadiyya Muslims teach that armed jihad for religion is not permitted today; jihad of the pen is the duty of the age.
Mission 5

Terrorism is not jihad

  • Terrorism: violence against civilians to spread fear for political goals.
  • Mainstream scholars across Sunni and Shi'a Islam reject calling it jihad: no legitimate authority, civilians targeted, suicide forbidden (4:29).
  • "If any one slew a person - unless it be for murder or for spreading mischief in the land - it would be as if he slew the whole people" (Qur'an 5:32, Yusuf Ali).
  • Examples: the Amman Message (2004–05); Tahir-ul-Qadri's Fatwa on Terrorism and Suicide Bombings (2010); scholars' open letter to the leader of ISIS (2014).
  • Groups that use the word for terror are a small minority, condemned by most Muslim bodies; many of their victims are Muslims.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Ihsan (worship as if you see God) is the goal; tazkiyat al-nafs (purifying the self) and remembrance (dhikr) are the means.
  • A shaykh trains a disciple within a tariqa (path and order), through bay'a, companionship, litanies and, for some orders, sama'.
  • Rumi's reed: the soul cut from its source longs to return; love drives the journey.

Mission 2

  • Fana' (passing away) and baqa' (remaining) describe the soul's oneness with God in awareness, not essence.

Mission 3

  • Shari'a is God's way; fiqh is the human understanding of it. Sources: Qur'an and Sunnah, then ijma, qiyas and custom (Twelvers: reason instead of qiyas).
  • Al-Shafi'i (767–820) set the order of sources; five schools (Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, Hanbali, Ja'fari) apply them.
  • Every act falls under five rulings: obligatory, recommended, permitted, disliked (makruh), forbidden (haram).

Mission 4

  • Fard, mustahab, mubah (halal), makruh, haram: a scale of how strongly God wills an act, linked to reward and sin.
  • Most of life is permitted; bans need evidence (5:87, 16:116); necessity and intention matter.
  • Schools differ at the edges (Hanafi fard/wajib; prawns; smoking); Shi'a fiqh uses the same five.

Mission 5

  • Jihad = striving: greater (against the nafs) and lesser (armed, under conditions).
  • Lesser jihad in the Prophet's time was a response to persecution and attack, limited by the Qur'an (22:39, 2:190, 8:61) and the Prophet's rules.
