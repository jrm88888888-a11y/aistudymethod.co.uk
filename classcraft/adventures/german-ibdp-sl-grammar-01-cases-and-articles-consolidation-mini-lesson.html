Cases and articles

German has four cases — Nominativ, Akkusativ, Dativ and Genitiv. The case tells you the job a noun does in the sentence, and it shows up on the article (der/die/das and ein-words) and sometimes on the noun itself. Master this and the whole grammar falls into place.

The big picture

What each case does

The case marks a noun's role. English shows this with word order; German shows it with the case ending, which frees the word order up:

  • Nominativ — the subject (who/what does the verb): Der Mann schläft.
  • Akkusativ — the direct object (who/what is affected): Ich sehe den Mann.
  • Dativ — the indirect object (to/for whom): Ich gebe dem Mann das Buch.
  • Genitiv — possession (whose): das Auto des Mannes.

The tell: ask wer/was? for the subject (Nominativ), wen/was? for the direct object (Akkusativ), wem? for the indirect object (Dativ), and wessen? for possession (Genitiv).

The dative

Indirect objects and dative verbs

The dative marks the indirect object — the person to or for whom something happens: Ich gebe dem Kind das Buch. ("I give the book to the child.")

A group of verbs takes a dative object even where English feels like a direct object. Learn them as a set:

  • helfen (to help) · danken (to thank) · folgen (to follow)
  • gefallen (to please) · gehören (to belong to) · antworten (to answer)

Watch out: Das Buch gehört dem Mann. — "gehören" needs the dative (dem), never the accusative.

Prepositions fix the case

Every preposition forces a fixed case on the noun after it. You will drill these fully in cluster 6, but meet the families now:

  • Accusative preps: durch, für, gegen, ohne, um — für den Mann.
  • Dative preps: aus, bei, mit, nach, seit, von, zu — mit dem Bus.
  • Genitive preps: während, trotz, wegen, statt — trotz des Regens.

Learn the group, not the guess: once you know mit is dative, mit dem/der/dem follows automatically.

ein-words

ein, kein and the possessives

The ein-words — ein/eine, kein ("no/not a") and the possessives mein, dein, sein, ihr, unser, euer — take the same endings. They have no ending in three slots (masc. nom., neut. nom./acc.):

Nominativeineineein
Akkusativeineneineein
Dativeinemeinereinem
Genitiveineseinereines

Same skeleton: mein Bruder → meinen Bruder (acc.) → meinem Bruder (dat.). The endings mirror der/die/das minus the leading d.

The genitive

Whose? Possession and the noun ending

The genitive answers wessen? (whose?). In modern German it follows the noun it depends on:

  • das Haus der Frau — the woman's house (feminine → der)
  • der Titel des Buches — the book's title (neuter → des + noun ending)

Noun endings: masculine and neuter nouns add -s or -es in the genitive (des Autos, des Kindes). Feminine and plural nouns add nothing (der Frau, der Kinder).

Recap

Your case-and-article toolkit

The four cases: Nominativ (subject), Akkusativ (direct object), Dativ (indirect object), Genitiv (possession).

Definite article: der/die/das/die → only masculine changes in the accusative (den); dative dem/der/dem/den; genitive des/der/des/der.

ein-words: ein/kein/mein… take the same endings, with no ending in masc. nom. and neut. nom./acc.

Dative verbs: helfen, danken, folgen, gefallen, gehören, antworten. Genitive nouns (m./n.) add -s/-es.
