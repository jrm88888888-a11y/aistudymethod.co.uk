Mission 1

Mission 1: Why must a country's total spending, total income and total output always be equal?

Make a guess: Why must a country's total spending, total income and total output always be equal?

Mission 1

Households and firms

  • The simplest model has two groups:
    • Households: own the factors of production (land, labour, capital, enterprise) and buy goods and services.
    • Firms: hire factors and produce goods and services.
  • Two flows go round in opposite directions:
    • Real flow: factor services → firms; goods and services → households.
    • Money flow: factor incomes (wages, rent, interest, profit) → households; spending → firms.
  • Assumptions of this simple model: no saving, no government, no trade. Households spend all their income.
Mission 1

Income = output = expenditure

  • Illustrative data. In one year, firms produce final goods worth 500 billion.
  • Households buy them: expenditure = 500 billion.
  • Firms pay out what they receive:
    • wages 320 + rent 40 + interest 30 = 390 billion
    • the rest is profit: 500 − 390 = 110 billion, income to the firms' owners.
  • Income = 390 + 110 = 500 billion.
  • Because profit is whatever is left, total income must equal the value of output.
  • And every unit spent is someone's income. So:
    • national output = national income = national expenditure
  • Three ways to measure national income: output (value added), income (factor incomes), expenditure (spending on final output).
  • In real data, the three measures differ a little, because of measurement errors (a "statistical discrepancy").

Mission 1

Withdrawals and injections

  • Withdrawals (also called leakages): income not passed on as spending on domestic output.
  • Injections: spending on domestic output that does not come from households' consumption.
    • Investment (I) by firms, Government spending (G), Exports (X).
  • Withdrawals shrink the flow; injections enlarge it.
  • Say: "investment is spending by firms on capital goods" — not: buying shares, which is saving.
  • Say: "government spending (G) on goods and services" — not: transfer payments, which only move income between households.
Mission 1

The five sectors

  • Households: supply factors; spend, save, pay tax, buy imports.
  • Firms: produce; pay factor incomes; invest.
  • Government: collects taxes (T); buys goods and services (G); pays transfers.
  • Banks and the financial sector: take households' savings; lend to firms for investment.
  • Foreign sector: buys exports (X); sells imports (M).
  • Every sector depends on the others: one group's spending is another's income.
Quick check
?Which of these is an injection into the circular flow of Canada?
Mission 1

Equilibrium

  • National income is in equilibrium when total withdrawals (W) = total injections (J):
    • S + T + M = I + G + X
  • Injections > withdrawals: more is entering the flow than leaving → national income rises.
  • Withdrawals > injections: more is leaving than entering → national income falls.
  • As income changes, withdrawals change too (more income → more saving, tax, imports), until W = J again.
  • The pairs need not match: S can be above I, as long as the totals are equal.

Mission 1

Case: spring 2020

  • April 2020, United States, during COVID-19 lockdowns (first estimates):
    • Household spending fell 13.6% in one month.
    • Households saved 33% of disposable income (12.7% in March).
  • More saving = a bigger withdrawal. Less spending = less income for firms and their workers.
  • Personal income actually rose 10.5%, mainly from government benefit payments: a reminder that transfers move income but are not G.
  • Many countries saw the same pattern: saving up, spending and output down.
Mission 1

Nominal or real? Income or wealth?

  • Income is a flow: received over a period (per year).
  • Wealth is a stock: assets owned at a point in time (houses, shares, savings).
  • Saving adds to wealth; wealth earns income (rent, interest, dividends).

Income and output are the same flow seen from two sides. Profit is the balancing item, so total income always equals the value of output.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How can you sum up who gets what in a whole country with a single number?

Make a guess: How can you sum up who gets what in a whole country with a single number?

Mission 2

Income is a flow, wealth is a stock

  • Income: a flow of money over a period, e.g. per year.
    • wages, rent, interest, dividends, profit, government benefits
  • Wealth: a stock of assets owned at a point in time, minus debts.
    • property, savings, shares, pension funds
  • They feed each other: wealth earns income (rent, interest, dividends); saved income builds wealth.
  • So inequality in wealth tends to create inequality in income, and the reverse.
  • Say: "wealth is a stock; income is a flow" — not: "wealthy" means "high income". A retired homeowner can be wealthy on a low income.
Quick check
?Which of these is a change in a household's wealth rather than its income?
Mission 2

The Lorenz curve

  • Line up everyone from poorest to richest.
  • Horizontal axis: cumulative % of population. Vertical axis: cumulative % of income (or wealth).
  • Line of equality: the 45° line. The bottom 20% get 20%, the bottom 40% get 40%, and so on.
  • The Lorenz curve shows the actual shares. It sags below the line of equality.
  • Read it: a point at (40, 15) means the poorest 40% receive 15% of income.
  • The further the curve from the line of equality, the more unequal the distribution.
Mission 2

The Gini coefficient

  • Gini coefficient = A ÷ (A + B).
    • A: area between the line of equality and the Lorenz curve.
    • B: area under the Lorenz curve.
  • 0 = perfect equality. 1 = perfect inequality (one person has everything). Often shown as 0–100.
  • UK disposable household income: Gini 32.9 on the 0–100 scale (financial year ending 2024, ONS).
  • Great Britain household wealth: Gini about 0.6, or 60 on the 0–100 scale (2018–2020, ONS). The richest 10% of households held 43% of all wealth; the poorest half held 9%.
  • World Bank Gini estimates (0–100 scale): South Africa 63.0 (2014); Brazil 48.9 (2020); Slovak Republic 23.2 (2019).
  • Countries survey in different years, so compare with care.
  • Limits: two different Lorenz curves can give the same Gini; a Gini does not show where the inequality lies, or how rich the country is.

Mission 2

Why is income unequal?

  • Human capital: education and skills raise productivity and pay.
  • Ownership of wealth: land, property and shares earn rent, interest and dividends; inheritance passes them on.
  • Inequality of opportunity: family background affects schooling, contacts and health.
  • Discrimination by gender, ethnicity and others.
  • Power: bargaining power of unions, employers with monopsony power, top executives.
  • Tax and benefit policy: progressive taxes and benefits narrow gaps; regressive taxes widen them.
  • Globalisation and technological change: raise the pay of scarce skills; some routine jobs move abroad or are automated.
Mission 2

Between countries, and capitalism

  • Between countries: differences in capital per worker, technology, education, health, institutions and the terms of trade.
  • Capitalism and inequality:
    • markets reward those who own capital and scarce skills; returns on wealth can be reinvested, so wealth can concentrate
    • Thomas Piketty (2014) argued that when the return on capital exceeds the growth rate, wealth inequality tends to rise
    • other economists dispute this, pointing to falling returns as capital builds up and to taxes on capital
    • the same rewards create incentives to work, invest and innovate, which can raise growth and incomes for most people
  • Simon Kuznets (1955) suggested inequality first rises, then falls, as countries develop; evidence since is mixed.
Mission 2Evaluate

How much inequality is a problem?

It depends on:

  • Values. Fairness is a normative judgement; people disagree on how equal outcomes should be.
  • Causes. Inequality from effort and skill is viewed differently from inequality from inheritance, luck or discrimination.
  • Mobility. Wide gaps matter more if children of poor families stay poor.
  • Living standards of the poorest. Rising inequality with rising absolute incomes differs from falling incomes at the bottom.
  • Measurement. Income or wealth? Before or after taxes and benefits? Wealth is distributed far more unequally than income.
Mission check

?In country P, the Gini coefficient for household income is 0.30 and for household wealth is 0.65. (Illustrative data.) What can you conclude?

A Gini coefficient measures how evenly something is shared, not how much there is: here wealth is shared less evenly than income.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why can prices and unemployment rise at the same time?

Make a guess: Why can prices and unemployment rise at the same time?

Mission 3

Where AD meets AS

  • Macroeconomic equilibrium (short run): where AD = SRAS.
  • It sets two things at once: the price level and real output (real GDP).
  • Above it: planned output > planned spending. Unsold stock piles up, so firms cut output and prices.
  • Below it: spending > output. Stocks run down, so firms raise output and prices.
  • A change in the price level → a movement along AD or SRAS.
  • A change in anything else (confidence, interest rates, costs) → a shift of the curve.
  • Say: "an extension of SRAS" for a movement up the curve — not: "SRAS increases".
Mission 3

Long-run equilibrium

  • LRAS is vertical at full-employment (potential) output, Yfe.
  • Long-run equilibrium: AD and SRAS cross on LRAS.
  • There, unemployment is at its natural rate: no cyclical unemployment, but some frictional and structural unemployment.
  • Inflation is stable: no pressure from too much or too little spending.
  • In the short run, equilibrium can sit left or right of LRAS. Then the economy has an output gap.
Mission 3

The Keynesian picture

  • Keynesian AS is reverse-L shaped. Three sections:
    • flat: lots of spare capacity; more output with no rise in the price level
    • rising: bottlenecks appear; output and prices both rise
    • vertical at full-employment output: full capacity; only prices can rise
  • Label full-employment output Yfe.
  • Equilibrium is where AD crosses AS, and it can sit on the flat section, far below full-employment output.
  • Same idea: an equilibrium with spare capacity and high unemployment that need not correct itself.
Quick check
?A rise in consumer confidence shifts AD to the right. In the diagram, the price level rises from P1 to P2. In the short run, what happens to aggregate supply?
Mission 3

Demand shocks

  • Demand shock: a sudden, unexpected shift of AD.
  • Negative: AD shifts left. Examples: a collapse in confidence, a credit crunch, a world recession cutting exports.
    • Price level falls (or rises more slowly), real output falls, unemployment rises.
  • Positive: AD shifts right. Examples: an export boom, a burst of confidence, a sudden rise in government spending.
    • Price level rises, real output rises, unemployment falls.
  • Key pattern: after a demand shock, prices and output move in the same direction.
  • Real world: in 2008–09 banks cut lending and confidence collapsed. Real GDP fell in 2009 in the US, the UK, the euro area and Japan.

Mission 3

Supply shocks

  • Supply shock: a sudden, unexpected shift of SRAS, usually from a change in costs.
  • Negative: SRAS shifts left. Examples: a jump in oil or gas prices, a poor harvest, a jump in import prices.
    • Price level rises, real output falls, unemployment rises.
    • Rising prices and rising unemployment: stagflation.
  • Positive: SRAS shifts right (e.g. a fall in world commodity prices).
    • Price level falls, real output rises, unemployment falls.
  • Key pattern: after a supply shock, prices and output move in opposite directions.
  • Some shocks hit capacity itself: war or a natural disaster destroys capital, so LRAS shifts left too.
  • Real world: in 1973–74 oil exporters cut supply and the oil price roughly quadrupled. Many rich countries had rising inflation and rising unemployment.
  • In 2022 energy prices jumped after Russia's invasion of Ukraine. UK CPI inflation reached 11.1% in October 2022 (ONS).

  • Takeaway: a negative supply shock brings stagflation: higher prices and lower output at the same time.
Mission 3

Output gaps

  • Output gap: the difference between actual real output and potential (full-employment) output.
  • Negative output gap: output below Yfe. Spare capacity; unemployment above the natural rate.
  • Positive output gap: output above Yfe. Firms run beyond normal capacity (overtime, deferred maintenance); unemployment below the natural rate; inflation pressure.
  • The unemployment caused by a shortfall in AD is cyclical (demand-deficient) unemployment.
  • Output gap (%) = (actual − potential) ÷ potential × 100.
  • Say: "output below potential is a negative gap", even if output is still growing.

Mission 3

AD/AS, growth and the PPC

  • Short-run growth: AD shifts right into spare capacity. Output rises towards Yfe; little upward pressure on prices.
  • Once there is no spare capacity, more AD mainly raises the price level.
  • Long-run growth: LRAS shifts right (more or better workers and capital, new technology). Output can rise without inflation pressure.
  • On a PPC (production possibility curve):
    • a negative output gap is a point inside the curve
    • long-run growth shifts the PPC outward, just as it shifts LRAS right
  • A positive output gap means resources are used faster than is sustainable: it cannot last.
Mission 3Evaluate

How big is the effect of a shock?

It depends on:

  • Where the economy starts. With spare capacity, a rise in AD mostly raises output. Near full capacity, it mostly raises prices.
  • Size and length of the shock. A short, one-off oil price spike differs from a lasting rise.
  • Time. Short-run effects can fade as wages and prices adjust.
  • Policy response. Central banks and governments may offset a demand shock; a supply shock is harder.
Mission check

?An economy starts in long-run equilibrium. The world prices of the raw materials it imports fall sharply and stay low; nothing else changes at first. Using AD, SRAS and LRAS, what happens in the short run?

Supply shocks move prices and output in opposite directions, the good way as well as the bad.

Mission 4

Mission 4: If a government spends 1 billion, why can the country's income end up growing by much more?

Make a guess: If a government spends 1 billion, why can the country's income end up growing by much more?

Mission 4

One injection, many rounds

  • The government spends 100 (million) on a new bridge. An injection.
  • Builders earn 100. They spend some of it and withdraw the rest (saving, tax, imports).
  • Illustrative data: 80% of each extra unit of income is spent on domestic output.
  • Round 1: 100 → round 2: 80 → round 3: 64 → round 4: 51.2 → …
  • Each person's spending is someone else's income.
  • The rounds add up to 500: national income rises by 5 times the injection.
  • Multiplier effect: a change in an injection causes a larger final change in national income.
  • It works in reverse too: a cut in an injection reduces income by more than the cut.
Mission 4Graph lab

Re-spend more

  • Takeaway: the more of each round is re-spent, the bigger the multiplier.
Mission 4

The multiplier formula

  • Marginal propensity to consume (MPC): the share of each extra unit of income that is spent. MPC = ΔC ÷ ΔY. On the first screen of this mission it was 0.8.
  • Marginal propensity to save (MPS): the share of extra income saved.
  • Multiplier (k) = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC).
  • Closed economy, no government: 1 − MPC = MPS, so k = 1 ÷ MPS.
  • Final change in national income = k × change in injection.
  • Example: MPC = 0.8. k = 1 ÷ 0.2 = 5. A rise of 100 in investment → national income rises by 500.
  • Say: "real national income rises by k times the injection" — not: "the injection is multiplied at once". It takes many rounds.
Quick check
?In a closed economy with no government, the MPC is 0.75. Investment rises by 40 billion. If there is spare capacity, by how much does national income finally rise?
Mission 4

Saving, tax and imports: MPW

  • Out of each extra unit of national income, three withdrawals: saving, tax, imports.
  • MPS, MPT, MPM: the shares of extra income saved, paid in tax and spent on imports (marginal propensities to save, tax and import).
  • MPW = MPS + MPT + MPM. Here MPC = the share spent on domestic output, so MPC + MPW = 1.
  • k = 1 ÷ MPW = 1 ÷ (MPS + MPT + MPM).
  • Illustrative data: MPS = 0.1, MPT = 0.2, MPM = 0.2.
EconomyWithdrawalsk
Closed, no governmentMPS = 0.11 ÷ 0.1 = 10
Closed, with government0.1 + 0.2 = 0.31 ÷ 0.3 = 3.33

  • More withdrawals → a smaller multiplier.
Quick check
?An open economy has MPS = 0.15, MPT = 0.25 and MPM = 0.1 (illustrative data). Government spending rises by 20 billion. By how much does national income finally rise?
Mission 4

The multiplier on the AD/AS diagram

  • Step 1: an injection rises (say G). AD shifts right by the first-round amount: AD1 → AD2.
  • Step 2: the multiplier adds further rounds: AD2 → AD3.
  • Step 3: with upward-sloping AS, part of the extra demand raises the price level. Real output rises by less than the full shift.
  • Near full capacity, most of the effect is on prices, not real output.
Mission 4

How big in reality?

  • Real-world multipliers are estimated, not observed. Estimates vary widely.
  • IMF (2012): forecasts for 2010–11 had assumed fiscal multipliers of about 0.5. Its own estimate for the years after the 2008 crisis was higher: about 0.9 to 1.7.
  • Multipliers tend to be larger in deep recessions with spare capacity.
  • They tend to be smaller near full capacity, in very open economies, or if interest rates rise in response.
  • Economists still disagree on the size: it matters for debates on stimulus and austerity.
Mission 4Evaluate

How strong is the multiplier?

It depends on:

  • Spare capacity: near full capacity, extra demand mostly raises prices.
  • Withdrawals: the more of each round that is saved, taxed or spent on imports, the smaller the multiplier.
  • Confidence: worried households may save extra income.
  • Time lags: the rounds take months to work through.
  • Size: a small injection in a large economy barely shows.
Quick check
?A large car factory closes, and investment in the region falls sharply. What happens to national income, other things equal?
Mission check

?Two economies get the same rise in government spending. In Economy X, households spend much of any extra income on imports. In Economy Y, they buy mostly home-produced goods. Saving and tax rates are the same. Which statement is correct?

The more of each round that is withdrawn (saving, tax, imports), the smaller the multiplier.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Spending by one sector is income to another, so national output = national income = national expenditure.
  • Withdrawals (S, T, M) leave the flow; injections (I, G, X) enter it. Equilibrium: S + T + M = I + G + X.

Mission 2

  • Income is a flow; wealth is a stock. Wealth is usually distributed more unequally than income.
  • The Lorenz curve shows cumulative shares; the further it sags from the line of equality, the higher the Gini coefficient (0 = perfect equality, 1 = perfect inequality).
  • Causes include human capital, wealth ownership, discrimination, power, tax and benefits, and technology.

Mission 3

  • Short-run equilibrium: AD = SRAS. Long-run equilibrium: on LRAS, at full employment and the natural rate of unemployment.
  • Demand shocks move the price level and real output the same way; supply shocks move them opposite ways (stagflation).
  • Output below Yfe is a negative output gap; above it, a positive output gap.

Mission 4

  • An injection raises national income by more than itself, through rounds of spending.
  • k = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC).
  • With saving, tax and imports: k = 1 ÷ MPW.
