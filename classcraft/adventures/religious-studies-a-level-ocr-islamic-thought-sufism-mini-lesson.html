Mission 1

What Sufism is

  • Sufism (Arabic tasawwuf): the inward, mystical dimension of Islam. Its goal is to know and love God directly, not only to obey.
  • A Sufi seeks to purify the heart, remember God constantly and draw near to Him.
  • Most Sufis have been Sunni Muslims who follow a law school (madhhab) and pray the five daily prayers; some are Shi'a.
  • The name probably comes from suf, the coarse wool early ascetics wore. Other suggested roots: safa' (purity) and the ahl al-suffa, poor Companions who lived at the Prophet's mosque.
  • "Sufism" is a European coinage of about 1800; Sufis usually speak of the path (tariqa) or of ihsan.
Mission 1

Roots in the Qur'an and the Prophet's life

  • Sufis see their path as rooted in Islam's own sources, not borrowed.
  • Qur'an: God is close ("We are nearer to him than his jugular vein", Qur'an 50:16, Pickthall); God is to be remembered much (33:41).
  • The Prophet's practice: his retreats in the cave of Hira, his long night prayers, and the Night Journey and Ascension (isra' and mi'raj) are read as models of inner devotion.
  • Hadith: the Gabriel hadith defines ihsan: worshipping God as if you see Him (paraphrase; Sahih Muslim, Book of Faith).
  • Early Western scholars looked for outside sources: Christian monks, Neoplatonist philosophy, Indian ascetics. Most specialists now see some contact but stress Qur'anic roots (Louis Massignon, 1883–1962, argued this from the vocabulary of early Sufis).

Mission 1

Why a more personal faith developed

  • Wealth and empire: under the Umayyads (661–750) the Muslim state grew rich. Some pious Muslims felt luxury was choking faith and turned to renunciation (zuhd).
  • Civil wars (fitna) among Muslims left many wary of power and politics.
  • Law and theology were becoming technical sciences. Some looked for the inner life that rules and debates could not supply.
  • Early figures:
  • Hasan al-Basri (642–728), Basra: fear of God, sorrow for sin, distrust of the world.
  • Rabi'a al-'Adawiyya (d. 801), Basra: worship from love of God alone, not from fear of Hell or hope of Paradise (her prayers survive only in later sources).
  • The movement shifted from fear to love and from renunciation to knowledge of God (ma'rifa).
Mission 1

Baghdad, sobriety and synthesis

  • Al-Junayd of Baghdad (d. 910): taught a sober Sufism, in which the mystic returns from experience of God to ordinary life under the law.
  • Al-Hallaj (c. 858–922): his ecstatic saying "I am the Real" (Ana al-Haqq) and political suspicion led to his execution in Baghdad in 922 (see Mission 3).
  • Manuals showed Sufism inside Sunni orthodoxy: al-Sarraj (d. 988), Kitab al-Luma'; al-Qushayri (d. 1072), Risala (1045).
  • Al-Ghazali (1058–1111), The Revival of the Religious Sciences (Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din): a leading Sunni jurist and theologian who found certainty on the Sufi path and argued that law, theology and Sufism belong together.
  • After al-Ghazali, sober Sufism was widely accepted as part of Sunni learning.
Mission 1

The orders and the spread

  • From the 12th century, orders (tariqa, plural turuq) formed around a founder and a chain of teachers (silsila):
  • Qadiriyya: Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani (1077–1166), Baghdad.
  • Chishtiyya: Mu'in al-Din Chishti (d. 1236), Ajmer, India.
  • Mevleviyya: followers of Jalal al-Din Rumi (1207–1273), Konya, Anatolia.
  • Naqshbandiyya: Baha' al-Din Naqshband (1318–1389), Bukhara, Central Asia.
  • Shadhiliyya: Abu'l-Hasan al-Shadhili (d. 1258), North Africa and Egypt.
  • Sufis travelled with traders and armies to South Asia, Central Asia, Anatolia, West Africa and Southeast Asia. Many historians credit them with much conversion there; others stress rulers and trade. Usually all three worked together.

Mission 1Diversity

Sunni responses

  • Integration: most Sunni scholars, after al-Qushayri and al-Ghazali, accepted sober Sufism; many jurists were themselves Sufis.
  • Ibn Taymiyya (1263–1328): attacked the idea of the "oneness of being" linked with Ibn 'Arabi, and asking saints at shrines for help. He respected early Sufis and the ideal of purifying the heart.
  • Muhammad ibn 'Abd al-Wahhab (1703–1792) and later Salafi reformers: saw shrine veneration and appeals to saints as shirk (associating others with God) and many practices as bid'a (blameworthy innovation).
  • South Asia: Deobandis (from 1867) kept Sufi initiation but criticised shrine customs; Barelwis defend saint-veneration and celebrate the Prophet's birthday.
  • Modernist states: Turkey closed the orders and lodges in 1925 as obstacles to a modern secular nation.
Mission 1Diversity

Shi'a responses

  • Shared ground: most Sufi chains trace back to the Prophet through 'Ali; Shi'a and Sufis both speak of walaya (closeness to God, spiritual authority) and of an inner meaning of the Qur'an.
  • Safavid Iran: the dynasty began as the Safaviyya Sufi order; after 1501 it made Twelver Shi'ism the state faith. Later Safavid scholars, notably Muhammad Baqir Majlisi (d. 1699), opposed the orders.
  • Why the tension: for Twelver Shi'a the Imam is the true spiritual guide. A Sufi shaykh's authority can seem to compete with the Imams and the scholars (mujtahids).
  • Today: Shi'a orders exist (e.g. the Ni'matullahi); many Shi'a scholars prefer 'irfan (mystical knowledge, studied in seminaries) to the orders.
Mission 1

Scholars on Sufism's place

  • Carl W. Ernst, The Shambhala Guide to Sufism (1997), ch. 1: European scholars coined "Sufism" and often cut it off from Islam; some modern Muslim critics accept the same split from the other side (paraphrase).
  • Common ground: Sufis themselves claim to be Muslims living Islam fully, not members of a separate religion.
Mission 1Evaluate

Is Sufism a separate tradition?

  • Claim: Sufism is a separate tradition within Islam, like Sunni and Shi'a.
  • For: distinct institutions (orders, lodges, shrines), leaders (shaykhs, saints), rituals (dhikr gatherings, sama') and texts.
  • For: Salafi critics and secular states have treated it as a distinct, even rival, form of religion.
  • Against: it has no separate creed, law or line of succession; Sufis are Sunni or Shi'a and follow a law school.
  • Against: "Sufism" is partly an outside label for what Muslims call ihsan or tasawwuf.
  • Scholar: Ernst shows that European scholars coined "Sufism" and often cut it off from Islam.
  • Judgement criteria: What makes a "tradition" (creed? law? institutions?)? Whose view counts: Sufis, critics or historians? Does the answer differ by time and place?

Mission 2

Ihsan: worship as if you see God

  • The Gabriel hadith (Sahih Muslim, Book of Faith, from 'Umar; also Sahih al-Bukhari, Book of Faith): a stranger in white questions the Prophet; the Prophet later says it was the angel Gabriel, come to teach the religion.
  • Three levels (paraphrase):
  • Islam: the five pillars (outward practice).
  • Iman: faith in God, angels, books, messengers, the Last Day and God's decree.
  • Ihsan: "to worship God as though you see Him; and if you do not see Him, He sees you."
  • Sufis take ihsan (doing what is beautiful; spiritual excellence) as their goal: the same religion, lived with full awareness of God.
  • Spec: "the concept of ihsan (spiritual perfection) in the Gabriel hadith".
Mission 2

Purifying the self

  • Tazkiyat al-nafs: purifying (and growing) the self (nafs), the part of us that wants and grasps.
  • Sufis map three states of the nafs from Qur'anic phrases:
  • The soul that commands evil (al-nafs al-ammara, 12:53).
  • The self-reproaching soul (al-nafs al-lawwama, 75:2): conscience awake.
  • The soul at peace (al-nafs al-mutma'inna, 89:27): "But ah! thou soul at peace!" (Qur'an 89:27, Pickthall)
  • The struggle against the ego is often called the greater jihad (a saying attributed to the Prophet; many hadith scholars judge its chain weak).
  • "He is indeed successful who causeth it to grow, And he is indeed a failure who stunteth it." (Qur'an 91:9–10, Pickthall). Yusuf Ali has "purifies it": tazkiya means both.
Mission 2

The role of asceticism

  • Renunciation (zuhd): letting go of attachment to wealth, status and comfort, not of the world itself.
  • Practices: extra fasting, night vigils, simple food and clothes, retreat (khalwa; the Chishti forty-day chilla).
  • Poverty (faqr): Sufis are often called fuqara' (the poor); the aim is to need God alone.
  • Limits: most Sufis reject lifelong celibacy and withdrawal; the Qur'an says monasticism was "invented", not ordained (57:27). The Prophet married, traded and led a community.
  • So asceticism is a training stage: it weakens the grasping nafs so the heart can turn to God.

Mission 2

Dhikr and muraqaba

  • Dhikr (remembrance): repeating God's names or short formulas, e.g. la ilaha illa'llah (no god but God) or Allah, often counted on prayer beads.
  • Aloud (jahri), often in a group circle (Qadiriyya); or silent (khafi), in the heart (Naqshbandiyya).
  • A litany (wird) is a daily set of prayers and formulas given by the shaykh.
  • Goal: to move remembrance from the tongue to the heart, until the whole person is aware of God.

Mission 2

Master and disciple

  • The shaykh (also pir, murshid: guide) trains the disciple (murid: one who wills, seeks).
  • The disciple gives an oath of allegiance (bay'a), often by clasping hands, and joins the shaykh's chain (silsila) back to the Prophet.
  • The shaykh knows the soul's illnesses and prescribes: fasting, a litany, service, silence. Teaching comes through companionship (suhba) as much as words.
  • Qur'anic model: Moses asks a servant of God (named Khidr in tradition) to teach him: "May I follow thee, to the end that thou mayst teach me right conduct of that which thou hast been taught?" (Qur'an 18:66, Pickthall). The lesson is patience with what one does not yet understand.
Mission 2

The tariqa: path and order

  • Tariqa means path: the inner way to God, beside the shari'a (the broad road of law).
  • A classic image: the shari'a is the boat, the tariqa the sea, and haqiqa (the Reality) the pearl. Without the boat, no one reaches the pearl.
  • Tariqa also means an order: a community sharing a founder, a silsila, a litany and meetings (e.g. a weekly dhikr circle).
  • The path is described as stations to be earned (repentance, renunciation, patience, trust, contentment) and states given by God.
  • So "tariqa" names both the journey and the school that trains people for it.

Mission 2

Sama': spiritual listening

  • Sama' ("listening"): hearing poetry, chant or music to stir love and longing for God.
  • Mevlevi ceremony (the "whirling dervishes", Konya): the reed flute (ney) plays; dervishes turn, right palm up to receive from God, left palm down to pass it to the earth.
  • Their tall hat stands for a tombstone, the white skirt for a shroud: turning is dying to self.
  • Chishti gatherings in South Asia use qawwali (sung devotional poetry).
  • Listening can bring wajd (ecstasy), a state in which the listener is overcome.
  • Debate: many jurists forbid musical instruments; the Naqshbandiyya avoid music; al-Ghazali allowed sama' with conditions of time, place and company (Ihya', Book of Listening).
Mission 2Read the source

Rumi's Song of the Reed

Jalal al-Din Rumi (1207–1273), Masnavi, Book I, opening lines (paraphrase):

  • Listen to the reed flute, telling of separation.
  • Since I was cut from the reed-bed, my cry has made men and women weep.
  • Whoever is far from their source longs to return to the time of union.
  • Its sound is fire, not wind: the fire of love.
  • Meaning (as Sufis read it): the reed is the soul; the reed-bed is its origin with God; the lament is longing; the path is the journey home.
  • William Chittick, The Sufi Path of Love (1983), Part III, reads Rumi's whole path as love drawing the soul back to God (paraphrase).

Mission 2Evaluate

Is a master necessary?

  • Claim: spiritual training under a Sufi master is necessary to reach ihsan.
  • For: the nafs deceives itself; a guide sees what the seeker cannot (Moses and Khidr, 18:65–82). A classical saying, attributed to Abu Yazid al-Bistami: whoever has no shaykh has Satan for a guide (paraphrase).
  • For: the chain (silsila) keeps teaching tested and tied to the Prophet.
  • Against: the Qur'an and Sunna are open to all; Muslims need no mediator between themselves and God.
  • Against: obedience to a shaykh can be abused; Shi'a may see the Imam, and Salafis the scholars, as the proper guides.
  • Judgement criteria: Is ihsan a skill learned by apprenticeship, or a gift open to every believer? What safeguards keep a master honest?

Mission 3

God beyond comparison

  • Sufis begin from Tawhid: God is One, and nothing is like Him.
  • Incomparable (tanzih): "Naught is as His likeness; and He is the Hearer, the Seer." (Qur'an 42:11, Pickthall)
  • God's essence (dhat) is unknowable in Himself: no mind can grasp it.
  • Yet the same verse also names God as Hearer and Seer: God is known through His names and signs in creation and the self (41:53).
  • Ibn 'Arabi (1165–1240) taught that both are needed: incomparability (tanzih) and likeness (tashbih) together give true knowledge of God.
Mission 3

Creation as overflowing light; God within

  • A saying Sufis love, "I was a hidden treasure and loved to be known, so I created creation" (paraphrase; a hadith qudsi not found in the main collections).
  • So creation is God's self-disclosure (tajalli): God's names shown in the world, like light shining from the sun.
  • Emanation: creation flows from God as light overflows from its source. The idea came through Muslim philosophers (al-Farabi, Ibn Sina) who drew on Neoplatonism. Theologians, al-Ghazali among them, insisted that creation is God's free act, not a necessary overflow.
  • Things have no being of their own: they borrow it from God, moment by moment.
  • Immanent: "We are nearer to him than his jugular vein" (Qur'an 50:16, Pickthall); "whithersoever ye turn, there is Allah's Countenance" (Qur'an 2:115, Pickthall).
  • Debate: Ibn 'Arabi's followers spoke of the "oneness of being" (wahdat al-wujud). Critics such as Ibn Taymiyya called this blurring God and the world; Ahmad Sirhindi (1564–1624) taught instead the "oneness of witnessing" (wahdat al-shuhud): the oneness is in the mystic's vision, not in being.

Mission 3Diversity

Reading the Light

  • Early commentary (attributed to Ibn 'Abbas and Ubayy ibn Ka'b): God is the guide of all in heaven and earth; the lamp is the light of faith in the believer's heart.
  • Al-Ghazali, The Niche of Lights (Mishkat al-Anwar, c. 1106): God is the only true Light; every other light is borrowed. He maps the images onto five levels of the human spirit: niche = senses; glass = imagination; lamp = intellect; tree = reflective reason; oil = the holy prophetic spirit, which "almost glows" by itself.
  • Many Sufi commentators: niche = the breast; glass = the purified heart; lamp = the inmost secret where God's light shines; oil = the natural disposition to know God (fitra).
  • "Light upon light": for Sufis, the light of revelation meeting the light of a purified heart.

Mission 3

Fana: passing away in God

  • Fana' ("passing away", annihilation): the self's awareness of itself dissolves, until only God is in view.
  • Qur'anic root: "Everyone that is thereon will pass away; There remaineth but the Countenance of thy Lord" (Qur'an 55:26–27, Pickthall).
  • A hadith qudsi (Sahih al-Bukhari, Book of Softening of Hearts, no. 6502): God draws near to the servant who draws near through extra worship, until God becomes "the hearing with which he hears, the sight with which he sees" (paraphrase).
  • What fana is not: the soul does not become God (ittihad), and God does not move into a body (hulul). Mainstream Sufis rejected both.
  • Sober Sufis such as al-Junayd of Baghdad (d. 910) stress what follows: after fana comes baqa' ("remaining"): the self returns, transformed, to live in God and keep the law.
Mission 3

Drunken Sufism: ecstatic sayings

  • Drunken (sukr, intoxication) Sufism stresses being overwhelmed by God in fana.
  • In that state some spoke shatahat (ecstatic utterances) that sound like claims to divinity:
  • Abu Yazid al-Bistami (d. c. 874): "Glory be to me! How great is my majesty!" (paraphrase of the Arabic subhani).
  • Al-Hallaj (c. 858–922): "I am the Real" (Ana al-Haqq; al-Haqq is a name of God). Executed in Baghdad in 922: in Sufi tradition, for these words; historians also point to political charges.
  • Readings: (1) blasphemy, as his accusers and later critics held; (2) the human "I" had passed away, so God was speaking: as glowing iron in a fire might say "I am fire" (a classic Sufi image); (3) words of the drunk: excused, but not to be repeated (al-Junayd, al-Ghazali).
Mission 3

Sober Sufism: baqa, states and stations

  • Al-Junayd of Baghdad (d. 910): after fana comes baqa' ("persistence", remaining): the self returns, transformed, to live in God and keep the law.
  • Sobriety (sahw) ranks above intoxication: the perfect Sufi lives in the world as the Prophet did.
  • Stations (maqamat): stages earned by effort and lasting. Al-Sarraj (d. 988), Kitab al-Luma', lists seven: repentance, scrupulousness, renunciation, poverty, patience, trust in God, contentment.
  • States (ahwal): gifts of God that come and go, e.g. nearness, love, fear, hope, longing, intimacy, certainty.
  • Al-Qushayri: stations are earnings; states are gifts.

Mission 3

Friends of God: wilaya

  • "Lo! verily the friends of Allah are (those) on whom fear (cometh) not, nor do they grieve?" (Qur'an 10:62, Pickthall)
  • Wali (plural awliya'): friend of God, one brought near; wilaya: that friendship and the authority it brings.
  • Al-Hakim al-Tirmidhi (d. c. 910) wrote the first full theory: a hierarchy of friends of God, and a "seal of the friends".
  • Friends of God may receive karamat (marvels, graces); prophets alone work mu'jizat (evidentiary miracles). Mainstream teaching: no friend of God is above a prophet.
  • Shrines of friends of God are visited for blessing (baraka); reformers object when visitors ask the dead for help (see Mission 1).
  • Shi'a Muslims speak of the walaya of the Imams; Sufis of the wilaya of the saints: related ideas, different holders.
Mission 3Evaluate

Can the soul be one with God without denying Tawhid?

  • Claim: Sufi teaching that the soul becomes one with God is compatible with Tawhid.
  • For: oneness in fana is of awareness, not essence; mainstream Sufis reject hulul and ittihad.
  • For: Qur'anic support: 50:16 (nearness), 55:26–27 (all passes but God's Face), the hadith qudsi of the servant God loves.
  • Against: sayings such as al-Hallaj's blur Creator and creature; his accusers and later critics (Ibn Taymiyya) judged them unbelief.
  • Against: "oneness of being" can seem to deny the real difference between God and the world.
  • Judgement criteria: Are Sufi words to be read literally or as language of experience? Who interprets: jurists, theologians, Sufi masters? Does sober Sufism answer the objection?

Mission 4

Knowledge as a duty

  • Hadith: seeking knowledge ('ilm) is an obligation on every Muslim (paraphrase; Sunan Ibn Majah, no. 224).
  • "O my Lord! advance me in knowledge." (Qur'an 20:114, Yusuf Ali)
  • Al-Ghazali (1058–1111), Revival of the Religious Sciences, Book of Knowledge: medicine and arithmetic are fard kifaya, a communal duty: someone in every community must master them, or all are at fault.
  • Khalifa (2:30): humans are trustees of the earth; understanding creation helps them care for it.
Mission 4

Worship needed science

  • Qibla: facing Makkah from Spain or India needs spherical trigonometry.
  • Prayer times: shift with the sun and season; mosque timekeepers (muwaqqit) built tables and instruments.
  • Calendar: a lunar calendar needs prediction of the new crescent (Ramadan, Hajj).
  • Inheritance: the Qur'an's shares (4:11–12) need careful arithmetic; al-Khwarizmi's algebra book includes inheritance problems.
  • So religious practice gave scholars real problems to solve.

Mission 4

The translation movement

  • Under the Abbasid caliphs (from 750), Baghdad became a centre of learning.
  • Greek, Persian, Syriac and Sanskrit works on medicine, mathematics, astronomy and philosophy were translated into Arabic (8th–10th centuries).
  • The House of Wisdom (Bayt al-Hikma), associated with caliph al-Ma'mun (r. 813–833), linked libraries and translators.
  • Translators included Christians such as Hunayn ibn Ishaq (809–873), translator of Galen; Jews and Sabians also took part.
  • Paper, learned from China, spread from Samarkand to Baghdad in the late 8th century and made books cheaper.
  • Scholars checked, criticised and extended the texts: Arabic became the international language of science.
Mission 4

Mathematics

  • Al-Khwarizmi (c. 780–c. 850), The Compendious Book on Calculation by Completion and Balancing (c. 820): systematic solving of equations. "Al-jabr" in the title gave us algebra; his name gave algorithm.
  • He also explained the Indian decimal numerals with zero; Europe later learned them in Arabic form (hence "Arabic numerals").
  • Trigonometry: sine and tangent tables developed for astronomy and the qibla (e.g. al-Battani, d. 929).
  • 'Umar al-Khayyam (1048–1131): geometric solutions of cubic equations.
Mission 4

Medicine

  • Hadith: God has not sent down a disease without sending down its cure (paraphrase; Sahih al-Bukhari, Book of Medicine, no. 5678). Seeking cures is part of trusting God.
  • Hospitals (bimaristan) in Baghdad, Damascus and Cairo treated patients free, with wards and teaching.
  • Al-Razi (c. 865–925): clinical case notes; first clear account distinguishing smallpox from measles.
  • Ibn Sina (980–1037), The Canon of Medicine (c. 1025): a systematic medical encyclopaedia, used in European universities for about five centuries.
  • Al-Zahrawi (c. 936–1013): surgery, with drawings of instruments.
  • Ibn al-Nafis (1213–1288): described the passage of blood through the lungs (pulmonary circulation).
Mission 4

Astronomy and optics

  • Observatories: Maragha (founded 1259) and Samarkand (1420s, under Ulugh Beg).
  • Nasir al-Din al-Tusi (1201–1274) and Ibn al-Shatir (1304–1375), timekeeper of the Umayyad Mosque in Damascus, built models of planetary motion that appear in mathematically similar form in Copernicus. How they reached him is still debated.
  • Al-Biruni (973–c. 1050): measured the radius of the Earth.
  • Ibn al-Haytham (c. 965–c. 1040), Book of Optics: sight happens when light enters the eye; he tested claims with controlled experiments, so he is often called a pioneer of experimental method.

Mission 4

Did Muslim science decline after al-Ghazali?

  • The old "decline" story (e.g. Ernest Renan, 1883): orthodoxy, led by al-Ghazali, killed science after the 11th century.
  • Historians now: major work came after al-Ghazali: Maragha (1259), Ibn al-Nafis, Ibn al-Shatir, Samarkand (1420s). George Saliba, Islamic Science and the Making of the European Renaissance (2007).
  • Ahmad Dallal, Islam, Science and the Challenge of History (2010), ch. 2–3: science in Islam was neither a mere copy of the Greeks nor hostile to religion; religion shaped which questions mattered (paraphrase).
  • Later slowdowns had many causes: the Mongol sack of Baghdad (1258), shifts in trade and wealth, late adoption of printing.

Mission 4Evaluate

Was the medieval achievement driven by Islam?

  • Claim: the medieval Muslim contribution to science shows that Islam itself encourages science.
  • For: the Qur'an calls nature "signs" and commands reflection; worship needed astronomy and mathematics; hadith on knowledge and cures.
  • For: many scientists were also religious scholars (Ibn al-Shatir a mosque timekeeper; Ibn al-Nafis wrote on law and hadith).
  • Against: much built on Greek, Indian and Persian learning; Christians, Jews and Sabians took part; courts, not mosques, paid for much of it.
  • Against: some scholars distrusted philosophy; science later slowed in the same societies.
  • Judgement criteria: Is "Islam" its texts, its scholars or its civilisation? Does motivation matter, or only results? What else explains the later slowdown?

Mission 5: Can a believer follow reason all the way, or does knowing God need more than reason?

Make a guess: Can a believer follow reason all the way, or does knowing God need more than reason?

Mission 5

Two thinkers, one question

  • Abu Hamid al-Ghazali (1058–1111), from Tus in Persia: jurist, theologian, later Sufi. Head teacher at the Nizamiyya college in Baghdad from 1091.
  • Ibn Rushd (Averroes, 1126–1198), from Córdoba in al-Andalus: judge (qadi), doctor and the great commentator on Aristotle.
  • The question: Greek philosophy (falsafa) had become part of Islamic learning. Should Muslims adopt it, and how far?
  • Al-Ghazali: use its tools, reject its errors, and look beyond reason to experience of God.
  • Ibn Rushd: philosophy, rightly done, is commanded by the law, and cannot contradict revelation.
Mission 5

The first crisis: doubt

  • Source: al-Ghazali's autobiography, al-Munqidh min al-Dalal (Deliverance from Error), written c. 1106–1110.
  • As a young scholar he asked: where does certain knowledge come from?
  • Senses deceive: a star looks the size of a coin; a shadow seems still but moves.
  • Reason might deceive too: in dreams we feel certain, then wake. Could waking life be another dream?
  • For nearly two months he was a sceptic in fact.
  • Cure: not an argument, but "a light which God Most High cast into the breast" (paraphrase): trust in basic reason returned as a gift.
  • Then he examined four groups who claimed truth: theologians, philosophers, the Ta'limis (followers of an infallible Imam) and the Sufis.
Mission 5

The second crisis: Sufism

  • Al-Ghazali studied Sufi books and saw that the path could not be learned by reading: it needed "taste" (dhawq), direct experience, like the difference between knowing the definition of drunkenness and being drunk.
  • He saw his own motive for teaching was fame, not God.
  • 1095: for six months he was torn; then God "locked his tongue" (paraphrase): he could not lecture or eat, and doctors could not help.
  • He left Baghdad, gave away his wealth and lived as a Sufi for about ten years (Damascus, Jerusalem, Hajj).
  • Conclusion: the Sufis are those who truly travel the path to God; certainty comes through experience and the light of prophecy, beyond reason (see Mission 2).
  • He returned to teach at Nishapur (1106) to revive religion.

Mission 5Read the source

Munqidh III.2: the sciences of the philosophers

Al-Ghazali, Deliverance from Error, III.2 (paraphrase; tr. W. Montgomery Watt, The Faith and Practice of al-Ghazali, 1953):

  • Three kinds of philosopher: materialists (deny a Creator), naturalists (deny the afterlife), theists (Socrates, Plato, Aristotle). Al-Farabi and Ibn Sina transmitted the theists' views.
  • Six sciences, judged one by one:
  • Mathematics and logic: true and neutral. Danger: people who admire their precision trust the philosophers on everything; people who reject them out of zeal make Islam look foolish.
  • Natural science: acceptable, if nature is seen as subject to God.
  • Metaphysics: here are most of their errors.
  • Politics and ethics: borrowed from prophets and Sufis; true in themselves, but mixed with error.
  • Rule: know the truth by itself, not by who said it (he cites a saying attributed to 'Ali).

Mission 5

Three points of unbelief

  • In The Incoherence of the Philosophers (1095) al-Ghazali listed twenty errors: three are unbelief (kufr), seventeen are innovation.
  • 1. The world is eternal: it denies that God created it in time.
  • 2. God knows only universals, not particular events: it denies God's knowledge of each person and act.
  • 3. Only souls, not bodies, are resurrected: it denies the Qur'an's bodily resurrection.
  • He repeats these three in the Munqidh as the points where philosophers must be judged unbelievers.

Mission 5

Ibn Rushd: the law commands philosophy

  • The Decisive Treatise (Fasl al-Maqal, c. 1180): a legal ruling on philosophy.
  • The Qur'an commands reflection on creation: "So learn a lesson, O ye who have eyes!" (Qur'an 59:2, Pickthall); "Have they not considered the dominion of the heavens and the earth" (Qur'an 7:185, Pickthall).
  • Reflection at its best is demonstration (burhan), the method of philosophy. So philosophy is obligatory or recommended for those able to do it.
  • Learning from non-Muslims (the Greeks) is like using a knife for a sacrifice: what matters is whether the tool is sound, not who made it.
  • Truth does not contradict truth: where scripture seems to clash with demonstration, scripture is to be interpreted (ta'wil).
  • George F. Hourani's translation, Averroes on the Harmony of Religion and Philosophy (1961), is the edition OCR suggests.
Mission 5

Three ways people reach truth

  • Ibn Rushd: people differ in nature, so the Qur'an speaks to all three:
  • Demonstrative (burhani): philosophers, by strict proof.
  • Dialectical (jadali): theologians, by argument from accepted premises.
  • Rhetorical (khatabi): most people, by persuasion, images and examples.
  • Each reaches the same truth in a form suited to them.
  • So interpretations should not be spread to the public: the theologians did harm by publishing debates that confused ordinary believers.
Mission 5

Ibn Rushd's reply to al-Ghazali

  • The Incoherence of the Incoherence (Tahafut al-Tahafut, c. 1180) answers al-Ghazali point by point; the Decisive Treatise gives the short replies below.
  • No consensus: on theoretical questions Muslim scholars never reached consensus (ijma'), so calling philosophers unbelievers is unjustified.
  • Eternity: the dispute is largely about names: the world is neither eternal in the way God is, nor created in time like things in it.
  • God's knowledge: philosophers do not deny God knows particulars; God's knowledge causes things and is unlike ours, neither "universal" nor "particular".
  • Resurrection: philosophers affirm it; they differ only on its manner, which is open to interpretation.

Mission 5Evaluate

Who better served Islam's relation to reason?

  • Claim: Ibn Rushd's defence of philosophy is more convincing than al-Ghazali's critique.
  • For Ibn Rushd: the Qur'an commands reflection; truth is one; his reply exposes al-Ghazali's charges as resting on no consensus.
  • For Ibn Rushd: in Latin Europe his commentaries fed the rise of university philosophy and science.
  • For al-Ghazali: reason has limits: certainty about God needs experience and prophecy; his own crisis shows this.
  • For al-Ghazali: he did not reject science: he defended mathematics and logic and brought logic into theology; his influence on Islamic thought far exceeded Ibn Rushd's.
  • Judgement criteria: Can reason judge revelation, or only serve it? Does three-tier truth treat ordinary believers fairly? Which view best explains the role of science and philosophy in later Islamic thought?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Sufism (tasawwuf) is the inward path of Islam; it grew from Qur'anic devotion and early renunciation into the orders that spread across the Muslim world from the 12th century.
  • Most Sunni scholars integrated sober Sufism after al-Ghazali; Ibn Taymiyya, Wahhabi and Salafi reformers attacked shrine practices and monism; Shi'a scholars share ideas of walaya but often distrust the orders.

Mission 2

  • Ihsan (worship as if you see God) is the goal; tazkiyat al-nafs (purifying the self) and remembrance (dhikr) are the means.
  • A shaykh trains a disciple within a tariqa (path and order), through bay'a, companionship, litanies and, for some orders, sama'.
  • Rumi's reed: the soul cut from its source longs to return; love drives the journey.

Mission 3

  • God is incomparable and unknowable in essence, yet shows Himself in His names and in creation; God is nearer than the jugular vein.
  • The verse of Light (24:35): for al-Ghazali, God alone is true Light; the heart, purified, receives it.
  • Fana' (passing away) and baqa' (remaining) describe the soul's oneness with God in awareness, not essence.
  • Drunken Sufism (al-Bistami, al-Hallaj, shatahat) vs sober Sufism (al-Junayd, baqa', stations earned and states given); wilaya: friendship of God.

Mission 4

  • The Qur'an calls creation "signs" and urges reflection (3:190, 88:17); hadith make seeking knowledge a duty; al-Ghazali made medicine and arithmetic communal duties.
  • Medieval Muslim scholars translated and extended Greek, Persian and Indian learning: algebra (al-Khwarizmi), medicine (al-Razi, Ibn Sina, Ibn al-Nafis), astronomy and optics (al-Tusi, Ibn al-Shatir, Ibn al-Haytham).
  • Historians now reject the idea that science stopped after al-Ghazali.

Mission 5

  • Al-Ghazali finds unbelief in three of the philosophers' claims: an eternal world, God knowing only universals, and resurrection of souls only.
  • Al-Ghazali: doubt cured by God's light; a second crisis led him to Sufism and to certainty through experience. In Munqidh III.2, he accepts mathematics and logic.
  • Ibn Rushd: the law commands philosophy; truth cannot contradict truth.
  • Ibn Rushd: the Qur'an speaks to demonstrative, dialectical and rhetorical minds; al-Ghazali's charges rest on no consensus.
