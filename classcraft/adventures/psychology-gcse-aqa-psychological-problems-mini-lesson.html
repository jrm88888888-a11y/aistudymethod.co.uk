Mission 1

Where does a bad week end and a mental health problem begin?

Make a guess: Where does a bad week end and a mental health problem begin?

Psychological problems · mental health

What good mental health looks like

  • Mental health is how we think, feel and cope. Everyone has it.
  • Good mental health includes positive engagement with society and coping effectively with challenges.
  • Everyone has bad days. A significant mental health problem is more severe, lasts longer and disrupts daily life.
  • A clinician makes a diagnosis using ICD (the International Classification of Diseases).
Psychological problems · mental health

How common, and is it rising?

  • About 1 in 5 adults in England (20.2%) had a common mental health condition, such as depression or anxiety, in 2023/4 (NHS England 2025).
  • Ages 16 to 64: up from 15.5% (1993) to 22.6% (2023/4).
  • About 1 in 5 people aged 8 to 25 had a probable mental disorder (NHS England 2023).
  • These show prevalence (cases at one time); incidence is new cases over a period.
Psychological problems · mental health

Why recorded rates change

  • Modern living brings extra challenges, eg isolation and loneliness.
  • Recognition: people, schools and doctors now spot problems more often, so more cases are recorded.
  • Less stigma: stigma means negative attitudes and stereotypes. As it lessens, more people ask for help and get diagnosed.
  • Culture: beliefs differ about what counts as a problem, what causes it (spiritual or medical) and whether to seek help.
Quick check
?A school survey finds that more students report anxiety than ten years ago. The head says, "Students must simply be more anxious now." What else could explain the rise?
Psychological problems · mental health

Effects on the person and on society

  • Individual: damage to relationships, difficulty coping with day-to-day life (school, work, looking after yourself) and harm to physical wellbeing.
  • Society: more need for social care and health services, and costs to the economy when people can't work.
  • Some problems are linked with higher crime rates, but most people with mental health problems never commit a crime.

Mission check

?Where does a bad week end and a mental health problem begin?

There is no sharp line: everyone has mental health, and everyone has bad weeks. A problem becomes significant when it is severe, lasts and gets in the way of relationships, daily life and physical health; a clinician decides, using a classification system such as ICD, and those systems and society's attitudes change over time.

Mission 2

Everyone feels sad sometimes. When do doctors call it depression?

Make a guess: Everyone feels sad sometimes. When do doctors call it depression?

Psychological problems · clinical depression

Sadness or depression?

  • Sadness is a normal emotion, usually triggered by an event such as a loss. It lifts.
  • Clinical depression is a mental health problem: persistent low mood with other symptoms that affect daily life, with or without an obvious trigger.
  • Unipolar depression: mood moves one way only, down.
  • Bipolar depression (bipolar disorder): periods of depression alternate with mania: high mood or irritability, high energy, racing thoughts, less need for sleep.
Psychological problems · clinical depression

The ICD symptoms of depression

  • Clinicians use ICD (the International Classification of Diseases), published by the World Health Organization (WHO).
  • Key features: low mood (sad, empty or irritable) or loss of interest or pleasure in activities.
  • Other symptoms include reduced energy, changes in sleep, changes in appetite or weight, decreased self-confidence or self-worth, excessive guilt, poor concentration and hopelessness about the future.
Psychological problems · clinical depression

Number, severity and time

  • Symptoms must be present most of the day, nearly every day, for at least two weeks.
  • The clinician judges the number of symptoms, how severe they are and how much they affect daily life.
  • Depression is then classed as mild, moderate or severe: more symptoms, and more severe ones, mean a more severe episode.
  • A clinician makes the diagnosis after talking with the person, never from a checklist alone.

?Everyone feels sad sometimes. When do doctors call it depression?

Sadness is a normal reaction that lifts. A clinician diagnoses depression using ICD when low mood or loss of interest comes with other symptoms, such as low energy, poor sleep, appetite change and low self-confidence, most of the day, nearly every day, for at least two weeks. The number and severity of symptoms decide whether it is mild, moderate or severe.

Mission 3

Why do the same hard times trigger depression in only some people?

Make a guess: Why do the same hard times trigger depression in only some people?

Psychological problems · biological explanations of depression

Looking inside the body: nature

  • A biological explanation says depression has a physical cause in the body, eg in brain chemistry, genes or our evolution.
  • This is the nature side of the nature–nurture debate. Nature means inherited, biological influences; nurture means the environment and experience.
  • Stressful life events, such as losing a job or a bereavement, make depression more likely. That is nurture.
Psychological problems · biological explanations of depression

Serotonin and the synapse

  • Neurotransmitters are chemicals that carry signals across the synapse, the gap between two neurons.
  • Serotonin is a neurotransmitter linked to mood, sleep and appetite.
  • The biological explanation says depression is caused by an imbalance of neurotransmitters, especially too little serotonin activity in the brain.
  • Evidence: drugs that increase serotonin activity reduce symptoms for many people.

Psychological problems · biological explanations of depression

Nature, nurture or both?

  • Biological explanations show that nature matters, but they rarely explain depression on their own.
  • The diathesis–stress idea: a vulnerability (diathesis), eg in a person's biology, plus stress from life events together trigger depression.
  • This explains why the same hard times lead to depression in some people but not others.
  • The psychological explanation adds the nurture side.
Quick check
?Two friends both lose their jobs in the same month. Afterwards one becomes depressed and the other does not. Which explanation best fits the diathesis–stress idea?
Mission check

?Why do the same hard times trigger depression in only some people?

Biological explanations look for causes in the body (nature). People seem to differ in vulnerability, for example in their genes or brain chemistry, and stressful life events (nurture) trigger depression in those who are vulnerable. Nature and nurture work together.

Mission 4

Same bad news, two people: one shrugs, one spirals. Why?

Make a guess: Same bad news, two people: one shrugs, one spirals. Why?

Psychological problems · psychological explanations of depression

It's how you think about it

  • Psychological (cognitive) explanations say depression comes from the way a person thinks about events, not from the events alone.
  • Negative, distorted thinking leads to low mood, and low mood makes negative thoughts easier to believe.
  • These thinking patterns are learned through experience, eg criticism or loss in childhood. That is the nurture side of the nature–nurture debate.
Psychological problems · psychological explanations of depression

Negative schemas and the negative triad

  • A schema is a mental framework of beliefs built from experience.
  • Beck's cognitive theory: negative experiences in childhood, such as criticism, rejection or loss, create negative schemas ("I'm a failure").
  • Later events switch these schemas on, producing automatic negative thoughts.
  • The negative triad: negative views of the self ("I'm useless"), the world ("everyone's against me") and the future ("it'll never get better").
Psychological problems · psychological explanations of depression

Negative attributions

  • An attribution is how you explain why something happened.
  • People with depression tend to explain bad events in a way that is:
  • internal: "It's my fault", not the situation;
  • stable: "It'll always be like this";
  • global: "I'm bad at everything".
  • If the cause is you, permanent and everywhere, nothing seems able to change, which leads to hopelessness.
Psychological problems · psychological explanations of depression

Cognitive explanations and nurture

  • In the nature–nurture debate, cognitive explanations are on the nurture side: negative thinking is learned from experience, so it can be unlearned.
  • Evidence: people with depression show more negative thinking than people without depression.
  • But thinking is not the whole story: biology (nature) may also play a part, and the two may interact.

?Same bad news, two people: one shrugs, one spirals. Why?

Cognitive explanations say it isn't the event but how a person thinks about it. Negative schemas and internal, stable, global attributions turn the same news into low mood for one person but not the other.

Mission 5

Why do doctors often treat depression with pills and talking?

Make a guess: Why do doctors often treat depression with pills and talking?

Psychological problems · treating depression

Antidepressants at the synapse

  • A common type of antidepressant is the SSRI (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor), eg fluoxetine.
  • Normally the sending neuron takes serotonin back from the synapse: reuptake.
  • SSRIs block reuptake, so serotonin stays in the synapse longer and keeps stimulating the receiving neuron's receptors.
  • This changes how neurons communicate, and over several weeks can lift mood. A doctor prescribes and monitors them.
Psychological problems · treating depression

CBT: changing thoughts and behaviour

  • Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) is a talking therapy based on the cognitive explanation.
  • The therapist helps the client identify negative thoughts and challenge them: what is the evidence?
  • The behavioural part: planning enjoyable or useful activities, and homework to test thoughts in real life.
  • Usually a course of regular sessions; the client learns skills to use alone.
Key study Psychological problems · treating depression

Wiles et al. (2013)

Aim
To test whether adding CBT to usual GP care helps people not improved by antidepressants.
Method
Randomised controlled trial. 469 UK adults from GP practices, still depressed despite antidepressants, were randomly given usual care (staying on medication) or usual care plus CBT. Depression was measured by questionnaire at 6 and 12 months.
Results
At 6 months, 46% of the CBT group showed a big drop in symptoms compared with 22% of the usual-care group, and the benefit lasted at 12 months.
Conclusion
CBT added to medication is an effective treatment when antidepressants alone have not worked.
Strength
Random allocation means group differences are likely to be due to CBT.
Weakness
Participants knew whether they had CBT, which might affect how they reported symptoms (no blinding).
Psychological problems · treating depression

Reductionist or holistic?

  • A reductionist treatment targets one level of explanation. Antidepressants reduce depression to brain chemistry.
  • CBT works on thoughts and behaviour: broader, but still one part of the person.
  • A holistic approach treats the whole person: biology, thinking, relationships and life situation together, eg combining medication, therapy and practical support.
  • Reductionist treatments are easier to test; holistic ones may fit real lives better.
Psychological problems · treating depression

Why combine treatments?

  • Psychological treatments come first; for moderate or severe depression they can be combined with antidepressants. Mild depression doesn't need medication (WHO 2026).
  • Medication can lift mood and energy enough for a person to engage with therapy.
  • Therapy teaches skills that may help prevent depression returning after medication stops.
  • Together they treat both biology and thinking: a more holistic approach.

Mission check

?Why do doctors often treat depression with pills and talking?

Antidepressants such as SSRIs change how neurons communicate at the synapse; talking therapies such as CBT change thoughts and behaviour. For moderate or severe depression they can be combined: medication can lift mood enough to engage with therapy, and therapy builds skills that last, treating the whole person.

Mission 6

Same habit, same years: why can one person stop and another can't?

Make a guess: Same habit, same years: why can one person stop and another can't?

Psychological problems · addiction and genes

Misuse or addiction?

  • Substance misuse (or abuse) is using a substance in a harmful way or not as intended, e.g. above recommended limits, or a medicine not as prescribed.
  • Addiction (medical term: dependence) is when the person has lost control: a strong need drives them to keep using despite harm.
  • Misuse can happen without addiction, but repeated misuse can lead to dependence.
  • People can also become addicted to behaviours, such as gambling.
Psychological problems · addiction and genes

How a clinician diagnoses dependence

  • Clinicians use the ICD (the International Classification of Diseases), which calls addiction dependence syndrome.
  • Strong desire to use the substance, despite harmful consequences.
  • Difficulty controlling use: starting, stopping or how much.
  • Higher priority for the substance than for other activities and obligations.
  • Physical signs can include tolerance (needing more for the same effect) and withdrawal (feeling ill on stopping).
  • Several features persist over months; a clinician makes the diagnosis.
Quick check
?Over two years, Alex's alcohol use has grown. Alex has dropped football and keeps missing work deadlines because evenings are now planned around alcohol. Which ICD feature does this show most clearly?
Psychological problems · addiction and genes

Can addiction run in families?

  • The biological explanation (influence of nature): some people inherit genetic vulnerability, genes that raise the risk of addiction.
  • Genes may affect how strongly the brain's reward system responds, or how the body breaks a substance down.
  • Twin studies compare identical (MZ) twins, who share 100% of their genes, with non-identical (DZ) twins, who share about 50%.
  • Higher concordance (both twins affected) in MZ twins suggests genes play a part.
Key study Psychological problems · addiction and genes

Kaij (1960)

Aim
To find out whether hereditary factors are involved in alcohol abuse.
Method
Twin study in Sweden: 174 male twin pairs (48 identical, 126 non-identical), at least one twin in each pair on a public register of alcohol abusers. Twins and relatives were interviewed, and each twin's alcohol use was rated in one of five categories.
Results
In 54% of identical pairs both twins were in the same category, compared with 28% of non-identical pairs.
Conclusion
Hereditary factors are involved in alcohol abuse.
Strength
Comparing identical with non-identical twins gives a natural test of how much shared genes matter.
Weakness
Only Swedish men were studied, and interview answers may be inaccurate or socially desirable.

Mission check

?Same habit, same years: why can one person stop and another can't?

Using a substance, even harmfully, is not the same as dependence, which means a strong desire, loss of control and a higher priority than other things. People differ in genetic vulnerability, so the same habit can lead to dependence in one person and not another, though genes raise the risk rather than decide it.

Mission 7

If friends and rewards build an addiction, can learning undo it?

Make a guess: If friends and rewards build an addiction, can learning undo it?

Psychological problems · addiction, learning and treatment

Learning it from friends

  • The psychological explanation (influence of nurture) says addiction is learned from the people around us.
  • Peer influence: young people often start because their friends do. They observe and imitate peers they identify with.
  • Seeing friends gain fun or status is vicarious reinforcement.
  • Joining in brings acceptance; refusing risks rejection. The behaviour starts to seem normal.
Psychological problems · addiction, learning and treatment

Rewards that keep it going

  • Operant conditioning: behaviour that is rewarded is repeated.
  • Positive reinforcement: it brings pleasure, a "buzz" or a win.
  • Negative reinforcement: it removes something unpleasant, such as stress or withdrawal.
  • Through classical conditioning, cues (places, people, times of day) become linked with the reward and can trigger cravings.
  • These learning processes explain how peer influence can turn first use into a habit.

Psychological problems · addiction, learning and treatment

Unlearning an addiction

  • If an addiction was learned, new learning can change it: therapies try to break the link between the behaviour and its reward and build new habits.
  • Aversion therapy uses classical conditioning: the substance is paired with something unpleasant, e.g. a prescribed drug (Antabuse) that causes sickness if alcohol is drunk. The person learns to link alcohol with feeling ill.
Psychological problems · addiction, learning and treatment

Self-management programmes

  • Self-management programmes put the person in charge of recovery, e.g. self-help groups such as Alcoholics Anonymous.
  • 12-step recovery programmes: admit loss of control, accept help, make amends and support others, often with a mentor.
  • Support, shared experience and accountability improve mental health by reducing isolation.
  • Holistic approaches treat the whole person; reductionist ones focus on one part, such as a single learned link.

Mission check

?If friends and rewards build an addiction, can learning undo it?

Friends model the behaviour and reward it with acceptance, and the behaviour itself brings pleasure or relief, so the habit is learned. Because it was learned, therapies based on learning can change it, though results are not guaranteed and support over time matters.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Everyone has mental health; a significant mental health problem is severe, lasting and gets in the way of daily life, and a clinician makes the diagnosis.
  • Recorded rates have risen, partly because of modern challenges such as isolation, partly because problems are better recognised and less stigmatised.
  • Mental health problems affect the person (relationships, daily life, physical health) and society (care, health services, the economy).
  • Sadness is a normal emotion that lifts; clinical depression is persistent low mood or loss of interest with other symptoms that affect daily life.
  • Unipolar depression is only low; bipolar depression alternates with mania.
  • ICD symptoms include low mood, reduced energy, changes in sleep and appetite and decreased self-confidence, most days for at least two weeks; the number and severity of symptoms set mild, moderate or severe.
  • Biological explanations are the nature side of the debate: depression is linked to an imbalance of neurotransmitters, especially too little serotonin.
  • Many psychologists now think a vulnerability (nature) plus stressful events (nurture) together trigger depression: the diathesis–stress idea.
  • Psychological (cognitive) explanations say depression comes from how a person thinks about events, learned through experience: the nurture side.
  • Negative schemas (the negative triad) and internal, stable, global attributions lead to depression.
  • SSRIs block the reuptake of serotonin, so more stays in the synapse; they take weeks to work and can have side effects.
  • CBT helps people identify and challenge negative thoughts and change behaviour, building skills that last.
  • Wiles et al. found CBT added to medication helped people whose depression hadn't improved with antidepressants alone. Drugs alone are reductionist; combining treatments is more holistic.
  • Substance misuse is harmful use; addiction (dependence syndrome in the ICD) adds a strong desire despite harm, difficulty controlling use and a higher priority than other activities.
  • Some people inherit genetic vulnerability: Kaij's twin study found higher concordance in identical twins, but well below 100%, so the environment matters too.
  • Peer influence (nurture): people observe and imitate friends and are rewarded with acceptance; aversion therapy re-conditions the link, and self-management programmes such as 12-step groups take a holistic approach.
  • Addiction that was learned can be changed by new learning, but it takes time and support.
