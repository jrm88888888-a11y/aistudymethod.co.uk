Foundations of Ethics with Special Reference to Issues in Medical Ethics

Area 3: Religion and Ethics · Unit AS 7 (AS level)

This mini-lesson covers Unit AS 7: Foundations of Ethics with Special Reference to Issues in Medical Ethics — deontological ethics (Christian Scriptures and Natural Moral Law), teleological ethics (utilitarianism and situation ethics), and their application to abortion, euthanasia and bioethics.

Learning focus

Deontological approaches

Deontological ethics judges actions by duty and rule, not by results. Two foundations are studied:

  • Christian Scriptures — the Decalogue (Ten Commandments) and the Sermon on the Mount, the nature of biblical ethics and the role of moral commands; and
  • Natural Moral Law — its origins and historical development, its principles and key features, and proportionalism as a later modification.

Natural Law holds that reason can discern the natural purposes God built into human life, and that we should act in line with them.

Teleological approaches: utilitarianism

Teleological ethics judges actions by their ends or consequences. Utilitarianism seeks the greatest good:

  • Jeremy Bentham — the greatest happiness of the greatest number, measured by a 'hedonic calculus';
  • J. S. Mill — refined this by distinguishing higher and lower pleasures; and
  • Peter Singer — a modern preference utilitarian.

The specification also asks about Christian utilitarianism.

Learning focus

Situation ethics

Situation ethics, developed by Joseph Fletcher, holds that the one absolute is agape — self-giving love. In each situation you do 'the most loving thing'.

  • its origins and development;
  • the influence of the moral teaching of Jesus and Paul;
  • its key principles and its understanding of the nature of love; and
  • its relationship to utilitarianism (both are consequentialist, but situation ethics centres on love, not pleasure).

Learning focus

Life and death: abortion

The specification applies these theories to abortion:

  • the moral status, interests and rights of the unborn and of the woman;
  • religious, secular and feminist views;
  • the sanctity of life principle and the personhood debate;
  • therapeutic abortion and the doctrine of double effect; and
  • the slippery slope argument.

Life and death: euthanasia

On euthanasia, candidates weigh:

  • the types of euthanasia and the role of personal autonomy;
  • the sanctity of life principle against the quality of life argument;
  • the acts and omissions doctrine;
  • the doctor–patient relationship and issues of trust; and
  • the ethics of assisted dying and the slippery slope argument.

Developments in bioethics

Finally, the unit examines assisted conception and new reproductive technologies:

  • human infertility, IVF and surrogacy;
  • the status of the embryo, personal rights and ownership;
  • pre-implantation genetic diagnosis (PGD), embryo research and mitochondrial donation ('three-parent babies'); and
  • the role of the Human Fertilisation and Embryology Authority (HFEA).

The big ideas to know

Deontological: Christian Scriptures (Decalogue, Sermon on the Mount); Natural Moral Law and proportionalism

Teleological: Utilitarianism (Bentham, Mill, Singer); situation ethics and agape love

Abortion: Rights of the unborn and the woman; sanctity of life; personhood; double effect

Euthanasia: Types; autonomy; sanctity vs quality of life; acts and omissions; slippery slope

Bioethics: IVF, surrogacy, the status of the embryo; PGD and mitochondrial donation; the HFEA
