Mission 1

Mission 1: Nobody buys workers for their own sake, so what decides how many a business wants to hire?

Make a guess: Nobody buys workers for their own sake, so what decides how many a business wants to hire?

Mission 1

Derived demand

  • Firms do not want workers for their own sake. They want the goods and services workers produce.
  • So the demand for labour is a derived demand: derived from the demand for the product.
  • More people order home deliveries → firms want more delivery drivers.
Mission 1

Why labour demand slopes down

  • A higher wage → firms want fewer workers: a movement along the demand curve.
  • Call it a contraction in the demand for labour.
  • Substitution effect: labour is now dearer relative to machines, so firms switch to capital where they can.
  • Output (scale) effect: higher costs → higher prices → fewer products sold → fewer workers needed.
  • And diminishing returns: each extra worker adds less output, so is worth hiring only at a lower wage.
  • Axes: "Wage rate (W)" and "Quantity of labour (L)".
Mission 1

  • Illustrative data. A bakery sells loaves in a competitive market at 5 each.
  • Marginal product (MP): the extra output from one more worker.
  • Marginal revenue product (MRP): the extra revenue from one more worker. MRP = MP × MR.
  • Here P = MR = 5, so MRP = MP × 5.

Mission 1

How many to hire: MRP = wage

  • In a competitive labour market, the firm is a wage taker: each extra worker costs the market wage.
  • Rule: hire while MRP ≥ W; stop where MRP = W.
  • Bakery at W = 30: workers 1–4 have MRP of 60, 50, 40, 30. The 5th adds only 20. Hire 4.
  • So the firm's MRP curve is its demand curve for labour: at each wage, it shows how many workers it hires.
  • Label: "MRP = D(L)". This is marginal productivity theory.
Mission 1

Shifts and movements

  • A change in the wage → movement along the curve.
  • A wage fall gives an extension; a wage rise gives a contraction.
  • These shift labour demand (D1 → D2):
    • Demand for the product, and so its price (derived demand).
    • Productivity of workers: training, better equipment, better management.
    • Price of capital: cheaper machines that replace workers shift demand left; machines that workers use can shift it right.
    • Non-wage costs of employing workers, e.g. employer payroll taxes.
    • The number of firms hiring this kind of labour.
  • In MRP terms: a higher output price or higher MP shifts the MRP curve right.
  • Say: "a rise in the wage causes a contraction in the demand for labour" — not: "the demand for labour falls".

Mission 1

Wage elasticity of demand for labour

  • Wage elasticity of demand for labour = % change in quantity of labour demanded ÷ % change in the wage.
  • Like PED, it is negative; we often discuss its size.
  • Worked example (illustrative data): the wage rises 10%; employment falls 4%. Elasticity = −4 ÷ 10 = −0.4: inelastic.
  • Demand for labour is more elastic when:
    • demand for the product is price elastic (derived demand);
    • labour is a large share of total costs;
    • capital is an easy substitute for workers;
    • the time period is long (firms can re-plan production).
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A warehouse raises its hourly wage from 20 to 21. Over the year, the number of workers it employs falls from 1,000 to 920. What is the wage elasticity of demand for labour, and what happens to the total wage bill?
Mission 1Evaluate

How useful is the theory?

  • It depends on elasticity: a wage rise costs few jobs where labour demand is inelastic, many where it is elastic.
  • It depends on productivity: pay can rise without job losses if output per worker rises too.
  • Limits of marginal productivity theory:
    • The MP of one worker is hard to measure in teams and in services (a nurse, a teacher).
    • Wages are often set by bargaining, monopsony or law, not by MRP alone.
    • Firms may not aim only to maximise profit.
  • But it predicts the direction of change well: higher product prices or productivity → more hiring.
Mission check

Workers are hired for what they produce, so a change in the product's price moves the whole demand for labour.

Make a guess: What makes people willing to do a job, and why isn't the pay the whole story?

Mission 2

Who supplies labour, and why it slopes up

  • Households supply labour; firms demand it.
  • Each hour of work is an hour less of leisure. The wage is the reward for giving it up.
  • Supply to one occupation slopes up: a higher wage draws workers in from other jobs, and from outside the labour force.
  • A change in the wage → movement along the supply curve.
  • Call it an extension (wage up) or contraction (wage down) of supply.
  • Three levels: an individual's hours; supply to an occupation or industry; supply to the whole economy.
  • Axes: "Wage rate (W)" and "Quantity of labour (L)"; curve "S(L)".
Mission 2

Monetary and non-monetary factors

  • Monetary (wage) factors: basic pay, overtime, bonuses, commission, pensions, perks.
  • Non-monetary (non-wage) factors:
    • working conditions: safety, hours, stress, flexibility, holidays;
    • job security and promotion and training chances;
    • status, satisfaction, location, commuting time.
  • Workers weigh the net advantages of a job: pay plus everything else.
  • Say: "an improvement in working conditions shifts labour supply to the right" — not: "it causes an extension of supply".
  • Only the wage moves along the curve.
Mission 2

What shifts the supply of labour

  • These shift supply to an occupation (S1 → S2):
    • Wages in other jobs (alternatives): higher pay elsewhere → supply here shifts left.
    • Working conditions and other non-wage factors.
    • Qualifications and training needed, and licensing rules.
    • Education: more people with the right skills → shifts right.
    • Immigration: more workers arriving → shifts right.
    • Demographics: the size and age of the population.
    • Leisure preferences and culture, e.g. attitudes to working parents.
    • Taxes and benefits: affect the reward from working compared with not working.

Mission 2

Income and substitution effects

  • A wage rise has two effects on an individual's hours:
    • Substitution effect: each hour of leisure now costs more lost pay → work more.
    • Income effect: you can reach the same income in fewer hours → buy more leisure, work less (leisure is a normal good).
  • Worked example (illustrative data):

  • 15 → 25: income effect stronger: hours fall, though pay still rises.
  • So an individual's supply curve can bend backwards at high wages.
  • Supply to an occupation still usually slopes up: higher pay attracts new workers.

Mission 2

The whole economy: population, migration, participation

  • Labour supply to the whole economy depends on:
    • population size and age structure: more people of working age → more labour;
    • net migration: workers moving in or out;
    • participation: the share of working-age people in the labour force (working or looking for work).
  • Participation rate = labour force ÷ working-age population × 100.
  • Participation depends on childcare, retirement age, health, education (students), and culture.
  • Example: in many high-income countries, far more women joined the labour force from the 1970s onward.
Quick check
?In a country, a large generation of workers reaches retirement age and leaves the labour force. Few young people are entering work, and wages have not changed. What happens to the economy's labour supply?
Mission 2Evaluate

Will higher pay bring more workers?

  • It depends on non-monetary factors: poor conditions can outweigh a pay rise (e.g. jobs with long hours or high stress).
  • It depends on time: jobs with long training respond slowly; supply is more elastic in the long run.
  • It depends on mobility: workers may lack the skills, or live too far away.
  • It depends on income vs substitution effects: existing workers may choose fewer hours.
  • It depends on taxes and benefits: what workers keep, compared with not working.
Mission check

?Illustrative case: in past years, a hospital found that each pay rise attracted more nurses. This year it raises nurses' pay by 10%, and at the same time brings in longer, less flexible shifts. The number of nurses willing to work there barely changes. What is the best explanation?

Pay is only part of a job's reward. Working conditions shift the whole supply curve.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why do some of the most useful jobs pay less than jobs that seem far less useful?

Make a guess: What mostly decides how much a job pays?

Mission 3

Wages are a price

  • The wage rate is the price of labour.
  • Demand for labour comes from firms. It is a derived demand: firms want workers for the goods and services they make (see Mission 1).
  • Supply of labour comes from workers. More people offer to work in a job at a higher wage (see Mission 2).
  • In a competitive labour market (many firms, many workers, none able to set the wage), the wage settles where demand meets supply: We, Le.
  • Above We: more workers want the job than firms want to hire, so wages are bid down. Below We: firms cannot fill jobs, so wages are bid up.
  • The demand curve for labour is the MRP curve: D = MRP.
Mission 3

What shifts the curves

  • Demand for labour shifts right when: demand for the product rises · its price rises · workers become more productive (training, better machines) · machines that replace workers become dearer.
  • Supply of labour shifts right when: more people have the skills or qualifications · the job becomes more attractive (safer, flexible hours, status) · the working population grows (including migration) · wages in other jobs fall.
  • A change in the wage itself moves along the curves; it does not shift them.
  • Wage elasticity of labour demand or supply = % change in quantity of labour ÷ % change in the wage.
  • Say: "an extension of the supply of labour" for a move along S — not: "supply of labour increases".
Quick check
?A new rule means it takes two more years to qualify as an electrician. Other things equal, what happens in the market for electricians?
Mission 3

One firm: hire until MRP equals the wage

  • A firm in a competitive labour market is a wage taker: it can hire as many workers as it wants at the market wage.
  • So the supply of labour to the firm is horizontal at We. Each extra worker adds exactly We to costs.
  • Label it S = ACL = MCL (average and marginal cost of labour).
  • MRP (marginal revenue product): the extra revenue from one more worker. MRP = MP × MR.
  • Rule: hire while MRP > wage; stop where MRP = wage.

When demand for labour rises

  • Takeaway: when supply of labour is inelastic, a rise in demand mostly raises the wage; when it is elastic, it mostly raises employment.
Mission 3

Worked example: demand for labour rises

  • Illustrative data. Labour demand: Ld = 1000 − 40W. Labour supply: Ls = 60W − 200. (W = wage per hour, L = workers.)
  • Equilibrium: 1000 − 40W = 60W − 200 → 100W = 1200 → W = 12, L = 520.
  • Demand for the product rises: Ld = 1200 − 40W.
  • New equilibrium: 1200 − 40W = 60W − 200 → W = 14, L = 640.
  • Wage: +2 ÷ 12 = +16.7%. Employment: +120 ÷ 520 = +23.1%.
  • Wage elasticity of supply of labour along S ≈ 23.1 ÷ 16.7 ≈ 1.38: elastic, so employment rises by a larger % than the wage.
  • Say: "demand for labour increased, causing an extension of supply" — not: "supply increased".
Mission 3

Why wages differ

  • Wage differentials: gaps in pay between jobs, people, regions or sectors.
  • Examiners call these relative wages.
  • Demand side: high productivity or a valuable product means high demand for the worker.
  • Supply side: long training, rare skills or talent keep supply small and inelastic.
  • Compensating differentials: dangerous, dirty or night-shift jobs must pay more to attract workers.
  • Non-money rewards: jobs people love (a sense of vocation, status, long holidays) attract more workers, so supply is larger and pay lower.
  • Immobility: workers who cannot move or retrain let gaps last.
  • Market power and discrimination also matter.
  • Like water and diamonds: water is vital but cheap; a diamond is not vital but costs far more.
  • How useful a thing is does not set its price. Scarcity relative to demand does.

Mission 3

What differentials do

  • Signals: high pay tells workers where to train and move. Over time supply shifts right and the gap can narrow.
  • Skill shortages: if training takes years, a gap can last and posts stay unfilled.
  • Inequality: wider differentials mean a more unequal spread of income.
  • Technology: machines that replace routine tasks cut demand for some workers and raise it for workers whose skills work with the machines.
Mission 3

Evaluate: will the gap last?

It depends on…

  • Time to train: supply of surgeons responds over years; supply of shelf-stackers in weeks.
  • Mobility and information: workers must know about better-paid jobs and be able to move to them.
  • Market power: a single big employer or a strong union can hold the wage away from We.
  • Discrimination: groups paid less for the same productivity (see Mission 7).
  • Is the demand change lasting? A short boom may end before new workers qualify.
Mission check

Pay follows demand and supply in the job market, not how useful a job feels.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why would a firm refuse to hire a worker who would add more to its sales than they are paid?

Make a guess: A firm is the only big employer in town. One more worker would add more to its sales than the wage it pays. Why might it still not hire them?

Mission 4

One buyer

  • Monopsony: a market with a single buyer (or one dominant buyer).
  • In a labour market: one employer hires most of the workers with a particular skill or in a particular place.
  • Examples: a mine or factory in a remote town · a state health service that employs most of a country's nurses.
  • In a product market: a few large retailers buying from many small farmers.
  • A monopsonist is a wage setter, not a wage taker.
  • Say: "monopsony" (one buyer) — not: "monopoly" (one seller).
Mission 4

Why one more worker costs more than the wage

  • A monopsonist faces the whole upward-sloping supply curve of labour.
  • To attract one more worker it must raise the wage.
  • It must pay that higher wage to all its workers, not just the new one.
  • So the marginal cost of labour is above the wage.
  • Labels: ACL = S (average cost of labour = the wage) and MCL (marginal cost of labour), drawn above ACL.
Mission 4

Mission 4

The monopsony diagram

  • Step 1: the supply of labour and the MRP curve. If the employer acted as a wage taker, it would hire where they cross: Wc, Lc.
  • Step 2: draw MCL above ACL = S. Profit is greatest where MRP = MCL: employment Lm.
  • Step 3: the firm pays the lowest wage that attracts Lm workers. Read down to the supply curve: wage Wm.
  • Result: Wm < Wc and Lm < Lc. Fewer workers, lower pay.
  • The last worker hired at Lm adds more revenue (MRP) than they are paid (Wm).
  • Say: "read the wage from the supply curve" — not: from the MRP or the marginal cost curve.

Mission 4

Monopsony without one employer

  • Firms can set wages even when there are several employers, if workers cannot easily switch.
  • Imperfect information: workers do not know what other firms pay or which jobs are open.
  • Immobility and switching costs: long commutes, housing costs, family ties, firm-specific skills.
  • Each firm then faces an upward-sloping supply of labour, so it has some monopsony power.
  • Employers acting together can do the same. Real case: in 2015 a US court approved a $415 million settlement of a lawsuit claiming that Apple, Google, Intel and Adobe agreed not to recruit each other's staff, holding pay down.
  • Union power can offset monopsony power (a later screen, Mission 5).
Mission 4

Limiting monopsony power

  • Trade unions: bargain as one seller of labour.
  • Minimum wages: can raise both pay and employment in a monopsony (see Mission 6).
  • Regulation and competition policy: codes of practice, fines, bans on no-poaching deals.
  • Better information and mobility: job adverts with pay ranges, help with moving.
Mission 4

Bilateral monopoly

  • Bilateral monopoly: a monopsony buyer faces a monopoly seller. In labour: one employer and one trade union.
  • The employer wants Wm at Lm.
  • At Lm, each worker's MRP is above Wm, so the union can press for more. The most the firm would pay to keep Lm workers is the MRP at Lm.
  • The wage settles somewhere between Wm and MRP at Lm. Where exactly is indeterminate: it depends on bargaining power.
  • Union power: share of workers in the union, strike threat, the firm's profits, the law, public support.
  • A union wage between Wm and Wc can raise both pay and employment (see Mission 5).

Mission 4

Evaluate: is monopsony always harmful?

It depends on…

  • Elasticity of labour supply to the firm: the more easily workers can leave, the less the firm can cut pay.
  • Whether savings reach consumers: in a competitive retail market, lower costs can mean lower prices.
  • Countervailing power: a big buyer facing a big seller may bring the price closer to the competitive level.
  • The long run: low pay and low supplier prices can reduce training, investment and supply.
  • Who is protected: unions, minimum wages and regulators can limit the power.
Mission check

A single buyer that must pay more on every unit to buy one more unit chooses to buy less, and pays less.

Mission 5

What a trade union does

  • A trade union: an organisation of workers that acts for its members.
  • Collective bargaining: the union negotiates pay and conditions for all its members at once, instead of each worker alone.
  • Aims: higher pay · better conditions and safety · job security · training · a voice in how work changes.
  • Tools: negotiation; if talks fail, industrial action (strikes, overtime bans, working to rule).
  • A union acts as a single seller of labour: some of the market power of a monopoly.
Mission 5

Real data: unions in the UK

  • UK, 2025 (Department for Business and Trade):
  • 6.6 million employees were union members.
  • Union density (members as % of employees): 22.4%, down from 32.4% in 1995.
  • Public sector 48.5%; private sector 12.1%.
  • Union membership peaked at 13.2 million in 1979 (unions listed in Great Britain).
  • Union wage premium: members' average hourly pay was 5.3% above non-members' in 2025 (before allowing for other differences between the two groups).
Quick check

Mission 5

A union in a competitive labour market

  • Without a union: wage We, employment Le.
  • The union negotiates Wu above We. Firms will not pay less, so the supply of labour to firms is horizontal at Wu, then follows S.
  • At Wu firms move up their demand curve: employment falls to Lu.
  • At Wu more people want these jobs (Ls). The gap Ls − Lu is excess supply of labour.
  • Illustrative data: Ld = 1000 − 40W, Ls = 60W − 200. We = £12, Le = 520.
  • Union wage £14: Ld = 1000 − 560 = 440; Ls = 840 − 200 = 640.
  • Employment falls by 80; excess supply 200.
  • Wage +16.7%, employment −15.4%: the wage elasticity of demand for labour is about −0.92.
Mission 5

Raising pay without losing jobs (competitive market)

  • Inelastic demand for labour: if firms cannot easily cut jobs, a higher wage costs few jobs.
  • Productivity deals: the union agrees new working methods or training. Higher productivity shifts demand for labour right.
  • Shifting demand in other ways: campaigns for the industry's product, health and safety that cuts sick days.
  • High profits: a firm earning supernormal profit can pay more without closing.
  • Otherwise, in a competitive market, a wage above We means fewer jobs: union members gain, but others (outsiders) lose.
  • Say: "a contraction of demand for labour" — not: "demand for labour falls" (the curve has not moved).
Mission 5

A union facing a monopsony

  • Recall (see Mission 4): a monopsonist hires where MRP = MCL and pays the low wage Wm on the supply curve.
  • The union sets a wage Wu between Wm and Wc. Now the firm can hire up to the supply curve at Wu, with no need to raise anyone's pay.
  • So the MCL becomes horizontal at Wu (MCL = ACL = Wu) up to the supply curve.
  • The firm hires more workers, because each extra worker now costs only Wu.
  • Illustrative data: ACL = S: W = 5 + 0.5L; MCL = 5 + L; MRP = 20 − 0.5L (L = workers).
  • Monopsony: 5 + L = 20 − 0.5L → L = 10, W = £10.
Mission 5Graph lab

At a union wage of £12: workers willing = (12 − 5) ÷ 0.5 = 14; the firm wants (20 − 12) ÷ 0.5 = 16. Employment = 14, so pay and employment rise: £10 → £12, 10 → 14 workers.

Mission 5

What gives a union power?

  • Membership: a high share of the workforce in the union, so a strike stops production.
  • Inelastic demand for labour, which is more likely when:
  • wages are a small share of total costs · workers are hard to replace with machines or other workers · demand for the product is price inelastic.
  • The firm's profits: profitable firms can afford to pay more.
  • The state of the economy: low unemployment makes strikers harder to replace.
  • The law: rules on ballots, strikes and picketing.
  • Information: unions can tell members what similar workers earn, which reduces employers' power from imperfect information.
Mission 5

Unions and labour market flexibility

  • Labour market flexibility: how quickly wages, hours and jobs adjust to changes in demand.
  • Unions may reduce flexibility:
  • Wage rigidity: wages slow to fall when demand falls, so more jobs are lost.
  • Resisting job cuts, new methods or changes to hours.
  • Unions may also help:
  • Voice: workers raise problems instead of quitting, cutting staff turnover and training costs.
  • Agreeing change: productivity deals can be easier with one negotiating partner.
  • Countering monopsony power.
Quick check
?Unions in four industries each win a 10% pay rise. Other things equal, in which industry are job losses likely to be smallest?
Mission 5

Evaluate: do unions help or harm?

It depends on…

  • Market structure: in a monopsony, unions can raise pay and jobs; in a competitive market, higher pay usually costs some jobs.
  • Elasticity of demand for labour: the more inelastic, the smaller the job loss.
  • Productivity: deals that raise output per worker can pay for higher wages.
  • Who is counted: insiders (members in work) may gain while outsiders lose jobs or chances.
  • Scale and timing: strikes cost output; wage rises above productivity growth across the economy can push up costs and prices.

Mission 6

A legal wage floor

  • A minimum wage: the lowest hourly wage an employer may legally pay.
  • It is a price floor in the labour market. It only has an effect if set above the equilibrium wage.
  • Aims: raise low pay · reduce in-work poverty and inequality · make work pay more than benefits.
  • UK example: a National Minimum Wage since 1999. The rate for workers aged 21 and over is called the National Living Wage.
  • From 1 April 2026: £12.71 an hour for 21 and over (it was £12.21 in 2025); £10.85 for ages 18–20; £8.00 for ages 16–17 and apprentices.
  • Rates are set by the government on the advice of the Low Pay Commission.

  • Step 1: labour market clears at We, Le.
  • Step 2: a minimum wage Wmin above We.
  • At Wmin, firms move up their demand curve: employment contracts to Ld.
  • More people want to work at Wmin: supply of labour extends to Ls.
  • Step 3: Ls − Ld is excess supply of labour: unemployment caused by the minimum wage.
  • Workers who keep their jobs earn more; those who lose jobs or cannot find one lose.
  • Say: "a contraction of demand for labour" — not: "demand for labour falls" (the curve has not moved).
Mission 6

Worked example

  • Illustrative data. Ld = 900 − 50W; Ls = 100W − 300 (W in £ per hour, L = workers).
  • Equilibrium: 900 − 50W = 100W − 300 → We = £8, Le = 500.
  • Minimum wage £9: Ld = 900 − 450 = 450; Ls = 900 − 300 = 600.
  • Employment falls by 50. Unemployment (excess supply) = 600 − 450 = 150.
  • Wage +12.5%; employment −10%.
  • Wage elasticity of demand for labour ≈ −10 ÷ 12.5 = −0.8 (inelastic): the wage bill rises from £4000 to £4050 an hour.

Mission 6

How big is the effect?

  • The bite: the minimum wage as a % of the median wage. A higher bite affects more jobs.
  • UK bite: 45.6% in 1999, 52.5% in 2015; the government then set a target of two-thirds of median hourly pay, to be reached in 2024.
  • Elasticity of demand for labour: job losses are bigger where machines or other workers can replace low-paid staff, wages are a big share of costs, or customers can switch.
  • Elasticity of supply of labour: the more new job-seekers a higher wage draws in, the bigger the excess supply.
  • Firms can also respond in other ways: cut hours, training or perks · raise prices · accept lower profit · reorganise to raise productivity.
Mission 6Graph lab

Set the minimum wage

  • Takeaway: a floor below We does nothing; above We, the job loss grows with the bite and with the elasticity of demand for labour.
Mission 6

A minimum wage facing a monopsony

  • Recall (see Mission 4): a monopsony employer hires Lm at wage Wm, below the competitive Wc, Lc.
  • A minimum wage between Wm and Wc makes the MCL horizontal at Wmin: an extra worker no longer raises everyone's pay.
  • The firm hires more, up to the supply curve: wages and employment both rise.
  • Set above the MRP at Lm, it would cut employment below Lm.
  • The same logic as a union wage (see Mission 5).
Mission 6

Evidence: what happened?

  • New Jersey, 1992: the state minimum wage rose from $4.25 to $5.05 an hour. Neighbouring Pennsylvania's did not change.
  • Card and Krueger (1994) surveyed fast-food restaurants in both states. They found no fall in New Jersey's fast-food employment relative to Pennsylvania's.
  • Critics (Neumark and Wascher, 2000), using payroll records, found a small fall. The debate on methods continues.
  • UK: the Low Pay Commission (2024) said it had "not found strong evidence" that the National Living Wage reduced employment or hours.
  • Effects depend on the bite: evidence from moderate rises may not apply to much larger ones.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. Country X has a minimum wage of 6 dollars an hour and a median wage of 10 dollars. Country Y has a minimum wage of 9 dollars and a median wage of 18 dollars. Other things equal, which minimum wage is more likely to reduce employment?
Mission 6

Evaluate: does a minimum wage help?

It depends on…

  • The bite: moderate floors show small job effects; very high ones risk more.
  • Elasticity of demand for labour: easy substitution by machines means larger losses.
  • Market structure: with monopsony power, a floor can raise jobs and pay.
  • The economy: in a recession, firms are less able to absorb higher costs.
  • Who gains: many minimum-wage earners are not in the poorest households; benefits and taxes may target poverty better.
  • Other effects: higher productivity and lower staff turnover can offset the cost; fewer hours or perks can offset the gain.

In industry B, there is no practical substitute for the workers, and wages are a small share of costs. Other things equal, where is the fall in employment likely to be larger?

The same rise in the minimum wage costs more jobs where workers are easy to replace.

Mission 7

What counts as discrimination?

  • Labour market discrimination: workers who are equally productive are treated differently in pay, hiring or promotion because of a characteristic such as gender or ethnicity.
  • Kinds:
    • employer discrimination: an employer prefers not to hire or promote one group
    • statistical discrimination: an employer judges an individual by a group average (e.g. "this group quits more often"), not by the person
    • occupational crowding: one group is kept out of some jobs and pushed into others
    • customer or co-worker discrimination: employers respond to others' prejudice
  • Before the labour market: unequal access to education and training lowers a group's skills before they apply for a job.
  • Say: "discrimination: equal productivity, unequal treatment" — not: any difference in average pay between groups.
Mission 7

Discrimination on a graph

  • Take the labour market for one group, group B.
  • An employer who discriminates acts as if group B's workers produce less than they do.
  • So the demand for B's labour, MRP = D(L), shifts left: D1 → D2.
  • The supply of B's labour has not changed: the workers are just as willing and able to work.
  • New equilibrium: lower wage (W1 → W2) and lower employment (L1 → L2) for group B.
  • The wage falls by less than the shift: some of B's workers leave this market rather than work for less.

Mission 7

Crowding and monopsony

  • Crowding: if group B is kept out of occupation X, B's workers crowd into occupation Y.
    • Occupation Y: supply of labour shifts right → wages in Y fall.
    • Occupation X: supply shifts left → wages in X rise for the favoured group.
  • Result: a pay gap between groups even if each employer pays everyone in a job the same.
  • Monopsony: an employer with wage-setting power pays less to a group whose labour supply is less elastic (e.g. workers who cannot easily move or commute).
  • So a pay gap can arise from differences in mobility, not only from prejudice.
Mission 7

Worked example: the pay gap

  • Pay gap = (men's median pay − women's median pay) ÷ men's median pay × 100.
  • UK, April 2024 (ONS, median hourly pay excluding overtime):
    • full-time employees: gap 7.0%; all employees: 13.1% (part-time jobs pay less per hour, and more women work part-time)
    • full-time, aged 22–29: 1.3%; aged 60 and over: 13.5%
  • A gap is not all discrimination. It also reflects occupation, hours, experience and career breaks for childcare.
  • But those choices can themselves be shaped by discrimination and social norms.

Mission 7

Real world: testing for discrimination

  • How can economists separate discrimination from differences in skills?
  • Field experiment: send employers job applications that are identical except for one thing.
  • US, published 2004 (Bertrand and Mullainathan): nearly 5,000 résumés sent to over 1,300 job adverts in Boston and Chicago.
  • Each résumé was randomly given a name that sounded white or one that sounded Black.
  • Résumés with white-sounding names received 50% more callbacks.
  • Same qualifications, so the difference measures discrimination in hiring, not productivity.
Mission 7

Who loses, and what can be done?

  • Workers in the group: lower pay and fewer jobs; less reason to invest in their own skills.
  • Firms that discriminate pay more for favoured workers than for equally productive others, so their costs are higher.
  • The economy: talent is misallocated, so output is lower.
  • Policies:
    • laws: Equal Pay Act 1970; Equality Act 2010 (equal pay for equal work; protected characteristics include sex and race)
    • employers with 250 or more staff must publish their gender pay gap (since 2017)
    • childcare support and shared parental leave; name-blind recruitment
Mission 7Evaluate

Will competition remove discrimination?

  • Argument: firms that do not discriminate hire equally productive workers for less, so they undercut discriminating rivals.
  • It depends on:
    • Competition. A firm with monopoly profit can afford to discriminate; in a competitive market it may not survive.
    • Information. Statistical discrimination can seem profitable to a firm that cannot easily judge individuals.
    • Customers. If customers discriminate, the market rewards the firm that gives in to them.
    • Enforcement. Laws work only if discrimination can be proved; pay is often secret.
    • Measurement. Part of a pay gap reflects hours and occupation, so closing the gap fully may need more than anti-discrimination law.
Mission check

?In a country's computing industry, many employers start acting as if workers over 50 are less productive than younger workers, although tests show they are just as productive. What happens in the market for older computing workers?

Discrimination works through demand: employers value a group's work as if it were worth less, so pay and jobs both fall.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Labour demand is derived demand: it depends on demand for the product.
  • MRP = MP × MR; a firm hires until MRP = wage, so the MRP curve is its labour demand curve.
  • A wage change moves along the curve; product price, productivity, capital prices and non-wage costs shift it.
  • Wage elasticity of labour demand is higher when product demand is elastic, labour costs are a large share, and capital substitutes easily.

Mission 2

  • Labour supply to a job slopes up: a higher wage draws workers from other jobs and from outside the labour force.
  • The wage moves along the curve; non-wage factors, alternatives, education, migration and demographics shift it.
  • For an individual, a strong income effect can mean fewer hours at a higher wage.

Mission 3

  • In a competitive labour market the wage is set where demand for labour meets supply of labour.
  • Wage differentials come from differences in demand (productivity) and supply (skills, training, how pleasant the job is).
  • A wage-taking firm hires until MRP = wage.

Mission 4

  • A monopsonist must raise the wage for all workers to hire one more, so the marginal cost of labour is above the wage.
  • It hires where MRP = MCL and pays the wage on the supply (ACL) curve: lower wage, lower employment than a competitive market.
  • In bilateral monopoly the wage is indeterminate between Wm and the MRP at Lm.

Mission 5

  • A union bargains collectively and acts as a single seller of labour.
  • In a competitive labour market, a wage above equilibrium usually cuts employment unless demand for labour is inelastic or shifts right.
  • Facing a monopsony, a union wage between Wm and Wc can raise both pay and employment.

Mission 6

  • A minimum wage above equilibrium creates excess supply of labour in a competitive market.
  • How many jobs it costs depends on the bite and on the elasticity of demand for labour.
  • In a monopsony, a minimum wage between Wm and Wc can raise both pay and employment.

Mission 7

  • Discrimination: equally productive workers treated differently because of who they are.
  • On a graph, it shifts the demand for the group's labour left: lower wage and lower employment.
  • Crowding and monopsony can also create pay gaps between groups.
