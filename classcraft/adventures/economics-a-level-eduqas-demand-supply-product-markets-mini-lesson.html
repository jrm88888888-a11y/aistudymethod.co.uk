Mission 1

What "rational" means

  • Economics models choices as rational: agents weigh benefits against costs and pick the option that best meets their objective.
  • Consumers aim to maximise utility (satisfaction).
  • Firms aim to maximise profit (total revenue − total cost).
Mission 1

Total and marginal utility

  • Total utility (TU): the whole satisfaction from all units consumed.
  • Marginal utility (MU): the change in TU from one more unit. MU = ΔTU ÷ ΔQ.
  • Slices of pizza at one meal (illustrative data, in "utils"):

  • Law of diminishing marginal utility: as more units are consumed, each extra unit adds less utility.
  • TU still rises while MU is positive. TU is at its maximum where MU = 0.
  • Say: "marginal utility falls" — not: "total utility falls" (TU rises until MU turns negative).

Mission 1

From diminishing MU to the demand curve

  • A consumer buys one more unit only if its extra satisfaction is worth at least the price.
  • MU falls with each unit, so the most the consumer will pay for each extra unit falls too.
  • To persuade them to buy more, the price must be lower.
  • So quantity demanded rises as price falls: the demand curve slopes downward.
  • Say: "a fall in price causes an extension of demand" (a movement along D) — not: "demand increases".

Worked example: equal MU per unit of money

  • Equi-marginal principle: with a limited income, utility is maximised when MU/P is equal for every good and all income is spent.
  • Good X costs £3, good Y costs £1. Income is £9.
  • Illustrative data:

  • Step 2: spending = 2 × 3 + 3 × 1 = 9. All income spent.

Mission 1

Deriving demand from MU/P

  • Now the price of X falls from 3 to 2. MU/P of X rises: 18, 13.5, 9, 4.5.
  • MUx/Px > MUy/Py, so switching spending towards X raises total utility.
  • New optimum: 3 X and 3 Y (3 × 2 + 3 × 1 = 9; MU/P = 9 for both; TU = 117).
  • Two points on the demand curve for X: price 3 → 2 units; price 2 → 3 units.
  • Lower price, larger quantity demanded: a downward-sloping demand curve.
Mission 1

Mission 1

Water and diamonds

  • The paradox of value: water is vital, diamonds are not, yet diamonds cost far more.
  • Water: plentiful. People already use a lot, so the MU of one more litre is tiny.
  • Diamonds: scarce. Few are owned, so the MU of one more diamond is high.
  • Price reflects marginal utility (what the next unit is worth), not total utility.
  • TU from water is enormous. MU of the last litre is small.
Mission 1Evaluate

How useful is the rational model?

  • Utility cannot be measured directly. "Utils" are a teaching device; economists infer preferences from choices.
  • It depends on information: real consumers rarely know every option and price.
  • It depends on effort and stakes: people use habits and shortcuts for small, frequent buys.
  • The model still predicts the direction of many responses well, e.g. buying less when price rises.
  • More on the limits: a later lesson.

Mission 2

What demand means

  • Demand: the quantity of a good that buyers are willing and able to buy at each price, over a given time period.
  • Law of demand: as price rises, quantity demanded falls, ceteris paribus (other things equal).
  • A demand schedule lists the quantity demanded at each price. Illustrative data:
Price per cupQuantity demanded (cups per week)
6100
5200
4300
3400
2500
  • Plot it: the demand curve (D) slopes downwards.
  • Demand is the whole curve, not one point on it.
Mission 2

Why the curve slopes down

  • Diminishing marginal utility: each extra unit consumed adds less satisfaction than the one before.
  • So a buyer is willing to pay less for the 4th cup than for the 1st. To sell more, the price must fall.
  • When the price of a good falls, two effects raise quantity demanded:
  • Substitution effect: the good is now cheaper relative to other goods, so buyers switch towards it.
  • Income effect: the same money income now buys more, so real income (purchasing power) rises and buyers can afford more.
  • Say: "the price fall raises real income" — not: "the price fall raises people's income".
Quick check
?The price of bus tickets falls while the price of train tickets is unchanged. Which describes the substitution effect for a commuter?
Mission 2

Movement or shift?

  • A change in the good's own price → a movement along D.
  • A change in anything else that affects buyers → a shift of the whole curve.
  • Step 1: price falls from P1 to P2 → slide along D from point a to point b.
  • Step 2: D1 → D2 to the right: more is demanded at every price.
  • Say: "a fall in price causes an extension of demand (a rise in quantity demanded)" — not: "demand increases".
  • A price rise → contraction of demand. A shift right → increase in demand; shift left → decrease.

Mission 3

What supply means

  • Supply: the quantity that producers are willing and able to sell at each price, over a given time period.
  • Law of supply: as price rises, quantity supplied rises, ceteris paribus.
  • Why? The profit incentive: a higher price makes extra output more profitable, so firms produce more.
  • Illustrative data: a strawberry farm.
Price per kgQuantity supplied (kg per week)

  • Plot it: the supply curve (S) slopes upwards.
Mission 3

Why supply slopes up: rising marginal cost

  • Marginal cost (MC): the extra cost of producing one more unit.
  • In the short run some inputs are fixed (land, machines). Add more workers and each adds less extra output: diminishing marginal returns.
  • So each extra unit costs more to make: MC rises.
  • A firm that is a price taker sells every unit at the market price. It produces each unit whose MC is no higher than the price.
  • Illustrative data:
Unit1st2nd3rd4th5th6th
MC2346811
  • Price 6 → produce 4 units (the 5th would cost 8). Price 8 → produce 5.
  • A higher price makes higher-cost units worth producing. So the supply curve traces the MC curve.
  • Under perfect competition, the firm's supply curve is its MC curve (above average variable cost: see a later lesson).

Mission 3

Movement or shift?

  • A change in the good's own price → a movement along S.
  • A change in anything else that affects producers → a shift of the whole curve.
  • Step 1: price rises P1 → P2 → slide up along S.
  • Step 2: S1 → S2 to the right = increase in supply: more offered at every price.
  • A shift left = decrease in supply.
  • Say: "an increase in supply shifts the curve right" — not: "the supply curve shifts up". A rightward shift looks lower on the graph.
  • Say: "a rise in price causes an extension of supply" — not: "supply increases".

Mission 4

Mission 4: When a price is too high or too low, what pulls it back to where buyers and sellers agree?

Mission 4

Equilibrium: where the curves cross

  • Equilibrium: the price at which quantity demanded = quantity supplied.
  • At that price the market clears: every unit offered is bought, every buyer willing to pay it gets one.
  • No pressure for the price to change, unless something else changes.
  • Label it Pe (equilibrium price) and Qe (equilibrium quantity).
  • In this model: many buyers and many sellers, none big enough to set the price alone (a competitive market).
Mission 4

Price too high: excess supply

  • At a price above Pe, quantity supplied > quantity demanded.
  • This is excess supply, or a surplus.
  • Size of the surplus = Qs − Qd at that price.
  • Signs: unsold stock piling up, discounts, "clearance" sales.
  • Only Qd is actually sold: firms cannot sell more than buyers want.
  • Market forces: sellers cut the price to shift their stock.
Mission 4

Price too low: excess demand

  • At a price below Pe, quantity demanded > quantity supplied.
  • This is excess demand, or a shortage.
  • Size of the shortage = Qd − Qs at that price.
  • Signs: queues, waiting lists, "sold out", resale above the original price.
  • Only Qs is actually bought: buyers cannot buy what is not supplied.
  • Market forces: buyers compete, so the price is bid up.
  • A shortage is not the same as scarcity. Scarcity is always present; a shortage exists only when the price is below equilibrium.

Mission 4

Market forces: movements, not shifts

  • Surplus → price falls → quantity demanded rises and quantity supplied falls → until Qd = Qs.
  • Shortage → price rises → quantity demanded falls and quantity supplied rises → until Qd = Qs.
  • These are movements along the curves. Neither curve shifts: the price is on the axis, so it cannot shift its own curve.
  • Say: "the price falls, causing an extension of demand and a contraction of supply" — not: "demand rises and supply falls".

Mission 4Evaluate

Does a market always clear?

  • The model predicts the price moves to Pe. How fast, and whether it gets there, depends on:
  • Price flexibility: some prices are sticky. Firms with printed price lists, long contracts, or worries about customer goodwill (e.g. concert and sports tickets) may leave a shortage.
  • Information: buyers and sellers must notice the surplus or shortage before they react.
  • Time lags: farmers cannot grow more until the next harvest, so a shortage can last a season.
  • Intervention: a legal maximum or minimum price stops the market clearing.
  • And the target moves: if a curve shifts, the market chases a new equilibrium (see Mission 5).

In a shortage the short side, supply, limits sales, so a rising price can raise the quantity traded until Qd = Qs.

Mission 5

A shift starts a chain

  • Something other than the good's own price changes. One curve shifts.
  • Example: incomes rise and the good is normal. Demand shifts right, D1 → D2.
  • At the old price P1 there is now excess demand (a shortage).
  • The price is bid up. Suppliers respond by moving along S.
  • New equilibrium: higher price P2, higher quantity Q2.
  • Say: "a rise in demand causes an extension of supply" — not: "supply increases".

Mission 5

How far? It depends on elasticity

  • Same leftward shift of supply, two demand curves:
  • Price inelastic demand (drawn steeper through the starting point): large price rise, small fall in quantity.
  • Price elastic demand (drawn flatter through the same point): small price rise, large fall in quantity.
  • Slope is only a drawing guide here: elasticity is about percentage changes.
  • The same logic applies to supply: inelastic supply turns a demand shift mainly into a price change.
  • So markets with inelastic demand and supply have volatile prices. Example: farm commodities, where a harvest cannot be changed until next season.
  • Elasticity in full: (demand) (supply).
Mission 5Case

Cocoa, 2024

  • Most of the world's cocoa is grown in West Africa.
  • Late 2024: hot weather with less rain threatened crops in Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana. Another global supply deficit was expected.
  • Supply shifted left. Chocolate makers have few substitutes for cocoa beans.
  • Result: cocoa futures in London rose 66% in three months: from US$6,896 per tonne (1 Oct 2024) to US$11,441 (31 Dec 2024).
  • On the diagram: S1 → S2, price P1 → P2 up sharply, quantity Q1 → Q2 down.
Mission 5

Real markets: housing and energy

  • Housing: demand rises with population, incomes and cheaper or easier mortgages.
  • Supply is price inelastic: land, planning rules and years of building. So rising demand mostly raises prices, not the number of homes.
  • Energy: in 2022, Russia cut pipeline gas deliveries to Europe. Supply shifted left.
  • Demand for gas is inelastic in the short run (heating, power stations), so European wholesale gas prices hit record highs.
  • Over time, higher prices brought more liquefied gas by ship (extension of supply) and lower use (contraction of demand).

Mission 5Evaluate

How far can we trust the model?

  • The model predicts the direction of change well. How useful it is depends on:
  • Ceteris paribus: real markets face many changes at once, so outcomes are often double shifts with uncertain results.
  • Competition: it assumes many buyers and sellers who take the price. Firms with market power set prices.
  • Information and rationality: it assumes buyers and sellers know prices and respond to them. Habits and limited information slow responses.
  • Speed of adjustment: prices can be sticky, or overshoot, so markets can spend time out of equilibrium.
  • We never see the curves: data show only prices and quantities traded, so we must work out which curve shifted from other evidence.

Mission 6

Consumer surplus

  • Willingness to pay: the most a buyer would pay for a unit.
  • You would pay 15 for a concert ticket. It costs 10. You gain 5 that you never pay.
  • Consumer surplus = what buyers are willing to pay − what they actually pay.
  • The demand curve shows willingness to pay for each unit.
  • On a graph: the area below the demand curve and above the price, up to the quantity bought.
Mission 6

Producer surplus

  • Each seller has a minimum price they would accept for a unit: its extra cost.
  • The supply curve shows that minimum price for each unit.
  • Producer surplus = price received − minimum price the seller would accept.
  • On a graph: the area above the supply curve and below the price, up to the quantity sold.
  • Say: "producer surplus" — not: "profit". It ignores fixed costs.
Quick check
?Illustrative data: four buyers would each pay at most 12, 10, 8 and 6 for one unit of a good. The market price is 8. What is the total consumer surplus in this market?
Mission 6

Worked example: measuring the areas

  • Illustrative data. Demand meets the price axis at 16. Supply meets it at 4.
  • Equilibrium: price 12, quantity 40 units a week.
  • Both areas are triangles: ½ × base × height.
  • Consumer surplus = ½ × 40 × (16 − 12) = 80.
  • Producer surplus = ½ × 40 × (12 − 4) = 160.
  • Total (community) surplus = 80 + 160 = 240.
  • Height is always measured from the price line.
Mission 6

Total surplus is largest at equilibrium

  • Community surplus = consumer surplus + producer surplus.
  • Up to Qe, each unit is worth more to buyers than it costs to make: trading it adds surplus.
  • Beyond Qe, a unit costs more to make than buyers value it: trading it would reduce surplus.
  • So, with no market failure, a competitive market maximises total surplus at equilibrium.
  • This is allocative efficiency.
  • Lost total surplus from producing the wrong quantity is a deadweight loss.
Mission 6

Worked example: deadweight loss

  • Same market (illustrative data). Output is held at 30, not 40.
  • At Q = 30, buyers value the 30th unit at 13 (demand); it costs 10 (supply).
  • The units from 30 to 40 are not traded. The lost triangle is the deadweight loss.
  • Deadweight loss = ½ × (40 − 30) × (13 − 10) = 15.
  • Total surplus falls from 240 to 225.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. On a diagram, demand meets the price axis at 60 and supply meets it at 15. The market clears at a price of 30 and a quantity of 150. What is total surplus at equilibrium?
Mission 6

When demand or supply shifts

  • Build the new equilibrium first, then redraw the areas.
  • Supply rises (costs fall): price falls, quantity rises.
    • Consumer surplus rises: existing buyers pay less, and new buyers join.
    • Producer surplus: the price is lower, but costs are lower and more units sell. With a parallel shift it rises too.
  • Demand rises: price and quantity rise.
    • Producer surplus rises: a higher price on more units.
    • Consumer surplus is measured under the new demand curve. With a parallel shift it rises too, even though price rose.
  • Demand or supply falls: the reverse.
  • Say: "an increase in supply" (a shift) — not: "an extension of supply" (a movement along the curve).

Mission 6Evaluate

Is the biggest total surplus always best?

  • It depends on third parties: the areas count only buyers and sellers. External costs and benefits are left out.
  • It depends on who gains: a gain of 1 to a rich person counts the same as 1 to a poor person. Equity is a separate question.
  • It depends on ability to pay: willingness to pay reflects income as well as need.
  • It depends on measurement: real demand and supply curves are estimated, not observed.
Mission check

?Illustrative case: a city caps the number of taxi licences. Fewer rides take place than at the free-market equilibrium, and fares rise. Compared with the free-market equilibrium, what happens to total surplus (consumer plus producer surplus)?

The extra value buyers keep is consumer surplus. Moving surplus from buyers to sellers is a transfer; trades that never happen are a deadweight loss.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Marginal utility falls as consumption rises; this explains a downward-sloping demand curve.
  • Rational choice: keep going while MB ≥ MC; the best choice is where MB = MC.
  • Utility is maximised when MU/P is equal across goods and all income is spent.

Mission 2

  • Demand is the whole schedule or curve; its own price moves you along it, every other factor shifts it.
  • Price fall: extension of demand; non-price change: increase or decrease in demand.
  • The curve slopes down because of diminishing marginal utility.
  • A price fall also works through the substitution effect and the income effect.

Mission 3

  • Supply slopes up because a higher price makes extra output profitable; own price moves you along S, every other factor shifts it.
  • Rising marginal cost is why: a price taker supplies each unit whose MC is no higher than the price.
  • Increase in supply = shift right (it looks lower), decrease = shift left.

Mission 4

  • Equilibrium: the price where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, so the market clears.
  • Above it: surplus (Qs − Qd), the price falls. Below it: shortage (Qd − Qs), the price rises. Both adjust by movements along the curves.

Mission 5

  • A shift creates excess demand or supply at the old price; the price then changes and the market moves along the other curve to a new equilibrium.
  • Demand shifts move price and quantity the same way; supply shifts move them opposite ways. Two shifts at once leave one result uncertain.
  • The less elastic the curves, the more the price moves.

Mission 6

  • Consumer surplus: below demand, above price. Producer surplus: above supply, below price.
  • Community surplus is maximised at competitive equilibrium (MB = MC): allocative efficiency.
