Make a guess: Why do Christians sing their prayers, and why do some still use chants over 1,000 years old?

Mission 1

Why sing at all?

  • Christians have sung in worship from the start: "speaking to one another in psalms, hymns, and spiritual songs; singing and making melody in your heart to the Lord" (Ephesians 5:19).
  • Music does three jobs:
  • Praise: it gives words to joy and thanks to God.
  • Unity: many voices become one voice, one community.
  • Memory and teaching: sung words stick, and carry belief.
Mission 1

Psalms: the Bible's songbook

  • The Book of Psalms: 150 songs and prayers of ancient Israel, sung by Jesus and the first Christians.
  • They cover every mood: praise, thanks, sorrow, anger, trust.
  • "Oh come, let's sing to the LORD. Let's shout aloud to the rock of our salvation!" (Psalm 95:1)
  • At Mass: the Responsorial Psalm follows the first reading; a cantor sings verses, everyone sings the response.
  • Daily prayer: monks, nuns, priests and many lay Catholics pray the psalms in the Liturgy of the Hours.
Mission 1

Plainchant

  • Plainchant (plainsong): one unaccompanied melody line, sung in unison, with a free rhythm that follows the words.
  • Gregorian chant is the Latin plainchant of the Western Church, named after Pope Gregory I (pope 590–604). Most of it took shape later, in the 8th–9th centuries.
  • Written in neumes: square notes on a four-line stave.
  • Words come first: no harmony, no beat, nothing to distract from the prayer.
  • Significance: calm and prayerful; the same chants are sung across the world and down the centuries.
  • Used today in monasteries, cathedrals, Latin Masses and seasons such as Lent and Holy Week.
Mission 1Read the source

Musicam Sacram (1967)

After the Second Vatican Council, the Church issued Musicam Sacram ("Sacred Music"), an Instruction of the Sacred Congregation of Rites, 5 March 1967. In paraphrase:

  • Worship takes a nobler form when it is sung: prayer is expressed more beautifully, hearts are united by the union of voices, minds are raised to heavenly things, and the singing looks forward to the worship of heaven (§5).
  • The people should take part by singing, especially the acclamations and responses (§16).
  • In sung Latin services, Gregorian chant has "pride of place" (§50).

Q1 Give one reason the document gives for singing worship rather than saying it.

Show answer

It unites hearts; raises minds to heaven; foreshadows heaven's worship.

Q2 Who is meant to sing: the choir alone, or everyone?

Show answer

Mission 1

Traditional hymns

  • A hymn is a song of praise in verses (stanzas), sung by the whole congregation to a repeated tune, often with organ.
  • Hymns spread fast at the Reformation: Martin Luther's "A Mighty Fortress" (c. 1529).
  • English hymn writers: Isaac Watts, "When I survey the wondrous cross" (1707); Charles Wesley, "And can it be" (1738).
  • Catholic hymns: Thomas Aquinas's Pange lingua (1264); John Henry Newman's "Praise to the Holiest in the height" (1865).
  • At Mass, hymns are often sung at the entrance, the offertory, Communion and the end.
  • Significance: hymns teach doctrine in memorable words ("sung theology") and let everyone join in.
Mission 1

Mass settings

  • The Ordinary of the Mass: the sung texts that stay the same each week: Kyrie, Gloria, Credo, Sanctus (with Benedictus) and Agnus Dei.
  • A Mass setting is one composer's music for these texts.
  • Chant: e.g. Missa de Angelis, still sung by congregations.
  • Polyphony (several independent voice parts): Palestrina, Missa Papae Marcelli (published 1567); William Byrd, Mass for Four Voices (c. 1592–93), written for English Catholics who had to worship in secret.
  • Classical: Mozart, Coronation Mass (1779).
  • Modern congregational: James MacMillan, Mass of Blessed John Henry Newman (2010), written for Pope Benedict XVI's visit to Britain.
Mission 1

Why Mass settings matter

  • Participation: a simple setting lets the whole congregation sing the Mass, as Vatican II asked.
  • Beauty: Catholics believe beauty can lead people towards God; great settings honour the Eucharist.
  • Unity: one setting can be sung by a whole diocese, or at a papal Mass.
  • Influence beyond church: Mass settings shaped Western music (harmony, choirs, the orchestra); many are now performed in concert halls.
  • A tension: an elaborate choral setting can leave the people listening rather than singing; parishes balance choir and congregation.
Mission 1

Four acclamations

  • Gloria: begins with the angels' song at Jesus' birth, "Glory to God in the highest" (Luke 2:14). Sung on Sundays (not in Advent or Lent) and feasts. Praise of Father, Son and Spirit.
  • Alleluia: Hebrew for "praise the LORD". Greets Christ present in the Gospel reading. Not sung in Lent; another acclamation replaces it.
  • Sanctus ("Holy"): the angels' cry, "Holy, holy, holy" (Isaiah 6:3), with the crowd's welcome, "Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord! Hosanna in the highest!" (Matthew 21:9). Earth joins heaven's worship.
  • Mystery of Faith: sung after the consecration. The people proclaim Christ's death and resurrection until he comes again (1 Corinthians 11:26).
  • Significance: these are the people's parts: short, sung shouts of faith.

Mission 1Diversity

Who sings what, and where?

  • Catholic: chant, hymns, Mass settings, cantor and organ; Responsorial Psalm every Sunday.
  • Orthodox: the whole Liturgy is sung, usually by an unaccompanied choir; traditionally no instruments.
  • Anglican: cathedral choirs sing Choral Evensong; psalms in Anglican chant.
  • Presbyterian: metrical psalms and hymns; some congregations sing psalms only.
  • Quakers: usually no set music; silence is the setting for worship.
Mission 1Evaluate

"Traditional music is the best music for worship."

  • Reasons for: tested by centuries; deep, biblical words; links worshippers to the whole Church, past and present; calm, not about performers.
  • Also for: chant has "pride of place" in Latin liturgy (Musicam Sacram §50).
  • Reasons against: Latin chant and old hymn language can be hard to understand; young people may find it remote; the Bible calls for "a new song" (Psalm 96:1).
  • Also against: Musicam Sacram also values music that lets the people take part.
  • Another view: "best" depends on the occasion: chant at a requiem, a lively hymn at a school Mass.
  • Judge it by: Does it help everyone pray and take part? Do the words express the Church's beliefs? Does it suit the occasion?

Mission 2

What counts as "contemporary"?

  • Contemporary worship music: songs written from the 1960s onwards in current musical styles.
  • Typical features: simple, repeated choruses; everyday language; guitars, keyboard, drums; words on a screen; a worship band leading.
  • Worship songs often speak directly to God ("you", "I", "we").
  • Modern hymns keep verses and doctrine but use modern tunes, e.g. Keith Getty and Stuart Townend, "In Christ Alone" (2001).
  • Also contemporary, but quiet: Taizé chants from the ecumenical Taizé community in France: short lines repeated many times as a prayer.
Mission 2

Graham Kendrick

  • Graham Kendrick (born 1950): English songwriter, from a Baptist background; one of the pioneers of British worship songs.
  • "The Servant King" (1983), also known as "From heaven you came": Jesus leaves heaven to serve, and suffers in Gethsemane; it echoes Philippians 2:6–8.
  • "Meekness and Majesty" (1986): Jesus fully God and fully human.
  • "Shine, Jesus, Shine" (1987): Christ as light of the world, asked to fill the nation.
  • Co-founded March for Jesus (1987): Christians singing in city streets as public witness.
  • His songs are in Catholic and Protestant hymn books and sung in schools.
Mission 2

Casting Crowns

  • Casting Crowns: American Christian rock band formed in 1999 by Mark Hall, a youth pastor, with students from his church.
  • Hall kept working as a youth pastor while the band toured: the songs come from church life.
  • "Who Am I": wonder that the God of the universe knows and loves each person (compare Psalm 8:4).
  • "Praise You in This Storm": trusting and praising God in suffering, even when prayers seem unanswered.
  • "If We Are the Body": challenges churches that ignore outsiders; Christians as the Body of Christ must welcome them.
  • Heard more on Christian radio, at concerts and at youth events than sung at Mass.

Mission 2

Charismatic worship and tongues

  • Charismatic and Pentecostal worship stresses the gifts (charismata) of the Holy Spirit, as at Pentecost (Acts 2:4).
  • Long "times of worship": singing flows from song to song; people raise hands, clap, pray aloud.
  • Catholics too: the Catholic Charismatic Renewal began in 1967 and has prayer groups in many parishes.
Mission 2Read the source

Catechism 2641

The Catechism (CCC 2641) begins by quoting Paul: "speaking to one another in psalms, hymns, and spiritual songs; singing and making melody in your heart to the Lord" (Ephesians 5:19). Then, in paraphrase:

  • The first Christians read the psalms in a new way, singing in them the mystery of Christ.
  • Filled with the Spirit, they also wrote new hymns about what God had done in Jesus: his Incarnation, death, resurrection and ascension.
  • Praise of God (doxology) springs from wonder at all God has done to save.

Show answer

Christ: his Incarnation, death, resurrection and ascension.

  • Link: "Sing to the LORD a new song!" (Psalm 96:1).
Mission 2

Where and when it is used

  • Protestant churches: in many evangelical, charismatic and Pentecostal churches, the band-led time of worship is the heart of the service.
  • Catholic parishes: often at youth and school Masses, Confirmation, prayer groups; worship songs sit alongside hymns and the Responsorial Psalm.
  • Other settings: youth festivals and large Catholic youth events, Christian concerts and radio, school assemblies, street marches, streaming at home.
  • Why: to reach young people, to let people pray in their own musical language, to share faith with outsiders.
Mission 2Diversity

The debates

  • For contemporary music: everyday language people understand; emotional and personal; draws young people and newcomers; the Bible calls for "a new song" (Psalm 96:1); early Christians wrote new hymns.
  • The Catechism makes that last point: the first Christians composed new hymns about Christ (CCC 2641).
  • Against (or cautious): some lyrics are thin on doctrine, or focus on "I" more than "we"; repetition can feel shallow; can feel like a concert, with the band performing; can push out the Church's older treasury of music.
  • Some Catholic writers, including Joseph Ratzinger (later Pope Benedict XVI) in The Spirit of the Liturgy (2000), argued that some pop and rock styles do not suit the liturgy.
  • Orthodox churches keep unaccompanied singing; Quakers often use no music at all.
  • Most churches mix old and new.

Mission 2Evaluate

"Contemporary worship songs are better than traditional hymns for worship today."

  • Reasons for: understood by everyone; personal and heartfelt; attract young people and newcomers; a "new song" (Psalm 96:1); continues what the first Christians did, who wrote new hymns.
  • Also for: CCC 2641 says the first Christians composed new hymns about Christ.
  • Reasons against: traditional hymns carry richer doctrine in many verses; tested over centuries; shared by all generations; less dependent on a band's skill; less risk of performance.
  • Another view: both are needed; the question is which helps this congregation pray on this occasion.
  • Judge it by: Do the words express Christian belief accurately? Can the whole congregation join in? Does it suit the occasion?

Mission 3

The shape of the Mass

  • Mass: the Catholic celebration of the Eucharist. Every Mass in the world has the same four-part shape.
  • Introductory Rites: gather, confess sin, praise God.
  • Liturgy of the Word: God speaks through the Bible.
  • Liturgy of the Eucharist: bread and wine offered, consecrated and received.
  • Concluding Rites: blessing and sending out.
Mission 3

Gathering and the Word

  • Introductory Rites: entrance hymn and procession; sign of the cross; Penitential Act ("Lord, have mercy"); Gloria on Sundays (not in Advent or Lent); the Collect (opening prayer).
  • Liturgy of the Word: First Reading (usually the Old Testament); Responsorial Psalm; Second Reading (a New Testament letter, on Sundays).
  • Gospel Acclamation (Alleluia) as all stand; the Gospel, read by a priest or deacon.
  • Homily: the priest explains the readings.
  • Creed (on Sundays) and Prayer of the Faithful (bidding prayers for the Church and the world).
Mission 3

The Liturgy of the Eucharist

  • Preparation of the Gifts: bread and wine brought to the altar; collection.
  • Eucharistic Prayer: "Lift up your hearts"; the Sanctus ("Holy, holy, holy"); the priest asks the Holy Spirit to make the gifts holy.
  • Consecration: the priest speaks Jesus' Words of Institution over the bread and wine; he raises each; bells may ring; people kneel.
  • Mystery of Faith: all proclaim Christ's death and resurrection; the prayer ends with the Great Amen.
  • Communion Rite: Lord's Prayer; sign of peace; the host is broken while all sing the Lamb of God; Holy Communion.
  • Concluding Rites: blessing; dismissal: "Go forth, the Mass is ended."

Mission 3

"Source and summit"

  • The Second Vatican Council calls the Eucharist the source and summit of the whole Christian life (Lumen Gentium 11; CCC 1324).
  • Source: the Church's life and grace flow from it, as a river flows from a spring.
  • Summit: everything the Church does leads to it, as a path leads to a mountain top.
  • So Sunday Mass is an obligation for Catholics: the week flows from it and back to it.
  • Other denominations: A later screen.
Mission 3Read the source

Lumen Gentium 7 and 11

Lumen Gentium (Second Vatican Council, 1964), paraphrase:

  • LG 7: really sharing in the Lord's body in the breaking of the bread, we are lifted into communion with Christ and with one another. As Paul says, because there is one bread, we who are many are one body (1 Corinthians 10:17).
  • LG 11: taking part in the Eucharistic sacrifice, the "source and summit" of the whole Christian life, the faithful offer Christ, and themselves with him, to God.

Mission 3

The Real Presence

  • Catholics believe that at the consecration the bread and wine become the Body and Blood of Christ: the Real Presence.
  • Transubstantiation: the substance (what it really is) changes; the appearances (taste, look, chemistry) of bread and wine remain.
  • Aquinas: "the whole substance of the bread is changed into the whole substance of Christ's body" (Summa Theologiae III, q. 75, a. 4).
  • "I am the living bread which came down out of heaven… the bread which I will give for the life of the world is my flesh." (John 6:51)
  • Catholics believe Christ remains present after Mass: hosts are kept in the tabernacle, a lamp burns beside it, and Catholics genuflect.
  • So Catholics adore Christ in the Blessed Sacrament outside Mass.
Mission 3

Words, Lamb, presence, sacrifice

  • Words of Institution: the priest repeats Jesus' words: "This is my body which is given for you" (Luke 22:19). He acts in the person of Christ.
  • Real Presence: Catholics believe that through these words, by the Spirit's power, Christ becomes present.
  • Sacrifice of the Mass: Catholics believe the body "given" and the blood "poured out" are the one sacrifice of Calvary, made present now in an unbloody way. It is not a new or repeated sacrifice.
  • Agnus Dei: as the host is broken, all sing to the Lamb of God who takes away the sins of the world: "Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!" (John 1:29). Jesus is the new Passover lamb (1 Corinthians 5:7).
  • The chain: words → presence → sacrifice → Lamb received in Communion.
  • Many Protestants object: Christ was offered "once for all" (Hebrews 10:10). Catholics reply: yes, and the Mass makes that one offering present.

Mission 3

The acclamations

  • Gloria: a hymn of praise opening with the angels' song at Jesus' birth: "Glory to God in the highest, on earth peace" (Luke 2:14).
  • Alleluia: Hebrew for "Praise the LORD". It greets Christ before the Gospel; in Lent it is replaced by another acclamation.
  • Sanctus: "Holy, holy, holy" (Isaiah 6:3) joined with the crowd's "Hosanna… Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord!" (Matthew 21:9). Earth joins heaven's praise before the consecration.
  • Mystery of Faith: after the consecration, the people proclaim Christ's death and resurrection until he comes, echoing 1 Corinthians 11:26.
  • Each acclamation lets the whole congregation take part, often sung.
Mission 3

Mass settings

  • A Mass setting is music for the fixed parts of the Mass: Kyrie, Gloria, Creed, Sanctus, Agnus Dei (and today the Mystery of Faith).
  • Plainchant (Gregorian chant): single melody in Latin, e.g. the Missa de Angelis.
  • Polyphony: many voices woven together, e.g. Palestrina's Missa Papae Marcelli (published 1567).
  • Classical: orchestra and soloists, e.g. Mozart's Coronation Mass (1779).
  • Modern parish settings in English, simple enough for all to sing, e.g. James MacMillan's St Anne's Mass.
  • Significance: music raises hearts to God and helps "full and active participation" (Vatican II). Influence: many settings are now concert works; some say grand settings make the people listeners instead of singers.

Mission 3Diversity

The Eucharist and worship in other churches

  • Orthodox: the Divine Liturgy; the bread and wine truly become Christ's Body and Blood, a mystery not explained by "transubstantiation".
  • Anglican: Holy Communion, weekly in many parishes; views range from real presence to spiritual presence.
  • Lutheran: Christ truly present "in, with and under" the bread and wine.
  • Methodist, Presbyterian: Christ spiritually present; the Lord's Supper monthly or a few times a year.
  • Baptist, Pentecostal, many evangelicals: a memorial ("Do this in memory of me"); worship centres on Bible preaching.
  • Less structured evangelical worship: a band leads songs; prayer is spontaneous; long sermon; room for the Spirit's gifts. They value freedom and personal response; Catholics value a liturgy shared by the whole Church in every place.
Mission 3Evaluate

"Catholics should go to Mass every Sunday."

  • Claim: weekly Mass should be a duty for every Catholic.
  • For: the Church teaches that the Eucharist is the source and summit of Christian life (all its life flows from it and leads to it). Sunday is the day of the resurrection. The Church is one body and cannot be lived alone: "not forsaking our own assembling together" (Hebrews 10:25). The Church's law asks it.
  • Against: love cannot be forced; duty can make worship routine. God is also met in prayer, creation and service of others. Illness, work or distance can prevent it. Most Protestants have no such rule, and stress a free, personal response.
  • Judge it by: Is a duty a help or a burden? Can faith grow without a community? What makes worship real?

Wherever it is celebrated, the Mass has one shape and one centre: Christ's sacrifice made present and received.

Mission 4

One God, not three

  • Christians are monotheists, like Jews and Muslims.
  • They hold to the Jewish Shema, which Jesus himself quoted (Mark 12:29): "Hear, Israel: The LORD is our God. The LORD is one." (Deuteronomy 6:4)
  • So the Trinity (Latin trinitas, "threeness": God as three-in-one) never means three gods.
  • The word "Trinity" is not in the Bible. It names a belief Christians find in the Bible.
  • This is the oneness of God: one being, one will, one God.
Mission 4

Three Persons, one God

  • Most Christians believe in one God in three Persons: Father, Son and Holy Spirit.
  • Each Person is fully God, not a third of God.
  • The three are distinct (not the same as each other) but not separate (not three beings).
  • All three are equal and eternal. None was made.
  • Not three gods. Not one God wearing three masks in turn.
Mission 4

Each Person

  • Trinitarian Christians describe each Person like this:
  • Father: Creator and source of all; loves and cares for people.
  • Son: Jesus Christ, God become human (the Incarnation); saves humanity.
  • Holy Spirit: God present and active in the world now; guides, strengthens and gives life.

Mission 4

More roots in scripture

  • Jesus sends his followers to baptise "in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 28:19): one name, three Persons.
  • "God sent out the Spirit of his Son into your hearts, crying, 'Abba, Father!'" (Galatians 4:6): Father, Son and Spirit in one verse.
  • So the doctrine grew from texts like these, then was defined by the Church.
Mission 4

The Nicene Creed

  • A priest, Arius (c. 250–336), taught that the Son was created by the Father.
  • The Council of Nicaea (325) rejected this. The Son is "Begotten, not made, Being of one substance with the Father" (Nicene Creed, Book of Common Prayer).
  • Of one substance (Greek homoousios): the same divine being. Catholics say "consubstantial with the Father" at Mass.
  • The Council of Constantinople (381) added more on the Spirit: "the Lord and giver of life", worshipped with the Father and the Son.
  • The Nicene Creed is built in three parts: Father, Son, Holy Spirit.
Quick check

Mission 4

The Trinity in worship and life

  • Baptism is "in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit".
  • Sign of the cross (Catholic, Orthodox, many Anglicans and Lutherans) traces the same words.
  • Prayer is often to the Father, through the Son, in the Spirit. Many hymns and blessings end with all three ("Glory be to the Father…").
  • Catholics: the Mass begins with the sign of the cross; the Creed is said on Sundays; the Catechism calls the Trinity the central mystery of Christian faith and life (CCC 234).
  • Trinity Sunday, the Sunday after Pentecost in Western churches, celebrates the belief.
  • Daily life: God is a communion of love, so Christians aim to live in loving community; they rely on the Spirit's help.
Mission 4Evaluate

"The Trinity is a contradiction."

  • Reasons for: one cannot equal three. The word "Trinity" is not in the Bible. Deuteronomy 6:4 stresses that God is one. Unitarians and Christadelphians reject it. Every everyday analogy seems to fail.
  • Reasons against: God is one in being and three in Persons: "one" and "three" count different things, so there is no logical contradiction. Scripture treats Father, Son and Spirit as God (Matthew 28:19; John 10:30; Galatians 4:6). Christians call it a mystery: beyond full understanding, but not against reason.
  • Experience: Christians say they meet God as above them (Father), with them (Son) and within them (Spirit).
  • Judge it by: Do "one" and "three" count the same thing? Must a belief be fully understood to be reasonable? Should belief rest on the Bible alone or also on the Church's councils?
Mission check

One being, three Persons, all at once: that is what keeps the Trinity from being three gods or one God in disguise.

Mission 5

One God, three Persons

  • "Hear, Israel: The LORD is our God. The LORD is one." (Deuteronomy 6:4) Catholics keep this: there is one God.
  • That one God is three Persons: Father, Son and Holy Spirit. Not three gods; not one Person wearing three masks.
  • Each Person is fully God; none is a part, and none came first.
  • The Persons are distinct by their relationships: the Father begets the Son; the Son is begotten; the Spirit proceeds.
  • The Catechism calls the Trinity the central mystery of Christian faith (CCC 234, paraphrase).

The Council of Nicaea (325)

  • A priest, Arius (c. 250–336), taught that the Son was the first and greatest thing God made: "there was when he was not".
  • Emperor Constantine called the bishops to Nicaea (in modern Turkey) in 325.
  • The Council taught that the Son is "Begotten, not made, Being of one substance with the Father" (Nicene Creed, Book of Common Prayer).
  • Of one substance (Greek homoousios): the same divine being, not a lesser god. At Mass Catholics say "consubstantial with the Father".
  • Athanasius (c. 296–373), later bishop of Alexandria, defended Nicaea for decades.
  • Nicaea said little about the Spirit: its creed said only "And in the Holy Spirit".
Mission 5

The Council of Constantinople (381)

  • Some still denied that the Holy Spirit is fully God.
  • Emperor Theodosius I called the Council of Constantinople in 381.
  • It expanded the creed: the Spirit is "The Lord and giver of life… Who with the Father and the Son together is worshipped and glorified" (Book of Common Prayer).
  • This Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed is the "Nicene Creed" said at Sunday Mass today.
  • Later, the Latin West added that the Spirit proceeds from the Father "and the Son" (Filioque). Catholics say this; the Orthodox Churches do not, and it remains a point of difference.
  • Development: Catholics see the councils as clarifying what scripture already teaches, not inventing new beliefs.
Mission 5

Why councils carry authority

  • The Magisterium: the teaching authority of the Pope and the bishops in communion with him.
  • An ecumenical council gathers bishops from the whole Church; its solemn teaching on faith is a high exercise of that authority.
  • Catholics believe the Holy Spirit guides such teaching: "he will guide you into all truth" (John 16:13).
  • Model: the apostles and elders met in Jerusalem to settle a dispute (Acts 15).
  • Significance: Nicaea and Constantinople fixed the shape of belief in the Trinity; their creed unites Catholics, Orthodox and most Protestants.

Mission 5

Reading Genesis 1:1–3 with the Trinity

"In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth. The earth was formless and empty. Darkness was on the surface of the deep and God's Spirit was hovering over the surface of the waters. God said, 'Let there be light,' and there was light." (Genesis 1:1–3)

  • The Father: the Creator, the source ("God created").
  • The Spirit: "hovering over the waters", bringing life and order.
  • The Word (Son): God speaks. John 1:1–3 says all things were made through the Word.
  • "Let's make man in our image" (Genesis 1:26): many Church Fathers heard all three Persons here.
  • Jewish readers do not read these verses as a Trinity; Catholics read the Old Testament in the light of the New.
  • Significance: creation is the work of the whole Trinity, an overflow of love.
Mission 5Read the source

Augustine: lover, beloved, love

St Augustine of Hippo (354–430), De Trinitate ("On the Trinity", c. 400–420), Book VIII, chapter 10:

> "Behold, then, there are three things: he that loves, and that which is loved, and love." (Haddan translation, 1873)

  • Love involves three: one who loves (the lover), one who is loved (the beloved), and the love itself. Catholic teaching applies this to the Trinity: the Father as lover, the Son as beloved, the Holy Spirit as the love between them (Augustine calls the Spirit the love of Father and Son in De Trinitate XV.17).
  • So, Catholics believe, God can be love from all eternity, before creating anything.
  • Augustine: "thou dost see the Trinity if thou seest love" (De Trinitate VIII.8.12).
  • He treats this as a starting-point, not a full explanation; he goes on to look for the Trinity's image in the human mind.

Mission 5

Catherine LaCugna: God for us

  • Catherine Mowry LaCugna (1952–1997), American Catholic theologian at the University of Notre Dame; God for Us: The Trinity and Christian Life (1991).
  • She called the Trinity "a practical doctrine with radical consequences for Christian life".
  • We know God as Trinity through what God has done for us: the Father sends the Son, the Spirit draws people in. God's inner life and God's life with us are not two separate stories.
  • God's very being is communion: persons in loving relationship.
  • She argued Western theology after Augustine had often looked at God's inner life on its own, which made the Trinity seem remote (paraphrase).
  • Significance: Christians should live in communion: inclusive, just, loving relationships.
Mission 5Diversity

Augustine and LaCugna compared

  • Same: both are Catholic; both say God is love; both say God's love is intimate: it reaches into the human heart.
  • Augustine on intimacy: God was "more inward to me than my most inward part" (Confessions III.6, Pusey translation).
  • Different starting-point: Augustine looks inward: love and the human mind as images of the Trinity. LaCugna looks at salvation history: what God has done for us.
  • Different focus: Augustine dwells on God's eternal inner life; LaCugna on God's life shared with us in community.
  • LaCugna's critique: Augustine's inward approach risked making the Trinity seem far from everyday life. Defenders reply that Augustine's love analogy is exactly about intimacy.
Mission 5

The Trinity in Catholic life

  • Baptism "in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 28:19).
  • Mass: begins with the Sign of the Cross; the Gloria and Creed praise all three; the Eucharistic Prayer ends "Through him, and with him, and in him".
  • Prayer: the Glory Be; most prayers go to the Father, through the Son, in the Spirit.
  • Life: Catholics are called to love as the Trinity loves: as a community, not alone.
  • Before creation: Catholics believe the Father, Son and Spirit have loved one another from all eternity, so God did not need the world in order to love; creation is a free gift.
  • Deus Caritas Est 19 (Benedict XVI, 2005, paraphrase): quoting Augustine, "if you see charity, you see the Trinity". The Spirit changes believers' hearts so they love as Christ loved. The Church's charity and its mission (spreading the Gospel) are the Spirit's work.
  • Compare Acts 1:8: the Spirit gives power to be Jesus' witnesses.
Mission 5Evaluate

"Human love is a good way to understand the Trinity."

  • Reasons for: "God is love" (1 John 4:8); love shared by Father, Son and Spirit shows how God could love before anything was made; it makes the mystery personal.
  • Also for: Augustine's lover–beloved–love shows how God can be love eternally.
  • Also for: it explains why the Trinity matters in daily life (Deus Caritas Est 19).
  • Reasons against: human lovers are two separate beings, but the Persons are one God (risk of three gods); love as a "thing" can make the Spirit sound less than a Person; every analogy falls short of a mystery.
  • Another view: start from what God has done in Jesus and the Spirit, not from human love.
  • This is LaCugna's approach.
  • Judge it by: Does the analogy keep both oneness and three Persons? Does it fit scripture and the Creed? Does it help believers pray and live?
Mission check

?A student says: "Before creation God must have been lonely, so God made the world in order to have someone to love." How would Catholic teaching on the Trinity most likely respond?

Because God is a Trinity, love is eternal: God did not need the world, but made it out of love.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Christians sing to praise God, unite as one body, and remember what they believe: psalms, plainchant and hymns are the traditional forms.
  • Mass settings set the Ordinary to music; the people's acclamations (Gloria, Alleluia, Sanctus, Mystery of Faith) are short sung professions of faith.

Mission 2

  • Contemporary worship music uses current styles, simple repeated choruses and bands; modern hymns keep doctrine with new tunes.

Mission 3

  • The Mass: Introductory Rites → Liturgy of the Word → Liturgy of the Eucharist → Concluding Rites.
  • At the consecration Catholics believe Christ becomes really present (transubstantiation), and the one sacrifice of Calvary is made present, not repeated.
  • The Eucharist is the "source and summit" of Christian life (LG 11; LG 7: it makes the Church one body).

Mission 4

  • Christians believe in one God in three Persons: Father, Son and Holy Spirit, each fully God, distinct but not separate.
  • At Jesus' baptism (Matthew 3:16–17) all three Persons are present.
  • The Nicene Creed (Nicaea, 325) says the Son is "of one substance" with the Father.

Mission 5

  • One God in three Persons, rooted in Deuteronomy 6:4 and Jesus' baptism (Matthew 3:16–17); Nicaea (325) taught the Son is "of one substance" with the Father.
  • Constantinople (381) taught the Spirit's full divinity; the creed said at Mass comes from both councils.
  • Augustine starts from the three of love (lover, beloved, love); Catholic teaching applies it to the Trinity: God is love from all eternity.
