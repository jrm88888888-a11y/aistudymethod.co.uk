Mission 1

Why end the play with a phone call, not an arrest?

Make a guess: Why end the play with a phone call, not an arrest?

An Inspector Calls · structureScreen 1

One room, one evening

This lesson doesn't quote the play, which is still in copyright: learn your key quotations from your own copy.

  • Setting: the Birlings' dining room in Brumley, an industrial city in the North Midlands, on an evening in spring 1912.
  • Three acts, with time continuous: each act picks up the moment the last one stopped.
  • The family is celebrating Sheila's engagement to Gerald Croft when Inspector Goole arrives to ask about the death of a young woman, Eva Smith.
An Inspector Calls · structureScreen 2

How your exam asks this

  • Paper 1 Section B: one essay from a choice of two, each opening with a short quotation; 40 marks (16 AO1, 16 AO3 context, 8 AO4).
  • Closed book. Essay practice is for class.
An Inspector Calls · structureScreen 3

Two borrowed shapes

  • Well-made play: a tightly built plot in which hidden secrets come out one by one, building to a climax at the end of each act.
  • Whodunnit: a detective arrives, questions suspects one at a time and pieces together a crime.
  • Priestley twists both. The Inspector already seems to know the answers, and there is no single culprit. The question changes from who did it to who is responsible.
An Inspector Calls · structureScreen 4

Cliffhangers and the final twist

  • Act 1 ends as Sheila realises Gerald knew the girl, and the Inspector reappears to continue.
  • Act 2 ends as Eric walks in, just after his mother has demanded that the father of Eva's child be punished. The audience has guessed before she has: dramatic irony.
  • Act 3: two phone calls suggest the whole evening was a hoax. Then a final call: a girl has just died and an inspector is on his way.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Why end the play with a phone call, not an arrest?

An arrest would close the case and let the audience off. The phone call reopens it just after the older Birlings and Gerald have decided nothing happened, so the structure tests who has learned the Inspector's lesson and leaves the audience to judge.

Mission 2

Who, or what, is Inspector Goole?

Make a guess: Who, or what, is Inspector Goole?

An Inspector Calls · the InspectorScreen 1

The arrival

  • The doorbell rings in Act 1 straight after Birling has lectured Gerald and Eric that each person should put himself and his family first. The Inspector arrives to challenge exactly that idea.
  • Priestley's stage directions describe the lighting as soft and warm at first, then harsher and brighter once the Inspector arrives.
  • They make him seem weighty and determined, and he fixes each person with a long look before speaking.
An Inspector Calls · the InspectorScreen 2

How he controls the evening

  • He takes the story in order, questioning each person separately, and shows the photograph to one person at a time.
  • He is not impressed by status: Birling's mention of his friendship with the Chief Constable has no effect.
  • He seems to know the answers before he asks, and he decides who leaves and who stays.
  • He interrupts and contradicts the Birlings, reversing the usual power of class.
An Inspector Calls · the InspectorScreen 3

Readings of the Inspector

  • Priestley's mouthpiece: he voices the playwright's socialist views on shared responsibility.
  • A conscience or judge: like a figure in a medieval morality play, he makes each character face what they did.
  • Something supernatural: his name sounds like ghoul, and he seems to know events in advance.
  • Gerald later finds no Inspector Goole is on the force. Priestley never explains him.
An Inspector Calls · the InspectorScreen 4

His last speech

  • Before he leaves in Act 3, the Inspector tells the family that Eva is gone, but there are countless others like her whose lives are tied to theirs.
  • He warns that if people will not learn they are responsible for each other, they will learn it through suffering.
  • A 1945 audience had lived through two world wars, so they hear the warning as already come true.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Who, or what, is Inspector Goole?

Priestley never tells us. The strongest answers weigh the readings (mouthpiece, conscience, something supernatural) and show that his job in the play is the same whichever is true: to make the characters and the audience face their responsibility for Eva Smith.

Mission 3

The parents hear everything. Why do they learn nothing?

Make a guess: The parents hear everything. Why do they learn nothing?

An Inspector Calls · Mr and Mrs BirlingScreen 1

Arthur Birling

  • Priestley's stage directions present him as a large, pompous man in his mid-fifties whose speech gives away that he is not from the upper class.
  • A wealthy manufacturer, former Lord Mayor and local magistrate, he hopes for a knighthood.
  • He sees Sheila's engagement to Gerald as good for business: the Crofts' firm is a larger, longer-established rival.
  • In Act 1 he admits sacking Eva in 1910 for leading a strike for higher pay, and insists he was right.
An Inspector Calls · Mr and Mrs BirlingScreen 2

Dramatic irony in Birling's speeches

  • At dinner in Act 1, Birling confidently predicts that there will be no war, that strikes will fade away, and that the Titanic, about to sail, can never sink.
  • The audience in 1945 knows the Titanic sank in April 1912 and two world wars followed.
  • This dramatic irony makes him look foolish, so we distrust his views on responsibility too.
An Inspector Calls · Mr and Mrs BirlingScreen 3

Sybil Birling

  • Stage directions present her as an aloof woman from a higher social class than her husband. She corrects his manners at dinner.
  • She chairs the Brumley Women's Charity Organisation. In Act 2 she admits she persuaded its committee to refuse Eva's appeal two weeks earlier, partly because the pregnant girl had called herself Mrs Birling.
  • She insists the father of the child is to blame, without realising it is her son.
An Inspector Calls · Mr and Mrs BirlingScreen 4

After the Inspector leaves

  • In Act 3 Birling's first worry is scandal and his knighthood; he offers to pay thousands to put things right.
  • When the phone calls suggest a hoax, the parents are relieved. Mrs Birling praises how firmly she stood up to the Inspector, and Birling mocks the young ones for taking it so hard.
  • Then the final call comes.

Mission checkScreen 8

?The parents hear everything. Why do they learn nothing?

Priestley presents the parents as protected by their money and position. Birling cares about his knighthood and Mrs Birling about her reputation, so once the hoax seems proven they go back to their old views. That refusal is what Priestley wants his audience to reject.

Mission 4

Do Sheila and Eric really change, or just feel guilty?

Make a guess: Do Sheila and Eric really change, or just feel guilty?

An Inspector Calls · Sheila and EricScreen 1

Sheila at the start

  • Priestley introduces Sheila as an attractive, happy young woman in her early twenties, thrilled by the engagement.
  • Even at dinner she teases Gerald about the previous summer, when he hardly came near her: a hint of what is to come.
  • In Act 1 she admits she had Eva sacked from Milwards, a department store, in early 1911. She was jealous and in a bad temper, and threatened that her mother would close their account.
An Inspector Calls · Sheila and EricScreen 2

Sheila's journey

  • She is upset straight away and accepts her share of the blame.
  • She is first to see how the Inspector works, and she warns her mother not to build barriers he will only break down.
  • In Act 2 she hands back Gerald's ring.
  • In Act 3, after the phone calls, she still refuses to pretend nothing happened.
An Inspector Calls · Sheila and EricScreen 3

Eric

  • In his early twenties, he seems awkward and uncomfortable, sometimes shy and sometimes pushy. Hints in Act 1 suggest he drinks heavily.
  • In Act 3 he admits he met Eva at the Palace bar in November 1911. Drunk, he forced his way into her lodgings; many readers see what followed as rape.
  • She became pregnant. He stole about £50 from his father's office to help her, but she refused it once she knew it was stolen.
An Inspector Calls · Sheila and EricScreen 4

The younger generation

  • The Inspector notes that the young are more open to change.
  • At the end, Sheila and Eric still feel responsible. Their parents, and Gerald, relax.
  • Many readers argue Priestley places his hope in the young: a 1945 audience included a generation that had fought a war and would rebuild Britain.
  • Some readers find Eric's change less convincing, given what he did.

Mission 5

Gerald was kindest to Eva. Does that make him less guilty?

Make a guess: Gerald was kindest to Eva. Does that make him less guilty?

An Inspector Calls · Gerald and EvaScreen 1

Gerald Croft

  • Priestley presents him as a polished, upper-class man of about thirty, comfortable in any company.
  • He is the son of Sir George and Lady Croft, whose firm, Crofts Limited, is a larger, longer-established rival to Birling's.
  • In Act 1 he backs Birling: sacking Eva was the only sensible choice.
An Inspector Calls · Gerald and EvaScreen 2

Gerald and Daisy Renton

  • In Act 2 Gerald admits he met Eva, now calling herself Daisy Renton, at the bar of the Palace Variety Theatre in March 1911.
  • He rescued her from the unwanted attention of Alderman Meggarty, then let her stay in a friend's empty rooms. She became his mistress.
  • He ended it in early September 1911, giving her money to live on. He is clearly moved, and leaves for a walk.
An Inspector Calls · Gerald and EvaScreen 3

Gerald in Act 3

  • He returns with news: a police sergeant has told him there is no Inspector Goole on the force.
  • He suggests there may have been different girls and different photographs, and phones the Infirmary: no recent suicide.
  • Relieved, he offers Sheila the ring again. She is not ready to take it.
An Inspector Calls · Gerald and EvaScreen 4

Eva Smith, the absent centre

  • Eva never appears: we know her only through others' accounts and a photograph we never see.
  • Her story links every character: Birling (sacked, 1910), Sheila (Milwards, 1911), Gerald (as Daisy Renton, 1911), Eric (late 1911), Mrs Birling (two weeks before).
  • Many readers see her names as symbolic: Eva suggests Eve, the first woman; Smith is one of the commonest surnames. She stands for countless working women.

?Gerald was kindest to Eva. Does that make him less guilty?

Gerald gave Eva the happiest months the play describes, and he is moved by her death. But he used his money and power over her, ended it on his terms and, in Act 3, rushes to prove it was all a hoax. The strongest answers weigh both sides.

Mission 6

Who killed Eva Smith?

Make a guess: Who killed Eva Smith?

An Inspector Calls · responsibility and classScreen 1

Two views of society

  • Individualism: in Act 1 Birling argues that a man's duty is to himself and his own family, and scoffs at the idea that people share a duty to one another. This fits capitalism: profit and self-interest come first.
  • Collective responsibility: the Inspector argues that we are all linked and responsible for each other. This fits socialism, which Priestley supported.
An Inspector Calls · responsibility and classScreen 2

The chain of events

  • Each action alone might seem small: a sacking, a complaint, an affair ended, a drunken night, a refused appeal.
  • Together they leave Eva with no job, no home, no money and no help.
  • In Act 3 the Inspector tells them each helped to bring about her death. Priestley moves the question from one culprit to shared responsibility.
An Inspector Calls · responsibility and classScreen 3

Class and power

  • Eva is working class, with no savings, union strength or real state help: in 1912 there was no welfare state.
  • The Birlings use power without noticing: Birling's position as employer and magistrate, Sheila's family account at Milwards, Gerald's money, Mrs Birling's control of charity.
  • Gender adds to it: as a young working woman, Eva is exposed to men like Meggarty, Gerald and Eric.

?Who killed Eva Smith?

Priestley gives no single culprit. Each character's act closes another door, and together they leave Eva with nothing. By making responsibility shared, he argues for a society where people with power look after those without it.

Mission 7

Why set a play written in 1944–45 back in 1912?

Make a guess: Why set a play written in 1944–45 back in 1912?

An Inspector Calls · 1912 and 1945Screen 1

Two dates

  • Set: an evening in spring 1912, just before the Titanic sails.
  • Written: 1944–45, as the Second World War was ending.
  • First performed: in Moscow in 1945, because no suitable British theatre was free; London followed in 1946.
  • Between the two dates: the Titanic sinks (April 1912), the First World War (1914–18) and the Second (1939–45).
An Inspector Calls · 1912 and 1945Screen 2

Britain in 1912

  • A rigid class system: wealth and power with factory owners and landowners, little security for workers.
  • No welfare state: the National Insurance Act 1911 had just been passed, bringing limited sick pay for some workers from 1913.
  • Big strikes, such as the national coal strike of 1912.
  • Women could not vote in general elections; some women won the vote in 1918, all adult women in 1928.
An Inspector Calls · 1912 and 1945Screen 3

Britain in 1945

  • In 1940 Priestley's Sunday-night BBC talks, Postscripts, drew huge audiences; he urged a fairer Britain after the war. The talks were later dropped.
  • In 1942 he co-founded the socialist Common Wealth party. The Beveridge Report (1942) proposed a welfare state.
  • In July 1945 Labour won a landslide; the NHS followed in 1948.
Quick checkScreen 4
?The first audiences watched Birling's Act 1 dinner speech in 1945 and 1946. What would most of them know that he doesn't?
An Inspector Calls · 1912 and 1945Screen 5

Using context well

  • Use context to explain a choice Priestley makes, not as a history lesson.
  • Birling's predictions: the 1945 audience knows he is wrong, so they distrust his values.
  • The Inspector's warning about suffering: the audience had lived through two wars.
  • Sheila and Eric: Priestley's hope rests on the young, the generation rebuilding Britain.
  • On your paper context carries 16 of the 40 marks, so link it in every paragraph.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Why set a play written in 1944–45 back in 1912?

The gap between 1912 and 1945 is the point. The audience knew about the wars and hardships that followed the Birlings' confident world, so Priestley could show them where selfishness and class division led, just as Britain was choosing how to rebuild.

Recap

Big ideas

  • The play is a well-made play shaped like a whodunnit: one room, one evening, three continuous acts, secrets revealed one at a time.
  • Priestley twists the detective story: everyone is involved, so the question becomes who is responsible.
  • Each act ends on a cliffhanger; the final call reopens the case and tests the characters and the audience.
  • The Inspector arrives straight after Birling's speech on putting yourself first; the lighting becomes harsher and brighter.
  • He controls the evening: events in order, one person at a time, unmoved by status.
  • Readers see him as Priestley's mouthpiece, a conscience or judge, or something supernatural; Priestley leaves it open.
  • Priestley presents Birling as a self-important businessman whose confident predictions, through dramatic irony, are proved wrong.
  • Mrs Birling uses her charity role to refuse Eva, then condemns the father without realising it is Eric.
  • Both parents return to their old views when the hoax seems proven: status and self-interest stop them learning.
  • Sheila moves from a delighted, sheltered young woman to the one who sees most clearly; she keeps the lesson after the hoax.
  • Eric's Act 3 confession reveals sexual violence, theft and his family's hidden flaws; he turns on his parents.
  • Priestley contrasts the generations: the young are open to change, the old are not.
  • Gerald, a wealthy, well-connected young man, rescued Eva as Daisy Renton, kept her as his mistress and ended it when it suited him.
  • In Act 3 he leads the hoax theory and offers the ring again: he sides with the older Birlings.
  • Eva never appears: she is seen only through the people who harmed her, and many readers see her as standing for countless working women.
  • Birling's individualism (capitalism) is set against the Inspector's collective responsibility (socialism).
  • Each small act is one link in a chain that leaves Eva with nothing; responsibility is shared.
  • Class and gender give the Birlings and Gerald power over Eva; Priestley argues that power brings responsibility.
  • Set in spring 1912; written in 1944–45; first performed in Moscow in 1945 and in London in 1946.
  • 1912: rigid class, little help for the poor, strikes, no vote for women. 1945: Postscripts, Labour's landslide and the coming welfare state.
  • Use context to explain Priestley's choices, especially the audience's hindsight.
