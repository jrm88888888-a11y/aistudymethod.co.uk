Mission 1

Exile: worship without the Temple

  • 586 BCE: the Babylonians destroy the First Temple in Jerusalem; many Judeans are taken to Babylon.
  • "By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down, yea, we wept, when we remembered Zion." (Psalm 137:1, JPS 1917)
  • Sacrifice could happen only at the Temple. So how could exiles worship?
  • Jewish tradition answers: they gathered to pray, read Torah and study: the beginnings of the synagogue (Greek: "gathering together").
  • The Second Temple was rebuilt (516 BCE) and destroyed by Rome (70 CE). After 70 CE the synagogue became the centre of Jewish worship everywhere.

A "little sanctuary"

> "Although I have removed them far off among the nations, and although I have scattered them among the countries, yet have I been to them as a little sanctuary in the countries where they are come" (Ezekiel 11:16, JPS 1917)

> "And they read in the book, in the Law of God, distinctly; and they gave the sense, and caused them to understand the reading." (Nehemiah 8:8, JPS 1917)

  • The Talmud (Megillah 29a, paraphrase) reads the "little sanctuary" as the synagogues and houses of study of Babylon.
  • Nehemiah 8: after the return, Ezra reads the Torah aloud to the people, with explanation: a model for synagogue Torah reading.

Q1 Where does Ezekiel say God is present for the exiles?

Show answer

Among them in exile, "as a little sanctuary": not only in the Jerusalem Temple.

Q2 Which two activities in Nehemiah 8:8 still happen in synagogues?

Show answer

Public reading of the Torah and explaining it, as in a sermon or study.

Mission 1

What historians say; after 70 CE

  • The Babylonian origin is the traditional view. Direct evidence from the exile itself has not been found.
  • The earliest firm evidence: inscriptions about Jewish "prayer houses" in Egypt (3rd century BCE) and synagogue buildings in the Land of Israel (1st century BCE–1st century CE).
  • After the Romans destroyed the Second Temple (70 CE), rabbis at Yavneh rebuilt Judaism around Torah study, prayer and the synagogue.
  • Prayer took the place of sacrifice: the three daily services match the Temple's daily offerings (Talmud Berakhot 26b).
Mission 1

Three houses in one

  • Beit Knesset (house of meeting/assembly): the community gathers for social life, decisions, celebrations and help.
  • Beit Midrash (house of study): Torah reading and learning; the Yiddish name shul comes from the German for "school".
  • Beit Tefillah (house of prayer): daily and Shabbat services, festivals.
  • A synagogue is defined by these functions, not by its size: any room with a Torah scroll, an ark and a minyan can serve.
  • Your spec spells these bet k'nesset, bet midrash and bet din.
Mission 1

Inside: the ark and the Temple

  • Aron Hakodesh (holy ark): a cupboard holding the Sefer Torah scrolls, on the wall facing Jerusalem. It recalls the Ark of the Covenant: "thou shalt put into the ark the testimony which I shall give thee" (Exodus 25:16, JPS 1917).
  • Parochet (curtain) before the ark: recalls the veil before the Holy of Holies (Exodus 26:33).
  • Ner Tamid (eternal light) above it: recalls the lamp that burned "continually" in the Sanctuary (Exodus 27:20, JPS 1917).
  • Bimah: the raised platform from which the Torah is read.
  • Often the Ten Commandments above the ark.
  • So the synagogue recalls the Temple, but is not a replacement Temple: no altar, no sacrifice, no priests leading worship.

Mission 1Diversity

Synagogue worship

  • Orthodox: prayer mostly in Hebrew; men and women seated apart (mechitzah: a screen or gallery); a minyan of ten men for public prayer; no instruments or microphones on Shabbat; the bimah often in the centre; men lead services.
  • Masorti: traditional liturgy in Hebrew; many communities seat men and women together and count women in the minyan.
  • Reform and Liberal: mixed seating; women and men count equally and lead services; more prayers in English; some use instruments; the bimah usually at the front; women rabbis.
  • Hasidic shtiebels: small, informal prayer rooms, often tied to a rebbe's following (see Mission 4).
Mission 1

Festivals, prayer and the mikveh

  • Festivals: the synagogue is where the community hears the shofar (Rosh Hashanah), keeps Yom Kippur, reads the Megillah at Purim and dances with the scrolls at Simchat Torah.
  • Prayer: three daily services; Kaddish for mourners and the Torah reading need a minyan.
  • Mikveh (pool of "living" natural water), in some synagogues or nearby: for women after menstruation (niddah), for converts, and by custom for some men before Shabbat or Yom Kippur.
  • It gives religious, not physical, cleanliness: a person immerses after washing.
Mission 1

Rabbis: role and authority

  • Rabbi means "my teacher". Rabbis are not priests: they offer no sacrifice and are not needed for prayer; any adult Jew may lead a service.
  • Trained and ordained (semikhah) after study of Talmud and halakhah.
  • Roles: teach; preach; answer questions of halakhah (a posek gives rulings); pastoral care; weddings, funerals, bar/bat mitzvah.
  • Authority: the Torah tells Israel to follow the judges of each generation, "thou shalt not turn aside from the sentence which they shall declare unto thee" (Deuteronomy 17:11, JPS 1917). The rabbis saw themselves as these judges' heirs.
  • Orthodox: a rabbi's halakhic rulings bind those who ask; many Haredi Jews also follow the guidance of leading sages on wider matters.
  • Progressive: the rabbi is a teacher and guide; individuals make informed choices. Women have been rabbis since the 20th century.
Mission 1

The Beth Din

  • Beth Din ("house of judgement"): a rabbinical court of three judges (dayanim); compare Moses appointing judges (Exodus 18:22).
  • Main work today:
  • Divorce: supervising the get (bill of divorce); the problem of agunot ("chained" women whose husbands refuse a get) in an earlier lesson.
  • Conversion and questions of Jewish status.
  • Kashrut supervision and licensing.
  • Arbitration of civil and business disputes between Jews who agree to it; in the UK such decisions can be binding under arbitration law, but never override UK criminal or family law.
  • Each movement has its own: e.g. the London Beth Din (United Synagogue, Orthodox) and the Reform and Masorti batei din. Orthodox courts do not recognise Progressive rulings on status.

Mission 1

The synagogue's changing role today

  • Community centre: kosher kitchens and halls; youth groups; care for the elderly; food banks; security teams.
  • Education: cheder (Hebrew classes for children), adult study, bar/bat mitzvah preparation.
  • Life cycle: weddings, bar/bat mitzvah, mourning prayers.
  • Changes: in Britain, membership of mainstream synagogues has fallen over recent decades while strictly Orthodox communities have grown; small communities merge; Jewish community centres and schools now share roles once held only by the synagogue.
  • Online: since 2020 many Progressive synagogues stream services; Orthodox synagogues do not use technology on Shabbat or festivals.
  • From exile to today: a portable institution that adapts to where Jews live.
Mission 1Evaluate

Is the synagogue the heart of the Jewish community?

  • Claim: The synagogue is the heart of the Jewish community.
  • For: It joins prayer, study and meeting in one place; since the exile and 70 CE it has kept Jewish identity alive in every land (Ezekiel 11:16).
  • For: Public prayer needs a minyan; Torah reading, festivals and life-cycle events gather there.
  • Against: The home is central too: Shabbat, Pesach, kashrut and teaching children.
  • Against: Many Jews rarely attend; schools, community centres and online life now share its roles.
  • Judgement criteria: Main use (prayer? study? meeting?) or main purpose (keeping the people together)? Does the answer differ for Orthodox and secular Jews?

Mission 2

The story: Exodus 12–15

  • Pesach (Passover): spring festival, from 15 Nisan, for 7 days (8 for many Jews outside Israel).
  • Exodus 12: before the tenth plague, each family kills a lamb and marks the doorposts with its blood; God "will pass over" those houses (Exodus 12:13). Israel leaves in haste: the dough has no time to rise (Exodus 12:39).
  • Exodus 13: keep the festival; eat unleavened bread; tell your children.
  • Exodus 14: the sea divides; Israel crosses on dry land; the Egyptian army is drowned.
  • Exodus 15: the Song of the Sea: "I will sing unto the LORD, for He is highly exalted" (Exodus 15:1, JPS 1917).
  • The core theme: redemption, God freeing a people from slavery to serve God.
Mission 2Read the source

"Because of what the LORD did for me"

> "And this day shall be unto you for a memorial, and ye shall keep it a feast to the LORD; throughout your generations ye shall keep it a feast by an ordinance for ever." (Exodus 12:14, JPS 1917)

> "And thou shalt tell thy son in that day, saying: It is because of that which the LORD did for me when I came forth out of Egypt." (Exodus 13:8, JPS 1917)

  • The Mishnah draws the lesson: in every generation, each person must see themselves as if they personally came out of Egypt (Pesachim 10:5, paraphrase).

Mission 2

Preparing: removing chametz

  • Chametz: leavened food from wheat, barley, rye, oats or spelt (bread, pasta, beer).
  • "the first day ye shall put away leaven out of your houses" (Exodus 12:15, JPS 1917).
  • Houses are cleaned; the night before, a search (bedikat chametz) by candlelight; remaining chametz burned or sold.
  • Many families use separate Pesach dishes.
  • Matzah (unleavened bread) is eaten: the "bread of affliction" and the bread of haste.
  • Meaning often drawn: removing "puffed-up" pride along with leaven.
Mission 2

The seder plate

  • Seder means "order": a home meal with a fixed order of 15 steps.
  • On the plate:
  • Zeroa (roasted shank bone): the Pesach lamb offered in the Temple. Vegetarians often use a roasted beetroot, an option the Talmud already mentions (Pesachim 114b).
  • Beitzah (roasted egg): the festival offering; also read as mourning for the Temple.
  • Maror (bitter herbs, often horseradish): "they made their lives bitter with hard service" (Exodus 1:14, JPS 1917).
  • Charoset (apple, nuts, wine): the mortar the slaves used for bricks.
  • Karpas (green vegetable) dipped in salt water: spring and new life; tears.
  • Chazeret (a second bitter herb, often lettuce).
  • Beside it: three matzot and wine for four cups.

Mission 2

The Haggadah: telling the story

  • Haggadah ("telling"): the book that guides the seder, fulfilling "thou shalt tell thy son" (Exodus 13:8).
  • Mah Nishtanah (the Four Questions), asked by the youngest: Why matzah? Why bitter herbs? Why dip twice? Why recline?
  • The answer: "We were slaves to Pharaoh in Egypt …", then the story told through rabbinic interpretation, the ten plagues (a drop of wine removed for each, as joy is lessened by others' suffering) and the song Dayenu ("it would have been enough").
  • Four cups of wine: linked to God's four promises of redemption, "I will bring you out … deliver you … redeem you … take you" (Exodus 6:6–7).
  • Rabban Gamaliel (Mishnah Pesachim 10:5, paraphrase): you must explain three things: the Pesach offering, matzah and maror.
  • The afikoman (hidden half-matzah) keeps children awake and involved.
Mission 2

Elijah and "next year in Jerusalem"

  • A fifth cup is poured: the cup of Elijah; the door is opened for him.
  • The prophet Malachi says God will send Elijah "before the coming of the great and terrible day of the LORD" (Malachi 3:23, 4:5 in many English Bibles; paraphrase). Jewish tradition sees Elijah as the herald of the Messiah.
  • One explanation of the extra cup: a fifth promise, "I will bring you in unto the land" (Exodus 6:8), still to be fulfilled.
  • The seder ends: "Next year in Jerusalem" (L'shanah haba'ah birushalayim): hope for the messianic redemption and the rebuilding of Jerusalem. In Israel, some say "next year in Jerusalem rebuilt".
  • So the seder moves from past redemption to future hope.
Mission 2

Redemption: past, present, future

  • Past: God redeemed Israel from Egypt, the model for all redemption.
  • The world: redemption is God's plan for all humanity: freedom to serve God, a world ruled by God's justice (the messianic age).
  • Present: Jews have read Pesach into later persecutions; seders were held in secret in times of danger, and campaigns for Soviet Jewry used the theme of "Let my people go".
  • Today: many Jews add readings on modern slavery, refugees or hunger; for Orthodox Jews redemption remains a literal hope for the Messiah.
  • Issue: does "redemption" still mean anything in a free, secular society? For: freedom is never finished; the story inspires justice. Against: many Jews live free and secure; the word may feel historic rather than present.

Mission 2Diversity

Community, family and identity

  • Pesach is one of the most widely kept Jewish practices, including among Jews who rarely go to synagogue.
  • It is a home festival: parents teach children; the whole family takes part; guests are invited.
  • Orthodox: full traditional Haggadah (the book that guides the seder) in Hebrew; two seders outside Israel; strict chametz (leavened food) rules.
  • Progressive: one seder (Reform follows the Israeli 7 days); new Haggadot with English, discussion, and additions such as Miriam's cup (water, honouring Miriam and women in the story).
  • Secular and communal seders: freedom as a cultural and ethical theme.
Mission 2Evaluate

Is Pesach the central festival of Judaism?

  • Claim: Pesach is the central festival of Judaism.
  • For: The Exodus is the founding event: God is "the LORD thy God, who brought thee out of the land of Egypt" (Exodus 20:2).
  • For: It is the most widely observed; the Exodus is recalled daily (in the Shema) and every Shabbat (Kiddush).
  • Against: Yom Kippur is the holiest day of the year, with the highest synagogue attendance (see Mission 3).
  • Against: Shabbat, kept weekly and commanded in the Ten Commandments, may matter more than any annual festival.
  • Judgement criteria: Central by holiness, by popularity, or by meaning? Does "central" mean the same for Orthodox and secular Jews?

Mission 3

Origins in the Torah

  • Rosh Hashanah ("head of the year"): 1–2 Tishri (autumn). The Torah calls it "a memorial proclaimed with the blast of horns" (Leviticus 23:24, JPS 1917) and "a day of blowing the horn" (Numbers 29:1, JPS 1917).
  • The rabbis name it the New Year, the anniversary of creation, and the Day of Judgement (Yom ha-Din).
  • Yom Kippur ("Day of Atonement"): 10 Tishri. "ye shall afflict your souls" (Leviticus 23:27, JPS 1917).
  • In Temple times, the High Priest made atonement in the Holy of Holies (Leviticus 16). Since 70 CE, prayer, repentance and fasting take its place.
  • From Rosh Hashanah to Yom Kippur: the Ten Days of Repentance (Aseret Yemei Teshuvah, 1–10 Tishri), the Days of Awe.
Mission 3Read the source

Leviticus 16 and 23

> "And it shall be a statute for ever unto you: in the seventh month, on the tenth day of the month, ye shall afflict your souls, and shall do no manner of work" (Leviticus 16:29, JPS 1917)

> "For on this day shall atonement be made for you, to cleanse you; from all your sins shall ye be clean before the LORD." (Leviticus 16:30, JPS 1917)

> "from even unto even, shall ye keep your sabbath." (Leviticus 23:32, JPS 1917)

  • The rabbis read "afflict your souls" as fasting and other self-denial.

Q1 What two things does Leviticus 16:30 say the day does?

Show answer

Atonement and cleansing: being made clean from sins before God.

Q2 Which phrase gives the day its evening-to-evening length?

Show answer

"from even unto even": a full day, extended to about 25 hours.

Mission 3

The shofar in the Hebrew scriptures

  • A shofar is a ram's horn (or another kosher animal's horn, not a cow's).
  • Sinai: "the voice of a horn exceeding loud" as God gives the Torah (Exodus 19:16, JPS 1917).
  • Abraham and Isaac: a ram caught by its horns is offered instead of Isaac (Genesis 22:13). The Talmud links the ram's horn to God's remembering the binding of Isaac (Rosh Hashanah 16a).
  • Kings: Solomon is anointed: "blow ye with the horn, and say: Long live king Solomon" (1 Kings 1:34, JPS 1917). On Rosh Hashanah God is proclaimed King.
  • Alarm and repentance: "Blow the horn in Zion, sanctify a fast" (Joel 2:15, JPS 1917).
  • Freedom and future: the Jubilee is proclaimed with the horn on Yom Kippur (Leviticus 25:9); a "great horn" will gather the exiles (Isaiah 27:13).
  • Maimonides (Laws of Repentance 3:4, paraphrase): its call says "wake up, sleepers; examine your deeds and return".
Mission 3

Rosh Hashanah: rituals

  • Shofar sounded in the synagogue: tekiah (long), shevarim (three broken), teruah (rapid short), tekiah gedolah (very long). Customarily 100 blasts. Not blown when the day falls on Shabbat.
  • At home: apples dipped in honey for "a sweet year"; round challah (the cycle of the year, or a crown).
  • Tashlikh ("you will cast"): on the first afternoon, Jews say prayers by flowing water, often shaking out pockets or throwing crumbs. It acts out "Thou wilt cast all their sins into the depths of the sea" (Micah 7:19, JPS 1917).
  • Tashlikh is a custom first recorded in medieval Germany; some rabbis criticised it as open to superstition, and many stress the prayers, not the crumbs.
  • Teshuvah (returning), tefillah (prayer) and tzedakah (charity) mark the Ten Days.

Mission 3

The machzor and the changed Amidah

  • Machzor ("cycle"): the special prayer book for the festivals, especially the High Holy Days, with long poems and extra services.
  • Key prayers: Unetaneh Tokef (God as judge deciding "who shall live and who shall die"; repentance, prayer and charity change the decree's severity); Avinu Malkeinu ("Our Father, our King").
  • Amidah (standing prayer) amended for the Ten Days: "Remember us for life"; "inscribe us in the book of life"; God called "the holy King" instead of "the holy God".
  • The confession (Vidui: Ashamnu, Al Chet) is said in the plural: "we have sinned", even by those who did not do a given sin.
  • So the whole people stands together before God: unity and identity.
Mission 3

Kol Nidre

  • Kol Nidre ("all vows"): an Aramaic formula sung three times as Yom Kippur begins, with the Torah scrolls held.
  • It cancels vows made to God (rash personal vows), not promises or contracts made to other people.
  • Many Ashkenazi texts refer to vows of the coming year (a wording linked to Rabbeinu Tam, 12th century).
  • Its haunting melody makes it the best-known moment of the year, drawing even Jews who rarely attend.
  • History: some rabbis opposed it; some anti-Jewish writers claimed it made Jews' oaths worthless; 19th-century Reform removed it, but most Progressive communities later restored it.
  • Its meaning for many: the community stands together, free of empty words, ready for honest repentance.
Mission 3

The 25-hour fast

  • Five afflictions (Mishnah Yoma 8:1): no eating or drinking, no washing, no anointing (oils, creams), no leather shoes, no marital relations.
  • About 25 hours: the fast begins before sunset and ends after nightfall, adding time to the holy day.
  • Exempt: children under bar/bat mitzvah age; anyone whose health would be endangered (pikuach nephesh overrides).
  • Many wear white (some men a kittel): purity and simplicity.
  • Five services, ending with Ne'ilah ("closing of the gates") and a final shofar blast; the book of Jonah is read in the afternoon.
  • Mishnah Yoma 8:9 (paraphrase): Yom Kippur atones for sins against God; for sins against another person it does not atone until you have made peace with that person.

Mission 3Diversity

How far are they kept?

  • Yom Kippur has the highest synagogue attendance of the year; many Jews who keep little else fast.
  • Orthodox: the full day in synagogue, every service, the full fast; two days of Rosh Hashanah, including in Israel.
  • Progressive: shorter services, much English, egalitarian participation; most keep the fast; Kol Nidre kept; Reform often keeps one day of Rosh Hashanah.
  • Secular Jews: some fast as a mark of identity; in Israel the roads fall quiet on Yom Kippur.
  • Value of the fast: focus on the soul instead of the body; empathy with the hungry (Isaiah 58:6–7 is read in the morning: the fast God wants is freeing the oppressed). Challenge: hunger and tiredness may distract from prayer; health exemptions apply.
Mission 3Evaluate

Is yearly confession an admission of failure?

  • Claim: Regularly confessing sins every year shows a failure to grow spiritually.
  • For: If the same sins are confessed every year, it might seem little has changed.
  • For: Plural confession may let individuals avoid facing their own wrongs.
  • Against: Judaism expects humans to sin (yetzer hara); teshuvah is a lifelong process, not one event.
  • Against: Admitting fault is itself growth; the Mishnah demands real change towards others (Yoma 8:9).
  • Judgement criteria: What counts as "spiritual development"? Does regular confession lead to changed behaviour? Is honesty about failure a weakness or a strength?

Mission 4

Eastern Europe in the 1700s

  • Most of the world's Jews lived in the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, in small towns (shtetls).
  • Memories of the massacres of 1648–49 (the Chmielnicki uprising) and of the false messiah Sabbatai Zevi (who converted to Islam in 1666) left disappointment.
  • Religious life was led by a learned elite: Talmud scholars. Many poor, unlearned Jews felt shut out.
  • Into this world came a new movement of joyful piety: Hasidism (from hasid, "pious one").
  • Historians debate how far these events caused Hasidism.
Mission 4

The Baal Shem Tov

  • Israel ben Eliezer (c. 1700–1760), in Podolia (now Ukraine), from about 1740 based in Medzhybizh.
  • Called the Baal Shem Tov ("Master of the Good Name"), or Besht. A baal shem was a healer who used divine names, amulets and prayer.
  • Stories of miraculous healing and wonders gathered around him (In Praise of the Baal Shem Tov, 1814).
  • Teachings (passed on by disciples): God is present everywhere, no place is empty of him; serve God with joy; a simple Jew's sincere prayer is precious to God.
  • He left almost no writings; his disciple Dov Ber of Mezeritch (d. 1772) spread the movement.

Mission 4

Worship with the heart

  • Simcha (joy): sadness and despair are seen as obstacles to serving God.
  • Devekut ("cleaving" to God): constant attachment to God, in prayer and in everyday acts.
  • Kavvanah: intention and inner focus in prayer, more important than speed or quantity.
  • Niggun: wordless melody; dancing, clapping, swaying in prayer: a charismatic, emotional style.
  • Serving God through the physical (avodah be-gashmiyut): eating, work and even walking can be holy if done for God.
  • Worship rather than traditional study: study still matters, but prayer and devotion come first; the unlearned are included.

Mission 4

A new prayer rite

  • Hasidim adopted Nusach Sefard (or Nusach Ari): a prayer rite shaped by the kabbalist Isaac Luria (1534–1572, Safed).
  • Modified liturgy: Lurianic additions and order, mystical intentions (kavvanot), prayers before prayers.
  • Some Hasidim delayed prayer beyond the usual times to prepare properly (e.g. through mikveh immersion).
  • Separate prayer houses (shtiblekh) formed.
  • To opponents this looked like splitting the community and changing a sacred order.
Mission 4

The rebbe

  • The rebbe (or tzaddik, "righteous one") leads each Hasidic group.
  • Seen as a channel between followers and God: his prayers and blessings can bring healing, children, livelihood.
  • Followers visit his court, hand him a written request (kvitl), and join his tish (table) on Shabbat: songs, Torah teaching, sharing food he has blessed.
  • Leadership often passes by dynasty (father to son or son-in-law): Chabad-Lubavitch, Satmar, Ger, Belz.
  • Exception: Breslov Hasidim have had no living rebbe since Rabbi Nachman (1772–1810).
Mission 4

The Mitnagdim

  • Mitnagdim: "opponents". Led by the Vilna Gaon (Elijah ben Solomon Zalman, 1720–1797), a great Talmud scholar.
  • 1772: bans (herem) against the Hasidim in Vilna and Brody; more followed in 1781.
  • Their objections: neglect of Torah study; a changed prayer rite and late prayer; separate prayer houses; too much power given to the rebbe; noisy, ecstatic worship; fear of another false-messiah movement.
  • The conflict grew bitter: Hasidic leader Shneur Zalman of Liadi was denounced to the Russian authorities and imprisoned in 1798 (released).
  • Mitnagdic answer: the great yeshiva of Volozhin (1803).
  • Later, both sides united against the Haskalah; both are part of Haredi Orthodoxy today.

Mission 4Diversity

Hasidism today

  • Chabad-Lubavitch: emphasises the mind (ChaBaD = wisdom, understanding, knowledge; Shneur Zalman's Tanya); worldwide outreach to non-observant Jews; some followers held the last rebbe, Menachem Mendel Schneerson (1902–1994), to be the Messiah, a claim other Jews dispute.
  • Satmar: strongly anti-Zionist; separate from wider society.
  • Breslov: personal prayer in solitude (hitbodedut); pilgrimage to Rabbi Nachman's grave in Uman.
  • All keep halakhah fully; dress (e.g. long coats, fur hats on Shabbat) and customs mark each group.
  • Martin Buber (1878–1965), Tales of the Hasidim (1947): retold Hasidic stories for modern readers; scholars such as Gershom Scholem said he chose the parts that suited his own philosophy.
Mission 4Evaluate

Did Hasidism help Judaism survive?

  • Claim: Hasidism contributed to the survival of Judaism.
  • For: it renewed faith among poor, unlearned Jews who might have drifted away.
  • For: it built strong, close communities around the rebbe that resisted assimilation in the 19th century.
  • For: after the Holocaust, rebuilt Hasidic communities grew fast (Israel, New York, London); Chabad brings many back to observance.
  • Against: Judaism had survived for centuries without it; the Mitnagdic yeshivas and other movements also kept Judaism alive.
  • Against: separation from wider society may preserve a group but not engage most modern Jews.
  • Judgement criteria: survival of what (practice, belief, communities)? In which period? Compared with which alternatives?
Mission 4Evaluate

How far does Hasidism divide Judaism?

  • Claim: Hasidism divides Judaism.
  • For: the 18th-century bans split communities; separate prayer houses and rites remain.
  • For: Hasidic groups differ sharply from each other (e.g. Chabad and Satmar on Zionism and outreach).
  • For: messianic claims about the last Lubavitcher Rebbe have caused tension.
  • Against: Hasidim and Mitnagdim reconciled and now share Haredi Orthodoxy and full halakhah.
  • Against: Chabad outreach serves Jews of every background; many non-Hasidic Jews use Hasidic melodies and stories.
  • Judgement criteria: division over essentials or over style? Past or present? Within Orthodoxy or across all Jews?

For Hasidim, joy and sincerity open the way to God for every Jew, learned or not. Their opponents feared what would happen to learning; today both live inside Orthodoxy.

Mission 5: Some Jewish mystics say a soul can return to finish unfinished work. Where does that idea come from?

Make a guess: Some Jewish mystics say a soul can return to finish unfinished work. Where does that idea come from?

Mission 5

What Kabbalah is

  • Kabbalah: "received tradition". The main stream of Jewish mysticism: seeking direct experience and knowledge of God.
  • Esoteric (hidden): traditionally taught only to mature, learned students; the Mishnah limits teaching the mysteries of creation and of God's chariot (Hagigah 2:1).
  • Key stages: Sefer ha-Bahir (Provence, c. 1180) · the Zohar (Spain, c. 1280s) · Isaac Luria in Safed (1534–1572) · then Hasidism (see Mission 4).
  • Kabbalists keep halakhah fully; mysticism deepens the commandments, it does not replace them.
Mission 5

The Zohar

  • Zohar ("Radiance"): a mystical commentary on the Torah, mostly in Aramaic.
  • Traditionally attributed to Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai (2nd century CE).
  • It appeared in 13th-century Castile through Moses de León (c. 1240–1305).
  • Gershom Scholem (1897–1982), Major Trends in Jewish Mysticism (1941): de León was the main author. Many traditional Jews hold that it preserves ancient teaching.
  • Role: for many Jews, especially Hasidic and Sephardi communities, a sacred text alongside Talmud; read for its hidden meanings of Torah; shaped prayer and custom.
Mission 5

En Sof and the Sefirot

  • En Sof (also spelled Ein Sof): "without end". God in God's own essence: infinite, beyond all knowledge and description.
  • From En Sof come ten Sefirot (emanations): the ways God is revealed and acts in the world.
  • Keter (crown) · Chokhmah (wisdom) · Binah (understanding) · Chesed (loving-kindness) · Gevurah (strength, judgement) · Tiferet (beauty) · Netzach (endurance) · Hod (splendour) · Yesod (foundation) · Malkhut (kingship), also called Shekhinah (God's presence).
  • Kabbalists insist the Sefirot are not ten gods: God is one; the Sefirot are like light through ten different glasses.
  • Kabbalists teach that human deeds affect the harmony of the Sefirot: a mitzvah done with intention unites them.
Mission 5

Devekut: clinging to God

> "But ye that did cleave unto the LORD your God are alive every one of you this day." (Deuteronomy 4:4, JPS 1917)

  • Devekut: "cleaving" to God; the goal of kabbalistic prayer and meditation.
  • Most kabbalists describe communion, not the soul dissolving into God: the person remains distinct.
  • Some went further, describing near-union (e.g. Abraham Abulafia, 1240–c. 1291).
  • Penetrating God's essence: kabbalists seek to know God through the Sefirot; En Sof itself stays beyond reach.
Mission 5

Esoteric practice

  • Meditation: focusing on divine names and letter combinations (Abulafia); Lurianic kavvanot: intentions held during prayer.
  • Visual aids: the ilan ("tree"): diagrams of the Sefirot; the shiviti: a plaque of Psalm 16:8 with God's name, placed before the prayer leader to aid focus.
  • Art: illuminated texts, papercuts, Sefirot imagery in synagogues and homes.
  • Magic ("practical Kabbalah"): amulets and holy names for protection or healing. Most kabbalists (e.g. Luria's circle) warned against it as dangerous.
  • Issue: are aids a help to experiencing God, or a distraction from it?

Mission 5

Tikkun: repairing the world

  • Isaac Luria's account (16th century, Safed):
  • Tzimtzum: En Sof ("without end": God as infinite, in kabbalistic thought) "contracts" to make room for a world.
  • Shevirat ha-kelim: vessels meant to hold divine light break; sparks of holiness fall, trapped in kelippot ("husks").
  • Tikkun ("repair"): Jews free the sparks through mitzvot, prayer with intention and holy living.
  • When repair is complete, the Messiah comes.
  • Every act counts: ordinary deeds have cosmic effects.
  • Modern "tikkun olam" (social action) borrows the word.
Mission 5

Gilgul: the return of souls

  • Gilgul neshamot: "the rolling of souls": reincarnation in some kabbalistic thought.
  • First taught in the Bahir (c. 1180); developed in the Zohar; fully systematised by Luria in Gate of Reincarnations (written down by Hayyim Vital, 1542–1620).
  • Why return? to fulfil commandments not yet kept, to repair a sin, or to complete the soul's tikkun.
  • Kabbalists use it to explain God's justice: why the righteous suffer or a child dies young (finishing a past task).
  • The Zohar links it to levirate marriage (Deuteronomy 25:5): the dead brother's soul returns in the child.
  • Not endless: the soul aims to complete its repair, then rests. Not the same as Hindu samsara and karma.
Mission 5Read the source

"Twice, yea thrice"

> "Lo, all these things doth God work, twice, yea thrice, with a man, To bring back his soul from the pit, that he may be enlightened with the light of the living." (Job 33:29–30, JPS 1917)

  • Plain sense: God rescues a person from death more than once in this life.
  • Kabbalistic reading (Zohar, paraphrase): a soul may be sent back into the world more than once to complete its repair.

Mission 5Diversity

Who accepts gilgul?

  • Rejected by Saadia Gaon (882–942), Book of Beliefs and Opinions (933): a foreign idea, not Jewish.
  • Not among Maimonides' Thirteen Principles; not in the Talmud.
  • Widely accepted in Hasidic, many Haredi and many Sephardi communities, following the Zohar and Luria.
  • Modern Orthodox: many treat it as a permitted but optional belief.
  • Reform and Liberal: generally do not teach it; focus on this life and the soul's immortality (Pittsburgh Platform, 1885, §7).
  • Can sit beside resurrection: kabbalists taught that soul-sparks shared across lives are restored at the end.

Mission 5Evaluate

Is personal mystical union with God possible in Judaism?

  • Claim: A personal mystical union with God is possible in Judaism.
  • For: devekut is commanded ("to cleave unto Him", Deuteronomy 30:20); Hasidic and kabbalistic writers describe deep experience of God.
  • For: Abulafia and some Hasidic teachers speak of the self being nullified before God.
  • Against: En Sof is beyond all knowledge; Judaism stresses the gap between Creator and creature.
  • Against: most kabbalists describe communion, not union; complete union risks losing the self who keeps the commandments.
  • Also: value of aids to worship: they focus the mind (shiviti, ilan), but can become magic or distraction.
  • Judgement criteria: what counts as "union"? Whose writings are the test? Does experience need doctrine to interpret it?

In some kabbalistic thought a soul may return to finish what it left undone. Many Jews hold this; others reject it. Both views are found within Judaism.

Mission 6

Every life a world

  • Sanctity of life: Jews believe human life is holy because it comes from God and belongs to God.
  • Humans are made "in the image of God" (Genesis 1:27, JPS 1917), so in Jewish teaching killing a person attacks God's image (Genesis 9:6).
  • Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5 (paraphrase): whoever destroys one life is as if they destroyed a whole world; whoever saves one life, as if they saved a whole world.
  • In Jewish teaching, every life has infinite value: one life is not worth less than many.
  • "Thou shalt not murder" (Exodus 20:13, JPS 1917).
  • Your spec writes G-d, a form many Jews use out of respect.
Mission 6Read the source

"He shall live by them"

> "Ye shall therefore keep My statutes, and Mine ordinances, which if a man do, he shall live by them: I am the LORD." (Leviticus 18:5, JPS 1917)

> "… neither shalt thou stand idly by the blood of thy neighbour: I am the LORD." (Leviticus 19:16, JPS 1917)

  • Talmud (Yoma 85b, paraphrase): "live by them", not die by them. The mitzvot are given for life.

Q1 How does Yoma 85b use Leviticus 18:5 to justify breaking a mitzvah?

Show answer

The mitzvot exist for life, so when keeping one would cost a life, life comes first.

Q2 What extra duty does Leviticus 19:16 add?

Show answer

A duty to act to rescue, not only to avoid killing.

Mission 6

Pikuach nefesh overrides

  • Pikuach nefesh = "watching over a life": saving a life overrides almost every mitzvah.
  • Examples: driving to hospital on Shabbat; eating non-kosher food or medicine when needed; breaking a fast.
  • It is a duty, not just a permission: the person who acts fastest is praised (Yoma 84b).
  • It applies in doubt too: if a life might be at risk, act.
  • Many Jews see it as showing the Torah's purpose: "Her ways are ways of pleasantness" (Proverbs 3:17, JPS 1917).
  • Eduqas spelling: pikuach nefesh (the sanctity of life).
Mission 6

The limits

  • Three exceptions (Sanhedrin 74a): a Jew must die rather than commit idolatry, murder or forbidden sexual relations.
  • On murder the Talmud asks: "Who says your blood is redder?" You may not kill an innocent person to save yourself (Sanhedrin 74a; Pesachim 25b).
  • So pikuach nefesh does not allow trading one life for another.
  • It applies to an identifiable person in danger; how far it reaches to future or unknown people is debated (key for embryo research).
  • Applied to war: weapons that kill without telling fighters from civilians trade countless lives, so many Jews see them as breaking these limits; others stress self-defence and deterrence. Jews disagree.

Mission 7

When does a person begin?

  • In halakhah the unborn child's status grows with time:
  • Up to 40 days after conception: "mere water" (Yevamot 69b), a striking phrase for its low legal status.
  • After 40 days: like "a limb of its mother" (Chullin 58a): part of her body, not yet a separate person.
  • At birth (when the head emerges): a full nefesh (person) (Mishnah Ohalot 7:6; Sanhedrin 72b).
  • Before birth, the mother's life comes first: if a birth endangers her life, the fetus is removed (Mishnah Ohalot 7:6, paraphrase).
  • But the unborn child is still potential life, never valueless: harming it without serious reason is forbidden.
  • Your spec writes G-d, a form many Jews use out of respect.

> "But if any harm follow, then thou shalt give life for life," (Exodus 21:23, JPS 1917)

  • The rabbis read "harm" as the death of the woman.

Mission 7

Embryos and stem cells

  • Stem cells can become many types of cell; embryonic stem cells (from an embryo about 5 days old) can become almost any.
  • Taking them destroys the embryo. Most come from spare IVF embryos, made for fertility treatment and not used.
  • Hope: treatments for diseases such as Parkinson's, diabetes, spinal injury (pikuach nefesh).
  • UK law allows research on embryos only up to 14 days (Human Fertilisation and Embryology Act 1990).
  • Alternatives: adult stem cells; induced pluripotent cells (adult cells reprogrammed, since 2006).
Mission 7

An embryo outside the womb

  • A pre-implantation embryo in a dish cannot develop into a baby unless placed in a womb.
  • Many rabbis argue it has less status than even a 40-day fetus: it is not "in its mother" at all.
  • So destroying it is not murder, and some say not abortion either.
  • Others warn: it is still potential human life; creating embryos to destroy them treats life as a tool.
  • Israel's law, shaped partly by these rabbinic views, permits embryonic stem-cell research and bans reproductive cloning.
  • Spec term: "pre-implantation embryo research".
Mission 7

SCNT: cloning for cells

  • Somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT): the nucleus of a body cell is put into an egg with its nucleus removed; the egg develops into an early embryo.
  • Used for Dolly the sheep (born 1996).
  • Therapeutic cloning: grow stem cells that match a patient, so the body will not reject them.
  • Reproductive cloning: a cloned baby; banned in the UK and Israel.
  • Jewish responses: many rabbis cautiously allow therapeutic SCNT under pikuach nefesh; reproductive cloning raises deeper concerns (family, identity, human dignity).
Mission 7

Pikuach nefesh and research

  • The Torah permits and commands healing: "cause him to be thoroughly healed" (Exodus 21:19, JPS 1917; Bava Kamma 85a).
  • For research: if pikuach nefesh overrides almost every mitzvah, it can justify using embryos that will be discarded anyway, to save future patients.
  • Against: pikuach nefesh classically needs a person in front of us in present danger (Rabbi Ezekiel Landau, 1713–1793); future patients from research may not count.
  • Reply: today, sick people who could benefit are real and identifiable worldwide.
  • And pikuach nefesh never permits murder (see Mission 6): so the question turns on whether the embryo is a person.

Mission 7

Rabbi Moshe David Tendler

  • Moshe David Tendler (1926–2021): Orthodox rabbi and professor of biology at Yeshiva University, New York; expert in medical halakhah.
  • Argued (paraphrase) that a pre-implantation embryo outside the womb has no human status in halakhah.
  • So using spare embryos for stem-cell research is permitted, and may be a duty, because it serves pikuach nefesh.
  • Destroying embryos that would be discarded anyway, to heal the sick, honours the sanctity of life rather than violating it.
  • Permissive position.
Mission 7

Rabbi J. David Bleich

  • J. David Bleich (b. 1936): Orthodox rabbi, professor of Talmud and of Jewish law at Yeshiva University; author of Contemporary Halakhic Problems.
  • More cautious (paraphrase): destroying a pre-implantation embryo may not be murder, but that does not make it permitted.
  • Jewish law protects unborn life even before it counts as a person: some authorities allow Shabbat to be broken to save a fetus.
  • So the embryo's status needs careful weighing, case by case, not a simple yes.

Mission 7

Professor Clare Blackburn

  • Clare Blackburn: stem-cell scientist, Professor of Tissue Stem Cell Biology at the University of Edinburgh (MRC Centre for Regenerative Medicine): a voice from outside the Jewish tradition.
  • Her research: how the thymus (an organ of the immune system) develops; her team (2014) turned ordinary mouse cells into thymus cells that formed a working organ in mice.
  • Shows the medical promise of stem-cell science: repairing organs and immune systems.
  • Her team's method used reprogrammed ordinary cells, not cells from embryos.
  • Why it matters for Jewish ethics: it strengthens the pikuach nefesh case, and also shows alternatives that may reduce the need to use embryos.
Mission 7Evaluate

Is pikuach nefesh compatible with embryo research?

  • Claim: Pikuach nefesh is compatible with embryo research.
  • For: Healing is a mitzvah; some Orthodox rabbis argue a dish embryo is not a person; spare embryos would be discarded anyway.
  • For: Stem-cell research shows real potential to save and improve lives.
  • Tendler argues the dish embryo has no human status; Blackburn's thymus research shows the promise.
  • Against: Pikuach nefesh classically needs an identifiable person in present danger; research benefits are uncertain and future.
  • Against: Other rabbis say early life is still protected; alternatives such as reprogrammed cells exist; creating embryos to destroy them risks treating life as a tool.
  • Bleich: not murder, but that does not make it permitted.
  • Judgement criteria: What is the embryo's status? Does pikuach nefesh cover future patients? Spare embryos or embryos made for research? Are alternatives good enough?
  • Spec issue: "the extent to which pikuach nefesh is compatible with embryo research".
Mission check

?A rabbi is asked about three cases: a 5-day embryo frozen in a fertility clinic; a 10-week fetus in the womb; a baby whose head has emerged during birth. How do most Orthodox authorities rank their status, lowest first?

Jewish law weighs potential life by stage and place, while an actual life comes first. That scale is what lets many rabbis permit research on the cells in the dish, and what makes others cautious.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Tradition traces the synagogue to the Babylonian exile ("a little sanctuary", Ezekiel 11:16); after 70 CE it replaced the Temple as the centre of worship.
  • Beit knesset, beit midrash, beit tefillah: meeting, study, prayer; the ark, parochet and ner tamid recall the Temple.
  • Rabbis teach and rule on halakhah (Deuteronomy 17:11); a beth din of three handles divorce, conversion, kashrut and disputes.

Mission 2

  • Pesach recalls the Exodus (Exodus 12–15): redemption from slavery; "because of that which the LORD did for me" (Exodus 13:8).
  • The seder plate and the Haggadah teach the story through taste, questions and song; the family and community pass on identity.
  • Elijah's cup and "next year in Jerusalem" turn memory into hope for the Messiah and the redemption of the world.

Mission 3

  • Rosh Hashanah (1–2 Tishri): New Year and Day of Judgement; the shofar wakes people to repentance; tashlikh casts sins away symbolically.
  • Yom Kippur (10 Tishri): the 25-hour fast, Kol Nidre, plural confession and Ne'ilah; it atones for sins against God, and against people only once peace is made.
  • The machzor and the amended Amidah show the people united before God as King and Judge.

Mission 4

  • Hasidism began with the Baal Shem Tov (c. 1700–1760), who taught that God is present everywhere, and stressed joy, devekut, sincere prayer; stories of miraculous healing.
  • Distinctive practice: Nusach Sefard/Ari prayer rite, niggun and dance, worship before study, the rebbe (tzaddik) and his court.
  • The Mitnagdim (Vilna Gaon) banned the Hasidim from 1772; later both united against the Haskalah. Scholars debate whether Hasidism aided Jewish survival and how far it divides Judaism.

Mission 5

  • Kabbalah is Jewish mysticism; the Zohar (13th-century Spain, attributed to Shimon bar Yochai) is its central text.
  • En Sof is God beyond knowledge, revealed through ten Sefirot; devekut is cleaving to God; meditation, visual aids (ilan, shiviti), art and (discouraged) magic are esoteric aids.
  • Luria's tikkun repairs the world through mitzvot; gilgul (reincarnation), accepted by some Jews and rejected by others, lets a soul return to complete its repair.

Mission 6

  • In Jewish teaching, life is holy because humans are made in God's image; saving one life is like saving a world.
  • Pikuach nefesh overrides almost every mitzvah, even in doubt; it never permits idolatry, murder or forbidden relations.

Mission 7

  • The unborn child's status grows: "mere water" up to 40 days, part of the mother after, a full person at birth; the mother's life comes first.
  • Many Orthodox rabbis permit research on spare embryos outside the womb, as not yet persons, under pikuach nefesh; others urge caution.
  • SCNT makes patient-matched embryos for cells; Tendler (permissive) and Bleich (cautious) agree it is not murder but differ on what follows; Blackburn's science shows the promise and the alternatives.
