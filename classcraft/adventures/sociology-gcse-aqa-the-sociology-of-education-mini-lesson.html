The sociology of education

This mini-lesson covers AQA Topic 3.4 — Education: the roles and functions of education, perspectives (functionalism, Marxism), processes inside schools (labelling, the self-fulfilling prophecy, setting), and differences in achievement by class, gender and ethnicity.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go and collect ⭐ stars. Some questions are multiple-choice, some are sort-and-match games, and a few use simple research-methods maths (percentages and sample figures). Press Start when you're ready.

Education · functions

What is education for?

Perspectives disagree about the role of education:

  • Functionalists — education promotes social solidarity (Durkheim), teaches specialist skills, and acts as a meritocracy where the best are selected for the best jobs (Parsons — school as a 'bridge' between family and society; Davis and Moore — role allocation).
  • Marxists — education reproduces class inequality. Bowles and Gintis argued a 'correspondence principle' links school to work, and a 'hidden curriculum' teaches obedience. Althusser called school part of the ideological state apparatus.

Education · in-school processes

Labelling & the self-fulfilling prophecy

Interactionists look inside schools at how teachers and pupils interact:

  • Labelling — teachers judge pupils (e.g. as 'bright' or 'troublemaker'), often on stereotypes.
  • Self-fulfilling prophecy — a label can come true because the pupil lives up (or down) to it. Rosenthal and Jacobson's study suggested teacher expectations affect achievement.
  • Setting and streaming can create anti-school subcultures among those placed in lower sets.

Key term — self-fulfilling prophecy: a prediction that comes true simply because it was made and acted upon.

Education · achievement

Differences in achievement

AQA expects internal (in-school) and external (home) explanations for gaps in achievement:

  • Social class — material deprivation (poverty, poor housing) and cultural deprivation (less educational support); Bernstein's elaborated vs restricted speech codes; cultural capital (Bourdieu).
  • Gender — girls now generally outperform boys; explanations include changing ambitions, feminism, and the 'crisis of masculinity'.
  • Ethnicity — explanations include labelling, ethnocentric curriculum, and material factors.

Education · cultural capital

Cultural capital & speech codes

Two influential ideas about why middle-class pupils often do better:

  • Bourdieu — middle-class families pass on cultural capital (knowledge, tastes and confidence) that schools reward.
  • Bernstein — schools use the elaborated code of language; working-class pupils more used to the restricted code may be disadvantaged.

Evaluation: critics say these ideas risk blaming home culture and ignore in-school labelling and material poverty.

Education — the big ideas

Functions: solidarity (Durkheim), skills, meritocracy & role allocation (Parsons, Davis & Moore)
