Mission 1

Mission 1: Rainforest at the equator, desert a little further north. Why do the world's big ecosystems come in bands?

Make a guess: Rainforest at the equator, desert a little further north. Why do the world's big ecosystems come in bands?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 5.1a distribution and characteristics of biomes: tropical, temperate and boreal forests, tropical and temperate grasslands, deserts, tundra
Mission 1

What is a biome?

  • A biome is "a community of plants and animals living together in a certain kind of climate". Biomes are large-scale ecosystems.
  • Climate (temperature and rainfall) does the most to decide which biome grows where.
Mission 1

The world map of biomes

  • Biomes form rough bands across the map, by latitude.
  • Near the equator: tropical forest. Either side: tropical grassland, then hot desert.
  • Further from the equator: temperate grassland and temperate forest, then boreal forest, then tundra and polar ice.
Mission 1

Why the bands? Rising and sinking air

  • The equator gets more direct sunlight; the poles get less.
  • Near the equator warm air rises in the ITCZ (Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone), "forming thunderstorms": heavy rain all year feeds rainforest.
  • The air sinks over the subtropics (about 30°), "creating high-pressure regions over … deserts like the Sahara". Sinking air warms and dries, so clouds cannot form.
  • Near the poles there is "little or no solar energy in the winter", and the sun is at a low angle: cold biomes.
Quick check
?Why are many of the world's hot deserts found around 30° north and south?
Mission 1

Tropical rainforest

  • Where: between the Tropics of Cancer and Capricorn (e.g. Amazon, Congo).
  • Climate: hot all year (at Manaus, Brazil, every month averages 27–28 °C); 2,000–10,000 mm of rain a year.
  • Plants: giant trees up to 75 m form the canopy; vines, ferns and palms below.
  • Soil: leaf litter rots fast in the heat and wet, "sending nutrients back into the soil".
Mission 1

Hot deserts

  • Where: around 30° N and S under sinking air (e.g. Sahara).
  • Rain: about 250 mm a year; most get under 300 mm.
  • Temperature: days average 38 °C; nights fall to about −3.9 °C, because dry air lets heat escape.
  • Plants: cacti store water in their stems; some plants lie dormant until rain comes.
Mission 1

Grasslands: tropical and temperate

  • Rain: about 500–900 mm a year: too little for dense forest.
  • Tropical grassland (savanna): warm all year with a wet and a dry season; between rainforest and desert.
  • Temperate grassland: cold winters, warm summers: prairies (North America), pampas (South America), steppes (Central Eurasia), veldt (South Africa).
  • Plants: grasses die back to their roots each year.
Mission 1

Temperate and boreal forests

  • Temperate deciduous forest (eastern USA, Europe, China, Japan): 750–1,500 mm of rain; average 10 °C; four seasons; leaves fall in winter.
  • Boreal (coniferous) forest: about 50–60° N; winters very cold, summers around 10 °C; 300–900 mm of rain.
  • Conifers are evergreen, with needles that survive cold and dry conditions.
Mission 1

Tundra and polar regions

  • Tundra: south of the Arctic ice caps, across North America, Europe and Siberia.
  • Below 0 °C for 6 to 10 months; only 150–250 mm of rain a year.
  • Permafrost (permanently frozen ground) and a short growing season mean almost no trees: mosses, lichens, grasses and shrubs.
  • Polar regions: little or no winter sun; some areas are so dry they are polar deserts.
Quick check
?A climate graph shows: total rain 200 mm a year; monthly temperatures below 0 °C for eight months; warmest month about 6 °C. Which biome is it?
Mission check

?Imagine the belt of sinking air moved to about 25° north and south instead of 30°. Where would the hot deserts most likely have been?

Biomes form bands because air rises near the equator (rain, rainforest) and sinks around 30° (dry, deserts like the Sahara).

Mission 2

Mission 2: Forests, reefs and rivers feed, shelter and protect us for free. So why do we keep cutting them down?

Make a guess: Forests, reefs and rivers feed, shelter and protect us for free. So why do we keep cutting them down?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 5.1b goods and services from natural ecosystems: timber, tourism, food, energy, water, health, natural protection, climate regulation
Mission 2

Goods and services

  • The biosphere is the living layer of the Earth: plants, animals and the soils and waters they live in.
  • Ecosystem services are "the benefits people obtain from ecosystems".
  • Goods: things we take — food, water, timber, fuel, medicine.
  • Services: things ecosystems do for us — protect coasts, regulate climate, clean water, attract tourists.
Mission 2

The goods

  • Food: crops, fish, wild fruit and game.
  • Medicine: "Many drugs are now being developed from compounds discovered in coral reef animals and plants".
  • Building materials: timber, bamboo, thatch.
  • Fuel: wood and charcoal. In 2024 only 22% of people in sub-Saharan Africa had clean cooking, against 75% worldwide; most of the rest cook with fuels such as wood.
  • Also water and energy: rivers and wetlands supply fresh water; forests supply fuelwood.
Quick check
?Which of these is a good, not a service, from an ecosystem?
Mission 2

The services

  • Natural protection: mangroves "stabilize the coastline, reducing erosion from storm surges, currents, waves, and tides".
  • Climate regulation: about half the CO₂ people emit each year stays in the air; "the rest is absorbed by Earth's land ecosystems and oceans".
  • Tourism: Australia's Great Barrier Reef is reported to contribute $9 billion a year to the economy (a study quoted by the park authority).
  • Health: medicines from reefs and forests, clean water and clean air.
Mission 2

Why services get lost

  • Goods can be sold; most services are free, so nobody is paid to keep them.
  • Clearing an ecosystem for goods can remove its services: cut the mangroves and the coast loses its protection.
  • Exam move: name the good and the service, then explain the trade-off between them.
Mission 2

Using goods can cost services

  • Between 2000 and 2020, the biggest single cause of mangrove loss worldwide was aquaculture (fish and shrimp ponds): 27% of losses.
  • The pond owner gains a good (shrimp to sell). The coast loses a service (protection from waves).
  • The people who gain are often not the people who lose.
Mission check

?A government plans to clear a mangrove forest on a low coast to build shrimp ponds for export. Which outcome is most likely?

Ecosystems give goods that can be sold (shrimp, timber) and services that are usually free (protection, climate regulation). Clearing for goods is why ecosystems keep being cut down: the losses fall on people who are not paid for them.

Mission 3

Mission 3: A lettuce can grow with its roots in water, not soil. Is that still farming?

Make a guess: A lettuce can grow with its roots in water, not soil. Is that still farming?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 5.1c farming systems that use and modify ecosystems: arable/pastoral, commercial/subsistence, intensive/extensive
Mission 3

A farm is a system

  • Inputs go in: physical (sunlight, rain, soil, temperature) and human (labour, seeds, animals, fertiliser, machines, money).
  • Processes happen on the farm: ploughing, sowing, feeding, milking, harvesting.
  • Outputs come out: crops, milk, meat, wool, income, and also waste and pollution.
  • Some outputs are fed back in, such as manure or money for next year's seed.
  • Not every farm uses soil. In hydroponics, roots grow in a water-based nutrient solution instead of soil.
Quick check
?On a dairy farm, which is a process?
Mission 3

Arable, pastoral, mixed

  • Arable: growing crops (wheat, rice, vegetables).
  • Pastoral: rearing animals (cattle, sheep, goats) for meat, milk or wool.
  • Mixed: both crops and animals on the same farm.
  • Arable needs fertile, flatter land; pastoral often uses steeper, wetter or drier land unsuited to crops.
Mission 3

Subsistence and commercial

  • Subsistence: food grown mainly to feed the farmer's family, with little left to sell.
  • Commercial: food grown to sell for profit, often on a larger scale.
  • The world has more than 570 million farms, most of them small and family-run.
  • Small farms (under 2 hectares) work only about 12% of the world's farmland.
Quick check
?A family grows maize and beans on a small plot and eats almost all of it. What type of farming is this?
Mission 3

Intensive and extensive

  • Intensive: high inputs of labour, money, fertiliser or machinery on a small area, giving high output per hectare (e.g. greenhouses, rice paddies).
  • Extensive: low inputs spread over a large area (e.g. cattle ranching, hill sheep farming).
  • Any farm can be described three ways: arable/pastoral, subsistence/commercial, intensive/extensive.
Mission 3

How farming uses and changes ecosystems

  • Uses: soil, water, sunlight and natural grassland or forest.
  • Changes: natural vegetation is cleared and replaced by one or a few crops (monoculture).
  • Nutrient cycle: fertiliser is added; nutrients leave in harvested crops.
  • Water cycle: irrigation and drainage change how water moves.
  • Biodiversity: intensive methods usually reduce it; organic farms had about 30% more species on average in one review of 94 studies.
Quick check
?Which change by an intensive arable farm most changes the local nutrient cycle?
Mission 3

Describe any farm

  • Hill sheep farm: pastoral, commercial. Also extensive.
  • Family rice paddy feeding a household: arable, subsistence. Also intensive.
  • Wheat and cattle farm: mixed, commercial.
Mission check

?A rooftop greenhouse grows tomatoes in nutrient-rich water. It uses electricity, workers and seed, and sells tomatoes to restaurants. Why does it count as a farming system?

Yes, it is farming. Any farm, with soil or without, is a system: inputs (light, water, nutrients, labour, money) go through processes (planting, feeding, harvesting) to make outputs (food, income, waste).

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why are some UK villages growing fast while others are losing people?

Make a guess: Why are some UK villages growing fast while others are losing people?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 5.2a characteristics of a rural environment
  • 5.2b rural change in a named developed country (isolation, farm job decline, tourism, suburbanisation, counter-urbanisation, negative multiplier)
  • 5.3 rural case study in a developed country
Mission 4

Rural England in numbers

  • In 2024, 17% of England's people lived in rural settlements; 27% of them were aged 65+.
  • Farming makes up 14% of rural businesses but only 8% of rural employment.
  • Rural people travel further, mostly by car, and have poorer access to services.
  • Describe a rural area by its landscape, climate, settlement, population, land use, employment, accessibility and management.
Mission 4

Two kinds of rural change

  • Growth near cities: people move out of towns into villages (counter-urbanisation); towns spread outwards (suburbanisation).
  • Decline in remote areas: more people leave or die than arrive or are born (rural depopulation).
  • In England, people on average move into rural areas, except 15–19-year-olds, who move out.
Mission 4

Growth: South Cambridgeshire

  • A rural district around the city of Cambridge.
  • Population: 148,800 (2011) → 162,000 (2021), up 8.9%; England grew 6.6%.
  • People aged 65+ rose by 28.7%; children under 15 by 8.5%.
Mission 4

Northstowe: a new town in the fields

  • Northstowe, South Cambridgeshire, is planned to have 10,000 homes and about 25,000 people.
  • New schools, roads, public transport routes, cycleways and green space are part of the plan.
  • Social change: new families and commuters; pressure on services until new ones are built.
  • Economic change: building jobs; more customers for local shops.
Quick check
?A family moves from Cambridge to a nearby village and the parents still work in the city. What process is this?
Mission 4

Decline: the Outer Hebrides

  • Na h-Eileanan Siar (Outer Hebrides): islands off north-west Scotland.
  • Population fell 5.5% between the 2011 and 2022 censuses: the biggest fall of any Scottish council area.
  • It has Scotland's lowest population density.
  • In Scotland's shrinking areas, mostly in the west and south-west, more people died than were born.
Mission 4

What changes when people leave?

  • 13.7% of people are aged 55+ and living alone: the highest share in Scotland.
  • Only 19.7% of households have dependent children, among the lowest in Scotland.
  • Social change: an ageing population; fewer children for schools; more need for care.
  • Economic change: fewer workers and customers, so shops, buses and services are harder to keep open.
Quick check
?South Cambridgeshire grew from about 148,800 to 162,000 people (2011–2021). How many extra people is that?
Mission 4

Forces of change in a developed country

  • Rural isolation: remote places far from jobs and services.
  • Decline in farm jobs: farming now gives only 8% of rural jobs in England.
  • Tourist pressures: in majority-rural areas, more than 2 in every 100 homes is a second home, twice the national average.
  • Suburbanisation and counter-urbanisation near cities (South Cambridgeshire).
Mission 4

The negative multiplier

  • Negative multiplier effect: young people leave → fewer customers and pupils → shop, school or bus closes → fewer jobs and services → more people leave.
  • Each loss makes the next more likely: a downward spiral.
  • It explains why remote areas such as the Outer Hebrides find decline hard to reverse.
Mission check

?Village X is 10 km from a growing city and has a railway station. Village Y is on a remote peninsula, two hours from any city. What is most likely?

Accessibility to city jobs decides it. South Cambridgeshire, around Cambridge, grew 8.9% (2011–21); the remote Outer Hebrides fell 5.5% (2011–22).

Remote decline can become a negative multiplier: services close, and more people leave.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Most Indians still live in villages. Why do so many young villagers leave for the cities?

Make a guess: Most Indians still live in villages. Why do so many young villagers leave for the cities?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 5.2a characteristics of a rural environment
  • 5.2c rural change in a named developing or emerging country (population growth, changing farm economy and landholdings, natural hazards, rural-urban migration)
  • 5.3 rural case study in a developing or emerging country
Mission 5

Rural India

  • About two-thirds of Indians live in rural areas.
  • In 2024, 35.4% of India's people lived in urban areas, so about 65% were rural (our calculation).
  • About 70% of rural households depend mainly on farming.
Mission 5

What is it like?

  • Climate: farming depends on the monsoon; about 60% of farmland is rain-fed (not irrigated).
  • Land use: over 60% of India's land is farmed.
  • Employment: 41.6% of India's workers were in agriculture in 2025.
  • Settlement: villages of small family farms. Accessibility: varies, from roadside villages to remote hill settlements.
Quick check
?Which fact best shows that rural India is vulnerable to drought?
Mission 5

More people, smaller farms

  • When a farmer dies, land is often divided between children. As families grow, each plot gets smaller.
  • Average farm holding: 1.23 ha (2005–06) → 1.10 ha (2010–11) → 1.08 ha (2015–16).
  • The government lists fragmented landholdings as a key challenge.
  • About 30% of rural households have no land at all.
Quick check
?The average holding fell from 1.23 ha to 1.08 ha. What is the percentage fall?
Mission 5

A changing farm economy

  • Share of workers in farming: 63.1% (1991) → 41.6% (2025).
  • Yet farming produced only 16.2% of India's GDP in 2025: many workers share a small slice of income.
  • Small plots need small, affordable machines; the government supports hire centres.
  • PM-KISAN pays farmers direct income support.
Mission 5

Natural hazards

  • Rain-fed farming is "vulnerable to the vagaries of the monsoon".
  • In 2014–15, erratic rains damaged the winter crop; farm growth slipped to 1.1%.
  • The government lists climate change as a growing challenge for farming.
  • A failed harvest can push families into debt, or send a member to find work elsewhere.
Mission 5

Rural poverty

  • Multidimensional poverty (health, education, living standards) in rural India: 32.6% (2015–16) → 19.3% (2019–21).
  • In urban India: 8.7% → 5.3%.
  • Rural poverty fell fast, but is still about 3.7 times the urban rate (our calculation from the unrounded 19.28% and 5.27%).
Mission 5

Moving away to work

  • Census data suggest about 5–6.5 million people a year moved between states in 2001–11; railway data suggest nearly 9 million a year since 2011.
  • These figures count all moves between states, not only moves from villages to cities.
  • Poorer states such as Bihar and Uttar Pradesh have high net out-migration.
  • Among people aged 20–29, out-of-state net migrants in 2001–11 exceeded 11 million; nearly 80% were male.
Mission 5

What migration does to villages

  • Remittances (money sent home) raise household spending in poorer home regions.
  • With many young men away, farm work falls more on those who stay: over 75% of rural women workers work in agriculture.
  • Local jobs can slow migration: farm assets built under the rural jobs scheme MGNREGS were linked to less migration.
Quick check
?Which is a pull factor drawing young people from rural India to cities?
Mission check

?In a farming village in another emerging country, each generation splits the family land, and the rains have failed twice in five years. A factory zone 300 km away is hiring. Which change would most likely reduce the number of young people leaving?

In rural India, shrinking holdings (1.23 → 1.08 ha), rain-fed farming (60%) and poverty push people out; city jobs pull them in. Farm ponds and wells built under the rural jobs scheme were linked to less migration.

Mission 6

Mission 6: What keeps a countryside community alive when farming alone no longer pays?

Make a guess: What keeps a countryside community alive when farming alone no longer pays?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 5.3 case studies of rural environments in a developed and a developing or emerging country
  • 5.3a farm diversification: GM crops, specialist crops and food, organic farming, recreation and leisure
  • 5.3b strategies to make rural living sustainable and improve quality of life
  • 5.3c roles of government, IGOs, NGOs and local communities
Mission 6

Why diversify?

  • In rural England, farming gives only 8% of jobs.
  • Defra calls diversification a "rational response to the changing position of agriculture in the economy".
  • Farm diversification: earning new income from non-farming uses of the farm's land, buildings or skills.
Mission 6

How English farms diversify

  • In 2024/25, 72% of farm businesses in England had a diversified activity, 10 points more than in 2015/16.
  • Most common: letting out buildings (50% of farms) and solar energy (27%).
  • Average income from diversification: £31,100 per diversifying farm; sport and recreation earned least (£3,600).
Quick check
?Which is an example of farm diversification?
Mission 6

Recreation, leisure and specialist food

  • Recreation and leisure: glamping, event venues, farm stays. The Rural England Prosperity Fund lists "development of glamping site on farm" as a fundable project.
  • Specialist crops and food: higher-value local products, e.g. organic eggs and malt beer sold in the Great Ryburgh community shop, Norfolk.
  • Benefit: more income per hectare; jobs for local people.
Mission 6

Organic farming

  • Organic: no synthetic (man-made) fertilisers or pesticides; this improves soil health and reduces pollution.
  • UK: 540,000 ha farmed organically in 2025, up 7.3% on 2024; 5,004 organic operators.
  • India: 4.43 million (44.3 lakh) organic farmers, the most in the world; Sikkim became the first fully organic state.
  • India's PKVY scheme gives farmers ₹50,000 per hectare over three years to switch.
Mission 6

GM and gene-edited crops

  • GM crops carry genes changed in the lab, e.g. for pest resistance or higher yields.
  • In the UK, releasing GM organisms is controlled by strict legislation.
  • England now allows precision-bred (gene-edited) plants, whose changes "could have also been achieved through traditional breeding", once authorised.
  • For: possible higher yields, fewer sprays. Against: public concern, cost, strict rules.
Quick check
?In 2024/25, 72% of English farm businesses had diversified, 10 percentage points more than in 2015/16. What was the figure in 2015/16?
Mission 6

Better rural living: India

  • Water: the Jal Jeevan Mission aimed to give every rural home a tap by 2024, using a "community approach".
  • Employment: the MGNREGS rural jobs scheme built farm ponds, wells and animal sheds on farmers' land; these were linked to higher income and less migration.
  • Soil and crop yields: organic and natural farming to improve soil health.
  • Employment for women: self-help groups give savings and loans.
Mission 6

Better rural living: UK

  • Housing: outside London, majority-rural areas built the most affordable homes relative to their housing stock.
  • Jobs and services: the Rural England Prosperity Fund pays for capital projects for small rural businesses and community buildings.
  • Community services: a village-owned shop, post office and café at Great Ryburgh.
Mission 6

Who manages rural India?

  • National government: MGNREGS, PM-KISAN income support, organic schemes.
  • Local communities: about 12 million (1.2 crore) self-help groups, 88% all-women; cooperatives with 290 million (29 crore) members.
  • NGOs: the self-help group movement traces its roots to the Self-Employed Women's Association (1972).
  • IGOs: IFAD (a UN agency) has given India US$1.4 billion for rural projects.
Mission 6

Who manages rural England?

  • National government: Defra's Rural England Prosperity Fund.
  • Local government: councils bid for and spend that fund.
  • NGOs and charities: the Plunkett Foundation and Norfolk Community Foundation lent or gave money for Great Ryburgh's shop.
  • Local communities: 124 residents bought shares to help raise £130,000.
  • IGOs: until the UK left the EU, the EU's LEADER programme funded rural projects; this fund replaced it.
Quick check
?IFAD, which funds rural projects in India, is which type of organisation?
Mission check

?A hill farm in Wales earns too little from sheep alone. Each plan below brings in new money. Which is most sustainable socially, economically and environmentally?

Rural communities survive when they diversify income (72% of English farms do) and when governments, IGOs, NGOs and communities each play a part, from Great Ryburgh's shop to India's self-help groups.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Biomes are large-scale ecosystems; their distribution follows climate: rainforest at the equator, deserts at about 30°, temperate and boreal forest further out, tundra and ice near the poles.

Mission 2

  • Goods (food, medicine, building materials, fuel, water) are taken; services (protection, climate regulation, tourism, health) are provided.

Mission 3

  • A farm is a system of inputs, processes and outputs, described as arable/pastoral/mixed and subsistence/commercial. Also intensive/extensive; farming uses and changes ecosystems' nutrient cycles, water and biodiversity.

Mission 4

  • Growth area (South Cambridgeshire, +8.9%): counter-urbanisation and new towns such as Northstowe; more homes, schools and traffic. Decline area (Outer Hebrides, −5.5%): an ageing population, more deaths than births, fewer families.

Mission 5

  • Rural India: about two-thirds of Indians; mostly small, rain-fed family farms; 41.6% of workers in agriculture (2025).
  • Change: population growth splits farms (1.23 → 1.08 ha), the farm economy is shrinking as a share of jobs, the monsoon brings hazards, and millions move between states each year, many for city work, sending remittances home.

Mission 6

  • Diversification: letting buildings, solar, tourism, specialist and organic food; GM and gene-edited crops are tightly regulated in the UK.
  • Sustainable rural living needs water, jobs, housing and services. Roles: national and local government (funds, schemes), IGOs (IFAD, formerly EU LEADER), NGOs (SEWA, Plunkett Foundation) and local communities (self-help groups, community shops).
