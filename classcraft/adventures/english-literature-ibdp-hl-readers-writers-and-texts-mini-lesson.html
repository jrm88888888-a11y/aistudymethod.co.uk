Readers, Writers and Texts

Readers, writers and texts is the first of the three areas of exploration. Its question is deceptively simple: how is a literary text made, and how does a reader make meaning from it? The focus is the text itself — the writer’s choices and the effects they create — and your part in completing them.

  • Foregrounds the concepts of communication, creativity, perspective and representation.
  • Trains the close-analysis muscle you will use in Paper 1 (the guided analysis of an unseen text) and in the content and form thinking of the Individual Oral.
  • At HL, you study thirteen works across the whole course; this area is where you learn to read any of them precisely.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go and collect ⭐ stars. Any named work appears only as a swappable, illustrative example — the IB reading lists are chosen by your school and rotate, so this lesson teaches the skills and ideas, never a set text. Press Start when you are ready.

Close reading

How, not just what

To read as a literary critic is to attend to how a text means, not only what it says. Paraphrase reports the content; analysis explains the craft that produced an effect.

  • Diction — word choice and its connotations.
  • Imagery & figuration — metaphor, simile, symbol, personification.
  • Syntax & structure — sentence shape, order, sequence, framing.
  • Sound — rhythm, metre, rhyme, alliteration, assonance.
  • Voice & point of view — who speaks, who sees, and with what attitude (tone).

The test of close reading: could your sentence be true of almost any text? If so, it is feature-spotting. Real analysis ties a specific choice to a specific effect and then to meaning.

Literary form

The toolkit across the three forms

The same reading skills apply across all three literary forms, but each form has its own toolkit.

  • Poetry — lineation, enjambment, caesura, metre, rhyme, stanza, the volta (a turn in thought).
  • Prose fiction — narrative voice, focalisation (whose eyes we see through), free indirect discourse, pace, dialogue, motif.
  • Drama — dialogue, soliloquy, aside, stage directions, structure of acts and scenes, dramatic irony.

Illustrative only: a poet might place a line-break so a word hangs, momentarily, over empty space before the sentence completes (an enjambment) — the form enacts hesitation. Swap in whatever poem you are actually studying.

The reader

The reader completes the text

A text is not a sealed message; it is a set of prompts that you complete. Meaning is co-produced by writer and reader.

  • Gaps — what a text withholds invites inference. A skilled reader notices silence.
  • Inference — a defensible reading is grounded in evidence, not imported wholesale.
  • Plurality — more than one interpretation can be valid, provided each is anchored in the text.

Communication as a concept: literature communicates indirectly — through implication, image and structure — which is exactly why it needs a reader willing to do interpretive work.

Diction

Denotation and connotation

Words carry two kinds of meaning at once.

  • Denotation — the dictionary meaning.
  • Connotation — the associations and feelings a word summons. A choice between near-synonyms (slim / scrawny / slender) is a choice of connotation, and therefore of attitude.
  • Tone — the attitude a text takes to its subject; mood is the feeling it creates in the reader.
  • Ambiguity — deliberate openness of meaning, a resource rather than a fault.

Form

Form makes meaning

Form is not decoration wrapped around content; form makes meaning. A structural fact can be an argument.

  • An enjambment can spill sense across a line-break to mimic overflow; a caesura can stop a line dead.
  • Free indirect discourse lets a third-person narrator borrow a character’s idiom, quietly making that character’s assumptions the reader’s own.
  • Dramatic irony gives the audience knowledge a character lacks, turning an ordinary line into a threat or a joke.

Ask of any effect: what does this do to my experience as a reader, and why here?

Method

From observation to argument

Turn observation into argument with a repeatable move.

  1. Claim — a precise assertion about the text’s effect or meaning.
  2. Evidence — a short, exact quotation or a structural fact.
  3. Analysis — explain the method: what the writer did and how it works.
  4. Significance — why it matters to the whole text’s meaning.

Representation is the concept lurking here: a text does not mirror reality, it constructs a version of it. Analysing method is analysing how that construction is achieved.

Concepts

The concepts in this area

Four of the seven course concepts live most naturally in this area.

  • Communication — how meaning is transmitted, and what is lost or gained in the transmission.
  • Creativity — the inventive shaping of language and form.
  • Perspective — the position from which a text sees, and asks us to see.
  • Representation — how reality is selected, arranged and constructed on the page.

Keeping a reader’s journal (the IB’s learner portfolio) as you read is where these skills are rehearsed before assessment.

Assessment

How it feeds the assessments

This area feeds directly into assessment.

  • Paper 1 asks you to analyse an unseen literary passage — pure Readers, writers and texts under exam conditions.
  • The Individual Oral demands attention to content and form together — the habit this area builds.
  • Paper 2 and the HL essay still reward close textual analysis over generalisation.

Golden rule: never lose contact with the words on the page. Every idea returns to a specific choice a writer made.
