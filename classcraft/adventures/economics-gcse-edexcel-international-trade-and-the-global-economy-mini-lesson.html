Mission 1

Mission 1: Why do countries buy so much from abroad, and does open trade leave everyone better off?

Make a guess: Why do countries buy so much from abroad, and does open trade leave everyone better off?

Mission 1

Why countries trade

  • Export: a good or service sold to another country. Import: one bought from another country.
  • Countries import because:
    • they cannot make it at all: no oil, the wrong climate for coffee or bananas
    • it is cheaper to make abroad: lower costs, better technology, larger scale
    • foreign goods offer choice, variety or better quality
  • Countries export to earn the money that pays for imports, and to reach a much bigger market.
  • Free trade: trade between countries with no barriers such as tariffs (taxes on imports) or quotas (limits on the amount imported).
Mission 1

Worked example: gains from specialising

  • Illustrative data. Each country has 10 workers. One worker can make in a day:
Rice (kg)Shirts
Country A102
Country B48
  • No trade: each puts 5 workers on each good.
    • A: 50 rice, 10 shirts. B: 20 rice, 40 shirts.
    • Total: 70 rice, 50 shirts.
  • Specialise: A makes only rice, B only shirts.
    • A: 10 × 10 = 100 rice. B: 10 × 8 = 80 shirts.
  • The world gains 30 rice and 30 shirts from the same workers. Trade lets both countries share the gain.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. Each country has 10 workers. One worker in X makes 8 bikes or 2 tables a day; one worker in Y makes 2 bikes or 6 tables. With no trade, each country puts half its workers on each good. If X makes only bikes and Y only tables, how much extra output is produced?
Mission 1

Trade patterns: richer and poorer countries

  • Many developing countries export mainly primary products (crops, minerals, oil) or low-cost manufactures such as clothing.
    • Bangladesh: ready-made garments were about 85% of its exports (2022–23).
  • Developed countries export more high-value manufactures and services, as the UK does.
  • Depending on a few exports is risky: if world prices or demand fall, export earnings and jobs fall.
  • Patterns change: some developing countries, such as China, have become major exporters of manufactures.
Mission 1

Free trade: the case for

  • Lower prices and more choice for consumers.
  • Firms reach bigger markets: more sales, economies of scale, lower costs.
  • Competition from abroad pushes home firms to be more efficient.
  • Countries can specialise: total output rises.
  • Access to raw materials, parts and technology from abroad.
  • Exports create jobs in successful industries.
Mission 1

Free trade: the case against

  • Home firms that cannot compete may close: job losses, often in particular towns and regions.
  • New industries (infant industries) may struggle to grow against established foreign rivals.
  • Dumping: foreign firms may sell below cost to win the market.
  • Over-specialisation: a country depending on one or two exports is hit hard if their prices fall.
  • More transport means more emissions; production may move to countries with weaker environmental or labour rules.
  • The gains are often spread thinly (slightly lower prices for many), while the losses are concentrated (whole factories close).
Quick check
?A country removes all barriers to imports of cheap steel. Which group is most likely to lose?
Mission 1

Weigh it up: does free trade leave everyone better off?

It depends on:

  • Who you are: consumers and exporters usually gain; workers in industries facing imports may lose.
  • The time scale: job losses come fast; gains from lower prices and new industries build up over years.
  • Help for those who lose: retraining and support can share the gains more widely.
  • The country's stage of development: some argue young industries need time and protection to grow; others say protection keeps them inefficient.
  • Most economists agree trade raises total output. Whether it is fair depends on how the gains are shared.
Quick check
?Which is an advantage of specialisation by a country?
Mission check

?A country removes its tariff on imported shoes. Shoes from abroad become cheaper. What is the most likely result?

Free trade usually makes a country better off in total, but not everyone in it.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why did the world start trading so much more, and does everyone come out ahead?

Mission 2

What is globalisation?

  • Globalisation: the world's economies becoming more and more connected and dependent on each other.
  • Its main features:
    • more trade in goods and services
    • more investment across borders: firms build or buy factories abroad
    • more movement of people for work and study
    • global supply chains: one product made in stages in many countries
    • more multinational companies (MNCs), and the spread of technology and ideas.
  • Example: a phone designed in one country, with chips from a second and a screen from a third, assembled in a fourth and sold everywhere.
  • MNCs are also called multinational corporations.
Mission 2

Mission 2

Why globalisation sped up

  • Lower tariffs and quotas: countries cut trade barriers through world trade talks (GATT from 1947, then the WTO from 1995) and trade deals (see Mission 4).
  • Lower transport costs: containers, larger ships, cheaper air freight.
  • Lower communication costs: the internet and mobile phones let one firm run a supply chain across continents.
  • Technology: new products and ways of working spread quickly.
  • Growth of MNCs: firms spreading production and sales across many countries.
  • More countries joined world markets: China (from 1978, WTO member 2001), Eastern Europe and the former Soviet Union from 1989 to 1991.
Mission 2

Multinationals and FDI

  • A multinational company (MNC): a firm with its head office in one country (the home country) and production or offices in others (host countries).
  • Foreign direct investment (FDI): a firm builds or buys a business abroad.
  • Why MNCs invest abroad:
    • to be close to customers in big markets
    • lower costs: wages, land or energy
    • to get raw materials
    • to get inside trade barriers: a factory inside a bloc sells tariff-free to all members (see Mission 4)
    • lower taxes in some countries.
Mission 2

MNCs: effects on host and home countries

Possible benefitsPossible costs
Host countryJobs; new skills and technology; tax revenue; more exportsProfits sent back to the home country; local firms may close; low pay or poor conditions; pollution
Home countryProfits flow home; bigger, more competitive firmsJobs lost if production moves abroad; less tax if profits are booked elsewhere
  • Profit repatriation: the MNC sends profits from the host country back home, so the host keeps less of the income.
  • Tax avoidance: an MNC may record its profits in a low-tax country, even if its sales are elsewhere. It is legal, but it cuts tax revenue where the firm really works.
  • Real case: a global minimum tax of 15% on large MNCs, agreed through the OECD, began to apply in many countries from 2024.
Quick check
?A car maker based in Country H opens a new factory in Country L. Which effect on Country L is most likely?
Mission 2

Developed countries, including the UK

Possible gains:

  • Cheaper imports: incomes go further; more choice.
  • Bigger export markets, especially for services such as finance, education and business services.
  • Investment from foreign MNCs, e.g. car factories in the UK.

Possible costs:

  • Firms and workers that compete with imports can lose sales and jobs: import-competing manufacturing shrinks.
  • Structural unemployment in towns that relied on those industries.
  • Shocks abroad, such as a world recession, spread quickly.
Mission 2

Less developed countries

Possible gains:

  • Export-led growth: jobs in factories making goods for the world.
  • FDI brings money, skills and technology.
  • Real data (World Bank): the share of the world's people living on under $3.00 a day fell from 43.4% in 1990 to 10.1% in 2024. That fall happened while trade grew; how much trade caused it is debated.

Possible costs:

  • Low pay and poor, sometimes unsafe, working conditions.
  • Dependence on one or two exports with prices that swing.
  • Weak rules on pollution.
  • Some of the poorest countries are left out; inequality can widen.
Quick check
?A low-income country's clothing exports have grown fast since foreign firms opened factories there. Which is the most likely cost for the country?
Mission 2

Ethics and sustainability

  • Working conditions: should firms that buy from abroad check that suppliers pay fairly and keep workers safe?
  • Environment: shipping and flying goods around the world adds to carbon emissions; factories may move to countries with weaker pollution rules.
  • Fair trade schemes pay producers a minimum price.
  • Sustainability: can growth from trade last without using up resources that future generations need?
  • These are normative questions: economics can show the costs and benefits, but the answers depend on values.
Mission 2

Weigh it up: does everyone come out ahead?

It depends on:

  • Which group: consumers and exporters gain; workers in industries that compete with imports may lose.
  • What the country sells: a country with many different exports is less exposed than one selling one crop.
  • Help for those who lose: training, help to move and benefits can spread the gains.
  • Time: job losses come fast; gains in incomes build up slowly.
  • Say: "globalisation increases interdependence" — not: "globalisation makes everyone better off".
Mission check

?A clothing firm moves its factory from Country D, a developed country, to Country E, a less developed country with lower wages. Which outcome is most likely?

Globalisation's gains are real but uneven: who gains and who loses depends on where you work and what you buy.

Mission 3

Mission 3: When a country taxes goods from abroad, who ends up paying, and who comes out ahead?

Make a guess: When a country taxes goods from abroad, who ends up paying, and who comes out ahead?

Mission 3

Free trade and the world price

Illustrative data: a small country's market for rice.

  • With no trade, home demand and home supply meet at a price of 7: 50 tonnes are bought.
  • Open the border. The country can buy as much as it wants at the world price (Pw) of 4.
  • At 4:
    • home buyers want 80
    • home farmers supply only 20 (the higher-cost farmers stop)
    • the gap, 80 − 20 = 60, comes in as imports.
  • A small country is a price taker: it buys too little to change the world price.
  • Say: "domestic supply" — not just "supply": with trade, imports add to what is on sale.
Mission 3

The protectionist toolbox

Protectionism: government measures that shield domestic producers from foreign competition.

  • Tariff: a tax on imports.
  • Quota: a legal limit on the quantity of a good that may be imported.
  • Subsidy: a payment to domestic producers, so they can sell more cheaply than imports.
  • Embargo: a ban on trade in a good, or with a country.
  • Red tape (administrative barriers): slow border checks, forms and strict standards that make importing harder.
Quick check
?A government says imported cars may only enter through one small port, and each car needs 40 pages of forms checked by hand. There is no tax and no limit on numbers. Which tool of protection is this?
Mission 3

The tariff diagram

Same rice market (illustrative data). The government adds a tariff of 2 per tonne.

1. Free trade at Pw = 4: home output 20, home buying 80, imports 60.

2. Imported rice now costs 4 + 2 = 6. Home farmers can charge 6 too: the new line is Pw + tariff.

3. Home output rises to 40; home buying falls to 60; imports shrink to 60 − 40 = 20.

4. Tariff revenue = tariff × imports = 2 × 20 = 40: the shaded rectangle.

  • Two small triangles show value lost to the country: high-cost home farmers replace cheaper imports, and some buyers stop buying.
  • Say: "home output rises along the supply curve" — not: "supply increases": the curve does not move.
Mission 3

Worked example: where does the money go?

Illustrative data, from the tariff diagram:

  • Home buyers pay the higher price: 6 instead of 4 on every tonne, including rice grown at home.
Mission 3Graph lab

Raise the tariff

Illustrative data. The tariff is 1 per tonne: price 5, imports 40, tariff revenue 40.

  • Takeaway: a bigger tariff cuts imports, so revenue does not keep rising. At a tariff of 3 imports stop and revenue is zero.
Mission 3

Quotas and subsidies on a diagram

Same market (illustrative data): Pw 4, imports 60.

  • Quota of 20 tonnes: draw S + quota, 20 units to the right of domestic supply.
    • The price rises to 6, as with the tariff of 2: home output 40, home buying 60.
    • But the government collects no revenue. The extra 2 per tonne goes to whoever holds the import licences.
  • Subsidy of 2 per tonne to home farmers: domestic supply shifts right (down by 2).
    • The price stays at Pw = 4, so buyers still get 80 tonnes.
    • Home output rises 20 → 40; imports fall 60 → 40.
    • Taxpayers pay 2 × 40 = 80.
Quick check
?A government wants to cut rice imports without raising the price that shoppers pay. Which tool should it choose?
Mission 3

Why protect?

Reasons governments give:

  • Infant industries: new firms need time to grow big enough to compete.
  • Dumping: stop foreign firms selling below their costs to drive home firms out.
  • Jobs: protect workers in industries hit by imports.
  • Revenue: tariffs raise money for the government.
  • Safety: keep out unsafe goods.
  • Current account: fewer imports can cut a deficit.
  • Retaliation: hit back when another country puts up barriers.
Quick check
?A foreign firm sells steel in Country P at a price below what it costs to make. Country P's steel makers are losing sales fast. Country P puts a tariff on this firm's steel. Which reason for protection does this fit best?
Mission 3

Real case: washing machines, 2018

  • In 2018 the USA put tariffs on imported washing machines.
  • Researchers (Flaaen, Hortaçsu and Tintelnot, 2020) found:
    • washer prices rose by nearly 12%
    • dryers were not taxed, but their prices rose by about the same amount. People often buy the two as a pair, so sellers raised both.
  • Washers made in the USA went up in price too.
  • Lesson: buyers paid more, even for goods the tariff did not touch.
Mission 3

Consequences: weigh it up

Possible gains:

  • Home producers sell more, at higher prices; jobs may be saved, at least for a time.
  • Tariffs raise revenue.
  • Infant industries may grow strong enough to compete.

Possible costs:

  • Higher prices and less choice for consumers.
  • Firms that use imported inputs face higher costs.
  • Protected firms may become inefficient: less pressure to cut costs.
  • Retaliation: other countries tax this country's exports, so exporters lose jobs.
  • Subsidies cost taxpayers.

It depends on: how long protection lasts, whether other countries retaliate, and whether protected firms use the time to improve.

Mission check

?A small country puts a tariff on imported bicycles. It buys too few bicycles to change the world price. What is most likely to happen in its bicycle market?

In a small country, home buyers pay for a tariff through higher prices; home firms and the government gain.

Mission 4

What is a free-trade agreement?

  • A free-trade agreement (FTA): two or more countries agree to remove tariffs and quotas on trade between them.
  • A trading bloc: a group of countries joined by such an agreement.
  • Members trade freely with each other. Non-members still face each member's barriers.
  • Examples:
    • USMCA: the United States, Mexico and Canada
    • ASEAN: 11 countries in South-East Asia
    • the UK and Australia (in force 2023)
  • Say: "free trade between members" — not: "free trade with the world".
Mission 4

Three levels of bloc

LevelInside the blocTowards outsiders
Free trade areaNo tariffs or quotasEach member sets its own tariffs
Customs unionNo tariffs or quotasOne common external tariff for all members
Single marketNo tariffs, quotas or most other barriers; free movement of goods, services, money and peopleOne common external tariff
  • Each level joins the members more closely.
  • Example: Mercosur (South America) is a customs union.
  • The EU is a customs union and a single market.
Quick check
?Countries A, B and C trade with no tariffs between them. Each still sets its own tariff on goods from outside. What kind of bloc is this?
Mission 4

Worked example: the tariff-free price

Illustrative data. A machine costs 20,000 to make in Country F. Country H puts a 10% tariff on machines from outside its bloc.

  • If F is outside H's bloc: tariff = 10% × 20,000 = 2,000. Buyers in H pay 22,000.
  • If F is a member: no tariff. Buyers in H pay 20,000.
  • A rival from Country G, outside the bloc, makes the same machine for 19,000.
    • In H it costs 19,000 + 1,900 = 20,900.
    • So buyers in H choose F's machine, even though G makes it more cheaply.
  • Members gain an edge in each other's markets. Outsiders lose sales.
Mission 4

The European Union

  • The EU: 27 member countries.
  • A customs union: no tariffs between members; one common tariff on goods from outside.
  • A single market: free movement of goods, services, money and people; shared product rules, so a good sold in one member can be sold in all.
  • The EU also signs free trade agreements with other countries as one group, such as Japan and Canada.
Mission 4

Members and non-members

For members:

  • Firms sell to a bigger market without tariffs: more sales, economies of scale.
  • Consumers get lower prices and more choice from member countries.
  • More competition pushes firms to be efficient.
  • More investment from abroad: firms build factories inside the bloc to sell tariff-free to all members.

For non-members:

  • Their exports still face the bloc's tariffs, so they lose sales to member firms.
  • Members may switch from a cheaper outside supplier to a dearer member supplier: the world's resources are used less efficiently.
Quick check
?Countries J and K form a customs union. Before, both bought their sugar from Country L, the cheapest producer in the world. Now K's sugar is tariff-free in J, but L's sugar still pays the common external tariff. What is most likely?
Mission 4

Free-trade agreements: weigh it up

For:

  • Lower prices and more choice for consumers.
  • Bigger markets for exporters; economies of scale.
  • More competition and investment.

Against:

  • Firms that cannot compete with member rivals may close; some workers lose jobs.
  • Cheaper outside suppliers may lose out to dearer members.

It depends on:

  • how much members already trade with each other
  • how close the agreement is: an FTA leaves more barriers than a single market
  • whether workers who lose jobs can move to growing industries.
Mission 4

The World Trade Organization (WTO)

  • Set up on 1 January 1995. 166 members, covering about 98% of world trade.
  • What it does:
    • a place where members negotiate to cut trade barriers
    • sets rules for trade
    • settles disputes between members
  • Key rule: no discrimination. A tariff cut for one member must go to all members.
  • Exception: members may form free trade areas and customs unions, which give better terms to each other.
  • Aim: more trade, to raise living standards and create jobs.
Quick check
?Country M, a WTO member, cuts its tariff on imported shoes from Country N, also a WTO member. M and N are not in any trading bloc together. What do WTO rules expect M to do?
Mission check

?Countries R, S and T form a customs union. Country V is outside it. Before the union, V sold cars to all three. After, which is most likely?

A bloc gives its members an edge inside, while outsiders still face its tariffs.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Countries trade to get goods they cannot make, goods that are cheaper abroad, and more choice; exports pay for imports.
  • Specialising in what each country does best raises total output.
  • Free trade brings lower prices, bigger markets and competition, but also job losses in some industries and risks from over-specialisation.

Mission 2

  • Globalisation: economies becoming more connected through trade, investment, people, supply chains and MNCs.
  • Drivers: lower tariffs and quotas, cheaper transport and communication, technology, MNCs.
  • MNCs bring jobs, skills and tax to host countries, but profits may flow home and taxes may be avoided.
  • Gains are large but uneven: some groups and places lose, at least for a time.

Mission 3

  • Free trade: a world price below the home price means more buying, less home output and imports filling the gap.
  • A tariff raises the home price: home producers and the government gain; buyers pay more; imports fall.
  • A quota raises the price without tax revenue; a subsidy cuts imports without raising the price, but taxpayers pay.
  • Reasons for protection include infant industries, dumping, jobs, revenue and the current account; the costs include higher prices, inefficiency and retaliation.

Mission 4

  • A free-trade agreement removes tariffs and quotas between members only.
  • Free trade area, customs union (common external tariff) and single market (free movement of goods, services, money and people) join members ever more closely.
  • Members gain bigger markets and lower prices; outsiders face the bloc's tariffs and may lose sales.
  • The WTO sets trade rules, hosts talks and settles disputes; blocs are an allowed exception to non-discrimination.
