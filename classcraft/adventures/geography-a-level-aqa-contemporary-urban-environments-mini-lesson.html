Mission 1

Urbanisation and urban growth

  • Urbanisation: a rising share of people living in urban areas. Urban growth: a rising number of urban people.
  • Three engines: natural increase in cities, net migration into cities, and reclassification of rural places as urban.
  • Later stages of the urban cycle: suburbanisation, counter-urbanisation, then urban resurgence (re-urbanisation).
Mission 1

Global patterns since 1950

  • 1950: 20% of the world's 2.5 billion people lived in cities; 40% in towns.
  • 2025: 45% in cities, 36% in towns and 19% in rural areas, of 8.2 billion.
  • Cities overtook towns as the most common setting in 1996.
  • Megacities (10 million+): 8 in 1975, 33 in 2025 (19 in Asia), 37 projected by 2050.
  • Regions differ (2025): cities hold 59% in Northern Africa and Western Asia, 38% in sub-Saharan Africa.
Mission 1

Why urbanisation matters

  • Urban areas use 75% of global energy and produce 75% of greenhouse gas emissions.
  • Two-thirds of world population growth to 2050 is projected to be in cities.
  • India, Nigeria, Pakistan, DR Congo, Egypt, Bangladesh and Ethiopia are projected to add over 500 million city residents: over half of the 986 million increase.
  • So where cities grow, and how, shapes jobs, emissions and services worldwide.
Quick check
?In 2025, 45% of the world's 8.2 billion people lived in cities. About how many people is that?
Mission 1

Five kinds of process

  • Economic: factory and service jobs pull workers to cities.
  • Social: schools, hospitals and city lifestyles attract young people.
  • Technological: farm mechanisation frees rural labour; transport makes moving easier.
  • Political: decisions such as special economic zones, and reclassifying places as urban.
  • Demographic: natural increase, and the young age of migrants, who then start families in cities.
Mission 1

The urban cycle: New York

  • New York city's population fell from 7,891,957 (1950) to 7,071,639 (1980): suburbanisation and counter-urbanisation.
  • It then rose to 7,322,564 by 1990: the start of urban resurgence.
  • In 15 low-income neighbourhoods classed as gentrifying, average rents rose 34.3% between 1990 and 2010–14.
  • Resurgence drivers: service jobs in the centre, older housing to renovate, and policy-led regeneration.
Mission 1

China opens up

  • 1978: China began its "reform and opening up period". This is the Open Door Policy.
  • Special economic zone (SEZ): an area with lower taxes, cheaper land and fewer trade rules to attract foreign firms. These subsidies and incentives lower costs.
  • Shenzhen was established in March 1979 and became China's first SEZ in August 1980.
  • Foreign direct investment (FDI) into China, net inflows: $0.43 billion (1982); $243.7 billion (2010); $344.1 billion (2021); $42.6 billion (2024).
Mission 1

Shenzhen

  • 17.98 million permanent residents at the end of 2024.
  • Under the UN's density-based definition, the Shenzhen city area had about 13.9 million people in 2025: a different boundary, a different number.
  • GDP in 2024: 3.68 trillion yuan, up 5.8% on the year. Exports ranked first among Chinese mainland cities for 32 consecutive years.
  • 2015–2025: Beijing, Guangzhou, Shanghai and Shenzhen each grew by more than 0.8% a year, against 0.1% for China as a whole.
Mission 1

Where China's city growth came from

  • China's urban share (national definition): 20.6% in 1982, 63.8% in 2020.
  • Urban growth, 2010–2020 censuses: 56% rural-to-urban migration, 26% administrative changes, 18% natural increase.
  • Push: low farm incomes, few rural jobs. Pull: factory and service work, higher wages.
  • Migration is selective: mostly young adults. Rural areas face "population ageing and out-migration".
Quick check
?In the UN's breakdown, 26% of China's urban growth in 2010–2020 came from "administrative changes". What does that mean?
Mission check

Shenzhen is the same story at a larger scale: it calls itself "a young immigrant city", and across China 56% of urban growth in 2010–2020 came from rural-to-urban migration. The source gives no breakdown for Shenzhen itself.

Mission 2

Who counts as urban?

  • Countries define "urban" differently. Population thresholds range from 200 inhabitants in Denmark to 50,000 in Japan.
  • Some countries also use density, functions (e.g. share of non-farm jobs) or administrative status.
  • So a place of 5,000 people can be a town in one country and a village in another.
Mission 2

The rural–urban continuum

  • Settlements form a continuum, not two boxes: isolated dwelling → hamlet → village → town → city → conurbation.
  • Moving along it, population, density, range of functions, share of non-farm jobs and land use intensity all rise.
  • There is no sharp break: the rural–urban fringe and commuter villages share rural and urban traits.
  • Any line between "rural" and "urban" is drawn by people, for a purpose.
Quick check
?Under the UN's international rule, a town needs at least 5,000 people and 300 people per km². A settlement has 8,000 people living at 200 per km². What is it?
Mission 2

One world, two answers

  • The UN's Degree of Urbanisation applies one rule everywhere: city ≥50,000 people and ≥1,500 per km²; town ≥5,000 and ≥300 per km².
  • Adding countries' own definitions: 58% of the world was urban in 2025.
  • Using the single rule: 45% lived in cities and 36% in towns, 81% together.
  • The gap: many places that meet the town rule are classed as rural by their own countries, especially in Central and Southern Asia and sub-Saharan Africa.
Mission 2

The emergence of megacities

  • Megacity: a city of 10 million or more.
  • Megacities rose from 8 in 1975 to 33 in 2025; 19 are in Asia. 37 are projected by 2050.
  • Largest in 2025: Jakarta (nearly 42 million), Dhaka (almost 37 million), Tokyo (33 million).
  • New megacities expected by 2050 include Addis Ababa and Dar es Salaam: growth has shifted to Asia and Africa.
Mission 2

World cities

  • A world (global) city is a node of the global economy, measured by connections, not by size.
  • GaWC (Loughborough University) ranks cities by the offices of 175 leading advanced producer service firms across 785 cities.
  • 2024: Alpha++ London, New York; Alpha+ Hong Kong, Beijing, Singapore, Shanghai, Paris, Dubai, Tokyo, Sydney.
  • Mumbai, Jakarta and São Paulo are Alpha.
Quick check
?Dhaka is one of the world's largest cities, with almost 37 million people, but it is not in GaWC's top three levels (Alpha++, Alpha+, Alpha). What does this show?
Mission 2

Their role in global and regional economies

  • World cities host headquarters, finance, law, advertising and consulting: the "command and control" of the global economy.
  • Regional hubs link a region to the world: Dubai (Alpha+) for the Gulf; Singapore (Alpha+) for South-East Asia.
  • Megacities concentrate a country's people, markets and jobs; most growth is now in Asia and Africa.
  • Some cities are both: Tokyo is a megacity and Alpha+.
Mission 2

Contrasting characteristics

  • Density: Mumbai was the densest city of 1 million or more in 2025, at 27,000 people per km²; Perth was the least dense, under 2,000.
  • 21 of the 50 least dense cities of 1 million or more were in the USA.
  • Growth: Dhaka is expected to become the largest city by mid-century; Tokyo to shrink to around 31 million by 2050.
  • More than 3,000 cities lost population between 2015 and 2025.
Quick check
?Mumbai had 27,000 people per km² and Perth fewer than 2,000 per km² in 2025. Which statement is correct?
Mission check

?Country Z classes every settlement under 20,000 people as rural. Many of its settlements have 5,000–15,000 people living at more than 300 per km². If Z switched to the UN's single rule, what would happen to its reported urban share?

"Urban" is a definition, not a fact of nature. Adding national definitions gives 58% urban; the UN's single rule puts 81% in cities or towns (2025). Settlements sit on a continuum. Megacities (size) and world cities (connections) are different ideas.

Mission 3

Moving out from the centre

  • Suburbanisation: people, then shops and offices, move from the inner city to the suburbs. Drivers: cars and commuter rail, cheaper land, more space.
  • Counter-urbanisation: people move beyond the urban edge, to small towns and villages. Drivers: retirement, quality of life, remote working.
  • Decentralisation: shops, offices and industry move out of the centre to edge-of-town sites.
  • In parts of Europe, city populations have fallen with suburbanisation or counter-urbanisation, and with boundary reclassification.
Quick check
?A retired couple sell their flat in central Leeds and buy a cottage in a village 50 km away. Which movement is this?
Mission 3

London: in and out at once

  • In the year to mid-2025 London had positive natural change and net international inward migration.
  • But "this was more than offset by net outward migration to other areas of the UK".
  • London's population fell by 0.29%, "the first decrease since the year to mid-2021".
  • A city can gain from births and arrivals while losing more people outwards.
Mission 3

Deindustrialisation and the rise of services

  • Deindustrialisation: a long-term fall in manufacturing jobs and output in a place.
  • Causes: the global shift of factories to lower-cost countries, automation, firms relocating, and recessions.
  • Service economy: UK employment in services rose from 66.8% (1991) to 83.0% (2024); industry fell from 30.8% to 16.1% (ILO modelled).
Mission 3

Detroit's population

  • US census counts for Detroit city:
    • 465,766 (1910); 1,849,568 (1950, the 5th largest US city); 1,027,974 (1990); 713,777 (2010); 639,111 (2020).
  • Most of the loss came early: 821,594 of the 1,210,457 people lost by 2020 had gone by 1990 (our calculation).
Mission 3

Detroit: the jobs left

  • Historian Thomas Sugrue told the Associated Press that Detroit lost 140,000 jobs between 1947 and 1963; "The postwar manufacturing boom was largely over by the mid-1950s" (reported by HNN).
  • Manufacturing jobs in the Detroit metro area: 380,900 (2000), 174,000 (2009).
  • People who could move for work, often younger and better paid, left first: selective out-migration.
Quick check

Mission 3

A deindustrialised city

  • Depopulation: down about 65% from 1950 to 2020.
  • Unemployment: the metro area's unemployment rate averaged 14.6% in 2009 (calculated from monthly data).
  • Low incomes: median household income (2020–24, in 2024 dollars) $39,938, about half the US $80,734.
  • Housing: 50.3% of homes owner-occupied, against 65.2% in the USA.
Mission 3

The spiral of decline

  • Factory closes → jobs lost → less local spending → shops and services close → those who can, leave → smaller tax base → fewer services, more dereliction → investors stay away → more closures.
  • England's index finds deprivation concentrated in "areas that have historically had large heavy industry manufacturing and/or mining sectors".
Quick check
?In the spiral of decline, why does a smaller tax base make things worse?
Mission 3

Signs of change?

  • Detroit's estimated population for July 2025 is 649,095, above the 2020 census count.
  • Metro manufacturing jobs were 237,900 in 2025: above 2009, far below 2000.
  • Caution: 2025 is an estimate; the jobs series covers the much larger metro area and starts only in 1990.
  • Exam move: separate structural decline (decades) from cyclical swings (years).
Mission check

?A northern English town loses its last large factory. Over 20 years its population falls, while commuter villages 20 km away grow. Its boundary and birth rate do not change. What best explains the town's decline?

Detroit lost about 65% of its people from 1950 to 2020, two-thirds of that loss before 1990, as factory jobs left from the 1950s and people moved out. Deindustrialisation and centrifugal movement (moving outwards from the centre) worked together.

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

Mission 4

Why urban policy?

  • After the 1960s, UK manufacturing employment "declined drastically".
  • Inner cities lost jobs and people. Tower Hamlets (east London): 230,790 (1951) → 139,996 (1981).
  • Since 1979, governments have tried different answers to one question: who should lead regeneration, and for whom?
Mission 4

1980s: Urban Development Corporations (UDCs)

  • Local Government, Planning and Land Act 1980: the Secretary of State may designate an urban development area if "expedient in the national interest".
  • A UDC's object: "to secure the regeneration of its area".
  • Means: bring land into use, encourage industry and commerce, create "an attractive environment", ensure housing and social facilities.
  • Top-down and property-led: appointed boards, not elected councils, led.
  • Best known: the London Docklands Development Corporation (LDDC); its functions passed back to councils in 1998.
Mission 4

Docklands: what changed?

  • Tower Hamlets' population: 139,996 (1981) → 310,306 (2021): more than doubled.
  • Yet in 2025, deprivation was still concentrated in "parts of east London".
  • Tower Hamlets in 2021: 35.9% of households in social rented homes (England 17.1%).
  • Critique: new offices and homes arrived; many existing residents did not share the gains.
Quick check
?Which feature most clearly marks the UDC approach of the 1980s?
Mission 4

1990s–2000s: partnership and community

  • Area-based initiatives followed: City Challenge, the Single Regeneration Budget, then New Deal for Communities (NDC).
  • NDC (from 1998): 39 deprived neighbourhoods, about 9,900 people each, about £50m each, over 10 years.
  • Six outcomes: crime, community, housing and environment (place); education, health, worklessness (people).
  • Aim: "close the gaps" with the rest of the country, with the community "at the heart of" the initiative.
Mission 4

NDC: the results

  • Spent £1.71bn on about 6,900 projects (1999–2000 to 2007–08); a further £730m levered in.
  • 2002–2008: improvement in 32 of 36 core indicators.
  • Compared with national equivalents: more improvement on 18 of 24 indicators.
  • But "more net positive change" for place-related than people-related outcomes.
Quick check
?NDC areas improved most on how residents felt about their neighbourhoods, and least on worklessness and health. What does this suggest for regeneration policy?
Mission 4

2000s–2010s: flagship legacy

  • London 2012 Olympic and Paralympic Games, Stratford, east London.
  • Legacy: Queen Elizabeth Olympic Park, 560 acres, now an "innovation district".
  • Its development corporation reports £3.5bn GVA and 38,300 jobs in the legacy area.
  • Question to ask: are the jobs taken by local people, or by newcomers to new homes?
Mission 4

2020s: levelling up

  • Levelling Up White Paper (2 Feb 2022): "spread opportunity more equally across the UK".
  • Levelling-up and Regeneration Act 2023 (26 Oct 2023): duty to set "levelling-up missions" and report on progress; also planning and regeneration powers.
  • National, not just neighbourhood, scale: the target is regional inequality.
  • Persistent pattern: deprivation still concentrated in large conurbations, former industrial and mining areas, coastal towns and parts of east London.

?A northern city is offered two schemes of the same cost. Scheme X: an appointed corporation clears waterfront land for offices and flats. Scheme Y: a ten-year resident-led partnership funds jobs, health and housing in three estates. Using evidence from British policy since 1979, which judgement is best?

Mission 5

Land values and bid rent

  • Bid-rent theory (Alonso): land users bid for each site by how much they gain from its accessibility.
  • Accessibility peaks in the central business district (CBD), so land values peak there: the peak land value intersection.
  • Shops and offices gain most from being central, so they outbid industry and housing near the centre.
  • Values fall with distance, but rise again at secondary centres, stations and main roads.
Mission 5

Tokyo's land value gradient

  • Japan's highest commercial land value (1 January 2026): Ginza 4-chome, ¥67.1 million per m², up 10.9% in a year.
  • Average residential values per m², 2026: Chiyoda ward ¥3,631,400; Setagaya ward ¥779,900; Hachioji ¥131,300; Ome ¥98,600.
  • Even Japan's highest residential value (Akasaka, Minato ward: ¥7.11 million per m²) is about a tenth of Ginza's commercial value.
Mission 5

Physical and human factors in urban form

  • Physical: relief, rivers and coasts, flood risk and geology fix where building is easy and where it is costly or banned.
  • Human: transport routes, land values, planning and zoning, history (old cores, walls, ports) and who owns the land.
  • Land use pattern: offices and high-order shops in the CBD; industry beside rivers, ports, railways and ring roads; housing density falling outwards.
Mission 5

Models of urban form

  • Concentric zones (Burgess): rings from the CBD, housing status rising outwards.
  • Sectors (Hoyt): wedges along routes; high-status housing in one sector, industry along rail or river.
  • Multiple nuclei (Harris and Ullman): several centres.
  • Latin American city (Griffin and Ford): CBD with a commercial spine and elite sector; peripheral squatter settlements.
  • Limits: drawn from US and Latin American cities of the 20th century; static; underplay planning and physical geography.
Quick check
?Average residential land in Chiyoda ward (central Tokyo) is ¥3,631,400 per m²; in Ome (outer Tokyo) it is ¥98,600. About how many times higher is Chiyoda?
Mission 5

Economic inequality: a spatial pattern

  • Inequality shows as a pattern in space: rich and poor districts, often side by side.
  • Mumbai, 2011 census: 10.0% of D ward's residents lived in slums; in M/East ward, 84.9%.
  • Factors: land values, flood-prone or marginal land, distance from jobs, and the history of who could build where.
  • Gated communities and informal settlements are two ends of the same land market.
Quick check
?In a fast-growing city, informal settlements cluster on a floodplain and beside a railway line. Which explanation fits best?
Mission 5

Segregation and cultural diversity

  • Residential segregation: groups (by income, ethnicity or religion) living apart in different areas.
  • Voluntary factors: support networks, places of worship, shops, language, safety in numbers.
  • Involuntary factors: cost, discrimination by landlords or employers, allocation of social housing.
  • Cultural diversity: many groups, languages and religions in one city; multiculturalism: groups keep distinct identities while sharing the city, often backed by policy.
Mission 5

Planning against segregation: Singapore

  • 76.0% of Singapore's resident population lived in public housing (HDB) flats at end-March 2025.
  • In 1989, after "signs that segregation among the races had once again started to take root", the government introduced the Ethnic Integration Policy.
  • It caps each ethnic group's share in every HDB block and neighbourhood, because "it was not possible to uproot residents to break up the enclaves".
  • In 2010 the limits for Indians and other minority groups were raised to 12% (neighbourhood) and 15% (block).
Mission 5

Evaluating the policy

  • Aim: mixed neighbourhoods, not enclaves: a deliberate multicultural settlement pattern.
  • Trade-off: when a block reaches a group's limit, owners there have fewer possible buyers, a direct effect of a quota.
  • Transferability: it works where the state houses most people; it would be far harder where most housing is private.
Quick check
?Which is an involuntary cause of residential segregation?
Mission check

?In a European city, land beside the main railway station is worth far more per m² than land in an outer suburb, and offices fill the station area. Why?

Bid rent: Ginza's land is worth about 680 times Ome's average residential value (67,100,000 ÷ 98,600) because central accessibility lets commercial users outbid everyone. Physical factors, planning and, in Singapore, a deliberate integration policy then shape who lives where.

Mission 6

Modern to post-modern city

  • Modern city: one dominant centre (CBD), zones of housing and industry, large public housing estates.
  • Post-modern western city: many centres, fragmented and privatised spaces, mixed uses, heritage and culture used to sell places.
  • New urban landscapes are the pieces of this city:
    • town centre mixed developments
    • cultural and heritage quarters
    • fortress developments
    • gentrified areas
    • edge cities
Mission 6

Town centre mixed developments

  • Mixed development: homes, shops, offices and public space on one site, so people live, work and shop nearby.
  • Elephant Park, Elephant and Castle (south London), built on the Heygate estate site: approved in January 2013 with "3,000 new homes".
  • "Nearly half of the new development is public space, including one of central London's largest new parks".
  • Developed in partnership with Lend Lease (now Lendlease) from 2007: a public–private scheme.
Mission 6

Cultural and heritage quarters

  • Cultural/heritage quarter: an area that uses its history, arts or industrial past to attract visitors, firms and residents.
  • Titanic Quarter, Belfast: the Titanic Belfast attraction stands "beside the Titanic Slipways", "the very place where Titanic was designed, built and launched".
  • The quarter mixes heritage with new uses: "Also within Titanic Quarter is a Premier Inn".
  • Heritage becomes a brand for regeneration.
Quick check
?A city turns old shipyards into a museum, hotels and offices, marketed under the shipyard's famous name. Which new urban landscape is this?
Mission 6

Fortress developments

  • Fortress development: space designed for security and control: gates, walls, CCTV, private guards, limited access.
  • Roots in defensible space (Oscar Newman, 1972): "restructure the physical layout of communities to allow residents to control the areas around their homes".
  • Five Oaks, Dayton (Ohio): gates cost "about $10,000 for each gate serving 30 to 40 households"; "cut-through traffic was reduced by 67 percent" and "overall crime had been reduced by 26 percent".
  • UK: Secured by Design, "the official police security initiative", promotes "maximising natural surveillance and limiting excessive through movement".
  • Critics: such spaces can exclude outsiders and divide the city.
Quick check
?Why are fortress developments seen as part of the post-modern city?
Mission 6

Gentrified areas: Heygate to Elephant Park

  • Gentrification: wealthier people move into a lower-income area, renovating or rebuilding it; prices rise and the population changes.
  • Heygate: 1,212 homes, of which 1,020 were council-owned.
  • By 2005, 595 council homes were still occupied; all these tenants got top priority for rehousing, and 99% chose to stay in Southwark.
  • Affordable housing in the new scheme rose from 11% to 25% after renegotiation.
  • Debate: more homes and a park, but "affordable" is a wider category than council rent, and many tenants "chose to move to other areas within the borough".
Mission 6

Measuring gentrification

  • New York City: "of the 22 neighborhoods that were low-income in 1990, we classify 15 as 'gentrifying'".
  • Rents in gentrifying areas rose 34.3% (1990 to 2010–14) vs 22.1% citywide.
  • The share of college-educated residents rose by "nearly 15.6 percentage point[s]".
  • But: "We cannot directly observe if low-income residents are moving out". Displacement is hard to measure.
Quick check
?Which evidence most directly shows that an area is gentrifying?
Mission 6

Edge cities

  • Edge city (Joel Garreau, 1991): "five million square feet or more of office space"; "600,000 square feet or more of retail space"; "more jobs than bedrooms"; "perceived by the population as one place"; "nothing like a 'city' as recently as thirty years ago".
  • Example: Tysons Corner, outside Washington, DC.
  • Detroit: Southfield's office cluster is "now bigger than downtown Detroit".
  • Edge cities make the city polycentric: many centres instead of one CBD.
Mission 6

The post-modern western city

  • Fragmented: a patchwork of quarters, enclaves and gated spaces, not neat zones.
  • Polycentric: edge cities and suburban clusters rival the CBD.
  • Consumption and image: heritage, culture and design used to sell places.
  • Privatised: developers, partnerships and security shape public space.
  • Polarised: gentrified and wealthy areas sit beside deprived ones.
Quick check
?Which feature best separates the post-modern city from the modern city?
Mission check

?A city's old dock area now has luxury flats behind security gates, a restored warehouse museum, and offices beside a new park. Prices have risen and some former residents have moved away. What does this landscape best show?

New urban landscapes rarely appear alone. Mixed developments, heritage quarters, fortress spaces and gentrification often overlap in the same regenerated area, which is what makes the post-modern city fragmented and contested.

Mission 7

Mission 7: In 2011, 1 in 10 people in Mumbai's D ward lived in slums. In M/East ward, more than 3 in 4 did. Why such a gap inside one city?

Make a guess: In 2011, 1 in 10 people in Mumbai's D ward lived in slums. In M/East ward, more than 3 in 4 did. Why such a gap inside one city?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.2.3.3 economic inequality, social segregation and cultural diversity in contrasting urban areas
  • 3.2.3.3 strategies to manage these issues
Mission 7

Key terms

  • Social inequality: unequal access to income, housing, health, education, work and services.
  • Deprivation: lacking what most people in a society have. Multiple deprivation: several lacks at once.
  • Social segregation: groups living apart, by income or by background.
  • Informal settlement: homes without legal title or planning approval, often without services.
Mission 7

Measuring inequality

  • England's Index of Multiple Deprivation 2025 ranks 33,755 small areas, of about 1,500 people each.
  • Seven weighted domains: income 22.5%; employment 22.5%; education 13.5%; health 13.5%; crime 9.3%; barriers to housing and services 9.3%; living environment 9.3%.
  • It is relative: it ranks areas, so some area is always "most deprived".
Quick check
?Residents of an area live far from a GP surgery and cannot afford housing nearby. Which IMD domain records this?
Mission 7

Between places: the global pattern

  • In 2022, 1.12 billion people lived in slums or informal settlements: 24.8% of the urban population.
  • Over 85% were in three regions: Eastern and South-Eastern Asia (362 million), Central and Southern Asia (334 million) and sub-Saharan Africa (265 million).
  • Informal jobs as a share of all jobs, 2024 (ILO modelled): low-income countries 88.3%; lower-middle 81.5%; upper-middle 50.8%; high-income 12.9%.
  • Inequality varies between places with development, and within every city.
Mission 7

Within one city: Mumbai

  • Greater Mumbai, Census 2011, in the city's own ward tables:
    • D ward: 34,699 of 346,866 people in slums (10.0%, calculated).
    • M/East ward: 685,994 of 807,720 (84.9%, calculated).
  • Praja Foundation, citing Census 2011, gives 77.5% for M/East.
  • The share depends on who defines and counts slums. Both sources put M/East above 3 in 4.
Mission 7

Daily life in M/East

Figures from Apnalaya, an NGO working in the ward, reported by Praja Foundation:

  • Health: infant mortality 66 per 1,000 live births, against India's 41. Average age at death: 39.
  • Employment: "Every second male and six out of seven females are unemployed."
  • Environment: a landfill receiving about 4,500 tonnes of waste a day; contaminated air and groundwater.
  • Services: the ward ranked last of Mumbai's 24 wards on human development (2009), and had the city's most complaints about water supply.
Mission 7

Why the gap?

  • Land values: formal housing takes the most valuable land; low-income households are pushed to cheaper, less serviced land.
  • Migration: since the 1960s M/East "has given shelter to migrants from different parts of India".
  • Policy: it also took people "'resettled' from slums in the inner recesses of the city".
  • Services: weaker water, health and schooling make it hard to escape low pay.
  • Exam move: name the factor, then the evidence, then the effect on daily life.
Quick check
?In the city's Census 2011 tables, 6,534,460 of Greater Mumbai's 12,442,373 people lived in slums. What percentage is that?
Mission 7

England: deprivation persists

  • The most deprived area is east of Jaywick, Clacton-on-Sea; seven of the ten most deprived are in Blackpool.
  • Deprivation clusters in large conurbations, former heavy-industry and mining areas, coastal towns and parts of east London.
  • 82% of the most deprived tenth of areas in 2025 were also in the most deprived tenth in 2019.
Mission 7

The cycle of deprivation

  • Low income → poor housing and diet → poorer health and schooling → fewer qualifications → low-paid or no work → low income for the next generation.
  • Area effects reinforce it: weak services, crime, and a poor reputation that deters investment.
  • Breaking it needs action at several points at once.
Mission 7

Segregation and cultural diversity

  • Segregation by income: in Mumbai, resettlement moved slum dwellers from the inner city to M/East, concentrating the poorest in one ward.
  • Cultural diversity: M/East has drawn migrants "from different parts of India".
  • In London, 78.4% of residents aged 3+ had English as a main language in 2021, the lowest of any English region.
  • Diversity is not itself a problem: issues arise when it combines with poverty, poor services or language barriers to work and services.
Mission 7

Strategies to manage the issues

  • Upgrade in place: the UN calls for upgrading slums with basic services to reduce poverty and prevent displacement.
  • Community action: in M/East, Apnalaya reports families gaining legal water and electricity connections, cooking gas and ration cards.
  • Target areas: an index like England's IMD lets funding go to the most deprived places.
  • Public health approach: Glasgow's Violence Reduction Unit (2005) uses data to find causes of violence, test what works, then scale it up.
Mission 7

Evaluating strategies

  • Resettlement cleared central land, but concentrated the poorest in a poorly serviced ward.
  • Area targeting misses deprived people who live outside the target areas.
  • Persistence: 82% of England's most deprived tenth stayed there from 2019 to 2025: progress is slow.
  • Evidence quality: Apnalaya and the Glasgow unit report on their own work; check claims against official data.
Quick check
?Mumbai moved slum dwellers from the inner city to M/East ward. What does the evidence show this resettlement did?
Mission check

?In England, 82% of the most deprived tenth of neighbourhoods in 2025 were also in the most deprived tenth in 2019. Seven of the ten most deprived are in Blackpool. Which explanation fits best?

The Mumbai gap and England's pattern share causes: land and housing markets, history (migration, resettlement, lost industry) and the cycle of deprivation. MCGM's tables give 10.0% (D) against 84.9% (M/East).

Mission 8

Mission 8: Two neighbourhoods on two continents: both were demolished and redeveloped. Who decides what replaces a neighbourhood?

Make a guess: Two neighbourhoods on two continents: both were demolished and redeveloped. Who decides what replaces a neighbourhood?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.2.3.3 economic inequality, social segregation and cultural diversity in contrasting urban areas
  • 3.2.3.3 strategies to manage these issues
Mission 8

Contested change

  • Regeneration (placemaking): improving a run-down area through investment in buildings, jobs and services.
  • Rebranding (place marketing): changing a place's image to attract investors, residents or visitors.
  • Players or stakeholders: residents, landowners, developers, councils, national government and NGOs.
  • Change is contested when these groups want different outcomes; rising land value often drives it.
Mission 8

Lagos: Otodo-Gbame

  • Lagos State's planning ministry estimated in 2013 that over 70% of Lagos's population lived in informal settlements (reported by Amnesty International).
  • On 9 October 2016 the State Governor announced that informal settlements along the waterways would be demolished within seven days.
  • Amnesty estimates that over 30,000 residents of the waterfront community of Otodo-Gbame were forcibly evicted, in three operations from November 2016 to April 2017.
  • The estimate deducts the people who remained (about 4,700) from an earlier population estimate.
Mission 8

Lagos: process and outcome

  • All 75 Otodo-Gbame evictees Amnesty interviewed said they had no prior written notice.
  • Amnesty reports that the evictions went ahead in violation of court orders of 7 November 2016 and 26 January 2017.
  • The government gave varying reasons, Amnesty reports, from denying responsibility to "claiming to save the environment from degradation … to preventing crime".
  • A luxury housing estate was being built on part of the land and water area (Amnesty, 2017).
Mission 8

London: the Heygate estate

  • A council estate in Southwark, completed in 1974, originally housing about 3,000 people; 1,212 homes, 1,020 council-owned.
  • A late-1990s survey found the buildings "structurally sound, but in need of complete refurbishment".
  • In a 1998 consultation, 96% of 500 responses favoured major regeneration.
  • The redevelopment "has been the subject of a two-decade long controversy".
Mission 8

Heygate: decisions and players

  • 2005: the 595 council tenants were given top priority to move; 99% chose to stay in Southwark, and all were offered the right to return.
  • 2007: Southwark Council partnered with the developer Lend Lease (now Lendlease).
  • 2010: renegotiated terms raised affordable housing from 11% to 25%, plus a council share of any extra profits.
  • January 2013: plans for 3,000 new homes approved; July 2014: demolition complete. "Nearly half of the new development is public space".
Mission 8

Heygate: two views

  • The council: "the combination of housing management challenges and regeneration opportunities" justified demolition.
  • Some residents: "releasing the very significant value of the land was the key factor".
  • A local campaign called the years of uncertainty "a decade long act of attrition".
  • Across London, 50 regenerated estates almost doubled their homes, but lost some 8,000 social rented homes in all.
Quick check
?Which statement fits the London-wide evidence on 50 regenerated estates?
Mission 8

Strategies to manage inequality and segregation

  • Resident ballots: from 18 July 2018, London landlords seeking GLA funding for estate regeneration that demolishes any social homes and builds 150 or more homes must win a residents' ballot.
  • No net loss: the Mayor requires "no net loss of affordable homes" and "full rights to return or remain for social tenants".
  • Upgrade, don't clear: the UN calls for upgrading slums with basic services, to prevent displacement.
  • Secure tenure and courts: protections only work if they are respected; Amnesty reports Lagos court orders were not.
Mission 8

Comparing the two neighbourhoods

  • Similar: lower-income homes cleared on valuable land; luxury development on part of the Lagos site; some Heygate residents say land value decided it.
  • Process: London, a council decision with rehousing priority and a right to return; Lagos, operations Amnesty documents as without notice or compensation, despite court orders.
  • Scale: the Heygate originally housed about 3,000 people; Amnesty estimates over 30,000 were evicted from Otodo-Gbame.
Quick check
?What is the strongest contrast between the two cases?
Mission check

?A London housing association wants GLA funding to demolish an estate of 400 social-rent homes and build 1,000 new homes. Who now has a formal say over whether demolition goes ahead?

Power over land decides: councils, developers and governments, in Lagos and London. Residents gain a real say only through protections such as secure tenure, enforced court orders, rehousing rights and, in London since 2018, resident ballots.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Urbanisation (share) differs from urban growth (number); three engines: natural increase, migration, reclassification.
  • City living rose from 20% (1950) to 45% (2025); processes are economic, social, technological, political and demographic; New York shows urban resurgence.

Mission 2

  • Settlements form a rural–urban continuum; "urban" thresholds range from 200 (Denmark) to 50,000 (Japan).
  • Megacities (10 million+) rose from 8 to 33 (1975–2025); world cities are ranked by global business connections (GaWC).
  • Mega and world cities contrast in density, growth and role.

Mission 3

  • Centrifugal movements: suburbanisation, counter-urbanisation and decentralisation.
  • Deindustrialisation (global shift, automation) can trigger a spiral of decline: depopulation, unemployment, low incomes.

Mission 4

  • 1980 Act UDCs (appointed, property-led; e.g. LDDC to 1998) → NDC (39 areas, £1.71bn, 32 of 36 indicators improved; place outcomes > people outcomes) → London 2012 legacy → Levelling Up White Paper (2022) and Act (2023).
  • Evaluate by who led, who benefited and over what time: Tower Hamlets' population more than doubled after 1981, but deprivation in east London persists.

Mission 5

  • Bid rent: accessibility sets land values, which sort land uses (CBD offices and shops; housing further out).
  • Urban form reflects physical and human factors; models (Burgess, Hoyt, Harris and Ullman, Griffin and Ford) are yardsticks with limits.
  • Segregation has voluntary and involuntary causes; Singapore's quotas aim to keep neighbourhoods mixed.

Mission 6

  • Mixed developments (Elephant Park), cultural and heritage quarters (Titanic Quarter), fortress developments (defensible space, gating), gentrified areas (Heygate; NYC) and edge cities (Tysons Corner) are the building blocks of the post-modern western city.
  • The post-modern city is fragmented, polycentric, privatised, image-led and polarised.

Mission 7

  • Inequality is measured with several domains: income, employment, health, education, housing and access to services.
  • Land values, migration and resettlement concentrate deprivation in some wards; the cycle of deprivation keeps it there.
  • Strategies: upgrade in place, community action, area targeting and public-health approaches; judge them on evidence.

Mission 8

  • Regeneration and rebranding are contested because gains and losses fall on different groups.
  • Strategies: ballots, no net loss of affordable homes, rights to return, upgrading in place.
