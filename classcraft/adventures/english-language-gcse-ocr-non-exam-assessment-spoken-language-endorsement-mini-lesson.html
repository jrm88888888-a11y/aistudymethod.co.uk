Mission 1

A few minutes, a room of classmates. What makes them listen on?

Make a guess: A few minutes, a room of classmates. What makes them listen on?

Spoken language · planning a talkScreen 1

How your exam asks this

  • Component 03 or 04 (the number only shows how your school sends recordings): one prepared presentation on an agreed topic, then questions and feedback. Pass, Merit or Distinction; 0% of your GCSE grade.
Spoken language · planning a talkScreen 2

A topic that can carry a talk

  • Pick something you know well and care about: interest is easier to perform than to fake.
  • Narrow it to a question or claim: not 'sport' but 'Should our town keep its outdoor pool open all year?'
  • Test it: can you list five questions a listener might ask? If not, it's too thin.
  • Your spec says you must agree the subject with your teacher in advance.
Spoken language · planning a talkScreen 3

Purpose and audience first

  • Purpose: what you want listeners to think, feel or do by the end (inform, persuade, explain, entertain).
  • Audience: who they are and what they already know. Classmates need different examples from a panel of governors.
  • Write one sentence before anything else: 'By the end, my audience will…'
  • Every fact, story and slide must earn its place against that sentence.
Quick checkScreen 4
?Amira wants to talk to Year 10 about volunteering at an animal shelter. Which plan best fits her purpose and audience?
Spoken language · planning a talkScreen 5

Build the shape

  • Opening: a hook that gives a reason to care (a question, a surprising fact, a short story), then your claim.
  • Roadmap: tell them the plan ('three reasons…') so they can follow without slides.
  • Body: two to four sections, each one point plus one example. Use signposts ('My second reason…').
  • Ending: loop back to the opening and leave one clear thought or action.
  • Plan the timing: most of it goes on the body.

Notes, not a script

  • Write key words, not sentences: a script pulls your eyes down and flattens your voice.
  • Rehearse aloud, standing up, with a timer; cut what overruns.
  • Your spec sets no rule on notes: check with your teacher what you may use.

Mission 2

Same talk, Pass or Distinction. What changes besides the words?

Make a guess: Same talk, Pass or Distinction. What changes besides the words?

Spoken language · delivery and registerScreen 1

Spoken Standard English and register

  • Spoken Standard English: the grammar and vocabulary of standard English ('we were', 'I did'), not dialect forms ('we was', 'I done').
  • It is not an accent. You can use Standard English in any accent.
  • Register: how formal your language is. A formal presentation needs a formal register, adjusted to your audience.
  • At every grade you must be audible, intelligible and generally use language that suits a formal setting.

Spoken language · delivery and registerScreen 3

Your voice is a tool

  • Pace: slow down for key points; most nervous speakers rush.
  • Pause: a beat before and after an important line lets it land.
  • Emphasis: stress the word that carries the meaning ('three out of four students').
  • Volume and tone: loud enough for the back row; vary tone so you don't drone.
  • Mark pauses (/) and stressed words (underline) on your notes.
Spoken language · delivery and registerScreen 4

Your body speaks too

  • Eye contact: look round the whole room, not just at the teacher or your notes.
  • Stance: feet planted, hands free; avoid swaying or hiding behind a screen.
  • Gesture: use it to count points or show size, not to fidget.
  • Glance down at key-word notes, then look up before you speak.
Spoken language · delivery and registerScreen 5

Devices that engage listeners

  • Direct address and inclusive 'we': speak to them, not at them.
  • Rhetorical questions to make listeners think.
  • Patterns of three and repetition to make key ideas memorable.
  • A short anecdote or vivid example to make a point human.
  • Choose devices for this audience and purpose; a device used for its own sake is noise.

How your exam asks this

  • Grades are competency-based: you must meet every criterion for a grade. Moving up asks for more challenging ideas and wider vocabulary, structure that meets the audience's needs, achieving your purpose and, for Distinction, a well-chosen range of ways to engage the audience.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Same talk, Pass or Distinction. What changes besides the words?

Besides the words, what changes is how the talk works on its audience: Standard English in a fitting register, a voice that uses pace, pause and emphasis, eye contact, and devices chosen for these listeners. The words still matter too: higher grades also need more challenging ideas and vocabulary.

Mission 3

An unexpected question. What do strong speakers do next?

Make a guess: An unexpected question. What do strong speakers do next?

Spoken language · questions and discussionScreen 1

How your exam asks this

  • As part of, or after, your presentation you must listen and respond to questions and feedback. Responses move from straightforward (Pass), to formal and in some detail (Merit), to perceptive and, where it helps, elaborated with further ideas (Distinction).
Spoken language · questions and discussionScreen 2

Listen first, then decide

  • Hear the whole question. Don't start answering the first half.
  • Work out what kind it is: a fact, an opinion, a challenge to your view, or a request to explain more.
  • A short pause, or 'That's a good question', buys thinking time. Silence of two seconds feels long to you, not to them.
  • If it's unclear, ask: 'Do you mean…?' Checking shows you are listening.
Spoken language · questions and discussionScreen 3

Shape the answer

  • Answer the question directly in your first sentence.
  • Give a reason.
  • Add an example or detail, ideally something new that wasn't in the talk.
  • Link back to your main point.
  • This is a planning frame, not a formula to recite: in a real reply it should sound natural.

Spoken language · questions and discussionScreen 7

Feedback and challenge

  • Feedback isn't an attack. Thank the person and respond to the point: 'You're right that…; I'd add…'
  • When challenged, concede what's fair, then defend what still stands (as Leila does).
  • If you don't know, say so and offer what you do know.
  • Prepare: list five questions you might be asked and plan a reply to each.

Mission checkScreen 9

?An unexpected question. What do strong speakers do next?

In the next five seconds they listen to the whole question, work out what kind it is and take a breath (or ask 'Do you mean…?'). Then they answer it directly, give a reason and a new example, and link back to their main point, conceding what's fair and defending the rest.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Start from purpose and audience: one sentence saying what listeners should think or do by the end.
  • Shape it: hook, claim, roadmap, signposted points with examples, an ending that loops back. Speak from key-word notes and rehearse aloud.
  • One prepared formal presentation, then questions and feedback; graded Pass, Merit or Distinction, reported separately from your grade.
  • Spoken Standard English is grammar and vocabulary, not accent; register is how formal you are, adjusted to the audience.
  • Voice (pace, pause, emphasis, volume) and body (eye contact, stance, gesture) make key points land; devices engage only when chosen for the audience.
  • Grades are competency-based: meet all criteria at a level. Distinction needs demanding ideas, precise vocabulary and a well-chosen range of ways to engage the audience.
  • Listen to the whole question, identify its type, pause or clarify; then answer, give a reason and an example, and link back.
  • Treat feedback and challenge as a chance to show thinking: concede what's fair, defend what stands, never dismiss the questioner.
  • Questions and feedback are assessed: Distinction responses are perceptive and add further ideas.
