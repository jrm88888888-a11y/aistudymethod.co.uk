Mission 1

Mission 1: Christ isn't Jesus' surname. So what were his followers claiming when they called him Christ?

Make a guess: Christ isn't Jesus' surname. So what were his followers claiming when they called him Christ?

Mission 1

Why titles matter

  • A title says who someone is and what they are for: "Doctor", "Captain", "King".
  • Jesus' first followers were Jews. They used titles from the Jewish scriptures (the Christian Old Testament) to say who Jesus was.
  • Main titles: Messiah (Christ), Son of God, Son of Man.
  • Also Lord and Saviour.
  • Each title is a claim: Christians say Jesus fulfils it; others disagree.
Mission 1

Messiah, Christ, Son of David

  • Messiah (Hebrew mashiach) = "anointed one". Kings of Israel were anointed with oil.
  • Christ (Greek Christos) means the same. "Jesus Christ" = "Jesus the Messiah".
  • Many Jews hoped for a Messiah from King David's family who would free Israel and bring God's peace.
  • Cambridge's phrase: the "Chosen One". Christians believe Jesus as Messiah begins the New Covenant (Jeremiah 31:31; Luke 22:20).
  • Christians believe Jesus is the Messiah, but a servant who suffers, not a warrior king. Jews do not accept Jesus as the Messiah: many look for a Messiah, or a messianic age, still to come, bringing peace to the whole world.
Mission 1

Son of God, Son of Man

  • Son of God: the title says Jesus has a unique relationship with God the Father. Christians believe he is the eternal Son, the second Person of the Trinity.
  • Son of Man: Jesus' favourite way of speaking about himself in the Gospels.
  • It stresses his humanity, and that he will suffer (Mark 8:31).
  • It also recalls Daniel 7:13–14: "one like a son of man" who comes with the clouds and is given everlasting authority.
Mission 1

Lord and Saviour

  • Lord (Greek Kyrios): the word Greek-speaking Jews used for God's name in their scriptures.
  • "Jesus is Lord" is one of the earliest Christian creeds (Romans 10:9; Philippians 2:11).
  • Saviour: one who rescues. The name Jesus (Hebrew Yeshua) means "God saves" (Matthew 1:21).
  • At his birth the angels announce "a Saviour, who is Christ the Lord" (Luke 2:11).

Mission 1Diversity

What the titles mean today

  • Lord: Christians pray "Lord, have mercy" (Kyrie eleison) in the Mass and many services; "Jesus is Lord" is sung in Pentecostal worship.
  • Saviour: evangelical Christians often speak of accepting Jesus as personal Lord and Saviour.
  • Son of God: confessed in the creeds (Nicene Creed: "the only-begotten Son of God").
  • Messiah: Christians read Jewish prophecy (e.g. Isaiah 9:6–7) as pointing to Jesus.
  • Other faiths: Jews do not see Jesus as Messiah; Muslims call Jesus al-Masih (the Messiah) and a great prophet, but not the Son of God.
Mission 1Evaluate

"Son of God is the most important title for Jesus."

  • Reasons for: it expresses his divinity, central to the Trinity and the creeds.
  • It is the title that marks out Christian belief: Muslims call Jesus the Messiah, but not the Son of God.
  • Reasons against: "Son of Man" is the title Jesus himself uses most. "Christ" became his name for all Christians.
  • "Lord" was the earliest creed (Romans 10:9). For many, "Saviour" says most about what he does.
  • Another view: each title needs the others: one says who he is, another what he does.
  • Judge it by: Which title did Jesus use? Which do the creeds stress? Which matters most to Christians today?

Mission 2

Who Mary is

  • Mary of Nazareth: the mother of Jesus. Catholics and Orthodox honour her above all other saints.
  • Annunciation: the angel Gabriel tells her she will bear God's Son; she agrees (Luke 1:26–38).
  • Cana: she tells the servants, "Whatever he says to you, do it." (John 2:5)
  • The cross: she stands by Jesus as he dies (John 19:25); he entrusts her to the beloved disciple: "Behold, your mother!" (John 19:27)
  • Pentecost: she prays with the apostles, waiting for the Spirit (Acts 1:14).
  • She "kept all these sayings, pondering them in her heart" (Luke 2:19): a woman of faith and prayer.
Mission 2

Mother of God

  • Mother of God (Greek Theotokos, "God-bearer"): defined at the Council of Ephesus (431).
  • It is first a claim about Jesus: the child Mary bore is one person, truly God and truly human. So his mother is the Mother of God.
  • It does not mean Mary is divine, existed before God, or is mother of the Father or the Trinity. She is a human being, a creature.
  • Catholics and Orthodox use the title; many Anglicans and Lutherans accept it too.
  • Catholics also believe Mary was kept free from original sin from the start of her life (the Immaculate Conception) and taken body and soul into heaven (the Assumption); most Protestants do not.

The Annunciation (Luke 1:26–39)

  • Gabriel: "Rejoice, you highly favoured one! The Lord is with you." (Luke 1:28)
  • "You will conceive in your womb and give birth to a son, and shall name him 'Jesus.'" (Luke 1:31)
  • Mary asks how this can be. "The Holy Spirit will come on you, and the power of the Most High will overshadow you." (Luke 1:35)
  • "For nothing spoken by God is impossible." (Luke 1:37)
  • Mary: "Behold, the servant of the Lord; let it be done to me according to your word." (Luke 1:38)
  • Then she sets out "with haste" to help her relative Elizabeth (Luke 1:39).

Mission 2

Mary as guide and role model

  • Model of faith: she says "yes" to God without knowing what it will cost (Luke 1:38).
  • Model of discipleship: "blessed are those who hear the word of God, and keep it" (Luke 11:28).
  • Model of charity: she hurries to help Elizabeth; at Cana she notices others' needs.
  • Model of courage in suffering: she stays at the cross.
  • In prayer: the Hail Mary, the Rosary, the Angelus (prayed at 6 a.m., noon and 6 p.m.), May devotions, Marian feasts.
  • Pilgrimage: shrines such as Lourdes (France) and Knock (Ireland).
  • Catholics ask Mary to pray for them ("pray for us sinners"); they do not worship her.
  • Impact on daily life: prayer, trust in God's plan, care for others, respect for mothers and family.
Mission 2Diversity

Different views of Mary

  • Orthodox: deep love for the Theotokos; icons of her in every church; celebrate her Dormition ("falling asleep") and taking up into heaven, but do not accept the Immaculate Conception as defined by Rome.
  • Catholics: Mother of God, Mother of the Church, model and intercessor; Immaculate Conception and Assumption are dogmas.
  • Most Protestants: honour Mary as the faithful mother of Jesus and a model of faith; do not pray to her or to the saints: "there is one God and one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus" (1 Timothy 2:5).
  • They reject the Immaculate Conception and Assumption as not taught in the Bible.
  • Anglicans vary: some keep Marian feasts and shrines (e.g. Walsingham); others share the Protestant view.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Christians call the Bible "the Word of God", but people wrote it. How can it be both?

Make a guess: Christians call the Bible "the Word of God", but people wrote it. How can it be both?

Mission 3

Revelation: God makes himself known

  • Revelation: Christians believe God shows people what they could not find out alone: who God is and what God wants.
  • General revelation: through the world, nature and conscience: open to everyone.
  • Special revelation: through particular events and people: the prophets, the scriptures, and above all Jesus, the Word made flesh (John 1:14).
  • Christians believe the Bible records and passes on God's revelation: it is the Word of God.
  • So the Bible is a source of authority: it guides what Christians believe and how they live.
Mission 3

Inspiration

  • "Every Scripture is God-breathed and profitable for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for instruction in righteousness" (2 Timothy 3:16).
  • Inspired (Greek theopneustos, "God-breathed"): God guided the human writers.
  • Most Christians believe the writers used their own language, style and culture, while God worked through them.
  • So, for most Christians, the Bible is both fully human words and the Word of God.
  • Christians differ on what follows: is every detail without error, or the message God intended?
Quick check
?What does it mean to say the Bible is inspired?
Mission 3Diversity

Three views of the Bible's authority

  • The literal Word of God: every word is from God and true in every detail, including history and science (e.g. creation in six 24-hour days). Held by fundamentalist Christians and some evangelicals.
  • The inspired (revealed) Word of God: God's message comes through human writers; some parts are symbolic or reflect their time. Held by most Catholics, Orthodox and many Protestants.
  • A source of guidance and teaching: written by people inspired by their faith in God; a wise and treasured guide, open to reinterpretation. Held by many liberal Christians.
  • Implications: views on creation and science, on women's roles in church, on ethical questions.

Mission 3

The Bible among other authorities

  • Many Protestants: sola scriptura: scripture alone is the final authority; preaching explains it.
  • Catholics: Scripture and Tradition together form one sacred deposit of God's Word, interpreted by the Magisterium (Dei Verbum 10).
  • Orthodox: scripture lives within Holy Tradition: the Creeds, the councils, the liturgy.
  • Anglicans often speak of scripture, tradition and reason; Methodists add experience.
  • Quakers: the Inner Light (God's Spirit within) guides; the Bible is valued but tested by the Spirit.
Mission 3

The Bible as a moral guide: the Ten Commandments

  • Ten Commandments (Exodus 20:1–17): God's covenant laws given to Moses at Sinai.
  • Requirements (things to do): worship God alone; keep the Sabbath holy; honour father and mother.
  • Restrictions (things not to do): no idols; no misuse of God's name; do not murder, commit adultery, steal, give false witness, or covet.
  • Jesus sums them up as love of God and love of neighbour (see Mission 4).
  • Most Christians keep Sunday as their day of rest and worship, the day of the resurrection.
  • The Beatitudes as moral teaching.
Mission 3

The Bible in daily life

  • Formal worship: set readings (a lectionary) at Mass or Communion; psalms sung; a sermon explains the readings.
  • Informal worship: Bible study groups, house churches, preaching in free churches.
  • Home and private worship: daily reading and prayer. "Your word is a lamp to my feet, and a light for my path." (Psalm 119:105)
  • Bible reading aids: daily reading notes, reading plans and apps, commentaries.
  • Comfort and guidance: e.g. Psalm 23 at funerals; looking to the Bible when making decisions.

Mission 3Evaluate

"The Bible is the only authority Christians need."

  • Reasons for: the Bible is "God-breathed" (2 Timothy 3:16). Protestant sola scriptura: Church teaching and tradition can err, but God's Word does not. Every Christian can read it for themselves.
  • Reasons against: the Bible needs interpreting, and Christians disagree on what it means. The Church chose which books belong in it. Catholics: Scripture, Tradition and Magisterium belong together. Modern questions (e.g. IVF, AI) are not addressed directly; reason and conscience are needed.
  • Another view: many Protestants hold scripture as the highest authority, but still value creeds, reason and experience.
  • Judge it by: Can a text interpret itself? Who decides between interpretations? How do Christians handle questions the Bible does not mention?

Mission 4

Why Jesus has moral authority

  • Christians believe Jesus is the Son of God and the Word of God made flesh (John 1:14): his teaching is God's teaching.
  • The crowds noticed: "he taught them with authority, and not like the scribes" (Matthew 7:29).
  • He teaches by words (sermons, parables) and by example (how he lived and died).
Mission 4Read the source

Fulfilling the law (Matthew 5:17–24)

  • "Don't think that I came to destroy the law or the prophets. I didn't come to destroy, but to fulfil." (Matthew 5:17)
  • Fulfil: to bring the law to its full meaning, and to complete what it pointed towards.
  • Then Jesus goes deeper (5:21–22, paraphrase): the law says "Do not murder"; Jesus says even anger and insults put a person in danger of judgement.
  • Before offering a gift at the altar, first go and make peace with your brother (5:23–24, paraphrase).

The Beatitudes (Matthew 5:3–12)

  • Beatitude: from Latin beatus, "blessed" or "truly happy".
  • "Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the Kingdom of Heaven." (5:3)
  • Also blessed (5:4–10, paraphrase): those who mourn; the gentle; those who hunger and thirst for righteousness; the merciful; the pure in heart; the peacemakers; those persecuted for doing right.
  • "Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called children of God." (5:9)
  • Requirements they suggest: be merciful, make peace, seek justice. Restrictions: do not seek revenge or put yourself first.
  • Q: Which Beatitude would encourage a Christian to help settle a quarrel? (Blessed are the peacemakers.)

Mission 4

Agape and the Two Great Commandments

  • Asked which commandment is greatest, Jesus answers: "You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your mind." (Matthew 22:37)
  • "A second likewise is this, 'You shall love your neighbour as yourself.'" (Matthew 22:39)
  • Agape: selfless, unconditional love that seeks the good of the other, even an enemy.
  • Agape in practice: giving time (volunteering), work (skills, careers that serve) and money (charity, tithing: giving a tenth).
Mission 4Read the source

The sheep and the goats (Matthew 25:31–46)

  • At the final judgement, the King separates people "as a shepherd separates the sheep from the goats" (25:32).
  • The sheep fed the hungry, gave drink to the thirsty, welcomed the stranger, clothed the naked, cared for the sick, visited the prisoner.
  • "Most certainly I tell you, because you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me." (Matthew 25:40)
  • Q: According to the King, whom were the sheep really serving? (Christ himself, in the people they helped.)

Mission 4Evaluate

"Jesus' moral teaching is too demanding to follow today."

  • Reasons for: Jesus' commands in the Sermon on the Mount, "love your enemies" (Matthew 5:44) and "don't resist him who is evil" (5:39), seem impossible in a violent world. Even anger is condemned (5:22). Lutheran Christians say no one keeps it perfectly.
  • Reasons against: many Christians do live it: e.g. forgiving a family member's killer, or giving up wealth. Jesus promises help: the Spirit and grace (–). The Beatitudes describe God's Kingdom, which Christians grow towards. Ordinary acts of love, such as caring for a stranger in need, count too.
  • Another view: it is a demanding ideal to aim at, not a set of rules to tick off; failure is met with forgiveness.
  • Judge it by: Is it meant as rules or ideals? Does "too demanding" mean impossible or just hard? Do real examples show it can be lived?

Mission 5

What is a pilgrimage?

  • A pilgrimage is a journey to a holy place for a religious purpose. The traveller is a pilgrim.
  • It is both an outer journey (travel, walking, effort) and an inner journey (prayer, change of heart).
  • Christians often describe the whole of life as a pilgrimage towards God (Hebrews 11:13–16 calls believers strangers and pilgrims on earth).
  • No Christian is obliged to go on pilgrimage. Unlike Hajj in Islam, it is a free choice.
Mission 5

Goals, challenges, effects

  • Spiritual goals: to grow closer to God; to pray where Jesus lived, died and rose; to repent or give thanks; to ask for healing or guidance; to deepen faith.
  • Physical goals: to reach and see the holy place; to walk the route; sometimes to seek physical healing.
  • Challenges: cost, distance, heat, crowds, tiredness; in the Holy Land, travel can be affected by conflict and checkpoints. Spiritual challenges: doubt, disappointment, staying prayerful amid tourists.
  • Effects: a renewed faith, a sense of the Bible "coming alive", friendships with other pilgrims, a changed life back home.
Mission 5

A short history

  • Jewish roots: Jesus' family went up to Jerusalem for festivals such as Passover (Luke 2:41).
  • 4th century: Emperor Constantine's mother Helena visited the Holy Land (c. 326–328); churches were built at the sites of Jesus' birth and tomb.
  • c. 381–384: Egeria, a woman pilgrim, kept a diary of worship in Jerusalem.
  • Middle Ages: pilgrimages to Jerusalem, Rome and shrines of the saints were very popular.
  • 16th century: the Reformation criticised pilgrimage as trying to earn salvation and a cause of superstition about relics; many shrines in England were closed.
  • 19th–21st centuries: pilgrimage revived among Catholics and Orthodox, and in new forms among Anglicans and other Protestants.
Mission 5Read the source

Jesus at the Passover

> "His parents went every year to Jerusalem at the feast of the Passover." (Luke 2:41)

  • Luke 2:42–43 (paraphrase): when Jesus was twelve, they went up as usual for the feast. When it ended and they set off home, the boy Jesus stayed behind in Jerusalem without his parents knowing. (They find him in the Temple: "I must be in my Father's house", 2:49.)
  • Catholic reading: the Holy Family models pilgrimage; Christians follow their example.
  • Protestant reading: the family was obeying Jewish law; this is a story about who Jesus is, not a command for Christians to go on pilgrimage.

Mission 5

Bethlehem and Nazareth

  • Church of the Nativity, Bethlehem: one of the oldest churches in the world (built over the cave traditionally believed to be Jesus' birthplace; first church 4th century).
  • Pilgrims stoop through the low Door of Humility, then go down to the Grotto, where a silver star marks the traditional place of the birth.
  • Nazareth: the Basilica of the Annunciation (completed 1969), over the place where many believe the angel Gabriel appeared to Mary (Luke 1:26–38); the Church of St Joseph nearby, over the traditional site of Joseph's home and workshop, where Jesus grew up.
  • Meaning: for Christians these places speak of the Incarnation: the belief that God became human in a real place and a real family.
Mission 5

Jerusalem

  • Via Dolorosa ("Way of Sorrows"): the route Jesus is believed to have carried his cross; 14 Stations of the Cross; Franciscan friars lead a procession every Friday.
  • Garden of Gethsemane: ancient olive trees and the Church of All Nations, where Christians remember Jesus praying before his arrest.
  • Church of the Holy Sepulchre: built over the places long honoured as Golgotha (Calvary), where Jesus was crucified, and the tomb from which Christians believe he rose. Pilgrims climb to the chapel of Calvary, kneel at the Stone of Anointing and queue to enter the tomb.
  • The church is shared by several Christian communities: Greek Orthodox, Catholic (Franciscans) and Armenian, with Coptic, Ethiopian and Syriac Christians.
  • Some Protestants also pray at the Garden Tomb, a quieter alternative site outside the old city walls.

Mission 5

Catholic teaching on pilgrimage

  • Catholics value pilgrimage as a form of prayer, not a duty.
  • The Church calls itself a pilgrim people, journeying through history towards God's Kingdom (Second Vatican Council, Lumen Gentium, chapter 7).
  • Popes have declared Jubilee years in which Catholics are invited to make pilgrimage to Rome and to local churches.
  • Orthodox Christians share this love of holy places, relics and icons.
Mission 5Diversity

Is pilgrimage important today?

  • Catholic and Orthodox: valued, encouraged, never compulsory; holy places and relics help prayer.
  • Anglican: views vary; many Anglicans make pilgrimages (e.g. the Anglican shrine at Walsingham, the Holy Land) after the practice revived in the 19th and 20th centuries.
  • Many Protestants: not needed, because God is everywhere. Jesus told the Samaritan woman that true worship is "in spirit and truth", not tied to one mountain or city (John 4:21–24, paraphrase).
  • Reformation concerns: pilgrimage can become an attempt to earn salvation, or superstition about relics.
  • Still, many Protestants visit the Holy Land on study tours to see the places of the Bible, and find the experience deepens faith.
  • Quakers: the journey that matters is the inner one.
Quick check

Mission 5Evaluate

"Pilgrimage is still important for Christians today."

  • Reasons for: Jesus' own family went on pilgrimage (Luke 2:41). Walking where Jesus walked makes faith vivid. The journey brings time for prayer and change. Catholic teaching sees it as a sign of life's journey to heaven (CCC 2691).
  • Reasons against: God is everywhere (John 4:21–24). Money could be given to the poor instead. Pilgrimage can become tourism, or an attempt to earn favour with God.
  • Another view: an "inner pilgrimage" of prayer, or a local walk, can have the same purpose.
  • Judge it by: Is a holy place really closer to God? Does the journey change people? Is it the best use of time and money?
Mission 5Worked answer

Two reasons, with a source

Question: Explain two reasons why pilgrimage to Jerusalem is important for many Christians.

  • Reason 1: pilgrims can pray where Jesus died and, Christians believe, rose. Develop: walking the Via Dolorosa to the Holy Sepulchre helps them share in his suffering and resurrection, strengthening faith.
  • Reason 2: it reflects life's journey towards God. Source: CCC 2691 teaches that pilgrimages call to mind our earthly journey towards heaven (paraphrase). Develop: so pilgrims return renewed in prayer.

Mission 6

Shrines: holy places of prayer

  • A shrine is a holy place linked to a saint, a relic or a reported vision.
  • Many famous shrines are Marian: linked to Mary, the mother of Jesus.
  • Catholics honour (venerate) Mary and ask her to pray for them. They worship God alone.
  • Say: "Catholics honour Mary and ask her prayers" — not: "Catholics worship Mary".
  • Pilgrims go to pray, to ask for help, to give thanks, and to grow closer to God with others.
Mission 6

Lourdes: what happened in 1858

  • Lourdes: a small town in the Pyrenees, south-west France.
  • 11 February 1858: Bernadette Soubirous, a poor 14-year-old, says she sees a young lady in the Grotto (cave) of Massabielle by the river.
  • Over five months she reports 18 visions (apparitions).
  • She says the lady asks for prayer, penance, a chapel and processions.
  • Bernadette digs where she says she was told; a spring appears. It still flows.
  • 1862: the local bishop declares that the apparitions can be believed. Bernadette becomes a nun and is declared a saint (1933).
Mission 6

Lourdes: what pilgrims do

  • At the Grotto: pray before the statue of Mary; touch the rock; light a candle as a sign of continuing prayer.
  • The water: drink it, wash in it, or bathe in the baths. A sign of cleansing and new life.
  • Torchlight procession each evening: thousands sing the Ave Maria, candles in hand.
  • Eucharistic procession: the Blessed Sacrament is carried; the sick sit at the front and are blessed.
  • Mass, confession, the Stations of the Cross on the hillside.
  • The sick come first: volunteers push wheelchairs, carry stretchers and serve meals.

Mission 6Read the source

Bernadette's account

Words Bernadette reported the lady saying (1858, translated from the local dialect):

> "I do not promise to make you happy in this world, but in the other." (18 February)

> "I am the Immaculate Conception." (25 March)

  • Immaculate Conception: the Catholic belief that Mary was kept free from original sin from the moment she was conceived (defined in 1854).
  • Say: "the Immaculate Conception is about Mary's own conception" — not: "it is the virgin birth of Jesus".

Q1 What does the first saying suggest pilgrims should expect from Lourdes?

Show answer

Not a promise of earthly happiness or cure; hope of joy with God after this life.

Q2 In the second saying, who does the lady say she is?

Show answer

Mission 6

Healing and suffering

  • Some pilgrims report cures. Doctors at the Lourdes Medical Bureau examine claims.
  • Very few are declared miraculous: 72 by 2025. The Church is cautious.
  • Most pilgrims seek spiritual healing: peace, strength to cope, forgiveness, hope.
  • The importance of Lourdes: a place to seek healing and spiritual comfort.
Mission 6

Rome: the city of Peter

  • Jesus calls Peter the rock on which he will build his Church (Matthew 16:18, paraphrase).
  • Catholics believe Peter became the first Bishop of Rome; each pope is his successor.
  • Tradition: Peter was martyred in Rome (c. AD 64) and buried on the Vatican Hill.
  • St Peter's Basilica (completed 1626) stands over the site; Catholics believe his tomb lies beneath the high altar.
  • The Pope lives and works in Vatican City, beside the basilica.
  • St Paul was also martyred in Rome. Pilgrims visit his basilica and the catacombs, early Christian burial places.
Mission 6

Rome: what pilgrims do

  • Pray at the tomb of Peter; many say the Creed there, professing the faith of the apostles.
  • Papal audience (usually Wednesdays): the Pope teaches, prays and greets pilgrims.
  • Papal appearances: on Sundays at noon he prays the Angelus from a window above St Peter's Square.
  • At Christmas and Easter he gives the blessing Urbi et Orbi ("to the city and the world").
  • In Jubilee years (every 25 years; the latest in 2025) pilgrims pass through the Holy Door, a sign of turning to Christ.
  • Significance: unity with the worldwide Catholic Church and the Pope.

Mission 6Diversity

Catholic and Protestant views

  • Catholic: pilgrimage is valuable but not compulsory; it renews prayer and pictures life as a journey to God.
  • Orthodox: shrines, relics and icons are important; pilgrimage is common.
  • Anglican: varied. Anglo-Catholics go to Walsingham; many others do not.
  • Many Protestants (e.g. evangelical, Baptist, Reformed):
    • God can be worshipped anywhere, "in spirit and truth" (John 4:21–24, paraphrase).
    • Christ is the one mediator (1 Timothy 2:5): devotion to Mary or relics may distract from him.
    • Salvation is by faith, not by journeys.
  • Some Protestants still make pilgrimages to the Holy Land, or to communities like Iona and Taizé.

Mission 6Evaluate

"Visiting a shrine brings a Christian closer to God than praying at home."

  • For: time set apart from daily life; a place soaked in centuries of prayer; praying with thousands of others; the sacraments; many pilgrims report lasting change.
  • Against: God is present everywhere; Jesus told his followers to pray in a private room (Matthew 6:6, paraphrase).
  • Against: cost and travel: the money could help the poor (Matthew 25:31–46); a trip can become tourism.
  • Against: many Protestants: focus on a place, statue or relic may distract from Christ.
  • Judge it by: Is God's presence tied to places? What the pilgrim does there, and how they live afterwards? Is "closer to God" a feeling or a change of life?
Mission check

?Knock, in Ireland, is a Marian shrine where villagers reported a vision in 1879. Siobhan, a Catholic with a long-term illness, prays there and receives the Anointing of the Sick. She comes home not cured, but says she has peace and feels less alone. Which explanation best fits Catholic teaching?

Lourdes, Rome and Walsingham draw pilgrims for prayer, unity and spiritual healing; a cure is hoped for, but it is not the measure of the journey.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Christ = Messiah = "anointed one": the king Jews hoped for from David's family.
  • Son of God expresses Jesus' unique relationship with the Father; Son of Man his humanity, suffering and future glory.

Mission 2

  • "Mother of God" (Theotokos, Ephesus 431) means the child Mary bore is truly God; Mary herself is human.
  • Catholics see Mary as model of the Church: of faith ("let it be done to me", Luke 1:38), discipleship and charity, and pray with her and to the saints for their prayers.

Mission 3

  • Christians believe God reveals himself, and the Bible is the inspired ("God-breathed") record of that revelation (2 Timothy 3:16).
  • Views of authority: literal Word, inspired Word (most Catholics, Orthodox and many Protestants), or a human guide inspired by faith.
  • The Bible is used in worship, at home, through reading aids and groups, for comfort and moral guidance.

Mission 4

  • Christians see Jesus as the Word of God: his teaching and example carry God's authority.
  • In Matthew 5:17–24 Jesus says he came to fulfil the law, and he goes deeper, to attitudes such as anger.
  • The Beatitudes describe who is truly blessed.
  • Agape and the Two Great Commandments: love God, and love your neighbour as yourself.
  • The sheep and the goats (Matthew 25): serving people in need is serving Christ.

Mission 5

  • A pilgrimage is a journey to a holy place for a religious purpose: an outer and an inner journey, never compulsory.
  • Holy Land sites: Bethlehem (Church of the Nativity), Nazareth (Annunciation, St Joseph), Jerusalem (Via Dolorosa, Gethsemane, Holy Sepulchre at Golgotha).
  • Catholics and Orthodox value pilgrimage (CCC 2691: a sign of the journey to heaven); many Protestants say God can be worshipped anywhere (John 4:21–24), though many still go.

Mission 6

  • Lourdes (1858, Bernadette, the Grotto and spring) is a place of prayer and healing where the sick come first; most healing sought is spiritual.
  • Rome: tomb of Peter, St Peter's Basilica, the Pope, audiences and blessings; unity with the worldwide Church.
