Mission 1

Mission 1: Molecules hit the walls at random. How can that add up to a pressure you can calculate exactly?

Make a guess: Molecules hit the walls at random. How can that add up to a pressure you can calculate exactly?

Mission 1

Using the kinetic theory equation

  • pV = ⅓Nm⟨c²⟩
  • Nm is the total mass of gas, so Nm/V = ρ and p = ⅓ρ × (mean square speed).
  • Air: p = 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa, ρ = 1.2 kg m⁻³.
  • Mean square speed = 3p/ρ = 2.5 × 10⁵ m² s⁻². rms speed = 500 m s⁻¹.
Quick check
?Nitrogen in a cylinder has a density of 3.40 kg m⁻³. The root-mean-square speed of its molecules is 517 m s⁻¹. Treat it as an ideal gas. What is its pressure?
Quick check
?An ideal gas is heated from 27 °C to 927 °C. By what factor does the rms speed of its molecules increase?
Mission check

?Two sealed containers of equal volume are at the same temperature. Each holds the same number N of molecules of an ideal gas. Each molecule of gas Y has 4 times the mass of a molecule of gas X. What is (pressure of Y)/(pressure of X)?

About 10²⁷ collisions on each square metre every second average out. Momentum change per hit × hits per second gives a steady force: pV = ⅓Nm × (mean square speed), which agrees with pV = NkT.

Mission 2: Under the same Sun, beach sand gets scorching while the sea stays cool. Why?

Make a guess: Under the same Sun, beach sand gets scorching while the sea stays cool. Why?

Mission 2

Specific heat capacity

  • Specific heat capacity, c: energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg by 1 K. Unit: J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.
  • Q = mcΔθ
  • Water: c ≈ 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. Dry sand: about 800. Same energy per kg → water warms about one fifth as much.
  • A change of 1 °C is a change of 1 K: Δθ needs no conversion.
Mission 2

Worked example

  • 0.500 kg aluminium block, 20 °C → 45 °C. c = 900 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.
  • Temperature rise: 45 − 20 = 25 K.
  • Energy: 0.500 × 900 × 25 = 1.1 × 10⁴ J (11 250 J).
  • With a 50 W heater and no losses: t = 11 250 ÷ 50 = 225 s.
Quick check
?An electric kettle heats 250 g of water from 18 °C to 90 °C. The specific heat capacity of water is 4.18 × 10³ J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. How much energy is transferred to the water? Ignore energy transferred to the kettle and surroundings.
Mission check

?0.20 kg of water and 0.20 kg of cooking oil, both at 20 °C, are each supplied with 12 kJ of energy. Specific heat capacities: water 4180 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, oil 2000 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. Assume no energy is lost. Which statement about their final temperatures is correct?

Same energy, same mass: the lower c, the bigger temperature rise. Sand's c is about a fifth of water's, so in the same sunshine each kilogram of sand warms about five times as much. The sea also spreads its energy through metres of mixing water and loses some by evaporation; dry sand keeps it in a thin top layer.

Mission 3

Mission 3: How do you measure the energy a material needs, when some always leaks away?

Make a guess: How do you measure the energy a material needs, when some always leaks away?

Mission 3

Electrical method for c

  • Metal block (known mass), wrapped in insulation. Holes for an immersion heater and a thermometer (a drop of oil helps contact).
  • Heater circuit: ammeter in series, voltmeter across the heater. Or a joulemeter.
  • Energy supplied: E = VIt.
  • Record temperature every 30 s. Plot θ against t.
  • Use the gradient of the straight part: c = VI ÷ (m × gradient). This avoids the early lag while the heater warms up.
Mission 3

Worked example

  • 1.00 kg aluminium block. Heater: 12.0 V, 4.00 A → P = 48.0 W.
  • Gradient of θ–t graph: 0.0500 K s⁻¹.
  • c = 48.0 ÷ (1.00 × 0.0500) = 960 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.
  • Data-book value ≈ 900. Measured value is too high: some energy went to the surroundings, so less raised the block's temperature.
Quick check
?A 0.80 kg copper block is heated by a 24 W electrical heater. On the straight part of its temperature–time graph, the temperature rises at 0.075 K s⁻¹. What is the specific heat capacity of copper from these data?
Mission check

?A student uses the electrical method to find the specific heat capacity of a brass block. Her value is 10% higher than the data-book value. Which change to her method would most reduce this difference?

Two ways to beat the leak: insulate to make it small, and design it out (use the gradient of the straight part, not a single reading).

Make a guess: A car rounds a bend at a steady speed. Is it accelerating, and if so, how much?

Mission 4

The radian

  • Angle in radians: θ = arc length ÷ radius = s/r.
  • 1 radian: the angle at the centre when the arc length equals the radius.
  • Full circle: s = 2πr, so 2π rad = 360°. 1 rad ≈ 57.3°.
  • Convert: degrees × π/180 → radians.
Mission 4

Period, frequency, speed

  • Period T: time for one full revolution (s).
  • Frequency f: revolutions per second (Hz). f = 1/T.
  • One revolution = one circumference, 2πr. Constant speed: v = 2πr/T.
Mission 4

Angular velocity

  • Angular velocity ω: angle swept per second, in rad s⁻¹.
  • One revolution sweeps 2π rad in time T: ω = 2π/T = 2πf.
  • v = ωr.
  • Every point on a rigid turning object has the same ω. Points further out have larger v.
Quick check
?A bicycle wheel of diameter 0.700 m rotates at a steady rate about a fixed axle, making one complete revolution every 0.250 s. What is the linear speed of a point on the rim of the wheel?
Mission 4

Steady speed, changing velocity

  • Velocity is a vector. Going around a circle, its direction keeps changing.
  • Changing velocity = acceleration, even at constant speed.
  • Draw v1 and v2 at two nearby points. The change, Δv = v2 − v1, points towards the centre.
  • So the acceleration points to the centre: centripetal acceleration.
  • It needs a resultant force towards the centre.
Mission 4

Centripetal acceleration

  • a = v²/r = ω²r, towards the centre.
  • Example: a child on a spinning playground ride, 1.5 m from the centre, one revolution every 4.0 s.
  • v = 2πr/T = 2π × 1.5 ÷ 4.0 = 2.4 m s⁻¹.
  • ω = 2π/T = 2π ÷ 4.0 = 1.6 rad s⁻¹.
  • a = v²/r = 2.36² ÷ 1.5 = 3.7 m s⁻², towards the centre.
  • Check: ω²r = 1.57² × 1.5 = 3.7 m s⁻².
Quick check
?Car X rounds a curve of radius 50 m and car Y rounds a curve of radius 100 m. Both cars move at the same constant speed. How does the magnitude of car X's centripetal acceleration compare with that of car Y?
Mission 4

Recap: three ways to write it

  • Given v and r: a = v²/r.
  • Given ω (or f) and r: a = ω²r, with ω = 2πf.
  • Given T and r: ω = 2π/T, so a = 4π²r/T².
  • Always use the radius, not the diameter. ω in rad s⁻¹, not degrees per second.
Mission check

?The drum of a washing machine has a diameter of 0.50 m. It rotates about a horizontal axis at constant speed, with a time period of 0.050 s. What is the centripetal acceleration of a sock pressed against the inside of the drum?

Constant speed is not constant velocity. The sock's direction keeps changing, so it accelerates towards the centre of the drum, with a = v²/r.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Whirl a ball on a string in a circle. Is anything pulling it outwards?

Make a guess: Whirl a ball on a string in a circle. Is anything pulling it outwards?

Mission 5

Centripetal force is the resultant

  • Circular motion at constant speed: acceleration v²/r towards the centre.
  • Newton's second law: it needs a resultant force towards the centre: the centripetal force.
  • F = mv²/r = mω²r, towards the centre.
  • It is perpendicular to the velocity: it changes the direction, not the speed.
  • It is not an extra force. It is the resultant of real forces: tension, friction, gravity, normal force.
Mission 5

No outward force

  • Nothing pulls the ball outwards. The string pulls it inwards.
  • Cut the string: the ball flies off along the tangent, in a straight line (first law). Not outwards along the radius.
  • The "flung outwards" feeling in a turning car: your body keeps going straight while the car turns under you.
  • On a free-body diagram, never draw a "centripetal force" arrow or a "centrifugal force" arrow. Draw only real forces.
Quick check
?A cyclist rides around a flat circular track at a constant speed of 8.0 m s⁻¹. Which statement about her motion is correct?
Quick check
?A car travels at constant speed around a bend on a flat horizontal road; its path is part of a horizontal circle. Air resistance is negligible. Which statement about the horizontal forces on the car is correct?
Mission 5

Worked example: a car on a flat bend

  • A 1200 kg car rounds a flat bend of radius 50 m at 15 m s⁻¹.
  • Only friction from the road acts sideways, so friction is the centripetal force.
  • F = mv²/r = 1200 × 15² ÷ 50 = 5400 N, towards the centre.
  • If the greatest friction the tyres can give is 7000 N: vmax = √(Fr/m) = √(7000 × 50 ÷ 1200) = 17 m s⁻¹.
  • Faster than that: friction cannot supply mv²/r, and the car skids towards the outside of the bend.
Mission check

?A car drives at constant speed over the top of a rounded hill in the road. Near the top, the road's vertical cross-section is a circular arc. The car stays in contact with the road. At the top, how does the normal force from the road compare with the car's weight?

Nothing pulls the ball outwards. The string's inward pull is the centripetal force; cut it and the ball flies off along the tangent.

Mission 6: Why does an oscillating mass on a spring move fastest at the one point where no overall force acts on it?

Make a guess: Why does an oscillating mass on a spring move fastest at the one point where no overall force acts on it?

Mission 6

What makes motion simple harmonic

  • Periodic motion: repeats in equal time intervals. SHM is a special case. Every SHM is periodic; not every periodic motion is SHM.
  • Equilibrium position: where the object would rest; the restoring force is zero there.
  • Restoring force: always points back towards equilibrium, opposite to the displacement.
  • SHM: the restoring force is proportional to the displacement from equilibrium.
  • Condition: a ∝ −x
Mission 6

Describing an oscillation

  • Displacement x: distance from equilibrium, with a sign (+ one side, − the other).
  • Amplitude A: maximum displacement.
  • Period T: time for one complete cycle. Frequency f: cycles per second (Hz). T = 1/f.
  • Angular frequency ω = 2π/T = 2πf, in rad s⁻¹. One cycle = 2π rad.
Quick check
?Each object below is displaced and released, and then moves back and forth. Which one moves with simple harmonic motion?
Mission 6

The defining equation

  • a = −ω²x
  • The minus sign: acceleration always points opposite to the displacement, towards equilibrium.
  • It does not mean "slowing down": moving towards equilibrium, the object speeds up.
  • a against x: a straight line through the origin with a negative gradient.
  • Gradient of the a–x graph = −ω².
Mission 6

Worked example

  • f = 2.5 Hz → ω = 2π × 2.5 = 15.7 rad s⁻¹.
  • At x = +0.030 m: a = −ω²x = −(15.7)² × 0.030 = −7.4 m s⁻² (towards equilibrium).
  • At x = 0: a = 0.
Mission 6

x, v and a against time

  • Released from rest at x = +A at t = 0: x–t is a cosine curve.
  • v = gradient of x–t. v–t is a negative sine curve, a quarter cycle ahead of x.
  • a = gradient of v–t. a–t is x–t upside down, because a = −(constant)x.
  • At x = ±A: v = 0, |a| maximum (pointing inwards).
  • At x = 0: |v| maximum, a = 0.
  • Speeding up when moving towards equilibrium; slowing down when moving away.
Quick check
?The graph shows the position x of an object in simple harmonic motion as a function of time t. Points P, Q and R are marked on the graph. Let aP, aQ and aR be the magnitudes of the object's acceleration at P, Q and R. Which of the following ranks them?
Mission 6

Displacement as a function of time

  • x = A cos ωt: at t = 0, x = +A.
  • Calculator in radians.
  • A = 0.050 m, f = 2.0 Hz, t = 0.10 s: x = 0.050 cos(2π × 2.0 × 0.10) = 0.050 cos(1.26 rad) = 0.015 m.
Mission check

?A pendulum bob swings to and fro in a small arc. At the lowest point of its swing, what are its acceleration along its path and its speed?

All the way in from a turning point, the acceleration points along the motion, so the object keeps speeding up. It stops speeding up only where the acceleration (and the restoring force) is zero: at equilibrium. That is where it is fastest.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Two identical oscillators start a moment apart. What single number says how far out of step they are?

Make a guess: Two identical oscillators start a moment apart. What single number says how far out of step they are?

Mission 7

Phase and phase difference

  • Phase: how far through a cycle an oscillator is, as an angle. One cycle = 2π rad.
  • Phase difference between two oscillators with the same T: Δφ = 2π × Δt/T.
  • Δt = time between matching points (e.g. each one's maximum displacement).
  • In phase: Δφ = 0 (or 2π, 4π…). Antiphase: Δφ = π.
Quick check
?Two oscillators, P and Q, each have a time period of 0.80 s. Q reaches its maximum positive displacement 0.20 s after P does. What is the phase difference between P and Q, in radians?
Mission 7

Worked example

  • A = 0.040 m, T = 0.80 s, released from rest at x = +A at t = 0. ω = 2π/T = 7.85 rad s⁻¹.
  • x at t = 0.10 s: x = 0.040 cos(7.85 × 0.10) = 0.040 cos(0.785 rad) = 0.028 m.
  • First time at x = +0.020 m: cos ωt = 0.020/0.040 = 0.50 → ωt = 1.05 rad → t = 1.05 ÷ 7.85 = 0.13 s.
  • Calculator in radians for every step.
Quick check
?An object in SHM is released from rest at x = +0.060 m at time t = 0 and oscillates about x = 0 with a period of 1.2 s. What is the object's position at t = 0.20 s?
Mission 7

Isochronous oscillators

  • In SHM the period does not depend on the amplitude: an isochronous oscillator.
  • Larger amplitude → larger restoring force → larger speeds. More distance, covered faster: same T.
  • Test: time 20 oscillations at two different amplitudes. Same T.
  • A real pendulum is isochronous only for small amplitudes.
Quick check
?A student sets the same block–spring system oscillating twice, with different amplitudes. The graph shows the block's position x as a function of time t for trial 1 (solid line) and trial 2 (dashed line). Which claim is supported by the graph?
Mission check

?The graph shows displacement against time for two identical floats, P and Q, bobbing on water. Read the graph. What is the phase difference between the two floats?

Out of step is measured as a phase difference: the fraction of a cycle between matching points, times 2π rad. A third of a cycle apart is 2.09 rad whatever the period.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • pV = ⅓Nm⟨c²⟩.

Mission 2

  • Q = mcΔθ. Same energy and mass: lower c, bigger temperature rise.

Mission 3

  • Electrical method: E = VIt; c = P ÷ (m × gradient of θ–t). Energy lost to the surroundings makes c come out too high: insulate.

Mission 4

  • ω = 2π/T = 2πf (rad s⁻¹); v = ωr.
  • Constant speed in a circle means acceleration towards the centre: a = v²/r.
  • Also a = ω²r.

Mission 5

  • Centripetal force is the resultant of the real forces, towards the centre: F = mv²/r. There is no outward force.

Mission 6

  • SHM: restoring force ∝ displacement, always towards equilibrium.
  • a = −ω²x, with ω = 2π/T = 2πf.
  • Fastest at equilibrium (a = 0); at rest at ±A (|a| largest). v = gradient of x–t; a = gradient of v–t.

Mission 7

  • Phase difference Δφ = 2π Δt/T (radians); in phase 0, antiphase π.
  • SHM is isochronous: T does not depend on amplitude.
