Mission 1

What is an organelle?

  • An organelle is a distinct part of a cell with a specific function.
  • Eukaryotic cells have a nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles.
  • The cell surface (plasma) membrane controls what enters and leaves the cell.
  • Internal membranes divide the cell into compartments, each with its own enzymes and reactions.
Mission 1

The nucleus

  • Nuclear envelope: a double membrane with nuclear pores. mRNA leaves through the pores; proteins enter.
  • Chromatin: DNA wound around histone proteins. It condenses into visible chromosomes when the cell divides.
  • Nucleolus: a dense region that makes ribosomal RNA and assembles ribosomes.
  • The nucleus holds the cell's genes and controls which proteins are made.
Mission 1

Ribosomes

  • Made of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and protein; no membrane.
  • Two subunits. They read mRNA and join amino acids: protein synthesis.
  • Free in the cytoplasm, or bound to the RER.
  • Eukaryotic cytoplasm: 80S ribosomes. Prokaryotes, mitochondria and chloroplasts: smaller 70S.
  • Say: "ribosomes translate mRNA into protein" — not: "ribosomes copy DNA".
Mission 1

The endoplasmic reticulum

  • Rough ER (RER): flattened membrane sacs studded with ribosomes. Makes and transports proteins, e.g. proteins for secretion.
  • Smooth ER (SER): a network of tubes with no ribosomes. Makes lipids.
Mission 1

Golgi apparatus, vesicles and lysosomes

  • Golgi apparatus: a stack of curved, flattened membrane sacs; no ribosomes.
  • It modifies proteins (e.g. adds carbohydrate) and packages them into vesicles.
  • Vesicles: small membrane sacs that carry substances around the cell.
  • Lysosome: a vesicle with a single membrane, full of hydrolytic enzymes. It digests engulfed material and worn-out organelles.
  • Lysosomes are formed from vesicles that bud off the Golgi apparatus.
Mission 1

Mitochondria

  • Double membrane. The inner membrane folds into cristae; the fluid inside is the matrix.
  • Site of aerobic respiration, which makes ATP.
  • Cristae give a large surface area for the membrane-bound steps that make ATP.
  • Mitochondria contain their own DNA and 70S ribosomes.
  • Very active cells, such as muscle cells and sperm (midpiece), have many mitochondria.
  • Say: "mitochondria transfer energy from glucose to ATP" — not: "mitochondria make energy".

Mission 1

Centrioles, cilia and flagella

  • Centrioles: a pair of short, hollow cylinders of microtubules, at right angles, near the nucleus of animal cells.
  • They help organise the spindle when an animal cell divides.
  • Microtubules belong to the cytoskeleton, a network of protein fibres through the cytoplasm (roles in Mission 3).
  • Cilia: many short, hair-like projections that beat to move fluid or mucus across a surface.
  • Flagella: longer, usually one or two; they move the whole cell, e.g. a sperm.
  • Both are built from microtubules in a 9 + 2 arrangement.

Mission check

Each organelle has a recognisable outline and a job. Read the outlines, and a micrograph tells you what the cell does.

Mission 2

Mission 2: A plant cell shares most of its parts with yours. So what does it have that yours lacks, and why?

Make a guess: A plant cell shares most of its parts with yours. So what does it have that yours lacks, and why?

Mission 2

The cell wall

  • A cell wall lies outside the plasma membrane.
  • Plant walls are made of cellulose. Fungal walls are made of chitin.
  • The wall gives strength and shape, and stops the cell bursting when water enters by osmosis.
  • Water and most small solutes pass through the wall. The plasma membrane controls what enters the cell.
  • Say: "the membrane controls entry" — not: "the wall controls entry".
Mission 2

Chloroplasts

  • Found in plants and algae: the site of photosynthesis.
  • Double membrane (envelope).
  • Thylakoids: flattened membrane sacs holding chlorophyll, stacked into grana.
  • Stroma: the fluid around the thylakoids, with the enzymes that make sugars.
  • Chloroplasts contain their own DNA and 70S ribosomes.
  • Not every plant cell has them: root cells, for example, do not.
  • Say: "plant cells have chloroplasts and mitochondria" — not: "chloroplasts instead of mitochondria".
Mission 2

The vacuole

  • Plant cells have a large, permanent vacuole filled with cell sap.
  • Water enters the vacuole; the contents press against the wall: the cell is turgid (firm).

Mission check

?Cells from a potato tuber have a cellulose cell wall, a large vacuole and many starch grains, but no chloroplasts. A student says they cannot be plant cells, because plants photosynthesise. Which response is correct?

A cellulose wall and a large vacuole are the reliable plant features. Chloroplasts appear only in cells that receive light.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Your pancreas makes enzymes that digest protein. How does it ship them out without digesting itself?

Make a guess: Your pancreas makes enzymes that digest protein. How does it ship them out without digesting itself?

Mission 3

The secretory pathway

  • Ribosomes on the rough ER make the protein. It passes into the space inside the RER.
  • The protein is folded, then carried in transport vesicles that bud off the RER.
  • The vesicles fuse with the Golgi apparatus, which modifies and packages the protein.
  • Secretory vesicles bud off the Golgi and move to the cell surface.
  • They fuse with the plasma membrane, releasing the protein outside: exocytosis.
Mission 3

Read the data: following a labelled protein

  • Pulse-chase: cells get radioactive amino acids for a few minutes (pulse), then unlabelled ones (chase).
  • Then: where is the labelled protein at each time? George Palade's group used this method on pancreas cells in the 1960s.

  • The label peaks in the RER first, then the Golgi, then the vesicles.
  • As it falls in one place, it rises in the next: the same labelled protein moves from one compartment to the next.
Quick check
?Pancreatic cells were supplied with radioactive amino acids for 3 minutes and then with non-radioactive amino acids. Samples of cells were taken at intervals and the location of radioactive protein was found. After 5 minutes it was mainly in the rough endoplasmic reticulum, after 20 minutes mainly in the Golgi apparatus, and after 60 minutes mainly in vesicles close to the plasma membrane. Which conclusion do these results support?
Mission 3

Finishing the protein

  • Many polypeptides do not work until they are modified.
  • Carbohydrate chains are added to many proteins, making glycoproteins. This starts in the RER and is finished in the Golgi.
  • The Golgi sorts proteins into vesicles by destination, e.g. secretion or the plasma membrane.
  • Some Golgi vesicles become lysosomes.
  • Say: "the Golgi modifies and packages proteins" — not: "the Golgi makes proteins".
Mission 3

Vesicles: bud, travel, fuse

  • A vesicle buds from one membrane and fuses with another. Membranes are fluid, so they can bend, pinch off and fuse.
  • Exocytosis: a vesicle fuses with the plasma membrane. Its contents leave; its membrane becomes part of the plasma membrane.
  • Endocytosis: the plasma membrane folds in around material and pinches off as a vesicle.
  • Both use energy from ATP.
Quick check
?A vesicle from the Golgi apparatus carries a protein to the plasma membrane for secretion. What happens to the membrane of the vesicle when the protein is released?
Mission 3

The cytoskeleton at work

  • A network of protein fibres: microtubules, microfilaments and intermediate filaments.
  • Mechanical strength: supports the cell's shape and resists stretching.
  • Transport within cells: motor proteins carry vesicles and organelles along microtubules.
  • Cell movement: microtubules in cilia and flagella; microfilaments help cells crawl.
  • Microtubules also form the spindle in cell division.
  • Say: "the cytoskeleton is dynamic: its fibres assemble and break down" — not: "a rigid, fixed frame".
Mission 3

Why build compartments?

  • Membranes keep enzymes and their substrates together in a small volume, so reactions run faster.
  • Membranes keep incompatible processes apart: hydrolytic enzymes stay inside lysosomes.
  • Each compartment can keep its own conditions: the inside of a lysosome is acidic (about pH 5).
  • Proteins made for export stay inside membranes until they leave the cell.
Mission check

?Plasma cells make antibodies, which are proteins they secrete into the blood. At which stage, if any, is a newly made antibody molecule free in the cell's cytoplasm?

Like the pancreas's enzymes, an exported protein is never loose in the cytoplasm. Membranes surround it from the RER until exocytosis.

Mission 4

Mission 4: A bacterium has no nucleus. So where does it keep its genes, and what else does it manage without?

Make a guess: A bacterium has no nucleus. So where does it keep its genes, and what else does it manage without?

Mission 4

What every cell has

  • Every cell has: a plasma membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes and DNA.
  • Two kinds of cell:
    • Prokaryotic (bacteria, archaea): no nucleus.
Mission 4

Inside a bacterium

  • No nucleus. One circular DNA molecule lies free in the cytoplasm, in the nucleoid region.
  • Prokaryotic DNA is short and not associated with proteins (no histones).
  • Ribosomes are smaller: 70S (eukaryotic cytoplasm: 80S).
  • No membrane-bound organelles: no mitochondria, no ER, no Golgi.
  • Cell wall of peptidoglycan, outside the plasma membrane.
  • Often present: plasmids (small extra DNA circles), a capsule, one or more flagella.
  • Say: "the DNA is in the nucleoid" — not: "the DNA is in a small nucleus".
Quick check
?Which description of the structure of a Bacillus cell is correct?

  • Shared by both: plasma membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes, DNA.
  • A cell wall does not tell you the cell type. Look for a nucleus.
Quick check
?A unicellular organism is found to have a cell wall, a plasma membrane and ribosomes, but no nucleus. Its DNA is a single circular molecule, not associated with histones, lying in the cytoplasm. A student concludes that the organism is a prokaryote. Which evaluation of this conclusion is correct?
Mission 4

Why a bacterium needs its wall

  • Bacterial cytoplasm is rich in solutes, so in dilute surroundings water enters by osmosis.
  • The wall is strong: it resists swelling and stops the cell bursting.
  • Water and small solutes pass through the wall; the plasma membrane, inside it, controls what enters the cell.
  • Strip the wall away (e.g. with the enzyme lysozyme) and a bacterium in water bursts.
Mission check

?An electron micrograph shows a cell about 3 µm long. It has a cell wall, a paler fibrous region of DNA not enclosed by any membrane, many small dense granules, and no membrane-bound organelles. How should the cell be classified?

No nucleus, but not disorganised: the genes sit in the nucleoid, and the cell manages without membrane-bound organelles.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Nucleus, mitochondrion: double membranes; RER (ribosomes), SER, Golgi, lysosomes: single membranes; ribosomes: no membrane, 80S in the cytoplasm.
  • Many of one organelle means a lot of its job: lots of RER and Golgi means protein export.

Mission 2

  • Plant cells add a cellulose wall, chloroplasts (in cells that get light) and a large permanent vacuole; fungal walls are chitin; animal cells have no wall.

Mission 3

  • Secreted proteins: made on RER → transport vesicles → Golgi (modified, packaged) → secretory vesicles → exocytosis.
  • Compartments concentrate enzymes and keep incompatible processes apart; exported or digestive enzymes stay behind membranes.

Mission 4

  • A prokaryote keeps one circular DNA molecule in the nucleoid, with no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles; it still has a membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes and (usually) a wall.
