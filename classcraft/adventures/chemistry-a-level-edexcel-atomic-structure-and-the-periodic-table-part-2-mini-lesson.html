Mission 1

Mission 1: Why do fireworks come in different colours?

Make a guess: Why do fireworks come in different colours?

Mission 1

Light comes in packets

  • Background: light travels as photons.
  • Shorter wavelength → higher frequency → more energy per photon.
  • In visible light, red photons carry the least energy, violet the most.
  • So a different colour means a different photon energy.
Mission 1

How atoms give out light

  • Heat or electricity excites an electron to a higher energy level.
  • In Edexcel's words, the levels are quantum shells.
  • When it falls back, it emits a photon. Photon energy = the energy gap.
  • Only certain gaps exist, so only certain colours: an emission line spectrum, bright lines on a dark background.
Quick check
?When sodium compounds are heated in a flame, the flame turns yellow. Which process in the sodium atoms produces this yellow light?
Mission 1

Flame colours

  • Each metal has its own energy gaps, so its compounds give their own flame colour.
  • Li crimson red · Na yellow-orange · K lilac.
  • Ca brick red · Sr red (crimson) · Ba pale green.
  • Rb red-violet · Cs blue-violet · Be, Mg: no colour.
  • Fireworks: strontium salts for red, barium for green, sodium for yellow (and copper for blue-green).
Mission 1

What hydrogen's spectrum proves

  • Hydrogen gives lines, not a continuous band, so its electron has fixed energy levels.
  • This is the evidence for quantum shells.
  • Levels get closer together as n rises, so each set of lines converges at high frequency.
  • Falls to n = 1 emit ultraviolet; to n = 2, mainly visible; to n = 3, infrared.
  • One electron, many lines: different transitions happen in different atoms of the sample.
Quick check
?Which electron transition in a hydrogen atom emits the photon of highest frequency?
Mission 1

How many electrons a level holds

  • Main levels (shells) are numbered n = 1, 2, 3, 4 outwards.
  • Level n holds at most 2n² electrons: 2, 8, 18, 32.
  • The third level holds 18, not 8. "2, 8, 8" is how the first 20 elements fill, not a capacity.
Quick check
?What is the maximum total number of electrons that the first three main energy levels of an atom (n = 1, 2 and 3) can hold?
Mission check

?Lithium compounds give a crimson-red flame. Sodium compounds give a yellow-orange flame. Which statement explains the difference?

That's the firework: each metal has its own energy gaps, so its falling electrons emit their own photon energies, and so their own colours.

Mission 2

Mission 2: If an electron isn't in an orbit, where is it?

Make a guess: If an electron isn't in an orbit, where is it?

Mission 2

Shells split into sub-shells

  • Each shell (main energy level, n) splits into sub-shells: s, p, d, f.
  • n = 1: 1s. n = 2: 2s, 2p. n = 3: 3s, 3p, 3d. n = 4: 4s, 4p, 4d, 4f.
  • s, p, d, f have 1, 3, 5, 7 orbitals, so hold 2, 6, 10, 14 electrons.
  • So the first four shells hold 2, 8, 18, 32.
  • Up to Z = 36 no f orbitals are filled; 4f only matters for the 32.
Mission 2

Orbitals: where an electron is

  • An orbital is a region around the nucleus where an electron is likely to be found. Not a path.
  • Each orbital holds up to two electrons, with opposite spins.
  • s orbital: a sphere centred on the nucleus.
  • p orbitals: three dumbbells along the x, y and z axes, at 90° to each other.
Quick check
?What is the maximum number of electrons that a 3d sub-shell can hold, and why?
Mission 2

Three filling rules

  • Aufbau ("building up"): fill the lowest energy first: 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p.
  • Pauli: at most two electrons per orbital, with opposite spins.
  • Hund: in orbitals of equal energy, electrons go in singly, spins parallel, before pairing.
  • Electrons-in-boxes: one box per orbital; arrows ↑↓ for spins. Oxygen 2p⁴: [↑↓][↑ ][↑ ].
Mission 2

Writing configurations

  • Iron, Z = 26: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d⁶ 4s², shortened to [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s².
  • 4s fills before 3d, but either written order is fine: [Ar] 4s² 3d⁶ is also correct.
  • Two exceptions up to krypton: Cr [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ and Cu [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹.
Quick check
?Which full electron configuration of a ground-state atom is correct?
Quick check
?Which electrons-in-boxes diagram correctly shows the 3p sub-shell of a ground-state sulfur atom (… 3s² 3p⁴)?
Mission 2

Configurations of ions

  • s- and p-block ions: add or remove electrons in the outer sub-shell.
  • Na⁺: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶. S²⁻: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ (same as Ar).
  • d-block ions lose their 4s electrons first.
  • Fe²⁺: [Ar] 3d⁶. Fe³⁺: [Ar] 3d⁵. Cu²⁺ (from [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹): [Ar] 3d⁹.
  • 4s fills first but empties first too.
Quick check
?An iron atom (Z = 26) has the condensed electron configuration [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s². How is an Fe²⁺ ion formed from this atom?
Mission 2

Configurations and the periodic table

  • Blocks are named after the sub-shell being filled: s-block (groups 1–2), p-block (13–18), d-block.
  • Across Periods 2 and 3: two s electrons are added, then six p electrons.
  • Down a group the outer configuration repeats: Mg 3s², Ca 4s². So their chemistry is similar (both form 2+ ions).
Mission check

?A ground-state phosphorus atom is [Ne] 3s² 3p³. Where are its three 3p electrons?

An electron isn't on a track. It is in an orbital: a region where it is likely to be. The configuration tells you which orbitals are occupied.

Mission 3

Mission 3: If nuclear charge rises across period 3, why does aluminium lose an electron more easily than magnesium?

Make a guess: If nuclear charge rises across period 3, why does aluminium lose an electron more easily than magnesium?

Mission 3

Two dips in period 3

  • First ionisation energy: energy to remove 1 mol of electrons from 1 mol of gaseous atoms. X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻.
  • Across period 3 it rises overall: more protons, same shell, similar shielding.
  • But it falls twice: Mg → Al (738 → 578) and P → S (1012 → 1000 kJ mol⁻¹).
  • After argon it falls steeply to potassium: a new shell. That is evidence for shells.
Mission 3

Why dips mean sub-shells

  • If every electron in shell 3 had the same energy, the rise would be smooth.
  • The dips show that shell 3 is split into sub-shells of different energy: 3s, then 3p.
  • In Edexcel's words: the first ionisation energy of successive elements provides evidence for electron sub-shells.
  • To explain a dip, compare the electron removed from each atom: which sub-shell, which orbital.
Mission 3

Mg → Al: a new sub-shell

  • Mg: [Ne] 3s². Al: [Ne] 3s² 3p¹.
  • Al's electron is removed from 3p, which is higher in energy than 3s.
  • The 3p electron is also slightly further from the nucleus and partly shielded by the 3s electrons.
  • So less energy is needed, even though Al has one more proton.
Quick check
?After the dip, the rise resumes: silicon's first ionisation energy is 787 kJ mol⁻¹, higher than aluminium's 578 kJ mol⁻¹. Why?
Mission 3

P → S: p-orbital repulsion

  • P: [Ne] 3s² 3p³. Three 3p electrons, one in each orbital: [↑ ] [↑ ] [↑ ].
  • S: [Ne] 3s² 3p⁴. One 3p orbital now holds a pair: [↑↓] [↑ ] [↑ ].
  • The paired electrons repel, so one of them is easier to remove.
  • Both electrons come from 3p: this dip is about orbitals, not a new sub-shell.
Quick check
?The first ionisation energies of phosphorus and sulfur are 1012 kJ mol⁻¹ and 1000 kJ mol⁻¹. Which explanation of the lower value for sulfur is correct?
Mission 3

The same pattern in period 2

  • Be → B (900 → 801 kJ mol⁻¹): B's electron comes from 2p, higher in energy than Be's 2s.
  • N → O (1402 → 1314 kJ mol⁻¹): O's electron comes from a paired 2p orbital: p-orbital repulsion.
  • Period 3 repeats this one shell further out: Mg → Al and P → S.
  • Be ready to sketch this plot from memory, or explain it from given data.
Quick check
?A student sketches first ionisation energy from Na to Ar as a smooth, steady rise. Which correction does the sketch need?
Mission check

?The first ionisation energies of beryllium and boron are 900 kJ mol⁻¹ and 801 kJ mol⁻¹. Which explanation of the lower value for boron is correct?

Same reason as aluminium. Its outer electron is in 3p, higher in energy than magnesium's 3s, so it is easier to remove despite the extra proton.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why is helium in group 18 when its atoms have only two electrons?

Make a guess: Why is helium in group 18 when its atoms have only two electrons?

Mission 4

Periods, groups and blocks

  • Elements are arranged in order of atomic (proton) number.
  • A period is a row. Its number is the outer occupied shell (energy level).
  • A group is a column, numbered 1 to 18. Elements in a group have similar chemical properties.
  • Blocks are named after the sub-shell being filled: s-block groups 1–2; p-block groups 13–18; d-block groups 3–12.
Mission 4

Metals, metalloids, non-metals

  • Background: useful for reading the table.
  • Metals fill the left and centre. Non-metals sit at the upper right, plus hydrogen.
  • Metalloids lie along the stepped line: B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te.
  • Metalloids have in-between properties: silicon is shiny but only a semiconductor.
  • Aluminium touches the line but is a metal: shiny, malleable, a good conductor.
Mission 4

Valence electrons and the group

  • Valence electrons: the electrons in the outer shell.
  • Groups 1–2: valence electrons = group number.
  • Groups 13–18: valence electrons = group number − 10.
  • Group 16 atoms have 6 valence electrons, not 16. Group 13 atoms have 3.
  • Helium is the one exception. A later screen explains it.
Mission 4

From position to configuration

  • Period gives the outer shell, n. Block gives the sub-shell being filled.
  • Bromine: period 4, p-block, group 17 → 7 valence electrons, 4s² 4p⁵.
  • Bromine: [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s² 4p⁵. The 3d¹⁰ is inner: not valence.
  • Manganese: period 4, d-block, group 7 → [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s².
Mission 4

From configuration to position

  • Highest n → period. Sub-shell being filled → block.
  • [Ne] 3s² 3p¹: period 3, p-block, 3 outer electrons → group 13 (aluminium).
  • [Ar] 3d⁸ 4s²: period 4, d-block, 8 + 2 → group 10 (nickel).
  • Count the whole outer shell, not just the last sub-shell: 3s² 3p¹ is three.
Quick check
?Germanium is in period 4, group 14. Which is the full electron configuration of a germanium atom?
Quick check
?An atom has the configuration [Ne] 3s² 3p⁵. Where is the element in the periodic table?
Mission 4

Outer configuration decides the chemistry

  • Chemical reactions involve the outer electrons.
  • Same group → same outer configuration → similar chemical properties.
  • Group 2: outer ns². The highest-energy electrons are in an s sub-shell, so Group 2 is in the s-block.
  • Each Group 2 metal loses its two s electrons: Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺, Sr²⁺, Ba²⁺.
  • Same pattern of formulae: MgO, CaO, SrO, BaO; H2O, H2S, H2Se.
Mission 4

So where does helium go?

  • Helium is 1s². By configuration alone, its last electron is in an s sub-shell.
  • But shell n = 1 holds at most 2 electrons (2n² = 2). So 1s² is a full outer shell.
  • A full outer shell → very hard to remove or add electrons → unreactive, like Ne, Ar, Kr.
  • It does not behave like group 2: those metals readily lose their ns² electrons.
  • So helium is placed with the chemistry it shares: group 18.
  • By configuration helium is s² (s-block); by position and chemistry it sits in group 18.
Mission check

?Beryllium, 1s² 2s², and helium, 1s², both have two electrons in their outer s sub-shell. Why is beryllium placed in group 2, but helium in group 18?

Position in the table follows the outer shell and the chemistry it gives, not a simple count of electrons.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • An electron falling between levels emits a photon; photon energy = the gap. Fixed gaps → fixed colours.
  • Level n holds 2n²: 2, 8, 18, 32.

Mission 2

  • Sub-shells s, p, d, f hold 2, 6, 10, 14; orbitals hold two electrons of opposite spin.
  • Fill 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p; singly before pairing. Cr and Cu are exceptions; 4s empties first in ions.

Mission 3

  • First IE rises across a period, with dips where a new sub-shell starts (Be→B, Mg→Al) and where p electrons first pair (N→O, P→S).
  • The dips are evidence that shells are split into sub-shells.

Mission 4

  • Period = outer shell; group = similar outer configuration and chemistry; block = sub-shell being filled.
  • Classify an element as s, p or d (and f) block from its position.
  • Helium's 1s² is a full shell, so it sits with the noble gases.
