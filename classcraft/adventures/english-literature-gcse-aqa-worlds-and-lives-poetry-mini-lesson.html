Mission 1

Spring is joyful. So why is Wordsworth sad?

Make a guess: Spring is joyful. So why is Wordsworth sad?

Worlds and Lives · Lines Written in Early SpringScreen 1

Spring 1798

  • Wordsworth wrote the poem in spring 1798; it was printed that year in Lyrical Ballads, with poems by Coleridge, and no author's name on the title page.
  • The book called its poems "experiments" in the language of ordinary conversation.
  • Britain had been at war with revolutionary France since 1793, and change was coming to work and towns.
  • Your anthology prints Wordsworth's later revised text (his collected editions from 1837 on), quoted here.

Worlds and Lives · Lines Written in Early SpringScreen 5

Form and structure

  • Six quatrains (four-line stanzas) rhyming ABAB, like a simple song or ballad.
  • Mostly iambic tetrameter (four beats: i HEARD a THOUsand BLENDed NOTES); the last line of most stanzas is shorter, with three beats: what MAN has MADE of MAN.
  • The line "What man has made of man" comes twice: a statement in stanza 2, then a question at the end.
  • Words of belief build up: "faith", "I must think", "belief". He trusts nature's joy but can't prove it.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Spring is joyful. So why is Wordsworth sad?

The sadness comes from contrast. Everything in the wood seems to share in joy, so the speaker grieves at "what man has made of man": humans, part of the same nature, have made misery for each other. He can't prove nature's joy, which is why he calls it his "faith".

Mission 2

Fourteen lines of fury. Is there any hope in them?

Make a guess: Fourteen lines of fury. Is there any hope in them?

Worlds and Lives · England in 1819Screen 1

England, 1819

  • George III, king since 1760, was old, blind and mentally ill; his son had ruled as Prince Regent since 1811.
  • 16 August 1819, Peterloo: cavalry charged a peaceful crowd at St Peter's Field, Manchester, who had gathered to demand reform; people were killed and hundreds injured.
  • Shelley sent the sonnet to his friend Leigh Hunt, who judged it too dangerous to print: it could count as seditious libel.
  • It was first published in 1839, after Shelley's death, by Mary Shelley.

Worlds and Lives · England in 1819Screen 5

Form: a sonnet that breaks the rules

  • Fourteen lines of iambic pentameter, but the rhyme scheme is unusual: ABABAB CDCDCC DD, neither Petrarchan nor Shakespearean.
  • The whole poem is one sentence. Twelve lines list subjects; the main verb, "Are", arrives only in line 13.
  • So the volta (turn) is held back to the last two lines, after the list has built up anger.
  • Line 1 crams in heavy stresses: an OLD, MAD, BLIND, deSPISED, and DYing KING.

?Fourteen lines of fury. Is there any hope in them?

There is hope, but Shelley makes you wait for it. The single sentence piles up twelve lines of corruption, and only in line 13 do all these evils become "graves from which a glorious Phantom may / Burst". "May" matters: the better future is possible, not certain.

Mission 3

A voice calls a dreamer home. Whose voice is it?

Make a guess: A voice calls a dreamer home. Whose voice is it?

Worlds and Lives · Shall earth no more inspire theeScreen 1

A poem without a title

  • Emily Brontë (1818–1848) lived most of her life at Haworth, on the Yorkshire moors. In 1846 she published poems as "Ellis Bell".
  • This poem was printed in 1850, after her death, in a selection made by her sister Charlotte.
  • Charlotte noted that "The following little piece has no title", so anthologies use the first line.
  • Charlotte read it as a spirit of a lonely place calling back its "wandering and wayward" follower.

Worlds and Lives · Shall earth no more inspire theeScreen 5

Form and structure

  • Seven quatrains rhyming ABAB, in short iambic trimeter lines (three beats): shall EARTH no MORE inSPIRE thee.
  • Lines 1 and 3 usually end on an unstressed syllable (feminine rhyme: inspire thee / fire thee); lines 2 and 4 end on a stress. The rocking pattern is like a song or lullaby.
  • It opens with questions, then "I know…" is repeated (stanzas 3 and 5), and it ends with a command: "Return".
  • "dwell with me" ends stanzas 2 and 7, like a refrain.

Links in your cluster

Mission checkScreen 9

?A voice calls a dreamer home. Whose voice is it?

The voice owns the "mountain breezes", the "sunshine" and the "winds", so it is most often read as the earth or nature itself, personified, calling back a dreamer whose mind has wandered into "regions dark". Charlotte heard the spirit of a lonely place; the strongest answers make a case from the "my" phrases and note the alternatives.

Mission 4

A view from a London window. What is missing from it?

Make a guess: A view from a London window. What is missing from it?

Worlds and Lives · In a London DrawingroomScreen 1

George Eliot's London

  • George Eliot was the pen name of Mary Ann Evans (1819–1880), author of Silas Marner and Middlemarch.
  • She wrote this poem in London in the 1860s; it was not published until 1959, long after her death.
  • Victorian London burned coal in homes and works, so smoke hung over the streets.
  • This lesson doesn't quote the poem: learn your key lines from your anthology.
Worlds and Lives · In a London DrawingroomScreen 2

LinesWhat the speaker sees (paraphrase)
1–6A cloudy sky stained by smoke; the houses opposite make one long wall, flat and endless
7–10No bird casts a shadow, because everything is shadow; sunlight seems muffled under cloth
10–14Nobody stops to rest; people hurry, eyes down, barely noticing each other
15–16Cabs and carriages hurry too, all closed and all alike
17–19The world seems a huge prison where people are punished cheaply, given the least possible colour, warmth and joy
Quick checkScreen 3
?The title names a drawing room, but almost every line describes the street. What does that suggest?
Worlds and Lives · In a London DrawingroomScreen 4

Form and structure

  • One unbroken stanza of 19 lines: no break, like the view itself.
  • Unrhymed lines of about ten syllables, mostly iambic pentameter: blank verse (some guides call it free verse).
  • Enjambment runs the sight on and on; the one full stop that falls mid-line (a caesura, line 10) feels like a dead end.
  • Negatives pile up (no bird, no figure, no break), so the poem is built from what is missing.
  • It ends on a list of three: colour, warmth and joy, all rationed.

Links in your cluster

  • Pair with "Lines Written in Early Spring" (nature full of joy against a city with none; both grieve at what people have made of their world), "England in 1819" (a society that punishes its people), or "Shall earth no more inspire thee" (nature calling a lonely mind home).
Mission checkScreen 8

?A view from a London window. What is missing from it?

Almost everything is missing. Built from negatives, the view lacks birds, light, colour and anyone who pauses, and people hurry past each other without noticing. By the end the whole world seems a prison that hands out the smallest ration of colour, warmth and joy.

Poetry skills · comparing poemsScreen 1

One argument, two poems

  • A comparison is one argument about both poems, not two essays joined by "on the other hand".
  • Compare what the poems say (ideas, attitudes) and how they say it (language, form, structure).
  • Find a link first, then the differences within it.
  • Keep both poems in play in each paragraph, using whereas, similarly, in contrast, both.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Spring 1798, printed in Lyrical Ballads: a speaker in a wood finds joy in nature and grief at "what man has made of man".
  • Six ABAB quatrains with shorter final lines; the repeated line moves from statement to question.
  • In Paper 2 Section B, compare it with another cluster poem on nature, power or the city.
  • Written in 1819, the year of Peterloo; too dangerous to print until 1839.
  • One long sentence lists England's evils; the volta is held back to the final couplet.
  • In Paper 2 Section B, compare it with another cluster poem about power, society or suffering.
  • First printed 1850, after Emily Brontë's death; untitled, so known by its first line.
  • A voice, most often read as nature, calls a "lonely dreamer" home: questions, then "I know…", then the command "Return".
  • In Paper 2 Section B, compare it with another cluster poem about nature, belonging or home.
  • Written in the 1860s, published 1959: a watcher in a London drawing room sees a city of sameness and absence.
  • One unbroken stanza of unrhymed lines, built from negatives, ending in a prison image and a list of three.
  • In Paper 2 Section B, compare it with a cluster poem about nature, society or belonging.
  • A comparison is one argument about both poems: find a link, then explore the differences within it.
  • Compare ideas and methods together (voice, metre, rhyme, imagery), using comparative connectives in every paragraph.
  • Your exam pairs a named poem with a second poem (one you choose, or an unseen one): know which, and write about both.

Key quotations to learn

  • "In that sweet mood when pleasant thoughts / Bring sad thoughts to the mind." (Stanza 1) The turn from joy to sadness, set up at once
  • "To her fair works did Nature link / The human soul" (Stanza 2) Nature personified as a mother joining people to the natural world
  • "What man has made of man" (Stanzas 2 and 6) The key line: statement, then question; humans have harmed each other
  • "Enjoys the air it breathes" (Stanza 3) Personification: nature lives in joy
  • "It seemed a thrill of pleasure" (Stanza 4) Joy is what nature "seemed": belief, not proof
  • "If such be Nature's holy plan" (Stanza 6) The final question rests on an "If": nature's joy may be a holy plan that humans break
  • "An old, mad, blind, despised, and dying king" (Line 1) Heavy stresses attack George III at once
  • "mud from a muddy spring" (Line 3) Metaphor: corruption inherited from the king
  • "to their fainting country cling" (Line 5) Rulers compared to leeches, draining a country that is already weak
  • "A people starved and stabbed" (Line 7) Hunger and violence; recalls Peterloo
  • "a glorious Phantom may / Burst, to illumine our tempestuous day" (Lines 13–14) Delayed hope, only a possibility
  • "Shall earth no more inspire thee, / Thou lonely dreamer now?" (Stanza 1) Opens with a question: the dreamer has turned away
  • "Recall its useless roving, / Come back, and dwell with me." (Stanza 2) The voice judges the wandering mind and calls it home
  • "I know my mountain breezes / Enchant and soothe thee still" (Stanza 3) "My": the voice owns the natural world
  • "I know my magic power / To drive thy griefs away." (Stanza 5) Confident repetition: nature heals
  • "Then let my winds caress thee" (Stanza 7) Personification: nature as a tender companion
