Mission 1

From stimulus to response

  • A stimulus is a change in the environment, inside or outside the body.
  • Receptor detects it → sensory neurone → CNS → motor neurone → effector responds.
  • CNS (central nervous system) = brain + spinal cord. It coordinates: it links input to output.
  • Effectors: muscles contract; glands secrete.
  • Sensory neurones carry input from receptor cells to the spinal cord and the cerebral hemispheres.
  • Motor neurones carry output from the cerebral hemispheres to muscles, which are stimulated to contract.
Mission 1

A nerve is a bundle, not a cell

  • A neurone is one cell. A nerve is a bundle of nerve fibres from many neurones.
  • Most nerves carry both sensory and motor fibres.
  • Transverse section of a nerve:
    • Outer protective sheath of connective tissue around the whole bundle.
    • Myelinated fibres: axon (dot) inside a pale ring of myelin.
    • Unmyelinated fibres: bare axons, no ring. Normal, not damaged.
  • Say: "the protective sheath surrounds the nerve" — not: "the myelin sheath surrounds the nerve".
Quick check
?A transverse section of a nerve shows an outer layer of connective tissue enclosing many small circular profiles. Some profiles have a thick pale ring around a central dot; others have no ring. What does the section show?
Mission 1

The reflex arc

  • A reflex: a rapid, automatic response to a stimulus, with no conscious decision.
  • Frame 1 — Receptor detects the stimulus; sensory neurone carries impulses into the spinal cord.
  • Frame 2 — In the grey matter of the spinal cord, one relay neurone (interneuron) passes impulses on, across synapses.
  • Frame 3 — Motor neurone carries impulses out to the effector: a skeletal muscle, which contracts.
  • Three neurones, two synapses: a three-neurone simple reflex.
  • Pain reflex: the receptor is a free nerve ending of a sensory neuron in the skin of the hand.
  • Involuntary, yet the effector is skeletal muscle, not smooth muscle.
Quick check
?A person touches a hot pan and withdraws their hand before feeling any pain. Which sequence describes the reflex arc that produced the withdrawal?
Mission 1

Fast, automatic, protective

  • Fast: a short pathway with only two synapses.
  • Involuntary: the response is produced in the spinal cord. The brain does not decide.
  • Protective: the body part is removed before much damage is done.
  • The brain still finds out: other neurones carry impulses up the cord. Pain is felt after the movement.
  • Conscious processes: we are aware of them and can choose, e.g. deciding to pick up a cup. Cerebral hemispheres.
  • Unconscious processes: no awareness, no choice. The spinal cord is an integrating centre for many of them.
  • Say: "the reflex is coordinated in the spinal cord" — not: "the brain decides very quickly".
Quick check
?Which statement about the spinal cord and unconscious processes is correct?
Mission check

?Months after an injury that cut through the spinal cord in the neck, a doctor pricks the sole of a patient's foot. The leg pulls the foot away, but the patient says they felt nothing. Which explanation is correct?

The movement and the feeling travel separate routes. The spinal cord moves you first; the brain finds out later.

Mission 2

Two ways to send a message

  • Nervous system:
    • Electrical impulses along neurones.
    • To specific cells. Fast (milliseconds). Short-lived.
  • Endocrine (hormonal) system:
    • Hormones secreted by glands into the blood.
    • Carried to every organ. Only target cells with matching receptors respond.
    • Slower to start. Longer-lasting.
  • The two often work together, e.g. fight or flight.
  • The blood also integrates organs by transporting materials: e.g. glucose from the intestine to muscles, urea from the liver to the kidneys.
Quick check
?Which statement correctly distinguishes the roles of the nervous system and the endocrine system in sending messages between organs?
Mission 2

Fight or flight

  • Threat detected → brain (hypothalamus) → sympathetic neurones activated: a nervous response in milliseconds.
  • Sympathetic neurones also stimulate the adrenal glands to secrete adrenaline into the blood.
  • IB name: epinephrine.
  • Adrenaline reaches every organ, extending and spreading the response:
    • Heart rate and stroke volume up.
    • Bronchioles widen; breathing deeper and faster.
    • Liver: glycogen → glucose; blood glucose up.
    • Blood diverted: arterioles to skeletal muscle widen; to gut and skin narrow.
    • Pupils dilate.
  • Result: more oxygen and glucose reach the muscles, ready for intense contraction.
  • Say: "adrenaline prepares the muscles; motor neurones make them contract" — not: "adrenaline makes the muscles contract".
Quick check
?A sprinter waits for the starting signal and epinephrine (adrenaline) is secreted into the blood. Which set of effects of epinephrine helps to facilitate intense contraction of the leg muscles during the race?
Mission 2

Moving food: two nervous systems

  • Swallowing is started voluntarily: controlled by the CNS.
  • Then peristalsis: waves of muscle contraction push food along the gut.
  • Peristalsis is involuntary: controlled by the enteric nervous system (ENS), a network of neurons in the gut wall.
  • The ENS coordinates the waves: muscle contracts behind the food and relaxes in front of it.
  • Egestion of faeces is voluntary again: CNS.
  • Say: "the ENS controls peristalsis" — not: "the brain sends an impulse for each wave".
Quick check
?A person swallows a mouthful of food. Which statement correctly describes how the movement of this food through the digestive system is controlled?
Mission check

?After a heart transplant, the new heart has no nerve connections to the brain. During exercise its rate still rises, but more slowly than a normal heart's. Which explanation fits?

Nerves give speed; hormones give reach. Lose one route, and the other still works, more slowly.

Mission 3

Chemical messengers from the tips

  • Plants coordinate growth with chemical signals: plant hormones.
  • IB calls them phytohormones: signalling chemicals controlling growth, development and responses to stimuli.
  • Made in growing regions (shoot and root tips); they move to other tissues and act there.
  • No glands, no blood: they move from cell to cell or in the vascular tissue.
  • The best known: auxin. The main natural auxin is IAA (indoleacetic acid).
  • A tropism is a directional growth response to a directional stimulus: towards it = positive; away from it = negative.
Mission 3

Auxin makes cells longer

  • IAA acts in the region just behind the tip.
  • It promotes cell elongation, not cell division.
  • IAA stimulates proton pumps: H⁺ ions are secreted into the cell wall (the apoplast).
  • The wall's pH falls. Cross-links between cellulose molecules loosen.
  • The cell takes up water by osmosis; turgor pressure stretches the loosened wall.
  • Say: "the cross-links loosen and the wall stretches" — not: "the acid dissolves the cellulose".
  • More elongation on one side than the other → the organ bends.
Quick check
?Which statement describes how auxin promotes the elongation of a plant cell?
Mission 3

Bending towards the light

  • Light from one side (lateral light) is detected at the shoot tip.
  • IAA is redistributed to the shaded side. It is not destroyed by light.
  • Shaded-side cells elongate more than lit-side cells.
  • Unequal growth bends the shoot towards the light: positive phototropism.
  • Takes hours to days: it is growth, not movement.
  • Say: "the shaded side elongates more" — not: "the lit side grows towards the light".
Mission 3

Efflux carriers set the direction

  • Auxin diffuses freely into cells, but cannot diffuse out.
  • It leaves only through auxin efflux carriers in the plasma membrane.
  • A cell can place its carriers on one side only.
  • All cells place carriers on the same side → auxin is actively transported cell to cell, in one direction.
  • Auxin becomes concentrated in one part of the tissue: a concentration gradient is kept.
  • Lateral light: carriers move auxin towards the shaded side → gradient → phototropism.
Quick check
?A seedling is grown in a box with a window on its left side only. After two days the shoot has curved towards the window. Which statement explains the curvature?
Mission 3Practical

Observing tropisms in seedlings (IB skill)

  • Grow seedlings in a box lit through one side slit; control box evenly lit.
  • Qualitative data: labelled diagrams of the curved shoots.
  • Quantitative data: angle of curvature, measured from the vertical with a protractor.
  • Precision (repeats agree): many seedlings; give a mean.
  • Accuracy (close to the true value): measure from a photograph; align the protractor with the vertical, not the pot edge.
  • More repeats reduce random error only. They cannot remove a systematic error.
Quick check
?Students investigating phototropism recorded three observations of seedlings lit from one side: (1) a labelled drawing showing the shoot curved towards the lamp; (2) 'the shoots bent much more than those of the control'; (3) an angle of curvature of 32° measured with a protractor. Which classification is correct?
Mission check

?A thin plastic sheet is pushed down through the tip of a shoot so that it separates the half facing a lamp from the shaded half. The plastic stops any chemical crossing from one half to the other. The shoot is lit from one side for a day. It grows upwards without bending. What is the best explanation?

Bending is just unequal elongation: block the hormone gradient and the bend disappears.

Mission 4

A team of plant hormones

  • Plants use several hormones, not just auxin. They often act together.
  • Auxins: cell elongation (see Mission 3).
  • Cytokinins: cell division.
  • Ethylene (IUPAC name ethene): fruit ripening.
  • A variety of chemicals act as phytohormones; their interactions control growth.
Mission 4

Cytokinins: more cells, balanced growth

  • Cytokinins promote cell division.
  • Root tips make cytokinin → transported to shoots → promotes shoot growth.
  • Shoot tips make auxin → transported to roots → promotes root growth (e.g. new side roots).
  • Each organ's signal tells the other how much it is growing: root and shoot growth stay integrated.
Quick check
?A plant's roots are damaged, reducing the number of root tips. Using the interaction between auxin and cytokinin, what is the predicted effect on the shoots?
Mission 4

Ethene ripens fruit, and fruit makes more ethene

  • Ethylene (ethene) is a gas, made by ripening fruit.
  • It triggers ripening: starch → sugars, cell walls softened, colour changes.
  • Ripening stimulates more ethylene production: positive feedback.
  • The gas diffuses to nearby fruits, so they ripen too.
  • Benefit: ripening is rapid and synchronised → many ripe fruits at once attract seed-dispersing animals.
  • Say: "positive feedback amplifies the change" — not: "positive feedback is the helpful kind".
Quick check
?The concentration of ethylene in the air around a ripening fruit was measured each day: 0.1, 0.3, 1.2 and 4.8 ppm on days 1 to 4, by which time the fruit was fully ripe. Which statement best interprets these data?
Mission 4

Phenology: getting the timing right

  • Phenology: research into the timing of biological events.
  • Examples: flowering, budburst and bud set in deciduous trees, bird migration and nesting.
  • Two key cues: photoperiod (day length) and temperature patterns.
  • Photoperiod on a given date and latitude is the same every year.
  • Temperature patterns vary between years, and shift as the climate warms.
  • Species cued by different variables can get out of step (D4.3).
Quick check
?A migratory bird species arrives at its northern breeding grounds on almost the same date every year, whether the spring is warm or cold. In the same region, the date of budburst in birch trees varies by up to three weeks between years. What can be concluded about the cues?
Mission check

?Apples are stored in a sealed room. One apple is bruised and starts to ripen early. Within a few days, apples all around it are ripening too. What is the best explanation?

Without a brain or nerves, a plant times its life with chemical signals and light: one ripe fruit can set off the rest.

Mission 5

What makes a pathogen

  • A pathogen is an organism that causes disease.
  • Diseases caused by pathogens are infectious: they can pass from host to host.
  • Four groups include pathogens: bacteria, viruses, fungi, protoctists (protists).
  • Archaea are not known to cause any human disease.
  • Most microbes on and in you cause no disease. Being a microbe does not make something a pathogen.
Quick check
?Which statement about the organisms that cause infectious diseases in humans is correct?
Mission 5

Zoonoses

  • A zoonosis is an infectious disease that can transfer from another species to humans.
  • Tuberculosis: M. bovis in cattle passes to humans, e.g. in unpasteurised milk.
  • Rabies: virus in the saliva of an infected mammal enters by a bite.
  • Japanese encephalitis: virus in pigs and wading birds; carried to humans by mosquitoes.
  • COVID-19: SARS-CoV-2 recently transferred from another species, then spread from person to person, with profound consequences.
  • Zoonosis describes where the pathogen came from, not the route. Many zoonoses then spread between people.

Mission 6

Skin and mucous membranes

  • Skin: an outer layer of tough, dead cells. Few pathogens can get through: a physical barrier.
  • Oily sebum keeps the skin surface acidic, inhibiting many pathogens: a chemical barrier.
  • Mucous membranes line the airways, gut, reproductive tract and eyes. No dead-cell layer here.
  • Their mucus traps pathogens. In the airways, cilia sweep the mucus up to the throat to be swallowed.
  • These primary defences are always in place, before any infection.
Quick check
?Sebaceous glands in the skin secrete sebum. Which statement explains how sebum contributes to the skin's role as a primary defence against pathogens?
Mission 6

Sealing a cut

  • At a cut, platelets release clotting factors.
  • A cascade of reactions follows, producing the enzyme thrombin.
  • Thrombin converts soluble fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin.
  • Fibrin threads form a mesh that traps red blood cells (erythrocytes): a clot seals the wound.
  • Full mechanism: a later lesson.

Mission 6

Phagocytosis

  • Phagocytes squeeze out of capillaries (amoeboid movement) and move to the infection site.
  • A phagocyte recognises foreign molecules on the pathogen.
  • It engulfs the pathogen by endocytosis, enclosing it in a vesicle: a phagosome.
  • Lysosomes fuse with the phagosome and release hydrolytic enzymes, which digest the pathogen.
  • Soluble products are absorbed.
  • Say: "digested inside a phagosome by lysosomal enzymes" — not: "enzymes are released onto the pathogen".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly describes how a phagocyte destroys a bacterium?
Mission check

?In a phagocyte, the lysosomes cannot fuse with phagosomes, but every other step works normally. What happens to a bacterium that meets this phagocyte?

Barriers keep most pathogens out. For those that get in, a phagocyte must both engulf and digest.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Receptor → sensory neurone → relay neurone in the spinal cord → motor neurone → effector: a fast, involuntary, protective reflex, with no decision by the brain.
  • Nerves are bundles of sensory and motor fibres in a protective sheath; the spinal cord integrates unconscious processes.

Mission 2

  • Nerves are fast and targeted; hormones are slower, carried everywhere and act on cells with receptors. In fight or flight, adrenaline spreads and prolongs the sympathetic response.
  • Swallowing and egestion are voluntary (CNS); peristalsis between them is involuntary (ENS).

Mission 3

  • Plant hormones are made in growing regions and move to other tissues; auxin (IAA) promotes cell elongation, and unequal elongation bends an organ.
  • Phototropism: auxin moves to the shaded side; shaded cells elongate more; the shoot bends towards light.

Mission 4

  • Ethylene ripens fruit, and ripening makes more ethylene: positive feedback synchronises ripening.
  • Photoperiod (same every year) and temperature (variable) cue the timing of flowering and other events.

Mission 5

  • A pathogen is something that causes disease: bacteria, viruses, fungi and protoctists include pathogens; most microbes do not.
  • Zoonoses transfer from other species; careful observation controlled disease before germ theory.

Mission 6

  • Barriers stop entry: skin (physical and chemical), mucus and cilia; clotting seals cuts.
  • Innate defences respond the same way to any pathogen; phagocytes engulf by endocytosis and digest with lysosomal enzymes in a phagosome.
