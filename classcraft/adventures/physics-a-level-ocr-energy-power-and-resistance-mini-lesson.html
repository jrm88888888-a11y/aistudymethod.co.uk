Mission 1

Mission 1: A battery is marked one and a half volts, a lamp six. What exactly does a volt count?

Make a guess: A battery is marked one and a half volts, a lamp six. What exactly does a volt count?

Mission 1

Potential difference

  • Potential difference (pd) between two points: the work done (energy transferred) per unit charge moving between them.
  • V = W/Q
  • 1 volt = 1 J C⁻¹: 1 J transferred for each coulomb passing between the points.
Mission 1

Energy in a lamp

  • Charge passing through a lamp or resistor transfers electrical energy to internal energy and light.
  • The charge comes out; the energy stays behind.
  • Worked example: potential difference across a lamp 6.0 V; 3.0 C passes through.
  • W = VQ = 6.0 × 3.0 = 18 J transferred in the lamp.
Quick check
?A steady current of 0.50 A flows through a small electric motor for 2.0 minutes. In this time, 360 J of energy is transferred from electrical energy to other forms in the motor. What is the potential difference across the motor?
Quick check
?Predict first. A cell has an emf of 1.5 V. What does this mean?
Mission 1

Electromotive force (emf)

  • A cell or supply does work on the charge: energy from another store (chemical, light) becomes electrical energy.
  • emf: energy transferred to electrical energy per unit charge passing through the source.
  • Symbol E.
  • Energy transferred by the source: W = EQ.
  • emf is measured in volts, like potential difference. It is not a force.
Mission 1

emf or pd?

  • emf: other forms → electrical energy. Happens in the source.
  • pd: electrical energy → other forms. Happens in components (lamps, resistors, motors).
  • Both: energy per unit charge, in J C⁻¹ = V.
  • Round a complete circuit, all the energy the source gives each coulomb is transferred in the components.
Mission 1

Where emf comes from

  • Chemical cell: reactions push positive and negative charge apart, to opposite terminals. Chemical energy → electrical.
  • A cell does not store charge or make new charge. It gives energy to charge that is already in the circuit.
Mission 1

The electronvolt

  • 1 electronvolt (eV): the energy transferred when an electron moves through a pd of 1 V.
  • W = QV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C × 1 V → 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
  • eV → J: × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹. J → eV: ÷ 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹.
  • Example: 5.0 keV = 5.0 × 10³ × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 8.0 × 10⁻¹⁶ J.
  • Used for particle and photon energies, where joules give awkward powers of ten.
Mission 1

Accelerating a charged particle

  • A particle of charge e, from rest, through a pd V: work done on it = eV. It all becomes kinetic energy.
  • eV = ½mv²
  • Electron through 100 V: KE = 100 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁷ J.
  • v = √(2 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁷ ÷ 9.11 × 10⁻³¹) = 5.9 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹.
  • Valid while v is much less than the speed of light.
Quick check
?An electron, initially at rest, is accelerated through a potential difference of 2.0 kV. What kinetic energy does it gain, in joules?
Mission check

?A battery of emf 12 V drives a steady current of 40 A through a starter motor for 1.5 s. How much energy does the battery transfer to the charge that passes through it in this time?

A volt counts joules per coulomb. A 1.5 V cell gives each coulomb 1.5 J; a 12 V battery gives each coulomb 12 J.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Double the potential difference across a wire. When does the current exactly double?

Make a guess: Double the potential difference across a wire. When does the current exactly double?

Mission 2

Resistance

  • Resistance: how strongly a component opposes the flow of charge.
  • R = V/I: the pd across it ÷ the current through it.
  • Unit: the ohm, 1 Ω = 1 V A⁻¹.
  • Same potential difference: bigger R → smaller current.
Quick check
?Predict first. What is the origin of the electrical resistance of a metal wire?
Mission 2

Where resistance comes from

  • The potential difference sets up an electric field in the wire. Free electrons are accelerated by it (opposite to the field: they are negative)…
  • …then collide with the vibrating positive ions of the lattice, giving them energy.
  • Speed up, collide, speed up again: a steady average drift.
  • Ions gain energy → vibrate more → the conductor warms: the heating effect of a current.
  • The energy comes from the charges' electrical energy. No charge is used up.
Mission 2

Ohm's law

  • Ohm's law: the current in a metallic conductor is proportional to the pd across it, provided its temperature (and other physical conditions) stays constant.
  • Ohmic I–V graph: a straight line through the origin, same in both directions.
  • R = V/I defines resistance for any component, at any point. Ohm's law is the special case where R stays constant.
Mission 2

Worked example: is it ohmic?

  • Readings: 2.0 V, 0.40 A and 6.0 V, 1.2 A.
  • R = V/I: 2.0 ÷ 0.40 = 5.0 Ω; 6.0 ÷ 1.2 = 5.0 Ω. Same → consistent with ohmic.
  • Graph of I (vertical) against V (horizontal): gradient = 1.2 ÷ 6.0 = 0.20 A V⁻¹ = 1/R.
  • With V on the vertical axis, the gradient is R itself.
Quick check
?The I–V characteristic of a filament lamp is plotted with current I on the vertical axis and pd V on the horizontal axis. Up to 0.10 V the graph is a straight line through the origin, and at 0.10 V the current is 0.050 A. At 6.0 V the current is 0.50 A, and the tangent to the curve at this point has a gradient of 0.040 A V⁻¹. What is the resistance of the lamp at 6.0 V, and how is it found?
Mission 2

The filament lamp

  • I–V graph: through the origin, curving towards the V axis; the same shape for reversed pd.
  • Larger current → filament hotter → ions vibrate more → more collisions → R rises.
  • So equal steps in V give smaller and smaller steps in I. Non-ohmic.
  • Resistance at a point = V/I at that point. Not the gradient of the tangent.
  • A metal wire kept at constant temperature: a straight line. Same metal, different temperature behaviour.
Quick check
?A student investigates whether a length of metal wire obeys Ohm's law. At the highest currents, V/I is clearly larger than at low currents. What is the best change to the method?
Mission 2

Practical: measuring an I–V characteristic

  • Ammeter in series with the component; voltmeter in parallel across it.
  • Vary the pd with a potential divider (or variable resistor). Take readings in steps.
  • Reverse the component (or supply) for negative values.
  • Wire: keep the current small (or the wire in a water bath) so its temperature stays constant.
  • Lamp: let each reading settle before recording.
Mission 2

Reading your results

  • Plot I (vertical) against V.
  • Wire at constant temperature: straight line through the origin → ohmic; R = 1/gradient.
  • Filament lamp: curve bending towards the V axis → R = V/I rises with current.
  • Find R at any point from V/I, not from the tangent.
Mission check

?Three components X, Y and Z are tested. The currents at pds of 1.0 V, 2.0 V and 4.0 V are: X — 0.10 A, 0.20 A, 0.40 A; Y — 0.05 A, 0.15 A, 0.35 A; Z — 0.20 A, 0.30 A, 0.40 A. Which of the components are ohmic, and why?

The current doubles exactly when the resistance stays the same: an ohmic conductor, such as a metal wire kept at constant temperature.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Heat a metal wire and its resistance rises. Heat a thermistor and it falls. Why the difference?

Make a guess: Heat a metal wire and its resistance rises. Heat a thermistor and it falls. Why the difference?

Mission 3

The semiconductor diode

  • A diode lets current through in one direction only.
  • Forward bias: almost no current until the pd reaches the threshold (≈ 0.6 V for silicon). Then the current rises steeply.
  • Reverse bias: a tiny current: very high resistance.
  • Resistance = V/I at each point: very high below threshold, low above it.
  • LED: the same shape, with a higher threshold (≈ 1.5–3 V, depending on colour). It emits light when it conducts.
  • Measuring a diode or LED characteristic: add a protective resistor in series and use a milliammeter.
Quick check
?The pd across a forward-biased silicon diode is raised from 0.2 V to 0.8 V. What happens to its resistance?
Mission 3

The NTC thermistor

  • NTC: negative temperature coefficient. Temperature up → resistance down.
  • R–T graph: falls steeply at low temperatures, then less steeply. Not a straight line.
  • I–V graph: curves upwards (towards the I axis, I vertical). A larger current heats it, R falls, so I rises faster than V.
  • Use: temperature sensor, e.g. in a potential divider.
Mission 3

Metal or thermistor: why opposite?

  • Two effects of warming:
    • ions vibrate more → more collisions → R up;
    • more charge carriers freed → R down.
  • Metal: carrier number (n) hardly changes. Collisions win → R rises (roughly in proportion to temperature rise).
  • NTC thermistor (semiconductor): n rises hugely. Carriers win → R falls.
  • In I = Anev terms: n grows far faster than v falls.
  • Semiconductors in general: resistivity falls as temperature rises.
Quick check
?A copper wire has a fixed pd across it. As it warms up, the current falls. A student explains: "Warming reduces the number of free electrons in the copper." Which correction is right?
Mission 3

The LDR

  • LDR: light-dependent resistor, made of a semiconductor.
  • Light intensity up → resistance down: very high in the dark, much lower in bright light.
  • Why: absorbed light (photons) frees more conduction electrons. n rises, so R falls.
  • Use: light sensors, e.g. switching street lights.
Quick check
?With a fixed pd across an LDR, the current is 0.20 mA in the dark and 10 mA in bright light. Assume the carriers' drift velocity is about the same in both. By what factor has the number of charge carriers per unit volume changed?
Mission check

?An NTC thermistor senses the temperature inside a fridge. After the door is closed, the thermistor cools from 15 °C to 5 °C. What happens to its resistance, and why?

Temperature changes both the ions' vibration and the number of charge carriers. In a metal the vibration wins; in a thermistor the carrier number wins, so its resistance moves the opposite way.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Stretch a metal wire to twice its length. What happens to its resistance?

Make a guess: Stretch a metal wire to twice its length. What happens to its resistance?

Mission 4

Resistivity

  • Longer wire → more collisions in the way: R ∝ length.
  • Thicker wire → more paths side by side: R ∝ 1/A.
  • R = ρL/A.
  • Resistivity ρ depends only on the material (and its temperature), not on its shape.
  • Unit: Ω m.
  • Copper: 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ Ω m. Constantan: 4.9 × 10⁻⁷ Ω m.
Quick check
?A uniform copper wire is cut into two pieces of equal length. How do the resistance and the resistivity of one piece compare with those of the original wire?
Mission 4

Worked example

  • Constantan wire: length 0.80 m, diameter 0.30 mm, ρ = 4.9 × 10⁻⁷ Ω m. Find R.
  • Radius = 0.15 mm = 1.5 × 10⁻⁴ m. Halve the diameter first.
  • A = πr² = π × (1.5 × 10⁻⁴)² = 7.07 × 10⁻⁸ m².
  • R = ρ × length ÷ A = (4.9 × 10⁻⁷ × 0.80) ÷ (7.07 × 10⁻⁸) = 5.5 Ω.
  • Trap: 1 mm² = 10⁻⁶ m², not 10⁻³ m².
  • Stretch a wire to twice the length: its volume is unchanged, so A halves. The resistance becomes 2 ÷ ½ = 4 times as large.
Quick check
?A copper cable has length 5.0 m and diameter 1.0 mm. The resistivity of copper is 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ Ω m. What is the resistance of the cable?
Mission 4

Resistance against length

  • Same wire, different lengths: R against length is a straight line through the origin.
  • Gradient = R ÷ length = ρ/A.
  • So ρ = gradient × A.
  • Unit check: Ω m⁻¹ × m² = Ω m.
Quick check
?A copper wire is connected across an ideal battery. The wire is then cooled from 60 °C to 20 °C; its length and cross-sectional area change by negligible amounts. How do the wire's resistance and the current in it change as it cools?
Mission check

?Wire X has resistance R and is made of a material of resistivity ρ. Wire Y is made of the same material at the same temperature, but it is half as long as X and has half the diameter of X. What are the resistance and the resistivity of wire Y?

Stretching a wire to twice its length keeps its volume, so its area halves. R = ρ(2L)/(A/2) = 4 × the resistance, with the same resistivity.

Mission 5

Mission 5: A ruler, a micrometer, an ammeter and a voltmeter. How can they tell you what a wire is made of?

Make a guess: A ruler, a micrometer, an ammeter and a voltmeter. How can they tell you what a wire is made of?

Mission 5

Measuring resistivity

  • Wire taped along a metre rule. A sliding crocodile clip sets the length.
  • Micrometer: measure the diameter at several points, turning the wire each time. Use the mean d.
  • A = πd²/4.
  • Ammeter in series, voltmeter across that length: R = V/I.
  • Repeat for at least six lengths.
Mission 5

From readings to ρ

  • Plot R against length. Gradient = ρ/A, so ρ = gradient × A.
  • Contact resistance at the clips shifts the line up. It does not change the gradient.
  • Keep the current small and switch off between readings: a warm wire has a larger ρ.
  • Check the micrometer for zero error.
  • Example: gradient 4.3 Ω m⁻¹, d = 0.38 mm → A = 1.13 × 10⁻⁷ m² → ρ = 4.9 × 10⁻⁷ Ω m.
Quick check
?A student plots R against length for a uniform wire of diameter 0.40 mm. The best-fit line has a gradient of 4.0 Ω m⁻¹ and crosses the R axis at 0.30 Ω. What is the resistivity of the wire?
Quick check
?A wire has a resistance of 4.0 Ω per metre, and the clips add a constant 0.50 Ω to every reading. A student finds the resistivity from a single reading at a length of 0.50 m, rather than from the gradient of a graph. How does her value compare with the true value?
Quick check
?A copper wire and a sample of pure silicon, a semiconductor, are both cooled from 20 °C to −20 °C. How does the resistance of each change, and why?
Mission check

?An unlabelled wire has a mean diameter of 0.32 mm. Its resistance between the clips is 1.72 Ω at a length of 0.20 m and 5.37 Ω at 0.80 m. Resistivities: copper 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ Ω m; iron 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ Ω m; constantan 4.9 × 10⁻⁷ Ω m; nichrome 1.1 × 10⁻⁶ Ω m. Which metal is the wire most likely made of?

Resistance, length and diameter give the resistivity, a property of the material, not of the wire. Use the gradient of R against length, so contact resistance drops out, then match ρ to a table.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Copper can conduct a million billion billion times better than plastic. What makes such a huge difference?

Make a guess: Copper can conduct a million billion billion times better than plastic. What makes such a huge difference?

Mission 6

Charge carriers

  • A current is a flow of charge carriers: charged particles that are free to move.
  • Metals: free (delocalised) electrons. Salt solutions: positive and negative ions.
  • Number density n: the number of charge carriers per m³.
  • Conductor (copper): n ≈ 10²⁹ m⁻³. Semiconductor (pure silicon, room temperature): n ≈ 10¹⁶ m⁻³. Insulator: far fewer still.
Quick check
?Solid sodium chloride (salt) is an insulator. When it is melted, it conducts. Which statement explains this?
Mission 6

Why resistivities differ so much

  • I = Anev: current depends on the number density n.
  • Same wire size and same drift velocity v: current ∝ n. Fewer carriers, less current for a given p.d.: higher resistivity.
  • Resistivity at room temperature (Ω m): copper 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ · pure silicon about 10³ · glass 10¹⁰ to 10¹⁴ · polythene about 10¹⁶.
  • Copper to polythene: about 24 orders of magnitude.
Mission 6

Worked example: drift velocity

  • Copper wire, cross-section 1.0 mm² = 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ m², current 1.0 A, n = 8.5 × 10²⁸ m⁻³.
  • v = I/(nAe) = 1.0 ÷ (8.5 × 10²⁸ × 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹).
  • v = 7.4 × 10⁻⁵ m s⁻¹: under a tenth of a millimetre per second.
  • So many carriers that a slow drift still gives 1 A.
Quick check
?Metal wire X has n = 8.0 × 10²⁸ m⁻³. Semiconductor rod Y has n = 4.0 × 10²² m⁻³. They have the same cross-sectional area and carry the same current. How does the drift velocity of the carriers in Y compare with that in X?
Mission check

?At room temperature, pure germanium has a resistivity roughly 10⁷ times that of aluminium. What is the main reason?

What matters is not how many charged particles a material has, but how many are free to move. Copper has about 10²⁹ per m³; plastic has almost none.

Mission 7

Mission 7: A kettle's element gets very hot, yet the cable carrying the same current stays cool. Why?

Make a guess: A kettle's element gets very hot, yet the cable carrying the same current stays cool. Why?

Mission 7

Power: energy per second

  • Power = rate of energy transfer. 1 W = 1 J s⁻¹.
  • pd = joules per coulomb. Current = coulombs per second.
  • Multiply: joules per second.
  • P = IV.
Mission 7

Three forms of one law

  • Use V = IR: P = IV = I²R = V²/R.
  • Same current (in series): use I²R. Larger R → more power.
  • Same pd (in parallel): use V²/R. Smaller R → more power.
Quick check
?The potential difference across an ohmic resistor is increased so that the current in the resistor becomes three times as large. What is the ratio of the new power dissipated in the resistor, P2, to the original power, P1?
Mission 7

Energy transferred

  • W = VIt.
  • A heater on 12 V takes 3.0 A for 5.0 minutes.
  • P = 12 × 3.0 = 36 W. t = 5.0 × 60 = 300 s.
  • Energy = 36 × 300 = 1.1 × 10⁴ J.
  • Check: R = 12 ÷ 3.0 = 4.0 Ω; I²R = 3.0² × 4.0 = 36 W.
Quick check
?A steady current of 0.25 A flows in a 24 Ω resistor for 2.0 minutes. How much energy is dissipated in the resistor?
Mission 7

The heating effect

  • Electrons collide with the lattice ions and pass on energy. The ions vibrate more: the resistor heats up.
  • Kettle element and its cable are in series: the same current.
  • P = I²R: the element's resistance is far larger, so nearly all the power is dissipated there.
  • The cable's resistance is tiny: very little heating.
  • Wanted heating: kettles, heaters. Unwanted: cables, chargers.
Mission 7

The kilowatt-hour

  • 1 kWh: energy transferred by 1 kW in 1 hour.
  • 1 kWh = 1000 W × 3600 s = 3.6 × 10⁶ J.
  • Energy (kWh) = power (kW) × time (h).
  • A 2.0 kW heater for 3.0 h: 6.0 kWh = 2.2 × 10⁷ J.
  • Cost = energy in kWh × price per kWh. At 25p per kWh: 6.0 × 25p = £1.50.
Quick check
?A 3.0 kW kettle is switched on for 4.0 minutes. How much energy does it transfer, in kWh?
Mission check

?A 60 W lamp and a 100 W lamp are both rated for a 230 V supply. They are connected in series across a 230 V supply. Assume each keeps its normal working resistance. Which lamp is brighter?

Same potential difference: the smaller resistance takes more power. Same current, as in the kettle and its cable: the larger resistance takes more power (P = I²R).

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Potential difference = energy transferred per unit charge; 1 V = 1 J C⁻¹.
  • V = W/Q.
  • emf: energy given to the charge per unit charge, in the source; pd: energy given up per unit charge, in components.
  • 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
  • Accelerated from rest: eV = ½mv².

Mission 2

  • R = V/I, in ohms (1 Ω = 1 V A⁻¹). Ohm's law: I ∝ V at constant temperature.
  • Resistance comes from collisions of free electrons with vibrating lattice ions, which also heat the conductor.
  • I–V practical: ammeter in series, voltmeter in parallel, small currents to keep a wire at constant temperature.
  • Filament lamp: R rises as it heats; find R from V/I, not from a tangent.

Mission 3

  • Diode: current in one direction only, after a threshold of about 0.6 V (silicon).
  • Warming: metal R rises (more collisions); NTC thermistor R falls (many more carriers).
  • LDR: brighter light frees more conduction electrons, so R falls.

Mission 4

  • R = ρL/A: resistivity is set by the material and temperature; length and area set the resistance.
  • Hotter metal: ions vibrate more, more collisions, more resistance.

Mission 5

  • Measure ρ: mean diameter by micrometer, R against length, ρ = gradient × A.
  • Heating a metal raises R (more ion vibration); heating an NTC thermistor lowers R (many more carriers).

Mission 6

  • Current needs mobile charge carriers: conductors have many, insulators almost none.
  • I = Anev: n ranges from about 10²⁹ m⁻³ (metals) to about 10¹⁶ m⁻³ (pure silicon), so resistivities span many orders of magnitude.

Mission 7

  • P = IV = I²R = V²/R; energy = IVt. Same current: larger R, more power. Same pd: smaller R, more power.
  • 1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J.
