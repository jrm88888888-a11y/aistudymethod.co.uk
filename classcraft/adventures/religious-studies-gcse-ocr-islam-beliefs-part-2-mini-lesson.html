Mission 1

Mission 1: Muslims believe the Qur'an is God's own words. How does that change the way it is read and kept?

Make a guess: Muslims believe the Qur'an is God's own words. How does that change the way it is read and kept?

Mission 1

What the Qur'an is

  • Qur'an means "recitation": it is meant to be heard and recited aloud.
  • Muslims believe it is the word of God (Allah), in Arabic, revealed through the angel Jibril to the Prophet Muhammad from 610 to 632 CE.
  • It is the last of the revealed books (Kutub), and Muslims believe it is the only one kept exactly as revealed.
  • 114 surahs (chapters) made of ayat (verses; the word also means "signs").
  • After the short opening surah, al-Fatihah, surahs run roughly from longest to shortest, not in the order they were revealed.
  • Makkan surahs stress belief: one God, prophets, judgement. Madinan surahs add guidance for the community: worship, family, law.
Mission 1Read the source

Not his own words

> "Nor doth he speak of (his own) desire. It is naught save an inspiration that is inspired" (Surah 53:3–4, Pickthall)

  • The first words revealed were "Read: In the name of thy Lord Who createth" (Surah 96:1, Pickthall), in the cave of Hira'.

Q1 According to 53:3–4, where do the Prophet's words in the Qur'an come from?

Show answer

Not from his own wishes: from God's inspiration.

Q2 Why does this matter for the Qur'an's authority?

Show answer

If the words are God's, they carry God's authority, not a human teacher's.

Mission 1

The Night of Power and a gradual revelation

  • Muslims believe the revelation began on Laylat al-Qadr, the Night of Power, in Ramadan: "Lo! We revealed it on the Night of Predestination." (Surah 97:1, Pickthall)
  • "The Night of Power is better than a thousand months." (Surah 97:3, Pickthall)
  • After that night, it came in parts over about 23 years, often in answer to events, to strengthen the Prophet's heart (Surah 25:32).
  • The Prophet recited each revelation; companions memorised it and scribes wrote it down on parchment, palm leaves and bone.

Mission 1

Why the Qur'an has supreme authority

  • God's own words: for Muslims, not a book about God, but God speaking. It is the highest source of guidance and of Shari'ah law.
  • Unchanged: Muslims believe it is preserved exactly (15:9): the final, undistorted message.
  • Beyond imitation: "though mankind and the jinn should assemble to produce the like of this Qur'an, they could not" (Surah 17:88, Pickthall). Muslims see its language as a miracle (i'jaz).
  • In Arabic: "a Lecture in Arabic, that ye may understand" (Surah 12:2, Pickthall). A translation is treated as an interpretation of the meaning, not the Qur'an itself.
  • Complete: it confirms the earlier books and needs no addition.

Mission 1

Understanding the Qur'an: interpretation

  • Tafsir: explanation of the Qur'an by scholars, using Arabic, the Hadith, and the occasions on which verses were revealed.
  • The Prophet's Sunnah explains it: the Qur'an commands prayer; the Sunnah shows how (Surah 16:44).
  • The Qur'an says some verses are clear, "the substance of the Book", and others "allegorical" (Surah 3:7, Pickthall).
  • Different approaches: some Muslims keep closely to the literal meaning and the early scholars; others look for the principles behind a verse (justice, mercy) and apply them to new situations.
  • Shi'a Muslims: the Imams of the Prophet's family are its most trusted interpreters. Sufis: look also for inner, spiritual meanings.
  • All agree the text is God's; they differ on how to read it.
Mission 1

The Qur'an in daily life

  • Formal worship: al-Fatihah is recited in every unit (rak'ah) of salah; the Friday sermon is built on the Qur'an.
  • Informal worship: personal reading and recitation; verses used in du'a (personal prayer) and for comfort; many learn it by heart and become a hafiz.
  • Home: read daily by many families; recited at a birth, a marriage and a death; verses in calligraphy on walls.
  • Mosque: children learn to read and recite it at madrasah; in Ramadan many Sunni mosques recite the whole Qur'an over the nights of tarawih prayer.
  • Guidance: for decisions about family, money, food and conduct; with the Sunnah, the basis of Shari'ah.
Mission 1

Treated with respect

  • Many Muslims make wudu (washing) before touching the Arabic Qur'an. They point to "Which none toucheth save the purified" (Surah 56:79, Pickthall). Some scholars read this verse as about angels and the heavenly book; most still see wudu as the respectful way.
  • It is kept on a high shelf, often wrapped in cloth, with nothing placed on top.
  • It is read on a stand (rahl), not on the floor; it is not taken into a bathroom.
  • Recitation begins by seeking God's protection from Satan (Surah 16:98).
  • Worn-out copies are not thrown away: they are buried or respectfully disposed of.
  • Many scholars apply these rules to the Arabic text more strictly than to translations.

Mission 1Evaluate

"Reciting the Qur'an in Arabic matters more than understanding it."

  • For: the Arabic words are God's own; translation loses meaning and beauty. Recitation is itself worship (Surah 73:4: "chant the Qur'an in measure", Pickthall). One Arabic Qur'an unites Muslims everywhere.
  • Against: it was revealed "that ye may understand" (12:2). "Will they then not meditate on the Qur'an" (47:24). Guidance only guides if it is understood; it is meant to change how people live.
  • Another view: most Muslim scholars say both matter: recite in Arabic and learn its meaning, through study, tafsir and translation.
  • Judge it by: What is the Qur'an for: worship, guidance, or both? Can a translation carry God's words? What do Muslims in different communities actually do?

Mission 2: Muslims believe angels never disobey God. Why would that change how a Muslim lives?

Make a guess: Muslims believe angels never disobey God. Why would that change how a Muslim lives?

Mission 2

What angels are

  • Belief in angels (Mala'ikah; Edexcel: Malaikah) is an article of faith. The Arabic word means "messengers".
  • A hadith teaches that angels were created from light (Sahih Muslim, paraphrase); humans from clay; the jinn from smokeless fire.
  • They are usually unseen, but can take human form: an angel "appeared before her as a man in all respects" to Maryam (19:17, Yusuf Ali).
  • Allah "made the angels, messengers with wings, two, or three, or four (pairs)" (35:1, Yusuf Ali). OCR also describes angels as Allah's first creation and immortal.
  • They are not divine: they are servants who worship Allah and are never worshipped themselves.
Mission 2

Free will: angels, jinn and humans

  • Muslims believe angels have no free will: they always obey. "They act (in all things) by His Command." (21:27, Yusuf Ali)
  • Angels over the Fire "flinch not (from executing) the Commands they receive from Allah, but do (precisely) what they are commanded" (66:6, Yusuf Ali).
  • So angels are sinless.
  • Humans and jinn have free will: they can obey or disobey, and will be judged.
  • Iblis (Satan) refused to bow to Adam. The Qur'an says "He was one of the Jinns" (18:50), which is why he could choose to disobey.

Mission 2

Jibril, the angel of revelation

  • Muslims believe Jibril (Gabriel) is the chief angel and Allah's messenger to the prophets.
  • He brought the Qur'an to Muhammad over about 23 years, beginning with "Read!" in the cave of Hira (96:1).
  • The Qur'an also calls him "the holy Spirit": "The holy Spirit hath delivered it from thy Lord with truth" (16:102, Pickthall).
  • He told Maryam she would have a son, Isa (19:17–21).
  • In the Hadith of Jibril he appears as a man and asks the Prophet about Islam, iman and ihsan, to teach the companions.

Mission 2

Mika'il, the angel of provision

  • Mika'il (Michael) is named in the Qur'an once, beside Jibril (2:98).
  • Muslim tradition teaches that Allah placed him in charge of rain and plants: the provision (rizq) that keeps creation alive.
  • OCR's specification also calls him the guardian of heaven and bringer of thunder, who rewards the righteous (these roles come from tradition, not the Qur'an).
  • Significance: every harvest and rainfall reminds Muslims that provision comes from Allah, which encourages gratitude and sharing (e.g. zakah).
  • He shows Allah's beneficence at work in the world.
Mission 2

Izra'il and Israfil

  • Izra'il, the Angel of Death: "The Angel of Death, put in charge of you, will (duly) take your souls: then shall ye be brought back to your Lord." (32:11, Yusuf Ali)
  • Muslims believe he takes the soul at the time Allah has set: gently for the righteous, harshly for wrongdoers (hadith, paraphrase).
  • OCR: he takes a person's final breath.
  • The name Izra'il comes from tradition; the Qur'an says "the Angel of Death".
  • Israfil will blow the trumpet (sur) to end the world and then to raise the dead: "a second one be sounded, when, behold, they will be standing and looking on!" (39:68, Yusuf Ali)
  • Significance: death is not chance but Allah's decree; Muslims prepare for it by living well.

Mission 2

Recording and guardian angels

  • Muslims believe two recording angels (Kiraman Katibin, "honourable scribes") note every deed: "Kind and honourable,- Writing down (your deeds): They know (and understand) all that ye do." (82:11–12, Yusuf Ali)
  • "…one sitting on the right and one on the left. Not a word does he utter but there is a sentinel by him." (50:17–18): good deeds on the right, bad on the left.
  • The record is shown on the Day of Judgement (see Mission 4).
  • Guardian angels protect each person "by command of Allah" (13:11).
  • Angels guard the Fire: "We have set none but angels as Guardians of the Fire" (74:31, Yusuf Ali).
  • Angels also pray for the believers' forgiveness (40:7).
Mission 2

Why angels matter today

  • Accountability: if every word is recorded, honesty matters even when no one is watching.
  • Trust in revelation: an obedient angel brought the Qur'an, so it can be trusted.
  • Comfort: guardian angels and angels' prayers mean believers are not alone.
  • Prayer: at the end of salah, Muslims turn to the right and left saying "Peace be upon you"; many teachers say this also greets the angels.
  • Preparation for death: Muslims believe the Angel of Death comes at the time Allah has set.
  • Divergent views: Sunni and Shi'a Muslims agree on angels; some Muslims are cautious about stories not found in the Qur'an or strong hadith. A few modernist thinkers, e.g. Sayyid Ahmad Khan (1817–1898), read angels as God-given forces in nature and the mind; most scholars reject this.

Mission 2Evaluate

"Belief in angels makes little difference to how Muslims live today."

  • For: angels are unseen and rarely mentioned in daily life; the Five Pillars and the Qur'an seem to matter more. Belief in Allah's knowledge alone would make people accountable.
  • Against: belief in recording angels shapes behaviour (honesty, kindness); belief in Jibril underpins trust in the Qur'an; angels are an article of faith, named with Allah in 2:285 and 4:136.
  • Another view: angels matter mainly as reminders: they make Allah's knowledge, care and judgement feel close.
  • Judge it by: Would a Muslim's practice change if belief in angels were removed? Is the effect on behaviour or on belief? Does it differ between Muslims?

Mission 3: If God already knows everything you will do, are you still free, and is Judgement fair?

Make a guess: If God already knows everything you will do, are you still free, and is Judgement fair?

Mission 3

Al-Qadr: Allah's decree

  • Al-Qadr means "measure" or "decree". It is the sixth Sunni article of faith: predestination.
  • Allah knows and has decreed all that happens, good and bad. "Verily, all things have We created in proportion and measure." (54:49, Yusuf Ali)
  • "No misfortune can happen on earth or in your souls but is recorded in a decree before We bring it into existence." (57:22, Yusuf Ali)
  • A divine masterplan: "the command of Allah is a decree determined" (33:38, Yusuf Ali).
  • In Surah 12:42, Satan makes a freed prisoner forget Yusuf, who "lingered in prison a few (more) years": human forgetting, yet within Allah's plan, which later brings Yusuf to power.
Mission 3

Four parts of the decree

Many Sunni scholars explain al-Qadr in four parts:

1. Knowledge ('ilm): Allah knows everything, past, present and future.

2. Writing (kitabah): it is all written in the Preserved Tablet (al-Lawh al-Mahfuz, 85:22).

3. Will (mashi'ah): nothing happens unless Allah wills it.

4. Creation (khalq): Allah creates all things, including human actions.

  • OCR calls this sifat: Allah's attributes, here His foreknowledge.
Mission 3

Free and responsible

  • The Qur'an also teaches human freedom: "Let him who will believe, and let him who will, reject (it)" (18:29, Yusuf Ali); "We showed him the Way: whether he be grateful or ungrateful (rests on his will)" (76:3).
  • "Verily never will Allah change the condition of a people until they change it themselves (with their own souls)." (13:11)
  • Humans are Allah's khalifah (stewards), tested by their choices (67:2).
  • Knowing is not forcing: Allah knows what a person will choose, but the person still chooses.
  • So Muslims must still act. When a man asked whether to tie his camel or trust in Allah, the Prophet said: tie it, and trust (Sunan al-Tirmidhi, paraphrase).

Mission 3

Al-Qadr and the Day of Judgement

  • If Allah decrees everything, why judge? Muslims answer: people are judged on what they freely choose, which Allah knows but does not force.
  • Allah judges fairly: "On no soul doth Allah Place a burden greater than it can bear." (2:286)
  • People are judged on intentions as well as deeds: actions are judged by their intentions (Sahih al-Bukhari 1, paraphrase).
  • Those without the capacity to choose (young children, people without reason) are not held accountable in the same way (hadith, paraphrase).
  • Each person's deeds are written in a book of deeds: "Read thine (own) record" (17:14, Yusuf Ali) (see Mission 4).
  • So Judgement gives human freedom its weight: choices matter forever.
Mission 3Diversity

Ash'ari, Mu'tazili and Shi'a views

  • Ash'ari (most Sunnis; Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari, d. 936): Allah creates every act; the human acquires it (kasb) by choosing, and so is responsible. Stresses Allah's power.
  • Maturidi (many Sunnis): similar, but gives more room to genuine human choice.
  • Mu'tazili (8th–10th centuries): humans themselves bring about their free acts; a just God could not punish people for acts He made them do. Stresses Allah's justice.
  • Shi'a (Twelver): close to the Mu'tazili stress on 'Adl; Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq taught "neither compulsion nor complete delegation, but something between the two" (paraphrase).
  • OCR pairs Mu'tazilite with Shi'a and Ash'ari with Sunni thought: the Mu'tazila were not themselves Shi'a, but Shi'a theology took up much of their reasoning on justice.

Mission 3

Living with al-Qadr today

  • Trust (tawakkul): "Nothing will happen to us except what Allah has decreed for us: He is our protector." (9:51)
  • Insha'Allah ("if Allah wills") when making plans.
  • Patience (sabr) in hardship, and gratitude (shukr) in success: both come from Allah.
  • Du'a: a hadith says nothing turns back the decree except prayer (Sunan al-Tirmidhi, paraphrase): prayer is itself part of the decree.
  • Effort and responsibility: Muslims work, study and seek justice, knowing they will answer for their choices.
  • Hope: "it is possible that ye dislike a thing which is good for you … Allah knoweth, and ye know not" (2:216, Yusuf Ali).

Mission 3Evaluate

"If Allah has decided everything, it is unfair to judge people."

  • For: if every act is written and created by Allah, people seem not to be the true authors of their choices. Some early Muslim thinkers felt this force, and so stressed human freedom.
  • For: the thinkers who stressed human freedom in this way were the Mu'tazila.
  • Against: knowing is not causing; people experience real choice and are judged only on what they could bear (2:286); intentions count; Allah is never unjust (4:40).
  • Against: Ash'ari kasb keeps both Allah's power and human responsibility.
  • Another view: some Muslims say the relationship is a mystery beyond human understanding: the Qur'an affirms both, and that is enough.
  • Judge it by: Is foreknowledge the same as control? Does feeling free mean being free? Should faith accept what reason cannot fully explain?
Mission check

?Hassan's small shop closes even though he worked hard and dealt honestly. One relative says: "It was written, so nothing you did mattered." Another says: "God has nothing to do with business; it was all down to you." What would most Sunni Muslims say?

For most Muslims, Allah's decree and human choice are not rivals: Allah knows and wills all things, and people still choose, and are judged fairly.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Muslims believe you live only once. What happens after death, and why does it matter now?

Make a guess: Muslims believe you live only once. What happens after death, and why does it matter now?

Mission 4

Akhirah: one life, then the hereafter

  • Akhirah means "the hereafter" or "the last": life after death. It is an article of faith.
  • Muslims believe each person has one life; there is no rebirth. "Every soul shall have a taste of death: And only on the Day of Judgment shall you be paid your full recompense." (3:185, Yusuf Ali)
  • This life is a test and a preparation (2:155; 67:2): what a person believes and does now shapes eternity.
  • So the purpose of life is to worship Allah and live as He asks.
Mission 4

Death and barzakh

  • Muslims believe that, at the time Allah has set, the Angel of Death (Izra'il) takes the soul (32:11).
  • The soul enters barzakh, "barrier": the state between death and the Day of Resurrection. "Before them is a Partition till the Day they are raised up." (23:100, Yusuf Ali)
  • There is no going back: in 23:100 a wrongdoer begs to return and do good, and is refused.
  • Hadith teach that two angels, Munkar and Nakir, question the dead in the grave: Who is your Lord? What is your religion? Who is your prophet?
  • The grave becomes a foretaste: wide and peaceful for the faithful, narrow and painful for wrongdoers (hadith, paraphrase).
Mission 4

The Day of Judgement

  • Muslims believe Yawm al-Qiyamah (Day of Resurrection) begins when Israfil sounds the trumpet (39:68).
  • All the dead are raised, body and soul, and gathered before Allah.
  • Each person receives their book of deeds: in the right hand for the saved (69:19), the left for the lost (69:25).
  • Deeds are weighed on the scales (mizan): "We shall set up scales of justice … not a soul will be dealt with unjustly in the least" (21:47, Yusuf Ali).
  • Many Muslims believe all must cross a bridge, as-Sirat, over hell to reach Paradise (hadith).
  • Muslims believe the Prophet will intercede (shafa'ah) for believers, by Allah's permission; Shi'a Muslims believe the Imams will also intercede.

"They say: 'What! when we are reduced to bones and dust, should we really be raised up (to be) a new creation?'" (17:49, Yusuf Ali) … "Then will they say: 'Who will cause us to return?' Say: 'He who created you first!'" (17:51, Yusuf Ali)

Mission 4

Responsible and accountable

  • Responsibility: humans have free will and know right from wrong, through the Qur'an and the prophets.
  • Accountability: every deed counts. "Anyone who has done an atom's weight of good, see it! And anyone who has done an atom's weight of evil, shall see it." (99:7–8, Yusuf Ali)
  • Each person answers for themselves: "no bearer of burdens can bear the burden of another" (6:164).
  • Allah is generous: a good deed is rewarded "ten times" (6:160), and no one should despair of His mercy (39:53).
  • A hadith says no one enters Paradise by deeds alone, but by Allah's mercy (Sahih al-Bukhari, paraphrase).
  • Stewardship: as khalifah, Muslims will answer for how they treated the earth, its creatures and their wealth.
Mission 4

Al-Jannah, Paradise

  • Jannah ("garden"): the reward for faith and good deeds.
  • Described as gardens "under which rivers flow", with "beautiful mansions" (9:72); rivers of water, milk and honey (47:15).
  • Yet "no person knows what delights of the eye are kept hidden (in reserve) for them" (32:17, Yusuf Ali).
  • The greatest joy is not physical: "the greatest bliss is the good pleasure of Allah" (9:72, Yusuf Ali).
Mission 4

Jahannam, hell

  • Muslims believe Jahannam is a place of fire and suffering, and of separation from Allah's mercy.
  • "To it are seven gates: for each of those gates is a (special) class (of sinners) assigned." (15:44, Yusuf Ali)
  • Many scholars teach that believers who sinned may be purified in hell and then leave, by Allah's mercy and the Prophet's intercession; those who knowingly rejected Allah remain.
  • Only Allah knows who will be where: Muslims are taught not to declare others destined for hell.

Mission 4Diversity

Literal or metaphorical?

  • Most Muslims: heaven and hell are real and the resurrection is bodily, though their nature is beyond imagining (32:17).
  • Al-Ghazali (1058–1111) argued strongly that denying bodily resurrection goes against the Qur'an.
  • Some Muslim philosophers, Sufis and modern thinkers read the imagery as symbols of spiritual states. In Yusuf Ali's translation the Qur'an itself calls the description "a Parable of the Garden" (47:15); the Arabic word (mathal) can mean "likeness" or "description".
  • Muhammad Iqbal (1877–1938) wrote that heaven and hell "are states, not localities" (The Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam, 1930).
  • Effect on life: literal readers find in vivid images a strong motive for good deeds; metaphorical readers stress closeness to, or distance from, Allah as the heart of reward and punishment. Both hold that accountability is real.
Mission 4

Martyrdom

  • A shahid (martyr; literally "witness") is someone who dies for Allah's cause.
  • "Think not of those who are slain in Allah's way as dead. Nay, they live, finding their sustenance in the presence of their Lord" (3:169, Yusuf Ali).
  • Hadith widen the idea: those who die of plague, drowning or in childbirth may also be counted as martyrs (paraphrase).
  • Shi'a Muslims remember Husayn, killed at Karbala (680 CE), as the model martyr for justice.
  • Mainstream scholars teach that suicide ("nor kill … yourselves", 4:29) and killing non-combatants are forbidden, so such acts are not martyrdom.
Mission 4Evaluate

"Belief in akhirah is the most important influence on how a Muslim lives."

  • For: every deed is recorded and weighed (99:7–8), so akhirah gives each choice eternal weight. It motivates prayer, charity, honesty and patience in suffering. The Qur'an returns to it constantly.
  • Against: Tawhid and love of Allah come first: many Muslims say they obey Allah because He is worthy of worship, not for reward. The Prophet's example (Sunnah) and the ummah shape daily life directly. Acting only from fear or hope of reward can be seen as a lesser motive.
  • Another view: the beliefs work together: Tawhid gives the reason, the Sunnah the pattern, akhirah the urgency.
  • Judge it by: What do Muslims themselves say motivates them? Is acting for reward a weaker motive? Which belief would change practice most if removed?
Mission check

For Muslims, one life, then barzakh, resurrection and judgement: Allah's mercy is real, and so is every deed.

Mission 5

Created with a purpose

  • Muslims believe Allah created everything and keeps it in being; nothing exists by chance.
  • The ordered universe is full of signs (ayat) pointing to its Creator.
  • Humans are honoured above much of creation: "Verily we have honoured the Children of Adam" (Qur'an 17:70, Pickthall).
  • Their purpose: "I created the jinn and humankind only that they might worship Me." (Qur'an 51:56, Pickthall)
  • Worship (ibadah) here means all of life lived for Allah, including how people treat the world.
Mission 5

Adam: first human, first prophet, first khalifah

  • Muslims believe, from the Qur'an's account, that Allah formed Adam from clay and breathed into him of His spirit (15:29).
  • He taught Adam "all the names" (2:31): knowledge and reason, which set humans apart.
  • The angels bowed to Adam; Iblis, a jinn, refused out of pride (2:34, 18:50).
  • Adam and his wife ate from the forbidden tree, repented and were forgiven (7:23, 2:37). Muslims believe there is no original sin: every child is born sinless.
  • First prophet: Allah "chose him, and relented toward him, and guided him" (Qur'an 20:122, Pickthall). Adam received guidance and taught his children to worship Allah alone.
  • First khalifah: placed on Earth as Allah's khalifah: a deputy or steward who looks after it on Allah's behalf.

Mission 5

Khalifah: stewards of the Earth

  • Muslims believe the Earth belongs to Allah, not to humans, and that humans hold it in trust (amanah): "We offered the trust unto the heavens and the earth and the hills, but they shrank from bearing it ... And man assumed it." (Qur'an 33:72, Pickthall)
  • Balance (mizan): Allah "hath set the measure, That ye exceed not the measure" (Qur'an 55:7–8, Pickthall). Pollution and waste upset Allah's balance.
  • "Eat and drink, but be not prodigal" (Qur'an 7:31, Pickthall): no waste.
  • The Prophet taught that planting a tree that feeds people or animals counts as charity, and to plant a sapling even if the end of the world is coming (hadiths, paraphrase).
  • Accountability: every khalifah will answer to Allah on the Day of Judgement for how they used the Earth.
  • So stewardship is part of Akhirah: this life is preparation for the next.

Life as a test

"Be sure we shall test you with something of fear and hunger, some loss in goods or lives or the fruits (of your toil), but give glad tidings to those who patiently persevere" (Qur'an 2:155, Yusuf Ali)

  • Allah "created life and death that He may try you which of you is best in conduct" (Qur'an 67:2, Pickthall).
  • Suffering can test patience (sabr) and faith, purify from sin, and build compassion in others. Those afflicted say: "Lo! we are Allah's and lo! unto Him we are returning." (2:156, Pickthall)
  • Much evil comes from human free will misused; Allah's wisdom is greater than ours (2:216).
  • 17:49–51: those who doubt ask who could raise dry bones; the answer: "He Who created you at the first" (Pickthall). Surah 101: on the Day of Calamity, those whose scales are heavy with good deeds are blessed, those whose scales are light are lost (paraphrase).

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Muslims believe the Qur'an is God's word in Arabic, revealed through Jibril to Muhammad from 610 to 632, beginning on the Night of Power.
  • For Muslims it has supreme authority; it shapes worship, home life and moral choices; it is handled with special respect.

Mission 2

  • Muslims believe angels are created from light, unseen, sinless servants of Allah with no free will; humans and jinn (including Iblis) have free will.
  • In Muslim belief, Jibril brought the Qur'an; Mika'il is linked with rain and provision; recording angels note every deed.
  • Muslims believe Izra'il takes souls at death (32:11); Israfil will blow the trumpet for the Resurrection.

Mission 3

  • Al-Qadr: Allah knows, records, wills and creates all things; humans still choose freely and are responsible.
  • On the Day of Judgement people answer for their free choices and intentions; Allah never burdens a soul beyond its capacity.
  • Ash'ari (kasb) stresses Allah's power; the Mu'tazila and Twelver Shi'a stress His justice and human freedom.

Mission 4

  • In Muslim belief, Akhirah means one life, then death, barzakh, resurrection and judgement; this life is a test and a preparation.
  • Muslims believe every deed is recorded and weighed; Allah is just and merciful; Jannah rewards, Jahannam brings justice.
  • Most Muslims read heaven and hell literally; some read the imagery as symbols of spiritual states.

Mission 5

  • Muslims believe Allah created everything with purpose; humans are honoured, and placed on Earth as khalifah (2:30), holding it in trust (amanah).
  • In Muslim belief, Adam was the first human, first prophet and first khalifah; he repented and was forgiven, so there is no original sin.
