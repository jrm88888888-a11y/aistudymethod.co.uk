Mission 1

Mission 1: How can you tell from the outside how well a person's lungs are working?

Make a guess: How can you tell from the outside how well a person's lungs are working?

Mission 1

Lung volumes on a spirometer trace

  • A spirometer records the volume of air breathed in and out against time.
  • Tidal volume: volume in or out in one normal breath (about 0.5 dm³ at rest).
  • Inspiratory reserve volume: the extra you can breathe in after a normal breath in.
  • Expiratory reserve volume: the extra you can breathe out after a normal breath out.
  • Vital capacity = tidal volume + inspiratory reserve + expiratory reserve: the most you can breathe out after the deepest breath in.
  • Residual volume: air left after the hardest breath out. A spirometer cannot measure it.
Quick check
?A student's spirometer readings are: tidal volume 0.5 dm³, inspiratory reserve volume 3.0 dm³, expiratory reserve volume 1.2 dm³. What is the student's vital capacity?
Mission 1

What sets the breathing rate?

  • (1) Muscles respire faster → more CO2 enters the blood.
  • (2) CO2 forms carbonic acid → more H⁺ → blood pH falls.
  • (3) Chemoreceptors detect the fall in pH.
  • (3) These chemoreceptors are in the brainstem.
  • (4) More nerve impulses go to the diaphragm and intercostal muscles: breathing becomes faster and deeper.
  • (5) Extra CO2 is breathed out; pH returns towards normal: negative feedback.
  • Say: "rising CO2 (falling pH) drives faster breathing" — not: "breathing speeds up because the body detects low oxygen".
Quick check
?During a run, a student's blood carbon dioxide concentration rises, blood pH falls from 7.40 to 7.32 and ventilation rate rises from 12 to 30 breaths per minute. Which statement correctly links these changes?
Mission check

?At rest, an athlete's tidal volume is 0.6 dm³, inspiratory reserve volume 2.8 dm³ and expiratory reserve volume 1.4 dm³. During hard exercise her tidal volume rises to 2.2 dm³. Which statement is correct?

Lung volumes, breathing rate and disease data all answer one question: how much air reaches a large, thin, well-supplied surface.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Not one filament in a contracting muscle gets shorter. So how does the whole muscle shorten?

Make a guess: Not one filament in a contracting muscle gets shorter. So how does the whole muscle shorten?

Mission 2

From muscle to myofibril

  • Muscle → bundles → muscle fibres → myofibrils → sarcomeres.
  • A muscle fibre is very long and has many nuclei. It forms when many cells fuse.
  • Fibre membrane: sarcolemma. Fibre cytoplasm: sarcoplasm.
  • Inside each fibre:
    • Many myofibrils, packed side by side.
    • Many mitochondria: supply ATP.
    • Sarcoplasmic reticulum: stores calcium ions (Ca²⁺).
    • T-tubules: inward folds of the sarcolemma that carry impulses deep into the fibre.
Mission 2

The sarcomere

  • A sarcomere runs from one Z line to the next.
  • Thin actin filaments: attached to the Z lines.
  • Thick myosin filaments: in the middle, held at the M line.
  • Bands:
    • A band (dark): the full length of the myosin filaments, including overlap.
    • I band (light): actin only. Z line in its centre.
    • H zone: myosin only, in the centre of the A band.
  • Alternating dark and light bands: skeletal muscle is striated.
Mission 2

Sliding filaments

  • Frame 1 — Relaxed: little overlap; wide I band; wide H zone.
  • Frame 2 — Contracted: myosin heads pull actin towards the M line.
  • The filaments slide past each other. Neither gets shorter.
  • Z lines move closer → the sarcomere shortens → the whole muscle shortens.
  • I band narrows. H zone narrows, and may disappear. A band stays the same.
  • Say: "the filaments slide past each other" — not: "the filaments contract".
Quick check
?Electron micrographs of a sarcomere are taken before and after it contracts. Which set of changes is observed?
Mission 2

Read the data: overlap and force

  • Gordon, Huxley and Julian (1966) held single frog muscle fibres at fixed sarcomere lengths, stimulated them, and measured the force.
  • Maximum force: sarcomere length 2.0–2.25 µm.
  • Longer than 2.25 µm: force falls in a straight line, to zero at 3.65 µm.
  • Shorter than 2.0 µm: force also falls, as the filaments start to collide.
  • Why zero at 3.65 µm?
    • Myosin filament: 1.6 µm. Each actin filament (to the Z line): about 1.025 µm.
    • 1.6 + 1.025 + 1.025 = 3.65 µm: actin and myosin no longer overlap.
  • More overlap → more myosin heads reach actin → more force. Strong evidence for sliding filaments.
Quick check
?In the data on the earlier screen, a sarcomere held at 3.0 µm produces about 46% of the maximum force. Why is the force lower than at 2.2 µm?
Mission 2

The cross-bridge cycle

  • Frame 1 — An impulse spreads along the sarcolemma and down the T-tubules → the sarcoplasmic reticulum releases Ca²⁺ into the sarcoplasm.
  • Frame 2 — Ca²⁺ binds troponin; troponin changes shape and pulls tropomyosin off the binding sites on actin.
  • Frame 3 — A myosin head (carrying ADP + Pi) binds actin: a cross-bridge. The head pivots: the power stroke pulls actin towards the M line. ADP and Pi are released.
  • Frame 4 — A new ATP binds the head → the head detaches. ATPase on the head hydrolyses ATP → ADP + Pi; this re-cocks the head.
  • The cycle repeats while Ca²⁺ and ATP are present.
  • Relaxation: Ca²⁺ is actively transported back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum (uses ATP); binding sites are blocked again.
  • Say: "Ca²⁺ uncovers the binding sites; ATP detaches the head" — not: "Ca²⁺ powers the stroke".
Quick check
?A drug blocks the release of calcium ions from the sarcoplasmic reticulum of a skeletal muscle fibre. The motor neuron still stimulates the fibre normally. What is the effect on the fibre?
Mission 2

Muscles only pull

  • Muscle exerts force only by contracting. It can pull; it cannot push.
  • So muscles work in antagonistic pairs: one contracts, the other relaxes and is stretched.
  • Elbow: biceps contracts → arm bends. Triceps contracts → arm straightens.
  • Tendons: tough, inelastic cords joining muscle to bone. They pass the pull to the bone.
  • Say: "the triceps contracts to straighten the arm" — not: "the biceps pushes the arm straight".
Mission 2

Titin and the ribcage

  • Titin: an immense protein running from the Z line to the middle of the sarcomere.
  • It acts as a spring:
    • Sarcomere stretched → titin extends, storing potential energy → it recoils.
    • Near full stretch it becomes very stiff → prevents overstretching.
  • Titin recoil does not undo contraction; an antagonist must lengthen a shortened muscle.
  • Intercostal muscles: two layers whose fibres run in different directions.
    • External layer contracts → ribcage moves up and out (inhalation). Internal layer is stretched; its titin stores energy.
    • Internal layer contracts (forced exhalation) → ribcage moves down and in, stretching the external layer.
Quick check
?During inhalation the external intercostal muscles contract and move the ribcage up and outwards. What happens to the internal intercostal muscles at the same time, and why?
Mission check

?After death, cells stop making ATP. A few hours later the skeletal muscles become rigid and cannot be stretched (rigor mortis). Which explanation fits the sliding filament model?

The filaments slide; the heads must let go to slide again. No ATP, no letting go.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Some muscle works all your life without tiring; some tires in seconds. What makes the difference?

Make a guess: Some muscle works all your life without tiring; some tires in seconds. What makes the difference?

Mission 3

Three kinds of muscle

  • Skeletal muscle:
    • Striated. Long, unbranched fibres with many nuclei at the edge.
    • Voluntary: stimulated by motor neurones. Contracts fast; fatigues.
  • Cardiac muscle:
    • Striated. Short, branched cells, usually one central nucleus.
    • Joined end to end by intercalated discs: impulses pass from cell to cell.
    • Myogenic and involuntary. Does not fatigue.
  • Why cardiac muscle does not fatigue: its cells are packed with mitochondria and have a dense capillary supply, so aerobic respiration keeps ATP coming.
  • Both striated types contain myofibrils. Hypothesis for branching: one cardiac cell connects to several others, so contraction spreads through the heart wall in all directions.
Mission 3

Is a muscle fibre a cell?

  • Arguments that it is a cell:
    • One continuous plasma membrane around one cytoplasm.
    • It contracts as a single unit.
  • Arguments that it is not a typical cell:
    • Hundreds of nuclei.
    • Forms by fusion of many cells (myoblasts): a syncytium.
    • Can be several centimetres long.
  • Cardiac muscle cells, by contrast: short, branched, usually one nucleus.
  • No single answer: the case depends on which definition of a cell is used.
Quick check
?A striated muscle fibre has one continuous plasma membrane and cytoplasm and contains hundreds of nuclei. Which is the best assessment of whether it is a cell?
Mission 3

The neuromuscular junction

  • Where a motor neurone meets a muscle fibre.
  • Impulse arrives → Ca²⁺ enters the axon terminal → vesicles release acetylcholine (ACh) by exocytosis.
  • ACh diffuses across the cleft → binds receptors on the sarcolemma → Na⁺ enters → depolarisation.
  • An action potential spreads along the sarcolemma and down the T-tubules → Ca²⁺ released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum (see Mission 2).
  • Acetylcholinesterase in the cleft hydrolyses ACh, so each impulse gives one response.
  • The sarcolemma at the junction is folded: more area, more ACh receptors.
Mission 3

Motor units

  • Motor unit = one motor neuron + all the muscle fibres it supplies.
  • Its axon branches; each branch forms a neuromuscular junction with one fibre.
  • When the neuron fires, all its fibres contract together.
  • More force: recruit more motor units.
  • Small units (few fibres each) → force rises in small steps → fine control (e.g. eye muscles).
  • Large units (many fibres each) → big steps → power (e.g. calf muscles).
Quick check
?Illustrative data for two human muscles: a muscle that moves the eye (P) had about 20 000 fibres supplied by 2000 motor neurons, and a calf muscle (Q) had about 1 000 000 fibres supplied by 800 motor neurons. Which conclusion is supported by these data?
Mission check

?In some diseases, motor neurons die. Surviving motor neurons grow new axon branches to the muscle fibres that have lost their nerve supply. How does this change control of the muscle?

Fewer, bigger motor units: the same muscle, but with coarser steps of force.

Mission 4

Mission 4: An egg is a giant cell and a sperm is a tiny one. Why are the two cells built so differently?

Make a guess: An egg is a giant cell and a sperm is a tiny one. Why are the two cells built so differently?

Mission 4

Why have two parents at all?

  • Asexual: one parent, mitosis, genetically identical offspring.
  • Best when the parent is already adapted to a stable environment.
  • Sexual: meiosis + fertilisation give new gene combinations.
  • That variation is needed when the environment changes.
  • Say: "some offspring happen to suit the new conditions" — not: "variation is produced to meet a need".
Mission 4

The prime difference: one gamete travels

  • The male gamete travels to the female gamete. The egg stays put.
  • So the sperm is small, streamlined, with almost no food reserves.
  • The egg is large: its cytoplasm holds reserves for the embryo's first divisions.
  • Small and cheap → sperm made in vast numbers. Large → few eggs.
  • Both carry one haploid set of chromosomes. The size difference is cytoplasm, not DNA.
  • Human egg about 100 µm across; sperm head about 5 µm.
Mission 4

The male-typical system

  • Testis: makes sperm and testosterone. Held in the scrotum, below body temperature.
  • Epididymis: coiled tube on the testis; sperm mature and are stored here.
  • Sperm duct: carries sperm to the urethra.
  • Seminal vesicle and prostate gland: add fluid. Sperm + fluid = semen.
  • The seminal vesicle fluid contains fructose, a respiratory substrate for sperm.
  • Urethra: carries semen at ejaculation, urine at other times. Penis: erectile tissue.
  • Say: "seminal vesicles add fluid" — not: "seminal vesicles store sperm".
Mission 4

The female-typical system

  • Ovary: releases an egg each cycle (ovulation, around day 14); secretes oestrogen and progesterone.
  • Oviduct (fallopian tube): cilia move the egg; fertilisation happens here.
  • Uterus: muscular wall; the embryo implants in its lining.
  • Endometrium: the inner lining. Thickens each cycle; shed at menstruation.
  • Cervix: the neck of the uterus. Vagina: receives sperm; the birth canal.
  • Say: "fertilisation in the oviduct" — not: "fertilisation in the uterus".

?A student annotates a diagram of the female reproductive system. Which annotation is correct?
Mission 4

Built for the job

  • Sperm acrosome: a sac of digestive enzymes in the head.
  • Sperm midpiece: packed with mitochondria → ATP for the flagellum (tail).
  • Sperm: haploid nucleus, little cytoplasm → small and fast.
  • Egg zona pellucida: a glycoprotein layer outside the membrane. Sperm bind to it.
  • Egg cortical granules: vesicles just under the membrane, ready to block extra sperm.
  • Egg cytoplasm: nutrient stores (yolk droplets) for the first divisions.
  • The cell released at ovulation is a secondary oocyte: meiosis is not yet finished (see Mission 5).
  • Say: "the zona pellucida is a glycoprotein layer" — not: "a cell wall".
Quick check
?Which pairing correctly matches an adaptation of a human gamete to its function?
Mission 4

Getting in: the acrosome reaction

  • (1) Many sperm reach the egg. One binds to the zona pellucida.
  • (2) Acrosome reaction: the acrosome releases its enzymes. They digest a path through the zona.
  • (3) The sperm's cell membrane fuses with the egg's cell membrane.
  • (4) The sperm nucleus enters the egg cytoplasm.
  • Say: "enzymes digest the zona pellucida" — not: "enzymes digest a hole in the egg membrane". Entry is by membrane fusion.
Mission 4

One sperm only: the cortical reaction

  • (5) Fusion triggers the cortical reaction.
  • Cortical granules fuse with the egg membrane and release enzymes into the zona (exocytosis).
  • The zona pellucida hardens; its sperm-binding sites are changed.
  • No further sperm can bind or pass through: polyspermy is blocked.
  • Say: "acrosome reaction lets one in; cortical reaction keeps the rest out" — not the reverse.
Quick check
?In an experiment, eggs treated so that they could not carry out the cortical reaction were each entered by several sperm. Which explanation is correct?
Mission 4

After entry

  • The sperm's tail and mitochondria usually enter too, but are destroyed. All the zygote's mitochondria come from the egg.
  • Sperm entry triggers the secondary oocyte to complete meiosis II. A second polar body forms.
  • Both nuclear membranes dissolve. All the condensed chromosomes join one mitosis.
  • That first mitosis makes two diploid nuclei: a two-cell embryo.
  • Say: "chromosomes from both gametes join a joint mitosis" — not: "the nuclei fuse into one nucleus".
Quick check
?Which event occurs during fertilisation in humans?
Mission check

?In a fish species the female lays a few hundred large eggs in a nest and the male releases many millions of tiny sperm over them. Which statement explains the difference in size and number?

One cell travels, the other supplies the embryo. Size, numbers and every special structure follow from that.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Meiosis in a testis gives four sperm, but meiosis in an ovary gives just one egg. Why?

Make a guess: Meiosis in a testis gives four sperm, but meiosis in an ovary gives just one egg. Why?

Mission 5

Unequal cytokinesis

  • Nuclear division shares the chromosomes equally. Always.
  • Cytokinesis (division of the cytoplasm) is usually equal, but not always.
  • Unequal example 1: making an egg. One big cell keeps almost all the cytoplasm; a tiny polar body gets almost none.
  • Unequal example 2: budding yeast. A small bud pinches off a large parent cell.
  • Each daughter cell must get at least one mitochondrion. Mitochondria form only by division of existing ones.
Quick check
?In human oogenesis, the first meiotic division produces a large secondary oocyte and a very small polar body. What does this unequal cytokinesis achieve?
Mission 5

Spermatogenesis

  • (1) Mitosis: spermatogonia (2n) divide, all through adult life.
  • (2) Growth: some enlarge into primary spermatocytes (2n).
  • (3) Meiosis I → two secondary spermatocytes (n).
  • (4) Meiosis II → four spermatids (n). Both divisions equal.
  • (5) Differentiation: each spermatid grows a flagellum and acrosome and loses most cytoplasm → spermatozoon.
  • Result: four sperm per meiosis; millions every day.
Mission 5

Oogenesis

  • Before birth: oogonia (2n) divide by mitosis, then grow into primary oocytes (2n).
  • Primary oocytes start meiosis I, then pause. Each sits in a follicle.
  • From puberty, each cycle: one primary oocyte completes meiosis I → secondary oocyte (n) + first polar body. Unequal.
  • The secondary oocyte starts meiosis II, pauses again, and is released at ovulation.
  • Meiosis II finishes only if a sperm enters → ovum + second polar body. Unequal again.
  • Differentiation: the oocyte builds up cytoplasm, nutrient stores and cortical granules; the follicle cells grow around it.
  • Polar bodies degenerate. Result: one egg per meiosis.

Mission 5

From zygote to blastocyst to implantation

  • (1) The zygote divides by mitosis while cilia move it down the oviduct.
  • (2) About day 5: a blastocyst. A hollow ball with a fluid-filled cavity.
  • Inside: the inner cell mass → the embryo (later the foetus).
  • Outside: an outer layer of cells → attaches to the uterus; later helps form the placenta.
  • (3) About day 7: the blastocyst sheds the zona pellucida, attaches to the endometrium and sinks into it: implantation.
  • Say: "the blastocyst implants" — not: "the zygote implants".
Quick check

Mission check

?Yeast can reproduce by budding. The nucleus divides by mitosis, and a small bud then pinches off a much larger parent cell. Which statement about the bud, as it separates, is correct?

Chromosomes are always shared equally; cytoplasm need not be. Give it all to one cell and you get one large egg, not four small ones.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Vital capacity = tidal volume + inspiratory reserve + expiratory reserve; measured with a spirometer.
  • Rising CO2 lowers blood pH → chemoreceptors → medulla → faster, deeper breathing (negative feedback).

Mission 2

  • Sarcomeres shorten because actin slides over myosin: I band and H zone narrow, A band stays the same. More overlap, more force.
  • Ca²⁺ uncovers binding sites on actin; myosin heads bind, pull, and use ATP to detach and reset.
  • Muscles only pull, so they work in antagonistic pairs; tendons pass the pull to bone.
  • Titin stores energy when stretched and prevents overstretching.

Mission 3

  • Skeletal muscle (striated, multinucleate fibres) and cardiac muscle (striated, branched, intercalated discs) differ in structure and function.
  • At the neuromuscular junction, ACh depolarises the sarcolemma.
  • A motor unit is one motor neuron and all its fibres.

Mission 4

  • The sperm travels, so it is small, cheap and numerous; the egg stays, so it is large and stocked with reserves. Both are haploid.
  • Acrosome reaction: enzymes digest the zona pellucida; membranes fuse. Cortical reaction: the zona hardens, blocking polyspermy.
  • Tail and mitochondria are destroyed; nuclear membranes dissolve and the chromosomes join one mitosis, giving two diploid nuclei.

Mission 5

  • Unequal cytokinesis: chromosomes are shared equally, cytoplasm is not; every daughter cell still needs a mitochondrion.
  • Spermatogenesis: mitosis, growth, two equal meiotic divisions, differentiation → four sperm, continuously. Oogenesis: begins before birth; two unequal divisions → one egg + polar bodies; meiosis II finishes only after a sperm enters.
  • About day 5 a hollow blastocyst forms; about day 7 it implants in the endometrium.
