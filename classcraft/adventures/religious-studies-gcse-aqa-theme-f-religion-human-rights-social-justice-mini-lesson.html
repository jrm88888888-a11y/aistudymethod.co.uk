Mission 1

What are human rights?

  • Human rights: basic rights and freedoms that belong to every person simply because they are human.
  • The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR) was adopted by the United Nations on 10 December 1948, after the horrors of the Second World War.
  • "All human beings are born free and equal in dignity and rights." (UDHR, Article 1)
  • Examples: life; liberty; equality before the law and a fair trial; freedom of thought, conscience and religion; freedom of opinion and speech; education; work.
  • In the UK, the Human Rights Act 1998 makes the European Convention on Human Rights part of UK law.
Mission 1

Rights bring responsibilities

  • Every right brings responsibilities: if I have a right to free speech, I must respect your right not to be threatened or slandered.
  • "Everyone has duties to the community" (UDHR, Article 29).
  • Most rights can be limited when they clash with others' rights or public safety: e.g. free speech does not cover inciting violence.
  • Believers add duties to God and neighbour: protect the rights of others, especially the weak.

Mission 1Diversity

Christians in action, and different views

  • Examples: Christian Aid and CAFOD campaign for justice for the poor; Martin Luther King (civil rights, USA); Desmond Tutu (against apartheid, South Africa).
  • Most Christians strongly support human rights.
  • Divergent views: some rights claimed today (e.g. to abortion or assisted dying) are rejected by many Christians as against the sanctity of life; Christians differ among themselves on such claims.
Mission 1Diversity

Islam and Judaism

  • Islam: "Verily we have honoured the Children of Adam." (Qur'an 17:70, Pickthall): every human has dignity from Allah.
  • Judaism: every person is made "in the image of God" (Genesis 1:27, JPS 1917), so every life is of infinite worth.
  • René Cassin (1887–1976), a French Jewish lawyer, was a principal drafter of the UDHR.
Mission 1Diversity

Hinduism, Sikhism, Buddhism

  • Hinduism: the same atman (self) is in every being, one with Brahman, so all deserve respect.
  • Sikhism: one Creator, present in all; all are equal.
  • Buddhism: teaching focuses on duties and compassion rather than "rights", but leads to the same care.
Mission 1

Non-religious views

  • Humanists strongly support human rights: they rest on our shared humanity, reason and the wish for everyone to flourish, not on God.
  • The UDHR itself does not mention God, so people of all beliefs can agree on it.
  • Many atheists and Humanists campaign for rights, e.g. freedom of belief, equality.
  • Religious responses: many welcome the agreement; some argue that without God (imago Dei) human dignity has no firm foundation.
  • Some non-religious people criticise religions where they see teachings that limit rights (e.g. on equality); believers reply that their faiths have also driven rights forward.

Mission 1Evaluate

"Human rights only make sense if people are made by God."

  • Reasons for: imago Dei gives every person equal worth that no government can remove (Genesis 1:27). Rights came largely from religious ideas. Without God, rights might seem just a human agreement that can change.
  • Reasons against: Humanists ground rights in shared humanity, reason and wellbeing. The UDHR does not mention God and is accepted across beliefs. Many non-religious people defend rights strongly. Religions have sometimes resisted rights.
  • Another view: people may agree on rights for different reasons; what matters is that they are protected.
  • Judge it by: Where does human worth come from? Can agreement without a shared foundation last?

Mission 2

Prejudice and discrimination

  • Prejudice: judging a person or group unfairly, usually before knowing them. It is an attitude (in the mind).
  • Discrimination: treating a person or group unfairly because of a prejudice. It is an action.
  • Equality: every person has the same worth and should have the same rights and chances.
  • Common types: race, religion, gender, sexuality, disability, age, class.
  • UK law: the Equality Act 2010 makes discrimination unlawful on grounds such as race, religion or belief, sex, sexual orientation and disability.
  • Most religions teach that every person has equal worth. Believers disagree over whether equal worth means identical roles, for example in who may lead worship.

  • "Love one another. Just as I have loved you" (John 13:34): love that excludes no one.

Mission 2

Equality in Islam and Judaism

  • Islam: "O mankind! Lo! We have created you male and female, and have made you nations and tribes that ye may know one another. Lo! the noblest of you, in the sight of Allah, is the best in conduct." (Surah 49:13, Pickthall)
  • Judaism: every person is made in God's image (Genesis 1:27).
Mission 2

Equality in Buddhism, Hinduism and Sikhism

  • Buddhism: all beings can become enlightened. The Buddha taught that a person is noble or low by their deeds, not their birth (Vasala Sutta, Sutta Nipata 1.7, paraphrase).
  • Hinduism: the same atman (eternal self) is in every being.
  • Sikhism: Guru Nanak taught: recognise God's light in everyone and do not ask about caste (Guru Granth Sahib 349, paraphrase). Langar: all sit and eat together; women may lead any part of worship.

Mission 2

The status of women

  • Most religions teach men and women have equal worth. They differ on whether that means the same roles.
  • Catholic and Orthodox Churches: only men are ordained priests, because Jesus chose twelve men as apostles. Women lead in many other roles (religious sisters, teachers, theologians).
  • Church of England: women priests since 1994; women bishops allowed since 2014.
  • Some evangelical churches teach male headship in church and family (1 Timothy 2:11–14); others, and Methodists, Baptists and Pentecostals in Britain, ordain women.
  • Islam: men and women are equal before God (Surah 33:35). Men and women usually pray in separate rows or rooms; women may lead other women in prayer.
  • Judaism: Orthodox Jews separate men and women in the synagogue and count only men in a minyan. Reform and Liberal Judaism have women rabbis.
  • Buddhism: the Buddha founded an order of nuns (bhikkhunis), though with extra rules for nuns. Full nuns' ordination continues in many Mahayana lands; in Theravada it died out and its revival is debated.
  • Hinduism: the divine feminine (Shakti) is worshipped, and Shiva is shown as Ardhanarishvara, half male and half female. Manusmriti 5.147–148 places women under the protection of father, husband or son; many Hindus say this reflects ancient culture, and some groups (e.g. Arya Samaj) train women as priests.
  • Sikhism: women may perform every role, including reading the Guru Granth Sahib in worship.
Mission 2

Same-sex relationships

  • Catholic Church: people with same-sex attraction must be treated with "respect, compassion, and sensitivity" and never unjustly discriminated against (Catechism 2358). It teaches that sexual acts belong only in marriage between a man and a woman.
  • Church of England: marriage is between a man and a woman; since 2023 priests may pray blessings for same-sex couples.
  • Methodist Church, United Reformed Church, Quakers: allow same-sex marriages in their churches where the local church agrees (Quakers decided in 2009, URC 2016, Methodists 2021).
  • Many evangelical Christians, most Muslims and Orthodox Jews hold that same-sex sexual relationships are not permitted by God, while saying every person must be treated with dignity.
  • Reform and Liberal Jews hold same-sex weddings; some Muslim groups work for LGBT inclusion.
  • Humanists (and many religious believers) see excluding same-sex couples from marriage as unjust discrimination.
Mission 2Diversity

Difference or discrimination?

  • "Equal but different" (complementarian): men and women have equal worth but different God-given roles. Supporters say this is faithfulness to scripture and tradition, not prejudice.
  • "Equal and the same" (egalitarian): Galatians 3:28 means no role should be closed by gender. Supporters say limits on roles are discrimination.
  • On sexuality: some say the Church can welcome people while teaching that same-sex sexual acts are wrong; others say this still harms people.
  • Non-religious view: Humanists judge rules by their effect on human well-being and equal rights.
  • The law: the Equality Act 2010 lets religious groups keep their own rules on who may be ordained.
Quick check
?Which of these Christian Churches in England has both women priests and women bishops?
Mission 2Evaluate

"Religion does more to fight prejudice than to cause it."

  • Reasons for: core teachings of equal worth (Genesis 1:27; Surah 49:13; Guru Granth Sahib 349); believers led campaigns against slavery, racism and caste; churches, mosques and gurdwaras serve everyone.
  • Reasons against: scripture has been used to justify slavery, caste and unequal treatment of women; some religious groups exclude women from leadership or reject same-sex relationships; religious prejudice (antisemitism, Islamophobia, sectarianism) still causes harm.
  • Another view: prejudice comes from people, not from religion; the same is true of non-religious ideologies.
  • Judge it by: Should we judge a religion by its teachings or by its followers' actions? Is "different roles" a form of prejudice?
Mission check

Mission 3

What freedom of religion and belief means

  • Freedom of religion and belief: the right to hold any religion or none, to change it, and to practise it.
  • "Everyone has the right to freedom of thought, conscience and religion; this right includes freedom to change his religion or belief" (UDHR, Article 18, 1948).
  • It covers religious expression: worship, teaching, wearing symbols and clothing, keeping festivals and food laws, alone or with others, in private or in public.
  • It protects non-belief too: atheists and Humanists have the same freedom.
  • UK law: the Human Rights Act 1998 (Article 9) and the Equality Act 2010 protect "religion or belief" from discrimination.
Mission 3

Limits on religious expression

  • Article 9 allows limits only where the law sets them and they are necessary: for public safety, order, health, or the rights and freedoms of others.
  • Nadia Eweida (British Airways) won her case to wear a small cross at work (European Court of Human Rights, 2013).
  • Shirley Chaplin, a nurse, lost a similar case: the court accepted a hospital's health-and-safety reasons.
  • Sikhs may carry a kirpan (a small sword, an article of faith) where a "religious reasons" defence applies in law.
  • Freedom of expression also limits religion: in England and Wales, blasphemy is no longer a crime (abolished 2008); using threatening words or behaviour intended to stir up hatred against people because of their religion is (Racial and Religious Hatred Act 2006).

Mission 3

Censorship, extremism and Islamophobia

  • Censorship: controlling or banning what people may say, publish or show. Some censorship protects people (e.g. from incitement to violence); too much silences beliefs.
  • Some believers want limits on mocking what they hold sacred; others, religious and non-religious, defend free speech even when it offends.
  • Religious extremism: holding religious views so rigidly that they lead to hatred, intolerance or violence against others. Found in many traditions; rejected by the great majority of believers.
  • Extremism and prejudice feed each other: extremists blame whole groups; prejudice can drive people towards extremism.
Mission 3Diversity

Islam and Judaism

  • Islam: "There is no compulsion in religion. The right direction is henceforth distinct from error." (Qur'an 2:256, Pickthall)
  • "Unto you your religion, and unto me my religion." (Qur'an 109:6, Pickthall)
  • Divergent views: many Muslims defend freedom of belief; classical law punished leaving Islam, and some countries still do, while many modern scholars argue there should be no worldly penalty. Muslims differ on whether insulting the Prophet should be limited by law; almost all reject violence.
  • Judaism: Judaism does not actively seek converts; good people of all nations have a share in the world to come (keeping the Noahide laws).
  • Long experience of persecution leads many Jews to defend freedom of religion for all.
Mission 3Diversity

Hinduism, Sikhism, Buddhism

  • Hinduism: many paths can lead to the divine, so most Hindus accept other faiths.
  • Sikhism: Guru Tegh Bahadur, the ninth Guru, was executed in Delhi in 1675 after defending the right of Kashmiri Hindus to practise their own faith.
  • Buddhism: the emperor Ashoka (3rd century BCE) ordered respect for all religious groups (Rock Edict XII).
  • Some Buddhist-majority countries have seen intolerance towards religious minorities; many Buddhists condemn it.

Mission 3Diversity

Living in a multi-faith society

  • Multi-faith society: people of many religions and none living side by side, as in the UK today.
  • Benefits: learning from others; working together (interfaith groups, joint charity work); greater understanding and less prejudice; a richer culture.
  • Challenges: religions make different truth claims; questions about mixed-faith marriages and raising children; tension between sharing your faith (evangelism) and respecting others; prejudice and extremism.
  • Contrasting Christian views: exclusivists believe salvation comes only through Christ and share their faith actively; inclusivists (including Catholic teaching) see truth in other faiths; pluralists see many paths to God.
  • Contrasting views on expression: some Christians and Muslims feel public expression of faith is increasingly restricted; others think current limits are fair.
Mission 3

Non-religious arguments

  • Humanists defend freedom of religion and belief for everyone, including the freedom to criticise or leave religion.
  • They argue against some religious privileges: state-funded faith schools selecting pupils by religion; compulsory collective worship in schools; bishops sitting in the House of Lords; religious exemptions from equality law; blasphemy laws in other countries.
  • Religious responses: faith schools are popular and successful; Christianity shaped British law and culture; freedom of religion includes freedom to run institutions by faith; many believers agree that blasphemy laws are wrong.
  • Both sides often agree: no one should be harmed for their belief, or for having none.
Mission 3Evaluate

"Freedom of religious expression should have no limits."

  • Reasons for: belief is central to a person's identity and dignity (Dignitatis Humanae; UDHR Article 18). "No compulsion in religion" (Qur'an 2:256). Limits are often used to persecute minorities (Esther; Guru Tegh Bahadur).
  • Reasons against: some expression harms others (stirring up hatred; extremism). Health and safety matter (Chaplin). Others' rights, including non-believers', must be protected. Humanists: religious freedom should not become privilege.
  • Another view: limits should be as few as possible, set by law, and applied equally to all beliefs.
  • Judge it by: Does the expression harm anyone? Are limits applied fairly to every belief? Who decides what is "necessary"?
Mission check

?A school bans all jewellery. A Sikh pupil asks to wear her kara (a steel bangle worn as an article of faith). Under UK human rights law, when may the school refuse?

Freedom of religion and belief is a basic right for everyone, believers and non-believers alike; it may be limited only when truly necessary to protect others.

Mission 4

Wealth: gift or danger?

  • Wealth: money, property and possessions.
  • Most religions see wealth as a gift that can do great good: "The rich and the poor have this in common: The LORD is the maker of them all." (Proverbs 22:2)
  • Many believers see themselves as stewards (trustees): wealth is lent by God, to be used well.
  • The danger is not money but the attitude to it:
  • Greed: wanting more than you need. Materialism: valuing possessions above people and God.
  • Valuing people by what they own goes against teaching that all have equal worth.
  • Key question: how is wealth gained, and how is it used?
Mission 4Read the source

The needle's eye

"It is easier for a camel to go through a needle's eye than for a rich man to enter into God's Kingdom." (Mark 10:25)

"For the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil." (1 Timothy 6:10)

  • Jesus said this after a rich man went away sad, unwilling to sell his possessions and follow him (Mark 10:17–27). He added that with God all things are possible.

Mission 4

The right attitude and right use

  • "A man's life doesn't consist of the abundance of the things which he possesses." (Luke 12:15)
  • "You can't serve both God and Mammon" (Matthew 6:24): money must never become a master.
  • The rich man and Lazarus (Luke 16:19–31): a rich man ignores the poor man at his gate and ends in torment. His sin is not wealth but ignoring need.
  • Acquiring wealth: honest work and fair trade; not by exploiting others, cheating or harmful trades.
  • Using wealth: provide for family, support the Church, give to those in need; many give a tithe (a tenth).

Mission 4

Wealth in other religions

  • Islam: all wealth belongs to Allah; people are trustees: "spend of that whereof He hath made you trustees" (Surah 57:7, Pickthall). Zakah (2.5% of savings each year) purifies wealth; riba (interest) is forbidden.
  • Judaism: wealth is a blessing from God, to be shared through tzedakah (charity as justice).
  • Hinduism: artha (wealth and success) is a proper aim of life, if gained by dharma (right duty), especially for a householder.
  • Sikhism: kirat karni (earn honestly), vand chhakna (share), sewa (selfless service).
  • Buddhism: the Four Noble Truths: craving causes suffering. Siddhartha left a life of palace luxury, then turned from harsh self-denial too: the Middle Way. Many Buddhists, including the Triratna community, stress Right Livelihood (earning without harm), the Middle Way and dana (generosity).

Mission 4

Social justice

  • Social justice: a fair share of wealth, opportunities and rights for everyone in society.
  • "But let justice roll on like rivers, and righteousness like a mighty stream." (Amos 5:24).
  • Catholic social teaching: the common good. Churches back the Living Wage, food banks and Church Action on Poverty.
  • Campaigns for justice: Jubilee 2000 (cancelling poor countries' debts), Make Poverty History (2005). People can sign petitions, lobby MPs and join marches.
Mission 4

Social justice across religions

  • Islam: zakah redistributes wealth from rich to poor every year.
  • Judaism: tikkun olam: repairing the world.
  • Hinduism: seva (service) and dana (giving) are part of dharma.
  • Sikhism: sewa and langar serve everyone.
  • Buddhism: compassion (karuna) for all who suffer.
Mission 4Diversity

Contrasting uses of wealth

  • Prosperity gospel (some Pentecostal and charismatic churches): God rewards faith and generous giving with wealth and health.
  • Most Churches (Catholic, Anglican, Methodist and many evangelicals) reject this: Jesus was poor and blessed the poor (Luke 6:20).
  • Simplicity: Quakers and many Christians live simply to share more. Catholic religious orders hold goods in common.
  • Other tradition: Muslims see wealth as a test; zakah and sadaqah are acts of worship, and living simply follows the Prophet's example.
  • Non-religious: Humanists say wealth should be used to increase human well-being; many support fair taxes and effective giving.
Mission 4Evaluate

"Religious believers should not be rich."

  • Reasons for: Mark 10:25 and Matthew 19:21; wealth tempts to greed and pride; while others starve, keeping riches seems wrong; Siddhartha left his palace.
  • Reasons against: 1 Timothy 6:10 blames the love of money, not money; wealth can fund great good (stewardship; zakah; vand chhakna); artha is a proper aim; businesses create jobs.
  • Other views: it depends how wealth is gained and used, and how much is given away.
  • Judge it by: Is the problem wealth or attitude? How much is "rich"? Can someone be rich and still put God and others first?

Mission 5

Racism and a multi-ethnic society

  • Racism: the belief that one race or ethnic group is better than another, and the prejudice and discrimination that follow.
  • Multi-ethnic society: many ethnic and cultural groups living together, as in Britain today.
  • Benefits: shared food, music, ideas and skills; understanding between peoples.
  • Problems: racism, fear of difference, separate communities, unfair treatment at work, in housing or by police.
  • Racial harmony: people of different ethnic groups living together peacefully and with equal respect.
  • UK law: the Race Relations Act 1965 began making racial discrimination illegal; today the Equality Act 2010 covers it.
Mission 5Read the source

"From one blood"

"He made from one blood every nation of men to dwell on all the surface of the earth" (Acts 17:26)

  • Paul, speaking to the people of Athens (Acts 17:22–28), says all nations come from one origin and seek the same God.
  • How should worth be judged? "Man looks at the outward appearance, but the LORD looks at the heart." (1 Samuel 16:7)

Mission 5

Christian teaching against racism

  • All people are made in God's image (Genesis 1:27): no race has more worth.
  • "There is neither Jew nor Greek … for you are all one in Christ Jesus" (Galatians 3:28).
  • The Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25–37): a Samaritan, from a despised group, is the one who loves his neighbour.
  • So worth is judged by character and actions, never by colour or origin.
  • Honest history: some Christians once used the Bible to defend slavery and apartheid. Most Churches now teach that racism is a sin, and some have apologised.
Mission 5

Christians who worked for racial equality

  • Martin Luther King Jr (1929–1968): Baptist minister; led the US Civil Rights Movement with non-violent protest (Montgomery bus boycott, 1955–56). His 1963 "I Have a Dream" speech hoped his children would be judged by "the content of their character", not their skin colour.
  • Desmond Tutu (1931–2021): Anglican Archbishop of Cape Town; opposed apartheid in South Africa without violence (Nobel Peace Prize 1984); chaired the Truth and Reconciliation Commission (1996–98). He spoke of a "rainbow nation".

Mission 5

Islam and Judaism on race

  • Islam: "We have … made you nations and tribes that ye may know one another. Lo! the noblest of you, in the sight of Allah, is the best in conduct." (Surah 49:13, Pickthall). Different "languages and colours" are among God's signs (Surah 30:22).
  • The ummah (worldwide Muslim community) is multi-ethnic; on Hajj all wear simple white ihram. Bilal, a freed African slave, was the first to call Muslims to prayer.
  • Judaism: "The stranger that sojourneth with you shall be unto you as the home-born among you, and thou shalt love him as thyself; for ye were strangers in the land of Egypt" (Leviticus 19:34, JPS 1917).
Mission 5

Buddhism, Hinduism and Sikhism on race

  • Buddhism: "All men tremble at punishment, all men fear death; remember that you are like unto them" (Dhammapada 129, Müller).
  • Hinduism: the same atman is in all, so all deserve respect.
  • Sikhism: one God, one humanity; Sikhs are taught to see God's light in all.
  • The Khalsa (1699): Guru Gobind Singh's first five initiates came from different castes and regions; all drank amrit from one bowl and took the same name, Singh. Today men initiated into the Khalsa take the name Singh and women Kaur.

Mission 5

Positive discrimination

  • Positive discrimination: deliberately favouring people from a disadvantaged group, e.g. choosing them over a better-qualified candidate.
  • In the UK this is generally illegal.
  • Positive action is legal: encouraging under-represented groups to apply, offering extra training, or choosing them when two candidates are equally qualified (Equality Act 2010).
  • Aim: to correct past and present unfairness, and to give role models.
Mission 5Evaluate

"Positive discrimination is the fairest way to tackle racism."

  • Reasons for (justice): justice means repairing past wrongs; equal treatment in an unequal world keeps people where they are. Many Christians see this as caring for the disadvantaged (Luke 4:18).
  • Reasons against (equality): it judges people by race again, the very thing racism does; it may cause resentment; it can make people doubt that someone earned their place.
  • Other views: positive action without lowering standards; tackling causes (education, housing) instead. Humanists stress fairness and evidence of what actually works.
  • Judge it by: Is equality treating everyone the same, or giving everyone a fair chance? Does a good aim justify unequal treatment?

Christians, Muslims, Jews, Buddhists, Hindus and Sikhs each ground racial equality in their own teachings: one human family, judged by conduct, not colour.

Mission 6

Absolute and relative poverty

  • Absolute poverty: lacking the basics needed to survive: food, clean water, shelter, healthcare.
  • The World Bank's international poverty line counts people living on less than about $3 a day.
  • Relative poverty: being much poorer than most people in your own society. In the UK: household income below 60% of the median (middle) income.
  • Someone in relative poverty in Britain may have a home but be unable to heat it, feed a family well or join in normal life.
Mission 6

Causes of poverty

  • Personal circumstances: illness, disability, losing a job, family breakdown, addiction, debt.
  • Economic: low pay, unemployment, insecure work, high prices.
  • Global: war, natural disaster and climate change, corruption, unfair trade, debt owed by poor countries.
  • Lack of opportunity: poor education, discrimination.
  • Causes often combine and trap families for generations (the poverty cycle).

Mission 6

Exploitation: fair pay

  • Exploitation: taking unfair advantage of people, often of the poor.
  • "The wages of the labourers who mowed your fields, which you have kept back by fraud, cry out" (James 5:4). Workers must be paid promptly (Deuteronomy 24:14–15).
  • Pope Leo XIII, Rerum Novarum (1891): workers have a right to a just wage that can support a family (paraphrase).
  • Many churches, mosques and synagogues support the Real Living Wage (Living Wage Foundation).
  • Islam: the Prophet taught that a worker should be paid "before his sweat dries" (Ibn Majah, paraphrase).
Mission 6

Excessive interest

  • Payday loans and loan sharks can charge huge interest; poor borrowers fall into debt traps.
  • "If you lend money to any of my people with you who is poor, you shall not be to him as a creditor. You shall not charge him interest." (Exodus 22:25)
  • Islam forbids riba (interest): "Allah permitteth trading and forbiddeth usury" (Surah 2:275, Pickthall). Islamic banks share profit and risk instead.
  • Churches back credit unions (low-cost community lenders). In 2013 Archbishop Justin Welby criticised payday lenders; in 2015 the UK capped payday loan costs.
Mission 6

People-trafficking

  • People-trafficking: moving people by force or deceit to exploit them: forced labour, sexual exploitation, domestic servitude. It is a form of modern slavery.
  • UK law: Modern Slavery Act 2015.
  • Every person is made in God's image: no one can be owned or used (Genesis 1:27).
  • Catholic Church: Pope Francis called trafficking "a crime against humanity" (2014). The Santa Marta Group (2014) links bishops and police against it. St Josephine Bakhita, once enslaved, is honoured as patron of trafficking survivors (feast and world day of prayer: 8 February).
  • The Salvation Army supports trafficking survivors across England and Wales; the Church of England's Clewer Initiative helps parishes spot modern slavery.
Quick check
?A man borrows £200 from a lender who charges so much interest that he soon owes £1,000. Which teaching speaks most directly against the lender?
Mission 6

Who is responsible?

  • Everyone: governments (welfare, overseas aid, fair laws), businesses (fair pay), communities, individuals.
  • Preferential option for the poor (Evangelii Gaudium 198, Pope Francis, 2013): God shows the poor "his first mercy", so the Church must put the poor first in its choices and learn from them (paraphrase).
  • Those in poverty also have responsibilities where they can: "If anyone is not willing to work, don't let him eat." (2 Thessalonians 3:10)
  • This verse is about those unwilling, not those unable. Christians Against Poverty helps people clear debt and regain control; Catholic teaching stresses the dignity of work.
  • Many say help should empower, not create dependence: the Jewish scholar Maimonides ranked helping someone become self-supporting as the highest form of charity.
  • Humanists: reduce suffering through fair systems and evidence-based aid.
Mission 6Evaluate

"People in poverty are responsible for getting themselves out of it."

  • Reasons for: 2 Thessalonians 3:10; work brings dignity and self-respect; aid without effort can create dependence; many people have escaped poverty through their own effort.
  • Reasons against: many causes are beyond a person's control (illness, war, low pay, disability); the preferential option for the poor (EG 198) and Matthew 25 put the duty on everyone; exploitation keeps people poor however hard they work.
  • Other views: a shared responsibility: help people help themselves (training, fair wages, loans).
  • Judge it by: Is the cause personal or systemic? Can someone be blamed for what they cannot control?
Mission check

?A cleaning company pays its workers below a living wage. A Christian shareholder can vote at the company's yearly meeting. Which choice best fits Christian teaching on exploitation and responsibility?

Poverty has many causes. Religions say the poor must not be exploited, and everyone, including the poor themselves where they can, has a part in ending it.

Mission 7

A duty, not an extra

  • Charity: giving money, time or goods to help those in need. The word comes from caritas: love.
  • "You did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me." (Matthew 25:40)
  • "Whoever has the world's goods and sees his brother in need, then closes his heart of compassion against him, how does God's love remain in him?" (1 John 3:17)
  • The Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25–37): a neighbour is anyone in need. Amos 5:24: God wants justice, not only kindness.
  • For Christians, helping the poor is part of agape (selfless love), not an optional extra.
  • Charity treats symptoms; justice also tackles causes of poverty.

Mission 7

The preferential option for the poor

  • Catholic teaching: the Church must make a preferential option for the poor: put the needs of the poor first.
  • Evangelii Gaudium 198 (Pope Francis, 2013): God shows the poor "his first mercy"; Christians should want a Church that is poor and for the poor, and learn from the poor themselves (paraphrase).
  • This means both charity (meeting needs now) and justice (changing unfair systems).
  • So Christians give money, volunteer at food banks, pray, and campaign.
Mission 7

Duties to give across religions

  • Christianity: many give a tithe (10%); collections for the poor; volunteering.
  • Islam: zakah: 2.5% of savings each year, a pillar of Islam. Qur'an 9:60 lists who receives it: the poor, the needy, debtors, captives, travellers and others. Sadaqah: extra voluntary giving.
  • Judaism: tzedakah: giving as justice, not just kindness. "Thou shalt surely open thy hand unto thy poor and needy brother" (Deuteronomy 15:11, JPS 1917).
  • Maimonides' Eight Levels of Charity: the highest is helping someone become self-supporting (a loan, a partnership or a job); next, giving where neither giver nor receiver knows the other; the lowest, giving grudgingly.
  • Sikhism: sewa (selfless service) and dasvandh (a tenth of income); free langar for all.
  • Hinduism: dana (giving) and seva (service) are part of dharma.
  • Buddhism: dana (generosity) is the first of the perfections; it weakens craving.

Mission 7

Christian charities

  • CAFOD (Catholic Agency for Overseas Development, 1962): the official aid agency of the Catholic Church in England and Wales.
  • Christian Aid (1945): agency of many British and Irish churches; Christian Aid Week each May.
  • Three kinds of work: emergency relief (after disasters), long-term development (wells, schools, farming), advocacy (campaigning to change unjust laws and trade).
  • Many work through local partners in each country rather than sending outsiders.
Mission 7

Charities of other faiths

  • Islamic Relief (Birmingham, 1984): emergency and development work; collects zakah and runs Ramadan and Qurbani food programmes.
  • World Jewish Relief (from 1933): began by rescuing Jews from Nazi Germany, including the Kindertransport; now helps people in poverty and disaster worldwide.
  • Food for Life (ISKCON): serves free vegetarian meals, blessed as prasad, to those in need.
  • Khalsa Aid (1999): Sikh relief in disasters and war zones, inspired by sewa and langar.
  • Karuna Trust (1980): Buddhist charity (Triratna) supporting education and dignity for dalit and other marginalised communities in Asia.

Mission 7

Fair trade and campaigns for justice

  • Fair trade: producers in poorer countries receive a fair, stable price plus a premium for community projects.
  • The Fairtrade Foundation (1992) was set up by groups including CAFOD, Christian Aid and Traidcraft (a Christian trading company).
  • Campaigns for justice: Jubilee 2000 (cancelling unpayable debts of poor countries), Make Poverty History (2005).
  • What individuals and communities can do: buy Fairtrade goods; become a Fairtrade church or school; sign petitions; write to MPs; join marches; give time.
  • Fair trade is justice, not charity: people earn a fair living through their own work.
Mission 7Evaluate

"Giving money is the best way to help people in poverty."

  • Reasons for: money is quick and lets people choose what they need; zakah and tithes are given as money; Jesus tells his followers to give (Matthew 6:1–4); charities need funds to work.
  • Reasons against: money may not tackle causes (debt, unfair trade); Maimonides ranks self-support highest; time, skills, prayer and campaigning also help; giving to someone begging might not reach their real need, so some churches suggest food vouchers or charities instead.
  • Other views: a Brazilian archbishop, Hélder Câmara, said people praised him for feeding the poor but attacked him for asking why they were poor (paraphrase). Charity and justice together.
  • Judge it by: Does the help last? Does it respect dignity? Does it tackle the cause?
Mission check

?A youth group has £300. Which plan would most clearly reflect both charity and justice?

Giving money matters, but religious traditions also value giving time, helping people support themselves, and changing unjust systems.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Human rights belong to every person because they are human (UDHR 1948; Human Rights Act 1998); every right brings responsibilities.
  • Religious basis: for Christians and Jews, imago Dei (Genesis 1:27) and love of neighbour.
  • Muslims: Qur'an 17:70; Hindus: atman in all; Sikhs: one Creator in all; Buddhists: duties and compassion.

Mission 2

  • Prejudice is an unfair attitude; discrimination is an unfair action. Religions teach the equal worth of all (imago Dei; Surah 49:13; God's light in all).
  • Religions differ over women's roles and same-sex relationships: "equal but different" or "equal and the same".

Mission 3

  • Freedom of religion and belief: to hold, change and practise any religion or none (UDHR Article 18; Article 9; Equality Act 2010).
  • Limits only when necessary for safety or others' rights (Eweida won; Chaplin lost). Censorship, extremism and Islamophobia threaten freedom from different sides.
  • Teachings: Qur'an 2:256; Esther; Rig Veda 1.164.46; Guru Tegh Bahadur; Ashoka; Catholic teaching in Dignitatis Humanae.

Mission 4

  • Wealth is a gift and a trust; the danger is greed and "the love of money" (1 Timothy 6:10).
  • Social justice means a fair share of wealth, opportunity and rights.
  • Amos calls for justice to "roll on like rivers" (Amos 5:24).
  • Christians differ: stewardship, simplicity and vows of poverty, or the prosperity gospel.

Mission 5

  • Christians teach that racism contradicts one human origin and equal worth (Acts 17:26; Genesis 1:27; 1 Samuel 16:7).
  • Other faiths ground equality in their own teachings (Surah 49:13; Leviticus 19:34; Dhammapada 129; God's light in all).
  • Believers such as Martin Luther King worked for equality without violence.
  • Desmond Tutu opposed apartheid in the same way.
  • Positive discrimination is illegal in the UK; positive action is legal. Arguments draw on both equality and justice.

Mission 6

  • Absolute poverty: lacking the basics to survive. Relative poverty: far below the normal standard in one's society.
  • Exploitation includes unfair pay, excessive interest and people-trafficking; religions condemn all three.
  • Responsibility is shared: governments, businesses, believers, and those in poverty where they are able.

Mission 7

  • Christians see helping the poor as agape (Matthew 25:40; 1 John 3:17); give quietly (Matthew 6:1–4).
  • The preferential option for the poor (EG 198): put the poor first, with charity and justice.
  • Religious charities give emergency relief, long-term development and advocacy; fair trade and campaigns tackle causes.
