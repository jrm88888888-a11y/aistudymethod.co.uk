Mission 1: Can a woman become a Buddha? Why do Buddhist texts give different answers?

Make a guess: Can a woman become a Buddha? Why do Buddhist texts give different answers?

Mission 1

Two different questions

  • Q1: Can a woman reach awakening (Nirvana; become an arhat, Pali arahant)?
  • Q2: Can a woman become a fully enlightened Buddha (samma-sambuddha), who rediscovers and teaches the Dharma for a whole age?
  • Many texts answer yes to Q1 and no, not in a female body to Q2.
  • Keep two more levels apart: theoretically (what the teaching allows) and realistically (whether women get ordination, teachers, education and respect).
Mission 1

Early texts: women can awaken

  • When Ananda asks whether women who go forth can reach the fruits of the path up to arahantship, the Buddha says yes (Cullavagga X.1; see Mission 2).
  • Therigatha ("verses of the elder nuns"): about 73 poems by early nuns describing their awakening, e.g. Patacara, Kisa Gotami.
  • Soma and Mara (Samyutta Nikaya 5.2, paraphrase): Mara taunts that a woman's "two-finger wisdom" cannot reach the goal. Soma replies: what does being a woman matter, when the mind is concentrated and insight arises? Only someone who thinks "I am a woman" or "I am a man" is Mara's target.
  • So the earliest teaching treats insight, not sex, as what liberates.

Mission 1

Rita Gross: Buddhism after patriarchy

  • Rita M. Gross (1943–2015), American scholar and practitioner; Buddhism After Patriarchy (1993).
  • History: Buddhist institutions have been androcentric (male-centred); women's stories were often forgotten.
  • Doctrine: core teachings (anatta, emptiness, buddha-nature) give no basis for fixed gender roles; the Dharma is "neither male nor female".
  • So: Buddhism's commitment to ending suffering for all, and its teaching that gender is empty, make it inherently feminist at its core.
  • Reconstruction: recover women's history; train and recognise women teachers; change institutions to fit the teaching.
  • Feminist approaches in Theravada focus on ordination: see Dhammananda and the Sakyadhita association in Mission 2.
Mission 1Evaluate

Does Buddhism align with feminism?

  • Claim: Buddhism is inherently feminist.
  • For: anatta and emptiness: no fixed male or female self; women's awakening affirmed (Therigatha, Soma); Gross.
  • For: modern movements (Sakyadhita, bhikkhuni revival) draw on the Buddha's own founding of the nuns' order.
  • Against: texts barring a female Buddha (e.g. MN 115) and the eight garudhammas (see Mission 2) place women below men.
  • Also against: Gross herself shows that Buddhist institutions have been androcentric (male-centred).
  • Criteria: Teaching or institutions? Early texts or later ones? Whose feminism, and whose voices count: scholars, monks, nuns, laywomen?

Mission 2

The fourfold assembly

  • The Buddha's community has four parts: bhikkhus (monks), bhikkhunis (nuns), upasakas (laymen), upasikas (laywomen).
  • Bhikkhuni (Pali) / bhikshuni (Sanskrit): a fully ordained nun, who keeps the nuns' Vinaya (311 rules in the Theravada Vinaya; 348 in the Dharmaguptaka Vinaya used in East Asia).
  • Full ordination (upasampada) is given by a quorum of the Sangha; for nuns the Vinaya requires a dual ordination: by bhikkhunis, then by bhikkhus.
  • Below full ordination: novices (samaneri) and probationers (sikkhamana).
Mission 2Read the source

Mahapajapati Gotami asks to go forth

Cullavagga X.1 (also Anguttara Nikaya 8.51), paraphrase:

  • Mahapajapati Gotami, the Buddha's aunt and foster mother, asks him three times to let women go forth. Three times he refuses.
  • She and many Sakyan women shave their heads, put on robes and walk to Vesali. She stands outside, feet swollen, in tears.
  • Ananda asks: are women able to reach stream-entry and the further fruits, up to arahantship? The Buddha: they are.
  • Ananda reminds him she nursed him as a baby. The Buddha agrees, if she accepts eight weighty rules. She accepts them "like a garland".
  • The text adds a prediction: because women have gone forth, the true Dhamma will last 500 years instead of 1,000.

Q1 What reason does Ananda's question give for admitting women?

Show answer

They can reach the same goal as men.

Q2 Which parts of the story do critics see as later additions?

Show answer

The eight rules and the 500-year prediction.

Mission 2

The eight garudhammas

Pali garudhamma ("weighty rules"), paraphrased:

  • 1. A nun, even of 100 years' standing, bows to a monk ordained that day.
  • 2. Nuns do not spend the rains retreat where there are no monks.
  • 3. Every fortnight nuns ask the monks for the observance day and for instruction.
  • 4. At the end of the rains, nuns invite criticism from both Sanghas.
  • 5. A nun guilty of a serious offence undergoes discipline before both Sanghas.
  • 6. A probationer trained for two years seeks full ordination from both Sanghas.
  • 7. A nun must never abuse or revile a monk.
  • 8. Nuns may not admonish monks; monks may admonish nuns.
  • Effect: the nuns' order depends on and ranks below the monks' order.
Mission 2

Origins, controversies, and the role of men

  • Traditional readings: the rules protected women in ancient Indian society and kept the Sangha respected; Mahapajapati accepted them freely.
  • Critical readings (e.g. Bhikkhu Analayo; Bhikkhu Sujato; Ute Hüsken): rule 6 assumes a probationer stage that did not yet exist; some rules duplicate lesser offences found elsewhere in the Vinaya; the 500-year prediction did not come true. So the rules and the prediction may be later additions by a male monastic community.
  • Today: some nuns accept them as a training in humility; others reject or reinterpret rule 1.

Mission 2

How the line was broken, and where it survives

  • Ordination needs living bhikkhunis (dual ordination). Once the last nuns die, who can ordain new ones?
  • Sri Lanka: the Theravada bhikkhuni order dies out by about the 11th century, after war and invasion; Myanmar by about the 13th century. Thailand has no record of a bhikkhuni order.
  • 433 CE: Sri Lankan nuns travel to China and help give the dual ordination at Nanjing: the Chinese lineage descends partly from Sri Lanka.
  • Mahayana lineages continue unbroken today in China, Taiwan, Korea and Vietnam, under the Dharmaguptaka Vinaya.
  • Tibet: the full bhikshuni ordination of its own Vinaya (Mulasarvastivada) was never established; women ordain as novices. Some have taken full ordination from East Asian nuns. Separately, in Bhutan, 2022, over 140 women received full ordination.
  • Thailand today: a 1928 order of the Supreme Patriarch forbids Thai monks to ordain women; the Thai Sangha still applies it.

Mission 2

Thailand: a changing society

  • Tourism after the 1980s: mass tourism brings Western lifestyles and values; it also brings a visible sex industry, linked to rural poverty and lack of education for girls.
  • Changing roles outside religion: women in higher education, business and politics (e.g. a woman Prime Minister, 2011).
  • Rights from secular governments: women vote from 1932; equality of men and women in the 1997 Constitution; the Gender Equality Act 2015.
  • But in religion: a 1928 order of the Thai Supreme Patriarch forbids Thai monks to ordain women as nuns or novices. The Thai Sangha still applies it.
  • So Thai women have more secular rights than religious ones: a gap campaigners stress.
Mission 2

The late 1990s revival

  • 1987: Sakyadhita ("Daughters of the Buddha"), the International Association of Buddhist Women, founded at Bodh Gaya (Karma Lekshe Tsomo, Ayya Khema, Chatsumarn Kabilsingh and others). Aims: unite Buddhist women, education, research, and full ordination where women want it.
  • 1996, Sarnath: ten Sri Lankan women receive full ordination with Korean bhikkhunis and Sri Lankan monks.
  • 1998, Bodh Gaya: an international ordination led by the Taiwanese order Fo Guang Shan includes Theravada women.
  • From 1998, Sri Lanka: bhikkhuni ordinations held in Sri Lanka itself (Dambulla), now continuing in Sri Lanka's own Sangha.
  • 2009, Australia: Ajahn Brahm's monastery ordains four bhikkhunis; his monastery is removed from the Thai forest lineage that trained him.
  • Your spec: "the debate leading to the revival of the Theravada order of nuns in the late 1990s".
Mission 2

Dhammananda

  • Bhikkhuni Dhammananda (Chatsumarn Kabilsingh, b. 1944): Thai scholar of Buddhist philosophy; co-founder of Sakyadhita.
  • Her mother, Voramai Kabilsingh, took full ordination in Taiwan (1971).
  • She becomes a novice in Sri Lanka (2001) and a bhikkhuni (2003): widely described as the first Thai woman fully ordained as a Theravada bhikkhuni; abbess of Songdhammakalyani monastery near Bangkok.
  • Her arguments (paraphrase):
  • The Buddha himself founded the bhikkhuni Sangha; Buddhism needs all four assemblies.
  • The 1928 order binds Thai monks, not Sri Lankan ones; it is not a rule of the Vinaya.
  • Ordination gives women education, dignity and teaching roles, and protects poor rural women from exploitation.
  • The Thai Sangha does not recognise her ordination.
Mission 2Diversity

The ordination debate

  • For revival:
  • The Buddha ordained women and affirmed their awakening.
  • Dharmaguptaka and Theravada Vinayas descend from close early schools; and Sri Lankan nuns founded the Chinese line.
  • Some (e.g. Bhikkhu Bodhi; Bhikkhu Analayo) argue monks alone may ordain nuns when no nuns exist, as at the very start.
  • Against revival:
  • The Vinaya requires dual Theravada ordination; once broken, only a future Buddha could restart it.
  • East Asian nuns follow a different Vinaya school; mixing lineages is invalid.
  • Harmony of the Sangha and respect for senior monks' rulings.
  • Another voice: some mae chi and dasa sil matas prefer their freedom from monks' authority and do not want full ordination (see Salgado).

Mission 2Evaluate

Why does the treatment of women differ so widely?

  • Claim: Differences in how Buddhist women are treated come more from culture than from Buddhist teaching.
  • For: the same tradition varies by country: thilashin respected in Myanmar, mae chi often marginal in Thailand; secular rights in Thailand moved faster than religious ones.
  • For: where lineages survived (Taiwan, Korea), nuns lead large, educated communities.
  • Against: the Vinaya itself (dual ordination, garudhammas) creates the barrier, whatever the culture.
  • Against: some women's choices (dasa sil mata, mae chi) reflect their own religious aims, not only culture.
  • Criteria: Text, law, or practice? Whose authority: senior monks, scholars, or women themselves? Does restoring ordination serve all women, or only some?
  • Skill tip (AO2): weigh Dhammananda's arguments against the Vinaya argument; reach a supported judgement on whether the debate is settled.

Mission 3: The Buddha left his wife and newborn son. What do Buddhists teach about marriage, family and sex?

Mission 3

Two ways of living the dhamma

  • Buddhism has two main ways of life: monastic (monks and nuns of the Sangha) and lay (householders).
  • Monastics are celibate: no sexual activity at all. For a monk, sexual intercourse is the first parajika ("defeat"): it ends his monkhood.
  • Lay Buddhists keep the third precept: to refrain from sexual misconduct (Pali kamesu micchacara). It does not ask for celibacy.
  • Behind both: craving (tanha) and attachment lead to suffering. Monastics cut the ties; lay people try to hold them without clinging.
Mission 3Read the source

Fetters of family

Dhammapada (Müller translation, 1881):

> "Wise people do not call that a strong fetter which is made of iron, wood, or hemp; far stronger is the care for precious stones and rings, for sons and a wife." (Dhammapada 345)

> "'These sons belong to me, and this wealth belongs to me,' with such thoughts a fool is tormented. He himself does not belong to himself; how much less sons and wealth?" (Dhammapada 62)

Q1 What kind of "fetter" is the verse warning about?

Show answer

Not family itself, but the anxious care and possessive attachment that bind the mind.

Q2 Which Buddhist teaching does verse 62 rely on?

Show answer

Anatta: not even the self is "mine", so sons and wealth cannot be owned.

  • Many lay Buddhists read these verses as a warning against clinging, not a ban on family love.
Mission 3

Celibacy: why, and who

  • The Buddha's example: Siddhattha left his wife Yasodhara and baby son Rahula to seek awakening (the Great Renunciation).
  • Celibacy frees time and mind for meditation, and removes a strong source of craving.
  • Theravada (Thailand, Sri Lanka, Myanmar): monks are celibate; many Thai men ordain temporarily, then marry.
  • Japan: Shinran (1173–1263), founder of Jodo Shinshu, married openly; a government decree of 1872 allowed all monks to marry. Today most Japanese temple priests are married.
  • Tibet and Korea: Tibetan ngakpas (lay tantric practitioners) may marry; Korea's Taego order has married clergy, the Jogye order does not.
  • So different Buddhists disagree about whether a religious specialist must be celibate.

Mission 3

Marriage: a social contract

  • The Buddha gave no wedding rite; in Buddhist countries marriage is a civil and family matter, not a sacrament.
  • In Theravada countries, monks traditionally do not conduct weddings; a couple may invite monks to chant blessings (paritta) and receive food, making merit.
  • Marriage is valued as a setting for kindness, faithfulness and generosity: the third precept protects trust.
  • Divorce is also a civil matter. No Buddhist text forbids it; many Buddhists accept it when a marriage causes harm.
  • So "Buddhist views of marriage" are mostly about how to live in it (precepts, intention, non-harm), not who may marry.
Mission 3Read the source

The Sigalovada Sutta

Digha Nikaya 31 (paraphrase): the Buddha tells a young layman, Sigala, to "worship the six directions" by keeping duties to six groups. Two pairs:

  • Parents and children (the east). Children support their parents, keep the family tradition and make offerings for them after death. Parents keep children from wrong, train them for a living, help them marry well and pass on their inheritance.
  • Husband and wife (the west). The husband respects his wife, does not look down on her, is faithful, gives her authority in the home and provides for her. The wife manages the home well, is hospitable to both families, is faithful, guards their earnings and works skilfully.

Q1 What makes these duties reciprocal?

Show answer

Each side owes something to the other; neither has only rights.

Q2 Which duty supports the third precept?

Show answer

Faithfulness, owed by both husband and wife.

Mission 3

Same-sex relationships: what the texts say

  • The Pali suttas do not single out same-sex relationships between lay people.
  • The Vinaya forbids all sexual activity for monastics, same-sex included. It also bars a pandaka from ordination; what the word means (a eunuch? a man with an unusual sexuality?) is debated.
  • Later commentaries (e.g. Vasubandhu's Abhidharmakosa, Tibetan teachers such as Gampopa and Tsongkhapa) define misconduct by improper partners, body parts, places and times: this rules out some acts for everyone, married couples included.
  • The 14th Dalai Lama has said this traditional teaching applies to Buddhists, but that discrimination against gay people is wrong and that same-sex marriage is a matter for each country's law (paraphrase of interviews, 1997 and 2014).
Mission 3Diversity

Views today

  • Traditional and commentarial: same-sex sexual activity counts as misconduct under the third precept, as some acts do for heterosexual couples.
  • Intention and harm: many Buddhists read the third precept as forbidding harmful sex (deceit, coercion, betrayal). On this view a loving, faithful same-sex relationship breaks no precept.
  • Karma readings in Thailand: some Thai Buddhists explain being gay or kathoey as the result of past karma, a view criticised by others as blaming people for who they are.
  • Practice: Taiwan's Venerable Shih Chao-hwei conducted a Buddhist wedding for two women in 2012; many Western sanghas (e.g. Triratna, Soka Gakkai, many Zen centres) welcome same-sex couples.
  • Law: Taiwan (2019) and Thailand (2025) legalised same-sex marriage; Thailand was the first country in South-East Asia to do so.
Mission 3

Transgender matters

  • Vinaya case (Vinaya, Suttavibhanga, Parajika 1; paraphrase): when a monk's sex changed to female, the Buddha allowed her to join the nuns, keeping her ordination and seniority. The same applied the other way.
  • So the Vinaya recognises that sex can change and treats it as a practical matter, not a sin.
  • But ordination rules depend on sex: Theravada monks must be male, and Thai monasteries generally do not ordain trans women as monks.
  • Thailand: kathoey (trans women and feminine men) are highly visible; attitudes range from acceptance to karmic explanations.
  • Principles used today: anatta (no fixed self), karuna (compassion) and ahimsa (non-harm) lead many Buddhists to support trans people.

Mission 3Evaluate

Is intention more important than rules in Buddhist sexual ethics?

  • Claim: For Buddhists, intention and harm, not fixed rules, should decide what sexual conduct is right.
  • For: Karma is intention (cetana); the third precept protects people from harm and betrayal.
  • For: The suttas say little about same-sex acts; the Vinaya's sex-change case shows a practical, compassionate approach.
  • Against: The Vinaya and later commentaries do give detailed rules; tradition carries authority.
  • Against: "Intention" alone can excuse harmful acts; rules protect the community and monastic discipline.
  • Judgement criteria: Which sources carry most authority (sutta, Vinaya, commentary, living teacher)? How should ancient rules apply to new questions? Does a view reduce suffering?

Mission 4: If science can't detect karma or rebirth, what should Buddhists do with those beliefs?

Make a guess: If science can't detect karma or rebirth, what should Buddhists do with those beliefs?

Mission 4

Why Buddhism and science keep meeting

  • From the late 19th century, Asian and Western writers present Buddhism as a "scientific religion": no creator God, no demand for blind faith, cause and effect at its centre.
  • Your spec names three pressures: science's emphasis on evidence and reason; specific discoveries (evolution, cosmology, neuroscience, quantum physics); science as a stimulus to ethical thinking (genetics, medicine, ecology).
  • Buddhist responses range from "science confirms the Dharma", to "science and Dharma answer different questions", to "drop what science disproves".
Mission 4Read the source

The Kalama Sutta

Anguttara Nikaya 3.65 (paraphrase). The Kalamas are confused: visiting teachers each praise their own doctrine and attack others'. The Buddha replies:

  • Section 9: do not accept a teaching merely because of: repeated report; tradition; rumour; scripture; logic or inference; how things appear; agreement with a view you already hold; the speaker's apparent ability; or because "this monk is our teacher".
  • When you yourselves know that something is unwholesome, blameworthy, criticised by the wise, and leads to harm and suffering when done, abandon it.
  • Section 10: when you yourselves know that something is wholesome, blameless, praised by the wise, and leads to benefit and happiness, take it up and stay with it.
  • Earlier in the sutta (sections 5–8), he asks them: does greed (then hatred, then delusion) arise in a person for their benefit or harm? They answer: for harm.

Q1 What is the test the Buddha gives?

Show answer

Your own experience of whether something leads to harm or to well-being, checked against the wise.

Q2 Is this the same as a laboratory test?

Show answer

Not quite: it is a test of ethical and mental results in one's own life.

Mission 4

Two readings of the Kalama Sutta

  • "Charter of free inquiry" (popular, especially in the West): accept nothing without evidence, as in science.
  • Bhikkhu Bodhi ("A Look at the Kalama Sutta", 1988): a warning against that reading.
  • The Kalamas were not yet followers; the Buddha starts from what they can see now (greed, hatred and delusion cause harm).
  • Faith (saddha) still has a place: the Buddha later teaches what cannot be checked at once (e.g. rebirth).
  • The same sutta offers four assurances: if there is rebirth and karma, a good life leads to a good rebirth; if not, a good life is still happy here and now. Belief in rebirth is not demanded first.
  • So the sutta supports testing in experience, but it is not a claim that Buddhism is a laboratory science.
Mission 4

Worlds full of beings

  • Traditional Asian Buddhist cosmology: six realms of rebirth (gods, asuras / demigods, humans, animals, hungry ghosts, hell beings), many heavens (31 planes of existence in the Theravada scheme), nagas, devas and a world-mountain, Mount Meru.
  • Science has no evidence of these realms, and modern cosmology replaces Meru.
  • Responses:
  • Literal: many Asian Buddhists honour devas and spirits and make offerings for the dead; science studies only the physical, not every realm.
  • Psychological: the realms are states of mind in this life (e.g. Thai monk Buddhadasa, 1906–1993: hell is anxiety, hungry ghosts are craving).
  • Revision: the Dalai Lama says the Abhidharma cosmology of Mount Meru can be set aside, since science has shown it wrong.
Mission 4

The challenge to karma and rebirth

  • Challenge: science finds no mechanism by which moral actions shape future lives; most neuroscientists see consciousness as produced by the brain, ending at death.
  • Response 1, karma as intention: the Buddha says that intention is karma (Anguttara Nikaya 6.63, paraphrase). Intentions shape habits and character; psychology can observe this in one life.
  • Response 2, outside science's reach: the Dalai Lama: science has not disproved rebirth; it has not investigated it. Not finding something is different from showing it is false.
  • Response 3, evidence offered: some Buddhists cite the psychiatrist Ian Stevenson (1918–2007) and his studies of children who report past lives; critics dispute the method.
  • Response 4, agnostic / secular: Stephen Batchelor, Buddhism Without Beliefs (1997): practise without needing to believe in rebirth.
  • Karma is not fate: present intention matters. And the Buddha listed the exact results of karma among four things not to be speculated about (Anguttara Nikaya 4.77).
Mission 4

Miracles

  • Pali texts report iddhi (psychic powers) from deep meditation: e.g. the Buddha's Twin Miracle at Savatthi (fire and water from his body).
  • Challenge: such events break known laws of nature; science treats the reports as legend.
  • The Buddha's own caution: in the Kevaddha Sutta (Digha Nikaya 11) he names three "wonders": psychic power, reading minds, and instruction. He says he dislikes and is ashamed of the first two, because a sceptic could explain them away as a mere charm; only instruction truly transforms people (paraphrase).
  • Vinaya: monks are forbidden to display psychic powers to lay people (Cullavagga V.8, the sandalwood bowl).
  • Responses: powers are real but irrelevant to liberation; or the stories are symbolic; or, like rebirth, outside current science. All agree they are not the basis of faith.

Mission 4

Questions the Buddha would not answer

  • The unanswered questions (avyakata): is the world eternal or not? finite or infinite? is the self the same as the body? does a Buddha exist after death?
  • Parable of the poisoned arrow (Cula-Malunkyovada Sutta, Majjhima Nikaya 63): a wounded man refuses treatment until he knows who shot the arrow, his caste, the bow and the feathers. He dies first. Speculation does not cure dukkha.
  • For a link with science: the Buddha refused to claim knowledge beyond the evidence, like a careful scientist.
  • Against: science asks exactly such questions (the age and size of the universe); the Buddha set them aside because they do not lead to liberation, not because they lacked data.
  • So Buddhism and science may share an attitude, but have different aims.
Mission 4

"Buddhism agrees with quantum physics"?

  • The claim: emptiness and dependent origination resemble quantum findings: particles exist only in relation; the observer affects what is observed; no solid, independent "thing".
  • Popularised by Fritjof Capra, The Tao of Physics (1975), and the dialogue The Quantum and the Lotus (Matthieu Ricard and Trinh Xuan Thuan, 2001).
  • Thich Nhat Hanh: interbeing echoes the physicists' discovery that the observer is not wholly separate from what is observed.
  • Criticisms: the parallels use words ("emptiness", "observer") in different senses; physics is mathematics tested by experiment, Buddhist emptiness is insight aimed at ending suffering. Donald Lopez, Buddhism and Science: A Guide for the Perplexed (2008), argues the "Buddhism is scientific" claim is a modern construction with a history.
  • The Dalai Lama welcomes dialogue but warns against claiming science proves Buddhist teaching.
Mission 4

Three Buddhist voices on science

  • 14th Dalai Lama (b. 1935), The Universe in a Single Atom (2005); dialogues with scientists since 1987 (Mind and Life Institute).
  • Buddhism and science share investigation; if science conclusively disproves a Buddhist claim, Buddhists should give that claim up (paraphrase).
  • But science has limits: it cannot settle questions of value, and it is not the whole of knowledge (first-person experience matters too).
  • Science needs compassion to guide its use.
  • Thich Nhat Hanh (1926–2022): science and Buddhism both "look deeply"; interbeing fits ecology and physics; knowledge without mindfulness and compassion can serve destruction.
Mission 4

Genetic engineering

  • Science as a stimulus: new technologies force fresh Buddhist ethical thinking.
  • Key principles: the first precept (ahimsa); intention (cetana); compassion (karuna); the Middle Way; avoiding craving.
  • Gene therapy for disease: widely supported if motivated by compassion and not harming others.
  • Embryo research: many Buddhists hold that a new life (rebirth consciousness) begins at conception, so destroying embryos breaks the first precept (Damien Keown, Buddhism and Bioethics, 1995).
  • "Designer babies" / enhancement: often criticised as driven by craving and attachment to self.
  • GM crops: some support them to reduce hunger (compassion); others fear harm to ecosystems and farmers.
  • The Dalai Lama supports research that relieves suffering, but warns that ethics must keep pace with genetic power and that enhancement needs great caution.

Mission 4Evaluate

How close are Buddhism and science?

  • Claim: Buddhism and science are closely compatible.
  • For: no creator God; cause and effect; testing in experience (Kalama Sutta); meditation research; the Dalai Lama's readiness to revise.
  • For: interdependence and impermanence fit ecology, evolution and physics.
  • Against: karma, rebirth, devas and realms are not scientific claims; miracle stories remain.
  • Against: different aims: liberation from dukkha vs explanation of nature; the "scientific Buddhism" image is partly a modern construction (Lopez).
  • Criteria: Compatible with which Buddhism (modernist, traditional Asian, secular)? Does compatibility need agreement on facts, or only on method? Must Buddhists change beliefs, or can they set limits to science?
Mission check

?A neuroscientist tells a meditation group: "Brain scans show meditation changes attention, so Buddhism is proved true." Which reply would the Dalai Lama's approach most support?

For many Buddhists, science is a partner in investigation, not the judge of everything. Which claims it can test is the real debate.

Mission 5

Two lives, two exiles

  • The 14th Dalai Lama, Tenzin Gyatso (b. 1935): recognised as a child in Tibet; fled to India in 1959 after the failed uprising against Chinese rule; based in Dharamsala; Nobel Peace Prize 1989; gave up his political role in 2011.
  • Thich Nhat Hanh (1926–2022): Vietnamese Zen (Thiền) monk; worked for peace during the Vietnam War; exiled from 1966; founded Plum Village in France (1982); returned to live in Vietnam in 2018 and died there (2022).
  • Both: monks, refugees, prolific authors, teaching Western audiences in plain language.
  • Dalai Lama's key note: compassion and kindness as the basis of happiness, for believers and non-believers alike.
  • Thich Nhat Hanh's key note: mindfulness in ordinary actions, and interbeing: nothing exists by itself alone.
Mission 5

Buddhism and science

  • Dalai Lama: Buddhism and science both seek truth through investigation. In The Universe in a Single Atom (2005) he says that if science clearly showed a Buddhist claim to be false, Buddhists should accept science and give up the claim (paraphrase).
  • He adds a limit: science not finding something (e.g. rebirth) is different from showing it does not exist.
  • He holds the Mind and Life dialogues with scientists and has had monks trained in science.
  • Thich Nhat Hanh: compared interbeing with modern physics' picture of a world without separate, independent things, and the role of the observer (e.g. The Sun My Heart, 1988); he called mindfulness a kind of science of the mind.
  • Critics warn that matching Buddhist ideas to quantum physics can stretch both (see Mission 4).

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Early texts affirm women's awakening (arahantship); texts such as MN 115, and later Theravada, say a Buddha cannot be female.

Mission 2

  • Mahapajapati Gotami became the first bhikkhuni after Ananda's plea; the eight garudhammas placed nuns under monks, and critics question whether they are original.
  • The Theravada line died out (Sri Lanka by the 11th century); Mahayana lines survive.
  • Eight- and ten-precept women (mae chi, thilashin, dasa sil mata) filled the gap with very different status.
  • Revival from 1996–98 (Sarnath, Bodh Gaya, Sri Lanka); Sakyadhita (1987) and Dhammananda (bhikkhuni 2003) lead the Thai debate against the 1928 ban.

Mission 3

  • Monastics are celibate; lay Buddhists keep the third precept (no sexual misconduct). Some schools, especially in Japan, have married clergy.
  • Marriage is a civil matter; the Sigalovada Sutta sets reciprocal duties for husbands, wives, parents and children.
  • On same-sex and transgender matters, Buddhists differ: traditional commentaries list improper acts; many today stress intention, harm and compassion; the Vinaya records a monk whose sex changed.

Mission 4

  • The Kalama Sutta encourages testing teachings in experience, though not as a laboratory science; Buddhists respond to challenges to karma, rebirth, realms and miracles by reinterpretation, by setting limits to science, or by revising.
  • The Buddha's unanswered questions, quantum parallels (and their critics), Thich Nhat Hanh and the Dalai Lama on science; genetic engineering judged by intention, non-harm and compassion.

Mission 5

  • The Dalai Lama (Tibetan, exile from 1959) teaches compassion and kindness as a secular ethic; Thich Nhat Hanh (Vietnamese Zen, exile from 1966) taught mindfulness in daily acts and interbeing.
  • The Dalai Lama accepts science's verdict where it clearly disproves a claim; Thich Nhat Hanh linked interbeing with modern physics.
