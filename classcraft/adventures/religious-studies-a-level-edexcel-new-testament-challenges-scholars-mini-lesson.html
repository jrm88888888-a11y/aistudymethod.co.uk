Mission 1

What is at stake

  • Paul makes the resurrection the hinge of Christian faith:
  • "If Christ has not been raised, then our preaching is in vain and your faith also is in vain." (1 Corinthians 15:14, WEBBE)
  • Three separate questions:
  • Did it happen? (history)
  • Can a historian show it? (method)
  • What kind of event was it? (theology: a body revived, a body transformed, or a change in the disciples)
  • Christians answer these differently. This mission maps the answers.
Mission 1

The scientific challenge

  • Modern science studies a world of regular natural laws. A dead man rising looks like the clearest violation of them.
  • David Hume (1711–1776), An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding (1748), Section X: "it is a miracle, that a dead man should come to life; because that has never been observed in any age or country."
  • So, for Hume, uniform experience always outweighs testimony to such an event.
  • Ernst Troeltsch (1865–1923), "On Historical and Dogmatic Method" (1898): historians judge the past by analogy with the present. Dead people do not rise now, so a historian cannot conclude that one did then (paraphrase).
  • Reply from many believers: the resurrection claims to be unique, an act of God, so analogy cannot rule it out in advance.
Mission 1

Fiction: fraud or a swoon

  • Hermann Samuel Reimarus (1694–1768): fragments published after his death by Lessing (1774–78). The disciples stole the body and invented the story to keep their movement going: a fiction (fraud).
  • Matthew already knew this charge (Matthew 28:13).
  • H. E. G. Paulus (1761–1851), Life of Jesus (1828): a rationalist swoon theory: Jesus did not die, revived in the cool tomb and was seen alive.
  • That would be resuscitation of a man who never died, not resurrection.
  • Standard criticisms: the disciples suffered for their claim (fraud gives no motive); a half-dead man could not have inspired worship of a conqueror of death (D. F. Strauss' own reply to swoon theories).
Mission 1

Myth

  • David Friedrich Strauss (1808–1874), The Life of Jesus Critically Examined (1835–36).
  • Myth does not mean "lie": a religious idea told in the form of a history, formed unconsciously by a believing community.
  • Jewish hopes (a messiah, God vindicating the righteous) and Old Testament patterns shaped stories about Jesus.
  • On the resurrection Strauss also suggests visions: the disciples, convinced Jesus was Messiah, saw him in experiences produced by their own minds.
  • Criticism: the time between Jesus' death and Paul's early list (1 Corinthians 15:3–8) seems short for myth to grow while eyewitnesses lived.
Mission 1

Experience: Bultmann

  • Rudolf Bultmann (1884–1976), "New Testament and Mythology" (1941).
  • The New Testament world-view is mythical (heaven above, demons, miracles). Modern people cannot share it, so the message must be demythologised: interpreted for what it says about human existence.
  • The resurrection is not an event of past history that a historian could check (paraphrase). Easter is the rise of faith in the disciples: they came to see the cross as God's saving act.
  • The risen Christ meets people now in the kerygma (the preached message), which calls for a decision of faith.
  • Related: Willi Marxsen (1919–1993), The Resurrection of Jesus of Nazareth (1968): "the cause of Jesus continues" (paraphrase).

Mission 1

N. T. Wright: the best explanation

  • N. T. Wright (b. 1948), The Resurrection of the Son of God (2003).
  • In Jewish and pagan usage, "resurrection" meant a new bodily life after death, not a spirit surviving. Wright's phrase: "life after life after death".
  • Early Christians held a remarkably consistent, changed form of Jewish hope: one man raised before the end of the age, in a transformed body (Wright: "transphysical").
  • Historian's question: what explains this belief?
  • An empty tomb alone suggests theft; visions alone would be read as a ghost. Together, the empty tomb and the meetings with Jesus are, in his view, the best historical explanation (paraphrase).

Mission 1

Resuscitation or resurrection

  • Resuscitation: a dead body returns to the same mortal life. The person will die again: the widow's son at Nain (Luke 7:15), Jairus' daughter, Lazarus (John 11:44).
  • Resurrection: raised to a new, transformed, imperishable life beyond death.
  • "Christ, being raised from the dead, dies no more. Death no longer has dominion over him!" (Romans 6:9, WEBBE)
  • Catholic teaching (Catechism of the Catholic Church, 646): Christ's resurrection was not a return to earthly life like the raisings he worked (paraphrase).
  • Contemporary critique: some read Luke's fish and flesh as a crude "resuscitation"; others (Rudolf Bultmann, 1884–1976, who read Easter as the rise of the disciples' faith; John Shelby Spong, 1931–2021, Resurrection: Myth or Reality?, 1994) reject a bodily event altogether and read resurrection as transformed faith.

Mission 1Evaluate

Can history decide the resurrection?

  • Claim: Historical study can show whether Jesus rose from the dead.
  • For (Wright): historians explain events by their best causes; early belief, the empty tomb and the meetings need explaining, and natural theories fail.
  • For: Paul's early list (1 Corinthians 15:3–8) names witnesses, some still living: a claim open to checking.
  • Against (Bultmann): faith built on historical proof is no longer faith; the resurrection's meaning is met in decision, not evidence.
  • Against (Troeltsch, Hume): history works by analogy and probability; a unique act of God lies outside its method.
  • Weaknesses each way: Bultmann seems to cut loose from what Paul meant (1 Corinthians 15:14); Wright's "best explanation" depends on whether God's action is allowed as an explanation.
  • Judgement criteria: What can historians prove? What does faith need? What did the first witnesses claim?

Mission 2

Two investigators

  • Frank Morison: pen-name of Albert Henry Ross (1881–1950), a journalist and advertising writer. Who Moved the Stone? (1930). Set extract (8): chapter 8, "Between Sunset and Dawn".
  • He says he set out to write a sceptical study of Jesus' last days and found the evidence led him the other way.
  • Ian Wilson (b. 1941), historian by training (Oxford) and writer, known for work on the Turin Shroud. Jesus: The Evidence (1984; revised 1996). Set extract (9): chapter 10, "Did Jesus Really Rise From the Dead?"
  • Both write for general readers, not as academic theologians.
  • Both ask a historian's question: what best explains the evidence?
  • Anthology extracts: paraphrased here; read the extracts themselves.
Mission 2

The evidence they work with

  • The burial: Joseph of Arimathea, a council member, lays the body in a rock tomb; women see where (Mark 15:43–47; Luke 23:53–55).
  • The empty tomb: women find it open on the first day of the week (Mark 16; Matthew 28; Luke 24).
  • The appearances: the earliest list is Paul's (1 Corinthians 15:5–8).
  • The rumour: the body was stolen (Matthew 28:13–15).
  • The change: frightened followers become public preachers in Jerusalem within weeks (Acts 2).
  • Any explanation must fit all five.
Mission 2

Morison: the clock

  • Morison narrows the question to one night: what happened between sunset on Saturday and dawn on Sunday?
  • He first sets aside theft by the disciples: these men were not the kind to carry off such a deception, and a movement could not have grown on a lie they knew (paraphrase).
  • He then tests the remaining natural explanations one by one, each against the timetable of that night.
  • After setting the disciples' theft aside, Morison lists six lines of approach: Joseph; the Roman power; the Jewish authorities; Jesus not really dead; the women at the wrong grave; the grave never visited.
Mission 2

Morison: who else could have moved it?

  • Joseph of Arimathea moved it. Looks likeliest at first. But a removal at night is oddly timed; the women would have met his party; Joseph acted from devotion, so why hide the body? And no shrine or relic cult grew up at a known grave.
  • The Roman power moved it. Pilate, an obstinate man, is unlikely to have changed his order.
  • The Jewish authorities moved it (to prevent veneration of the tomb). The priests' own story ("the disciples stole him") only makes sense if they did not know where the body was; if they had, they could have produced it and ended the preaching.
  • Jesus did not die (swoon). Morison calls this the least rational option: the wounds were severe; and he quotes Strauss: a half-dead man could not have inspired worship of the conqueror of death (paraphrase).
Mission 2

Morison: the women at the wrong tomb?

  • Kirsopp Lake (1872–1946), The Historical Evidence for the Resurrection of Jesus Christ (1907): in the dim light the women went to the wrong tomb; the young man was a gardener who told them "he is not here".
  • Morison's replies:
  • If it was dark enough to mistake the tomb, would a gardener already be at work?
  • Lake's idea that the disciples had already fled to Galilee rests on thin evidence; Mark 16:7 and Peter's presence in Jerusalem point the other way.
  • The women's family ties to the disciples make it unlikely they were left alone in the city.
  • If the priests had had a witness to disprove the empty tomb, they would have produced him.
  • The tomb never visited; the story invented later: contradicted, he says, at every turn by what followed.
  • His conclusion: the Gospel account of an empty tomb is the only explanation that fits the wider facts.

Mission 2

Wilson: a historian's method

  • Wilson treats the resurrection as a historical problem to investigate, not a doctrine to defend.
  • Where was the tomb? He weighs the Church of the Holy Sepulchre and the Garden Tomb: archaeology cannot settle which tomb, or what happened in it.
  • The discrepancies (women, angels, places, order of appearances): he compares them to honest witnesses of a sudden event, such as a road accident, who agree on what happened but differ in detail (paraphrase).
  • He accepts that the Gospels were written later, by believers, with purposes of their own.
Mission 2

Wilson: weighing the explanations

  • He tests alternatives: the wrong tomb; theft by the disciples or by outsiders; hallucination; Jesus surviving the cross.
  • He finds the swoon theory unlikely: Roman crucifixion was efficient, and a wounded survivor does not fit the confident preaching that followed.
  • The evidence he stresses: the transformation of the disciples, Peter above all, and Paul's list of witnesses (1 Corinthians 15:5–8).
  • So for Wilson the empty tomb alone proves little; the changed lives and the appearances are the facts that need explaining.

Mission 2Diversity

Comparing Morison and Wilson

  • Context: Morison 1930, a lay apologist answering liberal criticism such as Lake's; Wilson 1984/1996, writing alongside a television series and after decades of form and redaction criticism.
  • Method: Morison reconstructs one night and eliminates rivals; Wilson investigates sites, testimony and psychology.
  • Use of the Gospels: Morison harmonises them into one timetable; Wilson accepts their discrepancies and purposes.
  • Strengths, Morison: systematic; takes natural explanations seriously; uses the opponents' own sources (the theft story).
  • Weaknesses, Morison: passes over awkward details (e.g. Matthew's angel); assumes the Gospels' basic reliability; elimination only works if every alternative has been listed.
  • Strengths, Wilson: historical setting; honest about discrepancies; argues from the disciples' changed lives.
  • Weaknesses, Wilson: admits the sources were shaped by belief; gives space to speculative theories; relies on what the disciples believed.
Mission 2Evaluate

Can historical argument establish the resurrection?

  • Claim: Morison and Wilson show that the resurrection can be established by historical evidence.
  • For: natural explanations each meet strong objections; the priests' story concedes an empty tomb; the disciples' change needs a cause.
  • For: Paul's list is early and names living witnesses.
  • Against: the sources are written by believers decades later; historians can show belief, not an act of God (Troeltsch).
  • Against: Bultmann: faith that rests on evidence is not faith; the Easter message is met in decision.
  • Significance for the texts: both treat the Gospels as evidence, where form and redaction critics treat them as proclamation (preaching meant to win faith) shaped by communities.
  • Impact over time: Morison's method became a model for popular evidential apologetics (defending faith from historical evidence) later in the 20th century.
  • Judgement criteria: Are all alternatives considered? Are the sources independent? Can history judge a miracle?

Mission 3

The Sermon on the Plain

  • Luke 6:17–49. Jesus "stood on a level place" (6:17) with disciples and crowds, including people from Gentile Tyre and Sidon.
  • Matthew's longer Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5–7) shares much of the material.
  • Shape of Luke's sermon:
  • 4 blessings and 4 woes (6:20–26).
  • Love your enemies (6:27–36).
  • Do not judge (6:37–42).
  • A tree and its fruit (6:43–45); house on the rock (6:46–49): hearing must become doing.
  • Anthology extract (10) begins with Luke 6:17–26.

Blessings and woes

> "Blessed are you who are poor, for God's Kingdom is yours." (Luke 6:20, WEBBE)

> "But woe to you who are rich! For you have received your consolation." (Luke 6:24, WEBBE)

Mission 3

Love your enemies (Luke 6:27–36)

  • "Love your enemies, do good to those who hate you, bless those who curse you, and pray for those who mistreat you." (6:27–28)
  • Offer the other cheek; give to everyone who asks (6:29–30).
  • Golden Rule: "As you would like people to do to you, do exactly so to them." (6:31)
  • Beyond reciprocity: "If you love those who love you, what credit is that to you?" (6:32). Lend "expecting nothing back" (6:35).
  • Reason: imitate God, who "is kind towards the unthankful and evil" (6:35).
  • Climax: "Therefore be merciful, even as your Father is also merciful." (6:36)
  • Matthew 5:38–48 has the same core (love your enemies, 5:44; the other cheek, 5:39) but ends: "be perfect, just as your Father in heaven is perfect" (5:48); Luke ends with mercy.

Mission 3

Jesus and the Judaism of his time

  • Jesus' ethics grow from the Hebrew Scriptures; many scholars stress continuity.
  • Neighbour and stranger: "you shall love your neighbour as yourself" (Leviticus 19:18); love the foreigner "as yourself" (Leviticus 19:34).
  • Enemies: return your enemy's stray ox (Exodus 23:4); "If your enemy is hungry, give him food to eat" (Proverbs 25:21).
  • The poor: "open your hand… to your needy, and to your poor" (Deuteronomy 15:11); the prophets condemn oppression of the poor (e.g. Amos 2:6–7).
  • Golden Rule: "what you yourself hate, do to no man" (Tobit 4:15); Rabbi Hillel taught the same in negative form (Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 31a, paraphrase). Jesus states it positively.
  • Distinctive emphases: love of enemies as a command for all; mercy modelled on God; reversal of rich and poor.
  • Note: "hate your enemy" (Matthew 5:43) is not a command in the Torah; it describes a popular attitude.
Mission 3

The Rich Man: three versions

  • A man asks how to inherit eternal life; he has kept the commandments. Jesus: sell, give to the poor, follow me. He leaves sad.
  • Mark 10:17–31: "Jesus looking at him loved him" (10:21): only Mark.
  • Matthew 19:16–30: a "young man"; "If you want to be perfect, go, sell what you have" (19:21).
  • Luke 18:18–30: a "ruler"; "Sell all that you have and distribute it to the poor" (18:22).
  • All three: "easier for a camel to enter in through a needle's eye" (Luke 18:25); "The things which are impossible with men are possible with God." (18:27)
  • Peter: "we have left everything" (18:28): followers receive "many times more" now and eternal life (18:30).
Mission 3

The Rich Fool and riches in Heaven

  • Rich Fool (Luke 12:13–21, only Luke): a dispute over inheritance prompts: "a man's life doesn't consist of the abundance of the things which he possesses" (12:15).
  • A farmer builds bigger barns; God says: "You foolish one, tonight your soul is required of you." (12:20)
  • Point: "So is he who lays up treasure for himself, and is not rich towards God." (12:21) His fault is self-centred security, not farming.
  • Riches in Heaven (Luke 12:32–34; Matthew 6:19–21): "Sell what you have and give gifts to the needy" (Luke 12:33).
  • Both versions: "where your treasure is, there will your heart be also" (Luke 12:34).
Mission 3

The poor, the oppressed and the blind

  • Warning and the Widow's Offering (Luke 20:45–21:4; Mark 12:38–44): scribes "who devour widows' houses" (20:47); then a poor widow gives two small coins: "out of her poverty, put in all that she had to live on" (21:4).
  • Two readings: praise of total generosity; or (read with 20:47) a lament that the system takes a widow's last coins.
  • Blind beggar: Mark names Bartimaeus, leaving Jericho (10:46); Matthew has two blind men (20:30); Luke an unnamed man as Jesus approaches Jericho (18:35). He cries "son of David, have mercy on me!"; healed, he "followed him, glorifying God" (18:43).
  • The poor (Luke 14:12–14): invite "the poor, the maimed, the lame, or the blind", because "they don't have the resources to repay you" (14:14).

Mission 3

Marshall and Matera

  • I. Howard Marshall (1934–2015), Luke: Historian and Theologian (1970): Luke is above all a theologian of salvation. Good news to the poor (4:18) shows salvation reaching the outcast; the wealth teaching shows what blocks or expresses a response to it (paraphrase).
  • Frank J. Matera (US Catholic New Testament scholar), New Testament Ethics: The Legacies of Jesus and Paul (1996): Jesus' ethics are the ethics of the Kingdom: a call to repentance and discipleship, not a free-standing moral code. In Luke the right use of possessions is a sign of conversion (e.g. Zacchaeus, 19:8) (paraphrase).
  • Both read the ethics theologically: they flow from God's mercy and the Kingdom's arrival, so they are not a programme that can be lifted out of faith.
Mission 3Diversity

How Christians read the wealth texts

  • Literal renunciation: e.g. St Antony of Egypt and St Francis of Assisi gave all away after hearing these texts; monks and nuns take vows of poverty; Hutterite and Bruderhof communities share goods (Acts 2:44–45).
  • Counsel, not command: Catholic teaching (following Aquinas) treats "sell all" as a counsel of perfection for some; all are bound to detachment and care for the poor.
  • Stewardship: many Protestants see wealth as entrusted by God: John Wesley, "Gain all you can, save all you can, give all you can" ("The Use of Money", 1760).
  • Liberation theology: God's preferential option for the poor; the texts call for changing unjust structures.
  • Prosperity teaching (some Pentecostal and charismatic churches) sees wealth as God's blessing; critics within Christianity say it ignores Luke's woes (6:24).
Mission 3Evaluate

Is Jesus' ethic too demanding to live by?

  • Claim: Jesus' teaching on enemies and wealth in Luke is too demanding to be a practical guide.
  • For: Literal non-retaliation could leave the vulnerable unprotected; no state could govern by it.
  • For: Schweitzer called it an interim ethic for a short time before the end.
  • For: Reinhold Niebuhr (1892–1971), An Interpretation of Christian Ethics (1935): an "impossible" ideal that judges and inspires, but cannot be applied directly.
  • Against: People live it: e.g. the Amish community at Nickel Mines (2006) forgave the man who killed their children; many Christians fund food banks and give sacrificially.
  • Against: Matera and Marshall: it is a response to grace, not a law to earn salvation; God's mercy makes it possible ("possible with God", 18:27).
  • Against: Secular ethics echo it: Peter Singer (b. 1946), "Famine, Affluence, and Morality" (1972), argues the comfortable have strong duties to the poor.
  • Judgement criteria: Is it a rule or an ideal? For individuals or for states? Does it assume an imminent end?

Mission 4

Parables of mercy

  • Three of Luke's best-known parables are found only in Luke: the Good Samaritan, the Forgiving Father (often called the Prodigal Son), and the Rich Man and Lazarus.
  • Each turns on mercy (compassion acted out) and each reverses expectations.
  • Key word: the Samaritan and the father are both "moved with compassion" (10:33; 15:20): the same Greek verb.
  • Luke's setting matters: the parables answer questions or complaints (10:29; 15:2; 16:14).
  • Anthology extract (10): Luke 10:25–37, 15:1–32, 16:19–31.
Mission 4

The command to love

  • A lawyer asks: "what shall I do to inherit eternal life?" (10:25).
  • In Luke, the lawyer answers, joining two Torah texts: love God (Deuteronomy 6:5) and "your neighbour as yourself" (Leviticus 19:18). Jesus: "Do this, and you will live." (10:28)
  • In Mark 12:28–34 and Matthew 22:34–40, Jesus gives the two commands, as the greatest commandments.
  • Mark: the scribe agrees and is "not far from God's Kingdom" (Mark 12:34). Matthew: "The whole law and the prophets depend on these two commandments." (Matthew 22:40)
  • Luke's twist: the lawyer then asks, "Who is my neighbour?" (10:29). The parable answers.

Mission 4

Why a Samaritan?

  • Samaritans worshipped the God of Israel at Mount Gerizim and accepted only the Torah. Jews and Samaritans had centuries of hostility (cf. 9:52–53).
  • The priest and Levite may have feared ritual impurity from a corpse (Leviticus 21:1); the text gives no reason. Many scholars note the Law also commands saving life.
  • The surprise: the despised outsider fulfils the Law's command to love.
  • The question shifts from "whom must I love?" (a boundary) to "how do I become a neighbour?" (an action with no boundary).
  • For Christians: love has no ethnic or religious limit.
Mission 4

The Lost (Luke 15)

  • Setting: tax collectors and sinners come to listen; Pharisees and scribes murmur: "This man welcomes sinners, and eats with them." (15:2)
  • Three parables with the same pattern: lost → found → joy.
  • Lost sheep: one of a hundred; the shepherd searches.
  • Lost coin: one of ten; a woman searches the house (often read as an image of God searching).
  • Lost son: one of two (15:11–32).
  • "There will be more joy in heaven over one sinner who repents, than over ninety-nine righteous people" (15:7).
Mission 4

The Forgiving Father and the elder brother

  • Setting (15:1–2): Pharisees and scribes grumble that Jesus welcomes sinners and eats with them.
  • The younger son takes his inheritance, wastes it, feeds pigs (unclean for a Jew), and "came to himself" (15:17).
  • "While he was still far off, his father saw him and was moved with compassion, and ran, fell on his neck, and kissed him." (15:20) Many commentators note that running was undignified for an older man in that culture: mercy before any speech.
  • Robe, ring, sandals, fattened calf: restored as son, not servant.
  • The elder brother refuses to come in: "I never disobeyed a commandment of yours" (15:29).
  • The father goes out to him too: "Son, you are always with me" (15:31).
  • The story ends open: does the elder brother join the feast? Luke's setting (15:2) suggests the grumblers are invited to decide.

Mission 4

Mercy to sinners

  • The woman who was a sinner (Luke 7:36–50): at a Pharisee's meal she weeps on Jesus' feet and anoints them. The host, Simon, thinks a prophet would know "what kind of woman this is" (7:39).
  • Jesus tells a short parable of two debtors: "her sins, which are many, are forgiven, for she loved much" (7:47). "Your faith has saved you. Go in peace." (7:50)
  • Zacchaeus (19:1–10): "a chief tax collector, and he was rich" (19:2). Jesus invites himself to stay; the crowd murmur.
  • Zacchaeus gives half his goods to the poor and repays fourfold (19:8). "Today, salvation has come to this house… the Son of Man came to seek and to save that which was lost." (19:9–10)
  • Pattern: welcome first, change follows.
Mission 4

The Rich Man and Lazarus (16:19–31)

  • A rich man in purple and fine linen; at his gate a beggar, Lazarus (apart from Abraham, the only character in Jesus' parables given a name; it means "God helps").
  • Both die. Lazarus is carried "to Abraham's bosom"; the rich man is "in anguish in this flame" (16:22, 24).
  • Reversal: "you, in your lifetime, received your good things, and Lazarus… bad things" (16:25): the woes of 6:24 told as a story.
  • A "great gulf" is fixed (16:26). His brothers have "Moses and the prophets" (16:29).
  • "If they don't listen to Moses and the prophets, neither will they be persuaded if one rises from the dead." (16:31) Christians hear a hint of the resurrection.
  • Most scholars read it as a warning about wealth and neglect, not a map of the afterlife.

Mission 4Diversity

How the parables have been read

  • Allegory: Augustine (354–430), Questions on the Gospels II.19: the wounded man is Adam; the robbers, the devil; the Samaritan, Christ; the inn, the Church.
  • One main point: Jülicher and later historical critics: read each parable in Jesus' setting for its central challenge.
  • Social and liberation readings: the parables expose who is excluded and call for structural change (e.g. Luke 16 and global poverty).
  • Martin Luther King Jr (1929–1968), in his last speech (Memphis, 3 April 1968), turned the priest's question round: not "what will happen to me if I stop?" but "what will happen to him if I don't?" (paraphrase).
  • Jewish scholarship: Amy-Jill Levine (b. 1956), Short Stories by Jesus (2014), warns against reading the priest, Levite or elder brother as symbols of a "legalistic" Judaism: the parables are Jewish stories told to Jews.
Mission 4

Judaism, and the parables today

  • Jewish roots: God is "merciful and gracious" (Exodus 34:6); God has no pleasure in the death of the wicked but wants them to "return… and live" (Ezekiel 18:23). Repentance (teshuvah) and welcome are central Jewish themes.
  • What stood out: Jesus' table fellowship with sinners before repentance, and outsiders as heroes.
  • Codes of living: hospices, food banks and charities rooted in these texts (e.g. Christian Aid; the Trussell Trust); Catholic social teaching's option for the poor.
  • Equality: worth does not depend on ethnicity, status or wealth (Samaritan, Lazarus, the sinful woman).
  • Pluralism: the Samaritan held different beliefs, yet acted rightly; some Christians use this to support interfaith cooperation.
  • Secular views: humanists accept the ethic of the Good Samaritan without its religious basis; "Good Samaritan" laws in many countries protect those who help.
Mission 4Evaluate

Is mercy the heart of the believer's life?

  • Claim: Mercy is the most important quality in the life of a religious believer.
  • For: "Be merciful, even as your Father is also merciful" (Luke 6:36); the parables of mercy; "I desire mercy, and not sacrifice" (Hosea 6:6).
  • For: Christians are themselves forgiven, so must forgive (11:4). Pope Francis' Jubilee of Mercy (2015–16) made mercy the Church's focus.
  • For (outreach): Jesus sought "that which was lost" (19:10); outreach to the marginalised follows from this.
  • Against: Justice matters too: mercy without justice can excuse wrongdoers or ignore victims (the elder brother's complaint has some force).
  • Against: Some stress faith or holiness as primary; others love of God (the first commandment) over love of neighbour.
  • Against (outreach): Outreach can be paternalistic or used mainly to win converts; some argue change must come from the marginalised themselves.
  • Judgement criteria: Can mercy and justice be separated? Is mercy a quality, or the sum of the others? Who decides who is "marginalised"?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Enlightenment challenges: fiction (Reimarus' fraud; Paulus' swoon), myth (Strauss), experience (Bultmann, Marxsen); behind them, Hume and Troeltsch on miracles and history.
  • Bultmann: the resurrection is the rise of faith, met now in the kerygma; Wright: the empty tomb and the meetings together best explain early belief in a transformed bodily resurrection.
  • Resuscitation returns a person to mortal life; resurrection, as Christians mean it, is a transformed life that cannot die.

Mission 2

  • Morison (Who Moved the Stone?, 1930, ch. 8): between sunset and dawn; sets aside theft by the disciples, then tests six: Joseph, the Romans, the Jewish authorities, swoon, wrong tomb (Lake) and later invention; concludes the empty tomb is the only explanation that fits.
  • Wilson (Jesus: The Evidence, ch. 10): the discrepancies as normal testimony; tomb sites inconclusive; alternatives weighed; the disciples' change and Paul's list as the facts to explain.
  • Strengths and weaknesses turn on harmonising, the reliability of the sources and whether history can judge a miracle.

Mission 3

  • The Sermon on the Plain pairs blessings for the poor with woes for the rich, and calls for love of enemies modelled on God's mercy (6:36).
  • The wealth texts (Rich Man, Rich Fool, riches in Heaven, the widow, the poor at table) warn against trusting possessions; Christians read them literally, as counsels, as stewardship or as a call to justice.
  • Whether the ethic is practical or an ideal is debated (Schweitzer, Niebuhr, lived examples).

Mission 4

  • The Good Samaritan, the Lost (including the Forgiving Father) and the Rich Man and Lazarus turn on mercy and reversal: outsiders show it, sinners receive it, the comfortable are warned.
  • Jesus' teaching grows from Jewish teaching on love and mercy; the parables have been read as allegory, as single-point challenges, and as social critique.
  • Whether mercy is the heart of a believer's life, and how outreach should work, is debated.
