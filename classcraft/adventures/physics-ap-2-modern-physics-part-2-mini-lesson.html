Mission 1

Mission 1: Why can't the electron in a hydrogen atom orbit at any distance it likes?

Make a guess: Why can't the electron in a hydrogen atom orbit at any distance it likes?

Mission 1

Bohr's picture

  • Hydrogen: one electron in a circular orbit around one proton.
  • The electric attraction provides the centripetal force.
  • ke²/r² = mv²/r, so v = √(ke²/mr).
  • Classical physics allows any radius, so any energy.
  • Classical physics also says an orbiting (accelerating) charge radiates and spirals in. Real atoms do not.
Mission 1

Bohr's rule

  • Bohr (1913): the electron's angular momentum L = mvr is quantized.
  • mvr = nh/2π, n = 1, 2, 3, …
  • Only certain radii obey this, so only certain orbits, each with its own energy.
  • In an allowed orbit the electron does not radiate. It emits only when it jumps between orbits.
Mission 1

Why the rule? Standing waves

  • de Broglie: the electron has a wavelength λ = h/mv.
  • Allowed orbit: a whole number of wavelengths fits around it, a standing wave.
  • 2πr = nλ.
  • Substitute λ = h/mv: 2πr = nh/mv → mvr = nh/2π. Bohr's rule.
  • Any other radius: the wave fails to join up with itself. Not allowed.
Mission 1

Worked example: the ground state

  • Ground-state orbit: r1 = 5.29 × 10⁻¹¹ m.
  • v = √(ke²/mr) = √(8.99 × 10⁹ × (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹)² ÷ (9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × 5.29 × 10⁻¹¹)) = 2.19 × 10⁶ m/s.
  • λ = h/mv = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ ÷ (9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × 2.19 × 10⁶) = 3.3 × 10⁻¹⁰ m.
  • Circumference: 2πr1 = 3.3 × 10⁻¹⁰ m. Exactly one wavelength: n = 1.
Quick check
?The diagram shows the standing-wave model of an electron in one allowed orbit of a hydrogen atom. Which claim about this orbit is supported by the diagram and the model?
Mission 1

Energy and orbit size

  • Electric potential energy: U = −ke²/r. Kinetic energy: K = ½mv² = ke²/2r.
  • Total energy of the atom: E = K + U = −ke²/2r.
  • Standing-wave rule + force rule → allowed radii rₙ = n²r1.
  • Bigger orbit → E less negative → more energy. Free electron: E = 0.
  • These energies match the measured hydrogen levels: −13.6 eV, −3.40 eV, −1.51 eV, …
Quick check
?In the standing-wave model of the hydrogen atom, the electron's orbit in the n = 2 state has 4 times the radius of its orbit in the n = 1 state. What is the ratio λ2/λ1 of the electron's de Broglie wavelength in the n = 2 state to its de Broglie wavelength in the n = 1 state?
Quick check
?Predict first. Which statement correctly describes the Bohr model of the atom?
Mission 1

Successes and limits

  • Success: predicted the hydrogen lines and the 13.6 eV ionization energy.
  • Limits: fails for atoms with more than one electron. Gives no reason for its own rule beyond the standing-wave picture.
  • Electrons do not really follow orbits. Quantum theory replaced the model.
  • Still useful: the first model to explain discrete energy levels.
Mission check

?In the standing-wave model of the hydrogen atom, the electron in the n = 3 state moves in a circular orbit of radius 4.77 × 10⁻¹⁰ m. What is the speed of the electron? Use h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s and me = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg.

Only orbits whose circumference holds a whole number of electron wavelengths are allowed. So the radii, and the energies, come in steps.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How can we read the surface temperature of a star from its light alone?

Make a guess: How can we read the surface temperature of a star from its light alone?

Mission 2

A perfect absorber and emitter

  • All matter above absolute zero turns some of its internal energy into electromagnetic radiation.
  • A blackbody absorbs all the radiation that falls on it. None is reflected or transmitted.
  • It is also the best possible emitter at its temperature.
  • Its spectrum depends only on its temperature, not on what it is made of.
  • At a steady temperature it must emit energy at the same rate as it absorbs it.
Quick check
?Predict first. A blackbody is heated to a higher temperature. How does its emission spectrum change?
Mission 2

Spectrum curves and temperature

  • Plot intensity per unit wavelength against wavelength: a smooth, continuous curve.
  • Hotter body: more at every wavelength. The peak is higher.
  • Hotter body: the peak moves to shorter wavelength.
  • The total power (area under the curve) rises steeply with temperature.
  • Color shows this: red-hot (cooler) → yellow → white → bluish (hotter).
  • Classical physics could not predict the curve's shape. Planck matched it by assuming energy is exchanged with the hot body only in discrete packets (quanta).
Mission 2

Wien's displacement law

  • Peak wavelength is inversely proportional to absolute temperature.
  • λmax = b/T, b = 2.90 × 10⁻³ m·K.
  • T must be in kelvin: temperature in K = temperature in °C + 273.15, usually rounded to + 273.
  • The Sun: λmax ≈ 500 nm, so T ≈ 5800 K.
Mission 2

Worked example: a red star

  • A star's spectrum peaks at 850 nm (infrared, just beyond red light).
  • Use meters: 850 nm = 850 × 10⁻⁹ m.
  • T = 2.90 × 10⁻³ ÷ 850 × 10⁻⁹ = 3.4 × 10³ K
  • Longer peak wavelength → cooler surface. This star looks red.
Mission 2

Stefan–Boltzmann law

  • Total power radiated ∝ surface area × T⁴.
  • P = AσT⁴
  • σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W/(m²·K⁴) (Stefan–Boltzmann constant).
  • T⁴ matters: double T → 16 × the power.
Quick check
?A small metal sphere of radius 5.0 cm is heated to 527 °C. Treat it as a blackbody and take σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W/(m²·K⁴). What power does the sphere emit as thermal radiation?
Mission check

?The graph shows the intensity per unit wavelength emitted by a blackbody as a function of wavelength λ. The absolute temperature of the blackbody is then doubled. At what wavelength does the spectrum of the heated blackbody peak?

Peak wavelength ∝ 1/T. So the color of a star (where its spectrum peaks) gives its surface temperature: bluish stars are hotter than reddish ones.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Scatter X-rays off electrons and their wavelength gets longer. Why would light do that?

Make a guess: Scatter X-rays off electrons and their wavelength gets longer. Why would light do that?

Mission 3

Compton's experiment

  • 1923: X-rays of one known wavelength aimed at graphite. Its outer electrons are almost free.
  • A detector measured the wavelength of X-rays scattered at different angles θ.
  • Result: a scattered component with a longer wavelength λ′.
  • The shift grew with the angle.
  • Wave theory predicted the same wavelength at every angle. It could not explain the shift.
Quick check
?Predict first. An X-ray photon collides with a free electron that is initially at rest, and a photon emerges moving in a new direction. How does the emerging photon compare with the incident photon?
Mission 3

A photon–electron collision

  • Treat the X-ray as a stream of photons.
  • Each photon has energy E = hf = hc/λ and momentum p = h/λ (= E/c).
  • One photon hits one electron at rest. The electron recoils, gaining kinetic energy.
  • Energy and momentum of the photon–electron system are conserved.
  • The photon keeps less energy → lower f → longer λ. Its speed is still c.
Mission 3

The Compton shift

  • Δλ = λ′ − λ = (h/me c)(1 − cos θ).
  • θ = angle between the incident and scattered photon directions.
  • h/me c = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ ÷ (9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × 3.00 × 10⁸) = 2.43 × 10⁻¹² m.
  • θ = 0°: no shift. θ = 90°: Δλ = 2.43 × 10⁻¹² m. θ = 180°: largest, 4.85 × 10⁻¹² m.
  • The shift does not depend on the incident wavelength.
Mission 3

Worked example

  • X-rays, λ = 2.00 × 10⁻¹¹ m, scattered through 90° by free electrons at rest.
  • Δλ = 2.43 × 10⁻¹² × (1 − cos 90°) = 2.43 × 10⁻¹² m.
  • λ′ = 2.00 × 10⁻¹¹ + 0.243 × 10⁻¹¹ = 2.24 × 10⁻¹¹ m.
  • Electron's kinetic energy = photon energy lost = hc/λ − hc/λ′.
  • = 9.95 × 10⁻¹⁵ − 8.87 × 10⁻¹⁵ = 1.08 × 10⁻¹⁵ J.
Quick check
?An X-ray photon of wavelength 5.00 × 10⁻¹¹ m is scattered by a free electron that is initially at rest, and the scattered photon moves at 37° to the incident direction. What is the wavelength of the scattered photon? Use h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s, me = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg, and c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s.
Mission 3

Momentum in two dimensions

  • Incident photon along +x: momentum h/λ. Electron at rest.
  • After: photon h/λ′ at angle θ; electron pe on the other side of the x-axis.
  • x: h/λ = (h/λ′)cos θ + pe,x.
  • y: 0 = (h/λ′)sin θ + pe,y, so the y-components are equal and opposite.
  • Vectors: pphoton before = pphoton after + pelectron. Draw the triangle.
Quick check
?A photon with energy E, moving in the +x-direction, collides with a free electron that is initially at rest. After the collision, the scattered photon moves in the +y-direction with energy E′. The speed of light is c. Which expression gives the magnitude of the electron's momentum just after the collision?
Mission 3

Why it matters

  • Photoelectric effect: light delivers energy in quanta, hf.
  • Compton scattering: each photon also carries momentum h/λ and collides with one electron, like a particle.
  • Additional evidence that light is made of photons.
  • Why X-rays? Δλ is about 10⁻¹² m whatever λ is. For visible light (λ ≈ 5 × 10⁻⁷ m) that is about 5 millionths of λ, too small to measure.
Quick check
?In Compton's experiment, X-rays of a single wavelength were scattered by electrons in a graphite target, and the scattered X-rays were examined. Which observation provides evidence that light is a collection of discrete photons?
Mission check

?A gamma-ray photon of wavelength 5.00 × 10⁻¹³ m collides with a free electron that is initially at rest, and the scattered photon moves at 60° to the incident direction. What is the kinetic energy of the electron just after the collision? Use h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s, me = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg, and c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s.

The photon hands energy to the recoiling electron. Less energy means a lower frequency, so a longer wavelength.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Can a nucleus decay three times and end up as the same element it started as?

Make a guess: Can a nucleus decay three times and end up as the same element it started as?

Mission 4

Unstable nuclei

  • Some nuclei are unstable. They decay: they emit radiation and become more stable.
  • Decay is spontaneous: nothing triggers it. Heat, pressure and chemistry do not change it.
  • The emitted particle or photon is the radiation: α, β or γ.
  • The type of decay is set by the isotope: carbon-14 decays by β⁻; carbon-11 by β⁺.
Mission 4

Meet the radiations

  • α particle: a helium-4 nucleus. 2 protons + 2 neutrons. Charge +2e. Symbol ⁴2He or ⁴2α.
  • An α particle is also written He²⁺.
  • β⁻ particle: a fast electron from the nucleus. Charge −e. Symbol ⁰₋1e or ⁰₋1β.
  • β⁺ particle: a fast positron, the electron's antiparticle. Charge +e. Symbol ⁰₊1e or ⁰₊1β.
  • γ ray: a high-energy photon. No charge, no mass.
Mission 4

Balancing decay equations

  • Top numbers (nucleon number, A) add up the same on both sides.
  • Bottom numbers (proton number or charge, Z) add up the same on both sides.
  • An electron counts as ⁰₋1e: no nucleons, charge −1.
  • Lepton number is also conserved: electron and neutrino +1; positron and antineutrino −1.
Mission 4

Worked example: α and β⁻

  • α decay: ²³⁸92U → ²³⁴90Th + ⁴2He
  • A: 238 = 234 + 4 ✓. Z: 92 = 90 + 2 ✓.
  • New element: Z falls by 2 (uranium → thorium). A falls by 4.
  • β⁻ decay: ¹⁴6C → ¹⁴7N + ⁰₋1e + ν̄
  • ν̄ is an antineutrino: uncharged, almost no mass, emitted with the β⁻ particle.
  • A: 14 = 14 + 0 ✓. Z: 6 = 7 + (−1) ✓.
  • Inside: a neutron → proton. Z rises by 1; A unchanged.
Quick check
?A nucleus has nucleon number A and proton number Z. It undergoes an alpha decay, and the nucleus formed then undergoes a beta-minus decay. Which expression gives the number of neutrons in the final nucleus?
Mission 4

β⁺ and γ decay

  • β⁺ decay: ¹⁸9F → ¹⁸8O + ⁰₊1e + ν
  • Inside: a proton → neutron. Z falls by 1; A unchanged.
  • γ decay: after α or β decay the new nucleus is often left excited. It drops to a lower energy state by emitting a γ photon.
  • ⁶⁰28Ni* → ⁶⁰28Ni + γ. A and Z do not change. Same nuclide, less energy.
Mission 4

Neutrinos

  • β⁻ decay emits an antineutrino ν̄; β⁺ decay emits a neutrino ν.
  • Both are uncharged, have tiny mass, and very rarely interact with matter.
  • They interact only through the weak force and gravity.
Quick check
?A student proposes that a free neutron decays into a proton and an electron and nothing else: n → p + e⁻. Which conservation law that holds in all nuclear decays would this proposal break?
Mission check

?Thorium-232 (²³²90Th) decays through a chain of α and β⁻ decays to stable lead-208 (²⁰⁸82Pb). How many α decays and how many β⁻ decays are there in the chain?

An α decay lowers Z by 2; two β⁻ decays raise it by 2. So α, β⁻, β⁻ returns a nucleus to the same element: an isotope with 4 fewer nucleons.

Mission 5

Mission 5: If half a radioactive sample decays in an hour, why doesn't the rest decay in the next hour?

Make a guess: If half a radioactive sample decays in an hour, why doesn't the rest decay in the next hour?

Quick check
?Predict first. Radioactive decay is described as random and spontaneous. Which statement correctly describes what this means?
Mission 5

Random and spontaneous

  • Random: you cannot predict which nucleus decays next, or when.
  • Spontaneous: nothing outside triggers it. Temperature, pressure and chemistry make no difference.
  • Every undecayed nucleus of an isotope has the same, constant chance of decaying per second. A nucleus does not "age".
  • With huge numbers of nuclei, the fraction that decays in a given time is predictable.
Mission 5

Half-life

  • Half-life: the time for the number of undecayed nuclei of an isotope to halve.
  • After n half-lives, the fraction left is (½)ⁿ: ½, ¼, ⅛, 1/16 …
  • The decay curve never quite reaches zero.
  • Half-lives range from far less than a second (polonium-214: 164 μs) to billions of years (uranium-238: 4.5 × 10⁹ years).
Mission 5

Worked example: whole half-lives

  • A sample has 6.4 × 10⁶ undecayed nuclei. Half-life 2.0 hours.
  • After 8.0 hours: 8.0 ÷ 2.0 = 4 half-lives.
  • Left: 6.4 × 10⁶ × (½)⁴ = 6.4 × 10⁶ ÷ 16 = 4.0 × 10⁵.
  • Decayed: 6.4 × 10⁶ − 4.0 × 10⁵ = 6.0 × 10⁶.
  • Read the question: "left" or "decayed"?
Quick check
?A sample of iodine-131 has a half-life of 8.0 days. A second sample contains twice as many iodine-131 nuclei. What is the half-life of the second sample?
Mission 5

The decay constant

  • Decay constant λ: the probability per unit time that a nucleus decays. Unit: s⁻¹ (or h⁻¹, yr⁻¹).
  • Larger λ → faster decay → shorter half-life.
Mission 5

The exponential decay law

  • N = N0e−λt: N0 nuclei at t = 0; N at time t.
  • Taking logs: ln(N/N0) = −λt.
  • Half-life: λ = ln 2 / t½.
  • Works for any time, not just whole half-lives.
Mission 5

Worked example: carbon dating

  • Old wood has 0.40 of the carbon-14 per gram of carbon found in living wood. Half-life 5730 years.
  • λ = ln 2 ÷ 5730 = 1.21 × 10⁻⁴ yr⁻¹.
  • N/N0 = e−λt → t = ln(N0/N) ÷ λ = ln(1/0.40) ÷ 1.21 × 10⁻⁴.
  • t = 0.916 ÷ 1.21 × 10⁻⁴ = 7.6 × 10³ years.
  • Assumes the carbon-14 fraction in living things has stayed the same.
Quick check
?A sample contains 8.0 g of a radioactive isotope whose half-life is 5.3 years. What mass of the isotope has decayed after 12 years?
Mission check

?A sample of radon-222 is sealed in a tube. What is the probability that a given nucleus in the original sample has decayed after three half-lives?

Each surviving nucleus keeps the same chance of decaying. So in the next hour, half of what is left decays: a quarter of the original sample, not the other half.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Bohr: electric force gives circular orbits; a whole number of de Broglie wavelengths must fit (2πr = nλ), so only certain radii and energies.
  • The model explains hydrogen's lines but fails for atoms with more than one electron.

Mission 2

  • A blackbody absorbs all incident radiation; its spectrum depends only on T. Hotter: more at every λ, peak at shorter λ.
  • Wien: λmax T = constant (≈ 2.9 × 10⁻³ m·K).
  • P = AσT⁴.

Mission 3

  • A photon has energy hc/λ and momentum h/λ. Scattered by a free electron, it loses energy, so its wavelength increases.
  • Δλ = (h/me c)(1 − cos θ): zero at 0°, largest at 180°, independent of λ.
  • Energy and momentum (in 2D) of the photon–electron system are conserved.
  • Compton scattering shows that photons carry momentum: more evidence for the particle nature of light.

Mission 4

  • α: ⁴2He, A − 4, Z − 2. β⁻: neutron → proton, Z + 1. γ: no change to A or Z.
  • β⁺: proton → neutron, Z − 1. β⁻ emits an antineutrino, β⁺ a neutrino.
  • Nucleon number, charge and lepton number are conserved in every decay.

Mission 5

  • Decay is random and spontaneous; each nucleus has a constant chance of decaying.
  • Half-life: the time for N to halve. After n half-lives, (½)ⁿ is left.
  • N = N0e−λt; half-life = ln 2 / λ.
