Mission 1

History, memory or invention?

  • 1–2 Samuel picture a united monarchy: David and Solomon ruling Judah and Israel from Jerusalem, c. 1000–930 BCE.
  • The texts reached their final form centuries later, probably in or after the exile (sixth century BCE or later).
  • So scholars ask: how much is history, how much memory reshaped, how much later construction?
  • From the 1990s the debate polarised: minimalists and maximalists.
  • The question is about the historical value of the texts. Their religious meaning is a separate question.
Mission 1

The minimalists

  • The biblical "ancient Israel" is largely a literary creation of later scribes, in the Persian or Hellenistic period.
  • The narratives tell us about the writers' own time (identity after exile), not the tenth century.
  • Philip R. Davies (1945–2018, In Search of 'Ancient Israel', 1992): distinguish biblical "Israel" from historical Israel.
  • Thomas L. Thompson (The Mythic Past, 1999) and Niels Peter Lemche ("Copenhagen school"): little or no evidence for a tenth-century kingdom.
  • Keith Whitelam (The Invention of Ancient Israel, 1996): the focus on Israel has silenced Palestinian history.
  • Method: begin with archaeology and inscriptions; use the Bible only where independent evidence supports it.
Mission 1

Maximalists and the middle ground

  • Maximalist: the narratives preserve reliable memory unless there is good evidence against them.
  • Kenneth Kitchen (On the Reliability of the Old Testament, 2003): the biblical picture fits what is known of the ancient Near East.
  • William G. Dever (What Did the Biblical Writers Know and When Did They Know It?, 2001): an archaeologist and fierce critic of minimalism, though not a defender of every biblical detail.
  • Middle ground: Israel Finkelstein and Neil Asher Silberman (The Bible Unearthed, 2001; David and Solomon, 2006): David was real, but a chieftain of a small highland polity; the grand empire is a later picture, from Josiah's time (seventh century BCE).
  • Most scholars today sit between the poles: a historical David, a modest kingdom, a story enlarged in the retelling.
Mission 1

The evidence on the ground

  • Tel Dan stele (found 1993–94): Aramaic inscription, ninth century BCE; an Aramean king boasts of killing a king of the "House of David" (bytdwd). First mention of David outside the Bible.
  • Mesha stele (Moab, c. 840 BCE): a possible reading "House of David" (Lemaire, 1994); disputed.
  • Khirbet Qeiyafa (excavated 2007–2013, Yosef Garfinkel): a fortified town overlooking the Elah valley, tenth century BCE. Garfinkel saw a Judahite state under David; others argue it was Philistine, Canaanite or linked to Saul.
  • Jerusalem: Eilat Mazar's "Large Stone Structure" (2005), claimed as David's palace; its date is disputed. Tenth-century Jerusalem remains hard to measure under the later city.
  • Absence of evidence: little monumental building or writing in tenth-century Judah. Is that because there was no state, or because of later building and limited digs?

Mission 1

1 Samuel 12 and the two voices on kingship

  • 1 Samuel 12: Samuel's farewell. He asks the people to witness his honesty ("Whose ox have I taken?", 12:3) and retells God's saving acts.
  • Asking for a king was wrong (12:17–19), yet "the LORD has set a king over you" (12:13).
  • Condition: if people and king fear and serve the LORD, all will be well; "if you keep doing evil, you will be consumed, both you and your king" (12:25).
  • Two strands in 1 Samuel 8–12: anti-monarchic (chapters 8; 10:17–27; 12) and pro-monarchic (9:1–10:16; 11: God chooses Saul to save his people).
  • Julius Wellhausen (1844–1918, Prolegomena, 1878) saw the pro-monarchic stories as older and the anti-monarchic speeches as later, reflecting the failure of the kings.
  • Many now read the final text as a deliberate balance: kingship is a concession, legitimate only under God and the law.
Mission 1

The new king chosen (1 Samuel 15:34–16:23)

  • "The LORD grieved that he had made Saul king over Israel" (15:35).
  • Samuel is sent to Jesse of Bethlehem. The eldest sons are passed over: "man looks at the outward appearance, but the LORD looks at the heart" (1 Samuel 16:7).
  • The youngest, the shepherd, is anointed; "the LORD's Spirit came mightily on David from that day forward" (16:13).
  • At once: "the LORD's Spirit departed from Saul, and an evil spirit from the LORD troubled him" (16:14).
  • David enters Saul's court as a harpist whose playing brings relief (16:18–23).
  • Effect: before David does anything, the narrative establishes that God has transferred the kingdom.

Mission 1

Why were the narratives written?

  • Apology (defence) of David: P. Kyle McCarter ("The Apology of David", 1980) argued the story answers charges that David was a usurper: he did not seek the throne, did not kill Saul, did not order Abner's or Ishbosheth's deaths. Compare the Hittite Apology of Hattusili III.
  • Legitimation of the dynasty: God chose David and promised his house the throne (2 Samuel 7); useful for Judah's kings and for Jerusalem's Temple.
  • Theology for the exile: why the kingdom fell; that kings are under God and the law (Deuteronomy 17; 1 Samuel 12).
  • Hope: a future son of David after the monarchy's fall (Psalm 89).
  • Moral and spiritual teaching: God looks at the heart (16:7); sin and repentance (2 Samuel 12).
  • Literary art: Robert Alter (The David Story, 1999) reads it as narrative of rare psychological depth, deliberately ambiguous about David's motives.

Mission 1Evaluate

How historical are the Davidic narratives?

  • Claim: The Davidic narratives are reliable history.
  • For: "House of David" on the Tel Dan stele; tenth-century sites such as Khirbet Qeiyafa; vivid, unflattering detail (Bathsheba; David with the Philistines) that a propagandist would not invent; local knowledge of places.
  • Against: written centuries later; little evidence of a large tenth-century state; a pattern of theological themes (God's choice, the law) shaping the story; scenes no historian could know (private conversations, God's words).
  • The middle: a real David and a modest kingdom, remembered and enlarged (Finkelstein); unflattering details may themselves be apologetic (McCarter).
  • Judge it by: What counts as evidence: texts, archaeology or both? Who carries the burden of proof? Does "history" in the ancient world mean what it means today?
Mission 1Evaluate

Interpreting religious texts

  • The issue (OAd): how should sacred texts be read, and does their meaning depend on their history?
  • Historical-critical: reconstruct sources, dates and purposes (Noth, Wellhausen); meaning lies in the original setting.
  • Literary: read the final text as story, with its art and ambiguity (Alter).
  • Canonical: Brevard Childs (1923–2007, Introduction to the Old Testament as Scripture, 1979): read the books in the shape the community received and used as scripture.
  • Faith communities: many Jews read the stories through rabbinic tradition (e.g. David as the model penitent and psalmist); many Christians read them as pointing to Christ, "son of David"; conservative readers hold historicity essential, others locate truth in theology rather than chronicle.
  • For "history matters": biblical faith claims God acts in history; a purely fictional David would weaken the covenant promise.
  • Against: a text can be true about God and humanity without being an exact record; the narratives' own aims are theological.
  • Judge it by: Who has authority to interpret: scholars, communities, or individual readers? Can different methods be combined?

Mission 2

Solomon's centralised state (1 Kings 4:1–7:12)

  • Officials: priests, secretaries, a recorder, an army commander, a palace manager, an officer over forced labour (4:1–6).
  • Twelve districts, each feeding the court for one month a year (4:7). They cut across the old tribal territories.
  • Forced labour: a levy of thirty thousand men from Israel (5:13), cutting cedar in Lebanon in shifts.
  • Building: the Temple, seven years (6:38); Solomon's own palace complex, thirteen years (7:1).
  • The idyll: "every man under his vine and under his fig tree, from Dan even to Beersheba" (4:25); wisdom "like the sand that is on the seashore" (4:29).
  • The cost: tax, labour and a court in Jerusalem drawing wealth from the north.
Mission 2

Alliances, wealth and division (1 Kings 10:1–12:20)

  • Trade and diplomacy: the Queen of Sheba's visit (10:1–13); gold, horses and chariots (10:14–29); silver "as common as stones in Jerusalem" (10:27).
  • Marriage alliances: Pharaoh's daughter and "many foreign women" (11:1); seven hundred wives and three hundred concubines (11:3).
  • "His wives turned away his heart after other gods" (11:4); God will tear the kingdom away (11:11).
  • Jeroboam, Solomon's officer over the northern labour gangs (11:28), is promised ten tribes by the prophet Ahijah (11:29–39).
  • At Shechem the northern tribes ask Rehoboam to lighten "his heavy yoke" (1 Kings 12:4). He answers: "My father chastised you with whips, but I will chastise you with scorpions." (1 Kings 12:11)
  • "What portion have we in David?… To your tents, Israel!" (12:16). The kingdom divides, c. 930 BCE: Israel (north) and Judah (south).

The king's role (Psalm 72)

> "God, give the king your justice; your righteousness to the royal son. He will judge your people with righteousness, and your poor with justice." (Psalm 72:1–2, WEBBE)

> "For he will deliver the needy when he cries; the poor, who has no helper." (Psalm 72:12, WEBBE)

  • In the psalm the king is God's agent: his justice comes from God.
  • His first duty: the poor and needy: "Their blood will be precious in his sight" (72:14).
  • His reward: long life, prosperity for the land, dominion "from sea to sea" (72:8), the blessing of the nations.
  • The heading links it to Solomon. Jews and Christians later read it as a picture of the Messiah.

Mission 2

Royal propaganda?

  • Ancient kings advertised themselves: inscriptions of wisdom, wealth, building and peace. Solomon's portrait in 1 Kings 3–10 matches the pattern.
  • Royal ideology: God chose the king and the city; the Temple stands beside the palace; Psalm 72 makes the king God's channel of blessing.
  • Archaeology: Yigael Yadin dated similar gates at Hazor, Megiddo and Gezer (1 Kings 9:15) to Solomon; Israel Finkelstein redates them to the ninth century. The scale of Solomon's kingdom is debated (see Mission 1).
  • Critique inside the text: the Deuteronomistic writers also show Solomon breaking the law of the king: horses, wives, gold (Deuteronomy 17:16–17; 1 Kings 10–11).
  • Walter Brueggemann (The Prophetic Imagination, 1978) contrasts the "royal consciousness" of Solomon's state (wealth, control, a domesticated God) with the prophets' alternative.
  • So the narrative both preserves royal propaganda and subverts it.

Mission 2

Elijah and the prophetic movement

  • Elijah the Tishbite, ninth century BCE, in the northern kingdom under Ahab, who married Jezebel, a princess of Sidon, and served Baal (1 Kings 16:31).
  • A movement, not a lone voice: "the sons of the prophets" live in communities at Bethel and Jericho (2 Kings 2:3, 5); Obadiah hides a hundred prophets of the LORD (1 Kings 18:4).
  • Call of Elisha (1 Kings 19:19–21): Elijah throws his mantle (cloak) over Elisha at the plough; Elisha slaughters his oxen and follows.
  • Succession (2 Kings 2:1–18): Elisha asks for "a double portion of your spirit" (2:9), the eldest son's share.
  • "A chariot of fire and horses of fire separated them; and Elijah went up by a whirlwind into heaven." (2 Kings 2:11)
  • Elisha strikes the Jordan with the mantle; the waters part. "The spirit of Elijah rests on Elisha." (2:15)
  • Echoes of Moses and Joshua: parted waters, a successor commissioned. Elijah's return is awaited (Malachi 4:5).

Carmel: who sends the rain? (1 Kings 18)

Mission 2

Two visions of government, society and religion

  • Israel's covenant ideal: the land belongs to God, given to families as inheritance ("the land is mine", Leviticus 25:23); the king is a brother under the law (Deuteronomy 17:20).
  • Royal absolutism (as the narrative shows Jezebel bringing it from Sidon): the king owns and disposes; law serves the throne; false witnesses can be arranged.
  • Religion: worship of the LORD alone, who demands justice, versus Baal worship, supporting royal and fertility religion.
  • Society: free landholding families versus a court elite drawing land and wealth to itself.
  • The prophet stands outside the power structure and speaks for God against the king. The king's own officials cannot.
  • Carmel is about which god; Naboth is about what kind of society the true God requires. Together they show the two are linked.

Mission 2

Divine power: where is it shown?

  • 1 Kings 17: Elijah announces drought (17:1). At Zarephath, in Sidon (Baal's own territory), a starving widow's flour and oil do not run out (17:16); her son is raised: "Now I know that you are a man of God" (17:24).
  • 2 Kings 4:8–37: a woman of Shunem who welcomed Elisha is promised a son; when he dies, Elisha restores him to life (4:34).
  • 2 Kings 5:1–19: Naaman, an enemy Aramean general, is healed in the Jordan (5:14): "I know that there is no God in all the earth, but in Israel" (5:15). The king of Israel had despaired: "Am I God, to kill and to make alive?" (5:7)
  • 2 Kings 6:8–23: Elisha's servant sees "horses and chariots of fire" around them (6:17); the Aramean raiders are blinded, led to Samaria, then fed and sent home. "The bands of Syria stopped raiding" (6:23).
  • The pattern: power over rain, food, death and armies; shown to widows, mothers, foreigners and enemies; in mercy as much as might.
  • The narratives' point: power belongs to God, not the king, and is mediated by the prophet.
Mission 2Evaluate

The responsibilities of leaders

  • The issue (OAc): what religious, moral and legal duties do leaders have, in any age?
  • Biblical standards: justice for the poor (Psalm 72); the king under the law (Deuteronomy 17); "If you will be a servant to this people today… they will be your servants forever" (1 Kings 12:7); a prophet may call the king to account (1 Kings 21).
  • Failures shown: Solomon's burdens and idolatry; Rehoboam's harshness; Ahab and Jezebel's abuse of law; Jeroboam's political religion.
  • Religious leaders too: the prophets of Baal serve the throne; Elijah's violence at Kishon raises questions about religious leaders using force.
  • Today: rule of law, accountability to courts and the press, protection of property and of the weak; Christian ideas of servant leadership (Mark 10:43).
  • Judge it by: Should a leader's private faith shape public duty? Who holds leaders to account when they control the law? Are biblical standards transferable to modern democracies?
Mission 2Evaluate

Monarchy, religion and politics

  • The issue (OAe): should religion support political power, check it, or keep apart from it?
  • Religion supporting the throne: Solomon's Temple beside the palace; Psalm 72; Jeroboam's golden calves at Bethel and Dan, made so the people need not go "up to Jerusalem" (12:28).
  • Religion checking the throne: Ahijah, Elijah and Nathan confront kings; God, not the king, owns the land and the rain.
  • Later history: the divine right of kings (James VI and I, The Trew Law of Free Monarchies, 1598); established churches with a monarch as Supreme Governor (Church of England); constitutional separation of church and state (e.g. the United States).
  • For close ties: shared values, moral restraint on rulers, national identity.
  • Against: religion used as propaganda; dissenters harmed (Naboth); faith compromised by power (Brueggemann's "royal consciousness").
  • Judge it by: Who speaks for God when king and prophet disagree? Does an established religion protect or silence the prophetic voice?

Mission 3

What a prophet is

  • A prophet (Hebrew navi) speaks for God to the people, often to kings.
  • Older titles: ro'eh and ḥozeh, both "seer": one who sees what God shows.
  • Prophecy is mainly forth-telling (God's verdict on the present), not fore-telling; predictions follow from the verdict.
  • In Israel many prophets were professionals: groups attached to sanctuaries and royal courts, the "sons of the prophets" (see Mission 2).
  • That is why "true and false prophecy" mattered: rival prophets spoke in the same God's name.
Mission 3

Biblical criteria for a true prophet

  • Fulfilment: if the word "doesn't follow, nor happen", the LORD has not spoken it (Deuteronomy 18:21–22).
  • Loyalty: a prophet whose sign comes true but who says "Let's go after other gods" must be rejected (Deuteronomy 13:1–3). Fulfilment is not enough.
  • The divine council: the true prophet has "stood in the council of the LORD" (Jeremiah 23:18, 22); false prophets "speak a vision of their own heart" (Jeremiah 23:16).
  • Moral content: a true word turns people "from their evil way" (Jeremiah 23:22).
  • Independence: false prophets cry "Peace!" to those who feed them and "tell fortunes for money" (Micah 3:5, 11).
  • Message: Jeremiah tells Hananiah that a prophet of peace needs proof by fulfilment; earlier prophets mostly announced war and judgement (Jeremiah 28:8–9).
Mission 3

Word, vision and spirit

  • Word: writing prophets stress the word of God: "The Lord GOD has spoken. Who can but prophesy?" (Amos 3:8). The messenger formula "Thus says the LORD" frames the oracle.
  • Vision: God "shows" the prophet something (Amos 7–9); the vision carries the word.
  • Spirit: earlier, group prophets were seized by the spirit in ecstasy (1 Samuel 10:10).
  • The pre-exilic writing prophets (Amos, Isaiah, Jeremiah) rarely claim the spirit. Micah 3:8 is an exception; Hosea 9:7 reports that the people call the "man who is inspired" insane.
  • Sigmund Mowinckel (1884–1965), "The 'Spirit' and the 'Word' in the Pre-exilic Reforming Prophets" (1934): these prophets based their authority on the word, perhaps to stand apart from ecstatic professionals.
  • In 1 Kings 22 a "spirit" misleads the court prophets: spirit alone proves nothing.
Mission 3

Amos: call and visions

  • Amos of Tekoa, in the southern kingdom of Judah, speaks at Bethel in the northern kingdom of Israel, c. 760–750 BCE, under Jeroboam II (Amos 1:1).
  • A herdsman and dresser of sycamore figs, called "from following the flock" (Amos 7:14–15).
  • Five visions (7:1–9:4):
  • (1) Locusts and (2) fire: Amos pleads, "How could Jacob stand? For he is small", and "The LORD relented" (7:2–3, 5–6).
  • (3) Plumb line: Israel is measured and found crooked; no more pleading (7:7–9).
  • (4) Summer fruit: Hebrew qayiṣ (summer fruit) sounds like qēṣ (end): "The end has come on my people Israel" (8:1–2).
  • (5) The Lord beside the altar, striking the sanctuary: no one escapes (9:1–4).
  • The prophet moves from intercessor to witness of judgement.

Mission 3

What the clash shows

  • Two authorities: Amaziah speaks for king and sanctuary; Amos for the God who called him.
  • "I was no prophet": the Hebrew has no verb. It can mean "I am no (professional) prophet", rejecting the guilds; or "I was no prophet" until God called me. Either way his authority is the call, not an office or a wage.
  • Witness in conflict: Amos repeats his word and adds a personal oracle against Amaziah and his family (7:16–17).
  • Compare 2:12: Israel "commanded the prophets, saying, 'Don't prophesy!'" Amaziah does exactly that.
  • Amaziah may sincerely have believed he was protecting order and worship; the text gives only Amos's side.

Mission 3

Prophets as witnesses and signs

  • A prophet is a witness in God's case against the people: "Listen, and testify against the house of Jacob" (Amos 3:13).
  • A prophet's life and actions can be signs: Isaiah's children are "for signs and for wonders in Israel" (Isaiah 8:18); Zedekiah's iron horns are a (false) sign.
  • Conflict is normal: Amos with Amaziah, Micaiah with Zedekiah and Ahab, Jeremiah with Hananiah. True prophets are often rejected and alone.
  • Hope remains: Amos pleads in his first visions; even judgement oracles aim to turn people back ("Seek me, and you will live", Amos 5:4;).

Can a true prophetic voice be discerned in any age?

  • Claim: There are reliable criteria for telling a true prophetic voice, then or now.
  • For: The criteria in the earlier screen work together: loyalty, moral content, independence from power and payment, readiness to suffer. Amos and Micaiah pass all of them.
  • For: Later voices are often judged this way: a costly challenge to power for the sake of the poor sounds like Amos (e.g. many Christians and Jews read the civil rights movement so).
  • Against: Fulfilment comes too late to help hearers, and prophets' words are often conditional (Amos 7:3; Jonah 3:10).
  • Against: Hananiah was sincere and patriotic. James L. Crenshaw (b. 1934; Prophetic Conflict, 1971) argued that no criterion separated true from false prophets at the time; only hindsight did.
  • Against: Unpopularity proves nothing: false prophets can also be lonely, and true ones can be popular.
  • Judgement criteria: Can the test be applied before events? Does it fit the biblical cases? Does it depend on already sharing the prophet's faith? Which authority judges today: scripture, community, conscience?

  • Develop: For him, religion serves national stability; Amos's words threaten the king (7:10–11).

Mission 4

Israel in Amos's day

  • c. 760–750 BCE: Jeroboam II rules a wealthy, secure northern kingdom.
  • Assyria is quiet for a time; trade and building boom: houses of "cut stone" (Amos 5:11) and "beds of ivory" (Amos 6:4).
  • Wealth flows to an elite in Samaria; small farmers fall into debt and lose land.
  • Worship is busy and generous at Bethel and Gilgal (4:4–5).
  • Within about 30 years, Assyria destroys the northern kingdom (722 BCE).
Mission 4

Amos's God

  • Lord of all nations: he judges Damascus, Gaza, Tyre, Edom, Ammon and Moab, not only Israel (1:3–2:3).
  • Lord of creation: hymn fragments (doxologies) praise the one "who forms the mountains, creates the wind" (4:13; also 5:8–9; 9:5–6). Many scholars think these were added later; they still shape the book's picture.
  • A God of justice: he cares more for justice than for worship (5:21–24).
  • A roaring lion: "The LORD will roar from Zion" (1:2); "The lion has roared. Who will not fear?" (3:8).
  • Yet he relents when Amos pleads (7:3, 6): judgement is not his only word.
Mission 4

The oracles against the nations

  • Formula: "For three transgressions of …, yes, for four, I will not turn away its punishment" (1:3).
  • Six neighbours are condemned for atrocities in war: threshing Gilead with iron (Damascus), selling whole populations as slaves (Gaza, Tyre), ripping open pregnant women (Ammon), burning a king's bones (Moab).
  • They have no Torah; they are judged by a standard of common humanity.
  • Judah (2:4–5) is judged for rejecting "the LORD's law" (many scholars see this oracle as a later addition).
  • The trap: listeners cheer as the circle closes in, then the eighth oracle hits Israel (2:6–16), the longest.
Mission 4Read the source

Election and responsibility

> "I have chosen only you of all the families of the earth. Therefore I will punish you for all of your sins." (Amos 3:2, WEBBE)

> "Are you not like the children of the Ethiopians to me, children of Israel?" says the LORD. "Haven't I brought up Israel out of the land of Egypt, and the Philistines from Caphtor, and the Syrians from Kir?" (Amos 9:7, WEBBE)

  • "Chosen" translates Hebrew yada', "known": an intimate, covenant relationship.
  • Popular belief: election means protection. Amos: election means responsibility.
  • 9:7: God also guided other peoples' migrations; the Exodus is not a guarantee.
  • 9:8 then softens: God "will not utterly destroy the house of Jacob" (often read as a later qualification).

Mission 4

Social, religious, political criticism

  • Social: they "sold the righteous for silver, and the needy for a pair of sandals" (2:6); bribes in the gate (the town court) (5:10–12); false weights and scales (8:5); rich women of Samaria, "cows of Bashan", "oppress the poor" (4:1).
  • Luxury without grief: winter and summer houses, ivory beds, wine in bowls, "but they are not grieved for the affliction of Joseph" (6:4–6).
  • Religious: "Go to Bethel, and sin" (4:4). God rejects their feasts and offerings (5:21–23) because worship sits alongside injustice.
  • Political: "I will rise against the house of Jeroboam with the sword" (7:9); the leaders "at ease in Zion" and "secure on the mountain of Samaria" (6:1).
  • Amos is not against worship as such; scholars differ on whether he rejects the cult itself or only cult without justice.
Mission 4Read the source

Justice and the Day of the Lord

> "Woe to you who desire the day of the LORD! Why do you long for the day of the LORD? It is darkness, and not light." (Amos 5:18, WEBBE)

> "But let justice roll on like rivers, and righteousness like a mighty stream." (Amos 5:24, WEBBE)

  • Day of the LORD: people expected a day of victory, when God would defeat Israel's enemies.
  • Amos reverses it: darkness for Israel. Like escaping a lion and meeting a bear (5:19).
  • mishpat (justice: fair judgement in the courts) and tsedaqah (righteousness: right relationship in the community) are paired.

Mission 4

Is there hope in Amos?

  • Conditional hope: "Seek me, and you will live" (5:4); "It may be that the LORD … will be gracious to the remnant of Joseph" (5:15). Note the "may be".
  • Ending (9:11–15): God will "raise up the tent of David who is fallen", rebuild ruined cities, and plant Israel so it is "no more plucked up".
  • Redaction view: many scholars think 9:11–15 was added after 586 BCE: the "fallen" house of David fits the exile; the tone differs sharply from 9:1–4. Julius Wellhausen (1844–1918) complained of "roses and lavender instead of blood and iron".
  • Unity view: others argue an eighth-century prophet could hope beyond judgement; the northern kingdom split from David, so the "fallen tent" could mean the divided kingdom.
  • Either way, the final form of the book ends with hope; readers in exile read Amos as judgement and promise.

Mission 4Evaluate

Opposing values in a relativistic age

  • Claim: Amos's absolute moral demands can still guide a believer in a relativistic age.
  • For: Amos judges the nations by standards they share without revelation (1:3–2:3). John Barton (Amos's Oracles against the Nations, 1980) argues Amos assumes a kind of international moral law, a common ground with non-believers.
  • For: Many moral norms that both religious and secular people hold (no torture of civilians, fair courts) echo these oracles.
  • Against: Relativism holds that moral claims are true only relative to a culture; Amos's God-given standard is one voice among many.
  • Against: A believer who speaks like Amos may seem intolerant in a plural society; Amos had no democratic forum or shared constitution.
  • Against: Amos's ethics belong to an 8th-century covenant people; applying them directly risks anachronism.
  • Judgement criteria: Is there a shared moral core across cultures? Must a believer persuade on shared grounds or simply testify? Does Amos's universal standard answer relativism or presuppose faith?
Mission 4Evaluate

Social justice, human rights, moral responsibility

  • Claim: Amos is a source for modern social justice and human rights.
  • For: He defends the poor, debtors and those denied justice in court (2:6–7; 5:12). Martin Luther King Jr (1929–1968) quoted 5:24 in the "Letter from Birmingham Jail" and "I Have a Dream" (1963).
  • For: Responsibility rests on leaders and the comfortable, who are judged for not grieving (6:6).
  • Against: Amos speaks of covenant duty to God, not of individual rights; "human rights" is a modern concept.
  • Against: Amos announces judgement; he offers no social programme or reform plan.
  • Against: Religious and secular readers may use the same text for opposing policies.
  • Judgement criteria: Can duties ground rights? Does a text need a programme to inspire action? Who bears moral responsibility: individuals, leaders, systems?

Building an AO1 paragraph

Task: "Explain Amos's teaching on the Day of the Lord."

  • Point: Israel expected the Day of the LORD to be God's victory over its enemies.
  • Source: Amos reverses this: "It is darkness, and not light" (5:18).
  • Develop: Because of injustice (5:10–12) and empty worship (5:21–23), the Day brings judgement on Israel itself; there is no escape (5:19).
  • Link: This follows from election as responsibility (3:2).
  • Context: The prophecy was read later in the light of Assyria's conquest of Israel (722 BCE).

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Minimalists (Davies, Thompson, Lemche, Whitelam) see the David story as a late literary creation; maximalists (Kitchen) trust it unless disproved; many (Finkelstein, Dever in his own way) see a real but smaller David. Key evidence: Tel Dan, Khirbet Qeiyafa, Jerusalem.
  • 1 Samuel 8 and 12 present kingship as a rejection of God yet allowed under the law; 9–11 present it as God's gift; 15:34–16:23 shows God transferring the kingdom to David.
  • Reasons for the narratives include apology, dynastic legitimation, theology for the exile, hope and literary art; interpreting them involves historical, literary, canonical and faith-based readings.

Mission 2

  • Solomon built a centralised state (officials, twelve districts, forced labour, Temple and palace) and an empire of trade and marriage alliances; its burdens and his idolatry led to the division under Rehoboam. Psalm 72 sets the ideal: the king as God's agent of justice for the poor. The narratives both preserve and criticise royal propaganda.
  • Elijah belongs to a prophetic movement and passes his mantle to Elisha; at Carmel and over Naboth's vineyard he confronts a royal religion and an absolutist view of kingship with covenant faith and justice.
  • Divine power, in the Elijah and Elisha stories, reaches widows, mothers, foreigners and enemies. Leaders' responsibilities and the relation of monarchy, religion and politics are evaluated.

Mission 3

  • True and false prophets spoke in the same God's name; the Bible offers several tests (fulfilment, loyalty, the council, moral content, independence), none decisive alone.
  • Amos, called from the flock, is told by Amaziah to leave the king's sanctuary; Micaiah stands against 400 prophets and a lying spirit.
  • Writing prophets rest their authority on the word and the call, not on the spirit or an office.

Mission 4

  • Amos's God is Lord of all nations and of creation; he judges Israel's neighbours by a common standard, then Israel most of all.
  • Election means responsibility (3:2); the Day of the LORD is darkness for an unjust people (5:18); justice matters more than ritual (5:24).
  • Hope is conditional in Amos (5:4, 15); the ending (9:11–15) is widely seen as a later, exilic message of restoration.
