The American Dream: Reality & Illusion 1945-1980

This is an optional route. AQA A-level History is option-based. 2Q The American Dream is one of nineteen Component 2 depth studies (2A–2T). It is a non-British option, so pair it with a British Component 1 breadth study, plus the Component 3 Historical Investigation.

This mini-lesson covers both parts of 2Q: prosperity, inequality and Superpower status, 1945–1963 and challenges to the American Dream, 1963–1980. The option's title is its question: for whom was the Dream real, and for whom was it an illusion?

1945–1960 · the age of affluence

Who the boom was for

Post-war America was extraordinarily rich — and unequally so.

  • The engines: wartime savings released into consumption; the GI Bill (1944) funding education and mortgages for veterans; federal highway spending (the Interstate Highway Act, 1956); cheap oil; and mass suburbanisation — the Levittown developments became the symbol.
  • The catch: the GI Bill and federal mortgage insurance were administered in ways that systematically excluded Black Americans; suburbs used restrictive covenants and redlining was standard practice. Affluence itself was segregated.
  • Labour: the Taft–Hartley Act (1947), passed over Truman's veto, curbed union power — a decisive check on the New Deal order.
  • Poverty persisted in Appalachia, the rural South and the inner cities. Michael Harrington's The Other America (1962) forced it back into political view and helped shape Johnson's War on Poverty.

The core analytical move in 2Q: never write about 'Americans' in the aggregate. Disaggregate by race, class, gender and region — and the same decade produces opposite verdicts.

1947–1954 · the Red Scare

McCarthyism and the limits of freedom

A superpower's anxiety turned inward.

  • Federal loyalty programme (1947); the House Un-American Activities Committee and the blacklisting of the Hollywood Ten (1947); the Alger Hiss case; the trial and execution of the Rosenbergs.
  • Senator Joseph McCarthy rose after his Wheeling speech (February 1950) claiming Communists in the State Department, and fell after the televised Army–McCarthy hearings (1954); the Senate censured him in December 1954.
  • Why it mattered: McCarthyism narrowed permissible dissent for a generation. It also made civil rights and labour organisations vulnerable — anti-communism was routinely used to smear them.

Nuance: Soviet espionage in the 1940s was real. The historical judgement is not 'was there any threat' but whether the response was proportionate — and what it cost American political liberty.

1954–1965 · the civil rights revolution

Law, protest and the ballot

The movement's achievement was to make segregation ungovernable.

  • Brown v. Board of Education (1954) overturned the 'separate but equal' doctrine of Plessy — but 'all deliberate speed' (Brown II, 1955) permitted years of delay and massive resistance.
  • Montgomery Bus Boycott (December 1955 – December 1956): Rosa Parks's arrest and a year-long boycott; the emergence of Martin Luther King Jr; victory came through the courts.
  • Little Rock (1957): Eisenhower sent federal troops to enforce desegregation. The Civil Rights Act of 1957 was weak but symbolically important.
  • 1960–63: the Greensboro sit-ins and the founding of SNCC (1960); the Freedom Rides (1961); Birmingham (1963), where televised police violence forced Kennedy's hand; the March on Washington (28 August 1963).
  • The statutes: the Civil Rights Act (1964) banning segregation in public accommodation and employment discrimination; the Voting Rights Act (1965), after Selma, which transformed Black registration in the Deep South.
  • The limits: the Watts riots (August 1965) came days after the Voting Rights Act. Northern segregation, policing and poverty were untouched by southern legislation. Black Power — Stokely Carmichael from 1966 — grew from that gap. King was assassinated on 4 April 1968.

1963–1968 · the Great Society and Vietnam

Johnson: the most ambitious — and the most destroyed

Lyndon Johnson became President on 22 November 1963. His domestic programme was the most far-reaching since the New Deal; his war destroyed him.

  • The Great Society: the Economic Opportunity Act (1964) and the War on Poverty; Medicare and Medicaid (1965); the Elementary and Secondary Education Act (1965); the Immigration and Nationality Act (1965), which abolished the national-origins quota system and reshaped American demography.
  • Vietnam: the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution (August 1964) gave Johnson a blank cheque; escalation followed to over half a million US troops. The Tet Offensive (January 1968) was a military defeat for the Viet Cong but a political catastrophe for Johnson: it destroyed the credibility of official optimism.
  • 31 March 1968: Johnson announced he would not seek re-election. 1968 also brought the assassinations of King (April) and Robert Kennedy (June), and violence at the Chicago convention.

The examinable tension: the Great Society is the high point of the belief that the state could deliver the Dream. Vietnam destroyed the trust that made such a project possible — and 'guns and butter' together helped produce the inflation of the 1970s.

1968–1980 · the unravelling

Nixon, Watergate, stagflation, Carter

The 1970s are where the Dream's promises met their bill.

  • Nixon (1969–1974) appealed to the 'silent majority' and pursued Vietnamisation; the war spread into Cambodia (1970), provoking the Kent State shootings. The Paris Peace Accords (January 1973) ended US involvement; Saigon fell in April 1975.
  • Watergate: the break-in of June 1972, the cover-up, the tapes, and Nixon's resignation on 9 August 1974 — the deepest blow to trust in the presidency in modern American history.
  • Economics: the end of dollar–gold convertibility (1971) and the OPEC oil embargo (1973) produced stagflation — inflation and unemployment together, which the Keynesian consensus had said was impossible. The post-war boom was over.
  • Carter (1977–1981): energy crisis, inflation, and the Iran hostage crisis (from November 1979). His presidency became the emblem of national impotence.
  • Rights and backlash: Betty Friedan's The Feminine Mystique (1963) and the founding of NOW (1966); Roe v. Wade (1973). The Equal Rights Amendment, passed by Congress in 1972, was defeated by a conservative counter-mobilisation led by Phyllis Schlafly. In 1980 Ronald Reagan was elected — the New Right had arrived.

Interpretations · rewriting the movement

Top-down, bottom-up, and the 'long' movement

The historiography of 2Q has been reshaped in the last thirty years. Know how.

The classical narrative ran from Brown (1954) to the Voting Rights Act (1965), starred Martin Luther King Jr and the federal government, and treated Black Power as a tragic dead end.
The organising tradition. Charles Payne, I've Got the Light of Freedom (1995), shifted attention to local, grassroots organisers in Mississippi — figures such as Ella Baker and Fannie Lou Hamer. On this reading the movement was built from below, and national leadership arrived late to work already done.
The 'long civil rights movement'. Jacquelyn Dowd Hall (2005) argued that confining the movement to 1954–65 tames it: its roots lie in the labour and left activism of the 1930s and 1940s, and its unfinished economic agenda was cut short by anti-communism and later by the backlash. The tidy success-story chronology, she argued, is politically convenient.
The backlash and the New Right. Rick Perlstein (Nixonland, 2008) traced how the disorder of the 1960s was harvested electorally by the right — a reading that makes 1980, not 1968, the real turning point of the period.

Wider frame: behind all this sits the older quarrel between the consensus historians of the 1950s (a broadly harmonious, liberal America) and the New Left (a history of conflict, exclusion and empire). 2Q is designed to make you take a side, with evidence.

Exam technique · Component 2

The extract paper

AQA Component 2: 80 marks, 2 hours 30 minutes, 40%.

  • Section A (30 marks): three historians' extracts, evaluated for how convincing their arguments are — using your own precise knowledge, not adjectives about tone.
  • Sections B and C (25 marks each): two judgement essays from three.

2Q-specific advice: the question 'was the American Dream real?' is unanswerable in the aggregate — so always specify for whom, and when. A white suburban veteran in 1955 and a Black tenant farmer in Mississippi in 1955 inhabited different countries. Write that, with evidence, and you are arguing at Level 5.

Key question · women

The strand students forget

2Q covers women as well as race and class, and questions frequently demand them.

  • The 1950s: the post-war ideal pushed women out of wartime jobs and into the suburbs. Betty Friedan's The Feminine Mystique (1963) named the resulting dissatisfaction — and it sold in enormous numbers, which is itself evidence.
  • The breakthroughs: the Equal Pay Act (1963); Title VII of the Civil Rights Act (1964), which banned employment discrimination on grounds of sex; the founding of NOW (1966); and Roe v. Wade (1973).
  • The limit: the Equal Rights Amendment passed Congress in 1972 and then failed ratification, defeated by a formidable conservative counter-mobilisation led by Phyllis Schlafly — a reminder that the backlash was itself a mass movement, largely of women.

The comparative insight: the same decade delivered the Voting Rights Act and defeated the ERA. Change ran through the courts and federal statute where it could, and stalled where it needed state-by-state ratification. Institutions, not just attitudes, decide outcomes.
