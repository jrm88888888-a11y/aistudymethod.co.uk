Mission 1

Mission 1: With no one in charge, how do millions of buyers and sellers end up producing what people want?

Make a guess: With no one in charge, how do millions of buyers and sellers end up producing what people want?

Mission 1

No one is in charge

  • Every day, millions of buyers and sellers decide what to buy, make and sell.
  • No one gives the orders. Yet shops are mostly stocked with things people want.
  • The price mechanism: changes in prices, driven by demand and supply, decide how scarce resources are used.
  • It answers the basic questions: what to produce, how, and for whom.
  • Prices do three jobs: signalling, incentive and rationing.
Mission 1

Signalling

  • A price change is a signal: it carries information.
  • A rising price says: buyers want more than is on offer here. The good has become scarcer.
  • A falling price says: there is too much on offer. Use resources elsewhere.
  • Buyers and sellers need not know why the price changed (a drought, a fashion, a new use). The price alone tells them how to react.
  • Signals work in both directions: from buyers to producers, and from producers back to buyers.
Mission 1

Incentive (and the role of profit)

  • A higher price makes producing the good more profitable.
  • Firms already in the market produce more: quantity supplied rises (a movement along S).
  • Call this an extension of supply.
  • In time, new firms enter (market supply shifts right), and resources (workers, land, machines) move into the industry.
  • A falling price cuts profit. Firms produce less, and resources move out to better-paid uses.
  • Profit is the reward that drives this. Losses are the warning to leave.
  • Say: "the higher price gives existing firms an incentive to extend supply" — not: "the higher price shifts the supply curve".
Mission 1

Rationing

  • Scarce goods must be shared out somehow.
  • A higher price cuts quantity demanded: some buyers buy less, or stop buying.
  • Call this a contraction of demand.
  • The good goes to those willing and able to pay the price.
  • Price rationing removes a shortage without queues, lotteries or ration books.
  • But "able to pay" depends on income. Price rationing is not the same as giving goods to those who need them most.
  • Say: "a contraction of demand" — not: "demand falls", for a movement along D.
Quick check
?A frost destroys much of a country's orange harvest, and the price of oranges rises. Which statement describes the rationing function of price at work?

Mission 1

One shock, three jobs: Europe's gas, 2022

  • 2022: Russia cut pipeline gas supplies to Europe. Wholesale gas prices soared.
  • Signal: the price told every user and supplier that gas was now very scarce.
  • Incentive: suppliers shipped in more liquefied natural gas from other countries, by sea.
  • Rationing: factories and households cut their use.
  • Real data: EU gas use, August 2022 to March 2023, was 17.7% lower than the average for those months in 2017–2022 (Eurostat, 2023). The EU had set a voluntary target of 15%.
  • Caution: a mild winter and government campaigns to save energy also played a part. Price was not the only cause.
Mission 1

Local, national and global markets

  • Local: a town's market for haircuts or rented flats. Prices reflect local demand and supply.
  • National: a country's market for, say, new houses or nurses. Prices signal shortages in one region and pull resources in from others.
  • Global: crude oil, wheat, cocoa. One price signal reaches buyers and producers in every country within hours.
  • The same three functions work at each level.
  • But signals travel faster than resources. A global price can change in a day; a new mine or farm can take years.
Quick check
?A band sells 5,000 concert tickets at a price well below the equilibrium price (illustrative data). What is the most likely result?
Mission 1Evaluate

How well does the price mechanism allocate resources?

Strengths

  • Uses information spread across millions of people. No planner could gather it all (Hayek, 1945).
  • Automatic: signals and incentives work without orders.
  • Rewards efficiency and new ideas through profit.

Limits. It depends on …

  • Income distribution: price rations by ability to pay, so the poorest can be priced out of essentials.
  • Market failure: prices miss external costs and benefits (pollution), and public goods may not be provided at all.
  • Information and competition: monopoly power or poor information distort the signals.
  • Speed of response: factors move slowly (new skills, new mines), so prices can swing sharply.
  • Judge: a powerful way to coordinate choices, but its results are only as good as the prices it works with.
Mission check

?A new type of battery greatly increases demand for lithium. The price of lithium rises sharply. Which statement matches each function of price correctly?

One price change does three jobs at once: it informs, it rewards, and it shares out what is scarce.

Mission 2

Mission 2: When you would gladly pay more than the price, where does that extra value go?

Make a guess: When you would gladly pay more than the price, where does that extra value go?

Mission 2

Consumer surplus

  • Willingness to pay: the most a buyer would pay for a unit.
  • You would pay 15 for a concert ticket. It costs 10. You gain 5 that you never pay.
  • Consumer surplus = what buyers are willing to pay − what they actually pay.
  • The demand curve shows willingness to pay for each unit.
  • On a graph: the area below the demand curve and above the price, up to the quantity bought.
Mission 2

Producer surplus

  • Each seller has a minimum price they would accept for a unit: its extra cost.
  • The supply curve shows that minimum price for each unit.
  • Producer surplus = price received − minimum price the seller would accept.
  • On a graph: the area above the supply curve and below the price, up to the quantity sold.
  • Say: "producer surplus" — not: "profit". It ignores fixed costs.
Quick check
?Illustrative data: four buyers would each pay at most 12, 10, 8 and 6 for one unit of a good. The market price is 8. What is the total consumer surplus in this market?
Mission 2

Worked example: measuring the areas

  • Illustrative data. Demand meets the price axis at 16. Supply meets it at 4.
  • Equilibrium: price 12, quantity 40 units a week.
  • Both areas are triangles: ½ × base × height.
  • Consumer surplus = ½ × 40 × (16 − 12) = 80.
  • Producer surplus = ½ × 40 × (12 − 4) = 160.
  • Total (community) surplus = 80 + 160 = 240.
  • Height is always measured from the price line.
Mission 2

Total surplus is largest at equilibrium

  • Community surplus = consumer surplus + producer surplus.
  • Up to Qe, each unit is worth more to buyers than it costs to make: trading it adds surplus.
  • Beyond Qe, a unit costs more to make than buyers value it: trading it would reduce surplus.
  • So, with no market failure, a competitive market maximises total surplus at equilibrium.
  • This is allocative efficiency.
  • Lost total surplus from producing the wrong quantity is a deadweight loss.
Mission 2

When demand or supply shifts

  • Build the new equilibrium first, then redraw the areas.
  • Supply rises (costs fall): price falls, quantity rises.
    • Consumer surplus rises: existing buyers pay less, and new buyers join.
    • Producer surplus: the price is lower, but costs are lower and more units sell. With a parallel shift it rises too.
  • Demand rises: price and quantity rise.
    • Producer surplus rises: a higher price on more units.
    • Consumer surplus is measured under the new demand curve. With a parallel shift it rises too, even though price rose.
  • Demand or supply falls: the reverse.
  • Say: "an increase in supply" (a shift) — not: "an extension of supply" (a movement along the curve).
Mission 2Graph lab

Demand rises

Demand for the good rises (a parallel shift right). Supply does not change.

  • Takeaway: a parallel rise in demand raises both surpluses; the higher price is more than offset by more units and a higher demand curve.

Mission 2Evaluate

Is the biggest total surplus always best?

  • It depends on third parties: the areas count only buyers and sellers. External costs and benefits are left out.
  • It depends on who gains: a gain of 1 to a rich person counts the same as 1 to a poor person. Equity is a separate question.
  • It depends on ability to pay: willingness to pay reflects income as well as need.
  • It depends on measurement: real demand and supply curves are estimated, not observed.
Mission check

?Illustrative case: a city caps the number of taxi licences. Fewer rides take place than at the free-market equilibrium, and fares rise. Compared with the free-market equilibrium, what happens to total surplus (consumer plus producer surplus)?

The extra value buyers keep is consumer surplus. Moving surplus from buyers to sellers is a transfer; trades that never happen are a deadweight loss.

Mission 3

A tax on spending

  • An indirect tax is a tax on spending on goods and services.
  • The seller hands the tax to the government. Buyers pay it only through the price.
  • Examples: VAT and sales taxes; duties on fuel, alcohol and tobacco.
  • To the seller, the tax is like an extra cost of each unit sold.
Mission 3

Specific or ad valorem?

  • Specific tax: a fixed amount per unit (e.g. per litre of fuel).
    • S shifts up by the same amount at every quantity: parallel.
  • Ad valorem tax: a percentage of the price (e.g. VAT).
    • The tax per unit grows as the price rises: the new curve pivots, diverging from S.
  • Example (illustrative data), a 20% tax on the pre-tax price:
    • pre-tax price £5 → tax £1
    • pre-tax price £10 → tax £2
Quick check
?A government charges a tax of 15% on the pre-tax price of every phone sold. How does it change the supply curve?
Mission 3

The tax diagram

  • Build it in steps (a tax of t per unit):
  • 1. Start at equilibrium: Pe, Qe.
  • 2. Supply shifts up by the tax: S → S + tax. Vertical gap = t.
  • 3. New equilibrium: price buyers pay rises to P1; quantity falls to Q1.
  • Sellers keep P1 − t per unit.
  • The price rises by less than the tax (unless demand is perfectly inelastic or supply perfectly elastic).
  • Say: "supply decreases; there is a contraction of demand" — not: "demand falls".
Mission 3

Who bears the tax?

  • The incidence of a tax: how its burden is shared between consumers and producers.
  • Consumers' share per unit = P1 − Pe (the rise in the price they pay).
  • Producers' share per unit = Pe − (P1 − t) (the fall in the price they keep).
  • The two shares add up to the tax, t.
  • Government revenue = t × Q1: the rectangle between P1 and P1 − t, from 0 to Q1.
  • Who hands the money over (the formal incidence or impact) does not decide who bears it.

Mission 3

Worked example

  • Illustrative data. Before the tax: Pe = £6, Qe = 60 units.
  • Specific tax: £1.50 per unit. After the tax: consumers pay £7, Q1 = 50.
  • Producers keep £7 − £1.50 = £5.50.
  • Consumers' burden: (£7 − £6) × 50 = £50.
  • Producers' burden: (£6 − £5.50) × 50 = £25.
  • Government revenue: £1.50 × 50 = £75 (= £50 + £25 ✓).
  • Consumers bear 2/3 of the tax: demand here is less elastic than supply (PED −1, PES 2 at the start).
Quick check
?Illustrative data. Before a tax, a good sells at 5.00 per unit and 100 units are traded. After a tax of 2.00 per unit, consumers pay 6.20 and 76 units are traded. What is the total burden of the tax on producers?
Mission 3

Elasticity decides the split

  • The side of the market that is less price elastic bears more of the tax.
  • Inelastic demand (e.g. cigarettes, fuel): sellers raise the price and buyers keep buying, so consumers bear most.
  • Elastic demand: buyers switch away, so sellers must absorb the tax: producers bear most.
  • Extreme cases:
    • perfectly inelastic demand: consumers bear it all
    • perfectly elastic demand: producers bear it all
  • Approximate consumers' share = PES ÷ (PES + |PED|).
  • Say: "demand is price inelastic" — not: "demand is low".
Mission 3Graph lab

Shift the burden

Start: a tax of 1.50 per unit; PED −1, PES 2. Consumers bear 2/3 of each unit's tax.

  • Takeaway: less elastic demand → consumers bear a bigger share (here 8/9), and Q falls less.
Quick check
?A government wants to raise as much revenue as possible from a tax of the same amount per unit, with the smallest fall in quantity sold. Supply conditions are similar. Which good should it tax?
Mission 3

Stakeholders

  • Consumers: pay a higher price, buy less; consumer surplus falls.
  • Producers: keep less per unit, sell less; producer surplus falls. Revenue falls.
  • Workers: fewer units made can mean fewer jobs in the industry.
  • Government: gains revenue (t × Q1) to spend; can target goods it wants used less.
  • Society:
    • where the good has a negative externality, the tax can move output toward the social optimum
  • Indirect taxes are often regressive: lower-income households spend a larger share of income on them.
Mission 3

Case: the UK sugar levy

  • Soft Drinks Industry Levy, from April 2018, charged on producers and importers. Rates at the start:
    • 18p per litre: drinks with 5 g to 8 g of sugar per 100 ml
    • 24p per litre: 8 g or more
  • (Both rates have risen each year since April 2025.)
  • Below 5 g: no levy. So firms could avoid it by cutting sugar.
  • 2015 to 2018, average sugar in UK soft drinks fell from 4.4 g to 2.9 g per 100 ml.
  • Most of the fall came from reformulation, not from buyers switching.
  • Caution: the study shows a trend; it cannot prove the levy alone caused it.
Mission 3Evaluate

Does the tax work?

  • It depends on PED:
    • inelastic demand → high revenue, small fall in Q
    • elastic demand → bigger cut in consumption, less revenue
    • one tax rarely maximises both
  • It depends on information: to correct an externality, the tax should match the external cost. That is hard to measure.
  • It depends on time and side effects: PED grows in the long run; very high taxes can encourage smuggling and illegal markets.
  • Governments use elasticity estimates to predict both the revenue and the change in behaviour.
Mission check

?A city has a fixed number of parking spaces and no space to add more. The city now charges the owners a fixed tax for each space used per day. Demand for parking is unchanged. Who bears the tax?

The less elastic side bears more. When one side cannot respond at all, it bears the whole tax, whoever is legally charged.

Mission 4

Mission 4: When a government pays firms for every item they make, who ends up with the money?

Make a guess: When a government pays firms for every item they make, who ends up with the money?

Mission 4

A payment per unit

  • A subsidy is a payment from the government to producers that lowers their costs.
  • A specific subsidy pays a fixed amount per unit.
  • An ad valorem subsidy pays a percentage of the price.
  • Why governments use them:
    • lower prices of goods they want used more, e.g. public transport
    • support producers' incomes, e.g. farmers
    • raise output where there are external benefits
Mission 4

The subsidy diagram

  • Build it in steps (a subsidy of s per unit):
  • 1. Start at equilibrium: Pe, Qe.
  • 2. Supply shifts down by the subsidy: S → S − subsidy. Vertical gap = s.
  • 3. New equilibrium: the price buyers pay falls to P1; quantity rises to Q1.
  • Producers receive P1 + s per unit.
  • The price falls by less than the subsidy (unless demand is perfectly inelastic or supply perfectly elastic).
  • Say: "supply increases; there is an extension of demand" — not: "demand increases".
Mission 4

Who gains, and what it costs

  • Cost to the government = s × Q1: the rectangle between P1 + s and P1, from 0 to Q1.
  • That rectangle splits in two:
    • consumers' gain per unit = Pe − P1
    • producers' gain per unit = (P1 + s) − Pe
  • The two gains per unit add up to the subsidy, s.
  • This split is the incidence of the subsidy.

Mission 4

Worked example

  • Illustrative data. Before: Pe = £8, Qe = 60 units.
  • Specific subsidy: £3 per unit. After: consumers pay £6, Q1 = 70.
  • Producers receive £6 + £3 = £9.
  • Cost to government: £3 × 70 = £210.
  • Consumers' share: (£8 − £6) × 70 = £140.
  • Producers' share: (£9 − £8) × 70 = £70.
  • Consumers get 2/3: here demand is less elastic than supply (PED −0.67, PES 1.33 at the start).
Quick check
?Illustrative data. Before a subsidy, a good sells at 10.00 per unit and 200 units are traded. After a subsidy of 4.00 per unit, consumers pay 7.00 and 260 units are traded. What is the total cost of the subsidy to the government?
Mission 4

Elasticity decides the split

  • The side of the market that is less price elastic gets more of the subsidy.
  • Inelastic demand: the price falls a lot → consumers gain most.
  • Elastic demand: the price barely falls → producers keep most.
  • Extreme cases:
    • perfectly inelastic demand: consumers get it all; Q does not change
    • perfectly elastic demand: price unchanged; producers get it all
  • More elastic demand and supply → a bigger rise in Q (and a bigger bill).
  • Approximate consumers' share = PES ÷ (PES + |PED|).
Mission 4Graph lab

Who keeps the subsidy?

Start: a subsidy of 2 per unit; PED −0.5, PES 1. Consumers get 2/3 of each unit's subsidy.

  • Takeaway: more elastic demand → the price falls less, so producers keep more (consumers' share 1/3) and Q rises more.
Quick check
?A government subsidises a good to cut the price that buyers pay. In which market will buyers get the largest share of each unit's subsidy?
Mission 4

Stakeholders

  • Consumers: pay less, buy more; consumer surplus rises.
  • Producers: receive more per unit, sell more; producer surplus rises.
  • Workers: more output can mean more jobs in the industry.
  • Government: pays s × Q1. Opportunity cost: that money cannot fund schools or hospitals, or must come from taxes or borrowing.
  • Producers may come to depend on the subsidy, with less pressure to cut costs.
Mission 4

Case: paying the buyer instead

  • From 2023 to 30 September 2025, US buyers of qualifying new electric cars could claim a federal tax credit of up to $7,500.
  • This subsidy went to buyers, so it shifted demand up, not supply.
  • The split is the same: it depends on elasticities, not on who receives the payment.
  • If car supply is inelastic, sellers can raise prices and keep much of the credit.
Mission 4Evaluate

Is the subsidy worth it?

  • It depends on elasticities: with elastic demand, the price barely falls and most of the money goes to producers.
  • It depends on information: to correct an external benefit, the subsidy should match its size. That is hard to measure.
  • It depends on the opportunity cost: the same money could fund other spending or lower taxes.
  • It depends on time and side effects: subsidies are hard to remove once firms and voters rely on them; firms may stay inefficient.
Mission check

?A small country's wheat farmers sell all their wheat at the world price, which they are too small to change. The government pays farmers a subsidy for every tonne they grow. Who gains the subsidy?

Farmers can sell any amount at the world price, so the price does not fall. They receive the world price plus the whole subsidy.

Mission 5

The rational benchmark

  • Standard theory assumes consumers:
    • are rational: they weigh costs and benefits consistently;
    • maximise utility;
    • have perfect information;
    • are self-interested.
  • Behavioural economics uses psychology to test these assumptions against how people really choose.
  • Where people depart from the benchmark in predictable ways, economists call it a bias.
Mission 5

Bounded rationality

  • Bounded rationality: people try to choose well, but are limited by the information they have, the time they have and the brainpower they can spare.
  • So they often satisfice: choose an option that is "good enough" rather than the best.
  • Say: "bounded rationality" (limited ability to optimise) — not: "irrational" (no reasoning at all).
Mission 5

Weak at sums

  • Many shoppers are weak at computation: working out unit prices, interest or the value of an offer.
  • Example: "Buy one, get one half price" on two items costing 4 each.
    • Pay 4 + 2 = 6 for goods worth 8.
    • Discount = 2 ÷ 8 = 25%, not 50%.
  • Sellers can frame offers so they look bigger than they are.
Mission 5

Rules of thumb and habit

  • A rule of thumb (heuristic): a mental shortcut that saves time.
    • "Buy the brand I bought last time."
    • "Pick the second-cheapest item on the menu."
  • Shortcuts are often sensible, but they can lead to choices a fully informed optimiser would not make.
  • Habits: repeated choices made with little thought, e.g. the same coffee every morning. A price rise may barely change behaviour in the short run.
Mission 5

Anchoring

  • Anchoring: judgements are pulled towards an initial number (the anchor), even an irrelevant one.
  • Classic experiment (Tversky and Kahneman, 1974): people saw a "random" number, then estimated the percentage of African countries in the United Nations.
    • Group shown 10: median estimate 25%.
    • Group shown 65: median estimate 45%.
  • In shops: "Was 200, now 120" makes 120 look cheap.
Quick check
?A sofa shop displays a "Was 1,500" label above each sofa, crossed out, with the sale price of 900 underneath. Shoppers rate 900 as a bargain, even though similar sofas elsewhere cost 850. Which bias is the shop using?
Mission 5

Bounded self-control

  • Bounded self-control: people make plans for their future selves, then fail to stick to them.
  • Present benefits and costs loom larger than future ones (present bias).
  • Evidence (DellaVigna and Malmendier, 2006, US gyms):
    • Members on monthly contracts paid over $70 a month.
    • They went on average 4.3 times a month.
    • Cost per visit: more than $17, though a 10-visit pass cost $10 per visit.
  • When signing up, people overestimate how often their future self will go.
  • Other examples: saving too little for retirement; snacking against a diet plan.
Mission 5

Others' behaviour and social norms

  • Social norms: unwritten rules about what people around us do and approve of.
  • People copy others (herd behaviour): a long queue outside a restaurant signals "it must be good".
  • Fashion, smartphone choices and saving behaviour are all shaped by peers.
  • Demand then depends on what others buy, not only on price, income and own tastes.
Mission 5Evaluate

Should economists drop rationality?

  • It depends on stakes and repetition: with large stakes, practice and feedback, people learn and biases shrink.
  • It depends on market forces: firms that misprice lose to rivals, so some biases are offset at market level. Others are exploited (e.g. gym contracts).
  • It depends on predictability: biases that are systematic (anchoring, present bias) can be built into models and policy.
  • Some behavioural results have been hard to replicate; others, such as anchoring and present bias, are well established.
  • The rational model remains a useful benchmark: it shows what to compare real behaviour against.
Quick check
?A supermarket offers "3 for the price of 2" on cereal boxes that cost 3 each. What percentage discount does a shopper who buys 3 boxes get on the usual price?
Mission check

?Many workers say they should save more for retirement but do not start. In one scheme, workers sign up now to put part of each future pay rise into their pension; nothing leaves today's pay. Many more workers saved more under the scheme. Which bias does the design best get round?

People who mean well today often fail tomorrow; designs that commit the future self can close the gap.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The price mechanism allocates scarce resources with no one in charge: prices signal, give incentives and ration.
  • It works well when prices reflect all costs and benefits; it struggles with market failure and unequal incomes.

Mission 2

  • Consumer surplus: below demand, above price. Producer surplus: above supply, below price.
  • Community surplus is maximised at competitive equilibrium (MB = MC): allocative efficiency.

Mission 3

  • An indirect tax shifts supply up by the tax; the price rises by less than the tax and quantity falls.
  • The burden falls more on whichever side is less price elastic, whoever hands the money over.
  • Specific taxes shift S in parallel; ad valorem taxes pivot it.

Mission 4

  • A subsidy shifts supply down by the subsidy; the price falls by less than the subsidy and quantity rises.
  • The government pays s × Q1; the less elastic side of the market gets more of it.

Mission 5

  • Bounded rationality, bounded self-control and bounded selfishness are the three main limits to the rational model.
  • Biases such as rules of thumb, anchoring and availability are predictable departures from rational choice.
  • Weakness at computation and habits shape real spending.
