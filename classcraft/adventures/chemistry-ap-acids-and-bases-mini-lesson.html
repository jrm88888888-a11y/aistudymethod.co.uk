Mission 1Topics 8.1, 8.2

Mission 1: Lemon juice is about pH 2; black coffee is about pH 5. How many times more H⁺ is in the lemon juice?

Make a guess: Lemon juice is about pH 2; black coffee is about pH 5. How many times more H⁺ is in the lemon juice?

Mission 1

What pH measures

  • pH = −log[H3O⁺], with [H3O⁺] in M. pH has no units.
  • Rearranged: [H⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ.
  • Lower pH means higher [H⁺].
  • One pH unit is a tenfold change in [H⁺]; two units, a hundredfold.
  • Very concentrated solutions can have pH below 0 or above 14.
Mission 1

Non-integer pH: worked examples

  • [H3O⁺] = 3.2 × 10⁻⁴ M: pH = −log(3.2 × 10⁻⁴) = 3.49.
  • pH 4.60: [H3O⁺] = 10⁻⁴·⁶⁰ = 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ M.
  • Use the log key (base 10), not ln.
  • Sense check: pH 3.49 lies between 3 and 4, so [H⁺] lies between 10⁻⁴ and 10⁻³.
Calculate
?A sample of rainwater has [H3O⁺] = 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ M. Calculate its pH to two decimal places.
Quick check
?Hydrobromic acid, HBr, is a strong acid.
What is the pH of 4.0 × 10⁻³ M HBr(aq)?
Mission 1

Measuring pH two ways

  • Universal indicator (paper or solution): match the color to a chart to estimate pH, to about 1 unit.
  • pH meter or data-logger probe: a precise reading, usually to 0.01.
  • Calibrate the meter first with buffer solutions of known pH (for example pH 4, 7 and 10).
  • Rinse the probe with distilled water between solutions; let the reading settle before you record it.
Mission 1

Water ionizes, a little: Kw

  • 2H2O(l) ⇌ H3O⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq): Kw = [H3O⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C; pKw = 14.0.
  • Every aqueous solution contains both H⁺ and OH⁻. Neutral means [H⁺] = [OH⁻].
  • At 25 °C, neutral means pH = pOH = 7.0.
  • Kw changes with temperature, so neutral is pH 7 only at 298 K (25 °C). More in a later mission.
Mission 1

Strong base: pH from Kw

  • The product is fixed, so [H⁺] up tenfold means [OH⁻] down tenfold.
  • 0.020 M KOH dissociates fully: [OH⁻] = 0.020 M.
  • [H3O⁺] = Kw ÷ [OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ ÷ 0.020 = 5.0 × 10⁻¹³ M; pH = 12.30.
  • Sense check: a base, so pH is above 7 at 25 °C.
Mission 1

pOH, and group 2 hydroxides

  • pOH = −log[OH⁻]. At 25 °C, pH + pOH = 14.00 (pKw).
  • KOH again: pOH = −log(0.020) = 1.70; pH = 14.00 − 1.70 = 12.30.
  • Group 1 hydroxides give one OH⁻ per formula unit; group 2 hydroxides give two.
  • 0.0150 M Sr(OH)2: [OH⁻] = 0.0300 M, pOH = 1.52, pH = 12.48.
Quick check
?What is the pH of a 0.025 M solution of Ba(OH)2 at 25°C?
Mission check

?Two samples of rain are tested with a pH meter. Sample A has pH 4.2; sample B has pH 5.7. How does [H⁺] in sample A compare with [H⁺] in sample B?

Back to the video. Lemon juice (pH ≈ 2) and coffee (pH ≈ 5) differ by 3 units: 10³, so about 1000 times more H⁺ in the lemon juice.

Mission 2Topics 8.2, 8.3, 8.6

Mission 2: Can a strong acid have a higher pH than a weak acid?

Make a guess: Can a strong acid have a higher pH than a weak acid?

Mission 2

Strong and weak

  • Strong acids ionize completely; strong bases dissociate completely. Weak acids and bases ionize only slightly.
  • Strong acids: HCl, HBr, HI, HClO4, H2SO4, HNO3. Strong bases: group I and II hydroxides.
  • Weak: carboxylic acids (CH3COOH, HCOOH), NH3 and amines.
  • Strong: HNO3 → H⁺ + NO3⁻. Weak: CH3COOH ⇌ CH3COO⁻ + H⁺ (lies far to the left).
  • Weak base: NH3 + H2O ⇌ NH4⁺ + OH⁻, so [OH⁻] is far below the NH3 concentration.
Mission 2

Strength is not concentration

  • Concentration: amount of acid per unit volume. Strength: the extent of dissociation.
  • Diluting a strong acid raises its pH, but it stays strong.
  • At equal concentration, a weak acid has a higher pH and lower conductivity than a strong one.
  • At equal concentration, a weak base has a lower pH than a strong base.
  • A weak acid still neutralizes the same amount of base: as H⁺ is used up, more of the acid dissociates.
Quick check
?Two beakers at 25°C each contain 50 mL of an acid solution: one contains 0.10 M HCl(aq), and the other contains 0.10 M HA(aq), where HA is a weak monoprotic acid. Identical pieces of magnesium ribbon are dropped into the two beakers at the same moment. Which prediction about the first minute of the experiment is correct?
Quick check
?Two solutions are prepared at 25°C: 0.10 M NH3(aq), a weak base, and 0.10 M NaOH(aq). Which statement correctly compares the solutions?
Mission 2

Percent ionization

  • Percent ionization = [H3O⁺] at equilibrium ÷ [HA]0 × 100%.
  • Acetic acid, Ka = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵.
  • 0.10 M acetic acid: [H3O⁺] = 1.3 × 10⁻³ M, so 1.3% ionized.
  • 0.0010 M acetic acid: 13% ionized.
  • Diluting a weak acid raises its percent ionization: the equilibrium shifts toward the ions.
  • A strong acid is about 100% ionized at any concentration.
Mission 2

Which way does the equilibrium lie?

  • An acid–base equilibrium lies on the side of the weaker acid and weaker base.
  • The stronger the acid, the weaker its conjugate base.
  • HNO3 + H2O → H3O⁺ + NO3⁻ is effectively complete: NO3⁻ barely accepts H⁺.
  • HCOOH + H2O ⇌ HCOO⁻ + H3O⁺ lies left: H3O⁺ is a stronger acid than HCOOH.
Quick check
?Formic acid is a weak acid: HCOOH(aq) + H2O(l) ⇌ HCOO⁻(aq) + H3O⁺(aq). Which statement is correct?
Mission 2

Strength from structure

  • Strong acids have very weak, stable conjugate bases.
  • Electronegativity and induction: ClCH2COOH is stronger than CH3COOH. Cl pulls electron density, spreading the negative charge on the anion.
  • Resonance: in CH3COO⁻ the charge is shared over two O atoms. In CH3CH2O⁻ it sits on one, so ethanol barely acts as an acid in water.
  • Carboxylic acids are a common class of weak acid. NH3, amines and carboxylate ions are common weak bases.
Mission check

?Which statement about solutions P and Q is correct?
  • P is 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ M HCl(aq). Q is 0.50 M CH3COOH(aq), measured pH 2.53.

Yes. A dilute enough strong acid has a higher pH than a concentrated weak one. Strength and concentration are different things.

Mission 3Topics 8.1, 8.3, 8.5, 8.7

Mission 3: What is the pH of boiling pure water?

Make a guess: What is the pH of boiling pure water?

Mission 3

pOH, and the four-way switch

  • pOH = −log[OH⁻], just as pH = −log[H⁺].
  • At 25 °C, pH + pOH = 14.00, because Kw = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴.
  • Worked: [OH⁻] = 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ M → pOH 3.60 → pH 10.40 → [H3O⁺] = 4.0 × 10⁻¹¹ M.
Mission 3

Kw changes with temperature

  • H2O ⇌ H⁺ + OH⁻ is endothermic. Heat it: equilibrium shifts right, so Kw rises.
  • Kw: 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C; about 5.4 × 10⁻¹³ at 100 °C.
  • Boiling pure water: [H⁺] = [OH⁻], so [H⁺] = √Kw = 7.35 × 10⁻⁷. pH = 6.13.
  • Still neutral: neutral means [H⁺] = [OH⁻]. It means pH 7.00 only at 25 °C.
Calculate
?At 0 °C, Kw = 1.14 × 10⁻¹⁵. Calculate the pH of pure water at 0 °C.
Mission 3

Ka and pKa

  • Weak acid: HA + H2O ⇌ H3O⁺ + A⁻. Acid ionization constant Ka = [H3O⁺][A⁻] / [HA].
  • Ka is just the equilibrium constant for the dissociation. Water, the solvent, is left out.
  • pKa = −log10Ka, and Ka = 10⁻ᵖᴷᵃ. pKa has no units.
  • Larger Ka → smaller pKa → stronger weak acid (more dissociated).
  • Acetic acid: Ka = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵, so pKa = 4.74.
Quick check
?Benzoic acid has pKa = 4.20. What is its Ka?
Quick check
?At 25 °C the pKa of formic acid, HCOOH, is 3.75 and the pKa of acetic acid, CH3COOH, is 4.76.
Which statement about 0.10 M solutions of the two acids is correct?
Mission 3

Weak bases: Kb and pKb

  • Weak base: B + H2O ⇌ BH⁺ + OH⁻. Kb = [BH⁺][OH⁻] / [B].
  • pKb = −log10Kb. Larger Kb, smaller pKb: stronger base.
  • Ammonia: pKb 4.75. Methylamine: pKb 3.34, a stronger base.
Mission 3

Ka × Kb = Kw

  • Acid: HA + H2O ⇌ H3O⁺ + A⁻ (Ka). Its conjugate base: A⁻ + H2O ⇌ HA + OH⁻ (Kb).
  • Add the two equations: 2H2O ⇌ H3O⁺ + OH⁻. Adding reactions multiplies their K values.
  • So Ka × Kb = Kw, and pKa + pKb = pKw = 14.00 at 25 °C.
  • Formic acid, pKa 3.75: formate ion HCOO⁻ has pKb = 10.25.
  • Stronger acid, weaker conjugate base.
Quick check
?A student claims that for the conjugate acid-base pair HA and A⁻, Kb = 1/Ka, because the reaction of A⁻ with water is the reverse of the ionization of HA in water. Which statement best evaluates the student's claim?
Mission 3

pH vs pKa: which form wins?

  • From Ka: [A⁻] / [HA] = Ka ÷ [H3O⁺].
  • pH < pKa: HA dominates. pH > pKa: A⁻ dominates. pH = pKa: [HA] = [A⁻].
  • Formic acid (pKa 3.75) at pH 2.75: [HCOO⁻]/[HCOOH] = 0.10. Mostly HCOOH.
  • Titrating HA with a strong base: at the half-equivalence point half the HA has become A⁻, so [HA] = [A⁻] and pH = pKa.
Quick check
?Formic acid, HCOOH, has a pKa of 3.75. A formic acid solution is adjusted to pH 3.00 at 25°C. What is the ratio [HCOO⁻]/[HCOOH], and which form predominates?
Mission check

?What is the pH of pure water at this temperature, and is it neutral?
  • At 50 °C, Kw = 5.5 × 10⁻¹⁴.

Boiling pure water is about pH 6.1, and it is still neutral. Kw rises with temperature, so [H⁺] and [OH⁻] rise together.

Mission 4Topics 8.3–8.5

Mission 4: Why is a solution of sodium acetate alkaline when no hydroxide was put in?

Make a guess: Why is a solution of sodium acetate alkaline when no hydroxide was put in?

Mission 4

pH of a weak acid: two approximations

  • HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻, with Ka = [H⁺][A⁻]/[HA].
  • Approximation 1: x, the amount that ionizes, is small: [HA] ≈ [HA]0.
  • Approximation 2: [H3O⁺] = [A⁻] = x.
  • So Ka ≈ [H⁺]²/[HA], giving [H⁺] = √(Ka × [HA]). No quadratic needed.
Mission 4

Worked example: formic acid

  • 0.050 M HCOOH, pKa 3.75, at 25°C.
  • Ka = 10⁻³·⁷⁵ = 1.78 × 10⁻⁴.
  • [H⁺] = √(1.78 × 10⁻⁴ × 0.050) = 2.98 × 10⁻³.
  • pH = 2.53.
  • A strong acid at the same concentration: pH 1.30. Weak means fewer H⁺ ions.
Quick check
?A 0.10 M solution of a weak monoprotic acid, HA, is prepared at 25°C. The pKa of HA is 5.20. What is the pH of the solution?
Quick check
?A 0.20 M solution of a weak acid, HA, has pH 2.70 at 25°C. What is Ka?
Mission 4

Percent ionization

  • Percent ionization = [H3O⁺]eq ÷ [HA]0 × 100%.
  • 0.050 M HCOOH: 2.98 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.050 × 100% = 6.0%.
  • 0.100 M CH3COOH (Ka 1.74 × 10⁻⁵): [H3O⁺] = 1.32 × 10⁻³ M, so 1.3%.
  • Dilute a weak acid: percent ionization rises, because [H3O⁺] falls by a smaller factor than [HA]0.
  • If percent ionization is above about 5%, the "x is small" shortcut is less reliable.
  • An earlier screen is borderline: without the shortcut, its pH is 2.54, not 2.53.
Mission 4

pH of a weak base

  • B + H2O ⇌ BH⁺ + OH⁻. Same two approximations: [OH⁻] = √(Kb × [B]).
  • Then pOH = −log[OH⁻], and pH = 14.00 − pOH at 25°C.
  • Worked: 0.10 M CH3NH2, pKb 3.34. Kb = 4.57 × 10⁻⁴.
  • [OH⁻] = √(4.57 × 10⁻⁵) = 6.76 × 10⁻³. pOH 2.17, so pH = 11.83.
Quick check
?A 0.10 M solution of a weak base, B, is prepared at 25°C. The pKb of B is 4.40. What is the pOH of the solution?
Mission 4

Salt hydrolysis: the answer

  • Sodium acetate dissolves: CH3COONa(s) → Na⁺(aq) + CH3COO⁻(aq).
  • CH3COO⁻ is the conjugate base of a weak acid, so it takes H⁺ from water:
  • CH3COO⁻ + H2O ⇌ CH3COOH + OH⁻
  • OH⁻ is left behind, so [OH⁻] > [H⁺]: alkaline. No hydroxide was added; water supplied it.
  • Na⁺ comes from a strong base and does not react with water.
  • This reaction of an ion with water is hydrolysis.
Mission 4

Predicting the pH of a salt

  • Strong acid + strong base (NaCl, KNO3): neither ion hydrolyzes → pH 7.
  • Weak acid + strong base (CH3COONa, Na2CO3): anion takes H⁺ from water → pH > 7.
  • Strong acid + weak base (NH4Cl): NH4⁺ + H2O ⇌ NH3 + H3O⁺ → pH < 7.
  • The conjugate base's strength: Kb = Kw ÷ Ka. The weaker the acid, the more basic its salt.
Quick check
?Which 0.10 M salt solution has a pH above 7 at 25°C?
Mission 4

Mixing equal amounts of weak and strong

  • HA + OH⁻ → A⁻ + H2O goes to completion. Equal moles leave only A⁻.
  • A⁻ + H2O ⇌ HA + OH⁻: pH slightly basic. This is the equivalence point of a weak acid–strong base titration.
  • B + H3O⁺ → BH⁺ + H2O, equal moles: BH⁺ + H2O ⇌ B + H3O⁺: slightly acidic.
  • Steps: moles react → [A⁻] in the total volume → Kb = Kw/Ka → [OH⁻] = √(Kb[A⁻]) → pH.
  • Weak acid + weak base: HA + B ⇌ A⁻ + BH⁺ reaches equilibrium. Products are favored if HA is a stronger acid than BH⁺.
  • (Unequal amounts give a buffer or an excess of OH⁻/H3O⁺: see a later lesson.)
Quick check
?At 25°C, 50.0 mL of 0.20 M HA(aq), a weak acid with Ka = 4.0 × 10⁻⁶, is mixed with 50.0 mL of 0.20 M NaOH(aq). What is the pH of the resulting solution?
Mission check

?Sodium benzoate, C6H5COONa, is a food preservative. Benzoic acid is a weak acid. A solution of sodium benzoate in pure water is alkaline. Which equation explains why?

Same as sodium acetate. The anion of a weak acid takes H⁺ from water, so OH⁻ builds up without any hydroxide being added.

Mission 5Topic 8.4

Mission 5: Why can an antacid tablet make you burp?

Make a guess: Why can an antacid tablet make you burp?

Mission 5

Oxides and hydroxides: salt + water

  • In neutralization, a base accepts the H⁺ from an acid.
  • Acid + metal hydroxide → salt + water: KOH + HNO3 → KNO3 + H2O.
  • Acid + metal oxide → salt + water: MgO + 2HCl → MgCl2 + H2O.
  • Net ionic equation: H3O⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → 2H2O(l) (or H⁺ + OH⁻ → H2O).
  • A salt forms when the acid's H is replaced by a metal ion or another cation, such as NH4⁺.
  • Background: oxide and carbonate reactions are not AP essential knowledge, but they are proton transfers too.
Mission 5

Carbonates and hydrogen carbonates: CO2 too

  • Acid + carbonate or hydrogen carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide.
  • CO3²⁻ accepts two H⁺; HCO3⁻ accepts one.
  • K2CO3 + 2HNO3 → 2KNO3 + H2O + CO2.
  • Ionic: CO3²⁻ + 2H⁺ → H2O + CO2. HCO3⁻ + H⁺ → H2O + CO2.
  • Observation: fizzing as CO2 escapes; a solid carbonate disappears.
Mission 5

Ammonia, amines; and metals

  • Ammonia or an amine + acid → a salt only; no water forms.
  • NH3 + HNO3 → NH4NO3. CH3CH2NH2 + HCl → CH3CH2NH3Cl.
  • The N lone pair takes the H⁺; there is no OH⁻ or O²⁻ to make water.
  • Net ionic: NH3(aq) + H3O⁺(aq) → NH4⁺(aq) + H2O(l), a weak base with a strong acid.
  • Metal + acid → salt + hydrogen: Mg + 2HCl → MgCl2 + H2. That is redox, not neutralization.
Quick check
?Which reaction produces a salt and water, with no other product?
Mission 5

The parent acid and base of a salt

  • A salt's anion comes from its parent acid; its cation from its parent base.
  • KNO3: parent acid HNO3, parent base KOH.
  • (NH4)2SO4: parent acid H2SO4, parent base NH3.
  • HCOOK (potassium formate): parent acid HCOOH, parent base KOH.
  • Parents are the neutral acid and base, not the ions in the salt.
Quick check
?What are the parent acid and parent base of ammonium formate, HCOONH4?
Mission 5

Mixing a strong acid and a strong base

  • H3O⁺ + OH⁻ → 2H2O goes to completion. Whatever is in excess sets the pH.
  • 25.0 mL of 0.200 M HNO3 + 30.0 mL of 0.150 M KOH.
  • n(H3O⁺) = 0.0250 L × 0.200 M = 5.00 × 10⁻³ mol. n(OH⁻) = 0.0300 L × 0.150 M = 4.50 × 10⁻³ mol.
  • Excess H3O⁺: 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol in the total 0.0550 L: [H3O⁺] = 9.1 × 10⁻³ M.
  • pH = 2.04.
Mission 5

When one partner is weak

  • Weak acid + strong base: HA + OH⁻ → A⁻ + H2O goes to completion.
  • After mixing: HA left over → a buffer (pH from Henderson–Hasselbalch, in a later lesson). OH⁻ left over → pH from excess OH⁻ and total volume.
  • Equimolar: only A⁻ remains; A⁻ + H2O ⇌ HA + OH⁻ makes the solution slightly basic.
  • Weak base + strong acid mirrors this: B + H3O⁺ → HB⁺ + H2O; equimolar gives slightly acidic HB⁺.
  • Weak acid + weak base: HA + B ⇌ A⁻ + HB⁺ reaches an equilibrium, not completion.
Quick check
?At 25°C, 30.0 mL of 0.10 M HCl(aq) is mixed with 20.0 mL of 0.10 M NaOH(aq). What is the pH of the resulting solution?
Mission 5

The pH curve: strong acid, strong base

  • Flask: 25.0 mL of 0.100 M HCl. Buret: 0.100 M NaOH.
  • Starts at the flask's pH, 1.00, and rises slowly.
  • Close to 25.0 mL, pH jumps from about 3 to about 11.
  • Equivalence point: amounts match the equation; here pH 7 (at 298 K, 25 °C), mid-jump.
  • After it, excess NaOH builds up: pH rises slowly toward 13 (about 12.5 at double the volume).
Mission check

?Baking powder contains sodium hydrogen carbonate and a solid acid. When the cake batter gets wet, the two react. Why does the cake rise?

Antacid tablets with CaCO3 do the same: CaCO3 + 2HCl → CaCl2 + H2O + CO2. The CO2 gas is what you burp up.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • pH = −log10[H⁺]; one unit is a tenfold change in [H⁺].
  • Kw = [H3O⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C; pH + pOH = 14.00.

Mission 2

  • Strong: completely dissociated; weak: only slightly. Strength is not concentration.
  • Equal concentrations: weak acid, higher pH. Different concentrations: anything can happen.
  • An acid–base equilibrium lies toward the weaker acid and weaker base.

Mission 3

  • Ka = [H⁺][A⁻]/[HA]; pKa = −log10Ka. Smaller pKa: stronger acid.
  • Kw rises with temperature: pure water is always neutral, but pH 7.00 only at 298 K.
  • Ka × Kb = Kw; pH < pKa favors HA, pH > pKa favors A⁻.

Mission 4

  • Weak acid: [H⁺] = √(Ka × [HA]), assuming little dissociation and [H⁺] = [A⁻].
  • Weak base: [OH⁻] = √(Kb × [B]); percent ionization = [H3O⁺]/[HA]0 × 100%.
  • Salt of a weak acid + strong base → pH > 7; strong acid + weak base → pH < 7; strong + strong → pH 7.

Mission 5

  • Oxides and hydroxides: salt + water. Carbonates: salt + water + CO2. Ammonia and amines: salt only.
  • Metal + acid gives H2, but that is redox.
  • Mixing acid and base: react first, then find what is left over; it sets the pH.
