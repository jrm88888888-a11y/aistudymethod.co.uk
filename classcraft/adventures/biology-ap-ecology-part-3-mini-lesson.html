Mission 1

Mission 1: The carbon atoms in your body were once in the air. How do they keep being recycled?

Make a guess: The carbon atoms in your body were once in the air. How do they keep being recycled?

Mission 1

Where carbon is stored

  • Carbon sits in stores (reservoirs):
  • the atmosphere, as CO2;
  • the oceans, as dissolved CO2 and hydrogencarbonate ions;
  • living organisms, as carbon compounds;
  • dead organic matter and soil;
  • fossil fuels, peat and rocks such as limestone.
  • Biotic reservoir: living organisms. Abiotic reservoirs: atmosphere, oceans, rocks, fossil fuels.
  • Carbon atoms move between stores. None is made or destroyed.
Mission 1

The processes that move it

  • Photosynthesis removes CO2 from air or water and fixes it in producers.
  • Feeding passes carbon compounds along food chains.
  • Respiration in every organism, producers included, returns CO2.
  • Decomposition: decomposers respire the carbon in dead matter and feces, returning CO2.
  • Combustion of fuels and biomass releases CO2.
  • Cycles are interdependent: photosynthesis uses water as well as CO2, linking the carbon and water cycles.
  • Say: "plants respire all the time" — not: "plants photosynthesize instead of respiring".
Quick check
?In a forest, carbon is found in CO2 in the atmosphere, in the wood of living trees, and in limestone rock beneath the soil. Which statement correctly classifies these stores of carbon?
Mission 1

Decomposers unlock the dead

  • Dead organisms and feces lock carbon and other elements inside large organic molecules.
  • Decomposers (saprotrophic fungi and bacteria) secrete enzymes, digest the matter outside their cells, absorb the products and respire them: CO2 is released.
  • They also release mineral ions, such as ammonium and phosphate, that plants can absorb.
  • So all the elements living things need are recycled, not just carbon.
  • No decomposers, no recycling: carbon builds up in dead matter.
  • Example: waterlogged soil lacks O2, so decay is very slow and peat forms.
  • Say: "decomposers return carbon to the air as CO2" — not: "decomposition puts the carbon into the soil".

Mission 1

Slow stores: oceans, rocks and fuels

  • CO2 dissolves in the oceans. Marine organisms build calcium carbonate shells; their remains form limestone.
  • Where decomposition was incomplete (waterlogged, little O2), plant remains became peat and, over millions of years, coal.
  • Oil and natural gas formed from marine plankton buried in sediments with little O2.
  • Combustion returns that stored carbon to the air as CO2 very quickly.
  • Say: "fossil-fuel carbon was fixed by photosynthesis" — not: "fossil fuels are just minerals".
Mission 1

The water cycle

  • Reservoirs: oceans, surface water (lakes, rivers), the atmosphere (water vapor), living organisms.
  • Processes: evaporation, condensation, precipitation, transpiration.
  • Transpiration: water taken up by roots evaporates from leaves. Most of the water a plant absorbs returns to the air this way.
  • Land cover changes the cycle: forests return much water as vapor; paved ground sends more off as runoff.
  • Water is conserved: every millimeter of rain is stored or leaves by some route.

Mission check

?A peat bog has stored carbon for thousands of years in waterlogged soil. It is drained for farmland, and the peat dries out. What happens to much of the carbon in the peat?

Carbon only keeps moving while something moves it: photosynthesis takes it in; respiration, decomposition and combustion send it back.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why can a warming of just a degree or two force species to move, or vanish from a place?

Make a guess: Why can a warming of just a degree or two force species to move, or vanish from a place?

Mission 2

Species on the move

  • Each species survives only within a band of conditions: temperature, rainfall, season length.
  • As the climate warms, that band shifts toward the poles and up mountains.
  • New populations establish at the cool edge; old ones die out at the warm edge. The range shifts.
  • This happens over generations, through dispersal and the survival of young, not by individuals migrating.
  • No cooler place to go (mountain tops, poles), or barriers in the way? Local extinction, or extinction of the species.
  • Climate change, like other geological and meteorological events, changes habitats and where ecosystems are found.
  • Say: "the range shifts" — not: "the range grows" or "the animals move house".
Quick check
?The bird species of a mountain in New Guinea were surveyed in 1965 and again in 2012. For most species both the upper and the lower limits of the altitudinal range had moved upslope, by about 100 m on average, over a period in which the region warmed. What is the best interpretation?
Mission 2

Warmer seas: upwelling fails

  • Phytoplankton need mineral nutrients that rise (upwell) from cold, deep water.
  • Warm surface water is less dense: it forms a stable layer that blocks upwelling.
  • Less phytoplankton → less energy for zooplankton, fish and seabirds.
  • El Niño: every few years, warm water spreads across the eastern Pacific and upwelling off South America weakens. Fish populations fall; rainfall patterns on land shift.
  • Other geological and meteorological events also reshape habitats: volcanic eruptions, continental drift, and the meteor impact linked to the extinction of the dinosaurs.
Quick check
?Off the coast of Peru, cold nutrient-rich water normally rises to the surface. In some years the surface water is unusually warm, and the catch of anchovies falls sharply. Which explanation is correct?
Mission check

?A hypothetical small mammal lives only in cool meadows near the top of an isolated mountain. Over 40 years the region warms by 1.5 °C. Which outcome is most likely?

A degree or two moves the conditions a species needs. Species that can follow survive in new places; those with nowhere to go are lost.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Nearly four-fifths of the air is nitrogen. So why do crops so often run short of it?

Make a guess: Nearly four-fifths of the air is nitrogen. So why do crops so often run short of it?

Mission 3

Atoms go round; energy flows through

  • Every element an organism needs is recycled: carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus and the rest.
  • Energy is not recycled. It leaves each trophic level as heat.
  • Decomposers break down dead matter and wastes. They return elements as simple inorganic ions that plants can absorb.
  • Each cycle has abiotic reservoirs (air, water, soil, rock) and biotic reservoirs (organisms). Processes move matter between them.
Quick check
?Which statement about the recycling of chemical elements in ecosystems is correct?
Mission 3

Nitrogen cycle 1: getting nitrogen in

  • The air is about 78% N2. Its triple bond is very strong: plants cannot use N2.
  • Nitrogen fixation: bacteria reduce N2 to ammonia (NH3).
  • NH3 ionizes to ammonium (NH4⁺) by gaining H⁺ from the soil solution. The atmosphere is the largest nitrogen reservoir.
  • Ammonification: saprobionts turn the nitrogen in proteins, nucleic acids and urea of dead matter and wastes into ammonia / ammonium ions (NH4⁺).
Mission 3

Nitrogen cycle 2: nitrate, and back to the air

  • Nitrification: ammonium → nitrite (NO2⁻) → nitrate (NO3⁻). An oxidation: needs oxygen.
  • Assimilation: plants take up NH4⁺ and NO3⁻ and build them into proteins and nucleic acids; consumers get nitrogen by eating.
  • Denitrification: bacteria convert nitrate back to N2. Happens in anaerobic soil, e.g. waterlogged.
  • Say: "nitrifying bacteria make nitrate from ammonium" — not: "nitrifying bacteria fix nitrogen".
Quick check
?A field is flooded for several weeks and the soil becomes waterlogged. Afterward, the nitrate content of the soil is much lower. Which process best explains the loss?
Mission 3

Partners that feed plants

  • Mutualism: a relationship between two species in which both benefit.
  • Root nodules of legumes (peas, beans, clover: the Fabaceae): Rhizobium bacteria fix N2. Plant gets ammonia / amino acids; bacteria get sugars and shelter.
  • Say: "bacteria in the nodules fix nitrogen" — not: "legumes fix nitrogen themselves".
Mission 3

The phosphorus cycle

  • Phosphorus is in ATP, DNA, RNA and phospholipids.
  • Weathering of rocks releases phosphate ions (PO4³⁻) into soil and water.
  • Producers absorb phosphate (helped by mycorrhizae); consumers get it by eating.
  • Excretion and decomposition return phosphate to the soil.
  • Some is washed into the sea and forms sediments, which become rock over millions of years.
  • No major gas phase: unlike carbon and nitrogen, there is no atmospheric reservoir. Phosphate returns to soil only slowly.
Quick check
?In a hypothetical forest, phosphate enters the soil mainly from the slow weathering of rock. Every 20 years, the trees are harvested and the wood is removed instead of being left to decompose. Which prediction about the soil's phosphate over many harvests is best supported?
Mission 3

Leaching and eutrophication

  • Leaching: rain washes soluble nitrate and phosphate out of the soil into rivers and lakes.
  • The nutrient no longer limits growth: algal bloom at the surface.
  • Algae block light; plants below die. Algae die too.
  • Aerobic bacteria (decomposers) multiply and decompose the dead matter.
  • Their respiration raises BOD (biochemical oxygen demand) and lowers dissolved oxygen.
  • Fish and other aerobic animals die.
  • Say: "BOD rises, so dissolved oxygen falls" — not: "BOD falls" or "the fertilizer poisons the fish".
Quick check
?Which flowchart best represents the effect on fish of eutrophication caused by fertilizer runoff into a lake?
Mission check

?A farmer grows clover, a legume, in a field for a year, then ploughs the clover into the soil. Wheat sown next needs much less nitrogen fertilizer than wheat sown after wheat. Which explanation is correct?

Air is full of nitrogen, but plants can use it only after microorganisms have converted it. That is why crops run short.

Mission 4

Mission 4: How does water reach the top of the tallest tree with no pump and no energy spent by the tree?

Make a guess: How does water reach the top of the tallest tree with no pump and no energy spent by the tree?

Mission 4

Transpiration: the price of carbon dioxide

  • Stomata open to let carbon dioxide in for photosynthesis.
  • Water evaporates from the walls of mesophyll cells into the air spaces.
  • Water vapor diffuses out through the open stomata: transpiration.
  • Transpiration is an unavoidable consequence of gas exchange.
  • Transpiration moves water from plants into the atmosphere as vapor.
  • Evaporation from the leaves pulls water up through the xylem; cohesion (hydrogen bonds between water molecules) keeps the column unbroken. No pump, no ATP: the Sun's energy drives it.
  • Say: "water vapor diffuses out while stomata are open" — not: "the plant opens stomata to lose water".
Mission 4

Plants in the water cycle

  • Reservoirs of water: oceans, surface water, the atmosphere, living organisms.
  • Processes: evaporation, condensation, precipitation, transpiration.
  • Roots take up water from the soil; most of it leaves the leaves as vapor.
  • So plant cover returns water to the atmosphere that would otherwise run off.
  • Water is not lost from the cycle: a little is split in photosynthesis and remade in respiration, but almost all simply passes through.
Quick check
?During a summer, a large tree takes up thousands of liters of water from the soil through its roots. Which statement best describes what happens to most of this water?
Mission check

?In a tall tree on a sunny day, water evaporates from the leaves and the whole column of water in the xylem moves upward. Which statement best explains how the water reaches the top without a pump?

Trees lift water from the soil and release it as vapor: the pull from the leaves, carried down an unbroken column held together by cohesion.

Mission 5

Mission 5: How can people keep taking food and materials from nature without wrecking it?

Make a guess: How can people keep taking food and materials from nature without wrecking it?

Mission 5

Biomagnification

  • Some pollutants are persistent (not broken down) and fat-soluble (stored in fat, not excreted).
  • A consumer eats many prey and retains the pollutant from all of them.
  • So the concentration rises at each trophic level: highest in top predators.
  • DDT (insecticide): thin eggshells in birds of prey; populations crashed in the 1950s–60s; banned in many countries from the 1970s.
  • Biomagnification can drive populations of top predators toward extinction.
  • Persistent pesticides sprayed to protect crops are one way food production harms wildlife far from the field. Using nature sustainably means keeping such impacts within what ecosystems can absorb, e.g. by banning or replacing persistent chemicals.
  • Say: "the concentration increases up the food chain" — not: "top predators take in more from the water".
Quick check
?Illustrative data, simplified from the pattern in a real estuary.
In a study of an estuary, DDT concentrations were measured in parts per million (ppm): water 0.00005, plankton 0.04, small fish 0.5, larger predatory fish 2, fish-eating birds 25. Which statement explains this pattern?
Mission 5

The Earth changes ecosystems too

  • Geological events: continental drift, volcanic eruptions, asteroid impacts.
  • Meteorological events: hurricanes, floods, droughts, ice ages.
  • These change habitats and shift where ecosystems are found.
  • Example: about 66 million years ago an asteroid impact blocked sunlight; food webs collapsed and many species, including non-avian dinosaurs, went extinct.
  • Biogeography (where species live) records these changes: Australia drifted away from other continents long ago, and marsupials dominate its mammals.
  • Human impact speeds up change: species have less time to adapt or move.
Quick check
?About 66 million years ago, a large asteroid struck Earth. Dust and other particles thrown into the atmosphere are thought to have blocked much of the sunlight reaching Earth's surface for months or longer. Predict the most likely effect on ecosystems during that time.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Carbon moves between stores by photosynthesis (removes CO2), feeding, and respiration, decomposition and combustion (all add CO2).
  • Decomposers recycle carbon and every other element; without them, carbon piles up in dead matter such as peat.
  • Biogeochemical cycles have abiotic and biotic reservoirs; the water cycle runs by evaporation, condensation, precipitation and transpiration.

Mission 2

  • Warming shifts the conditions species need poleward and upslope; ranges follow over generations, and species with nowhere to go face local extinction.
  • Warmer surface water blocks nutrient upwelling, cutting primary production in the oceans.

Mission 3

  • Elements are recycled by microorganisms; energy is not. Plants cannot use N2 until bacteria fix it.
  • Fixation (N2 → NH3/NH4⁺), ammonification (organic N → NH4⁺), nitrification (NH4⁺ → NO2⁻ → NO3⁻, aerobic), denitrification (NO3⁻ → N2, anaerobic).
  • Leached nutrients → algal bloom → decomposition → BOD up, oxygen down → fish die.

Mission 4

  • Transpiration returns most of the water a plant takes up to the atmosphere.

Mission 5

  • Persistent, fat-soluble pollutants biomagnify: concentration rises at each trophic level.
