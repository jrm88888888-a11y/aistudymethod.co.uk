Mission 1: With no one in charge, how do millions of buyers and sellers end up producing what people want?

Mission 1

No one is in charge

  • Every day, millions of buyers and sellers decide what to buy, make and sell.
  • No one gives the orders. Yet shops are mostly stocked with things people want.
  • The price mechanism: changes in prices, driven by demand and supply, decide how scarce resources are used.
  • It answers the basic questions: what to produce, how, and for whom.
  • Prices do three jobs: signaling, incentive and rationing.
Mission 1

Signaling

  • A price change is a signal: it carries information.
  • A rising price says: buyers want more than is on offer here. The good has become scarcer.
  • A falling price says: there is too much on offer. Use resources elsewhere.
  • Buyers and sellers need not know why the price changed (a drought, a fashion, a new use). The price alone tells them how to react.
  • The equilibrium price is information: it tells producers and consumers how to allocate resources.
Mission 1

Incentive (and the role of profit)

  • A higher price makes producing the good more profitable.
  • Firms already in the market produce more: quantity supplied rises (a movement along S).
  • In time, new firms enter (market supply shifts right), and resources (workers, land, machines) move into the industry.
  • A falling price cuts profit. Firms produce less, and resources move out to better-paid uses.
  • Profit is the reward that drives this. Losses are the warning to leave.
  • Say: "the higher price gives existing firms an incentive to raise quantity supplied" — not: "the higher price shifts the supply curve".
Mission 1

Rationing

  • Scarce goods must be shared out somehow.
  • A higher price cuts quantity demanded: some buyers buy less, or stop buying.
  • The good goes to those willing and able to pay the price.
  • Price rationing removes a shortage without queues, lotteries or ration books.
  • But "able to pay" depends on income. Price rationing is not the same as giving goods to those who need them most.
  • Say: "quantity demanded falls" — not: "demand falls", for a movement along D.

Mission 1

One shock, three jobs: US eggs

  • Bird flu forced US egg producers to cull millions of hens.
  • Supply of eggs decreased. The price rose to a record $6.23 a dozen (BLS average price, grade A large, March 2025).
  • Signal: the price told buyers and producers that eggs were scarce.
  • Incentive: a high price made it profitable to restock flocks and to import eggs. The US imported 218 million shell eggs in January–March 2025, more than 20 times as many as a year earlier (Congressional Research Service, 2025).
  • Rationing: households and bakeries bought fewer eggs (a decrease in quantity demanded).
  • No one ordered any of this. The price carried the information.
Mission 1

Markets need rules

  • Markets work only where property rights are well defined and enforced: you can own, use and sell something, and stop others taking it.
  • Without them, no one will plant, build or invest: the reward could be taken.
  • Example: fish in open waters belong to no one. Each boat has an incentive to catch as much as it can, before others do.
  • Buyers and sellers respond to incentives, but face constraints:
  • income (a budget), time (hours in the day) and legal limits (laws, for example age limits on some purchases).
  • Prices guide choices within these constraints.

Mission 2

Why the curve slopes down

  • Diminishing marginal utility: each extra unit consumed adds less satisfaction than the one before.
  • So a buyer is willing to pay less for the 4th cup than for the 1st. To sell more, the price must fall.
  • When the price of a good falls, two effects raise quantity demanded:
  • Substitution effect: the good is now cheaper relative to other goods, so buyers switch toward it.
  • Income effect: the same money income now buys more, so real income (purchasing power) rises and buyers can afford more.
  • Say: "the price fall raises real income" — not: "the price fall raises people's income".
Quick check

Mission 2

Incentives and constraints

  • Markets work when property rights are well defined: you can own, use and sell what you buy.
  • Buyers respond to incentives: a lower price makes a good more attractive.
  • But choices face constraints: income, time and the law.
  • Demand records what buyers choose within those constraints.
Mission 2

Movement or shift?

  • A change in the good's own price → a movement along D.
  • A change in anything else that affects buyers → a shift of the whole curve.
  • Step 1: price falls from P1 to P2 → slide along D from point a to point b.
  • Step 2: D1 → D2 to the right: more is demanded at every price.
  • Say: "a change in quantity demanded" for a movement; "a change in demand" for a shift.

What supply means

  • Supply: the quantity that producers are willing and able to sell at each price, over a given time period.
  • Law of supply: as price rises, quantity supplied rises, ceteris paribus.
  • Why? A higher price gives sellers an incentive to produce more: extra output becomes more profitable.
  • Illustrative data: a strawberry farm.

Mission 3

From one firm to the market

  • Individual supply: one producer's schedule. Market supply: all producers together.
  • At each price, add the quantities of every producer: a horizontal sum.
  • Illustrative data: at a price of 6, Farm A supplies 4 metric tons and Farm B supplies 3 metric tons → market supply 7 metric tons.
  • More firms → market supply rises at every price.

?At a price of 5, three bakeries supply 40, 30 and 20 loaves a day. A fourth bakery opens and supplies 30 loaves at that price. What is market quantity supplied at a price of 5 now?
Mission 3

Movement or shift?

  • A change in the good's own price → a movement along S.
  • A change in anything else that affects producers → a shift of the whole curve.
  • Step 1: price rises P1 → P2 → slide up along S.
  • Step 2: S1 → S2 to the right = increase in supply: more offered at every price.
  • A shift left = decrease in supply.
  • Say: "an increase in supply shifts the curve right" — not: "the supply curve shifts up". A rightward shift looks lower on the graph.
  • Say: "a change in quantity supplied" for a movement; "a change in supply" for a shift.

Mission 4

Measuring how strongly buyers react

  • A price rises 10%. Do buyers cut back by 1%? Or by 30%?
  • Price elasticity of demand (PED) measures how responsive quantity demanded is to a change in the good's own price.
  • PED = %ΔQd ÷ %ΔP
  • Percentages, not units, so we can compare goods measured in liters, tickets or metric tons.
  • Price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions, so PED is negative.
  • AP usually reports PED as an absolute value (e.g. 0.4).
Mission 4

Worked example: the midpoint method

  • Illustrative data. A café raises the price of a coffee from $2.50 to $3.00. Sales fall from 400 to 340 cups a day.
  • Midpoint method: divide each change by the average of the old and new values.
  • %ΔQd = (340 − 400) ÷ 370 × 100 = −16.2%
  • %ΔP = (3.00 − 2.50) ÷ 2.75 × 100 = +18.2%
  • PED = 16.2 ÷ 18.2 ≈ 0.89 (absolute value). Less than 1: demand is inelastic.
  • The midpoint method gives the same answer whether price rises or falls between the two points.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A shop cuts the price of a phone case from $8 to $6. Weekly sales rise from 200 to 300. Using the midpoint method, what is the price elasticity of demand?

Mission 4

Slope is not elasticity

  • Illustrative data. Straight-line demand: P = 10 − Q. Every 1 off the price adds 1 to quantity. The slope never changes.
  • Price 8 → 7 (−12.5%): Q 2 → 3 (+50%). PED = −4. Elastic.
  • Price 3 → 2 (−33.3%): Q 7 → 8 (+14.3%). PED = −0.43. Inelastic.
  • Same 1-unit changes, very different percentages.
  • Point PED = (ΔQ ÷ ΔP) × (P ÷ Q). At P = 5, Q = 5: PED = −1 × 5 ÷ 5 = −1.
  • Upper half: elastic. Midpoint: unitary. Lower half: inelastic.
  • The minus signs here show direction. As absolute values: 4 near the top, 1 (unit elastic) at the midpoint, 0.25 at P = 2, Q = 8.
  • Say: "demand is relatively inelastic over this price range" — not: "the curve is steep, so demand is inelastic".

Mission 4

What makes demand elastic or inelastic?

  • Substitutes: more and closer substitutes → more elastic. Buyers can switch.
  • Necessity or luxury: necessities and habit-forming goods → less elastic.
  • Share of income: a small share of the budget (salt, matches) → less elastic.
  • Time: buyers find substitutes over time, so demand is more elastic in the long run.
  • How widely the market is defined: one brand of bread has many substitutes; "food" has none. Narrow → more elastic.
  • Say: "demand for gasoline is price inelastic" — not: "gasoline is inelastic".
Mission 4

Real evidence: US gasoline

  • Real data (Hughes, Knittel and Sperling, 2008). Short-run PED for gasoline (gasoline) in the US:
  • 1975–1980: −0.21 to −0.34
  • 2001–2006: −0.034 to −0.077
  • In 2001–2006, a 10% price rise cut gasoline use by less than 1% in the short run.
  • Both periods: inelastic. But demand became less elastic.
  • Think: which determinants on the previous screen might explain it? (How easy is it to switch away from driving? How big a share of income is fuel?)
  • Long run: buyers can change cars, homes and jobs, so demand is more elastic than in the short run.

Mission 4

Elasticity decides who feels a shift

  • Supply decreases (S1 → S2), for example after a crop failure.
  • Draw two demand curves through the same starting point. The steeper one is the less elastic at that point.
  • Less elastic demand: price rises a lot, quantity falls a little.
  • More elastic demand: price rises a little, quantity falls a lot.
  • Compare steepness only at a shared point: slope alone is not elasticity.
  • So the size of the change in price and quantity after a shift depends on the elasticities.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. A city raises the price of tap water from 2.00 to 2.20 per cubic meter. Average household use falls from 150 to 147 liters a day. Which statement is correct?

Buyers react strongly only when they have good substitutes, time to find them, or a big share of their budget at stake.

Mission 5

Revenue is the buyers' spending

  • Total revenue (TR) = price × quantity sold.
  • What sellers receive, buyers spend: TR = total expenditure.
  • On a demand curve, TR is the rectangle under the chosen point: height P, width Q.
  • Example (illustrative data): 1,000 tickets at $4 → TR = $4,000.
Mission 5

Two effects pull in opposite directions

  • Raise the price and two things happen:
  • Price effect: each unit still sold earns more → TR up.
  • Quantity effect: fewer units are sold → TR down.
  • Which effect is bigger? Compare the percentage changes.
  • That comparison is exactly what PED measures.
Mission 5

The revenue rule

DemandPED (absolute value)Price risesPrice falls
Price elasticgreater than 1TR fallsTR rises
Unitary (unit) elasticequal to 1TR unchangedTR unchanged
Price inelasticless than 1TR risesTR falls
  • Inelastic: price and TR move the same way.
  • Elastic: price and TR move opposite ways.
  • This is the total revenue test. AP often reports PED as an absolute value.
  • Say: "demand is price inelastic, so a price rise increases total revenue" — not: "a price rise always increases revenue".
Mission 5

Worked example: the total revenue test

  • Illustrative data. A bus company raises the fare from $4 to $5. Tickets sold fall from 1,000 to 900 a day.
  • Midpoint method: %ΔQd = (900 − 1,000) ÷ 950 × 100 = −10.5%.
  • %ΔP = (5 − 4) ÷ 4.5 × 100 = +22.2%.
  • PED = 10.5 ÷ 22.2 ≈ 0.47 (absolute value) → inelastic.
  • Total revenue test: TR goes from $4 × 1,000 = $4,000 to $5 × 900 = $4,500.
  • Price up, TR up: the same direction confirms inelastic demand.
Quick check
?A cinema raises its ticket price from $8 to $10, and tickets sold fall from 60 to 40 a night. Using the midpoint method, which statement is correct?
Mission 5

Revenue diagrams: gain and loss areas

  • Price rises from P1 to P2. Compare two rectangles:
  • Gain: (P2 − P1) × Q2, the extra paid on units still sold.
  • Loss: P1 × (Q1 − Q2), revenue on the units no longer sold.
  • Inelastic demand (steep over this range): small fall in Q → gain > loss → TR rises.
  • Elastic demand (flat over this range): big fall in Q → loss > gain → TR falls.
  • From an earlier screen (illustrative data): gain $1 × 900 = $900; loss $4 × 100 = $400; TR up $500.
  • Say: "demand is relatively inelastic over this price range" — steepness alone is not elasticity.

Mission 5

Along a straight-line demand curve

  • On a straight-line demand curve, PED changes along the line (from Mission 4).
  • Top half: elastic. Cutting price raises TR.
  • Bottom half: inelastic. Cutting price lowers TR.
  • Midpoint: unit elastic. TR is at its maximum.
  • Illustrative data, P = 10 − Q: TR = 16 at P = 8, 25 at P = 5, 16 at P = 2.
  • Total revenue is maximized where demand is unit elastic.
Quick check
?A straight-line demand curve meets the price axis at 12 and the quantity axis at 12 (illustrative data). The firm charges a price of 8 and sells 4 units. It is thinking of cutting the price to 6. Which statement is correct?
Mission 5

Why firms care

  • Inelastic demand (few close substitutes, a necessity, brand loyalty): a price rise raises TR.
  • Elastic demand (many substitutes): price cuts and discounts can raise TR.
  • Firms try to make demand less elastic: branding, advertising, loyalty schemes.
  • Revenue is not profit. Selling fewer units also cuts total costs, so with inelastic demand a price rise usually raises profit too.
  • Say: "total revenue" — not: "profit", when you apply the revenue rule.
Mission check

?A city bus company cuts all its fares by 20%. The number of journeys taken each week rises by 10%. What happens to fare revenue, and what does this show?

A price cut raises revenue only when the quantity bought rises by a bigger percentage than the price falls.

Mission 6

What PES measures

  • Price elasticity of supply (PES): how strongly quantity supplied responds to a change in price.
  • PES = %ΔQs ÷ %ΔP
  • Price up → quantity supplied up. So PES is normally positive.
  • A bigger number = a stronger response.
  • Say: "an increase in quantity supplied" — not: "an increase in supply" (that is a shift).
Mission 6

  • AP thresholds: elastic > 1, unit elastic = 1, inelastic < 1.
Mission 6

Worked example

  • Illustrative data. A furniture maker's price rises from $20 to $25. Quantity supplied rises from 400 to 450 tables a month.
  • Simple method: 12.5% ÷ 25% = 0.5.
  • Midpoint method: %ΔQs = 50 ÷ 425 = 11.8%; %ΔP = 5 ÷ 22.5 = 22.2%.
  • PES = 11.8 ÷ 22.2 ≈ 0.53 → inelastic (less than 1).
Quick check
?A firm supplies 1,200 units a week. The market price rises by 8%, and the firm's PES is 2.5. How many units a week will it now supply?
Mission 6

What makes supply elastic?

  • Time: the longer firms have to respond, the more elastic supply is.
  • Spare capacity: idle machines and staff → output can rise fast.
  • Stocks and storage: goods held in stock, or that can be stored, can be released quickly.
  • Mobility of factors: workers, machines and land that can switch from other uses → more elastic.
  • Price of alternative inputs: if firms can switch to other inputs, or draw extra inputs from other uses, at little extra cost, supply is more elastic.
Mission 6

Short run vs long run

  • Momentary (very short) run: output is fixed. Supply is vertical (PES = 0). E.g. fish already landed today.
  • Short run: firms use spare capacity, overtime and stocks. Supply is inelastic.
  • Long run: firms build capacity, and new firms enter. Supply becomes more elastic.
  • The same rise in demand → in the short run mostly a price rise; in the long run more of a quantity rise.
  • AP: supply is usually more elastic in the long run.

Mission 6

Real world: cocoa, 2024

  • Cocoa beans come mainly from West Africa.
  • Poor West African harvests in the 2023–24 season cut supply.
  • In March 2024, cocoa futures in New York passed $10,000 a metric ton for the first time. Prices had roughly doubled since January.
  • Why could other growers not fill the gap? Newly planted cocoa trees take a few years to bear beans. In the short run, PES is very low.
  • Long-run response: growers elsewhere (e.g. Brazil, Ecuador) are working to expand production.

Mission 7

Elasticity for any cause of demand

  • PED measures how quantity demanded responds to a good's own price.
  • The same idea works for any determinant of demand.
  • Income elasticity of demand (YED) = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in income.
  • Cross elasticity of demand (XED) = % change in quantity demanded of good A ÷ % change in the price of good B.
  • AP calls it the cross-price elasticity of demand.
  • A change in a determinant other than the good's own price shifts the demand curve.
Mission 7

Normal and inferior goods

  • The sign of YED tells you the type of good.
  • YED > 0: normal good. Income rises → demand rises (shifts right). E.g. clothes, holidays, eating out.
  • YED < 0: inferior good. Income rises → demand falls (shifts left). E.g. supermarket value ranges, instant noodles.
  • Why? With more income, people switch to alternatives they prefer.
  • The same good can be normal for one group and inferior for another.
  • Say: "inferior" means demand falls as income rises — not: poor quality.
Mission 7Graph lab

Income rises

Average incomes rise 10%. Demand for a value-range pasta has a YED of −0.5.

  • Takeaway: a rise in income shifts demand for a normal good right and for an inferior good left.
Mission 7

Worked example: calculating YED

  • Illustrative data: weekly household income rises from 500 to 520.
  • Instant noodles bought each week fall from 20 packs to 18.
  • % change in income = (520 − 500) ÷ 500 × 100 = +4%.
  • % change in quantity demanded = (18 − 20) ÷ 20 × 100 = −10%.
  • YED = −10 ÷ +4 = −2.5.
  • Negative: an inferior good. Strongly responsive (size 2.5).
  • Always put quantity on top.

?Average incomes rise by 5%. Other things equal, quantity demanded of good X rises by 2%. Which is correct?
Mission 7

Cross elasticity: related goods

  • XED = % change in Qd of good A ÷ % change in price of good B.
  • XED > 0: substitutes. Price of coffee rises → demand for tea rises.
  • XED < 0: complements. Price of games consoles falls → demand for games rises.
  • XED = 0 (or close): unrelated. E.g. pens and bus fares.
  • Size shows how close the link is: +3 means close substitutes; +0.2 means weak substitutes.
  • Say: "a rise in the price of B shifts the demand curve for A" — not: "a movement along A's demand curve".
Mission 7Graph lab

The price of games consoles falls by 20%.

  • Takeaway: a change in a related good's price shifts demand. Complements move the opposite way to the price; substitutes move the same way.
Mission 7

Worked example: calculating XED

  • Illustrative data.
  • Price of coffee rises 10%. Quantity of tea demanded rises 5%.
  • XED = +5 ÷ +10 = +0.5. Positive: substitutes (weak ones).
  • Price of consoles falls 20%. Quantity of games demanded rises 30%.
  • XED = +30 ÷ −20 = −1.5. Negative: complements (strong ones).
  • Signs matter: write the % change in price with its sign.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The price mechanism allocates scarce resources with no one in charge: prices signal, give incentives and ration.
  • Equilibrium price is information that guides resource allocation, in markets built on well-defined property rights.

Mission 2

  • Demand is the whole schedule or curve; its own price moves you along it, every other factor shifts it.
  • Price change: change in quantity demanded; determinant change: change in demand.
  • The curve slopes down because of diminishing marginal utility.
  • A price fall also works through the substitution effect and the income effect.

Mission 3

  • Supply slopes up because a higher price makes extra output profitable; own price moves you along S, every other factor shifts it.
  • Increase in supply = shift right (it looks lower), decrease = shift left.

Mission 4

  • PED = %ΔQd ÷ %ΔP: it measures how strongly quantity demanded responds to price. Size above 1: elastic; below 1: inelastic.
  • Substitutes, necessity, share of income, time and market definition decide how elastic demand is.
  • Slope is not elasticity: along a straight-line demand curve PED changes from elastic to inelastic.
  • With elastic demand, a shift in supply changes quantity more and price less.

Mission 5

  • When demand is price inelastic, price and total revenue move the same way; when it is elastic, they move in opposite ways; at unit elasticity TR is unchanged.
  • The total revenue test: if price and TR move in the same direction, demand is inelastic.

Mission 6

  • PES = %ΔQs ÷ %ΔP: below 1 is inelastic, above 1 is elastic.
  • Supply is more elastic with spare capacity, stocks, mobile factors and, above all, more time.

Mission 7

  • YED = % change in Qd ÷ % change in income. Positive: normal. Negative: inferior.
  • XED = % change in Qd of A ÷ % change in price of B. Positive: substitutes. Negative: complements. Zero: unrelated.
