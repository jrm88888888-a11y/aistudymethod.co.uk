Mission 1

Three levels of biodiversity

  • Biodiversity: the variety of life, at three levels.
  • Ecosystem (habitat) diversity: the range of habitats or ecosystems, e.g. sand dune, woodland, meadow, stream.
  • Species diversity: the number of species and how evenly the individuals are spread among them.
  • Genetic diversity: the variety of alleles within a species.
  • Endemic species occur naturally in only one place. Every lemur species is endemic to Madagascar.
Quick check
?A survey of an island records three findings: the island contains six distinct ecosystems; 420 species have been identified; and its population of one lizard species carries 18 alleles at a studied gene, compared with 4 alleles in the same species on a neighbouring island. Which of these findings describe the island's biodiversity?
Mission 1

Richness and evenness

Illustrative data: two meadows, same four species, 40 plants each.

SpeciesMeadow AMeadow B
Buttercup1031

Daisy103
Plantain103
  • Species richness: the number of species. Both meadows: 4.
  • Species evenness: how evenly individuals are spread among the species. A is even; B is dominated by buttercup.
  • Species diversity needs both. Meadow A is more diverse.
  • Say: "diversity depends on richness and evenness" — not: "diversity is the number of species".
Mission 1

The index of diversity

  • An index of diversity combines the number of species with the number of individuals of each.
  • d = N(N − 1) ÷ Σn(n − 1)
  • Edexcel writes it as D: same formula.
  • N = total number of organisms of all species; n = number of organisms of each species.
  • Lowest value 1 (only one species). Higher value = more diverse.
  • Meadow A: 4.33. Meadow B: 1.65.
Mission 1

Worked example

Illustrative data: pitfall traps in a wood. Species P 12, Q 6, R 2.

  • N = 12 + 6 + 2 = 20. N(N − 1) = 20 × 19 = 380.
  • Σn(n − 1) = (12 × 11) + (6 × 5) + (2 × 1) = 132 + 30 + 2 = 164.
  • d = 380 ÷ 164 = 2.32.
  • Compare it only with a site sampled by the same method and effort.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A rock pool holds limpets 8, barnacles 6, periwinkles 4 and sea anemones 2. Using d = N(N − 1) ÷ Σn(n − 1), what is the index of diversity?
Mission 1

Sampling for an index

  • You cannot count every organism. Take samples that represent the habitat.
  • Random sampling: random-number coordinates on a grid; quadrats of equal size. Avoids bias.
  • Take many samples. One quadrat misses species and shows nothing about variation.
  • Analyse random data: calculate an index for each habitat, then compare.
  • Compare two sites only with the same method and effort.
Quick check
?Ecologists want to compare the species diversity of the plant communities in two meadows by calculating an index of diversity, N(N − 1) ÷ Σn(n − 1), for each. Which procedure is most appropriate?
Mission check

?Illustrative data. Two hedgerows were sampled for insects with the same method and effort. Each had the same 6 species and 120 insects in total. In hedgerow X, one species made up 100 of the 120 insects. In hedgerow Y, each species had 20 insects. Which statement is correct?

Counting species is only half the story. How evenly the individuals are shared out decides which habitat is more diverse.

Mission 2

Genetic diversity

  • Genetic diversity: the variety of alleles within a species or population.
  • Every member of a species has the same genes. They differ in which alleles they carry.
  • Small, isolated or captive populations often have low genetic diversity.
  • Say: "different alleles of the same genes" — not: "different genes".
Quick check
?Cheetahs (Acinonyx jubatus) show extremely little genetic variation: unrelated cheetahs are so alike that skin grafts between them are not rejected. In a reserve, cheetah numbers have been stable for 20 years, and the species present and their relative abundances have not changed. Which statement about biodiversity in the reserve is correct?
Mission 2

Heterozygosity index

  • H = number of heterozygotes ÷ number of individuals in the population
  • Count heterozygotes at a locus. Many heterozygotes = many different alleles in circulation.
  • Illustrative example: 50 animals tested at one locus; 18 heterozygous.
  • H = 18 ÷ 50 = 0.36.
  • Higher H = more genetic diversity. H can be averaged over several loci.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. 80 voles from an island population were tested at one locus. 20 were heterozygous, 45 homozygous dominant and 15 homozygous recessive. What is the heterozygosity index, H?
Mission 2

Why genetic diversity matters

  • Environments change: new diseases, new climates, new chemicals.
  • High genetic diversity: some individuals are more likely to carry alleles that let them survive. The population persists.
  • Low genetic diversity: if one is susceptible, nearly all are. The population can crash.
  • Example: California condors fell to 22 birds in 1982. All living condors descend from the 27 taken into captivity by 1987.
  • The variety of life is extensive, but human activity now threatens it (see Mission 3).
  • Say: "the population is more likely to contain resistant individuals" — not: "each individual is more resistant".
Quick checkRead the data
?Real data (Zhu et al. 2000). In Yunnan, China, farmers planted rows of disease-susceptible rice varieties among disease-resistant varieties in every rice field of 5 townships (1998) and 10 townships (1999). Plots of the susceptible varieties grown alone (monoculture) were the comparison.
Which conclusion is best supported?
  • Rice blast (a fungal disease) on the susceptible varieties: 94% less severe in mixtures.
  • Yield of the susceptible varieties: 89% greater in mixtures.
Mission check

?Almost all bananas in international trade are one variety, Cavendish. Every plant is grown from a piece of another plant, so all are genetically identical. A fungal wilt disease that kills Cavendish plants is spreading between plantations. Why are biologists concerned for the whole crop?

Numbers do not protect a population; variety does. Genetic diversity is a population's insurance against change.

Mission 3

Extinction, then and now

  • Species have always gone extinct, at a slow background rate.
  • Today the rate is far higher. Vertebrate species lost since 1900 would have taken 800–10,000 years to disappear at the background rate (depending on the group).
  • Over time, the variety of life has become extensive. It is now being threatened by human activity.

The report estimates that about 1 million animal and plant species face extinction. Which statement is supported by the data?

Mission 3

What is driving the losses?

  • Overarching cause: human population growth. It drives every specific cause.
  • Habitat loss: deforestation, clearance for farming, urbanisation, drainage of wetlands.
  • Over-exploitation: hunting, overfishing, collecting.
  • Pollution: pesticides, fertiliser run-off, plastics.
  • Invasive species, pests and diseases, spread by global transport.
Quick check
?Which factor is the overarching cause of the current biodiversity crisis, driving each of the specific causes?
Mission check

?In the early 1800s the passenger pigeon was probably the most numerous bird in North America, with flocks of billions. Through the 1800s it was hunted on a commercial scale, and the eastern forests where it fed and nested were cleared for farming. The last known bird died in a zoo in 1914. Which explanation is best supported?

Extinction is natural; this pace is not. Human activity is removing species far faster than new ones arise.

Make a guess: If the last of a species survives safely in a zoo, has that species really been saved?

Mission 4

Why conserve biodiversity?

  • Ecological: species depend on each other. Losing a keystone species changes the whole community.
  • Genetic resource: wild relatives of crops carry alleles for disease resistance and drought tolerance.
  • Economic: food, timber, medicines, tourism; healthy soils (continuous monoculture depletes them).
  • Aesthetic: landscapes and wildlife people value and enjoy.
Mission 4

In situ conservation

  • In situ: protecting a species in its natural habitat.
  • Nature reserves and wildlife reserves.
  • Reserves need active management: control invasive species and poaching; keep habitats suitable.
  • Advantage: the species keeps its habitat, its interactions and its natural selection pressures.
Mission 4

Ex situ conservation

  • Ex situ: conserving a species outside its natural habitat.
  • Zoos: captive breeding; studbooks pair unrelated animals to limit inbreeding; reintroduction.
  • Botanic gardens: living collections of rare plants; propagation; research.
  • Seed banks: seeds dried and frozen, then germinated and tested at intervals. Kew's Millennium Seed Bank holds seeds of over 40,000 species.
Quick check
?Seeds of many tropical rainforest trees are killed by the drying and freezing used in seed banks. A conservation programme wants to conserve one such tree species, whose natural forest is being logged. Which approach to conserving this species is appropriate?

?A zoo has bred a rare antelope for 30 years from six founding animals. Which is the strongest scientific concern about releasing their descendants into the wild?
Mission check

?A rare orchid survives in one wild meadow. Its seeds are now stored in a seed bank, and plants grow in a botanic garden. The meadow is due to be ploughed. Orchid seeds need a particular soil fungus to germinate. Which conclusion is best supported?

A species kept only in a zoo or a seed bank survives, but it is not truly saved. Conservation needs the habitat as well.

Mission 5

What is an adaptation?

  • An adaptation is an inherited feature that increases an organism's chance of surviving and reproducing in its environment.
  • It arises by natural selection: random variation exists; individuals with a useful allele survive and breed more; the allele becomes more common over generations.
  • Individuals do not adapt in their lifetime. Populations change over generations.
  • An adaptation helps in one environment; the same trait may be useless or harmful in another.
  • A species' niche is its role in its habitat: the resources it uses and the conditions it tolerates. Adaptations suit a species to its niche.
  • Say: "individuals with the allele survived and bred more" — not: "the fox adapted because it needed to".
Mission 5

Three kinds of adaptation

  • Anatomical: body structure. Fennec fox: huge ears with blood vessels near the surface radiate heat.
  • Physiological: body processes. Fennec fox: kidneys conserve water; it gets most of its water from food.
  • Behavioural: what it does. Fennec fox: active at night; shelters in a burrow by day.
  • Most organisms show all three, each meeting the same challenge.
Quick check
?Which adaptation is classified correctly?
Quick check
?A hypothetical grass species grows both on soil contaminated with zinc around an old mine and in nearby uncontaminated fields. When grown on zinc soil, plants from the mine site survive, but plants from the fields die. When grown on normal soil, plants from the mine site grow more slowly than plants from the fields. Which reasoning best explains why zinc tolerance is common at the mine site but rare in the fields?
Mission check

?Arctic foxes have much smaller ears than fennec foxes. Which statement best explains how small ears became typical of Arctic foxes?

The fennec's big ears and the Arctic fox's small ones arose the same way: selection acting on inherited variation, in different environments.

Mission 6

Levels of ecology

  • Population: all the organisms of one species in one area at one time.
  • Community: all the populations of every species in an area: plants, animals, fungi and bacteria.
  • Ecosystem: a community plus its abiotic (non-living) environment.
Mission 6

Habitats and ecosystems of every size

  • Habitat: the place where an organism, population or community lives.
  • Ecosystems range in size from very small to very large: a rock pool, a playing field, a large tree, a lake, an ocean.
  • The answer to the hook: each is a community plus its non-living surroundings. Size does not matter.
Quick check
?Two groups of great crested newts breed in ponds 5 km apart, and no newt ever moves between the ponds. How should the two groups be described?
Mission 6

Abiotic and biotic factors

  • Abiotic factors: non-living. Temperature, light, water, pH, salinity, oxygen, wind.
  • Biotic factors: living. Food, predators, competitors, parasites, pathogens.
  • Both control where a species is found (distribution) and how many (abundance).
Quick check
?A student finds a woodland plant only on the shaded side of a hedge. Which pair names one abiotic factor and one biotic factor that could explain this?
Mission check

?A fallen, rotting log in a wood holds beetle larvae, woodlice, fungi and bacteria. Inside it are damp, decaying wood and still air. Which statement is correct?

Like a rock pool and a rainforest, the log is living things plus the non-living world they depend on. That is all an ecosystem needs.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Species diversity = richness (number of species) + evenness (how individuals are spread).
  • d = N(N − 1) ÷ Σn(n − 1): higher = more diverse.

Mission 2

  • Genetic diversity is the variety of alleles in a population; high diversity makes it more likely that some individuals survive a change.
  • H = heterozygotes ÷ individuals.

Mission 3

  • Today's extinction rate is far above the background rate, driven by human activity: habitat loss, over-exploitation, pollution, invasive species and climate change.
  • Human population growth is the overarching cause.

Mission 4

  • In situ conservation keeps species and their interactions in the wild; ex situ (zoos, botanic gardens, seed and tissue banks) is insurance. No single approach is enough.

Mission 5

  • An adaptation is an inherited feature, favoured by natural selection, that improves survival and reproduction in a particular environment.
  • Adaptations are anatomical (or morphological), physiological or behavioural.

Mission 6

  • Population (one species) → community (all species) → ecosystem (community + abiotic environment); ecosystems can be any size.
