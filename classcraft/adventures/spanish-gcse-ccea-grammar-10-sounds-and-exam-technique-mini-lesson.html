Exam technique

Grammar wins marks — but only if you know where and how the CCEA papers ask for it. This lesson maps out the four units, the controlled speaking assessment, the two translation directions, and the one habit that saves the most marks: working out a word you've never seen.

Work through each screen and answer the questions as you go — some ask you to recognise the right approach, some are mini-tasks where you type the answer. Collect ⭐ stars and press Start.

The shape of the exam

Four units, 25% each

CCEA GCSE Spanish is assessed across four units, and each one carries exactly a quarter of your final grade. No single skill dominates — plan your revision evenly:

unitskill · weighting
Unit 1Listening — 25%
Unit 2Speaking (controlled assessment) — 25%
Unit 3Reading — 25%
Unit 4Writing — 25%

Why it matters: because each unit is worth the same, a strong Reading paper can't rescue a weak Speaking one. The good news — the grammar spine you've just worked through feeds all four.

Unit 1 · Listening

Listening — comprehension

You listen to recordings and answer comprehension questions. A couple of features are worth knowing before you sit it:

  • Answer in English or Spanish. Some questions accept a response in either language — read the rubric so you know which is being asked for.
  • Four common questions sit on both the Foundation and Higher papers — they act as a bridge between the tiers. If you're borderline, these are exactly the items that carry across.
  • You control the pauses. Read the questions first, listen for the key detail each one asks for, and don't panic if a whole sentence rushes past — you only need the answer.

Listening habit: before the recording starts, underline the question word — ¿cuándo? (when), ¿dónde? (where), ¿por qué? (why). It tells you what kind of detail to listen out for.

Unit 2 · Speaking

Speaking — a controlled assessment

This unit is different from the others: it is a controlled assessment, conducted by your teacher and recorded. It has two parts:

  • Two role-plays — both drawn from one "Context for Learning".
  • A general conversation on two topics — one topic from each of the other two Contexts. So across the whole assessment your three Contexts are all represented.

The prepared advantage: Conversation Topic 1 is prepared from a pre-released Context — you know the area in advance, so build and rehearse strong material (opinions, tenses, connectives) before the day.

Delivery tips: lift your marks by weaving in more than one tense (a past, a present, a future/conditional), a clear opinion (creo que…, en mi opinión…) and a reason (porque…). Because it's recorded, keep talking — a short silence costs fluency marks.

Unit 3 · Reading

Reading — comprehension + translation into English

The Reading unit tests how carefully you handle Spanish on the page. Two things happen:

  • Comprehension. You answer questions on texts — again, some responses may be in English, some in Spanish; check each rubric.
  • Translation Spanish → English. You render a short Spanish passage into accurate English. Translate faithfully — every word carries a mark, so don't paraphrase or drop details.

Translation-into-English habit: translate the whole sentence, then re-read your English on its own — does it make natural sense? If a phrase reads oddly, you've probably mistranslated a small word (a tense ending, a negative, a no).

Unit 4 · Writing

Writing — short responses, translation + extended task

The Writing unit has three kinds of task. Know exactly what each asks:

  • Short responses. Brief written answers — cover the point precisely; you don't need a paragraph.
  • Translation English → Spanish. Render English sentences into accurate Spanish. Watch word order, agreement (gender/number) and verb endings — this is where your grammar spine pays off.
  • Extended writing. One structured extended task, from a choice. Pick the option you have the most language for, cover every bullet, and reach for a range of tenses and opinions to hit the top bands.

Translation-into-Spanish checklist: after each sentence, ask three things — does the adjective agree, does the verb ending match the subject, and is the word order Spanish (not word-for-word English)?

Beyond the word list

Expect some unfamiliar vocabulary

CCEA sets a "Core Minimum" vocabulary list — but it is a floor, not a ceiling. You will meet words that aren't on it, especially in Reading and Listening. That's deliberate: deducing meaning from context is an assessed skill.

  • Use the sentence around it. The other words, the topic and the punctuation all box in what the new word can mean.
  • Spot cognates. Many words look like English — el problema (problem), la información (information), necesario (necessary).
  • Break the word down. A prefix, a familiar root or a verb ending you know can reveal the rest — reciclar (re- + a cycle word → "to recycle").

Golden rule: never leave a gap. A sensible guess from context often earns the mark; a blank never does.

Recap

Your exam-technique toolkit

Four units, 25% each: Unit 1 Listening · Unit 2 Speaking · Unit 3 Reading · Unit 4 Writing.

Unit 1 Listening: comprehension; some answers in English or Spanish; four common questions bridge Foundation & Higher.

Unit 2 Speaking (controlled assessment, teacher-conducted, recorded): two role-plays from one Context + a general conversation on two topics, one from each of the other two Contexts; Topic 1 is prepared from a pre-released Context.

Unit 3 Reading: comprehension + translation Spanish→English.

Unit 4 Writing: short responses + translation English→Spanish + one structured extended task, from a choice.

Unfamiliar vocabulary: the Core Minimum list is a floor, not a ceiling — deduce from context, spot cognates, and never leave a gap.
