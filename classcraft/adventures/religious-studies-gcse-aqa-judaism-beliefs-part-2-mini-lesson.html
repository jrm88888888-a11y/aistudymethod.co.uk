Mission 1

Mission 1: A baby rescued from a river became the man Jews call "our teacher". What did God give through him?

Make a guess: A baby rescued from a river became the man Jews call "our teacher". What did God give through him?

Mission 1

Moses: from the Nile to Midian

  • The Israelites, Abraham's descendants, are slaves in Egypt. Pharaoh orders every Hebrew baby boy to be thrown into the Nile.
  • Moses' mother hides him in a basket among the reeds. Pharaoh's daughter finds him and raises him (Exodus 2:5–10).
  • His name: "Because I drew him out of the water." (Exodus 2:10)
  • Grown up, he sees an Egyptian beating a Hebrew and kills him. He flees to Midian and becomes a shepherd.
  • Jews call him Moshe Rabbenu, "Moses our teacher".
Mission 1Read the source

The burning bush

At Mount Horeb (Sinai), Moses sees a bush that "burned with fire, and the bush was not consumed" (Exodus 3:2). God sends him to free Israel.

> "And Moses said unto God: 'Who am I, that I should go unto Pharaoh, and that I should bring forth the children of Israel out of Egypt?'" (Exodus 3:11)

  • God answers: "Certainly I will be with thee" (Exodus 3:12).
  • Moses asks God's name. God says: "I AM THAT I AM" (Exodus 3:14), and "The LORD, the God of your fathers, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob" (Exodus 3:15).

Q1 Why does Moses hesitate?

Show answer

He feels unworthy: "Who am I…?"

Q2 How does verse 15 link Moses to Abraham?

Show answer

The same God of the covenant with Abraham, Isaac and Jacob is sending him.

Mission 1

The Exodus

  • Pharaoh refuses to let the people go. Ten plagues strike Egypt.
  • On the night of the last plague the Israelites eat the first Passover meal and leave.
  • Exodus tells how God parts the Sea of Reeds; they cross on dry land.
  • Exodus means "going out". For Jews it shows God keeping the promise to Abraham's family and acting in history to free them.
  • Jews relive it every year at the Pesach seder and remember it in daily prayers and the Kiddush on Shabbat.

Mission 1

The covenant at Sinai

  • The Torah tells how, at Mount Sinai, God offers a covenant to the whole people, through Moses.
  • God's promise: "ye shall be Mine own treasure from among all peoples" (Exodus 19:5); protection, and the land promised to Abraham.
  • The people's duty: "if ye will hearken unto My voice indeed, and keep My covenant" (Exodus 19:5): live by God's law, the Torah.
  • "Moses wrote all the words of the LORD" (Exodus 24:4); he reads the book of the covenant aloud.
  • The people answer: "All that the LORD hath spoken will we do, and obey." (Exodus 24:7) In Hebrew na'aseh v'nishma: commitment before full understanding.
  • Abraham's covenant was with a family; Sinai's is with a nation, and adds a full way of life.
  • Today: the festival of Shavuot celebrates the giving of the Torah.
Mission 1Read the source

The Ten Commandments

> "I am the LORD thy God, who brought thee out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage. Thou shalt have no other gods before Me." (Exodus 20:2–3)

  • Jews call them Aseret ha-Dibrot, "the Ten Sayings". In Jewish counting the first is "I am the LORD thy God".
  • Tradition places five on each tablet: 1–5 mainly duties towards God (including honouring parents, God's partners in creation); 6–10 duties towards other people.

Show answer

Mission 1

The greatest prophet

  • "there hath not arisen a prophet since in Israel like unto Moses, whom the LORD knew face to face" (Deuteronomy 34:10)
  • Prophet: one who speaks God's word. Moses receives the whole Torah and teaches it.
  • Leader through 40 years in the wilderness; mediator who pleads for the people after the golden calf; law-giver who writes the covenant down.
  • Orthodox teaching: Moses received the Written Torah and the Oral Torah at Sinai, and passed it on to Joshua, and so down the generations (Pirkei Avot 1:1, paraphrase).
  • He sees the Promised Land from Mount Nebo but dies before entering (Deuteronomy 34:4).
  • Moses is honoured but never worshipped: his burial place is unknown (Deuteronomy 34:6).
Mission 1Diversity

The Decalogue today

  • Shared: read aloud at Shavuot and twice a year in the Torah cycle; the congregation stands; two tablets often appear above the Ark.
  • Orthodox: the whole Torah, all 613 mitzvot, was given at Sinai and is binding. The Ten are part of it, not above it.
  • The rabbis stopped reciting the Ten daily so no one would claim only these were given at Sinai (Talmud Berakhot 12a, paraphrase).
  • Reform and Liberal: the Torah is inspired but written by people over time; the Ten are an ethical core that still binds; many ritual laws may be adapted.
  • Many Jews see the Ten as a summary of the whole Torah; many see them as a basis for justice in wider society too.
Mission 1Evaluate

"The Ten Commandments are the most important part of the Torah for Jews today."

  • For: spoken by God to the whole people at Sinai; written on stone; read standing; cover duties to God and to people; many Reform Jews see them as the ethical core.
  • Against: Orthodox teaching gives all 613 mitzvot equal divine authority; the rabbis deliberately stopped reciting them daily (Berakhot 12a).
  • Against: other summaries exist: the Shema, "love thy neighbour as thyself" (Leviticus 19:18), or Hillel's rule: do not do to others what is hateful to you (Talmud Shabbat 31a, paraphrase).
  • Judge it by: how Orthodox and Reform Jews see the Torah's authority; what shapes daily life most (Shabbat, kashrut, ethics); whether any part can be ranked above the rest.
Mission 1Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain two reasons why the Covenant at Sinai is important for Jews. Refer to sacred writings.

  • Reason 1: it made Israel God's people. Source: "ye shall be Mine own treasure from among all peoples" (Exodus 19:5). Development: so Jews see themselves as chosen for a task: to keep God's law and show it to the world.
  • Reason 2: it gave the Torah, including the Ten Commandments. Development: these shape everyday life, from Shabbat (Exodus 20:8) to honesty and respect for life, so Jews meet God through how they live.
  • 6-mark tip: explain two beliefs and develop each; refer to sacred writings or another source of religious belief and teaching, and show how it supports your point, not just its name.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Judaism gives 613 commandments. If God commands so much, are Jews still free to choose?

Make a guess: Judaism gives 613 commandments. If God commands so much, are Jews still free to choose?

Mission 2

What a mitzvah is

  • A mitzvah (plural mitzvot) is a commandment from God in the Torah.
  • The rabbis counted 613 in the Torah: 248 positive and 365 negative (Talmud Makkot 23b).
  • They cover worship, food, Shabbat, family life, business, courts, care for the poor and for animals.
  • At 13 (boys) or 12 (girls, in Orthodox practice) a young Jew becomes bar or bat mitzvah: "son/daughter of the commandment", now responsible for keeping them.
  • Keeping mitzvot is seen as a response of love to God and a way to make ordinary life holy.
Mission 2

Ways to group the mitzvot

  • Positive (mitzvot aseh, "do"): e.g. honour parents, keep Shabbat, give tzedakah.
  • Negative (mitzvot lo ta'aseh, "do not"): e.g. do not steal, do not eat pork.
  • Between people and God (bein adam la-Makom): prayer, Shabbat, kashrut, festivals.
  • Between people (bein adam la-chavero): honesty, charity, kindness, respect for parents.
Mission 2Read the source

Reminders to keep the mitzvot

> "And it shall be unto you for a fringe, that ye may look upon it, and remember all the commandments of the LORD, and do them" (Numbers 15:39)

  • This is the command of tzitzit: fringes on the corners of a garment, worn on the tallit and by many Orthodox men every day.
  • "Therefore shall ye lay up these My words in your heart and in your soul; and ye shall bind them for a sign upon your hand" (Deuteronomy 11:18): tefillin.
  • "And thou shalt write them upon the door-posts of thy house" (Deuteronomy 11:20): the mezuzah.
  • Both passages are part of the Shema, said twice daily.

Show answer

Mission 2

Free will: "choose life"

  • "I have set before thee life and death, the blessing and the curse; therefore choose life" (Deuteronomy 30:19).
  • Judaism teaches that God gave every person free will (bechirah): the power to choose good or evil.
  • Free will and the mitzvot fit together: a command only makes sense if you can obey or disobey it.
  • Choice makes reward and punishment fair, and makes keeping a mitzvah a real act of love.
  • Even after doing wrong, a person can choose teshuvah (return, repentance).
Mission 2

Two inclinations

  • The rabbis taught that every person has a yetzer ha-tov (good inclination) and a yetzer ha-ra (bad inclination).
  • "the imagination of man's heart is evil from his youth" (Genesis 8:21); but God tells Cain: "sin coucheth at the door … but thou mayest rule over it" (Genesis 4:7).
  • The yetzer ha-ra is not a devil: it is selfish desire. Used rightly, it drives people to build, marry and work (Genesis Rabbah 9:7, paraphrase).
  • Sin (chet, "missing the mark") happens when the yetzer ha-ra rules. The Torah is its remedy (Talmud Kiddushin 30b, paraphrase).
  • A teaching links bar/bat mitzvah age with the yetzer ha-tov growing strong enough to guide choices.
Mission 2Read the source

Maimonides, Sefer Madda

  • Maimonides (1138–1204) wrote the Mishneh Torah (completed c. 1180), a code of all Jewish law. Book 1 is Sefer Madda, "the Book of Knowledge".
  • It begins: the foundation of all is to know that God exists (Laws of the Foundations of the Torah 1:1, paraphrase). Every mitzvah grows from that.
  • Laws of Repentance 5:1–4 (paraphrase): freedom is given to every person. Anyone can become as righteous as Moses or as wicked as Jeroboam. No one forces us; God does not decree whether we will be good or bad.
  • If God decided our actions, the Torah's commands, and reward and punishment, would make no sense.

Q1 Why, for Maimonides, must humans be free?

Show answer

Otherwise commands and judgement would be pointless.

Q2 Which two people mark the range of human choice?

Show answer

Moses and Jeroboam.

Quick check

Mission 2Diversity

Which mitzvot matter most?

  • Orthodox: all 613 come from God; both kinds are equally binding. You may not choose between them.
  • Yet they differ in one way: Yom Kippur atones for sins against God, but not for sins against people until you have made peace with them (Mishnah Yoma 8:9, paraphrase).
  • Reform and Liberal: the ethical mitzvot are central; ritual mitzvot are valued and chosen by informed choice.
  • The Talmud shows prophets summing up the 613: David in 11 principles, Micah in 3, Habakkuk in 1: "the righteous shall live by his faith" (Habakkuk 2:4) (Makkot 24a).
  • Hillel: not doing to others what is hateful to you is the whole Torah; the rest is commentary (Talmud Shabbat 31a, paraphrase).
Mission 2Evaluate

"Mitzvot between people matter more than mitzvot between people and God."

  • For: Yoma 8:9: wrongs against people need their forgiveness. Hillel calls not harming others "the whole Torah". Reform Judaism puts ethics first. Prophets condemn worship without justice.
  • Against: Orthodox teaching: all 613 are God's will, equally binding. The Ten Commandments start with duties to God. Ritual mitzvot (Shabbat, kashrut) keep Jewish identity alive.
  • Another view: the two are linked: treating people well is serving God, since every person is made in God's image.
  • Judge it by: Orthodox and Reform views; what the sources stress; whether the two kinds can really be separated.
Mission check

?A Jewish shop owner finds a full wallet left on her counter. She is tempted to keep the cash, but returns it all, remembering the Torah's command to return lost property (Deuteronomy 22:1–3). Which statement best explains her choice in Jewish terms?

In Jewish teaching, 613 commands do not cancel freedom: they assume it. Every mitzvah is a choice, made by a person with two inclinations.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why do Jews call giving to the poor "justice", not "charity"?

Make a guess: Why do Jews call giving to the poor "justice", not "charity"?

Mission 3

Justice (tzedek)

  • "Justice, justice shalt thou follow" (Deuteronomy 16:20). The word is doubled: justice matters intensely.
  • God "doth execute justice for the fatherless and widow, and loveth the stranger" (Deuteronomy 10:18). Jews are to do the same.
  • "only to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God" (Micah 6:8).
  • Justice means fairness in courts and business, honest weights, paying workers on time, and protecting the weak.
  • The prophets condemn worship without justice: "let justice well up as waters" (Amos 5:24).
  • A judge who judges truly becomes God's partner in creation (Talmud Shabbat 10a, paraphrase).
Mission 3

Tzedakah: righteous giving

  • Tzedakah comes from tzedek (justice). It is a duty, not a favour: the poor have a right to help.
  • Behind it: "The earth is the LORD'S, and the fulness thereof" (Psalm 24:1). Jews take this to mean wealth is held on trust from God.
  • "thou shalt surely open thy hand unto him" (Deuteronomy 15:8).
  • Many Jews give about 10% of income (ma'aser). Even a person who receives tzedakah is expected to give a little.
  • Maimonides' ladder (Laws of Gifts to the Poor 10:7–14, paraphrase): eight levels, from giving grudgingly at the bottom, through giving anonymously, up to the highest: helping a person become self-supporting with a loan, a partnership or a job.
Quick check
?According to Maimonides' ladder of tzedakah, which is the highest level of giving?
Mission 3

Gemilut hasadim: acts of loving-kindness

  • Gemilut hasadim: practical kindness, done with time and effort, not only money.
  • "thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself" (Leviticus 19:18), and "thou shalt love him as thyself" of the stranger (Leviticus 19:34).
  • Examples: visiting the sick (bikur cholim), welcoming guests (hachnasat orchim), comforting mourners, helping at a funeral.
  • The rabbis said it is greater than tzedakah in three ways: it can be done with your body as well as money; for rich and poor; for the living and the dead (Talmud Sukkah 49b, paraphrase).
  • The world stands on three things: Torah, worship, and acts of loving-kindness (Pirkei Avot 1:2, paraphrase).
Mission 3Read the source

Kindness in the Torah

> "And when ye reap the harvest of your land, thou shalt not wholly reap the corner of thy field, neither shalt thou gather the gleaning of thy harvest." (Leviticus 19:9)

  • The next verse adds: "thou shalt leave them for the poor and for the stranger" (Leviticus 19:10).
  • "Thou shalt not take vengeance, nor bear any grudge … but thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself: I am the LORD." (Leviticus 19:18)

Q1 Who has a right to the corners of the field?

Show answer

The poor and the stranger.

Q2 How does verse 18 link kindness to God?

Show answer

It ends "I am the LORD": loving others is part of serving God.

Mission 3

Tikkun olam: healing the world

  • Tikkun olam means "repairing (or healing) the world".
  • Origins: the Mishnah uses it for laws made "for the good order of the world" (Mishnah Gittin 4:2–3); the Aleinu prayer hopes "to perfect the world under the kingdom of the Almighty".
  • Kabbalah: Isaac Luria (1534–1572) taught that sparks of holiness were scattered at creation; each mitzvah helps gather them.
  • Today: working for justice, fighting poverty, welcoming refugees, protecting the environment.
  • Many Jews see humans as partners with God: the world is good but left for people to help complete.
  • Orthodox Jews tend to see tikkun olam as coming through all the mitzvot; Reform and Liberal Jews stress social action.
Quick check
?A synagogue youth group spends a Sunday clearing litter from a river and campaigns for cleaner water. Which belief best explains why?
Mission 3

Jewish charities in action

  • Jewish Care (UK, formed 1990): care homes, community centres, dementia and mental-health support, mainly in London and the South East.
  • It meets spiritual needs too: kosher food, Shabbat and festival celebrations, prayer, and a centre for Holocaust survivors.
  • World Jewish Relief (founded 1933 to rescue Jews from Nazi Germany, including children on the Kindertransport): today it helps people out of poverty and into work, supports refugees and responds to disasters worldwide, for people of every faith.
  • Local groups: synagogue food banks, kosher food parcels, volunteer visiting schemes; in the UK, Norwood supports children, families and people with learning disabilities.
  • Motivation: tzedakah is justice; gemilut hasadim is kindness; tikkun olam heals the world; every person is made in God's image.
Mission 3Evaluate

"For Jews, acts of kindness matter more than giving money."

  • For: Sukkah 49b: gemilut hasadim is greater than tzedakah in three ways. Kindness gives time and presence, which money cannot buy. It can help the rich and the dead.
  • Against: tzedakah is a duty of justice: the poor need food and rent, not only visits. Maimonides' highest level is financial (a loan or a job). Charities like World Jewish Relief depend on giving.
  • Another view: they work together: Jewish Care uses money and volunteers; Pirkei Avot 1:2 places loving-kindness beside Torah and worship.
  • Judge it by: what the poor most need; what the sources rank; whether the two can be separated in practice.
Mission check

?A wealthy Jewish businessman says, "My money is mine. I'll give to the poor when I feel generous." His rabbi says tzedakah is not optional. Which belief best supports the rabbi?

For Jews, giving is justice: the world is God's, the poor have a claim, and kindness and repair of the world complete the picture.

Mission 4

Make a guess: Jews who drive on Shabbat to save a life are praised, not blamed. Why?

Mission 4

Why life is holy

  • Sanctity of life: Jews believe human life is holy (sacred) because it comes from God and belongs to God.
  • Every person is made in the image of God (Genesis 1:27), so every life has equal and infinite worth.
  • Genesis tells how God breathes life into the first human (Genesis 2:7). Jews see life as a gift, held on trust.
  • Murder is forbidden "for in the image of God made He man" (Genesis 9:6).
  • God alone gives and takes life: "I kill, and I make alive" (Deuteronomy 32:39).
  • The Mishnah: whoever destroys one life is as if they destroyed a whole world; whoever saves one life, as if they saved a whole world (Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5, paraphrase).
Mission 4Read the source

Life is God's

> "And God created man in His own image, in the image of God created He him; male and female created He them." (Genesis 1:27)

  • "Thou shalt not murder." (Exodus 20:13)
  • "neither shalt thou stand idly by the blood of thy neighbour: I am the LORD." (Leviticus 19:16): not only "do not kill", but act to save.

Mission 4

Pikuach nefesh: saving a life

  • Pikuach nefesh: "watching over a soul". Saving a human life overrides almost every other mitzvah, including Shabbat and Yom Kippur.
  • The source: "Ye shall therefore keep My statutes … which if a man do, he shall live by them" (Leviticus 18:5).
  • The Talmud reads this: you should live by the mitzvot, not die because of them (Talmud Yoma 85b, paraphrase).
  • So breaking Shabbat to save a life is not a sin: it is itself a mitzvah.
  • Three exceptions: a Jew must not commit idolatry, murder or forbidden sexual relations (e.g. adultery, incest), even to save their own life (Talmud Sanhedrin 74a, paraphrase).
Mission 4Read the source

Talmud Yoma 83–85

All paraphrased from the Talmud tractate Yoma ("The Day", about Yom Kippur):

  • 83a (Mishnah): a seriously ill person may be fed on Yom Kippur; any doubt about danger to life overrides Shabbat.
  • 84b: if a child falls into the sea, spread a net and pull them out on Shabbat; if a child is locked in a room, break the door.
  • 84b: do it at once, and do not hand the task to a non-Jew or a child: the one who acts quickly is praiseworthy.
  • 85a: if a building collapses on Shabbat, dig out the rubble even if you are unsure anyone is alive.
  • 85b: "live by them": profaning one Shabbat lets a person keep many Shabbats.

Q1 Should you wait until you are certain someone's life is at risk?

Show answer

No: any doubt is enough.

Q2 Who should do the saving?

Show answer

The most capable Jews, at once: not left to others.

Mission 4

Pikuach nefesh in practice

  • Shabbat: phone an ambulance, drive to hospital, switch on lights for a doctor.
  • Yom Kippur: a person whose health is at risk must eat and drink; fasting would be wrong.
  • Kashrut: non-kosher medicine may be taken when it is needed for health.
  • Community: in the UK, Hatzola volunteer ambulance teams serve Jewish neighbourhoods, working on Shabbat and festivals.
  • Social: blood donation, medical careers and (for many) organ donation are seen as mitzvot.
Quick check
?A doctor tells an Orthodox Jewish woman with diabetes that fasting on Yom Kippur could put her life at risk. What would most Orthodox rabbis tell her?
Mission 4Diversity

Applying pikuach nefesh today

  • Agreed by all groups: saving life overrides Shabbat, fasting and food laws.
  • Relieving suffering: does it cover pain or illness that is not life-threatening? Orthodox law relaxes some Shabbat rules for the sick but not all; many rabbis now treat serious mental illness as pikuach nefesh.
  • Organ donation: living donation is widely praised. For donation after death, Orthodox authorities differ, mainly over how death is defined. Reform and Liberal Judaism encourage it.
  • End of life: all mainstream groups reject actively killing a patient. Many Orthodox authorities allow stopping treatment that only prolongs dying. Some Reform and Liberal Jews support assisted dying for the terminally ill.
  • Abortion: all permit it, and Orthodox law requires it, when the mother's life is in danger (Mishnah Oholot 7:6); views on other reasons differ.
Mission 4Evaluate

"Saving a life should always come before religious laws."

  • For: "he shall live by them" (Leviticus 18:5; Yoma 85b). Saving one life is like saving a world (Sanhedrin 4:5). Even doubt about danger overrides Shabbat.
  • Against: the three exceptions (Sanhedrin 74a): some laws may not be broken even to save life; many Jews were martyred rather than worship idols.
  • Against: "always" is too broad: the danger must be real, and many rabbis say pikuach nefesh does not cover every kind of suffering.
  • Judge it by: the Talmud's own limits; Orthodox and Reform views; whether saving life is itself a religious law.
Mission 4Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain two reasons why pikuach nefesh is important for Jews. Refer to a source of wisdom and authority.

  • Reason 1: human life is holy. Source: "God created man in His own image" (Genesis 1:27). Development: so saving any person matters more than keeping almost any other mitzvah.
  • Reason 2: the Torah is meant to give life. Source: "he shall live by them" (Leviticus 18:5), which the Talmud reads as "not die by them" (Yoma 85b). Development: so a Jew who breaks Shabbat to save a life is praised, not blamed.
Mission check

For Jews, because every life bears God's image, saving one is a mitzvah that comes before almost every other.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Moses was called at the burning bush (Exodus 3) and led Israel out of Egypt; Jews honour him as the greatest prophet and teacher (Deuteronomy 34:10).
  • The Torah tells how, at Sinai, God made a covenant with the whole people: God's treasure and protection, in return for keeping the Torah, beginning with the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20).
  • Orthodox Jews hold all 613 mitzvot binding; Reform Jews see the Ten as an ethical core and may adapt ritual law.

Mission 2

  • The Torah contains 613 mitzvot: positive and negative; between people and God, and between people.
  • Jews believe God gave every person free will (Deuteronomy 30:19); the yetzer ha-tov and yetzer ha-ra pull both ways, and the Torah guides the choice.
  • Maimonides (Sefer Madda): anyone can be as righteous as Moses; commands would be pointless without freedom.
  • Orthodox Jews hold all mitzvot equally binding; Reform Jews put ethical mitzvot first.

Mission 3

  • Justice (tzedek) is central: "Justice, justice shalt thou follow" (Deuteronomy 16:20).
  • Tzedakah is righteous giving, a duty; gemilut hasadim is practical kindness; tikkun olam is repairing the world, which many Jews see as partnership with God.
  • Jewish Care and World Jewish Relief put these principles into practice.

Mission 4

  • Jews believe human life is holy because each person is made in God's image (Genesis 1:27) and life belongs to God.
  • Pikuach nefesh: saving a life overrides almost all mitzvot, including Shabbat and Yom Kippur (Leviticus 18:5; Talmud Yoma 83–85), except idolatry, murder and forbidden relations.
  • Jews agree on the principle but differ on applications: relieving suffering, organ donation, end of life.
