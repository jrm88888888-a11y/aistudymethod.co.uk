Mission 1

Mission 1: Two acids at the same concentration need the same volume of alkali. How can a titration show which one is weak?

Make a guess: Two acids at the same concentration need the same volume of alkali. How can a titration show which one is weak?

Mission 1

Four curves, four shapes

  • Base added to 25.0 mL of acid, all 0.100 M:
  • Strong acid + strong base: starts near pH 1; long steep section; equivalence at pH 7.
  • Weak acid + strong base: starts higher; gentle buffer region; equivalence above 7.
  • Strong acid + weak base: starts near pH 1; equivalence below 7; levels off well below the strong-base curve.
  • Weak acid + weak base: no sharp steep section.
  • Acid added to base: the same features, with the curve falling.
Mission 1

Measuring a pH curve

  • Calibrate the pH meter with buffer solutions of known pH (e.g. 4.00 and 7.00).
  • Acid in a beaker with the probe; stir throughout.
  • Add base from a buret: 1 mL at a time, then 0.1–0.2 mL near the steep section.
  • Record the pH once steady after each addition. Plot pH (y) against volume added (x).
  • The curve gives the analyte's concentration and its pKa (or pKb).
Mission 1

Reading the key points

  • Intercept: pH before any titrant is added.
  • Equivalence point: middle of the steep section; amounts match the equation.
  • At equivalence, mol titrant = mol analyte. E.g. 18.40 mL of 0.100 M NaOH for 25.0 mL of HA: [HA] = 0.0736 M.
  • Buffer region: gentle slope before equivalence; HA and A⁻ both present.
  • Half-equivalence: [HA] = [A⁻], so Ka = [H⁺] and pH = pKa.
  • Weak base titrated with acid: at half-equivalence, pH = pKa of BH⁺ (pOH = pKb).
Quick check
?A student titrates 25.0 mL of 0.10 M HA, a weak monoprotic acid, with 0.10 M NaOH and finds that the pH at the half-equivalence point is 4.70. The student then titrates 25.0 mL of 0.010 M HA with 0.010 M NaOH at the same temperature. How does the pH at the half-equivalence point of the second titration compare with that of the first?
Mission 1

Same volume, different shape

  • Equal amounts of acid need equal volumes of base, strong or weak. As OH⁻ removes H⁺, more HA dissociates until all has reacted.
  • But a weak acid's curve starts higher: it is only partly dissociated.
  • It has a buffer region: HA and A⁻ together resist pH change.
  • Its equivalence point is above 7: the flask holds A⁻, and A⁻ + H2O ⇌ HA + OH⁻.
  • Strong acid + weak base: the flask holds BH⁺, which makes equivalence below 7.
Mission 1

Ka from half-neutralization

  • Find the equivalence volume, then read the pH at half that volume. pH = pKa.
  • Example: half-neutralization pH = 4.20.
  • Ka = 10⁻⁴·²⁰ = 6.3 × 10⁻⁵.
  • Lower pH at half-neutralization → larger Ka → stronger acid.
Quick check
?A pH curve starts at pH 1.0. It rises slowly, then steeply from about pH 3 to pH 7, with the equivalence point at about pH 5.5. It then levels off below pH 10. Which titration does it show?
Mission 1

Strong + strong: pH from the excess

  • H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H2O(l) goes to completion.
  • Before equivalence: pH from the excess H3O⁺. After: from the excess OH⁻.
  • Steps: moles of each → subtract → excess moles ÷ total volume → pH.
  • E.g. 25.0 mL of 0.100 M HCl + 30.0 mL of 0.100 M NaOH: excess OH⁻ = 0.500 mmol in 55.0 mL = 9.09 × 10⁻³ M. pOH 2.04, pH 11.96.
Quick check
?Which equation represents the equilibrium that determines the pH at the equivalence point of the titration of CH3COOH(aq) with NaOH(aq)?
Mission 1

The curve picks the indicator

  • The indicator must change color within the steep section, at the equivalence point.
  • Weak acid + strong base: phenolphthalein (changes about pH 8–10).
  • Strong acid + weak base: methyl orange (changes about pH 3–4.5).
  • Strong + strong: either works. Weak + weak: no indicator gives a sharp end point.
  • Choose an indicator whose pKa is close to the equivalence-point pH.
  • Why indicators work: Mission 4.
Mission 1

Polyprotic acids

  • A diprotic acid, H2A, loses its protons one at a time.
  • Its curve has two steep sections, so two equivalence points: one per acidic proton.
  • First half-equivalence: pH = pKa1. Midway between the two equivalence points: pH = pKa2.
  • At the first equivalence point, the major species is HA⁻; at the second, A²⁻.
  • Reason about which species are major; no species calculations needed.
Quick check
?A solution of a weak diprotic acid, H2A, is titrated with NaOH(aq). The pH at the first equivalence point is 8.5. Which species, other than H2O and Na⁺, is present at the highest concentration at the first equivalence point?
Mission check

?Separate 20.0 mL portions of 0.100 M NaOH(aq) and of 0.100 M base B(aq) are each titrated with 0.100 M HCl(aq). Both need 20.0 mL of acid. Which feature of the curves shows that B is a weak base?

Same volume, different shape. A weak partner raises (or lowers) the start, adds a buffer region, and moves equivalence away from pH 7.

Mission 2

What a buffer is

  • A buffer holds large concentrations of both members of a conjugate acid–base pair.
  • Acidic buffer: a weak acid + its salt, e.g. CH3COOH with CH3COONa.
  • Basic buffer: a weak base + its salt, e.g. NH3 with NH4Cl.
Mission 2

Two ways to make one

  • Mix a weak acid with its salt.
  • Or part-neutralize: excess weak acid + some NaOH. CH3COOH + OH⁻ → CH3COO⁻ + H2O makes the salt; leftover acid stays.
  • Basic buffer: excess weak base + some strong acid, e.g. NH3 + HCl.
  • Weak acid + strong base: weak in excess → buffer; OH⁻ in excess → [OH⁻] = excess mol OH⁻ ÷ total volume, then pH; equal → A⁻ hydrolysis. Same for weak base + strong acid.
Mission 2

How a buffer works

  • Acidic buffer: big reservoirs of HA and A⁻.
  • Add H⁺: the base of the pair removes it. CH3COO⁻ + H⁺ → CH3COOH.
  • Add OH⁻: the acid of the pair removes it. CH3COOH + OH⁻ → CH3COO⁻ + H2O.
  • The ratio [HA]/[A⁻] changes only a little, so the pH changes only slightly.
  • Basic buffer: NH3 + H⁺ → NH4⁺, and NH4⁺ + OH⁻ → NH3 + H2O.
  • A buffer reduces a pH change; it can't stop it completely.
Quick check
?Equal volumes of the two solutions in each pair are mixed at 25°C. Which mixture is a buffer solution?
Mission 2

Buffer pH

  • Henderson–Hasselbalch: pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]).
  • [HA] = [A⁻] gives pH = pKa.
  • Worked: 0.10 M HCOOH + 0.25 M HCOONa, pKa 3.75. pH = 3.75 + log(2.5) = 4.15.
  • More A⁻ than HA → pH above pKa.
Quick check
?A buffer at 25°C contains 0.15 M NH3 and 0.30 M NH4Cl. The pKa of NH4⁺ is 9.25. What is the pH of the buffer?
Calculate

A buffer from part-neutralization

?50.0 mL of 0.200 M propanoic acid (pKa 4.87) is mixed with 25.0 mL of 0.100 M NaOH(aq) at 25°C. Calculate the pH.
Mission 2

Adding a little acid: the ratio

  • 1.00 L buffer: 0.100 mol CH3COOH + 0.100 mol CH3COO⁻.
  • Add 0.010 mol HCl: CH3COO⁻ + H3O⁺ → CH3COOH + H2O.
  • Now 0.110 mol CH3COOH and 0.090 mol CH3COO⁻: [A⁻]/[HA] goes from 1.00 to 0.82.
  • A small change in the ratio means a small change in pH.
  • The same HCl in 1.00 L of pure water: pH 7.00 → 2.00.
  • You need to explain this, not calculate the buffer's new pH.
Mission 2

Making a buffer of a chosen pH

  • Pick a weak acid with pKa close to the target pH.
  • Target pH 5.00 with acetic acid (Ka 1.74 × 10⁻⁵): [A⁻]/[HA] = Ka ÷ [H⁺] = 1.74 × 10⁻⁵ ÷ 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ = 1.74.
  • With 0.100 M CH3COOH you need 0.174 M NaCH3COO: 14.3 g per liter (82.0 g/mol).
  • Lab: then test its capacity by adding acid or base until the pH starts to change sharply.
Mission 2

Diluting a buffer

  • Dilution lowers [HA] and [A⁻] by the same factor: the ratio, and so the pH, stays about the same.
  • Compare: diluting a strong acid tenfold raises its pH by 1.
  • But each portion now holds less HA and A⁻, so the buffer capacity falls.
  • Same ratio, higher concentration: same pH, larger capacity.
  • More HA than A⁻: more capacity for added base. More A⁻ than HA: more for added acid.
Quick check
?A buffer is made from CH3COOH and NaCH3COO. Which quantity sets the maximum amount of HCl that the buffer can neutralize before its pH begins to fall sharply?
Mission 2

Your blood after a sprint

  • Background: not in your course, but it answers the video's question.
  • Blood plasma holds carbonic acid, H2CO3, and hydrogen carbonate ions, HCO3⁻.
  • Muscles add H⁺: HCO3⁻ + H⁺ → H2CO3. Most of the H⁺ is removed.
  • H2CO3 → CO2 + H2O, and you breathe out the extra CO2.
  • Result: pH stays 7.35–7.45.
Mission check

?Inside cells, a buffer of dihydrogenphosphate ions, H2PO4⁻, and hydrogenphosphate ions, HPO4²⁻, helps keep the pH close to 7. A process in the cell releases some OH⁻ ions. Which reaction removes most of the added OH⁻?

Same idea as in blood. The acid of the pair removes OH⁻, the base removes H⁺, and the ratio barely changes.

Make a guess: Why does swapping three hydrogens in vinegar's acid for chlorines make it about ten thousand times stronger?

Mission 3

Strength lives in the conjugate base

  • HA + H2O ⇌ H3O⁺ + A⁻.
  • The more stable A⁻ is, the further right this lies, and the stronger the acid.
  • Stable A⁻ = a weak conjugate base: it has little pull on H⁺.
  • Strong acids (HCl, HBr, HI, HClO4, H2SO4, HNO3) have very weak conjugate bases.
  • Three things stabilize A⁻: electronegativity, inductive effects, resonance.
Mission 3

Which hydrogen leaves?

  • Acidic H atoms are bonded to an electronegative atom, usually O: the O–H bond is polar.
  • H atoms bonded to C are not acidic in water.
  • Carboxylic acids, R–COOH, are the common class of weak acid. Only the –COOH hydrogen is donated.
  • Lactic acid, CH3CH(OH)COOH: 6 H atoms, but 1 acidic one.
  • Ethanoic (acetic) acid: Ka = 1.75 × 10⁻⁵, pKa 4.76.
Mission 3

Electronegativity

  • Compare acids built the same way, H–O–X:
  • HOCl pKa 7.5 · HOBr pKa 8.6 · HOI pKa 10.6.
  • More electronegative X pulls electron density away from O, spreading out the negative charge of XO⁻.
  • More stable XO⁻ → stronger acid: HOCl > HOBr > HOI.
  • Careful: HF, HCl, HBr, HI have H bonded to the halogen. There, bond strength and ion size win: HF is weak; the rest are strong.
Quick check
?Hypochlorous acid, HClO, and hypoiodous acid, HIO, are both weak acids. Which choice identifies the stronger of the two acids and gives a correct reason?
Mission 3

Inductive effect: the answer

  • Cl atoms pull electron density through the σ bonds toward themselves: an inductive effect.
  • This spreads the negative charge on –COO⁻, so the anion is more stable.
  • pKa: CH3COOH 4.76 · ClCH2COOH 2.87 · Cl2CHCOOH 1.35 · Cl3CCOOH 0.66.
  • Each Cl lowers pKa; three Cl: 4.10 units lower, so Ka is about 10⁴ times larger.
  • The effect fades with distance. Chlorobutanoic acids: Cl on C-2, pKa 2.86; C-3, 4.05; C-4, 4.52 (butanoic acid 4.82).
Quick check
?A student measures the pH of 0.10 M CH3COOH(aq) at 25°C. The student then measures the pH of 0.10 M ClCH2COOH(aq), an acid in which one H atom of the CH3 group has been replaced by a Cl atom, at the same temperature. How should the second pH reading compare with the first, and why?
Mission 3

Resonance

  • Acetate, CH3COO⁻: two resonance structures, so the charge is shared over two O atoms. Both C–O bonds are the same length.
  • Ethoxide, CH3CH2O⁻: the charge sits on one O.
  • So acetic acid (pKa 4.76) is about 10¹¹ times stronger than ethanol (pKa about 16).
  • Oxyacids of chlorine: HOCl < HOClO < HOClO2 < HOClO3 (HClO4, strong).
  • Each extra O pulls electron density and spreads the charge over more atoms.
  • Nitrate, NO3⁻: charge spread over three O atoms, so HNO3 is strong.
Quick check
?Acetic acid, CH3COOH, and ethanol, CH3CH2OH, both contain an O–H group. Acetic acid is a weak acid; ethanol does not measurably ionize in water. Which statement best explains the difference?
Mission 3

Bases from structure

  • Strong bases: group 1 hydroxides and the heavier group 2 hydroxides (Ca, Sr, Ba). Their conjugate acid, H2O, is a very weak acid.
  • Weak bases: nitrogen bases such as NH3 and amines (CH3NH2). The lone pair on N accepts H⁺.
  • Carboxylate ions (CH3COO⁻) are weak bases too: they are conjugate bases of weak acids.
  • Conjugate bases of strong acids (Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻, NO3⁻, ClO4⁻) are too weak to change the pH of water.
Quick check
?HBr is a strong acid. A student dissolves KBr(s) in pure water at 25°C to make a 0.10 M solution; the K⁺ ion does not react with water. Which prediction of the pH of the solution, with its reason, is correct?
Mission check

?Four acids are each made up as 0.10 M solutions at 25°C: CH3COOH, CH3CH2COOH, FCH2COOH and BrCH2COOH. Which solution has the lowest pH, and why?

Same reason as for the chlorines. An electronegative atom near –COOH spreads out the charge of the anion, so the acid gives up H⁺ more readily.

Mission 4

Mission 4: How does a colorless dye suddenly turn pink at the end of a titration?

Make a guess: How does a colorless dye suddenly turn pink at the end of a titration?

Mission 4

Indicators are weak acids

  • An indicator is a weak acid: HInd ⇌ H⁺ + Ind⁻.
  • HInd and Ind⁻ have different colors.
  • Add acid: [H⁺] rises, the equilibrium shifts left: HInd's color.
  • Add base: OH⁻ removes H⁺, it shifts right: Ind⁻'s color.
  • Phenolphthalein: HInd colorless, Ind⁻ pink.
  • HInd is the protonated form; Ind⁻ is the deprotonated form.
  • A few drops only, so the indicator barely affects the pH.
Mission 4

Where the color changes

  • Ka = [H⁺][Ind⁻]/[HInd], so [Ind⁻]/[HInd] = Ka/[H⁺].
  • pH < pKa: HInd dominates. pH > pKa: Ind⁻ dominates.
  • At pH = pKa, [HInd] = [Ind⁻]: the in-between color.
  • The eye sees the change over about pKa ± 1: the indicator's range.
  • The same rule works for any weak acid: pH below pKa, mostly HA; above, mostly A⁻.
Quick check
?Acetic acid, CH3COOH, has a pKa of 4.74. A solution of acetic acid is adjusted to a pH of 6.00 at 25°C by adding NaOH(aq). Which species, CH3COOH or CH3COO⁻, is present at the higher concentration in the final solution, and why?
Mission 4

Why not universal indicator?

  • Universal indicator is a mixture of indicators with different pKa values.
  • Their changes follow one another: a different color at most pH values.
  • Good for estimating pH.
  • Useless for a titration: it changes gradually, with no single sharp change to mark the end.
Mission 4

End point vs equivalence point

  • Equivalence point: acid and base have reacted in the stoichiometric ratio.
  • End point: the indicator changes color.
  • A good indicator changes color within the steep section, so the end point matches the equivalence point.
  • In the steep section, one drop (about 0.05 mL) moves the pH by several units, right across the range.
Mission 4

Which indicator?

  • Weak acid + strong base: equivalence above 7 → phenolphthalein (range about 8–10).
  • Strong acid + weak base: equivalence below 7 → methyl orange (range about 3–4.5).
  • Strong acid + strong base: steep section about 3–11 → either.
  • Weak acid + weak base: no steep section → no indicator is suitable.
  • Choose the indicator whose pKa is closest to the equivalence-point pH.
Quick check

Which value should the student compare with the pKa values of the indicators in order to choose the indicator that gives the most accurate result?
Quick check
?25.0 mL of 0.100 M NH3 is titrated with 0.100 M HCl. Which indicator is the most suitable? Ranges (each centered near the indicator's pKa): methyl orange 3.1–4.4; phenolphthalein 8.3–10.0; thymol blue 8.0–9.6; alizarin yellow 10.1–12.0.
Quick check
?An acid-base indicator, HIn, is yellow, and its conjugate base, In⁻, is blue. In a solution of pH 5.40 at 25°C, absorbance measurements show that [In⁻] is three times [HIn]. What is the pKa of HIn?
Mission check

?Methyl orange can be written HInd(aq) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + Ind⁻(aq), with HInd red and Ind⁻ yellow. A few drops are added to aqueous ammonia: the solution is yellow. Hydrochloric acid is run in. Near the end it turns orange, then red. Why?

Phenolphthalein in reverse. Removing H⁺ pulls HInd ⇌ H⁺ + Ind⁻ right (pink); adding H⁺ pushes it left (red, for methyl orange).

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Equal amounts need equal volumes; strength shows in the shape: intercept, buffer region, equivalence pH.
  • Half-equivalence: pH = pKa. Equivalence: above 7 for a weak acid, below 7 for a weak base.
  • Strong + strong: pH from the excess in the total volume.

Mission 2

  • A buffer holds a weak acid and its conjugate base (or a weak base and its conjugate acid).
  • pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]); concentration sets capacity, the ratio sets pH.

Mission 3

  • Acid strength = stability of the conjugate base: the more stable A⁻, the stronger HA.
  • Electronegative atoms (directly or by induction, fading with distance) and resonance stabilize A⁻.
  • Strong acids and strong bases have very weak conjugate partners; amines and carboxylates are weak bases.

Mission 4

  • An indicator is a weak acid, HInd ⇌ H⁺ + Ind⁻, with two differently colored forms.
  • It changes color around pH = pKa; pick one that changes within the steep section.
  • Weak acid + strong base: phenolphthalein. Strong acid + weak base: methyl orange. Weak + weak: none.
