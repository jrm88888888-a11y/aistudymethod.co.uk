Mission 1

Saved from what?

  • Salvation: being saved from sin and its effects, to live with God for ever (eternal life).
  • Sin is anything that goes against God's will. It damages the relationship with God.
Mission 1

Atonement: making "at one"

  • Atonement: sin is dealt with and the broken relationship is repaired: people are made "at one" with God.
  • Christians believe Jesus, fully God and fully human, did this by his death on the cross.
  • Grace: God's free, undeserved love and help. Christians believe it cannot be earned (Ephesians 2:8–9).
  • Forgiveness: Christians believe that through Jesus, God forgives the sins of all who repent (turn back).

Mission 1

The whole story saves

  • Christians (Catholics call it the Paschal Mystery) see Jesus' life, death, resurrection and ascension as one saving work: redemption (being bought back, set free).
  • Life: in the Incarnation God shares human life; Jesus lives in perfect obedience and shows God's love.
  • Death: Jesus gives his life for others; Christians believe it takes away sin.
  • Resurrection: Jesus defeats death; believers are promised new life.
  • Ascension: Jesus returns to the Father, and the Spirit is sent to the Church.
  • Catholic teaching: grace does not cancel human freedom: people must freely accept God's offer.
Mission 1Read the source

Jesus and Nicodemus (John 3:10–21)

  • Context: Nicodemus, a Jewish teacher, visits Jesus by night. Jesus says he must be "born again" (from above) by the Spirit (John 3:3–8).
  • Jesus says the Son of Man must be "lifted up", like the bronze snake Moses lifted up to heal the people (John 3:14; Numbers 21:9).
  • "For God so loved the world, that he gave his only born Son, that whoever believes in him should not perish, but have eternal life." (John 3:16)
  • "For God didn't send his Son into the world to judge the world, but that the world should be saved through him." (John 3:17)
  • Those who refuse to believe have chosen darkness instead of light (John 3:18–21, paraphrase).

Mission 1

Pictures of the cross

  • No single picture says it all. Christians use several theories of atonement, side by side.
  • Sacrifice: as animals were offered in the Temple for sin, Jesus offers himself, the Lamb of God (John 1:29), once for all.
  • Substitution: Jesus takes the punishment sinners deserve, in their place. Stressed by many evangelical Protestants.
  • Ransom: "For the Son of Man also came not to be served, but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many." (Mark 10:45)
  • Reconciliation / example: the cross shows God's love so fully that it wins people back and inspires them to love.
  • Most Christians hold several of these together.

Mission 1Diversity

Saved when? Saved how?

  • Past: "I have been saved": Jesus' death has already won salvation (Ephesians 2:8).
  • Present: "I am being saved": grace changes a person day by day (1 Corinthians 1:18).
  • Future: "I will be saved": salvation is completed at the final judgement (Romans 5:9).
  • Many evangelical Protestants: saved by faith alone (Luther, 1517 onwards). A moment of conversion ("born again") brings assurance. Good works are the fruit of faith, not its cause.
  • Catholics: saved by grace, received in faith and lived out in love and good works; grace comes especially through the sacraments (baptism, Mass, reconciliation). "Faith, if it has no works, is dead in itself." (James 2:17)
  • Orthodox: salvation is a lifelong journey of becoming more like God (theosis).

Mission 1Evaluate

"Faith in Jesus is all a person needs to be saved."

  • Reasons for: "By grace you have been saved through faith … not of works" (Ephesians 2:8–9). The criminal crucified beside Jesus was promised paradise with no time for good works (Luke 23:43). The 39 Articles (XI) teach justification "by Faith, and not for our own works". Grace that had to be earned would not be grace.
  • Reasons against: "Faith, if it has no works, is dead" (James 2:17). In Matthew 25:31–46 people are judged by how they treated the hungry and the stranger. Catholics stress the sacraments, especially baptism (John 3:5). Faith that does not change how someone lives may not be real faith.
  • Another view: many Christians say the question is badly posed: real faith always shows itself in love, so faith and works cannot be separated.
  • Judge it by: What does "faith" mean: belief, or trust that changes a life? Which texts carry most weight, and why? Can a person earn what is a gift?

Mission 2

Redemption and the Paschal Mystery

  • Redemption: "buying back" or setting free. Jesus came "to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45).
  • Salvation: being saved from sin and death, for eternal life with God.
  • Grace: God's free, undeserved gift of his own life and help.
  • Pascha = Passover. The Paschal Mystery: Jesus' life, death, resurrection and ascension seen as one saving work.
  • Catholics believe the Paschal Mystery is made present at every Mass.
Mission 2

Life: the whole of Jesus' life saves

  • Catholic teaching: Jesus' whole life is part of redemption, not only its end (CCC 517, paraphrase).
  • Adam's disobedience is undone by Christ's obedience: "as through the one man's disobedience many were made sinners, even so through the obedience of the one, many will be made righteous" (Romans 5:19).
  • His healing, forgiving and welcoming sinners show what salvation looks like.
  • His Incarnation joins God and humanity: what was broken begins to be restored.
  • Significance: Catholics follow Jesus' life as a pattern for their own (prayer, service, forgiveness).
Mission 2

Death: restoration through sacrifice

  • A sacrifice is an offering to God. Jesus offers his whole self in love and obedience.
  • "Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!" (John 1:29): like the Passover lamb, his death brings freedom.
  • At the Last Supper: "This cup is the new covenant in my blood, which is poured out for you." (Luke 22:20)
  • Significance: the Mass makes this one sacrifice present (it is not repeated); the crucifix keeps it before Catholics' eyes.
Mission 2

Resurrection: restoration through recreation

  • The Gospels say Jesus rose on the first day of the week: a new first day, as in Genesis 1.
  • Catholics believe his risen body is real but transformed: the start of a new creation.
  • "If anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation. The old things have passed away. Behold, all things have become new." (2 Corinthians 5:17)
  • Significance: in baptism Catholics die and rise with Christ (Romans 6:4); hope of resurrection at every funeral; Sunday as a weekly Easter.

Mission 2

Ascension and Pentecost: restoration of the cosmic order

  • "As they were looking, he was taken up, and a cloud received him out of their sight." (Acts 1:9) Angels promise he "will come back in the same way" (Acts 1:11).
  • Catholics believe Jesus' risen humanity enters God's presence: heaven and earth are joined; Christ reigns over all creation.
  • Pentecost (Acts 2:1–4): wind and tongues of fire; "They were all filled with the Holy Spirit" (Acts 2:4). The Spirit makes Christ's saving work available to all people.
  • Significance: Christians are sent on mission; Christ's return is awaited in hope.
Mission 2

Salvation, grace and freedom

  • "For by grace you have been saved through faith, and that not of yourselves; it is the gift of God" (Ephesians 2:8).
  • Grace comes first: no one can earn salvation.
  • Freedom: God does not force anyone. Catholics believe grace invites a free response of faith, shown in love and good works: "faith, if it has no works, is dead in itself" (James 2:17).
  • Grace is given especially through the sacraments: baptism, Eucharist, reconciliation.
  • Other Christians: many Protestants stress salvation by grace through faith alone (sola fide), while agreeing that true faith bears fruit. In 1999 Catholics and Lutherans signed a Joint Declaration agreeing on much of this.

Mission 2Evaluate

"Jesus' death is the most important part of the Paschal Mystery."

  • Reasons for: "to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45); the cross is Christianity's central symbol; the Mass makes Christ's sacrifice present; Paul: "Christ died for our sins" (1 Corinthians 15:3).
  • Reasons against: "If Christ has not been raised, then our preaching is in vain" (1 Corinthians 15:14); the resurrection begins the new creation; the ascension and Pentecost bring salvation to all; Orthodox worship centres on Easter.
  • Another view: Catholic teaching calls it one mystery: each event makes sense only with the others.
  • Judge it by: Could any event save without the others? What does scripture stress? What do Catholic worship and the creeds stress?

Mission 3

Grace: a gift, not a wage

  • Salvation: being rescued from sin and death and brought into God's life for ever.
  • Catholics believe it comes through the whole Paschal Mystery: Jesus' life, death, resurrection and ascension (see Mission 2).
  • Grace: God's free, undeserved gift of his own life and help. No one can earn it.
  • Sanctifying grace: a lasting share in God's life, first given in baptism; it makes a person holy.
  • Actual grace: God's help at particular moments, to choose good and turn back to God.
  • Grace comes especially through the sacraments, and also through prayer, Scripture and other people.
  • So Catholics say: I have been saved (baptism), I am being saved (living by grace), I hope to be saved (at the end).

Mission 3

Saved, being saved, will be saved

  • Past: "by grace you have been saved through faith" (Ephesians 2:8). Christ's death and resurrection won salvation; a Catholic receives it in baptism.
  • Present: "work out your own salvation with fear and trembling" (Philippians 2:12). Grace grows through the sacraments, prayer and love.
  • Future: "he who endures to the end will be saved" (Matthew 24:13). Salvation is completed after death and at the final judgement.
Mission 3

Grace and freedom

  • Grace comes first. No one can turn to God without God's help.
  • Freedom remains. God never forces love: a person can accept grace or refuse it.
  • Council of Trent (Decree on Justification, 1547): free will, moved by grace, cooperates with it, and can also reject it (paraphrase).
  • Martin Luther (On the Bondage of the Will, 1525): fallen humans contribute nothing; God alone saves.
  • Later Calvinists (Synod of Dort, 1618–19): the grace that saves cannot finally be resisted.
  • Catholic image: grace is like light; a person can open or close the shutters.
  • So God's love saves, but each person must freely respond: in faith, in the sacraments and in love of neighbour.

Mission 3

Irenaeus: Christ re-lives our story

  • Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 130–c. 202), Against Heresies (Adversus Haereses, c. 180), Books III and V.
  • Key idea: recapitulation (Greek anakephalaiōsis, "summing up again"), from Ephesians 1:10: God's plan is "to sum up all things in Christ".
  • Christ goes through every stage of human life and does right where Adam went wrong: obedience in place of disobedience.
  • "As in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive." (1 Corinthians 15:22)
  • Mary's "yes" undoes Eve's "no": the knot tied by Eve is untied by Mary (Against Heresies III.22.4, paraphrase).
  • Christ became what we are, so that we might become what he is (Book V, preface, paraphrase).
  • Metaphors: a new head for humanity; healing; victory over sin, death and the devil; humanity growing up into God's likeness.
Mission 3

Anselm: a debt only the God-man can pay

  • Anselm of Canterbury (1033–1109), Cur Deus Homo ("Why God Became Man", 1098).
  • Sin refuses God the honour owed to him and breaks the order of creation.
  • That order can be restored only by satisfaction: giving back more than was taken. Otherwise, punishment follows.
  • Humans ought to pay, but cannot: they already owe God everything.
  • Only God can pay; only a human ought to pay. So the payer must be both: the God-man.
  • Jesus, sinless, did not owe death. He gave his life freely, a gift of infinite worth that makes satisfaction for all.
  • Metaphors: debt, honour and satisfaction, drawn from law and the society of Anselm's day.
  • Not the same as penal substitution (later Reformers): for Anselm, Christ's satisfaction is offered instead of punishment.
Quick check

Mission 3

Hell and God's mercy

  • Catholic teaching: hell is real. It is self-exclusion: freely and finally refusing communion with God (CCC 1033, paraphrase).
  • God predestines no one to hell (CCC 1037). God "desires all people to be saved" (1 Timothy 2:4).
  • Scripture also warns: "These will go away into eternal punishment, but the righteous into eternal life." (Matthew 25:46)
  • The tension: how can unconditional love and universal mercy allow anyone to be lost for ever?
  • Freedom response: love cannot be forced. If people are truly free, God must respect a final "no".
  • Hope response: the Church declares some people saints, but has never declared any named person to be in hell. Hans Urs von Balthasar (1905–1988) argued that Christians may hope all will be saved.
  • Universalism: Origen (c. 185–c. 253) taught that all will finally be restored; the Church rejected this as doctrine.
  • Non-religious view: many humanists see eternal punishment for finite wrongs as unjust and reject the idea of hell.
Mission 3Evaluate

"A loving God would not send anyone to hell."

  • Claim: belief in hell cannot fit with God's unconditional love and universal mercy.
  • For: God "desires all people to be saved" (1 Timothy 2:4). Endless punishment for a finite life seems out of proportion. A loving parent never gives up on a child.
  • Against: Catholic teaching says God sends no one: hell is self-exclusion (CCC 1033). Love that forces itself is not love. Jesus himself warns of judgement (Matthew 25:46). Justice for victims requires that evil matters.
  • Another view: Christians may hope that hell is empty while still teaching that it is possible (von Balthasar).
  • Judge it by: What is freedom worth? Is hell a punishment or a choice? Can mercy and justice both be complete?
Mission check

Salvation is a gift already given, a life now being lived, and a hope still ahead: that is why Catholics say "being saved".

Mission 4

Who Mary is

  • Mary of Nazareth: the mother of Jesus. Catholics and Orthodox honour her above all other saints.
  • Annunciation: the angel Gabriel tells her she will bear God's Son; she agrees (Luke 1:26–38).
  • Cana: she tells the servants, "Whatever he says to you, do it." (John 2:5)
  • The cross: she stands by Jesus as he dies (John 19:25); he entrusts her to the beloved disciple: "Behold, your mother!" (John 19:27)
  • Pentecost: she prays with the apostles, waiting for the Spirit (Acts 1:14).
  • She "kept all these sayings, pondering them in her heart" (Luke 2:19): a woman of faith and prayer.
Mission 4

Mother of God

  • Mother of God (Greek Theotokos, "God-bearer"): defined at the Council of Ephesus (431).
  • It is first a claim about Jesus: the child Mary bore is one person, truly God and truly human. So his mother is the Mother of God.
  • It does not mean Mary is divine, existed before God, or is mother of the Father or the Trinity. She is a human being, a creature.
  • Catholics and Orthodox use the title; many Anglicans and Lutherans accept it too.
  • Other Catholic teachings about Mary:
  • Immaculate Conception (defined 1854): Mary was kept free from original sin from the first moment of her own life.
  • Assumption (defined 1950): at the end of her life Mary was taken body and soul into heaven.

Mission 4Read the source

Mary, model of the Church (CCC 963–975)

Catechism of the Catholic Church 963–975 (paraphrase):

  • Mary is the Mother of Christ and Mother of the Church (963).
  • She is joined with Christ in the work of salvation, from her "yes" at the Annunciation to the cross (964).
  • By her faith, hope and charity she is the Church's model ("type") of faith and charity (967).
  • She is the first and best disciple: she hears God's word and keeps it.
  • Her Assumption shows what the whole Church hopes for: resurrection in glory (966, 972).
  • Her prayers for the Church do not compete with Christ; they depend on him (970).
  • Catholics give her special honour, which is different in kind from the worship given to God alone (971).

Mission 4

Mary as guide and role model

  • Model of faith: she says "yes" to God without knowing what it will cost (Luke 1:38).
  • Model of discipleship: "blessed are those who hear the word of God, and keep it" (Luke 11:28).
  • Model of charity: she hurries to help Elizabeth; at Cana she notices others' needs.
  • Model of courage in suffering: she stays at the cross.
  • In prayer: the Hail Mary, the Rosary, the Angelus (prayed at 6 a.m., noon and 6 p.m.), May devotions, Marian feasts.
  • Pilgrimage: shrines such as Lourdes (France) and Knock (Ireland).
  • Catholics ask Mary to pray for them ("pray for us sinners"); they do not worship her.
Mission 4Diversity

Different views of Mary

  • Orthodox: deep love for the Theotokos; icons of her in every church; celebrate her Dormition ("falling asleep") and taking up into heaven, but do not accept the Immaculate Conception as defined by Rome.
  • Catholics: Mother of God, Mother of the Church, model and intercessor; Immaculate Conception and Assumption are dogmas.
  • Most Protestants: honour Mary as the faithful mother of Jesus and a model of faith; do not pray to her or to the saints: "there is one God and one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus" (1 Timothy 2:5).
  • They reject the Immaculate Conception and Assumption as not taught in the Bible.
  • Anglicans vary: some keep Marian feasts and shrines (e.g. Walsingham); others share the Protestant view.
Mission 4Evaluate

"Mary is the most important example for Catholics today."

  • Claim: Mary is the best model of Christian life.
  • For: she says "yes" to God (Luke 1:38); she is the first disciple, faithful at the cross; the Catechism calls her the model of faith and charity (CCC 967); the Magnificat shows care for justice.
  • Against: Jesus is the perfect example: "I have given you an example" (John 13:15). Some find Mary's sinlessness makes her hard to imitate; modern saints may feel closer to everyday lives. Protestants note the Bible says little about her.
  • Judge it by: example of what? for whom? Is a model someone you copy, or someone who points you to Christ?

Mission 5

The Church is people

  • In the New Testament, "church" translates the Greek ekklesia: an assembly, people called together.
  • The first Christians had no church buildings. They met in homes and in the Temple courts (Acts 2:46).
  • So, for Christians, the Church (capital C) is the community of everyone who follows Jesus.
  • A church (small c) is a building, or one local congregation.
  • Two pictures from the Bible describe this community: the Body of Christ and the People of God.
Mission 5Read the source

One body, many members

"For as the body is one and has many members, and all the members of the body, being many, are one body; so also is Christ." (1 Corinthians 12:12)

  • Paul wrote to Christians in Corinth who were divided and competing over whose gifts mattered most.
  • The foot cannot say it is not part of the body because it is not a hand; the ear cannot leave because it is not an eye (12:15–16, paraphrase).
  • "The eye can't tell the hand, 'I have no need for you'" (12:21). The weaker-looking parts are necessary (12:22).
  • "When one member suffers, all the members suffer with it. When one member is honoured, all the members rejoice with it." (12:26)
  • "Now you are the body of Christ, and members individually." (12:27)

Mission 5

Romans 12: one body in Christ

  • "For even as we have many members in one body, and all the members don't have the same function, so we, who are many, are one body in Christ, and individually members of one another" (Romans 12:4–5).
  • Verse 6: "having gifts differing according to the grace that was given to us".
  • Gifts named next: prophecy, serving, teaching, encouraging, giving, leading, showing mercy (12:6–8).
  • "Members of one another": Christians belong to each other, not only to God.
  • Paul stresses humility: no one should think of themselves more highly than they ought (12:3, paraphrase).
  • In other letters (Ephesians 1:22–23; Colossians 1:18) Christ is called the head of the body.
Mission 5

The People of God

  • In the Old Testament God makes a covenant with Israel: "a kingdom of priests and a holy nation" (Exodus 19:6).
  • The New Testament applies this to the Church: "you are a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for God's own possession" (1 Peter 2:9).
  • Christians who "were not a people" are "now … God's people" (1 Peter 2:10).
  • Lumen Gentium (Vatican II, 1964), chapter 2, "The People of God": God chose to save people not as isolated individuals but as one people (paraphrase, LG 9).
  • The People of God is a pilgrim people: still on a journey towards God's Kingdom.
  • Body of Christ stresses unity with Christ; People of God stresses all the baptised together, laity as much as clergy.

Mission 5

The body in action: Acts 2

  • After Pentecost, the first Christians "continued steadfastly in the apostles' teaching and fellowship, in the breaking of bread, and prayer." (Acts 2:42)
  • "All who believed were together, and had all things in common." (Acts 2:44)
  • They sold possessions and shared "according as anyone had need" (2:45).
  • They met daily, ate together "with gladness", and praised God (2:46–47).
Mission 5Diversity

What is "the Body"?

  • All Christians: Christ is the head; believers are the members; the Spirit unites them.
  • Catholic: the Church is a visible, ordered community; the one Church of Christ "subsists in" the Catholic Church, led by the pope and bishops (Lumen Gentium 8, paraphrase). Elements of holiness are found in other Churches too.
  • Catholic: the Eucharist builds the body: "we, who are many, are one body; for we all partake of the one loaf of bread" (1 Corinthians 10:17).
  • Orthodox: the Church is most fully the Body when it gathers for the Divine Liturgy around its bishop.
  • Many Protestants: the true Body is all genuine believers everywhere, known fully to God (the invisible Church), seen in local congregations.
Mission 5

Why it matters today

  • Belonging: every baptised person has a place and a gift; no one is a spectator.
  • Lay ministry: since Vatican II, Catholics stress the laity's part: readers, catechists, parish councils, charity work.
  • Care: "When one member suffers, all … suffer" (1 Corinthians 12:26): visiting the sick, food banks, praying for persecuted Christians.
  • Unity: Christians work together across denominations (ecumenism); Catholic teaching sees all the baptised as joined to Christ.
  • Mission: a pilgrim People of God looks outward, inviting others in.

Mission 6Read the source

Four words in the Creed

  • At Sunday Mass Catholics profess: "I believe in one, holy, catholic and apostolic Church" (Nicene Creed, Roman Missal).
  • These four words are the marks of the Church: signs by which, Catholics believe, the true Church can be recognised.
  • Shared by Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican and many Protestant Churches.
  • Q: Which of the four words do you think is hardest to see in a real parish? (No wrong answer: hold on to it.)
Mission 6

One and holy

  • One: one Lord, one faith, one baptism (Ephesians 4:4–5). Catholics are united in the same faith, the same sacraments, and communion with the Pope and bishops.
  • Jesus prayed "that they may all be one" (John 17:21): unity is a gift and a task.
  • Holy: Catholics believe the Church is holy because Christ is holy and gives it the Holy Spirit and the sacraments.
  • Its members are sinners being made holy; it is "always in need of being purified" (LG 8, paraphrase).
  • So "holy" is not a claim that Catholics are better people. It names where holiness comes from and what the Church is called to.
  • The saints show what holiness looks like.
Mission 6

Catholic and apostolic

  • Catholic means universal (Greek katholikos, "according to the whole").
  • Two senses: Catholics believe the Church has the whole faith and all the means of salvation; and it is sent to the whole world (Matthew 28:19).
  • Apostolic: built "on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Christ Jesus himself being the chief cornerstone" (Ephesians 2:20).
  • It keeps the apostles' teaching, and its bishops are the apostles' successors.
  • Importance today: Mass in any country is the same Mass; a Catholic belongs to a worldwide family linked to the first Christians.

Mission 6

Apostolic succession and the Magisterium

  • Apostolic succession: the apostles appointed leaders, who appointed others; Catholics believe that, by ordination, bishops today stand in an unbroken line from the apostles.
  • Peter was given a special role: "on this rock I will build my assembly" (Matthew 16:18). Catholics believe the Pope, Bishop of Rome, is Peter's successor.
  • So the bishops, in communion with the Pope, inherit the apostles' task of teaching with Christ's authority.
  • This is the Magisterium: the Church's living teaching office.
Mission 6Diversity

Same words, different meanings

  • Most Churches accept the Nicene Creed, and many say it in worship. They read the marks differently.
  • Catholic: the one Church of Christ, with its four marks, "subsists in" the Catholic Church (LG 8, paraphrase), though elements of truth and holiness are found in other Churches.
  • Orthodox: the Orthodox Church is the one, holy, catholic and apostolic Church; apostolic succession through bishops, but without the Pope's authority over all.
  • Anglican: keeps bishops in historic succession; sees itself as part of the one Church.
  • Many Protestants: "catholic" = the universal Church of all true believers, across denominations; "apostolic" = faithful to the apostles' teaching in the Bible, not a line of bishops.
  • So for many Christians, unity is spiritual and visible unity is a goal (the ecumenical movement).
Mission 6

Body of Christ, People of God

  • Recap from Mission 5. Paul: "we, who are many, are one body in Christ, and individually members of one another" (Romans 12:5); each has different gifts (12:4–6).
  • "Now you are the body of Christ, and members individually" (1 Corinthians 12:27).
  • Body of Christ stresses unity, many gifts, and Christ as the head: links to one and holy.
  • People of God (1 Peter 2:9–10; Lumen Gentium ch. 2) stresses a people on a journey, all the baptised sharing in Christ's mission: links to catholic and apostolic.
Mission 6

Mary, model of the Church

  • Catholics see in Mary what the whole Church is called to be.
  • Faith: "let it be done to me according to your word" (Luke 1:38).
  • Holiness and discipleship: she follows Jesus to the cross and prays with the apostles at Pentecost.
  • Mother: as Mary brought Christ into the world, the Church brings new members to Christ in baptism.
  • She is what the Church hopes to become: fully united with God (CCC 963–972, paraphrase).
Mission 6Read the source

"Outside the Church there is no salvation"

  • An old saying, found in St Cyprian of Carthage (died 258).
  • CCC 846 rephrases it positively: all salvation comes from Christ the Head through the Church, his Body. Those who know the Church is necessary and still refuse to enter or remain in it could not be saved (paraphrase).
  • CCC 847: this is not aimed at those who, through no fault of their own, do not know Christ and the Church, but seek God sincerely and try to do his will as their conscience shows; they too may reach eternal salvation (paraphrase, citing Lumen Gentium 16).
  • CCC 848: God can lead them to faith in ways known to himself, yet the Church still has the duty to share the Gospel (paraphrase).

Q1 Through whom, according to CCC 846, does all salvation come?

Show answer

Q2 Who does CCC 847 say the saying is not aimed at?

Mission 6Evaluate

"Being one is the most important mark of the Church."

  • Reasons for: Jesus prayed "that they may all be one … that the world may believe" (John 17:21): unity makes the Church's message believable. Without unity, the other marks are disputed between Churches.
  • Reasons against: holiness matters more: unity around something unholy would be worthless. Apostolic faith is the test of truth: unity without the apostles' teaching would not be the Church. Catholicity: the universal mission to the whole world is the reason the Church exists (Matthew 28:19).
  • Another view: the four marks belong together; each depends on the others, as they all depend on Christ.
  • Judge it by: Which mark could the Church lose and still be the Church? Which mark do Christians of different traditions disagree about most? Which mark most affects daily Catholic life?

The four marks are gifts Catholics believe come from Christ and tasks the Church is still growing into, as a sign of the Kingdom.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • For Christians, salvation is being saved from sin to eternal life with God; atonement is the repairing of the relationship through Jesus.
  • Christians believe law shows sin, grace is God's free, undeserved love, and the Spirit helps believers live it out.
  • Christians picture the cross as sacrifice, ransom and reconciliation; Catholics and many Protestants differ on how faith and works relate.

Mission 2

  • The Paschal Mystery is Jesus' life, death, resurrection and ascension as one saving work; Catholics believe it is made present at Mass and in the sacraments.
  • Salvation is God's gift of grace, freely accepted in faith shown in love.

Mission 3

  • Grace is God's free gift of his life and help; Catholics receive it especially through the sacraments.
  • Salvation is past (baptism), present (living by grace, with real freedom) and future (completed at the end).
  • Irenaeus: Christ re-lives and heals the human story (recapitulation); Anselm: Christ's freely given death makes satisfaction for the debt of sin.

Mission 4

  • "Mother of God" (Theotokos, Ephesus 431) means the child Mary bore is truly God; Mary herself is human.
  • Catholics see Mary as model of the Church: of faith ("let it be done to me", Luke 1:38), discipleship and charity, and pray with her and to the saints for their prayers.

Mission 5

  • The Church (ekklesia) is the community of Christians; a church building is where they gather.
  • 1 Corinthians 12: one body, many members, many gifts from one Spirit; every member needed, all share suffering and joy.
  • Romans 12:4–6 and 1 Peter 2:9–10: "members of one another" and one People of God; Catholics, Orthodox and Protestants understand the Body in different ways.

Mission 6

  • The Church is professed as one, holy, catholic (universal) and apostolic (Nicene Creed).
  • Catholics believe apostolic succession links today's bishops, with the Pope, to the apostles; this is the root of the Magisterium.
  • "Outside the Church no salvation" means all salvation comes through Christ and his Church; CCC 847 says those who do not know Christ through no fault of their own may still be saved.
