Mission 1

Mission 1: Double a satellite's orbital radius. How many times longer does one orbit then take?

Make a guess: Double a satellite's orbital radius. How many times longer does one orbit then take?

Mission 1

Kepler's three laws

  • First law: each planet moves in an ellipse, with the Sun at one focus.
  • Second law: the line from the Sun to the planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times.
  • Third law: T² ∝ r³ for all planets of the same star (r = mean distance; for a circle, the radius).
Quick check
?A comet moves around the Sun in a highly elliptical orbit. Which statement about the comet agrees with Kepler's laws?
Mission 1

Gravity is the centripetal force

  • In a circular orbit, gravity is the only force on the satellite. It points to the centre of the planet.
  • So gravity provides the centripetal force: GMm/r² = mv²/r.
  • There is no outward "centrifugal" force. The satellite is in free fall all the way around.
  • m cancels: the orbit does not depend on the satellite's mass.
  • Orbital speed: v = √(GM/r). Smaller orbit → faster.
Mission 1

Deriving T² ∝ r³

  • One orbit: distance 2πr in time T, so v = 2πr/T.
  • Substitute into GMm/r² = mv²/r:
  • GM/r² = 4π²r/T²
  • T² = (4π²/GM)r³
  • T² ∝ r³. The constant 4π²/GM depends only on the central mass.
  • Same law for moons of Jupiter or planets of another star; only the constant changes.
  • Double r: T² rises 2³ = 8 times, so T rises √8 ≈ 2.8 times.
Quick check
?Tethys and Rhea are moons of Saturn, and their orbits may be taken as circular. Tethys has an orbital radius of 2.95 × 10⁸ m and a period of 1.89 days. Rhea has an orbital radius of 5.27 × 10⁸ m. What is the period of Rhea?
Mission 1

Worked example: weighing the Earth

  • The Moon: r = 3.84 × 10⁸ m, T = 27.3 days.
  • T = 27.3 × 86 400 = 2.36 × 10⁶ s.
  • Rearrange: M = 4π²r³/(GT²).
  • M = 4π² × (3.84 × 10⁸)³ ÷ (6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × (2.36 × 10⁶)²) = 6.0 × 10²⁴ kg.
  • Any orbiting body works: measure r and T (or v), get the central mass.
  • A graph of T² against r³ is a straight line through the origin; gradient = 4π²/GM.
Quick check
?Astronomers measure the orbital period T and orbital radius R of several moons that move in circular orbits around a planet. The graph shows T² plotted against R³, with a best-fit line through the origin. What is the mass of the planet? Use G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻².
Mission check

?Two planets, P and Q, move in circular orbits around the same distant star. The orbital period of Q is 8.0 times the orbital period of P. What is the ratio (orbital radius of Q)/(orbital radius of P)?

T² ∝ r³ for anything orbiting the same central mass. Doubling the radius makes the period √8 ≈ 2.8 times longer.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why does a passing siren drop in pitch, and what can that shift tell us about speed?

Make a guess: Why does a passing siren drop in pitch, and what can that shift tell us about speed?

Quick check
?Predict first. Imagine a wavefront diagram of the sound from a source moving at constant speed through still air towards a stationary observer X. Each wavefront is drawn as a circle centred on the point where the source was when that wavefront was emitted. Compared with the waves the same source emits when it is at rest, how do the waves reaching X differ?
Mission 2

A moving source

  • A source emits wavefronts at a steady frequency. Each spreads out from where the source was when it was emitted.
  • Moving source: wavefronts bunched ahead (shorter λ), spread out behind (longer λ).
  • Wave speed through the air is unchanged. So ahead: higher f; behind: lower f.
  • Approaching: higher pitch. Receding: lower pitch. Constant velocity → constant (shifted) pitch.
  • Faster relative motion → bigger shift. Same velocity as the listener → no shift.
Quick check
?Predict first. Every absorption line in the spectrum of a star in our galaxy is at a slightly longer wavelength than the same line measured in the laboratory. What does this show about the star?
Mission 2

Doppler shift of light

  • Same effect for light and all EM waves.
  • Receding source: λ longer → red shift. Approaching: λ shorter → blue shift.
  • For v ≪ c:
  • Δλ/λ = v/c
  • v is the radial velocity: the part along the line of sight.
Mission 2

Worked example

  • A hydrogen line: 486.1 nm in the lab, 487.6 nm in a star's spectrum.
  • Δλ = 487.6 − 486.1 = 1.5 nm. Longer → moving away.
  • v = c × Δλ/λ = 3.00 × 10⁸ × 1.5 ÷ 486.1 = 9.3 × 10⁵ m s⁻¹ (about 0.3% of c).
  • v ≪ c, so the approximation is fine.
Quick check
?A spectral line of hydrogen has a wavelength of 656.3 nm when measured in the laboratory. A galaxy is moving directly away from Earth at 3.00 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹. Take c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. At what wavelength is this line observed in the spectrum of the galaxy?
Mission 2

Stars that wobble

  • A star with a companion (another star, or a planet) orbits their common centre of mass.
  • Its line-of-sight velocity swings: towards us, away, towards…
  • Its spectral lines shift blue, red, blue with the orbital period.
  • Plot radial velocity against time: a radial-velocity curve (a sine wave for a circular orbit).
Mission check

?A calcium absorption line has a wavelength of 393.4 nm in the laboratory. In the spectrum of a distant galaxy the same line is at 401.3 nm. Take c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. What is the galaxy's velocity relative to Earth?

Approaching: waves bunched, higher f; receding: stretched, lower f. The size of the shift grows with the relative speed, so measuring it measures the speed.

Mission 3

Mission 3: A star wobbles because of a planet nobody can see. How can the wobble reveal the planet's mass?

Make a guess: A star wobbles because of a planet nobody can see. How can the wobble reveal the planet's mass?

Mission 3

Both bodies orbit the centre of mass

  • A star and a planet (or two stars) both orbit their common centre of mass, with the same period.
  • They stay on opposite sides of it.
  • Balance about the centre of mass: M1r1 = M2r2, with r1 + r2 = d.
  • So r1 = M2d/(M1 + M2). The heavier body has the smaller orbit.
Mission 3

Worked example: Earth and Moon

  • MEarth = 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg, MMoon = 7.35 × 10²² kg, d = 3.84 × 10⁸ m.
  • rEarth = MMoon d/(MEarth + MMoon) = (7.35 × 10²² × 3.84 × 10⁸)/(6.04 × 10²⁴).
  • rEarth = 4.67 × 10⁶ m from Earth's centre.
  • Earth's radius is 6.37 × 10⁶ m: the centre of mass lies inside Earth. Earth wobbles; the Moon orbits.
Quick check
?The Sun (mass 1.99 × 10³⁰ kg) and Jupiter (mass 1.90 × 10²⁷ kg) are 7.78 × 10¹¹ m apart. How far is their centre of mass from the centre of the Sun? (Radius of the Sun: 6.96 × 10⁸ m.)
Mission 3

The mutual orbital period

  • Gravity on body 1 gives its centripetal force: GM1M2/d² = M1ω²r1.
  • Put in r1 = M2d/(M1 + M2): ω² = G(M1 + M2)/d³.
  • T = 2π √(d³/(G(M1 + M2))). Period depends on the total mass and the separation d.
  • Planet much lighter than its star: M1 + M2 ≈ Mstar and d ≈ planet's orbit radius. This is Kepler's third law.
Mission 3

Radial-velocity curves

  • Circular orbit seen edge on: each body's radial velocity varies sinusoidally.
  • Amplitude = orbital speed v. Period = orbital period T. Found from the Doppler shift, Δλ/λ = v/c.
  • The two curves are in antiphase: one body approaches as the other recedes.
  • Same T, so v = 2πr/T ∝ r: v1/v2 = r1/r2 = M2/M1. The lighter body moves faster.
  • Orbit radius from a curve: r = vT/2π.
  • If the orbit is not edge on, only part of v is seen, and the mass found is a minimum.
Quick check
?The radial-velocity curves of two stars, P and Q, in a binary system have the same period. Star P's curve has an amplitude of 40 km s⁻¹ and star Q's an amplitude of 120 km s⁻¹. The orbits are circular and seen edge on. Which statement is correct?
Mission 3

Worked example: weighing an exoplanet

  • Star: Ms = 2.0 × 10³⁰ kg. Its curve: vs = 60 m s⁻¹, T = 4.0 days = 3.46 × 10⁵ s.
  • Planet ≪ star, so d³ = GMsT²/4π² → d = 7.4 × 10⁹ m.
  • Planet's speed: vp = 2πd/T = 1.3 × 10⁵ m s⁻¹.
  • Mp = Ms vs/vp = (2.0 × 10³⁰ × 60)/(1.34 × 10⁵) = 8.9 × 10²⁶ kg, about half Jupiter's mass.
  • The star moves at only tens of metres per second: the Doppler shifts are tiny.
Mission 3

Worked example: weighing two stars

  • Curves: T = 10.0 days = 8.64 × 10⁵ s; v1 = 40 km s⁻¹, v2 = 80 km s⁻¹.
  • r1 = v1T/2π = 5.50 × 10⁹ m; r2 = v2T/2π = 1.10 × 10¹⁰ m; d = r1 + r2 = 1.65 × 10¹⁰ m.
  • M1 + M2 = 4π²d³/(GT²) = 3.56 × 10³⁰ kg.
  • M1/M2 = v2/v1 = 2 → M1 = 2.37 × 10³⁰ kg, M2 = 1.19 × 10³⁰ kg.
Quick check
?A star of mass 1.6 × 10³⁰ kg moves in a circular orbit seen edge on. Its radial-velocity curve has an amplitude of 25 m s⁻¹. Its planet's orbital speed is calculated to be 92 km s⁻¹. What is the mass of the planet?
Mission check

?Stars A and B have equal masses. Each has one planet, much lighter than the star, with the same orbital period. Both orbits are circular and seen edge on. Star A's radial-velocity curve has three times the amplitude of star B's. How do the planets' masses compare?

The wobble's speed and period give the planet's orbit; momentum balance about the centre of mass then gives its mass: Mplanet = Mstar × vstar/vplanet.

Mission 4

Mission 4: If every distant galaxy is rushing away from us, can we work out how long ago they were all together?

Make a guess: If every distant galaxy is rushing away from us, can we work out how long ago they were all together?

Mission 4

Hubble's law

  • Plot recession speed (from red shift) against distance (measured independently, e.g. from stars of known luminosity).
  • Distant galaxies: a straight line through the origin. Speed ∝ distance.
  • v = H0D
  • H0, the Hubble constant: the gradient of the graph.
  • Twice as far → receding twice as fast.
  • Nearby galaxies scatter about the line: their own motions through space matter more.
Mission 4

Worked example: how far?

  • A galaxy's red shift gives v = 6.0 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹. Take H0 = 2.20 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹ (data booklet).
  • D = v/H0 = 6.0 × 10⁶ ÷ 2.20 × 10⁻¹⁸ = 2.7 × 10²⁴ m.
  • In light-years: 2.7 × 10²⁴ ÷ 9.46 × 10¹⁵ = 2.9 × 10⁸ ly.
  • The light we see left that galaxy about 290 million years ago.
Quick check
?A galaxy at a distance of 1.2 × 10²⁴ m recedes from the Earth at 2800 km s⁻¹. What value of the Hubble constant does this give?
Mission 4

The age of the universe

  • Run the expansion backwards: every galaxy was once in the same place as every other.
  • Time for a galaxy to cover distance D at speed v: D/v.
  • Hubble's law: D/v = 1/H0, the same for every galaxy. So they were all together at the same time.
  • t ≈ 1/H0
  • H0 = 2.20 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹ (data booklet) → t = 4.5 × 10¹⁷ s ÷ 3.16 × 10⁷ s per year = 1.4 × 10¹⁰ years.
  • Only an estimate: it assumes the galaxies' speeds have never changed.
Quick check
?A measured value of the Hubble constant is 2.0 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹. What is the estimated age of the universe? (1 year = 3.16 × 10⁷ s)
Mission check

?Galaxy P is 2.0 × 10²⁴ m away and recedes at 5.2 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹. Galaxy Q is 6.0 × 10²⁴ m away and recedes at 1.56 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹. Assuming each has always moved at its present speed, how long ago was each at our position? (1 year = 3.16 × 10⁷ s)

Yes: every galaxy gives about the same distance ÷ speed, so they were all together about 1/H0 ago: roughly 13 to 15 billion years.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Stars at a galaxy's edge orbit as fast as stars much nearer its centre. What is holding them in?

Make a guess: Stars at a galaxy's edge orbit as fast as stars much nearer its centre. What is holding them in?

Mission 5

What orbits should look like

  • Circular orbit: gravity provides the centripetal force.
  • GMm/r² = mv²/r → v = √(GM/r), where M = mass inside the orbit.
  • Solar System: almost all the mass is in the Sun, so v falls as r grows. Neptune orbits at about a fifth of Earth's speed.
  • Spiral galaxy: most of the light comes from the crowded centre.
  • So we expect outer stars to be slower: v ∝ 1/√r.
Quick check
?In a spiral galaxy, stars and gas far beyond the region where almost all its stars are orbit at the same speed as stars further in. Using v = √(GM/r), what does this show about the mass inside radius r, in that outer region?
Mission 5

What galaxies actually do

  • Orbital speeds come from the Doppler shift of spectral lines: stars, and the 21 cm radio line of hydrogen gas far beyond the visible disc.
  • A graph of v against r is a rotation curve.
  • Measured: v rises, then stays flat far beyond where the light fades.
  • Flat v: M = v²r/G, so M ∝ r. Mass keeps growing where there is almost no light.
  • Leading explanation: each galaxy sits in a halo of dark matter: mass that emits and absorbs no detectable light, several times the visible mass. (A minority of physicists instead modify the law of gravity at low accelerations.)
Mission 5

Worked example

  • A spiral galaxy: v = 200 km s⁻¹ at r = 10 kpc and also at r = 30 kpc. 1 kpc = 3.09 × 10¹⁹ m.
  • Inside 10 kpc: M = v²r/G = (2.00 × 10⁵)² × 3.09 × 10²⁰ ÷ 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ = 1.9 × 10⁴¹ kg.
  • Inside 30 kpc: three times r, same v → 5.6 × 10⁴¹ kg.
  • If all the mass were inside 10 kpc: v at 30 kpc = 200 ÷ √3 = 115 km s⁻¹.
  • Measured 200, not 115: about 3.7 × 10⁴¹ kg lies between 10 and 30 kpc, where few stars shine.
Mission 5

Dark matter and the Higgs boson

  • Leading idea: dark matter is a new kind of particle, not made of atoms. No charge, so no light.
  • The Higgs field gives fundamental particles (e.g. electrons, quarks, W and Z bosons) their mass. The Higgs boson is its particle.
  • Found at CERN's Large Hadron Collider in 2012 by two independent experiments, ATLAS and CMS. Each reached the 5σ discovery standard; results were peer-reviewed and confirmed with more data.
  • Possible link: if dark matter particles get their mass from the Higgs field, and each has less than half the Higgs mass, Higgs bosons could sometimes decay into them.
  • Those decays would be invisible: the products escape the detectors, leaving missing energy and momentum.
  • LHC searches have not found them yet. The link is a hypothesis being tested.
Quick check
?Which result at the LHC would support the idea that dark matter particles get their mass from the Higgs field?
Mission 5

Critical density

  • A galaxy of mass m at the edge of a sphere of radius r, uniform density ρ. Only the mass inside pulls it back: M = (4/3)πr³ρ.
  • Hubble's law: it recedes at v = H0r.
  • Flat universe: the galaxy has just enough energy to escape: ½mv² = GMm/r.
  • m cancels: ½H0²r² = G(4/3)πr³ρ/r = (4/3)πGρr².
  • Divide by r² and rearrange: ρc = 3H0²/(8πG).
  • r cancels: the same value everywhere.
  • ρ > ρc: expansion would eventually stop and reverse. ρ < ρc: it would go on forever.
Mission 5

Worked example

  • Use the data booklet value: H0 = 2.20 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹ (about 68 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹).
  • ρc = 3 × (2.20 × 10⁻¹⁸)² ÷ (8π × 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹) = 8.7 × 10⁻²⁷ kg m⁻³.
  • That is about 5 hydrogen atoms per cubic metre (mH = 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg).
Quick check
?Two methods give values of the Hubble constant of about 67 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹ and 73 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹. By what factor is the critical density calculated from the larger value greater than that from the smaller value?
Mission 5

The fate of the universe: a controversy

  • Gravity slows the expansion. Enough mass: it stops and reverses. Too little: it expands forever.
  • The dividing line is ρc: compare the mean density with 3H0²/(8πG).
  • Dark matter is most of the mass, and it is hard to measure, so the mean density is uncertain.
  • H0 sets both the age estimate (≈ 1/H0) and the critical density; methods disagree (≈ 67 vs ≈ 73 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹).
  • Since 1998: distant supernovae show the expansion is speeding up, credited to "dark energy". Mass alone does not settle the fate.
Mission check

?The galaxies in a large cluster move so fast that the gravity of all the cluster's visible stars and gas could not hold them together. Yet the cluster has existed for billions of years. Which is the best conclusion?

Speeds reveal mass. The edge of a galaxy orbits fast because a halo of dark matter, several times the mass of the stars, pulls on it.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Gravity alone provides the centripetal force: GMm/r² = mv²/r, so T² = (4π²/GM)r³; the satellite's mass cancels.
  • v = √(GM/r): lower orbits are faster.
  • Kepler: ellipses, equal areas in equal times, T² ∝ r³.

Mission 2

  • Approaching → higher f, shorter λ; receding → lower f, longer λ; bigger relative speed → bigger shift.
  • Light, v ≪ c: Δλ/λ ≈ v/c; red shift means moving away.

Mission 3

  • Two bodies orbit their centre of mass: r1 = M2d/(M1 + M2); common period T = 2π√(d³/(G(M1 + M2))).
  • Radial-velocity curves (edge on): amplitude = orbital speed, r = vT/2π, M1/M2 = v2/v1.

Mission 4

  • Hubble's law v = H0D: recession speed is proportional to distance.
  • Age of the universe ≈ 1/H0, roughly 13–15 billion years (assumes constant expansion).

Mission 5

  • Flat galaxy rotation curves: mass inside r grows with r where there is little light: evidence for dark matter.
  • ρc = 3H0²/(8πG) from ½mv² = GMm/r with v = H0r. The Higgs field may give dark matter its mass; searched for as invisible Higgs decays.
  • The fate of the universe depends on its density, uncertain because of dark matter and disputed values of H0.
