Mission 1

Adding up: summation

  • One EPSP is usually too small to reach threshold.
  • Spatial summation: several presynaptic neurones release neurotransmitter at the same time → their EPSPs add.
  • Temporal summation: one presynaptic neurone fires several times in quick succession → its EPSPs add before they fade.
  • Total reaches threshold → action potential. Below threshold → none (all-or-nothing).
Mission 1

Brakes: inhibitory synapses

  • An inhibitory neurotransmitter (e.g. GABA) opens channels that let Cl⁻ in or K⁺ out.
  • The inside becomes more negative than at rest: hyperpolarised.
  • This is an inhibitory postsynaptic potential (IPSP).
  • The membrane is now further from threshold: an action potential is much less likely.
  • EPSPs and IPSPs act on the same membrane, so they sum: excitation minus inhibition.
  • Say: "an IPSP hyperpolarises the membrane" — not: "an inhibitory synapse is just a weaker excitatory one".
Quick check
?A postsynaptic neurone has a resting potential of −70 mV and a threshold of −55 mV. At the same moment it receives two excitatory inputs, each producing a +8 mV change, and one inhibitory input producing a −6 mV change. What is the result?
Quick check
?One presynaptic neurone synapses with a postsynaptic neurone. A single impulse gives an EPSP below threshold. When the presynaptic neurone fires several impulses in rapid succession, the postsynaptic neurone fires an action potential. Which explanation is correct?
Mission check

?A drug binds to GABA receptors on a postsynaptic neurone. When GABA is also bound, the drug makes the receptor's chloride ion channel open more often. What is the effect on the postsynaptic neurone?

A neurone fires only when its accelerators outweigh its brakes; a drug that strengthens the brakes quietens it.

Mission 2

Receptors are transducers

  • A receptor responds to one kind of stimulus only: it is specific.
  • It is a transducer: it converts stimulus energy (pressure, light, heat) into an electrical change in a membrane.
  • If the change reaches threshold, action potentials start in a sensory neurone.
  • In the eye, the action potentials start further along the pathway, in neurones of the optic nerve.
  • Below threshold: no impulses at all.
  • Say: "the receptor converts the stimulus into an electrical signal" — not: "the receptor sends the stimulus to the brain".
Mission 2

Rods and cones

  • Both are photoreceptors in the retina.
  • Rods: pigment rhodopsin; broken down by dim light. Very sensitive. One pigment, so no colour.
  • Cones: pigment iodopsin, in three types of cone (red-, green-, blue-sensitive). Need bright light. Give colour vision.
  • Fovea: cones only. Rods: more towards the edge of the retina.
  • Dark adaptation: bright light breaks down rhodopsin; in the dark it is re-synthesised, so rod sensitivity slowly returns.
Mission 2

Wiring: sensitivity versus acuity

  • Many rods share one bipolar neurone (convergence).
  • Small (below-threshold) responses from several rods summate → threshold reached in dim light → high sensitivity.
  • But the brain cannot tell which rod fired → low visual acuity.
  • Each cone has its own bipolar neurone → separate impulses from neighbouring points → high visual acuity.
  • Cones need more light: no summation → low sensitivity.
Quick check
?Why does the fovea give the sharpest image in bright light?
Mission check

?An owl hunts at night. Its retina has far more rods than cones, and many rods converge on each bipolar neurone. Which prediction is correct?

Convergence trades detail for sensitivity: the same wiring that lets you see a faint star hides its shape.

Mission 3

The path of light

  • Conjunctiva: thin, transparent membrane over the front of the eye and lining the eyelids. Protects.
  • Cornea: transparent, curved front. Does most of the refraction.
  • Aqueous humour: watery fluid behind the cornea. Keeps the shape; feeds the cornea and lens.
  • Iris: ring of muscle around the pupil, the hole that lets light in.
  • Lens: transparent and elastic. Fine-tunes the focus.
  • Vitreous humour: clear jelly filling the eyeball. Keeps its shape.
Mission 3

The coats and the detectors

  • Sclera: tough, white outer coat. Protects; eye muscles attach to it.
  • Choroid: middle layer with blood vessels and dark pigment. The pigment absorbs light that passes through the retina, preventing internal reflection, so the image stays sharp.
  • Retina: inner layer with the receptors, rods and cones, and neurones.
  • Fovea: a small pit in the retina, on the line of sight; packed with cones.
  • Blind spot: where the optic nerve leaves the eye. No rods or cones.
  • Ciliary body and suspensory ligaments: hold the lens and change its shape.
Mission 3Practical

Finding the parts on a slide

  • Prepared slides or photomicrographs of the mammalian eye.
  • Low power, whole eye: three coats from outside in: sclera (pale, fibrous; continuous with the cornea at the front), choroid (dark), retina (innermost).
  • At the front: the iris with the gap of the pupil; the ciliary body, a thickened, folded ring where the choroid meets the iris; fine suspensory ligaments running to the lens.
  • The humours often look like empty spaces: the fluid is lost when the slide is made.
  • Blind spot: a break in the retina where nerve fibres gather into the optic nerve.
  • High power, retina: from the vitreous side, nerve fibres → ganglion cells → bipolar neurones → rods and cones → pigmented layer and choroid. Light passes the neurones first.
  • Rods: long, thin outer segments. Cones: shorter, tapered outer segments.
Quick check
?On a low-power slide of a mammalian eye, a student finds a gap in the innermost layer at the back of the eye, where a thick bundle of fibres leaves the eyeball. No rods or cones are present at that point. Which structure is this?
Mission 3

Controlling the pupil

  • The iris has two sets of muscle: circular and radial.
  • Bright light: circular muscles contract, radial muscles relax. The pupil narrows: less light enters, protecting the retina.
  • Dim light: radial muscles contract, circular muscles relax. The pupil widens: more light reaches the retina.
  • The two sets are antagonistic: when one contracts, the other relaxes.
  • This is a reflex: fast and automatic.
Mission 3

Accommodation

  • Accommodation: changing the curvature of the lens to focus near or distant objects.
  • Near object: ciliary muscle contracts → ciliary ring gets smaller → suspensory ligaments slacken → elastic lens becomes more convex → more refraction.
  • Distant object: ciliary muscle relaxes → ring widens → suspensory ligaments are pulled taut → lens pulled thinner → less refraction.
  • Say: "the ciliary muscle contracts and the suspensory ligaments slacken" — not: "the ligaments contract". Ligaments are not muscle.
Quick check
?A person looks up from a distant hill to read a book in their hand. What happens in the eye as the book comes into focus?
Mission 3Read the data

Rods and cones across the retina

Data: Østerberg (1935), counts of rods and cones across a human retina (approximate values).

  • About 120 million rods and 6 million cones in each retina.
  • Fovea (0°): cones peak at about 150 000 per mm²; no rods.
  • Rods peak about 18° from the fovea, at about 160 000 per mm², then fall towards the edge.
  • Away from the fovea, cones are few and spread thinly.
  • Blind spot, about 15° from the fovea towards the nose: no rods or cones.
Mission 3

Convergence: sensitivity against detail

  • Rods: several rods connect to one bipolar neurone (convergence).
  • In dim light, each rod gives a small, sub-threshold generator potential. Together they summate at the bipolar neurone and reach threshold: high sensitivity.
  • Cost: the brain cannot tell which of the rods was stimulated. Low visual acuity.
  • Cones (at the fovea): each cone has its own bipolar neurone. High acuity, but cones need bright light.
  • Colour vision: three types of cone, with pigments sensitive to different wavelengths (red, green, blue light).
  • Say: "summation of sub-threshold stimuli" — not: "rods are bigger, so they catch more light".
Quick check
?Why do rods give greater sensitivity than cones in dim light?
Mission 3

Dark adaptation

  • Rods contain the pigment rhodopsin.
  • Bright light breaks down (bleaches) rhodopsin into retinal and opsin. Rods stop working.
  • In the dark, rhodopsin is re-synthesised. This takes time, and needs ATP.
  • So, entering a dark room, you see little at first. Sensitivity rises over about 20–30 minutes.
  • Cones recover within a few minutes but are far less sensitive; then the rods take over.
Mission 3

Two eyes are better than one

  • Binocular vision: both eyes face forward, so their visual fields overlap.
  • Each eye sees the scene from a slightly different angle. The brain compares the two images.
  • This gives stereoscopic vision: good perception of depth and distance.
  • Two eyes together also give a wider visual field than either eye alone.
Quick check
?A person walks from bright sunshine into a dark cinema. At first they can hardly see the seats; after about 20 minutes they see them clearly. What best explains the slow improvement?
Mission check

?Astronomers know that a very faint star can vanish when they look straight at it, but reappear when they look slightly to one side of it. Which explanation is correct?

Cones at the fovea give detail and colour in bright light; converging rods give sensitivity in dim light. That is why starlight gives shapes, not small print.

Mission 4

From muscle to myofibril

  • Muscle → bundles → muscle fibres → myofibrils → sarcomeres.
  • A muscle fibre is very long and has many nuclei. It forms when many cells fuse.
  • Fibre membrane: sarcolemma. Fibre cytoplasm: sarcoplasm.
  • Inside each fibre:
    • Many myofibrils, packed side by side.
    • Many mitochondria: supply ATP.
    • Sarcoplasmic reticulum: stores calcium ions (Ca²⁺).
    • T-tubules: inward folds of the sarcolemma that carry impulses deep into the fibre.
  • Muscle = organ. Muscle fibre = one multinucleate cell. Myofibril = a strand inside the fibre.
Mission 4

The sarcomere

  • A sarcomere runs from one Z line to the next.
  • Thin actin filaments: attached to the Z lines.
  • Thick myosin filaments: in the middle, held at the M line.
  • Bands:
    • A band (dark): the full length of the myosin filaments, including overlap.
    • I band (light): actin only. Z line in its centre.
    • H zone: myosin only, in the centre of the A band.
  • Also called the H band.
  • Alternating dark and light bands: skeletal muscle is striated.
Mission 4

Sliding filaments

  • Frame 1 — Relaxed: little overlap; wide I band; wide H zone.
  • Frame 2 — Contracted: myosin heads pull actin towards the M line.
  • The filaments slide past each other. Neither gets shorter.
  • Z lines move closer → the sarcomere shortens → the whole muscle shortens.
  • I band narrows. H zone narrows, and may disappear. A band stays the same.
  • Say: "the filaments slide past each other" — not: "the filaments contract".
Quick check
?Electron micrographs of a sarcomere are taken before and after it contracts. Which set of changes is observed?
Mission 4

Read the data: overlap and force

  • Gordon, Huxley and Julian (1966) held single frog muscle fibres at fixed sarcomere lengths, stimulated them, and measured the force.
  • Maximum force: sarcomere length 2.0–2.25 µm.
  • Longer than 2.25 µm: force falls in a straight line, to zero at 3.65 µm.
  • Shorter than 2.0 µm: force also falls, as the filaments start to collide.
  • Why zero at 3.65 µm?
    • Myosin filament: 1.6 µm. Each actin filament (to the Z line): about 1.025 µm.
    • 1.6 + 1.025 + 1.025 = 3.65 µm: actin and myosin no longer overlap.
  • More overlap → more myosin heads reach actin → more force. Strong evidence for sliding filaments.
Quick check
?In the data on the earlier screen, a sarcomere held at 3.0 µm produces about 46% of the maximum force. Why is the force lower than at 2.2 µm?
Mission 4

What moves the myosin heads

  • An impulse makes the sarcoplasmic reticulum release Ca²⁺ into the sarcoplasm.
  • In the presence of Ca²⁺, myosin heads attach to actin filaments.
  • Each head changes orientation (tilts), pulling the actin filament over the myosin.
  • The head detaches and reattaches further along: again and again.
  • Every cycle uses ATP.
  • No Ca²⁺: heads cannot attach. No ATP: heads cannot detach and reset.
Mission check

?After death, cells stop making ATP. A few hours later the skeletal muscles become rigid and cannot be stretched (rigor mortis). Which explanation fits the sliding filament model?

The filaments slide; the heads must let go to slide again. No ATP, no letting go.

Mission 5

Three kinds of muscle

  • Skeletal muscle:
    • Striated. Long, unbranched fibres with many nuclei at the edge.
    • Voluntary: stimulated by motor neurones. Contracts fast; fatigues.
  • Cardiac muscle:
    • Striated. Short, branched cells, usually one central nucleus.
    • Joined end to end by intercalated discs: impulses pass from cell to cell.
    • Myogenic and involuntary. Does not fatigue.
  • Smooth (involuntary) muscle:
  • No striations. Spindle-shaped cells, one central nucleus.
  • Walls of gut, blood vessels, airways. Contracts slowly; sustained; autonomic control.
  • Why cardiac muscle does not fatigue: its cells are packed with mitochondria and have a dense capillary supply, so aerobic respiration keeps ATP coming.
Mission 5Practical

Identifying muscle under the microscope

  • Practical 4.3.9: examine skeletal, cardiac and smooth muscle in prepared slides, photomicrographs and electron micrographs.
  • Method:
    • Focus at low power first; then switch to high power and use the fine focus only.
    • Look for: striations; number and position of nuclei; branching; intercalated discs; cell shape.
    • Draw a few cells: clear single lines, no shading, labels, magnification stated.
    • Calibrate the eyepiece graticule against a stage micrometer for each objective lens.
  • Worked example (illustrative data), ×400:
    • Step 1: 100 µm on the stage micrometer = 40 eyepiece units → 1 unit = 100 ÷ 40 = 2.5 µm.
    • Step 2: a row of 10 sarcomeres (from one dark band to the 11th) spans 9 eyepiece units → 9 × 2.5 = 22.5 µm.
    • Step 3: one sarcomere = 22.5 ÷ 10 = 2.25 µm. Measure across many to reduce error.
Quick check
?A photomicrograph shows striated cells that branch. Each cell has one central nucleus, and dark lines cross the tissue where cells join end to end. Which tissue is it?
Mission check

?A student examines a prepared slide of diaphragm muscle. The diaphragm contracts in every breath, usually without conscious thought. What will the student see?

Identify muscle by what you see, not by what it does: striations, branching and nuclei tell you the type.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • EPSPs and IPSPs sum on one membrane; an action potential fires only if the total reaches threshold. IPSPs hyperpolarise.

Mission 2

  • Receptors are specific transducers: a stimulus opens (or closes) ion channels, changing the membrane potential; impulses start only at threshold.
  • Rods converge (sensitive, low acuity, no colour); cones each have their own bipolar neurone (high acuity, colour).

Mission 3

  • Cones at the fovea, each with its own bipolar neurone, give acuity and colour vision; converging rods summate sub-threshold stimuli for sensitivity, at the cost of acuity.
  • The iris muscles control the pupil; the ciliary muscle and suspensory ligaments change lens shape; rhodopsin re-synthesis gives dark adaptation; two forward-facing eyes give depth perception.

Mission 4

  • Sarcomeres shorten because actin slides over myosin: I band and H zone narrow, A band stays the same. More overlap, more force.
  • In the presence of Ca²⁺, myosin heads attach to actin and tilt, pulling actin over myosin; each cycle needs ATP.

Mission 5

  • Skeletal muscle (striated, multinucleate fibres) and cardiac muscle (striated, branched, intercalated discs) differ in structure and function.
  • Smooth muscle is unstriated, with spindle-shaped cells.
