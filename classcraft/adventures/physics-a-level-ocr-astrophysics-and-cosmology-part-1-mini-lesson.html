Mission 1

Mission 1: A smudge of light in a telescope: how do astronomers work out what they are looking at?

Make a guess: A smudge of light in a telescope: how do astronomers work out what they are looking at?

Mission 1

Things that orbit

  • Planet: a large body orbiting a star. Massive enough to pull itself round and to clear its orbit of other bodies. The Solar System has 8.
  • Planetary satellite: a body orbiting a planet. Natural ones are moons: the Moon, Titan. Artificial ones are spacecraft.
  • Comet: a small body of ice and dust on a highly elliptical orbit around the Sun.
  • Near the Sun its ice turns to gas: a glowing coma and tails that point away from the Sun.
Mission 1

Bigger and bigger

  • Solar system: a star and everything in orbit around it. Ours: the Sun, 8 planets, their moons, dwarf planets, asteroids, comets.
  • Galaxy: a vast collection of stars, gas, dust and dark matter held together by gravity. Typically about 10¹¹ stars. Ours is the Milky Way.
  • Universe: all of space and time and everything in it. The observable part holds at least 10¹¹ galaxies.
  • Rough sizes:
  • Earth to Sun: 1.5 × 10¹¹ m · Sun to Neptune: 4.5 × 10¹² m
  • Nearest other star: 4.0 × 10¹⁶ m · Milky Way: ~10²¹ m across · Observable universe: ~10²⁷ m across
Quick check
?Which list is in order of increasing size?
Mission check

?Over several nights, a faint object is seen to circle Jupiter, returning to the same side of it every 7 days. What is it?

Astronomers sort objects by what they orbit and by what holds them together: a moon orbits a planet, a planet orbits a star, stars are bound by gravity into galaxies.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How can we read the surface temperature of a star from its light alone?

Make a guess: How can we read the surface temperature of a star from its light alone?

Mission 2

A perfect absorber and emitter

  • All matter above absolute zero turns some of its internal energy into electromagnetic radiation.
  • A black body absorbs all the radiation that falls on it. None is reflected or transmitted.
  • It is also the best possible emitter at its temperature.
  • Its spectrum depends only on its temperature, not on what it is made of.
  • Stars are very good approximations to black bodies.
Quick check
?Predict first. A black body is heated to a higher temperature. How does its emission spectrum change?
Mission 2

Spectrum curves and temperature

  • Plot intensity per unit wavelength against wavelength: a smooth, continuous curve.
  • Hotter body: more at every wavelength. The peak is higher.
  • Hotter body: the peak moves to shorter wavelength.
  • The total power (area under the curve) rises steeply with temperature.
  • Colour shows this: red-hot (cooler) → yellow → white → bluish (hotter).
Mission 2

Wien's displacement law

  • Peak wavelength is inversely proportional to absolute temperature.
  • λmax ∝ 1/T, so λmax T is the same for every black body. Compare with a known star: T2 = T1 × λ1/λ2.
  • T must be in kelvin: T(K) = θ(°C) + 273.15, usually rounded to + 273.
  • The Sun: λmax ≈ 500 nm, so T ≈ 5800 K.
Mission 2

Worked example: a red star

  • A star's spectrum peaks at 850 nm (infrared, just beyond red light).
  • Compare with the Sun (500 nm, 5800 K): T = 5800 × 500/850 = 3.4 × 10³ K
  • Longer peak wavelength → cooler surface. This star looks red.
Mission 2

Stefan–Boltzmann law

  • Total power radiated ∝ surface area × T⁴.
  • Stefan's law: L = 4πr²σT⁴
  • σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴ (Stefan constant).
  • T⁴ matters: double T → 16 × the power.
Quick check
?A small metal sphere of radius 5.0 cm is heated to 527 °C. Treat it as a black body and take σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴. What power does the sphere emit as thermal radiation?
Mission 2

Worked example: the size of a star

  • A star's spectrum peaks at 290 nm. Its luminosity is 1.0 × 10²⁸ W.
  • Step 1, temperature (Wien):
  • T = 5800 × 500/290 = 1.0 × 10⁴ K
  • Step 2, radius (Stefan): L = 4πr²σT⁴ → r = √(L / 4πσT⁴)
  • r = √(1.0 × 10²⁸ ÷ (4π × 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ × (1.0 × 10⁴)⁴)) = 1.2 × 10⁹ m, about 1.7 times the Sun's radius.
Quick check
?A red giant has a luminosity of 100 L⊙ and a surface temperature of 4000 K. A white dwarf has a luminosity of 0.010 L⊙ and a surface temperature of 16 000 K. Both are modelled as black bodies. What is (radius of the red giant)/(radius of the white dwarf)?
Mission check

?The graph shows the intensity per unit wavelength emitted by a black body as a function of wavelength λ. The absolute temperature of the black body is then doubled. At what wavelength does the spectrum of the heated black body peak?

Peak wavelength ∝ 1/T. So the colour of a star (where its spectrum peaks) gives its surface temperature: bluish stars are hotter than reddish ones.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why will the Sun end as a small, fading ember, while far heavier stars blow themselves apart?

Make a guess: Why will the Sun end as a small, fading ember, while far heavier stars blow themselves apart?

Mission 3

A star in balance

  • Gravity pulls every layer of a star inwards.
  • Pressure pushes outwards: hot gas (gas pressure) and radiation (radiation pressure).
  • Fusion in the core keeps the core hot, so the pressure is maintained.
  • Balanced: the star is stable, neither collapsing nor expanding. Huge forces, zero resultant.
  • Birth: a cloud of dust and gas collapses under gravity. It heats up as it shrinks: a protostar.
  • Once the core is hot and dense enough, hydrogen fuses into helium. The pressure stops the collapse: a main-sequence star.
Quick check
?Suppose the rate of fusion in a main-sequence star's core suddenly fell. What would happen to the core at first?
Mission 3

The HR diagram

  • Luminosity (vertical, log scale, in units of the Sun's) against surface temperature (horizontal, log scale).
  • Temperature decreases to the right: hot blue stars on the left, cool red stars on the right.
  • Main sequence: diagonal band, upper left to lower right. About 90% of stars.
  • Red giants: upper right. Red supergiants: across the top.
  • White dwarfs: lower left.
Quick check
?The HR diagram shows the main sequence, the Sun and a star X. Which statement about star X is correct?
Mission 3

What position tells you

  • Same temperature, more luminous → bigger (more surface area).
  • Upper right: cool, yet very luminous → huge.
  • Lower left: hot, yet dim → tiny.
  • On the main sequence, mass sets the position: more massive stars are higher, hotter and more luminous.
  • More massive → burns fuel much faster → shorter main-sequence life.
Mission 3

Mass decides the path

  • Core hydrogen runs out → core contracts and heats up; outer layers expand and cool.
  • Low mass (like the Sun): main sequence → red giant → outer layers drift off as a planetary nebula → core left as a white dwarf.
  • High mass (more than roughly 8 times the Sun's mass): main sequence → red supergiant → supernova → neutron star, or a black hole for the most massive.
  • More mass → stronger gravity → hotter core → heavier elements fused.
  • A white dwarf has no fusion: it slowly cools and fades.
Mission 3

Paths on the HR diagram

  • A star does not slide along the main sequence as it ages.
  • Sun-like star: up and to the right (red giant) → leftwards as the core is exposed → down to the white-dwarf region → fades downwards and to the right as it cools.
  • Massive star: from the upper main sequence, across to the right (red supergiant). After the supernova, the remnant (a neutron star or black hole) is not plotted on the HR diagram.
Mission 3

White dwarfs

  • The hot, exposed core: about the size of the Earth, with about half the Sun's mass (never more than 1.4 times). Extremely dense.
  • No fusion. What stops it collapsing? Electron degeneracy pressure.
  • Electrons cannot be squeezed into the same quantum state: packed tightly, they resist further compression.
  • Chandrasekhar limit: about 1.4 times the Sun's mass. Above this, electron degeneracy pressure cannot support the core.
Mission 3

Neutron stars and black holes

  • Core above the Chandrasekhar limit → collapses further in a supernova.
  • Neutron star: mostly neutrons, about 10 km in radius. Density about that of a nucleus. Often spins rapidly with a strong magnetic field.
  • Even more massive core → black hole: gravity so strong that the escape velocity exceeds the speed of light.
  • Not even light can escape from inside its event horizon.
Quick check
?Astronomers observe a glowing shell of gas expanding gently away from a very hot, very small star at its centre: a planetary nebula. Which describes the central star?
Quick check
?After a star's supernova, the core left behind has a mass of 1.7 times the Sun's mass. What does the core become?
Mission check

?Astronomers find a neutron star at the centre of the expanding remains of a supernova. What can be concluded about the star that produced it, compared with the Sun?

The Sun has too little mass. Its core will never get hot enough to fuse heavy elements, and its leftover core is too small to collapse further: a white dwarf that fades.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why does a neon sign glow in a few sharp shades instead of a full rainbow?

Make a guess: Why does a neon sign glow in a few sharp shades instead of a full rainbow?

Mission 4

Energy levels

  • An electron bound in an atom can have only certain energies: energy levels.
  • Lowest level: the ground state (n = 1). Higher levels: excited states.
  • Zero energy = electron just free of the atom (ionised).
  • So every bound level is negative: energy must be supplied to free the electron.
  • Hydrogen: −13.6 eV, −3.40 eV, −1.51 eV, −0.85 eV, … crowding towards 0.
  • 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Levels may be given in eV or J.
Mission 4

Emitting a photon

  • An electron drops from a higher level to a lower one. The atom emits one photon.
  • Photon energy = energy lost: hf = ΔE.
  • With c = fλ: λ = hc/ΔE.
  • Bigger drop → higher frequency → shorter wavelength.
  • Each transition gives one frequency: one spectral line.
Mission 4

Worked example

  • Hydrogen: n = 2 (−3.40 eV) → n = 1 (−13.6 eV).
  • ΔE = −3.40 − (−13.6) = 10.2 eV.
  • In joules: 10.2 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 1.63 × 10⁻¹⁸ J.
  • f = ΔE/h = 1.632 × 10⁻¹⁸ ÷ 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ = 2.46 × 10¹⁵ Hz.
  • λ = c/f = 1.22 × 10⁻⁷ m (122 nm, ultraviolet).
  • Always use the difference between levels, never one level's value.
Quick check
?A hypothetical atom has only four energy levels below its ionisation energy: −8.00 eV, −5.00 eV, −3.50 eV and −1.00 eV. In a hot gas of these atoms, collisions put atoms into all four levels, and none of the atoms is ionised. What is the longest wavelength of the photons that the gas emits in transitions among these levels? Take h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ and 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
Mission 4

How many lines?

  • An excited electron can drop straight down or in steps.
  • Every pair of levels gives one possible line.
  • 3 levels → 3 lines: 3→2, 3→1, 2→1.
  • Different gaps → different wavelengths. The largest gap gives the shortest wavelength.
Quick check
?An atom has only four energy levels: −1.50 eV, −2.50 eV, −4.00 eV and −6.00 eV. What is the energy of the emitted photon that has the SHORTEST wavelength?
Mission 4

Absorbing a photon; ionisation

  • A photon is absorbed only if its energy exactly equals a gap from the electron's level. All or nothing.
  • Any other photon passes straight through.
  • Ionisation energy: energy to free the electron from the ground state = 0 − E1. Hydrogen: 13.6 eV (2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ J).
  • Any photon with at least this energy can ionise; the extra becomes kinetic energy of the freed electron.
  • From an excited level, less is needed: the ground state needs the most.
Mission 4

Three kinds of spectrum

  • Continuous: all wavelengths. Hot, dense source: a lamp filament, a star's surface.
  • Emission line: bright lines on a dark background. Hot gas at low pressure.
  • Absorption line: a continuous spectrum crossed by dark lines. White light through a cooler gas.
  • The dark lines sit at the same wavelengths as that gas's emission lines.
  • They look dark because the absorbed energy is re-emitted in all directions, so little continues forward.
Quick check
?Predict first. Two discharge tubes, P and Q, each contain a single gas. The emission spectrum of each gas shows exactly four bright lines in the visible region, but none of the wavelengths of the lines from P matches any of the wavelengths of the lines from Q. A student claims that P and Q contain the same element. Which statement is correct?
Mission 4

Evidence and fingerprints

  • Sharp lines, not a smear: atoms change energy only by fixed amounts. Evidence for discrete levels.
  • Each element has its own set of levels, so its own set of lines.
  • Match the lines with laboratory spectra → identify the element.
  • Works in a lamp, a gas cloud or a star's outer layers.
  • Helium was discovered in 1868 from a yellow line in the Sun's spectrum that matched no known element.
Mission check

?An atom has energy levels at −10.0 eV (the ground state), −5.0 eV and −3.0 eV. An atom in its ground state is struck by a photon of energy 3.0 eV. What happens?

An atom can only take in or give out energy equal to a gap between its levels. Neon's gaps are fixed, so its photons, and its colours, are too.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Starlight spread into a rainbow has thin dark gaps. What makes them, and what do they reveal?

Make a guess: Starlight spread into a rainbow has thin dark gaps. What makes them, and what do they reveal?

Mission 5

Where a star's spectrum comes from

  • The star's dense, hot surface emits a continuous spectrum (close to a black body).
  • The light then passes through the star's tenuous atmosphere.
  • Atoms there absorb photons of exactly the energies that lift electrons between their energy levels.
  • They re-emit in all directions, so less light of those wavelengths travels on towards us.
  • Result: dark lines across the continuous spectrum.
Mission 5

Lines are fingerprints

  • Each element has its own energy levels, so its own set of line wavelengths.
  • Compare the star's dark lines with lines measured in the laboratory.
  • A full match → that element is in the star's atmosphere.
  • Hydrogen: 656, 486, 434 and 410 nm. Sodium: a close pair near 589 nm.
Mission 5

Seen through a diffraction grating

  • Each wavelength gives bright maxima where d sin θ = nλ.
  • Zero order (straight through): all wavelengths overlap. The source's own colour.
  • Line source: in each order, a set of separate coloured lines, mirrored either side of the centre.
  • Longer wavelength → larger angle: red further out than violet.
  • Absorption: in each order, a continuous band crossed by dark lines.
Quick check
?A student looks at an unknown source through a diffraction grating. At the centre she sees a white line. On each side, she sees a full band from violet to red, crossed by a few thin dark lines. What is the source?
Mission 5

Worked example: line to energy gap

  • A dark line at 656 nm in a star's spectrum.
  • Photon energy: E = hc/λ = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸) ÷ (656 × 10⁻⁹) = 3.03 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
  • In eV: 3.03 × 10⁻¹⁹ ÷ 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 1.90 eV.
  • Hydrogen has levels at −3.40 eV and −1.51 eV: gap 1.89 eV ✓ (matches within rounding).
  • So hydrogen atoms in the atmosphere absorb these photons: electrons jump from −3.40 eV up to −1.51 eV.
Quick check
?A star's continuous spectrum has dark lines at 410, 434, 486, 589.0, 589.6 and 656 nm. Laboratory emission lines: hydrogen 410, 434, 486, 656 nm; sodium 589.0, 589.6 nm; helium 447, 588, 668 nm. What can be deduced?
Quick check
?A star's spectrum has a dark line at 589 nm. What energy gap in the absorbing atoms does it match?
Mission check

?During a total solar eclipse, the Moon just covers the Sun's bright, dense surface, but the Sun's thin outer atmosphere still shows round the edge. A spectrum of that thin rim is taken. What does it show?

The dark gaps are made by atoms in a star's thin outer layers absorbing photons of exact energies. Their wavelengths reveal which elements are there.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Satellite → planet → solar system → galaxy → universe: each is held in orbit or bound by gravity within the next.

Mission 2

  • A black body absorbs all incident radiation; its spectrum depends only on T. Hotter: more at every λ, peak at shorter λ.
  • Wien: λmax ∝ 1/T.
  • L = 4πr²σT⁴.
  • Star's radius: T from Wien, then r from Stefan.

Mission 3

  • Stable star: gravity in, gas and radiation pressure out, kept up by fusion.
  • HR diagram: luminosity up, temperature decreasing to the right; main sequence, giants upper right, white dwarfs lower left.
  • Mass decides the end: low mass → planetary nebula + white dwarf; high mass → supernova + neutron star or black hole.
  • White dwarfs are held up by electron degeneracy pressure, up to the Chandrasekhar limit (about 1.4 solar masses).

Mission 4

  • Levels are negative (0 = ionised). A transition emits or absorbs one photon: hf = ΔE, λ = hc/ΔE.
  • Line spectra are evidence of discrete levels, and each element's lines identify it, in a lamp or a star.

Mission 5

  • A star's spectrum: continuous emission from the dense surface, with dark absorption lines from the thin atmosphere.
  • Each element absorbs at its own wavelengths, so the dark lines identify the elements in a star.
