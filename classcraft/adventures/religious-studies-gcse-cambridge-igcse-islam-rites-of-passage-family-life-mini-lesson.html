Mission 1

Mission 1: Why are the first words a Muslim baby hears the same as the call to prayer?

Make a guess: Why are the first words a Muslim baby hears the same as the call to prayer?

Mission 1

First words: adhan and iqamah

  • Soon after birth, the father or an elder whispers the adhan (call to prayer) into the baby's right ear.
  • Many also say the iqamah (the call that begins prayer) into the left ear.
  • So the first words the child hears are "Allahu akbar" and the Shahadah: there is no god but Allah, and Muhammad is His messenger.
  • Meaning: the child is born into the ummah; life begins with Tawhid and the call to worship.
  • Tahnik: a tiny piece of softened date (or honey) is rubbed on the baby's gums, following the Prophet's practice (Sahih al-Bukhari, paraphrase). It expresses hope for a sweet, good life.
  • Muslims believe every child is born in a natural state of submission to Allah (fitrah).
Mission 1

The seventh day: name, hair and aqiqah

  • Tasmiyah (naming): often on the 7th day. A name with a good meaning: a prophet's name, or 'Abd ("servant") with one of Allah's names, e.g. Abdullah, Abdur-Rahman.
  • Head-shaving: the baby's hair is shaved; its weight in silver (or its value) is given to the poor, following the example of Fatimah for her son Hasan.
  • Aqiqah: an animal is sacrificed in thanks for the child. In Sunni practice, often two sheep or goats for a boy and one for a girl; in Shi'a practice one for either. The meat is shared with family, friends and the poor.
  • Khitan (circumcision) of boys: a sunnah from Ibrahim. Some do it in the first week; others later in childhood.
  • Importance: thanks to Allah, a good start in the faith, and the community sharing the family's joy.

Mission 1

Marriage: the nikah

  • Nikah: marriage, a contract (aqd nikah) and a sacred bond. Marriage is strongly encouraged as the Prophet's sunnah.
  • Consent: both bride and groom must freely agree; a forced marriage is not valid.
  • Offer and acceptance (ijab and qabul) are spoken before witnesses: in Sunni law two adult Muslim witnesses are required; Shi'a law recommends witnesses but does not require them.
  • Wali: a guardian, usually the bride's father, represents her in most Sunni schools; the Hanafi school allows an adult woman to contract her own marriage.
  • Mahr: a gift from the groom to the bride, agreed in the contract. It belongs to her alone.
  • The contract is signed; a short sermon (khutbah) and du'a follow, often in a mosque or at home.
  • Conditions can be added to the contract, e.g. the bride's right to continue her studies.

Husbands are told to give the women they marry a "free gift of their marriage portions" (Qur'an 4:4, Pickthall).

Show answer

Show answer

Mission 1

Customs and the walimah

  • Before: families meet; in South Asian families, a mehndi (henna) evening for the bride.
  • During: the nikah itself is short and simple: contract, mahr, witnesses, khutbah, du'a for the couple.
  • After: the walimah, a wedding feast given by the groom's family. The Prophet told a newly married companion to give a walimah "even with one sheep" (Sahih al-Bukhari, paraphrase).
  • Much of the rest (dress colours, music, gold) is culture, varying between countries.
  • Britain: a nikah alone is not a legal marriage. Many couples also have a civil ceremony, or marry in a mosque registered for marriages.

Mission 1

Divorce, khul' and remarriage

  • Divorce is allowed but a last resort; many Muslims cite a hadith calling it the most disliked of permitted things (Sunan Abu Dawud, paraphrase).
  • First, reconciliation: "appoint an arbiter from his folk and an arbiter from her folk" (Qur'an 4:35, Pickthall).
  • Talaq: divorce pronounced by the husband, followed by a waiting period (iddah, about three months, 2:228). Reconciliation is possible during it, up to twice (2:229).
  • Khul': the wife may end the marriage, usually returning the mahr ("if the woman ransom herself", Qur'an 2:229, Pickthall).
  • A qadi or, in Britain, a Shari'ah council can also dissolve a marriage at the wife's request.
  • Differences: Shi'a law needs two witnesses for talaq and rejects "triple talaq" said at once; many Muslim countries have restricted it too.
  • Remarriage is allowed for both after the iddah.
Mission 1

Family roles, young and old

  • Roles: traditionally the husband provides and protects; the wife leads the home and children's upbringing. Many families now share roles.
  • Nurture of the young: a good name, love, education in faith (Qur'an, salah) and in the world; the Prophet taught that every person is a shepherd responsible for those in their care (Sahih al-Bukhari, paraphrase).
  • Care of the elderly:

> "If one of them or both of them attain old age with thee, say not "Fie" unto them nor repulse them, but speak unto them a gracious word." (Qur'an 17:23, Pickthall)

  • Many Muslims care for parents at home rather than in care homes; some families feel strain where both partners work.
  • Tradition and change: some communities keep traditional roles as Islamic; others support change as true to Islam's justice.
Mission 1

Death and the funeral

  • Beliefs: life is a test; death comes at the time Allah decrees (sovereignty of Allah); then judgement and akhirah (life after death).

> "Every soul will taste of death. And ye will be paid on the Day of Resurrection only that which ye have fairly earned." (Qur'an 3:185, Pickthall)

  • At death: the dying person hears or says the Shahadah; eyes are closed; mourners say "we are Allah's and unto Him we are returning" (2:156).
  • Ghusl: the body is washed, by people of the same sex, usually family.
  • Kafan: wrapped in simple white cloths: rich and poor alike.
  • Salat al-janazah: funeral prayer said standing, with no bowing or prostration.
  • Burial: as soon as possible, usually within a day; never cremation. The body lies on its right side, facing Makkah.
  • Graves are simple. Mourners pray for the dead.
Mission 1Diversity

How long do Muslims mourn?

  • Weeping is allowed: the Prophet wept when his infant son Ibrahim died (Sahih al-Bukhari, paraphrase). Loud wailing and tearing clothes are discouraged.
  • Three days of mourning for relatives (hadith in Sahih al-Bukhari); visitors bring food so the family need not cook.
  • Widows: a waiting period of "four months and ten days" (Qur'an 2:234, Pickthall), with no remarriage and simple dress.
  • Shi'a Muslims often hold majalis for the dead on the 3rd, 7th and 40th days, and yearly.
  • Some Sunni cultures, e.g. in South Asia, also mark the 40th day; other Sunni scholars reject this as bid'ah (an added practice).
  • Why the differences: different hadith collections and schools, and local culture.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Muslims believe you live only once. What happens after death, and why does it matter now?

Make a guess: Muslims believe you live only once. What happens after death, and why does it matter now?

Mission 2

Akhirah: one life, then the hereafter

  • Akhirah means "the hereafter" or "the last": life after death. It is an article of faith.
  • Muslims believe each person has one life; there is no rebirth. "Every soul shall have a taste of death: And only on the Day of Judgment shall you be paid your full recompense." (3:185, Yusuf Ali)
  • This life is a test and a preparation (2:155; 67:2): what a person believes and does now shapes eternity.
  • Belief in the sovereignty of Allah: life and death belong to Him. On hearing of a death, Muslims say: "To Allah We belong, and to Him is our return." (2:156, Yusuf Ali)
  • So the purpose of life is to worship Allah and live as He asks.
Mission 2

Death and barzakh

  • Muslims believe that, at the time Allah has set, the Angel of Death (Izra'il) takes the soul (32:11).
  • The soul enters barzakh, "barrier": the state between death and the Day of Resurrection. "Before them is a Partition till the Day they are raised up." (23:100, Yusuf Ali)
  • There is no going back: in 23:100 a wrongdoer begs to return and do good, and is refused.
  • Hadith teach that two angels, Munkar and Nakir, question the dead in the grave: Who is your Lord? What is your religion? Who is your prophet?
  • The grave becomes a foretaste: wide and peaceful for the faithful, narrow and painful for wrongdoers (hadith, paraphrase).
Mission 2

The Day of Judgement

  • Muslims believe Yawm al-Qiyamah (Day of Resurrection) begins when Israfil sounds the trumpet (39:68).
  • All the dead are raised, body and soul, and gathered before Allah.
  • Each person receives their book of deeds: in the right hand for the saved (69:19), the left for the lost (69:25).
  • Deeds are weighed on the scales (mizan): "We shall set up scales of justice … not a soul will be dealt with unjustly in the least" (21:47, Yusuf Ali).
  • Many Muslims believe all must cross a bridge, as-Sirat, over hell to reach Paradise (hadith).
  • Muslims believe the Prophet will intercede (shafa'ah) for believers, by Allah's permission; Shi'a Muslims believe the Imams will also intercede.
  • Judgement belongs to Allah alone: He is "Master of the Day of Judgment" (1:4).

Mission 2

Al-Jannah, Paradise

  • Jannah ("garden"): the reward for faith and good deeds.
  • Described as gardens "under which rivers flow", with "beautiful mansions" (9:72); rivers of water, milk and honey (47:15).
  • Yet "no person knows what delights of the eye are kept hidden (in reserve) for them" (32:17, Yusuf Ali).
  • The greatest joy is not physical: "the greatest bliss is the good pleasure of Allah" (9:72, Yusuf Ali).
Mission 2

Jahannam, hell

  • Muslims believe Jahannam is a place of fire and suffering, and of separation from Allah's mercy.
  • "To it are seven gates: for each of those gates is a (special) class (of sinners) assigned." (15:44, Yusuf Ali)
  • Many scholars teach that believers who sinned may be purified in hell and then leave, by Allah's mercy and the Prophet's intercession; those who knowingly rejected Allah remain.
  • Only Allah knows who will be where: Muslims are taught not to declare others destined for hell.
Mission check

?Nadia says: "Allah is Most Merciful, so what I do now doesn't really matter for the akhirah." Which reply would most Muslims give?

For Muslims, one life, then barzakh, resurrection and judgement: Allah's mercy is real, and so is every deed.

Mission 3

Halal and haram

  • Halal: permitted, lawful.
  • Haram: forbidden, unlawful.
  • These are rulings of shari'ah, the "straight path" of Islamic law.
  • They cover far more than food: drink, clothing, money, speech, relationships, work.
  • Starting point: most scholars hold that everyday things are halal unless forbidden. The list of haram things is short; the halal world is wide.
  • Significance: keeping halal is worship. Muslims believe the rules come from Allah, who knows what is good and harmful for people.
  • It also marks Muslim identity: a halal meal, a hijab or refusing a drink shows faith in public.
Mission 3

Where the rules come from, and five categories

  • Origins: the Qur'an; the Sunnah of the Prophet (hadith); and scholars, using agreement (ijma) and reasoning by analogy (qiyas).
  • Example of qiyas: the Qur'an forbids wine because it intoxicates, so scholars also forbid drugs that intoxicate.
  • The Prophet taught that the halal is clear and the haram is clear, with doubtful matters between them that the careful person avoids (Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim, paraphrase).
  • Scholars sort actions into five categories:
  • Fard or wajib (obligatory): e.g. the five daily prayers.
  • Mustahabb (recommended): e.g. extra prayers, giving sadaqah.
  • Mubah (neutral, simply allowed): e.g. choosing which fruit to eat.
  • Makruh (disliked): better avoided, but not sinful.
  • Haram (forbidden): sinful, e.g. drinking alcohol.
  • The first four all fall within the halal (permitted) in its wide sense.

Mission 3Read the source

Forbidden foods

> "He hath forbidden you only carrion, and blood, and swineflesh, and that which hath been immolated to (the name of) any other than Allah. But he who is driven by necessity, neither craving nor transgressing, it is no sin for him." (Qur'an 2:173, Pickthall)

  • Surah 5:3 repeats this list and adds animals killed by strangling, beating, falling or goring, unless properly slaughtered (Qur'an 5:3, paraphrase).
  • "Immolated" means sacrificed or slaughtered.

Q1 Name the four forbidden foods.

Show answer

Animals that died by themselves, blood, pork, and meat offered to anything other than Allah.

Q2 When may a Muslim eat a forbidden food?

Show answer

Only under real necessity, such as starvation, and no more than needed.

Mission 3

Halal food in practice

  • Dhabihah (halal slaughter): a sane adult Muslim (many Sunni scholars also accept Jews and Christians) uses a very sharp knife, says Allah's name (bismillah, Allahu akbar), and cuts the throat in one swift stroke. Blood is drained.
  • Reason: respect for the life Allah gave, and as little suffering as possible.
  • Stunning: some Muslims accept meat from animals stunned first if the animal is still alive when cut; others accept only unstunned meat.
  • Fish and seafood: most Sunni scholars allow all; the Hanafi school allows only fish; Shi'a scholars allow only fish with scales, and prawns.
  • Hidden ingredients: gelatine, animal fats, alcohol in flavourings. Many Muslims look for a halal certificate.
  • Some Muslims treat doubtful products as makruh and avoid them; others eat them if no haram ingredient is known.
Mission 3

Alcohol and intoxicants

> "Strong drink and games of chance and idols and divining arrows are only an infamy of Satan's handiwork. Leave it aside in order that ye may succeed." (Qur'an 5:90, Pickthall)

  • The next verse gives the reason: Satan uses drink and gambling to bring enmity and to turn people from remembering Allah and from prayer (Qur'an 5:91, paraphrase).
  • The ban came in stages: drink has some benefit but more harm (2:219); do not pray while drunk (4:43); then leave it entirely (5:90).
  • The Prophet taught that every intoxicant is forbidden (Sahih Muslim, paraphrase). By qiyas this covers drugs.
  • Many Muslims also avoid selling or serving alcohol, following a hadith that blames all who make, carry, serve or sell it.

Modesty for men and women

> "Tell the believing men to lower their gaze and be modest. That is purer for them. Lo! Allah is aware of what they do." (Qur'an 24:30, Pickthall)

> "And tell the believing women to lower their gaze and be modest, and to display of their adornment only that which is apparent, and to draw their veils over their bosoms" (Qur'an 24:31, Pickthall)

  • 24:31 then lists close family before whom women need not cover their adornment.

Show answer

Mission 3

Modesty and dress

  • Haya (modesty): a quality of character, shown in behaviour, speech and dress.
  • Men: cover at least from navel to knee; loose clothing. The Prophet forbade gold and silk for men, permitting them to women (Sunan Abu Dawud, paraphrase).
  • Women: most scholars teach covering the whole body except face and hands in front of men outside close family.
  • Hijab means "covering": in everyday use, a headscarf. Niqab: a face veil. Jilbab, abaya: long outer garments. Chador, burqa: full coverings in some countries.
  • Both: clothes should not be tight or see-through, worn for pride, or imitate the other sex.
  • So halal and haram apply to clothing as well as food: silk and gold for men, and immodest dress, are haram.
Mission 3Diversity

Divergent interpretations of dress

  • Majority view: 24:31 and 33:59 require women to cover their hair; the hijab is obligatory.
  • Niqab: a minority of scholars hold it obligatory; many see it as recommended or a personal choice; some say it is not required at all.
  • A minority of modern Muslims: the Qur'an commands modesty, not one garment; covering the hair reflects culture and history.
  • Reasons women give for wearing hijab: obedience to Allah, Muslim identity, being valued for character not looks, dignity.
  • Reasons some do not: they see modesty as being in the heart and behaviour; local laws or safety; personal conviction.
  • Laws differ: Iran requires women to cover their hair; France bans religious symbols such as the headscarf in state schools. In Britain, schools set uniform rules.
  • Many Muslims stress that modesty must be sincere, coming from the heart, not only from rules or pressure.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Birth: adhan and iqamah, tahnik, tasmiyah on the 7th day, head-shaving with charity, aqiqah, khitan for boys.
  • Marriage: nikah with consent, offer and acceptance, witnesses, contract and mahr (4:4), then walimah; love and mercy (30:21).
  • Divorce is a last resort: talaq, khul', iddah, remarriage.
  • Death: ghusl, white kafan, salat al-janazah, quick burial facing Makkah; mourning periods differ (three days; widows four months and ten days; Shi'a 3rd, 7th, 40th days).

Mission 2

  • In Muslim belief, Akhirah means one life, then death, barzakh, resurrection and judgement; this life is a test and a preparation.
  • Muslims believe every deed is recorded and weighed; Allah is just and merciful; Jannah rewards, Jahannam brings justice.

Mission 3

  • Halal (permitted) and haram (forbidden) come from the Qur'an, Sunnah and scholars; most things are halal unless forbidden.
  • Actions fall into five categories, from obligatory to forbidden.
  • Forbidden: carrion, blood, pork, meat dedicated to others than Allah (2:173), and all intoxicants (5:90); Surah 24:30–31 commands modesty for both sexes, and Muslims interpret dress differently.
