Mission 1

A baby has nearly all its neurons. So why does its brain triple?

Make a guess: A baby has nearly all its neurons. So why does its brain triple?

Development · the developing brain

From a tube to a brain

  • In the first few weeks after conception, the nervous system is a simple neural tube.
  • Its front end swells into three parts: the forebrain, midbrain and hindbrain. The rest becomes the spinal cord.
Early partDevelops into
Forebrainthe cortex (cerebrum), thalamus and hypothalamus
Midbrainthe top part of the brain stem
Hindbrainthe medulla and pons (lower brain stem) and the cerebellum
Development · the developing brain

Built from the bottom up

  • The parts mature in order, from the oldest, deepest parts to the newest.
PartMain jobWhen it is ready
Brain stem (incl. medulla)autonomic functions: breathing, heart rate, swallowingalready working at birth
Thalamusrelays information from the senses to the cortex: sensory processingworking at birth, then refined
Cerebellummovement, balance and coordinationgrows fast as babies learn to sit, crawl and walk
Cortexcognition: thinking, language, planninglast; the front is still maturing in the teens
Development · the developing brain

What grows after birth

  • A neuron is a nerve cell that carries signals. A newborn has almost all its neurons.
  • Neurons pass signals across tiny gaps called synapses.
  • After birth, neurons branch and form huge numbers of new synapses. Fatty myelin sheaths and support cells add weight.
  • Rarely used synapses are later removed: synaptic pruning.

Development · the developing brain

Nature and nurture

  • Nature: genes set the basic plan, such as the order in which brain parts form and mature.
  • Nurture: experience shapes the details. Synapses a child uses are kept; unused ones are pruned.
  • Poor nutrition in pregnancy or neglect can harm brain development; talk, play and care support it.
  • So brain development is an interaction of nature and nurture.

Mission 2

A hidden toy is "gone" to a baby; a year later they find it. Why?

Make a guess: A hidden toy is "gone" to a baby; a year later they find it. Why?

Development · Piaget's theory

Schemas: how knowledge is built

  • A schema is a mental framework of knowledge about something, built from experience.
  • Assimilation: fitting a new experience into an existing schema (a toddler calls a cow "doggy").
  • Accommodation: changing a schema, or making a new one, when an experience doesn't fit ("cows say moo").
  • Children build schemas actively, by exploring.
Quick check
?A girl's schema for "bird" is "things that fly". At the zoo she learns a penguin is a bird, so her schema becomes "birds have feathers and beaks, and some can't fly". Which process is this?
Development · Piaget's theory

Four stages in a fixed order

StageAge (approx.)Key features
Sensorimotor0–2learns through senses and movement
Pre-operational2–7uses language and symbols; thinking is not yet logical
Concrete operational7–11logical thinking about real objects
Formal operational11+abstract and hypothetical thinking
  • The stages are invariant: every child goes through them in the same order without skipping, though ages vary.
  • Maturation makes a child ready for the next stage; exploring the world moves them on.
Development · Piaget's theory

Three ideas from the early stages

  • Object permanence: knowing that an object still exists when out of sight. It develops at about 8 months, in the sensorimotor stage. Before that, out of sight is out of mind.
  • Egocentrism (pre-operational): seeing the world only from your own point of view.
  • Animism (pre-operational): believing objects are alive and have feelings ("the sun follows me because it likes me").

Development · Piaget's theory

Piaget in the classroom

  • Readiness: a child learns a concept only when at the right stage, so teaching matches the stage.
  • Active learning: children learn by exploring and doing; the teacher sets up tasks, not just tells.
  • Concept of intelligence: not a fixed amount but a way of thinking that develops as schemas grow.

Mission check

?A hidden toy is "gone" to a baby; a year later they find it. Why?

The younger baby hasn't yet developed object permanence, so out of sight is out of mind. At about 8 months, in the sensorimotor stage, the baby's schemas change: it understands that hidden objects still exist, so it searches for them.

Mission 3

Pour juice into a taller glass and a 5-year-old sees more. Why?

Make a guess: Pour juice into a taller glass and a 5-year-old sees more. Why?

Development · testing Piaget's theory

Conservation

  • Conservation: understanding that a quantity stays the same when its appearance changes (number, volume, mass).
  • Pre-operational children fail because of centration: they focus on one feature, such as the height of the juice, and ignore the width.
  • They also lack reversibility: they can't mentally pour the juice back.
  • At about 7 (concrete operational), children use decentration (weighing height and width together) and reversibility, so they conserve.
Development · testing Piaget's theory

Egocentrism and how it fades

  • Egocentrism: a pre-operational child assumes other people see what they see.
  • Piaget tested this with a model of three mountains, asking which view a doll could see. Young children tended to pick their own view.
  • Egocentrism declines as children learn to decentre from their own viewpoint, mainly during the concrete operational stage: the reduction of egocentricity.
Key study Development · testing Piaget's theory

McGarrigle and Donaldson (1974)

Aim
To see whether children conserve number when the change to the counters looks accidental rather than deliberate.
Method
80 children aged 4 to 6 in Edinburgh. Two equal rows of counters were changed either by the researcher or "by accident" by a "naughty teddy" glove puppet. Children were asked if the rows still had the same number.
Results
Many more children conserved when the teddy changed the row than when the researcher did.
Conclusion
Children can conserve earlier than Piaget claimed; an adult's deliberate change suggests the answer should change.
Strength
The task made human sense to young children, so it may show their real ability better.
Weakness
Excited by the teddy, some children may not have noticed the row had changed at all.
Key study Development · testing Piaget's theory

Hughes (1975)

Aim
To test whether children are less egocentric when a perspective task makes sense to them.
Method
30 children aged 3½ to 5. Two walls crossed to make four sections; a policeman doll looked into some sections. The child hid a boy doll where the policeman couldn't see him, then hid him from two policemen.
Results
Most children hid the boy correctly; with two policemen, about 90% of the 4-year-olds still succeeded.
Conclusion
Children of about 4 can take another person's viewpoint; Piaget underestimated them because his mountains task made little sense to them.
Strength
A familiar hide-and-seek game made the task meaningful.
Weakness
Mistakes in practice trials were explained, so children may have been helped to the answer.

Mission 4

Telling a child "you're so clever" can make them do worse. Why?

Make a guess: Telling a child "you're so clever" can make them do worse. Why?

Development · Dweck's mindset theory

Two mindsets

  • A mindset is a belief about whether your abilities can change (Dweck).
  • Fixed mindset: ability is fixed. Needing effort means you're not clever, so you avoid challenges and give up after failing.
  • Growth mindset: ability grows through effort, practice and good strategies. Mistakes are chances to learn.
Development · Dweck's mindset theory

Praise and beliefs about ability

  • Person praise ("You're so clever") links success to a fixed trait; after failing, the child decides they aren't clever.
  • Praise for effort (process praise: "You worked hard") links success to things they control, building a growth mindset.
  • Example study: Mueller and Dweck (1998): children praised for intelligence did worse after failing than those praised for effort.
  • Self-efficacy: belief that you can succeed at a task. A growth mindset protects it.
Quick check
?After a race, a coach tells a young swimmer: "You're a natural. You were born to swim." According to Dweck, which mindset might this encourage?
Development · Dweck's mindset theory

Mindsets in school, and the critics

  • Schools praise effort and strategy, say "not yet" instead of "fail", and teach that the brain changes with practice.
  • Large reviews find mindset programmes raise grades only slightly on average, perhaps more for students at risk of low achievement.

Mission check

?Telling a child "you're so clever" can make them do worse. Why?

Praise for being clever suggests success comes from a fixed trait. When a task goes wrong, the child concludes they're not clever, avoids challenges and gives up: a fixed mindset. Praise for effort and strategy links success to things they can change, encouraging a growth mindset.

Mission 5

Teach a "visual learner" with pictures: will they learn more?

Make a guess: Teach a "visual learner" with pictures: will they learn more?

Development · Willingham's learning theory

The learning styles idea

  • A learning style is a person's preferred way of taking in information, e.g. visual, auditory or kinaesthetic (VAK).
  • Another version divides people into verbalisers, who prefer words (reading, listening, talking), and visualisers, who prefer pictures, diagrams and images.
  • The claim: students learn more when teaching matches their style.
  • In one survey, 93% of UK teachers believed this.
Development · Willingham's learning theory

Willingham's criticism

  • Daniel Willingham, a cognitive psychologist, calls learning styles a myth: there is no good evidence that matching teaching to a style improves learning.
  • People do have preferences, but in controlled tests students taught "in their style" don't learn more.
  • The best method depends on the content, not the learner: everyone learns a map from a picture and pronunciation by listening.
  • People are more alike than different in how they learn.
Development · Willingham's learning theory

What does help learning

  • Meaning: you remember what you think about ("memory is the residue of thought"). Lessons should make students think about what material means.
  • Factual knowledge precedes skill: you need facts before you can think critically or solve problems in a topic. Known facts free up working memory.
  • Practice and effort: practise until knowledge and skills become automatic. Ability can grow with effort.

Mission check

?Teach a "visual learner" with pictures: will they learn more?

Probably not. Controlled tests find no benefit from matching teaching to a preferred style, and the best method depends on the content. Willingham says learning improves when students think about meaning, build factual knowledge and practise with effort.

Recap

Big ideas

  • The brain starts as a neural tube; the forebrain, midbrain and hindbrain develop into the cortex, thalamus, brain stem (medulla) and cerebellum, maturing from autonomic functions to sensory processing, movement and cognition.
  • After birth the brain grows mainly by forming synapses and myelin, and then pruning unused synapses.
  • Nature sets the plan and order; nurture (experience, nutrition, care) shapes the connections.
  • Children build schemas through assimilation and accommodation as they explore the world.
  • Thinking develops through four invariant stages: sensorimotor (object permanence), pre-operational (egocentrism, animism), concrete operational (logic about real objects) and formal operational (abstract thought).
  • Applied to education: readiness, active learning and a view of intelligence that develops.
  • Pre-operational children fail conservation tasks through centration and a lack of reversibility; at about 7 they decentre, and egocentrism reduces.
  • The naughty teddy and policeman doll studies suggest Piaget underestimated young children: when tasks make sense, they conserve and take others' views earlier.
  • A fixed mindset sees ability as fixed; a growth mindset sees it as growing through effort, practice and strategies.
  • Praise for effort builds a growth mindset and protects self-efficacy; person praise can do the opposite.
  • Evidence supports the theory, but large reviews suggest school mindset programmes have only small average effects.
  • Learning styles (e.g. VAK, verbalisers and visualisers) are a popular idea, but Willingham argues that matching teaching to them doesn't improve learning; the method should fit the content.
  • What helps: thinking about meaning, factual knowledge before skill, and practice with effort.
