Mission 1

Mission 1: How can just two words tell you where any living thing fits among millions?

Make a guess: How can just two words tell you where any living thing fits among millions?

Mission 1

Too many to know one by one

  • About 1.2 million species are catalogued; an estimated 8.7 million species of eukaryotes exist.
  • Classification: sorting organisms into groups.
  • Why classify?
    • identify an organism and name it without confusion;
    • predict features of a group's members;
    • show evolutionary relationships;
Mission 1

Groups within groups

  • Each group is a taxon (plural taxa).
  • Domain → kingdom → phylum → class → order → family → genus → species.
  • Smaller groups sit inside larger ones, with no overlap.
  • Lower taxa: fewer species, more features shared.
  • Humans: Eukarya · Animalia · Chordata · Mammalia · Primates · Hominidae · Homo · Homo sapiens.
Quick check
?The red fox, Vulpes vulpes, and the grey wolf, Canis lupus, are both placed in the family Canidae. Which statement must be true?
Mission 1

Two-part names

  • Binomial system (Linnaeus): every species has a two-part name.
  • 1st word: genus, with a capital letter.
  • 2nd word: species, all lower case. It means nothing on its own.
  • Italics in print; underline when handwritten.
  • Same genus = similar traits, usually close relatives: Panthera leo (lion), Panthera tigris (tiger).
  • After first use, shorten the genus: P. leo.
  • Say: "Homo sapiens" — not: "Homo Sapiens", and not "sapiens" alone.
Mission 1

Why not just use common names?

  • A binomial is universal: the same in every country and language.
  • Common names mislead. "Robin" means Erithacus rubecula in Britain but Turdus migratorius in North America.
  • One species can have many local names; a "starfish" is not a fish.
  • The genus shows at once which species are close relatives.
  • These are the advantages of the binomial system.
Quick check
?Which statement about the scientific name of the tiger, Panthera tigris, is correct?
Mission 1

Classify by ancestry

  • Phylogenetic classification: groups reflect evolutionary origins and relationships.
  • Ideal: every member of a group descends from one common ancestor.
  • Advantage: features of a group can be predicted. A newly found member probably shares them.
  • Evidence: anatomy, plus DNA and protein sequences.
  • Classification is tentative: it changes as new evidence arrives.
Quick check
?What is the main advantage of a classification in which every group corresponds to evolutionary relationships?
Mission check

?The raven is Corvus corax, the rook is Corvus frugilegus and the magpie is Pica pica. What can be concluded from these names alone?

Two words: the genus places it among its closest relatives; the species word picks it out from them.

  • Fewer differences → more recent common ancestor. P and Q are the closest relatives here.
  • DNA differs more than protein: some base changes do not change the amino acid.
  • Anatomy points the same way: a human arm, a bat wing and a whale flipper share one bone plan, inherited from a common ancestor.

Quick check
?The amino acid sequence of the same protein was compared in four species. Compared with species W, species X differs at 2 positions, species Y at 15 positions and species Z at 31 positions. Which conclusion is supported by the data?
Mission 2

Shared by all life

  • The genetic code is almost universal: the same codons code for the same amino acids in bacteria, plants and animals.
  • The code is not the only one possible: slightly different codes work (e.g. in mitochondria). Sharing one code is best explained by common ancestry of all life.
  • So a copy of a human gene, with its introns removed, can be put into a bacterium, and the bacterium makes a chain with the same amino acid sequence.
Quick check
?A copy of the human insulin gene, with its introns removed, was inserted into a bacterium, which then made a polypeptide with the same amino acid sequence as the human one. Which conclusion does this best support, and why?
Mission check

?Three hypothetical beetle species are compared. Species A and C look almost identical; species B looks quite different. Illustrative genomic DNA data: A and B differ at 0.8% of aligned bases, A and C at 6.1%, and B and C at 6.0%. Which conclusion is best supported?

When looks and genomes disagree, the genome records the shared ancestry.

Mission 3

Clues to who is related

  • Relatives share features inherited from a common ancestor.
  • Evidence:
    • anatomy: homologous structures, in living species and fossils;
    • DNA and mRNA base sequences;
    • amino acid sequences of proteins.
  • After two lineages split, mutations build up separately in each.
  • So: fewer differences → more recent common ancestor → closer relatives.
  • Say: "share a more recent common ancestor" — not: "one evolved from the other".

Mission 3

Reading a cladogram

  • A cladogram shows the order in which lineages branched.
  • Root: the base; the common ancestor of every group shown.
  • Node: a branch point; a hypothetical common ancestor, where one lineage split in two.
  • Terminal branch: ends at one group.
  • Clade: an ancestor and all of its descendants.
  • How related? Find the most recent node they share. Not which tips sit side by side.
  • No living group at a tip is the ancestor of another.
Quick check

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why are some microbes that look just like bacteria placed further from them than you are from a mushroom?

Make a guess: Why are some microbes that look just like bacteria placed further from them than you are from a mushroom?

Mission 4

One chemistry, many cell plans

  • Every living thing contains the same four classes of biochemicals: carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, nucleic acids.
  • All store genetic information in DNA and use ATP.
  • The big differences are in cell structure and in how organisms get energy and carbon.
  • These differences sort life into its largest groups.
Mission 4

Five kingdoms: the small and simple

  • The five-kingdom system was built on observable features: cell type, nucleus, cell wall, body form, nutrition.
  • Prokaryotae:
    • no nucleus, no membrane-bound organelles; circular DNA;
    • small (70S) ribosomes; cell wall usually present (in bacteria, made of peptidoglycan);
    • mostly single cells; reproduce by division (binary fission).
  • Protoctista:
    • eukaryotic;
    • mostly single cells, or little cell differentiation;
    • some autotrophs, some heterotrophs.
Mission 4

Five kingdoms: fungi, plants, animals

  • Fungi:
    • eukaryotic; threads called hyphae, with chitin cell walls;
    • heterotrophs: release enzymes onto food, digest it outside the body, absorb the products (many are saprotrophs).
  • Plantae: eukaryotic, multicellular; autotrophs (producers); chlorophyll in chloroplasts; cellulose cell walls.
  • Animalia: eukaryotic, multicellular; heterotrophs; no cell walls; capable of locomotion.
  • Say: "fungi are heterotrophs with chitin walls" — not: "fungi are plants".
Quick check
?A newly described organism grows as a mass of branching threads through dead leaves. Its cells have nuclei and walls containing chitin. It releases enzymes onto the leaves and absorbs the digested products. In which kingdom should it be placed?
Mission 4

Three domains

  • 1977: Carl Woese and George Fox compared ribosomal RNA (rRNA) base sequences from many organisms.
  • Prokaryotes fell into two groups, as different from each other as either is from eukaryotes.
  • So life has three domains, a level above kingdoms: Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya.
  • You and a mushroom: both Eukarya, one domain.
  • Archaea look like bacteria: no nucleus. But their key molecules differ, and some are closer to ours.
  • Say: "archaea are a separate domain" — not: "archaea are unusual bacteria".

?Which evidence was used in 1977 to propose that all organisms should be classified into three domains?
Mission 4

Kingdoms or domains? New data on trial

  • Five kingdoms: based on observable features. All prokaryotes: one kingdom.
  • Three domains: based on molecular evidence. Prokaryotes: two domains. Protoctista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia all sit inside Eukarya.
  • Domains add a level above kingdoms. The eukaryote kingdoms stay; kingdom Prokaryotae is split into two domains.
  • How it was accepted:
    • published in a peer-reviewed journal (1977);
    • doubted at first; other scientists tested it with other genes and molecules;
    • three domains formally proposed in 1990, then widely accepted.
Mission check

?A single-celled organism from a salt lake has no nucleus. Its cell wall contains no peptidoglycan, and its rRNA base sequence is closest to those of known archaea. How is it classified in the two systems?

Looks put it with the bacteria; its molecules put it in a domain of its own.

Mission 5

Mission 5: How can we trust the evidence that living things have changed over time?

Make a guess: How can we trust the evidence that living things have changed over time?

Mission 5

Fossils in order

  • Fossils: preserved remains or traces of organisms, usually in sedimentary rock.
  • Undisturbed layers: lower = older. So fossils show the order in which life forms appeared.
  • Simpler forms appear earlier; transitional forms link groups, e.g. Tiktaalik, part fish, part four-limbed animal.
  • The record is incomplete: fossilisation is rare, and soft bodies seldom fossilise.
  • Like any evidence, fossil finds are trusted only after other scientists check them: peer review before publication, then independent repeats.
Mission 5

Selective breeding: evolution you can watch

  • Artificial selection: humans choose individuals with desired heritable traits and breed only from them, generation after generation.
  • Wild cabbage (Brassica oleracea) gave cabbage, kale, broccoli, cauliflower, kohlrabi and Brussels sprouts in a few thousand years.
  • All domestic dogs descend from wolves.
  • Artificial = deliberate human choice of traits. Unintended effects of human action (e.g. antibiotic resistance) are natural selection.
  • Selecting a narrow set of traits, often by mating relatives, reduces genetic variation in the breed.
  • Breeders cannot create new alleles. They select among variation that already exists.
Quick check
?Cabbage, kale, broccoli, cauliflower, kohlrabi and Brussels sprouts were all produced from wild cabbage (Brassica oleracea) by selective breeding over the past few thousand years. What does this show about evolution?
Mission 5

Breeding: uses, gene banks and the arguments

  • Uses: higher crop yield, disease resistance, more milk or meat, animal temperament.
  • Genetic resources: keep wild types and old varieties (seed banks, rare breeds). They hold alleles, e.g. for disease resistance, that modern varieties have lost.
  • Ethics: arguments, not a verdict.
    • For: people value particular traits; breeding has fed populations and produced working and companion animals.
    • Against: some extreme breeds have documented welfare problems, e.g. breathing difficulties in very flat-faced dogs; inbreeding raises inherited disorders.
    • Debated responses: change breed standards, restrict breeding of affected animals, cross with other breeds.
Quick check
?For many generations, farmers have propagated a hypothetical crop variety from cuttings of a few selected plants, so the plants in their fields are nearly genetically identical. A new fungal disease arrives in the region. Which prediction is best supported?
Mission 5

Darwin and Wallace

  • Charles Darwin: evidence from the Beagle voyage (e.g. Galápagos), from breeders' artificial selection and decades of study.
  • Alfred Russel Wallace: reached the same idea independently, from fieldwork in the Malay Archipelago.
  • Their papers were presented together at the Linnean Society in 1858. Darwin's On the Origin of Species followed in 1859.
  • Their contribution: a mechanism, natural selection, backed by a mass of evidence.
  • Later evidence they never saw (DNA, proteins) has supported it.
Mission check

?Whales were once thought to be most closely related to an extinct group of hoofed carnivores. DNA comparisons then placed whales closest to hippopotamuses, among the even-toed hoofed mammals. Soon after, fossils of early whales were found with an ankle bone of a shape found only in even-toed hoofed mammals. Why does this agreement strengthen confidence in the relationship?

No single fossil or sequence settles it. Confidence comes from independent evidence that keeps agreeing.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A binomial names the genus (capital) and the species (lower case); together they name one species, used worldwide.
  • Taxa are nested with no overlap, from domain (or kingdom) down to species, and the ideal grouping follows common ancestry.

Mission 2

  • Fewer differences in DNA or protein sequences mean a more recent common ancestor.
  • A near-universal genetic code points to common ancestry of all life.

Mission 3

  • Differences in DNA, mRNA and protein sequences build up after lineages split, so fewer differences mean a more recent common ancestor.
  • On a cladogram, relatedness is the most recent shared node; every tree is a hypothesis open to new evidence.

Mission 4

  • Five kingdoms (Prokaryotae, Protoctista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia) rest on observable features; three domains (Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya) rest on rRNA sequences and sit above them.

Mission 5

  • Fossils in rock layers show the order in which life changed.
  • Selective breeding changes heritable characteristics fast, showing evolution can be rapid; it reduces genetic variation.
