Mission 1: Hindus say some scriptures were "heard" and others "remembered". Why does the difference matter?

Make a guess: Hindus say some scriptures were "heard" and others "remembered". Why does the difference matter?

Mission 1

Heard and remembered

  • Shruti ("that which is heard"): texts Hindus believe were heard or seen by ancient sages (rishis) in deep meditation, not composed by them.
  • Shruti = the Vedas, each with its hymns, ritual texts and Upanishads.
  • Smriti ("that which is remembered"): texts composed and handed down by humans: the epics (Ramayana, Mahabharata, including the Bhagavad Gita), the Puranas, and law books such as the Manusmriti.
  • Many Hindus believe shruti is eternal and without a human author (apaurusheya).
Mission 1

Which has more authority?

  • Traditionally, shruti has the highest authority: it is revealed, timeless knowledge.
  • Smriti is authoritative because it explains and applies shruti.
  • Classical rule (Mimamsa school): if a smriti text conflicts with shruti, shruti wins.
  • Manusmriti 2.6 (paraphrase) lists the sources of dharma in order: the Veda, then smriti, then the conduct of good people, then one's own conscience.
  • Hindu schools that accept Vedic authority are called astika ("orthodox"); Buddhists and Jains, who reject it, are nastika.
Mission 1

The Vedas in worship

  • The Vedas are mostly heard in ritual, not read: priests recite them in Sanskrit with exact sound and accent.
  • Samskaras (life-cycle rites): Vedic mantras are chanted at the sacred-thread ceremony, weddings and funerals.
  • The Gayatri mantra (Rig Veda 3.62.10) is recited daily by many Hindus.
  • Havan and temple rites use Vedic verses alongside later ritual texts.
  • Traditionally only "twice-born" men learned the Vedas; reform movements such as the Arya Samaj teach them to everyone, including women.
Mission 1

The Upanishads: the end of the Veda

  • The Upanishads are the last part of the Vedas, c. 800–200 BCE.
  • Distinctive: philosophical dialogues between teacher and pupil, not hymns or ritual rules.
  • Key teaching: Brahman (ultimate reality) and atman (the true self) and how they relate.
  • They are called Vedanta, "end of the Veda", and are the basis of the Vedanta schools (Shankara's Advaita, Ramanuja's Vishishtadvaita, Madhva's Dvaita).
  • Detail in a later mission.

Mission 1

The great smriti texts

  • Ramayana (attributed to Valmiki): Prince Rama's exile, the rescue of Sita and his rule. Rama is the ideal of dharma.
  • Mahabharata (attributed to Vyasa): the war between cousins, the Pandavas and Kauravas; duty in impossible situations.
  • Bhagavad Gita: Krishna's teaching to the warrior Arjuna, within the Mahabharata. Often treated almost as shruti.
  • Puranas: stories of the gods and avatars; the Manusmriti: rules for varna, stage of life, family and kingship.
  • Smriti is known and loved: through festivals, storytelling, temple art, drama and television.
Mission 1Read the source

The Gita on the Vedas

Bhagavad Gita 2:46–47 (Edwin Arnold's verse translation, 1885):

> "Look! like as when a tank pours water forth

> To suit all needs, so do these Brahmans draw

> Text for all wants from tank of Holy Writ.

> But thou, want not! ask not! Find full reward

> Of doing right in right! Let right deeds be

> Thy motive, not the fruit which comes from them."

  • Arnold translates freely. Most translations of 2:46 read: for one who knows, the Vedas are as much use as a well in a flood. The criticism of using the Vedas for rewards is in 2:42–45.

Q1 In Arnold's version, what do some Brahmins do with the Vedas ("Holy Writ")?

Show answer

Draw texts from them for every wish, using ritual to gain rewards.

Q2 What does he ask of Arjuna instead?

Show answer

To act rightly for its own sake, not for the fruits.

  • So a smriti text reinterprets how to use shruti: one reason the Gita is so influential.
Mission 1

The Manusmriti: authority and controversy

  • A dharmashastra (law book), c. 200 BCE–200 CE: duties of each varna and ashrama, marriage, kingship, purity, penance.
  • Some verses honour women (3.56, paraphrase: where women are honoured, the gods rejoice); others restrict them (9.3, paraphrase: a woman is protected by father, husband, then sons, and should not be independent).
  • It ranks varnas and gives harsh rules for Shudras.
  • Ambedkar publicly burned it in 1927 as a symbol of caste oppression.
  • Today: some Hindus treat it as a guide to dharma; many see it as historical, not binding. Indian law (the Hindu Code Bills, 1955–56) replaced it in family law.
Mission 1

Which texts are shruti? Relative authority

  • General agreement: the Vedas, including the Upanishads, are shruti.
  • Disputed: Vaishnavas treat the Pancharatra Samhitas, and Shaivas the Shaiva Agamas, as revealed; others do not. Tamil Shrivaishnavas call their hymn collection a "Tamil Veda".
  • So "highest authority" in theory and "greatest influence" in practice can differ.

Mission 1Evaluate

Does smriti matter more than shruti?

  • Claim: For Hindus today, smriti is more important than shruti.
  • For: The epics and Gita are widely known among Hindus; they shape festivals (Diwali, Ramnavami), ethics and devotion.
  • For: Stories teach dharma in situations people recognise.
  • Against: Shruti defines what counts as Hindu (astika) and is present in every samskara.
  • Against: Smriti's own authority rests on agreeing with shruti; the Gita itself is called an Upanishad by tradition.
  • Judgement criteria: "Important" as authority or as influence? For which Hindus (priests, Vedanta scholars, devotees, reformers)?

Shruti holds the highest authority in principle; smriti carries most of daily Hindu life. Next: what the Upanishads teach about the self.

Mission 2: Hindus have no pope. So who has the authority to teach them, and why do people listen?

Make a guess: Hindus have no pope. So who has the authority to teach them, and why do people listen?

Mission 2

Living sources of authority

  • Hinduism has no single head, no central council and no one creed.
  • Alongside scripture (see Mission 1), authority comes from people: those who live what the texts teach.
  • Four kinds of holy person:
  • Sadhu / sadhvi: an ascetic devoted wholly to the religious life.
  • Yogi / yogini: a practitioner of yoga disciplines.
  • Guru / swami: a spiritual teacher.
  • Sannyasi: a renunciate who has given up worldly life.
  • The labels overlap: one person can be all four.
Mission 2

Sadhus and sadhvis

  • Sadhu (male), sadhvi (female): "good one"; holy people dedicated to religious aims above all else.
  • Many leave home, family, work and possessions; many wander, living on alms.
  • Distinctive appearance (it varies): saffron or ochre cloth, or near-nakedness; ash smeared on the body; matted hair (jata); sect marks (tilak) on the forehead; rudraksha beads; a staff or trident.
  • Shaiva sadhus often wear three horizontal lines; Vaishnava sadhus a U-shaped mark.
  • Huge gatherings at the Kumbh Mela, where the Naga (naked) sadhus lead the bathing processions.
  • Respected as reminders of the spiritual goal; some lay Hindus doubt that every sadhu is genuine.
Mission 2

Yogis and yoginis

  • Yogi (male), yogini (female): one who practises yoga, "joining", "discipline".
  • Practices may be physical (postures, asana; breath control, pranayama), mental (concentration, meditation) and spiritual (devotion, study).
  • Patanjali's Yoga Sutras (c. 2nd–4th century CE): eight limbs, from moral restraints to samadhi (absorption).
  • The ultimate aim is moksha: liberation from samsara, not health or fitness.
  • Yoga in the West is often only postures; for Hindu yogis, postures prepare the body for meditation.
Mission 2

Sannyasis and the four ashramas

  • Traditional ashramas (stages of life) for a high-caste man in classical texts:
  • Brahmacharya (student) → grihastha (householder) → vanaprastha (retiring "forest-dweller") → sannyasa (renunciate).
  • A sannyasi renounces home, possessions, caste and ritual duties to seek liberation alone.
  • Initiation is often marked by rites that treat the old life as ended; many sannyasis wear ochre.
  • Some take sannyasa young, skipping the middle stages (e.g. Shankara, traditionally credited with founding the Dashanami monastic orders).
  • Women renunciates (sannyasini) exist and are growing in number, though traditional texts mostly addressed men.

Mission 2

The guru

  • Guru: a spiritual teacher. A popular derivation: gu (darkness) + ru (remover), "one who removes darkness".
  • The guru–shishya (teacher–disciple) relationship passes knowledge on in a parampara (lineage).
  • Diksha (OCR: diksa): initiation. The guru may give a new name and a personal mantra to repeat.
  • Disciples serve, listen, ask and obey; many touch the guru's feet as a sign of respect.
  • A swami ("master", often explained as "master of oneself") is usually a monk of an order; many swamis act as gurus.
  • Some traditions honour the guru as a form of God; Guru Purnima celebrates gurus every year.
Mission 2Read the source

Seek a teacher

Bhagavad Gita 4:34 (Edwin Arnold's verse translation, 1885), Krishna to Arjuna:

> "these are gained by reverence, by strong search,

> By humble heed of those who see the Truth

> And teach it."

  • The Mundaka Upanishad (1.2.12, paraphrase) says the seeker should go, fuel in hand, to a teacher who knows the Vedas and is established in Brahman.

Q1 According to the Gita, what three things lead to wisdom?

Show answer

Reverence, strong search and humbly listening to those who see and teach the truth.

Q2 What makes a teacher worth hearing in these texts?

Show answer

They have seen the truth themselves, not only studied it.

Mission 2

Swami Sivananda: a life

  • Swami Sivananda Saraswati (1887–1963), born in Pattamadai, Tamil Nadu.
  • Trained in medicine; worked as a doctor in Malaya (now Malaysia) for about ten years.
  • Gave up his career, wandered as a seeker, and took sannyasa in Rishikesh in 1924.
  • Founded the Divine Life Society (1936) and an ashram on the Ganges at Rishikesh.
  • Wrote over 200 books on yoga, Vedanta and devotion, many in plain English for a wide readership.
  • His disciples spread his teaching worldwide: e.g. Swami Vishnudevananda (Sivananda Yoga Vedanta Centres) and Swami Chidananda.
Mission 2

Sivananda's teaching

  • Yoga of Synthesis: combine all the paths: karma (service), bhakti (devotion), raja (meditation) and jnana (knowledge).
  • His motto: "Serve, Love, Give, Purify, Meditate, Realise."
  • Also: "Be good, do good."
  • Service of the sick and poor: he ran a free dispensary and later a hospital at the ashram.
  • Spiritual life is open to everyone, of any background or nation.
  • Authority for him came from realisation and example, rooted in Vedanta scripture.

Mission 2Diversity

Gurus' authority today

  • Sources of a guru's authority: personal realisation; a recognised lineage; faithfulness to scripture; the disciples' experience of change.
  • Global gurus: since the 20th century many gurus teach in the West and online; yoga and meditation centres spread their influence.
  • Different Hindu attitudes: some say a guru is essential for spiritual progress; others rely on family, scripture, temple priests or their own devotion.
  • Concerns: some gurus have faced scandals over money, abuse of power or sexual misconduct; critics warn against blind obedience.
  • Responses: test a guru by their life and by scripture; value lineages with accountability; follow the teaching rather than the person.
  • Holy persons as a whole, and their place in Hindu society.
Mission 2Evaluate

Does a Hindu today need a guru?

  • Claim: A guru is essential for a Hindu's spiritual life today.
  • For: The Upanishads and the Gita (4:34) send the seeker to a teacher; spiritual knowledge is caught from a person, not only from books.
  • For: In a world of confusing information, a lineage gives tested guidance.
  • Against: Many Hindus live devout lives through family puja, temple worship and scripture without a personal guru.
  • Against: Scandals show the danger of placing too much authority in one person.
  • Against: Bhakti says God is reached by love, open to all; some say God is the true guru within.
  • Judgement criteria: Which kind of path (jnana, bhakti, karma)? Which sampradaya? How is a genuine guru recognised?
Mission check

?Meena, a British Hindu, joins an online meditation course run by a celebrity teacher who calls himself a guru. He gives no lineage, sells costly "secret" mantras and says students must never question him. Which response best reflects traditional Hindu teaching about gurus?

A guru's authority does not come from a title: it comes from a life that shows the truth and a teaching faithful to the tradition.

Mission 3: An ashram was once a sage's forest hut. How did it become a yoga school in Kerala and a temple in Wales?

Make a guess: An ashram was once a sage's forest hut. How did it become a yoga school in Kerala and a temple in Wales?

Mission 3

Ashram and ashrama

  • Two words from the same root, shram: "effort", "striving".
  • Ashram: a place: a spiritual retreat where people live, study and practise under a teacher.
  • Ashrama: a stage of life: student, householder, forest-dweller, renunciate (see Mission 2).
  • They connect: students and forest-dwellers traditionally lived in ashrams; renunciates often found or lead them.
  • Say: "ashram" for the place; "ashrama" for the stage.
Mission 3

Ashrams before the 20th century

  • In the epics and Puranas, ashrams are forest hermitages of rishis (sages), far from cities.
  • Valmiki's ashram shelters Sita, where her sons Lava and Kusha are born and taught (Ramayana).
  • Krishna and his friend Sudama study at the ashram of the teacher Sandipani (Puranas).
  • Roles: gurukula (students live with the guru, serve and learn the Vedas); retreat for those in the vanaprastha stage; a place of austerity and meditation.
  • Alongside them grew mathas (monasteries), such as those traditionally founded by Shankara (c. 8th century) at Sringeri, Puri, Dwarka and Joshimath.
  • Usually small, simple, for a few disciples, and open mainly to men of the higher varnas.
Mission 3

What changed in the 20th century

  • Social and political ashrams: Gandhi's Sabarmati Ashram (Ahmedabad, 1917): simple living, spinning, work against untouchability; the Salt March began there (1930).
  • Ashrams around a realised teacher: Sri Aurobindo Ashram (Pondicherry, 1926); Ramana Maharshi's ashram (Tiruvannamalai).
  • Teaching ashrams for the world: Sivananda's Divine Life Society (Rishikesh, 1936) trained monks and welcomed visitors of any nation.
  • Western seekers came to India in large numbers from the 1960s; ashrams opened in Europe and America.
  • So ashrams became open to women, lay people and non-Hindus, often with set courses and a public timetable.

Mission 3Read the source

A quiet place

Bhagavad Gita 6:10–11 (Edwin Arnold's verse translation, 1885), on the one who practises yoga:

> "Sequestered should he sit,

> Steadfastly meditating, solitary,"

> "In a fair, still spot

> Having his fixed abode"

Q1 What kind of place does the Gita recommend for meditation?

Show answer

A quiet, set-apart, steady place, away from distraction.

Q2 How might a modern ashram claim to keep this ideal, even with many visitors?

Show answer

By a quiet setting, silence at set times and a daily discipline of meditation.

Mission 3Diversity

Holy people in Hindu society today

  • Sadhus: honoured at pilgrimage sites and at the Kumbh Mela; lay Hindus give food and alms and seek blessings.
  • Gurus: lead movements with millions of followers; run schools, hospitals and relief work; advise families on rites and life choices.
  • Yogis: teach yoga worldwide; some, from traditions such as the Bihar School of Yoga (founded by a disciple of Sivananda), combine practice with Vedanta.
  • Sannyasis: lead monastic orders and mathas; some, like the Shankaracharyas, are consulted on doctrine.
  • Diverse practice: forest renunciates, city teachers, online gurus, women gurus and sadhvis; some holy persons also take part in social or political campaigns.
  • Their authority rests on renunciation, realisation and lineage, not on office.
Mission 3Evaluate

How important are holy people today?

  • Claim: Holy persons remain the most important source of wisdom and authority for Hindus today.
  • For: With no central authority, living teachers interpret scripture for each generation.
  • For: Ordinary Hindus seek blessings, initiation and guidance from gurus and sadhus.
  • Against: Many Hindus rely mainly on family tradition, temple priests and texts such as the Gita.
  • Against: Scandals and commercialisation have reduced trust in some holy persons.
  • Judgement criteria: Which Hindus (rural or urban, India or diaspora)? Which kind of holy person? Authority for belief, or for practice?
Mission check

?A new centre in Leicester calls itself an ashram. It runs weekend yoga retreats online and in person, has a café open to the public, and has a resident teacher whose students live with him, serve in the centre and study the Upanishads. Which feature most clearly continues the role of ashrams before the 20th century?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Shruti ("heard": the Vedas, including the Upanishads) has the highest authority; smriti ("remembered": epics, Gita, Puranas, Manusmriti) applies it.
  • The Vedas live mainly in ritual recitation; smriti is better known and shapes festivals and ethics.
  • Which texts are shruti is disputed (Agamas, Pancharatra); influence and authority can differ.

Mission 2

  • Hinduism has no single head: holy persons (sadhus, yogis, gurus, sannyasis) are living sources of authority, and the roles overlap.
  • A guru passes on knowledge through initiation (diksha) and mantra in a lineage; Swami Sivananda (1887–1963) taught the Yoga of Synthesis and service to all.
  • Today gurus teach worldwide; Hindus differ on whether a guru is essential, and test gurus by life, lineage and scripture.

Mission 3

  • An ashram is a place of spiritual effort; an ashrama is a stage of life.
  • Before the 20th century: small forest retreats and gurukulas for students and forest-dwellers; in the 20th century: social, teaching and global ashrams open to all.
  • Holy persons remain living sources of wisdom and authority, diverse in practice.
