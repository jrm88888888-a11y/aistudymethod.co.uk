Crown and Parliament in England 1625–1714

Charles I 1625–49

Charles I and the road to war

  • Clashes over money and prerogative led to the Petition of Right (1628), limiting taxation and imprisonment without cause.
  • Charles then ruled without Parliament — the Personal Rule (1629–40) — raising revenue through devices such as Ship Money.
  • Religious policy under Archbishop Laud alarmed Puritans; war with Scotland forced Charles to recall Parliament in 1640 (the Short and then the Long Parliament).

Charles I 1625–49

Civil war and regicide

The breakdown between King and Parliament led to the English Civil War (1642–46), won by Parliament. After a second war, Charles I was tried and executed on 30 January 1649 — an event without precedent in England. There followed the Interregnum (a republic and then Oliver Cromwell's Protectorate) before the monarchy was restored.

Charles II 1660–85

The Restoration

The monarchy was restored in 1660 under Charles II. Tensions over religion and succession persisted:

  • The Clarendon Code penalised religious dissenters.
  • The Test Act (1673) barred Catholics from public office.
  • The Exclusion Crisis (1679–81) saw attempts to bar Charles's Catholic brother James from the succession — giving rise to the 'Whig' and 'Tory' groupings.

James II 1685–88

James II and the Glorious Revolution

  • James II (from 1685) survived the Monmouth Rebellion but alarmed the political nation by promoting Catholics and issuing a Declaration of Indulgence.
  • The prosecution of the Seven Bishops (1688) and the birth of a Catholic heir triggered a crisis.
  • In the Glorious Revolution of 1688, William of Orange was invited over; James fled, and William and Mary took the throne.

The Revolution Settlement

  • The Bill of Rights (1689) set clear limits on royal power and confirmed Parliament's rights, including regular parliaments and control of taxation.
  • The Toleration Act (1689) eased worship for Protestant dissenters; the Triennial Act (1694) required frequent elections.
  • The Act of Settlement (1701) secured a Protestant succession.
  • Under Anne, the Act of Union (1707) united England and Scotland; Anne died on 1 August 1714, bringing in the Hanoverians.

Change over time

The big picture, 1625–1714

Judged as a whole, the period saw a decisive shift in the balance of power towards Parliament. A monarch who tried to rule without Parliament in the 1630s was, by 1714, part of a system in which Parliament met regularly and controlled finance — a constitutional monarchy. The recurring drivers were finance, religion and foreign policy, which repeatedly forced Crown and Parliament to negotiate.
