Mission 1

Biography or hagiography?

  • Siddhattha Gotama (Sanskrit Siddhartha Gautama), a prince of the Sakya clan, in what is now the Nepal–India border.
  • Dates are uncertain: older textbooks give c. 563–483 BCE; many historians now prefer c. 480–400 BCE.
  • The Pali Canon has no full life story: only scattered memories in the Buddha's own words (e.g. Majjhima Nikaya 26 and 36; Anguttara Nikaya 3.38).
  • Full lives came centuries later: e.g. Ashvaghosha's Buddhacarita (Sanskrit poem, c. 1st–2nd century CE); the Nidanakatha (Pali introduction to the Jataka, c. 5th century CE).
  • Hagiography: a sacred life, written to inspire and teach, not to record events as a modern historian would.
  • Your spec pairs Pali and Sanskrit: Siddhattha/Siddhartha, dukkha/duhkha.
Mission 1

Maya's dream and the birth

  • Parents: King Suddhodana and Queen Maya (Mahamaya), at Kapilavatthu.
  • Conception dream (later biographies): a white elephant with six tusks enters Maya's side. Interpreters say she will bear a great being.
  • Birth in the grove at Lumbini: Maya stands holding a tree branch; the child is born from her side, without pain.
  • The newborn takes seven steps and declares that this is his last birth (Majjhima Nikaya 123, paraphrase). Later lives add lotuses springing up under his feet.
  • Maya dies seven days later. Her sister Mahapajapati Gotami raises him (later the first Buddhist nun).
  • History check: Emperor Ashoka's pillar at Lumbini (c. 249 BCE) marks it as the birthplace.
Mission 1

The prophecy

  • The sage Asita sees the gods rejoicing, visits, and weeps: he will die before this child teaches (Sutta Nipata 3.11, paraphrase).
  • Brahmin seers find the 32 marks of a great man on the baby's body.
  • Their prediction: he will become either a cakkavatti (world-ruling king) or a Buddha (an awakened one).
  • The youngest seer, Kondañña, predicts only one path: Buddha. (He later becomes the first disciple to understand the teaching.)
  • The name Siddhattha: "one whose aim is accomplished".
Mission 1Read the source

A sheltered life

In Anguttara Nikaya 3.38 the Buddha remembers (paraphrase of the Pali):

  • He was "delicately brought up": lotus ponds made for him; clothes of the finest cloth; a white sunshade held over him day and night.
  • He had three palaces: one for the cold season, one for the hot season, one for the rains.
  • Then he reflected: ordinary people feel disgust when they see someone old, sick or dead, forgetting that they too will age, sicken and die.
  • As he reflected, his pride in youth, health and life fell away.
  • Later lives add his marriage at 16 to Yasodhara and the birth of their son Rahula.

Q1 In this early source, what changes him: an outing, or a reflection?

Show answer

A reflection: no journey or sights are mentioned.

Q2 Which three kinds of pride does he lose?

Show answer

Pride in youth, in health and in life.

Mission 1

The Four Sights

  • Despite his father's guard, Siddhattha rides out with his charioteer Channa. On four outings he sees:
  • 1. An old man, bent and shaking.
  • 2. A sick man, in pain.
  • 3. A corpse, carried to the cremation ground.
  • 4. A wandering ascetic (samana), calm, having left home to seek freedom from suffering.
  • Each time Channa explains: this happens to everyone, including you.
  • In the Nidanakatha the gods take these forms; in the Canon the same pattern is told of the past Buddha Vipassi (Digha Nikaya 14). A pattern that every Buddha's life follows.
Mission 1

What the sights reveal

  • For Buddhists, the sights reveal three truths about life:
  • Dukkha (unsatisfactoriness, suffering): ageing, sickness and death touch every life.
  • Anicca (impermanence): youth, health and the palace itself do not last.
  • Anatta (no-self, insubstantiality): there is no fixed, lasting self that can be protected from change.
  • The fourth sight adds hope: there is a way to seek freedom from dukkha.
  • Together these become the three marks of existence.
  • Sanskrit pairs: duhkha, anitya, anatman.

Mission 1Diversity

Four ways to read the story

  • Historical: many Buddhists, especially in traditional Asian communities, accept the events as they are told, marvels included.
  • Hagiographical: details are shaped to teach. A prince shielded from suffering until 29 pictures how all people hide from ageing and death.
  • Mythological (cosmic): the life follows a pattern shared by all Buddhas (Vipassi's sights); marvels show his greatness. Some Mahayana texts present the whole life as a display for beings' benefit (see Mission 2).
  • Psychological: the sights are inner moments of seeing clearly (as in Anguttara Nikaya 3.38). Popular with many Western Buddhists.
  • Most Buddhists combine readings. What matters most is the teaching the story carries.
  • Siddhattha as example: the story shows a path anyone can follow; but Buddhists also stress self-reliance and ehipassiko ("come and see") over devotion to a person.
Mission 1

Two scholars on the life

  • Karen Armstrong (b. 1944), Buddha (2001), paraphrase: the legends are not history, but they are not idle: they carry truths about every human life. The sheltered prince is a picture of our own denial of suffering.
  • Ñāṇamoli Bhikkhu (1905–1960), The Life of the Buddha according to the Pali Canon (1972): builds the life from translated canonical passages, arranged as voices.
  • His approach shows how little the Canon itself says of the early life, and how much comes from later biography.
  • So the two meet: Armstrong reads the legend for meaning; Ñāṇamoli shows what the earliest sources contain.
Mission 1Evaluate

Does it matter whether the story happened?

  • Claim: For Buddhists, the historical accuracy of the Buddha's early life does not matter.
  • For: the Buddha taught that the Dhamma (his teaching) is tested in experience (ehipassiko), not believed because of a story.
  • For: the truths of the sights (dukkha, anicca, anatta) are true in anyone's life, whether or not the outings happened.
  • Against: the Buddha's authority rests on his being a real human who found the path; that shows the path is open to humans.
  • Against: for many Buddhists, the marvels show the Buddha's unique greatness and inspire devotion (puja, pilgrimage to Lumbini).
  • Judgement criteria: What is the story for: history, devotion or teaching? Does the Buddha's example need to be historical to inspire? Which Buddhists are we asking?

Mission 2

The Going Forth

  • At 29, after the Four Sights (see Mission 1), Siddhattha leaves the palace: the Great Going Forth.
  • Later biographies: he leaves at night on his horse Kanthaka with Channa, glances at his sleeping wife Yasodhara and newborn son Rahula, then cuts off his hair and puts on an ascetic's robe.
  • The Buddha's own account is plainer (Majjhima Nikaya 26, paraphrase): while still young and black-haired, he shaved his head and went forth, though his mother and father wept.
  • He becomes a samana (wandering seeker), seeking what is "beyond ageing and death".
Mission 2

Teachers and austerities

  • He studies with two meditation teachers (Majjhima Nikaya 26):
  • Alara Kalama: the meditative state of "nothingness".
  • Uddaka Ramaputta: the state of "neither perception nor non-perception".
  • He masters both, but they do not end suffering: when the meditation ends, the problem returns.
  • Then about six years of extreme austerity with five companions: holding the breath, eating almost nothing.
  • His own memory (Majjhima Nikaya 36, paraphrase): his spine stood out like a string of beads; when he touched his belly he felt his backbone.
  • Austerity did not bring awakening either.
Mission 2

The Middle Way

  • He remembers a childhood moment: sitting under a rose-apple tree, he entered calm, joyful meditation (jhana) without effort (Majjhima Nikaya 36).
  • Insight: that joy is not to be feared; it may be the path. But a starved body cannot reach it.
  • He eats again. Later lives name the giver: Sujata, who offers milk-rice.
  • His five companions leave him, thinking he has given up.
  • The Middle Way: neither sensual indulgence (the palace) nor self-torment (the austerities). Taught in his first sermon (Samyutta Nikaya 56.11).

Mission 2

Mara: the night of the Awakening

  • At Bodh Gaya, he sits under a tree (later called the Bodhi tree) and resolves not to rise until he is awake.
  • Mara ("death", "the killer"), lord of desire and death, tries to stop him: storms and armies, his daughters as temptation, then a challenge to his right to the seat.
  • Readings of Mara:
  • A real being: in traditional cosmology, a powerful deity of the realm of desire.
  • Inner forces: the Sutta Nipata lists Mara's ten "armies" as sensual desire, discontent, hunger and thirst, craving, sloth, fear, doubt, hypocrisy, gain and fame, and pride (Sutta Nipata 3.2, paraphrase).
  • Both readings agree: awakening means overcoming craving, fear and doubt.
Mission 2Read the source

Three knowledges

The Buddha describes the three watches of that night (Majjhima Nikaya 36, paraphrase):

  • First watch: he recalls his past lives: names, families, joys and sufferings, over many ages.
  • Second watch: with the "divine eye" he sees beings dying and being reborn according to their karma.
  • Third watch: he knows dukkha, its cause, its ending and the path to its ending. The "taints" are destroyed: he is awake (Buddha).

The Dhammapada gives words for that moment, addressed to craving as the builder of the house of rebirth:

> "But now, maker of the tabernacle, thou hast been seen; thou shalt not make up this tabernacle again." (Dhammapada 153–154, Müller 1881)

Q1 Who or what is the "maker of the tabernacle"?

Show answer

Craving: the cause of rebirth, now seen clearly.

Q2 Which watch is this verse about?

Show answer

The third: the ending of dukkha.

Mission 2

Teaching career and parinibbana

  • At first he hesitates: the truth is subtle. The god Brahma Sahampati asks him to teach (Majjhima Nikaya 26).
  • Deer Park at Isipatana (Sarnath): first sermon to the five companions, the Four Noble Truths and the Middle Way (Samyutta Nikaya 56.11). Kondañña understands first.
  • The sangha grows: monks, then nuns (Mahapajapati Gotami), and lay followers of every class.
  • About 45 years of teaching across the Ganges plain.
  • At 80, at Kusinara, he dies: the parinibbana (final passing away, with no further rebirth).
  • Last words (Digha Nikaya 16, paraphrase): all conditioned things decay; strive on with diligence.
Mission check

?A Buddhist meditator decides to sleep only two hours a night and eat once every two days, to reach awakening faster. Which response best fits the Buddha's own story?

The turning point was not the rice but the insight it showed: neither comfort nor self-torment leads to awakening.

Mission 3

A world on the move

  • The Ganges plain, c. 5th century BCE: a time of fast social change.
  • Cities grew: Rajagaha, Savatthi, Vesali, Kosambi. Trade, coins and rich merchants appeared.
  • Kingdoms expanded: Magadha (King Bimbisara) and Kosala (King Pasenadi).
  • Alongside them, republics (gana-sanghas) ruled by assemblies: the Sakyas (the Buddha's own clan), the Vajjis.
  • Old village certainties weakened. People asked new questions: What happens after death? What is a good life? Who has religious authority?
  • Many rival teachers answered. The Buddha was one voice among many.
Mission 3

Brahmanism: Veda and sacrifice

  • Brahmanism: scholars' term for the Vedic religion of the time (one root of later Hinduism).
  • Vedas: sacred hymns and ritual texts in Sanskrit, held to be eternal and revealed.
  • Yajna (sacrifice): offerings into the sacred fire (Agni) to the gods (devas). Some rites included animal sacrifice.
  • Brahmins: hereditary priests who knew the Vedas and performed the rites. Correct ritual was believed to keep the cosmos in order.
  • Karma first meant ritual action and its results.
  • Upanishads (some before or around the Buddha): an inward turn: the true self (atman) is one with ultimate reality (Brahman); liberation comes through knowledge.
Mission 3

Varna, purity and kingship

  • Society pictured as four varnas (classes), from the body of the cosmic person (Rig Veda 10.90): brahmins (priests), kshatriyas (rulers and warriors), vaishyas (farmers and traders), shudras (servants).
  • Birth decided class, duty and degree of ritual purity.
  • Kingship: the kshatriya king protected order (dharma); brahmins consecrated him with great sacrifices. Priest and king supported each other's authority.
  • The Buddha was born a kshatriya (Pali: khattiya). Buddhist texts often list khattiyas first, above brahmins.
Mission 3

The samanas: those who strive

  • Samanas (Sanskrit sramanas): wandering renouncers who left home, begged for food, meditated and debated.
  • Most rejected the authority of the Vedas and the brahmins' sacrifices.
  • Shared ideas: samsara (rebirth), karma, and liberation through discipline.
  • Six famous teachers are named in Digha Nikaya 2, each with a different answer:
  • Makkhali Gosala (Ajivikas): everything is fixed by fate.
  • Ajita Kesakambali: materialist; nothing survives death.
  • Nigantha Nataputta: the Jain leader (Mahavira).
  • Others taught that actions have no moral result, or refused to commit to any answer (sceptics).
  • The Buddha himself went forth as a samana and learned meditation from Alara Kalama and Uddaka Ramaputta (see Mission 2).
Mission 3

Jainism

  • Mahavira (Vardhamana): for Jains, the 24th Tirthankara ("ford-maker"); an older contemporary of the Buddha.
  • Every living being has a jiva (soul). Karma is a fine matter that sticks to the soul and weighs it down.
  • Release comes by austerity (tapas), which burns off old karma, and strict ahimsa (non-harm) to every living thing.
  • The Buddha's reply: he rejects the idea that all experience comes from past karma, burned off by austerity (Majjhima Nikaya 101).
  • For the Buddha, karma is intention: "it is intention that I call karma" (Anguttara Nikaya 6.63, paraphrase). The mind matters most (Majjhima Nikaya 56).
  • Shared ground: non-harm, renunciation, monastic life, a path without a creator god.

Mission 3Read the source

Who is a real brahmin?

> "A man does not become a Brahmana by his platted hair, by his family, or by birth; in whom there is truth and righteousness, he is blessed, he is a Brahmana." (Dhammapada 393, Müller 1881)

> "If a man for a hundred years sacrifice month after month with a thousand, and if he but for one moment pay homage to a man whose soul is grounded (in true knowledge), better is that homage than sacrifice for a hundred years." (Dhammapada 106, Müller 1881)

Q1 What makes someone a "brahmin" in verse 393?

Show answer

Truth and right conduct, not birth, family or appearance.

Q2 What does verse 106 rank above a century of sacrifice?

Show answer

A moment's honour to someone wise.

Mission 3

Criticism and adaptation

  • Criticised:
  • Vedic authority: brahmins who have never seen Brahma are like a line of blind men, each holding the one in front (Digha Nikaya 13, paraphrase).
  • Animal sacrifice: the ideal sacrifice harms no living being; better still are giving, keeping the precepts and following the path (Digha Nikaya 5).
  • Purity by birth: brahmins are born from women like everyone else (Majjhima Nikaya 93).
  • Atman: no permanent self (anatta).
  • Adapted:
  • "Brahmin" now means an awakened, virtuous person (Dhammapada 393).
  • Brahmaviharas ("abodes of Brahma"): loving-kindness, compassion, joy in others' joy, equanimity: taught as the way to companionship with Brahma (Digha Nikaya 13).
  • Karma made ethical: intention, not ritual.
  • Fire: to former fire-worshippers the Buddha preached that all is burning with greed, hatred and delusion (Samyutta Nikaya 35.28).

Mission 3

Kings, republics and the sangha

  • Kingship by agreement: in Digha Nikaya 27, the first king is "Mahasammata" ("the great elect"), chosen by the people to keep order and paid a share of rice. Not appointed by the gods.
  • The ideal king (cakkavatti, "wheel-turner") rules by Dhamma: when he fails to give to the poor, theft and violence spread (Digha Nikaya 26).
  • Royal patrons: Bimbisara gave the Bamboo Grove at Rajagaha; Pasenadi of Kosala was a lay follower.
  • The sangha and republics: the Buddha's seven conditions for the Vajjis' welfare (meet often, decide in harmony, respect elders) were applied to the monks (Digha Nikaya 16).
  • The sangha accepted members of every class: Upali, a barber, became the expert on monastic discipline.
Mission 3

Scholars on the Buddha's world

  • Karen Armstrong (b. 1944), Buddha (2001), paraphrase: the Buddha belongs to the "Axial Age", when new cities, markets and kingdoms brought anxiety and individualism; old sacrificial religion no longer satisfied, and many sought a spiritual answer within.
  • Ñāṇamoli Bhikkhu (1905–1960), The Life of the Buddha (1972): by arranging the Canon's dialogues, shows the Buddha teaching among brahmins, wandering samanas and kings, often in debate with them.
  • Richard Gombrich (b. 1937), What the Buddha Thought (2009), paraphrase: many of the Buddha's teachings are replies to Brahmin ideas, especially in the Upanishads; making karma a matter of intention was his key move.
Mission 3Evaluate

Reformer or founder?

  • Claim: The Buddha was a reformer of the religion of his time, not the founder of a new one.
  • For: he kept karma, rebirth, samsara, renunciation, meditation and many Brahmin words (brahmin, Brahma, dhamma).
  • For: some Hindus today honour him within their own tradition, e.g. as an avatar of Vishnu.
  • Against: he rejected the Vedas, sacrifice, birth-purity and atman: the foundations of Brahmanism.
  • Against: he founded a new community (the sangha) with its own rules and goal (nibbana); most Buddhists reject being counted as a branch of Hinduism.
  • Judgement criteria: Do shared words mean shared beliefs? Is it the ideas or the community that makes a new religion? Whose view (Buddhist, Hindu, historian)?
  • Your spec issue: how cultural context shapes the development of ideas.
Mission check

?A modern Buddhist teacher says: "Real wealth is not money but generosity; the true 'rich man' is the one who gives most." Which of the Buddha's methods is this teacher following?

The Buddha often kept the words of his world and changed their meaning: brahmin, sacrifice, karma and Brahma all became ethical.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The full life story comes from later biographies; the earliest sources are short memories in the Pali Canon.
  • Birth stories (Maya's dream, Lumbini, Asita's prophecy, the 32 marks) and the Four Sights can be read historically, hagiographically, mythologically or psychologically.
  • The sights reveal dukkha, anicca and anatta, and the fourth points to a way out.

Mission 2

  • Siddhattha went forth, tried extreme austerity, then took the Middle Way; he taught for about 45 years and reached parinibbana at Kusinara.

Mission 3

  • The Buddha taught in a changing world of new cities, kingdoms and republics, among brahmin priests and samana wanderers.
  • He criticised Vedic authority, sacrifice, birth-based purity and atman; he adapted Brahmin words (brahmin, Brahma, karma, fire) and shared samana ideas (karma, rebirth, renunciation, non-harm).
  • Kingship in his teaching rests on agreement and Dhamma; the sangha borrowed the republics' habits of assembly.
