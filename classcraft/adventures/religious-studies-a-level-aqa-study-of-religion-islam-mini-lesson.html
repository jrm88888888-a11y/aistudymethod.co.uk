Mission 1

Mission 1: If the Qur'an came in pieces over 23 years, how did it become one book?

Make a guess: If the Qur'an came in pieces over 23 years, how did it become one book?

Mission 1

What Muslims believe the Qur'an is

  • Qur'an means "recitation": a text meant to be heard and recited.
  • Muslims believe it is kalam Allah, the speech of God, not the words of Muhammad.
  • Revealed in Arabic, through the angel Jibril, to Muhammad, over about 23 years (610–632 CE).
  • Muhammad is the recipient and messenger, not the author.
  • It is the final revelation, confirming and completing earlier scriptures (see Mission 3).
Mission 1

Wahy: revelation through Jibril

  • Wahy is the revelation God gives to a prophet.
  • Jibril is the medium: "for he brings down the (revelation) to thy heart by Allah's will" (Qur'an 2:97, Yusuf Ali).
  • "With it came down the spirit of Faith and Truth- To thy heart and mind, that thou mayest admonish. In the perspicuous Arabic tongue." (Qur'an 26:193–195, Yusuf Ali)
  • How it came (Bukhari, paraphrase of Aisha's report): sometimes like the ringing of a bell, the hardest form for him; sometimes the angel came as a man and spoke.
  • Companions saw him sweat on a cold day as revelation came.
Mission 1

Revealed in stages

  • "(It is) a Qur'an which We have divided (into parts from time to time), in order that thou mightest recite it to men at intervals: We have revealed it by stages." (Qur'an 17:106, Yusuf Ali)
  • Makkans asked why it did not come "all at once"; the reply: "that We may strengthen thy heart thereby" (Qur'an 25:32, Yusuf Ali).
  • Reasons Muslims give: to strengthen the Prophet; to answer events as they happened (asbab al-nuzul, occasions of revelation); to make memorising easy; to bring in laws step by step.
  • And the Night of Power? Many scholars, following Ibn Abbas, say the whole Qur'an came down to the lowest heaven that night, then to Muhammad in stages.

Mission 1

The structure of the Qur'an

  • 114 surahs (chapters), each made of ayat (verses; ayah also means "sign").
  • Order: al-Fatiha first, then roughly longest to shortest. Not chronological: the first revealed (96) comes near the end.
  • Every surah but Surah 9 opens with the basmala: "In the name of Allah, Most Gracious, Most Merciful" (Qur'an 1:1, Yusuf Ali).
  • Divided into 30 juz' (parts) for reading across a month.
  • Muslims believe the order of verses was set by the Prophet as Jibril directed; each Ramadan Jibril reviewed the revealed Qur'an with him.
  • Scholars differ on whether the order of surahs came from the Prophet or from the companions.

Mission 1

Memorised and written in the Prophet's lifetime

  • Revelation was first memorised: companions who learned it all were called huffaz (singular hafiz).
  • Scribes, such as Zayd ibn Thabit, wrote verses down as they came: on palm stalks, flat stones, shoulder bones, leather and parchment.
  • Muslims believe the Prophet checked the recitation, and Jibril reviewed it with him each Ramadan.
  • At his death (632) there was no single bound copy: revelation had continued almost to the end.
Mission 1

Abu Bakr and Uthman

  • Abu Bakr (caliph 632–634): many huffaz were killed at the battle of Yamamah. Urged by Umar, he asked Zayd ibn Thabit to gather the written pieces, checked against memory and witnesses.
  • The result, suhuf (sheets), passed to Umar, then to his daughter Hafsah, a widow of the Prophet.
  • Uthman (caliph 644–656), c. 650: Muslims in distant provinces recited differently. A committee led by Zayd copied Hafsah's sheets into a standard mushaf (written copy) in the Quraysh dialect.
  • Copies went to the main cities; other copies were destroyed. Today's Qur'an follows this Uthmanic text.
  • Later, dots and vowel signs were added to make the script easier to read. Recognised readings (qira'at) remain, e.g. Hafs (most widespread) and Warsh (North and West Africa).
Mission 1Diversity

Sunni, Shi'a and academic views

  • Sunni account: as on the earlier screen; the Uthmanic text is complete and exact.
  • Shi'a accounts add that Ali compiled his own copy after the Prophet's death, in order of revelation with explanations.
  • Mainstream Twelver Shi'a scholars, e.g. Abu al-Qasim al-Khoei (1899–1992), al-Bayan fi Tafsir al-Qur'an, hold today's text is complete; some argue it was already gathered in the Prophet's lifetime. Early reports alleging missing verses are rejected by most.
  • Academic views: most historians accept a fixed text by the mid-7th century. John Wansbrough (1928–2002), Quranic Studies (1977), argued for a later formation; early manuscripts have since weakened that view.
  • In 2015 the University of Birmingham reported two Qur'an leaves radiocarbon-dated to 568–645 CE.

  • Muslims read this as God's promise to preserve the Qur'an, unlike earlier scriptures they believe were altered by human hands (see Mission 3).

Mission 1Evaluate

How far is the Qur'an's compilation a human work?

  • Claim: The Qur'an as Muslims have it today is the product of human compilation.
  • For: People chose scribes, gathered sheets, standardised dialect, burned other copies, added dots and vowels.
  • For: Different readings (qira'at) and early variant manuscripts (e.g. the Sana'a palimpsest) show a history.
  • Against: The words are believed revealed by God; humans gathered, they did not compose. Memorisation by huffaz checked every copy.
  • Against: The process was early and public, under the Prophet's own scribes, within about 20 years of his death.
  • Judgement criteria: Is gathering the same as authoring? What does 15:9 promise: the words or every manuscript? What can historical evidence show about divine origin?
  • Your spec: "Muslim beliefs about the revelation and compilation of the Qur'an".

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why do Muslims who don't speak Arabic still learn to recite the Qur'an in Arabic?

Make a guess: Why do Muslims who don't speak Arabic still learn to recite the Qur'an in Arabic?

Mission 2

An Arabic Qur'an

  • "We have sent it down as an Arabic Qur'an, in order that ye may learn wisdom." (Qur'an 12:2, Yusuf Ali)
  • Muslims believe the Arabic words and sounds themselves are revealed, not only the ideas.
  • I'jaz (inimitability): Muslims hold no human could produce its like: "they could not produce the like thereof" (Qur'an 17:88, Yusuf Ali).
  • So Muslims everywhere learn to recite in Arabic, even without understanding every word. Salah is always in Arabic.
  • Arabic unites a worldwide ummah: the same words in Jakarta, Lagos and Leeds.
Mission 2

Translation as interpretation

  • Every translation must choose between meanings, so every translation is an interpretation.
  • Example: khalifah (2:30) can be rendered "vicegerent", "successor" or "steward": each suggests a different role for humans.
  • So many Muslims call an English version a translation of the meaning. Pickthall titled his The Meaning of the Glorious Koran (1930).
  • Translations are valued for understanding and for sharing the message, but they are not used in salah and are not "the Qur'an" itself.
  • Trustworthy interpretation: tafsir (commentary) by qualified scholars, using the Arabic, the hadith and the context of each revelation.
  • Your spec: "translation as interpretation and the importance of trustworthy interpretation".

Mission 2

Treating the mushaf with respect

  • "Which none shall touch but those who are clean" (Qur'an 56:79, Yusuf Ali). Most scholars take this to mean wudu (ritual washing) before touching an Arabic copy.
  • Some read 56:79 as describing angels and the heavenly book; they still urge respect.
  • A copy is kept high, often wrapped, nothing placed on top; read on a stand (rehal); never put on the floor or taken into a toilet.
  • Worn-out copies are buried or burned respectfully, never thrown away.
  • Many treat translations with respect but with fewer rules, as they are not the Arabic text.
Mission 2

Recited, memorised, remembered

  • Tilawah (recitation) with tajwid: precise rules of pronunciation and rhythm. A skilled reciter is a qari.
  • Hifz: memorising the whole Qur'an; a hafiz is honoured in family and mosque.
  • Al-Fatiha is recited in every rak'ah (unit) of salah.
  • Ramadan: "the (month) in which was sent down the Qur'an" (Qur'an 2:185, Yusuf Ali). Many read the whole Qur'an across the month; in many mosques it is recited in full in the night prayers (tarawih).
  • Laylat al-Qadr: in the last ten nights, Muslims pray through the night; some stay in the mosque (i'tikaf).
  • Recited at births, weddings, sickness and funerals; written as calligraphy in mosques and homes.
Mission 2Read the source

Surah al-Fatiha

> "In the name of Allah, Most Gracious, Most Merciful. Praise be to Allah, the Cherisher and Sustainer of the worlds; Most Gracious, Most Merciful; Master of the Day of Judgment. Thee do we worship, and Thine aid we seek. Show us the straight way," (Qur'an 1:1–6, Yusuf Ali)

  • Verse 7 asks for the way of those whom God has blessed, not of those who earn wrath or go astray.
  • Called Umm al-Kitab ("mother of the Book"): the whole Qur'an in miniature.
  • Declaration of Allah: merciful, Lord of all worlds, Judge.
  • Submission: the believer worships God alone and depends on Him alone.

Mission 2Evaluate

Can the Qur'an ever be translated accurately?

  • Claim: The message of the Qur'an can be translated accurately.
  • For: Meanings can be carried across: millions of Muslims understand their faith through translations.
  • For: Careful translations with notes (e.g. Yusuf Ali) give context and alternatives.
  • Against: Arabic words carry many meanings at once (e.g. khalifah); translators must choose.
  • Against: Sound, rhythm and rhyme are part of the revelation (i'jaz); they are lost in any other language.
  • Judgement criteria: Is "the message" the meaning or the words? Accurate enough for what: understanding, law, or worship?
Mission check

Arabic words, believed to be God's own, are recited, memorised and honoured; translations help people understand them. That is why Muslims learn to recite in Arabic.

Mission 3: If God sent many prophets and books before Muhammad, why do Muslims need the Qur'an?

Make a guess: If God sent many prophets and books before Muhammad, why do Muslims need the Qur'an?

Mission 3

Risalah: God's message through people

  • Risalah means "the message": God's guidance sent through chosen humans.
  • Muslims believe humans need guidance to know God and live rightly; sending it is God's mercy.
  • "For We assuredly sent amongst every People a messenger, (with the Command), 'Serve Allah, and eschew Evil'" (Qur'an 16:36, Yusuf Ali).
  • The core message is always the same: tawhid (one God) and accountability.
  • 25 prophets are named in the Qur'an (e.g. Adam, Nuh, Ibrahim, Musa, Dawud, Isa, Muhammad). A hadith (Musnad Ahmad) speaks of 124,000; scholars differ on its strength.
  • Belief in God's messengers is one of the articles of faith: "We make no distinction (they say) between one and another of His messengers." (Qur'an 2:285, Yusuf Ali)
Mission 3

Nabi, rasul, nadhir

  • Nabi (prophet): one who receives news (revelation) from God.
  • Rasul (messenger): one sent with a message to a people, often with a scripture or law.
  • Popular distinction: every rasul is a nabi, but not every nabi is a rasul.
  • The Qur'an uses both words without defining them: Musa "was a messenger (and) a prophet" (Qur'an 19:51, Yusuf Ali); 22:52 names "a messenger or a prophet" side by side. Scholars differ on how sharp the line is.
  • Nadhir (warner): every people has had one: "there never was a people, without a warner having lived among them" (Qur'an 35:24, Yusuf Ali).
  • Muhammad is called both bashir (bringer of good news) and nadhir (Qur'an 33:45).
Mission 3Read the source

> "Muhammad is no more than a messenger: many Were the messenger that passed away before him." (Qur'an 3:144, Yusuf Ali)

Mission 4Read the source

The seal of the prophets

> "Muhammad is not the father of any of your men, but (he is) the Messenger of Allah, and the Seal of the Prophets: and Allah has full knowledge of all things." (Qur'an 33:40, Yusuf Ali)

  • Khatam al-nabiyyin (AQA: Khatam an-Nabiyyin): "seal of the prophets".
  • A seal closes a document and confirms it is genuine: Muhammad ends the line of prophets and confirms those before him.
  • A hadith reported by both Sunni and Shi'a sources has the Prophet tell Ali (paraphrase): you are to me as Harun was to Musa, except that there is no prophet after me.

Q1 Which two roles does 33:40 give Muhammad?

Show answer

Messenger of God; seal of the prophets.

Q2 Why does the image of a seal suit both "last" and "confirming"?

Show answer

A seal comes at the end and guarantees what is sealed.

Mission 4

What "seal" means

  • The line before him: the Qur'an honours many earlier prophets, among them Ibrahim (Abraham), whom it calls the friend of God (4:125); Musa (Moses), to whom God spoke directly (4:164); and Isa (Jesus), whom it calls God's Word and a spirit from Him (4:171).
  • Final: the message is complete: "This day have I perfected your religion for you" (Qur'an 5:3, Yusuf Ali).
  • Universal: earlier prophets were sent to their own people; Muhammad was sent "as a universal (Messenger) to men" (Qur'an 34:28, Yusuf Ali).
  • Preserved: the Qur'an is guarded (15:9), so no new prophet is needed to restore it.
  • Isa's return is not new prophethood: he was a prophet before Muhammad and returns without a new revelation.
  • Ahmadi Muslims read "seal" as "best" or "authenticating" and believe Mirza Ghulam Ahmad (d. 1908) was a prophet without a new law. Mainstream Sunni and Shi'a scholars reject this reading.
Quick check
?If most Muslims believe Isa will return before the Day of Judgement, how can Muhammad still be the seal of the prophets?
Mission 4

The Prophet's example today

  • "Ye have indeed in the Messenger of Allah a beautiful pattern (of conduct)" (Qur'an 33:21, Yusuf Ali).
  • Character: trustworthy (al-Amin), merciful, humble, just. "We sent thee not, but as a Mercy for all creatures." (Qur'an 21:107, Yusuf Ali)
  • Sunnah in daily life: how to pray, wash, eat, greet, trade, treat family, neighbours and animals.
  • Authority: after the Qur'an, his Sunnah is the second source of guidance (see Mission 5).
  • Love: deep love for the Prophet is part of faith; this is why disrespect towards him causes Muslims great hurt.
  • Emphases differ: Sunni Muslims stress following his Sunnah; Shi'a Muslims also honour his family (ahl al-bayt) as guides; Sufis see him as the Perfect Human (al-insan al-kamil).
  • Your spec: "his significance for Muslims today".
Mission 4

Commemorating the Prophet

  • Blessings: "Allah and His angels send blessings on the Prophet: O ye that believe! Send ye blessings on him" (Qur'an 33:56, Yusuf Ali).
  • After his name Muslims say salla Allahu alayhi wa sallam; in English, "peace be upon him" (pbuh).
  • In salah: the final sitting includes blessings on Muhammad (and, in Sunni practice, on Ibrahim).
  • Mawlid an-Nabi (the Prophet's birthday): 12 Rabi' al-Awwal for most Sunnis, 17 for Shi'a; recitation, poetry (e.g. al-Busiri's Burda), gatherings.
  • Diversity: many Sunnis, Sufis and Shi'a celebrate Mawlid; many Salafi Muslims reject it as an innovation (bid'ah) not practised by the first Muslims.
Mission 4Evaluate

Was Muhammad the ideal prophet and messenger?

  • Claim: Muhammad was the ideal prophet and messenger.
  • For (Muslim view): He united roles other prophets held separately: preacher (like Nuh), lawgiver and leader (like Musa), ruler (like Dawud), teacher of mercy (like Isa).
  • For: His whole life is recorded (Sunnah), so he can be followed in detail, unlike earlier prophets.
  • Against (critics): Some non-Muslims question whether war and political power fit a prophet; Muslims reply that justice sometimes requires leadership and defence (22:39).
  • Against (within Islam): Some Muslims are wary of ranking prophets, "We make no distinction" (Qur'an 2:285, Yusuf Ali), though 2:253 says some were raised above others.
  • Judgement criteria: Ideal by whose standards? Ideal as person, as messenger, or as model for all times and places?

Mission 5: A message says "the Prophet said…". How do Muslims decide whether he really said it?

Make a guess: A message says "the Prophet said…". How do Muslims decide whether he really said it?

Mission 5

Three words: Sunnah, Hadith, Sira

  • Sunnah ("trodden path", custom): the Prophet's example: what he said, did, and silently approved.
  • Hadith ("report", plural ahadith): a single report of one saying or action. Hadiths are how the Sunnah is known.
  • Sira ("way of going", biography): the story of the Prophet's life, told in order.
  • Muslims see the Qur'an as God's word; the Sunnah as the Prophet's lived explanation of it.
Mission 5Read the source

Why follow the Prophet's example?

> "Ye have indeed in the Messenger of Allah a beautiful pattern (of conduct) for any one whose hope is in Allah and the Final Day" (Qur'an 33:21, Yusuf Ali)

> "He who obeys the Messenger, obeys Allah" (Qur'an 4:80, Yusuf Ali)

  • The Qur'an tells the Prophet to "explain clearly to men" what was revealed (16:44, paraphrase): the Sunnah is that explanation.
  • As the seal of the Prophets (33:40;), his example is the last prophetic model: no later one will replace it.

Q1 Which verse makes the Prophet a model for how to live?

Show answer

33:21: "a beautiful pattern".

Q2 Which verse ties obedience to the Prophet to obedience to God?

Mission 5

Anatomy of a hadith

  • Every hadith has two parts.
  • Isnad (chain, "support"): the names of those who passed the report on, back to the Prophet: "A told me that B told him that C heard the Prophet say…".
  • Matn (text): the words or action reported.
  • Scholars test both: is every link in the chain known, trustworthy and able to have met the next? Does the text fit the Qur'an and well-established Sunnah?
  • The study of narrators ('ilm al-rijal, "knowledge of the men") produced huge biographical dictionaries.
  • Hadith qudsi ("sacred report"): God's words in the Prophet's phrasing. It is not Qur'an: it is not recited in salah as Qur'an.

Mission 5

From memory to collections

  • 632 CE: the Prophet dies. Companions remember and teach his words; some write notes.
  • 8th century: reports are gathered; fabrication (for politics, sects, piety) makes scholars insist on chains.
  • 9th century: the great Sunni collections. Muhammad al-Bukhari (810–870) chose only reports he judged sound: Sahih al-Bukhari. With Sahih Muslim (Muslim ibn al-Hajjaj, d. 875) it is the most trusted Sunni book after the Qur'an.
  • Sunni Six Books add Abu Dawud, al-Tirmidhi, al-Nasa'i and Ibn Majah.
  • Shi'a Islam accepts reports from the Prophet and from the Imams of his family, mainly through narrators loyal to them. Its Four Books begin with al-Kafi of al-Kulayni (d. 941).
Mission 5Diversity

How much can hadiths be trusted?

  • Mainstream Sunni: hadiths graded sahih are authoritative; the Sunnah is revelation in meaning (53:3–4 is often cited).
  • Shi'a: hadiths are trusted when they come through reliable narrators, especially from the Imams; many Sunni-transmitted reports are treated with caution.
  • Qur'an-only Muslims (Qur'aniyyun), e.g. Ghulam Ahmad Parwez (1903–1985) in Pakistan: hadiths are human records and not binding; the Qur'an is enough. A small minority.
  • Western sceptics: Ignaz Goldziher (Muslim Studies, vol. 2, 1890) saw many hadiths as products of later debates; Joseph Schacht (The Origins of Muhammadan Jurisprudence, 1950) argued that chains "grew backwards" to earlier authorities.
  • Replies: Harald Motzki (1948–2019) dated some legal material to the 7th century through study of early collections; Jonathan A. C. Brown (Hadith, 2009) shows that Muslim critics examined texts, not just chains.
Mission 5Evaluate

"Without the Hadith, Muslims could not live by the Qur'an."

  • Reasons for: the Qur'an gives commands without detail (salah, zakah); 16:44 and 4:80 give the Prophet's explanation authority; 14 centuries of law and worship depend on it.
  • Reasons against: the Qur'an calls itself clear and complete (Qur'an-only Muslims cite 6:38 and 16:89); some practice is known by continuous community practice (the Maliki stress on the practice of Madinah), not single reports; weak and fabricated hadiths have misled people.
  • Middle view: the Qur'an needs the Sunnah, but the Sunnah can be known through practice and mass transmission, so not every single hadith carries equal weight.
  • Judge it by: Could salah be performed from the Qur'an alone? Is the Sunnah the same as the written Hadith? How reliable is the grading system? Who has authority to decide?

Mission 6: Most Shi'a Muslims say a guide chosen by God has been hidden for over 1,000 years. Who leads them now?

Make a guess: Most Shi'a Muslims say a guide chosen by God has been hidden for over 1,000 years. Who leads them now?

Mission 6

The party of Ali

  • Shi'a comes from Shi'at Ali: "the party (followers) of Ali".
  • A minority of Muslims worldwide; the majority in Iran, Iraq, Azerbaijan and Bahrain; large communities in Lebanon, Pakistan, India and Britain.
  • Shared with Sunnis: one God, the Qur'an, the Prophet as the final messenger, salah, fasting, Hajj, the Day of Judgement.
  • The distinctive belief: Imamah (the Imamate): after the Prophet, God appointed Imams from his family to guide the community.
  • Origins: Ali's designation at Ghadir Khumm (as Shi'a understand it); the martyrdom of Husain at Karbala in 680.
Mission 6

Who is an Imam?

  • Designated (nass): each Imam is named by the Prophet or the Imam before him, by God's command.
  • Ma'sum (infallible): protected by God from sin and error ('ismah).
  • Knowledge ('ilm): the Imam knows the outer and inner meaning of the Qur'an and the Prophet's teaching.
  • Hujjah (proof of God): the earth is never without one (al-Kulayni, al-Kafi; paraphrase).
  • So the Imams' words and actions are an authoritative source beside the Qur'an and the Prophet's Sunnah. Their reported sayings fill Shi'a hadith collections such as al-Kafi (10th century).
  • The Imam is not a prophet: revelation ended with Muhammad. He explains and guards it.
  • Your spec: "the authority of the Imams in Shi'a Islam".
Mission 6Read the source

Verses Shi'a scholars cite

> "He said: 'I will make thee an Imam to the Nations.' He pleaded: 'And also (Imams) from my offspring!' He answered: 'But My Promise is not within the reach of evil-doers.'" (Qur'an 2:124, Yusuf Ali)

> "Obey Allah, and obey the Messenger, and those charged with authority among you." (Qur'an 4:59, Yusuf Ali)

  • 2:124: God makes Abraham an Imam after testing him. Shi'a reading: Imamate is God's appointment, and no wrongdoer can hold it; so the Imam must be sinless.
  • 4:59: Shi'a reading: "those charged with authority" are the infallible Imams, since God would not command obedience to someone who might lead into error. Sunni reading: rulers and scholars, obeyed within the limits of God's law.
  • With 33:33 (which speaks of God purifying the Prophet's Family; Shi'a apply it to the Prophet, Ali, Fatimah, Hasan and Husain), these verses ground belief in the Imams.

Mission 6

The Twelve Imams

  • 1 Ali (d. 661, Najaf) · 2 Hasan (d. 670, Madinah) · 3 Husain (d. 680, Karbala)
  • 4 Ali Zayn al-Abidin · 5 Muhammad al-Baqir · 6 Ja'far al-Sadiq (d. 765): great teacher of law; Shi'a law is called Ja'fari
  • 7 Musa al-Kazim · 8 Ali al-Rida (d. 818; shrine in Mashhad, Iran) · 9 Muhammad al-Jawad
  • 10 Ali al-Hadi · 11 Hasan al-Askari (d. 874, Samarra) · 12 Muhammad al-Mahdi: believed by Twelvers to be in occultation
  • Shi'a accounts hold that most Imams were killed or poisoned by rulers: a line of martyrs for justice.
  • Their tombs are major places of ziyarah (visiting pilgrimage).

Mission 6

Occultation and the Mahdi

  • 874: Twelvers believe the twelfth Imam, Muhammad al-Mahdi, a child, withdrew from view: ghaybah (occultation).
  • Lesser occultation (874–941): he is said to have guided the community through four deputies in turn.
  • Greater occultation (from 941): no named deputy. Twelvers believe he is alive, hidden by God.
  • They expect him to return as the Mahdi ("rightly guided one") to fill the earth with justice as it is now filled with injustice (hadith; paraphrase), with Jesus (Isa) at his side.
  • Intizar (waiting) is active: living justly, praying for his return; his birthday (15 Sha'ban) is celebrated.
  • Sunnis also expect a Mahdi from the Prophet's family before the end, but most do not identify him with the hidden twelfth Imam.
Mission 6Diversity

Twelvers, Seveners and others

  • Shi'a groups split over which son carried the Imamate.
  • Twelvers (Ithna 'Ashariyyah): the great majority of Shi'a; twelve Imams; the last in occultation.
  • Isma'ilis ("Seveners"): after Ja'far al-Sadiq (d. 765), they followed his son Isma'il and his line, not Musa al-Kazim. Strictly, "Seveners" were those who held the line ended with the seventh Imam. The Fatimid caliphs were Isma'ili.
  • Isma'ilis today: Nizaris, led by a living, present Imam, the Aga Khan; Bohras, whose Imam is hidden and who are led by a Da'i.
  • Zaydis ("Fivers"): follow Zayd, son of the fourth Imam; any learned descendant of Hasan or Husain who rises against injustice may be Imam; mainly in Yemen.
Mission 6

Authority while the Imam is hidden

  • In the greater occultation, Twelvers hold that qualified jurists (mujtahids) represent the Imam's guidance in law.
  • Each believer follows (taqlid) a living marja' al-taqlid ("source of imitation"), e.g. Grand Ayatollah Ali al-Sistani (b. 1930) in Najaf; the marja' receives part of the khums.
  • Wilayat al-faqih (guardianship of the jurist): Ayatollah Khomeini (1902–1989), lectures of 1970: a just jurist should also hold political rule. This became the basis of the Islamic Republic of Iran (1979).
  • Many Shi'a jurists, including al-Sistani, give jurists a narrower role in politics.
  • So authority flows: God → Prophet → Imams → (in occultation) jurists on the Imam's behalf, never replacing him.
  • The Imams' authority continues: their teachings, and the jurists who interpret them.

Mission 6Diversity

What makes someone a Muslim?

  • Both: anyone who sincerely says the shahadah is a Muslim; both pray, fast, give and go on Hajj.
  • Sunni frame: six articles of faith (God, angels, books, prophets, Last Day, al-Qadr) and five pillars.
  • Shi'a frame: five Usul al-Din (roots): Tawhid, 'Adl (justice), Nubuwwah, Imamah, Ma'ad; and ten Furu' al-Din (branches) of practice, including khums, jihad, enjoining good, forbidding evil, loving the ahl al-bayt (tawalla) and dissociating from their enemies (tabarra).
  • Many Shi'a scholars distinguish a Muslim (who says the shahadah) from a mu'min (believer, who also accepts the Imamate).
  • The Amman Message (2004–2005), endorsed by hundreds of Sunni and Shi'a scholars, recognises the Sunni schools, the Ja'fari and Zaydi schools and the Ibadi school as Muslim and forbids declaring their followers unbelievers (takfir).
  • Your spec: "the similarities and differences between Shi'a and Sunni views about what it means to be a Muslim".
Mission 6Evaluate

More alike or more different?

  • Claim: The differences between Sunni and Shi'a Islam matter more than what they share.
  • For: Imamah is a root of faith for Shi'a; it changes who holds religious authority, which hadith count, and how law is made.
  • For: distinct practices (khums, muta, ziyarah, Muharram) mark separate identities; politics has deepened the division.
  • Against: the shahadah, Qur'an, salah, sawm, zakah and Hajj are shared; the Amman Message recognises both.
  • Against: Sunnis also love the ahl al-bayt and expect a Mahdi; Sufi orders honour Ali.
  • Criteria: Are differences of authority deeper than differences of practice? Should we judge by doctrine, daily life, or history?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The Qur'an: God's speech (kalam Allah), revealed through Jibril (wahy) to Muhammad in Arabic, in stages, 610–632 (2:97, 17:106).
  • Makkan surahs: short, tawhid and judgement; Madinan: longer, law and community. 114 surahs, al-Fatiha first, then roughly by length.
  • Memorised and written in the Prophet's life; gathered under Abu Bakr (Zayd); standardised under Uthman (c. 650); "We will assuredly guard it" (15:9).

Mission 2

  • Muslims believe the Arabic words are revealed (12:2) and inimitable (17:88); translation is interpretation, so salah stays in Arabic.
  • Treated with respect (56:79: wudu, kept high, never on the floor); recited with tajwid, memorised by huffaz, read through in Ramadan.

Mission 3

  • Risalah: God sent messengers to every people (16:36) with one message, tawhid. Nabi receives revelation; rasul is sent with a message (popular distinction); nadhir warns.
  • Books: Sahifa (Ibrahim), Tawrat (Musa), Zabur (Dawud), Injil (Isa), Qur'an (Muhammad), which confirms and guards the others (5:48). Tahrif: altered text or altered meaning.
  • Sunni six articles and Shi'a five roots both include prophethood; Shi'a add imamah; views on ismah differ.

Mission 4

  • Muhammad is "the Seal of the Prophets" (33:40): last, confirming, universal; Isa's return brings no new revelation; Ahmadi reading rejected by mainstream scholars.
  • His example (33:21) guides daily life; he is commemorated in salawat (33:56), salah and, for many, Mawlid.

Mission 5

  • Sunnah is the Prophet's example; Hadith are the reports that carry it, each with an isnad (chain) and matn (text); Sira is his biography.
  • Scholars grade hadiths (sahih, hasan, da'if, mawdu'); al-Bukhari and Muslim compiled the most trusted Sunni collections; Shi'a Muslims have their own Four Books.

Mission 6

  • Shi'a Muslims believe God appointed twelve (or, for Isma'ilis, a different line of) Imams from the Prophet's family: designated, infallible, sources of guidance.
  • Twelvers believe the twelfth Imam has been in occultation since 874/941 and will return as the Mahdi; jurists (marja') act for him meanwhile.
