Mission 1

Why Jewish feminism arose

  • 1960s–70s: second-wave feminism changed women's roles in work, law and education. Many Jewish women asked why their religious lives had not changed too.
  • Their questions: women could not be counted in a minyan, lead most prayer, be rabbis, or end their own marriages.
  • 1971: Rachel Adler, then Orthodox, published "The Jew Who Wasn't There": women in halakhah treated like those exempt from many commandments.
  • 1972: the group Ezrat Nashim presented a "Call for Change" to Conservative rabbis.
  • Jewish feminist theology goes further: it asks how texts, memory and images of God were shaped by men, and how they could include women.

Who was spoken to at Sinai?

Before the Torah is given, Moses prepares the people:

> "And he said unto the people: 'Be ready against the third day; come not near a woman.'" (Exodus 19:15, JPS 1917)

  • Judith Plaskow (b. 1947), Standing Again at Sinai: Judaism from a Feminist Perspective (1990), chapter 2.
  • Her reading (paraphrase): at the moment the covenant is made, "the people" are addressed as men. Women are present, but invisible as hearers.

Show answer

Mission 1

Plaskow: reshaping Jewish memory

  • Torah for Plaskow is more than the Five Books: it is the whole of Jewish memory and teaching, still being written.
  • That Torah is partial: it records men's experience and leaves women's mostly unspoken.
  • So women must reclaim Torah, not reject it, in three ways (paraphrase):
  • History: recover what women actually did in Jewish life, often missing from the record.
  • Midrash: tell new stories that fill the gaps, as the rabbis did. Her own example: "The Coming of Lilith" (1972).
  • Liturgy and ritual: new prayers and ceremonies that mark women's lives.
  • Covenant: the covenant is made with the whole people, so women must be full members of the community that receives it.

Mission 1

Traditional marriage: acquisition and the agunah

  • Jewish marriage begins with kiddushin. Classical halakhah describes it as kinyan (acquisition): "a woman is acquired in three ways", by money, by document or by intercourse (Mishnah Kiddushin 1:1, paraphrase).
  • Today: the groom gives a ring before witnesses; the ketubah sets out his duties to his wife.
  • Divorce: only the husband can give a get (bill of divorce): "he writeth her a bill of divorcement, and giveth it in her hand" (Deuteronomy 24:1, JPS 1917).
  • If he refuses, or disappears, she cannot remarry under halakhah: she is an agunah ("chained"; plural agunot).
  • Defenders: kinyan is a legal form, not ownership of a person; the ketubah protects wives.
Mission 1

Adler: Brit Ahuvim, the lovers' covenant

  • Rachel Adler (b. 1943), Engendering Judaism: An Inclusive Theology and Ethics (1998), chapter 5.
  • Her diagnosis (paraphrase): a marriage built on acquisition makes one partner the subject and the other the object. The agunah problem follows from that.
  • Her proposal: Brit Ahuvim ("covenant of lovers"), a marriage between two subjects.
  • She builds it from a different part of halakhah: the law of partnership (shutafut), used for business partners.
  • In the ceremony both partners place a symbolic item of value into a shared bag and lift it together: a partnership, not a purchase.
  • Both make promises; neither acquires the other.

Mission 1

Dissolving and reconfiguring

  • Dissolution: a partnership can be ended by the partners. So a Brit Ahuvim can be dissolved without a husband's get: no one can be left an agunah.
  • Adler also proposes a ritual for ending, so that a broken covenant is marked honestly before the community.
  • Inclusive: the ceremony works for same-sex couples as well as for a woman and a man.
  • Reconfiguring the tradition (paraphrase): Adler does not invent from nothing. She reads Jewish sources again and rebuilds from materials already inside them.
  • Contrast with Plaskow: Adler works more closely within halakhah; Plaskow is wary of making halakhah the main frame for feminist change.
Mission 1Diversity

Orthodox and Progressive responses

  • Orthodox: Torah and halakhah are given by God, so change must come within halakhah. Responses to agunot: prenuptial agreements that put pressure on a husband to give a get; rabbinic courts using sanctions.
  • Orthodox feminists (e.g. JOFA, founded 1997) push for women's learning and leadership within halakhah; some Orthodox women now serve as clergy (e.g. graduates of Yeshivat Maharat, from 2013). Many Orthodox rabbis reject this.
  • Conservative: the Lieberman clause (1954) added to the ketubah lets a rabbinic court act if a husband refuses a get.
  • Reform and Liberal: egalitarian worship, women rabbis, inclusive God-language, women's names (the matriarchs) in the Amidah. Many accept civil divorce or use egalitarian marriage documents.
  • Reconstructionist and Renewal: most open to Plaskow's new midrash and to ceremonies like Brit Ahuvim.
  • Critics of Plaskow and Adler: a covenant "reshaped" by each generation may stop being the covenant given at Sinai.
Mission 1Evaluate

Can Torah be reshaped and still be Torah?

  • Claim: Feminist theology like Plaskow's reshapes Judaism so far that it is no longer the same tradition.
  • For: Orthodox Judaism holds Torah as given by God (Torah min ha-shamayim); new texts cannot share its authority.
  • For: New God-language (Wenig) and new ceremonies may change what Jews mean by God and covenant.
  • Against: Judaism has always reinterpreted: midrash, Talmud and changes such as the ban on polygamy (attributed to Rabbenu Gershom, c. 1000 CE).
  • Against: Plaskow and Adler work with Jewish sources, aiming to include women in the covenant, not to leave it.
  • Judgement criteria: Who has authority to interpret? Is change of law different from change of memory and prayer? Do women in each movement feel included?

Mission 2

The Shoah

  • Shoah ("catastrophe"): the murder of about six million Jews by Nazi Germany and its collaborators, 1941–45.
  • Centuries of Christian anti-Judaism and modern racial antisemitism prepared the ground.
  • 1933 Hitler in power · 1935 Nuremberg Laws strip Jews of citizenship · 1938 Kristallnacht, a nationwide pogrom (9–10 November) · 1941 mass shootings in the east · 1942 Wannsee Conference coordinates the "Final Solution" · death camps such as Auschwitz-Birkenau and Treblinka.
  • About one third of the world's Jews were killed; whole communities of Eastern Europe vanished.
  • Jews also use churban ("destruction"), the word for the destruction of the Temples.
Mission 2

Why it shakes belief

  • Classical Jewish belief: God is all-powerful, just and acts in history: God brought Israel out of Egypt.
  • The covenant: God chose Israel and promised to protect it; Israel keeps the mitzvot.
  • So where was God at Auschwitz? Four possible answers:
  • Punishment: a traditional prayer says "because of our sins we were exiled" (festival Musaf). A few Haredi leaders applied this to the Holocaust; most Jews find it unbearable.
  • Loss of faith: many survivors stopped believing.
  • God hidden or limited (see Mission 3).
  • A purpose within God's plan (Maybaum, later in this mission).
  • Your spec writes G-d, a form many Jews use out of respect.

Mission 2

Rubenstein: the "death of God"

  • Richard L. Rubenstein (1924–2021), American rabbi, After Auschwitz (1966; 2nd edition 1992).
  • In 1961 a German Protestant dean, Heinrich Grüber, told him the Holocaust was part of God's will for the Jews. Rubenstein concluded: if God controls history, then Hitler was God's instrument, and he could not worship such a God.
  • So the God of history who chose Israel and acts in events is "dead" (paraphrase): belief in that God is no longer possible.
  • The covenant and chosenness die with it: being "chosen" made Jews a target.
  • "Death of God" describes a cultural event in human belief, not a claim that God once lived and then died.
  • Yet he kept Jewish community, ritual and festivals: they help people face life and death together.
  • OCR key knowledge: G-d and the death camps; doubt of G-d.
Mission 2

After the death of God: Holy Nothingness

  • Rubenstein did not become a simple atheist.
  • God as the Holy Nothingness: the ground of all being, from which we come and to which we return; not a person who intervenes (paraphrase, influenced by Kabbalah's En Sof).
  • OCR wording: G-d as the "Ultimate Nothing".
  • So Judaism still matters as a religious community, not as a covenant with a God who directs history.
  • He valued the land of Israel and a return to nature's rhythms.
  • Critics: many Jews felt he gave Hitler the last word by giving up the covenant (Fackenheim); others found his honesty more faithful than easy answers.

Mission 2

Maybaum: the third churban

  • Ignaz Maybaum (1897–1976), Reform rabbi from Vienna who led communities in Germany, came to London in 1939; his mother and two sisters were murdered. The Face of God after Auschwitz (1965).
  • Churban: a destruction that ends an old era and makes way for a new one.
  • 1st churban (586 BCE): Temple destroyed; exile spread Jews and their God among the nations.
  • 2nd churban (70 CE): sacrifice ended; the synagogue and rabbinic Judaism grew.
  • 3rd churban: the Holocaust ended the medieval world of East European Jewry and moved Jews into the modern world.
  • God's providential plan: God is still Lord of history, bringing progress through catastrophe.
Mission 2Read the source

The suffering servant

> "Surely our diseases he did bear, and our pains he carried; whereas we did esteem him stricken, smitten of God, and afflicted. But he was wounded because of our transgressions, he was crushed because of our iniquities" (Isaiah 53:4–5, JPS 1917)

  • Jewish tradition (e.g. Rashi) reads the servant as the people of Israel, suffering among the nations; Christians read it as Jesus.
  • Maybaum: Israel is the suffering servant; the six million died as an innocent sacrifice (vicarious atonement), not for their own sins.
  • Like the crucifixion for Christians, he said, Auschwitz should shock the Western world into turning from evil (paraphrase).

Show answer

Others': "our transgressions".

Q2 Why does this suit Maybaum but not the punishment view?

Show answer

The servant is innocent; he suffers for others, not for his own sins.

Mission 2

"Hitler, my servant" and the remnant

> "Nebuchadrezzar the king of Babylon, My servant" (Jeremiah 25:9, JPS 1917)

  • Role of Hitler: as God used Babylon, Maybaum said, God used Hitler as an instrument, without making him any less guilty (paraphrase).
  • Not punishment: Maybaum explicitly rejects the idea that the victims were punished for sin.

> "A remnant shall return, even the remnant of Jacob, unto God the Mighty." (Isaiah 10:21, JPS 1917)

  • Remnant: the survivors, especially Jews in the West, carry Judaism into the new era.
  • Critics: making God use genocide for "progress" seems monstrous; the Christian imagery troubles many Jews; Rubenstein said the God of history could not be worshipped.

Mission 2Worked answer

Comparing two responses

Question: "Compare Maybaum's and Rubenstein's responses to the Holocaust."

  • Shared starting point: both accept that the traditional God of history is challenged by Auschwitz.
  • Difference 1 (God): Rubenstein: the God of history is "dead"; God as Holy Nothingness. Maybaum: God still directs history (Jeremiah 25:9).
  • Difference 2 (covenant): Rubenstein: the covenant and chosenness end. Maybaum: Israel remains God's servant (Isaiah 53).
  • Difference 3 (meaning): Rubenstein: no purpose. Maybaum: churban as painful progress; the remnant goes on.

Mission 3

What the Holocaust did to the Jewish world

  • About one third of the world's Jews were murdered; Eastern Europe's Yiddish-speaking world of towns, yeshivot and Hasidic courts was largely destroyed.
  • The centres of Jewish life moved to the USA and to Israel (state founded 1948).
  • Antisemitism did not end: in Kielce, Poland, a pogrom in 1946 killed survivors returning home; Holocaust denial later spread.
  • Commemoration: Yom HaShoah (27 Nisan, set by Israel's Knesset in 1951), observed by many Jews; some Haredi Jews mourn the Holocaust on Tisha B'Av instead.
  • : Rubenstein and Maybaum. Now: Fackenheim, Berkovits, Wiesel.
Mission 3

Fackenheim: the commanding voice of Auschwitz

  • Emil L. Fackenheim (1916–2003), rabbi and philosopher; held in Sachsenhausen after Kristallnacht (1938); later a professor in Toronto; moved to Israel in 1983. God's Presence in History (1970).
  • Jewish tradition holds that at Sinai God's commanding voice gave the Torah's 613 commandments.
  • From Auschwitz, Fackenheim says, comes a commanding voice too: a new revelation, though without any explanation of why it happened.
  • Its command is the 614th commandment (first set out in 1967).
Mission 3Read the source

The 614th commandment

Fackenheim, God's Presence in History (1970), summary of the commandment (paraphrase except the quoted phrase):

  • Jews are "forbidden to hand Hitler posthumous victories".
  • They must survive as Jews, lest the Jewish people perish.
  • They must remember the victims, lest their memory perish.
  • They must not despair of humanity and the world, retreating into cynicism.
  • They must not despair of the God of Israel, lest Judaism perish.

Q1 Why is giving up Judaism a "victory" for Hitler, on this view?

Show answer

Hitler aimed to end the Jewish people; Jews who abandon Judaism complete his work.

Q2 Does the commandment explain why God allowed the Holocaust?

Show answer

No: it commands a response, not an explanation.

Mission 3

Return into history: the State of Israel

  • Fackenheim, The Jewish Return into History (1978): after Auschwitz, Jews cannot stay passive victims; they must act in history.
  • The State of Israel (1948) is, for him, a central response: a collective "No" to Hitler; Jews defending Jewish life.
  • God acting in history? Fackenheim keeps God present in history, but only as commanding, not as saving at Auschwitz.
  • Critics: making survival a commandment may turn Judaism into a reaction to Hitler; secular Jews can obey it without any belief in God.
Mission 3

Berkovits: the hidden face

  • Eliezer Berkovits (1908–1992), Orthodox rabbi (Berlin, Leeds, Sydney, Boston, Chicago; Israel from the mid-1970s). Faith after the Holocaust (1973).
  • Your spec spells his name Berkovitz.

> "Verily Thou art a God that hidest Thyself, O God of Israel, the Saviour." (Isaiah 45:15, JPS 1917)

  • Hester panim ("hiding of the face"): in Deuteronomy 31:17 God says "I will hide My face from them" as a response to sin; Berkovits uses the idea without the sin.
  • Free will: for humans to be truly free and responsible, God must hold back and not stop every evil act.
  • So the Holocaust is a human and historical event: the crime of the Nazis, not an act of God.
  • The hidden God is still the Saviour: Israel's survival through history shows God's presence.
Mission 3

Job and the modern Jew

  • Job lost everything and argued with God, but did not let go of God: "Though He slay me, yet will I trust in Him" (Job 13:15, JPS 1917).
  • Berkovits (paraphrase): those who were not in the camps are not Job; we are Job's brother. We must not judge either the faith or the loss of faith of those who suffered.
  • Both the believer who prayed in the camps and the one who lost faith in them are honoured.
  • No theodicy fully justifies the suffering; faith lives with the unanswered question.
  • Critics: if God's hiding cost six million lives, is human freedom worth that price? Why does God hide then and not at other times?

Mission 3Diversity

Five responses compared

  • Rubenstein: God of history "dead"; covenant ends.
  • Maybaum: God's plan; churban; Israel as suffering servant.
  • Fackenheim: no explanation; a command to survive and remember.
  • Berkovits: God hidden for the sake of free will; humans to blame.
  • Orthodox: many keep faith without an explanation; Rabbi Joseph B. Soloveitchik, "Kol Dodi Dofek" (1956): ask not "why?" but "what must I do now?" (paraphrase). A minority give the punishment answer.
  • Progressive: more open to a limited God or to new theology; Rubenstein, Maybaum and Fackenheim were all trained in non-Orthodox seminaries.
Mission 3Evaluate

Can any Holocaust theology succeed?

  • Claim: No Holocaust theology is legitimate: silence is the only fitting response.
  • For: any explanation risks justifying the murder of six million (Maybaum's critics).
  • For: the questions about God's power and goodness remain unanswered by every thinker.
  • Against: Fackenheim and Berkovits do not explain the Holocaust; they guide how to live after it.
  • Against: silence can let memory fade; survivors themselves demanded to be heard.

  • Judgement criteria: faithful to the victims? to Jewish tradition? does it help Jews live and believe now?

Mission 4

Chagall's world

  • Marc Chagall (1887–1985), born near Vitebsk (now Belarus), in the Russian Empire's Pale of Settlement: the area where most Jews were forced to live.
  • Grew up in a poor Hasidic family: shtetl life of synagogue, Shabbat, market, music, and Christian neighbours.
  • Paris 1910–14; back in Vitebsk 1914–22 (war and revolution); Paris again from 1923; French citizen 1937.
  • His images return again and again to the shtetl: fiddlers, rabbis, animals, floating lovers, wooden houses.
  • He painted Jewish life from the inside, in a modern style (colour and dream, not realism).
Mission 4

"Degenerate art"

  • Entartete Kunst ("degenerate art"): the Nazi label, from 1933, for modern art they called sick, un-German or "Jewish-Bolshevik".
  • Targets: abstract and expressionist art, art by Jews, art that distorted the body or criticised war and society.
  • Ideal "German" art: realistic, heroic, rural, racially "pure".
  • Chagall was doubly condemned: a modernist and a Jew painting Jewish life.
  • In Mannheim (1933) one of his paintings of a rabbi was paraded and mocked in public.
Mission 4

The 1937 exhibition and the fate of the artists

  • From 1937 thousands of works were seized from German museums.
  • Entartete Kunst exhibition, Munich, opened July 1937: about 650 works hung chaotically with mocking slogans; then toured Germany and Austria. Chagall's paintings were included.
  • Fate of works: some sold abroad (auction in Lucerne, 1939); many destroyed.
  • Fate of artists: banned from teaching, exhibiting, even painting; many fled into exile.
  • Some were murdered: e.g. Felix Nussbaum (Auschwitz, 1944) and Otto Freundlich (1943).

Mission 4

Escape, 1940–41

  • 1940: Germany defeats France; Chagall and his wife Bella live in the unoccupied south (Gordes).
  • Vichy France passes anti-Jewish laws (from October 1940).
  • Varian Fry of the American Emergency Rescue Committee arranges an invitation from New York's Museum of Modern Art.
  • April 1941: Chagall is arrested in a round-up of Jews in Marseille; released after pressure from Fry.
  • Escapes via Spain and Lisbon; reaches New York in June 1941. Bella dies there in 1944; he returns to France in 1948.
Mission 4

The Fiddler (1913)

  • A huge fiddler with a green face, one foot on a rooftop, playing over a snowy village of small wooden houses.
  • Role of music: the fiddler (klezmer) played at weddings, festivals and funerals; music marked every stage of shtetl life.
  • Hasidic Judaism: Hasidim worship God with joy, song and dance; the wordless melody (niggun) lifts the soul.
  • Shtetl life: the fiddler balances on the roof, a picture of a precarious life (the source of the musical title Fiddler on the Roof, 1964).
  • Jewish–Christian relations: the Jewish town lived beside, and depended on, Christian neighbours and authorities, with periodic pogroms; commentators read the village setting this way.

Mission 4Evaluate

Was Chagall's art resistance?

  • Claim: Chagall's art was a real act of Jewish resistance.
  • For: it preserved the memory and dignity of a world the Nazis set out to erase (Pale of Settlement, Hasidic life).
  • For: White Crucifixion protested to the Christian world in 1938, naming persecution while others were silent.
  • For: he kept painting Jewish subjects though labelled degenerate; after the war his art became memorial (e.g. windows for Hadassah Hospital, Jerusalem, 1962).
  • Against: "resistance" usually means risk and action; Chagall painted from France and, later, safety in the USA.
  • Against: his Christian imagery was criticised by some Jews; was it Jewish resistance or universal protest?
  • Judgement criteria: what counts as resistance (armed, spiritual, cultural)? Whose memory does it keep? Who saw it then?

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The Shoah challenges belief in a just, all-powerful God who acts in history and keeps covenant with Israel.

  • Maybaum (The Face of God after Auschwitz, 1965): the third churban; Hitler as God's instrument; Israel as suffering servant (vicarious atonement); the remnant carries Judaism into a new age.

Mission 3

  • Fackenheim: from Auschwitz comes a commanding voice: the 614th commandment forbids handing Hitler posthumous victories (survive, remember, do not despair of humanity or of God); Israel as return into history.
  • Berkovits: hester panim; God hides to allow human free will, so the Holocaust is a human crime; we are "Job's brother".

Mission 4

  • The Nazis branded Chagall "degenerate" as a Jew and a modernist; his work hung in the 1937 Entartete Kunst exhibition; he escaped from Vichy France to New York in 1941 with Varian Fry's help.
  • The Fiddler and The Praying Jew show shtetl life, music and Hasidic prayer (tallit and tefillin); Solitude shows sacrifice, destruction and hope.
  • White Crucifixion (1938) uses a Jewish Jesus to protest against persecution; whether this is "resistance" depends on what we mean by the word.
