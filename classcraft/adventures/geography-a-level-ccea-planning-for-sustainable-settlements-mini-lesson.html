Mission 1

Ecological and carbon footprints

  • Ecological footprint: the biologically productive area a population needs to produce what it consumes and absorb its waste, especially carbon emissions, in global hectares.
  • Carbon footprint: the greenhouse gases caused by an activity or population, in tonnes of CO₂-equivalent.
  • A city's footprint lies mostly outside it: food, goods, energy and waste sinks elsewhere.
  • Production-based accounts count emissions released in the city; consumption-based accounts add those embodied in what residents buy.
  • Globally, Earth Overshoot Day fell on 30 July 2026: by then humanity had used a whole year's biocapacity.
Quick check
?A city has closed its factories and now imports most goods. What happens to its two carbon accounts?
Mission 1

Dimensions of sustainability

  • CCEA's two considerations: environmental (footprints, resources, pollution) and social (equity, community, quality of life).
  • A sustainable city meets residents' needs now without stopping future generations meeting theirs.
  • Features: compact form, public transport, efficient buildings, renewable energy, green space, mixed and affordable housing, local participation.
Mission 1

Social sustainability

  • Equity: affordable homes and services for all incomes, not only for those who can pay a premium for "green" housing.
  • Community: mixed neighbourhoods, safe public space, a say in planning.
  • Health and access: clean air, walkable services, transport that does not need a car.
  • Exam move: an eco-district that only the well-off can afford is environmentally strong but socially weak.
Mission 1

Eco-district: Vauban, Freiburg (Germany)

  • A former French army barracks, redeveloped after the forces left in 1992.
  • About 41 ha and 5,267 residents (December 2020).
  • "Not a car-free but a car-reduced district" (translated): no parking on residential streets; car owners buy a space in one of two district garages (about 470 spaces).
  • A tram line has served it since April 2006.
Mission 1

Vauban: cutting the footprint

  • Low-energy construction is compulsory (65 kWh/m²); 277 homes were built to passive-house standard (at most 15 kWh/m² a year).
  • In the Solar Settlement's plus-energy houses, more energy is produced than used.
  • Heat comes from a woodchip-fired combined heat and power plant, whose electricity covers about 700 households.
  • No rainwater sewers: rain runs in open channels and soaks into the ground.
  • December 2020: 1,132 private cars, about 215 per 1,000 residents.
Mission 1

Eco-city: Sino-Singapore Tianjin (China)

  • Agreed by China and Singapore in November 2007; built on 34.2 km² of "non-arable land, deserted salt pans, and polluted water bodies" about 40 km from Tianjin's centre.
  • Targets for 2020 included 100% green buildings, 90% of trips by walking, cycling or public transport, and 20% renewable energy.
  • Planned for "350,000 residents and 190,000 jobs by 2020".
  • World Bank completion report (2017): "about 40,000 residents" by 2016; about 93% of buildings exceeded the green building standard; renewable energy in the public housing pilot 9.9% against a 60% target.
Quick check
?Tianjin Eco-City had about 40,000 residents in 2016 against 350,000 planned for 2020. What percentage of the planned population was that?
Mission 1

Smart cities: retrofitting and purpose-built

  • Smart city: digital technology (sensors, data platforms) to run traffic, lighting, energy and waste. The UN lists such technologies among ways to build resilience.
  • Retrofitting: Barcelona's council began a smart-city project in November 2012; its Sentilo platform is open-source software for city sensor data. Superblocks turned parts of four Eixample streets into green hubs with four squares of about 2,000 m² each.
  • Purpose-built: Masdar City (Abu Dhabi), begun in 2008, claims buildings "use 40% less energy and water"; its own 2024 report gives a 22.7% cut in energy use intensity and 13.1% water savings.
Mission check

?A national government has money for one programme to cut urban carbon emissions. Which choice is most likely to make the biggest difference, and why?

Eco-districts such as Vauban prove the ideas (compulsory low-energy building, car-reduced streets), but new eco-cities house few people (Tianjin: about 40,000 of a planned 350,000 by 2016). The real difference comes from retrofitting existing cities, judged by independent evidence, not developer claims.

Specification: what this mission covers

Mission 2

The city and the test

  • Belfast: about 352,000 people (2024); 18% of NI's population.
  • Sustainable resource use: meeting the city's needs now without damaging the environment or limiting future needs.
  • Three systems: waste, energy, water. Judge each on environment, cost and future capacity.
  • Belfast's territorial emissions: 8.7 t CO₂e per person in 2005.
Mission 2

Waste: where it goes

  • Belfast City Council collected 161,320 t in 2024/25.
  • Household waste per person: 397 kg, the lowest of NI's 11 councils.
  • Fate of collected waste: recycled/composted 39.4%; energy recovery 59.5%; landfill 0.9%.
  • NI average: recycled 50.4%; landfill 14.0%.
Mission 2

The waste hierarchy

  • Best to worst: reduce → reuse → recycle/compost → recover energy → dispose (landfill).
  • Belfast does well at the top (least waste per person) and bottom (almost no landfill).
  • It does worst in NI at the middle: household recycling 42.4%, the lowest of all councils; composting 18.0%.
  • DAERA notes composting varies with urban–rural character: cities have fewer gardens.
Quick check
?Which judgement on Belfast's waste management is best supported?
Mission 2

Waste and emissions

  • Belfast's waste-sector emissions: 509 kt CO₂e (2005) → 123 kt (2024), about −76% (our calculation).
  • Less landfill means less methane from rotting waste.
  • Burning residual waste recovers energy but still emits CO₂, and recycled materials are lost.
  • NI Water also incinerates sewage sludge at Belfast's Duncrue Street.
Mission 2

Energy: heating homes

  • Census 2021 central heating: Belfast 70.9% mains gas only, 20.2% oil only.
  • Northern Ireland: 31.6% gas only, 49.5% oil only.
  • Gas emits less CO₂ than oil per unit of heat; but both are fossil fuels.
  • Homes were Belfast's largest emitting sector in 2024 (459 kt), then transport (322 kt).
Quick check
?Belfast emitted 1,432 kt CO₂e in 2024 with a population of 352,390. About how much per person?
Mission 2

Water: the wastewater bottleneck

  • The Living With Water Programme (set up 2015; plan published October 2021) aimed to upgrade Greater Belfast's sewers and treatment works to cut flood risk, improve water quality and "provide increased capacity for housing".
  • NI Water estimated its part at £1.9 bn, over 10+ years (2023).
  • In late 2024 government concluded delivery by 2033 was "no longer achievable"; NI Water says development constraints "will increase in severity".
Mission 2

Water: supply losses

  • NI Water leakage, 2023/24: 155 million litres a day, about 25% of water put into supply (all NI).
  • Target: 150 million litres a day by 2027/28.
  • Leakage wastes treated water and the energy used to treat and pump it.
Mission 2

Why this matters for homes

  • Belfast's plan requires 31,660 new homes, 2020–2035.
  • NI's regional strategy: new housing "will be dependent on the availability of water resources and sewerage capacity".
  • So a city can manage waste well and still be limited by water infrastructure.
Mission 2

Exam move: judge each system

  • Waste: low landfill, low waste per person; weak recycling.
  • Energy: gas network lowers heating emissions v NI; still fossil-fuelled.
  • Water: leakage high; wastewater capacity limits growth.
  • Overall: Belfast is more sustainable than in 2005 (emissions halved), but water infrastructure is the weakest link.
Mission check

?City V recycles 60% of its waste and landfills 20%. Nearly all homes heat with oil. Its sewer works have spare capacity after an upgrade. Compared with Belfast, which is the best judgement?

Belfast diverts almost all collected waste from landfill and heats most homes with gas, but recycles less than other NI councils, loses about a quarter of supplied water to leakage (NI-wide) and faces wastewater limits that could constrain new housing.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Two neighbouring St Louis estates: one never filled and was torn down; the other stayed full. Why?

Make a guess: Two neighbouring St Louis estates: one never filled and was torn down; the other stayed full. Why?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • A2-2.B2.ii explain how urban design, planning and management relate to sustainability in residential space: defensible space and associated housing design
Mission 3

Defensible space

  • Oscar Newman's Defensible Space appeared in 1972.
  • Defensible-space programmes "restructure the physical layout of communities to allow residents to control the areas around their homes".
  • The approach "relies on self-help rather than on government intervention".
  • Big idea: design can make residents feel that space is theirs, so they watch over it.
Mission 3

Pruitt-Igoe and Carr Square

  • Pruitt-Igoe: 2,740 public-housing flats in 11-storey blocks.
  • Newman: it "never achieved more than 60 percent occupancy" and was torn down, at a loss of $300 million.
  • Next door, Carr Square Village, low-rise row houses, "remained fully occupied and trouble-free".
  • Newman's reading: the difference lay in design, which shaped who controlled the shared spaces. (Others disagree: see the limits screen.)
Mission 3

Principle 1: territoriality

  • The more families share a space, the weaker each family's claim to it.
  • "Residents will jealously guard and maintain that which is theirs".
  • So: divide shared land into private and semi-private areas owned by few households.
  • Unowned space (long corridors, big open grounds) becomes no one's job to watch.
Mission 3

Principle 2: surveillance and small groups

  • Front doors and private grounds that face the street extend residents' control out to the pavement.
  • Fewer families per entrance and landing make it easier to recognise neighbours and spot strangers.
  • UK police guidance aims at "maximising natural surveillance and limiting excessive through movement".
Mission 3

Principle 3: image and milieu

  • Housing that looks like "institutional" estates can mark residents out.
  • In one scheme, new homes were designed to "carefully echo the style and materials of the surrounding middle-class single-family houses".
  • Milieu: link housing to safe surroundings (streets with activity), not isolated grounds.
Quick check
?A redesign gives each ground-floor flat a small fenced front garden facing the street. Which defensible-space principle does this mainly apply?
Mission 3

Retrofitting an estate: Clason Point, New York

  • Shared grounds were divided and assigned to families, reducing the "turf" where offenders could operate.
  • Crime fell sharply in the first year.
  • Average monthly crime fell from 6.91 to 3.16 per 1,000 residents.
  • Residents who felt entitled to question strangers rose from 27% to 50%.
Mission 3

Mini-neighbourhoods: Five Oaks, Dayton

  • Streets were closed with gates to form small mini-neighbourhoods, costing about $10,000 per gate serving 30–40 households.
  • Cut-through traffic fell by 67%.
  • Overall crime fell by 26% and violent crime by 50%.
  • House values rose 15% in the first year, against 4% in the region.
Quick check
?At Clason Point, average monthly crime fell from 6.91 to 3.16 per 1,000 residents. What percentage fall is that?
Mission 3

Defensible space in UK practice

  • Secured by Design is "the official police security initiative, owned by UK Policing".
  • It was created in 1989.
  • It applies the same ideas to new housing layouts: natural surveillance and limited through movement.
  • Planners use such guidance when they approve new residential schemes.
Mission 3

Sustainability, and the limits

  • Social: less crime and fear; residents gain control of their surroundings.
  • Economic: Five Oaks house values rose faster than the region's; Pruitt-Igoe was a $300 million loss.
  • Limits (Newman's own): residents' socioeconomic characteristics also predict crime; early analyses were correlational.
  • Gates bring inconvenience, and mini-neighbourhoods "only work if the community and the city staff really accept the idea".
  • Critics: Bristol (1991) argues Pruitt-Igoe's fate was shaped by "economic and political conditions" in St Louis; blaming design "shifts attention" from institutional causes.
  • Exam move (CCEA "Evaluate"): weigh design against social causes of crime, using figures from one scheme.
Mission check

?A housing association in Northern Ireland is planning 60 new homes. Which layout best applies defensible space?

Defensible space links design to behaviour: small groups, owned space and overlooked streets make residents guard their surroundings. It supports social and economic sustainability, but design alone does not remove the social and economic causes of crime, and critics say Pruitt-Igoe's failure owed as much to those as to design.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Out-of-town shopping parks are easy to drive to. So who loses when one opens?

Make a guess: Out-of-town shopping parks are easy to drive to. So who loses when one opens?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • A2-2.B2.iv environmental and social consequences of retail parks, including competition with town centres
  • A2-2.B2.v leisure and sports facilities, open space and urban parks
Mission 4

What is a retail park?

  • A cluster of large stores outside the town centre, usually by a main road, with a big free car park.
  • Built for car-borne shoppers buying bulky or bulk goods.
  • England, the decade to 2011: out-of-town retail floorspace rose by 30%, while in-town floorspace fell by 14%.
Mission 4

Why shoppers go

  • Out-of-town centres "provide consumers with a clean and attractive destination, cheap parking, a healthy retail mix, things to do".
  • Single ownership: one landlord chooses the store mix, manages and markets the whole site.
  • Town centres, by contrast, often have many owners and "little to no management".
Mission 4

Environmental consequences

  • Traffic: trips that could be walked or bused in a centre become car trips: congestion, emissions, noise.
  • Land: sites on the urban edge can take greenfield land.
  • Surfaces: large car parks and roofs speed runoff.
  • Northern Ireland's policy allows out-of-centre sites only "where sites are accessible by a choice of good public transport modes".
Mission 4

Social consequences

  • Access: people without a car (often older or poorer) find out-of-town stores harder to reach.
  • Jobs: new jobs at the park; possible losses in the centre.
  • Community: Portas describes the high street as having social, not just economic, worth to communities.
  • Consumers gain choice and convenience: Portas credits out-of-town stores with "highly convenient, needs-based retailing".
Mission 4

Competition with town centres

  • Portas: "there are many compelling instances when out-of-town retail has drained the traffic and retail offer from our town centres".
  • Fewer visitors → lower takings → vacant units → a less attractive centre: a downward spiral.
  • But "it would be naïve and far too easy to simply think that they are to blame for the decline of our high streets": online shopping and centres' slow adaptation matter too.
Quick check
?Which is an environmental consequence of a new retail park on the edge of a town?
Mission 4

Northern Ireland's answer: town centres first

  • The Strategic Planning Policy Statement (SPPS, 2015) aims to "secure a town centres first approach".
  • Sequential test: sites are considered in order: primary retail core → town centre → edge of centre → out of centre (only with good public transport).
  • A preferable site in the catchment means an application on a less preferred one "should be refused".
  • Out-of-centre schemes over 1,000 m² must assess retail impact and need.
  • Belfast City Centre is "the primary retail location in Northern Ireland".
Quick check
?A developer wants a 5,000 m² clothing store on a ring-road site. A suitable empty site exists at the edge of the town centre. Under the SPPS, what should happen?
Mission 4

Leisure, sport and open space in planning

  • Open space provides "important green lungs, visual breaks and wildlife habitats" and can help regeneration.
  • Presumption against loss of open space "irrespective of its physical condition and appearance".
  • New housing of about 25+ units (or 1 ha and above) must provide open space.
  • Intensive sports facilities go inside settlements, to use existing infrastructure; stadiums outside only by exception.
  • Access matters: facilities should suit "children, older people and those with disabilities".
Mission 4

An urban park: Connswater Community Greenway, Belfast

  • East Belfast: 19.4 km of new cycle and walkways, allotments, tree planting and river works "to improve the natural diversity and reduce the risk of flooding".
  • Development began in 2011; the route links the Titanic Quarter, Victoria Park and east Belfast.
  • A social return on investment study found it "a viable investment", with benefits in property values, flood alleviation, tourism and air pollution.
Mission 4

Who benefits from a park?

  • Household surveys of about 1,000 adults in 2010/11, 2017/18 and 2023/24.
  • Income-related inequalities in physical activity, wellbeing and quality of life widened over 15 years, mainly during construction (2010–2017), then held steady.
  • Conclusion: the greenway was "limited in reducing health inequalities", which were driven mainly by long-term illness and unemployment.
  • Lesson: a park brings wider benefits (property, flood relief, tourism, air), but closing health gaps needs other policies too.
Quick check
?What does the Connswater evidence suggest about urban parks and health?
Mission check

?A Northern Ireland town council is offered two schemes: (1) a retail park on farmland beside the bypass; (2) reusing empty town-centre buildings for shops, plus a riverside park. Scheme (1) is built. Five years later, town-centre traders say the park has taken their trade. Which evidence would best show that the park caused their losses?

Retail parks bring convenience for drivers, but costs fall on town centres, non-drivers and the environment. Northern Ireland's town-centres-first policy and open-space policy aim to keep shopping, leisure and green space where most people can reach them.

Mission 5: Belfast needs over thirty thousand new homes by 2035. Where, and how, should it build them?

Specification: what this mission covers

Mission 5

One city, one plan

  • Belfast's Local Development Plan (Plan Strategy, adopted May 2023).
  • It plans for 66,000 more people, over 400,000 by 2035.
  • Requirement: 31,660 new homes, 2020–2035.
  • The question: where (greenfield or brownfield) and how (design, shops, parks).
Mission 5

Defensible space

  • Oscar Newman (1972): restructure layouts "to allow residents to control the areas around their homes".
  • Fewer families per entrance; entrances facing the street; clear private and semi-private space.
  • UK police's Secured by Design (1989): "maximising natural surveillance and limiting excessive through movement".
Mission 5

Defensible space in Belfast's plan

  • Design policy DES1: "natural surveillance of public spaces to reduce the opportunity for crime and anti-social behaviour".
  • Main rooms and front doors on the street; private gardens to the rear; avoid "large areas of blank walls".
  • Private open space is valued "especially where principles of defensible open space are applied".
Quick check
?Which housing layout best follows defensible-space principles?
Mission 5

Belfast's other "defensible space"

  • Peace walls: barriers between Protestant/Loyalist and Catholic/Nationalist areas; about 35 in NI by 2001, 26 in Belfast.
  • They "grew out of barricades that the local communities erected themselves" in 1969.
  • Interpretation: such barriers can make residents feel safer, but they also mark segregation and can limit shared housing and services.
Mission 5

Brownfield before greenfield

  • NI target: 60% of new housing on brownfield sites within settlements over 5,000 people.
  • Belfast's plan gives "priority" to brownfield land within the urban footprint.
  • Benefits: returns derelict sites to use; reduces the need for greenfield building.
  • Costs: old industrial land may need decontamination first, and old areas may need new sewers and roads before homes can be added.
  • Example: Titanic Quarter, the former shipyard where Titanic "was designed, built and launched", now with offices, apartments, retail and education.
Quick check
?Which is a sustainability cost of building on brownfield land rather than greenfield?
Mission 5

Retail parks v the city centre

  • NI policy: "town centres first" and a sequential test: city centre, then edge of centre, then out-of-centre.
  • Aim: vibrant centres and reduced travel demand.
  • Belfast's plan manages retail parks "to prevent sprawl of retail use and maintain the viability of the city centre".
  • City centre health check: retail was 60% of uses (excluding vacant units).
Mission 5

Consequences of retail parks

  • Environmental: large car parks and car trips; more traffic and emissions than central shopping reached by public transport.
  • Social: in Belfast, 33.9% of households have no car or van (NI 19.5%).
  • Out-of-town shops serve car owners best; households without cars depend on the centre.
  • Competition: trade drawn to retail parks can leave city-centre units vacant.
Quick check
?In 2021, 50,594 of Belfast's 149,207 households had no car or van. About what percentage is that?
Mission 5

Open space and urban parks

  • NI policy: a "presumption against the loss of open space"; new developments of about 25+ homes (or 1 ha+) must include open space.
  • Open space gives "green lungs, visual breaks and wildlife habitats".
  • Connswater Community Greenway: 16 km of continuous walking and cycling route through east Belfast, linking Titanic Quarter, Victoria Park and C.S. Lewis Square.
  • Sports facilities: leisure centres, stadia, sports halls and pools must be at "appropriate and accessible locations within settlement limits", reachable on foot, by bike and by public transport (policy OS5).
Mission check

?City W must house 20,000 more people. Site 1: farmland at the edge, beside a new retail park; cheap to build, and its sewers have room. Site 2: a former dockyard near the centre; it needs clean-up, and its sewers are near capacity. Which judgement best fits the planning principles in this mission?

Belfast's plan answers "where" with brownfield first (e.g. Titanic Quarter) and "how" with natural surveillance, town centres first and protected open space such as the Connswater Greenway. Each choice has costs: clean-up, sewer capacity and segregation.

Mission 6: A giant container ship burns huge amounts of fuel, yet may be the cleanest way to move goods. How?

Specification: what this mission covers

Mission 6

What makes transport sustainable?

  • Environmental: greenhouse gases, air pollution, noise, land taken.
  • Economic: cost, speed, reliability, jobs and trade.
  • Social: access to jobs and services, safety, health.
  • No mode wins on all three: sustainability means weighing trade-offs.
Mission 6

Compare fairly

  • A ship burns more fuel than a lorry, but it carries far more.
  • So compare emissions per unit of work done:
    • Freight: kg CO₂e per tonne-km (1 tonne moved 1 km).
    • Passengers: kg CO₂e per passenger-km (1 person moved 1 km).
  • CO₂e counts all greenhouse gases as their CO₂ equivalent.
Mission 6

Mission 6

Passengers

UK government factors, kg CO₂e per passenger-km, 2025:

  • Domestic flight (with non-CO₂ effects): 0.229
  • Long-haul flight, economy (with non-CO₂ effects): 0.117
  • Average local bus: 0.104
  • National rail: 0.035
  • Coach: 0.028
  • Ferry, foot passenger: 0.019; average ferry passenger (most bring a car): 0.113
  • Average car: 0.167 per car-km, shared among those on board.
Mission 6

Sea

  • Environmental: lowest emissions per tonne-km; but shipping is huge in total: 2.89% of global human greenhouse gas emissions in 2018, up from 2.76% in 2012.
  • Air pollution: since 1 January 2020 ("IMO 2020") ships' fuel sulfur is limited to 0.50%, down from 3.50%, outside emission control areas.
  • Economic: cheap for heavy, bulky and non-urgent goods; slow.
  • Social: ports bring jobs, but also traffic and pollution to port cities.
  • Bigger is cleaner per tonne: container ships of 8,000+ TEU emit 0.013 kg per tonne-km; ships under 1,000 TEU 0.037.
Mission 6

Air

  • Environmental: highest emissions per tonne-km and per passenger-km on short flights; non-CO₂ effects at altitude add more. Noise affects people near airports.
  • Economic: fastest; suits light, high-value or perishable goods; links global business and tourism.
  • Social: connects distant places and families; benefits go mostly to those who can afford to fly.
Mission 6

Land

  • Road: door-to-door and flexible; higher emissions per tonne-km than rail or sea; congestion, air pollution and road deaths.
  • Rail: low emissions per tonne-km and per passenger-km; fixed routes, and goods often need a lorry at each end.
  • Northern Ireland, 2023: domestic transport made 21% of greenhouse gas emissions, second only to agriculture (31%).
  • Since 1990, NI transport emissions rose 5%, while electricity supply emissions fell 60%.
Quick check
?In Northern Ireland, electricity emissions fell 60% from 1990 to 2023, but transport emissions rose 5%. What does this suggest?

Mission 6

Making modes more sustainable

  • Modal shift: move freight from road to rail or sea; move people from cars to coach, bus and rail.
  • Scale and load: bigger, fuller ships and lorries cut emissions per tonne-km.
  • Cleaner fuel: lower-sulfur marine fuel since 2020.
  • Fewer, shorter trips: local sourcing and better planning reduce tonne-km in the first place.
Mission check

?A freight train burns far more diesel on one trip than a single lorry. A haulier says the lorry is therefore the greener way to move goods. Which response is best supported?

The fair test is emissions per unit of work: per tonne-km or per passenger-km. Ships and trains burn a lot in total but carry so much that each tonne's share is small; air is the opposite. A full judgement also weighs speed, cost, flexibility and local impacts.

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • A2-2.B3.ii evaluate urban traffic management strategies: public transport, integrated transport networks, car restrictions, car parking, pedestrian and cycling policies (one city case study: London)
Mission 7

Measuring congestion

  • Congestion: extra journey time caused by other traffic.
  • Transport for London (TfL) measures excess delay: minutes per km above the free-flow rate, taken as the night-time rate.
  • Congestion depends on traffic and on road capacity.
Mission 7

London's traffic problem

  • TfL reports "a sustained rise in congestion in all parts of London during the years leading up to the pandemic".
  • Average bus speeds in 2023/24 were 9.3 mph, 1% lower than the year before.
  • In 2023, 3,710 people were killed or seriously injured on London's roads; 80% were pedestrians, cyclists or motorcyclists.
Mission 7

Car restriction: the congestion charge

  • February 2003: a £5 daily charge to drive in central London. July 2005: £8. February 2007: zone extended westwards.
  • Aims: less congestion, better buses, more reliable journey times.
  • Today: £15 a day, 07:00–18:00 on weekdays and 12:00–18:00 at weekends and bank holidays.
Mission 7

What happened to congestion?

  • Excess delay in the original zone (minutes per km, charging hours), against a "representative" 2002 value of 2.3:
    • 2003: 1.6 · 2004: 1.6 · 2005: 1.8 · 2006: 2.1 · 2007: 2.3.
  • In 2007 the original zone still had 21% fewer vehicles with four or more wheels than in 2002.
Quick check
?By what percentage did excess delay fall from 2002 (2.3) to 2003 (1.6)?
Mission 7

Why the benefit faded

  • Road space was taken for "pedestrian, cyclist and bus priority measures and several major urban realm improvement schemes".
  • Road works rose sharply, mainly an "accelerated programme of infrastructure replacement by the utility companies".
  • Less capacity for general traffic meant more delay, even with fewer vehicles.
Mission 7

Public transport

  • The Elizabeth line (opened May 2022) carried about 660,000 journeys a day by July 2024.
  • Buses gained priority lanes from the road space the charge freed.
  • But slower buses (9.3 mph in 2023/24) show that congestion also hurts public transport.
Mission 7

Integrated transport networks

  • Integrated transport: modes linked by shared fares, ticketing, timetables and interchanges, so one journey can use several.
  • London's Hopper fare: "unlimited journeys for £1.75, made within one hour of touching in" on buses and trams.
  • The Mayor's target: 80% of trips by walking, cycling or public transport by 2041; 63.2% in 2023.
Mission 7

Car parking

  • London Plan 2021, Policy T6: "Car parking should be restricted in line with levels of existing and future public transport accessibility".
  • "Car-free development should be the starting point" where places are well connected by public transport.
  • Less parking at destinations discourages driving there, without a charge.
Mission 7

Pedestrian and cycling policies

  • London's strategic cycle network: over 400 km by October 2024, against 90 km in 2016.
  • 27.4% of Londoners live within 400 m of it, up from 5% in 2016.
  • 1.33 million cycle journey stages a day in 2024, 26% more than 2019. Cycling was 4.5% of trips in 2023; walking 26%.
Quick check
?Which London cycling figure shows the cycle network's reach rather than its use?
Mission 7

Car restriction: the ULEZ

  • From 29 August 2023 the Ultra Low Emission Zone covers all London boroughs.
  • Its main aim is cleaner air, not less congestion; around 97% of vehicles seen in London were compliant.
  • So it restricts the oldest cars, not car use in general.

Mission check

?A city pedestrianises its main street and adds bus lanes. Car traffic falls by a tenth, but the remaining drivers' journeys get slower. How?

In London, the charge cut congestion by about 30% at first, but road space was reallocated to buses, cyclists and pedestrians and road works rose; by 2007 congestion was back to 2002 levels with 21% less traffic.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Cities use 75% of global energy; their ecological and carbon footprints lie mostly outside them.
  • Eco-districts and eco-cities test ideas; retrofitting existing cities spreads them.

Mission 2

  • Waste: 397 kg per person; 39.4% recycled, 59.5% energy recovery, 0.9% landfill (2024/25). Energy: 70.9% of homes on gas only; emissions per person 8.7 t → 4.1 t (2005–2024).
  • Water: Living With Water plan (£1.9 bn NI Water share) deferred; sewer capacity may limit 31,660 planned homes; NI leakage 155 Ml/day.

Mission 3

  • Newman (1972): territoriality, natural surveillance, small groups per entrance, image and milieu; Pruitt-Igoe vs Carr Square.
  • Retrofits cut crime (Clason Point −54% in a year; Five Oaks −26% overall, −50% violent); UK Secured by Design (1989); limits: socioeconomic factors, inconvenience, community acceptance; critics (Bristol 1991) stress structural causes.

Mission 4

  • Retail parks grew on cheap parking and single management; they add car travel, can take greenfield land, and draw trade from town centres (though they are not the only cause of decline).
  • Planning answers: NI's SPPS (town centres first, sequential test, protection of open space); parks such as the Connswater Community Greenway bring real but uneven benefits.

Mission 5

  • Belfast plans 31,660 homes (2020–35): brownfield priority (NI target 60%), designs with natural surveillance and defensible open space.
  • Retail parks are managed to protect the city centre (a third of households have no car); open space is protected and linked by routes such as the 16 km Connswater Community Greenway.

Mission 6

  • Per tonne-km (UK 2025): bulk ship 0.004 < container ship 0.016 < rail 0.028 < lorry 0.102 < long-haul air 0.531–0.899 kg CO₂e.
  • Sustainability is environmental, economic and social: sea is cleanest per tonne but large in total (2.89% of global emissions in 2018) and slow; air is fastest but most polluting; road is flexible but congests; rail is low-carbon but fixed.

Mission 7

  • Congestion depends on traffic and road capacity.
  • London uses all five strategy types: car restrictions (congestion charge, ULEZ), public transport (Elizabeth line, buses), integrated fares (Hopper), parking limits (London Plan) and cycling and walking (cycle network).
  • Evaluate each with evidence; progress towards 80% sustainable trips is slow.
