Mission 1

Mission 1: Mendel's peas came out at almost three to one, never exactly. How close is close enough?

Make a guess: Mendel's peas came out at almost three to one, never exactly. How close is close enough?

Mission 1

Chance or a real difference?

  • Which gametes fuse is random. So observed counts scatter around the expected ones.
  • The χ² test asks: is the difference small enough to be due to chance?
  • Null hypothesis: there is no significant difference between observed and expected results.
  • The test uses counts in categories (phenotype classes). Never percentages or ratios.
  • The counted offspring are a sample of all the offspring the cross could produce.
Mission 1

The formula

  • χ² = Σ (O − E)² ÷ E. O = observed, E = expected, Σ = sum over all classes.
  • AQA prints the symbol as Χ².
  • E = total counted × expected fraction for that class.
  • Degrees of freedom = number of classes − 1.
  • Big differences → big χ². A perfect fit → χ² = 0.
Mission 1

Worked example: Mendel's seed shape

  • Find the critical value for your degrees of freedom at p = 0.05.
  • χ² below it: the difference is not significant. A difference this large would arise by chance more than 5% of the time.
  • So accept the null hypothesis: the difference is due to chance.
  • χ² above it: a significant difference. Reject the null hypothesis.
  • Mendel: 0.26 < 3.84. His data fit 3 : 1.
  • Say: "the data are consistent with 3 : 1" — not: "the data prove 3 : 1".

?A cross between two double heterozygotes gives 160 F2 offspring: 95 show both dominant traits, 28 show the first dominant and the second recessive, 30 show the first recessive and the second dominant, and 7 show both recessive traits (illustrative data). Mission 1

When the null hypothesis is rejected

  • A significant difference means the expected ratio is probably wrong for this cross.
  • Then look for a biological reason:
    • two genes linked (too many parental types);
    • genes interacting (epistasis);
    • a sex-linked gene;
    • unequal survival of one phenotype.
  • χ² tells you whether the difference is significant, not why.
  • AQA suggests testing ratios from crosses of Drosophila or Fast Plants.
Mission 1

Common slips

  • Use raw counts, not percentages.
  • Scale E to your total. Never use 9, 3, 3, 1 as E.
  • Degrees of freedom = classes − 1.
  • Big χ² = big difference. Above the critical value → significant.
  • Not significant ≠ proved.
Mission check

?In tomatoes, purple stems (A) are dominant to green stems (a). An Aa plant is crossed with an aa plant. Of 200 seedlings, 115 are purple and 85 are green (illustrative data). Critical values at p = 0.05: 3.84 for 1 degree of freedom, 5.99 for 2. Which conclusion is correct?

Unlike Mendel's seed counts, this gap is too large to be chance. Next step: look for a reason, such as fewer green seedlings surviving.

Mission 2

Mission 2: A harmful allele can hide in healthy carriers. How can you work out how many people carry it?

Make a guess: A harmful allele can hide in healthy carriers. How can you work out how many people carry it?

Mission 2

Populations and gene pools

  • Population: organisms of one species, in the same place at the same time, that can interbreed.
  • A species exists as one or more populations.
  • Gene pool: all the genes, and all their alleles, in a population.
  • Allele frequency: the fraction of all copies of a gene that are one allele.
  • Two alleles: frequency of A = p, frequency of a = q.
  • Each diploid has 2 copies of each gene. 500 individuals → 1000 copies.
  • Say: "allele frequency counts copies" — not: "the fraction of individuals that carry it".
Mission 2Worked example

Count the copies

A population of 400 snails: 196 AA, 168 Aa, 36 aa.

  • Step 1: total copies = 2 × 400 = 800.
  • Step 2: copies of A = (2 × 196) + 168 = 392 + 168 = 560.
  • Step 3: p = 560 ÷ 800 = 0.70.
  • Step 4: q = 1 − 0.70 = 0.30. Check: (2 × 36 + 168) ÷ 800 = 240 ÷ 800 = 0.30.
  • AA individuals give 2 copies; Aa give 1. Never count individuals.

Mission 2

The Hardy–Weinberg equations

  • p + q = 1 (all copies are A or a).
  • p² + 2pq + q² = 1 (all individuals are AA, Aa or aa).
  • p² = AA. 2pq = Aa. q² = aa.
  • Why 2pq? Aa forms two ways: A egg + a sperm, or a egg + A sperm.
  • Dominant phenotype = p² + 2pq = 1 − q². Recessive phenotype = q².
  • Prediction: allele frequencies stay the same from generation to generation.
  • p is usually the dominant allele; q the recessive.
Mission 2Worked example

Find the hidden carriers

Real figure: in the UK, about 1 in 2,500 babies has cystic fibrosis, a recessive condition.

  • Step 1: affected = aa = q². So q² = 1 ÷ 2,500 = 0.0004.
  • Step 2: q = √0.0004 = 0.02.
  • Step 3: p = 1 − 0.02 = 0.98.
  • Step 4: carriers = Aa = 2pq = 2 × 0.98 × 0.02 = 0.039.
  • So about 1 in 25 people is a carrier: about 98 carriers for every affected person.
  • Start from q², the only phenotype that shows one genotype.
Quick check
?In a large population, 16% of individuals show the phenotype of the homozygous recessive genotype for a gene with two alleles. Assuming Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, what is the frequency of the homozygous dominant genotype?
Mission 2

When the prediction holds

The allele frequencies stay constant only if:

  • the population is large (no chance changes: genetic drift),
  • mating is random,
  • there are no new mutations,
  • there is no migration in or out,
  • there is no natural selection: every genotype survives and reproduces equally.
  • Say: "equilibrium: frequencies stay the same" — not: "equilibrium: p = q = 0.5".
Mission 2

Read the data: MN blood groups

Real data: MN blood group genotypes in 6,129 people in the USA (a classic dataset). M and N are codominant, so each blood type shows its genotype.

MMMNNN
Observed178730391303
Expected (p², 2pq, q² × 6129)178430451300
  • p(M) = (2 × 1787 + 3039) ÷ (2 × 6129) = 6613 ÷ 12 258 = 0.5395. q(N) = 0.4605.
  • Expected MM = 0.5395² × 6129 ≈ 1784. Expected MN = 2 × 0.5395 × 0.4605 × 6129 ≈ 3045.
  • Keep four decimal places in p and q, or expected counts drift.
  • Observed and expected are very close.
  • A χ² test (see Mission 1) on these numbers gives a value far below any critical value.

Mission 2

When frequencies change

  • Evolution: a change in allele frequencies in a population over generations.
  • Selection, drift, migration and mutation change allele frequencies.
  • Say: "populations evolve" — not: "individuals evolve".
Mission 2

Recessive alleles hide

  • A harmful recessive allele is selected against only in aa individuals.
  • Most copies sit in healthy Aa carriers (cystic fibrosis: about 98 carriers per affected person).
  • So selection lowers q slowly, and ever more slowly as q falls.
  • Being dominant does not make an allele spread.
Quick check
?A large population of a hypothetical mammal species is in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium at a gene with alleles R and r, with r at a frequency of 0.2; R is completely dominant to r. A new disease then appears, and all rr individuals die before reproducing, while RR and Rr individuals survive and reproduce equally well. Which prediction about the frequency of r over the next several generations is best supported?
Mission check

?In a large, randomly mating population of a hypothetical moth species, pale wings are caused by a recessive allele. 9% of the moths have pale wings. Assuming Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, what proportion of the moths are heterozygous?

Count the ones you can see (q²), take the square root, and the hidden carriers appear: 2pq.

Mission 3

Mission 3: A rock pool and a rainforest are both called ecosystems. What do they have in common?

Make a guess: A rock pool and a rainforest are both called ecosystems. What do they have in common?

Mission 3

Levels of ecology

  • Population: all the organisms of one species in one area at one time.
  • They can potentially interbreed. A species exists as one or more populations.
  • Community: all the populations of every species in an area: plants, animals, fungi and bacteria.
  • Ecosystem: a community plus its abiotic (non-living) environment.
Mission 3

Habitats and ecosystems of every size

  • Habitat: the place where an organism, population or community lives.
  • Ecosystems range in size from very small to very large: a rock pool, a playing field, a large tree, a lake, an ocean.
  • Ecosystems are dynamic: populations rise and fall; species arrive and leave.
  • The answer to the hook: each is a community plus its non-living surroundings. Size does not matter.
Quick check
?Two groups of great crested newts breed in ponds 5 km apart, and no newt ever moves between the ponds. How should the two groups be described?
Mission 3

Abiotic and biotic factors

  • Abiotic factors: non-living. Temperature, light, water, pH, salinity, oxygen, wind.
  • Biotic factors: living. Food, predators, competitors, parasites, pathogens.
  • Both control where a species is found (distribution) and how many (abundance).
Quick check
?A student finds a woodland plant only on the shaded side of a hedge. Which pair names one abiotic factor and one biotic factor that could explain this?
Mission check

?A fallen, rotting log in a wood holds beetle larvae, woodlice, fungi and bacteria. Inside it are damp, decaying wood and still air. Which statement is correct?

Like a rock pool and a rainforest, the log is living things plus the non-living world they depend on. That is all an ecosystem needs.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Two kinds of barnacle can both live low on the shore. Why is only one of them ever found there?

Make a guess: Two kinds of barnacle can both live low on the shore. Why is only one of them ever found there?

Mission 4

Niche: a role, not a place

  • Habitat = where a species lives. Niche = how it lives there: its role in the ecosystem.
  • A niche includes its biotic interactions: what it eats, what eats it, what it competes with.
  • And its abiotic interactions: the temperature, water, light and pH it tolerates.
  • A niche is governed by adaptation to both abiotic and biotic conditions.
  • Say: "the niche is the species' role" — not: "the niche is where it lives".
Quick check
?Koalas (Phascolarctos cinereus) feed on Eucalyptus leaves, are active mainly at night, and are found in eucalypt woodland in eastern Australia where rainfall and temperature stay within particular ranges. Which statement about the ecological niche of the koala is correct?
Mission 4

One niche, one species

  • Two species with the same niche compete for the same limited resources.
  • Competitive exclusion: the better competitor survives; the other is eliminated from that area.
  • Gause (1934): Paramecium aurelia and P. caudatum, both fed on bacteria. Alone, each grew to a steady population.
  • Together, P. caudatum declined until almost none remained.
  • Example: Balanus barnacles keep Chthamalus barnacles off the lower shore, although Chthamalus can live there.
  • So no two species in a community have exactly the same niche.
Quick check
?Two species of Paramecium, P. aurelia and P. caudatum, both feed on bacteria. Grown in separate cultures with the same food supply, each species reached a stable population. Grown together in one culture, P. aurelia reached a similar density to when alone, while the population of P. caudatum declined steadily until almost none remained. Which conclusion is supported?
Mission check

?Two cattail species grow in North American ponds. Broad-leaved cattail grows in shallow water at the edge; narrow-leaved cattail grows further out, in deeper water. Where broad-leaved cattail is absent, narrow-leaved cattail grows well in shallow water too. Which statement best explains its distribution?

The barnacle in the hook could live low on the shore. Competition keeps it out, just as it keeps narrow-leaved cattail out of the shallows.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Why do small, isolated populations end up so genetically different from everyone else?

Make a guess: Why do small, isolated populations end up so genetically different from everyone else?

Mission 5

Chance in the gene pool

  • Gene pool: all the alleles of all the genes in a population.
  • Each generation's alleles are a sample of the last generation's.
  • Which individuals happen to breed, and which alleles go into the gametes, is partly chance.
  • Genetic drift: a random change in allele frequencies from one generation to the next, unrelated to whether the alleles help survival.
  • Drift can make an allele fixed (frequency 1) or lost (frequency 0), even a harmful or useful one.
  • When a river, mountains or distance split a population, no alleles move between the parts, so drift changes each part separately.
  • Say: "drift: chance change in allele frequency" — not: "drift: alleles drifting between populations".

Mission check

?A storm carries six birds of a mainland species to a distant island whose habitat is very like the mainland's. A hundred generations later, the island population is still small, and it differs greatly from the mainland in the frequencies of several alleles that have no known effect on survival. Which explanation is best supported?

Few individuals, little gene flow: chance alone can make a population's gene pool unlike any other.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • χ² = Σ (O − E)²/E with degrees of freedom = classes − 1; compare with the critical value at p = 0.05.
  • Below it: accept the null hypothesis (difference due to chance). Above it: reject it (significant difference).

Mission 2

  • p + q = 1 and p² + 2pq + q² = 1: from the recessive phenotype (q²) you can find every allele and genotype frequency.
  • Allele frequencies stay constant only in a large, randomly mating population with no mutation, migration or selection; a change in allele frequencies is evolution.

Mission 3

  • Population (one species) → community (all species) → ecosystem (community + abiotic environment); ecosystems can be any size.

Mission 4

  • A niche is a species' role: its biotic and abiotic interactions. Two species cannot share one niche for long; competition eliminates one or squeezes both.

Mission 5

  • Genetic drift is a random change in allele frequencies; it matters most in small populations, where chance sampling has large effects.
