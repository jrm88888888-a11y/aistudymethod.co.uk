Mission 1

Mission 1: In Hindu worship, why does seeing the deity matter, and being seen by it matter just as much?

Make a guess: In Hindu worship, why does seeing the deity matter, and being seen by it matter just as much?

Mission 1

What puja is

  • Puja: worship that honours a deity with offerings, as a beloved and honoured guest.
  • Offerings engage every sense: flowers, incense, a lamp, food, water, sandalwood paste, bells and song.
  • A short form has five offerings (panchopachara): scent, flower, incense, lamp, food. Fuller temple puja has sixteen (shodashopachara).
  • The worshipper often chants mantras and makes pranam (bowing, palms joined).
  • Aim: loving relationship with the deity (bhakti), and the deity's blessing.
Mission 1

Murti and darshan

  • A murti is a sacred image in which the deity is believed to be present, not an "idol" worshipped as mere stone or metal.
  • In a temple, a ceremony called prana pratishtha invites the deity's presence into the murti.
  • Darshan: "seeing". The devotee sees the deity and is seen by it; this exchange of sight is itself a blessing.
  • That is why the murti's eyes are prominent, and why curtains open for darshan at set times.
  • Scholar Diana Eck, Darśan: Seeing the Divine Image in India (1981), paraphrase: in Hindu worship, seeing is a way of touching and knowing the divine; darshan flows both ways.
Mission 1

Puja at home

  • Most Hindu homes have a shrine: a shelf, cabinet or small room, with murtis or pictures of the family's chosen deity (ishta-devata).
  • Daily: wash first; light a diya (lamp) and incense; offer flowers, water and food; ring a small bell; say mantras (e.g. the Gayatri mantra); perform a short arti.
  • Often led by women of the household; no priest needed.
  • Food cooked at home may be offered first, then eaten as blessed.
  • Personal and family: worship fits the rhythm of the day and the family's own tradition.
Mission 1

Puja in the mandir

  • A mandir is the home of the deity. Worshippers remove shoes, may ring the bell on entering, and approach the shrine.
  • The garbhagriha ("womb-house") holds the main murti, under the tower (shikhara); the hall (mandapa) is where people gather.
  • Pujaris (priests) care for the deity through the day: waking, bathing (abhisheka), dressing, offering food, arti, and putting to rest at night.
  • Worshippers bring offerings, receive darshan, join arti and bhajans (devotional songs).
  • Many British mandirs are also community centres: classes, weddings, meals, festivals.
Mission 1

Arti and prashad

  • Arti: a tray with a lamp of ghee wicks is waved in circles before the murti, with bells and a hymn (e.g. "Om Jai Jagdish Hare").
  • Worshippers pass their palms over the flame, then over their eyes and head: receiving the deity's light and blessing.
  • Prashad ("grace"): food offered to the deity is returned to the worshippers as blessed. Accepted with the right hand, and never thrown away.
  • Sharing prashad shares the deity's grace and binds the community.
  • Spelling: prasad.
Mission 1

Circumambulation

  • Pradakshina or parikrama: walking clockwise around the shrine or the murti, keeping the deity on one's right.
  • Meaning: the deity is the centre of life; the devotee's life revolves around God.
  • Done inside the temple's walkway around the garbhagriha, around sacred trees (the tulsi plant, the pipal) and around whole holy towns on pilgrimage.
  • Some devotees circle a set number of times, or prostrate at each step as a vow.

Mission 2

Holi: the spring full moon

  • Holi falls on the full moon of the month Phalguna (February or March): the end of winter and the start of the spring harvest.
  • Night one: Holika Dahan. A bonfire is lit; grains, coconuts and new wheat are offered to the fire.
  • Day two: the festival of colours. People throw coloured powder (gulal) and coloured water at friends, family and strangers.
  • Rules of status relax for the day: old and young, rich and poor play together; quarrels are made up.
  • Two different stories explain Holi in different traditions.
Mission 2

Holika and Prahlada

  • Hiranyakashipu, a demon king, had a boon that made him almost impossible to kill. He demanded that everyone worship him.
  • His son Prahlada would worship only Vishnu. His father tried again and again to kill him; each time Vishnu protected him.
  • The king's sister Holika could not be harmed by fire. She sat in a fire holding Prahlada on her lap.
  • Holika burned; Prahlada was unharmed, because of his devotion to Vishnu.
  • Vishnu then appeared as Narasimha (man-lion avatar) and killed Hiranyakashipu in a way his boon had not foreseen.
  • The bonfire recalls Holika's burning: good defeats evil, and God protects the devotee.
Mission 2

Krishna and Radha

  • In Braj (the region of Mathura and Vrindavan), Holi recalls the young Krishna and Radha.
  • In a well-known story, Krishna, dark-skinned, worried that fair Radha would not love him; his mother said he could colour Radha's face. He and the gopis (cowherd girls) played with colour.
  • The colours celebrate divine love and the playful joy (lila) of Krishna with his devotees.
  • In Barsana (Radha's village) and Vrindavan, Holi lasts for days, with songs, flowers and colour.
  • For Vaishnava devotees, Radha's love for Krishna is the model of the soul's love for God.

Mission 2

Durga and the buffalo demon

  • Durga is a form of the Goddess (Devi, Shakti: divine power).
  • The Devi Mahatmya (a text within the Markandeya Purana) tells the story (paraphrase):
  • The demon Mahishasura, who could take the form of a buffalo, had a boon that no man or god could kill him. He drove the gods from heaven.
  • The gods' combined energies took form as Durga. Each god gave her a weapon: Shiva's trident, Vishnu's discus. She rode a lion.
  • After a long battle, she killed Mahishasura: good defeats evil, through the power of the Mother.
  • Many Hindus read the demon also as inner evil: pride, anger, ignorance.
Mission 2

Durga Puja: the festival days

  • Held in the month Ashvin (September or October), at the same time as Navaratri ("nine nights") elsewhere in India; greatest in Bengal, Assam, Odisha and Tripura.
  • Mahalaya: Durga is invoked; many also make offerings to their ancestors.
  • Shashthi (day 6): the Goddess is welcomed and awakened in a clay murti in a pandal (temporary shrine). She stands with her children Lakshmi, Sarasvati, Ganesha and Kartikeya, with Mahishasura at her feet.
  • Saptami to Navami (days 7–9): puja, mantras, offerings, arti; the Sandhi Puja with 108 lamps and 108 lotuses recalls Rama.
  • Vijaya Dashami (day 10): married women smear red sindoor on the Goddess and each other; the murti is immersed in a river (visarjan).
  • In Bengali tradition, Durga is a daughter coming home to her parents for these days, then returning to Shiva.

Mission 2Diversity

One season, many festivals

  • Bengal: Durga Puja; community pandals and the homecoming of the Goddess.
  • Gujarat: Navaratri with garba and dandiya dances around a lamp for the Goddess.
  • North India: Ramlila plays and Dussehra, burning effigies of Ravana: focus on Rama's victory.
  • South India: Golu displays of dolls; Sarasvati and Lakshmi honoured too.
  • Holi too differs: Braj colour play for Krishna; the Holika bonfire for Prahlada; in parts of South India the focus is the god of love, Kama, burnt by Shiva.
  • Community: neighbourhood committees fund and run sarbojanin (public) pujas; families, priests, artists and volunteers keep each tradition alive. In 2021 UNESCO listed Durga Puja in Kolkata as intangible cultural heritage.

Mission 3

Tirtha: a crossing place

  • A tirtha is a holy place. The word means a ford: a place to cross a river.
  • Hindus speak of tirthas as places where it is easier to cross over from this world towards the divine, and from samsara towards moksha (liberation).
  • Many tirthas are on rivers, coasts or mountains; many are linked to a story of a deity or a saint.
  • Yatra (or tirtha-yatra): a pilgrimage, a journey to a tirtha.
  • Pilgrims go for many reasons: to fulfil a vow, to ask for help, to give thanks, to remove the effects of wrongdoing, to perform rites for the dead, or simply for darshan.
Mission 3

Darshan: seeing and being seen

  • Darshan means seeing: the devotee sees the deity, and is seen by the deity.
  • At a shrine or mandir, the murti (sacred image) is not "just a statue": many Hindus believe the deity is present in it, especially after its consecration.
  • Darshan is a moment of blessing, not mainly an act of looking at art. Many murtis have large, open eyes for this reason.
  • Darshan can also be received from a holy person (a guru or saint) or from a sacred place: the river Ganges itself is "seen" as a goddess.
  • On pilgrimage, the long journey leads to darshan at the end: at a temple, a river or a mountain.
Mission 3

Ganga Ma: the river as goddess

  • Hindus call the Ganges Ganga or Ganga Ma (Mother Ganga).
  • The story (told in the Ramayana and the Puranas, paraphrase): King Bhagiratha practised long austerities so that Ganga would come down from heaven to purify the ashes of his ancestors. Shiva caught her in his matted hair, so that her fall would not shatter the earth.
  • So the river is believed to flow from the divine world into this one: a tirtha along its whole length.
  • Bathing in Ganga is believed to purify: to wash away the effects of wrongdoing (papa).
  • Key places: Haridwar (where the river leaves the mountains), Prayagraj (meeting of Ganga, Yamuna and the unseen Sarasvati), Varanasi (Kashi, city of Shiva).
  • Kumbh Mela: a great bathing festival that rotates between Prayagraj, Haridwar, Ujjain and Nashik.
Mission 3

Death and the river

  • Water from the Ganges (Ganga jal) is kept in many Hindu homes, and a few drops may be placed in the mouth of a dying person.
  • Varanasi: many Hindus believe that to die in Kashi brings moksha, because Shiva himself whispers the words of liberation to the dying.
  • Cremation takes place on the ghats (riverside steps), e.g. Manikarnika Ghat.
  • After cremation, the ashes are immersed in the Ganges (asthi visarjan) if possible; families living elsewhere may carry or send the ashes to India.
  • Rites for ancestors (shraddha) are often performed at tirthas such as Gaya and Varanasi.
  • Not all Hindus share every belief: many see bathing as a symbol of inner purification, which still needs right action and devotion.

Yatra as devotion

Krishna speaks in the Bhagavad Gita:

> "Whoso shall offer Me in faith and love / A leaf, a flower, a fruit, water poured forth, / That offering I accept, lovingly made / With pious will." (Bhagavad Gita 9:26, Arnold)

  • In bhakti (loving devotion) what matters is love, not the cost of the gift. A pilgrimage can be such an offering: a journey made for God.
  • Vaishnava example: the Pandharpur Wari in Maharashtra. Varkari devotees walk for weeks, singing, to Vithoba (a form of Vishnu/Krishna), carrying the sandals of the saints Jnaneshwar and Tukaram.
  • Shaiva example: the Kanwar Yatra. Devotees carry Ganga water from Haridwar and other places to pour over the linga of Shiva.

Q1 What does the verse suggest God values in an offering?

Show answer

The love and faith with which it is made, not its value.

Q2 Why might the Kanwar Yatra be called bhakti and not only ritual?

Show answer

The journey and the carried water are offered to Shiva in love.

Mission 3

Why India matters

  • Most tirthas are tied to stories set in India: Ayodhya (Rama), Mathura and Vrindavan (Krishna), Kurukshetra (the Gita), Varanasi (Shiva).
  • Many Hindus call India punya bhumi (holy land) or Bharat Mata (Mother India). The land itself, its rivers and mountains, is treated as sacred.
  • The Char Dham (four abodes: Badrinath, Dwarka, Puri, Rameswaram) mark the four corners of India: the whole land as one sacred circuit.
  • In the past, some orthodox Hindus believed crossing the ocean (kala pani, "black water") broke caste purity. Gandhi's caste group expelled him for sailing to England in 1888.
  • Hindus living elsewhere often keep links: pilgrimages to India, ashes taken to the Ganges, Ganga jal kept at home.
Mission 3Diversity

Sacred sites outside India

Old sites beyond modern India's borders:

  • Pashupatinath in Kathmandu, Nepal: a great Shiva temple.
  • Mount Kailash in Tibet: believed to be Shiva's abode; pilgrims walk round it.
  • Hinglaj in Pakistan: one of the Shakti Peethas (seats of the Goddess).

Newer sites, made by migrant communities:

  • Grand Bassin (Ganga Talao), Mauritius: a crater lake linked to the Ganges; pilgrims walk there at Maha Shivaratri.
  • Batu Caves, Malaysia: a Murugan shrine, focus of the Thaipusam festival.
  • UK: Skanda Vale in Wales; Bhaktivedanta Manor in Hertfordshire (ISKCON); the Neasden mandir in London.
  • How a site becomes sacred: a deity is installed in a murti by prana pratishtha (rite inviting the deity's presence); a saint lives there; a story or a "Ganga" is linked to it; pilgrims keep coming.

Mission 4: Hindus say some scriptures were "heard" and others "remembered". Why does the difference matter?

Make a guess: Hindus say some scriptures were "heard" and others "remembered". Why does the difference matter?

Mission 4

Heard and remembered

  • Shruti ("that which is heard"): texts Hindus believe were heard or seen by ancient sages (rishis) in deep meditation, not composed by them.
  • Shruti = the Vedas, each with its hymns, ritual texts and Upanishads.
  • Smriti ("that which is remembered"): texts composed and handed down by humans: the epics (Ramayana, Mahabharata, including the Bhagavad Gita), the Puranas, and law books such as the Manusmriti.
  • Many Hindus believe shruti is eternal and without a human author (apaurusheya).
Mission 4

Which has more authority?

  • Traditionally, shruti has the highest authority: it is revealed, timeless knowledge.
  • Smriti is authoritative because it explains and applies shruti.
  • Classical rule (Mimamsa school): if a smriti text conflicts with shruti, shruti wins.
  • Manusmriti 2.6 (paraphrase) lists the sources of dharma in order: the Veda, then smriti, then the conduct of good people, then one's own conscience.
  • Hindu schools that accept Vedic authority are called astika ("orthodox"); Buddhists and Jains, who reject it, are nastika.
Mission 4

The Vedas in worship

  • The Vedas are mostly heard in ritual, not read: priests recite them in Sanskrit with exact sound and accent.
  • Samskaras (life-cycle rites): Vedic mantras are chanted at the sacred-thread ceremony, weddings and funerals.
  • The Gayatri mantra (Rig Veda 3.62.10) is recited daily by many Hindus.
  • Havan and temple rites use Vedic verses alongside later ritual texts.
  • Traditionally only "twice-born" men learned the Vedas; reform movements such as the Arya Samaj teach them to everyone, including women.
Mission 4

The Upanishads: the end of the Veda

  • The Upanishads are the last part of the Vedas, c. 800–200 BCE.
  • Distinctive: philosophical dialogues between teacher and pupil, not hymns or ritual rules.
  • Key teaching: Brahman (ultimate reality) and atman (the true self) and how they relate.
  • They are called Vedanta, "end of the Veda", and are the basis of the Vedanta schools (Shankara's Advaita, Ramanuja's Vishishtadvaita, Madhva's Dvaita).
  • Detail in a later mission.

Mission 4

The great smriti texts

  • Ramayana (attributed to Valmiki): Prince Rama's exile, the rescue of Sita and his rule. Rama is the ideal of dharma.
  • Mahabharata (attributed to Vyasa): the war between cousins, the Pandavas and Kauravas; duty in impossible situations.
  • Bhagavad Gita: Krishna's teaching to the warrior Arjuna, within the Mahabharata. Often treated almost as shruti.
  • Puranas: stories of the gods and avatars; the Manusmriti: rules for varna, stage of life, family and kingship.
  • Smriti is known and loved: through festivals, storytelling, temple art, drama and television.

Mission 5

Sitting near the teacher

  • Upanishad is often explained as "sitting down near" a teacher to receive hidden teaching.
  • The final part of the Vedas (shruti), so also called Vedanta, "end of the Veda".
  • The oldest (Brihadaranyaka, Chandogya) date from c. 700–500 BCE; others are later. Tradition counts 108; about 13 are "principal".
  • Setting: teachers (gurus) with pupils, often in forest retreats. Teachers include Yajnavalkya and Uddalaka; women such as Gargi and Maitreyi question and debate.
  • A shift: from performing outer sacrifice to gaining inner knowledge (jnana) of reality.
Mission 5

Nachiketas and Yama

  • Katha Upanishad: Vajashravas gives away worn-out cows at a great sacrifice (yajna). His son Nachiketas asks to whom he himself will be given; in anger his father replies that he gives him to Death.
  • Nachiketas waits three days at the house of Yama, god of death. As amends Yama grants three boons.
  • (1) His father's peace of mind. (2) Knowledge of the fire-sacrifice that leads to heaven. (3) What happens to a person after death.
  • Yama offers wealth, long life and pleasures instead; Nachiketas refuses, because such things are fleeting.
  • Yama's answer to the third boon: "This Self is never born, nor does It die. ... It is not slain even though the body is slain." (Katha 2:18, Paramananda)
  • Yajna: the story moves from ritual (the second boon) to knowledge of the self (the third), which ritual cannot give.
Mission 5

Brahman and atman

  • Brahman: the ultimate reality, the ground of everything, beyond all names and forms.
  • Atman: the true self in every living being, beneath body, senses and mind.
  • The Upanishads' central teaching: atman is Brahman, or at least inseparable from it. To know this is liberation (moksha).
Mission 5Read the source

The chariot

Katha Upanishad 3:3–4 (paraphrase of Paramananda): the atman is the lord of the chariot; the body is the chariot; the intellect is the driver; the mind is the reins; the senses are the horses; the objects of sense are the roads.

> "But he who is full of discrimination and whose mind is always controlled, his senses are manageable, like the good horses of a driver." (Katha 3:6)

> "This Atman (Self), hidden in all beings, does not shine forth; but It is seen by subtle seers through keen and subtle understanding." (Katha 3:12)

  • Katha 3:15 (paraphrase): knowing the soundless, formless, eternal reality, a person escapes "from the mouth of death".
  • Q: Who is the driver, and what does a good driver do? (The intellect; it uses discrimination to keep the senses under control.)

Salt in water

Chandogya Upanishad 6:13:1–3 (paraphrase): The teacher Uddalaka tells his son Shvetaketu to put salt in water overnight. In the morning the salt cannot be seen, yet every sip tastes salty. "In the same way," says Uddalaka, "you do not see the Real here, but it is here. That finest essence is the self of all this world. That is the Real. That is the atman. You are that (tat tvam asi), Shvetaketu."

Q1 What does the salt stand for?

Show answer

Brahman: unseen but present everywhere.

Q2 What does "you are that" claim?

Show answer

Shvetaketu's true self is one with that essence.

Mission 5

Jnana and the Vedanta schools

  • Jnana (knowledge) is realisation, not information: "This Self cannot be attained by study of the Scriptures, nor by intellectual perception" (Katha 2:23, Paramananda).
  • The path: a guru, ethical life, control of the senses, meditation (e.g. on Om).
  • The Upanishads begin the philosophical traditions of India, especially Vedanta. Its schools read the same texts differently:
  • Advaita (Shankara, c. 8th century): atman and Brahman are identical; the world of difference is maya.
  • Vishishtadvaita (Ramanuja, 11th–12th century): selves are real and distinct, yet part of Brahman, a personal God.
  • Dvaita (Madhva, 13th century): God and selves are eternally different.

Mission 6

The Song of the Lord

  • Bhagavad Gita: "Song of the Lord". 700 verses in 18 chapters.
  • It sits in book 6 of the Mahabharata, on the eve of the war at Kurukshetra.
  • Krishna, whom many Hindus worship as God (an avatar of Vishnu, or for some the Supreme himself), is Arjuna's charioteer.
  • Arjuna is the great archer of the five Pandava brothers.
  • Usually dated somewhere between c. 400 BCE and 200 CE; scholars disagree.
  • The most widely read Hindu text: recited at home and in temples, learned by heart, read at funerals, commented on by every major school of Vedanta.
Mission 6

Arjuna's crisis

  • Arjuna looks across the field and sees teachers, uncles, cousins and friends in the enemy army.
  • He drops his bow: killing kin would destroy family dharma and bring chaos and sin (chapter 1).
  • But he is a kshatriya (warrior); his duty is to fight a just war.
  • So his personal dharma (svadharma) as a warrior clashes with his dharma as a kinsman.
  • Krishna's answer fills the next 17 chapters: who you really are; how to act; how to love God.
Mission 6Read the source

Do your own duty

Bhagavad Gita 2:31 (Edwin Arnold's verse translation, 1885), Krishna to Arjuna:

> "Nought better can betide a martial soul

> Than lawful war"

Bhagavad Gita 18:47 (Arnold):

> "Better thine own work is, though done with fault,

> Than doing others' work, ev'n excellently."

Q1 What kind of war does 2:31 approve?

Show answer

A "lawful" war: a righteous war, fought according to dharma, not any war.

Q2 According to 18:47, why should Arjuna not leave the battle to become a peaceful hermit?

Show answer

His own duty, even done imperfectly, is better than another's duty done well.

Mission 6

Karma yoga: act without clinging

  • Karma yoga: the path of action, done as duty, without attachment to its results.
  • 3:5 (Arnold): "no jot of time, at any time, / Rests any actionless; his nature's law / Compels him, even unwilling, into act".
  • So giving up action is impossible: everyone acts. What matters is how.
  • 3:19 (Arnold): "thy task prescribed / With spirit unattached gladly perform".
  • Selfless action builds no binding karma, so it leads towards moksha (liberation).
  • Arjuna should fight, not for glory or gain, but because it is his duty, offered to God.
Mission 6

Bhakti yoga: love and surrender

  • Bhakti yoga: the path of loving devotion to a personal God.
  • 9:26 (Arnold): "Whoso shall offer Me in faith and love / A leaf, a flower, a fruit, water poured forth, / That offering I accept".
  • 9:27: whatever you do (eating, giving, fasting) do it as an offering to God.
  • 9:32 (Arnold): "Woman or man; sprung of the Vaisya caste / Or lowly disregarded Sudra,--all / Plant foot upon the highest path". Bhakti is open to everyone.
  • 11:54 (Arnold): "Only by fullest service, perfect faith, / And uttermost surrender am I known".
  • After Arjuna sees Krishna's universal form (chapter 11), many readers take the Gita to present devotion as the highest way to know God (11:54; 12:2).
  • A third path, jnana yoga, is the path of knowledge: seeing the eternal self as distinct from the body (2:20).

One text, many readings

  • Shankara (c. 8th century), Advaita: the Gita's goal is jnana, knowledge that atman is Brahman; action prepares the mind.
  • Ramanuja (trad. 1017–1137), Vishishtadvaita: the goal is bhakti, loving surrender to a personal God.
  • B. G. Tilak (1856–1920), Gita Rahasya (1915): the Gita teaches karma yoga, active duty in the world (including the struggle for Indian independence).
  • M. K. Gandhi (1869–1948): the battle is an allegory of the inner war between good and evil; the Gita teaches selfless action and, for him, non-violence.
  • ISKCON: A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada, Bhagavad-gita As It Is (1968): Krishna is the Supreme Person; bhakti is the goal.
  • In daily life: the Gita is recited, sung, studied in groups, and celebrated at Gita Jayanti.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Puja honours a deity with offerings to the senses; darshan is the two-way sight between devotee and deity, believed present in the murti.
  • In the mandir: garbhagriha, pujaris, arti, prashad and clockwise circumambulation; at home: a family shrine, often led by women.

Mission 2

  • Holi (Phalguna full moon): the Holika bonfire recalls Prahlada saved by Vishnu; the colours recall Krishna and Radha's love; both teach that good overcomes evil.
  • Durga Puja (Ashvin): Durga, the divine mother made from the gods' energies, defeats Mahishasura; Rama worshipped her before fighting Ravana.

Mission 3

  • A tirtha is a "crossing place"; on yatra, pilgrims seek darshan (seeing and being seen by the deity), purification and help for the dead.
  • Ganga is a goddess believed to have come down from heaven; bathing purifies, and ashes are immersed in her waters.

Mission 4

  • Shruti ("heard": the Vedas, including the Upanishads) has the highest authority; smriti ("remembered": epics, Gita, Puranas, Manusmriti) applies it.
  • The Vedas live mainly in ritual recitation; smriti is better known and shapes festivals and ethics.

Mission 5

  • The Upanishads are the end of the Vedas (Vedanta): teachings of gurus on Brahman and atman, moving from outer sacrifice to inner knowledge.
  • The Katha Upanishad: Nachiketas learns from Yama; the chariot image (Katha 3) shows the intellect controlling the senses.
  • "You are that" (Chandogya 6:13) is read as identity (Advaita), part and whole (Vishishtadvaita), or difference (Dvaita).

Mission 6

  • The Gita is Krishna's teaching to Arjuna at Kurukshetra: do your own dharma (2:31, 18:47), even when duties clash.
  • Three paths: karma yoga (action without attachment, 3:19), bhakti yoga (devotion, 9:26, 11:54), jnana yoga (knowledge of the self).
