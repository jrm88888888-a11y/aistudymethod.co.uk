Mission 1

Three Jewels, three refuges

  • The Three Jewels (Pali Tiratana, Sanskrit Triratna): the Buddha, the Dhamma (teaching, truth) and the Sangha (community).
  • A refuge (sarana) is a shelter. Here: shelter from dukkha and the round of rebirth (samsara).
  • Going for refuge is the basic act that marks a person as a Buddhist, in every school.
  • A common picture: the Buddha is the doctor, the Dhamma the medicine, the Sangha the nurses and fellow patients who help one recover.
  • They are called jewels: rare, precious, and worth more than anything else one could rely on.
  • Your spec pairs Dhamma/Dharma and Sangha/Samgha.
Mission 1Read the source

The refuge formula

In Pali, from the Khuddakapatha (Khp 1), chanted by Buddhists across the world:

  • Buddham saranam gacchami · Dhammam saranam gacchami · Sangham saranam gacchami
  • Literal rendering: "To the Buddha for refuge I go. To the Dhamma for refuge I go. To the Sangha for refuge I go."
  • Then repeated with dutiyam pi ("for a second time…") and tatiyam pi ("for a third time…").

Q1 Why might the formula be said three times?

Show answer

To make the commitment firm and deliberate, not a passing thought; repetition also marks it as a formal act.

Q2 Who is "I" here?

Show answer

The person committing: the formula is a personal choice, said aloud.

Mission 1

Refuge in the Buddha

  • Historical: Siddhattha Gotama, the teacher who found the path and showed it to others. Not a creator god; for most schools, a guide rather than a saviour who acts for us (Pure Land differs: A later screen).
  • Ideal: awakening itself, the goal anyone can reach. Refuge is in the qualities of a Buddha: wisdom, compassion, purity.
  • "Whoever sees the Dhamma sees me" (Samyutta Nikaya 22.87, paraphrase): the Buddha is met in his teaching.
  • Theravada: stresses the historical Buddha as teacher and model.
  • Mahayana: many Buddhas (e.g. Amitabha) and the Buddha-nature in all beings; the Buddha is also a reality beyond one human life.
Mission 1

Refuge in the Dhamma

  • Dhamma as teaching: the words of the Buddha: the Four Noble Truths, the path.
  • Dhamma as truth: the way things really are; ultimately nibbana, unconditioned reality, which the teaching points to.
  • Traditional qualities: well proclaimed; seen here and now; timeless; ehipassiko ("come and see"); leading onward; known by the wise for themselves.
  • The raft (Majjhima Nikaya 22): the teaching is like a raft for crossing a river, to be used, not carried on one's head after crossing.

Mission 1

Refuge in the Sangha

  • Noble Sangha (ariya-sangha): those who have reached at least the first stage of awakening (stream-entry). In Theravada the strict object of refuge.
  • Monastic Sangha: monks (bhikkhus) and nuns (bhikkhunis). They keep and teach the Dhamma; lay people support them with food and gifts; the monks give teaching and blessings in return.
  • Fourfold assembly: monks, nuns, laymen, laywomen.
  • All Buddhists past, present and future.
  • Spiritual friends: one's close companions in practice. The Buddha told Ananda that good friendship is "the whole of the holy life" (Samyutta Nikaya 45.2, paraphrase).

Mission 1

How the three fit together

  • Each refuge needs the others: the Buddha discovered the Dhamma; the Dhamma is what he taught; the Sangha preserves it and lives it.
  • Refuge in one without the others is incomplete: a teacher without teaching, a teaching without anyone practising it.
  • Why the "heart" of Buddhism: every practice (meditation, precepts, giving) begins from this commitment.
Mission 1

Going for refuge in practice

  • Daily puja: homage to the Buddha (Namo tassa…, three times), then the three refuges, often the Five Precepts. In Sri Lanka this is called pansil.
  • Bowing three times before a shrine: one bow for each jewel.
  • Ceremony: a new Buddhist may take the refuges and precepts formally before a monk or teacher.
  • Tibetan: refuge also in the lama (teacher); many make 100,000 refuge prostrations as a preliminary practice.
  • Pure Land: the nembutsu is a calling on, and refuge in, Amida (Amitabha) Buddha.
  • Triratna (founded by Sangharakshita): Going for Refuge is the central act; ordination is "effective going for refuge".
Mission 1Diversity

Heritage and convert Buddhists

  • Heritage Buddhists: born into Buddhist families and cultures (e.g. Sri Lanka, Thailand, Tibet, and their communities in Britain).
  • Refuges chanted in Pali or another traditional language from childhood; temple-centred; the monastic Sangha central; making merit by giving.
  • Convert Buddhists: adults who choose Buddhism, often in the West.
  • A formal, often emotional decision; refuge ceremony as a turning point; often meditation-centred; "sangha" may mean one's practice group or friends.
  • Both chant the same formula. These are tendencies, not rules: many heritage Buddhists meditate; many converts join traditional temples.
Mission 1Read the source

Which refuge is safe?

> "Men, driven by fear, go to many a refuge, to mountains and forests, to groves and sacred trees." (Dhammapada 188, Müller 1881)

> "He who takes refuge with Buddha, the Law, and the Church; he who, with clear understanding, sees the four holy truths" (Dhammapada 190, Müller 1881)

  • Müller's "the Law" is Dhamma; "the Church" is Sangha. Verse 192: that refuge is "the best refuge".
  • Buddhist analysis: worldly refuges, ancient (sacred trees) or modern (money, status), are impermanent (anicca), so they cannot give final security. They may still be wholesome supports.

Q1 What drives people to the first kind of refuge?

Show answer

Fear.

Q2 Why might a Buddhist say a bank account is a refuge like the "sacred trees"?

Show answer

It is relied on for safety, but it changes and cannot end dukkha.

Mission 1Evaluate

Which refuge matters most?

  • Claim: The Dhamma is the most important of the three refuges.
  • For: before his death the Buddha said the Dhamma and discipline would be the teacher after him (Digha Nikaya 16).
  • For: the Buddha and the Sangha both point to the Dhamma; "whoever sees the Dhamma sees me".
  • Against: without the Buddha, the Dhamma would not have been found and taught.
  • Against: without the Sangha, the Dhamma would not have been kept or passed on; for many converts, the Sangha is what makes practice possible.
  • Against: refuge is taken in all three together; the jewels may not be meant to be ranked.
  • Judgement criteria: Which refuge is most needed now that the Buddha has died? Do heritage and convert Buddhists answer differently? Is ranking the jewels a Buddhist question?

Mission 2

What is the Sangha?

  • Sangha: "community"; the third of the Three Refuges (Buddha, Dhamma, Sangha).
  • Monastic Sangha: ordained monks (bhikkhus) and nuns (bhikkhunis), living by the Vinaya.
  • Noble Sangha (ariya-sangha): all who have reached at least stream-entry, monastic or lay. For Theravada, the refuge in its fullest sense.
  • Fourfold assembly: monks, nuns, laymen and laywomen together.
  • Also one's close spiritual friends, and all Buddhists past, present and future.
Mission 2

Traditional roles of monks

  • The wat (temple-monastery) was the centre of village life.
  • Education: before state schools spread (from the 1920s), boys learned to read at the wat; novices still study Pali and Dhamma.
  • Healing and counsel: herbal medicine, advice, settling disputes.
  • Ceremonies: chanting at funerals, house-blessings and new ventures; paritta chants for protection.
  • Keepers of the Dhamma: teaching, sermons on observance days.
  • Two vocations: village and town monks (study, teaching, ritual) and forest monks (meditation), e.g. Ajahn Mun (1870–1949) and Ajahn Chah (1918–1992).
Mission 2

The exchange

  • Monks may not farm, cook stored food or (by the Vinaya) handle money. They depend on lay people.
  • Lay people give material support: food on the dawn alms round, robes at Kathina (after the rains retreat), medicine, lodging.
  • Monks give the Dhamma: teaching, example, chanting, counsel.
  • Lay people gain merit: the Sangha is called the best field of merit, where good deeds grow most.
  • On the alms round the monk does not ask and does not thank: the giver is grateful for the chance to give.
  • On observance days (uposatha), lay people visit the wat and take extra precepts.
Mission 2Read the source

Mutual help

The Itivuttaka (107), paraphrase: the Buddha tells his monks that householders are very helpful to them, providing robes, food, lodging and medicine; and the monks are very helpful to householders, teaching them the Dhamma. So, supporting each other, both live the holy life.

"As the bee collects nectar and departs without injuring the flower, or its colour or scent, so let a sage dwell in his village." (Dhammapada 49, Müller)

Q1 What does each side give the other in Itivuttaka 107?

Show answer

Lay people give material needs; monks give the Dhamma.

Q2 What does the image of the bee say about how a monk should receive alms?

Show answer

Take only what is needed, without burdening or harming the givers.

Mission 2Diversity

Many kinds of sangha

  • Thailand: forest monks and town monks; and mae chi: women in white robes who keep eight or ten precepts but are not recognised as ordained nuns by the Thai Sangha.
  • Japan: since 1872, most temple priests in most schools marry and run family temples; Jodo Shinshu priests never were celibate.
  • Tibet: large monasteries; lamas as teachers; lay devotees support them.
  • Triratna (UK): an order neither monastic nor lay; "sangha" means the whole community.
  • So "sangha" can mean the monastic Sangha, the noble Sangha, or the whole spiritual community.

Mission 3

Changing roles

  • Over the 20th century, the Thai state took over many monastic roles: schools (from the 1920s), hospitals, social welfare.
  • Cities grew; many people now meet monks mainly at ceremonies, funerals and on the alms round.
  • Fewer men make the monastic life lifelong; temporary ordination continues but often for shorter periods.
  • Money scandals and a trade in blessed amulets have damaged trust in some monks.
  • New roles have grown:
  • Development monks: village co-operatives, rice banks.
  • Ecology monks: "ordaining" trees with saffron cloth to protect forests.
  • Care: hospices, e.g. for people with HIV/AIDS (from the 1990s).
  • Media: Dhamma talks on radio, TV and social media.
Mission 3Evaluate

"Monastic life and the precepts are still relevant today."

  • For: monks keep the Dhamma alive and teach it, now online as well as in person.
  • For: a life of few possessions is a living challenge to consumerism.
  • For: new roles (hospices, ecology, media teaching) show the Sangha serving modern needs.
  • Against: the state now does what monks once did (schools, health care).
  • Against: scandals and commercialisation suggest the Vinaya is hard to keep in a money economy.
  • Against: lay meditation movements and Western groups such as Triratna show the path without traditional monasticism.
  • Judgement criteria: Relevant to whom: Thai villagers, city workers, Western converts? Is relevance measured by numbers, by service, or by faithfulness to the Dhamma?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Going for refuge to the Buddha, Dhamma and Sangha, chanted three times in Pali, marks a person as a Buddhist.
  • Buddha: historical teacher and ideal of awakening; Dhamma: teaching and ultimate truth; Sangha: noble, monastic, fourfold, all Buddhists, spiritual friends.
  • Heritage and convert Buddhists share the formula but often live it differently; Buddhists see other "refuges" today as impermanent.

Mission 2

  • "Sangha" means the monastic community, the noble ones (stream-entry and beyond), or the whole community of Buddhists.
  • Lay people give material support and make merit; monks give the Dhamma.

Mission 3

  • The Thai state has taken over schooling and health care; monks have found new roles (development, ecology, hospices, media); scandals have damaged trust in some monks.
  • Whether monastic life and the precepts are still relevant is debated: service and simplicity versus changed roles and scandal.
