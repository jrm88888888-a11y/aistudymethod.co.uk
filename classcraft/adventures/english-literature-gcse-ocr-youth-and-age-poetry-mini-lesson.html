Mission 1

Poor children sing in St Paul's. Is Blake cheering or warning?

Make a guess: Poor children sing in St Paul's. Is Blake cheering or warning?

Youth and Age · "Holy Thursday"Screen 1

The day behind the poem

  • William Blake (1757–1827) printed and coloured his own books. This poem is in Songs of Innocence (1789).
  • Holy Thursday here means Ascension Day. From 1782, thousands of children from London's charity schools (free schools for the poor, paid for by donors) marched to a service at St Paul's Cathedral.
  • Beadles were parish officers who kept order.
  • In Songs of Experience (1794) Blake wrote a second "Holy Thursday": "Is this a holy thing to see". Your anthology prints the Innocence poem.

First reading: a procession in three stanzas

StanzaWhat happens
1Clean, uniformed children walk "two and two" behind the beadles, flowing into St Paul's.
2Seated, they are "flowers of London town" and "multitudes of lambs", raising their hands.
3Their song rises "to heaven". The "aged men" sit "Beneath them". The last line gives a moral.
  • Youth and Age: young and old are placed in a hierarchy, and the song lifts the poor children above their elderly "guardians".

Youth and Age · "Holy Thursday"Screen 5

Celebration or warning?

  • Form: long fourteen-syllable lines in rhyming couplets (clean / green, snow / flow) move at the slow, orderly pace of a procession.
  • Many readers take the speaker's joy at face value; others, such as David Erdman, find the speaker's view naive, a picture Blake asks us to question.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Poor children sing in St Paul's. Is Blake cheering or warning?

Both readings can be argued. The speaker delights in the children's "radiance" and their song, which rises like "a mighty wind" above the "aged men"; but the scrubbed faces, the beadles' "wands" and the possibly ironic "wise guardians" hint at control. The strongest answers weigh both, and Songs of Experience later asks the question outright.

Mission 2

One small flower by a road makes a young woman cry. Why?

Make a guess: One small flower by a road makes a young woman cry. Why?

Youth and Age · "The Bluebell"Screen 1

Away from home

  • Anne Brontë (1820–1849), the youngest Brontë sister, worked as a governess: a teacher living in a wealthy family's house, far from her own home at Haworth. She held a post at Blake Hall in 1839 and, from May 1840, at Thorp Green Hall near York.
  • She wrote "The Bluebell" on 22 August 1840, aged twenty.
  • It stayed in manuscript and was first published in 1902, long after her death.

Youth and Age · "The Bluebell"Screen 5

Form: a hymn-like memory

  • Twelve quatrains in common metre, the metre of many hymns: lines of eight and six syllables alternate, with four beats then three. Lines 2 and 4 rhyme (day / away).
  • The steady, song-like form holds a painful feeling under control.
  • Stanza 8 breaks into rhetorical questions: "Whence came that rising in my throat".
  • The last stanza gives the flower a voice (personification), addressing her as "Sad wanderer".

Mission checkScreen 9

?One small flower by a road makes a young woman cry. Why?

The bluebell recalls her "happy childhood's hours", when flowers seemed "like fairy gifts" and she lived with people who loved her. Set against her present "thankless life" among "heartless crowds", it shows her how much has gone, and the flower itself tells her to weep for "blissful times / That never may return".

Mission 3

A boy alone on a night train. What does the watcher see in him?

Make a guess: A boy alone on a night train. What does the watcher see in him?

Youth and Age · "Midnight on the Great Western"Screen 1

A journey, and a title

  • Thomas Hardy (1840–1928), novelist and poet, published this poem in Moments of Vision (1917).
  • The Great Western was the railway running west from London. Third class was the cheapest seat.
  • The speaker watches a boy travelling alone at midnight. We never learn who the boy is.
  • OCR's anthology takes its title, Towards a World Unknown, from this poem's third stanza.

"Midnight on the Great Western", stanzas 1 and 3

Youth and Age · "Midnight on the Great Western"Screen 3

First reading: watching, then wondering

StanzaWhat happens
1Lamplight falls on the tired boy, who seems not to know where he's going "Or whence he came".
2A ticket is stuck in his hat band; a string round his neck holds "the key of his box" (his trunk). He has been sent off alone.
3The speaker addresses him: what past does he have, travelling calmly into "This plunge alone"?
4Does his soul come from a higher place, so he lives in "This region of sin that you find you in, / But are not of"?

Youth and Age · "Midnight on the Great Western"Screen 5

Form: a stanza that drops

  • Five-line stanzas. Line 1 always returns to the "journeying boy", like a refrain.
  • Lines 2 and 5 rhyme (flame / came, unknown / alone), and line 4 carries an internal rhyme (stake / undertake).
  • Each stanza ends on a very short line, a sudden drop: "This plunge alone?"
  • Structure: stanzas 1–2 describe "he"; stanzas 3–4 address "you" in questions that the boy never answers.

Mission 4

Two war poems, a century apart. How do you compare, not list?

Make a guess: Two war poems, a century apart. How do you compare, not list?

Poetry skills · comparing poemsScreen 1

One argument, two poems

  • A comparison is one argument about both poems, not two essays joined by "on the other hand".
  • Compare what the poems say (ideas, attitudes) and how they say it (language, form, structure).
  • Find a link first, then the differences within it.
  • Keep both poems in play in each paragraph, using whereas, similarly, in contrast, both.

Mission 5

A poem you've never seen, twenty minutes. What do you look for first?

Make a guess: A poem you've never seen, twenty minutes. What do you look for first?

Literature skills · unseen poetryScreen 1

Five minutes, four questions

Before you write a word:

  • The question: underline its focus (e.g. 'how the poet presents memory').
  • The poem, twice: title included. First for sense, second with a pen.
  • The situation: who is speaking, to whom, where and when? What happens?
  • The turn: where does it shift in time, place or tone? Look for 'but', 'yet', a new stanza or a change of tense.

Then choose 2–3 methods that serve the focus. Don't start labelling at line 1.

Form and structure

  • Three quatrains (four-line stanzas), rhymed ABAB: floor/door, thin/in.
  • Short lines, most with three strong beats: HERE is the AN-cient FLOOR.
  • Structure: present place → remembered people → the child's joy → the turn. The title, "The Self-Unseeing", names the idea: they could not see their own happiness while it was happening.
  • The final exclamation sounds like a sudden realisation, or regret.

Recap

Big ideas

  • A real event: London's charity-school children at St Paul's on Ascension Day, from 1782, described by an "innocent" speaker.
  • Joy and power: the children are "flowers", "lambs" and "a mighty wind", while the old sit "Beneath them".
  • Both readings are valid; part (b) answers that weigh celebration against warning show the "different valid responses" the spec asks for.
  • The poem layers three times: the speaker now, a sunny day "not long ago", and her childhood.
  • Childhood ("fairy gifts", "kindred hearts") is set against adult work ("heartless crowds", "a thankless life"); a hymn-like form holds the grief steady.
  • A strong part (b) choice for memory, loss of childhood, or nature that triggers feeling.
  • Precise details (third-class seat, ticket in the hat band, key on a string) show a child sent off alone.
  • The speaker moves from describing "he" to questioning "you"; the boy becomes a symbol of youth facing "a world unknown".
  • Good for comparing journeys, innocence, and older speakers watching the young; the anthology's title comes from this poem.
  • A comparison is one argument about both poems: find a link, then explore the differences within it.
  • Compare ideas and methods together (voice, metre, rhyme, imagery), using comparative connectives in every paragraph.
  • Your exam pairs a named poem with a second poem (one you choose, or an unseen one): know which, and write about both.
  • First five minutes: underline the question's focus, read the poem twice, work out the situation, find the turn.
  • Read clues for inference ("Bowing it" suggests a fiddle); you don't need to know the poet.
  • Choose 2–3 methods that serve the focus: here the tense shift, the light imagery and the final "Yet".

Key quotations to learn

  • "’Twas on a holy Thursday, their innocent faces clean," (stanza 1, line 1) innocence, or a scrubbed public show
  • "Grey-headed beadles walked before, with wands as white as snow," (stanza 1, line 3) age leads and controls youth
  • "O what a multitude they seemed, these flowers of London town!" (stanza 2, line 1) the speaker's delight; children as flowers
  • "Now like a mighty wind they raise to heaven the voice of song," (stanza 3, line 1) the children's power in song
  • "Beneath them sit the aged men, wise guardians of the poor." (stanza 3, line 3) young above old; praise or irony?
  • "Then cherish pity, lest you drive an angel from your door." (stanza 3, line 4) the moral aimed at the reader
  • "A fine and subtle spirit dwells / In every little flower," (stanza 1, lines 1–2) flowers carry feeling and memory
  • "And spoke and laughed to those around, / As if I knew no care." (stanza 6, lines 3–4) a cheerful mask before the memory hits
  • "When bluebells seemed like fairy gifts," (stanza 9, line 3) childhood's magic, now past
  • "I had not then mid heartless crowds / To spend a thankless life," (stanza 11, lines 1–2) the cold, unthanked adult world
  • "'Sad wanderer, weep those blissful times / That never may return!'" (stanza 12, lines 1–2) the flower's voice: childhood is lost
  • "In the third-class seat sat the journeying boy," (stanza 1, line 1) the specific, humble setting
  • "Bewrapt past knowing to what he was going," (stanza 1, line 4) internal rhyme; he moves on without knowing
  • "That twinkled gleams of the lamp’s sad beams / Like a living thing." (stanza 2, lines 4–5) the key seems alive; the scene feels sad
  • "Towards a world unknown," (stanza 3, line 2) the boy's journey as life itself
  • "On all at stake, can undertake / This plunge alone?" (stanza 3, lines 4–5) the risk he faces, with nobody beside him
  • "This region of sin that you find you in, / But are not of?" (stanza 4, lines 4–5) his innocence set against the adult world
