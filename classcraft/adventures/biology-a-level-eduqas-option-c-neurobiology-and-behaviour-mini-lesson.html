Mission 1

Mission 1: Your brain has separate parts with separate jobs. So how do they add up to one thinking mind?

Make a guess: Your brain has separate parts with separate jobs. So how do they add up to one thinking mind?

Mission 1

Where the main parts are

  • Cerebrum: the largest part, on top. Two cerebral hemispheres, left and right.
  • Its outer layer, the cortex, is folded grey matter.
  • Cerebellum: at the back, below the cerebrum.
  • Medulla oblongata: in the brain stem, where the brain joins the spinal cord.
  • Hypothalamus: small, at the base of the brain in the centre, just above the pituitary gland.
  • Hippocampus: deep inside each hemisphere (temporal lobe), curved.
Mission 1

What each part does

  • Cerebral hemispheres: conscious thought, voluntary movement, interpreting the senses, language, learning and memory.
  • Cerebellum: coordinates skeletal muscle contraction: smooth, accurate movement; balance and posture.
  • Medulla oblongata: involuntary control of heart rate and breathing.
  • Hypothalamus: homeostasis, e.g. body temperature and water balance; controls the pituitary gland.
  • Hippocampus: forming new memories.
  • No part works alone: neurones link the regions, and one action draws on several, e.g. the cerebrum starts a reach and the cerebellum makes it smooth.
  • Say: "the cerebrum starts a movement; the cerebellum coordinates it" — not: "the cerebellum decides to move".
Quick check
?Which statement describes the role of the cerebellum in the control of body movements?
Mission 1

Hypothalamus: where nerves meet hormones

  • The hypothalamus contains neurosecretory cells: neurones that secrete hormones.
  • Posterior pituitary: hypothalamus neurones make ADH; their axons carry it down; it is released into the blood.
  • Anterior pituitary: hypothalamus secretes releasing hormones into small blood vessels → anterior pituitary secretes its own hormones (e.g. TSH, FSH).
  • The hypothalamus also controls the autonomic nervous system.
  • So one structure turns nerve signals into hormonal signals.
Mission 1

Maps on the cortex

  • Motor cortex (back of the frontal lobe): sends impulses that start voluntary movements.
  • Sensory cortex (front of the parietal lobe): receives touch, pain, temperature and position input.
  • Other sensory areas: visual (occipital lobe), auditory (temporal lobe).
  • Each hemisphere serves the opposite side of the body.
  • Homunculi: body maps drawn in proportion to the cortex devoted to each part.
    • Hands, lips, tongue: huge. Trunk, legs: small.
    • Area depends on complexity of innervation (density of receptors; precision of control), not body size.
  • Evidence: Penfield stimulated the cortex of awake patients during brain surgery and recorded the movement or sensation produced.
Quick check
?On the motor homunculus, the hand is drawn larger than the whole leg. What does this show?
Mission 1

Areas for language

  • In most people, the main language areas are in the left hemisphere.
  • Broca's area (frontal lobe): speech production.
    • Damage: slow, effortful speech in short phrases; understanding largely intact.
    • Evidence: Broca (1861) found damage here, post mortem, in a patient who could say only one syllable.
  • Wernicke's area (temporal lobe): language comprehension.
    • Damage: fluent speech that makes little sense; poor understanding.
    • Evidence: Wernicke (1874), patients with damage at the back of the temporal lobe.
  • Linked by a bundle of nerve fibres: hearing a word and saying it uses both.
Quick check
?After a stroke, a patient understands what is said to her but speaks slowly, with great effort, in short phrases. Which area is most likely damaged?

Mission check

Mission 2

Mission 2: Cover one eye for a few weeks early in life and it may never see properly. Why does timing matter so much?

Make a guess: Cover one eye for a few weeks early in life and it may never see properly. Why does timing matter so much?

Mission 2

From eye to visual cortex

  • Retina → optic nerve → relay in the thalamus → visual cortex (occipital lobe).
  • In the visual cortex, neurones are grouped in columns.
  • Neighbouring columns respond mainly to the left eye or the right eye: ocular dominance columns.
  • At birth, the connections are present but rough.
  • Visual experience refines them: synapses that are used are strengthened and kept; unused ones are lost.
Mission 2

The critical period

  • A critical period: a window early in life when certain experience must happen for normal development.
  • During it, inputs compete for synapses in the cortex. Active inputs win; inactive ones lose their connections.
  • Missing the experience in this window causes lasting change. The same deprivation in an adult has little effect.
  • Human example: a child born with a cloudy lens (cataract) in one eye needs it removed early. If not, that eye stays poor-sighted even after surgery.
  • Critical (or sensitive) periods also exist for some kinds of learning, e.g. language.
Mission 2

Critical periods for language

  • Children pick up their first language with no teaching; the brain is primed for it early.
  • Evidence: Johnson and Newport (1989) tested the English grammar of adults who had moved to the USA from Korea or China.
    • Arrived aged 3–7: scored like native speakers.
    • Later arrival: scores fell with age of arrival.
  • Children who meet any language late (e.g. deaf children first shown sign language late) rarely become fully fluent.
  • The decline is gradual, not a sudden cut-off, so many researchers say sensitive period.
  • Adults can still learn languages, but usually not to the same native level.
Mission 2

Neuroplasticity

  • Neuroplasticity: the brain changes its pathways in response to experience, injury or disease.
  • How: new synapses form; used synapses strengthen; unused ones are pruned; dendrites branch.
  • Environment: London taxi drivers, who learn thousands of streets, had a larger rear hippocampus than non-drivers; the longer they had driven, the larger (Maguire et al. 2000).
  • Injury: after a stroke, undamaged areas can take over some lost functions, helped by rehabilitation.
  • Plasticity is greatest in young brains, but continues through life.
Quick check
?London taxi drivers had a larger rear hippocampus than non-drivers, and the size increased with years spent driving a taxi. Which conclusion is best supported?
Mission 2

Genes build the brain

  • Brain development follows genes being switched on and off in sequence.
  • Expressed genes control where neurones are made, where they move, and which connections they form.
  • Example: FOXP2 codes for a transcription factor (it switches other genes on and off).
    • In one family (the "KE family"), members with one faulty copy have a severe speech and language disorder (Lai et al. 2001).
  • So a change in gene expression during development can change behaviour.
  • Genes and environment act together: experience also changes which genes are expressed.
Mission 2

Early life, gene expression and later risk

  • Epigenetic marks, e.g. methyl groups on DNA, can switch genes off without changing the base sequence.
  • Rats: pups that received little licking and grooming from their mother had more methylation of the glucocorticoid receptor (stress hormone receptor) gene in the hippocampus → fewer receptors → stronger stress responses as adults (Weaver et al. 2004).
  • Cross-fostering: pups born to low-care mothers but raised by high-care mothers did not show this. The care, not the inherited DNA sequence, made the difference.
  • Humans: brain tissue from people with a history of severe childhood adversity showed similar extra methylation of this gene (McGowan et al. 2009).
  • Stronger, longer stress responses are linked to a higher risk of depression and anxiety. Risk, not certainty.
Quick check
?In the rat study on the earlier screen, pups born to low-care mothers but raised by high-care mothers had normal glucocorticoid receptor levels and normal stress responses. What does this show?
Mission check

?Some children born deaf receive a cochlear implant, which stimulates the auditory nerve directly. Children implanted in the first few years of life usually develop spoken language far better than children implanted at age seven or older. Which explanation fits?

Timing matters because the brain wires itself with what it receives, and some wiring happens only once.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Moving costs energy. So what makes it worth an animal's while to move at all?

Make a guess: Moving costs energy. So what makes it worth an animal's while to move at all?

Mission 3

Responding pays

  • Environments change: light, humidity, temperature, food, predators.
  • Organisms that respond to change increase their chance of survival and of reproducing.
  • Locomotion: moving the whole body from place to place. It costs energy.
  • It pays when the move brings a benefit: food, safety, a mate or better conditions.
Mission 3

Taxes and kineses

  • Innate behaviour: inherited; performed correctly the first time; no learning needed.
  • Taxis: a directional movement in response to the direction of a stimulus.
  • Towards the stimulus: positive. Away from it: negative.
  • Woodlice move away from light: negative phototaxis. Euglena swims towards light: positive phototaxis.
  • Kinesis: a non-directional response. The rate of movement, or of turning, changes with the intensity of a stimulus.
Mission 3

How a kinesis gathers woodlice in the damp

  • Dry air: a woodlouse moves fast and turns rarely. It soon leaves the dry area.
  • Damp air: it slows down and turns more often. It stays.
  • Over time, most woodlice end up in the damp, without ever steering towards it.
  • The advantage: woodlice lose water quickly through their exoskeleton. Damp places cut water loss; dark, damp places also hide them from predators.
  • Escape reflex: fast, automatic and innate. A pill woodlouse rolls into a ball when disturbed; an earthworm jerks back into its burrow when touched.
  • Advantage: protection within a fraction of a second, with no learning needed.
Quick check
?Woodlice are placed in a dish that is damp on one half and dry on the other, under even, dim light. On the dry half they walk faster and turn less often; on the damp half they slow down and turn more. After 15 minutes most are on the damp half. Which term describes this response?
Mission 3Practical

Choice chamber

  • Option C: measuring animal responses with choice chambers for woodlice.
  • Damp side: water under the gauze. Dry side: a drying agent, such as silica gel.
  • Cover the chamber so light is even. Add 10 woodlice through the centre; wait 10 minutes; count each side.
  • Repeat with fresh woodlice, swapping the damp and dry sides between runs.
  • Keep constant: light, temperature, number and type of animal, time.
  • A control differs from the experimental set-up only in the variable being tested.
  • Handle woodlice gently with a soft paintbrush; return them to where they were found.
  • Analysis: a χ² test on the counts (next screen).
Mission 3

Worked example: is the preference real?

Illustrative data: 40 woodlice; after 10 minutes, 29 on the damp side and 11 on the dry side.

  • Null hypothesis: no preference. Expected: 20 on each side.
  • χ² = Σ (O − E)²/E
  • Damp: (29 − 20)²/20 = 4.05. Dry: (11 − 20)²/20 = 4.05. χ² = 8.10.
  • Degrees of freedom = number of categories − 1 = 1. Critical value at p = 0.05: 3.84.
  • 8.10 > 3.84: reject the null hypothesis. The difference is significant: unlikely to be due to chance.
  • Say: "the difference is significant, so we reject the null hypothesis" — not: "this proves woodlice prefer damp".

Mission check

?Larvae of a hypothetical beetle lose water quickly in dry air. Forty larvae are placed in a dark choice chamber with a damp side and a dry side. After 15 minutes, 34 are on the damp side and 6 on the dry side. A chi-squared test gives χ² = 19.6; the critical value at p = 0.05 is 3.84. Which conclusion is best supported?

Moving costs energy, but a response that keeps an animal in damp, dark or food-rich places raises its chance of surviving.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why does an animal stop reacting to a harmless touch that keeps coming back?

Make a guess: Why does an animal stop reacting to a harmless touch that keeps coming back?

Mission 4

Innate or learned?

  • Innate behaviour: inherited; much the same in every member of the species; done correctly the first time.
  • Learned behaviour: changes with experience.
  • Learning changes the individual, not its alleles. Offspring must learn the behaviour again.
Mission 4

Habituation

  • Habituation: a reduced response to a repeated stimulus that brings neither harm nor reward.
  • A snail touched between its tentacles pulls them in. Touch it again and again: it pulls them in less, and comes out sooner.
  • It is stimulus-specific: a different stimulus still gives a full response. So it is not tired muscles.
  • Advantage: no time or energy wasted on harmless stimuli; more time to feed, while staying alert to new ones.
  • Humans habituate too: you stop noticing a ticking clock.
  • Say: "a reduced response to a repeated stimulus" — not: "the animal is too tired to respond".
Mission 4Practical

Habituation in snails

  • Let a snail settle on a clean, damp surface until its tentacles are fully out.
  • Touch it between the tentacles with a moist cotton bud. Time how long until the tentacles are fully out again.
  • Repeat the touch each time it re-emerges, 10 or more times. Use several snails.
  • Independent variable: number of times touched. Dependent variable: time to re-emerge.
  • Keep constant: force and place of touch, interval, temperature, light, surface.
  • Handle gently; keep snails damp; return them afterwards.
Mission 4Read the data

Mission 4

Imprinting

  • Imprinting: rapid learning in a short sensitive period soon after birth or hatching.
  • Lorenz: greylag goslings that first saw him, not their mother, followed him.
  • Normally the first moving object is the parent. Young stay close: warmth, protection, food.
  • Hess: mallard ducklings imprinted most strongly about 13–16 hours after hatching.
  • It is learned, but only within a time window, and it is hard to reverse.
Mission 4

Conditioning

  • Classical conditioning: a new stimulus becomes linked to an existing reflex.
  • Pavlov: food → salivation (unconditioned stimulus → unconditioned response).
  • A sound, such as a metronome, came just before food, many times. Then the sound alone → salivation: the conditioned response to a conditioned stimulus.
  • Operant conditioning: a behaviour becomes linked to its consequence.
  • Skinner: a rat presses a lever by chance and food drops in. It presses more often: the behaviour is reinforced. Trial and error.
  • Classical: stimulus linked to stimulus; involuntary response. Operant: behaviour linked to outcome; voluntary behaviour.
Mission 4

Imitation and insight

  • Imitation: copying another individual's behaviour.
  • Koshima Island, Japan, 1953: a young macaque began washing sweet potatoes in water. Over the next years others in her troop copied, younger animals first.
  • Insight: solving a new problem without trial and error, by seeing how the parts fit together.
  • Köhler: chimpanzees stacked boxes, or joined two sticks, to reach bananas hung out of reach.
  • Both are common in primates, whose complex societies reward learning from others.
Quick check
?Each time a puff of air is blown at a rabbit's eye, a tone sounds just before it. The rabbit blinks at each puff. After many pairings, the adult rabbit blinks when it hears the tone alone. Which type of learning is this?
Mission check

?In one field, birds feeding on newly sown seed fly up whenever a gas-powered bird scarer gives a loud bang. It bangs every 10 minutes and never harms them. After ten days, most of the birds keep feeding when it bangs. On day eleven, a recording of a hawk's call is played; every bird flies up at once. Which explanation is best supported?

The snail and the birds both stop reacting to a stimulus that keeps coming and never hurts. Ignoring it saves time and energy; responding to anything new keeps them safe.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Why would an animal spend energy singing, displaying or fighting instead of just feeding?

Make a guess: Why would an animal spend energy singing, displaying or fighting instead of just feeding?

Mission 5

Signals change behaviour

  • A signal changes the behaviour of another individual.
  • Visual: a peacock's train. Audible: bird song. Tactile: grooming.
  • Chemical: a female moth's scent. Electrical: pulses from weakly electric fish.
  • Triggers can be internal (readiness to breed) or external (a predator approaching).
  • Animals use signals to show dominance, find food, hold territory and win mates.
  • Say: "the signal spreads because it increases survival or reproduction" — not: "the animal signals because it wants to help".
Mission 5

Courtship: a chain of signals

  • Courtship: behaviour before mating; usually specific to one species.
  • Three-spined stickleback (Tinbergen):
  • The male, with a red belly, swims a zigzag dance. A female swollen with eggs swims towards him, head up.
  • He leads her to his nest; she enters.
  • He nudges the base of her tail; she lays her eggs. He follows and fertilises them.
  • Each action is the stimulus for the partner's next action. If one step fails, the chain stops.
Mission 5

Why court?

  • Species recognition: only partners of the same species give and answer the right signals. No gametes wasted on matings that cannot give fertile offspring.
  • Recognise a partner of the opposite sex.
  • Show that the partner is sexually mature and ready: mating is synchronised. Vital when fertilisation is external.
  • Choose a high-quality mate.
Quick check
?Female three-spined sticklebacks were given a choice of males. They chose males with a brighter red belly more often. Males infected with parasites had duller red bellies. Under green light, which hides the red, the preference disappeared. Which conclusion is best supported?
Mission 5

Holding a territory

  • Territory: an area an animal defends, usually against members of its own species.
  • It holds resources: food, nest sites, a place to attract mates.
  • Defended mostly by signals: song, scent marks, displays. Threats are far commoner than fights.
  • Costs: energy, time, risk of injury. It pays when the resources are worth more.
  • Great tits: when territory owners were removed and their song played from loudspeakers, new males took longer to move in (Krebs 1977).
  • Males without territories often fail to breed: territory raises reproductive success.
Mission 5

Sexual selection

  • Sexual selection: selection for traits that increase mating success.
  • Competition between males: red deer stags roar and fight; winners hold harems and father more calves.
  • Choice by females: females prefer males with exaggerated traits, such as long tails or elaborate song.
  • These traits can cost survival, but they spread if they add more offspring than they cost.
  • So territorial and courtship behaviours evolve by sexual selection.
Mission 5Read the data

Long tails win mates

Andersson (1982), long-tailed widowbirds, Kenya. Males have tails about half a metre long.

  • Four groups of 9 territory-holding males:
  • (1) tails shortened; (2) tails cut and glued back at the same length; (3) tails untouched; (4) tails lengthened with the pieces cut from group 1.
  • Before the change: the groups had similar numbers of nests (females) on their territories.
  • After: males with lengthened tails gained significantly more new nests than males in the other groups.
  • Conclusion: females choose males with longer tails. That is sexual selection by female choice.
Quick check
?In Andersson's experiment, why was a group included whose tails were cut and glued back at the same length?
Mission 5

Living in groups

  • Advantages: more eyes to spot predators; each animal less likely to be the one caught; group defence.
  • Advantages: hunting together catches bigger prey; shared care of young; information about food.
  • Disadvantages: competition for food and mates; faster spread of disease and parasites.
  • Disadvantages: large groups are easier for predators to find.

Mission 5

Social insects

  • Honeybees live in a caste system:
  • Queen: the only fertile female; lays the eggs. Workers: sterile females; build comb, feed larvae, guard, forage. Drones: males; mate with queens.
  • Caste is set by diet: a larva fed royal jelly throughout becomes a queen.
  • Communication is innate. The waggle dance: the angle of the waggle run from vertical gives the direction of food relative to the sun; the length of the waggle run gives its distance.
  • Chemical signals (pheromones) from the queen help hold the colony together.
Mission 5

Dominance hierarchies

  • Dominance hierarchy: a ranking within a group. Higher rank: first access to food, mates and shelter.
  • Hens: the "pecking order" (Schjelderup-Ebbe, 1922). Each hen pecks those below her.
  • It needs individual recognition and learning: each animal remembers whom it has beaten and lost to.
  • Advantage: once ranks are settled, fights are rarer. Fewer injuries; less energy spent.
  • Primates, including humans, live in complex societies: long-term relationships, alliances, grooming, learning from others.
Quick check
?Hens that have never met are put together in a new flock. Pecking is frequent for the first few days, then falls as each hen settles into a rank. Which explanation is best supported?
Mission check

?Males of two related fiddler crab species live on the same mudflat. Each male waves one enlarged claw in a rhythm typical of his own species. Waving uses energy and draws the attention of birds that eat crabs. Females approach only males waving in their own species' rhythm. Which explanation best accounts for waving persisting?

Singing, displaying and fighting cost energy and risk, but they win mates, territory or rank. An individual that leaves more offspring passes on the alleles for the behaviour.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Cerebral hemispheres (conscious thought, voluntary movement, memory), cerebellum (coordination, balance), medulla (heart rate, breathing), hypothalamus (homeostasis, pituitary).
  • Cortex maps (homunculi) reflect complexity of innervation; Broca's area for speech, Wernicke's for comprehension.
  • CT and MRI show structure; fMRI and PET show activity.

Mission 2

  • During a critical period, active inputs keep and strengthen their synapses in the cortex and inactive ones lose them; the same deprivation later has little effect.
  • Language has a sensitive period; the brain stays plastic through life; genes and early experience (via methylation) shape brain development and later risk.

Mission 3

  • Responses that keep an animal in favourable conditions increase its chance of survival: a taxis is directional; a kinesis changes rate, not direction.
  • A choice chamber plus a chi-squared test shows whether a preference is significant.

Mission 4

  • Habituation: a learned, stimulus-specific drop in response to a repeated, harmless stimulus; it saves time and energy.
  • Other learning: imprinting (sensitive period), classical and operant conditioning, imitation and insight.

Mission 5

  • Courtship lets animals recognise their own species and a ready, high-quality mate: costly signals persist because they raise reproductive success.
  • Territories, dominance hierarchies, social insect castes and sexual selection all come back to who leaves more offspring.
