Mission 1

Why does the novel jump sixteen years in the middle?

Make a guess: Why does the novel jump sixteen years in the middle?

Two parts, sixteen years apart

PartChaptersTimeMain events
Part One1–15"the early years of this century", 15 years after Silas reached RaveloeLantern Yard backstory (ch 1); the robbery (ch 4–5); a child on the hearth (ch 12)

  • The novel was published in 1861 but set about half a century earlier, before the factory age reached villages like Raveloe.
  • Part One closes with Eppie growing up and Godfrey courting Nancy (ch 14–15); Part Two opens on "a bright autumn Sunday".

Silas Marner · structure and the two plotsScreen 6

Seeds and harvest

  • The gap lets consequences grow: "the seed brings forth a crop after its kind" (ch 9).
  • Parallels across the halves: Silas loses gold and gains a child; Godfrey hides a child and later has none at home.
  • Secrets buried in Part One surface in Part Two, when the drained Stone-pit gives up what it hid (ch 18).
  • Eliot said the story first came to her like a legend; many readers call it a moral fable.

Part One plants the choices: Silas keeps the child, Godfrey stays silent. The sixteen-year gap lets those choices grow, so Part Two can show the harvest, a loving daughter for Silas and a childless home for Godfrey, when the buried past comes to light.

Mission 2

What turns a man who loved gold into a man who loves people?

Make a guess: What turns a man who loved gold into a man who loves people?

Silas Marner · SilasScreen 1

Before Raveloe: trust, then betrayal

  • In Lantern Yard, a chapel community in a northern town, Silas is "a young man of exemplary life and ardent faith" (ch 1).
  • His friend William Dane frames him for stealing church money; the congregation draws lots, which declare him guilty. Sarah, his fiancée, marries William.
  • Silas cries that there is "no just God that governs the earth righteously" (ch 1). He loses faith in God and in people at once.

Silas Marner · SilasScreen 4

Signs that he can still love

  • The narrator stops us dismissing him: "few men could be more harmless than poor Marner" (ch 5).
  • He grieves over his broken brown pot and props the pieces up "for a memorial" (ch 2): "the sap of affection was not all gone".
  • He helps Sally Oates with foxglove, his mother's remedy (ch 2), out of pity.
  • His love of gold grows from loss, not wickedness: Eliot presents a damaged man, not a villain.

Mission 3

Godfrey is Eppie's real father. Why does she turn him down?

Make a guess: Godfrey is Eppie's real father. Why does she turn him down?

Silas Marner · Eppie and GodfreyScreen 1

Godfrey's secret

  • Godfrey, the Squire's eldest son, is "good-natured" but weak: the narrator names his "natural irresolution and moral cowardice" (ch 3).
  • He has secretly married Molly Farren, an opium addict in Batherley. His brother Dunstan uses the secret to blackmail him.
  • Godfrey loves Nancy Lammeter and hopes luck will save him: "Favourable Chance" is his god (ch 9).
Silas Marner · Eppie and GodfreyScreen 2

New Year's Eve: the choice not to speak

  • Molly walks to Raveloe to expose him and dies in the snow; her child toddles to Silas's open door (ch 12).
  • When Silas brings the child to the dance, Godfrey's "one terror" is that the woman might not be dead (ch 13).
  • He gives Silas half a guinea and stays silent: "he would do everything but own it" (ch 13).
  • Eliot presents him as culpable but not monstrous: his conscience keeps him "for ever uneasy".

Silas Marner · Eppie and GodfreyScreen 5

Eppie's choice

  • Godfrey offers to "make a lady of her", then claims her as "my own child" (ch 19).
  • Eppie: "I can’t feel as I’ve got any father but one" (ch 19). She will marry Aaron Winthrop.
  • Godfrey accepts it: "there’s debts we can’t pay like money debts" (ch 20).
  • Many readers see the novel ranking nurture above blood; others note Eliot gives Godfrey real remorse, so he is not simply punished.

Eppie refuses because Silas has been her father in everything but blood, and because she belongs to the working life she was raised in. Godfrey's silence in Part One means that, in Part Two, the blessing he turned away belongs to someone else.

Mission 4

Silas loses his faith in a chapel. Where does he find it again?

Make a guess: Silas loses his faith in a chapel. Where does he find it again?

Silas Marner · community and religionScreen 1

Lantern Yard: a Dissenting chapel

  • Dissenters (Nonconformists) were Protestants who worshipped outside the Church of England. By 1851 about half of England's churchgoers attended Nonconformist services.
  • Lantern Yard is a "narrow religious sect" in a manufacturing town, where even "the poorest layman" has a voice (ch 1).
  • Its members seek signs of God's will: Silas's trance, William's dream of "calling and election sure" (ch 1), and drawing lots, which Silas says is found "in the Bible" (ch 16).

  • Old superstitions linger: Mr. Macey thinks a man's soul can go "out and in, like a bird" (ch 1).
  • Dolly pricks "I. H. S." on her lard-cakes, though she can't read the letters (ch 10).
Silas Marner · community and religionScreen 5

Dolly's faith: feeling, not doctrine

  • Dolly speaks of God as "Them above" (ch 16, 21) or "Them as are above" (ch 10), to avoid sounding too familiar.
  • She can't explain the lots, but trusts that God has "a deal tenderer heart" than she has: "all as we’ve got to do is to trusten" (ch 16).
  • Silas comes to agree: "there’s good i’ this world", and the child "was sent" to him (ch 16).
  • Eliot presents faith as something learned through human kindness and community.
Silas Marner · community and religionScreen 6

Eliot and her own loss of faith

  • George Eliot was Mary Ann Evans (1819–1880). Devout and Evangelical as a schoolgirl, in 1842 she told her father she could no longer go to church.
  • She translated Strauss's Life of Jesus (1846), which treated the Gospels as myth, and Feuerbach's The Essence of Christianity (1854).
  • Many critics link the novel's respect for simple, loving belief, rather than doctrine, to this history.
  • Context isn't marked on your paper: use it only to sharpen a point.

Mission 5

Silas mistakes a child's curls for gold. Why make them alike?

Make a guess: Silas mistakes a child's curls for gold. Why make them alike?

Silas Marner · gold and loveScreen 1

What the gold means

  • Silas is first paid "five bright guineas" in Raveloe; he likes "their bright faces, which were all his own" (ch 2).
  • He counts the coins into squares each night and thinks of future guineas "as if they had been unborn children" (ch 2).
  • The gold is a symbol: it stands for the love and purpose Silas has lost. It asks nothing of him and gives nothing back.

?Silas mistakes a child's curls for gold. Why make them alike?

The gold stood in for all the love Silas had lost: it needed nothing and gave nothing. By making Eppie's curls look like gold, Eliot shows a living child taking the coins' place, and the rest of the novel shows that love forcing his thoughts "onward" into the world.

Mission 6

Godfrey keeps his secret and his status. Does he get away with it?

Make a guess: Godfrey keeps his secret and his status. Does he get away with it?

Silas Marner · class and moralityScreen 1

Squires and the landed interest

  • A squire was a country landowner of standing, often lord of the manor: below the nobility, far above yeomen farmers.
  • Squire Cass is "The greatest man in Raveloe" and has tenants of his own (ch 3).
  • The novel is set during the Napoleonic Wars, "that glorious war-time" of high prices for "the landed interest" (ch 3). After peace in 1815, the Corn Laws protected landowners from cheap foreign grain.
  • Eliot shows the Casses living idly on that wealth: "bad husbandry", drink and debt.

Silas Marner · class and moralityScreen 5

Choices and consequences

  • Dunstan steals and dies the same night: his skeleton lies in the Stone-pit with the gold for sixteen years (ch 18).
  • Godfrey worships "Favourable Chance" (ch 9). Later he has no living child, and Eppie refuses him: "I shall pass for childless now against my wish" (ch 20).
  • Silas, poor and honest, ends with a daughter and a community.
  • Interpretations differ: some readers think Godfrey gets off lightly, keeping his land, wife and good name; others see his private loss as the novel's real punishment.

Silas Marner · industrial change and the villageScreen 3

Hand weavers and the machine age

  • The weavers were "emigrants from the town into the country" (ch 1), working at handlooms at home.
  • Machines took over spinning from the 1760s; by the 1820s factory power looms had made handloom weavers' wages collapse.
  • In Part Two Raveloe's weaving "was going down too—for there was less and less flax spun" (ch 16).
  • Eliot wrote in 1861, looking back about sixty years to a world already gone.
  • Context isn't marked on your paper.
Silas Marner · industrial change and the villageScreen 4

Raveloe: a village out of time

  • Raveloe lies "aloof from the currents of industrial energy and Puritan earnestness" (ch 3).
  • It is "an hour’s journey on horseback from any turnpike", "never reached by the vibrations of the coach-horn, or of public opinion" (ch 1).
  • Change still arrives quietly: Godfrey drains Mr. Osgood's fields, and the Stone-pit empties (ch 16, 18).
  • The narrator is fond but not blind: Raveloe is also idle, superstitious and slow to trust strangers.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Why does Eliot replace Silas's old chapel with a factory?

Lantern Yard, its chapel and its answers have been "swep’ away" by industry, so Silas can never clear his name there. The scene turns him back to Raveloe: "I’ve no home but this now". Eliot sets a changing industrial England against a village where trust and love have been rebuilt.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Part One (ch 1–15) and Part Two (ch 16–21, Conclusion) are sixteen years apart; the novel is set early in the century and published in 1861.
  • Silas's plot and the Cass plot alternate and cross at turning points (ch 4, ch 13, ch 18–19).
  • Part (a) is about language in the extract; part (b) asks about the whole novel, so know the order of events.
  • In Lantern Yard Silas is trusting and devout; William Dane's betrayal and the lots destroy his faith in God and people (ch 1).
  • In Raveloe he shrinks into weaving and hoarding, but the narrator shows the "sap of affection" is still there (ch 2).
  • Loss of the gold, then Eppie, change him gradually; by ch 21 he can "trusten".
  • Godfrey's secret marriage and his silence on New Year's Eve (ch 13) shape the whole of Part Two.
  • In ch 19 Godfrey's claim of blood meets Silas's and Eppie's claim of love, and love wins.
  • Godfrey accepts that some debts can't be paid with money (ch 20); his punishment is a childless home.
  • For part (b), track Godfrey or Eppie across both halves of the novel, not just ch 19.
  • Lantern Yard is a strict Dissenting chapel; its drawing of lots wrongly declares Silas guilty and destroys his faith (ch 1).
  • Raveloe's religion is customary, comfortable and mixed with superstition; Dolly's simple trust helps Silas most (ch 10, 16).
  • Silas regains trust through Eppie and community, not through doctrine (ch 16, 21).
  • The gold is a symbol of Silas's starved love: he treats coins like companions or "unborn children" (ch 2).
  • In ch 12 gold becomes "soft warm curls"; in ch 14 the narrator contrasts the gold and Eppie point by point.
  • When the gold returns (ch 18–19) it "takes no hold" of him; Godfrey learns money can't pay every debt (ch 20).
  • Part (a) rewards close language analysis like screen 4 and screen 7: quote short, name the method, say the effect.
  • Squire Cass leads a rank-conscious village; the war-time "landed interest" lets the Casses live idly (ch 3).
  • Godfrey assumes "a higher station" gives him a right to Eppie (ch 17); Eppie chooses the "working-folks" (ch 19).
  • Eliot links wrongdoing to consequences: Dunstan dies; Godfrey's punishment is private (ch 18, 20).
  • For part (b), tracing Godfrey across both halves is a strong way to answer a question on class or morality.
  • The novel opens with linen-weavers as outsiders "emigrants from the town into the country" (ch 1); Raveloe lies "aloof" from industry (ch 3).
  • Context: power looms and factories replaced handloom weavers in the decades after 1800; Eliot wrote in 1861 about a vanished world.
  • In ch 21 Lantern Yard has become a factory, so Silas's home and faith are now in Raveloe.
