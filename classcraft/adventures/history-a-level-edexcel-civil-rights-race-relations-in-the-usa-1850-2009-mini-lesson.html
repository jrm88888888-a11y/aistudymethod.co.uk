Civil Rights and Race Relations in the USA 1850-2009

The option follows the long, uneven struggle for Black civil rights: from slavery and Reconstruction, through the era of Jim Crow segregation, to the civil rights movement and beyond, ending with the election of the first African American president.

1850-1877 - slavery and Reconstruction

Emancipation and its limits

The mid-century crisis over slavery led to the Civil War (1861-65) and, in its aftermath, a brief revolution in Black rights:

  • Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation (1863) declared enslaved people in the rebel states free; the 13th Amendment (1865) abolished slavery throughout the USA.
  • During Reconstruction (1865-77), the 14th Amendment (1868) guaranteed citizenship and equal protection, and the 15th Amendment (1870) barred denial of the vote on grounds of race. Black men voted and held office across the South.
  • But white 'Redemption', terror by the Ku Klux Klan, and the Compromise of 1877 ended Reconstruction, withdrawing federal troops and abandoning Black southerners to renewed white supremacy.

Continuity to weigh: the amendments promised equality; the reality after 1877 denied it. That gap between formal rights and lived experience runs through the whole option.

1877-1945 - the Jim Crow era

Segregation and the 'nadir'

After Reconstruction the South built a system of legal segregation and disenfranchisement:

  • Jim Crow laws segregated schools, transport and public life. In Plessy v Ferguson (1896) the Supreme Court upheld segregation under the doctrine of 'separate but equal' - facilities that were separate but almost never equal.
  • Black southerners were stripped of the vote by literacy tests, poll taxes and grandfather clauses, and terrorised by lynching.
  • Responses divided: Booker T. Washington urged patient self-improvement, while W. E. B. Du Bois demanded agitation for full rights and helped found the NAACP (1909). The Great Migration carried millions north in search of work and freedom.

1945-1968 - the civil rights movement

From Brown to the Voting Rights Act

After 1945 a mass movement dismantled legal segregation:

  • In Brown v Board of Education (1954) the Supreme Court ruled segregated schooling unconstitutional, overturning Plessy.
  • The Montgomery Bus Boycott (1955-56), sparked by Rosa Parks, brought Martin Luther King Jr to prominence and showed the power of non-violent mass protest. Little Rock (1957), the sit-ins (1960) and the Freedom Rides (1961) followed.
  • The March on Washington (1963), where King gave his 'I Have a Dream' speech, and the Birmingham and Selma campaigns pushed Congress to act: the Civil Rights Act (1964) outlawed segregation and discrimination, and the Voting Rights Act (1965) restored the ballot.
  • Yet many, especially in the North, saw little change. A more radical Black Power movement (Malcolm X, the Black Panthers from 1966) challenged King's approach, and King was assassinated in 1968.

1968-2009 - after the movement

Progress, backlash and a milestone

The decades after 1968 saw real advance alongside stubborn inequality:

  • Affirmative action and school busing aimed to tackle entrenched disadvantage, but provoked fierce 'backlash' and legal challenge.
  • Economic inequality, segregated housing and tension with the police persisted - exposed by episodes such as the Los Angeles riots of 1992 after the Rodney King verdict.
  • The option ends on a landmark: the election of Barack Obama in 2008 as the first African American president - a moment many read as the fruit of the long struggle, even as it prompted debate about how much racial inequality remained.

Interpretations - the historians' debate

Two great debates: Reconstruction and the movement

This option carries two of the richest debates in American history, and they power your essays:

Reconstruction: the early-twentieth-century Dunning School (following William A. Dunning) condemned Reconstruction as a corrupt failure of misrule - a view that shaped popular culture for decades. Eric Foner, in Reconstruction: America's Unfinished Revolution (1988), decisively overturned it, recasting Reconstruction as a genuine, if incomplete, democratic experiment.

The movement: a 'great man' narrative centres change on the leadership of Martin Luther King Jr. Against it, historians of the grassroots such as Charles Payne and John Dittmer stress the 'local people' - the organisers, churches and ordinary activists who built the movement from below.

Exam skill: when a source leans on great leaders or on federal law, test it against the evidence of grassroots organising and local struggle - and reach a supported judgement.

Exam technique - Paper 3

How Paper 3 is assessed

Edexcel Paper 3 (Option 39.1): 2 hours 15 minutes, 60 marks, 30% of the A-level. It is 'themes in breadth with aspects in depth'.

  • Section A (AO2): analyse and evaluate a primary source - judge its value for an enquiry using provenance, content and context.
  • Section B (AO1): an essay on a depth aspect.
  • Section C (AO1): essays on the breadth themes across 1850-2009.

Note: Section A rewards judging a source's value (AO2), not spotting 'bias'. The historians' debates above enrich your Section B and C essays.
