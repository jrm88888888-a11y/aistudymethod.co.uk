Mission 1

Mission 1: Antimatter is real. How could you tell an antiparticle from its ordinary twin?

Make a guess: Antimatter is real. How could you tell an antiparticle from its ordinary twin?

Mission 1

Antimatter

  • Every particle has an antiparticle: the same mass, the opposite charge.
  • electron e⁻ ↔ positron e⁺ · proton p ↔ antiproton p̄ · neutron n ↔ antineutron n̄ · neutrino ν ↔ antineutrino ν̄.
  • Antiparticles are real: positrons come out of β⁺ decay, and hospitals use them in PET scans.
  • A particle and its antiparticle can annihilate: their mass becomes photons.
Quick check
?Predict first. The table shows the charge and mass of four particles that can be emitted when nuclei decay. One entry in the table is incorrect. Which row contains the incorrect entry?
Mission 1

Particles from nuclear decay

  • α particle (α, ⁴2He): 2 protons + 2 neutrons, a helium-4 nucleus. Charge +2e, mass ≈ 4 u.
  • Electron (e⁻, β⁻): charge −e, mass ≈ 0.00055 u.
  • Positron (e⁺, β⁺): charge +e, the same mass as the electron.
  • Neutrino (ν) and antineutrino (ν̄): no charge, negligible mass.
Mission 1

Reading off an antiparticle

  • Same: mass, so the same rest energy.
  • Opposite: charge (and other conserved quantities you will meet later).
  • Rest energies: electron and positron 0.511 MeV; proton and antiproton 938.3 MeV; neutron and antineutron 939.6 MeV.
  • Symbol: a bar over the particle's symbol: p̄, n̄, ν̄. Exception: e⁺ for the positron.
  • The antineutron is neutral, like the neutron, but it is not the same particle.
Quick check
?A proton has charge +e and rest energy 938.3 MeV. Which describes the antiproton?
Mission check

?A beam of electrons enters the region between two oppositely charged parallel plates with velocity v, as shown, and follows track 3. A beam of positrons then enters with the same velocity. The figure shows four possible tracks, numbered 1 to 4 (not drawn to scale). Which track do the positrons follow?

An antiparticle has the same mass as its particle but the opposite charge. In a field it bends by the same amount, the opposite way: a mirror-image track.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Protons, electrons and neutrinos: what decides which family a particle belongs to?

Make a guess: Protons, electrons and neutrinos: what decides which family a particle belongs to?

Mission 2

Fundamental or composite?

  • Fundamental particle: no internal structure, as far as any experiment shows.
  • Fundamental: quarks and leptons.
  • Composite: made of smaller particles. Protons and neutrons are made of quarks.
  • Quarks are never found alone: they are always bound inside composite particles (quark confinement).
Mission 2

The particle families

  • Hadrons: made of quarks; feel the strong interaction.
  • Baryons: three quarks (proton, neutron). Antibaryons: three antiquarks (antiproton, antineutron).
  • Mesons: a quark and an antiquark, e.g. pions (π).
  • Kaons (K) are mesons too.
  • Leptons: fundamental; do not feel the strong interaction. Electron, neutrino.
Quick check
?A particle is made of an up quark and a down antiquark. How is it classified?
Mission 2

Leptons

  • Leptons are fundamental and feel no strong interaction.
  • Know four, plus antiparticles: electron e⁻, muon μ⁻, electron neutrino νₑ, muon neutrino νμ.
  • The muon is like a heavy electron (rest energy 105.7 MeV, about 207 times the electron's). It is unstable and decays into an electron (plus two neutrinos).
Quick check
?A particle found in 1975 has charge −e and about twice the mass of a proton. It shows no internal structure, decays in under a picosecond and never feels the strong interaction. Which family is it in?
Mission 2

Baryon number

  • Baryon number B: baryons +1; antibaryons −1; mesons and leptons 0.
  • Baryon number is conserved in every interaction.
  • The proton is the lightest baryon and the only stable one.
  • Every other baryon eventually decays into a proton. A free neutron: on average about 15 minutes.
Quick check
?Two protons collide: p + p → p + p + p + X. Charge and baryon number are both conserved. What is X?
Mission 2

How particle physics is done

  • Modern experiments need thousands of scientists and engineers, from many countries.
  • New results are validated by independent teams: two separate detectors at CERN confirmed the Higgs boson in 2012.
Mission check

?A beam from an accelerator contains antineutrons, negative pions (π⁻), positrons and protons. Which of these particles never feel the strong interaction?

Family is decided by what a particle is made of, not by mass, charge or lifetime, and an antiparticle stays in its partner's family. Quarks or antiquarks inside: a hadron, which feels the strong interaction. Fundamental: a lepton, which does not.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Every particle can be pushed and pulled by four kinds of force. How do you know which ones it feels?

Make a guess: Every particle can be pushed and pulled by four kinds of force. How do you know which ones it feels?

Mission 3

Four fundamental interactions

  • Strong: acts on quarks, so on all hadrons. Range about 10⁻¹⁵ m, roughly a nucleon's diameter. Strongest.
  • Electromagnetic: acts on charged particles. Infinite range.
  • Weak: acts on all quarks and all leptons. Range about 10⁻¹⁸ m.
  • Gravitational: acts on all mass. Infinite range. By far the weakest.
  • Strength order: strong > electromagnetic > weak > gravitational.
Mission 3

Who feels what

  • Ask two questions: made of quarks? Charged?
  • Hadrons (made of quarks): strong and weak, plus gravity. Electromagnetic too if charged (neutral hadrons only slightly, through their charged quarks).
  • Leptons: weak, never strong, plus gravity. Electromagnetic too if charged.
  • Electron: gravity, electromagnetic, weak.
  • Neutrino: weak and gravity only. That is why neutrinos pass through the Earth.
Quick check
?Which of these particles never feels the strong interaction?
Mission 3

Forces by exchange

  • Particles exert forces by swapping exchange particles.
  • Picture two skaters throwing a heavy ball back and forth: they push apart. (The picture cannot show attraction.)
  • Electromagnetic: the virtual photon.
  • Weak: W⁺ and W⁻.
  • Strong force between nucleons: pions (π⁺, π⁻, π⁰).
Quick check
?In a hydrogen atom, the electron is attracted to the proton. Which particle is exchanged between them to produce this force?
Mission 3

Drawing an exchange

  • A simple interaction diagram: incoming particles below, outgoing above.
  • Two electrons repel: each line comes in, a virtual photon (wavy line) passes between them, each line goes out.
  • Charge is conserved at every junction (vertex).
  • The diagram shows which particles go in and out and what is exchanged. It is not a map of the paths.
Mission check

?Which interactions does a negative pion, π⁻ (quark content dū), experience?

Two questions decide it. Made of quarks? Then it feels the strong interaction. Charged? Then it feels the electromagnetic one. All quarks and leptons feel the weak interaction and gravity.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Protons and neutrons are built from the same two kinds of quark. Why is only one of them charged?

Make a guess: Protons and neutrons are built from the same two kinds of quark. Why is only one of them charged?

Mission 4

The quarks

  • Up (u): charge +⅔e, baryon number +⅓.
  • Down (d): charge −⅓e, baryon number +⅓.
  • Strange (s): charge −⅓e, baryon number +⅓.
  • Strangeness S: u and d: 0; s: −1.
  • Antiquarks (ū, d̄, s̄): every number reversed. ū: −⅔e, d̄: +⅓e, baryon number −⅓.
  • s̄: strangeness +1.
Mission 4

Building baryons

  • Three quarks. Add the charges.
  • Proton uud: ⅔ + ⅔ − ⅓ = +1.
  • Neutron udd: ⅔ − ⅓ − ⅓ = 0.
  • Antiproton ūūd̄: −1. Antineutron ūd̄d̄: 0.
  • Baryon number: 3 × ⅓ = +1 (antibaryons: −1).
Quick check
?An antibaryon is made only of up and down antiquarks and has a charge of −2e. What is its quark content?
Mission 4

Building mesons

  • A quark and an antiquark.
  • Baryon number: ⅓ − ⅓ = 0.
  • π⁺ = ud̄: ⅔ + ⅓ = +1.
  • π⁻ = dū: −⅓ − ⅔ = −1. The π⁻ is the antiparticle of the π⁺.
  • π⁰ = uū or dd̄: 0.
  • Kaons contain a strange quark or antiquark: K⁺ = us̄; K⁻ = sū; K⁰ = ds̄; K̄⁰ = sd̄.
Quick check
?A meson is built from up and down quarks and antiquarks only. Which charge can it not have?
Mission 4

Strange particles

  • Strange particles contain an s or s̄ quark, e.g. kaons.
  • Produced by the strong interaction: fast (about 10⁻²³ s), and, from non-strange particles, always in pairs (one with s, one with s̄).
  • Decay by the weak interaction: slowly (about 10⁻¹⁰ to 10⁻⁸ s).
  • Strangeness is conserved in strong interactions; it can change in weak ones.
  • Kaons decay into pions, e.g. K⁺ → π⁺ + π⁰.
Quick check
?Two baryons have similar masses. The Δ⁰ (udd) decays into a proton and a pion in about 10⁻²³ s. The Λ⁰ (uds) decays into the same two particles but lasts about 10⁻¹⁰ s. Why does the Λ⁰ last so much longer?
Mission check

?Quarks combine only in certain ways. Which of these combinations can not exist as a particle?

Quark charges are fractions: up +⅔, down −⅓. A proton (uud) totals +1; a neutron (udd) totals 0.

Mission 5

Mission 5: A neutron left on its own soon decays. What changes inside it when it does?

Make a guess: A neutron left on its own soon decays. What changes inside it when it does?

Mission 5

The weak interaction changes quarks

  • Quarks come in types, called flavours, such as up and down.
  • Only the weak interaction can change a quark's flavour.
  • Only the weak interaction involves neutrinos.
  • So β decay is a weak interaction: a quark changes flavour and a neutrino or antineutrino is emitted.
  • The weak interaction is carried by W⁺ and W⁻ bosons.
Mission 5

β⁻ decay at quark level

  • In a nucleus, or on its own: a neutron becomes a proton.
  • n → p + e⁻ + ν̄ₑ
  • udd → uud: one down quark becomes an up quark.
  • d → u + e⁻ + ν̄ₑ
  • Charge check: −⅓ = +⅔ + (−1) + 0 ✓
  • A free neutron decays the same way (on average after about 15 minutes).
Mission 5

β⁺ decay at quark level

  • Only inside a nucleus: a proton becomes a neutron.
  • p → n + e⁺ + νₑ
  • uud → udd: one up quark becomes a down quark.
  • u → d + e⁺ + νₑ
  • Charge check: +⅔ = −⅓ + (+1) + 0 ✓
  • A free proton cannot do this: the products have more mass. The nucleus supplies the energy.
Quick check
?A carbon-11 nucleus (6 protons, 5 neutrons) holds 17 up quarks and 16 down quarks. It decays by β⁺ emission to boron-11. How many up and down quarks does the boron-11 nucleus hold?
Mission 5

Why a neutrino?

  • In β⁻ decay, electrons come out with a continuous range of kinetic energies, up to a maximum.
  • If only the electron and the new nucleus were produced, every electron would have the same energy.
  • The missing energy is not lost: a third, undetected particle carries it.
  • So the neutrino was hypothesised (Pauli, 1930): neutral, almost no mass, sharing the energy. It was detected in 1956.
Quick check
?Every carbon-14 β⁻ decay releases about 156 keV. A detector measures one emitted electron with 50 keV of kinetic energy. What is the best account of the other 106 keV or so?
Quick check
?The weak interaction is carried by W⁺ and W⁻. In electron capture, p + e⁻ → n + νₑ, a W boson leaves the proton (which becomes a neutron) and reaches the electron (which becomes a neutrino). Charge is conserved at each point. Which boson is it?
Mission 5

Weak interaction diagrams

  • β⁻: n → p by emitting a W⁻; the W⁻ → e⁻ + ν̄ₑ.
  • β⁺: p → n by emitting a W⁺; the W⁺ → e⁺ + νₑ.
  • Electron capture: p + e⁻ → n + νₑ. W⁺ passes from the proton to the electron.
  • Electron–proton collision: p + e⁻ → n + νₑ. W⁻ passes from the electron to the proton.
  • At every vertex: charge conserved. Check the W's sign this way.
Mission check

?In the Sun, the first step of fusion joins two protons into a deuterium nucleus (one proton and one neutron), and a positron and a neutrino are emitted. What happens at quark level, and which interaction is responsible?

One proton becomes a neutron (uud → udd) by the weak interaction, the same change as in β⁺ decay. The neutrino is the giveaway. This slow weak step is why the Sun burns for billions of years.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Every particle has an antiparticle with the same mass (and rest energy) and the opposite charge.
  • Pairs to know: e⁻/e⁺, p/p̄, n/n̄, ν/ν̄; a bar marks the antiparticle.

Mission 2

  • Hadrons (made of quarks; feel the strong interaction): baryons (qqq), antibaryons (q̄q̄q̄), mesons (qq̄). Leptons: fundamental, no strong interaction.
  • Leptons to know: e, μ, νₑ, νμ and antiparticles; the muon decays into an electron.
  • Baryon number (+1, −1, 0) is always conserved; the proton is the only stable baryon.

Mission 3

  • Four interactions: strong (quarks, hadrons; ~10⁻¹⁵ m), electromagnetic (charges; infinite), weak (all quarks and leptons; ~10⁻¹⁸ m), gravitational (all mass; infinite).
  • Hadrons feel strong and weak; leptons feel weak but never strong.
  • Forces come from exchange particles: virtual photon (electromagnetic), W± (weak).
  • Pions between nucleons (strong).

Mission 4

  • u: +⅔e; d: −⅓e; each quark has baryon number +⅓; antiquarks have everything reversed.
  • Proton uud (+1), neutron udd (0); antiproton ūūd̄, antineutron ūd̄d̄; π⁺ ud̄, π⁻ dū.
  • s: −⅓e, baryon number +⅓.
  • Strangeness: s −1, s̄ +1. Kaons: K⁺ us̄, K⁻ sū, K⁰ ds̄.
  • Strange particles: made in pairs by the strong interaction, decay by the weak interaction; kaons decay into pions.

Mission 5

  • Only the weak interaction changes quark flavour or involves neutrinos.
  • β⁻: d → u + e⁻ + ν̄ₑ (a neutron becomes a proton). β⁺: u → d + e⁺ + νₑ (a proton becomes a neutron, only in a nucleus).
  • The neutrino was hypothesised because β⁻ electrons have a continuous range of energies: energy conservation.
  • Exchange particles: W⁻ in β⁻ and electron–proton collisions; W⁺ in β⁺ and electron capture. Check charge at every vertex.
