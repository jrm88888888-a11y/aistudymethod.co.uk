Mission 1: Siddhattha nearly starved himself to find the truth. Why did he stop, and what did he find instead?

Make a guess: Siddhattha nearly starved himself to find the truth. Why did he stop, and what did he find instead?

Mission 1

Learning from teachers

  • After leaving home at 29, Siddhattha became a samana: a wandering seeker.
  • He studied meditation with two famous teachers, Alara Kalama and Uddaka Ramaputta.
  • He quickly reached the deep, calm states of mind they taught.
  • But when he came out of meditation, old age, sickness and death were still there. These states did not end suffering.
  • So he left both teachers to search on his own.
Mission 1

Six years as an ascetic

  • Many seekers in India believed that punishing the body would free the mind. This is asceticism.
  • With five companions, Siddhattha tried the hardest practices: holding his breath, standing for hours, living on almost no food.
  • He grew so thin that, in his own later account, he could feel his spine through his stomach (Majjhima Nikaya 36, paraphrase).
  • He nearly died. He had pushed harder than anyone, and still had not found the answer.
  • His conclusion: extreme self-denial weakens the mind; it does not lead to awakening.
Mission 1

The Middle Way

  • He remembered a day in childhood: sitting under a rose-apple tree, he had slipped into calm, happy meditation, without any pain (Majjhima Nikaya 36).
  • Perhaps calm, clear meditation was the way.
  • He accepted a bowl of milk-rice from a young woman, Sujata, and grew strong again.
  • His five companions thought he had given up, and left him.
  • He had found the Middle Way: neither a life of luxury (the palace) nor harsh self-punishment (the forest).
  • The Middle Way runs through all Buddhist practice, from diet to meditation.

Mission 1

The night under the Bodhi tree

  • At Bodh Gaya (north-east India), at about 35, he sat beneath a fig tree, later called the Bodhi tree ("tree of awakening").
  • He vowed not to get up until he had found the truth.
  • Mara, the tempter (in Buddhism, the power of desire, fear and death), tried to stop him:
  • armies and storms to frighten him; Mara's daughters to tempt him; doubt: "who are you to claim this seat?"
  • Buddhist tradition tells that Siddhattha touched the ground. The earth bore witness to his many lives of good deeds, and Mara fled.
  • Many statues show this moment: the earth-touching gesture (bhumisparsha mudra).
Mission 1

Enlightenment

  • Buddhists believe that through the night, in deep meditation, he came to three knowledges (one in each watch of the night):
  • 1. he remembered his past lives;
  • 2. he saw beings dying and being reborn according to their kamma (karma: intentional actions);
  • 3. he understood the Four Noble Truths and was freed from craving and ignorance.
  • At dawn he was Enlightened: the Buddha, "the awakened one". Buddhists believe he was now free from suffering and would not be reborn.

Mission 1

The first sermon and the Sangha

  • At first the Buddha doubted anyone could understand. Buddhist texts say the god Brahma Sahampati begged him to teach (Samyutta Nikaya 6.1).
  • He went to the Deer Park at Sarnath, near Benares (Varanasi), and found his five old companions.
  • He taught them the Middle Way and the Four Noble Truths. This first sermon is called "Setting in motion the Wheel of the Dhamma" (Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta).
  • Kondanna understood first. The five became the first bhikkhus (monks): the start of the Sangha (community).
  • Soon the Buddha sent followers out to teach "for the welfare of many".
  • Later, his foster-mother Mahapajapati became the first bhikkhuni (nun).
  • Significance: the Sangha is one of the Three Jewels and still passes on the Dhamma today.
Mission 1Read the source

The first sermon

Samyutta Nikaya 56.11, Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta (paraphrase):

  • There are two extremes a seeker should not follow: chasing sense pleasures, which is low and unprofitable, and tormenting oneself, which is painful and unprofitable.
  • Avoiding both, the Buddha found the Middle Way. It gives vision and knowledge, and leads to peace, awakening and nibbana.
  • This Middle Way is the Noble Eightfold Path.

Q1 Which two extremes does the sutta reject?

Show answer

Sense pleasure; self-torment.

Q2 Which two stages of Siddhattha's life match those extremes?

Show answer

The palace; six years as an ascetic.

Mission 1Diversity

Different emphases

  • Theravada: the Buddha was a human being who found the path by his own effort; his example matters most. Followers aim to become arahants (fully freed ones) by the path he taught.
  • Mahayana: the Buddha shows what every being can become, since all have Buddha-nature. Many teach that he was enlightened long before his life on earth (Lotus Sutra) and that his death was a skilful lesson about impermanence.
  • Zen: stresses that he woke up by meditation, not by study; Zen practice centres on sitting meditation.
  • Pure Land: stresses the help of another Buddha, Amitabha, as well as Gotama's example.
  • All honour the Enlightenment as the moment the Dhamma began.
Mission 1Evaluate

"The Enlightenment is the most important event in the Buddha's life."

  • Reasons for: it made Siddhattha a Buddha; without it there would be no Dhamma to teach. Bodh Gaya is the most important pilgrimage place for many Buddhists. Wesak celebrates it.
  • Reasons against: without the Four Sights there would have been no search. Without the first sermon and the Sangha, no one else would have heard the Dhamma.
  • Another view: Buddhists honour three jewels: the Buddha, the Dhamma and the Sangha. Each event served all three.
  • Judge it by: Which event changed the most for other people? Which do Buddhists remember most in practice? Can one event be separated from the rest of the story?

The Buddha found freedom not in the palace and not in starvation, but in a calm, clear middle way.

Mission 2: Why did the Buddha say even your happiest moments are a kind of suffering?

Make a guess: Why did the Buddha say even your happiest moments are a kind of suffering?

Mission 2

Four truths, like a doctor

  • The Buddha's first sermon, at Sarnath, taught the Four Noble Truths (Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta, Samyutta Nikaya 56.11):
  • 1. dukkha: there is suffering;
  • 2. samudaya: suffering has a cause (craving, tanha);
  • 3. nirodha: suffering can end;
  • 4. magga: there is a path to its end, the Eightfold Path.
  • Buddhists often compare the Buddha to a doctor: diagnosis (the illness), cause, prognosis (it can be cured), treatment.
Mission 2Read the source

The First Truth

Samyutta Nikaya 56.11, Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta (paraphrase):

  • Birth is dukkha; ageing is dukkha; sickness is dukkha; death is dukkha.
  • Sorrow, grief, pain and despair are dukkha.
  • Being with what you dislike is dukkha; being parted from what you love is dukkha.
  • Not getting what you want is dukkha.
  • In short, the five khandhas that we cling to are dukkha.

Q1 Which items on the list match the Four Sights?

Show answer

Ageing, sickness, death.

Q2 Which item covers even a happy life?

Show answer

The five khandhas we cling to: everything we are is impermanent.

Mission 2

What "dukkha" means

  • Dukkha has no single English word. Translations include suffering, unsatisfactoriness, stress, dis-ease.
  • It covers pain and grief, but also the restlessness of never being fully satisfied.
  • The First Truth is a fact to understand, not a feeling to wallow in.
  • Buddhists believe it applies to all beings in samsara, not just humans: animals, ghosts and even gods.
Mission 2

Three types of dukkha

  • 1. Dukkha-dukkha: ordinary pain, of body and mind: a broken arm, grief, fear.
  • 2. Viparinama-dukkha: the suffering of change: pleasant things end, so even happiness carries loss.
  • 3. Sankhara-dukkha: the suffering of conditioned existence: everything is made of changing parts (khandhas), so there is a deep, built-in unsatisfactoriness.

The mind and suffering

> "All that we are is the result of what we have thought: it is founded on our thoughts, it is made up of our thoughts. If a man speaks or acts with an evil thought, pain follows him, as the wheel follows the foot of the ox that draws the carriage." (Dhammapada 1, Müller)

  • The verse links suffering to the mind: harmful thoughts lead to harmful actions, and pain follows as surely as a cart follows the ox.

Q1 What does the wheel following the ox show?

Show answer

Q2 Which Noble Truth does this verse point towards?

Show answer

Mission 2

Realistic, not pessimistic

  • Buddhists say the First Truth is realistic: it names what everyone faces.
  • The Truths do not stop at suffering: the Third says it can end; the Fourth shows how.
  • Understanding dukkha is the first step to compassion (karuna): all beings suffer, just as I do.
Mission 2Diversity

Can every being be freed?

  • All Buddhists agree that dukkha can end. They differ in emphasis.
  • Theravada: each person must walk the path; the goal is to become an arahant, freed from suffering. The Buddha shows the way; he cannot walk it for you.
  • Mahayana: all sentient beings have Buddha-nature and can be liberated. The Bodhisattva vows to work until all beings are freed.
  • Pure Land: the Buddha Amitabha vowed to bring all who call on him in faith to his Pure Land, where awakening is easier.

Mission 2Evaluate

"Buddhism is a pessimistic religion."

  • Reasons for: the first teaching is about suffering. Even happiness is called dukkha. Birth itself is on the list.
  • Reasons against: the First Truth is a diagnosis; a doctor who names an illness is not pessimistic. The Third Truth says suffering can end; the Fourth gives a practical path. The Dhammapada calls nibbana "the highest happiness" (verse 204).
  • Another view: Buddhists call their outlook realistic: neither ignoring suffering nor despairing of it.
  • Judge it by: Does naming suffering make a view pessimistic? Do Buddhists live with hope? Which of the Four Truths is the "last word"?

Mission 3

The Second Truth: samudaya

  • Samudaya means "arising" or "origin": suffering has a cause.
  • The Buddha named the cause tanha: literally "thirst", usually translated craving.
  • Craving is wanting things to be other than they are: holding on to what changes, pushing away what is unpleasant.
  • Because everything is impermanent (anicca), craving can never be fully satisfied. So it keeps producing dukkha.
  • Craving also keeps beings in samsara: it drives rebirth.
Mission 3

Three kinds of craving

  • 1. Kama-tanha: craving for sense pleasures: tastes, sights, sounds, comfort.
  • 2. Bhava-tanha: craving to be or become: to exist, to be someone, to be admired.
  • 3. Vibhava-tanha: craving not to be: to get rid of something, to escape, to disappear.
  • Even wanting something to stop can be craving.

Mission 3Read the source

The Sermon at Benares

The first sermon was given in the Deer Park near Benares (Varanasi): the Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta, Samyutta Nikaya 56.11. Its Second Truth (paraphrase):

  • The origin of suffering is craving, which leads to renewed existence (rebirth).
  • It comes with delight and passion, seeking pleasure now here, now there.
  • It is craving for sense pleasures, craving for existence, craving for non-existence.

Q1 Name the three kinds of craving in the sutta.

Show answer

Sense pleasures; existence; non-existence.

Q2 What does craving lead to, beyond suffering in this life?

Show answer

Renewed existence: rebirth.

Mission 3

The Three Poisons

  • Craving grows from three roots of unskilful action, called the Three Poisons (or three fires):
  • lobha: greed, attachment, grasping;
  • dosa: hatred, aversion, anger;
  • moha: ignorance, delusion, confusion.
  • AQA: ignorance, greed and hate.
  • In Tibetan Wheel of Life paintings they appear as a pig (ignorance), a rooster (greed) and a snake (hatred), each biting the next one's tail.
  • Ignorance is the deepest: not seeing the three marks; treating what changes as permanent, and a changing process as a fixed self.
  • From ignorance come greed (grabbing what we think will last) and hatred (pushing away what threatens "me").
Mission 3Read the source

Thirst and fire

> "Whomsoever this fierce thirst overcomes, full of poison, in this world, his sufferings increase like the abounding Birana grass." (Dhammapada 335, Müller)

> "There is no fire like passion, there is no shark like hatred, there is no snare like folly, there is no torrent like greed." (Dhammapada 251, Müller)

> "For hatred does not cease by hatred at any time: hatred ceases by love, this is an old rule." (Dhammapada 5, Müller)

Q1 In verse 335, what happens to someone overcome by thirst?

Show answer

Q2 According to verse 5, what is the cure for hatred?

Show answer

Mission 3

Different readings, and the antidotes

  • Each poison has a positive opposite:
  • greed → generosity (dana) and letting go;
  • hatred → loving-kindness (metta) and compassion (karuna);
  • ignorance → wisdom (panna, Sanskrit prajna).
  • Buddhists train in these: giving to monks and charities, metta bhavana meditation, study and insight meditation.
Mission 3Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain two Buddhist teachings about the causes of suffering.

  • Teaching 1: suffering is caused by tanha (craving). Source: the Buddha's first sermon (SN 56.11) says craving leads to renewed existence. Development: because everything is impermanent, craving can never be satisfied, so Buddhists try to let go.
  • Teaching 2: the Three Poisons, greed, hatred and ignorance, are the roots of unskilful action. Source: "hatred ceases by love" (Dhammapada 5). Development: so Buddhists practise metta to replace hatred with loving-kindness.

Make a guess: When a flame is blown out, where does it go? And what does that tell Buddhists about nibbana?

Mission 4

The Third Truth: nirodha

  • Nirodha means cessation: suffering can end.
  • The first sermon (Samyutta Nikaya 56.11, paraphrase): the end of suffering is the complete fading away and ending of craving: giving it up, letting it go, being free of it.
  • The logic: suffering has a cause (craving). Remove the cause, and suffering ceases.
  • This is the good news of the Four Truths: dukkha is not the last word.
Mission 4

Samsara: the cycle

  • Samsara: the cycle of birth, death and rebirth, with no known beginning.
  • Beings are reborn in one of the six realms, according to their kamma.
  • Craving and ignorance keep the cycle turning (see Mission 3).
  • Rebirth is not a soul moving to a new body; it is a stream of causes continuing (anatta).
  • Even a good rebirth, as a god, is still inside samsara and still impermanent.
  • Release from samsara is nibbana.
Mission 4

What "nibbana" means

  • Nibbana (Sanskrit nirvana) means "blowing out" or "extinguishing".
  • What is blown out? The three fires: greed, hatred and ignorance.
  • So nibbana is not a heaven or a place, and not the destruction of a person.
  • It is described as peace, freedom, the unconditioned, the highest happiness.
  • A blown-out fire: does it go north, south, east or west? The Buddha said the question does not fit (Majjhima Nikaya 72, paraphrase). Nibbana is beyond ordinary words.
Mission 4

Nibbana in life, and at death

  • Nibbana in this life: the fires are out, but body and mind continue. The Buddha reached this at 35 under the Bodhi tree.
  • Such a person is an arahant ("worthy one"): free of craving, living with wisdom and compassion.
  • Parinibbana ("final nibbana"): at death, an arahant is not reborn. The Buddha passed into parinibbana at 80.
  • Theravada texts call these nibbana "with remainder" and "without remainder".

> "He who overcomes this fierce thirst, difficult to be conquered in this world, sufferings fall off from him, like water-drops from a lotus leaf." (Dhammapada 336, Müller)

Mission 4

What is Enlightenment?

  • Enlightenment (bodhi: "awakening") means seeing reality as it really is: the three marks, dependent arising, the Four Truths.
  • With it come freedom from the three poisons, wisdom and compassion.
  • Different meanings, depending on who is enlightened:
  • an arahant: freed by following the Buddha's path;
  • a Buddha: found the path and teaches it to others;
  • in Mahayana, a bodhisattva on the way to full Buddhahood, for the sake of all beings.
  • Your spec asks for interpretations of nibbana and Enlightenment: next screen.
Mission 4Diversity

Interpretations

  • Theravada: the goal is nibbana as an arahant, by one's own effort on the Eightfold Path. Nibbana is the unconditioned, wholly different from samsara.
  • Mahayana: the highest goal is full Buddhahood. Bodhisattvas work for the release of all beings.
  • Nagarjuna (c. 150–250 CE) taught that nirvana and samsara are not two separate places: nirvana is seeing samsara truly, as empty (Mulamadhyamakakarika 25, paraphrase).
  • Zen: awakening (satori, kensho) can come suddenly, here and now, through meditation.
  • Pure Land: faith in Amitabha Buddha leads to rebirth in his Pure Land, where Enlightenment is easier to reach.
Quick check
?Which is a Mahayana emphasis rather than a teaching shared by all Buddhists?
Mission 4Evaluate

"Nibbana cannot be described, so it cannot be a useful goal."

  • Reasons for: the texts describe nibbana mostly by negatives (unborn, unmade). The Buddha refused to say what happens to an enlightened one after death. It is hard to aim at something you cannot picture.
  • Reasons against: nibbana is known by experience, like a taste, not by definition. Its effects can be described: freedom from greed, hatred and ignorance; peace; "the highest happiness" (Dhammapada 204). The path is described step by step, and each step brings some benefit now.
  • Another view: Mahayana and Zen stress awakening here and now, so the goal is not far away.
  • Judge it by: Must a goal be described to be worth pursuing? Can partial experience give confidence? Is the path more important than a description of the end?
Mission check

For Buddhists, nibbana is the fires of craving going out: not a place, not nothingness, but release from suffering and rebirth.

Mission 5

The Fourth Noble Truth

  • The Four Noble Truths work like a doctor's visit:
  • Dukkha: the illness (suffering, unsatisfactoriness).
  • Samudaya: the cause (craving, tanha).
  • Nirodha: the cure is possible (craving can cease: nibbana).
  • Magga: the medicine, a path that leads there.
  • The Fourth Truth is the practical one: it says what to do.
Mission 5

The Middle Way

  • The Buddha had lived both extremes: a prince's luxury, then years of harsh fasting.
  • Neither brought awakening. He called the path between them the Middle Way.
  • A well-known image: a lute string too tight snaps; too loose, it makes no sound. Tuned just right, it plays.
  • Say: the Middle Way avoids both indulgence and self-punishment — not: the Middle Way means "halfway between good and bad".
Mission 5

Eight factors, one path

  • The Noble Eightfold Path (each factor starts samma, "right": complete, wholesome):
  • 1 Right view (understanding) · 2 Right intention (thought, resolve)
  • 3 Right speech · 4 Right action · 5 Right livelihood
  • 6 Right effort · 7 Right mindfulness · 8 Right concentration
  • Not eight steps climbed in order: they are practised together.
  • Symbol: the Dhamma wheel (dhammacakka) with eight spokes. Remove one spoke and the wheel is weaker.
Mission 5

What each factor asks

  • View: understanding the Four Noble Truths. Intention: letting go, goodwill, harmlessness.
  • Speech: no lies, divisive talk, harsh words or idle chatter.
  • Action: no killing, stealing or sexual misconduct.
  • Livelihood: work that does not harm (traditionally: no trade in weapons, living beings, meat, intoxicants or poisons).
  • Effort: stop unskilful states arising; let go of those present; grow and keep skilful ones.
  • Mindfulness: clear awareness of body, feelings, mind and mental objects.
  • Concentration: a calm, focused mind developed in meditation.
Mission 5

The Threefold Way

  • The eight factors fall into three groups, the Threefold Way:
  • Panna (wisdom): right view, right intention.
  • Sila (ethics): right speech, right action, right livelihood.
  • Samadhi (meditation): right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration.
  • They support each other: ethical living calms the mind; a calm mind sees clearly; clear seeing guides how to live.
  • Many teachers present them as a training order (sila → samadhi → panna), while the list starts with view, because some understanding is needed to set out at all.

  • Verse 192 adds: that is the safe refuge, the best refuge (paraphrase).
  • Müller's 1881 words: "the Law" = the Dhamma; "the Church" = the Sangha (community).
  • Buddha, Dhamma, Sangha: the Three Refuges (Three Jewels).

Mission 5Diversity

One path, lived differently

  • All Buddhist traditions accept the Four Noble Truths and the Eightfold Path. Emphases differ.
  • Theravada: the path to nibbana as an arahant. Monks and nuns give most time to meditation and study; lay Buddhists often stress sila and generosity (dana).
  • Zen (Mahayana): sitting meditation (zazen) at the heart of practice; wisdom seen directly in the present moment.
  • Tibetan (Mahayana): the path within the bodhisattva's six perfections; study and debate train wisdom.
  • Pure Land (Mahayana): many rely on the power of Amida Buddha rather than their own effort; the ethics of the path are a grateful response.
  • Triratna (UK-founded): lay and ordained members follow the path in daily jobs and families; some have run team-based right-livelihood businesses.
  • Today: right livelihood and right speech raise questions about jobs, online posting and gossip.
Quick check

Mission 5Evaluate

"Wisdom is the most important part of the Threefold Way."

  • Reasons for: right view comes first in the list. Ignorance is the root of suffering, so only wisdom finally removes it. Ethics and meditation serve wisdom.
  • Reasons against: without sila the mind is too restless to meditate or see clearly. Many lay Buddhists build their practice mainly on precepts and generosity. The wheel image: no spoke is "most important".
  • Another view: in Pure Land Buddhism, entrusting oneself to Amida matters more than one's own wisdom or effort.
  • Judge it by: Is the path a sequence or a whole? Whose practice are we describing: monastic or lay? Theravada or Mahayana?
Mission 5Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain two Buddhist teachings about the Eightfold Path. Refer to sacred writings or another source of Buddhist belief and teaching.

  • Teaching 1: the path is the Middle Way. Development: it avoids indulgence and self-torment, which the Buddha had tried; so Buddhists aim for a simple, balanced life.
  • Teaching 2: the path leads to the end of suffering. Source: Dhammapada 191 calls it "the eightfold holy way that leads to the quieting of pain". Development: practising sila, samadhi and panna together removes craving, the cause of suffering.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • After his teachers and six years of harsh asceticism, Siddhattha chose the Middle Way and meditated under the Bodhi tree at Bodh Gaya.
  • Buddhists believe he defeated Mara, understood the Four Noble Truths and became the Buddha; at Sarnath he taught the first sermon (SN 56.11), and the five companions became the first Sangha.

Mission 2

  • The First Noble Truth: there is dukkha (suffering, unsatisfactoriness), taught in the first sermon (SN 56.11).
  • Three types: dukkha-dukkha (pain), viparinama-dukkha (change), sankhara-dukkha (conditioned existence; Edexcel "fabrication").
  • Buddhists see the truth as realistic, not pessimistic; Theravada and Mahayana differ on emphasis, e.g. the Bodhisattva vow to free all beings.

Mission 3

  • The Second Noble Truth (samudaya): suffering is caused by tanha (craving) for sense pleasures, for existence and for non-existence.
  • The Three Poisons: greed (lobha), hatred (dosa) and ignorance (moha); ignorance is the deepest root.

Mission 4

  • The Third Noble Truth (nirodha): Buddhists teach that suffering ends when craving ends; samsara is the cycle of rebirth driven by craving and kamma.
  • Nibbana means "blowing out" the fires of greed, hatred and ignorance; reached in life, with parinibbana at death; Enlightenment (bodhi) is awakening to reality.
  • Theravada aims at arahantship; Mahayana at Buddhahood for all beings; Zen stresses sudden awakening, Pure Land faith in Amitabha.

Mission 5

  • The Fourth Noble Truth (magga): the Noble Eightfold Path, the Middle Way between indulgence and self-torment, leads to the end of suffering.
  • Eight factors in three groups: panna (view, intention), sila (speech, action, livelihood), samadhi (effort, mindfulness, concentration).
  • Dhammapada 190–191 links the Three Refuges with seeing the Four Noble Truths and the eightfold way.
