Mission 1: Can one ultimate reality be beyond all description and also a God you can love?

Make a guess: Can one ultimate reality be beyond all description and also a God you can love?

Mission 1

Brahman: ultimate reality

  • Most Hindus believe in one ultimate reality behind everything: Brahman.
  • Brahman is eternal and unchanging; the source, support and end of all that exists.
  • Many Hindus simply call Brahman God.
  • Do not confuse: Brahman (ultimate reality) · Brahma (one deity, the creator in the Trimurti) · Brahmin (the priestly class).
  • Hindus understand Brahman in two main ways: nirguna (without qualities) and saguna (with qualities).
Mission 1Read the source

Brahman in the Upanishads

"The Being who remains awake while all sleep, who grants all desires, That is pure, That is Brahman, That alone is said to be immortal. On That all the worlds rest." (Katha Upanishad 2.2.8, Paramananda)

  • "Breath is Brahman, Ka (pleasure) is Brahman, Kha (ether) is Brahman." (Chandogya Upanishad 4.10.5, Müller): Brahman is life, joy and the space that holds all things.

Mission 1

Nirguna Brahman

  • Nirguna: "without qualities" (attributes).
  • Brahman is beyond every name, shape and description; no human idea can contain it.
  • The Upanishads describe it by negation: neti, neti, "not this, not this".
  • Impersonal: not a "someone" to pray to, but pure being, consciousness and bliss.
  • "There the eye does not go, nor speech, nor mind." (Kena Upanishad 1.3, Paramananda)
Mission 1

Saguna Brahman

  • Saguna: "with qualities".
  • Brahman known as a personal God with qualities such as love, power, beauty and mercy, who can be known, worshipped and loved.
  • Many Hindus know saguna Brahman as Vishnu (and his avatars Rama and Krishna), Shiva, or the Goddess (Devi, Shakti).
  • Hindu deities are seen as manifestations of saguna Brahman: forms of the one divine reality, not rival gods.

Mission 1

One reality, two ways

  • Most Hindus see nirguna and saguna as one Brahman, understood in two ways, not two Gods.
  • An image often used: water is one substance, whether as formless vapour or as ice with a shape.
  • Humans need form to relate to the infinite: saguna Brahman lets people love and serve God.
  • In daily life (saguna): worship (puja) before a murti, singing devotional songs (bhajans), festivals, prayer to a chosen deity.
  • In daily life (nirguna): meditation, study of the Upanishads, seeking knowledge (jnana) that sees Brahman in everything.
Mission 1Diversity

Monist and monotheist views

  • Monist (Advaita Vedanta, "non-dual"; taught by Shankara, traditionally 8th century CE): only Brahman is ultimately real.
  • For Advaita, nirguna Brahman is the highest truth. A personal God (Ishvara) is real at the level of everyday experience, but not ultimate.
  • Monotheist (many Vaishnavas, e.g. followers of Ramanuja or Madhva; also many Shaivas and Shaktas): Brahman is supremely personal.
  • For them, saguna is the highest: Vishnu (or Krishna, Shiva, Devi) is the Supreme Being. "Without qualities" means without any imperfect qualities.
  • Both views hold that there is one ultimate reality; they differ on whether the personal or the impersonal is final.
Mission 1

Vaikuntha and spiritual worlds

  • Vaikuntha: the eternal spiritual world of Vishnu, beyond the material universe. Its name suggests "without anxiety".
  • There is no birth, death or decay; liberated souls live in Vishnu's presence and serve him with love.
  • Other traditions speak of Goloka (Krishna's realm) and Kailasa (Shiva's).
  • For many Vaishnavas, moksha (liberation) means reaching Vaikuntha. For Advaitins, moksha means realising oneness with Brahman; spiritual worlds are not the final goal.
  • Chandogya Upanishad 5.10.1–3 (paraphrase): those who know Brahman go by the path of the gods and are led to Brahman; those who only perform rituals and good works go by the path of the ancestors and return to rebirth.

Mission 1Evaluate

"Brahman is best understood as nirguna."

  • Reasons for: the Upanishads say Brahman is beyond eye, speech and mind (Kena 1.3). Any quality would limit the infinite. Advaita teachers (the school that holds only Brahman is ultimately real) hold nirguna as the highest truth. It avoids picturing God as a human.
  • Reasons against: in the Gita, Krishna reveals himself as the Supreme, and says the path to the unmanifest is harder for embodied beings (Bhagavad Gita 12.5, paraphrase). Love (bhakti) needs a personal God. Most Hindus worship Brahman in saguna form.
  • Another view: both are true: one reality seen from two sides, as the Gita and many teachers hold.
  • Judge it by: Which scriptures carry most weight? Can the infinite have qualities? Which view makes sense of Hindu worship?

Mission 2

One reality, three ways of knowing it

  • Many Hindus say the one ultimate reality can be known in three ways.
  • A well-known verse of the Bhagavata Purana (1.2.11) names them: Brahman, Paramatma and Bhagavan (paraphrase).
  • Everywhere: Brahman, present in all things, non-personal.
  • Within the heart: the divine living inside each being.
  • Beyond: a personal, loving God.
  • Your spec calls the second feature Antaryami ("inner controller"). Paramatma ("supreme self") names the same idea.
  • Not three gods: one divine, seen from three distances.
Mission 2

Brahman: everywhere

  • Brahman is the divine reality that pervades everything: every atom, every person, every animal.
  • Seen this way, Brahman is non-personal: like light or space, not someone you talk to.
  • This is Brahman as nirguna: unlimited, infinite, without attributes.
  • "All this is Brahman" (Chandogya Upanishad 3.14.1, paraphrase).
  • Chandogya Upanishad IV.x.4–5: the sacred fires teach the student Upakosala that Brahman is life, Brahman is joy, Brahman is space (paraphrase).
  • Significance: if the divine is in everything, everything deserves respect.
Mission 2

Antaryami: within the heart

  • The same divine is present inside every living being, guiding it from within.
  • "The Purusha (Self), of the size of a thumb, resides in the middle of the body" (Katha Upanishad 4.12, Paramananda).
  • "There lives a Master in the hearts of men" (Bhagavad Gita 18.61, Arnold).
  • "It is the ear of the ear, the mind of the mind" (Kena Upanishad 1.2, Paramananda): the power by which we see, hear and think.
  • Careful: the atman is the individual's own eternal self. Antaryami is God dwelling beside it in the heart (in most theistic readings).
  • Significance: God is as close as your own heart; Hindus meditate to become aware of this inner presence.

Mission 2

Bhagavan: beyond, a personal loving God

  • Bhagavan means "the one who possesses all excellences": beauty, power, knowledge, fame, wealth, detachment.
  • God as a person who can be known, loved, served and spoken to.
  • Also called Ishvara ("Lord"); the supreme person living in a spiritual realm (e.g. Vaikuntha for Vishnu, Kailasa for Shiva).
  • Worshipped through forms such as Vishnu, Krishna, Shiva or the Goddess.
  • God returns love: "A leaf, a flower, a fruit, water poured forth, / That offering I accept, lovingly made" (Bhagavad Gita 9.26, Arnold).
Mission 2Read the source

Kena Upanishad, part 1

> "That which cannot be thought by mind, but by which, they say, mind is able to think: know that alone to be the Brahman, not this which people worship here." (Kena Upanishad 1.5, Paramananda)

  • The verse repeats for speech, sight, hearing and breath (1.4–1.8).

Mission 2Diversity

Which feature comes first?

  • All three features are widely accepted. Hindu traditions differ on which is highest.
  • Advaita Vedanta (Shankara, c. 8th century CE): non-personal Brahman is the highest truth; the personal God is real for worship, but is Brahman seen through maya. Liberation: knowing that atman is Brahman.
  • Vaishnava traditions (e.g. Ramanuja; Gaudiya Vaishnavas and ISKCON): Bhagavan (Vishnu or Krishna) is the fullest truth. ISKCON teaches that the everywhere-Brahman is like the sun's light, and Bhagavan like the sun itself.
  • Shaiva traditions: Shiva is Bhagavan, and also pervades and indwells all.
  • Many Hindus combine all three in daily life without ranking them.
  • Link to moksha: for Advaitins, merging into Brahman; for Vaishnavas, loving service to Bhagavan in his spiritual realm.

Mission 2

Why it matters in Hindu life

  • Everywhere → respect for all life: ahimsa (non-violence), care for animals and nature; "namaste" honours the divine in another person.
  • Within the heart → meditation, japa and quiet prayer: looking inward to find God.
  • Beyond → devotion (bhakti): puja to a murti, singing bhajans, offering food, a loving relationship with God.
  • One person may do all three in a day.
  • The feature a Hindu stresses shapes how they seek moksha: knowledge, meditation or devotion.
Mission 2Evaluate

"Bhagavan is the most important feature of the divine."

  • Reasons for: a personal God can be loved and can love back (Gita 9.26). Most Hindu worship (puja, bhajans, festivals) is directed to personal forms. Vaishnavas see Bhagavan as the source of Brahman (Gita 14.27).
  • Reasons against: Advaitins hold that non-personal Brahman is the final truth; personal forms are a step towards it. The Upanishads stress Brahman beyond all form (Kena 1.4–1.8). Antaryami makes God closest of all: within the heart.
  • Another view: the three are one reality; ranking them misses the point. Different features suit different people and stages of life.
  • Judge it by: Which feature shapes most Hindus' daily practice? Which do the scriptures stress most? Can one reality have a "most important" feature?

  • Belief 2: the divine lives within the heart as Antaryami. Source: "There lives a Master in the hearts of men" (Gita 18.61). Development: so Hindus meditate and pray quietly, seeking the God who is already inside them.

Mission 3

Four aims of life

  • Hindus speak of four purusharthas (aims of human life):
  • Dharma: duty, right living, righteousness.
  • Artha: wealth, work, success, security, gained honestly.
  • Kama: pleasure, love, enjoyment of the arts and family life.
  • Moksha: liberation from samsara.
  • Artha and kama are good, not sins, as long as they are pursued within dharma.
  • Moksha is the final aim; the other three prepare for it.
Mission 3

What is dharma?

  • From a root meaning "to hold" or "support": dharma is what holds the world and society together.
  • It means cosmic order, right conduct and personal duty.
  • Dharma depends on context: a soldier's duty differs from a doctor's; a student's from a grandparent's.
  • Some duties are for everyone (sadharana dharma): truthfulness, non-violence (ahimsa), self-control, generosity.
Mission 3Read the source

Your own duty

> "Finally, this is better, that one do / His own task as he may, even though he fail, / Than take tasks not his own, though they seem good." (Bhagavad Gita 3.35, Arnold)

  • Context: Arjuna, a warrior, wants to give up the fight. Krishna tells him to do the duty that is his.
  • Gita 2.31: for a warrior there is nothing better than a righteous battle; Arjuna should not shrink from his duty (paraphrase).

Mission 3

Sanatana dharma

  • Sanatana dharma = "eternal dharma" or "eternal law".
  • Many Hindus prefer it as the name of their religion: "Hinduism" is a later, outsiders' word.
  • It suggests ancient and universal truths: the eternal atman, karma, the divine in all, and duties that apply to everyone.
  • Sanatana dharma frames the other ideas: varna, ashrama, artha, karma and moksha all sit inside the eternal order.
Mission 3

Varna: four groups in society

  • Varnashrama dharma: duty according to your varna (social group) and ashrama (stage of life).
  • Four varnas: Brahmins (priests, teachers), Kshatriyas (rulers, warriors), Vaishyas (merchants, farmers), Shudras (workers, service).
  • Purusha Sukta (Rig Veda 10.90.12, paraphrase): the Brahmin came from the cosmic Person's mouth, the Kshatriya from his arms, the Vaishya from his thighs, the Shudra from his feet: one body, four functions.
  • Gita 4.13: "I am He / Made the Four Castes, and portioned them a place / After their qualities and gifts." (Bhagavad Gita 4.13, Arnold)
  • Diversity: many traditional Hindus see varna as set by birth; many reformers and ISKCON read Gita 4.13 as varna by qualities and work; others, such as B. R. Ambedkar (1891–1956), rejected the whole system as discrimination.
  • Jati (birth groups, "castes") and untouchability are distinct from the four varnas; untouchability was abolished in India's Constitution (1950). Gandhi campaigned against it.
Mission 3

Four ashramas: stages of life

  • Brahmacharya: student. Learn, show self-discipline, respect the teacher.
  • Grihastha: householder. Marry, work, raise a family, support others; artha and kama matter most here.
  • Vanaprastha: retirement ("forest-dweller"). Hand over to the next generation; turn to spiritual life.
  • Sannyasa: renunciation. Give up possessions and ties; focus on moksha. Few Hindus take this stage today.
  • Dharma changes with each stage: the same person's duty at 15, 35, 60 and 80 is different.

Mission 3

Svadharma, sewa and dana

  • Svadharma: your own duty, shaped by varna, ashrama, nature and situation (Gita 3.35).
  • Sewa (seva): selfless service, e.g. volunteering at a temple kitchen or caring for the sick; ideally nishkam karma.
  • Dana: giving: money, food, time, knowledge; to the needy and to holy causes.
  • "The good is one thing and the pleasant another. … It is well with him who chooses the good." (Katha Upanishad 2.1, Paramananda)
  • Gita 2.62–65: brooding on sense objects breeds desire, then anger, then ruin; the self-controlled find peace (paraphrase).
  • These are Hindu responses to social concerns: duty to family, society and the poor.
Mission 3Evaluate

"Varnashrama dharma is still relevant today."

  • Reasons for: the ashramas give a wise shape to life: learning, family, handing on, spiritual focus. Varna, read as qualities and work (Gita 4.13), says everyone has a role and a duty. Svadharma (your own duty, Gita 3.35) helps people find their own path rather than copy others.
  • Reasons against: varna by birth has been used to justify caste discrimination, which many Hindus and the Indian Constitution reject. Modern careers, mobility and long lives do not fit four neat stages. Sannyasa is rare today.
  • Another view: keep the ashramas and the idea of duty; reject birth-based varna.
  • Judge it by: Which version of varna is meant? Can an old ideal guide without being followed exactly? What do Hindus in Britain actually do?

Mission 4

Why is there suffering?

  • Hindu teachings point to several linked causes, not one.
  • Karma: actions have results. Harmful actions bring suffering later, in this life or a future one (samsara, the cycle of birth and death).
  • Avidya (ignorance): not knowing the true self. People think "I am this body", so loss, illness and death seem to destroy them.
  • Maya (illusion): taking the changing world as if it were the lasting reality.
  • Desire and attachment: grasping at things that cannot last leads to anger, fear and grief.
  • Karma and ignorance keep the atman (eternal self) bound in samsara, and so suffering goes on, until it gains moksha (liberation).
Mission 4

Free, but not unlimited

  • Most Hindus believe humans have free will: we choose how to act.
  • But some things are already set by past karma: the family, body and situation you are born into.
  • A common picture: you cannot choose the cards you are dealt, but you choose how you play them.
  • A human birth is seen as precious: humans, unlike animals, can choose to act on dharma and seek moksha.
  • So karma is not fate: the past shapes the situation, the present choice is still yours.
  • The Gita adds that the thoughts a person dwells on all their life shape their mind at death, and so their next birth; one who remembers God at death reaches God (Bhagavad Gita 8.5–6, 8.8–12, paraphrase).
Mission 4

Ignorance and knowledge

  • Avidya is not lack of information. It is not knowing what you really are: the atman, not the body.
  • From that ignorance come selfishness ("I and mine"), greed (lobha) and anger (krodha).
  • The Gita names three "gates" to ruin: lust, anger and greed (Bhagavad Gita 16.21, paraphrase).
  • Knowledge (vidya, jnana) means spiritual insight, gained from scripture, a guru and practice.
  • Knowledge removes ignorance as light removes darkness; with it, fear and selfishness lose their grip.

Mission 4Diversity

Responding to suffering

  • Advaita (non-dual) Hindus: the atman is one with Brahman and never really suffers. Suffering belongs to the world of maya; knowledge of the true self ends it.
  • Vaishnava and other devotional Hindus: suffering comes from forgetting God. Devotion and God's grace free the soul; hardship can turn the heart towards God.
  • Practical response, shared by most: accept what cannot be changed as karma, without blaming the sufferer; act now to relieve pain through seva (selfless service) and compassion.
Mission 4

Ahimsa: do no harm

  • Ahimsa: non-violence, not harming any living being in thought, word or deed.
  • Reason 1: the same atman lives in every being, so harming another harms the divine in them.
  • Reason 2: harm creates bad karma.
  • In practice: many Hindus are vegetarian; care for animals and nature; speaking without cruelty.
  • Mohandas Gandhi (1869–1948) made ahimsa the basis of non-violent resistance.
  • Not all Hindus see it as absolute: the Gita teaches Arjuna that a warrior's dharma is to fight a just war (Bhagavad Gita 2.31, paraphrase), and some Hindus eat meat.
Mission 4

The other virtues

  • Respect: for all beings, and especially for parents, teachers, elders and guests.
  • Empathy: the best yogi judges others' pleasure and pain by comparison with their own (Bhagavad Gita 6.32, paraphrase).
  • Mind and sense control (dama): not being ruled by appetites; the wise draw the senses in "as the wise tortoise draws its four feet" (Bhagavad Gita 2.58, Arnold).
  • Humility: no pride in birth, wealth or learning; everyone is equally an atman.
  • Love: love for God (bhakti) overflowing into love for all beings.
  • OCR names four duties (it calls them yamas, restraints): ahimsa, daya (compassion), dama (self-control) and dana (giving, without expecting a return).

Mission 4Diversity

Which virtues come first?

  • Gandhi: ahimsa (with truth) above all; it guided his whole politics.
  • Warrior dharma: protecting the innocent can require force; so ahimsa is weighed against justice (the Gita's setting is a battlefield).
  • Virtue by role: the Gita lists calm, self-control, purity, patience and knowledge for the brahmin, and courage, firmness, skill and generosity for the warrior (Bhagavad Gita 18.42–43, paraphrase). Virtues link to each person's dharma.
  • Devotional traditions (e.g. ISKCON, many Vaishnavas): love of God is the root virtue; the others follow from it.
  • Lists of yamas differ: the Yoga Sutras of Patanjali give five (ahimsa, truthfulness, not stealing, sexual restraint, non-possessiveness); other texts give ten, including daya.
Mission 4Evaluate

"Suffering is always the result of a person's own karma."

  • For: the law of karma is that actions bring results; nothing happens without a cause (Gita, Upanishads). It explains why the innocent seem to suffer: past lives.
  • For: it gives suffering meaning and hope: acting well now changes the future.
  • Against: many Hindus say suffering also comes from avidya and maya, not only from one's own actions; others' free choices can harm the innocent.
  • Against: it risks blaming victims; most Hindu teachers say the right response is compassion (daya, seva), never judgement.
  • Against: devotional Hindus say God's grace can lift karma, so karma is not the whole story.
  • Judge it by: Does it fit what scripture says? Does it lead people to act more kindly? Can it explain the suffering of a newborn child?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Most Hindus believe Brahman is the one ultimate reality: spirit, absolute truth, the source and support of all.
  • For Hindus, nirguna Brahman is beyond all qualities; saguna Brahman is Brahman with qualities, known as a personal God.
  • Monists (Advaita) put nirguna first; monotheists (e.g. many Vaishnavas) put the personal God first.
  • Vaikuntha is Vishnu's eternal spiritual world.

Mission 2

  • Many Hindus know the one divine in three ways: Brahman (everywhere, non-personal), Antaryami or Paramatma (within the heart), Bhagavan (beyond, a personal loving God).
  • Traditions rank them differently: Advaitins put non-personal Brahman first; Vaishnavas put Bhagavan first; many Hindus hold all three.

Mission 3

  • The four aims: dharma (duty), artha (wealth), kama (pleasure), moksha (liberation); Hindus see artha and kama as good within dharma.
  • Sanatana dharma ("eternal dharma") is what many Hindus call their religion; varnashrama dharma links duty to varna and ashrama (student, householder, retired, renunciate).
  • Varna is understood by some as birth, by others as qualities and work; many reject caste discrimination.

Mission 4

  • Hindus link suffering to karma, avidya (ignorance of the true self), maya (illusion) and attachment; humans have free will within limits set by past karma.
  • Virtues: ahimsa, respect, empathy, mind and sense control, humility, love; OCR adds daya and dana. Mahabharata 5.39: do not do to others what harms yourself.
  • Different Hindus stress knowledge, devotion or service as the answer to suffering.
