Mission 1

What is a biome?

  • A biome is a global-scale ecosystem: a large region with a typical climax vegetation and the animals that go with it.
  • Climate sets it; soils and wildlife follow.
  • One widely used map divides land into 14 biomes made of 846 ecoregions.
  • Your four: tundra, tropical rainforest, hot desert, temperate grassland.
Mission 1

Climate sorts biomes

Annual figures, 1991–2020 normals:

  • Utqiaġvik, Alaska (tundra): mean −10.2 °C; 137 mm.
  • Phoenix, Arizona (hot desert): mean 24.2 °C; 183 mm.
  • Dodge City, Kansas (temperate grassland): mean 13.3 °C; 559 mm.
  • Hilo, Hawaiʻi (tropical wet forest): mean 23.3 °C; 3,058 mm.
  • Temperature sets the energy and growing season; precipitation sets the water.
Quick check
?Utqiaġvik averages −10.2 °C and 137 mm a year; Phoenix averages 24.2 °C and 183 mm. Why are they in different biomes?
Mission 1

Latitude and the global circulation

  • Equator: strong sun; rising air cools and gives heavy rain all year → tropical rainforest.
  • About 30° N and S: "the poleward flowing air begins to descend"; the sinking air is dry. "Most of the nonpolar deserts lie within the two trade winds belts" → hot deserts.
  • Polar edges: weak sun, long winters, cold dry air → tundra.
  • So biomes form rough belts by latitude.
Mission 1

What breaks the belts

  • Continentality: inland areas are far from ocean moisture and swing between hot summers and cold winters. Dodge City's monthly means run from 0.6 °C (Jan) to 26.7 °C (Jul) → temperate grassland.
  • Altitude: high mountains carry alpine tundra even near the equator.
  • Relief: mountains cast rain shadows on their lee side.
  • Ocean currents: cold currents along some west coasts help keep coastal deserts dry.
Mission 1

Tropical rainforest: where

  • A belt close to the equator, mostly within about 10° N and S.
  • Main blocks: the Amazon basin, the Congo basin, South-East Asia and New Guinea.
  • Tropical and subtropical moist forests cover about 14.4% of Earth's land.
Mission 1

Hot desert: where

  • Mainly between about 15° and 30° N and S, often on the west sides of continents.
  • Examples: Sahara, Arabian, Kalahari and Namib, Australian interior, Sonoran, Atacama.
  • Deserts and xeric shrublands cover about 19.3% of land. That includes cold deserts too: deserts "may be hot, they may be cold".
Mission 1

Temperate grassland: where

  • Mid-latitude continental interiors, roughly 35°–55°, far from the sea.
  • Examples: North American prairies, Eurasian steppes, Argentina's pampas, South Africa's veld.
  • Temperate grasslands, savannas and shrublands cover about 7.8% of land.
Mission 1

Tundra: where

  • North of the Arctic treeline: northern Alaska and Canada, coastal Greenland, northern Scandinavia and Russia.
  • Almost none in the Southern Hemisphere: little land lies at those latitudes outside Antarctica.
  • Alpine tundra sits above the treeline on high mountains.
  • Tundra covers about 8.7% of land.

Mission check

?Two places each get about 500 mm of precipitation a year. One lies at 40° N in a continental interior with hot summers; the other lies at 70° N on an Arctic coast. Why do they carry different biomes?

Biomes are sorted by temperature and precipitation together, and those follow latitude, the global circulation and continentality. Tundra and hot desert are both dry, but one is cold and one is hot.

Make a guess: Tundra and grassland are both treeless and both have cold winters. Why is only one soil so fertile?

Mission 2

Two treeless biomes

  • Tundra: Arctic lowlands beyond the tree line, and the tops of high mountains. Mosses, sedges and lichens; almost no trees.
  • Temperate grassland: mid-latitude continental interiors: the steppes of Eurasia and the prairies (Great Plains) of North America.
  • Both lack trees, but for different reasons: in the tundra, cold and a short growing season; in the grassland, limited rain.
  • Our two climate stations: Utqiaġvik, Alaska (tundra) and Dodge City, Kansas (grassland).
Mission 2

Tundra climate

  • Utqiaġvik, 1991–2020 normals (converted to °C; our calculation):
  • Annual mean −10.2 °C; February −24.4 °C; July 5.4 °C.
  • Eight months average below 0 °C; only June–September are above.
  • Precipitation only 137 mm a year, much of it as snow; 53% falls in July–September.
  • Under 250 mm and a warmest month under 10 °C: by the USGS definition it is a polar desert.
  • NASA's tundra summary: mean temperatures below 0 °C for six to ten months; 150–250 mm of precipitation a year.
Mission 2

Temperate grassland climate

  • Dodge City, 1991–2020 normals (converted; our calculation):
  • January 0.6 °C; July 26.7 °C: an annual range of about 26 °C. This is continentality: far from the sea, land heats and cools fast.
  • Precipitation 559 mm a year; 65% falls April–August, in the growing season.
  • FAO: chernozem regions have "a continental climate with cold winters and hot summers", with annual rainfall roughly equal to evaporation.
  • Enough rain for tall grass, but summer drought and fire stop most trees.
Quick check
?A station has a July mean of 6 °C, eight months below freezing and 140 mm of precipitation a year. Which biome is it?
Mission 2

Permafrost and the active layer

  • Permafrost: rock or soil at or below 0 °C for two or more years.
  • Active layer: the top layer that thaws each summer, normally 40–80 cm deep in Cryosols.
  • Below it, permafrost "acts like a waterproof barrier".
  • So summer meltwater and rain cannot drain down. With low evaporation in a cool summer, the active layer stays saturated.
Mission 2

Tundra soils: gley and cryosols

  • Waterlogging removes oxygen. Iron is reduced, turning the soil grey, bluish or greenish, with rusty mottles where air gets in: gleying.
  • In Cryosols, gleyic properties form from "prolonged saturation … during the thaw period".
  • Cryoturbation (frost churning) leaves "irregular or broken soil horizons".
  • Organic matter builds up on top of the permafrost; once frozen, its decay stops.
  • Most plant nutrients are "locked into the surface organic matter": a thin, cold, wet store that plants can use only slowly.
Quick check
?The tundra gets very little precipitation. Why are its soils often waterlogged in summer?
Mission 2

Chernozem: "black earth"

  • Name from Russian chern (black) and zemlja (earth).
  • A thick black surface layer rich in organic matter; in well-developed chernozems it reaches more than 2 m.
  • The zonal soil of the tall-grass steppes. On the US Great Plains the related soils are Mollisols, "base rich throughout and therefore quite fertile".
  • Calcium carbonate often builds up lower down.
  • Burrowing animals mix the soil; their filled burrows (krotovinas) "stand out as black".
  • About 230 million ha worldwide; wheat, barley and maize are the main crops.
Mission 2

Why chernozem forms

  • Input: dense grass roots die back and add organic matter deep in the profile, not just at the surface.
  • Slow decay: cold winters and dry late summers slow decomposition, so humus accumulates.
  • Little leaching: rainfall roughly equals evaporation, so water rarely flushes through. Bases (calcium, magnesium, potassium) stay in the soil; carbonate collects lower down.
  • Mixing: burrowing animals spread humus through the profile.
  • Result: deep, well-drained, base-rich, crumbly soil: excellent for cereals.
Quick check
?A soil pit shows 1 m of black, crumbly topsoil, then a pale layer with white calcium carbonate nodules. What best explains the white layer?
Mission 2

Side by side

  • Temperature: tundra summer mean ~5 °C; grassland summer ~27 °C. Growing season short vs long.
  • Water: tundra soil waterlogged above permafrost; grassland soil well-drained, with little leaching.
  • Organic matter: tundra: thin, frozen or wet, slow to release nutrients; grassland: thick humus mixed deep.
  • Profile: tundra gley with cryoturbation; chernozem with black A horizon and carbonate below.
  • Exam move ("Explain"): link each soil property to a climate cause (cold → slow decay; permafrost → waterlogging; P ≈ E → little leaching).
Mission check

?Over centuries, a steppe's climate becomes much wetter: rainfall now far exceeds evaporation, and summers stay warm. There is no permafrost. What would most likely happen to its chernozem?

Both biomes have cold winters, but the grassland's deep roots, slow decay and limited leaching (P ≈ E) build a deep, base-rich humus layer. Upset that water balance, or trap water above permafrost as in the tundra, and fertility falls.

Mission 3

Climate zones are moving

  • IPCC: warming has shifted climate zones in many regions: arid zones expanding, polar zones contracting.
  • Many species have changed their ranges, numbers and timing of seasonal activity.
  • About half of species assessed have shifted towards the poles or, on land, uphill.
Mission 3

In the ocean

  • Upper-ocean species (top 200 m): shifted poleward 52 ± 33 km per decade since the 1950s; seafloor species 29 ± 16 km.
  • Species track the temperatures they are adapted to: the habitat moves, so they move.
  • Timing shifts too: when predators and prey shift at different rates, food chains can fall out of step.
Quick check

Mission 3

Tundra case study: impacts now

  • Region: the Arctic tundra of North America, Fennoscandia and Siberia.
  • Greening overall across the tundra; some areas browning (lower productivity).
  • Arctic June snow cover on land shrank 13.4 ± 5.4% per decade (1967–2018); permafrost is at record temperatures.
  • More wildfire and abrupt thaw have changed disturbance, with positive and negative impacts on wildlife such as reindeer and salmon.
  • Swedish Saami reindeer herders report thawing ground and "rotten spring vegetation" in summer grazing areas since the mid-1990s.
Mission 3

Tundra case study: potential impacts

  • Shrubs and trees projected to cover 24–52% of Arctic tundra by 2050.
  • High-Arctic species with few refuges risk being outcompeted by temperate species: a loss of globally unique biodiversity.
  • The boreal forest is projected to spread north at its northern edge and shrink at its southern edge.
  • About 20% of Arctic land permafrost is vulnerable to abrupt thaw and ground subsidence; wildfire projected to increase.
  • Exam move ("Evaluate"): separate observed (high confidence) from projected (medium confidence) impacts, and who gains or loses.
Quick check
?Which statement about the tundra is best supported by the IPCC?
Mission 3

Limits to adapting

  • On land, 3–14% of species assessed face very high extinction risk at 1.5 °C; 3–18% at 2 °C.
  • "Hard limits to adaptation have been reached in some ecosystems".
  • Most at risk: species that cannot move (corals) or have nowhere left to go (mountain tops, polar edges).
Mission 3

Farms: winners and losers

  • IPCC: climate change has slowed the growth of farm productivity over 50 years globally.
  • Studies that separate out climate change find it has lowered yields of some crops (maize, wheat) in many lower-latitude regions and raised yields of some (maize, wheat, sugar beet) in many higher-latitude regions.
  • Africa's pastoral systems show lower animal growth and productivity.
  • Higher CO₂ can lower crops' nutritional quality.

Mission check

?A snow-dependent alpine plant grows near a mountain summit. With continued warming, what is most likely, and why?

Living things follow their climate zone: upper-ocean species have moved poleward about 52 km a decade; on land, biomes shift (tundra greening, shrubs spreading, boreal forest moving north). Where they cannot move (corals, summits), they decline. Farming has winners and losers: higher-latitude gains, lower-latitude losses.

Mission 4

Case study: Alaska's North Slope

  • Alaska's Arctic coastal plain, north of the Brooks Range.
  • Permafrost underlies about 85% of Alaska.
  • Utqiaġvik (1991–2020): annual mean −10.2 °C; 137 mm of precipitation (our conversions).
  • Oil was discovered at Prudhoe Bay in 1968; the Trans-Alaska Pipeline (TAPS) was completed in 1977.
  • Your regional case study of a tundra ecosystem under climate change.
Mission 4

Carbon stores

  • Permafrost soils: Arctic and boreal permafrost hold 1,460–1,600 Gt of organic carbon, "almost twice the carbon in the atmosphere".
  • NOAA: at least 1.4–1.6 trillion tonnes of carbon have accumulated in the region's soils and permafrost.
  • Why so much? Plants grow in summer; when they die, cold and frozen ground stop decay: "Once frozen, the decay of organic matter stops".
  • Vegetation: low mosses, sedges, lichens and shrubs: a small store compared with the soil.
Mission 4

Carbon flows and seasons

  • Summer (short growing season): photosynthesis takes CO₂ in. Warmer recent summers brought more uptake.
  • Non-growing season: microbes keep respiring in unfrozen soil; this CO₂ has "offset growing season uptake".
  • Result: the tundra region has shifted from a CO₂ sink "to a small CO₂ source".
  • Methane: the permafrost region releases 15–39 Tg CH₄-C a year (2000–20).
Quick check
?Thawed tundra soil that stays waterlogged releases methane rather than carbon dioxide. Why?
Mission 4

Climate change: actual impacts

  • The Arctic warmed nearly four times faster than the globe over 43 years.
  • 2024: permafrost was the warmest on record at 9 of 20 Alaskan monitoring sites; colder permafrost in northern Alaska warmed 0.3–0.7 °C per decade over four decades.
  • Greening: tundra greenness 2024 was the second highest in the satellite record; northern Alaska "much higher than normal".
  • Shrubification: more, taller willows, birches and alders; shrubs "crowd out low-growing tundra plants, particularly lichens", a key caribou food.
  • Carbon: the tundra region is now a small net CO₂ source.
Mission 4

Climate change: potential impacts

  • Woody shrubs and trees could cover 24–52% of Arctic tundra by 2050.
  • Some High-Arctic species with few refuges may be outcompeted by temperate species.
  • Near-surface permafrost area could fall by 2–66% (low emissions, RCP2.6) or 30–99% (high emissions, RCP8.5) by 2100.
  • Loss of permafrost carbon after thaw is irreversible at centennial time scales (high confidence).
  • Infrastructure: about 550 km of TAPS lies where near-surface permafrost may thaw by 2050.
  • Exam move ("Evaluate"): weigh observed change (measured, high confidence) against projections (ranges that depend on emissions). Name who gains (shrubs, boreal species) and who loses (lichens, caribou, High-Arctic species).
Quick check
?Which is an actual (observed) impact of climate change on the tundra, rather than a potential one?
Mission check

?At a Siberian tundra site, measured summer CO₂ uptake has risen each decade, yet the site's yearly carbon balance has turned into a net source. Which finding would best explain this?

Cold has kept the tundra's huge soil carbon store locked up. Warming lengthens the unfrozen season, so microbes release more CO₂ (and methane where soils are waterlogged) than the extra plant growth takes in.

Mission 5

What is an ecosystem?

  • An ecosystem: living things and their non-living surroundings, linked by flows of energy and matter.
  • Biotic components: plants, animals, fungi, bacteria.
  • Abiotic components: climate, water, rock, soil, relief.
  • Change one component and the others respond.
Mission 5

The ecosystem as a system

  • Inputs: solar energy; water; nutrients from rainfall and weathered rock.
  • Stores: living biomass, dead litter, soil.
  • Transfers: photosynthesis, feeding, litter fall, decomposition, uptake by roots.
  • Outputs: heat; water lost by evapotranspiration and runoff; nutrients leached out of the soil.
  • Energy passes through the system; nutrients cycle within it.
Mission 5

Trophic levels

  • Trophic level: a feeding level in an ecosystem.
  • Level 1: producers (autotrophs): green plants fixing the Sun's energy by photosynthesis.
  • Level 2 and above: consumers (heterotrophs): herbivores (primary consumers), then carnivores (secondary, tertiary).
  • Decomposers (fungi, bacteria) feed on dead matter at every level and release nutrients.
  • Food chain: one feeding path. Food web: the interlocking chains of a real ecosystem.
  • At each level much energy is lost as heat in respiration, so energy flows one way and chains are short. The trophic-level idea comes from Lindeman (1942).
Quick check
?In a woodland, fungi break down fallen oak leaves. What is their role?
Mission 5

Ecological pyramids

  • Pyramid of numbers: organisms at each level. Can be inverted (one oak tree supports many caterpillars).
  • Pyramid of biomass: mass of living material at each level.
  • Pyramid of energy: energy passing through each level. Always upright, because energy is lost at every step.
Quick check
?Model food chain: producers fix 10,000 kJ. Assume 10% passes to each next level (a model value). How much reaches the secondary consumers?
Mission 5

Nutrient cycling

  • Nutrients (e.g. nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium) cycle between three stores: biomass, litter, soil.
  • Transfers: litter fall (biomass → litter); decomposition (litter → soil); uptake by roots (soil → biomass).
  • Inputs: dissolved in rainfall; released by weathering of rock.
  • Outputs: leaching in drainage water; runoff; removal of plants (harvest, fire).
Mission 5

The Gersmehl model

  • Gersmehl's nutrient cycling model (1976): three circles (biomass, litter, soil) sized by the store; arrows sized by the flow.
  • Tropical rainforest: biomass is the largest store; litter is small because warm, wet conditions make decay fast; heavy rain leaches the soil.
  • Taiga: litter is large because cold slows decay; flows are small.
  • Temperate grassland: soil is the largest store; grasses die back each year into thick, fertile soil.
Quick check
?A Gersmehl diagram shows a large litter circle, small biomass and soil circles, and thin arrows. Which ecosystem fits?
Mission check

?A temperate grassland is ploughed and cropped every year, with the crop taken away. After decades, yields fall unless fertiliser is added. Which explanation is best?

Where nutrients are stored decides how an ecosystem responds to use. Grassland's large soil store lasts decades; a rainforest's riches are in its biomass, so clearing removes them quickly and heavy rain leaches the rest.

Mission 6

The case: Murlough, County Down

  • A dune system on Dundrum Bay, estimated at up to 6,000 years old.
  • Owned by the National Trust; managed as Ireland's first nature reserve since 1967.
  • Protected as an ASSI and a Special Area of Conservation.
  • "The largest and most important dune heath site in Northern Ireland".
Mission 6

Characteristics: a complete sequence

  • From the sea inland: foredunes → yellow dunes → grey dunes → dune grassland → acid dune heath and scrub → saltmarsh.
  • Older dunes are leached: calcium removed, soil more acidic, so heather and bell heather dominate.
  • Rich in insects: 21 butterfly species (71% of Northern Ireland's), 213 moth species (48%).
  • Home to the marsh fritillary, a butterfly "threatened throughout Europe".
Mission 6

Abiotic, biotic and the system

  • Abiotic: mobile and fixed sand, wind, salt spray, leached acidic soil, a fairly dry climate (possibly a rain shadow of the Mourne Mountains).
  • Biotic: marram, heather, grasses, insects, birds, rabbits, grazing cattle and ponies.
  • Inputs: solar energy, rain, blown sand, salt spray. Outputs: heat, leached nutrients, wind-blown sand.
  • Stores: biomass, litter, soil. Transfers: photosynthesis, feeding, litter fall, decomposition, uptake.
Mission 6

Trophic structure at Murlough

  • Autotrophs (producers): marram, heather, devil's-bit scabious.
  • Heterotrophs: marsh fritillary caterpillars eat devil's-bit scabious (their larval food plant); insects feed birds such as meadow pipits.
  • Decomposers: fungi and bacteria break down litter.
  • Pyramids: energy is lost as heat at each level, so each level holds less energy and usually less biomass than the one below.
Mission 6

Nutrient cycling: Gersmehl at Murlough

  • Gersmehl's model: three stores (biomass, litter, soil), sized by amount, linked by flows.
  • On old dunes, leaching has removed much of the calcium: a large output from the soil and a small soil store.
  • Little input is likely from weathering of the already leached sand.
  • So heathland plants must be adapted to low-nutrient, acidic soil.
Quick check
?In a Gersmehl diagram for Murlough's dune heath, which feature would you expect?
Mission 6

Adaptations to climate, soil and soil moisture

  • Marram thrives where sand is moving and dominates dune building; shifting dunes need active sand movement.
  • Sand couch and lyme-grass are "salt-tolerant, sand-binding grasses".
  • Dune soils drain freely; Murlough "is rather dry" and wet slack communities "are not well developed".
  • Heather dominates the acidic, leached older dunes.
  • Fauna: heat-loving insects need bare sand that warms quickly; solitary bees and wasps dig nesting burrows in it.
Quick check
?Why are patches of bare sand important for Murlough's insects?
Mission 6

Local factors in ecological change

  • Planned introduction: sea buckthorn, "not native to Ireland", introduced by the Downshire family in the 1890s to stabilise the dunes.
    • Its thorny thickets shade out native plants; in storms it can leave "a sheer cliff rather than a smooth slope".
    • Only sycamore survives its shade; "sycamore woodland now stands where once had been sand dune".
  • Agriculture (grazing): without grazing, "Bracken and coarse grasses can quickly take over and ultimately woody species may become dominant".
  • Unplanned introductions: species that arrive by accident rather than by planting.
  • Urban and recreational change: cleaning amenity beaches removes the strandline that traps sand for new dunes; heavy trampling causes "loss of cover and soil erosion".
Mission 6

Impacts: how is Murlough doing?

  • DAERA 2025/26 condition data for Murlough ASSI:
    • all five dune habitat features and the saltmarsh: unfavourable (last assessed 2011);
    • rare dune plants such as carline thistle and viper's-bugloss: unfavourable (2022);
    • grass/heath and coastal moth invertebrate assemblages: favourable (2022).
  • Designation has not kept every feature in good condition.
Mission 6

Management and conservation

  • Grazing: low-intensity grazing by cattle and ponies keeps open areas and a diverse sward; rabbits help keep the sward short.
  • Holding succession at the heath stage by management creates a plagioclimax.
  • Removing the introduction: a five-year project, part-funded by NIEA, to clear about 4 ha of sea buckthorn, working in winter before bird nesting.
  • Access: boardwalk restoration to protect the dunes; "sensitive management of access" where trampling prevents recovery.
  • Law: as an ASSI, changes in grazing and burning need consent.
Quick check
?Why does the reserve use grazing rather than leaving the dunes untouched?
Mission check

?A town-edge heathland has rhododendron (planted on a Victorian estate) spreading into it, birch saplings invading since grazing stopped, and a popular path widening into bare ground. Which plan best fits the Murlough evidence?

At Murlough, sea buckthorn planted in the 1890s to fix the dunes became an invasive that shades out native species, so it is being removed; grazing, access management and ASSI protection keep the dune heath as a managed plagioclimax.

  • AS1.2b.ii plant succession: seral stages, climatic climax and plagioclimax vegetation (small or regional scale case study)
Mission 7

Succession

  • Succession: the change in a plant community over time at one site.
  • Sere: the whole sequence; each step is a seral stage.
  • Pioneer species colonise first; each stage alters the site so others can follow.
  • Pioneers are low plants that can live with little or no soil. A UK team studying bare chalk found that short bryophytes (mosses and their relatives) that often produce spores were more abundant early in succession, reflecting their "high colonising abilities".
  • Primary succession starts on a surface with no soil (bare rock, open water, new sand). Secondary succession starts where soil remains (abandoned field, felled wood).
  • Climatic climax: the final, stable community in balance with the regional climate.
Mission 7

Why stages replace each other

  • Pioneers trap dust, add dead matter and help weather the surface: soil begins and deepens.
  • Deeper soil holds more water and nutrients; taller plants then shade out the pioneers.
  • Over the sere: biomass, height, soil depth and usually species diversity increase.
  • Competition decides which species win at each stage; the climax species are the best competitors in that climate.

?Why are low plants such as mosses usually among the first colonisers of bare rock?
Mission 7

Climatic climax: temperate deciduous woodland

  • In most of lowland Britain and Ireland, seres on bare rock and in open water both end in temperate deciduous woodland, dominated by oak (with ash, beech, hazel). The climate decides the end point, not the starting surface.
  • Climate (Oxford, 1991–2020; our calculation from Met Office station data):
  • January mean about 5 °C; July about 18 °C.
  • About 680 mm of rain a year, spread through the year (monthly means 43–73 mm).
  • Mild, moist conditions give a long growing season; cold winters make shedding leaves the best strategy.
Mission 7

Woodland structure, soil and life

  • Layers: canopy (oak, ash, beech); shrub layer (hazel, holly, hawthorn); field layer (bluebell, bramble, ferns); ground layer (mosses).
  • Soil: brown earth: leaf litter decays within a year or so; earthworms mix humus into a fertile, well-drained soil.
  • Fauna: many insects on oak; birds (woodpeckers, tits); mammals such as badgers and deer.
  • Biomass is mostly in the trees; nutrients cycle quickly through litter and soil.
Quick check
?Why do many woodland flowers, such as bluebells, flower in early spring?
Mission 7

Case study: Umbra sand dunes

  • Umbra, near Limavady, Co. Londonderry: a 45 ha nature reserve, part of an ASSI and a Special Area of Conservation.
  • Habitats: "species-rich dune grassland, wet dune slacks, scrub and woodland"; many orchids.
  • A psammosere (sere on sand). Near the beach, sand couch and lyme grass form low embryo dunes.
  • Mobile (yellow) dunes: dominated by "sand-binding marram".
  • Fixed (grey) dunes further inland: the vegetation "replaces marram as the dune stabilises and the organic content of the sand increases"; short turf of red fescue.
  • In the wider Magilligan dune system, fixed-dune vegetation "dominates most of the site".
Mission 7

Plagioclimax at Umbra

  • Left alone, the fixed dunes would move on to scrub and then woodland, towards the climatic climax.
  • Ulster Wildlife grazes the reserve from August to April and controls scrub, removing non-native sea buckthorn.
  • This holds the site at species-rich dune grassland: a plagioclimax, a stage where human activity stops succession short of the climatic climax.
  • Grazing animals keep the turf short; on other dune sites rabbits do this, "aided by sheep and cattle".
  • Exam move: name the stage, the process that would follow, and the human action that arrests it.

Mission check

?Two identical bare-chalk quarries are left alone for 300 years: one in lowland England, one in Arctic tundra far beyond the tree line. They end with very different vegetation. Why?

The starting surface sets the sere, but the regional climate sets the end point. In lowland Britain and Ireland that end point is temperate deciduous woodland, unless people or local conditions hold it back.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A biome is a global-scale ecosystem defined by its climax vegetation, set mainly by climate.
  • Tropical rainforest near the equator; hot deserts under sinking air near 15°–30°; temperate grasslands in mid-latitude continental interiors; tundra beyond the Arctic treeline and on high mountains.

Mission 2

  • Tundra: very cold (eight months below 0 °C at Utqiaġvik), dry (137 mm), short cool summer; gleyed, cryoturbated soils, waterlogged above permafrost, with slow decay.
  • Temperate grassland: continental (26 °C annual range at Dodge City), ~560 mm with a summer peak; chernozem: deep black humus, little leaching, base-rich, carbonate lower down.

Mission 3

  • Tundra: observed greening, snow loss, fire and thaw; projected shrub spread (24–52% by 2050), boreal advance, loss of High-Arctic species.

Mission 4

  • Actual: record permafrost warmth, greening and shrubs, net CO₂ source. Potential: 24–52% shrub/tree cover by 2050, large permafrost loss by 2100, irreversible carbon loss.

Mission 5

  • Ecosystems are systems: energy flows one way through trophic levels (autotrophs → heterotrophs, with decomposers), while nutrients cycle between biomass, litter and soil.
  • Pyramids show numbers, biomass and energy by trophic level; the Gersmehl model compares nutrient stores and flows between biomes.

Mission 6

  • Murlough: a dune system up to 6,000 years old with a full dune sequence (a psammosere) from foredunes to acid dune heath; rich insect fauna (71% of NI butterflies); dry, leached, low-nutrient sands.
  • Change: planned introduction (sea buckthorn, 1890s), grazing change (bracken and scrub), beach cleaning and trampling. Management: grazing, buckthorn removal (4 ha), boardwalks, ASSI consent.
  • Functioning: abiotic/biotic, inputs/outputs/stores/transfers; trophic chain devil's-bit scabious → marsh fritillary → birds; Gersmehl: small soil store, large leaching output.

Mission 7

  • Seres move through seral stages from pioneers to a climatic climax; each stage changes soil, light and water so new species win.
  • Umbra dunes: embryo → mobile (marram) → fixed dune grassland, held as a plagioclimax by grazing and scrub control.
