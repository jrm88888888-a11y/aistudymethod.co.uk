Mission 1: Why would someone Christians call sinless queue up to be baptised for forgiveness?

Make a guess: Why would someone Christians call sinless queue up to be baptised for forgiveness?

Mission 1

The messenger in the wilderness

  • Mark opens with prophecy: "Behold, I send my messenger before your face, who will prepare your way before you" (Mark 1:2, echoing Malachi 3:1) and "the voice of one crying in the wilderness" (Mark 1:3, echoing Isaiah 40:3).
  • John the Baptist is that messenger: he prepares people for someone greater.
  • He preached "the baptism of repentance for forgiveness of sins" (Mark 1:4). Repentance means turning away from sin and back to God.
  • Crowds from Judea and Jerusalem came to the River Jordan, confessing their sins (1:5).
  • Clothing and food: camel's hair, a leather belt, locusts and wild honey (1:6). The belt recalls the prophet Elijah (2 Kings 1:8), whom Jews expected to return before God's day.

Someone mightier

"After me comes he who is mightier than I, the strap of whose sandals I am not worthy to stoop down and loosen. I baptised you in water, but he will baptise you in the Holy Spirit." (Mark 1:7–8)

  • Untying sandals was a slave's job: John says he is not fit even for that.

Mission 1

The baptism of Jesus

  • Jesus came from Nazareth in Galilee and was baptised by John in the Jordan (1:9).
  • Coming up from the water, "he saw the heavens parting and the Spirit descending on him like a dove." (Mark 1:10)
  • A voice: "You are my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased." (Mark 1:11)
  • In Mark, Jesus himself sees and hears: it is addressed to him.
  • The words echo Psalm 2:7 (the king as God's son) and Isaiah 42:1 (God's chosen servant, on whom God puts his Spirit).
Mission 1

What the baptism shows

  • For Christians, the baptism shows:
  • Identity: Jesus is God's Son, approved by God.
  • Anointing: the Spirit comes on him; "Messiah" means "anointed one".
  • The start of the ministry: from here Jesus begins preaching (1:14–15).
  • The Trinity: Christians see Father (voice), Son (Jesus) and Holy Spirit (dove) together.
  • Solidarity: Christians believe Jesus was sinless, yet stood in line with sinners: he identifies with the people he came to save.
  • Example: he shows his followers the way of baptism.
Mission 1Diversity

The baptism for Christians today

  • Most churches baptise "in the name of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit", using water.
  • Infant baptism (Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican, Methodist): grace is God's gift before a person can choose; parents and godparents make promises.
  • Believer's baptism (Baptists, many Pentecostals and independent churches): only those old enough to repent and believe, often by full immersion, like Jesus in the Jordan.
  • Orthodox Christians celebrate the baptism at Theophany (6 January) with the blessing of waters.
  • Quakers and the Salvation Army do not baptise with water; they stress inner baptism by the Spirit (Mark 1:8).

Mission 1

Matthew's version

  • John warns Pharisees and Sadducees: "You offspring of vipers, who warned you to flee from the wrath to come?" (Matthew 3:7) Ancestry is not enough: they must bear good fruit.
  • John protests: "I need to be baptised by you, and you come to me?" (Matthew 3:14)
  • Jesus: "this is the fitting way for us to fulfil all righteousness" (Matthew 3:15): doing all God asks.
  • The voice speaks to everyone: "This is my beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased." (Matthew 3:17)
  • Three temptations (Matthew 4:1–11): turn stones into bread; throw himself from the Temple; worship the devil for the kingdoms of the world.
  • Jesus answers each from Deuteronomy: "Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceeds out of God's mouth." (Matthew 4:4)
Mission 1

The temptation

  • "Immediately the Spirit drove him out into the wilderness." (Mark 1:12)
  • Forty days, tempted by Satan, with the wild animals; angels served him (1:13).
  • Forty recalls Israel's forty years in the desert and the forty days of Moses (Exodus 34:28) and Elijah (1 Kings 19:8).
  • Mark gives no details and no result: Jesus' whole ministry becomes the battle with evil.
  • Significance: Jesus faces real temptation, so Christians believe he understands theirs: "one who has been in all points tempted like we are, yet without sin" (Hebrews 4:15). Many Christians keep Lent, forty days of prayer and self-denial.
Mission 1Evaluate

"The baptism proves that Jesus is the Son of God."

  • Reasons for: a voice from heaven names him as Son; the Spirit descends; John, a prophet, says someone greater is coming; Christians across traditions read this as God's own testimony.
  • Reasons against (non-religious, e.g. atheist, Humanist): in Mark it is Jesus who sees the Spirit and the voice speaks to him, so it reads as a private experience that others cannot check; the account was written decades later by believers; a dove and a voice could be later symbolic language.
  • Christian responses: the story is a revelation to be received in faith, not a scientific proof; its meaning is confirmed by Jesus' whole life, death and resurrection; the centurion's words at the cross, "Truly this man was the Son of God!" (15:39), echo it.
  • Judge it by: What would count as "proof" of a religious claim? Is a private experience evidence for others? Does the story aim to prove or to proclaim?
Mission 1Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain two ways the baptism of Jesus is significant for Christians today.

  • Way 1: it shows who Jesus is. Source: "You are my beloved Son" (Mark 1:11). Development: Christians worship Jesus as the Son of God, the second person of the Trinity.
  • Way 2: it is an example to follow. Development: Christians are baptised as Jesus was; Baptists do this by full immersion as believers, while Catholics baptise infants into God's family.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Messiah, Son of Man, Son of God: why did Jesus' followers need so many names for him?

Make a guess: Messiah, Son of Man, Son of God: why did Jesus' followers need so many names for him?

Mission 2

What Jews hoped for

  • Messiah (Hebrew) and Christ (Greek) both mean "anointed one": someone set apart by God, as kings and priests were anointed with oil.
  • Under Roman rule, many Jews hoped God would send a Messiah. Hopes varied:
  • a king from David's family who would drive out the Romans and restore Israel;
  • a priestly leader (some groups, such as the Dead Sea Scrolls community);
  • a prophet like Moses (Deuteronomy 18:15).
  • Most expected a human leader sent by God, not a Messiah who would suffer and die.
Mission 2

Son of David

  • God promised King David: "I will establish the throne of his kingdom forever." (2 Samuel 7:13)
  • So "Son of David" meant the royal Messiah: a king of David's line.
  • Blind Bartimaeus calls out: "Jesus, you son of David, have mercy on me!" (Mark 10:47)
  • At the entry into Jerusalem the crowd shouts about "the kingdom of our father David" (11:10).
  • Jesus himself questions the title: David calls the Messiah "Lord", so he must be more than David's son (12:35–37).
  • Today: Christians see Jesus as fulfilling God's promises to Israel; but his kingdom is not political.
Mission 2

Son of Man

  • Jesus' favourite name for himself in Mark; others never use it of him.
  • In Hebrew idiom "son of man" can simply mean "a human being" (God addresses Ezekiel as "Son of man", Ezekiel 2:1).
  • In Daniel's vision it is a heavenly figure: "there came with the clouds of the sky one like a son of man" (Daniel 7:13), given everlasting dominion (7:14).
  • In Mark, Jesus uses it in three ways:
  • Authority now: "the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins" (Mark 2:10).
  • Suffering: he "must suffer many things… and be killed, and after three days rise again" (8:31).
  • Future glory: "coming with the clouds of the sky" (14:62).
Mission 2Read the source

The third passion prediction

"Behold, we are going up to Jerusalem. The Son of Man will be delivered to the chief priests and the scribes. They will condemn him to death, and will deliver him to the Gentiles. They will mock him, spit on him, scourge him, and kill him. On the third day he will rise again." (Mark 10:33–34)

  • This is the third prediction (after 8:31 and 9:31). The disciples were "amazed" and "afraid" (10:32).

Mission 2

The suffering servant (Isaiah 53)

  • Isaiah 52:13–53:12 describes God's servant who suffers for others:
  • "But he was pierced for our transgressions. He was crushed for our iniquities. The punishment that brought our peace was on him; and by his wounds we are healed." (Isaiah 53:5)
  • Christians read this as a prophecy of Jesus' death for the sins of others; Mark echoes it: the Son of Man came "to give his life as a ransom for many" (10:45; compare Isaiah 53:12).
  • Jews usually read the servant as the people of Israel suffering among the nations ("You are my servant, Israel", Isaiah 49:3), and do not see a suffering Messiah there.
Mission 2

Son of God

  • 1st-century Jewish meaning: God's people (Israel is "my son", Exodus 4:22), the king ("You are my son", Psalm 2:7), or a righteous person close to God. Not usually a divine being.
  • In Mark the title frames the Gospel: the opening (1:1), the voice at the baptism (1:11) and transfiguration (9:7), the unclean spirits (3:11), the high priest's question (14:61–62) and the centurion: "Truly this man was the Son of God!" (Mark 15:39)
  • In Mark it is a Gentile soldier who says it at the cross: for Mark, Jesus is shown to be Son of God most clearly in his death.
  • Christians today: Jesus is the eternal Son, "true God from true God" (Nicene Creed, 325/381), the second person of the Trinity.

Mission 2

Saviour

  • The name Jesus (Hebrew Yeshua) means "the LORD saves".
  • "You shall name him Jesus, for it is he who shall save his people from their sins." (Matthew 1:21)
  • "For there is born to you today, in David's city, a Saviour, who is Christ the Lord." (Luke 2:11)
  • Saviour means he rescues people from sin and death and restores them to God.
  • For Christians today, this title is at the heart of worship and evangelism: "Jesus saves".
Mission 2Diversity

What the titles mean today

  • Many Christians today read the titles like this:
  • Son of Man: Jesus shared fully in human life and suffering, and will return as judge (the creeds: "he will come again in glory").
  • Son of God: Catholic, Orthodox and most Protestant Christians: Jesus is fully divine. Some liberal Christians read it as Jesus' unique closeness to God.
  • Christ / Messiah: Jesus as king; Catholics keep the feast of Christ the King. But his kingship is service (10:45), a model for Christian leaders.
  • Son of David: God keeps his promises; Christians read the Old Testament as pointing to Jesus.
  • Jesus' own understanding reshapes each title: power through suffering and service. This calls 21st-century Christians to serve rather than dominate.
  • Other views: Jews do not accept Jesus as Messiah (no age of peace has come); many non-religious scholars think the higher titles grew in the early Church.
Mission 2Evaluate

"'Son of Man' is the most important title for understanding Jesus."

  • Reasons for: it is the title Jesus chose for himself; it holds together his authority, suffering and future glory; it avoids political misunderstanding.
  • Reasons against: "Son of God" frames the whole Gospel (1:1; 15:39) and is central in the creeds; "Christ" is so important it became part of his name; for many Christians "Saviour" says most about what he does for them.
  • Another view: no title is enough alone; each corrects the others (e.g. Messiah redefined by Son of Man).
  • Judge it by: Whose understanding counts most: Jesus', Mark's, or the Church's? Which title best explains the cross?

Mission 3: Why does Matthew tell Jesus' birth through dreams, a star and a jealous king?

Make a guess: Why does Matthew tell Jesus' birth through dreams, a star and a jealous king?

Mission 3

Matthew's way in

  • Matthew writes for a mainly Jewish-Christian audience.
  • His Gospel opens: "The book of the genealogy of Jesus Christ, the son of David, the son of Abraham." (Matthew 1:1)
  • His aim: show that Jesus is the Messiah the Jewish scriptures promised.
  • Again and again he says an event happened "that it might be fulfilled" what a prophet said. Chapters 1–2 alone link to five prophecies.
  • Luke tells the birth from Mary's side (shepherds, manger). Matthew tells it from Joseph's side (dreams, wise men, Herod).
Mission 3

Joseph's dilemma (1:18–25)

  • Mary is engaged (betrothed) to Joseph. In Jewish law this was a binding agreement: ending it needed a divorce.
  • She is "found pregnant by the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 1:18).
  • Joseph is "a righteous man" (1:19): he plans to end it quietly, to spare her public shame.
  • An angel speaks to him in a dream: take Mary as your wife; the child is from the Holy Spirit.
  • He must name the child Jesus ("the LORD saves"), "for it is he who shall save his people from their sins." (Matthew 1:21)
  • Joseph obeys at once. By naming the child he adopts him into David's family line.
Mission 3

"God with us"

  • Matthew quotes Isaiah: "Behold, the virgin shall be with child, and shall give birth to a son. They shall call his name Immanuel." (Matthew 1:23)
  • Immanuel means "God with us".
  • The same promise closes the Gospel: "I am with you always" (Matthew 28:20).
  • For Christians this frames the Incarnation: God becomes human in Jesus.
  • Mary conceives by the Holy Spirit, without a human father: the virginal conception (usually called the virgin birth).

Mission 3

Visitors from the East (2:1–12)

  • "Wise men" (Greek magoi: Magi) arrive from the East: probably astrologers or priestly scholars from Persia or Babylon.
  • They are Gentiles (non-Jews). Matthew does not say how many; "three" comes later, from the three gifts.
  • They follow a star and ask for the newborn "King of the Jews".
  • King Herod asks the chief priests and scribes where the Christ will be born: Bethlehem, as the prophet Micah said.
  • They worship the child and offer gold, frankincense and myrrh.
  • Warned in a dream, they go home "another way" (2:12).

Mission 3

What the gifts mean

  • A traditional Christian reading, found in early writers (e.g. Irenaeus, c. 180) and in carols:
  • Gold: a gift for a king.
  • Frankincense: incense burned in worship: a sign of God.
  • Myrrh: a spice used in burial: a sign of his death.
  • Matthew himself gives no meanings: these are later Christian interpretations.
  • Many Christians see the Gentile visitors as a sign that Jesus is for all nations, as the Gospel's last command says (28:19).
  • Western churches celebrate the visit at Epiphany (6 January): Jesus "shown" to the world.
Mission 3

Herod and the flight to Egypt (2:13–16)

  • An angel warns Joseph in a dream: flee to Egypt, because Herod will seek the child "to destroy him" (2:13).
  • They stay until Herod dies (4 BCE). Matthew: "Out of Egypt I called my son." (Matthew 2:15; Hosea 11:1)
  • Herod, "exceedingly angry", has all boys in and around Bethlehem aged two and under killed (2:16): the massacre of the innocents.
  • Echoes of Moses: a king kills Hebrew baby boys (Exodus 1:22); the chosen child survives. Matthew presents Jesus as a new Moses.
  • Hosea spoke of Israel as God's son; Matthew applies it to Jesus as Son of God.
Mission 3

The titles in the story

  • Son of David: Joseph, "son of David" (1:20), names and adopts him; born in Bethlehem, David's town.
  • Saviour: the name Jesus, "it is he who shall save his people from their sins" (1:21).
  • Messiah (Christ): Herod asks where "the Christ" will be born (2:4).
  • Son of God: conceived by the Holy Spirit; "Out of Egypt I called my son" (2:15).
  • King of the Jews: the wise men's title (2:2): a rival to Herod.
  • Today: Christians sing these titles at Christmas and Epiphany; Catholics, Orthodox and many Protestants say in the creed that he was "born of the Virgin Mary".

Mission 3Diversity

How Christians read the story

  • Most Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant Christians: the virginal conception happened; it shows Jesus is both human and divine.
  • Catholic and Orthodox: Mary remained a virgin all her life. "Until" (1:25) says nothing about afterwards; Jesus' "brothers" are cousins or Joseph's older children.
  • Many Protestants: Mary and Joseph had children later (Mark 6:3 names Jesus' brothers).
  • Some liberal Christians: the birth stories are symbolic: they teach who Jesus is, not biology.
  • Isaiah 7:14: the Hebrew word almah means "young woman"; the Greek translation Matthew used has "virgin". Jews read the verse as a sign to King Ahaz in Isaiah's own time.
  • History: no other ancient source records the massacre; Herod's known cruelty makes it plausible to some historians, unlikely to others.

Mission 4

A turning point at Caesarea Philippi

  • Caesarea Philippi: a city in the far north, near the source of the Jordan, with a shrine to the Greek god Pan and a temple honouring the Roman emperor.
  • A place of other gods and Roman power: a striking setting to ask who Jesus really is.
  • It falls near the middle of Mark. Before it, Jesus' power is shown; after it, the road leads to the cross in Jerusalem.
Mission 4Read the source

"Who do you say that I am?"

On the way, Jesus asks his disciples who people say he is. They answer: John the Baptiser, Elijah, or one of the prophets (Mark 8:27–28, paraphrase).

"But who do you say that I am?" Peter answered, "You are the Christ." (Mark 8:29)

Mission 4

The rebuke

  • Jesus "commanded them that they should tell no one about him." (Mark 8:30)
  • Then the first passion prediction: "the Son of Man must suffer many things… and be killed, and after three days rise again." (Mark 8:31)
  • Peter takes him aside and rebukes him (8:32).
  • Jesus: "Get behind me, Satan! For you have in mind not the things of God, but the things of men." (Mark 8:33)
  • Peter's title was right; his idea of the Messiah was wrong. He expected victory; Jesus teaches suffering, death and resurrection.
  • "Satan" here: Peter is tempting Jesus away from his path, as Satan did in the wilderness.

Mission 4

Matthew's version

  • Peter: "You are the Christ, the Son of the living God." (Matthew 16:16)
  • Jesus: "you are Peter, and on this rock I will build my assembly" (Matthew 16:18). "Peter" (Petros) means "rock".
  • He gives Peter "the keys of the Kingdom of Heaven" (16:19) and orders silence (16:20).
  • Catholic teaching: Peter is the rock on whom Christ built the Church; his successors, the Popes, hold his office.
  • Orthodox Christians honour Peter as first among the apostles, but see all bishops sharing in his faith.
  • Most Protestants: the "rock" is Peter's confession of faith (or Christ himself), not an office handed on.
Mission 4

The transfiguration

  • After six days Jesus takes Peter, James and John up a high mountain.
  • He is "changed into another form"; his clothes become dazzling white (9:2–3).
  • Elijah and Moses appear, talking with him (9:4).
  • Peter, afraid, offers to build three tents (9:5–6).
  • A cloud overshadows them; a voice: "This is my beloved Son. Listen to him." (Mark 9:7)
  • Suddenly they see only Jesus (9:8).
Mission 4

Why Moses and Elijah?

  • Moses = the Law: the lawgiver who received the Torah on Mount Sinai, where the cloud of God's glory covered the mountain (Exodus 24:16).
  • Elijah = the Prophets: the great prophet, who met God on Mount Horeb; expected to return: "I will send you Elijah the prophet before the great and terrible day of the LORD comes." (Malachi 4:5)
  • Together they stand for the whole Jewish scriptures. Christians believe Jesus fulfils both.
  • "Listen to him" recalls Moses' promise of a prophet like himself: "You shall listen to him." (Deuteronomy 18:15)
  • Coming down (9:9): tell no one until the Son of Man has risen.
  • (9:10) The disciples wonder what "rising from the dead" means.
  • (9:11–13) "Elijah has come": Jesus means John the Baptist, who suffered. Matthew says so plainly: "Then the disciples understood that he spoke to them of John the Baptiser." (Matthew 17:13)
Quick check

Mission 4

What the transfiguration shows

  • For Christians, the transfiguration shows:
  • Identity: God's own voice calls Jesus "my beloved Son", as at the baptism.
  • Glory: the three disciples glimpse his divine glory before the suffering ahead.
  • Authority: "Listen to him": his teaching, including about suffering, carries God's authority.
  • Link with Caesarea Philippi: Peter has just heard that the Messiah must suffer; now he sees that suffering is not the end.
  • Resurrection: the glory points ahead to Easter.
Mission 4Diversity

The transfiguration today

  • Orthodox Christians celebrate it as a major feast (6 August); they see in the light on the mountain God's own uncreated glory, which believers can share (theosis: becoming like God by grace).
  • Catholics also keep 6 August, and pray it as one of the Luminous Mysteries of the Rosary (added 2002).
  • Many Protestants stress "Listen to him": the authority of Jesus' words, and encouragement in hard times.
  • Christians across traditions see it as a "mountain-top" experience that prepares for the "valley" of service.
  • Peter's declaration today: each Christian is asked, "Who do you say that I am?", and is called to confess faith in Christ publicly.
Mission 4Evaluate

"The transfiguration proves that Jesus is the Messiah."

  • Reasons for: God's voice declares him Son; Moses and Elijah, the Law and Prophets, confirm him; three witnesses were present; it fits Jesus' resurrection.
  • Reasons against (non-religious, e.g. atheist, Humanist): a vision is a private experience; it may have been a dream, a mistake in a frightened state, or a story written later to express faith; three close followers are not neutral witnesses; visions occur in many religions with conflicting messages.
  • Christian responses: the transfiguration is not offered as proof but as revelation, received by faith; it is consistent with the whole story of Jesus, especially the resurrection; two of the three witnesses, James (Acts 12:2) and, by tradition, Peter, were later killed for their faith.
  • Judge it by: What counts as evidence for a religious claim? Can an experience be real but not provable? Did Mark intend it as proof or as proclamation?

Mission 5

Entry into Jerusalem

  • Near the Mount of Olives, Jesus sends two disciples for a young donkey "on which no one has sat" (Mark 11:2).
  • He rides into Jerusalem. People spread cloaks and branches on the road.
  • The crowd shout: "Hosanna! Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord! Blessed is the kingdom of our father David that is coming in the name of the Lord! Hosanna in the highest!" (Mark 11:9–10)
  • Hosanna: Hebrew "save us, please!", by then also a shout of praise.
  • He enters the Temple, looks around at everything, and leaves for Bethany (11:11).
  • Matthew 21:1–11: a donkey and a colt; the crowd: "Hosanna to the son of David!" (Matthew 21:9). The city asks, "Who is this?"; the crowds answer, "the prophet" (21:10–11).
Mission 5

What the entry shows

  • Zechariah 9:9: "your King comes to you… lowly, and riding on a donkey".
  • Matthew quotes it as fulfilled (21:4–5).
  • A king of peace: conquerors rode warhorses; a donkey signals humility.
  • "Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord" is from Psalm 118:26, sung by pilgrims at the great festivals.
  • The Son of David title is acted out publicly: in Mark, the "Messianic secret" begins to break.
  • Many in the crowd hoped for a king who would drive out Rome; Jesus' kingship will be shown on the cross.
  • Today: Palm Sunday begins Holy Week; many churches give out palm crosses and hold processions.

Mission 5

Temple, plot and Judas

  • Next day Jesus drives out traders and money changers from the Temple courts (Mark 11:15–19).
  • "Isn't it written, 'My house will be called a house of prayer for all the nations'? But you have made it a den of robbers!" (Mark 11:17)
  • Jesus joins two prophets: Isaiah 56:7 and Jeremiah 7:11.
  • The chief priests and scribes "sought how they might destroy him", because the crowds hung on his teaching (11:18).
  • Two days before Passover they plot to arrest him by stealth: "Not during the feast", for fear of a riot (Mark 14:1–2).
  • Judas Iscariot, one of the Twelve, goes to the chief priests; they promise money (14:10–11).
  • Matthew: "thirty pieces of silver" (26:15); the plot meets at the house of Caiaphas, the high priest (26:3).
  • Reasons for the arrest: the Temple action threatened the priests' authority and Roman order; huge Passover crowds; claims to be Messiah; conflict over the Law.
  • Judas' motive is not given: suggestions include money, disappointment that Jesus was not a political Messiah, or (Luke 22:3; John 13:27) Satan.
Mission 5

Passover

  • Passover (Pesach) remembers God freeing the Israelites from slavery in Egypt (Exodus 12).
  • The blood of a lamb on the doorposts: God passed over their homes when the firstborn of Egypt died (Exodus 12:13).
  • In Jesus' time, lambs were sacrificed at the Temple and eaten in Jerusalem by families and groups.
  • The meal: lamb, unleavened bread (no time for dough to rise), bitter herbs (slavery), cups of wine, the story retold, psalms of praise (the Hallel, Psalms 113–118).
  • Pilgrims flooded Jerusalem, and hopes of a new liberation ran high: a tense time for the Romans.
Mission 5

The meal and the betrayer

  • "On the first day of unleavened bread, when they sacrificed the Passover" (Mark 14:12), two disciples follow a man carrying a water jar to a furnished upper room.
  • At table, Jesus: "one of you will betray me—he who eats with me." (Mark 14:18)
  • Each asks, "Surely not I?" (14:19).
  • "The Son of Man goes as it is written about him, but woe to that man by whom the Son of Man is betrayed!" (14:21)
  • Betrayal by someone sharing your table was a deep breach of friendship.
  • Mark presents Jesus going knowingly: his death is part of God's purpose, yet Judas is still responsible.

Bread and cup

"As they were eating, Jesus took bread, and when he had blessed it, he broke it and gave to them, and said, 'Take, eat. This is my body.' He took the cup, and when he had given thanks, he gave to them. They all drank of it. He said to them, 'This is my blood of the new covenant, which is poured out for many.'" (Mark 14:22–24)

  • "I will no more drink of the fruit of the vine until that day when I drink it anew in God's Kingdom." (14:25)
  • They sing a hymn and go out to the Mount of Olives (14:26).

Mission 5

What the words meant

  • Body broken: his death is coming; he gives himself.
  • Blood of the covenant: at Sinai Moses sprinkled "the blood of the covenant" (Exodus 24:8); Jeremiah promised a new covenant (Jeremiah 31:31).
  • Poured out for many: a sacrifice for others (compare Mark 10:45, "a ransom for many"; Isaiah 53:12).
  • New Passover: as the lamb freed Israel from slavery, Christians believe Jesus' death frees people from sin.
  • Future hope: he will drink wine again "in God's Kingdom": a coming banquet.
  • Matthew adds "for the remission of sins" (Matthew 26:28).
  • Paul's earlier account adds "Do this in memory of me" (1 Corinthians 11:24): the basis of the Church's practice.

Mission 5

After the meal: the warning

  • On the way to the Mount of Olives Jesus predicts that all will fall away, quoting Zechariah 13:7: "I will strike the shepherd, and the sheep will be scattered" (Mark 14:27).
  • He promises to go before them to Galilee after he is raised (14:28).
  • Peter insists he will never deny him; Jesus predicts three denials before the rooster crows twice (14:29–31). All say the same.
  • Matthew 26:20–25: Judas asks "It isn't me, is it, Rabbi?"; Jesus answers, "You said it." (26:25)
  • Matthew 26:31–35 gives the same warning to Peter (rooster crows once).
  • The scene sets up Gethsemane and the denials.
Mission 5Diversity

What happens at the Eucharist?

  • Catholic: transubstantiation: at the consecration the bread and wine become Christ's body and blood in substance, while their appearance remains. The Mass makes present the one sacrifice of the cross.
  • Orthodox: a real change in the Divine Liturgy, called a holy mystery, without defining how.
  • Lutheran: Christ is truly present "in, with and under" the bread and wine (sacramental union).
  • Anglican: a range: many affirm a real, spiritual presence; some hold views close to Catholic or to memorial.
  • Reformed (Calvin): Christ is spiritually present and received by faith.
  • Memorial (Zwingli; many Baptists and other free churches): the bread and wine are symbols; the meal remembers Christ's death ("in memory of me").
  • Quakers and the Salvation Army do not use bread and wine: they seek inward communion with Christ.

Mission 5Evaluate

"At the Last Supper Jesus meant the bread and wine only as symbols."

  • Reasons for: Jesus was physically present holding the bread, so "is" may mean "represents" (as "I am the door", John 10:9); Paul says "in memory of me"; at a Passover meal every food was symbolic.
  • Reasons against: the words are plain: "This is my body"; John 6:55, "my flesh is food indeed"; the early Church (e.g. Ignatius of Antioch, c. 110) spoke of the Eucharist as Christ's flesh; Paul warns against eating "in a way unworthy of the Lord" (1 Corinthians 11:27).
  • Another view: "symbol" and "real" are not opposites: in Jewish thought, remembering makes a past saving event present.
  • Judge it by: How should "is" be read? How much weight do the early Church and tradition carry? Does the setting (Passover) favour one reading?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • John the Baptist is the messenger foretold by Malachi and Isaiah: he preaches repentance and points to one mightier than himself.
  • At the baptism the Spirit descends and the voice calls Jesus "my beloved Son": for Christians this reveals who he is, and his ministry begins.
  • Matthew adds John's protest, "to fulfil all righteousness", and three temptations each answered from scripture.

Mission 2

  • Messiah/Christ means "anointed"; many 1st-century Jews hoped for a royal Son of David to free Israel.
  • Jesus calls himself the Son of Man (Daniel 7:13): authority, suffering (10:33–34) and future glory; Mark frames him as Son of God (1:1; 15:39).
  • Saviour: "it is he who shall save his people from their sins" (Matthew 1:21).

Mission 3

  • Matthew tells the birth from Joseph's side: dreams, obedience, and the naming of Jesus ("he who shall save his people", 1:21).
  • Fulfilment texts (Isaiah 7:14; Micah on Bethlehem; Hosea 11:1) show Jesus as Messiah, Son of David, Son of God and Immanuel.
  • Gentile wise men worship the "King of the Jews"; Herod tries to destroy him.

Mission 4

  • At Caesarea Philippi Peter confesses "You are the Christ", but rebukes Jesus' prediction of suffering and is called "Satan".
  • At the transfiguration Jesus is changed in glory; Moses (the Law) and Elijah (the Prophets) appear; God says "This is my beloved Son. Listen to him."
  • In Matthew, Jesus calls Peter the rock; Catholics, Orthodox and Protestants interpret this differently.

Mission 5

  • Jesus enters Jerusalem on a donkey as a humble king (Zechariah 9:9); crowds shout "Hosanna" (Mark 11:1–11).
  • At a Passover meal he gives bread and wine as his body and his "blood of the new covenant" (Mark 14:22–24).
  • Catholics, Orthodox, Lutherans, Anglicans, Reformed and memorialist Christians understand his words in different ways.
  • The Temple action, the leaders' fear of the crowds and Judas' offer lead to the arrest.
