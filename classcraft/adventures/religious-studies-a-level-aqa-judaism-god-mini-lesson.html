Make a guess: Jews believe in one God who has no body. So how can anyone say what God is like?

Mission 1

One God, no other

  • Judaism is absolute monotheism: one God, with no partners, rivals or parts.
  • Jews say it twice a day in the Shema: God is one (Hebrew echad).
  • God alone is worthy of worship: no intermediaries, no idols (Exodus 20:3–5).
  • Ethical monotheism: the one God is also the source of justice and goodness, and demands them from people. Belief and conduct cannot be separated.
  • The phrase became central in 19th-century Jewish thought (e.g. Hermann Cohen, 1842–1918), but the idea runs through the prophets.
Mission 1Read the source

"The LORD is One"

> "HEAR, O ISRAEL: THE LORD OUR GOD, THE LORD IS ONE." (Deuteronomy 6:4, JPS 1917)

> "I am the first, and I am the last, and beside Me there is no God." (Isaiah 44:6, JPS 1917)

> "I form the light, and create darkness; I make peace, and create evil; I am the LORD, that doeth all these things." (Isaiah 45:7, JPS 1917)

Q1 What does Isaiah 44:6 add to "one"?

Show answer

God is eternal, first and last, and there is no other god at all.

Q2 How does Isaiah 45:7 rule out a second, evil power?

Show answer

One God is the source of light and darkness alike: no rival god of evil.

Mission 1

Creator, sustainer, judge

  • Jewish teaching describes God in these roles:
  • Creator: "IN THE beginning God created the heaven and the earth." (Genesis 1:1, JPS 1917) Most Jewish thinkers: creation from nothing.
  • Sustainer / controller: creation is not a one-off. The morning prayer praises God who "in his goodness renews every day, continually, the work of creation" (paraphrase).
  • Eternal: "from everlasting to everlasting, Thou art God" (Psalm 90:2). God does not change (Malachi 3:6).
  • Judge: God rewards and punishes: "shall not the judge of all the earth do justly?" (Genesis 18:25, JPS 1917).
  • Judgement is just and merciful; Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur are the "days of judgement".
Mission 1

No body, no likeness

  • Jews believe God is incorporeal: no body, no shape, no physical comparison.
  • At Sinai (Horeb) "ye saw no manner of form" (Deuteronomy 4:15, JPS 1917): so no image of God may be made.
  • Bible phrases like God's "hand" or "voice" are metaphors: "the Torah speaks in human language" (Talmud, Berakhot 31b, paraphrase), a principle Maimonides uses.
  • God is neither male nor female. Hebrew grammar uses "he", but this says nothing about God's nature (more in Mission 4).

Mission 1

What God is like: attributes

  • Jewish teaching describes God as:
  • Omnipotent: all-powerful; nothing is beyond God.
  • Omniscient: all-knowing, including human thoughts and the future.
  • Omnibenevolent / merciful: "merciful and gracious, long-suffering, and abundant in goodness and truth" (Exodus 34:6, JPS 1917): the start of the Thirteen Attributes of Mercy, recited on fast days and festivals.
  • Holy: "Holy, holy, holy, is the LORD of hosts" (Isaiah 6:3), set apart and pure.
  • Just and perfect: God's acts are right and complete.
  • God is creator and controller of all things.
  • Many Jewish thinkers (e.g. Maimonides) say such words describe what God does, not what God is in essence.
Mission 1

Maimonides' Thirteen Principles

  • Moses Maimonides (Rambam, 1138–1204), Commentary on the Mishnah, Sanhedrin chapter 10 (c. 1168).
  • About God (1–5): (1) God exists and is the cause of all; (2) God is one; (3) God has no body; (4) God is eternal (first and last); (5) only God may be worshipped.
  • Revelation (6–9): prophecy; Moses is the greatest prophet; the Torah is from God; it will not be changed.
  • Reward and the end (10–13): God knows human deeds; rewards and punishes; the Messiah will come; the dead will be raised.
  • Sung in Yigdal and recited as Ani Ma'amin ("I believe") in many prayer books.
  • Critics: Hasdai Crescas (c. 1340–1410/11) and Joseph Albo (Sefer ha-Ikkarim, c. 1425) counted fewer principles (Albo: three). Some argue Judaism stresses practice more than creed.
Mission 1

Maimonides: saying what God is not

  • The Guide for the Perplexed (c. 1190), Book I, chapters 50–60.
  • Human words describe things with bodies and limits; God has neither. So positive attributes risk limiting God.
  • Negative attributes (via negativa): we know God best by saying what God is not. "God is living" means "God is not lifeless"; "God is one" means "God has no parts and no equal".
  • Each denial brings us closer to the truth, like narrowing down what an unseen object is.
  • Attributes of action: words like "merciful" (Exodus 34:6) describe what God does, not God's essence.

Mission 1Diversity

One God, many understandings

  • Orthodox: God as in Maimonides' principles: personal, revealing the Torah, rewarding and punishing.
  • Kabbalah and Hasidism: God is Ein Sof ("without end"), beyond all thought, revealed through ten sefirot; some Hasidic teachers say nothing exists apart from God.
  • Reform: ethical monotheism is the essence of Judaism; God known through conscience and history more than through law.
  • Reconstructionist: Mordecai Kaplan (1881–1983) described God as "the power that makes for salvation": a force in nature and people, not a supernatural person.
  • Humanistic Judaism: Jewish identity without belief in God.
  • Orthodox, Reform and Reconstructionist Jews all recite the Shema; they read "one" differently.
Mission 1Evaluate

Is belief in one God the foundation of Judaism?

  • Claim: Monotheism is the essence and foundation of Judaism.
  • For: The Shema is the first prayer learned and the last said; Maimonides' first principles are about God.
  • For: Ethics depends on the one God: one God, one moral law for all people.
  • Against: Judaism stresses practice (halakhah) over creed; some argue there is no single required belief.
  • Against: Humanistic and many secular Jews keep Jewish identity without belief in God.
  • Against: Covenant, Torah and peoplehood may be as central as monotheism.
  • Judgement criteria: Essence for whom: the Torah, the rabbis, or Jews today? Is a foundation the same as the most important thing?

Mission 2

Maimonides writes a list

  • Moses ben Maimon, called Maimonides or Rambam (1138–1204): rabbi, legal scholar, philosopher, physician.
  • Born in Córdoba, Spain; his family fled persecution; he settled in Fustat (Old Cairo), Egypt.
  • In his Commentary on the Mishnah (Arabic, completed c. 1168) he reached Mishnah Sanhedrin 10:1: "All Israel have a share in the world to come" (paraphrase).
  • Who counts as "Israel" here? His answer: anyone who holds thirteen foundations (Hebrew yesodot) of faith.
  • They are known as the Thirteen Principles of Faith.
Mission 2

Thirteen principles, three groups

  • About God (1–5): God exists and causes all · is one · has no body · is eternal · alone is to be worshipped.
  • About revelation (6–9): prophecy · Moses the greatest prophet · the Torah from heaven · the Torah unchanging.
  • About God and humanity (10–13): God knows our deeds · reward and punishment · the Messiah · resurrection of the dead.
  • Principles 6–13: a later mission (see Mission 3).
Mission 2

Principles 1 and 2: God exists; God is one

  • 1. Existence: God exists, is perfect, and is the cause of everything else that exists. Everything depends on God; God depends on nothing.
  • Root: "I am the LORD thy God" (Exodus 20:2), which Maimonides counts as the first commandment: to know that God exists.
  • 2. Unity: God is one, but not one as a pair has one member, nor one as a species, nor one as a body made of parts (paraphrase).
  • God's unity is unique: nothing else is one in this way.
  • Root: "HEAR, O ISRAEL: THE LORD OUR GOD, THE LORD IS ONE." (Deuteronomy 6:4, JPS 1917)
  • This is Jewish monotheism at its strictest: one God, with no division at all.
Mission 2Read the source

Principle 3: no body

> "for ye saw no manner of form on the day that the LORD spoke unto you in Horeb out of the midst of the fire" (Deuteronomy 4:15, JPS 1917)

  • 3. Incorporeality: God has no body, no shape, and nothing that happens to bodies (moving, resting, being in a place).
  • So what about God's "hand" or "eyes"? Maimonides uses the Talmud's rule: "The Torah speaks in the language of human beings" (paraphrase).
  • These words are figurative: "hand" means power; "eyes" means knowledge.

Q1 What did the Israelites not see at Horeb (Sinai)?

Show answer

Any form: they heard a voice but saw no shape.

Q2 Why does the Torah still use body words for God?

Show answer

To speak in a way ordinary people understand.

Mission 2

Principles 4 and 5: eternal; worship God alone

  • 4. Eternity: God is first: before everything, without beginning. Later versions add "and last".
  • Roots: "The eternal God is a dwelling-place" (Deuteronomy 33:27, JPS 1917); "I am the first, and I am the last" (Isaiah 44:6, JPS 1917).
  • 5. Worship God alone: prayer and praise go to God only.
  • Not to angels, stars or forces of nature, and not through them as go-betweens.
  • Root: "Thou shalt have no other gods before Me." (Exodus 20:3, JPS 1917)
  • Principle 5 is about practice: what a Jew may do in prayer, not only what a Jew thinks.
Mission 2

Roots, development, living Judaism

  • Biblical roots: Exodus 20:2–3; Deuteronomy 4:15, 6:4, 33:27; Isaiah 44:6.
  • Maimonides' development: turns these into defined beliefs that mark who is inside Judaism; adds philosophical precision (unique unity; no body at all; negative attributes).
  • Living Judaism: the hymn Yigdal (c. 14th century) sings all thirteen at the start or end of services in many synagogues.
  • Ani Ma'amin ("I believe with perfect faith…"), printed in many prayer books after the morning service, turns each principle into a sentence of faith.
  • Principles 1–5 are the least disputed today: almost all Jewish movements affirm one God, beyond the world, to be worshipped alone.
  • AQA: link these to God as one, eternal, omniscient and omnipotent creator.

Mission 3

Principle 6: prophecy

  • 6. Prophecy: God communicates with chosen human beings, the prophets.
  • Root: "the Lord GOD will do nothing, but He revealeth His counsel unto His servants the prophets" (Amos 3:7, JPS 1917).
  • In the Guide (II.32–48) Maimonides explains prophecy as the highest human perfection: God's influence reaches a person's intellect and then their imagination.
  • A prophet must be wise and morally perfected; even then, God may withhold prophecy.
Mission 3Read the source

Principle 7: Moses

> "if there be a prophet among you, I the LORD do make Myself known unto him in a vision, I do speak with him in a dream. My servant Moses is not so; … with him do I speak mouth to mouth" (Numbers 12:6–8, JPS 1917)

  • 7. Moses is the greatest of all prophets, before or after him.
  • Maimonides lists four differences (paraphrase): Moses received prophecy awake, not in a dream; directly, not through an angel; without terror; and whenever he chose.
  • "And there hath not arisen a prophet since in Israel like unto Moses" (Deuteronomy 34:10, JPS 1917).

Q1 How does God speak to other prophets, according to Numbers 12?

Show answer

In a vision or a dream.

Q2 Why does principle 7 matter for principle 8?

Show answer

If Moses' prophecy is unique, no later prophet can overrule the Torah he received.

Mission 3

Principles 8 and 9: the Torah from heaven, unchanging

  • 8. Torah from heaven (Torah min ha-shamayim): the whole Torah we now have was given by God to Moses.
  • Every verse is equally divine: a list of Esau's descendants (Genesis 36) as much as "Hear, O Israel" (Maimonides' own example, paraphrase).
  • The Oral Torah, the explanation of the commandments, also comes from God.
  • 9. Unchanging: the Torah will not be replaced, and nothing will be added or taken away. Root: Deuteronomy 12:32 (13:1 in Hebrew numbering).
  • Context: many Christian and Muslim teachers of his time held that a later revelation completed or replaced the Torah; principle 9 denies this.

Mission 3

Principles 10 and 11: God knows; reward and punishment

  • 10. God knows human deeds and thoughts: "He that fashioneth the hearts of them all, that considereth all their doings" (Psalm 33:15, JPS 1917).
  • 11. Reward and punishment: God rewards those who keep the commandments and punishes those who break them.
  • The greatest reward is the world to come (olam ha-ba); the greatest punishment is for the soul to be cut off (karet).
  • But Maimonides adds: serve God out of love, not for reward. He cites Antigonus: be not like servants who serve their master for a reward (Pirkei Avot 1:3, paraphrase).
  • His image: a child is given sweets to learn; an adult learns for the sake of truth (paraphrase).
Mission 3

Principles 12 and 13: the Messiah and resurrection

  • 12. The Messiah will come: "though it tarry, wait for it" (Habakkuk 2:3, JPS 1917). Do not calculate the date. (Full picture in a later lesson.)
  • 13. Resurrection of the dead (techiyat ha-metim): "many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake" (Daniel 12:2, JPS 1917).
  • Maimonides stressed a spiritual world to come for souls, so some accused him of denying bodily resurrection.
  • His reply, the Treatise on Resurrection (1191): resurrection is real, a miracle, a foundation of the Torah; the risen will later die, and the final reward is for the soul (paraphrase).

Mission 3Diversity

The principles today

  • Orthodox: the principles mark the boundary of Orthodox belief; Yigdal and Ani Ma'amin are in Orthodox prayer books. Some Orthodox scholars note that respected authorities disputed individual principles in the past.
  • Masorti / Conservative: God, Torah and Israel affirmed; revelation includes a human element, so principle 8 is reinterpreted.
  • Reform: the Pittsburgh Platform (1885) rejected bodily resurrection and a personal Messiah (a Messianic Age instead), and saw the Torah as inspired but written by people. Reform versions of Yigdal change the resurrection line.
  • Reconstructionist: Kaplan rejected a supernatural revelation altogether.
  • Principles 1–5 unite most Jews; 8, 12 and 13 divide them most.
  • The list was argued over from the start: Raavad objected to calling sincere Jews heretics over beliefs such as a bodily God; some rabbis in Provence banned Maimonides' books, and copies were burned in Montpellier (c. 1233); Joseph Albo (Sefer ha-Ikkarim, 1425) cut the essentials to three roots.

Mission 4

A God who speaks and listens

  • The God of the Tenakh is personal: God speaks, calls, hears, remembers, loves, is angry and forgives.
  • Abraham argues with God: "shall not the judge of all the earth do justly?" (Genesis 18:25, JPS 1917).
  • Moses speaks with God "mouth to mouth" (Numbers 12:8); the Psalms speak to God as "Thou".
  • Jewish blessings (berakhot) use the words "Barukh atah": "Blessed are You". God is addressed directly.
  • But God is also incorporeal and beyond human ideas (see Mission 1). How can both be true?
Mission 4Read the source

Genesis 3:8

> "And they heard the voice of the LORD God walking in the garden toward the cool of the day; and the man and his wife hid themselves from the presence of the LORD God amongst the trees of the garden." (Genesis 3:8, JPS 1917)

Q1 Which words describe God in human, bodily terms?

Show answer

"walking in the garden"; also a "voice" that can be heard.

Q2 What do the man and woman do, and why might that matter?

Show answer

They hide: the verse is about a broken relationship with a God who comes looking for them.

Mission 4

Reading anthropomorphism

  • Anthropomorphism: describing God in human form or with human feelings (walking, a hand, an arm, regret, anger).
  • Targum Onkelos (Aramaic translation, early centuries CE) often softens these: at Genesis 3:8 they hear "the voice of the Memra (Word) of the LORD God".
  • Maimonides, Guide for the Perplexed (c. 1190), Book I: every bodily word about God is figurative. "Walking" means God's action or presence becoming known; Genesis 1:27's "image" means human intellect, not shape.
  • Rabbinic principle: "the Torah speaks in human language" (Talmud, Berakhot 31b, paraphrase): God is described so people can understand.
  • Kabbalah reads the bodily images symbolically, as the structure of God's ten sefirot.
  • A few medieval Jews read some anthropomorphisms more literally; Maimonides' view became mainstream.

Mission 4

King and Father

  • King: "The LORD shall reign for ever and ever." (Exodus 15:18, JPS 1917) The standard berakhah calls God Melekh ha-olam, "King of the universe".
  • King means sovereignty (God rules all), law (God commands) and judgement: on Rosh Hashanah Jews proclaim God King (Malkhuyot prayers).
  • Father: "Thou, O LORD, art our Father, our Redeemer from everlasting is Thy name" (Isaiah 63:16, JPS 1917).
  • "Have we not all one father? Hath not one God created us?" (Malachi 2:10, JPS 1917): Father as creator of all, and the basis for treating others as kin.
  • Father means love, care and discipline: "Like as a father hath compassion upon his children" (Psalm 103:13, JPS 1917).
  • Avinu Malkeinu ("Our Father, our King"), said from Rosh Hashanah to Yom Kippur, holds both together: closeness and awe.
Mission 4Diversity

Gendered language about God

  • Hebrew grammar uses masculine forms for God; King, Father and Lord are male images.
  • Traditional view: God is neither male nor female (Maimonides); masculine words are a feature of language, not a claim about God.
  • Feminine images exist in the Tenakh: "As one whom his mother comforteth, so will I comfort you" (Isaiah 66:13, JPS 1917); God compared to a mother who cannot forget her baby (Isaiah 49:15).
  • Shekhinah (God's indwelling presence) is grammatically feminine; Kabbalah treats it as the feminine aspect of God.
  • Feminist critique: Judith Plaskow (b. 1947), Standing Again at Sinai (1990): only-male images of God support male power in Jewish life; new images are needed.
  • Liturgy: Marcia Falk, The Book of Blessings (1996), offers new blessings addressing God as "source of life"; many Reform and Liberal prayer books use gender-neutral English.
  • Orthodox prayer books keep the traditional Hebrew, understood as metaphor.

Mission 4Evaluate

Should language about God be read literally?

  • Claim: Anthropomorphic and gendered language about God should be read literally.
  • For (literal force): Without human images, God becomes abstract and distant; a "Father" who "walks" is someone people can love and fear.
  • For: The Torah chose these words; the images may say something true about God's closeness.
  • Against: Literal readings clash with incorporeality (Deuteronomy 4:15; Maimonides' third principle).
  • Against: Only-male images may shape how people see power in the community (Plaskow).
  • Against: Images can be kept as metaphor: true in what they point to, not in their form.
  • Judgement criteria: What is lost if the images go? Can metaphor still be true? Who decides how liturgy changes: tradition, community, or individuals?
Mission 4

Buber: two ways of relating

  • Martin Buber (1878–1965), Austrian-born Jewish philosopher; Ich und Du (I and Thou, 1923; English 1937).
  • People can say two basic "words": I–It and I–Thou (or I–You).
  • I–It: using, measuring, describing. The other is an object: useful, but held at a distance.
  • I–Thou: meeting the other with your whole being, in the present, mutually. The other is a person, not a thing to use.
  • I–It is necessary for life (work, science); a life with only I–It is not fully human.
  • I–Thou can happen with nature, with people, and with spiritual forms (art, ideas). Buber drew on the warmth of Hasidism.
Mission 4

The Eternal Thou

  • In every true I–Thou meeting, we glimpse the Eternal Thou: God (paraphrase of Buber, I and Thou, Part Three).
  • Every human "Thou" slips back into "It"; the Eternal Thou never becomes an It.
  • So God is known in and through relationships, not as an object of proof or description. God can be addressed, not expressed.
  • Revelation is presence, not information: a meeting that changes us, not a list of facts.
  • This fits prayer as address ("Blessed are You") and the God who calls "Where art thou?" after Genesis 3:8 (Genesis 3:9).

Mission 4Evaluate

Is Buber's Eternal Thou a good account of God?

  • Claim: Buber's idea of the Eternal Thou is the best way to understand the personal God of Judaism.
  • For: It keeps God personal without giving God a body: God is "You", not "It".
  • For: It fits the Bible's God who calls and answers, and Jewish prayer as direct address.
  • For: It links love of God with love of neighbour (Leviticus 19:18).
  • Against: It is subjective: how can one tell a meeting with God from a strong feeling?
  • Against: Buber did not accept the Torah's laws as binding revelation; Franz Rosenzweig (1886–1929), in his open letter "The Builders" (1923), urged him to see law as a place of meeting too.
  • Against: Emmanuel Levinas (1906–1995) argued the relation to the other is not mutual but asymmetrical: I am responsible for the other before any meeting.
  • Judgement criteria: Does it fit the Jewish tradition as a whole, or only some parts? Can knowledge of God be non-descriptive? Does it make room for halakhah?

Jews speak to God through human images and in personal address, while holding that God is beyond any image. A later mission: what being human means in God's image.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Judaism is absolute monotheism: one, eternal, incorporeal God, creator, sustainer and judge, who alone is worshipped and who demands justice (ethical monotheism).
  • Maimonides' Thirteen Principles summarise belief; Jewish traditions interpret the one God differently.
  • Maimonides teaches that we know God best by saying what God is not.

Mission 2

  • Maimonides (1138–1204) set out Thirteen Principles in his Commentary on the Mishnah; principles 1–5 are about God: exists, one, without body, eternal, alone to be worshipped.
  • Body words for God are figurative: "the Torah speaks in the language of human beings".

Mission 3

  • Principles 6–9 are about revelation (prophecy, Moses, Torah from heaven, Torah unchanging); 10–13 about God and humanity (knowledge, reward and punishment, Messiah, resurrection).
  • Today Orthodox Jews affirm all thirteen; Progressive Jews reinterpret especially 8, 12 and 13.

Mission 4

  • The Tenakh's God is personal; images like "walking in the garden" (Genesis 3:8), King and Father are read by most Jews (with Maimonides) as metaphors, since God has no body.
  • Gendered language is debated: tradition says God is neither male nor female; feminist thinkers (Plaskow, Falk) call for new images; Orthodox liturgy keeps the traditional words.
  • Buber: God is the Eternal Thou, met in and through I–Thou relationships, never an It; critics question its subjectivity and its place for law.
