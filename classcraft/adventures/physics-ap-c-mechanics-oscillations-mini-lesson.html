Mission 1Topics 7.1–7.3

Mission 1: Why does an oscillating mass on a spring move fastest at the one point where no overall force acts on it?

Make a guess: Why does an oscillating mass on a spring move fastest at the one point where no overall force acts on it?

Mission 1

What makes motion simple harmonic

  • Periodic motion: repeats in equal time intervals. SHM is a special case. Every SHM is periodic; not every periodic motion is SHM.
  • Equilibrium position: where the object would rest; the restoring force is zero there.
  • Restoring force: always points back toward equilibrium, opposite to the displacement.
  • SHM: the restoring force is proportional to the displacement from equilibrium.
  • Fx = −kΔx
Mission 1

Describing an oscillation

  • Displacement x: distance from equilibrium, with a sign (+ one side, − the other).
  • Amplitude A: maximum displacement.
  • Period T: time for one complete cycle. Frequency f: cycles per second (Hz). T = 1/f.
  • Angular frequency ω = 2π/T = 2πf, in rad/s. One cycle = 2π rad.
  • In max = −kΔx, Δx is this displacement x from equilibrium.
Quick check
?A student claims that an object's motion is simple harmonic motion. Each graph shows a possible set of measurements of the net force Fx exerted on the object as a function of its position x, where x = 0 is the object's equilibrium position. Which graph would support the student's claim?
Mission 1

The defining equation

  • a = −ω²x
  • The minus sign: acceleration always points opposite to the displacement, toward equilibrium.
  • It does not mean "slowing down": moving toward equilibrium, the object speeds up.
  • a against x: a straight line through the origin with a negative slope.
  • Slope of the a–x graph = −ω².
Mission 1

Worked example

  • f = 2.5 Hz → ω = 2π × 2.5 = 15.7 rad/s.
  • At x = +0.030 m: a = −ω²x = −(15.7)² × 0.030 = −7.4 m/s² (toward equilibrium).
  • At x = 0: a = 0.
Mission 1

x, v and a against time

  • Released from rest at x = +A at t = 0: x–t is a cosine curve.
  • v = slope of x–t. v–t is a negative sine curve, a quarter cycle ahead of x.
  • a = slope of v–t. a–t is x–t upside down, because a = −(constant)x.
  • At x = ±A: v = 0, |a| maximum (pointing inward).
  • At x = 0: |v| maximum, a = 0.
  • Speeding up when moving toward equilibrium; slowing down when moving away.
Quick check
?The graph shows the position x of an object in simple harmonic motion as a function of time t. Points P, Q and R are marked on the graph. Let aP, aQ and aR be the magnitudes of the object's acceleration at P, Q and R. Which of the following ranks them?
Mission 1

Displacement as a function of time

  • x = A cos(2πft): at t = 0, x = +A (released from rest there).
  • x = A sin(2πft): at t = 0, x = 0, moving in the + direction.
  • General form: x = A cos(ωt + φ); φ is set by where the motion starts.
  • Calculator in radians.
  • A = 0.050 m, f = 2.0 Hz, t = 0.10 s: x = 0.050 cos(2π × 2.0 × 0.10) = 0.050 cos(1.26 rad) = 0.015 m.
Mission 1

Maximum speed and acceleration

  • vmax = ωA, at x = 0.
  • amax = ω²A, at x = ±A.
  • Same oscillator: A = 0.050 m, f = 2.0 Hz → ω = 2π × 2.0 = 12.6 rad/s.
  • vmax = 12.6 × 0.050 = 0.63 m/s. amax = 12.6² × 0.050 = 7.9 m/s².
Quick check
?The graph shows the position x of an object in SHM as a function of time t. What is the maximum speed of the object?
Mission check

?A pendulum bob swings to and fro in a small arc. At the lowest point of its swing, what are its acceleration along its path and its speed?

All the way in from a turning point, the acceleration points along the motion, so the object keeps speeding up. It stops speeding up only where the acceleration (and the restoring force) is zero: at equilibrium. That is where it is fastest.

Mission 2Topics 7.2, 7.3

Mission 2: Take a pendulum clock to the Moon. Does it run fast, run slow, or keep time?

Make a guess: Take a pendulum clock to the Moon. Does it run fast, run slow, or keep time?

Mission 2

Mass on a spring

  • Ts = 2π√(m/k)
  • m = oscillating mass; k = spring constant.
  • Bigger mass → longer T. Stiffer spring → shorter T.
  • Not on g: hung vertically, gravity only shifts the equilibrium position. Same T as horizontal.
  • Not on amplitude.
Mission 2

Simple pendulum

  • Tp = 2π√(ℓ/g)
  • Length from the pivot to the center of mass of the bob.
  • Small angles only. Then the restoring force is (nearly) proportional to the displacement.
  • Not on the bob's mass. Not on the (small) amplitude.
Mission 2

Worked examples

  • Spring: m = 0.25 kg, k = 40 N/m. T = 2π√(0.25/40) = 0.50 s.
  • Pendulum, length 0.80 m:
  • T = 2π√(0.80/10) = 1.8 s (g = 10 m/s²).
  • To make the pendulum's period three times as long: nine times the length.
Quick check
?A block attached to an ideal spring oscillates on a horizontal surface with negligible friction, with period T. The block is replaced by a block with 4 times the mass, and the new block is set oscillating with 3 times the original amplitude. The new period is how many times T?
Mission 2

What sets the period?

  • Mass on a spring: longer with more mass; shorter with a stiffer spring. Unchanged by g and by amplitude.
  • Pendulum: longer with a longer string; shorter where g is larger. Unchanged by the bob's mass and by small changes of amplitude.
  • Large swings (beyond about 10°): a pendulum's period grows slightly. The motion is no longer quite SHM.
Quick check
?The diagram shows two simple pendulums, A and B, labeled with the mass of each bob and the length of each light string. Both pendulums swing through small angles at the same location. What is the ratio TB/TA of the period of B to the period of A?
Mission check

?A simple pendulum and a mass–spring oscillator each have a period of 1.0 s on Earth. Both are taken to a planet where the gravitational field strength is four times as large as on Earth, and both oscillate with small amplitude. What are their new periods?

A pendulum's period depends on its length and on g. On the Moon, g is about one-sixth of Earth's, so each swing takes longer: the clock runs slow. A watch regulated by a balance wheel and hairspring would keep time.

Mission 3Topic 7.4

Mission 3: A mass on a spring keeps stopping and starting again. Where is its energy at the instant it stops?

Make a guess: A mass on a spring keeps stopping and starting again. Where is its energy at the instant it stops?

Quick check
?A block attached to a horizontal ideal spring oscillates on a surface with negligible friction. At an instant when the block's kinetic energy has its minimum value, which of the following is true of the spring potential energy Us of the block–spring system?
Mission 3

Energy swaps back and forth

  • Etotal = U + K
  • At ±A (turning points): at rest. Kinetic energy zero; potential energy maximum = the total.
  • At equilibrium: fastest. Kinetic energy maximum; potential energy minimum.
  • Potential energy: in the spring (mass–spring) or gravitational (pendulum).
  • Undamped: the total stays constant. Energy is only exchanged.
Mission 3

Energy against displacement

  • Potential energy: a U-shaped parabola, zero at x = 0, maximum at ±A.
  • Kinetic energy: an upside-down parabola, maximum at x = 0, zero at ±A.
  • Total: a horizontal line. At every x, kinetic + potential = total.
  • Mass–spring: Us = ½kx²
Mission 3

Energy against time

  • Kinetic and potential energy each rise and fall twice per period: two peaks per cycle.
  • Both are never negative: each is 0 at its minimum.
  • When one is at a peak, the other is zero. Their sum is constant.
  • (x = A cos ωt gives Us ∝ cos²ωt and K ∝ sin²ωt.)
Quick check
?A block attached to an ideal spring oscillates on a horizontal surface with negligible friction. The graph shows the spring potential energy Us of the block–spring system as a function of the block's position x, measured from equilibrium; the curve is a parabola, and the dashed line shows the system's total energy. What is the kinetic energy of the block when it is at x = 0.050 m?
Mission 3

Total energy and amplitude

  • At x = ±A all the energy is potential.
  • Mass–spring: Etotal = ½kA².
  • E ∝ A²: double the amplitude → four times the energy, twice the maximum speed.
  • Halfway out (x = A/2): Us = ¼ of the total. K = ¾, not ½.
Mission 3

Worked example

  • m = 0.50 kg on a spring, k = 200 N/m, amplitude 0.060 m.
  • Total = ½kA² = ½ × 200 × 0.060² = 0.36 J.
  • At x = 0.030 m: Us = ½ × 200 × 0.030² = 0.090 J → K = 0.36 − 0.090 = 0.27 J.
  • Speed: ½ × 0.50 × v² = 0.27 → v = 1.0 m/s.
Quick check
?A block of mass m attached to an ideal spring of spring constant k oscillates with amplitude A on a horizontal surface with negligible friction. Which expression gives the speed of the block when it is at x = A/2, halfway between the equilibrium position and a turning point?
Mission check

?A glider on an air track is attached between two springs and oscillates with simple harmonic motion of amplitude 0.10 m. Friction is negligible. At what distance from the equilibrium position are the glider's kinetic energy and the elastic potential energy of the system equal?

At each stop, all of the energy is potential (in the spring, or gravitational for a pendulum). It flows back into kinetic energy on the way in, and the total stays the same throughout.

Mission 4Topics 7.3, 7.5

Mission 4: Why do so many different systems all oscillate in exactly the same way?

Make a guess: Why do so many different systems all oscillate in exactly the same way?

Mission 4

The SHM equation

  • Block on an ideal spring: F = −kx.
  • Newton's second law: m d²x/dt² = −kx → d²x/dt² = −ω²x, with ω = √(k/m).
  • The pattern: second derivative = −(positive constant) × the quantity itself.
  • Any quantity that obeys it oscillates in SHM, with ω = √(constant) and T = 2π/ω.
Mission 4

The solution

  • Solution: x = A cos(ωt + φ). A and φ are set by how the motion starts.
  • v = dx/dt = −Aω sin(ωt + φ) → vmax = Aω.
  • a = dv/dt = −Aω² cos(ωt + φ) = −ω²x → amax = Aω².
  • You need to know this solution and recognize SHM; you do not need to prove the solution is the only one.
Quick check
?A particle of mass m moves along the x-axis. The only force exerted on it is a conservative force for which the system's potential energy is U(x) = βx², where β is a positive constant. Applying Newton's second law gives an equation of the form d²x/dt² = −ω²x. What is the period T of the motion?
Quick check
?The position of an object in SHM is x = A cos(ωt + φ). At t = 0 the object is at x = +A/2 and is moving in the +x direction. What is φ?
Mission 4

Physical pendulum

  • A rigid body swinging about a fixed axis; center of mass a distance d below the axis.
  • Gravity gives a restoring torque: τ = −mgd sin θ.
  • Small angles (θ in radians): sin θ ≈ θ, so Iα = −mgdθ.
  • d²θ/dt² = −(mgd/I)θ → ω = √(mgd/I), T = 2π√(I/(mgd)).
  • I is about the pivot: I = Icm + md² (parallel-axis theorem).
  • Simple pendulum: I = mℓ², d = ℓ → T = 2π√(ℓ/g).
Mission 4

Worked example: rod or bob?

  • Uniform rod, length L = 1.00 m, pivoted at one end. g = 10 m/s².
  • I = (1/3)mL², d = L/2.
  • T = 2π√[(1/3)mL² / (mg · L/2)] = 2π√(2L/(3g)).
  • T = 2π√(2 × 1.00 / 30) = 1.6 s.
  • A simple pendulum 1.00 m long: T = 2π√(1.00/10) = 2.0 s.
  • The rod is quicker: much of its mass is close to the pivot.
Quick check
?A uniform disk of radius R = 0.10 m hangs from a fixed horizontal axle that passes through a point on its rim, perpendicular to the face of the disk. The rotational inertia of a uniform disk of mass M about an axis through its center, perpendicular to its face, is (1/2)MR². The disk is displaced through a small angle and released. What is the period of its oscillations? Use g = 10 m/s².
Mission 4

Torsion pendulum

  • Horizontal disk hung from a wire through its center. Twist it through Δθ and release.
  • The wire gives a restoring torque proportional to the twist: Iα = −kΔθ (k = torsion constant, N·m/rad).
  • d²θ/dt² = −(k/I)θ → ω = √(k/I), T = 2π√(I/k).
Quick check
?A torsion pendulum consists of a horizontal uniform disk suspended from a vertical wire attached to its center. The disk undergoes small rotational oscillations about the wire, for which Iα = −kΔθ. Which single change would increase the period of the oscillations?
Mission check

?A pendulum consists of a light rigid rod of length L, pivoted about a fixed horizontal axis through its upper end, with two small objects, each of mass m, fastened to it: one at a distance L/4 from the axis and one at the rod's lower end. For small angular displacements θ, the small-angle approximation and Newton's second law in rotational form give d²θ/dt² = −ω²θ. What is ω? g is the acceleration due to gravity.

Write the restoring effect, put it in Newton's second law, and read off ω² as the constant. Spring, rod or twisting disk: if the restoring effect is proportional to the displacement, the equation is the same.

Mission 5Topic 7.3

Mission 5: Why can small pushes, timed just right, build up a huge swing?

Make a guess: Why can small pushes, timed just right, build up a huge swing?

Mission 5

Free and forced oscillations

  • Free oscillation: displace it, let go, no further force. It oscillates at its natural frequency, f0.
  • Block on a spring: f0 = (1/2π)√(k/m). The natural frequency is set by the system, not by how it was started.
  • Forced oscillation: a periodic driving force keeps acting.
  • Once steady, a forced system oscillates at the driving frequency, not at f0.
Quick check
?A 0.10 kg block on a horizontal frictionless surface is attached to an ideal spring of spring constant 1000 N/m. A sinusoidal force drives the block at 0.50 Hz for some time; then the driving force is removed while the block is displaced from equilibrium. Damping is negligible. At what frequency does the block oscillate after the driving force is removed?
Mission 5

Resonance

  • Resonance: driving frequency = natural frequency → maximum amplitude.
  • Why: each push is in step with the motion, so the driver does positive work on every cycle. Energy builds up.
  • Amplitude–driving frequency graph: small at low frequency, a peak at f0, then falls toward zero at high frequency.
  • Real systems always lose some energy, so the amplitude stops growing at a large but finite value.
Quick check
?The graph shows the steady-state amplitude of a lightly damped block–spring system as a function of the frequency f of a sinusoidal driving force. The block is replaced by one with one-fourth the mass; the spring and the maximum driving force are unchanged. At approximately what driving frequency is the amplitude of the new system largest?
Mission check

?A singer shatters a wine glass by singing one steady note close to it. Which condition is essential for this to happen?

Same idea as the swing. A driver at the natural frequency adds energy on every cycle, so small pushes build a large amplitude; at other frequencies the pushes work against the motion for part of each cycle.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • SHM: restoring force ∝ displacement, always toward equilibrium.
  • a = −ω²x, with ω = 2π/T = 2πf.
  • Fastest at equilibrium (a = 0); at rest at ±A (|a| largest). v = slope of x–t; a = slope of v–t.
  • vmax = ωA; amax = ω²A.

Mission 2

  • Ts = 2π√(m/k); Tp = 2π√(ℓ/g), for small angles.
  • Spring: no g, no amplitude. Pendulum: no mass, no (small) amplitude.

Mission 3

  • K and U swap twice per cycle; the total is constant if there is no damping. At ±A all U; at equilibrium all K.
  • Mass–spring: Etotal = ½kA²; Us = ½kx².

Mission 4

  • Anything obeying d²x/dt² = −ω²x is SHM: x = A cos(ωt + φ), with ω read off as the square root of the constant.
  • Physical pendulum: T = 2π√(I/(mgd)), I about the pivot (small angles). Torsion pendulum: Iα = −kΔθ, T = 2π√(I/k).

Mission 5

  • A forced oscillator settles at the driving frequency; resonance (maximum amplitude) happens when the driving frequency equals the natural frequency.
