Mission 1

The big four objectives

ObjectiveWhat "success" looks likeWhy it matters
Economic growthSteady, sustainable rise in real GDPHigher living standards; more tax revenue

Price stabilityLow and stable inflationUncertainty and lost spending power when inflation is high or erratic

  • UK: the Bank of England's inflation target is 2% (CPI), set by the government.
  • Say: "low and stable inflation" — not: "falling prices". Deflation is not the goal.
Mission 1

More objectives

  • Environment / sustainability: growth that does not run down natural resources or harm future generations.
  • Greater equality: a fairer distribution of income and wealth (redistribution).
  • Governments set these because markets alone may not deliver them: pollution is a market failure, and market outcomes can be very unequal.
  • Objectives can support each other: growth creates jobs and raises tax revenue.
  • But they can also conflict: a trade-off means getting more of one costs some of another.

Mission 1

Conflict: low unemployment vs low inflation

  • (1) AD1 → AD2 in a slump: real output rises a lot, the price level a little. Unemployment falls.
  • (2) AD2 → AD3 near full capacity: real output rises a little, the price level a lot.
  • Near capacity, firms compete for scarce workers: wages and prices rise.
  • So pushing unemployment ever lower tends to raise inflation. Cooling inflation tends to raise unemployment.
  • This is the short-run trade-off shown by the Phillips curve.
Mission 1

Conflict: growth vs inflation and the current account

  • Demand-led growth faster than potential output opens a positive output gap: inflation rises.
  • Supply-led growth (LRAS shifts right) raises output without adding to inflation.
  • Faster growth raises incomes, and part of that income is spent on imports: the current account can worsen.
  • Higher inflation than in trading partners makes exports less competitive: another drag on the current account.
  • Raising interest rates to curb inflation may strengthen the currency, making exports dearer.

Mission 1

Conflict: growth vs the environment

  • More output usually means more energy and raw materials, more emissions and more waste.
  • These are negative externalities: the costs fall on third parties and future generations.
  • But the link can weaken: UK territorial greenhouse gas emissions fell by about 50% between 1990 and 2022, while real GDP grew.
  • Caution: part of that fall came from making less at home and importing more. Emissions measured by what the UK consumes fell by less.
  • Green technology, carbon taxes and regulation aim to decouple growth from emissions. How far that can go is debated.
Mission 1

Conflict: growth vs equity

  • Growth may reward some groups more: owners of capital, highly skilled workers, booming regions.
  • So inequality can widen even as average incomes rise.
  • Redistribution (progressive taxes, benefits) can narrow the gap.
  • One view: high taxes and generous benefits reduce incentives to work and invest, so equity costs some efficiency (Okun, 1975).
  • Another view: IMF research (Ostry, Berg and Tsangarides, 2014) found lower inequality was linked to faster and more durable growth, and redistribution was generally not harmful to growth except in extreme cases.
  • Which effect dominates depends on the policy design and the country.
Mission 1

Can conflicts be reconciled?

  • Supply-side policies (education, training, infrastructure, competition) shift LRAS right.
  • Then growth and falling unemployment come with less inflation.
  • But supply-side policies take years to work.
  • Policy mix: use different tools for different goals, e.g. interest rates for inflation, supply-side policies for growth.
  • Prioritise: tackle the most pressing problem first, accepting a cost elsewhere for a time.
Mission 1

Priorities change over time (UK)

  • 1944: White Paper commits government to "a high and stable level of employment". Full employment comes first.
  • 1970s: high inflation and high unemployment (stagflation). Faith in demand management falls.
  • 1980s: beating inflation becomes the priority.
  • 1992: the UK adopts an inflation target. 1997: the Bank of England sets interest rates independently.
  • 2008 onwards: financial stability; then reducing the budget deficit.
  • 2019: a legal target of net zero greenhouse gas emissions by 2050: sustainability rises up the list.
Mission 1

UK performance and other economies

  • UK, last 20 years:
    • Growth: the 2008–09 recession cut real GDP by more than 6%; growth afterwards was slower than before 2008, with weak productivity growth.
    • Unemployment: fell below 4% in 2019, low by historical standards.
    • Inflation: mostly near the 2% target, then a peak of 11.1% in October 2022.
    • Current account: in deficit every year since 1984.
  • Emerging economies often grow faster: China's growth averaged over 9% a year from 1978 (World Bank). They are catching up, adopting existing technology and moving workers out of farming.
  • Developing economies may grow fast or slowly, and are more exposed to commodity prices, conflict and weak institutions.
  • Reasons for differences: investment and productivity, education, demography, institutions, openness to trade, and the shocks each economy faced.
Quick check
?A government wants faster growth without higher inflation. Which policy is most likely to achieve both over time?
Mission 1Evaluate

Which objective should come first?

It depends on:

  • Where the economy is: a deep slump makes jobs the priority; a positive output gap makes inflation the priority.
  • The cause: a demand shock and a supply shock call for different responses.
  • Time horizon: short-run trade-offs may ease in the long run if capacity grows.
  • Value judgements: how much inflation is worth a lower jobless rate, or how much growth is worth more emissions, is normative. Economists can show the trade-off; voters and governments choose.

Pushing demand when capacity is nearly used up trades one objective (growth) against another (low inflation).

Mission 2

Inflation, deflation, disinflation

  • Inflation: a sustained rise in the general price level. The inflation rate is its % change, usually over 12 months.
  • Deflation: a sustained fall in the general price level. The inflation rate is negative.
  • Disinflation: the inflation rate falls but stays positive. Prices still rise, just more slowly.
  • Real data: UK CPI inflation was 11.1% in October 2022 and 2.0% in May 2024. That fall was disinflation: prices kept rising.
  • Say: "inflation fell, so prices rose more slowly" — not: "inflation fell, so prices fell".
Mission 2

Index numbers and the base year

  • An index number shows a value relative to a base year, which is set to 100.
  • Index = (value ÷ value in base year) × 100.
  • Illustrative data: a price is 2.00 in the base year, 2.50 a year later, 2.60 the year after.
  • Index: 100 → 125 → 130.
  • Change from the base year: read it straight off: 125 means 25% higher.
  • Change between two later years: % change, not points. 125 → 130 is a 5-point rise but (130 − 125) ÷ 125 × 100 = 4%.
  • Index numbers have no units, so series in different units or currencies can be compared.
  • Say: "the index rose by 5 points, a 4% rise" — not: "the index rose by 5%".
Quick check
?A country's consumer price index (base year 2015 = 100) was 125 in 2023 and 130 in 2024 (illustrative data). What was the inflation rate in 2024?
Mission 2

Measuring inflation with a basket

  • Statisticians pick a basket of goods and services that typical households buy.
  • They record the prices of the basket's items every month, in many shops and places.
  • A household spending survey shows how much households spend on each item. These shares become the weights.
  • Big spending shares (e.g. housing, food) get big weights: their price changes move the index more.
  • The consumer price index (CPI) tracks the cost of this basket over time.
  • In the UK, the ONS collects about 180,000 prices a month for about 760 items.
  • The government sets the Bank of England an inflation target of 2% on the CPI measure.

Mission 2

CPI, CPIH and RPI

  • The ONS publishes three main measures of consumer price inflation.
  • CPI: the Bank of England's target measure (2%). Uses mainly geometric averages within each item.
  • CPIH: CPI plus owner occupiers' housing costs and Council Tax. The ONS's lead measure since 2017.
  • RPI: the older index. It differs from the CPI in three ways:
    • Coverage: excludes the top 4% of households by income and pensioner households who get at least three-quarters of their income from benefits.
    • Housing: includes mortgage interest payments and Council Tax, which the CPI leaves out.
    • Formula: uses mainly arithmetic averages, which push it up relative to the CPI (the formula effect).
  • RPI inflation has usually been higher than CPI inflation.
  • The RPI lost its National Statistics status in 2013, but is still used in some contracts and index-linked payments.
  • Say: "the RPI includes mortgage interest payments; the CPI does not" — not: "the RPI and CPI measure the same thing".
Mission 2

Real versus nominal

  • Nominal (money) value: measured in the prices of the time (current prices).
  • Real value: adjusted for inflation, measured in the prices of one year (constant prices).
  • Real value = nominal value × (price index in base year ÷ price index in current year).
  • Illustrative data: weekly pay rises from 400 to 520 while the CPI rises from 100 to 135.
  • Real pay now, in base-year prices: 520 × 100 ÷ 135 = 385.19.
  • Nominal pay is up 30%. Real pay is down 3.7%: prices rose faster than pay.
  • Shortcut: real growth ≈ nominal growth − inflation. It works well only for small changes (here: 30 − 35 = −5, against an exact −3.7).
  • Say: "real pay fell" — not: "pay fell", when nominal pay rose.

Mission 2

A trade-weighted exchange rate index

  • A currency can rise against one currency and fall against another. A trade-weighted index combines them.
  • Each partner's exchange-rate index is weighted by its share of the country's trade.
  • Illustrative data (base year = 100):
Partner currencyTrade weightExchange-rate index now

  • The currency fell against its biggest partner, but overall it is 0.7% stronger than in the base year.
Mission 2

Limits of the CPI

  • Substitution bias: when one item's price jumps, people switch to cheaper alternatives. A fixed basket misses this, so it overstates the rise in the cost of living.
  • New products: new goods enter the basket only after a delay.
  • Quality changes: a dearer phone may also be a much better phone. Not all of the price rise is inflation.
  • Not your basket: the CPI tracks an average household. Students, pensioners and renters spend differently, so face different inflation rates.
  • Weights lag behind: they come from past spending surveys and are updated only from time to time (often yearly).
  • The UK CPI leaves out owner occupiers' housing costs, a big part of many households' spending (hence CPIH).
  • Real data: in 1996 the US Boskin Commission estimated that the CPI overstated the rise in the cost of living by about 1.1 percentage points a year; about 0.4 points of this was substitution bias.
  • Say: "the CPI measures changes in the price of a basket" — not: "the CPI measures each person's cost of living".
Quick check
?The price of beef rises by 30% while the price of chicken does not change. Many households buy less beef and more chicken. The consumer price index uses a fixed basket bought in the base year. What is the most likely effect on the measured inflation rate?
Mission check

?Illustrative data. In one year, food prices rise by 10% and all other prices stay the same. Household X spends 40% of its budget on food. Household Y spends 10% on food. In the country's consumer price index, food has a weight of 20%. Which set of inflation rates is correct?

A price index is only as good as its weights. Each household has its own inflation rate, and the CPI is the average one.

Mission 3

Two routes to inflation

  • Inflation: a sustained rise in the general price level (see Mission 2).
  • Deflation: a sustained fall in the price level. Disinflation: the inflation rate falls but stays positive.
  • In the AD/AS model, the price level rises in two ways:
    • AD shifts right: spending pulls prices up → demand-pull inflation
    • SRAS shifts left: costs push prices up → cost-push inflation
  • One shift gives a one-off rise in the price level. Ongoing inflation needs the shift to keep happening.
  • Say: "inflation is a rising price level" — not: "inflation means prices are high".
Mission 3

Demand-pull inflation

  • Demand-pull: AD grows faster than the economy's capacity to produce.
  • "Too much money chasing too few goods."
  • What can push AD right: lower interest rates, easy credit, rising house prices (wealth), high confidence, an export boom, more government spending, tax cuts.
  • With spare capacity, extra AD mostly raises real output.
  • Near full capacity, firms cannot expand much, so extra AD mostly raises the price level.
  • On a Keynesian AS curve: the steeper the section AD moves along, the more of the rise is in prices.
Quick check
?Interest rates are cut and house prices climb, so households borrow and spend more. The economy is already close to full capacity. Which best describes what happens in the short run?
Mission 3

Cost-push inflation

  • Cost-push: firms' costs rise, so they supply less at each price level. SRAS shifts left.
  • Sources of higher costs:
    • wages rising faster than productivity
    • energy and raw materials (commodity prices)
    • imported inputs (a weaker currency or dearer world prices)
    • indirect taxes on inputs or goods
  • Result: the price level rises and real output falls. Unemployment rises: stagflation.
  • Real world: wholesale energy prices jumped in 2021–22. UK CPI inflation reached 11.1% in October 2022; energy and food were the largest contributors.
  • Say: "costs shift SRAS left" — not: "costs shift AD".

Mission 3

Imported inflation and commodity prices

  • Imported inflation: higher prices of imports feed through to home prices.
  • Two triggers:
    • world prices rise (oil, gas, metals, food: commodity prices)
    • the home currency depreciates, so each import costs more in home currency
  • Imported inputs raise firms' costs: a cost-push shift of SRAS. Imported consumer goods raise the CPI directly.
  • Real world: sterling fell sharply after the UK's EU referendum in June 2016. UK CPI inflation rose from 0.5% (June 2016) to 3.1% (November 2017).
  • Say: "a depreciation makes imports dearer" — not: "a weaker currency makes imports cheaper".
Quick check

Mission 3

Expectations and the wage-price spiral

  • Inflation expectations: what workers and firms think inflation will be.
  • If workers expect prices to rise 5%, they bargain for pay rises of at least 5% to protect real wages.
  • Firms expecting higher costs raise prices in advance.
  • Wage-price spiral: higher prices → higher wage claims → higher costs → higher prices → …
  • Expectations can make inflation self-fulfilling and slow to fall.
  • That is why central banks try to keep expectations anchored to their inflation target.
  • Real world: in the UK in the 1970s, oil price rises fed into large wage claims. UK RPI inflation peaked at about 27% in August 1975.
Mission 3

Causes of deflation

  • Deflation also has a demand side and a supply side.
  • AD falls (weak confidence, a credit crunch, a world recession): the price level falls, real output falls, unemployment rises.
  • SRAS rises (higher productivity, cheaper commodities, new technology): the price level falls, real output rises.
  • So falling prices are not always bad news. The cause matters.
  • Say: "disinflation: inflation falls from 4% to 2%" — not: "prices fell".
Mission 3Evaluate

Which cause, and does it matter?

It depends on:

  • Spare capacity. The closer to full capacity, the more a rise in AD shows up as inflation.
  • One-off or ongoing. A single jump in oil prices raises the price level once. It becomes lasting inflation only if wages, expectations or money growth follow.
  • The policy response. Higher interest rates curb demand-pull well. Against cost-push, they cut inflation only by deepening the fall in output.
  • Mixed causes. Real episodes often combine both. 2021–22 saw energy shocks and strong post-pandemic demand.

Mission 4

Four words for changing prices

  • Inflation: a sustained rise in the general price level.
  • Deflation: a sustained fall in the price level. The inflation rate is negative.
  • Disinflation: the inflation rate falls but stays positive. Prices still rise, more slowly.
  • Hyperinflation: extremely rapid inflation (a common benchmark: over 50% a month). Money loses its value fast.
  • Say: "inflation fell from 4% to 2%: disinflation" — not: "prices fell".
Mission 4

Worked example: reading the inflation rate

  • Illustrative data. Consumer price index (CPI) at the end of each year:
Year1234

Mission 4

Expected or unexpected?

  • Nominal: measured in money. Real: adjusted for inflation (what the money buys).
  • Real interest rate ≈ nominal interest rate − inflation rate.
  • If inflation is fully expected, people build it in:
    • wages, pensions and benefits rise with prices
    • lenders set interest rates high enough to cover it
  • Then real incomes and real debts stay about the same.
  • If inflation is unexpected, contracts fixed in money terms no longer match prices. Real values change: wealth is redistributed.
  • Even fully expected inflation still has some costs.
  • Say: "a fall in real income" — not: "income fell" when money income rose more slowly than prices.
Mission 4

Worked example: who wins from a surprise?

  • Illustrative data. A bank lends 10,000 for one year at a fixed 5%. Repayment: 10,500.
  • Both sides expect 2% inflation. Expected real interest rate ≈ 5 − 2 = 3%.
  • Inflation turns out to be 7%.
  • Real interest rate ≈ 5 − 7 = −2%.
  • In start-of-year prices, 10,500 ÷ 1.07 = 9,813. The bank gets back less buying power than it lent.
  • Lender loses; borrower gains.
  • Also losing: savers on fixed rates, workers on fixed money wages, people on fixed money pensions.
  • Unexpected deflation works the other way: lenders gain, borrowers lose.

Mission 4

The costs of inflation

  • Menu costs: time and money spent changing prices (price lists, labels, websites).
  • Shoe-leather costs: time and effort spent holding less cash and moving money around to protect its value.
  • Uncertainty: high, variable inflation makes future costs and revenue hard to predict, so firms invest less. Growth can suffer.
  • Savings discouraged: if interest rates lag behind inflation, saving loses real value.
  • International competitiveness: if inflation is higher than in trading partners, exports get dearer and imports relatively cheaper (unless the exchange rate falls to offset it). The current account can worsen.
  • Inefficient resource allocation: when all prices rise fast, changes in relative prices are hard to spot, so price signals work less well.
  • Fiscal drag: if tax thresholds stay fixed while pay rises with prices, more income is taxed at higher rates.
  • Most costs grow with the rate of inflation and with how unpredictable it is.
Mission 4

Hyperinflation: when money stops working

  • At very high inflation, money fails at its jobs:
    • store of value: savings in cash are wiped out
    • medium of exchange: sellers refuse it and people turn to barter or foreign currency
    • unit of account: prices change too fast to compare
  • Real world: Zimbabwe's official annual inflation reached 231 million per cent in July 2008.
  • In 2009 Zimbabwe allowed foreign currencies such as the US dollar and the South African rand, and people stopped using its own currency.
  • Hyperinflations usually end only with a new currency or a new monetary regime (money growth).
Mission 4

Deflation: good news or bad?

  • Benign ("good") deflation: SRAS shifts right (higher productivity, cheaper commodities, new technology).
    • Price level falls, real output rises, unemployment falls.
  • Malign ("bad") deflation: AD shifts left (weak confidence, a credit crunch, a world recession).
    • Price level falls, real output falls, unemployment rises.
  • Most worries about deflation are about the malign kind.

Mission 4

The costs of deflation

  • Deferred consumption: if prices are expected to fall, households wait to buy. AD falls further.
  • Real value of debt rises: debts are fixed in money, but prices and incomes fall. Borrowers cut spending; some go bankrupt.
  • Unemployment: firms' revenue falls, but money wages are sticky downwards, so real wages rise and firms cut jobs (cyclical unemployment).
  • Policy ineffectiveness: interest rates cannot go far below zero, so real interest rates (≈ nominal − inflation) stay high when prices fall.
  • Deflationary spiral: falling prices → spending delayed → AD falls → output and jobs fall → prices fall further → …
  • Real world: US consumer prices fell by about a quarter from 1929 to 1933.
  • Real world: Japan's consumer prices fell in most years from 1999 to 2012, with interest rates close to zero.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A firm owes 100,000 on a loan with no interest. Over a year, the price level falls by 10%, and so do the prices the firm charges. What happens to the real burden of the debt?
Mission 4Evaluate

How much does inflation hurt?

It depends on:

  • The rate and its stability. Low, steady inflation costs little; high, volatile inflation costs a lot.
  • Whether it is expected. Expected inflation is built into wages and interest rates; surprises redistribute.
  • Indexation. Pensions, benefits and tax thresholds linked to prices protect people on fixed incomes.
  • Inflation abroad. What matters for competitiveness is inflation relative to trading partners, and the exchange rate.
  • Low inflation is not zero inflation. Many central banks target about 2%: a small positive rate lets real wages adjust without money pay cuts and leaves room above zero for interest rates.
Mission check

What matters is the surprise: when prices do not match what contracts assumed, wealth moves between lenders and borrowers.

Mission 5

What monetary policy is

  • Monetary policy: the central bank's use of interest rates and the money supply to influence demand and inflation.
  • UK: the Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee (MPC), 9 members, sets Bank Rate.
  • The Bank has had operational independence since 1997: the government sets the target; the MPC chooses the rate.
  • Expansionary policy: lower rates, more money. Contractionary: higher rates, less money.
Mission 5

Goals: an inflation target

  • Main goal: price stability, which means low and stable inflation.
  • UK: 2% CPI inflation, set by the government. The target is symmetric: undershooting is treated as seriously as overshooting.
  • If inflation is more than 1 percentage point away from 2%, the Governor writes an open letter to the Chancellor explaining why.
  • Why a target: it anchors expectations. If people expect 2%, wages and prices are set to match.
Mission 5

The transmission mechanism

  • How a rise in the policy rate reaches inflation:
    • Market rates rise: mortgages, loans, savings. Borrowing costs more; saving pays more. Consumption (C) and investment (I) fall.
    • Asset prices fall (houses, shares, bonds). Households feel less wealthy and spend less.
    • Confidence and expectations: a signal that the central bank is serious about inflation.
    • Exchange rate rises: exports more expensive abroad, imports cheaper. Net exports fall; import prices fall.
  • Result: AD grows more slowly. Less pressure on firms' capacity, wages and prices. Inflation slows.
  • A cut works the other way.
  • Say: "Bank Rate" (UK) — not: "the interest rate", when you mean the Bank of England's policy rate.
Mission 5

Monetary policy on AD/AS

  • Contractionary (rate rise): AD shifts left, AD1 → AD2.
    • Real output falls. The price level is lower than it would have been.
    • Closes an inflationary (positive) gap.
  • Expansionary (rate cut): AD shifts right.
    • Real output and the price level rise. Unemployment falls.
    • Closes a deflationary or recessionary (negative) gap.
  • Say: "inflation slows" (disinflation) — not: "prices fall", unless the price level actually falls (deflation).

  • Takeaway: a rate rise shifts AD left. Too small leaves a gap; too large pushes output below full employment.

Mission 5

The exchange-rate channel

  • Higher UK interest rates attract hot money: savers abroad buy pounds to earn the higher return.
  • Demand for pounds rises: D£1 → D£2. The pound appreciates.
  • UK exports become dearer abroad; imports become cheaper.
    • Net exports fall, so AD falls.
    • Cheaper imports lower costs and prices: inflation falls further.
  • The current account of the balance of payments may worsen.
  • In a global economy, rates abroad matter: if other central banks raise rates too, the pound may not rise.
  • Say: "the pound appreciates" — not: "the pound depreciates", after a rise in UK rates relative to rates abroad.
Mission 5

What the committee weighs

  • The MPC forecasts inflation about two years ahead, because rate changes take time to work.
  • It looks at: spare capacity and unemployment, wage growth, inflation expectations, the exchange rate, house prices and credit, and world energy and commodity prices.
  • Other central banks do the same. The European Central Bank sets rates for the whole euro area, so it cannot fit every member's economy.
Mission 5

Real case: the rate rises after the pandemic

  • UK: Bank Rate was 0.1% (March 2020). The MPC raised it from December 2021 to 5.25% (August 2023).
  • CPI inflation peaked at 11.1% (October 2022) and was back at 2.0% in May 2024 (ONS).
  • Inflation then fell, but economists debate how much was due to higher rates and how much to falling energy prices.
Mission 5

Who gains and who loses?

  • A rate rise:
    • Borrowers pay more; savers earn more.
    • Firms: dearer loans, so less investment. Sales of income-elastic goods (cars, holidays) and goods bought on credit fall most.
    • Exporters lose if the currency rises; importers gain.
    • Government: higher interest payments on its debt.
Mission 5

Strengths and limits

  • Strengths:
    • Flexible: rates can change in small steps, several times a year.
    • Reversible, and quick to decide: no new law needed.
    • An independent central bank is credible: a promise to hit the target shapes expectations.
  • Limits:
    • Time lags: up to about two years for the full effect on inflation (Bank of England).
    • Supply shocks: rate rises can lower inflation, but at the cost of lower output and jobs.
    • Low confidence: firms and households may not borrow, however cheap credit is.
    • One rate for everyone: regions, and borrowers and savers, are hit differently.
Mission 5

The zero lower bound

  • Rates cannot be cut far below zero: people could hold cash instead.
  • Zero lower bound: once rates are near zero, conventional policy has little room left.
  • Real cases: Bank Rate 0.1% (March 2020). The European Central Bank's deposit rate reached −0.5% (2019); the Bank of Japan's short-term rate was −0.1% from 2016 to March 2024.
  • Liquidity trap (Keynes): rates are so low that more money does not raise spending; people just hold it.
  • Central banks then turn to QE and forward guidance.
Quick check
?A recession has cut confidence. The central bank's policy rate is already 0.25%, and banks are unwilling to lend. Why might a further rate cut do little to raise AD?
Mission 5Evaluate

How well can interest rates steer the economy?

It depends on:

  • The cause of inflation: demand-pull responds well; a supply shock forces a choice between inflation and output.
  • Time lags: a rate change made now acts on demand up to two years later, when conditions may have changed.
  • Confidence and debt: with many variable-rate borrowers, rates bite fast; when confidence is low, cuts may not work.
  • How low rates already are: near the zero lower bound, other tools are needed.
Mission check

?A sharp rise in world oil prices pushes a country's inflation well above target, while real output falls. The central bank raises its policy rate. What is the most likely result in the short run?

Interest rates work through demand. After a supply shock, slowing inflation comes at the cost of lower output and jobs in the short run.

Mission 6

What fiscal policy is

  • Fiscal policy: the use of government spending and taxation to influence the economy.
  • Main aim: steady AD, so output stays close to potential: growth, jobs, low inflation.
  • Budget deficit: spending > tax revenue in a year. Budget surplus: tax revenue > spending.
  • National debt: the total stock of past borrowing still owed.
  • Illustrative data: debt 1,000 bn; deficit of 50 bn this year → debt 1,050 bn. A surplus of 10 bn next year → debt 1,040 bn.
  • Say: "the deficit is a yearly flow; the debt is the stock" — not: "deficit and debt mean the same".
Mission 6

Where the money goes and where it comes from

  • Current spending: day-to-day items: public-sector wages, medicines, school supplies.
  • Capital spending: investment that lasts: roads, hospitals, broadband.
  • Transfer payments: pensions and benefits; nothing is produced in return, so they are not part of G in GDP.
  • Revenue: taxes (on income, profits, spending), plus charges for state-provided goods and services and sales of state assets.
Mission 6

Expansionary and contractionary fiscal policy

  • Expansionary: raise G and/or cut taxes → AD shifts right.
  • Used to close a negative output gap: real output and employment rise; the price level rises a little.
  • Contractionary: cut G and/or raise taxes → AD shifts left.
  • Used to close a positive output gap: inflationary pressure eases.
  • Multiplier: the final shift in AD is larger than the first change in G or taxes.
  • Agents: households gain jobs and income; firms gain sales; the government's budget balance worsens with expansion.

Mission 6

Two views: Keynesian vs monetarist and new classical

  • Keynesian view: AS is flat when there is lots of spare capacity.
    • In a deep recession, expansionary fiscal policy raises real output and jobs with little inflation.
    • Near full capacity, it mainly raises the price level.
  • Monetarist / new classical view: LRAS is vertical at potential output.
    • In the short run, more G raises output and the price level.
    • In the long run, wages and costs adjust: SRAS shifts left. Output returns to potential, at a higher price level.
    • Government borrowing may push up interest rates and crowd out private investment.
  • So the two views disagree on how far fiscal policy can raise output for long.
Mission 6

Fiscal policy on the supply side

  • Some fiscal measures also shift LRAS right:
    • capital spending on infrastructure, broadband, transport
    • spending on education, training and health
    • tax incentives for investment or research
  • These raise productive capacity: more output with less pressure on prices.
  • But they take years, and the first effect is on AD (spending now).
Mission 6

Time lags and other limits

  • Automatic stabilisers: in a recession, tax revenue falls and benefit spending rises with no new decision, so AD falls less. In a boom, the reverse.
  • Discretionary policy: the government chooses to change spending or tax rates.
  • Time lags: spotting the problem, deciding, passing it into law, then waiting for it to work.
  • A stimulus that arrives late may push up demand when recovery has already begun.
  • Political pressure: tax cuts and spending rises are popular; tax rises and spending cuts are not.
  • Sustainable debt: high debt raises interest costs and may limit room for future stimulus.
Mission 6

Strengths and a real case

  • Strengths: fiscal policy can target sectors or regions (e.g. house building, low-income households).
  • It can work when interest rates are already near zero and monetary policy has little room left: a deep recession.
  • UK 2008–09: VAT was cut from 17.5% to 15% from December 2008 to December 2009, to support spending during the recession. Its effect is debated: some spending was brought forward rather than added.
  • Economists disagree on how much such measures added to output.
  • Estimates of the multiplier vary.
Quick check
?A government's budget deficit falls from 50 billion last year to 30 billion this year. What happened to the national debt this year, other things equal?
Mission 6Evaluate

How effective is fiscal policy?

It depends on:

  • Spare capacity: in a deep recession, extra demand raises output; near capacity it mostly raises prices.
  • Size of the multiplier: high saving or imports weaken the effect.
  • Time lags: stimulus can arrive too late.
  • Confidence and debt: if people expect future tax rises to repay debt, they may save more. If markets doubt debt is sustainable, interest rates may rise.
  • Crowding out: borrowing may push up interest rates and reduce private investment (stronger near full capacity).
Mission check

?A government spots a recession and plans a large spending programme. It takes 18 months to pass and start, and by then the economy is already recovering strongly. What is the main risk?

Fiscal policy can lift a slump, but its effect depends on timing and on how much spare capacity is left.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Main objectives: growth, low unemployment, low and stable inflation, a sustainable current account; also budget, environment and equality.
  • Near full capacity, pushing unemployment down tends to push inflation up.
  • Demand-led growth risks inflation; growth may harm the environment and widen inequality.
  • Supply-side policies can ease conflicts, but slowly. Choosing priorities involves value judgements.

Mission 2

  • Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level; disinflation is a fall in the inflation rate while prices still rise; deflation is a falling price level.
  • A price index compares the cost of a weighted basket with its cost in a base year (= 100). Inflation is the % change in the index, not the change in points.
  • Real values remove inflation: real = nominal × (base-year index ÷ current index). The CPI has limits, including substitution bias, quality change and its average-household basket.

Mission 3

  • Demand-pull: AD shifts right faster than capacity; prices and real output rise together.
  • Cost-push: higher costs shift SRAS left; prices rise while output falls.
  • Expectations, wage claims and import prices can keep inflation going.

Mission 4

  • Inflation is a rising price level; disinflation is a falling positive rate; deflation is a falling price level.
  • Unexpected inflation moves wealth from lenders, savers and people on fixed incomes to borrowers; unexpected deflation does the reverse.
  • Inflation also brings menu and shoe-leather costs, uncertainty and weaker competitiveness; malign deflation can spiral into falling output and jobs.

Mission 5

  • The central bank changes the policy rate to steer AD and keep inflation near its target.
  • Higher rates: borrowing costs more, asset prices lower, currency stronger. AD shifts left; inflation slows.
  • Lags, supply shocks and low confidence limit what interest rates can do.
  • Near the zero lower bound, rate cuts lose their power.

Mission 6

  • Fiscal policy uses government spending and taxes (and transfers) to influence AD, and some measures also raise AS.
  • Expansionary policy shifts AD right to close a negative (recessionary) gap; contractionary policy shifts AD left to close a positive (inflationary) gap.
  • Deficit = yearly flow; debt = stock. Lags, debt and spare capacity limit effectiveness.
  • Crowding out may limit it too.
