Mission 1

Mission 1: Why does barium fizz in cold water when magnesium, in the same group, barely reacts?

Make a guess: Why does barium fizz in cold water when magnesium, in the same group, barely reacts?

Mission 1

Group 2 atoms

  • Outer configuration ns²: Mg [Ne] 3s², Ca [Ar] 4s², Sr [Kr] 5s², Ba [Xe] 6s².
  • The outer electrons are in an s sub-shell: Group 2 is in the s-block.
  • The metals react by losing both s electrons: M → M²⁺ + 2e⁻.
  • Losing electrons is oxidation: oxidation state 0 → +2. The metals are reducing agents.
Mission 1

Trends down the group

  • Atomic radius increases: one extra shell per period.
  • First ionisation energy decreases: the outer electrons are further from the nucleus and more shielded. This outweighs the rising nuclear charge.
  • Second ionisation energy: M⁺(g) → M²⁺(g) + e⁻. It also decreases down the group, for the same reasons.
Mission 1

The metals react

  • Water: Ca, Sr, Ba fizz, faster down the group: M(s) + 2H2O(l) → M(OH)2(aq) + H2(g).
  • Mg with cold water: very slow. Mg(s) + 2H2O(l) → Mg(OH)2(s) + H2(g).
  • Mg with steam: burns with a bright white light. Mg(s) + H2O(g) → MgO(s) + H2(g).
  • Oxygen: 2M(s) + O2(g) → 2MO(s), white solids. Mg burns with a brilliant white flame.
  • Dilute acids: M(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MCl2(aq) + H2(g), faster down the group.
Mission 1

Why reactivity increases down the group

  • To react, each atom must lose two electrons: energy needed ≈ first IE + second IE.
  • This total falls down the group: Mg 2189 → Ca 1735 → Sr 1614 → Ba 1468 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Ba's outer electrons are in shell 6: far from the nucleus and heavily shielded. Mg's are in shell 3.
  • So barium loses its electrons far more easily: it fizzes, while Mg barely reacts with cold water.
Quick check
?Which equation shows the reaction of strontium with cold water?
Mission 1

Oxides and hydroxides are bases

  • Oxides with water: MO(s) + H2O(l) → M(OH)2. CaO(s) + H2O(l) → Ca(OH)2(aq).
  • Approximate pH: Mg(OH)2 ≈ 10 · Ca(OH)2 ≈ 12 · Sr(OH)2, Ba(OH)2 ≈ 13.
  • Alkalinity increases down the group: the hydroxides get more soluble, so more OH⁻ is released.
  • With dilute acids: MgO(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2O(l).
  • Ca(OH)2(aq) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + 2H2O(l).
Quick check
?Equal amounts of magnesium oxide and barium oxide are each shaken with water containing universal indicator. Which results are expected?
Mission 1

Group 2 compounds as bases

  • Ca(OH)2 in agriculture neutralises acid soils: Ca(OH)2 + 2H⁺ → Ca²⁺ + 2H2O.
  • Antacids neutralise excess stomach acid (HCl):
  • Mg(OH)2 + 2HCl → MgCl2 + 2H2O.
  • CaCO3 + 2HCl → CaCl2 + H2O + CO2.
Mission check

?Strontium reacts more vigorously than calcium with dilute hydrochloric acid. The sum of the first and second ionisation energies is 1735 kJ mol⁻¹ for calcium and 1614 kJ mol⁻¹ for strontium. Which explains the difference?

Same reason barium fizzes and magnesium barely reacts: down Group 2 the outer electrons are lost more easily.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Chlorine was a poison gas in the First World War. So why do we put it in our drinking water?

Make a guess: Chlorine was a poison gas in the First World War. So why do we put it in our drinking water?

Mission 2

Meet the halogens

  • Halogens exist as diatomic molecules: F2, Cl2, Br2, I2.
  • Outer shell s²p⁵: one electron short of a full shell.
  • In most reactions they gain one electron, forming 1− ions (halide ions).
  • At room temperature: F2 pale yellow gas · Cl2 pale green gas · Br2 red-brown liquid · I2 grey-black solid, giving a purple vapour on warming.
Mission 2

Boiling points rise down the group

  • Boiling points: F2 −188 °C · Cl2 −34 °C · Br2 59 °C · I2 184 °C. Melting points rise the same way.
  • Down the group, molecules have more electrons.
  • More electrons → bigger temporary (induced) dipoles → stronger London forces between molecules.
  • More energy is needed to separate the molecules, so the state changes: gas → gas → liquid → solid.
  • Boiling separates molecules; the covalent X–X bond does not break.
Quick check
?At room temperature iodine is a solid but chlorine is a gas. Which statement explains this?
Mission 2

A halogen displaces halides below it

  • Add a halogen solution to a halide solution. A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive one.
  • Cl2(aq) + 2Br⁻(aq) → 2Cl⁻(aq) + Br2(aq): solution turns yellow-orange.
  • Cl2(aq) + 2I⁻(aq) → 2Cl⁻(aq) + I2(aq): solution turns brown.
  • Br2(aq) + 2I⁻(aq) → 2Br⁻(aq) + I2(aq): solution turns brown.
  • I2 does not react with Cl⁻ or Br⁻. Br2 does not react with Cl⁻.
  • These are redox reactions. In the first, Cl goes 0 → −1 (reduced); Br goes −1 → 0 (oxidised).
  • The halogen is the oxidising agent. Oxidising power: Cl2 > Br2 > I2.
Mission 2

Shake with an organic solvent

  • Bromine water and iodine solution can look alike. Add hexane (or cyclohexane), shake and let it settle.
  • Halogens are non-polar, so they are only slightly soluble in water but dissolve well in hexane.
  • They move into the top, organic layer (hexane is less dense than water).
  • In hexane: Cl2 pale green (almost colourless) · Br2 orange-red · I2 violet (purple is also accepted).
  • In water: Cl2 pale green · Br2 yellow-orange · I2 brown.
  • A violet top layer means iodine has formed.
Mission 2

Why oxidising power falls

  • A halogen reacts by gaining an electron into its outer shell.
  • Down the group: larger atomic radius, so the incoming electron is further from the nucleus.
  • More shielding by inner shells.
  • Nuclear charge rises, but distance and shielding win: weaker attraction for the extra electron.
  • So it gets harder to form 1− ions: reactivity and oxidising power decrease down the group.
Quick check
?Bromine water is added separately to aqueous potassium chloride and to aqueous potassium iodide. Which observation is expected?
Mission 2

Chlorine and water

  • Cl2(aq) + H2O(l) ⇌ HCl(aq) + HClO(aq).
  • HClO is chloric(I) acid. It ionises a little: HClO ⇌ H⁺ + ClO⁻ (chlorate(I) ion).
  • Chlorine's oxidation number: 0 → −1 (in Cl⁻) and 0 → +1 (in ClO⁻).
  • The same element is oxidised and reduced in one reaction: disproportionation.
Quick check
?In which reaction is chlorine both oxidised and reduced?
Mission 2

Water treatment: benefits and risks

  • HClO and ClO⁻ are strong oxidising agents: they kill bacteria and other microbes.
  • Where water is chlorinated, outbreaks of water-borne disease such as cholera and typhoid have largely ended.
  • A small amount stays in the water, so it keeps protecting it in the pipes.
  • The dose is tiny: well under 1 mg of chlorine per dm³ at the tap.
  • Risks: chlorine gas is toxic, so storing and handling it is hazardous.
  • Chlorine reacts with organic matter in water to form chlorinated hydrocarbons (e.g. CHCl3), a possible long-term health risk.
  • Ethics: people's right to choose. Alternatives include ozone and UV light.
Mission 2

Bleach: chlorine and cold dilute alkali

  • Cl2 + 2NaOH → NaCl + NaClO + H2O (cold, dilute NaOH).
  • Ionic: Cl2 + 2OH⁻ → Cl⁻ + ClO⁻ + H2O. Chlorine again goes 0 → −1 and 0 → +1.
  • The solution of sodium chlorate(I) is bleach.
  • Uses: bleaching paper and fabric; disinfecting surfaces; cleaning; treating water.
  • Other halogens react in the same way: Br2 + 2OH⁻ → Br⁻ + BrO⁻ + H2O.
Mission check

?Swimming pools are often disinfected by adding a white solid, Ca(ClO)2, instead of chlorine gas. Why does it kill bacteria in the same way as chlorine does?

Chlorine kills germs because in water it forms HClO and ClO⁻, strong oxidising agents. A tiny dose protects millions of people; a lungful of the gas is a very different thing.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why does zinc fizz in acid when a gold ring doesn't?

Make a guess: Why does zinc fizz in acid when a gold ring doesn't?

Mission 3

Halogens: oxidising power

  • Halogens react by gaining electrons: X2 + 2e⁻ → 2X⁻. They are oxidising agents.
  • Oxidising power decreases down the group: Cl2 > Br2 > I2.
  • Down the group the atom is larger and there is more shielding.
  • So an incoming electron is attracted less strongly by the nucleus.
  • Gaining an electron (forming X⁻) gets harder down the group.
Quick check
?Which statement about the relative ease of reduction of the halogens is correct?
Mission 3

Halogen displacement

  • A halogen oxidises the halide ions of any halogen below it.
  • Cl2(aq) + 2Br⁻(aq) → 2Cl⁻(aq) + Br2(aq)
  • Full equation: Cl2(aq) + 2KBr(aq) → 2KCl(aq) + Br2(aq).
  • Cl2 + 2I⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + I2 · Br2 + 2I⁻ → 2Br⁻ + I2.
  • Br2 + Cl⁻, I2 + Cl⁻, I2 + Br⁻: no reaction.
  • K⁺ is a spectator ion: it is left out of the ionic equation.
Mission 3

Seeing it: colours

  • Br2 and I2 can look alike in water. Add cyclohexane and shake.
  • The halogen moves into the top (organic) layer. Violet means iodine; orange means bromine.
  • Test each halogen with each halide. The more reactions it gives, the more reactive it is.
Quick check
?Which mixture reacts, as shown by its equation?
Quick check
?Chlorine water is added to potassium iodide solution: Cl2(aq) + 2I⁻(aq) → 2Cl⁻(aq) + I2(aq). Which statement is correct?
Mission 3

Background: metals and the gold ring

  • Metals react by losing electrons: they are reducing agents. This is the opposite of halogens.
  • A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive one from its ions: Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s).
  • Metals more reactive than hydrogen reduce H⁺ to hydrogen gas.
  • Zn(s) + 2HCl(aq) → ZnCl2(aq) + H2(g). Ionic: Zn(s) + 2H⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + H2(g). Fizzing = H2.
  • Gold loses electrons far less readily than hydrogen does: it can't reduce H⁺, so no reaction.
  • Your course meets metals with acids in redox (2.1.5) and group 2 reactions, not a metal reactivity series.
Mission check

?A few drops of bromine water are added to a colourless solution containing both potassium chloride and potassium iodide, in excess. Cyclohexane is then added and the mixture shaken. What colour is the top layer, and why?

Same rule as zinc and gold. What reacts depends on how readily electrons are lost or gained.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Three salt solutions look exactly alike. How can a few drops of one reagent tell them apart?

Make a guess: Three salt solutions look exactly alike. How can a few drops of one reagent tell them apart?

Mission 4

The silver nitrate test

  • Add dilute nitric acid, then silver nitrate solution.
  • Silver halides are insoluble, so a precipitate forms: Ag⁺(aq) + X⁻(aq) → AgX(s).
  • Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s): white.
  • Ag⁺(aq) + Br⁻(aq) → AgBr(s): cream.
  • Ag⁺(aq) + I⁻(aq) → AgI(s): yellow.
Mission 4

Why acidify, and why nitric acid?

  • Carbonate and hydroxide ions also give precipitates with Ag⁺ (Ag2CO3, Ag2O).
  • Acid removes them first: CO3²⁻ + 2H⁺ → CO2 + H2O; OH⁻ + H⁺ → H2O.
  • Use nitric acid: hydrochloric acid would add Cl⁻ and give AgCl whatever the sample.
  • Not sulfuric acid either: silver sulfate is only sparingly soluble.
  • Silver nitrate is used because silver halides are insoluble and each has its own colour.
Quick check
?A student tests a solution for halide ions by adding silver nitrate solution acidified with dilute hydrochloric acid. A white precipitate forms. What can the student conclude?
Mission 4

Ammonia confirms the halide

  • White, cream and yellow are hard to tell apart. Add ammonia solution to the precipitate.
  • AgCl: dissolves in dilute NH3(aq).
  • AgBr: does not dissolve in dilute NH3(aq); dissolves in concentrated NH3(aq).
  • AgI: does not dissolve, even in concentrated NH3(aq).
  • Solubility in ammonia decreases from AgCl to AgI.
  • You need the observations only, not the complex.
Quick check
?An unknown sodium halide solution is acidified and silver nitrate is added. The precipitate does not dissolve in dilute ammonia solution, but does dissolve in concentrated ammonia solution. Which ion is present?
Mission check

?A solution contains both potassium chloride and potassium iodide. Acidified silver nitrate is added until no more precipitate forms. Then excess concentrated ammonia solution is added and the mixture is shaken. What is seen at the end?

Silver nitrate gives each halide its own coloured precipitate, and ammonia confirms which is which, even in a mixture.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Down Group 2: radius up, IE down, so the metals lose their two s electrons more easily and reactivity rises.

Mission 2

  • Down Group 7: boiling point rises (stronger London forces); oxidising power falls (bigger atoms, more shielding). A halogen displaces halides below it.
  • Chlorine disproportionates (0 → −1 and +1) in water and cold dilute alkali; the HClO/ClO⁻ formed disinfects water and bleaches.

Mission 3

  • Oxidising power: Cl2 > Br2 > I2. A halogen displaces the halides below it; colours in cyclohexane: Br2 orange, I2 violet.
  • Background: zinc is oxidised readily enough to reduce H⁺ to H2; gold is not.

Mission 4

  • Acidify with dilute HNO3, add AgNO3(aq): AgCl white, AgBr cream, AgI yellow. AgCl dissolves in dilute NH3, AgBr in concentrated NH3, AgI in neither.
