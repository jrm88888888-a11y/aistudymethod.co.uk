Mission 1

Mission 1: Why does an economy grow in bursts and slumps instead of at a steady pace?

Make a guess: Why does an economy grow in bursts and slumps instead of at a steady pace?

Mission 1

The trend and the cycle

  • Plot real GDP against time.
  • The trend line shows potential output: it rises steadily as productive capacity grows (long-term growth).
  • Actual real GDP wobbles above and below the trend: short-term fluctuations.
  • These ups and downs are the business cycle.
  • Also called the economic cycle or trade cycle: the same thing.
  • Say: "actual output fell below potential" — not: "potential output fell", when the economy has spare capacity.
Mission 1

The phases of the cycle

  • Boom: fast growth, actual output above trend. Peak: the top.
  • Downturn (slowdown): growth falls. Recession: real GDP falls.
  • Trough: the bottom. Recovery (upturn): growth returns.
  • Common definition of a recession: real GDP falls for two consecutive quarters.
  • Say: "a slowdown: growth falls but stays positive" — not: "a recession".

Mission 1

Output gaps

  • Output gap = actual output − potential output.
  • Negative output gap: actual below potential.
  • Spare capacity, idle machines, cyclical unemployment. Downward pressure on inflation.
  • Positive output gap: actual above potential.
  • Overtime, shortages of workers, capacity stretched. Upward pressure on wages and prices. It cannot last.
  • Significance: the gap shows whether inflation is likely to rise or fall, and how much room the economy has to grow without inflation.
Mission 1

Output gaps on AD/AS and the PPC

  • AD/AS: LRAS is vertical at full-employment output (Yf).
  • AD1 meets SRAS left of Yf: output Y1 < Yf.
  • That is a negative output gap.
  • AD2 meets SRAS right of Yf: output Y2 > Yf.
  • That is a positive output gap.
  • On a PPC, a negative gap is a point inside the curve: resources are unemployed.
  • A positive gap is output beyond its sustainable level. Resources are over-used (overtime, machines run without maintenance).
Mission 1

Worked example: the size of the gap

  • Illustrative data.
  • Potential output = 2,000 bn. Actual real GDP = 1,950 bn.
  • Gap = 1,950 − 2,000 = −50 bn.
  • As % of potential: −50 / 2,000 × 100 = −2.5%.
  • A negative output gap of 2.5% of potential output.
  • Two years later, actual = 2,030 bn and potential = 2,000 bn: gap = +30 bn = +1.5% of potential.

Mission 1

Why gaps are hard to measure

  • Actual output is measured; potential output is not. It has to be estimated.
  • Estimates use past trends, business surveys of spare capacity, and unemployment data.
  • Different institutions get different answers, and revise them later.
  • GDP itself is revised. UK example: the fall in real GDP in the 2008–09 recession was estimated at 5.8% in earlier data, then revised to 6.3% (OBR, 2012).
  • So policymakers may not know in real time whether the gap is positive or negative.
Mission 1

Why output swings: shocks and instability

  • Demand shocks shift AD: a collapse in confidence, a financial crisis, falling export demand, a big change in government spending.
  • Supply shocks shift SRAS: a sharp rise in oil prices, a pandemic, a harvest failure.
  • Real case: UK real GDP fell for five quarters in a row and shrank by more than 6% between early 2008 and mid-2009. It took five years to regain its pre-recession size (ONS, 2018).
Mission 1

Smoothing the cycle

  • Automatic stabilisers: changes in tax revenue and spending that happen without any new decision.
    • Recession: incomes fall → tax revenue falls; unemployment rises → benefit spending rises. AD falls by less.
    • Boom: tax revenue rises; benefit spending falls. AD rises by less.
  • Discretionary fiscal policy: the government chooses to raise spending or cut taxes in a recession, and the reverse in a boom.
Quick check
?Illustrative data: potential output is estimated at 800 bn. Actual real GDP is 776 bn. Which statement is correct?
Mission 1Evaluate

Should governments try to close every output gap?

It depends on:

  • Measurement: potential output is estimated, so the size and even the sign of the gap may be wrong.
  • Time lags: policy can take a year or more to work, so it may arrive when the gap has closed.
  • The cause: a demand shock suits demand-side policy; after a supply shock, boosting AD can add to inflation.
  • Size and length: a small, short gap may close on its own; a deep recession can leave lasting damage.
Mission check

?A surge in confidence and borrowing has kept a country's actual output above its potential output for two years. Unemployment is below its natural rate. Which is most likely to happen next?

When demand pushes output above what the economy can sustain, inflation tends to rise. When it falls below, workers and machines sit idle.

Mission 2: Why does a government's borrowing jump in a recession even when nobody votes for it?

Mission 2

Two ways the budget changes

  • Discretionary fiscal policy: a deliberate decision to change tax rates or government spending. E.g. a budget that cuts income tax or funds new roads.
  • Automatic stabilisers: changes in tax revenue and transfer spending that happen on their own as the economy moves through the trade cycle (also called the business cycle). No new decision is needed.
  • Tax rates and benefit rules stay the same. What changes is how much income, spending and unemployment there is.
Mission 2

How stabilisers work

In a recession:

  • Incomes fall → income tax paid falls. Spending falls → sales taxes (VAT) collected fall.
  • With a progressive tax, tax falls proportionally more than income.
  • Unemployment rises → unemployment benefit payments rise.
  • So disposable income falls by less than income. Consumption, and AD, fall by less.

Illustrative data:

  • Income tax: 0% on the first 10,000 · 20% from 10,000 to 50,000 · 40% above 50,000.
  • Income falls from 60,000 to 40,000 (−33%). Tax falls from 12,000 to 6,000 (−50%).
  • In a boom, the reverse: tax rises faster than income and benefit spending falls. This slows the rise in AD.
  • Say: "automatic stabilisers reduce the size of fluctuations" — not: "they stop recessions".

Mission 2

Stabilisers and the deficit

  • Lower tax revenue + higher benefit spending = a bigger deficit in a recession, with no new decision.
  • In a boom the deficit shrinks (or a surplus grows) automatically.
  • 2008–09 example: UK borrowing rose to about 10% of GDP in 2009–10. Part was automatic (falling tax receipts, rising benefit claims). Part was discretionary, e.g. a temporary VAT cut from 17.5% to 15% (December 2008 to December 2009).
Mission 2

Automatic vs discretionary: strengths and limits

Automatic stabilisers:

  • Fast: no time lag for recognising the problem or passing a law.
  • Work in both directions: they cushion recessions and cool booms.
  • But partial: they reduce fluctuations; they do not remove them.

Discretionary policy:

  • Can be larger and targeted (e.g. at infrastructure or at low-income households).
  • But faces time lags: recognising the problem, deciding, then putting it into effect. It may arrive after recovery has begun.
  • Can face political pressure: tax cuts and spending rises are popular; reversing them in a boom is not.
Mission 2

Fiscal rules

  • A fiscal rule is a lasting limit on borrowing or debt, set to build lenders' trust and resist political pressure to overspend.
  • Examples:
  • EU rules: deficit no more than 3% of GDP; debt no more than 60% of GDP.
  • UK (2024): balance the current budget (borrow only to invest) and have a debt measure falling as a share of GDP, both by 2029–30.
  • Design problem: a rule on the actual deficit can force tax rises or cuts in a recession, which works against the automatic stabilisers.
  • So many rules target the structural (cyclically adjusted) balance, a forecast year, or have escape clauses. The EU suspended its deficit limits from 2020 to 2023.
  • Trade-off: flexible rules let stabilisers work; strict rules are more credible.
Quick check
?A country has a rule that its actual deficit must not exceed 3% of GDP. A recession pushes the deficit from 2% to 4% of GDP. To obey the rule at once, the government raises tax rates. What is the likely effect?
Mission 2Evaluate

How much can stabilisers do?

It depends on:

  • Progressivity and benefits: steeper tax rates and fuller unemployment benefits give a bigger cushion. Countries with larger public sectors tend to have stronger stabilisers.
  • Size of the shock: in a deep recession stabilisers cushion only part of the fall; discretionary policy may be needed too.
  • Debt and rules: high debt or strict rules on the actual deficit can force governments to work against the stabilisers.
  • Incentives: high marginal tax rates and generous benefits may reduce incentives to work, a long-run cost to weigh.
  • Confidence: if households save rather than spend, even well-designed support raises AD less.
Mission check

?Country A has a progressive income tax and pays unemployment benefits. Country B charges every adult the same fixed amount of tax and pays no unemployment benefits. Both suffer the same fall in exports. Neither government takes any new decision. Which statement is correct?

Automatic stabilisers cushion AD in a downturn, and the price is a larger cyclical deficit.

Mission 3

What sustainable development means

  • Sustainable development: "development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs" (Brundtland Report, 1987).
  • Three parts that must hold together:
    • economic: rising incomes and output
    • social: health, education, fairness, less poverty
    • environmental: clean air and water, stable climate, renewable stocks of fish, forests and soil
  • Natural capital: the stock of natural resources that yields output, such as forests, fish stocks, soil and minerals.
  • Say: "sustainable growth" — growth that does not reduce the ability to produce in the future — not just "fast growth".
Mission 3

Growth in poorer countries: benefits and costs

  • Benefits:
    • higher incomes and fewer people in absolute poverty
    • more tax revenue for health, education and clean water
    • more jobs; more saving to fund investment
  • Costs:
    • pollution, deforestation and loss of soil and fish stocks
    • rising inequality if gains go mainly to some groups or regions
    • crowded cities and strained services as people move from the countryside
    • non-renewable resources (oil, minerals) used up
  • Whether growth is worth its costs depends on how it happens, not only how fast.

Mission 3

The Millennium Development Goals (2000–2015)

  • In 2000, UN members agreed 8 MDGs for developing countries, with targets for 2015.

1. Eradicate extreme poverty and hunger (target: halve the poverty rate)

2. Universal primary education

3. Gender equality

4. Reduce child mortality

5. Improve maternal health

6. Combat HIV/AIDS, malaria and other diseases

7. Environmental sustainability

8. A global partnership for development

  • Results (UN MDG Report, 2015):
    • extreme poverty in developing regions: 47% of people in 1990 → 14% in 2015
    • people in extreme poverty: 1.9 billion → 836 million
    • under-five deaths: 90 → 43 per 1,000 live births
  • Progress was uneven: China and India played a central role in the fall in poverty.
Mission 3

The Sustainable Development Goals (2015–2030)

  • Adopted by the UN in 2015: 17 goals, 169 targets, for 2030.
  • Examples: No poverty (1) · Zero hunger (2) · Quality education (4) · Clean water and sanitation (6) · Affordable and clean energy (7) · Reduced inequalities (10) · Climate action (13).
  • How they differ from the MDGs:
    • they apply to all countries, not only developing ones
    • they give the environment and inequality a bigger place
    • they aim to end poverty, not halve it
  • Critics: 169 targets are hard to prioritise and measure; supporters say they cover what development really means.

Mission 3

Inclusive growth and climate mitigation

  • Inclusive growth: growth whose gains are shared widely, across income groups, regions and genders, with access to jobs, education and health.
  • Growth that is not inclusive can raise GDP per head while poverty and inequality persist.
  • Sustainable growth must also avoid running down natural capital.
  • Climate mitigation: reducing greenhouse gas emissions, e.g. carbon taxes, emissions trading, investment in renewable energy, energy efficiency.
  • Trade-off: mitigation can raise costs now (higher energy prices) to avoid larger costs later.
  • (Adaptation is different: adjusting to climate change, e.g. flood defences, drought-resistant crops.)
Mission 3Evaluate

Can growth be sustainable?

It depends on:

  • How growth is produced: renewable vs non-renewable resources; clean vs polluting technology.
  • Who gains: inclusive growth spreads gains; narrow growth can raise inequality.
  • Policy and institutions: property rights, pollution taxes, catch limits, and whether they are enforced.
  • Time horizon: costs often fall on future generations, who cannot vote today.
  • Measurement: GDP does not subtract the loss of natural capital, so growth figures can flatter.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. A coastal country raises its fish catch so that fish exports grow fast. GDP rises 6% this year. Scientists report that the fish stock is now shrinking each year, because the catch is larger than the number of new fish born. Which conclusion is best supported?

Growth is sustainable only if it does not reduce the ability of future generations to produce and meet their needs.

Mission 4

Two kinds of immobility

  • Labour mobility: how easily workers can move between jobs and places.
  • Occupational immobility: barriers to changing job or occupation.
    • lack of skills or qualifications for the new job
    • time and cost of training; licences (nursing, electricians)
    • age, confidence, discrimination
  • Geographical immobility: barriers to changing location.
    • big gaps in house prices and rents between regions
    • family ties, children's schooling
    • cost of moving; poor information about jobs elsewhere
  • Say: "immobility of labour causes structural unemployment" — not: "immobility is unemployment". Immobility is a cause; unemployment can be its effect.
Quick check
?A steelworker loses her job when the plant closes. There are vacancies for software testers in the same town, but she has none of the skills they ask for. Which barrier is she facing?
Mission 4

Why immobility is a market failure

  • Demand for labour keeps changing: some industries shrink, others grow.
  • Mobile workers move to the growing jobs, and wage differences signal where to go.
  • Immobile workers cannot respond to those signals.
  • Result: surpluses of labour in some markets, shortages in others, at the same time.
  • Labour is misallocated, so output is below what the economy could produce.
Mission 4

Policies to improve mobility

  • Occupational mobility:
    • education, training and apprenticeships; retraining grants for adults
    • recognising qualifications from other regions and countries
    • better careers information
  • Geographical mobility:
    • more housing where the jobs are (planning reform, house building)
    • relocation grants; better transport links
    • online job information across regions
  • Move jobs to people: regional policy, e.g. grants or tax breaks for firms that invest in high-unemployment areas.
  • Match the policy to the cause: supply-side policies for structural and frictional unemployment; demand-side (fiscal, monetary) policies for cyclical unemployment.
Mission 4

Migration and the labour market

  • Immigration adds workers: the supply of labour shifts right (S1 → S2).
  • On its own: the wage falls and employment rises in that market.
  • But migrants also spend their wages. Labour is a derived demand, so the demand for labour shifts right too (D1 → D2).
  • Migrants may complement local workers (different skills), raising local workers' productivity, not competing with them.
  • They can fill skill shortages, e.g. in health and social care.
  • Most migrants are of working age, so they can add more in taxes than they use in services; the size of this effect depends on their jobs and pay.
  • Emigration has its own effects on the country people leave:
    • brain drain: it loses skilled workers it paid to train
    • but it gains money sent home by emigrants (remittances), and unemployment can fall

  • Takeaway: if demand for labour rises as much as supply, the wage need not fall; who gains and who loses depends on the size of each shift.
Mission 4

Real world: what the evidence says

  • Many countries, e.g. Canada and Australia, use points-based systems that admit workers by skills, qualifications and language.
  • A UK government advisory body (the Migration Advisory Committee, 2018) found migrants had "no or little impact" on the overall employment and unemployment of UK-born workers.
  • It added that effects may be more negative for lower-skilled and more positive for higher-skilled UK-born workers.
  • Geographical immobility: in England in 2023, a typical home cost 8.3 times the annual earnings of a full-time employee working in the same area. In 82% of London local authorities the ratio was above 12 (ONS).
  • So high-wage areas with high housing costs can find it hard to attract workers from cheaper regions.
Mission 4Evaluate

Will the policy work?

It depends on:

  • Substitutes or complements. Migrants with the same skills as local workers compete for jobs; those with different skills can raise local workers' pay.
  • Time. In the short run supply shifts first; over time demand rises as migrants spend and firms invest.
  • The cause of the problem. Retraining fixes occupational immobility but not a housing shortage; relocation grants fail if there are no affordable homes.
  • Government failure. Training schemes may teach skills employers do not want; policies are costly and take years.
Mission check

?A car factory closes. Hundreds of former car workers in the town are unemployed, while the town's hospital cannot fill vacancies for health care assistants. Which policy targets the main cause best?

Match the cure to the barrier: skills for occupational immobility, housing and information for geographical immobility.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Actual real GDP fluctuates around a rising trend (potential output): the business cycle.
  • Phases: boom, peak, downturn, recession, trough, recovery.
  • Output gap = actual − potential. Below potential: spare capacity. Above potential: inflationary pressure.
  • Demand and supply shocks cause the swings.

Mission 2

  • Discretionary policy = a deliberate decision. Automatic stabilisers = tax and transfers that change with the cycle, with no decision.
  • In a recession, tax revenue falls and benefit spending rises: AD falls by less, the deficit rises.
  • Fiscal rules must leave room for stabilisers to work, or they can deepen a recession.

Mission 3

  • Sustainable development meets present needs without compromising future generations' ability to meet theirs: economic, social and environmental.
  • MDGs (2000–2015, 8 goals) → SDGs (2015–2030, 17 goals, for all countries). Progress is uneven between countries.
  • Inclusive growth shares gains widely; climate mitigation cuts emissions now to avoid larger costs later.

Mission 4

  • Occupational immobility (skills) and geographical immobility (place) leave surpluses and shortages side by side: a market failure and a cause of structural unemployment.
  • Immigration shifts labour supply right, and demand right as migrants spend: the wage effect depends on both shifts.
