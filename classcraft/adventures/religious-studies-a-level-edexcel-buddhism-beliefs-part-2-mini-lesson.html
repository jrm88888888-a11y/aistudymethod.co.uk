Mission 1

What anatta denies

  • Anatta (Sanskrit anatman): not-self, the third mark of existence.
  • In the Brahmanical thought of the Buddha's day (e.g. the Upanishads), the atman is an eternal, unchanging self, the true owner of experience, one with ultimate reality (Brahman).
  • The Buddha taught that no such self is found in any part of experience: nothing permanent, independent and fully under our control.
  • Anatta does not say people do not exist. "Ruth" is a real, changing person; what is denied is a fixed core behind her.
Mission 1

The five khandhas

  • A person is analysed into five khandhas ("heaps", "aggregates"; Sanskrit skandhas):
  • rupa: form: the body and the physical world it meets.
  • vedana: feeling: each experience as pleasant, unpleasant or neutral.
  • sanna: perception: recognising and labelling ("red", "friend").
  • sankhara: mental formations: will, habits, intentions; this is where karma is made.
  • vinnana: consciousness: awareness through the six senses (the mind counts as the sixth).
  • Buddhists teach that all five are impermanent and depend on each other, and that none of them, and nothing beyond them, is a self.
Mission 1Read the source

The second sermon

Anattalakkhana Sutta ("Discourse on the Characteristic of Not-self"), Samyutta Nikaya 22.59, given to the five ascetics after the first sermon (paraphrase):

  • If form were self, it would not lead to affliction, and one could say: "Let my form be thus; let it not be thus." Because form is not-self, it leads to affliction and cannot be ordered about. The same holds for feeling, perception, formations and consciousness.
  • The Buddha asks: is form permanent or impermanent? Impermanent. Is what is impermanent dukkha or happiness? Dukkha. Is it fitting to regard what is impermanent, dukkha and subject to change as "this is mine, this I am, this is my self"? No.

Mission 1

Neither eternalism nor annihilationism

  • Eternalism (sassatavada): an eternal self survives death unchanged.
  • Annihilationism (ucchedavada): a self exists now but is destroyed at death, so nothing carries on and deeds bear no further fruit.
  • The Buddha rejects both. In Samyutta Nikaya 44.10 the wanderer Vacchagotta asks "Is there a self?" and "Is there no self?" The Buddha stays silent.
  • He tells Ananda why (paraphrase): "yes" would side with the eternalists; "no" would side with the annihilationists and confuse Vacchagotta further.
  • The middle way between them is dependent origination: a process of causes and effects continues, with no lasting owner (Samyutta Nikaya 12.15;).
Mission 1Read the source

Nagasena's chariot

The Questions of King Milinda (Milinda Panha), Book II, chapter 1, section 1 (paraphrase). The Greek king Milinda (Menander I, 2nd century BCE) meets the monk Nagasena.

  • Nagasena: "I am called Nagasena, but that is only a name, a way of speaking. No permanent individual is found here."
  • The king objects: then who keeps the precepts, who meditates, who reaches nibbana? Is the hair Nagasena? The body? Feeling, perception, formations, consciousness? All together? Something apart from them? To each, Nagasena says no. "Then 'Nagasena' is an empty sound!"
  • Nagasena asks how the king came. "By chariot." Is the pole the chariot? The axle? The wheels? The frame? The yoke? All together? Something apart? No to each. "Then 'chariot' is an empty sound!"
  • The king: "No: 'chariot' is the name used because of all these parts." Nagasena: "Just so: because of the body and the five khandhas, I am called Nagasena."
  • He quotes the nun Vajira (Samyutta Nikaya 5.10): as "chariot" is said when the parts come together, so "a being" is said when the khandhas are present.

Q1 Does Nagasena say that he does not exist?

Show answer

No: "Nagasena" is a correct name for a real, changing process; there is just no separate owner.

Mission 1

Two levels of truth

  • Theravada Abhidhamma distinguishes:
  • conventional truth (sammuti-sacca): persons, chariots, names; true for everyday speech.
  • ultimate truth (paramattha-sacca): momentary physical and mental events (dhammas) with no self among them.
  • Buddhaghosa (5th century CE) uses the chariot simile again in the Visuddhimagga (XVIII): "being" and "person" are only ways of speaking about name-and-form.
  • His famous verse (Visuddhimagga XVI, paraphrase): there is suffering, but no one who suffers; there are deeds, but no doer; there is nibbana, but no one who enters it; there is a path, but no traveller on it.
  • The two levels let Buddhists speak of people, choices and responsibility without positing a self.
Mission 1

Not-self or no-self? Different Buddhist views

  • Mainstream Theravada: analysis finds no self at all; anatta is a statement about reality (see Steven Collins, 1951–2018, Selfless Persons, 1982).
  • "Not-self" as a strategy: some teachers, e.g. Thanissaro Bhikkhu (b. 1949), read anatta as a practice: stop identifying with anything, without asserting "there is no self" (the Buddha's silence in SN 44.10).
  • Personalists (Pudgalavada, early Indian schools): a "person" exists that is neither the same as nor different from the khandhas. Other schools criticised this as a disguised self.
  • Mahayana: some Buddha-nature texts speak of a "true self"; most Mahayana thinkers read this as skilful means, not an atman. Emptiness extends anatta to all things.
  • Dhammakaya (Thailand) teaches nibbana as true self (atta); many Thai scholar-monks, e.g. P. A. Payutto, reject this as contrary to the Pali Canon.
Mission 1Evaluate

Does anatta undermine moral responsibility?

  • Claim: If there is no lasting self, no one can be held responsible for past actions.
  • For: Milinda's own worry: if there is no Nagasena, who keeps precepts or does wrong? Reward and blame seem to need a lasting agent.
  • For: If the person who acted and the person punished are different moments, punishment looks unfair.
  • Against: Causal continuity is enough. Nagasena's image (Milinda Panha, Book II, paraphrase): a man who steals mangoes cannot plead that they are not the ones the owner planted, because they grew from them.
  • Against: The khandhas include sankhara, intention: actions shape the same continuing stream that experiences their results.
  • Judgement criteria: Does responsibility need sameness or only connection? Does anatta make compassion easier or harder (less "me" to defend)?

Mission 2

Karma is intention

  • Karma (Pali kamma) means action: in Buddhist teaching, intentional action.
  • The Buddha (Anguttara Nikaya 6.63, paraphrase): it is intention (cetana) that I call karma; having intended, one acts by body, speech and mind.
  • Karma is not fate. It is what you are doing now, and it shapes what you will become.
  • Contrast: the Jains of the Buddha's day treated karma as a kind of matter that sticks to the soul even through unintended acts. The Buddha put intention at the centre.
Mission 2

Seeds and fruits

  • In Buddhist teaching, an intentional act plants a seed; its fruit (vipaka) ripens later: in this life, the next, or a later one.
  • Kusala (skilful, wholesome) acts spring from generosity, kindness and wisdom; they bring happiness and good rebirths. Merit (punna; Sanskrit punya) is their store of good results.
  • Akusala (unskilful, unwholesome) acts spring from greed, hatred and delusion; they bring suffering and lower rebirths.
  • Which acts make karma? Intentional acts of body, speech and mind. A truly accidental act makes little or none.
  • An arhat's acts make no new karma: with no craving left, there is no seed to sprout. Old karma can still ripen in the arhat's body until death.
Mission 2Read the source

The Dhammapada on karma

From Chapter 1, "The Twin-Verses" (Dhammapada 1, Müller, 1881):

> "All that we are is the result of what we have thought: it is founded on our thoughts, it is made up of our thoughts. If a man speaks or acts with an evil thought, pain follows him, as the wheel follows the foot of the ox that draws the carriage."

Verse 2 (paraphrase): with a pure thought, happiness follows "like a shadow". From Chapter 9, "Evil" (Dhammapada 127, Müller, 1881):

> "Not in the sky, not in the midst of the sea, not if we enter into the clefts of the mountains, is there known a spot in the whole world where a man might be freed from an evil deed."

Q1 What do the images of the cart wheel and the shadow say about karma?

Show answer

Q2 What does verse 127 rule out?

Show answer

Escaping karma by place or hiding; there is no outside judge to avoid, only results.

Mission 2

Rebirth without a soul

  • Rebirth is not a soul moving house (reincarnation of an atman). With anatta (see Mission 1), there is no traveller.
  • Buddhists explain that what continues is a stream of causes: the khandhas of one life condition the khandhas of the next, shaped by karma and craving.
  • The Questions of King Milinda, Book II, chapter 2 (paraphrase): Milinda asks whether the one reborn is the same or another. Nagasena: neither the same nor another.
  • His lamp: a flame burns all night. The flame of the last watch is not the same flame as the first, nor a different one: it depends on it.
  • His milk: milk becomes curds, then butter, then ghee: not the same, yet not unconnected.
  • Theravada Abhidhamma speaks of a rebirth-linking consciousness that arises at conception, conditioned by the last moment of the previous life.

Mission 2

Different Buddhist views of the moment of rebirth

  • Theravada (orthodox teaching): rebirth is immediate; there is no in-between state. Many lay Theravadins also make merit and dedicate it to dead relatives, who may be among the hungry ghosts.
  • Tibetan Buddhism: an intermediate state (bardo) of up to 49 days between death and rebirth.
  • Texts such as the Bardo Thodol ("Liberation through Hearing in the Intermediate State", often called The Tibetan Book of the Dead) are read to guide the dying and the dead.
  • Tibetan Buddhists also recognise tulkus: teachers reborn on purpose out of compassion.
  • Both agree: the state of mind at death strongly shapes the next rebirth.

Mission 3

Three Jewels, three refuges

  • The Three Jewels (Pali Tiratana, Sanskrit Triratna): the Buddha, the Dhamma (teaching, truth) and the Sangha (community).
  • A refuge (sarana) is a shelter. Here: shelter from dukkha and the round of rebirth (samsara).
  • Going for refuge is the basic act that marks a person as a Buddhist, in every school.
  • A common picture: the Buddha is the doctor, the Dhamma the medicine, the Sangha the nurses and fellow patients who help one recover.
  • They are called jewels: rare, precious, and worth more than anything else one could rely on.
Mission 3Read the source

The refuge formula

In Pali, from the Khuddakapatha (Khp 1), chanted by Buddhists across the world:

  • Buddham saranam gacchami · Dhammam saranam gacchami · Sangham saranam gacchami
  • Literal rendering: "To the Buddha for refuge I go. To the Dhamma for refuge I go. To the Sangha for refuge I go."
  • Then repeated with dutiyam pi ("for a second time…") and tatiyam pi ("for a third time…").

Q1 Why might the formula be said three times?

Show answer

To make the commitment firm and deliberate, not a passing thought; repetition also marks it as a formal act.

Q2 Who is "I" here?

Show answer

The person committing: the formula is a personal choice, said aloud.

Mission 3

Refuge in the Buddha

  • Historical: Siddhattha Gotama, the teacher who found the path and showed it to others. Not a creator god; for most schools, a guide rather than a saviour who acts for us (Pure Land differs: A later screen).
  • Ideal: awakening itself, the goal anyone can reach. Refuge is in the qualities of a Buddha: wisdom, compassion, purity.
  • "Whoever sees the Dhamma sees me" (Samyutta Nikaya 22.87, paraphrase): the Buddha is met in his teaching.
  • Theravada: stresses the historical Buddha as teacher and model.
  • Mahayana: many Buddhas (e.g. Amitabha) and the Buddha-nature in all beings; the Buddha is also a reality beyond one human life.
Mission 3

Refuge in the Dhamma

  • Dhamma as teaching: the words of the Buddha: the Four Noble Truths, the path.
  • Dhamma as truth: the way things really are; ultimately nibbana, unconditioned reality, which the teaching points to.
  • Traditional qualities: well proclaimed; seen here and now; timeless; ehipassiko ("come and see"); leading onward; known by the wise for themselves.
  • The raft (Majjhima Nikaya 22): the teaching is like a raft for crossing a river, to be used, not carried on one's head after crossing.

Mission 3

Refuge in the Sangha

  • Noble Sangha (ariya-sangha): those who have reached at least the first stage of awakening (stream-entry). In Theravada the strict object of refuge.
  • Monastic Sangha: monks (bhikkhus) and nuns (bhikkhunis). They keep and teach the Dhamma; lay people support them with food and gifts; the monks give teaching and blessings in return.
  • Fourfold assembly: monks, nuns, laymen, laywomen.
  • All Buddhists past, present and future.
  • Spiritual friends: one's close companions in practice. The Buddha told Ananda that good friendship is "the whole of the holy life" (Samyutta Nikaya 45.2, paraphrase).
  • Interpretations vary: Theravada stresses the monastic and noble Sangha; many Mahayana and Western groups use "sangha" for any community of practitioners.
Quick check

Mission 3

How the three fit together

  • Each refuge needs the others: the Buddha discovered the Dhamma; the Dhamma is what he taught; the Sangha preserves it and lives it.
  • Refuge in one without the others is incomplete: a teacher without teaching, a teaching without anyone practising it.
  • Bhikkhu Bodhi (b. 1944), Going for Refuge (1981), paraphrase: going for refuge is the entrance to the Buddha's teaching. Ordinary (mundane) refuge can be weakened by doubt; at stream-entry it becomes unshakeable.
  • Thanissaro Bhikkhu (b. 1949), Refuge (1996), paraphrase: the external refuges are models; the real aim is to develop the same qualities in oneself, an internal refuge.
Mission 3

Going for refuge in practice

  • Daily puja: homage to the Buddha (Namo tassa…, three times), then the three refuges, often the Five Precepts. In Sri Lanka this is called pansil.
  • Bowing three times before a shrine: one bow for each jewel.
  • Ceremony: a new Buddhist may take the refuges and precepts formally before a monk or teacher.
  • Tibetan: refuge also in the lama (teacher); many make 100,000 refuge prostrations as a preliminary practice.
  • Pure Land: the nembutsu is a calling on, and refuge in, Amida (Amitabha) Buddha.
  • Triratna (founded by Sangharakshita): Going for Refuge is the central act; ordination is "effective going for refuge".
Mission 3Diversity

Heritage and convert Buddhists

  • Heritage Buddhists: born into Buddhist families and cultures (e.g. Sri Lanka, Thailand, Tibet, and their communities in Britain).
  • Refuges chanted in Pali or another traditional language from childhood; temple-centred; the monastic Sangha central; making merit by giving.
  • Convert Buddhists: adults who choose Buddhism, often in the West.
  • A formal, often emotional decision; refuge ceremony as a turning point; often meditation-centred; "sangha" may mean one's practice group or friends.
  • Both chant the same formula. These are tendencies, not rules: many heritage Buddhists meditate; many converts join traditional temples.
Mission 3Read the source

Which refuge is safe?

> "Men, driven by fear, go to many a refuge, to mountains and forests, to groves and sacred trees." (Dhammapada 188, Müller 1881)

> "He who takes refuge with Buddha, the Law, and the Church; he who, with clear understanding, sees the four holy truths" (Dhammapada 190, Müller 1881)

  • Müller's "the Law" is Dhamma; "the Church" is Sangha. Verse 192: that refuge is "the best refuge".
  • Buddhist analysis: worldly refuges, ancient (sacred trees) or modern (money, status), are impermanent (anicca), so they cannot give final security. They may still be wholesome supports.

Q1 What drives people to the first kind of refuge?

Show answer

Fear.

Q2 Why might a Buddhist say a bank account is a refuge like the "sacred trees"?

Show answer

It is relied on for safety, but it changes and cannot end dukkha.

Mission 3Evaluate

Which refuge matters most?

  • Claim: The Dhamma is the most important of the three refuges.
  • For: before his death the Buddha said the Dhamma and discipline would be the teacher after him (Digha Nikaya 16).
  • For: the Buddha and the Sangha both point to the Dhamma; "whoever sees the Dhamma sees me".
  • Against: without the Buddha, the Dhamma would not have been found and taught.
  • Against: without the Sangha, the Dhamma would not have been kept or passed on; for many converts, the Sangha is what makes practice possible.
  • Against: refuge is taken in all three together; the jewels may not be meant to be ranked.
  • Judgement criteria: Which refuge is most needed now that the Buddha has died? Do heritage and convert Buddhists answer differently? Is ranking the jewels a Buddhist question?

Mission 4

What is the Sangha?

  • Sangha: "community"; the third of the Three Refuges (Buddha, Dhamma, Sangha).
  • Monastic Sangha: ordained monks (bhikkhus) and nuns (bhikkhunis), living by the Vinaya.
  • Noble Sangha (ariya-sangha): all who have reached at least stream-entry, monastic or lay. For Theravada, the refuge in its fullest sense.
  • Fourfold assembly: monks, nuns, laymen and laywomen together.
  • Interpretations differ: Theravada stresses the monastic Sangha; many Mahayana and Western groups use "sangha" for the whole community.
Mission 4

Traditional roles of monks

  • The wat (temple-monastery) was the centre of village life.
  • Education: before state schools spread (from the 1920s), boys learned to read at the wat; novices still study Pali and Dhamma.
  • Healing and counsel: herbal medicine, advice, settling disputes.
  • Ceremonies: chanting at funerals, house-blessings and new ventures; paritta chants for protection.
  • Keepers of the Dhamma: teaching, sermons on observance days.
  • Two vocations: village and town monks (study, teaching, ritual) and forest monks (meditation), e.g. Ajahn Mun (1870–1949) and Ajahn Chah (1918–1992).
Mission 4

The exchange

  • Monks may not farm, cook stored food or (by the Vinaya) handle money. They depend on lay people.
  • Lay people give material support: food on the dawn alms round, robes at Kathina (after the rains retreat), medicine, lodging.
  • Monks give the Dhamma: teaching, example, chanting, counsel.
  • Lay people gain merit: the Sangha is called the best field of merit, where good deeds grow most.
  • On the alms round the monk does not ask and does not thank: the giver is grateful for the chance to give.
  • On observance days (uposatha), lay people visit the wat and take extra precepts.
  • So the refuges shape daily life: Sangha is not only an idea but a daily relationship.
Mission 4Diversity

Many kinds of sangha

  • Thailand: forest monks and town monks; and mae chi: women in white robes who keep eight or ten precepts but are not recognised as ordained nuns by the Thai Sangha.
  • Japan: since 1872, most temple priests in most schools marry and run family temples; Jodo Shinshu priests never were celibate.
  • Tibet: large monasteries; lamas as teachers; lay devotees support them.
  • Triratna (UK): an order neither monastic nor lay; "sangha" means the whole community.

Mission 5

Changing roles

  • Over the 20th century, the Thai state took over many monastic roles: schools (from the 1920s), hospitals, social welfare.
  • Cities grew; many people now meet monks mainly at ceremonies, funerals and on the alms round.
  • Fewer men make the monastic life lifelong; temporary ordination continues but often for shorter periods.
  • Money scandals and a trade in blessed amulets have damaged trust in some monks.
  • New roles have grown:
  • Development monks: village co-operatives, rice banks.
  • Ecology monks: "ordaining" trees with saffron cloth to protect forests.
  • Care: hospices, e.g. for people with HIV/AIDS (from the 1990s).
  • Media: Dhamma talks on radio, TV and social media.
Mission 5Evaluate

"Monastic life and the precepts are still relevant today."

  • For: monks keep the Dhamma alive and teach it, now online as well as in person.
  • For: a life of few possessions is a living challenge to consumerism.
  • For: new roles (hospices, ecology, media teaching) show the Sangha serving modern needs.
  • Against: the state now does what monks once did (schools, health care).
  • Against: scandals and commercialisation suggest the Vinaya is hard to keep in a money economy.
  • Against: lay meditation movements and Western groups such as Triratna show the path without traditional monasticism.
  • Judgement criteria: Relevant to whom: Thai villagers, city workers, Western converts? Is relevance measured by numbers, by service, or by faithfulness to the Dhamma?

Mission 6

Training rules, not commandments

  • Each precept is a sikkhapada: a training rule (literally a "step of training").
  • The formula is spoken in the first person: "I undertake the training rule to abstain from …" (sikkhapadam samadiyami).
  • No god gives them and no one enforces them on lay people: they are undertaken voluntarily, usually after going for refuge (see Mission 3).
  • Breaking one is not a sin against God. It is akusala action with karmic results; the response is to recognise it, resolve again and retake the precept.
  • They describe how an awakened person naturally behaves; for everyone else they are aspirations to train towards.
Mission 6

The five precepts

  • I undertake the training rule to abstain from:
  • 1 Killing or harming living beings (panatipata).
  • 2 Taking what is not given (adinnadana).
  • 3 Sexual misconduct (kamesu micchacara).
  • 4 False speech (musavada).
  • 5 Intoxicating drinks and drugs that cause heedlessness (sura-meraya-majja-pamadatthana).
  • Lay Buddhists in Theravada countries often chant them in Pali at the temple, after the three refuges.
  • The fifth protects the other four: an intoxicated mind breaks precepts more easily.
Mission 6

The positive side

  • Each restraint has a positive quality to grow:
  • 1 not harming → loving-kindness (metta) and compassion.
  • 2 not stealing → generosity (dana).
  • 3 no sexual misconduct → contentment and faithfulness.
  • 4 no false speech → truthfulness.
  • 5 no intoxicants → mindfulness and clarity.
  • This pairing is widely taught today (e.g. the Triratna "positive precepts"; Thich Nhat Hanh's Five Mindfulness Trainings, which reword the precepts as commitments).
  • So the precepts are about building character, not only avoiding acts (kusala roots).

Mission 6

When is a precept broken?

  • Theravada commentaries list factors that must all be present for a full breach. For the first precept (paraphrase, as set out by Bhikkhu Bodhi):
  • 1 a living being; 2 knowing it is a living being; 3 the intention to kill; 4 an effort to kill; 5 death results.
  • So stepping on an unseen ant does not break the first precept; intending to kill and failing is still akusala, though the precept is not fully broken.
  • Bhikkhu Bodhi (b. 1944), Going for Refuge and Taking the Precepts (1981): the precepts are abstentions (virati): the moment of choosing not to act is itself wholesome.
  • Seriousness also varies with the being harmed, the strength of the intention and the effort used.
Mission 6

Eight and ten precepts

  • Eight precepts: kept by many lay Buddhists on uposatha (observance) days and on retreat. The third becomes celibacy, and three more are added: no food after midday; no entertainment or adornment; no high or luxurious beds.
  • Ten precepts (dasa sila): kept by novices (samaneras and samaneris), and by some lay women renunciants (e.g. dasa sil matas in Sri Lanka).
  • Ten = the eight, with entertainment and adornment as separate rules (7 and 8), plus 10: not accepting gold and silver (money).
  • Fully ordained monks (bhikkhus) keep the Patimokkha: 227 rules in the Theravada Vinaya.
Mission 6Diversity

Theravada and Mahayana

  • Theravada: the five precepts are chanted at almost every lay ceremony; monastics keep the Vinaya closely; the precepts are mainly restraints.
  • Mahayana: many keep the bodhisattva precepts, e.g. the Brahmajala Sutra (Chinese, c. 5th century CE): 10 major and 48 minor precepts. They add positive duties (to help, protect and teach others) and, in East Asia, a ban on eating meat.
  • Intention and compassion may weigh more than the letter: some Mahayana texts allow a bodhisattva to break a precept through skilful means to prevent greater harm.
  • Japan: Jodo Shinshu priests have married since its founder Shinran (1173–1263); from 1872 the government permitted marriage for all clergy. Many Japanese priests marry today.
  • Peter Harvey, An Introduction to Buddhist Ethics (2000): the precepts are shared by all schools, but Mahayana places them within the bodhisattva's aim of benefiting all beings.
  • Bodhi: in Theravada the precepts are the indispensable base of the path, never to be outgrown.
  • Your named scholars: Harvey and Bodhi.
Mission 6Evaluate

Are the precepts still relevant today?

  • Claim: The traditional precepts are still a sufficient guide for Buddhists today.
  • For: They are short, shared by all schools and easy to apply; they cover the main harms in any society.
  • For: As training rules they are flexible: intention and circumstance are taken into account.
  • Against: Modern questions (online speech, factory farming, recreational drugs, digital piracy) need interpretation the wording does not give; hence reworded trainings (Thich Nhat Hanh).
  • Against: Some monastic rules (e.g. not handling money) are hard to keep in modern economies; some monasteries use lay stewards, others are criticised for laxity.
  • Judgement criteria: Is the spirit or the letter more important? Who interprets them: the individual, the sangha, or tradition? Do the dasa sila still regulate the monastic sangha effectively?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Anatta: no permanent, independent self in the five khandhas (form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness) or beyond them.
  • The Buddha rejects eternalism and annihilationism; Nagasena's chariot shows "person" is a valid name for parts in relation, with no owner.
  • Buddhists differ on how to read anatta (Theravada no-self, not-self as strategy, Personalists, Buddha-nature, Dhammakaya).

Mission 2

  • In Buddhist teaching, karma is intentional action of body, speech and mind; it plants seeds whose fruits ripen later. Accidents make little or none; an arhat makes none.
  • Rebirth is "neither the same nor another" (Milinda): a stream of causes, not a soul.

Mission 3

  • Going for refuge to the Buddha, Dhamma and Sangha, chanted three times in Pali, marks a person as a Buddhist.
  • Buddha: historical teacher and ideal of awakening; Dhamma: teaching and ultimate truth; Sangha: noble, monastic, fourfold, all Buddhists, spiritual friends.
  • Heritage and convert Buddhists share the formula but often live it differently; Buddhists see other "refuges" today as impermanent.

Mission 4

  • "Sangha" means the monastic community, the noble ones (stream-entry and beyond), or the whole community of Buddhists.
  • Lay people give material support and make merit; monks give the Dhamma.

Mission 5

  • The Thai state has taken over schooling and health care; monks have found new roles (development, ecology, hospices, media); scandals have damaged trust in some monks.
  • Whether monastic life and the precepts are still relevant is debated: service and simplicity versus changed roles and scandal.

Mission 6

  • The precepts are training rules (sikkhapada), undertaken voluntarily, not commandments; breaking one is akusala and is met by renewed resolve.
  • Five for lay people, eight on observance days, ten (dasa sila) for novices; each restraint has a positive quality to grow.
  • Mahayana keeps the precepts within the bodhisattva aim (bodhisattva precepts, six perfections); Theravada stresses them as the unshakeable base.
