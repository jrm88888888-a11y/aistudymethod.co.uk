Mission 1

Mission 1: When someone breaks the law, what should punishment actually achieve?

Make a guess: When someone breaks the law, what should punishment actually achieve?

Mission 1

What punishment is for

  • Punishment: a penalty given by a lawful authority (a court, a judge) to someone who has broken the law.
  • It is not revenge: it follows fair rules, and fits the crime.
  • Why society needs it: to keep order, to protect people, and to show that laws matter.
  • Why it can be justice: the offender is treated fairly, and the victim's harm is recognised.
  • Religions agree that wrongdoing has consequences. They differ on what punishment should mainly aim to do.
Mission 1

Retribution, deterrence, reformation

  • Retribution: the offender gets what they deserve; the punishment fits the crime. Looks back at the crime.
  • Deterrence: punishment puts the offender, and others, off committing crimes. Looks forward.
  • Reformation: helping the offender change, so they become a law-abiding member of society (education, courses, counselling). Looks forward.
Mission 1

Protection, reparation, vindication

  • Protection: keeping the public safe from a dangerous offender (e.g. by prison).
  • Vindication: the law must be respected; punishing crime shows that the law is upheld and has authority.
Quick check
?A judge says: "This sentence must send a clear message to anyone else thinking of carrying a knife." Which aim is the judge stressing?
Mission 1

Strengths and weaknesses

  • Retribution: strength: fair to victims; the punishment fits the crime. Weakness: can look like revenge; does not stop future crime.
  • Deterrence: strength: may prevent crime before it happens. Weakness: many crimes are done in anger, under the influence, or by people who expect not to be caught; punishing one person to warn others can seem unfair.
  • Reformation: strength: the offender may change, so less reoffending; respects human worth. Weakness: not every offender wants to change; victims may feel it is too soft.
  • Protection: strength: keeps the public safe. Weakness: only lasts while the offender is held.
Mission 1Read the source

Christian teachings on punishment

"eye for eye, tooth for tooth, hand for hand, foot for foot" (Exodus 21:24)

"he is a servant of God, an avenger for wrath to him who does evil." (Romans 13:4)

"Brothers, even if a man is caught in some fault, you who are spiritual must restore such a one in a spirit of gentleness" (Galatians 6:1)

  • Exodus 21:24 limits punishment to what fits the crime: retribution in proportion, not unlimited revenge.
  • Romans 13:4: the state has God-given authority to punish (retribution, deterrence).

Show answer

Q2 Which source gives the state the right to punish?

Mission 1Diversity

Christian views on the aims

  • All Christians: the state may punish (Romans 13); punishment must be just and respect the offender's dignity.
  • Catholic teaching (Catechism 2266, paraphrase): punishment first redresses the disorder the crime caused (retribution), and should also protect public safety and help correct the offender.
  • Many Christians stress reformation: Jesus came to call sinners to repentance; no one is beyond change.
  • Some, often more conservative, Christians give more weight to retribution and deterrence (Exodus 21:24; Romans 13:4).
  • Quakers and many others: punishment should heal; they support reform and restorative justice (see Mission 2).
Quick check
?A prison chaplain leads a course that helps prisoners face what they did and plan a new life. Which Christian teaching supports her aim most directly?
Mission 1Evaluate

"Reformation is the most important aim of punishment."

  • Reasons for: offenders can change; Galatians 6:1: restore the wrongdoer. It protects society in the long run. It respects human worth.
  • Reasons against: victims deserve justice (retribution; Exodus 21:24). Some offenders will not change, so the public needs protection. Without deterrence, crime may rise. Romans 13:4 gives the state a duty to punish.
  • Another view: the aims work together: a just sentence can punish, protect and reform at once (Catholic teaching).
  • Judge it by: What does the victim need? Can the offender change? What best reduces future crime?
Mission check

?A school catches a pupil vandalising the science lab. The head makes him repaint it on Saturdays, meet the technician he upset, and join a mentoring scheme. Which aims of punishment does this mainly show?

The same punishment can serve several aims; what matters is which aim it mainly serves, and why.

Mission 2

Mission 2: If you forgive someone who hurt you, should they still be punished?

Make a guess: If you forgive someone who hurt you, should they still be punished?

Mission 2

Forgiveness, repentance, reconciliation

  • Forgiveness: choosing to let go of anger and the wish for revenge against someone who has wronged you.
  • It is not pretending the wrong did not happen, and not saying it was acceptable.
  • Repentance: being truly sorry for wrongdoing and turning away from it (metanoia in Christianity; teshuvah in Judaism; tawbah in Islam).
  • Reconciliation: restoring a broken relationship, so both sides can live in peace again.
  • Restorative justice brings offender and victim together to repair harm.
Mission 2Read the source

Jesus on forgiveness

"Lord, how often shall my brother sin against me, and I forgive him? Until seven times?" Jesus said to him, "I don't tell you until seven times, but, until seventy times seven." (Matthew 18:21–22)

"For if you forgive men their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you. But if you don't forgive men their trespasses, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses." (Matthew 6:14–15)

  • On the cross: "Father, forgive them, for they don't know what they are doing." (Luke 23:34)

Q1 What does "seventy times seven" mean?

Show answer

Forgive without limit; stop counting.

Q2 Why does Matthew 6:14–15 link our forgiving to God's?

Show answer

Christians who refuse to forgive close themselves to God's forgiveness.

Mission 2

Forgiveness and punishment

  • The tension: if Christians must forgive without limit, should offenders be punished at all?
  • Most Christians answer: forgiveness is personal; justice is the state's duty (Romans 13:4). A victim can forgive and still want a fair sentence.
  • Punishment can itself serve the offender's good: it helps them face the wrong and change.
  • Example: Pope John Paul II was shot in 1981 by Mehmet Ali Ağca. In 1983 he visited Ağca in prison and forgave him, yet Ağca stayed in prison.
  • Some argue the other way: punishing someone you have forgiven still looks like holding on to the wrong.
Quick check
?Which statement best describes what most Christians mean by forgiveness?
Mission 2Diversity

Different Christian and non-religious views

  • Unconditional forgiveness: many Christians forgive whether or not the offender repents, following Jesus on the cross (Luke 23:34).
  • Conditional forgiveness: others point to "If he repents, forgive him" (Luke 17:3); full reconciliation needs repentance.
  • Catholic teaching: God's forgiveness is offered to all and received through repentance, especially in the Sacrament of Reconciliation; Catholics are called to forgive as they are forgiven.
  • Humanists: forgiveness can heal both victim and offender and help society; but it is a choice, not a duty, and victims should never be pressured to forgive.
  • Some non-religious people argue some acts should not be forgiven, out of respect for victims.
Mission 2

Restorative justice

  • Restorative justice: the offender and the victim (or the community) meet, with a trained facilitator, if both agree.
  • The victim can explain the harm; the offender takes responsibility, apologises and agrees how to repair it.
  • Aims: healing for victims; reformation of offenders; less reoffending; reconciliation.
  • Christian support: reconciliation is at the heart of the Gospel (Matthew 5:24; 2 Corinthians 5:18). Prison Fellowship's Sycamore Tree course teaches it in prisons.
  • Criticisms: not suitable for every crime; some victims find it painful; some say it is too soft without other punishment.
Mission 2

Examples of forgiveness

  • Gordon Wilson (Methodist): his daughter Marie was killed in the Enniskillen bombing in 1987. He said he bore the bombers no ill will and prayed for them; he later worked for peace.
  • Gee Walker (Christian): her son Anthony was murdered in a racist attack in Liverpool in 2005. She said she forgave his killers, because of her faith.
  • Azim Khamisa (Muslim): his son Tariq was shot by a 14-year-old in 1995. He forgave the boy and set up a foundation with the boy's grandfather to stop youth violence.
  • Each acted from personal belief; none said the crime did not matter.
Quick check
?In a restorative justice meeting, a burglar meets the elderly woman whose house he broke into. What is the meeting mainly for?
Mission 2Evaluate

"Some actions are unforgivable."

  • Reasons for: some crimes (murder, genocide) cause harm that can never be repaired. Forgiving may seem to disrespect victims. Some offenders show no repentance. Humanists: forgiveness is a choice, not a duty.
  • Reasons against: Jesus taught "seventy times seven" and forgave on the cross. Examples like Gordon Wilson and Gee Walker show it is possible.
  • Another view: forgiving the person is possible, while the act stays wrong and justice is still done.
  • Judge it by: Who has the right to forgive? Must the offender repent? Does forgiveness cancel punishment?
Mission check

?A football fan was badly injured when a rival fan attacked him. In court, the injured man says he forgives his attacker, but he also asks the judge to give a fair sentence. Most Christians would say this is:

Forgiveness lets go of revenge; justice deals with the wrong. Most believers hold that you can offer one without cancelling the other.

Mission 3

Mission 3: If a couple cannot have a baby, how far should science help, and who should decide?

Make a guess: If a couple cannot have a baby, how far should science help, and who should decide?

Mission 3

Infertility and childlessness

  • Infertility: being unable to conceive after a year or more of regular sex without contraception.
  • Causes in women: eggs not released (ovulation problems), blocked fallopian tubes, endometriosis.
  • Causes in men: few sperm, or sperm that do not move well.
  • Either partner: age, illness, smoking, weight. Sometimes no cause is found.
  • For many couples who long for a child, infertility brings deep grief.
Mission 3

Ways to overcome infertility

  • Drugs to help ovulation; surgery to clear blocked tubes.
  • Artificial insemination: sperm placed in the womb; from the husband or partner (AIH) or a donor (AID).
  • IVF (in vitro fertilisation, "in glass"): eggs collected, fertilised with sperm in a lab; an embryo is placed in the womb. First IVF baby: Louise Brown, 1978, Oldham.
  • Egg, sperm or embryo donation: a third person's genetic material is used.
  • Surrogacy: another woman carries the baby.
  • Adoption (a child becomes legally and permanently part of a new family) and fostering (care for a child for a time).
Mission 3

IVF: the issues

  • Spare embryos: more are made than used. They may be frozen, donated, given to research, or allowed to perish.
  • Multiple births: placing more than one embryo raises the risk of twins or triplets, which is riskier for mother and babies.
  • Success falls with age; many cycles fail; the process is costly and stressful. NHS funding varies by area.
  • Donors: in the UK, children conceived from donations since 2005 can learn the donor's identity at 18.
  • Meaning: conception happens in a lab, apart from the couple's act of love; a third party may be involved.
  • Embryo status and research: a later mission.
Mission 3

Surrogacy

  • Partial (traditional): the surrogate's own egg is used; she is the genetic mother.
  • Full (gestational): an IVF embryo from the intended parents or donors; the surrogate carries it.
  • UK law: surrogacy is legal, but commercial surrogacy is not (Surrogacy Arrangements Act 1985). Only reasonable expenses may be paid.
  • The surrogate is the legal mother at birth; the intended parents apply for a parental order. Agreements cannot be enforced in court.
  • Issues: the surrogate may not want to give up the baby; risk of exploiting poorer women, especially abroad; the child's questions about identity; "renting a womb" treats pregnancy as a service.
  • Supporters: an act of great generosity that gives a family a child it could not otherwise have.
Quick check
?A woman carries a baby for her sister. The embryo was made by IVF from her sister's egg and her brother-in-law's sperm. What is this called?
Mission 3

The HFEA

  • Human Fertilisation and Embryology Authority: the UK's independent regulator, set up by the Human Fertilisation and Embryology Act 1990.
  • Licenses and inspects every fertility clinic and every centre doing human embryo research.
  • Keeps a register of donors, treatments and children born, and publishes clinic success rates.
  • Sets a Code of Practice: e.g. clinics must consider the welfare of the child; limits on how many families one donor may help.
  • Enforces legal limits: e.g. no embryo kept beyond 14 days in the lab; no sex selection for social reasons.
  • Supporters: careful, democratic control of fast-moving science. Critics: some say it allows too much (destroying embryos); others, that it is too slow or restrictive.
Mission 3Read the source

Hannah's prayer

Hannah, childless, prays at the house of the LORD at Shiloh (1 Samuel 1:9–18):

"She was in bitterness of soul, and prayed to the LORD, weeping bitterly." (1 Samuel 1:10)

  • She vows that if God gives her a son, she will give him to the LORD all his life (1:11). The priest Eli at first thinks she is drunk, then blesses her (1:12–17); her "facial expression wasn't sad any more" (1:18). Her son is Samuel.
  • "Children are a heritage of the LORD." (Psalm 127:3)

Q1 What does the passage show about infertility?

Show answer

It can cause real anguish, which God takes seriously.

Q2 How might Christians use it differently?

Show answer

Some: children are God's gift, so pray and accept God's will. Others: longing for a child is good, so medicine can be one way God answers.

Mission 3

Catholic teaching

  • Every human life is sacred from conception; a child should come from the loving sexual union of a married couple.
  • So IVF is rejected, even with the couple's own egg and sperm: it separates conception from the act of love, and usually destroys spare embryos.
  • Donors and surrogates are rejected more strongly: a third person enters the marriage.
  • "A child is not something owed to one, but is a gift" (Catechism of the Catholic Church 2378).
  • Allowed: drugs and surgery that help a couple conceive naturally.
  • The Church urges compassion for childless couples and encourages adoption and fostering.
  • Exodus 20:13: "You shall not murder": Catholics apply it to spare embryos they see as human lives.
Mission 3Diversity

A range of responses

  • Most Protestant and Anglican churches: IVF with the couple's own egg and sperm is a good use of God-given skill; many have concerns about spare embryos and donors.
  • Some evangelical Christians accept IVF only if no embryos are discarded.
  • Humanists: support regulated treatment, guided by reason, compassion and the welfare of the child.
Mission 3

Is there a right to a child?

  • UK law: single people and same-sex couples can have fertility treatment and adopt; female same-sex couples can both be legal parents (HFE Act 2008).
  • Catholic teaching: a child has a right to be born to, and raised by, a married mother and father; so the Church opposes IVF for single people and same-sex couples.
  • Other Christians: views differ: some agree with Catholic teaching; many liberal Protestants and Quakers say loving, stable care matters more than the parents' sex.
  • Humanists: equal access; what matters is the child's wellbeing.
Quick check
?A UK clinic wants to keep a human embryo in the lab for research for three weeks. Which body decides, and what would it say?
Mission 3Evaluate

"Everyone has the right to have a child."

  • Reasons for: having a family is a deep human good; IVF and surrogacy relieve real suffering; Humanists: equal treatment for all; God gave humans skill to heal.
  • Reasons against: Catholic: a child is a gift, not a right; the child's own rights come first; IVF often destroys embryos; surrogacy can exploit women.
  • Another view: a right to try for a child with support, but not a right to any child by any method.
  • Judge it by: Whose rights come first: adults' or the child's? Is a "right" something others must provide? Does the method matter, or only the love?
Mission check

?A married Catholic couple are told that IVF using only their own egg and sperm is their best chance of a baby. What does Catholic teaching say?

Most traditions welcome some fertility help; they disagree about embryos, donors and surrogates.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Is a five-day-old embryo a person, a future person, or just a cluster of cells?

Make a guess: Is a five-day-old embryo a person, a future person, or just a cluster of cells?

Mission 4

From fertilisation to fourteen days

  • Fertilisation: egg and sperm join to form a single cell, the zygote, with a full set of human DNA.
  • Day 5–6: a hollow ball of about 100–200 cells, the blastocyst. This is when IVF embryos are usually transferred or frozen.
  • Days 6–12: implantation in the lining of the womb.
  • About day 14: the primitive streak appears, the first sign of the body's head-to-tail axis; the nervous system starts to form soon after. After this the embryo can no longer split into twins.
  • From about 8 weeks it is called a foetus.
  • Embryo: the early stage of development, from fertilisation to about 8 weeks.
Mission 4

What is the status of an embryo?

  • A person from conception: a new human life with its own DNA; it must be protected like any person (Catholic teaching; many evangelicals; many Buddhists, who hold that consciousness begins at conception).
  • A potential person: it deserves special respect, but not the full rights of a born child (e.g. the Warnock Report, 1984; many Anglicans).
  • Gradual: its value grows as it develops, e.g. after the primitive streak, or when the heart or brain forms.
  • A cluster of cells: with no brain and no feelings, it is not yet "someone" (some Humanists and philosophers).
Mission 4Read the source

Known before birth

"For you formed my inmost being. You knit me together in my mother's womb." (Psalm 139:13)

"Before I formed you in the womb, I knew you. Before you were born, I sanctified you." (Jeremiah 1:5)

  • Psalm 139:13–16a goes on: God saw "my body" before it was formed; every day was written in God's book.
  • "You shall not murder." (Exodus 20:13)

Mission 4

Destroyed embryos and embryo research

  • Spare IVF embryos are frozen, donated to other couples, given to research, or allowed to perish.
  • Embryo research: studying embryos to understand infertility, miscarriage and disease.
  • Embryonic stem cells can become any type of cell. Scientists hope to treat conditions such as Parkinson's, diabetes and spinal injury. Taking them destroys the embryo.
  • UK law: research allowed only under an HFEA licence, and never beyond 14 days; embryos used in research are never implanted.
  • Alternatives: adult stem cells and "reprogrammed" (iPS) cells, which do not need embryos.
  • The clash: possible cures for many versus the destruction of what some see as human lives.
Quick check
?Why does the primitive streak, at about 14 days, matter in the debate about embryos?
Mission 4

Genetic manipulation and "creating life"

  • Gene therapy: repairing faulty genes. Somatic (body cells): affects only the patient. Germline (eggs, sperm, embryos): passed to future generations; illegal for reproduction in the UK.
  • Embryo screening (PGD): testing IVF embryos for serious inherited conditions and choosing a healthy one; "saviour siblings" chosen to match a sick brother or sister.
  • Designer babies: choosing traits like looks or intelligence: not allowed, but feared.
  • Mitochondrial donation: replaces faulty mitochondria with a donor's; legal in the UK since 2015.
  • Cloning: Dolly the sheep (1996). Reproductive human cloning is banned in the UK.
  • Creating life: making new life in the lab (IVF, cloning, synthetic cells). Critics call it "playing God"; supporters call it using God-given skill.
Mission 4

Christian responses

  • Catholic: the embryo is to be treated as a person from conception, so no research that destroys embryos, no embryo selection, no cloning. Somatic gene therapy and adult stem-cell research are welcomed.
  • Catholic teaching: Dignitas Personae (2008).
  • Many Anglicans and other Protestants: research up to 14 days can be right if it may relieve great suffering, with the embryo treated with special respect.
  • "Playing God": only God gives life; changing the human germline crosses a line.
  • "Co-creators": humans are made in God's image and given skill and stewardship (Genesis 1:28); healing is Christ-like.
Mission 4

The HFEA and embryo research

  • The HFEA (set up by the HFE Act 1990) licenses every research project on human embryos.
  • It enforces the 14-day limit and bans placing research embryos in a womb.
  • It decides which conditions clinics may screen embryos for.
  • It approved mitochondrial donation case by case (law of 2015).
  • Supporters: careful, public control. Critics: Catholic and pro-life groups say no licence can make destroying embryos right; some scientists want the 14-day limit extended.
Quick check
?A Catholic and an Anglican both read Psalm 139:13 ("You knit me together in my mother's womb"). Which statement fits their likely positions on embryo research?
Mission 4Evaluate

"Scientists who change human embryos are playing God."

  • Reasons for: only God gives life; germline changes affect people not yet born; risk of designer babies; destroyed embryos may be human lives.
  • Sources for: Psalm 139:13; Exodus 20:13.
  • Reasons against: curing disease is a duty; humans are stewards and co-creators; Humanists: the phrase assumes a God; outcomes and consent matter.
  • Another view: some changes (preventing a deadly disease) may be right while others (choosing looks) are not.
  • Judge it by: Where does healing end and "enhancement" begin? Whose consent counts? Does the embryo's status decide everything?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Aims: retribution (deserved), deterrence (put others off), reformation (change the offender).
  • Also protection (keep the public safe).
  • Also vindication (uphold the law).
  • Christian sources: Exodus 21:24 (proportion), Romans 13:4 (authority), Galatians 6:1 (restore).

Mission 2

  • Forgiveness: letting go of anger and revenge; repentance: turning from wrong; reconciliation: restoring the relationship.
  • Jesus: forgive "seventy times seven" (Matthew 18:22).
  • Forgiving the person can go with justice for the act.
  • Restorative justice tries to do both: repair harm and reform the offender.

Mission 3

  • Infertility can be treated by drugs, surgery, insemination, IVF, donation and surrogacy; adoption and fostering are alternatives.
  • The HFEA licenses clinics and research and enforces limits such as the 14-day rule.
  • Catholic teaching rejects IVF and surrogacy; most other Christians and many other faiths accept IVF within marriage; Humanists support regulated access.

Mission 4

  • Views on the embryo: a person from conception, a potential person, gradual value, or a cluster of cells.
  • UK law (HFEA): embryo research only under licence and never beyond 14 days.
  • Genetic manipulation (gene therapy, screening, mitochondrial donation, cloning) divides religious believers between "playing God" and using God-given skill to heal.
