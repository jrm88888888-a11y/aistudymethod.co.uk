Paper 1: Explorations in Creative Reading & Writing

This mini-lesson trains the skills AQA 8700 Paper 1 tests: reading an unseen piece of literary fiction, analysing language and structure, judging how a writer succeeds (the evaluation question), and producing your own descriptive or narrative writing.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go (spot the device, pick the best comment, match method to effect) and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you're ready.

Know the paper

What Paper 1 asks of you

Section A gives you one unseen literary fiction extract (20th or 21st century). You answer four reading questions worth 40 marks:

  • Q1 — List four things (4 marks) — quick retrieval from a set part of the text (AO1).
  • Q2 — Language (8 marks) — how the writer uses words, phrases, language features and sentence forms (AO2).
  • Q3 — Structure (8 marks) — how the writer has structured the whole text to interest the reader (AO2).
  • Q4 — Evaluation (20 marks) — how far you agree with a statement, evaluating the writer's methods (AO4).

Section B — Writing (40 marks): a choice of a descriptive or narrative task, often prompted by a picture or a title. Assessed on content & organisation (AO5, 24 marks) and technical accuracy (AO6, 16 marks).

The extract is unseen — so you can't revise the text itself. What you can revise is the skill of analysing language, structure and effect, and your own writing craft.

Reading skill

Read the extract like a detective

Before you write a word, annotate. Marks are lost by readers who skim, so build every answer on evidence:

  • Underline striking verbs, adjectives and images.
  • Bracket structural moves — where the focus shifts, a change of pace or a short punchy sentence.
  • Note the tone in the margin (tense? peaceful? menacing?).
  • Track the reader's journey — what are you made to notice first, next, last?

Read for effect, not plot. In Q2–Q4 the examiner rewards how the writing works on the reader — not a retelling of what happens.

Toolkit · AO2

Language techniques & their effects

Naming a device earns almost nothing. The marks are in the effect — what the word makes the reader feel or picture.

  • Metaphor / simile — paint a vivid picture; make the abstract concrete.
  • Personification — gives objects or nature feelings, creating mood.
  • Powerful verbs — a precise verb ("crept", "shattered") controls the whole image.
  • Sensory imagery — sight, sound, touch pull the reader into the scene.
  • Sentence forms — a short sentence after long ones lands like a shock.
  • Semantic field — a cluster of related words (e.g. of decay, of light) builds atmosphere.

The golden rule: device → quotation → effect on the reader. Miss the effect and you're only "feature-spotting".

Analysis skill · AO2

Comment on the effect, not the label

Compare two comments on the same line:

"The shadows crawled across the nursery floor." ▸ verb "crawled" — personification / powerful verb
Weak (feature-spotting)

"The writer uses personification here. This is effective and makes it interesting."

Strong (device → effect)

"The verb 'crawled' makes the shadows seem alive and predatory, creeping like an insect, so the reader feels the nursery is being slowly invaded by something threatening."

Misconception: the marks are not for spotting personification — they're for explaining why 'crawled' works and what it does to the reader.

Q3 · Structure · AO2

Structure — the shape of the whole text

Q3 is the structure question. Structure is how the writer orders and shapes the extract to guide the reader through it:

  • The opening — where does the writer focus your attention first?
  • Shifts — zooming from a wide view to a tiny detail; outside to inside; then to now.
  • Perspective — following a character's eyes, or pulling back to a narrator.
  • The ending — a cliffhanger, a return to the opening image, a final revelation.
Q4 · Evaluation · AO4

How to structure the Q4 evaluation

Q4 (20 marks) gives you a statement about part of the text and asks how far you agree, evaluating the writer's methods. Judge the effect and back it with evidence — a What → How → Why chain with a verdict:

Evaluation = analysis + verdict. Keep coming back to the statement: "The writer does succeed here, because…", "This is convincing, though…".

Section B · Writing

Descriptive vs narrative writing

Section B offers a choice — usually a descriptive task (often from a picture) or a narrative task (a title or opening line). Pick the one you can do best and plan it:

  • Descriptive — creates a vivid picture of a place, moment or person. Little plot; rich imagery and the senses.
  • Narrative — tells a story with a clear shape. Keep it small: one moment done well beats a rushed epic.

AO5 rewards craft, not length. A tightly-controlled, vividly-described single scene scores higher than a sprawling plot with weak description.

Writing craft · AO5

Show, don't tell

  • Use the senses — sight, sound, smell, touch, taste build a real place.
  • Choose precise verbs and nouns — "sprinted", not "went fast"; "cathedral", not "big building".
  • Zoom like a camera — wide shot, then a close-up on one telling detail.
  • Vary sentence length for pace and control.
  • Use a motif — a repeated image (light, cold, a colour) ties the piece together.

Plan first. 4–5 minutes planning a shape (an arc of images, or a story's turning point) saves the whole answer and boosts AO5 organisation.

Worked model

Descriptive writing in action

A short model opening describing an abandoned house, with the techniques labelled:

"The gate screamed on its hinges. Beyond it, the house crouched in the long grass, windows like blind eyes. Somewhere inside, a door breathed open. Then silence." ▸ powerful verb ("screamed") · simile ("windows like blind eyes") · personification ("breathed") · short sentence for impact ("Then silence.")

Notice each device does a job: unsettling the reader, then leaving them tense in the silence. That is craft, not decoration.

Writing craft · AO5/AO6

Control your sentences

Sentence variety is one of the clearest markers of a confident writer:

  • Long, flowing sentences — build atmosphere and detail.
  • Short, blunt sentences — create shock, tension or a full stop of drama.
  • Openers — start some sentences with a verb (-ing) or adverb for variety.
  • Punctuation for effect — a dash, colon or ellipsis used deliberately.

Read it aloud in your head. If every sentence sounds the same length, vary it — rhythm is part of AO5.

Technical accuracy · AO6

AO6 — spelling, punctuation, grammar & vocabulary

16 of the 40 Section B marks are for technical accuracy and ambitious vocabulary — nearly a quarter of your writing marks. Easy to gain, easy to throw away.

  • Punctuate accurately — full stops, commas, and the occasional colon, semicolon or dash used correctly.
  • Reach for precise vocabulary — "desolate" not "empty"; "menacing" not "scary".
  • Vary sentence structures securely, using a range of punctuation.
  • Proofread — leave 3–4 minutes to catch slips.

Ambition with control beats safety with errors. Only use words you can spell and punctuate correctly.

Exam-day plan

Working the paper for marks

  • Watch the mark tariff — Q1 (4 marks) takes a moment; Q4 (20 marks) and Section B (40 marks) need the most time.
  • Analyse the whole extract for Q2–Q4 — don't stall on the first line.
  • Quote briefly and precisely — short, well-chosen quotations beat long copied chunks.
  • In Section B, plan first — 5 minutes on a shape saves the whole answer.
  • Save time to proofread for AO6.

Split your time by marks: Section A and Section B are worth 40 marks each — give each roughly half the paper (about 45 min reading, 45 min writing, plus reading time).

Recap

The skills to take into the exam

Q1 retrieval: list exactly what's asked, from the right lines.

Q2 language / Q3 structure (AO2): device → quotation → effect on the reader (never just name it).

Q4 evaluation (AO4): analysis + a clear judgement of how far you agree.

Section B (AO5): show don't tell; senses, precise verbs, varied sentences, a clear shape.

AO6: punctuate accurately, reach for ambitious vocabulary, proofread.
