Human Systems and Resource Use

This mini-lesson examines humanity as an environmental force: population dynamics (birth and death rates, natural increase, doubling time and the demographic transition), the drivers that raise or lower population growth, our resource use and ecological footprint, solid domestic waste and the waste hierarchy, the shift from a linear to a circular economy, and how human development links to environmental impact.

Sustainability lens: total impact depends on population size, consumption per person and the technology used. Reducing waste and closing material loops is central to living within planetary limits.

Human systems · population

Population dynamics

Key demographic rates describe how populations change:

  • Crude birth rate (CBR) — births per 1000 people per year.
  • Crude death rate (CDR) — deaths per 1000 people per year.
  • Natural increase rate (NIR) — (CBR − CDR), usually expressed as a percentage per year.
  • Total fertility rate (TFR) — average children per woman; ~2.1 gives a stable population (replacement level).

Human systems · transition

The demographic transition model

The demographic transition model (DTM) describes how birth and death rates change as a country develops:

  • Stage 1 — high, fluctuating CBR and CDR; slow growth (pre-industrial).
  • Stage 2 — CDR falls (better food, medicine); CBR stays high; rapid growth.
  • Stage 3 — CBR falls (education, contraception); growth slows.
  • Stage 4 — low CBR and CDR; stable, large population.
  • Stage 5 — CBR below CDR; population ages and may shrink.

Age–sex pyramids show this: a wide base means rapid growth (Stage 2); a column or top-heavy shape means an ageing, stable or shrinking population (Stage 4–5).

Human systems · drivers

What changes population growth

Birth rates fall — and growth slows — as countries develop, driven by:

  • Education, especially of girls and women — the single strongest factor lowering fertility.
  • Access to healthcare and contraception, and family planning.
  • Falling child mortality, so families choose fewer children.
  • Urbanisation and rising incomes, where children cost more and work less.

Cultural, religious and political factors (and some pro- or anti-natalist policies) also shape fertility, so trajectories differ between countries.

Resource use · footprints

Resource use and the ecological footprint

The ecological footprint measures the land and water area needed to supply a population’s resources and absorb its wastes, in global hectares (gha). It rises with consumption and with carbon-heavy lifestyles.

If a population’s footprint exceeds the biocapacity available, it runs an ecological deficit — living beyond the planet’s means. Wealthy nations typically have footprints several times larger than low-income nations.

Resource use · waste

Solid domestic waste and the waste hierarchy

Solid domestic waste (SDW) — household rubbish (food, paper, plastics, glass, metals) — is rising as consumption grows. Disposal options differ in impact:

  • Landfill — cheap but takes land, leaks leachate and emits methane.
  • Incineration — cuts volume and can recover energy, but emits pollutants.
  • Recycling and composting — recover materials and nutrients, saving resources.

Waste hierarchy (best first): Reduce → Reuse → Recycle → Recover → Dispose. Preventing waste beats dealing with it.

Resource use · circular economy

From a linear to a circular economy

The traditional linear economy is "take → make → use → dispose" — it depletes resources and creates waste.

A circular economy designs waste out: products are made to be repaired, reused, remanufactured and recycled, so materials cycle back into production (a "cradle-to-cradle" approach). This cuts resource extraction, waste and emissions while keeping economic value.

Human development · HDI

Development and environmental impact

The Human Development Index (HDI) combines income (GNI per capita), education and life expectancy into a single 0–1 measure of development.

Development brings clear benefits, but higher incomes usually mean higher consumption and a larger ecological footprint. A central sustainability challenge is decoupling improvements in human wellbeing from rising environmental impact.

The big ideas to know
