Germany and West Germany 1918-89

This is a Paper 1 option. Edexcel Option 1G, Germany and West Germany, 1918-89 is a breadth study with interpretations. It follows political authority, opposition, the economy and society across three very different regimes, and in Section C it asks you to weigh historians' interpretations (AO3).

In seventy years Germany moved from a fragile democracy (Weimar), to dictatorship and total war (the Third Reich), to division and a stable, prosperous democracy in the west (the Federal Republic). The option asks why authority collapsed once, hardened into tyranny, and then took root.

1918-1933 - the Weimar Republic

A democracy born in defeat

The Republic was proclaimed on 9 November 1918 as the Kaiser abdicated, and it never escaped the circumstances of its birth:

  • The Constitution (1919): universal suffrage and proportional representation, but PR fragmented the Reichstag into unstable coalitions, and Article 48 let the President rule by emergency decree.
  • The Treaty of Versailles (28 June 1919): territorial loss, disarmament, reparations and the 'war guilt' clause fed the stab-in-the-back myth and delegitimised the 'November criminals' who signed it.
  • Crisis and recovery: the hyperinflation of 1923 wiped out savings; then under Gustav Stresemann the Rentenmark, the Dawes Plan (1924) and Locarno (1925) brought a fragile 'golden' stabilisation — but one built on short-term American loans.
  • Collapse: the Wall Street Crash (1929) pulled those loans, mass unemployment returned, and the extremes surged. Rule by presidential decree replaced parliamentary government even before Hitler.

1933-1945 - the Third Reich

From Chancellor to dictator

Hitler was appointed Chancellor on 30 January 1933 by President Hindenburg, at the head of a coalition in which conservatives believed they could 'tame' him. Within months the Nazis dismantled the Republic:

  • The Reichstag Fire Decree (February 1933) suspended civil liberties; the Enabling Act (23 March 1933) let the government legislate without the Reichstag — the legal foundation of the dictatorship.
  • Gleichschaltung co-ordinated society under Nazi control; the Night of the Long Knives (1934) destroyed rivals; on Hindenburg's death Hitler merged the offices of President and Chancellor as Fuhrer.
  • The racial state deepened — the Nuremberg Laws (1935), Kristallnacht (1938) — and after the invasion of Poland in 1939 war unleashed genocide and, in 1945, total defeat and occupation.

Continuity to weigh: how far was the Third Reich a monolithic machine obeying one will, and how far a chaotic tangle of competing agencies? That question shapes Section C.

1945-1949 - division

Occupation and the birth of two Germanies

After unconditional surrender in 1945, Germany was divided into four occupation zones (US, British, French, Soviet), and Berlin likewise. Cold War tension hardened the division:

  • The western zones merged economically and introduced the Deutsche Mark (1948), prompting the Soviet Berlin Blockade (1948-49) and the Allied airlift.
  • In May 1949 the western zones adopted the Basic Law and became the Federal Republic of Germany (West Germany), with Konrad Adenauer as first Chancellor. In October 1949 the Soviet zone became the German Democratic Republic (East Germany).

Design lesson learned: the Basic Law was written to prevent another Weimar - a 'constructive vote of no confidence', a 5% electoral threshold to keep out splinter parties, and strong protection of basic rights.

1949-1989 - the Federal Republic

The economic miracle and a democracy that held

Where Weimar failed, West Germany succeeded. The Wirtschaftswunder ('economic miracle'), associated with economics minister Ludwig Erhard, delivered rapid growth and rising living standards, underpinning a stable democracy.

  • Integration: Adenauer anchored the Republic in the West — the European Coal and Steel Community, membership of NATO (1955), and reconciliation with France.
  • The Wall: the building of the Berlin Wall in 1961 sealed the division of Germany and of Berlin, and became the Cold War's starkest symbol.
  • Ostpolitik: as Chancellor from 1969, the Social Democrat Willy Brandt pursued reconciliation with the East — treaties with the USSR and Poland and the Basic Treaty (1972) recognising the GDR — easing tension without abandoning the goal of unity.
  • Challenges and continuity: the Republic weathered 1968 protest and Red Army Faction terrorism in the 1970s; under Helmut Kohl (from 1982) it remained stable until the Berlin Wall fell on 9 November 1989, opening the road to reunification.

Interpretations - Section C (AO3)

How was Nazi power actually exercised?

The richest interpretation debate in this option concerns the nature of the Third Reich - a question you can also apply to why Weimar fell and how consent was won:

Intentionalists (such as Klaus Hildebrand and Karl Dietrich Bracher) stressed Hitler's will and a coherent ideological programme driving events from the top. Structuralists or functionalists (Hans Mommsen) saw instead a chaotic polycracy of rival agencies and a 'weak dictator' who avoided decisions, with policy radicalising through improvisation. Ian Kershaw offered an influential synthesis: officials and institutions 'working towards the Fuhrer' radicalised policy by anticipating his wishes. A parallel debate on terror versus consent - argued by Robert Gellately - asks how far the regime rested on a genuinely popular basis of denunciation and support, not just the Gestapo.

Exam technique - Paper 1

How Paper 1 is assessed

Edexcel Paper 1 (option 1G): 2 hours 15 minutes, 60 marks, 30% of the A-level.

  • Sections A and B (AO1): a choice of breadth essays on change across 1918-89.
  • Section C (AO3): one compulsory question on two historians' interpretations — evaluate how convincing each is with your own knowledge.

Note: at Edexcel, historians' interpretations (AO3) are examined in Paper 1 and the Coursework; primary sources (AO2) belong to Paper 2 and Paper 3.
