Mission 1

Mission 1: In January 2018 Cape Town, a city of just over 4 million people, warned that its taps could be turned off in April: "Day Zero". What pushed it to the edge, and how did it pull back?

Make a guess: In January 2018 Cape Town, a city of just over 4 million people, warned that its taps could be turned off in April: "Day Zero". What pushed it to the edge, and how did it pull back?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel GCSE Geography A (1GA0)

  • 6.8 global distribution of fresh water
  • 6.8 how availability varies at global, national and local scales
  • 6.8 why some places have a surplus and others a deficit
  • 6.8 how and why supply and demand changed over the past 50 years through human intervention
Mission 1

Where the fresh water is

  • Only about 2.5% of Earth's water is fresh.
  • Of that fresh water, 68.7% is locked in ice and snow and 30.1% is underground.
  • Rivers hold about 0.006% of it.
  • So the water people can easily reach is a tiny, unevenly spread share.
Mission 1

Surplus and deficit

  • Water surplus: supply is greater than demand. Water deficit: demand is greater than supply.
  • Water stress: demand uses up a high share of the supply. WRI counts "extremely high" stress as using at least 80% of the available supply.
  • In 2023, 25 countries, home to a quarter of the world's people, faced extremely high water stress each year.
  • The most water-stressed region is the Middle East and North Africa.
Mission 1

Two kinds of scarcity

  • Physical scarcity: more than 75% of river flows are already withdrawn for farms, industry and homes. Common in dry regions.
  • Economic scarcity: water exists, but money, skills and institutions limit access; less than 25% is withdrawn.
  • Much of sub-Saharan Africa has economic scarcity.
Quick check
?A country has large rivers, and it withdraws less than a quarter of their flow. Many villages have no pipes, wells or treatment. What is this?
Mission 1

What water is used for

  • Worldwide, agriculture takes about 71% of fresh water withdrawals; industry and domestic use (homes and services) share the rest.
  • Richer countries usually use a bigger share for industry and homes.
Mission 1

Why use keeps rising

  • Global water demand "has more than doubled since 1960".
  • Drivers: "growing populations and industries like irrigated agriculture, livestock, energy production and manufacturing".
  • Economic development: richer people eat more meat, buy more goods and use more water at home.
  • Demand is growing fastest in sub-Saharan Africa.
  • Supply grew too, through human intervention: dams and reservoirs, transfers, and pumping groundwater.
  • Example: in 2011 Ethiopia began the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam on the Blue Nile, able to store 74 billion m³.
  • Pumping can run supply down: in the US High Plains, large-scale irrigation pumping began around 1950, and water levels have fallen since.
Mission 1

Different scales

  • Global: extreme stress clusters in the Middle East and North Africa.
  • National (England): in 2021, Thames Water, Southern Water and Affinity Water areas were classed as seriously water stressed; United Utilities (north-west) and Yorkshire Water areas were not.
  • The south-east has less rain and more people; the north-west is wetter.
  • Local: in Cape Town, growing numbers of people in backyard shacks added to demand.
Mission 1

What affects availability

  • Climate: low rainfall and high evaporation mean less water.
  • Geology: permeable rocks (chalk, sandstone) store groundwater; impermeable rocks make water run off quickly.
  • Pollution: dirty water cannot be used without treatment.
  • Over-abstraction: taking water faster than it is replaced.
  • Limited infrastructure and poverty: without pipes, pumps and treatment works, people cannot reach water that exists.
Quick check
?In which place is water shortage caused mainly by people, not the climate?
Mission 1

Cape Town: what pushed it to the edge

  • 2015–2017: "three consecutive years of below average rainfall", a drought with a return time of more than 100 years.
  • Climate change made such a drought about three times as likely.
  • Storage in the six largest dams fell from 100% (2014) to 71%, 60% and then 38%, measured each November.
  • Demand had risen as the city grew.
Mission 1

Cape Town: how it pulled back

  • From 1 February 2018, each person was limited to 50 litres a day.
  • Farm water was cut by up to 60%, and water prices for businesses more than doubled.
  • Daily use fell from over 1,000 million litres in early 2016 to just below 500 million litres, about half.
  • Day Zero was put off on 7 March 2018; winter rain then filled the dams to above 75%.
Mission check

?A city's reservoirs fall for three dry years in a row while its population grows. Which plan best matches how Cape Town avoided Day Zero?

A rare three-year drought met rising demand. Cape Town halved its daily use, from over 1,000 to just below 500 million litres, and winter rain in 2018 refilled the dams.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why do farms take most of India's water, while in Britain most goes to homes?

Make a guess: Why do farms take most of India's water, while in Britain most goes to homes?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel GCSE Geography A (1GA0)

  • 6.9 shares of water used by agriculture, industry and domestic in developed vs emerging/developing countries
  • 6.9 why water usage differs between developed and emerging/developing countries
  • 6.10 why emerging/developing countries have water supply problems (untreated water, polluted water courses, low rainfall)
Mission 2

Three uses of water

  • Agriculture: irrigating crops and watering livestock.
  • Industry: factories, mining, and cooling power stations.
  • Domestic: homes and the public water supply (drinking, cooking, washing, toilets).
  • Worldwide, about 71% of fresh water taken goes to agriculture, 16% to industry and 13% to domestic use.
Mission 2

Developed vs developing

  • High-income (developed) countries: agriculture 43%, industry 40%, domestic 17%.
  • Low-income (developing) countries: agriculture 91%, industry 3%, domestic 7%.
  • UK: agriculture 14%, industry 12%, domestic 74%.
  • India (emerging): agriculture 90%, industry 2%, domestic 7%.
Quick check
?A country uses 88% of its water for agriculture, 2% for industry and 10% for domestic use. What kind of country is it most likely to be?
Mission 2

Why farms dominate in developing countries

  • Many people work in farming, and farming feeds a large, growing population.
  • In hot climates with a long dry season, crops need irrigation. India's rain falls mostly in the monsoon months.
  • There is less industry, so it takes a small share.
  • Many homes have no piped water, so domestic use per person stays low.
Mission 2

Why industry and homes matter more in developed countries

  • More industry and power stations, which need water for processing and cooling. In the USA, industry takes 47%.
  • Almost every home has piped water, plus showers, washing machines and dishwashers: domestic use is high.
  • In wet, cool countries such as the UK, most crops are rain-fed, so farms need little irrigation.
  • Farming is a smaller part of the economy.
Quick check
?Why does UK farming use only about 14% of the water the UK takes?
Mission 2

Supply problem 1: untreated water

  • In 2022, 2.2 billion people lacked safely managed drinking water.
  • 115 million people collected untreated surface water from lakes, ponds, rivers and streams.
  • Contaminated drinking water is estimated to cause about 505,000 diarrhoeal deaths each year.
  • Treating water and piping it to homes is expensive, so poorer places have less of it.
Mission 2

Supply problem 2: polluted water courses

  • Rivers and lakes are polluted by untreated sewage, factory waste and farm chemicals.
  • Only 58% of household wastewater was safely treated (data from 140 countries).
  • In 2023, data on 91,000 water bodies showed 56% had good water quality. Monitoring has large gaps in low-income countries.
  • If people drink from or wash in a polluted river, disease spreads.
Mission 2

Supply problem 3: low annual rainfall

  • In dry regions, rivers and wells can run low or dry.
  • From October 2020, southern Ethiopia, southern Somalia and eastern Kenya had five failed rainy seasons in a row.
  • Households that depend on rain-fed farming and herding were most at risk.
  • Northern Africa takes more than 100% of its renewable water each year: "critical" water stress.
Quick check
?A village takes its drinking water from a river just downstream of a town whose sewage goes straight into the river. Which supply problem is this?
Mission check

?Germany uses about 4% of its water for agriculture; Bangladesh about 88%. Which explanation fits best?

Germany: industry 54%, domestic 42%. Bangladesh: industry 2%, domestic 10%. The same pattern as the UK and India.

Exam move: for "Explain" questions, pair a share with a reason: "India uses about 90% for agriculture because crops need irrigation in the dry season."

Mission 3

Mission 3: Most of Britain's rain falls in the north and west. Why is the south-east the part running short of water?

Make a guess: Most of Britain's rain falls in the north and west. Why is the south-east the part running short of water?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel GCSE Geography A (1GA0)

  • 6.10 why the UK has water supply problems: rainfall–demand imbalance, seasonal imbalance, ageing pipes and sewers
Mission 3

How much water do we use?

  • Each person uses about 137 litres a day at home, compared with 120–123 litres in Germany.
  • England's population is forecast to pass 71 million by 2055, over 8 million more than now.
  • More homes, more food and energy production, and data centres all need water.
  • Without action, England's public water supply could be up to 5,000 million litres a day (Ml/d) short by 2055.
Mission 3

Rain in one place, people in another

  • Rainfall: over 4,000 mm a year in the western Scottish mountains; around 500 mm in the driest parts of eastern England. The higher Lake District averages over 3,200 mm.
  • People: the greatest population growth is forecast in London and the south and east of England.
  • So much of the rain falls on the hills of the north and west, while demand is growing fastest in the south and east.
Quick check
?Why is south-east England more at risk of water shortage than north-west England?
Mission 3

Seasonal imbalance

  • Demand rises in hot spells: watering gardens, filling paddling pools.
  • But summer is when rivers run lowest. Groundwater is refilled mainly in winter (the recharge season), and "hotter, drier periods in summer months may well shorten the recharge season".
  • England had droughts in 2022, 2025 and 2026.
  • So the gap between supply and demand is widest in summer.
Mission 3

Ageing pipes and sewers

  • Water companies in England and Wales own 352,119 km of water mains.
  • About 2,967 million litres leak from them every day (2022–2025 average).
  • London's Victorian sewers were "built for four million people", but now serve "over nine million".
  • Old pipes leak and old sewers overflow, so they waste water and pollute rivers.
Quick check
?About 2,967 million litres of water leak each day. England's forecast shortfall by 2055 is up to 5,000 million litres a day. Leakage is about what percentage of that shortfall?
Mission check

?Two imaginary regions both have leaky old mains. Region R gets 1,800 mm of rain a year and its population is steady. Region S gets 650 mm and is planning thousands of new homes. Which judgement is best?

Rain falls mostly in the north and west, but people and growth are concentrated in the south-east. Summer peaks, droughts and leaking pipes widen the gap, so the south-east runs short first.

Mission 4

Mission 4: About 85% of the Nile's water starts in Ethiopia, yet a 1959 agreement shared it between Egypt and Sudan. Who decides how it is used now, and what would make a deal work?

Make a guess: About 85% of the Nile's water starts in Ethiopia, yet a 1959 agreement shared it between Egypt and Sudan. Who decides how it is used now, and what would make a deal work?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel GCSE Geography A (1GA0)

  • 6.11 how attitudes to water exploitation and consumption vary among individuals, organisations and governments
  • 6.12 different views of individuals, organisations and governments on managing water sustainably
Mission 4

Why shared rivers cause conflict

  • Many rivers cross national borders. Countries upstream control how much water flows on, and when.
  • Countries downstream depend on what arrives.
  • Pressure grows with population, irrigation, hydropower and drought.
  • Stakeholders: governments, organisations (basin groups, companies, researchers) and individuals (farmers, households).
Mission 4

The Nile basin

  • Flow at Aswan, Egypt, averaged 86.5 billion m³ a year (1900–2018).
  • The Blue Nile gives about 55% of it, the White Nile 32% and the Atbara 13%.
  • "85% of Nile waters originate in Ethiopia", yet "nearly all consumptive use occurs downstream in Egypt and Sudan".
Mission 4

The 1959 agreement

  • Signed by Egypt and Sudan in Cairo on 8 November 1959.
  • It shared an expected 84 billion m³ a year: 55.5 billion for Egypt, 18.5 billion for Sudan, and about 10 billion lost from storage.
  • "Not being signatories, Ethiopia and other upstream states do not recognize this agreement".
Quick check
?Use the 1959 figures. What percentage of the 84 billion m³ in the 1959 agreement went to Egypt?
Mission 4

Ethiopia's dam: the GERD

  • Ethiopia began the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam (GERD) on the Blue Nile in 2011.
  • Capacity 5,150 MW, expected to generate about 16 TWh of electricity a year.
  • Its reservoir can hold 74 billion m³.
  • Ethiopia's prime minister inaugurated it on 9 September 2025.
Mission 4

Governments' views

  • Ethiopia (upstream): needs electricity. Demand is projected to rise from 11.1 TWh (2020) to 27.3 TWh (2030).
  • Egypt (downstream): its foreign minister told the UN Security Council that Egypt is "entirely dependent on the Nile River for its survival".
  • Sudan (between them): smoother Blue Nile flows could end flood losses and add hydropower and irrigation water.
  • Each view depends on where the country sits in the basin.
Mission 4

Organisations' views

  • The Nile Basin Initiative (1999) links 10 basin countries.
  • Its Cooperative Framework Agreement (CFA) is based on "equitable and reasonable utilization" and "the obligation not to cause significant harm".
  • The CFA came into force on 13 October 2024. Egypt and Sudan rejected opening it for signature in 2010 and are not parties.
  • Researchers modelled the GERD: it should help Ethiopia and Sudan "without significantly affecting water users in Egypt", but "multi-year droughts will require careful coordination".
Quick check
?Why do Ethiopia and Egypt hold such different views on the GERD?
Mission 4

Individuals: attitudes to using water

  • Individuals' attitudes shape how much water is consumed.
  • In Cape Town's drought (South Africa), residents were limited to 50 litres a person a day from 1 February 2018.
  • The city's daily use fell from over 1,000 million litres (early 2016) to just below 500 million litres at times.
  • Some individuals save water willingly; others resist rules or cannot afford higher prices. Farmers, households and businesses want different things.
Mission 4

Consequences of managing a shared river

  • Economic: Ethiopia gains electricity to use and sell; Sudan could avoid flood losses.
  • Social: Egypt says its water per person is "approaching the absolute water scarcity threshold of 500 cubic metres"; that is Egypt's own statement.
  • Environmental: dams trap sediment; Egypt's High Aswan Dam has lost about 7 billion m³ of storage to sediment. Big reservoirs also lose water to evaporation.
  • Political: the CFA is in force, but without Egypt and Sudan.
Mission check

?On the Mekong, China has 12 dams on the upper river but is not one of the four member countries of the Mekong River Commission. What would most help the basin's countries share the river?

Ethiopia supplies about 85% of the Nile's water but was not part of the 1959 deal. A deal that works needs all the basin countries, with agreed rules for droughts.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Deserts get more sunshine than they can use, and many lie beside the sea. Can technology turn sun and seawater into reliable power and water?

Make a guess: Deserts get more sunshine than they can use, and many lie beside the sea. Can technology turn sun and seawater into reliable power and water?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel GCSE Geography A (1GA0)

  • 6.11 how technology (desalination) can resolve water shortages
Mission 5

Why drylands run short of water

  • Rain is low and unreliable, and hot, sunny weather evaporates much of it.
  • Drylands are home to about 3 billion people, and demand for water grows with population, farming and industry.
  • Technology can add new supplies (desalination) or make each drop go further (drip irrigation, water harvesting).
Mission 5

How desalination works

  • Desalination removes salt from sea water or salty groundwater.
  • Distillation: boil the water, capture the steam and condense it back into fresh water.
  • Reverse osmosis: force salty water through a semi-permeable membrane (a very fine filter). Water passes through; the dissolved salt is held back.
  • Both are "expensive, energy-intensive, and involve large-scale facilities" (USGS).
Mission 5

Desalination worldwide

  • A 2019 global study counted 15,906 working desalination plants, producing about 95 million m³ of fresh water a day.
  • 48% of it was produced in the Middle East and North Africa, where deserts meet the sea.
Mission 5

Case: Carlsbad, California

  • Southern California is dry and often in drought. The Carlsbad plant began commercial operations in 2015.
  • It makes more than 50 million gallons of drinking water a day, enough for about 400,000 people: about 10% of the San Diego region's demand.
  • It uses reverse osmosis. Energy-recovery devices cut the energy the process uses by 46%.
  • Its water is "more expensive than the region's traditional imported water sources", but it is drought-proof.
Quick check
?How does reverse osmosis make fresh water?
Mission 5

The salty leftovers: brine

  • For every plant, the salt has to go somewhere: a very salty liquid called brine.
  • The 2019 study estimated brine output at about 142 million m³ a day, 55% of it from Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Kuwait and Qatar.
  • Disposal is "both costly and associated with negative environmental impacts".
  • At Carlsbad the brine has about twice the salt of sea water; it is diluted with sea water before release.
Quick check
?The world's plants make about 95 million m³ of fresh water and about 142 million m³ of brine a day. About how many m³ of brine is made for each m³ of fresh water?
Mission 5

Can the sun power it?

  • Deserts get strong sunshine: Coober Pedy, Australia, averages 28.3 MJ of solar energy per m² a day in January.
  • Morocco's Noor-Ouarzazate concentrated solar power complex: the World Bank helped finance a first 160 MW phase of a 500 MW plant (2011), then two more phases (2014), to raise output "especially during peak hours".
  • The project needed money to cover the gap between the price paid for the power and the price it was sold at.
  • Stronger sand storms and moving dunes damage solar and wind infrastructure (IPCC, high confidence).
Mission 5

Does technology solve water shortages?

  • For: desalination gives a supply that does not depend on rain, and solar power can run it.
  • Against: high cost and energy use; about 1.5 m³ of brine for each m³ of water.
  • Who benefits? Rich coastal cities. "Many oases are located far from water sources that are suitable for desalination".
  • Exam move: judge each technology by cost, energy, side effects, scale and who benefits.
Mission check

?A dry, wealthy coastal city plans a large solar-powered desalination plant. Which prediction is most accurate?

Yes, with costs. Solar power and desalination already supply power and water in drylands, but solar needed price support at Noor-Ouarzazate, and desalination leaves about 1.5 m³ of brine for each m³ of fresh water. Technology helps most where people can afford it and live near the sea.

Mission 6

Mission 6: The Lower Mekong's fisheries land an estimated 2.3 million tonnes of fish a year, worth about US$11 billion. Its hydropower capacity could more than double. Can one river be both a great fishery and a giant power station?

Make a guess: The Lower Mekong's fisheries land an estimated 2.3 million tonnes of fish a year, worth about US$11 billion. Its hydropower capacity could more than double. Can one river be both a great fishery and a giant power station?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel GCSE Geography A (1GA0)

  • 6.12 why water resources require sustainable management
  • 6.12 how one developed and one emerging/developing country have tried to manage water sustainably
Mission 6

Why water needs sustainable management

  • Demand rises with population, farming, industry and wealth; supply is fixed and uneven.
  • In 2024, 2.1 billion people (1 in 4) still lacked safely managed drinking water.
  • Over-use can drain rivers, lakes and aquifers, and harm ecosystems.
  • Sustainable management meets today's needs without leaving less water, or worse water, for the future.
Mission 6

Dams and reservoirs: the Mekong

  • The Mekong flows nearly 5,000 km through six countries.
  • Lower Mekong: 88 hydropower projects already operate, with over 13,257 MW of capacity.
  • 20 more are being built and 14 planned. If all are built, capacity could reach 27,302 MW.
  • Benefits: electricity, income from selling power to neighbours, and flood and drought mitigation.
Quick check
?Use the capacity figures on the last screen. By what factor could the Lower Mekong's hydropower capacity grow?
Mission 6

Who gains, who loses?

  • "Dams trap sediment" that keeps the Mekong Delta fertile.
  • Dams block fish migration, which "threatens the food security and income of millions of people".
  • About 80% of the nearly 65 million people in the lower basin depend on the river.
  • Gains go mostly to power users and governments; losses fall mostly on fishers and delta farmers downstream.
Mission 6

Small scale: rainwater harvesting

  • Appropriate technology: simple, cheap kit that local people can build and repair, e.g. roof gutters feeding a storage tank, or small ponds and check dams.
  • India's "Catch the Rain" campaign (launched 22 March 2021) aims to "catch the rain, where it falls, when it falls", with "people's active participation".
  • Social: water close to home; less time spent fetching it.
  • Economic: low cost; local people build and maintain it.
  • Environmental: tops up groundwater; little damage. But it fails in long droughts and can only store small amounts.
Mission 6

Two countries compared

  • Edexcel uses the Human Development Index (HDI). Australia: 0.958, very high (developed). India: 0.685, medium (emerging).
  • Australia: the Murray–Darling Basin Plan aims to recover 2,750 GL a year of water for the environment; this has "improved river flows" but will not be fully implemented on time or within budget.
  • India: the Jal Jeevan Mission aims to give every rural home a tap, using "a community approach to water"; Catch the Rain adds rainwater harvesting.
  • Australia recovers water from users across a whole basin; India focuses on getting safe water to homes and storing rain locally.
Quick check
?Which statement fits the evidence on the last screen?
Mission check

?A country plans a large dam on a river that flows on into a neighbouring country with big river fisheries and a farmed delta. What is the most likely outcome?

Lower Mekong capacity could grow about 2.06 times, but dams trap sediment and block fish migration in a fishery worth about US$11 billion a year. One river can do both only with trade-offs.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Fresh water is scarce and uneven: deficits come from climate (physical scarcity) or from lack of money and pipes (economic scarcity).

Mission 2

  • Developing and emerging countries use most of their water for agriculture; developed countries use far more for industry and domestic supply.
  • Supply problems in developing countries: untreated water, polluted water courses and low rainfall.

Mission 3

  • Demand: about 137 litres per person per day; population rising to over 71 million in England by 2055; up to 5,000 Ml/d short without action.
  • Supply–demand mismatch: over 4,000 mm of rain in the west, about 500 mm in the east; most growth in the south-east. Summer demand peaks when rivers are low. Leakage: about 2,967 Ml/d.

Mission 4

  • Upstream and downstream governments see the same river differently.
  • Individuals, organisations and governments hold different views on using and saving water.

Mission 5

  • Desalination (distillation or reverse osmosis) turns sea water into fresh water: reliable, but costly, energy-hungry and brine-producing.

Mission 6

  • Dams and reservoirs store water and make power, but trap sediment and block fish.
  • Australia (very high HDI) and India (medium HDI) manage water in different ways.
