Make a guess: Jesus' most common name for himself was "Son of Man". What did it mean, and why not "Messiah"?

Mission 1

"Who do you say that I am?"

  • At the centre of Mark's Gospel Jesus asks his disciples who he is (Mark 8:27–30).
  • Peter: "You are the Christ." (Mark 8:29). Matthew adds "the Son of the living God" (Matthew 16:16); Luke has "The Christ of God" (Luke 9:20).
  • Jesus tells them to tell no one, then speaks of himself as the Son of Man who "must suffer" (Mark 8:31).
  • Three titles meet in one scene: Messiah (Christ), Son of God, Son of Man.
  • A Christological title is a name that says who Jesus is and what he came to do.
  • Each title had a meaning before Jesus. Each evangelist uses it in his own way.
Mission 1

Messiah: the anointed one

  • Hebrew mashiach, Greek christos: "anointed". Oil marked someone set apart by God.
  • Kings were anointed: Samuel anoints David, and "the LORD's Spirit came mightily on David" (1 Samuel 16:13). Priests were anointed too.
  • God promises David a lasting dynasty (2 Samuel 7:12–16). Hope grew for a future son of David.
  • Expectation in Jesus' time was varied, not one fixed picture:
  • A royal liberator from David's line (Psalms of Solomon 17, 1st century BC).
  • At Qumran, two messiahs, of Aaron (priestly) and of Israel (royal) (Community Rule, 1QS 9:11).
  • Some hoped for a prophet like Moses; many expected no individual messiah at all.
  • A suffering Messiah was not a common expectation (the Suffering Servant of Isaiah 53 was not usually read as a suffering Messiah: see an earlier lesson).
Mission 1

How the Synoptics handle "Messiah"

  • Mark: Jesus accepts Peter's confession but orders silence (8:30), the messianic secret. Only at his trial does he say openly: "I am." (14:62)
  • He at once re-defines the title through suffering (8:31) and points to the Son of Man.
  • On the cross he is mocked as "the Christ, the King of Israel" (15:32): the title is true, but not in the way the mockers mean.
  • Matthew: opens with "Jesus Christ, the son of David" (1:1); the genealogy and proof texts show him fulfilling Israel's hopes.
  • Luke: the angels announce "a Saviour, who is Christ the Lord" (2:11); at Nazareth Jesus reads Isaiah 61:1, "he has anointed me", about himself (4:18).
  • R. E. Brown (1928–1998), An Introduction to New Testament Christology (1994), paraphrase: Jesus probably did not reject the title outright, but would not accept it on the terms of a political king; the Gospels show it being reshaped by the cross.
Mission 1Read the source

"My son" in the Old Testament

"Son of God" did not mean "divine" to most Jews of Jesus' day. It named someone in a close, chosen relationship with God.

> "Israel is my son, my firstborn" (Exodus 4:22, WEBBE)

> "I will be his father, and he will be my son." (2 Samuel 7:14, WEBBE)

> "You are my son. Today I have become your father." (Psalm 2:7, WEBBE)

  • Angels are "sons of God" too (Job 1:6).

Mission 1

Son of God in each evangelist

  • Mark: "the Good News of Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (1:1; "Son of God" is missing from some early manuscripts).
  • In Mark only God (baptism 1:11; transfiguration 9:7) and demons (3:11) know it, until a Gentile centurion at the cross: "Truly this man was the Son of God!" (15:39). The title is revealed in his death.
  • Matthew: the disciples worship him as "truly the Son of God" after he walks on water (14:33); Peter's confession adds the title (16:16).
  • Luke: the title is linked to his conception by the Holy Spirit (1:35) and to the human race: the genealogy runs back to "Adam, the son of God" (3:38).
  • Jesus calls God "Abba, Father" in prayer (Mark 14:36): a relationship of trust and obedience.
  • Brown (The Birth of the Messiah, 1977), paraphrase: Christians first linked Jesus' divine sonship with the resurrection (Romans 1:4), then the baptism (Mark), then the conception (Matthew, Luke), then pre-existence (John 1). Insight about Jesus' identity moved backwards through his life.

Mission 1

Son of Man: the background

  • Hebrew ben adam, Aramaic bar enash: literally "son of a human", that is, a human being.
  • Psalm 8:4: "What is the son of man, that you care for him?": a frail human.
  • Ezekiel: God addresses the prophet as "Son of man" over 90 times (e.g. Ezekiel 2:1).
  • Daniel 7:13–14: in a vision, "one like a son of man" comes "with the clouds of the sky" to the Ancient of Days and is given "dominion, and glory, and a kingdom" for ever.
  • In Daniel the kingdom is then given to "the people of the saints of the Most High" (7:27): many scholars read the figure as representing God's faithful people, persecuted and then vindicated; others see an individual heavenly or messianic figure.
  • So the phrase can mean ordinary humanity or a heavenly figure of glory. Jesus' use could carry either, or both.
Mission 1

Three groups of sayings

  • In the Synoptics "Son of Man" is almost always on Jesus' own lips, and no one ever confesses him by it.
  • A common grouping (from Rudolf Bultmann, 1884–1976):
  • Earthly ministry: authority "to forgive sins" (Mark 2:10); "lord even of the Sabbath" (Mark 2:28); "no place to lay his head" (Luke 9:58).
  • Suffering: "must suffer many things … and be killed, and after three days rise again" (Mark 8:31; also 9:31; 10:33–34); "came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many" (10:45).
  • Future glory: "coming in clouds with great power and glory" (13:26); at his trial, "sitting at the right hand of Power" (14:62, joining Daniel 7:13 and Psalm 110:1).
  • Luke adds "came to seek and to save that which was lost" (19:10): the title serves his theme of salvation for outsiders.
  • Matthew has the Son of Man as judge of the nations (25:31).
Mission 1Diversity

What did "Son of Man" mean?

  • Geza Vermes (1924–2013), Jesus the Jew (1973): in Aramaic it could be a modest way of saying "I"; no title was intended.
  • Bultmann, Theology of the New Testament (1948–53): only the future sayings go back to Jesus, and in them he spoke of another figure (Mark 8:38 can be read that way); the Church later identified him with Jesus.
  • Morna Hooker (b. 1931), The Son of Man in Mark (1967): drawn from Daniel 7: the Son of Man has authority from God, is rejected and suffers, and is vindicated. All three groups belong together.
  • C. H. Dodd (1884–1973), According to the Scriptures (1952): the early Church read Daniel 7, Psalm 8 and Psalm 80:17 together; the Son of Man is an inclusive figure who stands for the new people of God.
  • Brown (1994): it is likely that Jesus did use "Son of Man" of himself, drawing on Daniel; the debate is about how much.
  • Many scholars add that the phrase was ambiguous enough to let Jesus speak about his mission without the political weight of "Messiah".

Mission 1Evaluate

Which title best expresses who Jesus is in the Synoptics?

  • Claim: "Son of Man" says more about Jesus in the Synoptic Gospels than "Son of God".
  • For: It is Jesus' own preferred self-designation; it holds together his authority, his suffering and his future glory.
  • For: It drew on Daniel 7 without the political danger of "Messiah" or the misunderstanding of "Son of God".
  • Against: Its meaning is disputed (Vermes: just "I"); it was never used in the early Church's confessions of faith.
  • Against: "Son of God" frames Mark (1:1; 15:39) and marks the turning points (baptism, transfiguration, trial, cross).
  • Against: Each evangelist has his own emphasis: Matthew stresses Jesus as Son of David and Son of God; Luke stresses Saviour and Lord.
  • Judgement criteria: Which title is closest to Jesus' own words? Which one shapes each Gospel's structure? Should the titles be read in their first-century or later Christian meaning?
Mission 1

The titles and ways of living

  • Son of Man who serves (Mark 10:45): service, not domination, as the pattern for Christian leadership. A traditional papal title is "servant of the servants of God".
  • Son of God: Christians who see Jesus as divine worship him; believers become God's children too (Romans 8:14), so every person has dignity: a basis for equality.
  • Messiah: those who stress a liberating Messiah (Luke 4:18) link faith to justice for the poor (e.g. liberation theology). Those who stress a spiritual kingdom focus on inner change and the Church.
  • Beyond the Church: most Jews do not accept that Jesus was the Messiah, since the messianic age of peace has not come. Islam honours Jesus as al-Masih (the Messiah) but rejects "Son of God" (Qur'an 112). Different understandings of the titles shape interfaith relations.
  • Different understandings lead to different codes: imitation of a suffering servant, obedience to a divine Lord, or work for a just society.

Mission 2

Two kinds of "I am"

  • Greek egō eimi: "I am". In John it appears in two main ways.
  • With a picture (a predicate): "I am the bread of life", "the light of the world", "the good shepherd", "the true vine". Your spec names these four (anthology extract 3).
  • Absolute, with nothing after it: "before Abraham came into existence, I AM." (John 8:58)
  • R. E. Brown (1928–1998), The Gospel according to John (Anchor Bible, 1966–70), Appendix IV, paraphrase: three uses: (1) absolute (8:24, 28, 58; 13:19); (2) a plain "It is I" where a deeper sense may be heard (6:20; 18:5); (3) with a predicate (the picture sayings).
  • None of the picture sayings appears in Matthew, Mark or Luke.
  • John also has "the sheep's door" (10:7), "the resurrection and the life" (11:25), "the way, the truth, and the life" (14:6).
Mission 2Read the source

The divine name

> "God said to Moses, 'I AM WHO I AM' … 'I AM has sent me to you.'" (Exodus 3:14, WEBBE)

> "that you may know and believe me, and understand that I am he." (Isaiah 43:10, WEBBE)

> "Most certainly, I tell you, before Abraham came into existence, I AM." (John 8:58, WEBBE)

  • In the Greek Old Testament (Septuagint), God's "I am he" in Isaiah is egō eimi.
  • After John 8:58 the hearers "took up stones" (8:59): they heard a claim to share God's name.

Q1 What does the contrast between "came into existence" and "I AM" in John 8:58 suggest?

Show answer

Abraham began to exist; Jesus claims an existence without beginning, like God's.

Q2 Why does the reaction in 8:59 matter for interpreting the saying?

Show answer

It shows the narrator wants readers to hear a claim to divine status, judged blasphemy by the hearers.

Mission 2

"I am the bread of life"

  • Said after the feeding of the 5,000, near Passover (John 6:4, 35, 48).
  • Manna: God promised to "rain bread from the sky" in the wilderness (Exodus 16:4). The crowd cites this (6:31); Jesus says the true bread from heaven gives eternal life (6:32–33, 50).
  • Wisdom: "Come, eat some of my bread" (Proverbs 9:5): to eat Wisdom's bread is to take in God's teaching.
  • "Whoever comes to me will not be hungry, and whoever believes in me will never be thirsty" (6:35): coming and believing are the eating.
  • "the bread which I will give for the life of the world is my flesh" (6:51); "unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his blood" (6:53): most Christians hear the Eucharist.
  • Symbolism: Jesus is what people need to live, as basic as bread.
Mission 2

"I am the light of the world"

  • "He who follows me will not walk in the darkness, but will have the light of life." (John 8:12; repeated at 9:5 before healing a man born blind.)
  • Setting: the Feast of Booths (Tabernacles, 7:2), when great lamps were lit in the Temple courts (described in the Mishnah, Sukkah 5).
  • Creation: "Let there be light" (Genesis 1:3). God: "The LORD is my light and my salvation" (Psalm 27:1).
  • Mission: the Servant is given "as a light to the nations" (Isaiah 49:6).
  • Torah: "Your word is a lamp to my feet" (Psalm 119:105).
  • Symbolism: light reveals truth and judges (9:39); following Jesus means a life not lived "in the darkness".

Mission 2

"I am the good shepherd"

  • "The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep." (John 10:11); "I know my own, and I'm known by my own" (10:14).
  • God as shepherd: "The LORD is my shepherd" (Psalm 23:1); "Shepherd of Israel" (Psalm 80:1).
  • Ezekiel 34: God condemns Israel's leaders as bad shepherds who feed themselves. God promises: "I myself will be the shepherd of my sheep" (34:15) and "one shepherd over them … my servant David" (34:23).
  • John 10 follows the expulsion of the healed blind man by the authorities (ch. 9): Jesus contrasts himself with thieves and hired hands.
  • "Other sheep which are not of this fold … one flock with one shepherd" (10:16): often read as Gentiles joining.
  • Symbolism: care, knowledge of each person, and self-sacrifice: the shepherd dies for the sheep.
Mission 2

"I am the true vine"

  • "I am the true vine, and my Father is the farmer." (John 15:1); "I am the vine. You are the branches." (15:5)
  • In the Old Testament Israel is God's vine:
  • "You brought a vine out of Egypt" (Psalm 80:8).
  • "the vineyard of the LORD of Armies is the house of Israel … He looked for justice, but behold, oppression" (Isaiah 5:7).
  • "I had planted you a noble vine … How then have you turned into the degenerate branches" (Jeremiah 2:21).
  • So "true vine" (Greek alēthinos, real, genuine) claims that Jesus is what Israel was meant to be; believers belong to God's people by being joined to him.
  • Key word: remain (abide): "Remain in me, and I in you" (15:4). Fruit means love: "love one another, even as I have loved you" (15:12).

Mission 2Diversity

Brown and Dodd on the sayings

  • C. H. Dodd (1884–1973), The Interpretation of the Fourth Gospel (1953), paraphrase:
  • The symbols answer the deepest religious longings of both Jewish and Greek readers.
  • "True" (alēthinos) means the real, eternal thing, of which earthly bread, light and vines are copies: close to Plato and to Philo of Alexandria.
  • John writes for educated non-Christians in a Hellenistic city.
  • Brown (1966–70), paraphrase:
  • The background is mainly Jewish: the Old Testament, Wisdom literature, the festivals, and the Dead Sea Scrolls (whose language of light and darkness is close to John's).
  • The predicate sayings tell us what Jesus is for people (their bread, their light), more than his inner nature.
  • They agree that the sayings are revelation: Jesus gives what God gives.
  • A further debate: did Jesus speak these words, or are they the evangelist's inspired meditation on him? Both scholars think John develops genuine tradition in his own idiom.
Mission 2Evaluate

Are the 'I am' sayings claims to divinity?

  • Claim: In the 'I am' sayings Jesus claims to be God.
  • For: The absolute "I AM" (8:58) echoes Exodus 3:14 and Isaiah 43:10, and the hearers try to stone him (8:59).
  • For: Each picture takes a role the Old Testament gives to God (shepherd of Israel, light, giver of bread from heaven).
  • Against: The same images are used of humans: David as shepherd (Ezekiel 34:23), the Servant as light (Isaiah 49:6), Israel as vine.
  • Against: "I am he" can be ordinary speech: the healed blind man says it of himself (9:9).
  • Against: Brown: the picture sayings stress Jesus' role for believers, not a statement of his nature; and "the Father is greater than I" (14:28) keeps a distinction.
  • Judgement criteria: Read in context of the whole Gospel (1:1; 10:30; 20:28)? Read as first hearers would? Absolute and picture sayings treated alike or apart?
Mission 2

The sayings and ways of living

  • Bread: the Eucharist at the centre of worship. Catholic and Orthodox Christians read John 6:53 as real presence; many Protestants read it as spiritual feeding by faith. Many churches run food banks: physical and spiritual hunger both matter.
  • Light: follow Jesus' teaching rather than "walk in the darkness"; honesty and moral clarity; Christians as witnesses (Matthew 5:14).
  • Shepherd: the model for church leaders: pastor means shepherd; a bishop carries a crook-shaped crozier. Leaders must serve and protect, not exploit (the warning of Ezekiel 34).
  • Vine: remain in Jesus through prayer and worship; the fruit is love (15:12); unity of the Church as one plant.
  • Beyond the Church: the images shape art and public language. Holman Hunt's painting The Light of the World (1851–53) became one of the best-known religious images in Britain. "Good shepherd" names hospitals, schools and charities.
  • A high reading (Jesus is God) leads to worship of Jesus; a lower reading stresses following him as teacher and example.
Mission 2Worked answer

Linking a saying, its background and a scholar

Question type: explain the significance of one 'I am' saying for understanding the identity of Jesus.

  • Saying: "I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep." (John 10:11)
  • Background: in Ezekiel 34 God condemns Israel's leaders and promises to shepherd his people himself (34:15).
  • Develop: so the saying claims Jesus does what God promised, and replaces failed leaders (in John 9 the authorities have just thrown out the healed man).
  • Scholar: Brown would stress the role: Jesus is shepherd for his people, and the cross (laying down his life) is the proof.
  • Evaluate: but David is also called shepherd (34:23), so the saying may present Jesus as the promised messianic king rather than as God; the absolute "I AM" of 8:58 makes the stronger claim.
  • Strong answers keep each step linked: saying → background → meaning → scholar → judgement.

Mission 3

An overture to the Gospel

  • The Prologue is John 1:1–18 (anthology extract 2). Like an overture, it states the themes the Gospel will play out.
  • A common outline:
  • 1:1–5 The Word with God before creation; life and light.
  • 1:6–8 John the Baptist, a witness to the light.
  • 1:9–13 The light comes into the world; rejected by some, received by others.
  • 1:14–18 The Word becomes flesh; glory, grace and truth; the Son makes the Father known.
  • Many scholars think verses 1–5, 9–14 and 16 may come from an early hymn, with the Baptist verses (6–8, 15) added; others (including Hooker) see the whole as one unit.
  • Key words that return later: life, light, darkness, world, believe, glory, truth.
Mission 3Read the source

In the beginning

> "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God." (John 1:1, WEBBE)

> "The Word became flesh and lived amongst us. We saw his glory, such glory as of the only born Son of the Father, full of grace and truth." (John 1:14, WEBBE)

  • "In the beginning" repeats the first words of Genesis 1:1.
  • The Word (Greek Logos) is with God (distinct) and is God (one with God).

Mission 3

The Word made flesh

  • Logos means word, reason, account. The Word is God's self-expression: through him "All things were made" (1:3).
  • Flesh (Greek sarx): full, frail humanity, not a costume. This is the incarnation.
  • "Lived amongst us": the Greek verb means pitched his tent (eskēnōsen), recalling God's presence in the wilderness tabernacle.
  • "We saw his glory": as God's glory filled the tabernacle (Exodus 40:34), it is now seen in a human life.
  • "the only born Son" (1:14, 18): the Word is now named as Son; the name Jesus Christ appears only at 1:17.
  • Against Docetism (the later idea that Christ only seemed human), 1:14 insists the Word was truly flesh.
Mission 3

Life, light and darkness

  • "In him was life, and the life was the light of men." (1:4)
  • "The light shines in the darkness, and the darkness hasn't overcome it." (1:5): the Greek can also mean "hasn't understood it"; John may intend both.
  • Light: God's truth and life revealed. Darkness: the world turned away from God, unbelief, evil.
  • "The true light that enlightens everyone" (1:9): the light is offered to all people.
  • The theme runs through the Gospel: "I am the light of the world" (8:12); Nicodemus comes "by night" (3:2); Judas goes out: "It was night." (13:30).
  • Dualism: John contrasts light and darkness, above and below, spirit and flesh, but the world is still God's creation, made through the Word (1:10).
Mission 3

Belief, children of God, flesh and spirit

  • "He came to his own, and those who were his own didn't receive him." (1:11): rejection by many in Israel, a theme of the Gospel.
  • "As many as received him, to them he gave the right to become God's children, to those who believe in his name" (1:12).
  • Belief in John is a verb, never a noun: trusting in the person of Jesus (the Gospel's stated aim, 20:31).
  • Children of God are "born, not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God" (1:13): not by ancestry or human choice, but by new birth.
  • Flesh and spirit: "That which is born of the flesh is flesh. That which is born of the Spirit is spirit." (3:6) Flesh is not evil, but on its own it cannot give life with God.
  • Significance: membership of God's people is by faith, open to anyone.

Mission 3

Law, grace and truth

  • "For the law was given through Moses. Grace and truth were realised through Jesus Christ." (1:17)
  • Background: at Sinai God is proclaimed "merciful and gracious … abundant in loving kindness and truth" (Exodus 34:6). Hebrew hesed (steadfast love) and emet (faithfulness, truth) lie behind "grace and truth".
  • Moses was told "man may not see me and live" (Exodus 33:20). John: "No one has seen God at any time. The only born Son … has declared him." (1:18)
  • "From his fullness we all received grace upon grace" (1:16): the Greek can mean grace in place of grace (the gift of the Law followed by the gift of Christ).
  • Most interpreters read 1:17 as fulfilment, not rejection: the Law was a gift; Christ brings God's gift in person.
  • Some later Christian readings set Law against grace sharply; many scholars warn that this misreads John and Judaism.
Mission 3

Jewish influences

  • Genesis 1: "In the beginning"; God creates by speaking ("God said", 1:3); light first.
  • The word of the LORD: "By the LORD's word, the heavens were made" (Psalm 33:6); God's word goes out and accomplishes his will (Isaiah 55:11).
  • Wisdom: present at creation, "the craftsman by his side" (Proverbs 8:30); "a reflection of everlasting light" (Wisdom 7:26); told by the Creator, "Let your dwelling be in Jacob" (Sirach 24:8): compare "lived amongst us".
  • Torah: later Jewish tradition identified Wisdom with the Law, a "light" for the path (Psalm 119:105).
  • Tabernacle and glory (Exodus 40:34): God's presence among his people. Later rabbis spoke of the Shekhinah (indwelling presence).
  • Aramaic translations (Targums) speak of God's Memra (Word) acting in the world; their dating is debated.
Mission 3

Hellenistic influences

  • Heraclitus (c. 535–c. 475 BC): the Logos is the rational order behind the changing world.
  • Stoics (from c. 300 BC): the Logos is divine reason running through the universe; every human shares in it.
  • Philo of Alexandria (c. 20 BC–c. AD 50), a Jewish thinker writing in Greek: the Logos is God's agent in creation and his image, a bridge between God and the world; he calls it God's "firstborn".
  • A Greek-speaking reader would hear "Logos" as the reason that orders all things, and the light that "enlightens everyone" (1:9) as reason in every person.
  • But no Greek philosopher said the Logos became flesh: 1:14 would shock this audience.
  • The line between "Jewish" and "Hellenistic" is blurred: Judaism had been part of the Greek-speaking world for three centuries, and Philo was a Jew.
Mission 3Diversity

Dodd and Hooker

  • C. H. Dodd (1884–1973), The Interpretation of the Fourth Gospel (1953), paraphrase:
  • John writes for educated, religious-minded people in a Hellenistic city (perhaps Ephesus).
  • "Logos" is chosen because it speaks to both worlds: Hebrew "word of the Lord" and Wisdom, and the Logos of Philo and the Stoics.
  • The Prologue is a summary of the whole life of Jesus, told as the story of the eternal Logos in the world.
  • Morna Hooker (b. 1931), "The Johannine Prologue and the Messianic Secret", New Testament Studies 21 (1974), and Beginnings: Keys that Open the Gospels (1997), paraphrase:
  • Like the opening of Mark, the Prologue gives readers the key to the story: they know who Jesus is, while characters in the story struggle to see it.
  • The key background is Jewish: Moses, Sinai, the Law and the glory (Exodus 33–34); the witness of John the Baptist belongs to the design.
  • Both: the Prologue must be read with the rest of the Gospel.

Jewish or Hellenistic?

  • Claim: The Prologue is best understood against a Jewish rather than a Hellenistic background.
  • For: It opens with Genesis 1 and ends with Moses and Sinai (1:17–18); Wisdom texts supply almost every idea (pre-existence, creation, light, dwelling).
  • For: The Dead Sea Scrolls show that light/darkness language was at home in Palestinian Judaism.
  • Against: "Logos" is the key word of Greek philosophy and of Philo; Dodd argues John chose it to reach Greek readers.
  • Against: The Gospel was probably written in a Greek city for a mixed audience (12:20–21 has Greeks seeking Jesus).
  • Against the question itself: Hellenistic Judaism (Philo, the Wisdom of Solomon) shows the two were already mixed.
  • Judgement criteria: Which background explains the most verses? Which explains 1:14, the most original claim? What audience does the rest of the Gospel imply?
Mission 3

The Prologue and ways of living

  • Incarnation: if God became flesh, bodies and the material world matter. Christians ground care for the sick, the poor and creation in this, and many traditions value sacraments (God through material things).
  • Light for everyone (1:9): every person has dignity and some access to truth: a basis for universal human rights and for dialogue with other faiths and with non-religious people.
  • Children of God by faith (1:12–13): Christian community is not based on race or birth; churches cite this against racism and exclusion.
  • Grace and truth together: forgiveness without lying about wrong; Christian codes join mercy with honesty.
  • Law and grace (1:17): different Christians draw different codes. Some stress freedom from detailed rules; others say grace fulfils the moral law. Some readings fed contempt for Judaism; many churches now reject this.
  • Beyond the Church: the idea that every person carries the "light" has influenced Western ideas of conscience and equal human worth; secular humanists find a similar value in reason shared by all.
Mission check

?A first-century reader knows Philo but not Christianity. Reading the Prologue, which verse would surprise this reader most, and why?

The Prologue speaks the language of both Jewish and Greek readers, then says what neither expected: the Word became flesh. A later mission: the signs that show his glory.

Make a guess: John never calls Jesus' miracles "miracles". He calls them signs. What are they pointing to?

Mission 4

What is a sign?

  • Matthew, Mark and Luke mostly call Jesus' miracles dunameis: acts of power.
  • John calls them sēmeia: signs. Jesus himself calls them his works (erga).
  • A sign points beyond itself: to Jesus' identity, his glory, and the life he gives.
  • Background: God's "signs" in Egypt, done "that you may know that I am the LORD" (Exodus 10:2).
  • A sign can lead to faith or be rejected: "though he had done so many signs before them, yet they didn't believe in him" (John 12:37).
  • Dodd named John 2–12 the Book of Signs; Brown calls 1:19–12:50 the Book of Signs and 13:1–20:31 the Book of Glory.
Mission 4

The seven signs

Your spec lists seven (anthology extract 4):

  • 1. Water into wine at Cana (2:1–11): "the beginning of his signs".
  • 2. The official's son healed from a distance (4:43–54): "the second sign".
  • 3. The healing at the pool of Bethesda, on the Sabbath (5:1–15).
  • 4. The feeding of the 5,000 near Passover (6:1–15).
  • 5. Walking on water (6:16–24).
  • 6. The man born blind (9:1–12; the trial that follows runs to 9:41).
  • 7. The raising of Lazarus (11:1–44).
  • Only the first two are numbered by John. Some scholars do not count walking on water (no public witnesses; not called a sign) and count the miraculous catch of fish (21:1–14) or the resurrection instead.
Mission 4

Cana to Cana

  • Water into wine: six stone jars "after the Jews' way of purifying" (2:6) are filled with wine.
  • Meaning: many Christian interpreters read it this way: the new age has come, and Jesus fulfils the Jewish rites of purification with abundance. Others warn against reading the jars as a put-down of Judaism. The prophets pictured the messianic age overflowing with wine (Amos 9:13).
  • "You have kept the good wine until now!" (2:10): the best is last. Jesus "revealed his glory; and his disciples believed in him." (2:11)
  • "My hour has not yet come" (2:4): the sign points ahead to the cross, his hour of glory.
  • The official's son (4:46–54): Jesus says, "Go your way. Your son lives." "The man believed the word that Jesus spoke to him" (4:50).
  • Meaning: faith in Jesus' word, without seeing; Jesus gives life, even at a distance. The official's whole household believes (4:53).
Mission 4

The pool: a Sabbath sign

  • A man ill for 38 years lies by the pool. "Do you want to be made well?" (5:6) "Arise, take up your mat, and walk." (5:8)
  • "Now that day was a Sabbath." (5:9): carrying a mat breaks the Sabbath as the authorities interpret it.
  • Jesus: "My Father is still working, so I am working, too." (5:17)
  • The authorities seek to kill him, because he "called God his own Father, making himself equal with God." (5:18)
  • Meaning: Jesus shares God's work of giving life and judging (5:21–22), work God does even on the Sabbath.
  • This sign begins open conflict with the authorities.

Mission 4

Bread and the sea

  • The feeding (6:1–15): near Passover (6:4); a boy's "five barley loaves and two fish" (6:9) feed thousands; twelve baskets are left.
  • The crowd says, "This is truly the prophet who comes into the world" (John 6:14), recalling the prophet like Moses (Deuteronomy 18:15). They try to make him king (6:15); Jesus withdraws.
  • The sign leads into the bread of life discourse (6:26–59): manna, true bread, the Eucharist (see Mission 2).
  • Walking on water (6:16–21): Jesus comes across the sea to the frightened disciples: "It is I. Don't be afraid." (6:20)
  • The Greek is egō eimi: on the surface "It is I", but many readers (and Brown) hear an echo of God's "I am". In the Old Testament God "treads on the waves of the sea" (Job 9:8) and "Your way was through the sea" (Psalm 77:19).
  • Meaning for John: Jesus does what the Old Testament says only God does: he provides bread like the God of the Exodus and masters the waters.
Mission 4

Sight and life

  • The man born blind (9:1–12): the disciples ask, "who sinned, this man or his parents?" Jesus: "This man didn't sin, nor did his parents" (9:2–3).
  • Jesus says, "I am the light of the world" (9:5), makes mud, and sends him to wash in Siloam ("Sent"). He comes back seeing.
  • The trial that follows: the man moves from "the man called Jesus" to "Lord, I believe!" (9:38); the authorities move into spiritual blindness (9:39–41).
  • Lazarus (11:1–44): dead four days (11:39). "I am the resurrection and the life." (11:25) Martha: "you are the Christ, God's Son" (11:27). "Jesus wept." (11:35) "Lazarus, come out!" (11:43)
  • Meaning: Jesus gives life now and after death; the sign anticipates his own resurrection.
  • Irony: the greatest sign leads the council to plan his death (11:47–53).

Show answer

Q2 How does 4:48 complicate this?

Show answer

Jesus criticises faith that depends on wonders; true faith trusts his word, as the official then does, 4:50.

  • John also shows faith based on signs being shallow: some follow for the bread (6:26), and many leave (6:66).
Mission 4Diversity

Dodd and Brown on the signs

  • C. H. Dodd, The Interpretation of the Fourth Gospel (1953), paraphrase:
  • Chapters 2–12 form seven episodes; in each, a sign and a discourse interpret each other.
  • The signs are like the prophets' symbolic actions: events in time that show eternal realities (life, light, bread from heaven).
  • In Historical Tradition in the Fourth Gospel (1963) he argued that John drew on early tradition independent of the Synoptics.
  • R. E. Brown, The Gospel according to John (1966–70), paraphrase:
  • Signs belong to the Old Testament pattern of Moses' signs in the Exodus; they reveal Jesus' glory and point to his hour (the cross and resurrection).
  • Some signs have a secondary sacramental meaning (wine and bread: the Eucharist; washing in Siloam: baptism).
  • Other scholars (Bultmann, 1941; R. T. Fortna, The Gospel of Signs, 1970) proposed a written signs source behind John. Many accept some such source; its exact contents are debated.

Mission 4Evaluate

Must the signs be historical to matter?

  • Claim: The meaning of John's signs does not depend on whether they happened as described.
  • For: John says they were written "that you may believe" (20:31): their purpose is theological.
  • For: Dodd stresses the eternal reality the sign symbolises; symbols can be true without being literal.
  • For: John shapes the material (seven signs, discourses, irony).
  • Against: John insists on witnesses and real places (the pool of Bethesda "having five porches", 5:2; a site matching this has been excavated in Jerusalem).
  • Against: The point of the Prologue is that "the Word became flesh": the Gospel's claim is that God acted in history. A sign that never happened points to nothing.
  • Against: Dodd himself argued John preserves early, independent tradition.
  • Judgement criteria: What did John intend? What does faith need? What can historians test? Does "symbolic" mean "not historical"?
Mission 4

The signs and ways of living

  • Faith: believe without needing proof: "Blessed are those who have not seen and have believed." (20:29)
  • Healing and care: Christians have founded hospitals and hospices, seeing healing as part of God's work.
  • Disability and sin: Jesus rejects the idea that the blind man's condition was a punishment (9:3). Churches use this to challenge stigma; the principle is accepted well beyond them.
  • Sabbath and law: the pool and the blind man show people put before rules; Christians debate how far Sunday rest still binds.
  • Marriage: Cana is read at many weddings; the Book of Common Prayer says Christ "adorned and beautified" marriage by his first miracle there.
  • Death: "I am the resurrection and the life" (11:25) opens many Christian funeral services; it shapes Christian hope and care for the dying.
  • A literal reader sees God's power still at work; a symbolic reader looks for spiritual sight, bread and life.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Messiah (anointed), Son of God (in a chosen relationship with God) and Son of Man (a human being; Daniel's figure of glory) all had Old Testament meanings before Jesus.
  • Mark reveals Jesus as Son of God at the cross; Matthew stresses fulfilment; Luke links the titles to salvation for all.
  • Son of Man sayings fall into earthly, suffering and future groups; scholars (Vermes, Bultmann, Hooker, Dodd, Brown) disagree about what Jesus meant by them.

Mission 2

  • Four picture sayings: bread of life (manna, Wisdom), light of the world (Booths, creation, the Servant), good shepherd (Psalm 23, Ezekiel 34), true vine (Israel as vine).
  • The absolute "I AM" (8:58) echoes the divine name of Exodus 3:14 and Isaiah 43:10.
  • Dodd stresses the "real" behind the symbols and Hellenistic readers; Brown stresses the Jewish background and Jesus' role for believers.

Mission 3

  • John 1:1–18 introduces the Word (Logos): with God, God, creator, life and light, become flesh, full of grace and truth.
  • Jewish background: Genesis 1, the word of the LORD, Wisdom, Torah, the tabernacle and glory, Sinai. Hellenistic background: Heraclitus, the Stoics, Philo.
  • Dodd: the Logos speaks to Hellenistic readers and sums up Jesus' life; Hooker: the Prologue is the key for readers, rooted in Moses and Sinai.

Mission 4

  • John calls Jesus' miracles signs: acts that point to his identity and glory and call for faith (20:30–31).
  • The seven signs: Cana wine, the official's son, the pool, the feeding, walking on water, the man born blind, Lazarus.
  • Dodd: signs and discourses interpret each other and show eternal realities; Brown: signs reveal glory, echo Moses, point to the hour, sometimes with sacramental meaning.
