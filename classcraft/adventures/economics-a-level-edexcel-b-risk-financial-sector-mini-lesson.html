Mission 1

What financial markets do

  • Financial markets link savers (who have spare money) with borrowers (who need it).
  • Their roles:
    • Help people save: a safe place and a return.
    • Lend to households and firms: mortgages, business loans.
    • Make exchange easy: payments for goods and services.
    • Provide forward markets: fix a price today for a deal in the future.
    • Provide a market for equities (shares), so firms can raise money.
  • Benefit to the real economy: savings flow to investment, which raises productive capacity and growth.
Mission 1

Money, capital and foreign exchange markets

  • Money market: short-term lending and borrowing (under a year), e.g. bills between banks and firms.
  • Capital market: long-term finance through shares and bonds.
    • Primary market: new shares or bonds are sold, raising money for the issuer.
    • Secondary market: existing ones are traded between investors (e.g. the stock exchange).
  • Foreign exchange (forex) market: currencies are bought and sold.
  • Forward market example (illustrative): an exporter expects to receive 100,000 euros in 3 months. It agrees a fixed exchange rate today, so a fall in the euro cannot hurt it.
Mission 1

Debt versus equity

  • Debt: borrowed money that must be repaid with interest.
    • Loans from banks.
    • Bonds: a borrower (government or firm) sells an IOU that pays fixed yearly interest and repays the face value at maturity.
  • Equity: shares give part-ownership of a firm.
    • Shareholders may get dividends if the firm makes a profit; nothing is guaranteed.
    • If a firm fails, lenders are paid before shareholders.
  • So: shares usually carry more risk but a higher expected return.
  • Say: "a firm raises equity finance by selling shares" — not: "a firm borrows by selling shares".

Mission 1

Liquidity, return and risk

  • Every asset can be judged on three things:
    • Liquidity: how easily it turns into spending power.
    • Rate of return: what it earns (interest, dividends, price gains).
    • Risk: how far that return could vary, or be lost.
  • Cash and current account deposits: most liquid, little or no return, low risk.
  • Bonds: less liquid, fixed interest, prices can change.
  • Shares: higher expected return, more risk.
  • Opportunity cost of holding money = the interest forgone by not holding bonds or savings instead.
    • When interest rates rise, holding money costs more, so people hold less of it.
Mission 1

How bonds work

  • A bond has:
    • Face value: what is repaid at the end, e.g. 1,000.
    • Coupon: the fixed yearly interest, e.g. 5% of 1,000 = 50 a year.
    • Maturity: the date it is repaid, e.g. in 10 years.
  • After it is sold, a bond can be traded. Its market price can change; its coupon cannot.
  • Suppose new bonds now pay more interest.
    • The old bond still pays only 50 a year.
    • No one will pay 1,000 for it when a new 1,000 bond pays more.
    • Its price must fall until it offers a similar return.
  • Bond prices and market interest rates move in opposite directions.

Mission 2

Risk or uncertainty?

  • Risk: the chance of a bad outcome can be measured from past data, e.g. how often warehouses catch fire, or delivery vans crash.
  • Uncertainty: the chance cannot be measured, because the event is new or unique, e.g. a new rival technology, a pandemic, a change of government.
  • Distinction from Frank Knight, Risk, Uncertainty and Profit (1921).
  • Risk can often be insured or hedged; uncertainty must be managed by judgement, spare cash and flexibility.
  • Say: "measurable risk" vs "unmeasurable uncertainty" — not: "risk is small, uncertainty is big".
Mission 2

Shocks

  • Shock: a sudden, unexpected event that hits demand or costs.
  • Demand shock: e.g. 2020, pandemic lockdowns closed shops, cafés and travel overnight.
  • Supply (cost) shock: e.g. 2022, energy prices surged after Russia's invasion of Ukraine, raising firms' costs.
  • Financial shock: e.g. 2008, banks cut lending in the global financial crisis.
  • Shocks are hard to predict, so they are mostly uncertainty, not measurable risk.
  • Firms with spare cash, flexible suppliers and sales in many markets cope better.

Mission 2

Insurance

  • A firm pays a premium to an insurer; the insurer pays out if a covered loss happens.
  • Insurers pool many firms' premiums: only a few will claim, so the pool covers them.
  • Insurable risks: fire, theft, flood, injury to staff, damage caused to the public (liability).
  • In Great Britain, most employers must by law hold employers' liability insurance (a 1969 Act, in force since 1972).
  • Not insurable: uncertainty, such as falling demand or a rival's new product, because the chance cannot be priced.
  • Insurance turns a large, unpredictable loss into a small, known cost.
Mission 2

Exchange rate risk

  • A UK exporter sells to a US buyer for $1,000,000, paid in 3 months.
  • Today, £1 = $1.25 (illustrative data), so the sale is worth $1,000,000 ÷ 1.25 = £800,000.
  • If the pound appreciates to £1 = $1.40, the dollars buy fewer pounds: £714,286.
  • If the pound depreciates to £1 = $1.10: £909,091.
  • Exchange rate risk: a change in the exchange rate changes the value of future payments or costs.
  • Say: "a stronger pound cuts the sterling value of dollar sales" — not: "a stronger pound is always good for UK firms".
Mission 2

Forward markets

  • Forward contract: an agreement today to exchange a fixed amount of currency (or a commodity) at a fixed price on a set future date.
  • Banks offer forward contracts in currencies; commodity forwards fix the price of oil, wheat or metals.
  • Hedging: using a forward contract to remove the risk of price changes.
  • Worked example (illustrative data): the exporter agrees a 3-month forward rate of £1 = $1.24.
    • In 3 months it receives $1,000,000 ÷ 1.24 = £806,452, whatever the spot rate is then.
  • The forward rate is fixed both ways: if the pound falls to $1.10, the firm still gets £806,452, not £909,091.

Mission 2

What the financial sector does

  • Mobilises savings for lending: banks gather many savers' deposits and lend to firms and households.
  • Lends to businesses: loans to pay for investment.
  • Business loans also cover working capital (paying suppliers and wages before customers pay).
  • Lends to individuals: mortgages, loans, credit cards.
  • Facilitates exchange: payment systems (cards, transfers) let goods and services be bought and sold.
  • Assesses creditor risk: checks whether borrowers can repay, and sets interest rates to match the risk.
  • Provides forward markets in currencies and commodities.
  • Provides a market for equities (shares): firms raise capital by selling shares; investors can sell them on later.
Mission 2

Forwards and shares in action

  • Commodities: an airline can buy jet fuel forward, fixing next year's fuel price before it sets ticket prices.
  • Currencies: a car maker that imports parts can fix their cost in its own currency.
  • Equities: a firm sells new shares on a stock exchange (the primary market); later, investors trade those shares with each other (the secondary market).
  • Share prices also carry information: a falling share price signals investors' doubts about the firm.
Quick check
?A coffee roaster signs a contract today to buy 20 tonnes of coffee beans in 6 months at a fixed price. Which role of financial markets does this show?
Mission 2Evaluate

Should a firm always hedge?

  • It depends on cost: banks charge for forward contracts, and the forward rate may be worse than today's spot rate.
  • It depends on direction: a hedge removes losses but also gains; a firm that hedges misses out if the currency moves its way.
  • It depends on size: a small, one-off sale may not be worth hedging; a big contract on thin margins usually is.
  • It depends on natural hedges: a firm that both sells and buys in dollars is partly protected already.
  • Hedging handles measurable risk in prices; it cannot protect against uncertainty, such as the buyer cancelling the order.
Mission check

?A UK chocolate maker signs a forward contract to buy cocoa in 6 months at a fixed price. When the 6 months are up, the spot price of cocoa has fallen well below the contract price. Which statement is correct?

A forward contract swaps an unknown future price for a known one, whichever way the market then moves.

Mission 3

The bank behind the banks

  • A central bank is the bank at the centre of a country's money system. Examples:
  • Bank of England (founded 1694); US Federal Reserve (1913); European Central Bank (1998), for the countries that use the euro.
  • Its customers are the government and commercial banks, not households or firms.
  • Commercial banks hold accounts there called reserves.
  • When customers of one bank pay customers of another, the two banks settle by moving reserves between their accounts at the central bank.
  • It is usually not run for profit: its aim is a stable economy.
Mission 3

What a central bank does

  • Monetary policy: sets the policy interest rate to keep inflation low and stable.
  • Issues banknotes.
  • Banker to the government: holds its main account and helps manage its borrowing.
  • Banker to the banks: holds their reserves and runs the system they use to pay each other.
  • Lender of last resort: lends to banks that run short of cash.
  • Financial stability: watches for risks to the whole system and, in many countries, supervises banks.
Mission 3

The goal: stable prices

  • Most central banks aim for low, stable inflation, often a numerical target.
  • UK: the government sets an inflation target of 2% (CPI). The Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee (MPC), nine members, sets Bank Rate to hit it.
  • The UK target is symmetric: inflation below 2% is treated as seriously as inflation above it.
  • Stable prices help firms and households plan, borrow and invest.
Mission 3

The tools

  • Policy interest rate: the rate at which the central bank lends to, or pays on reserves of, commercial banks. Other rates tend to follow.
  • UK: Bank Rate (often called the base rate), set by the MPC; it feeds through to loan and savings rates (the transmission mechanism).
  • Open market operations: buying or selling government bonds to change bank reserves and the money supply.
  • Quantitative easing (QE): buying bonds on a large scale with newly created money, to push down long-term rates.
  • The Bank of England sets no minimum reserve ratio for monetary policy.
Quick check

Mission 3

Lender of last resort

  • A bank can be solvent (assets worth more than it owes) but illiquid: it cannot turn its loans into cash fast enough.
  • If depositors panic and withdraw at once (a bank run), even a sound bank can fail.
  • The central bank can lend it cash against good collateral: it is the lender of last resort.
  • Classic rule (Walter Bagehot, 1873): lend freely, against good collateral, at a high rate.
  • Real-world: on 14 September 2007 the Bank of England gave emergency support to Northern Rock. Queues of savers still formed outside its branches.
  • Risk: moral hazard. If banks expect rescue, they may take bigger risks (see Mission 4).
Quick check
?A bank's loans are worth more than it owes, but depositors suddenly withdraw more cash than it holds. What can the central bank do as lender of last resort?
Mission 3

Central banks, banks and the money supply

  • Most money is bank deposits, created when commercial banks lend.
  • The central bank shapes that lending:
  • Policy rate: a higher rate makes loans dearer, so banks lend less and deposits grow more slowly.
  • QE: buying bonds creates reserves for banks and deposits for the sellers, so broad money rises directly.
  • Regulation: rules on capital and liquidity limit how far banks can expand lending.
  • So the central bank influences the money supply and the banking sector, but does not fix the quantity of money directly.
Mission 3

Keeping the system safe

  • Banks fail in ways that can spread to the whole economy. Central banks often help regulate them.
  • UK (since 2013): the Bank of England's Financial Policy Committee (FPC) looks at risks to the whole system.
  • The Prudential Regulation Authority (PRA), part of the Bank of England, supervises individual banks and insurers.
  • The Financial Conduct Authority (FCA), separate from the Bank, regulates how firms treat customers.
  • More on regulation and crises in Mission 4.
Mission 3Evaluate

Should central banks be independent?

  • UK: the Bank of England has set interest rates independently since 1997. The ECB's independence is written into EU treaties.
  • For: rate-setters cannot be pushed to cut rates before an election, so promises of low inflation are more credible. Inflation expectations may stay anchored.
  • Against: unelected officials make decisions that affect jobs, house prices and savers; accountability is weaker.
  • Against: monetary and fiscal policy may pull in opposite directions without coordination.
  • It depends on: how the target is set and reviewed, how open the central bank is, and how far it strays into areas such as QE that change who gains and loses.

Mission 4

What finance does for the real economy

  • The financial sector: banks, insurers, pension funds, markets.
  • It channels savings to firms and households that want to borrow and invest.
  • It runs the payments system: wages, card payments, bills.
  • It spreads and prices risk: insurance, diversified funds.
  • It turns short-term deposits into long-term loans (maturity transformation).
  • UK: financial services were about 9% of economic output in 2023, a large share for a rich economy.
Mission 4

Two ways a bank fails

  • A bank's assets: loans, bonds, reserves. Its liabilities: deposits and other borrowing.
  • Capital (equity) = assets − liabilities: the owners' stake that absorbs losses.
  • Insolvency: loans go bad, assets fall below liabilities, capital is wiped out.
  • Illiquidity: the bank is solvent but cannot turn loans into cash fast enough when depositors withdraw (a run).
  • The two feed each other: rumours of losses start a run; selling assets in a hurry causes losses.
  • Say: "capital is the owners' funding that absorbs losses" — not: "capital is cash the bank keeps in the vault".
Mission 4Worked example

Reading a bank's ratios

Illustrative data, Bank A (billions):

AssetsLiabilities and capital
Cash and reserves5Deposits85
Government bonds15Other borrowing9
Loans80Capital6
Total100Total100
  • Leverage ratio = capital ÷ total assets = 6 ÷ 100 = 6%.
  • Capital ratio = capital ÷ risk-weighted assets. Safe assets count for less; say risk-weighted assets are 50. Ratio = 6 ÷ 50 = 12%.
  • Liquidity: liquid assets ÷ deposits = (5 + 15) ÷ 85 = 23.5%.
  • Stress: 8% of loans go bad. Loss = 0.08 × 80 = 6.4, more than capital of 6. Bank A is insolvent.
  • More capital = a bigger cushion. More liquid assets = more time in a run.

Mission 4

Systemic risk

  • Systemic risk: the risk that trouble at one bank spreads to the whole financial system.
  • Banks lend to each other, so one failure means losses for others (contagion).
  • Fire sales: a bank dumps assets, prices fall, other banks' assets lose value.
  • Panic: depositors run from banks that look similar.
  • Lending shrinks (a credit crunch), so firms cannot borrow; output and jobs fall.
  • These costs fall on people outside the bank: a negative externality, a reason to regulate.
Mission 4

Moral hazard and too big to fail

  • Moral hazard: being protected from the cost of a risk makes people take more risk.
  • If a bank is too big to fail, its owners and lenders expect a rescue.
  • So profits from risky lending stay private, while big losses fall on taxpayers.
  • Lenders charge such banks less, so they can grow even bigger and riskier.
  • Deposit insurance and the lender of last resort (see Mission 3) help to prevent runs, but can add to moral hazard.
  • Say: "moral hazard: behaviour changes after protection is given" — not: "adverse selection", which is hidden information before a deal (see Mission 5).
Quick check
?In a crisis, a government rescues a large bank but lets a large clothing retailer go bust. Which is the strongest economic reason for treating them differently?
Mission 4

The crisis of 2007–08

  • (1) 2000s: US house prices boom. Lenders sell sub-prime mortgages to borrowers with weak credit.
  • (2) Mortgages are bundled into securities and sold worldwide; banks borrow heavily (high leverage) to buy them.
  • (3) From 2006, US house prices fall. Defaults rise; nobody knows which banks hold the losses.
  • (4) August 2007: banks stop lending to each other. September 2007: run on Northern Rock (UK). 15 September 2008: Lehman Brothers goes bankrupt.
  • (5) Governments rescue banks: in 2008–09 the UK government took large stakes in RBS and Lloyds Banking Group.
  • An asset bubble in housing, fed by cheap credit, burst.
Mission 4

From bank losses to the real economy

  • Banks with less capital cut lending to rebuild their ratios: the lending collapse.
  • Firms cancel investment; households cut spending; AD falls.
  • UK real GDP fell by just over 6% from early 2008 to mid-2009: at the time, the deepest UK recession since the Second World War.
  • Financial culture: bonuses rewarded short-term profits from risk, not long-term safety.
Mission 4

Who regulates UK finance

  • Before 2013 the FSA, the Bank of England and the Treasury shared the job; the gaps were blamed for missing the risks.
  • Since April 2013:
  • Financial Policy Committee (FPC), at the Bank of England: watches risks to the whole system (macroprudential), e.g. sets extra capital buffers when credit grows fast.
  • Prudential Regulation Authority (PRA), part of the Bank of England: supervises the safety of individual banks and insurers (microprudential).
  • Financial Conduct Authority (FCA), separate: regulates how firms treat customers and keeps markets honest.
  • The Bank of England is still lender of last resort: it lends to solvent banks short of cash (see Mission 3).
Mission 4

The rules after the crisis

  • More capital (Basel III): banks must hold core capital of at least 4.5% of risk-weighted assets, plus a 2.5% buffer, and more for the biggest banks.
  • Leverage ratio: capital of at least 3% of total assets, with no risk weights (UK: 3.25% for large banks).
  • Liquidity coverage ratio: enough liquid assets to survive 30 days of stressed outflows.
  • UK ring-fencing (from 2019): big banks separate everyday deposits from riskier investment banking.
  • Stress tests: regulators test whether banks could survive a severe recession.
  • Bail-in: losses fall on a failing bank's owners and lenders first, not taxpayers.
Quick check
?The Bank of England notices that mortgage lending across the whole UK is rising very fast and house prices look stretched. Which body's job is it to respond to this risk to the whole system?
Mission 4

Why regulating finance is hard

  • Global finance: banks and money move across borders; a crisis abroad can hit home banks.
  • Regulatory arbitrage: activity moves to where rules are lighter, or to shadow banks (funds and lenders outside bank rules).
  • Information: regulators know less than banks; risks look small until they are not.
  • Regulatory capture: regulators may come to see things the industry's way.
Mission 4Evaluate

Do tighter rules make us safer?

  • For: more capital and liquidity mean banks absorb losses and survive runs; fewer rescues; less moral hazard.
  • Against: capital costs banks more than deposits, so loans may be dearer and scarcer, slowing investment and growth.
  • Against: risk can move to shadow banks, where it is harder to see.
  • It depends on: how high the requirements are; whether rules are international; whether regulators can spot risks in time; and whether a failing bank can really be wound down without a rescue.
Mission check

?A country's government announces that it will cover every loss of its three largest banks, with no new rules on how much capital they hold. What does economic theory predict?

Protection from losses changes behaviour. Because bank failures spread to the whole economy, governments protect banks, and then regulate them to limit the extra risk this invites.

Mission 5

Finance and the real economy

  • The real economy: the production of goods and services, jobs and incomes.
  • The financial sector serves it: it moves savings to borrowers, runs payments, and shares out risk.
  • It works well when prices of assets and loans reflect real risks and returns.
  • When finance misprices risk, money flows to the wrong uses, and a crash can cut lending, spending and jobs.
  • So failures in financial markets matter far beyond the banks.
Mission 5

Asymmetric information

  • Asymmetric information: one side of a deal knows more than the other.
  • Borrowers know more about their ability to repay than lenders.
  • Sellers of financial products know more than buyers: complex terms, hidden fees.
  • Adverse selection (before the deal): high interest rates attract the riskiest borrowers.
  • Moral hazard (after the deal): someone protected from risk takes more of it (see Mission 4).
  • UK example: payment protection insurance (PPI) was mis-sold with loans for years; banks paid about 38 billion pounds in compensation by 2020.
  • 2000s US: lenders who sold mortgages on to investors had little reason to check borrowers; buyers of the bundles could not see the risk.
Quick check
?A lender sells each mortgage it makes to investors within weeks, earning a fee every time. The investors cannot check each borrower. What market failure does this create?
Mission 5

Externalities: risk that others pay for

  • When a bank takes risks, it weighs its own costs and gains (MPC, MPB).
  • A crash also hurts third parties: firms that lose credit, workers who lose jobs, taxpayers who fund rescues.
  • So the marginal social cost of risky lending is above the private cost: MSC > MPC.
  • The market lends at Q1 (MPB = MPC). The social optimum is Q* (MSB = MSC).
  • Too much risky lending: the shaded triangle is the welfare loss.
Mission 5

Speculation and bubbles

  • Speculation: buying an asset to sell it later at a higher price, not to use it.
  • A bubble: an asset's price rises far above the value its future income can justify.
  • Rising prices draw in buyers who expect more rises; demand shifts right; prices rise again.
  • When expectations turn, everyone tries to sell; the price crashes.
  • Real-world: the US technology share index (the Nasdaq Composite) peaked in March 2000, then fell by about 78% by October 2002.
  • 2000s: a US housing bubble, fed by easy credit, burst from 2006 and set off the 2007–08 crisis (see Mission 4).
  • Say: "a rise in expected future prices shifts demand to the right" — not: "higher prices increase demand".

Mission 5

Herding and animal spirits

  • Herding: people copy what others are doing, assuming the crowd knows something.
  • For a fund manager, being wrong with everyone else is safer for their career than being wrong alone.
  • Animal spirits (Keynes, 1936): waves of optimism and fear drive decisions, not careful calculation.
  • Long booms make people forget risk: borrowing and debt build up while things look safe.
  • Financial culture: bonuses paid on short-term profits reward taking risks now, with losses landing later.
Mission 5

Market rigging

  • Market rigging: manipulating prices or benchmarks for profit; it breaks the price signal.
  • LIBOR: an interest-rate benchmark used to price loans and contracts worldwide. Banks set it from their own estimates.
  • Traders asked colleagues to submit false rates. In 2012 Barclays was fined about 290 million pounds by UK and US regulators.
  • Foreign exchange: in 2014 the FCA fined five banks about 1.1 billion pounds for attempts to rig currency rates.
  • Why it matters: borrowers and savers pay or receive the wrong price; trust in markets falls.
  • Culture: rigging spread through chat rooms between traders at rival banks.
Quick check
?Several traders at rival banks agree to nudge a benchmark interest rate in a direction that profits their trades. Why is this a market failure, not just a crime?
Mission 5

When finance fails, the real economy pays

  • Credit: banks with losses cut lending; firms cancel investment.
  • Debt: households with big debts cut spending when house prices fall.
  • Wealth effect: falling asset prices make people feel poorer, so consumption falls.
  • Confidence: fear spreads, and firms and households wait.
  • AD falls, output and jobs fall: a financial crash becomes a recession.
Mission 5Evaluate

Can regulators stop bubbles?

  • For acting: lean against booms with macroprudential tools. UK example: from 2014 the FPC limited the share of new mortgages at 4.5 times income or more to 15%.
  • For acting: better information rules and fines reduce mis-selling and rigging.
  • Against: bubbles are hard to spot in time; rising prices may reflect real changes.
  • Against: rules can push risk to less regulated lenders; and popping a bubble can cause the crash it aims to avoid.
  • It depends on: how quickly a bubble can be recognised; how much of it is funded by borrowing; and whether rules cover all lenders, not just banks.
Mission check

?Illustrative data: the price of a rare collectible digital token rises by 300% in a year. Nothing about how it can be used has changed. Most buyers say they bought it because they expect to sell it at a higher price. Which explanation and risk fit best?

When buyers buy because they expect prices to rise, expectations can drive a market far from real value, and then into a crash.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Financial markets link savers and borrowers; money markets are short-term, capital markets long-term.
  • Debt (loans, bonds) must be repaid with interest; equity (shares) gives ownership and uncertain dividends.
  • Bond prices and market interest rates move in opposite directions.

Mission 2

  • Risk can be measured and often insured; uncertainty cannot. Shocks are mostly uncertainty.
  • Forward contracts fix a future exchange rate or commodity price today, removing price risk in both directions.
  • Financial markets channel savings to lending, enable payments, provide forward markets and a market for equities.

Mission 3

  • A central bank is banker to the government and to the banks; it issues notes and runs monetary policy.
  • Its main goal is low, stable inflation; its tools include the policy rate, open market operations and QE.
  • It is lender of last resort, helps keep the financial system safe, and is often independent of government.

Mission 4

  • Banks fail through insolvency (losses wipe out capital) or illiquidity (a run); capital and liquidity ratios measure the cushions.
  • Bank failures spread (systemic risk), and expected rescues encourage risk (moral hazard, too big to fail): two reasons to regulate.
  • After 2007–08: FPC, PRA and FCA, Basel III capital and liquidity rules, ring-fencing and stress tests; regulation has costs and limits.

Mission 5

  • Financial markets fail through asymmetric information, externalities (crashes hurt third parties), moral hazard, and speculation and bubbles.
  • Expectations, herding and animal spirits can shift demand for assets, so prices overshoot and then crash.
  • Market rigging breaks the price signals others rely on.
  • Failures in finance spread to the real economy through credit, debt, wealth and confidence.
