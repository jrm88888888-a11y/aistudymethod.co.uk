Mission 1

Mission 1: A reaction leaves a cloudy mix of product, leftovers and water. How do chemists get the product out pure?

Make a guess: A reaction leaves a cloudy mix of product, leftovers and water. How do chemists get the product out pure?

Mission 1

Heating under reflux

  • Use it for slow reactions with volatile liquids: heat for a long time, lose nothing.
  • Quickfit glassware: ground-glass joints fit together without bungs.
  • Round-bottomed flask + anti-bumping granules (smooth boiling).
  • Condenser vertical: vapour condenses and drips back into the flask.
  • Water in at the bottom, out at the top: the jacket stays full.
  • Top left open. Never heat a sealed apparatus.
  • Heat with an electric heater, water bath or sand bath: organic liquids are flammable.
Mission 1

Distillation

  • Separates liquids by boiling point.
  • Flask + still head + thermometer bulb level with the side arm: it reads the vapour that is distilling.
  • Condenser sloping down, water in at the lower end.
  • Collect the fraction that distils within a degree or two of the product's boiling point.
  • Cool the receiver in ice for a very volatile product.
Mission 1

Reflux or distil? Oxidising ethanol

  • Ethanol + acidified potassium dichromate(VI). Boiling points: ethanal 20 °C, ethanol 78 °C, ethanoic acid 118 °C.
  • Want the aldehyde? Add the oxidant slowly to warm ethanol and distil off ethanal as it forms.
  • It leaves before it can be oxidised further.
  • Want the carboxylic acid? Heat under reflux with excess oxidant. Ethanal can't escape, so it is oxidised to the acid.
  • Then distil to collect the acid.
Quick check
?A student wants to make propanal from propan-1-ol using acidified potassium dichromate(VI). Which method gives the most propanal?
Mission 1

Separating funnel and washing

  • Two liquids that don't mix form layers. The denser layer is at the bottom.
  • Not sure which is which? Add a little water and see which layer grows.
  • Washing: shake the crude product with an aqueous solution, then separate.
  • Water removes water-soluble impurities. Sodium hydrogencarbonate solution removes acid.
  • Stopper, invert, open the tap often to release pressure (NaHCO3 + acid gives CO2).
  • Run off the lower layer through the tap. Keep the layer that holds your product.
Mission 1

Drying and redistillation

  • The washed organic layer is cloudy: it holds tiny drops of water.
  • Add an anhydrous salt: MgSO4, CaCl2 or Na2SO4. Swirl.
  • Keep adding until the salt no longer clumps and the liquid is clear.
  • Decant or filter off the drying agent.
  • Redistil, collecting only near the product's boiling point.
Mission 1

Worked example: 2-chloro-2-methylpropane

  • Shake 2-methylpropan-2-ol with concentrated HCl in a separating funnel (fast at room temperature: no reflux).
  • Two layers form. The product (density 0.84 g cm⁻³) is the upper layer. Discard the lower aqueous layer.
  • Wash with NaHCO3(aq), venting often, to remove acid. Then wash with water.
  • Dry with anhydrous CaCl2 or Na2SO4 until clear.
  • Distil, collecting at about 50–52 °C (bp 51 °C).
Quick check
?A student shakes a crude organic product with sodium hydrogencarbonate solution in a separating funnel. Why must the tap be opened often?
Mission 1

Purifying a solid: recrystallisation

  • Choose a solvent the product dissolves in well when hot, poorly when cold.
  • Dissolve the crude solid in the minimum volume of hot solvent.
  • Filter while hot to remove insoluble impurities.
  • Cool slowly: the product crystallises; soluble impurities, present in small amounts, stay in solution.
  • Filter under reduced pressure (Büchner funnel), wash with a little cold solvent, dry.
Mission 1

Melting point: is it pure?

  • Melting point apparatus: a little solid in a capillary tube, heated slowly near the melting point.
  • Pure: melts sharply (within about 1 °C), at the data-book value. Aspirin: 136 °C.
  • Impure: melts lower and over a wider range.
  • Identify a carbonyl: make its 2,4-DNP derivative (orange crystals), recrystallise, measure its melting point, match it to a table.
Quick check
?Pure aspirin melts at 136 °C. A student's recrystallised aspirin melts over the range 126–131 °C. What does this show?
Mission check

?A student makes 1-bromobutane (bp 102 °C, density 1.27 g cm⁻³) from butan-1-ol (bp 118 °C). The crude product contains water, acid and some unreacted alcohol. Which purification is best?

Each step uses a different property: density (separate), reactivity (wash), water uptake (dry), boiling point (distil). Then a melting or boiling point proves purity.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why does making a polyester give off water, when making some other plastics loses no atoms at all?

Make a guess: Why does making a polyester give off water, when making some other plastics loses no atoms at all?

Mission 2

Two ways to build a polymer

  • Addition polymerisation: monomers with a C=C join up. The polymer is the only product: no atoms lost.
  • Condensation polymerisation: each monomer has two functional groups. Two groups react to make a link.
  • Every link releases a small molecule: usually H2O.
  • So a condensation polymer has fewer atoms than the monomers it came from.
Mission 2

Making a polyester

  • Diol + dicarboxylic acid → polyester + water.
  • –OH reacts with –COOH: an ester link, –COO–, plus H2O.
  • Each monomer has a group at both ends, so the chain grows in both directions.
  • Example: ethane-1,2-diol + benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid → PET.
Mission 2

Drawing the repeat unit

  • Draw the two monomers end to end.
  • Remove H from each –OH and OH from each –COOH.
  • Join the fragments with ester links. Add open bonds at both ends, brackets and n.
  • The repeat unit holds one residue of each monomer: two links, two H2O.
  • PET: –[–OCH2CH2OOC–C6H4–CO–]n–
Quick check
?Ethane-1,2-diol, HOCH2CH2OH, and hexanedioic acid, HOOC(CH2)4COOH, react to form a polyester. Which option gives the repeat unit and describes it correctly?
Mission 2

Polyamides

  • Diamine + dicarboxylic acid → polyamide + water.
  • –NH2 reacts with –COOH: an amide link, –CONH–, plus H2O.
  • Nylon 6,6: hexane-1,6-diamine + hexanedioic acid → –[–NH(CH2)6NHCO(CH2)4CO–]n–
Mission 2

Amino acids make polyamides too

  • An amino acid has –NH2 and –COOH on the same molecule.
  • So one kind of monomer is enough: –NH2 of one reacts with –COOH of the next.
  • The link is an amide link. In proteins it is called a peptide link.
  • Repeat unit for one amino acid, H2NCH(R)COOH: –[–NHCH(R)CO–]n–
  • Chains of many amino acids are polypeptides; proteins are polypeptides.
Quick check
?Hexane-1,6-diamine, H2N(CH2)6NH2, and decanedioic acid, HOOC(CH2)8COOH, react to form the polyamide nylon 6,10. Which option gives the repeat unit and describes it correctly?
Mission 2

Working backwards: chain → monomers

  • Find the links in the chain: –COO– (ester) or –CONH– (amide).
  • Break each link at the C–O or C–N single bond.
  • Add OH back to the C=O carbon; add H back to the O or N.
  • Diacid + diol, or diacid + diamine, or one amino acid: read off the monomers.
  • Which type? Main chain all carbon, no links → addition. Ester or amide links in the chain → condensation.
Mission check

?Lactic acid, CH3CH(OH)COOH, is made from plant sugars. Its molecules join into poly(lactic acid), PLA, used for compostable cups. Which statement describes how PLA forms?

Same reason as for PET. Two groups react to make each link, and the leftover H and OH leave as water.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why do chemists plan the route to a new molecule by starting from the finished product?

Make a guess: Why do chemists plan the route to a new molecule by starting from the finished product?

Mission 3

Work backwards

  • Start from the target. Name its functional groups and count its carbons.
  • Ask: which one known reaction makes this group? That gives the compound one step back.
  • Repeat until you reach the starting material you were given.
  • Carbon count goes up? You need a C–C bond-forming step.
  • Then write the route forwards, with reagents and conditions for every step.
Mission 3

Your reaction map

  • Alkene → halogenoalkane (HBr) · → alcohol (steam, acid catalyst).
  • Halogenoalkane → alcohol (NaOH(aq)) · → amine (NH3) · → nitrile (KCN, +1 C) · → alkene (KOH in ethanol).
  • Primary alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid (oxidation). Secondary alcohol → ketone. Acid + alcohol → ester.
  • Aldehyde or ketone → hydroxynitrile (cyanide, +1 C) · → alcohol (reduction).
  • Nitrile → amine (reduction).
  • Nitrile → carboxylic acid (hydrolysis).
Mission 3

Worked example: three steps

  • Target: propylamine, CH3CH2CH2NH2 (3 C). Start: ethene (2 C).
  • Back 1: an amine from a nitrile by reduction → propanenitrile, CH3CH2CN.
  • Back 2: a nitrile from a halogenoalkane, adding 1 C → bromoethane, CH3CH2Br.
  • Back 3: bromoethane from ethene + HBr. Reached the start.
  • Forwards: (1) HBr, room temperature. (2) KCN in ethanol, reflux. (3) Reduce the nitrile.
  • Step 3 reagent: LiAlH4 in dry ether.
Quick check
?Which compound reacts with KCN in ethanol to give butanenitrile, CH3CH2CH2CN?
Mission 3

Two-step synthesis in the lab

  • Step 1: make the intermediate (often heating under reflux). Isolate it, e.g. by filtration.
  • Step 2: react the intermediate to make the product.
  • Recrystallise the solid: dissolve in the minimum of hot solvent, filter hot if needed, cool, filter under reduced pressure, wash with cold solvent, dry.
  • Melting temperature: a pure solid melts sharply, at the data-book value.
  • An impure solid melts lower and over a range of temperatures.
Quick check
?The data-book melting temperature of benzoic acid is 122 °C. A student's recrystallised sample melts between 115 °C and 119 °C. What should the student conclude?
Mission 3

Nitriles in routes

  • Adding a carbon: bromoethane → (KCN) propanenitrile → (hydrolyse) propanoic acid, or → (LiAlH4) propylamine.
  • Keeping the carbons: ethanoic acid → (PCl5) ethanoyl chloride → (NH3) ethanamide → (P4O10, heat) ethanenitrile → (LiAlH4) ethylamine.
  • Count carbons at every step: they only change at a C–C step.
Quick check
?Ethanamide, CH3CONH2, is heated with phosphorus(V) oxide. The organic product is then reduced with LiAlH4. What is the final product?
Mission check

?A chemist needs 2-hydroxybutanenitrile, CH3CH2CH(OH)CN, and has propan-1-ol, CH3CH2CH2OH. Which two-step plan works?

Start from the target, find the one reaction that makes it, then step back again. The route writes itself.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Propanal and propanone share the formula C3H6O. Which spectrum tells the two bottles apart at a glance?

Make a guess: Propanal and propanone share the formula C3H6O. Which spectrum tells the two bottles apart at a glance?

Mission 4

A strategy

  • 1. Molecular formula: % composition → empirical formula; Mᵣ from the M⁺ peak.
  • 2. IR: which bonds are present (O–H? C=O? both? neither?).
  • 3. MS fragments: mass lost → pieces such as CH3 (15), C2H5 (29), OH (17).
  • 4. ¹H NMR: signals = environments; areas = H ratio; splitting = neighbouring H; shift = what is nearby.
  • 5. ¹³C NMR: signals = carbon environments; shift = type of carbon (e.g. aldehyde/ketone C=O near 200; ester/acid C=O near 170).
  • 6. Chemical tests: a quick reaction can confirm a functional group.
  • Finally: propose a structure, then check it against every piece of data.
Mission 4

Worked example: compound W (step 1, the formula)

  • W contains C 54.5%, H 9.1%, O 36.4% by mass.
  • Divide by Ar: C 54.5 ÷ 12.0 = 4.54 · H 9.1 ÷ 1.0 = 9.1 · O 36.4 ÷ 16.0 = 2.28.
  • Divide by the smallest (2.28): C 2.0 : H 4.0 : O 1.0.
  • Empirical formula C2H4O (empirical formula mass 44.0).
  • MS: M⁺ at m/z 44, so the molecular formula is also C2H4O.
Mission 4

Worked example: W (step 2, MS and IR)

  • MS: a peak at m/z 29: mass lost 44 − 29 = 15, a CH3.
  • IR: strong band at 1730 cm⁻¹: C=O.
  • IR: no broad band at 3200–3600 cm⁻¹: no O–H.
  • So far: a C=O and a CH3, with only two carbons.
  • Exact IR ranges: use the data sheet you get in your exam.
Mission 4

Worked example: W (step 3, NMR and confirming)

  • ¹H NMR: δ 9.8, 1H, quartet: an aldehyde H (–CHO), next to a CH3.
  • δ 2.2, 3H, doublet: CH3 next to C=O, next to one H.
  • ¹³C NMR: two signals, near δ 200 (C=O) and δ 31 (CH3).
  • W is ethanal, CH3CHO.
  • Confirm: Mᵣ 44 ✓ · C=O, no O–H ✓ · CHO⁺ at 29 ✓ · 1H quartet, 3H doublet ✓.
  • ¹³C: two carbon environments ✓.
Quick check
?Compound Z, C3H8O, gives these data. IR: a strong, broad absorption centred near 3350 cm⁻¹. MS: a small M⁺ peak at m/z 60 and the base peak at m/z 45. Low-resolution ¹H NMR: three signals, areas 6 : 1 : 1. Which identification is consistent with all the data?
Quick check
?Back to the two bottles. Propanal, CH3CH2CHO, and propanone, CH3COCH3, are both C3H6O. Which technique tells them apart most directly, and how?
Mission 4

Propanal vs propanone: every technique

  • M⁺: both at m/z 58. No help.
  • IR: both have a strong C=O band, no O–H. No help.
  • MS fragments: propanone's base peak is m/z 43 (CH3CO⁺); propanal gives a strong peak at 29 (C2H5⁺ or CHO⁺). A clue, not a glance.
  • ¹H NMR: propanone 1 signal (singlet); propanal 3 signals, including the aldehyde H near δ 9–10. The glance answer.
  • ¹³C NMR: propanone 2 signals; propanal 3. Also decisive.
  • Tollens' reagent: silver mirror with propanal (an aldehyde); no change with propanone.
  • Rule: pick the technique that "sees" the difference: here, the environments.
Quick check
?Compound X, C4H8O2 (Mᵣ = 88), gives these data. IR: sharp absorptions at 2850–3000 cm⁻¹, a strong absorption at 1740 cm⁻¹ and no broad absorption (ranges: C–H 2850–3090 cm⁻¹; C=O 1700–1750 cm⁻¹; O–H in carboxylic acids 2500–3000 cm⁻¹, very broad). MS: M⁺ at m/z 88 and a peak at m/z 29 (CHO⁺ or C2H5⁺). ¹H NMR: δ 1.3 (triplet, 3H), δ 2.0 (singlet, 3H), δ 4.1 (quartet, 2H); H on a carbon bonded to the singly bonded O of an ester, –C(=O)–O–CH2–, appears at 3.7–4.8 ppm. Which identification is consistent with all the data?
Quick check
?Compound T, C4H8O, gives these data. IR: strong absorption at 1730 cm⁻¹; no broad absorption at 3200–3600 cm⁻¹. ¹³C NMR: three signals, one near δ 205. Low-resolution ¹H NMR: three signals, areas 6 : 1 : 1, one of them near δ 9.6. Which structure fits all the data?
Mission check

?Methyl ethanoate, CH3COOCH3, and ethyl methanoate, HCOOCH2CH3, are both C3H6O2. Which evidence tells them apart most directly?

Same formula, same M⁺, same IR bands: look for the technique that sees the different environments. Then check your structure against every piece of data.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Why do the dyes in one drop of ink travel different distances up the same strip of paper?

Make a guess: Why do the dyes in one drop of ink travel different distances up the same strip of paper?

Mission 5

Two phases

  • Chromatography separates the components of a mixture between two phases.
  • Mobile phase: moves (a liquid solvent, or a gas).
  • Stationary phase: stays put (paper, a coated plate, a packed column).
  • Each component keeps switching: dissolved in the mobile phase ↔ held by the stationary phase.
  • More time in the mobile phase → it moves further (or comes out sooner).
Mission 5

Running a TLC or paper chromatogram

  • Draw the origin line in pencil (ink would itself separate).
  • Spot the mixture and reference samples on the line; let the spots dry.
  • Stand the plate in solvent below the origin (or the spots dissolve away into the solvent).
  • Cover the tank: the air inside stays saturated with solvent vapour.
  • Remove before the solvent reaches the top; mark the solvent front at once.
  • Colourless spots: view under UV light (plates with a fluorescent coating), or use iodine vapour or a locating spray.
  • Safety: solvents are often flammable and harmful: no flames, use a fume cupboard; never look into the UV lamp.
Mission 5

Why some components move less

  • Silica (TLC) and paper both have many polar –OH groups on their surface (paper: cellulose, with water held on it).
  • Polar components are held strongly (dipole–dipole forces, hydrogen bonds): they move less.
  • Less polar components spend more time in a less polar solvent: they move further.
  • So the order of spots tells you the relative polarity of the components.
  • Mass is not the main cause: a small polar molecule can stay behind a large non-polar one.
Quick check
?The dyes in a black ink are separated by paper chromatography. Which difference between the dyes is mainly responsible for the separation?
Mission 5

Rf values

  • Rf = distance moved by the spot ÷ distance moved by the solvent front, both from the origin.
  • Measure to the centre of the spot.
  • Rf has no units and lies between 0 and 1.
  • Example: spot 2.9 cm, solvent front 7.6 cm: Rf = 2.9 ÷ 7.6 = 0.38.
  • Rf depends on the solvent, the plate and the temperature: compare with standards run on the same plate.
Quick check
?On a TLC plate, the solvent front is 6.4 cm above the origin line, which is 1.0 cm above the bottom edge of the plate. The centre of spot S is 4.8 cm above the origin; its top edge is 5.1 cm above the origin. What is the Rf value of S?
Mission 5

Two-way paper chromatography

  • Two components can have almost the same Rf in one solvent and stay as one spot.
  • Spot the mixture near one corner; run in solvent 1; dry.
  • Turn the paper 90°; run in solvent 2.
  • Spots that overlapped now spread across the paper.
  • Each spot has two Rf values, one for each solvent: match both to identify it.
Mission 5

Chromatography in a column

  • Gas chromatography (GC): an unreactive carrier gas (the mobile phase) pushes the vaporised sample through a long, heated column.
  • Stationary phase: a solid, or (usually) a high-boiling liquid coated on a solid support or on the column wall.
  • HPLC: a liquid mobile phase is pumped at high pressure through a column of very fine solid particles.
  • Retention time: time from injecting the sample to the component reaching the detector.
Mission 5

Reading a gas chromatogram

  • Each peak = one component. Same retention time as a standard (same conditions) → likely the same substance.
  • Retention time depends on column temperature, gas flow rate and the stationary phase: keep them the same.
  • Peak area is proportional to the amount of that component.
  • % of a component = its peak area ÷ total peak area × 100 (assuming the detector responds equally to each).
  • Example: areas 1.8, 5.4, 10.8 → total 18.0 → 10%, 30%, 60%.
Quick check
?A gas chromatogram of a mixture of three esters, P, Q and R, gives peak areas P 2.4, Q 3.6 and R 6.0 (arbitrary units). Assume the detector responds equally to each ester. What is the percentage of Q in the mixture?
Mission check

?A student runs TLC of a mixture of two polar compounds on a silica plate, using hexane as the solvent. Both spots barely leave the origin. Which change is most likely to make the spots move further up the plate and separate?

Every component shares its time between the two phases. Change that balance, and you change how far it goes.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Reflux: heat without loss. Distil: separate by boiling point. Separating funnel: by density. Recrystallise: by solubility, hot v cold.
  • Pure solid: sharp melting point at the data value. Impure: lower and broader.
  • Your specified practicals: reflux with 1-bromobutane, ester by distillation, 2,4-DNPH derivatives, liquid and solid syntheses.

Mission 2

  • Addition: C=C monomers, no atoms lost. Condensation: two groups react at each link, losing H2O (or HCl).
  • Polyester: –COO– links from diol + diacid. Polyamide: –CONH– links from diamine + diacid, or from amino acids.

Mission 3

  • Work backwards from the target, one known reaction per step; count carbons to spot a C–C step.

Mission 4

  • Formula from % composition (and M⁺) → bonds from IR → pieces from MS → environments, ratios and neighbours from ¹H NMR → check against everything.
  • ¹³C NMR counts carbon environments; it is simpler to read than ¹H NMR.
  • Isomers: M⁺ and IR often match; NMR usually tells them apart.

Mission 5

  • Components separate because each is attracted differently to the stationary phase and the mobile phase.
  • Rf = spot distance ÷ solvent-front distance; retention time identifies GC peaks; compare with standards under the same conditions.
