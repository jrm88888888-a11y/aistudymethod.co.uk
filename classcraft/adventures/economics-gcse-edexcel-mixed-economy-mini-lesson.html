Mission 1

Mission 1: Who decides what gets made and who gets it: buyers and sellers, the government, or both?

Make a guess: In most countries today, what mainly decides what gets made?

Mission 1

Three questions every economy must answer

  • Resources are scarce, so every society must choose:
  • What to produce? (phones or bus services? how many of each?)
  • How to produce it? (more machines or more workers?)
  • For whom? (who gets the goods and services?)
  • Who answers them? Buyers and sellers in markets, the government, or a mix.
Mission 1

The market economy

  • A market: anywhere buyers and sellers meet to trade, in person or online.
  • In a market economy, resources are privately owned and prices answer the three questions.
  • What: firms make what buyers are willing and able to pay for.
  • How: firms choose the cheapest methods, to make the most profit.
  • For whom: goods go to those who can afford the price.
  • Prices allocate (share out) scarce resources: you will see how in an earlier lesson.
Mission 1

Consumers, producers and government

  • Consumers (households) buy goods and services. Their spending tells firms what to make.
  • Producers make goods and services. They can be:
  • individuals (a self-employed plumber), firms (a supermarket chain), or the government (state schools, public hospitals).
  • Government sets rules (laws, minimum wage), collects taxes, provides some services and buys goods from firms.
  • They interact: consumers pay firms; firms pay wages to workers (who are also consumers); government taxes both and spends on both.

?Which of these is an example of the government acting as a producer?

Mission 1

The mixed economy: private and public sectors

  • Mixed economy: the market (private sector) and the government (public sector) both decide what is made.

Mission 1

Privatisation

  • Privatisation: selling a state-owned firm or industry to the private sector.
  • Examples: British Telecom (UK, 1984); water in England and Wales (1989); the airline Air India, sold to the Tata Group (India, 2022).
Arguments forArguments against
The profit motive pushes owners to cut costs and improve serviceA state monopoly may become a private monopoly that raises prices
Can bring competition: more choice, lower pricesOwners may cut jobs and loss-making services people need
Government receives money from the saleThe state loses future profits from the firm
Less political interference in business decisionsProfit may come before safety or long-term investment
  • Results depend on competition and regulation: e.g. UK regulators cap the prices of privatised water companies.
  • Economists disagree about how well privatisation has worked; evidence differs by industry.

In a market economy, prices answer what, how and for whom. In a mixed economy, the government steps in where the market leaves gaps.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why might a busy factory make more than is best for the people who live around it?

Make a guess: Why might a busy factory make more than is best for the people who live around it?

Mission 2

When markets get it wrong

  • Markets use prices to decide what gets made and how much.
  • Usually this works: if people want more of something, its price rises and firms make more.
  • Market failure: the market does not allocate resources efficiently.
  • This is a misallocation of resources: too much of some things is made, too little of others.
  • The main cause in this mission: externalities. Public goods are in Mission 3.
  • Market failure is a key reason for government intervention.
Mission 2

Private, external, social

  • Private cost: paid by the person or firm making the decision. A factory pays for fuel, wages and machines.
  • External cost: falls on a third party, someone outside the deal, who is not paid for it. Smoke harms the health of people nearby.
  • Social cost = private cost + external cost. The full cost to society.
  • Private benefit: gained by the buyer. You gain from your own education.
  • External benefit: gained by a third party. Your employer and the wider economy gain from your skills.
  • Social benefit = private benefit + external benefit.
  • An externality is the gap between the social and the private cost or benefit.
  • Say: "an external cost falls on a third party" — not: "an external cost is a cost to the firm".
Mission 2

Worked example: adding up the costs

Illustrative data.

  • A factory makes a batch of paint. It pays 30 for materials, energy and wages.
  • Fumes from the batch cause breathing problems nearby, costing 12 in health care and lost work.
  • Private cost = 30. External cost = 12.
  • Social cost = 30 + 12 = 42.
  • The factory decides using 30. Society bears 42.
  • Benefits work the same way. A vaccination gives the person a private benefit of 20 (they don't get ill).
  • It also gives others an external benefit of 15 (fewer people catch the disease from them).
  • Social benefit = 20 + 15 = 35.
Quick check
?A farm uses a chemical spray that drains into a river and harms fishing downstream. For each tonne of crops, the farm pays 200 in costs, and the damage to fishing is 50. (Illustrative data.) What is the social cost of each tonne?
Mission 2

Four kinds of externality

Externalities come from making things (production) and from using them (consumption).

Negative (external cost)Positive (external benefit)
ProductionFactory smoke; noise from a building siteA firm trains workers, who later take their skills to other firms
ConsumptionDriving at rush hour adds to congestion; smoking near othersVaccination protects others too; education raises skills across the economy
  • Ask two questions: Who makes the decision? Who else is affected, and are they harmed or helped?
Quick check
?A student plays loud music in their flat late at night. The neighbours cannot sleep. What kind of externality is this?
Mission 2Graph lab

The smoky factory

Illustrative data. A factory sells each batch of paint for 20: this is what buyers think a batch is worth. Each extra batch costs the factory more to make: 9, 11, 13, 15, 17, 19, 21, 23. Each batch also causes an external cost: smoke damage to people nearby. The factory makes a batch if 20 covers its own cost. Society is better off with a batch only if 20 covers the social cost.

  • Takeaway: the factory ignores the external cost, so it makes more than is best for society. The bigger the external cost, the bigger the gap.
Mission 2

Too much and too little

  • Negative externality: the decision-maker ignores the external cost. The price is too low for the full cost to society.
    • Result: overproduction or overconsumption. Too many resources go into making it.
  • Positive externality: the decision-maker ignores the external benefit.
    • Result: underproduction or underconsumption. Too few resources go into it.
  • The amount that is best for society is called the social optimum.
  • Either way, resources are misallocated: society would be better off with a different mix of goods.
  • The costs of misallocation: damage to health and the environment, wasted resources, and benefits that society misses out on (e.g. a less skilled workforce).
  • Say: "overconsumption of the good compared with the social optimum" — not: "people buy too much because it is cheap".
Quick check
?A flu vaccination costs 40. It gives the person a private benefit worth 30 and other people an external benefit worth 20. (Illustrative data.) Each person decides for themselves. What is the most likely result?
Mission 2

Why governments step in

  • Market failure gives a reason for government intervention: to move output closer to what is best for society.
  • Make the decision-maker face the external cost or benefit:
    • tax goods with external costs; subsidise goods with external benefits
    • regulation and fines: limits on pollution, penalties for breaking them
    • pollution permits: a fixed number of rights to pollute, which firms can buy and sell
    • information: health campaigns and labels
  • Pros: can cut harmful output or raise helpful output. Cons: the size of an external cost is hard to measure, rules cost money to enforce, and firms may pass costs on to consumers.
  • How each tool works, with diagrams where your course needs them.
Mission check

?Flights from an airport cause noise for people who live nearby. Each extra flight costs the airline 50 thousand to run and causes noise damage worth 20 thousand to residents. (Illustrative data.) Which statement is correct?

When a cost lands on a third party, the decision-maker doesn't count it, so the market makes too much.

Mission 3

Mission 3: If everyone gains from a lighthouse, why might no one choose to pay for it?

Make a guess: If everyone gains from a lighthouse, why might no one choose to pay for it?

Mission 3

Two tests for any good

  • Rival: one person's use leaves less for others. A sandwich you eat cannot be eaten by me.
  • Excludable: the seller can stop people who have not paid from using it.
  • Most goods pass both tests: a sandwich, a bus seat, a phone. These are private goods.
  • Some goods fail both tests: national defence, a flood barrier, street lighting. These are public goods.
ExcludableNon-excludable
RivalPrivate good: a sandwich, a bus seat(other goods)
Non-rival(other goods)Public good: national defence, a flood barrier, street lighting
  • Ask the two questions separately.
Mission 3

Public goods: the lighthouse

  • A public good is non-rival and non-excludable.
  • Think of a lighthouse beam:
    • non-rival: one more ship sees the beam at no extra cost, and the light is no dimmer for the others
    • non-excludable: at sea, a ship that has not paid cannot be stopped from seeing it
  • Every ship gains. But a lighthouse is not free to society: it uses resources (land, workers, energy) and must be paid for somehow.
  • Say: "non-excludable: people who don't pay cannot be stopped from benefiting" — not: "it is free".
Quick check
?Which of these is a public good?
Mission 3

The free rider problem

  • Free rider: someone who benefits from a good without paying for it.
  • Non-excludable → a firm cannot make people pay, because they benefit anyway.
  • Each person thinks: "If others pay, I benefit for free. If they don't, my money alone won't be enough."
  • So too few people pay. A firm cannot cover its costs, so it does not supply the good.
  • Result: the good is under-provided, or not provided at all: a missing market.
  • This is a market failure: a good that would make people better off is not made.
Mission 3Graph lab

Will anyone pay for the flood barrier?

Two neighbours, Ana and Ben, live by a river. A barrier would protect both houses: neither can be left out. Illustrative data. Each values the protection at 100. The barrier costs 150. Together they value it at 200, more than it costs. If both pay, they split the cost (75 each). If one pays, that person pays all 150.

  • Takeaway: below a cost of 100, one neighbour may pay alone; between 100 and 200, a barrier worth building is not built.
Mission 3

So who pays?

  • Usually the government provides public goods and pays for them through taxes.
  • Taxes must be paid by law, so nobody can free ride.
  • Examples: national defence, flood defences, street lighting, lighthouses.
  • Real world: UK lighthouses are paid for by light dues, a charge on commercial ships when they arrive at a port.
  • A ship can't be stopped from seeing the light, but it can be stopped from using the port. The dues are set by government, so they work much like a tax.
  • The hard part: with no market price, how does the government know how much people value the good?
Quick check
?A town has 2,000 households. Each values new street lights at 30. The lights cost 40,000 to install. (Illustrative data.) Which statement is correct?
Mission check

?A town plans a fireworks display, fired high above a hill, that can be seen from anywhere in town. It costs 30,000. The 10,000 residents value it at 10 each. (Illustrative data.) A firm offers to put on the display if enough residents buy tickets. Why is the market likely to under-provide it?

When people who don't pay can't be kept out, payment collapses, even for a good worth more than it costs.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Every economy answers what, how and for whom; markets answer with prices, governments with decisions.
  • Private sector: privately owned, aims at profit. Public sector: state-owned, aims at public service.
  • Privatisation sells state firms to private owners; whether it helps depends on competition and regulation.

Mission 2

  • Market failure: the market misallocates resources, making too much of some things and too little of others.
  • Social cost = private cost + external cost; social benefit = private benefit + external benefit.
  • Negative externalities lead to overproduction or overconsumption; positive externalities to underproduction or underconsumption.

Mission 3

  • Public goods are non-rival and non-excludable; private goods are rival and excludable.
  • Non-excludability creates free riders, so the market under-provides or fails to provide: a missing market. Governments usually provide public goods through taxes.
