Mission 1

Mill and the "pig philosophy" objection

  • John Stuart Mill (1806–1873), raised by his father James Mill as a utilitarian; Bentham was a family friend.
  • Works: On Liberty (1859); Utilitarianism (magazine 1861, book 1863); The Subjection of Women (1869).
  • Critics mocked Bentham's theory as "a doctrine worthy only of swine": if all pleasures are equal, a pig's contentment is as good as a human life.
  • Mill keeps the greatest happiness principle, but argues that pleasures differ in quality, not only quantity.
Mission 1

Higher and lower pleasures

  • Higher pleasures: of the mind: intellect, imagination, feelings, moral sentiments (learning, art, friendship, doing good).
  • Lower pleasures: of the body: food, drink, sex, rest.
  • Higher pleasures are worth more in kind: a small amount can outweigh a large amount of lower pleasure.
  • Competent judges: the test is what people who have experienced both prefer.
  • Mill does not say lower pleasures are bad. They matter; they are simply not the best.
Mission 1Read the source

Socrates dissatisfied

Mill, Utilitarianism (1863), chapter 2:

> "It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied."

  • Mill adds (paraphrase): if the fool or the pig thinks otherwise, it is because they know only their own side of the question.

Q1 Why does Mill trust Socrates' judgement over the fool's?

Show answer

Socrates has experienced both kinds of pleasure; the fool only one.

Q2 What does "dissatisfied" admit about higher pleasures?

Show answer

They can bring frustration; their value is in quality, not ease.

Mission 1

The harm principle

On Liberty (1859), chapter 1:

> "…the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilised community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others."

  • Society may not compel you for your own good: it may advise, persuade and warn.
  • Liberty of thought and speech, of tastes and pursuits, and of association.
  • Mill's example (paraphrase): you may stop someone stepping onto an unsafe bridge if there is no time to warn them; if they know the risk, the choice is theirs.
  • Why it is utilitarian: free people develop themselves and discover new truths, which increases happiness in the long run.
Quick check

Mission 1

Rules from experience

  • Objection to Bentham: there is no time to calculate before every act.
  • Mill (paraphrase): humanity has been learning the effects of actions throughout its history. Those lessons are stored in secondary principles (rules such as "do not lie", "keep promises").
  • Sailors do not calculate the stars before every voyage; they take the Nautical Almanac, already calculated (Utilitarianism, chapter 2).
  • So in normal life we follow the rules; the principle of utility settles conflicts between them.
Mission 1

Rule utilitarianism: strong and weak

  • Rule utilitarianism: an act is right if it follows a rule which, if generally followed, produces the greatest happiness.
  • Strong rule utilitarianism: never break a well-founded rule, even when breaking it would bring more happiness this time.
  • Weak rule utilitarianism: follow the rules normally, but break one in an exceptional case where obeying would clearly cause more harm.
  • Example: "do not lie" maximises trust in general. Strong: keep it always. Weak: may lie to save a life.
Quick check
?A doctor's rule is to keep patients' information confidential. Which describes a weak rule utilitarian doctor?
Mission 1

Was Mill a rule utilitarian?

  • Mill never uses the term. "Rule utilitarianism" was named in the 20th century; J. O. Urmson (1953) argued Mill held a version of it.
  • Others read Mill as an act utilitarian who used rules as rules of thumb.
  • Either way, Mill gives rules a far bigger place than Bentham.
  • Eduqas: Mill's utilitarianism as a teleological/deontological hybrid: rules (duties) are followed, but justified by their consequences.
Mission 1Evaluate

Is rule utilitarianism better than act utilitarianism?

  • Claim: Rule utilitarianism is a better basis for moral decisions than act utilitarianism.
  • For: Protects justice and rights (no punishing the innocent for the majority's pleasure); quicker; predictable; promotes trust and the common good.
  • Against: J. J. C. Smart (1956): if a rule should be broken whenever breaking it produces more happiness, rule utilitarianism collapses into act utilitarianism; if not, it is irrational "rule worship".
  • Against (higher pleasures): who decides what is "higher"? The ranking can look elitist and culturally biased.
  • Judgement criteria: Can rules be justified only by consequences? What about exceptional cases? Does it promote justice better?
  • Spec issue: "the extent to which Rule Utilitarianism provides a better basis for making moral decisions than Act Utilitarianism".
Mission check

Mill changed utilitarianism twice: some pleasures are higher, and rules and liberty matter. Critics ask whether he is still a utilitarian at all.

Mission 2

Preference utilitarianism

  • Maximise the satisfaction of preferences (or interests), not pleasure: what people would choose, on reflection.
  • R. M. Hare (1919–2002), Moral Thinking (1981): put yourself in the place of everyone affected.
  • Peter Singer (b. 1946), Animal Liberation (1975), Practical Ethics (1979; 3rd edition 2011): equal consideration of interests.
  • Any being that can suffer has interests, so animals count. Favouring humans simply for being human is speciesism.
  • Respects choices that do not maximise pleasure (e.g. a person who prefers truth to comfort).
Quick check
?Mira, fully informed, refuses a treatment that would make her feel better, because it would cloud her mind and she prefers to stay clear-headed. Which form of utilitarianism most clearly supports respecting her choice?
Mission 2

Applied: animal experimentation for medical research

  • Bentham (act): animals' suffering counts ("Can they suffer?"). Weigh each experiment: the intensity and duration of the animals' pain against the extent and certainty of human benefit. Some experiments pass; many trivial ones fail.
  • Mill (rule): adopt the rules that maximise happiness overall, e.g. experiment only where necessary for serious medical good, with the least suffering possible.
  • UK law follows a similar rule-based approach: the Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act 1986 and the "3Rs" (replace, reduce, refine).
  • Mill's higher pleasures may give human interests more weight; Singer replies that equal suffering deserves equal consideration.
Mission 2

Applied: nuclear weapons as a deterrent

  • Deterrence: possessing nuclear weapons, ready to use them, so that no enemy dares attack.
  • Bentham (act): deterrence may preserve peace for a vast extent and duration; but if it fails, the intensity of suffering is catastrophic. The calculus depends on how certain the deterrent is.
  • Mill (rule): which rule, generally followed, maximises happiness? "Possess, but never use first"? Or "abolish", because the general practice risks disaster?
  • Critics: deterrence needs a real readiness to kill millions of civilians; Pope Francis (2019) called even possession immoral. Supporters: it has helped prevent war between the major powers since 1945.
Quick check
?How would a Mill-style rule utilitarian approach nuclear deterrence?
Mission 2

Can pleasure be measured?

  • Problems: pleasures are subjective; different kinds cannot be put on one scale; consequences are hard to predict; how far ahead should we count?
  • Problems: who counts? Future generations, animals, people far away?
  • Replies: rough estimates are enough for most choices; rules store past experience (Mill's almanac); preferences can be asked about and compared.
  • Practice: modern health systems measure "quality-adjusted life years" to compare treatments: a utilitarian kind of measure.
Quick check
?Which is the strongest form of the objection that utilitarianism can justify injustice?
Mission 2Evaluate

Is utilitarianism a helpful method?

  • Claim: Utilitarianism provides a helpful method of moral decision-making.
  • For: Clear principle; impartial ("everybody to count for one"); takes real consequences seriously; usable by believers and non-believers alike; adaptable (rule, preference).
  • For: Has driven reforms widely seen as progress; extends concern to animals and distant strangers.
  • Against: Can justify injustice to minorities; ignores motives, rights and special duties (to family, promises).
  • Against: Measurement and prediction problems; no intrinsic wrongs: no act is wrong in itself, so any act could be permitted if the consequences are good enough; may encourage immoral acts "for the greater good".
  • Judgement criteria: Is pleasure (or preference) the only intrinsic good? Does it promote justice? Can it work in contemporary society? Is it compatible with religious ethics?
  • Spec issues: pleasure as the sole intrinsic good; whether utilitarianism promotes justice.
Mission 2Worked answer

Evaluating with a scholar

Task: "Utilitarianism cannot protect justice." Evaluate.

  • Line of argument: the claim is strong against act utilitarianism, weaker against rule utilitarianism.
  • Support: Bentham's calculus could approve punishing an innocent person if enough people gain.
  • Counter: Mill's rules (e.g. fair trials) maximise happiness generally; a weak rule utilitarian keeps them in almost all cases.
  • Critique of the counter: Smart: then either rules collapse into act utilitarianism, or they become "rule worship".
  • Judgement: decided by your own criteria (e.g. can rules be justified only by consequences?), stated and defended.
Mission check

?A hospital could secretly test a new drug on one unwilling patient, and the results would save thousands. Which statement about utilitarian responses is accurate?

Utilitarians do not all say yes. But they all answer by asking about consequences, which is exactly what their critics question.

Mission 3

Animals in science

  • Animal experimentation: using live animals in research, mainly mice, rats and fish; also used to test medicines for safety before human trials.
  • Purposes: understanding disease; developing medicines and vaccines; safety testing; producing genetically altered animals for research.
  • UK law: the Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act 1986. Every project needs a Home Office licence, granted only after a harm–benefit analysis.
  • The 3Rs (Russell and Burch, 1959): Replace animals where possible (cell cultures, computer models); Reduce the numbers used; Refine procedures to cause less suffering.
  • Testing cosmetics on animals is banned in the UK (since 1998) and across the EU (sale of newly tested products, 2013).
Mission 3

Bentham's act utilitarianism applied

  • Act utilitarianism: judge each act by its consequences. Bentham's hedonic calculus weighs pleasure and pain.
  • Animals count. Bentham (1789): "The question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?"
  • Apply the calculus to one experiment:
  • Intensity, duration of the animals' pain against the extent (how many patients helped) and certainty of the benefit.
  • Fecundity: will the result lead to further cures? Purity: will it bring side effects or later suffering?
  • Result: some experiments pass (a serious disease, a good chance of success); many fail (trivial aims, uncertain results).
  • Bentham himself approved painful experiments with a definite, useful object and a fair prospect of success, but not cruelty without purpose (paraphrase, 1825 letter).
Mission 3Read the source

Mill on the whole sentient creation

John Stuart Mill (1806–1873), Utilitarianism (1863), ch. 2, defines the standard of morality as:

> "the rules and precepts for human conduct, by the observance of which an existence such as has been described might be, to the greatest extent possible, secured to all mankind; and not to them only, but, so far as the nature of things admits, to the whole sentient creation."

Q1 Who must the rules of morality benefit, according to Mill?

Show answer

All humans and, as far as possible, all sentient beings, including animals.

Q2 Which word shows this is a rule approach?

Show answer

"rules and precepts": morality is the set of rules whose general observance gives the greatest happiness.

Mission 3

Mill's rule utilitarianism applied

  • Rule utilitarianism: follow the rules that, if generally kept, maximise happiness.
  • Likely rules: experiment only for serious medical good; use no animal where an alternative works; keep suffering to the minimum (the 3Rs as rules).
  • Rules give predictability and trust: public confidence in research and in animal care.
  • Higher pleasures: Mill ranks the pleasures of the intellect above bodily pleasures: "better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied" (ch. 2). Some infer that human interests may weigh more.
  • Peter Singer (b. 1946), Animal Liberation (1975): equal interests deserve equal consideration, whatever the species; favouring humans just for being human is speciesism.
  • Tom Regan (1938–2017), The Case for Animal Rights (1983): every subject-of-a-life (a being with beliefs, desires, memory and a welfare of its own) has inherent value and rights that may not be traded for benefits.
  • Critics (Singer): equal suffering deserves equal weight; Mill's ranking may hide bias by species.
  • Weak rule utilitarians may break a rule when keeping it would clearly cause more suffering; strong rule utilitarians keep it.
Quick check
?A lab could skip pain relief for one batch of mice to save time. The harm seems small and the results would be the same. Which reply fits a rule utilitarian who accepts the 3Rs?
Mission 3Evaluate

Can utilitarianism justify animal experiments?

  • Claim: Utilitarianism provides a sound basis for deciding about animal experiments.
  • For: Counts animal suffering directly; distinguishes serious research from trivial testing; matches the law's harm–benefit test.
  • For: Usable by religious believers and non-believers alike; rule forms (the 3Rs) give clear limits.
  • Against: Benefits are hard to predict; animal pain is hard to measure against human health.
  • Against: It could justify great suffering for large benefits, or, applied consistently, experiments on humans with similar capacities (Singer's challenge). Rights theorists (Regan) say no total justifies using a subject-of-a-life.
  • Judgement criteria: Is pleasure (or freedom from pain) the only intrinsic good? Does utilitarianism give justice to the weak? Does it work in contemporary society?
  • Spec issues: whether act or rule utilitarianism gives the better basis; whether utilitarianism promotes immoral behaviour.
Mission 3Worked answer

Act and rule compared

Task: "Rule utilitarianism gives a better basis than act utilitarianism for decisions about animal experiments." Evaluate.

  • Line of argument: rule utilitarianism is better here, because research needs consistent standards.
  • Support: Bentham's case-by-case calculus is slow and open to bias by researchers; Mill's rules (the 3Rs) protect animals predictably and keep public trust.
  • Counter: a rule may forbid a single experiment that would save many lives; act utilitarianism allows for this.
  • Critique of the counter: a weak rule utilitarian can break a rule in a clear emergency, so most of this flexibility is kept.
  • Judgement: decided by your own criteria (e.g. predictability vs best outcome in each case), stated and defended.
Quick check

Mission 4

Why weapons of mass destruction are different

  • WMD: nuclear, chemical, biological and radiological weapons, able to kill vast numbers at once.
  • Hiroshima (6 August 1945) and Nagasaki (9 August 1945): the only nuclear weapons used in war.
  • Effects: blast and fire over whole cities; radiation sickness and cancers for decades; possible "nuclear winter" after a large exchange.
  • So they struggle to meet jus in bello: they cannot discriminate between soldiers and civilians, and their harm is hard to call proportionate.
  • Law: Biological Weapons Convention (1972); Chemical Weapons Convention (1993); Non-Proliferation Treaty (1968); Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons (2017; the UK and other nuclear states have not joined).
  • Second Vatican Council (Gaudium et Spes, 1965, §80): any act of war aimed at destroying whole cities is a crime against God and humanity (paraphrase).
Mission 4

Deterrence: the peace of fear

  • Deterrence: possessing nuclear weapons, ready to use them, so that no enemy dares attack. MAD (mutually assured destruction): each side could destroy the other.
  • Bentham (act utilitarian): weigh this policy's likely pleasures and pains: peace kept for decades (extent, duration) against a small chance of catastrophic suffering (intensity).
  • Mill (rule utilitarian): which general rule maximises happiness: "Possess, never use first" or "abolish"?
  • The paradox of deterrence: if using the weapons would be wrong, can it be right to intend or threaten to use them?
  • Catholic teaching moved: John Paul II (1982) judged deterrence acceptable only as a step towards disarmament; Pope Francis (Hiroshima, 2019) called even the possession of nuclear weapons immoral.
  • Full application of Bentham and Mill.
Quick check
?How would a rule utilitarian assess nuclear deterrence?
Mission 4Evaluate

Can nuclear deterrence be justified?

  • Claim: Possessing nuclear weapons as a deterrent is morally justified.
  • For: No war between the major nuclear powers since 1945 (supporters credit deterrence); the act utilitarian calculation may favour it.
  • For: One-sided disarmament could invite blackmail; just war thinkers accept defence of the innocent.
  • Against: It depends on a readiness to kill millions of civilians: the paradox of deterrence; nuclear pacifists and Pope Francis judge possession itself wrong.
  • Against: Accidents, false alarms and proliferation: a small risk each year adds up over decades.
  • Judgement criteria: Is it wrong to intend what it would be wrong to do? How should tiny risks of catastrophe be weighed? Are there realistic paths to disarmament?
  • Spec issues: "whether utilitarianism promotes justice"; "the effectiveness of utilitarianism in dealing with ethical issues".
Mission check

?A country keeps chemical weapons only as a deterrent and promises never to use them first. A critic says: "Even if this has kept the peace, it is wrong, because the policy depends on being willing to do something that is always wrong." Which position is the critic taking?

Deterrence turns on one question: are we judged by what our threats achieve, or by what we are ready to do?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Mill: pleasures differ in quality; higher (mental) pleasures outweigh lower (bodily) ones, by the verdict of competent judges.
  • Harm principle: power over a person is justified only to prevent harm to others. Rule utilitarianism (strong and weak) follows rules that maximise happiness; critics say it collapses into act utilitarianism or becomes rule worship.

Mission 2

  • Preference utilitarianism (Hare, Singer) satisfies interests, and animals count. Evaluation: measurement, prediction, justice for minorities, no act wrong in itself; rule and preference forms reply in part.
  • Act and rule utilitarianism give different routes on animal experiments and nuclear deterrence.

Mission 3

  • Experiments are regulated by harm–benefit analysis and the 3Rs: replace, reduce, refine.
  • Act utilitarianism (Bentham) weighs each experiment; rule utilitarianism (Mill) sets rules for "the whole sentient creation"; Singer challenges bias by species.

Mission 4

  • WMD struggle to meet discrimination and proportionality; deterrence rests on a threat that raises the paradox of intending a wrong act.
  • Bentham weighs the policy's consequences; Mill asks which general rule maximises happiness.
