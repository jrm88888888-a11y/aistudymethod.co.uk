Mission 1

The question: how is a sinner put right with God?

  • Justification: being put right (made or declared righteous) before God. From Greek dikaioō, "to justify, acquit".
  • Christians agree: all have sinned (Romans 3:23), and God's grace saves through Christ.
  • They have disagreed on how: by faith alone? By faith and works? Who decides who is saved?
  • 1517: Martin Luther (1483–1546), an Augustinian friar and professor at Wittenberg, posts the 95 Theses against the sale of indulgences.
  • The quarrel over indulgences became a quarrel over justification: the heart of the Reformation.
Mission 1

Luther's discovery

  • Luther tried to earn peace with God through monastic discipline, fasting and confession. It failed.
  • He came to hate the phrase "God's righteousness" (Romans 1:17; Latin iustitia Dei), reading it as the standard by which God punishes sinners.
  • Breakthrough (he recalls it in his 1545 Preface to his Latin works, paraphrase): it means the righteousness God gives as a gift, by which the righteous "live by faith".
  • He said it was as if he had been born again and entered paradise through open gates (paraphrase).
  • So righteousness is not achieved by us (active) but received (passive).

Mission 1

Sola fide: faith alone

  • Sola fide: we are justified by faith alone, not by faith plus works.
  • Faith here is trust (fiducia) in Christ's promise, not only agreeing to doctrines.
  • Imputed (credited) righteousness: Christ's righteousness is counted as ours, like a debt paid into our account. Luther called it an alien righteousness: it comes from outside us.
  • So the believer is simul iustus et peccator: at the same time righteous (in God's verdict) and a sinner (in himself).
  • Luther's German New Testament (1522) translated Romans 3:28 "by faith alone" (allein); he defended the word in his Open Letter on Translating (1530).

Mission 1

Luther and James 2:24

> "You see then that by works a man is justified, and not only by faith." (James 2:24, WEBBE)

  • In his 1522 Preface to the New Testament, Luther called James "an epistle of straw" compared with John, Romans, Galatians and 1 Peter, because it has nothing of the gospel's character (paraphrase).
  • His reasons: James seems to contradict Paul; it hardly mentions Christ's death and resurrection; he doubted it was by an apostle.
  • He moved James (with Hebrews, Jude and Revelation) to the end of his New Testament. He did not remove it, and later editions dropped the "straw" remark.
  • Other readings: James attacks a dead faith that is mere belief (2:17, 2:26); "justified" may mean shown to be righteous before others. Many Protestants hold that Paul and James do not contradict.
Mission 1

So why be good?

  • Reformers' answer: good works do not make us righteous; they follow from being made right, as fruit follows from a good tree (Matthew 7:17).
  • Luther, The Freedom of a Christian (1520): a Christian is free from the need to earn salvation, and so free to serve the neighbour in love (paraphrase).
  • "We are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works" (Ephesians 2:10).
  • Calvin: faith alone justifies, yet the faith that justifies is never alone; it always brings a new life (sanctification) (Antidote to Trent, 1547, paraphrase).
Mission 1Evaluate

Do the New Testament letters support faith alone?

  • Claim: The New Testament letters support justification by faith alone.
  • For: Romans 3:28; Galatians 2:16; Ephesians 2:8–9 contrast faith with works directly.
  • For: Paul's model is Abraham, counted righteous by believing before he was circumcised (Romans 4:3, 4:10).
  • Against: "Alone" is not in Paul's Greek; Luther added it (allein) when he translated Romans 3:28 into German.
  • Against: James 2:24 says "not only by faith"; Paul too says God will "pay back to everyone according to their works" (Romans 2:6).
  • Against: "Works of the law" may mean Jewish law-observance, not good deeds in general (see Mission 2).
  • Judge it by: What does Paul mean by "works"? Can James and Paul be reconciled? Is Luther's reading shaped by his own struggle?
  • Spec issue: "the extent to which the New Testament letters support arguments for justification by faith alone".

Mission 2

Trent: the Catholic response

  • The Council of Trent (northern Italy, 1545–1563): the Catholic Church's response to the Reformation.
  • Session VI, the Decree on Justification (13 January 1547): 16 chapters of teaching and 33 canons (short rulings ending "let him be anathema", i.e. excluded from the Church).
  • It rejected both extremes:
  • Against Pelagius: no one can be justified by their own works without God's grace (canon 1).
  • Against the Reformers: justification is not by faith alone, and not by Christ's righteousness merely credited to us.
Mission 2

Justification as a gift that changes you

  • Grace comes first: God's "prevenient" grace calls the sinner, who has no merit (ch. 5).
  • Free will cooperates: the person can accept or reject that grace (ch. 5; canon 4).
  • Instrumental cause: the sacrament of baptism (ch. 7).
  • Formal cause: the justice of God "whereby he maketh us just": righteousness infused (poured in), not only imputed (ch. 7).
  • With forgiveness, the person receives faith, hope and charity (ch. 7): faith is the beginning, but it must be living, formed by love.
  • "Justified freely": nothing before justification, faith or works, merits it (ch. 8).
  • Justification is a process: it can grow, be lost through mortal sin, and be restored through the sacrament of penance (ch. 10, 14).
Mission 2Read the source

Trent's canons

Council of Trent, Session VI, canons (trans. J. Waterworth, 1848):

  • Canon 9 condemns anyone who says "by faith alone the impious is justified", meaning that nothing else is required to co-operate in receiving grace.
  • Canon 11 condemns the view that we are justified "by the sole imputation of the justice of Christ", without grace and charity poured into our hearts.
  • Canon 24 condemns the view that good works are only the fruits and signs of justification, and not a cause of its increase (paraphrase).

Mission 2

Works and merit

  • Trent ch. 10 quotes James 2:24 for the increase of justification: the justified grow in righteousness by keeping God's commandments.
  • Merit (ch. 16): eternal life is both a grace promised through Christ and a reward for good works done in grace.
  • But God's gifts are the source: when God rewards merit, he "crowns his own gifts" (an Augustinian idea, paraphrase).
  • So Catholic "justification by works" means works done by grace grow and keep justification. It does not mean earning God's favour by unaided effort.
  • A common misreading is that Catholics believe you earn heaven by being good. Trent's view: grace makes good works possible and meritorious.

Mission 2

Sanders: what was first-century Judaism really like?

  • E. P. Sanders (1937–2022), Paul and Palestinian Judaism (1977).
  • Many Protestant scholars had pictured Judaism as legalism: earning salvation by piling up good works. Paul was read as Luther against medieval Catholicism.
  • Sanders studied the Jewish sources (rabbinic writings, Dead Sea Scrolls, Apocrypha) and found a different pattern: covenantal nomism.
  • Getting in: by grace. God chose Israel and made the covenant; no one earned it.
  • Staying in: by obedience to the Law (nomos), with God's provision of repentance and atonement for failure.
  • So works were the response to grace and the condition of remaining in the covenant, not the way to earn it.
Mission 2

Sanders on Paul and works

  • If Judaism was not legalism, what was Paul's problem? Sanders (paraphrase): not that Jews tried to earn salvation, but that salvation is now in Christ, for Jews and Gentiles alike.
  • Sanders' own summary: "this is what Paul finds wrong in Judaism: it is not Christianity" (1977, p. 552).
  • The question was entry: must Gentiles become Jews to belong? Paul: no; membership is by faith in Christ.
  • James D. G. Dunn developed this: "Works of the law" in Galatians are the Law as the marker of who belongs: circumcision, food laws, Sabbath (see Galatians 2:11–16, the meal at Antioch).
  • Yet for Paul too, believers will be judged by their works (Romans 2:6; 2 Corinthians 5:10). Works are the condition of staying in, not of getting in (paraphrase of Sanders).
  • This "New Perspective on Paul" (name from Dunn, 1983) was developed by Dunn and N. T. Wright.

Mission 2Diversity

Where the churches stand now

  • 1999: the Lutheran World Federation and the Catholic Church signed the Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification (Augsburg, 31 October).
  • Core agreement: we are accepted "by grace alone, in faith in Christ's saving work", and the Spirit renews hearts and calls to good works (§15, short quotation).
  • The mutual condemnations of the 1500s are said not to apply to the teaching of the other side as set out in the Declaration.
  • Later joined by the World Methodist Council (2006), the World Communion of Reformed Churches (2017); the Anglican Communion welcomed and affirmed it (2016–17).
  • Differences remain: merit, the role of sacraments, how far a believer is "righteous and sinner" at once. Some Lutherans and evangelicals reject the Declaration.
  • Orthodox Christians frame salvation more as theosis (sharing God's life) than as a courtroom verdict.
  • Critics of Sanders (e.g. Stephen Westerholm, Perspectives Old and New on Paul, 2004) argue the "Lutheran" Paul is still right.
Mission 2

Why good conduct matters in each view

  • Catholic (Trent): works done in grace grow and keep justification; mortal sin can lose it.
  • Lutheran: works are the fruit of faith and serve the neighbour; they add nothing to justification.
  • Reformed (Calvin): works are the evidence of a living faith and of election; they give a secondary assurance.
  • New Perspective (Sanders, Dunn, Wright): believers will be judged by their works, which show they are "in Christ".
  • Methodist (Wesley): faith leads to growing holiness of heart and life.
  • In every view, good conduct is required of a Christian. The disagreement is about why.
Mission 2Worked answer

Linking belief, source and development

Question: "Examine Catholic teaching on justification by works."

  • Belief: works done in grace increase justification. Source: Trent, Session VI, ch. 10, citing James 2:24. Development: so the Christian life is growth, which is why Catholics value the sacraments and works of mercy.
  • Belief: grace comes first and cannot be earned. Source: ch. 8: nothing before justification merits it. Development: this answers the charge that Catholics believe in salvation by effort.
Mission 2Evaluate

Are faith and works both aspects of justification?

  • Claim: Both faith and works are aspects of justification.
  • For: James 2:24; Romans 2:6; Matthew 25:31–46: judgement looks at deeds.
  • For: Trent and the 1999 Declaration both link faith to a changed life.
  • For: Sanders too: for Paul, believers are judged by their works.
  • Against: Romans 3:28, Galatians 2:16, Ephesians 2:8–9: faith apart from works.
  • Against: If works count, assurance is lost: no one knows if they have done enough (Luther's experience).
  • Middle position: faith alone justifies; works follow necessarily. Does that make works an aspect, or only a result?
  • Judge it by: What does "works" mean (moral deeds or Torah observance)? Is justification a moment or a process? Does the answer fit Paul and James?
  • Spec issue: "the extent to which both faith and works are aspects of justification".

Mission 3

What marked the community

  • Teaching: the apostles' witness to Jesus, later the New Testament.
  • Fellowship: Greek koinonia: shared life, not just friendliness.
  • Breaking of bread: shared meals and, most scholars think, an early form of the Eucharist.
  • Prayer and worship: daily "in the temple", and "breaking bread at home" (Acts 2:46).
  • Sharing: goods sold and given "according as anyone had need".
  • Awe and growth: "wonders and signs" (2:43); numbers added daily (2:47).
Mission 3

A model or a snapshot?

  • Voluntary, not a rule: Peter asks Ananias about his land: "While you kept it, didn't it remain your own?" (Acts 5:4).
  • Local and short-lived: Paul later collects money for poor believers in Jerusalem (Romans 15:26), not for a common purse everywhere.
  • Idealised? Many scholars read Acts 2 and 4:32–35 as Luke's picture of what a Spirit-filled church looks like.
  • Taken literally by some: monastic communities hold goods in common (e.g. the Rule of St Benedict, c. 530); Hutterites and the Bruderhof today share all property.
  • Taken as a pattern by others: base communities in liberation theology; the house-church movement.

Mission 3

Role 1: worship and sacraments

  • Churches gather people to worship God together (Matthew 18:20).
  • Sacraments: outward signs of inward grace.
  • Catholic and Orthodox: seven sacraments; the Eucharist (Mass, Divine Liturgy) is the centre of church life.
  • Most Protestants: two sacraments, baptism and the Lord's Supper, with the preached word central.
  • Quakers and the Salvation Army: no outward sacraments; the whole of life is lived sacramentally.
  • Link to Acts 2: "the breaking of bread, and prayer".
Mission 3

Role 2: teaching and mission

  • Religious teaching: sermons, Bible study, catechesis before baptism or confirmation, Sunday schools, church schools.
  • Link to Acts 2: "the apostles' teaching".
  • Mission: "Go and make disciples of all nations" (Matthew 28:19).
  • Evangelism courses (e.g. Alpha, from Holy Trinity Brompton, London); church planting.
  • Mission also means witness through action, not only words.
  • Link to Acts 2: "the Lord added to the assembly day by day".
Mission 3

Role 3: service, outreach and fellowship

  • Service and outreach: care for the poor, the sick and the stranger (Matthew 25:31–46).
  • Examples: church-run food banks (many in the Trussell network); Street Pastors (from 2003); Christians Against Poverty (debt help); CAFOD, Christian Aid and Caritas abroad.
  • Fellowship: belonging, support and accountability; small groups, cell groups, parish groups, pastoral visiting.
  • Link to Acts 2: koinonia and "distributed them to all, according as anyone had need".
Mission 3Diversity

Same roles, different emphases

  • Catholic and Orthodox: sacraments and liturgy first; the parish as a eucharistic community.
  • Evangelical: teaching and mission; preaching the Bible and calling people to faith.
  • Pentecostal and Charismatic: Spirit-filled worship, healing and "signs" (Acts 2:43) expected today.
  • Liberal Protestant and many social-action churches: service and justice as the gospel lived.
  • Quaker: fellowship in silent worship and service, with no ordained clergy.
  • New forms: house churches and Fresh Expressions try to recover Acts-style community.

Mission 3Evaluate

"The main role of the Church is to provide religious teaching."

  • For: Acts 2:42 lists teaching first; Matthew 28:19–20 links making disciples with "teaching them"; without teaching, worship and service lose their Christian meaning.
  • For: many evangelical churches see preaching the Bible as the heart of church life.
  • Against: Catholic and Orthodox Christians see the Eucharist as the "source and summit" of the Christian life (Lumen Gentium 11, 1964; Catechism 1324).
  • Against: Matthew 25:31–46 judges people by service, not by doctrine learned.
  • Against: the roles depend on each other: no one role is "main".
  • Judge it by: What did Jesus command most? What draws people in today? Can the roles be ranked at all?
Mission 3Evaluate

"Churches today should follow the New Testament model."

  • For: Acts 2 shows the Church as the Spirit shaped it; sharing by need challenges consumer culture.
  • For: communities that do share (Bruderhof, new monastic groups) show it can work.
  • Against: Acts 5:4 makes sharing voluntary; Paul's churches did not copy Jerusalem.
  • Against: today's churches are large, legal bodies in complex economies; they need structures Acts 2 lacks.
  • Against: the New Testament gives several models (Jerusalem, Corinth, the Pastoral Letters), not one.
  • Judge it by: Is Acts 2 description or command? Which features are essential (teaching, fellowship, bread, prayer) and which local (common purse)?

Mission 4

Luke's summary portraits

  • Acts pauses three times to sum up the Jerusalem church: 2:42–47, 4:32–35, 5:12–16.
  • Each gives a picture of an ideal community: shared life, prayer, generosity, signs, growth.
  • Each follows a crisis or turning point: Pentecost; the first arrest; Ananias and Sapphira.
  • Luke writes decades later, for readers in other cities: the portraits teach as well as record.
Mission 4Read the source

Four marks of the church

> "They continued steadfastly in the apostles' teaching and fellowship, in the breaking of bread, and prayer." (Acts 2:42, WEBBE)

  • Apostles' teaching (didachē): instruction in the faith, from the witnesses.
  • Fellowship (koinōnia): a shared life, including possessions.
  • Breaking of bread: shared meals and, for most interpreters, the Eucharist.
  • Prayer: they still worship daily "in the temple" (2:46): these are Jews who believe Jesus is Messiah.

Q1 Which mark shows the believers still belonged to Jewish worship?

Show answer

Daily attendance at the Temple, 2:46.

Q2 What result does Luke report?

Show answer

Favour with the people; "The Lord added to the assembly day by day those who were being saved" (2:47).

Mission 4

Sharing everything?

  • "All who believed were together, and had all things in common." (Acts 2:44, WEBBE)
  • They sold property "according as anyone had need" (Acts 2:45). "Neither was there amongst them any who lacked" (Acts 4:34).
  • That echoes God's promise to Israel: "there will be no poor with you" (Deuteronomy 15:4).
  • Barnabas (Joseph, a Levite from Cyprus, "Son of Encouragement") sells a field and lays the money at the apostles' feet (4:36–37).
  • Ananias and Sapphira sold land, kept part of the price and presented the rest as the whole; Acts 5:1–11 says both fell dead after Peter told them they had lied to God.
  • Voluntary: Peter tells Ananias the land "remained your own" (5:4). This was generosity, not compulsory communism.
  • Some think the sharing left the Jerusalem church poor later (Paul's collection, Romans 15:26); others see it as a model never meant to be permanent.

Mission 5

Love as the principal value

  • Christians call the highest love agape: self-giving love that seeks the good of the other, even an enemy.
  • Jesus: the greatest commandments are to love God and to love "your neighbour as yourself" (Mark 12:29–31).
  • Paul: "faith, hope, and love remain … The greatest of these is love" (1 Corinthians 13:13, WEBBE).
  • "Above all these things, walk in love, which is the bond of perfection" (Colossians 3:14, WEBBE): love holds the other virtues together.
Mission 5

Love starts with God

  • "God is love" (1 John 4:8, WEBBE).
  • "We love him, because he first loved us" (1 John 4:19, WEBBE).
  • So Christian love is a response: people love because they have first been loved by God.
  • This makes love more than a feeling: it is sharing in God's own life and way of acting.
  • Your spec: God's love as a potential model for Christian behaviour.

Mission 5

Love of neighbour

  • Leviticus 19:34: the foreigner living among you must be treated as native-born: "you shall love him as yourself". Israel remembers being foreigners in Egypt.
  • Luke 10:25–28: a lawyer asks how to inherit eternal life; he answers with love of God and neighbour; Jesus: "Do this, and you will live" (Luke 10:28, WEBBE).
  • His next question, "Who is my neighbour?" (10:29), leads to the parable of the Good Samaritan: the neighbour is anyone in need, even an outsider or enemy.
  • Love of neighbour is practical: action, not just goodwill.
  • Issue: is love of neighbour the most important moral principle in Christianity?
Mission 5

God's love as a model

> "The LORD, a merciful and gracious God, slow to anger, and abundant in loving kindness and truth, keeping loving kindness for thousands, forgiving iniquity and disobedience and sin; and who will by no means clear the guilty …" (Exodus 34:6–7, WEBBE)

  • God's love here includes mercy and justice: forgiving, yet taking wrong seriously.
  • 1 John 4:19–21: because God loved first, "he who loves God should also love his brother" (4:21, WEBBE).
  • Issue: how far is God's behaviour the basis for Christian morality? Christians copy God's mercy; but not all of God's actions are for humans to copy (e.g. judgement belongs to God, Romans 12:19; Exodus 34:7 on punishing children is set against Ezekiel 18:20).
Mission 5

Truth, conscience, forgiveness

  • Truth: "serve him in truth with all your heart" (1 Samuel 12:24, WEBBE); "putting away falsehood, speak truth each one with his neighbour" (Ephesians 4:25, WEBBE). Love "rejoices with the truth" (1 Corinthians 13:6).
  • Conscience: Paul appeals to "the testimony of our conscience" (2 Corinthians 1:12, WEBBE); the goal is "love from a pure heart, a good conscience, and sincere faith" (1 Timothy 1:5, WEBBE).
  • Forgiveness: "if you forgive men their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you" (Matthew 6:14, WEBBE); "even as Christ forgave you, so you also do" (Colossians 3:13, WEBBE).
  • These are the verses your spec names: learn one per principle.

Mission 5Evaluate

Is love of neighbour the most important principle?

  • Claim: Love of neighbour is the most important moral principle in Christianity.
  • For: "The whole law is fulfilled in one word": love your neighbour (Galatians 5:14); the Sheep and the Goats judges by care for the needy (Matthew 25:31–46).
  • For: 1 John 4:20: love of God is tested by love of others.
  • Against: Jesus calls love of God the "first commandment" (Mark 12:30); love of neighbour flows from it.
  • Against: Love needs truth and justice to guide it; without them "love" can excuse wrong.
  • Judgement criteria: What Jesus ranks first · whether the principles can be separated · how each guides real decisions.

?A Christian charity worker, asked why she helps refugees who do not share her faith, says: "God showed us love first, so I pass it on. Forgiving, telling the truth, welcoming the stranger: they're all just ways of loving." Which idea does her answer express most clearly?

For Christians, love is not one rule among others: it is the root, and truth, conscience and forgiveness are its branches.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Luther: justification by grace through faith alone; Christ's righteousness imputed; the believer is righteous and sinner at once (Romans 1:17; 5:1; Ephesians 2:8–9; Galatians 2:16).
  • Good works matter as the fruit and evidence of faith, not as a means of earning salvation.

Mission 2

  • Trent (1547): justification is God's free gift, received with cooperating free will; righteousness is infused and grows through works done in grace.
  • Sanders: Judaism was "covenantal nomism"; Paul's dispute over "works of the law" was whether Gentiles must keep the Law to belong (Dunn: "identity markers"); judgement by works stays.

Mission 3

  • Acts 2:42–47: teaching, fellowship (koinonia), breaking of bread, prayer, sharing by need, worship and growth.
  • Churches today provide worship and sacraments, religious teaching, mission, service and outreach, and fellowship; traditions stress different roles.
  • Whether Acts 2 is a model for today, and which role is "main", are debated.

Mission 4

  • The Jerusalem church: apostles' teaching, fellowship, breaking of bread, prayer (2:42); voluntary sharing by need (2:45; 4:34; 5:4).
  • Churches today copy Acts' structures or its values, and differ on which.

Mission 5

  • Love (agape) is the principal Christian value: it holds all others together (Colossians 3:14).
  • Key principles and texts: neighbour (Leviticus 19:34; Luke 10:25–28), God's love (Exodus 34:6–7; 1 John 4:19–21), truth (1 Samuel 12:24; Ephesians 4:25–27), conscience (2 Corinthians 1:12; 1 Timothy 1:5), forgiveness (Matthew 6:14–15; Colossians 3:12–13).
