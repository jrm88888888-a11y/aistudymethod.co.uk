Mission 1

Mission 1: If a country printed twice as much money, would everything really cost twice as much?

Make a guess: If a country printed twice as much money, would everything really cost twice as much?

Mission 1

The equation of exchange

  • MV = PY
  • M = the money supply.
  • V = the velocity of circulation: how many times, on average, each unit of money is spent in a year.
  • P = the average price level.
  • Y = real output (real GDP).
  • Left side: money × times spent = total spending. Right side: prices × output = nominal GDP.
  • The two sides are equal by definition: an identity, not yet a theory.
Mission 1Worked example

Finding velocity

Illustrative data:

  • Money supply M = 500 billion. Nominal GDP = 2,000 billion.
  • V = nominal GDP ÷ M = 2,000 ÷ 500 = 4. Each unit of money is spent 4 times a year.
  • Real output (at base-year prices) = 1,600 billion.
  • P = nominal GDP ÷ real output = 2,000 ÷ 1,600 = 1.25 (prices are 25% above the base year).
  • Check: M × V = 500 × 4 = 2,000 = P × output = 1.25 × 1,600. ✔
  • Any one of the four can be found from the other three: M = P × output ÷ V = 1.25 × 1,600 ÷ 4 = 500; output = M × V ÷ P = 500 × 4 ÷ 1.25 = 1,600.
Mission 1

From identity to theory

  • The quantity theory of money adds three assumptions:
  • (1) V is stable: it depends on slow-changing habits and payment technology.
  • (2) Real output is set by real factors (labor, capital, technology), not by money, at least in the long run.
  • (3) Causation runs from M to P: money changes first.
  • So if M rises and V and output are fixed, P must rise in proportion.
  • Double M → double P.
  • Say: "the quantity theory says money growth causes inflation" — not: "MV = PQ proves it". The equation is always true; the theory is the assumptions.
Mission 1Worked example

In growth rates

  • In growth rates: % change in M + % change in V ≈ % change in P + % change in real output.
  • Illustrative data: money grows 10%, V is constant, real output grows 3%.
  • Inflation ≈ 10 + 0 − 3 = 7%.
  • (Exact: 1.10 ÷ 1.03 − 1 = 6.8%; the approximation is close for small changes.)
  • Rule of thumb: lasting inflation ≈ money growth − output growth, if V is stable.
Quick check
?Illustrative data: M = 800 billion, V = 5, real output = 2,000 billion. Using the quantity equation, what is the price level P?
Mission 1Graph lab

More money, same output

  • Takeaway: with output fixed at full capacity and V stable, more money means a proportionately higher price level.
Mission 1

Money neutrality and monetarism

  • Money neutrality: in the long run, money changes nominal variables (prices, wages) but not real ones (output, jobs).
  • Milton Friedman: "Inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon."
  • Monetarists: to control inflation, control the growth of the money supply; keep it close to the growth of real output.
  • Sustained, rapid growth in the money supply causes inflation; money is neutral in the long run.
Mission 1

Real case: Zimbabwe

  • 2000s: Zimbabwe's output fell sharply, while its central bank created money to pay for government spending.
  • M soared while real output shrank, so P rose explosively.
  • Official annual inflation reached 231 million percent in July 2008.
  • In January 2009 a 100 trillion Zimbabwe dollar note was issued.
  • People spent money as fast as they could before it lost value: V rose too, pushing prices up even faster.
  • In 2009 the Zimbabwe dollar was abandoned; people used US dollars and other currencies.

Mission 1

Short run and long run

  • Short run: more money lowers interest rates, AD shifts right; real GDP rises above full employment (Yf); unemployment falls below the natural rate.
  • Long run: workers and firms expect higher prices; nominal wages rise; SRAS shifts left.
  • The economy returns to Yf at a higher PL: money is neutral in the long run, not in the short run.
  • Show it on an AD-AS graph: AD1 → AD2, then SRAS1 → SRAS2, ending on LRAS.
  • Say: "real GDP returns to Yf; the price level is permanently higher" — not: "the economy goes back to where it started".
Quick check
?Illustrative data: over five years, an economy's money supply rises by 50%. Real output rises by 5% and the price level hardly changes. According to the quantity equation, what must have happened to V?
Mission check

?A country's central bank doubles the money supply. In the same period, people start holding on to money twice as long before spending it, so V halves. Real output does not change. What happens to the price level?

More money raises prices only if it is spent: what matters is M × V compared with real output.

Mission 2

Mission 2: If a country's yearly deficit keeps shrinking, how can its debt still be growing?

Make a guess: If a country's yearly deficit keeps shrinking, how can its debt still be growing?

Mission 2

Three kinds of spending

  • Current spending: day-to-day running costs that are used up within the year. Salaries of teachers and nurses, medicines, office supplies.
  • Capital spending: investment in assets that last for years. New roads, rail lines, hospitals, school buildings.
  • Transfer payments: money paid to people with no good or service in return. Pensions, unemployment benefits, child benefit.
  • Transfers move income from taxpayers to recipients. They are not part of government spending (G) in AD, because no output is bought.
  • AP: government purchases (G) include both day-to-day and capital purchases. Transfer payments (Social Security, unemployment insurance) are not in G.
  • G is a component of AD. Capital spending can also raise LRAS over time (better infrastructure, more productive workers).
Quick check
?Which of these is an example of government capital expenditure?
Mission 2

The budget balance

  • Budget balance = tax revenue − government spending (incl. transfers), over one year.
  • Deficit: spending > revenue. Surplus: revenue > spending. Balanced budget: equal.
  • Illustrative data (billion): tax revenue 900 · current spending 650 · capital spending 100 · transfers 250.
  • Spending = 650 + 100 + 250 = 1,000.
  • Budget balance = 900 − 1,000 = −100: a deficit of 100.
  • AP: budget deficit = government purchases + transfers − taxes = (750 + 250) − 900 = $100 billion.
  • A deficit means the government injects more into the economy than it takes out in tax: other things equal, it adds to AD.
Mission 2

Deficit vs debt

  • Deficit is a flow: how much more the government spends than it raises in one year.
  • National debt is a stock: the total owed, built up from all past borrowing.
  • Each year's deficit is borrowed, so it is added to the debt. A surplus can be used to pay debt down.
  • Picture a bath: the deficit is the water flowing in this year; the debt is the water in the tub.
  • So a smaller deficit still adds to the debt, just more slowly. Paying the debt down needs a surplus.
  • Say: "the deficit fell, so the debt grew more slowly" — not: "the deficit fell, so the debt fell".
Quick check
?A government's deficit falls from 80 billion last year to 50 billion this year. Nothing else changes. What happens to the national debt this year?
Mission 2

Measuring the burden: debt as a % of GDP

  • Debt is compared with nominal GDP, because GDP is the income from which taxes are paid.
  • Debt-to-GDP ratio = (national debt ÷ nominal GDP) × 100.
  • Rough figures for gross government debt, 2023: Japan over 200% · Italy about 135% · US about 120% · UK about 100% · Germany about 62%.
  • AP: federal debt held by the public was about 98% of GDP at the end of fiscal year 2024.
  • Worked example (illustrative data, billion): debt 800 · nominal GDP 1,000 → ratio 80%.
  • Next year nominal GDP grows 5% to 1,050.

Quick check
?Illustrative data: a country's national debt is 600 billion and its nominal GDP is 1,200 billion. Next year it runs a deficit of 36 billion and nominal GDP grows by 4%. What is the new debt-to-GDP ratio, to one decimal place?
Mission 2

Why high debt can limit a government

Costs of a high debt:

  • Debt interest: money spent on interest cannot be spent on schools or health, or returned in lower taxes.
  • AP: in fiscal year 2024, US federal net interest costs were about $880 billion, more than federal spending on national defense.
  • Credit ratings: agencies grade the risk that a government will not repay. A downgrade can raise the interest rate it must pay.
  • Future taxes and spending: higher taxes or spending cuts may be needed later.
  • Less room to borrow in the next recession.

Possible benefits of a deficit:

  • It supports AD and jobs in a recession.
  • Borrowing for capital spending can raise future output, which helps pay the interest.
Mission check

?Illustrative data: a government's national debt is 300 billion at the start of Year 1. Its budget balances are: Year 1, surplus of 20 billion · Year 2, surplus of 5 billion · Year 3, deficit of 15 billion. What is the national debt at the end of Year 3?

The deficit is the yearly flow; the debt is the stock. Every deficit adds to the debt, however small.

Mission 3

Mission 3: When a government borrows more, can it leave firms with less to invest?

Make a guess: When a government borrows more, can it leave firms with less to invest?

Mission 3

Competing for the same savings

  • To finance a deficit, the government borrows: it sells bonds to savers, banks and pension funds.
  • Firms also borrow to invest. Both compete for the same pool of saving.
  • More government borrowing can push up interest rates and so reduce private investment.
  • Crowding out: an increase in government spending or borrowing that reduces private sector spending.
  • AP: crowding out = government borrowing raises the real interest rate, which reduces private investment.
Mission 3

The loanable funds market

  • Supply of loanable funds: from saving. Higher real interest rates encourage more saving.
  • Demand for loanable funds: from borrowers: firms' investment plus government borrowing.
  • Illustrative data (billion): at a real interest rate of 4%, saving = private investment = 500.
  • The government borrows an extra 100. Demand shifts right by 100: D1 → D2.
  • New equilibrium: real interest rate 5%, total lending 550.
  • At 5%, private investment falls to 450. 50 is crowded out.
  • The other 50 comes from extra saving drawn out by the higher rate.
  • AP: show it as a rightward shift of the demand for loanable funds. Foreign lending (a net capital inflow) adds to supply and reduces the rise in the real interest rate.
  • Say: "private investment falls" — not: "the government takes firms' money". The higher price of borrowing does the work.

Mission 3

Short run and long run

Short run:

  • A higher real interest rate cuts interest-sensitive spending: business investment, new homes, cars bought on credit.
  • This offsets part of the rise in AD from the extra government spending.

Long run:

  • Less private investment → the capital stock grows more slowly.
  • So productive capacity grows more slowly: a smaller rightward shift of LRAS (the PPC grows less).
  • AP: lower capital accumulation reduces long-run economic growth.
  • Exception: if the government borrows to build public capital (roads, broadband, training), total capital may still grow.
Quick check
?At full employment, a government increases its deficit spending, financed by borrowing. Saving responds only slightly to interest rates. Which is the most likely long-run effect?
Mission 3

Debt, interest and credit ratings

  • A large debt means large interest payments every year. That money cannot pay for schools, health or lower taxes: debt interest crowds out other public spending.
  • If lenders worry about repayment, they demand a higher yield on government bonds. Agencies may downgrade the credit rating.
  • Government bond yields are a benchmark: when they rise, firms' and households' borrowing costs tend to rise too, deepening crowding out.
  • AP example: S&P (2011) and Fitch (2023) each cut the US government's credit rating one notch, from AAA to AA+.
Mission check

?An economy is at full employment. The government cuts its budget deficit, so it borrows 100 billion less each year. The supply of loanable funds slopes upward. What happens in the loanable funds market?

Crowding out works in reverse: less government borrowing lowers the real interest rate and lets private investment rise, by less than the cut.

Mission 4

Mission 4: If a kitchen hires twice as many cooks, why might it not make twice as many meals?

Make a guess: If a kitchen hires twice as many cooks, why might it not make twice as many meals?

Mission 4

Labor productivity

  • Labor productivity = output ÷ labor input: output per worker or per hour worked.
  • An economy's aggregate production function: real GDP depends on labor, physical capital, human capital and technology.
  • Labor productivity = real GDP ÷ hours worked. It rises with more capital per worker and better technology.
  • What raises it:
    • more and better capital per worker (machines, computers)
    • technology: better methods and equipment
    • human capital: education, training, skills, health
    • better management and organization; motivation
  • More workers sharing the same equipment means less capital per worker, so output per worker can fall.
Mission 4

Worked example: capital per worker

  • Illustrative data. An economy has 100 workers and produces 1,000 units of real GDP per day.
  • Labor productivity = 1,000 ÷ 100 = 10 units per worker.
  • With more machines per worker, the same 100 workers produce 1,200 units: productivity 12.
  • Productivity growth = (12 − 10) ÷ 10 × 100 = 20%.
  • Hiring 20 more workers with no extra machines adds output, but output per worker can fall, because capital per worker falls.
  • Sustained growth in real GDP per person comes mainly from rising productivity.
Quick check

Mission check

?A delivery firm hires 50% more drivers but keeps the same number of vans. Which outcome is most likely?

Output per worker depends on what each worker has to work with, not just on how many workers there are.

Mission 5

Mission 5: How can a government raise the most its economy can produce, and why does it take so long?

Make a guess: How can a government raise the most its economy can produce, and why does it take so long?

Mission 5

What supply-side policies aim to do

  • Supply-side policies: government policies that aim to raise the economy's productive capacity.
  • Supply-side fiscal policies use taxes and spending to change incentives to work, save and invest, and to fund infrastructure and technology.
  • Goals:
    • long-term growth: LRAS shifts right
Mission 5

On the AD/AS diagram

  • Capacity rises: LRAS shifts right.
  • Full-employment output rises from Yf1 to Yf2.
  • With AD unchanged: real output rises, the price level falls.
  • Lower costs per unit often shift SRAS right too.
  • Employment can rise at a lower natural rate of unemployment.
Quick check
?Which policy is most clearly aimed at the supply side of the economy?
Mission 5

Worked example: per person, what can policy change?

  • Illustrative data. Real GDP per person = output per hour × hours per worker × workers ÷ population.
  • Population 10 million; workers 5 million; 1,600 hours each a year; output per hour 50.
  • Real GDP = 50 × 1,600 × 5 million = 400 billion. Per person: 40,000.
  • Policy 1: childcare and job support bring 0.5 million more people into work. Real GDP = 440 billion; per person 44,000 (+10%).
  • This is a rise in labor force participation, with the new entrants employed.
  • Policy 2: training and better equipment raise output per hour to 55. Per person also 44,000 (+10%).
  • Both together: 48,400 per person: +21%, not +20%, because the effects multiply.
Mission 5

Two effects at different speeds

  • Many supply-side policies also change spending:
    • building roads or schools is government spending: AD shifts right at once
    • a tax cut raises disposable income: AD shifts right
  • The supply-side effect comes later, once the road is built or the trained workers arrive: LRAS shifts right.
  • Supply-side fiscal policy can shift AD, short-run AS and long-run AS.

Mission 5

Real world: cutting taxes on company profits

  • The US cut its main federal corporate tax rate from 35% to 21% from 2018.
  • Aim: more investment in capital, so higher productivity and pay.
  • One 2024 study estimated investment rose by about 11% at firms given the average tax cut, compared with firms given none, but wages and growth rose by less than supporters had claimed.
  • Question to weigh: how would you judge whether a tax cut on profits raised capacity, and over how many years?

Mission check

?A government expands free childcare and job training, paid for by cutting other spending, so AD does not change. Over several years, more parents take jobs and workers become more skilled. According to the AD/AS model, what happens in the long run?

Supply-side policies work on capacity, not spending, and capacity takes years to build.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Why can the right cure for one slump be the wrong cure for the next?

Make a guess: When an economy slumps, what should a government do first?

Mission 6

The toolkit and the policy mix

  • Three sets of tools:
    • Fiscal policy: government spending and taxes.
    • Monetary policy: interest rates, the money supply, QE.
    • Supply-side policies: raise productive capacity (education, incentives, competition).
  • Policy mix: how these are combined at one time.
  • Fiscal and monetary policy can push the same way (both expansionary) or opposite ways (one expansionary, one contractionary).
  • In the AP model: expansionary fiscal = more G or lower taxes; expansionary monetary = the Fed buys bonds, so the money supply rises and the nominal interest rate falls.
Mission 6

Combining fiscal and monetary policy

  • Step 1: start with real output below full employment.
  • This is a recessionary gap.
  • Step 2: both expansionary: AD shifts right for two reasons. Real output and the price level both rise.
  • Step 3: the interest rate:
    • Fiscal expansion raises incomes and spending, so demand for money rises: rates are pushed up.
    • Monetary expansion raises the money supply: rates are pushed down.
    • Overall change: indeterminate. It depends on which effect is bigger.
  • Opposite directions flip this: the interest-rate change is certain, the AD change is not.
    • Expansionary fiscal + contractionary monetary: interest rate up; AD uncertain.
    • Contractionary fiscal + expansionary monetary: interest rate down; AD uncertain.
  • For an inflationary gap, reverse it: higher taxes or lower G, and the Fed sells bonds. AD shifts left; real GDP and the price level fall; the interest-rate change is again indeterminate.
  • Say: "the change in the interest rate is indeterminate" — not: "it stays the same".
Mission 6Graph lab

Illustrative data. Start: real output 960, full employment at 1,000, interest rate 5%.

  • Takeaway: two pushes the same way make the AD change certain; two pushes in opposite ways make the interest-rate change certain.

?Last year the economy had a recessionary gap. Congress cut taxes, the Fed bought bonds, and the gap closed. Now the economy has an inflationary gap, and a senator proposes using the same mix again. What would it do in the short run?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The equation of exchange is an identity: money × velocity = price level × real output.
  • The quantity theory assumes stable V and fixed real output, so money growth causes proportional inflation; money is neutral in the long run.
  • Money growth can raise real GDP in the short run, but in the long run only the price level rises.

Mission 2

  • Current, capital and transfer spending. Transfers are not part of G.
  • Deficit = flow (one year); debt = stock (all past borrowing). A smaller deficit still adds to the debt.
  • The burden is measured as debt ÷ nominal GDP; the ratio can fall even when the debt rises.

Mission 3

  • Government borrowing raises the demand for loanable funds and the real interest rate; private investment falls.
  • Crowding out is usually partial: higher rates also draw out extra saving.
  • Short run: interest-sensitive spending falls. Long run: slower capital accumulation and growth.

Mission 4

  • Labor productivity is output per worker or per hour; it rises with capital per worker, technology and human capital.

Mission 5

  • Supply-side policies aim to raise capacity: LRAS shifts right, raising real output without adding to inflation.
  • Supply-side fiscal policies change incentives and fund infrastructure and technology, shifting AD now and LRAS later.
  • Effects take years and are uncertain in size.

Mission 6

  • The policy mix combines fiscal, monetary and supply-side tools. When two policies push the same way, the AD change is certain and the interest-rate change is not, and the reverse.
  • Match the mix to the gap: expansionary policies for a recessionary gap, contractionary policies for an inflationary gap.
