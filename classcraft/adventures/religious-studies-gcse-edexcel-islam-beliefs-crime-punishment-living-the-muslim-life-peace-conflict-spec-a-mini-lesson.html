The big picture

What does this topic cover?

Edexcel Spec A studies Islam across four linked areas. Each area is founded on Muslim beliefs about Allah and the guidance of the Qur'an:

  • Beliefs & teachings — Tawhid, the articles of faith, prophethood, angels, holy books, al-Qadr and akhirah.
  • Crime & punishment — justice, the aims of punishment, forgiveness and treatment of criminals.
  • Living the Muslim life — the Five Pillars, the Ten Obligatory Acts, prayer and festivals.
  • Peace & conflict — peace, justice, greater and lesser jihad, pacifism and reconciliation.

Spec focus: Islam is strictly monotheistic. Muslims believe in one God (Allah) who has no partners — the belief in Allah's oneness is called Tawhid and underpins everything else in the course.

Key vocabulary

Words you must be able to define

  • Tawhid — the belief in the oneness and unity of Allah; the most important belief in Islam.
  • Risalah — prophethood; the way Allah's message is sent through prophets, ending with Muhammad.
  • Akhirah — belief in life after death, including the Day of Judgement, Paradise and Jahannam.
  • Al-Qadr — belief that Allah has predestined/decreed all that happens (emphasised in Sunni Islam).
  • Jihad — struggle: the greater jihad is the inner struggle to obey Allah; the lesser jihad is the outward, military struggle under strict conditions.

Common mix-up: the greater jihad (personal, inner struggle to follow Allah) is the more important one. Jihad is not the same as terrorism — the lesser jihad is tightly limited and never permits harming innocents.

Beliefs & teachings

The foundations of Muslim belief

Sunni Muslims hold to six articles of faith; Shia Muslims frame their beliefs as the five roots of Usul ad-Din. Both share belief in the oneness of Allah (Tawhid) and in life after death.

Qur'an: "Say, He is Allah, [who is] One." (Surah 112:1) — a clear statement of Tawhid at the heart of every belief a Muslim holds.
Watch out

Jihad does not mean terrorism

A very common exam error is to say jihad means "holy war" or terrorism. This is wrong. Jihad means struggle. The greater jihad — the inner, personal struggle to follow Allah and resist temptation — is the more important. The lesser jihad (military struggle) is only permitted under strict conditions and never allows harming innocent people.

Say it like this: "Jihad means struggle. The greater jihad is the personal effort to obey Allah; the lesser jihad is strictly limited self-defence — it is not the same as terrorism, which Islam condemns." That sentence protects you from the most common mistake in this topic.

The nature of Allah

Tawhid — the oneness of Allah

Islam is strictly monotheistic. Its central belief is Tawhid: the absolute oneness and unity of Allah. Allah has no partners and is unlike anything in creation. Muslims describe Allah as immanent (close and involved in the world) and transcendent (beyond human understanding), as well as merciful, just and the Creator of all — as expressed in Surah al-Fatihah, the opening chapter of the Qur'an.

Scripture: "Say, He is Allah, [who is] One." (Qur'an, Surah 112:1) — a clear statement of Tawhid.

Beliefs & teachings

Akhirah and al-Qadr

Two key beliefs shape how Muslims live now. Akhirah is belief in life after death: on the Day of Judgement Allah will resurrect and judge every person, sending them to Paradise (Jannah) or hell (Jahannam) according to their deeds and faith.

Qur'an: "Every soul will taste death, and you will only be given your [full] compensation on the Day of Resurrection." (Surah 3:185) — belief in accountability before Allah.

Al-Qadr is belief that Allah has decreed all that happens (strongly emphasised in Sunni Islam). Because Muslims believe they will be judged, and that Allah knows all, they respond by living morally, obeying Allah and taking responsibility for their free choices.

Crime & punishment

The aims of punishment

Islam teaches that justice matters to Allah, so when crimes are committed, punishment has clear aims. The four you must know are:

  • Deterrence — to put people off committing crimes.
  • Retribution — a fair, proportionate response so the criminal pays for the wrong.
  • Reformation — to help the criminal change and become a better person.
  • Protection — to keep society and its members safe.

Many Muslims stress reformation and forgiveness: Allah is merciful, so criminals should be treated justly and given the chance to repent. Views on the death penalty differ — some Muslims accept it for the most serious crimes, while others argue mercy and reform should come first.

Careful: saying "Islam is only about harsh punishment" is inaccurate — mercy, forgiveness and justice are central, and the treatment of criminals must be fair.

Peace & conflict

Peace, justice and reconciliation

Islam highly values peace — the word "Islam" is linked to peace and submission to Allah. Muslims believe Allah wants people to live in harmony, so they promote justice, forgiveness and reconciliation (restoring good relationships after conflict).

Muslims teach that conflict should be avoided where possible, and some Muslims are pacifists who refuse to fight at all. Others accept that lesser jihad (military struggle) may be justified only in self-defence and under strict rules.

Misconception: Islam is not a violent religion — it teaches peace, mercy and forgiveness. Fighting is only ever permitted as a last resort, for a just cause, and never against innocent civilians.

Peace & conflict

Lesser jihad — the strict conditions

The lesser jihad is the outward, military struggle. It is only permitted under strict conditions, and Muslims stress it is far less important than the greater (inner) jihad. Its rules include:

  • It must be declared by a proper authority, not by individuals.
  • There must be a just cause — usually self-defence or defending the faith.
  • Innocent civilians must not be harmed.
  • The force used must be proportionate, and it must be a last resort.

Key point: Because these conditions are so strict, terrorism — which deliberately harms innocent people — can never count as jihad. Islam condemns it.

Prayer & festivals

Jummah and the Islamic festivals

Alongside the daily Salah, Muslims gather for Jummah — the Friday congregational prayer at the mosque, which includes a sermon (khutbah). The specification also covers three festivals:

  • Id-ul-Fitr — celebrates the end of Ramadan and the completion of the fast.
  • Id-ul-Adha — the "festival of sacrifice", recalling Ibrahim's willingness to obey Allah; it comes at the end of Hajj.
  • Ashura — especially important for Shia Muslims, remembering the martyrdom of Husayn at Karbala.

These occasions build the community (ummah) and strengthen a Muslim's devotion to Allah.

Memory hook: Id-ul-Fitr = Fast finished (Ramadan); Id-ul-Adha = Abraham/Ibrahim's sacrifice (end of Hajj).

Why it matters

How beliefs shape the Muslim life

Exam questions often ask about the impact of beliefs. In Islam, beliefs are not just ideas — they shape how Muslims live:

  • Belief in Tawhid leads to worship of Allah alone through the Five Pillars.
  • Belief in akhirah (judgement) and al-Qadr encourages honest, moral behaviour, since Allah sees and judges all.
  • Belief that Allah is merciful and just shapes attitudes to crime — favouring fairness, forgiveness and reform.
  • Belief in the greater jihad drives the daily struggle to resist temptation and follow Allah.
  • Belief in peace and the strict limits on lesser jihad lead many Muslims to promote justice, reconciliation and even pacifism.

Recap

The key points to know

Beliefs: Tawhid — the oneness of Allah; six articles / five roots of Usul ad-Din.

Prophethood: risalah — a line of prophets ending with Muhammad, the final prophet.

Akhirah: Day of Judgement; Paradise (Jannah) or hell (Jahannam); al-Qadr (Allah's decree).

Crime & punishment: justice, forgiveness; aims = deterrence, retribution, reformation, protection.

Five Pillars: Shahadah, Salah, Zakah, Sawm, Hajj (+ Ten Obligatory Acts in Shia Islam).

Peace & conflict: greater jihad (inner, more important) vs lesser jihad (strictly-limited); jihad ≠ terrorism.
