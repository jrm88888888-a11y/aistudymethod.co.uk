Mission 1

Mission 1: How could you test whether a crown is pure gold without damaging it?

Make a guess: How could you test whether a crown is pure gold without damaging it?

Mission 1

Density

  • Density = mass per unit volume: ρ = m/V.
  • SI unit: kg m⁻³. Also common: g cm⁻³.
  • 1 g cm⁻³ = 1000 kg m⁻³. Water: about 1.0 g cm⁻³ = 1000 kg m⁻³.
  • Density belongs to the material, not the object. Cut a block in half: half the mass, half the volume, same ρ.
  • Rearranged: m = ρV and V = m/ρ.
Quick check
?The density of aluminium is 2.7 g cm⁻³. What is the volume, in m³, of an aluminium block of mass 0.081 kg?
Mission 1

Worked example

  • A brass cylinder: radius 1.0 cm, height 5.0 cm, mass 133 g.
  • V = πr²h = π × 1.0² × 5.0 = 15.7 cm³.
  • ρ = m/V = 133 ÷ 15.7 = 8.5 g cm⁻³.
  • In SI: 8.5 g cm⁻³ × 1000 = 8.5 × 10³ kg m⁻³.
  • Convert at the end, or work in kg and m³ throughout. Never mix g with m³.
Mission 1

Measuring density

  • Mass: top-pan balance (zeroed).
  • Regular solid: measure dimensions with vernier callipers or a micrometer; repeat at several places; calculate V.
  • Irregular solid: sink it in a displacement can or measuring cylinder; V = volume of water displaced.
  • Liquid: mass of an empty measuring cylinder, then with liquid added; read V at the bottom of the meniscus, eye level.
  • Better: plot mass against volume for several readings. Gradient = ρ.
  • Cube of side L: % uncertainty in ρ = % in m + 3 × % in L.
Quick check
?A student pours a liquid into a beaker in steps. After each step she records the volume V of liquid in the beaker and the total mass m of the beaker and liquid. The graph shows her results. What is the mass of 400 cm³ of this liquid?
Mission check

?A coin that looks like silver has a mass of 26.9 g. Lowered into a measuring cylinder of water, it raises the reading by 3.0 cm³. The table gives densities in kg m⁻³: aluminium 2.70 × 10³; copper 8.96 × 10³; silver 10.5 × 10³; lead 11.3 × 10³. Which metal is the coin most likely made of?

Same idea for the crown: find its mass, find its volume by displacement, and compare ρ = m/V with gold's 19.3 × 10³ kg m⁻³. No damage needed.

Mission 2

Mission 2: A steel ship floats, but a steel bolt sinks. What decides which?

Make a guess: A steel ship floats, but a steel bolt sinks. What decides which?

Mission 2

Archimedes' principle

  • A fluid pushes up on an object in it: upthrust.
  • Archimedes' principle: it equals the weight of the fluid displaced.
  • Upthrust = ρVg: ρ = density of the fluid; V = volume of fluid displaced (the submerged volume).
Quick check
?A solid steel sphere and a solid aluminium sphere have the same volume. Both hang from threads, completely under water: the steel sphere 0.20 m below the surface and the aluminium sphere 0.50 m below it. Steel is about three times as dense as aluminium. How do the upthrusts on the two spheres compare?
Mission 2

Float or sink?

  • Fully submerged object, volume V: upward force ρfluid Vg; weight ρobject Vg.
  • ρobject > ρfluid: weight wins. It sinks.
  • ρobject < ρfluid: it rises until only part is submerged, then floats.
  • Floating: upward force = weight. It displaces its own weight of fluid.
  • Fraction submerged = ρobject / ρfluid.
  • A steel ship is mostly air inside: its average density is less than water's.
Mission 2

Worked example

  • A 2.0 kg stone, volume 8.0 × 10⁻⁴ m³, hangs from a spring balance, fully under water (ρ = 1000 kg m⁻³).
  • Upthrust = ρVg = 1000 × 8.0 × 10⁻⁴ × 9.81 = 7.8 N. Weight = 2.0 × 9.81 = 19.6 N.
  • Balance reading = 19.6 − 7.8 = 11.8 N.
  • The stone feels lighter in water, but its weight has not changed.
Quick check
?A foam ball of volume V would float, but it is held at rest completely under water by a light string tied to the bottom of a tank. The density of the foam is a quarter of the density ρ of water. Which expression gives the tension in the string?
Mission check

?A wooden block floats in water (density 1000 kg m⁻³) with 60% of its volume below the surface. It is then placed in oil of density 800 kg m⁻³. What fraction of its volume is below the surface of the oil?

Floating or sinking is set by density: the ship's steel hull encloses so much air that its average density is below water's, so it floats once it has displaced its own weight of water. A solid bolt cannot.

Make a guess: Why does fine silt keep water cloudy for hours, when sand settles in seconds?

Mission 3

Viscous drag and Stokes' law

  • Viscosity η: a fluid's resistance to flow; internal friction between layers. Unit: Pa s (N s m⁻²).
  • Honey: high η. Water: low η (about 1 × 10⁻³ Pa s at room temperature).
  • A sphere moving through a fluid feels a viscous drag force opposing its motion.
  • Stokes' law: F = 6πηrv (r = radius of the sphere, v = its speed).
  • Drag ∝ r and ∝ v.
Quick check
?Sphere X moves through an oil at speed v and feels a viscous drag F. Sphere Y, with twice the radius of X, moves through the same oil at half the speed. Stokes' law applies. What is the viscous drag on Y?
Mission 3

Terminal velocity of a sphere

  • Three forces on a falling sphere: weight (down), upthrust (up), viscous drag (up).
  • As it speeds up, drag grows. At terminal velocity: weight = upthrust + drag. Resultant force = 0.
  • (4/3)πr³ρs g = (4/3)πr³ρf g + 6πηrv
  • Rearranged: v = 2r²g(ρs − ρf) / 9η.
  • v ∝ r²: halve the radius, and the terminal velocity is a quarter.
Mission 3

Worked example

  • Steel ball bearing, radius 1.5 mm, ρs = 7800 kg m⁻³, falling in oil: ρf = 920 kg m⁻³, η = 0.85 Pa s.
  • Weight = (4/3)π(1.5 × 10⁻³)³ × 7800 × 9.81 = 1.08 × 10⁻³ N. Upthrust = 1.3 × 10⁻⁴ N.
  • At terminal velocity, drag = 1.08 × 10⁻³ − 1.3 × 10⁻⁴ = 9.5 × 10⁻⁴ N.
  • v = F/6πηr = 9.5 × 10⁻⁴ ÷ (6π × 0.85 × 1.5 × 10⁻³) = 0.040 m s⁻¹.
Quick check
?A small aluminium sphere of radius 0.50 mm falls at terminal velocity through glycerol. The density of aluminium is 2.7 × 10³ kg m⁻³. The density of glycerol is 1.26 × 10³ kg m⁻³ and its viscosity is 1.4 Pa s. Take g = 9.81 m s⁻². What is the terminal velocity of the sphere?
Mission 3

When Stokes' law works

  • Only for small spheres moving at low speeds.
  • The flow round the sphere must be laminar: smooth layers, no eddies.
  • Faster or larger objects cause turbulent flow: swirling eddies. Drag is then much larger, and Stokes' law fails.
  • Viscosity depends strongly on temperature. Most liquids get less viscous when warmer: warm syrup pours easily.
  • So always measure and state the temperature with a viscosity value.
Quick check
?A student measures the viscosity of an oil by the falling-ball method on a warm day, with the oil at 28 °C. Her value is lower than the data-book value, which is quoted for 20 °C. Which is the most likely explanation?
Mission 3

Core practical 4: falling-ball method

  • Tall, wide tube of the liquid. Rubber bands round it at measured intervals, the first well below the surface.
  • Measure ball diameter with a micrometer; release it at the centre of the surface.
  • Time it between bands (stopwatch, or video frame by frame). Equal times for equal distances → terminal velocity reached.
  • Measure ρs, ρf and the liquid's temperature.
  • η = 2r²g(ρs − ρf) / 9v. Better: plot v against r² for several balls. Gradient = 2g(ρs − ρf)/9η.
Quick check
?A student times steel balls (density 7800 kg m⁻³) of several radii r falling at terminal velocity v through an oil of density 900 kg m⁻³. Her graph of v against r² is a straight line through the origin with gradient 2.5 × 10⁴ m⁻¹ s⁻¹. Take g = 9.81 m s⁻². What is the viscosity of the oil?
Mission check

?Two water droplets in fog fall at terminal velocity through still air. Droplet P has twice the radius of droplet Q. Stokes' law applies to both. What is the terminal velocity of P divided by the terminal velocity of Q?

v ∝ r². A silt grain 1/100 the radius of a sand grain settles 10 000 times more slowly: seconds for sand become hours for silt.

Make a guess: Why are the markings on a spring weighing scale evenly spaced?

Mission 4

Hooke's law

  • Stretch a spring: the force it exerts is proportional to its extension.
  • This holds only up to a limit.
  • Extension = stretched length − original (natural) length. Not the whole length.
  • ΔF = kΔx (formulae sheet: F = kΔx). k is the stiffness of the object.
  • Unit of k: N m⁻¹. Larger k: stiffer spring.
  • Works for compression too: the extension is then the decrease in length.
Quick check
?In a lab, a student models a spring as a light spring that obeys Hooke's law. Which statement describes how the spring's force behaves in this model?
Mission 4

Worked example

  • A spring hangs with natural length 12.0 cm. A 4.0 N weight stretches it to 17.0 cm.
  • Extension = 17.0 − 12.0 = 5.0 cm = 0.050 m.
  • k = force ÷ extension = 4.0 ÷ 0.050 = 80 N m⁻¹.
  • Hang 6.0 N instead: extension = 6.0 ÷ 80 = 0.075 m = 7.5 cm.
  • New length = 12.0 + 7.5 = 19.5 cm. Always add the extension back to the natural length.
Mission 4

The force–extension graph

  • Plot force against extension. Hooke's law: a straight line through the origin.
  • Gradient = k.
  • Beyond the limit of proportionality the line curves.
  • Below the elastic limit: remove the load and it returns to its original length.
  • Beyond the elastic limit: it is permanently stretched.
Quick check
?The graph shows the magnitude F of the force exerted by a light spring that obeys Hooke's law, as a function of the spring's length L, from its natural length to a length of 20 cm. What is the spring constant of the spring?
Mission check

?A spring that obeys Hooke's law is 15.0 cm long when it supports a 3.0 N load and 18.0 cm long when it supports a 6.0 N load. What is its natural (unloaded) length?

Extension ∝ force, so each extra newton adds the same extension: evenly spaced markings.

As long as the spring stays within its limit of proportionality.

Mission 5

Mission 5: A thin steel wire and a thick steel cable carry the same load. Why does the wire stretch more?

Make a guess: A thin steel wire and a thick steel cable carry the same load. Why does the wire stretch more?

Mission 5

Stress and strain

  • Tensile stress = force ÷ cross-sectional area. Unit: Pa (N m⁻²).
  • Tensile strain = extension ÷ original length. No unit (a ratio).
  • σ = F/A; ε = Δx/x.
  • Compressive stress and strain: the same, for a squashing force and a decrease in length.
  • Same load on a thinner wire → larger stress.
Mission 5

The Young modulus

  • Young modulus = tensile stress ÷ tensile strain. Unit: Pa.
  • E = σ/ε, which gives E = Fx/AΔx.
  • Valid only where Hooke's law holds (up to the limit of proportionality).
  • It is the gradient of the straight part of a stress–strain graph.
  • It belongs to the material. The spring constant (Young modulus × area ÷ length) belongs to the object.
  • Steel: about 2 × 10¹¹ Pa. Rubber: about 10⁶ Pa.
Mission 5

Worked example

  • Steel wire: length 2.50 m, diameter 0.40 mm, load 20 N, E = 2.0 × 10¹¹ Pa. Extension?
  • A = π(d/2)² = π(0.20 × 10⁻³)² = 1.26 × 10⁻⁷ m². (Radius, not diameter.)
  • Stress = F/A = 20 ÷ 1.26 × 10⁻⁷ = 1.6 × 10⁸ Pa.
  • Strain = stress/E = 1.6 × 10⁸ ÷ 2.0 × 10¹¹ = 8.0 × 10⁻⁴.
  • Extension = strain × original length = 8.0 × 10⁻⁴ × 2.50 = 2.0 × 10⁻³ m (2.0 mm).
Quick check
?A metal wire of length 1.80 m and diameter 0.30 mm is loaded, and a graph of force F against extension is plotted. The straight part of the graph has a gradient of 7.9 × 10³ N m⁻¹. What is the Young modulus of the metal?
Mission 5

Measuring the Young modulus

  • A long, thin wire: a large extension from a modest load, so its percentage uncertainty is small.
  • Clamp one end. Run it over a pulley, or hang it beside an identical reference wire (Searle's apparatus).
  • Diameter: micrometer, several places and directions; average. Length: metre rule to the marker.
  • Add masses in steps; read the extension from a marker against a scale (or a vernier). Unload to check it returns: still elastic.
  • Plot force against extension. Young modulus = gradient × original length ÷ area. (Or plot stress against strain: gradient = Young modulus.)
  • The reference wire cancels temperature changes and sag of the support. Wear eye protection: the wire may snap.
Quick check
?A student repeats the Young modulus experiment with the same loads on a wire of the same metal and diameter, but only one tenth as long. The extension is read with the same scale. What is the main effect?
Mission check

?Two cables are made of the same aluminium alloy and carry the same load. Cable Q is 3 times as long as cable P and has 3 times its diameter. Both obey Hooke's law. What is the extension of Q divided by the extension of P?

Same steel means the same Young modulus. The thin wire has a smaller area, so the same load gives a larger stress, so a larger strain and a larger extension.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Bend a paperclip a little and it springs back. Bend it further and it stays bent. Why?

Make a guess: Bend a paperclip a little and it springs back. Bend it further and it stays bent. Why?

Mission 6

Force–extension graph of a metal wire

  • Limit of proportionality (P): up to here, extension ∝ force (Hooke's law). Straight line.
  • Elastic limit (EL): the largest force after which the wire still returns to its original length.
  • Yield point (Y): the wire suddenly extends a lot for little extra force; plastic flow begins.
  • Beyond the elastic limit: plastic region. Then the wire breaks.
  • A force–compression graph has the same features for a squashing force.
Mission 6

Elastic and plastic

  • Elastic deformation: removed load → returns to its original length.
  • Plastic deformation: removed load → permanent extension.
  • The limit of proportionality and the elastic limit are different points.
  • A metal loaded into the plastic region unloads along a line parallel to the original straight line. It ends up permanently longer.
Quick check
?A wire is loaded until its force–extension graph has just started to curve. The load is then removed, and the wire returns exactly to its original length. What can you conclude about the load?
Mission 6

Three kinds of stress–strain curve

  • Ductile (copper, mild steel): straight, then a long plastic region. Can be drawn into wires. Thins (necks) before it breaks.
  • Brittle (glass, cast iron): straight right up to fracture. No plastic region. Snaps suddenly.
  • Polymeric (rubber): low stiffness, very large strain, curved. (Polythene: stretches plastically, cold-drawing.)
  • Ultimate tensile strength (UTS): the highest stress on the curve.
  • Breaking stress: the stress at which it fractures.
Quick check
?Two materials are tested to destruction. Material X: the stress–strain graph is straight all the way to fracture, at a strain of 0.001. Material Y: the graph is straight to a strain of 0.002, then curves and continues to a strain of 0.40 before fracture. Which describes the materials?
Mission check

?A copper wire is loaded beyond its elastic limit. At the maximum load of 30 N its extension is 6.0 mm. The straight first part of its force–extension graph has a gradient of 20 N per mm. The load is then removed completely. What is the permanent extension of the wire?

A small bend stays below the elastic limit: elastic, so the clip springs back. A bigger bend passes the elastic limit: the deformation becomes plastic, and a permanent bend remains.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Stretch a spring twice as far. Does it store twice the energy?

Make a guess: Stretch a spring twice as far. Does it store twice the energy?

Mission 7

Work done stretching = area

  • Stretching a spring or wire takes work: force × distance moved.
  • The force is not constant: it rises from 0 to its final value as the extension grows from 0.
  • Work done = area under the force–extension graph.
  • In the elastic region this work is stored as elastic strain energy (also called elastic potential energy). Unload, and you get it back.
Mission 7

Energy stored = ½ × force × extension

  • Hooke's law region: the graph is a straight line, so the area is a triangle.
  • ΔEel = ½FΔx (F = final force, Δx = extension).
  • Use F = kΔx: ΔEel = ½(kΔx)Δx = ½k(Δx)².
  • Double the extension → four times the energy.
Mission 7

Worked example

  • Spring: k = 80 N m⁻¹, stretched by 0.050 m.
  • Force: F = kΔx = 80 × 0.050 = 4.0 N.
  • Energy: ΔEel = ½FΔx = ½ × 4.0 × 0.050 = 0.10 J.
  • Check: ½k(Δx)² = ½ × 80 × 0.050² = 0.10 J.
  • Stretched to 0.10 m: ½ × 80 × 0.10² = 0.40 J. Twice the extension, four times the energy.
  • Not ½ × 4.0 × 0.10: the force is now 8.0 N, not 4.0 N.
Quick check
?A steel wire obeys Hooke's law. A tension of 60 N gives an extension of 1.2 mm. How much elastic strain energy does the wire store?
Mission 7

Curved graphs: use the area

  • Beyond the limit of proportionality the graph curves. ½ × force × extension no longer works.
  • Find the area: count squares, or split it into triangles and trapezia.
  • Energy of one square = (force per square) × (extension per square).
  • Plastic region (the sample stays permanently stretched when unloaded): the work done is not all stored. Most becomes internal energy (the sample warms); the wire stays longer.
Quick check
?The graph shows an idealised force–extension graph for a metal wire. The force rises linearly from 0 to 30 N over the first 4.0 mm, then stays at 30 N as the extension increases to 10.0 mm. How much work is done stretching the wire to 10.0 mm?
Mission 7

Spring energy becomes kinetic and potential energy

  • A launcher spring, k = 200 N m⁻¹, compressed by 0.050 m, fires a 0.020 kg ball.
  • Stored: ΔEel = ½k(Δx)² = ½ × 200 × 0.050² = 0.25 J.
  • Fired horizontally, all to kinetic energy: ½mv² = 0.25 → v = √(2 × 0.25 ÷ 0.020) = 5.0 m s⁻¹.
  • Fired straight up, all to gravitational potential energy at the top: Δh = 0.25 ÷ (0.020 × 9.81) = 1.3 m above the release point.
  • Real launchers lose some energy to friction and air resistance: the ball rises less.
Quick check
?The spring in a toy dart launcher has a spring constant of 400 N m⁻¹. It is compressed by 0.030 m and then released, firing a dart of mass 0.012 kg horizontally. All the elastic potential energy is transferred to the dart's kinetic energy. What is the speed of the dart?
Mission check

?A spring obeys Hooke's law. When it is compressed by 3.0 cm it stores 0.45 J. How much energy does it store when it is compressed by 4.5 cm?

Energy = ½k × (extension)², so twice the stretch stores four times the energy: twice the extension, at twice the final force.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • ρ = m/V, in kg m⁻³ (1 g cm⁻³ = 1000 kg m⁻³); density belongs to the material, not the object.
  • Measure m with a balance and V from dimensions or displacement; the gradient of m against V gives ρ.

Mission 2

  • Upthrust = weight of fluid displaced = ρVg (ρ of the fluid, V displaced).
  • An object floats if its average density is less than the fluid's; a floating object displaces its own weight.

Mission 3

  • Stokes' law F = 6πηrv: small spheres, low speed, laminar flow; η falls as a liquid warms.
  • At terminal velocity weight = upthrust + drag, so v = 2r²g(ρs − ρf)/9η ∝ r².

Mission 4

  • Extension (not length) is proportional to force: gradient of the force–extension graph = k, in N m⁻¹.
  • This holds up to the limit of proportionality.
  • Beyond the elastic limit the stretch is permanent.

Mission 5

  • Stress = F/A (Pa); strain = extension/original length (no unit); Young modulus = stress/strain, the gradient of the linear part of a stress–strain graph.
  • Measure the Young modulus with a long, thin wire: Young modulus = gradient of force against extension × original length ÷ area.

Mission 6

  • Limit of proportionality: end of the straight line. Elastic limit: beyond it, deformation is plastic and permanent.
  • Ductile: long plastic region; brittle: straight to fracture; polymeric: large strain, low stiffness.
  • UTS = maximum stress on the stress–strain curve.

Mission 7

  • Elastic strain energy = area under the force–extension graph: ΔEel = ½FΔx = ½k(Δx)² while Hooke's law holds; for a curved graph, find the area.
