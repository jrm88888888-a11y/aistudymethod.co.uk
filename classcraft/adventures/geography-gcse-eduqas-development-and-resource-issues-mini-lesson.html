Mission 1

What is development?

  • Development: improvement in people's lives and in a country's economy.
  • Economic view: how much wealth and income a country has.
  • Social view: health, education, housing, food and water.
  • Political view: freedom, rights and how honestly a country is governed.
Mission 1

Classifying countries

  • The World Bank groups countries by GNI per head (2025 data, used from July 2026):
    • low income: $1,175 or less · lower-middle: $1,176–$4,635 · upper-middle: $4,636–$14,375 · high: more than $14,375
  • The UK is high income, India lower-middle income and Rwanda low income.
Mission 1

Economic measures

  • GDP (gross domestic product): the value of everything produced inside a country in a year.
  • GNI (gross national income): GDP plus income earned abroad by its people and firms, minus income sent out by foreign firms.
  • Divide by population to get per head (per capita) figures.
  • GNI per head, 2025: UK $54,550, India $2,760, Rwanda $1,150.

Mission 1

The Human Development Index (HDI)

  • The HDI combines three dimensions into one score from 0 to 1:
    • a long and healthy life (life expectancy)
    • knowledge (years of schooling)
    • a decent standard of living (GNI per head)
  • 2023: UK 0.946 (13th), India 0.685 (130th), Rwanda 0.578 (159th) of 193 countries.

Mission 1

Why income cannot buy a high HDI

  • In the HDI, each extra dollar counts for less and less: income is scored on a logarithmic scale, and any income above $75,000 counts the same as $75,000.
  • The three dimension scores are multiplied together (then the cube root is taken), not simply averaged. A weak score pulls the total down, and a strong one cannot fully make up for it.
  • So Guyana's high income is held back by its lower health and education scores.
Mission 1

Limitations of measures

  • Averages hide inequality: GNI per head says nothing about how income is shared.
  • Money is not well-being: Guyana's real GDP grew 43.8% in 2024 after oil and gas production began; health and schooling change much more slowly.
  • Data quality: the World Bank lists India's adult literacy as 81.7% for 2023 but 78.2% for 2024; different surveys can give different answers.
  • Missing parts: no single measure covers rights, safety or the environment; even the HDI leaves them out.
  • Single measures (GNI) are simple; composite ones (HDI) are broader but still incomplete.
Mission 1

A continuum of development

  • Countries do not fall into two boxes, "rich" and "poor". They sit along a sliding scale: a continuum.
  • GNI per head, 2025: Rwanda $1,150 · India $2,760 · UK $54,550.
  • Income groups are lines drawn across the continuum: a country can cross a line with a small change in income.

Mission 2

Where is India?

  • In South Asia: the Himalayas to the north, the Indian Ocean to the south, between the Arabian Sea and the Bay of Bengal.
  • 1.46 billion people in 2025, more than China's 1.41 billion.
  • India has about 87% of South Asia's people and 86% of its GDP (our calculation).
Mission 2

Why India matters globally

  • The world's 6th-largest economy in 2025 at market exchange rates: about US$3.9 trillion (World Bank US$3.96 trillion; IMF US$3.92 trillion), about 3% of world GDP (our calculation).
  • A member of BRICS, which it chairs in 2026.
  • Mumbai is a global financial centre: home of the Reserve Bank of India since 1937.
  • But income per person (GNI) is only US$2,760 (2025): "lower middle income".
  • India is on Eduqas's list of NICs (Appendix B).

Mission 2

Political context

  • A federal country: 36 states and union territories and 707 districts.
  • Central and state governments share power, so the same policy can work differently in different states.
  • National programmes set by the central government: e.g. Make in India (2014) to grow manufacturing.
  • Member of BRICS: links to Brazil, China, Russia, South Africa and others.
Mission 2

Social and cultural context

  • Young: 24% of people were under 15 in 2025.
  • Smaller families: about 2.0 births per woman (2024), down from 3.7 in 1995.
  • Still mostly rural: 36% lived in towns and cities in 2025.
  • Education: 76% of adults could read in 2022.
  • Culture: a mix of influences, e.g. Mumbai's World Heritage buildings "blend Indian design with Art Deco imagery".
Mission 2

Environmental context

  • Monsoon: most rain falls in a few months. At Patna, 76% of the Ganges' yearly flow came in the monsoon months (1980).
  • Farming needs water: 90% of India's water withdrawals go to agriculture (2022).
  • Cyclones hit the coasts; October–December is the main cyclone season.

Society: smaller families, healthier children

  • Births per woman: 8.1 (1975) → 3.6 (2024).
  • Children dying before age 5: 245 per 1,000 (1975) → 38 (2024).
  • Adults who can read: 58% (1991) → 79% (2022).
  • Society still matters for development: 37% of people were aged 0–14 in 2025, so schools and jobs must keep growing.
Mission 3

Environment: hills, land and power

  • Landlocked: imports and exports cross other countries to reach the sea, which adds cost.
  • Hilly and crowded: about 600 people per km² (2024); farmland is scarce.
  • Natural assets: volcanoes, 23 lakes and rivers that feed the Nile; mountain gorillas draw tourists.
  • Energy from the landscape: Lake Kivu holds methane gas; since 2016 KivuWatt has used it to generate 26 MW.
Mission 3

Types of aid

  • Emergency aid: short-term help after a disaster or conflict. In 1994, after the genocide, total aid was worth 95% of Rwanda's national income; much of it was emergency help.
  • Long-term development aid: builds health, schools, power. In 2023 Rwanda received US$1.39 billion, 10% of national income.
  • Government aid: from one government (bilateral) or pooled through bodies such as the Global Fund (multilateral).
  • NGO aid: from charities and non-profit groups.
  • Development targets: aid is often measured against the UN's 17 Sustainable Development Goals (2030).
Mission 3

Can aid reduce inequality?

  • Government aid: the US government's PEPFAR programme and the Global Fund paid for HIV treatment; the Ministry of Health began in rural districts first.
  • People on HIV treatment: fewer than 150 outside Kigali by 2003; 114,600 in 2012.
  • NGO aid: One Acre Fund began in Rwanda in 2007 with 38 farm families; it now reports serving over 2.7 million farmers, with seed, fertiliser and training.
  • Result: people below the US$3-a-day line fell from 82% (2000) to 39% (2023); the Gini index of inequality fell from 48.5 to 39.4.
  • Caution: aid is one cause among many; these figures do not separate its effect from growth and government policy.

Mission 4

The development gap

  • The development gap is the difference in wealth and wellbeing between the richest and poorest countries.
  • UNDP's Human Development Index (2023): Iceland is top at 0.972; South Sudan is bottom of 193 countries at 0.388.
  • Life expectancy at birth: Norway 83.3 years, Somalia 58.8, a gap of about 24.5 years (our calculation).
Mission 4

Physical factors

  • Landlocked: no coast, so trade goes through neighbours. Landlocked developing countries paid $3,204 to export a container against $1,268 for transit countries (World Bank data, 2014).
  • The UN estimates their development is "about 20 percent lower than it would be were they not landlocked".
  • Disease: in 2024 the WHO African Region had 95% of the world's malaria cases and deaths. Illness keeps children out of school and adults out of work.
  • Hazards and climate: drought, floods and tropical storms destroy crops, homes and roads again and again.
Mission 4

Historical factors

  • Colonialism: from the 1500s to the mid-1900s, European powers ruled much of Africa, Asia and the Americas.
  • Colonies were organised to export raw materials (cotton, rubber, minerals) to the ruling country, which made the profitable goods.
  • Railways and ports often ran from mines and plantations to the coast, not between towns.
  • Many countries became independent with few factories, few trained officials and borders drawn by others.
  • These links are geo-political relationships: old ties still shape who trades with whom.
Quick check
?Which is a physical (environmental) factor that holds back development?
Mission 4

Economic: relying on primary products

  • Primary products: raw materials such as crops, minerals and oil, sold unprocessed.
  • UNCTAD counts a country as commodity-dependent when commodities make up more than 60% of its goods exports: 95 of 142 developing countries were, in 2019–2021. In Africa it was 45 of 53.
  • Raw materials earn less than processed goods, and their prices rise and fall sharply, so income is hard to plan.
  • Exports earn money; imports cost money. A country that exports cheap raw materials and imports expensive manufactured goods can run short of money.
Mission 4

Debt

  • Poor countries borrow from banks, governments and international lenders to build roads, dams and schools.
  • If export earnings fall, repayments and interest take money that could pay for health and education.
  • The HIPC debt-relief scheme: of 39 eligible countries, 36 had completed it by February 2023; Somalia became the 37th in December 2023.
  • Debt can trap a country in poverty (a poverty trap), because each year's repayments leave less to invest.
Mission 4

Trade and geo-political relationships

  • Trade is buying (imports) and selling (exports) goods and services between countries.
  • Powerful countries and companies set many of the rules and prices of world trade.
  • Geo-political relationships: alliances, former colonial ties and aid agreements decide who can sell where, and on what terms.
Mission 4

Trade blocs

  • A trade bloc is a group of countries that agree to trade with fewer barriers between them.
  • The EU single market allows "free movement of goods, services, capital and persons" between its members.
  • Members gain easy access to each other's markets. Countries outside may face tariffs (taxes on imports) or rules that make selling harder.
  • Exam move: explain how a trade bloc helps its members and how it can disadvantage poorer countries outside it.
Quick check
?Which sequence best describes a poverty trap?
Mission check

?A landlocked country earns most of its export income from one crop, coffee. The world coffee price halves, and the country owes large debts in US dollars. What is the most likely result?

Uneven development has many linked causes: physical (landlocked, disease, hazards), historical (colonialism) and economic (primary products, debt, trade). They add up, which is why a baby in Somalia expects to live about 24.5 years less than one in Norway.

Mission 5

Protectionism

  • Protectionism: a government protects its own producers from foreign competition.
  • Tariff: a tax on imports, which makes them dearer.
  • Quota: a limit on the amount of a good that can be imported.
  • Subsidy: government money paid to home producers, so they can sell more cheaply.
  • A country can also ban imports or exports.
Mission 5

Indonesia closes the door

  • Indonesia banned exports of raw nickel ore from January 2020, reportedly to keep ore for its own processing industry.
  • Nickel is used in stainless steel and electric-vehicle batteries.
  • Indonesia's share of world nickel mining (USGS estimates): about 30% in 2019, two-thirds in 2025.
Mission 5

Who gained, who lost?

  • Indonesia gained output and processing jobs at home.
  • Other producers lost: prices fell, and Australia's production dropped by an estimated 54% in 2025 as mines closed.
  • The EU took the ban to the WTO, whose panel ruled against it in 2022; Indonesia appealed, and the case has no final ruling.
Quick check
?A government allows only 10,000 tonnes of foreign steel into the country each year. Which kind of protectionism is this?
Mission 5

Rwanda (LIC): a tariff on used clothes

  • In 2016 the East African Community, which includes Rwanda, announced it would phase in a ban on imports of used clothing and footwear by 2019.
  • Rwanda kept a policy that raised tariffs on imported used clothing and footwear "by more than one thousand percent", which the USA said effectively banned them.
  • A tariff like this aims to help local clothing makers by making cheap imported clothes dearer.
Mission 5

Consequences for Rwanda

  • Rwanda's imports of worn clothing halved: about $30.5 million in 2016, $15.3 million in 2017.
  • The USA said the policy broke the rules of its AGOA trade deal, and in July 2018 it suspended duty-free entry for Rwandan clothing.
  • The USA said this affected imports worth $1.5 million in 2017, about 3% of Rwanda's exports to the USA.
  • Imports of worn clothing were back to about $32.4 million in 2023; the data do not show why.
Quick check

Mission 5

India (NIC): when quotas ended

  • From 1974, the Multifibre Arrangement let importing countries set quotas on textiles and clothing from individual exporting countries.
  • These quotas ended on 1 January 2005.
  • India's clothing exports rose from about $6.4 billion in 2004 to $8.2 billion in 2005: up about 28% in one year.
  • So quotas imposed by richer importing countries had limited a key industry in a NIC.
Mission 5

Subsidies

  • A subsidy lets home producers sell more cheaply, at home or abroad.
  • In richer countries: subsidised farm goods can undercut farmers in LICs and NICs, at home and in export markets.
  • In LICs and NICs: governments may subsidise a new industry until it can compete.
  • Either way, someone pays: taxpayers fund the subsidy, and unsubsidised producers elsewhere lose sales.
Mission 5

Weighing the consequences

  • Who can gain: protected producers and their workers (Indonesia's nickel processing; Rwanda's intended clothing makers).
  • Who can lose: consumers paying higher prices; producers elsewhere (Australia's nickel mines); exporters hit by retaliation (Rwanda's clothing exports to the USA).
  • Development: protection can build industries in LICs and NICs, but barriers set by rich importers (quotas, subsidies) can hold their industries back (India's textiles before 2005).
  • Exam move: for each tool, name the country, the gainers, the losers and the evidence.
Quick check
?Which statement about India's clothing exports is best supported by the figures for 2004 and 2005?
Mission check

?Country Q, an LIC, sets a quota on imported rice to protect its own rice farmers. Who is most likely to gain and who to lose?

When a country closes a door, protected producers gain and others lose. Indonesia's nickel share rose from about 30% to two-thirds, while higher-cost mines elsewhere closed; Rwanda's used-clothing imports halved, but its clothing lost duty-free access to the USA; India's clothing exports jumped when rich countries' quotas ended.

Mission 6

Transnational corporations

  • A transnational corporation (TNC) is a company that operates in more than one country.
  • Its headquarters is in its home country; a country where it sets up is a host country.
  • Eduqas calls these multinational companies (MNCs).
  • Foreign direct investment (FDI): a company builds or buys a business abroad and runs it.
  • Outsourcing: a company pays another firm to do part of its work, such as making its products.
Mission 6

How TNCs organise themselves

  • Headquarters, design and research usually stay in the home country: Apple is based in Cupertino, California.
  • Making the product can happen anywhere. Apple's manufacturing is mostly done by outsourcing partners in China mainland, India, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and Vietnam.
  • Toyota and its affiliates run more than 50 manufacturing operations in 27 countries and regions outside Japan.
Mission 6

Why locate in many countries?

  • Lower costs: Apple says outsourcing "can lower operating costs".
  • Near customers: in the year to March 2026 Toyota made 73.9% of the vehicles it sold outside Japan in factories outside Japan. Cars are big and heavy to ship.
  • Local supplies and money: buying parts locally matches local costs to local sales.
  • Avoiding trade barriers: Toyota notes a "significant increase" in US tariffs, including on cars, announced in 2025; making cars inside a market avoids them.
  • Government incentives such as tax breaks also attract TNCs.
Quick check

Mission 6

Apple in India: outsourcing

  • India is one of the main countries where Apple's outsourcing partners make its products.
  • The partner firms own and run the factories and employ the workers; Apple designs the products and sells them worldwide.
  • Apple says outsourcing lowers costs but "reduce[s] the Company's direct control over production".
  • Because partners work in six countries, production can shift between them.
Mission 6

Toyota in India: FDI

  • Toyota Kirloskar Motor has made cars in India since December 1999.
  • Models include the Innova, Fortuner and Urban Cruiser Hyryder, some as hybrids.
  • A Toyota group company, Toyota Kirloskar Auto Parts, has made axles and transmissions in India since 2002.
  • Toyota invests in its own factories and in nearby suppliers: its Just in Time system needs parts to arrive as they are needed.
Mission 6

FDI into India

  • Foreign direct investment into India (World Bank):
    • 2000: $3.6 billion · 2020: $64.4 billion (peak) · 2024: $27.1 billion · 2025: $40.8 billion
  • FDI grew hugely after 2000, but it rises and falls from year to year.
Mission 6

Advantages for the host country

  • Jobs and wages in factories, and in partner and supplier firms.
  • Skills and technology: workers learn new methods (for example Toyota's production system).
  • Multiplier effect: suppliers (such as parts makers) and local services grow too.
  • Exports and taxes: goods made in India are sold abroad, and firms pay tax.
Mission 6

Disadvantages for the host country

  • Profits flow back to the home country.
  • Top jobs (design, research, management) often stay at headquarters.
  • Footloose: contracts and investment can move. FDI into India fell by about 58% from 2020 to 2024.
  • Competition: local firms may struggle against large TNCs.
  • Working conditions and the environment depend on how well laws are enforced.

Mission 6

MNCs in the UK: Toyota

  • Toyota Motor Manufacturing (UK) was set up in 1989. It has two plants: Burnaston, Derbyshire (cars) and Deeside, North Wales (engines).
  • Total investment: £2.75 billion; about 3,000 workers, including agency staff.
  • Advantages: skilled jobs, training, local suppliers; the first car was built on 16 December 1992.
  • Disadvantages: decisions are made by Toyota, not the UK. Models change: Avensis production ended in July 2018.
Mission check

?Company P makes heavy washing machines. Company Q designs small wireless earbuds. Which strategies are they most likely to use?

Apple keeps design and services and outsources assembly to partners in Asia, including India: lower costs, less control. Toyota uses FDI, with its own factories in India since 1999, building cars near customers. Both bring jobs and skills to host countries, but profits and top jobs mostly stay at home, and contracts and investment can move.

Make a guess: Tourism makes up about a fifth of the Maldives' economy. Yet in 2022 most people working in its resorts were not Maldivian. Who really gains from a resort?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 6.2.1 growing economic and employment contribution of tourism; drivers of a globalised tourist industry incl. technology and communications
  • 6.2.1 impact of tourism growth on employment structures (e.g. over-dependence on tourism)
  • 6.2.1 enclave tourism and the informal economy, and their consequences
Mission 7

A growing global industry

  • About 1.52 billion international tourist arrivals were recorded in 2025, almost 60 million more than in 2024.
  • The industry body WTTC estimates travel and tourism was 9.8% of the world economy in 2025 and supported 366 million jobs, about 1 in 9 jobs.
  • WTTC speaks for tourism businesses and counts indirect effects, so its totals are at the high end.
Mission 7

What drives globalised tourism?

  • Technology and communications: online booking and reviews. Booking Holdings' platforms serve more than 220 countries and territories; 1.2 billion room nights were booked in 2025.
  • Transport: more flights and routes connect distant islands to big cities.
  • Rising incomes: UN Tourism expected "growing outbound travel from emerging markets".
  • TNCs (hotel chains, airlines, tour operators) bring money, brands and customers to new destinations.
Mission 7

Benefits of tourism

  • Economy: jobs, foreign currency, taxes for the government, and new airports and roads.
  • Multiplier effect: wages and spending circulate locally, so each tourist's money supports more local jobs.
  • People and environment: money for services and conservation. In Rwanda, from 2017, 10% of national park tourism revenue was to be shared with communities next to the parks.
Mission 7

Problems of tourism

  • Leakage: money that leaves the destination: profits to foreign owners, imported food and fuel, commission to foreign booking sites.
  • Jobs: often seasonal, low-paid, and taken by migrant workers.
  • Environment: crowds damage fragile places. Thailand closed Maya Bay in 2018 so its coral could recover from damage caused by thousands of daily visitors.
  • People: crowding, rising prices, and culture turned into a product to sell.
Quick check
?Which is an example of leakage?
Mission 7

Case study: the Maldives

  • An upper-middle-income country of small islands in the Indian Ocean.
  • 2,246,516 tourist arrivals in 2025; tourism was 20.3% of GDP. The World Bank calls it "the largest economic sector".
  • 179 resorts, 16 hotels, 973 guest houses and 168 safari boats in 2025.
Mission 7

Enclave tourism

  • Enclave tourism: tourists stay inside a self-contained resort and spend almost all their money there.
  • Example: a resort on its own island, where guests sleep, eat, shop and take trips without leaving the resort.
  • At the end of 2022, resorts employed 55,874 people: 59% foreign workers and 41% Maldivian. Women were 11% of resort workers; Maldivian women 2%.
  • Consequences: high leakage, few links to local businesses, and few jobs for local people, especially women.
  • Employment structure: a tourism economy moves jobs into the tertiary (service) sector, often away from fishing and farming.
Mission 7

Over-dependence on tourism

  • When one industry dominates, a shock to tourism hits the whole economy.
  • Tourist arrivals in the Maldives: 1,702,887 in 2019; 555,494 in 2020.
  • The World Bank warns of "potential external shocks to tourism".
  • This is over-dependence: jobs, taxes and foreign currency all rest on one sector.
Quick check
?Using the figures earlier in this mission, by what percentage did tourist arrivals in the Maldives fall from 2019 to 2020?
Mission 7

The informal economy

  • Informal economy: work that is not registered, taxed or protected by law, such as street vendors, unlicensed guides and drivers.
  • The ILO says over 60% of the world's workforce works in the informal economy.
  • Consequences: easy to enter, so poor people can earn from tourists; but no sick pay or legal protection, unsteady income, and no tax for the government.
  • Small, locally owned guest houses (973 in the Maldives in 2025) spread tourist spending more widely than enclave resorts.
Quick check
?Which judgement about tourism in the Maldives is best supported by the evidence?
Mission check

?A luxury resort on a private island in a lower-income country is owned by a foreign hotel chain. It imports most of its food, recruits most staff from abroad, and its guests rarely leave the resort. Who gains most from each dollar a guest spends?

Several groups gain, unequally. In the Maldives, tourism is about a fifth of GDP, but 59% of resort jobs in 2022 went to foreign workers and only 2% to Maldivian women. Enclave tourism and leakage limit local gains; over-dependence adds risk.

Mission 8: In 2017 Rwanda doubled the price of a permit to see mountain gorillas, to US$1,500. Can making tourism more expensive make it more sustainable?

Make a guess: In 2017 Rwanda doubled the price of a permit to see mountain gorillas, to US$1,500. Can making tourism more expensive make it more sustainable?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 6.2.1 one LIC: tourism's effects on uneven development, employment, environment, culture, infrastructure
  • 8.2.1 making tourism more sustainable; ecotourism, ethical tourism, responsible travel
Mission 8

Rwanda: a low-income country

  • The World Bank classes Rwanda as a low-income country. Income per person (GNI per capita) was US$1,150 in 2025.
  • Its mountain gorillas live in Volcanoes National Park, part of the Virunga Massif, shared with DR Congo and Uganda.
  • Rwanda is on Eduqas's list of LICs (Appendix B).
Mission 8

Why tourism grew

  • International arrivals rose from 667,000 in 2010 to 1,634,000 in 2019, about 2.5 times (our calculation).
  • Rare wildlife: mountain gorillas live in only a few protected forests.
  • Marketing: "Visit Rwanda" became Arsenal football club's first sleeve sponsor in 2018.
  • Conferences: business meetings and events earned US$94.7 million in 2025.
  • By 2018, the national parks were "attracting a record number of tourists".
Mission 8

Sustainable tourism and ecotourism

  • Sustainable tourism takes full account of its economic, social and environmental impacts, now and in the future, for visitors, businesses, the environment and host communities.
  • Ecotourism: "responsible travel to natural areas that conserves the environment, sustains the well-being of the local people, and involves interpretation and education".
  • One of its principles: provide direct money for conservation.
  • Not every nature holiday is ecotourism.
Quick check
?Which trip best fits the definition of ecotourism?
Mission 8

High value, low volume

  • On 6 May 2017 the Rwanda Development Board (RDB) raised the gorilla permit from US$750 to US$1,500.
  • RDB called this "high-value, low-volume": fewer visitors, each paying more.
  • Visits are limited: at most 8 visitors per group, and one hour with the gorillas.
  • Visitors who also spent 3 days in Rwanda's other national parks got a 30% discount.
Mission 8

Sharing the money

  • Since 2005, a revenue-sharing scheme has given part of park income to communities living next to the parks.
  • In 2017 the communities' share rose from 5% to 10% of park tourism revenue.
  • Since 2005 it has funded nearly 1,300 projects worth more than 23 billion Rwandan francs: schools, health centres and water supplies.
Quick check
?Communities receive 10% of park tourism revenue. How much of one US$1,500 permit is that?
Mission 8

Protecting the environment

  • Mountain gorillas in the Virunga Massif rose from as few as 242 in 1981 to 480 in 2010 and 604 in 2016.
  • The world total was about 1,063 in the 2019 census results.
  • Tourism pays for protection, and gives local people jobs as rangers, wardens and porters, so they gain from the forest standing.
  • Careful: these counts come before the 2017 price rise. They show recovery under long-term protection, not the effect of the new price.
Mission 8

Closing the development gap?

  • Income: tourism earned US$685 million in 2025. In 2019 it brought in about 28% of Rwanda's export earnings.
  • Jobs: porters, rangers, wardens, guides, hotel and transport staff.
  • Multiplier effect: workers spend their wages locally, so shops and farms gain, creating more jobs.
  • Infrastructure and services: revenue sharing has built schools and health facilities.
  • The gap: Rwanda's income per person rose from US$580 (2010) to US$1,150 (2025); the world average rose from about US$9,410 to US$14,240. The world figure fell from about 16 times Rwanda's to about 12 times (our calculation), but the gap in dollars grew.
  • Culture: since 2005, Kwita Izina, a gorilla-naming ceremony modelled on the centuries-old Rwandan tradition of naming children, has grown into an internationally recognised event. Tourism can give a tradition a new, public role.
Quick check
?World income per person was about 16 times Rwanda's in 2010 and about 12 times in 2025, but the gap in dollars grew. Which conclusion fits?
Mission 8

Problems and trade-offs

  • Access: a foreign visitor's permit (US$1,500) costs more than Rwanda's average income per person (US$1,150). Rwandan and East African citizens pay a promotional US$200.
  • Wildlife: diseases caught from people remain a threat to gorillas.
  • Land: the park is being expanded; the first phase affects 547 households, who are being moved to a new village.
  • Dependence: tourism fell from about 28% of export earnings in 2019 to about 11% in 2020. A sector that depends on travel is easily hit by global shocks.
Mission 8

Who makes tourism sustainable?

  • Government: RDB sets prices, limits group sizes and shares revenue with communities.
  • Organisations: groups such as the African Wildlife Foundation support the park and its neighbours; The International Ecotourism Society sets out ecotourism principles.
  • Individuals (responsible tourists):
    • keep to group sizes, time limits and the rangers' rules;
    • do not trek when ill: human diseases can infect gorillas;
    • hire local guides and porters, and buy local food and crafts;
    • respect local customs; take litter home.
Quick check
?Which action is most typical of a responsible tourist on a gorilla trek?
Mission check

?A national park in another low-income country plans to double its entry fee. Which plan is most likely to make its tourism more sustainable?

Rwanda chose high value, low volume: a US$1,500 permit, small groups and 10% of park revenue shared with neighbouring communities. Gorillas were already recovering under protection. Tourism earned US$685 million in 2025. The trade-offs: access, disease risk, relocation and dependence on travel.

Mission 9

Mission 9: Thailand welcomed almost 40 million foreign tourists in one year. Who in Thailand actually gained?

Make a guess: Thailand welcomed almost 40 million foreign tourists in one year. Who in Thailand actually gained?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 6.2.1 detailed study in one NIC of the positive and negative effects of tourism on patterns of uneven development, employment, environment, culture and infrastructure (with the concepts of enclave tourism and the informal economy)
Mission 9

Thailand: an NIC built partly on tourism

  • Thailand is on Eduqas's list of newly industrialised countries; the World Bank classes it as upper-middle income.
  • Foreign arrivals grew from 15.9 million in 2010 to 39.9 million in 2019: about 2.5 times.
  • Its first year of tourism promotion (1960) brought 81,340 visitors.
Mission 9

Employment

  • In 2019 tourism gave 17% of GDP and jobs for 4.4 million people.
  • Formal jobs: hotels, airlines, tour companies, restaurants.
  • Informal economy: work that is not registered, taxed or protected by contracts, e.g. street-food stalls, beach sellers, unlicensed guides.
  • In upper-middle-income countries about half of all workers are informal (ILO estimate, 2024).
Quick check
?Which worker is part of the informal economy?
Mission 9

Uneven development

  • Benefits are concentrated: in 2018, Thailand's main tourist cities got about 70% of visitors and secondary cities 30%.
  • The tourism authority's target was 65:35.
  • Tourism can widen the gap between busy resort areas and the rest of the country.
Mission 9

Enclave tourism

  • Enclave tourism: visitors stay inside a resort or hotel complex where they sleep, eat and play.
  • Most spending stays with the resort, which may be owned by a company from outside the area, so little reaches local businesses.
  • This leakage limits how much tourism helps nearby villages.
Quick check
?Which policy would most help spread tourism's benefits more evenly across Thailand?
Mission 9

Environment: Maya Bay

  • Maya Bay (Krabi) became famous after the film The Beach.
  • At its 2017 peak, almost 5,000 visitors a day came; the coral was damaged.
  • It was closed from June 2018 for about three and a half years so the coral could recover.
  • It reopened with a jetty on the other side of the island, so boats stay out of the bay, and controlled numbers.
Mission 9

Culture

  • Positive: temples, festivals and village life attract visitors and can earn money that helps keep traditions alive.
  • Negative: culture can become a show for tourists, changed to suit them.
  • A study of a Karen hill-tribe village found tourism built on its cultural and spiritual heritage, but with limited community say in planning; it called for planning that safeguards cultural identity.
Mission 9

Infrastructure

  • Positive: airports, roads, water and power built for tourists also serve local people and businesses.
  • Example: the new jetty at Maya Bay protects the reef and handles boats.
  • Negative: in busy resorts, water, sewage and waste systems can be overloaded in peak season.
Quick check
?A new airport is built on a Thai island for tourists. Farmers then use it to fly fresh fruit to Bangkok. Which effect of tourism is this?
Mission 9

Over-dependence

  • In 2019 tourism earned 19.9% of Thailand's export income; in 2020, during COVID-19, just 6.0%.
  • Workers in tourist areas lost their incomes; informal workers had no contracts to protect them.
  • Recovery: 10 million arrivals by December 2022; 35 million in 2024.
Quick check
?Tourism's share of Thailand's export income fell from 19.9% to 6.0%. Roughly how many times smaller was it?
Mission 9

Weighing it up

  • Employment: 4.4 million jobs (+); many informal and insecure (−).
  • Uneven development: money concentrated in main tourist cities (−); policies to spread it (+).
  • Environment: crowds damaged Maya Bay (−); closure and limits helped it recover (+).
  • Culture and infrastructure: income and new facilities such as the Maya Bay jetty (+); limited community say in planning (−); overloaded services in peak season (−).
  • Exam move: in a "to what extent" answer, weigh positives against negatives for each theme, then judge.
Mission check

?In a coastal province of another NIC, a foreign hotel chain plans a large all-inclusive resort. Which condition would most increase what local people gain from it?

Thailand's tourism created 4.4 million jobs in 2019, but benefits concentrated in the main tourist cities (70:30) and collapsed in 2020. Enclave resorts keep spending inside, so local gains depend on how tourism is planned.

Mission 10

Mission 10: In 2022 Sri Lanka defaulted on its foreign debt and turned to the IMF. Who sets the terms of rescue loans, and is there an alternative?

Make a guess: In 2022 Sri Lanka defaulted on its foreign debt and turned to the IMF. Who sets the terms of rescue loans, and is there an alternative?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 6.2.2 how government and NGO international aid can reduce inequality in one LIC or NIC
  • 6.2.2 emergency aid, long-term development aid and development targets
Mission 10

Closing the development gap

  • The development gap: the difference in wealth and well-being between richer and poorer countries.
  • Strategies to narrow it:
    • aid and debt relief
    • trade (including fairtrade) and investment
    • industrial development and tourism
    • small-scale tools: intermediate technology, microfinance.
Mission 10

Types of aid

  • Bilateral: from one government to another. Multilateral: through bodies such as the World Bank or UN.
  • Government aid (official development assistance) and NGO aid (charities such as Oxfam or Partners In Health).
  • Emergency aid: short-term help after a disaster: food, tents, medicine.
  • Long-term development aid: projects that build schools, clinics, water supplies and skills.
  • Grants are gifts; loans must be repaid.
Quick check
?Which is long-term development aid?
Mission 10

Development targets, and falling aid

  • Since a 1970 UN resolution, rich countries have been asked to give 0.7% of their national income (GNI) as aid.
  • The Sustainable Development Goals (2015) set development targets for 2030, such as ending hunger.
  • Aid from rich (OECD DAC) countries: $215.1 billion (2024) → $174.3 billion (2025, preliminary): 0.34% → 0.26% of GNI.
  • In 2025 only Norway, Luxembourg, Sweden and Denmark reached 0.7%.
Quick check
?Removing inflation, DAC aid fell from $215.14 billion (2024) to $165.51 billion (2025). What was the percentage fall?
Mission 10

Aid in Rwanda, a low-income country

  • Rwanda is a low-income country (World Bank).
  • Aid received: $1.39 billion in 2023, 9.5% of national income, down from 19.7% in 2005.
  • Government aid: the UK reports that its aid in Rwanda helped 364,000 children gain a decent education and reached 327,000 children and women with nutrition support (2015–2020).
  • NGO aid: Partners In Health has worked in Rwanda since 2005, helping the government bring health care to more than 860,000 people in three districts; it supports Butaro District Hospital, opened in 2011.
Mission 10

Aid: for and against

  • For: saves lives in emergencies; pays for schools, clinics and clean water; NGOs work with communities.
  • Against: can create dependency; may be tied to buying the donor's goods; may not reach the poorest; donor priorities can change.
  • Unreliable: DAC aid fell by about 23% in one year, 2025.
  • Best when it builds local skills and services, as when NGOs work through the national health system.
Quick check
?Which is the strongest disadvantage of relying on aid?
Mission 10

Debt relief

  • Many poor countries owed more than they could repay. Repayments took money from schools and hospitals.
  • The IMF and World Bank started the Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) Initiative in 1996; in 2005 another scheme added 100% relief on some debts.
  • To qualify, countries need a track record of reform and a poverty reduction strategy carried out for at least a year.
  • Rwanda is one of the countries that completed HIPC; Somalia became the 37th in December 2023.
  • + frees money for services. − strict conditions; countries can fall into debt again.
Mission 10

Rescue loans: Sri Lanka

  • In 2022 Sri Lanka stopped paying its foreign debts: "the external default in 2022" (IMF).
  • Its economy shrank by 7.3% in 2022. Inflation peaked at 70% in September 2022.
  • March 2023: the IMF approved a four-year loan of about US$3 billion.
  • Conditions (conditionality) included higher taxes and cost-recovery energy pricing (charging what energy really costs).
  • Results: inflation fell "to around 4 percent in 2023", and the economy grew 5.0% in 2024.
Mission 10

Who sets the terms?

  • The government negotiates conditions with IMF staff; the IMF's Executive Board approves the loan.
  • Votes follow each country's quota, roughly its economic weight.
  • Big decisions, such as changing quotas, need 85% of the votes. The USA holds 16.49%, enough to block them.
  • Creditors also had to accept less: governments, Chinese state banks and private bondholders.
  • Alternatives: grants from donors, or loans from newer banks such as the BRICS countries' New Development Bank, which funds projects rather than rescues.
Quick check
?What does conditionality mean for an IMF loan?
Mission check

?A low-income country asks to have its debts to the IMF and World Bank cancelled. Who sets the conditions it must meet?

Sri Lanka's government agreed conditions with the IMF; the IMF's Executive Board, where votes follow quotas, approved them, and creditors had to accept less. Alternatives include grants, debt relief and newer lenders such as the New Development Bank.

Mission 11

Mission 11: In 2006 a bank for poor villagers won the Nobel Peace Prize. Do small loans and fairer prices really lift people out of poverty?

Make a guess: In 2006 a bank for poor villagers won the Nobel Peace Prize. Do small loans and fairer prices really lift people out of poverty?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 6.2.2 the concept of fair trade and its benefits
Mission 11

Microfinance

  • In 2006 Muhammad Yunus and Grameen Bank (Bangladesh) won the Nobel Peace Prize "for their efforts to create economic and social development from below".
  • Microfinance: small loans, savings and insurance for people that ordinary banks will not serve.
  • In 2018, microfinance lenders had 139.9 million borrowers: 80% women, 65% in rural areas.
Mission 11

Does microfinance work?

  • Six randomised trials in six countries on four continents found "a consistent pattern of modestly positive, but not transformative, effects".
  • In Andhra Pradesh, India, rapid lending left many households over-indebted: by 2010, 83% had loans from more than one source.
  • So small loans help some families run businesses and manage money, but do not on their own lift large numbers out of poverty.
Quick check
?What do the six randomised trials suggest about microfinance?
Mission 11

Fair trade: how it works

  • Fair trade: a system that gives producers in poorer countries a fairer, more stable deal.
  • Minimum price: a floor paid when world prices fall. For washed Arabica coffee it rose from $1.40 to $1.80 per pound for contracts from 1 August 2023.
  • Fairtrade Premium: an extra sum that producer groups decide together how to spend.
  • In 2024 Fairtrade covered 1.87 million farmers and workers and paid €201.6 million in Premium.
Mission 11

The benefits for producers

  • A safety net: the minimum price protects income when world prices crash.
  • Investment: in 2024 small-scale producer groups spent 35% of their Premium on improving production and farming, and 19% on payments to farmers.
  • Communities: workers on certified plantations put 67% of their Premium into social investments.
  • Reach: 75% of Fairtrade farmers and workers were in Africa and the Middle East.
Mission 11

The limits

  • In 2024 only about 27% of reported certified coffee production was sold on Fairtrade terms; the rest was sold at ordinary prices.
  • Fair Trade coffee was 1.8% of world coffee exports in 2009: a small share of trade.
  • A study in Ethiopia and Uganda found that, in some cases, hired workers in areas with Fairtrade groups were "significantly worse paid" than elsewhere.
  • Fairtrade itself says its Premium data do not show "outcomes or impacts".
Quick check
?Which is a benefit of fair trade for producers?
Mission check

?A charity starts offering small loans and a guaranteed minimum price to cocoa farmers in a new region. What is the most realistic expectation?

Modestly. Microcredit's effects are "modestly positive, but not transformative", and too much lending caused a crisis in Andhra Pradesh. Fairtrade sets a floor price and pays a Premium, but only about 27% of certified coffee was sold on Fairtrade terms in 2024. Both are tools, not cures.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Development is economic, social and political; measures include GNI per head, birth and death rates, infant mortality, life expectancy, doctors, literacy, water and the HDI.
  • Every measure has limits (averages, data quality, missing dimensions), so use several.
  • Development is a continuum, and the gap is dynamic: UK–India GNI per head fell from about 67 times to 20 times, but widened in dollars.

Mission 2

  • India: 1.46 billion people (about 87% of South Asia's), 6th-largest economy, but GNI per person US$2,760.
  • Context: federal (36 states/UTs); young; mostly rural; monsoon-dependent farming.

Mission 3

  • Rwanda (landlocked, crowded, low income) grew from US$90 to US$1,150 GNI per person (1975–2025); 1994 was a catastrophic break.
  • Government aid (PEPFAR, Global Fund) and NGO aid (One Acre Fund) reached rural and poor people; poverty and the Gini index fell, but aid was not the only cause.

Mission 4

  • Causes: physical/environmental, historical (colonialism) and economic (primary products, debt, trade and trade blocs).

Mission 5

  • Tariffs, quotas and subsidies protect some producers and harm others.
  • LIC (Rwanda): a tariff cut used-clothing imports but brought US retaliation. NIC (India): rich countries' quotas held back clothing exports until 2005.

Mission 6

  • TNCs keep headquarters and design at home and spread production abroad, by outsourcing (Apple) or FDI (Toyota).
  • For India: jobs, skills, exports and suppliers; but profits leave, top jobs stay away, and FDI rises and falls.
  • In the UK, Toyota brings £2.75 billion of investment and about 3,000 jobs, but decisions are made abroad.

Mission 7

  • Tourism brings jobs, foreign currency, taxes and infrastructure (benefits), but also leakage, low-paid seasonal work, environmental damage and crowding (problems).
  • Maldives: 20.3% of GDP; 59% of resort workers foreign in 2022; arrivals fell 67.4% in 2020.
  • Enclave tourism raises leakage; the informal economy spreads income but gives no protection; over-dependence leaves an economy exposed.

Mission 8

  • Ecotourism protects nature, benefits local people and educates visitors.
  • Tourism brings income, jobs and services to an LIC, helping narrow the development gap in relative terms; access, land and dependence are the trade-offs.

Mission 9

  • Thailand (NIC): tourism gave 17% of GDP and 4.4 million jobs (2019), many in the informal economy; 70% of visitors went to main tourist cities.
  • Effects: jobs and infrastructure (+), uneven development, enclave tourism, cultural change and environmental damage (−; Maya Bay closed 2018–2022), and over-dependence (tourism fell from 19.9% to 6.0% of exports in 2020).

Mission 10

  • Strategies to reduce the gap: aid, debt relief, trade, fairtrade, investment, industry, tourism, intermediate technology and microfinance, each with gains and limits.
  • Lenders (the IMF and World Bank) set the conditions for rescue loans and debt relief; voting follows quotas.
  • In Rwanda, government and NGO aid support education, nutrition and health; aid fell from 19.7% to 9.5% of national income (2005–2023).

Mission 11

  • Microfinance and fair trade bring modest, real benefits (stable prices, Premium, small loans for women), but reach only part of trade and need careful lending.
