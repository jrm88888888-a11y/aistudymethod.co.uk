Mission 1

Mission 1: Since 1981 London has added over two million people. Why has the North East barely grown?

Make a guess: Since 1981 London has added over two million people. Why has the North East barely grown?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel A-level Geography (9GE0)

  • 4B.1a uneven UK population growth over 50 years (London and South East v North East)
  • 4B.1b population structure and density along the rural-urban continuum: accessibility, physical factors, history, planning
  • 4B.1c population structure and dynamics from fertility, mortality, international and internal migration
  • 4B.7a UK internal migration has created uneven demographic and cultural patterns (London and South East)
Mission 1

Uneven growth

  • ONS estimates, 1981 → 2025:
  • London: 6,805,000 → 9,122,909 (+34.1%).
  • South East: 7,243,055 → 9,691,844 (+33.8%).
  • North East: 2,636,194 → 2,762,746 (+4.8%).
  • London had fallen before this: 7,452,343 at the 1971 census.
Quick check
?The North East grew from 2,636,194 (1981) to 2,762,746 (2025). What was its percentage growth?
Mission 1

The four drivers

  • Change = natural change (births − deaths) + international net migration + internal net migration.
  • Year to mid-2025:
  • London: natural +54,211; international +51,839; internal −131,961.
  • North East: natural −5,452; international +12,201; internal +7,439.
  • London's population fell 0.29% that year: internal losses outweighed the rest.
Mission 1

Fertility, mortality and age

  • Births per 1,000 people (our calculation, 2024–25): London 11.4; North East 8.8.
  • Deaths per 1,000: London 5.5; North East 10.8.
  • Median age, mid-2025: London 36.2; North East 42.2; England 40.6.
  • A young population has more parents and fewer elderly: natural increase reflects age structure, not just fertility.
  • Most regions now have more deaths than births: "a long-term decline in fertility and an increasing number of deaths".
Quick check
?Why can London have positive natural change while the North East has negative natural change?
Mission 1

Internal migration by age

  • Year to June 2025, net internal migration:
  • London: +30,356 aged 20–24; but −25,678 aged 30–34, −23,542 aged 35–39, −16,854 aged 0–4.
  • North East: +6,588 aged 15–19; −4,696 aged 20–24; small gains at older ages.
  • Pattern: young adults move in to London; families with children move out.
  • Students arrive in the North East, then many graduates leave.

?London gains 20–24-year-olds but loses people aged 30–39 and children aged 0–4. What best explains this?

Mission 1

Why density and structure vary

  • Accessibility: jobs and universities cluster where transport converges; young adults follow.
  • Physical factors: relief, uplands and coasts limit where people can live at high density.
  • History: industrial and port cities grew on coal, ships and trade; decline left older populations.
  • Planning: Green Belt aims "to prevent urban sprawl", keeping edges low density.
  • Rural areas gain internal migrants at most ages; 15–19-year-olds are the exception.
Quick check
?A rural district has a median age near 50 and more deaths than births. Which process is most likely to keep its population growing?
Mission 1

Cultural patterns

  • Born outside the UK, 2021: London 40.6%; England 17.4%; North East 6.8%.
  • International migrants settle first where jobs and existing communities are.
  • Internal migration then sorts by age: young adults concentrate in inner London (Tower Hamlets: median age 30.8).
  • Result: uneven demographic and cultural patterns between London and the South East and the North East.
Mission check

?In the year to mid-2025, Northumberland had 2,341 births and 3,926 deaths, net internal migration of +4,437 and net international migration of +582. Its population rose by 3,360. What drove the growth?

London grew by a third since 1981 because it attracts young adults and international migrants, whose youth also brings natural increase; but it loses families to the rest of the UK. The North East grew slowly: an older population means natural decrease, and it loses young graduates. Rural areas like Northumberland grow, if at all, through internal in-migration.

Mission 2: Why would migrants choose a small market town in the Lincolnshire fens over a big city?

Make a guess: Why would migrants choose a small market town in the Lincolnshire fens over a big city?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel A-level Geography (9GE0)

  • 4B.2a variation in gender and ethnicity within and between settlements
  • 4B.2b cultural diversity explained by social clustering, accessibility, physical factors, planning policy
  • 4B.2c fertility, mortality and migration changing places' cultural characteristics
  • 4B.7c international migrants in rural areas: challenges and opportunities (East Europeans in Lincolnshire)
Mission 2

Gender and ethnicity in the census

  • Gender here means the balance of men and women (often the sex ratio).
  • England and Wales, 2021: 51.0% women and 49.0% men.
  • Ethnicity in the census is self-identified: people choose their own ethnic group.
  • England's "White" share fell from 85.4% (2011) to 81.0% (2021).
  • National averages hide big local differences.
Mission 2

Variation between settlements

  • Boston (Lincolnshire market town), 2021: 94.7% "White"; 2.0% "Asian".
  • Newham (east London borough), 2021: 30.8% "White"; 42.2% "Asian"; 17.5% "Black".
  • England: 81.0% "White".
  • But diversity is not only about broad ethnic groups. In Boston, 5.6% were born in Lithuania and 5.4% in Poland: diversity that a 94.7% "White" figure does not show.
  • So use country of birth and language as well as ethnic group to judge cultural diversity.
Mission 2

Variation within a settlement

  • Two boroughs of the same city, London, 2021:
  • Newham: 30.8% "White"; 45.5% born in England; median age 32.
  • Havering (outer east London): 75.3% "White"; 79.6% born in England; median age 39.
  • London overall: 53.8% "White".
  • Havering is changing too: its "White" share was 87.7% in 2011.
Mission 2

Variation in gender

  • The sex ratio changes with age: women on average live longer, so places with many older people tend to have more women.
  • Oldest populations: North Norfolk (33.4% aged 65+) and Rother (32.4%).
  • Youngest: Barking and Dagenham (24.5% under 15).
  • Migration can skew the ratio: Boston's MP described "predominantly young men" working in the constituency's fields.
  • Within a city, student areas, barracks or care homes can shift a neighbourhood's balance.
Quick check
?Which comparison shows variation in ethnicity within a settlement, rather than between settlements?
Mission 2

Explaining different levels of diversity

  • Social clustering: migrants join family, friends and community institutions (shops, places of worship). Newham: 7.7% born in Bangladesh.
  • Accessibility to key cities: London's airports, jobs and transport links draw international migrants.
  • Physical factors: flat, fertile fenland suits labour-intensive vegetable and flower growing; "Boston's agricultural economy relies on migrant labour from eastern Europe" (MP, 2016).
  • Government planning policy can foster or suppress diversity. EU enlargement on 1 May 2004 brought Lithuania and Poland into the EU, and free movement opened the UK labour market to their citizens. Asylum seekers are dispersed by the Home Office: "You cannot choose where you live".
Mission 2

Fertility, mortality and migration

  • Migration changes places directly: Boston's England-born share fell from 83.2% to 75.1% (2011–2021).
  • Fertility: in 2021, 28.8% of live births in England and Wales were to mothers born outside the UK.
  • Total fertility rate: UK-born women 1.54; non-UK-born women 2.03.
  • Romania was the most common country of birth of non-UK-born mothers in 2021.
  • Mortality: as older long-term residents die and young migrant families have children, a place's cultural mix shifts even without new arrivals.
Quick check
?Boston's England-born share fell from 83.2% (2011) to 75.1% (2021). What was the change in percentage points?
Mission 2

East Europeans in Lincolnshire: Boston

  • Boston's population rose 9.1% (2011–2021) to about 70,500; England grew 6.6%.
  • About 4,000 residents were born in Lithuania (5.6%).
  • Poland-born residents rose from just under 3,000 (4.6%) to about 3,800 (5.4%).
  • Why a rural town? Its MP said "tens of thousands of people have come from Poland, Latvia, Lithuania", "drawn by the UK's relatively high minimum wages", to an agricultural economy that "relies on migrant labour".
  • Private renting rose from 15.7% to 20.3% of households.
Mission 2

Challenges and opportunities (as described by Boston's MP, 2016)

  • Opportunities: labour for farms and food businesses; new businesses: "We have a lower rate of empty shops than the comparable national average".
  • Services: "schools have caught up but the NHS has not".
  • Housing: he described "a rented room unfit for human habitation" and praised work "in tackling rogue landlords".
  • Integration: "predominantly young men work in my constituency's fields, largely in groups from their own countries"; "There is no easy way to encourage integration".
  • Constituents he quoted expected few locals to get new farm jobs.
  • These are one MP's views in Parliament: use them as evidence of perceptions, and test them against data (census, housing).
Quick check
?Which statement best explains why many East European migrants settled in rural Lincolnshire rather than only in big cities?
Mission check

?A rural district has large vegetable farms and packing factories but no city nearby. Over ten years, its share of residents born abroad doubles. Which explanation fits best?

Diversity varies between and within settlements because migrants follow work (physical factors and accessibility) and each other (social clustering), within rules set by government. Once settled, births and deaths keep changing a place's cultural mix, though children born locally count as UK-born.

Mission 3: Victorian London was feared as dangerous. Why do so many young adults now choose to live there?

Make a guess: Victorian London was feared as dangerous. Why do so many young adults now choose to live there?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel A-level Geography (9GE0)

  • 4B.4a changing perceptions of urban places: dangerous (Victorian London) to attractive to young people and migrants
  • 4B.4b urban places seen as undesirable: crime, environment, population, reputation, lived experience, media
  • 4B.4c suburban vs inner-city desirability for different age, ethnic and life-cycle groups
Mission 3

Perception is not the same as data

  • A perception is the image a person holds of a place.
  • It is built from three things:
    • quantitative data (crime rates, pollution, census);
    • lived experience (living, working or visiting there);
    • media representation (news, film, social media).
  • Different groups see the same place differently, and images change over time.
Mission 3

Victorian London: dangerous and threatening

  • 1831: cholera arrived "bringing with it fear and panic"; further outbreaks in 1848–49, 1853–54 and 1866.
  • Most Londoners lived in "overcrowded slums"; the "Great Stink" came in summer 1858.
  • Many blamed miasma, "a poisonous vapour" in the air.
  • Charles Booth's inquiry (1886–1903) coloured every street by "the income and social class of its inhabitants": poverty was mapped, and fear was attached to place.
Mission 3

London now: a magnet for young adults and migrants

  • Census 2021 (calculated from ONS tables): ages 20–34 were 24.8% of London's residents, 19.6% of England's.
  • 40.6% of Londoners were born outside the UK; England 17.4%.
  • In 2021 the largest five-year age group in London was 30–34.
  • Draws: a wide range of economic opportunities and social and leisure activities. The city's image has moved from threat to opportunity for these groups.
Quick check
?Which statement about London's changing image is best supported?
Mission 3

Why some urban places are seen as undesirable

  • Crime rates: recorded or surveyed crime.
  • Environmental quality: in London, NO₂ was 13% higher in the most deprived areas than the least deprived (2019).
  • Population characteristics: for example, high deprivation or rapid turnover.
  • Reputation: a label ("rough", "no-go") can outlast the conditions that created it.
Mission 3

Lived experience vs media

  • ONS survey (year to March 2016): 60% of adults thought crime nationally had gone up.
  • Asked about their local area, far fewer said so: 29% of non-victims and 46% of victims.
  • For the national picture, people's "main source of information was the media".
  • Local views rest more on lived experience. So a place known only from the news can seem worse than residents find it.
Quick check
?Why did far more people think crime was rising nationally than in their own area?

Different groups, different "desirable" places

  • Age and life-cycle stage:
    • young single adults often value jobs, transport, nightlife and renting near work → inner city;
    • families often value space, gardens and schools → suburbs;
    • older people often value quiet, green space and established networks → suburbs and beyond.
  • Ethnicity: some people value being near family, community organisations, places of worship and specialist shops. That can make particular inner or outer districts more desirable to them.
  • Attitudes vary within every group.

  • Judge whose view each source shows: a newspaper, a resident and an estate agent may describe one street three ways.
  • Conclude on how far perception and data agree.
Mission check

?A northern UK city centre was once widely seen as run-down and unsafe. Today most of its flats are rented by people aged 20–34, while most families with children in the city live in the suburbs. Which explanation fits best?

Perceptions of urban places come from data, lived experience and media, and differ by group. Victorian London's dangers were real and widely feared; today London attracts young adults and migrants for jobs and lifestyle, while families and older people are more often found in the suburbs.

Mission 4: Why do some people dream of moving to a village while teenagers can't wait to leave?

Make a guess: Why do some people dream of moving to a village while teenagers can't wait to leave?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel A-level Geography (9GE0)

  • 4B.5b some rural places perceived as undesirable: remoteness, limited social opportunities and services, high transport costs, population characteristics, reputation from data, lived experience and media
  • 4B.5c rural areas viewed in different ways: from very remote areas to retirement and commuter villages
Mission 4

The rural idyll

  • Rural idyll: an image of the countryside as tranquil, natural and rich in history and culture.
  • Some data fit it: in 2022/23, rural residents rated wellbeing better on all four ONS measures; life satisfaction 7.6 vs 7.4 (out of 10) in urban areas.
  • But the gap is small, and wellbeing depends most on health, marital status and employment, so "comparisons … should be made with caution".
Mission 4

Hardy's Wessex: a place made by a novel

  • Thomas Hardy took the word "Wessex" "from the pages of early English history" for the district of that "extinct kingdom".
  • One county "did not afford a canvas large enough".
  • He meant "a merely realistic dream-country".
  • By 1895: "the dream-country has, by degrees, solidified into a utilitarian region"; the name was "taken up elsewhere as a local designation".
  • Cultural associations can turn a fictional place into a real place identity.
Quick check
?What does Hardy's 1895 Preface show about rural perception?
Mission 4

Remote and short of services

  • 3.2 million people live in rural settlements more than a 30-minute drive from a major town or city.
  • Remote smaller settlements are "typically too small to support services locally" and residents rely on driving.
  • Rural residents travelled about 8,600 miles each in 2024; urban residents 5,600.
  • Secondary-age children in smaller rural settlements travel 8.8 miles to school; urban children 2.7 miles.
Mission 4

High transport costs

  • Rural households spend about £33 a week more on transport than urban ones.
  • Transport is their biggest share of spending: 12% of disposable income (£115 a week), more than housing, water and energy (10%).
  • Higher average incomes are "more than offset" by higher spending, "particularly on transport".
  • Broadband in the most rural areas is "behind that of the rest of England".
Mission 4

Population characteristics: who lives there?

  • 27% of rural residents are aged 65+; 18% in urban areas outside London.
  • Median age in smaller rural settlements: 51; London: 35.
  • Net migration into majority rural authorities for every age band except 15–19: about 13,700 more left than arrived (year to June 2023).
  • Defra: this "is likely to reflect young people leaving home to attend education elsewhere".
  • For a teenager still living there: fewer peers, fewer social opportunities, long trips to college and work.
Mission 4

Reputation: data, lived experience and media

  • Quantitative data build a reputation: e.g. only 1% of rural residents live in England's most deprived 10% of neighbourhoods (urban: 12%).
  • Lived experience can contradict the averages: the same village can be calm for a retiree and isolating for a 16-year-old without a car.
  • Media representations (novels, TV, advertising, news) can spread either the idyll or a reputation for isolation, and these images can outlast the facts.
Quick check
?Which is evidence from lived experience, not from quantitative data?
Mission 4

A rural spectrum, not one countryside

  • England's 2021 rural-urban classification splits rural settlements into smaller and larger, and nearer to or further from a major town or city.
  • "Nearer" = within a 30-minute drive of a town of at least 75,000 people.
  • 6.5 million rural residents live nearer to a major town; 3.2 million further away.
  • Rural populations grew faster in settlements nearer to major towns (2014–2024).
Mission 4

Remote, retirement and commuter villages

  • Commuter villages: within reach of towns; largest net inflow to majority rural authorities was aged 30–39 (about 15,600); 35% of rural workers work from home.
  • Retirement villages: high shares of over-65s; migration of people aged 70+ "favoured movement towards majority rural areas".
  • Second homes: more than 2 in 100 homes in majority rural authorities, twice the national average.
  • Remote areas: limited services and long drives.
  • Each type is perceived differently by residents and outsiders.
Quick check
?A village 20 minutes' drive from a city has a new estate of family homes, a busy school and many residents who work from home or drive to the city. Where does it sit on the spectrum?
Mission check

?In a Scottish island community, a retired incomer calls it "paradise"; a 17-year-old born there wants to leave. Which factor would explain the teenager's view but matter least to the retiree?

The same place is perceived differently by life stage and lived experience. Rural places are seen as idyllic (tranquillity, landscape, culture, as in Hardy's Wessex) or undesirable (remoteness, services, transport costs, ageing), depending on who is looking: commuters, retirees and teenagers experience different countrysides.

Mission 5

What is a diaspora?

  • A diaspora is a population living outside its country or region of origin that keeps links to it, including descendants born abroad.
  • Diasporas are networks: people, money, ideas and goods flow both ways between origin and destinations.
Mission 5

The world's largest diaspora

  • UN estimate, 2024: 18.5 million people born in India lived in other countries, the most of any country of origin. Next: China 11.7 million, Mexico 11.6 million.
  • India's emigrant stock was 6.6 million in 1990.
  • These are migrant stocks: people counted where they live, by country of birth.
  • India's Ministry of External Affairs counts 37.3 million "Overseas Indians" (January 2026), because it adds Persons of Indian Origin born abroad.
Mission 5

Where the India-born live

  • UN, 2024: United Arab Emirates 3.25 million · USA 3.17 million · Saudi Arabia 1.95 million · Kuwait 1.23 million · UK 1.04 million · Canada 1.02 million.
  • Western Asia (including the Gulf states): 8.33 million.
  • Two streams: contract work in the Gulf, and longer-term settlement in North America, Europe and Australia.
Quick check
?In 2024, 8,327,502 of the 18,533,845 India-born emigrants lived in Western Asia. What share is that?
Mission 5

Changing the origin: money home

  • Personal remittances to India were US$150.7 billion in 2025: 3.8% of GDP.
  • Kerala, in south-west India, had about 2.2 million emigrants in 2023 and received ₹2.16 lakh crore (₹2.16 trillion) in remittances; the UAE has the most emigrants from Kerala.
  • Remittances build houses and pay for schooling, but tie household incomes to jobs abroad.
Mission 5

Changing a destination: Leicester

  • Leicester has the highest share of India-born residents of any local authority in England and Wales: 16.2% (59,842 people) in 2021.
  • Gujarati was the main language of 12.6% of residents aged 3 and over.
  • Some arrived via East Africa: after Uganda ordered the expulsion of all Asians on 4 August 1972, just over 28,000 people came to the UK.
  • Leicester's Diwali is described by the University of Leicester as "one of the largest Diwali celebrations outside of India".
Mission 5

Flows from former colonies and the EU

  • Census 2021, England and Wales, people born in:
    • India 920,361 · Pakistan 623,557 · Bangladesh 273,042 · Jamaica 142,154 (former colonies);
    • Poland 743,083 · Romania 538,840 (EU members from 2004 and 2007).
  • Two waves with different drivers: Commonwealth migration after 1948, and EU free movement after enlargement.
  • The UK's Poland-born population rose from about 75,000 in 1990 to about 902,000 in 2024.
Mission 5

Government as gatekeeper

  • 1948: the British Nationality Act created "citizenship of the United Kingdom and Colonies".
  • 1962: the Commonwealth Immigrants Act made "temporary provision for controlling the immigration into the United Kingdom of Commonwealth citizens"; entry for work required a voucher from the Ministry of Labour.
  • 2021: free movement between the EU and the UK ended in January 2021; EU nationals now need a visa or settled status.
  • Exam move (Edexcel 4B.7b): the government is the main gatekeeper: each law changed which flows could arrive.
Quick check
?Which best shows the UK government acting as a gatekeeper?
Mission 5

How UK culture changed

  • Language: in Ealing, Panjabi is the main language of 4.9% of residents aged 3 and over, and Polish of 4.4%.
  • Faith: Hindus 1.8% and Sikhs 0.9% of England's population in 2021, concentrated in particular places, such as Southall (below).
  • Food, festivals, media spread beyond migrant communities: the culture of the whole country changes, not only of the migrants.
Mission 5

Southall: places of worship

  • In three Southall wards (Ealing), 31.7% of residents were Sikh in 2021; in England as a whole, 0.9% (our calculation).
  • Southall Green: Sikh 36.8%, Muslim 19.6%, Christian 24.2%, Hindu 11.6%. A mixed, not single-faith, place.
  • Sri Guru Singh Sabha Southall says it "welcomes over 50,000 people each week"; its langar (free vegetarian meal) is open to everyone "whatever their faith or background".
Mission 5

Southall: retail and leisure

  • Ealing in 2021: 8.7% of residents were born in India, 2.1% in Pakistan and 5.1% in Poland.
  • Demand from these communities supports distinctive retail: specialist grocers, clothing, jewellery, restaurants and money-transfer shops, and leisure linked to faith, food and festivals.
  • Fieldwork move: map shop types and places of worship along a Southall street and compare with a town centre elsewhere: evidence of a diverse living space.
Mission 5

Identity and hybridity

  • Many cultures, not one: Gujarati in Leicester; Panjabi and Sikh institutions in Southall; Malayali migrants from Kerala in the Gulf.

  • Luton: a TNC closure (Stellantis), government-backed airport growth and town-centre investment; fast population change (16.6% arrived 2011–21); tensions and a contested media image, with attributed views from MPs and peers.
  • Glasgow: dispersal since 2000 (3,664 in dispersed accommodation, March 2023, most in the UK); hostility around Sighthill; exclusion through low support, no choice of home and limited right to work.

Mission 8

  • Economic: Luton gained 3,000 people in work, but its estimated rate fell and the gap to England widened (6.0 → 9.3 points), both within sampling error; pay rose ~45% everywhere, so relative positions held.
  • Social and integration: IMD ranks are relative; England's life-expectancy gap widened (9.1 → 10.4 years, males); turnout, volunteering, belonging and hate crime (with survey checks) measure integration.

Mission 9

  • Slough: +13.0% population (2011–21); Asian 46.7%, White 36.0%; 1990s youth violence led to Aik Saath (now Together As One), a youth-led conflict-resolution charity; national intervention in the council since 2021.
  • Judge change with economic (employment, pay), social (IMD, renting), demographic and health (life expectancy) and environmental (4 NO₂ AQMAs) variables; stakeholders weigh them differently.

Mission 10

  • Lake District National Park Partnership (24 partners, Plan 2026–31): ageing (29.4% aged 65+), 29.5% of homes not in permanent use, £380,000 average price, tourism £2.37bn, farm incomes falling as subsidies change.
  • Groups view the Park differently (farmers, young locals, visitors, ethnic minorities under-represented); stakeholders judge success with contrasting criteria (nature ambition vs economy vs housing).
