Mission 1

Nobody is born knowing how to shoplift. So who teaches it?

Make a guess: Nobody is born knowing how to shoplift. So who teaches it?

Criminal psychology · learning theories

Learning by watching

  • Social learning theory (Bandura, 1977): behaviour, including crime, is learned by watching others.
  • A role model is someone we look up to, such as a parent, older sibling, friend or celebrity.
  • Identification: we copy people we feel similar to or want to be like.
  • Observational learning: we watch how the role model behaves. Modelling (imitation): we later copy that behaviour.
Criminal psychology · learning theories

Consequences you see, and consequences you get

  • Vicarious reinforcement: seeing a role model rewarded for a behaviour makes us more likely to imitate it. Seeing them punished makes imitation less likely.
  • Example: an older friend steals trainers and is admired by the group.
Quick check
?Zane, 14, admires his older cousin. He watches the cousin spray graffiti on a bus shelter, and the cousin's friends laugh and cheer. A week later Zane sprays graffiti too. Which idea best explains why the cheering made Zane more likely to copy?
Criminal psychology · learning theories

Operant conditioning

  • Skinner (1948): behaviour followed by a pleasant consequence is repeated; behaviour followed by an unpleasant one is not.
  • Primary reinforcers meet biological needs, such as food or warmth. Secondary reinforcers gain value from association with primary ones, such as money or status.
Something addedSomething removed
Behaviour increasesPositive reinforcement: stolen goods, praise from a gangNegative reinforcement: stealing food ends hunger
Behaviour decreasesPositive punishment: being given extra unpaid workNegative punishment: a fine, losing privileges
Quick check
?Robin is fined £200 for shoplifting and does not shoplift again. Which consequence is this?
Key study Criminal psychology · learning theories

Bandura, Ross and Ross (1961)

Aim
To see whether children imitate an aggressive adult model, and whether the model's sex matters.
Method
Lab experiment; 72 children aged about 3 to 5 from a Stanford University nursery: 24 saw an adult hit a Bobo doll, 24 a calm adult, 24 no model. After mild frustration, each was observed for 20 minutes through a one-way mirror.
Results
Children who saw the aggressive model copied more physical and verbal aggression. Boys imitated more physical aggression, especially from a male model.
Conclusion
Aggression can be learned by observing a role model, even when the model is not reinforced.
Strength
Standardised, controlled procedure, so it can be replicated.
Weakness
A Bobo doll is made to be hit, so low ecological validity.
Key study Criminal psychology · learning theories

Charlton et al. (2000)

Aim
To see whether the arrival of broadcast TV made children on the remote island of St Helena more anti-social.
Method
Natural experiment; children aged 3 to 8 at two primary schools were filmed in the playground before TV arrived and five years after. Pro-social and anti-social acts were coded.
Results
Only 9 of 64 before-and-after comparisons changed significantly, and none showed a rise in anti-social behaviour.
Conclusion
TV did not make these children more aggressive; a close community, family and school may stop them imitating it.
Strength
Natural behaviour in a real setting, so high ecological validity.
Weakness
One small, close-knit community, so the results may not generalise to other places.

Mission 2

Could a personality test predict who ends up in court?

Make a guess: Could a personality test predict who ends up in court?

Criminal psychology · Eysenck's personality theory

Three dimensions of personality

  • Eysenck measured personality with a questionnaire, scoring people along dimensions.
  • Extraversion: sociable, lively, seeks excitement. At the other end, introversion: quiet, reserved, avoids stimulation.
  • Neuroticism: anxious, moody, emotionally unstable. The other end is emotional stability.
  • Psychoticism: cold, aggressive, lacking empathy.
Criminal psychology · Eysenck's personality theory

The criminal personality

  • Eysenck (1964, adding psychoticism later) proposed that offenders tend to score high on extraversion, neuroticism and psychoticism.
  • High extraversion: seeks thrills and takes risks.
  • High neuroticism: reacts strongly and unpredictably to stress.
  • High psychoticism: cold and aggressive, with little guilt about hurting others.
  • A stable introvert (low on all three) is the least likely to offend.
Criminal psychology · Eysenck's personality theory

Born with it, shaped by childhood

  • Eysenck said personality is largely inherited: it comes from the nervous system we are born with, making this a biological explanation.
  • Extraverts have an under-aroused nervous system, so they seek extra stimulation.
  • High E and high N children are harder to condition, so they learn less from punishment.
  • Poor socialisation follows: they don't learn to link wrongdoing with anxiety, so they develop a weaker conscience.

Mission 3

Many people leave prison and offend again. What might work better?

Make a guess: Many people leave prison and offend again. What might work better?

Criminal psychology · punishment and rehabilitation

Recidivism and the aims of punishment

  • Recidivism means reoffending after being punished for a crime.
  • Punishment aims to deter (put the offender and others off), incapacitate (stop them offending, e.g. prison), give retribution (deserved payback) and rehabilitate (help the person change).
  • The Ministry of Justice (MoJ) measures proven reoffending: a new offence within a year that leads to a conviction or caution.
Criminal psychology · punishment and rehabilitation

Prison

  • Prison removes freedom: it incapacitates offenders and is meant to deter.
  • Reoffending within a year: 40.6% of adults released from custody (MoJ, 2026), and much higher after sentences under 12 months.
  • Strength: protects the public; time for education and treatment.
  • Weakness: offenders may learn crime from other prisoners, and lose jobs, homes and family links.
Criminal psychology · punishment and rehabilitation

Punishment in the community

  • A community sentence is served outside prison: unpaid work, a curfew, or drug or alcohol treatment.
  • Strength: the offender keeps a job and family. A MoJ study (2019) found similar offenders reoffended less after a court order than after a short prison sentence.
  • Weakness: may be seen as a soft option that fails to deter.
Criminal psychology · punishment and rehabilitation

Restorative justice

  • In restorative justice, a trained facilitator brings the offender and victim together (or they exchange letters). The offender hears the harm caused and makes amends.
  • Strength: in UK trials most victims who took part were satisfied, and reoffending fell slightly.
  • Weakness: both sides must agree; offenders may fake remorse to look good.

Mission 4

Rewarding prisoners with tokens: bribery, or psychology that works?

Make a guess: Rewarding prisoners with tokens: bribery, or psychology that works?

Criminal psychology · treatment programmes

How a token economy works

  • A token economy is a treatment based on operant conditioning.
  • Staff set clear target behaviours, such as attending education, keeping a cell tidy or not fighting.
  • Each time the behaviour happens, the offender earns a token: a secondary reinforcer with no value of its own.
  • Tokens are exchanged for rewards, such as snacks (a primary reinforcer), phone calls or gym time. Breaking rules can lose tokens.
Criminal psychology · treatment programmes

Do token economies work?

  • Strength: easy to run; staff can be trained quickly.
  • Strength: behaviour inside the institution often improves, making it safer.
  • Weakness: improvements may not last after release, because nobody hands out tokens in everyday life.
  • Weakness: it changes behaviour but not the causes of offending.
  • Weakness: ethics: offenders must never be denied basic rights, such as food or family visits, if they don't earn tokens.
Quick check
?In a young offenders' institution, Lee earns a token each time he goes to his maths class. At the end of the week he swaps his tokens for a chocolate bar. He loses a token if he swears at staff. Which is the secondary reinforcer?
Criminal psychology · treatment programmes

Anger management

  • Anger management is a cognitive-behavioural programme: offenders learn to recognise and control the thoughts and feelings that lead to aggression.
  • Novaco's (1975) three stages:
    • Cognitive preparation: identify your triggers and notice anger building.
    • Skill acquisition: learn coping skills, such as calming self-talk, slow breathing and walking away.
    • Application practice: use the skills in role-plays of trigger situations.

Mission check

?Rewarding prisoners with tokens: bribery, or psychology that works?

A token economy is operant conditioning, not bribery: tokens are secondary reinforcers that strengthen target behaviours. It often improves behaviour inside the institution, but the effect may fade after release, when nobody hands out tokens.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Social learning theory: crime is learned by observing and imitating role models we identify with, especially when we see them rewarded (vicarious reinforcement).
  • Operant conditioning: behaviour is shaped by reinforcement (positive and negative) and punishment (positive and negative), using primary and secondary reinforcers. Key question: why do people become criminals? Partly because crime is learned.
  • Eysenck: personality varies on extraversion–introversion, neuroticism and psychoticism; the "criminal personality" scores high on all three.
  • Personality is largely inherited through the nervous system; under-arousal and poor conditioning lead to weak socialisation. Key question: why do people become criminals? Partly because of the personality they are born with.
  • Offenders often score higher, but many high scorers never offend, so the theory explains only part of criminal behaviour.
  • Recidivism is reoffending; prison, community sentences and restorative justice each have strengths and weaknesses.
  • Short prison sentences have very high reoffending rates; community sentences and restorative justice are linked to less reoffending for similar offenders.
  • Key question: why do people become criminals, and can punishment stop them reoffending? The evidence suggests changing behaviour works better than punishment alone.
  • Token economies use operant conditioning: tokens (secondary reinforcers) for target behaviours, exchanged for rewards; behaviour improves inside but may not last outside.
  • Anger management is cognitive-behavioural: cognitive preparation, skill acquisition and application practice; the skills can be used after release, but only help anger-driven offending.
  • Key question: why do people become criminals? If offending is learned, treatments based on learning and thinking can help reduce it.
