Mission 1

The nation's accounts

  • The balance of payments records all transactions between a country's residents and the rest of the world over a period (a quarter or a year).
  • Credit (+): money flows in (a payment received from abroad).
  • Debit (−): money flows out (a payment made abroad).
  • Three accounts:
    • current account: trade and income flows
    • capital account: capital transfers (small)
    • financial account: buying and selling assets, lending and borrowing
  • Purpose: it shows how a country pays its way in the world, and whether it is borrowing from abroad or lending.
  • The current account balance, often as a % of GDP, is a key macroeconomic indicator.
Mission 1

Inside the current account

PartCredit (+) exampleDebit (−) example
Trade in goodsCar exportedOil imported
Trade in servicesForeign tourist's hotel billInsurance bought from abroad
Primary incomeProfits and interest earned on assets abroadDividends paid to foreign shareholders
Secondary incomeMoney sent home by nationals working abroadForeign aid given
  • Balance of trade = exports − imports of goods and services.
  • Primary income: wages, interest, profits and dividends earned abroad.
  • Secondary income: transfers with nothing in return.
  • Credits > debits on an account: a surplus. Debits > credits: a deficit.
  • Say: "a current account deficit" when debits exceed credits — not: "the country made a loss".

Mission 1

Capital and financial accounts

  • Capital account (small): capital transfers, e.g. debt forgiveness; buying or selling non-produced assets, e.g. land for an embassy or a brand name.
  • Financial account: cross-border buying and selling of assets.
    • Foreign direct investment (FDI): buying at least 10% of a firm, or building a factory abroad. A lasting interest and some control.
    • Portfolio investment: buying shares (under 10%) or bonds for a financial return. No control, and easy to sell quickly.
    • Other investment: bank loans and deposits.
    • Reserve assets: the central bank's foreign currency and gold.
  • Credit (+): foreign residents buy our assets or lend to us (money in).
  • Debit (−): our residents buy foreign assets; or the central bank adds to its reserves (money out).
  • Say: "FDI is a financial account flow" — not: "an export", even though money comes in.
Mission 1

Why it always sums to zero

  • Every transaction is recorded twice: once as a credit and once as a debit (double-entry).
  • Example: a firm imports machines worth 10 bn.
    • Current account: import of goods, −10.
    • The foreign seller is paid in our currency and holds it in our banks: a foreign-owned asset, so financial account +10.
  • So a current account deficit is matched by a net inflow on the financial account: borrowing from abroad, selling assets to foreigners, or running down reserves.
  • Current + capital + financial account + net errors and omissions = 0.
  • Errors and omissions: a balancing item for transactions that are missed or measured wrongly.
  • Say: "a current account deficit" — not: "a balance of payments deficit". The balance of payments as a whole always balances.

Mission 2

Why imbalances arise

  • Cyclical deficit: in a boom, incomes rise and spending on imports rises. Firms also sell more at home and less abroad. It shrinks in a downturn.
  • Structural deficit: it persists even at normal levels of output, because of weak competitiveness:
    • productivity growing more slowly than in other countries
    • inflation (costs and prices) higher than in trading partners
    • a strong, overvalued exchange rate
    • non-price weakness: quality, design, a small export sector
  • Saving and investment: a country that invests more than it saves must borrow the gap from abroad, so it runs a current account deficit. A country that saves more than it invests runs a surplus.
  • External shocks: a rise in oil prices widens deficits of oil importers and surpluses of oil exporters; a world recession cuts everyone's exports.
  • Say: "a current account deficit" — not: "a balance of payments deficit".
Quick check
?Illustrative data. Country Q's current account deficit widened from 1% to 4% of GDP during a two-year consumer boom. When growth slowed, it shrank back to 1%. Q's costs and prices rose no faster than its trading partners'. What best describes the extra deficit?
Mission 2

A persistent deficit: why it can matter

  • Exchange rate: pressure to depreciate; dearer imports add to inflation.
  • Interest rates: higher rates may be needed to attract the inflows that finance the deficit. This hurts investment.
  • Foreign ownership: assets are sold to foreigners, so future profits and dividends flow out (primary income debits).
  • Debt: borrowing builds up debt to the rest of the world, with interest to pay.
  • Credit rating: a large, persistent deficit can lower it, raising the cost of borrowing.
  • Demand management: the government may have to use contractionary fiscal or monetary policy to cut spending on imports, at a cost to output and jobs.
  • Economic growth: negative net exports are a drag on AD now, and jobs are lost in export and import-competing industries. But if the deficit pays for investment, it can raise growth later.
  • Links to other objectives: fast growth often worsens the current account as imports rise. Consumers gain cheap imports; firms competing with imports lose.
Mission 2

A persistent surplus: problems too

  • Domestic consumption and investment: lower than the economy's output. Living standards could be higher now.
  • Exchange rate: pressure to appreciate, which erodes export competitiveness over time.
  • Inflation: strong export demand adds to AD and can pull prices up; an appreciating currency makes imports cheaper, which pulls the other way.
  • Employment: good for jobs in export industries, while it lasts.
  • The surplus is lent or invested abroad: it may earn low returns or be lost.
  • One country's surplus is others' deficit: trading partners may complain or turn to protectionism.

Mission 2

Two kinds of policy

  • Expenditure switching: changes relative prices, so spending switches from imports to home-produced goods.
    • a depreciation or devaluation of the currency
    • protectionism: tariffs, quotas, export subsidies
  • Expenditure reducing: cuts total spending, so imports fall too.
    • contractionary fiscal policy: higher taxes, lower government spending
    • contractionary monetary policy: higher interest rates
  • Supply-side policies: long-term competitiveness: training, infrastructure, research, flexible markets.
  • Say: "expenditure reducing cuts all spending, so imports fall with it" — not: "it cuts spending on imports only".
Mission 2

Worked example: the cost of expenditure reducing

  • Illustrative data. Current account deficit: 20 bn. GDP: 1,000 bn.
  • For every 1 bn fall in income, spending on imports falls by 0.25 bn.
  • This is the marginal propensity to import (MPM) = 0.25.
  • Fall in income needed = 20 ÷ 0.25 = 80 bn.
  • That is 80 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = 8% of GDP: a deep recession, with higher unemployment.
  • Not 20 bn: most of the lost spending falls on domestic goods, not imports.

Mission 2

Switching, supply side and surpluses

  • Depreciation: works only if demand for exports and imports is elastic enough (Marshall–Lerner), and only after a lag (J-curve); dearer imports raise inflation.
  • Protectionism: risks retaliation, can break trade rules (WTO), and raises prices for consumers and firms.
  • Supply-side policies: tackle the cause of a structural deficit, but are slow and uncertain.
  • To reduce a surplus:
    • expansionary fiscal or monetary policy: more spending at home, so more imports
    • allow the currency to appreciate
    • remove barriers to imports
Mission 2Evaluate

How much does a deficit matter, and which policy works?

It depends on:

  • The cause: a cyclical deficit corrects itself; a structural one needs supply-side policy.
  • Size, persistence and financing: a small deficit funded by long-term FDI is safer than a large one funded by short-term portfolio flows ("hot money"), which can leave suddenly.
  • What it pays for: investment can raise future output; consumption cannot repay the debt.
  • Conflicts with other objectives: expenditure reducing costs growth and jobs; depreciation adds to inflation.
  • Elasticities and time lags: switching works slowly, and only if demand responds enough.
Mission check

?Country W has a large, persistent current account surplus that its trading partners complain about. Which policy would reduce the surplus by expenditure switching?

Switching policies work through relative prices. To shrink a surplus, they must make imports relatively cheaper, not dearer.

Mission 3

Who wants a currency, and who sells it?

  • A floating exchange rate is set by demand for and supply of a currency.
  • Demand for the home currency comes from foreigners who need it to:
    • buy the country's exports of goods and services
    • buy its financial assets: bonds, shares, bank deposits (portfolio investment)
    • set up or buy firms there (inward FDI)
  • Supply of the home currency comes from residents who sell it to buy imports and foreign assets.
  • Financial flows are huge and move in seconds. News about interest rates hits the currency within minutes.
Mission 3

Money flows toward the higher real interest rate

  • Investors compare the return on assets in different countries.
  • Real interest rate ≈ nominal interest rate − inflation rate.
  • Illustrative data:
Nominal rateInflationReal rate
Country A5%4%1%
Country B3%1%2%
  • A has the higher nominal rate, but B has the higher real rate.
  • Other things equal, financial capital flows out of A and into B.
  • Demand for B's currency rises: B's currency appreciates.
  • Large, fast, short-term flows chasing returns are called hot money.
  • Say: "capital flows toward the higher real interest rate" — not: toward the higher nominal rate.
Quick check

Mission 3

From a rate decision to the whole economy

  • The central bank cuts Bank Rate:
  • Real interest rates fall in the short run → financial capital flows out → the currency depreciates.
  • Exports become cheaper abroad; imports more expensive at home → net exports rise → AD rises.
  • Effects:
    • output rises and unemployment falls (if there is spare capacity)
    • inflation rises: more expensive imports, and AD closer to capacity
    • the current account tends to improve, after a time lag
  • A rate rise works the other way: appreciation, lower net exports, lower AD.
  • Quantitative easing (QE): the central bank creates money to buy bonds. Bond yields fall, so investors move money abroad: the currency tends to depreciate.
  • So in a global economy, monetary policy works through two channels: domestic spending and the exchange rate.
Quick check
?A central bank raises its policy interest rate by 1 percentage point. Inflation expectations do not change. Which chain of effects is most likely?
Mission 3

Beyond interest rates

  • Relative inflation: faster inflation at home makes exports less competitive. Demand for the currency falls; it depreciates.
  • Relative growth: faster growth draws in FDI and portfolio investment (currency up), but also raises imports (currency down).
  • Confidence: investors avoid countries with political or financial risk.
  • Speculation: if traders expect a currency to rise, they buy it now, and it rises.
  • Safe haven: in a crisis, investors buy currencies seen as safe.
  • Central bank intervention: buying the home currency with foreign reserves raises demand for it; selling it lowers its value.
  • Real case: in 2011, during the euro-area debt crisis, money poured into the Swiss franc. The Swiss National Bank set a minimum exchange rate of 1.20 francs per euro and sold francs to defend it.
  • On 15 January 2015 it dropped the minimum rate and cut its interest rate to −0.75%. The franc rose sharply against the euro.
  • The rate cut alone would pull the franc down. Ending the sales of francs outweighed it.
Quick check
?Investors expect Country K's currency to fall next month. Many sell it today. What is the most likely result?
Mission 3

Case: the yen and the dollar in 2022

  • In 2022 the US central bank (the Federal Reserve) raised its target for the federal funds rate from 0–0.25% to 4.25–4.50%.
  • The Bank of Japan kept its short-term policy rate at −0.1%.
  • The gap in interest rates widened, and funds flowed toward US assets.
  • Real returns rose too: the yield on 10-year inflation-protected US government bonds went from −0.97% (3 January) to 1.73% (20 October).
  • Yen per US dollar: 115.27 on 3 January 2022; 149.82 on 20 October 2022.
  • Worked numbers:
    • Value of the dollar in yen: (149.82 − 115.27) ÷ 115.27 × 100 = +30.0%
    • Value of the yen in dollars: 1 ÷ 115.27 = 0.008675; 1 ÷ 149.82 = 0.006675. Change = −23.1%
  • A 30% rise in one currency is not a 30% fall in the other.
  • Japan's authorities intervened as the yen fell: on 22 September, 21 October and 24 October 2022 they sold dollars and bought yen.
Mission 3Evaluate

Does a higher interest rate always lift the currency?

It depends on:

  • Real, not nominal: if inflation rises faster than the rate, the real return falls.
  • Expectations: markets often price in a rise before it happens. A rise smaller than expected can make a currency fall.
  • Other countries: if foreign central banks raise rates too, the gap may not change.
  • Confidence and risk: a rate rise to defend a currency in a crisis may be read as a sign of trouble.
  • Other forces: trade flows, growth, speculation and safe-haven moves can outweigh the rate change.
  • So: a rate rise tends to cause appreciation, other things equal. In practice other things are rarely equal.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. Country R's central bank keeps its nominal interest rate at 4%, while R's inflation rate rises from 2% to 5%. Interest rates and inflation abroad do not change. What is the most likely effect on R's currency?

Investors look through the headline rate to the real return, so inflation can move a currency even when the central bank stands still.

Mission 4

Steps of economic integration

  • Countries can tie their economies together step by step.
  • Free trade area: no tariffs or quotas between members; each keeps its own tariffs on outsiders.
  • Customs union: free trade area + a common external tariff.
  • Common market: customs union + free movement of labour and capital.
  • Monetary union: members share a single currency, managed by one central bank that sets one monetary policy (one interest rate) for all.
  • No exchange rate between members, so no member can devalue against another.
  • Say: "a single currency and a single monetary policy" — not: "a single currency" alone.
Mission 4

The eurozone and the ECB

  • The euro began on 1 January 1999 (accounts and electronic payments); notes and coins followed in 2002.
  • In 2026, 21 of the 27 EU countries use the euro. Bulgaria joined most recently (2026).
  • The European Central Bank (ECB) sets monetary policy for the whole euro area. Its Governing Council includes the governors of every member's national central bank.
  • Primary objective: price stability, aiming for 2% inflation over the medium term.
  • One ECB interest rate applies in every member, whatever its own inflation or unemployment.
  • Fiscal policy (taxes, government spending) stays with each national government, within EU rules on deficits and debt.
Quick check
?Three neighbouring countries already have free trade, a common external tariff and free movement of workers and capital between them. Which extra step would turn them into a monetary union?
Mission 4

What a single currency gains

  • No transaction costs: no fees for changing currency when trading within the union.
  • No exchange-rate uncertainty within the union: firms can price and plan without currency risk.
  • So more trade and more investment (FDI) between members are likely.
  • Price transparency: prices in one currency are easy to compare, which increases competition.
  • Credibility: a country with a history of high inflation can "borrow" the low-inflation record of a strong central bank, so inflation expectations and interest rates may fall.
  • Illustrative data: a firm sells 10 million worth of goods a year to other members, and currency changing costs 1%. Single currency saves 10 m × 0.01 = 0.1 m a year.
Mission 4

What a member gives up

  • No independent monetary policy: the central bank sets one interest rate for the whole union.
  • No exchange rate of its own to fall when its exports become uncompetitive.
  • So it loses two tools for dealing with an asymmetric shock: a shock that hits one member but not others.
  • A rate that suits the average member may be too high for one in recession and too low for one that is booming: "one size fits all".
  • Fiscal rules limit how far a government can borrow to support demand.
  • Real data: in 2013, unemployment averaged 27.8% in Greece and 4.9% in Germany. Both had the same ECB interest rate.
  • Adjustment then falls on wages and prices: an internal devaluation, which is usually slow and painful.
Mission 4

Worked example: one interest rate, different real rates

  • Illustrative data. Union central bank rate = 2% for all members.
  • Real interest rate ≈ nominal rate − inflation rate.
  • Country X is booming; inflation 5%. Real rate ≈ 2 − 5 = −3%.
  • Country Y is in recession; inflation 1%. Real rate ≈ 2 − 1 = +1%.
  • Borrowing is cheapest in real terms where the economy is already overheating.
  • So one rate can make the boom bigger in X and the slump deeper in Y.

  • Takeaway: outside the union, a fall in B's currency and interest rate cushions the shock. Inside, B must wait for internal devaluation.

Mission 4

When does sharing a currency work? The optimal currency area

  • Optimal currency area (Mundell, 1961): a region where the gains of one currency outweigh the costs of losing separate exchange rates.
  • Conditions that make a monetary union more likely to succeed:
    • similar economic cycles: shocks hit members at the same time (symmetric), so one interest rate suits them all
    • labour mobility: workers move from slumping to booming regions
    • flexible wages and prices: costs can fall quickly where demand is weak
    • fiscal transfers: a shared budget sends money to regions in recession
    • high trade between members: the savings on transaction costs and currency risk are larger
  • Compare: US states share one currency, with a large federal budget that pays more benefits and collects less tax in states with weak economies.
  • Each condition is a matter of degree. Economists disagree on how far the eurozone meets them.

Mission 4

Joining the euro: the convergence criteria

  • A prospective member must first meet the convergence criteria (set in the Maastricht Treaty):
CriterionReference value
InflationNot more than 1.5 percentage points above the average of the three best-performing EU countries
Government deficitNot more than 3% of GDP
Government debtNot more than 60% of GDP, or falling towards it at a satisfactory pace
Exchange rateAt least 2 years in ERM II without severe tensions or devaluing
Long-term interest rateNot more than 2 percentage points above the three best-performing EU countries
  • Aim: members should have converged before they lose their own monetary policy.
  • Critics argue these criteria test nominal convergence (inflation, interest rates, budgets), not similar cycles, labour mobility or flexible wages.
  • For a prospective member: gains in trade and credibility vs loss of its own interest rate and exchange rate.

Mission 4

Expansion and contraction: the UK

  • Expansion: in 2004, ten countries joined the EU, most of them from central and eastern Europe.
  • Effects on the UK included a larger single market and inward migration that added to the labour supply. Effects on wages and public services are debated.
  • Contraction: the UK left the EU on 31 January 2020; the transition period ended on 31 December 2020.
  • Possible costs: new non-tariff barriers (customs checks, rules of origin) on trade with the EU; less migration from the EU.
  • Possible gains: freedom to set its own trade policy and agreements, and its own regulation.
  • Changes in the EU's structure: if the EU changes how it works (for example, closer fiscal integration among euro members, or new single market rules), UK firms that trade with the EU are affected even though the UK is outside.
  • The size of the long-run effects is still estimated and debated: weigh the evidence.
Mission 4Evaluate

Is a single currency worth it?

  • It depends on:
    • how similar members' economies are: symmetric shocks make one interest rate suit everyone
    • how well other shock absorbers work: labour mobility, flexible wages, fiscal transfers
    • how much members trade with each other: bigger trade means bigger gains from one currency
    • credibility: a country with a record of high inflation may gain more than one with a trusted central bank
  • Time matters: gains (lower costs, more trade) build slowly; the cost of a bad shock can arrive suddenly.
  • For a prospective member, the answer may change as its economy converges with the union's.
Mission check

?Northland and Southland share one currency and one central bank. World demand for timber, Southland's main export, collapses. Northland sells no timber and is unaffected. Why might Southland's recession last longer than it would with its own floating currency?

Sharing a currency removes two shock absorbers, so a shock that hits one member alone must be absorbed by its wages, prices and jobs.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Current account: goods, services, primary income, secondary income.
  • Capital and financial accounts record capital transfers and the buying and selling of assets (FDI, portfolio, other investment, reserves).
  • Current + capital + financial + errors and omissions = 0.

Mission 2

  • Imbalances can be cyclical or structural, and reflect saving and investment.
  • Persistent deficits and persistent surpluses both carry costs.
  • Expenditure switching changes relative prices; expenditure reducing cuts total spending (and output); supply-side policy works on competitiveness over time.

Mission 3

  • Capital flows toward the higher real interest rate, so a rise in a country's real rate tends to make its currency appreciate.
  • Monetary policy works through the exchange rate: a rate cut → depreciation → higher net exports and AD.
  • Inflation, growth, confidence, speculation, safe-haven demand and intervention also move currencies.

Mission 4

  • A monetary union shares one currency and one central bank setting one interest rate.
  • Gains: no transaction costs or currency risk within the union, more trade and investment, price transparency. Costs: no own interest rate or exchange rate to deal with asymmetric shocks.
  • It works best in an optimal currency area: similar cycles, mobile labour, flexible wages, fiscal transfers, high trade.
