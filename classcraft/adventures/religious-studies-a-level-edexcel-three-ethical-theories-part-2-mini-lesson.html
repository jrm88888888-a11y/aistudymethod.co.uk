Mission 1

How to apply situation ethics

  • 1. The situation: what exactly is happening, and what is at stake?
  • 2. The people: who is affected? (personalism: persons, not rules, come first.)
  • 3. The rules: what guidance does the community's experience give? (useful, not binding.)
  • 4. Love's verdict: which choice brings the most agape to everyone affected? (pragmatism, relativism, with love as the one absolute.)
  • Fletcher's answers can differ from case to case, by design.
Mission 1

Fletcher's own case: Mrs Bergmeier

  • From Fletcher's book (paraphrase): at the end of the Second World War, a German mother is held in a Soviet camp in Ukraine. Her husband and children, back in Berlin, are searching for her.
  • Only the sick and the pregnant are being released. She asks a guard to make her pregnant; she is released and reunited with her family.
  • The family welcome the baby, and love him for what he did for them.
  • Fletcher asks: did she do right? The rule against adultery is set beside the good of her husband, her children and the new child.
  • Fletcher's point is the question, not a fixed answer: legalism cannot even see the dilemma.
Quick check

Mission 1

Situation ethics and utilitarianism

  • Similar: both are teleological and consequentialist; both judge acts case by case; both reject fixed rules as final.
  • Fletcher borrows the utilitarian procedure: an "agapeic calculus", seeking the greatest amount of neighbour welfare for the largest number of neighbours (paraphrase).
  • Different: the good maximised is agape, not pleasure or happiness.
  • Different: Fletcher's starting point is a faith commitment (positivism); utilitarianism claims to rest on observable facts about pleasure and pain.
Mission 1Read the source

Barclay's critique

William Barclay (1907–1978), Scottish New Testament scholar, Ethics in a Permissive Society (1971), chapter 4 (paraphrase):

  • Agrees: love is central; rigid legalism is wrong; morality needs freedom.
  • Extreme cases: most of Fletcher's examples are extraordinary. Ordinary life needs ordinary rules; hard cases do not show we can do without law.
  • Too much freedom: deciding everything alone, with no ready answer, is a burden most people are not ready to carry.
  • People need law: as a child is trained by habit, people need rules; without love, freedom easily becomes licence.
  • Grace is missing: Fletcher says little about God's power to change people.
  • This is anthology extract 1: paraphrase it; do not quote at length.

Q1 What does Barclay accept from Fletcher?

Show answer

Love is central; legalism is wrong.

Q2 Why does he think law is still needed?

Show answer

Most people do not want total freedom; rules train us, and freedom without love becomes licence.

Mission 1

Is Fletcher's agape really religious?

  • Religious, yes: agape comes from the New Testament (1 Corinthians 13; Luke 10); positivism means accepting "God is love" by faith.
  • Not really: the method never needs God; love could mean "wanting the best for the person involved". A humanist could use it unchanged.
  • Fletcher himself later left Christian belief and was named Humanist of the Year by the American Humanist Association (1974).
  • Individualistic and subjective? Without absolute rules, each person decides alone; two people may reach opposite "loving" answers.
  • Reply: love is not whatever I feel; it is judged by its effects on others, and community rules still guide.
Mission 1Diversity

How churches responded

  • Catholic: Pope Pius XII criticised "situation ethics" in 1952, and the Holy Office (1956) forbade it in Catholic teaching; Veritatis Splendor (1993) teaches that some acts are always wrong.
  • Protestant critics: Paul Ramsey, Deeds and Rules in Christian Ethics (1967): love itself needs rules ("rule agapism").
  • Liberal Protestants: Robinson and others welcomed the stress on persons over codes.
  • Evangelicals: generally reject it: scripture's commands are God's revealed will, not optional guides.
  • Spec: "compatibility or otherwise with religious approaches".
Mission 1

The new morality, law and society

  • Britain in the late 1960s changed several laws on personal morality: the Abortion Act 1967; the Sexual Offences Act 1967 (partial decriminalisation of male homosexual acts in England and Wales); the Divorce Reform Act 1969.
  • Supporters often argued from compassion for persons in hard situations, close to situationist reasoning.
  • How far Robinson and Fletcher caused these changes is debated; wider secular and liberal forces were at work too.
  • Continuing application: medical ethics (e.g. end-of-life decisions) still uses case-by-case, person-centred reasoning.
  • Critics link the period to the "permissive society" Barclay's title names.
Mission 1Evaluate

Is situation ethics a helpful method?

  • Claim: Situation ethics is a helpful method of moral decision-making.
  • For: Person-centred; flexible in hard cases; avoids legalism's cruelty; one clear principle; rooted in Jesus' ethic of love.
  • Against: Consequences are hard to predict; love's verdict is subjective; it can seem to justify adultery or killing (Barclay; Pius XII).
  • Against: It may promote immoral behaviour by those who rationalise their wishes as "love"; it may be humanism with Christian words.
  • Judgement criteria: Can ordinary people use it day to day? Is agape the only intrinsic good? Does it keep what religious rules protect? Does it need God?
Quick check

Barclay's challenge is not that love is wrong. It is that ordinary people need rules to learn to love well. Weigh that against Fletcher's hard cases.

Mission 2

Mission 2: If humans are made for a purpose, can reason alone work out how we should live?

Make a guess: If humans are made for a purpose, can reason alone work out how we should live?

Mission 2

Aristotle: everything has a telos

  • Telos (Greek): end, goal, purpose.
  • Aristotle (384–322 BCE), Physics II.3: four causes (explanations) of a thing: material, formal, efficient and final: "that for the sake of which" it exists.
  • An acorn's telos is to become an oak; a knife's is to cut well.
  • A thing is good when it fulfils its telos.
  • Nicomachean Ethics I: the human telos is eudaimonia (flourishing), achieved by living according to reason and virtue.
  • Classical development: the Stoics and Cicero (106–43 BCE, On the Republic III) spoke of a true law of right reason, in agreement with nature, the same everywhere (paraphrase).
Mission 2

Aquinas: God's purpose, found by reason

  • Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274), Summa Theologiae I-II, questions 90–97 (the "Treatise on Law").
  • He joins Aristotle's telos to Christian belief: God created everything with a purpose.
  • The human ultimate end is union with God (the beatific vision), which needs grace; our natural ends point towards it.
  • Biblical foundation: Paul says that Gentiles who do not have the law show "the work of the law written in their hearts, their conscience testifying with them" (Romans 2:15, World English Bible, British Edition).
  • So moral truth is built into human nature, and every rational person can discover it: believer or not.
Mission 2

Four tiers of law

  • Eternal law: the principles by which God made and governs the universe; fully known only to God.
  • Divine law: God's law revealed in the Bible, especially the Ten Commandments and the Sermon on the Mount. Needed because human reason is limited and we are made for an end beyond nature.
  • Natural law: the moral law within human nature, discoverable by reason. Aquinas: the rational creature's "participation" in the eternal law (ST I-II q. 91 a. 2).
  • Human law: the laws of nations, which should be derived from natural law (ST I-II q. 95).
  • They form a chain: eternal → (revealed as) divine, (shared with reason as) natural → (applied as) human.

?A country passes a law setting a speed limit of 30 mph near schools, so that children are protected. Which tier of law is this, and how is it related to the others?
Mission 2Read the source

The first precept

Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I-II, question 94, article 2 (translated by the Fathers of the English Dominican Province, 1920):

> "Hence this is the first precept of law, that 'good is to be done and pursued, and evil is to be avoided.'"

  • All other precepts are based on this one.
  • Humans have natural inclinations (paraphrase of the same article): to preserve their life; what nature teaches all animals, such as sexual union and the education of offspring; and, as rational beings, to know the truth about God and to live in society.

Q1 Why is this first precept self-evident, for Aquinas?

Show answer

Everyone acts for what they see as good; "good" means what all things seek.

Q2 Which inclination is shared with all animals, and which is distinctly human?

Show answer

Reproduction and raising young are shared; knowing truth about God and living in society are rational.

Mission 2

The five primary precepts

  • Derived from the first precept and from our natural inclinations:
  • Preservation of life (self and innocent others)
  • Reproduction of the human species
  • Education of children
  • Living in an ordered society (ordering of society)
  • Worship of God
  • Primary precepts are universal and unchanging: true for all people at all times.
  • The list of five is a modern summary of ST I-II q. 94 a. 2; Aquinas does not number them.
Mission 2

Secondary precepts

  • Secondary precepts: more specific rules derived by reason from the primary precepts (e.g. preservation of life → do not murder; ordered society → do not steal; reproduction → marriage).
  • They are less certain: the more detail, the more exceptions may appear.
  • Aquinas' own example (ST I-II q. 94 a. 4, paraphrase): goods held in trust should be returned to the owner. But not if the owner wants them to fight against his own country.
  • People can mistake secondary precepts because of bad habits or customs (q. 94 a. 6).
  • So: primary precepts fixed; secondary precepts reasoned, open to error and to some variation.
Quick check
?Which statement is a secondary precept rather than a primary precept?
Mission 2

Absolutist, legalist, deontological, teleological?

  • Absolutist: primary precepts hold always and everywhere; some acts (e.g. killing the innocent) are always wrong.
  • Legalism: following moral law strictly; critics say natural law can become rigid rule-keeping. Defenders: secondary precepts need prudence, not mechanical rules.
  • Deontological: we have a duty to follow the precepts, whatever the consequences.
  • Teleological: the precepts exist because humans have a telos; acts are right when they fulfil our God-given ends.
Mission 2

Acts and goods

  • Interior act: the intention of the moral agent. Exterior act: the action actually done.
  • Both must be good (ST I-II qq. 18–20). Giving to charity in order to be praised: good exterior act, bad interior act. Lying to help a friend: good intention, bad act.
  • Intention matters, but a good intention cannot make a wrong act right.
  • Real goods: correctly reasoned goods that help us reach our telos.
  • Apparent goods: wrongly reasoned goods that seem good but do not fulfil our God-given purpose (e.g. an affair pursued for pleasure).
  • Aquinas: no one chooses evil as evil; sin comes from mistaking an apparent good for a real one, so moral failure is a failure of reason.
Quick check
?Leo cheats in a test to win a scholarship so that he can support his family. How would Aquinas assess his act?
Mission 2Worked answer

Explaining the precepts

Task: explain how the primary and secondary precepts are related.

  • Point: Primary precepts are general and universal; secondary precepts are specific rules derived from them by reason.
  • Source: Aquinas, ST I-II q. 94 a. 2: the first precept "good is to be done and pursued, and evil is to be avoided".
  • Develop: From "preserve life" reason derives "do not murder"; from "ordered society", "do not steal".
  • Develop further: Secondary precepts are less certain; Aquinas' example of goods held in trust shows that they can fail in particular cases.
  • Link: This flexibility is the basis of later debate about whether natural law is legalistic.

Mission 3: Can an act that causes a death still be good if the death was not what you aimed at?

Make a guess: Can an act that causes a death still be good if the death was not what you aimed at?

Mission 3

One act, two effects

  • Primary precepts can clash: protecting my life vs preserving another's.
  • Aquinas' answer: one act can have two effects: one intended, the other foreseen but not intended.
  • An act's moral character comes from what is intended, not from what is beside the intention.
  • So it can be right to do a good act with a bad side-effect, but never to aim at an evil.
  • This is the principle (doctrine) of double effect.
Mission 3Read the source

Self-defence

Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II, question 64, article 7 (translated by the Fathers of the English Dominican Province, 1920):

> "Nothing hinders one act from having two effects, only one of which is intended, while the other is beside the intention."

  • Same article, paraphrased: self-defence may have two effects: saving one's own life and killing the attacker. Saving one's life is intended, so the act is not unlawful.
  • But if someone uses more violence than necessary, the act becomes unlawful: it must be proportionate.
  • A private person may not intend to kill; only public authority may (for the common good).

Q1 What makes the killing permissible?

Show answer

It is not intended: the intention is to save one's life, and the force is proportionate.

Q2 What would make the same act wrong?

Show answer

Intending the death, or using more force than needed.

Mission 3

The four conditions

  • Later moral theologians set out four conditions (classic form: Jean-Pierre Gury, Compendium Theologiae Moralis, 1850):
  • 1. The act itself is good or morally neutral.
  • 2. Intention: only the good effect is intended; the bad effect is foreseen, not willed.
  • 3. Means: the bad effect is not the means to the good effect.
  • 4. Proportion: there is a proportionately serious reason for allowing the bad effect.
  • All four must be met. Condition 3 is the one most often misunderstood.
Quick check
?A bomber pilot destroys a weapons factory, foreseeing that some nearby civilians will die. A second pilot bombs civilians deliberately so that the enemy will lose the will to fight. Why does double effect treat them differently?
Mission 3

Applying it: abortion

  • Primary precepts: preservation of life and reproduction. Catholic teaching applies them from conception: direct abortion is always gravely wrong (John Paul II, Evangelium Vitae, 1995, §62).
  • Double effect allows some indirect cases, in which the fetus' death is foreseen but not intended:
  • Cancerous womb: removing it to save the mother's life, though the fetus dies.
  • Ectopic pregnancy: removing the damaged fallopian tube (salpingectomy) is widely accepted by Catholic moralists; other methods are debated among them.
  • Critics: the distinction can seem artificial when the outcome is the same; Aquinas himself (following Aristotle) held that the rational soul comes some weeks after conception, though the Church now protects life from conception.
Quick check
?A pregnant woman is diagnosed with aggressive cancer of the womb. Doctors remove the womb to save her life; the fetus, too young to survive, dies. How do most Catholic moral theologians, using natural law, judge this?
Mission 3Diversity

Natural law and religious approaches

  • Catholic: central to moral teaching (e.g. Humanae Vitae, 1968, on contraception; Veritatis Splendor, 1993). Magisterium interprets it.
  • Anglican: Richard Hooker, Of the Laws of Ecclesiastical Polity (from 1594), uses natural law alongside scripture.
  • Many Protestants are wary: the Fall has damaged human reason; scripture should come first. Karl Barth (1886–1968) rejected natural theology.
  • Others use natural law widely: C. S. Lewis, The Abolition of Man (1943), on a shared moral law ("the Tao") across cultures.
  • Reason without revelation: some natural-law thinkers (e.g. Hugo Grotius, a Protestant; John Finnis, a Catholic) argue it can be defended by reason alone, without first appealing to revelation.
  • Also weigh: is natural law compatible with religious approaches to ethics, or not?
Mission 3

Strengths

  • Universal and objective: the same basic goods for all humans; gives a basis for criticising unjust laws and for human rights. Martin Luther King Jr appealed to Aquinas on unjust law in his "Letter from Birmingham Jail" (1963).
  • Reason-based: open to people of any religion or none.
  • Clear guidance: primary precepts are firm; protects the vulnerable (unborn, disabled, dying).
  • Flexible in application: secondary precepts, prudence and double effect allow for hard cases.
  • Focus on character: the virtues and the interior act, not just rules.
Mission 3

Weaknesses

  • Is–ought gap: David Hume (1711–1776), A Treatise of Human Nature (1739–40): we cannot derive what ought to be from what is. G. E. Moore, Principia Ethica (1903): the naturalistic fallacy.
  • Does nature have a telos? After Darwin (On the Origin of Species, 1859), many see nature as the product of natural selection, not design.
  • Human nature varies: people and cultures differ; critics say "natural" has been used to condemn contraception and same-sex relationships, which defenders of natural law and their critics dispute.
  • Rigidity: absolute precepts can produce harsh results in extreme cases (proportionalism, is one response).
  • Double effect can seem like re-describing what you intend.
Mission 3Evaluate

Does natural law still work?

  • Claim: Natural law is an effective approach to ethical issues today.
  • For: Gives firm protection to life; based on reason, so it can guide law in plural societies; double effect handles real dilemmas (self-defence, bombing in war).
  • For: double effect allows treatment of a cancerous womb.
  • Against: Is–ought gap; contested telos after Darwin; absolutes may cause suffering in extreme cases; intention is hard to judge.
  • Against: e.g. natural law allows no direct abortion even for a girl pregnant after rape.
  • Law and attitudes: The law in England and Wales now permits abortion (Abortion Act 1967), contraception and same-sex marriage (Marriage (Same Sex Couples) Act 2013), all contrary to traditional natural-law teaching. Critics see the theory as out of step; defenders say law should follow moral truth, not opinion.
  • Without God? Grotius (On the Law of War and Peace, 1625) said natural law would hold even if, hypothetically, God did not exist; Aquinas grounds it in the eternal law.
  • Judgement criteria: Can ethics be grounded in human nature? Should human law follow natural law? Do intentions change the morality of an act?

Double effect turns on intention and means, not just outcome. Whether that distinction is wise or artificial is the heart of the debate about natural law.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Can it be right to break a moral rule without doing anything morally evil?

Make a guess: Can it be right to break a moral rule without doing anything morally evil?

Mission 4

The problem with exceptionless rules

  • Aquinas' natural law gives secondary precepts: e.g. do not kill the innocent, do not lie.
  • Traditional Catholic moral theology holds that some acts are intrinsically evil: wrong in themselves, whatever the intention or circumstances.
  • Hard cases press on this: a lie that would save a life; a treatment that saves a mother but ends a pregnancy.
  • The doctrine of double effect helps in some cases, but only where the harm is not intended (see Mission 3).
  • Proportionalism asks: can a rule be broken, in a rare case, without the act being morally wrong?
Mission 4

Where proportionalism came from

  • A debate among Catholic moral theologians, mostly from the 1960s to the 1980s.
  • Peter Knauer SJ re-read double effect (1965): what matters is whether there is a proportionate reason for allowing a harm.
  • Louis Janssens (1908–2001), "Ontic Evil and Moral Evil" (1972); Josef Fuchs SJ (1912–2005); Richard McCormick SJ (1922–2000) developed the idea, especially in the United States.
  • The debate sharpened after Humanae Vitae (1968), Pope Paul VI's teaching that artificial contraception is always wrong.
  • Bernard Hoose, Proportionalism: The American Debate and its European Roots (1987), surveys the whole debate and draws out what the proportionalists share.
  • Your spec's scholars for natural law: Aquinas and B Hoose.
Mission 4Read the source

The proportionalist maxim

Hoose's overview gathers the proportionalists' central principle (paraphrase):

> A moral rule should not be broken, unless there is a proportionate reason that would justify breaking it.

  • Rules stay in force. They are the normal guide, not mere advice.
  • The exception needs a reason in proportion to the harm done: serious harm needs a serious reason.

Q1 Does the maxim say rules are unimportant?

Show answer

No: breaking one always needs a justifying reason.

Q2 What must the reason be "in proportion" to?

Show answer

The evil involved in breaking the rule.

Mission 4

Pre-moral (ontic) evil and moral evil

  • Pre-moral evil (also ontic or non-moral evil): a harm or loss in itself, before any moral judgement: pain, injury, death, loss of property, a false statement.
  • Moral evil: an act that is morally wrong: causing pre-moral evil without a proportionate reason.
  • Example: a surgeon amputates a gangrenous leg. Losing a leg is a pre-moral evil; saving the patient's life is a proportionate reason; the act is not morally evil.
  • So for proportionalists, an act cannot be judged wrong from its physical description alone ("cutting off a leg").
  • Intention, circumstances and consequences are all weighed.
Quick check
?To shelter a refugee family from a militia, Lena tells the militia that no one is in her house. How would a proportionalist describe the false statement itself?
Mission 4

Good acts and right acts

  • Proportionalists separate two questions.
  • Goodness belongs to the person: acting from love, with good motives. Good or bad.
  • Rightness belongs to the act: does it bring about the best possible balance of pre-moral good over pre-moral evil? Right or wrong.
  • So a good person can do a wrong act (meaning well, misjudging the situation), and a right act can be done from bad motives.
  • Example: Lena's lie breaks the rule (not a good act, on Eduqas' wording), yet it is the right act.
Mission 4

Proportion based on agape; a hybrid

  • How is proportion measured? Hoose's overview stresses agape: Christian self-giving love for every person affected.
  • The proportionate reason must be the one love would recognise, not convenience or self-interest.
  • Deontological side: rules and duties remain the starting point and normally bind.
  • Teleological side: in a conflict, the outcome (the balance of goods and evils) decides.
  • So proportionalism is often called a hybrid of natural law.
Quick check
?Which statement best expresses how a proportionalist treats a rule such as "do not lie"?
Mission 4Diversity

Critics and defenders within Catholic ethics

  • Pope John Paul II, Veritatis Splendor (1993), §§ 75–83 (paraphrase): rejects proportionalism by name; some acts are intrinsically evil "on account of their very object" (§ 80), and no intention or circumstance makes them right.
  • Germain Grisez and John Finnis: basic goods are incommensurable: they cannot be measured on one scale, so "weighing" them is an illusion.
  • Defenders (McCormick; Hoose): proportionalists do not say "the end justifies any means"; they say an act's object cannot be fixed without its circumstances. Many claim this continues Aquinas' own reasoning.
  • Official Catholic teaching since 1993 follows Veritatis Splendor; proportionalist reasoning survives mainly among revisionist theologians.
  • Protestant ethicists, with no fixed list of intrinsically evil acts, often reason in a similar way.
Mission 4Evaluate

Does proportionalism promote immoral behaviour?

  • Claim: Proportionalism opens the door to immoral behaviour.
  • For: Anyone can claim a "proportionate reason"; the test is subjective. Small exceptions may grow (slippery slope).
  • For: If goods are incommensurable (Grisez, Finnis), the weighing cannot be done. Veritatis Splendor: some acts are always wrong.
  • Against: Rules remain the normal guide; only a reason in proportion, judged by agape, can justify an exception.
  • Against: It avoids legalism's cruelty in rare hard cases, and double effect already weighs proportion.
  • Judgement criteria: Can goods really be compared? Who judges proportion? Does it keep or dissolve natural law? Does it fit how Christians actually reason in hard cases?
Mission 4Worked answer

Explaining a key distinction

Task: explain the proportionalist distinction between pre-moral and moral evil.

  • Point: Pre-moral (ontic) evil is a harm in itself, such as pain, death or a false statement; moral evil is causing it without a proportionate reason.
  • Scholar: Janssens named the distinction; Hoose's overview treats it as central to the debate.
  • Example: amputating a gangrenous leg causes pre-moral evil, but saving life is a proportionate reason, so the act is right.
  • Develop: So an act cannot be condemned from its physical description alone, against the idea of intrinsically evil acts.
Mission check

?A ship's captain, caught in a storm, throws the passengers' cargo overboard so the ship will not sink. A proportionalist is asked to judge the act. Which judgement fits proportionalism?

A proportionalist never says the rule does not matter. The harm stays real; the question is whether love can find a reason big enough to justify it.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Applying situation ethics: the situation, the people, the rules as guides, then love's verdict.
  • Close to utilitarianism in method (an "agapeic calculus"), but the good is agape. Critics: Barclay (extreme cases; the need for law; too much freedom), Catholic teaching (Pius XII, Veritatis Splendor), Ramsey; is agape really religious, and is the method subjective?

Mission 2

  • Telos: Aristotle's final cause, developed by Aquinas into God's purpose for creation. Four tiers of law: eternal, divine, natural, human; natural law is our rational share in eternal law.
  • Key precept "do good, avoid evil" → five primary precepts (life, reproduction, education, ordered society, worship of God) → secondary precepts. Interior and exterior acts must both be good; sin mistakes an apparent good for a real one.

Mission 3

  • Double effect (Aquinas, ST II-II 64.7): one act, two effects; the act must be good, the bad effect unintended, not the means, and proportionate.
  • Applied by Catholic natural-law thinkers: direct abortion is wrong; indirect cases (cancerous womb, ectopic pregnancy) may be permitted.
  • Strengths: universal, reason-based, protective, flexible in application; weaknesses: is–ought gap, contested telos, rigidity, hard-to-judge intentions.

Mission 4

  • Proportionalist maxim: do not break a moral rule unless there is a proportionate reason that justifies it.
  • Pre-moral (ontic) evil is harm in itself; moral evil is causing it without proportionate reason. Goodness belongs to the person, rightness to the act; proportion is judged by agape.
  • Critics (Veritatis Splendor; Grisez and Finnis) say some acts are intrinsically evil and goods cannot be weighed.
