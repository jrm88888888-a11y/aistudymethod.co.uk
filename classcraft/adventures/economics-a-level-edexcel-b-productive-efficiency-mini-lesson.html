Make a guess: If a kitchen hires twice as many cooks, why might it not make twice as many meals?

Mission 1

Labour productivity

  • Labour productivity = output ÷ labour input: output per worker or per hour worked.
  • What raises it:
    • more and better capital per worker (machines, computers)
    • technology: better methods and equipment
    • human capital: education, training, skills, health
    • better management and organisation; motivation
    • infrastructure (transport, broadband)
  • More workers sharing the same equipment means less capital per worker, so output per worker can fall.
  • Productivity is not production: a firm can produce more by hiring more workers while output per worker stays the same.
Mission 1

Worked example: why productivity matters

  • Illustrative data. A worker paid 600 a week makes 300 units.
  • Unit labour cost = 600 ÷ 300 = 2 per unit.
  • Training raises output to 360 units: productivity up (360 − 300) ÷ 300 × 100 = 20%.
  • Same wage: unit labour cost = 600 ÷ 360 ≈ 1.67.
  • Even if the wage rises 10% to 660: 660 ÷ 360 ≈ 1.83, still below 2.
  • Significance:
    • Competitiveness: lower costs per unit let firms cut prices or raise profits, at home and in export markets.
    • Wages: higher productivity lets firms pay higher real wages without raising costs per unit.
    • Growth: higher output per worker raises an economy's productive potential.

Mission 2: What makes one country's goods cheaper to buy than another's, even when wages are higher?

Make a guess: What makes one country's goods cheaper to buy than another's, even when wages are higher?

Mission 2

What competitiveness means

  • International competitiveness: the ability of a country's firms to sell goods and services, at home and abroad, against foreign rivals.
  • Price competitiveness: selling at lower prices than rivals for similar quality.
  • Non-price competitiveness: quality, design, reliability, innovation, brand, after-sales service, delivery times.
  • Two price measures: relative unit labour costs and relative export prices.
Mission 2

Unit labour costs

  • Unit labour cost (ULC) = total labour costs ÷ output.
  • Or: labour cost per worker ÷ output per worker (productivity).
  • Illustrative data, same currency:
Labour cost per workerOutput per workerULC
Country A50,0002,000 units50,000 ÷ 2,000 = 25
Country B20,000500 units20,000 ÷ 500 = 40
  • A pays 2.5 times the wage, but its ULC is lower: each A worker produces 4 times as much.
  • Productivity is the key: it lets firms pay higher wages without raising ULC.
  • Rising productivity raises competitiveness, makes room for higher wages and raises potential growth.
  • Say: "unit labour costs rose" — not: "wages rose", unless productivity stood still.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. In a year, labour cost per worker rises by 4% and output per worker rises by 4%. What happens to unit labour costs?
Mission 2

Worked example: relative unit labour costs

  • Relative ULC = home ULC ÷ trading partners' ULC (in a common currency) × 100.
  • Rising: less competitive than in the base year. Falling: more competitive.
  • Illustrative data. Year 1: home ULC = partners' ULC = 40. Index = 100.
  • Year 2: home pay per worker +5%, productivity +1%.
    • Home ULC = 40 × 1.05 ÷ 1.01 = 41.58 (about +4%: 5% − 1%).
    • Partners' ULC +2% = 40.80.
    • Relative ULC = 41.58 ÷ 40.80 × 100 = 101.9: less competitive.
  • Now the home currency depreciates 5% against partners' currencies.
    • Home ULC in partners' currency falls by 5%: 101.9 × 0.95 = 96.8: more competitive.
  • So the exchange rate can outweigh wage and productivity changes.
Mission 2

Relative export prices

  • Relative export prices = index of home export prices ÷ index of rivals' export prices (in a common currency) × 100.
  • Illustrative data: home export price index 104, rivals' 102. Relative export prices = 104 ÷ 102 × 100 = 102.0: less price competitive.
  • Home currency depreciates 3%: 104 × 0.97 ÷ 102 × 100 = 98.9: more price competitive.
  • Export prices depend on ULC, but also on other costs (energy, materials), profit margins and the exchange rate.
  • Say: "relative export prices" compare our export prices with rivals' export prices — not: with our import prices (that is the terms of trade).
Quick check
?Which change would make a country's relative export price index fall, so it becomes more price competitive?
Mission 2

What drives competitiveness

  • Price factors:
    • wage costs relative to productivity (investment, skills, technology, management)
    • the exchange rate
    • inflation relative to trading partners
    • taxes, regulation, energy costs, infrastructure
  • Non-price factors: quality, design, innovation (research and development), reliability, branding.
Mission 2

  • ULC is not the whole story: Spain's credit and construction boom also pulled in imports.

Mission 2

Benefits and problems

  • Benefits of being competitive:
    • export-led growth and more jobs
    • a stronger current account
    • more inward FDI
    • economies of scale from bigger markets
  • Problems of being uncompetitive:
    • current account deficits and falling shares of world markets
    • deindustrialisation and structural unemployment in exposed industries
    • slower growth; pressure on the currency, so dearer imports and inflation
    • less inward investment
  • Competing on low wages keeps costs down but also keeps living standards down. Competing on productivity raises both.
Mission 2Evaluate

How useful are the measures?

It depends on:

  • Non-price factors: a country with high relative ULC can still win on quality and brand.
  • Sector mix: ULC averages over the whole economy; exporters may differ from the average.
  • The exchange rate: it can swing relative costs fast, and reverse just as fast.
  • The route: lower relative ULC from productivity growth is lasting; from wage cuts it lowers living standards.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. In a year, Country K's labour cost per worker rises 6% and its output per worker rises 6%. Its trading partners' unit labour costs, in their own currencies, do not change. K's currency appreciates 10% against theirs. What happens to K's relative unit labour costs?

Competitiveness is relative and measured in a common currency. Pay, productivity and the exchange rate all count.

Mission 3

Labour-intensive or capital-intensive?

  • Labour-intensive: a high proportion of labour compared with capital. Hairdressing, care homes, hand-made furniture.
  • Capital-intensive: a high proportion of machinery and equipment. Car assembly, oil refining, chip making.
  • Capital-intensive: high fixed costs, low cost per unit at high output, consistent quality; less flexible.
  • Labour-intensive: lower fixed costs, flexible and easy to customise; wages make up much of costs, so wage rises hit harder.
  • The choice depends on: the relative cost of labour and capital, the scale of output, and whether the product is standard or made to order.
Quick check
?Compared with a labour-intensive firm making a similar product, which is most likely true of a capital-intensive firm?
Mission 3

Capacity and capacity utilisation

  • Full capacity: the maximum output a firm can produce with its current premises, machines and staff in a period.
  • Spare capacity: output is below that maximum.
  • Capacity utilisation = current output ÷ maximum possible output × 100.
  • Illustrative data: a bakery can bake 5,000 loaves a week. It bakes 3,500.
  • Capacity utilisation = 3,500 ÷ 5,000 × 100 = 70%. Spare capacity: 1,500 loaves.
  • Say: "capacity utilisation rose to 80%" — not: "capacity rose". Capacity is the maximum; utilisation is how much of it is used.
Mission 3

Why utilisation matters for unit cost

  • The bakery's fixed costs: £7,000 a week. Variable cost: £0.60 a loaf.
  • Average cost = fixed costs ÷ output + variable cost per unit.

  • Under-used capacity is costly: you pay for the oven whether it bakes or not.

Mission 3

Under- and over-utilisation

  • Under-utilisation (well below full capacity):
  • High average cost: fixed costs spread thinly.
  • Idle staff and machines; worried, bored staff.
  • Often a sign of falling demand.
  • Upside: room to take a sudden big order; time for maintenance and training.
  • Over-utilisation (at or near 100%):
  • Lowest average cost: good, at first.
  • No time for maintenance: breakdowns become more likely.
  • Overtime and stress: tired staff, more mistakes, quality slips.
  • No room for new orders: customers turned away or delivered late.
  • So many firms aim a little below 100%.
Mission 3

Improving capacity utilisation

  • Use more of what you have: sell more (marketing, new markets, lower prices); take on subcontracted work from other firms; rent out spare space.
  • Cut capacity to match demand: close a site, sell machines, cut shifts. Called rationalisation.
  • If over-utilised: subcontract work out; add shifts or overtime; invest in more capacity if high demand will last.
  • Cutting capacity is hard to reverse: if demand returns, the firm may lose sales.
Mission 3

Lean production: getting quality right

  • Lean production: producing with as little waste as possible: of materials, time, stock, space and effort.
  • Quality control: inspecting products, often at the end, to find and reject faults. Detects problems.
  • Quality assurance: checking each stage of the process so faults do not happen. Prevents problems.
  • Total Quality Management (TQM): every worker, in every stage, is responsible for quality. Aim: "right first time".
  • Say: "quality control finds faults; quality assurance prevents them" — not: the same thing.
Mission 3

Kaizen and just-in-time

  • Kaizen: continuous improvement through many small changes, often suggested by the workers who do the job.
  • Just-in-time (JIT): stock arrives just when it is needed, so little or no buffer stock is held.
  • Real case: Toyota, where JIT and Kaizen grew. Toyota sums up JIT: "Only make what is needed by the customer, when it is needed, and in the amount needed."
  • Toyota's own warning: "A complete car cannot be built if even one part is missing."
  • JIT gains: less storage space, less cash tied up in stock, less out-of-date stock.
  • JIT risks: depends on reliable suppliers; one late delivery can stop production; more frequent, smaller deliveries; fewer bulk discounts.
Quick check
?A sandwich chain switches to just-in-time deliveries of fresh fillings. Which pair is the most likely benefit and risk?
Mission 3

Lean production, costs and revenue

  • Lower average costs: less waste, less storage, fewer faulty products to remake or replace.
  • Minimising waste of resources: materials, energy and time saved.
  • Short product development lead times: the time from idea to product on sale.
  • Shorter lead times let a firm reach the market first, follow new trends, and charge a premium before rivals copy.
  • Competitive advantage: lower costs allow lower prices or higher margins; better quality builds reputation and repeat sales.
Mission 3

Evaluate: does lean production always pay?

It depends on…

  • Supplier reliability. JIT works when deliveries are dependable. One disruption can halt the line.
  • Staff commitment. Kaizen and TQM need trained, trusted staff who want to suggest improvements.
  • Set-up costs and time. Training and new systems cost money now; savings come later.
  • How predictable demand is. Little buffer stock means a sudden surge in orders can go unmet.
  • Judgement: lean methods can cut costs a lot, but they make the firm more exposed to shocks.
Mission check

?A parcel delivery firm has used 99% of its vans' capacity for over a year. Its cost per parcel is the lowest it has ever been. Which is the most likely risk if this continues?

High utilisation gives the lowest unit cost, but with no slack, a breakdown or a new order causes trouble.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Labour productivity is output per worker or per hour; it rises with capital per worker, technology and human capital.

Mission 2

  • ULC = labour cost per worker ÷ output per worker. High wages with high productivity can still mean low ULC.
  • Relative ULC and relative export prices compare a country with its rivals in a common currency. Rising = less competitive.
  • Productivity, inflation, the exchange rate and non-price factors drive competitiveness.

Mission 3

  • Labour-intensive firms have lower fixed costs and flexibility; capital-intensive firms have low unit costs at high output.
  • Capacity utilisation = current output ÷ maximum possible output × 100. Higher utilisation lowers average cost, but near 100% leaves no slack.
  • Lean production (TQM, Kaizen, JIT) cuts waste, average costs and lead times, at the cost of less room for shocks.
