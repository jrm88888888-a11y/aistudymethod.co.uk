Make a guess: Jesus said "No one comes to the Father except through me." Does that shut everyone else out?

Mission 1

Three answers to one question

  • The question: can people of other faiths, or none, be saved (brought into a right relationship with God, now and for ever)?
  • Christian theology of religions usually gives three answers (Alan Race, Christians and Religious Pluralism, 1983):
  • Exclusivism: salvation only through explicit faith in Christ.
  • Inclusivism: salvation is only through Christ, but Christ's grace can reach people who do not know him.
  • Pluralism: the great religions are different, valid paths to salvation (see Mission 2).
  • Context: the end of the colonial missionary age, the Holocaust and migration made the question urgent for twentieth-century churches.
Mission 1Read the source

Two "no other" texts

> "Jesus said to him, 'I am the way, the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father, except through me.'" (John 14:6, WEBBE)

> "There is salvation in no one else, for there is no other name under heaven that is given amongst men, by which we must be saved!" (Acts 4:12, WEBBE)

  • John 14: Jesus speaks to his disciples (Thomas has asked "how can we know the way?").
  • Acts 4: Peter speaks to the Jewish council after a healing done "in the name of Jesus".

Q1 What do both texts claim is unique about Jesus?

Show answer

He is the only way to God / the only name by which people are saved.

Q2 Does either text say how people who never hear of Jesus will be judged?

Show answer

No: both speak to people who have met Jesus or his apostles. That gap is what the debate is about.

Mission 1

Exclusivism developed

  • "No salvation outside the Church" (extra ecclesiam nulla salus): Cyprian (d. 258); defined strongly at the Council of Florence (1442).
  • Karl Barth (1886–1968), Church Dogmatics I/2, §17 (1938): all religion, Christianity included, is human striving and "unbelief"; only God's revelation in Christ saves, by grace. True religion exists only as a sinner is justified.
  • Evangelical exclusivism: the Lausanne Covenant (1974) rejects any dialogue that implies Christ speaks equally through all religions (paraphrase).
  • OCR wording: only Christianity fully offers the means of salvation.
  • Strengths: takes John 14:6 and Acts 4:12 at face value; gives urgency to mission; keeps Christ unique.
  • Problem: what of those who never hear the gospel through no fault of their own (the unevangelised)? Does that fit a God who "desires all people to be saved" (1 Timothy 2:4, WEBBE)?

Mission 1

Strengths and limits

  • Restrictivism (strict exclusivism): only those who hear and believe in this life are saved.
  • Some exclusivists leave the fate of the unevangelised to God's mercy without claiming to know it (agnostic exclusivism).
  • Critics: strict exclusivism seems to make salvation depend on where and when a person is born.
  • Defenders reply: truth is not decided by fairness as we judge it; God's justice is greater than ours; and the remedy is mission, not changing doctrine.
Mission 1Read the source

Texts that point wider

> "Truly I perceive that God doesn't show favouritism; but in every nation he who fears him and works righteousness is acceptable to him." (Acts 10:34–35, WEBBE)

> "The true light that enlightens everyone was coming into the world." (John 1:9, WEBBE)

  • Also cited: Acts 17:23 (Paul in Athens: "TO AN UNKNOWN GOD"); Romans 2:14–15 (Gentiles who do "by nature the things of the law").
  • Q: Is Acts 10:34–35 inclusivist or pluralist? (Usually read as inclusivist: Peter goes on to preach Christ to Cornelius, so the acceptance leads to Christ, not past him.)
Mission 1

Rahner: the anonymous Christian

  • Karl Rahner (1904–1984), Jesuit; "Christianity and the Non-Christian Religions" (lecture, 1961; Theological Investigations vol. 5).
  • Every person is created open to God and offered grace: the supernatural existential.
  • Until the gospel truly reaches a person, their religion can be a lawful religion: a means through which Christ's grace works.
  • Someone who responds to that grace in conscience and love is an anonymous Christian: saved through Christ without knowing his name.
  • The Church is the explicit expression of what is hidden elsewhere. Mission still matters: explicit faith is a fuller, clearer form of salvation.
Mission 1

The Catholic Church after Vatican II

  • Lumen Gentium 16 (1964): those who, through no fault of their own, do not know Christ but seek God sincerely and try to do his will "may achieve eternal salvation" (paraphrase).
  • Nostra Aetate 2 (1965): the Church "rejects nothing that is true and holy" in other religions.
  • Dominus Iesus (2000): Christ is the one saviour; followers of other religions are, objectively, in a "gravely deficient situation" compared with those in the Church.
  • So official Catholic teaching is inclusivist, while insisting on the uniqueness of Christ and the Church.
  • Criticisms of "anonymous Christian": patronising (it labels people with a name they reject); it may weaken mission; Hans Küng thought it smuggled others into the Church without asking them.

Mission 1Diversity

How denominations see others

  • Catholic: inclusivist towards other faiths. Other Christians are "separated brethren" joined by baptism (Unitatis Redintegratio, 1964); Dominus Iesus says Protestant communities are not churches "in the proper sense".
  • Orthodox: the Orthodox Church as the one Church; many Orthodox leave the salvation of others to God.
  • Evangelical: mostly exclusivist about other faiths; some are wary of Catholic teaching (e.g. on justification), but joint statements have grown (Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification, Catholic and Lutheran, 1999).
  • Liberal Protestant: more often inclusivist or pluralist.
  • Interdenominational relations: see Mission 4 (ecumenism).
Mission 1Evaluate

Does the Bible teach exclusivism?

  • Claim: The Christian Bible teaches that only those with explicit faith in Christ can be saved.
  • For: John 14:6 and Acts 4:12 are direct and unqualified; the Great Commission assumes people need the gospel (Matthew 28:19).
  • Against: The texts say salvation is through Christ; they do not say it requires knowing him (inclusivist reading).
  • Against: Acts 10:34–35, John 1:9 and 1 Timothy 2:4 (God "desires all people to be saved") point to God's will to save all.
  • Judgement criteria: context and audience of each text; how to treat texts in tension; the character of God; the fate of those who never hear.

Mission 2

Diversity is a fact; pluralism is a claim

  • Religious diversity (sociological): many faiths live side by side, as in modern Britain (see Mission 3). That is a fact.
  • Tolerance: accepting people's right to hold other beliefs, while still thinking one's own beliefs true.
  • Religious pluralism (philosophical, or theological): the claim that the great religions are equally valid routes to the same ultimate reality.
  • A Christian can welcome diversity and practise tolerance without being a pluralist.
Mission 2

Hick's Copernican revolution

  • John Hick (1922–2012), philosopher of religion; an evangelical convert as a student. Working in multi-faith Birmingham from 1967, he met devout Muslims, Sikhs, Hindus and Jews whose lives seemed as holy as Christians'.
  • God and the Universe of Faiths (1973): theology needs a "Copernican revolution".
  • Old picture (Ptolemaic): Christianity at the centre; other faiths circle it.
  • New picture (Copernican): God at the centre; all religions, Christianity included, circle God.
  • Hick calls inclusivism an epicycle: a patch that keeps the old picture going, as astronomers added epicycles to save the earth-centred model.
Mission 2

The Real

  • An Interpretation of Religion (1989; Gifford Lectures 1986–87).
  • The Real in itself (an sich) is beyond all human concepts: neither personal nor impersonal.
  • Borrowing Kant's distinction between things in themselves and things as they appear: we know the Real only as humanly experienced, through a culture's concepts.
  • Personae (the Real experienced as personal): the Holy Trinity, Allah, the LORD of Israel, Vishnu.
  • Impersonae (the Real experienced as impersonal): Brahman, Nirvana, the Tao.
  • Hick uses the old parable of blind men and an elephant: each touches one part and describes the whole wrongly.
Mission 2

Salvation as transformation

  • For Hick, salvation (or liberation) is the transformation of human life from self-centredness to Reality-centredness.
  • Test (soteriological criterion): look at the fruits. Love, compassion and holiness (saints) appear in all the great traditions, as far as we can tell, to a roughly similar degree.
  • So no single religion can claim to be uniquely saving.
  • Doctrines that clash (one life or many; a personal God or not) are, for Hick, human responses to the Real, not literal descriptions of it.

Mission 2

Rethinking the incarnation

  • Pluralism faces a problem: if Jesus is God incarnate, Christianity seems uniquely founded by God.
  • Hick edited The Myth of God Incarnate (1977) and wrote The Metaphor of God Incarnate (1993; 2nd ed. 2005).
  • Incarnation is a metaphor: Jesus was so open to God that God's love was embodied in his life, as it can be in other lives.
  • Jesus did not claim to be God; the doctrine developed later in the Church (Hick's reading).
Mission 2

Criticisms of pluralism

  • It contradicts central Christian beliefs: Incarnation, Trinity, the resurrection and John 14:6 must all be reinterpreted; critics such as Alister McGrath (Christian Theology, ch. 17) say the result is no longer recognisable Christianity.
  • It is a hidden exclusivism: Gavin D'Costa (b. 1958) argues that pluralism judges every religion by its own standard (Hick's Real), so it is one more exclusive position, not a neutral one ("The Impossibility of a Pluralist View of Religions", 1996).
  • The Real is empty: if nothing can be said of the Real, why call it the source of all religions?
  • Religions do not see themselves this way: most believers think their tradition's claims are literally true; pluralism reinterprets them without their consent.
  • Conflicting truths: a personal God and an impersonal Brahman cannot both be literally the ultimate reality.
  • Hick's reply: these are mythological truths about our response, not literal descriptions of the Real.
Mission 2Diversity

Hick and Rahner compared

  • Centre: Rahner, Christ; Hick, the Real.
  • Other religions: Rahner, lawful paths of Christ's grace until the gospel arrives; Hick, valid responses to the Real in their own right.
  • Mission: Rahner, still needed for explicit, fuller faith; Hick, dialogue rather than conversion.
  • Incarnation: Rahner, literal and unique; Hick, metaphor.
  • Each one's critique: Hick, Rahner's view is a patronising epicycle; Rahner's followers, Hick's view gives up the uniqueness of Christ.
Mission 2

Implications for relations

  • Interfaith: pluralism supports dialogue as equals, shared worship and joint action (e.g. Hick's own interfaith work in Birmingham).
  • Critics: real dialogue needs real difference; if all are already equal, there may be little to discuss.
  • Interdenominational: if even religions are equal paths, quarrels between denominations matter far less; but most churches seek unity in truth (see Mission 4), not by setting doctrine aside.
Mission 2Evaluate

Can a committed Christian be a pluralist?

  • Claim: It is possible to be both a committed Christian and a religious pluralist.
  • For: Hick himself remained a Christian (an ordained Presbyterian minister).
  • For: God's love for all (1 Timothy 2:4) suggests many ways; pluralism respects the holiness found in other faiths.
  • Against: Pluralism reinterprets the Incarnation, the Trinity and John 14:6 as metaphor.
  • Against: D'Costa: pluralism is itself an exclusive standpoint.
  • Judgement criteria: What is essential to being Christian? Is a metaphorical incarnation enough? Does the soteriological criterion (Hick's test of changed lives) work? Can there be neutral ground?
Mission check

?A government leaflet for schools says: "All religions teach the same basic truth: be kind to others." Which criticism would Gavin D'Costa make of this claim?

Pluralism claims to stand above the religions; its critics say no one can stand there. That is the heart of the debate.

Mission 3

How Britain became multi-faith

  • 1948 on: Caribbean migrants (the Windrush generation), mostly Christian.
  • 1950s–70s: migrants from India, Pakistan and Bangladesh bring Islam, Sikhism and Hinduism to British cities.
  • 1968–72: East African Asians arrive from Kenya and Uganda.
  • 1980s on: growing migration from West Africa (Nigeria, Ghana); later from the EU (2004: e.g. Polish Catholics) and as refugees.
  • 2021 Census (England and Wales): Christian 46.2%; no religion 37.2%; Muslim 6.5%; Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist and Jewish communities each 2% or less.
  • Migration has made Britain more religiously diverse and brought many Christians: much church growth in London is in migrant-led churches.
Mission 3

Freedom of religion in a secular state

  • European Convention on Human Rights (1950), Article 9: freedom of thought, conscience and religion, including freedom to change religion and to manifest it "in worship, teaching, practice and observance".
  • Holding a belief is protected absolutely; manifesting it may be limited, but only where limits are lawful and "necessary in a democratic society" (e.g. public safety, others' rights), and no wider than needed (proportionate).
  • In UK law through the Human Rights Act 1998; the Equality Act 2010 makes "religion or belief" a protected characteristic.
  • Secular state: the state does not take sides between religions. Religious pluralism, many faiths with equal rights, is a feature of such states.
  • Britain is a mixed case: an established Church of England, with bishops in the House of Lords, in a pluralist society.
  • Influence on Christian thought: Christianity becomes one voice among many, needing to argue its case in public.
Mission 3

Freedom of expression: the hard cases

  • Eweida and Others v United Kingdom (European Court of Human Rights, 2013), four Christians:
  • Eweida (airline check-in worker, small cross): won; the employer's uniform rule did not strike a fair balance.
  • Chaplin (nurse, cross): lost; health and safety on the ward justified the limit.
  • Ladele (registrar) and McFarlane (relationship counsellor), who would not serve same-sex couples: lost; employers could protect others from discrimination.
  • Christian responses differ: some groups (e.g. the Christian Institute) see a growing marginalisation of Christian belief in public life; others, including many church leaders, stress that rights must be balanced and that Christians should defend the freedom of all faiths.
  • England and Wales abolished the offence of blasphemy in 2008, with support from some church leaders.

Mission 3Read the source

Redemptoris Missio 55–57

Pope John Paul II, encyclical Redemptoris Missio (1990), on dialogue with other religions (paraphrase):

  • 55: Interreligious dialogue is part of the Church's mission, not opposed to proclaiming Christ. God is present in other religions, though they have "gaps, insufficiencies and errors"; Christ is the one saviour and the Church "the ordinary means of salvation".
  • 56: Dialogue is not a tactic. It rests on respect, hope and love; each side stays true to its own convictions while listening honestly. Other religions challenge the Church to understand itself more deeply.
  • 57: Dialogue takes many forms: exchanges between experts, working together for human development, sharing spiritual experience, and the "dialogue of life" between ordinary neighbours. All Catholics are called to it.

Q1 Does dialogue replace evangelism in Redemptoris Missio?

Show answer

No: the two are distinct but connected; both are part of mission.

Q2 Which kind of dialogue could any parishioner take part in?

Show answer

The dialogue of life: witnessing to values in daily life alongside neighbours.

Mission 3

Sharing the Gospel of Salvation (Church of England)

  • 2009: General Synod passes a motion (proposed by Paul Eddy) asking the bishops to report on sharing the gospel of salvation through Christ in multi-faith Britain.
  • 2010: the House of Bishops' report, Sharing the Gospel of Salvation (GS Misc 956).
  • Reaffirms the uniqueness of Christ (God incarnate; the creeds) and the call to invite people to explicit faith and baptism.
  • Commits the Church both to evangelism and to dialogue and good neighbourliness; witness must be respectful, never coercive.
  • Gives good practice from multi-faith areas (e.g. Leicester, Southall, Birmingham, Bradford).
  • Its foreword insists that Christians should not be shy of speaking about conversion (paraphrase).
  • Compare: both documents hold mission and dialogue together; neither is pluralist (see Mission 2).
Mission 3Evaluate

Should Christians try to convert people of other faiths?

  • Claim: Christians in a multi-faith society should seek to convert people of other faiths.
  • For: The Great Commission: "make disciples of all nations" (Matthew 28:19); exclusivist reading of John 14:6.
  • For: Both Redemptoris Missio and Sharing the Gospel of Salvation hold that proclaiming Christ remains part of mission.
  • Against: Pluralists (see Mission 2): other faiths are valid paths; conversion is unnecessary and may harm community relations.
  • Against: Pressure to convert can damage trust between communities; the "dialogue of life" and service may witness better.
  • Judgement criteria: What do Christians believe about salvation? Can mission be respectful? Does it serve social cohesion? Does freedom of religion include the freedom to persuade?

Mission 4

What ecumenism is

  • Ecumenism (Greek oikoumenē, "the whole inhabited world"): the movement to restore unity among Christian churches.
  • Main families: Catholic, Orthodox, Protestant (Anglican, Lutheran, Reformed, Methodist, Baptist …), Pentecostal, and many independent churches.
  • Divisions come from history (1054 East–West schism; 16th-century Reformation) and from doctrine: authority, sacraments, ministry.
  • Ecumenism asks: what unity do Christians already share, and what would visible unity look like?
  • Unity can mean organic union (churches merging into one) or spiritual ecumenism (praying and working together while staying separate). The World Council of Churches (1948) is a fellowship of churches with no authority over them.
Mission 4Read the source

Jesus' prayer for unity

> "that they may all be one; even as you, Father, are in me, and I in you, that they also may be one in us; that the world may believe that you sent me." (John 17:21, WEBBE)

> "There is one body and one Spirit, even as you also were called in one hope of your calling, one Lord, one faith, one baptism" (Ephesians 4:4–5, WEBBE)

  • John 17:21 is often called the charter of the ecumenical movement.

Q1 What reason does Jesus give for unity in John 17:21?

Show answer

So that the world may believe: division harms Christian witness.

Q2 Which shared practice does Ephesians 4:5 name, often used today as a basis for mutual recognition?

Show answer

One baptism.

Mission 4

Why the Catholic Church is not a member

  • Catholic teaching: the one Church of Christ "subsists in" the Catholic Church (Vatican II, Lumen Gentium 8, 1964). It cannot join as one member church among many.
  • Practical reasons too: its size, and its worldwide structure under the Pope differ from national member churches.
  • Yet since Vatican II (Unitatis Redintegratio, 1964) Catholics are committed to ecumenism: a Joint Working Group with the WCC (since 1965); full members of the Faith and Order Commission (since 1968); popes visited Geneva (1969, 1984, 2018).
  • In Britain, Catholics are full members of Churches Together in England (1990).
  • Others outside or uneasy: many Pentecostal and evangelical churches are not members; some Orthodox churches (Georgia, Bulgaria) left in the late 1990s, and a special commission changed WCC decision-making to consensus (adopted 2006).
Mission 4Diversity

How denominations view each other

  • Catholic: other Christians are "separated brethren", in real but imperfect communion with the Catholic Church through baptism (Unitatis Redintegratio 3). Dominus Iesus (2000): Protestant communities are not "Churches in the proper sense".
  • Orthodox: the Orthodox Church is the one Church; other Christians are valued, but sharing communion requires unity in faith.
  • Anglican: sees itself as part of the one Church; dialogues with Catholics (ARCIC, since 1970), communion with Nordic and Baltic Lutherans (Porvoo, 1990s), covenant with Methodists (2003).
  • Evangelical: unity is first spiritual, in the gospel; the Evangelical Alliance (1846) links individuals across denominations. Some evangelicals reject the WCC as doctrinally too broad.
  • Exclusivist readings of John 14:6 ("No one comes to the Father, except through me") can shape views of other denominations too; inclusivism (taught in the pluralism missions) extends grace beyond visible church boundaries.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Exclusivism (particularism): salvation only through explicit faith in Christ (John 14:6; Acts 4:12; Barth).
  • Inclusivism: salvation only through Christ, but Christ's grace can reach those who do not know him (Rahner's anonymous Christians; Vatican II).
  • Denominations differ in how they see other faiths and each other.

Mission 2

  • Diversity is a fact; tolerance is an attitude; pluralism is a claim that religions are equally valid routes to the Real.
  • Hick: a Copernican revolution; the Real beyond all concepts; salvation as transformation; the Incarnation as metaphor.
  • Critics: it rewrites central Christian beliefs; it is itself exclusive (D'Costa); the Real is empty.

Mission 3

  • Migration since 1948 has made Britain multi-faith and brought new Christian communities; Article 9 protects belief and, within limits, its expression.

Mission 4

  • Ecumenism seeks the unity Jesus prayed for (John 17:21); it grew from Edinburgh 1910 into the World Council of Churches (1948), a fellowship, not a super-church.
  • The Catholic Church is not a member but cooperates closely; denominations differ on whether others are fully "Church".
