Civil Rights in the USA 1865-1992

This is an optional route. OCR History A is option-based. Civil Rights in the USA 1865–1992 is one of twenty-one options in Unit Group 3 (thematic study and historical interpretations) — the largest component, worth 80 marks and 40%. You also take one unit from Unit Group 1, one from Unit Group 2, and the Y100 topic-based essay.

This is a thematic unit spanning 127 years. It follows four groups — African Americans, trade unions and labour, Native Americans and women — and asks how far, and why, their rights changed. It is also the unit where historical interpretations are examined directly.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go and collect ⭐ stars. Look out for the Interpretations flags — A-level History is judged on how well you weigh competing arguments. Press Start when you're ready.

Thinking thematically

How a 127-year unit actually works

This is not four separate histories bolted together. Unit Group 3 rewards students who can compare across groups and track change across the whole period.

  • The recurring factors: the role of federal government (President, Congress, Supreme Court); the impact of war; economic change; the tactics of organisation and protest; and the strength of opposition.
  • The recurring pattern: each group gains during periods of national emergency or reform — Reconstruction, the New Deal, the Second World War, the 1960s — and loses ground in the reaction that follows. That is a thesis, and you can argue it across all four groups.
  • The trap: writing an essay on African Americans when the question says 'ethnic minorities and women'. Read the question's scope — thematic questions frequently demand more than one group, and an answer that covers only one is capped.

African Americans · 1865–1945

Emancipation, betrayal and slow rebuilding

The pattern of advance and retreat is starkest here.

  • Reconstruction: the Thirteenth Amendment (1865) abolished slavery; the Fourteenth (1868) guaranteed equal protection; the Fifteenth (1870) the vote. Black men were elected to Congress and to state legislatures.
  • The retreat: Black Codes, the Ku Klux Klan, and the withdrawal of federal troops after the disputed election of 1876 (the Compromise of 1877). Then came disenfranchisement by poll tax, literacy test and grandfather clause — and segregation, blessed by the Supreme Court in Plessy v. Ferguson (1896).
  • Strategies in dispute: Booker T. Washington's Atlanta address (1895) urged economic self-help and accommodation; W.E.B. Du Bois demanded political and civil rights immediately, and helped found the NAACP (1909). This is the movement's oldest internal argument, and it recurs — Washington and Du Bois prefigure King and Malcolm X.
  • War and migration: the Great Migration moved millions north, creating urban Black political weight. The New Deal brought a 'Black Cabinet' of advisers but administered relief through segregated southern structures. Executive Order 8802 (1941) — extracted by A. Philip Randolph's threatened march on Washington — banned discrimination in defence industries. Executive Order 9981 (1948) desegregated the armed forces.

Pressure works. Note that 8802 was won by the threat of a march, not by presidential goodwill. That single fact is a whole paragraph in an essay about how change was achieved.

African Americans · 1945–1992

The movement, the backlash and the limits

  • Legal breakthrough: Brown v. Board of Education (1954) overturned Plessy — but 'all deliberate speed' (1955) licensed years of massive resistance.
  • Direct action: the Montgomery bus boycott (1955–56); Little Rock (1957); the Greensboro sit-ins and SNCC (1960); the Freedom Rides (1961); Birmingham (1963), where televised violence forced Kennedy's hand; the March on Washington (28 August 1963).
  • The statutes: the Civil Rights Act (1964) and the Voting Rights Act (1965) — the legal architecture of Jim Crow destroyed in two years.
  • The limits: the Watts riots (August 1965) erupted days after the Voting Rights Act. Northern segregation, policing and poverty were untouched. Malcolm X (assassinated 1965), Black Power (Carmichael, 1966) and the Black Panthers (1966) spoke to that gap. King was assassinated on 4 April 1968, while campaigning on economic justice.
  • After: affirmative action, contested in Regents of the University of California v. Bakke (1978); and, at the end of the period, the Los Angeles riots of 1992 following the acquittal of the officers who beat Rodney King — a brutal demonstration of how much remained undone.

Why OCR ends in 1992: so that you cannot finish the story in 1965. The unit is designed to make you weigh legal equality against lived reality — and to notice the difference.

Labour · trade union rights

The most reversible gains of all

Labour is the group whose rights most clearly rise and then fall — which makes it invaluable for a change-and-continuity argument.

  • Repression, 1865–1932: the Homestead strike (1892) and the Pullman strike (1894) were broken by private armies, injunctions and federal troops. The courts treated unions as conspiracies in restraint of trade. The AFL (1886) under Samuel Gompers survived by organising skilled craft workers and avoiding politics.
  • Breakthrough: the Norris–La Guardia Act (1932) limited injunctions; Section 7(a) of the NIRA (1933) and then the Wagner Act (1935) guaranteed the right to organise and bargain collectively, and created the National Labor Relations Board. The CIO organised mass-production industry, using the sit-down strike — decisively at Flint in 1936–37.
  • Reversal: the Taft–Hartley Act (1947), passed over Truman's veto, banned the closed shop, permitted state 'right to work' laws and allowed the President to break strikes. Union density fell for the rest of the century, and President Reagan's dismissal of the striking air traffic controllers in 1981 is the emblem of the collapse.

The comparative point: African Americans' legal gains after 1964 proved hard to reverse — they were constitutional and judicially enforced. Labour's gains after 1935 proved easy to reverse — they were statutory and could be amended by a hostile Congress. That is a genuinely sophisticated comparative argument, and it is exactly what a thematic unit is for.

Native Americans & women

The other two strands

These are the strands students neglect — which is precisely why examiners ask about them.

Native Americans

  • Dispossession: the Plains wars, Little Bighorn (1876), and the massacre at Wounded Knee (1890). The Dawes Severalty Act (1887) broke up communally held reservations into individual allotments and cost tribes an enormous share of their remaining land — assimilation by legislation.
  • Reversal and reversal again: the Indian Citizenship Act (1924); John Collier's Indian Reorganization Act (1934), which restored tribal government and ended allotment; then the termination policy of the 1950s, which tried to dissolve tribal status altogether.
  • Red Power: the American Indian Movement (1968), the occupation of Alcatraz (1969–71) and of Wounded Knee (1973); then the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act (1975).

Women

  • The vote: the Nineteenth Amendment (1920), after decades of campaigning — but it did not deliver political power or economic equality.
  • Work: Frances Perkins became the first woman in a US Cabinet in 1933; the Second World War drew millions of women into industry, and demobilisation pushed most of them out again.
  • The second wave: the Equal Pay Act (1963); Betty Friedan's The Feminine Mystique (1963); Title VII of the Civil Rights Act (1964), which — almost by accident of its drafting — banned sex discrimination in employment; the founding of NOW (1966); Roe v. Wade (1973). The Equal Rights Amendment, passed by Congress in 1972, failed ratification and expired in 1982.

The comparison worth making: the ERA's defeat and the Voting Rights Act's success in the same era show that federal power was not simply available on demand — outcomes depended on mobilisation, opposition, and whether change could be routed through the courts rather than the states.

Interpretations · the set-piece debate

The Dunning School and Eric Foner

Unit Group 3 examines historical interpretations directly. The Reconstruction debate is the model case, and it shows why interpretations matter politically as well as academically.

The Dunning School, dominant in American universities in the early twentieth century, portrayed Reconstruction as a disaster: corrupt, vindictive, imposed on a prostrate South by carpetbaggers, and demonstrating that Black Americans were unfit for political power. This was racist scholarship, and it was enormously influential — it supplied the historical justification for Jim Crow, and it shaped popular culture for decades.
W.E.B. Du Bois challenged it in Black Reconstruction in America (1935), arguing that Reconstruction was a genuine, if defeated, democratic experiment in which Black Americans were agents rather than passive objects.
Eric Foner, Reconstruction: America's Unfinished Revolution (1988), consolidated the revision: Reconstruction was a real revolution in citizenship, and its failure was a failure of white political will and violence, not of Black capacity. Foner's title — 'unfinished' — is itself an argument about 1992.

The lesson OCR wants you to draw: historians write from within their own moment. The Dunning School flourished in the era of segregation and was funded and read by a society that wanted its conclusions. Foner wrote after the civil rights movement had made those conclusions unthinkable. Explaining why an interpretation was persuasive in its time is a higher-order skill than agreeing or disagreeing with it.

Exam technique · Unit Group 3

The biggest paper on the specification

OCR Unit Group 3 is 2 hours 30 minutes, 80 marks, 40% of the A-level — by far the largest single component. It has two parts.

  • Historical interpretations: a compulsory question on passages of historians' writing about the specification's named depth topics. You must explain the interpretation, evaluate it against your own knowledge, and account for it — why does this historian, writing then, see it this way?
  • Thematic essays across the whole 1865–1992 period. These demand synthesis: evidence drawn from across the century and, usually, across more than one group.

The two commonest failures. First, treating an interpretation as a source to be 'checked for bias' — it is an argument to be weighed. Second, answering a 127-year thematic question with evidence from 1955–65 only. Build a mental grid: four groups down the side, the decades across the top, and make sure you can put something precise in every box.

Key question · the role of the federal government

President, Congress and Court across 127 years

The thematic question OCR returns to most often is: how important was federal government? Build a grid.

  • The Supreme Court cuts both ways. It legitimised segregation in Plessy (1896) and destroyed it in Brown (1954); it struck down early labour protections and later upheld the Wagner Act. The Court is not a friend of rights — it is an arena.
  • Presidents follow more often than they lead. Roosevelt issued Executive Order 8802 (1941) only when faced with a threatened march; Kennedy acted after Birmingham; Johnson after Selma. The pattern is pressure, then legislation.
  • Congress is the veto point. The southern bloc blocked anti-lynching bills for decades and shaped New Deal programmes to preserve segregation; and it was Congress that passed Taft–Hartley over a presidential veto.
  • War is the great accelerator — 1861–65, 1917–18, 1941–45. Each war created labour shortages, moral leverage and federal power that groups could use.

The thesis to argue: federal government was necessary but never sufficient. It acted decisively only when mobilisation, war or crisis made inaction costlier than action. That single sentence, evidenced across four groups and 127 years, is a Level 5 essay.
