Mission 1

Mission 1: Why does salt vanish into water while oil sits on top in a separate layer?

Make a guess: Why does salt vanish into water while oil sits on top in a separate layer?

Mission 1

The medium for life

  • Water is the most abundant molecule in cells: about 70% of a typical cell's mass.
  • Most reactions of life happen in aqueous solution (dissolved in water).
  • Living systems depend on the properties of water to sustain life.
  • Every one of those properties starts with the shape and charges of one small molecule.
Mission 1

A polar molecule

  • Each O–H bond is covalent: a pair of electrons is shared.
  • Oxygen attracts the shared electrons more strongly than hydrogen does.
  • Unequal sharing makes a polar covalent bond: oxygen is δ−, each hydrogen δ+.
  • The molecule is bent (about 104.5°), so the charges do not cancel. Water is a dipole: a δ− end and a δ+ end.
  • Overall, the molecule is neutral.
  • Say: "partial charges (δ+, δ−)" — not: "water is made of ions".
Mission 1

Hydrogen bonds

  • The δ+ hydrogen of one molecule is attracted to the δ− oxygen of another molecule: a hydrogen bond.
  • Hydrogen bonds are between molecules. The O–H bonds within a molecule are covalent.
  • Each hydrogen bond is weak, much weaker than a covalent bond. In liquid water they constantly break and re-form.
  • One molecule can hydrogen-bond to up to four neighbors. Millions of weak bonds add up to a strong effect.
  • Polarity also causes hydrogen bonding within other biological molecules, e.g. between the two strands of DNA.
  • Say: "hydrogen bond between water molecules" — not: "the hydrogen bond between H and O in the molecule".
Quick check
?In a water molecule, each of the two hydrogen atoms is joined to the oxygen atom by a covalent bond. Which statement best explains why a water molecule is polar?
Quick check
?The model shows three water molecules. The symbols δ+ and δ− show partial charges. Which statement correctly describes the interaction labeled 2 in the model?
Mission 1

Why some things dissolve and others don't

  • Ions (e.g. Na⁺, Cl⁻): water's δ− oxygens face cations; its δ+ hydrogens face anions. Each ion is surrounded by water and separates from the crystal.
  • Polar molecules (e.g. glucose, with many –OH groups): form hydrogen bonds with water.
  • Charged and polar substances are hydrophilic: they dissolve.
  • Non-polar molecules (e.g. oils): no charges to attract water. Water molecules stay hydrogen-bonded to each other and exclude them. They are hydrophobic.
  • Dissolving is physical: molecules stay whole; no covalent bonds break.
  • Say: "ions are attracted to the partial charges of water" — not: "ions form hydrogen bonds with water".
Quick check
?Molecule X, a small hypothetical molecule, dissolves readily in water. It has several hydroxyl (–OH) groups, which are polar. A chemist makes a modified form of X in which every –OH group is replaced by a methyl (–CH3) group; the rest of the molecule is unchanged. Which prediction about the modified molecule is best supported?
Mission check

?In blood plasma, glucose travels freely dissolved. Cholesterol does not: it travels inside lipoprotein particles whose outer surface carries polar and charged groups. Why does cholesterol need this carrier?

Like oil in the hook, cholesterol is non-polar, so water holds onto itself instead. Like salt, anything charged or polar is pulled into solution.

Mission 2

Cohesion and surface tension

  • Cohesion: water molecules attract each other by hydrogen bonds.
  • At the surface, molecules are pulled inward and sideways, not outward.
  • Result: surface tension. The surface resists breaking, like a stretched film.
  • A pond skater stands on the surface without breaking it, held up by surface tension: a habitat on the water surface.
  • Say: "cohesion" for water attracted to water — not: for water attracted to anything else.
Mission 2

Adhesion and capillary action

  • Adhesion: water is attracted to other polar or charged substances by hydrogen bonds or charge attractions.
  • Examples: cellulose, glass, soil particles. Not wax: non-polar.
  • Adhesion + cohesion pull water along narrow spaces: capillary action.
  • In a narrow glass tube, water climbs the wall and forms a curved surface (meniscus).
  • Capillary action works over short distances only; it does not lift water up a tree.
Quick check
?The model shows water molecules next to the surface of a glass tube; the glass surface carries polar O–H groups. Dashed lines show attractions between molecules. Which property of water is represented by the attractions labeled 1?
Mission 2

A temperature buffer

  • Specific heat capacity: energy needed to raise 1 g by 1 °C. Water: about 4.2 J/(g·°C).
  • That is high: about 4 times that of air, per kilogram.
  • Why: much of the energy goes into breaking hydrogen bonds, not into making molecules move faster.
  • So water's temperature changes slowly. Lakes and seas are stable habitats.
  • Bodies are mostly water, so body temperature is buffered too: enzymes stay near their optimum.
  • Say: "temperature changes slowly / stays stable" — not: "water keeps things warm".
Mission 2

Worked example: cooling by evaporation

  • To evaporate, a molecule must break all its hydrogen bonds. That takes a lot of energy.
  • Heat of vaporization at body temperature: about 2400 J/g.
  • Energy comes from the skin, so the skin cools. Sweating (mammals) and transpiration (plants) use this.
  • Example (illustrative data): a runner must lose 1200 kJ of heat by sweating.
  • Mass of sweat = 1 200 000 J ÷ 2400 J/g = 500 g, about half a liter.
  • Compare: warming 1 g of water by 1 °C takes only 4.2 J. Evaporating it takes 2400 J: about 570 times as much.
  • Big cooling effect, little water lost.
Quick check
?Consider three observations: (1) the temperature of a large lake changes little between day and night; (2) sweat evaporating from the skin cools the body; (3) small insects can walk on the surface of a pond without sinking. Which statement best explains what the three observations have in common?
Mission check

?Dogs lose little heat by sweating. On a hot day a dog pants: air moves quickly over its wet tongue and airways. How does panting cool the dog?

The same hydrogen bonds that steady the hook's lake make evaporation a powerful way to cool.

Mission 3

One chemistry for all life

  • Four groups of biological molecules: carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, nucleic acids.
  • C, H and O are in all four, and are the most common elements in them.
MoleculesElements
CarbohydratesC, H, O
LipidsC, H, O (phospholipids also P)
ProteinsC, H, O, N, S
Nucleic acidsC, H, O, N, P
  • Cells cannot make atoms. Every N, P and S atom must be taken in from the environment.

Mission 3

Monomers and polymers

  • Monomer: a small unit from which larger molecules are made.
  • Polymer: a large molecule made of many monomers joined together.
MonomerPolymer
Monosaccharide (e.g. glucose)Polysaccharide (starch, glycogen, cellulose)
Amino acidPolypeptide (protein)
NucleotidePolynucleotide (DNA, RNA)
  • Joining many monomers is polymerization.
  • Triglycerides are built the same way, but are not polymers: one glycerol + three fatty acids, not a repeating chain.
Mission 3

Condensation: building

  • Dehydration synthesis: joins two monomers with a new covalent bond; the equivalent of a water molecule is lost.
  • One monomer loses an –OH, the other an –H. Together: H2O.
  • One water per bond formed.
  • Repeat: monomer → dimer → polymer.
  • Cells use enzymes for each joining reaction.
Mission 3

Hydrolysis: breaking

  • Hydrolysis: adding water cleaves the covalent bond between monomers.
  • The water is split: –H goes to one product, –OH to the other.
  • One water per bond broken.
  • Digestion is hydrolysis. Enzymes make it fast; polymers in plain water break down extremely slowly.
  • Say: "condensation releases water; hydrolysis uses water" — not: the other way round.
Quick check
?In a cell, one reaction builds a polymer from monomers while another reaction breaks a different polymer into its monomers. Which statement correctly compares the role of water in the two reactions?
Mission 3

Worked example: counting the water

  • Bonds sit between monomers. An unbranched chain of n monomers has n − 1 bonds.
  • So building it releases n − 1 water molecules; hydrolyzing it uses n − 1.
  • Example: 5 glucose molecules joined into one unbranched chain.
  • Glucose 180 g/mol; water 18 g/mol.
  • Bonds: 5 − 1 = 4. Water released: 4.
  • Molar mass of chain = 5 × 180 − 4 × 18 = 900 − 72 = 828 g/mol.
  • Hydrolysis reverses it exactly: 828 + 4 × 18 = 900.
Quick check
?In a cell, 10 molecules of a hypothetical monomer, each with a molar mass of 180 g/mol, are joined into one unbranched chain by dehydration synthesis. The molar mass of water is 18 g/mol. What is the molar mass of the polymer formed?
Mission check

?Seeds store starch: unbranched and branched chains of thousands of glucose units. During germination, enzymes hydrolyze the starch completely to glucose. Starting from 1.00 g of starch, about what mass of glucose forms?
  • Glucose 180 g/mol; water 18 g/mol.

Building releases one water per link, so breaking down has to put each one back.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Glucose and fructose are built from exactly the same atoms. So what makes them different sugars?

Make a guess: Glucose and fructose are built from exactly the same atoms. So what makes them different sugars?

Mission 4

Single sugars

  • A monosaccharide is a single sugar unit: the monomer of carbohydrates.
  • Glucose and fructose are both C6H12O6: same atoms, arranged differently. They are isomers.
  • Monosaccharides (simple sugars) are the monomers of polysaccharides (complex carbohydrates).
Mission 4

Sugars join into chains

  • Monosaccharides join by dehydration synthesis: a covalent bond forms; water is lost.
  • Many joined units make a polysaccharide.
  • Polysaccharide chains may be linear or branched (more in the next mission).
  • Hydrolysis breaks the bonds and releases monosaccharides again.
Quick check
?Which statement correctly describes the relationship between monosaccharides and polysaccharides?
Mission check

?Glycogen and cellulose are both built from glucose monomers. Glycogen molecules are highly branched, while cellulose molecules are linear. A student claims that, because they are built from the same monomer, glycogen and cellulose must be the same molecule. Which response to the claim is correct?

Same building blocks, different connections, different molecule: the same idea that separates glucose from fructose.

Mission 5: Starch and cellulose are both chains of glucose. Why can one feed you while the other builds a tree?

Make a guess: Starch and cellulose are both chains of glucose. Why can one feed you while the other builds a tree?

Mission 5

One monomer, three polymers

  • Starch: energy store in plants.
  • Glycogen: energy store in animals and fungi.
  • Cellulose: strong walls of plant cells.
  • All three are polymers of glucose, built by condensation.
  • Glucose units may be joined into linear chains (cellulose) or branched ones (glycogen, much of starch).
  • What differs: which form of glucose, and how the units are connected.
  • Different connections give different shapes and properties: an enzyme that fits the links in starch does not fit the links in cellulose, so your own enzymes cannot digest cellulose.

Mission check

?In a hypothetical species of yeast, enzyme E catalyzes the dehydration synthesis reaction involving a storage polysaccharide. A mutation makes enzyme E nonfunctional; the yeast's other enzymes are unaffected. Which outcome is most likely in the mutant cells?

Mission 6

What makes a lipid

  • Lipids dissolve in non-polar solvents but only sparingly in water.
  • Mostly non-polar C–H bonds: few δ+ or δ− regions to attract water. They are hydrophobic.
  • Lipids include fats, oils, waxes, phospholipids and steroids.
  • Say: "insoluble because non-polar" — not: "because the molecules are large" or "because they are lighter than water".
Mission 6

How a fat is assembled

  • A fat (triglyceride) is assembled from one glycerol and three fatty acid chains.
  • They join by dehydration synthesis. The fat is not a polymer.
  • The fatty acid chains are long, nonpolar hydrocarbons: they make the fat hydrophobic.
Mission 6

Saturated and unsaturated

  • Saturated: only single C–C bonds. Every carbon holds as many H atoms as it can: saturated with hydrogen.
  • Unsaturated: at least one C=C double bond.
  • The more double bonds, the more unsaturated the fatty acid.
  • In natural fats, each C=C puts a kink in the chain. Saturated chains are straight.
  • Only C=C in the chain counts. The C=O in –COOH does not make a fatty acid unsaturated.
  • Say: "saturated with hydrogen" — not: "full of double bonds".
Mission 6

Kinks and melting points

  • Straight saturated chains pack closely: strong attractions between molecules → higher melting point.
  • Kinked unsaturated chains cannot pack closely: weaker attractions → lower melting point.
  • Rich in saturated fatty acids: fat, solid at room temperature (butter).
  • Rich in unsaturated fatty acids: oil, liquid at room temperature (olive oil).
  • Melting breaks no covalent bonds. Molecules just move apart.
Quick checkRead the data
?The graph shows the approximate melting points of four fatty acids. Each has a chain of 18 carbon atoms; they differ only in the number of double bonds between carbon atoms in the chain. Which statement is best supported by the data?
Mission 6

A dense energy store

  • Oxidizing fats releases about 37 kJ/g; carbohydrate about 17 kJ/g. About twice as much per gram.
  • Why: long hydrocarbon chains, rich in C–H bonds, with little oxygen.
  • Insoluble: no osmotic effect, and stored without the water that glycogen holds.
  • So a lot of energy fits in a small, light store: adipose tissue in animals, oil droplets in seeds.
  • Say: "energy is released when fatty acids are oxidized in respiration" — not: "energy is stored in the ester bonds".
Mission 6

Insulation

  • Adipose tissue conducts heat poorly. It slows heat loss from a warm body to colder surroundings.
  • Endotherms (mammals, birds) keep their bodies warmer than their surroundings.
  • The bigger the gap between body and habitat temperature, the thicker the insulation needed: e.g. blubber in seals in polar seas.
  • Insulating fat does not make heat: it slows heat loss. It does not work by trapping air.
Quick check
?Seals that live in polar seas have a thick layer of fat, called blubber, beneath the skin. A seal's body is kept at about 37 °C, while the surrounding water is near 0 °C. Which statement best describes how the blubber helps keep the seal's body warm?
Mission check

?To make a spread, manufacturers react a liquid vegetable oil with hydrogen, using a catalyst. Some C=C double bonds in its fatty acid chains become C–C single bonds. How does the oil change?

Same molecule type, different tails: straight tails pack into a solid; kinked tails slide past each other as a liquid.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Unequal sharing of electrons makes water polar (δ− O, δ+ H); hydrogen bonds form between molecules.
  • Charged and polar substances dissolve (hydrophilic); non-polar ones are excluded (hydrophobic). That makes water the medium for metabolism and transport.

Mission 2

  • Hydrogen bonds give cohesion (surface tension; unbroken xylem columns) and a high specific heat capacity (stable temperatures).
  • Evaporation must break hydrogen bonds: a large cooling effect for little water lost.

Mission 3

  • Monomers join by condensation (one water released per bond) and polymers are split by hydrolysis (one water used per bond).
  • AP calls condensation "dehydration synthesis".
  • C, H, O in all four groups; N and S in proteins; N and P in nucleic acids.

Mission 4

  • Monosaccharides are the monomers of polysaccharides, joined by covalent bonds into linear or branched chains.

Mission 5

  • Glucose monomers can be joined into linear or branched polysaccharides; the arrangement, not the monomer, sets the function.

Mission 6

  • A triglyceride is one glycerol + three fatty acids joined by ester bonds (three condensations); hydrophobic, energy-dense store.
  • Saturated chains are straight and pack closely (fats, higher melting point); C=C kinks unsaturated chains (oils, lower melting point).
