A land and a hope

> "Now the LORD said unto Abram: 'Get thee out of thy country, and from thy kindred, and from thy father's house, unto the land that I will show thee. And I will make of thee a great nation, and I will bless thee" (Genesis 12:1–2, JPS 1917)

  • Zion is another name for Jerusalem, and for the land and people.
  • Isaiah speaks to exiles: God will restore the ruined land.

Mission 1

Jerusalem and the Temple

  • King David made Jerusalem his capital; Solomon built the First Temple there.
  • The Babylonians destroyed it in 586 BCE; the Second Temple was rebuilt and later expanded by Herod; the Romans destroyed it in 70 CE.
  • After 70 CE most Jews lived in exile (diaspora), but Jerusalem stayed at the centre of prayer.
  • Synagogues face Jerusalem; the Amidah asks God to rebuild it.
  • "If I forget thee, O Jerusalem, let my right hand forget her cunning." (Psalm 137:5, JPS 1917)
  • The fast of Tisha B'Av mourns both Temples; a glass is broken at weddings in their memory.
  • The Western Wall in Jerusalem is part of the wall around the Temple Mount, the nearest place Jews can pray to where the Temple stood; many place written prayers in its cracks.
  • Not every religious Jew gives the land the same weight: views of the modern State of Israel differ.
Mission 1Diversity

Different views of the land and the State of Israel

  • The State of Israel was founded in 1948. Most Jews worldwide see it as central to Jewish life; they differ on what it means.
  • Religious Zionists: the State is the beginning of redemption; Rabbi Abraham Isaac Kook (1865–1935) taught that even secular pioneers were part of God's plan. Many say special prayers on Israel's Independence Day.
  • Secular Zionists: a homeland and safe refuge for a people, after centuries of persecution; Theodor Herzl (1860–1904), The Jewish State (1896).
  • Haredi non-Zionists: the land is holy, and many live there, but a Jewish state should wait for the Messiah; small groups (e.g. Satmar Hasidim) oppose the State outright.
  • Reform and Liberal: once saw Judaism as a religion, not a nation (e.g. American Reform's Pittsburgh Platform, 1885, did not seek a return to Zion); since the Shoah and 1948 most strongly support Israel, often with a stress on equality and justice.
  • Many Jews support the State while disagreeing with particular government policies; many diaspora Jews see their own country as home.
  • So the Promised Land is not equally important in the same way for all religious Jews.

Mission 1Evaluate

"Every Jew should visit Israel at least once."

  • For: the land is part of the covenant; prayers face Jerusalem; seeing the Western Wall and Yad Vashem (Israel's Holocaust memorial) deepens faith and memory; it ties diaspora Jews to Jews in Israel.
  • Against: since 70 CE there is no command to go; God can be worshipped anywhere (Shekhinah); cost, health or safety may make it impossible.
  • Against: some Haredi and some progressive Jews feel uneasy about what a visit says about the State.
  • Another view: studying Torah and living Jewishly where you are can matter more than any journey.
  • Judge it by: Is there a religious duty, or a personal benefit? What about Jews who cannot go? Do different views of the State change the answer?

Mission 2: Judaism gives 613 commandments. If God commands so much, are Jews still free to choose?

Make a guess: Judaism gives 613 commandments. If God commands so much, are Jews still free to choose?

Mission 2

What a mitzvah is

  • A mitzvah (plural mitzvot) is a commandment from God in the Torah.
  • The rabbis counted 613 in the Torah: 248 positive and 365 negative (Talmud Makkot 23b).
  • They cover worship, food, Shabbat, family life, business, courts, care for the poor and for animals.
  • At 13 (boys) or 12 (girls, in Orthodox practice) a young Jew becomes bar or bat mitzvah: "son/daughter of the commandment", now responsible for keeping them.
  • Keeping mitzvot is seen as a response of love to God and a way to make ordinary life holy.
Mission 2

Ways to group the mitzvot

  • Positive (mitzvot aseh, "do"): e.g. honour parents, keep Shabbat, give tzedakah.
  • Negative (mitzvot lo ta'aseh, "do not"): e.g. do not steal, do not eat pork.
  • Between people and God (bein adam la-Makom): prayer, Shabbat, kashrut, festivals.
  • Between people (bein adam la-chavero): honesty, charity, kindness, respect for parents.
  • Ethical mitzvot (how to treat others) and ritual mitzvot (worship and holiness). Ritual mitzvot shape the relationship with God; ethical ones shape relationships with people, and with God too.
  • Different lists: the Torah does not number them. Maimonides (Sefer HaMitzvot, c. 1170) listed 613; Nachmanides (1194–1270) disputed some entries. Many mitzvot cannot be kept today: they concern the Temple or only the Land of Israel.
  • Your spec writes the positive term as aseh.
Mission 2Read the source

Reminders to keep the mitzvot

> "And it shall be unto you for a fringe, that ye may look upon it, and remember all the commandments of the LORD, and do them" (Numbers 15:39)

  • This is the command of tzitzit: fringes on the corners of a garment, worn on the tallit and by many Orthodox men every day.
  • "Therefore shall ye lay up these My words in your heart and in your soul; and ye shall bind them for a sign upon your hand" (Deuteronomy 11:18): tefillin.
  • "And thou shalt write them upon the door-posts of thy house" (Deuteronomy 11:20): the mezuzah.
  • Both passages are part of the Shema, said twice daily.

Mission 2

Free will: "choose life"

  • "I have set before thee life and death, the blessing and the curse; therefore choose life" (Deuteronomy 30:19).
  • Judaism teaches that God gave every person free will (bechirah): the power to choose good or evil.
  • Free will and the mitzvot fit together: a command only makes sense if you can obey or disobey it.
  • Choice makes reward and punishment fair, and makes keeping a mitzvah a real act of love.
  • Even after doing wrong, a person can choose teshuvah (return, repentance).
  • Rabbi Akiva: everything is foreseen by God, yet freedom of choice is given (Pirkei Avot 3:15, paraphrase).
Mission 2

Two inclinations

  • The rabbis taught that every person has a yetzer ha-tov (good inclination) and a yetzer ha-ra (bad inclination).
  • "the imagination of man's heart is evil from his youth" (Genesis 8:21); but God tells Cain: "sin coucheth at the door … but thou mayest rule over it" (Genesis 4:7).
  • The yetzer ha-ra is not a devil: it is selfish desire. Used rightly, it drives people to build, marry and work (Genesis Rabbah 9:7, paraphrase).
  • Sin (chet, "missing the mark") happens when the yetzer ha-ra rules. The Torah is its remedy (Talmud Kiddushin 30b, paraphrase).
  • A teaching links bar/bat mitzvah age with the yetzer ha-tov growing strong enough to guide choices.
Mission 2Read the source

Maimonides, Sefer Madda

  • Maimonides (1138–1204) wrote the Mishneh Torah (completed c. 1180), a code of all Jewish law. Book 1 is Sefer Madda, "the Book of Knowledge".
  • It begins: the foundation of all is to know that God exists (Laws of the Foundations of the Torah 1:1, paraphrase). Every mitzvah grows from that.
  • Laws of Repentance 5:1–4 (paraphrase): freedom is given to every person. Anyone can become as righteous as Moses or as wicked as Jeroboam. No one forces us; God does not decree whether we will be good or bad.
  • If God decided our actions, the Torah's commands, and reward and punishment, would make no sense.

Q1 Why, for Maimonides, must humans be free?

Show answer

Otherwise commands and judgement would be pointless.

Q2 Which two people mark the range of human choice?

Show answer

Moses and Jeroboam.

Mission 2

Providence and the limits of freedom

  • Divine providence (hashgachah): the belief that God knows, cares for and guides the world and each life.
  • Job confesses: "I know that Thou canst do every thing, and that no purpose can be withholden from Thee." (Job 42:2)
  • Jonah: he flees God's call, yet a storm and a great fish bring him back. Providence guides; Jonah still chooses how to respond.
  • Limits of freedom: we do not choose our birth, family, health or what happens to us. Everything is in God's hands except whether we revere God (Talmud Berakhot 33b, paraphrase): our moral choices remain ours.
  • Jews hold both together: God's providence and human responsibility.

Mission 2Diversity

Which mitzvot matter most?

  • Orthodox: all 613 come from God; both kinds are equally binding. You may not choose between them.
  • Yet they differ in one way: Yom Kippur atones for sins against God, but not for sins against people until you have made peace with them (Mishnah Yoma 8:9, paraphrase).
  • Reform and Liberal: the ethical mitzvot are central; ritual mitzvot are valued and chosen by informed choice.
  • The Talmud shows prophets summing up the 613: David in 11 principles, Micah in 3, Habakkuk in 1: "the righteous shall live by his faith" (Habakkuk 2:4) (Makkot 24a).
  • Hillel: not doing to others what is hateful to you is the whole Torah; the rest is commentary (Talmud Shabbat 31a, paraphrase).
Mission 2Evaluate

"Mitzvot between people matter more than mitzvot between people and God."

  • For: Yoma 8:9: wrongs against people need their forgiveness. Hillel calls not harming others "the whole Torah". Reform Judaism puts ethics first. Prophets condemn worship without justice.
  • Against: Orthodox teaching: all 613 are God's will, equally binding. The Ten Commandments start with duties to God. Ritual mitzvot (Shabbat, kashrut) keep Jewish identity alive.
  • Another view: the two are linked: treating people well is serving God, since every person is made in God's image.
  • Judge it by: Orthodox and Reform views; what the sources stress; whether the two kinds can really be separated.
Mission check

In Jewish teaching, 613 commands do not cancel freedom: they assume it. Every mitzvah is a choice, made by a person with two inclinations.

Mission 3

Why life is holy

  • Sanctity of life: Jews believe human life is holy (sacred) because it comes from God and belongs to God.
  • Every person is made in the image of God (Genesis 1:27), so every life has equal and infinite worth.
  • Genesis tells how God breathes life into the first human (Genesis 2:7). Jews see life as a gift, held on trust.
  • Murder is forbidden "for in the image of God made He man" (Genesis 9:6).
  • God alone gives and takes life: "I kill, and I make alive" (Deuteronomy 32:39).
  • The Mishnah: whoever destroys one life is as if they destroyed a whole world; whoever saves one life, as if they saved a whole world (Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5, paraphrase).
Mission 3Read the source

Life is God's

> "And God created man in His own image, in the image of God created He him; male and female created He them." (Genesis 1:27)

  • "Thou shalt not murder." (Exodus 20:13)
  • "neither shalt thou stand idly by the blood of thy neighbour: I am the LORD." (Leviticus 19:16): not only "do not kill", but act to save.

Mission 3

Pikuach nefesh: saving a life

  • Pikuach nefesh: "watching over a soul". Saving a human life overrides almost every other mitzvah, including Shabbat and Yom Kippur.
  • The source: "Ye shall therefore keep My statutes … which if a man do, he shall live by them" (Leviticus 18:5).
  • The Talmud reads this: you should live by the mitzvot, not die because of them (Talmud Yoma 85b, paraphrase).
  • So breaking Shabbat to save a life is not a sin: it is itself a mitzvah.
  • Three exceptions: a Jew must not commit idolatry, murder or forbidden sexual relations (e.g. adultery, incest), even to save their own life (Talmud Sanhedrin 74a, paraphrase).
Mission 3Read the source

Talmud Yoma 83–85

All paraphrased from the Talmud tractate Yoma ("The Day", about Yom Kippur):

  • 83a (Mishnah): a seriously ill person may be fed on Yom Kippur; any doubt about danger to life overrides Shabbat.
  • 84b: if a child falls into the sea, spread a net and pull them out on Shabbat; if a child is locked in a room, break the door.
  • 84b: do it at once, and do not hand the task to a non-Jew or a child: the one who acts quickly is praiseworthy.
  • 85a: if a building collapses on Shabbat, dig out the rubble even if you are unsure anyone is alive.
  • 85b: "live by them": profaning one Shabbat lets a person keep many Shabbats.

Show answer

Mission 3

Pikuach nefesh in practice

  • Shabbat: phone an ambulance, drive to hospital, switch on lights for a doctor.
  • Yom Kippur: a person whose health is at risk must eat and drink; fasting would be wrong.
  • Kashrut: non-kosher medicine may be taken when it is needed for health.
  • Community: in the UK, Hatzola volunteer ambulance teams serve Jewish neighbourhoods, working on Shabbat and festivals.
  • Social: blood donation, medical careers and (for many) organ donation are seen as mitzvot.
  • Your spec asks for personal, social and community life: individual choices, medical ethics, and community services like Hatzola.

Mission 3Diversity

Applying pikuach nefesh today

  • Agreed by all groups: saving life overrides Shabbat, fasting and food laws.
  • Relieving suffering: does it cover pain or illness that is not life-threatening? Orthodox law relaxes some Shabbat rules for the sick but not all; many rabbis now treat serious mental illness as pikuach nefesh.
  • Organ donation: living donation is widely praised. For donation after death, Orthodox authorities differ, mainly over how death is defined. Reform and Liberal Judaism encourage it.
  • End of life: all mainstream groups reject actively killing a patient. Many Orthodox authorities allow stopping treatment that only prolongs dying. Some Reform and Liberal Jews support assisted dying for the terminally ill.
  • Abortion: all permit it, and Orthodox law requires it, when the mother's life is in danger (Mishnah Oholot 7:6); views on other reasons differ.
Mission 3

Which lives are sacred?

  • In Jewish teaching, human life is uniquely sacred: only humans bear God's image.
  • Animal life matters too: tza'ar ba'alei chayim (preventing animal suffering). Even an enemy's donkey must be helped: "thou shalt surely release it with him" (Exodus 23:5).
  • Shechita (kosher slaughter) aims to minimise pain; eating meat is permitted (Genesis 9:3).
  • But pikuach nefesh applies to human life: a human life outweighs an animal's.
  • Before birth: most authorities see the foetus as potential life: precious, but the mother's life comes first until birth (Mishnah Oholot 7:6).
  • Groups differ: some Jews are vegetarian for ethical reasons; most see eating meat as permitted.
Mission 3Evaluate

"Saving a life should always come before religious laws."

  • For: "he shall live by them" (Leviticus 18:5; Yoma 85b). Saving one life is like saving a world (Sanhedrin 4:5). Even doubt about danger overrides Shabbat.
  • Against: the three exceptions (Sanhedrin 74a): some laws may not be broken even to save life; many Jews were martyred rather than worship idols.
  • Against: "always" is too broad: the danger must be real, and many rabbis say pikuach nefesh does not cover every kind of suffering.
  • Judge it by: the Talmud's own limits; Orthodox and Reform views; whether saving life is itself a religious law.
Mission 3Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain two reasons why pikuach nefesh is important for Jews. Refer to a source of wisdom and authority.

  • Reason 1: human life is holy. Source: "God created man in His own image" (Genesis 1:27). Development: so saving any person matters more than keeping almost any other mitzvah.
  • Reason 2: the Torah is meant to give life. Source: "he shall live by them" (Leviticus 18:5), which the Talmud reads as "not die by them" (Yoma 85b). Development: so a Jew who breaks Shabbat to save a life is praised, not blamed.

Mission 4

Body and soul

  • "the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul." (Genesis 2:7)
  • In Jewish teaching, a human is one being: a body from the earth and a soul (neshamah) breathed by God.
  • The morning prayers thank God for a soul that is pure when given (Talmud Berakhot 60b, paraphrase).
  • The body is not a prison: it is God's gift, used to keep the mitzvot. So the dead are buried with care and respect.
  • A Talmud parable: a blind man and a lame man steal fruit together; the owner judges them together. So God judges body and soul together (Talmud Sanhedrin 91a–b, paraphrase).
Mission 4

A faith centred on this life

  • The Torah says almost nothing about life after death. Its rewards are long life, family and the land.
  • Sheol, in the earliest books, is the shadowy place of all the dead, good and bad alike: "in the nether-world who will give Thee thanks?" (Psalm 6:5)
  • Later books and the rabbis developed fuller ideas: resurrection, judgement, a world to come.
  • The rabbis still put this life first: this world is like a hallway before the banquet hall; prepare in the hallway (Pirkei Avot 4:16, paraphrase).
  • One hour of repentance and good deeds in this world is better than all the life of the world to come (Pirkei Avot 4:17, paraphrase).
Mission 4Read the source

Ecclesiastes 12

> "And the dust returneth to the earth as it was, and the spirit returneth unto God who gave it." (Ecclesiastes 12:7)

> "The end of the matter, all having been heard: fear God, and keep His commandments; for this is the whole man. For God shall bring every work into the judgment concerning every hidden thing, whether it be good or whether it be evil." (Ecclesiastes 12:13–14)

  • The chapter opens: "Remember then thy Creator in the days of thy youth" (Ecclesiastes 12:1).
  • Readings: many Jews see verse 7 as the soul surviving and returning to God, and verses 13–14 as judgement.
  • Others note the book's doubts: "Who knoweth the spirit of man whether it goeth upward" (Ecclesiastes 3:21). For them, the message is: live well now.

Mission 4

Gan Eden, Gehinnom and Olam Ha-Ba

  • Gan Eden ("Garden of Eden"): in rabbinic teaching, where the souls of the righteous enjoy closeness to God.
  • Gehinnom: named after a valley outside Jerusalem. For most souls it is a place of cleansing, lasting at most twelve months (Mishnah Eduyot 2:10, paraphrase), not eternal hell.
  • So mourners say Kaddish for eleven months: they do not suggest a parent needed the full twelve.
  • Olam Ha-Ba ("the world to come"): the soul's life after death, or the renewed world after the resurrection. Rabbis disagree which.
  • All Israel have a share in the world to come (Mishnah Sanhedrin 10:1, paraphrase), and so do the righteous of all nations (Tosefta Sanhedrin 13:2, paraphrase).
  • Your spec spells it Gehenna.
Mission 4

Resurrection and judgement

> "And many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to reproaches and everlasting abhorrence." (Daniel 12:2)

  • "And they that are wise shall shine as the brightness of the firmament" (Daniel 12:3).
  • Resurrection (techiyat ha-metim): Jewish tradition teaches that at the end of days, in the Messianic Age, God will raise the dead, body and soul reunited.
  • The Amidah, prayed daily, praises God who "revives the dead". Maimonides made it his thirteenth principle.
  • Judgement in three stages: each year at Rosh Hashanah; at death, when the soul is judged; and at the end of days.
  • God judges with justice and mercy: by deeds and by heart, not by being Jewish or not.
Quick check

Mission 4Diversity

Orthodox and Reform beliefs

  • Orthodox: the soul is immortal; bodily resurrection in the Messianic Age; judgement after death and at the end of days; Gan Eden and Gehinnom.
  • Because of resurrection and respect for the body, Orthodox Jews bury the dead quickly and do not cremate.
  • Reform: classical Reform (Pittsburgh, 1885) kept the immortality of the soul but rejected bodily resurrection and Gehenna and Eden as places of reward and punishment.
  • Many Reform prayer books replaced "who revives the dead" with "who gives life to all"; some recent ones offer both.
  • Many Reform Jews speak of living on through deeds, memory and children; some believe in spiritual resurrection; many stay agnostic. Cremation is permitted.
  • Both stress this life: what happens next is God's concern; ours is how we live now.
Mission 4Evaluate

"Belief in life after death is not important for Jews."

  • For: the Torah barely mentions it; Avot 4:17 puts good deeds in this world first; many Reform Jews are agnostic about it.
  • Against: Maimonides made resurrection the thirteenth principle; the Amidah praises God who revives the dead every day; Orthodox burial practices rest on it.
  • Against: belief in judgement gives a reason to live justly, and hope gives comfort in mourning (Kaddish, shiva).
  • Judge it by: Orthodox and Reform views; what Jews pray daily; whether "this life first" means "the next life does not matter".
Mission check

?An Orthodox Jewish family in Leeds holds a funeral within a day of their grandmother's death. She is buried, not cremated, and her grandson will say Kaddish for eleven months. Which belief do these practices most clearly reflect?

Jews hold that the soul returns to God and, for many, the dead will rise; but the focus stays on living this life well.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The land is part of God's covenant with Abraham (Genesis 12:1–3); Jerusalem and the Temple stay central in prayer and memory (Psalm 137:5; Isaiah 51:3).
  • Religious Zionist, secular Zionist, Haredi and Reform Jews see the State of Israel differently.

Mission 2

  • The Torah contains 613 mitzvot: positive and negative; between people and God, and between people.
  • Jews believe God gave every person free will (Deuteronomy 30:19); the yetzer ha-tov and yetzer ha-ra pull both ways, and the Torah guides the choice.
  • Maimonides (Sefer Madda): anyone can be as righteous as Moses; commands would be pointless without freedom.
  • Orthodox Jews hold all mitzvot equally binding; Reform Jews put ethical mitzvot first.

Mission 3

  • Jews believe human life is holy because each person is made in God's image (Genesis 1:27) and life belongs to God.
  • Pikuach nefesh: saving a life overrides almost all mitzvot, including Shabbat and Yom Kippur (Leviticus 18:5; Talmud Yoma 83–85), except idolatry, murder and forbidden relations.
  • Jews agree on the principle but differ on applications: relieving suffering, organ donation, end of life.

Mission 4

  • In Jewish teaching, body and soul form one person (Genesis 2:7); at death "the spirit returneth unto God who gave it" (Ecclesiastes 12:7).
  • Ideas developed from Sheol to Gan Eden, Gehinnom (mostly temporary) and Olam Ha-Ba; resurrection and judgement (Daniel 12:2).
  • Orthodox Jews believe in bodily resurrection; many Reform Jews in the immortality of the soul only; all put this life first.
