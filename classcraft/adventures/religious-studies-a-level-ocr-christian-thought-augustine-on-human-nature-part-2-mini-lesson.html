Mission 1

Dietrich Bonhoeffer

  • Dietrich Bonhoeffer (1906–1945): German Lutheran pastor and theologian.
  • 1933: Hitler comes to power. Bonhoeffer opposes the Nazi takeover of the Church from the start.
  • 1935–37: directs the Confessing Church's seminary at Finkenwalde; The Cost of Discipleship (1937).
  • 1940 onwards: works for German military intelligence (the Abwehr) as cover for the resistance.
  • Arrested 5 April 1943; executed at Flossenbürg concentration camp on 9 April 1945.
  • Key works: The Cost of Discipleship (1937), Life Together (1939), Ethics (1949, unfinished), Letters and Papers from Prison (1951).
Mission 1

Duty to the State

  • Lutheran tradition: God rules the world through two "kingdoms": the Church (gospel) and the State (law and the sword).
  • Romans 13:1–7: authorities are "ordained by God"; Christians should normally obey.
  • Bonhoeffer accepted this: the State has a God-given task: to keep order and justice (later he called this one of God's mandates).
  • But a State that destroys order and justice is failing that task.
  • Then the Church must speak, because duty to God comes first.

Mission 1

Leadership: leader or misleader?

  • 1 February 1933, two days after Hitler became Chancellor: Bonhoeffer gave a radio talk on the concept of the leader (Führer). The broadcast was cut off before the end.
  • His point (paraphrase): a true leader serves those he leads and points beyond himself, to God's authority. A leader who makes himself an idol becomes a misleader (Verführer).
  • Obedience is owed to God first; any human leader is under God's will.
  • So leadership and obedience are both judged by doing God's will.
Mission 1

The Church and the Jewish question

  • April 1933: the Nazi Aryan paragraph excluded Jews (including Christians of Jewish descent) from public offices.
  • Bonhoeffer's essay "The Church and the Jewish Question" (1933) gives the Church three possible responses to an unjust State (paraphrase):
  • 1. Question the State: ask whether its actions are legitimate.
  • 2. Help the victims of the State's actions, whether or not they are Christians.
  • 3. Not just bandage the victims under the wheel, but put a spoke in the wheel itself: direct political action.
  • The third step is the root of his later civil disobedience.

Mission 1

The Confessing Church

  • The German Christians (Deutsche Christen): a pro-Nazi movement that wanted a Reich Church, the Aryan paragraph and a Jesus stripped of Jewish roots.
  • Opponents formed the Pastors' Emergency League (September 1933, led by Martin Niemöller), then the Confessing Church (1934).
  • Barmen Declaration (May 1934, drafted mainly by Karl Barth): Jesus Christ is the one Word of God whom Christians must obey; the Church may not accept other "lords" or let the State define its message (paraphrase).
  • Bonhoeffer was a pastor in London in 1933–35 but supported Barmen; he pushed the Confessing Church to speak for Jews, not only for its own freedom.
  • The Barmen Declaration is an OCR suggested source.
Mission 1

Finkenwalde: a community of discipline

  • 1935: the Confessing Church asked Bonhoeffer to train its ordinands (trainee pastors) in an illegal seminary, first at Zingst, then at Finkenwalde (Pomerania).
  • A shared daily life: morning and evening prayer, psalms, hymns, scripture meditation in silence, study, shared work, confession to one another.
  • A House of Brethren lived there in community.
  • Life Together (1939) describes it: Christian community is a gift of God, not an ideal to build; "Bear one another's burdens" (Galatians 6:2).
  • Luke 10:38–42 (Mary and Martha), an OCR suggested source: listening to Christ comes before activity, but Finkenwalde joined both.
  • The Gestapo closed the seminary in September 1937; training went on in secret "collective pastorates" until 1940.

Mission 1

Civil disobedience and the conspiracy

  • Civil disobedience: deliberately breaking the law for reasons of conscience.
  • Bonhoeffer had been close to pacifism, shaped by the Sermon on the Mount.
  • From 1940, through his brother-in-law Hans von Dohnanyi, he joined the resistance inside the Abwehr: carrying messages abroad, and helping Jews escape to Switzerland (Operation 7, 1942).
  • Ethics: a responsible person may have to act in a situation where every choice incurs guilt, and must accept that guilt before God, trusting in grace (paraphrase).
  • Scholars debate how far he took part in plans to kill Hitler: many biographies say he supported them; others (e.g. Nation, Siegrist and Umbel, Bonhoeffer the Assassin?, 2013) argue his role has been overstated.
  • His justification: the State had become lawless; obedience to God required resistance.
Mission 1Evaluate

Should Christians practise civil disobedience?

  • Claim: Christians should practise civil disobedience when the State acts unjustly.
  • For: "We must obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29); a State that destroys justice abandons its God-given task.
  • For: Bonhoeffer's own example: silence would have made the Church complicit.
  • Against: Romans 13:1–7 teaches submission; the early Church suffered rather than rebelled.
  • Against: "Unjust" is easily claimed; disobedience can spread disorder.
  • Linked issue: is it always possible to know God's will? Bonhoeffer: no ready-made rule; discern through prayer, scripture and community, and accept the risk of guilt (Romans 12:2: "prove what is the good … will of God").
  • Judgement criteria: How grave the injustice is · whether other means have failed · whether the act is non-violent · whether the believer accepts the consequences.

Mission 2

Ethics as action: following Christ

  • Nachfolge (1937), in English The Cost of Discipleship: "following" Christ.
  • For Bonhoeffer, ethics is not first a theory but obedient action: answering Christ's call in a concrete situation.
  • The call of Levi: "He said to him, 'Follow me.' And he arose and followed him" (Mark 2:14, WEBBE). No explanation, no programme: just the step.
  • Faith and obedience belong together: only the one who believes obeys, and only the one who obeys believes (paraphrase of his famous pairing).
  • The book reads the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5–7) as commands to be lived, not ideals to admire.
Mission 2

Cheap grace

  • Grace: God's free, undeserved love and forgiveness. Lutherans stress that people are saved by grace through faith, not by works.
  • Bonhoeffer's charge: German Lutheranism had turned grace into a doctrine to agree with, not a call to follow.
  • Cheap grace = forgiveness without repentance, baptism without discipline, communion without confession; in his words, "grace without discipleship, grace without the cross" (The Cost of Discipleship, ch. 1).
  • He opens the book by calling cheap grace the deadly enemy of the Church (ch. 1, paraphrase).
  • Result: a Church that could comfort Nazi supporters without challenging them.
Mission 2

Costly grace

  • Costly grace is the treasure hidden in a field, for which a man sells everything (Matthew 13:44).
  • Costly, because it calls a person to follow Jesus, and costs them their life.
  • Grace, because it gives the only true life, and is still God's free gift.
  • Costly for God too: it cost the life of his Son.
  • So costly grace does not mean earning salvation: it means grace that changes the one who receives it.
  • Not "faith or works" but faith that acts: compare James 2:17 ("faith, if it has no works, is dead").

Mission 2

Sacrifice and suffering

  • "When Christ calls a man, he bids him come and die" (The Cost of Discipleship, ch. 4, "Discipleship and the Cross"): dying to self, and for some, literal death.
  • Suffering is a mark of discipleship: the disciple shares in Christ's rejection and cross.
  • Bonhoeffer did not seek suffering; it came from obedience.
  • His own choice, 1939: safe in New York, he returned to Germany within weeks; he wrote to Reinhold Niebuhr that he would have no right to help rebuild Christian life in Germany after the war unless he shared its trials now (paraphrase).
  • Prison (1943–45) and execution (9 April 1945) completed the pattern his book had described.
Mission 2

Solidarity

  • Solidarity: standing with those who suffer, sharing their situation.
  • In prison he wrote that "only the suffering God can help" (letter of 16 July 1944): God is with people in weakness, not as a rescuer from outside.
  • "The view from below" ("After Ten Years", 1942–43): learning to see history from the side of the outcast, the suspected and the powerless (paraphrase).
  • The Church is the Church only when it exists for others ("Outline for a Book", 1944, paraphrase).
  • Solidarity with Jews: from 1933 he insisted the Church must stand with them, not only defend its own members.
  • Costly grace leads outward: to share Christ's suffering is to share the suffering of others.
Mission 2Diversity

Who claims Bonhoeffer?

  • Evangelicals: the call to wholehearted discipleship; The Cost of Discipleship as devotional classic.
  • Radical theologians of the 1960s (e.g. John Robinson, Honest to God, 1963): his prison idea of "religionless Christianity".
  • Liberation and anti-apartheid theologians: the "view from below"; the Church for the oppressed.
  • Political movements of very different kinds have invoked him as a model of resistance; scholars warn that this can turn him into a symbol that fits any cause.
  • One life, read in many ways: a sign of his influence, and a warning to read him in his own context.

Mission 2Evaluate

Too much emphasis on suffering?

  • Claim: Bonhoeffer puts too much emphasis on suffering.
  • For: "Come and die" can make suffering an ideal; some feminist theologians argue that praising self-sacrifice has been used to keep the powerless, especially women, in harmful situations.
  • For: Most Christians are not called to martyrdom; a theology shaped by Nazi Germany may not fit ordinary life.
  • Against: He spoke of suffering as the cost of obedience, not its goal; Life Together is full of joy, thanks and shared meals.
  • Against: Mark 8:34 and the cross put suffering at the heart of Christian discipleship; he is following the gospel, not adding to it.
  • Judgement criteria: Whether suffering is chosen or accepted · who pays the cost · faithfulness to the New Testament · context then and now.
Mission 2Evaluate

Is his theology relevant today?

  • Relevant: cheap grace is a lasting temptation: belief without change of life.
  • Relevant: his questions (when to resist, how to act with no clean option, how a church keeps its voice) recur wherever states abuse power.
  • Less relevant? His categories assume a State Church and a totalitarian regime; liberal democracies offer legal routes of protest.
  • Less relevant? His late "religionless Christianity" is fragmentary and disputed: it is not clear what he meant.
  • Strong answers use specific cases and stay fair to his context.

Mission 3

Three answers to one question

  • The question: can people of other faiths, or none, be saved (brought into a right relationship with God, now and for ever)?
  • Christian theology of religions usually gives three answers (Alan Race, Christians and Religious Pluralism, 1983):
  • Exclusivism: salvation only through explicit faith in Christ.
  • Inclusivism: salvation is only through Christ, but Christ's grace can reach people who do not know him.
  • Pluralism: the great religions are different, valid paths to salvation (see Mission 4).
  • Context: the end of the colonial missionary age, the Holocaust and migration made the question urgent for twentieth-century churches.
Mission 3Read the source

Two "no other" texts

> "Jesus said to him, 'I am the way, the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father, except through me.'" (John 14:6, WEBBE)

> "There is salvation in no one else, for there is no other name under heaven that is given amongst men, by which we must be saved!" (Acts 4:12, WEBBE)

  • John 14: Jesus speaks to his disciples (Thomas has asked "how can we know the way?").
  • Acts 4: Peter speaks to the Jewish council after a healing done "in the name of Jesus".

Q1 What do both texts claim is unique about Jesus?

Show answer

He is the only way to God / the only name by which people are saved.

Q2 Does either text say how people who never hear of Jesus will be judged?

Show answer

No: both speak to people who have met Jesus or his apostles. That gap is what the debate is about.

Mission 3

Exclusivism developed

  • "No salvation outside the Church" (extra ecclesiam nulla salus): Cyprian (d. 258); defined strongly at the Council of Florence (1442).
  • Karl Barth (1886–1968), Church Dogmatics I/2, §17 (1938): all religion, Christianity included, is human striving and "unbelief"; only God's revelation in Christ saves, by grace. True religion exists only as a sinner is justified.
  • Evangelical exclusivism: the Lausanne Covenant (1974) rejects any dialogue that implies Christ speaks equally through all religions (paraphrase).
  • OCR wording: only Christianity fully offers the means of salvation.
  • Strengths: takes John 14:6 and Acts 4:12 at face value; gives urgency to mission; keeps Christ unique.
  • Problem: what of those who never hear the gospel through no fault of their own (the unevangelised)? Does that fit a God who "desires all people to be saved" (1 Timothy 2:4, WEBBE)?

Mission 3

Strengths and limits

  • Restrictivism (strict exclusivism): only those who hear and believe in this life are saved.
  • Some exclusivists leave the fate of the unevangelised to God's mercy without claiming to know it (agnostic exclusivism).
  • Critics: strict exclusivism seems to make salvation depend on where and when a person is born.
  • Defenders reply: truth is not decided by fairness as we judge it; God's justice is greater than ours; and the remedy is mission, not changing doctrine.

Texts that point wider

> "Truly I perceive that God doesn't show favouritism; but in every nation he who fears him and works righteousness is acceptable to him." (Acts 10:34–35, WEBBE)

> "The true light that enlightens everyone was coming into the world." (John 1:9, WEBBE)

  • Also cited: Acts 17:23 (Paul in Athens: "TO AN UNKNOWN GOD"); Romans 2:14–15 (Gentiles who do "by nature the things of the law").
  • Q: Is Acts 10:34–35 inclusivist or pluralist? (Usually read as inclusivist: Peter goes on to preach Christ to Cornelius, so the acceptance leads to Christ, not past him.)
Mission 3

Rahner: the anonymous Christian

  • Karl Rahner (1904–1984), Jesuit; "Christianity and the Non-Christian Religions" (lecture, 1961; Theological Investigations vol. 5).
  • Every person is created open to God and offered grace: the supernatural existential.
  • Until the gospel truly reaches a person, their religion can be a lawful religion: a means through which Christ's grace works.
  • Someone who responds to that grace in conscience and love is an anonymous Christian: saved through Christ without knowing his name.
  • The Church is the explicit expression of what is hidden elsewhere. Mission still matters: explicit faith is a fuller, clearer form of salvation.
Mission 3

The Catholic Church after Vatican II

  • Lumen Gentium 16 (1964): those who, through no fault of their own, do not know Christ but seek God sincerely and try to do his will "may achieve eternal salvation" (paraphrase).
  • Nostra Aetate 2 (1965): the Church "rejects nothing that is true and holy" in other religions.
  • Dominus Iesus (2000): Christ is the one saviour; followers of other religions are, objectively, in a "gravely deficient situation" compared with those in the Church.
  • So official Catholic teaching is inclusivist, while insisting on the uniqueness of Christ and the Church.
  • Criticisms of "anonymous Christian": patronising (it labels people with a name they reject); it may weaken mission; Hans Küng thought it smuggled others into the Church without asking them.

Mission 3Evaluate

Does the Bible teach exclusivism?

  • Claim: The Christian Bible teaches that only those with explicit faith in Christ can be saved.
  • For: John 14:6 and Acts 4:12 are direct and unqualified; the Great Commission assumes people need the gospel (Matthew 28:19).
  • Against: The texts say salvation is through Christ; they do not say it requires knowing him (inclusivist reading).
  • Against: Acts 10:34–35, John 1:9 and 1 Timothy 2:4 (God "desires all people to be saved") point to God's will to save all.
  • Judgement criteria: context and audience of each text; how to treat texts in tension; the character of God; the fate of those who never hear.

Mission 4

Diversity is a fact; pluralism is a claim

  • Religious diversity (sociological): many faiths live side by side, as in modern Britain (see Mission 5). That is a fact.
  • Tolerance: accepting people's right to hold other beliefs, while still thinking one's own beliefs true.
  • Religious pluralism (philosophical, or theological): the claim that the great religions are equally valid routes to the same ultimate reality.
  • A Christian can welcome diversity and practise tolerance without being a pluralist.
Mission 4

Hick's Copernican revolution

  • John Hick (1922–2012), philosopher of religion; an evangelical convert as a student. Working in multi-faith Birmingham from 1967, he met devout Muslims, Sikhs, Hindus and Jews whose lives seemed as holy as Christians'.
  • God and the Universe of Faiths (1973): theology needs a "Copernican revolution".
  • Old picture (Ptolemaic): Christianity at the centre; other faiths circle it.
  • New picture (Copernican): God at the centre; all religions, Christianity included, circle God.
  • Hick calls inclusivism an epicycle: a patch that keeps the old picture going, as astronomers added epicycles to save the earth-centred model.
Mission 4

The Real

  • An Interpretation of Religion (1989; Gifford Lectures 1986–87).
  • The Real in itself (an sich) is beyond all human concepts: neither personal nor impersonal.
  • Borrowing Kant's distinction between things in themselves and things as they appear: we know the Real only as humanly experienced, through a culture's concepts.
  • Personae (the Real experienced as personal): the Holy Trinity, Allah, the LORD of Israel, Vishnu.
  • Impersonae (the Real experienced as impersonal): Brahman, Nirvana, the Tao.
  • Hick uses the old parable of blind men and an elephant: each touches one part and describes the whole wrongly.
Mission 4

Salvation as transformation

  • For Hick, salvation (or liberation) is the transformation of human life from self-centredness to Reality-centredness.
  • Test (soteriological criterion): look at the fruits. Love, compassion and holiness (saints) appear in all the great traditions, as far as we can tell, to a roughly similar degree.
  • So no single religion can claim to be uniquely saving.
  • Doctrines that clash (one life or many; a personal God or not) are, for Hick, human responses to the Real, not literal descriptions of it.

Mission 4

Rethinking the incarnation

  • Pluralism faces a problem: if Jesus is God incarnate, Christianity seems uniquely founded by God.
  • Hick edited The Myth of God Incarnate (1977) and wrote The Metaphor of God Incarnate (1993; 2nd ed. 2005).
  • Incarnation is a metaphor: Jesus was so open to God that God's love was embodied in his life, as it can be in other lives.
  • Jesus did not claim to be God; the doctrine developed later in the Church (Hick's reading).
Mission 4

Criticisms of pluralism

  • It contradicts central Christian beliefs: Incarnation, Trinity, the resurrection and John 14:6 must all be reinterpreted; critics such as Alister McGrath (Christian Theology, ch. 17) say the result is no longer recognisable Christianity.
  • It is a hidden exclusivism: Gavin D'Costa (b. 1958) argues that pluralism judges every religion by its own standard (Hick's Real), so it is one more exclusive position, not a neutral one ("The Impossibility of a Pluralist View of Religions", 1996).
  • The Real is empty: if nothing can be said of the Real, why call it the source of all religions?
  • Religions do not see themselves this way: most believers think their tradition's claims are literally true; pluralism reinterprets them without their consent.
  • Conflicting truths: a personal God and an impersonal Brahman cannot both be literally the ultimate reality.
  • Hick's reply: these are mythological truths about our response, not literal descriptions of the Real.
Mission 4Read the source

Will all be saved?

> "For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive." (1 Corinthians 15:22, WEBBE)

> "and through him to reconcile all things to himself by him" (Colossians 1:20, WEBBE)

  • Christian universalism: in the end all will be saved, through Christ. Origen (c. 185–c. 254) hoped for the restoration of all things (apokatastasis); Barth spoke of all humanity elected in Christ, though he refused to call himself a universalist.
  • Pluralistic universalism: all will be saved, through many paths, with no single saviour. Hick held this (Death and Eternal Life, 1976).
  • OCR issues: Would a loving God deny anyone salvation? Will all good people be saved?
  • Q: Which of the two kinds of universalism does 1 Corinthians 15:22 support, if either? (Christian universalism at most: it says "in Christ".)

Mission 4Diversity

Hick and Rahner compared

  • Centre: Rahner, Christ; Hick, the Real.
  • Other religions: Rahner, lawful paths of Christ's grace until the gospel arrives; Hick, valid responses to the Real in their own right.
  • Mission: Rahner, still needed for explicit, fuller faith; Hick, dialogue rather than conversion.
  • Incarnation: Rahner, literal and unique; Hick, metaphor.
  • Each one's critique: Hick, Rahner's view is a patronising epicycle; Rahner's followers, Hick's view gives up the uniqueness of Christ.
Mission 4Evaluate

Can a committed Christian be a pluralist?

  • Claim: It is possible to be both a committed Christian and a religious pluralist.
  • For: Hick himself remained a Christian (an ordained Presbyterian minister).
  • For: God's love for all (1 Timothy 2:4) suggests many ways; pluralism respects the holiness found in other faiths.
  • Against: Pluralism reinterprets the Incarnation, the Trinity and John 14:6 as metaphor.
  • Against: D'Costa: pluralism is itself an exclusive standpoint.
  • Judgement criteria: What is essential to being Christian? Is a metaphorical incarnation enough? Does the soteriological criterion (Hick's test of changed lives) work? Can there be neutral ground?
  • Spec issue: whether theological pluralism undermines central Christian beliefs.

Mission 5

How Britain became multi-faith

  • 1948 on: Caribbean migrants (the Windrush generation), mostly Christian.
  • 1950s–70s: migrants from India, Pakistan and Bangladesh bring Islam, Sikhism and Hinduism to British cities.
  • 1968–72: East African Asians arrive from Kenya and Uganda.
  • 1980s on: growing migration from West Africa (Nigeria, Ghana); later from the EU (2004: e.g. Polish Catholics) and as refugees.
  • 2021 Census (England and Wales): Christian 46.2%; no religion 37.2%; Muslim 6.5%; Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist and Jewish communities each 2% or less.
  • Migration has made Britain more religiously diverse and brought many Christians: much church growth in London is in migrant-led churches.
Mission 5

Freedom of religion in a secular state

  • European Convention on Human Rights (1950), Article 9: freedom of thought, conscience and religion, including freedom to change religion and to manifest it "in worship, teaching, practice and observance".
  • Holding a belief is protected absolutely; manifesting it may be limited, but only where limits are lawful and "necessary in a democratic society" (e.g. public safety, others' rights), and no wider than needed (proportionate).
  • In UK law through the Human Rights Act 1998; the Equality Act 2010 makes "religion or belief" a protected characteristic.
  • Secular state: the state does not take sides between religions. Religious pluralism, many faiths with equal rights, is a feature of such states.
  • Britain is a mixed case: an established Church of England, with bishops in the House of Lords, in a pluralist society.
Mission 5

When Christians migrate

  • Many Windrush-era Christians, Anglicans, Methodists and Baptists at home, found a cool welcome in some English churches; many founded their own (e.g. the New Testament Church of God, in Britain from 1953).
  • Assimilation: join existing British churches, or keep a distinct identity?
  • Provision of worship: new congregations rent school halls, buy warehouses or former cinemas; planning permission and noise can be issues.
  • Style of worship: often lively, Pentecostal or Charismatic; long services; prayer and preaching in the home language.
  • Culture: second and third generations may prefer English-language worship; tension between keeping heritage and reaching British neighbours.
  • Other migrant churches: Polish Catholic parishes; Romanian and Eritrean Orthodox communities; Chinese and Korean churches.
Mission 5Read the source

Redemptoris Missio 55–57

Pope John Paul II, encyclical Redemptoris Missio (1990), on dialogue with other religions (paraphrase):

  • 55: Interreligious dialogue is part of the Church's mission, not opposed to proclaiming Christ. God is present in other religions, though they have "gaps, insufficiencies and errors"; Christ is the one saviour and the Church "the ordinary means of salvation".
  • 56: Dialogue is not a tactic. It rests on respect, hope and love; each side stays true to its own convictions while listening honestly. Other religions challenge the Church to understand itself more deeply.
  • 57: Dialogue takes many forms: exchanges between experts, working together for human development, sharing spiritual experience, and the "dialogue of life" between ordinary neighbours. All Catholics are called to it.

Q1 Does dialogue replace evangelism in Redemptoris Missio?

Show answer

No: the two are distinct but connected; both are part of mission.

Q2 Which kind of dialogue could any parishioner take part in?

Show answer

The dialogue of life: witnessing to values in daily life alongside neighbours.

Mission 5

Sharing the Gospel of Salvation (Church of England)

  • 2009: General Synod passes a motion (proposed by Paul Eddy) asking the bishops to report on sharing the gospel of salvation through Christ in multi-faith Britain.
  • 2010: the House of Bishops' report, Sharing the Gospel of Salvation (GS Misc 956).
  • Reaffirms the uniqueness of Christ (God incarnate; the creeds) and the call to invite people to explicit faith and baptism.
  • Commits the Church both to evangelism and to dialogue and good neighbourliness; witness must be respectful, never coercive.
  • Gives good practice from multi-faith areas (e.g. Leicester, Southall, Birmingham, Bradford).
  • Its foreword insists that Christians should not be shy of speaking about conversion (paraphrase).
  • Compare: both documents hold mission and dialogue together; neither is pluralist (see Mission 4).
Mission 5

Scriptural reasoning

  • Origins: 1990s; Jewish scholars' "Textual Reasoning" (Peter Ochs) joined by Christians (David Ford, Daniel Hardy) and Muslims. Now practised in universities, faith communities, schools and prisons.
  • Method: a small group reads short passages from the Tanakh, the Bible and the Qur'an on one theme (e.g. hospitality). Members of each faith host their own text, explaining how it is read in their tradition. Read slowly; ask questions.
  • Aims: understanding and friendship; better disagreement, not agreement.
  • Ford's image: each tradition has its house; the academy is a campus; scriptural reasoning is a tent where people meet (Christian Wisdom, 2007, paraphrase).
  • Truth claims: participants bring their full claims; the practice does not assume all are equally true.

Mission 5Evaluate

Should Christians try to convert people of other faiths?

  • Claim: Christians in a multi-faith society should seek to convert people of other faiths.
  • For: The Great Commission: "make disciples of all nations" (Matthew 28:19); exclusivist reading of John 14:6.
  • For: Both Redemptoris Missio and Sharing the Gospel of Salvation hold that proclaiming Christ remains part of mission.
  • Against: Pluralists (see Mission 4): other faiths are valid paths; conversion is unnecessary and may harm community relations.
  • Against: Pressure to convert can damage trust between communities; the "dialogue of life" and service may witness better.
  • Judgement criteria: What do Christians believe about salvation? Can mission be respectful? Does it serve social cohesion? Does freedom of religion include the freedom to persuade?
  • Spec issues: whether Christians should seek to convert people of other faiths; whether they have a mission to people of no faith.

Mission 6

What Christians mean by mission

  • Mission (Latin mittere, to send): the Church's task of being sent into the world.
  • Evangelism: one part of mission: sharing the gospel ("good news") of Jesus so that people may come to faith.
  • Many Christians also count service (care, healing, education), justice and dialogue as mission.
  • Debates in this mission: Is mission mainly about converting people? Who sends whom? How should Christians share faith among people of other religions and none?
Mission 6Read the source

The Great Commission

> "Go and make disciples of all nations, baptising them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, teaching them to observe all things that I commanded you." (Matthew 28:19–20, WEBBE)

> "You will be witnesses to me in Jerusalem, in all Judea and Samaria, and to the uttermost parts of the earth." (Acts 1:8, WEBBE)

  • Christians take these words of the risen Jesus as the mandate for mission.

Q1 Which three activities does Matthew 28:19–20 name?

Show answer

Making disciples, baptising, teaching.

Q2 Why might Acts 1:8 suggest that mission spreads outward from a centre?

Show answer

It moves from Jerusalem, to the surrounding regions, to the ends of the earth.

Mission 6

How ideas of mission changed

  • 1910, Edinburgh: mission pictured as going from the "Christian West" to the "non-Christian world".
  • 1952, Willingen: mission is missio Dei, God's own mission; the Church does not own mission but takes part in what God is already doing.
  • 1963, Mexico City: "mission in six continents": every continent, the West included, is a mission field.
  • 1965, Vatican II, Ad Gentes: the whole Church is missionary by its nature. 1975, Evangelii Nuntiandi (Paul VI): evangelisation includes witness of life and changing society.
  • 1974, Lausanne Congress (evangelicals; John Stott, Billy Graham): evangelism and social action are both Christian duty.
  • 1984–90, Anglican Five Marks of Mission: tell, teach, tend, transform, treasure creation.
  • Shift in short: from sending to "heathen lands" to mission from everywhere to everywhere, with word and deed.
Mission 6

Mission among other faiths: two church documents

  • Catholic: Redemptoris Missio (John Paul II, 1990), sections 55–57 (paraphrase):
  • 55: inter-religious dialogue is "a part of the Church's evangelizing mission"; it is not opposed to mission to the nations. God is present in other religions' spiritual riches, but salvation comes from Christ, and dialogue does not replace proclamation.
  • 56: dialogue is not a tactic; Christians seek the "seeds of the Word" in others, stay faithful to their own convictions, and avoid false compromise.
  • 57: dialogue takes many forms: exchange between experts, working together, sharing spiritual experience, and the "dialogue of life" of ordinary neighbours.
  • Church of England: Sharing the Gospel of Salvation (2010): a report to General Synod, after a 2009 motion asking the bishops how Christ's uniqueness should shape witness in multi-faith Britain. It affirms that Christians should share the gospel with people of every faith and none, with respect, without coercion, alongside dialogue and good neighbourliness, and gives examples of local practice.
  • Both hold together witness and dialogue: the question is how, not whether, to share faith.

Mission 6Evaluate

Is mission still relevant to the spread of Christianity?

  • Claim: Mission and evangelism are no longer appropriate in a pluralist, secular society.
  • For: Many people see seeking converts as disrespectful of other faiths and of non-belief.
  • For: The link between historic mission and colonialism damaged trust.
  • Against: Christianity grew worldwide in the 20th century largely through local evangelism; mission still spreads belief.
  • Against: Mission has broadened: service, justice and dialogue sit beside proclamation (e.g. the Five Marks of Mission).
  • Against: Reverse mission shows that former mission fields still see evangelism as good news to share.
  • Judgement criteria: What counts as mission (conversion only, or word and deed)? Is respect compatible with seeking to persuade? Is the UK a mission field?

Mission 7

Equal in God's image

> "God created man in his own image. In God's image he created him; male and female he created them." (Genesis 1:27, WEBBE)

  • Both sexes bear the image of God (imago Dei): equal in dignity.
  • Genesis 2:18: the woman is a "helper comparable to him". The Hebrew ezer (helper) is used elsewhere of God himself (e.g. Psalm 121:2), so it does not mean inferior.
  • After the Fall, "he will rule over you" (Genesis 3:16): many Christians read male rule as a result of sin, not God's design.
  • In Christ "there is neither male nor female" (Galatians 3:28).
Mission 7Diversity

Equal, but are roles different?

  • Complementarian (many evangelicals; Danvers Statement, 1987): men and women are equal in worth, with different, complementary roles; husbands lead in the home and men lead in the church.
  • Egalitarian (Christians for Biblical Equality, established 1988): equal in worth and in roles; leadership by gifting, not sex.
  • Catholic (John Paul II, Mulieris Dignitatem, 1988): equal dignity; a distinct "genius" of women (§30); mutual self-gift; all-male priesthood.
  • Orthodox: equal in salvation; distinct roles; an all-male priesthood; great honour for Mary, the Theotokos.
  • Quakers: equal ministry from the 1650s (Margaret Fell, Women's Speaking Justified, 1666).
Mission 7Read the source

Ephesians 5:21–33

> "subjecting yourselves to one another in the fear of Christ. Wives, be subject to your own husbands, as to the Lord." (Ephesians 5:21–22, WEBBE)

> "Husbands, love your wives, even as Christ also loved the assembly and gave himself up for her," (Ephesians 5:25, WEBBE)

  • "Assembly" here means the Church. The husband is "head" as Christ is head of the Church (5:23).

Mission 7

Reading Ephesians 5 today

  • Complementarian reading: headship is real and permanent, but it means servant leadership, never domination; the wife's submission is freely given.
  • Egalitarian reading: verse 21 frames the passage: mutual submission. The "household code" adapts Greco-Roman rules (compare Aristotle's Politics) to Christ's love.
  • The same code tells slaves to obey masters (Ephesians 6:5): few Christians now read that as God's permanent order.
  • John Paul II (Mulieris Dignitatem §24): the newness of Ephesians is mutual subjection "out of reverence for Christ"; the husband's love excludes any domination.
  • OCR names Ephesians 5:22–33; verse 21 sets its context.
Mission 7

Mulieris Dignitatem 18–19

  • Apostolic letter of John Paul II, On the Dignity and Vocation of Women (15 August 1988).
  • §18, Motherhood: parenthood belongs to both parents, but the mother has a special part in it: a unique communion with new life growing within her. The father, paraphrased, must learn his fatherhood from the mother.
  • Motherhood shapes a woman's openness to others; it is a gift of self, not just biology.
  • §19, Motherhood and the Covenant: Mary's motherhood is linked to God's new covenant in Christ. Jesus teaches that hearing and keeping God's word is greater still (Luke 11:27–28): spiritual motherhood is open to all women, including those without children (developed in §21).
  • Catholic teaching: women's dignity is equal to men's; their vocation is distinct, not lesser.

Mission 7

1 Timothy 2:8–15

> "But I don't permit a woman to teach, nor to exercise authority over a man, but to be in quietness." (1 Timothy 2:12, WEBBE)

  • Reasons given: Adam was formed first; Eve was deceived (2:13–14); a woman "will be saved through her childbearing" (2:15).
  • Traditional reading: a rule for all times, grounded in creation, not culture; so women may not lead or preach to men in church.
  • This passage was long used to keep women out of church leadership and out of public life.
Mission 7

Biblical criticism opens the question

  • Biblical criticism studies the Bible as historical texts: who wrote them, when, why, for whom.
  • 19th century: German scholars such as Friedrich Schleiermacher (1807) and F. C. Baur (1835) argued that Paul did not write 1 Timothy. Many scholars now date it to c. 100 CE, written in Paul's name.
  • Context: the letter fights local false teaching (1 Timothy 1:3–7); the ban may target that situation, not all women for all time.
  • Elizabeth Cady Stanton, The Woman's Bible (1895–98): a critical commentary on texts used against women.
  • Result: the freedom to challenge traditional readings: a verse may express its author's culture rather than God's timeless will.
  • Conservative scholars still defend Paul's authorship and note that verse 13 appeals to creation, not culture.
Mission 7

Changing roles and secular rights

  • Rights from secular governments (UK): votes for some women over 30 (1918); equal franchise (1928); Equal Pay Act (1970); Sex Discrimination Act (1975); Equality Act (2010).
  • Changing roles: women in higher education and every profession; reliable contraception (from the 1960s); fathers sharing childcare.
  • Churches responded: some changed teaching and practice (women ministers; in the Church of England, marriage vows without "obey", first offered in the proposed 1928 Prayer Book and kept as the usual form in the Alternative Service Book, 1980, and Common Worship); others resisted, holding that scripture, not society, sets roles.
Mission 7

Parenthood and types of family

  • Family types: nuclear; extended; single-parent; blended (step-families); same-sex parents; cohabiting couples.
  • Catholic teaching: the family, founded on the marriage of a man and a woman, is the domestic church (Familiaris Consortio, 1981). Amoris Laetitia (2016) calls for pastoral care of families in every situation without changing that teaching.
  • Other churches vary: many Protestant churches accept and support all family types; the Church of England allows prayers of blessing for same-sex couples (from 2023), without changing its doctrine of marriage.
  • Motherhood: liberating (a vocation and gift of self: Mulieris Dignitatem) or restricting (feminist critics: it ties women to the home)?
  • Spec issues: "whether or not motherhood is liberating or restricting"; "whether or not the idea of family is entirely culturally determined".

Mission 7Evaluate

Should Christian teaching resist secular views of gender?

  • Claim: Christian teaching should resist current secular views of gender roles.
  • For: Scripture and tradition, not changing culture, are the Church's authority; creation order (1 Timothy 2:13).
  • For: Equal dignity does not require identical roles: complementarity protects motherhood and family (Mulieris Dignitatem).
  • Against: Secular equality echoes Christian roots: imago Dei (Genesis 1:27) and Galatians 3:28.
  • Against: Biblical criticism shows that some "biblical roles" are ancient culture, like slavery rules.
  • Judgement criteria: Is a text cultural or timeless? Do roles express equal dignity or limit it? Who decides: scripture, tradition, reason or experience?
  • Spec issues: official teaching and secular views; "whether or not secular views of gender equality have undermined Christian gender roles".

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The State has a God-given task (order and justice); Christians normally obey (Romans 13), but obey God first (Acts 5:29).
  • Three responses to an unjust State (1933): question it, help its victims, put a spoke in the wheel.
  • The Confessing Church (Barmen, 1934) and Finkenwalde (1935–37): the Church as a disciplined community of prayer and resistance.

Mission 2

  • Cheap grace: forgiveness without discipleship. Costly grace: free gift that claims the whole life.
  • Sacrifice and suffering: the cost of following Christ, accepted, not sought.
  • Solidarity: the suffering God; the view from below; the Church for others.

Mission 3

  • Exclusivism (particularism): salvation only through explicit faith in Christ (John 14:6; Acts 4:12; Barth).
  • Inclusivism: salvation only through Christ, but Christ's grace can reach those who do not know him (Rahner's anonymous Christians; Vatican II).

Mission 4

  • Diversity is a fact; tolerance is an attitude; pluralism is a claim that religions are equally valid routes to the Real.
  • Hick: a Copernican revolution; the Real beyond all concepts; salvation as transformation; the Incarnation as metaphor.
  • Critics: it rewrites central Christian beliefs; it is itself exclusive (D'Costa); the Real is empty.
  • Christian universalism (all saved through Christ) differs from pluralistic universalism (all saved by many paths).

Mission 5

  • Migration since 1948 has made Britain multi-faith and brought new Christian communities; Article 9 protects belief and, within limits, its expression.
  • Redemptoris Missio 55–57 and Sharing the Gospel of Salvation hold dialogue and mission together; scriptural reasoning reads three scriptures side by side for understanding, not agreement.

Mission 6

  • Mission is the Church being sent (Matthew 28:19–20); evangelism is one part of it.
  • Since 1910 the picture changed: from West-to-rest conversion to missio Dei and mission from everywhere to everywhere, in word and deed; reverse mission sends Christians from the global South to Britain.
  • Redemptoris Missio 55–57 and Sharing the Gospel of Salvation hold witness and dialogue together.

Mission 7

  • Equal dignity (Genesis 1:27; Galatians 3:28) is common ground; Christians differ on roles: complementarian, egalitarian, Catholic "equal dignity, distinct vocation".
  • Ephesians 5:21–33 (mutual submission and loving headship) and Mulieris Dignitatem 18–19 (motherhood; covenant); responses to parenthood and family types.
