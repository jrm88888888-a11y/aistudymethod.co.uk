Mission 1

Mission 1: What holds the world together, and why do Hindus say it is slowly wearing away?

Make a guess: What holds the world together, and why do Hindus say it is slowly wearing away?

Mission 1

Dharma: what holds things up

  • Dharma comes from the root dhr: to hold, uphold, support.
  • English needs many words for it: duty, righteousness, law, virtue, religion, the right way.
  • It also means a thing's nature: the dharma of fire is to burn; of water, to flow.
  • So dharma is at once cosmic (how the world is ordered), social (how society works) and personal (how I should live).
  • A much-quoted saying: dharma protects those who protect it (Manusmriti 8.15, paraphrase).
Mission 1

Sanatana dharma: the eternal way

  • Sanatana means eternal: without beginning or end.
  • Sanatana dharma has two common uses:
  • 1. A name for the religion. Many Hindus prefer it to "Hinduism" (a name from outside): their way is not founded at a point in time but is eternal truth.
  • 2. Universal dharma. Duties for everyone, whatever their class or stage of life (also called sadharana dharma, common dharma).
  • Manusmriti 10.63 (paraphrase) sums them up: non-violence, truthfulness, not stealing, purity, control of the senses.
Mission 1

Universal and personal dharma

  • Alongside universal dharma, each person has svadharma: their own dharma.
  • Traditionally this is fixed by varna (class) and ashrama (stage of life): varnashrama dharma.
  • Krishna to Arjuna:

> "this is better, that one do / His own task as he may, even though he fail, / Than take tasks not his own, though they seem good." (Gita 3:35, Arnold)

  • Tension: Arjuna's universal dharma says "do not harm"; his svadharma as a warrior says "fight a just war". The Gita is built on that conflict.

When dharma declines

  • Adharma: the opposite of dharma: disorder, injustice, unrighteousness. It harms the world, society and the self, and brings bad karma.

> "When Righteousness / Declines, O Bharata! when Wickedness / Is strong, I rise, from age to age, and take / Visible shape, and move a man with men, / Succouring the good, thrusting the evil back, / And setting Virtue on her seat again." (Gita 4:7–8, Arnold)

  • Arnold translates dharma as "Righteousness" and adharma as "Wickedness".
  • Tradition pictures dharma as a bull that loses a leg in each of four ages (yugas); many Hindus believe we live in the last, the Kali Yuga.

Show answer

Mission 2

Varna + ashrama + dharma

  • Varnashramadharma: the dharma that fits your varna (class) and your ashrama (stage of life).
  • Its defenders present it as how universal order becomes social order: each person has a place and a duty, and society is held together as the cosmos is.
  • It is presented in the classical texts as an ideal social order: everyone's needs met by everyone's work. Critics see it as a justification of inequality.
Mission 2Read the source

The Purusha Sukta

Rig Veda 10.90 (paraphrase): the gods offer the cosmic Purusha (Person) in a great sacrifice. From his body the world is made. Verses 11–12:

  • From his mouth: the brahmana (priest).
  • From his arms: the rajanya (ruler, later called kshatriya).
  • From his thighs: the vaishya (merchant, farmer).
  • From his feet: the shudra (servant, labourer).
  • In the next verses the moon comes from his mind, the sun from his eye, the sky from his head.

Q1 What does the hymn suggest about society?

Show answer

Society as one body, each part with its own function; readers disagree whether head-to-foot order also implies a ranking.

Q2 What does it not mention?

Show answer

Jatis, untouchability, or rules about marriage and food.

Mission 2

Four varnas, four kinds of duty

  • As the classical texts describe them:
  • Brahmanas (brahmins): study and teach the Veda, perform ritual.
  • Kshatriyas: protect and rule; fight just wars.
  • Vaishyas: trade, farming, cattle.
  • Shudras: service of the other three.
  • The first three are dvija, "twice-born": the sacred-thread ceremony (upanayana) marks a second, spiritual birth.
  • The Gita lists each varna's qualities (Gita 18:41–44): serenity and learning for brahmins; courage for kshatriyas; trade and farming for vaishyas; service for shudras (paraphrase).

Mission 2Diversity

By birth or by nature?

Krishna in the Gita:

> "I am He / Made the Four Castes, and portioned them a place / After their qualities and gifts." (Gita 4:13, Arnold)

  • The Sanskrit says by guna (qualities) and karma (actions).
  • By birth: the Dharma Shastras (e.g. Manusmriti) assign varna by birth; duties are inherited.
  • By nature: many reformers read varna as a matter of qualities: Dayananda Saraswati (Arya Samaj); Vivekananda; today ISKCON and others.
Mission 2

Varna and jati

  • Varna: the four-fold scheme of the texts; ideal, all-India.
  • Jati: "birth group": thousands of local communities, often linked to an occupation, usually marrying within the group (endogamy), ranked by ideas of ritual purity.
  • Overlap: jatis claim or are given a place within a varna; that place can be disputed and has shifted over time.
  • Difference: the texts know four varnas; daily social life in India has been organised far more by jati.
  • English "caste" (from Portuguese casta) is used for both, which causes confusion.
  • Communities placed outside the four varnas were treated as "untouchable"; many now call themselves Dalits.
Mission 2

Jati in modern India: the debate

  • The Constitution of India (1950) abolished "untouchability" (Article 17) and banned discrimination by caste (Article 15); reserved places in education and jobs help disadvantaged groups.
  • Jati still shapes marriage, social networks and politics for many Indians; caste discrimination continues and is contested.
  • Views differ:
  • Reform: varna as an ideal of interdependent duties; jati ranking and untouchability as corruption (Gandhi's position for much of his life).
  • Abolition: the whole system is unjust and must go (B. R. Ambedkar, 1891–1956, who left Hinduism for Buddhism in 1956).
  • Identity: some value jati as community and heritage, without hierarchy.

Mission 3

Four stages of life

  • Brahmacharya: the student. Learn from a guru; celibacy, discipline, study of dharma.
  • Grihastha: the householder. Marry, work, raise children, give, worship.
  • Vanaprastha: the "forest-dweller", retirement. Hand on responsibilities; turn to spiritual practice.
  • Sannyasa: the renouncer (a sannyasi). Give up home, property and name; seek moksha.
  • Traditionally for twice-born men, beginning with the sacred thread; often pictured as four spans of about 25 years.
Mission 3

The householder holds it all up

  • Manusmriti 3.77–78 (paraphrase): as all creatures live by the air, so all the other stages live by the householder; it is the most important stage.
  • Householders feed students, support renouncers, care for elders and children, and keep worship going.
  • Traditional five daily offerings (pancha mahayajna): to the gods, the sages (study), the ancestors, other people (hospitality) and all beings (food for animals).
  • So a full worldly life is not a lesser path: it is dharma.
Quick check
?Why does Manusmriti call the grihastha stage the most important?
Mission 3

The stages today

  • Few Hindus follow all four stages strictly. Student and householder stages are almost universal; vanaprastha is often a gradual shift in retirement towards prayer, pilgrimage and service.
  • Sannyasis are a small minority; some renounce young without marrying (Shankara, by tradition, as a boy).
  • Many orders once accepted men only; women renouncers exist (e.g. the Sri Sarada Math, a women's order founded 1954).
  • In Britain and elsewhere, the stages are often taught as an ideal of a balanced life rather than a rule.
Mission 3Read the source

Own duty and universal duty

> "Better thine own work is, though done with fault, / Than doing others' work, ev'n excellently." (Gita 18:47, Arnold)

  • Link: both are dharma; varnashrama dharma is how universal dharma becomes concrete for one person at one stage.
  • Difference: sanatana dharma (non-violence, truth, purity, self-control) binds everyone always; varnashrama dharma changes with class and stage.
  • Tension: a warrior's duty to fight against universal non-violence (Arjuna).

Q1 What does Gita 18:47 value more than doing well?

Show answer

Doing one's own dharma, even imperfectly.

Q2 Which kind of dharma is it talking about?

Show answer

Svadharma: varnashrama dharma.

Mission 3

Bhakti changes the rules

  • Bhakti: loving devotion to God. In bhakti, what counts most is love, not birth, stage or ritual rank.

> "whoso will turn to Me, / Though they be born from the very womb of Sin, / Woman or man; sprung of the Vaisya caste / Or lowly disregarded Sudra,--all / Plant foot upon the highest path" (Gita 9:32, Arnold)

  • Bhakti poet-saints came from every background, including communities treated as untouchable: Nandanar (Tamil Shaiva saint), Chokhamela (14th-century Maharashtra), Ravidas (15th–16th-century north India, from a leather-working community).
  • Chaitanya (1486–1534) shared the chanting of Krishna's names with all, regardless of caste; Basava (12th century) founded a Shaiva movement that rejected caste.
  • Bhakti often relaxed the rules in worship more than in marriage or daily life: the change was real but uneven.
Mission 3

Community and the meaning of life

  • Community of believers: family (where most practice happens), jati networks, sampradayas (traditions), gurus and satsang (gatherings of devotees), the mandir.
  • The community passes on and interprets dharma, supports each stage (feeding sannyasis, teaching students) and, in the diaspora, keeps identity alive.
  • Meaning and purpose of life: to fulfil one's dharma through every stage, pay the three debts (rinas) to the gods (worship), the sages (study) and the ancestors (children), and finally reach moksha.

Mission 4

What ahimsa asks

  • Ahimsa: non-violence, non-harming (a- "not" + himsa "harm").
  • It covers thought, word and deed: hatred and cruel speech are himsa too.
  • It also covers harm you cause or approve through others.
  • Why: the same atman is in every living being.

> "… sees the Life-Soul resident / In all things living, and all living things / In that Life-Soul contained." (Bhagavad Gita 6:29, Arnold)

  • Karma: harm returns as suffering, in this life or the next.
  • Positive side: compassion and respect for all life.
Mission 4

The Jain roots

  • Jainism: an Indian tradition shaped by Mahavira (traditionally 599–527 BCE), the 24th and last Tirthankara ("ford-maker") of this age.
  • For Jains, ahimsa is the first and greatest vow. Monks and nuns take it absolutely; lay Jains as far as they can.
  • Every living being has a soul (jiva): humans, animals, insects, plants, even beings in earth, water, fire and air.
  • So: strict vegetarianism (all Jains), many avoid root vegetables (uprooting kills the whole plant), no eating after dark; monks sweep their path and some cover the mouth to protect tiny beings.
  • Many scholars think Jain and Buddhist teaching made ahimsa central in India; others stress that it also grew within Vedic and Upanishadic thought. The influence on Hinduism, and later on Gandhi, is clear.
Mission 4

Ahimsa in Hindu tradition

  • Chandogya Upanishad 3.17.4: non-violence is listed with austerity, generosity, uprightness and truthfulness as the "gifts" of a life lived as sacrifice (paraphrase).
  • Mahabharata (Anushasana Parva): ahimsa is called the highest dharma (ahimsa paramo dharmah).
  • Yoga Sutras 2.30–2.35: ahimsa is the first yama (restraint); where it is firmly established, hostility ceases (paraphrase).
  • Manusmrti 10.63: ahimsa is among the duties common to all four varnas (paraphrase).
  • But Hindu ahimsa is usually weighed against other duties: sacrifice in the Vedic period, the warrior's duty, self-defence. It means minimising harm rather than an absolute vow.
  • By contrast, Jains (an Indian tradition with ahimsa at its heart) take it further: monks and nuns vow ahimsa absolutely, some sweeping their path to protect tiny beings; Jains hold that harm binds karma to the soul.

Mission 4

Animals

  • Animals have atman and are part of samsara: a human may be reborn as an animal, and an animal as a human.
  • The cow is honoured as a mother (gau mata): she gives milk, and Krishna was a cowherd. Most Hindus do not eat beef; many states in India restrict cow slaughter.
  • Vegetarianism is widespread and seen as sattvic, but varies: many Bengali and coastal Hindus eat fish; some communities eat meat; some Shakta temples still offer animal sacrifice.
  • Many Hindus support gaushalas (cow shelters) and avoid leather, silk or products tested on animals; others accept animal testing for human medicine as a lesser harm.
Mission 4Evaluate

Can a person live by ahimsa today?

  • Claim: Ahimsa, meaning never harming any living being, is a realistic standard in the modern world.
  • For: Millions of Jains and Hindus live as strict vegetarians; ahimsa shapes diet, work and medicine.
  • For: Ahimsa is about intention and minimising harm, which anyone can practise.
  • Against: Harm is built into life: farming, transport, medicine. Even the Manusmrti, a Hindu law text, admits that daily housework kills small creatures (3.68).
  • Against: Modern supply chains hide harm; the Manusmrti says the buyer of meat shares in the killing (5.51).
  • Judgement criteria: Is ahimsa an absolute rule or an ideal to approach? Does intention or outcome matter more? Is Jain or Hindu ahimsa the standard being tested?
Mission check

Make a guess: Krishna tells Arjuna to fight; Gandhi called the same book his guide to non-violence. How?

Mission 5

Ahimsa meets the warrior's duty

  • In the Bhagavad Gita, Arjuna, a kshatriya (warrior), refuses to fight his relatives at Kurukshetra.
  • Krishna answers: a warrior's svadharma (own duty) is to fight a righteous war (dharma yuddha); refusing would be sin.

> "Nought better can betide a martial soul / Than lawful war" (Bhagavad Gita 2:31, Arnold)

  • The atman cannot be killed (2:19–20), so the soldier destroys only bodies.
  • So in traditional Hindu ethics, ahimsa is the general rule, but force to protect dharma can be a duty for those whose role it is.
Mission 5

Dharma yuddha: rules of righteous war

  • Just cause: to defend dharma, protect the weak, resist aggression; not greed.
  • Last resort: in the Mahabharata, Krishna goes as envoy to seek peace before the war.
  • Rules of conduct (Mahabharata; Manusmrti 7.90–93, paraphrase): fight only equals and armed opponents; do not strike those who surrender, flee, sleep or have lost their weapons; no hidden, poisoned or barbed weapons; spare non-combatants.
  • Even in war, the warrior should fight without hatred and without desire for gain.
Mission 5Read the source

Two ways to read the battle

> "… if thou shunn'st / This honourable field--a Kshattriya-- / If, knowing thy duty and thy task, thou bidd'st / Duty and task go by--that shall be sin!" (Bhagavad Gita 2:33, Arnold)

  • Literal reading (e.g. Sri Aurobindo, 1872–1950, Essays on the Gita): the Gita really does teach that righteous war can be a duty.
  • Allegorical reading (Gandhi, Anasaktiyoga, 1929): Kurukshetra is the human heart; the battle is between good and evil impulses; the Gita teaches selfless action and so, in the end, non-violence.

Q1 What does Krishna say Arjuna's refusal would be?

Show answer

Sin: a failure in his duty as a kshatriya.

Q2 How can Gandhi accept this verse yet reject war?

Show answer

He reads the "field" and the "battle" as the inner struggle of the soul, not a call to physical fighting.

Mission 5

Gandhi's ahimsa

  • Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi (1869–1948).
  • Influences: his Gujarati Vaishnava home, Jain friends and teachers (e.g. Raychandbhai), the Gita, Tolstoy and the Sermon on the Mount.
  • Ahimsa for Gandhi is active love, not passivity: refusing to hate, while resisting injustice.
  • It is the courage of the strong: he wrote (1920, paraphrase) that if the only choice were cowardice or violence, he would choose violence.
  • Linked with truth: he came to say that "Truth is God". Non-violence is the means; truth is the end.
  • Discipline: brahmacharya, simple living, vegetarianism, fasting.
Mission 5

Satyagraha: truth force

  • Satyagraha: satya (truth) + agraha (holding firmly): "truth force" or "soul force". Name chosen in South Africa, 1906–08.
  • Method: non-violent resistance to unjust laws: non-cooperation, boycott, strikes, marches, civil disobedience, fasting.
  • The satyagrahi accepts suffering (arrest, beatings) rather than inflicts it, aiming to convert the opponent, not to defeat them.
  • Rules: no hatred, no secrecy, no damage to persons, accept the legal penalty.
  • Salt March (12 March – 6 April 1930): about 385 km to Dandi to make salt in defiance of the British salt tax.
Mission 5Evaluate

Is ahimsa a workable standard in the modern world?

  • Claim: Ahimsa is a relevant and viable standard to live by today.
  • For: Gandhi's non-violent resistance (satyagraha, "truth force") helped win Indian independence (1947) and shaped the US civil rights movement.
  • For: Modern weapons make war's harm uncontrollable; non-violence may be the only rational path.
  • Against: Non-violence relies on the opponent's conscience. Gandhi's 1938 advice that Jews in Germany resist non-violently was strongly criticised (e.g. by Martin Buber, 1939).
  • Against: Independence came with the violence of Partition; India itself keeps an army and nuclear weapons.
  • Judgement criteria: Is ahimsa a personal virtue or a state policy? Does it require results to be valid? Does dharma yuddha already make ahimsa realistic?
Mission check

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Dharma (from dhr, "uphold"): the order that sustains the cosmos, society and the person; the successor to Vedic rta.
  • Sanatana dharma: the eternal way (a name for the religion) and the universal duties of all; svadharma: duties of one's own class and stage of life.

Mission 2

  • Varnashramadharma: duty by class (varna) and stage of life (ashrama), cosmic order as social order.
  • The Purusha Sukta (Rig Veda 10.90.11–12): brahmana from the mouth, rajanya from the arms, vaishya from the thighs, shudra from the feet.
  • Varna is a four-fold textual scheme; jatis are thousands of local birth groups; Hindus debate birth versus qualities (Gita 4:13) and the place of jati today.

Mission 3

  • Four ashramas: brahmacharya (student), grihastha (householder, the support of all), vanaprastha (retirement), sannyasa (renunciation).
  • Varnashrama dharma makes universal (sanatana) dharma concrete for each person; Gita 18:47 values one's own dharma over another's.
  • Bhakti made devotion, not birth, the measure in worship (Gita 9:32), though caste often persisted in social life.

Mission 4

  • Ahimsa is non-harming in thought, word and deed, based on atman in all beings and on karma.
  • Jain ahimsa is an absolute vow resting on a soul in every being; Hindu ahimsa is a very high duty usually weighed against others.

Mission 5

  • Traditional Hindu ethics weighs ahimsa against a warrior's duty: dharma yuddha allows just war under strict rules.
  • Gandhi read the Gita as an inner battle and made ahimsa a political force through satyagraha.
