Mission 1

Mission 1: Bacteria make the enzymes for milk sugar only when it is there. How do they know when to start?

Make a guess: Bacteria make the enzymes for milk sugar only when it is there. How do they know when to start?

Mission 1

Enzymes made only when needed

  • The bacterium Escherichia coli can feed on the sugar lactose.
  • β-galactosidase hydrolyzes lactose → glucose + galactose.
  • Lactose permease carries lactose into the cell.
  • No lactose: the cell makes almost none of these enzymes. Lactose added: they appear within minutes.
  • Making them only when lactose is present saves energy and amino acids.
  • These genes are inducible: expressed only when an inducer is present.
Mission 1

The lac operon

  • Operon: a group of genes transcribed together, under the control of one promoter and one operator.
  • Promoter: where RNA polymerase binds.
  • Operator: where a repressor protein binds.
  • Structural genes: lacZ (β-galactosidase) and lacY (lactose permease).
  • A separate regulatory gene, lacI, codes for the repressor. It is transcribed all the time.
  • The repressor is a negative regulatory molecule: it binds DNA and blocks transcription.
Mission 1

Lactose lifts the block

  • (1) No lactose: the repressor binds the operator. RNA polymerase cannot transcribe the genes.
  • (2) Lactose present: lactose (as allolactose) binds the repressor. Its shape changes; it falls off the operator.
  • (3) RNA polymerase binds the promoter and transcribes the genes → enzymes made → lactose used.
  • Lactose used up → the repressor binds the operator again → genes off.
  • Say: "lactose binds the repressor" — not: "lactose binds the DNA".

?Escherichia coli bacteria growing in a medium without lactose are transferred to a medium containing lactose. Within minutes they begin to synthesize beta-galactosidase, the enzyme that hydrolyzes lactose. What causes this change in gene expression?

Mission 1

Inducible and repressible operons

  • Inducible operon (like lac): usually off. An inducer binds the repressor and inactivates it → genes on. Typical of genes for breaking down a nutrient.
  • Repressible operon (like trp): usually on. When the end product (e.g. tryptophan) builds up, it binds the repressor and activates it → repressor binds operator → genes off. Typical of genes for making a product.
  • The end product here is a corepressor.
  • In both, the off switch is a repressor bound to DNA blocking transcription.
  • Some operons are also turned up by an activator protein that helps RNA polymerase bind: positive regulation.
Quick check
?In a hypothetical species of bacterium, the enzymes that make the amino acid histidine are encoded by one operon. A student hypothesizes that this operon is a repressible operon. The student will grow the bacteria in medium with and without added histidine and measure the operon's mRNA. If the hypothesis is correct, which result is predicted?
Mission check

?A mutant strain of E. coli makes a lac repressor that still binds the operator normally, but can no longer bind lactose. What happens when these bacteria are given lactose?

The switch is the repressor's shape. A repressor that cannot bind lactose never lets go, so the genes stay locked off.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Your nerve cells and muscle cells carry the same genes. So why are they so different?

Make a guess: Your nerve cells and muscle cells carry the same genes. So why are they so different?

Mission 2

One cell becomes hundreds of cell types

  • You began as one cell: a zygote.
  • Mitosis made many genetically identical, unspecialized cells.
  • Differentiation: each cell becomes specialized, with a structure suited to one function.
  • A nerve cell and a muscle cell keep the same genes.
  • They differ in which genes are switched on.
  • Visible differentiation comes from expressing genes for tissue-specific proteins.
  • Say: "different genes are expressed" — not: "different genes are kept".
Mission 2

From switched-on gene to specialized cell

  • Switched-on gene → transcribed to active mRNA → translated to protein.
  • Proteins build the cell's structure and run its processes.
  • Muscle cell: genes for actin and myosin on. Red cell precursor: globin genes (for hemoglobin) on.
  • Genes not needed stay in the nucleus, silent.
  • A cell's phenotype depends on which genes are expressed and at what level.
Quick check
?A person's skin cells and liver cells have very different structures and functions. Which statement best explains how these differences arise?
Mission 2

What tells a cell which genes to switch on?

  • Identical cells must receive different signals.
  • A signal induces a transcription factor. That factor switches on the next gene, which may code for another transcription factor.
  • Result: sequential gene expression. Remove one step and every later step stops.
  • The amount of a gene product also matters: more enzyme can give more product, until another factor limits it.
Quick check

Protein A is a transcription factor that activates gene B, and protein B is a transcription factor that activates gene C. In a mutant fly, gene B is deleted. Which proteins are predicted to be made in the mutant's cells that normally make all three proteins?

Mission check

?In a hypothetical species of plant, leaf cells make large amounts of a protein that binds chlorophyll, but the root cells of the same plant make none of it. Which statement best explains this difference?

Same genes in every cell; different genes switched on. That is what makes a leaf cell, a root cell, a nerve cell or a muscle cell.

Mission 3

Mission 3: An early embryo is a ball of identical cells. How does it know where to build a head and a tail?

Make a guess: An early embryo is a ball of identical cells. How does it know where to build a head and a tail?

Mission 3

The body plan problem

  • A zygote divides by mitosis into many unspecialized, genetically identical cells.
  • They must become different in the right places: head at one end, tail at the other.
  • Body plan: the arrangement of body parts along the body's axes, such as head to tail.
  • The genes that set up body plans code for transcription factors. Each one switches on many other genes.
Mission 3

Gradients tell cells where they are

  • (1) In a fruit fly egg, the mother places mRNA for the protein Bicoid at the head end.
  • (2) After fertilization, Bicoid protein spreads out: a concentration gradient, high at the head end, low toward the tail.
  • At this stage the fly embryo is one large cell with many nuclei, so the protein can spread between them.
  • (3) Bicoid is a transcription factor. Different genes switch on above different threshold concentrations, so each region expresses its own set.
  • Those genes code for more transcription factors, which switch on the next set: sequential gene expression.
  • Every nucleus keeps all its genes; the gradient changes which are expressed.
  • Say: "cells respond to the concentration they receive" — not: "the signal is simply on or off".
Quick check
?Female fruit flies with extra copies of the bicoid gene lay eggs containing more Bicoid mRNA than normal at the head end. What is predicted for their embryos?
Mission 3

Homeobox genes: an ancient toolkit

  • Homeobox: a 180-base-pair sequence found in some genes.
  • It codes for a 60-amino-acid homeodomain, which binds DNA. So homeobox genes code for transcription factors.
  • Hox genes: a group of homeobox genes in animals that control the body plan along the head-to-tail axis.
  • HOX genes are one example of transcription factors that switch on genes in sequence during development.
Mission 3

Hox genes in order, head to tail

  • Hox genes lie in clusters on chromosomes.
  • Their order along the chromosome matches the order of the body regions they control, head to tail.
  • Each Hox gene switches on the genes that build its region, e.g. legs on a thoracic segment.
  • Mutations can turn one body part into another: Antennapedia flies grow legs where antennae should be; Ultrabithorax mutant flies grow a second pair of wings.
Quick check
?Homeobox sequences from a fruit fly, a mouse and a yeast are clearly similar, although these species last shared an ancestor hundreds of millions of years ago. What is the best explanation?
Mission check

?In a chick wing bud, cells at the rear edge release a signaling protein that spreads toward the front edge. Each digit has its own shape, and the digits lie in a set order from rear to front. When extra signaling cells are grafted onto the front edge, the wing grows an extra set of digits in mirror image. What is the best explanation?

Position is read as concentration. Move the source and the body plan moves with it.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Change one letter in a gene: does the protein always change?

Make a guess: Change one letter in a gene: does the protein always change?

Mission 4

What is a gene mutation?

  • A gene mutation is a change in the base sequence of DNA.
  • Many arise as errors during DNA replication that are not corrected.
  • The changed DNA is transcribed into changed mRNA. That may change the polypeptide.
  • Larger changes, to whole chromosomes, are chromosome mutations (see Mission 6).
Mission 4

Three ways to change a sequence

  • (1) Substitution: one base replaced by another. Same length.
  • (2) Deletion: a base removed. One shorter.
  • (3) Insertion: a base added. One longer.
  • A single-base substitution is a point mutation.
  • Compare the sequences base by base, and count their lengths.
Quick check
?Part of the base sequence of a gene is GAT TCA GGC ATT before a mutation and GAT TCG GCA TT after it. Which type of gene mutation has occurred?
Mission 4

A substitution: three possible outcomes

  • A substitution changes one codon only. The reading frame is untouched.
  • Outcome 1: the new codon codes for the same amino acid. No change. The code is degenerate.
  • Outcome 2: the new codon codes for a different amino acid. One amino acid changes.
  • Outcome 3: the new codon is a stop codon. The polypeptide is cut short.
  • Names: outcome 1 is a silent mutation; outcome 3 is a nonsense mutation. The code is redundant: many amino acids have more than one codon.
Mission 4

Worked example: four mutations, one gene

  • Code table: AUG Met · GCA, GCU Ala · UGG Trp · AAG Lys · GAG Glu · CUU Leu · CAU His · GGA Gly · AGC Ser · UGA stop.
  • Normal mRNA: AUG GCA UGG AAG CUU → Met–Ala–Trp–Lys–Leu.
  • (a) GCA → GCU: Met–Ala–Trp–Lys–Leu. No change.
  • (b) AAG → GAG: Met–Ala–Trp–Glu–Leu. One amino acid changed.
  • (c) UGG → UGA: Met–Ala–stop. Two amino acids only.
  • (d) Delete the G of codon 2: AUG CAU GGA AGC UU… → Met–His–Gly–Ser… Every codon after the deletion changes.
Quick check
?A student finds that a hypothetical plant carries a substitution of one nucleotide in the coding region of gene P and claims that the substitution is a silent mutation. Which observation, if made, would best support the student's claim?
Mission 4

Deletions and insertions: the frame shifts

  • The ribosome reads in threes from the start codon. There are no gaps or commas.
  • Delete one base and every later base moves up one place: a frameshift.
  • Inserting one base does the same.
  • Every downstream codon is regrouped. Codons before the mutation are unchanged.
  • A new stop codon usually appears early. The polypeptide almost always stops working.
  • Three bases added or removed: the frame is kept. One amino acid is gained or lost (a neighbor may change too).
  • Say: "frameshift: all downstream triplets change" — not: "one base, one amino acid".
Quick check
?In a hypothetical bacterium, strain A has a deletion of one nucleotide in codon 10 of a gene that has 300 codons. Strain B has a deletion of the three adjacent nucleotides that make up codon 10 of the same gene. Which prediction about the polypeptides translated from the two genes is best supported?
Mission 4

Harmful, neutral or beneficial?

  • Harmful: the protein no longer works, or works worse.
  • Neutral: no detectable effect on the organism. Silent mutations are usually neutral.
  • Beneficial: the new protein gives an advantage.
  • The environment decides: a mutation that lets a bacterium survive an antibiotic helps only where that antibiotic is present.
  • AP words: beneficial, detrimental, neutral. A mutation can change the type or the amount of protein made.
  • Say: "silent: same amino acid sequence" — not: "silent: no effect on the phenotype". That is neutral.
Mission check

?A gene codes for a polypeptide of 400 amino acids. A single base substitution changes the fifth codon of its mRNA from UAC to UAA. In the code table, UAC codes for tyrosine and UAA is a stop codon. What is the most likely effect?

One letter can do nothing, change one amino acid, or stop the whole chain. It depends on which codon it creates.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Can a living thing ever mutate because it needs to?

Make a guess: Can a living thing ever mutate because it needs to?

Mission 5

Copying is almost perfect

  • Every cell division copies billions of bases.
  • DNA polymerase sometimes pairs the wrong base. Proofreading and repair fix almost all errors.
  • The few that escape become permanent: spontaneous mutations.
  • Errors in DNA repair add more.
  • So mutations happen even with no harmful agent present. Each person carries dozens of new mutations neither parent had.
Mission 5

Mutagens raise the rate

  • A mutagen increases the rate of mutation.
  • Chemical mutagens: e.g. nitrous acid (alters bases so they mispair); benzo[a]pyrene in tobacco smoke (binds to bases).
  • Ultraviolet light: joins neighboring thymines, distorting the DNA.
  • Ionizing radiation (X-rays, gamma rays): breaks DNA strands and damages bases.
  • Not mutagens: visible light, microwaves, radio waves. Too little energy to change DNA.
  • AP wording: radiation and reactive chemicals.
Quick check
?Cultures of cells were exposed separately to (i) ultraviolet light, (ii) visible light and (iii) nitrous acid, a chemical that alters bases in DNA. Which treatments are expected to raise the mutation rate above the background rate?
Mission 5

Random, not directed

  • A mutation can happen anywhere in the genome.
  • No natural mechanism makes a chosen change to a chosen base to produce a useful trait.
  • A mutagen raises the rate. It does not pick the gene.
  • The environment selects among variants that already exist. It does not create them.
  • Say: "a random mutation that happened to give resistance" — not: "the bacteria mutated to become resistant".
Mission 5

Read the data: chance or need?

  • 1943: Luria and Delbrück asked whether bacteria become resistant to a virus because they meet it.
  • Set-up: grow 20 separate small cultures of E. coli from a few cells each; also take 10 samples from one big culture. Spread each on plates with virus. Count resistant colonies.
  • Real data (Luria and Delbrück, 1943), resistant colonies per plate:

  • If the virus caused resistance, every plate would give a similar count.
  • Separate cultures vary wildly. A mutation early in growth leaves many resistant descendants; a late one, few; often none.

Mission 5

The only source of new alleles

  • A new allele can arise only by mutation.
  • Meiosis and fertilization reshuffle existing alleles into new combinations. They make no new alleles.
  • Most mutations are harmful or neutral to the individual. A few are beneficial.
  • Even bacteria that only divide gain variation: each round of copying can add mutations.
Quick check
?Why is gene mutation described as the original source of all genetic variation?
Mission check

?When a new insecticide was first used on a crop, it killed nearly all individuals of a hypothetical pest insect. After several years of use, most of the pest population was resistant. Which statement best explains how resistance became common?

Mutation supplies variation by chance; the environment only chooses among what is already there.

Mission 6

Mission 6: One extra chromosome can change a whole body. So how do some plants thrive with doubled sets?

Make a guess: One extra chromosome can change a whole body. So how do some plants thrive with doubled sets?

Mission 6

Gene or chromosome mutation?

  • Gene mutation: a change in the bases of one gene (see Mission 4).
  • Chromosome mutation: a change in the number or structure of whole chromosomes.
  • One chromosome carries hundreds of genes. Gain or lose one, and hundreds of genes change dose at once.
  • Development depends on genes being in the right balance.
  • A whole extra set keeps every gene in proportion. Many plants live well with one.
Mission 6

Non-disjunction in meiosis

  • Normal meiosis: each pair of homologous chromosomes separates (meiosis I); then each pair of chromatids separates (meiosis II).
  • Non-disjunction: a pair fails to separate. Both go to the same pole.
  • (1) In meiosis I: all four gametes are abnormal. Two have one extra chromosome (n + 1); two have one missing (n − 1).
  • (2) In meiosis II: two gametes are normal, one is n + 1, one is n − 1.
  • Fertilization by a normal gamete gives a zygote with 2n + 1 or 2n − 1. Every cell inherits it by mitosis.
  • The CED spells it nondisjunction.
Mission 6

One extra, or a whole extra set?

  • Aneuploidy: one chromosome extra or missing. Trisomy (2n + 1), monosomy (2n − 1).
  • Polyploidy: whole extra sets. Triploid (3n), tetraploid (4n).
  • Aneuploidy unbalances gene doses. In animals it usually causes serious developmental effects or death.
  • Polyploidy keeps every gene in the same ratio. Many plants tolerate it well; many crops are polyploid.
  • Say: "trisomy is aneuploidy" — not: "triploidy is aneuploidy". Triploidy is a whole extra set: polyploidy.
  • Your course framework words this as "triploidy (aneuploidy)". Triploidy is polyploidy.
Mission 6

Worked example: counting chromosomes

  • Human body cells: 2n = 46. Normal gametes: n = 23.
  • Non-disjunction of one pair gives gametes with 24 or 22.
  • Zygote: 24 + 23 = 47 (one chromosome present three times).
  • Zygote: 22 + 23 = 45 (one chromosome present once).
  • Error in meiosis I: gametes 24, 24, 22, 22. Error in meiosis II: 24, 22, 23, 23.
Quick check
?In an adult of a hypothetical mammal, nondisjunction occurs in two cells: during mitosis in a skin cell, and during meiosis in a cell in the testes that gives rise to sperm. Which prediction about the effects of these two errors is best supported?
Mission 6

Errors in mitosis, broken chromosomes

  • Non-disjunction in mitosis affects only the descendants of that one cell. A body-cell error is not inherited.
  • Structure can change too, when chromosomes break and rejoin wrongly:
  • Deletion (segment lost) · duplication (segment repeated) · inversion (segment reversed) · translocation (segment moved to another chromosome).
  • Deletions and duplications change gene dose. Breaks can disrupt genes at the break points.
  • These changes can cause genetic disorders, often with developmental effects.
Quick check
?In a hypothetical animal, a segment carrying several genes breaks off one copy of chromosome 4 and is lost, while the rest of that chromosome remains in the cell. Which statement best explains why this change can lead to a genetic disorder?
Mission check

?A species has 2n = 8. In one cell undergoing meiosis, one pair of homologous chromosomes fails to separate in meiosis I; meiosis II is normal. How many chromosomes are in each of the four gametes?

A normal gamete (4) fertilizing these gives 9 or 7: one chromosome too many or too few, which unbalances hundreds of genes. A doubled set keeps them balanced.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Lac operon: with no lactose, the repressor sits on the operator and blocks transcription; lactose binds the repressor, it lets go, and the genes for lactose use are transcribed.
  • Inducible operons are switched on by an inducer; repressible operons are switched off when an end product activates the repressor.

Mission 2

  • Differentiation: cells with the same genes become specialized by expressing different genes.
  • Induced transcription factors give sequential expression of tissue-specific genes.

Mission 3

  • Gradients of transcription factors in the early embryo switch on different genes at different concentrations, so cells in different places differentiate differently.
  • Homeobox genes code for DNA-binding transcription factors; Hox genes, in head-to-tail order, control the body plan.

Mission 4

  • Substitutions change one codon: same amino acid (degenerate code), one different amino acid, or an early stop. Deletions and insertions not in multiples of three shift the frame and change every downstream codon.

Mission 5

  • Mutations arise spontaneously from copying and repair errors, at random: the environment selects among them but does not direct them.
  • Mutagens such as UV, ionizing radiation and some chemicals raise the rate but never aim it.
  • Mutation is the only source of new alleles; selection then changes their frequency.

Mission 6

  • Non-disjunction in meiosis gives gametes with n + 1 or n − 1; fertilization then gives 2n + 1 or 2n − 1 in every cell.
  • Aneuploidy unbalances gene dose; polyploidy adds whole sets and can create a new plant species in one generation.
