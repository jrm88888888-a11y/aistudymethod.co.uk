Mission 1

Mission 1: Is the gap in how long people live between rich and poor countries closing, or getting wider?

Make a guess: Is the gap in how long people live between rich and poor countries closing, or getting wider?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 7.1.1 gender and health as comparative measures of social development
  • 7.1.1 the concept of a continuum of social development
  • 7.1.1 social development evidence and the dynamic development gap (e.g. changing birth rate, life expectancy)
Mission 1

Social development

  • Social development means improvements in people's lives: health, education, equality and well-being, not just income.
  • To compare countries we use measures (indicators) with the same definition everywhere.
  • Two key groups: health measures and gender measures.
Mission 1

Health measures

  • Life expectancy at birth: average years a newborn can expect to live. 2023: UK 81.3, India 72.0, Rwanda 67.8.
  • Maternal mortality ratio: mothers' deaths per 100,000 live births. 2020: UK 10, India 103, Rwanda 259.
  • Birth rate: births per 1,000 people per year. 2023: UK 10.0, India 16.1, Rwanda 28.3.
Mission 1

Gender measures

  • Gender measures compare the lives of women and men.
  • The UN's Gender Inequality Index (GII) combines reproductive health, empowerment and the labour market.
  • It runs from 0 (women and men fare equally) to 1 (most unequal).
  • 2023: UK 0.083, Rwanda 0.394, India 0.403, world 0.455.
Quick check
?Rwanda's life expectancy is lower than India's, but its Gender Inequality Index is slightly better. What does this show?
Mission 1

A continuum of development

  • Countries do not fall into two boxes, "developed" and "developing".
  • They sit along a sliding scale: a continuum.
  • On life expectancy (2023): Rwanda 67.8, India 72.0, world 73.2, UK 81.3.
  • Countries can move along the continuum, and their order can change from one measure to another.
Quick check
?Which statement best describes the idea of a continuum of social development?
Mission 1

The gap is dynamic

  • The development gap is the difference in development between richer and poorer countries.
  • It is dynamic: it changes over time.
  • Life expectancy, UK vs India: 1960, 71.0 vs 45.6 years; 2023, 81.3 vs 72.0 years.
  • India gained much faster, so the gap shrank.
Mission 1

Birth rates are falling

  • Births per 1,000 people, 1960 → 2023:
  • UK 17.3 → 10.0 · India 42.9 → 16.1 · Rwanda 52.9 → 28.3.
  • Falling birth rates follow better healthcare, family planning and girls' education.
  • The gap in birth rates between the UK and India has narrowed sharply.
Mission 1

Gaps can widen too

  • Progress is not smooth. In 1994, the year of the genocide in Rwanda, its life expectancy fell to about 12 years.
  • This figure is worked out from that year's death rates. It does not mean people lived only 12 years.
  • By 2023 it had recovered to 67.8 years.
  • So the gap can widen suddenly through conflict, disease or disaster, then narrow again.
Quick check
?Use the figures earlier in this mission. By how many years did the life expectancy gap between the UK and India shrink from 1960 to 2023?
Mission check

?Life expectancy in Bangladesh rose from 44.0 years in 1960 to 74.7 in 2023. In the UK it rose from 71.0 to 81.3. What happened to the gap?

For life expectancy, the gap between richer and poorer countries has mostly narrowed since 1960. But it is dynamic: it can widen suddenly, as in Rwanda in 1994.

Exam move: quote two dates and the change ("the gap fell from 25.4 to 9.3 years"), then explain why.

Mission 2

Mission 2: At today's rate, Niger's population would double in about 21 years. Japan's is shrinking. Why?

Make a guess: At today's rate, Niger's population would double in about 21 years. Japan's is shrinking. Why?

Mission 2

Births, deaths and natural change

  • Crude birth rate (CBR): births per 1,000 people per year.
  • Crude death rate (CDR): deaths per 1,000 people per year.
  • Natural increase: births exceed deaths. Natural decrease: deaths exceed births.
  • Natural increase rate (% a year) = (CBR − CDR) ÷ 10.

Mission 2

Fertility and replacement

  • Total fertility rate (TFR): the average number of children a woman would have at today's rates.
  • Replacement level is about 2.1: "the level required for a population to maintain a constant size in the long run without migration".
  • World TFR, 2024: 2.25. Niger 5.94; Japan 1.22.
  • At 3.27% a year, Niger doubles in about 70 ÷ 3.27 ≈ 21 years (the "rule of 70").
Mission 2

The demographic transition model (DTM)

  • Stage 1: high birth and death rates; little growth.
  • Stage 2: death rates fall (food, clean water, vaccination); births stay high; rapid growth.
  • Stage 3: birth rates fall (fewer child deaths, education, contraception, cities); growth slows.
  • Stage 4: low birth and death rates; little growth.
  • Stage 5: births below deaths; the population falls.
Mission 2

The DTM and development

  • Stage tends to rise with development: low-income countries in stages 2–3, high-income countries in stages 4–5.
  • Niger (low income) fits late stage 2: deaths low, births still very high.
  • Japan (high income) fits stage 5: deaths (12.4) exceed births (6.1).
Quick check
?A country has a birth rate of 28 per 1,000, a death rate of 6, and its birth rate has fallen for 20 years. Which stage fits best?

Why rates change: South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa

  • Social: girls' schooling, clean water, vaccination; fewer child deaths mean fewer births are needed.
  • Economic: city jobs and the cost of raising children reduce family size; poverty keeps it high.
  • Political: government family-planning and health programmes; conflict slows progress.
Mission 2

Infant and maternal mortality

  • Infant mortality rate: deaths under age 1 per 1,000 live births. 2024: Niger 63.3, Japan 1.7.
  • Maternal mortality ratio: mothers' deaths per 100,000 live births. 2023: Niger 350, Japan 3.
  • A woman giving birth in Niger is about 117 times as likely to die as one in Japan (350 ÷ 3, calculated).
  • Where many babies die, families tend to have more children.
Mission 2

Population pyramids

  • Bars show % of the population in each 5-year age group; males left, females right.
  • Niger: wide base. 46.6% under 15; 2.6% aged 65+. Youth dependent.
  • Japan: narrow base, wide top. 11.4% under 15; 29.8% aged 65+. Aged dependent.
  • Bangladesh (South Asia): 28.0% under 15; 6.5% aged 65+. Its base is narrowing as births fall.
Quick check
?A pyramid has a narrow base and wide bars above age 65. What does it show?
Mission 2

Putting it together

  • Niger: many births, a young population, few deaths → rapid natural increase.
  • Japan: few births, many older people, more deaths → natural decrease.
  • Age structure matters: a young population has few deaths even where health care is weaker.
Mission check

?Bangladesh: birth rate 48.2 and death rate 17.1 in 1972; 20.0 and 5.1 in 2024. Why has its growth slowed?

(48.2 − 17.1) ÷ 10 = 3.11% in 1972; (20.0 − 5.1) ÷ 10 = 1.49% in 2024. The gap between births and deaths, shaped by fertility and age structure, decides growth.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why do millions of children go to work instead of school, and what gets them back into class?

Make a guess: Why do millions of children go to work instead of school, and what gets them back into class?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 7.2.1 reasons for child labour
  • 7.2.1 challenges for primary education, especially of girls, in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia
  • 7.2.1 tackling these issues locally in one SSA/Asian country and globally (e.g. UN targets)
Mission 3

What counts as child labour?

  • Not every job a child does is child labour. Helping at home for a short time is not.
  • Child labour: work that is "hazardous to a child's health and development", demands too many hours, or is done by children who are too young.
  • It usually stops children going to school, playing and resting.
Mission 3

How many, and where?

  • In 2024, nearly 138 million children were in child labour: 78 million boys and 59 million girls.
  • About 54 million were in hazardous work likely to harm their health or safety.
  • Sub-Saharan Africa: 87 million, "nearly two thirds of the global total"; the highest rate, 22% of children.
  • Central and Southern Asia is second, with 17 million.
Quick check
?Sub-Saharan Africa had 87 million of the world's 138 million children in child labour. What fraction is that?
Mission 3

Why do children work?

  • Poverty: families need the extra food or income a child brings.
  • Family farms: 61% of child labour is in agriculture, mostly "family subsistence and on smallholder farms".
  • Conflict and crisis: child labour is "strongly associated with conflict, fragility, poverty and low human development".
  • No school nearby, or school costs (fees, uniforms, books) a family cannot pay.
  • But "correlation does not imply causation": these factors overlap, so no single one explains it.
Mission 3

Hidden work: household chores

  • The usual count leaves out chores in a child's own home: caring for siblings, cooking, cleaning, carrying water.
  • Counting chores of 21 hours or more a week, child labour among 5–14-year-olds rises from 8% to 11%.
  • Then girls' rate "slightly exceeds" boys': girls "bear a disproportionate burden of household chores".
Quick check
?Why might official counts underestimate girls' child labour?
Mission 3

Work versus school

  • Nearly one third of children of compulsory school age in child labour are missing from class, against 8% of children who do not work.
  • For children in hazardous work, nearly half are out of school.
  • Even those who attend often fall behind: in 34 countries, children in child labour were over 30% less likely to show basic reading and maths skills.
Mission 3

Barriers to primary education, especially for girls

  • 64 million children of primary school age "remain out of school"; 53% of them are girls.
  • In Africa and South Asia, girls have "lower attendance rates than boys".
  • Poverty: 23% of children from the poorest families are out of school, against 3% from the richest. "Poor families often favour boys when investing in education".
  • Child marriage and gender-based violence.
  • Distance, and schools that do not meet girls' "safety, hygiene or sanitation needs" (e.g. no separate toilets).
  • Conflict: girls in conflict-affected countries are "more than twice as likely to be out of school".
Quick check
?Which barrier affects girls more than boys?
Mission 3

Local scale: Burkina Faso's "girl-friendly" schools

  • Burkina Faso, West Africa: the BRIGHT programme built high-quality "girl-friendly" primary schools in rural villages.
  • Researchers collected data in almost 300 rural villages; the evaluation was funded by the US Millennium Challenge Corporation.
  • They compared villages just above and just below the cut-off score for getting a school, so the groups were alike.
Mission 3

Did it work?

  • After 2.5 years, enrolment rose by 19 percentage points and test scores rose.
  • Girls' enrolment rose by 5 percentage points more than boys'.
  • The schools' special girl-friendly features explain the whole extra effect for girls.
  • Six years on, enrolment was still 15.5 points higher, and pupils progressed further through school.
Mission 3

Global scale: UN targets

  • SDG Target 8.7 (2015): end child labour by 2025. The deadline has passed; nearly 138 million children remain in child labour.
  • Meeting it even by 2030 would need progress 11 times faster than in 2020–2024.
  • SDG 4.1: "By 2030, ensure that all girls and boys complete free, equitable and quality primary and secondary education".
  • SDG 4.5: "eliminate gender disparities in education".
  • Targets set goals and focus aid and data; they do not pay for schools themselves.
Quick check
?SDG Target 8.7 aimed to end child labour by 2025. In 2024, nearly 138 million children were still in child labour, though numbers have fallen by over 100 million since 2000. What is the best judgement about the target?
Mission check

?In a rural district of South Asia, many girls miss primary school to look after younger siblings and fetch water. The nearest school is 6 km away and has no toilets. Which local plan is most likely to raise girls' attendance?

Children work mainly because of poverty, family farms and crisis; girls also carry heavy chores. Getting them into class means removing barriers locally (near, safe, girl-friendly schools, as in Burkina Faso) while global targets such as SDG 8.7 and 4.1 set the goal.

Mission 4: Within a year of August 2017, nearly 725,000 people had fled from Myanmar into Bangladesh. Why do so many people flee at once, and what happens where they arrive?

Make a guess: Within a year of August 2017, nearly 725,000 people had fled from Myanmar into Bangladesh. Why do so many people flee at once, and what happens where they arrive?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 7.2.1 reasons for international refugee movement and asylum seekers from sub-Saharan Africa and Asia
  • 7.2.1 how national governments and international agreements tackle refugee movement (e.g. the Mediterranean)
Mission 4

Who is who

  • Voluntary migration: people choose to move, usually for work or family.
  • Forced migration: people are compelled to move by conflict, persecution or disaster.
  • Refugee: outside their own country "owing to well-founded fear of being persecuted" for race, religion, nationality, social group or political opinion (1951 Convention).
  • Asylum seeker: has asked for protection; the claim is not yet decided.
  • Internally displaced person (IDP): forced to flee but has "not crossed an internationally recognized State border".
Quick check
?A family flees fighting and crosses into a neighbouring country, where they ask for protection. Their claim has not yet been decided. What are they?
Mission 4

The Rohingya: long-term causes

  • Origin: northern Rakhine State, Myanmar. Destination: Cox's Bazar, Bangladesh.
  • A UN fact-finding mission found: "Most Rohingya have become de facto stateless, arbitrarily deprived of nationality".
  • Their travel "has long been restricted", affecting rights "to food, health and education".
  • These conditions left them vulnerable before 2017.
Mission 4

The trigger

  • 25 August 2017: an armed group (ARSA) attacked a military base and "up to 30 security force outposts".
  • Security forces responded with what the authorities called "clearance operations".
  • The UN mission found the response "immediate, brutal and grossly disproportionate".
  • By mid-August 2018, nearly 725,000 Rohingya had fled to Bangladesh.
  • Push: persecution and violence. Pull: safety across the nearest border.
Mission 4

Challenges for the refugees

  • 31 October 2026: 1,208,018 Rohingya refugees in Bangladesh.
  • Most live in "33 highly congested camps in Cox's Bazar District".
  • They depend on aid: the 2025 response plan required US$455.6 million.
  • Safe from violence, but crowded camps, dependence on aid and no clear way home.
Mission 4

Challenges for Bangladesh, the destination

  • Scale and time: about 1.2 million refugees at the end of 2025; the UN found returns "not possible" in 2018.
  • Cost and land: aid must be raised every year, and camps are so crowded that "some 35,000 refugees relocated to Bhasan Char", an island.
  • Host communities: the 2025–26 response plan covers the needs of "Rohingya refugees and host communities".
  • Host area (Cox's Bazar), UNDP 2018:
    • about 4,818 acres of forest reserve destroyed (Forest Department estimate)
    • wages for unskilled work in Teknaf and Ukhiya depressed by about 20%, because Rohingya work for less
    • so poverty rose by 2.73 (Teknaf) and 2.63 (Ukhiya) percentage points.
  • Law: Bangladesh is not a party to the 1951 Refugee Convention (146 states are).
  • National government response: Bangladesh hosts the refugees in camps, with UN agencies, under a joint response plan.
Quick check
?Which is a challenge for Bangladesh, the destination country, rather than for the refugees themselves?
Mission 4

Refugees from sub-Saharan Africa: Sudan

  • As "the conflict in Sudan enters its fourth year", 9.1 million people remained displaced inside Sudan at the end of 2025.
  • Refugees from Sudan grew to 2.8 million, up 35% in a year.
  • More than 7 in 10 refugees come from just six countries: Afghanistan, South Sudan, Sudan, Syria, Ukraine and Venezuela.
  • Reasons: armed conflict, persecution, and the collapse of services and food supplies.
Mission 4

Agreements and governments

  • 1951 Convention: no state may return a refugee to where "his life or freedom would be threatened" (non-refoulement, Article 33).
  • EU–Turkey Statement (18 March 2016): "All new irregular migrants crossing from Turkey into Greek islands as from 20 March 2016 will be returned to Turkey"; for each Syrian returned, another Syrian would be resettled in the EU; an extra €3 billion for refugees in Turkey.
  • EU Pact on Migration and Asylum (applies from June 2026): screening at the border, faster border procedures, and solidarity between EU states (relocation, money or support).
  • Supporters say such deals reduce dangerous sea crossings; critics question whether protection is kept. Judge by evidence.
Quick check
?What was the main aim of the 2016 EU–Turkey Statement?
Mission check

?By the end of 2025, 2.8 million people had fled Sudan to other countries, and 9.1 million were displaced inside Sudan. Which statement is correct?

The Rohingya fled persecution and violence (a political push) to the nearest safety. Bangladesh now hosts 1,208,018 refugees (31 Oct 2026), mostly in crowded camps funded by aid: a long-term challenge for refugees and host alike.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Why do so many babies in sub-Saharan Africa die before their first birthday, when most causes are preventable?

Make a guess: Why do so many babies in sub-Saharan Africa die before their first birthday, when most causes are preventable?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 7.2.2 reasons for high rates of infant mortality
  • 7.2.2 how health progress is measured and what progress is being made
Mission 5

Measuring child health

  • Infant mortality rate (IMR): deaths of babies before their first birthday, per 1,000 live births.
  • Under-five mortality rate: deaths before age five, per 1,000 live births. Neonatal: deaths in the first 28 days.
  • IMR is a key development indicator: it reflects health care, clean water, food and mothers' education all at once.
Mission 5

The global pattern

  • IMR, 2024: sub-Saharan Africa 47.1 · world 27.7 · UK 4.1 · Nigeria 69.8.
  • In 2024, 2.8 million children under five died in sub-Saharan Africa: 58% of the world's under-five deaths.
  • A child born there faced an under-five mortality rate of 71.6 per 1,000, "nearly 14 times higher than in Europe and Northern America".
Quick check
?Using the figures earlier in this mission, how many times higher was sub-Saharan Africa's IMR than the UK's in 2024?
Mission 5

What do babies die of?

  • Leading causes of under-five deaths: preterm birth complications, birth asphyxia or trauma, pneumonia, diarrhoea and malaria, "all of which are largely preventable or treatable".
  • Newborns die mostly of premature birth, birth complications, infections and birth defects. Sub-Saharan Africa had the world's highest newborn death rate in 2022: 27 per 1,000 live births.
  • Children under five made up about 75% of malaria deaths in the WHO African Region in 2024.
Mission 5

Why are these causes not prevented?

  • No trained help at birth: in 2019, only 64% of births in sub-Saharan Africa were attended by skilled health staff.
  • Poverty and place: deaths are higher in the poorest households, and about 1.5 times higher in rural than urban areas.
  • Mothers: higher risk for mothers with limited education, very young mothers, and short gaps between births.
  • Conflict: in fragile and conflict-affected countries, under-five mortality was nearly three times higher in 2024.
  • Water, sanitation and food: WHO lists better nutrition, water and sanitation among the changes needed.
Quick check
?Which is an underlying reason for high infant mortality, rather than a medical cause of death?
Mission 5

Progress in sub-Saharan Africa

  • IMR in sub-Saharan Africa: 102.1 (1990) → 88.9 (2000) → 60.6 (2010) → 47.1 (2024).
  • That is a fall of about 54% since 1990.
  • But WHO warns that progress "slowed markedly since 2015".
Mission 5

Measuring progress against targets

  • Millennium Development Goal 4 (2000–2015): "Reduce by two thirds, between 1990 and 2015, the under-five mortality rate". The world fell from 90 to 43 per 1,000: more than half, short of two-thirds.
  • Sustainable Development Goal 3.2: by 2030, end preventable deaths of newborns and under-fives; under-five mortality "at least as low as 25 per 1,000 live births" in all countries.
  • Targets let us measure progress the same way everywhere, year by year.
Quick check
?Sub-Saharan Africa's under-five mortality rate was 71.6 per 1,000 in 2024. By what percentage must it fall to reach the SDG target of 25?
Mission 5

One country: Rwanda

  • Rwanda is a low-income country (LIC) in East Africa.
  • IMR: 86.9 (1990); 212.9 in 1994, the year of the genocide against the Tutsi; 29.4 in 2024, below the sub-Saharan average of 47.1.
  • Births attended by skilled health staff: 25.9% (1992) → 94.2% (2020).
  • In each of Rwanda's 14,837 villages, three community health workers are elected by villagers, then trained and equipped by the Ministry of Health.
Mission 5

Other ways to measure health progress

  • Life expectancy at birth: Rwanda 47.8 years (1990) → 67.8 (2023).
  • Inputs such as births with skilled attendants, and outcomes such as infant, under-five and maternal death rates.
  • Limits: many figures are modelled estimates, and a national average can hide gaps, such as rural babies' higher risk.
  • Exam move: to show progress, give the indicator, two dated values and the source; then say what the average may hide.
Mission check

?Two low-income countries have the same number of doctors per person. Country A's infant mortality rate is 25; Country B's is 60. Which difference most likely explains the gap?

Most infant deaths come from causes that are preventable or treatable. They are not prevented where births lack skilled help, families are poor and rural, and malaria and unsafe water are common. Rwanda's IMR fell to 29.4 by 2024 as births with skilled staff rose to over 90%.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Malaria killed an estimated 610,000 people in 2024. Why does it kill mostly in one continent, and mostly young children?

Make a guess: Malaria killed an estimated 610,000 people in 2024. Why does it kill mostly in one continent, and mostly young children?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 7.2.2 challenges created by malaria in sub-Saharan Africa
  • 7.2.2 how these issues are tackled at the local scale in one SSA country and at the global scale (e.g. UN targets)
Mission 6

A disease carried by mosquitoes

  • Malaria is caused by Plasmodium parasites. It spreads mainly through the bites of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes.
  • It is a vector-borne disease: a living carrier (the vector) passes it from person to person.
  • Symptoms (fever, headache, chills) usually start 10–15 days after the bite. Untreated, the most dangerous type can kill within 24 hours.
Mission 6

Where malaria kills

  • 2024: about 282 million cases and 610,000 deaths in 80 countries.
  • The WHO African Region had 95% of cases (265 million) and 95% of deaths (579,000).
  • Children under five made up about 75% of malaria deaths in the Region.
Mission 6

Physical factors

  • Temperature: a model tested with African data puts the best temperature for transmission at 25 °C, falling sharply above 28 °C.
  • Rainfall: pools of still water are where mosquitoes breed.
  • Altitude: cool highlands limit malaria. In warmer years it reached higher altitudes in Ethiopia and Colombia.
  • Much of tropical Africa is warm and has rainy seasons: ideal for the parasite and its mosquitoes.
Quick check
?Which place would most likely have the highest malaria transmission?
Mission 6

Why young children?

  • WHO notes symptoms may be milder for people who have had malaria before.
  • Young children have had few infections, so they have little protection.
  • Infants, children under five, pregnant women and girls, travellers and people with HIV or AIDS are at higher risk of severe malaria.
Mission 6

The challenges malaria creates

  • Poverty: unscreened homes let mosquitoes in; nets and treatment cost money; days off school and work cut family income.
  • Economy: countries with intensive malaria grew 1.3% less per person per year (1965–1990), a study found. That is a link, not proof of cause: poverty also makes malaria harder to fight.
  • Funding: US$3.9 billion was invested in 2024, less than half the US$9.3 billion target.
  • Resistance: partial resistance to artemisinin, the main drug, is confirmed in Eritrea, Rwanda, Uganda and Tanzania. An invasive mosquito from south Asia and the Arabian Peninsula, An. stephensi, has spread to 9 African countries and thrives in urban settings.
Quick check
?Why are young children in sub-Saharan Africa most likely to die of malaria?
Mission 6

Rwanda: a national campaign

  • Rwanda is a low-income country in East Africa.
  • From 2005 the government and its partners distributed insecticide-treated nets and made artemisinin-based drugs widely available.
  • People potentially protected by nets: nearly 0% (2005) → 38% (2006) → 76% (2010).
  • By 2010, compared with the 2000–2005 trend, confirmed malaria cases in district hospitals had fallen by 72%, and malaria admissions of children under five by 82%.
Quick check
?In Rwanda, hospital malaria cases rose again in 2009, when fewer people were covered by nets, then fell in 2010 after a new net campaign. What does this show?
Mission 6

Rwanda: health care in every village

  • In each of Rwanda's 14,837 villages, three community health workers are elected by villagers, then trained and equipped by the Ministry of Health.
  • They deliver prevention, diagnosis and treatment close to home, and link patients to clinics.
  • New challenge: partial drug resistance is now confirmed in Rwanda, so testing that drugs still work is vital.
Mission 6

The global scale

  • UN target, SDG 3.3: "By 2030, end the epidemics of AIDS, tuberculosis, malaria".
  • WHO: malaria tools prevented an estimated 170 million cases and 1 million deaths in 2024; vaccines (RTS,S, recommended 2021; R21, 2023) are now used routinely in 24 countries.
  • Global funders such as the Global Fund to Fight AIDS, Tuberculosis and Malaria work with Rwanda's Ministry of Health.
  • But funding is less than half of what is needed.
Quick check
?Which of these is action at the global scale?
Mission check

?Country P is a low-income tropical lowland with long rainy seasons. Country Q is a temperate high-income country. Both have Anopheles mosquitoes. Why does P have many malaria deaths, mostly young children, while Q has almost none?

In 2024, 95% of malaria deaths were in the WHO African Region, and about 75% of those were children under five. Warm, wet conditions suit the parasite and mosquito; poverty and funding gaps limit nets and treatment; young children have little protection.

Mission 7: New HIV infections have fallen 65% since their peak in 1994. Was that prevention or treatment, and why are some groups still left behind?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 7.2.2 challenges created by HIV in sub-Saharan Africa
  • 7.2.2 how these issues are tackled at the local scale in one SSA country and at the global scale (e.g. UN targets)
  • 7.2.2 top-down and bottom-up approaches to development
Mission 7

What HIV is

  • HIV is a virus that attacks the body's immune system. AIDS is the most advanced stage of infection.
  • It spreads through blood, breast milk, semen and vaginal fluids, and from mother to baby; not through kisses, hugs or sharing food.
  • There is no cure, but antiretroviral therapy (ART) controls the virus, so people can live long, healthy lives.
Mission 7

HIV in sub-Saharan Africa

  • At the end of 2025, 41.0 million people were living with HIV; 64% of them in the WHO African Region.
  • In 2025, 1.2 million people became infected and 570,000 died of HIV-related causes.
  • Share of adults aged 15–49 living with HIV in sub-Saharan Africa: 2.7% (1990) → 5.1% (2000) → 3.0% (2024).
Mission 7

Challenge 1: lives and life expectancy

  • HIV weakens the immune system, so people catch tuberculosis and other infections more easily.
  • Without treatment, it kills mostly working-age adults and parents.
  • Life expectancy at birth: Botswana 60.1 years (1990) → 52.3 (2000) → 69.2 (2023); South Africa 62.9 (1990) → 53.9 (2005) → 66.1 (2023).
  • Read the timing: life expectancy fell as HIV spread, and rose again as treatment spread. The graph alone does not prove the cause.
Quick check
?Botswana's life expectancy fell from 60.1 years in 1990 to 52.3 in 2000, then rose to 69.2 by 2023. Which explanation fits best?
Mission 7

Challenge 2: people, money and stigma

  • Women and girls: UNAIDS says they are "still disproportionately affected, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa"; worldwide, 4,000 young women and girls were infected every week in 2022.
  • Costs: ART is lifelong, so health services must pay for it, year after year.
  • Families and work: illness and early deaths remove earners and carers.
  • Stigma: people who fear being judged, or punished by law, may avoid testing and treatment.
Mission 7

Treatment is also prevention

  • A trial (HPTN 052, 2011) followed 1,763 couples in nine countries in which one partner had HIV.
  • Of 28 infections passed from the partner with HIV, only 1 was in the group that started ART early.
  • ART lowers the amount of virus in the body, so it cuts the spread too.
  • Prevention tools: condoms, testing, medicines that stop infection, and ART for pregnant women to protect babies.
Quick check
?What does the HPTN 052 trial show?
Mission 7

One country: Rwanda

  • Rwanda is a low-income country in East Africa.
  • By 2003, fewer than 150 people with HIV outside the capital, Kigali, were receiving ART. By mid-2007: 50,000. By 2012: 114,600.
  • Money from the US PEPFAR programme and the Global Fund paid for the start of treatment. The Ministry of Health began the scale-up in rural districts first.
  • By 2023, UNAIDS reported that Rwanda had reached the 95-95-95 targets: 95% of people with HIV know their status, 95% of those are on treatment and 95% of those have the virus suppressed.
Mission 7

Top-down and bottom-up

  • Top-down: decided and run by governments or large organisations, often big, national projects funded from outside.
  • Bottom-up: local people decide and run projects themselves, using their own knowledge.
  • Rwanda's ART programme is mainly top-down: a national plan, funded with global donors.
  • Its village health system has a bottom-up part: in each of 14,837 villages, villagers elect three community health workers, whom the Ministry trains.
Quick check
?Which is the best example of a bottom-up approach to HIV?
Mission 7

The global scale

  • UN target, SDG 3.3: "By 2030, end the epidemics of AIDS" (with tuberculosis and malaria).
  • 95-95-95: 95% of people with HIV know their status; 95% of those are on treatment; 95% of those have the virus suppressed.
  • World, 2025: 88% knew their status, 89% of those were on ART, and 95% of those were virally suppressed.
  • When governments do not act, people die: modelling estimates that more than 330,000 lives were lost in South Africa (2000–2005) because a treatment programme was not put in place in time.
Mission 7

Top-down or bottom-up: which works?

  • Top-down strengths: money and drugs on a national scale, fast. Weaknesses: can miss local needs; depends on donors.
  • Bottom-up strengths: trusted by local people; reaches homes. Weaknesses: small, and needs supplies from above.
  • Rwanda used both: national ART funding plus elected village health workers.
  • Exam move: for "evaluate", give a strength and a weakness of each approach, then judge with a named example.
Mission check

?In Country Z, free ART reaches most people with HIV and new infections fall. But infections stay high in one group whose members fear arrest if they visit clinics. What best explains both?

Both. ART treats people and cuts the spread (HPTN 052: 1 of 28 linked infections in the early-treatment group). New infections are down 65% since 1994. But groups facing stigma or criminal penalties are reached least, and government choices decide who gets treatment.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Health (life expectancy, maternal mortality, birth rate) and gender (GII) measures compare social development; countries sit on a continuum.
  • The development gap is dynamic: in life expectancy and birth rates it has mostly narrowed since 1960, but it can widen again.

Mission 2

  • Natural change = births − deaths. Niger: 3.27% a year, doubling in about 21 years. Japan: −0.63%.
  • DTM: Niger late stage 2 (low income); Japan stage 5 (high income).
  • Pyramids: wide base (youth dependent) vs narrow base and wide top (aged dependent).

Mission 3

  • Reasons for child labour: poverty, family farming, conflict and crisis, lack of affordable schools; girls' chores are often hidden.
  • Girls' primary education is blocked by poverty, preference for boys, child marriage, distance, unsafe schools and conflict.
  • Responses: local (Burkina Faso's girl-friendly schools raised enrolment, more for girls) and global (SDG 8.7, missed in 2025; SDG 4.1 and 4.5).

Mission 4

  • Refugees cross a border fleeing persecution; IDPs do not. Rohingya: statelessness, then the 2017 "clearance operations".
  • Challenges: congested, aid-dependent camps (refugees); land, money and time (Bangladesh).
  • Agreements: 1951 Convention (non-refoulement), EU–Turkey Statement (2016), EU Pact (2026).

Mission 5

  • IMR = deaths before age one per 1,000 live births. Sub-Saharan Africa: 47.1 in 2024, against 27.7 for the world.
  • Medical causes: preterm birth, birth complications, pneumonia, diarrhoea, malaria. Underlying reasons: few trained birth attendants, poverty, rural isolation, mothers' education, conflict, unsafe water.
  • Progress is measured with IMR, under-five mortality, life expectancy and births with skilled staff, against MDG and SDG targets.

Mission 6

  • Malaria is vector-borne, spread by female Anopheles mosquitoes. 2024: 610,000 deaths, 95% in the WHO African Region, about 75% of them under-fives.
  • Challenges: climate suits it; poverty; funding gaps; drug resistance and an invasive mosquito (An. stephensi); slower economic growth.
  • Rwanda (local and national): nets and drugs from 2005 cut hospital cases 72% by 2010; elected village health workers. Global: SDG 3.3, vaccines, the Global Fund.

Mission 7

  • HIV: no cure, but ART controls it. 64% of people with HIV live in the WHO African Region. Challenges: deaths of working-age adults, lower life expectancy, young women at risk, lifelong costs, stigma.
  • Rwanda: ART from fewer than 150 people outside Kigali (2003) to 114,600 (2012); 95-95-95 reached by 2023. Global: SDG 3.3 and 95-95-95.
  • Top-down (national ART funded by donors) and bottom-up (village-elected health workers) worked together in Rwanda.
