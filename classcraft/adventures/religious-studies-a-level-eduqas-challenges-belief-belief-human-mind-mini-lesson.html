Mission 1: Could belief in God grow out of a small child's need for a protective father?

Make a guess: Could belief in God grow out of a small child's need for a protective father?

Mission 1

Freud's question

  • Sigmund Freud (1856–1939), Viennese doctor, founder of psychoanalysis; raised in a Jewish family, himself an atheist.
  • Much of the mind is unconscious: wishes, fears and conflicts we do not see drive what we think and do.
  • His question: not "is religion true?" but "why do people believe it so strongly?"
  • Key works: Obsessive Actions and Religious Practices (1907); Totem and Taboo (1913); The Future of an Illusion (1927); Moses and Monotheism (1939).
  • His answer: religion is a product of the human mind, shaped by childhood and by humanity's early history.
Mission 1

Illusion and neurosis

  • An illusion (Freud's sense): a belief held because it fulfils a wish, without regard to evidence.
  • An illusion need not be false. Freud's example: a girl believes a prince will come to marry her; occasionally one has.
  • A delusion contradicts reality. Freud calls religious doctrines illusions: they cannot be proved or refuted, though he judged them very improbable.
  • A neurosis: compulsive feelings and actions driven by repressed conflict.
  • Religion is a collective neurosis: in Freud's phrase, "the universal obsessional neurosis of humanity" (The Future of an Illusion, 1927).
  • Shared by a whole society, it may spare individuals from forming their own neuroses.
Quick check
?When Freud calls religious belief an "illusion", what does he mean?
Mission 1

Wish fulfilment and helplessness

  • A small child is helpless and is protected by a powerful father.
  • Adults are helpless too: before the forces of nature, before fate, and before death.
  • We long again for a protecting father, and project him onto the universe: God.
  • So the gods have three tasks: to tame the terrors of nature, to reconcile us to fate and death, and to make up for the sufferings civilisation demands (paraphrase, The Future of an Illusion).
  • Religion is a reaction against helplessness: a wish fulfilled.
Mission 1

The Oedipus complex

  • Named after Sophocles' Oedipus the King, in which Oedipus unknowingly kills his father and marries his mother.
  • Freud: a young boy desires his mother and sees his father as a rival.
  • He feels ambivalence to the father: love and admiration, but also fear and hostility, and then guilt.
  • Resolved when the child identifies with the father and takes in his authority as conscience (the superego).
  • God, as an exalted father, inherits this ambivalence: loved as protector, feared as judge.
Mission 1

The primal horde

Totem and Taboo (1913), building on Darwin (paraphrase):

  • Early humans lived in a horde ruled by a jealous, violent father who kept all the women.
  • The sons, driven out, banded together, killed and ate the father.
  • Afterwards they felt remorse and guilt, since they had also loved him.
  • They set up a totem animal as a father-substitute, forbade killing it, and gave up the women: the first taboos (on murder and incest).
  • A yearly totem meal repeated the deed in ritual. In time, the totem became a god.
  • Freud: religion, morality and society all begin from this guilt, passed down the generations.
Mission 1Read the source

Freud's evidence from Darwin

Charles Darwin, The Descent of Man (1871), on early humans:

> "lived in small communities, each with as many wives as he could support and obtain"

  • Freud built the primal horde on this suggestion, on Darwin's observations of other primates, and on reports of totem meals among some peoples.

Q1 Is Darwin's remark an observation of early humans, or an inference?

Show answer

An inference, from animal behaviour; no one observed early human groups.

Q2 Why does this matter for Freud's theory?

Show answer

The primal horde rests on speculation, so the theory's historical base is weak: an EDQ-listed challenge.

Mission 1

Ritual and guilt

  • Obsessive Actions and Religious Practices (1907): obsessional patients perform private rituals exactly, and feel anxious and guilty if they miss a step.
  • Freud saw the same pattern in religious rites: exact performance, anxiety at omission, guilt relieved by observance.
  • Religion redirects guilt (from the Oedipal conflict and the primal deed) into ritual, sacrifice, confession and atonement.
  • Freud read Christian sacrifice and communion this way (as a re-enacted totem meal). Christians understand them very differently: as sharing in Christ's self-giving sacrifice and receiving God's forgiveness, not as repeating a prehistoric killing.
Mission 1

Freud's supporting evidence

  • Clinical: in his patients, ritual and confession relieved guilt, as religious practice seems to.
  • Redirection of guilt complexes: religion offers shared ways to handle guilt (atonement, forgiveness).
  • Evolutionary basis: humans are evolved animals with instinctive drives (Darwin); primate groups dominated by one male gave the horde its model.
  • Imagery: many religions call God "Father"; believers often turn to God most in fear and helplessness.
  • For Freud, these fit his theory better than the claim that God is really there.
Mission 1Evaluate

How strong is Freud's case?

  • Claim: Freud adequately explains religious belief.
  • For: Explains the father image, ambivalence (love and fear of God) and ritual guilt in one theory.
  • For (EDQ list): religion does redirect guilt into shared ritual, confession and atonement.
  • For (EDQ list): fits Darwin's picture of humans as animals with instinctive drives.
  • Against (EDQ list): no anthropological evidence for a primal horde; A. L. Kroeber's review (1920) found totemism neither universal nor linked to such a crime.
  • Against (EDQ list): no firm evidence for a universal Oedipus complex: Bronisław Malinowski (1884–1942), Sex and Repression in Savage Society (1927), found Trobriand Islanders' family conflicts centred on the mother's brother, not the father.
  • Against (EDQ list): evidence base too narrow: mainly a small number of Viennese patients, many with neuroses.
  • Against: a wish's existence does not make its object unreal (genetic fallacy); and the wish cuts both ways: unbelief could be a wish too.
  • Judgement criteria: Is the theory testable? Does it explain religion's demands, not just its comforts? Does origin decide truth?
Quick check
?Malinowski's findings among the Trobriand Islanders are used to challenge which of Freud's claims?
Mission check

?Some believers picture God mainly as a stern judge, not as a comforting protector. How could Freud account for this?

For Freud, a child's ambivalent bond with a father explains both the comfort and the fear in belief. Whether that settles God's reality is the question critics press.

Mission 2: Could a psychologist think religion is good for you without claiming that God exists?

Make a guess: Could a psychologist think religion is good for you without claiming that God exists?

Mission 2

Jung breaks with Freud

  • Carl Gustav Jung (1875–1961), Swiss psychiatrist; son of a Swiss Reformed pastor.
  • Freud's closest colleague from 1907; they split in 1912–13.
  • Jung rejected Freud's view that the mind's energy is basically sexual, and his view of religion as neurosis.
  • For Jung, religion is a natural, often healthy function of the psyche.
  • Key works: Modern Man in Search of a Soul (1933); Psychology and Religion (1938); Aion (1951); Answer to Job (1952).
Mission 2

The collective unconscious

  • Ego: the conscious "I".
  • Personal unconscious: forgotten and repressed experiences of one person's life.
  • Collective unconscious: a deeper layer shared by all humans and inherited, not learned from personal experience.
  • It shows itself in myths, dreams, visions and religious symbols that recur across cultures and ages.
  • Contrast Freud: the unconscious is personal, formed by each person's history.
Mission 2

Archetypes

  • Archetypes are inherited patterns in the collective unconscious. Jung insisted they are forms, not ready-made pictures: each culture fills them with its own images.
  • Examples: the mother, the wise old man, the hero, the trickster.
  • Parts of the self: the persona (the social mask), the shadow (what we deny in ourselves), the anima/animus (the inner other-sex side).
  • The Self: the archetype of wholeness, the centre of the whole psyche. Symbols include the circle and the mandala; Jung saw Christ as a symbol of the Self (Aion).
  • Religions are rich in archetypal images: that is why they move people so deeply.
Quick check
?Which statement about Jung's archetypes is accurate?
Mission 2

The God within

  • The psyche contains a God-image (imago Dei): the archetype of the Self.
  • So God is psychologically real: a powerful fact of the psyche, whatever is true beyond it.
  • Jung said that, as a psychologist, he could speak only of the God-image, not of God beyond the psyche.
  • The God within is the inner source of wholeness and meaning, met in dreams, symbols and religious experience.
  • This is very different from Freud's projected father: for Jung the God-image is a healthy, necessary part of the mind.
Mission 2Read the source

Jung on religion and healing

  • In a BBC interview (Face to Face, 1959), asked whether he believed in God, Jung replied: "I don't need to believe. I know."
  • Paraphrase from Modern Man in Search of a Soul (1933): among his patients in the second half of life (over about 35), every one's problem was, in the last resort, finding a religious outlook on life; none was really healed without regaining one.

Q1 Given the earlier screen, what might Jung "know"?

Show answer

Most likely the reality of the God-image and of experiences of it; he did not claim a proof of God beyond the psyche.

Q2 Does the claim about his patients show that God exists?

Show answer

No: it is evidence that a religious outlook helps healing, not about what exists beyond the psyche.

Mission 2

Individuation

  • Individuation: the lifelong process of becoming a whole, unique person.
  • The ego comes to terms with the persona, faces the shadow, integrates the anima/animus, and is centred on the Self.
  • Mainly a task for the second half of life.
  • Religion's symbols, stories and rituals give a map for this journey.
  • So religion is necessary for personal growth (EDQ wording); losing it can leave people without meaning, and ill.
Mission 2

Supporting evidence

  • Comfort: religion gives meaning, hope and consolation in suffering and death (EDQ list).
  • Positive mindsets: religious belief can promote hope, purpose and self-acceptance in individuals, and belonging and care in communities (EDQ list).
  • Recurring symbols: similar motifs (mother, hero, rebirth, the circle) appear in myths and religions far apart in time and place.
  • Clinical: Jung reported patients whose dreams and visions echoed ancient myths they claimed never to have known.
Mission 2

Challenges

  • No empirical evidence (EDQ list): the collective unconscious and archetypes cannot be observed, measured or tested.
  • Shared symbols may come from shared human experience (birth, parents, death) or from cultures borrowing from each other, not from an inherited layer of mind.
  • Patients may have met the myths somewhere and forgotten (critics' reply to Jung's clinical cases).
  • Reductionism (EDQ list): if God is an archetype "within", religion is reduced to psychology. Martin Buber (1878–1965), Eclipse of God (1952), argued that Jung left no room for a God who is truly other and can be addressed.
  • Many believers agree: God is within and beyond, not only a part of the psyche.
Quick check
?Critics call Jung's account of religion "reductionist". What do they mean?
Mission 2

Freud and Jung compared

  • Unconscious: Freud: personal, shaped by childhood. Jung: personal and collective, inherited.
  • Source of religion: Freud: wish for a protecting father; guilt. Jung: archetypes; the Self.
  • Verdict on religion: Freud: illusion and neurosis, to be outgrown. Jung: healthy, necessary for growth.
  • God: Freud: a projection, very probably unreal. Jung: a psychological reality; silent beyond the psyche.
  • Shared: both explain religion as a product of the human mind; both are challenged for lack of empirical evidence.
Mission 2Evaluate

Was Jung more positive than Freud about God?

  • Claim: Jung was more positive than Freud about the idea of God.
  • For: Jung calls religion necessary for growth; Freud wants it outgrown.
  • For: Jung treats the God-image as a real and healthy part of the psyche, not a neurosis.
  • Against: Jung is silent on whether God exists beyond the psyche; Buber thought this made God less real, not more.
  • Against: both are reductionist in the end: religion is explained by the mind, not by God.
  • Judgement criteria: Positive about religion, or about God? Does valuing a belief's effects say anything about its truth?
  • Spec issue: "the extent to which Jung was more positive than Freud about the idea of God".
Mission check

?A Buddhist who does not believe in a creator God spends weeks making a mandala in meditation and says the practice makes her feel whole. How would Jung most likely interpret this?

Jung can value religion's symbols as vital for growth while saying nothing about God beyond the psyche: positive about religion, and critics say reductive about God.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Is "I don't believe in God" the same claim as "There is no God"?

Make a guess: Is "I don't believe in God" the same claim as "There is no God"?

Mission 3

Three answers to one question

  • Question: does God exist?
  • Theism: yes, God exists.
  • Atheism: from Greek a-theos, "without God": rejection of belief in God or gods.
  • Agnosticism: from Greek a-gnōstos, "not known": we do not (or cannot) know whether God exists.
  • The word agnostic was coined by T. H. Huxley (1825–1895) in 1869; he explained it in his essay "Agnosticism" (1889).
  • Atheism answers "what do you believe?"; agnosticism answers "what can we know?". So one person can be both.
Mission 3

Kinds of atheism and agnosticism

  • Positive (strong) atheism: asserts "there is no God".
  • Negative (weak) atheism: simply lacks belief in God; makes no claim to disprove God.
  • Practical atheism: lives as if God does not exist, whatever is said.
  • Protest atheism: rejects God on moral grounds, often because of suffering (e.g. Ivan in Dostoevsky's The Brothers Karamazov, 1880).
  • Strong (permanent) agnosticism: God's existence cannot be known by anyone.
  • Weak (temporary) agnosticism: it is not yet known; evidence may settle it.
Quick check
?Priya says: "I have no belief in God. I can't prove there isn't one, and I don't think anyone ever will settle it either way." Which labels fit her best?
Mission 3

New Atheism

  • A movement after the attacks of 11 September 2001; best-selling books, debates, media.
  • Leading writers: Richard Dawkins, The God Delusion (2006); Christopher Hitchens, God Is Not Great (2007); Sam Harris, The End of Faith (2004); Daniel Dennett, Breaking the Spell (2006).
  • Older atheism mostly said religion is false. New Atheism adds: religion is harmful and should be challenged openly.
  • Also called antitheism: opposition to religion itself, not only disbelief.
  • Treats "God exists" as a hypothesis to be tested by the standards of science.
Mission 3Read the source

Dawkins' case

Richard Dawkins (b. 1941), The God Delusion (2006), paraphrased:

  • God's existence is a scientific question; the "God hypothesis" should be judged on evidence.
  • A designer God would need to be more complex than what it designs, so it explains nothing and is itself very improbable ("Ultimate Boeing 747"; chapter 4).
  • He places beliefs on a spectrum of theistic probability from 1 (strong theist: "I know God exists") to 7 (strong atheist: "I know there is no God"); 4 is a completely even-handed agnostic.
  • He calls himself a 6: God very improbable, but not disproved ("leaning towards 7").

Q1 Does Dawkins claim to know that there is no God?

Show answer

No: he thinks God very improbable and lives on that assumption, but claims no certainty: a 6, not a 7.

Q2 Why does he think "God designed it" is not a good explanation?

Show answer

The designer would need explaining even more than the design.

Mission 3

The criticisms of religion

  • Three criticisms to know:
  • Non-thinking: faith is belief without evidence, so it discourages questioning (Dawkins).
  • Infantile worldview: religion is a comfort from humanity's childhood (Hitchens: religion comes from the "infancy" of our species).
  • Impedes scientific progress: religion gives "God did it" answers and has resisted science (e.g. the Galileo affair, 1633; opposition to evolution; limits on embryo research).
  • Also: harm from religious violence and from teaching children that doubt is wrong.
  • Believers reply that these describe some religion, not faith as most traditions understand it.
Quick check
?Which claim is most typical of New Atheism rather than of older forms of atheism?
Mission 3Diversity

Religious responses to New Atheism

  • Science and religion are compatible: many religious groups reject the claim that they conflict. Francis Collins (geneticist, led the Human Genome Project), The Language of God (2006); John Lennox, God's Undertaker (2007).
  • Apologists in the media: Alister McGrath and Joanna Collicutt McGrath, The Dawkins Delusion? (2007); public debates (e.g. Dawkins and Lennox, 2007). Main replies: faith is not "belief without evidence"; Dawkins attacks a God few theologians believe in.
  • Fundamentalist activity: some groups respond by stressing a firm, separate moral community (e.g. on marriage, education, creation teaching).
  • Self-critique: following Westphal, some believers accept parts of the critique as prophetic.
Mission 3Evaluate

Have the New Atheists made their case?

  • Claim: New Atheism has successfully shown that religious belief is unreasonable.
  • For: Religion makes testable claims (e.g. prayer, design), and science explains much that once needed God.
  • For: Real harm has been done in religion's name; probability (Dawkins) puts the burden of proof on believers.
  • Against: It attacks a "God of the gaps" that most theologians reject; it ignores philosophy of religion (critics: McGrath, and the philosopher Alvin Plantinga).
  • Against: Faith is often trust based on reasons and experience, not "belief without evidence"; many leading scientists are religious believers.
  • Judgement criteria: Does the critique describe faith as believers hold it? Is science the only route to truth? Do harmful acts show that a belief is false?
  • Also weigh: how successful atheistic arguments have been, and how successful religious responses to New Atheism have been.
Mission check

?At a university debate, Sam says: "I'm a scientist. The evidence makes God very unlikely, so I live without God, but I can't rule God out." His opponent says: "Then you are really an agnostic, not an atheist." Which reply is most accurate?

"I don't believe in God" and "there is no God" are different claims: the first reports a lack of belief; the second denies that God exists. Whether either speaker claims to know is a further question. Next: what existentialists and humanists make of a world without God.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Freud: religion is an illusion (wish-driven, not necessarily false) and a collective neurosis, rooted in helplessness and wish fulfilment.
  • The Oedipus complex (individual) and the primal horde (prehistory) explain God as an exalted, loved and feared father, and ritual as redirected guilt.
  • Challenges: no anthropological evidence for the primal horde; no universal Oedipus complex (Malinowski); a narrow evidence base; origin does not decide truth.

Mission 2

  • Jung: a collective unconscious, inherited and shared, holds archetypes; the Self is the archetype of wholeness and the God-image, "the God within".
  • Individuation, guided by religious symbols, makes religion necessary for growth; evidence: comfort, positive mindsets, recurring symbols.
  • Challenges: no empirical evidence; reductionism (Buber). Compared with Freud, Jung values religion but stays silent on God beyond the psyche.

Mission 3

  • Atheism answers what you believe; agnosticism answers what can be known. Atheism can be positive or negative; agnosticism strong or weak.
  • New Atheism (antitheism: Dawkins, Hitchens, Harris, Dennett) says religion is false and harmful; religious responses stress science-religion compatibility and public apologetics.
