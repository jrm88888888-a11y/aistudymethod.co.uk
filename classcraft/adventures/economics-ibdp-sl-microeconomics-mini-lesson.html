Mission 1

What demand means

  • Demand: the quantity of a good that buyers are willing and able to buy at each price, over a given time period.
  • Law of demand: as price rises, quantity demanded falls, ceteris paribus (other things equal).
  • A demand schedule lists the quantity demanded at each price. Illustrative data:

  • Plot it: the demand curve (D) slopes downwards.
  • Demand is the whole curve, not one point on it.

From one buyer to the market

  • Individual demand: one buyer's schedule. Market demand: all buyers together.
  • At each price, add the quantities of every buyer.
  • On a graph this is a horizontal sum: same price, add across.

  • More buyers in the market → market demand rises at every price.

Mission 1

Movement or shift?

  • A change in the good's own price → a movement along D.
  • A change in anything else that affects buyers → a shift of the whole curve.
  • Step 1: price falls from P1 to P2 → slide along D from point a to point b.
  • Step 2: D1 → D2 to the right: more is demanded at every price.
  • Say: "a fall in price causes an extension of demand (a rise in quantity demanded)" — not: "demand increases".
  • A price rise → contraction of demand. A shift right → increase in demand; shift left → decrease.
Mission 1

What shifts demand

Shift factorIncrease (D shifts right) when…
Incomeincome rises (normal good); income falls (inferior good)
Price of a substitutethe substitute's price rises
Price of a complementthe complement's price falls
Tastes and preferencesthe good becomes more fashionable or popular
Future price expectationsbuyers expect the price to rise soon
Number of consumersmore buyers enter the market
  • The opposite change shifts D left.

Mission 2

What supply means

  • Supply: the quantity that producers are willing and able to sell at each price, over a given time period.
  • Law of supply: as price rises, quantity supplied rises, ceteris paribus.
  • Why? The profit incentive: a higher price makes extra output more profitable, so firms produce more.
  • Illustrative data: a strawberry farm.
Price per kgQuantity supplied (kg per week)
2100
3200
4300
5400
  • Plot it: the supply curve (S) slopes upwards.
Mission 2

From one firm to the market

  • Individual supply: one producer's schedule. Market supply: all producers together.
  • At each price, add the quantities of every producer: a horizontal sum.
  • Illustrative data: at a price of 6, Farm A supplies 4 tonnes and Farm B supplies 3 tonnes → market supply 7 tonnes.
  • More firms → market supply rises at every price.
Quick check
?At a price of 5, three bakeries supply 40, 30 and 20 loaves a day. A fourth bakery opens and supplies 30 loaves at that price. What is market quantity supplied at a price of 5 now?
Mission 2

Movement or shift?

  • A change in the good's own price → a movement along S.
  • A change in anything else that affects producers → a shift of the whole curve.
  • Step 1: price rises P1 → P2 → slide up along S.
  • Step 2: S1 → S2 to the right = increase in supply: more offered at every price.
  • A shift left = decrease in supply.
  • Say: "an increase in supply shifts the curve right" — not: "the supply curve shifts up". A rightward shift looks lower on the graph.
  • Say: "a rise in price causes an extension of supply" — not: "supply increases".
Mission 2

What shifts supply

Shift factorSupply increases (S shifts right) when…
Costs of productionwages, raw material or energy prices fall
Technologybetter methods raise output from the same inputs
Indirect taxes and subsidiesa tax is cut or a subsidy is given
Number of firmsmore firms enter the market
Expectationsproducers expect lower prices later, so they sell more now
Shocksgood weather, no disease, no supply disruption
  • An indirect tax per unit shifts S left (vertically up by the tax). A subsidy shifts S right (detail in a later lesson).
  • The full list: costs of factors of production, prices of related goods, indirect taxes and subsidies, future price expectations, changes in technology, number of firms.

  • At a price of 4: 300 → 200 kg. Same price, less offered → decrease in supply (shift left).
  • To supply 300 kg now, the farm needs a price of 5, not 4. The curve sits higher but has moved left.
Mission 2

Real case: frost hits coffee

  • July 2021: frosts hit coffee-growing areas of Brazil, the world's largest coffee producer.
  • Damaged trees mean less coffee for sale at every price in later harvests.
  • That is a shock to supply: S shifts left, not a movement along it.
  • The price rise that followed is the result of the shift; it does not cause the shift.
Mission 2

Joint and competitive supply

  • Joint supply: producing one good automatically produces another. Cattle give beef and leather.
  • More beef produced → supply of leather increases.
  • Competitive supply: the same resources can make either good. A field can grow wheat or barley.
  • Barley price rises → farmers switch land → supply of wheat decreases.
  • Say: "a change in the price of a related good shifts supply" — not: "moves along it".
Quick check
?The price of oranges rises and orange growers sell more oranges. Nothing else changes. How should an economist describe this?
Mission check

?Which change causes a decrease in the supply of electric scooters?

Only the good's own price moves producers along S. A change in costs, technology, taxes, subsidies or the number of firms shifts it.

Mission 3

Mission 3: How can a change in one market move prices in a completely different one?

Make a guess: The price of fuel jumps. What happens to sales of big cars that use a lot of fuel?

Mission 3

Joint supply: by-products

  • Joint supply: making one good automatically makes another.
  • Cattle → beef and leather. Crude oil refining → petrol, diesel and jet fuel.
  • Step 1: demand for beef rises → more cattle are reared and slaughtered.
  • Step 2: more hides → supply of leather shifts right.
  • Step 3: leather price falls, quantity rises.
  • The two goods move in opposite price directions here.
Mission 3

Case: carbon dioxide, 2021

  • In the UK, food-grade CO₂ is a by-product of making fertiliser from natural gas.
  • In 2021, gas prices nearly quadrupled. In September, the fertiliser firm CF Industries halted its two UK plants.
  • Those plants made about 60% of the UK's food-grade CO₂.
  • CO₂ supply fell sharply. Poultry processors (which use it to stun birds) and drinks makers (for the fizz) warned of disruption. The government told food firms to expect CO₂ prices to rise about 400%.
  • The government agreed short-term financial support to restart one plant, to give the market time to adjust.
  • A gas market shock cut the supply of CO₂ and raised its price, in a market that seems to have nothing to do with gas.
Mission 3

Competitive supply: one resource, two uses

  • Competitive supply: firms can make one good or another with the same resources.
  • A farmer's field: wheat or barley. A factory line: cars or vans.
  • Price of barley rises → farmers switch land to barley → supply of wheat shifts left.
  • Wheat price rises, quantity falls.
  • Joint and competitive supply are non-price determinants of supply: they shift the curve.

Quick check

Mission check

?Crushing sunflower seeds produces sunflower oil and sunflower meal, which is sold as animal feed. Demand for sunflower oil rises sharply. What is the most likely effect in the market for sunflower meal?

When goods come from the same process, a boom in one makes the other more plentiful.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Demand is the whole schedule or curve; its own price moves you along it, every other factor shifts it.
  • Price fall: extension of demand; non-price change: increase or decrease in demand.

Mission 2

  • Supply slopes up because a higher price makes extra output profitable; own price moves you along S, every other factor shifts it.
  • Increase in supply = shift right (it looks lower), decrease = shift left.

Mission 3

  • Joint supply: more of one product means more of its by-product.
  • Competitive supply: more of one product means less of the other.
