Mission 1

Mission 1: Two neighbours share a language and a passport. Can they still belong to different ethnic groups?

Make a guess: Two neighbours share a language and a passport. Can they still belong to different ethnic groups?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • A2-2.C1.i factors that define ethnicity: race, nationality, language and religion
  • A2-2.C1.ii how role, residential concentration, age and gender influence perceived ethnic and social identity
Mission 1

Ethnicity is many things at once

  • ONS: ethnic group is "self-defined and subjectively meaningful to an individual".
  • It combines "common ancestry and elements of culture, identity, religion, language and physical appearance".
  • No single marker is enough: "ethnicity includes all these aspects, and others, in combination".
  • It is "a multifaceted and changing phenomenon".
Mission 1

Race

  • Race groups people by perceived physical appearance and ancestry.
  • ONS: skin colour is "not an adequate criterion in its own right".
  • Censuses use self-chosen categories. Northern Ireland 2021: 96.6% white; 3.4% (65,600) minority ethnic.
  • Largest minority groups: Mixed (14,400), Black (11,000), Indian (9,900), Chinese (9,500).
Mission 1

Nationality and national identity

  • Nationality (citizenship) is legal; national identity is how people feel, "the subjective nature of national identity".
  • ONS asks them separately from ethnic group.
  • Northern Ireland 2021: British only 31.9%, Irish only 29.1%, Northern Irish only 19.8%, British and Northern Irish 8.0%.
  • Same passport, different identities: both are possible at once.
Mission 1

Language

  • In Northern Ireland, 4.6% of people aged 3+ had a main language other than English; the most common were Polish (20,100) and Lithuanian (9,000).
  • 12.4% had some ability in Irish; 10.4% in Ulster-Scots.
  • ONS: as a marker of ethnic group, language is "becoming increasingly less useful", as later generations may speak English at home.
Mission 1

Religion

  • Religion as identity is affiliation, "irrespective of actual practice or belief".
  • Northern Ireland 2021, religion or religion brought up in: Catholic 45.7%, Protestant and other Christian 43.5%, other religions 1.5%, none 9.3%.
  • Where religion marks community background, it can work as an ethnic marker even for people who do not practise.
Quick check
?ONS says language is "becoming increasingly less useful" as a measure of ethnic group. Why?
Mission 1

Identity is perceived

  • Self-perception: the group a person feels they belong to.
  • Perception by others: the group others assign, often from visible or audible markers.
  • Even self-identification is shaped by the options offered: the choice is "constrained and influenced by the options presented".
  • So identity can shift with context: who is asking, where, and why.
Mission 1

Age

  • Northern Ireland Life and Times Survey 2023: "Which of these best describes the way you think of yourself?"
  • British: 15% of 18–24s vs 37% of over-65s.
  • "Other answer": 12% of each age group under 45 vs 4% of over-55s.
  • Identity can differ between generations who grew up in different times.
Mission 1

Gender

  • Same survey: Northern Irish chosen by 28% of men and 36% of women.
  • British (27% vs 25%) and Irish (31% vs 30%) differ little by sex.
  • Survey differences describe groups, not individuals; they do not show why the gap exists.
Quick check
?In the 2023 survey, 15% of 18–24s and 37% of over-65s described themselves as British. What is the difference?
Mission 1

Residential concentration

  • Minority ethnic share in 2021: Belfast 7.1%, the highest district; Causeway Coast and Glens 1.4%.
  • National identity also varies by place: four districts, including Newry, Mourne and Down, have "markedly more people identifying as Irish... than British"; Belfast's figures are "broadly similar".
  • Concentration makes a group visible, supports its own institutions (places of worship, shops, schools), and shapes how others see the area.
Mission 1

Role

  • Role: the positions a person holds (worker, parent, worshipper, team member, community leader).
  • Different roles bring different identities to the fore: one person may be Polish at home, British at work and Northern Irish at a match.
  • Others' perceptions shift with role too: identity is situational, not fixed.
  • Exam move (CCEA "Explain"): give a marker, a place, and a figure, then say how it shapes perceived identity.
Mission check

?Marta was born in Poland and has lived in Belfast for 15 years. She is a British citizen, speaks Polish at home and English at work, and ticked "White" on her census form. Which statement best fits how ethnicity is defined?

Yes, the two neighbours can differ. Ethnicity combines race, nationality, language and religion, but it is self-defined, and age, gender, residential concentration and role shape how it is perceived.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Nearly half of France's immigrants were born in Africa. How did an empire help make France so diverse?

Make a guess: Nearly half of France's immigrants were born in Africa. How did an empire help make France so diverse?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • A2-2.C2.i processes creating ethnic diversity (colonisation, annexation, international migration), in one country case
Mission 2

Three processes that create diversity

  • Colonisation: a state takes control of distant lands and peoples, often moving settlers, workers or enslaved people.
  • Annexation: a state formally makes territory part of itself, so its people become part of the state.
  • International migration: people move across borders to live, for work, family, study or refuge.
  • In France, all three are linked through the history of its empire.
Mission 2

Colonisation and slavery

  • France's overseas possessions became royal colonies in 1674.
  • 1713–1791: about 1 million enslaved Africans were sold and deported to the French Caribbean (the Antilles), over 775,000 of them to Saint-Domingue.
  • Slavery was abolished again in April 1848, but plantations then used contracted labourers ("engagés") from Asia and Africa: nearly 50,000 went to Réunion, 1848–1860.
  • Result: the populations of France's overseas territories have African, Asian and European origins.
Mission 2

Annexation: making territory part of France

  • Algeria, France's main settler colony, was turned into three French départements in 1848; it drew French and other European migrants.
  • The Antilles, French Guiana and Réunion became départements after the Second World War.
  • After 1945, Algerians were legally French nationals with free movement between Algeria and France.
  • Annexation made colonial peoples part of the French state, which shaped who could later move to mainland France.
Quick check
?What makes annexation different from colonisation in general?
Mission 2

Moving within the French state

  • On 1 November 1954, about 210,000 Algerians lived in mainland France.
  • The Bumidom (1963–1981) organised the move of 170,000 people from the overseas départements to mainland France, often into care and domestic work.
  • These moves were by French citizens, so in law they are internal, not international, migration, but they added to mainland France's ethnic diversity.
Mission 2

Decolonisation brings new migrations

  • After the Indochina war ended in 1954, over 30,000 people were repatriated and allowed to settle in France.
  • 1962: Algerian independence; about 1 million Europeans (the "pieds-noirs") left for France, many having never lived there; harkis, Algerians who had served with the French, also arrived.
  • The Évian agreements kept free movement between France and Algeria, allowing migration outside ordinary rules.
Quick check
?INSEE defines an immigrant as a person born a foreigner, abroad, and living in France. Are the "pieds-noirs" born in French Algeria counted as immigrants?
Mission 2

Labour migration and family reunion

  • 1945–1975: six million foreigners were recruited through the national immigration office (ONI) to fill labour shortages.
  • Flows were mainly male, for post-war reconstruction and the boom years; labour immigration was slowed in 1974.
  • Since then, family reunion, study and other reasons have grown: women were 44% of immigrants in 1975 and 52% in 2025.
Mission 2

France today

  • 2025: 8.0 million immigrants, 11.6% of the population, up from 7.3% in 1999.
  • Born in Africa 49.2%, Europe 30.3%.
  • Most common birth countries: Algeria 12.6%, Morocco 11.7%, Portugal 7.2%, Tunisia 4.9%, Italy 3.5%, Turkey 3.5%, Spain 3.0%.
  • Algeria at the top reflects colonisation and annexation; Portugal, Italy and Spain reflect European labour migration.
Quick check
?In 2025, about 8.0 million immigrants lived in France, and 12.6% of them were born in Algeria. About how many is that?
Mission check

?A country's census shows many residents born in a former colony, and many citizens whose ancestors were enslaved in territories the country still governs. Which processes best explain this diversity?

France became diverse through colonisation (slavery, indentured labour, settlement), annexation (Algeria's départements; the overseas départements) and international migration (post-war labour, decolonisation, family reunion). That is why nearly half of today's immigrants were born in Africa.

Mission 3: A square metre of land in Ginza, central Tokyo, is valued at ¥67.1 million. A square metre of housing land in Ome, also in Tokyo, averages ¥98,600. Why the gap?

Make a guess: A square metre of land in Ginza, central Tokyo, is valued at ¥67.1 million. A square metre of housing land in Ome, also in Tokyo, averages ¥98,600. Why the gap?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • A2-2.C2.ii processes maintaining ethnic diversity: segregation and multiculturalism (general illustration)
Mission 3

Land values and bid rent

  • Bid-rent theory (Alonso): land users bid for each site by how much they gain from its accessibility.
  • Accessibility peaks in the central business district (CBD), so land values peak there: the peak land value intersection.
  • Shops and offices gain most from being central, so they outbid industry and housing near the centre.
  • Values fall with distance, but rise again at secondary centres, stations and main roads.
Mission 3

Tokyo's land value gradient

  • Japan's highest commercial land value (1 January 2026): Ginza 4-chome, ¥67.1 million per m², up 10.9% in a year.
  • Average residential values per m², 2026: Chiyoda ward ¥3,631,400; Setagaya ward ¥779,900; Hachioji ¥131,300; Ome ¥98,600.
  • Even Japan's highest residential value (Akasaka, Minato ward: ¥7.11 million per m²) is about a tenth of Ginza's commercial value.
Mission 3

Segregation and cultural diversity

  • Residential segregation: groups (by income, ethnicity or religion) living apart in different areas.
  • Voluntary factors: support networks, places of worship, shops, language, safety in numbers.
  • Involuntary factors: cost, discrimination by landlords or employers, allocation of social housing.
  • Cultural diversity: many groups, languages and religions in one city; multiculturalism: groups keep distinct identities while sharing the city, often backed by policy.
Mission 3

Planning against segregation: Singapore

  • 76.0% of Singapore's resident population lived in public housing (HDB) flats at end-March 2025.
  • In 1989, after "signs that segregation among the races had once again started to take root", the government introduced the Ethnic Integration Policy.
  • It caps each ethnic group's share in every HDB block and neighbourhood, because "it was not possible to uproot residents to break up the enclaves".
  • In 2010 the limits for Indians and other minority groups were raised to 12% (neighbourhood) and 15% (block).
Mission 3

Evaluating the policy

  • Aim: mixed neighbourhoods, not enclaves: a deliberate multicultural settlement pattern.
  • Trade-off: when a block reaches a group's limit, owners there have fewer possible buyers, a direct effect of a quota.
  • Transferability: it works where the state houses most people; it would be far harder where most housing is private.
  • Exam move: say which processes maintain diversity (voluntary clustering, multicultural policy) and which reduce segregation (quotas).
Quick check
?Which is an involuntary cause of residential segregation?
Mission check

?In a city where most homes are privately owned or rented, one ethnic group is concentrated in a few districts. The council proposes Singapore-style limits on each group's share of every block. Which evaluation is best?

Segregation has voluntary and involuntary causes. Singapore's Ethnic Integration Policy caps each group's share of every block and neighbourhood; it fits a city where the state houses most people (76.0% in Singapore) and would be far harder where most housing is private. The opening question: Ginza's land is worth about 680 times Ome's average residential value (67,100,000 ÷ 98,600) because central accessibility lets commercial users outbid everyone. That is bid rent.

Mission 4: In parts of Belfast, nine in ten people share one religious background. Why does the city stay divided?

Make a guess: In parts of Belfast, nine in ten people share one religious background. Why does the city stay divided?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

Mission 4

Belfast's diversity

  • Census 2021, religion or religion brought up in, Belfast district:
    • Catholic 48.7% · Protestant and other Christian 36.4% · other religions 3.3% · none 11.6%
  • 7.1% of residents belong to ethnic groups other than White (2021); the largest are Chinese (4,738), Indian (4,361), Mixed (4,159) and Black African (4,127).
  • Two kinds of diversity: an old ethno-religious divide (Catholic/nationalist and Protestant/unionist) and newer migrant communities.
Mission 4

Segregation: the pattern

  • Belfast has 60 wards. In 16, at least 80% of residents are Catholic or brought up Catholic (calculated from Census 2021).
  • Highest: Turf Lodge 94.9%, Andersonstown 94.0%, Falls Park 93.7% Catholic, all in west Belfast.
  • Protestant and other Christian majorities: Shankill 80.4%, Woodvale 77.0%.
  • Some wards are mixed: Ballymacarrett is 44.4% Catholic and 39.4% Protestant and other Christian.
Quick check
?Only 1 of Belfast's 60 wards is at least 80% Protestant and other Christian, but 16 are at least 80% Catholic. Which conclusion is most valid?
Mission 4

Processes maintaining segregation

  • Interface areas: where highly segregated areas meet; in many, the boundary is marked by a peace line.
  • Peace lines grew out of community barricades in 1969; the army replaced them with barriers, later made permanent, extended and raised.
  • About 35 peace lines existed by 2001, 26 of them in Belfast, mainly in the west and north.
  • In 2013 the Department of Justice was responsible for 54 interface structures: 41 walls or fences and 13 gates.
  • Why segregation persists: safety and fear at interfaces, belonging and identity, and separate schools, churches and clubs. Sectarian interfaces are "more prone to outbreaks of sectarian violence".
Mission 4

Multiculturalism: policies for living together

  • Multiculturalism: different groups keep their identities while sharing space, services and rights.
  • Together: Building a United Community (2013) aimed to create 10 new shared neighbourhood developments and to "reduce, and remove by 2023, all interface barriers".
  • The Racial Equality Strategy 2015–2025 aims to tackle racial inequalities and racism and to promote good race relations.
  • Integrated schools educate pupils from different religious backgrounds together; there are 70 grant-aided integrated schools in Northern Ireland.
Quick check
?Which of these is a multicultural policy rather than a process that maintains segregation?
Mission 4

New diversity in the inner city

  • Central ward: 26.3% of residents are in ethnic groups other than White; 11.0% belong to other religions (Census 2021, calculated).
  • The highest shares in groups other than White are in wards in or close to the city centre: Central 26.3%, Windsor 17.9%, Blackstaff 16.0%, Duncairn 12.9% (our calculation).
  • This adds a second pattern: mixed, multicultural inner areas beside segregated outer estates.
Mission 4

Outcomes: economic

  • In the 2017 deprivation measure, 50 of Northern Ireland's 100 most deprived small areas were in Belfast.
  • They include areas in both communities: Ardoyne, Falls, New Lodge and Whiterock, and Shankill and Woodvale.
  • Deprivation and segregation overlap: several strongly single-community areas (Falls, Shankill, Woodvale) appear on the list.
Mission 4

Outcomes: social and spatial

  • Social: separate schools and social lives limit contact; interface areas are prone to sectarian violence.
  • Spatial: walls, gates and interface "dead" land divide neighbourhoods; services such as shops and leisure centres may be duplicated so each community has its own.
  • New diversity: in Central ward, 11.0% of residents belong to religions other than Christianity, against 3.3% across Belfast.
Quick check
?A peace wall with gates that close at night separates two housing estates. Which kind of outcome is this, in CCEA's terms?
Mission 4

How far has Belfast changed?

  • The peace process: paramilitary ceasefires in 1994, then the Good Friday Agreement in 1998.
  • In 2013 the Executive set a target to remove all interface barriers by 2023. In 2007 CAIN noted there was "no widespread support" for removing peace walls, and some communities wanted more.
  • Census 2021 still shows wards over 90% one community.
  • Exam move (CCEA "Evaluate"): weigh segregation (walls, single-community wards, deprivation) against multiculturalism (shared housing, integrated schools, new migrant diversity) and reach a judgement.
Mission check

?In another city, two communities mostly live in separate estates divided by walls, but a growing migrant population settles in mixed inner-city streets. Which judgement fits the Belfast evidence best?

Belfast stays divided because segregation is maintained by interfaces, peace walls, fear, identity and separate institutions, and it overlaps with deprivation. Multicultural policies (shared housing, integrated schools, racial equality) and new migrant communities create mixed spaces, but they sit alongside the old divide rather than replacing it.

Mission 5: Why do some ethnic tensions end in a peace deal, and others in war or a divided country?

Make a guess: Why do some ethnic tensions end in a peace deal, and others in war or a divided country?

Specification: what this mission covers

  • A2-2.C3.ii nature of ethnic conflict: civil disobedience, civil war, terrorism
  • A2-2.C3.iii outcomes: social and economic impacts, territorial division, autonomy, ethnic cleansing, international intervention, peace processes
Mission 5

What is ethnic conflict?

  • Ethnic conflict: conflict between groups who see themselves as different by nationality, language, religion or race, where that identity becomes the line of division.
  • Conflict ranges from protest to war. It is usually about power, territory and rights, not identity alone.
  • Most cases have several causes acting together.
Mission 5

Causes

  • Discrimination: Northern Ireland's civil rights campaign (1960s) said Catholics faced disadvantage in public housing, jobs and electoral practices.
  • Sectarianism: conflict between religious communities; e.g. on 15 August 1969 sectarian riots in Belfast killed six people and forced many families from their homes.
  • Historical animosities: in Rwanda, Tutsis generally held the higher social strata before colonial rule, though people could move between the groups; the 1959–1961 Hutu uprising sharpened ethnic tensions, and by 1962 about 120,000 people, mainly Tutsis, had fled.
  • Racism: from 1990, Rwandan propaganda labelled all Tutsis as accomplices of the rebels; radio spread unfounded rumours.
  • Territorial disputes: Cyprus, between Greek Cypriot and Turkish Cypriot communities.
  • Human rights abuses and cultural conflict (e.g. over language or religion) often deepen these divides.
Quick check
?Northern Ireland's civil rights campaigners complained about how public housing and jobs were allocated. Which cause of ethnic conflict does this best illustrate?
Mission 5

When one ethnic group dominates the state

  • Political dominance: one group controls government, the army and resources; others feel excluded.
  • Rwanda: by 1994 Hutu were about 85%, Tutsi 14%, Twa 1% of the population. A Hutu-dominated government faced the Rwandan Patriotic Front (RPF), mainly Tutsi exiles, which invaded from Uganda in 1990 demanding return of refugees and power sharing.
  • Bosnia and Herzegovina: the post-war constitution names Bosniacs, Croats and Serbs as "constituent peoples" and reserves seats for each: the House of Peoples has 5 Croats, 5 Bosniacs, 5 Serbs.
  • Iraq: the 2005 constitution recognises the region of Kurdistan, with its existing authorities, as a federal region.
  • Outcomes differ: the Dayton Agreement, signed 14 December 1995, ended the 3½-year Bosnian War; in Cyprus, the UN has kept a buffer zone between the two communities since the hostilities of 1974.
Quick check
?Bosnia and Herzegovina's constitution reserves seats in the House of Peoples for Bosniacs, Croats and Serbs. What is the main aim of this arrangement?
Mission 5

The nature of ethnic conflict

  • Civil disobedience: deliberately breaking laws, usually non-violently, to press for change. Derry, 5 October 1968: about 400 people marched in defiance of a ban; the next march drew over 10,000.
  • Civil war: organised armed conflict within a state, e.g. Rwanda from 1990; the Bosnian War (about 3½ years, ended 1995).
  • Terrorism: violence by non-state groups, often against civilians, to pursue political aims. In Northern Ireland, the Sutton Index records 3,532 deaths from the conflict, 1969–2001, including 1,840 civilians.
  • Conflicts often escalate from protest to violence, as in Northern Ireland after 1968.
Quick check
?Marchers deliberately walk a banned route, without weapons, to demand equal rights. Which form of conflict is this?
Mission 5

Outcomes: ethnic cleansing, division, autonomy

  • Ethnic cleansing: forcing a group out of an area. Srebrenica, July 1995: the ICTY's Krstić case concerned the alleged mass execution of 7,000–8,000 Bosnian Muslim men and the forcible transfer of about 25,000 women, children and elderly; the Trial Chamber found that genocide was committed.
  • Territorial division: Bosnia and Herzegovina consists of two Entities, the Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina and the Republika Srpska. In Cyprus, the UN has maintained a buffer zone since the hostilities of 1974.
  • Autonomy: Iraq's Kurdistan Region keeps its own authorities and laws made since 1992.
Mission 5

Outcomes: impacts, intervention, peace

  • Social and economic impacts: in Rwanda, more than one million people are estimated to have been killed in 1994; by the end of the 1980s some 480,000 Rwandans had become refugees.
  • International intervention: UN peacekeepers in Cyprus since 1964. In Rwanda, the UN force was cut from 2,165 to 270 in April 1994, as the killing went on. Interventions can help or fail.
  • Peace processes: paramilitary ceasefires (1994) led to the Good Friday Agreement on 10 April 1998 in Northern Ireland; the Dayton Agreement, signed 14 December 1995, ended the Bosnian War.
Quick check
?Which outcome is an example of territorial division rather than autonomy?
Mission check

?Two countries each have one ethnic majority. In Country A, the minority has guaranteed seats and regional self-government. In Country B, the minority is excluded from power, and its armed group holds part of the territory. Which outcome is most likely?

Outcomes depend less on how old the differences are than on how power is shared. Where groups are excluded, protest can escalate into civil war, terrorism or ethnic cleansing, ending in division or intervention. Where power is shared, through autonomy or peace processes, states more often stay whole.

  • A2-2.C3.ii nature of ethnic conflict: civil disobedience, civil war, terrorism, in the national case

Mission 6

Sri Lanka's people

  • 2012 census: Sinhalese about 75%, mostly Buddhist; Sri Lankan Tamils 11.2% and Indian Tamils 4.2%, mostly Hindu.
  • Muslims (Moors and Malays) are largely Tamil-speaking.
  • Northern Province: 93.8% Tamil. Eastern Province: Tamil 39.5%, Muslim 36.9%, Sinhalese 23.2%.
  • So language, religion and region overlap: the north and east are the Tamil-speaking heartland.
Mission 6

Causes

  • After independence in 1948, "ethnic and social tensions" grew; Tamil youth in the north (and Sinhalese youth in the south) began to oppose the government from the 1970s.
  • Territorial dispute: the LTTE, founded in 1976, from the late 1970s fought to establish Tamil Eelam in the north and east.
  • Human rights abuses: the 1979 Prevention of Terrorism Act and emergency rules gave security forces extraordinary powers; the state's response was marked by disappearances, unlawful killings and torture.
  • Cultural conflict and discrimination: language and religion divide the main groups (see the census figures above). A Netherlands government report, quoted by the UK Home Office, says recent governments pursued a Sinhalese-nationalist policy with "little room for the grievances of Tamils and Muslims".
Quick check
?The LTTE fought to create an independent Tamil Eelam in Sri Lanka's north and east. Which cause of ethnic conflict does this aim show most directly?
Mission 6

Nature: civil war and terrorism

  • Civil war: about 26 years, 1983–2009, between the government and the LTTE.
  • Terrorism: the LTTE used suicide bombings against military, political and civilian targets, and violence to silence other Tamil groups. The government proscribed the LTTE in January 2009.
  • State violence: Sri Lanka had one of the world's highest rates of disappearances by the state, plus unlawful killings and torture by both state and non-state actors.
  • Violence came from both sides: a UN investigation found reasonable grounds to believe security forces and the LTTE both unlawfully killed civilians.
Mission 6

A peace process that failed

  • February 2002: a ceasefire sponsored by Norway, watched by an international Sri Lanka Monitoring Mission.
  • It reduced armed clashes, but both sides used it to strengthen their positions, and violations continued.
  • July 2006: the government launched a large offensive in the east.
  • 2 January 2008: the government formally ended the ceasefire agreement.
Quick check
?Why did the 2002 ceasefire not lead to a lasting peace?
Mission 6

The end of the war: human impacts

  • 19 May 2009: the government claimed victory after the death of the LTTE's leadership.
  • In the final months, an estimated 300,000–330,000 civilians were trapped in a small area.
  • Civilian deaths are contested: a UN Panel of Experts cited credible sources estimating "as many as 40,000"; some government sources put it "well below 10,000"; other sources referred to over 70,000 people unaccounted for.
  • About 280,000 people leaving the area were held in military-run closed camps.
Quick check
?Estimates of civilian deaths at the end of Sri Lanka's war range from well below 10,000 to as many as 40,000. How should a geographer use these figures?
Mission 6

Outcomes: autonomy and intervention

  • No territorial division: no Tamil Eelam was created; the LTTE "no longer exists as an organised entity" (Australian government report, 2024).
  • Autonomy: the constitution provides a Provincial Council for every province. Tamil parties still campaign for devolution.
  • Language: Sinhala is the official language, and "Tamil shall also be an official language".
  • International intervention: a UN human rights investigation (2015) found violations by all sides and recommended a hybrid special court for alleged war crimes and crimes against humanity.
Mission 6

After 2009: is the conflict over?

  • Land: Sri Lanka's Ministry of Foreign Affairs says 90–92% of the land occupied by security forces in 2009 has been returned, but 3,754 acres were still occupied (quoted by the UK Home Office, 2025).
  • Security and discrimination: the UK Home Office (2025) reports a "heavy military presence in Tamil-majority areas" and that Tamils "continue to face discrimination in employment, education, and access to justice".
  • Reconciliation: an Office on Missing Persons, an Office for Reparations and an Office for National Unity and Reconciliation were set up.
  • Accountability: Human Rights Watch (2025) says the current president, like previous ones, "has not supported accountability" for violations in the 1983–2009 war (quoted by the UK Home Office).
  • Exam move (CCEA "Evaluate"): separate the war (ended 2009) from the conflict (causes and grievances), and judge each outcome by its evidence.
Mission check

?In another country, the army defeats a separatist rebel group. Language rights and regional self-government for the minority are still disputed. Using Sri Lanka, which judgement is best supported?

Sri Lanka's civil war ended in 2009, but its ethnic conflict did not simply stop. Territorial claims, human rights abuses, land, security, devolution and accountability remain disputed, so a military victory has not yet become a full peace process.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Ethnicity: self-defined, multifaceted (ancestry, culture, religion, language, appearance); no single marker suffices (ONS).
  • Perceived identity varies with age (British 15% of 18–24s vs 37% of over-65s, NILT 2023), gender, concentration (Belfast 7.1% minority ethnic) and role.

Mission 2

  • Colonisation moved enslaved and contracted people to French territories; annexation made Algeria and the overseas territories part of France; international migration brought workers and families from former colonies and southern Europe.
  • 2025: 8.0 million immigrants (11.6%); 49.2% born in Africa; Algeria the top birth country (12.6%).

Mission 3

  • Bid rent: accessibility sets land values, which sort land uses (CBD offices and shops; housing further out).
  • Segregation has voluntary and involuntary causes; Singapore's quotas aim to keep neighbourhoods mixed.

Mission 4

  • Belfast 2021: Catholic 48.7%, Protestant and other Christian 36.4%; 16 of 60 wards ≥80% Catholic, Shankill 80.4% Protestant and other Christian; 7.1% in ethnic groups other than White.
  • Segregation maintained by interfaces and peace lines; multiculturalism through shared housing, integrated schools and racial equality policy. Outcomes: 50 of NI's 100 most deprived areas in Belfast (2017); divided space; mixed inner city.

Mission 5

  • Causes (discrimination, sectarianism, historical animosity, racism, territorial disputes, human rights abuses, cultural conflict) → nature (civil disobedience, civil war, terrorism) → outcomes (impacts, division, autonomy, ethnic cleansing, intervention, peace processes).

Mission 6

  • Causes: overlapping language, religion and region; Tamil territorial claim (Eelam); human rights abuses under emergency laws. Nature: 26-year civil war (1983–2009), terrorism by the LTTE, abuses by state forces.
  • Outcomes: failed 2002 ceasefire; contested civilian deaths (up to 40,000, by UN-cited estimates); camps; partial autonomy (Provincial Councils); UN investigation; land and accountability disputes after 2009.
