Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • AS1.1a.i drainage basin as an open system with inputs, outputs, stores and transfers of energy and matter
Mission 1

Systems: open and closed

  • A system has inputs, outputs, stores and flows (transfers) between stores.
  • Closed system: energy crosses the boundary, but matter does not. The global hydrological cycle is closed: Earth's water is neither gained nor lost.
  • Open system: energy and matter cross the boundary. A drainage basin is open: water enters as precipitation and leaves as river flow and evapotranspiration.
  • Energy: the Sun's energy evaporates water; gravity moves it downhill, turning potential energy into kinetic energy in the channel.
  • Matter: water, sediment and dissolved material enter, are stored and leave the basin.
Mission 1

Basin inputs and outputs

  • Input: precipitation. Its type (rain or snow), intensity and duration decide what happens next.
  • Outputs: evaporation and transpiration (together evapotranspiration), and channel flow (runoff) leaving at the mouth.
Mission 1

Stores

  • Interception: water held on leaves and stems.
  • Surface storage: puddles, ponds and lakes. Soil water: water held in the soil.
  • Groundwater: water in permeable rock (aquifers). Channel storage: water in the river itself.
  • Stores delay water on its way to the river.
Mission 1

Flows

  • Throughfall (drips through the canopy) and stemflow (runs down stems and trunks) carry intercepted water to the ground.
  • Infiltration: water enters the soil. Percolation: it moves down into the rock.
  • Overland flow: infiltration-excess flow when rain is more intense than the soil can absorb; saturated overland flow when the soil is already full.
  • Throughflow: downslope through the soil. Groundwater flow: slowly through rock to the channel (base flow). Channel flow: in the river.
Quick check
?Which route usually takes longest to carry rainwater to the river?
Mission 1

The water balance

  • P = Q + E ± ΔS: precipitation = runoff + evapotranspiration ± change in storage.
  • Over many years storage changes little, so E ≈ P − Q.
  • River Lambourn (234.1 km², on chalk): mean catchment rainfall 768 mm a year, 1962–2019.
  • Its mean flow, 1.743 m³/s, equals 235 mm of runoff a year spread over the catchment (our calculation).
Quick check
?Use the figures earlier in this mission. How much water does the Lambourn lose to evapotranspiration each year?
Mission 1

Groundwater feeds rivers

  • In a USGS study of 54 US streams, groundwater supplied on average 52% of streamflow, from 14% to 90%.
  • The Lambourn's chalk aquifer keeps it flowing: its flow is at least 0.77 m³/s for 95% of the time. For the Beult, a clay river in Kent, it is only 0.057 m³/s.
  • So how a basin stores water decides how its river behaves in dry spells.
Mission 1

Ice feeds rivers

  • In the upper Indus basin, glacier melt supplied 40.6% of total runoff (1998–2007); in the upper Ganges, 11.5%.
  • Meltwater arrives in the warm season, often when rain is scarce.
  • The river is the smallest, fastest store. Groundwater and ice are the big, slow stores that refill it.
Mission check

?After a dry month with almost no rain, the River Lambourn is still flowing. Which explanation is best?

The river is a tiny, fast store. It keeps flowing because big, slow stores drain into it: groundwater as base flow (on average 52% of flow in 54 US streams) and meltwater from ice and snow (40.6% of runoff in the upper Indus).

CCEA A-level Geography

Mission 2

The storm hydrograph

  • A storm hydrograph plots discharge against time after a rainfall event; rainfall is shown as bars.
  • Rising limb, peak discharge, falling (recession) limb.
  • Lag time: from peak rainfall to peak discharge.
  • Base flow (slow groundwater) lies beneath; storm flow (quickflow) sits on top.
Quick check
?What is lag time?
Mission 2

Two rivers, one storm

  • Beult, Kent: 277.1 km². Lambourn, Berkshire: 234.1 km².
  • 23 December 2013 catchment rainfall: Beult 42.6 mm; Lambourn 44.9 mm.
  • Beult: daily mean flow rose from 32.0 to 79.4 m³/s on 24 December, the highest daily mean in its record from 1958 (the record has gaps).
  • Lambourn: from 1.42 to 2.39 m³/s. It peaked only on 15 February 2014, at 9.53 m³/s, after a very wet January and February.
Mission 2

Chalk and clay

  • The Lambourn drains the Chalk: about 97% of its bedrock is highly permeable. Rain infiltrates and percolates, then reaches the river slowly as base flow.
  • The Beult drains a "predominantly clay catchment": about 74% of its bedrock has low permeability. Rain runs off quickly as overland flow and throughflow.
  • Drainage density (NRFA index): Beult 1.11, Lambourn 0.17. More channels carry water away faster.
  • Base flow index: Lambourn 0.97, Beult 0.23.
Quick check
?The Beult's mean flow is 2.126 m³/s. How many times its mean flow was its peak of 79.4 m³/s?
Mission 2

Physical factors

  • Basin size: large basins have longer lag times. Shape: in a round basin water from all parts arrives together, giving a higher, earlier peak.
  • Relief: steep slopes speed runoff. Soil and geology: clay and impermeable rock shed water; sand, chalk and limestone absorb it.
  • Drainage density: more channels per km² means a faster, higher peak.
  • Precipitation: intense rain exceeds infiltration capacity; snow is stored until it melts. Antecedent conditions: the Beult was already high after rain on 16–22 December.
  • Vegetation: interception and transpiration reduce and delay runoff.
Mission 2

Human factors

  • Urbanisation: impermeable roofs and roads, plus drains, cut infiltration. Lag time shortens and the peak rises.
  • Land use: deforestation and bare, compacted farmland speed runoff; woodland and permanent grass slow it.
Quick check
?Which basin would have the shortest lag time and highest peak after the same storm?
Mission 2

Annual regimes: seasonal change

  • A river regime is the pattern of discharge through an average year.
  • Monthly mean flows (calculated from NRFA daily data): the Beult peaks in January (5.53 m³/s) and is lowest in July (0.22). The Lambourn peaks in March (2.59) and is lowest in September–October (1.11, 1.10).
  • Monthly rainfall varies much less: 47–84 mm on the Beult, 53–79 mm on the Lambourn.
Mission 2

Why regimes differ

  • Summer flows fall mainly because evapotranspiration is higher, not because rain stops.
  • Clay (Beult): little storage, so flow follows the season closely.
  • Chalk (Lambourn): the aquifer fills in winter and drains slowly, so the peak comes late, in March, and summer flow stays high.
  • In cold climates, snowmelt gives a spring or summer peak, and frozen ground acts like an impermeable surface.
Mission 2

A cold-climate regime: the Yukon

  • Yukon River at Pilot Station, Alaska: mean flow is lowest in April (about 1,410 m³/s) and highest in June (about 16,200 m³/s), about 11 times more (calculated from USGS monthly data, 1975–2026).
  • "Most of the flow occurs in the summer months from snowmelt, rainfall, and glacial melt".
  • Glacier-fed tributaries (the Tanana and White) give 29% of the Yukon's flow; the Porcupine and Koyukuk, on continuous permafrost, give 22%.
  • In winter, precipitation is stored as snow and ice, so flow falls to its minimum.
Mission check

?Two basins of similar size receive the same 40 mm storm. Basin X is on permeable limestone (base flow index 0.9); Basin Y is on clay (0.3). Which pair of hydrographs is most likely?

Geology decided it. The Beult's clay (base flow index 0.23) sent the rain straight to the river: 79.4 m³/s, about 37 times its mean flow. The Lambourn's chalk (0.97) soaked it up; its groundwater kept rising through a wet winter and it peaked on 15 February 2014. That February peak is a seasonal groundwater peak, not a storm lag time.

Mission 3

Four erosion processes

  • Hydraulic action: the force of moving water, including air forced into cracks in the bank.
  • Abrasion (corrasion): the load scrapes and grinds the bed and banks.
  • Attrition: particles in the load collide and become smaller and rounder.
  • Solution (corrosion): soluble rock, such as limestone, dissolves in the water.
  • Vertical erosion deepens the channel; lateral erosion widens it.
Quick check
?Pebbles on a riverbed get smaller and rounder downstream. Which process does this?
Mission 3

Four modes of transport

  • Traction: the largest particles roll or slide along the bed.
  • Saltation: smaller bed-load particles, such as sand, bounce along the bed.
  • The bed load "may only be moved during rapid flows under flood conditions".
  • Suspension: fine silt and clay carried within the flow, colouring the water.
  • Solution: dissolved material. On average it is about 15% of the mass a stream transports.
Mission 3

Competence, capacity and deposition

  • Competence: the largest particle a river can move. Capacity: the total load it can carry.
  • Both rise steeply as velocity rises, so most transport happens in high flows.
  • Deposition: when velocity falls, the largest particles are dropped first.
  • Velocity falls where the river enters a lake or the sea, on the inside of bends, and as a flood recedes.
Quick check
?A boulder 30 cm across moves only during floods, rolling along the bed. Which mode of transport is this?
Mission 3

The Hjulström curve

  • It plots particle size (mm) against velocity (cm/s); both axes are logarithmic.
  • Upper curve: the critical erosion velocity, needed to lift a particle from the bed.
  • Lower curve: the settling (fall) velocity; below it, the particle is deposited.
  • Between them, particles already moving stay in transport.
  • Read values as approximate: versions of the curve differ.
Mission 3

Reading the curve

  • In one textbook reading, a 1 mm sand grain is eroded at around 20 cm/s and stays in suspension until velocity drops below about 10 cm/s.
  • A 0.01 mm silt grain needs about 60 cm/s to be eroded.
  • Fine grains, especially clay, "stick together": they are cohesive. They also lie in a thin layer of slow water at the bed, so turbulence barely reaches them.
  • So the erosion curve dips to a minimum in the sand range and rises again for silt and clay.
Quick check
?A river slows from 30 cm/s to 5 cm/s as it enters a lake. What is most likely?
Mission 3

Where and when processes happen

  • Upper course: steep gradient and large, angular load; vertical erosion dominates.
  • Lower course: more discharge and finer load; lateral erosion and deposition dominate.
  • Seasons: on the Ganges at Patna in 1980, about 88% of the year's sediment load moved in the monsoon, which carried 76% of the year's water.
  • Exam move: name the process, the particle size, the velocity read from the curve, and where or when it happens.
Quick check
?Using the curve, which particles need the lowest velocity to be eroded?
Mission check

?A river flows at 40 cm/s over a bed of stiff clay. Sand grains bounce along on top, but the clay bed is not eroded. Why?

Fine grains are cohesive and sheltered in the slow layer at the bed, so the Hjulström curve rises again for silt and clay. In one reading, a 0.01 mm silt grain needs about 60 cm/s to be eroded, three times the 20 cm/s for a 1 mm sand grain.

Mission 4

How a waterfall forms

  • A band of resistant rock lies over weaker rock.
  • The weaker rock erodes faster, leaving the resistant rock as an overhanging caprock.
  • Hydraulic action and abrasion in the plunge pool undercut the face; the unsupported caprock collapses.
  • Repeated collapse makes the waterfall retreat upstream, leaving a steep-sided gorge.
Mission 4

Niagara Falls

  • Resistant dolostones and limestones of the Lockport Group lie over weaker shales and limestones, such as the Rochester Shale.
  • About 12,000 years ago water found a single low path; since then the falls have migrated about 11 km (7 miles) upstream.
  • Over the last 500+ years, retreat was about 0.9–1.5 m (3–5 feet) a year. It is now estimated at 0.3 m (1 foot), largely because water is withdrawn for hydroelectric power.
Quick check
?Using the figures just taught (1 mile = 1.609 km), what was Niagara's average rate of retreat over 12,000 years?
Mission 4

Rapids

  • Rapids are steep, shallow, turbulent stretches where the bed is broken by rock steps.
  • They form where bands of resistant and weaker rock alternate along the bed: the weaker bands erode faster, leaving a series of small steps.
  • They can also form where a waterfall has been worn down, or downstream of one as it retreats.
  • Turbulence increases hydraulic action and abrasion, so rapids slowly erode.
Mission 4

Pools and riffles

  • Riffles: shallow sections with coarse gravel; at low flow the water is fast and broken.
  • Pools: deeper sections with finer sediment; at low flow the water is slow.
  • They alternate down a channel. The line of fastest flow (thalweg) swings from side to side between them.
  • One explanation of meanders: this sideways swing erodes the banks, so pools develop on the outside of bends and riffles between bends.
Mission 4

Meanders

  • On a bend the thalweg swings to the outer bank. A corkscrew current (helicoidal flow) carries sediment across the bed to the inner bank.
  • Outer bank: erosion forms a steep river cliff. Inner bank: deposition forms a point bar.
  • Cross-section: deep on the outside of the bend, shallow on the inside.
  • Over time the channel "migrates around within its flood plain".
Mission 4

Oxbow lakes

  • Erosion on the outsides of two neighbouring bends narrows the neck of land between them.
  • In a flood the river cuts through the neck and takes the shorter, steeper route.
  • Deposition seals off the old bend, leaving an oxbow lake, which slowly fills with sediment and vegetation.
Quick check
?Where on a meander would you find a point bar?
Mission 4

Floodplains and levees

  • A floodplain is the wide, flat valley floor that floods. It grows sideways as meanders migrate and point bars build (lateral accretion), and upwards as floods leave layers of alluvium (vertical accretion).
  • When a river overflows, velocity falls sharply at the bank edge, so the coarsest sediment is dropped first, beside the channel.
  • Flood after flood, this builds raised banks: natural levees. Finer silt and clay settle further out.
Quick check
?A river floods and spreads across its floodplain. Where is the coarsest sediment deposited?
Mission 4

How a delta forms

  • A delta forms where a river enters a sea or lake, slows, and deposits sediment faster than waves and tides remove it.
  • "The formation of a delta depends on the interaction between the river flow and sediment supply", and on the waves and tides that redistribute it.
  • In salt water, fine clay particles clump together (flocculation) and settle faster.
  • The river splits into distributaries. Sediment builds out in bottomset, foreset and topset beds.
Mission 4

Arcuate and bird's-foot deltas

  • Bird's-foot: where wave and tide action is weak, distributaries extend far out to sea, each lined by its own levees. The Mississippi is the classic example: river-dominated, it can "grow out into a bird foot type of delta".
  • Arcuate: a fan-shaped delta with a curved, smooth front. Waves are strong enough to rework sediment along the front, so distributaries do not extend far.
  • Where waves dominate, the shoreline is smoother still, with beaches and dunes: "symmetrically cuspate", like the São Francisco in Brazil.
  • The shape shows the balance between sediment supply and wave and tide energy.
Quick check
?Which conditions are most likely to produce a bird's-foot delta?
Mission check

?A river crosses a band of hard sandstone lying on top of soft shale and falls 20 m into a plunge pool. Which sequence explains how this waterfall moves upstream?

Resistant caprock over weaker rock is undercut in the plunge pool until it collapses. Repeated collapse moved Niagara 11 km (7 miles) in about 12,000 years, about 0.94 m a year on average; taking water for hydropower has slowed it to about 0.3 m a year.

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

Mission 5

Urbanisation

  • Impermeable surfaces (roofs, roads, car parks) cut infiltration and increase overland flow.
  • Drains and sewers carry water to rivers quickly.
  • Result: shorter lag time, steeper rising limb, higher peak discharge.
  • Building on floodplains also puts more people and property in the way (exposure).
Mission 5

Hurricane Harvey, Houston, 2017

  • Harvey stalled over or near the Texas coast "for four days"; the highest storm total was about 1,540 mm (60.58 inches), near Nederland, Texas.
  • A study in Nature found urbanisation "exacerbated not only the flood response but also the storm total rainfall".
  • It estimated that urbanisation made such extreme floods in the basins studied about 21 times as likely, on average (25–30 August 2017).
  • One modelling study of one event: strong evidence, but test it against others.
Quick check
?A housing estate is planned upstream of a town. Which design would best stop it raising flood peaks downstream?
Mission check

?Over 20 years, a small rural catchment becomes 40% covered by housing and roads with conventional storm drains. Rainfall does not change. What happens to its storm hydrograph, and why?

Impermeable surfaces and drains cut infiltration and lag time and raise the peak. In Houston, urbanisation also increased storm rainfall, making Harvey-scale flooding about 21 times as likely in the basins studied.

Mission 6

Why channelise a river?

  • Channelisation: changing a river's channel to control where and how fast water flows.
  • Reasons: protect towns and farmland from flooding, drain wet land for farming, keep a channel deep enough for navigation, stop banks eroding.
  • It is hard engineering: it works against the river's natural behaviour.
Mission 6

Realignment

  • Realignment: straightening a meandering river, often by cutting through meander necks.
  • A shorter course over the same drop gives a steeper gradient, so water leaves the reach faster.
  • Kissimmee River, Florida: it "once meandered for 103 miles". After flooding in 1947, it was turned into a dredged straight channel in the 1960s.
Mission 6

Re-sectioning

  • Re-sectioning: reshaping the cross-section, usually wider and deeper, often with smooth or concrete-lined banks.
  • A bigger cross-sectional area holds more water before it overtops the banks.
  • A larger hydraulic radius and smoother bed mean less friction, so the water flows faster.
Mission 6

Dredging

  • Dredging: digging sediment out of the bed to deepen the channel and raise its capacity.
  • The Kissimmee work involved "cutting and dredging a 30-feet-deep straightaway".
  • Sediment keeps arriving from upstream, so dredging must be repeated: an ongoing cost.
Quick check
?Engineers dig out silt from a river bed every few years so that barges can pass. Which channelisation method is this?
Mission 6

The costs of hard channels

  • Faster flow moves the flood problem downstream: water reaches the next town sooner.
  • Straight, uniform channels lose pools, riffles and wetland edges: habitat loss.
  • Kissimmee: channelisation "destroyed much of a floodplain-dependent ecosystem". A restoration project to put back much of the old river was completed in July 2021.
  • So many managers now ask for strategies that work with the river.
Mission 6

Washlands

  • A washland is floodplain land set aside to flood, storing water so less reaches towns downstream.
  • Ouse Washes, Cambridgeshire: "the largest area of washland in the UK".
  • It is "grazing pasture that floods in the winter", which "creates a rich habitat" for waterfowl and wading birds.
  • Land keeps a use (grazing) and gains a second (flood storage and wildlife).
Mission 6

Afforestation

  • Afforestation: planting trees in the catchment. Trees intercept rain and help water soak into the soil, lengthening lag time.
  • FAO: forests "can reduce flooding from frequent, low-intensity, short-duration storms", but "cannot stop catastrophic large-scale floods".
  • Pickering, North Yorkshire: 44 ha of woodland, 167 leaky woody dams, 187 heather bale dams, two timber bunds and a large flood storage area.
  • Result: the yearly chance of the town flooding fell from 25% to less than 4%.
Mission 6

Land-use zoning

  • Land-use zoning: mapping flood risk and deciding which land uses go where.
  • England: planners use flood zones based on flood probability; the river design flood is a 1% chance each year (1 in 100).
  • A Sequential Test steers new development to the lowest-risk land first. Floodplains are kept for low-value uses: farmland, parks, playing fields.
  • Insurance backs this up: Flood Re only covers homes "built before 1st January 2009", so it does not reward new building on floodplains.
Quick check
?A council refuses permission for 200 new houses on a floodplain and zones the land as playing fields. Which strategy is this?
Mission 6

The Netherlands: why act?

  • High water in 1993 and 1995, when more than 250,000 residents had to leave, was "the direct reason" for a new programme.
  • The design flood for the Rhine: 16,000 m³/s at Lobith, on the German border, a 1 in 1,250 chance each year.
  • The aim: "give rivers space again" rather than only raising dikes.
Mission 6

Room for the River

  • Measures at 34 places along the Rhine branches; total cost €2.3 billion; officially completed in early 2019.
  • Types: dike relocation, floodplain lowering, high-water channels, depoldering (returning reclaimed land to the river) and removing obstacles.
  • Nijmegen: the dike was moved 350 m inland to make room for a side channel 3 km long. Flood levels fell by 35 cm; cost about €351 million.
  • The cost to people: about 50 houses in Lent were demolished.
Quick check
?Which statement best explains why the Nijmegen scheme counts as environmentally sensitive management?
Mission check

?A riverside town wants to cut its flood levels the way the Dutch did after 1995, without sending more water to towns downstream. Which plan follows the same approach?

The Dutch gave the rivers room: dikes moved back, floodplains lowered, side channels dug (Room for the River, 34 places, €2.3 billion). Channelisation speeds water away; washlands, afforestation and zoning store it, slow it or keep people out of its way.

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

Mission 7

The place: a wetland that is farmed

  • Somerset Levels and Moors, south-west England (an MEDC case).
  • About 35,000 ha of floodplain of the rivers Axe, Brue, Parrett and Tone.
  • Most of it is "only a few metres above mean sea level".
  • It drains through a large network of ditches, rhynes, drains and rivers.
  • Flooding "may affect large areas in winter depending on rainfall and tidal conditions".
Mission 7

Cause 1: a record wet winter

  • The Met Office reported winter 2013/14 as "the wettest winter in England and Wales since at least 1766".
  • UK precipitation: 165% of average.
  • A "large number of low pressure centres (storms)" crossed the UK.
  • Result: saturated soils, high groundwater and exceptionally high river flows.
Mission 7

Cause 2: low, flat land next to a tidal river

  • Land a few metres above sea level gives water little gradient to drain away.
  • Water leaves the Levels by the tidal River Parrett to the sea, so tidal conditions matter as well as rain.
  • Silt enters the Parrett and Tone from the sea.
  • Water also arrives from upstream: "water is coming from all over the place—Taunton, Yeovil, Glastonbury" (Association of Drainage Authorities).
Mission 7

Cause 3: channels that carried less

  • The Flood Action Plan says dredging the Parrett and Tone could "restore the system to the channel capacity put in place in the 1960s".
  • So the channels were carrying less than their 1960s design.
  • Dredging was identified locally as key to "reducing the duration of flooding".
  • But: "changed land management practices would not have lessened the impact of an event on the scale of the floods this year".
  • Physical causes set the scale; human factors (channel capacity) affected how long water stayed.
Quick check
?Which cause best explains why the floodwater on the Levels lasted so long, rather than why it began?
Mission 7

How long it lasted

  • Flooding began in mid-December 2013.
  • A soil study found flooding of "up to 3 months with floodwater depths up to 3 m" in low-lying farmland such as the Somerset Levels.
  • Across the UK, inland flooding lasted in some areas "until the end of April 2014".
Mission 7

Effects on people

  • "Over 200 homes in several communities have been cut off, some for more than two months".
  • Several A roads blocked; strategic road and rail routes into the South West peninsula cut.
  • 57 BT telecommunications boxes under water; waste water treatment "not possible for some isolated communities".
  • Initial estimate of these transport and infrastructure impacts: "in the region of £100m".
Mission 7

Effects on property

  • On the Levels and Moors, "over 150 properties are now flooded internally" (Flood Action Plan, early 2014).
  • 44 km of damaged roads to repair and resurface (planned cost £1.6m).
  • National context, winter 2013–14: "over 7,000 properties were flooded" (Environment Agency period figure reported by Parliament).
Mission 7

Effects on land

  • 11,000 ha of farmland still under water when the Plan was written (= 110 km²; our conversion).
  • Farming employs 10,000 Somerset residents and is worth about £200m a year.
  • In flooded fields, a soil study found bulk density down 19%, pH down 0.4 units, available phosphorus down by up to 42%, fewer earthworms; arable crop biomass down 19–34%.
  • Winter crops were destroyed, but most soil indicators recovered within a year.
  • Wildlife: reintroduced cranes were restricted to "a small partially unflooded area".
Quick check
?Which effect is an effect on land, not on people or property?
Quick check
?Which conclusion about the effects on farmland is best supported by the soil study?
Mission check

?Another floodplain is just as low and flat as the Levels and floods after an equally wet winter. Unlike the Levels, it drains to a large non-tidal lake, through channels recently dredged to full capacity. Compared with the Levels in 2014, what would you expect?

Low relief alone does not decide duration: the Levels flooded because a record wet winter fell on low, flat land draining to a tidal river through channels with less capacity than in the 1960s; that is why water stayed for months, cutting off 200+ homes and drowning 11,000 ha of farmland.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A drainage basin is an open system of inputs, stores, flows and outputs inside the closed global cycle; P = Q + E ± ΔS.

Mission 2

  • Hydrograph shape depends on basin size, shape, relief, soil, geology, drainage density, precipitation, antecedent conditions and land use; the Beult (clay) and Lambourn (chalk) show geology at work.
  • Regimes show seasonal change: ET in temperate basins, snowmelt and frozen ground in the Yukon.

Mission 3

  • Rivers erode by hydraulic action, abrasion (corrasion), attrition and solution (corrosion), and transport by traction, saltation, suspension and solution.
  • The Hjulström curve links particle size to the velocities for erosion, transport and deposition; sand is the easiest to erode.

Mission 4

  • Waterfalls and rapids form where rocks of different resistance meet; pools, riffles and the thalweg start meanders, which build point bars, oxbow lakes and floodplains.
  • Levees form from coarse sediment dropped at the channel edge; delta shape (bird's-foot or arcuate) reflects sediment supply against wave and tide energy.

Mission 5

  • Urbanisation cuts infiltration, and drains carry water to rivers quickly: lag time shortens and peaks rise.

Mission 6

  • Channelisation (realignment, re-sectioning, dredging) moves water faster, but shifts flooding downstream, destroys habitat and needs maintenance.
  • Washlands, afforestation and land-use zoning work with the river; Room for the River gave the Rhine space and cut Nijmegen's flood level by 35 cm.

Mission 7

  • Causes: record rain (wettest winter in England and Wales since at least 1766; UK 165% of average) on saturated ground; land a few metres above sea level; tidal outlet; silted channels below their 1960s capacity.
  • Effects: people (200+ homes cut off for up to two months; roads, rail, telecoms, sewage), property (150+ properties flooded internally), land (11,000 ha under water; short-term soil and crop damage, mostly recovered within a year).
  • Exam move: for "investigate the causes", separate physical causes (rain, relief, tides) from human ones (channel maintenance) and say which mattered for duration.
