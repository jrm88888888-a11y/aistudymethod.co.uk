Mission 1

Mission 1: Sikhs treat a book as their living Guru. What does that mean, and is it worship?

Make a guess: Sikhs treat a book as their living Guru. What does that mean, and is it worship?

Mission 1

The Word is the Guru

  • Guru: often explained as the teacher who leads from darkness to light. Sikhs believe God is the true Guru (Satguru), who speaks through the Word (shabad, bani).
  • The Word is the Guru, and the Guru is the Word; in the Word is all nectar (GGS 982, paraphrase).
  • The ten Gurus carried one light; since 1708 the Word in the scripture is the Guru.
  • So Sikhs speak of the Guru Granth Sahib, not "the Sikh Bible".
Mission 1

Treated as a living Guru

  • Placed on a raised throne (takht or manji sahib), under a canopy, wrapped in cloths (rumalas).
  • A chaur (fan) is waved over it, as over a king.
  • Prakash: opened at dawn, often carried in on the head. Sukhasan: closed at night and carried to its resting room.
  • Everyone covers the head, removes shoes, bows and sits lower than the Guru.
  • No image of God or the Gurus is worshipped: the Word is at the centre.
Mission 1

In worship and ceremony

  • All worship happens in its presence: kirtan (singing its hymns), katha (sermon on a hymn), Ardas (prayer).
  • Akhand path: unbroken reading of all 1,430 pages, about 48 hours, by a relay of readers.
  • Naming: a name begins with the first letter of a hymn opened at random.
  • Anand Karaj (marriage): the couple walks four times around it while the Lavan (GGS 773–774) are sung.
  • Amrit initiation and funerals (Kirtan Sohila; a reading completed afterwards) take place in its presence.
  • Your spec: all ceremonies and rites of passage "in its presence".
Mission 1Read the source

Vak lao: taking the Guru's word

  • Vak lao ("take a saying"): open the Guru Granth Sahib at random; read the hymn at the top of the left-hand page (if it began on the page before, go back to its start).
  • This is the hukamnama: the Guru's command for the day, often written up for the sangat.
  • The daily hukamnama from the Harmandir, Amritsar, is shared worldwide.
  • Sikhs take a vak before decisions and at ceremonies, as advice from the living Guru.

Show answer

To receive the Guru's guidance rather than choose a passage to suit oneself.

Q2 How does this show the scripture is "more than a focal point"?

Show answer

Mission 1

Theological and legal status

  • Theological: the final and eternal Guru; the authority for teaching on God, faith and salvation; nothing may be added. Sermons explain it; they do not add to it.
  • Sikh law: the code of conduct (1950) says only the Guru Granth Sahib is installed as Guru in a gurdwara; it lays down how it is treated.
  • Your spec calls the code the Rahit Maryada.
  • Collective decisions (gurmatta) are made in its presence; the Akal Takht issues edicts before it.
  • State law: India's Supreme Court (2000) ruled that the Guru Granth Sahib is a juristic person, able to hold property.
Mission 1Diversity

Showing respect today

  • Some families keep a Guru Granth Sahib at home in its own room, with daily prakash and sukhasan; many keep only a small prayer book (gutka) and go to the gurdwara.
  • Worn or damaged copies are respectfully cremated, not thrown away.
  • The Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee (SGPC, Amritsar) controls printing of the full text.
  • Debates: the use of translations and screens in worship, especially among young Sikhs abroad.
  • Practice varies, but all aim at the same respect: treating the Word as the Guru.
Mission 1Evaluate

"Sikhs worship the Guru Granth Sahib."

  • It can look like it: royal treatment, bowing, offerings, the chaur, rest at night.
  • It can look like it: some Sikh reformers warn against ritualism, e.g. paying for akhand paths without listening.
  • Against: Sikhs say they bow to the Guru's Word, through which God is known; worship is of God alone (Waheguru, "Wonderful Lord", the name Sikhs most use for God).
  • Against: the scripture itself rejects idol worship; it is consulted and obeyed, like a living teacher, not prayed to as a god.
  • Judge it by: is "worship" about the act or the intention? Does it function like an idol? How do Sikhs describe their own practice?
  • Your spec issue: "the extent to which the Guru Granth Sahib can be viewed as an object of worship".

Mission 2: Why do Sikhs start each day with a poem that says God cannot be grasped by thinking?

Make a guess: Why do Sikhs start each day with a poem that says God cannot be grasped by thinking?

Mission 2

Waheguru: one, wonderful

  • Waheguru: the name for God most used by Sikhs; roughly "Wonderful Lord" or "Wondrous Enlightener".
  • Sikhs repeat it in meditation; it is the Gurmantar given at Khalsa initiation.
  • Oneness: God is one, the only one, with no second and no rival.
  • The scripture begins with the numeral 1: Ik Onkar, "One God" (Guru Granth Sahib 1).
  • One God for all people, not a tribal God of Sikhs.
  • Your spec spells the opening passage Mul Mantra.
Mission 2Read the source

The Mool Mantar

Guru Granth Sahib 1, word by word (paraphrase):

  • Ik Onkar: one God, the one reality.
  • Satnam: Truth is God's name.
  • Karta Purakh: the Creator, present in creation.
  • Nirbhau, Nirvair: without fear, without hate.
  • Akal Murat: beyond time, undying.
  • Ajuni, Saibhang: never born; self-existent.
  • Gurprasad: known by the Guru's grace.
  • Sikhs believe Guru Nanak (1469–1539) first spoke it. Mool = root: the root of Sikh belief.

Show answer

Show answer

Mission 2

The Japji: shape and use

  • Japji Sahib, by Guru Nanak, opens the Guru Granth Sahib (pages 1–8).
  • Shape: Mool Mantar → opening verse → 38 pauris (stanzas) → closing verse (salok).
  • Personal meditation: the first of the daily prayers (Nitnem), recited in the early morning (amrit vela).
  • Initiation: Japji is the first of the five prayers recited as amrit is prepared; the Panj Piare recite the Mool Mantar and the initiates repeat it.
  • Worship: your spec calls it preparation for worship in the gurdwara and an epilogue to services.
  • Many Sikhs know it by heart.
Mission 2Read the source

Japji, pauris 1, 2 and 20

Guru Granth Sahib 1–4 (paraphrase):

  • Pauri 1: God is not reached by thinking, even a hundred thousand times; nor by silence; nor by piling up the world's goods. The way is to walk in God's hukam (will).
  • Many translations say "by thinking"; others read the word as ritual washing. Either way: no human technique reaches God.
  • Pauri 2: all things come to be by God's command; no one is outside it. Whoever understands it does not speak in haumai (ego).
  • Pauri 20: water washes dirty hands; but a mind stained by wrongdoing is cleansed only by love of the Name (nam).

Q1 According to pauri 20, what cleanses a mind stained by wrongdoing?

Show answer

Love of God's Name.

Q2 On the "by thinking" reading, why does pauri 1 reject thinking alone?

Show answer

God is beyond human reasoning; God is reached by living in God's will. On either reading, no human technique reaches God.

Mission 2

An awesome, liberating presence

  • Awe: Japji piles up the countless (asankh): countless worlds, devotees, scholars; even the time of creation is known only to the Creator (pauris 17–22, paraphrase).
  • Wonder at God's greatness, not argument, is the fitting response.
  • Closing verse: air is the Guru, water the father, earth the great mother; deeds are read out before Dharam, the righteous judge; those who meditated on the Name are freed, and many are freed with them (Guru Granth Sahib 8, paraphrase).
  • Liberating: release comes by grace and the Name, not by ritual (pilgrimage and austerity earn only "a sesame seed" of merit: pauri 21, paraphrase).
  • (Nam, hukam, grace and the five realms of pauris 34–37: next missions.)

Mission 2

A summary of Sikh belief

  • Mool Mantar: compact, creed-like; says who God is.
  • Japji: longer, poetic; says how to live towards God: hukam, nam, grace, the five khands, truthful living.
  • Together they are often called the summary of Sikh belief about God.
  • Sikh traditions differ on where the Mool Mantar ends: at Gurprasad (Sikh Rehat Maryada) or after the following verse "true… for ever" (some traditions, e.g. Damdami Taksal).
Mission 2Evaluate

"The Mool Mantar is more important than the Japji as a statement of Sikh faith."

  • For: it opens the scripture and almost every Sikh text and service; it is learned first and repeated at initiation.
  • For: short and memorable: a creed any Sikh can recite.
  • Against: the Mool Mantar is itself part of the Japji; they are not rivals.
  • Against: the Japji gives what a creed lacks: how to live (hukam, nam, truthful living) and the path to liberation.
  • Against: Japji itself warns that no human technique, whether thinking (or ritual washing), silence or cleverness, reaches God (pauri 1).
  • Judge it by: what "statement of faith" means (a definition or a way of life?); use in worship; content; Guru Nanak's own ordering.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Can a God "without qualities" also be a friend, a mother and a king?

Make a guess: Can a God "without qualities" also be a friend, a mother and a king?

Mission 3

Ik Onkar: one without a second

  • Ik Onkar: God is one; nothing exists beside God or outside God's power.
  • Before creation, Guru Nanak says, there was only darkness for countless ages: no earth, no sky, only God's hukam (will) (Guru Granth Sahib 1035, paraphrase).
  • Creation came from God's single word, and the whole expanse appeared (Japji pauri 16, paraphrase).
  • Your spec lists Adi Granth 929, 1035 and 1037 for Ik Onkar.
  • Symbol ੴ: the numeral 1 joined to Onkar, with an arc reaching outwards: one God, without end.
Mission 3

Nirgun and sargun

  • Nirgun: God without qualities: beyond form, beyond anything the mind can grasp.
  • Sargun: God with qualities: present and knowable in creation, the Word and the Guru.
  • One God, two ways of being known: God is nirgun and is also sargun (Sukhmani, Guru Granth Sahib 287, paraphrase).
  • Your spec spells these nirguna and saguna.
  • Sargun does not mean God is born as an avatar (Ajuni): God is present in creation, never incarnate in one body.
Mission 3

Beyond the world, within it

  • Transcendent: beyond creation, time and space (Akal, Nirankar: timeless, formless).
  • Immanent: present in everything: the light within all is God's light (Guru Granth Sahib 13, paraphrase).
  • Creator and sustainer: God makes, supports and watches over all life.
  • Omnipotent and omniscient: all-powerful (everything happens within hukam) and all-knowing.
  • Your spec lists Adi Granth 25, 684 and 700 for God as creator and sustainer.
Mission 3

A personal God

  • Sikh scripture speaks to God as you: you are my father, my mother, my kin and my brother (Guru Granth Sahib 103, paraphrase).
  • God is also friend, beloved husband (the soul as bride), True King (Sacha Patshah).
  • So the formless God can be loved and spoken to.
  • Your spec lists Adi Granth 784 and 1190 for God as personal.
  • These images are ways of relating, not claims that God has a body or gender.

Mission 3Evaluate

"The most important Sikh teaching about God is that God is personal."

  • For: scripture addresses God as father, mother, friend and beloved; devotion and nam simran assume a God who can be loved.
  • For: a personal God gives meaning to grace (nadar) and to prayer.
  • Against: Ik Onkar comes first in the Mool Mantar: oneness is the root of every other teaching, including equality.
  • Against: God is also nirgun; stressing the personal could shrink God to human terms.
  • Judge it by: order and emphasis in the Mool Mantar; what shapes Sikh practice most; whether personal and nirgun teachings can be ranked at all.

The Sikh God is beyond every image, yet close enough to be called mother, friend and king; how you explain that shapes how you live.

Mission 4: Why did the Guru who built the Golden Temple die at the hands of an emperor?

Make a guess: Why did the Guru who built the Golden Temple die at the hands of an emperor?

Mission 4

Gurus 2 to 4 build a community

  • Guru Angad (Guru 1539–1552): tradition credits him with shaping the Gurmukhi script used for the hymns; with his wife Mata Khivi, the free kitchen (langar).
  • Guru Amar Das (1552–1574): at Goindval, "first langar, then the Guru": everyone, even (tradition says) Emperor Akbar, ate sitting together.
  • He set up manjis (preaching districts), spoke against sati and the veiling of women, and wrote Anand Sahib.
  • Guru Ram Das (1574–1581): founded Ramdaspur, later Amritsar, and began its pool; wrote the Lavan (wedding hymns).
  • Each Guru was chosen for service, not birth; from Ram Das on, from one family (the Sodhis).
Mission 4

Guru Arjan: a time of growth

  • Guru Arjan (1563–1606; Guru 1581–1606), youngest son of Ram Das. His elder brother Prithi Chand disputed the succession.
  • Missionary journeys in the Majha region (central Punjab); many Jat farmers joined the Panth.
  • Dasvandh: Sikhs gave a tenth of income, collected by agents (masands), to fund langar, building and the poor.
  • Founded towns (e.g. Tarn Taran) and supported a leprosy refuge there (tradition).
  • He safeguarded Nanak's principles: one God, the Name, equality, honest work; and gave the Panth a centre and a scripture.
  • His example joins spiritual devotion with temporal organisation.
Mission 4

The Harmandir

  • Built from 1588 in the pool at Amritsar; tradition says the Sufi Mian Mir of Lahore laid the foundation stone.
  • Lower than the surrounding ground: worshippers step down, showing humility before God.
  • Four doors, one on each side: open to all four directions, all castes, everyone.
  • Reached by a causeway across the sarovar (pool); the scripture installed at the centre in 1604: the Word, not an image.
  • The gold covering came later (early 19th century, Maharaja Ranjit Singh): hence "Golden Temple".

Mission 4

The Adi Granth, 1604

  • Guru Arjan compiled the Adi Granth ("first book") with Bhai Gurdas as scribe.
  • It contains hymns of the first five Gurus and of bhagats, Hindu and Muslim, whose teaching agreed with the Gurus'.
  • Installed in the Harmandir in 1604, with Baba Buddha as the first reader (granthi).
  • Why: rival hymns were circulating under Nanak's name (from Prithi Chand's family); the Panth needed an authentic text.
  • Your spec: compiled "to promote truth, peace and contemplation". Compilation debates.
Mission 4Read the source

Sukhmani and Guru Arjan's vision

  • Sukhmani (GGS 262–296), "jewel of peace"; your spec spells it Sukhamni. 24 sections (ashtapadis), each a short verse then eight stanzas.
  • It opens by bowing to the primal Guru, the Guru through the ages, the True Guru, the divine Guru (GGS 262, paraphrase).
  • Its theme: remembering the Name brings peace and frees the mind from fear and pain (paraphrase).
  • GGS 1136: I keep neither the Hindu fast nor Ramadan; I serve the One who protects all. I am neither Hindu nor Muslim; my body and breath belong to the One God, called Allah and Ram (paraphrase).
  • Your spec also cites Adi Granth 396 (Asa M5): the Guru offers himself as God's servant, trusting God alone, not human cleverness or other supports (paraphrase).

Q1 What does Sukhmani say brings peace?

Show answer

Remembering God's Name.

Q2 What does GGS 1136 reject, and what does it keep?

Show answer

It rejects religious labels and set rituals; it keeps devotion to the One God.

Mission 4

The martyrdom of Guru Arjan, 1606

  • Emperor Jahangir (ruled 1605–1627). Arjan was said to have blessed Jahangir's rebel son Khusrau.
  • Jahangir's memoirs: the Guru at Goindval had drawn many Hindus and even Muslims; Jahangir had long wished to end this "shop", and ordered his death and the seizure of his property (paraphrase).
  • Sikh tradition: in Lahore he was seated on a red-hot iron plate and burning sand was poured over him; then taken to the river Ravi, where he died.
  • He accepted God's will: Your will is sweet to me (GGS 394, paraphrase).
  • Sikhs remember him as the first Sikh martyr (shaheed).
Mission 4Diversity

Why was Guru Arjan killed?

  • Religious persecution (Sikh tradition): he refused to change his scripture or convert; his death shows the cost of faith under tyranny.
  • Political (Jahangir's own words): the Khusrau blessing and a growing following the emperor saw as a threat.
  • Personal enmities (Sikh tradition): Chandu Shah, a Lahore official, and the rival Prithi Chand.
  • Some historians also cite a Naqshbandi Sufi leader, Ahmad Sirhindi, whose letter welcomed the execution.
  • Most historians combine causes; many Sikhs see in all of them the same meaning: standing firm in truth.
Mission 4

Hargobind to Har Krishan

  • Guru Hargobind (Guru 1606–1644), aged 11 when his father died: wore two swords, miri (worldly power) and piri (spiritual authority).
  • Built the Akal Takht, kept horsemen, and fought Mughal forces in the 1620s–30s; imprisoned at Gwalior and released.
  • Guru Har Rai (1644–1661): kept the army but avoided war; ran a herbal dispensary.
  • Guru Har Krishan (1661–1664): Guru at five; died of smallpox in Delhi, caring for the sick.
  • Arjan's death is often seen as the turning point: the Panth learns to defend itself.
Mission 4

The martyrdom of Guru Tegh Bahadur, 1675

  • Guru Tegh Bahadur (1621–1675; Guru 1664–1675): found at Bakala by the trader Makhan Shah; founded Anandpur; his hymns are in the GGS.
  • Sikh tradition (the Bachittar Natak in the Dasam Granth, and later Sikh sources): Kashmiri Pandits (Brahmins), facing forced conversion under Emperor Aurangzeb, asked his help, and he told them: if the Guru converts, they will.
  • He was arrested, refused to convert and was beheaded in Delhi (Gurdwara Sis Ganj marks the place).
  • Companions Bhai Mati Das, Bhai Sati Das and Bhai Dayal Das were killed before him.
  • Sikhs remember him as dying for the freedom of a faith not his own, and call him Hind di Chadar, "shield of India".
  • Mughal court sources present him as a political threat; historians debate the causes.

Mission 4Evaluate

"Guru Arjan's greatest contribution was the Adi Granth."

  • For: the Adi Granth became the Guru in 1708; it fixed the authentic hymns and still unites Sikhs worldwide.
  • For: including bhagats set a lasting model of openness to truth wherever it is found.
  • Against: the Harmandir gave the Panth a centre and a symbol of equality and humility.
  • Against: his martyrdom shaped Sikh identity: readiness to suffer, then (with Hargobind) to defend.
  • Against: dasvandh and the masands organised a growing Panth.
  • Judge it by: lasting effect today? effect in his own time? Can the contributions be separated at all?

Two martyrdoms framed the century from Arjan to Tegh Bahadur. Sikhs remember both with service and with a readiness to defend others.

Mission 5

Punjab before Ranjit Singh: the misls

  • After Banda Singh Bahadur's death (1716), Sikhs survived decades of persecution.
  • They organised in misls: about twelve bands, each led by a sardar (chief), together forming the Dal Khalsa (army of the Khalsa).
  • They met at the Akal Takht in Sarbat Khalsa assemblies and passed gurmatas (resolutions in the Guru's presence).
  • Afghan invasions under Ahmad Shah Abdali (from 1747) devastated Punjab; the Harmandir was destroyed in 1762 and rebuilt.
  • By the 1790s the Afghans were weaker, but the misls fought each other. Punjab had no single ruler.
Mission 5

Background and early life

  • Born 13 November 1780 at Gujranwala (now in Pakistan), son of Maha Singh, chief of the Sukerchakia misl.
  • Smallpox in infancy left him blind in his left eye and his face scarred.
  • Maha Singh died in 1790 (some sources say 1792); Ranjit, still a boy, inherited the misl.
  • Married into the rival Kanhaiya misl; his mother-in-law Sada Kaur, a skilled commander, became his key early ally.
  • He had little formal schooling but was a superb horseman, soldier and judge of people.
Mission 5

From misl chief to Maharaja

  • July 1799: takes Lahore, the old capital of Punjab.
  • 12 April 1801 (Vaisakhi): proclaimed Maharaja of Punjab.
  • 1802: takes Amritsar, the Sikh holy city.
  • 1809: the Treaty of Amritsar with the British East India Company fixes the river Sutlej as his south-eastern limit; he expands north and west instead.
  • 1818 Multan · 1819 Kashmir · 1834 Peshawar.
  • Political sovereignty: for the first time in centuries, Punjab was one independent state ruled from Lahore. He became known as Sher-e-Punjab, the Lion of Punjab.

Mission 5

Sarkar-i-Khalsa: a state in the Khalsa's name

  • He called his government Sarkar-i-Khalsa (the Khalsa government) and his court the Darbar Khalsa.
  • He took the title Maharaja but avoided acting as a king above the Panth: he is said to have refused to sit on a throne, holding court cross-legged.
  • His coins, the Nanakshahi, were struck in the name of Guru Nanak and Guru Gobind Singh, not his own.
  • Tradition: when he married Moran, a Muslim dancer, the Akal Takht's leader Akali Phula Singh summoned him and sentenced him to a flogging; he submitted, and was forgiven.
  • So he presented his rule as the Khalsa's rule under the Guru, with himself as its servant.
  • Your spec calls this "the founding of the Khalsa": here the Khalsa state, not the Khalsa of 1699.
Mission 5Read the source

The Nanakshahi coin

  • The Persian couplet on his coins (paraphrase): Deg, Tegh, Fateh: the cauldron (food for all), the sword (power), victory and unfailing help, received from Guru Nanak and Guru Gobind Singh.
  • Deg (cooking pot) stands for langar and generosity; Tegh (sword) for protection of the weak.
  • The same words were used on the seal of Banda Singh Bahadur a century earlier.

Q2 Which two duties does the coin give the ruler?

Mission 5

A golden age for Sikhism

  • The Harmandir (Golden Temple) rebuilt in marble and its upper storeys covered in gilded copper: hence "Golden Temple".
  • Takhts built or rebuilt under his patronage: Patna Sahib (birthplace of Guru Gobind Singh) and Hazur Sahib, Nanded (where Guru Gobind Singh died), though Nanded lay outside his realm.
  • Gurdwaras endowed with land; Sikh art, manuscripts and music flourished at court.
  • A modern Khalsa army (trained partly by European officers) made Punjab secure; famous Sikh generals such as Hari Singh Nalwa.
  • For many Sikhs, his reign is the high point of Sikh political power: Raj karega Khalsa ("the Khalsa shall rule") come true.
Mission 5

Religious freedom and no discrimination

  • Most of his subjects were Muslims and Hindus; Sikhs were a minority.
  • Ministers of all faiths: Fakir Azizuddin (Muslim, foreign minister), Dhian Singh (Hindu Dogra, prime minister), Dina Nath (Hindu, finance), Mian Ghausa (Muslim, artillery).
  • Muslim qazis judged Muslims' personal cases; Hindu and Muslim holy places received grants.
  • He gave gold to Hindu temples (tradition: gold for the dome of the Kashi Vishwanath temple, Varanasi).
  • He joined in the festivals of all communities, such as Diwali, Holi and Dussehra.
  • Historians often call his state secular in practice: no forced conversion, posts open to talent.

Mission 5Diversity

Defenders and critics

  • Defenders: he united Punjab, ended invasions, protected all faiths and gave Sikhs a homeland; many Sikhs remember his reign as a golden age.
  • Critics (historical): his rule was personal: he stopped calling the Sarbat Khalsa, so the gurmata, the Panth's collective decision-making, lapsed.
  • Critics (religious): his personal life (wine, many wives) did not match the Khalsa discipline; at his death some of his wives and maidservants were burned on his pyre (sati), a practice the Gurus condemned.
  • Critics (Muslim historians): some describe his rule as despotic; cow slaughter was punished severely, a law that bore on Muslims; Lahore's Badshahi mosque was used as a military store.
  • After him: he died on 27 June 1839; court factions weakened the state; the British annexed Punjab in 1849 after two Anglo-Sikh wars.
Mission 5Evaluate

How far was Ranjit Singh's empire religious?

  • Claim: The Sikh empire of Maharaja Ranjit Singh was a religious state.
  • For: ruled in the name of the Khalsa; coins honour the Gurus; he submitted to the Akal Takht (tradition); huge patronage of Sikh shrines.
  • Against: office open to all faiths; Muslim and Hindu law and shrines protected; most subjects not Sikh; the Sarbat Khalsa and gurmata lapsed.
  • Against: his personal life did not follow the Rahit.
  • Judgement criteria: Does "religious" mean ruled by religious law, ruled in the name of a faith, or ruled by a devout ruler? Can a state be Sikh in identity and secular in practice? Does Sikh teaching (sarbat da bhala, Guru Tegh Bahadur's defence of others) itself call for such tolerance?
  • Issue in your spec, verbatim: "The extent to which the Sikh empire of Maharaja Ranjit Singh was religious".
Mission 5Worked answer

Ranjit Singh or Guru Arjan: who mattered more?

Issue: "The extent to which Maharaja Ranjit Singh can be regarded as more important in the development of Sikhism than Guru Arjan."

  • Line of argument: decide what "development of Sikhism" means: belief and scripture, or the community's survival and identity.
  • For Ranjit Singh: political sovereignty; protection after a century of persecution; the Golden Temple as we know it; a lasting memory of Sikh rule.
  • For Guru Arjan: compiled the Adi Granth (1604), which became the Guru; built the Harmandir; first Sikh martyr (1606), which shaped Sikh readiness to defend faith.
  • Model sentence: "Ranjit Singh made the Panth powerful, but he built on foundations Guru Arjan laid: the scripture, the Harmandir and the example of martyrdom. Without them there would have been no Sikh identity for his state to serve."
  • Weighing: a Guru's spiritual authority and a ruler's political success are different kinds of importance; a strong answer says which matters more and why.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The Word is the Guru; since 1708 the scripture is the living Guru, treated with royal respect (takht, chaur, prakash, sukhasan).
  • All worship and rites of passage take place in its presence; vak lao takes its guidance.

Mission 2

  • Waheguru is one, true (Sat) and revealed through the Word (shabad); the Mool Mantar is the root statement of these beliefs.
  • The Japji (Guru Nanak) moves from who God is to how to reach God: hukam, the Name, grace and awe.
  • The Mul Mantra and Japji are used in personal meditation, initiation and gurdwara worship.

Mission 3

  • Ik Onkar: one God without a second, both nirgun (beyond qualities) and sargun (present in creation), transcendent and immanent, personal, creator and sustainer.
  • Sikhs debate which teaching about God matters most: oneness or a personal God.

Mission 4

  • Gurus 2–4 built the community: Gurmukhi, langar, manjis, Amritsar; sangat grew with each Guru.
  • Guru Arjan: Harmandir (humility, openness), Adi Granth (1604), Sukhmani, dasvandh; martyred in 1606 under Jahangir.
  • Hargobind's miri-piri answered that death; Guru Tegh Bahadur was executed in 1675; Sikhs remember him as defending others' freedom of faith.

Mission 5

  • Ranjit Singh (1780–1839) rose from Sukerchakia misl chief to Maharaja (Lahore 1799, crowned 1801), giving Punjab political sovereignty.
  • He ruled in the Khalsa's name and enriched Sikh shrines (a golden age for Sikhism) while giving office and protection to Hindus and Muslims.
  • Critics point to personal rule, the lapse of the gurmata, a lifestyle at odds with the Rahit, and the empire's fall in 1849.
