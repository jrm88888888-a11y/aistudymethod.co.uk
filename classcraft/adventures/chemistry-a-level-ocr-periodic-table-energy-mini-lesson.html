Mission 1

Mission 1: A jar of white crystals has lost its label. How can a few test tubes reveal exactly which salt it is?

Make a guess: A jar of white crystals has lost its label. How can a few test tubes reveal exactly which salt it is?

Mission 1

One salt, two ions

  • A salt has a positive ion (cation) and a negative ion (anion). Identify each one.
  • Dissolve a little in water. Test separate small portions.
  • A good test gives one clear observation: a coloured precipitate, a gas, a flame colour.
  • Halide ions: acidified silver nitrate, then ammonia.
  • Record observations precisely: "white precipitate", not "it went cloudy".
Mission 1

Ammonium ions

  • Add sodium hydroxide solution and warm gently.
  • NH4⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → NH3(g) + H2O(l).
  • Ammonia gas turns damp red litmus paper blue.
  • The paper must be damp: NH3 must dissolve to form OH⁻ ions. Hold it at the mouth of the tube, not touching the sides.
Quick check
?Which result shows that a solid contains ammonium ions?
Mission 1

Carbonate ions

  • Add a dilute acid (e.g. hydrochloric or nitric acid). Carbonates fizz.
  • CO3²⁻(s or aq) + 2H⁺(aq) → CO2(g) + H2O(l).
  • Bubble the gas into limewater: it turns milky (cloudy). The gas is CO2.
Mission 1

Sulfate ions

  • Add dilute hydrochloric acid, then barium chloride solution.
  • Ba²⁺(aq) + SO4²⁻(aq) → BaSO4(s): white precipitate.
  • Why barium? BaSO4 is extremely insoluble.
  • Why acidify? Carbonate ions would also give a white precipitate, BaCO3. Acid removes them as CO2 first.
  • Not sulfuric acid: it would add sulfate ions.
  • OCR: any Ba²⁺(aq) works. Use barium nitrate if you will test the same portion for halides next.
Quick check
?A student adds barium chloride solution to a solution of an unknown sodium salt, without adding acid first. A white precipitate forms. Why can the student not yet conclude that sulfate ions are present?
Mission 1

Order matters

  • For an unknown anion, test in this sequence: carbonate → sulfate → halide.
  • 1. Carbonate: add dilute HNO3. Fizzing = CO3²⁻. Keep adding acid until fizzing stops: this removes all the carbonate.
  • 2. Sulfate: add Ba(NO3)2(aq). White precipitate = SO4²⁻. If CO3²⁻ were still present, BaCO3 would also precipitate.
  • 3. Halide: filter if needed, then add AgNO3(aq). Ag2SO4 and Ag2CO3 are also insoluble, so they must be gone first.
  • Use nitric acid and nitrate salts throughout: they add no ions that interfere.
Mission check

?A garden-centre sack of white fertiliser crystals has lost its label. It could be ammonium sulfate, ammonium carbonate, ammonium chloride or potassium sulfate. Which pair of results shows it is ammonium sulfate?

Test the cation and the anion separately, each with a test that only one ion passes.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why is helium in group 18 when its atoms have only two electrons?

Make a guess: Why is helium in group 18 when its atoms have only two electrons?

Mission 2

Periods, groups and blocks

  • Elements are arranged in order of atomic (proton) number.
  • A period is a row. Its number is the outer occupied shell (energy level).
  • A group is a column, numbered 1 to 18. Elements in a group have similar chemical properties.
  • Repeating trends across each period are called periodicity.
  • Blocks are named after the sub-shell being filled: s-block groups 1–2; p-block groups 13–18; d-block groups 3–12.
Mission 2

Metals, metalloids, non-metals

  • Background: useful for reading the table.
  • Metals fill the left and centre. Non-metals sit at the upper right, plus hydrogen.
  • Metalloids lie along the stepped line: B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te.
  • Metalloids have in-between properties: silicon is shiny but only a semiconductor.
  • Aluminium touches the line but is a metal: shiny, malleable, a good conductor.
Mission 2

Valence electrons and the group

  • Valence electrons: the electrons in the outer shell.
  • Groups 1–2: valence electrons = group number.
  • Groups 13–18: valence electrons = group number − 10.
  • Group 16 atoms have 6 valence electrons, not 16. Group 13 atoms have 3.
  • Helium is the one exception. A later screen explains it.
Mission 2

From position to configuration

  • Period gives the outer shell, n. Block gives the sub-shell being filled.
  • Bromine: period 4, p-block, group 17 → 7 valence electrons, 4s² 4p⁵.
  • Bromine: [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s² 4p⁵. The 3d¹⁰ is inner: not valence.
  • Manganese: period 4, d-block, group 7 → [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s².
  • Across Periods 2 and 3 the pattern repeats: 2s¹ (Li) → 2s² 2p⁶ (Ne), then 3s¹ (Na) → 3s² 3p⁶ (Ar).
Mission 2

From configuration to position

  • Highest n → period. Sub-shell being filled → block.
  • [Ne] 3s² 3p¹: period 3, p-block, 3 outer electrons → group 13 (aluminium).
  • [Ar] 3d⁸ 4s²: period 4, d-block, 8 + 2 → group 10 (nickel).
  • Count the whole outer shell, not just the last sub-shell: 3s² 3p¹ is three.

Mission 2

Outer configuration decides the chemistry

  • Chemical reactions involve the outer electrons.
  • Same group → same outer configuration → similar chemical properties.
  • Group 2: outer ns². The highest-energy electrons are in an s sub-shell, so Group 2 is in the s-block.
  • Each Group 2 metal loses its two s electrons: Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺, Sr²⁺, Ba²⁺.
  • Same pattern of formulae: MgO, CaO, SrO, BaO; H2O, H2S, H2Se.
Mission 2

Ionic charge from position

  • Metals lose their valence electrons: group 1 → +1, group 2 → +2, group 13 → +3.
  • Non-metals gain electrons to fill the outer shell: group 15 → −3, group 16 → −2, group 17 → −1.
  • Then balance the charges: Al³⁺ and O²⁻ → Al2O3; Mg²⁺ and N³⁻ → Mg3N2.
  • You must also recall NO3⁻, CO3²⁻, SO4²⁻, OH⁻, NH4⁺, Zn²⁺ and Ag⁺.
Quick check
?An element has the ground-state electron configuration 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁴. What is the typical charge of the ions of this element in its ionic compounds?
Mission 2

So where does helium go?

  • Helium is 1s². By configuration alone, its last electron is in an s sub-shell.
  • But shell n = 1 holds at most 2 electrons (2n² = 2). So 1s² is a full outer shell.
  • A full outer shell → very hard to remove or add electrons → unreactive, like Ne, Ar, Kr.
  • It does not behave like group 2: those metals readily lose their ns² electrons.
  • So helium is placed with the chemistry it shares: group 18.
  • By configuration helium is s² (s-block); by position and chemistry it sits in group 18.
Mission check

?Beryllium, 1s² 2s², and helium, 1s², both have two electrons in their outer s sub-shell. Why is beryllium placed in group 2, but helium in group 18?

Position in the table follows the outer shell and the chemistry it gives, not a simple count of electrons.

Mission 3

Mission 3: How much energy does it take to pull an electron out of an atom?

Make a guess: How much energy does it take to pull an electron out of an atom?

Mission 3

From the convergence limit to kJ mol⁻¹

  • Background: this shows where an IE value comes from. You won't be asked to calculate it.
  • Hydrogen's lines for falls to n = 1 crowd together to a convergence limit: a fall from n = ∞ to n = 1.
  • Run it backwards: the same energy removes the electron completely. That is ionisation.
  • f = 3.29 × 10¹⁵ Hz. E = hf = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.29 × 10¹⁵ = 2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ J per atom.
  • × 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ = 1.31 × 10⁶ J mol⁻¹ = 1310 kJ mol⁻¹ (measured: 1312).
Mission 3

Defining ionisation energy

  • First ionisation energy: the energy needed to remove one mole of electrons from one mole of gaseous atoms, forming one mole of gaseous 1+ ions.
  • Na(g) → Na⁺(g) + e⁻. Always gaseous; always endothermic (positive).
  • Second: Na⁺(g) → Na²⁺(g) + e⁻. Each further one is a successive ionisation energy.
  • You define only the first, but write equations for the second and later ones.
Quick check
?Which equation represents the second ionisation energy of calcium?
Mission 3

What sets the size of an IE

  • The electron is held by its attraction to the nucleus. Stronger attraction → higher IE.
  • Nuclear charge: more protons → stronger attraction.
  • Distance (atomic radius): electron further out → weaker attraction.
  • Shielding: inner electrons repel the outer electron, so it feels less of the nuclear charge.
Mission 3

The pattern across the periodic table

  • Across a period: IE generally rises. Nuclear charge increases; electrons enter the same shell, with similar shielding; the radius shrinks.
  • Period 3: Na 496 → Ar 1521 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Down a group: IE falls. Each new shell puts the outer electron further away and more shielded; this outweighs the extra protons.
  • Peaks at the noble gases, sharp drops at group 1: the evidence for shells.
  • Two small dips in each period (e.g. Be → B, N → O) come from sub-shells: Mission 4.
Quick check
?How does the first ionisation energy generally change across Period 3 from sodium to argon, and why?
Mission 3

Group 2: IE and reactivity

  • First IE falls down Group 2: Be 900 · Mg 738 · Ca 590 · Sr 550 · Ba 503 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Each step down adds a shell: the outer s electrons are further out and more shielded. Evidence for shells.
  • Group 2 metals react by losing two electrons, so 1st + 2nd IE matter.
  • 1st + 2nd: Mg 2188 · Ca 1735 · Sr 1614 · Ba 1468 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Less energy needed to form M²⁺ → reactivity increases down the group.
Mission 3

Successive IEs reveal shells and the group

  • Successive IEs always rise: each electron is pulled from an ion that is more positive than the last.
  • A big jump marks the first electron taken from a shell nearer the nucleus, less shielded.
  • Magnesium: 738, 1451, then 7733 kJ mol⁻¹. Jump after 2 → 2 outer electrons → Group 2.
  • Count the electrons before the first big jump = outer electrons.
  • Three outer electrons → aluminium's group (13 in 1–18 numbering; Group 3 or III on some tables).
Quick check
?The first four successive ionisation energies of an element X are 578, 1817, 2745 and 11 577 kJ mol⁻¹. How many electrons does an atom of X have in its outer shell?
Mission check

?Element X is in Period 3.

How much energy? It depends on how strongly the nucleus attracts that electron: nuclear charge, distance and shielding. Removing an electron from an inner shell takes far more.

Mission 4

Mission 4: If nuclear charge rises across period 3, why does aluminium lose an electron more easily than magnesium?

Make a guess: If nuclear charge rises across period 3, why does aluminium lose an electron more easily than magnesium?

Mission 4

Two dips in period 3

  • First ionisation energy: energy to remove 1 mol of electrons from 1 mol of gaseous atoms. X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻.
  • Across period 3 it rises overall: more protons, same shell, similar shielding.
  • But it falls twice: Mg → Al (738 → 578) and P → S (1012 → 1000 kJ mol⁻¹).
  • After argon it falls steeply to potassium: a new shell. That is evidence for shells.
Mission 4

Why dips mean sub-shells

  • If every electron in shell 3 had the same energy, the rise would be smooth.
  • The dips show that shell 3 is split into sub-shells of different energy: 3s, then 3p.
  • To explain a dip, compare the electron removed from each atom: which sub-shell, which orbital.
Mission 4

Mg → Al: a new sub-shell

  • Mg: [Ne] 3s². Al: [Ne] 3s² 3p¹.
  • Al's electron is removed from 3p, which is higher in energy than 3s.
  • The 3p electron is also slightly further from the nucleus and partly shielded by the 3s electrons.
  • So less energy is needed, even though Al has one more proton.
Quick check
?After the dip, the rise resumes: silicon's first ionisation energy is 787 kJ mol⁻¹, higher than aluminium's 578 kJ mol⁻¹. Why?
Mission 4

P → S: p-orbital repulsion

  • P: [Ne] 3s² 3p³. Three 3p electrons, one in each orbital: [↑ ] [↑ ] [↑ ].
  • S: [Ne] 3s² 3p⁴. One 3p orbital now holds a pair: [↑↓] [↑ ] [↑ ].
  • The paired electrons repel, so one of them is easier to remove.
  • Both electrons come from 3p: this dip is about orbitals, not a new sub-shell.
Quick check
?The first ionisation energies of phosphorus and sulfur are 1012 kJ mol⁻¹ and 1000 kJ mol⁻¹. Which explanation of the lower value for sulfur is correct?
Mission 4

The same pattern in period 2

  • Be → B (900 → 801 kJ mol⁻¹): B's electron comes from 2p, higher in energy than Be's 2s.
  • N → O (1402 → 1314 kJ mol⁻¹): O's electron comes from a paired 2p orbital: p-orbital repulsion.
  • Period 3 repeats this one shell further out: Mg → Al and P → S.
Quick check
?A student sketches first ionisation energy from Na to Ar as a smooth, steady rise. Which correction does the sketch need?
Mission check

?The first ionisation energies of beryllium and boron are 900 kJ mol⁻¹ and 801 kJ mol⁻¹. Which explanation of the lower value for boron is correct?

Same reason as aluminium. Its outer electron is in 3p, higher in energy than magnesium's 3s, so it is easier to remove despite the extra proton.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Diamond and graphite are both pure carbon. Why does only graphite conduct electricity?

Make a guess: Diamond and graphite are both pure carbon. Why does only graphite conduct electricity?

Mission 5

Diamond: a giant covalent lattice

  • Covalent bonds run through the whole solid. There are no separate molecules.
  • Name: giant covalent lattice.
  • Diamond: each C bonded to four others, tetrahedrally (109.5°), in 3D.
  • Very hard, very high melting point: melting breaks strong covalent bonds.
  • No conduction: all four outer electrons of each C are in localised bonds.
Mission 5

Graphite

  • Flat layers of hexagons. Each C bonded to three others (120°).
  • Each C's fourth outer electron is delocalised across its layer.
  • Delocalised electrons move when a voltage is applied: graphite conducts.
  • Weak forces between the layers. Layers slide, so graphite is soft.
  • Still a very high melting point: strong covalent bonds within layers must break.
Quick check
?The diagram represents parts of the structures of diamond and graphite, two forms of carbon. Which statement best explains why graphite is soft and diamond is hard?
Mission 5

Graphene and fullerenes

  • Graphene is on your course. Fullerenes are background only.
  • Graphene: a single layer of graphite, one atom thick.
  • Each C bonded to three others, with delocalised electrons: it conducts.
  • Very strong for its mass: strong covalent bonds run across the whole sheet.
  • Strength + conductivity + transparency: possible uses in flexible screens, sensors and composites.
  • Fullerenes, e.g. C60: closed cages of carbon atoms. These are separate molecules.
  • Only weak forces act between C60 molecules: far lower sublimation temperature than diamond.
  • Diamond, graphite, graphene and fullerenes are allotropes: one element, different structures.
Mission 5

Silicon and silicon dioxide

  • Silicon is on your course. SiO2 is background only.
  • Silicon: the diamond structure. Each Si bonded to four Si, tetrahedrally.
  • Si–Si bonds are longer and weaker than C–C: silicon is less hard than diamond and melts lower (1414 °C).
  • Silicon conducts only slightly: it is a semiconductor, not a metal.
  • Silicon dioxide, SiO2: each Si bonded to four O; each O bonded to two Si.
  • SiO2 is a giant lattice, so its formula is just the ratio Si : O = 1 : 2.
  • Melts near 1700 °C. Hard, insoluble, non-conducting.

Quick check

Mission check

?Graphite conducts electricity far better along its layers than at right angles to them. Which statement explains this?

Conduction needs mobile charged particles. Graphite has delocalised electrons within each layer; diamond uses all four outer electrons in localised bonds, so it has none.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Tin conducts electricity. So why doesn't liquid tin(IV) chloride conduct at all?

Make a guess: Tin conducts electricity. So why doesn't liquid tin(IV) chloride conduct at all?

Mission 6

The metallic bond

  • Metal atoms release their outer electrons into a shared pool of delocalised electrons.
  • Metallic bonding: strong electrostatic attraction between positive ions (cations) and delocalised electrons.
  • The ions form a regular giant metallic lattice.
  • Bonding is non-directional: each ion attracts the electrons all around it.
Mission 6

Why metals conduct and bend

  • Electrical conductivity: delocalised electrons drift when a voltage is applied. The ions stay put.
  • Thermal conductivity: delocalised electrons carry kinetic energy quickly through the metal.
  • Malleable (hammered into shape) and ductile (drawn into wires): layers of ions slide.
  • After sliding, every ion is still surrounded by delocalised electrons: the bonding is not broken.
  • An ionic lattice is different: a shift brings like charges together. They repel and the crystal shatters.
Quick check
?Aluminium is rolled into kitchen foil less than 0.02 mm thick without cracking. Which statement explains this property of aluminium?
Mission 6

What makes metallic bonding stronger

  • Stronger with a higher ion charge and a smaller ion.
  • More delocalised electrons per ion, packed closer to each ion's centre: stronger attraction.
  • Na⁺ → Mg²⁺ → Al³⁺: melting point 98 °C → 650 °C → 660 °C.
  • Down group 1 the ions get larger: Li 181 °C, Na 98 °C, K 63 °C.
  • Mass does not decide: heavier potassium melts lower than lithium.
Mission 6

Alloys

  • Background from the video: alloys are not examined on your course.
  • An alloy is a mixture of a metal with other elements: brass (copper, zinc), steel (iron, carbon).
  • Metallic bonding is non-directional, so other atoms fit into the lattice. The alloy still conducts.
  • Atoms of a different size disrupt the layers, so they slide less easily.
  • So alloys are usually harder and stronger than the pure metal.
Mission 6

Four types of structure

  • Giant ionic (e.g. NaCl): ions held by electrostatic attraction in all directions.
  • Giant metallic (e.g. magnesium): ions in a sea of delocalised electrons.
  • Giant covalent (e.g. diamond, graphite): atoms joined by covalent bonds throughout.
  • Simple molecular (e.g. iodine, ice): small molecules held by weak intermolecular forces.
  • Identify the particles and the forces between them. The properties follow.
Mission 6

Simple molecular: iodine and ice

  • Iodine: I2 molecules in a regular lattice, held by weak London (van der Waals) forces.
  • Low melting point (114 °C); iodine sublimes on gentle warming. The I–I bonds do not break.
  • Ice: each H2O hydrogen-bonded to four others, tetrahedrally. An open lattice.
  • The open lattice makes ice less dense than liquid water, so ice floats.
  • Hydrogen bonds are stronger than London forces, but far weaker than covalent bonds.

Mission 6

Melting points across periods 2 and 3

  • Period 3: Na 98, Mg 650, Al 660, Si 1414, P 44, S 115, Cl −101, Ar −189 (°C).
  • Na → Al: giant metallic. Ion charge rises and ion size falls, so metallic bonding gets stronger.
  • Si: giant covalent. Melting breaks many strong covalent bonds: the highest.
  • P4, S8, Cl2: simple molecular. Only weak London forces are overcome: low.
  • S8 > P4 > Cl2: bigger molecules, more electrons, stronger London forces. Ar: single atoms, lowest.
  • Period 2 shows the same pattern: Li, Be metallic; B, C giant covalent (highest); N2, O2, F2, Ne very low.
Quick check
?Silicon melts at 1414 °C. Phosphorus (white, P4) melts at 44 °C. Which statement explains the difference?
Mission 6

Bonding is a continuum

  • Ionic, covalent and metallic are models. Real bonds usually lie in between.
  • Bigger electronegativity difference: more ionic character. Smaller difference: more covalent.
  • Background (not examined on any course here): the bonding triangle plots average electronegativity (x) against difference (y). Corners: metallic, ionic, covalent.
  • NaCl: average 2.05, difference 2.3: near the ionic corner. High melting point, conducts when molten.
  • SnCl4: average 2.6, difference 1.2: polar covalent. Small molecules: liquid, no ions, no conduction.
Mission check

?Each liquid is tested with two electrodes and a bulb. Which liquid will not conduct electricity?

Tin metal has delocalised electrons. SnCl4, like TiCl4, is simple molecular: electrons are held in bonds inside molecules and there are no ions, so neither liquid conducts.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Why is a chlorine atom smaller than a sodium atom, even though it has six more electrons?

Make a guess: Why is a chlorine atom smaller than a sodium atom, even though it has six more electrons?

Mission 7

Three factors explain every trend

  • Nuclear charge: more protons, a stronger pull on the outer electrons.
  • Distance: outer electrons in a higher shell are further away, so pulled less.
  • Shielding: inner electrons repel outer ones, reducing the pull they feel.
  • Across a period: nuclear charge rises, same shell, similar shielding → stronger pull.
  • Down a group: a new shell adds distance and shielding → weaker pull, despite more protons.
Mission 7

Atomic radius

  • Across a period, atomic radius decreases. Down a group, it increases.
  • Na: 11 protons. Cl: 17 protons. Both have 10 inner electrons and outer electrons in shell 3.
  • Chlorine's six extra electrons join the same shell. Electrons in one shell shield each other poorly.
  • So the outer shell feels a much larger net pull and is drawn closer: Cl is smaller.
Mission 7

Ionic radius

  • Background: ionic size returns in lattice energy and bonding.
  • Positive ions are smaller than their atoms: Na → Na⁺ loses the whole outer shell.
  • Negative ions are larger than their atoms: Cl → Cl⁻ has the same 17 protons but more electron repulsion.
  • Down a group, ions with the same charge get larger: Li⁺ < Na⁺ < K⁺; F⁻ < Cl⁻ < Br⁻.
  • Isoelectronic ions share a configuration; more protons → smaller ion.
  • N³⁻ > O²⁻ > F⁻ > Na⁺ > Mg²⁺ > Al³⁺: all 1s² 2s² 2p⁶, with 7 to 13 protons.
Mission 7

First ionisation energy

  • First ionisation energy: the energy needed to remove 1 mol of electrons from 1 mol of gaseous atoms, forming 1 mol of gaseous 1+ ions.
  • Na(g) → Na⁺(g) + e⁻.
  • Across a period it generally increases: more protons, same shell, similar shielding.
  • Down a group it decreases: the outer electron is further away and more shielded.
  • Small dips (Mg → Al, P → S) are explained in Mission 4.
Quick check
?The first ionisation energy of magnesium is lower than that of beryllium. Which explains this?
Mission 7

Electron affinity

  • Background here; you'll use it in Born–Haber cycles.
  • First electron affinity: the enthalpy change when each atom in 1 mol of gaseous atoms gains one electron, forming 1 mol of gaseous 1− ions: X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g).
  • Energy is usually released: the nucleus attracts the incoming electron.
  • Across a period: more protons, same shell → more energy released.
  • Down a group: further away, more shielded → less energy released.
  • Many exceptions. Cl (−349 kJ mol⁻¹) releases more than F (−328 kJ mol⁻¹): fluorine's small, crowded atom repels the newcomer.
Mission 7

Electronegativity

  • Electronegativity: the ability of an atom to attract the pair of electrons in a covalent bond.
  • Pauling scale, no units. Fluorine is highest, 4.0.
  • Increases across a period and decreases down a group: the same three factors.
  • Period 3: Na 0.9 → Cl 3.0.
  • Not electron affinity: that is measured on isolated gaseous atoms.
Quick check
?Fluorine is more electronegative than chlorine. Which explains this?
Mission 7

Melting points across Periods 2 and 3

  • Period 3: m.p. rises Na → Mg → Al, peaks at Si, then drops sharply.
  • Na, Mg, Al: giant metallic. More delocalised electrons and smaller, more highly charged ions → stronger metallic bonding → m.p. rises.
  • Si: giant covalent. Melting breaks many strong covalent bonds → highest m.p.
  • P4, S8, Cl2, Ar: simple molecular (Ar: atoms). Melting overcomes only weak London forces.
  • S8 > P4 > Cl2 > Ar: more electrons per molecule → stronger London forces.
  • Period 2 shows the same pattern: Li, Be metallic; B, C giant covalent; N2, O2, F2, Ne simple molecular.
Quick check
?Why does silicon have the highest melting point of the Period 3 elements?
Mission 7

All the trends on one map

  • Across a period → radius decreases; IE and electronegativity increase.
  • Down a group → radius increases; IE and electronegativity decrease.
  • The pattern repeats in each period: this is periodicity.
  • Metallic character increases down a group and towards the left.
Mission check

?An argon atom has seven more electrons than a sodium atom, yet argon's first ionisation energy is about three times sodium's. Which explains this?

Same rule as chlorine vs sodium. Across a period, more protons pull the same shell in tighter.

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • NH4⁺: warm with NaOH(aq) → NH3 turns damp red litmus blue. CO3²⁻: dilute acid → CO2 turns limewater milky. SO4²⁻: dilute HCl then BaCl2(aq) → white BaSO4.

Mission 2

  • Period = outer shell; group = similar outer configuration and chemistry; block = sub-shell being filled.
  • Classify an element as s, p or d (and f) block from its position.
  • Helium's 1s² is a full shell, so it sits with the noble gases.

Mission 3

  • First IE: 1 mol of electrons from 1 mol of gaseous atoms: X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻.
  • IE rises across a period, falls down a group. Electrons before the big jump in successive IEs = outer electrons.

Mission 4

  • First IE rises across a period, with dips where a new sub-shell starts (Be→B, Mg→Al) and where p electrons first pair (N→O, P→S).
  • The dips are evidence that shells are split into sub-shells.

Mission 5

  • Giant covalent: strong covalent bonds throughout, so very high melting point and insoluble.
  • Diamond: 4 bonds per C, all electrons localised, hard, no conduction. Graphite: 3 bonds per C, one delocalised electron per C, conducts along layers, layers slide.

Mission 6

  • Metallic bonding: attraction between cations and delocalised electrons; non-directional, so metals conduct, are malleable and (usually) melt high.
  • To conduct, a substance needs mobile charged particles. Decide the structure, then explain the property.

Mission 7

  • Nuclear charge, distance and shielding explain radius, ionisation energy, electron affinity and electronegativity.
  • Across a period: more protons, same shell → smaller atoms that hold electrons more tightly.
  • Melting points across a period follow structure: giant metallic → giant covalent → simple molecular.
