Mission 1

Mission 1: Carbon and lead both have four outer electrons. Why does lead usually use only two of them?

Make a guess: Carbon and lead both have four outer electrons. Why does lead usually use only two of them?

Mission 1

Group 4: non-metal to metal

  • Outer shell ns² np²: C, Si, Ge, Sn, Pb all have four outer electrons.
  • Carbon: non-metal. Silicon, germanium: semi-metals (semiconductors). Tin, lead: metals.
  • Two oxidation states: +4 (all four electrons used) and +2 (only the two p electrons).
  • Down the group, +2 becomes more stable; +4 becomes less stable.
  • Carbon: +4 almost always (CO2, CCl4). Lead: +2 in most compounds (PbO, PbCl2, Pb²⁺).
Mission 1

The inert pair effect

  • The inert pair is the outer ns² pair. Down a group it is used in bonding less and less.
  • In heavy atoms the s pair is held more tightly than expected: filled d and f shells shield poorly, so it feels a large nuclear charge.
  • Big atoms form weaker bonds, so making two extra bonds releases less energy.
  • The energy needed to use the s pair is no longer repaid: the lower state, two below the group maximum, wins.
  • Groups 3, 4 and 5: Tl⁺ more stable than Tl³⁺; Pb²⁺ than Pb(IV); Bi³⁺ than Bi(V).
  • A heavy element in its higher state is therefore a strong oxidising agent.
Quick check
?Which statement about Group 4 is correct?
Mission 1

Carbon(II): carbon monoxide reduces oxides

  • For carbon, +4 is more stable than +2. So CO is easily oxidised to CO2: CO is a reducing agent.
  • CuO(s) + CO(g) → Cu(s) + CO2(g) (heated).
  • Fe2O3(s) + 3CO(g) → 2Fe(s) + 3CO2(g): the main reduction in the blast furnace.
  • PbO(s) + CO(g) → Pb(s) + CO2(g): one way lead is extracted.
  • Carbon: +2 → +4 (oxidised). The metal ion is reduced to the metal.
Mission 1

Lead(IV): PbO2 oxidises hydrochloric acid

  • For lead, +2 is more stable than +4. So PbO2 is easily reduced: it is an oxidising agent.
  • Warm PbO2 with concentrated hydrochloric acid:
  • PbO2(s) + 4HCl(aq) → PbCl2(s) + Cl2(g) + 2H2O(l).
  • Lead: +4 → +2 (reduced). Chlorine: −1 → 0 (oxidised).
  • Observation: dark brown solid reacts; pale green gas that bleaches damp litmus.
  • Compare the earlier screen: down Group 4 the stable state switches from +4 (carbon) to +2 (lead).
Mission 1

Two oxides: CO2 and PbO

  • CO2: simple molecular, O=C=O. Weak London forces: a gas (solid sublimes at −78 °C).
  • CO2 is an acidic oxide: it dissolves slightly in water to give a weakly acidic solution; it reacts with alkalis.
  • CO2(g) + 2NaOH(aq) → Na2CO3(aq) + H2O(l).
  • PbO: a giant lattice, mainly ionic. A yellow solid with a high melting point (888 °C).
  • PbO is amphoteric: it reacts with acids and with alkalis.
  • PbO(s) + 2HNO3(aq) → Pb(NO3)2(aq) + H2O(l).
  • PbO(s) + 2OH⁻(aq) + H2O(l) → [Pb(OH)4]²⁻(aq) (a plumbate(II) ion).
Mission 1

Amphoteric aluminium and lead(II)

  • Amphoteric: reacts as a base with acids and as an acid with alkalis.
  • Add NaOH(aq) dropwise to Al³⁺(aq) or Pb²⁺(aq): a white precipitate of the hydroxide forms.
  • Al³⁺(aq) + 3OH⁻(aq) → Al(OH)3(s). Pb²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Pb(OH)2(s).
  • In excess NaOH(aq) both precipitates dissolve: colourless solutions.
  • Al(OH)3(s) + OH⁻(aq) → [Al(OH)4]⁻(aq). Pb(OH)2(s) + 2OH⁻(aq) → [Pb(OH)4]²⁻(aq).
  • They also dissolve in acids: Al(OH)3 + 3H⁺ → Al³⁺ + 3H2O.
  • Aluminium metal reacts with both too: 2Al + 6HCl → 2AlCl3 + 3H2; 2Al + 2NaOH + 6H2O → 2Na[Al(OH)4] + 3H2.
Mission 1

Lead(II) ions with chloride and iodide

  • Pb²⁺(aq) + 2Cl⁻(aq) → PbCl2(s): white precipitate. It dissolves on heating and reappears on cooling.
  • Pb²⁺(aq) + 2I⁻(aq) → PbI2(s): bright yellow precipitate.
  • PbI2 dissolves in hot water and crystallises on cooling as shiny golden plates.
  • With NaOH(aq): white Pb(OH)2, soluble in excess.
Quick check
?Two colourless solutions, P and Q, each give a white precipitate with a few drops of NaOH(aq) that dissolves in excess NaOH(aq). One contains Al³⁺ ions and the other contains Pb²⁺ ions. Which test tells them apart?
Mission 1

Chlorides down Group 4

  • CCl4: covalent, simple molecular, colourless liquid. No reaction with water: they form two layers.
  • SiCl4: covalent, simple molecular liquid. Reacts violently with water: steamy fumes of HCl.
  • SiCl4(l) + 2H2O(l) → SiO2(s) + 4HCl(aq).
  • PbCl2: a white ionic solid (lead is +2). Sparingly soluble in cold water; dissolves, unchanged, in hot water.
  • Down the group: covalent → ionic bonding, and +4 → +2 oxidation state.
Mission 1

Why SiCl4 reacts but CCl4 doesn't

  • Water attacks the central atom with a lone pair on its oxygen.
  • Silicon is larger and has empty 3d orbitals at low energy: it can accept the lone pair, then lose Cl⁻.
  • Carbon is small, with no 2d orbitals; four Cl atoms crowd round it. Water cannot attack.
  • So CCl4 is kinetically stable in water, though hydrolysis would be energetically favourable.
Mission check

?Bismuth is at the bottom of Group 5. Sodium bismuthate, NaBiO3, contains bismuth in the +5 state. It is such a strong oxidising agent that it oxidises Mn²⁺ ions to purple manganate(VII) ions. Which explains why bismuth(V) is such a strong oxidising agent?

The same idea explains the hook. Lead's 6s² pair is an inert pair, so lead usually uses only its two 6p electrons and sits at +2.

Mission 2: Why does extra alkali make some metal precipitates vanish but leave others untouched?

Make a guess: Why does extra alkali make some metal precipitates vanish but leave others untouched?

Mission 2

A little base: the same first step

  • NaOH(aq) supplies OH⁻, which removes H⁺ from water ligands.
  • A few drops: the neutral hydroxide precipitates.
  • The interesting part comes next: add the reagent in excess.
Mission 2

Precipitate colours

  • Cu²⁺: blue solution → pale blue precipitate.
  • Fe²⁺: pale green → green precipitate; turns brown at the surface on standing (air oxidises Fe(II) to Fe(III)).
  • Fe³⁺: yellow-brown → orange-brown precipitate.
  • Al³⁺: colourless → white precipitate.
  • Cr³⁺: green (or violet) → grey-green precipitate.
Quick check
?A few drops of NaOH(aq) are added to a pale green solution. A green precipitate forms, and its surface turns brown on standing in air. Which ion was in the solution?
Mission 2

Excess NaOH: amphoteric hydroxides

  • An amphoteric hydroxide dissolves in both acids and bases.
  • Al: [Al(H2O)3(OH)3](s) + OH⁻(aq) → [Al(OH)4]⁻(aq) + 3H2O(l). White precipitate → colourless solution.
  • With acid: [Al(H2O)3(OH)3](s) + 3H⁺(aq) → [Al(H2O)6]³⁺(aq). Precipitate dissolves: colourless solution.
  • Cr: [Cr(H2O)3(OH)3](s) + 3OH⁻(aq) → [Cr(OH)6]³⁻(aq) + 3H2O(l). Grey-green precipitate → dark green solution.
  • Pb²⁺ too: white Pb(OH)2 dissolves in excess OH⁻ to give [Pb(OH)4]²⁻.
  • Simple formulae are fine: Cr(OH)3 + 3OH⁻ → [Cr(OH)6]³⁻.
  • Cu, Fe(II) and Fe(III) hydroxides: no change in excess NaOH.
Quick check
?NaOH(aq) is added, until in excess, to four separate solutions. Which one ends as a clear green solution with no precipitate?
Mission 2

Running the tests

  • Wear eye protection: NaOH(aq) is corrosive.
  • A few cm³ of the unknown solution in a test tube. Add NaOH(aq) dropwise: note the precipitate colour.
  • Keep adding until in excess, shaking: does the precipitate dissolve?
  • Leave the precipitates to stand: some change colour in air (oxidation).
Mission check

?Zinc ions are not in your table of tests. A few drops of NaOH(aq) added to Zn²⁺(aq) give a white precipitate. With excess NaOH(aq), the precipitate dissolves to give a colourless solution. Which equation shows what the excess NaOH(aq) does?

The precipitates that vanish turn into charged complex ions.

They do it by losing more H⁺ (amphoteric). The rest stay neutral and stay solid.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Inert pair effect: down Groups 3, 4 and 5 the ns² pair is used less, so the lower state (group maximum − 2) becomes more stable. CO is a reducing agent (C +2 → +4); PbO2 an oxidising agent (Pb +4 → +2).
  • Down Group 4, the oxides change from covalent and acidic (CO2) to ionic and amphoteric (PbO); the chlorides change from covalent (CCl4, SiCl4) to ionic (PbCl2). Pb²⁺ gives white Pb(OH)2 (soluble in excess), white PbCl2 and yellow PbI2.

Mission 2

  • A little OH⁻ (or NH3): neutral hydroxide precipitate. Its colour identifies the ion.
  • Excess NaOH dissolves amphoteric hydroxides; excess NH3 dissolves some by ligand exchange.
