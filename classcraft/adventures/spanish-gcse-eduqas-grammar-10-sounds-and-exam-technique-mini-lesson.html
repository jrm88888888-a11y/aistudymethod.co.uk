Sounds & exam technique

Spanish spelling is beautifully regular — once you know the sound rules you can read almost any word aloud, and spell almost any word you hear. This lesson builds your ear and voice: the sound–symbol rules, where the stress falls, the written accents, and how to attack each Eduqas task — dictation, reading aloud, role play, photo card, translation and writing.

Work through each screen and answer the questions as you go — some ask you to recognise the right sound, some are mini dictation/accent tasks where you type the word. Collect ⭐ stars and press Start.

Foundation · the five vowels

Five pure, short vowels

Spanish has just five vowel sounds and they are always short and pure — you never glide or "drift" them the way English does. Say each one crisply:

  • a — "ah" — casa (house)
  • e — "eh" — mesa (table)
  • i — "ee" — sí (yes)
  • o — "oh" — no (no)
  • u — "oo" — tú (you)

Why this matters: in reading aloud and dictation the vowels never change. English speakers tend to say "KAH-suh" — the final a is a full clear "ah", not a mumbled "uh".

Foundation · c, qu and z

Hard and soft c

The letter c has two jobs, decided entirely by the vowel that follows it:

  • c + a/o/u = hard [k] — casa, como, cuando.
  • c + e/i = soft — cena, cine. In Spain a "th" sound; in Latin America an "s".
  • qu = [k], used only before e/i — que, quién. The u is silent.
  • z = soft "th"/"s" — zapato, luz. Same sound as soft c.
  • To keep the [k] sound before e/i you must use qu; for [k] before a/o/u you write c (and cu + vowel: cuatro, cuenta).

Read-aloud tip: never say "kwe" for que — the u after q is silent, so it is just "keh".

Foundation · g, gu and j

The throaty sound: g and j

Like c, the letter g changes with the next vowel — and there's a trick to keep it hard:

spellingsound & example
g + a/o/uhard g — gato, gordo, gustar
g + e/ithroaty [x] — gente, gimnasio
gu + e/ihard g, silent u — guitarra, guerra
j (any vowel)throaty [x] — jamón, joven

The "loch" sound: g before e/i and j everywhere make the same rasping sound at the back of the throat — like the "ch" in Scottish loch. So gente and joven both start with that throaty noise, not a soft English "j".

Foundation · special letters

ll, ñ, ch, v and the rolling r

A handful of letters and pairs make sounds English doesn't spell the same way:

  • ll ≈ "y" — llamar (to call), calle (street).
  • ñ = "ny" — España, niño (like the "ny" in "canyon").
  • ch = "ch" — chico (boy), mochila (backpack).
  • v sounds like b — vivir (to live), vaca (cow). No English "v" buzz.
  • single r = a tap — pero (but). One quick flick of the tongue.
  • rr, and r at the start of a word or after n/l/s = a rolled trill — perro (dog), rojo (red), Enrique.

Minimal pair: pero ("but", single tap) vs perro ("dog", rolled). The trill is the difference between them — worth practising for reading aloud.

Stress & accents Higher

Where does the stress fall?

Every Spanish word has one stressed syllable. If there is no written accent, two default rules decide where it lands:

Ends in vowel, n or s

Stress the penultimate (second-to-last) syllable: ca-sa → ca-sa, ha-blan, lu-nes.

Ends in any other consonant

Stress the last syllable: ha-blar, ciu-dad, fe-liz.

A written accent beats both rules — it marks the stressed vowel directly, wherever it is: te-lé-fo-no, in-glés, rá-pi-do.

Why it matters: in dictation and writing, a missing or wrong accent can be marked wrong — and can change the word. Learn to hear which syllable is loudest, then apply the rule.

Accents that change meaning Higher

Small mark, big difference

Some pairs of words are spelt with the same letters — the written accent is the only thing that tells them apart. Getting it right (or wrong) changes the meaning:

with accentwithout accent
sí = yessi = if
tú = youtu = your
él = heel = the
qué = what?que = that / which

Watch out in writing: Tú tienes tu libro = "You have your book" — the first needs the accent, the second doesn't. Question words (qué, cómo, cuándo, dónde) take an accent; the same words as ordinary connectors do not.

Eduqas · Component 1 Speaking & Component 2 Listening

Speaking (NEA) and dictation

Eduqas has four components, each worth 25%, tiered Foundation / Higher. Two of them reward your sound rules directly:

  • Component 1 — Speaking (NEA, teacher-conducted). Your teacher runs it. It has three parts: a read-aloud passage plus a short conversation, a role play, and a discussion of two connected photographs — describe both photos, then answer unseen questions and hold a conversation.
  • Read-aloud rules to apply out loud: silent h, soft c/g, rolled rr, ll≈"y", ñ="ny".
  • Component 2 — Listening. Comprehension questions plus a dictation: you write down what you hear and spelling and accents are marked. Extracts are played 3 times. Spell any tricky word by ear using the sound–symbol rules (silent h, v=b, c/z="th/s").
  • Photo-card language: describe with Hay… (there is/are), En la foto veo… (in the photo I see…), Está… (it is / is located…).

Read-aloud checklist: before you speak, scan for silent h, any rr to roll, and soft c/g. Getting these right sounds far more Spanish than perfect grammar with an English accent.

Eduqas · Component 3 Reading & Component 4 Writing

Translation and writing

The last two components — each 25% — test how carefully you handle words on the page:

  • Component 3 — Reading. Comprehension that includes inference questions, plus a translation from Spanish into English. Translate faithfully — every word counts. If a word is new, infer it from context; that is an assessed skill, so don't leave a gap.
  • Component 4 — Writing. A mix of structured tasks and open tasks (respond to bullet points, extended writing), plus a translation from English into Spanish. There is no standalone grammar cloze on Eduqas.
  • Translation into Spanish. Watch word order, agreement and accents — the accent rules from this lesson are marked here.
  • Structured & open writing. Cover every bullet point and use a range of tenses to reach the top bands.

Translation-into-Spanish habit: after you write a sentence, re-read it for three things — does the adjective agree, does the verb ending match the subject, and does any word need an accent (sí, tú, él, qué, plus stress accents like inglés)?

Recap

Your sounds & exam toolkit

Vowels: five short, pure sounds — a, e, i, o, u — never drifting.

c / g: hard before a/o/u; soft (c) or throaty (g) before e/i. qu/gu keep the hard [k]/[g] before e/i, silent u.

Silent & special: h silent; j throaty; ll≈"y"; ñ="ny"; ch="ch"; v=b; z="th/s".

The r: single = tap (pero); rr / start / after n,l,s = rolled trill (perro).

Stress Higher: vowel/n/s → penultimate; other consonant → last; a written accent overrides both.

Meaning accents Higher: sí/si, tú/tu, él/el, qué/que.

Eduqas components (25% each): Speaking NEA (read-aloud, role play, two-photo discussion) · Listening (comprehension + dictation, played 3×) · Reading (comprehension incl. inference + translation into English) · Writing (structured & open + translation into Spanish — watch agreement, order, accents).
