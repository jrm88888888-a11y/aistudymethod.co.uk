Component 2: 19th & 21st Century Non-Fiction Reading & Transactional/Persuasive Writing

This mini-lesson trains the skills Eduqas Component 2 tests: reading two non-fiction texts (one from the 19th century, one from the 21st), analysing language and structure, comparing the writers' ideas and perspectives, evaluating, and producing convincing transactional/persuasive writing.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go (spot the device, pick the best comment, compare the texts, match form to features) and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you're ready.

Know the component

What Component 2 asks of you

Section A gives you two unseen non-fiction texts (about 900–1200 words total): one from the 19th century and one from the 21st century. You answer structured questions worth 40 marks:

  • Retrieval / understanding — find and explain ideas and information (tests AO1).
  • Language & structure analysis — how a writer achieves effects (AO2).
  • Comparison — compare the writers' ideas and perspectives across the two texts (AO3).
  • Evaluation — a critical judgement backed by the text (AO4).

Section B is transactional/persuasive writing (40 marks): two compulsory tasks (20 marks each). You write in a set form for a set purpose and audience — assessed on communication & organisation (AO5) and technical accuracy (AO6).

Two writing tasks, not one: unlike some boards, Eduqas Section B has two compulsory tasks — manage your time so both get done well.

Reading skill

Read like a detective — annotate

Marks are lost by readers who skim. Before you write a word, annotate both texts: mark them up so your analysis is built on evidence, not vibes.

  • Purpose — is it to inform, persuade, describe, entertain, argue?
  • Audience — who is it aimed at, and how do you know?
  • Underline striking words and phrases (verbs, adjectives, imagery).
  • Bracket structural moves — where the focus shifts, short punchy sentences, a change of tone.
  • Perspective — jot each writer's viewpoint, ready to compare (AO3).

PAT — Purpose, Audience, Tone. Nail these three for each text in 60 seconds and every answer that follows is sharper.

Toolkit · AO2

Language techniques & their effects

Naming a device earns almost nothing. The marks are in the effect — what it makes the reader feel or think.

  • Metaphor / simile — paint a vivid picture; make the abstract concrete.
  • Rhetorical question — pulls the reader in; makes them agree in their head.
  • Direct address ("you") — makes it feel personal, hard to ignore.
  • Statistics / facts — sound authoritative; feel like proof.
  • Emotive language — targets the reader's feelings (pity, fear, anger).
  • Triples (list of three) — build rhythm and emphasis; feel complete.
  • Imperative verbs — command the reader; create urgency.

The golden rule: device → quotation → effect on the reader. Miss the effect and you're only "feature-spotting".

Analysis skill · AO2

Comment on the effect, not the label

Compare two comments on the same line:

"The factory swallowed the little valley whole." ▸ verb "swallowed" — metaphor
Weak (feature-spotting)

"The writer uses a metaphor here. This is effective and makes it interesting."

Strong (device → effect)

"The verb 'swallowed' presents the factory as a hungry predator, making it feel monstrous and unstoppable — so the reader sees the valley as a helpless victim."

Misconception: the marks are not for spotting the metaphor — they're for explaining why 'swallowed' works and what it does to the reader.

Comparison · AO3

Comparing the two texts

Component 2 has a comparison question (AO3): you compare the writers' ideas and perspectives — and often how those views are conveyed — across the 19th and 21st century texts.

  • Compare viewpoints: do the writers agree, disagree, or feel differently about the topic?
  • Use comparative connectives: similarly, likewise, both writers, whereas, however, by contrast, unlike.
  • Weigh the two texts together — don't write everything about Text A, then Text B.

Misconception: the comparison question is not about analysing devices in detail — it's about the writers' ideas and perspectives. Keep both texts side by side throughout.

The evaluation question · AO4

How to structure an evaluation answer

The evaluation question asks for a critical judgement (e.g. "how effectively does the writer argue…"). You judge the effect and back it with evidence. Use a What → How → Why chain (this is just PEE with a judgement on top):

Evaluation = analysis + verdict. Always end the paragraph with a judgement: "This is highly effective because…", "This works well, though…".

Section B · Writing

Transactional writing: form, purpose, audience

Transactional writing gets something done — it informs, persuades, advises or argues to a real reader. Before writing each of the two tasks, decode it:

  • Form — letter? article? speech? report? review? Each has its own layout and conventions.
  • Purpose — persuade, argue, inform, advise, describe.
  • Audience — a head teacher, a local newspaper's readers, MPs, teenagers.

Match the register to the audience. A letter to a council officer is formal ("I am writing to urge…"); a speech to your peers can be warmer and punchier. Getting the register wrong costs AO5 marks even if your ideas are good.

Forms & conventions

Know each form's features

  • Letter — greeting ("Dear…"), formal register, clear paragraphs, sign-off ("Yours faithfully / sincerely").
  • Article — a catchy headline, an engaging opening hook, subheadings possible, a clear voice.
  • Speech — direct address to the audience, rhetorical questions, repetition, a rousing final line.
  • Report — a title, neutral/formal tone, findings and often a recommendation.
  • Review — a personal opinion with reasons, a recommendation (or warning) to the reader.

Structure your writing: plan 4–5 paragraphs, open with a hook, group ideas logically, and close memorably. Organisation is a big slice of AO5.

Persuasive toolkit

The DAFOREST toolkit

For persuasive tasks, DAFOREST is a checklist of moves that win readers over. Weave several in — don't just tick boxes.

Some teachers add an extra A for Alliteration, giving AFOREST. Use whichever you know — the point is the techniques, not the acronym.

Worked model

DAFOREST in action

Here is a short model opening for a speech persuading students to walk to school, with the techniques labelled:

"Picture your street on a still, grey morning. Every year, thousands of tonnes of exhaust choke the air our little brothers and sisters breathe. Doesn't that make you angry? Walk, cycle, share — small choices, cleaner lungs, a fairer future." ▸ direct address ("your") · emotive ("choke", "little brothers and sisters") · rhetorical question · imperative triple ("walk, cycle, share") · triple ("cleaner lungs…")

Notice it doesn't just list devices — each one does a job: pulling you in, stirring feeling, then driving you to act.

Technical accuracy · AO6

AO6 — spelling, punctuation, grammar & vocabulary

Across the GCSE, 20% of the marks are for technical accuracy and ambitious vocabulary — much of it earned in Section B. Easy marks to gain, or throw away.

  • Vary your sentences — mix short and long for control and effect.
  • Punctuate accurately — full stops, commas, and the occasional colon, semicolon or dash used correctly.
  • Reach for precise vocabulary — "devastating" not "very bad"; "compelling" not "good".
  • Proofread — leave 3–4 minutes to catch slips across both tasks.

Ambition with control beats safety with errors. Aim high on vocabulary, but only use words you can spell and punctuate correctly.

Exam-day plan

Working the component for marks

  • Watch the mark tariff — a short retrieval needs a moment; a 10-mark AO2/AO3 answer needs range and depth.
  • Watch the tricky 19th century text — older language is harder; read it twice.
  • Quote briefly and precisely — short, well-chosen quotations beat long copied chunks.
  • In Section B, plan both tasks — a few minutes on form, audience and paragraphs saves each answer.
  • Save time to proofread for AO6.

Split your time by marks: Section A and Section B are worth 40 marks each — give each roughly half the paper (about an hour on each).

Recap

The skills to take into the exam

Read & annotate: Purpose, Audience, Tone (PAT) for both texts.

AO2 analysis: device → quotation → effect on the reader (never just name it).

AO3 comparison: compare the writers' ideas and perspectives, side by side.

AO4 evaluation: analysis + a clear judgement of how successful it is.

Transactional writing: two tasks — match form + purpose + audience; use DAFOREST.

AO5/AO6: organise clearly, vary sentences, punctuate accurately, proofread.
