Mission 1

Why do audiences pay to watch a good person destroyed?

Make a guess: Why do audiences pay to watch a good person destroyed?

Tragedy · Aristotle and the tragic heroScreen 1

Aristotle's definition

In the Poetics (4th century BCE), Aristotle built a theory of tragedy from the Greek plays he knew. In S. H. Butcher's 1895 translation:

  • Tragedy is "an imitation of an action that is serious, complete, and of a certain magnitude;"
  • It works "through pity and fear effecting the proper purgation of these emotions." This purgation is catharsis.
  • Plot matters most: it is "the soul of a tragedy".
  • We pity suffering that is undeserved, and fear for someone like ourselves.

Four terms from Aristotle

The hero is "not eminently good and just", and falls "not by vice or depravity, but by some error or frailty". Aristotle's own example is Sophocles' Oedipus.

Tragedy · Aristotle and the tragic heroScreen 6

Bradley's tragic hero

A. C. Bradley, Shakespearean Tragedy (1904):

  • Tragedy is "essentially a tale of suffering and calamity conducting to death", centred on "a man in high estate".
  • Its centre is "action issuing from character, or in character issuing in action."
  • The hero's one-sidedness is "the fundamental tragic trait": "It is a fatal gift, but it carries with it a touch of greatness."
  • The central feeling is "the impression of waste".
  • Later critics objected that he treats characters as real people.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Why do audiences pay to watch a good person destroyed?

Aristotle's answer is catharsis: watching a good but flawed person fall through an error arouses pity and fear, and the play shapes those feelings into release and understanding. Bradley adds that we feel the waste of something great. Critics still argue about what catharsis means, so treat it as a debate, not a fact.

Mission 2

Why is a wedding ending a convention, not just a happy ending?

Make a guess: Why is a wedding ending a convention, not just a happy ending?

Comedy · conventions and festivityScreen 1

What makes a comedy

  • Aristotle: comedy is "an imitation of characters of a lower type"; their fault "is not painful or destructive".
  • AQA: stories that mainly amuse, where no great disaster occurs, with a love interest and marriage as a focal point.
  • Satire aims the laughter at a society's follies and vices, to expose them. AQA says its comedies ridicule society itself.
  • Tragedy moves from order to catastrophe; comedy moves from disorder to order.
Comedy · conventions and festivityScreen 2

How your exam asks this

  • Edexcel Component 1 (open book, clean copies): Section A, one essay on your Shakespeare comedy using critical reading, 35 marks; Section B, one essay on your other drama, such as The Importance of Being Earnest, 25 marks.
  • Essay practice is for class; this mission gives you the conventions to argue about.
Comedy · conventions and festivityScreen 3

The New Comedy plot

  • Greek New Comedy (Menander) and the Roman comedies of Plautus and Terence gave Europe a stock plot.
  • Young lovers are blocked by a senex (an old father) or a rival.
  • Trickery, disguise and mistaken identity follow; a recognition or discovery clears the way.
  • Northrop Frye (1948) argued that the blockers are overcome or absorbed into a new society round the couple, sealed by a festive ending: a feast, a dance, a wedding.
Comedy · conventions and festivityScreen 4

Festive comedy

  • C. L. Barber's Shakespeare's Festive Comedy (1959) links the comedies to holidays such as May Day and Twelfth Night.
  • On holiday, everyday rules are suspended: misrule, mockery and licence, as under a Lord of Misrule.
  • Barber traces a movement from release to clarification: misrule lets characters see their everyday world more clearly.
  • Festivity has enemies. Many readers see Twelfth Night's steward, Malvolio, as its killjoy.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Why is a wedding ending a convention, not just a happy ending?

Marriage is a convention because it does a job in the plot. From Roman New Comedy onwards it marks the moment when blockers are overcome and disorder becomes a new social order, often with a feast or dance. Because audiences expect it, writers can use it to satisfy, to question (who is left out?) or to unsettle.

Mission 3

Why does The Merchant of Venice play differently now than in 1600?

Make a guess: Why does The Merchant of Venice play differently now than in 1600?

A-level skills · contexts of production and reception (AO3)Screen 1

Two kinds of context

  • Context of production: when, where and how the text was written: its society, beliefs, genre conventions, theatre or publishing, the writer's situation.
  • Context of reception: how readers and audiences have received it since, from first reviews to modern productions and criticism.
  • The words on the page stay the same; what audiences notice, fear and sympathise with changes.
  • Context earns marks when it explains something in the text: ‘this helps explain why…’.

How your exam asks this

  • Edexcel: AO3 is marked in Components 1 and 2, Component 3 Section B and the NEA. The spec asks for the contexts in which texts were written and received.

Jane Eyre's restlessness

A-level skills · contexts of production and reception (AO3)Screen 7

Jane Eyre's first readers and later ones

  • 1847: published under the name Currer Bell.
  • 1848: Elizabeth Rigby, in the Quarterly Review (December), condemned the novel as irreligious and linked its spirit to the revolutions abroad and Chartism at home, the unrest of that year.
  • 1966: Jean Rhys's novel Wide Sargasso Sea retells Bertha's story from the Caribbean.
  • 1979: Sandra Gilbert and Susan Gubar read Bertha as Jane's dark double, her suppressed anger.
  • 1985: Gayatri Spivak argued that Jane's freedom is bought at the colonised Bertha's expense.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Why does The Merchant of Venice play differently now than in 1600?

The text hasn't changed; its audiences have. Written in a society that had expelled its Jews and was gripped by the Lopez affair, the play is now received after the Holocaust, so many audiences hear Shylock's speech as a plea for shared humanity. The strongest answers use both contexts to explain what the text does.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Aristotle's Poetics defines tragedy as a serious action that works "through pity and fear" towards catharsis; plot is "the soul of a tragedy".
  • Four terms: hamartia (error), peripeteia (reversal), anagnorisis (recognition), catharsis (purgation). The hero is neither saint nor villain.
  • Bradley (1904): "action issuing from character", a "fatal gift" with "a touch of greatness", and "the impression of waste". Use critics as views to test, not verdicts to repeat.
  • In Section A, critical reading is assessed (AO5): learn what critics argued and test it against the play.
  • Comedy (Aristotle) shows faults that are "not painful or destructive"; AQA's comedies amuse, avoid disaster, centre on love and marriage, and ridicule society.
  • The New Comedy plot: lovers blocked by a senex or rival, trickery and disguise, a discovery, then a new society sealed by a wedding (Frye).
  • Festive comedy (Barber): holiday misrule moves from release to clarification; festivity has its killjoy, and the ending may leave someone out.
  • In Section A, set a critic's idea (Barber's festivity, Frye's new society) against the ending of your play.
  • Context of production: when, where and how a text was written. Context of reception: how it has been read and staged since.
  • The Merchant of Venice came from an England without a Jewish community and fresh from the Lopez affair; after the Holocaust, its antisemitism is staged and read very differently.
  • Jane Eyre was attacked in 1848 as irreligious and rebellious; feminist and post-colonial critics since 1966 have reread it, including through Bertha.
  • Context earns marks only when it explains something in the text.

Key quotations to learn

  • through pity and fear effecting the proper purgation of these emotions. (Aristotle, Poetics VI, trans. Butcher (1895)) The definition of catharsis.
  • not by vice or depravity, but by some error or frailty (Aristotle, Poetics XIII, trans. Butcher) The basis of hamartia: the hero is not wicked.
  • Of one that loved not wisely, but too well; (Othello, Act 5 Scene 2 (Othello)) Recognition and self-judgement at the end.
  • I stumbled when I saw. (King Lear, Act 4 Scene 1 (Gloucester)) Anagnorisis through blindness.
  • This central feeling is the impression of waste. (Bradley, Shakespearean Tragedy (1904), Lecture I) Why tragedy moves us: loss of greatness.
  • Comedy is, as we have said, an imitation of characters of a lower type (Aristotle, Poetics V, trans. Butcher (1895)) The classical starting point for comedy.
  • It consists in some defect or ugliness which is not painful or destructive. (Aristotle, Poetics V, trans. Butcher) Why comic suffering doesn't hurt.
  • Dost thou think, because thou art virtuous, there shall be no more cakes and ale? (Twelfth Night, Act 2 Scene 3 (Sir Toby)) Festivity against the killjoy.
  • Is there no respect of place, persons, nor time, in you? (Twelfth Night, Act 2 Scene 3 (Malvolio)) Everyday order versus holiday misrule.
  • He hath been most notoriously abus’d. (Twelfth Night, Act 5 Scene 1 (Olivia)) The cost of the festive joke.
