Other Drama: Pre- or Post-1900

Section B of Component 1 is your second play, studied in either the tragic or the comic tradition, and drawn from either before or after 1900. This lesson teaches how to analyse dramatic method and place a play within its period conventions — skills that survive any change of set text.

  • One single-text essay on your other drama play (no comparison is required here).
  • Period matters: a pre-1900 comedy of manners and a post-1900 absurdist play use very different stage languages.
  • AO focus: AO2 dramatic method, AO3 theatrical context, AO5 interpretations dominate this answer.

AO2 · drama as a method

What it means to analyse a play

A play is a score for performance, so AO2 means reading its theatrical grammar, not just its words:

  • Dialogue and subtext — what characters mean beneath what they say; the gap is where drama lives.
  • Stage directions — setting, lighting, silence and business are authored meaning, not stage-manager notes.
  • Structure — act and scene division, the placing of the crisis, the curtain line.
  • Off-stage action — what the dramatist chooses not to show.
  • Space and objects — a room, a door, a prop can carry the play’s central idea.

Test: could your point only be made about a play? If it would work for a novel unchanged, you have not yet analysed drama.

Pre-1900 · traditions

Manners, the well-made play and naturalism

Before 1900, several dramatic traditions matter:

  • Comedy of manners — from Restoration comedy onward: witty, epigrammatic satire of a fashionable society’s hypocrisies and marriage market.
  • The well-made play (la pièce bien faite, associated with Scribe) — tight, machine-like plotting built on secrets, letters and a climactic revelation.
  • Melodrama — heightened emotion, clear moral polarities, spectacle.
  • Naturalism and the problem play — Henrik Ibsen turned the well-made play’s machinery on social issues (marriage, reputation, money), while Chekhov developed indirect action where the real drama happens in the pauses.

AO3 hook: Ibsen’s “problem plays” scandalised first audiences precisely because they refused the tidy moral endings melodrama had trained them to expect.

Post-1900 · movements

Epic theatre, the Absurd and realism

Twentieth-century drama broke the naturalist frame in several directions:

  • Epic theatre — Bertolt Brecht’s Verfremdungseffekt (the alienation or estrangement effect): devices that stop the audience losing themselves in illusion so they judge the action politically rather than weep at it.
  • Theatre of the Absurd — the term coined by critic Martin Esslin (1961) for plays (Beckett, Ionesco) in which a meaningless universe is staged through circularity, non-sequitur and stalled action.
  • Kitchen-sink realism — post-war British drama bringing working-class life and anger onto the stage.
  • Political and verbatim theatre — drama built to intervene in public debate.

Attribution check: “Theatre of the Absurd” is Esslin’s coinage, not the playwrights’ own label — a useful, accurate AO5 point.

Genre · tragedy and comedy after Shakespeare

Modern tragedy and modern comedy

The genre categories carry forward, but they are reworked:

  • Modern / domestic tragedy — Arthur Miller argued in Tragedy and the Common Man (1949) that an ordinary person is as fit a tragic subject as a king. Raymond Williams, in Modern Tragedy, insisted that suffering produced by social systems is genuinely tragic.
  • Against them, George Steiner (The Death of Tragedy) argued that a secular world can only produce pathos, not true tragedy.
  • Modern comedy — satire, tragicomedy and black comedy blur Frye’s clean resolutions; the “happy ending” becomes ironic or withheld.

This debate is pure AO5: two defensible readings of what tragedy even is. Enter it; do not merely report it.

AO3 · theatrical context

The conditions of the stage

For drama, context is often theatrical as much as historical:

  • Censorship — in Britain the Lord Chamberlain licensed and cut plays until 1968, shaping what could be staged and how.
  • Staging conditions — proscenium realism, the bare Brechtian stage, or the intimate studio each produce different meanings.
  • First performance versus revival — a play’s reception shifts as the society around it changes.
  • Audience — who paid, who was addressed, who was scandalised.

Weld context to method: because the censor forbade X, the dramatist displaced the meaning into Y — context that changes the reading, not decoration.

AO5 · interpretation

Reading the other drama through theory

Bring a critical lens that produces a claim about this play’s staging:

  • Feminist — who is granted stage-agency and who is only looked at?
  • Marxist — how do class and money structure who can act and who is acted upon?
  • Genre criticism — is the “comic” resolution genuinely restorative or quietly coercive?

Discipline: not “a Marxist would say the play is about class”, but “the play never lets the servant finish a sentence before a superior enters — the stage itself is organised by rank.”
