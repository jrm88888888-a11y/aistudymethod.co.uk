Mission 1

Mission 1: Sing a higher note. Does the sound reach the far side of the room any sooner?

Make a guess: Sing a higher note. Does the sound reach the far side of the room any sooner?

Quick check
?A loudspeaker plays a steady note to a listener 5 m away in a room with still air. Which of the following travels from the loudspeaker to the listener?
Mission 1

What a wave carries

  • A progressive (travelling) wave transfers energy from place to place.
  • It does not transfer matter. Each particle oscillates about a fixed position.
Mission 1

Transverse and longitudinal

  • Transverse: oscillations perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer. Waves on a string; all EM waves.
  • Longitudinal: oscillations parallel to the direction of energy transfer. Sound; a slinky pushed along its length.
  • Longitudinal waves have compressions (particles close, pressure higher) and rarefactions (spread out, pressure lower).
Quick check
?An electric bell rings inside a sealed glass jar and can be both seen and heard. A pump gradually removes the air from the jar while the bell keeps working. Which prediction, with its explanation, is correct?
Mission 1

Mechanical and electromagnetic

  • Mechanical waves (sound, waves on strings, water waves) need a material medium.
  • Electromagnetic waves need no medium: they cross a vacuum.
Mission 1

Describing a wave

  • Displacement: distance and direction of a particle from its rest position.
  • Amplitude A: maximum displacement.
  • Wavelength λ: distance between neighbouring points that move in step, e.g. crest to crest.
  • Period T: time for one complete oscillation. Frequency f: oscillations per second, in Hz.
  • f = 1/T.
Mission 1

The wave equation

  • In one period the wave moves on one wavelength: v = λ/T, so v = fλ.
  • Written c = fλ.
  • For sound in air and waves on a string, the medium sets v and the source sets f.
  • Change f in the same medium: λ changes, v stays the same.
  • Example: sound at 340 m s⁻¹, f = 680 Hz → λ = 340/680 = 0.50 m.
Mission 1

Two graphs, two meanings

  • Displacement–position graph: a snapshot of the whole wave. Read λ and A.
  • Displacement–time graph: one particle over time. Read T and A.
  • Same shape, different axis: always check the x-axis label.
  • Particle direction: for a wave moving right, a point copies what the point just to its left did a moment ago.
Quick check
?A transverse wave travels along a stretched string. Adjacent crests are 1.2 m apart. A graph of displacement y against time t for one point on the string is a sine curve: y = 0 at t = 0, y is a maximum at t = 5 ms, y = 0 at t = 10 ms, y is a minimum at t = 15 ms and y = 0 at t = 20 ms, after which the pattern repeats. What is the wave speed?
Mission check

?A vibrator sends waves along a stretched string at constant tension. At 25 Hz the wavelength is 0.80 m. The frequency is raised to 100 Hz. What is the new wavelength, and how long does a wave now take to travel 4.0 m along the string?

The medium sets the speed. A higher note has a higher frequency and a shorter wavelength, but the same speed: it arrives no sooner.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Two corks bob on the same ripple, half a wavelength apart. How do their movements compare?

Make a guess: Two corks bob on the same ripple, half a wavelength apart. How do their movements compare?

Mission 2

Phase

  • Phase: how far through its cycle a particle is.
  • One cycle = 360° = 2π rad.
  • Points one wavelength apart move exactly together: in phase.
  • Points half a wavelength apart always move opposite ways: antiphase (180°, π rad).
Mission 2

Phase difference from distance

  • Fraction of a cycle = Δx/λ.
  • Phase difference φ = 2πΔx/λ (rad) = 360° × Δx/λ.
  • Example: λ = 0.80 m, points 0.20 m apart. Δx/λ = ¼ cycle = 90° = π/2 rad.
  • Give phase difference in degrees, radians or as a fraction of a cycle, as the question asks.
Quick check
?Two points on a progressive wave of wavelength 1.2 m are 0.40 m apart along the direction of travel. What is the phase difference between their oscillations?
Mission 2

Phase difference from graphs

  • Two displacement–time traces, same period T. Find the time shift Δt between matching points (crest to crest).
  • φ = 2πΔt/T = 360° × Δt/T.
  • Example: T = 8.0 ms; crests 2.0 ms apart → ¼ cycle = 90°.
  • On a displacement–position graph, use the separation Δx and λ instead.
  • The trace that reaches its crest first leads.
Quick check
?Two displacement–time graphs, P and Q, show the oscillations of two points on a wave. Both have a period of 6.0 ms. Each crest of Q comes 1.0 ms after the matching crest of P. What is the phase difference between P and Q?
Mission check

?A loudspeaker emits a steady 500 Hz note. Two microphones are placed on a straight line from the loudspeaker, 0.17 m apart. The speed of sound is 340 m s⁻¹. How do the oscillations detected by the two microphones compare?

Phase difference = 2πΔx/λ. Half a wavelength apart means antiphase: the two corks always move in opposite directions.

Mission 3: Radio waves and X-rays: the same kind of wave or not? And what really tells them apart?

Make a guess: Radio waves and X-rays: the same kind of wave or not? And what really tells them apart?

Quick check
?Predict first. Which statement describes the nature of electromagnetic waves?
Quick check
?Predict first. A distant star flares, emitting radio waves, visible light and X-rays at the same instant. All three travel to Earth through the vacuum of space. How do their arrival times at Earth compare?
Mission 3

What an EM wave is

  • An electromagnetic (EM) wave is an oscillating electric field and an oscillating magnetic field, travelling together.
  • The two fields are perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel: EM waves are transverse.
  • Energy travels along the direction of travel; the fields oscillate across it.
  • EM waves need no medium: they cross a vacuum.
  • In a vacuum all EM waves travel at the same speed, c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹, whatever their wavelength.
  • Examples: the radio waves from an FM radio station, visible light, the X-rays used in medical imaging.
Mission check

?In a vacuum, the microwaves in a speed camera have a wavelength of 3.0 cm, and the light from a yellow lamp has a wavelength of 600 nm. How do their frequencies compare?

Microwaves and light, like radio waves and X-rays, are the same kind of wave, with the same speed c in a vacuum. Only the wavelength, and so the frequency, tells them apart.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Turning one filter against another can block light, but not sound. What does that tell us about light?

Make a guess: Turning one filter against another can block light, but not sound. What does that tell us about light?

Mission 4

Plane polarised

  • Transverse wave: oscillations perpendicular to the direction of travel. They can point in many directions across it.
  • Plane polarised: the oscillations stay in one plane that contains the direction of travel.
  • For light (EM waves), the plane of oscillation is the plane of the electric field.
  • Unpolarised light (Sun, lamps): the oscillation direction changes rapidly and randomly.
  • Longitudinal wave: oscillations along the direction of travel. Only one direction is possible, so it cannot be polarised.
  • So: if a wave can be polarised, it must be transverse.
Quick check
?Predict first. A long rope passes through a narrow slot in a board. The figure shows the view along the rope towards the board, with the slot and the direction in which a student moves the rope back and forth to send a transverse wave towards the board. Which describes the wave on the far side of the board?
Mission 4

A rope through a slot

  • Shake a rope that passes through a vertical slot.
  • Shake it up and down: the wave passes through.
  • Shake it side to side: the slot stops the rope moving. No wave beyond the slot.
  • Shake it in every direction: only the up-and-down part gets through. The wave beyond is plane polarised.
Quick check
?Predict first. Unpolarised light from a lamp passes through two ideal polarising filters, P1 and P2. The lines drawn on the filters in the figure show their transmission axes. I1, I2 and I3 are the intensities of the light at points 1, 2 and 3. Which ranking of the intensities is correct?
Mission 4

Polarising filters

  • A polarising filter (Polaroid) lets through only oscillations along its transmission axis.
  • Unpolarised light in → plane polarised light out. An ideal filter passes half the intensity.
  • Add a second filter, the analyser. Axes parallel: the light passes on. Axes crossed (at 90°): no light gets through.
  • Rotate the analyser through a full turn: brightest at 0° and 180°, dark at 90° and 270°.
Mission 4

Microwaves and metal grilles

  • A school microwave transmitter sends out plane-polarised microwaves (λ ≈ 3 cm).
  • Rotate the receiver about the line between them: signal maximum when aligned, zero at 90°. Microwaves are transverse.
  • A metal grille (parallel rods) between them:
  • Rods parallel to the electric field: microwaves blocked. The field drives electrons along the rods, which absorb and reflect the energy.
  • Rods perpendicular to the electric field: microwaves pass.
  • This is the opposite of the rope and slot. Learn it separately.
Quick check
?Predict first. Sunlight reflected from a wet road produces glare. Polarising sunglasses reduce this glare much more than tinted sunglasses that let through the same fraction of direct sunlight. Which explanation is supported by physics?
Mission 4

Using polarisation

  • Glare: light reflected from water, wet roads or glass is partly polarised, mostly horizontally.
  • Polarising sunglasses have a vertical transmission axis: they block much of the glare but pass other light.
  • Aerials: radio and TV transmitters send plane-polarised waves.
  • The receiving aerial's rods must line up with the transmitted plane for the strongest signal.
  • Neighbouring transmitters can use different planes (horizontal and vertical) to reduce interference.
Mission check

?A student looks at a calculator's display through a polarising filter and slowly rotates the filter. The display is bright, goes dark after 90° of rotation, and is bright again after 180°. What does this show?

Only transverse waves can be polarised. Light can be polarised and sound cannot: light is transverse, sound is longitudinal.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A progressive wave transfers energy, not matter. Transverse: oscillation ⟂ travel; longitudinal: oscillation ∥ travel.
  • f = 1/T and v = fλ. For sound and waves on strings, the medium sets v and the source sets f.
  • Mechanical waves need a medium; EM waves can cross a vacuum.

Mission 2

  • Phase difference φ = 2πΔx/λ = 2πΔt/T, in radians; × 360°/2π for degrees. λ apart: in phase. λ/2 apart: antiphase.

Mission 3

  • EM waves: oscillating E and B fields, perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel (transverse); no medium; all travel at c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ in a vacuum.

Mission 4

  • Plane polarised: oscillations in one plane. Only transverse waves can be polarised, so polarisation is evidence that light (and all EM waves) are transverse.
  • Crossed polarising filters block all the light; rotating one varies the intensity from maximum to zero every 90°.
  • Metal grille: blocks microwaves when its rods are parallel to the electric field.
