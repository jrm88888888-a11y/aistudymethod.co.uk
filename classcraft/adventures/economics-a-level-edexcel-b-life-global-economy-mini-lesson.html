Mission 1

Mission 1: Can a country's income tell you how well its people really live?

Make a guess: Can a country's income tell you how well its people really live?

Mission 1

Income per person

  • Living standards: how well people live.
    • Material: the goods and services they can consume.
    • Non-material: health, education, safety, free time, a clean environment.
  • Total GDP shows the size of an economy, not how well-off its people are.
  • GDP per capita = GDP ÷ population: average output, and income, per person.
Mission 1

What GDP misses

  • Nonmarket output: cooking, cleaning, caring for children or relatives, home repairs, volunteering. It is produced but not sold, so it is not in GDP.
  • Hidden economy: work not reported, to avoid tax or rules.
  • Leisure: GDP can be the same whether people work 35 or 50 hours a week.
  • Environment: pollution and lost resources are not subtracted; spending to clean up adds to GDP.
  • Health, education, safety and freedom are not measured directly.
  • Distribution: per capita is an average; it does not show who gets the income.
  • Say: "GDP measures market output" — not: "GDP measures welfare".
Quick check
?A parent leaves a paid job to look after their own child at home. The family stops paying for childcare. Nothing else changes. What happens to measured GDP?
Mission 1

Average or typical? Mean and median

  • GDP per capita is a mean: total ÷ number of people.
  • The median is the middle income when incomes are ranked: the typical person.
  • Illustrative data. Five incomes (thousands): 10, 15, 20, 25, 80.
    • Mean = 30. Median = 20.
  • Next year only the top income rises, to 130.
    • Mean = 40 (up 33%). Median = 20 (unchanged).
  • So GDP per capita can rise while the typical person is no better off.
  • When incomes are unequal, median income is the better guide to a typical household.
Mission 1

HDI and social indicators

  • Human Development Index (HDI), published by the UN Development Programme (UNDP) since 1990. Three dimensions:
    • a long and healthy life: life expectancy at birth
    • knowledge: mean years of schooling (adults) and expected years of schooling (children)
    • a decent standard of living: GNI per capita at PPP
  • Each is scaled from 0 to 1 and combined: HDI runs from 0 to 1.
  • Countries with similar GNI per capita can have very different HDI scores, because health and education differ.
  • Other indicators: literacy rate, infant mortality, access to clean water, doctors per 1,000 people, internet access.
  • Limits of HDI: only three dimensions, fixed weights; it ignores inequality and the environment.
Quick check
?Countries X and Y have the same GNI per capita at PPP. X has higher life expectancy and more years of schooling than Y. Which statement is correct?
Mission 1Evaluate

How useful is GDP per capita for comparing living standards?

It depends on:

  • What you compare: between countries, price levels differ, so the same income buys different amounts; over long periods, quality and new products are hard to capture.
  • The nonmarket and hidden economy: larger in some countries, so their output is understated more.
  • Distribution: the same mean can hide very different median incomes.
  • What else differs: working hours, health, environment, freedom.
  • But: GDP data are regular, follow international rules (the UN System of National Accounts), and tend to go with better health and education.
  • So: use GDP per capita alongside other indicators, not alone.
Mission check

?Real GDP per capita in Country P is 20% higher than in Country Q, measured in the same prices. Which finding would most weaken the claim that people in P have a higher material living standard?

GDP counts only what is bought and sold, so compare like with like before judging who lives better.

Mission 2

Growth is not the same as development

  • Economic growth: an increase in real GDP (output) over time.
  • Economic development: an improvement in people's well-being and living standards. It is multidimensional:
    • higher incomes and less poverty
    • better health and longer lives
    • more education and skills
    • more freedom and choice (Amartya Sen: development as expanding what people are able to do and be)
  • Growth can happen without much development: the gains may go to a few, or come with pollution.
  • Development can happen with slow growth: cheap public health and schooling can lengthen and improve lives.
  • Say: "growth is a rise in real output; development is a rise in well-being" — not: "development means growth".
Mission 2

Monetary indicators

  • GDP per capita: output per person. GNI per capita adds net income from abroad.
  • Compare countries at purchasing power parity (PPP): it corrects for differences in price levels (prices tend to be lower in poorer countries).
  • An average (mean) can be pulled up by a few very rich people. The median shows the typical person.
Mission 2

Non-monetary indicators

  • Health: life expectancy at birth, infant mortality rate, maternal mortality.
  • Education: adult literacy rate, years of schooling.
  • Inequality: Gini coefficient, income share of the richest 10%.
  • One indicator alone gives only part of the picture.
Quick check
?A government wants one indicator to track whether its new clinics are reducing deaths among babies. Which is best?
Mission 2

The Human Development Index (HDI)

  • Published by the UN Development Programme (UNDP) since 1990.
  • Three dimensions, four indicators:
    • a long and healthy life: life expectancy at birth
    • knowledge (education): expected years of schooling for children; mean years of schooling for adults
    • a decent standard of living: GNI per capita at PPP
  • Each dimension is scored from 0 to 1, then combined. The HDI runs from 0 to 1.
  • UNDP groups: very high (0.800 and above), high (0.700–0.799), medium (0.550–0.699), low (below 0.550).
  • Say: "HDI measures health, education and income" — not: "HDI measures income".
Mission 2

Worked example: same income, different HDI

  • Illustrative data. Countries P and Q both have GNI per capita of 10,000 international dollars (PPP).
Life expectancyExpected schoolingMean schooling
P72 years13.5 years9 years
Q59 years12 years6 years
  • Each dimension is scaled between a minimum and a maximum (life expectancy: 20 to 85 years):
    • Health: P (72 − 20) ÷ 65 = 0.800; Q (59 − 20) ÷ 65 = 0.600
    • Education: P 0.675; Q 0.533
    • Income: both 0.696
  • HDI: P = 0.722 (high); Q = 0.606 (medium).
  • Same income, but P's people live longer and learn more.
  • Two design features:
    • Income enters as a logarithm: doubling P's income to 20,000 raises HDI only to 0.756. An extra dollar matters more to the poor.
    • The three scores are combined by a geometric mean, so a very low score in one dimension pulls the HDI down more than a simple average would.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. Country S has a GNI per capita at PPP far higher than Country T's, but T has the higher HDI. What best explains this?
Mission 2

Why development is hard to measure

  • Data quality: large informal and subsistence sectors; surveys are costly and infrequent.
  • Averages hide distribution: a rising mean can leave the poor behind.
  • Value judgements: which dimensions count, and how to weight them?
  • Missing dimensions: freedom, security, culture and the environment are hard to put in numbers.
  • PPP estimates depend on price surveys that may be out of date.
Mission 2

How growth and development are linked

  • Growth can fund development:
    • higher incomes for families
    • more tax revenue for schools, clinics, water and roads
  • Development can fuel growth: healthier, better-educated workers are more productive.
  • But growth may not bring development if:
    • the gains go to a few (rising inequality)
    • growth comes from exporting a resource that employs few people
    • it damages health and the environment
  • So the link is likely but not automatic: it depends on how the gains are shared and spent.
Mission 2Evaluate

Is HDI a better measure of development than GNI per capita?

It depends on:

  • The purpose: HDI captures health and education; GNI per capita at PPP better shows material living standards.
  • Distribution: both are averages.
  • What is left out: HDI has no measure of inequality, freedom, the environment or happiness.
  • Weights: HDI weights its three dimensions equally. That is a judgement, not a fact.
  • So: use HDI as a better starting point than income alone, alongside other indicators.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. Over 20 years, Country X's GNI per capita at PPP doubled, mainly from oil exports owned by a small group. Life expectancy and years of schooling barely changed. Which conclusion is best supported?

Income is one part of development, so the same money can buy very different lives.

Mission 3

Developed, emerging, developing

  • Developed economies: high income per head; most output from services; deep financial markets (e.g. UK, US, Japan).
  • Emerging economies: middle income; growing fast; moving from farming to industry and services; opening to trade and investment (e.g. China, India, Brazil, Indonesia).
  • Developing economies: lower income per head; many people in farming; high poverty (e.g. Ethiopia, Malawi).
  • The lines are blurry: groups such as the IMF and World Bank use different definitions.
  • Compare with more than one measure: GDP per head, HDI, literacy, life expectancy (see Mission 2).
Mission 3

From BRICs to BRICS

  • BRICs: Brazil, Russia, India, China. Term coined by economist Jim O'Neill at Goldman Sachs in 2001, predicting their fast growth.
  • South Africa joined the political group in 2010, making BRICS. The group has taken in more members since 2024.
  • Their records since 2000 differ a lot: China and India grew fast; Brazil, Russia and South Africa grew more slowly, with booms and slumps tied to commodity prices.
  • Say: "emerging economy" — not "developing" or "poor" for all of them.

Mission 3

Worked example: nominal to real

  • Nominal GDP uses current prices. Real GDP removes the effect of inflation.
  • Illustrative data: nominal GDP grows 12% in a year; inflation is 5%.

1. Quick estimate: real growth ≈ 12 − 5 = 7%.

2. Exact: 1.12 ÷ 1.05 = 1.0667 → real growth 6.7%.

  • To compare countries, also use PPP: it adjusts for different price levels, so a dollar buys the same basket everywhere.
  • Say: "real GDP per head at PPP" for living standards — not: "GDP" alone.
Mission 3

Why China and India grew so fast

  • Catch-up: a poor economy can copy existing technology, so returns to new capital are high.
  • Structural change: workers moved from low-productivity farming to factories and services.
  • High saving and investment, especially in China: infrastructure, factories, housing.
  • Opening up: China's reforms from 1978 and entry to the WTO in 2001; India's reforms from 1991. Trade and FDI rose.
  • Different paths: China led in manufacturing exports; India grew more through services such as IT.
  • Result: the number of people in China living on under 1.90 dollars a day fell by close to 800 million over 40 years (World Bank, 2022).

Mission 3

Winners and losers for the UK and others

  • Opportunities:
    • bigger export markets, e.g. for financial services, education, cars, machinery
    • cheaper imports: lower prices and higher real incomes for consumers
    • investment flows both ways
  • Threats:
    • competition for UK manufacturing → job losses in some regions (structural unemployment)
    • higher world demand pushes up prices of oil, metals and food
  • Also: economic and political influence in global decisions shifts towards the emerging economies.
  • Overall effect: debated; it differs by sector, region and skill group.
Quick check
?Fast growth in emerging economies raises world demand for oil and metals. For a country that imports most of its oil and metals, what is the most likely short-run effect?
Mission 3Evaluate

Will the fast growth last, and who gains?

It depends on:

  • Catch-up slows as the gap narrows: China's growth rate has fallen since the 2010s.
  • Demographics: China's workforce is ageing; India and much of Africa have young populations.
  • Debt and imbalances: high borrowing for property and infrastructure can end in a slowdown.
  • Inequality and environment: growth can widen gaps and add pollution.
  • Data quality: some statistics are disputed; PPP estimates are revised.
  • For the UK: whether firms and workers move into sectors where the UK has comparative advantage.
Mission check

?GDP per head, PPP, constant prices (World Bank): Vietnam 4,350 (2000) → 13,530 (2023). United States 55,050 (2000) → 74,350 (2023). Which conclusion is correct?

Growth rates and levels tell different stories: fast growth from a low base can leave a large gap for decades.

Mission 4

Mission 4: As countries grow richer, why do workers move from farms to factories and then to services?

Make a guess: As countries grow richer, why do workers move from farms to factories and then to services?

Mission 4

What less-developed economies tend to have in common

  • Low income per head and a large share of people in poverty.
  • A large share of workers in agriculture, often small-scale and low-productivity.
  • A large informal sector: unregistered, untaxed work.
  • High birth rates and a young population; higher infant mortality.
  • Low savings and investment; weak infrastructure.
  • Exports concentrated in a few primary commodities.
  • These are tendencies, not rules: developing economies differ hugely from each other.
  • Developed economies: high income, mostly services. Emerging: fast growth and rapid industrialisation. Developing: lower income, larger primary sector.
Mission 4

Three sectors

  • Primary: extracting natural resources: farming, fishing, forestry, mining.
  • Secondary: manufacturing and construction.
  • Tertiary: services: retail, finance, health, education, tourism.
  • As countries develop, the main share of jobs and output moves primary → secondary → tertiary.
  • Real data: farming's share of the world's jobs fell from 42.8% in 1991 to 26.6% in 2023.
  • In high-income economies, most workers are in services. The share of jobs in manufacturing has fallen: deindustrialisation.
Mission 4

Why the structure changes

  • Productivity in farming rises: better seeds, fertiliser, machines. Fewer workers produce the food a country needs.
  • Demand shifts: as incomes rise, people spend only a little more on food. Spending on manufactured goods, then services, grows faster.
    • Food has a low income elasticity of demand; many services are income elastic.
  • So workers move to factories and cities, and later into services.
  • In rich economies, manufacturing becomes more capital-intensive, and some moves to lower-cost countries. Its share of jobs falls even if its output rises.
  • Global labour force: hundreds of millions of workers in China and India have moved out of farming into the world economy, changing where goods are made.
  • Say: "the share of workers in farming falls" — not: "farm output falls".
Quick check
?Illustrative data. In Country H today, 3% of workers are in the primary sector, 20% in the secondary sector and 77% in the tertiary sector. Thirty years ago the secondary share was 35%. Which statement is best supported?
Mission 4

Trade patterns at different levels of development

  • Low-income countries: mostly export primary commodities (crops, minerals, oil); import manufactures and fuel.
  • Emerging economies: export more manufactures; China became a major exporter of manufactured goods.
  • High-income countries: trade much in services (finance, education, tourism) and in similar manufactured goods with each other (intra-industry trade).
Mission 4

The UK and the BRICs since 2000

  • BRIC: Brazil, Russia, India and China, a group named in 2001 by economist Jim O'Neill. South Africa was invited to join the group in 2010 (BRICS).
  • They were picked out for their large populations and expected fast growth.
  • Since 2000, China and India have grown much faster than the UK, with big shifts from farming into industry and services.
  • The UK is a developed, service-based economy: most jobs and output are in services, after decades of deindustrialisation.
  • Growth among the BRICs has varied a lot: commodity exporters (Brazil, Russia) have grown more unevenly than China and India.
Mission 4Evaluate

Are these characteristics causes or results of low development?

It depends on:

  • Direction of cause: a large farm sector may hold incomes down, but low incomes also keep demand for other goods low.
  • Population: rapid growth strains resources, but a young workforce can be a dividend if there are jobs and schools.
  • Urbanisation: cities raise productivity if housing, water and transport keep up; otherwise slums grow.
  • Policy and institutions: two countries with the same features can develop very differently.
  • So: these features describe where a country is; they do not on their own decide where it goes.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. In Country Z, new seeds and machines double the output of each farm worker. As incomes rise, people spend only a little more on food. What is most likely to happen to the share of Z's workers in farming?

Rising productivity on farms and changing patterns of spending together move workers out of farming.

Mission 5

Why countries trade

Benefits of international trade:

  • Increased competition: domestic firms face rivals, so they cut costs and innovate.
  • Lower prices for consumers and for firms buying inputs.
  • Greater choice: goods a country cannot make at all, and more varieties of those it can.
  • Acquisition of resources: oil, metals, foods a country lacks.
  • More foreign exchange earnings from exports.
  • Access to larger markets, so firms gain economies of scale.
  • More efficient resource allocation and production: resources shift into what each country does relatively well, so less is given up to get each good.
Mission 5

The costs of trade

  • Workers in import-competing industries can lose jobs: structural unemployment if skills do not transfer.
  • Dependence: on imports of food, energy or key parts; exposed to supply shocks.
  • Infant industries may struggle against established foreign firms.
  • Gains are often unequal: some groups gain a lot, others lose.
  • Environmental costs of transport and of production moved abroad.

Mission 5

Absolute advantage

  • Absolute advantage: producing more of a good than another country with the same resources.
  • Two countries have the same quantity of resources. Output if all of them go to one good (illustrative data):
Food (tonnes)Cloth (rolls)
Country A600300
Country B240240
  • A can make more food and more cloth: A has absolute advantage in both.
  • Careful with input tables (hours or workers per unit): there, fewer inputs per unit means absolute advantage.
  • So should A make everything itself? Not if it wants the most it can get.
Mission 5

Comparative advantage

  • Comparative advantage: producing a good at a lower opportunity cost than another country.
  • Output table: opportunity cost of 1 unit of X = output of Y ÷ output of X.
Opportunity cost of…1 tonne of food1 roll of cloth
Country A300 ÷ 600 = 0.5 cloth600 ÷ 300 = 2 food
Country B240 ÷ 240 = 1 cloth240 ÷ 240 = 1 food
  • Food is cheaper (in cloth given up) in A: A has comparative advantage in food.
  • Cloth is cheaper (in food given up) in B: B has comparative advantage in cloth.
  • With different opportunity costs, each country has a comparative advantage in one good.
  • Input table: opportunity cost of 1 unit of X = input per unit of X ÷ input per unit of Y.
  • Same idea for people: a surgeon who types faster than her receptionist still leaves the typing to the receptionist. Each hour typing costs the surgeon an hour of surgery.
  • Say: "lower opportunity cost" — not: "can make more", which is absolute advantage.

Mission 5

Where does comparative advantage come from?

  • Factor endowments (Heckscher–Ohlin): a country exports goods that use its abundant factor intensively.
    • Plenty of labour → labour-intensive goods (garments, assembly).
    • Plenty of capital and skills → capital-intensive goods (aircraft, chemicals).
  • Climate and natural resources: land, minerals, sunshine, rainfall.
  • Technology and human capital: education, research, know-how.
  • History and scale: early starters gain experience and economies of scale.
  • Comparative advantage is dynamic: investment in skills and capital can change it over time.
Mission 5

Assumptions and limitations

The model assumes:

  • Constant opportunity costs (linear PPCs). In reality costs usually rise as a country specialises, so full specialisation is unlikely.
  • No transport costs and no trade barriers. Both can wipe out the gain.
  • Factors move freely between industries inside a country. In reality workers and machines are not easily switched.
  • Perfect knowledge, full employment, and only two countries and two goods.
  • Terms of trade fall between the two opportunity costs; where they settle decides who gains most.
  • Gains are totals: some groups inside each country lose.
Mission 5

Evaluate: how useful is comparative advantage?

It depends on…

  • Transport costs and barriers. Low costs make the gains real; high tariffs or freight cancel them.
  • Who gains. Total output rises, but workers in shrinking industries may lose unless they retrain or are compensated.
  • Risk of over-specialisation. Real case: ready-made garments earned over 80% of Bangladesh's export revenue in 2022–23. Strong comparative advantage, but heavy reliance on one industry.
  • Time. Today's comparative advantage may not be tomorrow's; some countries protect infant industries to build a new one.
  • What it misses. Much trade is between similar rich countries in similar goods (cars for cars), driven by economies of scale and variety.

Mission 6

What globalisation means

  • Globalisation: the growing integration and interdependence of the world's economies.
  • Its main characteristics:
    • more trade in goods and services, faster than world output grows
    • more capital flows across borders: foreign direct investment (FDI) and portfolio investment
    • more movement of people: migration for work and study
    • global supply chains: one product made in stages in many countries
    • the spread of multinational corporations (MNCs), technology and ideas
  • Edexcel also calls MNCs transnational corporations.
  • Interdependence cuts both ways: shocks in one economy now spread quickly to others.

Mission 6

Why globalisation sped up

  • Trade liberalisation: tariffs cut through GATT rounds (from 1947) and the WTO (from 1995); more trading blocs and agreements.
  • New economies joined world markets: China's reforms (from 1978) and WTO entry (2001); the former USSR and Eastern Europe after 1991. Hundreds of millions more workers became part of world trade.
  • Lower transport costs: containers, larger ships, cheaper air freight.
  • Lower communication costs: the internet and mobile networks let a firm run a supply chain across continents.
  • Capital market liberalisation: exchange controls removed, so money moves freely; global financial markets.
  • Growth of MNCs: firms spreading production and sales across many countries.
  • Specialisation by country follows comparative advantage: each stage of production moves to where its opportunity cost is lowest.
Mission 6

Supply chains, capital and people

  • A global supply chain: design in one country, parts from several, assembly in another, sales worldwide.
  • Much trade is now in parts and components, not finished goods.
  • Capital flows: firms build or buy factories abroad (FDI); investors buy foreign shares and bonds (portfolio).
  • Labour mobility: migrants fill skill gaps and send home remittances; students and skilled workers move between countries.
  • Result: specialisation by country and by stage of production.

Mission 6

Consequences for developed economies

Possible gains:

  • Cheaper imports: higher real incomes; lower inflation.
  • Bigger export markets, especially for high-value services.
  • Access to foreign capital and skills.

Possible costs:

  • Deindustrialisation: import-competing manufacturing shrinks.
  • Structural unemployment in regions that relied on those industries; pressure on low-skill wages; wider inequality.
  • Real case: US local areas more exposed to Chinese imports saw larger falls in manufacturing jobs in the 1990s and 2000s (Autor, Dorn and Hanson, 2013).
  • Exposure to global shocks, and pressure to compete on tax and regulation.
Mission 6

Consequences for emerging and developing economies

  • Emerging economies (e.g. China, India, Vietnam) grew fast through manufacturing exports and inward FDI.

Possible gains:

  • Export-led growth, jobs, new technology and skills from MNCs.
  • World poverty: the share of people living on under $3.00 a day (2021 prices, PPP) fell from 43.4% in 1990 to 10.1% in 2024.
  • That fall happened while trade grew; how much trade caused it is debated.

Possible costs:

  • Dependence on a few primary commodities with volatile prices.
  • Poor pay and working conditions; weak environmental rules.
  • Widening inequality inside countries; some low-income countries are left out.
  • Brain drain: skilled workers emigrate.

Mission 6

Agents, macro objectives and global events

Who gains and who loses:

AgentPossible gainsPossible losses
Consumerslower prices, more choice—
Workersjobs in export industriesjobs and wages in import-competing industries
Firmslarger markets, cheaper inputs, scalestronger foreign competition
Governmenttax from growthharder to tax mobile firms and capital
  • Interdependence means global events hit every macro objective:
  • A world recession cuts demand for exports → AD falls → lower output, employment; the current account often worsens.
  • A world commodity price surge raises firms' costs → SRAS falls → higher prices, lower output; importers' current account worsens.
  • Real case: in the 2009 global recession world trade fell from 60.3% to 51.9% of world GDP.
Mission 6Graph lab

Two kinds of global shock

  • Takeaway: a fall in export demand is a demand-side shock (AD); a rise in world input prices is a supply-side shock (SRAS). Same "global event", different effects on prices.
Mission 6

Evaluate: does globalisation benefit a country?

It depends on…

  • Which group. Consumers and exporters gain; workers in import-competing industries may lose unless they retrain, move or are compensated.
  • What the country sells. Diversified manufactures and services are less exposed than one or two primary commodities.
  • Institutions. Strong education, infrastructure and rules on pay and pollution help a country capture gains and limit harm.
  • Time. Job losses come fast; gains in productivity and real incomes build up slowly.
  • Direction of travel. World trade relative to GDP has grown little since 2008 (60.3% then, 56.7% in 2024): some call this "slowbalisation".
  • Say: "globalisation increases interdependence" — not: "globalisation means everyone is better off".

Globalisation's gains are real but uneven: who gains and who loses decides the politics as well as the economics.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Real GDP per capita is the usual starting point for comparing living standards.
  • GDP leaves out nonmarket output.
  • It also misses the hidden economy, leisure, the environment and distribution.
  • HDI adds health and education to income.

Mission 2

  • Growth is a rise in real output; development is a rise in well-being on many dimensions.
  • HDI combines health, education and income (GNI per capita at PPP) into one index from 0 to 1.
  • No single number captures development: use several indicators.

Mission 3

  • Emerging economies: middle income, fast growth, structural change, opening up. BRICs (2001) → BRICS.
  • Index = value ÷ base × 100; real growth ≈ nominal growth − inflation. Use real GDP per head at PPP to compare.
  • Their growth brings opportunities (markets, cheaper imports) and threats (competition, dearer commodities) to the UK and others.

Mission 4

  • Less-developed economies tend to have more workers in agriculture, younger populations and more informal work.
  • As incomes rise, jobs move from primary to secondary to tertiary sectors.
  • Emerging economies such as China and India have reshaped the global labour force since 2000.

Mission 5

  • Absolute advantage: more output from the same resources. Comparative advantage: lower opportunity cost.
  • Specialising by comparative advantage and trading at terms between the two opportunity costs lets both countries consume beyond their own PPC.
  • The model rests on strong assumptions; the gains are real but unequally shared.

Mission 6

  • Globalisation: growing integration and interdependence through trade, capital flows, migration, supply chains and MNCs.
  • Causes: liberalisation, new economies joining world markets, cheaper transport and communication, free capital movement, MNCs.
  • Gains are large in total but uneven between groups and countries; global shocks spread faster.
