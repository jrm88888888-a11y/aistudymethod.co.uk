Mission 1

Mission 1: Oily molecules avoid water, yet they wrap every cell in a thin film. How does that film build itself?

Make a guess: Oily molecules avoid water, yet they wrap every cell in a thin film. How does that film build itself?

Mission 1

Lipids are more than fat

  • Lipids dissolve in non-polar solvents but only sparingly in water: they are hydrophobic.
  • Lipids include fats, phospholipids and steroids, such as cholesterol.
  • Not every lipid is an energy store.
Mission 1

A head and two tails

  • A phospholipid has a phosphate group and two fatty acid chains, joined to glycerol.
  • The phosphate end is the head; the fatty acids are the tails.
Mission 1

Amphipathic: two personalities

  • Head: phosphate group, charged. It is hydrophilic: it interacts with water.
  • Tails: hydrocarbon chains, non-polar. They are hydrophobic.
  • Polar, hydrophilic head; non-polar, hydrophobic tails.
  • Say: "the tails are excluded from water" — not: "the tails repel water".
Mission 1

The bilayer builds itself

  • (1) On water, phospholipids form a single layer: heads in the water, tails in the air.
  • (2) Under water, tails cannot stay in the air. They hide by pairing up tail to tail.
  • (3) Result: a phospholipid bilayer. Heads face the water on both sides; tails form a non-polar core.
  • No enzymes, no energy from the cell: water molecules hydrogen-bond to each other and exclude the tails.
  • A sheet's edges still expose tails, so it closes into a sphere.
  • The molecules are not covalently bonded to each other, so the bilayer is fluid.
Quick check
?In a plasma membrane, the phosphate heads of the phospholipids face the cytosol and the extracellular fluid, and the fatty acid tails face each other in the interior of the membrane. Which statement best explains this arrangement?
Mission 1

Steroids and cholesterol

  • Cholesterol is a steroid lipid.
  • In animal cell membranes, cholesterol sits among the phospholipids and gives stability.
  • Steroid hormones support growth and development, energy metabolism and homeostasis.
Quick check
?A drug removes most of the cholesterol from the plasma membranes of cultured animal cells but does not otherwise damage the cells. Which prediction about the treated cells is most likely correct?
Mission check

?Lipoproteins carry cholesterol and triglycerides in blood plasma, which is mostly water. Each lipoprotein has an oily core of cholesterol and triglyceride, surrounded by a single layer of phospholipids (with some proteins). How are the phospholipids in this layer most likely arranged?

One rule builds membranes, vesicles and lipoproteins: heads to the water, tails away from it.

Mission 2: DNA's two strands are held together only by weak bonds. So why doesn't the molecule fall apart?

Make a guess: DNA's two strands are held together only by weak bonds. So why doesn't the molecule fall apart?

Mission 2

The nucleotide

  • DNA and RNA are polymers. Their monomers are nucleotides.
  • Each nucleotide has three parts:
  • a pentose (5-carbon sugar) in the middle
  • a phosphate group, bonded to the sugar
  • a nitrogen-containing base, also bonded to the sugar
  • DNA nucleotide: deoxyribose + phosphate + one base: Adenine, Cytosine, Guanine or Thymine.
  • RNA nucleotide: ribose + phosphate + one base: A, C, G or Uracil.
  • Say: "a nucleotide contains a base" — not: "a nucleotide is a base".
Quick check
?Each nucleotide in a nucleic acid contains a phosphate group and a nitrogenous base. What is its third structural component?
Mission 2

Nucleotides join into a strand

  • Dehydration synthesis: the phosphate of one nucleotide forms a covalent bond with the sugar of the next. Water is released.
  • Repeated, this builds a sugar–phosphate backbone: one continuous chain of covalent bonds. Strong.
  • The bases stick out from the backbone. Each is bonded only to its own sugar.
  • n nucleotides in a chain: n − 1 bonds, n − 1 waters released.
  • Say: "the backbone is sugar–phosphate–sugar" — not: "the bases are joined into a chain".
Mission 2

A strand has two different ends

  • The pentose's carbons are numbered 1′ to 5′ ("five prime").
  • One end of a strand has a free phosphate on a 5′ carbon: the 5′ end.
  • The other end has a free –OH on a 3′ carbon: the 3′ end.
  • New nucleotides join only at the 3′ end. The incoming phosphate bonds to the 3′ –OH.
  • So a strand grows in the 5′ → 3′ direction.
  • Say: "5′ and 3′ name the two ends of every strand" — not: "the 5′ strand and the 3′ strand".
Quick check
?A modified nucleotide lacks the hydroxyl (–OH) group on the 3′ carbon of its sugar but is otherwise normal. During the synthesis of a DNA strand, one of these modified nucleotides is added to the growing strand. Which prediction about the strand is most likely correct?
Mission 2

Two strands: the double helix

  • DNA has two polynucleotide strands.
  • Bases on opposite strands pair by hydrogen bonds.
  • Pairing is complementary: A with T, G with C. Never A–G or C–T.
  • The strands are antiparallel: one runs 5′ → 3′, its partner 3′ → 5′.
  • The paired strands twist into a double helix.
  • One hydrogen bond is weak. Thousands along a molecule hold the strands firmly together.
  • Yet each can break: the strands can be separated without breaking the backbone.
  • Say: "complementary bases" — not: "matching" or "identical bases".
Mission 2

Purines and pyrimidines

  • Purines: A and G. Two rings (a double ring).
  • Pyrimidines: C, T and U. One ring.
  • Every base pair is one purine + one pyrimidine: A–T, G–C.
  • So every pair is the same width. Two purines would be too wide; two pyrimidines too narrow.
  • The helix keeps one regular shape whatever the base sequence: a stable structure.
  • In RNA, A pairs with U: still purine with pyrimidine.
  • The same pairing rules are found in all organisms: conserved through evolution.
  • Say: "a purine pairs with a pyrimidine" — not: "purines pair with purines".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly describes how the two strands of a DNA double helix are held together?
Quick check
?Analysis of a sample of double-stranded DNA from a hypothetical bacterium shows that 21% of its nitrogenous bases are adenine. What percentage of its bases are cytosine?

DNA and RNA compared

  • DNA is typically double-stranded; RNA is typically single-stranded.
  • In RNA, A pairs with U wherever bases pair.
Quick check
?The genetic material of a newly discovered hypothetical virus is a nucleic acid, and a researcher predicts that it is RNA. The nucleic acid will be broken down into its components, which will then be identified. If the prediction is correct, which result is expected?
Mission check

?In DNA, each A–T pair is held together by two hydrogen bonds and each C–G pair by three. Samples of DNA from five hypothetical species were heated slowly. The graph shows the temperature at which half of the DNA in each sample had separated into single strands, plotted against the percentage of the sample's base pairs that are C–G pairs. Which reasoning best explains the trend in the data?

Covalent backbones stay intact; only the hydrogen bonds between strands give way, and more of them means a firmer hold.

Mission 3

Mission 3: DNA uses just four kinds of base. How can that be enough to hold the instructions for a whole organism?

Make a guess: DNA uses just four kinds of base. How can that be enough to hold the instructions for a whole organism?

Mission 3

The message is in the sequence

  • The sugar–phosphate backbone is the same all along. Only the bases vary.
  • The order of bases, the base sequence, is the information.
  • Any base can follow any other: nothing fixes the order.
  • Same four bases, different order, different message. Like letters spelling different words.
  • Say: "the information is in the base sequence" — not: "in the shape of the helix".
Mission 3

Pairing copies the message and sends it out

  • Copy: the strands separate; each is a template. Free nucleotides pair with complementary bases. Two identical molecules result.
  • Express: a gene's sequence is copied into messenger RNA (mRNA), again by complementary pairing (A with U).
  • In eukaryotes, mRNA carries information from DNA in the nucleus to ribosomes in the cytoplasm.
  • One rule, two jobs. Details: DNA replication, transcription.
Mission 3

Three kinds of RNA

  • mRNA: carries a gene's base sequence to a ribosome.
  • tRNA: each kind binds a specific amino acid. Its anticodon pairs with an mRNA codon.
  • rRNA: with proteins, forms ribosomes.
  • An RNA's base sequence and its structure decide its function.
  • A tRNA is one strand that folds: parts of the strand base-pair with each other.
Quick check
?In a hypothetical species of bacterium, a mutation changes three bases in the gene for one kind of tRNA. The changed bases are outside the anticodon, and the tRNA has the same number of nucleotides as before, but it no longer works in translation. Which statement best explains why the tRNA no longer works?
Mission check

?Two DNA molecules from two hypothetical species of bacteria each contain the same number of nucleotides, and in both, 30% of the bases are adenine, 30% thymine, 20% guanine and 20% cytosine. The two molecules carry different genetic information. Which statement best explains how this is possible?

Four bases are plenty: the message is in their order, and the order can be anything, at any length.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Every protein in your body is built from just twenty building blocks. How can so few make so many?

Make a guess: Every protein in your body is built from just twenty building blocks. How can so few make so many?

Mission 4

One plan, twenty versions

  • Amino acids are the monomers of proteins.
  • A central carbon atom carries four groups:
  • Amine group (–NH2), carboxyl group (–COOH), a hydrogen atom, and an R group (side chain).
  • The twenty amino acids in proteins differ only in their R group.
  • Say: "amine group, –NH2" — not: "–NH3". Nitrogen's third bond is to the central carbon.
Quick check
?Which statement correctly describes the R groups of amino acids?
Mission 4

The peptide bond

  • (1) Line up two amino acids: the carboxyl group of one beside the amine group of the next.
  • (2) –OH from the carboxyl and –H from the amine leave as one water molecule.
  • (3) C joins N: a peptide bond, –CO–NH–. A covalent bond. Two amino acids make a dipeptide.
  • This is dehydration synthesis.
  • Hydrolysis reverses it: adding water breaks the peptide bond.
  • The R groups take no part.
Quick check
?During protein synthesis, a free amino acid is joined to the end of a growing polypeptide. Which statement correctly describes how the amino acid becomes part of the polypeptide?
Mission 4

Worked example: counting bonds and water

  • Many amino acids joined in a chain: a polypeptide.
  • A chain has two ends: a free amine group at one, a free carboxyl group at the other.
  • n amino acids → n − 1 peptide bonds → n − 1 water molecules released.
  • 3 amino acids: 2 bonds, 2 H2O.
  • Any of the twenty can sit at any position, repeats allowed, in any order, in chains of any length. So the number of possible sequences is effectively unlimited.
Quick check
?A polypeptide of 150 amino acids is assembled by dehydration synthesis. How does the number of water molecules change as the whole chain is formed?
Mission 4

Three families of R group

  • R groups are hydrophobic/nonpolar, hydrophilic/polar, or ionic (charged).
  • The backbone is the same in every chain. The R groups give a polypeptide its properties.
  • R groups interact with water and with each other. That decides how the chain folds (see Mission 5).
Quick check
?In a water-soluble protein of a hypothetical bacterium, the amino acid at position 40 has a nonpolar R group. It lies in the protein's interior, surrounded by other nonpolar R groups. Which prediction about replacing it with an amino acid that has an ionic R group is best supported?
Mission check

?Two short polypeptides, P and Q, are each made of the same five amino acids, but the amino acids are joined in a different order in P and in Q. Which statement about P and Q is correct?

Twenty kinds, joined in any order, any number of times, to any length. That is how so few make so many.

Make a guess: A protein is only a chain of amino acids. So how does it fold into the exact shape its job needs?

Mission 5

Sequence, shape, job

  • A polypeptide folds into a precise three-dimensional shape.
  • Bonds between parts of the chain hold that shape. Most are weak; together they are strong.
  • The shape decides the function: enzymes, hormones, antibodies, transport, support.
  • The same sequence folds the same way, every time.
  • Different sequences fold differently. So endless sequences give endless proteins.
  • Four levels describe the shape: primary, secondary, tertiary, quaternary.
Mission 5

Primary structure

  • Primary structure: the sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide.
  • Held by peptide bonds. Set by the gene.
  • The sequence, with the exact position of each amino acid, determines every later level.
  • Change one amino acid, anywhere, and the shape can change.
  • Say: "primary structure = the order of amino acids" — not: "which amino acids are present".
Mission 5

Secondary structure

  • The backbone carries polar C=O and N–H groups.
  • Hydrogen bonds form between the C=O of one peptide bond and the N–H of another, at regular positions.
  • α-helix: the chain coils; each turn is held to the next.
  • β-pleated sheet: lengths of chain lie side by side, folded like pleats, held to each other.
  • R groups point away from the backbone and take no part in these bonds, so helices and sheets form in many sequences.
  • Say: "backbone hydrogen bonds" — not: "bonds between R groups".
Quick check
?Alpha-helices are found in many proteins whose amino acid sequences are very different from one another. Which statement best explains why alpha-helices can form in polypeptides with such different sequences?
Mission 5

Tertiary structure: R groups lock the fold

  • The chain, with its helices and sheets, folds further into its overall 3D shape: tertiary structure.
  • Held by bonds between R groups:
  • Hydrogen bonds: between polar R groups. Weak, but many.
  • Ionic bonds: between a –COO⁻ and an –NH3⁺ R group. pH-sensitive.
  • AP calls these ionic interactions.
  • Disulfide bridges: covalent S–S bonds between two cysteines. Strong.
  • Hydrophobic interactions: non-polar R groups cluster together, away from water.
Mission 5

Oily inside, watery outside

  • In a water-soluble protein: non-polar R groups cluster in the core.
  • Polar and charged R groups sit on the surface, in contact with water. So the protein dissolves.
  • Why: water molecules hydrogen-bond to each other and exclude non-polar groups.
  • Say: "non-polar R groups are excluded from water" — not: "they repel water".
Quick check
?A hypothetical protein consists of one polypeptide whose tertiary structure includes three disulfide bridges. A researcher adds a chemical that breaks disulfide bridges but does not break peptide bonds or affect any other interactions in the protein. Which prediction is best supported?
Mission 5

Quaternary structure

  • Some proteins are built from more than one polypeptide: quaternary structure.
  • The polypeptides are held together by the same kinds of bond as tertiary structure.
  • Example: hemoglobin, four polypeptides.
  • All four levels together decide what the protein does.
Mission 5

Denaturation

  • (1) A folded protein: its shape held by many weak bonds.
  • (2) High temperature: more vibration breaks hydrogen bonds and ionic bonds. The chain unfolds.
  • (3) pH change: gaining or losing H⁺ changes the charges on R groups. Ionic and hydrogen bonds break.
  • A change in the chemical environment can do the same.
  • This loss of shape is denaturation. The protein can no longer do its job.
  • Peptide bonds stay intact: the sequence is unchanged.
  • Sometimes the shape returns when conditions are restored; often it does not.
  • Cold does not denature: it slows molecules down, and activity returns on warming.
  • Say: "the enzyme is denatured" — not: "the enzyme is killed".
Quick check
?A strong acid is added to a solution of an enzyme that normally works at about pH 7, lowering the pH to 1. The enzyme loses all of its activity. Which statement best describes what has happened to the enzyme?
Mission 5

Enzymes: globular proteins with an active site

  • Enzymes are globular proteins: compact, folded, usually soluble.
  • The active site is a small pocket made of only a few amino acids.
  • But the rest of the folded chain holds those few in exactly the right places.
  • So the active site's shape and properties come from the enzyme's tertiary structure.
  • The substrate binds to the active site, forming an enzyme–substrate complex.
  • Denature the enzyme: the active site changes and the substrate no longer binds.
Quick check
?A hypothetical enzyme has its highest rate of reaction at 40 °C. At 45 °C, its rate is lower. Which statement best explains the lower rate at 45 °C?
Mission check

?A mutation replaces one amino acid in an enzyme at a position on the surface far from the active site in the folded protein. The enzyme loses most of its activity. What is the best explanation?

Sequence sets shape, and shape sets function. One amino acid in the wrong place can be enough.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Phospholipids are amphipathic: a hydrophilic phosphate head and two hydrophobic tails; in water they form bilayers by themselves.
  • Steroids, such as cholesterol, are non-polar lipids with different jobs: membrane stability, hormones.

Mission 2

  • Nucleotide = pentose + phosphate + base. Condensation joins nucleotides into a strand with a covalent sugar–phosphate backbone.
  • DNA: two strands held by hydrogen bonds between complementary bases (A–T, G–C), twisted into a double helix. RNA: ribose, uracil, one strand.
  • The two strands are antiparallel.
  • Strands have a 5′ end and a 3′ end and grow only at the 3′ end; every pair is purine + pyrimidine, so the helix has a constant width.

Mission 3

  • The base sequence is the information; the backbone is the same throughout. Complementary pairing lets the sequence be copied and passed to RNA.
  • mRNA carries the message to ribosomes; ribosomes are built from rRNA and protein.

Mission 4

  • Every amino acid: central carbon + amine group + carboxyl group + H + R group; only R differs.
  • Dehydration synthesis joins carboxyl to amine: a covalent peptide bond; R groups (nonpolar, polar, ionic) shape the protein.

Mission 5

  • The amino acid sequence decides how a chain folds; bonds between parts of the chain hold the shape; the shape decides the job.
  • Primary (sequence) → secondary (backbone H bonds: α-helix, β-pleated sheet) → tertiary (R-group bonds) → quaternary (more than one polypeptide).
  • Heat and pH changes break the weak bonds: the protein is denatured, though its peptide bonds survive.
