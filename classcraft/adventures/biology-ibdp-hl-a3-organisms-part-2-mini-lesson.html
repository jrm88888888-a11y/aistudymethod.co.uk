Mission 1

Speciation without a barrier

  • Sympatric speciation: populations in the same area become reproductively isolated.
  • No geographical barrier. Gene flow is cut by:
    • timing: they breed at different times (temporal isolation);
    • behaviour: different courtship or mate choice;
    • ecology: they use different foods, hosts or soils;
    • chromosomes: a sudden change in chromosome number.
  • Example: two Howea palm species on tiny Lord Howe Island. They grow on different soils and flower at different times.
Mission 1

Allopatric and sympatric side by side

  • Both need reproductive isolation: gene flow must stop.
  • Both then need the gene pools to diverge until fertile interbreeding fails.
  • Allopatric: a geographical barrier; populations live apart.
  • Sympatric: same area; isolation by timing, behaviour, ecology or chromosomes.
  • Isolation can be geographic, behavioural or temporal.
  • Say: "sympatric means without a geographical barrier" — not: "a smaller barrier".
Quick check
?What do allopatric speciation and sympatric speciation have in common?
Mission 1

Chromosome number: a shared trait

  • Members of one species share the same chromosome number.
  • A hybrid of parents with different numbers gets unmatched sets.
  • In meiosis, chromosomes must pair with a homologous partner. Unmatched ones cannot.
  • Mule: 32 (horse) + 31 (donkey) = 63. Pairing fails: almost no viable gametes.
  • So crosses between species with different chromosome numbers rarely give fertile offspring.
Mission 1

A new species in one generation

  • (1) Species P (2n = 14) × species Q (2n = 18). Hybrid: 7 + 9 = 16 chromosomes, none with a partner. Sterile.
  • (2) The chromosome number doubles (e.g. a failed cell division): 32. Every chromosome now has a partner. Meiosis works: fertile.
  • (3) Polyploid × P: offspring get 16 + 7 = 23, unpaired. Sterile. The polyploid is isolated from both parents: a new species, formed sympatrically, at once.
  • Polyploid: more than two complete sets of chromosomes. Common in plants (bread wheat has six sets).
  • Named example: knotweeds and smartweeds (Persicaria): many species formed by hybridisation and polyploidy.
  • Illustrative chromosome numbers.
Quick check
?Suppose one Persicaria species has 20 chromosomes in each diploid cell and another has 24. A hybrid forms between them, and in one hybrid plant the chromosome number doubles. Which statement is correct?
Mission 1

When "can they interbreed?" makes no sense

  • The biological species concept needs sexual reproduction.
  • Asexual species: no interbreeding at all, so the test cannot be applied.
  • Bacteria: genes move between species by horizontal gene transfer (e.g. plasmids carrying antibiotic-resistance genes).
  • So bacterial gene pools are not closed, and "species" there is set by other criteria, such as DNA similarity.
Mission 1

One ancestor, many species

  • Adaptive radiation: one ancestral species gives rise to many species, each adapted to a different niche.
  • Fastest when new habitats or vacant niches appear: new islands, or after a mass extinction.
  • Example: Hawaiian honeycreepers. One finch-like colonist gave rise to dozens of species, with beaks for nectar, seeds, insects and grubs.
  • Close relatives can then coexist without competing, so biodiversity rises.
Quick check
?A single species of finch colonised a remote group of islands that had no other land birds. Its descendants now form many species, each with a beak suited to a different food. Which statement explains this?
Mission check

?Two populations of a fly species live in the same orchard but lay their eggs on different fruit species, which ripen at different times, so the flies rarely mate with each other. Which type of speciation could this lead to, and why?

No mountain or sea is needed. Anything that stops gene flow, even a calendar or a doubled set of chromosomes, can start a new species.

Mission 2

Mission 2: The first human genome took years and a fortune to read. What can we do now that it is quick and cheap?

Make a guess: The first human genome took years and a fortune to read. What can we do now that it is quick and cheap?

Mission 2

Reading whole genomes

  • Genome sequencing finds the order of nucleotides in all of an organism's DNA.
  • Human Genome Project (1990–2003): about 3 billion base pairs; about 20 000–21 000 protein-coding genes.
  • Thousands of genomes are now sequenced: bacteria, crops, the malaria mosquito, and extinct Neanderthals, from bone.
Mission 2

Faster and cheaper

  • High-throughput ("next-generation") sequencing:
    • DNA is cut into millions of short fragments.
    • All are sequenced at the same time.
    • Computers assemble overlapping reads into the genome.
  • A human genome now takes about a day.

Mission 2

Comparing genomes: bioinformatics

  • Bioinformatics: storing and analysing sequence data in databases, using computer software.
  • Between individuals: single-base differences (SNPs) are linked to traits and disease risk.
  • Between species: more similar sequences mean a more recent common ancestor. This tests evolutionary relationships.
Mission 2

Genomes in medicine

  • Personalised medicine: choose a treatment or dose to suit a person's genotype.
  • Cancer: sequence a tumour; choose drugs that target its particular mutations.
  • Rare diseases: a genome can reveal the cause, ending years of uncertain diagnosis.
  • A genome shows risks and likely responses, not a fixed future.
Quick check

Mission 3

Three levels of biodiversity

  • Biodiversity: the variety of life, at three levels.
  • Ecosystem (habitat) diversity: the range of habitats or ecosystems, e.g. sand dune, woodland, meadow, stream.
  • Species diversity: the number of species and how evenly the individuals are spread among them.
  • Genetic diversity: the variety of alleles within a species.
Quick check
?A survey of an island records three findings: the island contains six distinct ecosystems; 420 species have been identified; and its population of one lizard species carries 18 alleles at a studied gene, compared with 4 alleles in the same species on a neighbouring island. Which of these findings describe the island's biodiversity?
Mission 3

Richness and evenness

Illustrative data: two meadows, same four species, 40 plants each.

SpeciesMeadow AMeadow B
Buttercup1031
Clover103
Daisy103
Plantain103
  • Species richness: the number of species. Both meadows: 4.
  • Species evenness: how evenly individuals are spread among the species. A is even; B is dominated by buttercup.
  • Species diversity needs both. Meadow A is more diverse.
  • Say: "diversity depends on richness and evenness" — not: "diversity is the number of species".
Mission 3

How many species are there?

  • About 2 million species have been discovered, named and described.
  • Estimates of the true total are several times higher. Many insects, fungi and microorganisms are still unknown.
  • Fossil evidence suggests more species are alive today than at any time in Earth's past.
  • Counts depend on classification. Splitters recognise more species; lumpers fewer.
  • Classification is pattern recognition: the same observations can be classified in different ways.
Quick check
?Two taxonomists study the same collection of ground beetles. One recognises 12 species. The other recognises 4 species, treating the small differences in colour and size that separate the other taxonomist's species as variation within a species. Which statement about this situation is correct?
Mission 3

One survey is not enough

  • Biodiversity varies from place to place and over time.
  • One survey is a snapshot. It cannot show a decline.
  • Repeat the survey with the same method and effort to show a change in richness or evenness.
  • Richness can stay the same while evenness falls.
  • Surveys by expert scientists and by citizen scientists both contribute.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A woodland was surveyed for ground beetles in 2000 and again in 2020 using the same pitfall-trap method and the same trapping effort. In 2000, 8 species were caught and the most abundant species made up 20% of the individuals. In 2020, 8 species were caught and the most abundant species made up 75% of the individuals. What do these results show?
Mission check

?Illustrative data. Two hedgerows were sampled for insects with the same method and effort. Each had the same 6 species and 120 insects in total. In hedgerow X, one species made up 100 of the 120 insects. In hedgerow Y, each species had 20 insects. Which statement is correct?

Counting species is only half the story. How evenly the individuals are shared out decides which habitat is more diverse.

Mission 4

Genetic diversity

  • Genetic diversity: the variety of alleles within a species or population.
  • Every member of a species has the same genes. They differ in which alleles they carry.
  • Small, isolated or captive populations often have low genetic diversity.
  • Say: "different alleles of the same genes" — not: "different genes".
Quick check
?Cheetahs (Acinonyx jubatus) show extremely little genetic variation: unrelated cheetahs are so alike that skin grafts between them are not rejected. In a reserve, cheetah numbers have been stable for 20 years, and the species present and their relative abundances have not changed. Which statement about biodiversity in the reserve is correct?
Mission 4

Environmental DNA

  • Organisms shed DNA into water, soil and air: skin, mucus, faeces. This is environmental DNA (eDNA).
  • Sample, filter, extract the DNA, sequence a barcode region.
  • A barcode is a short, standard DNA region that differs between species but little within one (e.g. part of the COI gene in animals).
  • Match each sequence to a reference database to identify species.
  • Detects rare or hidden species quickly, without catching them.
  • Limit: eDNA persists for days to weeks and can be carried in. It shows a species has probably been there, not where it is now.
Quick check
?Water samples from a pond were filtered, the DNA in them was extracted, and a barcode region was sequenced. Sequences matching the reference barcode of the great crested newt were found, although no newts were seen during the survey. Which is the best interpretation?
Mission 4

Why genetic diversity matters

  • Environments change: new diseases, new climates, new chemicals.
  • High genetic diversity: some individuals are more likely to carry alleles that let them survive. The population persists.
  • Low genetic diversity: if one is susceptible, nearly all are. The population can crash.
  • Example: California condors fell to 22 birds in 1982. All living condors descend from the 27 taken into captivity by 1987.
  • Say: "the population is more likely to contain resistant individuals" — not: "each individual is more resistant".
Quick checkRead the data
?Real data (Zhu et al. 2000). In Yunnan, China, farmers planted rows of disease-susceptible rice varieties among disease-resistant varieties in every rice field of 5 townships (1998) and 10 townships (1999). Plots of the susceptible varieties grown alone (monoculture) were the comparison.
Which conclusion is best supported?
  • Rice blast (a fungal disease) on the susceptible varieties: 94% less severe in mixtures.
  • Yield of the susceptible varieties: 89% greater in mixtures.
Mission check

?Almost all bananas in international trade are one variety, Cavendish. Every plant is grown from a piece of another plant, so all are genetically identical. A fungal wilt disease that kills Cavendish plants is spreading between plantations. Why are biologists concerned for the whole crop?

Numbers do not protect a population; variety does. Genetic diversity is a population's insurance against change.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Sympatric speciation happens in one area when timing, behaviour, ecology or chromosomes stop gene flow; allopatric needs a geographical barrier; both need reproductive isolation, then divergence.
  • Hybridisation plus doubling of the chromosome number can make a fertile, isolated plant species in one generation.

Mission 2

  • Sequencing reads nucleotide order; it has become automated, faster and vastly cheaper, so whole genomes are compared routinely.
  • Uses: relationships between species, outbreaks, personalised medicine; a genome gives risks, not a fixed future.

Mission 3

  • Species diversity = richness (number of species) + evenness (how individuals are spread).
  • Biodiversity has three levels; repeated surveys reveal changes in richness and evenness.

Mission 4

  • Genetic diversity is the variety of alleles in a population; high diversity makes it more likely that some individuals survive a change.
