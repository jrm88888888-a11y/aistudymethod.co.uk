Mission 1

Mission 1: Sunni and Shi'a Muslims both honour Ali. Why did one question in 632 divide them?

Make a guess: Sunni and Shi'a Muslims both honour Ali. Why did one question in 632 divide them?

Mission 1

632: who leads now?

  • The Prophet died in June 632. He had been prophet, judge and leader of the Muslim community (ummah).
  • No new prophet would come. But who would lead?
  • One answer: the community chooses the best-qualified Muslim. Those who followed this became the Sunni Muslims: "people of the Sunnah and the community".
  • Another answer: God had already chosen a leader from the Prophet's family, through the Prophet: his cousin and son-in-law Ali. His supporters were the Shi'at Ali, "the party of Ali": Shi'a Muslims.
  • At first the question was about leadership. Over time it shaped belief, law and worship.
Mission 1

Abu Bakr chosen

  • While Ali and the Prophet's family prepared his burial, leaders of Madinah met at the Saqifah (a meeting hall).
  • Umar pledged loyalty (bay'ah) to Abu Bakr; most of those present followed. Abu Bakr became the first caliph (khalifah: successor).
  • Sunni view: a right choice by consultation (shura): Abu Bakr was the Prophet's closest friend, his companion in the cave, and led the prayers in the Prophet's last illness.
  • Shi'a view: the meeting ignored the Prophet's choice. Ali and the family were absent.
  • Ali later gave his pledge to Abu Bakr: Sunni sources present this as agreement; Shi'a sources, as accepting what he could not change, for the sake of unity.
Mission 1

Ghadir Khumm: two readings

  • March 632: returning from the Farewell Pilgrimage, the Prophet stopped at the pool (ghadir) of Khumm, took Ali's hand and said: for whoever I am his mawla, Ali is his mawla (paraphrase of a hadith in Sunni and Shi'a collections).
  • Mawla can mean "master, leader" or "friend, ally".
  • Shi'a reading: "master": a public appointment of Ali as leader after him. This is nass: explicit designation of the next Imam, by God's command through the Prophet.
  • Sunni reading: "friend": the Prophet answered complaints about Ali and told Muslims to love and support him; it was not about the caliphate.
  • Shi'a Muslims celebrate the day as Eid al-Ghadir.

Mission 1

Two traditions today

  • Sunni Muslims (about 87–90% of Muslims): leadership by the best-qualified, chosen by the community; authority in the Qur'an, the Sunnah and the scholars.
  • Shi'a Muslims (about 10–13%): the imamate (Imamah): leadership belongs to Imams from the Prophet's family, appointed by God through nass, beginning with Ali.
  • Role of the Imam (Shi'a): to guide the community without error, to interpret the Qur'an, to keep the Prophet's teaching pure (more in Mission 2).
  • Significance: for Shi'a Muslims, Imamah is a root of faith (Usul ad-Din); Shi'a groups include Twelvers (the largest), Isma'ilis and Zaydis (whose view of the imamate is less strict).
  • Shared: one God, the Qur'an, the Prophet; prayer, fasting, zakah and Hajj (Sunni Muslims call these acts, with the Shahadah, the Five Pillars). Sunni Muslims also love the Prophet's family: every salah sends blessings on "Muhammad and the family of Muhammad".
Quick check

Mission 1Evaluate

"The split between Sunni and Shi'a Muslims was only about politics."

  • For: it began with a practical question: who leads the community? Both share the Qur'an, the Prophet, prayer, fasting, zakah and Hajj. Ali himself worked with the first three caliphs.
  • Against: for Shi'a Muslims the leader is chosen by God, and Imamah is a root of faith. The two traditions developed different hadith collections, schools of law and practices (e.g. Ashura). Readings of the Qur'an (33:33) and Hadith (Ghadir Khumm) differ.
  • Another view: it began as a question of authority, which in Islam is both religious and political, so the two cannot be separated.
  • Judge it by: Is choosing a leader a religious question in Islam? How much do the differences affect worship and belief today? How do Sunni and Shi'a Muslims describe the split themselves?

Mission 2

Mission 2: Shi'a Muslims follow twelve Imams, and every mosque has an imam. Is that the same word for the same job?

Make a guess: Shi'a Muslims follow twelve Imams, and every mosque has an imam. Is that the same word for the same job?

Mission 2

One word, two meanings

  • Imam (Arabic) means "one who stands in front": a leader.
  • Every mosque has an imam: the person who leads the prayer and serves the community. Sunni and Shi'a mosques both have one.
  • For Shi'a Muslims, the Imams (capital I) are something more: leaders chosen by Allah from the Prophet's family to guide the ummah after him.
  • Belief in these Imams, Imamah (the imamate), is one of the Shi'a five roots of faith.
  • So the same word can mean a local prayer leader or a divinely chosen guide.
Mission 2

The imamate: who guides after the Prophet?

  • Shi'a Muslims believe Allah would not leave the community without a guide after the Seal of the Prophets.
  • No new prophet comes, but each age needs a guide to explain the Qur'an without error.
  • So Allah appointed Imams from the Ahl al-Bayt (the Prophet's household), beginning with Ali, his cousin and son-in-law.
  • Role: to lead, to interpret the Qur'an and Sunnah, and to be a perfect example.
  • Significance: the imamate continues the guidance of prophethood. Sunni Muslims see leadership as chosen by the community, not a root of faith (see Mission 1).
Mission 2Read the source

Ghadir Khumm and "leaders who guide"

  • In 632, returning from his Farewell Pilgrimage, the Prophet stopped at the pool (Ghadir) of Khumm. He took Ali's hand and said: "Of whomever I am mawla, Ali is his mawla" (Hadith of the pond of Khumm, paraphrase).
  • Shi'a reading: mawla means master and guardian; this was Ali's public appointment as leader.
  • Sunni reading: most Sunni scholars accept the hadith but read mawla as friend and supporter: an honour for Ali, not a succession.
  • Allah told Ibrahim: "I will make thee an Imam to the Nations." (Qur'an 2:124, Yusuf Ali) Ibrahim asked for Imams from his offspring too; Allah replied that His promise does not reach wrongdoers (paraphrase).

Q1 In 2:124, who makes someone an Imam?

Show answer

Allah: Shi'a Muslims see the imamate as a divine appointment.

Q2 Why do Sunni and Shi'a Muslims read Ghadir Khumm differently?

Show answer

They understand "mawla" differently: guardian and leader, or friend.

Mission 2

What makes an Imam

  • Nass (designation): each Imam is named by the one before, by Allah's command. The community does not elect him.
  • Inerrancy ('ismah; OCR: infallible; EDXI: sinless): Allah protects the Imams from sin and from error in teaching. Shi'a scholars cite Qur'an 33:33: Allah wishes to remove all abomination from "ye members of the Family" (Yusuf Ali), read as the Ahl al-Bayt.
  • Knowledge: the Imams inherit the Prophet's knowledge and are the true interpreters of the Qur'an.

Mission 2

The Twelve Imams and the Hidden Imam

  • Ali (d. 661): the first Imam; his sermons and letters are collected in Nahj al-Balagha.
  • Husayn (3rd), killed at Karbala in 680: his stand against injustice is remembered at Ashura.
  • Ja'far al-Sadiq (6th, d. 765): a great teacher; Twelver law is called the Ja'fari school after him.
  • Ali al-Rida (8th, d. 818): his shrine at Mashhad, Iran, is a major place of pilgrimage.
  • Muhammad al-Mahdi (12th), born 869: Twelvers believe Allah hid him (the occultation), that he is alive and that he will return with Isa (Jesus) to fill the earth with justice.
Mission 2

Twelvers and Seveners today

  • Twelvers (the large majority of Shi'a): while the Imam is hidden, senior scholars (mujtahids; the most senior, marja', "source to follow") guide believers in his place.
  • Scholars disagree how far this reaches: some say into government (Iran's system of rule by a scholar); others say mainly religious guidance (many scholars in Najaf, Iraq).

Mission 2Diversity

imam and Imam side by side

  • Sunni imam: chosen by the community · leads prayer, preaches, teaches · an ordinary believer with knowledge · many in every generation.
  • Shi'a prayer leader: the same role in Shi'a mosques, usually a trained scholar.
  • Shi'a Imam: appointed by Allah through nass (designation by the Imam before) · sinless and without error · the true interpreter of the Qur'an · twelve for most Shi'a (Twelvers), the last alive but hidden; a living Imam for most Isma'ilis (a smaller Shi'a group).
  • Different: for Shi'a Muslims, leadership of the ummah is a matter of faith (a root); for Sunni Muslims, a matter of practice and community choice.
Mission 2Evaluate

"The Imams are the most important source of guidance for Shi'a Muslims today."

  • For: Imamah is a root of faith. The Imams' sayings fill Shi'a hadith collections and shape Ja'fari law. Twelvers believe the Hidden Imam is alive and watches over the community. For most Isma'ilis the living Imam guides each age.
  • Against: the Qur'an is the word of Allah and comes first for all Muslims. The Prophet's Sunnah comes before the Imams' teaching. For Twelvers, day-to-day guidance now comes from scholars, because the Imam is hidden.
  • Another view: for Shi'a Muslims the sources do not compete: the Imams are the guardians of the Qur'an's meaning (the Hadith al-Thaqalayn joins "the Book" and "the family").
  • Judge it by: Which source settles disagreements? Does "guidance" mean law, example or spiritual help? Do Twelvers and Isma'ilis answer differently?
  • 12-mark tip: argue both sides with developed reasons and a source, then reach a justified conclusion of your own.

Mission 3: One sentence makes a person Muslim. What else holds up a Muslim's life?

Make a guess: One sentence makes a person Muslim. What else holds up a Muslim's life?

Mission 3

A house on five pillars

  • The Prophet taught that Islam is built on five: the Shahadah, prayer, zakah, Hajj and fasting in Ramadan (Sahih al-Bukhari, Book of Faith, paraphrase).
  • The pillars are ibadah (worship): acts of obedience that put iman (faith) into practice.
  • They are duties (fard) for every adult Muslim who is able, with exemptions (e.g. illness, poverty).
  • They bind the ummah together: the same words, prayers, fast and pilgrimage across the world.
Mission 3

The Five Pillars

1. Shahadah (CCEA: Shahada): declaring faith in one God and His messenger.

2. Salah (OCR: Salat): prayer five times a day (see Mission 4).

3. Zakah (OCR, CCEA: Zakat): giving 2.5% of savings above a minimum to those in need.

4. Sawm: fasting from dawn to sunset in the month of Ramadan (see Mission 6).

5. Hajj: pilgrimage to Makkah once in a lifetime, if able.

Mission 3

The Ten Obligatory Acts (Shi'a)

  • Shi'a Muslims call these duties the branches of religion (furu' ad-Din): they grow from the five roots of belief.

1. Salah · 2. Sawm · 3. Zakah: as for Sunni Muslims, with some differences in practice.

4. Khums: one-fifth of a year's surplus income.

5. Hajj · 6. Jihad: struggle in Allah's way, above all against one's own selfishness.

7. Amr bil-Ma'ruf: encouraging what is good. 8. Nahy 'anil-Munkar: discouraging what is wrong.

9. Tawalla: love for Allah, the Prophet, his family and the good. 10. Tabarra: disassociation from evil and from enemies of Allah.

Mission 3

History, purpose and links with the Five Pillars

  • History: Shi'a scholars gathered these duties from the Qur'an, the Prophet and the teaching of the Imams into ten "branches".
  • Why no Shahadah? For Shi'a Muslims, what the Shahadah declares is covered by the roots of belief; the branches are actions.
  • Shared: four Sunni pillars (salah, sawm, zakah, Hajj) are all branches.
  • Sunni understandings: Sunni Muslims also see jihad, enjoining good and forbidding wrong as duties (3:104, 9:71), and love the Prophet's family (they bless them in every salah). They do not list them as pillars, and they do not pay khums on yearly income (Sunni law applies it mainly to spoils of war).
  • Purpose: to turn the roots into a whole way of life, personal (salah, sawm) and social (amr, nahy, tawalla, tabarra).
Mission 3

Good, wrong, love and disassociation

  • Amr bil-Ma'ruf and Nahy 'anil-Munkar: every Muslim shares responsibility for the community's goodness. The Prophet taught: whoever sees a wrong should change it with his hand; if he cannot, with his tongue; if he cannot, then hate it in his heart (Sahih Muslim, paraphrase).
  • In practice: volunteering, charity, speaking out against injustice, drugs, racism; gently correcting friends; never by force outside the law.
  • Tawalla: loving the Prophet and the Ahl al-Bayt: "No reward do I ask of you for this except the love of those near of kin." (Qur'an 42:23, Yusuf Ali)
  • Tabarra: distancing oneself from evil and those who oppose Allah and the Prophet's family, shown in remembering Karbala and rejecting oppression.
Mission 3

Khums and Shi'a practice

  • Khums ("one-fifth"): 20% of a year's surplus income, after living costs (Qur'an 8:41).
  • Half (the Imam's share) goes to a senior scholar or his representative for religious work; half (the share of sayyids) to needy descendants of the Prophet.

Mission 3

The Shahadah

  • Arabic: lā ʾilāha ʾillā-llāh, muḥammadur-rasūlu-llāh
  • Meaning: "There is no god but God (Allah). Muhammad is the messenger of God."
  • Part 1: Tawhid: rejecting every false god, including money, fame or the self.
  • Part 2: Risalah: accepting Muhammad as the final messenger and following his example.
  • Usually said with "I bear witness that…" (ashhadu): a personal testimony.
  • Shi'a Muslims often add "and Ali is the friend (wali) of Allah". Most Shi'a scholars teach this is a recommended testimony, not part of the required words.
Mission 3Read the source

Allah bears witness

"There is no god but He: That is the witness of Allah, His angels, and those endued with knowledge, standing firm on justice. There is no god but He, the Exalted in Power, the Wise." (Qur'an 3:18, Yusuf Ali)

Q1 Who bears witness that there is no god but Allah, in 3:18?

Show answer

Allah Himself, the angels and people of knowledge.

Q2 How does 3:18 connect with the Shahadah?

Show answer

A Muslim who says the Shahadah joins the same witness, and witnesses to it personally.

Mission 3

The Shahadah in a Muslim's life

  • Birth: the adhan, containing the Shahadah, is whispered into a newborn's right ear: the first words heard.
  • Becoming Muslim: saying it sincerely, with belief in the heart, usually before witnesses.
  • Every day: in the adhan and in every salah (the tashahhud); many repeat it in dhikr.
  • Death: Muslims hope it will be their last words; it is said to and by the dying.
  • Sincerity: words alone are not enough; the intention (niyyah) and belief must be real, and life should show it.
Mission 3Evaluate

"The Shahadah is the most important pillar of Islam."

  • For: it is the foundation: without belief in Allah and His messenger, other acts have no meaning. Saying it sincerely makes a person Muslim. It contains Tawhid, the heart of Islam.
  • Against: a building needs all its pillars. Many Muslims teach that salah is the first deed judged on the Day of Judgement. Words without action may be empty: faith is shown in practice.
  • Shi'a view: belief belongs to the roots; among the branches, salah is often called the most important act.
  • Judge it by: Can belief and practice be separated? What makes someone a Muslim, and what keeps them one? Does "important" mean first, or most demanding?
  • 12-mark tip: give reasons on both sides, develop each, include a source, and end with a justified conclusion of your own.

Mission 4

What salah is

  • Salah (OCR: Salat): the second pillar; set prayer at fixed times, five times a day.
  • A duty (fard) for every Muslim from puberty who is of sound mind; exemptions include women during menstruation.
  • Different from du'a (personal prayer in one's own words;): salah follows set words and movements in Arabic.
Mission 4Read the source

Prostrate and remember

"But celebrate the praises of thy Lord, and be of those who prostrate themselves in adoration. And serve thy Lord until there come unto thee the Hour that is Certain." (Qur'an 15:98–99, Yusuf Ali)

"Establish regular Prayer: for Prayer restrains from shameful and unjust deeds; and remembrance of Allah is the greatest (thing in life) without doubt." (Qur'an 29:45, Yusuf Ali)

Q1 According to 29:45, what does prayer do for the person who prays?

Show answer

Q2 According to 15:99, how long should a Muslim keep serving Allah?

Show answer

Mission 4

Wudu: washing before prayer

  • Niyyah (intention) and "Bismillah" (in the name of Allah).
  • Sunni practice: wash hands, rinse mouth, nose, wash face, arms to the elbows (right then left), wipe the head and ears with wet hands, wash feet to the ankles; most parts three times.
  • Based on Qur'an 5:6: wash face, hands to the elbows, rub the head, feet to the ankles.
  • If no water: tayammum (clean earth or dust). After sex or menstruation: a full bath (ghusl).
  • Wudu is broken by e.g. using the toilet or deep sleep.
  • Significance: to come before Allah physically and spiritually clean; a time to leave behind distractions and sins.
  • Wudu must be done in the right order, with clean water, without waste.
Mission 4

Five times a day

  • Fajr: dawn to sunrise: 2 rak'ahs.
  • Zuhr (Dhuhr): just after midday: 4.
  • Asr: afternoon: 4.
  • Maghrib: just after sunset: 3.
  • Isha: night: 4.
  • Times follow the sun, so they change through the year; mosques publish timetables, and the adhan announces each one.
  • Rhythm: the day is framed by Allah, from waking to sleeping.
  • Shi'a Muslims usually combine Zuhr with Asr and Maghrib with Isha, so they pray at three times a day, but still pray all five prayers.

Mission 4

Direction, intention, place

  • Qiblah (OCR: qibla): all Muslims face the Ka'bah in Makkah. In Britain that is roughly south-east.
  • At first Muslims faced Jerusalem; the qiblah changed to Makkah (2:144), about 16 months after the Hijrah.
  • Facing one point unites the ummah worldwide: like rings round the Ka'bah.
  • Niyyah: the intention, made in the heart, to pray this particular prayer for Allah alone.
  • Clean place and clothes: often a prayer mat; shoes removed; body covered modestly (for men at least navel to knee; for women all but face, hands and, in many schools, feet).
Mission 4

One rak'ah, step by step

  • Rak'ah (OCR: raka't; CIE: rakʿah): one cycle of set words and movements.
  • Standing (qiyam): hands raised, "Allahu Akbar" (Allah is greatest). Recite al-Fatihah, then (in the first two rak'ahs) another passage.
  • Bowing (ruku'): "Glory to my Lord, the Most Great."
  • Standing again: "Allah hears the one who praises Him."
  • Prostration (sujud), twice, with a short sitting between: forehead, nose, palms, knees and toes on the ground: "Glory to my Lord, the Most High."
  • After every two rak'ahs and at the end: sitting, reciting the tashahhud (including the Shahadah) and blessings on the Prophet.
  • End: "As-salamu 'alaykum wa rahmatullah" (peace be upon you and the mercy of Allah), turning the head right and left.
  • Significance: standing in respect, bowing in humility, prostrating in total submission: the moment Muslims say they are closest to Allah.

  • Recited in every rak'ah: at least seventeen times a day.

Mission 4Diversity

Sunni and Shi'a salah

  • Shared: five prayers, same rak'ahs, qiblah, wudu, al-Fatihah, ruku' and sujud, Arabic.
  • Hands: most Sunni Muslims fold their arms while standing; Shi'a Muslims (and many Maliki Sunnis) keep them by their sides.
  • Prostration: Shi'a Muslims prostrate on earth or something natural, often a small clay tablet (turbah), many from Karbala; Sunni Muslims prostrate on any clean surface.
  • Times: Shi'a Muslims usually pray at three times; Sunni Muslims combine prayers only on journeys or in special cases.
  • Wudu: Shi'a Muslims wash the arms from elbow to fingertips and wipe (not wash) the feet.
  • Words: Shi'a Muslims add a qunut (raised-hands du'a) in the second rak'ah and do not say "Amin" after al-Fatihah; their adhan adds phrases (see Mission 2).

Mission 4

Why pray?

  • Obedience: Allah commands it (4:103); it is a pillar of Islam.
  • Remembrance: "establish worship for My remembrance" (Qur'an 20:14, Pickthall).
  • Protection: it "restrains from shameful and unjust deeds" (29:45).
  • Cleansing: the Prophet compared five daily prayers to bathing in a river five times a day: no dirt remains (hadith, paraphrase).
  • Discipline and unity: the day is shaped by Allah; Muslims everywhere pray the same way towards one point.
Mission 4Worked answer

Two contrasting practices

Question: Explain two contrasting ways in which Sunni and Shi'a Muslims perform salah.

  • Contrast 1: Most Sunni Muslims pray at five separate times; Shi'a Muslims usually combine prayers and pray at three times. Development: both still say all five prayers, and Shi'a Muslims see combining as permitted by the Prophet's example.
  • Contrast 2: Most Sunni Muslims fold their arms; Shi'a Muslims keep them at their sides and prostrate on a turbah. Development: prostrating on natural earth expresses humility before Allah, the Creator.
  • 4-mark tip ("two contrasting"): two different practices, each explained with a developed point.

Mission 5

Jumu'ah: Friday prayer

  • Jumu'ah (AQA, OCR, EDXA, EDXB, EDQ: Jummah; CIE: Jumʿah; EDXI: Jum'a): the Friday congregational prayer, held in place of Zuhr.
  • Form: a sermon (khutbah), then two rak'ahs led by the imam.
  • Who must attend (Sunni teaching): adult Muslim men who are healthy and not travelling. Women, children, travellers and the sick need not attend, but may; if they do, it replaces their Zuhr.
  • Preparation: a full wash (ghusl), clean clothes, arriving early.
  • Shi'a practice: during the Imam's occultation, many Twelver scholars teach that Jumu'ah may be prayed instead of Zuhr, not that it is required; large Friday prayers are held in Iran and Iraq, and in some British Shi'a centres.
Mission 5Read the source

Leave your trading

"O ye who believe! When the call is proclaimed to prayer on Friday … hasten earnestly to the Remembrance of Allah, and leave off business … That is best for you if ye but knew!" (Qur'an 62:9, Yusuf Ali)

"And when the prayer is ended, then disperse in the land and seek of Allah's bounty, and remember Allah much, that ye may be successful." (Qur'an 62:10, Pickthall)

  • Friday is not a day of rest like a Sabbath: work stops for the prayer, then continues.

Q1 What must believers do when the Friday call is heard?

Show answer

Hurry to remember Allah and leave business.

Q2 What may they do after the prayer?

Show answer

Go back to work and seek Allah's bounty, still remembering Allah.

Mission 5

The khutbah

  • Khutbah (OCR: khutba): a sermon in two parts, given by the imam from the minbar (pulpit); he sits briefly between them.
  • Contains praise of Allah, blessings on the Prophet, verses of the Qur'an and a du'a for the ummah.
  • Language: traditionally Arabic; in Britain usually partly in English or another community language, so all can understand.
  • Topics: faith, character, family life, current events, how to live as Muslims in society.
  • Worshippers sit in silence and listen: the khutbah is part of the worship.
Mission 5

Praying together

  • Rows: shoulder to shoulder, feet together, facing the qiblah behind one imam.
  • Equality: rich and poor, of every race and nation, stand side by side: the ummah made visible.
  • Reward: the Prophet taught that prayer in congregation is twenty-seven times better than prayer alone (Sahih al-Bukhari, paraphrase).
  • Mosque practice: men usually pray in front; women behind or in their own area or balcony; some mosques have no women's space, which many Muslims criticise.
  • Many Muslims pray all five prayers in the mosque; others go mainly for Jumu'ah, Ramadan and Eid.

Mission 5

Prayer at home and elsewhere

  • The Prophet taught that the whole earth has been made a place of prayer: any clean place will do (hadith, paraphrase).
  • Home: many Muslims pray most daily prayers at home, often on a prayer mat in a quiet room; families may pray together, with a parent leading.
  • The Prophet urged believers to pray some prayers (especially voluntary ones) at home, so that homes are not like graves without worship (hadith, paraphrase).
  • Elsewhere: prayer rooms at school, work, airports, hospitals; outdoors on a coat or mat. Travellers may shorten four-rak'ah prayers to two.
  • Women: prayer at home is fully valid; many women also attend mosques.
Mission 5

Du'a: calling on Allah

  • Du'a ("call"): personal prayer or supplication: asking Allah for help, forgiveness and guidance, or thanking Him.
  • Any time, any place, any language, in a person's own words or in prayers from the Qur'an and the Prophet; often with hands raised, palms up.
  • Preparation (CIE): being clean, facing the qiblah, praising Allah and blessing the Prophet first, being sincere and patient.
  • Special times: after salah, the last part of the night, while fasting, on Friday.
  • Content: e.g. "Our Lord! Give unto us in the world that which is good and in the Hereafter that which is good" (Qur'an 2:201, Pickthall).
  • Shi'a Muslims also use prayers taught by the Imams, e.g. Du'a Kumayl (from Ali), often recited on Thursday nights.
Mission 5Read the source

"When My servants ask thee concerning Me, I am indeed close (to them): I listen to the prayer of every suppliant when he calleth on Me" (Qur'an 2:186, Yusuf Ali)

Q1 What does 2:186 say about Allah's nearness?

Show answer

He is close and listens to everyone who calls on Him.

Q2 Why might this verse encourage private worship?

Show answer

Allah hears personal prayer anywhere, so no mosque or set words are needed.

Mission 5

Dhikr: remembering Allah

  • Dhikr (remembrance): repeating praises of Allah, aloud or silently.
  • After salah: "SubhanAllah" (glory be to Allah) 33 times, "Alhamdulillah" (praise be to Allah) 33 times, "Allahu Akbar" (Allah is greatest) 33 or 34 times, often counted on prayer beads (misbaha or tasbih).
  • Shi'a Muslims especially know this as the Tasbih of Fatimah, which the Prophet taught his daughter, and begin with Allahu Akbar (34).
  • Significance: "in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find rest" (Qur'an 13:28, Pickthall); it keeps Allah in mind between prayers.
  • Sufis make dhikr a central practice.

Mission 5Diversity

How important is prayer? Different views

  • All Muslims agree salah is a duty; many teach it is the first deed judged on the Day of Judgement.
  • Abandoning prayer: some scholars (e.g. many Hanbalis) teach that deliberately abandoning salah takes a person out of Islam; most teach it is a very grave sin, but not unbelief.
  • Inner and outer: some stress correct performance; others, especially Sufis, stress khushu' (humble attention): a prayer without the heart is empty.
  • Mosque and home: some Muslims see the mosque as the heart of the community and attend every prayer; others pray mostly at home and go to the mosque on Fridays.
  • In Britain: fixed times can clash with school or work; many Muslims combine or make up prayers, or use prayer rooms; some feel this weakens worship, others that it shows faith can fit any life.
Mission 5Evaluate

"Praying in the mosque is more important than praying at home."

  • For: congregational prayer is "twenty-seven times" better (hadith). Jumu'ah must be prayed in congregation. The khutbah teaches and guides. The ummah is strengthened when Muslims pray side by side.
  • Against: the whole earth is a place of prayer, and Allah is "close" to every caller (2:186). The Prophet encouraged prayer at home. Sincerity matters more than place. For many women, carers and people far from a mosque, home is where they can pray.
  • Another view: they do different jobs: the mosque builds the community; the home builds personal faith and passes it to children.
  • Judge it by: What is prayer for? Does "more important" mean more reward, more duty or more effect on a person's life? Does it differ for men and women?

Mission 6

Sawm: the fourth pillar

  • Sawm means fasting (literally "to abstain"). It is one of the Five Pillars of Islam.
  • Ramadan is the ninth month of the Islamic lunar calendar. It begins when the new crescent moon is sighted.
  • From dawn to sunset, adult Muslims give up: food, drink (including water), smoking and sexual relations.
  • They also try to avoid lying, anger, gossip and bad deeds: the Prophet said Allah has no need of someone giving up food and drink who does not give up false speech (Sahih al-Bukhari, paraphrase).
  • The lunar year is about 11 days shorter than the solar year, so Ramadan moves through the seasons over about 33 years.
Mission 6Read the source

Fasting is prescribed

"O ye who believe! Fasting is prescribed for you, even as it was prescribed for those before you, that ye may ward off (evil)" (Qur'an 2:183, Pickthall)

"The month of Ramadan in which was revealed the Qur'an, a guidance for mankind" (Qur'an 2:185, Pickthall)

Q1 According to 2:183, what is the purpose of fasting?

Show answer

To ward off evil: to develop taqwa, awareness of Allah and self-restraint.

Q2 Why is Ramadan the month chosen for fasting?

Show answer

It is the month in which the Qur'an was revealed, 2:185.

Mission 6

Origins and the Prophet's example

  • 2:183 says fasting was given to earlier peoples too: Muslims see Sawm as continuing the practice of earlier prophets.
  • In Makkah the Prophet fasted and spent nights in prayer; the Qur'an was first revealed to him in Ramadan.
  • In Madinah, in the second year after the Hijrah (624 CE), fasting the whole month of Ramadan was made compulsory.
  • He did not allow followers to harm themselves: he forbade fasting day after day without breaking at night.
Mission 6

A day in Ramadan

  • Intention (niyyah): made each night or at the start of the month.
  • Suhur: a meal before dawn, then Fajr prayer.
  • The day: work, school and salah as usual; extra Qur'an reading. Many read the whole Qur'an over the month (in 30 parts).
  • Iftar at sunset (Maghrib): often dates and water, then Maghrib prayer and a meal, often shared with family, the mosque or neighbours.
  • Night: Sunni Muslims pray tarawih (extra night prayers) in the mosque. Shi'a Muslims do not pray tarawih in congregation but keep other night prayers and du'as.

Mission 6

Who is excused, and how they make up

  • Excused from fasting:
  • children before puberty (many practise with half-days);
  • the ill, and travellers on a long journey (2:184);
  • women who are pregnant, breastfeeding, menstruating or recovering from childbirth;
  • the elderly or anyone whose health would be harmed; people with serious mental illness.
  • Why: "Allah desireth for you ease; He desireth not hardship for you" (Qur'an 2:185, Pickthall).
  • Making up: missed days are fasted later (qada). Those who can never fast feed a poor person for each day (fidyah, 2:184).
Mission 6

Benefits for the self and the ummah

  • Taqwa: awareness of Allah and self-restraint (2:183). A person who can control hunger can control anger and greed.
  • Obedience and reward: the Prophet taught that whoever fasts Ramadan with faith, hoping for Allah's reward, has past sins forgiven (Sahih al-Bukhari, paraphrase).
  • Empathy: hunger helps Muslims feel what the poor feel, and leads to giving.
  • Gratitude for food and water usually taken for granted.
  • Ummah: Muslims worldwide fast in the same month; iftars bring families and communities together.
Mission 6

Laylat al-Qadr, the Night of Power

  • The night in 610 CE when, Muslims believe, the angel Jibril brought the first verses of the Qur'an to Muhammad in the cave of Hira:
  • "Read: In the name of thy Lord Who createth, Createth man from a clot." (Qur'an 96:1–2, Pickthall)
  • Verses 3–5 continue: the Lord is most bounteous, who teaches by the pen, teaching humans what they did not know (Qur'an 96:3–5, paraphrase).
  • "The Night of Power is better than a thousand months." (Qur'an 97:3, Pickthall)
  • It falls on one of the odd nights of the last ten nights of Ramadan; many Sunni Muslims mark the 27th. Shi'a Muslims stress the 19th, 21st and 23rd (the 21st is also the anniversary of Imam Ali's death).
  • Today: Muslims stay up in prayer, Qur'an reading and du'a, asking forgiveness. It reminds them that the Qur'an is guidance from Allah.

Mission 6Evaluate

"Sawm benefits the ummah more than the individual."

  • Reasons for: the whole ummah fasts together; shared iftars and tarawih build community; Zakat al-Fitr and Ramadan giving help the poor; empathy with the hungry leads to action.
  • Reasons against: 2:183 names the purpose as taqwa, a quality of each heart; forgiveness of sins is personal; Allah judges each person's intention; someone fasting alone (e.g. a traveller making up days) gains the same spiritual benefit.
  • Another view: the two cannot be separated: individuals who grow in self-control and gratitude make a stronger ummah.
  • Judge it by: What does the Qur'an say fasting is for? Can a benefit to the ummah exist without benefits to individuals? How do Muslims who fast alone, e.g. new converts in Britain, experience it?
Mission 6Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain two reasons why Muslims fast during Ramadan. Refer to a source of wisdom and authority.

  • Reason 1: Allah commands it to build taqwa. Source: "Fasting is prescribed for you … that ye may ward off (evil)" (Qur'an 2:183). Development: self-control over hunger trains Muslims to resist sin all year.
  • Reason 2: Ramadan is the month of the Qur'an. Source: "The Night of Power is better than a thousand months" (Qur'an 97:3). Development: the first verses were revealed on Laylat al-Qadr, so Muslims fast and read the Qur'an to give thanks for its guidance.
  • 5-mark tip: two reasons, each developed, plus one relevant source of wisdom and authority.
Mission check

?Bilal, 16, has type 1 diabetes. His doctor says fasting would put his health at serious risk. His grandmother, 82, is now too frail to fast without becoming ill. Both want to take part in Ramadan. According to the Qur'an and most Muslim scholars, what should they do?

Sawm is a duty, not a punishment: Allah "desireth for you ease". Those who cannot fast still take part through prayer, the Qur'an and giving.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • In 632 the question "who leads?" had two answers: chosen by the community (Sunni: Abu Bakr, then Umar, Uthman, Ali) or appointed by God from the Prophet's family (Shi'a: Ali, by nass at Ghadir Khumm).

Mission 2

  • Every mosque has an imam: a prayer leader chosen for knowledge and character, with no priestly status.
  • Shi'a Muslims believe in the Imamate: Imams appointed by Allah (nass), free from sin and error, beginning with Ali at Ghadir Khumm; for Twelvers the twelfth is hidden and will return.

Mission 3

  • Islam is "built on five": Shahadah, Salah, Zakah, Sawm and Hajj, the acts of worship (ibadah) that hold up a Muslim's life.
  • Shi'a Muslims follow ten Obligatory Acts (branches): the four practical pillars plus khums, jihad, encouraging good, discouraging wrong, tawalla and tabarra.
  • The Shahadah declares Tawhid and Risalah; it must be said sincerely, and it marks a Muslim's life from birth to death.

Mission 4

  • Salah: set prayer five times a day (Fajr, Zuhr, Asr, Maghrib, Isha) after wudu, facing the qiblah, with niyyah and set rak'ahs reciting al-Fatihah.
  • Muslims pray to obey and remember Allah, to be cleansed and kept from wrong (29:45), and to unite the ummah.
  • Shi'a Muslims usually pray at three times, keep arms by their sides, prostrate on a turbah and wipe the feet in wudu.

Mission 5

  • Jumu'ah (Friday prayer) replaces Zuhr: a two-part khutbah then two rak'ahs in congregation (62:9–10); in Sunni teaching it is required of adult men.
  • Salah may be prayed at home or anywhere clean; congregational prayer brings unity and greater reward.
  • Du'a is personal prayer in any words or language (2:186); dhikr is remembrance of Allah, especially after salah.

Mission 6

  • Sawm is fasting from dawn to sunset in Ramadan (Qur'an 2:183–185); its aim is taqwa, with suhur before dawn and iftar at sunset.
  • The ill, travellers, children, pregnant women and the elderly are excused; missed days are made up (qada) or replaced by feeding the poor (fidyah).
  • Laylat al-Qadr, in the last ten nights, marks the first revelation (Qur'an 96:1–5) and is "better than a thousand months" (97:3).
