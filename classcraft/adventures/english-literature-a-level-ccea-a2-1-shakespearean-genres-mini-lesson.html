A2 1: Shakespearean Genres

This mini-lesson trains the skills CCEA Unit A2 1 rewards — analysing a Shakespeare play through the conventions of its genre and through criticism. It is not built around one set play; you will meet the frameworks of tragedy, comedy, problem plays and last plays.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you are ready.

The unit at a glance

What A2 1 actually assesses

A2 1 is a 1 hour 30 minute, closed-book paper. You answer one question on a single play from a chosen genre, and each question gives a printed extract as the way in.

Uniquely in this specification, A2 1 assesses all five assessment objectives:

  • AO1 informed personal response · AO2 dramatic methods · AO3 context
  • AO4 connections within the play · AO5 different interpretations (this is where criticism matters most)

Synoptic: as an A2 unit it demands higher-order skills — genre awareness, critical debate and a confident personal argument.

Genre conventions

The four genres in outline

  • Tragedy — a protagonist of high estate falls to catastrophe; suffering, error and death; classically shaped by Aristotle.
  • Comedy — obstacles to love and harmony are overcome; disguise, mistaken identity and multiple marriages; ends in reconciliation.
  • Problem plays — works that resist neat comic or tragic closure, morally uneasy and satirical.
  • Last plays (romances) — late tragicomedies of loss and wonder, ending in reunion and forgiveness across generations.

Read with the grain and against it: the sharpest essays notice where a play fulfils its genre and where it strains against it.

Genre spotlight · Tragedy

Reading Shakespearean tragedy

The foundational framework is Aristotle's Poetics. Its key terms:

  • Hamartia — the hero's error or fatal flaw of judgement.
  • Peripeteia — the reversal of fortune, from prosperity to ruin.
  • Anagnorisis — the recognition, moving from ignorance to knowledge.
  • Catharsis — the audience's purgation of pity and fear.

Two influential critics extend this: A.C. Bradley (Shakespearean Tragedy, 1904) reads the tragedies through character and the "tragic flaw"; Jan Kott (Shakespeare Our Contemporary, 1964) offers a political, absurdist reading, seeing a bleak "Grand Mechanism" of power.

Use criticism as debate: Bradley's character focus and Kott's political pessimism can be set against each other for AO5.

Genre spotlight · Comedy

Reading Shakespearean comedy

Comedy drives toward harmony and renewal. Key critical frames:

  • Northrop Frye (Anatomy of Criticism, 1957) — comedy moves the characters into a "green world" and ends by forming a renewed, reconciled society, usually sealed by marriage.
  • C.L. Barber — Shakespeare's festive comedy follows a saturnalian pattern of release and clarification.
  • Mikhail Bakhtin — the carnivalesque: a licensed, temporary inversion of hierarchy and rule (from Rabelais and His World).

Comic conventions: disguise and cross-dressing, mistaken identity, witty wordplay, and multiple marriages restoring social order.

Genre spotlight · Problem & Last plays

Problem plays and last plays

  • Problem plays — the term was coined by F.S. Boas (1896), borrowing from Ibsen-era drama, for plays that unsettle the comic/tragic divide and end in moral unease rather than clear resolution.
  • Last plays (romances) — Shakespeare's late tragicomedies: patterns of loss, apparent death and wondrous restoration; reunion and forgiveness across a generation; the marvellous and the redemptive.

Hybrid genres reward nuance: a "dark" comedy or a tragicomic romance lets you argue about how far a play obeys or bends its genre — ideal AO4/AO5 territory.

Connections & interpretations (AO4, AO5)

Using criticism and connections

A2 1 is where critical debate earns most. Two objectives drive this:

  • AO4 — explore connections within the play: motifs, parallel plots, echoes between scenes and characters.
  • AO5 — explore the play informed by different interpretations: critical readings (Bradley, Kott, Frye, feminist criticism) and performance choices.

AO5 is judgement, not quotation-dropping: set critics against each other, test them against the text, and reach your own considered view.

Exam skill · the extract

The extract-based question

Every A2 1 question prints an extract and asks about the play as a whole. The examiner wants both movements:

  • Zoom in (AO2) — close-read the extract's dramatic methods: language, verse, staging, characterisation.
  • Zoom out (AO4) — connect the extract to patterns, developments and echoes across the whole play.
  • Context (AO3) — the Elizabethan/Jacobean playhouse, genre expectations, and beliefs of the period that shape meaning.

Common trap: paraphrasing the extract in isolation. The extract is a springboard, not the whole task.

The big ideas to know

Shape of A2 1: one play · one genre · extract-based · closed book · all five AOs

Tragedy: Aristotle (hamartia, peripeteia, anagnorisis, catharsis) · Bradley (character) · Kott (political)

Comedy: Frye (green world, renewed society) · Barber (festive) · Bakhtin (carnivalesque)

Problem plays: Boas (1896) · moral ambiguity, unresolved closure

Last plays: tragicomic romance · loss and wondrous restoration

Skill: zoom in on the extract (AO2), connect to the whole play (AO4), debate criticism (AO5)
