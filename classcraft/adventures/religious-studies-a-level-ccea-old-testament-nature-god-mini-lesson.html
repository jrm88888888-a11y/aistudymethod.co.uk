Mission 1

What is a creedal statement?

  • A creed is a short, fixed summary of belief, said aloud by a community.
  • Deuteronomy 26:1–11: a farmer brings the firstfruits of the harvest to the sanctuary and recites God's saving acts.
  • The creed tells a story: a wandering ancestor ("My father was a Syrian ready to perish"), slavery in Egypt, a cry to God, rescue "with a mighty hand", the gift of the land (26:5–9).
  • God is known by what he has done: he hears, sees and rescues the oppressed (26:7).
  • Gerhard von Rad (1901–1971), "The Form-Critical Problem of the Hexateuch" (1938), called this a "short historical creed", one of Israel's oldest confessions (compare Deuteronomy 6:20–24; Joshua 24:2–13). Later scholars dispute its early date; many now see it as Deuteronomic.
Mission 1

Exodus 34: God reveals his name

  • Setting: after the golden calf (Exodus 32), the covenant is broken. Moses asks to see God's glory (33:18).
  • God passes by, hidden: Moses sees only his back (33:20–23). Moses brings new tablets (34:1–4).
  • God then "proclaimed" his name, not a story of deeds but a description of character (34:6–7).
  • So this is an alternative creed: who God is, not only what he has done.
  • Moses responds by asking forgiveness for a "stiff-necked people" (34:9).

The character of God

> "The LORD! The LORD, a merciful and gracious God, slow to anger, and abundant in loving kindness and truth, keeping loving kindness for thousands, forgiving iniquity and disobedience and sin; and who will by no means clear the guilty, visiting the iniquity of the fathers on the children, and on the children's children, on the third and on the fourth generation." (Exodus 34:6–7, WEBBE)

  • raḥum (merciful): related to reḥem, womb: a parent's tender love.
  • ḥannun (gracious): undeserved favour. 'erekh 'appayim (slow to anger): literally "long of nostrils".
  • ḥesed (loving kindness): loyal, covenant love. 'emet (truth): faithfulness.
  • Mercy reaches "thousands"; punishment reaches "the third and … fourth generation": the imbalance is deliberate.
  • In Jewish worship these verses are the Thirteen Attributes of Mercy, said on festivals and in the prayers for forgiveness before and on Yom Kippur.

The formula traced

  • Numbers 14:18: after the spies' report, Moses quotes God's words back to him to plead for pardon: "slow to anger, and abundant in loving kindness". God pardons, but that generation will not enter the land (14:20–23).
  • Psalm 86:15: an individual in distress appeals to the formula.
  • Psalm 103:8: praises the merciful God and leaves out the punishment clause: "He has not dealt with us according to our sins" (103:10).
  • Psalm 145:8–9: widens it: "The LORD is good to all. His tender mercies are over all his works."
  • Joel 2:13: "Tear your heart and not your garments": the formula grounds a call to repent; God "relents from sending calamity".
  • Jonah 4:2: Jonah quotes it as a complaint: he fled because he knew God would spare Nineveh, Israel's enemy.

A struggle within the heart of God

> "When Israel was a child, then I loved him, and called my son out of Egypt." (Hosea 11:1, WEBBE)

> "How can I give you up, Ephraim? How can I hand you over, Israel? How can I make you like Admah? How can I make you like Zeboiim? My heart is turned within me, my compassion is aroused." (Hosea 11:8, WEBBE)

  • Hosea: northern prophet, c. 750–722 BCE. Ephraim = the northern kingdom.
  • 11:1–4: God as a parent: teaching the child to walk, lifting, feeding.
  • 11:5–7: the child turns away; Assyria will rule; judgement is deserved.
  • 11:8–9: God argues with himself. Admah and Zeboiim were destroyed with Sodom (Deuteronomy 29:23). "Turned" uses the verb for the overthrow of Sodom: God's heart is overturned instead of Israel.
  • "I am God, and not man" (11:9): divine holiness shows itself as mercy, not as wrath.

Mission 1

Widow, orphan and foreigner

  • Three groups with no land, no male protector, or no kin: the widow, the fatherless and the foreigner (ger, resident alien).
  • Law: "You shall not wrong an alien … for you were aliens in the land of Egypt" (Exodus 22:21); God hears their cry (22:23).
  • Provision: the third-year tithe feeds Levite, foreigner, fatherless and widow (Deuteronomy 14:28–29; 26:12–13); the forgotten sheaf and the gleanings are left for them (24:19–22).
  • Protection: "Cursed is he who withholds justice from the foreigner, fatherless, and widow" (Deuteronomy 27:19).
  • Prophets: "Defend the fatherless. Plead for the widow" (Isaiah 1:17); the Temple is no refuge for those who "oppress the foreigner, the fatherless, and the widow" (Jeremiah 7:6).
  • Motive: memory of Egypt (Deuteronomy 24:18, 22). God's compassion becomes Israel's law.

Mission 1

Mercy and judgement held together

  • Exodus 34:7 keeps judgement inside the creed of mercy. Numbers 14 does too; Psalm 103 omits it; Jonah 4 is angry at mercy.
  • Rabbinic tradition speaks of two attributes, mercy (middat ha-raḥamim) and justice (middat ha-din), with mercy prevailing.
  • In synagogue liturgy the Thirteen Attributes stop before "will by no means clear the guilty", reading it as "and clearing (the penitent)".
  • Christian readers see the same God who is "merciful and gracious" and link the formula to the cross and forgiveness; Marcion (2nd century) set an Old Testament God of wrath against a New Testament God of love; the early Church rejected his view as heresy.
  • Phyllis Trible (b. 1932; God and the Rhetoric of Sexuality, 1978) traced raḥum to the womb, finding a maternal image of divine compassion.
Mission 1Evaluate

Is the God of the Old Testament essentially compassionate?

  • Claim: Compassion is the defining characteristic of God in the Old Testament.
  • For: God names himself first as "merciful and gracious" (Exodus 34:6); the formula recurs across law, psalms and prophets.
  • For: Hosea 11: even deserved judgement is overturned by compassion; the vulnerable are protected by law (Exodus 22; Deuteronomy 24).
  • Against: The same verse keeps punishment across generations (34:7); other texts present a warrior God who commands destruction (see Mission 2).
  • Against: Compassion is often for Israel; Jonah's anger shows its reach to enemies was contested.
  • Against: Texts come from many centuries; seeking one "essential" character may flatten a varied library.
  • Judgement criteria: Which texts are central (creeds) and which marginal? Does mercy limit judgement or sit beside it? Should the canon be read as a whole, or each text in its own setting?

Tracing the formula

Task: "Explain how Exodus 34:6–7 is developed elsewhere in the Old Testament."

  • Point: Exodus 34:6–7 describes God's character: merciful, gracious, slow to anger, yet not clearing the guilty.
  • Develop 1: Moses uses it in intercession (Numbers 14:18), and God pardons.
  • Develop 2: Psalm 103:8–10 drops the judgement clause; Psalm 145:9 extends mercy to "all his works".
  • Develop 3: Joel 2:13 uses it to call for repentance; Jonah 4:2 resents its reach to Nineveh.
  • Link: The formula becomes a living creed, reapplied to new crises.

Mission 2

God as warrior at the sea

  • Exodus 14: Israel is trapped between Pharaoh's army and the sea.
  • Moses: "The LORD will fight for you, and you shall be still" (14:14). Israel does not fight; God acts.
  • The Egyptians cry: "the LORD fights for them" (14:25).
  • Exodus 15:1–21, the Song of the Sea: "The LORD is a man of war. The LORD is his name" (15:3).
  • Miriam's song (15:21) is often thought one of the oldest pieces of Hebrew poetry.
  • Here the warrior God rescues slaves from an oppressor: war is liberation.
Mission 2

God fighting on Israel's side

  • Numbers 21:1–3: Israel vows to "utterly destroy" Canaanite cities if God gives victory; the place is named Hormah, from the same root as ḥerem.
  • Deuteronomy 2:31–35; 3:1–7: the kings Sihon and Og are defeated; whole cities destroyed "with the women and the little ones" (2:34).
  • Deuteronomy 7: the seven nations of Canaan are to be destroyed; no covenant, no intermarriage, their altars broken (7:1–5).
  • Reason given: to keep Israel from idolatry, because Israel is a "holy people", chosen out of love, not size (7:6–8).
  • Gerhard von Rad (1901–1971), Holy War in Ancient Israel (1951): the pattern of "holy war": God goes before the army, fear falls on the enemy, the victory is God's; herem completes it.

The law of herem

> "When you draw near to a city to fight against it, then proclaim peace to it." (Deuteronomy 20:10, WEBBE)

> "But of the cities of these peoples that the LORD your God gives you for an inheritance, you shall save alive nothing that breathes; but you shall utterly destroy them" (Deuteronomy 20:16–17, WEBBE)

  • ḥerem: something devoted to God, removed from human use; in war, the destruction of people and goods as an offering to God.
  • Distant cities (20:10–15): offer peace first; if refused, kill the men; women, children and goods are taken.
  • Cities of the land (20:16–18): total destruction, "that they not teach you to follow all their abominations".
  • Fruit trees must be spared: "is the tree of the field man, that it should be besieged by you?" (20:19).

The Amalekites

  • Exodus 17:8–16: Amalek attacks Israel at Rephidim; Joshua fights while Moses' hands are raised. "I will utterly blot out the memory of Amalek" (17:14); "war with Amalek from generation to generation" (17:16).

The warrior in the prophets

  • Isaiah 59: injustice fills Judah; no one intervenes, so God acts: "He put on righteousness as a breastplate, and a helmet of salvation on his head" (59:17).
  • The enemy here is injustice, including Israel's own; "A Redeemer will come to Zion, and to those who turn from disobedience" (59:20).
  • Isaiah 63:1–6: a warrior comes from Edom (Bozrah), garments red: "I have trodden the wine press alone" (63:3). God's judgement on the nations, and rescue of his people.
  • The image moves from national conquest to God's final judgement and salvation.
  • Christian reuse: the armour of God (Ephesians 6:14–17); the winepress (Revelation 14:19–20; 19:13–15).
Mission 2

How readers interpret herem

  • Ancient setting: the Mesha Stele (Moab, c. 840 BCE) records that King Mesha "devoted" the people of the Israelite town of Nebo to his god (Ashtar-)Chemosh: herem was shared Near-Eastern war practice and language.
  • Hyperbole: "no one remaining" is victory rhetoric; Joshua and Judges themselves show Canaanites remaining.
  • Late ideology: many scholars date these texts to the 7th–6th centuries BCE (Deuteronomistic school), long after events; many archaeologists find little sign of a single violent conquest (e.g. Kathleen Kenyon (1906–1978) at Jericho). On this view the point was loyalty to God, not a war plan.
  • Judgement: some readers see God judging Canaanite wickedness (Genesis 15:16; Deuteronomy 9:4–5), a unique, unrepeatable act.
  • Spiritual reading: Origen (c. 185–c. 254), Homilies on Joshua: the wars are the soul's struggle against vices.
  • Restriction: Maimonides (1138–1204), Mishneh Torah (Laws of Kings 6:1): peace must be offered to every city first, even in the land; he notes that the seven nations have long since vanished (Laws of Kings 5:4), and many later authorities held that Amalek, too, can no longer be identified.
  • Susan Niditch, War in the Hebrew Bible (1993): herem appears both as sacrifice to God and as justice against sin, alongside other war ideologies in the Bible.
Mission 2Evaluate

Can the violent God be reconciled with the compassionate God?

  • Claim: The warrior God of herem cannot be reconciled with the compassionate God of Exodus 34:6–7.
  • For: Commands to kill "infant and nursing baby" (1 Samuel 15:3) seem to contradict "slow to anger" and care for the vulnerable.
  • For: Critics say no context excuses such texts; they have been used to justify real violence (e.g. colonial conquest).
  • For: Marcion took this view in the 2nd century; some modern critics (e.g. Richard Dawkins, The God Delusion, 2006) call the Old Testament God morally monstrous.
  • Against: Exodus 34:7 already joins mercy and judgement; the warrior God of Exodus 14–15 liberates slaves.
  • Against: If herem texts are hyperbolic or late ideology, God's character is not described by an actual genocide.
  • Against: Jewish and Christian traditions limited the commands (peace first, Amalek unidentifiable; spiritual reading), so the texts are not read alone.
  • Judgement criteria: Is the issue what happened, or what the text endorses? Can the canon correct itself? Does judgement on wickedness differ from cruelty? How far does ancient context explain, or excuse?

Explaining herem

Task: "Explain the concept of herem."

  • Point: Herem means "devoted": people and goods given wholly to God by destruction.
  • Source: Deuteronomy 20:16–17: in the cities of the land, "save alive nothing that breathes".
  • Develop: The stated aim is to prevent idolatry (20:18); distant cities are offered peace (20:10).
  • Example: Jericho is "devoted … to the LORD" (Joshua 6:17), but Rahab is spared; Achan's theft brings herem on him.
  • Context: Moab's Mesha Stele uses the same idea, so herem belonged to wider ancient war practice.

Mission 3

Jeremiah's world

  • Jeremiah, from a priestly family of Anathoth near Jerusalem (1:1), is called in the 13th year of Josiah, 627 BCE (1:2).
  • 622: Josiah's reform; a law book found in the Temple; worship centralised in Jerusalem (2 Kings 22–23).
  • 609: Josiah killed at Megiddo; Jehoiakim (609–598) rules under Egypt, then Babylon.
  • 605: Babylon defeats Egypt at Carchemish; 597: first deportation (King Jehoiachin).
  • Zedekiah (597–586) rebels; Jerusalem and the Temple fall to Babylon in 587/586 BCE.
  • Jeremiah preaches for about 40 years, through the end of the kingdom. His scribe Baruch writes down his words (Jeremiah 36).
Mission 3

The call (Jeremiah 1:4–19)

  • Before birth: "Before I formed you in the womb, I knew you … I have appointed you a prophet to the nations" (1:5).
  • Objection: "I don't know how to speak; for I am a child" (1:6). Hebrew na'ar: a youth.
  • Word: God touches his mouth: "I have put my words in your mouth" (1:9). Task: "to uproot and to tear down, to destroy and to overthrow, to build and to plant" (1:10).
  • Almond branch: shaqed (almond) sounds like shoqed (watching): God watches over his word (1:11–12).
  • Boiling pot tipping from the north: disaster from the north (1:13–14), later Babylon.
  • Strength: "a fortified city, an iron pillar, and bronze walls" against kings, priests and people (1:18).

The Temple sermon

> "Don't trust in lying words, saying, 'The LORD's temple, the LORD's temple, the LORD's temple, are these.'" (Jeremiah 7:4, WEBBE)

> "Has this house, which is called by my name, become a den of robbers in your eyes? Behold, I myself have seen it," says the LORD. (Jeremiah 7:11, WEBBE)

  • Belief attacked: Zion is inviolable: God's house will never fall (strengthened when Jerusalem survived Assyria in 701 BCE).
  • Condition: safety only if they do justice, protect "the foreigner, the fatherless, and the widow", stop idolatry (7:5–7).
  • Shiloh: God's earlier sanctuary was destroyed; the Temple will go the same way (7:12–14).
  • God forbids Jeremiah to pray for the people (7:16); obedience, not sacrifice, was the Exodus command (7:22–23).
  • Jeremiah 26 tells what followed: priests and prophets call for his death; the officials declare him not worthy of death, and elders recall Micah's similar warning (26:8, 16–18).

Injustice and the monarchy

Mission 3

The undoing of creation (Jeremiah 4:23–28)

  • "I saw the earth and, behold, it was waste and void, and the heavens, and they had no light" (4:23).
  • Hebrew tohu wa-vohu: the same words as the formless earth before creation (Genesis 1:2).
  • Mountains quake, no people, no birds, fruitful land a desert (4:24–26): creation reversed by the people's sin.
  • Yet: "I will not make a full end" (4:27): even here, a limit.
  • The vision links social and religious failure to the whole cosmos.

The new covenant

> "I will put my law in their inward parts, and I will write it in their heart. I will be their God, and they shall be my people." (Jeremiah 31:33, WEBBE)

  • Setting: the Book of Consolation (Jeremiah 30–33), promises of return after exile.
  • Not like the covenant "which they broke" at the Exodus (31:32).
  • Inward: law on the heart, not stone tablets. Universal knowledge: "they will all know me, from their least to their greatest" (31:34). Forgiveness: "I will remember their sin no more" (31:34).

  • Also in chapter 31: Rachel weeping (31:15); "everyone will die for his own iniquity" (31:30): individual responsibility.

Mission 3Evaluate

How new is the new covenant?

  • Claim: Jeremiah's new covenant is a radically new relationship with God, not a renewal.
  • For: It is explicitly "not according to the covenant" of the Exodus (31:32); the law moves from stone to heart.
  • For: Christians read it as fulfilled in Jesus: "the new covenant in my blood" (Luke 22:20; 1 Corinthians 11:25); Hebrews 8:8–12 quotes it in full.
  • Against: The content is the same: "my law" and "I will be their God" (31:33). In Jewish reading it is the renewal of Torah within Israel's heart.
  • Against: Deuteronomy already calls for the law "on your heart" (Deuteronomy 6:6); the idea develops existing faith.
  • Against: Reading it as replacement of Israel (supersessionism) is rejected by many Christians today, as well as by Jews.
  • Judgement criteria: Does "new" refer to content, form, or people? Should the text be read in its exilic setting or through later use? What does 31:31's "Israel and Judah" imply?

The Temple sermon in context

Task: "Explain Jeremiah's condemnation of the Temple."

  • Point: Jeremiah attacks trust in the Temple as a guarantee of safety (7:4).
  • Develop: God's presence is conditional on justice for the vulnerable and on loyalty (7:5–7); without these, it is "a den of robbers" (7:11).
  • Source: Shiloh shows God can abandon his sanctuary (7:12–14).
  • Context: After 701 BCE many believed Zion could not fall; Jeremiah 26 shows the danger of saying otherwise.
  • Link: The same logic drives his condemnation of Jehoiakim (22:13–17): knowing God means doing justice.

Mission 4

The "confessions" of Jeremiah

  • Among Jeremiah's public oracles (chapters 11–20) are private poems: Jeremiah speaking to God, not for him.
  • Often called the confessions (a modern scholars' label; they confess pain, not sin).
  • Set texts: 12:1–6; 15:10–21; 17:14–18; 20:7–13; 20:14–18.
  • Themes: why the wicked prosper; loneliness and persecution; doubt about God's faithfulness; the burden of the word.
  • They use the language of the Psalms' individual laments.
Mission 4

The lament form

  • Hermann Gunkel (1862–1932), Einleitung in die Psalmen (1933, completed by Joachim Begrich; English Introduction to Psalms, 1998): classed psalms by form (Gattung); the individual lament is the most common.
  • Typical elements (not always all, not always in this order):
  • (1) Address: "LORD …"
  • (2) Complaint: about me, about enemies, about God ("How long?").
  • (3) Petition: "Hear", "Heal", "Save".
  • (4) Motivation: reasons for God to act (his reputation, love, the dead cannot praise him).
  • (5) Expression of trust: "But I trust in your loving kindness" (Psalm 13:5).
  • (6) Vow of praise or praise itself.
  • Claus Westermann (1909–2000), Praise and Lament in the Psalms (1954; English 1981): lament usually moves towards praise.

Autobiography or liturgy?

  • Claim: The confessions are Jeremiah's own record of his inner struggle.
  • For: They are tied to his life: his family (12:6), his call (15:20; 20:14–18 echo chapter 1), mockery of his unfulfilled word (17:15).

  • Against: Robert Carroll (1941–2000), Jeremiah (1986): the book's editors created the suffering-prophet figure.
  • Judgement criteria: Can a set form express real feeling? Do the personal details fit only one man? Does the meaning for believers depend on the answer?

Mission 5

A prophet in exile

  • 597 BC: Babylon (Nebuchadnezzar) takes Jerusalem; King Jehoiachin, priests and leading citizens are deported. Ezekiel goes with them.
  • He lives at Tel Aviv by the river Chebar, a canal near Nippur in Babylonia (1:1–3; 3:15).
  • 593 BC: "the fifth year of King Jehoiachin's captivity" (1:2): his call.
  • Zedekiah rules what is left of Judah. The exiles hope for a quick return; prophets in Jerusalem and Babylon promise peace.
  • 587/586 BC: Jerusalem falls, the Temple burns. The book pivots here: judgement (chapters 1–24), oracles against nations (25–32), hope (33–48).
  • Last dated oracle: 571 BC (29:17). Ezekiel's whole ministry is among exiles, not in the land.
Mission 5

The call (2:1–3:15)

  • It follows the throne-chariot vision (chapter 1): "the likeness of the LORD's glory" (1:28) appears in an unclean, foreign land.
  • "Son of man" (Hebrew ben adam): "mortal", a reminder of the gap between the prophet and God's glory. Used about 90 times.
  • The Spirit enters him and sets him on his feet (2:2): Ezekiel is moved by the Spirit throughout the book.
  • Sent to "a nation of rebels" (2:3), a "rebellious house". Success is not promised: "whether they will hear, or whether they will refuse… they will know that there has been a prophet amongst them" (2:5).
  • Made tough: "I have made your forehead as a diamond, harder than flint" (3:9).
  • He goes "in bitterness" (3:14) and sits "overwhelmed amongst them seven days" (3:15).
  • Compare Jeremiah 1:4–19: both are given God's words; Jeremiah's mouth is touched, Ezekiel eats a scroll.

Mission 5

The priestly prophet

  • "Ezekiel the priest, the son of Buzi" (1:3): probably of the Zadokite priestly line of the Jerusalem Temple.
  • "The thirtieth year" (Ezekiel 1:1) may be his age: the age priests began service (Numbers 4:3). An interpretation, not certain.
  • Priestly concerns run through the book:
  • Holiness and uncleanness: he protests at unclean food (4:14); idolatry "defiles" the land and Temple.
  • The glory (kavod) of God: it leaves the defiled Temple (10:18; 11:23) and returns to a new one (43:4).
  • Law and ritual: close links with the Holiness Code (Leviticus 17–26); chapters 40–48 are a priest's plan of Temple, land and worship.
  • The recognition formula "you will know that I am the LORD" (about 70 times; 6:7): judgement and restoration both aim at Israel knowing God.
  • So Ezekiel joins two roles: priest (holiness, Temple) and prophet (word, warning). Some scholars stress the tension; others the unity.

Mission 5

Sign-acts: the siege (4:1–5:17)

  • A symbolic action (sign-act) acts out the message; it does not only illustrate it but is believed to set the word in motion.
  • Brick and iron pan (4:1–3): he draws Jerusalem on a brick and "besieges" it. "This shall be a sign to the house of Israel" (4:3).
  • Lying on his side (4:4–8): 390 days on the left for Israel, 40 on the right for Judah, "each day for a year" (4:6): bearing their iniquity.
  • Siege rations (4:9–17): mixed-grain bread, weighed food and water. Told to bake it on human dung (unclean), he protests as a priest (4:14); God allows cow dung (4:15). Sign of exile in an unclean land.
  • Shaving with a sword (5:1–4): hair weighed in three parts: one burnt, one struck with the sword, one scattered to the wind; a few hairs kept in his robe (a remnant).
  • "This is Jerusalem" (5:5): the city has rebelled more than the nations around her.
Mission 5

Sign-acts: exile baggage (12:1–28)

  • He packs exile's baggage by day, digs through the wall in the evening, and goes out with his face covered (12:3–7).
  • "I am your sign. As I have done, so will it be done to them" (12:11).
  • The prince (12:10–14): Zedekiah will flee by night and be taken to Babylon, yet "not see it" (fulfilled: he was blinded, 2 Kings 25:4–7).
  • Ezekiel eats and drinks trembling (12:17–20): the fear of a besieged land.
  • Two sayings answered (12:21–28): "The days are prolonged, and every vision fails" (12:22) and "he prophesies of times that are far off" (12:27). God: "None of my words will be deferred any more" (12:28).
  • The audience did not doubt that prophets speak; they doubted that this prophet's doom was soon.
Mission 5

The death of his wife (24:15–27)

  • "I will take away from you the desire of your eyes with one stroke; yet you shall neither mourn nor weep" (24:16).
  • He speaks in the morning; his wife dies in the evening; he does as commanded (24:18).
  • No mourning rites: no uncovered head, bare feet, covered lips or mourners' bread (24:17).
  • The people ask what it means. Answer: God will profane the Temple, "the desire of your eyes" (24:21), and their children in Jerusalem will fall.
  • When it comes, they too will be too stunned to mourn: "Ezekiel will be a sign to you" (24:24).
  • A fugitive will bring news; then Ezekiel's muteness (3:26) will end: "your mouth will be opened" (24:27; fulfilled 33:21–22).
  • The most personal sign-act: the prophet's grief becomes the message.

Mission 5

False prophets and two unfaithful wives (13; 16; 23)

  • Chapter 13: "foolish prophets, who follow their own spirit, and have seen nothing" (13:3). They say "Peace" when there is no peace and whitewash a cracked wall (13:10). Women who make magic bands "to hunt souls" (13:18) are condemned.
  • Chapter 16: Jerusalem as a foundling girl, abandoned at birth; God passes by: "Though you are in your blood, live!" (16:6), raises and marries her. She turns to other lovers (idolatry and foreign alliances). Harsh judgement follows, yet: "I will establish an everlasting covenant with you" (16:60).
  • Chapter 23: two sisters, Oholah (Samaria) and Oholibah (Jerusalem) (23:4), unfaithful with Egypt, Assyria and Babylon. Jerusalem is worse than Samaria.

Mission 5Evaluate

"Chapters 16 and 23 still speak with authority today."

  • For: they expose idolatry and political compromise as betrayal of a covenant love, not a broken rule.
  • For: chapter 16 ends with grace: God remembers the covenant and forgives (16:60–63).
  • Against: feminist scholars (Renita Weems, Battered Love, 1995; J. Cheryl Exum, Plotted, Shot, and Painted, 1996) argue the imagery links God's love with violence against a woman, and readers can take it as permission.
  • Against: the shame is placed on female sexuality; male elites, the real decision-makers, are hidden behind the metaphor.
  • Another view: read it as a metaphor for a city and its rulers, with the violence belonging to invading armies; or read it against the grain (resisting the text's own viewpoint), with the critique included.
  • Judge it by: Can a text's purpose be separated from its imagery? Who is the intended audience (male exiled elite)? How do Jewish and Christian communities use these chapters in worship today (rarely read aloud)?
Mission 5Evaluate

Was Ezekiel's behaviour abnormal?

  • Claim: Ezekiel's visions, muteness and sign-acts show a disturbed personality.
  • For: August Klostermann (1877) suggested catalepsy (a trance-like state with a rigid body); Edwin Broome ("Ezekiel's Abnormal Personality", 1946) proposed paranoid schizophrenia; the philosopher Karl Jaspers (1947) also wrote a psychiatric study. David Halperin (Seeking Ezekiel, 1993) reads chapters 16 and 23 through Ezekiel's psychology.
  • Against: sign-acts are a known prophetic genre (Isaiah 20; Jeremiah 13, 19, 27). Walther Zimmerli (1907–1983) and Moshe Greenberg (1928–2010) read them as deliberate, public performances.
  • Against: diagnosing a man from a text written and edited for others is unreliable: the book is shaped literature, not a case-file.
  • Another view: some acts (390 days on one side) may have been carried out in part each day, or reported as vision.
  • Judge it by: Does a psychological reading explain the message, or only the messenger? Does "abnormal" mean "unhealthy" or "uncommon"? Would a believer accept that God can use unusual experience?

The valley of dry bones (37:1–14)

  • "I will open your graves… and I will bring you into the land of Israel" (37:12): national restoration from the "death" of exile.

Mission 6Evaluate

Was Ezekiel the "father of individualism"?

  • Claim: Ezekiel replaced Israel's corporate idea of sin and guilt with individual responsibility.
  • For: 18:4, 18:20 and 33:12–20 judge each person "after his ways"; the sour-grapes proverb is rejected; the watchman warns individuals.
  • Against: Paul Joyce (Divine Initiative and Human Response in Ezekiel, 1989) argues the point is the responsibility of this generation as a whole: it cannot blame its ancestors. The addressee is still "house of Israel".
  • Against: individual responsibility was not new: Deuteronomy 24:16 already forbids punishing children for parents' crimes; and much of Ezekiel stays corporate (chapters 16, 23, 36, 37).
  • For: even before the fall, chapter 9 marks out individuals who "sigh and… cry" over the city's sins to be spared (9:4).
  • Judge it by: Who is addressed (one person or the nation)? What did the exiles need to hear? Does "individualism" read a modern idea back into the text?
Mission 6Evaluate

Hope: God's gift or the people's task?

  • The tension: "make yourself a new heart" (18:31) versus "I will also give you a new heart" (36:26).
  • Divine initiative: after the fall the stress is on God: for his name (36:22), by his Spirit (36:27; 37:14), on dead bones that can do nothing (37:3).
  • Human response: the call to "turn" (33:11) stays; the watchman still warns; the new heart leads to walking in God's statutes (36:27).
  • Joyce's reading: both held together: radical responsibility, and the admission that only God can make the people able to respond.
  • For Jews and Christians today: the new heart and Spirit link to Jeremiah's new covenant (Jeremiah 31:31–34); many Christians see their fulfilment in the gift of the Spirit (Acts 2).
  • Judge it by: Does hope that depends wholly on God weaken responsibility? Does hope that depends on human effort survive despair like 37:11? Which suits a people who "pine away" (33:10)?

Texts that shape and limit leaders

  • In the Old Testament (A2 3), leaders stand under God's word, not above it:
  • The king's law: the king must copy the law and read it all his life (Deuteronomy 17:18–19).
  • Prophets confront kings: Nathan to David (2 Samuel 12); Elijah to Ahab over Naboth's vineyard (1 Kings 21); Amos against King Jeroboam II, before the priest Amaziah at Bethel (Amos 7:10–17).
  • Reform by the book: Josiah's covenant to keep "the words of this covenant that were written in this book" (2 Kings 23:3).
  • Ezra reads the Law to the people; the Levites "gave the sense, so that they understood the reading" (Nehemiah 8:8).

Mission 7

Leaders using texts in society

  • For justice: Martin Luther King Jr quoted Amos: "let justice roll on like rivers, and righteousness like a mighty stream" (Amos 5:24) in the civil rights movement (1963).
  • For liberation: the Exodus has inspired abolitionists, Black churches and liberation theologians.
  • For power: James VI and I, The True Law of Free Monarchies (1598), used scripture to support the divine right of kings.
  • For division: the Dutch Reformed Church's 1974 report used texts such as Babel (Genesis 11) to support apartheid; the church later rejected this (1986 onwards).
  • The same scriptures have been read for and against those in power.

Mission 7Evaluate

Do sacred texts make leadership better?

  • Claim: Religious texts give leaders a sound basis for leadership in religion and society.
  • For: they place leaders under a higher standard (Deuteronomy 17; Nathan); they give a vision of justice for the poor (Amos; Micah 6:8).
  • For: leaders such as King and Tutu drew moral courage and public language from scripture.
  • Against: the same texts were used for divine right, slavery and apartheid: a text can legitimise power as easily as check it.
  • Against: in a plural society, laws based on one community's scripture may exclude others.
  • Judgement criteria: does the text check the leader, or does the leader control the text? Whose interpretation? Religious community or wider society?
Mission 7

Ways of using texts for ethics

  • Rules: commands apply directly (e.g. the Ten Commandments); a form of divine command ethics.
  • Principles: the commands express deeper values, such as "love your neighbour as yourself" (Leviticus 19:18), "act justly, … love mercy, and … walk humbly with your God" (Micah 6:8).
  • Paradigm: Christopher J. H. Wright (b. 1947), Old Testament Ethics for the People of God (2004): Israel's laws are a model society; Christians ask what principles they embody and apply them in new settings.
  • Character and community: scripture forms a people with virtues, rather than giving a rulebook (Stanley Hauerwas, b. 1940).
  • Link to A2 7 (Global Ethics): compare with natural law, utilitarianism and virtue ethics.
Mission 7

Texts ahead of their time, and hard texts

  • Ahead of their time: love for the foreigner (Leviticus 19:34); a weekly rest even for servants, livestock and the stranger (Deuteronomy 5:14); leaving the harvest's edges for the poor (Leviticus 19:9).
  • Hard texts: laws regulating slavery (Exodus 21); herem (devoting enemies to destruction); the death penalty for many offences; texts on women's subordination.
  • Jewish interpretation has long read these through Oral Torah and rabbinic tradition; Christians through Christ and the New Testament.
  • The question is not only "what does the text say?" but "how does it apply now?"

Mission 7Evaluate

Are religious texts a valid guide for ethics today?

  • Claim: Religious texts remain a valid guide for contemporary ethics.
  • For: believers hold them to be God's word, whose core values (justice, mercy, care for the stranger) are timeless.
  • For: principles and paradigms can be applied to new issues (economics, ecology, migration).
  • Against: cultural distance: many laws reflect an ancient society; selecting some and rejecting others needs a standard outside the text.
  • Against: the Euthyphro dilemma (Plato): is something good because God commands it, or commanded because it is good? If the latter, reason may be enough.
  • Against: new issues (IVF, AI, climate) are not addressed directly.
  • Judgement criteria: rules or principles? Who interprets? A guide for believers, or for society as a whole?
Mission 7

Sin as alienation in Genesis 3–11

  • From God: the man and woman hide "from the presence of the LORD God" (Genesis 3:8).
  • From self: "they knew that they were naked" (Genesis 3:7): shame and fear enter.
  • From others: blame ("The woman whom you gave to be with me", Genesis 3:12); Cain kills Abel: "Am I my brother's keeper?" (Genesis 4:9).
  • From creation: the ground is cursed; work becomes toil (Genesis 3:17–19).
  • Between peoples: Babel ends in scattering and confusion (Genesis 11).
  • Hebrew words: chata' (to miss the mark), pesha' (rebellion), avon (iniquity, guilt).

Mission 7

Theology of sin as estrangement

  • Paul: the divided self: "the good which I desire, I don't do" (Romans 7:19).
  • Augustine (354–430): "Thou madest us for Thyself, and our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee" (Confessions I.1, Pusey's translation). Sin is love turned in on itself.
  • Søren Kierkegaard (1813–1855), The Sickness unto Death (1849): sin as despair before God: not willing to be oneself, or defiantly willing to be oneself apart from God.
  • Paul Tillich (1886–1965), Systematic Theology vol. 2 (1957): sin is estrangement from the ground of being (God), from other beings and from oneself.
  • Restoration: "I will also give you a new heart" (Ezekiel 36:26); the law written "in their heart" (Jeremiah 31:33); in Christian belief, atonement is reconciliation (at-one-ment).
Mission 7

Secular accounts of alienation

  • Ludwig Feuerbach (1804–1872), The Essence of Christianity (1841): God is a projection of humanity's own best qualities; religion alienates people from their own nature.
  • Karl Marx (1818–1883), Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts (1844): workers are alienated from their labour, its product, each other and their human nature; religion is "the opium of the people" (1844), a comfort that keeps people from changing their conditions.
  • Existentialists (e.g. Sartre): alienation as part of the human condition, without God.
  • Link to A2 8 (synoptic theme): the claim that religion is "world evading, perpetuating humanity's oppression and alienation".
  • These agree with the Bible that humans are estranged, but disagree on the cause and cure.
Mission 7Evaluate

Is sin best understood as alienation?

  • Claim: Alienation is the most helpful way to understand sin today.
  • For: it fits Genesis 3–4 and the prophets: sin breaks relationships with God, others, self and creation.
  • For: it speaks to modern experience (loneliness, broken communities) and connects with psychology and secular thought.
  • Against: it may soften sin into a feeling or a condition; scripture also speaks of guilt, rebellion and law-breaking (pesha', avon) needing forgiveness.
  • Against: secular thinkers explain alienation without sin or God (Feuerbach, Marx); some argue religion itself causes alienation.
  • Judgement criteria: does the model keep personal responsibility? Does it explain the need for forgiveness and atonement? Does it fit both the texts and experience?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Deuteronomy 26 is a creed of God's saving acts; Exodus 34:6–7 is a creed of his character: merciful, gracious, slow to anger, faithful, yet just.
  • The formula recurs in Numbers 14, Psalms 86, 103 and 145, Joel 2 and Jonah 4, each time reapplied; in Hosea 11 compassion overturns judgement.
  • God's compassion becomes law: protection for the widow, the fatherless and the foreigner, grounded in the memory of Egypt.

Mission 2

  • God is pictured as a warrior who rescues (Exodus 14–15), fights for Israel (Numbers 21; Deuteronomy 2–3, 7) and commands herem in the land (Deuteronomy 20; Joshua 6–11; Amalek in 1 Samuel 15).
  • The prophets turn the image to God's war on injustice and final judgement (Isaiah 59, 63).
  • Readers interpret herem through ancient context, hyperbole, late ideology, divine judgement, spiritual reading and rabbinic restriction; whether it can be reconciled with the compassionate God is debated.

Mission 3

  • Jeremiah (called 627 BCE) preaches through Judah's last decades: the Temple is no guarantee (7:4, 11, 14); kings must do justice (22:13–17; 21:12).
  • Sin undoes creation (4:23–28) and resists change (34:8–22; 13:23); Psalm 44 answers with a lament of the faithful.
  • The new covenant (31:31–34) promises the law written on the heart, universal knowledge of God and forgiveness; how "new" it is is read differently by Jews and Christians.

Mission 4

  • Jeremiah's private poems (12:1–6; 15:10–21; 17:14–18; 20:7–13; 20:14–18) protest to God about the wicked, his suffering and his calling.
  • The individual lament has address, complaint, petition, motivation, trust and praise; Psalm 30 shows the whole movement from distress to praise.
  • Scholars debate whether the confessions are personal, liturgical or editorial; 20:14–18 ends without the turn to praise.

Mission 5

  • Ezekiel, a priest deported in 597 BC, was called in 593 by the river Chebar: sent to a "rebellious house", he ate a scroll of woe.
  • Before the fall he announced judgement in oracles (6, 7, 13, 16, 23) and sign-acts (4, 5, 12, 24): siege, exile and a grief too deep to mourn.
  • Scholars debate the violent marriage imagery and whether his behaviour shows illness or deliberate prophetic performance.

Mission 6

  • The news of the fall (33) reopens Ezekiel's mouth: the watchman now calls each person to turn and live.
  • Hope comes from God: true shepherd (34), new heart and Spirit (36), dry bones raised and two sticks joined (37), a holy Temple with the glory returned (40–43) and a river of life (47).
  • God's presence is found in exile too: "a sanctuary for a little while" (11:16); "The LORD is there" (48:35).

Mission 7

  • Old Testament texts put leaders under God's law and the prophets' challenge; leaders since have used scripture both to check and to legitimise power.
  • Texts can guide ethics as rules, principles, paradigms (C. J. H. Wright) or character formation; critics point to cultural distance, hard texts and the Euthyphro dilemma.
  • Sin as alienation from God, others, self and creation runs from Genesis 3–11 through Paul, Augustine, Kierkegaard and Tillich; Feuerbach and Marx offer secular accounts.
