Mission 1

Levels of ecology

  • Population: all the organisms of one species in one area at one time.
  • Members normally breed with each other. Reproductive isolation separates one population from another.
  • Community: all the populations of every species in an area: plants, animals, fungi and bacteria.
  • Ecosystem: a community plus its abiotic (non-living) environment.
Mission 1

Habitats and ecosystems of every size

  • Habitat: the place where an organism, population or community lives.
  • Describe it by geographical location, physical location and type of ecosystem.
  • Example: marram grass lives on the seaward slopes of foredunes, in the sand-dune ecosystems of western Europe.
  • Ecosystems range in size from very small to very large: a rock pool, a playing field, a large tree, a lake, an ocean.
  • The answer to the hook: each is a community plus its non-living surroundings. Size does not matter.
Quick check
?Two groups of great crested newts breed in ponds 5 km apart, and no newt ever moves between the ponds. How should the two groups be described?
Mission 1

Abiotic and biotic factors

  • Abiotic factors: non-living. Temperature, light, water, pH, salinity, oxygen, wind.
  • Biotic factors: living. Food, predators, competitors, parasites, pathogens.
  • Both control where a species is found (distribution) and how many (abundance).
Quick check
?A student finds a woodland plant only on the shaded side of a hedge. Which pair names one abiotic factor and one biotic factor that could explain this?
Mission 1

Range of tolerance

  • For each abiotic variable, a species survives only between a lower limit and an upper limit: its range of tolerance.
  • Near the middle: the optimum. Growth and reproduction are greatest.
  • Towards each limit: a zone of stress. Individuals survive but grow and breed less.
  • Beyond a limit: the species is absent.
  • Its adaptations set the range. A species lives only where every variable is within range.
  • Examples: plants, light and soil pH; fish, temperature and dissolved oxygen.
  • Say: "the species' adaptations give it a range of tolerance" — not: "individuals adapt to the conditions".
Quick check
?A study reports that brown trout survive in water between about 0 °C and 25 °C and grow fastest between 10 °C and 16 °C. During a hot summer the water temperature in a river rises from 14 °C to 22 °C. Which statement correctly applies the idea of a range of tolerance?
Mission 1

Adapted to a harsh habitat

  • Marram grass (Ammophila arenaria), sand dunes: dry, windy, salt spray, shifting sand.
    • Deep roots and rhizomes reach water and bind the sand.
    • Leaves roll in dry wind. Stomata in grooves on the inner surface, lined with hairs, trap humid air. Less water is lost by transpiration.
  • Grey mangrove (Avicennia marina), tidal mud: salty and almost free of oxygen.
    • Pneumatophores: roots that grow up out of the mud. Oxygen diffuses in through lenticels.
    • Salt glands on the leaves secrete excess salt.
  • Each adaptation widens the range of tolerance for one abiotic variable.
Mission 1

Ranges move when the climate moves

  • As a region warms, a species' range of tolerance stays the same. So its range shifts.
  • Upslope: on Mt Karimui, New Guinea, birds' upper and lower range limits moved up by about 100 m on average between 1965 and 2012.
  • Poleward: in eastern US forests, seedlings of several northern tree species were found further north than adult trees.
  • Trees shift by seeds, over generations: spread at the poleward edge, failure to regenerate at the other.
  • A mountain narrows towards the top. Species near the summit run out of space.
Quick check
?In a survey of forests in the eastern United States, seedlings of several northern tree species were found on average further north than mature trees of the same species, while at the southern edge of the range there were mature trees but few seedlings. What does this indicate?
Mission check

?A fallen, rotting log in a wood holds beetle larvae, woodlice, fungi and bacteria. Inside it are damp, decaying wood and still air. Which statement is correct?

Like a rock pool and a rainforest, the log is living things plus the non-living world they depend on. That is all an ecosystem needs.

Mission 2

Niche: a role, not a place

  • Habitat = where a species lives. Niche = how it lives there: its role in the ecosystem.
  • A niche includes its biotic interactions: what it eats, what eats it, what it competes with.
  • And its abiotic interactions: the temperature, water, light and pH it tolerates.
  • Include everything that affects its growth, survival and reproduction, including how it obtains food.
  • Say: "the niche is the species' role" — not: "the niche is where it lives".
Quick check
?Koalas (Phascolarctos cinereus) feed on Eucalyptus leaves, are active mainly at night, and are found in eucalypt woodland in eastern Australia where rainfall and temperature stay within particular ranges. Which statement about the ecological niche of the koala is correct?
Mission 2

Fundamental and realised niche

  • Fundamental niche: everything a species could do and everywhere it could live, set by its adaptations and tolerance limits.
  • Realised niche: the part it actually occupies when other species compete with it.
  • Connell's barnacles, Scotland (1950s): Chthamalus adults only on the upper shore; Balanus on the middle and lower shore.
  • He removed Balanus from rocks on the lower shore. Chthamalus then settled, survived and grew there.
  • So the lower shore is in Chthamalus's fundamental niche. Competition from Balanus keeps it out.
  • The upper shore is too dry for Balanus: there, Chthamalus is safe.
Mission 2

One niche, one species

  • Two species with the same niche compete for the same limited resources.
  • Competitive exclusion: the better competitor survives; the other is eliminated from that area.
  • Gause (1934): Paramecium aurelia and P. caudatum, both fed on bacteria. Alone, each grew to a steady population.
  • Together, P. caudatum declined until almost none remained.
  • The other outcome: both survive, each restricted to part of its fundamental niche.
  • So no two species in a community have exactly the same niche.
Quick check
?Two species of Paramecium, P. aurelia and P. caudatum, both feed on bacteria. Grown in separate cultures with the same food supply, each species reached a stable population. Grown together in one culture, P. aurelia reached a similar density to when alone, while the population of P. caudatum declined steadily until almost none remained. Which conclusion is supported?
Mission 2

Sharing by dividing: niche partitioning

  • MacArthur (1958): five warbler species, all eating insects in the same spruce trees, in New England.
  • Each fed mostly in a different part of the tree: higher or lower, on outer twigs or inner branches near the trunk.
  • Different zones, different insects: less overlap, so less competition.
  • Niche partitioning: coexisting species divide a resource by place, time or food type.
  • This is the second outcome of competition: each species restricted to part of its fundamental niche.
Quick check
?The fundamental niches of two species overlap and the two species compete. What are the possible outcomes?

Mission 2

Six categories, one example each

  • Herbivory: a primary consumer eats a producer. Aphids pierce leaves for sap.
  • Predation: kills and eats prey. Lynx and snowshoe hare.
  • Interspecific competition: two species, one limited resource. Balanus and Chthamalus for space.
  • Mutualism: both benefit. Zooxanthellae in hard corals.
  • Parasitism: lives on or in a host, feeds, harms, rarely kills. Ticks on deer.
  • Pathogenicity: microorganism or virus causes disease. Plasmodium causes malaria.

Mission 3

Why sample, and why at random

  • Counting every individual is usually impossible: too slow, organisms hidden or moving, the habitat trampled.
  • So count a sample and scale up.
  • Random sampling: every point has an equal chance of being chosen. This avoids bias.
  • The estimate still differs from the true size. That difference is sampling error. More samples reduce it; only a full count removes it.
  • Say: "random placement avoids bias" — not: "choose typical spots".
Mission 3

Random quadrats

  • For sessile or slow-moving organisms: plants, limpets, barnacles.
  • Lay two tape measures at right angles. Use random numbers as coordinates for each quadrat.
  • Measures of abundance:
    • Density: number of individuals per m².
    • Frequency: % of quadrats in which the species occurs.
    • Percentage cover: % of the quadrat area it covers. Use where individuals can't be counted, e.g. grass.
  • The standard deviation of counts per quadrat shows how evenly the population is spread. Calculate it; no need to memorise the formula.
Mission 3

Worked example: from quadrats to a population

  • Plantain plants in a 1200 m² field. Ten random 0.5 m × 0.5 m quadrats.
  • Counts: 3, 0, 5, 2, 4, 1, 6, 3, 2, 4. Total 30.
  • Mean per quadrat = 30 ÷ 10 = 3.0.
  • Quadrat area = 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25 m². Density = 3.0 ÷ 0.25 = 12 per m².
  • Estimate = 12 × 1200 = 14 400 plants (14 000 to 2 significant figures).
  • Standard deviation of the counts = 1.8 per quadrat.
Quick check
?A student places ten 0.5 m × 0.5 m quadrats at random in a 250 m² meadow and counts 20 orchids in total. What is the estimated population of orchids in the meadow?
Mission 3

Transects: sampling along a change

  • Use a transect where conditions change across the site: shore, woodland edge, sand dune, path.
  • Line transect: record every species touching a line at regular intervals.
  • Belt transect: place quadrats along the line, continuously or at regular intervals (interrupted).
  • At each quadrat, measure the abiotic factor: light intensity, soil moisture, soil pH, salinity, temperature. Sensors and data loggers help.
  • Pair each abundance value with its abiotic value. Then look for a correlation.
  • A correlation is not proof of cause. Up a shore, several factors change together.
Mission 3Practical

Distribution and an abiotic factor

  • Skill: transect data from a natural or semi-natural habitat, correlated with an abiotic variable.
  • Method: lay a transect across the gradient; quadrat at fixed intervals; record % cover or density; measure the factor at each quadrat.
  • Control: same quadrat size, same observer, same time of day; repeat transects.
  • Analysis: plot abundance against the factor; test the correlation (e.g. Spearman's rank; choosing tests in another lesson).

Mission 3

Mark, release, recapture

  • For motile animals: woodlice, snails, beetles, voles.
  • Catch a sample. Mark each one harmlessly (paint dot, shell number). Release.
  • Later, catch a second sample. Count the marked ones in it.
  • Lincoln index: population size = M × N ÷ R. M = first caught and marked; N = total recaptured; R = marked recaptured.
  • Example: 60 snails marked; second sample 45, of which 9 marked. 60 × 45 ÷ 9 = 300 snails.
  • Assumptions: marks don't wear off or harm the animal; marked animals mix fully back in; no births, deaths, immigration or emigration between samples; equal chance of capture.
Quick check
?In a capture–mark–release–recapture study, 40 woodlice were caught, marked and released. Later, 50 woodlice were caught, of which 10 were marked. What is the Lincoln index estimate of the population?
Mission 3

Do two species avoid each other? Chi-squared

  • Record presence or absence of species A and B in many random quadrats. Sort into a 2 × 2 table.
  • Null hypothesis: the two species are distributed independently.
  • Expected (for each cell) = row total × column total ÷ grand total.
  • Example (illustrative), 60 quadrats: both 6; A only 24; B only 14; neither 16. A in 30, B in 20.
  • Expected "both" = 30 × 20 ÷ 60 = 10. Observed 6: fewer than expected.
  • χ² = Σ (O − E)² ÷ E = 4.8. Degrees of freedom = 1; critical value (p = 0.05) = 3.84.
  • 4.8 > 3.84: reject the null. A significant negative association: evidence for competition, not proof.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. Two lichen species were recorded as present or absent in 80 random quadrats on a stone wall: both present 16, only A present 32, only B present 24, neither 8. If the two species were distributed independently of each other, how many quadrats would be expected to contain both species?

Mission 4

What a biome is

  • Biome: a group of ecosystems with similar communities, found where the abiotic conditions are similar.
  • Example: tropical forest grows in South America, Central Africa and Southeast Asia.
  • The species differ between continents; the type of community is the same.
  • Two causes: similar conditions, and convergent evolution (similar conditions select similar features in unrelated species).
Mission 4

Two numbers predict the biome

  • On land, the biome depends mainly on temperature and precipitation (rain and snow).
  • Plot mean annual temperature against mean annual precipitation: each region of the graph has one likely natural biome.
  • Too dry for trees: desert or grassland. Wet and warm: tropical forest. Too cold: tundra.
  • Latitude matters only because it changes the climate.
  • Say: "temperature and rainfall together decide the biome" — not: "hot places have rainforest".

Mission 4

Same climate, similar solutions

  • The climate sets the selection pressures; organisms in each biome share adaptations to them.
  • Hot desert: water is scarce and days are hot. Water-storing stems, few or no leaves, deep or widespread roots; animals active at night.
  • Tropical forest: water is plentiful, light is scarce below the canopy, soils are poor in nutrients. Tall trunks with buttress roots, leaves with drip tips, climbers and plants growing on branches.
  • Convergent evolution: American cacti and African euphorbias are unrelated, yet both have spines and swollen, water-storing stems.
Mission 4

Coral reefs: a marine ecosystem

  • Reef-building corals contain zooxanthellae (photosynthetic algae). The algae need light; the coral needs calcium carbonate for its skeleton.
  • Depth: shallow, usually under about 50 m, so light reaches the algae.
  • Clarity: clear water; sediment blocks light and smothers polyps.
  • Temperature: about 23–29 °C. Too warm causes bleaching.
  • Salinity: full seawater, about 32–42 parts per thousand.
  • pH: seawater about pH 8.1–8.2. Lower pH leaves less carbonate for building skeletons.
Quick check

Mission 5

Make it or take it

  • Every cell needs carbon compounds: sugars, proteins, lipids, nucleic acids.
  • Autotroph: makes its own carbon compounds from simple inorganic substances (carbon dioxide, water, mineral ions).
  • Heterotroph: obtains carbon compounds from other organisms, living or dead.
  • Making carbon compounds needs energy from outside the organism.
  • Energy is needed twice: for carbon fixation (CO2 → sugars) and for the anabolic reactions that build macromolecules.
  • Say: "autotrophs use an external energy source" — not: "autotrophs make their own energy".
Mission 5

Two sources of energy

  • Photoautotroph: energy from light.
  • Plants, algae, and several groups of photosynthetic prokaryotes (e.g. cyanobacteria).
  • Algae are not plants. Prokaryotes photosynthesise without chloroplasts.
  • Chemoautotroph: energy from oxidation reactions of inorganic substances.
  • Oxidation releases energy. Example: iron-oxidising bacteria: Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + e⁻.
  • The energy released drives carbon fixation. No light needed.
  • Say: "chemoautotrophs oxidise inorganic substances" — not: "chemoautotrophs respire food they absorb".
Quick check
?Iron-oxidising bacteria live in mine drainage water in darkness. How do they obtain the energy they need to synthesise carbon compounds?
Mission 5

Heterotrophs: break down, then rebuild

  • Food holds large carbon compounds: proteins, nucleic acids, polysaccharides.
  • Digest: hydrolyse them into small molecules. Either outside the organism (fungi) or inside it (animals).
  • Absorb the small molecules.
  • Assimilate: build them into the heterotroph's own molecules, e.g. its own proteins.
  • Say: "amino acids are built into its own proteins" — not: "the protein eaten is used as it is".
Quick check
?A heterotroph eats food containing proteins. Which statement describes how it uses the carbon compounds in this food?
Mission 5

Mixotrophs: both at once

  • Some protists are mixotrophic: autotrophic and heterotrophic.
  • Euglena (fresh water): chloroplasts, so it photosynthesises in light. It also takes in carbon compounds from its surroundings.
  • Facultative mixotroph: can live on either mode alone.
  • Obligate mixotroph: must combine both modes to survive.
  • Not rare: many mixotrophs are in oceanic plankton.
  • Say: "obligate mixotroph: needs both modes" — not: "obliged to photosynthesise".
Quick check
?Euglena gracilis is a freshwater protist with chloroplasts. Cultures grow in the light in a medium with no organic carbon compounds, and also grow in the dark when organic carbon compounds are supplied. Which statement about the nutrition of Euglena is correct?
Mission 5

Archaea: one domain, many diets

  • Life has three domains: Bacteria, Archaea, Eukaryota.
  • Archaea are prokaryotes, but not bacteria.
  • They are metabolically very diverse. To make ATP, different species use:
    • light
    • oxidation of inorganic chemicals
    • oxidation of carbon compounds from other organisms
  • So some archaea are autotrophs and some are heterotrophs.
  • Many live in ordinary soil and seawater, not only in hot springs.
  • Say: "archaea are a domain of life" — not: "archaea are a kind of bacteria".
Quick check
?Three species of archaea were studied. Species 1 obtains energy by oxidising ammonia. Species 2 obtains energy by oxidising carbon compounds absorbed from decaying matter. Species 3 obtains energy from light. What do these observations illustrate?
Mission check

?At a deep-sea vent, far below the reach of sunlight, bacteria fix carbon dioxide using energy released by oxidising hydrogen sulfide. Giant tubeworms there have no mouth and no gut. The bacteria live inside the tubeworm's tissues, and the worm obtains its carbon compounds from them. Which statement is correct?

Light or chemicals, the question is always the same: does the organism build its carbon compounds from carbon dioxide, or take them from someone else?

Mission 6

Ways to take carbon compounds

  • Heterotrophs differ in where they digest and what they feed on.
  • Saprotrophic: feeds on dead organic matter; digests it outside the body.
  • Holozoic: takes food into the body; digests it inside.
Mission 6

Saprotrophs: digestion outside the body

  • Fungal hyphae grow through the food; a network of hyphae is a mycelium.
  • Hyphae secrete enzymes onto the food: extracellular digestion.
  • Large molecules (cellulose, proteins, starch) → small soluble ones (sugars, amino acids).
  • Products are absorbed across the hyphal walls and membranes.
  • Mycelium: thin and branched, so a large surface area for secretion and absorption.
  • Fungi and bacteria that feed this way are called decomposers.
  • Say: "saprotrophs digest externally, then absorb" — not: "fungi take in bits of food".
Quick check
?Which statement describes saprotrophic nutrition?
Mission 6

Holozoic: four stages

  • All animals are heterotrophic. Most feed by holozoic nutrition.
  • Ingestion: food taken into the body, e.g. through a mouth.
  • Digestion: large molecules hydrolysed to small ones inside the body.
  • Absorption: small molecules cross into the body's cells or blood.
  • Assimilation: absorbed molecules built into the animal's own molecules and tissues.
  • Say: "absorbed, then assimilated" — not: "absorption and assimilation are the same".
Quick check
?A dog eats meat. Proteins in the meat are hydrolysed in the gut, the amino acids pass across the wall of the small intestine into the blood, and later some of them are used in the dog's muscle cells to make new protein. Which term describes the use of the amino acids to make the dog's own protein?
Mission 6

Oxygen: needed, optional or deadly

  • Organisms differ in their tolerance of oxygen gas.
  • Obligate aerobe: grows only with oxygen present.
  • Facultative anaerobe: grows with or without oxygen.
  • Obligate anaerobe: grows only without oxygen; oxygen kills it or stops its growth.
  • Obligate anaerobes live in oxygen-free places: deep sediments, waterlogged soil, the gut.
  • Illustrative test: culture tubes where oxygen falls with depth. Where does each grow?
  • Say: "classify by whether it can grow at all with and without oxygen" — not: "by where it grows best".
Quick check
?Three bacteria were cultured in liquid medium. Bacterium X grew only in a tube open to the air. Bacterium Y grew in both an open tube and a sealed oxygen-free tube, forming a denser culture in the open tube. Bacterium Z grew only in the sealed oxygen-free tube. Which classification is correct?
Mission check

?In a compost heap, woodlice eat dead leaves and digest them in their gut. A mould grows hyphae over the same leaves, secretes enzymes onto them and absorbs the sugars released. Soil bacteria on the leaves feed in the same way as the mould. Which statement is correct?

Same dead leaf, two ways in: digest it where it lies, or swallow it and digest it inside.

Mission 7

Teeth match the work the food needs

  • Four tooth types: incisors, canines, premolars, molars.
  • Meat: cut and sheared. Sharp, blade-like cheek teeth; long pointed canines.
  • Tough plant food: ground for a long time. Broad, flat, ridged molars; thick enamel; big jaw muscles.
  • Mixed diet (omnivore): smaller, less specialised teeth.
  • Read the whole skull: teeth, jaw size, jaw joint, muscle attachments.
  • The theory came from living mammals whose diets we can watch. Applying it to fossils is a deduction from theory.
  • Say: "reduced canines and grinding molars" — not: "it has canines, so it eats meat".
Mission 7Practical

Reading a skull

  • Infer diet from skulls: use models or digital collections of hominid skulls.
  • Identify each tooth type; note size, shape and wear.
  • Check the canines, the cheek teeth, the jaw joint and muscle attachment areas (e.g. a sagittal crest).
  • Compare with living mammals of known diet. State a deduction and the evidence for it.
Mission 7

Hominid skulls and diet

  • Paranthropus robustus (extinct):
    • Very large, flat molars with thick enamel; small incisors and canines.
    • Massive jaw; sagittal crest for large chewing muscles.
    • Deduced diet: tough plant food: herbivorous.
  • Homo sapiens:
    • Small molars; small canines, little larger than incisors; light jaw; no crest.
    • Deduced diet: omnivorous.
  • Homo floresiensis (extinct, from Flores, Indonesia):
    • Small teeth with reduced canines; no heavy grinding equipment.
    • Deduced diet: omnivorous.
  • Nobody watched these species eat. The deduction rests on a theory from living mammals.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Population (one species) → community (all species) → ecosystem (community + abiotic environment); ecosystems can be any size.
  • Adaptations set a range of tolerance for each abiotic variable; a species lives only where every variable is within range.

Mission 2

  • A niche is a species' role: its biotic and abiotic interactions. Two species cannot share one niche for long; competition eliminates one or squeezes both.
  • Fundamental niche = potential; realised niche = actual, with competitors.
  • Interactions are scored + / − / 0: competition − / −; predation, herbivory, parasitism + / −; mutualism + / +.

Mission 3

  • Sample at random and scale up: quadrats for sessile organisms, transects where conditions change.
  • Motile animals: mark, release, recapture; population ≈ M × N ÷ R, valid only if its assumptions hold.

Mission 4

  • Temperature and precipitation together predict which terrestrial biome forms; similar conditions and convergent evolution give similar communities of different species.
  • Coral reefs need shallow, clear, warm (not too warm), fully salty water at about pH 8.1–8.2.

Mission 5

  • Autotrophs make carbon compounds from inorganic substances using external energy: light (photoautotrophs) or oxidation of inorganic substances (chemoautotrophs). Heterotrophs take carbon compounds from other organisms, digest them, absorb and assimilate the products.
  • Some protists, such as Euglena, are mixotrophs; archaea are a metabolically diverse domain using light, inorganic oxidation or carbon-compound oxidation.

Mission 6

  • Saprotrophs secrete enzymes onto dead matter and absorb the products; holozoic animals ingest, digest internally, absorb and assimilate.
  • Obligate aerobes need oxygen, obligate anaerobes cannot tolerate it, and facultative anaerobes grow with or without it.

Mission 7

  • Diet is deduced from the whole skull, using a theory built from living mammals.
  • Paranthropus robustus: grinding molars, massive jaw, sagittal crest: herbivorous. Homo sapiens and Homo floresiensis: small unspecialised teeth: omnivorous.
