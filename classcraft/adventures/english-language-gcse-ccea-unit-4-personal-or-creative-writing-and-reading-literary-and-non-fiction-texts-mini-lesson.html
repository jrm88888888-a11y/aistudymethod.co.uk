Mission 1

The text never says she's afraid. How do you know she is?

Make a guess: The text never says she's afraid. How do you know she is?

Reading skills · explicit and implicit informationScreen 1

Stated or suggested

  • Explicit information is stated on the page: you can point to it. She arrived at ten past nine.
  • Implicit information is suggested: you work it out from clues. She kept her coat on suggests she isn't planning to stay.
  • Working out what is suggested is inference. Every inference must be backed by evidence from the text.
  • Both skills are tested early in your paper, usually on a set group of lines.

Reading skills · explicit and implicit informationScreen 5

Making an inference that earns credit

  • Use a chain: detail → what it suggests → why. She did not take her coat off → she isn't settling in → she may need to leave fast.
  • Don't just repeat the text: she keeps her coat on is retrieval, not inference.
  • Don't overreach: nothing in the text says she has been burgled before.
  • Stay in the lines the question names. Evidence from elsewhere earns nothing.
  • When a question says in your own words, explain the idea; don't copy the phrase.

Mission checkScreen 9

?The text never says she's afraid. How do you know she is?

You know because the details imply it. She keeps her coat on, grips a key like a weapon, loses count and creeps away: each is evidence, and inference means saying what the evidence suggests and why.

Mission 2

Slim, skinny, scrawny: same body. Why three different verdicts?

Make a guess: Slim, skinny, scrawny: same body. Why three different verdicts?

Reading skills · word choiceScreen 1

Meaning and associations

  • Denotation: a word's dictionary meaning. Slim, skinny and scrawny all denote "thin".
  • Connotation: the associations and feelings a word carries. Slim sounds approving; skinny is more casual and can criticise; scrawny suggests weakness or poor health.
  • Writers choose words for their connotations, so a single word can steer your judgement.
Reading skills · word choiceScreen 2

Where the judgement hides

  • Verbs show how something acts: a waiter who slaps down a cup is ruder than one who puts it down.
  • Adjectives and adverbs describe; nouns name, and can judge too (a shack vs a cottage).
  • A semantic field is a group of words linked by one topic, such as decay or war.
  • Zoom in: name the word or its class, give its connotations, then say how it shapes the reader's view.

Mission 3

The fog 'crept'. Fog can't creep. Why does the writer say it did?

Make a guess: The fog 'crept'. Fog can't creep. Why does the writer say it did?

Reading skills · imageryScreen 1

Saying one thing in terms of another

  • Simile: compares two things using like or as. The lake was like a sheet of tin.
  • Metaphor: says one thing is another. The lake was a sheet of tin.
  • Personification: gives something non-human human actions or feelings. The wind howled at the door.
  • Extended metaphor: one comparison developed across several phrases or sentences.
  • Each one invites you to see a thing in a new light.
Reading skills · imageryScreen 2

The senses, and how to read an image

  • Sensory imagery appeals to sight, sound, touch, taste or smell.
  • Pathetic fallacy: giving weather or nature human feelings, often to reflect a character's mood.
  • Read any image in three steps: what is compared to what → what they share → what that makes you feel or think.
  • The streetlights were tired eyes → lights and eyes both glow and droop → the street seems weary and watchful.

?The fog 'crept'. Fog can't creep. Why does the writer say it did?

"Crept" is personification: the fog is given a living creature's sneaking movement. It makes the fog seem sly, deliberate and threatening, so the reader feels uneasy before anything happens.

Mission 4

A story opens on a broken window, then cuts to breakfast. Why?

Make a guess: A story opens on a broken window, then cuts to breakfast. Why?

Reading skills · structureScreen 1

What structure means

  • Structure is the order and shape of a whole text: where it starts, where the focus goes, and where it ends.
  • Openings can drop you into action, set a scene or introduce a character.
  • Shifts in focus move between characters, places or times, or zoom in on a detail or out to a wider view.
  • Flashback goes back in time; flash-forward jumps ahead.
  • Endings resolve, leave a question (cliffhanger) or return to the start (cyclical).

Reading skills · structureScreen 4

Writing about structure

  • Ask three questions: where is the focus? when does it shift? why there?
  • Track what the reader knows and what is held back. Structure controls the order in which we find things out.
  • Use terms with a reason: The writer shifts the focus from … to … so that …
  • Comment on the whole text: the start, the middle and the end.

Mission 5

'The storm is terrifying,' says a student. Is agreeing enough?

Make a guess: 'The storm is terrifying,' says a student. Is agreeing enough?

Reading skills · evaluationScreen 1

What evaluation asks

  • To evaluate is to judge: how far is a statement true, and how well does the writer achieve the effect?
  • A statement is a starting point. Test it against the text; don't just repeat it.
  • You may agree fully, partly, or with conditions. What matters is that the judgement is supported.
  • Each point needs three things: what the reader feels, how the writer creates it (method), and evidence.
Reading skills · evaluationScreen 2

Building a judgement

  • Find the key word in the statement (e.g. terrifying). Is it exactly right, or is menacing or gloomy closer?
  • Ask how far: all the way through? equally? for whom?
  • Judge with qualifiers: mostly, at first, especially when, more than.

Mission 6

‘The door opened’ is flat. Why does ‘the door gave way’ tell a story?

Make a guess: ‘The door opened’ is flat. Why does ‘the door gave way’ tell a story?

Writing · vocabulary and sentencesScreen 1

Verbs and nouns that do the work

  • A precise verb carries meaning a plain one leaves out. Compare ‘walked’ with ‘trudged’ (tired), ‘strode’ (confident) and ‘crept’ (secretive).
  • A precise noun can replace a stack of adjectives: ‘a big, loud, angry crowd’ → ‘a mob’.
  • Precision, not length: one exact word beats three loose ones.
  • Cut empty words such as ‘very’, ‘really’, ‘nice’ and ‘lots of’: find the word that says it.
Writing · vocabulary and sentencesScreen 2

Connotation, register and over-writing

  • Connotations are a word's associations: ‘slender’, ‘skinny’ and ‘scrawny’ share a meaning but pass different judgements.
  • Match register to the task: ‘kids’ suits a chatty blog; ‘young people’ suits a letter to a council.
  • Avoid over-writing: showy thesaurus words that don't fit, or an adjective on every noun, distract the reader.
  • Ask of each choice: what does this word make my reader see, feel or think?

Writing · vocabulary and sentencesScreen 4

Four sentence types

TypeBuilt fromExample
Simpleone main clauseThe lights failed.
Compoundtwo main clauses joined by and, but, or, so or a semicolonThe lights failed, and the hall fell silent.
Complexa main clause plus a subordinate clause (because, when, although, if…)When the lights failed, the hall fell silent.
Minorno main verb; used for effectSilence.
  • Simple doesn't mean short: one main clause can carry plenty of detail.
Writing · vocabulary and sentencesScreen 5

Shaping sentences for effect

  • Vary length: a short sentence after long ones lands with force.
  • Vary openings: start with a subordinate clause (‘Although it was late, …’), an adverb (‘Slowly, …’), a prepositional phrase (‘Beneath the bridge, …’) or an -ing verb (‘Shivering, …’).
  • Save the key word for the end: ‘At the bottom of the drawer, under the old letters, lay the key.’
  • Patterns: a list of three or a repeated structure builds rhythm. Use them on purpose, not by habit.

Mission checkScreen 9

?‘The door opened’ is flat. Why does ‘the door gave way’ tell a story?

The difference is the verb. ‘Opened’ reports a result; ‘gave way’ implies pressure, resistance and collapse, so the reader starts imagining what pushed against the door and what lies behind it. Precise words, chosen for their connotations and shaped into varied sentences, let a few words do a lot of work.

Mission 7

A busy market, without the word 'busy'. Where do you point the camera?

Make a guess: A busy market, without the word 'busy'. Where do you point the camera?

Writing · descriptionScreen 1

Point the camera

  • Good description works like a camera, not a list. You choose where it points.
  • Wide shot: the whole scene in a sentence or two. Mid shot: one group or corner. Close-up: one small detail.
  • Choose three or four shots, not twenty. Each new paragraph can be a new shot.
  • The close-up usually carries the feeling: one exact detail the reader remembers.
Writing · descriptionScreen 2

How your exam asks this

  • Unit 3 (previously Unit 4) Section A: personal or creative writing. The creative option may be based on an image, which must stay central to your writing.
Writing · descriptionScreen 3

Show it, don't label it

Label (tells)Detail (shows)
The café was crowded.Two queues for the counter have merged into one argument.
The kitchen was freezing.Her breath clouds over the cereal bowl.
He was nervous.He reads the same line of the menu four times.
  • Pick details only this scene would have.
  • One exact noun ("crate", "awning") beats three adjectives.
  • Use more than sight: one sound, one smell, one texture.

Writing · descriptionScreen 5

Shape without a plot

  • A description needs structure, even without events. Three reliable shapes:
  • Zoom: wide, then closer, then one detail.
  • Time shift: the same place at different times (dawn and dusk, summer and winter).
  • One moment stretched: a few seconds, described slowly.
  • End on purpose: a quiet close-up, or an image that echoes your first line.

Mission checkScreen 9

?A busy market, without the word 'busy'. Where do you point the camera?

Point it at a few chosen shots and finish close. A wide shot sets the scene, a mid shot finds a corner, and one exact detail (a man turning sideways with a crate and still not getting through) shows the crowd better than any label.

Mission 8

Many exam stories go wrong in one place. Beginning, middle or end?

Make a guess: Many exam stories go wrong in one place. Beginning, middle or end?

Writing · narrativeScreen 1

A short story is small

  • In about 45 minutes, write a small story: one main character, one place, a short stretch of time.
  • Something must change: a decision, a discovery, a realisation.
  • A simple shape: situation → problem → turning point → aftermath.
  • Choose a point of view (first or third person) and keep it consistent.
Writing · narrativeScreen 2

How your exam asks this

  • Unit 3 (previously Unit 4) Section A: personal or creative writing. The creative option may be based on an image, which must stay central to your story.
Writing · narrativeScreen 3

Where stories go wrong

  • Too many events: a crash, a rescue, a hospital, a funeral. Each gets one line, and none is felt.
  • A rushed ending: the last event is squeezed in as time runs out.
  • A slow start: the alarm clock, breakfast, the walk to school.
  • The fix: plan the ending first, cut events, and spend your words on the turning point, slowing it down.

Writing · narrativeScreen 5

Openings and endings

  • Open close to the change: start in the moment, or with one telling detail, not with the alarm clock.
  • End with an action or image, not an explanation. Let the reader work out what has changed.
  • An ending can be open: a character pauses, decides or notices, and the story stops.
  • Avoid endings that cancel the story (it was all a dream).

Mission checkScreen 9

?Many exam stories go wrong in one place. Beginning, middle or end?

Usually at the end, though the problem starts in the plan. Too many events mean every moment is summarised and the ending is squeezed in; a small story with one change, a planned ending and a slowed-down turning point has time to land.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Explicit information is stated; implicit information is suggested and must be inferred.
  • A creditworthy inference links a detail to what it suggests and why, using only the lines the question names.
  • When told to use your own words, explain the idea rather than copying it.
  • Denotation is a word's dictionary meaning; connotation is the associations it carries.
  • Verbs, nouns, adjectives and adverbs can all carry a writer's judgement; groups of linked words form a semantic field.
  • Strong answers zoom in on a word, give its connotations and link them to the effect on the reader.
  • Simile (like/as), metaphor (is), personification (human actions or feelings) and extended metaphor all describe one thing in terms of another.
  • Sensory imagery appeals to the five senses; pathetic fallacy gives weather or nature human feelings.
  • Read an image in three steps: what is compared, what they share, what it makes the reader feel or think.
  • Structure is the order and shape of the whole text: opening, shifts in focus (time, place, character, zoom), flashback, ending.
  • Ask where the focus is, when it shifts and why; track what the reader knows and what is held back.
  • Evaluating means judging how far a statement is true and how well the writer achieves an effect.
  • Test the statement's key word against the text; qualify your judgement (mostly, at first, especially when).
  • Back every judgement with what the reader feels, how the writer creates it, and evidence.
  • Choose precise verbs and nouns over plain words and adjective stacks; check connotation and register, and don't over-write.
  • Know the sentence types (simple, compound, complex, minor) and vary length, openings and word order for effect.
  • Your board's writing objective (AO4 i and iii, W3 or AO5) rewards a range of vocabulary and sentence structures.
  • Description works like a camera: choose wide, mid and close-up shots, and move closer on purpose.
  • Show qualities through exact details and nouns, not labels or piled adjectives.
  • Give a description a shape (zoom, time shift, or one moment stretched) and end on a deliberate close-up or echo.
  • Write a small story: one character, one place, a short time, and one change.
  • Shape it (situation, problem, turning point, aftermath), plan the ending first, and slow down at the turning point.
  • Open close to the change and end with an action or image, not an explanation.
