Mission 1

Mission 1: Why would someone risk their savings to start a business, and who else gets a say once it exists?

Make a guess: Why would someone risk their savings to start a business, and who else gets a say once it exists?

Mission 1

Objectives beyond profit

  • Social objectives: aims for society or the environment, e.g. cutting emissions.
  • Satisficing: aiming for a satisfactory level of profit while keeping other groups content, rather than the maximum.
  • Social welfare: some public and not-for-profit bodies aim to maximise the welfare of society, not profit.
Mission 1

Stakeholders

  • Stakeholder: any person or group with an interest in, or affected by, what a firm does.
StakeholderUsually wants
Shareholders (owners)Profit, dividends, a rising share price
ManagersPay, status, a growing firm
EmployeesGood pay, secure jobs, safe conditions
CustomersLow prices, quality, good service
SuppliersRegular orders, prompt payment
LendersInterest paid, loans repaid
Local community, governmentJobs, low pollution, taxes paid
  • Internal stakeholders are part of the firm (owners, managers, employees); external ones are outside it.
  • Owners put their own money at risk when they start or buy into a firm; profit is the reward they hope for, and it is never guaranteed.
  • Shareholders are stakeholders, but not every stakeholder is a shareholder.
Mission 1

When stakeholders clash

  • Illustrative case: a factory plans to automate its packing line, cutting 40 jobs and raising profit.
  • Shareholders gain. Employees lose. Customers may gain from lower prices. The local community loses jobs and spending.
  • Common conflicts: pay vs dividends, prices vs profit, expansion vs the local environment.
  • Whose aims win depends on power:
  • owners vote at meetings and can replace directors
  • employees can strike or leave; unions bargain
  • customers can switch to rivals or boycott
  • lenders set loan terms; governments regulate and tax.
  • Not every aim conflicts: well-paid, secure staff may work better, which helps profit in the long run.
Quick check
?A supermarket chain tells its suppliers that it will now pay invoices after 90 days instead of 30 days, which leaves the chain with more cash in hand. Which stakeholder conflict does this show most directly?
Mission 1

Corporate social responsibility

  • Corporate social responsibility (CSR): a firm takes account of its effects on society and the environment, beyond what the law requires.
  • Examples: cutting emissions, paying suppliers fairly, staff training, community projects.
  • The case for: it can attract customers and staff, protect the firm's reputation and so raise long-run profit.
  • The case against: Milton Friedman (1970) argued that a firm's social responsibility is to increase its profits within the rules; owners, not managers, should decide what good causes to fund.
  • Greenwashing: claiming more CSR than the firm actually does. Claims are hard for customers to check.
  • Say: "CSR goes beyond the law" — not: "CSR means obeying the law".
Mission 1Evaluate

Can a firm keep every stakeholder happy?

It depends on …

  • Time horizon: in the long run, satisfied staff and customers can raise profit, so aims may line up.
  • Power: a firm with one dominant shareholder may follow that owner's aims; a firm with many small shareholders gives managers more room.
  • Competition: fierce competition leaves less room for anything but low costs.
  • Information: it is hard to measure CSR and stakeholder satisfaction, so trade-offs are often hidden.
  • The law: minimum standards on pay, safety and pollution remove some conflicts for every firm.
  • Judge: firms usually balance stakeholders rather than satisfy them all; who wins depends on who holds power.
Mission check

So do passengers and the drivers' union. The company keeps the routes in return for a small council payment, even though cutting them would have raised profit. What best explains the decision?

A firm's objectives are not set by its owners alone: stakeholders with power can change them.

Mission 2

Mission 2: When the people who own a company are not the people who run it, whose goals win?

Make a guess: A large company is owned by thousands of shareholders but run by a team of hired managers. Whose goals usually win?

Mission 2

Owning versus controlling

  • In a small firm (a sole trader or a partnership) the owners usually run the business themselves.
  • A large public limited company (plc) may have thousands of shareholders. They own the company.
  • Shareholders elect a board of directors, who appoint managers to run the firm day to day. The managers control it.
  • This split is the divorce (separation) of ownership and control.
  • It lets a firm raise huge sums from many owners and hire expert managers.
  • But owners and managers may want different things.
  • Say: "a public limited company is privately owned by its shareholders" — not: "a plc is in the public sector".
Mission 2

The principal–agent problem

  • A principal hires an agent to act on the principal's behalf.
  • Here the shareholders are the principals; the managers are the agents.
  • The principal–agent problem: the agent may pursue their own goals rather than the principal's.
  • Two conditions make it a problem:
  • Different objectives: owners want profit (and a rising share price); managers may want pay, status, power or an easy life.
  • Asymmetric information: managers know far more about the business than scattered owners, so owners cannot easily check what managers do.
  • Other examples: voters (principals) and politicians (agents); a patient and a doctor; a homeowner and a builder.

Mission 2

What might managers aim for instead?

  • If owners cannot fully control managers, the firm may not maximise profit (MC = MR).
  • Managerial utility maximisation (Williamson): managers pursue their own satisfaction: salary, perks, large staffs, status.
  • Sales revenue maximisation (Baumol): managers' pay and prestige often rise with the firm's size, so they push revenue (MR = 0) rather than profit.
  • Growth maximisation: a bigger firm brings more power and is harder to take over.
  • Satisficing: managers aim for just enough profit to keep shareholders content, then follow other goals.
  • These objectives are compared on diagrams in an earlier lesson.
Mission 2

Lining up the incentives

Owners try to make the agents' goals match their own:

  • Performance-related pay: bonuses linked to profit or the share price.
  • Share options: managers gain if the share price rises, so they think like owners.
  • Monitoring: independent (non-executive) directors, audited accounts, published reports.
  • Shareholder votes: owners can vote down pay deals or remove directors.
  • UK example: since 2013, shareholders in UK quoted companies have a binding vote on the directors' pay policy.
  • Threat of takeover: if managers waste profit, the share price falls, a rival may buy the firm cheaply, and the managers lose their jobs.
  • Each fix has limits: targets can be gamed, and bonuses tied to this year's results can encourage short-termism.
Quick check
?To align managers with shareholders, a company pays its chief executive a large bonus if the share price is higher at the end of this year. Which unintended effect is most likely?
Mission 2Evaluate

How much does the divorce matter?

  • The principal–agent problem is a strong reason firms may not maximise profit. How serious it is depends on:
  • How spread out ownership is: a few large shareholders (e.g. pension funds) can monitor managers far better than thousands of small ones.
  • Information: better accounts, audits and independent directors shrink the information gap.
  • Competition: in a highly competitive market, a firm that wastes money makes losses and may not survive, which limits managers' freedom.
  • Pay design: rewards tied to long-run value align goals better than one-year targets, but no scheme is perfect.
  • Takeover threat: works only if shares are traded and rivals can buy them.
Mission check

?Lena founded a chain of gyms and still owns all of it. She has moved abroad and hired a chief executive on a salary plus a bonus based on the number of members. Lena checks the accounts twice a year. Which outcome is most likely?

An agent works towards whatever the reward measures, so the reward must measure what the owner wants.

Mission 3: If a kitchen hires twice as many cooks, why might it not make twice as many meals?

Mission 3

Short run and long run

  • Fixed factor: an input whose quantity cannot be changed quickly, e.g. a kitchen, ovens, a factory building.
  • Variable factor: an input that can be changed quickly, e.g. hours of work, ingredients, energy.
  • Short run: at least one factor of production is fixed.
  • Long run: all factors are variable. The firm can build a bigger kitchen.
  • The split is not a set number of months: a market stall can grow in weeks; a power station takes years.
Mission 3

Total, average and marginal product

  • Total product (TP): total output from a given number of workers.
  • Average product (AP): output per worker. AP = TP ÷ number of workers.
  • Marginal product (MP): the extra output from one more worker. MP = change in TP ÷ change in workers.
  • The fixed factor stays the same throughout.

Mission 3

The law of diminishing returns

  • Law of diminishing marginal returns: in the short run, as more of a variable factor is added to a fixed factor, the marginal product of the variable factor eventually falls.
  • Why: each extra cook has less of the fixed factor to work with: fewer ovens and less space per cook.
  • It is not because later cooks are worse: all cooks are assumed identical.
  • It applies only in the short run. In the long run nothing is fixed.
  • Say: "diminishing marginal returns: MP falls" — not: "total output falls".

Mission 3

How MP and AP fit together

  • When MP is above AP, AP rises: a cook who adds more than the average pulls the average up.
  • When MP is below AP, AP falls.
  • So MP cuts AP at AP's maximum (in the café kitchen: the 3rd cook, MP = AP = 12).
  • TP is at its maximum when MP = 0 (the 6th cook).
  • Same logic as test scores: a new score above your average raises it.
Quick check
?A bakery has one oven. Illustrative data: with 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 bakers it makes 8, 20, 30, 36 and 38 trays a day. With which baker do diminishing marginal returns set in?
Mission check

?A call centre has 20 desks and phones, which it cannot add to this year. It hires more staff for each shift. Which outcome is most likely as staff are added?

With one input fixed, adding more of another eventually brings smaller and smaller gains.

Mission 4

Fixed and variable costs

  • Fixed costs (FC, or total fixed cost TFC): do not change with output in the short run. Rent, insurance, loan interest, salaried staff.
  • Fixed costs must be paid even at zero output.
  • Variable costs (VC, or total variable cost TVC): change with output. Ingredients, energy, packaging, hourly wages.
  • Total cost (TC) = fixed cost + variable cost.
Mission 4

Average and marginal cost

  • Average fixed cost: AFC = TFC ÷ Q.
  • Average variable cost: AVC = TVC ÷ Q.
  • Average total cost (often just average cost, AC): ATC = TC ÷ Q = AFC + AVC.
  • Marginal cost: the extra cost of producing one more unit. MC = change in TC ÷ change in Q.
  • Fixed costs do not change, so MC is also the change in variable cost.
  • Say: "marginal cost is the cost of the next unit" — not: "the average cost of all units".

Quick check
?A firm's fixed costs are 200 a week. When it produces 40 units, its total variable cost is 600. Illustrative data. What is its average total cost at 40 units?
Mission 4

Why the curves have their shapes

  • MC falls at first, then rises. Why: with the ovens fixed, diminishing marginal returns set in. Each extra worker adds less output, so each extra batch needs more labour (MC = wage ÷ MP).
  • AFC falls all the time: the same fixed cost spread over more units.
  • AVC and ATC are U-shaped:
    • below its minimum, MC pulls the average down
    • above it, MC pulls the average up
  • So MC cuts AVC and ATC at their lowest points (bakery: AVC lowest at 3 batches, ATC at 4).
  • The gap between ATC and AVC is AFC, so it narrows as output rises.

Mission 4

What shifts the cost curves

  • Higher input prices (wages, energy, raw materials): AVC, ATC and MC shift up. AFC unchanged.
  • Higher fixed costs (rent, insurance, a licence fee): AFC and ATC shift up. AVC and MC unchanged.
  • Higher productivity (better technology, training, organisation): each worker makes more, so MC, AVC and ATC shift down.
  • Say: "a rise in fixed cost shifts ATC" — not: "it shifts MC".
Quick check
?A free software update lets each worker in a print shop produce more pages per hour. Wages and rent do not change. What happens to the print shop's cost curves?
Mission check

?A phone-case factory makes 1,000 cases a day at an average total cost of 12 per case. Making the 1,001st case would add 15 to its total cost. Illustrative data. If it makes that case, what happens to its average total cost?

When the next unit costs more than the average, producing it raises the average.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Stakeholders' aims often conflict; which aims win depends on power. CSR has a case for and against.

Mission 2

  • When owners (principals) hire managers (agents), different goals plus asymmetric information let managers pursue their own aims.
  • So firms may pursue sales, growth, managerial utility or satisficing instead of maximum profit.

Mission 3

  • Short run: at least one factor fixed. Long run: all factors variable.
  • Law of diminishing marginal returns: adding a variable factor to a fixed factor eventually makes MP fall.

Mission 4

  • TC = FC + VC; fixed costs are paid even at zero output.
  • AFC, AVC, ATC are costs per unit; MC is the cost of the next unit.
  • Diminishing returns make MC rise; MC cuts AVC and ATC at their lowest points.
  • Input prices move AVC, ATC and MC; fixed costs move AFC and ATC; productivity gains shift costs down.
