Music for Orchestra in the Twentieth Century

This is one of the three compulsory Areas of Study in CCEA A2 3 (Responding to Music). You listen to and appraise orchestral music of the twentieth century, drawing on four style-worlds: impressionism, neoclassicism, nationalism and jazz influences.

CCEA sets representative works by composers such as Debussy, Ravel, Bartók and Bernstein as examples of these styles. Your focus is the listening and analysis skills — recognising new scales, harmonies, rhythms and orchestral colours.

Big shift from AS: in the twentieth century, functional tonal harmony is stretched, blurred or abandoned. New scales, ostinati and irregular metres take centre stage.

Context · four style-worlds

Four twentieth-century styles

CCEA groups the music into four styles you must be able to hear and describe:

  • Impressionism — atmospheric, blurred tonality, new scales and shimmering orchestral colour (Debussy).
  • Neoclassicism — a return to the clarity and forms of earlier music, but with modern harmony (Ravel).
  • Nationalism — folk-derived modes and rhythms driving the music (Bartók).
  • Jazz influences — syncopation, blue notes and jazz harmony brought into the concert hall (Bernstein).

The orchestra expands: composers reach for new timbres — muted brass, harp glissandi, divided (divisi) strings, celeste and a large, colourful percussion section.

Style · impressionism

The impressionist sound

Impressionism paints atmosphere rather than telling a story. Listen for these devices:

  • The whole-tone scale — a six-note scale built entirely of tones (no semitones), giving a floating, keyless feel.
  • Parallel chords ("planing") — a chord shape moved up or down in parallel, so the harmony glides rather than following traditional progressions.
  • Added-note chords (sevenths and ninths left unresolved), pentatonic melodies and modal colour.
  • Rich orchestral colour: muted brass, harp glissandi, divisi strings, flute and clarinet in soft registers.

Why it blurs: without a clear tonic-and-dominant pull, and with whole-tone or pentatonic material, the ear loses its firm sense of key — exactly the hazy effect intended.

Style · neoclassicism

Neoclassicism — looking back to move forward

Neoclassicism revives the clarity, balance and forms of the Baroque and Classical past, but reharmonises them with a modern accent. Listen for:

  • Revived dance and Baroque forms — a movement might be a Menuet or Rigaudon, using the old dance patterns.
  • Clean, transparent textures and clear phrasing, in reaction against the heavy lushness of late Romanticism.
  • Modal inflections and crisp, sometimes spiky, but still largely tonal harmony.
  • Elegant, restrained orchestration that lets the counterpoint be heard.

Continuity and change: the forms are borrowed from the eighteenth century; the harmony and colour are unmistakably twentieth-century.

Elements · rhythm & metre

Rhythm takes the lead

Twentieth-century orchestral music often makes rhythm the driving force. Key devices:

  • Ostinato — a short pattern repeated persistently, building momentum or hypnotic energy.
  • Changing (irregular) metre — the time signature shifts frequently, or uses asymmetric metres like 5/8 or 7/8, so the pulse feels unpredictable.
  • Syncopation and cross rhythms / polyrhythms — accents off the beat and conflicting patterns layered together.
  • Driving folk-dance rhythms, especially in nationalist music.

Listen for the pulse: if you struggle to tap a steady beat because the metre keeps shifting, you are hearing changing or irregular metre.

Harmony · a widening spectrum

From tonal to modal to atonal

Harmony in this era covers a wide spectrum. Learn to place what you hear:

  • Extended tonality — still a sense of key, but stretched with added-note and unresolved chords.
  • Modal — melodies and harmony drawn from folk modes rather than major/minor.
  • Bitonality / polytonality — two or more keys sounding at once, creating a tangy clash.
  • Atonality — no home key at all; the music avoids any tonic.

Cadences still matter: where tonality survives you may still hear a perfect (V–I) or plagal (IV–I) cadence — but many passages deliberately avoid clear cadences to keep the harmony open.

Style · nationalism & jazz

Nationalism and jazz influences

Two more style-worlds complete the picture:

  • Nationalism — composers draw on the folk music of their country: modal melodies, folk-dance rhythms, driving ostinati and irregular metres, sometimes atmospheric "night music".
  • Jazz influences — the energy of American jazz enters orchestral writing: syncopation, blue notes, jazzy seventh and ninth harmony, and brassy, big-band colours.

Appraising · putting it together

Listening like an examiner

To appraise an unfamiliar twentieth-century orchestral extract, ask:

  • Scale/harmony — whole-tone or pentatonic and blurred (impressionism)? Modal folk material (nationalism)? Bluesy sevenths (jazz)? Bitonal clashes or atonal?
  • Form — a revived Baroque dance form points to neoclassicism.
  • Rhythm/metre — ostinato, changing metre, syncopation or driving folk dance?
  • Colour — muted, glinting impressionist scoring, or brassy jazz colours, or folk-flavoured woodwind?

Always justify. "Nationalist, because of the modal folk melody, driving ostinato and irregular metre."
