Mission 1

Mission 1: Not one filament in a contracting muscle gets shorter. So how does the whole muscle shorten?

Make a guess: Not one filament in a contracting muscle gets shorter. So how does the whole muscle shorten?

Mission 1

From muscle to myofibril

  • Muscle → bundles → muscle fibres → myofibrils → sarcomeres.
  • A muscle fibre is very long and has many nuclei. It forms when many cells fuse.
  • Fibre membrane: sarcolemma. Fibre cytoplasm: sarcoplasm.
  • Inside each fibre:
    • Many myofibrils, packed side by side.
    • Many mitochondria: supply ATP.
    • Sarcoplasmic reticulum: stores calcium ions (Ca²⁺).
    • T-tubules: inward folds of the sarcolemma that carry impulses deep into the fibre.
Mission 1

The sarcomere

  • A sarcomere runs from one Z line to the next.
  • Thin actin filaments: attached to the Z lines.
  • Thick myosin filaments: in the middle, held at the M line.
  • Bands:
    • A band (dark): the full length of the myosin filaments, including overlap.
    • I band (light): actin only. Z line in its centre.
    • H zone: myosin only, in the centre of the A band.
  • Alternating dark and light bands: skeletal muscle is striated.
Mission 1

Sliding filaments

  • Frame 1 — Relaxed: little overlap; wide I band; wide H zone.
  • Frame 2 — Contracted: myosin heads pull actin towards the M line.
  • The filaments slide past each other. Neither gets shorter.
  • Z lines move closer → the sarcomere shortens → the whole muscle shortens.
  • I band narrows. H zone narrows, and may disappear. A band stays the same.
  • Say: "the filaments slide past each other" — not: "the filaments contract".
Quick check
?Electron micrographs of a sarcomere are taken before and after it contracts. Which set of changes is observed?
Mission 1

Read the data: overlap and force

  • Gordon, Huxley and Julian (1966) held single frog muscle fibres at fixed sarcomere lengths, stimulated them, and measured the force.
  • Maximum force: sarcomere length 2.0–2.25 µm.
  • Longer than 2.25 µm: force falls in a straight line, to zero at 3.65 µm.
  • Shorter than 2.0 µm: force also falls, as the filaments start to collide.
  • Why zero at 3.65 µm?
    • Myosin filament: 1.6 µm. Each actin filament (to the Z line): about 1.025 µm.
    • 1.6 + 1.025 + 1.025 = 3.65 µm: actin and myosin no longer overlap.
  • More overlap → more myosin heads reach actin → more force. Strong evidence for sliding filaments.

?In the data on the earlier screen, a sarcomere held at 3.0 µm produces about 46% of the maximum force. Why is the force lower than at 2.2 µm?
Mission 1

The cross-bridge cycle

  • Frame 1 — An impulse spreads along the sarcolemma and down the T-tubules → the sarcoplasmic reticulum releases Ca²⁺ into the sarcoplasm.
  • Frame 2 — Ca²⁺ binds troponin; troponin changes shape and pulls tropomyosin off the binding sites on actin.
  • Frame 3 — A myosin head (carrying ADP + Pi) binds actin: a cross-bridge. The head pivots: the power stroke pulls actin towards the M line. ADP and Pi are released.
  • Frame 4 — A new ATP binds the head → the head detaches. ATPase on the head hydrolyses ATP → ADP + Pi; this re-cocks the head.
  • The cycle repeats while Ca²⁺ and ATP are present.
  • Relaxation: Ca²⁺ is actively transported back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum (uses ATP); binding sites are blocked again.
  • Say: "Ca²⁺ uncovers the binding sites; ATP detaches the head" — not: "Ca²⁺ powers the stroke".
Quick check
?A drug blocks the release of calcium ions from the sarcoplasmic reticulum of a skeletal muscle fibre. The motor neuron still stimulates the fibre normally. What is the effect on the fibre?
Mission 1

Muscles only pull

  • Muscle exerts force only by contracting. It can pull; it cannot push.
  • So muscles work in antagonistic pairs: one contracts, the other relaxes and is stretched.
  • Elbow: biceps contracts → arm bends. Triceps contracts → arm straightens.
  • Tendons: tough, inelastic cords joining muscle to bone. They pass the pull to the bone.
  • Forelimb: the biceps tendon attaches to the radius; the triceps tendon to the ulna, behind the elbow.
  • Say: "the triceps contracts to straighten the arm" — not: "the biceps pushes the arm straight".
Quick check
?The biceps contracts and shortens to flex the elbow. When the arm is then straightened, how is the biceps returned to its longer, resting length?
Mission check

?After death, cells stop making ATP. A few hours later the skeletal muscles become rigid and cannot be stretched (rigor mortis). Which explanation fits the sliding filament model?

The filaments slide; the heads must let go to slide again. No ATP, no letting go.

Make a guess: Some muscle works all your life without tiring; some tires in seconds. What makes the difference?

Mission 2

Keeping ATP topped up

  • Myosin heads and Ca²⁺ pumps use ATP fast. A fibre holds only enough ATP for a few seconds of hard work.
  • Phosphocreatine (creatine phosphate) is the first back-up:
    • PCr + ADP → creatine + ATP. One step: fast; no oxygen needed.
    • Lasts a few more seconds of maximum effort.
    • Rebuilt from creatine when the muscle rests, using ATP from respiration.
  • Your spec words this as "creatinine phosphate". The molecule is creatine phosphate; creatinine is the waste made from creatine.
  • Then respiration supplies ATP: aerobic if O2 is enough; anaerobic if not.
  • Anaerobic: pyruvate → lactate. Lactate builds up in the muscle and blood; the pH falls. Linked to fatigue. Lactate is later removed, e.g. converted back in the liver.
  • Fuel: glycogen in the muscle is the main store respired during contraction. Protein is used only a little, mainly in long exercise when glycogen runs low.
  • Your spec words this as "glycogen and protein as sources of energy during contraction". Protein is only a minor source.
  • Say: "ATP is used as soon as it is made" — not: "muscles store ATP for later".
Quick check
?In the first few seconds of a sprint, muscle fibres use ATP faster than respiration can make it. What keeps ATP available?

Mission 3: Your hip and your knee both carry your weight. Why can only one of them swing your leg in a circle?

Make a guess: Your hip and your knee both carry your weight. Why can only one of them swing your leg in a circle?

Mission 3

Joints: where bones meet

  • Immovable (fused): bones locked together. Skull sutures; the sacrum.
  • Gliding: flat surfaces slide a little. Between carpals; between tarsals; between vertebrae.
  • Hinge: movement in one plane, like a door. Elbow, knee, finger joints.
  • Ball and socket: a rounded head in a cup. Movement in every direction, including rotation. Hip, shoulder.
Mission 3

Inside a synovial joint

  • Hinge, ball-and-socket and gliding joints are all synovial joints: freely movable.
  • Articular cartilage (hyaline): smooth caps on the bone ends. Cuts friction; absorbs shock.
  • Joint capsule: a tough sleeve enclosing the joint.
  • Synovial membrane: lines the capsule; secretes synovial fluid.
  • Synovial fluid: lubricates the joint; nourishes the cartilage.
  • Ligaments: join bone to bone. Hold the joint together; limit movement to a safe range.
  • Say: "ligaments join bone to bone; tendons join muscle to bone" — not the other way round.
Mission 3

The hip joint

  • A ball-and-socket joint.
  • Ball: the rounded head of the femur.
  • Socket: a deep cup in the pelvis.
  • Cartilage covers both surfaces; synovial fluid fills the space between.
  • Strong ligaments hold the head of the femur in the socket.
  • Muscles, attached by tendons to the femur and pelvis, swing the thigh in any direction.
  • A deep socket: stable enough to carry body weight.
Quick check
?Which statement correctly describes the roles of structures in the human hip joint?
Mission 3

Working scientifically: dissecting a chicken leg

  • Remove the skin with scissors; cut away from your body; wash hands after (raw chicken carries bacteria).
  • Find tendons: shiny white cords running from muscle to bone. Pull one: a toe moves.
  • Cut into a joint capsule: a little clear, slippery synovial fluid.
  • Bone ends: smooth, bluish-white articular cartilage.
  • Ligaments: tough bands from bone to bone across the joint.
  • Classify the joints: knee = hinge; hip = ball and socket.
Quick check
?The wrist contains eight small carpal bones. Their flat surfaces slide slightly over one another when the hand bends. What type of joint lies between neighbouring carpals?
Mission check

?The shoulder is a ball-and-socket joint. Its socket is shallow, covering only part of the head of the humerus, and its capsule is loose. Which prediction about the shoulder, compared with the hip, is correct?

Same type of joint, different build: the deep hip socket carries weight; the shallow shoulder socket reaches in every direction.

Mission 4

Mission 4: A muscle can only pull. So how does a bent arm ever straighten again?

Make a guess: A muscle can only pull. So how does a bent arm ever straighten again?

Mission 4

Muscles need something to pull on

  • Muscle exerts force only by contracting: it pulls, never pushes.
  • Each end is anchored to a rigid part, across a joint.
  • The pull moves one part relative to the other: a lever.
  • Fulcrum = the joint. Effort = the muscle's pull. Load = the weight of the part moved, plus anything it carries.
Mission 4

Three orders of lever

  • Look at what lies in the middle.
  • 1st order: fulcrum in the middle. Nodding the head: neck muscles pull down at the back of the skull; the face is the load. Also the triceps straightening the elbow.
  • 2nd order: load in the middle. Standing on tiptoe: ball of the foot = fulcrum; body weight through the ankle; calf muscle pulls up on the heel.
  • 3rd order: effort in the middle. Biceps bending the elbow: it attaches to the radius, just below the elbow.
  • Memory: middle item for 1, 2, 3 = F, L, E.
Mission 4Worked example

The biceps as a 3rd-order lever

A hand holds a 50 N weight 30 cm from the elbow. The biceps attaches 4 cm from the elbow. Ignore the forearm's own weight. Illustrative values.

  • Balance turning effects about the elbow (fulcrum).
  • Effort × 4 cm = 50 N × 30 cm
  • Effort = 1500 ÷ 4 = 375 N: more than 7 times the load.
  • The pay-off: the hand moves 30 ÷ 4 = 7.5 times as far, and as fast, as the point where the biceps pulls.
  • 3rd-order levers trade force for range and speed.
Quick check
?A person stands on tiptoe. The ball of the foot is the pivot, the body's weight acts down through the ankle, and the calf muscle pulls up on the heel. Which order of lever is this?
Mission 4

Antagonistic pairs

  • A muscle cannot lengthen itself. So muscles work in antagonistic pairs.
  • Bend (flex) the elbow: biceps contracts, triceps relaxes and is stretched.
  • Straighten (extend) the elbow: triceps contracts, biceps relaxes and is stretched.
  • One muscle's contraction lengthens the other.
  • Tendons: cords of dense collagen joining muscle to bone. Strong and almost inextensible, so the muscle's pull reaches the bone.
  • Say: "the triceps pulls the arm straight" — not: "the biceps pushes the arm straight".
Quick check
?Contracting the quadriceps, at the front of the thigh, straightens the knee. How is the knee bent again?
Mission check

?A locust's hind leg has a large muscle that straightens the leg for a jump (the extensor) and a smaller muscle that bends it (the flexor). Both are attached inside the leg's hard outer skeleton, either side of the knee. If the flexor muscle were cut, what would happen?

Locust leg or human arm, the rule is the same: muscles only pull, so every movement needs a partner to pull it back.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Sarcomeres shorten because actin slides over myosin: I band and H zone narrow, A band stays the same. More overlap, more force.
  • Ca²⁺ uncovers binding sites on actin; myosin heads bind, pull, and use ATP to detach and reset.
  • Muscles only pull, so they work in antagonistic pairs; tendons pass the pull to bone.

Mission 2

  • Slow fibres (mitochondria, myoglobin, capillaries) respire aerobically and resist fatigue; fast fibres rely on anaerobic respiration and phosphocreatine and tire quickly.
  • Phosphocreatine regenerates ATP from ADP in the first seconds of hard work.

Mission 3

  • Joints: fused, gliding, hinge (one plane), ball and socket (all directions). Synovial joint: articular cartilage, capsule, synovial membrane secreting synovial fluid, ligaments (bone to bone).

Mission 4

  • Muscles only pull: anchored to a skeleton, they act on levers (joint = fulcrum) and work in antagonistic pairs; tendons pass the pull to the bone.
  • Lever order by the middle item: 1st fulcrum (nodding), 2nd load (tiptoe), 3rd effort (biceps).
