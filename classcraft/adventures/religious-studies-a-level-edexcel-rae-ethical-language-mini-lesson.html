Mission 1

Right because God commands

  • Divine command theory (DCT): an act is right because God commands it and wrong because God forbids it.
  • God is the origin of morality (God makes moral truth) and its regulator (God's will settles what is binding).
  • Moral goodness = complying with God's commands.
  • Moral truths are objective: true whatever any human thinks, because they rest on God's will.
  • A meta-ethical theory: it says what "right" and "wrong" mean or what makes them true, not only which acts are right.
  • Also called divine command ethics: a strong form of the view that morality depends on religion (see Mission 5).

Commands and a hard case

> "I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage. You shall have no other gods before me." (Exodus 20:2–3, WEBBE)

> "You shall not murder." (Exodus 20:13, WEBBE)

> "Now take your son, your only son, Isaac, whom you love, … Offer him there as a burnt offering …" (Genesis 22:2, WEBBE)

  • The Ten Commandments open with who commands, not with reasons: the authority is God's.
  • In Genesis 22 the angel of the LORD stops Abraham at the last moment (22:11–12). Jewish and Christian readers praise Abraham's faith and read the story in different ways (e.g. a test of trust; a rejection of child sacrifice).

Plato's Euthyphro

  • Plato (c. 427–347 BCE), Euthyphro, a dialogue set in 399 BCE outside the court where Socrates' trial is about to begin.
  • Euthyphro is prosecuting his own father, sure that the gods approve. Socrates asks what piety (holiness) is.

> "what all the gods love is pious and holy, and the opposite which they all hate, impious." (Euthyphro, 9e, tr. Jowett)

> "The point which I should first wish to understand is whether the pious or holy is beloved by the gods because it is holy, or holy because it is beloved of the gods." (10a, tr. Jowett)

Q1 Which half of Socrates' question matches DCT?

Show answer

Holy because beloved of the gods: the gods' attitude makes it so.

Q2 Euthyphro's gods are many and quarrel. Why do monotheists still face the question?

Show answer

Swap "the gods love" for "God commands": the same two options remain.

Mission 1

Two horns

The Euthyphro dilemma, restated for one God: is X good because God commands it, or does God command it because it is good?

  • Horn 1: God commands it because it is good.
    • Goodness exists independently of God; God recognises it.
    • Implications: God is not the source of morality; God seems bound by a standard (a problem for omnipotence); we could know right and wrong without God.
  • Horn 2: It is good because God commands it.
    • This is DCT.
    • Implications: if God had commanded cruelty, cruelty would be right (arbitrariness). "God is good" would mean only "God does what God commands" (emptiness).
  • One reply: Robert Adams (1937–2024) offers a modified divine command theory: God's commands flow from God's loving nature, so God would not command cruelty. A further objection (pluralism): religions report different divine commands, so which are God's?
Quick check

Mission 1Evaluate

Is morality what God commands?

  • Claim: Morality is what God commands.
  • For: gives objective, universal morality; keeps God sovereign (omnipotence); explains why morality feels like a command and not just advice; fits scripture's presentation of law.
  • For: Adams' version answers arbitrariness: commands flow from God's loving nature.
  • Against: the Euthyphro dilemma: either goodness is independent of God, or it is arbitrary.
  • Against: pluralism: claimed commands conflict; we must judge between them using something other than command.
  • Against: many non-religious people live morally; morality seems knowable without God (see Mission 5).
  • Judgement criteria: Does a reply to Euthyphro avoid both horns? Is "God is good" still meaningful? Can we know God's commands? Does the view fit believers' own sense that God is just?

Mission 2

Ethics about ethics

  • Normative ethics asks: what should I do? (Kant, utilitarianism, natural law.)
  • Meta-ethics asks about moral language and its basis: what does "good" mean? Can "murder is wrong" be true? What would make it true?
Mission 2

Two questions, four positions

  • Cognitivism: moral statements express beliefs; they can be true or false (factual language).
  • Non-cognitivism: they express attitudes or commands, not beliefs; not true or false (see Mission 4).
  • Realism: moral facts exist independently of human minds.
  • Anti-realism: there are no mind-independent moral facts; morality depends on human attitudes, choices or cultures.
  • Language as factual (describes how things are) or symbolic (expresses a stance, points beyond itself).
  • Ethical assertions as absolutist (true everywhere, always) or relative (true for a person, group or culture).
  • Naturalism and intuitionism are both cognitivist and realist. They disagree about what kind of fact a moral fact is.
Quick check
?Sam says: "'Murder is wrong' is either true or false, and it is made true by a fact that holds whatever anyone thinks." How should Sam's view be classified?
Mission 2

Ethical naturalism

  • Naturalism: moral terms can be defined by a natural property: something in the world we can observe or study.
  • So moral statements are cognitivist (they express beliefs that can be true or false): they can be verified or falsified like other facts.
  • Verified moral statements are objective truths and universal.
  • Moral facts exist independently of human beings: we discover them, as we discover facts in science.
  • Candidates for "good": pleasure (Bentham); happiness; what satisfies human needs or flourishing; what a well-ordered society requires (F. H. Bradley).
Mission 2Read the source

Utilitarianism as naturalism

Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832), An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789), ch. I:

> "Nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure."

John Stuart Mill (1806–1873), Utilitarianism (1863), ch. IV:

> "the sole evidence it is possible to produce that anything is desirable, is that people do actually desire it."

  • Right = what produces pleasure (or happiness); wrong = what produces pain. Both are natural, observable states.
  • So "this act is right" becomes a factual claim about consequences, checkable in principle.

Q1 Where does Bentham locate the standard of right and wrong?

Show answer

In nature: pleasure and pain.

Q2 Which step in Mill's sentence would a critic say slides from "is" to "ought"?

Show answer

From what people do desire to what is desirable, i.e. worth desiring.

Mission 2

Naturalism and absolutism

  • If "good" names a natural property, moral truths are facts, the same for everyone. That supports absolutism: some acts are right or wrong always and everywhere.
  • Example: if good = what meets basic human needs, then torturing for fun is wrong in every culture, because it never meets them.
  • But naturalism need not be absolutist: if good = what this society approves, or what gives this person pleasure, the facts vary, and naturalism supports relativism.
  • Spec link: ethical assertions as absolutist or relative.
Mission 2Read the source

Hume's is–ought gap

David Hume (1711–1776), A Treatise of Human Nature (1739–40), Book III, Part I, Section I:

> "… instead of the usual copulations of propositions, is, and is not, I meet with no proposition that is not connected with an ought, or an ought not."

  • Hume noticed moral writers move from facts (what is) to duties (what ought to be) without explanation.
  • Hume's Law (the is–ought gap): you cannot validly derive an "ought" from premises that contain only "is".
  • Against naturalism: facts about pleasure or needs do not by themselves give a duty.

Q1 What change in the writers' sentences surprised Hume?

Show answer

From "is / is not" to "ought / ought not".

Q2 What would a naturalist need to bridge the gap?

Show answer

A premise linking fact to value, e.g. "we ought to promote pleasure", which is itself an "ought".

Mission 2

Moore: the naturalistic fallacy and the open question

  • G. E. Moore (1873–1958), Principia Ethica (1903).
  • Good is indefinable: it is a simple notion, like yellow. You can point to it, not break it into parts.
  • The naturalistic fallacy: identifying "good" with any natural property (pleasure, what is desired, what is more evolved).
  • The open question argument: for any definition, "X is pleasant, but is it good?" is still a sensible, open question. "X is pleasant, but is it pleasant?" is not.
  • So "good" does not mean "pleasant". Moral facts cannot be reduced to natural ones.
  • Reply: William Frankena (1908–1994), "The Naturalistic Fallacy" (1939): calling it a "fallacy" assumes what it sets out to prove.
  • Later naturalists' reply: "water is H₂O" was once an open question, yet true: two terms can pick out the same property.
  • Moore's own view: good is a real, non-natural property known by intuition (see Mission 3).

Mission 2Evaluate

Can "good" be defined in natural terms?

  • Claim: Moral terms can be defined by natural properties.
  • For: makes morality objective and knowable by evidence; fits a scientific view of the world; explains why pleasure, pain and needs matter morally; makes moral disagreement resolvable in principle.
  • Against: Hume's is–ought gap; Moore's open question; naturalists disagree on which property (pleasure? needs? a well-ordered society?); some pleasures seem bad (cruelty enjoyed).
  • Replies to the critics: Frankena; "water = H₂O"; Mill offers more than a dictionary definition.
  • Judgement criteria: Does the theory explain why moral facts bind us? Can it survive the open question? Does it fit how people actually argue about morals?

Mission 3

Real but not natural

  • Non-naturalism: moral properties are real and objective, but not natural properties. You cannot see, measure or test them.
  • Like naturalism, it is cognitivist and realist: "torturing for fun is wrong" is true, whatever anyone thinks.
  • Unlike naturalism: "good" cannot be defined by pleasure, happiness or any other natural property (see Mission 2).
  • So how do we know moral truths? Not by the senses, but by intuition: a direct, rational grasp.
  • Intuitionism: basic moral truths are indefinable but self-evident.
Mission 3Read the source

Moore on "good"

G. E. Moore (1873–1958), Principia Ethica (1903), ch. I:

> "If I am asked 'What is good?' my answer is that good is good, and that is the end of the matter." (§6)

> "My point is that good is a simple notion, just as yellow is a simple notion" (§7)

  • You can explain "horse" by its parts. You cannot explain "yellow" to someone who has never seen it: you can only point.
  • "Good" is like yellow: simple, with no parts to define it by, yet perfectly real.
  • Moore: we know by intuition which things are good in themselves; he named personal affection and the enjoyment of beauty as the greatest (ch. VI).

Q1 Why is "good is good" not an empty answer, for Moore?

Show answer

Because good is simple: there is nothing more basic to define it by.

Q2 What does the yellow comparison suggest about how we know good?

Show answer

Directly, by acquaintance, not by definition or proof.

Mission 3

Self-evident is not the same as obvious

  • Self-evident: known by reflecting on the claim itself, without proof from other truths. Compare: "a whole is greater than its part".
  • Not "obvious to everyone at once": it may take a mature mind and careful thought.
  • So moral intuition is like the grasp of mathematical axioms, not like a hunch or gut feeling.
  • Examples intuitionists give: we ought to keep promises; we ought not harm the innocent; gratitude is owed to benefactors.
Quick check

Mission 3

Prichard: we see what we ought to do

  • H. A. Prichard (1871–1947), "Does Moral Philosophy Rest on a Mistake?", Mind (1912).
  • Moore intuits good; Prichard intuits obligation: "ought to do" has no definition.
  • The "mistake": trying to prove our duties, by showing they lead to good results or to our happiness.
  • Our sense of obligation is "absolutely underivative or immediate" (1912).
  • Two ways of thinking: general thinking (reasoning about facts and consequences) and moral thinking (directly apprehending what we ought to do in a situation).
  • If we doubt a duty, "the remedy lies not in any process of general thinking": we must face the actual situation and let moral thinking see the obligation.
Mission 3

Ross: duties seen, then weighed

  • W. D. Ross (1877–1971), The Right and the Good (1930).
  • Prima facie duties (fidelity, reparation, gratitude, justice, beneficence, self-improvement, non-maleficence) are self-evident, like axioms.
  • When they conflict, intuition cannot read off the answer: we need judgement about this case.
  • Ross: the considered moral convictions of thoughtful people are the data of ethics, as sense-perceptions are of science (paraphrase).
  • So intuitionism can be pluralist: several basic duties, not one master principle.

Mission 3

What intuitionism claims

  • Objective moral laws exist independently of human beings.
  • Moral truths are discovered by using our minds intuitively.
  • Intuitive ability is innate and the same for all moral agents.
  • But it needs a mature mind: children and careless thinkers can get it wrong. So intuition is not infallible.
  • It allows objective moral values without defining them by natural facts or by God's commands.
Mission 3

Challenges

  • No proof: there is no evidence that a moral "intuition" faculty exists; the claim cannot be tested.
  • Intuitions differ widely: across cultures and between thoughtful people (e.g. on eating animals, on war).
  • No way to resolve conflicts: if two intuitions clash, intuitionism has no further test to settle which is right.
  • Queerness: J. L. Mackie (1917–1981), Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong (1977): non-natural moral properties, and a special faculty to perceive them, would be utterly unlike anything else we know (paraphrase).
  • Origins: intuitions may come from upbringing, culture or evolution, not insight into moral truth.
  • Replies: many disputes are about the facts of a case or the weighting of duties (how much each counts when they clash), not about basic duties; disagreement in maths or science does not prove there is no truth; the mature-mind condition explains some differences.
Quick check
?Two thoughtful, well-informed people have opposite, firmly held intuitions about whether eating meat is wrong. Which challenge to intuitionism does this case illustrate most directly?
Mission 3Evaluate

Are moral terms intuitive?

  • Claim: We know basic moral truths by intuition.
  • For: avoids the naturalistic fallacy and Hume's gap; explains moral certainty (torturing for fun is wrong, whatever the argument); fits common sense: people do "just know"; allows objective values.
  • Against: no proof of the faculty; intuitions differ; no way to settle conflicts; queerness; intuitions may be products of culture.
  • Compare: naturalism (see Mission 2) makes morality testable but faces the open question.
  • Judgement criteria: Is disagreement at the level of basic principles or of application? Can a theory without a test still give knowledge? Does it explain why morality binds us?
Mission 3Worked answer

An evaluation paragraph

Question: "Moral truths are self-evident." Evaluate this view.

  • Point: Intuitionism explains moral certainty: some claims, such as "torturing for fun is wrong", seem known more surely than any argument against them.
  • Support: Moore: good is simple and indefinable, so it can only be known directly.
  • Support: Prichard: obligation is "absolutely underivative or immediate".
  • Counter: But intuitions differ widely, and intuitionism offers no test to decide between them.
  • Response: Ross: many disagreements concern facts or the weighting of duties, not basic duties themselves.
  • Mini-judgement: The view is strongest for a small core of basic duties, weakest where intuitions conflict.

Mission 4

Logical positivism: the test for meaning

  • Logical positivism: a movement of the 1920s–30s, centred on the Vienna Circle.
  • The verification principle: a statement is meaningful only if it is either
  • analytic: true by definition ("all bachelors are unmarried"), or
  • empirically verifiable: checkable, at least in principle, by sense experience ("there is milk in the fridge").
  • Anything else is, literally, meaningless as a statement of fact.
  • A. J. Ayer (1910–1989) brought it to Britain in Language, Truth and Logic (1936).
  • Spec: "the influence of logical positivism on emotivist theories of ethics".
Mission 4

Ayer: ethics fails the test

  • Language, Truth and Logic, ch. 6, "Critique of Ethics and Theology".
  • "Stealing is wrong" is not analytic (not true by definition) and not verifiable (no observation shows "wrongness").
  • So it states no fact: ethical words are pseudo-concepts. It is neither true nor false: non-cognitivism.
  • What it does: expresses the speaker's feeling (approval or disapproval) and aims to arouse feeling in others and stimulate action.
Mission 4Read the source

Ayer on stealing

Paraphrase of Ayer, Language, Truth and Logic (1936), ch. 6:

  • If I tell someone, "You acted wrongly in stealing that money", I state nothing more than "You stole that money".
  • Adding "wrongly" adds no fact. It is as if I had said "You stole that money" "in a peculiar tone of horror", or with exclamation marks.
  • If I go on to say "Stealing money is wrong", my sentence has no factual meaning: it expresses moral disapproval.

Q1 What does the word "wrongly" add to the sentence, on Ayer's view?

Show answer

No fact: only the speaker's feeling, like a tone of voice.

Q2 Can two people contradict each other about whether stealing is wrong, on this view?

Show answer

Not about a fact: they express different feelings. They can still disagree about the facts of the case.

Mission 4

Boo, hurrah, and not subjectivism

  • Critics nicknamed emotivism the "boo-hurrah" theory: "Stealing is wrong" ≈ "Stealing: boo!"; "Kindness is good" ≈ "Kindness: hurrah!"
  • Emotivism is not subjectivism:
  • Subjectivism: "Stealing is wrong" reports a feeling: it means "I disapprove of stealing", which is true or false (am I really disapproving?).
  • Emotivism: it expresses the feeling, as a groan does. It reports nothing, so it is neither true nor false.
  • Ayer: on subjectivism, "X is wrong" and "I disapprove of X" would mean the same, but someone can disapprove of X and deny that X is wrong. So subjectivism fails; emotivism does not face this.
  • Emotivism explains disagreement: people's attitudes really differ.

Mission 4

Stevenson: functional and persuasive language

  • C. L. Stevenson (1908–1979), "The Emotive Meaning of Ethical Terms" (1937) and Ethics and Language (1944): a development of emotivism.
  • Ethical words have emotive meaning: they express attitudes and influence others' attitudes. Language here is functional: it does a job.
  • Two kinds of disagreement: in belief (about facts) and in attitude (what we are for or against). Moral disputes involve both.
  • Persuasive definitions: redefining a word with strong emotive force (e.g. "true freedom", "real courage") to shift attitudes.
  • So moral argument is not pointless: giving reasons, especially facts, can change attitudes.
Mission 4

Hare: prescriptivism

  • R. M. Hare (1919–2002), The Language of Morals (1952), Freedom and Reason (1963).
  • Moral statements are prescriptions: they guide action, like commands. "Stealing is wrong" works like "Don't steal".
  • They are universalisable: if I say I ought to do X here, I commit myself to saying anyone in a relevantly similar situation ought to do X.
  • So moral language is non-cognitive but rational: we can test our prescriptions for consistency.
  • Difference from emotivism: not expressing feelings, but prescribing action that the speaker must apply to everyone, including herself.
  • Criticism: a consistent fanatic can universalise a terrible prescription and accept it even for himself.
Quick check

Mission 4

Emotivism and relativism

  • If moral statements only express attitudes, there is no objective standard to judge whose attitude is correct.
  • So emotivism fits a relativist picture: moral views vary with persons and cultures, and none is "the" truth.
  • But emotivism is not the same as cultural relativism: a relativist says "slavery is wrong" is true in some cultures. An emotivist says it is not true or false anywhere.
  • Emotivism cannot say tolerance is objectively right either: "We ought to tolerate other views" is also an expressed attitude.
  • Spec link: ethical assertions as absolutist or relative (absolutism in Mission 2).
Mission 4

Mackie: error theory

  • J. L. Mackie (1917–1981), Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong (1977), ch. 1, opens: "There are no objective values."
  • Moral statements aim to state objective facts (so Mackie is a cognitivist), but there are none, so they are all false: an error theory (anti-realist).
  • Argument from relativity: moral codes vary widely; this is better explained by different ways of life than by everyone perceiving objective values badly.
  • Argument from queerness: objective values would be strange entities, and knowing them would need a strange faculty (see Mission 3).
  • So we invent right and wrong. Mackie still thought morality useful and worth building.
Mission 4

Challenges and replies

  • No basic moral principles can be established: nothing is really right or wrong.
  • Ethical debate becomes pointless: if facts are agreed, all that is left is a clash of feelings.
  • No universal agreement that some actions are wrong: emotivism cannot say genocide is wrong for everyone, only that people express disapproval.
  • The verification principle fails its own test: it is neither analytic nor verifiable.
  • Moral progress (e.g. the abolition of slavery) seems to be more than a change of feelings.
  • Embedding problem (Peter Geach, 1916–2013): in "If stealing is wrong, getting your brother to steal is wrong", "stealing is wrong" expresses no feeling, yet the argument is valid. Reply: quasi-realism (Simon Blackburn, b. 1944, Spreading the Word, 1984).
  • Replies: most moral arguments are about facts, which can be settled (Ayer); attitudes can be changed by reasons (Stevenson).
Quick check
?Two students agree on every fact about animal testing: what is done, how much animals suffer, what medical benefits follow. They still disagree about whether it is wrong. What does Ayer's emotivism say is happening?
Mission 4Evaluate

Objective, subjective or meaningless?

  • Objective factual basis: naturalism and intuitionism: moral claims are true or false, made true by facts.
  • Only in the mind of the user: subjectivism (reports of feeling); emotivism (expressions of feeling).
  • Meaningless? Ayer: no factual meaning, but they have emotive meaning and a use.
  • Meaningless? Hare: no, they have prescriptive meaning.
  • Meaningful but false: Mackie's error theory.
  • For emotivism: explains deep moral disagreement; fits a scientific view of facts; explains why moral words move us to act.
  • Against: debate seems real; some acts seem wrong whatever anyone feels; the verification principle undermines itself.
  • Judgement criteria: Does the theory fit how people actually argue? Can it explain moral progress? Does it rest on a sound theory of meaning?
Mission check

?A historian says: "The abolition of the slave trade was moral progress." Which reply could an emotivist like Ayer consistently give?

For Ayer, moral words do things rather than describe things. The debate is whether that can do justice to moral reasoning and moral progress.

Mission 5

Three ways morality might depend on God

  • Dependence: morality depends on religion. Three versions:
  • Existence: there is no right or wrong without God (divine command theory).
  • Knowledge: we cannot know right and wrong reliably without revelation.
  • Motivation: without God and final justice, people have no strong reason to be moral.
  • Independence: morality stands on its own: reason, human nature, sympathy or social agreement are enough.
  • Many believers take a middle view: God is the ultimate source of goodness, but people can know much of it by reason and conscience (natural law).
  • Keep the three versions apart: a critic may reject one and accept another.
Mission 5

Autonomy, heteronomy, theonomy

  • Immanuel Kant (1724–1804), Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785).
  • Autonomy ("self-law"): the rational will gives the moral law to itself. Only this gives an act moral worth.
  • Heteronomy ("other-law"): the will is moved by something outside reason: desire, fear, reward, or a command obeyed because it is commanded.
  • Kant still links duty and religion: in Religion within the Bounds of Bare Reason (1793) religion is recognising our duties as divine commands.
  • Paul Tillich (1886–1965), Systematic Theology, vol. 1 (1951): theonomy ("God-law") is neither. God is the ground of our being, so God's law is the deepest law of our own nature, not an alien rule.
  • Tillich: heteronomy breeds rebellion; autonomy alone grows empty; theonomy unites them.

> "… when Gentiles who don't have the law do by nature the things of the law, these, not having the law, are a law to themselves, in that they show the work of the law written in their hearts, their conscience testifying with them …" (Romans 2:14–15, WEBBE)

  • Paul: people without the Jewish law still know much of what it requires.

Mission 5

Dawkins: good without God

  • Richard Dawkins (b. 1941), The God Delusion (2006), chapters 6–7.
  • Our moral sense has Darwinian roots: kin altruism, reciprocal altruism, reputation, and generosity as a signal. These shaped "rules of thumb" that now misfire usefully, as kindness to strangers.
  • The point of morality for the atheist: being good only for reward or from fear of God is not real morality. Someone who would be cruel if God did not exist reveals a poor moral character (paraphrase).
  • We do not really take morals from scripture: believers pick and choose texts, guided by a changing moral Zeitgeist (spirit of the age).
  • Responses: explaining where moral feelings came from does not show whether they are true or binding; many theists accept evolution as God's means; Alister McGrath (b. 1953) and Joanna Collicutt McGrath, The Dawkins Delusion? (2007), argue Dawkins misreads religion.
Mission 5

Rachels: pointless without religion, or opposed to it?

  • James Rachels (1941–2003).
  • "Morality is pointless without religion": the claim that without God there is no final justice and no reason to be good. In The Elements of Moral Philosophy (1986, ch. 4) Rachels examines this and rejects it: morality has its own reasons: the interests of the people our acts affect.
  • "Morality is opposed to religion": "God and Human Attitudes", Religious Studies (1971):
  • (1) If any being is God, it must be worthy of worship.
  • (2) Worship means total submission: giving up your role as an autonomous moral agent.
  • (3) A moral agent can never give that up. So no being could be worthy of worship; so there is no God (paraphrase).
  • Replies: worship of a perfectly good God need not mean giving up judgement: trusting a wiser being can itself be rational; theonomy denies that God's law is alien.
Mission 5

Sharpe: the moral case against religion

  • R. A. Sharpe, philosopher (University of Wales, Lampeter), The Moral Case against Religious Belief (1997).
  • Morality is autonomous: it needs no religious foundation.
  • Religious leaders are authorities on religion, not on morality; religious commitments often mislead them on moral questions.
  • Doctrine can override compassion: zeal leads believers to suppress pity, as when the burning of heretics (Huguenot families) was accepted as just (his historical example).
  • Acting for heavenly reward is self-interest, not morality.
  • Some religious demands (e.g. self-punishment, rules on sexual life) have no clear moral basis.
  • Responses: many religious reformers (abolitionists, civil rights leaders) acted from faith; abuses show misuse, not the nature of faith; secular ideologies have also overridden compassion.

Mission 5

Does religion make people behave better?

  • Reasons given for a link: teaching (commandments, parables, the Five Pillars, the Eightfold Path); community and example; accountability before God; practice (prayer, confession) that builds character; organised charity (hospitals, schools, food banks).
  • "By their fruits you will know them." (Matthew 7:20, WEBBE). "Faith, if it has no works, is dead" (James 2:17, WEBBE).
  • Reasons given against: many non-religious people are just as moral; abuse scandals and religious violence; hypocrisy; Sharpe and Dawkins: doctrine can override compassion.
  • Evidence is contested: some studies link generosity to belonging to a religious community more than to belief itself; results vary by country and by how behaviour is measured.
  • A philosophical point: even if believers behaved better, that would not settle whether morality depends on God.
Mission 5

Contemporary focuses

  • Religion and terror: some acts of terror are claimed in the name of religion. Dawkins: faith held without evidence can make people certain enough to kill, and moderate faith gives it cover. Replies: Karen Armstrong, Fields of Blood (2014): most such violence is driven chiefly by politics; Mark Juergensmeyer, Terror in the Mind of God (2000): religion can intensify conflicts by framing them as cosmic war. Many religious leaders condemn terror in religious terms (e.g. the Amman Message, 2004).
  • Conservative movements: groups that seek to keep traditional religious morality in law and public life (e.g. on abortion, marriage, education), such as the Christian Right in the USA. Supporters: conscience, family, objective morality. Critics (Sharpe): authority replaces moral reasoning.
  • Biblical parenting: "One who spares the rod hates his son" (Proverbs 13:24, WEBBE) and "don't provoke your children to wrath" (Ephesians 6:4, WEBBE). Some Christians defend loving physical discipline; many read "rod" as guidance and reject smacking. Scotland (2020) and Wales (2022) have banned physical punishment of children. Dawkins (ch. 9) objects to labelling children by their parents' religion.

Mission 5Evaluate

Does morality depend on religion?

  • Claim: Morality depends on religion.
  • For: objective moral truth needs a source beyond human minds (see Mission 1); final justice gives morality a point; scripture and tradition guide and motivate; theonomy shows religion can deepen, not deny, moral agency.
  • Against: the Euthyphro dilemma (see Mission 1); people know right and wrong without revelation (even Romans 2:14–15 says so); Dawkins: evolved moral sense; Rachels: worship conflicts with autonomy.
  • Against: Sharpe: religious authority can corrupt moral judgement; terror and harsh parenting justified by texts.
  • Judgement criteria: Which kind of dependence (existence, knowledge, motivation)? Are the abuses religion's nature or its misuse? Can secular ethics ground objective duties?

Mission 6

What a moral argument claims

  • Starts from morality: real moral obligations, or our sense of being bound by duty.
  • Concludes that God exists, or that belief in God is required.
  • Explanation type: objective goodness or obligation needs a source; God is the best one (Aquinas' Fourth Way is related).
  • Practical type: the moral life only makes sense if God exists (Kant).
  • Neither type claims to prove God from the definition of God (contrast the ontological argument).
Mission 6

Kant: duty and the highest good

  • Immanuel Kant (1724–1804), Critique of Practical Reason (1788).
  • Morality comes from reason, not from God's commands: the categorical imperative. Kant is not arguing that we need God to know what is right.
  • Reason commands us to aim at the highest good (summum bonum): perfect virtue, rewarded with happiness in proportion to it.
  • Ought implies can: if we must aim at it, it must be possible.
  • But in this life virtue and happiness often do not match. The good suffer; the wicked prosper.
Mission 6

The three postulates

A postulate is something reason must assume for the moral law to make sense, though it cannot be proved:

  • Freedom: to be bound by duty, we must be able to choose to obey it.
  • Immortality: perfect virtue (holiness) needs endless progress, so the soul must continue beyond death.
  • God: only a being who is all-good and all-powerful can match happiness to virtue.
  • Kant, Abbott translation (1873): "it is morally necessary to assume the existence of God."
  • Postulates are practical faith, not knowledge: Kant rejected the theoretical proofs of God.

Mission 6

Freud: conscience is the internal parent

  • Sigmund Freud (1856–1939), The Ego and the Id (1923); The Future of an Illusion (1927).
  • The superego forms in childhood as we take in the commands and prohibitions of our parents and society.
  • Conscience and guilt are the superego at work: they feel absolute, like a voice from beyond, because they carry a parent's authority.
  • Belief in God projects that parent onto the universe.
  • So the moral sense can be explained without God. The moral argument mistakes a psychological origin for a divine one.
  • Responses: Freud's evidence base is narrow and hard to test; a psychological origin would not show a belief false.
Mission 6

Russell: a dilemma and an upbringing

  • Bertrand Russell (1872–1970), Why I Am Not a Christian (lecture, 1927).
  • The dilemma (paraphrase): if right and wrong exist only because God decrees them, then for God there is no difference between them, and "God is good" says nothing. If they exist independently, they do not come from God (compare the Euthyphro dilemma).
  • On Kant (paraphrase): Kant's moral convictions were those he had learned in childhood; the argument dresses up an upbringing.
  • So moral experience is better explained by human sources.
  • Responses: many theists say goodness is God's nature, so neither arbitrary nor independent; and where a belief came from does not settle whether it is true.
Mission 6

Morality as social conditioning

  • Moral codes are learned: from family, religion, school, law and culture.
  • They vary across societies and change over time, which fits a human origin.
  • Evolutionary accounts: cooperation, care for kin and fairness helped groups survive; Richard Dawkins, The God Delusion (2006), explains our moral sense this way (paraphrase).
  • So the sense of obligation needs no divine source.
  • Responses: a shared moral core (e.g. against cruelty for its own sake) appears across cultures; reformers have resisted their own conditioning (e.g. opponents of slavery); explaining how we learned a belief does not show it false (genetic fallacy).
Mission 6

Moral arguments against God

  • James Rachels (1941–2003), "God and Human Attitudes" (1971): worship demands total submission; a moral agent cannot give up their own moral judgement; so no being could be worthy of worship, so God, so defined, does not exist (paraphrase).
  • R. A. Sharpe, The Moral Case Against Religious Belief (1997): religious commitment can override ordinary compassion, and helping others for heavenly reward is closer to self-interest than to morality (paraphrase).
  • Richard Dawkins (The God Delusion, 2006): we do not need God to be good; some scriptural commands are themselves morally troubling.
  • The problem of evil is also a moral argument against God.
  • Theists reply: worship is freely given to the source of goodness, not blind obedience.

Does moral experience point to God?

  • Claim: Our experience of moral obligation points to God.
  • For: Kant: duty commands the highest good; only God makes it possible.
  • Against: Freud and conditioning: obligation has human sources; Russell's dilemma.
  • Against: the highest good may be a hope, not something duty requires to be achieved: we must only aim at it.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • DCT: right is what God commands, wrong is what God forbids; morality is objective and rooted in God's will.
  • The Euthyphro dilemma: if God commands it because it is good, goodness is independent of God; if it is good because God commands it, morality looks arbitrary. Pluralism adds: which commands are God's?

Mission 2

  • Cognitivism (true or false) vs non-cognitivism; realism (mind-independent facts) vs anti-realism.
  • Naturalism defines "good" by a natural property, e.g. pleasure (Bentham, Mill).
  • Challenges: Hume's is–ought gap; Moore's naturalistic fallacy and open question argument.

Mission 3

  • Non-naturalism: "good" is real and objective but not a natural property; Moore: good is simple and indefinable, like yellow.
  • Intuitionism: basic moral truths are self-evident to a mature, reflective mind; intuition is not infallible.
  • Prichard: "ought" is indefinable and known immediately by moral thinking. Challenges: no proof, differing intuitions, no way to resolve conflicts, queerness.

Mission 4

  • Logical positivism: meaningful statements are analytic or verifiable; Ayer: moral statements are neither, so they express feelings and aim to persuade (non-cognitivism).
  • Emotivism is not subjectivism: it expresses feeling ("boo!"), it does not report it; challenges: no basic principles, pointless debate, no universal wrongs.
  • Developments: Stevenson (persuasive, attitude), Hare (universalisable prescriptions).
  • Relativism; Mackie's error theory: moral claims aim at facts but are all false.

Mission 5

  • Dependence (existence, knowledge, motivation) vs independence; Kant's autonomy vs heteronomy; Tillich's theonomy unites them.
  • Challenges: Dawkins (evolved morality; good without God), Rachels (worship vs autonomy).
  • Sharpe (religious authority can override compassion); contemporary focuses: terror, conservative movements, biblical parenting.

Mission 6

  • Kant: the moral law comes from reason, but the highest good (virtue with matching happiness) is possible only if we postulate freedom, immortality and God.
  • Critics: Freud (conscience as superego), Russell (dilemma; upbringing), social conditioning.
  • Rachels and Sharpe argue from morality against God.
