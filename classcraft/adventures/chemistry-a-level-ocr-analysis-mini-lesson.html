Mission 1

Mission 1: Why do the dyes in one drop of ink travel different distances up the same strip of paper?

Make a guess: Why do the dyes in one drop of ink travel different distances up the same strip of paper?

Mission 1

Two phases

  • Chromatography separates the components of a mixture between two phases.
  • Mobile phase: moves (a liquid solvent, or a gas).
  • Stationary phase: stays put (paper, a coated plate, a packed column).
  • Each component keeps switching: dissolved in the mobile phase ↔ held by the stationary phase.
  • More time in the mobile phase → it moves further (or comes out sooner).
Mission 1

Running a TLC or paper chromatogram

  • Draw the origin line in pencil (ink would itself separate).
  • Spot the mixture and reference samples on the line; let the spots dry.
  • Stand the plate in solvent below the origin (or the spots dissolve away into the solvent).
  • Cover the tank: the air inside stays saturated with solvent vapour.
  • Remove before the solvent reaches the top; mark the solvent front at once.
  • Colourless spots: view under UV light (plates with a fluorescent coating), or use iodine vapour or a locating spray.
  • Safety: solvents are often flammable and harmful: no flames, use a fume cupboard; never look into the UV lamp.
Mission 1

Why some components move less

  • Silica (TLC) and paper both have many polar –OH groups on their surface (paper: cellulose, with water held on it).
  • Polar components are held strongly (dipole–dipole forces, hydrogen bonds): they move less.
  • Less polar components spend more time in a less polar solvent: they move further.
  • So the order of spots tells you the relative polarity of the components.
  • Mass is not the main cause: a small polar molecule can stay behind a large non-polar one.
Quick check
?The dyes in a black ink are separated by paper chromatography. Which difference between the dyes is mainly responsible for the separation?
Mission 1

Rf values

  • Rf = distance moved by the spot ÷ distance moved by the solvent front, both from the origin.
  • Measure to the centre of the spot.
  • Rf has no units and lies between 0 and 1.
  • Example: spot 2.9 cm, solvent front 7.6 cm: Rf = 2.9 ÷ 7.6 = 0.38.
  • Rf depends on the solvent, the plate and the temperature: compare with standards run on the same plate.
Quick check
?On a TLC plate, the solvent front is 6.4 cm above the origin line, which is 1.0 cm above the bottom edge of the plate. The centre of spot S is 4.8 cm above the origin; its top edge is 5.1 cm above the origin. What is the Rf value of S?
Mission 1

Chromatography in a column

  • Gas chromatography (GC): an unreactive carrier gas (the mobile phase) pushes the vaporised sample through a long, heated column.
  • Stationary phase: a solid, or (usually) a high-boiling liquid coated on a solid support or on the column wall.
  • Retention time: time from injecting the sample to the component reaching the detector.
Mission 1

Reading a gas chromatogram

  • Each peak = one component. Same retention time as a standard (same conditions) → likely the same substance.
  • Retention time depends on column temperature, gas flow rate and the stationary phase: keep them the same.
  • Peak area is proportional to the amount of that component.
  • % of a component = its peak area ÷ total peak area × 100 (assuming the detector responds equally to each).
  • Example: areas 1.8, 5.4, 10.8 → total 18.0 → 10%, 30%, 60%.
  • External calibration curve: run standards of known concentration, plot peak area against concentration, then read the unknown's concentration from its peak area.
Quick check
?A gas chromatogram of a mixture of three esters, P, Q and R, gives peak areas P 2.4, Q 3.6 and R 6.0 (arbitrary units). Assume the detector responds equally to each ester. What is the percentage of Q in the mixture?
Mission check

?A student runs TLC of a mixture of two polar compounds on a silica plate, using hexane as the solvent. Both spots barely leave the origin. Which change is most likely to make the spots move further up the plate and separate?

Every component shares its time between the two phases. Change that balance, and you change how far it goes.

Mission 2

Mission 2: An MRI scanner images your body by detecting hydrogen nuclei. What can the same effect tell a chemist about a molecule?

Make a guess: An MRI scanner images your body by detecting hydrogen nuclei. What can the same effect tell a chemist about a molecule?

Mission 2

What ¹H NMR shows

  • In a strong magnetic field, ¹H nuclei absorb radio waves.
  • H atoms in different environments absorb at different frequencies: one signal per environment.
  • Position: chemical shift, δ in ppm, measured from TMS at δ = 0.
  • δ depends on what is nearby: an O atom or a C=O moves a signal to higher δ.
  • The area under a signal (shown by the integration trace) is proportional to the number of H in that environment.
Mission 2

TMS and solvents

  • TMS, tetramethylsilane, Si(CH3)4, is added as the standard: δ = 0 by definition.
  • Its 12 H are all equivalent: one strong, sharp signal.
  • That signal lies upfield of almost all organic H, so it doesn't overlap them.
  • It is inert, non-toxic and volatile (boils near 27 °C), so it is easily removed.
  • The sample is dissolved in a solvent with no ¹H: a deuterated solvent such as CDCl3.
  • Solvent ¹H would give a large signal that hides the sample's signals.
Mission 2

Counting environments

  • H atoms are equivalent if symmetry or free rotation makes them interchangeable. Equivalent H share one signal.
  • All three H on one CH3 group are equivalent.
  • 1,2-Dichloroethane, ClCH2CH2Cl: one signal (4H).
  • 2-Methylpropane, (CH3)3CH: two signals, areas 9 : 1.
Quick check
?Which describes the ¹H NMR spectrum of 2-methylpropan-2-ol, (CH3)3COH?
Mission 2

Worked example: ethanol, low resolution

  • CH3CH2OH has three H environments: three signals, areas 3 : 2 : 1.
  • δ 3.7, 2H: the CH2, on the carbon bonded to O (high δ).
  • δ 2.6, 1H: the O–H (its position varies with concentration and solvent).
  • δ 1.2, 3H: the CH3 (an alkyl CH3, nudged up slightly by the O two carbons away).
Quick check
?How many signals does the low-resolution ¹H NMR spectrum of 3-methylbutan-1-ol, (CH3)2CHCH2CH2OH, show, and in what ratio of areas?
Calculate
?A compound, C5H12O, gives three signals in its ¹H NMR spectrum. Their integration traces rise by 4.5 cm, 1.0 cm and 0.5 cm. How many H atoms are in the environment of the 4.5 cm signal?
Mission 2

Splitting: the n + 1 rule

  • High resolution: each signal can split into several close peaks.
  • A signal is split by H atoms on adjacent carbons: n neighbouring H → n + 1 peaks.
  • 0 → singlet, 1 → doublet, 2 → triplet, 3 → quartet.
  • Equivalent H don't split each other. An O–H usually gives a singlet and doesn't split its neighbours.
  • Splitting counts the neighbours' H; the group's own H count comes from the area.
Mission 2

Worked example: ethanol, high resolution

  • δ 1.2, 3H, triplet: CH3 next to a CH2 (2 neighbours → 3 peaks).
  • δ 3.7, 2H, quartet: CH2 next to a CH3 (3 neighbours → 4 peaks).
  • δ 2.6, 1H, singlet: O–H.
  • A 3H triplet with a 2H quartet is the sign of an ethyl group, CH3CH2–.
Quick check
?In a high-resolution ¹H NMR spectrum, one signal is split into a quartet. What does this show about the structure?
Mission 2

Finding O–H and N–H with D2O

  • O–H and N–H signals are hard to pin down: their δ varies.
  • Run the spectrum, then shake the sample with D2O and run it again.
  • O–H and N–H protons exchange with deuterium: –OH + D2O ⇌ –OD + HDO.
  • D gives no ¹H signal, so the O–H or N–H signal disappears.
  • The signal that vanished was the O–H or N–H.
Quick check
?In the high-resolution ¹H NMR spectrum of 3-methylbutan-1-ol, (CH3)2CHCH2CH2OH, what splitting does the 6H signal at δ 0.9 show?
Mission check

?Which describes the high-resolution ¹H NMR spectrum of methyl propanoate, CH3CH2COOCH3?

¹H NMR shows how many kinds of H (signals), how many of each (areas), what is nearby (shift) and how many H next door (splitting).

Mission 3

Mission 3: Only about one carbon atom in a hundred is magnetic. How can that be enough to map every carbon in a compound?

Make a guess: Only about one carbon atom in a hundred is magnetic. How can that be enough to map every carbon in a compound?

Mission 3

Why ¹³C NMR works

  • Only about 1.1% of carbon atoms are ¹³C. A ¹³C nucleus behaves like a tiny magnet, so it gives an NMR signal; ¹²C is not magnetic and gives none.
  • A sample has billions of molecules, so every carbon position holds a ¹³C atom in some of them.
  • Many scans are added together to build up the weak signal.
  • One peak per carbon environment. δ in ppm from TMS (δ = 0), range about 0–220.
  • Spectra are proton decoupled: every peak is a single line. No splitting to read.
  • Peak heights are not used to count carbons.
  • So ¹³C spectra are simpler than ¹H spectra: count the peaks, read their positions.
Mission 3

Counting carbon environments

  • Carbons are equivalent if symmetry makes them interchangeable. They share one peak.
  • Propan-1-ol, CH3CH2CH2OH: 3 peaks.
  • Propan-2-ol, (CH3)2CHOH: the two CH3 are equivalent: 2 peaks.
  • Propanone, CH3COCH3: 2 peaks (CH3 and C=O).
  • Benzene: all six carbons equivalent: 1 peak.
Quick check
?Which alcohol, C4H10O, gives only two peaks in its ¹³C NMR spectrum?

  • In exams your board's data sheet gives the ranges. Use those.
Mission 3

Worked example: ethyl ethanoate

  • CH3COOCH2CH3: four different carbons → 4 peaks.
  • δ 171: C=O of an ester (160–185).
  • δ 60: the CH2 bonded to O (50–90).
  • δ 21: the CH3 bonded to C=O (20–50).
  • δ 14: the CH3 of the ethyl group (5–40).
Quick check
?Compound R, C3H6O, gives two peaks in its ¹³C NMR spectrum, at δ 206 and δ 31. What is R?
Mission 3

Predicting a spectrum

  • Draw the full structure. Label each carbon.
  • Group equivalent carbons: number of peaks = number of environments.
  • For each environment, look up its δ range.
  • Methyl propanoate, CH3CH2COOCH3: 4 peaks.
  • C=O ester 160–185 · O–CH3 50–90 · CH2 next to C=O 20–50 · CH3 5–40.
Calculate
?How many peaks are in the ¹³C NMR spectrum of 1,4-dimethylbenzene?
Mission 3

¹³C and ¹H together

  • ¹³C: how many kinds of carbon, and what each is bonded to.
  • ¹H: how many kinds of H, and how many of each (areas).
  • Together they narrow the structure fast.
  • Propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol: ¹³C gives 3 v 2 peaks; ¹H gives 4 v 3 signals (2 : 2 : 3 : 1 v 6 : 1 : 1).
Quick check
?Compound T, C3H8O, gives two peaks in its ¹³C NMR spectrum. Its low-resolution ¹H NMR spectrum has three signals with areas 6 : 1 : 1. What is T?
Mission check

?A hydrocarbon, C5H12, gives four peaks in its ¹³C NMR spectrum. Which isomer is it?

Only 1 in 100 carbons is ¹³C, but billions of molecules mean every position shows up. One peak per environment maps the carbon skeleton.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Components separate because each is attracted differently to the stationary phase and the mobile phase.
  • Rf = spot distance ÷ solvent-front distance; retention time identifies GC peaks; compare with standards under the same conditions.

Mission 2

  • Signals = H environments; area = relative number of H; δ = what is nearby; n neighbouring H → n + 1 peaks.
  • TMS at δ = 0: 12 equivalent H, one sharp signal, inert and volatile. Solvent has no ¹H (CDCl3).
  • Shake with D2O: the O–H or N–H signal disappears.

Mission 3

  • ¹³C NMR: one peak per carbon environment; equivalent carbons share a peak.
  • δ shows what each carbon is bonded to: C=O highest (160–220), C–O 50–90, alkyl lowest.
  • No splitting (proton decoupled), and peak heights don't count carbons.
