Mission 1: If every price in a country goes up at once, why would people buy less of the country's output?

Make a guess: If every price in a country goes up at once, why would people buy less of the country's output?

Mission 1

Aggregate demand

  • Aggregate demand (AD): total planned spending on a country's goods and services at each price level, in a given time period.
  • AD = C + I + G + (X − M).
  • AD = C + I + G + NX, where NX = net exports (X − M).
  • Who spends: households (C), firms (I), government (G), the rest of the world (X), minus spending that goes abroad (M).
  • Axes: "Price level (PL)" and "Real GDP (Y)".
  • The AD curve slopes down: at a lower price level, more real output is demanded.
  • Say: "price level" — not: "price", on a macro diagram.
Mission 1

The four components

  • Consumption (C): household spending on goods and services.
  • Investment (I): firms' spending on capital goods: machines, buildings, equipment. (Plus new housing.)
  • Government spending (G): on goods and services, e.g. teachers' pay, roads. Transfer payments (pensions, benefits) are not in G: they are spent later as C.
  • Net exports (X − M): foreigners' spending on our exports minus our spending on imports.
  • Saving is income not spent. Investment is spending on new capital. Buying shares or putting money in a bank is saving, not investment.
  • C is by far the largest component. Real data: consumer spending was 67.5% of US GDP in 2023. It is also well over half of GDP in the UK.
  • Investment is smaller but swings far more over the business cycle.
Quick check
?Which of these is counted as investment (I) in aggregate demand?
Mission 1

Why the AD curve slopes down

  • AD is not a market demand curve. When the price level rises, there are no cheaper home-produced goods to switch to.
  • Instead, a higher price level cuts real output demanded through three effects:
  • Real wealth effect: money and savings buy less, so households feel poorer → C falls.
  • Interest rate effect: buying the same goods needs more money → demand for money rises → interest rates rise → borrowing costs more → C and I fall.
  • Exchange rate effect: higher interest rates attract foreign funds → the currency appreciates → exports more expensive, imports cheaper → NX falls.
  • A lower price level works the other way: real output demanded rises.
Mission 1

Movement along AD or shift of AD?

  • A change in the price level → a movement along AD.
  • Price level up: a decrease in the quantity of real GDP demanded.
  • A change in anything else that changes C, I, G or X − M → a shift of AD.
  • More spending at every price level: AD shifts right (AD1 → AD2). Less: AD shifts left.
  • The three effects of the previous screen explain the slope. They are never reasons for a shift.
  • Say: "a rise in the price level causes a contraction along AD" — not: "a rise in the price level shifts AD left".
Quick check
?A rise in world oil prices pushes up the general price level in a country. Nothing else that affects spending changes. How should this be shown on the country's AD diagram?
Mission 1

What shifts C and I

Consumption (C) rises, shifting AD right, when:

  • Disposable income rises (e.g. income taxes are cut).
  • Interest rates fall: borrowing is cheaper, saving earns less.
  • Wealth rises (house or share prices go up).
  • Consumer confidence improves.
  • Household debt is lower, or credit is easier to get.
  • People expect prices to rise, so they buy now.

Investment (I) rises, shifting AD right, when:

  • Interest rates fall: projects cost less to finance.
  • Business confidence improves: firms expect more sales and profit.
  • New technology makes new equipment worth buying.
  • Business taxes (e.g. on profits) are cut.
  • Corporate debt is lower, so firms can borrow more easily.

The reverse of each shifts AD left.

Mission 1

What shifts G and net exports

  • G: set by government priorities and fiscal policy, e.g. more spending on health or roads → AD right.
  • Exports (X) rise when:
    • Incomes abroad rise (trading partners grow; the world economy booms).
    • The currency depreciates: exports become cheaper abroad.
    • Other countries cut trade barriers (e.g. remove tariffs).
    • Non-price factors improve: quality, design, reliability, reputation.
  • Imports (M) rise, cutting X − M, when:
    • Real income at home rises: people buy more of everything, including imports.
    • The currency appreciates: imports become cheaper at home.
    • The country removes its own tariffs or quotas.
  • Say: "a depreciation makes exports cheaper abroad and imports more expensive at home" — not the other way round.

Mission 2

Spend it or save it

  • Disposable income (Yd): income after direct taxes, plus state benefits (transfers).
  • Households do two things with it: consume or save.
  • Consumption (C): household spending on goods and services.
  • Saving (S): disposable income not spent. Yd = C + S.
  • C is the largest part of AD = C + I + G + (X − M).
  • Saving is a withdrawal (leakage) from the circular flow.
  • AP: consumption spending is the largest component of GDP; saving is a leakage.
Mission 2

Average and marginal propensities

  • Marginal propensity to consume (MPC) = ΔC ÷ ΔYd. The share of extra income spent.
  • Marginal propensity to save (MPS) = ΔS ÷ ΔYd. The share of extra income saved.
  • Every extra unit of disposable income is spent or saved, so MPC + MPS = 1.
  • Say: "MPC is the change in consumption over the change in income" — not: "consumption over income". That is an average, not a marginal, share.

Mission 2

The consumption function

  • Consumption function: C = a + bYd. Illustrative: C = 40 + 0.75Yd.
  • Autonomous consumption (a = 40): spent even at zero income, from past saving or borrowing.
  • Induced consumption (bYd): rises with income. The slope b is the MPC (0.75).
  • Step 2: the 45° line, where C = Yd. The function crosses it at Yd = 160: the break-even income, where S = 0.
  • Below 160, C > Yd: dissaving. Above 160, the gap below the 45° line is saving.
  • A change in Yd is a movement along the function. Any other influence shifts it.
  • AP: the slope of the consumption function is the MPC.

Mission 2

What else moves spending

  • Interest rates: a rise makes borrowing more expensive (cars, furniture, mortgages) and saving more rewarding. C usually falls.
  • Confidence and expectations: fear of job loss or lower future income → more precautionary saving.
  • Wealth effects: rising house or share prices make owners feel richer and able to borrow → C rises.
  • Taxes and benefits: change disposable income.
  • Household debt and credit: heavily indebted households, or banks that stop lending, cut C.
  • Step 2: C rises with the price level unchanged → AD shifts right, AD1 → AD2.
  • Say: "a rise in consumer confidence shifts AD to the right" — not: "a movement along AD".
Mission 2

Real world: the 2020 saving surge

  • In spring 2020, lockdowns closed shops and jobs felt at risk.
  • UK: the household saving ratio rose from 9.6% (January to March) to a record 29.1% (April to June 2020).
  • US: the personal saving rate hit 33% in April 2020, up from 12.7% in March.
  • These are the first official estimates (ONS, BEA). Later revisions moved them a little; the surge stayed.
  • Two reasons:
  • Forced saving: little to spend on.
  • Precautionary saving: fear about jobs and health.
  • Most incomes were protected by furlough and support schemes, yet C fell sharply.
Quick check
?House prices fall sharply across a country. Incomes and interest rates do not change. What is the most likely effect on consumption?
Mission 2

Saving is not investment

  • Saving: income not spent by households. A withdrawal.
  • Investment: spending by firms on new capital goods (machines, buildings). An injection.
  • Buying shares or putting money in a bank is saving, not investment.
  • Saving and investment are done by different people, for different reasons.
  • Banks may lend savings to firms, but planned saving need not equal planned investment.
  • Say: "investment is spending on capital goods" — not: "investing in shares".
Quick check
?In economics, which of these counts as investment?
Mission check

?Share prices rise strongly over a year (illustrative). Households' disposable incomes and interest rates do not change, and consumer confidence improves. What happens?

Spending depends on income, but also on wealth, confidence, interest rates and credit. Any of them can move C even when pay is unchanged.

Mission 3

One injection, many rounds

  • The government spends 100 (million) on a new bridge. An injection.
  • Builders earn 100. They spend some of it and withdraw the rest (saving, tax, imports).
  • Illustrative data: 80% of each extra unit of income is spent on domestic output.
  • Round 1: 100 → round 2: 80 → round 3: 64 → round 4: 51.2 → …
  • Each person's spending is someone else's income.
  • The rounds add up to 500: national income rises by 5 times the injection.
  • Multiplier effect: a change in an injection causes a larger final change in national income.
  • It works in reverse too: a cut in an injection reduces income by more than the cut.
  • AP: a change in autonomous spending changes real GDP by a multiple of that change.
Mission 3Graph lab

Re-spend more

  • Takeaway: the more of each round is re-spent, the bigger the multiplier.
Mission 3

The multiplier formula

  • Marginal propensity to consume (MPC): the share of each extra unit of income that is spent. MPC = ΔC ÷ ΔY. On the first screen of this mission it was 0.8.
  • Marginal propensity to save (MPS): the share of extra income saved.
  • AP: MPC = ΔC ÷ ΔYd (disposable income). MPC + MPS = 1.
  • Multiplier (k) = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC).
  • Closed economy, no government: 1 − MPC = MPS, so k = 1 ÷ MPS.
  • Final change in national income = k × change in injection.
  • Example: MPC = 0.8. k = 1 ÷ 0.2 = 5. A rise of 100 in investment → national income rises by 500.
  • AP: spending multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC) = 1 ÷ MPS. Change in real GDP = multiplier × change in autonomous spending (C, I, G or net exports).
  • Say: "real national income rises by k times the injection" — not: "the injection is multiplied at once". It takes many rounds.
Quick check
?In a closed economy with no government, the MPC is 0.75. Investment rises by 40 billion. If there is spare capacity, by how much does national income finally rise?
Mission 3

The tax multiplier

  • A tax cut raises disposable income, but households save part of it before spending.
  • So the first round of spending is MPC × the tax cut.
  • Tax multiplier = −MPC ÷ MPS.
  • Example: MPC = 0.8, MPS = 0.2.
  • Spending multiplier = 1 ÷ 0.2 = 5. Tax multiplier = −0.8 ÷ 0.2 = −4.
  • Government spending +$100 billion → real GDP +$500 billion.
  • Taxes −$100 billion → real GDP +$400 billion.
  • So the spending multiplier is larger (in absolute value) than the tax multiplier.
  • Transfer payments work like a tax cut: multiplier MPC ÷ MPS.
  • Balanced budget multiplier = 1: raise G and taxes by $100 billion → real GDP +$100 billion.
Quick check
?The MPC is 0.9. Congress cuts taxes by $20 billion. Assuming no crowding out and no price-level change, what is the maximum change in real GDP?
Mission 3

The multiplier on the AD/AS diagram

  • Step 1: an injection rises (say G). AD shifts right by the first-round amount: AD1 → AD2.
  • Step 2: the multiplier adds further rounds: AD2 → AD3.
  • Step 3: with upward-sloping AS, part of the extra demand raises the price level. Real output rises by less than the full shift.
  • Near full capacity, most of the effect is on prices, not real output.
  • AP: the multiplier gives the maximum change in real GDP, assuming a horizontal AS.
Mission 3

How big in reality?

  • Real-world multipliers are estimated, not observed. Estimates vary widely.
  • IMF (2012): forecasts for 2010–11 had assumed fiscal multipliers of about 0.5. Its own estimate for the years after the 2008 crisis was higher: about 0.9 to 1.7.
  • US: CBO (2010) estimated multipliers of 1.0 to 2.5 for federal purchases of goods and services, but only 0.2 to 0.6 for a one-year tax cut for high-income earners.
  • Multipliers tend to be larger in deep recessions with spare capacity.
  • They tend to be smaller near full capacity, in very open economies, or if interest rates rise in response.
  • Economists still disagree on the size: it matters for debates on stimulus and austerity.
Quick check
?A large car factory closes, and investment in the region falls sharply. What happens to national income, other things equal?
Mission check

?Two economies get the same rise in government spending. In Economy X, households spend much of any extra income on imports. In Economy Y, they buy mostly home-produced goods. Saving and tax rates are the same. Which statement is correct?

The more of each round that is withdrawn (saving, tax, imports), the smaller the multiplier.

Mission 4

Aggregate supply in the short run

  • Aggregate supply (AS): the total real output that all firms in an economy plan to produce at each price level, in a given period.
  • Axes: PL (vertical), Real GDP (Y) (horizontal).
  • Short run: some costs, especially money wages (wages in cash terms, also called nominal wages), are fixed. Firms cannot change every cost straight away.
  • The SRAS curve slopes upward: a higher price level goes with more real output.
  • Label it Short-run aggregate supply (SRAS).
  • Long run: all costs can adjust, so the long-run curve (LRAS) is drawn differently.
Mission 4

Why SRAS slopes upward

  • Sticky wages: money wages are set in contracts and pay deals, often for a year. They do not rise straight away when prices rise.
  • Sticky wages and prices: some input prices (nominal wages, rents, contract prices) are slow to adjust to changes in the price level.
  • So when the price level rises:
    • firms get more for each unit they sell
    • many costs per unit stay the same
    • profit per unit rises, so firms supply more
  • Rising costs near capacity: to produce more, firms pay overtime and use older, less efficient machines. Extra output costs more, so it needs a higher price level.
  • The reverse holds too: a lower price level squeezes profit per unit, so firms cut output.
  • Say: "a higher price level", "real output" — not: "a higher price", "quantity". This is the whole economy, not one market.
Mission 4

Worked example: a firm with a fixed wage

  • Illustrative data. A firm sells each unit for 20.
  • Costs per unit: wages 12 (fixed by contract for a year), materials 4.
  • Profit per unit: 20 − 12 − 4 = 4.
  • The price level rises by 10%. The firm's price and its materials both rise by 10%:
    • price: 20 × 1.1 = 22
    • materials: 4 × 1.1 = 4.4
    • wages: still 12
  • New profit per unit: 22 − 12 − 4.4 = 5.6, up 40%.
  • Producing more now pays, so output rises: a movement up along SRAS.
  • When wages are renegotiated up 10% (to 13.2), profit per unit is 22 − 13.2 − 4.4 = 4.4: the same as 4 once prices are 10% higher. The extra reason to produce disappears in the long run.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A firm sells its product for 50. Per unit, it pays 30 in wages, fixed by contract for the year, and 10 for materials. The price level then rises by 10%, and the firm's price and materials cost rise by 10% too. What is the firm's new profit per unit?
Mission 4

Movement along SRAS or shift?

  • A change in the price level → a movement along SRAS.
    • PL rises → real GDP rises: a movement up along SRAS.
    • PL falls → real GDP falls: a movement down along SRAS.
  • A change in firms' costs (anything other than the price level) → a shift of SRAS.
    • Costs rise → SRAS shifts left (up): less output at each price level.
    • Costs fall → SRAS shifts right (down).
  • Say: "a decrease in SRAS" (shift left), "an increase in SRAS" (shift right).

  • Any factor that changes production costs shifts SRAS: input prices (incl. nominal wages), productivity, inflationary expectations and government actions on firms (business taxes, subsidies, regulation).

Mission 4

Real world: the 1973–74 oil shock

  • In October 1973, Arab oil producers began an embargo on oil exports to the United States and other countries.
  • The price of oil rose from 2.90 dollars a barrel before the embargo to 11.65 dollars a barrel in January 1974: about four times as much.
  • Oil is an input to transport, power, plastics and fertilizer, so costs rose across the economy: SRAS shifted left.
  • The US was in recession from November 1973 to March 1975, while prices rose fast.
  • Rising prices and falling output together: stagflation.
  • Caution: US inflation was already rising before the embargo. The oil shock added to it; it was not the only cause.
Mission 4

Along SRAS: a short-run trade-off

  • Illustrative data. AD increases. The economy moves up along SRAS:
    • PL: 100 → 104
    • real GDP: 1,000 → 1,040
    • unemployment rate: 6% → 5% (firms hire more workers to produce more)
  • Higher inflation, lower unemployment: a short-run trade-off between inflation and unemployment.
  • A decrease in AD moves the economy down along SRAS: lower inflation, higher unemployment.
  • This trade-off is what the short-run Phillips curve shows.
  • A decrease in SRAS is different: higher PL and lower real GDP, so higher inflation and higher unemployment. No trade-off.
Quick check
?The economy is in short-run equilibrium. Consumer spending increases sharply and nothing else changes. What happens in the short run?

Mission check

?New software lets workers in most firms produce 10% more each hour. Money wages and AD do not change. What is the most likely short-run effect?

Anything that changes firms' costs per unit shifts SRAS; only a change in the price level moves the economy along it. Higher productivity also raises capacity in the long run: a separate, long-run effect.

Mission 5

The original Phillips curve

  • In 1958, the economist A. W. Phillips plotted UK data from 1861 to 1957.
  • When unemployment was low, money wages rose fast. When unemployment was high, wages rose slowly.
  • Faster wage growth feeds into faster price rises, so the idea became a link between inflation and unemployment.
  • The curve slopes down: lower unemployment goes with higher inflation.
  • It is now called the short-run Phillips curve (SRPC).
  • Axes: inflation rate (%) up the side, unemployment rate (%) along the bottom.
Mission 5

Why a trade-off? Back to AD/AS

  • Step 1: AD rises. The economy moves up along SRAS.
  • Real output rises, so firms hire: unemployment falls.
  • The price level rises faster: inflation rises.
  • Step 2: the same change is a movement up along the SRPC.
  • A fall in AD does the reverse: down along SRAS, down along the SRPC.
  • Say: "a movement along the SRPC" — not: "a shift of the SRPC", when AD changes.

Mission 5

Real world: the trade-off holds, then breaks

  • 1961 to 1969 (US): unemployment fell from 6.7% to 3.5%; CPI inflation rose from about 1% to 5.5%. A movement up along the SRPC.
  • 1975 (US): unemployment 8.5% and inflation 9.1%, both high.
  • High inflation and high unemployment: stagflation.
  • Points no longer sat on one curve: the SRPC had shifted outward.
  • One big reason: oil-price supply shocks raised costs.
Mission 5

The long-run Phillips curve

  • The short-run trade-off does not last. The long-run Phillips curve (LRPC) is vertical.
  • In the long run there is no trade-off: pushing unemployment below the natural rate only raises inflation.
  • The LRPC is vertical at the natural rate of unemployment, matching the vertical LRAS at full-employment output.
  • Long-run equilibrium is where the SRPC crosses the LRPC.
Mission 5

Output gaps on the Phillips curve

  • Start at long-run equilibrium: SRPC meets LRPC at the natural rate.
  • A point on the SRPC left of the LRPC: unemployment below the natural rate, inflation higher. This matches an inflationary gap on AD/AS.
  • A point on the SRPC right of the LRPC: unemployment above the natural rate, inflation lower. This matches a recessionary gap.
  • AD decreases → down along SRAS on AD/AS → down and right along the SRPC.
Mission 5

Along the curve, or a shift?

  • Change in AD (demand shock): movement along the SRPC.
  • Supply shock (e.g. a sharp rise in oil or energy prices): SRAS shifts left, so the SRPC shifts right (up). Higher inflation at every unemployment rate.
  • A favorable supply shock (cheaper energy, faster productivity growth) shifts the SRPC left (down).
  • A change in the natural rate shifts the LRPC. Better skills matching, job search help or more flexible labor markets move it left. Long spells of unemployment that erode skills (hysteresis) can move it right.
  • AP summary: demand shocks → along the SRPC. Supply shocks → shift the SRPC. Natural rate changes → shift the LRPC.
Quick check
?A country invests heavily in solar and wind power. Over several years, firms' energy costs fall sharply. Other things equal, what happens on a Phillips curve diagram?

Mission check

Mission 6

LRAS: the economy's capacity

  • Long run: long enough for money (nominal) wages and all other input prices to adjust fully to the price level.
  • Long-run aggregate supply (LRAS): the real output the economy can produce when all its resources are fully employed.
  • This is full-employment output (potential output, Yf): the economy's maximum sustainable capacity.
  • LRAS is vertical at full-employment output.
  • Capacity depends on the quantity and quality of land, labor, capital and enterprise, and on technology, not on the price level.
  • "Full employment" still leaves some unemployment (people between jobs, for example). It is not zero.
Mission 6

Worked example: when all costs catch up

  • Illustrative data. The same firm as before: price 20, wages 12, materials 4, profit per unit 4.
  • Short run: the price level rises 10%, wages are stuck at 12, so profit per unit rises to 5.6. Output rises.
  • Long run: the wage contract is renegotiated. Wages catch up: 12 × 1.1 = 13.2.
  • Now every price and cost is 10% higher: 22 − 13.2 − 4.4 = 4.4.
  • 4.4 is exactly 4 × 1.1: real profit per unit is unchanged.
  • So firms have no lasting reason to produce more. Output returns to capacity.
  • With flexible wages and prices, SRAS adjusts until the economy is back at full-employment output.
  • A higher price level, the same real output: the long-run curve is vertical.
Quick check
?Why is the long-run aggregate supply curve drawn vertical?
Mission 6

What shifts LRAS

  • LRAS shifts right when capacity grows:
    • Quantity of factors: a larger labor force (population growth, net migration, more people choosing to work), more capital from investment, new natural resources.
    • Quality of factors: education, training and health raise human capital; better machines.
    • Technology: new methods let the same inputs make more.
  • LRAS shifts left if capacity shrinks: war or natural disaster destroys capital, emigration, an aging population.
  • Some changes shift both SRAS and LRAS: higher productivity cuts costs now and raises capacity. A temporary rise in oil prices shifts SRAS only.
Mission 6

LRAS and the PPC tell the same story

  • The PPC shows the most an economy can make of two goods with all resources fully used.
  • LRAS shows the same capacity as a single total of real output.
  • On the PPC → output at LRAS. Inside the PPC → output below capacity (unused resources).
  • PPC shifts outward ↔ LRAS shifts right: long-run economic growth.
  • An outward shift of the PPC corresponds to a rightward shift of LRAS: an increase in full-employment output.

Real world: Japan's shrinking workforce

  • Japan's working-age population (aged 15–64) peaked in 1995 at about 86.9 million.
  • By 2024 it was about 73.7 million: around 15% lower.
  • Fewer people of working age, other things equal, means less capacity: a force pushing LRAS left.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • AD = C + I + G + (X − M): planned spending on real output at each price level. Consumption is the largest component.
  • A change in the price level moves the economy along AD (wealth, interest rate and trade or exchange rate effects); a change in anything else that alters C, I, G or X − M shifts it.

Mission 2

  • Yd = C + S. Consumption is the largest part of AD.
  • Interest rates, confidence, wealth, taxes and credit move C; a change in C shifts AD.
  • MPC = ΔC ÷ ΔYd; MPC + MPS = 1. In C = a + bYd, b is the MPC.

Mission 3

  • An injection raises national income by more than itself, through rounds of spending.
  • k = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC).
  • Tax multiplier = −MPC ÷ MPS; the spending multiplier is larger.

Mission 4

  • SRAS slopes upward because money wages and some other costs are sticky in the short run.
  • A change in the price level → a movement along SRAS. A change in costs (wages, raw materials, energy, exchange rate, taxes, subsidies, productivity) → a shift.
  • Inflationary expectations shift SRAS. A movement along SRAS shows a short-run trade-off between inflation and unemployment.

Mission 5

  • The short-run Phillips curve slopes down: a rise in AD lowers unemployment and raises inflation.
  • Supply shocks shift the SRPC; changes in AD move the economy along it.
  • SRPC slopes down; LRPC vertical at the natural rate; left of the LRPC = inflationary gap, right = recessionary gap.

Mission 6

  • LRAS shows the economy's capacity: the output possible with all resources fully employed.
  • LRAS is vertical at full-employment output and matches the PPC.
  • LRAS shifts with the quantity and quality of factors, technology and institutions.
