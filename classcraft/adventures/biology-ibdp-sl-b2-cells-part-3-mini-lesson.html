Mission 1

Mission 1: An early embryo is a ball of identical cells. How does it know where to build a head and a tail?

Make a guess: An early embryo is a ball of identical cells. How does it know where to build a head and a tail?

Mission 1

The body plan problem

  • A zygote divides by mitosis into many unspecialised, genetically identical cells.
  • They must become different in the right places: head at one end, tail at the other.
  • Body plan: the arrangement of body parts along the body's axes, such as head to tail.
  • The genes that set up body plans code for transcription factors. Each one switches on many other genes.
Mission 1

Gradients tell cells where they are

  • (1) In a fruit fly egg, the mother places mRNA for the protein Bicoid at the head end.
  • (2) After fertilisation, Bicoid protein spreads out: a concentration gradient, high at the head end, low towards the tail.
  • At this stage the fly embryo is one large cell with many nuclei, so the protein can spread between them.
  • (3) Bicoid is a transcription factor. Different genes switch on above different threshold concentrations, so each region expresses its own set.
  • Every nucleus keeps all its genes; the gradient changes which are expressed.
  • Say: "cells respond to the concentration they receive" — not: "the signal is simply on or off".
Quick check
?Female fruit flies with extra copies of the bicoid gene lay eggs containing more Bicoid mRNA than normal at the head end. What is predicted for their embryos?
Mission check

?In a chick wing bud, cells at the rear edge release a signalling protein that spreads towards the front edge. Each digit has its own shape, and the digits lie in a set order from rear to front. When extra signalling cells are grafted onto the front edge, the wing grows an extra set of digits in mirror image. What is the best explanation?

Position is read as concentration. Move the source and the body plan moves with it.

Mission 2

What makes a stem cell?

  • A stem cell is undifferentiated. It can do two things:
  • (1) Divide again and again, renewing itself.
  • (2) Differentiate into specialised cell types.
  • Key phrase: a capacity to divide endlessly and to differentiate along different pathways.
  • Most specialised cells cannot do both.
  • Say: "able to divide endlessly" — not: "always dividing". Stem cells can rest between divisions.
Quick check
?Which pair of properties defines a stem cell?
Mission 2

Potency: how many cell types can it make?

  • Totipotent: any cell type, including the placenta and other extra-embryonic tissues. The zygote and the cells of the first few divisions.
  • Pluripotent: any body cell type, but not the placenta. Inner cell mass of the blastocyst: embryonic stem cells.
  • Multipotent: a limited range of types. Adult tissues, e.g. bone marrow → blood cells.
  • Say: "totipotent includes the placenta; pluripotent does not" — not: "both mean any cell".
Quick check
?A cell taken from a 4-cell human embryo can develop into a complete individual with its own placenta. A cell from the inner cell mass of a blastocyst can form any tissue of the body but not a placenta. A stem cell from adult bone marrow forms only blood cells. How should the three cells be classified?
Mission 2

Stem cells in the adult body

  • Bone marrow: stem cells make every type of blood cell.
  • Hair follicles: stem cells below the skin surface make a new hair each growth cycle.
  • A stem cell niche is the microenvironment around stem cells. It can maintain them (resting, undifferentiated) or promote their division and differentiation.
Quick check
?In a hair follicle, stem cells in a region below the skin surface stay inactive for months between hair growth cycles. At the start of a new cycle they begin to divide and produce the cells of a new hair. What does this show about the stem cell niche?
Mission check

?A cell is taken from adult bone marrow and grown in a dish. It divides many times. Some daughter cells stay like the parent; others become erythrocytes, neutrophils and other blood cells, but never nerve or muscle cells. Which statement is correct?

An early embryo cell can still become anything; a bone-marrow stem cell is limited to blood.

That limited range is what multipotent means.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why are almost all cells too small to see, even inside the largest animals?

Make a guess: Why are almost all cells too small to see, even inside the largest animals?

Mission 3

Supply and demand

  • Materials enter and leave across the surface. Supply depends on surface area.
  • Every part of the cytoplasm uses materials. Demand depends on volume.
  • The surface area to volume ratio (SA:V) compares the two.
  • High SA:V: plenty of surface for each unit of volume.
  • Say: "SA:V is surface area ÷ volume" — not: "volume ÷ surface area".
Mission 3

Worked example: growing cubes

Model cells as cubes of side s. Surface area = 6s². Volume = s³.

Side / µm1234
Surface area / µm²6245496
Volume / µm³182764
SA:V / µm⁻¹6.03.02.01.5
  • Side × 2: area × 4, volume × 8. SA:V halves.
  • Volume grows faster than surface. Big cells run short of surface.
  • Cubes are a simplified model. Any shape scales the same way: area × (scale factor)², volume × (scale factor)³.
  • So cells stay small, and large organisms are made of many small cells.
Quick check
?A cube of side 1 mm has a surface area of 6 mm², a volume of 1 mm³ and a surface area-to-volume ratio of 6. The side length is increased to 4 mm. What is the new surface area-to-volume ratio?

Mission check

?A spherical single-celled organism grows from a radius of 10 µm to a radius of 30 µm, keeping its shape. Which prediction is best supported?

Bigger always means less surface per unit of volume. That is why cells stay small and large organisms are built from many of them.

Mission 4

Mission 4: How do your lungs pull air in when they have no muscle of their own?

Make a guess: How do your lungs pull air in when they have no muscle of their own?

Mission 4

The airway tree

  • Air path: trachea → two bronchi → many bronchioles → alveoli (air sacs).
  • Repeated branching ends in hundreds of millions of alveoli: a surface of tens of square metres inside the chest.
  • Gas exchange happens only across the alveoli. The airways conduct air to them.
  • The lungs sit in the thorax, enclosed by the ribs and intercostal muscles, with the diaphragm below.
Mission 4

The alveolus: built for diffusion

  • Alveolar wall: one layer of flattened (squamous) epithelial cells.
  • Capillary wall: one layer of endothelial cells. Over much of the surface, air to blood is under 1 µm.
  • A dense capillary network wraps each alveolus. Continuous blood flow keeps the gradient.
  • Ventilation keeps alveolar air high in O2 and low in CO2.
  • A moist lining lets gases dissolve. Surfactant in it lowers surface tension, so alveoli do not collapse.
  • The branched network of bronchioles delivers air to every alveolus.
  • Say: "thin alveolar epithelium, one cell thick" — not: "thin cell walls". Animal cells have no cell walls.
Quick check
?Mammalian lungs contain a branched network of bronchioles ending in millions of alveoli. How does this branching contribute to gas exchange?
Mission 4

Breathing in

  • (1) The diaphragm contracts and flattens, moving down.
  • (1) The external intercostal muscles contract, moving the ribs up and out. The internal intercostals relax.
  • (1) The abdominal muscles relax, so the abdominal organs can move down.
  • (2) Thorax volume rises, so pressure in the lungs falls below atmospheric.
  • (3) Air flows in, down the pressure gradient. The lungs are pulled open; they do no work themselves.
Mission 4

Breathing out

  • At rest, breathing out is mostly passive. The diaphragm and external intercostals relax.
  • The diaphragm domes upward; the ribs move down and in; stretched elastic fibres in the lungs recoil.
  • Thorax volume falls, pressure rises above atmospheric, and air flows out.
  • Forced breathing out: the internal intercostal muscles contract, pulling the ribs down and in.
  • In forced breathing out, the abdominal muscles contract, pushing the organs up against the relaxed diaphragm.
  • External and internal intercostals are antagonistic: they move the ribs in opposite directions.
  • Say: "the diaphragm relaxes and domes upward" — not: "the diaphragm contracts and pushes up".
Quick check
?Which sequence of events draws air into the lungs during inspiration?
Mission check

?During a rugby match, a player is tackled hard. A small tear in the surface of one lung lets air leak into the space around that lung. The diaphragm and intercostal muscles on that side still contract normally, but that lung does not inflate. Which explanation is best?

The muscles change the volume of the thorax; the lungs simply follow. Break that link and the lung stays collapsed.

Mission 5: How can you tell from the outside how well a person's lungs are working?

Make a guess: How can you tell from the outside how well a person's lungs are working?

Mission 5

Lung volumes on a spirometer trace

  • A spirometer records the volume of air breathed in and out against time.
  • Tidal volume: volume in or out in one normal breath (about 0.5 dm³ at rest).
  • Inspiratory reserve volume: the extra you can breathe in after a normal breath in.
  • Expiratory reserve volume: the extra you can breathe out after a normal breath out.
  • Vital capacity = tidal volume + inspiratory reserve + expiratory reserve: the most you can breathe out after the deepest breath in.
  • Residual volume: air left after the hardest breath out. A spirometer cannot measure it.
Quick check
?A student's spirometer readings are: tidal volume 0.5 dm³, inspiratory reserve volume 3.0 dm³, expiratory reserve volume 1.2 dm³. What is the student's vital capacity?
Mission 5

What sets the breathing rate?

  • (1) Muscles respire faster → more CO2 enters the blood.
  • (2) CO2 forms carbonic acid → more H⁺ → blood pH falls.
  • (3) Chemoreceptors detect the fall in pH.
  • (3) These chemoreceptors are in the brainstem.
  • (4) More nerve impulses go to the diaphragm and intercostal muscles: breathing becomes faster and deeper.
  • (5) Extra CO2 is breathed out; pH returns towards normal: negative feedback.
  • Say: "rising CO2 (falling pH) drives faster breathing" — not: "breathing speeds up because the body detects low oxygen".
Quick check
?During a run, a student's blood carbon dioxide concentration rises, blood pH falls from 7.40 to 7.32 and ventilation rate rises from 12 to 30 breaths per minute. Which statement correctly links these changes?
Mission check

?At rest, an athlete's tidal volume is 0.6 dm³, inspiratory reserve volume 2.8 dm³ and expiratory reserve volume 1.4 dm³. During hard exercise her tidal volume rises to 2.2 dm³. Which statement is correct?

Lung volumes, breathing rate and disease data all answer one question: how much air reaches a large, thin, well-supplied surface.

Mission 6

Mission 6: An egg is a giant cell and a sperm is a tiny one. Why are the two cells built so differently?

Make a guess: An egg is a giant cell and a sperm is a tiny one. Why are the two cells built so differently?

Mission 6

Why have two parents at all?

  • Asexual: one parent, mitosis, genetically identical offspring.
  • Best when the parent is already adapted to a stable environment.
  • Sexual: meiosis + fertilisation give new gene combinations.
  • That variation is needed when the environment changes.
  • Say: "some offspring happen to suit the new conditions" — not: "variation is produced to meet a need".
Mission 6

The prime difference: one gamete travels

  • The male gamete travels to the female gamete. The egg stays put.
  • So the sperm is small, streamlined, with almost no food reserves.
  • The egg is large: its cytoplasm holds reserves for the embryo's first divisions.
  • Small and cheap → sperm made in vast numbers. Large → few eggs.
  • Both carry one haploid set of chromosomes. The size difference is cytoplasm, not DNA.
Mission 6

The male-typical system

  • Testis: makes sperm and testosterone. Held in the scrotum, below body temperature.
  • Epididymis: coiled tube on the testis; sperm mature and are stored here.
  • Sperm duct: carries sperm to the urethra.
  • Seminal vesicle and prostate gland: add fluid. Sperm + fluid = semen.
  • The seminal vesicle fluid contains fructose, a respiratory substrate for sperm.
  • Urethra: carries semen at ejaculation, urine at other times. Penis: erectile tissue.
  • Say: "seminal vesicles add fluid" — not: "seminal vesicles store sperm".
Mission 6

The female-typical system

  • Ovary: releases an egg each cycle (ovulation, around day 14); secretes oestrogen and progesterone.
  • Oviduct (fallopian tube): cilia move the egg; fertilisation happens here.
  • Uterus: muscular wall; the embryo implants in its lining.
  • Endometrium: the inner lining. Thickens each cycle; shed at menstruation.
  • Cervix: the neck of the uterus. Vagina: receives sperm; the birth canal.
  • Say: "fertilisation in the oviduct" — not: "fertilisation in the uterus".
Quick check
?A student annotates a diagram of the female reproductive system. Which annotation is correct?
Mission 6

Getting in

  • Many sperm reach the egg in the oviduct.
  • One sperm's cell membrane fuses with the egg's cell membrane.
  • The sperm nucleus passes into the egg cytoplasm.
  • Entry is by membrane fusion, not by drilling a hole.
Mission 6

After entry

  • The sperm's tail and mitochondria usually enter too, but are destroyed. All the zygote's mitochondria come from the egg.
  • Both nuclear membranes dissolve. All the condensed chromosomes join one mitosis.
  • That first mitosis makes two diploid nuclei: a two-cell embryo.
  • Say: "chromosomes from both gametes join a joint mitosis" — not: "the nuclei fuse into one nucleus".
Quick check
?Which event occurs during fertilisation in humans?
Mission check

?In a fish species the female lays a few hundred large eggs in a nest and the male releases many millions of tiny sperm over them. Which statement explains the difference in size and number?

One cell travels, the other supplies the embryo. Size, numbers and every special structure follow from that.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Meiosis in a testis gives four sperm, but meiosis in an ovary gives just one egg. Why?

Make a guess: Meiosis in a testis gives four sperm, but meiosis in an ovary gives just one egg. Why?

Mission 7

Unequal cytokinesis

  • Nuclear division shares the chromosomes equally. Always.
  • Cytokinesis (division of the cytoplasm) is usually equal, but not always.
  • Unequal example 1: making an egg. One big cell keeps almost all the cytoplasm; a tiny polar body gets almost none.
  • Unequal example 2: budding yeast. A small bud pinches off a large parent cell.
  • Each daughter cell must get at least one mitochondrion. Mitochondria form only by division of existing ones.
Quick check
?In human oogenesis, the first meiotic division produces a large secondary oocyte and a very small polar body. What does this unequal cytokinesis achieve?

Mission check

?Yeast can reproduce by budding. The nucleus divides by mitosis, and a small bud then pinches off a much larger parent cell. Which statement about the bud, as it separates, is correct?

Chromosomes are always shared equally; cytoplasm need not be. Give it all to one cell and you get one large egg, not four small ones.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Gradients of transcription factors in the early embryo switch on different genes at different concentrations, so cells in different places differentiate differently.

Mission 2

  • A stem cell can divide repeatedly and differentiate into specialised cells.
  • Totipotent: any cell, including placenta. Pluripotent: any body cell.
  • Multipotent: a limited range, as in adult bone marrow.

Mission 3

  • Supply depends on surface area, demand on volume; as size increases, volume grows faster, so SA:V falls and limits cell size.

Mission 4

  • Trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli; thin, moist, ventilated alveoli wrapped in capillaries give a huge, short diffusion path.
  • Breathing in: diaphragm and external intercostals contract → thorax volume up → pressure down → air in. Breathing out reverses this.

Mission 5

  • Vital capacity = tidal volume + inspiratory reserve + expiratory reserve; measured with a spirometer.
  • Rising CO2 lowers blood pH → chemoreceptors → medulla → faster, deeper breathing (negative feedback).

Mission 6

  • The sperm travels, so it is small, cheap and numerous; the egg stays, so it is large and stocked with reserves. Both are haploid.
  • Tail and mitochondria are destroyed; nuclear membranes dissolve and the chromosomes join one mitosis, giving two diploid nuclei.

Mission 7

  • Unequal cytokinesis: chromosomes are shared equally, cytoplasm is not; every daughter cell still needs a mitochondrion.
