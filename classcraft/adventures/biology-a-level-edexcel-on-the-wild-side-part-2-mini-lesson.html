Mission 1

Mission 1: Energy flows along every food chain but never comes back. So where does it start, and where does it go?

Make a guess: Energy flows along every food chain but never comes back. So where does it start, and where does it go?

Mission 1

Energy in, energy out

  • Sunlight is the source of energy for almost every ecosystem.
  • Producers (plants, algae) capture light and fix carbon dioxide from the air or water into organic compounds.
  • That energy is now chemical energy in carbon compounds.
  • It passes along food chains when one organism eats another.
  • At every step, respiration releases energy that leaves as heat. Heat cannot be captured again.
  • Decomposers feed on dead matter and faeces, and respire too.
  • So energy flows through an ecosystem, one way. It must be resupplied all the time.
  • Say: "energy is transferred, and lost as heat" — not: "energy is used up".
Mission 1

Food chains and food webs

  • Food chain: one feeding pathway, e.g. grass → rabbit → fox.
  • Each arrow points from the food to the feeder: the direction energy and biomass move.
  • Food web: many linked chains in one community.
  • Say: "the arrow means 'is eaten by'" — not: "the arrow means 'eats'".
Quick check
?What do the arrows in a food web represent?
Mission 1

Trophic levels

  • A trophic level is a feeding position in a food chain.
  • Level 1: producers. Level 2: primary consumers (herbivores). Level 3: secondary consumers. Level 4: tertiary consumers.
  • An animal with a varied diet sits at different levels in different chains.
  • Count from the producer: the producer is not a consumer.
Quick check
?In a garden, blackbirds eat berries from shrubs and also eat snails, which feed on the leaves of the same shrubs. Which trophic level or levels does the blackbird occupy?
Mission check

?In autumn, an oak tree drops its leaves. Over the next year, fungi and bacteria break the leaves down. What happens to the carbon and to the energy that were in the leaves?

Matter goes round; energy goes through. Sunlight in, heat out: that is why every food chain needs the sun, or another outside source, all the time.

Mission 2

Mission 2: A caterpillar eats a whole leaf. Why does so little of that leaf end up as caterpillar?

Make a guess: A caterpillar eats a whole leaf. Why does so little of that leaf end up as caterpillar?

Mission 2

Gross and net primary production

  • Producers use light energy to fix CO2 into sugars.
  • Most of the sugar is respired by the plant itself. The rest builds other molecules: the plant's biomass.
  • Gross primary production (GPP): the chemical energy stored by photosynthesis in plant biomass, in a given area or volume.
  • R: the energy the plants lose in respiration.
  • Net primary production: NPP = GPP − R.
  • NPP is what is left for plant growth and reproduction, and for consumers and decomposers.
  • Say: "producers capture and store energy" — not: "producers make energy".
Mission 2

Production is a rate

  • The biomass present now is a stock: e.g. g m⁻² or kJ m⁻².
  • Productivity is the rate of production: per area per unit time.
  • e.g. kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹.
  • Primary production: by producers. Secondary production: by consumers.
  • Say: "productivity is a rate" — not: "productivity is the biomass present".
Mission 2

Worked example: NPP

A grassland (illustrative data): GPP = 24 000 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹. Plant respiration, R = 14 400 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹.

  • Step 1: NPP = GPP − R = 24 000 − 14 400 = 9 600 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹.
  • Step 2: share of GPP left as NPP = 9 600 ÷ 24 000 × 100 = 40%. The plants respired 60%.
  • Keep the units: per area, per year, at every step.

Mission 2

Where a consumer's food goes

  • Energy in the food eaten (ingested): I.
  • Part is never absorbed: lost in faeces (egestion).
  • Part of what is absorbed leaves in urine (excretion, e.g. urea).
  • Most of the absorbed energy is respired (R) and lost as heat.
  • Only the rest becomes new biomass: the consumer's net production.
  • Example (illustrative): a bullock eats grass holding 100 kJ; 64 kJ is lost in faeces and urine; R = 32 kJ. Net production = 100 − (64 + 32) = 4 kJ.
Quick check
?A caterpillar ingests leaf material containing 100 kJ of energy. It egests 50 kJ in faeces, releases 35 kJ as heat through cell respiration and stores 15 kJ in new biomass. What happens to the energy in the faeces?
Mission 2

Why so much is lost

  • Not eaten: roots, wood, bones. Dead remains go to decomposers.
  • Not digested: lost in faeces. Cellulose is hard to digest, so herbivores lose much more this way than carnivores.
  • Excreted: nitrogenous waste such as urea, in urine.
  • Respired: every organism, plants included, respires; most absorbed energy ends up as heat.
  • Endotherms (mammals, birds) respire far more to keep warm, so less of their food becomes biomass.
  • Say: "energy is lost to the surroundings as heat" — not: "energy is used up".
Quick check
?Why is much less energy available to the secondary consumers in a food chain than to the primary consumers?

Mission check

?A wood mouse eats seeds containing 500 kJ of energy. 100 kJ is lost in faeces and urine, and 380 kJ is released in respiration. What percentage of the energy it ate is available to an owl that eats the mouse?

Like the caterpillar, the mouse loses most of its food in waste and as heat from respiration. Only the small remainder becomes body, and only that can feed the next level.

Mission 3

Mission 3: The carbon atoms in your body were once in the air. How do they keep being recycled?

Make a guess: The carbon atoms in your body were once in the air. How do they keep being recycled?

Mission 3

Where carbon is stored

  • Carbon sits in stores (reservoirs):
  • the atmosphere, as CO2;
  • the oceans, as dissolved CO2 and hydrogencarbonate ions;
  • living organisms, as carbon compounds;
  • dead organic matter and soil;
  • fossil fuels, peat and rocks such as limestone.
  • Carbon atoms move between stores. None is made or destroyed.
Mission 3

The processes that move it

  • Photosynthesis removes CO2 from air or water and fixes it in producers.
  • Feeding passes carbon compounds along food chains.
  • Respiration in every organism, producers included, returns CO2.
  • Decomposition: decomposers respire the carbon in dead matter and faeces, returning CO2.
  • Combustion of fuels and biomass releases CO2.
  • Say: "plants respire all the time" — not: "plants photosynthesise instead of respiring".
Quick check
?A student draws a carbon cycle diagram for a pond. Which set of labelled arrows is correct?
Mission 3

Decomposers unlock the dead

  • Dead organisms and faeces lock carbon and other elements inside large organic molecules.
  • Decomposers (saprotrophic fungi and bacteria) secrete enzymes, digest the matter outside their cells, absorb the products and respire them: CO2 is released.
  • They also release mineral ions, such as ammonium and phosphate, that plants can absorb.
  • So all the elements living things need are recycled, not just carbon.
  • No decomposers, no recycling: carbon builds up in dead matter.
  • Example: waterlogged soil lacks O2, so decay is very slow and peat forms.
  • Say: "decomposers return carbon to the air as CO2" — not: "decomposition puts the carbon into the soil".
Quick check
?In an experiment, all the decomposers in a woodland soil are killed, but the trees keep growing for a while. Which change would follow over the next few years?
Mission 3

Slow stores: oceans, rocks and fuels

  • CO2 dissolves in the oceans. Marine organisms build calcium carbonate shells; their remains form limestone.
  • Where decomposition was incomplete (waterlogged, little O2), plant remains became peat and, over millions of years, coal.
  • Oil and natural gas formed from marine plankton buried in sediments with little O2.
  • Combustion returns that stored carbon to the air as CO2 very quickly.
  • Say: "fossil-fuel carbon was fixed by photosynthesis" — not: "fossil fuels are just minerals".
Quick check
?Which statement about the combustion of biomass, peat, coal, oil and natural gas is correct?
Mission check

?A peat bog has stored carbon for thousands of years in waterlogged soil. It is drained for farmland, and the peat dries out. What happens to much of the carbon in the peat?

Carbon only keeps moving while something moves it: photosynthesis takes it in; respiration, decomposition and combustion send it back.

  • The rise is speeding up: about 1.1 ppm per year in 1960–1980; about 2.2 ppm per year in 2000–2020.
Mission 4

Two patterns, two causes

  • The yearly swing: most land, and most plants, are in the Northern Hemisphere.
  • Northern summer: photosynthesis > respiration, so CO2 falls.
  • Northern autumn and winter: respiration and decomposition > photosynthesis, so CO2 rises.
  • The long-term rise: combustion of fossil fuels, and burning and clearing of forests, add CO2 faster than photosynthesis and the oceans remove it.
  • Carbon locked away for millions of years is returned to the air in decades.
  • Say: "the rise comes from combustion" — not: "from more people breathing out".
Quick check
?At Mauna Loa, atmospheric carbon dioxide concentration rises to a peak each May and falls to a minimum each September or October, a swing of several parts per million. What causes this annual fluctuation?
Mission 4

The greenhouse effect

  • Sunlight passes through the atmosphere and warms the Earth's surface.
  • The warm surface gives off infrared radiation.
  • Greenhouse gases absorb infrared and re-emit it, partly back towards the surface. This keeps Earth warm enough for life.
  • More greenhouse gas → more infrared retained → the enhanced greenhouse effect → global warming.
  • CO2 rises from combustion of fossil fuels and from deforestation.
  • Methane rises from ruminant livestock, rice paddies, landfill and leaks from gas and coal extraction.
  • Say: "greenhouse gases absorb infrared" — not: "the ozone hole lets in more heat".
Quick check
?Which is an anthropogenic cause of the rise in the atmospheric concentration of methane since pre-industrial times?
Mission 4

Reading past climates

  • Ice cores: bubbles of air trapped in Antarctic ice record past CO2; the ice itself records past temperature.
  • Over 800 000 years, high CO2 goes with high temperature: a positive correlation.
  • Correlation alone does not prove cause. Other evidence, such as CO2 absorbing infrared in the lab, supplies the causal link.
  • Temperature records: thermometer measurements since the mid-1800s.
  • Pollen in peat bogs: waterlogged peat preserves pollen in layers. Identify the plants; their climate needs reveal past climate. Deeper layers are older (dated by radiocarbon).
  • Dendrochronology: one tree ring per year; wide rings mean good growing conditions. Matching ring patterns dates wood to the year.
  • Each has limits: pollen can blow in from afar; ring width also depends on rainfall and pests.
Quick check
?Antarctic ice cores show that, over the last 800 000 years, periods of high global temperature coincided with high atmospheric carbon dioxide concentrations and periods of low temperature with low concentrations. Which statement about this evidence is correct?
Mission 4

Models of future climate

  • Extrapolation: extend a trend beyond the measured data to predict.
  • Climate models combine physics with past data to predict warming under different emission scenarios.
  • Limits: future emissions depend on human choices.
  • Limits: feedbacks (clouds, melting ice, carbon released by soils) are uncertain.
  • Limits: past records are incomplete, and natural events (volcanoes, solar changes) are hard to predict.
  • The further ahead, the wider the uncertainty. So models give a range, not one number.
Mission 4

Same evidence, different conclusions?

  • On controversial questions (how far humans are changing the climate; what to do about it), conclusions can depend on who reaches them.
  • Ask: Who funded the work? Who gains or loses from the conclusion? Was it peer reviewed? Is it a primary study or a summary?
  • Confidence grows when many independent, peer-reviewed studies agree.
  • Example: the IPCC, which assesses thousands of published studies, concluded in 2021 that human influence has warmed the climate.
  • What action to take also depends on values and costs, not only on data.
  • In answers, weigh the evidence, its source and any interest; give arguments, not just a verdict.
Mission 4

Drawing carbon back down

  • Use the carbon cycle: increase photosynthesis, slow decomposition, cut combustion.
  • Reforestation and afforestation: growing trees fix CO2 and store it in wood and soil.
  • Restore peat-forming wetlands: re-wetting cuts O2, slows decomposers, and peat builds up again.
  • Biofuels: burning them returns CO2 fixed recently by the crop, in place of fossil carbon. Farming, processing and land clearance add emissions.
  • Burn less fossil fuel: renewable energy and greater efficiency.
Mission check

?At a monitoring station in Tasmania, in the Southern Hemisphere, carbon dioxide shows a much smaller yearly swing than at Mauna Loa, but a similar long-term rise. Which explanation fits both observations?

Two clocks run in the same curve: the yearly breath of the northern forests, and the steady climb from burning carbon stored for millions of years.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Why can a warming of just a degree or two force species to move, or vanish from a place?

Make a guess: Why can a warming of just a degree or two force species to move, or vanish from a place?

Mission 5

Species on the move

  • Each species survives only within a band of conditions: temperature, rainfall, season length.
  • As the climate warms, that band shifts towards the poles and up mountains.
  • New populations establish at the cool edge; old ones die out at the warm edge. The range shifts.
  • This happens over generations, through dispersal and the survival of young, not by individuals migrating.
  • No cooler place to go (mountain tops, poles), or barriers in the way? Local extinction, or extinction of the species.
  • Changing rainfall patterns and seasonal cycles shift where species can live, too.
  • Say: "the range shifts" — not: "the range grows" or "the animals move house".
Quick check
?The bird species of a mountain in New Guinea were surveyed in 1965 and again in 2012. For most species both the upper and the lower limits of the altitudinal range had moved upslope, by about 100 m on average, over a period in which the region warmed. What is the best interpretation?
Mission 5

Losers, winners and biodiversity

  • Likely losers: species with narrow tolerances, slow dispersal or specialist diets; mountain-top and polar species.
  • Roads, farms and towns can block movement, trapping species in shrinking ranges.
  • Local extinctions lower local biodiversity; some species may become extinct altogether.
  • Likely winners: species that spread easily, including some pests, pathogens and invasive species.
  • Species shift at different rates, so communities reassemble and interactions (predator–prey, pollinator–flower) change.
Quick check
?In a survey of forests in the eastern United States, seedlings of several northern tree species were found on average further north than mature trees of the same species, while at the southern edge of the range there were mature trees but few seedlings. What does this indicate?
Mission 5

Temperature, enzymes and development

  • Plants, microorganisms and most animals are ectotherms: their body temperature follows the surroundings.
  • Below the optimum, warmer means more kinetic energy, more enzyme–substrate collisions, faster reactions.
  • Above the optimum, enzymes denature: the active site changes shape and no longer fits the substrate.
  • So warming changes rates of growth and development, and the timing of life cycles:
  • Plants: earlier germination, budburst and flowering.
  • Animals: faster development, e.g. insects emerge earlier; in some reptiles nest temperature sets the sex ratio.
  • Microorganisms: faster decomposition, releasing more CO2.
  • Say: "cold slows enzymes" — not: "cold denatures enzymes".
Mission 5Practical

Core practical 13: temperature and development

  • Brine shrimp: equal numbers of eggs in equal volumes of salt water of the same concentration.
  • Incubate at a range of temperatures, e.g. 15, 20, 25, 30, 35 °C (water baths or incubators).
  • Count hatched larvae at fixed times, e.g. every 12 h for 48 h. Repeat at each temperature.
  • Control: salt concentration, volume, light, aeration, egg batch.
  • Calculate: % hatched = hatched ÷ eggs × 100; plot against temperature. (Seedling version: mean growth rate in mm per day.)

Mission check

?A hypothetical small mammal lives only in cool meadows near the top of an isolated mountain. Over 40 years the region warms by 1.5 °C. Which outcome is most likely?

A degree or two moves the conditions a species needs. Species that can follow survive in new places; those with nowhere to go are lost.

Mission 6

Mission 6: How can we trust the evidence that living things have changed over time?

Make a guess: How can we trust the evidence that living things have changed over time?

Mission 6

Genomes and proteins as evidence

  • Genomics: comparing whole DNA sequences. Proteomics: comparing the proteins an organism makes.
  • After lineages split, differences accumulate. Fewer differences → more recent common ancestor.
  • Example: aligned human and chimpanzee genomes differ at about 1.2% of single-base positions.
  • These comparisons can confirm, or overturn, relationships proposed from anatomy.
Quick check
?The amino acid sequence of the same protein was compared in four species. Compared with species W, species X differs at 2 positions, species Y at 15 positions and species Z at 31 positions. Which conclusion is supported by the data?
Mission 6

How scientists test the evidence

  • Scientific journals: results published with methods, so others can check and repeat them.
  • Peer review: before publication, other experts in the field scrutinise the methods, data and conclusions. They can ask for changes or reject the paper.
  • Conferences: new findings are presented and challenged; other groups then test them.
  • New genomic and proteomic evidence becomes accepted only after this checking and independent replication.
Quick check
?A research team compares protein sequences and concludes that species X is more closely related to species Y than anatomy had suggested. What is the main role of peer review for this work?
Mission check

?Whales were once thought to be most closely related to an extinct group of hoofed carnivores. DNA comparisons then placed whales closest to hippopotamuses, among the even-toed hoofed mammals. Soon after, fossils of early whales were found with an ankle bone of a shape found only in even-toed hoofed mammals. Why does this agreement strengthen confidence in the relationship?

No single fossil or sequence settles it. Confidence comes from independent evidence that keeps agreeing.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Why do small, isolated populations end up so genetically different from everyone else?

Make a guess: Why do small, isolated populations end up so genetically different from everyone else?

Mission 7

Gene flow

  • Gene flow: alleles moving between populations when individuals (or their pollen, seeds or gametes) arrive and breed.
  • Gene flow keeps populations' allele frequencies similar. It prevents divergence.
  • Isolation (a river, mountains, distance, or differences in breeding) reduces gene flow.
  • Then mutation, selection and chance change each gene pool separately: the populations diverge.
  • Enough divergence can lead to allopatric or sympatric speciation.
Quick check
?Which event would result in gene flow into a population of a hypothetical wildflower species that grows in a meadow?
Quick check
?Two populations of a hypothetical frog species live in neighbouring ponds connected by a stream, and frogs regularly move between the ponds and breed in both. The ponds have similar conditions. A dam is then built that stops all movement of frogs between the ponds. Which prediction about the two populations over many generations is best supported?
Mission check

?Two lakes were once joined by a stream, and fish of one species moved and bred freely between them. The stream dried up 2,000 years ago. Today the two fish populations differ in the frequencies of several alleles, but fish from the two lakes still produce fertile offspring when bred together. Which explanation is best supported?

Cut the flow of alleles, and two gene pools start to drift and diverge.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Energy enters as light (or, for chemoautotrophs, from oxidising inorganic substances), passes along food chains in carbon compounds, and leaves as heat; it is never recycled.
  • Trophic level is a feeding position; one species can occupy several. Decomposers feed on dead matter from every level.

Mission 2

  • NPP = GPP − R; productivity is a rate per area per time; efficiency = energy (or biomass) at a level ÷ that at the level below × 100.
  • Consumers lose energy in faeces, urine and, above all, as heat from respiration; only new biomass passes on.

Mission 3

  • Carbon moves between stores by photosynthesis (removes CO2), feeding, and respiration, decomposition and combustion (all add CO2).
  • Decomposers recycle carbon and every other element; without them, carbon piles up in dead matter such as peat.

Mission 4

  • The yearly swing in CO2 is photosynthesis against respiration in the northern growing season; the long-term rise is combustion of fossil fuels and forests.
  • CO2 and methane absorb infrared; more of them enhances the greenhouse effect.
  • Past climate evidence shows correlation; the causal link comes from other evidence.

Mission 5

  • Warming shifts the conditions species need poleward and upslope; ranges follow over generations, and species with nowhere to go face local extinction.
  • Temperature changes enzyme-controlled rates, so it changes growth, development and life cycles.

Mission 6

  • Evidence is trusted after scrutiny by other scientists and independent confirmation; a theory is a well-tested explanation, not a guess.

Mission 7

  • Gene flow keeps populations alike; isolation stops it, so allele frequencies diverge.
