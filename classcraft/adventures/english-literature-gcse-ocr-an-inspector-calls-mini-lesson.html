An Inspector Calls

This mini-lesson covers J.B. Priestley's An Inspector Calls: the plot, the Birling family and the Inspector, the big themes (social responsibility, class, gender, age, guilt), the crucial 1912-vs-1945 context and Priestley's dramatic devices — then how to turn all of it into a strong essay judged against AO1, AO2 and AO3.

The play at a glance

Who, where, when — and what happens

The action unfolds in a single evening in 1912, in the dining room of the prosperous Birling family in the fictional industrial city of Brumley. They are celebrating Sheila Birling's engagement to Gerald Croft when an Inspector (Goole) arrives to investigate the suicide of a young working-class woman, Eva Smith (who also used the name Daisy Renton).

  • One by one, the Inspector reveals each character's part in Eva's downfall — a "chain of events".
  • Arthur Birling sacked her from his factory for asking for higher wages.
  • Sheila had her dismissed from a shop (Milwards) out of jealousy.
  • Gerald kept her as his mistress; Eric got her pregnant and stole money; Mrs (Sybil) Birling refused her charity.

The twist: after the Inspector leaves, the Birlings learn no such inspector exists at the local police — and Eva may be several girls, not one. But a final phone call reports a girl has just died and a real inspector is on his way. The ending is cyclical and unresolved.

Priestley's big ideas

The themes that run through the play

Priestley uses the inquiry into Eva's death to argue a set of interlocking themes. Know what each one means:

Priestley's politics: a committed socialist, he sets Birling's selfish capitalism ("a man has to mind his own business and look after himself and his own") against the Inspector's collective, welfare-state vision — the play was first staged in 1945, the year of Labour's landslide.

The heart of the play

The Inspector is Priestley's mouthpiece

The Inspector (Goole) is mysterious, morally authoritative — possibly even supernatural or a dramatic device rather than a real policeman. Through him Priestley delivers the play's central message of social responsibility:

"We are members of one body"i.e. we are all responsible for one another — the opposite of Birling's "look after himself and his own"

His final warning is a threat about the cost of ignoring that lesson: "Fire and blood and anguish" — often read as a prophecy of the two World Wars the 1945 audience had just lived through.

Golden rule for essays: don't just say "the Inspector is important." Explain how Priestley uses him — as a catalyst, a voice of conscience, a morality-play device — and what effect that has on the audience.

Worked model · annotation

Annotating a key line

Here is the Inspector's final warning, annotated the way you'd mark up a quotation in the exam:

"…they will be taught it in fire and blood and anguish."
  • "fire and blood and anguish" — asyndetic triple: three nouns pile up without "and" slowing them, hammering the warning home.
  • biblical / apocalyptic imagery — "fire and blood" echoes scripture, making the Inspector sound like a prophet pronouncing judgement.
  • dramatic irony — a 1945 audience hears this as the two World Wars they have just survived: the warning already came true.
  • effect — the audience feels indicted: the lesson isn't just for the Birlings, it's for them.

Notice the pattern: each note is quote → method → effect. That is exactly the sentence shape your essay paragraphs need.

The Birlings & the Inspector

Know your characters

Priestley splits the family along a clear line — the unrepentant and the changed:

  • Arthur Birling — capitalist factory owner, pompous and self-made; his "unsinkable" Titanic and "no war" speeches are pure dramatic irony.
  • Sybil (Mrs) Birling — cold and snobbish; chairs the charity that turns Eva away, dismissing "Girls of that class —".
  • Sheila Birling — starts spoiled but changes most, accepting guilt: "It's you two who are being childish," she tells her parents.
  • Eric Birling — drinks heavily, fathers Eva's child, but also learns and accepts responsibility.
  • Gerald Croft — Sheila's fiancé; had the affair with Eva/Daisy, but ultimately reverts to his old attitudes.

Eva Smith / Daisy Renton never appears on stage. She is Priestley's symbol of the exploited working class — the unseen victim whose story exposes everyone else.

Essay skill

Start your essay with a thesis

Open with a thesis — a clear line of argument that answers the exact question. Everything after it is proof.

Model opening

Question: "How does Priestley present ideas about responsibility?"

Thesis: "Priestley presents responsibility as a collective duty we cannot escape, using the Inspector as a moral mouthpiece — 'we are members of one body' — to expose the Birlings' selfish individualism and warn a 1945 audience to choose community over greed."

Why it works: it takes a position (responsibility is collective), names a method (the Inspector as mouthpiece) and points to an effect / context (warning the 1945 audience). Now every paragraph can prove one part of it.

Evidence done well

Embed quotes — keep them short

The play is a closed-book text, so learn a bank of short quotations and weave them into your own sentences rather than dropping in whole lines:

Clumsy vs embedded

❌ "The Inspector is powerful. The quote is: 'We don't live alone. We are members of one body.'"

✔ "The Inspector's insistence that we are 'members of one body' reframes society as a single organism, so Birling's individualism sounds not just cruel but self-destructive."

Misconception: long quotations don't earn more marks — they waste time and bury your analysis. Quote the few words you'll actually analyse.

Context & AO3

Context serves the analysis

Context (the world the play was written in) enriches your points — but only if it explains a choice Priestley made. It must be woven in, not bolted on as a history lecture.

Bolted-on vs woven-in

❌ "The play was first staged in 1945. There had been two World Wars. Now back to the Inspector."

✔ "Writing in 1945, with the welfare state emerging, Priestley sets the play in 1912 so that hindsight exposes Birling's complacency, turning the Inspector into a voice for the collective responsibility of the new era."

Misconception: context is not a paragraph you paste in. One relevant clause that illuminates a method or theme beats a page of dates.

Exam technique

Planning under the clock

The extract-and-whole-text essay is closed book, so preparation matters. Spend the first 5 minutes planning — it saves time and lifts your mark:

  • Underline the key words in the question (the focus + the command word).
  • Jot 3 points — each linking a character or moment to a theme (responsibility, class, age).
  • Tag a short quote beside each point from your learned bank.
  • Write a one-line thesis, then write. Leave 2 minutes to check.

Closed book: you won't have the text in front of you, so learn short, flexible quotations in advance — "one body", "unsinkable", "fire and blood and anguish", "Girls of that class —".

Worked model · one paragraph

A paragraph in action

Watch how one paragraph fuses point, quote, method, effect and context:

Model paragraph

"Priestley uses the Inspector to voice his socialist conviction that society is one connected whole. When he declares that we are 'members of one body', the biological metaphor reframes the community as a single organism, so that harming one worker like Eva damages everyone — the exact opposite of Birling's earlier claim that a man must 'look after himself and his own'. Writing in 1945, as the welfare state was being built, Priestley makes the line sound less like idealism and more like a warning, reinforced by the apocalyptic triple 'fire and blood and anguish', which a war-weary audience would recognise as prophecy already fulfilled."

Spot the moves: point, short embedded quotes, method → effect ("biological metaphor… single organism"), and context woven in ("Writing in 1945…").

Avoid these traps

The five marks-killers

  • Story-telling: retelling the plot instead of arguing an answer to the question.
  • Feature-spotting: naming a device (dramatic irony, lighting) without explaining its effect.
  • Character as real people: forgetting they are Priestley's constructs serving a message.
  • Bolted-on context: a history block that doesn't illuminate a choice in the play.
  • Fat quotes: long copied lines instead of short, embedded ones.

Fix them all with one habit: every sentence should make a point, prove it with a short quote, or explain how Priestley's method creates an effect — always tied to his message.

Recap

What to take into the exam

Plot: 1912 Brumley · Birlings celebrate · Inspector Goole exposes each one's part in Eva Smith's death · cyclical, unresolved ending

Message: social responsibility — "we are members of one body"

Characters: Sheila & Eric change; Arthur, Sybil (& Gerald) do not — hope lies with the young

Context: written 1945, set 1912 → dramatic irony ("unsinkable"); Priestley the socialist vs Birling the capitalist

Devices: dramatic irony, the Inspector as morality device, lighting ("brighter and harder"), the cyclical structure, "fire and blood and anguish"

Essay: thesis → PEEL paragraphs → short embedded quotes → context woven in
