Mission 1

Three Jewels, three refuges

  • The Three Jewels (Pali Tiratana, Sanskrit Triratna): the Buddha, the Dhamma (teaching, truth) and the Sangha (community).
  • A refuge (sarana) is a shelter. Here: shelter from dukkha and the round of rebirth (samsara).
  • Going for refuge is the basic act that marks a person as a Buddhist, in every school.
  • A common picture: the Buddha is the doctor, the Dhamma the medicine, the Sangha the nurses and fellow patients who help one recover.
  • They are called jewels: rare, precious, and worth more than anything else one could rely on.
Mission 1Read the source

The refuge formula

In Pali, from the Khuddakapatha (Khp 1), chanted by Buddhists across the world:

  • Buddham saranam gacchami · Dhammam saranam gacchami · Sangham saranam gacchami
  • Literal rendering: "To the Buddha for refuge I go. To the Dhamma for refuge I go. To the Sangha for refuge I go."
  • Then repeated with dutiyam pi ("for a second time…") and tatiyam pi ("for a third time…").

Q1 Why might the formula be said three times?

Show answer

To make the commitment firm and deliberate, not a passing thought; repetition also marks it as a formal act.

Q2 Who is "I" here?

Show answer

The person committing: the formula is a personal choice, said aloud.

Mission 1

Refuge in the Buddha

  • Historical: Siddhattha Gotama, the teacher who found the path and showed it to others. Not a creator god; for most schools, a guide rather than a saviour who acts for us (Pure Land differs: A later screen).
  • Ideal: awakening itself, the goal anyone can reach. Refuge is in the qualities of a Buddha: wisdom, compassion, purity.
  • "Whoever sees the Dhamma sees me" (Samyutta Nikaya 22.87, paraphrase): the Buddha is met in his teaching.
  • Theravada: stresses the historical Buddha as teacher and model.
  • Mahayana: many Buddhas (e.g. Amitabha) and the Buddha-nature in all beings; the Buddha is also a reality beyond one human life.
Mission 1

Refuge in the Dhamma

  • Dhamma as teaching: the words of the Buddha: the Four Noble Truths, the path.
  • Dhamma as truth: the way things really are; ultimately nibbana, unconditioned reality, which the teaching points to.
  • Traditional qualities: well proclaimed; seen here and now; timeless; ehipassiko ("come and see"); leading onward; known by the wise for themselves.
  • The raft (Majjhima Nikaya 22): the teaching is like a raft for crossing a river, to be used, not carried on one's head after crossing.

Mission 1

Refuge in the Sangha

  • Noble Sangha (ariya-sangha): those who have reached at least the first stage of awakening (stream-entry). In Theravada the strict object of refuge.
  • Monastic Sangha: monks (bhikkhus) and nuns (bhikkhunis). They keep and teach the Dhamma; lay people support them with food and gifts; the monks give teaching and blessings in return.
  • Fourfold assembly: monks, nuns, laymen, laywomen.
  • All Buddhists past, present and future.
  • Spiritual friends: one's close companions in practice. The Buddha told Ananda that good friendship is "the whole of the holy life" (Samyutta Nikaya 45.2, paraphrase).
Mission 1

Going for refuge in practice

  • Daily puja: homage to the Buddha (Namo tassa…, three times), then the three refuges, often the Five Precepts. In Sri Lanka this is called pansil.
  • Bowing three times before a shrine: one bow for each jewel.
  • Ceremony: a new Buddhist may take the refuges and precepts formally before a monk or teacher.
  • Tibetan: refuge also in the lama (teacher); many make 100,000 refuge prostrations as a preliminary practice.
  • Pure Land: the nembutsu is a calling on, and refuge in, Amida (Amitabha) Buddha.
  • Triratna (founded by Sangharakshita): Going for Refuge is the central act; ordination is "effective going for refuge".
  • A ritual of identity: words, posture and repetition make the commitment public.

Which refuge is safe?

> "Men, driven by fear, go to many a refuge, to mountains and forests, to groves and sacred trees." (Dhammapada 188, Müller 1881)

> "He who takes refuge with Buddha, the Law, and the Church; he who, with clear understanding, sees the four holy truths" (Dhammapada 190, Müller 1881)

  • Müller's "the Law" is Dhamma; "the Church" is Sangha. Verse 192: that refuge is "the best refuge".
  • Buddhist analysis: worldly refuges, ancient (sacred trees) or modern (money, status), are impermanent (anicca), so they cannot give final security. They may still be wholesome supports.

Q1 What drives people to the first kind of refuge?

Show answer

Fear.

Q2 Why might a Buddhist say a bank account is a refuge like the "sacred trees"?

Show answer

It is relied on for safety, but it changes and cannot end dukkha.

Mission 2

Puja: acts of devotion

  • Puja (or vandana): devotion and respect, at a shrine at home or in a temple (vihara, wat).
  • Bowing three times: to the Buddha, the Dhamma and the Sangha.
  • Offerings and their meaning:
  • Flowers: fresh now, wilted soon: a reminder of anicca.
  • Candles or lamps: the light of wisdom dispelling ignorance.
  • Incense: the "fragrance" of good conduct spreading.
  • Chanting: homage ("Namo tassa bhagavato arahato samma-sambuddhassa": homage to the Blessed One, the Worthy One, the Fully Awakened One), the refuges and the precepts.
  • Walking clockwise around a shrine or stupa, keeping it on one's right, as a mark of respect.
Mission 2

Buddha images

  • The earliest Buddhist art showed no figure of the Buddha: only symbols (footprints, an empty seat, the Bodhi tree, the wheel).
  • Images appear around the 1st century CE (Gandhara, Mathura).
  • Mudras (hand gestures) carry meaning: earth-touching (the awakening), meditation, turning the wheel (teaching), fearlessness.
  • An image is often consecrated before use, e.g. an "eye-opening" ceremony in Sri Lanka.
  • Role: a reminder of the Buddha's qualities and a focus for the mind; treated with respect (shoes off, feet never pointed at it).
  • Mahayana shrines add other buddhas and bodhisattvas (Amitabha, Avalokitesvara/Guanyin, Tara).
Mission 2

Stupas

  • A stupa is a mound that holds relics: after the Buddha's death, his remains were divided and enshrined (Mahaparinibbana Sutta, DN 16).
  • Emperor Ashoka (3rd century BCE) is said to have built many; Sanchi (India) is a famous early example.
  • Names by region: chedi (Thailand), dagoba (Sri Lanka), pagoda (East Asia).
  • Devotees walk around it clockwise, offer flowers and lamps, and recollect the Buddha.

Theravada and Mahayana views of worship

  • Theravada: the Buddha has attained parinibbana: he does not hear or respond.
  • So devotion is respect and recollection (buddhanussati): it calms the mind, inspires practice and makes merit.
  • The Questions of King Milinda (paraphrase): honours paid to the Buddha bear fruit, though he does not accept them, as a fire that has gone out still helps those who make their own fire.
  • Many lay Theravadins also seek blessing and protection, e.g. through paritta (protective chanting).
  • Mahayana: buddhas and bodhisattvas are active now in many worlds and can respond to devotion.
  • E.g. Lotus Sutra ch. 25: calling on Avalokitesvara (Guanyin) brings help; Pure Land: reliance on Amitabha's vow.
  • Tibetan devotion: prostrations, mantras, prayer wheels; devotion also trains the mind.
Mission 2

Punya: merit

  • Punya (Sanskrit; Pali puñña): merit, the good kamma produced by wholesome actions.
  • Three bases of making merit: giving (dana), ethics (sila), meditation (bhavana).
  • Merit brings happiness, a better rebirth and good conditions for practice; it carries over into future lives.
  • Beyond merit: the awakened one is "above good and evil, above the bondage of both" (Dhammapada 412, Müller): no longer making kamma at all.
Mission 2

Sharing merit

  • Theravada: after giving, people share (dedicate) the merit, often to dead relatives, while monks chant; water is poured from a small jug into a bowl.
  • Source: the Tirokudda Sutta (Khuddakapatha 7, paraphrase): departed relatives wait outside the walls; gifts to the Sangha dedicated to them help them.
  • Kamma is personal: "by oneself one is purified … no one can purify another" (Dhammapada 165, Müller).
  • So many Theravada teachers explain: the dead (or living) person rejoices in the good deed (anumodana) and so makes merit of their own. Sharing merit is itself generous, so the giver loses nothing.
  • Mahayana: parinamana: bodhisattvas turn over their vast merit to all beings; practice ends with a dedication of merit.

Mission 3

The Tipitaka: three baskets

  • Pali Canon = Tipitaka ("three baskets"), the scripture of Theravada Buddhism.
  • Vinaya Pitaka: monastic discipline.
  • Sutta Pitaka: the Buddha's discourses (and some of his disciples').
  • Abhidhamma Pitaka: systematic analysis of the teaching ("higher Dhamma").
  • Pali: the language of these texts. Theravada tradition identifies it with the Buddha's own speech (Magadhi).
  • Status: for Theravada, buddhavacana, the word of the Buddha, and the final authority.
  • Mahayana Buddhists have other canons (Chinese, Tibetan) that include parallel early texts and Mahayana sutras.
Mission 3

From memory to palm leaf

  • First Council (Rajagaha), soon after the parinibbana: 500 arahants. Ananda recited the discourses ("Thus have I heard…"); Upali recited the discipline.
  • Oral transmission for about four centuries: groups of reciters (bhanakas) memorised whole collections, chanting together to check each other.
  • Memory aids still visible in the texts: repetition, numbered lists, set phrases, verse.
  • Second Council (Vesali, about a century later): disputes over monastic rules.
  • Third Council (Pataliputta, c. 250 BCE, under Emperor Ashoka), in the Theravada account; then the mission of Mahinda to Sri Lanka.
  • Written down at Aluvihara, Sri Lanka, in the 1st century BCE, when war and famine threatened the reciters.
  • Other early schools kept parallel collections (e.g. the Agamas preserved in Chinese). Much of their content matches the Pali discourses.
Mission 3

The Vinaya Pitaka

  • Rules for monks (bhikkhus) and nuns (bhikkhunis), each with the story of why it was made.
  • Includes the Patimokkha (227 rules for Theravada monks), recited every fortnight.
  • Procedures: ordination, the rains retreat, settling disputes.
  • Aim: a cohesive community that lives in harmony and earns the trust of lay supporters.
Mission 3Read the source

The Sutta Pitaka

  • Five collections (nikayas): Digha (long), Majjhima (middle-length), Samyutta (connected), Anguttara (numbered), Khuddaka (minor: includes the Dhammapada, Sutta Nipata, Jataka tales, the poems of the first monks and nuns).
  • Teachings adapted to each hearer: kings, farmers, brahmins, monks.
  • The search for Enlightenment: the Buddha's own quest (Majjhima Nikaya 26, 36), the Four Noble Truths, meditation guidance (Majjhima Nikaya 10).

> "Not to commit any sin, to do good, and to purify one's mind, that is the teaching of (all) the Awakened." (Dhammapada 183, Müller 1881)

Q1 Which collection contains this verse?

Show answer

The Khuddaka Nikaya, in the Sutta Pitaka.

Q2 The verse names three steps. What are they?

Show answer

Avoid evil, do good, purify the mind.

Mission 3

The Abhidhamma Pitaka

  • Seven books analysing experience into its ultimate elements (dhammas): consciousness, mental factors, matter, and nibbana.
  • Ultimate truth (described in dhammas) vs conventional truth (persons, things).
  • Is it the Buddha's own work?
  • Theravada tradition: the Buddha taught it to the gods, his mother among them, in the Tavatimsa heaven, and gave its outline to Sariputta.
  • Most historians: developed by monks over centuries after the Buddha. One book, the Kathavatthu, is itself credited to Moggaliputta Tissa at the Third Council.
  • Studied intensively in Myanmar; chanted at funerals in Thailand.
Mission 3

In worship and daily life

  • Paritta ("protection") chanting: monks chant suttas such as the Mangala Sutta (blessings), Karaniya Metta Sutta (loving-kindness) and Ratana Sutta at house blessings, illness, new ventures and festivals. A thread held during the chant is tied on wrists.
  • Funerals: monks chant passages on impermanence; in Thailand, from the Abhidhamma.
  • Uposatha days: lay people visit the temple, take precepts, hear a sermon based on a sutta.
  • Study and memory: novices learn chants; many lay people know Dhammapada verses; Myanmar holds national Tipitaka exams.
  • Meditation: guidance from suttas such as Majjhima Nikaya 10 (foundations of mindfulness).
  • Respect: texts are treated as precious objects, placed high, often on the shrine.
  • Most lay Buddhists meet the canon through hearing and chanting, not private reading.

Mission 4

The Lotus Sutra

  • Full title: Saddharmapundarika Sutra, "the Lotus of the True Dharma". A Mahayana text.
  • Composed in stages, roughly 1st century BCE to 2nd century CE, in India.
  • Kumarajiva's Chinese translation (406 CE) made it one of the most loved texts in East Asia: Tiantai (China), Tendai and Nichiren Buddhism (Japan).
  • Its big claims: there is really one vehicle (ekayana) to Buddhahood; all beings can become Buddhas; the Buddha is far greater than his one human life.
  • It teaches mainly through parables: stories that carry the doctrine.
Mission 4

Upaya: skilful means

  • Upaya (full form upaya-kausalya): skilful means, skill in adapting the teaching to the hearer.
  • A good teacher meets people where they are: a child, a scholar and a grieving parent need different words.
  • Lotus Sutra chapter 2 ("Skilful Means"): the Buddha's wisdom is too deep to be grasped at once, so he taught in many ways, to lead beings step by step (paraphrase).
  • So some teachings are provisional: true enough to help, but not the final word.
Mission 4Read the source

The burning house

Lotus Sutra, chapter 3 (paraphrase):

  • A rich old man owns a huge, decaying house with one narrow door. Fire breaks out.
  • His children are inside, absorbed in their games. They do not know what fire is, or fear it.
  • He calls them; they ignore him. So he says: the goat-carts, deer-carts and ox-carts you wanted are waiting outside the gate!
  • They rush out and are safe. Then he gives every child the same great carriage, drawn by white oxen: better than anything promised.
  • The Buddha asks Sariputra: did the father lie? Sariputra: no; he saved their lives, and that was his aim from the start.

Q1 Why did the father not simply tell the truth about the fire?

Show answer

The children did not understand fire, so the warning meant nothing to them; the promise of carts did.

Q2 Why does Sariputra say no falsehood was told?

Show answer

The father's purpose was their rescue, and he gave them more than he promised.

Mission 4

Decoding the parable

  • House on fire = samsara, burning with birth, old age, sickness, death and suffering.
  • Children = all beings, absorbed in pleasures, unaware of danger.
  • Father = the Buddha, who cares for all beings as his children.
  • Three carts = the three vehicles taught earlier: the way of disciples (sravaka), of solitary Buddhas (pratyekabuddha), and of bodhisattvas.
  • Great carriage = the one Buddha vehicle: all are finally led to full Buddhahood.

Mission 5

The first sermon

  • After his awakening, the Buddha walked to the Deer Park at Isipatana (Sarnath), near Varanasi (Benares), to his five former companions in asceticism.
  • The sermon is the Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta, "Setting the Wheel of the Dhamma in Motion" (Samyutta Nikaya 56.11).
  • It opens with the Middle Way: avoid both indulgence in sense pleasure and harsh self-torment.
  • Then it sets out the Four Noble Truths.
  • One listener, Kondanna, understood: whatever is subject to arising is subject to ceasing. He became the first disciple to "see the Dhamma".
Mission 5

Four truths, four tasks

  • In the sermon, the Buddha teaches:
  • Dukkha: there is suffering, unsatisfactoriness. Task: fully understand it.
  • Samudaya (origin): dukkha arises from craving (tanha). Task: abandon it.
  • Nirodha (cessation): dukkha ends when craving ends. Task: realise it.
  • Magga (path): the Noble Eightfold Path leads there. Task: develop it.
  • In the sutta the Buddha claims full awakening only after knowing each truth, its task, and that the task is done: twelve aspects in all.
  • They are "noble" (ariya) truths: truths as seen by the noble ones, who have realised them.
Mission 5

The doctor analogy

  • A traditional comparison treats the Buddha as a physician:
  • Illness = dukkha · Cause = tanha · Cure is possible = nirodha · Prescription = magga.
  • Buddhaghosa uses it in the Visuddhimagga (XVI): the truths are like a disease, its cause, its cure and the medicine.
  • What it teaches: the Buddha diagnoses in order to heal; the first truth is not the last word.
  • Its limits: the patient must take the medicine personally. No one can be cured by someone else's practice.

Mission 5

Three kinds of craving

  • Kama-tanha: craving for sense pleasures: food, comfort, sex, entertainment, possessions.
  • Bhava-tanha: craving for existence or becoming: to be someone, to be admired, to live on for ever.
  • Vibhava-tanha: craving for non-existence: to get rid of what we dislike, even to stop existing.
  • All three grasp at what is impermanent, so all three bring dukkha: the pleasure fades, the identity cracks, the escape never arrives.
  • Vibhava-tanha shows that pushing away is craving too, not freedom from it.
Mission 5

Nirodha and magga

  • Nirodha: the "cutting off" of craving through detachment: no longer gripping what changes. This is nibbana (see Mission 7).
  • Magga: the Noble Eightfold Path, in three trainings: wisdom (right view, right intention), ethics (right speech, action, livelihood), meditation (right effort, mindfulness, concentration). Full study.

Mission 6

What anatta denies

  • Anatta (Sanskrit anatman): not-self, the third mark of existence.
  • In the Brahmanical thought of the Buddha's day (e.g. the Upanishads), the atman is an eternal, unchanging self, the true owner of experience, one with ultimate reality (Brahman).
  • The Buddha taught that no such self is found in any part of experience: nothing permanent, independent and fully under our control.
  • Anatta does not say people do not exist. "Ruth" is a real, changing person; what is denied is a fixed core behind her.
  • Anatta is one of the clearest differences between Buddhist and Hindu views of the self.
Mission 6

The five khandhas

  • A person is analysed into five khandhas ("heaps", "aggregates"; Sanskrit skandhas):
  • rupa: form: the body and the physical world it meets.
  • vedana: feeling: each experience as pleasant, unpleasant or neutral.
  • sanna: perception: recognising and labelling ("red", "friend").
  • sankhara: mental formations: will, habits, intentions; this is where karma is made.
  • vinnana: consciousness: awareness through the six senses (the mind counts as the sixth).
  • Buddhists teach that all five are impermanent and depend on each other, and that none of them, and nothing beyond them, is a self.
Mission 6Read the source

The second sermon

Anattalakkhana Sutta ("Discourse on the Characteristic of Not-self"), Samyutta Nikaya 22.59, given to the five ascetics after the first sermon (paraphrase):

  • If form were self, it would not lead to affliction, and one could say: "Let my form be thus; let it not be thus." Because form is not-self, it leads to affliction and cannot be ordered about. The same holds for feeling, perception, formations and consciousness.
  • The Buddha asks: is form permanent or impermanent? Impermanent. Is what is impermanent dukkha or happiness? Dukkha. Is it fitting to regard what is impermanent, dukkha and subject to change as "this is mine, this I am, this is my self"? No.

Q1 What two tests for a self does the sutta use?

Show answer

A self would be under our control; a self would be permanent and not dukkha.

Q2 Which mark does the argument begin from?

Show answer

Anicca: it moves from impermanence to dukkha to not-self, as in another lesson.

Mission 6Read the source

Nagasena's chariot

The Questions of King Milinda (Milinda Panha), Book II, chapter 1, section 1 (paraphrase). The Greek king Milinda (Menander I, 2nd century BCE) meets the monk Nagasena.

  • Nagasena: "I am called Nagasena, but that is only a name, a way of speaking. No permanent individual is found here."
  • The king objects: then who keeps the precepts, who meditates, who reaches nibbana? Is the hair Nagasena? The body? Feeling, perception, formations, consciousness? All together? Something apart from them? To each, Nagasena says no. "Then 'Nagasena' is an empty sound!"
  • Nagasena asks how the king came. "By chariot." Is the pole the chariot? The axle? The wheels? The frame? The yoke? All together? Something apart? No to each. "Then 'chariot' is an empty sound!"
  • The king: "No: 'chariot' is the name used because of all these parts." Nagasena: "Just so: because of the body and the five khandhas, I am called Nagasena."
  • He quotes the nun Vajira (Samyutta Nikaya 5.10): as "chariot" is said when the parts come together, so "a being" is said when the khandhas are present.

Q1 Does Nagasena say that he does not exist?

Show answer

No: "Nagasena" is a correct name for a real, changing process; there is just no separate owner.

Mission 6

Not-self or no-self? Different Buddhist views

  • Mainstream Theravada: analysis finds no self at all; anatta is a statement about reality (see Steven Collins, 1951–2018, Selfless Persons, 1982).
  • "Not-self" as a strategy: some teachers, e.g. Thanissaro Bhikkhu (b. 1949), read anatta as a practice: stop identifying with anything, without asserting "there is no self" (the Buddha's silence in SN 44.10).
  • Personalists (Pudgalavada, early Indian schools): a "person" exists that is neither the same as nor different from the khandhas. Other schools criticised this as a disguised self.
  • Mahayana: some Buddha-nature texts speak of a "true self"; most Mahayana thinkers read this as skilful means, not an atman. Emptiness extends anatta to all things.
  • Dhammakaya (Thailand) teaches nibbana as true self (atta); many Thai scholar-monks, e.g. P. A. Payutto, reject this as contrary to the Pali Canon.

Mission 7

Blowing out

  • Nibbana (Pali; Sanskrit nirvana) means "blowing out" or "extinguishing", as a flame goes out.
  • What goes out are the three fires of greed, hatred and delusion. Samyutta Nikaya 38.1 (paraphrase): the destruction of greed, hatred and delusion: this is called nibbana.
  • It is the third noble truth, nirodha: craving "cut off" through detachment, letting go of what changes (see Mission 5).
  • It is not the destruction of a self: by anatta, there was no lasting self to destroy.
Mission 7

In this life and after death

  • Nibbana with remainder (sa-upadisesa): reached in this life. The fires are out, but the "remainder", the five khandhas, continues: the awakened one still has a body and feelings.
  • Nibbana without remainder (anupadisesa): at the death of an awakened one. The khandhas end and there is no rebirth.
  • Source: Itivuttaka 44 (paraphrase) names these two "elements" of nibbana.
  • The Buddha: awakening at Bodh Gaya (about age 35) = nibbana with remainder; death at Kusinara (about age 80) = parinibbana, final nibbana (Digha Nikaya 16).

Mission 7

Theravada and Mahayana emphases

  • Theravada: nibbana is the unconditioned, realised by the arhat; after parinibbana the awakened one is neither "somewhere" nor simply annihilated: the question does not apply.
  • Mahayana: a Buddha does not rest in a nirvana apart from the world but keeps helping beings: non-abiding nirvana (apratisthita-nirvana).
  • Nagarjuna (c. 2nd century CE): there is not the slightest difference between samsara and nirvana when both are seen as empty (Mulamadhyamakakarika 25, paraphrase;).
  • The Lotus Sutra treats the arhat's nirvana as a resting place on the way to Buddhahood (the "magic city").
  • Buddhist texts say nibbana's form cannot be described, but similes can point to its qualities (coolness, peace).
  • Both traditions agree: nibbana ends craving and dukkha; they differ on what follows for an awakened being.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Going for refuge to the Buddha, Dhamma and Sangha, chanted three times in Pali, marks a person as a Buddhist.
  • Buddha: historical teacher and ideal of awakening; Dhamma: teaching and ultimate truth; Sangha: noble, monastic, fourfold, all Buddhists, spiritual friends.

Mission 2

  • Puja, images and stupas focus devotion on the Buddha. Theravada: he does not respond; devotion trains the mind and makes merit. Mahayana: buddhas and bodhisattvas can respond.

Mission 3

  • The Tipitaka has three baskets: Vinaya (discipline), Sutta (discourses), Abhidhamma (analysis); for Theravada it is the word of the Buddha.
  • Oral for about four centuries, written down in Sri Lanka in the 1st century BCE; scholars debate how accurate a record it is.
  • It is used above all through chanting, sermons, study and meditation guidance.

Mission 4

  • Upaya (skilful means): the Buddha adapts his teaching to his hearers, so some teachings are provisional.
  • The burning house: the father lures his children out with promised carts, then gives all one great carriage; the three vehicles lead to the one Buddha vehicle.

Mission 5

  • The Deer Park sermon (SN 56.11): dukkha (understand), samudaya: craving (abandon), nirodha (realise), magga: the Eightfold Path (develop).
  • The doctor analogy: illness, cause, cure, prescription.
  • Three cravings: for sense pleasures, for existence, for non-existence.

Mission 6

  • Anatta: no permanent, independent self in the five khandhas (form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness) or beyond them.
  • The Buddha rejects eternalism and annihilationism; Nagasena's chariot shows "person" is a valid name for parts in relation, with no owner.

Mission 7

  • In Buddhist teaching, nibbana ("blowing out") is the end of greed, hatred and delusion: with remainder in this life, without remainder (parinibbana) at death.
  • It is unconditioned; questions about where an awakened one "goes" do not apply. Mahayana stresses non-abiding nirvana.
