Mission 1

Radicals

  • A radical is a species with an unpaired electron.
  • A dot marks the unpaired electron: Cl•, •CH₃.
  • The dot is not a charge. These radicals are neutral.
  • Odd number of outer electrons → one is unpaired. •CH₃: 4 + 3 = 7.
  • Radicals are highly reactive: the unpaired electron pairs up by forming a new bond.

Mission 1

Homolytic fission and fish-hook arrows

  • Homolytic fission: a bond breaks with one electron to each atom.
  • Two radicals form, and no ions.
  • A fish-hook arrow (one barb) moves one electron.
  • A curly arrow with two barbs moves an electron pair.
  • For Cl₂: two fish-hooks, each from the Cl–Cl bond to a different Cl atom.

Mission 1

Initiation

  • Initiation: UV light or heat splits the halogen: Br₂ → 2Br•.
  • The halogen–halogen bond breaks because it is the weakest bond present.
  • Data booklet: Br–Br 193 kJ mol⁻¹, C–C 346 kJ mol⁻¹. C–H is stronger still.
  • So initiation makes no H• and no alkyl radical.

Mission 1

Propagation: the chain

  • Propagation: radical + molecule → new molecule + new radical.
  • Br• removes an H atom from cyclohexane: C₆H₁₂ + Br• → •C₆H₁₁ + HBr.
  • Fish-hooks: one electron from Br• and one from the C–H bond form the H–Br bond.
  • The other C–H electron stays on carbon, giving the cyclohexyl radical, •C₆H₁₁.
  • Then •C₆H₁₁ + Br₂ → C₆H₁₁Br + Br•. Br• is regenerated, so the chain repeats.
  • Br• never swaps directly for H, and no H• forms.
Mission 1

Termination and the product mixture

  • Termination: two radicals combine. Radicals are removed; none are made.
  • Br• + Br• → Br₂ · •C₆H₁₁ + Br• → C₆H₁₁Br · •C₆H₁₁ + •C₆H₁₁ → C₁₂H₂₂.
  • Radicals are scarce, so termination is rare: most product forms in propagation.
  • Br• also attacks C₆H₁₁Br, so further substitution gives C₆H₁₀Br₂ and beyond.
  • The result is a mixture of products.

Mission 1

Why alkanes are so unreactive

  • C–C and C–H bonds are strong: breaking them needs a large energy input.
  • C and H have similar electronegativities: the bonds are essentially non-polar.
  • There are no significant δ+ or δ− sites, so polar reagents are not attracted.
  • Only very reactive species, such as radicals, attack alkanes.

Mission 2

Nucleophiles

  • A nucleophile donates both electrons of a new bond to its partner.
  • It needs an available electron pair, usually a lone pair: OH⁻, CN⁻, Cl⁻, Br⁻.
  • No charge needed: H₂O and NH₃ are neutral nucleophiles, with lone pairs on O and N.
  • Its partner, the electrophile, accepts the pair.

Mission 2

Heterolytic fission and curly arrows

  • Heterolytic fission: both bonding electrons go to one fragment, so ions form.
  • H–Br → H⁺ + Br⁻. The pair goes to Br, the more electronegative atom.
  • The fragment that takes the pair becomes negative.
  • A curly arrow (two barbs) moves an electron pair.
  • Start it at the pair's source, a lone pair or a bond. End it where the pair goes.

Mission 2

Nucleophilic substitution

  • In a halogenoalkane the C–X bond is polar: Cδ+–Xδ−.
  • The nucleophile donates a lone pair to the δ+ carbon.
  • The C–X pair moves onto X, which leaves as X⁻: the leaving group.
  • CH₃Cl + OH⁻ → CH₃OH + Cl⁻.
  • Two curly arrows: lone pair on O → C; C–Cl bond → Cl.

Mission check

?1-bromopropane is warmed with aqueous sodium hydroxide. Which equation represents the reaction that occurs?

OH⁻ donates a lone pair to the δ+ carbon; the C–Br pair leaves with Br as Br⁻. This is nucleophilic substitution, and the alcohol is propan-1-ol.

Mission 3

Electrophiles

  • An electrophile accepts both electrons of a new bond from a nucleophile.
  • It is electron-deficient: H⁺, NO₂⁺, or the δ+ atom of a polar molecule.
  • No charge needed: HBr (through Hδ+) and Br₂ act as electrophiles.
  • Pair donor = nucleophile. Pair acceptor = electrophile.

Mission 3

Why alkenes attract electrophiles

  • The C=C double bond is a region of high electron density.
  • It attracts electrophiles and donates an electron pair to them.
  • C=C is stronger than C–C. Alkenes react because of electron density, not weakness.
  • The reagent adds across C=C. The double bond becomes single; nothing is lost.
  • This is electrophilic addition.
Mission 3

Three addition reactions

  • Halogen: CH₂=CH₂ + Br₂ → CH₂BrCH₂Br. Fast in the dark, at room temperature.
  • Bromine turns orange-brown → colourless: the test for C=C.
  • Hydrogen halide: C₆H₁₀ + HBr → C₆H₁₁Br (cyclohexene → bromocyclohexane).
  • Water, with an acid catalyst (e.g. H₃PO₄): C₆H₁₀ + H₂O → C₆H₁₁OH (cyclohexanol).
  • With HX or H₂O, H goes to one carbon; X or OH to the other.

Mission check

?Why are alkenes susceptible to attack by electrophiles?

The electron-rich C=C attracts Br₂, which accepts a pair from it, so addition happens in the dark. An alkane has no such site: it waits for light to make Br• radicals.

Recap

The big ideas

  • Radicals have an unpaired electron. UV light or heat splits a halogen homolytically: fish-hook arrows.
  • Radical substitution: initiation, propagation (radical regenerated), termination. The product is a mixture.
  • Nucleophiles donate an electron pair; electrophiles accept one. A curly arrow runs from the pair to where it goes.
  • Halogenoalkanes undergo nucleophilic substitution. Alkenes add halogens, hydrogen halides and water.
