Judaism: Beliefs & Practices

This mini-lesson works through the Edexcel GCSE Religious Studies Judaism option: the core Jewish beliefs — the one God, the covenants, the mitzvot, the sanctity of life — and the key Jewish practices such as Shabbat, synagogue worship, kosher food laws, rites of passage and festivals.

The big idea

The heart of Jewish belief: one God

The foundation of Judaism is monotheism — the belief that there is one God. Jews teach that God is:

  • One and unique — there is only one God (declared in the Shema).
  • The Creator — the maker of the whole universe.
  • The Lawgiver — who gives the mitzvot (commandments) to live by.
  • The Judge — who is just and holds people accountable for their actions.

Spec focus: this Judaism option asks you to describe and explain Jewish beliefs (the nature of God, the covenants, the mitzvot, the sanctity of life, the Messiah and life after death) and Jewish practices (Shabbat, worship, kosher laws, rites of passage and festivals). Out of reverence, many Jews write "G-d" rather than the full word.

Key vocabulary

Words you must be able to define

  • Monotheism — belief in one God; the foundation of Judaism.
  • Shema — the central Jewish declaration that God is One (Deuteronomy 6:4).
  • Covenant — a solemn agreement (promise) between God and the Jewish people.
  • Mitzvot — the commandments (duties) God gives; there are traditionally 613.
  • Tenakh — the Jewish scriptures; the Torah (the first five books) is the most sacred part.

Common mix-up: the Torah is the first five books (the teaching of Moses); the whole Jewish Bible is called the Tenakh. And a mitzvah is a single commandment — mitzvot is the plural.

The covenants

The covenant: God's promise to the Jewish people

A covenant is a solemn agreement between God and the Jewish people. Two covenants are central in Judaism: the covenant with Abraham and the covenant with Moses at Mount Sinai.

Scripture — the covenant with Abraham: "I will establish my covenant... to be God to you and to your descendants after you." (Genesis 17:7) At Sinai, God gives Moses the Torah, including the Ten Commandments.
The mitzvot

The 613 mitzvot and the Ten Commandments

The mitzvot are the commandments God gives in the Torah. Traditionally there are 613 of them. They include both moral commands (how to treat people) and ritual commands (how to worship). They also fall into two groups:

  • Mitzvot between humans and God — e.g. keeping Shabbat, prayer, kosher laws.
  • Mitzvot between people and other people — e.g. not stealing, honesty, caring for the poor.

The Ten Commandments (given at Sinai) are among the most important mitzvot. They too cover duties to God (e.g. "have no other gods before me") and duties to others (e.g. "do not murder", "do not steal", "honour your father and mother").

Sanctity of life

Pikuach nefesh — the sanctity of life

Judaism teaches the sanctity of life: because God is the Creator, human life is sacred and precious. This leads to pikuach nefesh — the principle that saving a life overrides almost every other law, including keeping Shabbat.

The teaching: "Whoever saves a single life is considered to have saved a whole world" (Mishnah, Sanhedrin 4:5) — a classic statement of the sanctity of life.

Messiah & the afterlife

The Messiah and life after death

Many Jews believe in a future Messiah (Hebrew: Mashiach, "anointed one") who will bring a messianic age of peace and gather the Jewish people. Beliefs vary: some await a person, others a future age of peace.

Judaism also has beliefs about life after death — such as Olam Ha-Ba ("the world to come") and, in traditional belief, the future resurrection of the dead. However, Judaism keeps its main focus on living a good life now, in this world.

Remember: Jewish beliefs about the afterlife are less fixed than beliefs about the one God. The stress is on how you live this life — keeping the mitzvot and treating others well — rather than on detailed teaching about heaven.
Practices — Shabbat

Shabbat — the weekly day of rest

Shabbat (the Sabbath) is the Jewish day of rest, kept every week from Friday sunset to Saturday nightfall. It recalls that God rested on the seventh day of creation and is one of the Ten Commandments ("Remember the Sabbath day, to keep it holy").

  • Work is set aside so the day can be given to rest, family and God.
  • It begins with the lighting of Shabbat candles and a special family meal.
  • Many Jews attend the synagogue for worship on Shabbat.

Careful: Shabbat is about rest and holiness, not just "doing nothing" — it is a joyful, sacred day set apart. And remember pikuach nefesh: if a life is in danger, saving it comes before Shabbat rules.

Worship

The synagogue, prayer and the Torah

The synagogue is the Jewish place of worship, study and community. At its heart is the Ark, which holds the Torah scrolls. Jews worship through prayer (often using a prayer book, the siddur) and by reading from the scriptures.

The Tenakh is the whole Jewish Bible; the Torah (its first five books, the teaching of Moses) is the most sacred part. The Torah scroll is treated with great reverence and read aloud in the synagogue.

Prayer: Jews may pray at home and in the synagogue. The Shema and the Amidah are central prayers, and many Jews pray facing towards Jerusalem.

Food laws

Kashrut — keeping kosher

Many Jews follow kashrut, the Jewish food laws. Food that is allowed is called kosher ("fit"); food that is forbidden is treifah. These laws come from the Torah and are a way of obeying God in everyday life.

  • Only certain animals may be eaten — e.g. animals that chew the cud and have split hooves; pork and shellfish are forbidden.
  • Permitted meat must be slaughtered in the correct, humane way (shechitah).
  • Meat and dairy are not eaten together, and kitchens may keep them separate.

Why it matters: keeping kosher makes eating an act of obedience to God, bringing holiness into an ordinary daily routine — a mitzvah between the person and God.

Rites of passage

Brit Milah and Bar / Bat Mitzvah

Two important rites of passage mark a Jew's life:

  • Brit Milah — the circumcision of baby boys, usually on the 8th day. It is the sign of the covenant with Abraham (Genesis 17).
  • Bar / Bat Mitzvah — "son / daughter of the commandment". At 13 (boys) or 12 (girls) a young person becomes responsible for keeping the mitzvot, often reading from the Torah in the synagogue.

These moments connect each new generation to the covenant and to a life of keeping the mitzvot.

Memory hook: Brit Milah = the baby boy enters the covenant; Bar / Bat Mitzvah = the young adult takes on the mitzvot for themselves.

Why it matters

How Jewish beliefs shape practice

Exam questions often ask how beliefs affect the way Jews live. Jewish beliefs and practices fit tightly together:

  • Because God is One, Jews declare this daily in the Shema and pray to the one God.
  • Because of the covenant, boys have Brit Milah as its sign, and Jews keep the mitzvot.
  • Because God is the Lawgiver, Jews keep Shabbat and follow kosher food laws as obedience.
  • Because of the sanctity of life (pikuach nefesh), saving a life comes before other duties.
  • Because God is the Creator and Redeemer, festivals like Pesach remember God's saving acts.

Recap

The Judaism essentials

One God: monotheism — God as One, Creator, Lawgiver and Judge; the Shema (Deut. 6:4).

Covenant: with Abraham (Genesis 17) and with Moses at Sinai (Torah, Ten Commandments).

Mitzvot: 613 commandments — moral & ritual; between humans & God and between people.

Sanctity of life: pikuach nefesh — saving a life overrides almost all other laws.

Messiah & afterlife: a future messianic age; Olam Ha-Ba, resurrection — focus is on this life.

Practices: Shabbat, synagogue & prayer, Tenakh & Torah, kosher (kashrut).

Rites & festivals: Brit Milah, Bar/Bat Mitzvah; Rosh Hashanah, Yom Kippur, Pesach, Sukkot.
