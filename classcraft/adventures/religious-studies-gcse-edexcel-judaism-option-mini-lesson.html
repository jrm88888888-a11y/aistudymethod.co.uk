Mission 1

Mission 1: Jews say "the LORD is One" twice every day. What does that one word tell them about God?

Make a guess: Jews say "the LORD is One" twice every day. What does that one word tell them about God?

Mission 1

One

  • Judaism is monotheistic: there is one God, and nothing else is divine.
  • God is a unity: not divided into parts or persons, and with no partner or rival.
  • The central statement is the Shema: "HEAR, O ISRAEL: THE LORD OUR GOD, THE LORD IS ONE." (Deuteronomy 6:4)
  • The first of the Ten Commandments in Jewish counting: "I am the LORD thy God" (Exodus 20:2).
  • Maimonides (1138–1204) made God's unity the second of his Thirteen Principles of Faith (Commentary on the Mishnah, c. 1168).
  • Your spec calls God the Almighty.
Mission 1Read the source

The Shema

> "HEAR, O ISRAEL: THE LORD OUR GOD, THE LORD IS ONE. And thou shalt love the LORD thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy might." (Deuteronomy 6:4–5)

  • The passage continues: keep these words on your heart; teach them to your children; speak of them at home and on the way; bind them on your hand and between your eyes (tefillin); write them on your doorposts (mezuzah) (Deuteronomy 6:6–9, paraphrase).
  • Jews recite the Shema morning and evening, and ideally as their last words.

Q1 What response to the one God does verse 5 ask for?

Show answer

Love with the whole self: heart, soul and might.

Q2 Name two ways verses 6–9 keep God's oneness in daily life.

Show answer

Teaching children; tefillin; mezuzah on the doorpost.

Mission 1

Creator

  • "IN THE beginning God created the heaven and the earth." (Genesis 1:1)
  • Most Jews believe God created from nothing, freely and with purpose; the world is good.
  • God rested on the seventh day (Genesis 2:2–3): the origin of Shabbat.
  • Genesis 2:15 gives humans the task of looking after God's world: "to dress it and to keep it".
  • Importance today: blessings (berakhot) before food thank the Creator; Shabbat remembers creation each week.
Mission 1

Humans in creation

  • "And God created man in His own image, in the image of God created He him; male and female created He them." (Genesis 1:27)
  • Genesis 2: "the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life" (Genesis 2:7). God plants a garden, places the man there, gives him a command and makes a partner for him: a Creator who is close and personal.
  • "to dress it and to keep it" (Genesis 2:15): humans are stewards of God's world.

Mission 1

Law-Giver

  • Jews believe God gave the Torah to Moses at Mount Sinai: teaching and law for how to live.
  • The rabbis counted 613 mitzvot (commandments) in the Torah: 248 positive and 365 negative.
  • The law is part of the covenant: Jews believe God freed Israel and gave the law; Israel agreed to keep it.
  • Importance today: kashrut, Shabbat, prayer, charity and ethics are ways of obeying the Law-Giver. Keeping mitzvot is a response of love, not only duty.
Mission 1

Judge: just, loving and merciful

  • In Jewish belief:
  • Judge: God judges people's actions with perfect justice. Abraham asks: "shall not the judge of all the earth do justly?" (Genesis 18:25)
  • Merciful and loving: "The LORD, the LORD, God, merciful and gracious, long-suffering, and abundant in goodness and truth" (Exodus 34:6). This begins the Thirteen Attributes of Mercy, recited on festivals.
  • A rabbinic teaching: God created the world with both justice and mercy; with justice alone the world could not survive, with mercy alone wrong would run free (Genesis Rabbah 12:15, paraphrase).
  • Justice and mercy together: wrong matters, and God forgives those who return.
Quick check
?At Rosh Hashanah, Jews believe God judges each person, yet they spend the next ten days praying and asking forgiveness. What does this show about how Jews understand God as Judge?
Mission 1Evaluate

"For Jews, God as Law-Giver is the most important belief about God."

  • For: the Torah and 613 mitzvot shape every day: food, Shabbat, prayer, ethics. The covenant at Sinai defines the Jewish people. Judaism is often described as a religion of practice more than creed.
  • Against: the Shema puts oneness first, and all else depends on it; Maimonides' first principles are God's existence and unity. Without the Creator, there would be no one to give the law.
  • Against: many Reform and Liberal Jews see ethics and God's justice and mercy as central, more than detailed law.
  • Another view: the attributes belong together: the One Creator gives law and judges with mercy.
  • Judge it by: what Jews recite daily; what most shapes Jewish life; Orthodox and Reform views; whether attributes can be ranked.
Mission check

?A Jewish family moves into a new home. Before unpacking, they fix a mezuzah to the doorpost, with the words of the Shema handwritten inside. What belief are they most clearly expressing?

For Jews, "One" means more than a number: one God, Creator, Law-Giver and Judge, to be loved with the whole of life.

Mission 2

Mission 2: If God fills the whole universe, how could God also dwell in one tent in the desert?

Make a guess: If God fills the whole universe, how could God also dwell in one tent in the desert?

Mission 2

"Dwelling": what Shekhinah means

  • Shekhinah comes from the Hebrew root sh-k-n, "to dwell" or "to rest".
  • It means God's divine presence: God's presence made known, or felt, in a particular place or among people.
  • The same root gives mishkan, the portable sanctuary (Tabernacle) of the desert.
  • "And let them make Me a sanctuary, that I may dwell among them." (Exodus 25:8)
  • The word Shekhinah is not in the Tenakh. It comes from the rabbis, in the Aramaic translations of the Torah, the Mishnah and the Talmud.
  • Key idea: God is transcendent, beyond the universe, yet immanent, present within it. Shekhinah names that nearness.
  • Your spec calls God the Almighty.
Mission 2

Cloud, fire and glory

  • In the Torah, God's presence is shown through signs, never through a visible form.
  • Pillar of cloud by day and pillar of fire by night lead Israel out of Egypt (Exodus 13:21–22).
  • Sinai: "the glory of the LORD abode upon mount Sinai, and the cloud covered it six days" (Exodus 24:16).
  • The Tabernacle: the cloud covers the tent and the glory (Hebrew kavod) fills it (Exodus 40:34–35).
  • The journey: the cloud stays over the Tabernacle; when it lifts, Israel moves on (Numbers 9:15–23).
  • The rabbis later used "Shekhinah" for these moments of God's presence.
  • Significance: God is with the people, guiding and protecting them.

> "So it was alway: the cloud covered it, and the appearance of fire by night." (Numbers 9:16)

Glory in the Temple

Solomon dedicates the Temple in Jerusalem:

> "Now when Solomon had made an end of praying, the fire came down from heaven, and consumed the burnt-offering and the sacrifices; and the glory of the LORD filled the house." (2 Chronicles 7:1)

> "And the priests could not enter into the house of the LORD, because the glory of the LORD filled the LORD'S house." (2 Chronicles 7:2)

> "And all the children of Israel looked on, when the fire came down, and the glory of the LORD was upon the house; and they bowed themselves with their faces to the ground upon the pavement, and prostrated themselves, and gave thanks unto the LORD; 'for He is good, for His mercy endureth for ever.'" (2 Chronicles 7:3)

  • Interpretations: God accepts the Temple and the offerings; the Shekhinah now rests in the Holy of Holies, as it rested on the Tabernacle; the right response is awe and thanks.
  • Some read it more literally (God's presence truly dwelt there); others more figuratively (a sign of God's favour; Solomon himself says heaven cannot contain God, 1 Kings 8:27).

Q1 What two responses do the people show?

Show answer

Bowing in awe and giving thanks.

Q2 How does this link back to Exodus 40?

Show answer

In both, the glory fills the sanctuary so the priests or Moses cannot enter.

Mission 2

After the Temple: Shekhinah with the people

  • The First Temple was destroyed in 586 BCE, the Second in 70 CE. Where is the Shekhinah now?
  • The rabbis taught that the Shekhinah went into exile with Israel wherever they were scattered (Talmud, Megillah 29a, paraphrase).
  • It rests where people study Torah together (Mishnah, Avot 3:2 and 3:6, paraphrase) and where ten gather to pray, a minyan (Talmud, Berakhot 6a, paraphrase).
  • It rests on joy in doing a mitzvah, not on sadness or laziness (Talmud, Shabbat 30b, paraphrase).
  • A midrash says the Shekhinah has never left the Western Wall (paraphrase).
  • Importance today: God is close without a Temple: in the synagogue, the home, study, prayer and kindness; comfort in exile and suffering.
Quick check

Mission 2Diversity

What is the Shekhinah?

  • God's own presence: many rabbis use Shekhinah simply as another way of saying God (e.g. Nachmanides, 1194–1270).
  • Something God creates: Saadia Gaon (882–942) and Maimonides (1138–1204) taught that the "glory" seen in the cloud or Temple was a created light, a sign of God, not God's being; God does not move or take up space.
  • Hasidic Jews (an Orthodox movement), drawing on Kabbalah (Jewish mysticism), stress the Shekhinah present in everything, met through joyful prayer.
  • Different forms of Orthodox Judaism: Hasidic Jews stress the Shekhinah in everything (above). Rationalist (Modern Orthodox and many yeshiva-based Haredi) Jews often follow Maimonides, stressing presence through Torah study and mitzvot rather than mystical union.
  • Reform and Liberal Jews often see Shekhinah as a way of speaking about God's nearness, felt in community and justice.
Quick check
?A Hasidic Jew says, "The Shekhinah is in every corner of creation, waiting to be found in joy." A rationalist follower of Maimonides says, "The glory in the Temple was a created light; God does not occupy space." Which statement is accurate?
Mission 2Evaluate

"Without the Temple, the Shekhinah has no importance for Jews today."

  • For: in the Tenakh the glory rests on the Tabernacle and Temple (Exodus 40; 2 Chronicles 7). The Holy of Holies no longer exists. Many Jews pray for the Temple's rebuilding.
  • Against: the rabbis taught that the Shekhinah went into exile with Israel and rests on prayer, study and the minyan. It gives comfort that God is near in every place and in suffering.
  • Against: Kabbalah, Hasidism and Shabbat keep the idea alive; Reform Jews speak of God's nearness in community.
  • Another view: some rationalists say the idea was always a way of describing God's providence, which is unchanged.
  • Judge it by: the Tenakh or later teaching; Orthodox, Hasidic and Reform views; how far it shapes worship today.
Mission check

?Ten Jewish students in a university library form a minyan and pray the afternoon service there together. According to rabbinic teaching, what can they believe about the Shekhinah?

The same God who fills the universe is believed to make that presence known among people: once in cloud and Temple, now in prayer, study and joy.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Jews pray every day for the Messiah. Who do they expect, and what would change in the world?

Make a guess: Jews pray every day for the Messiah. Who do they expect, and what would change in the world?

Mission 3

"Anointed one"

  • Messiah comes from Hebrew mashiach, "anointed one".
  • In the Tenakh, kings and priests were anointed with oil to show God had chosen them.
  • "Samuel took the horn of oil, and anointed him" (1 Samuel 16:13): this is David, Israel's ideal king.
  • So the Messiah is expected to be a king from David's family, chosen by God.
  • The Greek translation of mashiach is christos, which gives the English word "Christ".
Mission 3

Where the hope came from

  • After David's kingdom fell and the people went into exile, the prophets promised a future king who would restore Israel.
  • The hope grew stronger after the Second Temple was destroyed (70 CE).
  • Jewish history includes men some hailed as Messiah, such as Bar Kokhba (revolt against Rome, 132–135 CE) and Shabbetai Tzvi (1666). They did not bring the Messianic Age, so Judaism did not accept them.
  • Jews believe Elijah the prophet will come first to announce him: "Behold, I will send you Elijah the prophet before the coming of the great and terrible day of the LORD." (Malachi 4:5; 3:23 in Hebrew Bibles)
  • At the Pesach seder a cup is poured for Elijah and the door is opened for him.
Mission 3Read the source

A righteous shoot for David

> "Behold, the days come, saith the LORD, that I will raise unto David a righteous shoot, and he shall reign as king and prosper, and shall execute justice and righteousness in the land." (Jeremiah 23:5)

  • "In his days Judah shall be saved, and Israel shall dwell safely" (Jeremiah 23:6).
  • Verses 7–8: people will praise God for bringing Israel back "from all the countries whither I had driven them"; "they shall dwell in their own land" (Jeremiah 23:8).

Q1 From whose family will the king come?

Show answer

David's: "a righteous shoot".

Q2 Name two things he will bring.

Show answer

Justice and righteousness; safety; the return of the exiles to their land.

Mission 3

Who is the Messiah?

  • In Jewish belief, a human being, born normally, from the family of David; not divine, and not a saviour from sin.
  • Wise and God-fearing: "the spirit of wisdom and understanding" will rest on him (Isaiah 11:2).
  • A leader who keeps and teaches the Torah.
  • He will gather the exiles to the Land of Israel, rebuild the Temple in Jerusalem and bring peace.
  • Maimonides (1138–1204): if a person does all this, he is certainly the Messiah; the world will otherwise keep its normal course (Mishneh Torah, Laws of Kings 11–12, paraphrase).

Mission 3Read the source

Visions of the Messianic Age

  • Peace: "they shall beat their swords into plowshares … neither shall they learn war any more" (Micah 4:3).
  • Harmony: "the wolf shall dwell with the lamb" (Isaiah 11:6); "the earth shall be full of the knowledge of the LORD" (Isaiah 11:9).

Q1 What will happen to weapons?

Show answer

They become farming tools: war ends.

Q2 Who benefits: Israel only, or all nations?

Show answer

All nations: "many nations shall go", Micah 4:2.

Mission 3

The Messianic Age, and whose job it is

  • The Messianic Age: a time of world peace, justice, the return of the exiles, the Temple rebuilt, and all people knowing God.
  • Many Orthodox Jews link it with the end of days, followed by the resurrection of the dead and judgement.
  • Human responsibility: Jews can help bring it about by keeping the mitzvot and repairing the world (tikkun olam).
  • A rabbinic teaching: if all Israel kept two Shabbats properly, they would be redeemed at once (Talmud Shabbat 118b, paraphrase).
  • Praying for it: the daily Amidah asks God to restore David's line; many Jews sing Maimonides' twelfth principle: "though he tarry, I will wait daily for his coming".
  • Your spec stresses the Jewish responsibility to bring the Messianic Age about.
Mission 3Diversity

Orthodox and Reform views

  • Orthodox: a personal Messiah, a special person sent by God; Jews pray for his coming daily. His arrival begins the end of days.
  • Hasidic groups such as Chabad stress that every mitzvah brings the Messiah closer.
  • Orthodox Jews differ on the State of Israel: a first step towards redemption (Religious Zionists) or something only the Messiah can bring (many Haredim).
  • Reform: most expect a Messianic Age, not a single person. "Ourselves": humans build it through justice and tikkun olam. Many Reform prayer books changed "a redeemer" to "redemption".
  • Many Jews, of all groups, focus on living by the mitzvot now rather than guessing when the Messiah will come.
Quick check
?Many Reform Jews do not pray for a personal Messiah. Which belief do most of them hold instead?
Mission 3Evaluate

"Belief in the Messiah is not important for Jews today."

  • For: Judaism stresses deeds: what matters is keeping the mitzvot now. Reform Jews do not await a person. Many Jews of all groups rarely speculate about the end.
  • Against: Maimonides made the Messiah a principle of faith (the twelfth); Orthodox Jews pray for him daily in the Amidah.
  • Against: hope for the Messianic Age gave Jews hope through exile and persecution, and drives tikkun olam for Reform Jews too.
  • Judge it by: a person or an age? Orthodox and Reform views; what Jews pray for; whether hope for the future shapes life now.
Mission check

?A Jewish youth group in Glasgow plants trees with Muslim and Christian students and runs a peace project, saying "We are helping to bring the Messianic Age." Which view of the Messiah is closest to theirs?

Jews await a human Messiah of David's line, or a Messianic Age; either way, peace for all nations is the goal, and deeds now help bring it.

Mission 4

Mission 4: God promised Abraham a land and a family. Why does that old promise still shape Jewish life today?

Make a guess: God promised Abraham a land and a family. Why does that old promise still shape Jewish life today?

Mission 4

Abraham, the first patriarch

  • Abraham (first called Abram) is the first patriarch (founding father) of the Jewish people: Avraham Avinu, "Abraham our father".
  • His family came from Ur and settled in Haran (Genesis 11:31).
  • A famous midrash says the young Abram smashed the idols in his father's shop: he had realised there is one God (Genesis Rabbah 38:13, paraphrase).
  • Genesis tells how, at 75, God called him to leave Haran for Canaan (Genesis 12:4). He went, with his wife Sarai (later Sarah).
  • Jews see him as the model of faith and obedience: he trusted God before he knew where he was going.
Mission 4Read the source

The call of Abraham

> "Now the LORD said unto Abram: 'Get thee out of thy country, and from thy kindred, and from thy father's house, unto the land that I will show thee. And I will make of thee a great nation, and I will bless thee, and make thy name great; and be thou a blessing.'" (Genesis 12:1–2)

  • Verse 3 ends: "in thee shall all the families of the earth be blessed" (Genesis 12:3).
  • Three promises: a land, a great nation (descendants), and blessing, for Abraham and through him for the world.

Mission 4

What a covenant is

  • A covenant (Hebrew brit) is a binding agreement between God and people, with promises and duties on both sides.
  • God's side: land, many descendants, and a lasting relationship: "to be a God unto thee and to thy seed after thee" (Genesis 17:7).
  • Abraham's side: faith in the one God, walking in God's ways ("walk before Me, and be thou wholehearted", Genesis 17:1) and circumcision of every male.
  • History: first made in Genesis 15 ("the covenant between the pieces"): God shows Abram the stars ("So shall thy seed be", Genesis 15:5) and promises the land (15:18).
  • Renewed in Genesis 17, with new names: Abram becomes Abraham, "father of a multitude of nations"; Sarai becomes Sarah.
  • Jews believe it is everlasting and passes down the generations: to Isaac, to Jacob, to all Jews today.
Mission 4Read the source

The covenant of circumcision

> "And I will give unto thee, and to thy seed after thee, the land of thy sojournings, all the land of Canaan, for an everlasting possession; and I will be their God." (Genesis 17:8)

> "This is My covenant, which ye shall keep, between Me and you and thy seed after thee: every male among you shall be circumcised." (Genesis 17:10)

Q1 What does God promise in verse 8?

Show answer

The whole land of Canaan, for ever, and to be their God.

Q2 What is the sign of the covenant?

Show answer

Circumcision of every male.

Mission 4

Brit milah today

  • Brit milah ("covenant of circumcision"): a baby boy is circumcised on his eighth day, as Genesis 17:12 commands.
  • It is carried out by a trained mohel, with blessings, and the boy receives his Hebrew name.
  • It goes ahead even on Shabbat or Yom Kippur; it is delayed only if the baby's health requires it.
  • It marks the child as part of the covenant from birth: Jewish identity is a family and community bond, not only a personal choice.
  • Girls are welcomed into the covenant with naming ceremonies (e.g. zeved habat or simchat bat), held in Orthodox, Reform and Liberal communities.
Quick check
?Why do most Jews hold a brit milah for a baby boy on his eighth day?
Mission 4

The Promised Land

  • To whom? "Unto thy seed will I give this land" (Genesis 12:7): Abraham's descendants through Isaac and Jacob (Israel).
  • In return for what? Faithfulness to the covenant: trusting God and keeping God's ways.
  • Canaan becomes the Land of Israel (Eretz Yisrael). Later, Moses leads the people towards it.
  • History: the Temple in Jerusalem; exile to Babylon (586 BCE); return; the Romans destroy the Second Temple (70 CE). Most Jews then lived in the diaspora (outside the land) for nearly 1,900 years.
  • Through exile, Jews kept the hope of return in prayer.
Mission 4

Jerusalem and the land in Jewish life

  • Jews pray the Amidah facing Jerusalem.
  • "If I forget thee, O Jerusalem, let my right hand forget her cunning." (Psalm 137:5)
  • A glass is broken at weddings to remember the destroyed Temple.
  • The Pesach seder and Yom Kippur end with "Next year in Jerusalem".
  • The Western Wall (Kotel), part of the Temple Mount's retaining wall, is the holiest place where Jews regularly pray today.
  • Some mitzvot (e.g. resting the land every seventh year) apply only in the Land of Israel.
Mission 4Diversity

The State of Israel today

  • The State of Israel was founded in 1948. Jews disagree about what it means religiously.
  • Religious Zionists: the State is "the first flowering of our redemption" (Prayer for the State of Israel); living in the land is a mitzvah; many make aliyah (move there).
  • Secular Zionists: Israel is a national homeland and a safe refuge after centuries of persecution and the Shoah; the reason is history and peoplehood more than God.
  • Many Haredi (strictly Orthodox) Jews: the land is holy, but full return belongs to the Messiah. Some cooperate with the State without calling it religious; a small minority (e.g. Neturei Karta) reject it.
  • Reform: early Reform (Pittsburgh, 1885) gave up hope of return; since the 1930s Reform has supported a Jewish homeland, with emphasis on justice and equal rights.
  • Many Jews support Israel while disagreeing with particular governments; the land is also holy to Christians and Muslims.
Quick check
?Religious Zionists say special prayers of thanks on Israel's Independence Day. Which belief best explains this?
Mission 4Evaluate

"The Promised Land is the most important part of the covenant with Abraham."

  • For: land is promised again and again (Genesis 12:7, 15:18, 17:8) as "an everlasting possession". Jews prayed for return for 1,900 years. Religious Zionists see living there as a mitzvah.
  • Against: the first and lasting promise is relationship: "I will be their God" (17:8). Jews kept the covenant through brit milah, Torah and prayer for centuries outside the land.
  • Against: many Haredi Jews leave the land's full restoration to the Messiah; many Reform Jews stress the blessing to "all the families of the earth" (12:3) and ethics.
  • Judge it by: what Genesis repeats most; what Jews actually do every day; whether the parts of the covenant can be separated.
Mission check

?A Jewish girl in Toronto has never been to Israel and has no plans to move there. At her family's Pesach seder she joins in with "Next year in Jerusalem". What does this most clearly show?

Jews see the covenant as a two-way bond: God promised land, descendants and blessing; Abraham's family answered with faith, brit milah and hope.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Jews believe in one God (Shema, Deuteronomy 6:4): Creator (Genesis 1–2), Law-Giver (Torah, Exodus 20) and Judge, both just and merciful (Genesis 18:25; Exodus 34:6).

Mission 2

  • Shekhinah ("dwelling") is God's divine presence, shown in the Torah by cloud, fire and glory (Exodus 13, 24, 40; Numbers 9) and in the Temple (2 Chronicles 7:1–3).
  • After the Temple, the rabbis taught that the Shekhinah is with the people in exile, in prayer, study and joyful mitzvot.
  • Jews differ: God's own presence, or a created light (Saadia, Maimonides).

Mission 3

  • Mashiach means "anointed one": Jews expect a human king from David's family who will bring peace, gather the exiles and rebuild the Temple (Jeremiah 23:5–8).
  • In Jewish belief, the Messianic Age is a time of world peace and knowledge of God (Micah 4:3); Elijah will announce it.
  • Orthodox Jews pray for a personal Messiah; many Reform Jews hope for a Messianic Age built by human effort.

Mission 4

  • Jews believe God called Abraham (Genesis 12:1–3) and made an everlasting covenant: land, descendants and blessing, in return for faith in the one God.
  • Circumcision (brit milah) on the eighth day is the sign of the covenant (Genesis 17).
  • The land and Jerusalem remain central in Jewish prayer; Jews differ on what the State of Israel means religiously.
