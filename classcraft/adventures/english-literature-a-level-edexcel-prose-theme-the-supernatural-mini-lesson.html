Prose Theme: The Supernatural

Component 2 is the Prose paper: one comparative essay on two thematically linked texts, at least one written pre-1900. This lesson teaches the theme of the Supernatural as a set of conventions and critical frames, plus the comparative method — so it holds for any pairing your centre studies.

  • One comparative essay linking two prose texts; AO4 (connections) is heavily weighted.
  • At least one text is pre-1900 — Victorian ghost fiction is not a draft of the modern uncanny.
  • The theme is the lens: hauntings, the uncanny and the fantastic, and the doubt that keeps a ghost ambiguous.

Named works are illustrative only, and no quotations are invented. Press Start.

AO4 · the comparative method

How to compare two prose texts

AO4 rewards texts that argue with each other, not a parallel report.

  1. Lead with a thematic proposition: e.g. both texts use the ghost less to frighten than to expose what a society will not say aloud.
  2. Interleave in theme-paragraphs (the haunting; the uncanny; the unreliable teller) that draw on both texts.
  3. Prize the productive difference — where one ghost demands justice and the other only exposes a mind.
  4. Let the pre-1900 text keep its own beliefs about death, God and the soul.

Stem: “Where [A] finally confirms the ghost as real, [B] refuses to, so that its horror is epistemological rather than physical.”

Theme · haunting conventions

The grammar of the ghost story

The supernatural tale has a recognisable machinery:

  • The revenant — the dead returning, often to demand justice or reveal a buried crime.
  • The haunted house — architecture as a mind or a family history made physical; forbidden rooms, thresholds, locked doors.
  • Atmosphere — cold, decay, liminal times (dusk, midnight), the pathetic fallacy of a hostile setting.
  • The buried secret — the supernatural as the return of something the household or society has concealed.

Comparative angle: ask what each text’s ghost is for — punishment, warning, grief, or social critique.

Theme · the uncanny and the fantastic

Two indispensable concepts

Two critical ideas anchor sophisticated answers:

  • The uncanny — Sigmund Freud’s essay Das Unheimliche (“The Uncanny”, 1919): the peculiar dread produced when the familiar becomes strange — the double, the automaton, the house that is home and not-home. It names the return of something repressed.
  • The fantastic — Tzvetan Todorov (The Fantastic, 1970) defines it as the reader’s hesitation between a natural and a supernatural explanation. While we cannot decide whether the ghost is real or delusion, we are in the fantastic; once the text settles the question, it tips into the uncanny or the marvellous.

Precision pays: using “uncanny” and “fantastic” accurately, with their theorists, is strong AO5 and AO2 at once.

Theme · doubt and the double

The unreliable teller and the doppelganger

The supernatural theme often turns on who is telling the story:

  • The unreliable narrator — grief, guilt, isolation or fever may be manufacturing the haunting; we read the ghost through a mind we cannot trust.
  • The double / doppelganger — a second self that externalises a divided psyche (a favourite of both Gothic and psychoanalytic reading).
  • Ambiguity as method — the best supernatural fiction keeps both the literal ghost and the psychological reading alive at once.

AO2: narrative framing (a story within a story, a found manuscript) is itself a way of holding the supernatural at a doubtful distance.

AO3 · context

Belief, doubt and the ghost

Track how belief shifts across your texts’ periods:

  • Victorian — a boom in spiritualism and séances alongside a crisis of religious faith after Darwin; ghosts do cultural work in a doubting age.
  • Gothic revival — the supernatural as a return of the pre-modern into a rationalist culture.
  • Modern and contemporary — the ghost secularised into trauma, memory and the return of history.

Weld it: because the earlier text was written amid genuine spiritualist belief, its ghost can be sincere; the later text’s ghost may be metaphor for trauma — an AO3 difference that drives an AO4 comparison.

AO5 · interpretation

Reading the supernatural through theory

Pick a lens that produces a textual claim:

  • Psychoanalytic — the ghost as the return of the repressed; the haunted house as the unconscious.
  • Feminist — whose desire or knowledge is pathologised as madness; the confined, “hysterical” woman whose vision is dismissed.
  • Historicist — the ghost as unfinished business of empire, class or family history.

Discipline: not “this could be psychological”, but “the text gives the ghost only to the woman no one believes, so the supernatural becomes a way of staging whose testimony a society refuses to hear.”
