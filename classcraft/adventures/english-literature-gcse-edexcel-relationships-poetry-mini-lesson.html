Mission 1

She said 'I love thee true'. So why is the knight left dying?

Make a guess: She said 'I love thee true'. So why is the knight left dying?

Keats · La Belle Dame sans MerciScreen 1

The poem in brief

  • John Keats (1795–1821) wrote it in April 1819, in a letter to his brother George in America. It was first printed, without Keats's name, in Leigh Hunt's The Indicator on 10 May 1820.
  • The title means the beautiful lady without pity. It comes from a 15th-century French poem by Alain Chartier.
  • It is a ballad: an old, song-like form that tells a story, here a medieval tale of a knight and a faery.
  • Your anthology prints Keats's letter version, which begins "knight-at-arms".

10–11He dreamed of pale kings and warriors who cried that she had him "in thrall"; he woke on the hillside.

Keats · La Belle Dame sans MerciScreen 4

Form: a ballad that goes in a circle

  • Three four-beat lines (iambic tetrameter), then a two-beat line: "And no birds sing." It stops short, like the knight's life.
  • Rhyme: ABCB, as in many folk ballads.
  • Frame: a speaker questions the knight (1–3); he answers (4–12).
  • Circular structure: stanza 12 echoes the words of stanza 1. He ends where he began, trapped.
  • The dream (10–11) is the turn: "Pale warriors, death-pale were they all;". Her earlier victims?

Keats · La Belle Dame sans MerciScreen 6

Language and the lady

  • Illness: "I see a lily on thy brow" (deathly white) and a "fading rose" on his cheek.
  • Pathetic fallacy: withered sedge and silent birds mirror him; the full "granary" shows the world ready for winter, but not him.
  • We see the lady only through him: wild eyes, sweet food, tears.
  • Readings: an enchantress; a love that can't last; grief for Keats's brother Tom, who died in December 1818. Amy Lowell argued it isn't autobiographical.

Mission checkScreen 9

?She said 'I love thee true'. So why is the knight left dying?

Everything we know comes from the knight. He reports her love in a "language strange" and her cruelty through a dream, then circles back to the same bleak hillside. Strong answers keep both readings in play and show how the ballad's frame and repetition create the mystery.

Mission 2

A child chats to his sick grandad. Why does the last line hurt?

Make a guess: A child chats to his sick grandad. Why does the last line hurt?

Baillie · A Child to his Sick GrandfatherScreen 1

Baillie and her child speaker

  • Joanna Baillie (1762–1851) was a Scottish poet and playwright. The poem is from her first book, Poems (1790), which doesn't name her.
  • The book aimed to show "the different influence which the same circumstances produce on different characters".
  • Its companion poem, "A Mother to her Waking Infant", has a mother talking to a baby who can't understand her.
  • A dramatic monologue: one speaker, a child, talks to a silent listener.
  • Scots-English words: wot (know), aye (always), bide (stay).

Baillie · A Child to his Sick GrandfatherScreen 5

Form: a child's song

  • Eight sestets (six-line stanzas) in rhyming couplets, AABBCC: frail / fail, horse / corse, sad / dad.
  • Five four-beat lines, then a shorter last line: "I'm vex'd to see you, dad." (6 syllables).
  • Six stanzas end on "dad", like a refrain: the child keeps calling his grandfather back.
  • Simple words and a steady beat sound like a child's voice. The ending stays calm and plain: "You do not hear me, dad."

?A child chats to his sick grandad. Why does the last line hurt?

Mission 3

A love poem that barely describes her body. What's it praising?

Make a guess: A love poem that barely describes her body. What's it praising?

Byron · She Walks in BeautyScreen 1

The poem in brief

  • Lord Byron wrote it in June 1814, the day after a party where he first saw his cousin's wife, Anne Wilmot, in a black mourning dress covered in spangles.
  • It was published in Hebrew Melodies (1815), a book of lyrics set to music by Isaac Nathan.
  • Many readers link the glittering black dress to the poem's starry night.
  • The speaker watches and admires. The woman never speaks.

Byron · She Walks in BeautyScreen 4

Dark and bright in balance

  • Antithesis: opposites set side by side. "all that's best of dark and bright" meet in her.
  • She has the "tender light" that "gaudy day" lacks: soft, not showy.
  • Stanza 2 adds that the balance is exact: one shade darker or one ray brighter would spoil it.
  • Her black hair ("raven tress") and the light on her face balance each other.

Byron · She Walks in BeautyScreen 6

Form and structure: from face to heart

  • Three sestets, ABABAB, in steady iambic tetrameter: only two rhymes per stanza. The calm, regular form matches the harmony it praises.

Mission 4

Love was a fountain. Now it's a well. Why is that so bad?

Make a guess: Love was a fountain. Now it's a well. Why is that so bad?

Wordsworth · A ComplaintScreen 1

The poem in brief

  • William Wordsworth published it in Poems, in Two Volumes (1807).
  • Editors think the changed friend was the poet Samuel Taylor Coleridge. In October 1806 Wordsworth saw him for the first time in almost three years.
  • The poem never names the friend, so it also works as a poem about any love that cools.
  • A complaint here means a lament: a poem of sorrow, not a grumble.

Wordsworth · A ComplaintScreen 5

Form and structure: a circle of loss

  • Three sestets rhyming ABABCC in iambic tetrameter. Each ends in a couplet, like a sad conclusion.
  • Circular structure: the first line ends "poor"; so does the last. The "door" of his "fond Heart" returns too.
  • "Poor" turns money into feeling: he has lost the love's "bounty".
  • Dashes and questions ("What have I? shall I dare to tell?", "What matter?") break the lines, like a speaker struggling to say it.

Mission 5

Grey leaves, a white sun. Why is this love poem so drained?

Make a guess: Grey leaves, a white sun. Why is this love poem so drained?

Relationships poetry · Neutral TonesScreen 1

Context in brief

  • Thomas Hardy (1840–1928) is best known for novels such as Tess of the d'Urbervilles, but wrote poetry all his life.
  • The poem is dated 1867, when Hardy was in his late twenties, but he published it only in his first collection, Wessex Poems, in 1898.
  • Neutral tones are colourless shades: grey, white, dull.
  • Many readers see the poem's bleakness as typical of Hardy's pessimism about love and fate.

Relationships poetry · Neutral TonesScreen 3

Structure: back to the pond

  • Four quatrains, each rhymed ABBA (day / God / sod / gray). The enclosing rhyme closes each stanza like a frame.
  • Stanzas 1–3: the memory of one winter day with a lover.
  • Stanza 4: "Since then", the present. He has learned "that love deceives".
  • Cyclical structure: stanza 4 lists the same face, sun, tree and pond. The memory returns whenever love hurts him.

Relationships poetry · Neutral TonesScreen 5

Language: a dead love

  • Pathetic fallacy: a pale sun, a "starving sod", gray leaves. The landscape is as lifeless as the love.

Mission 6

A duke shows a visitor a portrait. What is he really saying?

Make a guess: A duke shows a visitor a portrait. What is he really saying?

Poetry · "My Last Duchess"Screen 1

A duke, a portrait, a visitor

  • Browning published the poem in 1842. It is headed "Ferrara", a city in Italy.
  • The Duke is usually identified as Alfonso II d'Este, Duke of Ferrara. His first wife died in 1561, aged 16; poisoning was rumoured.
  • He then sought a new bride, negotiating through the Count of Tyrol.
  • In the poem the Duke shows the Count's envoy (messenger) a portrait of his "last Duchess" while they arrange his next marriage.

Poetry · "My Last Duchess"Screen 3

A dramatic monologue in disguised couplets

  • A dramatic monologue: one character speaks to a silent listener (the envoy), and gives away more than he means.
  • We only hear the Duke, so we judge him from his own words.
  • Rhyming couplets in iambic pentameter: that's MY | last DUCH | ess PAINT | ed ON | the WALL.
  • Heavy enjambment hides the rhymes ("I call / That piece a wonder"): the speech sounds casual, but the strict form shows control.

On the surface the Duke is showing off a painting. Underneath, he reveals his pride, his jealousy and his need to control, and hints that he had his wife silenced. Because he says this to the envoy arranging his next marriage, many readers hear a warning about how the next Duchess must behave.

Barrett Browning · Sonnet 43Screen 1

The poem in brief

  • Elizabeth Barrett wrote Sonnets from the Portuguese in 1845–46, while Robert Browning was courting her.
  • They married secretly on 12 September 1846; her father never forgave her.
  • She first showed him the sonnets in 1849, and they were published in her Poems (1850).
  • The title made them look like translations: Robert's pet name for her was "my little Portuguese".

Barrett Browning · Sonnet 43Screen 6

Form: a Petrarchan sonnet

  • 14 lines of iambic pentameter: an octave rhyming ABBAABBA (ways / height / sight / Grace…) and a sestet rhyming CDCDCD.
  • The octave covers the soul, daily life and goodness. From line 9 the volta turns to her own life: "old griefs", "lost saints".
  • Enjambment, "height / My soul can reach", lets love spill over the line end.
  • The sonnet was traditionally a man's love poem; here a woman speaks her love. "If God choose" adds humility to the final promise.

Key quotations to learn

  • O what can ail thee, knight-at-arms, (Stanza 1 (questioner)) The frame: the poem begins with a question.
  • Alone and palely loitering? (Stanzas 1 and 12) Repeated at the end: the circular structure.
  • And no birds sing. (Stanzas 1 and 12) Short line and bleak, silent nature.
  • And sure in language strange she said, (Stanza 7 (knight)) He reports words he may not have understood.
  • Pale warriors, death-pale were they all; (Stanza 10 (knight's dream)) Her earlier victims mirror the pale knight.
  • Thee hath in thrall (Stanza 10 (dream warriors)) The dream's verdict: he is enslaved by her.
  • I'm vex'd to see you, dad. (Stanza 1) The child's distress, in his own simple words.
  • I love my own old dad. (Stanza 3) Love stays, though his strength has gone.
  • You will not die, and leave us then? (Stanza 5) The fear he can only say as a question.
  • I'll lead you kindly by the hand: (Stanza 6) Roles reverse: the child will care for the adult.

  • How do I love thee? Let me count the ways. (Line 1) The question and the promise to count.
  • I love thee to the depth and breadth and height (Line 2) Love measured as space, in every direction.
  • Most quiet need, by sun and candlelight. (Line 6) Love in everyday life, day and night.
  • I love thee freely, as men strive for Right; (Line 7) Simile: love as chosen and morally good.
  • In my old griefs, and with my childhood’s faith. (Line 10) After the volta: her own past.
  • I shall but love thee better after death. (Line 14) The ending goes beyond life.
