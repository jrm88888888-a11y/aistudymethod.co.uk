Mission 1

Mission 1: Plants given a new fertilizer grew taller, but they also sat nearest the window. Can you trust the result?

Make a guess: Plants given a new fertilizer grew taller, but they also sat nearest the window. Can you trust the result?

Mission 1

Three kinds of variable

  • Independent variable: the one you change on purpose.
  • Dependent variable: the one you measure. It may depend on the independent variable.
  • Controlled variables: everything else that could affect the result. Keep them constant.
  • Example: lamp distance (independent) → bubbles of gas per minute from pondweed (dependent). Temperature, CO2 supply and the piece of pondweed stay the same.
  • Change one variable at a time. If two change together, you cannot tell which caused the effect.
  • The hook: fertilizer and light changed together. The result is confounded.
Quick check
?Researchers test the hypothesis that competition for food limits the number of offspring produced per beetle in a hypothetical species of flour beetle. They place 10, 20, 40 or 80 adult beetles in identical containers, each holding 20 g of flour as food, and count the offspring produced per adult beetle after 30 days. Which is the independent variable?
Mission 1

Controls

  • A control is a set-up identical to the others except that the factor being tested is absent (or at a standard level).
  • It shows what happens without the factor, so any difference can be put down to that factor.
  • Negative control: no effect expected (e.g. boiled enzyme, or water in place of an extract).
  • Positive control: an effect is known to occur. It shows the method can detect one.
  • Justify a control by saying what it rules out, e.g. "shows the substrate does not break down on its own at this rate".
  • A control need not show "nothing". It gives the baseline for comparison.
  • Say: "temperature was a controlled variable" — not: "temperature was the control".
Quick check
?A student tests the claim that an extract from a hypothetical plant contains an enzyme that speeds up the breakdown of compound P. When P breaks down, the solution turns yellow. Tube 1 contains P, buffer and the extract. Tube 2 contains P and buffer, with water in place of the extract. Both tubes are kept at 25 °C, and the time each takes to turn yellow is recorded. Which statement best justifies including tube 2?
Mission 1

Questions and hypotheses

  • A testable question names what you will change and what you will measure: "Does light intensity affect the rate of photosynthesis in pondweed?"
  • A hypothesis is a provisional explanation that can be tested: "Rate rises with light intensity, because light drives the light-dependent reactions."
  • A prediction says what you expect to measure if the hypothesis is right.
  • The null hypothesis says the independent variable has no effect (or there is no difference). Statistical tests check whether the data let you reject it.
  • After evaluating results or a design, propose a new investigation: change one thing, keep the rest, and say what it would test.
  • Data can support a hypothesis. They never prove it.
Quick check
?A student plans to test whether the number of double bonds in a fatty acid affects its melting point, using fatty acids with 0, 1, 2 and 3 double bonds that all have the same chain length. Which statement is an appropriate null hypothesis for this investigation?
Mission 1

Measuring the dependent variable

  • Biologists often measure a rate: change ÷ time.
  • Product formed over time: change in amount ÷ time taken.
  • Time for a fixed change (a disc rises, a color appears): rate ∝ 1 ÷ time. A disc that rises in 25 s → 1 ÷ 25 = 0.040 s⁻¹.
  • A shorter time means a faster rate.
  • Volumes in mL: 15 mL of oxygen in 30 s → 0.50 mL/s.
  • Measure the initial rate where you can: the reaction slows as substrate is used up.
  • Choose apparatus that suits the size of the change: a gas syringe or a colorimeter beats judging by eye.
Quick check
?A filter-paper disc soaked in catalase solution sinks in hydrogen peroxide and rises when enough oxygen has formed on it. In 1% hydrogen peroxide the disc took 50 s to rise; in 2% it took 20 s. Which statement about the reaction rate is correct?
Mission 1

Repeats, samples and validity

  • Living things vary. One measurement may be unusual, so repeat and use the mean.
  • Repeats also expose anomalies: results far from the others.
  • Sample size: more individuals make the mean more likely to be near the true population mean.
  • Sample selection: choose at random, so the sample represents the population and no one picks "good" specimens.
  • Valid: the method answers the question asked. It needs controlled variables and a suitable dependent variable.
  • Repeats cannot rescue an invalid design: the hook's plants could be measured 100 times and still be confounded.
Mission 1

Tables and graphs

  • Table: independent variable in the first column; repeats, then mean. Units in the headings, never in the cells. Same number of decimal places down each column.
  • Graph: independent variable on the x-axis, dependent on the y-axis.
  • Label each axis with the quantity and its unit.
  • Units in parentheses are fine: "Rate (mL/s)".
  • Choose a scale that uses over half the grid, in even steps.
  • Line graph: continuous independent variable. Bar chart: categories.
  • Scatter graph: looking for a correlation between two measured variables.
  • Also: histogram (distribution of one variable), log scale (values over several powers of 10), dual y-axes (two variables with different units).
  • Do not extend a line beyond your data.
Mission check

?A student investigates the effect of pH on the rate of a catalase reaction. She runs all the pH 4 and pH 5 tubes in the morning, when the lab is at 16 °C, and the pH 8 and pH 9 tubes in the afternoon, when it is at 24 °C. Which single change best removes the main flaw in her design?

Like the fertilizer and the window, two variables changed together. Control the second one, and the test becomes valid.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Two sets of results share the same average but look nothing alike. What number captures the difference?

Make a guess: Two sets of results share the same average but look nothing alike. What number captures the difference?

Mission 2

Three averages and a range

  • Mean = sum of values ÷ number of values.
  • Median = the middle value when the values are in order (for an even number, halfway between the middle two).
  • Mode = the most frequent value.
  • Range = largest − smallest.
  • One extreme value pulls the mean toward it. The median hardly moves.
Quick check
?The heights in centimeters of nine students are: 150, 152, 152, 155, 158, 160, 163, 170 and 175. What is the median height?
Mission 2

Standard deviation: the typical spread

  • Standard deviation (SD): how far values typically lie from the mean.
  • Small SD: values cluster tightly. Large SD: values are widely spread.
  • Two samples can share a mean and differ hugely in SD: the hook.
  • For data with a normal (bell-shaped) distribution, about 68% of values lie within ±1 SD of the mean, and about 95% within ±2 SD.
  • SD has the same unit as the data.
  • A large SD is not a "mistake". In biology it often reflects real variation between individuals.

Worked example: calculating SD

Stomata counted in five fields of view on one leaf: 22, 26, 19, 27, 21.

  • Mean, x̄ = 115 ÷ 5 = 23.
  • Σ(x − x̄)² = 46.
  • SD, s = √[Σ(x − x̄)² ÷ (n − 1)] = √(46 ÷ 4) = √11.5 = 3.4.
  • Divide by n − 1 (here 4), not n: this estimates the SD of the whole population from a sample.
  • The formula is on the equation sheet.
Quick check
?Limpets were counted in twenty random quadrats on each of two shores. On both shores the mean was 8 limpets per quadrat. The standard deviation was 1.2 on shore A and 6.5 on shore B. What can be concluded?
Mission 2

Percentage change and percentage difference

  • Percentage change = (final − initial) ÷ initial × 100.
  • Example: stomatal density rises from 120 to 150 per mm². (150 − 120) ÷ 120 × 100 = +25%.
  • A fall gives a negative value.
  • Always divide by the initial value for a change.
Quick check
?In a country, the number of new COVID-19 cases recorded per day fell from 2000 in one week to 1600 in the following week. What is the percentage change in the daily number of cases?
Mission 2

Box-and-whisker plots

  • A box plot shows a continuous variable's distribution at a glance.
  • Median: line inside the box. Box: first quartile (Q1) to third quartile (Q3).
  • Interquartile range (IQR) = Q3 − Q1: the spread of the middle half.
  • Whiskers run to the minimum and maximum values.
  • Whiskers are not error bars.
Mission 2

Reading graphs and reporting numbers

  • Gradient = change in y ÷ change in x: a rate.
  • Rate = dY/dt. Oxygen rises from 4.0 to 16.0 mL between 10 s and 40 s: 12.0 ÷ 30 = 0.40 mL/s.
  • Intercept: where the line crosses an axis. Where % change in mass = 0, the solution is isotonic with the tissue.
  • Describe a trend with data: "rises from 4.0 to 16.0 over 30 s, then levels off", with units.
  • Significant figures: give a calculated answer to the same number as the least precise data used.
  • Metric prefixes: kilo (k) 10³, centi (c) 10⁻², milli (m) 10⁻³, micro (µ) 10⁻⁶, nano (n) 10⁻⁹.
Mission check

?The lengths of five seedling roots, in mm, are 14, 16, 15, 18 and 17. Which gives the mean and standard deviation correctly?

Same mean, different spread: the SD is the number that tells two such data sets apart.

Mission 3

Mission 3: A difference between two groups could be real or just luck. How do biologists decide which?

Make a guess: A difference between two groups could be real or just luck. How do biologists decide which?

Mission 3

Could it just be chance?

  • Samples vary, so two groups almost never match exactly.
  • Start with the null hypothesis: there is no difference (or no association, or no correlation).
  • A statistical test turns the data into one number, the test statistic.
  • Compare it with the critical value at p = 0.05.
  • Above the critical value: if the null hypothesis were true, a result this extreme would happen by chance less than 5% of the time. The result is significant: reject the null hypothesis.
  • Below it: not significant. The difference could be due to chance.
  • Then fail to reject the null hypothesis.
  • Significant does not mean proved; not significant does not prove "no effect".
Mission 3

Worked example: chi-squared beyond genetics

Daisies counted in two areas of a field of equal size, one trampled and one not (illustrative data). Null hypothesis: no difference, so equal numbers are expected.

TrampledUntrampledTotal
Observed, O3862100
Expected, E5050100
(O − E)² ÷ E2.882.88
  • χ² = Σ (O − E)² ÷ E = 2.88 + 2.88 = 5.76.
  • Equation sheet form: χ² = Σ (o − e)² ÷ e.
  • Degrees of freedom = categories − 1 = 1. Critical value at p = 0.05: 3.84.
  • 5.76 > 3.84: significant. Reject the null hypothesis: daisies are not spread evenly between the two areas.
  • The test does not say why. Trampling is one explanation to test further.
  • Expected values come from the null hypothesis: here, equal areas → equal numbers.
Quick check
?Fifty individuals of a hypothetical species of small ground-dwelling invertebrate were placed in the center of a choice chamber with a moist side and a dry side. After 10 minutes, the number on each side was counted, as shown in the table. The null hypothesis is that the animals show no preference, so equal numbers are expected on each side. Using a chi-square test with p = 0.05, which conclusion is correct?

FeederLowMiddleHigh

Every comparison asks the same question: is this difference bigger than chance would usually produce? The test puts a number on it.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Two bars on a graph have different heights. When does that difference actually mean something?

Make a guess: Two bars on a graph have different heights. When does that difference actually mean something?

Mission 4

What an error bar can show

  • A bar or point shows a mean. The error bar shows uncertainty or spread.
  • Error bars can show different things. Read the legend first.
  • ±1 SD: the spread of the individual values.
  • ±1 SE or ±2 SE: how precisely the mean is known.
  • ±2 SE is roughly a 95% confidence interval.
  • Bars from the range (min to max) are sometimes drawn too. They depend on only two values.
Mission 4

Standard error of the mean

  • Standard error (SE) = s ÷ √n, where s is the SD and n the sample size.
  • SD describes the individuals. SE describes the mean: how close it is likely to be to the true population mean.
  • SE is always smaller than SD (for n > 1).
  • SE falls as n rises: four times as many samples halves the SE.
  • SE is on the equation sheet.
  • Say: "standard error shows how precise the mean is" — not: "standard error is the size of the mistakes".
Mission 4

Worked example: SE error bars

Root lengths of 10 seedlings: mean = 48 mm, s = 6.0 mm.

  • SE = 6.0 ÷ √10 = 1.9 mm.
  • ±2 SE = ±3.8 mm: bars from 44.2 to 51.8 mm, about a 95% confidence interval.
  • ±2 SE bars of two groups do not overlap: the difference is likely to be significant.
  • They overlap: a significant difference has not been shown. That does not prove the means are equal.
Quick check
?A hypothetical species of alga was grown for 10 days in three media that were identical except for their phosphate content. Five flasks were used for each medium. The table shows the mean cell density, with error bars of ±2 SE of the mean.

Mission check

?A student grew bean seedlings with fertilizer X or fertilizer Y, 10 plants each, and plotted mean root length with error bars:
X: mean 42 mm, bar from 39 to 45 mm. Y: mean 50 mm, bar from 47 to 53 mm. Which conclusion is best supported?
  • The bars show ±2 SE, about a 95% confidence interval.

Bar heights alone mean little. Bars that show the uncertainty in each mean tell you whether a difference is likely to be real.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Change one variable, measure one, keep the rest constant; a control gives the baseline for comparison.
  • A null hypothesis says "no effect"; data can support a hypothesis but never prove it.

Mission 2

  • Mean, median and mode describe the center; SD describes the typical spread about the mean, using every value.
  • Percentage change divides by the initial value; box plots show median, quartiles and spread.

Mission 3

  • Null hypothesis → test statistic → compare with the critical value at p = 0.05 → significant or not. Neither outcome is proof.
  • χ² compares counts with expected counts; degrees of freedom = categories − 1.

Mission 4

  • Read the legend: SD bars show the spread of individuals; SE and confidence bars show how precisely each mean is known.
  • SE = s ÷ √n; non-overlapping 95% bars (±2 SE or 95% confidence limits) indicate a significant difference.
