Mission 1Topic 2.6

Mission 1: Astronauts float on the space station. How much weaker is Earth's gravity up there?

Make a guess: Astronauts float on the space station. How much weaker is Earth's gravity up there?

Mission 1

Newton's law of gravitation

  • Every pair of masses attracts. Gravity never repels.
  • F = Gm1m2/r²
  • r: distance between the centers. G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg².
  • Acts along the line joining the centers. Each mass pulls the other equally hard (third law).
  • Inverse-square law: double r → force ÷ 4.
Mission 1

Spheres act as point masses

  • Outside a uniform sphere (or one made of uniform shells), its field is exactly that of a point mass at its center.
  • So for planets and stars, r is measured from the center, never from the surface.
Quick check
?Two objects exert gravitational forces of magnitude F on each other. The mass of each object is doubled, and the distance between their centers is tripled. What is the new magnitude of the gravitational force that each object exerts on the other?
Mission 1

Gravitational field strength

  • Gravitational field: a region where a mass experiences a force.
  • Field strength g = F/m: force per unit mass on a small test mass. Unit N/kg. Toward the mass.
  • Divide F = GMm/r² by m: g = GM/r².
  • Free fall: g in N/kg equals the acceleration in m/s².
  • Weight = the gravitational force by a planet on a nearby object: Fg = mg.
Quick check
?A ball is thrown straight up and rises 20 m above the ground before falling back. Earth's radius is 6.4 × 10⁶ m. Which statement about the gravitational force exerted on the ball by Earth during its flight is correct?
Mission 1

Field lines and the uniform field near the surface

  • Near the surface, over a few kilometers, g barely changes: treat the field as uniform.
  • Near Earth's surface, g ≈ 10 N/kg. Over small changes in height the gravitational force is approximately constant.
Mission 1

Worked example: the space station

  • Earth's radius R = 6.37 × 10⁶ m. Station about 4.0 × 10⁵ m up: r = 6.77 × 10⁶ m from the center.
  • g ∝ 1/r²: gstation/gsurface = (6.37/6.77)² = 0.89.
  • gstation ≈ 0.89 × 10 ≈ 8.9 N/kg.
  • Gravity there is only about 11% weaker. Astronauts float because they are in free fall with the station.
Quick check
?An astronaut's weight at Earth's surface is W. Planet Q has three times Earth's mass and twice Earth's radius. What is the astronaut's weight at the surface of planet Q?
Mission 1

Inside a planet: the shell theorem

  • Add the pulls of all the small masses in a sphere (by symmetry, sideways parts cancel).
  • Thin uniform shell: outside, it acts as a point mass at its center. Inside, the net force is zero.
  • Inside a uniform solid sphere, at radius r: the shells outside you pull nothing. Only the partial mass inside radius r counts.
  • Partial mass: M(r) = M r³/R³ (from the density).
  • F = GM(r)m/r² = (GMm/R³) r. F ∝ r: F = −kr, toward the center.
  • g against r: a straight line from 0 at the center to GM/R² at the surface, then 1/r² outside.
Quick check
?Planets A and B are uniform spheres with the same density. Planet A has radius R and planet B has radius 2R. Identical probes are placed in shafts inside the planets, each at a distance R/2 from its planet's center. FA and FB are the magnitudes of the gravitational forces exerted on the probes by their planets. Which is correct?
Mission check

?The diagram shows a planet of radius R and two points, P and Q, on a line through the planet's center. The arrow at P represents the planet's gravitational field at P, of magnitude 4.5 N/kg. What is the magnitude of the planet's gravitational field at Q?

g ∝ 1/r², with r from the center. At 400 km up, r is only 6% larger than Earth's radius, so g is about 89% of its surface value. Astronauts float because they are falling freely, not because gravity has gone.

Mission 2Topic 2.7

Mission 2: Can friction ever push an object forward, in the direction it is moving?

Make a guess: Can friction ever push an object forward, in the direction it is moving?

Quick check
?Predict first. A board is pulled to the right along a level floor. A block resting on the board slides on it: at the instant shown, both move to the right, the board faster than the block, as the velocity arrows in the diagram show. A student draws the free-body diagram shown for the block. Which statement about the student's diagram is correct?
Mission 2

Which way does friction act?

  • Friction acts parallel to the surfaces in contact.
  • It opposes the relative motion of the two surfaces (or the slipping that would happen without it).
  • Relative means: relative to the other surface, not to the ground.
  • Box on a belt that moves faster than the box: the box slips backward relative to the belt, so friction on the box points forward.
  • Kinetic friction does not depend (to a good approximation) on the area of contact.
Mission 2

Static friction

  • Surfaces not sliding relative to each other: static friction.
  • It is only as large as it needs to be, to stop slipping.
  • Push a heavy crate with 10 N: friction is 10 N. Push with 30 N: friction is 30 N.
  • It has a maximum:
  • Ff,s ≤ μs Fn
  • Push beyond the maximum: the surfaces start to slide.
Mission 2

Sliding friction

  • Surfaces sliding: kinetic friction, |Ff,k| = μk Fn.
  • μ has no unit: it is a ratio of two forces. It depends on the pair of materials.
  • Usually μs > μk: harder to start sliding than to keep sliding.
  • To a good approximation, sliding friction does not depend on speed.
Mission 2

Worked example

  • Box on a level floor. Normal force on it: 200 N. μs = 0.50; μ for sliding = 0.40.
  • Maximum static friction = 0.50 × 200 = 100 N.
  • Push 60 N: no sliding. Friction = 60 N (not 100 N).
  • Push just over 100 N: the box starts to slide.
  • Sliding: friction = 0.40 × 200 = 80 N.
  • Keep pushing with 100 N: net force = 100 − 80 = 20 N forward. The box speeds up.
Quick check
?A 0.80 kg box rests on a level floor. The coefficients of friction between the box and the floor are μs = 0.60 and μk = 0.35. A student pushes the box horizontally, increasing the push slowly from zero. Use g = 10 m/s². What is the magnitude of the push when the box starts to slide?
Quick check
?A block rests on a level table. A student pulls it with a horizontal force that increases slowly from zero, and a sensor records the friction force exerted on the block by the table. The block starts to slide when the pull reaches 12 N. The graph shows the friction force as a function of the pull. Which claim does the graph support?
Mission 2

The normal force is not always mg

  • Friction depends on the normal force: the push perpendicular to the surface.
  • Level floor, no other vertical forces: normal force = mg.
  • Slope at angle θ: normal force = mg cos θ. Less friction than on the level.
  • Pulled by a rope angled upward: the rope takes some of the weight. Normal force = mg − T sin θ.
  • Always find the normal force first, from the forces perpendicular to the surface.
Quick check
?A box of mass m is pushed across a level floor by a force of magnitude F directed at an angle θ below the horizontal. The box slides along the floor. The coefficients of static and kinetic friction between the box and the floor are μs and μk. Which expression gives the magnitude of the kinetic friction force exerted on the box?
Mission check

?A book lies on the flat bed of a pickup truck. The truck speeds up from rest along a straight, level road, and the book does not slide on the bed. Which statement about friction on the book is correct?

Yes. Friction opposes slipping between the surfaces. The bed tends to move forward under the book, so friction on the book points forward and speeds it up, just like a box on a faster belt.

Mission 3Topic 2.8

Mission 3: Why are the markings on a spring weighing scale evenly spaced?

Make a guess: Why are the markings on a spring weighing scale evenly spaced?

Mission 3

Hooke's law

  • Stretch a spring: the force it exerts is proportional to its extension.
  • Extension = stretched length − original (natural) length. Not the whole length.
  • Fs = −kΔx. k is the spring constant.
  • Unit of k: N/m. Larger k: stiffer spring.
  • Works for compression too: the extension is then the decrease in length.
Quick check
?An ideal spring has a relaxed length of 10 cm. When it is stretched to a length of 14 cm, it exerts a force of magnitude 3.0 N on the object attached to it. What is the magnitude of the force it exerts when it is stretched to a length of 22 cm?
Mission 3

A restoring force

  • The minus sign: the spring's force is opposite to its displacement from equilibrium.
  • Stretched to the right: the spring pulls left. Compressed to the left: it pushes right.
  • Always toward the equilibrium (natural length) position: a restoring force.
  • At the natural length the spring exerts no force.
Quick check
?A block on a level surface with negligible friction is attached to two identical ideal springs; the other end of each spring is fixed to a wall, one on each side of the block. When the block is at its equilibrium position, both springs have their relaxed lengths. The block is pulled a distance d to the right and released from rest. Which statement describes the forces exerted on the block by the two springs just after it is released?
Mission 3

Worked example

  • A spring hangs with natural length 12.0 cm. A 4.0 N weight stretches it to 17.0 cm.
  • Extension = 17.0 − 12.0 = 5.0 cm = 0.050 m.
  • k = force ÷ extension = 4.0 ÷ 0.050 = 80 N/m.
  • Hang 6.0 N instead: extension = 6.0 ÷ 80 = 0.075 m = 7.5 cm.
  • New length = 12.0 + 7.5 = 19.5 cm. Always add the extension back to the natural length.
Mission 3

The force–extension graph

  • Plot force against extension. Hooke's law: a straight line through the origin.
  • Slope = k.
Quick check
?The graph shows the magnitude F of the force exerted by an ideal spring as a function of the spring's length L, from its relaxed length to a length of 20 cm. What is the spring constant of the spring?
Mission 3

Ideal and nonideal springs

  • An ideal spring: massless, and its force is proportional to its change in length.
  • Massless: the force is the same at both ends of the spring.
  • A nonideal spring has significant mass, or a force that is not proportional to Δx.
  • A hanging spring with mass: the ceiling holds up the load and the spring's own weight.
Mission 3

Springs in series and in parallel

  • Parallel (side by side, same stretch): forces add. keq = k1 + k2. Stiffer.
  • Series (end to end, same force in each): stretches add. 1/keq = 1/k1 + 1/k2. Softer than either spring.
  • Two identical springs: parallel 2k, series k/2.
  • Cutting a spring in half doubles k for each half: each half stretches half as much under the same force.
Quick check
?Two ideal springs, of spring constants 300 N/m and 600 N/m, are joined end to end. One free end is held fixed, and a 12 N force is exerted on the other free end along the springs. By how much does the pair stretch in total?
Mission check

?A spring that obeys Hooke's law is 15.0 cm long when it supports a 3.0 N load and 18.0 cm long when it supports a 6.0 N load. What is its natural (unloaded) length?

Extension ∝ force, so each extra newton adds the same extension: evenly spaced markings.

Mission 4Topic 2.9

Mission 4: Raindrops fall from high clouds. Why don't they hit the ground like bullets?

Make a guess: Raindrops fall from high clouds. Why don't they hit the ground like bullets?

Mission 4

Drag

  • Drag: the resistive force on an object moving through a fluid (a liquid or a gas). Air resistance is drag in air.
  • It acts opposite to the velocity relative to the fluid.
  • It increases with speed.
Mission 4

Falling with drag

  • At release: v = 0, so drag = 0.
  • In air (buoyant force negligible), the only force is the weight.
  • Speed rises → drag rises → net force (weight − drag) falls.
  • When drag = weight: net force zero.
  • The object then falls at a constant terminal velocity.
  • v–t graph: starts as steeply as the no-drag line, then curves and levels off at vT.
Quick check
?A small steel ball is released from rest in air and falls until it reaches terminal velocity. How does its acceleration change during this time?
Mission 4

Worked example: a falling ball

  • A 0.20 kg ball falls. At one instant the drag on it is 0.80 N.
  • Weight = 0.20 × 10 = 2.0 N. Net force = 2.0 − 0.80 = 1.2 N downward.
  • a = 1.2 ÷ 0.20 = 6.0 m/s²: less than g.
  • It keeps speeding up until drag = weight, about 2.0 N. Then a = 0: terminal velocity.
Mission check

?Two paper cones have the same size and shape. A second cone is pushed inside one of them, doubling its mass without changing its outside shape. Both are dropped from a high balcony. Which statement about their terminal velocities is correct?

Drag grows with speed until it balances the weight. Falling raindrops reach this balance at under 10 m/s, not the 140 m/s they would reach falling 1 km without air.

Mission 5Topic 2.9

Mission 5: If drag grows with speed, does a falling ball ever actually reach its terminal speed?

Make a guess: If drag grows with speed, does a falling ball ever actually reach its terminal speed?

Mission 5

A velocity-dependent resistive force

  • Fr = −kv: resistive force proportional to velocity, opposite to it.
  • k is a positive constant, in kg/s. (Not a spring constant, despite the letter.)
  • Good model for slow motion through a viscous fluid.
  • The velocity reverses → the resistive force reverses.
Quick check
?A ball is thrown straight up, and air resistance on it is not negligible. The diagram shows the ball's velocity at two instants, P and Q. Which describes the direction of the resistive force exerted on the ball by the air at P and at Q?
Mission 5

The second law becomes a differential equation

  • Falling from rest, down positive: m dv/dt = mg − kv.
  • Any other motion: pick a positive direction, then give each force a + sign if it points that way and a − sign if not.
  • Terminal velocity: net force zero, dv/dt = 0 → vT = mg/k.
  • Check the sign of each term against the chosen positive direction.
Quick check
?A ball of mass m is thrown straight up and experiences a resistive force Fr = −kv. Upward is taken as positive, and g is the magnitude of the acceleration due to gravity. Which differential equation describes the ball's velocity v while it is rising?
Mission 5

Separate the variables

  • Write m dv/dt = k(vT − v). Separate: dv/(vT − v) = (k/m) dt.
  • Integrate from v = 0 at t = 0: −ln[(vT − v)/vT] = kt/m.
  • v(t) = vT(1 − e−kt/m).
  • Time constant τ = m/k. After τ: v = 0.63 vT. After 5τ: 0.993 vT.
  • v approaches vT asymptotically. a(t) = g e−kt/m → 0.
Mission 5

Worked example

  • A 0.050 kg ball falls from rest; k = 0.10 kg/s; g = 10 m/s².
  • vT = mg/k = 0.050 × 10 ÷ 0.10 = 5.0 m/s. τ = m/k = 0.50 s.
  • At t = 1.0 s (2τ): v = 5.0(1 − e⁻²) = 4.3 m/s.
  • a = 10e⁻² = 1.4 m/s².
  • Distance: y = vT t − vT τ(1 − e−t/τ) = 5.0 − 2.5 × 0.865 = 2.8 m.
Mission 5

Coasting to a stop

  • No other horizontal force: m dv/dt = −kv.
  • Separate: dv/v = −(k/m) dt → v = v0 e−kt/m.
  • Integrate again: x = (mv0/k)(1 − e−kt/m).
  • Asymptotes: v → 0 and x → mv0/k, a finite distance.
  • The velocity's asymptote is set by the forces alone; the position's asymptote, mv0/k, also depends on the initial speed.
Quick check
?A 0.40 kg toy boat glides across still water in a straight line with its motor off. The water exerts a resistive force Fr = −kv with k = 0.50 kg/s, and no other horizontal force acts. How long does it take the boat's speed to fall to half its initial value?
Quick check
?An object is dropped from rest and falls, experiencing a resistive force Fr = −kv. The graph shows how one quantity Q describing the object's motion changes with time t after release. Which quantity could Q be?
Mission check

?A ball of mass m is thrown straight down with speed 2vT, where vT = mg/k is its terminal speed for the resistive force Fr = −kv it experiences. What is its speed at time t = (m/k) ln 2 after it is thrown?

v − vT shrinks by e−kt/m from either side. In this model it never reaches zero, so the ball never quite arrives at vT; after 5m/k it is within 1%.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • F = Gm1m2/r²: always attractive, inverse square, r between centers.
  • g = F/m = GM/r²; outside a sphere, treat it as a point mass at its center.
  • Inside a uniform sphere only the mass within r counts: F ∝ r.

Mission 2

  • Friction acts parallel to the surfaces and opposes their relative slipping, so it can point forward (a box on a faster belt, a walking foot).
  • Static: Ff,s ≤ μs Fn, as large as needed. Kinetic: |Ff,k| = μk Fn. Usually μs > μk.

Mission 3

  • Extension (not length) is proportional to force: slope of the force–extension graph = k, in N/m.
  • The minus sign: the spring force is a restoring force, always toward the equilibrium position.
  • Parallel: keq = k1 + k2. Series: 1/keq = 1/k1 + 1/k2.

Mission 4

  • Drag grows with speed. Falling, the net force and acceleration fall to zero when drag = weight: terminal velocity.

Mission 5

  • Fr = −kv: m dv/dt = mg − kv gives v = vT(1 − e−kt/m), with vT = mg/k and time constant m/k.
