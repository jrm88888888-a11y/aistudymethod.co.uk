Mission 1: In January 2018 Cape Town, a city of just over 4 million people, warned that its taps could be turned off in April: "Day Zero". What pushed it to the edge, and how did it pull back?

Make a guess: In January 2018 Cape Town, a city of just over 4 million people, warned that its taps could be turned off in April: "Day Zero". What pushed it to the edge, and how did it pull back?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 3.4-4.a environmental impacts of raising food production: irrigation and salinisation, deforestation, landscape change, agrochemical water pollution
Mission 1

Climate and supply: Cape Town

  • Climate variability is a physical cause of insecurity: dry years empty reservoirs.
  • 2015–2017: "three consecutive years of below average rainfall", with "a return time of more than a hundred years".
  • Climate change made such a drought about three times as likely (95% range 1.5–6).
  • Storage in the six largest dams fell from 100% in 2014 to 71%, 60% and 38% in the following years (each 1 November).
Mission 1

Water quality

  • A supply is only useful if its quality is fit for use.
  • Agrochemicals: in Chesapeake Bay, USA, agriculture supplied about 46% of nitrogen loads (2024 modelling). Nutrients feed algae; their decay creates low-oxygen "dead zones".
  • Salinisation: more than 424 million ha of topsoil is salt-affected worldwide.
  • Industrial pollution and untreated sewage add metals and pathogens.
Quick check
?Fertiliser washes off farmland into a bay. A few weeks later, fish die in deep water. What links the two?
Mission 1

Food production and water

  • Irrigation and salinisation: in hot, dry places, irrigation water evaporates and leaves salts behind; poor drainage raises salty groundwater towards the surface.
  • FAO: more than 424 million ha of topsoil and 833 million ha of subsoil are salt-affected.
  • Deforestation: clearing for food changes landscapes and cuts biodiversity. Mangroves: aquaculture caused 27% of mangrove loss in 2000–2020; rice 8%, oil palm 8%, other agriculture 12%.
  • Agrochemicals: fertiliser runoff fuels algal blooms (Chesapeake).
Quick check
?In a hot, dry region, farmers irrigate heavily but the fields have poor drainage. Within years, crops fail and a white crust forms on the soil. Why?
Mission 1

How Cape Town pulled back

  • Farms: irrigation cuts rose to 60% (December 2017).
  • Homes: from 1 February 2018, a limit of 50 litres per person per day.
  • Daily use fell from over 1,000 Ml in early 2016 to "just below 500Ml a few times".
  • Day Zero was deferred on 7 March 2018; winter rain then filled the dams to above 75%.
Mission check

?A city's reservoirs fall for three dry winters in a row while its population keeps growing. Using Cape Town as evidence, which explanation and response fit best?

Climate variability (three dry years, about three times as likely with climate change) met rising demand. Restrictions and farm cuts brought use from over 1,000 Ml to just below 500 Ml a day, and Day Zero was deferred until rain returned.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Soy fields have replaced rainforest in Mato Grosso. Why might the farms themselves end up paying for it?

Make a guess: Soy fields have replaced rainforest in Mato Grosso. Why might the farms themselves end up paying for it?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 3.4-4.a case study of one physical environment impacted by food production: short- and long-term impacts (southern Brazilian Amazon, Mato Grosso, soy)
Mission 2

The place and the driver

  • Physical environment: the southern Brazilian Amazon, where tropical rainforest meets the farming frontier in Mato Grosso state.
  • Driver: global demand for soy, mainly for animal feed. In 2025 China imported 111.8 million tonnes of soybeans; 82.3 million tonnes (73.6%) came from Brazil.
  • Mato Grosso was the Brazilian state with "the highest deforestation rate and soybean production since 2001".
Mission 2

Direct conversion: forest to cropland

  • Mechanised cropland in the Brazilian Amazon grew by more than 3.6 million hectares in 2001–2004.
  • In Mato Grosso, direct conversion of forest to cropland exceeded 540,000 hectares in 2001–2004, peaking at 23% of 2003's deforestation.
  • Clearings for cropland were twice the size of clearings for pasture, and more than 90% were planted within a year.
  • Area cleared for cropland tracked the soybean price (R² = 0.72), suggesting world prices influenced the pace of clearance.
Mission 2

Indirect conversion: soy pushes cattle

  • Soybean area in the Brazilian Amazon rose more than 10-fold, from 0.4 to 4.6 million hectares, between 2000 and 2019.
  • "Most soybean expansion occurred on pastures originally converted from natural vegetation for cattle production".
  • So soy can drive deforestation indirectly, by displacing pasture into the forest.
Quick check
?Soybean area in the Brazilian Amazon rose from 0.4 to 4.6 million hectares, 2000–2019. By how many times did it grow?
Mission 2

Short-term impacts: clearance and fire

  • Vegetation: forest is removed within a season; more than 90% of cropland clearings were planted in the first year.
  • Carbon: burning and decay release carbon stored in trees and soil; carbon emissions are higher in eastern than western Amazonia "mostly as a result of" fire emissions.
  • Fragmentation: large, fast clearings break forest into smaller patches.
Mission 2

Short-term impacts: streams

  • Upper Xingu basin, Mato Grosso: by 2010, over 40% of catchments outside protected areas were dominated by agriculture, with an estimated 10,000 small dams (impoundments).
  • Streams in soya bean catchments had average daily maxima 3 °C higher than forested ones (4 °C in pasture).
  • Warmer water degrades habitat for stream life. Keeping or restoring riparian forest strips could cut predicted warming "by as much as fivefold".
Quick check
?Which is a short-term, local impact of converting forest to soy in Mato Grosso?
Mission 2

Long-term impact: less rain

  • Leite-Filho et al. (2021): forest loss of up to 55–60% within 28 km grid cells raises rainfall, but further loss reduces it "precipitously".
  • The threshold is lower at larger scales: 25–30% forest loss in 112 km cells; at 224 km, rainfall falls in step with forest loss.
  • "Lower rainfall and agricultural productivity at larger scales outdo local gains": a negative-sum game.
  • Reducing deforestation would prevent agricultural losses of up to US$1 billion a year in the southern Brazilian Amazon.
Mission 2

Long-term impact: from sink to source

  • Gatti et al. (2021) made 590 aircraft profiles of CO₂ and CO over four Amazon sites, 2010–2018.
  • Southeastern Amazonia "acts as a net carbon source".
  • In the east, more deforestation and a more intense dry season "seem to promote ecosystem stress, increase in fire occurrence, and higher carbon emissions".
Mission 2

Long-term impact: tipping risk

  • Flores et al. (2024): by 2050, 10% to 47% of Amazonian forests will be exposed to compounding disturbances that may trigger unexpected ecosystem transitions.
  • Drivers: warming, extreme droughts, deforestation and fires.
  • Long-term change could be regional, not only local: such transitions could "potentially exacerbate regional climate change".
Quick check
?Leite-Filho et al. found that clearing forest raises rainfall in small grid cells but lowers it at larger scales. What does this mean for one farmer who clears more forest?
Mission 2

Management: the Soy Moratorium

  • 2006: "an agreement by grain traders not to purchase soy grown on recently deforested land".
  • It prevented an estimated 18,000 ± 9,000 km² of deforestation in 2006–2016; its success "was dependent on complementarities with public property registries and deforestation monitoring".
  • Brazil's competition regulator voted on 30 September 2025 to suspend it; in January 2026 major traders left after a Mato Grosso law removed tax benefits for participants.
  • In August 2026 Brazil's Supreme Court upheld that state law; environmentalists say the traders' exit effectively ended the moratorium (news reports).
  • Without it, protection for this environment rests on public rules and monitoring.
Mission 2

Exam move: short and long term

  • Short-term (months to years, local): clearance, fire, fragmentation, warmer streams.
  • Long-term (decades, regional): less rain, a carbon sink becoming a source, tipping risk.
  • Who pays: local streams and wildlife first; then farmers, as rain declines; then the world, through carbon.
  • Strong answers link scales: one farm's decision → many farms → regional climate → farm yields.
Mission check

?A state government proposes allowing farms to clear more forest to expand soy, arguing it will raise farm incomes. Using the evidence, what is the strongest long-term objection?

In the short term, clearing for soy removes forest, releases carbon and warms streams. In the long term, widespread clearance can reduce rainfall over large areas, cutting the yields that clearance was meant to raise: losses "outdo local gains". That is how the farms themselves can end up paying.

Mission 3: In 2022, 149 million young children were stunted while 2.5 billion adults were overweight. How can the world have too little food and too much at once?

Make a guess: In 2022, 149 million young children were stunted while 2.5 billion adults were overweight. How can the world have too little food and too much at once?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 3.4-4.a health issues of food shortages; of food surpluses and poor diet; harmful impacts of increased use of chemicals and pesticides
Mission 3

Many forms of malnutrition

  • Undernutrition: stunting (low height-for-age), wasting (low weight-for-height) and underweight.
  • Micronutrient deficiencies: too little of a vitamin or mineral, such as iron.
  • Overweight and obesity: too much energy for the body's needs.
  • All are forms of malnutrition: "bad nutrition", not only too little food.
Mission 3

Health issues of food shortages

  • 2022: 149 million children under five were stunted and 45 million wasted.
  • Stunting fell from 26.4% (2012) to 23.2% (2024) of children under five: progress, but slow.
  • Anaemia among women aged 15–49 rose from 27.6% (2012) to 30.7% (2023).
  • In famine, hunger and disease combine: Somalia, 2010–12, an estimated 258,000 deaths, 133,000 of them children under five.
Quick check
?A child is the right weight for her height but much shorter than healthy children of her age. Which form of malnutrition does she show?
Mission 3

Surpluses and changing diets

  • Popkin's nutrition transition: as incomes rise, famine recedes, then diets shift.
  • Pattern 4: more processed food, more eating away from home, more edible oils and sugar-sweetened drinks, and less physical activity.
  • In lower-income countries these changes come "at great speed and at earlier stages" of development.
Mission 3

Health issues of surpluses and poor diet

  • 2022: 2.5 billion adults were overweight, 890 million of them living with obesity.
  • Adult obesity rose from 12.1% (2012) to 16.2% (2024).
  • Overweight and obesity are metabolic risk factors for non-communicable diseases (NCDs) such as heart disease and diabetes; unhealthy diet is one of WHO's four main behavioural NCD risks.
Mission 3

The double burden

  • Double burden of malnutrition: "the coexistence of undernutrition along with overweight, obesity or diet-related noncommunicable diseases".
  • It "affects most low-income and middle-income countries"; Indonesia is "the largest country with a severe DBM".
  • Causes include cheap ultra-processed food and drinks, and "major reductions in physical activity".
  • So one country, even one household, can hold a stunted child and an overweight adult.
Quick check
?Of the 2.5 billion overweight adults in 2022, 890 million were living with obesity. What percentage of overweight adults were obese?
Mission 3

Chemicals and pesticides: the health risks

  • More intensive farming uses more agrochemicals. WHO: pesticides "are potentially toxic to humans and can have both acute and chronic health effects".
  • Acute: poisoning soon after high exposure. Chronic: long-term effects "that may include cancer and adverse effects on reproduction".
  • "The population most at risk are those who are directly exposed": farm workers who mix and spray them.
  • Pesticides are "among the leading causes of death by self-poisoning, particularly in low- and middle-income countries".
Mission 3

Residues in food: how risk is managed

  • Consumers meet much lower doses, as residues on food.
  • An independent expert group (JMPR) assesses residues and sets limits for safe intake.
  • Governments use them to set maximum residue limits (MRLs); Codex Alimentarius, the intergovernmental food-standards body, sets international ones.
  • Exam move: separate occupational exposure (high dose, few people) from dietary exposure (low dose, many people); weak regulation and protective kit raise the risk.
Quick check
?Who faces the greatest health risk from increased pesticide use?
Mission check

?In one low-income district, farm wages buy too little food, a supermarket chain has arrived selling cheap sugary drinks and snacks, and spraying of pesticides has doubled. Which set of health risks should a health officer expect?

Too little food (stunting, wasting, anaemia) and too much of the wrong food (overweight, NCDs) now exist side by side, the double burden driven by the nutrition transition; intensive farming adds risks from pesticides, highest for those directly exposed.

Mission 4

Mission 4: What does going without enough food mean for a family in the UK, and for one in Somalia?

Make a guess: What does going without enough food mean for a family in the UK, and for one in Somalia?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 3.4-4.a case studies of two places at contrasting levels of development: implications of poor food security for lives
Mission 4

What food security means

  • FAO: "Food security exists when all people, at all times, have physical and economic access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food".
  • Four parts: availability (enough food produced or imported), access (money and markets), utilisation (safe, nutritious diet, clean water), stability (at all times).
  • Poor food security ranges from worry and cutting back (low food security) to skipping meals and hunger (very low), to famine.
Mission 4

Two places, two levels of development

  • United Kingdom: high-income country (World Bank); an advanced country in OCR's terms.
  • Somalia: low-income country (World Bank); a low-income developing country (LIDC).
  • Same question in both: what does poor food security do to people's lives?
Mission 4

UK: how many and who

  • 2023–24: 90% of UK households were food secure; 5% had low and 5% very low food security (DWP Family Resources Survey).
  • Households with children: 8% low, 8% very low.
  • Households on income-related benefits: 13% low, 17% very low.
  • Households with a disabled adult: 7% low, 9% very low.
Mission 4

UK: implications for lives

  • Access, not availability: shops are full; the problem is money.
  • Food banks: 3.6% of households used one in the past 12 months, 13% of those on income-related benefits. Trussell food banks gave out over 2.6 million emergency parcels in 2025.
  • Lives change through trade-offs: cheaper, less varied food, skipped meals, stress, and reliance on charity.
  • Mainly chronic, linked to income, disability and household type, rather than to a single shock.
Quick check
?Using the FRS 2023–24 data, which UK group had the highest share of households with low or very low food security?
Mission 4

Somalia: how severe

  • Global Hunger Index 2026: Somalia scored 47.0, ranked 122nd of 122, in the "alarming" band.
  • 2010–12 famine: about 258,000 people died between October 2010 and April 2012, 133,000 of them children under five.
  • Causes named: "the 2010-11 drought, subsequent spikes in staple food prices, and constraints on humanitarian access".
Mission 4

Somalia: implications for lives

  • Death: a study for Somalia's health ministry, WHO and UNICEF estimated 43,000 excess deaths in the 2022 drought, perhaps half of them children under five.
  • Livelihoods: "households engaged in rain-fed agriculture, agropastoralism, and pastoralism are vulnerable to drought"; when herds die, income and food go together.
  • Displacement: at least 180,000 refugees from Somalia and South Sudan crossed into drought-hit areas of Kenya and Ethiopia.
  • Dependence on aid: there "could have been many more deaths had humanitarian assistance not been scaled up".
  • Gender: in scarcity, "women and girls may eat a lower quantity and/or poorer quality food" (FAO).
Quick check
?In Somalia's 2010–12 famine, about 258,000 people died, 133,000 of them children under five. What share of deaths were under-fives?
Mission 4

Comparing the two

  • Scale: in the UK, about 1 household in 10 is food insecure; in Somalia, food insecurity is national and repeated.
  • Severity: UK — cutting back and skipping meals; Somalia — malnutrition and excess deaths, mostly of young children.
  • Cause: UK — low income in a full market (access); Somalia — drought, prices and conflict cut both production and access.
  • Safety nets: UK — benefits, food banks; Somalia — humanitarian aid, often limited by access and funding.
Quick check
?Which statement best compares food insecurity in the two places?
Mission check

?A country has full supermarkets, but some families skip meals near the end of each month. Another has empty markets after failed rains, and child deaths rise. Which conclusion is best supported?

Poor food security in an advanced country is mainly about access and hard choices; in a low-income country it can mean production failure, malnutrition and death.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Who really decides what ends up on your plate: farmers, governments or supermarkets?

Make a guess: Who really decides what ends up on your plate: farmers, governments or supermarkets?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 3.4-5.a opportunities between countries: agricultural trade policy, the WTO, appropriate aid
  • 3.4-5.a roles of agribusiness, TNCs (e.g. Unilever), retailers (e.g. Tesco) and fair trade organisations
Mission 5

Food is a geopolitical commodity

  • Food is traded, taxed, subsidised, given as aid and used for influence, so states and firms compete to control it.
  • Power concentrates where few firms control a stage: "just three companies could control more than 70% of agrochemicals, and more than 60% of proprietary seeds worldwide".
  • A few grain traders "have been estimated to account for up to 90% of global grain trade".
  • Agribusiness: large-scale, capital-intensive businesses that link farming with inputs, processing and trade.
Mission 5

Trade policy and the WTO

  • The WTO deals with "the rules of trade between nations"; 166 members, "98 per cent of world trade".
  • Its Agreement on Agriculture "came into force in 1995" and has three pillars:
  • Market access: "tariffs on imports". Domestic support: subsidies that "directly stimulate production and distort trade". Export competition: "export subsidies".
  • 2015, Nairobi: members agreed "to abolish subsidies for farm exports".
  • Why it matters: subsidised exports can undercut farmers in poorer countries; tariffs can protect them, or keep their exports out of rich markets.
Quick check
?A rich country pays its dairy farmers a fixed sum for every litre they produce. Which WTO pillar covers this?
Mission 5

Appropriate aid

  • Appropriate aid fits local needs and markets, and does not undercut local farmers.
  • Cash instead of shipped food: WFP sends money to give people "the flexibility to choose what they need", and "When people spend money in local economies, it creates jobs and supports markets".
  • Scale: in 2019 WFP transferred "US$2.1 billion of purchasing power to people in 64 countries", 38% of its assistance.
  • Cash works only "where markets are functioning but people can't afford to buy food"; where markets have collapsed, food itself is needed.
  • Aid budgets are falling: official development assistance "fell in 2025 by 23.1% in real terms" (OECD).
Quick check
?After a drought, markets in a region still have food, but families cannot afford it. Which aid is most appropriate?
Mission 5

TNCs: Unilever

  • Transnational corporations process and brand food across many countries, buying raw materials from farms worldwide.
  • Unilever: €50.5 billion turnover in 2025, 59% in emerging markets; products sold in 190 countries; used by 3.7 billion people every day; a Foods business group (condiments, cooking aids, food service).
  • Power: brands, marketing and huge purchasing set prices and standards for suppliers; Unilever says it is "working to improve the livelihoods of people in our global value chain".
Mission 5

Retailers: Tesco

  • Supermarkets are gatekeepers between farmers and shoppers: they set prices, specifications and payment terms.
  • Tesco employs "more than 330,000 colleagues".
  • The UK Groceries Code Adjudicator (from 25 June 2013) regulates retailers with groceries turnover over £1 billion and their direct suppliers; Tesco is one of 14 designated retailers.
  • Its investigation found Tesco seriously breached the Code between June 2013 and February 2015, including unilateral deductions and delayed payments; the Adjudicator saw "Tesco at times prioritising its own finances over treating suppliers fairly".
Quick check
?Why did the UK create a Groceries Code Adjudicator?
Mission 5

Fair trade organisations

  • Fairtrade sets standards with "social, economic, and environmental criteria", a minimum price and a Premium for communities.
  • Washed Arabica coffee minimum price: $1.80 a pound for contracts from 1 August 2023, up from $1.40.
  • 2024: €201.6 million Premium paid; 1,871,272 farmers and workers in Fairtrade.
  • Limits: certified coffee was 1.8% of global coffee exports in 2009; a DFID-funded study found wage workers near Fairtrade producer groups were, in some cases, "significantly worse paid, and treated".
Quick check
?Fairtrade paid €201.6 million in Premium in 2024 to 1,871,272 farmers and workers. Roughly what is that per person, and why is this misleading?
Mission 5

Who holds the power?

  • Governments and the WTO set the rules of trade and subsidies.
  • Agribusiness and traders control inputs and bulk flows.
  • TNCs and retailers control brands, shelves and payment terms.
  • Fair trade organisations bargain for producers, but over a small share of trade.
  • Aid agencies decide how emergency food reaches people.
  • Exam move (OCR "Assess"): weigh players by how much of the chain they control, and how far rules (WTO, the Adjudicator) limit them.
Mission check

?Smallholders grow cocoa that passes through two global traders and one chocolate TNC before reaching supermarkets. Which change would most raise the growers' share of the final price?

Power in the food system sits where few players control a stage. Rules (WTO, the Adjudicator) and fair trade can rebalance it, but only partly.

Mission 6

A spectrum of strategies

  • By time scale: short-term relief (food now) → capacity building (skills, incomes, institutions) → long-term system redesign (laws, markets, how food is produced and shared).
  • By scale of technique: large-scale technology (new crop varieties, indoor farms) → small-scale, bottom-up, appropriate approaches (led by communities, cheap, locally maintained).
  • By pillar: raise availability, improve access, improve use.
Mission 6

Short-term relief

  • Relief feeds people in a crisis, but does not remove the cause.
  • UK: food banks in the Trussell community gave out more than 2.6 million emergency food parcels in 2025.
  • Trussell itself says: "We need long-term solutions to ensure no one in the UK needs to turn to a food bank to get by".
Mission 6

Capacity building from the bottom up

  • Farmer Field Schools (FAO): groups of farmers, herders or fisherfolk learn by doing, through "Practical field exercises using direct observation, discussion and decision making".
  • FAO says they have "empowered more than 10 million farmers globally".
  • Strengths: cheap, locally owned, adapted to local conditions. Limits: slow to scale, and evidence is often reported by the programme itself.
Quick check
?Which is an example of capacity building rather than short-term relief?
Mission 6

Case 1: Brazil (upper-middle income)

  • Brazil is upper-middle income.
  • In OCR's terms, which follow the IMF's groups, Brazil is an emerging and developing country (EDC).
  • The Zero Hunger (Fome Zero) project, launched in October 2001, became the government's main strategy from 2003.
  • Bolsa Família gives cash to poor families who keep their children in school and attend health check-ups.
  • By 2013 it reached nearly 14 million households, about a quarter of the population, at around 0.5–0.6% of GDP.
Mission 6

Brazil: school meals and family farms

  • A 2009 law: at least 30% of federal school-meal funds must buy food directly from family farms.
  • A 2025 law raised the minimum to 45%.
  • One budget does two jobs: it feeds pupils (access) and gives small farmers a steady market (availability and incomes).
Mission 6

Brazil: results and setbacks

  • Brazil left the Hunger Map in 2014: undernourishment below 2.5%.
  • It returned in 2019–2022, after an economic crisis and COVID-19.
  • It left again in 2025: undernourishment averaged below 2.5% in 2022–2024.
Mission 6

Brazil on the spectrum

  • Relief: cash meets poor families' immediate needs.
  • Capacity building: school attendance and health check-ups are conditions of the cash.
  • System redesign: laws tie public food buying to family farms.
  • Exam move: weigh two exits (2014, 2025) against one return: success depended on sustained policy and incomes.
Mission 6

Large-scale technology: GM crops

  • US National Academies (2016): "no substantiated evidence of a difference in risks to human health" from GM crops then on sale; but "no evidence that GE crops had changed the rate of increase in yields".
  • Golden Rice carries extra beta-carotene against vitamin A deficiency, which affects more than 15% of Filipino children under six.
  • The Philippines approved it for commercial propagation in July 2021, the first country to do so; in April 2024 a court revoked the permit.
  • Technology can raise supply or nutrition, but law, politics and public trust decide whether it spreads.
Mission 6

Case 2: Singapore (high income)

  • In OCR's terms Singapore is an advanced country (AC).
  • Singapore is high income. It imports over 90% of its food, with about 1% of its land available for food production.
  • "Three food baskets": diversify import sources (about 170 countries and regions), grow locally, and grow overseas.
  • In November 2025 it set new 2035 targets, replacing its earlier "30-by-30" goal: local farms to supply 20% of fibre and 30% of protein eaten.
Mission 6

Singapore: high-tech farming

  • Vertical farming: crops grown indoors in stacked layers under artificial light.
  • Lettuce from an indoor plant factory needs 247 kWh of purchased energy per kg dry weight; a Dutch greenhouse 70 kWh.
  • But plant factories use water, CO₂ and land more productively.
  • Singapore approved cell-based meat for sale in 2020, the first country to do so. Its minister notes alternative proteins faced "higher production costs and weaker than expected consumer acceptance globally".
Quick check
?Using the figures just taught, how many times as much purchased energy does the plant factory use per kg of lettuce as the Dutch greenhouse?
Mission 6

Cutting loss and waste

  • Food loss: over 13% of food is lost after harvest and before retail.
  • Food waste: 1.05 billion tonnes in 2022, 132 kg per person; households 60%.
  • SDG 12.3: halve per capita food waste at retail and consumer levels by 2030.
  • France's 2016 law bans destroying unsold edible food and makes large supermarkets seek a food-aid charity partner. The source does not measure its effect.

Mission check

?Singapore has little hunger, yet imports over 90% of its food. What best keeps it food-secure over the long term?

The hook's answer is the same idea in a different place. Brazil tackled access with cash transfers and school meals bought from family farms. Hunger fell below 2.5% by 2014, returned in 2019–2022, and fell again by 2025: ending hunger takes sustained policy, not just more food.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Supply depends on climate, geology and drainage, and on quality. Raising food output brings salinisation, deforestation and agrochemical pollution.

Mission 2

  • Soy for global markets turned forest into cropland in Mato Grosso, directly (>540,000 ha in 2001–04) and indirectly by displacing pasture.
  • Short-term impacts are local (clearance, fire, warmer streams); long-term impacts are regional (less rain, carbon source, tipping risk) and feed back on farming itself.

Mission 3

  • Shortages bring stunting, wasting, micronutrient deficiency and, in famine, death from hunger and disease.
  • Surpluses and poor diets bring overweight, obesity and NCDs; both burdens can coexist in one country.
  • Pesticides cause acute poisoning and possible long-term harm; workers directly exposed face the greatest risk.

Mission 4

  • UK (high income): about 10% of households food insecure (2023–24), concentrated among benefit claimants, families with children and disabled adults; implications are trade-offs, stress and food bank use.
  • Somalia (low income): drought, prices and blocked access cause famine-level crises, with excess deaths (perhaps half among under-fives), lost livelihoods and displacement.

Mission 5

  • Trade policy (WTO Agreement on Agriculture), appropriate aid (e.g. cash where markets work) and fair trade create opportunities between countries.
  • Agribusiness, TNCs (Unilever) and retailers (Tesco) hold power where they control a stage of the chain; regulators and fair trade organisations try to rebalance it.

Mission 6

  • Strategies run from short-term relief to capacity building and system redesign, and from large-scale technology to small-scale, bottom-up approaches.
  • Brazil (upper-middle income) fixed access through cash and school meals; Singapore (high income) manages import dependence through diverse sources and technology. Both show that results last only if policy lasts.
