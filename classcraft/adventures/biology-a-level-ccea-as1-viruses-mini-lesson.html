Mission 1

Mission 1: A bacterium has no nucleus. So where does it keep its genes, and what else does it manage without?

Make a guess: A bacterium has no nucleus. So where does it keep its genes, and what else does it manage without?

Mission 1

What every cell has

  • Every cell has: a plasma membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes and DNA.
  • Two kinds of cell:
    • Prokaryotic (bacteria, archaea): no nucleus.
  • Your board's kingdom name for prokaryotes: Prokaryotae.
    • Eukaryotic (animals, plants, fungi, protists): a nucleus.
Mission 1

Inside a bacterium

  • No nucleus. One circular DNA molecule lies free in the cytoplasm, in the nucleoid region.
  • Prokaryotic DNA is short and not associated with proteins (no histones).
  • Ribosomes are smaller: 70S (eukaryotic cytoplasm: 80S).
  • No membrane-bound organelles: no mitochondria, no ER, no Golgi.
  • Cell wall of peptidoglycan, outside the plasma membrane.
  • Often present: plasmids (small extra DNA circles), a capsule, one or more flagella.
  • Say: "the DNA is in the nucleoid" — not: "the DNA is in a small nucleus".

Quick check
?A unicellular organism is found to have a cell wall, a plasma membrane and ribosomes, but no nucleus. Its DNA is a single circular molecule, not associated with histones, lying in the cytoplasm. A student concludes that the organism is a prokaryote. Which evaluation of this conclusion is correct?
Mission 1

Why a bacterium needs its wall

  • Bacterial cytoplasm is rich in solutes, so in dilute surroundings water enters by osmosis.
  • The wall is strong: it resists swelling and stops the cell bursting.
  • Water and small solutes pass through the wall; the plasma membrane, inside it, controls what enters the cell.
  • Strip the wall away (e.g. with the enzyme lysozyme) and a bacterium in water bursts.
Mission 1

Binary fission

  • Bacteria reproduce by binary fission: one cell → two.
  • (1) The circular DNA is replicated. Plasmids are replicated too.
  • (2) The cell grows; the two DNA copies move apart.
  • (3) The cytoplasm divides; new membrane and wall grow across the middle.
  • Each daughter cell: one copy of the circular DNA and a variable number of plasmids.
  • No nucleus, no spindle, no chromosomes condensing: binary fission is not mitosis.
  • Say: "binary fission" — not: "the bacterium divides by mitosis".
Quick check
?A bacterial cell contains one circular DNA molecule and six copies of a plasmid. It divides once by binary fission. Which statement about the two daughter cells is correct?
Mission check

?An electron micrograph shows a cell about 3 µm long. It has a cell wall, a paler fibrous region of DNA not enclosed by any membrane, many small dense granules, and no membrane-bound organelles. How should the cell be classified?

No nucleus, but not disorganised: the genes sit in the nucleoid, and the cell manages without membrane-bound organelles.

Mission 2: A virus cannot feed, grow or divide. So how does one virus become millions?

Make a guess: A virus cannot feed, grow or divide. So how does one virus become millions?

Mission 2

A virus is not a cell

  • A virus particle is acellular: no cytoplasm, no ribosomes, few or no enzymes.
  • It has genetic material, DNA or RNA, never both.
  • The genetic material sits inside a capsid: a coat of protein.
  • Attachment proteins on the surface bind to receptors on a host cell.
  • Viruses are non-living: they cannot replicate outside a host cell.
  • Viruses do not divide. The host cell's ribosomes, enzymes and ATP make new virus particles.
  • Say: "virus particle" — not: "virus cell".
Quick check
?Which feature is shared by all viruses?
Mission 2

Two very different viruses

  • Bacteriophage (phage): infects bacteria.
    • DNA inside a protein head; a protein tail injects the DNA.
  • HIV: infects human helper T cells.
    • RNA (two single strands) inside a protein capsid.
    • An envelope: a lipid bilayer taken from the host cell, studded with glycoproteins.
    • Carries the enzyme reverse transcriptase: HIV is a retrovirus.

Quick check
?A newly discovered particle 30 nm across contains one molecule of single-stranded RNA inside a protein shell. It has no ribosomes and it multiplies only inside living cells. How does the particle multiply?
Mission 2

How a host cell makes viruses

  • (1) Attachment: viral proteins bind to complementary receptors on the host cell.
  • (2) Entry: the nucleic acid enters. A phage injects its DNA; the capsid stays outside.
  • (3) Synthesis: the host's ribosomes, enzymes, nucleotides, amino acids and ATP make viral nucleic acid and proteins.
  • (4) Assembly: new particles self-assemble.
  • (5) Release: phage enzymes break down the wall; the cell bursts (lysis), releasing new phages.
  • The host cell is destroyed: phages destroy bacteria; HIV destroys helper T cells, weakening the immune system.
  • One cell can release hundreds of new particles: that is how numbers explode.
Quick check
?Bacteriophage lambda does not multiply in a sterile nutrient broth containing amino acids, nucleotides and ATP, but does multiply when living E. coli cells are added to the broth. Which explanation is correct?
Mission 2

Retroviruses: from RNA back to DNA

  • HIV is a retrovirus: its genome is RNA.
  • (1) Reverse transcriptase copies the viral RNA into DNA.
  • (2) The viral DNA joins the host cell's DNA.
  • (3) The host transcribes and translates it: new viral RNA and proteins.
  • (4) New particles assemble and leave the cell.
Quick check
?A hypothetical drug is proposed to block reverse transcriptase. Researchers add the drug to human cells in culture and then expose the cells to a retrovirus. If the drug works as proposed, which result is predicted?
Mission check

?The antibiotic tetracycline blocks bacterial ribosomes but does not block the ribosomes in the cytoplasm of human cells. A doctor is asked whether tetracycline would stop influenza viruses multiplying in the cells lining a patient's airways. Which answer is correct?

One virus becomes millions because each infected cell becomes a factory, using its own ribosomes, enzymes and ATP to build new particles.

Mission 3

Mission 3: How does one virus turn the body's own defenders into factories that make more of it?

Make a guess: How does one virus turn the body's own defenders into factories that make more of it?

Mission 3

The HIV particle

  • HIV (human immunodeficiency virus) is a retrovirus.
  • Genome: two copies of single-stranded RNA.
  • Enzymes packed inside: reverse transcriptase (and others).
  • Capsid: a protein coat around the RNA.
  • Envelope: a lipid bilayer, taken from the host cell membrane.
  • Attachment proteins (glycoproteins) stick out of the envelope.
Mission 3

Inside a helper T cell

  • (1) The attachment protein binds a CD4 receptor on a helper T cell.
  • (2) The envelope fuses with the cell membrane. Capsid enters, then releases RNA and enzymes.
  • (3) Reverse transcriptase copies the viral RNA into DNA.
  • (4) The viral DNA enters the nucleus and is inserted into the host cell's DNA.
  • (5) The host cell transcribes and translates it: viral RNA and proteins.
  • (6) New particles assemble and bud out, wrapped in host membrane.
  • The inserted DNA can stay inactive for years, then switch on.
Quick check
?HIV enters some white blood cells but not others. Which statement identifies the cells it mainly enters, and why?
Mission 3

Read the data: from HIV to AIDS

  • The graph: a typical untreated infection (schematic).
  • Weeks 2–6: virus multiplies fast. Helper T cells fall. Often flu-like illness.
  • Years: few or no symptoms. Helper T cells fall slowly.
  • AIDS: helper T cells below about 200 per mm³, or an AIDS-defining illness.
  • Few helper T cells: B cells are not activated (few antibodies); killer T cells are not stimulated.
  • So opportunistic infections and some cancers take hold: e.g. TB, pneumonia, Kaposi's sarcoma.
  • Say: "HIV destroys helper T cells" — not: "HIV destroys the immune system".
Quick check
?A person with untreated HIV infection eventually becomes unable to produce antibodies against new pathogens, even though tests show that B-lymphocytes are still present in their blood in substantial numbers. Which statement explains this?
Mission 3

Diseases that jump from animals

  • A zoonosis: an infectious disease that can pass from another species to humans.
  • Common: most known human pathogens are zoonotic.
  • COVID-19: SARS-CoV-2's closest known relatives are bat viruses; once in humans it spread person to person.
  • HIV itself came from related viruses in chimpanzees and other primates.
  • A reservoir: a species that carries a virus, often without illness. Bats carry rabies-like viruses, Nipah, Marburg and SARS-like coronaviruses.
  • Epidemic: cases rise well above normal in one region. Pandemic: an epidemic across many countries.
Quick check
?Which statement about COVID-19 is correct?
Mission check

?Two people infected with HIV were compared. Person X had 900 helper T-lymphocytes per mm³ of blood and no other infections. Person Y had 150 helper T-lymphocytes per mm³ and had developed pneumonia caused by a fungus that rarely causes disease in healthy people. Which conclusion is best supported?

HIV turns helper T cells into virus factories and destroys them. Without them, other pathogens do the killing.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A prokaryote keeps one circular DNA molecule in the nucleoid, with no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles; it still has a membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes and (usually) a wall.
  • Binary fission: DNA and plasmids replicate, the cytoplasm divides; each daughter gets one circular DNA and a variable number of plasmids.

Mission 2

  • A virus is an acellular particle: DNA or RNA in a protein capsid, with no metabolism; host cells make and assemble new particles, often hundreds per cell.
  • Retroviruses such as HIV use reverse transcriptase to copy RNA into DNA that joins the host genome.

Mission 3

  • HIV binds helper T cells, copies its RNA into DNA, inserts it into host DNA and is made by the cell; losing helper T cells leads to AIDS and opportunistic infections.
  • Zoonoses pass from other species to humans; bats are major virus reservoirs.
