Mission 1

Systems: open and closed

  • A system has inputs, outputs, stores and flows (transfers) between stores.
  • Closed system: energy crosses the boundary, but matter does not. The global hydrological cycle is closed: Earth's water is neither gained nor lost.
  • Open system: energy and matter cross the boundary. A drainage basin is open: water enters as precipitation and leaves as river flow and evapotranspiration.
Mission 1

Where Earth's water is stored

  • Oceans hold 96.54% of all Earth's water. Ice caps, glaciers and permanent snow hold 1.74%; groundwater 1.69%, just under half of it fresh.
  • The atmosphere holds 0.001%, rivers 0.0002% and living things (biological water) 0.0001%.
  • Only about 2.5% of Earth's water is fresh. Of that fresh water, 68.7% is ice and snow and 30.1% is groundwater.
Mission 1

Global fluxes and the energy that drives them

  • Solar energy drives evaporation and transpiration. Gravitational potential energy brings water back down as precipitation and moves it downhill to the sea.
  • Annual fluxes (thousand km³ a year): precipitation on land 112.6; evapotranspiration from land 72.6; runoff from land to ocean 40.0; precipitation on the ocean 372.8.
  • Land receives 40.0 more than it evaporates. The same 40.0 returns as runoff, so ocean evaporation must be about 413 (372.8 + 40.0, calculated, assuming the ocean store stays steady).
  • The difference is carried from ocean to land as water vapour in the atmosphere.
Mission 1

Residence time

  • Residence time: the average time water stays in a store.
  • Atmosphere 9–10 days; rivers 12–20 days; soil moisture about 280 days.
  • Lakes 1–100 years; groundwater about 300 years; ice caps, glaciers and permanent snow more than 1,000 years.
  • Residence time ≈ store ÷ flux through it. Small, fast stores (rivers, atmosphere) are refilled from big, slow ones.

Mission 1

Fossil water and cryosphere losses

  • The global water budget limits usable water: most fresh water is ice or deep groundwater, not rivers and lakes.
  • Fossil water: groundwater that infiltrated before the present warm period (the Holocene). It makes up 42–85% of the water stored in aquifers in the upper 1 km of the crust.
  • Pumping it out is non-renewable on human timescales: it is not being replaced.
  • Cryosphere losses: glaciers lost 267 ± 16 gigatonnes of ice a year in 2000–2019. Meltwater raises river flow for now, but the store is shrinking.
Mission 1

Basin inputs and outputs

  • Input: precipitation. Its type (rain or snow), intensity and duration decide what happens next.
  • The spec calls this precipitation patterns: how much, how intense, and when in the year.
  • Outputs: evaporation and transpiration (together evapotranspiration), and channel flow (runoff) leaving at the mouth.
Mission 1

Stores

  • Interception: water held on leaves and stems.
  • Surface storage: puddles, ponds and lakes. Soil water: water held in the soil.
  • Groundwater: water in permeable rock (aquifers). Channel storage: water in the river itself.
  • Stores delay water on its way to the river.
Mission 1

Flows

  • Throughfall (drips through the canopy) and stemflow (runs down stems and trunks) carry intercepted water to the ground.
  • Infiltration: water enters the soil. Percolation: it moves down into the rock.
  • Overland flow: infiltration-excess flow when rain is more intense than the soil can absorb; saturated overland flow when the soil is already full.
  • Throughflow: downslope through the soil. Groundwater flow: slowly through rock to the channel (base flow). Channel flow: in the river.
  • Overland flow and fast throughflow reaching the channel during a storm are called direct runoff.

Mission 1

Physical factors that control the basin

  • Climate: rainfall amount and intensity set the input; temperature sets evapotranspiration.
  • Soils: sandy soils let water infiltrate; clay soils have a low infiltration capacity, so more overland flow.
  • Vegetation: interception and transpiration reduce and delay runoff.
  • Geology: the Lambourn (Berkshire, on chalk) gets 97% of its long-term flow from stored sources; the Beult (Kent, mainly clay) 23% (base flow index 0.97 vs 0.23).
  • Relief: steep slopes speed overland flow and throughflow.
Mission 1

The water balance

  • P = Q + E ± ΔS: precipitation = runoff + evapotranspiration ± change in storage.
  • Over many years storage changes little, so E ≈ P − Q.
  • River Lambourn (234.1 km², on chalk): mean catchment rainfall 768 mm a year, 1962–2019.
  • Its mean flow, 1.743 m³/s, equals 235 mm of runoff a year spread over the catchment (our calculation).

Mission 1

Groundwater feeds rivers

  • In a USGS study of 54 US streams, groundwater supplied on average 52% of streamflow, from 14% to 90%.
  • The Lambourn's chalk aquifer keeps it flowing: its flow is at least 0.77 m³/s for 95% of the time. For the Beult, a clay river in Kent, it is only 0.057 m³/s.
  • So how a basin stores water decides how its river behaves in dry spells.
Mission 1

Ice feeds rivers

  • In the upper Indus basin, glacier melt supplied 40.6% of total runoff (1998–2007); in the upper Ganges, 11.5%.
  • Meltwater arrives in the warm season, often when rain is scarce.
  • The river is the smallest, fastest store. Groundwater and ice are the big, slow stores that refill it.
Mission check

?After a dry month with almost no rain, the River Lambourn is still flowing. Which explanation is best?

The river is a tiny, fast store. It keeps flowing because big, slow stores drain into it: groundwater as base flow (on average 52% of flow in 54 US streams) and meltwater from ice and snow (40.6% of runoff in the upper Indus).

Mission 2

The storm hydrograph

  • A storm hydrograph plots discharge against time after a rainfall event; rainfall is shown as bars.
  • Rising limb, peak discharge, falling (recession) limb.
  • Lag time: from peak rainfall to peak discharge.
  • Base flow (slow groundwater) lies beneath; storm flow (quickflow) sits on top.

Mission 2

Two rivers, one storm

  • Beult, Kent: 277.1 km². Lambourn, Berkshire: 234.1 km².
  • 23 December 2013 catchment rainfall: Beult 42.6 mm; Lambourn 44.9 mm.
  • Beult: daily mean flow rose from 32.0 to 79.4 m³/s on 24 December, the highest daily mean in its record from 1958 (the record has gaps).
  • Lambourn: from 1.42 to 2.39 m³/s. It peaked only on 15 February 2014, at 9.53 m³/s, after a very wet January and February.
Mission 2

Chalk and clay

  • The Lambourn drains the Chalk: about 97% of its bedrock is highly permeable. Rain infiltrates and percolates, then reaches the river slowly as base flow.
  • The Beult drains a "predominantly clay catchment": about 74% of its bedrock has low permeability. Rain runs off quickly as overland flow and throughflow.
  • Drainage density (NRFA index): Beult 1.11, Lambourn 0.17. More channels carry water away faster.
  • Base flow index: Lambourn 0.97, Beult 0.23.

Mission 2

Physical factors

  • Basin size: large basins have longer lag times. Shape: in a round basin water from all parts arrives together, giving a higher, earlier peak.
  • Relief: steep slopes speed runoff. Soil and geology: clay and impermeable rock shed water; sand, chalk and limestone absorb it.
  • Drainage density: more channels per km² means a faster, higher peak.
  • Precipitation: intense rain exceeds infiltration capacity; snow is stored until it melts. Antecedent conditions: the Beult was already high after rain on 16–22 December.
  • Vegetation: interception and transpiration reduce and delay runoff.
Mission 2

Human factors

  • Urbanisation: impermeable roofs and roads, plus drains, cut infiltration. Lag time shortens and the peak rises.
  • Land use: deforestation and bare, compacted farmland speed runoff; woodland and permanent grass slow it.
  • Planners manage this: zoning to keep building off floodplains, and sustainable drainage (permeable paving, ponds) to restore infiltration.

Mission 2

Annual regimes: seasonal change

  • A river regime is the pattern of discharge through an average year.
  • Monthly mean flows (calculated from NRFA daily data): the Beult peaks in January (5.53 m³/s) and is lowest in July (0.22). The Lambourn peaks in March (2.59) and is lowest in September–October (1.11, 1.10).
  • Monthly rainfall varies much less: 47–84 mm on the Beult, 53–79 mm on the Lambourn.
Mission 2

Why regimes differ

  • Summer flows fall mainly because evapotranspiration is higher, not because rain stops.
  • Clay (Beult): little storage, so flow follows the season closely.
  • Chalk (Lambourn): the aquifer fills in winter and drains slowly, so the peak comes late, in March, and summer flow stays high.
  • In cold climates, snowmelt gives a spring or summer peak, and frozen ground acts like an impermeable surface.
Mission 2

A cold-climate regime: the Yukon

  • Yukon River at Pilot Station, Alaska: mean flow is lowest in April (about 1,410 m³/s) and highest in June (about 16,200 m³/s), about 11 times more (calculated from USGS monthly data, 1975–2026).
  • "Most of the flow occurs in the summer months from snowmelt, rainfall, and glacial melt".
  • Glacier-fed tributaries (the Tanana and White) give 29% of the Yukon's flow; the Porcupine and Koyukuk, on continuous permafrost, give 22%.
  • In winter, precipitation is stored as snow and ice, so flow falls to its minimum.
Mission 2

Tropical and mountain regimes: Amazon and Indus

  • Amazon at Óbidos: discharge is highest in June and lowest in November. Rain is heaviest in January–March and lightest in August, so the lowest flow comes about three months after the driest month. The huge basin and its floodplains delay and smooth the response.
  • Indus above Tarbela: in the April–September season, modelled flow came 53% from snowmelt, 21% from glacier melt and 26% from rain (2003–2014).
  • So the Indus peaks in the warm season, when snow and ice melt; the Amazon peaks months after the rainy season.
Mission 2

Water budgets by climate

  • A water budget compares precipitation (P) with evapotranspiration (E) through the year, and shows what happens to soil water.
  • Soil moisture surplus: P > E and the soil is full; extra water feeds rivers and groundwater. Utilisation: E > P, so plants draw on soil water. Deficit: soil water runs out. Recharge: P > E again and the soil refills.
  • Temperate (Lambourn): rainfall 768 mm a year, runoff 235 mm, so about 533 mm is evapotranspired (our calculation); a winter surplus and a summer deficit.
  • Tropical rainforest: high P all year, so a surplus most months. Polar: low P and low E; water is stored as snow and released in a short summer melt.

Mission 3

Normal conditions in the tropical Pacific

  • Trade winds blow west along the equator, pushing warm surface water from South America towards Asia.
  • Cold, nutrient-rich water upwells off South America to replace it.
  • Warm water piles up in the west. Air rises over it and sinks over the cooler east: the Walker circulation.
Mission 3

El Niño and La Niña

  • El Niño: above-average sea surface temperatures in the central and eastern tropical Pacific; trade winds weaken or reverse.
  • The rising branch of the Walker circulation shifts east of 180°: rain falls over the central Pacific and decreases over Indonesia.
  • La Niña: cooler than average there, stronger trade winds, more upwelling, more rain over Indonesia.
  • The thermocline deepens in El Niño (typically 150–175 m) and shallows in La Niña (typically 50–100 m).
Mission 3

ENSO: one coupled system

  • ENSO (El Niño–Southern Oscillation) is coupled: ocean and atmosphere change together.
  • The Southern Oscillation is a see-saw of air pressure between Tahiti and Darwin. A strongly negative SOI goes with El Niño; strongly positive with La Niña.
  • NOAA's Oceanic Niño Index (ONI): +0.5 or higher means El Niño; −0.5 or lower, La Niña.
  • Episodes typically occur every 3–5 years (range 2–7) and last nine to 12 months.
Quick check

Mission 3

Changes to global weather patterns

  • When the Walker circulation shifts, rainfall belts shift with it: drier in some regions, wetter in others.
  • El Niño "contributes to more eastern Pacific hurricanes and fewer Atlantic hurricanes"; La Niña does the reverse.
  • These distant links are called teleconnections.
Mission 3

El Niño 2023–24: Panama and southern Africa

  • WMO: the 2023–24 El Niño peaked in December 2023 "as one of five strongest on record".
  • Panama: October 2023 was the driest October on record in the Canal watershed. Its lakes store water "for meeting the needs of the population and ensuring the smooth operation of the canal".
  • Transits were cut to 22 a day, against 36 in the "regular scenario", due to "the drought induced by the El Niño phenomenon".
  • Southern Africa: February 2024 was the driest on record in large parts of the region; 71 million people were food insecure. SADC called El Niño "the primary driver" of the increase, though "not the sole driver".
Mission 3

La Niña 2020–23: the Horn of Africa

  • La Niña lasted, with short interruptions, from September 2020: WMO called it the first "triple-dip" La Niña of the 21st century.
  • Southern Ethiopia, southern Somalia and eastern Kenya had five failed rainy seasons in a row from late 2020.
  • "There is a high correlation between below-average short rains and La Niña".
  • World Weather Attribution: climate change made such droughts "about 100 times more likely", mainly through higher evaporation.

Mission 3

Two kinds of drought

  • Drought: "a prolonged, abnormally dry period when the amount of available water is insufficient to meet normal use".
  • Meteorological drought: a deficit of precipitation against the long-term average.
  • Hydrological drought: the deficit reaches stores and flows: lower rivers, reservoirs and groundwater.
  • Hydrological drought lags behind meteorological drought, and can outlast it.
Mission 3

Three physical causes

  • Short-term precipitation deficit: one or more failed rainy seasons (Horn of Africa).
  • ENSO cycles: El Niño dried Panama and southern Africa; La Niña dried East Africa.
  • Longer-term trends: warming raises evaporation, so the same rainfall deficit dries land faster. IPCC: each extra 0.5 °C brings increases in agricultural and ecological droughts in some regions.
Mission 3

Australia's Millennium Drought

  • 1997 to 2009: "below-average to record-low rainfall across much of southwest and southeast Australia".
  • Not every season was exceptionally dry; there were no recovery years until 2010–11, when two strong La Niña events brought heavy rain.
  • El Niño and a positive Indian Ocean Dipole can reinforce each other's drying. "While El Niño is often associated with low rainfall, it isn't always the case".
Mission 3

The human contribution: over-abstraction

  • In the Murray–Darling Basin, irrigation, towns and industry draw on rivers and groundwater.
  • The Basin Plan (in effect November 2012) set out to recover 2,750 GL a year for the environment, about a 20% reduction in water for consumptive use, plus 450 GL a year more.
  • A cut that large shows how much had been allocated to users: in dry years, abstraction deepened the hydrological drought in rivers and wetlands.
  • Exam move: separate the trigger (low rainfall) from the amplifier (over-abstraction).
Mission 3

Ecosystems under stress, and resilience

  • Forest stress: during the Millennium Drought, more than 70% of the Basin's iconic river red gum stands were in poor health or dead.
  • Wetlands on floodplains depend on floods; with low flows they dry and lose habitat.
  • Resilience is an ecosystem's ability to recover once water returns. Environmental flows were tried to improve tree health.
  • Productivity Commission (2024): the Plan has "improved river flows, connectivity, and ecosystem and biodiversity outcomes", but will not be fully implemented on time or on budget.
Quick check
?Rainfall returns to normal after a two-year deficit, but river flows and groundwater stay low for months. What is happening?
Mission 3

ENSO, warming and uncertainty

  • IPCC: rainfall variability related to ENSO "is projected to be amplified" (very likely, in intermediate and high emissions scenarios).
  • Yet IPCC Chapter 4: "There is no consensus on changes in amplitude of ENSO SST variability" across model generations.
  • So planners face wetter wet phases and drier dry phases, but cannot be sure how ENSO itself will change.
  • Water security: Panama's lakes serve both the canal and the population; a stronger El Niño threatens both.
Mission check

The same Walker circulation explains Panama. In El Niño the warm water and rising air shifted east, rain over the Canal watershed failed, and the lakes ran too low for 36 ships a day. ENSO moves rain belts across the globe.

Mission 4

Urbanisation

  • Impermeable surfaces (roofs, roads, car parks) cut infiltration and increase overland flow.
  • Drains and sewers carry water to rivers quickly.
  • Result: shorter lag time, steeper rising limb, higher peak discharge.
  • Building on floodplains also puts more people and property in the way (exposure).
Mission 4

Hurricane Harvey, Houston, 2017

  • Harvey stalled over or near the Texas coast "for four days"; the highest storm total was about 1,540 mm (60.58 inches), near Nederland, Texas.
  • A study in Nature found urbanisation "exacerbated not only the flood response but also the storm total rainfall".
  • It estimated that urbanisation made such extreme floods in the basins studied about 21 times as likely, on average (25–30 August 2017).
  • One modelling study of one event: strong evidence, but test it against others.

Mission 4

Abstraction and reservoirs

  • Abstraction: pumping water from rivers and aquifers lowers river flow and the water table.
  • Beijing: heavy groundwater pumping lowered the mean groundwater level in the Beijing Plain from 15 m below the surface in 2000 to 26 m in 2014, about 0.7 m a year.
  • Reservoirs create new stores and change the timing of flow downstream. Danjiangkou Reservoir, on the Han River, holds 29 km³.
  • Since 2014 water transferred from it has helped Beijing's groundwater recover.
Mission 4

Deforestation in Amazonia

  • Trees intercept rain, roots open up the soil, and transpiration returns water to the air.
  • Tocantins River, south-eastern Amazonia (about 175,000 km² above Porto Nacional): farmland grew from about 30% of the basin (1960) to about 49% (1995).
  • Annual mean discharge rose by 24% and high-flow season discharge by 28%, while precipitation did not change significantly.
  • Less transpiration also means less water vapour for rain downwind.

Mission 4

Channelisation: why and how

  • Why: to carry floodwater away faster, to drain land for farming or building, and to keep a depth for navigation.
  • Realignment: straightening a meandering channel, for example by cutting through meander necks. Shorter and steeper, so water flows faster.
  • Re-sectioning: reshaping the channel to a wider, deeper, uniform cross-section, sometimes lined with concrete.
  • Dredging: removing sediment from the bed to keep the channel deep.
Mission 4

Channelisation: the Kissimmee River, Florida

  • After "prolonged flooding in 1947", the Kissimmee River, which "once meandered for 103 miles", was cut and dredged into a 30-feet-deep straight channel in the 1960s.
  • It "destroyed much of a floodplain-dependent ecosystem".
  • Restoration construction was completed in July 2021.
  • Speeding water away from one place can pass the flood peak downstream faster.

Mission 4

Meteorological causes of flooding

  • Intense storms: rain faster than the soil can absorb gives flash floods, especially in steep or urban catchments.
  • Prolonged rain: summer 2007 in England was "the wettest summer since records began"; saturated ground then sheds further rain.
  • Monsoon: rain is concentrated in a few months; on the Ganges at Patna in 1980 the monsoon months carried 76% of the year's water. An extreme monsoon adds heavier or longer rain on top of already high flows.
  • Snowmelt: a rapid spring melt, especially over frozen ground, sends a surge of water to rivers; the Yukon's mean flow rises from about 1,410 m³/s in April to about 16,200 m³/s in June (calculated from USGS data).
Mission 4

  • Smart irrigation: sensors and drip lines deliver water only when and where plants need it. Singapore has little farmland, so this matters more in farming countries.
  • Exam move: weigh sustainable schemes against hard engineering (dams, transfers, desalination): lower energy and impact, but smaller gains in supply.

Mission 10

Jordan: severe scarcity

  • Jordan has about 61 m³ of renewable water per person a year, "much less than the minimum 500 cubic metres a person needs as per global standards".
  • Water stress was an estimated 105.2% in 2023: Jordan withdrew more freshwater than its renewable supply.
  • 10 of its 12 main aquifers are "being used faster than they can naturally recharge"; some fall by more than 10 m a year.
  • "Close to half of Jordan's drinking water is lost before it reaches people's homes"; around half the population receives water only once a week.
Mission 10

Security is not self-sufficiency

  • Both countries import most of their food and energy. The difference is reliability of access.
  • Singapore: money, diverse sources (four taps), stockpiles and technology; its GNI per person is about 15.5 times Jordan's (our calculation).
  • For food, Singapore's government names four pillars: "Diversify Imports, Global Partnerships, Stockpile and Grow Local".
  • Jordan: overdrawn aquifers, high losses and weekly supply; new desalination will depend on imported energy or new solar.
  • Risks remain for both: dependence on partners, energy imports and shocks to shipping routes.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A drainage basin is an open system of inputs, stores, flows and outputs inside the closed global cycle; P = Q + E ± ΔS.
  • Stores differ hugely in size and residence time. Fossil groundwater and shrinking glaciers are not renewable on human timescales.

Mission 2

  • Hydrograph shape depends on basin size, shape, relief, soil, geology, drainage density, precipitation, antecedent conditions and land use; the Beult (clay) and Lambourn (chalk) show geology at work.
  • Regimes show seasonal change: ET in temperate basins, snowmelt and frozen ground in the Yukon, and rainfall lag in the Amazon and melt in the Indus.

Mission 3

  • ENSO shifts the Walker circulation and with it rainfall belts and storm tracks: El Niño dried Panama and southern Africa (2023–24); La Niña dried East Africa (2020–23).
  • Drought starts as a meteorological deficit (rainfall, ENSO, warming trends) and becomes hydrological; over-abstraction amplifies it, as in the Murray–Darling, and stressed ecosystems recover only if water returns.

Mission 4

  • Urbanisation, deforestation, abstraction and reservoirs change stores and flows; most speed runoff and raise peaks.
  • Channelisation (realignment, re-sectioning, dredging) speeds flow locally but can pass floods downstream and damage ecosystems.
  • Floods need a meteorological trigger, but land use and engineering shape the impact, as in England in 2007.

Mission 5

  • Sea level rose 0.20 m in 1901–2018 and is accelerating; thermal expansion and land ice are the main sources, sea ice almost none.
  • Warming intensifies the water cycle: more land precipitation, amplified P–E, shrinking snow and glacier stores, changing runoff.

Mission 6

  • Water stress: withdrawals vs supply (world 18%), or water per person (<1,700 m³ stress, <1,000 m³ scarcity). Physical scarcity is too little water; economic scarcity is too little access.
  • Supply depends on climate, geology and drainage, and on quality.

Mission 7

  • Water availability per person ranges from tens of thousands of m³ (Canada) to almost none (Egypt's internal supply); stress is highest in Northern Africa and Western and Southern Asia.
  • Agriculture withdraws 72% of freshwater; rising population, living standards, industry and energy push demand up.
  • Water underpins industry, energy, farming, sanitation and health; shortage spreads through the water–food–energy nexus.

Mission 8

  • Water conflicts occur locally (farms vs city, Cape Town), nationally (Colorado states) and internationally (the Nile).
  • Basin-wide management and frameworks such as the UNECE Water Convention and the WFD rest on equitable, reasonable use and cooperation.

Mission 9

  • Mega-dams, transfers and desalination increase supply, but shift environmental and social costs to other places and people.
  • The South–North Water Transfer (Danjiangkou Dam plus canals and pipelines) helped Beijing's groundwater recover while reducing flows on the Han River below the dam.

Mission 10

  • Jordan's dry climate and overdrawn aquifers make supply unreliable; its new desalination project needs energy it mostly imports.
  • Recycling (NEWater), conservation (pricing, labelling) and restoration (ABC Waters) make Singapore's supply more sustainable.
