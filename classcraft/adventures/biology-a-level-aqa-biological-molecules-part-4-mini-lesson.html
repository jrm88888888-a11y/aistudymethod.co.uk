Mission 1

Mission 1: A ribosome never sees a single amino acid's name. So how does it build the right protein?

Make a guess: A ribosome never sees a single amino acid's name. So how does it build the right protein?

Mission 1

From gene to ribosome

  • DNA holds the genetic information. In eukaryotic cells it stays in the nucleus.
  • mRNA carries a copy of one gene's information to the ribosomes in the cytoplasm.
  • Translation: the base sequence of mRNA is used to build the amino acid sequence of a polypeptide.
  • The mRNA is read in codons: three bases = one amino acid (or a stop signal).
  • Amino acids are not made from the bases. They already float in the cytoplasm.
  • Say: "translation makes a polypeptide on a ribosome" — not: "the ribosome converts bases into amino acids".
Mission 1

Two RNAs, two shapes

  • mRNA: a single, linear strand; hundreds to thousands of nucleotides; a run of codons. No folded clover-leaf.
  • tRNA: a single strand of about 80 nucleotides, folded into a clover-leaf shape.
  • The fold is held by hydrogen bonds between complementary bases in the same strand.
  • One loop carries the anticodon: three bases.
  • The 3′ end is the amino acid attachment site. Each tRNA carries one specific amino acid.
Mission 1

The ribosome

  • Made of rRNA and protein, in two subunits: small and large.
  • The small subunit binds the mRNA.
  • The large subunit holds two tRNAs at the same time.
  • rRNA gives the ribosome its shape and catalyses peptide bond formation.
Quick check
?A cell contains many different kinds of tRNA molecule. What is the role of a tRNA during translation?
Mission 1

Codon meets anticodon

  • The anticodon is complementary to the codon: A–U, U–A, G–C, C–G.
  • RNA has uracil, not thymine. So codon AUG pairs with anticodon UAC.
  • Codon and anticodon are held by hydrogen bonds: weak, so the tRNA can leave later.
  • The ribosome checks only this pairing. It never checks the amino acid.
  • Say: "complementary to the codon" — not: "the same as the codon".
Mission 1

Elongation, one codon at a time

  • (1) Two tRNAs sit on the ribosome, each paired with its codon.
  • (2) A peptide bond forms between their amino acids, by condensation. The chain now hangs on the second tRNA.
  • (3) The ribosome moves one codon along the mRNA. The empty tRNA leaves.
  • (4) The next tRNA pairs with the newly exposed codon. Repeat.
Quick check
?An antibiotic stops the ribosome from moving along the mRNA but does not affect the binding of tRNAs or the formation of peptide bonds. What effect will this have on translation of an mRNA?
Mission 1

Start, stop and the energy bill

  • Start codon AUG: translation begins here. AUG also codes for methionine, the first amino acid.
  • Stop codons (UAA, UAG, UGA): no tRNA pairs with them. The finished polypeptide is released.
  • You will not be asked to recall codons: the data are given.
  • ATP is hydrolysed to attach each amino acid to its tRNA. That stored energy is used when the peptide bond forms.
  • Many ribosomes can translate one mRNA at once, so many copies of the polypeptide are made quickly.
Mission 1

Worked example: decoding a gene

  • Template DNA strand: 3′–TAC AAG CCT TTC ACT–5′.
  • Step 1. mRNA is complementary to the template, with U for T: AUG UUC GGA AAG UGA.
  • Step 2. Read from AUG in threes. Use the table: AUG Met · UUC Phe · GGA Gly · AAG Lys · UGA stop.
  • Step 3. Polypeptide: Met–Phe–Gly–Lys. Four amino acids; the stop codon adds none.
  • Step 4. Anticodons used (each written 3′→5′): UAC, AAG, CCU, UUC. None for UGA.
  • Notice: the anticodons have the template's sequence, with U for T.
Quick check
?The coding region of an mRNA, from the first nucleotide of the start codon to the last nucleotide of the stop codon, is 903 nucleotides long. How many amino acids does the polypeptide translated from this region contain?

  • In each tube, one amino acid (or one group) was radioactive. They measured radioactivity built into protein.

Mission check

?In a test tube translation system, the tRNAs with the anticodon 3′-ACG-5′ normally carry the amino acid cysteine, and they are the only tRNAs in the system that pair with the codon 5′-UGC-3′. Before translation, the cysteine attached to these tRNAs is chemically changed into a different amino acid, alanine; the tRNAs themselves are unchanged. An mRNA containing the codon 5′-UGC-3′ is then translated. Which prediction about the polypeptide made is correct?

The ribosome reads codons, never amino acids. The tRNA is the adapter that links the two, so whatever it carries goes in.

Mission 2

Mission 2: An electron microscope shows a cell packed with odd shapes. How do you tell what each one is and does?

Make a guess: An electron microscope shows a cell packed with odd shapes. How do you tell what each one is and does?

Mission 2

What is an organelle?

  • An organelle is a distinct part of a cell with a specific function.
  • Eukaryotic cells have a nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles.
  • The cell surface (plasma) membrane controls what enters and leaves the cell.
  • Internal membranes divide the cell into compartments, each with its own enzymes and reactions.
Mission 2

The nucleus

  • Nuclear envelope: a double membrane with nuclear pores. mRNA leaves through the pores; proteins enter.
  • Chromatin: DNA wound around histone proteins. It condenses into visible chromosomes when the cell divides.
  • Chromosomes are protein-bound, linear DNA.
  • Nucleolus: a dense region that makes ribosomal RNA and assembles ribosomes.
  • The nucleus holds the cell's genes and controls which proteins are made.
Mission 2

Ribosomes

  • Made of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and protein; no membrane.
  • Two subunits. They read mRNA and join amino acids: protein synthesis.
  • Free in the cytoplasm, or bound to the RER.
  • Eukaryotic cytoplasm: 80S ribosomes. Prokaryotes, mitochondria and chloroplasts: smaller 70S.
  • Say: "ribosomes translate mRNA into protein" — not: "ribosomes copy DNA".
Mission 2

The endoplasmic reticulum

  • Rough ER (RER): flattened membrane sacs studded with ribosomes. Makes and transports proteins, e.g. proteins for secretion.
  • Smooth ER (SER): a network of tubes with no ribosomes. Makes lipids.
Mission 2

Golgi apparatus, vesicles and lysosomes

  • Golgi apparatus: a stack of curved, flattened membrane sacs; no ribosomes.
  • It modifies proteins (e.g. adds carbohydrate) and packages them into vesicles.
  • Vesicles: small membrane sacs that carry substances around the cell.
  • Lysosome: a vesicle with a single membrane, full of hydrolytic enzymes. It digests engulfed material and worn-out organelles.
  • Lysosomes are formed from vesicles that bud off the Golgi apparatus.
Mission 2

Mitochondria

  • Double membrane. The inner membrane folds into cristae; the fluid inside is the matrix.
  • Site of aerobic respiration, which makes ATP.
  • Cristae give a large surface area for the membrane-bound steps that make ATP.
  • Mitochondria contain their own DNA and 70S ribosomes.
  • Their DNA is short, circular and not associated with protein.
  • Very active cells, such as muscle cells and sperm (midpiece), have many mitochondria.
  • Say: "mitochondria transfer energy from glucose to ATP" — not: "mitochondria make energy".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly describes a membrane-bound organelle of a eukaryotic cell?

Mission check

?An electron micrograph of a cell shows a large amount of rough ER, a large Golgi and many small vesicles close to the cell surface. What does this cell most likely do?

Each organelle has a recognisable outline and a job. Read the outlines, and a micrograph tells you what the cell does.

Mission 3

Mission 3: A plant cell shares most of its parts with yours. So what does it have that yours lacks, and why?

Make a guess: A plant cell shares most of its parts with yours. So what does it have that yours lacks, and why?

Mission 3

The cell wall

  • A cell wall lies outside the plasma membrane.
  • Plant walls are made of cellulose. Fungal walls are made of chitin.
  • Cell walls are found in plants, algae and fungi.
  • The wall gives strength and shape, and stops the cell bursting when water enters by osmosis.
  • Water and most small solutes pass through the wall. The plasma membrane controls what enters the cell.
  • Say: "the membrane controls entry" — not: "the wall controls entry".
Mission 3

Chloroplasts

  • Found in plants and algae: the site of photosynthesis.
  • Double membrane (envelope).
  • Thylakoids: flattened membrane sacs holding chlorophyll, stacked into grana.
  • Stroma: the fluid around the thylakoids, with the enzymes that make sugars.
  • Chloroplasts contain their own DNA and 70S ribosomes.
  • Their DNA is short, circular and not associated with protein.
  • Not every plant cell has them: root cells, for example, do not.
  • Say: "plant cells have chloroplasts and mitochondria" — not: "chloroplasts instead of mitochondria".
Mission 3

The vacuole

  • Plant cells have a large, permanent vacuole filled with cell sap.
  • Water enters the vacuole; the contents press against the wall: the cell is turgid (firm).
Mission 3

  • Every one of these cells has a nucleus, mitochondria, ribosomes, ER and Golgi.
Quick check
?A mature cell is taken from the root of a bean plant. Which pair of structures would you expect to find in it?
Mission check

?Cells from a potato tuber have a cellulose cell wall, a large vacuole and many starch grains, but no chloroplasts. A student says they cannot be plant cells, because plants photosynthesise. Which response is correct?

A cellulose wall and a large vacuole are the reliable plant features. Chloroplasts appear only in cells that receive light.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Before a cell divides, it copies all of its DNA. How does the copy come out almost perfect?

Make a guess: Before a cell divides, it copies all of its DNA. How does the copy come out almost perfect?

Mission 4

Why copy DNA?

  • Every cell made by mitosis needs a complete copy of the DNA.
  • So DNA is copied before every mitosis: for growth, repair and asexual reproduction.
  • It is also copied once before meiosis, the division that makes gametes.
  • Copying happens in interphase, before the cell divides.
  • The copies are exact: identical base sequences.
Quick check
?A cut in the skin heals as new skin cells are produced by cell division. Why must DNA replication take place before each of these divisions?
Mission 4

Replication, step by step

  • Helicase unwinds the double helix and breaks the hydrogen bonds between complementary bases. The strands separate.
  • Each separated strand is a template.
  • Free DNA nucleotides are attracted to the exposed bases and pair by complementary base pairing: A with T, C with G. Hydrogen bonds hold each pair.
  • DNA polymerase joins adjacent nucleotides of the new strand in condensation reactions, building its sugar–phosphate backbone.
  • Result: two molecules, each with one original strand and one new strand. This is semi-conservative replication.
  • Say: "helicase breaks the hydrogen bonds between bases" — not: "helicase cuts the DNA".
  • Say: "DNA polymerase joins nucleotides to each other" — not: "DNA polymerase pairs the bases". Base pairs form on their own.
Quick check
?A drug inhibits helicase but has no effect on DNA polymerase. What will happen to DNA replication in a cell treated with the drug?
Mission 4

Worked example: complementary, not identical

One template strand reads G A T T C A.

  • Pair each base: G→C, A→T, T→A, T→A, C→G, A→T.
  • New strand: C T A A G T.
  • The new strand is complementary to its template.
  • It is identical to the strand that was paired with this template before.
  • The other template is copied the same way. Both daughter molecules match the parent.
  • Pairing is set by hydrogen bonding: each base can pair stably only with its complement.
Quick check
?During replication, a section of a template strand with the base sequence TACGGA is copied. Which statement describes the new strand built on this section?
Mission check

?A short section of a DNA molecule has one strand reading GATTC, paired base by base with a second strand reading CTAAG. The molecule is replicated once. Which statement describes the two DNA molecules produced?

Each old strand is the pattern for its new partner, and base pairing makes that pattern hard to misread. That is why the copy is almost perfect.

Mission 5

Mission 5: How did one experiment with heavy nitrogen show that every new DNA molecule keeps half of the old one?

Make a guess: How did one experiment with heavy nitrogen show that every new DNA molecule keeps half of the old one?

Mission 5

Three possible models

  • Conservative: the parent molecule stays whole; the copy is all new.
  • Semi-conservative: each new molecule has one parent strand + one new strand.
  • Dispersive: old and new DNA are mixed in pieces along every strand.
  • Watson and Crick (1953) proposed semi-conservative replication from their double-helix model: two complementary strands, each a template.
  • In 1958, Meselson and Stahl designed an experiment that gave each model a different prediction.
Mission 5

The experiment

  • Bases contain nitrogen. ¹⁵N is a heavy isotope; ¹⁴N is the normal, light one.
  • E. coli were grown for many generations on ¹⁵N: all their DNA became heavy.
  • They were moved to a ¹⁴N medium. Every new strand made after that is light.
  • Samples were taken after each generation (one round of replication).
  • DNA was extracted and spun in a caesium chloride density gradient.
  • DNA settles where its density matches the solution: heavier DNA settles lower.
Mission 5

Read the data

Generations in ¹⁴NBands seen
0one band: heavy
1one band: intermediate
2two bands, equal amounts: intermediate and light
  • Intermediate (hybrid) DNA = one ¹⁵N strand + one ¹⁴N strand.
  • Light DNA = two ¹⁴N strands.
  • Read each row as a test. Which models survive it?
Quick check
?Bacteria were grown for many generations with only heavy nitrogen (¹⁵N), so all their DNA was heavy. They were then moved to a medium with only light nitrogen (¹⁴N). After one round of replication, all the DNA had a density midway between heavy and light. After a second round, half the DNA was of this intermediate density and half was light. What do these results show about replication?
Mission 5

Which model fails, and when

  • Conservative predicts generation 1: two bands, heavy and light. Seen: one intermediate band. Refuted at generation 1.
  • Dispersive predicts generation 1: one intermediate band. That fits.
  • But dispersive predicts generation 2: still one band, lighter than intermediate. Seen: two bands. Refuted at generation 2.
  • Semi-conservative predicts both results. It is supported.
  • Say: "the results support the semi-conservative model" — not: "the results prove it".
Mission 5

Worked example: after n generations

Start: one ¹⁵N molecule. Grow it in ¹⁴N.

  • Molecules after n generations = 2ⁿ.
  • The two original ¹⁵N strands never break up. Each ends up in one hybrid molecule.
  • So there are always exactly 2 hybrid molecules; all the rest are light.

Quick check
?A double-stranded DNA molecule in which both strands contain only heavy nitrogen (¹⁵N) is replicated three times in a medium that supplies only light nitrogen (¹⁴N), producing 8 DNA molecules. How many of the 8 DNA molecules contain any ¹⁵N?
Mission 5

Judging the evidence

  • Each model made a different, testable prediction before the results were known.
  • The bands were sharp and clearly separated.
  • Pure ¹⁵N and pure ¹⁴N DNA gave reference positions for heavy and light.
  • Generation 2 was essential: generation 1 alone could not rule out dispersive.
  • The method and results were published in full (1958), so others could check and repeat them.
  • A limit: it was done in one bacterium. Other studies were needed to extend it to eukaryotes.
  • So it validated the Watson–Crick model of replication.
Quick check
?In the Meselson–Stahl experiment, which result after one generation in ¹⁴N would have supported the conservative model?
Mission check

?The experiment is run in reverse. Bacteria are grown for many generations on light nitrogen (¹⁴N), then moved to a medium containing only heavy nitrogen (¹⁵N). Replication is semi-conservative. What will the DNA show after two generations on ¹⁵N?

Change the labels and the logic still works. Each old strand survives intact, paired with a new partner, in every generation.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Your cells already get energy from sugar. Why do they spend it through a different molecule first?

Make a guess: Your cells already get energy from sugar. Why do they spend it through a different molecule first?

Mission 6

Meet ATP

  • ATP: adenosine triphosphate.
  • Adenine (a base) + ribose (a pentose) + three phosphate groups.
  • Adenine + ribose = adenosine.
  • ATP is a nucleotide derivative.
  • ADP: adenosine diphosphate. Two phosphate groups.
  • Same base and sugar as an RNA nucleotide carrying adenine.
Quick check
?Which statement about ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is correct?
Mission 6

Hydrolysis releases energy

  • ATP + H2O → ADP + Pi (inorganic phosphate).
  • The enzyme: ATP hydrolase.
  • Energy is released: enough for many single tasks in the cell.
  • Breaking any bond takes energy. Energy is released overall because ADP and Pi are more stable than ATP.
  • Say: "energy is released when ATP is hydrolysed" — not: "energy is stored in a high-energy bond".
Mission 6

Coupling: putting the energy to work

  • ATP hydrolysis is coupled to an energy-requiring process: one enzyme links them, so they happen together.
  • The released Pi can phosphorylate another compound, often making it more reactive.
  • What ATP powers:
  • active transport of ions and molecules across membranes
  • synthesis of macromolecules (anabolism), e.g. proteins, DNA
  • movement: muscle contraction, cilia, chromosomes in cell division
  • Say: "active transport uses ATP" — not: "diffusion and osmosis use ATP".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly describes a life process that ATP supplies with energy?
Mission 6

Remaking ATP: the ATP–ADP cycle

  • ADP + Pi → ATP + H2O: a condensation reaction.
  • The enzyme: ATP synthase.
  • Energy comes from respiration (every cell) and photosynthesis (in chloroplasts).
  • ATP is not stockpiled. A cell keeps a small pool and recycles it, second by second.
  • A resting adult turns over roughly their own body mass of ATP each day.
Quick check
?During a sprint, the small store of ATP in muscle fibres is hydrolysed and then re-formed several times over. Which statement about the energy transfers is correct?
Mission 6

Why not spend glucose directly?

  • Glucose releases its energy in many enzyme-controlled steps. ATP: one step, one enzyme.
  • One glucose holds far more energy than one reaction needs. ATP comes in small packets.
  • About 30 kJ per mole of ATP hydrolysed; about 2870 kJ per mole of glucose fully oxidised.
  • Released immediately, right where it is needed.
  • Soluble: moves freely within the cell.
  • Quickly remade from ADP + Pi.
Quick check
?A cell needs an energy currency that can supply many different reactions in the cytoplasm. Which property makes ATP suitable for this role?
Mission check

?A muscle fibre holds only enough ATP for a few seconds of hard contraction. Yet a marathon runner's leg muscles keep contracting for hours. Which statement explains this?

A small, fast-recycled pool of ATP, refilled by respiration, beats spending glucose directly.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • mRNA codons pair with complementary tRNA anticodons on the ribosome; each tRNA brings its amino acid, and peptide bonds join them one codon at a time from AUG to a stop codon.
  • ATP is used to attach amino acids to their tRNAs; that energy drives peptide bond formation.

Mission 2

  • Nucleus, mitochondrion: double membranes; RER (ribosomes), SER, Golgi, lysosomes: single membranes; ribosomes: no membrane, 80S in the cytoplasm.
  • Many of one organelle means a lot of its job: lots of RER and Golgi means protein export.

Mission 3

  • Plant cells add a cellulose wall, chloroplasts (in cells that get light) and a large permanent vacuole; fungal walls are chitin; animal cells have no wall.

Mission 4

  • Helicase unwinds DNA and breaks hydrogen bonds; free nucleotides pair with each exposed template; DNA polymerase joins them by condensation.
  • Each new molecule has one original and one new strand: semi-conservative replication, giving identical copies.

Mission 5

  • Meselson and Stahl (1958): heavy (¹⁵N) DNA, then growth on ¹⁴N; one intermediate band after 1 generation, intermediate + light after 2.
  • Generation 1 refutes conservative; generation 2 refutes dispersive; semi-conservative predicts both.

Mission 6

  • ATP = adenine + ribose + three phosphates. Hydrolysis to ADP + Pi releases energy in a small, immediate packet; respiration supplies the energy to remake it.
  • ATP hydrolase hydrolyses ATP; ATP synthase remakes it; Pi phosphorylates other compounds, making them more reactive.
