Mission 1

Mission 1: Can a new idea that wipes out whole industries still leave an economy better off?

Make a guess: A new technology wipes out an old industry. What happens to the economy as a whole?

Mission 1

Invention and innovation

  • Invention: creating a new idea, product or process, often protected by a patent.
  • Innovation: turning an invention into something used commercially: a product people buy, or a method firms adopt.
  • Many inventions are never innovated; the gain to the economy comes from innovation.
  • Research and development (R&D): spending on finding and developing new products and processes.
  • Example: a Kodak engineer built the first digital camera in 1975, but the firm's main business stayed camera film for decades.
  • Say: "invention creates the idea; innovation brings it to market" — not: using the two words as if they mean the same.
Mission 1

Product and process innovation

  • Product innovation: a new or improved good or service (e.g. the smartphone, streaming services).
  • Process innovation: a new or better way of producing, which raises productivity (output per worker, or per hour worked) and cuts average cost.
  • Example: on 1 December 1913 Henry Ford installed a moving assembly line. The time to build a car fell from more than 12 hours to 1 hour 33 minutes.
  • Technological change shifts the LRAC curve down: lower cost at every output.
  • Say: "technological change shifts the LRAC downwards" — not: "it is an economy of scale", which is a movement along the LRAC as output grows.
Mission 1

Worked example: productivity and cost

A bakery employs 20 workers, each paid 100 a day (illustrative data).

  • Before: 400 loaves a day. Productivity = 400 ÷ 20 = 20 loaves per worker.
  • Labour cost per loaf = (20 × 100) ÷ 400 = 2,000 ÷ 400 = 5.
  • A new oven (process innovation) lets the same 20 workers make 500 loaves.
  • Productivity = 500 ÷ 20 = 25 loaves per worker: up (25 − 20) ÷ 20 × 100 = 25%.
  • Labour cost per loaf = 2,000 ÷ 500 = 4: down (4 − 5) ÷ 5 × 100 = −20%.
  • The firm can cut its price, raise its profit margin, or both.

Mission 1

Creative destruction

  • Creative destruction (Joseph Schumpeter, 1942): innovation creates new products, firms and markets and destroys old ones.
  • Example: digital photography and then smartphone cameras replaced film. Kodak, long the giant of camera film, filed for bankruptcy protection in January 2012.
  • Effects on market structure:
  • It can break monopoly power: a newcomer with a better product can overtake a dominant firm that seemed safe behind its barriers to entry.
  • It rewards the innovator with temporary supernormal profit, until others copy or leapfrog it.
  • It can also create new dominant firms, especially in digital markets.
Mission 1

Entrepreneurs and digital markets

  • An entrepreneur organises the factors of production, takes the risk, and adds value: sells output for more than the cost of the bought-in inputs.
  • Entrepreneurs drive creative destruction: they spot a better product or method and back it.
  • Digital markets: low start-up costs, so firms are created and destroyed quickly.
  • The long tail (Chris Anderson, 2004): online sellers can profit from selling small quantities of a very large range of niche products, because storing and displaying one more item online costs almost nothing.
  • A physical shop stocks only best-sellers; an online seller can earn a large share of revenue from the "tail" of rarely bought items.
Mission 1

Product life cycle and state-funded R&D

  • Product life cycle: introduction → growth → maturity → decline (sales over time).
  • Innovation launches new products; creative destruction pushes old ones into decline.
  • Extension strategies (new features, new markets, rebranding) can delay decline.
  • State funding of R&D: governments fund university research, give grants, and offer tax credits for firms' R&D spending.
  • Why? New knowledge has positive externalities: other firms and consumers gain from it, so private firms on their own spend less on R&D than is best for society.
Quick check
?Streaming services spread quickly. Many shops renting out films on disc closed, while new firms making shows for streaming grew fast. How is this best described?
Mission 1

Does market power help innovation?

  • For (Schumpeter): firms with market power earn supernormal (abnormal) profit, which can finance R&D. Large firms can also bear the risk of failed projects.
  • Patents give an innovator a temporary monopoly (usually up to 20 years), so it can earn the profit that rewards its R&D.
  • Against: a monopolist that already earns high profit may feel little pressure to innovate. In competitive markets, firms must innovate or lose customers.
  • Oligopolies often compete through innovation and other non-price competition. Perfectly competitive firms earn only normal profit in the long run and new ideas are copied at once, so they have little means or reason to do R&D.
Quick check
?Which statement best supports the view that large firms with market power can benefit consumers through innovation?
Mission 1Evaluate

Is creative destruction worth it?

  • Consumers gain new and cheaper products; displaced workers and owners lose jobs and capital.
  • It depends on time: job losses come first; higher productivity and new jobs may take years.
  • It depends on mobility: workers whose skills fit the old industry may stay unemployed (structural unemployment) unless they retrain or move.
  • It depends on what follows: if new dominant firms build high barriers, competition may weaken until the next wave.
  • It depends on policy: patent length, competition policy and support for retraining shape both the gains and the losses.
Mission check

?Banking apps spread through a country. Banks close most of their branches and many branch staff lose their jobs, while new firms that build payment software grow fast. Which judgement of the effect on the economy is best supported?

Creative destruction can raise output over time, but its gains and its losses fall on different people.

Mission 2

The four factors of production

  • Factors of production: the resources used to produce goods and services.
  • Land: all natural resources: soil, water, minerals, fish, forests.
  • Labour: human effort, physical and mental, used in production.
  • Capital: man-made goods used to produce other goods and services: machines, tools, buildings, roads.
  • Enterprise: organising the other factors and taking the risk of production.
  • Capital depends on use: a car is capital for a taxi driver, a consumer good for a family.
  • Say: "capital = goods used to make other goods" — not: "capital = money". Money buys capital; it does not produce anything itself.
Quick check
?Which is the best example of enterprise (entrepreneurship) as a factor of production?
Mission 2

Worked example: workers or machines?

  • Illustrative data. Amounts in any one currency.
  • A bakery can make 500 loaves a day in two ways:
  • Method A (labour-intensive): 10 workers + 1 machine.
  • Method B (capital-intensive): 2 workers + 4 machines.
  • A machine costs 200 a day.
  • Wage 80 a day: A = 10 × 80 + 1 × 200 = 1,000; B = 2 × 80 + 4 × 200 = 960. Choose B.
  • Wage 60 a day: A = 10 × 60 + 200 = 800; B = 2 × 60 + 800 = 920. Choose A.
  • The cheaper method depends on the relative prices of labour and capital.
  • Labour-intensive: a high ratio of labour to capital (e.g. hand-picking tea). Capital-intensive: a high ratio of capital to labour (e.g. a car assembly line).
Quick check
?Use the bakery in the worked example (machine 200 a day). The wage rises from 60 to 90 per worker per day. Which method should the bakery now use to make 500 loaves at least cost?
Mission check

?A farmer uses her savings to buy a tractor. She then decides to risk planting a new crop in her fields. Enterprise is also called entrepreneurship. Which list correctly classifies (1) the tractor, (2) her decision to take the risk of the new crop, and (3) the soil of her fields?

Money is not a factor of production. What counts is what each resource does: natural resources, human effort, man-made tools, and the organising and risk-taking.

Mission 3

What is specialisation?

  • Specialisation: concentrating on a limited range of tasks or products.
  • It happens at every level:
    • Individual: a dentist, an electrician, a coder.
    • Firm: a company that only makes bicycle brakes.
    • Region: a city known for finance or for films.
    • Country: one economy exports coffee, another aircraft parts.
  • Division of labour: splitting the making of one product into separate tasks, each done by a different worker.
  • Division of labour is specialisation inside a firm.
Mission 3

Adam Smith's pin factory

  • Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations (1776), described a small pin workshop.
  • Pin-making was split into about 18 distinct operations: one draws the wire, one straightens it, one cuts it, one sharpens the point…
  • 10 workers made upwards of 48,000 pins a day: about 4,800 each.
  • Working alone and untrained, Smith wrote, each "certainly could not" have made twenty pins a day.
  • Smith's three reasons output soared:
    • Dexterity: repeating one task builds skill and speed.
    • Time saved: no switching between tasks and tools.
    • Machinery: a narrow task is easier to mechanise, so machines get invented.
  • Real case, 1913: Ford's moving assembly line cut the time to assemble a car from about 12.5 hours to 93 minutes by early 1914.
Mission 3

Worked example: output and cost per unit

A sandwich bar has 4 workers, each paid 12 per hour (illustrative data).

  • Before: each worker makes whole sandwiches: 30 per hour each, so 120 per hour in total.
  • After: one slices, one fills, one wraps, one serves: 200 per hour in total.
  • Step 1: output per worker after = 200 ÷ 4 = 50 per hour.
  • Step 2: labour productivity rises from 30 to 50: (50 − 30) ÷ 30 × 100 = +66.7%.
  • Step 3: labour cost per sandwich before = 12 ÷ 30 = 0.40.
  • Step 4: labour cost per sandwich after = 12 ÷ 50 = 0.24: down 40%.
  • Same workers, same wage, same hours: more output, lower cost per unit.
Quick check
?A bakery has 6 workers, each paid 90 per day. When each worker bakes whole loaves, the bakery makes 120 loaves a day. After it divides the work into separate tasks, the same 6 workers make 180 loaves a day (illustrative data). What is the labour cost per loaf after the change?
Mission 3

Who gains?

  • Firms: higher labour productivity, lower unit costs, more output from the same inputs.
  • Workers: can build expert skills; easier to train for one task; possibly higher pay as productivity rises.
  • Consumers: lower prices, more output, often better quality from experts.
  • The economy: more output from scarce resources: higher living standards.
  • Countries: specialising and trading lets each focus on what it does best.
Mission 3

The downsides

  • Workers: repetitive work → boredom, lower motivation, more mistakes, absence and staff turnover.
  • Smith himself warned (Book V) that a lifetime of "a few simple operations" can leave a worker's mind dulled.
  • Narrow skills: if a task is automated or demand changes, workers face structural unemployment.
  • Interdependence: if one stage stops, the whole line stops.
  • Countries and regions: over-reliance on a few products; exposed to a shock in one market or supply chain.
  • Real case, 2021: a global shortage of computer chips forced car makers around the world to cut production.
  • Loss of variety: standardised products may cut consumer choice.
Quick check
?A car plant buys a key electronic part from a single specialist supplier abroad. A fire at that supplier's factory halts its output, and within a week the car plant stops its own production line. Which disadvantage of specialisation does this show best?
Mission 3

Evaluate: how far should division of labour go?

It depends on…

  • The size of the market. Smith: division of labour is "limited by the extent of the market". A village baker selling 50 loaves a day cannot keep 6 specialists busy.
  • The type of task. Routine, repeated tasks gain most. Creative or varied work (design, care work) gains less, and repetition can harm quality.
  • How jobs are designed. Job rotation and teamwork can keep productivity gains while cutting boredom and absence.
  • Time period. Gains appear quickly; the risks (deskilling, dependence on one supplier or one export) show up later.
  • Say: "labour productivity rose" (output per worker) — not: "production rose", which could just mean more workers.
Mission 3

Specialise, then exchange

  • A specialist makes far more of one thing than they need, and none of everything else.
  • So specialisation only works with exchange: selling what you make to buy what you need.
  • Individual: a nurse sells her labour for a wage, then buys food, rent and transport.
  • National: a country exports what it specialises in and imports the rest.
  • The more people specialise, the more they depend on trade, and on a cheap, trusted way to trade.
Mission check

?A car-repair garage keeps the same 8 mechanics on the same hours. Instead of each mechanic doing whole jobs, each now does one type of job: tyres, brakes or servicing. Repairs completed per mechanic rise by a third. Which explanation fits the analysis of the division of labour?

Same workers, same hours, more output: the division of labour raises productivity.

Mission 4

Mission 4: How can one interest rate, set by a handful of people, slow down inflation across a whole country?

Make a guess: How can one interest rate, set by a handful of people, slow down inflation across a whole country?

Mission 4

What monetary policy is

  • Monetary policy: the central bank's use of interest rates and the money supply to influence demand and inflation.
  • UK: the Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee (MPC), 9 members, sets Bank Rate.
  • The Bank has had operational independence since 1997: the government sets the target; the MPC chooses the rate.
  • Expansionary policy: lower rates, more money. Contractionary: higher rates, less money.
Mission 4

Goals: an inflation target

  • Main goal: price stability, which means low and stable inflation.
  • UK: 2% CPI inflation, set by the government. The target is symmetric: undershooting is treated as seriously as overshooting.
  • If inflation is more than 1 percentage point away from 2%, the Governor writes an open letter to the Chancellor explaining why.
  • Why a target: it anchors expectations. If people expect 2%, wages and prices are set to match.
Mission 4

The transmission mechanism

  • How a rise in the policy rate reaches inflation:
    • Market rates rise: mortgages, loans, savings. Borrowing costs more; saving pays more. Consumption (C) and investment (I) fall.
    • Asset prices fall (houses, shares, bonds). Households feel less wealthy and spend less.
    • Confidence and expectations: a signal that the central bank is serious about inflation.
    • Exchange rate rises: exports more expensive abroad, imports cheaper. Net exports fall; import prices fall.
  • Result: AD grows more slowly. Less pressure on firms' capacity, wages and prices. Inflation slows.
  • A cut works the other way.
  • Say: "Bank Rate" (UK) — not: "the interest rate", when you mean the Bank of England's policy rate.
Mission 4

Monetary policy on AD/AS

  • Contractionary (rate rise): AD shifts left, AD1 → AD2.
    • Real output falls. The price level is lower than it would have been.
    • Closes an inflationary (positive) gap.
  • Expansionary (rate cut): AD shifts right.
    • Real output and the price level rise. Unemployment falls.
    • Closes a deflationary or recessionary (negative) gap.
  • Say: "inflation slows" (disinflation) — not: "prices fall", unless the price level actually falls (deflation).

Mission 4

What the committee weighs

  • The MPC forecasts inflation about two years ahead, because rate changes take time to work.
  • It looks at: spare capacity and unemployment, wage growth, inflation expectations, the exchange rate, house prices and credit, and world energy and commodity prices.
Mission 4

Real case: the rate rises after the pandemic

  • UK: Bank Rate was 0.1% (March 2020). The MPC raised it from December 2021 to 5.25% (August 2023).
  • CPI inflation peaked at 11.1% (October 2022) and was back at 2.0% in May 2024 (ONS).
  • Inflation then fell, but economists debate how much was due to higher rates and how much to falling energy prices.
Mission 4

Who gains and who loses?

  • A rate rise:
    • Borrowers pay more; savers earn more.
    • Firms: dearer loans, so less investment. Sales of income-elastic goods (cars, holidays) and goods bought on credit fall most.
    • Exporters lose if the currency rises; importers gain.
    • Government: higher interest payments on its debt.
Mission 4

Strengths and limits

  • Strengths:
    • Flexible: rates can change in small steps, several times a year.
    • Reversible, and quick to decide: no new law needed.
    • An independent central bank is credible: a promise to hit the target shapes expectations.
  • Limits:
    • Time lags: up to about two years for the full effect on inflation (Bank of England).
    • Supply shocks: rate rises can lower inflation, but at the cost of lower output and jobs.
    • Low confidence: firms and households may not borrow, however cheap credit is.
    • One rate for everyone: regions, and borrowers and savers, are hit differently.
Mission 4

The zero lower bound

  • Rates cannot be cut far below zero: people could hold cash instead.
  • Zero lower bound: once rates are near zero, conventional policy has little room left.
  • Real cases: Bank Rate 0.1% (March 2020). The European Central Bank's deposit rate reached −0.5% (2019); the Bank of Japan's short-term rate was −0.1% from 2016 to March 2024.
  • Liquidity trap (Keynes): rates are so low that more money does not raise spending; people just hold it.
  • Central banks then turn to QE and forward guidance.
Quick check
?A recession has cut confidence. The central bank's policy rate is already 0.25%, and banks are unwilling to lend. Why might a further rate cut do little to raise AD?
Mission 4Evaluate

How well can interest rates steer the economy?

It depends on:

  • The cause of inflation: demand-pull responds well; a supply shock forces a choice between inflation and output.
  • Time lags: a rate change made now acts on demand up to two years later, when conditions may have changed.
  • Confidence and debt: with many variable-rate borrowers, rates bite fast; when confidence is low, cuts may not work.
  • How low rates already are: near the zero lower bound, other tools are needed.
Mission check

?A sharp rise in world oil prices pushes a country's inflation well above target, while real output falls. The central bank raises its policy rate. What is the most likely result in the short run?

Interest rates work through demand. After a supply shock, slowing inflation comes at the cost of lower output and jobs in the short run.

Mission 5

Mission 5: When a country's money loses value abroad, who wins and who loses at home?

Make a guess: When a country's money loses value abroad, who wins and who loses at home?

Mission 5

Prices across the border

  • A firm sets its price in its own currency. The exchange rate decides what that price is abroad.
  • Illustrative data: the pound falls from 1.50 to 1.20 dollars: a 20% depreciation.
  • UK export priced £10: in the US, it was 10 × 1.50 = $15; now 10 × 1.20 = $12. Cheaper abroad.
  • US import priced $30: in the UK, it was 30 ÷ 1.50 = £20; now 30 ÷ 1.20 = £25. Dearer at home.
  • Memory aid: SPICED — Strong Pound, Imports Cheap, Exports Dear. A weak pound does the reverse.
Quick check
?A country's currency depreciates from 2.00 to 1.50 units of foreign currency. A firm keeps the price of its export at 50 in its own currency. What does a foreign buyer now pay?
Mission 5

Net exports and AD

  • Depreciation: exports cheaper abroad → export volumes rise. Imports cost more at home → import volumes fall.
  • So net exports (X − M) tend to rise, and AD shifts right (AD1 → AD2).
  • Step 1: AD1 meets SRAS below full capacity.
  • Step 2: AD1 → AD2: real GDP rises, the price level rises, unemployment tends to fall.
  • Step 3: imported raw materials and parts cost more, so SRAS shifts left (SRAS1 → SRAS2): more inflation, less of the output gain.
  • An appreciation does the reverse: net exports fall, AD shifts left.
  • A central bank interest rate cut tends to weaken the currency: a second channel from monetary policy to AD.
  • Say: "a depreciation shifts AD to the right" — not: "a movement along AD".

Wider effects of a depreciation

EffectLikely directionWhy
Growth and jobsUpNet exports and AD rise
InflationUpDearer imports (cost-push) and higher AD
Current accountMay improve, after a lagOnly if export and import volumes respond enough to the new prices

  • Winners: exporters, firms competing with imports, the tourism industry.
  • Losers: firms buying imported inputs, consumers, people travelling abroad, borrowers with debts in foreign currency.
  • Firms read exchange rate data for the currencies they buy and sell in. Many hedge by fixing a rate in advance.
  • Real example: the pound fell sharply after the UK's EU referendum in June 2016. UK CPI inflation rose to 3.1% by November 2017, above the 2% target; the Bank of England put most of the overshoot down to the weaker pound raising import prices.

Quick check
?A furniture maker buys most of its wood from Europe, priced in euros, and sells almost all its furniture at home. Its home currency depreciates against the euro. What is the most likely short-run effect on the firm?
Mission 5Evaluate

Does a weaker currency help?

It depends on:

  • Elasticities and time: buyers are slow to switch, so volumes change little at first; the gain may take years.
  • Spare capacity: with output below its full employment level, more AD raises output and jobs; near full capacity, mostly inflation.
  • Import content of exports: in global supply chains, dearer imported parts eat into the price advantage.
  • Non-price competitiveness: quality, design and reliability may matter more than price.
  • Why the currency fell, and others' response: a fall caused by lost confidence may also deter investment; rivals may devalue too (competitive devaluation).
Mission check

?Investors abroad move funds into a country, and its currency appreciates sharply. Other things equal, which effect of the appreciation is most likely in the short run?

An appreciation is the mirror image of a depreciation: net exports and AD fall, and imported inflation eases.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Invention creates an idea; innovation brings it into use. Creative destruction: new products and firms replace old ones.
  • Process innovation raises productivity and shifts cost curves down.
  • Supernormal profit from market power can finance R&D; competition can also force innovation.
  • Entrepreneurs add value; digital markets allow the long tail and fast firm creation and destruction.

Mission 2

  • Four factors: land, labour, capital, enterprise. Capital is man-made goods used in production, not money.

Mission 3

  • Specialisation and the division of labour raise labour productivity (Smith: dexterity, time saved, machinery) and cut unit costs.
  • Costs: monotony, narrow skills, interdependence and over-reliance; how far to go depends on market size and the task.

Mission 4

  • The central bank changes the policy rate to steer AD and keep inflation near its target.
  • Higher rates: borrowing costs more, asset prices lower, currency stronger. AD shifts left; inflation slows.
  • Lags, supply shocks and low confidence limit what interest rates can do.
  • Near the zero lower bound, rate cuts lose their power.

Mission 5

  • Depreciation: exports cheaper abroad, imports more expensive at home, net exports and AD rise. Appreciation: the reverse.
  • The price level rises (AD and dearer inputs); importers, consumers and travellers lose, exporters gain.
