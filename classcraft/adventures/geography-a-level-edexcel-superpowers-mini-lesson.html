Superpowers

Topic 7 of Edexcel A-level Geography. Who holds power in the world, how they got it, how they keep it — and what happens when it starts to move. This is the most theoretical topic on the course, and the one where examiners most want to see you argue.

EQ1 · what makes a superpower

Defining a superpower

A superpower is a state (or bloc) able to project influence on a global scale — not merely a strong country, but one whose decisions reshape the choices available to everyone else. Edexcel wants you to break that influence into pillars of power:

  • Economic — the size of the economy, control of trade, ownership of capital, the currency in which the world settles its debts.
  • Military — spending, technology, and above all global reach (blue-water navies, air bases, nuclear capability).
  • Political — the ability to set the agenda in international institutions, and to build alliances.
  • Cultural — media, language, universities, brands, values: the power to make others want what you want.
  • Demographic — population size and structure; a large working-age population is an asset, an ageing one a liability.
  • Resource — energy, minerals, food and water, and the ability to secure them elsewhere.

Examiner's eye: the weakest answers list pillars. The strongest ones weight them — arguing, with evidence, that (say) economic and cultural power now matter more than territorial size, or that military power without economic depth is unsustainable. Weighting is the evaluation.

EQ1 · Nye

Hard power, soft power, smart power

Joseph Nye distinguished the ways influence is exercised. This distinction is the spine of half the questions on this topic.

  • Hard power is fast, visible and expensive, and it breeds resentment. It works best when the target has no alternative.
  • Soft power is slow, cheap and durable — but it is hard to direct, and it can be lost by a single act of hard power that contradicts the values you claim to hold.
  • Smart power is Nye's argument that neither alone is sufficient: sustained hegemony needs both, and needs them to be consistent with each other.

EQ1 · patterns of power over time

Unipolar, bipolar, multipolar

Edexcel wants the historical shape of power, not just today's snapshot. The vocabulary:

  • Unipolar — one dominant power sets the rules. The nineteenth-century British Empire is usually offered as an imperial example; the post-Cold-War period is often described this way too.
  • Bipolar — two rival blocs, each with a sphere of influence. The Cold War is the archetype: an ideological, military and economic standoff conducted largely through proxies rather than direct war.
  • Multipolar — several powers of comparable weight. Historically associated with instability, because alliances are fluid and miscalculation is easier.

The colonial legacy. Nineteenth-century empire transferred resources, labour and land to the imperial core and organised colonial economies around primary exports. Decolonisation after 1945 handed over political sovereignty but frequently left behind arbitrary borders, narrow export-dependent economies, and institutions built for extraction. That is why so much of this topic is really a topic about history.

Evaluative hinge: is the world today unipolar, multipolar, or something in between? The honest answer is that it depends which pillar you look at — power can be concentrated militarily while being far more dispersed economically. Say that explicitly and you are already arguing at Level 4.

EQ1 · theories to evaluate

Three theories — and their critics

You are expected to use these theories as tools of analysis, and to know where each breaks down.

  • Modernisation theory (Rostow) — development is a linear sequence of stages, from traditional society through take-off to high mass consumption. Every country can climb the ladder if it invests. Critique: it treats countries as isolated, ignores colonial history entirely, assumes a Western model is the only destination, and takes no account of environmental limits.
  • Dependency theory (Frank) — the poverty of the periphery is actively produced by its relationship with the core: unequal terms of trade, debt, and reliance on primary exports. Critique: it struggles to explain why some formerly peripheral economies have grown dramatically, and it can slide into treating poor countries as passive victims with no agency.
  • World systems theory (Wallerstein) — a single capitalist world economy with three positions, and crucially a semi-periphery that can move. Strength: it allows mobility while keeping the structure. Critique: the categories are slippery, and it is better at description than prediction.

EQ1 · emerging powers

BRICs, MINT and the convergence debate

Groupings such as the BRICs (Brazil, Russia, India, China — later joined by South Africa in the political grouping) and the MINT economies (Mexico, Indonesia, Nigeria, Turkey) were coined by economists to label states with the scale and growth to matter globally. They are analytical labels, not natural categories — and it is legitimate, indeed expected, to say so in an essay.

The convergence argument (for): large working-age populations, rising education, industrialisation, growing domestic markets and a share of world output that has generally risen. Some emerging powers have real resource and demographic pillars.

The convergence argument (against):

  • The grouping is heterogeneous — a resource exporter, a manufacturing giant and a services economy face utterly different constraints. Averaging them hides more than it reveals.
  • Growth can stall in the middle-income trap: wages rise above cheap-labour competitors before productivity and innovation catch up with the core.
  • Emerging economic weight does not automatically buy political or cultural power — the institutions of global governance were designed before these states were at the table.
  • Demographic advantage is temporary and can reverse into rapid ageing.

Case-study honesty: avoid quoting half-remembered growth rates. Examiners reward the mechanism — "an export-led model becomes vulnerable once wage costs exceed those of newer entrants" — far above an invented percentage.

Skills · reading a power statistic

What a rising share actually tells you

That calculation contains a trap worth naming. Country X's share of world output rose by five percentage points — but note that everyone grew: world output rose too. A rising share means growth faster than the world average, not growth in isolation. Two further cautions belong in any answer that uses this kind of statistic:

  • Share is relative. A falling share can coexist with a rising absolute output. Political commentary routinely confuses "declining" with "growing more slowly than a rival".
  • Aggregate size is not power per head. A very large, very populous economy can have a huge total GDP and a modest GDP per capita — and per capita income is what funds the technology and the education behind long-run power.
  • Military spending behaves the same way: a large total may reflect a large economy rather than an unusual military priority, and spending does not equal reach or capability.

Skills link: Edexcel will hand you exactly this kind of table in Paper 2 or Paper 3. Calculate, then interrogate — what does the figure hide, and over what timescale would it have to persist to change the balance of power?

EQ2 · maintaining power

How power is maintained

Once acquired, power is defended. Edexcel groups the mechanisms:

  • Military reach and alliances — overseas bases, naval capability, and alliances such as NATO that convert one state's force into a collective guarantee. A sphere of influence is a region in which a power expects its interests to prevail without formal rule.
  • Intergovernmental organisations (IGOs). The UN Security Council grants five permanent members a veto — an institutional lock dating from 1945. The IMF and World Bank lend on conditions; the WTO writes and adjudicates trade rules; the G7 and G20 coordinate the largest economies.
  • The critical argument: these institutions are not neutral. They were designed by the victors of 1945 and their voting structures, headquarters and leadership conventions embed the interests of their founders. Reform is repeatedly proposed and repeatedly blocked — by those the current structure favours.
  • TNCs project economic power that outruns any single state: they set supply chains, shift profits between jurisdictions, and lobby. They can act as an instrument of a home state's influence, but they also have interests of their own.
  • Cultural and media influence — global media, language, brands, sport and universities normalise a way of living, so that the hegemon's preferences look simply like common sense. That is hegemony in the Gramscian sense: rule that does not feel like rule.

EQ3 · consequences of shifting power

Contested spaces: the Arctic, the Antarctic, the South China Sea

When power shifts, previously settled spaces become contested. The mechanism is usually the same: a resource or route becomes accessible or valuable, and the legal framework is either absent or ambiguous.

  • The Arctic. Declining sea ice is opening shipping routes and improving access to seabed resources. Under UNCLOS, a coastal state holds an Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) extending 200 nautical miles from its baselines, with rights over the resources of the water and seabed — and it may claim further seabed rights where it can demonstrate that the continental shelf extends naturally beyond that. Overlapping shelf claims are what makes the Arctic contested, and they are argued through a legal process, not just a naval one.
  • The Antarctic. Governed by the Antarctic Treaty System, which sets the continent aside for peaceful and scientific use and holds existing territorial claims in abeyance. Mineral exploitation is prohibited under the Environmental Protocol. The tension here is latent rather than active — the question is what happens if the consensus to leave it alone weakens.
  • The South China Sea. Overlapping maritime claims among several states, over a sea containing major shipping lanes, fisheries and potential hydrocarbons. Physical construction on reefs and features is significant because UNCLOS ties maritime entitlements to the legal status of land features — so control of a rock or reef is a claim to the sea around it.

Handle with care: these are live disputes with contested facts. Argue the mechanisms — resource access, legal ambiguity, strategic chokepoints, and the incentive to create facts on the ground — rather than asserting who is right or reciting figures you cannot source.

EQ3 · economic and environmental consequences

Restructuring, trade disputes and resource demand

  • Economic restructuring. As production shifts, the losing regions deindustrialise while the gaining ones urbanise rapidly. The costs are spatially concentrated — which is why the politics of trade is so much angrier than the economics.
  • Trade disputes and tariffs. Rising powers challenge the rules they did not write; incumbents respond with protection. Disputes over subsidies, technology and intellectual property are power politics conducted through the WTO and outside it.
  • Resource and environmental demand. Growing middle classes raise demand for energy, water, food and minerals — including the metals needed for low-carbon technology, whose supply is geographically concentrated and therefore strategically sensitive. This is the direct link to Topic 6 (carbon and energy security) and Topic 5 (water insecurity): a perfect Paper 3 synoptic thread.
  • The multipolar challenge. More players, more veto points, slower agreement — on trade, on climate, on conflict. Whether multipolarity is more peaceful (because power is balanced) or more dangerous (because it is unstable) is a genuine debate, and a good essay refuses to settle it cheaply.

EQ3 · the future of superpower influence

What happens next?

Edexcel asks you to weigh futures. Three plausible trajectories, each with evidence for and against:

  • Continued hegemony. The incumbent retains military reach, financial centrality, alliance networks and cultural dominance long after its economic share falls — institutional and cultural power is sticky. Against: relative economic decline eventually erodes the ability to pay for reach.
  • Genuine multipolarity. Several powers with regional dominance and global reach in some pillars but not others. Against: emerging powers face demographic ageing, middle-income traps and internal constraints; and they are not a bloc — they compete with each other as much as with the incumbent.
  • Power beyond the state. TNCs, financial markets, NGOs and technology platforms exercise real global influence, so the whole frame of "superpower" may be dated. Against: only states have militaries, borders and legitimacy — and in a crisis, that is what counts.

Uncertainty is the answer, not a failure to answer. A Level 4 conclusion states what would have to be true for each future to hold — and names the indicator you would watch. That is exactly the "futures and uncertainties" thinking Paper 3 rewards.

Exam technique · the 20-marker

Writing the evaluative essay

Edexcel's 20-mark essays are marked across AO1 (knowledge and understanding) and AO2 (application, analysis and evaluation), and the top level demands a coherent, evidenced line of argument with a genuine judgement.

  • Unpack the command and the key term. "To what extent…" wants a degree, not a yes/no. "Power" wants defining — which pillar are we talking about?
  • State your line in the introduction. Not "this essay will consider both sides" — an actual claim you intend to defend.
  • Argue in blocks: claim → mechanism → evidence → counter-evidence → interim judgement. Then the next block.
  • Use the theory as a lens, not a decoration. "Wallerstein would read this as semi-peripheral upgrading; but the persistence of core control over branding and R&D suggests the value capture has not moved" — that is analysis.
  • Weigh scale and timescale explicitly. Power that is rising economically but static politically is a real and markable distinction.
  • Conclude with a conditional judgement. Say to what extent, and on what it depends.

Try it: "Evaluate the view that emerging powers will inevitably converge with existing superpowers." Blocks: (1) the economic and demographic case for convergence; (2) the middle-income trap and the heterogeneity of the grouping; (3) the pillars where convergence is weakest — political voice inside IGOs, and cultural reach. Judgement: convergence is plausible in the economic pillar and far slower in the political and cultural ones, so "inevitable" overstates it — and power may become unevenly distributed across pillars rather than simply transferred.

Paper 3 · synoptic links

Where Superpowers connects

This is the most connected topic on the specification. Have these threads ready:

  • → Globalisation (Topic 3): superpowers write the rules of trade and investment that make globalisation possible; TNCs are the vehicle of both.
  • → Carbon Cycle and Energy Security (Topic 6): control of energy and of the minerals needed for the energy transition is a pillar of power; climate negotiation is superpower politics by another name.
  • → Water Cycle and Water Insecurity (Topic 5): transboundary rivers and aquifers are geopolitical assets; upstream power is real power.
  • → Health, Human Rights and Intervention (Topic 8A): who intervenes, where, and why — and whose sovereignty is treated as negotiable.
  • → Migration, Identity and Sovereignty (Topic 8B): the erosion of the nation state by supranational bodies and global capital is the same debate seen from below.

Paper 3 habit: for any issue in the resource booklet, ask the three synoptic questions — who are the players and what power does each hold; what attitudes and actions follow from their differing values; what futures are possible and what is genuinely uncertain?

Recap

The big ideas to know

Pillars of power: economic · military · political · cultural · demographic · resource — and you must weight them, not list them

Nye: hard power (coercion) · soft power (attraction) · smart power (the deliberate combination)

Patterns: unipolar · bipolar (the Cold War) · multipolar; the colonial legacy and decolonisation shaped the starting positions

Theories: Rostow (modernisation, linear, ahistorical) · Frank (dependency, structural, denies agency) · Wallerstein (world systems: core–semi-periphery–periphery, allows mobility)

Emerging powers: BRICs and MINT are labels, not blocs; convergence is contested — middle-income trap, heterogeneity, ageing, and no automatic political voice

Maintaining power: military reach and alliances (NATO) · spheres of influence · IGOs (UN Security Council veto, IMF, World Bank, WTO, G7/G20) · TNCs · cultural hegemony

The critical claim: IGOs are not neutral — their structures embed the interests of their 1945 founders

Consequences: tension over the Arctic, Antarctica and the South China Sea; UNCLOS, EEZs and continental-shelf claims; economic restructuring; trade disputes; rising resource and environmental demand

Futures: sticky hegemony · genuine multipolarity · power beyond the state — judge conditionally, and name your indicator
