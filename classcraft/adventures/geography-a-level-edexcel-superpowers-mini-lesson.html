Make a guess: In 2025 China's economy was larger than the USA's by one IMF measure and smaller by another. Which country is the superpower?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel A-level Geography (9GE0)

  • 7.1a defining superpowers, emerging and regional powers by economic, political, military, cultural, demographic and resource characteristics
  • 7.1b hard to soft power spectrum and its effectiveness
  • 7.1c changing importance of power characteristics and mechanisms over time (China's Belt and Road)
Mission 1

Three kinds of power

  • Superpower: projects influence across the whole world, in several dimensions at once.
  • Emerging power: rapidly gaining economic and political weight, but not yet global in every dimension.
  • Regional power: dominates its own world region, with limited reach beyond it.
  • Edexcel's six characteristics: economic, political, military, cultural, demographic and access to natural resources.
  • Edexcel's own examples of emerging powers: Brazil, Russia, India and China (BRIC) and other G20 members.
Mission 1

One economy, two sizes

  • Market exchange rates (nominal), 2025: USA $30.86 trillion, 26.0% of world GDP; China $19.63 trillion, 16.6%.
  • Purchasing power parity (PPP), 2025: China $41.26 trillion; USA $30.86 trillion.
  • PPP adjusts for price levels: better for comparing output volume. Nominal shows what output buys on world markets.
  • Per person (PPP), 2025: USA $90,267; China $29,369.

Mission 1

Financial and political power

  • The dollar: 56.70% of the world's allocated foreign-exchange reserves in 2026 Q2; the renminbi 2.11%.
  • The veto: China, France, Russia, the UK and the USA are permanent Security Council members; non-procedural decisions need "the concurring votes of the permanent members".
  • IMF votes: the USA holds 16.49%, China 6.08%. Quota changes need 85% of votes, so the USA alone can block them.
Mission 1

Military power

  • Military spending, 2025: USA $954 billion, 33% of the world total; China an estimated $336 billion, about 12%.
  • The 32 NATO members together spent $1,581 billion: 55% of the world total.
  • Military reach also depends on alliances and bases, not just budgets.
Mission 1

Demographic and resource power

  • Population, 2025 (IMF): India about 1.46 billion; China about 1.40 billion, below its 2020 level; USA about 342 million.
  • A large, young workforce can drive growth; an ageing, shrinking one can slow it.
  • Resources: the USA was the world's largest crude oil producer in 2025.
  • China produced 270,000 of the world's 390,000 tonnes of mined rare earths in 2025 (a quota figure); in April 2025 it tightened export controls on them. China's share was about 69% of world output (our calculation).
Mission 1

From hard to soft power

  • Hard power coerces or pays: military force, threats, sanctions, tariffs.
  • Soft power (Joseph Nye) attracts: culture, values, universities, media.
  • Between them sit economic tools that both reward and pressure, such as loans, investment and market access.
  • Brand Finance's 2026 soft-power survey: USA 1st (74.9 out of 100, down 4.6 points), China 2nd (73.5). It measures reputation, not actual flows.
Quick check
?Where does a government offering large infrastructure loans to other countries sit on the hard–soft spectrum?
Mission 1

How effective is each?

  • Hard power: fast and visible, but costly, and it can provoke resistance.
  • Soft power: cheap to sustain and long-lasting, but slow, hard to direct, and easily damaged by unpopular actions.
  • Smart power: combining the two.
  • Exam move (Edexcel "Evaluate"): judge effectiveness against a goal, a time scale and a place.
Mission 1

What counts as power has changed

  • Imperial era: power by direct control of territory.
  • Since 1945: more indirect control through trade, finance, alliances and culture.
  • China's goods exports: 3.8% of the world total in 2000, 14.5% in 2024. USA: 12.1% to 8.4%.
  • Belt and Road Initiative (BRI), proposed by China in 2013: "more than 200 BRI cooperation agreements with more than 150 countries and 30 international organizations" by June 2023 (Chinese government figure).
Mission 1

Drivers and responders

  • Drivers set the rules: veto holders, the largest IMF shareholders, issuers of reserve currencies.
  • Responders work within those rules: most states hold small IMF vote shares and no veto.
  • Responders can still resist: by joining blocs, or by using new lenders and partners.
  • A World Bank study found that transport projects under China's Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) could raise world trade by 1.7–6.2%, but some economies "could suffer a further medium-term deterioration in their outlook for debt sustainability".
Quick check
?Which evidence best shows unequal power relations in global systems?
Mission 1

Weighing the USA and China

  • USA: leads on nominal GDP, the dollar, military spending, alliances and soft power; holds a veto.
  • China: leads on PPP GDP and goods exports; holds a veto; builds new networks such as the BRI.
  • China's GDP per person (PPP) and military spending are each about a third of the USA's (our calculation).
  • Judgement: the USA is an actual superpower in every dimension; China a potential one, strongest economically.
Mission check

?A newspaper claims: "India's population is now the world's largest, so India is about to become a superpower." Which response is best supported by this mission?

The superpower question turns on which measure you use. At PPP China's economy is larger; at market rates, and in finance, military reach and soft power, the USA leads. The USA is the actual superpower; China the leading potential one.

Specification: what this mission covers

Mission 2

Four key ideas

  • Colonialism: a power rules another territory directly: its laws, officials and forces govern.
  • Neo-colonialism: a country is formally independent, but outside powers steer its economy and politics indirectly.
  • Hegemony: leadership by a dominant power whose rules others largely follow.
  • Polarity: how power is spread: unipolar (one superpower), bipolar (two), multipolar (several).
Mission 2

Direct control: the British Empire

  • Before 1957, UK Acts could extend to the Gold Coast and the UK government ran it.
  • Ghana Independence Act 1957: from 6 March 1957, no new UK Act would extend to Ghana unless requested, and "Her Majesty's Government in the United Kingdom shall have no responsibility for the government of Ghana".
  • Indian Independence Act 1947: from 15 August 1947, "two independent Dominions shall be set up in India, to be known respectively as India and Pakistan".
  • Direct control meant law-making, government and responsibility sat in London.
Mission 2

A multipolar world, 1919–39

  • Several great powers, none dominant; empires still held much of the world: "a large part of the world remained under colonial rule".
  • The League of Nations (1920–1946): "the first attempt to build a system of collective security".
  • But "the United States never joined the organization".
  • A later verdict quoted by the UN: "It failed because those principles were deserted by those states which brought it into being".
  • Multipolar risk: no power or institution strong enough to enforce the rules.
Quick check
?Which is the clearest evidence of direct colonial control?
Mission 2

Empires end, the Cold War begins

  • 1945–1960: "three dozen new states in Asia and Africa achieved autonomy or outright independence from their European colonial rulers".
  • Decolonisation "coincided with the new Cold War between the Soviet Union and the United States".
  • Some NATO allies argued their colonies gave them economic and military strength.
  • Formal empire shrank; influence over new states became the prize.
Mission 2

A bipolar world

  • NATO (1949): the USA, Canada and Western European nations.
  • Warsaw Pact (14 May 1955): the Soviet Union and several Eastern European countries, formed "as a counterbalance" to NATO.
  • Two blocs, each led by a superpower; most conflicts were fought indirectly (aid, arms, influence) rather than directly between the two.
Mission 2

Indirect control: neo-colonialism

  • Kwame Nkrumah (1965): "The essence of neo-colonialism is that the State which is subject to it is, in theory, independent and has all the outward trappings of international sovereignty. In reality its economic system and thus its political policy is directed from outside."
  • USA: the Marshall Plan (1948) gave over $12 billion to rebuild Western Europe, driven by "fear of Communist expansion".
  • USSR: used the Warsaw Pact to contain dissent in its European satellites: Hungary 1956, Czechoslovakia 1968, Poland 1981.
  • Tools: aid, loans, trade, military aid, alliances, intervention.
Quick check
?Which is an example of neo-colonial (indirect) control?
Mission 2

After 1991: a unipolar moment

  • The Warsaw Pact disbanded in March and July 1991, after the Soviet Union dissolved.
  • 1990: Krauthammer called the new order "The Unipolar Moment".
  • US military spending in 2025: $954 billion; China an estimated $336 billion.
  • Hegemony: a single power sets many global rules (finance, alliances, security).
Mission 2

China: a potential rival

  • China's GDP at purchasing-power parity has exceeded the USA's since 2016.
  • At market exchange rates (2025): USA 26.0% of world GDP, China 16.6%.
  • The US dollar: 56.70% of global foreign-exchange reserves.
  • China's Belt and Road could raise world trade by 1.7–6.2%, but some borrowers risk worse debt sustainability (World Bank).
  • Whether such lending is a form of indirect influence is debated: test each claim against evidence on terms, debts and outcomes.
Mission 2

Mission check

?Imagine 2040: three powers of similar economic size, shifting alliances, and a UN that cannot enforce its rulings. Which judgement does the evidence in this mission best support?

Power has shifted from direct rule (empire) to indirect influence (neo-colonialism), and between uni-, bi- and multipolar patterns. Each pattern brings its own stability and risks; scholars still disagree on which is safest.

Mission 3

Who are the emerging powers?

  • BRIC: Brazil, Russia, India, China. As BRICS: the four from 2006, South Africa from 2011.
  • 2024–25: Egypt, Ethiopia, Indonesia, Iran, Saudi Arabia and the UAE joined.
  • G20: 19 countries plus the EU; the African Union joined as a permanent member in September 2023.
  • G20 members hold about 85% of world GDP, over 75% of world trade and about two-thirds of world population.
Mission 3

Rising economic weight

  • Share of world GDP at PPP, 2000 → 2024:
    • China 7.5% → 19.1%; India 4.3% → 7.8%;
    • Russia 2.0% → 3.5%; Brazil 3.2% → 2.4%; South Africa 0.8% → 0.5%.
  • In US dollars (nominal), the USA's share fell from 30.2% to 26.2%; China's rose from 3.6% to 16.8%.
  • All shares are our calculations from these data.

Mission 3

Political importance

  • The G20 began in 1999 as a forum of finance ministers and central bank governors; after the 2008 financial crisis its leaders began meeting regularly.
  • The G20 chair rotates each year, so emerging powers set agendas in turn.
  • The BRICS created their own New Development Bank; its founding agreement was signed at the BRICS summit in Fortaleza (2014).
  • BRICS has also created partner countries, e.g. Nigeria, Malaysia, Kazakhstan.
Mission 3

Environmental governance

  • Share of world CO2 emissions, 2000 → 2024:
    • China 14.2% → 33.1%; India 3.9% → 8.0%; USA 23.1% → 11.7%.
  • No climate deal works without the biggest emitters at the table.
  • COP30, the UN Climate Change Conference, met in Belém, Brazil, 10–21 November 2025.
  • So emerging powers are both large emitters and hosts of climate diplomacy.

Mission 3

Strengths and weaknesses: economy and people

  • China: largest share of world GDP (PPP) among them, but ageing: people aged 65+ rose from 7.0% to 14.7% of the population, 2000–2024.
  • India: young (65+ only 7.1%) but GDP per person just US$2,592 in 2024.
  • Russia: 65+ share 17.2%, close to the USA's 17.9%.
  • South Africa: unemployment 32.3% in 2024 (ILO estimate).
  • Brazil: share of world GDP (PPP) fell from 3.2% to 2.4%.
Mission 3

Strengths and weaknesses: military, political, cultural, environmental

  • Military spending, 2024: USA US$1,005bn; China US$312bn; Russia US$149bn; India US$86bn; Brazil US$21bn; South Africa US$3bn.
  • Political: BRICS members have different political systems and foreign policies and do not always agree, which can limit joint action.
  • Cultural: influence through media, language and education is real but hard to capture in one figure.
  • Environmental: high and rising emissions bring pressure in climate talks.

Mission 3

Modernisation Theory

  • Rostow (1960): economies pass through stages to a "take-off" driven by investment, industry and trade.
  • Fits China and India: investment, industrialisation and opening to trade came with rising shares of world GDP.
  • Critique: assumes every country follows one Western path and ignores unequal power.
Mission 3

Dependency and World Systems theories

  • Dependency Theory (Frank, 1966): rich core countries keep the periphery "underdeveloped" by buying raw materials cheaply and selling manufactured goods.
    • Fits commodity exporters whose share of world GDP fell (Brazil, South Africa).
  • World Systems Theory (Wallerstein, 1974): core, semi-periphery and periphery; countries can move between them.
    • Fits China's rise from periphery towards the core.
Quick check
?Which theory best explains a country moving up from periphery to semi-periphery?
Mission 3

Weighing up the theories

  • Modernisation: explains China and India's growth path; weak on why others stall.
  • Dependency: explains commodity exporters' limits; weak on how China and India rose.
  • World Systems: explains movement and a multi-tier world; less precise about causes.
  • Exam move (Edexcel "Evaluate"): apply each theory to one named power with data, then judge which explains changing power best.
Mission check

?Country Z exports mainly raw materials to richer countries, has a young population and is a G20 member, but its share of world GDP has fallen for 20 years. Which conclusion is best supported?

Emerging powers matter more in the economy, politics and climate governance, but their strengths and weaknesses differ. Modernisation, Dependency and World Systems theories each explain part of their changing power.

Mission 4

The IGOs of the global economy

  • IMF: established in 1944 by 44 countries; now 191 members. Lends to countries in balance-of-payments trouble, with conditions.
  • World Bank: development lending; its IDA arm gives "grants and concessional loans" to low-income countries.
  • WTO: since 1 January 1995; 166 members "representing 98 per cent of world trade"; sets and polices trade rules.
  • World Economic Forum (WEF): founded in 1971, a not-for-profit "platform for public-private cooperation"; its Annual Meeting is in Davos. It makes no binding rules.
  • All four promote free trade, open markets and capitalism.
Mission 4

A shrinking share

  • G7 share of world GDP at PPP: 51.8% in 1980; 28.4% in 2025.
  • Emerging market and developing economies: 35.0% in 1980; 60.5% in 2025.
  • At market exchange rates the G7 still produced 44.5% of world GDP in 2025.
  • The change is real, but its size depends on the measure.

Mission 4

Who steers the IGOs?

  • IMF votes follow quotas: USA 16.49%, Japan 6.14%, China 6.08%.
  • Quota changes need "approval by 85% of the total voting power", so the USA alone can block them.
  • The WTO decides with its members, but its Appellate Body "is unable to review appeals given its ongoing vacancies"; the last member's term expired on 30 November 2020.
  • The WEF shapes agendas through dialogue, not votes.
Quick check

Mission 4

Global action: crises

  • The G20 began in 1999 as a forum of finance ministers; its leaders began meeting regularly after the 2008 financial crisis.
  • It brings emerging nations to the table: members "represent around 85% of the global GDP"; the African Union joined in 2023.
  • Crisis response, 2026: IEA members agreed to make 400 million barrels of emergency oil available, "by far the largest ever" collective action.
  • Superpowers lead such responses, but increasingly need emerging nations to join them.
Mission 4

Conflict: the "global police" role

  • The five permanent Security Council members can each block a non-procedural decision.
  • NATO's Article 5: "an armed attack against one or more of them in Europe or North America shall be considered an attack against them all".
  • It has been invoked once, after the 11 September 2001 attacks on the USA.
  • NATO members account for 55% of world military spending.
Mission 4

Military alliances and geostrategy

  • NATO: created by 12 countries on 4 April 1949; 32 members today.
  • ANZUS: Australia, New Zealand and the USA, signed in 1951; in force 1952.
  • In 1986 the USA suspended its ANZUS obligations to New Zealand after New Zealand refused entry to US nuclear-powered vessels.
  • Geostrategy: alliances extend a superpower's reach into regions far from home, and tie allies' security to it.
Mission 4

Economic alliances

  • EU: a single market with "free movement of goods, services, capital and persons".
  • USMCA: USA, Mexico and Canada; in force on 1 July 2020, replacing NAFTA.
  • ASEAN: founded on 8 August 1967 by Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, Singapore and Thailand; 11 members since Timor-Leste joined on 26 October 2025.
  • Each deepens interdependence: members' prosperity becomes tied to shared rules and markets.
Mission 4

Environmental alliances and climate conferences

  • IPCC: created in 1988 by the UN Environment Programme and the World Meteorological Organization; 195 member countries.
  • It assesses climate science for policymakers and does not carry out its own research; its reports are "policy-relevant but not policy-prescriptive".
  • UN climate conferences (COPs): at COP21 in Paris in 2015, 195 Parties adopted the Paris Agreement: hold warming "well below 2°C" and pursue 1.5°C.
  • Climate action needs both superpowers and emerging nations, whose emissions are large.

Mission 4

The UN and global stability

  • Security Council: 5 permanent members with a veto.
  • International Court of Justice: set up in June 1945 by the UN Charter; 15 judges at The Hague; settles legal disputes between states and gives advisory opinions.
  • Peacekeeping: 10 current operations, including South Sudan (UNMISS), the Democratic Republic of the Congo (MONUSCO) and Lebanon (UNIFIL).
  • Climate conferences: the annual COPs, such as COP21 in Paris.

Patents: who files?

  • A patent gives its owner the right to stop others using an invention for a set time.
  • International (PCT) applications, 2025: China 73,718, USA 52,617, Japan 47,922, Korea 25,016, Germany 16,441.

Patents: who earns?

  • Charges for the use of intellectual property = fees paid to use patents, trademarks, copyrights and franchises.
  • Receipts, 2024: USA $171.5 bn, China $7.1 bn; world $572.6 bn.
  • USA ≈ 30% of world receipts; China ≈ 1% (our calculation).
  • China paid $45.9 bn: a net payer of about $39 bn (our calculation).

Mission 5

TNCs run trade

  • UNCTAD (2013): global value chains are "typically coordinated by TNCs".
  • TNC-coordinated value chains "account for some 80 per cent of global trade".
  • Trade happens inside TNC networks: affiliates, contractors and suppliers.
  • So power over trade sits with firms that own technology and brands.
Mission 5

Link patents to power

  • 16 of the top 20 PCT filers are ICT firms.
  • Among the top 100 filers: Japan 32, China 24, USA 17.
  • But the USA earns about 24 × China's IP receipts (our calculation).
  • Exam move: patents show innovation capacity; IP receipts show who is paid for it. Use both.
Mission 5

Cultural reach through TNCs

  • Netflix (USA): "over half a billion people in more than 190 countries and 50 languages".
  • Coca-Cola (USA): drinks "enjoyed in over 200 countries every day".
  • Media, food and drink brands carry ways of life with them: this is cultural globalisation.
  • Westernisation: the spread of Western (often US) consumer culture, values and brands.
Mission 5

Not one-way

  • Netflix's most-viewed non-English shows: Squid Game S1 (Korea) 265.2 million views; Money Heist Part 4 (Spain) 106.0 million.
  • Non-US culture can reach the world, but often through a US platform.
  • So cultural power links to technology (platforms) and economics (ownership).

Mission 5

Counterfeiting

  • Counterfeiting = copying a brand or product without permission, breaking intellectual property rights (IPR).
  • The WTO's TRIPS agreement (in force 1 Jan 1995) sets global minimum IP rules.
  • Global trade in fake and pirated goods: $467 bn in 2021, 2.3% of world imports (OECD/EUIPO).
  • China and Hong Kong: over 87% of the value of fakes seized by US Customs, FY2025.

Mission 7

UNCLOS: the zones

  • The UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS, 1982) came into force in 1994; it has 172 parties.
  • Territorial waters: up to 12 nautical miles (nm) from the coast; full sovereignty, but foreign ships have a right of innocent passage.
  • Contiguous zone: up to 24 nm, for customs, immigration and sanitary control.
  • Exclusive economic zone (EEZ): up to 200 nm, with sovereign rights over living and non-living resources. Beyond lie the high seas, open to all states.
Mission 7

The seabed: shelf and the Area

  • A coastal state's continental shelf can extend beyond 200 nm where its continental margin does. Its outer limit may not lie beyond 350 nm from the coast or 100 nm beyond the 2,500 m depth line (whichever is further); on submarine ridges, 350 nm is the limit.
  • Claims beyond 200 nm go to the UN Commission on the Limits of the Continental Shelf (CLCS) for scientific review.
  • The seabed beyond national limits is the Area, "the common heritage of mankind", managed by the International Seabed Authority (ISA).

Mission 7

Arctic oil and gas

  • USGS (2008): 90 billion barrels of undiscovered oil and 1,669 trillion cubic feet of gas may lie north of the Arctic Circle, about 84% of it offshore.
  • September Arctic sea-ice area fell by about 40% between 1979–88 and 2010–19; the Arctic is likely to be practically ice-free in September at least once before 2050.

Mission 7

Overlapping claims

  • CLCS submissions: Russia (2001; revised 2015), Denmark/Greenland (December 2014), Canada (May 2019).
  • Denmark states that Canada's outer limits "would overlap" with Greenland's northern shelf, and that "there are potential overlaps of entitlement".
  • Central to the dispute: the Lomonosov Ridge, "a sliver of continental crust that divides the Arctic Ocean into its two main basins".
Mission 7

Attitudes and actions

  • Each state backs its claim with seabed science. Denmark argues that the ridges are "morphologically continuous with the land mass of Greenland".
  • In the 2008 Ilulissat Declaration, the five Arctic Ocean coastal states committed to the law of the sea and to "orderly settlement of any possible overlapping claims".
  • In 2014, Denmark/Greenland and Russia agreed not to object to the CLCS considering each other's submissions. The CLCS adopted recommendations on Russia's revised submission in February 2023.
Mission 7

Where the tension lies

  • Ownership: the CLCS judges the science, but where claims overlap, boundaries are left to the states themselves.
  • Exploitation: shrinking ice raises interest in oil and gas, but the resource estimates are old and probabilistic.
  • Disagreement over exploitation is a second tension: not who owns the oil and gas, but whether it should be extracted at all, given spill risk in icy seas and climate targets.
  • So far the process has used scientific submissions and diplomatic notes.
  • Exam move: separate the legal process (orderly so far) from the strategic stakes (rising as the ice retreats).

  • After 1991 a unipolar moment; China now a potential rival (largest GDP at PPP since 2016; USA still larger at market rates and in military spending).

Mission 3

  • BRICS and other G20 members have gained economic weight (BRICS-5 share of world GDP at PPP rose from 17.9% to 33.3%, 2000–2024), political forums (G20, BRICS, NDB) and a central role in climate talks (China 33.1% of CO2; COP30 in Belém).
  • Their future role depends on strengths and weaknesses such as ageing (China, Russia), low incomes (India), unemployment (South Africa) and commodity dependence; Modernisation, Dependency and World Systems theories each explain part of the change.

Mission 4

  • Superpowers shape the global economy through IGOs (IMF votes, WTO rules, World Bank lending, WEF agenda-setting), even as the G7's share of output falls.
  • Military (NATO, ANZUS), economic (EU, USMCA, ASEAN) and environmental (IPCC) alliances deepen interdependence; the UN (veto, ICJ, peacekeeping, COPs) supports stability but is limited when permanent members disagree.

Mission 5

  • Patent filings show innovation; IP receipts show who is paid. The USA earned $171.5 bn (2024), China $7.1 bn, despite China filing the most PCT applications.
  • TNCs coordinate about 80% of trade and carry culture (Netflix, Coca-Cola); counterfeiting ($467 bn, 2021) erodes IPR and strains US–China trade relations.

Mission 6

  • Both superpowers emit disproportionately; China leads annual totals, the USA leads per person and cumulative emissions.
  • Willingness differs: the USA has left Paris twice; China stays in with peak-by-2030 and neutrality-by-2060 targets. Middle-class growth raises demand for oil, rare earths, grain and water.

Mission 7

  • Zones: territorial waters (12 nm), contiguous zone (24 nm), EEZ (200 nm), high seas beyond; seabed: continental shelf, then the Area (ISA).
  • Arctic oil and gas raise tensions over overlapping shelf claims, managed so far through science and diplomacy.

Mission 8

  • Most trade moves by sea along East–West routes through a few choke points; when one fails, ships take the long way.
  • China's rise extends its influence but brings tension in the South China Sea; the Black Sea shows open conflict reaching world food supply.

Mission 9

  • Trade grew nearly thirtyfold in current dollars (2000–2024); Africa exports mainly ores, fuels and metals, and imports manufactures; China runs a surplus.
  • Opportunities: export earnings, infrastructure, jobs. Challenges: commodity dependence, debt, governance. Who gains most depends on each country's diversity and loan terms.

Mission 10

  • The Middle East produces about 31% of the world's oil; Hormuz carried about 20% of world oil use in 2024, mostly to Asia. Its effective closure in 2026 cut flows by about 87% and sent the North Sea Dated crude benchmark to a record $144 a barrel.
  • Economic (OPEC, UAE exit), cultural (contrasting state ideologies of Saudi Arabia and Iran), political (Iran's nuclear programme) and environmental (Nile dam) tensions overlap, so the region is an ongoing challenge for superpowers and emerging powers alike.

Mission 11

  • The USA and EU face rising debt (US 101% of GDP in 2026, projected 120% by 2036), unemployment and restructuring, which raise social costs and question spending on military power and space.
  • The future balance of power in 2030 and 2050 could be continued US dominance, bipolar or multipolar; the evidence points in different directions.
