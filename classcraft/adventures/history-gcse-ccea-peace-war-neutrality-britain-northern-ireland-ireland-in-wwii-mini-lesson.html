Peace, War & Neutrality: Britain, NI & Ireland in WWII

This option compares the wartime experience of Northern Ireland — inside the United Kingdom and at war — with that of Éire, which stayed neutral. It asks why de Valera chose neutrality, how Northern Ireland contributed, what the Belfast Blitz did, and how the war changed relations between Belfast, Dublin and London.

Background

Two states, one island — and a war

By 1939 the island of Ireland was divided in status as well as territory:

  • Northern Ireland was part of the United Kingdom, with its own devolved parliament at Stormont. When Britain declared war on Germany on 3 September 1939, Northern Ireland was at war.
  • Éire (the Irish Free State, renamed under the 1937 constitution) was effectively independent. In 1938 Britain had handed back the Treaty Ports — the naval bases it had retained under the 1921 Treaty — which made neutrality practically possible.

The Taoiseach, Éamon de Valera, declared Éire neutral. In Éire the war years were officially known as "the Emergency".

Neutrality

Why did de Valera choose neutrality?

De Valera's reasons were practical and political:

  • Sovereignty: neutrality was the clearest possible demonstration that Éire was independent of Britain.
  • Partition: many in Éire believed it was impossible to fight alongside a Britain that still governed the six counties.
  • Weakness: Éire's army, navy and air defences were tiny; the country was poorly equipped for modern war.
  • Domestic unity: after a civil war less than twenty years earlier, joining a British war risked reopening bitter divisions.

"Benevolent neutrality": Éire was neutral, but not evenhanded. Allied airmen who came down in Éire were often quietly returned across the border while German airmen were interned; weather reports were shared; the Irish government cooperated on security. Tens of thousands of Irish citizens also volunteered for the British forces, and many more crossed to work in British war industries.

Northern Ireland at war

The Northern Ireland war effort

Northern Ireland's contribution was industrial, agricultural and strategic:

  • Shipbuilding: Harland & Wolff built and repaired warships and merchant vessels, and employed tens of thousands.
  • Aircraft: Short & Harland built bombers and other aircraft in Belfast.
  • Linen and engineering: uniforms, parachutes, tents and munitions.
  • Agriculture: the "plough-up" campaign greatly increased tillage, and food was shipped to Britain.
  • Strategy: ports and airfields in Northern Ireland supported the Battle of the Atlantic — the convoy war on which Britain's survival depended.

Conscription was never applied to Northern Ireland. The government feared the nationalist reaction; recruitment was voluntary.

The Belfast Blitz

April–May 1941

Belfast was one of the least well-defended cities in the United Kingdom: few anti-aircraft guns, almost no searchlights, no night fighters based locally, and very limited public shelters. Planners had assumed it was beyond the range of serious attack.

  • On the night of 15–16 April 1941 — the Easter Raid — the Luftwaffe struck Belfast, killing around 900 people, the greatest loss of life in any single night raid on a UK city outside London.
  • On 4–5 May 1941 the Fire Raid devastated the docks and shipyard area.
  • Tens of thousands were made homeless, and many fled the city each night ("ditchers").

Cross-border help: after the April raid, fire brigades from Dublin, Dundalk, Drogheda and Dún Laoghaire crossed the border to help — a striking act of practical solidarity by a neutral state, and a moment often used in exam questions about changing relations.

The Americans

US troops in Northern Ireland

In January 1942 the first American troops to be stationed in Europe arrived in Northern Ireland. Over the following years hundreds of thousands passed through, training for the invasion of Europe.

  • Economic impact: bases, airfields and spending brought money and work.
  • Social impact: GIs brought jazz, dances, chewing gum and nylons; contact with American troops — including black GIs serving in a segregated US army — was a novelty for many.
  • Political impact: de Valera protested at the arrival of US forces on what he regarded as Irish soil — a reminder that partition remained bitterly contested even in wartime.

Relations under strain

Britain, Belfast and Dublin

  • 1940: with Britain in desperate straits, London offered a declaration in favour of a united Ireland in return for Éire entering the war. De Valera rejected it — the offer was vague, and Northern unionists had not agreed to it.
  • Northern unionists contrasted their war effort with Éire's neutrality, and argued that this proved the value of the Union. Neutrality strengthened partition rather than weakening it.
  • Churchill's frustration at being denied Irish bases during the Battle of the Atlantic was intense — and he said so publicly at the end of the war, drawing a famous reply from de Valera.
  • In Éire, "the Emergency" meant rationing, fuel shortages (turf instead of coal), strict censorship of press and post, and a small, ill-equipped defence force.

After the war

The war's legacy, 1945–49

The war reshaped politics on both sides of the border:

  • Northern Ireland shared in the post-war British welfare state — the National Health Service, family allowances, expanded education and housing. Living standards and services increasingly diverged from those in the South, and unionists argued this was a dividend of the Union.
  • Éire emerged from the war poorer and more isolated, and stayed outside NATO. It declared itself a republic (in force in 1949) and left the Commonwealth.
  • The Ireland Act (1949) confirmed that Northern Ireland would remain part of the UK unless its own parliament agreed otherwise — a guarantee unionists had sought.

The core judgement: the war hardened partition. The North's war effort and post-war welfare state deepened its ties to Britain, while neutrality reinforced the South's separate identity.

Recap

The big ideas to know

Two states: Northern Ireland at war from September 1939; Éire neutral — "the Emergency"

Neutrality: sovereignty, partition, military weakness and domestic unity; enabled by the 1938 return of the Treaty Ports; "benevolent" in practice

NI war effort: Harland & Wolff, Short & Harland, linen, munitions, food; ports and airfields for the Battle of the Atlantic; no conscription

Belfast Blitz: April–May 1941; around 900 killed in the Easter Raid; poor defences; Dublin fire crews crossed the border to help

The Americans: first US troops in Europe arrive in Northern Ireland, January 1942; de Valera protested

Legacy: Britain's 1940 unity offer rejected; the war hardened partition; post-war welfare state in the North; Éire a republic by 1949
