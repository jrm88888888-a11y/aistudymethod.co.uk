Mission 1

Shakespeare's source took months. Why squeeze it into two days?

Make a guess: Shakespeare's source took months. Why squeeze it into two days?

Othello · structure and the tragic shapeScreen 1

From Cinthio to Shakespeare

  • Source: Cinthio's prose tale in Gli Hecatommithi (1565), Decade 3, Story 7. Only Disdemona is named; her killers are "the Moor" and "the Ensign".
  • In Cinthio the Ensign's motive is rejected love, the killing is plotted coldly and the story runs over a long time.
  • Shakespeare names everyone, adds Roderigo and the Turkish threat, gives the Moor eloquence, and compresses time.
  • First recorded performance: at court, 1 November 1604. Printed in a Quarto (1622) and the First Folio (1623).
Othello · structure and the tragic shapeScreen 2

How your exam asks this

  • Edexcel Component 1, Section A: one essay from a choice of two, 35 marks, with no printed extract. You may take in a clean copy of the play but not the Critical Anthology. AO1, AO2, AO3 and AO5 are assessed: bring in critics' ideas in your own words.
  • Essay practice is for class. These seven missions build the knowledge every answer needs.
Othello · structure and the tragic shapeScreen 3

A comedy that turns into a tragedy

  • Act 1 has a comic shape: young lovers elope, an angry father blocks them, the Duke blesses the match. Susan Snyder (1979) reads the play as a comedy that tragedy then undoes.
  • Act 2: the storm drowns the Turks, so the war vanishes and the conflict turns private.
  • Act 3 Scene 3: the turning point (peripeteia). Suspicion, the handkerchief and Othello's vow fill one scene.
  • Act 5: catastrophe, then recognition (anagnorisis): "O fool! fool! fool!" Lodovico restores order: "And Cassio rules in Cyprus."

Othello · structure and the tragic shapeScreen 6

Two clocks: "double time"

  • Short time: the action in Cyprus fits two nights and a day. Bradley sets out the evidence that Desdemona "was murdered within a day or two of her arrival in Cyprus".
  • Long time: Bianca's "What, keep a week away?"; Emilia's "My wayward husband hath a hundred times / Woo’d me to steal it"; Venice's letter recalling Othello arrives impossibly fast.
  • John Wilson (Blackwood's Magazine, 1849–50) is credited with naming this double time.
  • Effect: speed creates pressure; the long-time hints keep an affair thinkable.

?Shakespeare's source took months. Why squeeze it into two days?

Shakespeare turned a slow Italian tale into a play whose conflict begins late, turns in one scene (Act 3 Scene 3) and runs without a pause to the catastrophe. The speed leaves Othello no time to check Iago's story, while double time makes an affair seem possible. Strong answers use this structure to explain tension, not just to retell the plot.

Mission 2

Othello's last speech: noble at last, or hiding from what he did?

Make a guess: Othello's last speech: noble at last, or hiding from what he did?

Othello · nobility and fallScreen 1

The noble Moor in Act 1

  • Self-command: facing armed men at night, he says "Keep up your bright swords, for the dew will rust them."
  • Status: "My parts, my title, and my perfect soul / Shall manifest me rightly."
  • Eloquence: "Rude am I in my speech" opens a speech that proves the opposite: "the Othello music" (G. Wilson Knight, 1930).
  • Bradley: "Othello is the greatest poet of them all".

Othello · nobility and fallScreen 6

The fall, and the voice that returns

  • Act 4: his verse breaks into prose fragments: "Noses, ears, and lips." He takes up Iago's animal images: "Goats and monkeys!"
  • Recognition: "O fool! fool! fool!"
  • Last speech: the music returns: "Speak of me as I am." He asks to be remembered as "one not easily jealous, but being wrought".
  • Textual crux: "Like the base Judean" (Folio, 1623; a Judas figure) or "Indian" (Quarto, 1622; an ignorant man).
  • He ends by killing himself as he once killed "the circumcised dog", Venice's enemy.

?Short argument, ungraded.

'By the end, Othello is still the noble Moor.' In 3–5 sentences, say how far you agree. Use one quotation from Act 1, one from Act 5 and one critic's view.

Model answer

In Act 1 nobility is self-command: "Keep up your bright swords, for the dew will rust them." In Act 5 the grand voice returns ("Speak of me as I am"), which supports Bradley's heroic reading. Yet "An honourable murderer, if you will" turns murder into a question of honour, the self-regard Leavis attacks. So nobility returns as style more than as moral insight, and I only partly agree.

Check your own answer

Mission checkScreen 9

?Othello's last speech: noble at last, or hiding from what he did?

The last speech brings back the "Othello music" of Act 1, so readers like Bradley see the noble Moor restored. But it also frames the murder as something done to him ("being wrought") and as honour, which Eliot and Leavis read as self-deception. The strongest answers weigh both readings against precise words.

Critics on the motive question

Othello · Iago, motive and methodScreen 5

Iago's method

  • Seeming: "I am not what I am." The line inverts the name God gives himself to Moses: "I AM THAT I AM" (Exodus 3:14, King James Version).
  • Soliloquy: he confides in us, like the Vice of the morality plays (Bernard Spivack, 1958). We become his audience, even his accomplices.
  • Improvisation: Stephen Greenblatt (1980) argues that Iago works by entering other people's stories and turning them: "She did deceive her father, marrying you".
  • Register: prose with Roderigo, verse with Othello. "Honest" sticks to him.

?Iago gives at least four motives. Why does none of them add up?

Iago's motives appear and vanish: he acts on suspicion, praises the man he destroys and finally refuses to speak. Coleridge called this "motive-hunting"; Bradley located the drive in a craving for power, and others find envy or racism. Strong answers treat the shifting motives as Shakespeare's method, not a puzzle with one solution.

Othello · Desdemona, Emilia and BiancaScreen 2

Marriage and reputation in 1604

  • Coverture: in English law a wife's legal identity was "covered" by her husband's.
  • Church teaching, such as the Homily of the State of Matrimony (1563), told wives to obey.
  • A woman's honour meant her chastity. Calling a woman a whore was public defamation, which could be taken to the church courts (Lisa Jardine, 1996).
  • Two texts: the 1622 Quarto lacks the Willow song, Emilia's speech on husbands and Desdemona's kneeling speech. The 1623 Folio has them.

?Each woman in Othello is branded unchaste. Who gets to say so?

The play's word for it, "whore", is thrown by men: Othello, Iago and, through Iago, Venice's idea of a woman's honour. But Emilia turns it on Bianca, showing how women can absorb the code too. All three women answer back, and critics from Bradley to Neely and Jardine read the play's tragedy through that clash.

Mission 5

Was Othello written to question racism, or does it repeat it?

Make a guess: Was Othello written to question racism, or does it repeat it?

Othello · race, the outsider and VeniceScreen 1

Venice, Cyprus and the "Moor"

  • Venice: a rich, mixed trading republic that hired foreign generals to lead its forces.
  • Cyprus: Venetian from 1489, taken by the Ottomans in 1570–71. A 1604 audience knew the island was already lost.
  • "Moor": a loose word for North Africans, Muslims or Black Africans. Othello is called "black".
  • London: Black Londoners lived and worked there. A 1596 Privy Council letter backed a scheme to deport some; it failed.

Othello · race, the outsider and VeniceScreen 6

Christian, convert, "Turk"

  • Othello is baptised: Iago says Desdemona could make him "renounce his baptism".
  • In the brawl he asks, "Are we turn’d Turks". Turning Turk meant converting to Islam, an English fear about the Mediterranean.
  • At the end he recalls killing "the circumcised dog" in Aleppo, then stabs himself: Venice's defender and its enemy in one body.
  • Daniel Vitkus (1997) reads the fall as a "conversion" back into the infidel Venice feared.

?Was Othello written to question racism, or does it repeat it?

The play puts racist language in the mouths of Iago, Roderigo and Brabantio and shows it destroying a great man, which supports reading it as a critique. But the plot also makes Othello act out Iago's slander, and audiences from Rymer to Olivier's era have read and staged it through their own prejudices. The strongest answers argue both, and say how context of production and of reception changes the play.

Othello · love and jealousyScreen 1

Kinds of love on stage

  • Mutual and romantic: "She lov’d me for the dangers I had pass’d, / And I lov’d her that she did pity them."
  • Absolute: "My life upon her faith!"
  • Possessive: "O curse of marriage, / That we can call these delicate creatures ours, / And not their appetites!"
  • Reductive: to Iago, love "is merely a lust of the blood and a permission of the will."
  • Unreturned: Roderigo's for Desdemona; Bianca's for Cassio.

One cloth, many meanings

?Short argument, ungraded.

'Othello loved not wisely, but too well.' In 3–5 sentences, say how far you agree. Use two quotations and one critic's view.

Model answer

Othello's love is intense: "My life upon her faith!" But "O curse of marriage, / That we can call these delicate creatures ours" treats love as possession, which Leavis would call self-regard rather than loving "too well". Bradley counters that his deepest pain is "the wreck of his faith and his love", so the love is real. He loved unwisely, but excess isn't the whole problem: he trusted Iago's word over hers.

Check your own answer

Mission checkScreen 9

?Othello says he 'loved not wisely, but too well'. Is that true?

Othello's love is real and intense, but it is also idealised and possessive, built on a story Iago can rewrite and tied to a token that can be "lost". So his verdict is partly true and partly a way of softening what he did. Strong answers weigh "wisely" against "too well".

Recap

Big ideas

  • Shakespeare reshaped Cinthio's 1565 tale: he named the characters, added the Turkish threat and compressed months into days.
  • The shape: a comic Act 1, a private conflict once the war vanishes, a turning point in Act 3 Scene 3, then catastrophe, recognition and restored order.
  • Double time: short-time pressure and long-time plausibility work together.
  • No extract: choose your own moments, and test critics such as Bradley against them.
  • Act 1 builds Othello's nobility through self-command, status and eloquent verse.
  • In Act 5 he recasts the killing as justice and sacrifice; in Act 4 his language had collapsed into Iago's.
  • The last speech divides critics: Bradley and Gardner hear nobility restored; Eliot and Leavis hear self-dramatising.
  • Iago offers many motives (promotion, Emilia, Desdemona, Cassio's goodness) and ends with "Demand me nothing."
  • Critics: Coleridge's "motive-hunting", Hazlitt's "amateur of tragedy", Bradley's sense of power, Auden's joker, Adelman's projection.
  • His method: seeming, soliloquy (the Vice tradition), improvisation and the word "honest".
  • Desdemona, Emilia and Bianca stand at different social levels, but all three are called whores.
  • Context: coverture, Church teaching on obedience and defamation show how a woman's honour was public property; the Folio adds the women's most outspoken lines.
  • Readings range from Hazlitt's will-less Desdemona to feminist readings of male anxiety (Snow) and women's clearer sight (Neely).
  • Use contexts of production (marriage law) and reception (feminist readings) together.
  • Venice hired foreign generals; Cyprus was lost to the Ottomans in 1570–71; "Moor" was a loose label; Black Londoners lived in Shakespeare's city.
  • Racist language comes from Iago, Roderigo and Brabantio; the Duke's praise keeps the same equation; Othello turns it on himself.
  • Faith matters too: baptism, "Turks" and the final "circumcised dog".
  • Reception: Rymer, Coleridge and Bradley reveal their own times; Aldridge, Olivier and Quarshie show staging changes the play.
  • Use context of production and of reception together, linked to precise moments.
  • The play shows many kinds of love: mutual, absolute, possessive, reductive, unreturned.
  • The courtship grows from story and pity; Desdemona is an eager listener.
  • Critics: Bradley's "wreck of his faith", Leavis's self-regarding love, Greenblatt's Christian unease about passion.
  • The handkerchief turns a love token into "proof", with two origins and many readings.
  • Readings span 1693 to now: Rymer's mockery, Coleridge's "motive-hunting", Bradley's noble hero, Leavis's attack, then gender, power and race.
  • Use a critic by stating, testing, placing and judging the view.
  • Bradley rejects the "noble barbarian" reading yet shows his own age's prejudice.
  • Paraphrase anthology essays; the anthology can't go into the exam.

Key quotations to learn

  • Excellent wretch! Perdition catch my soul, / But I do love thee! And when I love thee not, / Chaos is come again. (Act 3 Scene 3 (Othello)) Foreshadows the fall that follows in the same scene.
  • Pleasure and action make the hours seem short. (Act 2 Scene 3 (Iago)) Iago at dawn after the brawl: the speed of the plot.
  • What, keep a week away? Seven days and nights? (Act 3 Scene 4 (Bianca)) A "long time" hint: double time.
  • O fool! fool! fool! (Act 5 Scene 2 (Othello)) Recognition (anagnorisis).
  • The object poisons sight, / Let it be hid. (Act 5 Scene 2 (Lodovico)) The public tragedy ends hidden in a private room.
  • And Cassio rules in Cyprus. (Act 5 Scene 2 (Lodovico)) Order restored by Venice.
  • Keep up your bright swords, for the dew will rust them. (Act 1 Scene 2 (Othello)) Calm authority: the noble Moor.
  • My parts, my title, and my perfect soul / Shall manifest me rightly. (Act 1 Scene 2 (Othello)) Confidence in his own worth.
  • It is the cause, it is the cause, my soul, (Act 5 Scene 2 (Othello)) Murder framed as justice.
  • A murder, which I thought a sacrifice. (Act 5 Scene 2 (Othello)) He knows the frame has failed.
  • Speak of me as I am. (Act 5 Scene 2 (Othello)) The last speech shapes his story.
  • Of one that loved not wisely, but too well; (Act 5 Scene 2 (Othello)) Self-judgement or self-excuse?
  • I am not what I am. (Act 1 Scene 1 (Iago)) Seeming; an inversion of God's name.
  • I know not if ’t be true, / But I, for mere suspicion in that kind, / Will do as if for surety. (Act 1 Scene 3 (Iago)) Acting without evidence.
  • Doth, like a poisonous mineral, gnaw my inwards, (Act 2 Scene 1 (Iago)) Motive as inner poison.
  • So will I turn her virtue into pitch, (Act 2 Scene 3 (Iago)) His plan: goodness used as a trap.
  • He hath a daily beauty in his life / That makes me ugly. (Act 5 Scene 1 (Iago)) Envy of goodness.
  • Demand me nothing. What you know, you know. (Act 5 Scene 2 (Iago)) He refuses any motive.
  • I do perceive here a divided duty: (Act 1 Scene 3 (Desdemona)) She argues her own case before the senate.
  • Whate’er you be, I am obedient. (Act 3 Scene 3 (Desdemona)) Obedience, even before the crisis.
  • Then let them use us well: else let them know, / The ills we do, their ills instruct us so. (Act 4 Scene 3 (Emilia)) Emilia blames husbands (Folio-only).
  • ’Tis proper I obey him, but not now. (Act 5 Scene 2 (Emilia)) She breaks with obedience to speak.
  • I am no strumpet, but of life as honest / As you that thus abuse me. (Act 5 Scene 1 (Bianca)) The double standard answered.
  • Nobody; I myself. Farewell. (Act 5 Scene 2 (Desdemona)) Her last words protect Othello.
  • an extravagant and wheeling stranger / Of here and everywhere. (Act 1 Scene 1 (Roderigo)) The outsider as rootless.
  • Your son-in-law is far more fair than black. (Act 1 Scene 3 (Duke)) Praise built on prejudice.
  • Of her own clime, complexion, and degree, (Act 3 Scene 3 (Iago)) Iago turns difference into "unnatural" desire.
  • Haply, for I am black, (Act 3 Scene 3 (Othello)) He takes the prejudice into himself.
  • Are we turn’d Turks, and to ourselves do that / Which heaven hath forbid the Ottomites? (Act 2 Scene 3 (Othello)) Christian Venice vs the "Turk".
  • I took by the throat the circumcised dog, / And smote him, thus. (Act 5 Scene 2 (Othello)) Defender and enemy in one body.
  • She lov’d me for the dangers I had pass’d, / And I lov’d her that she did pity them. (Act 1 Scene 3 (Othello)) Love born from story and pity.
  • My life upon her faith! (Act 1 Scene 3 (Othello)) Absolute trust, cruelly ironic.
  • If she be false, O, then heaven mocks itself! (Act 3 Scene 3 (Othello)) Love as faith in a perfect ideal.
  • O curse of marriage, / That we can call these delicate creatures ours, / And not their appetites! (Act 3 Scene 3 (Othello)) Love as possession.
  • There’s magic in the web of it. (Act 3 Scene 4 (Othello)) The token made magical.
  • Of one that loved not wisely, but too well; (Act 5 Scene 2 (Othello)) His own verdict, to be weighed.
  • Speak of me as I am. Nothing extenuate, / Nor set down aught in malice. (Act 5 Scene 2 (Othello)) Othello asks to be read fairly: the critic's task.
  • Haply, for I am black, (Act 3 Scene 3 (Othello)) Central to race-centred readings.
  • They are all but stomachs and we all but food; (Act 3 Scene 4 (Emilia)) Central to feminist readings.
  • He hath a daily beauty in his life / That makes me ugly. (Act 5 Scene 1 (Iago)) Tests "motiveless" readings of Iago.
  • Thou art rash as fire, to say / That she was false: (Act 5 Scene 2 (Emilia)) Tests readings of Othello's judgement.
