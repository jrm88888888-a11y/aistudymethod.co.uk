Mission 1

Mission 1: Silence, set words or a live band: why do Christians worship in such different ways?

Make a guess: Silence, set words or a live band: why do Christians worship in such different ways?

Mission 1

What worship is

  • Worship: showing love, praise and honour to God; the old English word means giving God his worth.
  • "God is spirit, and those who worship him must worship in spirit and truth." (John 4:24)
  • Purposes: to praise and thank God, confess sin, hear God's word, ask for help, and grow as a community.
  • Public worship: together, usually on Sunday. Private worship: alone or at home.
  • "Where two or three are gathered together in my name, there I am in the middle of them." (Matthew 18:20)
Mission 1

Liturgical worship

  • Liturgical: a set order and set words, written down, often led by a priest.
  • Follows the liturgical year (Advent, Christmas, Lent, Easter, Pentecost) with set Bible readings.
  • Examples: the Catholic Mass, Orthodox Divine Liturgy, Anglican Holy Communion.
  • Why valued: unites Christians across the world and through history; words are tested and rich in belief; everyone can join in; dignified and reverent.
  • For Catholics the Mass is the "source and summit" of Christian life (Lumen Gentium; see Mission 5).
Mission 1

The Book of Common Prayer

  • The Book of Common Prayer (BCP): the Church of England's prayer book, first by Thomas Cranmer (1549), revised 1662.
  • "Common" prayer: the whole people praying the same words, in English, not Latin.
  • Contains Morning Prayer and Evening Prayer (Evensong), Holy Communion, baptism, marriage and funerals, and the Psalms.
  • Short set prayers called collects, one for each Sunday; Holy Communion opens with the Collect for Purity: "Almighty God, unto whom all hearts be open…"
  • Still authorised and used, especially at Evensong; most parishes now use modern Common Worship (2000).
  • BCP is also used in private and home prayer: a daily pattern of readings.
Mission 1

Non-liturgical worship

  • Non-liturgical: no fixed text; the minister plans the order and prays in his or her own words (extempore).
  • Bible reading and the sermon are at the centre.
  • Hymns and songs, prayers, offering, blessing.
  • Common in many Baptist, Presbyterian, evangelical and free churches.
  • Why valued: fits the needs of the day; sincere and personal; puts God's word first.
  • Evangelical worship: less structured; Bible preaching, personal faith, contemporary songs, invitation to respond.

Mission 1

Informal and charismatic worship

  • Informal: relaxed, spontaneous, led by the Spirit "in the moment".
  • Charismatic and Pentecostal worship: a band leads songs; people clap, raise hands, sometimes dance.
  • Gifts of the Spirit: speaking in tongues, prophecy, prayer for healing.
  • Worship may last a long time; anyone may pray aloud or share a testimony.
  • Why valued: joyful; expresses a personal relationship with God; open to the Spirit (Romans 8:14).
  • Some Pentecostal churches have very little set structure; others follow a regular pattern.
Mission 1

A Quaker meeting

  • Quakers (Religious Society of Friends): meet in a plain room, seats in a circle or square.
  • Worship is mostly silence, waiting on God, usually for about an hour.
  • Anyone who feels moved by the Spirit may stand and speak (ministry).
  • No clergy, no set words, no hymns, no outward sacraments.
  • Ends when two elders shake hands, then everyone shakes hands.
Mission 1

Private worship

  • Personal prayer and Bible reading, often daily ("quiet time").
  • Bible reading aids: daily reading notes, plans and apps that explain a passage and suggest a prayer.
  • Catholic and Orthodox aids: the rosary, icons, statues and candles (more in a later lesson).
  • Family prayer at meals and bedtime; listening to worship music.
  • Why it matters: Jesus taught "enter into your inner room" to pray (Matthew 6:6); a daily relationship with God, not only on Sundays.

Mission 1Evaluate

"Liturgical worship is the best way for Christians to worship."

  • Reasons for: unites Christians worldwide and across centuries; set words carry tested belief; everyone can join in; reverent; for Catholics and Orthodox, the Eucharist is the heart of worship.
  • Reasons against: set words can become routine (Jesus warned against "vain repetitions", Matthew 6:7); worship must be "in spirit and truth" (John 4:24), so sincerity matters more than form; the Spirit leads (Romans 8:14); Quakers and Pentecostals find God in silence or spontaneity.
  • Another view: different forms suit different people and occasions; many churches mix set prayers with free prayer and modern songs.
  • Judge it by: What is worship for: God's glory, community or personal growth? Does form help or hinder sincerity? Who decides what pleases God?

Mission 2: Why do some Christians count seven sacraments, some two, and some none at all?

Make a guess: Why do some Christians count seven sacraments, some two, and some none at all?

Mission 2

What is a sacrament?

  • A sacrament is a religious ceremony in which an outward sign (something seen and done) shows, and many Christians believe gives, an inward grace (God's free gift of love and help).
  • The Book of Common Prayer (1662) Catechism: "an outward and visible sign of an inward and spiritual grace".
  • Latin sacramentum: a sacred pledge. Greek mysterion: a mystery, God's hidden action made known.
  • Every sacrament has a sign (e.g. water, bread and wine, oil) and words (e.g. "I baptise you in the name of the Father…").
  • Christians believe the grace comes from God, not from the water or bread themselves.
Mission 2

The sacramental nature of reality

  • Catholic teaching: the whole of creation can show the presence of God.
  • Ordinary things (water, light, a meal, a touch) can become ways God meets people.
  • The sacraments are where this happens most fully: God uses created things to give grace.
  • So a Catholic can see God at work in a sunrise, a friendship or an act of kindness, as well as at Mass.
  • Significance: Catholics treat the world as holy, not just useful, and expect to meet God in daily life.
Mission 2

The seven sacraments

  • The Catholic Church teaches that Christ gave seven sacraments. They touch every stage of life: birth, growing up, food for the journey, illness, vocation.
  • Initiation: Baptism, Confirmation, Eucharist.
  • Healing: Reconciliation (Penance), Anointing of the Sick.
  • Service of communion and mission: Holy Orders, Matrimony.
  • Source: Catechism of the Catholic Church 1210 (Christ instituted seven sacraments touching all the stages of life) and 1211 (they form three groups: initiation, healing, service) (paraphrase).
  • Catholics call them efficacious signs: they do not only point to grace, they give it, because Christ acts through them.
  • (Each sacrament's rite and effects: Catholic missions.)

Mission 2Read the source

Article XXV

The Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles (1571, printed in the Book of Common Prayer, 1662):

> "There are two Sacraments ordained of Christ our Lord in the Gospel, that is to say, Baptism, and the Supper of the Lord."

  • The other five are "commonly called Sacraments" but are not "Sacraments of the Gospel", because they lack "any visible sign or ceremony ordained of God" (Article XXV).
  • The article still calls sacraments "effectual signs of grace": they are more than badges of membership.

Mission 2

Why two?

  • Most Protestants keep two: baptism and the Eucharist (Lord's Supper). They are called dominical sacraments: commanded by Jesus himself, with a sign.
  • Baptism: "Go and make disciples of all nations, baptising them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit," (Matthew 28:19)
  • Eucharist: "Do this in memory of me." (1 Corinthians 11:24)
  • Catholics and Orthodox reply: the other five also come from Christ, through the apostles and the Church's Tradition (e.g. anointing the sick in James 5:14; forgiving sins in John 20:23).
  • Protestants still value confirmation, marriage and ordination as holy rites; they just do not call them sacraments.

Mission 2Diversity

How many, and what do they do?

  • Catholic: seven sacraments; efficacious signs of grace.
  • Orthodox: the Holy Mysteries; usually seven are named, but the list is held less rigidly; chrismation and first Communion are given straight after baptism, even to babies.
  • Anglican: two "sacraments of the Gospel"; many Anglicans also treat the other five as sacramental rites.
  • Lutheran and Methodist: two sacraments, through which God really gives grace (some Lutherans also count confession and absolution).
  • Baptist and many Pentecostal and independent churches: two ordinances (practices Jesus ordered): acts of obedience and witness, not channels of grace in themselves.
  • Quakers and The Salvation Army: no outward sacraments (next screen).
Mission 2Diversity

No outward sacraments

  • Quakers (Religious Society of Friends, from the 1650s): all of life can be sacramental. They seek the inward baptism of the Holy Spirit and inward communion with Christ in silent meeting for worship. Outward rituals can become empty habits.
  • The Salvation Army stopped celebrating the sacraments in 1883 under William Booth. Its reasons: sacraments are not necessary for salvation; they had caused bitter division between churches; holy living matters most.
  • Salvationists have their own ceremonies (dedication of children; enrolling soldiers under the flag), but do not call them sacraments.
  • Neither group condemns Christians who do use sacraments.

Mission 2Evaluate

"Christians do not need sacraments to be close to God."

  • Reasons for: God is everywhere and can be met in prayer and silence (Quakers). Grace is God's free gift; no ritual can control it. Salvationists have worshipped and served others as Christians for over a century without sacraments.
  • Reasons against: Jesus commanded baptism and the Lord's Supper (Matthew 28:19; 1 Corinthians 11:24). Catholics and Orthodox believe sacraments are the main way Christ gives grace. Signs suit human beings, who are body as well as soul (the sacramental nature of reality).
  • Another view: sacraments are needed for the Church as a community, even if God can reach individuals in other ways.
  • Judge it by: What did Jesus command? Is grace tied to signs, or free? What do the different traditions mean by "close to God"?
Mission 2Worked answer

Two reasons, developed

Question: Explain two reasons why Christians disagree about the number of sacraments.

  • Reason 1: Most Protestants accept only sacraments commanded by Jesus with a sign. Source: Article XXV: "There are two Sacraments ordained of Christ our Lord in the Gospel". Development: so baptism and the Lord's Supper count, but confirmation and marriage do not.
  • Reason 2: Catholics accept Tradition as well as Scripture. Source: CCC 1210 teaches that Christ instituted seven sacraments (paraphrase). Development: so the Church's handing on of practices like anointing the sick shows they come from Christ too.
  • 5-mark (c) tip: two reasons, each developed, plus a source of wisdom and authority.

Mission 3

A world that shows God

  • Sacramental nature of reality: Catholics believe the whole of creation can show the presence of God.
  • "The heavens declare the glory of God." (Psalm 19:1)
  • Things that can be seen point to the God who cannot be seen.
  • Grace: God's free gift of his own life and help.
  • Catholics therefore see God at work in nature, people and events, and above all in the seven sacraments.
Mission 3

What a sacrament is

  • Sacrament: an outward, visible sign, given by Christ to the Church, that gives the inward grace it signifies (CCC 1131, paraphrase).
  • A long-standing summary: "a visible sign of invisible grace".
  • Each has matter (the sign: water, oil, bread) and form (the words that go with it).
  • Catholics believe Christ himself acts in every sacrament. It works by Christ's power, not the minister's holiness.
  • The fruit depends on the openness of the person receiving it.
  • So sacraments communicate grace: they are not only reminders, they do what they show.
Mission 3Read the source

CCC 1210–1211

Catechism of the Catholic Church 1210–1211 (paraphrase):

  • Christ gave the Church seven sacraments: baptism, confirmation, Eucharist, penance (reconciliation), anointing of the sick, holy orders and matrimony.
  • They touch every stage and key moment of life: they give birth and growth, healing and mission to the life of faith.
  • This mirrors natural life: being born, growing up, eating, being healed, serving others.
  • So there are three groups: initiation, healing, and sacraments at the service of communion (holy orders, matrimony).
  • The Eucharist has a unique place: the "sacrament of sacraments"; all the others lead to it.

Mission 3

Seven sacraments, seven effects

  • Baptism: new birth; original sin and all sins washed away; member of the Church.
  • Confirmation: strengthened by the Holy Spirit to live and witness to the faith.
  • Eucharist: receiving Christ himself; food for the journey; unity with the Church.
  • Reconciliation: forgiveness of sins committed after baptism.
  • Anointing of the sick: strength, peace and healing in serious illness.
  • Matrimony: grace for a faithful, lifelong love; holy orders: a deacon, priest or bishop given to serve the Church.

Mission 3

Baptism: the ceremony

  • Usually for babies, at the font; parents and godparents present the child.
  • Sign of the cross traced on the forehead: the child belongs to Christ.
  • Anointing with oil of catechumens: strength against evil.
  • Parents and godparents reject sin and profess the faith for the child.
  • Water poured three times (or immersion): "N., I baptise you in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit" (from Matthew 28:19).
  • Anointing with chrism on the head: sharing Christ's role as priest, prophet and king.
  • White garment: a new life, "clothed in Christ". Candle lit from the Easter (Paschal) candle: the light of the risen Christ.
Mission 3

Baptism: what it does

  • Catholics believe that baptism:
  • Washes away original sin and all sin.
  • New birth as a child of God; a member of the Church, the Body of Christ: "in one Spirit we were all baptised into one body" (1 Corinthians 12:13).
  • Gives sanctifying grace; leaves a permanent spiritual mark, so it is never repeated.
  • Water means both death and life: "buried… with him through baptism into death… so we also might walk in newness of life" (Romans 6:4).
Mission 3

Confirmation

  • Completes the grace of baptism. Usually received in the teenage years; in Ireland, often near the end of primary school.
  • The bishop is the usual minister. Candidates renew their baptismal promises; a sponsor stands with each one.
  • The bishop extends his hands over them and prays for the seven gifts of the Holy Spirit: wisdom, understanding, counsel, fortitude, knowledge, piety, fear of the Lord (from Isaiah 11:2).
  • Anointing with chrism on the forehead: "Be sealed with the Gift of the Holy Spirit."
  • Roots: "Then they laid their hands on them, and they received the Holy Spirit." (Acts 8:17)
  • Meaning: a closer bond with Christ and the Church; strength to witness; a permanent mark, received once. Many take a saint's name.
Mission 3

The Eucharist completes initiation

  • First Holy Communion, usually around age 7–8, after first reconciliation.
  • Baptism gives new life; confirmation strengthens it; the Eucharist feeds it, again and again.
  • Catholics believe they receive Christ himself under the appearances of bread and wine (see Mission 5).
  • Effects: closer union with Christ, forgiveness of lesser (venial) sins, unity with the whole Church.
  • Adults joining the Church usually receive all three together at the Easter Vigil.

Mission 3Diversity

Orthodox and Protestant views

  • Orthodox: call the sacraments holy mysteries; usually count seven, though the number is held less strictly.
  • Babies are baptised by triple immersion, then at once chrismated (confirmed) and given Communion: full initiation in one service.
  • Most Protestants: two sacraments, baptism and the Lord's Supper, because only these were clearly commanded by Christ in the Gospels (Article XXV, Church of England).
  • Anglicans and Lutherans keep confirmation as an important rite, not a sacrament of the Gospel.
  • Baptists: baptism is an ordinance (an act of obedience) for believers only.
  • Quakers and the Salvation Army: no outward sacraments; the whole of life can be lived in God's presence (see Mission 2).
Mission 3Evaluate

"Catholics need the sacraments to receive God's grace."

  • For: Christ gave the sacraments himself: "baptising them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 28:19). The Church teaches that for believers they are necessary for salvation (CCC 1129, paraphrase). Humans are bodily: visible signs meet them where they are.
  • Against: the sacramental view of reality says God's grace is found everywhere: in creation, prayer, Scripture and other people. The Catechism says God has bound salvation to baptism but is not himself bound by his sacraments (CCC 1257, paraphrase).
  • Other Christians: most Protestants stress grace received through faith and the Word; Quakers through inward experience.
  • Judge it by: Is "need" the same as "only"? What did Christ command? Can grace be found outside the sacraments and still lead to them?
Mission check

?At the Easter Vigil, Tom, an adult, is baptised, confirmed and receives his first Holy Communion in one service. How does Catholic teaching understand this?

For Catholics, ordinary things (water, oil, bread) become signs through which Christ really gives his grace.

Mission 4

Mission 4: If only God can forgive sins, why do Catholics confess them to a priest?

Make a guess: If only God can forgive sins, why do Catholics confess them to a priest?

Mission 4

Sacraments of healing and service

  • Healing: Jesus forgave sins and healed the sick. Catholics believe he continues both through two sacraments.
  • Reconciliation heals the soul from sin; anointing of the sick strengthens the seriously ill.
  • At the service of communion: two sacraments given for building up others.
  • Matrimony builds a family; holy orders gives the Church its deacons, priests and bishops.
Mission 4

Reconciliation: the rite

  • Also called confession or penance. For sins committed after baptism.
  • Four parts:
  • Contrition: real sorrow for sin and a firm intention not to sin again.
  • Confession: telling one's sins to a priest, in a confessional or reconciliation room.
  • Penance (satisfaction): a prayer or action the priest gives, to help repair the harm.
  • Absolution: the priest says, "I absolve you from your sins in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit."
  • The seal of confession: a priest may never reveal what he hears, for any reason.
  • Catholics must confess grave (mortal) sins at least once a year; many go far more often, especially in Advent and Lent.
Mission 4

Why a priest?

  • Catholics agree: "Who can forgive sins but God alone?" (Mark 2:7). The priest does not forgive by his own power.
  • The risen Jesus gave the apostles this task: "If you forgive anyone's sins, they have been forgiven them." (John 20:23)
  • Catholics believe bishops and priests share it through ordination; the priest acts in the person of Christ.
  • Sin also harms the Church, so the penitent is reconciled with God and the community.
  • People need to name their sins and hear that they are forgiven.
  • Effects: forgiveness, peace of conscience, restored friendship with God, strength to resist sin.

Mission 4

Anointing of the sick

  • "Is any amongst you sick? Let him call for the elders of the assembly, and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord" (James 5:14)
  • For: anyone seriously ill, frail through old age, or facing major surgery. It can be repeated if the illness gets worse.
  • Rite: the priest lays his hands on the person in silence, then anoints the forehead and hands with the Oil of the Sick, blessed by the bishop in Holy Week.
  • Effects: strength, peace and courage; union with Christ's own suffering; forgiveness of sins; healing of body, if God wills.
  • "The Lord will raise him up. If he has committed sins, he will be forgiven." (James 5:15)
  • Once called "extreme unction"; since the Second Vatican Council it is not only for the dying.

Mission 4

Matrimony

  • In the Western (Latin) Catholic Church, the bride and groom are the ministers: they give the sacrament to each other by their free consent.
  • The priest or deacon receives their consent for the Church; two witnesses are present.
  • Rite: questions (freely? faithfully? open to children?), vows, blessing and exchange of rings, nuptial blessing; often within Mass.
  • "The two will become one flesh… What therefore God has joined together, let no man separate." (Mark 10:8–9)
  • A sign of Christ's love: "Husbands, love your wives, even as Christ also loved the assembly and gave himself up for her" (Ephesians 5:25).
  • Effects: a lifelong, exclusive bond; grace to love faithfully; the family as a "domestic church".
Mission 4

Holy orders and the role of the priest

  • Three degrees: deacon, priest, bishop. Only a bishop ordains.
  • Ordination: the bishop lays hands on the candidate's head in silence and says the prayer of consecration. A new priest's hands are anointed with chrism; he receives the chalice and paten.
  • "Stir up the gift of God which is in you through the laying on of my hands." (2 Timothy 1:6)
  • It leaves a permanent mark: once a priest, always a priest. The Church ordains only baptised men; in the Latin Church priests promise celibacy.
  • Role of the priest: celebrates Mass ("Do this in memory of me", Luke 22:19), hears confessions, anoints the sick, baptises, marries, buries; preaches; cares for the parish.
  • He acts in the person of Christ and links the parish to the bishop and, through him, to the apostles.
Mission 4Diversity

Orthodox and Protestant views

  • Orthodox: all four are holy mysteries. Confession is often made standing before an icon of Christ or the Gospel book, with the priest as witness. Holy Unction may be given for any illness, and to all on Holy Wednesday in many churches.
  • Orthodox weddings centre on crowning; the priest is the minister. Married men may become priests; bishops are chosen from celibate monks.
  • Most Protestants: none of the four is a sacrament.
  • They confess directly to God: "If we confess our sins, he is faithful and righteous to forgive us" (1 John 1:9). Some Anglicans and Lutherans also offer private confession to a priest.
  • Marriage is holy but not a sacrament; ordination sets ministers apart, and many churches ordain women. They stress the priesthood of all believers (1 Peter 2:9).
  • Catholics also teach a common priesthood of all the baptised, but hold that ordination gives a distinct, ministerial priesthood.

Mission 4Evaluate

"Catholics should not need a priest to be forgiven."

  • Claim: confession to a priest is unnecessary, because God forgives directly.
  • For: "Who can forgive sins but God alone?" (Mark 2:7). "There is one God and one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus" (1 Timothy 2:5). Most Protestants confess directly to God (1 John 1:9). Catholic teaching itself says perfect sorrow, out of love for God, brings forgiveness, with the intention to confess (CCC 1452, paraphrase).
  • Against: Jesus gave the apostles authority to forgive (John 20:23). Sin harms the Church, so reconciliation with the Church matters. Hearing the words of absolution brings assurance. The priest acts for Christ, not instead of him.
  • Judge it by: Who does the forgiving? Is sin only private? What helps a person really change?
Mission check

?At a school retreat, a priest hears confessions and says to each person, "I absolve you from your sins…". A Protestant friend asks how a priest can forgive sins. Which answer fits Catholic teaching?

For Catholics, the priest is the instrument and God the forgiver: confession is meeting Christ through his Church.

Mission 5

The shape of the Mass

  • Mass: the Catholic celebration of the Eucharist. Every Mass in the world has the same four-part shape.
  • Introductory Rites: gather, confess sin, praise God.
  • Liturgy of the Word: God speaks through the Bible.
  • Liturgy of the Eucharist: bread and wine offered, consecrated and received.
  • Concluding Rites: blessing and sending out.
Mission 5

Gathering and the Word

  • Introductory Rites: entrance hymn and procession; sign of the cross; Penitential Act ("Lord, have mercy"); Gloria on Sundays (not in Advent or Lent); the Collect (opening prayer).
  • Liturgy of the Word: First Reading (usually the Old Testament); Responsorial Psalm; Second Reading (a New Testament letter, on Sundays).
  • Gospel Acclamation (Alleluia) as all stand; the Gospel, read by a priest or deacon.
  • Homily: the priest explains the readings.
  • Creed (on Sundays) and Prayer of the Faithful (bidding prayers for the Church and the world).
Mission 5

The Liturgy of the Eucharist

  • Preparation of the Gifts: bread and wine brought to the altar; collection.
  • Eucharistic Prayer: "Lift up your hearts"; the Sanctus ("Holy, holy, holy"); the priest asks the Holy Spirit to make the gifts holy.
  • Consecration: the priest speaks Jesus' Words of Institution over the bread and wine; he raises each; bells may ring; people kneel.
  • Mystery of Faith: all proclaim Christ's death and resurrection; the prayer ends with the Great Amen.
  • Communion Rite: Lord's Prayer; sign of peace; the host is broken while all sing the Lamb of God; Holy Communion.
  • Concluding Rites: blessing; dismissal: "Go forth, the Mass is ended."

Mission 5

"Source and summit"

  • The Second Vatican Council calls the Eucharist the source and summit of the whole Christian life (Lumen Gentium 11; CCC 1324).
  • Source: the Church's life and grace flow from it, as a river flows from a spring.
  • Summit: everything the Church does leads to it, as a path leads to a mountain top.
  • So Sunday Mass is an obligation for Catholics: the week flows from it and back to it.
Mission 5Read the source

Lumen Gentium 7 and 11

Lumen Gentium (Second Vatican Council, 1964), paraphrase:

  • LG 7: really sharing in the Lord's body in the breaking of the bread, we are lifted into communion with Christ and with one another. As Paul says, because there is one bread, we who are many are one body (1 Corinthians 10:17).
  • LG 11: taking part in the Eucharistic sacrifice, the "source and summit" of the whole Christian life, the faithful offer Christ, and themselves with him, to God.

Mission 5

The Real Presence

  • Catholics believe that at the consecration the bread and wine become the Body and Blood of Christ: the Real Presence.
  • Transubstantiation: the substance (what it really is) changes; the appearances (taste, look, chemistry) of bread and wine remain.
  • Aquinas: "the whole substance of the bread is changed into the whole substance of Christ's body" (Summa Theologiae III, q. 75, a. 4).
  • "I am the living bread which came down out of heaven… the bread which I will give for the life of the world is my flesh." (John 6:51)
  • Catholics believe Christ remains present after Mass: hosts are kept in the tabernacle, a lamp burns beside it, and Catholics genuflect.
Mission 5

Words, Lamb, presence, sacrifice

  • Words of Institution: the priest repeats Jesus' words: "This is my body which is given for you" (Luke 22:19). He acts in the person of Christ.
  • Real Presence: Catholics believe that through these words, by the Spirit's power, Christ becomes present.
  • Sacrifice of the Mass: Catholics believe the body "given" and the blood "poured out" are the one sacrifice of Calvary, made present now in an unbloody way. It is not a new or repeated sacrifice.
  • Agnus Dei: as the host is broken, all sing to the Lamb of God who takes away the sins of the world: "Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!" (John 1:29). Jesus is the new Passover lamb (1 Corinthians 5:7).
  • The chain: words → presence → sacrifice → Lamb received in Communion.
  • Many Protestants object: Christ was offered "once for all" (Hebrews 10:10). Catholics reply: yes, and the Mass makes that one offering present.

Mission 5Diversity

The Eucharist and worship in other churches

  • Orthodox: the Divine Liturgy; the bread and wine truly become Christ's Body and Blood, a mystery not explained by "transubstantiation".
  • Anglican: Holy Communion, weekly in many parishes; views range from real presence to spiritual presence.
  • Lutheran: Christ truly present "in, with and under" the bread and wine.
  • Methodist, Presbyterian: Christ spiritually present; the Lord's Supper monthly or a few times a year.
  • Baptist, Pentecostal, many evangelicals: a memorial ("Do this in memory of me"); worship centres on Bible preaching.
  • Less structured evangelical worship: a band leads songs; prayer is spontaneous; long sermon; room for the Spirit's gifts. They value freedom and personal response; Catholics value a liturgy shared by the whole Church in every place.
Mission 5Evaluate

"Catholics should go to Mass every Sunday."

  • Claim: weekly Mass should be a duty for every Catholic.
  • For: the Church teaches that the Eucharist is the source and summit of Christian life (all its life flows from it and leads to it). Sunday is the day of the resurrection. The Church is one body and cannot be lived alone: "not forsaking our own assembling together" (Hebrews 10:25). The Church's law asks it.
  • Against: love cannot be forced; duty can make worship routine. God is also met in prayer, creation and service of others. Illness, work or distance can prevent it. Most Protestants have no such rule, and stress a free, personal response.
  • Judge it by: Is a duty a help or a burden? Can faith grow without a community? What makes worship real?

Mission 6

Before death: the "last rites"

  • When a Catholic is dying, a priest is called to give the last rites: the sacraments that prepare for the journey to God.
  • Reconciliation (confession): sins forgiven; peace with God and the Church.
  • Anointing of the Sick: the priest lays hands on the person and anoints the forehead and hands with the Oil of the Sick (James 5:14–15).
  • Viaticum (Latin, "food for the journey"): Holy Communion as the last Eucharist, with Christ's promise "I will raise him up at the last day" (John 6:54).
  • Family and friends pray with the dying person (prayers of commendation).
Mission 6

The funeral in three stations

The Catholic funeral rite (Order of Christian Funerals) follows the body from home to church to cemetery, like a journey.

  • 1. Vigil (home): the evening before; prayers, Scripture, perhaps the Rosary, at home, in a funeral home or in church. A time of quiet support.
  • 2. Funeral liturgy (church): usually a Requiem Mass; sometimes a Liturgy of the Word.
  • 3. Committal (cemetery or crematorium): the body is laid to rest.
  • Each stage expresses the belief that the person is passing from death to eternal life with Christ.
Mission 6

In church: symbols of baptism and resurrection

  • Holy water sprinkled on the coffin as it enters: recalls baptism, when the person first shared Christ's death and rising (Romans 6:4).
  • White pall (cloth) over the coffin: the white baptismal garment; "you … have put on Christ" (Galatians 3:27). All are equal in death.
  • Paschal candle beside the coffin: Christ, the light who has overcome death.
  • Crucifix, Book of the Gospels or Bible on the coffin: life shaped by Christ's word.
  • Incense at the end: respect for the body, once a "temple of the Holy Spirit" (1 Corinthians 6:19), and prayers rising to God.
  • Vestments: white (resurrection), violet or black (mourning, prayer for mercy).
Mission 6

Prayers, readings and the farewell

  • Readings often chosen: "The souls of the righteous are in the hand of God" (Wisdom 3:1); "In my Father's house are many homes" (John 14:2); "I am the resurrection and the life" (John 11:25).
  • A homily proclaims the hope of resurrection; it is not just a eulogy.
  • The Mass is offered for the person who has died.
  • Final commendation: the community says farewell and commends the person to God.
  • Traditional prayer: "Eternal rest grant unto them, O Lord, and let perpetual light shine upon them."
  • As the coffin leaves, the In paradisum asks that angels lead the person into paradise (paraphrase).
  • Committal: at the grave or crematorium, the body is entrusted to the earth "in sure and certain hope" of resurrection. Catholics may choose cremation (allowed since 1963), but ashes are kept in a sacred place, not scattered (2016 guidance).
Mission 6Read the source

"Preparing my funeral" (Vincent Nichols)

  • Cardinal Vincent Nichols, Archbishop of Westminster, introduces a planning guide encouraging Catholics to prepare their own funeral (paraphrase).
  • Why prepare? It spares grieving relatives hard decisions, especially if they do not share the faith; and it can deepen one's own faith through the Church's Scriptures and songs.
  • It follows the three stages: Vigil, Funeral Liturgy (usually Mass), Committal.
  • Aims of the funeral rite: to worship and thank God; to pray for the person who has died; to support and console the bereaved.
  • Communion with the deceased: the living and the dead remain one in Christ; prayer for the dead continues the relationship.
  • Communion of the community: the parish gathers around the family.
  • Proclamation of eternal life: for the faithful, "life is changed, not ended" (Preface for the Dead).

Mission 6

Praying for the dead

  • Catholics believe in the communion of saints: the Church on earth, the souls in purgatory and the saints in heaven are united in Christ.
  • So the living can help the dead by prayer: "it would be superfluous and idle to pray for the dead" if they were not expected to rise (2 Maccabees 12:44).
  • Masses for the dead: a family asks a priest to offer Mass for a person (a Mass intention), often at the funeral, a month later, and on anniversaries.
  • All Souls' Day (2 November): the whole Church prays for all the dead. November is the month of the Holy Souls.
  • This is the communion with the deceased the funeral aims at.
Mission 6Evaluate

"A Catholic funeral is more for the living than for the dead."

  • Reasons for: much of the rite consoles the bereaved: the vigil's "quiet support", readings of hope, the gathered community; the aims include supporting the mourners.
  • Reasons against: the Mass is offered for the dead person; prayers and the final commendation entrust them to God's mercy; Catholics believe prayer really helps those in purgatory (communion of saints), so the dead are its first concern.
  • Another view: for Catholics the distinction is false: the living and the dead form one communion in Christ; the rite serves both and above all worships God.
  • Judge it by: Who are the prayers addressed to and about? What would be lost if the dead were not prayed for? Do non-Catholic funerals share the same aims?

A Catholic funeral completes what baptism began: water, white garment and light proclaim that the person who died with Christ will rise with him.

Mission 7: If God already knows what you need, why do Christians pray at all?

Mission 7

Raising the mind and heart

  • A classic Christian definition: prayer is the raising of the mind and heart to God (St John of Damascus, 8th century).
  • Edexcel's wording: "raising of hearts and minds to God".
  • For Christians, prayer is communication with God: speaking and listening.
  • Jesus prayed often, alone and with others (e.g. Luke 5:16; Mark 14:32–36).
  • Why pray, if God knows? "your Father knows what things you need before you ask him" (Matthew 6:8). Christians say prayer is about relationship and trust, not giving God information.
Mission 7

Types of prayer

  • Adoration: praising God for who God is.
  • Confession: admitting sin and asking forgiveness.
  • Thanksgiving: thanking God for gifts and blessings.
  • Supplication: asking. Petition = asking for yourself; intercession = praying for others.
  • Memory aid: ACTS (adoration, confession, thanksgiving, supplication).

Mission 7Read the source

The Lord's Prayer (Matthew 6:9–13)

"Our Father in heaven, may your name be kept holy.

Let your Kingdom come. Let your will be done on earth as it is in heaven.

Give us today our daily bread.

Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors.

Bring us not into temptation, but deliver us from the evil one."

  • Many churches add: "For yours is the Kingdom, the power, and the glory forever. Amen." (not in the earliest manuscripts).
  • In worship most Christians say a traditional wording ("Our Father, who art in heaven, hallowed be thy name…").

Mission 7

What the Lord's Prayer means

  • "Our Father": God is close and loving, like a parent; "our" makes it a prayer of the whole community.
  • "May your name be kept holy": adoration; God is honoured above all.
  • "Your Kingdom come": asks for God's rule of justice and peace on earth.
  • "Your will be done": trust and obedience, as Jesus prayed in Gethsemane that his Father's will, not his own, be done (Luke 22:42, paraphrase).
  • "Daily bread": needs, physical and spiritual (Catholics also think of the Eucharist).
  • "Forgive us… as we also forgive": forgiveness received is to be passed on.
  • "Deliver us": protection from evil.
  • Significance: Jesus gave it as a model for all prayer; said at every Mass and most services, and by Christians of almost every denomination.
Mission 7

Jesus' teaching around the prayer

  • Matthew 6:5–6: not like hypocrites who pray on street corners to be seen; "enter into your inner room", shut the door, and pray to your Father in secret.
  • Matthew 6:7–8: "don't use vain repetitions", as if many words will get you heard; your Father knows what you need.
  • Matthew 6:14–15: if you forgive others, your Father will forgive you; if you do not, he will not.
  • So: prayer should be sincere, humble, trusting and forgiving.
  • When used: private prayer, at every Mass and service, at baptisms, weddings and funerals; when someone does not know what to say.

Mission 7

Set and informal prayer

  • Set prayers: written words, said alone or together: the Lord's Prayer, the Hail Mary, the Book of Common Prayer collects, the Jesus Prayer.
  • Informal prayer: in your own words, as you feel led.
  • Your board's terms: formulaic (set) and extempore (informal).
  • Why set prayers: shared and unifying; rich in belief; help when words fail; connect with Christians through history.
  • Why informal prayer: personal and sincere; fits the moment; open to the Spirit's leading.
  • When each is used: set prayers in liturgical services (Mass, Holy Communion, baptisms, funerals) and in private (e.g. the Lord's Prayer, the Jesus Prayer); informal prayer in private, at prayer meetings and in non-liturgical services.
  • Different views: Catholics, Orthodox and Anglicans value set prayer highly; evangelicals and Pentecostals stress extempore prayer; most Christians use both.

Mission 7Evaluate

"Set prayers are more valuable than informal prayer."

  • Reasons for: Jesus gave a set prayer (Matthew 6:9–13); set prayers unite Christians across the world and through history; their words are tested and full of belief; they help when people cannot find words (grief, illness).
  • Reasons against: Jesus warned against empty repeated phrases (Matthew 6:7); informal prayer is personal and sincere; the Spirit helps believers pray (Romans 8:26); Quakers find God in silent, wordless prayer.
  • Another view: both are needed: set prayer shapes informal prayer, and informal prayer brings set words to life; the Lord's Prayer can be a pattern as well as a script.
  • Judge it by: What makes prayer "valuable": sincerity, unity, or faithfulness to Jesus' words? Can set prayers be sincere? Does it depend on the situation?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Liturgical (set order and words), non-liturgical (minister-led, sermon-centred), informal/charismatic (spontaneous, Spirit-led) and private worship; Quakers worship in silence.
  • The Book of Common Prayer (1549, 1662) shaped Anglican liturgical worship.
  • The Bible is used in almost every form of worship; Christians disagree about which form best expresses "spirit and truth" (John 4:24).

Mission 2

  • A sacrament is an outward, visible sign of an inward, spiritual grace.
  • Catholics and Orthodox count seven; most Protestants count two (baptism and the Lord's Supper); Quakers and The Salvation Army use no outward sacraments.
  • Sources: CCC 1210–1211 (seven, in three groups); Article XXV (two "Sacraments of the Gospel").

Mission 3

  • Catholics believe all creation can show God (sacramental reality), and the seven sacraments are visible signs that really give grace.
  • CCC 1210–1211: seven sacraments in three groups: initiation, healing, service of communion; the Eucharist at the centre.
  • Baptism gives new birth and membership of the Church; confirmation seals with the Spirit; the Eucharist completes initiation.

Mission 4

  • Reconciliation (contrition, confession, penance, absolution) and anointing of the sick are sacraments of healing; Catholics believe Christ forgives and strengthens through the priest.
  • Matrimony (the couple are the ministers) and holy orders (deacon, priest, bishop) are sacraments at the service of communion.

Mission 5

  • The Mass: Introductory Rites → Liturgy of the Word → Liturgy of the Eucharist → Concluding Rites.
  • At the consecration Catholics believe Christ becomes really present (transubstantiation), and the one sacrifice of Calvary is made present, not repeated.
  • The Eucharist is the "source and summit" of Christian life (LG 11; LG 7: it makes the Church one body).

Mission 6

  • The last rites (Reconciliation, Anointing, Viaticum) prepare the dying; the funeral follows the body from home to church to cemetery.
  • Holy water, white pall and Paschal candle link death to baptism and proclaim resurrection; the Mass and commendation pray for the dead.
  • Nichols' "Preparing my funeral": worship, prayer for the dead, support for mourners; communion with the deceased and proclamation of eternal life.

Mission 7

  • In Christian teaching, prayer is the raising of the mind and heart to God; types: adoration, confession, thanksgiving, supplication (petition and intercession).
  • The Lord's Prayer (Matthew 6:9–13) is Jesus' model prayer: God's name, Kingdom and will first; then bread, forgiveness and protection.
  • Set (formulaic, traditional) and informal (extempore, spontaneous) prayer are valued differently across denominations.
