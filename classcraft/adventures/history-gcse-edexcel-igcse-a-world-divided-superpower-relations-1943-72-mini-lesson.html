A World Divided

This mini-lesson walks you through the whole Edexcel depth study Superpower Relations 1943-72: the origins of the Cold War, how it developed after 1945, the great crises of Berlin, Hungary, Cuba and Czechoslovakia, and the first steps towards détente.

Setting the scene

Two rival ways of life

The Cold War grew out of a deep ideological clash between the two superpowers that emerged from the Second World War:

  • The USA was capitalist and democratic — private business, free elections, individual freedom.
  • The USSR was communist and a one-party state — state-controlled economy, rule by the Communist Party under Stalin.

Why "Cold" War? The USA and USSR never fought each other directly. Instead they competed through an arms race, propaganda, spying and rival alliances — a state of hostility just short of open war.

Origins · the wartime conferences

Tehran, Yalta and Potsdam

The wartime leaders — the "Big Three" — met three times. Agreement slid into suspicion as the war ended:

  • Tehran, 1943 (Roosevelt, Churchill, Stalin): agreed a second front in France; the USSR would help against Japan; broad agreement, but the USSR gained influence in Eastern Europe.
  • Yalta, February 1945 (Roosevelt, Churchill, Stalin): agreed to divide Germany into zones, set up the United Nations, and hold free elections in liberated Europe.
  • Potsdam, July–August 1945 (Truman, Attlee, Stalin): relations cooled. New leaders, distrust over Poland, and the USA now had the atomic bomb.

Key change: Roosevelt (who trusted Stalin) had died; the tougher Truman replaced him. That shift helps explain why Potsdam was frostier than Yalta.

Origins · the atomic bomb

The bomb changes everything

In August 1945 the USA dropped atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki, ending the war with Japan. It also transformed superpower relations:

  • It gave the USA a huge but temporary lead in weapons.
  • Stalin felt threatened and ordered the USSR to build its own bomb — which it tested in 1949.
  • This launched the arms race that shadowed the whole Cold War.

Misconception check: the atomic bomb did not start direct fighting between the superpowers. It made both sides more cautious — the fear of destruction is exactly why the war stayed "cold".

Developing Cold War 1945–49

Soviet expansion & the Iron Curtain

After 1945 the USSR set up communist governments across the countries of Eastern Europe it had liberated — Poland, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria, Czechoslovakia and more — creating a buffer zone of satellite states.

In March 1946, in a speech at Fulton in the USA, Churchill declared that an "iron curtain" had descended across Europe, dividing the free West from the Soviet-controlled East.

Source awareness: Churchill's phrase was a vivid interpretation, not a neutral fact. He wanted to alarm the West about Soviet power. When you meet the quote in an exam, ask who said it and why.

Developing Cold War · 1947

Containment: Truman Doctrine & Marshall Plan

In 1947 the USA committed to containment — stopping the spread of communism:

  • Truman Doctrine (March 1947): the USA promised money and support to any free country resisting communism (first to Greece and Turkey).
  • Marshall Plan (1947–48): around $13 billion of American aid to help rebuild Western Europe's economies.

Stalin saw the Marshall Plan as "dollar imperialism" and refused it for the East, setting up Cominform and Comecon to bind the satellite states to Moscow.

Misconception check: the Marshall Plan was economic containment. The idea: prosperous countries are far less likely to turn communist. It was money and rebuilding — not troops or invasion.

Developing Cold War · 1948–49

The Berlin Blockade & Airlift

Germany — and its capital Berlin, stranded deep inside the Soviet zone — was split into four occupation zones. In June 1948 Stalin blockaded West Berlin, cutting all road, rail and canal links to try to force the Western powers out.

The West replied with the Berlin Airlift: for almost a year, planes flew in food and fuel — at the peak, one landing roughly every minute. In May 1949 Stalin lifted the blockade, defeated.

Consequence: the crisis split Germany in two — the Federal Republic (West) and the Democratic Republic (East) — and pushed the West to form NATO.

Two rival blocs

NATO and the Warsaw Pact

The crisis hardened Europe into two armed alliances:

  • NATO (1949): a Western military alliance — the USA, Britain, France and others promising to defend each other. An attack on one was an attack on all.
  • Warsaw Pact (1955): the Soviet answer — the USSR and its Eastern European satellites, formed after West Germany joined NATO.

Cold War crises · the arms race

The arms race

From 1949, when the USSR tested its own atomic bomb, both sides raced to build ever more powerful weapons — the H-bomb, long-range missiles and huge stockpiles.

This produced the grim logic of Mutually Assured Destruction (MAD): if either side attacked, both would be destroyed. Terrifying — but it also made both superpowers very cautious about ever fighting directly.

Link back: MAD is the deep reason the Cold War stayed "cold". The weapons were real; the direct war never came.

Cold War crises · 1956

Hungary, 1956

After Stalin's death, Khrushchev spoke of "de-Stalinisation" and peaceful co-existence — raising hopes in the East. In 1956 Hungarians rose up: reformer Imre Nagy promised free elections and to leave the Warsaw Pact.

Khrushchev could not allow a satellite to break away. In November 1956 Soviet tanks rolled into Budapest and crushed the uprising; thousands died and Nagy was later executed.

Source awareness: the West protested loudly but sent no military help — a sign of the limits of "roll-back". Western and Soviet accounts of Hungary read very differently; always weigh the origin of a source.

Cold War crises · 1961

The Berlin Wall, 1961

Through the 1950s, millions of East Germans — many of them skilled workers — fled to the prosperous West through the open border in Berlin. This was a huge embarrassment for the USSR.

In August 1961, on Khrushchev's orders, East Germany sealed the border overnight and built the Berlin Wall, cutting the city in two.

Significance: the Wall stopped the flow of refugees and became the great physical symbol of a divided Europe — the "Iron Curtain" made of concrete. Yet it also, grimly, stabilised the crisis.

Cold War crises · 1962

The Cuban Missile Crisis, 1962

In 1959 Castro led a revolution in Cuba and turned to the USSR. After the failed US-backed Bay of Pigs invasion (1961), Khrushchev placed Soviet nuclear missiles on Cuba — just 90 miles from the USA.

In October 1962 US spy planes found the missiles. President Kennedy ordered a naval "quarantine" (blockade). For thirteen days the world held its breath.

Khrushchev agreed to remove the missiles; in return the USA secretly promised to remove missiles from Turkey and not to invade Cuba.

Misconception check: Cuba brought the world the closest it ever came to nuclear war — but it was resolved by negotiation, not fighting. The scare led directly to the "hotline" and the first arms-control talks.

Cold War crises · 1968

Czechoslovakia & the Prague Spring, 1968

In 1968 the reformer Alexander Dubček tried to build "socialism with a human face" — more freedom of speech and less censorship. This hopeful period was called the Prague Spring.

In August 1968 Warsaw Pact tanks invaded and crushed the reforms. Afterwards the Brezhnev Doctrine declared the USSR's right to intervene in any communist country that strayed from the Soviet line.

Pattern spotting: Hungary 1956 and Czechoslovakia 1968 rhyme — reform in a satellite state, then Soviet tanks. Being able to compare them is exactly the skill Edexcel rewards.

Towards détente · to 1972

The thaw begins

The shock of Cuba pushed both sides towards détente — an easing of tension. Steps to 1972 included:

  • The "hotline" (1963): a direct phone link between Washington and Moscow after Cuba.
  • The Test Ban Treaty (1963): ending nuclear tests in the atmosphere.
  • The Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (1968).
  • SALT 1 (1972): the Strategic Arms Limitation Talks — the first deal to limit nuclear weapons, the high point of this period.

Range check: the topic ends in 1972 with SALT 1. Détente meant reduced tension, not friendship — the rivalry continued, just more cautiously.

Exam skill

Judging sources & interpretations

Edexcel Paper 1 asks you to use and weigh sources. When you meet a source or interpretation, quickly ask:

  • Who produced it, and when? (A Soviet poster and a US newspaper tell the same event very differently.)
  • Why was it made — to inform, to persuade, to reassure?
  • How useful is it for the specific question — and what are its limits?

Golden rule: a source being biased does not make it useless — it can still be very useful as evidence of what a side wanted people to believe. Judge usefulness against the question, not by "bias" alone.
