Mission 1

Mission 1: The Holderness cliffs have been crumbling into the North Sea for centuries. Where does all that material go?

Make a guess: The Holderness cliffs have been crumbling into the North Sea for centuries. Where does all that material go?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.1.3.1 systems concepts applied to coastal landscapes (inputs, outputs, energy, stores, flows, feedback, dynamic equilibrium); landform and landscape
  • 3.1.3.2 sources of energy: winds, constructive and destructive waves, currents, tides; low- and high-energy coasts
  • 3.1.3.2 sediment sources, cells and budgets
Mission 1

The coast as an open system

  • Inputs: energy (waves, tides, wind) and sediment (from rivers, cliffs, the seabed, people).
  • Stores / components: beaches, dunes, cliffs, spits, nearshore bars.
  • Flows / transfers: sediment moved along the shore, onshore and offshore.
  • Outputs: sediment carried out of the stretch, or offshore into deep water.
  • Processes: erosion, transport and deposition (marine); weathering and mass movement (sub-aerial).
Mission 1

Feedback and dynamic equilibrium

  • Dynamic equilibrium: inputs and outputs balance over time, so the form stays roughly stable while sediment keeps moving.
  • Negative feedback restores balance: a storm strips a beach, the sand forms an offshore bar, waves break on the bar and lose energy, and the beach rebuilds.
  • Positive feedback amplifies change: where a beach is washed away, the coast "is left more vulnerable to wave action", so erosion speeds up.
Mission 1

Landform and landscape

  • A landform is a single feature: a cliff, a spit.
  • A landscape is a set of related landforms: on Holderness, retreating till cliffs feed sediment south to Spurn, a spit formed "from deposits moving along the coast".
  • Linked by sediment flows, the landforms make one landscape.
Mission 1

Sources of energy

  • Wind: drives waves; wave energy rises with wind speed, duration and fetch (distance of open water).
  • Waves: the main source of energy at most coasts.
  • Tides: Britain has some of the largest tides in the world, with a range of up to 15 m; tides set where waves attack.
  • Currents: move sediment and deposit it "wherever the current slows down".
Mission 1

Constructive and destructive waves

  • Constructive: low, long wavelength, few per minute; swash stronger than backwash; build beaches.
  • Destructive: high, steep, frequent; backwash stronger than swash; remove sediment.
  • The same beach can face both, with seasons and storms.
Mission 1

High- and low-energy coasts

  • High-energy: exposed, long fetch, frequent destructive waves; erosion exceeds deposition. Holderness faces North Sea waves, which attack its cliffs at high tide.
  • Low-energy: sheltered bays and estuaries, small waves; deposition dominates. On the Essex coast at Tillingham Marshes, saltmarsh "helps to attenuate the wave action".
  • One landscape can contain both.
Quick check
?Which describes a low-energy coast?
Mission 1

Sediment cells

  • A sediment cell is a stretch of coast "within which any changes do not affect adjacent sections".
  • The coast of England and Wales "has been divided into 11 main 'cells'", with sub-cells inside them.
  • Cell 5 runs from Selsey Bill to Portland Bill, in seven sub-cells.
  • Each cell has sources, transfers and sinks. Cells are a model: fine sediment can still cross their boundaries.
Mission 1

Where coastal sediment comes from

  • Terrestrial: rivers (fluvial), cliff erosion, weathering and mass movement on cliffs, wind-blown sand (aeolian), and longshore drift from further along the coast.
  • Offshore: sand and shingle moved onshore from the seabed by waves and tides.
  • Human: beach nourishment, where sand is pumped or dumped onto the coast.
  • Holderness: using data going back to 1786, up to 2 million m³ a year of material was removed from the cliffs.
Mission 1

A human source: the Sand Motor

  • In 2011 the Netherlands built the Sand Motor, a peninsula of 21.5 million m³ of sand on its Delfland coast.
  • "Wind, waves and currents gradually distribute the sand along the coast".
  • It is expected to remain until 2041: a human input that the natural system spreads.
Quick check
?Holderness's cliffs have supplied up to 2 million m³ a year. The Sand Motor held 21.5 million m³. About how many years of Holderness's maximum supply is that?
Mission 1

Sediment budgets

  • Sediment budget = inputs − outputs for a stretch of coast or a cell.
  • Positive budget (surplus): beaches grow, landforms such as spits build out.
  • Negative budget (deficit): beaches thin, so more wave energy reaches the cliffs.
  • Holderness's cliff material is a large input to the cell; much of it moves on, south towards Spurn.
Quick check
?A new harbour wall traps longshore drift on its updrift side. What happens to the sediment budget just downdrift?
Mission check

?A stretch of soft cliffs erodes fast, yet the beach at their foot stays thin. Further along the coast, a spit keeps growing. What best explains this?

Holderness works the same way. Its till cliffs are a sediment source: the fine clay leaves in suspension, and sand and shingle drift south along the coast towards Spurn. Because the debris is carried away rather than building a protective beach, the cliffs keep retreating: 2.16 m a year at Aldbrough in 1951–2004.

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.1.3.2 geomorphological processes: weathering, mass movement, erosion, transportation, deposition
  • 3.1.3.2 marine erosion (hydraulic action, wave quarrying, corrasion/abrasion, cavitation, solution, attrition); transportation (traction, suspension, longshore/littoral drift); deposition; sub-aerial weathering, mass movement and runoff
Mission 2

Two sets of processes

  • Marine processes work at the cliff foot: erosion, transportation, deposition by waves, tides and currents.
  • Sub-aerial processes work on the cliff face and top: weathering, mass movement and runoff.
  • A cliff retreats fast when both are strong and the debris is removed.
Mission 2

Marine erosion: the force of water

  • Hydraulic action: the force of a breaking wave, and air compressed into cracks, breaks rock apart.
  • Wave quarrying: hydraulic action prises out and removes whole blocks loosened along joints and bedding planes.
  • Cavitation: air bubbles in turbulent water collapse, sending tiny shock waves that weaken rock surfaces.
Mission 2

Marine erosion: the load and the chemistry

  • Corrasion / abrasion: waves hurl sand and pebbles at the cliff, wearing it away.
  • Attrition: pebbles knock together, becoming smaller and rounder.
  • Solution (corrosion): seawater dissolves soluble rock, such as chalk and limestone.
  • How much each matters depends on wave energy and lithology (rock type).
Quick check
?Storm waves throw shingle at the base of a cliff, scouring a notch. Which process is this?
Mission 2

Lithology: weak till and hard chalk

  • Holderness cliffs are glacial till (boulder clay), mostly late Devensian (18,000–13,000 years old).
  • At Aldbrough they are about 20 m high; at high tide, waves erode their lower and sometimes middle sections.
  • At Flamborough Head, to the north, the cliffs are chalk capped by till; the chalk "is more resistant to erosion than the overlying till".
Mission 2

Transportation

  • Traction: large pebbles roll along the sea bed.
  • Saltation: smaller particles bounce along it.
  • Suspension: fine silt and clay are carried in the water. Holderness till is rich in clay, so much of it leaves in suspension.
  • Solution: dissolved material moves with the water.
Mission 2

Longshore drift and deposition

  • Longshore (littoral) drift: waves arrive at an angle; swash carries sediment up the beach at that angle, backwash drags it straight down, so it zig-zags along the coast.
  • Deposition happens where energy falls: in sheltered water, or where currents slow. Currents deposit sand "wherever the current slows down".
  • Spurn, south of Holderness, formed "from deposits moving along the coast carried by currents".
Quick check
?On a coast where waves arrive from the north-east, which way does longshore drift move sediment along a north–south shoreline?
Mission 2

Sub-aerial weathering

  • Mechanical: freeze–thaw in cracks; salt crystals growing in pores; repeated wetting and drying of clay.
  • Chemical: rainwater (a weak acid) dissolves chalk and limestone; clay minerals break down.
  • Biological: roots widen cracks; burrowing animals loosen soil.
  • Weathering weakens the cliff face so that waves and gravity remove it more easily.
Mission 2

Mass movement and runoff

  • At Aldbrough, the main mode is rotational slumping, with smaller topples and falls, and short-lived mudflows.
  • Runoff: rain that cannot soak in flows down the cliff face, cutting gullies and washing loose till to the foot.
  • Rain that does soak in makes till heavier and weaker; for the till capping Flamborough Head, BGS warns that increased rainfall "could increase the sensitivity of the till deposits to destabilisation".
  • Slumps dump debris at the cliff foot, where waves remove it.
Quick check
?BGS measured retreat at Aldbrough of 2.16 m a year from 1951 to 2004. About how far did the cliff retreat in that period?
Mission 2

Marine and sub-aerial together

  • The cycle: waves erode the cliff foot at high tide → the face slumps → waves remove the debris → the cliff is steep again.
  • Retreat at Aldbrough averaged 1.16 m a year in 1852–1951 and 2.16 m a year in 1951–2004.
  • Up to 2 million m³ a year has been removed from the Holderness cliffs: little stays to protect the cliff foot.
  • Exam move: for "explain", link one marine and one sub-aerial process, then say why the debris does not protect the cliff.
Mission check

?After a very wet winter, a large section of a clay cliff on a stormy coast slumps in one event. Which explanation is best?

Aldbrough's cliffs fail the same way. Soft till is eroded by waves at high tide, slumps from above when wet, and the debris is carried away: 2.16 m of retreat a year in 1951–2004, while chalk at Flamborough resists.

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.1.3.3 erosional landforms (cliffs, wave-cut platforms, caves, arches, stacks); factors and processes in their development
  • 3.1.3.3 relationship between process, time, landforms and landscapes in coastal settings
Mission 3

How waves erode rock

  • Hydraulic action: breaking waves force water and compressed air into cracks; the pressure prises rock apart.
  • Abrasion (corrasion): waves hurl sand and pebbles at the cliff.
  • Attrition: the pebbles themselves knock together and become smaller and rounder.
  • Solution (corrosion): sea water dissolves calcium carbonate in rocks such as chalk and limestone.
  • Most erosion happens in storms, at high tide, when large waves reach the cliff foot.
Mission 3

Why some coasts erode faster

  • Lithology: Holderness's cliffs are glacial till, weak, unconsolidated sediment left by ice sheets, mostly 18,000–13,000 years old. At Flamborough Head the chalk "is more resistant to erosion than the overlying till".
  • Structure: joints, faults and bedding planes are lines of weakness that waves exploit.
  • Wave energy: fetch, wind speed and duration; exposed coasts receive more energy.
  • Subaerial processes: weathering and mass movement attack the cliff face from above; the sea removes the debris.
Mission 3

Headlands and bays

  • On a discordant coast, bands of rock run at right angles to the shore. Weaker rock is cut back into bays; resistant rock is left as headlands.
  • On a concordant coast, rock bands run parallel to the shore, so the outer band protects the rock behind it.
  • Purbeck, Dorset, has both. The southern cliffs at Durlston are concordant; to the east, "the variation in erosion resistance has caused the formation of Durlston Head, Swanage Bay, Ballard Point and Studland Bay".
  • Ballard Down, north of Swanage Bay, is a headland of chalk.
  • Once formed, headlands take more wave energy while bays become more sheltered: wave crests slow and bend in the shallower water off a headland (refraction), so energy converges on it.
Quick check
?Swanage Bay lies between a chalk headland (Ballard Down) and a limestone headland (Durlston Head). What best explains the bay?
Mission 3

Cliffs and wave-cut platforms

  • Waves erode a wave-cut notch between high- and low-tide levels by hydraulic action and abrasion.
  • The overhang above loses support and collapses; the cliff retreats and stays steep.
  • The cliff leaves behind a gently sloping rock surface, exposed at low tide: a wave-cut platform.
Mission 3

Platforms over time: negative feedback

  • As a shore platform widens, waves cross more of it and lose energy before reaching the cliff: wave dissipation increases.
  • So, all else equal, cliff retreat slows as the platform grows: negative feedback.
  • But if the whole platform is lowered at a similar rate, the profile keeps its shape and moves landward.
  • Lowering can be fast in weak rock: up to 3 m in one year (1992–93) on part of the Holderness shore platform.
Quick check
?A shore platform in front of a hard-rock cliff keeps widening while the platform surface stays at the same level. What will most likely happen to the rate of cliff retreat?
Mission 3

Cave, arch, stack, stump

  • Waves attack a line of weakness in a headland (a joint or fault): a crack widens into a cave.
  • Caves on both sides of a headland meet, or one cuts right through: an arch.
  • The arch roof is weathered from above and undercut from below until it collapses, leaving a stack.
  • The stack is undercut at its base and topples or is worn down to a stump, covered at high tide.
  • Ballard Down ends in "towering sea stacks and cliffs topped by chalk downland": Old Harry Rocks.
Mission 3

Beyond the UK: Port Campbell, Victoria

  • On the Port Campbell coast of Victoria, Australia, "limestone, wind and waves have shaped the shoreline into lookouts, sea caves, arches and offshore formations".
  • London Bridge was a natural arch joined to the mainland. It "collapsed in 1990"; two visitors were left on the new island until rescued by helicopter.
  • Nearby, the Twelve Apostles are limestone stacks. On 3 July 2005 one of the tallest, about 45 m high, collapsed, leaving seven.
  • The same sequence runs as in Dorset: lithology and structure decide where, wave energy decides how fast.
Mission 3

Process, time and landscape

  • Seconds to hours: a single collapse turns an arch into a stack (London Bridge, 1990).
  • Years to decades: Holderness's till cliffs retreat on average 1–4.5 m a year.
  • Centuries to millennia: over the last 1,000 years the Holderness coast has retreated about 2 km, with the loss of 26 villages listed in the Domesday survey of 1086.
  • A landscape is the assemblage these processes build over time: bays, headlands, platforms and stacks together.
  • Exam move (AQA): link each landform to its process and its timescale; say what controls the rate.
Quick check
?Which order shows the erosion of a headland from the earliest to the latest stage?
Mission check

?Old Harry Rocks stand in the sea off the end of Ballard Down, a chalk headland in Dorset. How did the sea cut them off from the land?

The sea makes islands by exploiting weakness: crack → cave → arch → stack → stump. London Bridge became an island when its arch fell in 1990; Old Harry Rocks are the same process in chalk.

Mission 4

Where deposition happens

  • Waves and currents drop sediment when they lose energy: in sheltered bays, behind headlands, where the coast changes direction, or where water deepens or spreads out.
  • Constructive waves (low, long, with a strong swash) build beaches; destructive waves (high, steep, with a strong backwash) remove material.
  • Sediment comes from cliffs, rivers and the sea bed. Holderness's eroding cliffs have supplied up to 2 million m³ a year.
Mission 4

Beaches

  • A beach is a store of sand or shingle between low tide and the highest reach of storm waves.
  • Sand beaches are gently sloping: little water soaks in, so a strong backwash drags sand seaward. Shingle beaches are steeper: water drains through the pebbles, so the backwash is weak and pebbles are left at the top.
  • Features: berms (ridges at successive high-tide levels), a storm beach at the top, cusps and ridges and runnels.
  • Beaches can be graded. Along Chesil Beach, Dorset, size varies from "pea"-sized shingle at West Bay to cobbles at Chiswell.
Mission 4

Simple spits

  • Waves arrive at an angle, so longshore (littoral) drift carries sediment along the coast.
  • Where the coast turns (often at an estuary), drift carries on in a straight line and deposits sediment into deeper water: a spit grows out, attached at one end.
  • Changing wave directions and currents curve the end into a recurve (hook). Sheltered water behind the spit becomes mudflat and salt marsh.
  • Spurn "formed as a long, narrow spit from deposits moving along the coast carried by currents"; it is over three miles long but as little as 50 m wide.
Mission 4

Compound spits: Hurst Spit

  • A compound spit has several recurves, each marking an earlier end of the spit.
  • Hurst Spit, Hampshire, is about 3.5 km of shingle across the entrance to the Western Solent.
  • Its growth "appears to have been episodic, or phased, as indicated by the three main 'fossil' recurves"; each "must represent a stage of temporary equilibrium between sediment supply and loss".
  • Low wave energy in its lee "promoted mudflat and salt-marsh accretion".
Quick check
?Why does a spit grow out across the mouth of an estuary rather than following the coast round into it?
Mission 4

Bars and barrier beaches

  • Classified by attachment: barrier islands (no ends attached), spits (one end) and barrier beaches (both ends).
  • A bar or barrier beach joins two headlands and encloses a lagoon. Slapton Sands, Devon, is a 5.6 km shingle barrier beach enclosing the freshwater lagoon Slapton Ley.
  • Much of Slapton's shingle was probably eroded from older coastlines drowned by rapid sea-level rise in the Holocene.
  • Offshore bars form where waves cross a shallow sea bed and break, dropping sediment before reaching the shore.
Mission 4

Tombolos: Chesil Beach

  • A tombolo is a beach or bar that joins an island to the mainland.
  • Chesil Beach, Dorset, is a large (28 km long) shingle bar that reaches the Isle of Portland: the Royal Geographical Society describes it as the UK's largest tombolo.
  • The accepted view is that Chesil formed as an offshore bar and migrated onshore during the rapid Holocene sea-level rise, gathering gravel from the floor of Lyme Bay.
  • Behind it lies the Fleet, a lagoon "extremely sheltered from wave action".
Mission 4

Barrier islands beyond the UK: the Outer Banks

  • Barrier islands are long sand islands parallel to the coast, separated from the mainland by lagoons; they are "often associated with increases in sea level following periods of glaciation".
  • North Carolina's Outer Banks are a barrier-island chain. On 18 September 2003, Hurricane Isabel opened a breach about 500 m wide that "completely severed the island southwest of Cape Hatteras".
  • Storm overwash carries sand across the island. The US National Park Service notes that stopping overwash threatens "the survival of the barrier island to evolve with sea level rise".
  • So barrier islands survive by moving landward.
Quick check
?A shingle ridge is attached to the coast at both ends and encloses a freshwater lake behind it. What is it?
Mission 4

Salt marshes

  • Salt marshes form in sheltered intertidal areas (behind spits, in estuaries) where mud settles from slack water.
  • Pioneer plants such as glasswort colonise mudflats "in areas protected from strong wave action"; they are "an important precursor to the development of more stable saltmarsh vegetation".
  • Plants trap more mud, the surface rises, and higher marsh develops where tides flood "with decreasing frequency and duration". This succession is a halosere.
  • Salt marsh "helps to attenuate the wave action, forming a natural defence against the sea".
Mission 4

Sand dunes

  • Onshore winds blow dry sand from wide beaches; it collects behind obstacles.
  • Embryo dunes form at the top of the beach, where pioneer plants trap sand.
  • Yellow (white) dunes: marram grass binds sand and grows up through it. Grey dunes: more stable, with more organic matter and grassland.
  • Dune slacks: damp hollows between dune ridges. Dunes need a continued supply of sand from the beach.
Mission 4

Process, time and supply

  • Depositional landforms last only as long as their sediment supply.
  • Hurst Spit is moving landward by overwash. Its rate of retreat rose from about 1.5 m a year (1867–1968) to 3.5 m a year (1968–1982).
  • One cause: groynes and defences built updrift since the 1940s at Bournemouth and Christchurch stopped cliff erosion, so less shingle drifted onto the spit.
  • Chesil Beach now receives almost no new shingle, so it is "a relict feature that will continue to decline in volume".
Quick check
?Engineers stop the cliffs updrift of a spit from eroding. What is the most likely effect on the spit over the following decades?
Mission check

?Hurst Spit stretches across the entrance to the Western Solent, with three old hooked ends along its inner side. How did it form?

Spurn and Hurst are spits: sediment drifting along the coast is deposited where the coastline turns, and recurves form at the end. Both depend on a supply of sediment from eroding cliffs updrift.

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.1.3.3 estuarine mudflat and saltmarsh environments and landscapes; factors and processes in their development
Mission 5

Estuaries: where rivers meet the tide

  • An estuary is the downstream part of a river valley, subject to the tide, with a salinity gradient from fresh to marine water.
  • River sediment, shelter from waves and low current flows produce extensive intertidal flats.
  • Most UK estuaries by area are coastal plain estuaries: valleys flooded at the end of the last glaciation.
Mission 5

Mudflats

  • Intertidal flats grade from clean sands (exposed, high energy) to muds (sheltered, low energy).
  • Mudflats "form in the most sheltered areas of the coast, usually where large quantities of silt derived from rivers are deposited in estuaries".
  • Rich in worms, bivalves and mud-snails, they feed waders and wildfowl such as knot and dunlin.
Mission 5

Processes: how fine sediment settles

  • At slack water (high and low tide) currents almost stop, so fine particles can settle.
  • Fine particles flocculate (clump into flocs) and settle faster; this depends on suspended concentration and organic content.
  • Biofilms on the mud enhance flocculation.
  • Once plants grow, they attenuate flow and promote deposition from suspension.
  • Saltmarsh "helps to attenuate the wave action, forming a natural defence against the sea" (Spurn, Humber).
Quick check
?Why do mudflats form in the inner Humber or Severn but not on an exposed Atlantic beach?
Mission 5

The halosere begins: pioneers

  • A halosere is a succession that begins in salt water.
  • Pioneers: glasswort Salicornia and annual seablite colonise mud "in areas protected from strong wave action".
  • They live at the lower marsh, "frequently flooded by the tide": halophytes (salt-tolerant plants).
  • They are "an important precursor to the development of more stable saltmarsh vegetation".
  • Plants slow the water; more sediment settles; the surface rises.
Mission 5

Cord-grass: a human twist

  • The UK's only native cord-grass is Spartina maritima.
  • S. alterniflora arrived accidentally in the 1820s in ships' ballast; crossing produced a fertile hybrid, common cord-grass S. anglica .
  • S. anglica was "extensively planted … as an aid to stabilisation of intertidal mudflats" and spread rapidly.
  • Dense swards have "little intrinsic value to wildlife" and can take over mudflats where waders feed.
Mission 5

Middle and upper marsh

  • Salt meadows form the middle and upper marsh, "where tidal inundation still occurs but with decreasing frequency and duration".
  • Low marsh: open common saltmarsh-grass Puccinellia; higher up: more herbs and more species.
  • Grazing by livestock shapes the upper marsh.
  • Carmarthen Bay (south Wales) shows "a complete sequence … from pioneer vegetation through to upper saltmarsh transitions".
Quick check
?Why do species change from the seaward edge to the upper marsh?
Mission 5

Factors at different scales

  • Sediment supply: pioneer saltmarsh is scarce in Scotland and Northern Ireland "because of a lack of new sediment".
  • Shelter: UK salt meadows (over 29,000 ha) lie mostly in "large, sheltered estuaries" of England and south Wales.
  • Beyond the UK: the Wadden Sea (Netherlands, Germany, Denmark) is "the largest unbroken system of intertidal sand and mud flats in the world"; its salt marshes host about 2,300 species.
  • Worldwide, 5.5 million ha of saltmarsh have been mapped across 43 countries.
Mission check

?Two sites are breached to let the sea in for new saltmarsh. Site X: a sheltered estuary with a muddy river. Site Y: a sea loch with clear water and little sediment. After ten years, what is most likely?

Saltmarsh grows where sheltered, sediment-rich water lets mud flocculate and settle; pioneers trap more mud, the surface rises, flooding becomes less frequent, and the halosere moves on to grazed upper marsh.

Mission 6

Mission 6: On the coast of the Gulf of Bothnia, the land rises out of the sea by about 0.9 m a century. Elsewhere, coasts are drowning. How can both be true?

Make a guess: On the coast of the Gulf of Bothnia, the land rises out of the sea by about 0.9 m a century. Elsewhere, coasts are drowning. How can both be true?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.1.3.3 eustatic, isostatic and tectonic sea-level change; major changes over the last 10,000 years
  • 3.1.3.3 coastlines of emergence and submergence: raised beaches, marine platforms, rias, fjords, Dalmatian coasts
  • 3.1.3.3 recent and predicted climate change and its potential impact on coasts
Mission 6

Eustatic change: the sea moves

  • Eustatic change: a worldwide change in sea level as the volume of ocean water (or the shape of ocean basins) changes.
  • Cold periods: water is locked in ice sheets on land, so sea level falls (glacio-eustatic fall). Warm periods: ice melts and water expands, so it rises.
  • Global mean sea level rose 0.20 m between 1901 and 2018; the rate rose from 1.3 mm a year (1901–71) to 3.7 mm a year (2006–18).
  • Thermal expansion explained 50% of the rise in 1971–2018.
Mission 6

Isostatic change: the land moves

  • Isostatic change: vertical movement of the land. An ice sheet's weight presses the crust down; when it melts, the land slowly rises (isostatic recovery, or rebound).
  • On the High Coast (Sweden) and Kvarken (Finland), about 290 m of uplift has been recorded over the past 10,500 years.
  • The land still rises by about 0.9 m a century, and new islands keep emerging.
  • Land can also sink: beyond the old ice margin, and where thick sediments compact.
Mission 6

Tectonic change

  • Plate movement can raise or lower a coast, gradually or in seconds.
  • On 13 November 2016 a magnitude 7.8 earthquake lifted the seabed by 0.5–2 m along 20 km of the Kaikōura coast, New Zealand, and by 5.5 m in one area.
  • Rocks, seaweed and sea creatures were lifted above the water line; GNS geologist Kelvin Berryman described the newly exposed "uplifted platform".
  • In 1931 the Hawke's Bay earthquake raised land around Napier by 1–2 m; a former estuary now holds Napier's airport.
Quick check
?Which is an example of isostatic change?
Quick check
?The Gulf of Bothnia coast rises by about 0.9 m a century. Suppose sea level there rose at the global rate of 3.7 mm a year. By how much does sea level change relative to the land each year?
Mission 6

The last 10,000 years

  • After the last glaciation, melting ice sheets raised sea level fast, then the rise slowed: the Holocene transgression.
  • In Start Bay, Devon, relative sea level is thought to have risen by 18 m in the past 9,000 years, with "a notable deceleration of the rate of rise during the past 5000 years".
  • Near the Solent, sea level "approached its present position" 4,000–5,000 years ago.
  • Meanwhile isostatic recovery continued where the ice had been thickest: hence about 290 m of uplift on the High Coast.
  • Off Victoria, Australia, researchers found drowned stacks 50 m under water, beside a submerged cliff about 6 km off the Twelve Apostles: sea level rose so fast that "the stacks were simply swamped in place".
Mission 6

Coasts of emergence

  • Where the land rises faster than the sea (or the sea falls), old shorelines are left high and dry.
  • Raised beach: a former beach (rounded pebbles, shells) now above the reach of the sea, often backed by a relict cliff that is no longer undercut and weathers to a gentler slope.
  • Marine platform (marine terrace): a raised shore platform. Kaikōura's "uplifted platform" was created in one earthquake.
  • At Arbroath, Scotland, the Needle E'e arch, "originally formed through coastal erosion", now lies "several metres above current sea level".
Quick check
?Which evidence would best show that a coast has emerged?
Mission 6

Coasts of submergence: rias and fjords

  • Where the sea rises faster than the land, it floods low ground.
  • Rias are drowned river valleys: V-shaped in cross-section, deepening seaward, with branching creeks. JNCC: "Rias are drowned river valleys, characteristically found in south-west Britain". The Fal and Helford, Cornwall, form a "ria system".
  • Fjords are glacial troughs drowned by the sea: a fjord forms "by submergence of a glaciated valley".
  • West Norwegian fjords: walls rise up to 1,400 m and extend 500 m below sea level, with deep basins and a shallower rock threshold near the mouth.
Mission 6

Dalmatian coasts

  • A Dalmatian coast forms where ridges and valleys run parallel to the coast and rising sea level drowns the valleys.
  • Croatia's coast: ridges and valleys aligned north-west to south-east; drowning left the hill tops "above modern sea level as an archipelago of some 1200 islands and islets".
  • Ridges (anticlines) became islands; valleys (synclines) became channels and bays. This coast "has given its name to this type of coast".
  • Contrast: rias drown valleys at right angles to the coast.
Quick check
?Why is a fjord usually deepest inland and shallower at its mouth?
Mission 6

Ria or fjord?

  • Ria: river valley; V-shaped; gets deeper and wider towards the sea; unglaciated (southern Britain).
  • Fjord: glacial trough; U-shaped; steep walls; deepest inland; threshold at the mouth; glaciated uplands (Norway, western Scotland).
  • Dalmatian: valleys parallel to the coast, drowned to leave long islands.
  • Exam move: name the cross-section shape and the long profile; they identify the origin.
Mission 6

Present and future change

  • IPCC: global sea level is likely to rise by 0.28–0.55 m by 2100 under very low emissions, and 0.63–1.01 m under very high emissions (relative to 1995–2014).
  • Emergent coasts can switch: where uplift is slower than future sea-level rise, raised features stay raised but the shoreline starts to retreat.
  • Low, soft coasts face more flooding and breaching: modelled UKCP18 scenarios suggest sea-level rise could completely cut off Spurn Head by 2050.
  • Barriers such as Slapton try to roll landward; where a road blocks them (the A379), the beach steepens instead.
Mission 6

Sea-level change as a risk

  • Global warming: slow, continuous rise; risk grows each decade for low, soft coasts and estuaries.
  • Tectonics: sudden. One earthquake at Kaikōura in 2016 lifted rocks, seaweed and sea creatures above the water line; subsidence in an earthquake can drop a coast below high tide.
  • Relative change is what matters locally: eustatic rise plus any sinking, minus any uplift.
  • Exam move: separate the cause (warming or tectonics), the speed and the place, then judge the risk.
Mission check

?At Arbroath, Scotland, a wave-cut arch stands several metres above today's sea. In Cornwall, river valleys are flooded by the sea far inland. How can both be true in Britain today?

Relative sea level = eustatic change in the sea ± isostatic (or tectonic) movement of the land. Where uplift wins, coasts emerge (raised arches and beaches; the Gulf of Bothnia); where the sea wins, they submerge (rias, fjords).

Mission 7

Mission 7: By 2014 the Environment Agency had built new banks inland at Medmerry, West Sussex, and let the sea flood the land between. Why give land to the sea?

Make a guess: By 2014 the Environment Agency had built new banks inland at Medmerry, West Sussex, and let the sea flood the land between. Why give land to the sea?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.1.3.4 human intervention at the coast; traditional hard and soft engineering for flood and erosion risk
  • 3.1.3.4 sustainable approaches: shoreline management plans and integrated coastal zone management
Mission 7

Why intervene?

  • Coasts are managed to reduce two risks: erosion (land lost) and flooding (land submerged, often in storm surges).
  • Holderness, East Yorkshire: over the last 1,000 years the coast has retreated about 2 km, with the loss of 26 villages listed in the Domesday survey.
  • Every intervention changes the flows of energy and sediment, so it also changes other places along the coast.
Mission 7

Hard engineering

  • Sea wall: concrete wall at the back of the beach; reflects wave energy. Reflected waves can scour the beach in front.
  • Revetment: sloping structure that absorbs some wave energy and protects the cliff or bank behind.
  • Rip rap (rock armour): large boulders at the cliff foot that break waves up.
  • Groynes: walls across the beach that trap littoral drift and widen the beach updrift, but starve beaches downdrift.
  • Offshore breakwaters: barriers offshore that make waves break before reaching the shore; sediment collects behind them.
Mission 7

Soft engineering

  • Beach nourishment: adding sand or shingle to a beach. On the Lincolnshire coast, the Lincshore scheme has nourished beaches since 1994, adding about 350,000 m³ a year from Mablethorpe to Skegness.
  • Cliff regrading and drainage: lower the slope angle and drain water out to reduce landslides. It matters most where landslides threaten towns: Ventnor, Isle of Wight, "sits at the bottom of the largest urbanised landslide complex in north-west Europe".
  • Dune stabilisation: fencing, boardwalks and planting marram to trap and hold sand.
  • Managed realignment: let the sea in behind a new, landward defence.
  • They work with natural processes, but most need repeating.
Quick check
?Which strategy works with natural processes rather than against them?
Mission 7

Sediment cells and Shoreline Management Plans

  • A sediment cell (littoral cell) is a stretch of coast within which sediment largely circulates; sub-cells divide it further.
  • Shoreline Management Plans (SMPs) plan how flood and erosion risk will be managed around England to 2105; there are 20 SMPs for the English coast.
  • SMP areas' boundaries "have been set based on analysis of coastal processes".
  • Each stretch gets one of four approaches: hold the line, advance the line, managed realignment, no active intervention.
  • Why cells matter: "defending one place from erosion can interrupt the movement of sediment down the coast, starving other places of material for beaches".
Mission 7

Case study: Mappleton, Holderness

  • Holderness's till cliffs retreat on average 1–4.5 m a year. Net drift is southward, about 200,000–350,000 m³ a year.
  • Strategy: in 1991, two rock groynes and a rock revetment were built "to prevent cliff recession" at Mappleton, with a design life of 50 years.
  • Intended effects: hold the cliff line in front of the village; trap drift to build a beach that absorbs wave energy.
  • The SMP keeps hold the line at Mappleton, though it finds only a "marginal economic case" for doing so.
Mission 7

Effects on the sediment budget and landforms

  • Mappleton is becoming a headland: undefended cliffs either side keep retreating, so the defended stretch sticks out.
  • Sediment budget: holding the line means "some interruption to the sediment supplied to downdrift coastlines", and beach narrowing is possible.
  • Landforms: the SMP expects "beach and platform lowering in front of the coastal defences" and a bay developing between Hornsea and Mappleton.
  • South of Mappleton, shore-platform lowering of up to 3.0 m in a year was measured in 1992–93, an extreme value.
  • Exam move: go beyond the choice of strategy; trace the effect on inputs, stores and outputs of sediment, then on landforms and the wider landscape.
Quick check
?Groynes are built on a cliffed coast where littoral drift runs south. What is the most likely effect on the coast to the south of them?
Mission 7

Cost-benefit analysis and EIA

  • Cost-benefit analysis (CBA) compares the costs of each option with the value of what it protects over the scheme's life, as present values (future sums discounted).
  • Medmerry (2007 estimates, present value): no active intervention £8–10 million; hold the line £80 million; managed realignment £10 million.
  • At Mappleton, CBA gave only a "marginal economic case" for holding the line.
  • Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) predicts a scheme's effects on habitats, landscape, water and people before it is approved. At Mappleton the SMP's assessment warned of "beach narrowing and steepening" and harm to seabed habitats.
Mission 7

Medmerry: managed realignment

  • A shingle bank west of Selsey protected 300 properties, large caravan sites and 650 ha. It had been breached many times in 15 years and needed extensive winter repairs.
  • Holding the line was judged "not practical"; inland banks "will not need to be as large", though "a relatively large area of land" would flood.
  • The government also had to create new intertidal habitat to compensate for losses elsewhere.
  • Completed in 2014 with 7 km of new embankments, protecting key infrastructure for Selsey. At the time, the £28 million scheme was "the largest open-coast scheme in Europe".
Mission 7

ICZM: managing a whole coast

  • Integrated coastal zone management (ICZM) plans a whole coast (ideally a whole sediment cell) across all its uses: settlement, industry, farming, tourism, conservation.
  • The Flamborough Head to Gibraltar Point SMP shows this. Holderness's eroding cliffs supply sand and gravel to Spurn Head and fine sand to Lincolnshire: "The continuation of erosion along the Holderness coast is crucial for the downdrift frontages".
  • So it holds the line at Bridlington, Hornsea, Mappleton, Withernsea and the Easington gas terminals (hard), lets the cliffs between keep eroding, and nourishes Lincolnshire's beaches (soft).
Quick check
?Why might hard defences at one village be judged unsustainable in the long term?
Mission check

?The plan for Holderness defends its towns but lets the cliffs between them keep eroding, even though farms and some homes are lost. Why let that land go?

Giving land to the sea can be the sustainable choice: at Medmerry, realignment was far cheaper than holding a failing bank and created habitat; at Holderness, letting cliffs erode keeps sediment flowing along the cell. Hard defences protect a point but change the coast around it.

Mission 8

Mission 8: A clay cliff can barely move one year, then lose metres the next. How do you find its real rate of retreat?

Make a guess: A clay cliff can barely move one year, then lose metres the next. How do you find its real rate of retreat?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.1.3.6 case study of local-scale coastal environment(s): fundamental coastal processes, landscape outcomes, engaging with field data, and challenges of sustainable management
Mission 8

The place: Holderness, Hornsea to Aldbrough

  • East Yorkshire coast between Flamborough Head and Spurn; our local stretch runs from Hornsea south past Mappleton to Aldbrough.
  • Cliffs of glacial till, mostly late Devensian (18,000–13,000 years old).
  • Over the last 1,000 years the coast has retreated about 2 km, losing 26 villages listed in the Domesday survey.
Mission 8

Processes

  • At high tides, waves erode the lower and middle cliff.
  • Mass movement: mainly rotational slumping; also topples and falls; minor mudflows.
  • Longshore drift carries eroded sediment south: about 200,000–350,000 m³ a year, wave-driven.
  • Cliff-foot erosion happens when tide level plus wave run-up exceeds the cliff–beach junction, above all in storms with extreme tides.
  • The SMP judges that wave energy at the cliff foot, set by beach level and platform lowering, controls the retreat rate more than differences in till strength.
Mission 8

Landforms

  • A soft till cliff line, a thin veneer of sand beach and a broad shore platform.
  • Defended towns act as hard points; between them the cliffs retreat into shallow bays.
  • A bay is forming between Hornsea and Mappleton; Mappleton is emerging as a headland.
Quick check
?The cliff materials vary along the coast, yet the long-term retreat rate is "remarkably constant". What did the SMP analysis conclude mainly controls the rate?
Mission 8

Field data 1: erosion posts

  • Since 1951, the councils have measured cliff retreat every year.
  • 120 marker posts at 500 m intervals from Sewerby to Spurn Head; each year, measure post → cliff top (the lip of the latest failure scar).
  • Posts are moved inland when the cliff gets too close.
  • Now also GPS surveys of the whole cliff line, Lidar and aerial surveys.
Mission 8

Field data 2: what the posts show

  • Long-term rate about 1.8 m a year; council post data give 1.5–2 m a year.
  • But single years range from 0 to 14.01 m (1951–2004).
  • Rolston to Mappleton (posts 47–51): average 1.6–2.6 m a year, and up to 20 m in a single year.
  • At Aldbrough: 1.16 m a year from old maps (1852–1951), 2.16 m a year from surveys (1951–2004).
Quick check
?At Aldbrough, cliff surveys gave an average retreat of 2.16 m a year from 1951 to 2004. About how far did the cliff retreat over that period?
Mission 8

Field data 3: beaches and platforms

  • Beach profiles change with the seasons: 2–2.5 m between summer and winter (1983); up to 3 m at Cowden (1982–86); 1–2 m (1992–93).
  • Since the late 1990s the council has monitored beach profiles along Holderness.
  • South of Mappleton, maximum shore-platform lowering in 1992–93 reached 3.0 m in a year, 1,326 m from the defences.
  • The SMP calls such rates "extreme"; a realistic long-term lowering is about 0.02 m a year (retreat 2 m a year × gradient 1 in 100).
Mission 8

Why the record jumps

  • Recession peaks recur about every 6.5 years.
  • North-east waves strip sand from the south side of a slump, exposing the next cliff; failure "tends to migrate southwards".
  • So one spot can swing from 0 to over 10 m a year on a 5–8-year cycle.
  • Field lesson: a short record can mislead; use long series and many posts.
Quick check
?A student measures one Holderness post for two years and finds 0.2 m of retreat a year. Which conclusion is best?
Mission 8

Sustainable management: Mappleton

  • 1991: two rock groynes and a rock revetment, design life 50 years.
  • Around Mappleton (posts 51–52), cliff retreat since 1991 reached up to 2.1 m a year.
  • Holding the line gives only a "marginal economic case" and interrupts sediment downdrift.
  • The SMP: "The continuation of erosion along the Holderness coast is crucial for the downdrift frontages".
Mission 8

When field data disagree

  • Cliff retreat 2 km north and south of Hornsea's defences:
  • 1993 study: north 0.38, south 2.81 m a year: difference 2.43.
  • Council post data (2008): north 1.04, south 2.45: difference 1.41.
  • Both show faster retreat downdrift, but they disagree on reach: IECS (1991, 1994) found accelerated erosion at least 10 km south of the groynes; the council concludes about 1–2 km.
  • Exam move (AQA "Assess"): weigh methods and periods before judging a defence's impact.
Mission check

?A caravan park sits on a till cliff 1 km south (downdrift) of a defended town, on a coast like Holderness. Which record gives the best estimate of its future retreat rate?

Holderness shows that field data must be long and local: posts every 500 m since 1951 reveal a steady average (about 1.8 m a year) hidden inside wild yearly swings, and faster retreat just downdrift of defences. Management must weigh local protection against the sediment that downdrift beaches need.

Mission 9: The sea is wrecking the rock towers that millions of visitors come to see. Should anyone try to stop it?

Make a guess: The sea is wrecking the rock towers that millions of visitors come to see. Should anyone try to stop it?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.1.3.6 case study of a contrasting coastal landscape beyond the UK: risks and opportunities for human occupation and development; evaluate human responses of resilience, mitigation and adaptation
Mission 9

The case study: Port Campbell, Australia

  • Where: Port Campbell National Park, on the Great Ocean Road coast of Victoria, south-east Australia, facing the Southern Ocean.
  • What: limestone cliffs, caves, arches, stacks and islands, including the Twelve Apostles and London Bridge.
  • Visitors: millions of people visit the Twelve Apostles each year.
  • Who manages it: the Great Ocean Road Coast and Parks Authority, which runs about 355 km of coastline and about 170,000 ha of parks and reserves.
  • Why it contrasts with a UK local case: Holderness is soft glacial till retreating 1–4.5 m a year; 26 villages have been lost there in about 1,000 years; Port Campbell is layered limestone whose landforms are the economic asset.
Mission 9

Physical factors: a high-energy coast

  • Waves: large swells from the Southern Ocean; landforms like The Arch stand exposed to "the full force of the ocean".
  • Geology: limestone, a sedimentary rock built from shells, coral and skeletons, in horizontal layers that show as stripes in the cliffs. The authority calls the rocks "relatively soft".
  • Weather: heavy rain, runoff, heat and cold extremes and moisture changes also weaken cliffs.
  • Vegetation: dense scrub on the cliff tops; roots help hold the cliff top together.
Mission 9

How the landforms link up

  • Seas and winds cut caves into the cliffs.
  • Caves become arches, which collapse to leave stacks standing in the ocean.
  • The Grotto frames the sea through a natural window of rock; London Bridge was an arch joined to the mainland.
  • Each landform is a stage in one system: today's headland is tomorrow's arch; today's arch is tomorrow's stack.
Quick check
?A headland at Port Campbell has a sea cave on each side. If the same processes continue, which sequence is most likely next?
Mission 9

Change in seconds and years

  • 1990: London Bridge's arch collapsed, leaving two visitors stranded on the new island until a helicopter rescued them.
  • 3 July 2005: one of the tallest Apostles, about 45 m high, suddenly collapsed.
  • Another stack fell in 2009; seven limestone stacks now remain.
  • Collapse takes seconds; the undercutting that causes it takes years to centuries.
Mission 9

Change over millennia

  • About 20,000 years ago, at the end of the last ice age, sea level was far lower and the coast lay farther out.
  • Scientists found five drowned stacks about 50 m under water, about 6 km offshore; they now stand up to nearly 7 m tall.
  • As the sea rose, the coastline moved inland and those older stacks were drowned. Today's Apostles were cut later, from cliffs farther inland.
  • The drowned stacks were shaped by the same processes as today's Apostles, but formed some 60,000 years apart.
Quick check
?Which change at Port Campbell happens over the longest timescale?
Mission 9

Opportunities for occupation and development

  • Tourism: millions of people visit the Twelve Apostles each year; the authority speaks of "millions of visitors to the region each year".
  • Investment: a $126m Twelve Apostles Precinct Redevelopment, funded by the Geelong City Deal; as of November 2026 its visitor centre was due to open in late 2026.
  • Jobs: the precinct is expected to support up to 40 roles in hospitality, retail, tourism and maintenance.
  • Cultural landscape: the visitor centre garden celebrates Eastern Maar cultural heritage; the authority acknowledges the Eastern Maar and Wadawurrung Peoples as Traditional Owners.
Mission 9

Risks to occupation and development

  • Cliff failures are unpredictable and can happen without warning, risking clifftop and beach users.
  • The Arch viewing area and day visitor site are closed because of unstable cliffs and track erosion.
  • Demons Bluff beach (Grinders), elsewhere on the authority's coast, is permanently closed because of cliff-fall risk.
  • Trampling damages cliff-top plants, and the more they are trampled, the more the cliffs erode.
Mission 9

Responses: resilience, mitigation, adaptation

  • Resilience (knowing and coping): regular geotechnical assessments; monitoring by site visits, aerial imagery and drone surveys. One authority manages the whole coast under the Great Ocean Road and Environs Protection Act.
  • Mitigation (reducing the risk to people): clifftop fencing; closing unsafe areas; asking visitors to stay on formed paths.
  • Adaptation (changing to fit the hazard): moving walking tracks inland as erosion approaches.
  • Note what is not done: no sea walls at the foot of the stacks. Here the eroding landforms are the attraction.
Quick check
?The authority moves a clifftop walking track 20 m inland after a survey shows the cliff edge retreating. Which response is this?
Mission check

?On another soft-limestone coast exposed to big ocean swells, a council sets its new lookout well back from the edge and accepts that the famous stack in view will one day fall. Which reasoning best supports this plan?

A high-energy limestone coast changes in seconds (collapse) and over millennia (sea-level rise), through one linked cave–arch–stack system. People live with it by monitoring, keeping back and moving assets, not by stopping the sea.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The coast is an open system: energy and sediment flow in, through and out; feedback keeps it in dynamic equilibrium or pushes it away.
  • Sediment cells (11 in England and Wales) organise sources, transfers and sinks; the budget decides whether beaches grow or cliffs are exposed.
  • Energy comes from winds, waves, tides and currents; constructive and destructive waves and high- and low-energy coasts follow from it.

Mission 2

  • Marine erosion (hydraulic action, wave quarrying, corrasion, cavitation, solution, attrition) attacks the cliff foot; transport (traction, saltation, suspension, solution, longshore drift) removes the debris; deposition happens where energy falls.
  • Sub-aerial weathering, mass movement and runoff weaken and lower the face; at Holderness both work on weak till, so retreat is fast.

Mission 3

  • Resistant rock forms headlands and weaker rock forms bays; waves exploit joints in headlands to make caves, arches, stacks and stumps.
  • Cliffs retreat by notching and collapse, leaving shore platforms that slow further retreat (negative feedback).
  • Landforms form over seconds (collapses), decades (cliff retreat) and millennia (Holderness: about 2 km in 1,000 years).

Mission 4

  • Spits (one end attached), barrier beaches and bars (both ends), tombolos (island to mainland) and barrier islands (neither end) are all built by waves where energy falls.
  • Salt marshes grow in the shelter of these landforms; dunes grow where onshore wind meets a supply of dry sand.
  • Every depositional landform depends on sediment supply over time: cut the supply and it retreats (Hurst Spit).

Mission 5

  • Factors: shelter (low energy), river and marine sediment, tides (slack water), salinity; processes: flocculation, settling, plant trapping and flow attenuation.
  • Halosere: bare mudflat → pioneers (Salicornia) → cord-grass/low marsh → middle and upper salt meadow (less frequent flooding; grazing). Spartina anglica shows human-assisted spread.

Mission 6

  • Eustatic change moves the sea worldwide; isostatic and tectonic change move the land locally. What matters at a coast is the relative change.
  • Emergence leaves raised beaches, relict cliffs and marine platforms; submergence makes rias, fjords and Dalmatian coasts.
  • Future sea-level rise will push many emergent and submergent coasts towards retreat.

Mission 7

  • Hard engineering fixes a line but alters processes and costs more over time; soft engineering works with processes but must be repeated.
  • SMPs set policies by sediment cell; CBA and EIA inform the choice, and ICZM plans the whole coast.

Mission 8

  • Holderness till cliffs retreat by wave attack at the foot and rotational slumping; drift carries the sediment south; defended towns become hard points with bays between.
  • Field data (erosion posts since 1951, beach profiles, platform lowering) show a long-term average of about 1.8 m a year inside large, cyclic yearly swings; defences hold villages but starve downdrift coasts.

Mission 9

  • Port Campbell: Southern Ocean swell + layered limestone + weathering → caves, arches, stacks; change from seconds (1990, 2005 collapses) to millennia (stacks drowned as sea level rose).
  • Tourism is the opportunity; unpredictable cliff falls are the risk; responses are resilience (monitoring), mitigation (fences, closures, paths) and adaptation (moving tracks inland).
