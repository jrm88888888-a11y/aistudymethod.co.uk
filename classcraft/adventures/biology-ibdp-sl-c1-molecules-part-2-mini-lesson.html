Mission 1

Mission 1: Your cells already get energy from sugar. Why do they spend it through a different molecule first?

Make a guess: Your cells already get energy from sugar. Why do they spend it through a different molecule first?

Mission 1

Meet ATP

  • ATP: adenosine triphosphate.
  • Adenine (a base) + ribose (a pentose) + three phosphate groups.
  • Adenine + ribose = adenosine.
  • ATP is a nucleotide.
  • ADP: adenosine diphosphate. Two phosphate groups.
  • Same base and sugar as an RNA nucleotide carrying adenine.
Quick check
?Which statement about ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is correct?
Mission 1

Hydrolysis releases energy

  • ATP + H2O → ADP + Pi (inorganic phosphate).
  • Energy is released: enough for many single tasks in the cell.
  • Breaking any bond takes energy. Energy is released overall because ADP and Pi are more stable than ATP.
  • Say: "energy is released when ATP is hydrolysed" — not: "energy is stored in a high-energy bond".
Mission 1

Coupling: putting the energy to work

  • ATP hydrolysis is coupled to an energy-requiring process: one enzyme links them, so they happen together.
  • What ATP powers:
  • active transport of ions and molecules across membranes
  • synthesis of macromolecules (anabolism), e.g. proteins, DNA
  • movement: muscle contraction, cilia, chromosomes in cell division
  • Say: "active transport uses ATP" — not: "diffusion and osmosis use ATP".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly describes a life process that ATP supplies with energy?
Mission 1

Remaking ATP: the ATP–ADP cycle

  • ADP + Pi → ATP + H2O: a condensation reaction.
  • Energy comes from respiration (every cell) and photosynthesis (in chloroplasts).
  • ATP is not stockpiled. A cell keeps a small pool and recycles it, second by second.
  • A resting adult turns over roughly their own body mass of ATP each day.
Quick check
?During a sprint, the small store of ATP in muscle fibres is hydrolysed and then re-formed several times over. Which statement about the energy transfers is correct?
Mission 1

Why not spend glucose directly?

  • Glucose releases its energy in many enzyme-controlled steps. ATP: one step, one enzyme.
  • One glucose holds far more energy than one reaction needs. ATP comes in small packets.
  • Released immediately, right where it is needed.
  • Soluble: moves freely within the cell.
  • Quickly remade from ADP + Pi.
  • So ATP is the molecule that distributes energy within cells.
Quick check
?A cell needs an energy currency that can supply many different reactions in the cytoplasm. Which property makes ATP suitable for this role?
Mission check

?A muscle fibre holds only enough ATP for a few seconds of hard contraction. Yet a marathon runner's leg muscles keep contracting for hours. Which statement explains this?

A small, fast-recycled pool of ATP, refilled by respiration, beats spending glucose directly.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How can a bead of liquid creeping along a thin tube show how fast seeds respire, and which fuel they use?

Make a guess: How can a bead of liquid creeping along a thin tube show how fast seeds respire, and which fuel they use?

Mission 2

Other fuels

  • Glucose and fatty acids are the main respiratory substrates. Many other carbon compounds can be respired.
Mission 2Practical

The respirometer

  • Organisms (germinating seeds, woodlice or yeast) sit in a sealed tube, above potassium hydroxide solution (or soda lime), which absorbs CO2.
  • O2 is used → gas volume falls → the liquid bead in the capillary moves towards the organisms.
  • O2 volume = distance moved × πr² (r = capillary radius).
  • Control tube: glass beads of the same volume. It corrects for temperature and pressure changes.
  • Constant-temperature water bath; let the tubes equilibrate first; reset the bead with the syringe.
  • Without the alkali, the bead shows O2 used − CO2 released (the net change).
  • Carbohydrate: CO2 released = O2 used, so no net change. Lipid: less CO2 than O2, so the bead still moves towards the organisms. The net change hints at the fuel.
Mission 2

Worked example: rate of oxygen uptake

Illustrative data: 2.0 g germinating seeds, 10 min, 25 °C, capillary diameter 1.0 mm. Bead moved 24 mm towards the seeds; the control's bead moved 2 mm the same way.

  • Step 1, correct with the control: 24 − 2 = 22 mm.
  • Step 2, volume: r = 0.50 mm, so π × 0.50² × 22 = 17.3 mm³ O2.
  • Step 3, rate: 17.3 ÷ 2.0 g ÷ 10 min = 0.86 mm³ g⁻¹ min⁻¹.
  • Always divide by time and by mass, so different samples can be compared.
Quick check
?In a respirometer, carbon dioxide is absorbed by potassium hydroxide. With 5.0 g of germinating seeds, the gas volume in the apparatus fell by 1.2 cm³ in 10 minutes. What is the rate of respiration per gram?
Quick checkRead the data
?Illustrative data.
A student measured oxygen uptake by yeast (cm³ g⁻¹ min⁻¹) at five temperatures: 20 °C 0.10; 30 °C 0.19; 40 °C 0.31; 50 °C 0.26; 60 °C 0.04. Which conclusion is best supported?
Mission check

?A student set up a respirometer with blowfly larvae above potassium hydroxide solution, and the bead moved steadily towards the larvae. In a second run, identical except that the potassium hydroxide was replaced with water, the bead hardly moved. Which explanation is best?

With the alkali, the bead measures oxygen uptake: the rate. Without it, it measures the balance of the two gases, and that balance points to the fuel.

Mission 3

Mission 3: One spot of a mixture goes in; a ladder of spots comes out. How do you work out what each spot is?

Make a guess: One spot of a mixture goes in; a ladder of spots comes out. How do you work out what each spot is?

Mission 3

How chromatography separates

  • (1) Stationary phase: the paper, or a thin layer of silica gel on a plate (TLC). It stays put.
  • (2) Mobile phase: the solvent. It creeps up the stationary phase by capillary action, carrying the mixture.
  • (3) Each molecule spends time dissolved in the solvent and time attached to the stationary phase.
  • More soluble in the solvent, less attracted to the stationary phase: it travels further.
  • So molecules in a mixture separate into spots.
  • TLC runs faster and gives sharper spots than paper.
  • Say: "distance depends on solubility and attraction" — not: "on how much of it there is".
Mission 3

Running a chromatogram

  • Draw an origin line in pencil (ink would itself separate), about 1.5 cm from the bottom.
  • Put a small, concentrated spot of the sample on the origin. Dry it; add more; dry again.
  • Stand the paper in solvent with the origin above the solvent level, so the sample does not wash off into it.
  • Cover the container, so the air inside stays saturated with solvent vapour.
  • Take it out before the solvent reaches the top. Mark the solvent front in pencil at once.
  • Coloured spots show themselves. Colourless ones need a locating agent.
Mission 3

Worked example: Rf values

  • Rf = distance moved by the solute ÷ distance moved by the solvent front.
  • Measure both from the origin; measure to the centre of the spot.
  • Illustrative data: origin 20 mm from the paper's bottom edge; spot centre 56 mm; solvent front 80 mm.
  • Solute: 56 − 20 = 36 mm. Solvent: 80 − 20 = 60 mm.
  • Rf = 36 ÷ 60 = 0.60. Always between 0 and 1; no units.
  • Same solute, solvent and stationary phase: same Rf. Compare with reference values, or run standards beside the sample.
Quick check
?On a paper chromatogram of leaf pigments, the origin line was 20 mm from the bottom edge of the paper, the centre of the carotene spot was 65 mm from the bottom edge and the solvent front was 70 mm from the bottom edge. What is the Rf value of carotene?
Mission 3

Leaf pigments

  • Grind a leaf with a little sand and propanone to extract its pigments. Spot on the origin.
  • A green leaf gives several spots, not one. Typical order, top to bottom:
  • carotene (orange) · xanthophyll (yellow) · chlorophyll a (blue-green) · chlorophyll b (yellow-green).
  • Each pigment absorbs different wavelengths of light.
  • Identify each by colour and Rf, against reference values for the same solvent.
Quick checkRead the data
?(Illustrative data.) In the solvent used, reference Rf values for leaf pigments are: carotene 0.95 (orange), xanthophyll 0.71 (yellow), chlorophyll a 0.65 (blue-green) and chlorophyll b 0.45 (yellow-green). A student's chromatogram shows three spots: orange at Rf 0.94, blue-green at Rf 0.66 and yellow-green at Rf 0.44. Which conclusion is best supported?
Mission check

?Sugars in a fruit juice are separated by thin-layer chromatography, with glucose, fructose and sucrose standards run on the same plate. The juice gives two spots, level with the glucose and fructose standards; nothing is level with sucrose. On a second plate, with a different solvent, every spot (juice and standards) has a different Rf from the first plate. Which conclusion is correct?

A spot is named by where it lands compared with a known, under the same conditions. Not by its size, and not by guesswork.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Brighter lamps stop making a greenhouse crop grow any faster. What should the grower change instead?

Make a guess: Brighter lamps stop making a greenhouse crop grow any faster. What should the grower change instead?

Mission 4

The limiting factor

  • Rate of photosynthesis depends on several factors at once: light intensity, CO2 concentration, temperature.
  • The one in shortest supply sets the rate: the limiting factor.
  • Raise the limiting factor → rate rises.
  • Raise any other factor → no change.
  • Light: powers the light-dependent stage. CO2: substrate for rubisco. Temperature: speed of enzyme-catalysed steps.
Mission 4

Reading the curves

1. Rate rises as light rises: light is limiting.

2. The curve flattens: light is no longer limiting. Another factor is.

3. A curve at higher CO2 reaches a higher plateau: CO2 was limiting the first plateau.

  • On the rising part, curves at different CO2 overlap: there, only light matters.
  • Say: "the plateau shows another factor is limiting" — not: "the plant has reached its maximum rate".
Quick check
?Pondweed submerged in water at 25 °C with the carbon dioxide concentration of ordinary air was exposed to increasing light intensity. The rate of photosynthesis rose at first and then levelled off, staying constant however much the light intensity was increased. Which change is most likely to raise the rate at this plateau?
Mission 4

Temperature and water

  • Light-independent reactions are enzyme-catalysed: warmer → faster, up to an optimum.
  • Above the optimum: enzymes such as rubisco denature; rate falls.
  • Low temperature can limit the rate even in bright light and high CO2.
  • Say: "water stress limits photosynthesis by closing stomata" — not: "water is short as a reactant".
Mission 4

Practical: measuring the rate

  • Skill: vary CO2, light intensity or temperature; suggest and test a hypothesis.
  • Pondweed (e.g. Elodea or Cabomba) in sodium hydrogencarbonate solution (CO2 source).
  • Measure O2: volume collected in a photosynthometer capillary per minute (better than counting bubbles).
  • Vary light: lamp distance; intensity falls about as 1 ÷ d². Use an LED or a water heat shield.
  • Vary CO2: hydrogencarbonate concentration. Vary temperature: water bath.
  • Let the plant acclimatise at each setting; repeat; keep other factors constant.
  • Alternatives: algal beads in hydrogencarbonate indicator; floating leaf discs.
Quick check
?A student places a lamp at different distances from a beaker of pondweed in sodium hydrogencarbonate solution kept at 25 °C, and counts the bubbles of gas released per minute at each distance. Which correctly identifies the variables?

Mission 5

Mission 5: The carbon atoms in your body were once in the air. How do they keep being recycled?

Make a guess: The carbon atoms in your body were once in the air. How do they keep being recycled?

Mission 5

Where carbon is stored

  • Carbon sits in stores (reservoirs):
  • the atmosphere, as CO2;
  • the oceans, as dissolved CO2 and hydrogencarbonate ions;
  • living organisms, as carbon compounds;
  • dead organic matter and soil;
  • fossil fuels, peat and rocks such as limestone.
  • Carbon atoms move between stores. None is made or destroyed.
Mission 5

The processes that move it

  • Photosynthesis removes CO2 from air or water and fixes it in producers.
  • Feeding passes carbon compounds along food chains.
  • Respiration in every organism, producers included, returns CO2.
  • Decomposition: decomposers respire the carbon in dead matter and faeces, returning CO2.
  • Combustion of fuels and biomass releases CO2.
  • Photosynthesis supplies the O2 for aerobic respiration; respiration supplies the CO2 for photosynthesis. These yearly fluxes are huge.
  • Say: "plants respire all the time" — not: "plants photosynthesise instead of respiring".
Quick check
?A student draws a carbon cycle diagram for a pond. Which set of labelled arrows is correct?
Mission 5

Decomposers unlock the dead

  • Dead organisms and faeces lock carbon and other elements inside large organic molecules.
  • Decomposers (saprotrophic fungi and bacteria) secrete enzymes, digest the matter outside their cells, absorb the products and respire them: CO2 is released.
  • They also release mineral ions, such as ammonium and phosphate, that plants can absorb.
  • So all the elements living things need are recycled, not just carbon.
  • No decomposers, no recycling: carbon builds up in dead matter.
  • Example: waterlogged soil lacks O2, so decay is very slow and peat forms.
  • Say: "decomposers return carbon to the air as CO2" — not: "decomposition puts the carbon into the soil".
Quick check
?In an experiment, all the decomposers in a woodland soil are killed, but the trees keep growing for a while. Which change would follow over the next few years?
Mission 5

Sink or source?

  • Compare carbon taken in by photosynthesis with carbon released by all respiration (and fire).
  • Photosynthesis > respiration: net uptake. The ecosystem is a carbon sink.
  • Respiration > photosynthesis: net release. It is a carbon source.
  • Example (illustrative): a grassland fixes 900 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹. Plants respire 450; animals, fungi and bacteria respire 380.
  • Net = 900 − (450 + 380) = +70 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹: a sink.
  • Lots of plants does not guarantee a sink: count all respiration.
Quick check
?Suppose measurements in a forest over one year give: carbon fixed by photosynthesis 1200 g m⁻²; carbon released by plant respiration 600 g m⁻²; carbon released by the respiration of animals, fungi and bacteria 650 g m⁻². Is the forest a carbon sink or a carbon source in that year?
Mission 5

Slow stores: oceans, rocks and fuels

  • CO2 dissolves in the oceans. Marine organisms build calcium carbonate shells; their remains form limestone.
  • Where decomposition was incomplete (waterlogged, little O2), plant remains became peat and, over millions of years, coal.
  • Oil and natural gas formed from marine plankton buried in sediments with little O2.
  • Combustion returns that stored carbon to the air as CO2 very quickly.
  • Fuels differ in age: biomass (years), peat (thousands of years), coal, oil and gas (millions of years). All their carbon was once fixed by photosynthesis.
  • Fires after lightning are natural, but human activity has greatly increased combustion.
  • Say: "fossil-fuel carbon was fixed by photosynthesis" — not: "fossil fuels are just minerals".
Quick check
?Which statement about the combustion of biomass, peat, coal, oil and natural gas is correct?
Mission check

?A peat bog has stored carbon for thousands of years in waterlogged soil. It is drained for farmland, and the peat dries out. What happens to much of the carbon in the peat?

Carbon only keeps moving while something moves it: photosynthesis takes it in; respiration, decomposition and combustion send it back.

Mission 6Read the data

The Keeling curve

CO2 / ppm316.9338.8369.7414.2
  • Every year it peaks in May and is lowest in September–October. In 2019: 414.9 ppm in May, 408.7 ppm in October. A swing of about 6 ppm.
  • 1960 to 2020: up 97 ppm, about 1.6 ppm per year on average.
  • The rise is speeding up: about 1.1 ppm per year in 1960–1980; about 2.2 ppm per year in 2000–2020.
Mission 6

Two patterns, two causes

  • The yearly swing: most land, and most plants, are in the Northern Hemisphere.
  • Northern summer: photosynthesis > respiration, so CO2 falls.
  • Northern autumn and winter: respiration and decomposition > photosynthesis, so CO2 rises.
  • The long-term rise: combustion of fossil fuels, and burning and clearing of forests, add CO2 faster than photosynthesis and the oceans remove it.
  • Carbon locked away for millions of years is returned to the air in decades.
  • Say: "the rise comes from combustion" — not: "from more people breathing out".
Quick check
?At Mauna Loa, atmospheric carbon dioxide concentration rises to a peak each May and falls to a minimum each September or October, a swing of several parts per million. What causes this annual fluctuation?
Mission 6

The greenhouse effect

  • Sunlight passes through the atmosphere and warms the Earth's surface.
  • The warm surface gives off infrared radiation.
  • Greenhouse gases absorb infrared and re-emit it, partly back towards the surface. This keeps Earth warm enough for life.
  • More greenhouse gas → more infrared retained → the enhanced greenhouse effect → global warming.
  • CO2 rises from combustion of fossil fuels and from deforestation.
  • Methane rises from ruminant livestock, rice paddies, landfill and leaks from gas and coal extraction.
  • Say: "greenhouse gases absorb infrared" — not: "the ozone hole lets in more heat".
Mission 6

Reading past climates

  • Ice cores: bubbles of air trapped in Antarctic ice record past CO2; the ice itself records past temperature.
  • Over 800 000 years, high CO2 goes with high temperature: a positive correlation.
  • Positive: both rise and fall together. Negative: one rises as the other falls.
  • Correlation alone does not prove cause. Other evidence, such as CO2 absorbing infrared in the lab, supplies the causal link.
Quick check
?Antarctic ice cores show that, over the last 800 000 years, periods of high global temperature coincided with high atmospheric carbon dioxide concentrations and periods of low temperature with low concentrations. Which statement about this evidence is correct?
Mission 6

Feedbacks and tipping points

  • Positive feedback: warming causes changes that cause more warming.
  • Warmer oceans hold less dissolved CO2; changes in circulation can also release CO2 stored in deep water.
  • Melting snow and ice expose darker sea and land, which absorb more solar radiation.
  • Thawing permafrost and drying peat: old organic matter decomposes, releasing CO2, and methane from waterlogged ground.
  • More droughts and forest fires release more CO2.
  • Tipping point, e.g. the boreal forest (taiga): warmer temperatures and less winter snow → drought → lower primary production, browning, more and fiercer fires that burn legacy carbon in the soil. The forest flips from carbon sink to source.
  • Say: "positive feedback amplifies a change" — not: "it restores the original state".
Quick check
?Which sequence of events explains how boreal forests in a warming climate change from net carbon accumulation to net carbon loss?
Mission 6

Will plants absorb the extra CO2?

  • Where CO2 limits photosynthesis, more CO2 could mean more growth.
  • Test predictions with CO2 enrichment experiments.
  • Enclosed greenhouses: CO2 raised; temperature, light and water held constant. Easy to control, but unlike the field.
  • FACE (free-air CO2 enrichment): pipes release CO2 over plots of open field or forest; control plots at today's CO2. Realistic, harder to control.
  • In the field the gain is usually smaller: water and nutrients also limit growth.
  • Lab or field? A trade-off between control of variables and realism.
Mission 6

Drawing carbon back down

  • Use the carbon cycle: increase photosynthesis, slow decomposition, cut combustion.
  • Reforestation and afforestation: growing trees fix CO2 and store it in wood and soil.
  • Forest regeneration: let native forest regrow on its own.
  • Restore peat-forming wetlands: re-wetting cuts O2, slows decomposers, and peat builds up again.
  • Scientists actively debate whether fast-growing non-native plantations or native woodland store more carbon in the long term.
Quick check
?A team wants to predict how a forest will grow at the carbon dioxide concentration expected in 2070. Which plan gives the more realistic prediction, and what does it give up?
Mission check

?At a monitoring station in Tasmania, in the Southern Hemisphere, carbon dioxide shows a much smaller yearly swing than at Mauna Loa, but a similar long-term rise. Which explanation fits both observations?

Two clocks run in the same curve: the yearly breath of the northern forests, and the steady climb from burning carbon stored for millions of years.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Why can a warming of just a degree or two force species to move, or vanish from a place?

Make a guess: Why can a warming of just a degree or two force species to move, or vanish from a place?

Mission 7

Species on the move

  • Each species survives only within a band of conditions: temperature, rainfall, season length.
  • As the climate warms, that band shifts towards the poles and up mountains.
  • New populations establish at the cool edge; old ones die out at the warm edge. The range shifts.
  • This happens over generations, through dispersal and the survival of young, not by individuals migrating.
  • No cooler place to go (mountain tops, poles), or barriers in the way? Local extinction, or extinction of the species.
  • Evidence: montane birds in New Guinea moved upslope; North American trees spread north while their southern edges contract.
  • Say: "the range shifts" — not: "the range grows" or "the animals move house".
Quick check
?The bird species of a mountain in New Guinea were surveyed in 1965 and again in 2012. For most species both the upper and the lower limits of the altitudinal range had moved upslope, by about 100 m on average, over a period in which the region warmed. What is the best interpretation?
Mission 7

Polar ice habitats

  • Sea ice floats on the ocean. Landfast ice is sea ice attached to the coast.
  • Emperor penguins (Antarctic) breed on landfast ice through the winter. If it breaks up early, before chicks grow waterproof feathers, chicks drown or die of cold.
  • Walruses (Arctic) rest on sea ice between dives for molluscs on the shallow seabed. Summer ice now retreats over deep water: they crowd onto beaches and swim much further to feed.
  • The threat is loss of habitat, not the heat itself.
  • Emperor penguins live only in the Antarctic; walruses only in the Arctic.
Mission 7

Warmer seas: upwelling fails

  • Phytoplankton need mineral nutrients that rise (upwell) from cold, deep water.
  • Warm surface water is less dense: it forms a stable layer that blocks upwelling.
  • Less phytoplankton → less energy for zooplankton, fish and seabirds.
  • Off Peru, years with unusually warm surface water bring anchovy catches crashing down.
  • Shifts in ocean currents change both the timing and the extent of upwelling.
Quick check
?Off the coast of Peru, cold nutrient-rich water normally rises to the surface. In some years the surface water is unusually warm, and the catch of anchovies falls sharply. Which explanation is correct?
Mission 7

Coral reefs at risk

  • Reef-building corals hold mutualistic algae (zooxanthellae) that photosynthesise and feed them.
  • Higher water temperature → corals expel their algae → bleaching. Prolonged heat → corals die.
  • More dissolved CO2 → lower pH and fewer carbonate ions (ocean acidification) → slower building of calcium carbonate skeletons (calcification suppressed).
  • Seawater stays alkaline, about pH 8.1; it is less alkaline, not acidic.
  • Corals are the reef's framework: lose them and the whole reef ecosystem can collapse.
  • Say: "heat causes bleaching; CO2 suppresses calcification" — not: "acid bleaches corals".
Quick check
?During a marine heatwave, the corals on a reef turned white. When the water cooled four weeks later, most of the corals regained their colour. Which explanation is correct?
Mission check

?A hypothetical small mammal lives only in cool meadows near the top of an isolated mountain. Over 40 years the region warms by 1.5 °C. Which outcome is most likely?

A degree or two moves the conditions a species needs. Species that can follow survive in new places; those with nowhere to go are lost.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • ATP = adenine + ribose + three phosphates. Hydrolysis to ADP + Pi releases energy in a small, immediate packet; respiration supplies the energy to remake it.

Mission 2

  • A respirometer with an alkali measures O2 uptake: rate = corrected volume ÷ mass ÷ time; without the alkali it shows O2 used − CO2 released.

Mission 3

  • Molecules separate by how they share time between the solvent and the stationary phase.
  • Rf = distance moved by the solute ÷ distance moved by the solvent front, both from the origin; compare with standards or reference values for the same solvent.
  • Leaf extracts give carotene, xanthophyll, chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b.

Mission 4

  • The rate is set by the factor in shortest supply (light, CO2 or temperature). A plateau means another factor now limits.
  • Extra CO2 raises yields less in open fields (FACE) than in enclosures, because other factors also limit growth.

Mission 5

  • Carbon moves between stores by photosynthesis (removes CO2), feeding, and respiration, decomposition and combustion (all add CO2).
  • Decomposers recycle carbon and every other element; without them, carbon piles up in dead matter such as peat.

Mission 6

  • The yearly swing in CO2 is photosynthesis against respiration in the northern growing season; the long-term rise is combustion of fossil fuels and forests.
  • CO2 and methane absorb infrared; more of them enhances the greenhouse effect.
  • Past climate evidence shows correlation; the causal link comes from other evidence.
  • Positive feedbacks and tipping points can amplify warming; enrichment experiments and sequestration show how ecosystems may respond.

Mission 7

  • Warming shifts the conditions species need poleward and upslope; ranges follow over generations, and species with nowhere to go face local extinction.
  • Warmer surface water blocks nutrient upwelling, cutting primary production in the oceans.
