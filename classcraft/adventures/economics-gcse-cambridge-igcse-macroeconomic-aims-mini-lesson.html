Mission 1

Mission 1: Can a country grow fast, keep everyone in work and hold prices steady, all at the same time?

Make a guess: Can a country grow fast, keep everyone in work and hold prices steady, all at the same time?

Mission 1

What governments aim for

ObjectiveWhat "success" looks like
Economic growthReal GDP rising at a steady, sustainable rate
Full employment (low unemployment)Almost everyone who wants a job has one
Price stabilityLow and stable inflation
Balance of paymentsA current account that is not in large, lasting deficit
  • UK example: the government sets the Bank of England an inflation target of 2% (CPI).
  • Also:
    • Fairer distribution of income (redistribution)
    • Protecting the environment (sustainability)
  • Cambridge calls these the six macroeconomic aims: growth, full employment, stable prices, BoP stability, redistribution of income, sustainability.
  • Say: "low and stable inflation" — not: "falling prices". Falling prices (deflation) are not the goal.
Quick check
?Illustrative data for one country this year: real GDP growth 2%, unemployment 9%, inflation 2%, current account deficit 1% of GDP. Which objective is the government furthest from meeting?
Mission 1

When goals help each other, and when they clash

  • Some goals support each other:
    • growth → firms hire more → unemployment falls
    • more people in work → more tax revenue → more to spend on schools or benefits
  • Others conflict. A trade-off means getting more of one goal costs some of another.
  • Main conflicts:
    • unemployment vs inflation
    • growth vs inflation
    • growth or inflation vs the current account
    • growth vs the environment
    • growth vs equality
  • Most conflicts come from total spending in the economy. Push it up and some goals improve while others get worse.
Mission 1

Conflict: unemployment vs inflation

  • To cut unemployment, a government can boost spending (lower taxes, more government spending, lower interest rates).
  • Firms sell more, so they hire more workers.
  • But as the economy gets close to full capacity:
    • firms compete for scarce workers, so wages rise
    • firms pass higher costs and strong demand into higher prices
  • So lower unemployment can come with higher inflation (demand-pull).
  • It works the other way too: raising interest rates to cut inflation reduces spending, so some workers lose their jobs.
Mission 1

Conflict: growth vs inflation

  • If spending grows faster than the economy's ability to produce, output cannot keep up.
  • Too much money chasing too few goods: demand-pull inflation.
  • A fast-growing economy also bids up the price of raw materials and energy, pushing costs up.
  • Growth from more capacity (better skills, new machines, new technology) is different: output can rise without extra inflation.
  • Say: "growth in productive capacity" — not just "growth", when you mean the economy can make more, not just that people are spending more.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. Over two years, unemployment falls from 6% to 4% while inflation rises from 2% to 5%. Which is the most likely cause?
Mission 1

Conflict: inflation, growth and the current account

  • Current account: exports minus imports of goods and services (plus income flows).
  • Inflation higher than in other countries:
    • home-made goods become less competitive abroad, so exports fall
    • foreign goods look cheaper at home, so imports rise
    • the current account worsens
  • Fast growth: people's incomes rise, and part of the extra income is spent on imports.
  • Say: "current account deficit" — not: "budget deficit". One is about trade with other countries; the other is about government spending and taxes.
Mission 1

Conflict: growth vs the environment

  • More output usually means more energy, raw materials, pollution and waste.
  • Some resources (oil, forests, fish stocks) can be used up: growth may not be sustainable.
  • Sustainable growth: growth that meets today's needs without harming future generations.
  • The link can weaken. UK greenhouse gas emissions fell by about 50% between 1990 and 2022, while real GDP grew.
  • Caution: part of that fall came from making fewer goods in the UK and importing more.
Mission 1

Conflict: growth vs equality

  • Growth does not reach everyone equally. Owners of businesses, highly skilled workers and booming regions may gain most.
  • So the gap between rich and poor can widen even as average income rises.
  • Redistribution (progressive taxes, benefits) can narrow the gap.
  • One view: very high taxes and benefits may weaken the reward for working and investing, so growth slows.
  • Another view: some research (IMF, 2014) found countries with lower inequality tended to have longer-lasting growth.
  • Which effect is bigger depends on how the policy is designed.
Quick check
?A country's inflation rate rises well above that of its trading partners, while their exchange rates stay the same. What is the most likely effect on its current account?
Mission 1

Managing the conflicts

  • Set priorities: tackle the most urgent problem first, e.g. cut very high inflation, accepting slower growth for a while.
  • Use different tools for different goals: interest rates for inflation; training and better transport links for growth.
  • Raise capacity (supply-side policies): then output and jobs can grow with less inflation. But this takes years.
  • Green technology, pollution taxes and regulation aim to keep growth while cutting emissions.
  • Choosing which goal comes first is normative: it depends on values, not just evidence.
Quick check
?A government wants faster growth without higher inflation. Which policy is most likely to achieve both over time?
Mission check

?During a boom, unemployment in a country falls to a record low and household incomes rise quickly. At the same time its current account deficit widens sharply. What is the most likely link?

Pushing one goal (jobs and growth) can make another (the current account) worse.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Where does a government's money come from, and what happens when it spends more than it collects?

Make a guess: Where does a government's money come from, and what happens when it spends more than it collects?

Mission 2

Where the money comes from

  • Taxes: by far the biggest source.
    • on income (income tax, social security contributions such as National Insurance)
    • on spending (VAT, duties on fuel, alcohol and tobacco)
    • on company profits (corporation tax), property and wealth
  • Other income: charges and fees, profits of state-owned firms, sales of state assets.
  • Borrowing: when taxes do not cover spending.
  • Example, UK, 2026–27 (OBR forecast):
    • total receipts about £1,316 billion
    • income tax and National Insurance together about £580 billion; VAT about £186 billion
  • Reasons for taxation: raise revenue for public services · redistribute income · discourage harmful goods (e.g. tobacco) · protect home industries (tariffs) · influence total spending in the economy.
Mission 2

What the money is spent on

  • Social protection: pensions, unemployment and other benefits.
  • Health and education.
  • Defence, police and courts.
  • Infrastructure: roads, rail, housing.
  • Interest on government debt.
  • Purposes: provide goods markets would not supply enough of (public and merit goods) · help people on low incomes · support growth (skills, transport) · steady the economy in a slump.
  • Example, UK, 2026–27 (OBR forecast): welfare about £353 billion; health department about £212 billion; education department about £95 billion; net debt interest about £124 billion.
Mission 2

Direct and indirect taxes

  • Direct tax: on income or wealth, paid straight to the government by the person or firm taxed.
    • income tax, corporation tax, National Insurance
  • Indirect tax: on spending on goods and services. The seller pays it to the government but passes some or all of it on in higher prices.
    • VAT, excise duties on fuel, alcohol and tobacco, tariffs on imports
  • Say: "VAT is an indirect tax" — not: "VAT is a direct tax because shoppers pay it directly at the till".
Quick check
?Which of these is an indirect tax?
Mission 2

Progressive, regressive, proportional

  • Judge a tax by the percentage of income paid, not the amount.
  • Progressive: the percentage rises as income rises.
  • Proportional: the same percentage at every income.
  • Regressive: the percentage falls as income rises.
  • Indirect taxes such as VAT tend to be regressive: poorer households spend a bigger share of their income.
  • UK data: in the financial year ending 2022, indirect taxes took 28.1% of the disposable income of the poorest fifth of people, and 9.8% for the richest fifth (ONS).
  • Say: "a progressive tax takes a higher percentage from higher incomes" — not: "a progressive tax is one where rich people pay more pounds".
Mission 2

Worked example: a progressive income tax

  • Illustrative data. Tax bands:
    • first 10,000: no tax
    • 10,001 to 40,000: 20%
    • above 40,000: 40%
  • Income 20,000: tax = 20% × 10,000 = 2,000. Share of income: 2,000 ÷ 20,000 = 10%.
  • Income 60,000:
    • 20% × 30,000 = 6,000
    • 40% × 20,000 = 8,000
    • total tax 14,000. Share: 14,000 ÷ 60,000 = 23.3%.
  • The share rises with income, so the tax is progressive.
  • Note: the 40% rate applies only to income above 40,000, not to all of it.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A new tax takes 500 from a person earning 10,000 and 1,500 from a person earning 50,000. What kind of tax is it?
Mission 2

The budget: balanced, surplus or deficit

  • Budget balance = government revenue − government spending.
  • Balanced budget: revenue = spending.
  • Budget surplus: revenue > spending. The government can repay some debt.
  • Budget deficit: spending > revenue. The government borrows the difference.
  • UK, 2026–27 (OBR forecast): spending £1,449 billion − receipts £1,316 billion = a deficit of £133 billion, about 4.2% of national income.
  • National debt: the total the government owes, built up from past deficits.
  • Say: "the deficit is one year's borrowing; the debt is the total owed" — not: "deficit" and "debt" as the same thing.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A government collects 380 billion in revenue and spends 410 billion. The country's GDP is 2,000 billion. What is its budget position?
Mission 2

Fiscal policy

  • Fiscal policy: using government spending and taxes to influence the economy.
  • Expansionary (in a slump): spend more or cut taxes.
    • households and firms have more to spend: total demand rises
    • output and jobs rise; the deficit usually grows
  • Contractionary (in a boom): spend less or raise taxes.
    • total demand falls: inflation pressure eases
    • but growth slows and some jobs may go
  • Impacts on the aims: growth and employment, inflation, the balance of payments (more spending pulls in imports), and redistribution.
Mission 2

Consequences of deficits and surpluses

  • A deficit:
    • can support jobs and incomes in a slump
    • adds to debt, and debt has to be serviced: interest payments
    • very large debts can raise borrowing costs if lenders lose confidence
  • A surplus:
    • lets the government repay debt
    • but means higher taxes or lower spending, which takes demand out of the economy
  • Economists disagree about how fast deficits should be cut: too fast may slow growth; too slow lets debt and interest costs keep rising.
Quick check
?An economy is in a deep recession, with unemployment rising fast. Which fiscal policy is most likely to help in the short run?
Mission 2

Weigh it up: when should a government borrow?

It depends on:

  • The state of the economy: borrowing in a slump supports jobs; borrowing in a boom can add to inflation.
  • What the money buys: borrowing for roads, schools or skills may raise future output; borrowing for day-to-day costs does not.
  • How big the debt already is, and the interest rate on it.
  • Who pays: future taxpayers repay today's borrowing.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. A country runs a budget deficit five years in a row. Each year the deficit is smaller than the year before: 50, 40, 30, 20, then 10 billion. What happens to its national debt over the five years?

A deficit is one year's borrowing; the debt keeps growing until the budget reaches surplus.

Mission 3

Mission 3: How can one interest rate, set by a handful of people, slow down inflation across a whole country?

Make a guess: How can one interest rate, set by a handful of people, slow down inflation across a whole country?

Mission 3

Who sets the rate?

  • Monetary policy: action by the central bank to change interest rates and the money supply, to influence spending and inflation.
  • UK: the Bank of England. Its Monetary Policy Committee (MPC), 9 members, sets Bank Rate.
  • The MPC meets about eight times a year and votes. A handful of people decide one rate for the whole country.
  • Since 1997 the Bank has been independent: the government sets the target, the MPC chooses the rate.
  • Other countries: a central bank such as the European Central Bank or the Reserve Bank of India sets its own policy interest rate.
  • Bank Rate feeds through to the rates that banks charge on loans and mortgages and pay on savings.
Mission 3

What it aims for

  • Main aim: price stability: low and stable inflation.
  • UK target: 2% CPI inflation, set by the government.
  • Many central banks aim for about 2%.
  • Other objectives: support growth and jobs, as long as inflation stays near target.
Mission 3

The transmission mechanism

  • How a rise in the interest rate reaches prices:
    • Borrowing costs more: mortgages, loans, credit cards. People and firms borrow less.
    • Saving pays more: people save more and spend less.
    • Households with variable-rate mortgages have less to spend.
    • Firms invest less in new machines and buildings.
    • The exchange rate tends to rise: exports dearer abroad, imports cheaper.
  • Result: total spending grows more slowly. Firms face weaker demand, so they raise prices less: inflation slows.
  • A cut works the other way: more borrowing and spending.
  • Say: "inflation slows" — not: "prices fall". Prices usually still rise, just more slowly.
Mission 3

Worked example: what a rate rise does to a family

  • Illustrative data. The interest rate on loans and savings rises by 3 percentage points.
  • A loan of 10,000:
    • at 2% a year: interest = 10,000 × 0.02 = 200
    • at 5% a year: interest = 10,000 × 0.05 = 500
    • 300 more a year to the lender, so 300 less to spend elsewhere
  • Savings of 5,000:
    • at 1%: interest = 50
    • at 4%: interest = 200
    • saving is more rewarding, so spending now is less attractive
  • Borrowers spend less; savers have a reason to save more. Total spending slows.
Quick check
?A central bank raises its interest rate. Which chain of effects is most likely?

  • So monetary policy can face a conflict: cutting inflation can cost jobs (see Mission 1).
  • Used to control inflation: raise rates when inflation is above target.
Mission 3

Other tools

  • Money supply: the central bank can increase or reduce the amount of money in the economy. More money tends to mean more spending.
  • Exchange rate: the central bank can buy or sell its own currency, or change interest rates, to raise or lower the exchange rate. A higher exchange rate makes imports cheaper and can lower inflation.
Quick check
?A central bank wants to reduce inflation. Which action fits?
Mission 3

Real case: the rate rises of 2021–23

  • Inflation rose sharply around the world. It peaked in October 2022:
    • UK CPI inflation: 11.1%
    • euro area: 10.6%
  • Central banks raised interest rates again and again.
    • UK: Bank Rate was 0.1% (March 2020). The MPC raised it from December 2021 to 5.25% (August 2023).
    • euro area: the European Central Bank raised its deposit rate from −0.5% (2022) to 4.0% (September 2023).
  • UK inflation was back at 2.0% in May 2024.
  • Economists debate how much of the fall came from higher rates and how much from falling energy prices.
Mission 3

Strengths and limits

  • Strengths:
    • flexible: rates can change in small steps, several times a year
    • quick to decide: no new law needed
    • an independent central bank is trusted to keep inflation low
  • Limits:
    • time lags: up to about two years for the full effect on inflation
    • if inflation comes from rising costs (e.g. oil prices), higher rates cut inflation only by cutting spending, output and jobs
    • when confidence is low, people may not borrow even if rates are low
    • one rate for everyone: borrowers lose, savers gain
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A household has a variable-rate mortgage of 200,000 and savings of 10,000. The interest rate on both rises by 2 percentage points. By how much does its yearly interest position change?
Mission 3

Weigh it up: how well do interest rates work?

It depends on:

  • The cause of inflation: too much spending responds well; a jump in costs forces a choice between inflation and jobs.
  • Time: a change made now works over the next two years, when things may have changed.
  • Debt: if many people have loans at variable rates, a rise bites quickly.
  • Confidence: when people are worried, cheap loans may not get them spending.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. Growth in a country has slowed and unemployment is rising. Inflation is 1.5%, below the 2% target. The central bank cuts its interest rate from 4% to 2%. What is most likely over the next year or two?

Interest rates work by changing how much people and firms borrow and spend, and that takes time.

Mission 4: Nobody sets the price of a dollar or a euro, so what decides it from one day to the next?

Make a guess: Nobody sets the price of a dollar or a euro, so what decides it from one day to the next?

Mission 4

An exchange rate is a price

  • Exchange rate: the price of one currency in terms of another.
  • Example (illustrative): €1 = $1.25.
  • Appreciation: the currency buys more of another currency. It is stronger.
  • Depreciation: the currency buys less of another currency. It is weaker.
  • Floating exchange rate: set by demand and supply in the foreign exchange market, with no target set by the government or central bank.
  • A central bank can also buy or sell its own currency, or change interest rates, to move the rate (see Mission 3).
Mission 4

Worked example: converting currencies

Illustrative data.

  • Rule (rate quoted as foreign currency per 1 unit of home currency): home → foreign, multiply; foreign → home, divide.
  • Rate €1 = $1.30.
  • A €500 phone made in Europe sells in the US for 500 × 1.30 = $650.
  • $65 jeans made in the US cost a European buyer 65 ÷ 1.30 = €50.
  • The euro appreciates to $1.50. Phone: $750. Jeans: €43.33.
  • A stronger currency: exports dearer abroad, imports cheaper at home.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. The exchange rate is 1 pound = 1.20 US dollars. A US-made guitar costs 600 dollars. What does it cost a buyer in the UK, ignoring other costs?
Mission 4

Real data: the euro, 2021 to 2022

  • The euro averaged $1.1830 in 2021 and $1.0534 in 2022 (about $1.18 and $1.05).
  • % change = (new rate − old rate) ÷ old rate × 100.
  • (1.0534 − 1.1830) ÷ 1.1830 × 100 = −11.0%. The euro depreciated against the dollar.
  • No government announced it, and no committee voted for it: the euro floats.
  • A €100 hotel room cost a US visitor $118.30 at the 2021 average rate, but only $105.34 at the 2022 average.
Mission 4

Who buys and sells a currency?

  • Currencies are traded worldwide, around the clock, mainly through banks. Traders, firms, tourists, investors and central banks all buy and sell.
  • Demand for a currency (e.g. the pound) comes from people abroad who need it:
    • to buy the country's exports of goods and services, including tourist trips
    • to invest in the country: build factories, buy shares, save in its banks.
  • Supply of a currency comes from its own people selling it:
    • to buy imports
    • to invest abroad.
  • Reasons for buying a currency:
    • trade: paying for exports
    • speculation: hoping to sell it later at a higher price
    • intervention: a central bank buys its own currency to raise its value
    • profits: firms that earn profits abroad change them into their home currency
    • remittances: workers abroad send money to their families
    • investment: buying firms, shares and bonds.
Mission 4

The currency diagram

  • Price of the currency on the vertical axis; quantity of the currency on the horizontal.
  • Demand slopes down: a cheaper currency makes the country's goods cheaper abroad, so more is wanted.
  • Supply slopes up: a dearer currency makes imports cheaper at home, so people sell more of it.
  • Equilibrium where demand = supply: the exchange rate.

Mission 4

Worked example: an exporter and a stronger currency

Illustrative data. A UK firm sells a pair of boots for £100.

  • At £1 = $1.25: price in the US = 100 × 1.25 = $125.
  • The pound appreciates to £1 = $1.50: price in the US = $150.
  • US buyers face a 20% higher price: (150 − 125) ÷ 125 × 100 = 20%. They buy fewer.
  • The firm can keep its £100 price and sell less, or cut its pound price to stay competitive and earn less per pair.
  • Its leather comes from the US: $50 cost £40 at $1.25, but only £33.33 at $1.50. So a stronger pound also cuts some costs.
Quick check
?A country's currency depreciates sharply. Which group is most likely to gain?
Mission 4

Weigh it up: is a strong currency good news?

It depends on:

  • Who you are: importers and travellers gain from a strong currency; exporters gain from a weak one.
  • How much buyers react: if foreign buyers barely change what they buy, exporters lose little when prices rise.
  • Imported inputs: firms that import materials gain some of their loss back.
  • Time: contracts are often fixed for months, so effects build up slowly.
  • Inflation: a weak currency raises import prices, which can push up inflation.
  • Say: "the pound appreciated against the dollar" — not: "the exchange rate went up", which depends on which way the rate is written.
Mission check

?Foreign demand for Country Z's wine rises sharply. Nothing else changes. What happens to Z's currency, and to the price of imported goods in Z?

Every change in a floating rate starts with a shift: who wants more of the currency, and who wants to sell more of it?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Governments aim for growth, full employment, low and stable inflation and a sustainable balance of payments; also fairer incomes and sustainability.
  • Boosting spending can cut unemployment and raise growth, but near full capacity it adds to inflation and pulls in imports.
  • Growth can harm the environment; raising capacity can ease some conflicts, but slowly.

Mission 2

  • Governments raise money mainly from direct and indirect taxes, and spend it on welfare, health, education, defence, infrastructure and debt interest.
  • Progressive taxes take a rising percentage of income; regressive ones a falling percentage.
  • Deficit: spending > revenue, so the government borrows and its debt grows. Surplus: revenue > spending.
  • Fiscal policy uses spending and taxes to steer the economy, with costs: debt interest, time lags and opportunity cost.

Mission 3

  • The central bank (UK: the Bank of England's MPC) sets the interest rate to keep inflation near target.
  • Higher rates: borrowing dearer, saving more rewarding, spending slows, inflation slows; output and jobs may suffer.
  • Other tools: money supply, asset purchases (QE), the exchange rate.
  • Rates work with time lags and work less well against rising costs or low confidence.

Mission 4

  • A floating exchange rate is set by demand for and supply of a currency.
  • Demand comes from foreigners buying exports and investing in the country; supply from residents buying imports and investing abroad.
  • Higher interest rates, more exports or more investment inflows raise demand: the currency appreciates.
  • A stronger currency makes imports cheaper and exports dearer; a weaker one does the reverse.
