Mission 1

Genetic diversity

  • Genetic diversity: the variety of alleles within a species or population.
  • Every member of a species has the same genes. They differ in which alleles they carry.
  • Eduqas: the variety of alleles in the gene pool of a population.
  • Small, isolated or captive populations often have low genetic diversity.
  • Say: "different alleles of the same genes" — not: "different genes".
Quick check
?Cheetahs (Acinonyx jubatus) show extremely little genetic variation: unrelated cheetahs are so alike that skin grafts between them are not rejected. In a reserve, cheetah numbers have been stable for 20 years, and the species present and their relative abundances have not changed. Which statement about biodiversity in the reserve is correct?
Mission 1

Proportion of polymorphic loci

  • A locus is the position of a gene on a chromosome.
  • Polymorphic locus: two or more alleles are present in the population. Monomorphic: only one.
  • Proportion of polymorphic gene loci = number of polymorphic loci ÷ total number of loci
  • Illustrative example: a captive population; 30 loci tested; 9 polymorphic.
  • 9 ÷ 30 = 0.30 (30%).
  • Higher proportion = more genetic diversity. Useful for zoos, rare breeds and pedigree animals.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. In a rare breed of sheep, 40 gene loci were tested. 6 were polymorphic and 34 were monomorphic. What is the proportion of polymorphic gene loci?
Mission 1

From features to sequences

  • Genetic diversity can be compared by:
  • the frequency of observable or measurable characteristics;
  • the base sequence of DNA or of mRNA;
  • the amino-acid sequence of the protein coded for.
  • Fewer differences = more closely related.
  • Gene technology changed the method: inferring DNA differences from characteristics has been replaced by sequencing DNA directly.
  • Why: characteristics are also affected by the environment, and one characteristic may be controlled by many genes.
  • DNA fingerprinting compares band patterns between individuals; sequencing reads the bases themselves.
  • Say: "compare base sequences" — a base change may not change the amino acid (the code is degenerate), so proteins can hide DNA differences.
Mission 1

Why genetic diversity matters

  • Environments change: new diseases, new climates, new chemicals.
  • High genetic diversity: some individuals are more likely to carry alleles that let them survive. The population persists.
  • Low genetic diversity: if one is susceptible, nearly all are. The population can crash.
  • Example: California condors fell to 22 birds in 1982. All living condors descend from the 27 taken into captivity by 1987.
  • Say: "the population is more likely to contain resistant individuals" — not: "each individual is more resistant".
Quick checkRead the data
?Real data (Zhu et al. 2000). In Yunnan, China, farmers planted rows of disease-susceptible rice varieties among disease-resistant varieties in every rice field of 5 townships (1998) and 10 townships (1999). Plots of the susceptible varieties grown alone (monoculture) were the comparison.
Which conclusion is best supported?
  • Rice blast (a fungal disease) on the susceptible varieties: 94% less severe in mixtures.
  • Yield of the susceptible varieties: 89% greater in mixtures.
Mission check

?Almost all bananas in international trade are one variety, Cavendish. Every plant is grown from a piece of another plant, so all are genetically identical. A fungal wilt disease that kills Cavendish plants is spreading between plantations. Why are biologists concerned for the whole crop?

Numbers do not protect a population; variety does. Genetic diversity is a population's insurance against change.

Mission 2

PCR: copy one stretch of DNA

  • PCR (polymerase chain reaction) copies one chosen stretch of DNA, in a tube.
  • It needs:
    • the template DNA sample
    • two primers: short single strands of DNA
    • free DNA nucleotides
    • a heat-stable DNA polymerase
  • The usual enzyme is Taq polymerase.
  • The cycle repeats, about 25–35 times. Each cycle doubles the target.
Mission 2

One cycle, three steps

  • 1 Denature, about 95 °C. Hydrogen bonds between bases break. The strands separate.
  • 2 Anneal, about 55 °C. Primers bind by complementary base pairing to each end of the target, one on each strand.
  • 3 Extend, about 72 °C. Taq polymerase adds nucleotides to each primer, building new complementary strands.
  • The new strands become templates in the next cycle.
  • Say: "heat breaks the hydrogen bonds between strands" — not: "heat breaks the DNA into nucleotides".
Mission 2

Why primers? Why a heat-stable enzyme?

  • DNA polymerase can only add to an existing strand. Primers give it a start.
  • The two primers sit either side of the target. Only the DNA between them is amplified.
  • Primers also stop the separated strands re-pairing at once.
  • Taq polymerase comes from Thermus aquaticus, a bacterium of hot springs.
  • It is not denatured at 95 °C, so it works through every cycle. A human DNA polymerase would be denatured in the first cycle.
  • 72 °C is close to Taq's optimum temperature.
  • Say: "primers anneal to complementary sequences" — not: "primers copy the DNA" or "primers separate the strands".
Quick check
?Why is Taq polymerase, rather than a DNA polymerase from human cells, used in the polymerase chain reaction?
Mission 2

Worked example: how many copies?

  • Every molecule is copied in every cycle, so the number doubles each cycle.
  • Copies after n cycles = starting number × 2ⁿ.
  • Example: 1 molecule, 30 cycles.
    • 1 × 2³⁰ = 1 073 741 824
    • About 1.1 × 10⁹ copies: over a billion.
  • Example: 5 molecules, 3 cycles: 5 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 5 × 2³ = 40.
  • This assumes every strand is copied each cycle. In a real tube, copying slows in later cycles as primers and nucleotides are used up.
  • Say: "multiply by 2ⁿ" — not: "multiply by 2 × n".
Quick check
?A PCR reaction starts with 3 double-stranded DNA molecules of the target section. How many double-stranded molecules of the target are present after 4 complete cycles?
Mission 2

Gel electrophoresis: sorting by length

  • DNA samples go into wells at one end of a gel. A voltage is applied across the gel.
  • DNA is negatively charged (its phosphate groups). Every fragment moves towards the positive electrode.
  • The gel is a mesh. Shorter fragments slip through more easily and travel further.
  • Fragments of the same length travel the same distance: they form one band.
  • A ladder of fragments of known length runs in one lane, for comparison.
  • Say: "shorter fragments travel further" — not: "longer fragments travel further because they carry more charge".
Quick check
?Three DNA fragments of 200, 500 and 1200 base pairs are loaded into the same well of a gel and a voltage is applied. Which statement describes the result?
Mission 2Practical

Running a DNA gel

  • Method:
    • Cast an agarose gel with a comb; remove the comb to leave wells.
    • Cover the gel with buffer. Wells at the negative end.
    • Load each sample with a dense loading dye. Load a ladder in one lane.
    • Run at a set voltage until the dye nears the far end. Stain to show the bands.
  • Keep the same for every lane: gel, voltage, run time. The ladder is the reference.
  • Measure each band's distance from its well. Compare with the ladder to estimate length.
Mission 2Read the data

Sizing a fragment

Illustrative data. A ladder and one PCR product were run on the same gel.

Ladder band / base pairs1000500300200100
Distance from well / mm12.022.830.837.248.0
  • The PCR product's band is 27.0 mm from its well.
  • Question: between which two ladder bands does it lie, and about how long is it?
  • Answer: between the 500 and 300 base pair bands.
  • For a closer estimate, plot log10(length) against distance: the ladder gives a straight line. At 27.0 mm, length ≈ 380 base pairs.
  • Distance is not proportional to length. Use the ladder, never a ruler-based ratio.
Mission 2

What PCR and gels are used for

  • Forensics: amplify DNA from a hair, a drop of blood or skin cells, then compare it (see Mission 3).
  • Paternity and relationships: compare amplified regions between people, animals or plants.
  • Diagnosing infections: detect a pathogen's DNA or RNA, even at low levels.
  • Genetic testing: amplify a gene, then test it for a particular allele.
  • Ancient and degraded DNA: amplify the little that survives.
  • Before sequencing: make enough copies of a gene to read its base sequence.
Mission check

?The human ACE gene has two common alleles. Allele I contains an extra stretch of about 300 base pairs that allele D lacks. One pair of primers on either side of this stretch gives a 490 base pair product from allele I and a 190 base pair product from allele D. After PCR and gel electrophoresis, one person's lane shows two bands. Which interpretation is correct?

Primers pick the stretch to copy; the gel sorts the copies by length. Together they turn a trace of DNA into a genotype.

Mission 3

Mission 3: If almost all human DNA is shared, how can a trace of it point to just one person?

Make a guess: If almost all human DNA is shared, how can a trace of it point to just one person?

Mission 3

Repeats that vary between people

  • Much of the genome does not code for proteins. Some of it is tandem repeats: a short sequence repeated end to end, e.g. GATA GATA GATA.
  • Forensic profiles use short tandem repeats (STRs): units of 2–6 bases.
  • The number of repeats at a locus varies between people. More repeats: a longer fragment.
  • You inherit one allele at each locus from each parent: one or two bands per locus.
  • Many loci, many alleles at each: the chance that two people share all of them is very low. Identical twins are the exception.
Mission 3

Making a profile

  • The method is called genetic fingerprinting.
  • 1 Extract DNA from the sample.
  • 2 Amplify several repeat loci by PCR. Primers bind either side of each repeat.
  • 3 Separate the fragments by length: electrophoresis.
  • 4 Detect the bands: labelled primers or labelled probes make them visible. Compare samples.
Quick check
?Two people have different alleles at one short tandem repeat locus. After PCR and gel electrophoresis, their bands for this locus are in different places. What difference between the alleles does the gel detect?
Mission 3

Worked example: why test many loci?

  • Illustrative: suppose 1 in 10 unrelated people share a profile at one locus by chance.
  • Loci on different chromosomes are inherited independently. Multiply the chances.
  • 2 loci: 1/10 × 1/10 = 1 in 100.
  • 10 loci: (1/10)¹⁰ = 1 in 10¹⁰: about one person in 10 000 000 000.
  • Real casework: the UK system tests 16 repeat loci; the US core set has 20.
  • Relatives share more alleles, so their chance of matching is higher.
  • Say: "the chance of a coincidental match is very low" — not: "the profile proves the suspect did it".
Quick check
?A forensic laboratory compares a sample from a crime scene with a suspect's DNA using 20 different markers rather than one. Why does this make the result more reliable?
Mission 3

Reading a profile

  • Forensic match: the sample could come from a person only if every band matches.
  • One band different: that person is excluded.
  • Paternity: each band in a child came from the mother or the father.
    • Cross out the child's bands that match the mother.
    • Every band left must be in the father's profile.
    • The father has other bands the child did not inherit: that is expected.
  • Matching bands are consistent with a relationship. Different bands exclude one.
Quick check
?A DNA profile was prepared for a mother, her child and a man who may be the father. The fragment lengths (in base pairs) in each profile were: mother 100, 220, 260, 340; child 100, 150, 220, 300; man 150, 180, 300, 330. What can be concluded?
Mission 3

Uses: identity and relationships

  • Forensics: link a sample from a scene to a person, or exclude a suspect.
  • Paternity and family relationships; identifying human remains.
  • Animals and plants: confirm the parents of a pedigree animal; identify a crop variety; trace the source of illegally traded wildlife.
Mission 3

Uses: health and populations

  • Genetic variability of a population: more different bands across individuals means more alleles: higher genetic diversity.
  • So fingerprinting assesses biodiversity at a molecular level, within and between populations.
Mission check

?A captive breeding programme needs to know which of two males fathered a chick. Profiles at three repeat loci (fragment lengths in base pairs; illustrative data):

Same logic as a crime scene: one mismatch excludes; matches at many loci make a coincidence very unlikely.

Mission 4

Mission 4: If no animal can change its own genes, how can a whole population change over time?

Make a guess: If no animal can change its own genes, how can a whole population change over time?

Mission 4

Where variation comes from

  • Individuals in a population differ in phenotype.
  • Causes: genes, environment, and both together.
  • Mutation is the original source of all new alleles. It is random: it does not happen because an allele is needed.
  • Meiosis and random fertilisation make new combinations of existing alleles. They make no new alleles.
  • Say: "a mutation arose by chance" — not: "the organism mutated to survive".
Quick check
?A population of a plant species has two alleles of a flower-colour gene. A third allele, never seen before, appears in one seedling. Which process could have produced it?
Mission 4

More young than the habitat can support

  • Populations overproduce: far more offspring than can survive.
  • Resources are limited: food, water, nest sites, territory, light.
  • So members of the same species compete (intraspecific competition).
  • Competition between and within species affects breeding success and survival.
  • Selection pressures decide who survives and breeds:
    • biotic: predation, disease, competition;
    • abiotic: e.g. extreme cold, drought.
  • Eduqas calls these selective agencies: food supply, breeding sites, climate, human impact.
Mission 4

Natural selection, step by step

  • (1) Variation: individuals differ in a heritable trait.
  • (2) Selection pressure: e.g. a predator that finds pale beetles more easily on dark bark.
  • (3) Differential survival and reproduction: individuals with the advantageous phenotype are more likely to survive and leave more offspring.
  • (4) Inheritance: they pass on the advantageous allele. Over many generations its frequency rises.
  • No individual changed. The proportions in the population changed.
Mission 4

Fitness

  • Fitness is about reproductive success: surviving offspring that go on to breed.
  • Not size, strength or speed. Those count only if they lead to more offspring.
  • A phenotype that raises fitness in one environment may lower it in another.
  • Say: "leaves more surviving offspring" — not: "is the fittest because strongest".
Quick check
?In a population of red deer, stag X is the largest and strongest male but leaves two offspring that survive to breed. Stag Y is smaller and weaker but leaves six offspring that survive to breed. Which stag has the higher fitness, and why?
Mission 4

Only heritable traits evolve

  • Selection changes a population only if the trait is heritable: coded in the base sequence of DNA and passed on in gametes.
  • Acquired characteristics are not inherited: trained muscles, an injury, a learned skill.
  • Darwin's theory: existing species arose by modification of ancestral species by natural selection (how species split: EV05).
Quick check
?A woman develops large, strong arm muscles through years of rowing training. Will her children inherit larger arm muscles as a result of her training?
Mission 4

Evolution: a change in allele frequency

  • Evolution = a change in the allele frequencies of a population over time.
  • Gene pool: all the genes, and all their alleles, in a population.
  • Over long periods, adaptation and selection (with isolation) produce new species and the diversity of life.
Mission 4

Worked example: allele frequency

Illustrative data: a gene with alleles D and d, 100 individuals (200 alleles).

GenotypeDDDddd
Generation 143264
Generation 10164836
  • Count alleles, not individuals. Each DD has two D; each Dd has one.
  • Generation 1: D = 2 × 4 + 32 = 40 of 200. Frequency of D = 0.20.
  • Generation 10: D = 2 × 16 + 48 = 80 of 200. Frequency of D = 0.40.
  • The allele frequency changed: the population evolved.
  • Trap: 36% of generation 1 carry D, but D's frequency is 0.20.
Quick check
?A population of 50 plants contains 8 RR, 24 Rr and 18 rr. What is the frequency of allele R?
Mission check

?A student builds a flowchart model of how natural selection increased the mean beak depth of a population of a hypothetical seed-eating bird species over several generations of drought. Which flowchart best represents this process?

No bird changed its genes. The population changed because some birds left more offspring than others.

Make a guess: Nobody designs a drug-resistant germ, so how do whole populations of them appear so quickly?

Mission 5

How a resistant population arises

  • (1) A random mutation gives a few bacteria an allele for resistance, before any antibiotic is used. Bacteria can also gain resistance genes on plasmids from other bacteria.
  • (2) The antibiotic kills susceptible bacteria. Resistant ones survive.
  • (3) Survivors reproduce fast (binary fission) and pass on the resistance allele.
  • (4) Over generations the frequency of the allele rises: the population is now mostly resistant.
  • Say: "the antibiotic selected resistant bacteria" — not: "the bacteria became resistant".
Mission 5

Read the data: was resistance there first?

Lederberg and Lederberg (1952): replica plating with the bacterium E. coli.

  • (1) Bacteria spread on a master plate with no antibiotic. Each cell grows into a colony.
  • (2) A sterile velvet pad picks up cells from every colony and presses them onto plates with the antibiotic, keeping their positions.
  • (3) A few colonies grew on the antibiotic plates, at the same positions on every replica.
  • Cells taken from those positions on the master plate, never exposed to the drug, already included resistant cells.
  • So resistance arose before exposure. The drug selected it; it did not cause it.
Quick check
?In a replica-plating test, colonies grow on the antibiotic plate only at the positions of colonies 4 and 11 on the master plate, which has no antibiotic. Cells are then taken from colony 4 on the master plate and placed in a broth containing the antibiotic. What result is expected, and why?
Mission 5

Many drugs, many pests

  • Resistance to a drug usually depends on a specific gene or mutation. Strains can accumulate several: multiresistant bacteria, e.g. MRSA.
  • Every use of an antibiotic is a selection pressure. More use, more selection.
  • Overuse (e.g. for viral infections, which antibiotics cannot treat) has spread resistance among pathogenic bacteria.
  • Careful use slows resistance: prescribe only when needed, the right drug, good infection control.
Quick check
?A hospital ward uses one particular antibiotic heavily. Over several years, an increasing proportion of the bacteria isolated from patients on the ward are found to be resistant to it. Which statement correctly explains this change?
Mission 5

Read the data: owls and snow

Tawny owls (Strix aluco) in Finland, studied 1981–2008 (Karell and colleagues, 2011).

  • Two colour forms: grey and brown. Colour is strongly inherited (about 80% heritable).
  • In winters with deep snow, brown owls survived less well than grey owls.
  • Over the study, snowy winters became less common as the climate warmed.
  • The proportion of brown owls rose.
  • Reading: less snow → brown owls' fitness rose → alleles for brown plumage rose in frequency. Evolution driven by climate change.
Quick check
?In Finland, tawny owls (Strix aluco) occur as a grey morph and a brown morph, and the colour is inherited. Brown owls survived snowy winters less well than grey owls. Between 1981 and 2008 winters became milder with less snow, and the proportion of brown owls rose markedly. What best explains the change?
Mission check

?A patient is given an antibiotic. Within a few days, the bacteria in the patient's infection change from almost all susceptible to almost all resistant. Which statement best explains how this happened so quickly?

Germs, insects, owls: the same rule. The allele was there first; the environment decided who left offspring.

Mission 6: A desert fox has enormous ears and almost never drinks. How did it end up so well suited to the desert?

Mission 6

What is an adaptation?

  • An adaptation is an inherited feature that increases an organism's chance of surviving and reproducing in its environment.
  • It arises by natural selection: random variation exists; individuals with a useful allele survive and breed more; the allele becomes more common over generations.
  • Individuals do not adapt in their lifetime. Populations change over generations.
  • An adaptation helps in one environment; the same trait may be useless or harmful in another.
  • Say: "individuals with the allele survived and bred more" — not: "the fox adapted because it needed to".
Mission 6

Three kinds of adaptation

  • Anatomical: body structure. Fennec fox: huge ears with blood vessels near the surface radiate heat.
  • Physiological: body processes. Fennec fox: kidneys conserve water; it gets most of its water from food.
  • Behavioural: what it does. Fennec fox: active at night; shelters in a burrow by day.
  • Most organisms show all three, each meeting the same challenge.
Quick check
?Which adaptation is classified correctly?
Quick check
?A hypothetical grass species grows both on soil contaminated with zinc around an old mine and in nearby uncontaminated fields. When grown on zinc soil, plants from the mine site survive, but plants from the fields die. When grown on normal soil, plants from the mine site grow more slowly than plants from the fields. Which reasoning best explains why zinc tolerance is common at the mine site but rare in the fields?
Mission check

?Arctic foxes have much smaller ears than fennec foxes. Which statement best explains how small ears became typical of Arctic foxes?

The fennec's big ears and the Arctic fox's small ones arose the same way: selection acting on inherited variation, in different environments.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Genetic diversity is the variety of alleles in a population; high diversity makes it more likely that some individuals survive a change.
  • Proportion of polymorphic loci = polymorphic loci ÷ total loci.

Mission 2

  • PCR repeats denature, anneal and extend; primers mark the target and each cycle doubles it (starting number × 2ⁿ).
  • Taq polymerase survives 95 °C; annealing about 55 °C; extension about 72 °C.
  • Gel electrophoresis: DNA moves towards the positive electrode; shorter fragments travel further.

Mission 3

  • Repeat loci vary in length between individuals; PCR copies them and a gel sorts them by length.
  • Chances at independent loci multiply, so more loci make a chance match less likely.
  • One band that cannot be explained excludes; matching bands are consistent with, not proof of, identity or relationship.

Mission 4

  • Random mutation supplies new alleles; selection pressures make individuals with advantageous heritable phenotypes leave more offspring, so those alleles rise in frequency.
  • Evolution is a change in allele frequencies; fitness is reproductive success.

Mission 5

  • Resistance alleles arise by random mutation (or gene transfer) before exposure; the drug or pesticide kills susceptible individuals, so resistant ones reproduce and the allele's frequency rises.

Mission 6

  • An adaptation is an inherited feature, favoured by natural selection, that improves survival and reproduction in a particular environment.
  • Adaptations are anatomical (or morphological), physiological or behavioural.
