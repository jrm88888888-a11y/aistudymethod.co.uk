Islam: Sacred places

This mini-lesson covers Section I3 — Sacred places: places of pilgrimage and of religious importance: why Muslims go on Hajj, what they do at each stage and what it means, how 'Umrah compares, and why Makkah and Madinah matter historically.

Cambridge asks for the meaning and importance of each place and action, not just a travel itinerary. Every screen here gives you both. Press Start.

I3.1 · the importance of Hajj

Why go on Hajj?

Hajj is the fifth pillar: the pilgrimage to Makkah, obligatory once in a lifetime for every Muslim who is physically able and financially able — and who can do it without leaving dependants in hardship or going into debt. Those unable are not blamed; intention counts.

Spiritual goals

  • Obedience — it is a command of God and a pillar of Islam.
  • Forgiveness and renewal — pilgrims hope to return with sins forgiven, "as on the day they were born", and many describe Hajj as a spiritual restart.
  • Following the prophets — the pilgrim walks in the footsteps of Ibrahim, Hajar and Isma'il, and of Muhammad (pbuh).
  • Rehearsal for the Day of Judgement — standing in white on the plain of 'Arafat, stripped of rank, is often described as a preview of standing before God.

Physical goals and effects

  • Unity of the ummah — millions of Muslims of every nation, colour and class in one place, doing exactly the same things. Malcolm X famously wrote that Hajj transformed his view of race.
  • Self-discipline — heat, crowds, exhaustion, sleeping in tents and on open ground: the body is made to serve the soul.
  • A changed life afterwards — many take the title Hajji, and describe returning more patient, more generous, more committed to prayer.

I3.2 · ihram

Entering ihram

Before crossing into the sacred area, pilgrims enter ihram — both a state and a dress.

  • The dress. Men wear two plain white unsewn sheets. Women wear ordinary modest clothing, usually plain, with face and hands uncovered.
  • The state. Having washed and declared the intention (niyyah), the pilgrim announces the talbiyah — "Here I am, O God, here I am" — and accepts restrictions: no perfume, no cutting hair or nails, no hunting or harming living creatures, no sexual relations, no arguing or quarrelling.

The meaning — this is the classic exam answer. Identical white cloth removes every marker of wealth, nationality and status: a king and a labourer look the same. It expresses the equality of all before God and the unity of the ummah. The plain white also recalls the shroud in which a Muslim is buried, so the pilgrim is dressed for death and judgement. The restrictions strip away vanity, comfort and conflict, so that nothing is left but God.

I3.2 · Makkah, the Ka'bah, tawaf, sa'y, Zamzam

At the Ka'bah

The Ka'bah is the cube-shaped building at the centre of the Grand Mosque in Makkah, draped in black cloth. Muslims believe it was built or rebuilt by Ibrahim and Isma'il as the first house dedicated to the worship of the one God. It is the qiblah: the point the whole ummah faces five times a day.

  • Tawaf — circling the Ka'bah seven times anticlockwise, reciting prayers, trying (if possible) to touch or point to the Black Stone. Its meaning: God is the centre, and the believer's whole life orbits Him. The unbroken circling of a vast crowd is a picture of the ummah in motion.
  • Sa'y — hurrying seven times between the hills of Safa and Marwa. This re-enacts the desperate search of Hajar, left in the barren valley with her baby Isma'il, running between the hills looking for water. Its meaning: trust in God combined with human effort — she did not sit and wait, she ran. Muslims also note that a central rite of Hajj commemorates the struggle of a woman.
  • Zamzam — the well that Muslims believe God caused to spring up for Hajar and Isma'il. Pilgrims drink from it and take some home. Its meaning: God provides, and provision may come after the hardest effort.

I3.2 · 'Arafat, the Mount of Mercy, Muzdalifah

The day of 'Arafat

On the 8th of Dhul-Hijjah pilgrims travel to Mina, a valley of tents, and stay overnight. The next day is the heart of the whole pilgrimage.

  • The stand at 'Arafat (wuquf). On the 9th, pilgrims gather on the plain of 'Arafat from midday until sunset, standing, praying, reciting the Qur'an and begging forgiveness. Hajj is not valid without it.
  • The Mount of Mercy (Jabal ar-Rahmah) is the small hill on that plain, near where Muslims believe the Prophet (pbuh) delivered his Farewell Sermon, teaching that no Arab is superior to a non-Arab, and that Muslims' lives and property are sacred to one another.
  • Muzdalifah. After sunset the pilgrims move on to Muzdalifah, pray, collect pebbles, and sleep in the open under the sky with no tents.

The meaning. 'Arafat is often described as a rehearsal of the Day of Judgement: a vast crowd in white, standing under the sun, with nothing but their deeds and their pleas. Muslims believe God is nearest and most forgiving on this day. Sleeping on the bare ground at Muzdalifah continues the lesson — no comfort, no status, nothing between the pilgrim and God.

I3.2 · Mina, the jamarat, completing Hajj

Mina: stoning, sacrifice, and coming out of ihram

On the 10th of Dhul-Hijjah, pilgrims return to Mina.

  • Stoning the pillars (jamarat). Pebbles are thrown at the largest pillar, and on the following days at all three. The pillars mark the places where, Muslims believe, Shaytan tempted Ibrahim to disobey God's command — and Ibrahim drove him away. The pilgrim is rejecting temptation and evil, and doing so with the body, not just the mind. Because of dangerous crowding in the past, the pillars are now walls with a multi-storey bridge and timed groups — a good example of religious practice adapting to keep people safe.
  • The sacrifice (qurbani). An animal is sacrificed, remembering the ram God provided in place of Ibrahim's son. The meat is distributed, much of it to the poor. Most pilgrims now pay for this to be done for them, and the meat is frozen and shipped to poor countries.
  • Hair cutting. Men shave or cut the hair; women cut a small lock. The pilgrim then leaves the state of ihram.
  • Tawaf al-ifadah back in Makkah, and sa'y — then the remaining days in Mina, and a farewell tawaf before leaving.

I3.1 · challenges and effects

The challenges of the journey

Cambridge specifically asks about the challenges and effects of the journey itself. Do not romanticise Hajj — say what is hard about it.

  • Physical. Extreme heat; enormous crowds; miles of walking; sleeping in tents and on open ground; illness spreading; risk of being crushed at bottlenecks. Older and disabled pilgrims may need wheelchairs pushed for miles.
  • Practical. Great expense — many Muslims save for decades. Visas are limited by quota, so some wait years. Travellers must arrange time off work and care for dependants at home.
  • Emotional and spiritual. Patience is tested by queues, noise and exhaustion — while in ihram, arguing is forbidden. Some pilgrims are disappointed to find the experience commercialised or crowded rather than serene; others feel unworthy or overwhelmed.

The effects. Precisely because it is hard, pilgrims describe it as transformative: a sense of forgiveness and a clean slate; profound solidarity with Muslims of every background; renewed commitment to prayer, charity and honesty; and pride in the title Hajji. Many say the hardship was the point — that it re-ordered what they thought mattered.

I3.3 · 'Umrah

'Umrah — the lesser pilgrimage

Similarities. 'Umrah is performed in Makkah, in a state of ihram, and includes tawaf around the Ka'bah, sa'y between Safa and Marwa, and the cutting of the hair. Pilgrims usually drink Zamzam water. The spiritual aims — devotion, forgiveness, focus on God — are much the same.

Differences.

  • Obligation: Hajj is a pillar and obligatory once in a lifetime if able. 'Umrah is voluntary (highly recommended, and regarded by some scholars as obligatory once if able, but it is not one of the five pillars).
  • Time: Hajj must be performed on fixed days of Dhul-Hijjah. 'Umrah can be done at any time of year, and can be repeated.
  • Rites: 'Umrah does not include 'Arafat, Muzdalifah, Mina, the stoning of the jamarat, or the sacrifice. It can be completed in a few hours.
  • Scale: Hajj brings millions together at once; 'Umrah is smaller and calmer.

Exam trap: "'Umrah is just a short Hajj" scores little. The precise point is that 'Umrah lacks the stand at 'Arafat — and without the stand at 'Arafat there is no Hajj at all.

I3.4 · Makkah and Madinah

Two cities of historic importance

Makkah

  • The birthplace of Prophet Muhammad (pbuh), and the city where the first revelations came.
  • Home of the Ka'bah, linked to Ibrahim and Isma'il, and the qiblah for all Muslims.
  • The place of the persecution of the early Muslims — boycott, torture, exile — and therefore of the courage of the first believers.
  • The city the Prophet (pbuh) left in the hijrah, and later returned to, clearing the Ka'bah of idols and restoring it to the worship of the one God.

Madinah

  • The destination of the hijrah in 622 CE — the city that welcomed the Prophet (pbuh) and invited him to lead.
  • The place where the first Muslim community was established: a society with law, welfare and public worship, not merely a set of private beliefs.
  • The site of the first mosque built by the Prophet (pbuh); his mosque there, Masjid an-Nabawi, remains one of the holiest places in Islam.
  • The place where the Prophet (pbuh) died and is buried. Many pilgrims visit Madinah before or after Hajj — although this visit is not part of Hajj itself, an easy mark to lose.

I3 Sacred places — the big ideas

I3.1 Why: a pillar, once in a lifetime if able · forgiveness, obedience, following Ibrahim and Hajar, unity of the ummah · challenges: heat, crowds, cost, patience

I3.2 How: ihram (equality; the shroud) → Mina → 'Arafat: the stand (Hajj is invalid without it) → Muzdalifah (pebbles, open sky) → Mina (jamarat, qurbani, hair) → Makkah (tawaf, sa'y, Zamzam) → farewell tawaf
