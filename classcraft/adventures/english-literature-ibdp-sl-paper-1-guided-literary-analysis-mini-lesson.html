Mission 1

An unseen passage and the clock running. Why not start writing at once?

Make a guess: An unseen passage and the clock running. Why not start writing at once?

IB Paper 1 · reading an unseen passageScreen 1

What Paper 1 puts in front of you

  • Two unseen passages, each with a guiding question.
  • They are literary passages, each from a different form: prose fiction, prose non-fiction, poetry or drama.
  • At SL you choose one.
  • You write a guided analysis: an argued reading of how the text makes meaning, not a summary of it.
IB Paper 1 · reading an unseen passageScreen 2

How your exam asks this

  • Paper 1, Guided literary analysis. SL: 1 hour 15 minutes, one of the two passages, 20 marks, 35% of your grade.
  • Each analysis is marked on four criteria, 5 marks each: A Understanding and interpretation, B Analysis and evaluation, C Focus and organization, D Language.
  • From May 2026 the passages carry no line numbers: point to the words themselves by quoting them.
IB Paper 1 · reading an unseen passageScreen 3

Three reads, then a plan

ReadAsk
1 · GistWhat is happening?
2 · The questionRe-read with the guiding question. Mark four to six moments that answer it.
3 · PatternsWhere does it shift? What repeats, builds or contrasts?
PlanOne sentence that answers the question (your claim), then three or four points in order.
  • At read 1, also ask: who speaks or narrates, and to whom?
  • The criteria reward an argued, organised reading. That comes from the reading, not from writing faster.

Mission checkScreen 8

?An unseen passage and the clock running. Why not start writing at once?

Reading time is where the marks are made. Read once for the gist, once with the guiding question, once for shifts and patterns; then write one claim and three or four ordered points. The writing goes faster, and stays focused, because you know what you are arguing.

Mission 2

The paper gives you a guiding question. Do you have to use it?

Make a guess: The paper gives you a guiding question. Do you have to use it?

IB Paper 1 · the guiding question and a thesisScreen 1

What the guiding question does

  • Each passage has its own guiding question. It points you to a technical or formal aspect, e.g. narrative voice, imagery, structure, sound.
  • It is optional. You may choose your own point of entry, as long as it is another technical or formal aspect and your analysis stays focused.
  • Most students use it: it gives you a way in when time is short.
  • Either way, your answer needs a thesis.
IB Paper 1 · the guiding question and a thesisScreen 2

From a topic to a thesis

  • A thesis answers the question in one or two arguable sentences: what the text does, how, and to what effect.
  • A topic names a subject; a thesis makes a claim someone could disagree with.
  • Topic: ‘The passage is about memory.’ Thesis: ‘Woolf's free indirect style lets two mornings run together, so an errand in London becomes a meeting with Clarissa's past.’
  • A good thesis is a plan: each part becomes a paragraph.

Mission checkScreen 8

?The paper gives you a guiding question. Do you have to use it?

No. The guiding question is a way in, not a rule. Use it, or choose another technical or formal aspect, but turn your focus into a thesis: one arguable claim about how the text works and to what effect, which every paragraph then develops.

Mission 3

A poem, a scene, a page of prose: does form change what you look for?

Make a guess: A poem, a scene, a page of prose: does form change what you look for?

IB Paper 1 · literary formsScreen 1

Four forms, one paper

  • Paper 1's two passages come from different literary forms: prose fiction, prose non-fiction (memoir, essay, travel writing, letters), poetry, drama.
  • Some questions fit any form: voice (who speaks?), structure (where does it turn?), imagery, tone.
  • Each form also has tools of its own. Name the tool, then say what it does.
  • Choosing your passage? Pick the form whose tools you can use with confidence.
IB Paper 1 · literary formsScreen 2

A toolkit by form

FormLook for
Prose fictionnarrator and narrative perspective; whose mind we are in; free indirect style; time shifts; dialogue; pace
Prose non-fictionthe writer's persona; purpose; anecdote and reflection; how argument and description mix
Poetrythe speaker; line and stanza; enjambment and caesura; metre and rhyme; sound; the volta
Dramastage directions; dialogue and subtext; who holds power in an exchange; what an audience sees; dramatic irony
  • A drama passage is written to be performed: ask what an audience would see and hear.

Mission checkScreen 9

?A poem, a scene, a page of prose: does form change what you look for?

Yes, partly. Every passage gets the same core questions: who speaks, where it turns, what images and tone do. Then each form adds its own tools: the narrator in prose fiction, the persona in non-fiction, line and sound in poetry, stage directions and audience in drama.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Paper 1 gives you two unseen passages with guiding questions; you analyse one at SL and both at HL.
  • The passages are literary, each from a different form.
  • Read three times (gist, the guiding question, patterns), then plan one claim and three or four ordered points.
  • From May 2026 there are no line numbers: quote the words you mean.
  • The guiding question points to a technical or formal aspect; it is optional, but your own way in must be one too.
  • A thesis is an arguable claim about how a text works and to what effect; a topic only names a subject.
  • In Rossetti's "Remember", the volta turns a command to remember into permission to forget.
  • Paper 1 passages come from different literary forms; at HL you write on both, so you meet two forms in one sitting.
  • Use a shared core (voice, structure, imagery, tone), then the form's own tools.
  • In drama, stage directions and what an audience sees are evidence, as Wilde's silent business with the sandwiches shows.
  • In poetry, form can carry an argument: Dickinson states a paradox, then proves it.

Key quotations to learn

  • Inspects them, takes two, and sits down on the sofa. (Wilde, The Importance of Being Earnest, Act 1) A stage direction as evidence: class shown, not stated.
  • I keep science for Life. (Wilde, The Importance of Being Earnest, Act 1) An epigram: Wilde's wit turning idleness into a pose.
  • Success is counted sweetest (Dickinson, "Success" (1890)) A paradox that the poem then argues.
  • The distant strains of triumph (Dickinson, "Success" (1890)) Sound reaches the loser who cannot share the victory.
