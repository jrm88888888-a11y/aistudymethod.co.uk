Mission 1

Which ions atoms form

  • Metal atoms lose electrons to form positive ions: cations.
  • Non-metal atoms gain electrons to form negative ions: anions.
  • Group 1 and 2 metals, and aluminium, lose all their outer-shell electrons.
  • Non-metals gain enough electrons to fill the outer p sublevel.
  • Mg 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² loses two: Mg²⁺. N 1s² 2s² 2p³ gains three: N³⁻.
  • Charge = protons − electrons. The number of protons never changes.

Mission 1

Transition elements: more than one ion

  • Transition elements can form ions with different charges.
  • They lose their 4s electrons before 3d.
  • Cobalt, [Ar] 3d⁷ 4s²: Co²⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁷; Co³⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁶.
  • A Roman numeral gives the charge: cobalt(II) chloride, CoCl₂.

Mission 1

Ionic bonds, formulae and names

  • Ionic bond: electrostatic attraction between ions of opposite charge.
  • It acts in all directions: each ion attracts every oppositely charged neighbour.
  • Formula: combine ions so the total charge is zero. Mg²⁺ and N³⁻ → Mg₃N₂.
  • Name the cation first, then the anion ending in -ide: magnesium nitride.
  • Formula to name: MnO₂ has 2 × O²⁻ (−4), so Mn⁴⁺: manganese(IV) oxide.
  • Number prefixes (di-, tri-) are for covalent compounds, never ionic ones.

Mission 1

The ionic lattice

  • Ionic compounds are giant 3D lattices of alternating cations and anions.
  • There are no molecules, so the formula is empirical: the simplest ratio of ions.
  • Al₂O₃ means two Al³⁺ for every three O²⁻, not a molecule of five atoms.

Mission 1

Properties from the lattice

  • Low volatility, high melting point: every ion is held by many strong attractions.
  • Conducts only when molten or aqueous: then the ions are free to move.
  • The solid does not conduct: its ions are fixed, and there are no free electrons.
  • Often soluble in water: polar water molecules attract the ions out of the lattice.
  • Insoluble in non-polar solvents such as hexane. CaCO₃ and BaSO₄ don't dissolve in water either.

Mission 1

Lattice enthalpy: how strong the ionic bonding is

  • Lattice enthalpy, ΔH⦵lattice: the enthalpy change to separate 1 mol of ionic solid into gaseous ions.
  • MgO(s) → Mg²⁺(g) + O²⁻(g). Always positive: larger means stronger ionic bonding.
  • Larger with higher ion charges, and with smaller ion radii (centres closer).
  • MgO 3791, NaCl 790 kJ mol⁻¹ (data booklet). MgO's ions are doubly charged and smaller.
  • Same charges? Radius decides. When charges differ, charge usually has the bigger effect.

Mission 2

What a covalent bond is

  • Covalent bond: the electrostatic attraction of both nuclei for a shared pair of electrons.
  • The pair sits between the nuclei. Both pull on it, holding the atoms together.
  • Octet rule: atoms tend to end up with eight valence electrons. A tendency, not a law.
  • The octet is an outcome. The attraction is the bond.
Quick check

Mission 2

Drawing a Lewis formula

  • A Lewis formula shows every valence electron, bonding and lone pairs, as dots, crosses or lines.
  • 1. Count valence electrons: add one per negative charge, remove one per positive charge.
  • 2. Join the atoms with single bonds. 3. Complete the outer atoms' octets with lone pairs.
  • 4. Spare pairs go on the central atom; if short, make multiple bonds (not B, Be).
  • CO₂ has 16 valence electrons: O=C=O, two lone pairs on each O.
  • An ion goes in square brackets, charge outside: [O–H]⁻, three lone pairs on O.
Mission 2

Fewer than eight

  • Some central atoms have an incomplete octet.
  • BF₃: boron's three electrons make three bonds. Boron has 6 electrons, no lone pair.
  • BeCl₂: beryllium has only 4 electrons around it.
  • Count both electrons of every shared pair for each atom.

Mission 2

Coordination bonds

  • Coordination bond: a covalent bond whose shared pair comes wholly from one atom.
  • The donor has a lone pair. The acceptor has room for a pair.
  • H₂O + H⁺ → H₃O⁺: an O lone pair goes to H⁺. Arrow: O→H.
  • In CO, one of the three shared pairs comes from oxygen alone.
  • Once formed, it is identical to the others: all three O–H bonds in H₃O⁺ match.

Complex ions

  • Transition element ions form complex ions: a central metal ion surrounded by ligands.
  • A ligand donates a lone pair to the metal ion, forming a coordination bond.
  • [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺: six water ligands, each O donating a lone pair to Cu²⁺.
  • Common ligands: H₂O, NH₃, Cl⁻, CN⁻. Each has a lone pair to give.

Mission 2AHL

Formal charge

  • Formal charge: FC = V − N − ½B.
  • V: valence electrons of the free atom. N: non-bonding electrons. B: bonding electrons.
  • H₃O⁺, oxygen: V = 6, N = 2, B = 6.
  • So FC(O) = 6 − 2 − 3 = +1.
  • Each H: 1 − 0 − 1 = 0.
  • Formal charges add up to the species' overall charge: +1 here.
Mission 2AHL

Choosing the best Lewis formula

  • Several Lewis formulas can obey the octet rule. Formal charge picks the preferred one.
  • First: formal charges closest to zero.
  • If charges remain, put the negative one on the more electronegative atom.
  • OCS: S=C=O gives 0, 0, 0. S≡C–O gives +1, 0, −1.
  • So S=C=O is preferred.
  • Formal charge is not oxidation state: it splits every bonding pair equally.

Mission 3

Electron domains repel

  • An electron domain: a lone pair or a bond. A multiple bond is one domain.
  • Domains round a central atom get as far apart as possible: the VSEPR model.
  • 2 domains: linear, 180°. 3: trigonal planar, 120°. 4: tetrahedral, 109.5°.
  • BeCl₂ is linear, BF₃ trigonal planar, CH₄ tetrahedral.

What changes the angles

  • Lone pairs repel more strongly than bonding pairs, so they squeeze bond angles.
  • CH₄ 109.5° → NH₃ about 107° → H₂O about 104.5°.
  • A multiple bond holds more electron density, so it repels more than a single bond.
  • Ethene: the H–C–H angle is about 117°, a little under 120°.
  • Lewis formulas are drawn flat. They show the pairs, not the angles.

Mission 3AHL

Expanded octets

  • From period 3 on, a central atom can hold more than eight electrons: an expanded octet.
  • Period 2 atoms (C, N, O, F) never can.
  • PCl₅: five bonding pairs round P, 10 electrons. SF₆: six bonding pairs, 12.
  • Find lone pairs by counting. ClF₃ has 7 + 3 × 7 = 28 valence electrons.
  • Bonds (6) and F lone pairs (18) use 24. Four remain: two lone pairs on Cl.

Mission 4

Polar bonds

  • Electronegativity: how strongly an atom pulls a shared pair. Values are in the data booklet.
  • Unequal electronegativities: the pair is shared unequally. That is a polar bond.
  • The more electronegative atom is δ−, the other δ+. Or draw a dipole arrow.
  • H–Cl: H 2.2, Cl 3.2. Difference 1.0, δ− on Cl.
  • Bigger difference, more polar. Only the difference counts, not the size of the values.
Quick check

Mission 4

Polar molecules

  • A molecule is polar if its bond dipoles leave a net dipole moment.
  • Equal dipoles arranged symmetrically cancel: CO₂, BF₃ and CCl₄ are non-polar.
  • In H₂O and CH₂Cl₂ they do not cancel: both molecules are polar.
  • Always check both: are the bonds polar, and does the shape cancel them?

Mission 4

Forces between molecules

  • London (dispersion) forces act between all molecules. More electrons, more contact: stronger.
  • Dipole–dipole: between two polar molecules, such as two CH₃Cl molecules.
  • Dipole–induced dipole: a polar molecule polarizes a non-polar one, such as H₂O and O₂.
  • Van der Waals forces is the umbrella term for these three.
  • Hydrogen bond: H on N/O/F, attracted to a lone pair on N/O/F of another molecule.
  • Hydrogen bonds act between molecules. The O–H bond inside water is covalent.

Mission 4

Which force is strongest?

  • Only at comparable molar mass: London < dipole–dipole < hydrogen bonding.
  • O₂ (Mr 32), London only: boils at −183 °C.
  • H₂S (34), polar, adds dipole–dipole: −60 °C.
  • CH₃OH (32), adds hydrogen bonds: 65 °C.
  • Masses very different? London forces can win: octane (Mr 114, non-polar) boils at 126 °C.

Mission 4

Properties of molecular substances

  • Melting and boiling overcome forces between molecules. Covalent bonds stay intact.
  • Weak intermolecular forces: low melting and boiling points, so volatile.
  • No conduction: no ions, and every electron is held in a bond or lone pair.
  • Solubility: it dissolves if new solute–solvent attractions replace those broken.
  • Polar and hydrogen-bonding solutes dissolve in water; non-polar ones in non-polar solvents.

Mission 4

Chromatography and RF

  • Components separate by their relative attraction to two phases.
  • Stationary phase stays put: in paper chromatography, the paper and the water it holds.
  • Mobile phase: the moving solvent. More attraction to the stationary phase: shorter distance, lower RF.
  • RF = distance moved by spot ÷ distance moved by solvent, both from the baseline.
  • Example: spot 2.4 cm, solvent 8.0 cm. RF = 2.4 ÷ 8.0 = 0.30, no units.
  • Same paper, solvent and temperature: a substance always gives the same RF.

Mission 5

Covalent networks: diamond

  • In a covalent network, covalent bonds run through the whole solid. There are no molecules.
  • Carbon and silicon both form these giant networks.
  • Diamond: each C bonded to four others, tetrahedrally.
  • Very hard, very high melting point: melting must break strong covalent bonds. Insoluble.
  • No conduction: all four outer electrons of each C are in localized bonds.
Mission 5

Graphite and graphene

  • Graphite: flat layers of hexagons. Each C is bonded to three others.
  • Each C's fourth electron is delocalized across its layer: graphite conducts along the layers.
  • Only London forces hold the layers together. They slide, so graphite is soft.
  • Graphene: a single layer of graphite. It conducts, and is very strong for its mass.
Mission 5

Fullerenes and allotropes

  • Fullerene C₆₀: a closed cage of 60 C atoms. These are separate molecules.
  • Only London forces act between C₆₀ molecules, so it vaporizes far more easily than diamond.
  • Allotropes: forms of one element with different bonding and structure.
  • Different structures give different physical and chemical properties.
Mission 5

Silicon and silicon dioxide

  • Silicon: the diamond structure, each Si bonded to four Si. It melts at 1414 °C.
  • Si–Si bonds are longer and weaker than C–C. Silicon is a semiconductor.
  • Silicon dioxide, SiO₂: each Si bonded to four O, and each O to two Si.
  • A network, so SiO₂ is an empirical formula: Si : O = 1 : 2.
  • Melts near 1700 °C. Hard, insoluble, non-conducting.

Mission 6AHL

Sigma and pi bonds

  • σ bond: head-on overlap of orbitals. Electron density lies on the bond axis.
  • π bond: sideways overlap of two p orbitals. Density lies above and below the axis.
  • Single bond = 1σ. Double = 1σ + 1π. Triple = 1σ + 2π.
  • Ethene, C₂H₄: 5σ, 1π. Ethyne, C₂H₂: 3σ, 2π. Nitrogen, N₂: 1σ, 2π.

Mission 6AHL

Resonance and delocalization

  • Double bond drawable in more than one place? Each drawing is a resonance structure.
  • The atoms stay put; only electrons move between drawings. Link them with ↔.
  • NO₃⁻ has three resonance structures: any of the three O can carry the double bond.
  • The real ion is one resonance hybrid: π electrons delocalized over all three N–O bonds.
  • So the three bonds are identical, between single and double. The ion never flips between drawings.

Mission 6AHL

Benzene: what the evidence shows

  • Benzene, C₆H₆, is often drawn with alternating C=C and C–C bonds. The evidence disagrees.
  • Bond lengths: all six C–C bonds are 140 pm, between C–C (154) and C=C (134).
  • Energy: adding H₂ releases much less energy than three C=C bonds would. Benzene is extra stable.
  • Reactions: benzene undergoes substitution, not addition, so its ring stays intact.
  • Isomers: only one 1,2-dibromobenzene exists. Alternating bonds would give two.
  • So: a flat ring of sp² carbons, six delocalized π electrons above and below it.

Mission 7

The metallic bond

  • Metal atoms release their valence electrons into a shared, delocalized sea.
  • Metallic bond: electrostatic attraction between the lattice of cations and the delocalized electrons.
  • It is non-directional: each cation attracts the electrons all around it.
  • The metal stays neutral: the electrons balance the cations' charge.
Mission 7

Properties and uses

  • Electrical conductivity: delocalized electrons drift when a voltage is applied. Cations stay put.
  • Thermal conductivity: delocalized electrons carry kinetic energy rapidly through the metal.
  • Malleability: cation layers slide; non-directional bonding holds them in their new places.
  • In an ionic lattice, a shift brings like charges together. They repel, and the crystal shatters.
  • Uses follow: copper wiring, aluminium cooking pans, gold beaten into thin leaf.

Mission 7

What makes metallic bonding stronger

  • Stronger with higher cation charge and smaller cation radius.
  • Both raise the electron density: more delocalized electrons, closer to each cation's centre.
  • Na⁺ → Mg²⁺: double the charge, a smaller ion. Magnesium melts far higher.
  • Down group 1 the cations get larger: K 63 °C, Rb 39 °C.
  • Mass does not decide: the heavier atom can melt lower.

Mission 7AHL

Transition elements

  • In transition elements, 3d electrons are delocalized as well as 4s electrons.
  • More delocalized electrons per cation: higher electron density, stronger metallic bonding.
  • So high melting points: vanadium 1910 °C, against potassium 63 °C in the same period.
  • More mobile electrons, too: transition elements are good electrical conductors.

Mission 7

Alloys

  • An alloy is a mixture: a metal plus other metals or non-metals. No fixed formula.
  • Brass (copper, zinc), bronze (copper, tin), stainless steel (iron, chromium, nickel, carbon).
  • Metallic bonding is non-directional, so other atoms fit in. The alloy still conducts.
  • Different-sized atoms disrupt the layers, so they slide less: alloys are usually harder.

Mission 7

Bonding is a continuum

  • Ionic, covalent and metallic are models. Real bonding usually mixes them.
  • The bonding triangle (data booklet, section 17) places any binary compound.
  • x-axis: average electronegativity, (χ₁ + χ₂) ÷ 2. y-axis: difference, Δχ.
  • Labels: metallic, ionic, polar covalent, covalent (the last two share one area).
  • Higher up means more ionic character. The right-hand scale gives % covalent.

Tin metal is cations in a sea of delocalized electrons, which carry a current. SnCl₄ (2.6, 1.2) lies in the polar covalent region: its electrons sit in Sn–Cl bonds inside molecules. With no ions and no delocalized electrons, nothing can carry charge.

Mission 8

Polymers

  • A polymer is a macromolecule: many small monomers joined by covalent bonds.
  • Natural: cellulose, starch, proteins, DNA, natural rubber. Synthetic: PVC, nylon, polyesters.
  • Plastics don't conduct: every electron is held in a localized bond.
  • Many plastics (thermoplastics) soften when heated: forces between chains are overcome. Chains stay intact.
  • They last for decades: strong, non-polar C–C and C–H bonds react very slowly.

Mission 8

Addition polymers

  • One bond of each alkene's C=C breaks, and the carbons link to neighbouring monomers.
  • Nothing else is made: the polymer contains every atom of the monomers.
  • Chloroethene, CH₂=CHCl → poly(chloroethene), PVC: [–CH₂–CHCl–]ₙ.
  • The repeating unit keeps the two former C=C carbons in the chain. Other groups hang off.
  • Back to the monomer: take two main-chain carbons and restore the C=C.

Mission 8AHL

Condensation polymers: polyamides

  • Two functional groups react, joining monomers and releasing a small molecule: H₂O, or HCl.
  • HCl comes from an acyl chloride, –COCl. Each monomer needs two reacting groups.
  • Polyamide: –NH₂ + –COOH → amide link –CONH– + H₂O.
  • H₂N(CH₂)₄NH₂ + HOOC(CH₂)₄COOH → repeating unit –NH(CH₂)₄NHCO(CH₂)₄CO–.
  • Two links per repeating unit, so two H₂O are released per unit.
Mission 8AHL

Polyesters and hydrolysis

  • Polyester: –OH + –COOH → ester link –COO– + H₂O.
  • HOCH₂CH₂OH + HOOC(CH₂)₂COOH → repeating unit –OCH₂CH₂OOC(CH₂)₂CO–.
  • Proteins (polyamides of amino acids), starch, cellulose and DNA all form by condensation.
  • Hydrolysis is the reverse: water breaks the links and gives back the monomers.

Recap

The big ideas

  • Ionic: charges from configurations; lattice enthalpy rises with ion charge, falls with radius.
  • Covalent: both nuclei attract a shared pair; more pairs, shorter and stronger. VSEPR gives shapes.
  • Polarity needs polar bonds and a non-cancelling shape. Similar mass: London < dipole–dipole < hydrogen bonding.
  • Networks melt very high; C₆₀ is molecular. RF = spot ÷ solvent distance, from the baseline.
  • Metals: cations in delocalized electrons; alloys harder. Triangle: average χ and Δχ. Addition polymers: C=C opens.
  • AHLFormal charge, expanded octets, σ/π, hybridization, resonance, benzene, d electrons, condensation polymers.
