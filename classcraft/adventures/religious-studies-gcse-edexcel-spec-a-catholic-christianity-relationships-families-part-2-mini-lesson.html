Mission 1

Methods and reasons

  • Contraception: deliberately preventing pregnancy. Family planning: deciding whether, when and how many children to have.
  • Artificial methods: barrier (condom), hormonal (pill, implant), IUD (coil).
  • Natural methods (natural family planning, NFP): avoiding sex on the woman's fertile days, tracked by body signs.
  • Permanent: sterilisation (vasectomy for men, tubal ligation for women).
  • Some methods may work after conception (some emergency pills, the IUD in some cases). Many believers who accept contraception reject these, seeing them as close to abortion.
  • Reasons: spacing children, health, money, the couple's relationship; condoms also protect against STIs.
Mission 1

Catholic teaching: Humanae Vitae

  • Humanae Vitae (Pope Paul VI, 1968), paraphrase: sex in marriage has two meanings, unitive (joining the couple) and procreative (open to new life). They must never be separated (HV 12).
  • So each act of intercourse must stay open to life (HV 11): artificial contraception and sterilisation are wrong (HV 14).
  • Responsible parenthood (HV 10): couples may space births for serious reasons using NFP, which works with the body's natural rhythm (HV 16).
  • Roots in natural law: Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274) saw reproduction as a basic human purpose.
Mission 1Read the source

"Be fruitful"

"God blessed them. God said to them, 'Be fruitful, multiply, fill the earth, and subdue it.'" (Genesis 1:28, WEBBE)

  • Catholic reading: children are a gift and a purpose of marriage; the couple cooperates with God the Creator, so sex should stay open to life.
  • Many Protestants: a blessing on humanity as a whole; it does not require every couple to have as many children as possible.

Q1 What does God tell the first humans to do?

Show answer

Be fruitful and multiply: have children.

Q2 Why might a Protestant say this verse still allows contraception?

Show answer

It blesses humanity's growth in general; responsible choices about family size can honour it.

Mission 1Diversity

Other Christians

  • Church of England: the Lambeth Conference of 1930 first accepted contraception within marriage for good reasons; by 1958 it called family planning a matter of the couple's conscience.
  • Methodists and most Protestants: responsible use of contraception within marriage is good stewardship; it can strengthen the couple's love.
  • Orthodox: many allow non-abortive methods within marriage after pastoral guidance.
  • Some conservative evangelicals: leave family size to God and avoid contraception.
  • All these Christians reject methods that act after conception if they see them as ending a life.
  • Many Catholics use contraception in practice, though the teaching has not changed.
Mission 1

Situation ethics and non-religious views

  • Situation ethics (Joseph Fletcher, 1905–1991, Situation Ethics, 1966): the only absolute is agape (selfless love). Do the most loving thing in each situation.
  • E.g. a couple where another pregnancy could endanger the mother's life: the loving act may be reliable contraception.
  • Humanists: decide by reason and consequences: health, the child's welfare, the couple's choice, the planet's resources.
  • Atheists: no divine command against it; most support easy access.
  • Catholic response: love is not measured only by results; Humanae Vitae warned that contraception could lead to less respect for women and more infidelity (HV 17).
  • Other Christians reply: agape and Jesus' command to love fit well with responsible parenthood.
Mission 1Diversity

Islam

  • Muslims believe children are a gift from Allah; having a family is a strong Sunnah.
  • Hadith report that Companions practised 'azl (withdrawal) and the Prophet did not forbid it.
  • Your spec names Sahih al-Bukhari 34:432 and 62:136 (paraphrase): this happened while the Qur'an was being revealed, and the Prophet added that any soul Allah wills to create will come into being.
  • So most scholars allow reversible methods within marriage, with both spouses' consent, for reasons such as health or spacing.
  • Permanent sterilisation: generally only for serious health reasons. Methods acting after conception: widely rejected.
  • Some Muslims oppose contraception from fear of poverty, citing: "Slay not your children, fearing a fall to poverty, We shall provide for them and for you." (Qur'an 17:31, Pickthall)
Mission 1Evaluate

"Family planning should be left to the couple, not religious teaching."

  • Reasons for: the couple knows their health, money and relationship; situation ethics: love decides case by case; Humanists: personal freedom; most Protestants and many Jews, Hindus, Buddhists and Sikhs leave it to conscience.
  • Reasons against: Catholics: the meaning of sex comes from God, not from choice (Humanae Vitae); a conscience needs forming by teaching; children are a gift, not a lifestyle decision; some methods may end a life.
  • Another view: the couple decides, but within religious limits (e.g. no methods that act after conception).
  • Judge it by: Who has authority over moral choices? Is every act or the whole marriage what must be open to life? How much do consequences matter?
Mission 1Worked answer

Contrasting beliefs with a source

Question: Explain two contrasting religious beliefs about contraception. Refer to sacred writings or another source of religious belief.

  • Belief 1: Catholics believe artificial contraception is wrong. Source: Humanae Vitae (1968) teaches that each act of intercourse must stay open to life (HV 11). Development: a condom or pill closes the act to life, so only natural family planning is allowed, and only for serious reasons.
  • Belief 2: many Protestants, e.g. the Church of England, accept contraception within marriage. Source: they read "Be fruitful, multiply" (Genesis 1:28) as a blessing on humanity as a whole, not a rule for every act. Development: so responsible parenthood can be loving (agape), helping the couple care for the children they have.

Faiths differ less on "whether" than on "which method" and "why": openness to life, consent, and not harming life are the key tests.

Mission 2

Three ways to decide

  • An ethical theory is a method for deciding what is right.
  • Rules and duties: some acts are right or wrong in themselves (e.g. Natural Law: preserve life, never kill the innocent). Often absolutist.
  • Consequences: the right act produces the best results (e.g. utilitarianism, situation ethics). Often relativist.
  • Character: become a good person, and good acts follow (virtue ethics).
  • Religious believers use all three, alongside scripture and religious authority.
Mission 2

Situation ethics

  • Joseph Fletcher (1905–1991), American Episcopal priest; Situation Ethics: The New Morality (1966).
  • Only one absolute rule: agape, unselfish love for others.
  • In each situation, do the most loving thing. Other rules are useful guidelines, not absolutes.
  • Fletcher points to Jesus putting people before Sabbath rules: "The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath." (Mark 2:27)
  • Criticisms: hard to predict results; people may call selfish choices "loving"; it may allow acts many see as always wrong (e.g. torture "for the greater good").
Mission 2Read the source

Love fulfils the law

"Love doesn't harm a neighbour. Love therefore is the fulfilment of the law." (Romans 13:10)

  • Jesus: "You shall love your neighbour as yourself." (Matthew 22:39)

Q1 How could a situationist use Romans 13:10?

Show answer

Love is the aim of every rule, so love can decide when rules clash.

Q2 How might a Catholic reply?

Show answer

Love works through God's commands, not against them: "fulfilment", not replacement.

Mission 2

Natural Law

  • Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274), Summa Theologiae: God built a moral order into human nature that reason can discover.
  • The first precept: "good is to be done and pursued, and evil is to be avoided" (Summa Theologiae I-II, q. 94, a. 2).
  • Primary precepts (as usually taught): preserve life; reproduce; educate the young; live in society; worship God.
  • Secondary precepts apply them: e.g. do not murder (from preserving life).
  • Catholic teaching on contraception (Humanae Vitae, 1968) applies the precept of reproduction: sex should stay open to new life.
Mission 2Read the source

Aquinas on natural inclinations

"whatever is a means of preserving human life, and of warding off its obstacles, belongs to the natural law" (Summa Theologiae I-II, q. 94, a. 2)

  • Aquinas adds: humans also share with animals the inclination to "sexual intercourse, education of offspring", and, by reason, "to know the truth about God, and to live in society".

Q1 Which primary precept does the first sentence describe?

Show answer

Preserve life.

Q2 How does Aquinas find these precepts?

Show answer

By reason, from the natural inclinations of human nature.

Mission 2Diversity

One issue, several theories

  • Divorce: sanctity of vows: a promise before God must be kept (absolutist) · compassion: love may mean letting an unhappy couple part (situation ethics).
  • Family planning: Catholic teaching applies Natural Law: sex should stay open to new life, so artificial contraception is wrong · situation ethics: contraception may be the most loving choice, for example to protect a mother's health or care for the children a couple already has.
Mission 2Evaluate

"Love is the only rule a Christian needs."

  • Reasons for: Romans 13:10 and Matthew 22:39: love fulfils the law; Jesus put people before Sabbath rules (Mark 2:27); rules cannot cover every situation.
  • Reasons against: Catholic teaching holds some acts are always wrong, whatever the motive; humans easily mistake selfish wishes for love; clear rules protect the weak; Jesus also kept and taught the commandments.
  • Another view: use both: rules as the normal guide, love for the hard cases.
  • Judge it by: Can humans predict which act is most loving? Do absolute rules ever cause harm? What do scripture and tradition say about rules?
Mission check

?A hospital has one donor heart. Patient X is a young parent; patient Y is older and has waited longer. Which reasoning best fits virtue ethics?

Ethical theories are tools: rules, love, results or character. Believers often combine them.

Mission 3

Ending a marriage

  • Divorce: the legal ending of a marriage. Separation: living apart while still married.
  • Annulment: a declaration that a marriage was never valid in the first place.
  • Remarriage: marrying again while a former spouse is still alive.
  • Law in England and Wales: since April 2022, no-fault divorce (Divorce, Dissolution and Separation Act 2020). The only ground: the marriage has broken down irretrievably; no one has to be blamed.
  • Common reasons: adultery, abuse, addiction, money worries, growing apart.
Mission 3

What Jesus taught

  • Marriage is God's work: "the two will become one flesh" (Mark 10:8; Genesis 2:24).
  • Matthew 19:1–12: Pharisees ask whether divorce is allowed. Jesus says Moses allowed it "because of the hardness of your hearts" (Matthew 19:8), but it was not God's plan.
  • In Matthew, one exception: sexual immorality (Matthew 19:9; 5:32). Mark has no exception (Mark 10:11–12).
Mission 3Read the source

Matthew 19

"What therefore God has joined together, don't let man tear apart." (Matthew 19:6)

"I tell you that whoever divorces his wife, except for sexual immorality, and marries another, commits adultery." (Matthew 19:9)

Q1 Who joins a couple in marriage, according to Matthew 19:6?

Show answer

God; so humans should not separate them.

Q2 What does Matthew 19:9 say remarriage after divorce usually is?

Show answer

Adultery, except where the divorce followed sexual immorality.

Mission 3

Catholic teaching: annulment, not divorce

  • A valid, consummated sacramental marriage cannot be dissolved by any human power (CCC 2382, paraphrase).
  • Divorce is a grave offence against the natural law (CCC 2384, paraphrase), but civil divorce may be tolerated to protect legal rights or children (CCC 2383); separation is allowed.
  • A spouse unjustly abandoned is not to blame (CCC 2386).
  • Remarriage while a former spouse lives is not accepted; the person cannot usually receive Holy Communion.
  • Annulment (declaration of nullity): a Church tribunal finds the marriage was never valid, e.g. no free consent, force or fear, no intention of a lifelong faithful union open to children. Both may then marry in church.
  • Pope Francis, Amoris Laetitia (2016): accompany divorced and remarried Catholics with care and discernment; some bishops allow Communion case by case.
Quick check

Mission 3Diversity

Other Christians

  • Orthodox: marriage is meant for life, but out of mercy (economy) the Church allows divorce and up to three marriages; a second wedding has a penitential tone.
  • Church of England: since 2002 (General Synod), divorced people may remarry in church at the priest's discretion.
  • Methodists and many Protestants: divorce is a sad last resort; God forgives; remarriage can be a fresh start.
  • Some evangelicals: divorce only for adultery (Matthew 19:9) or desertion by an unbelieving spouse (1 Corinthians 7:15).
Mission 3

Situation ethics and non-religious views

  • Situation ethics (Joseph Fletcher, Situation Ethics, 1966): do the most loving (agape) thing. If staying together causes lasting harm, divorce may be the loving act.
  • Humanists and atheists: marriage is a human commitment; if it has failed, divorce can be best for adults and children; remarriage is fine.
  • They support no-fault divorce: less conflict, less blame.
  • Catholic response: compassion is right, but love also means keeping promises; separation can protect people without denying the marriage.
  • Other Christian response: many agree that love and forgiveness can make divorce and remarriage right in some cases.
Mission 3Evaluate

Sanctity of vows or compassion?

  • Sanctity of vows: the promise was made before God for life; Jesus taught against divorce (Matthew 19:6); stability for children; easy divorce weakens commitment.
  • Compassion: no one should stay in abuse or misery; God forgives and offers new starts; children can suffer more from conflict; Moses allowed divorce because of human weakness (Matthew 19:8).
  • Another view: both matter: work hard to save the marriage, but allow separation or divorce when it truly fails.
  • Judge it by: Is a vow unbreakable or a commitment to try? Whose welfare counts most? Does allowing divorce show mercy or weaken marriage?
Mission 3Diversity

Islam

  • Divorce is allowed as a last resort; a hadith calls it the most disliked of permitted things (Abu Dawud, paraphrase).
  • First, try to reconcile: "appoint an arbiter from his folk and an arbiter from her folk." (Qur'an 4:35, Pickthall)
  • Talaq (divorce by the husband), then iddah: a waiting time of three monthly cycles, to allow reconciliation and check for pregnancy (Qur'an 2:228).
  • "Divorce must be pronounced twice and then (a woman) must be retained in honour or released in kindness." (Qur'an 2:229, Pickthall)
  • A wife can seek divorce by khul' (returning her dowry) or through a judge or Shari'ah council.
  • Surah 2:226–241 also covers a waiting period of four months after a husband's vow to stay apart (2:226), fair treatment and provision for divorced women.
  • Sunni and Shi'a rules: Shi'a law requires a set formula before two just witnesses, and three pronouncements at one sitting count as one at most. Sunni schools do not require witnesses; the classical Sunni schools mostly counted three at once as final, a view many modern Sunni scholars reject.
Mission check

Christians share Jesus' teaching on lifelong marriage, but differ on how to apply it: annulment, mercy, or pastoral discretion.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Equal but different, or equal and the same: who should do what in a family?

Make a guess: Equal but different, or equal and the same: who should do what in a family?

Mission 4

Roles then and now

  • Traditional roles: man as breadwinner and head; woman as homemaker and main carer.
  • Today: most couples both work; more fathers share childcare or stay at home; roles are negotiated.
  • Gender equality: men and women have equal worth, rights and opportunities.
  • Two religious approaches:
  • Complementarian: equal in worth, different and complementary roles.
  • Egalitarian: equal in worth, roles shared or interchangeable, decided by gifts and circumstances.
Mission 4

Genesis: equal in God's image

  • "God created man in his own image. In God's image he created him; male and female he created them." (Genesis 1:27)
  • Both share God's image and the same blessing and task (Genesis 1:26–31).
  • Genesis 2:18: the woman is "a helper comparable to him". The Hebrew word (ezer) is also used of God helping Israel, so it does not mean inferior.
  • Genesis 3:16: after the Fall, "he will rule over you". Many Christians and Jews read this as the result of sin, not God's original plan.
Mission 4Read the source

"Wives, be subject to your own husbands, as to the Lord." (Ephesians 5:22)

"Husbands, love your wives, even as Christ also loved the assembly and gave himself up for her" (Ephesians 5:25)

Q1 What does 5:21 ask of both spouses?

Show answer

To submit to one another.

Q2 What kind of love does 5:25 ask of husbands?

Mission 4Diversity

Christian views on roles

  • Complementarian (many evangelicals, some traditional Christians): the husband is a loving, serving head; the wife supports and respects him (Ephesians 5:22–25).
  • Egalitarian (many Anglicans, Methodists, liberal Christians): 5:21 means mutual submission; "neither male nor female" (Galatians 3:28); roles by gifts.
  • Catholic: man and woman are equal in dignity and complementary. John Paul II (Mulieris Dignitatem, 1988) read Ephesians 5 as mutual submission.
  • CCC 2207 (paraphrase): the family is the original cell of social life, where people first learn respect, care and responsibility.
  • Dignity of work in the home: John Paul II (Laborem Exercens, 1981, 19; Familiaris Consortio, 1981, 23) taught that caring for home and children is real work that society should value; women must not be penalised for it, nor shut out of paid work.
Quick check
?An egalitarian Christian is asked how she reads Ephesians 5:21–25. What would she most likely say?
Mission 4Diversity

Islam

  • Equal before Allah: "Whosoever doeth right, whether male or female, and is a believer..." (Qur'an 16:97, Pickthall); both created "from a single soul" (Qur'an 4:1).
  • Different responsibilities: the husband must provide for the family (Qur'an 4:34); a wife's own earnings and property remain hers; mahr (dowry) is paid to her.
  • "they (women) have rights similar to those (of men) over them in kindness, and men are a degree above them." (Qur'an 2:228, Pickthall) Scholars differ on what the "degree" means (e.g. extra responsibility).
  • Surah 4 (An-Nisa) sets out rights of wives, inheritance and the husband's duty (4:34, a verse read in very different ways).
  • The time of Muhammad: Khadijah ran a trading business and employed him; Aisha was a leading teacher; hadith say the Prophet mended his clothes and helped with housework.
  • Diversity: traditional Muslims stress distinct roles; many British Muslims share work and childcare.
Mission 4

Non-religious views

  • Humanists: equal rights and opportunities; roles decided by the couple, not by sex or scripture.
  • Feminist critiques: "different roles" can hide inequality (e.g. women doing most unpaid housework).
  • Many atheists and Humanists support shared parental leave and equal pay.
  • Religious responses: complementarity values women's distinct gifts; Catholic teaching says work in the home deserves recognition; many believers already share roles.
Mission 4Evaluate

"Men and women should have different roles in the family."

  • Reasons for: complementarian reading of Ephesians 5:22–25 and Qur'an 4:34; Orthodox Jewish and traditional Hindu teaching; biological differences (pregnancy, breastfeeding).
  • Reasons against: Genesis 1:27 and Galatians 3:28; Ephesians 5:21 mutual submission; GGS 788 "one light in two bodies"; Humanist equality; modern families need flexibility.
  • Another view: equal worth is essential; whether roles differ should be the couple's free choice.
  • Judge it by: Does "different" mean "unequal"? Are scripture's roles timeless or cultural? Who decides: tradition or the couple?
Mission check

The real disagreement is not about whether women and men are equal in worth, but whether equal means the same roles.

Mission 5

Prejudice, discrimination, equality

  • Prejudice: judging someone unfairly before knowing them (an attitude). Sexism: prejudice based on sex.
  • Discrimination: treating someone unfairly because of a prejudice (an action).
  • Gender equality: equal rights, status and opportunities for women and men.
  • UK law: Equal Pay Act 1970; Sex Discrimination Act 1975; now the Equality Act 2010.
  • Examples: unequal pay for the same work; women passed over for promotion; girls denied schooling (Malala Yousafzai, shot in 2012 for campaigning for girls' education).
Mission 5

Christian teaching on equality

  • Both sexes are made in God's image (Genesis 1:27).
  • "There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free man, there is neither male nor female; for you are all one in Christ Jesus." (Galatians 3:28)
  • Jesus treated women as disciples and witnesses: Mary Magdalene first told the others he had risen (John 20:18).
  • Catholic: CCC 1938 (paraphrase): sinful inequalities between people contradict the Gospel. Men and women have equal dignity (CCC 369).
  • John Paul II's theology of the body (1979–84): man and woman are equal and complementary; each is a gift to the other.
  • Catholic opposition to discrimination: the Church calls for equal pay, opposes violence against women, and supports girls' education worldwide (e.g. through CAFOD and religious orders' schools).
Mission 5Read the source

Equality and leadership

"But I don't permit a woman to teach, nor to exercise authority over a man, but to be in quietness." (1 Timothy 2:12)

  • Compare Galatians 3:28: "neither male nor female".
  • Galatians 3:23–29: before faith came, people were under the law; now, baptised into Christ, all are God's children and heirs.

Q1 What does 1 Timothy 2:12 forbid?

Show answer

Women teaching or having authority over men.

Q2 How might an Anglican who supports women priests read it?

Show answer

As advice for one church in Paul's time, while Galatians 3:28 states the lasting principle.

Mission 5Diversity

Women's ministry in the churches

  • Catholic: only men can be ordained priests. John Paul II (Ordinatio Sacerdotalis, 1994): the Church has no authority to ordain women. Reasons: Jesus chose twelve men as apostles; the priest acts in the person of Christ.
  • Catholics stress this is about roles, not worth; women lead as religious sisters, teachers, theologians; since 2021 they can be formally installed as readers and acolytes.
  • Orthodox: male priesthood, for similar reasons of tradition.
  • Church of England: women priests since 1994, bishops since 2014 (first, Libby Lane, 2015); in 2025 Sarah Mullally was chosen as the first woman Archbishop of Canterbury.
  • Methodists, Salvation Army, URC, many Baptists: women ministers; the Salvation Army from its start (Catherine Booth).
  • Some evangelical churches: women lead in many ways but not as senior pastors (1 Timothy 2:12).

Mission 5Diversity

Islam

  • Equal reward for faith and good deeds: Qur'an 33:35 lists "men who surrender unto Allah, and women who surrender" through ten pairs (Qur'an 33:35, Pickthall).
  • Worship: men and women pray in separate rows or rooms; women may lead other women; most scholars teach only men lead mixed congregational prayer. Attending the mosque is a duty for men at Jumu'ah, optional for women.
  • Equality in action: Khadijah, successful businesswoman; Aisha, a leading scholar; Fatima al-Fihri founded al-Qarawiyyin in Fez (859), often called the oldest university still working.
  • Many Muslims say discrimination against women comes from culture, not Islam; groups such as Muslim Women's Network UK campaign for women's rights.
Mission 5

Does equal mean the same?

  • Equality as sameness: the same rights, roles and opportunities everywhere, including religious leadership. Held by many Humanists and egalitarian believers.
  • Equality with difference (complementarity): equal worth and rights, but some roles linked to sex. Held by Catholic and Orthodox teaching, Orthodox Judaism and many Muslims.
  • Humanists: no reason, apart from tradition, to bar women from any role; tradition should change when it causes unfairness.
  • Catholic response: the male priesthood follows Christ's choice and does not judge women's worth; Mary is honoured above all saints.
Mission 5Evaluate

"Religions treat women as equals."

  • Reasons for: equal image of God (Genesis 1:27), Galatians 3:28, Qur'an 33:35, GGS 473, Soma Sutta; women priests, rabbis and leaders in many traditions; faiths campaign against sex discrimination.
  • Reasons against: male-only priesthood (Catholic, Orthodox) and rabbinate (Orthodox Judaism); separate roles in mosques; some texts (1 Timothy 2:12; some Manusmriti verses); practice often lags behind teaching.
  • Another view: equal worth is taught everywhere; the debate is whether different roles are fair.
  • Judge it by: Equal worth or equal roles? Teaching or practice? Which tradition or group?
Mission check

?On one street, the Anglican church's vicar is a woman, while the Catholic parish next door has only ever had male priests. Both churches teach that women and men are made equally in God's image. What best explains the difference?

Faiths teach equal worth and oppose sex discrimination; they disagree about whether every role in worship should be open to both, and why.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Catholics (Humanae Vitae): only natural family planning, for serious reasons; most other Christians accept contraception within marriage.
  • Islam ('azl hadith) and Judaism (first mitzvah, pill preferred) allow it with conditions; Hindus, Buddhists and Sikhs mostly accept it but reject methods that act after conception.
  • Situation ethics and Humanists judge by love and consequences.

Mission 2

  • Situation ethics: do the most loving thing (agape). Utilitarianism: the greatest happiness of the greatest number.
  • Natural Law: reason discovers God's moral order; primary precepts: life, reproduction, education, society, worship.

Mission 3

  • Divorce ends a marriage in law; annulment declares it was never valid. Catholics accept annulment, not remarriage after divorce; most other Christians allow remarriage.
  • The key debate: sanctity of vows (Matthew 19:6) vs compassion (Matthew 19:8).
  • Situation ethics and Humanists put compassion and the welfare of those involved first.
  • Islam (talaq, iddah, khul'), Judaism (get, agunah) and, reluctantly, Dharmic traditions allow divorce as a last resort.

Mission 4

  • Equal in God's image (Genesis 1:27); Ephesians 5 read as headship (complementarian) or mutual submission (egalitarian).
  • Catholic: equal dignity, complementarity; CCC 2207; work in the home has dignity.
  • Islam: equal before Allah, with different responsibilities (Qur'an 2:228 and 4:34).

Mission 5

  • Prejudice is an attitude; discrimination an action; sex discrimination is illegal under the Equality Act 2010.
  • Christians: Genesis 1:27, Galatians 3:28; Catholic and Orthodox churches ordain only men; Anglicans and most Protestants ordain women.
  • Islam: Qur'an 33:35 promises equal reward; practice does not always match teaching.
