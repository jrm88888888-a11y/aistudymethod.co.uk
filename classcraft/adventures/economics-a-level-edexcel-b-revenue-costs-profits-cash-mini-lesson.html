Mission 1

Mission 1: Why can the next batch of bread cost a bakery more to make than the batches before it?

Make a guess: Why can the next batch of bread cost a bakery more to make than the batches before it?

Mission 1

Fixed and variable costs

  • Fixed costs (FC, or total fixed cost TFC): do not change with output in the short run. Rent, insurance, loan interest, salaried staff.
  • Fixed costs must be paid even at zero output.
  • Variable costs (VC, or total variable cost TVC): change with output. Ingredients, energy, packaging, hourly wages.
  • Total cost (TC) = fixed cost + variable cost.
Mission 1

Average and marginal cost

  • Average fixed cost: AFC = TFC ÷ Q.
  • Average variable cost: AVC = TVC ÷ Q.
  • Average total cost (often just average cost, AC): ATC = TC ÷ Q = AFC + AVC.
  • Marginal cost: the extra cost of producing one more unit. MC = change in TC ÷ change in Q.
  • Fixed costs do not change, so MC is also the change in variable cost.
  • Say: "marginal cost is the cost of the next unit" — not: "the average cost of all units".

Mission 1

What changes costs

  • Higher input prices (wages, energy, raw materials): variable cost and average cost rise at every output. Fixed cost unchanged.
  • Higher fixed costs (rent, insurance, a licence fee): fixed cost and average cost rise. Variable cost unchanged.
  • Higher productivity (better technology, training, organisation): each worker makes more, so variable cost per unit and average cost fall.
  • Lean production cuts waste: less stock held (just-in-time), fewer faults, shorter lead times (the time from order to delivery). Costs per unit fall, and faster delivery can raise sales. Risk: little stock to fall back on if a supplier fails.
  • Say: "average cost fell" — not: "costs fell", if total cost rose with output.
Mission 1

Worked example: revenue, cost and profit

  • Same illustrative bakery as the earlier worked example. Each batch sells for 90.
  • Revenue = price × sales volume. At 5 batches: 90 × 5 = 450.
  • Profit = revenue − total cost = 450 − 360 = 90.
  • Per batch: 90 − ATC of 72 = 18; 18 × 5 = 90.
  • Percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100. From 4 to 5 batches:
    • sales volume: (5 − 4) ÷ 4 × 100 = 25%
    • revenue: 360 → 450, +25%
    • total cost: 280 → 360, +28.57%
    • profit: 80 → 90, +12.5%

Mission 2

Mission 2: If a seller must cut its price to sell one more, why does that extra sale bring in less than the price?

Make a guess: If a seller must cut its price to sell one more, why does that extra sale bring in less than the price?

Mission 2

Three measures of revenue

  • Total revenue (TR) = price × quantity sold.
  • Average revenue (AR) = TR ÷ quantity. With one price for all units, AR = price.
  • Marginal revenue (MR) = the change in TR from selling one more unit: MR = ΔTR ÷ ΔQ.
  • Revenue = selling price × sales volume. Percentage change in revenue = (new − old) ÷ old × 100.
  • Say: "marginal revenue is the extra revenue from one more unit" — not: "the price of one more unit".

Mission 2

Why MR is below price

  • To sell a 3rd unit, the stall cuts its price from 8 to 6, for every buyer.
  • Gain: the 3rd unit sells for +6.
  • Loss: the first 2 units now earn 2 less each: 2 × 2 = −4.
  • MR = 6 − 4 = 2, below the price of 6.
  • So when a firm must lower its price to sell more, MR < AR for every unit after the first.
  • Say: "AR is the demand curve; MR lies below it" — not: "MR equals the price".

Mission 2

A price taker

  • In a perfectly competitive market, a firm is a price taker: it can sell as much as it likes at the market price.
  • It never has to cut its price to sell more, so each extra unit adds exactly the price to TR.
  • So P = AR = MR, all one horizontal line.
  • TR is a straight line from the origin, rising by the price with each unit.
  • Illustrative data: price 5 → TR 5, 10, 15, 20 …; MR = 5 every time.
Mission 2

Drawing the curves

  • AR = D: the downward-sloping demand curve.
  • With a straight-line demand curve, MR starts at the same point on the price axis and is twice as steep.
  • MR meets the quantity axis halfway to where AR does.
  • TR is an arch: it rises while MR > 0, peaks where MR = 0, and falls once MR < 0.
  • Say: "TR is maximised where MR = 0" — not: "where AR = 0", where TR is zero.

Mission 3

Mission 3: How many customers does a new business need before it stops losing money?

Make a guess: How many customers does a new business need before it stops losing money?

Mission 3

Two kinds of cost

  • Illustrative data: a new smoothie bar.
  • Fixed costs do not change with output: rent, equipment lease, insurance. Here £2,000 a month.
  • Variable costs change with output: fruit, cups, card fees. Here £1.50 per smoothie.
  • Total costs = total fixed costs + total variable costs.
  • Total revenue = selling price × quantity sold. Price here: £4.
  • Say: "fixed costs stay the same whatever the output" — not: "fixed costs never change". The rent can still rise next year.
Mission 3

Contribution

  • Contribution per unit = selling price − variable cost per unit.
  • Smoothie: £4 − £1.50 = £2.50.
  • Total contribution = contribution per unit × units sold = total revenue − total variable costs.
  • Each smoothie's £2.50 first goes towards paying the fixed costs.
  • Once the fixed costs are covered, each extra £2.50 is profit.
  • Say: "contribution towards fixed costs" — not: "profit per unit". Profit per unit also subtracts a share of fixed costs.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A T-shirt printer sells each shirt for £12. Each blank shirt costs £4 and the ink for each costs £1.50. Rent is £800 a month, and it sells 500 shirts a month. What is the contribution per shirt?
Mission 3

The break-even point

  • Break-even: total revenue = total costs. No profit, no loss.
  • In symbols: total fixed costs + total variable costs = total sales revenue.
  • Using contribution: break-even output = total fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit.
  • Smoothie: £2,000 ÷ £2.50 = 800 smoothies a month.
  • Check: revenue 800 × £4 = £3,200. Costs £2,000 + 800 × £1.50 = £3,200. ✓
  • Break-even revenue = break-even output × price = £3,200.
  • If the answer is not a whole number, round up: a firm cannot sell part of a unit, and rounding down leaves a small loss.
Mission 3

The break-even chart

  • Vertical axis: costs and revenue (£). Horizontal axis: output (units per month).
  • Fixed costs: a horizontal line at £2,000.
  • Total costs: starts at £2,000 (the fixed costs) and rises by £1.50 per unit.
  • Total revenue: starts at 0 and rises by £4 per unit.
  • Break-even where TR crosses TC: 800 units, £3,200.
  • Left of 800: TC above TR, a loss. Right of 800: TR above TC, a profit.
  • Profit or loss at any output = the vertical gap between TR and TC.
Mission 3Graph lab

Mission 3

Margin of safety

  • Margin of safety = actual (or planned) output − break-even output.
  • Smoothie bar plans to sell 1,000 a month: 1,000 − 800 = 200 smoothies.
  • As a percentage of planned output: 200 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = 20%.
  • Meaning: sales could fall by 200 smoothies (20%) before the bar makes a loss.
  • In revenue: 200 × £4 = £800.
  • Profit at 1,000: 1,000 × £2.50 − £2,000 = £500. Same as 200 × £2.50: only sales above break-even make profit.
  • A bigger margin of safety = less risk.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A gym charges members £30 a month. Each member adds £5 a month in variable costs (towels, cleaning, card fees). Fixed costs are £18,000 a month. The gym has 1,000 members. What is its margin of safety?
Mission 3

Contribution, MC and MR

  • If variable cost per unit is constant, the marginal cost of one more unit = variable cost per unit (£1.50).
  • If price does not change, the marginal revenue of one more unit = the price (£4).
  • So contribution per unit = MR − MC for each extra unit (£2.50).
  • Fixed costs do not change when one more unit is made. So the decision on an extra order turns on contribution.
  • Example: with spare capacity, a gym offers to buy 200 smoothies at £2.50 each.
  • That is below the full cost per unit at 1,000 smoothies (£1.50 + £2,000 ÷ 1,000 = £3.50).
  • But each adds £2.50 − £1.50 = £1 of contribution: profit rises by £200.
  • Check first: is there spare capacity? Will regular customers demand the lower price too?
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A furniture maker sells 500 chairs a month at £150 each. Variable cost is £90 per chair and fixed costs are £24,000 a month, so average cost is £138. A hotel offers to buy 100 extra chairs this month at £110 each. The firm has spare capacity and its other sales are not affected. What happens to its monthly profit if it accepts?
Mission 3

Limitations of break-even analysis

  • Straight lines are assumptions. To sell more, firms may cut price, so TR bends. Bulk buying may cut variable cost per unit.
  • Everything made is sold. Unsold stock means costs without revenue.
  • One product. Most firms sell many products, and sharing fixed costs between them is guesswork.
  • Fixed costs can step up. Past capacity, a second machine or a bigger shop raises fixed costs.
  • Forecasts. Prices, costs and sales are estimates, often for a business with no sales history.
  • A snapshot. The chart is for one period; costs and demand change.
  • Still useful: quick "what if?" tests of price, cost and output plans, and evidence for a bank loan.
Mission 3

Evaluate: how much should a new firm trust its break-even output?

It depends on…

  • How good the forecasts are. A café with ten similar shops has data. A first-time founder is guessing demand.
  • Price elasticity of demand. A higher price cuts break-even output only if customers do not drift away. If demand is price elastic, actual sales may fall below the new break-even point.
  • How stable costs are. Fruit prices, wages and energy bills can move within months, shifting every line on the chart.
  • Judgement: break-even is a starting point for decisions, not a forecast of what will happen.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. A small theatre has 400 seats. Each show has fixed costs of £6,000. Tickets are priced at £25, and each ticket sold adds £5 of variable cost. It expects to sell 360 tickets per show. The manager suggests cutting the price to £20, which she expects would sell all 400 seats. Which statement is correct?

Break-even output is fixed costs divided by what each sale contributes. A price cut lowers contribution on every unit, so sales must rise a lot just to stand still.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why might a business that brings in money every year still be making no real profit?

Make a guess: Why might a business that brings in money every year still be making no real profit?

Mission 4

What profit is

  • Profit = total revenue − total costs.
  • Profit = TR − TC.
  • Total costs means the opportunity cost of every resource the firm uses.
  • Some costs are paid out in money. Some are not. Both count.
Mission 4

Explicit and implicit costs

  • Explicit costs: money actually paid out. Wages, rent, materials, energy, interest on loans.
  • Implicit costs: the value of the owner's own resources used in the business. No payment is made.
  • Examples: the salary the owner gives up by not working elsewhere; the interest her own savings could have earned; the rent her own building could have earned.
  • Accounting profit = TR − explicit costs.
  • Economic profit = TR − explicit costs − implicit costs.
  • Economists treat normal profit as a cost: the reward the owner must get to keep her resources in this business.
  • Say: "accounting profit ignores implicit costs" — not: "accounting profit is wrong". It answers a different question.
Mission 4

Worked example: the café

  • Illustrative data. A café, one year. Figures per year.
  • Total revenue: 120,000.
  • Explicit costs (food, staff, rent, energy): 85,000.
  • Accounting profit = 120,000 − 85,000 = 35,000.
  • Implicit costs: the owner gives up a salary of 30,000 as a manager elsewhere. She put 100,000 of her savings in; at 5% interest they would earn 100,000 × 0.05 = 5,000.
  • Implicit costs = 30,000 + 5,000 = 35,000.
  • Economic profit = 35,000 − 35,000 = 0.
  • The accounts show a profit. Yet she would do just as well elsewhere. That is normal profit.
Mission 4

Normal, supernormal and subnormal profit

  • Normal profit: the minimum profit needed to keep the firm in its current use. TR = TC, so AR = AC.
  • Supernormal profit: profit above normal profit. TR > TC, so AR > AC.
  • Subnormal profit (a loss): profit below normal profit. TR < TC, so AR < AC.
  • Here costs include normal profit. So TR = TC is not "no money": it is exactly enough.
  • Say: "the firm earns normal profit" — not: "the firm makes no profit".
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A designer runs her own studio. Last year her revenue was £200,000 and her explicit costs were £150,000. She gave up a salary of £45,000 to run it, and she put £80,000 of her savings into the business, which could have earned 5% interest. What kind of profit did the studio earn?

  • AR = 9: profit = 0. Normal profit.
  • AR = 8: profit = (8 − 9) × 100 = −100. A loss.
  • (Which output a firm picks comes in a later lesson.)

Mission 4

Why profit matters in a market economy

  • Signal: supernormal profit shows buyers want more. Resources move in. Losses show they want less. Resources move out.
  • Firms enter in search of supernormal profit and leave when they make subnormal profit.
  • Incentive: profit rewards risk-taking, effort and new ideas.
  • Source of finance: retained profit pays for investment and research.
  • Normal profit is the minimum that keeps resources in their current use.
Mission 4

Profit in company accounts

  • Business accounts measure accounting profit. Three levels:
  • Gross profit = revenue − cost of sales.
  • Operating profit = gross profit − operating expenses (overheads).
  • Profit for the year (net profit) = operating profit − interest.
  • Published company accounts then also take off tax.
  • Margin = profit ÷ revenue × 100.
  • Illustrative data, £000: revenue 500, cost of sales 300 → gross profit 200 (margin 40%).
  • Operating expenses 120 → operating profit 80 (16%).
  • Interest 10 → profit for the year 70 (14%).
  • None of these subtracts the owners' implicit costs.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A firm's revenue is £2.0m. Cost of sales is £1.2m, operating expenses are £0.5m and interest is £0.1m. What is its operating profit margin?
Mission 4Evaluate

Is supernormal profit good or bad?

It depends on …

  • Barriers to entry: with free entry, new firms compete it away. Behind high barriers it can last, with higher prices for buyers.
  • Where it comes from: a better product or lower costs, or market power?
  • How it is used: reinvested in research and new products (dynamic efficiency), or paid out while prices stay high.
  • Time: a short-run reward for risk is different from a profit that lasts for decades.
  • Judge: supernormal profit is a useful signal and reward, but it also needs watching when it persists.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. A software engineer leaves a job paying 60,000 a year to build her own app business. In its first year the app earns revenue of 90,000. She pays 50,000 for servers, design and marketing. She has no other costs. Which statement is correct?

Revenue has to cover the owner's best alternative too. Until it does, the business is not earning a real profit.

Mission 5

Mission 5: How can a business be making a profit and still run out of money?

Make a guess: How can a business be making a profit and still run out of money?

Mission 5

Profit as a signal

  • Profit = total revenue − total costs, over a period such as a year.
  • In a competitive market, high profits are a signal: new firms enter to share them.
  • Entry raises supply, so prices and profits are pushed down.
  • Losses send the opposite signal: firms exit, and resources move to uses customers value more.
  • So profit is an incentive: it rewards firms that meet demand at low cost.

Mission 5

Profit margins

  • A margin shows profit as a percentage of revenue: profit ÷ revenue × 100.
  • Gross profit margin = 160 ÷ 400 × 100 = 40%.
  • Operating profit margin = 60 ÷ 400 × 100 = 15%.
  • Profit for the year (net profit) margin = 48 ÷ 400 × 100 = 12%.
  • Gross margin: pricing and direct costs. Operating margin: control of overheads too. Net margin: also the cost of borrowing.
  • Compare margins over time or with similar firms. A supermarket and a software firm have very different "normal" margins.
  • Say: "the margin fell from 15% to 12%" — not: "profit fell". Profit can rise while the margin falls, if revenue rises faster.

Mission 5

How firms raise profit

  • Raise price: works if demand is price inelastic. If elastic, revenue may fall.
  • Cut cost of sales: cheaper suppliers, less waste, bulk buying. Watch quality.
  • Cut operating expenses: smaller offices, fewer managers, cheaper marketing. Watch future sales.
  • Sell more: marketing, new markets, new products. Each extra sale adds its contribution.
  • Every route has a trade-off: profit can rise now and fall later.
Mission 5

Profit is not cash

  • Profit: revenue − costs, counted when a sale is made and when a cost is incurred.
  • Cash flow: money actually coming into and going out of the bank in a period.
  • They differ because of timing and what counts:
  • Sales on credit: revenue now, cash in 30–90 days, or never if the customer fails.
  • Stock bought before it is sold: cash out now, cost counted later.
  • A new machine: cash out now; its cost spread over years in profit.
  • A bank loan: cash in, but not revenue.
  • Say: "cash flow" for money in and out of the bank — not: "profit".
Mission 5

Why cash keeps a firm alive

  • Wages, rent, suppliers and loan repayments must be paid in cash, on time.
  • A firm that cannot pay its debts when they are due is insolvent. Creditors can take it to court and close it.
  • Profit on paper does not pay wages.
  • Overtrading: growing fast, a firm pays for materials and staff long before customers pay. Profit rises; cash runs out.
  • Real case: Carillion, a large UK construction firm, reported a profit in its 2016 accounts.
  • In July 2017 it announced an £845 million write-down on contracts: money it had expected from them would never arrive.
  • On 15 January 2018 it went into compulsory liquidation. Some suppliers had waited up to 120 days to be paid.

Mission 5

Using the forecast to cut risk

  • The bike shop's forecast shows −£1,500 at the end of February.
  • So it needs credit: arrange an overdraft of at least £1,500 before February. Banks lend more readily to firms that plan.
  • Other ways to fill a gap:
  • Get cash in sooner: shorter credit terms for customers; chase late payers.
  • Pay out later: ask suppliers for longer credit. This shifts the problem to them.
  • Spend less up front: lease equipment instead of buying; hold less stock.
  • A forecast is only an estimate. Sales receipts are the hardest line to predict, especially for a new firm.
Mission 5

Evaluate: profit or cash, which matters more?

It depends on…

  • Time horizon. In the short run, cash decides survival. In the long run, a firm that never makes a profit cannot last.
  • Payment terms. Firms paid at the till (cafés) rarely have the gap. Firms paid weeks after delivering (builders, wholesalers) often do.
  • Access to credit. A firm with a good overdraft can bridge a short gap. A new firm with no record may not get one.
  • Judgement: a firm needs both, but cash shortages close firms faster.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. A maker of phone cases wins orders from two big retailers, so its sales double. Its profit margin stays at 15%. It must pay its suppliers within 30 days, but the retailers pay it 90 days after delivery. What is the most likely problem?

Profit records a sale when it happens; cash arrives when the customer pays. Growth widens the gap between the two.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • TC = FC + VC; fixed costs are paid even at zero output.
  • AFC, AVC, ATC are costs per unit; MC is the cost of the next unit.
  • Higher input prices raise variable and average cost; higher fixed costs raise fixed and average cost; productivity gains and lean production cut costs per unit.

Mission 2

  • TR = P × Q; AR = TR ÷ Q = price, so the AR curve is the demand curve; MR = ΔTR ÷ ΔQ.
  • When a firm must cut its price to sell more, MR is below AR; a price taker has P = AR = MR.
  • TR is highest where MR = 0, at unit elasticity; MR is positive where demand is elastic and negative where it is inelastic.

Mission 3

  • Contribution per unit = price − variable cost per unit. It covers fixed costs first, then becomes profit.
  • Break-even output = total fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit. Margin of safety = actual output − break-even output.
  • Break-even rests on forecasts and straight-line assumptions, so treat it as a guide.

Mission 4

  • Accounting profit = TR − explicit costs; economic profit also subtracts implicit costs (opportunity costs of the owner's own resources).
  • Normal profit: AR = AC. Supernormal (abnormal) profit: AR > AC. Subnormal profit (loss): AR < AC.
  • Gross, operating and net profit are accounting measures; margin = profit ÷ revenue × 100.

Mission 5

  • Gross profit = revenue − cost of sales; operating profit = gross profit − operating expenses; net profit = operating profit − interest. Margin = profit ÷ revenue × 100.
  • Profit is not cash: timing differences can leave a profitable firm unable to pay its bills.
  • A cash-flow forecast shows when the balance goes negative, so credit can be arranged in advance.
