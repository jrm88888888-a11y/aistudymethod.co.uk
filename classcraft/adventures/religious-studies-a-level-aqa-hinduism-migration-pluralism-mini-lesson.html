Mission 1

Mission 1: If all paths lead to God, can a Hindu still think one path is best?

Make a guess: If all paths lead to God, can a Hindu still think one path is best?

Mission 1

Diversity is not pluralism

  • Diversity: the fact that many religions (or many traditions) exist side by side.
  • Pluralism: a view about that fact: many religions are valid paths to the truth.
  • Exclusivism: only one religion leads to the truth or to liberation.
  • Inclusivism: one religion holds the fullest truth; others contain part of it.
  • Inside Hinduism itself: Vaishnavas, Shaivas, Shaktas, Smartas and many local traditions live together without one creed or authority.
Mission 1

Ishtadeva: your chosen form of God

  • Ishtadeva (or ishta-devata): "chosen" or "cherished" deity, the form of God a person or family is drawn to.
  • One Brahman, many names and forms: Vishnu, Krishna, Rama, Shiva, Durga, Ganesha.
  • Choosing one focus does not deny the others: a Krishna devotee may also honour Ganesha and visit a Shiva temple.
  • Many Hindus apply the same logic to other religions: Jesus or Allah as further names of the one God.
Mission 1Read the source

One truth, many names

  • "Truth is one; the wise call it by many names." (Rig Veda 1.164.46, paraphrase)

> "Whoso worship me, / Them I exalt; but all men everywhere / Shall fall into my path" (Bhagavad Gita 4:11, Arnold 1885)

> "Nay, and of hearts which follow other gods / In simple faith, their prayers arise to me, / O Kunti's Son! though they pray wrongfully" (Bhagavad Gita 9:23, Arnold 1885)

Mission 1

Vedanta as religion

  • Swami Vivekananda (1863–1902), Chicago, 11 September 1893, quoting a Hindu hymn: "As the different streams having their sources in different places all mingle their water in the sea, so, O Lord, the different paths which men take … all lead to Thee." (Vivekananda, Chicago address, 1893)
  • His claim: Vedanta (above all Advaita) is not one religion among many but the universal religion: every faith, and every Hindu sect, is a partial expression of it.
  • Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan (1888–1975), The Hindu View of Life (1927), paraphrase: Hinduism welcomes all forms of worship as approaches to the one reality, but ranks them, with worship of the formless Absolute highest.
  • Your spec: "the view that all faiths, and all traditions within Hinduism, are partial expressions of Vedanta".
Mission 1Diversity

Hindu responses to "Vedanta as religion"

  • Neo-Vedantins (Ramakrishna Mission): welcome it; Ramakrishna practised Christian and Muslim devotions and taught "as many faiths, so many paths".
  • Theistic Vaishnavas (e.g. Madhva's Dvaita tradition; ISKCON): reject it; God is a person, not impersonal Brahman, and Advaita is not the Hindu norm.
  • Arya Samaj: the Vedas alone are true; Dayananda's Satyarth Prakash (1875) criticised other religions.
  • Hindutva: uses tolerance as a mark of Hindu superiority but draws national boundaries (see Mission 2).
  • Scholars: Paul Hacker (1913–1979) called this pattern inclusivism: other views are absorbed and placed lower. Many Christians and Muslims object that their faith is not a "partial" Vedanta.
Quick check
?Which group is most likely to reject the claim that all religions are partial expressions of Vedanta?
Mission 1

Exclusivism in Hindu settings

  • Hindutva: not exclusive about salvation, but about belonging: full membership of the nation tied to holy land in India (see Mission 2).
  • Sectarian: some Vaishnavas say only devotion to Krishna/Vishnu brings full liberation; Madhva even taught that some souls are never liberated.
  • Conversion: most Hindu traditions do not seek converts; many Hindus see missionary conversion as disrespect for other paths.
  • So Hindu responses to freedom of religious expression differ: some defend everyone's right to preach and convert; others support limits on conversion (see Mission 2).
Mission 1Diversity

Living it: from India to Britain

  • Shared spaces: Hindus visit Sufi shrines and churches; in Britain many mandirs host school visits and interfaith events.
  • Hindu–Sikh–Jain ties: shared festivals (Diwali), sometimes shared buildings in the UK.
  • Organisations: Hindu Council UK and the Hindu Forum of Britain take part in national interfaith work.
  • Tensions: debates about conversion, about caste, and about events in India can strain relations abroad.
Mission 1Evaluate

"Hinduism is inclusivist rather than pluralist."

  • For inclusivism: the Gita includes other worship but calls it "wrongful" (9:23); Radhakrishnan ranks forms of religion; Vedanta-as-religion places Advaita at the top; Hindutva and some sects draw boundaries.
  • For pluralism: ishtadeva; "Truth is one, the wise call it by many names"; Ramakrishna practised several faiths; Gandhi's equal respect for religions; no Hindu creed to exclude anyone.
  • Complication: there is no single Hinduism (Flood): Advaitins, Dvaitins and Neo-Vedantins answer differently.
  • Judge it by: do Hindus rank paths, or only describe them? Is "all paths are Vedanta" generous or absorbing?
Mission check

?A Hindu student tells a Christian friend: "Jesus is a real path to God. In the end, though, your path and mine are both part of the one truth that Vedanta teaches." Which description fits this view best?

Saying all paths lead to God is generous, but if one path defines the summit, it is inclusivism, not equal pluralism.

Mission 2

Savarkar: "Hindu-ness"

  • Hindutva = "Hindu-ness". Set out by V. D. Savarkar (1883–1966) in Essentials of Hindutva (1923), written in prison.
  • Savarkar, himself a rationalist, separated Hindutva (a people's culture, history and nation) from Hinduism (religious belief).
  • His definition (paraphrase): a Hindu is someone who regards India (Bharatvarsha) as both pitribhumi (fatherland) and punyabhumi (holy land).
  • So Sikhs, Jains and Buddhists count: their faiths began in India.
  • Muslims and Christians, even if Indian for generations, do not fully belong: their holy lands are elsewhere.

Mission 2

The Sangh Parivar

  • Sangh Parivar = "family of organisations" around the RSS (Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh, "National Volunteer Organisation"), founded 1925 in Nagpur by K. B. Hedgewar.
  • RSS: daily shakhas (branches) for physical training, discipline and "character-building"; M. S. Golwalkar (1906–1973) led it 1940–1973.
  • VHP (Vishva Hindu Parishad, 1964): religious leaders and campaigns, e.g. for a Rama temple at Ayodhya.
  • BJP (Bharatiya Janata Party, 1980): the political party; it has led India's government since 2014.
  • Others: student, labour and youth wings (e.g. Bajrang Dal), and social service bodies.
  • Ayodhya: the Babri Masjid was demolished in 1992; the Supreme Court gave the site for a temple (2019); the Ram Mandir was consecrated in 2024.
  • The Sangh's definition of Hindu follows Savarkar: Indian culture (Bharatiya culture) is Hindu culture; all Indians share it.
Mission 2

Freedom of religious expression

  • India's Constitution gives the right to "profess, practise and propagate" religion (Article 25, paraphrase).
  • Anti-conversion laws ("freedom of religion" acts) in several states restrict conversion by "force, fraud or allurement". Supporters: they protect poor Hindus from pressure. Critics: they limit the right to propagate and to convert.
  • Cow protection laws in many states: for many Hindus the cow is sacred; for some Muslims, Christians and Dalits it limits diet and livelihood.
  • Ghar wapsi ("homecoming"): Sangh campaigns to reconvert Christians and Muslims to Hinduism.
  • Hindu responses differ: some see these as defending Hindu expression after centuries of conversion; others, including many Hindus, see them as restricting others' freedom.
Mission 2

Hindutva and secularisation

  • India is a secular state (Constitution 1950; "secular" added to the Preamble in 1976). Indian secularism means equal respect for all religions, not strict separation (see Mission 3).
  • Hindutva critics call this "pseudo-secularism": they argue it treats minorities better than Hindus (e.g. separate Muslim personal law, state control of Hindu temples).
  • Their response to secularisation (the weakening of religion in public life): reassert Hindu culture in public: temples, festivals, symbols, school history, the name Bharat.
  • So Hindutva is both a response to secularism (the state's policy) and to secularisation (social change).
Mission 2Diversity

Hindus on Hindutva

  • Supporters: Hindutva is cultural, not theocratic; it includes all Indic faiths; it corrects centuries of foreign rule and an unfair secularism. India's Supreme Court in 1995 described Hindutva as indicating a "way of life".
  • Gandhian and Neo-Vedantin critics: true Hinduism is tolerant and universal; Hindutva turns a spiritual tradition into a political identity.
  • Secular and liberal Hindus: Hindutva threatens minorities' equal citizenship; they point to violence in Ayodhya (1992) and Gujarat (2002).
  • Dalit critics: Hindutva speaks for upper-caste Hinduism and hides caste inequality.
  • Many Sikhs, Jains and Buddhists: reject being counted as "Hindu".
  • Scholars, e.g. Christophe Jaffrelot (The Hindu Nationalist Movement and Indian Politics, 1996), study it as ethnic nationalism; supporters reject this label.
Quick check
?A Hindu writes: "My Hinduism is the Gita and my daily puja. It has nothing to do with which party wins elections." Which position is this closest to?
Mission 2Evaluate

"Hindutva is the only effective Hindu response to secularisation."

  • For: it has mass support and electoral success; it gives a clear identity in a fast-changing society; it has brought Hindu symbols back into public life.
  • Against: other responses also work: Neo-Vedanta's universal spirituality; devotional movements (ISKCON, Swaminarayan) that grow without politics; reform movements serving the poor (Ramakrishna Mission).
  • Against: "effective" for whom? Critics say it secures power but divides society and narrows Hinduism.
  • Judge it by: what counts as "effective" (numbers, depth of faith, social harmony)? Is Hindutva mainly religious at all?
Mission check

?A Jain businessman and a Muslim teacher were both born in Mumbai and have never left India. On Savarkar's definition of Hindutva, how would they be classed?

Hindutva draws its line by holy land, not by belief: that is what makes it a cultural and political identity rather than a creed.

Mission 3

A different kind of secular

  • The Constitution (1950) has no state religion; "secular" was added to its Preamble in 1976.
  • Equality before the law and no discrimination on grounds of religion or caste (Articles 14–15).
  • Everyone may "profess, practise and propagate" religion (Article 25, paraphrase); religious groups may run their own affairs and schools (Articles 26, 30).
  • Not a wall of separation (as in the USA or France): the Indian state engages with all religions: it funds pilgrimages, manages many Hindu temples, and has reformed Hindu practice by law.
  • Political theorist Rajeev Bhargava calls this "principled distance": the state may treat religions differently, but for the sake of equality.
Mission 3

Diversity in fact

  • 2011 Census: Hindus 79.8%, Muslims 14.2%, Christians 2.3%, Sikhs 1.7%, Buddhists 0.7%, Jains 0.4%. (The next census is under way; its religion figures are not yet out.)
  • India has one of the world's largest Muslim populations.
  • Born in India (Hindu, Sikh, Jain, Buddhist): share ideas such as karma, rebirth, dharma.
  • Born outside (Islam, Christianity, Zoroastrianism, Judaism): sometimes seen by Hindu nationalists as foreign (see Mission 2).
  • Tensions: Partition (1947) and later riots; the 1984 anti-Sikh violence after Indira Gandhi's assassination.
  • Shared life too: Hindus visit Sufi shrines (dargahs); mixed neighbourhoods and festivals.
Quick check
?Which practice shows that Indian secularism differs from a strict separation of state and religion?
Mission 3

Personal law

  • Personal law: family matters (marriage, divorce, inheritance, adoption) are governed by each religion's law.
  • Hindu Code (1955–56): Hindu Marriage Act, Hindu Succession Act and others; brought monogamy, divorce and inheritance for daughters (made equal in 2005).
  • It also covers Sikhs, Jains and Buddhists: the Hindu Code Acts define them as "Hindus" (e.g. Hindu Marriage Act, section 2), as Article 25 (Explanation II) also does for its reform clause. Many Sikhs object.
  • Muslim Personal Law (Shariat Application Act, 1937), and separate Christian and Parsi laws.
  • A secular option for anyone: the Special Marriage Act (1954), e.g. for interfaith couples.
Mission 3

Shah Bano and a Uniform Civil Code

  • The Constitution asks the state to "endeavour" to secure a Uniform Civil Code (UCC) (Article 44): one family law for all.
  • Shah Bano (1985): the Supreme Court granted a divorced Muslim woman maintenance; Parliament's 1986 Act was seen as reversing it after Muslim protests.
  • Shayara Bano (2017): the Supreme Court struck down instant triple talaq; a 2019 Act made it a criminal offence.
  • Uttarakhand passed India's first state UCC (2024).
  • The debate: gender equality and national unity vs minorities' religious freedom.
Mission 3Diversity

Hindu responses to personal law

  • Hindutva (BJP, RSS): a UCC now; separate Muslim law is "appeasement" and divides the nation.
  • Liberal and feminist Hindus: equal rights for women of every faith, but reform should not be imposed by a Hindu majority; some prefer reform within each community.
  • Traditional Hindus in the 1950s: opposed the Hindu Code as interfering with dharma and the shastras; the RSS and Hindu Mahasabha joined the protests. B. R. Ambedkar, its chief drafter, resigned in 1951 partly over delays.
  • Hindus who stress pluralism: different communities, different laws, is itself a Hindu way of living with diversity.
  • Your spec: "different Hindu responses to this".
Quick check
?Which group opposed the reform of Hindu family law in the early 1950s?
Mission 3

Secularisation in India

  • Signs: cities, education, the internet, consumer culture; more inter-caste marriage; decline of some rituals.
  • Counter-signs: festivals and pilgrimages are larger than ever; television epics; new gurus and temples; religion very visible in politics.
  • So India shows change in religion more than simple decline.
  • Orthodox view: history runs in fixed cycles; this is the Kali Yuga, an age of foretold decline in dharma.
  • So orthodox Hindus can accept some secularisation as expected, not as a defeat, while keeping their own dharma.
Quick check
?Why might an orthodox Hindu accept some secularisation calmly?
Mission 3Evaluate

"India is a genuinely secular state."

  • For: no state religion; equal rights in the Constitution; courts strike down religious discrimination; minorities have held the highest offices.
  • Against (Hindutva critics): the state favours minorities (separate personal law) and controls Hindu temples but not mosques or churches: "pseudo-secular".
  • Against (minority and liberal critics): in practice Hindu majoritarianism is growing: cow-protection and anti-conversion laws; communal violence; exclusion of Dalit Christians and Muslims from SC status.
  • Another view: "secular" in India never meant separation; judged by equal respect, the question is whether respect is in fact equal.
  • Judge it by: which definition of secular? Constitution or practice? Which period?
Mission check

?A Sikh couple in Delhi marry. Unless they choose the Special Marriage Act, which law governs their marriage?

India's secularism lets each religion keep its own family law, and that is exactly why it is argued over.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Diversity is a fact; pluralism, inclusivism and exclusivism are views about it.
  • Ishtadeva, Rig Veda 1.164.46 and Gita 4:11 support openness; Gita 9:23 and Radhakrishnan show ranking (inclusivism).
  • "Vedanta as religion" (Vivekananda): every faith is a partial expression of Vedanta; Hindutva and some sects are exclusivist in different senses.
  • It is welcomed by Neo-Vedantins and rejected by theistic Vaishnavas (Madhva's Dvaita, ISKCON) and by many in other faiths.

Mission 2

  • Savarkar (1923): a Hindu treats India as both fatherland and holy land; the Sangh Parivar (RSS, VHP, BJP) builds on this.
  • Hindus are divided: supporters see cultural pride and fair treatment; critics see exclusion of minorities and a politicised Hinduism.

Mission 3

  • India's secularism is "principled distance": equal respect and state engagement, not separation.
  • Personal law: Hindu law (also for Sikhs, Jains, Buddhists) was reformed in 1955–56; the UCC debate divides Hindus and minorities.
  • Secularisation in India means change more than decline; orthodox Hindus may see it as part of the Kali Yuga.
