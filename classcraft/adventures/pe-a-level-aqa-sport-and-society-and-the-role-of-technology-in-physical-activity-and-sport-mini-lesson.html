Sport, Society & Technology

This mini-lesson works through AQA's Sport and Society and the Role of Technology: how modern sport emerged and evolved, how it became a global commercial product, the golden triangle and the media, and the growing role of technology in performance, officiating, equipment and the spectator experience — plus its ethics and limits.

Society · pre-industrial sport

Where modern sport began: popular recreation

Before industrialisation, physical activity in Britain took the form of popular recreation — the sports and pastimes of a largely rural, agricultural society.

  • Occasional and tied to festivals or holy days, because working time was dictated by the seasons.
  • Local and with few, unwritten rules that varied from village to village (for example the sprawling mob-football games).
  • Often violent, involving heavy wagering, and reflecting a sharp social divide between the pastimes of the gentry and those of the working population.

Key idea: sport did not exist in a vacuum — it mirrored the society around it. Limited transport, communication and free time meant activity was irregular and uncodified.

Society · rational recreation

Industrialisation & rational recreation

The nineteenth-century move to towns and factories transformed sport into rational recreation — organised, regular and rule-bound activity suited to an urban, industrial society.

  • Codification: written rules and standardised playing spaces, so teams from different places could compete fairly.
  • National Governing Bodies were formed to administer each sport, arrange fixtures and enforce the rules.
  • Regular competition became possible thanks to the railways, set factory hours and a growing urban public — with public schools and the idea of athleticism spreading team games.

Drivers to remember: urbanisation, transport (railways), communication, a decline in cruel sports, and greater free time all pushed sport towards a rational, respectable form.

Society · the global game

The rise of global, commercialised sport

Across the twentieth and twenty-first centuries sport became a global commercial product. Satellite and then digital broadcasting carried events worldwide, turning leading performers into international brands.

  • Globalisation: leagues and events reach audiences on every continent, and clubs recruit and market internationally.
  • Professionalism: top performers are full-time athletes funded by wages, prize money and endorsement.
  • Spectacle: events are staged as entertainment, with the paying and viewing audience central to the finances of sport.

Thread it together: popular recreation → rational recreation → the modern global, commercialised game. Each stage reflects the society and technology of its time.

Commerce · the golden triangle

Commercialisation & the golden triangle

Commercialisation means treating sport as a business that generates income. Its engine is often drawn as the golden triangle — three elements that feed one another.

  • Sport supplies the events, stars and drama that attract an audience.
  • The media pay for broadcasting rights, delivering that audience to advertisers.
  • Sponsors pay sport (and the media) for exposure to those large, engaged audiences.
Commerce · impact of the media

The impact of the media on sport

Media coverage brings huge revenue, but it also changes the sport itself — a genuinely two-sided relationship you should be able to argue in an exam.

  • Positive: funding through broadcast deals, wider global audiences, raised profile for minority sports, and growth in role models that can inspire participation.
  • Negative: rules, formats and timings altered to suit broadcasters; more breaks in play; over-exposure of a few "showpiece" sports while others are marginalised; and pressure on performers to entertain.

Evaluate, don't just list: the media both funds and reshapes sport — the same deal that pays the wages can also dictate kick-off times and playing conditions.

Technology · analytics & performance

Technology for performance & analysis

Technology now gathers rich objective data on how athletes train and perform, informing coaching decisions that used to rely on the naked eye.

  • GPS tracking (worn in vests) records distance covered, speed and workload, helping to manage training and fatigue.
  • Motion and video analysis break technique down frame by frame so faults can be identified and corrected.
  • Wearable technology — heart-rate monitors and similar sensors — tracks physiological responses during training.
  • Testing and monitoring in the lab and field help set training zones and check adaptation over time.

Why it matters: better data means more individualised, evidence-based training — but it also demands the money, expertise and time to collect and interpret it.

Technology · officiating

Technology for officiating & fair outcomes

A second strand of technology helps officials reach accurate, fair decisions, reducing the impact of human error on results.

  • Hawk-Eye: camera-based ball-tracking that reconstructs the ball's path, used for line calls in tennis and for decisions in cricket.
  • Goal-line technology: instantly confirms whether the whole ball has crossed the goal line in football.
  • VAR (Video Assistant Referee): a match official reviews video to check goals, penalties, red cards and mistaken identity in football.
  • TMO (Television Match Official): the video official used in rugby to check tries and foul play.

Aim: more correct and consistent decisions — but reviews can interrupt the flow of play and delay the celebration for players and fans.

Technology · equipment & facilities

Technology in equipment & facilities

Advances in materials and design have reshaped what performers can do, and improved facilities support both training and safety.

  • Equipment: lighter, stronger rackets, clubs, bikes and footwear; engineered playing surfaces; improved protective gear that reduces injury risk.
  • Facilities: indoor and all-weather venues, altitude and environmental chambers, and advanced medical and rehabilitation suites.

Watch the fairness angle: governing bodies sometimes regulate or ban equipment that gives an unfair advantage, to protect the integrity of a sport and keep records meaningful.

Technology · ethics & limits

The ethics & limits of technology

Technology is not an unqualified good. A strong answer weighs its benefits against its drawbacks.

  • Cost and access: the best analytics, equipment and facilities are expensive, which can widen the gap between rich and poorer performers or nations.
  • Over-reliance: too much dependence on technology can undermine the authority of officials and the traditional feel of a game.
  • Spectator experience: long reviews and stoppages can disrupt the atmosphere and delay celebrations.
  • Data and pressure: constant monitoring raises questions about workload, privacy and the pressure it places on athletes.

Balance it: the same technology that makes decisions fairer or training smarter can also raise costs, break up play and change the spectator's experience.

Society · ethics & deviance

Ethics & deviance: gamesmanship and doping

Commercial pressure to win raises ethical questions. Two ideas sit at the heart of this topic.

  • Sportsmanship — playing fairly and within the spirit of the game; contrasted with gamesmanship, bending the rules or their spirit to gain an advantage without technically breaking them (for example, deliberate time-wasting or gaining a psychological edge).
  • Doping — using banned substances or methods to enhance performance. WADA (the World Anti-Doping Agency) exists to harmonise anti-doping rules and testing across sports and countries.

Why athletes dope, and why it is condemned: the pull of winning, money and status against the health risks, the unfairness to clean competitors and the damage to the reputation of sport.
