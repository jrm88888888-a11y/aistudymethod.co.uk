Mission 1: Air bubbles trapped in Antarctic ice are up to 800,000 years old. What can they tell us about past climate?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel A-level Geography (9GE0)

  • 2A.1a chronology of glacial and interglacial periods since the start of the Pleistocene

Mission 1

Why we need proxies

  • Thermometer records cover only the last century or two. Older climates are read from proxy data: natural records that respond to climate.
  • Historical documents reach back centuries: harvest dates for grapes were used to reconstruct summer temperatures in Paris "from 1370 to 1879".
  • Long-term proxies (hundreds of thousands to millions of years): ice cores, ocean-floor deposits.
  • Medium-term proxies (thousands of years): pollen, lake sediments, tree rings.
  • Pollen in lake sediments records the vegetation that grew nearby, and so the conditions that suited it.
Mission 1

Ice cores

  • Snow builds up in layers that "contain dust, air bubbles, or isotopes of oxygen".
  • The ice "encloses small bubbles of air that contain a sample of the atmosphere": a direct record of past greenhouse gases.
  • Temperature comes from "the ratios of different water isotopes": less heavy oxygen in the ice "means that temperatures were cooler".
  • Oldest continuous record: EPICA Dome C, Antarctica, back 800,000 years.
Mission 1

What the ice shows

  • CO₂ was "low … during cold times, and high … during warm periods".
  • Glacials were "interspersed roughly every 100,000 years" by warmer interglacials.
  • In the ice-age cycles of the last million years or so, CO₂ "didn't get any higher than 300 ppm". In 2025 it reached 425.7 ppm.
  • Methane and nitrous oxide are higher than at any time in at least 800,000 years.
Quick check
?A section of an Antarctic ice core has less heavy oxygen than the layers above and below it, and its air bubbles hold less CO₂. What does it most likely record?
Mission 1

Ocean-floor deposits

  • Deep-sea sediments hold the shells of tiny plankton, including foraminifera; their chemistry, and which species are present, record past oceans.
  • In glacials, light oxygen is locked up in ice sheets, so seawater, and shells grown in it, gain heavy oxygen; colder water adds more heavy oxygen too.
  • So a shell's oxygen-isotope ratio (δ¹⁸O) tracks ice volume and temperature together.
  • The LR04 stack combines δ¹⁸O records from 57 sites and covers 5.3 million years.
Quick check
?Which proxy gives the longest continuous record?
Mission 1

From greenhouse to icehouse Earth

  • Benthic foraminifera records over the last 66 million years show four climate states: Hothouse, Warmhouse, Coolhouse, Icehouse.
  • Antarctica's ice sheet formed at the Eocene/Oligocene boundary, about 34 million years ago.
  • A model study found falling CO₂ was the main driver, with Southern Ocean gateways playing "a secondary role".
Mission 1

The Pleistocene: glacials and interglacials

  • The Pleistocene (and Quaternary) begins 2.58 million years ago; the Holocene begins 0.0117 million years (11,700 years) ago.
  • Over the last 800,000 years, cold glacials were broken by warmer interglacials roughly every 100,000 years.
  • The last interglacial, about 125,000 years ago, had sea level likely 5–10 m higher than today.
  • Last Glacial Maximum: nearly all ice sheets were at their largest from 26,500 to about 19,000–20,000 years ago.
Quick check
?How long ago did the Pleistocene begin, and roughly how often did glacials recur in the last 800,000 years?
Mission 1

End of the last glaciation and the Holocene

  • Northern deglaciation began 19,000–20,000 years ago, driven by rising northern summer sunlight.
  • Warming was not smooth: a last cold phase, the Younger Dryas (in Scotland, the Loch Lomond Stadial), ended abruptly.
  • The base of the Holocene is fixed at 11,700 years before AD 2000 in the NGRIP Greenland ice core, at the first sign of warming after the Younger Dryas.
  • The Holocene varied too: its most recent multi-century warm period was about 6,500 years ago.
Mission 1

Causes of past change

  • Milankovitch cycles: eccentricity (about 100,000 years), obliquity (about 41,000) and precession (about 23,000) change where and when sunlight falls.
  • Atmospheric gases: interglacials began with a small rise in northern summer sunlight; extra CO₂ then "greatly amplified" the warming.
  • Solar output: total solar irradiance varies by only about 0.1%.
  • Volcanic eruptions: Pinatubo (1991) cooled Earth by about 0.6 °C over 15 months.
Mission 1

The Anthropocene

  • Anthropocene: an informal name for the period of strong human influence on the Earth system.
  • A working group proposed a formal start in 1952, marked by a sharp rise in plutonium in varved lake sediment at Crawford Lake.
  • On 26 March 2024 the IUGS and ICS reported that the proposal for a formal Anthropocene Epoch was rejected.
  • Today's warming is not orbital: current orbital positions "predict our planet should be cooling, not warming".
Quick check
?Why can Milankovitch cycles not explain the warming of the last century?

Mission 2

Dryness, not heat, makes a desert

  • The USGS summarises Meigs's scheme: arid land gets under 250 mm of rain a year; semi-arid land 250–500 mm; extremely arid land has at least 12 consecutive months without rain.
  • "Arid and extremely arid land are deserts", while semi-arid grasslands are usually called steppes.
  • About one-third of Earth's land is desert. Deserts "may be hot, they may be cold … But deserts are always dry".
Mission 2

The cryosphere

  • The cryosphere "refers to Earth's ice in all its forms": snow, lake and river ice, frozen ground and permafrost, ice sheets, ice caps and glaciers, ice shelves, icebergs and sea ice.
  • Role in the energy balance: snow's bright surface "reflects sunlight, cooling the planet"; snow can reflect up to 90% of the Sun's energy, open ocean only up to 10%.
  • Role as a store: glaciers and ice caps hold about 68.7% of the world's fresh water.
  • Glacial ice covers about 10% of Earth's land today.
Mission 2

Classifying ice masses by scale and location

  • Ice sheet: glacial land ice over 50,000 km². Only two today: Greenland and Antarctica.
  • Ice cap: under 50,000 km², dome-shaped, e.g. Vatnajökull, Iceland.
  • Icefield: like an ice cap but usually smaller, with flow "more strongly influenced by underlying terrain".
  • Valley glaciers flow down valleys; cirque glaciers occupy bowl-shaped hollows high on mountainsides, "wide rather than long".
Mission 2

Polar and temperate glaciers

  • Polar (cold-based) glaciers are frozen to their beds. Where the base is very cold, movement there "can be a tiny fraction of the speed of flow at the surface". Little erosion or meltwater.
  • Temperate (warm-based) glaciers have ice at or near melting point at the base: a glacier can slide "on a thin layer of water at its base". Fast flow, strong erosion, abundant meltwater.
  • Many large glaciers are polythermal: cold at the margins or surface, warm-based beneath thick ice.
Quick check
?A dome of ice in Arctic Canada covers 20,000 km² and spreads outward in all directions. How should it be classed?
Mission 2

Ice sheets now and in the Pleistocene

  • Today: Greenland's ice sheet covers about 1.7 million km² and Antarctica's almost 14 million km².
  • At the last glacial maximum, glaciers covered about 32% of Earth's land. Ice sheets also covered most of Canada (the Laurentide Ice Sheet) and Scandinavia.
  • Evidence of that extent: erratics, till sheets and moraines far from today's ice. Big Rock, a 16,500-tonne quartzite boulder on the Alberta prairie, came from what is now Jasper National Park and was carried on a glacier.
  • In the UK, the north-west Highlands hold "erratic boulders, dropped by retreating glaciers at the end of the ice age".
Mission 2

High-altitude glaciated uplands and relict landscapes

  • Present-day: Glacier National Park, Montana, still has glaciers; physical modelling "predicts near total Glacier National Park glacier disappearance by 2100".
  • Relict (UK): in the Brecon Beacons, repeated ice during the late Devensian (26,000–15,000 years ago) created "modified river valleys, oversteepend valley heads, cirques and escarpments".
  • At Glen Roy, Scotland, the "Parallel Roads" are shorelines of a glacier-dammed lake from the Loch Lomond Stadial, over 11,700 years ago.
  • These landscapes are relict: shaped by glaciers that no longer exist.

Mission check

?Coober Pedy, in the Australian outback, gets about 144 mm of rain a year. Utqiaġvik, on Alaska's frozen Arctic coast, gets about 137 mm of precipitation. Why do both count as deserts?

Deserts are defined by dryness. Antarctica's ice has built up over long periods despite low precipitation, about 150 mm of water a year on average and under 50 mm on the plateau: it is a polar desert, like Utqiaġvik, while Coober Pedy is a hot desert.

Mission 3

Permafrost and the active layer

  • Periglacial areas are cold, unglaciated areas shaped by frost.
  • Permafrost: ground at or below 0 °C for at least two years. It is "defined solely by temperature".
  • Active layer: the top layer, which thaws each summer. Taliks: unfrozen ground within permafrost.
  • About 85% of Alaska has some form of permafrost.
Mission 3

Zones, now and in the past

  • Continuous (90–100% of the ground frozen), discontinuous (50–90%), sporadic (10–50%).
  • Permafrost underlies about 25% of the Northern Hemisphere's exposed land: about 65% of it in Eurasia, 35% in North America and Greenland.
  • Another estimate is about 15%, counting only ground modelled as frozen rather than whole zones.
  • In glacial periods, periglacial conditions reached far beyond today's limits: hollows interpreted as collapsed "paleo pingos" occur "in areas now without permafrost".
Quick check
?A survey finds that 60% of the ground in a region is permafrost. Which zone is it?
Mission 3

Frost processes

  • Freeze–thaw: water in cracks expands as it freezes and prises rock apart.
  • Frost heave: water drawn to the freezing front builds ice lenses, which lift the ground and push stones up. Under roads, "The expanding ice pushes up the road and creates a hump".
  • Nivation: freeze–thaw and meltwater under a lasting snow patch deepen a hollow.
Mission 3

The active layer on the move

  • Solifluction: permafrost acts "like a waterproof barrier", so the thawed active layer stays saturated and flows slowly downslope.
  • Meltwater erosion: spring meltwater cannot soak into frozen ground, so it runs off and erodes. High winds lift dry, fine sediment from bare ground.
Mission 3

Patterned ground

  • Circles, polygons and stripes that form "when water and ice in the ground move the rocky soil around". In polar deserts they can be up to 5 m across.
  • Frost heave pushes stones up and outwards, so they gather at the edges (sorted patterned ground).
  • On slopes, circles are drawn out into stripes.
Mission 3

Ice wedges

  • In winter, frozen ground contracts and cracks; in spring, meltwater enters, freezes and widens the crack.
  • Repeated each year, the crack grows into a vertical ice wedge.
  • Wedges join into ice-wedge polygons across the tundra.
Quick check
?Why do stones collect at the edges of the circles in sorted patterned ground?
Mission 3

Pingos

  • Pingos are ice-cored hills 3–70 m high and 30–1,000 m across, found only in permafrost.
  • Closed-system: under a lake lies unfrozen ground (a talik) within permafrost. When the lake drains, the bed freezes from the surface and from the sides, squeezing water to the centre under pressure; it lifts the ground and freezes into an ice core.
  • Ibyuk Pingo is 49 m high, the world's second-tallest. It is one of 1,350 pingos in the region, most of which grew in drained lake beds.
  • Open-system: groundwater under pressure rises through gaps in discontinuous permafrost and freezes near the surface.
Quick check
?On a valley floor in discontinuous permafrost, groundwater flowing down from the hills rises under pressure and freezes into a mound. What has formed?
Mission 3

Loess and a tundra landscape

  • Loess: wind-blown silt. Winds strip fine sediment from bare outwash and dry ground beyond the ice and drop it as thick, even layers far away.
  • Tuktoyaktuk Peninsula, Arctic Canada: flat tundra with about 1,350 pingos, most grown in drained lakes on sandy ground, set among thaw lakes and ice-wedge polygons.
  • Processes and landforms combine: frost heave, ice wedges and pingos make one characteristic tundra landscape.
Mission 3

Thermokarst

  • Thermokarst: thawing ground ice "can gradually cave in", leaving "irregular surfaces of marshy bowls".
  • Permafrost can contain up to 80% ice; when it melts, the ground can collapse.
  • Signs of thaw include thaw slumps, degraded ice-wedge polygons and thermokarst lakes.
Mission 3

Landforms from past climates

  • Pingos and patterned ground form only under permafrost, so finding them shows the climate when they formed.
  • When permafrost thaws, a pingo's ice core melts and it collapses. Mackay links such collapse features to "paleo pingos, in areas now without permafrost".
Mission 3

Modification now and in future

  • From 2007 to 2016, permafrost temperature rose by 0.29 ± 0.12 °C globally. From 1979 to 2021, the Arctic warmed nearly four times as fast as the globe.
  • By 2100, near-surface permafrost area is projected to fall by 2–66% under a low-emissions pathway (RCP2.6) and 30–99% under a high one (RCP8.5) (IPCC).
  • Thaw deepens the active layer, degrades ice wedges into thermokarst troughs and can make pingos collapse.
Mission check

?In continuous permafrost in Siberia, a lake drained 30 years ago. Now a round mound with an ice core is rising from the middle of the old lake bed. What is driving its growth?

Ibyuk is a pingo. Under a drained lake, unfrozen ground (a talik) was surrounded by permafrost. As it froze inward, water was forced to the centre under pressure, lifted the ground and froze into an ice core.

Mission 4

A glacier is a system

  • Inputs: snow, mainly in the accumulation zone, high on the glacier.
  • Store: ice, which flows downhill under its own weight.
  • Outputs: meltwater, vapour and icebergs, mainly from the ablation zone lower down.
  • Equilibrium line: the boundary between the two zones, where a year's gain equals a year's loss.
Mission 4

From snow to ice sheet

  • Buried snow compacts and recrystallises. After about a year it becomes firn, about two-thirds as dense as water; over time, firn becomes glacier ice.
  • An ice sheet is glacial ice covering more than 50,000 km². Only two exist today: Greenland and Antarctica.
  • In the last ice age, ice sheets also covered much of North America and Scandinavia.
Mission 4

How ice is gained and lost

  • Accumulation: direct snowfall; avalanches from slopes above; snow blown in by wind.
  • Ablation: melting; sublimation, ice turning straight to vapour; evaporation of meltwater; calving of icebergs where ice meets water; avalanches from the glacier's own front.
  • Ablation dominates at low altitude and in summer; accumulation at high altitude and in winter.
Mission 4

Mass balance

  • Mass balance = accumulation − ablation, over a year.
  • Positive: the glacier thickens and its snout may advance. Negative: it thins and its snout may retreat. Glaciers grow "when winter snowfall exceeds summer melting" (USGS).
  • Measured in water equivalent (w.e.): the depth of water if the ice gained or lost were spread evenly over the glacier.
  • Ice still flows downhill whatever the balance.
Quick check
?In one year a glacier gains 2.1 m w.e. and loses 1.4 m w.e. What follows?
Mission 4

Why rates vary over time

  • Daily: melt peaks in the warm afternoon and slows overnight.
  • Seasonal: winter is mostly accumulation; summer is mostly ablation.
  • Decades: reference glaciers lost on average 214 mm w.e. a year in the 1980s and 896 in the 2010s.
  • Millennia: glaciers covered about 32% of land at the last glacial maximum; about 10% today.
Mission 4

Greenland: feedbacks

  • The ice sheet was close to balance in the 1990s; losses peaked at 345 ± 66 Gt a year in 2011.
  • 1992–2018: 3,902 ± 342 Gt lost, raising sea level by 10.8 ± 0.9 mm. About half came from extra meltwater runoff, half from faster glacier flow and calving.
  • Positive feedback: melt darkens the surface, so it absorbs more sunlight and melts more. Box et al. found this albedo feedback positive over 97% of the ice sheet (2000–2011).
  • Negative feedback: in over 63% of the accumulation area, warmer years also brought more snow, damping the response.
Quick check
?Greenland lost 3,902 Gt of ice in 1992–2018, raising sea level by 10.8 mm. About how many gigatonnes raise sea level by 1 mm?
Mission check

?Stakes show a glacier's ice moving 80 m down-valley a year near its snout. Yet the snout ends 20 m further up-valley than a year ago. What explains this?

Retreat describes only where the snout is. NSIDC: a retreating glacier "simply melts faster than it flows". That is how Muir Glacier retreated 12 km while its ice kept moving towards the sea: its mass balance was negative.

Mission 5

Warm-based and cold-based ice

  • Pressure melting point: the weight of ice above lowers the temperature at which ice melts.
  • Warm-based: the base reaches it, so meltwater forms at the bed. Meltwater "encourages basal sliding, and rapid ice velocities".
  • Cold-based: the base stays below it, frozen to the rock; flow is "very slow or no flow".
  • Dry Valleys, Antarctica: mean annual air temperature about −19.8 °C; glaciers' basal temperatures about −17 °C, "no free running water".
  • Edexcel calls these temperate and polar glaciers.
Mission 5

What sets a glacier's base temperature?

  • Air and ground temperature: colder climates give colder ice.
  • Geothermal heat warms some glaciers from below.
  • Ice thickness: thick ice presses harder, so the base reaches melting point more easily.
  • Polythermal glaciers are "partly frozen to their bed": thick ice may be warm-based while thin margins stay frozen.
Quick check

Mission 5

How ice moves

  • Internal deformation (creep): "All glaciers flow by creep". Ice is plastic: under its own weight, ice crystals deform and slip.
  • Basal sliding: ice "melts under pressure, resulting in a film of water at the ice-bed interface", and slides on it.
  • Bed deformation: some glaciers sit on soft, wet sediment that itself deforms.
  • Speeds vary hugely: in 1986, Hubbard Glacier, Alaska, moved forward 10 m a day during a surge, a short burst of fast flow.
  • Edexcel calls basal sliding basal slip.
Mission 5

Regelation creep

  • Ice meets a bump on its bed. Pressure rises on the up-glacier side, so ice there melts.
  • The water flows round the bump to the low-pressure side and refreezes (regelation).
  • Around the bump, ice also deforms faster, because stress is higher there.
  • So a rough bed can increase "melting and ice flow".
Mission 5

What sets the speed?

  • Slope and thickness: the driving force depends on ice density, thickness and surface slope.
  • Basal temperature: a thawed bed allows sliding; a frozen one does not.
  • Altitude: low, warmer ground gives more meltwater at the bed.
  • Lithology: soft, deformable sediment lets ice move fast; hard bedrock slows it.
  • Size and mass balance: a big budget (much gained, much lost) pushes more ice through each year.
Mission 5

Polar vs temperate rates; feedbacks

  • Polar ice frozen to its bed: "very slow or no flow". Temperate ice slides as well as deforms, so it moves faster.
  • But polar ice sheets drain through ice streams faster than 0.8 km a year.
  • Positive feedback: Jakobshavn Isbræ, Greenland, ran nearly three times faster in 2012 than in the mid-1990s as its front retreated into a deep basin.
  • Surges: Hubbard Glacier, Alaska, moved about 10 m a day in 1986.
  • Negative feedback: a fast glacier thins, which lowers its driving force and slows it.

Mission 6

What glaciers do to rock

  • Erosion: ice and its debris wear away the bed and valley sides.
  • Entrainment: loosened rock is picked up, often by freezing onto the base of the ice.
  • Transport: debris rides on, in and under the ice.
  • Deposition: debris is dropped where the ice melts.
Mission 6

How ice erodes

  • Plucking (quarrying): meltwater freezes onto jointed rock, and moving ice tears blocks away. "As the ice moves, it plucks rock from the sides and bottom of the valleys" (NPS).
  • Abrasion: rock fragments in the base scrape the bed like sandpaper, polishing it and cutting striations.
  • Crushing: heavy ice presses debris onto the bed and fractures it. Basal melting: water at the base lets ice slide and scour, and carries debris away.
  • Freeze–thaw and mass movement on slopes above the ice drop more debris onto it.

Mission 6

Corries

  • A corrie (cirque) is a steep-sided, armchair-shaped hollow at a glacier's head, "a rounded amphitheater" (NPS).
  • Ice turns in rotational flow. Plucking steepens the back wall; abrasion deepens the floor; a rock lip is left at the front.
  • After the ice melts, a small lake, a tarn, may fill the hollow. Piegan Glacier still sits in a cirque in Glacier National Park, Montana.
Mission 6

Arêtes and pyramidal peaks

  • Arête: a knife-edged ridge left where two glaciers carved either side, such as the Garden Wall, Glacier National Park.
  • Corries cut backwards (headward erosion) until only a ridge separates them.
  • Pyramidal peak (horn): a peak "scraped nearly vertical by glaciers on three or more sides", such as Reynolds Mountain.

Mission 6

Glacial troughs

  • Rivers cut V-shaped valleys; glaciers scour them into U-shaped troughs, wide-floored and steep-sided.
  • A trough glacier fills the whole valley, so it erodes the sides as well as the floor.
  • Truncated spurs: the glacier cuts off the river's interlocking spurs, leaving steep cliffs along the trough sides.
Mission 6

Hanging valleys and ribbon lakes

  • Hanging valley: a tributary glacier carved less deeply, so its valley ends high above the main trough. Bird Woman Falls pours from one.
  • Ribbon lakes: long, deep lakes in over-deepened troughs, such as Lake McDonald and St. Mary Lake. Ice erodes deepest where it is thickest or the rock is weakest.
Mission 6

Roches moutonnées

  • A roche moutonnée is an "asymmetrical bedrock dome sculpted by glaciers" (NPS).
  • Stoss (up-ice) side: abrasion grinds it into a gentle, smoothed slope.
  • Lee (down-ice) side: plucking tears out joint blocks, leaving a steep, jagged face.
  • So the shape shows which way the ice flowed: from the gentle side towards the steep side.
  • Lembert Dome, in Tuolumne Meadows, Yosemite, is a roche moutonnée: its "steep western face and gradual eastern slope indicate the direction of glacial flow" (NPS).
Mission 6

Ice sheets scour lowlands: crag and tail

  • Crag and tail: a resistant rock (the crag) shelters softer rock and sediment behind it (the tail).
  • Edinburgh: the Castle stands on a basalt plug. Ice moving from the west struck its western face; the gentle Royal Mile is the tail, "sediments protected by the 'crag'" (BGS).
  • Ice pushed round either side scoured the hollows now holding Princes Street Gardens and the Grassmarket.
Mission 6

Knock and lochan; differential geology

  • Knock and lochan: an ice sheet scours hard rock unevenly, leaving rocky knolls (knocks) and small lakes in the hollows (lochans).
  • Ice digs out lines of weakness (joints, faults, softer bands) faster than solid rock.
  • Differential geology: resistant rock stands up as crags, knocks and lips; weaker rock becomes hollows and lake basins.

Mission 7

Till: what ice leaves behind

  • Ice carries debris "like a conveyor belt" and drops it where the ice melts (NPS).
  • Material dropped directly by ice is till (boulder clay).
  • Till is unsorted (clay to boulders, mixed) and angular, because ice does not sort or round what it carries.
Mission 7

Lodgement and ablation till

  • Lodgement till: plastered onto the bed beneath moving ice. Dense, compact; long stones often line up with the ice flow.
  • Ablation till: let down as stagnant ice melts out at the surface or margin. Looser, less compact, with stones more randomly arranged.
  • So till type shows whether the ice was moving or stagnant when it was laid down.
Mission 7

Moraines along the glacier

  • Lateral moraine: a ridge of debris along the valley side, much of it from frost-shattered walls above.
  • Medial moraine: where two glaciers join, their lateral moraines merge into a stripe down the middle, as on Alaska's Gilkey Glacier.
  • All moraines are till, so they are unsorted and angular.
Mission 7

Moraines across the valley

  • Terminal moraine: a ridge at the ice's furthest point. "The longer a glacier stays in one spot... the larger this moraine will be" (NPS).
  • Recessional moraines: smaller ridges behind it, where retreat paused. A glacier may stay "in a steady state for decades, or even longer" (NPS).
  • Moraines mark old ice limits: Glacier National Park's Little Ice Age maximum was reconstructed from them (USGS).

Drumlins

  • Drumlins: smooth, oval hills of till, shaped beneath moving ice.

Mission 7

Erratics: Big Rock, Alberta

  • An erratic is a rock carried by ice and dropped far from its source, often on different bedrock.
  • Okotoks "Big Rock": a 16,500-tonne quartzite boulder, about 41 m long, 18 m wide and 9 m tall.

Mission 7

Till plains and till sheets

  • A till plain (till sheet) is a broad, low, gently rolling surface of till laid down as an ice sheet melted.
  • Holderness, East Yorkshire, is a UK example: its glacial tills are "mostly late Devensian (18 000 to 13 000 years old)" (BGS).
  • Drumlins and moraines often stand out of the plain, giving its gentle relief.
Mission 7

  • Upland landscapes are dominated by erosion; lowland landscapes by deposition.
Mission 7

  • Movement: drumlins and crag and tails line up with the flow.
  • Provenance: erratics trace ice back to the source rock.
  • Bedforms can overlap: in north-central Ireland, ribbed moraine, drumlins and crag and tails formed at different times give "a palimpsest record of changing flow geometries".

Mission 8

Where meltwater flows

  • Meltwater flows supraglacially (on top of the ice), englacially (within the ice) and subglacially (below the ice).
  • Surface water reaches the bed "through the bases of crevasses and moulins".
  • Discharge peaks in summer and in the afternoon of each melt day, and is lowest in winter, so deposits record pulses of high and low flow.
  • Learn the three pathways as a system: supraglacial streams → moulins → englacial conduits → subglacial tunnels → portal at the snout.
Mission 8

Meltwater erodes and carries

  • Meltwater "is usually very rich in sediment, which increases its erosive power": it erodes by abrasion with its load, as rivers do.
  • Under the ice, water is under pressure. Its path is "controlled mostly by the slope of the ice surface, rather than the slope of the bed".
  • So subglacial water can flow uphill over obstacles for short distances, cutting channels a normal river could not.
  • It carries huge loads of sand and gravel when discharge is high, then drops them when it slows.

Mission 8

Fluvioglacial deposits are different

  • Till (ice-laid): unsorted, unlayered, angular to sub-angular clasts in a fine-grained matrix.
  • Fluvioglacial (water-laid): sorted by size, stratified (in layers), with rounded clasts.
  • Why: water drops the largest particles first as it slows, and each flood pulse lays a new bed.
  • Imbrication: flat pebbles stack and tilt, overlapping like roof tiles, dipping upstream; it records flow direction.
  • Grading: grain size changes upward through a bed. In north-west England, esker ridges have cobble cores overlain by "sand and gravel that fine upwards" (BGS).

Mission 8

Meltwater channels

  • Subglacial channels (Nye channels): cut into the bed by water under pressure, they "can flow uphill and therefore have undulating long profiles", and "will usually form at topographic low points or cols".
  • Ice-marginal (lateral) channels: cut along the ice edge, roughly along the contours; they "can terminate abruptly".
  • Proglacial channels: "In front of the glacier, water drains away downslope"; overflow channels drain ice-dammed lakes across cols.
  • UK: lateral channels in the Vale of Eden helped reconstruct an ice stream's surface; there "They record progressive lowering of the ice surface" as the ice retreated (BGS).
Mission 8

Eskers

  • An esker is a long ridge of sand and gravel, "deposited by glacial meltwater flowing through tunnels within and underneath glaciers".
  • The tunnel fills with sediment; "As the ice retreats, the sediments are left behind as a ridge".
  • Size: "usually metres to tens of metres high"; length "from hundreds of metres to hundreds of kilometres".
  • Eskers "tend to be oriented parallel to ice flow", and can cross hills because the water was under pressure.
  • UK: near Carlisle, "Several parallel eskers were created by meltwaters that flowed subglacially towards an ice margin" (BGS). Ireland: an esker exposed at Kinnitty, Co. Offaly.
Mission 8

Kames and kame terraces

  • Kames: mounds or flat-topped hills "made up of stratified sand and gravel", laid down in hollows in or on stagnant ice, then let down as the ice melted.
  • Kame terraces: "marginal deposition between the glacier and sidewalls results in kame terraces": benches along a valley side.
  • Kames often sit among kettle holes, as kame and kettle topography; Charlesbreen, Svalbard, is an example.
  • UK: glaciofluvial sand and gravel in the Tyne Gap and Vale of Eden form "ice-marginal fans, flat-topped plateaux, mounds, ridge systems and terraces" (BGS).
Mission 8

Outwash plains (sandur)

  • Outwash plains, also called "'Sandur' (after the Icelandic term for sand)", are built by "braided, sediment-rich streams" flowing away from the ice.
  • Beyond the ice, meltwater velocity falls: "The largest sediment (usually gravel) is deposited first", with finer sand "carried further downstream".
  • So grain size fines with distance from the snout, and channels braid around gravel bars.
  • Iceland's Skeiðarársandur is a classic sandur; in 1996 an eruption beneath the ice "collected in a subglacial lake at a tremendous rate", then burst out as a jökulhlaup (glacial outburst flood) across it.

Mission 8

Kettle holes and proglacial lakes

  • Kettle holes: "areas of subsidence caused by the melting of buried ice". Outwash buries blocks of ice; when they melt, the surface collapses.
  • They hold "kettle ponds" (seasonally wet) or "kettle lakes" (permanent).
  • In the 1996 Skeiðarársandur jökulhlaup, deposits near the ice were "characterized by the simultaneous deposition of ice blocks".
  • Proglacial lakes form where meltwater is ponded in front of the ice by a moraine or by the ice itself; deltas and laminated silts build into them, and overflow cuts channels across cols.

Mission 11

Himalayan glaciers: mass balance

  • Hindu Kush Himalayan glaciers lost mass 65% faster in 2011–2020 than in the decade before (ICIMOD).
  • On current emissions they could lose up to 80% of their volume by 2100.
  • Meltwater matters most in the west: glacier melt made up 40.6% of total runoff in the upper Indus basin, but 11.5% in the upper Ganges (1998–2007).
Mission 11

Hydrology disrupted

  • Meltwater and discharge: runoff is "expected to peak in mid-century, driven by accelerated glacial melt", then fall as ice shrinks (ICIMOD).
  • People: the region's ice and snow supply water to 240 million people in the mountains and 1.65 billion downstream.
  • Sediment: in 28 headwater basins of High Mountain Asia, runoff and sediment fluxes have risen over six decades; sediment flux could more than double by 2050 under an extreme scenario.
  • Water quality: more sediment makes rivers more turbid. Li et al. see "far-reaching implications for the region's hydropower, food, and environmental security".
  • Hazards: about 200 glacier lakes are deemed dangerous.

Mission 11

Managing a moving target

  • Global: only cutting emissions (mitigation) can slow ice loss; the ICIMOD figure of up to 80% loss is on current emissions.
  • Regional and international: ICIMOD calls "international support and regional cooperation" vital; ice and snow feed 12 rivers through 16 countries.
  • National and local: ICIMOD calls for adaptation to be "urgently scaled up".
  • Climate change shifts the baseline, so plans built on past river flows go out of date.
Mission check

?An Arctic village stands on a river bank of frozen silt. Each summer more of the bank slumps into the river. Residents have voted to relocate, yet the village is still there. Which explanation fits best?

Less sea ice and a thawing permafrost coast mean about 7 m of Shishmaref is lost each year, NSIDC reports. Residents adapt with seawalls and by moving buildings, and have voted three times to relocate, but one estimate put the cost of moving at about $179 million.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Proxies: ice cores (gases, isotopes, dust; 800,000 years), ocean-floor deposits (foraminifera δ¹⁸O; millions of years), pollen and lake sediments (thousands of years), tree rings (annual; centuries to millennia).
  • Earth cooled from greenhouse to icehouse (Antarctic ice about 34 million years ago).

Mission 2

  • A desert is defined by dryness: under 250 mm a year, or an aridity index (P ÷ PET) below 0.20 for arid land.
  • The cryosphere stores water and reflects energy; ice masses are classed by size (ice sheet over 50,000 km²) and by basal temperature (polar, temperate).

Mission 3

  • Permafrost is defined by temperature; the active layer thaws each summer. On slopes the thawed layer flows downslope by solifluction.
  • Frost heave sorts patterned ground; contraction cracking builds ice wedges; freezing of a drained lake bed builds a closed-system pingo.
  • Thaw degrades these landforms into thermokarst; collapsed pingos record past permafrost.

Mission 4

  • Mass balance = accumulation − ablation. A negative balance makes the snout retreat, even though the ice keeps flowing forward.
  • Greenland: albedo feedback amplifies melt; extra snowfall partly offsets it.

Mission 5

  • Warm-based ice slides on meltwater and moves faster; cold-based ice is frozen to its bed and creeps slowly.
  • Speed depends on slope, thickness, basal temperature, altitude, bed lithology, size and budget; feedbacks can speed or slow it.

Mission 6

  • Plucking makes steep, broken faces; abrasion makes smooth, striated slopes.
  • Corries, arêtes and troughs on high ground; roches moutonnées where ice sheets scoured lower ground.

Mission 7

  • Ice drops unsorted till: moraines at its edges, drumlins and till plains beneath it, erratics far from their source.
  • Landform assemblages reconstruct ice extent, flow and provenance.

Mission 8

  • Meltwater flows on, in and under ice; it erodes, carries and sorts sediment, so fluvioglacial deposits are sorted, stratified and rounded, unlike till.
  • Landforms: meltwater channels, eskers, kames and kame terraces (ice-contact); outwash plains (sandur), kettle holes and proglacial lakes (proglacial).

Mission 9

  • Value types: environmental (research, carbon, water, biodiversity), cultural (wilderness, spiritual), economic (HEP, tourism, farming, mining, forestry).
  • Contrasting environments give different mixes: Swiss Alps (power, tourism), Sierra Nevada (wilderness, tourism, water), Andes (pilgrimage), Arctic tundra (herding, carbon store).
  • Tundra has low but specialised biodiversity and stores vast carbon in permafrost.

Mission 10

  • Glaciated uplands face natural hazards (avalanches, outburst floods) and human pressures (tourism, reservoirs, building).
  • Trampling, erosion, deforestation and slope cutting degrade fragile landscapes that recover slowly.
  • Management sits on a spectrum from protection to multiple use, backed by laws at global, national and local scales.

Mission 11

  • Cold environments are warming fastest; permafrost and ice are retreating, with impacts on land, ecosystems and people.
  • Himalayan ice loss brings peak water, more sediment and more glacier-lake hazards; it needs coordinated management.
