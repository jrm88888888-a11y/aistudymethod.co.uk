Mission 1

Ethics from duty

  • Immanuel Kant (1724–1804), Groundwork of the Metaphysic of Morals (1785).
  • Deontological: from Greek deon, "duty". Right and wrong lie in the act and its motive, not in the consequences.
  • Absolutist: a moral duty holds in every case, for every rational being, whatever the outcome.
  • Morality comes from reason, not from feelings, custom or results. Any rational being can work out what is right.
  • Autonomy: the rational will gives itself the moral law. Obeying a rule only because someone (even God) commands it is heteronomy.
Mission 1

The good will and duty

  • "Nothing can possibly be conceived in the world, or even out of it, which can be called good, without qualification, except a good will." (Groundwork I, Abbott trans. 1873)
  • Talents, courage and even happiness can be used badly. Only a will set on doing right is good in itself.
  • A good will acts from duty: out of respect for the moral law.
  • Kant's shopkeeper does not overcharge a child. He acts in accordance with duty, but if he does it only because honesty is good for business, it has no moral worth.
  • An act done from inclination (because you like doing it) may be "amiable", but its moral worth comes only from the motive of duty.

Mission 1

Two kinds of command

  • A maxim is the personal rule behind an action: "When I need money, I will borrow it and promise to repay."
  • A hypothetical imperative: "If you want X, do Y." "If you want to pass, revise." It binds only those who want X.
  • So it cannot be morality: drop the desire and the command loses its force.
  • A categorical imperative: "Do Y." It commands unconditionally, whatever you want, because the act is good in itself.
  • Morality must be categorical: it binds every rational being, whatever their desires.
  • OCR wording: a command "to act that is good in itself regardless of consequences".
Mission 1Read the source

The formula of universal law

  • "Act only on that maxim whereby thou canst at the same time will that it should become a universal law." (Groundwork II, Abbott trans. 1873)
  • Kant's "law of nature" variant: "Act as if the maxim of thy action were to become by thy will a universal law of nature."
  • Contradiction in conception: Kant's lying promise. If everyone made false promises when in need, no one would believe a promise; the maxim destroys itself. Result: a perfect duty (never do this).
  • Contradiction in will: a world where no one helps others can exist, but no rational being could will it, since they will one day need help. Result: an imperfect duty (do this sometimes, as you judge).

Q1 Why can the lying promise not even be thought of as a universal law?

Show answer

Promising would cease to exist, so the lie could not work.

Q2 Is the test about whether universal lying would have bad results?

Show answer

No: it is about whether the maxim contradicts itself.

Mission 1

The formula of the end in itself

  • "So act as to treat humanity, whether in thine own person or in that of any other, in every case as an end withal, never as means only." (Groundwork II, Abbott trans. 1873)
  • Rational beings set their own goals, so they have intrinsic worth.
  • Using a bus driver to get to school is fine: he has agreed and is paid. Lying to someone to get their money is not: they cannot agree to what they do not know.
  • Applies to yourself too: Kant thought suicide and neglect of your own talents fail this test.
  • Say: "never merely as a means" — not: "never as a means". Everyday cooperation uses people as means, with their consent.
Mission 1

The kingdom of ends

  • Third formulation (paraphrase): act as if you were a law-making member of a kingdom of ends: an ideal community where every rational being is both author and subject of the moral law.
  • Combines the first two: laws everyone could will (universal law) for beings who are all ends (end in itself).
  • Every person is a legislator: morality is autonomous, not imposed.
  • OCR wording: "a society of rationality … in which people treat each other as ends and not means".

Mission 1Worked answer

Testing a maxim

Task: show how Kant's categorical imperative applies to handing in a friend's essay as your own.

  • Maxim: "When I am short of time, I will submit others' work as mine."
  • Universal law: if everyone did this, no submitted work would be taken as the author's own; the practice of assessment collapses, so the maxim cannot be conceived as law: a perfect duty not to do it.
  • End in itself: the teacher is deceived and used merely as a means to a grade.
  • Link: the act is wrong in itself, whatever the grade, whatever the risk of being caught.
  • AO1 tip: name the formulation, apply it to the maxim, and say which kind of contradiction it produces.

Mission 2

The three postulates

  • Critique of Practical Reason (1788). The moral law commands us to aim at the highest good (summum bonum): perfect virtue rewarded with happiness in proportion.
  • A postulate is something we must accept for that command to make sense, though reason cannot prove it.
  • Freedom: we ought, so we can. Duty makes no sense without a free will.
  • Immortality: perfect virtue cannot be reached in one lifetime, so the soul must go on.
  • God: only a just and all-powerful God can ensure that virtue and happiness finally match.
  • These are not the source of duty: we do our duty because it is right; the postulates follow from it.

Mission 2

Kant applied today

  • Human rights: every person has dignity, not a price; rights cannot be traded away for others' benefit.
  • Medical ethics: informed consent respects the patient as an end; no research on people without consent.
  • Business: deceiving customers or treating workers as mere tools fails the formula of humanity.
  • Onora O'Neill (b. 1941), "Kantian Approaches to Some Famine Problems" (1980): the formula of humanity forbids deceiving or coercing the vulnerable, and requires some help for those in famine (paraphrase).
Mission 2Evaluate

Is Kant's ethics a helpful method of moral decision-making?

  • Claim: Kantian ethics gives a helpful method for deciding what is right.
  • For: universal and impartial: the same rule for everyone; no special pleading.
  • For: dignity: forbids using people, even for good results; supports human rights.
  • For: motive matters: judges the will, which is within our control, not outcomes, which are not.
  • Against: conflicting duties: truth-telling vs protecting life. Kant ("On a Supposed Right to Lie", 1797) still forbade lying to the murderer at the door.
  • Against: cold reason: no moral worth for compassion or love; the friend who visits only from duty seems worse, not better.
  • Against: too abstract: the tests say what form a rule must take, but give little help in messy, fast-moving real cases.
  • Against: tailored maxims: a maxim can be described narrowly ("lie only to save a hiding friend") to pass the test.
  • Replies: some Kantians (e.g. Christine Korsgaard, 1986) argue that the Groundwork tests themselves permit lying to the murderer; Kant held we have a duty to cultivate sympathetic feelings (Metaphysics of Morals, 1797).
  • Judgement criteria: Can it handle clashes? Does it fit moral experience? Does it protect the vulnerable? Is it usable in real time?

Mission 2Worked answer

Weighing a criticism

Issue: Is Kantian ethics too reliant on reason?

  • Point: Critics say Kant ignores sympathy, empathy and love: compassion gives no moral worth.
  • Evidence: the philanthropist whose sympathy is extinguished but who helps from duty is Kant's example of moral worth (Groundwork I).
  • Counter: Kant does not condemn feelings; he wants a motive that holds when feelings fail, and says we should cultivate sympathy.
  • Evaluate: whether this reply works depends on whether love can itself be a moral motive. Many Christians say yes (1 Corinthians 13 praises love above every other gift); Kant says feelings cannot be commanded.
  • AO2 tip: end each paragraph with a mini-judgement that answers the question set.

Mission 3

What a moral argument claims

  • Starts from morality: real moral obligations, or our sense of being bound by duty.
  • Concludes that God exists, or that belief in God is required.
  • Explanation type: objective goodness or obligation needs a source; God is the best one (Aquinas' Fourth Way is related).
  • Practical type: the moral life only makes sense if God exists (Kant).
  • Neither type claims to prove God from the definition of God (contrast the ontological argument).
  • You meet this in Kantian ethics: the postulates.
Mission 3

Kant: duty and the highest good

  • Immanuel Kant (1724–1804), Critique of Practical Reason (1788).
  • Morality comes from reason, not from God's commands: the categorical imperative. Kant is not arguing that we need God to know what is right.
  • Reason commands us to aim at the highest good (summum bonum): perfect virtue, rewarded with happiness in proportion to it.
  • Ought implies can: if we must aim at it, it must be possible.
  • But in this life virtue and happiness often do not match. The good suffer; the wicked prosper.
Mission 3

The three postulates

A postulate is something reason must assume for the moral law to make sense, though it cannot be proved:

  • Freedom: to be bound by duty, we must be able to choose to obey it.
  • Immortality: perfect virtue (holiness) needs endless progress, so the soul must continue beyond death.
  • God: only a being who is all-good and all-powerful can match happiness to virtue.
  • Kant, Abbott translation (1873): "it is morally necessary to assume the existence of God."
  • Postulates are practical faith, not knowledge: Kant rejected the theoretical proofs of God.
  • Spec wording: what the postulates are and why, in obeying a moral command, they are being accepted.

Mission 3Evaluate

Does moral experience point to God?

  • Claim: Our experience of moral obligation points to God.
  • For: Kant: duty commands the highest good; only God makes it possible.
  • Against: the highest good may be a hope, not something duty requires to be achieved: we must only aim at it.
  • Judgement criteria: Are moral truths objective? Does a human origin show a belief false? Does "ought implies can" apply to the whole universe?
  • Discussion: does relying on God, freedom and immortality weaken an ethics built on reason alone?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Kant: only a good will is good without qualification; moral worth comes from acting from duty, not from inclination or self-interest.
  • Morality is a categorical imperative ("Do Y"), not a hypothetical one ("If you want X, do Y").
  • Formulations: universal law (contradictions in conception and will, perfect and imperfect duties); humanity as an end, never merely a means.
  • The kingdom of ends: act as a law-making member of a community of ends.

Mission 2

  • For Kant, obeying the moral law commits us to three postulates: freedom, immortality, God.
  • Strengths: universality, dignity, motive. Weaknesses: conflicting duties, the murderer at the door, emotion, abstraction.

Mission 3

  • Kant: the moral law comes from reason, but the highest good (virtue with matching happiness) is possible only if we postulate freedom, immortality and God.
