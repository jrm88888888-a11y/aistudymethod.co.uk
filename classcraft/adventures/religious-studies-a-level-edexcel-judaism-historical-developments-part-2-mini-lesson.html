Mission 1: Jews prayed for 1,800 years to return to Zion. Why did a modern movement decide not to wait?

Make a guess: Jews prayed for 1,800 years to return to Zion. Why did a modern movement decide not to wait?

Mission 1

Zion in Jewish memory

  • Zion: a hill in Jerusalem; then a name for Jerusalem and the whole Land of Israel.
  • After exile, Jews kept Zion at the centre of prayer:
  • the Amidah, three times daily, asks God to gather the exiles and rebuild Jerusalem;
  • synagogues face Jerusalem; the Pesach Seder ends "Next year in Jerusalem";
  • at weddings a glass is broken to remember the destroyed Temple.
  • Traditional belief: the return would come through God, in the days of the Messiah, not by human politics.
  • "Zion theology": the Hebrew Bible's hope that God will restore the people to Zion.
Mission 1Read the source

Amos 9:14–15

> "And I will turn the captivity of My people Israel, and they shall build the waste cities, and inhabit them; and they shall plant vineyards, and drink the wine thereof" (Amos 9:14, JPS 1917)

> "And I will plant them upon their land, and they shall no more be plucked up out of their land which I have given them, saith the LORD thy God." (Amos 9:15, JPS 1917)

Q1 Who acts in these verses: the people, or God?

Show answer

God: "I will turn… I will plant"; the people then build and farm.

Q2 Which phrase could later Zionists read as a promise that a return would be permanent?

Show answer

They "shall no more be plucked up out of their land".

  • Religious Zionists read verses like these as a promise now being fulfilled; most Haredi Jews read them as for the Messiah's time (see Mission 2).
Mission 1

Why the 19th century changed things

  • Haskalah and emancipation: Jews became citizens in much of western Europe; many hoped to be accepted as Germans or Frenchmen "of the Mosaic faith".
  • Assimilation: some left Jewish practice or converted; yet hostility did not end.
  • Nationalism: Italians, Germans, Poles and Greeks were claiming nations. Were Jews a nation too?
  • Modern antisemitism: hatred of Jews as a "race", not only as a religion (the word was popularised in Germany c. 1879).
  • Persecutions: pogroms in the Russian Empire (1881–82) and the restrictive May Laws (1882).
  • Response: Hovevei Zion ("Lovers of Zion") societies; Leon Pinsker's Autoemancipation (1882): Jews must liberate themselves.
  • First Aliyah (1882–1903): small groups settle farming colonies (e.g. Rishon LeZion, 1882), many helped by Baron Edmond de Rothschild. Immigration stayed limited: Ottoman restrictions, poverty, disease; most Jews leaving Russia went to America.
Mission 1

Rabbis who said: start now

  • Rabbi Yehuda Alkalai (1798–1878, Sarajevo) and Rabbi Zvi Hirsch Kalischer (1795–1874, Prussia), Derishat Tziyon ("Seeking Zion", 1862).
  • Their argument: redemption can begin naturally, through human effort: buying land, farming, settling. The miraculous stage follows.
  • Settling the Land is itself a mitzvah.
  • Roots of later Religious Zionism (see Mission 2).
  • Most rabbis of their time disagreed: forcing the end is forbidden; wait for God.

Mission 1

The Dreyfus affair

  • 1894, France: Captain Alfred Dreyfus, a Jewish army officer, is convicted of spying for Germany. He was innocent; the real spy was another officer, Esterhazy.
  • January 1895: Dreyfus is publicly stripped of rank in Paris; crowds shout against the Jews.
  • France was the first European country to emancipate its Jews (1791). If antisemitism could flare there, could assimilation ever protect Jews?
  • Theodor Herzl, reporting from Paris for a Vienna newspaper, saw the case unfold. He later said it made him a Zionist.
  • Émile Zola's open letter J'accuse…! (1898) defended Dreyfus; he was fully cleared in 1906.
Mission 1

Herzl's The Jewish State (1896)

  • Theodor Herzl (1860–1904): Budapest-born, Vienna-based, secular journalist and playwright.
  • Der Judenstaat (The Jewish State, 1896): the Jewish question "is a national question" (tr. Sylvie d'Avigdor, 1896).
  • "We are a people—one people." (same translation)
  • Antisemitism will not disappear through assimilation; the answer is sovereignty over a territory, secured by international agreement.
  • Method: diplomacy with the great powers, not small-scale settlement first.
  • Territory: Palestine ("our ever-memorable historic home") or Argentina; he did not yet insist on one.
  • This is Political Zionism: national, mostly secular, aiming at a recognised state.
Mission 1

Basel, 1897

  • First Zionist Congress, Basel, Switzerland, 29–31 August 1897: about 200 delegates.
  • Founds the Zionist Organisation (later World Zionist Organisation); Herzl its president.
  • Basel Programme (paraphrase): Zionism aims to create for the Jewish people a home in Palestine secured by public law.
  • The German word was Heimstätte ("home"), not "state": a cautious word for the Ottoman Empire's sake.
  • Means: settlement of farmers and workers; organising Jews worldwide; strengthening national feeling; seeking governments' consent.
  • Herzl in his diary (paraphrase): at Basel he had founded the Jewish State; in fifty years everyone would see it. Israel was declared in 1948.
Mission 1

Herzl the diplomat

  • 1898: Herzl's only visit to the "Holy Land", timed to meet Kaiser Wilhelm II there; the Kaiser offered no support.
  • 1901: meets Sultan Abdul Hamid II of the Ottoman Empire; asks for a charter for Palestine; refused.
  • Institutions: Jewish Colonial Trust (1899) and Jewish National Fund (1901) to buy land.
  • 1903: after the Kishinev pogrom, Britain offers land in East Africa (the "Uganda programme", in present-day Kenya) as a refuge.
  • The Sixth Congress (1903) votes only to investigate; many Russian delegates walk out: only Zion would do.
  • Herzl dies in 1904; the Seventh Congress (1905) rejects East Africa.
  • His novel Altneuland (1902) pictures a future Jewish society; its motto (paraphrase): if you will it, it is no fairy tale.

Mission 1Evaluate

Is Zionism a "solution" to antisemitism?

  • Claim: Zionism is the solution to antisemitism.
  • For: Herzl: assimilation did not stop hatred (Dreyfus); only a state can protect Jews.
  • For: since 1948, Jews fleeing persecution (e.g. from the Soviet Union, Arab states, Ethiopia) have had a state guaranteed to take them in.
  • Against: antisemitism has continued, in the diaspora and in hostility towards Israel; a state has not ended it.
  • Against: Ahad Ha'am: a state cannot solve the "problem of Judaism", the loss of identity (see Mission 2).
  • Against: Haredi critics: the true remedy for exile is repentance and the Messiah, not politics.
  • Judgement criteria: "Solution" as refuge, or as an end to hatred? Safety for whom: Jews in Israel, or everywhere?

Mission 2: Some Jews built a Jewish state; some very religious Jews refuse to recognise it. Why?

Make a guess: Some Jews built a Jewish state; some very religious Jews refuse to recognise it. Why?

Mission 2

Many Zionisms

  • All Zionists wanted a Jewish national home in the Land of Israel. They disagreed on why, how and what kind.
  • Political (Herzl): a recognised state, through diplomacy (see Mission 1).
  • Practical: settle and build now, without waiting for a charter.
  • Cultural: a centre for Hebrew culture and Jewish values.
  • Labour: a socialist society built by Jewish workers.
  • Revisionist: a state on both sides of the Jordan, defended by strength.
  • Religious: return to the Land as part of God's redemption, under the Torah.
  • Against them all, for different reasons: most Haredi Jews and, until the 1930s, most Reform Jews.
Mission 2

Ahad Ha'am: a spiritual centre

  • Asher Ginzberg (1856–1927), pen name Ahad Ha'am ("one of the people"), Hebrew essayist from Ukraine.
  • "This Is Not the Way" (1889): settlement without spiritual preparation will fail.
  • Visits to the Land (first in 1891): "Truth from the Land of Israel" criticised poor planning, dependence on charity and settlers' treatment of the Arab population, whose presence many ignored.
  • Herzl solves the "problem of the Jews" (persecution); the deeper problem is the "problem of Judaism": modern Jews losing their identity.
  • Aim: a centre in the Land for Jewish value, spirit and ethics, reviving Hebrew and Jewish culture, radiating to the diaspora. Most Jews would stay where they were.
  • Paraphrase of his famous remark: more than the Jews have kept the Sabbath, the Sabbath has kept the Jews.
  • He saw ethics, not ritual law, as Judaism's core, and himself as non-observant; Orthodox critics objected.
Mission 2

Labour Zionism and the kibbutz

  • Practical Zionism: build facts on the ground: land, villages, schools. Chaim Weizmann (1874–1952) joined practical and political work ("synthetic Zionism").
  • Labour Zionism: Jewish national rebirth through Jewish work on the land and a socialist society.
  • A. D. Gordon (1856–1922): a "religion of labour"; manual work heals the people and binds it to the land.
  • Kibbutz ("gathering"): first at Degania (1910), by the Jordan near the Sea of Galilee. Shared property, equal work, decisions by assembly; children often raised together.
  • Labour leaders, above all David Ben-Gurion (1886–1973), led the Jewish community and then Israel until 1977.
  • Most were secular: the pioneer, not the rabbi, would redeem the people. There were also religious kibbutzim (from the 1930s).
Mission 2

Revisionist and Liberal Zionism

  • Ze'ev (Vladimir) Jabotinsky (1880–1940) founded the Revisionist movement (1925): "revise" the cautious policy of Weizmann.
  • Goals: a Jewish majority and state on both banks of the Jordan; mass immigration; a Jewish defence force.
  • "The Iron Wall" (1923, paraphrase): Arab acceptance would come only once Jewish strength was unbreakable.
  • Youth movement Betar; later Herut and Likud (Menachem Begin, prime minister 1977).
  • General (Liberal) Zionists: middle-class, liberal in economics, between Labour and the Revisionists; later merged into Likud.

Mission 2

Religious Zionism

  • Mizrachi (founded 1902) by Rabbi Yitzchak Yaakov Reines (1839–1915): Orthodox Jews should work inside the Zionist movement.
  • Its slogan (paraphrase): the Land of Israel for the people of Israel according to the Torah of Israel.
  • Rabbi Abraham Isaac Kook (1865–1935), first Ashkenazi Chief Rabbi of the Land of Israel (1921):
  • even secular pioneers are unknowingly part of God's plan; their work is the beginning of redemption (atchalta de-geulah).
  • Settling the Land is a mitzvah; the state is a religious event, not only a political one.
  • After 1967 his son's followers founded Gush Emunim.
Mission 2Diversity

Political and Religious Zionism

  • Why a state? Political: Jews are a nation that needs safety. Religious: God promised the Land; return is part of redemption.
  • Who acts? Political: Jews, through diplomacy and settlement. Religious: Jews act, but God works through them.
  • What kind of state? Political: modern, mainly secular, for all Jews. Religious: shaped by Torah, Shabbat and halakhah in public life.
  • The Messiah? Political: not needed. Religious: the state is a step towards the Messianic age.
  • Shared ground: both support the State of Israel, Hebrew, immigration (aliyah) and defence.
Mission 2Read the source

"That ye awaken not"

> "I adjure you, O daughters of Jerusalem, by the gazelles, and by the hinds of the field, that ye awaken not, nor stir up love, until it please." (Song of Songs 2:7, JPS 1917)

  • The Talmud (Ketubot 110b–111a, paraphrase) reads the repeated oath as three oaths made at the start of exile:
  • Israel would not go up to the Land en masse ("as a wall");
  • Israel would not rebel against the nations;
  • the nations would not oppress Israel too much.

Q1 According to this reading, what was forbidden?

Show answer

A mass, forced return before God's time: "awakening love" too early.

Q2 How do religious Zionists answer?

Show answer

Many say the oaths are aggadah, not binding law, or lapsed when the nations broke their oath through persecution.

Mission 2

Haredi opposition

  • Haredi ("trembling" before God; ultra-Orthodox) leaders mostly opposed Zionism:
  • it "forces the end": only the Messiah may restore Jewish rule;
  • it replaced Torah with secular nationalism: "Jewish" became a nationality, not a covenant;
  • secular pioneers broke Shabbat and kashrut in the Holy Land.
  • Agudat Israel (1912): anti-Zionist at first, later non-Zionist: lives in the state and sits in parliament, without giving it religious meaning.
  • Satmar Hasidim (Rabbi Joel Teitelbaum, Vayoel Moshe, 1961): the state is a grave sin.
Mission 2

Neturei Karta

  • Neturei Karta: Aramaic for "guardians of the city", founded in Jerusalem in 1938, leaving Agudat Israel.
  • The name comes from a Talmudic story: the true guardians of a city are its teachers of Torah, not its soldiers.
  • Beliefs: a Jewish state before the Messiah is rebellion against God; exile ends only by divine redemption.
  • Practice: refuse to vote, take state money or recognise Israel; some join anti-Israel protests abroad.
  • A small group: most Haredi Jews reject its political actions, even while sharing its non-Zionist theology.
Mission 2

Critics and two scholars

  • Rejection by some Jews: Haredi; early Reform: Pittsburgh (1885) said Jews were "no longer a nation, but a religious community"; British Liberal Judaism.
  • Yosef Gorny (b. 1933), Israeli historian (Zionism and the Arabs, 1882–1948, 1987; The State of Israel in Jewish Public Thought, 1994): Zionism held rival ideologies (Labour, liberal, revisionist, religious) whose arguments shaped Israel's identity; Jewish thinkers still dispute whether the State is the centre of Jewish life or one partner with the diaspora (paraphrase).
  • Jacob Neusner (1932–2016), Stranger at Home (1981): for many American Jews, the Holocaust and the State of Israel became a substitute religion, replacing Torah and covenant; he rejected the Zionist claim that diaspora life is mere exile, arguing America could be a true home (paraphrase).
  • Covenant debate (also): is the State the fulfilment of God's promise, a human project, or something religiously neutral?
Mission 2Evaluate

How strong are the links between Zionism and Judaism?

  • Claim: Zionism is a Jewish religious movement.
  • For: the Land is central to the covenant (Genesis 12; 15); prayer for Zion is daily; religious Zionists see the State as the beginning of redemption.
  • For: cultural Zionism revived Hebrew and Jewish learning.
  • Against: its founders (Herzl, Labour) were mainly secular; it was a modern nationalism, like others in Europe.
  • Against: many religious Jews (Haredim, early Reform) rejected it on religious grounds; some non-Jews (Christian Zionists) support it.
  • Judgement criteria: the founders' motives, or the movement's meaning for Jews today? Is "Jewish" a religion, a people, or both?

Mission 3

The Shoah

  • Shoah ("catastrophe"): the murder of about six million Jews by Nazi Germany and its collaborators, 1941–45.
  • Centuries of Christian anti-Judaism and modern racial antisemitism prepared the ground.
  • 1933 Hitler in power · 1935 Nuremberg Laws strip Jews of citizenship · 1938 Kristallnacht, a nationwide pogrom (9–10 November) · 1941 mass shootings in the east · 1942 Wannsee Conference coordinates the "Final Solution" · death camps such as Auschwitz-Birkenau and Treblinka.
  • About one third of the world's Jews were killed; whole communities of Eastern Europe vanished.
  • Jews also use churban ("destruction"), the word for the destruction of the Temples.
Mission 3

Why it shakes belief

  • Classical Jewish belief: God is all-powerful, just and acts in history: God brought Israel out of Egypt.
  • The covenant: God chose Israel and promised to protect it; Israel keeps the mitzvot.
  • So where was God at Auschwitz? Four possible answers:
  • Punishment: a traditional prayer says "because of our sins we were exiled" (festival Musaf). A few Haredi leaders applied this to the Holocaust; most Jews find it unbearable.
  • Loss of faith: many survivors stopped believing.
  • God hidden or limited (see Mission 4).
  • A purpose within God's plan (Maybaum, later in this mission).
Mission 3Read the source

> "But it shall come to pass, if thou wilt not hearken unto the voice of the LORD thy God, … that all these curses shall come upon thee, and overtake thee." (Deuteronomy 28:15, JPS 1917)

> "And yet for all that, when they are in the land of their enemies, I will not reject them, neither will I abhor them, to destroy them utterly, and to break My covenant with them" (Leviticus 26:44, JPS 1917)

Mission 3

Rubenstein: the "death of God"

  • Richard L. Rubenstein (1924–2021), American rabbi, After Auschwitz (1966; 2nd edition 1992).
  • In 1961 a German Protestant dean, Heinrich Grüber, told him the Holocaust was part of God's will for the Jews. Rubenstein concluded: if God controls history, then Hitler was God's instrument, and he could not worship such a God.
  • So the God of history who chose Israel and acts in events is "dead" (paraphrase): belief in that God is no longer possible.
  • The covenant and chosenness die with it: being "chosen" made Jews a target.
  • "Death of God" describes a cultural event in human belief, not a claim that God once lived and then died.
  • Yet he kept Jewish community, ritual and festivals: they help people face life and death together.
  • He later spoke of God as the Holy Nothingness: the ground of being, not a God who intervenes (paraphrase).

Mission 3Read the source

The suffering servant

> "Surely our diseases he did bear, and our pains he carried; whereas we did esteem him stricken, smitten of God, and afflicted. But he was wounded because of our transgressions, he was crushed because of our iniquities" (Isaiah 53:4–5, JPS 1917)

  • Jewish tradition (e.g. Rashi) reads the servant as the people of Israel, suffering among the nations; Christians read it as Jesus.
  • Ignaz Maybaum (1897–1976), a Reform rabbi who came to London in 1939, called the Holocaust the third churban (a destruction that ends one era and opens another) within God's plan. As God once called the king of Babylon his servant (Jeremiah 25:9), he said, God used Hitler as an instrument; the remnant who survived carry Judaism into a new age (paraphrase).
  • Maybaum: Israel is the suffering servant; the six million died as an innocent sacrifice (vicarious atonement), not for their own sins.
  • Like the crucifixion for Christians, he said, Auschwitz should shock the Western world into turning from evil (paraphrase).

Q1 In verse 5, whose wrongdoing causes the servant's suffering?

Show answer

Others': "our transgressions".

Q2 Why does this suit Maybaum but not the punishment view?

Show answer

The servant is innocent; he suffers for others, not for his own sins.

Mission 3Evaluate

Maybaum or Rubenstein?

  • Claim: Rubenstein's response takes the Holocaust more seriously than Maybaum's.
  • For: Rubenstein refuses to make Auschwitz part of God's plan; he faces the evidence.
  • For: Maybaum's "Hitler, my servant" and sacrifice for progress seem to justify the unjustifiable.
  • Against: Maybaum keeps the covenant and gives survivors hope and purpose, as the prophets did after 586 BCE.
  • Against: Rubenstein lets the Holocaust destroy Judaism's core belief; critics call this a posthumous victory for Hitler.
  • Judgement criteria: faithful to the victims? to Jewish tradition? morally acceptable? does it help Jews live after it?

Mission 4

What the Holocaust did to the Jewish world

  • About one third of the world's Jews were murdered; Eastern Europe's Yiddish-speaking world of towns, yeshivot and Hasidic courts was largely destroyed.
  • The centres of Jewish life moved to the USA and to Israel (state founded 1948).
  • Antisemitism did not end: in Kielce, Poland, a pogrom in 1946 killed survivors returning home; Holocaust denial later spread.
  • Commemoration: Yom HaShoah (27 Nisan, set by Israel's Knesset in 1951), observed by many Jews; some Haredi Jews mourn the Holocaust on Tisha B'Av instead.
  • : Rubenstein and Maybaum. Now: Fackenheim, Berkovits, Wiesel.
Mission 4

Fackenheim: the commanding voice of Auschwitz

  • Emil L. Fackenheim (1916–2003), rabbi and philosopher; held in Sachsenhausen after Kristallnacht (1938); later a professor in Toronto; moved to Israel in 1983. God's Presence in History (1970).
  • Jewish tradition holds that at Sinai God's commanding voice gave the Torah's 613 commandments.
  • From Auschwitz, Fackenheim says, comes a commanding voice too: a new revelation, though without any explanation of why it happened.
  • Its command is the 614th commandment (first set out in 1967).
Mission 4Read the source

The 614th commandment

Fackenheim, God's Presence in History (1970), summary of the commandment (paraphrase except the quoted phrase):

  • Jews are "forbidden to hand Hitler posthumous victories".
  • They must survive as Jews, lest the Jewish people perish.
  • They must remember the victims, lest their memory perish.
  • They must not despair of humanity and the world, retreating into cynicism.
  • They must not despair of the God of Israel, lest Judaism perish.

Q1 Why is giving up Judaism a "victory" for Hitler, on this view?

Show answer

Hitler aimed to end the Jewish people; Jews who abandon Judaism complete his work.

Q2 Does the commandment explain why God allowed the Holocaust?

Show answer

No: it commands a response, not an explanation.

Mission 4

Return into history: the State of Israel

  • Fackenheim, The Jewish Return into History (1978): after Auschwitz, Jews cannot stay passive victims; they must act in history.
  • The State of Israel (1948) is, for him, a central response: a collective "No" to Hitler; Jews defending Jewish life.
  • Your spec's "resurrection": many Jews see the rebirth of the nation in its land after near-destruction in the light of Ezekiel 37's valley of dry bones.
  • God acting in history? Fackenheim keeps God present in history, but only as commanding, not as saving at Auschwitz.
  • Critics: making survival a commandment may turn Judaism into a reaction to Hitler; secular Jews can obey it without any belief in God.
Mission 4

Wiesel: Night

  • Elie Wiesel (1928–2016), from Sighet (Romania); deported to Auschwitz in 1944 at 15; survived Buchenwald; Nobel Peace Prize 1986.
  • Night (Yiddish 1956; French 1958; English 1960): his memoir of the camps.
  • Your anthology extract 2 is from Night.
  • On the first night, he says, the flames consumed his faith for ever (paraphrase).
  • At the hanging of a young boy, a prisoner asks where God is; a voice within Eliezer answers that God is there, on the gallows (paraphrase): faith dying, or God suffering with the victim?
  • At Rosh Hashanah he will not bless God: he feels like the accuser, and God the accused.
  • Key words: loss of faith; protest; the covenant questioned from inside it.

Mission 4Diversity

Five responses compared

  • Rubenstein: God of history "dead"; covenant ends.
  • Maybaum: God's plan; churban; Israel as suffering servant.
  • Fackenheim: no explanation; a command to survive and remember.
  • Wiesel: protest and memory; faith that argues.
Mission 4Evaluate

Can any Holocaust theology succeed?

  • Claim: No Holocaust theology is legitimate: silence is the only fitting response.
  • For: any explanation risks justifying the murder of six million (Maybaum's critics).
  • For: Wiesel distrusts answers.
  • For: the questions about God's power and goodness remain unanswered by every thinker.
  • Against: Fackenheim does not explain the Holocaust; he guides how to live after it.
  • Against: silence can let memory fade; survivors themselves demanded to be heard.
  • Judgement criteria: faithful to the victims? to Jewish tradition? does it help Jews live and believe now?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Jews prayed daily for a return to Zion, traditionally expected through God and the Messiah; Amos 9:14–15 promises a permanent replanting in the land.
  • 19th-century nationalism, racial antisemitism, pogroms and the Dreyfus affair convinced Herzl that Jews were a nation needing a homeland secured by public law (The Jewish State, 1896; Basel, 1897).
  • Herzl's diplomacy (Kaiser, Sultan, Britain) failed in his lifetime; the Uganda programme split the movement because many would accept only Zion.

Mission 2

  • Zionism took many forms: political (Herzl), practical and Labour (settlement, kibbutz), cultural (Ahad Ha'am's spiritual centre), Revisionist (Jabotinsky) and Religious (Mizrachi, Rav Kook).
  • Most Haredim reject any religious meaning in the State; Neturei Karta reject the State entirely; Reform moved from opposition (1885) to support (1937).

Mission 3

  • The Shoah challenges belief in a just, all-powerful God who acts in history and keeps covenant with Israel.
  • Rubenstein (After Auschwitz, 1966): the God of history and the covenant are "dead"; God as Holy Nothingness; Jewish community still matters.

Mission 4

  • Fackenheim: from Auschwitz comes a commanding voice: the 614th commandment forbids handing Hitler posthumous victories (survive, remember, do not despair of humanity or of God); Israel as return into history.
  • Wiesel: Night shows faith wounded and protesting; The Trial of God (Eduqas) gives God's defence to Satan.
