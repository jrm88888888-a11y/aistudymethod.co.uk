Mission 1

Reincarnation: the soul moves on

  • Reincarnation (transmigration of souls): an eternal soul passes from one body to another, life after life.
  • In Hinduism the soul is the atman, the true, unchanging self.
  • The cycle of birth, death and rebirth is samsara.
  • Karma: actions have consequences that shape future lives; good actions lead to better births.
  • The goal is moksha: release from samsara. For Advaita Vedanta, realising that atman is one with Brahman; for devotional traditions, eternal union with God.
  • Sikhs and Jains also hold beliefs in rebirth and karma, with their own goals.
Mission 1Read the source

The Bhagavad Gita

Bhagavad Gita 2.22 (Edwin Arnold translation, The Song Celestial, 1885):

> "Nay, but as when one layeth / His worn-out robes away, / And taking new ones, sayeth, / 'These will I wear to-day!' / So putteth by the spirit / Lightly its garb of flesh, / And passeth to inherit / A residence afresh."

Q1 In the image, what stays the same and what changes?

Show answer

The spirit, the atman, stays; the body, like clothing, is changed.

Q2 What does this suggest carries personal identity in reincarnation?

Show answer

The atman, not the body or memory.

Mission 1

Rebirth: no unchanging self

  • Buddhism teaches anatta (Sanskrit anatman): there is no unchanging soul or self.
  • A person is five changing khandhas (aggregates): body, feelings, perceptions, mental formations, consciousness.
  • "'All forms are unreal,' he who knows and sees this becomes passive in pain" (Dhammapada 279, Müller translation, 1881). Here "unreal" renders anatta, "not-self".
  • So what is reborn? A causal stream: the energy of karma (kamma, intentional action) shapes a new life.
  • The Questions of King Milinda (paraphrase): rebirth is like lighting one lamp from another. The new flame is neither the same nor another.
  • The goal is nibbana (nirvana): the end of craving and of rebirth.
  • Traditions differ: many Theravada teachers hold rebirth is immediate; Tibetan Buddhism teaches an intermediate state (bardo).

Mission 1

Who is reborn? The identity problem

  • Most people remember no past lives. If memory makes identity (Locke), the past person was not me.
  • Hindu reply: identity rests on the atman, not memory. The same self lives each life, as one actor plays many roles.
  • Buddhist reply: there is no fixed self to be "the same". Lives are linked by cause, like flames passed on. "Neither the same nor another."
  • Critique: if nothing carries over except karmic effects, is it me who is reborn, or someone affected by me?
  • Critique: why should I care about, or deserve, the results of actions I cannot remember?
Mission 1

Near-death experiences

  • Near-death experience (NDE): an experience reported by some people who come close to death, often in cardiac arrest.
  • Term popularised by Raymond Moody, Life After Life (1975).
  • Common features: feeling of peace; leaving the body and seeing it from above; a tunnel; a bright light or loving presence; meeting dead relatives; a life review; a border and a return.
  • Some report verifiable details seen while unconscious (e.g. the Pam Reynolds case, 1991, often cited).
  • Believers read NDEs as glimpses of the soul leaving the body; reports vary across cultures and faiths.
Mission 1Evaluate

Are NDEs evidence of life after death?

  • Claim: Near-death experiences show that the self can exist apart from the body.
  • For: Many, similar reports from people of different ages and cultures.
  • For: Some report accurate details from while they were unconscious.
  • For: Life-changing effects: many lose the fear of death.
  • Against: "Near" death is not death: the brain may still be active. Not one has returned from lasting death.
  • Against: Brain-based explanations: lack of oxygen, REM intrusion, drugs such as ketamine, temporal lobe activity (Susan Blackmore, Dying to Live, 1993: the "dying brain" hypothesis).
  • Against: Content varies with culture and religion; verifiable cases are few and hard to check.
  • Judgement criteria: What would count as evidence for life after death? Do brain explanations account for all features? Is testimony reliable here?

Mission 1

Evidence for reincarnation?

  • Ian Stevenson (1918–2007), Twenty Cases Suggestive of Reincarnation (1966): children who seem to recall details of a dead person's life, sometimes with matching birthmarks.
  • Critiques: the child may have heard the details (cryptomnesia); families may shape the story; cases cluster where reincarnation is already believed.
  • Most believers do not rest belief on such cases, but on scripture, tradition and the justice of karma.
Mission 1

Life after death and moral reasoning

  • Plato, Republic X (Myth of Er): souls are judged after death and choose new lives. Justice is rewarded beyond this life.
  • Aristotle: the soul is the form of the body, so it does not survive as a person; ethics aims at a good life (eudaimonia) now.
  • Immanuel Kant (1724–1804), Critique of Practical Reason (1788): morality commands us to seek perfect goodness, which no one reaches in a lifetime. So we must assume "an endless duration of the existence and personality of the same rational being" (Abbott translation, 1889). Immortality is a postulate of practical reason.
  • Karma also links morality and the next life.
  • Critique: morality may need no reward; hoping for justice does not show it will happen.
Mission 1

Can talk of life after death be meaningful?

  • Verification principle (logical positivists, A. J. Ayer, 1936): a statement is meaningful only if experience could verify it. Who could verify "I will survive death"?
  • Hick's eschatological verification ("Theology and Verification", 1960): the claim can be verified, after death, if it is true (the Celestial City parable).
  • But it could never be falsified: if there is no afterlife, no one will find out.
  • Non-realist reading: D. Z. Phillips (1934–2006), Death and Immortality (1970): "eternal life" is a quality of life now (self-giving love), not survival after death.
  • Links: religious language (–f).

Make a guess: Could any amount of eyewitness evidence make it reasonable to believe a miracle happened?

Mission 2

Hume: weigh the evidence

  • David Hume (1711–1776), Scottish empiricist: knowledge comes from experience.
  • An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding (1748), Section X, Part I.
  • A wise person proportions belief to the evidence.
  • A law of nature rests on uniform experience: every observed case, without exception.
  • A miracle report sets testimony against that uniform experience.
  • So the evidence against any miracle is as strong as evidence can be.
  • Hume does not claim miracles are logically impossible. He claims it is never reasonable to believe a report of one.
Mission 2Read the source

Hume's maxim

Hume, Enquiry (1748), Section X, Part I:

> "A wise man, therefore, proportions his belief to the evidence."

> "no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous, than the fact, which it endeavours to establish"

Q1 A friend says she saw a dead man walk. What two possibilities does Hume tell you to compare?

Show answer

That she is mistaken or lying; that the dead man walked.

Q2 Which does Hume think will always be the less miraculous?

Show answer

That the witness is mistaken or lying: people often are; the dead never rise in uniform experience.

Mission 2

Hume: four practical arguments

Part II adds four points about real miracle reports:

  • Witnesses: no miracle in history is attested by enough people of good sense, education and reputation to rule out error or deceit.
  • Susceptibility: people love surprise and wonder, and enjoy passing on marvellous stories; religious zeal can make even honest people exaggerate.
  • Origins: miracle stories abound chiefly among "ignorant and barbarous nations", and fade as learning grows (Hume's phrase).
  • Contrary claims: "in matters of religion, whatever is different is contrary". Miracles of rival religions are used to prove rival systems, so their testimonies cancel out.

Mission 2

Swinburne replies to Hume

  • Richard Swinburne (b. 1934), The Concept of Miracle (1970); The Existence of God (1979).
  • Laws of nature: a law describes what normally happens. A non-repeatable counter-instance breaks a law without giving any reason to revise it. So a violation is coherent.
  • More kinds of evidence than testimony: our own memories, others' testimony, physical traces (e.g. medical records), and our understanding of what is physically possible.
  • Principle of testimony: believe what others report unless there is good reason not to.
  • Witnesses: Hume's standards (a "sufficient number" of witnesses of "education"; "barbarous nations") are vague or prejudiced.
  • Contrary claims: reports cancel out only if each miracle supports a doctrine that contradicts the other's. Many miracle reports support no rival doctrine at all.
Quick check
?A sceptic says: "Christians, Muslims and Hindus all report miracles, so their reports cancel each other out." Which reply is Swinburne's?
Mission 2

Science, coincidence and the mind

  • Scientific method: observation → hypothesis → experiment, with results that can be repeated. A one-off miracle cannot be re-run in a laboratory.
  • Science can propose natural explanations for reported healings: misdiagnosis, natural remission, the placebo effect, and psychosomatic change (the mind affecting the body).
  • Coincidence: among very many prayers and events, some striking matches are to be expected.
  • But "no scientific explanation yet" does not mean "none ever": believers who rest a miracle on a gap risk a God of the gaps.
  • Many believers reply: God can work through mind and body; science describes how, not whether God is at work.
  • 6.3a: this is where miracles meet the methods of science; methods in full in Mission 3.
Mission check

?Two hundred villagers, including two doctors, say they saw a woman's paralysed arm move freely at once during a festival. A follower of Hume applies the maxim from Part I. What must they ask?

Hume never says a miracle cannot happen. He says testimony can never outweigh uniform experience. Swinburne answers him by defending both the idea of a violation and the evidence for one.

Mission 3

How science reaches beliefs

  • Observation: notice a pattern in nature.
  • Hypothesis: propose an explanation that makes predictions.
  • Experiment: test the predictions under controlled conditions; others repeat the test.
  • Revise: if predictions fail, change or drop the hypothesis.
  • Reasoning is inductive: from many cases to a general law. So conclusions are provisional, open to new evidence.
  • Science explains natural events by natural causes (methodological naturalism). That is a rule of method, not itself a claim that nothing else exists.
  • The scientific world view goes further: the claim that only what science can study is real.
Mission 3

How religions reach beliefs

  • Revelation: God makes truth known, through scripture, prophets and (for Christians) Jesus.
  • Tradition and authority: communities interpret scripture over centuries (e.g. creeds; Catholic magisterium).
  • Religious experience: encounters taken as contact with God.
  • Reason: natural theology argues from the world to God (–).
  • Faith: trust and commitment, not only belief that a claim is true.
  • Religious belief develops too: doctrines are interpreted afresh, and readings of scripture change.
Mission 3

Connections and differences

  • Connections: both seek truth; both interpret evidence through reasoning; both work in communities that test and correct ideas.
  • Many pioneers of modern science (e.g. Kepler, Boyle, Newton) saw science as studying God's ordered creation.
  • Some philosophers treat God as the best explanation of the evidence, much like a scientific hypothesis.
  • Differences: science needs repeatable tests; revelation and experience are often unrepeatable and personal.
  • Science asks how natural processes work; religion also asks why there is anything, and what life is for.
  • Science holds conclusions provisionally; faith involves commitment that is not given up at the first difficulty.

Mission 3Read the source

Flew's invisible gardener

Antony Flew (1923–2010), "Theology and Falsification" (1950), adapting a parable by John Wisdom (paraphrase):

  • Two explorers find a clearing with flowers and weeds. One says: "A gardener tends this plot." The other disagrees.
  • They watch. No gardener appears. They put up an electric fence and patrol with bloodhounds. Nothing.
  • The believer says: "The gardener is invisible, intangible, silent."
  • The sceptic asks: how does such a gardener differ from an imaginary one, or no gardener at all?
  • Flew's point: a claim that nothing could ever count against dies "the death by a thousand qualifications".
  • Anthology extract (2): Flew and Hare, "Theology and Falsification" (Mitchell, ed.). Paraphrased here.

Q1 What scientific step do the explorers carry out?

Show answer

They test a hypothesis by observation and experiment.

Q2 Why does Flew say the believer's claim loses its meaning?

Show answer

It is qualified until nothing could count against it, so it no longer asserts anything.

Mission 3

Kinds of truth

  • Empirical truth: matches what can be observed and tested.
  • Literal truth: a text means exactly what it says (e.g. six 24-hour days in Genesis 1).
  • Symbolic or mythological truth: a story teaches a truth that is not historical description (e.g. Genesis 1 teaches that the world depends on God and is good).
  • Existential truth: a belief is true in being lived by, shaping meaning and purpose.
  • Christian thinkers long warned against making scripture a science textbook: e.g. Augustine, The Literal Meaning of Genesis (c. 401–415), urged Christians not to contradict what can be known of nature by reason (paraphrase).
  • Many believers hold that religious statements make real truth-claims (e.g. "God exists") as well as symbolic ones.
Mission 3Diversity

Four ways to relate science and religion

Ian Barbour (1923–2013), Religion in an Age of Science (1990), sorted views into four models:

  • Conflict: one must defeat the other. Richard Dawkins (b. 1941), The God Delusion (2006): God is a scientific hypothesis, and a very improbable one. Some young-earth creationists also see conflict, from the other side.
  • Independence: separate domains that cannot clash. Stephen Jay Gould (1941–2002), Rocks of Ages (1999): "non-overlapping magisteria", science for facts, religion for meaning and value.
  • Dialogue: each raises questions for the other (e.g. why is nature orderly and understandable at all?).
  • Integration: both describe one reality. John Polkinghorne (1930–2021), physicist and Anglican priest: science and theology both seek truth through evidence and reflection.

Mission 3Evaluate

Can science and religion conflict?

  • Claim: Science and religion answer different questions, so they cannot conflict.
  • For: Science studies natural causes; it cannot measure meaning, value or purpose.
  • For: Many scientists have held religious faith without contradiction.
  • Against: Many religious claims are about the world (creation, miracles, the soul), where science also speaks.
  • Against: Dawkins: the existence of God is itself a factual claim, open to evidence.
  • Against (from belief): some believers reject independence too, because faith claims real truth about the world.
  • Judgement criteria: Are religious claims factual, symbolic or both? Can a claim be meaningful if untestable (Flew)? Does the scientific method answer every question worth asking?
Mission 3Worked answer

Exploring a difference

Task: explore one difference between scientific method and religious belief.

  • Scholar: Flew's gardener: the believer's claim is qualified until nothing could count against it.
  • Develop: For Flew, an untestable claim may assert nothing. For a believer, God is not one more object in the world to be tested.

Mission 4: If science can explain how the universe and life developed, is anything left for a creator to do?

Make a guess: If science can explain how the universe and life developed, is anything left for a creator to do?

Mission 4

The Big Bang

  • The universe is expanding from an extremely hot, dense early state, about 13.8 billion years ago.
  • 1927–31: Georges Lemaître (1894–1966), Belgian physicist and Catholic priest, proposes expansion from a "primeval atom".
  • 1929: Edwin Hubble: distant galaxies are moving away; the further, the faster.
  • 1965: the cosmic microwave background, the cooled afterglow of the early universe, is detected (Penzias and Wilson).
  • The name "Big Bang" was coined by its critic Fred Hoyle on BBC radio in 1949.
  • The theory describes how the universe developed from its early state. It does not say what, if anything, caused that state.
Mission 4

Steady state and the cosmological constant

  • Steady state (1948: Hermann Bondi, Thomas Gold, Fred Hoyle): the universe expands but has no beginning; new matter appears continuously.
  • Hoyle disliked a beginning partly because it seemed to invite a creator.
  • The 1965 background radiation fitted the Big Bang, not steady state; most cosmologists abandoned steady state.
  • Cosmological constant (Λ): added by Albert Einstein (1879–1955) in 1917 to keep his model universe static; dropped after expansion was found.
  • 1998: the expansion is accelerating. Λ returns as the simplest model of "dark energy".
  • Its observed value is far smaller than theory predicts; much larger, and galaxies could not form. Some see this fine-tuning as pointing to design; others to many universes, or to physics not yet understood.
Mission 4

Does the Big Bang help or hurt belief?

  • As an alternative explanation: the universe's development is explained by physics from its early state; no First Cause is needed to explain what happens next. This weakens inductive arguments that start from motion and change.
  • As support: a beginning seems to fit creation. William Lane Craig's Kalam argument cites it as evidence that the universe began to exist.
  • Catholic response: Pope Pius XII (1951) welcomed the theory as consistent with creation; Lemaître warned against using physics to prove theology.
  • Either way: science describes the universe from its earliest moments; whether there is a reason why it exists at all is a further question (Aquinas' Third Way).

Mission 4

Darwin: evolution by natural selection

  • Charles Darwin (1809–1882), On the Origin of Species (1859); humans included in The Descent of Man (1871).
  • Variation: individuals of a species differ.
  • Inheritance: many differences are passed on.
  • Struggle: more offspring are born than can survive.
  • Selection: those better suited to their environment survive and reproduce more.
  • Over vast time, small changes add up to new species: all life shares common descent.
  • This explains the appearance of design (as in Paley's watch) without a designer.
  • It challenges a literal reading of Genesis 1–2 and the idea of humans as a separate creation.
Mission 4Read the source

Darwin's closing sentence

On the Origin of Species, 1st edition (1859), final sentence (opening):

> "There is grandeur in this view of life, with its several powers, having been originally breathed into a few forms or into one;"

2nd edition (1860) onwards:

> "having been originally breathed by the Creator into a few forms or into one;"

  • Darwin later called himself an agnostic (letter, 1879).

Q1 What did Darwin add in 1860?

Show answer

The words "by the Creator".

Q2 Does natural selection, as Darwin states it here, rule out a creator?

Show answer

Mission 4

Dawkins: the blind watchmaker

  • Richard Dawkins (b. 1941): The Blind Watchmaker (1986); The God Delusion (2006).
  • Natural selection is a blind watchmaker: it produces the appearance of design with no foresight or purpose.
  • It is not chance: tiny variations are selected cumulatively, step by step, so complex organs build up gradually.
  • A designer would be even more complex than what it explains: so "who designed the designer?"
  • Darwin, Dawkins says, made it possible to be an intellectually fulfilled atheist (paraphrase, 1986).
  • Responses: many theists reply that evolution explains how life develops, not why a universe exists whose laws allow it.

Mission 4Diversity

Religious responses to evolution

  • Young-earth creationism: Genesis 1 read literally: six days, a world thousands of years old; species created as separate kinds (e.g. Answers in Genesis).
  • Old-earth creationism: accepts an ancient universe ("days" as long ages), but holds that God created kinds, and humans, directly.
  • Theistic evolution (evolutionary creation): God creates through evolution.
  • Catholic teaching: Pius XII, Humani Generis (1950), allowed study of the evolution of the human body, with each soul created directly by God; John Paul II (1996) called evolution "more than a hypothesis".
  • Many Orthodox, Anglican and other Protestant Christians also accept evolution; many evangelicals are creationists.
  • Judaism, Islam and other faiths show a similar range of views.
Mission 4

Intelligent design and irreducible complexity

  • Intelligent design (ID): some features of life are best explained by an intelligent cause. ID claims to be science and does not name the designer.
  • Michael Behe (b. 1952), Darwin's Black Box (1996): irreducible complexity: a system that fails if any one part is removed (his model: a mousetrap; his case: the bacterial flagellum).
  • So, Behe argues, it could not be built step by step by natural selection.
  • Critiques: parts can evolve for other functions first, then be co-opted (some flagellum proteins work in a cell's secretion system); "not yet explained" is not "unexplainable" (God of the gaps).
  • Kitzmiller v. Dover (US federal court, 2005): ID is not science, so cannot be taught as such in public-school science classes.
  • Dawkins: ID is creationism renamed. Some theologians also object: God becomes a tinkerer inside the world, not its creator.

Mission 4

The Gaia hypothesis

  • James Lovelock (1919–2022), Gaia: A New Look at Life on Earth (1979), developed with biologist Lynn Margulis.
  • Earth's living things, atmosphere, oceans and rocks form a self-regulating system that keeps conditions fit for life.
  • Named (at the novelist William Golding's suggestion) after the Greek goddess of the earth; Lovelock did not mean a conscious goddess.
  • Religious readings: some find in Gaia a sense of the earth as sacred; some Christians see a creator's ordered world and a call to stewardship.
  • Dawkins (The Extended Phenotype, 1982): natural selection cannot produce planet-wide regulation, because planets do not compete and reproduce.
  • Lovelock's reply, the "Daisyworld" model (1983, with Andrew Watson): regulation can emerge without foresight.
Mission 4Evaluate

Do scientific origins make God unnecessary?

  • Claim: Scientific explanations of origins make belief in a creator unnecessary.
  • For: The Big Bang and evolution explain the universe's and life's development by natural causes.
  • For: Ockham's razor: do not add a cause that explains nothing extra. Dawkins: a designer is more complex than what it explains.
  • Against: Science starts from laws and an early state; it does not explain why anything exists (Aquinas).
  • Against: Many scientists see evolution as the way God creates; fine-tuning raises further questions.
  • Judgement criteria: Is "creator" a cause inside the chain of events, or the reason the chain exists? Is the scientific or the philosophical explanation more complete?
Mission check

For theistic evolution, the creator's role is not to fill gaps in the science but to give the whole process its existence.

Mission 5

What "God" means in classical theism

  • Classical theism: the God of mainstream Jewish, Christian and Islamic philosophy.
  • Anselm (1033–1109), Proslogion 2: God is aliquid quo nihil maius cogitari possit, "something than which nothing greater can be thought" (Latin; plain translation).
  • So God has every perfection, to the greatest possible degree:
  • Omnipotent (all-powerful) · omniscient (all-knowing) · omnibenevolent (wholly good) · eternal.
  • Also: creator and sustainer of everything, immutable (unchanging), simple (not made of parts).
  • Swinburne's working definition (The Coherence of Theism, 1977, opening): a person without a body, everywhere present, creator, free, omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good, eternal, a necessary being, worthy of worship (paraphrase).
  • Religious experience adds a pair: transcendent (beyond the world) and immanent (active within it).
Mission 5

Necessary existence and aseity

  • Contingent being: could have not existed; depends on something else (you, a star).
  • Necessary being: cannot not exist.
  • Two senses (John Hick, 1922–2012):
  • Logical necessity: "God does not exist" is a contradiction (Anselm, Proslogion 3; Norman Malcolm). Ontological argument.
  • Factual (ontological) necessity: God depends on nothing and nothing can end God, but denying it is not a contradiction (Aquinas' Third Way; Hick's preference).
  • Aseity (Latin a se, "from itself"): God exists of God's own nature, not from or through anything else.
  • Link to the attributes: a being with aseity has no outside limit on its power, and nothing can change or end it (immutable, eternal).

Mission 5

Omnipotence within logic

  • Paradox of the stone: if God can make a stone God cannot lift, there is something God cannot do (lift it); if God cannot make it, there is something God cannot do (make it).
  • Aquinas (c. 1225–1274), Summa Theologica I, q. 25, a. 3: God can do all things that are possible absolutely: whatever involves no contradiction.
  • Read the source: "Hence it is better to say that such things cannot be done, than that God cannot do them." (English Dominican translation, 1920s)
  • A square circle, or a stone too heavy for an all-powerful being, is not a thing at all: a failure to describe anything.
  • C. S. Lewis (The Problem of Pain, 1940, ch. 2) agrees: putting "God can" in front of a meaningless string of words does not give it meaning (paraphrase).
  • Aquinas adds (same article, reply 2): God cannot sin, because to sin is to fall short; being able to fail is not a power.
  • Anselm, Proslogion 7, argues the same: being able to be corrupted or to lie is weakness, not power (paraphrase).
Mission check

For classical theists, "cannot" sometimes names nonsense rather than weakness. But foreknowledge and free will is not so easily dissolved: a later mission.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Reincarnation: the atman passes from body to body (Gita 2.22); karma shapes each birth; goal moksha.
  • Rebirth: no unchanging self (anatta); a causal stream shaped by karma continues, "neither the same nor another"; goal nibbana.
  • Near-death experiences: features, verifiable cases and the dying-brain explanation; evidence is disputed.
  • Moral arguments (Plato's Myth of Er, Kant's postulate), Aristotle's denial of personal survival, and the verification debate.

Mission 2

  • Hume: a wise person proportions belief to evidence; uniform experience always outweighs testimony to a miracle; Part II adds witnesses, susceptibility, origins and contrary claims.
  • Swinburne: violations are coherent (non-repeatable counter-instances); more evidence than testimony; contrary claims need not cancel.

Mission 3

  • Science reaches provisional conclusions by observation, hypothesis and repeatable experiment; religion by revelation, tradition, experience, reason and faith.
  • Flew's gardener: a claim that nothing could count against may assert nothing. Kinds of truth include empirical, literal, symbolic and existential.
  • Barbour's models: conflict (Dawkins), independence (Gould), dialogue, integration (Polkinghorne).

Mission 4

  • The Big Bang describes expansion from a hot, dense state (13.8 billion years); used both against and for belief in a creator.
  • Steady state lost to the background radiation; Einstein's cosmological constant returned as dark energy, its tiny value raising fine-tuning questions. Gaia: Earth as self-regulating system (Lovelock).
  • Darwin's natural selection explains apparent design; Dawkins' blind watchmaker has no foresight.
  • Responses: young-earth and old-earth creationism, intelligent design (Behe's irreducible complexity), theistic evolution.

Mission 5

  • Classical theism: God is the greatest conceivable being: omnipotent, omniscient, wholly good, eternal; necessary and a se (from itself).
