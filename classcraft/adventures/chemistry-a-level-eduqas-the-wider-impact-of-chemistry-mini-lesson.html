Mission 1

Mission 1: Can a reaction with a 100% yield still turn most of its reactants into waste?

Make a guess: Can a reaction with a 100% yield still turn most of its reactants into waste?

Mission 1

Atom economy

  • Atom economy: the percentage of the reactants' total mass that ends up in the desired product.
  • Atom economy = M of desired product ÷ sum of M of all reactants × 100%.
  • Mass is conserved, so the reactant total equals the product total: either gives the same answer.
  • Multiply each M by its coefficient in the balanced equation.
  • It is fixed by the equation. The masses used and the yield do not change it.
Mission 1

Worked example: limestone to lime

  • CaCO3(s) → CaO(s) + CO2(g). Desired product: CaO (lime).
  • M: CaCO3 100.1; CaO 56.1; CO2 44.0.
  • Atom economy = 56.1 ÷ 100.1 × 100 = 56.0%.
  • So 44% of the mass leaves as CO2, even at 100% yield.
  • An addition reaction with a single product has 100% atom economy.

Mission 1

Why high atom economy matters

  • Economic: less raw material bought per tonne of product; less waste to treat or dispose of.
  • Environmental: fewer finite resources used; less waste; often fewer steps and less energy.
  • Ethical / social: resources are kept for future generations; less waste dumped on communities.
  • This is sustainability: meeting today's needs without harming the future's.
  • If a by-product can be sold or reused, the process wastes less than its atom economy suggests.
Mission 1

Green chemistry

  • Design processes with a high atom economy and fewer steps.
  • Avoid solvents, or use safer ones such as water.
  • Use non-hazardous starting materials.
  • Use catalysts so reactions run at lower temperature and pressure: less energy.
  • Use renewable feedstocks and make products that biodegrade.
  • Example: ibuprofen. The older route has 6 steps and about 40% atom economy; the newer catalytic route has 3 steps and about 77%.
Mission 1

Impact of synthesis and energy

  • Social: synthesis gives medicines, fertilisers and materials; plants bring jobs, but also risk to nearby people.
  • Economic: costs of raw materials, energy and waste treatment decide whether a route is viable.
  • Environmental: by-products and pollutants; CO2 from fossil fuels burned to supply energy.
  • Energy production has the same three sides: power for industry and homes, cost, and emissions.
  • Green chemistry aims to improve all three together.
Mission check

?Hydrogen is manufactured by reacting methane with steam: CH4(g) + H2O(g) → CO(g) + 3H2(g). In one batch, 16.0 kg of methane gave 4.80 kg of hydrogen. What is the atom economy for producing hydrogen by this reaction? (Aᵣ: H 1.0, C 12.0, O 16.0)

Yes. Even at 100% yield, only 17.6% of the reactant mass becomes hydrogen; 82.4% leaves as carbon monoxide. Yield measures losses; atom economy measures by-products.

Mission 2

Alkanes as fuels: complete combustion

  • Alkanes burn in oxygen, releasing a lot of energy: they are fuels.
  • Complete combustion, in plenty of O2: all C → CO2, all H → H2O.
  • Balance C, then H, then O last.
  • Propane: C3H8(g) + 5O2(g) → 3CO2(g) + 4H2O(l).
  • Odd number of O atoms? Use ½O2, or double everything.
Mission 2

Background: elements burn too

  • Combustion is not only for fuels. Many elements burn in oxygen.
  • Metals form ionic oxides: 2Mg(s) + O2(g) → 2MgO(s); 4Al(s) + 3O2(g) → 2Al2O3(s).
  • Non-metals form covalent oxides: C(s) + O2(g) → CO2(g); S(s) + O2(g) → SO2(g).
  • Always write oxygen as O2.
  • Sulfur in a fuel burns to SO2, and in a hot engine N2 + O2 → 2NO. You'll meet these as pollutants.
Quick check
?When the equation for the complete combustion of octane, C8H18, is balanced with a coefficient of 2 for octane, what is the coefficient of O2?
Mission 2

Burning alcohols

  • Alcohols burn completely to CO2 and H2O, just like alkanes.
  • An alcohol already contains one O atom: subtract it before finding the O2.
  • Ethanol: C2H5OH(l) + 3O2(g) → 2CO2(g) + 3H2O(l).
  • Butan-1-ol: C4H9OH(l) + 6O2(g) → 4CO2(g) + 5H2O(l). Products have 13 O; 1 comes from the alcohol.
Quick check
?What amount of oxygen, O2, is needed to burn 0.500 mol of propan-1-ol, C3H7OH, completely?
Mission 2

Incomplete combustion

  • In limited O2, some carbon forms carbon monoxide, CO, or carbon (soot), C. Hydrogen still forms H2O.
  • To CO: 2C3H8(g) + 7O2(g) → 6CO(g) + 8H2O(l). To C: C3H8(g) + 2O2(g) → 3C(s) + 4H2O(l).
  • Less energy is released than in complete combustion.
  • Glowing soot particles make a yellow, smoky flame. Plenty of air: a blue flame.
  • The higher a fuel's carbon content, the smokier its flame: methane (75% C by mass) burns cleaner than octane (84% C).
  • In a spirit-burner experiment, soot on the can means incomplete combustion: your ΔcH comes out less exothermic than the true value.
Mission 2

Why carbon monoxide kills

  • CO is colourless, odourless and toxic.
  • It binds to haemoglobin in red blood cells far more strongly than O2 does.
  • So the blood carries less oxygen to the body's tissues.
  • You cannot see or smell it: that is why CO alarms save lives.
  • Soot (carbon particulates) also harms: it damages the lungs.
Quick check

Mission 2

Other pollutants from burning fuels

  • Carbon monoxide and carbon particulates: from incomplete combustion.
  • Unburned hydrocarbons: fuel that escapes burning.
  • Oxides of nitrogen, NOx: N2 and O2 from the air react at the high temperature in an engine. N2 + O2 → 2NO; then 2NO + O2 → 2NO2.
  • Sulfur dioxide, SO2: from sulfur impurities in the fuel. S + O2 → SO2.
  • NOx and SO2 are acidic gases: they dissolve in rain to form acid rain.
Mission 2

Biofuels: the carbon-neutral claim

  • A biofuel is a fuel made from recently living material (biomass).
  • Bioethanol: sugars are fermented by yeast, then the ethanol is separated by fractional distillation.
  • Photosynthesis: 6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2.
  • Fermentation: C6H12O6 → 2C2H5OH + 2CO2.
  • Combustion: 2C2H5OH + 6O2 → 4CO2 + 6H2O.
  • 6CO2 taken in; 2 + 4 = 6CO2 given out. So the claim: carbon neutral.
Mission 2

Why the claim fails; other issues

  • Not carbon neutral: fossil fuels power the tractors, make the fertiliser, transport the crop and heat the distillation.
  • For: renewable, as crops regrow in months; their carbon came recently from the air. Fossil fuels are finite.
  • Against: land for fuel crops cannot grow food: higher food prices, an ethical issue.
  • Against: clearing forest for crops releases stored carbon and destroys habitats.
Mission 2

Shale gas or low carbon?

  • Shale gas (mostly methane): less CO2 per kJ than coal, a home energy supply, jobs and tax.
  • But it is still a fossil fuel: it adds CO2, and leaked methane is a powerful greenhouse gas.
  • Fracking: large water use, a risk of polluting groundwater, small earthquakes.
  • Low-carbon sources (wind, solar, nuclear, biofuels): little CO2 in use, but costs, storage and supply that varies with the weather.
  • A judgement weighs social, economic and environmental evidence. Expect to argue both sides.
Quick check

Mission check

?After cooking outdoors, campers carry a still-glowing charcoal barbecue into their closed tent for warmth. Charcoal is almost pure carbon. Why is this deadly, even though there is no flame?

Same chemistry as the heater. Too little O2 turns carbon into CO, and CO stops your blood carrying oxygen.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Atom economy = M(desired product) ÷ total M of reactants (= total M of products) × 100, using coefficients.
  • It is set by the equation: a 100% yield can still waste most of the mass as by-product.
  • High atom economy saves resources, money and waste: a more sustainable process.

Mission 2

  • Plenty of O2: C → CO2, H → H2O. Limited O2: CO or C (soot), and a yellow, smoky flame.
  • CO is toxic: it binds to haemoglobin, so blood carries less O2.
  • Biofuels are renewable, but not carbon neutral: making them burns fossil fuels.
