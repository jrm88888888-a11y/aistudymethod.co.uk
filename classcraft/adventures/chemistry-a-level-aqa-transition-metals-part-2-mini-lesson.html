Mission 1

Mission 1: Two glasses of a blue sports drink look the same. How could a beam of light tell which holds more dye?

Make a guess: Two glasses of a blue sports drink look the same. How could a beam of light tell which holds more dye?

Mission 1

Why a solution looks coloured

  • White light contains every visible wavelength.
  • A coloured solution absorbs some wavelengths and transmits the rest.
  • You see the transmitted light. A blue solution absorbs mainly orange and red light.
  • More dye in the beam's path: more light absorbed.
Mission 1

Measuring absorbance

  • A colorimeter: lamp → coloured filter → sample in a cuvette → detector.
  • First, zero the instrument with a cuvette of the solvent alone (the blank).
  • The meter reads absorbance, A: how much light the sample absorbs. No units.
  • More light absorbed → less reaches the detector → higher absorbance.
Mission 1

Choosing the wavelength

  • Pick the filter whose light the solution absorbs most: the complementary colour.
  • Blue copper(II) solution: use an orange or red filter, not a blue one.
  • Here, a small change in concentration gives the biggest change in absorbance: maximum sensitivity.
Quick check
?A student uses a colorimeter to find the concentration of a blue solution of copper(II) sulfate. Which filter should the student use, and why?
Mission 1

The calibration graph

  • Make standard solutions of known concentration from one stock solution.
  • Measure each one's absorbance: same filter or wavelength, same cuvette.
  • Plot absorbance (y) against concentration (x). Here it is a straight line through the origin.
  • Measure the unknown the same way and read its concentration off the graph.
  • Example: copper(II) standards 0.020–0.100 mol dm⁻³ give A = 0.12–0.60. An unknown with A = 0.33 reads 0.055 mol dm⁻³.
  • The graph does the work: you don't need to know ε.
Quick check
?A student plots a calibration graph from copper(II) sulfate standards, then measures an unknown copper(II) solution. Which mistake would make the concentration found for the unknown too high?
Mission check

?A green solution of nickel(II) ions is taken from a lab waste container. Its absorbance is measured under the same conditions as these nickel(II) standards.
Standards (mol dm⁻³ : A): 0.050 : 0.16 · 0.100 : 0.32 · 0.150 : 0.48 · 0.200 : 0.64. The sample gives A = 0.40. What is its nickel(II) concentration, in mol dm⁻³?

Your eyes can't tell the two glasses apart, but a detector can. The drink with more dye absorbs more orange-red light; read each absorbance off a calibration graph, and you know exactly how much more dye it holds.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How can one metal give solutions of four different shades, one after another, in the same flask?

Make a guess: How can one metal give solutions of four different shades, one after another, in the same flask?

Mission 2

One metal, many states

  • Transition metals' 4s and 3d electrons are close in energy, so they can lose different numbers of them.
  • So one metal can exist in several oxidation states.
  • Each oxidation state, with its ligands, has its own colour.
  • Redox reagents move a metal between states; E⦵ values predict which reagent will work.
Mission 2

Vanadium's four colours

  • Start with ammonium vanadate(V), NH4VO3, in acid: VO2⁺, V +5, yellow.
  • Add zinc: VO²⁺ (+4) blue → V³⁺ (+3) green → V²⁺ (+2) violet.
  • Oxidation states: VO2⁺: x + 2(−2) = +1, so +5. VO²⁺: x − 2 = +2, so +4.
  • A green colour also appears briefly between yellow and blue: a mixture of the two.
  • Plug the flask with cotton wool: air slowly oxidises V²⁺ back.
Mission 2

Why zinc goes all the way

  • VO2⁺ + 2H⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ VO²⁺ + H2O · E⦵ = +1.00 V
  • VO²⁺ + 2H⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ V³⁺ + H2O · E⦵ = +0.34 V
  • V³⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ V²⁺ · E⦵ = −0.26 V
  • Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Zn · E⦵ = −0.76 V
  • Zinc reduces any couple with a more positive E⦵. All three vanadium steps are above −0.76 V, so all three go. Last step: E⦵cell = −0.26 − (−0.76) = +0.50 V.
  • V²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ V is −1.18 V, below zinc: no vanadium metal forms.
Quick check
?Use the vanadium E⦵ values above and: Fe³⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Fe²⁺, +0.77 V · MnO4⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ ⇌ Mn²⁺ + 4H2O, +1.51 V · Sn⁴⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Sn²⁺, +0.15 V. Which reagent will oxidise V³⁺ to VO²⁺ but no further?
Mission 2

pH and ligands change E⦵

  • The redox potential for a metal ion going to a lower state depends on pH and ligand.
  • pH: MnO4⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H2O uses H⁺, so manganate(VII) is a stronger oxidising agent in acid.
  • Chromium(III) is easily oxidised to chromium(VI) in alkali; in acid, Cr2O7²⁻ is instead a strong oxidising agent.
  • Ligand: [Co(H2O)6]³⁺/[Co(H2O)6]²⁺ is +1.81 V, but [Co(NH3)6]³⁺/[Co(NH3)6]²⁺ is +0.11 V.
  • Ligand: Ag⁺/Ag is +0.80 V, but [Ag(NH3)2]⁺/Ag is +0.37 V: a milder oxidising agent.
Mission 2

Tollens' reagent

  • Tollens' reagent contains [Ag(NH3)2]⁺ (silver nitrate, a little NaOH, then ammonia until the precipitate dissolves).
  • Warm it with an aldehyde: silver(I) is reduced to a silver mirror.
  • [Ag(NH3)2]⁺ + e⁻ → Ag + 2NH3
  • RCHO + 3OH⁻ → RCOO⁻ + 2H2O + 2e⁻ (the aldehyde is oxidised)
  • Ketones are not oxidised: no mirror. So Tollens' tells aldehydes from ketones.
Quick check
?O2 + 2H2O + 4e⁻ ⇌ 4OH⁻ has E⦵ = +0.40 V. A pink cobalt(II) solution is unchanged in air, but in excess ammonia the cobalt(II) is slowly oxidised to cobalt(III) by air. Why?
Mission check

?Zinc reduces acidified vanadate(V) all the way to violet V²⁺. A student uses tin instead: Sn²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Sn, E⦵ = −0.14 V. Vanadium: VO2⁺/VO²⁺ +1.00 V · VO²⁺/V³⁺ +0.34 V · V³⁺/V²⁺ −0.26 V. What is the final colour of the solution?

Each colour is a different oxidation state (with its own ligands). A redox reagent moves the metal from one to the next, and E⦵ values tell you how far it can go.

Mission 3

Mission 3: How can a purple solution measure the iron in a tablet without any added indicator?

Make a guess: How can a purple solution measure the iron in a tablet without any added indicator?

Mission 3

Redox titrations

  • Like an acid–base titration, but the reaction is redox.
  • An oxidising agent of known concentration reacts with a reducing agent (or the reverse).
  • You need the balanced equation to get the mole ratio.
  • Build it from the half-equations: electrons lost = electrons gained.
Mission 3

The manganate(VII) titration

  • Potassium manganate(VII), KMnO4(aq), goes in the burette. It is deep purple.
  • The flask holds the Fe²⁺(aq) plus excess dilute sulfuric acid.
  • Each drop turns colourless as MnO4⁻ is reduced to almost colourless Mn²⁺.
  • End-point: the first permanent pale pink. One drop of MnO4⁻ is now in excess.
  • Self-indicating: no indicator needed.
  • The solution is too dark to see the bottom of the meniscus: read the top.
Mission 3

The equation

  • Reduction: MnO4⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H2O
  • Oxidation: Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + e⁻ (× 5)
  • MnO4⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5Fe²⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 4H2O + 5Fe³⁺
  • Ratio: 1 mol MnO4⁻ : 5 mol Fe²⁺.
  • The H⁺ must be in excess; too little acid gives brown MnO2 instead of Mn²⁺.
Quick check
?Why is the iron(II) solution acidified with dilute sulfuric acid, not dilute hydrochloric acid?
Mission 3

Worked example: iron in a tablet

One tablet is dissolved in dilute H2SO4 and made up to 100.0 cm³. 25.0 cm³ portions need a mean titre of 23.45 cm³ of 0.00200 mol dm⁻³ KMnO4 (concordant titres, within 0.10 cm³). Find the mass of iron per tablet.

  • n(MnO4⁻) = 0.00200 × 23.45/1000 = 4.69 × 10⁻⁵ mol
  • n(Fe²⁺) in 25.0 cm³ = 5 × 4.69 × 10⁻⁵ = 2.345 × 10⁻⁴ mol
  • In 100.0 cm³: × 4 = 9.38 × 10⁻⁴ mol
  • Mass of Fe = 9.38 × 10⁻⁴ × 55.8 = 0.0523 g = 52.3 mg
  • Each burette reading is ±0.05 cm³, so a titre is ±0.10 cm³.
Quick check
?25.0 cm³ of an iron(II) solution needs 24.00 cm³ of 0.0200 mol dm⁻³ KMnO4 for complete reaction in acid. What is the concentration of Fe²⁺?
Mission 3

Ethanedioate with manganate(VII)

  • Ethanedioate (oxalate) ions are oxidised to CO2: C2O4²⁻ → 2CO2 + 2e⁻.
  • 2MnO4⁻ + 16H⁺ + 5C2O4²⁻ → 2Mn²⁺ + 8H2O + 10CO2
  • Ratio: 2 mol MnO4⁻ : 5 mol C2O4²⁻.
  • Warm the flask to about 60 °C: the reaction starts slowly.
  • It then speeds up: Mn²⁺ formed is the catalyst (autocatalysis).
  • Uses: the Mr of ethanedioic acid; H2O2 in hair bleach works the same way.
Quick check
?A solution of sodium ethanedioate is warmed and titrated with acidified KMnO4. 2MnO4⁻ + 16H⁺ + 5C2O4²⁻ → 2Mn²⁺ + 8H2O + 10CO2. The titre contains 4.00 × 10⁻⁴ mol MnO4⁻. How many moles of C2O4²⁻ were in the flask?
Mission check

?Hair bleach contains hydrogen peroxide. A diluted sample (25.0 cm³) is acidified and titrated with 0.0200 mol dm⁻³ KMnO4; the mean titre is 18.60 cm³. Half-equations: MnO4⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H2O and H2O2 → O2 + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻. What is the concentration of H2O2 in the diluted sample?

No indicator needed: the first lasting pink shows MnO4⁻ in excess. The ratio always comes from balancing the electrons.

Mission 4

Mission 4: How can a substance join in a reaction, change, and still come out unchanged at the end?

Make a guess: How can a substance join in a reaction, change, and still come out unchanged at the end?

Mission 4

What a catalyst does

  • A catalyst speeds up a reaction but is not used up overall.
  • It gives a different route with a lower activation energy.
  • It does react: it is used in one step and re-formed in a later one.
  • So its overall amount stays the same. One catalyst particle works again and again.
  • It does not change ΔH or the equilibrium yield.
Mission 4

Two kinds of catalyst

  • Homogeneous: in the same phase as the reactants (e.g. all in solution).
  • It reacts via an intermediate species, then is re-formed.
  • Heterogeneous: in a different phase (usually a solid with gases or liquids).
  • The reaction happens on its surface.
  • Transition metals and their compounds are good at both.
Mission 4

How a solid surface works

  • Adsorption: reactant molecules bond to active sites on the surface.
  • Bonds in the reactants weaken, and the molecules are held close, in a good orientation.
  • They react on the surface.
  • Desorption: products leave, freeing the sites for more reactants.
Mission 4

Catalysts at work

  • Catalysts let industry run at lower temperatures: less energy, less fossil fuel burned, less CO2.
  • Haber process (ammonia): iron, Fe.
  • Contact process (SO2 → SO3): vanadium(V) oxide, V2O5.
Mission 4

Support media and poisoning

  • Catalysts are spread thinly on a support medium (e.g. a ceramic honeycomb).
  • That maximises surface area and minimises the cost of the metal used.
  • Poisoning: impurities bind to active sites and block them, so the catalyst works less well.
  • Sulfur compounds poison the iron in the Haber process, so the gases are purified first.
  • Lead poisons catalytic converters: that's one reason fuel is unleaded.
  • Cost: purifying feedstocks, and replacing or regenerating the catalyst.
Quick check
?In which reaction is the catalyst homogeneous?
Mission 4

V2O5 in the Contact process

  • Overall: 2SO2 + O2 ⇌ 2SO3.
  • Step 1: SO2 + V2O5 → SO3 + V2O4. Vanadium: +5 → +4.
  • Step 2: 2V2O4 + O2 → 2V2O5. Vanadium: +4 → +5.
  • V2O5 is re-formed, so it is a catalyst (heterogeneous: solid with gases).
  • It works because vanadium has variable oxidation states: it can take an O and give it back.
Mission 4

Fe²⁺: a homogeneous catalyst

  • S2O8²⁻ + 2I⁻ → 2SO4²⁻ + I2 is slow: two negative ions repel, so Ea is high.
  • Step 1: S2O8²⁻ + 2Fe²⁺ → 2SO4²⁻ + 2Fe³⁺.
  • Step 2: 2Fe³⁺ + 2I⁻ → 2Fe²⁺ + I2.
  • Each step brings together oppositely charged ions: lower Ea. Fe²⁺ is re-formed.
  • Fe³⁺ is the intermediate species.
  • Both steps are feasible: E⦵(Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺) = +0.77 V lies between E⦵(I2/I⁻) = +0.54 V and E⦵(S2O8²⁻/SO4²⁻) = +2.01 V.
Mission 4

Autocatalysis: Mn²⁺

  • 2MnO4⁻ + 16H⁺ + 5C2O4²⁻ → 2Mn²⁺ + 8H2O + 10CO2.
  • Slow at first: MnO4⁻ and C2O4²⁻ are both negative.
  • The product Mn²⁺ is the catalyst: an autocatalyst.
  • Step 1: 4Mn²⁺ + MnO4⁻ + 8H⁺ → 5Mn³⁺ + 4H2O.
  • Step 2: 2Mn³⁺ + C2O4²⁻ → 2Mn²⁺ + 2CO2.
  • [MnO4⁻] against time: a slow start, then faster as Mn²⁺ builds up, then slower as reactants run out.
Quick check
?Fe²⁺ ions catalyse the reaction between S2O8²⁻ and I⁻ ions. Which other ion could catalyse this reaction?
Mission check

?High in the atmosphere, chlorine atoms destroy ozone in two steps. Overall: O3 + O → 2O2. Which statement is correct?
  • Step 1: Cl• + O3 → ClO• + O2. Step 2: ClO• + O → Cl• + O2.

That's how a catalyst joins in, changes, and comes out unchanged. One chlorine atom can go around this cycle many thousands of times.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Coloured species absorb light; absorbance rises in step with concentration. A calibration graph turns absorbance into concentration.

Mission 2

  • Transition metals have several oxidation states, each with its own colour, linked by redox.
  • Zinc in acid takes vanadium from yellow +5 to blue +4, green +3, violet +2; E⦵ values show why it stops at V²⁺.

Mission 3

  • Manganate(VII) titrations: excess dilute H2SO4; self-indicating; end-point first permanent pale pink.
  • MnO4⁻ : Fe²⁺ = 1 : 5; build any other ratio from the half-equations.
  • MnO4⁻ : C2O4²⁻ = 2 : 5 (warm; autocatalysed by Mn²⁺).

Mission 4

  • A catalyst reacts in one step and is re-formed in a later one, by a route with lower Ea.
  • Homogeneous: same phase, via an intermediate. Heterogeneous: different phase, on the surface.
