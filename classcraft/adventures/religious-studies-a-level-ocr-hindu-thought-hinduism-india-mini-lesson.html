Mission 1: What makes someone a Hindu: belief, birth, or loving India as a holy land?

Make a guess: What makes someone a Hindu: belief, birth, or loving India as a holy land?

Mission 1

India as a holy land

  • For many Hindus the land of India is itself sacred: Bharat, the land between the ocean and the Himalayas (Vishnu Purana 2.3.1, paraphrase).
  • Tirthas ("crossing places"): rivers, mountains and towns where the divine is close: the Ganges, Varanasi, the Char Dham at the four corners of India.
  • Avatars, saints and epic heroes walked this land: Rama's Ayodhya, Krishna's Vrindavan.
  • Bharat Mata ("Mother India"): a 19th–20th-century image of the nation as a goddess.
  • Traditional rule: orthodox high-caste Hindus were warned against crossing the sea (kala pani, "black water"), so India's sacred geography stayed central.
  • Holy land can be religious (pilgrimage) or political (the nation itself). Hindutva joins the two.
Mission 1

Savarkar: "Hindu-ness"

  • Hindutva = "Hindu-ness". Set out by V. D. Savarkar (1883–1966) in Essentials of Hindutva (1923), written in prison.
  • Savarkar, himself a rationalist, separated Hindutva (a people's culture, history and nation) from Hinduism (religious belief).
  • His definition (paraphrase): a Hindu is someone who regards India (Bharatvarsha) as both pitribhumi (fatherland) and punyabhumi (holy land).
  • So Sikhs, Jains and Buddhists count: their faiths began in India.
  • Muslims and Christians, even if Indian for generations, do not fully belong: their holy lands are elsewhere.
  • Your spec: "Hindu-ness" as a cultural and political identity.

Mission 1

The Sangh Parivar

  • Sangh Parivar = "family of organisations" around the RSS (Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh, "National Volunteer Organisation"), founded 1925 in Nagpur by K. B. Hedgewar.
  • RSS: daily shakhas (branches) for physical training, discipline and "character-building"; M. S. Golwalkar (1906–1973) led it 1940–1973.
  • VHP (Vishva Hindu Parishad, 1964): religious leaders and campaigns, e.g. for a Rama temple at Ayodhya.
  • BJP (Bharatiya Janata Party, 1980): the political party; it has led India's government since 2014.
  • Others: student, labour and youth wings (e.g. Bajrang Dal), and social service bodies.
  • Ayodhya: the Babri Masjid was demolished in 1992; the Supreme Court gave the site for a temple (2019); the Ram Mandir was consecrated in 2024.
Mission 1

Freedom of religious expression

  • India's Constitution gives the right to "profess, practise and propagate" religion (Article 25, paraphrase).
  • Anti-conversion laws ("freedom of religion" acts) in several states restrict conversion by "force, fraud or allurement". Supporters: they protect poor Hindus from pressure. Critics: they limit the right to propagate and to convert.
  • Cow protection laws in many states: for many Hindus the cow is sacred; for some Muslims, Christians and Dalits it limits diet and livelihood.
  • Ghar wapsi ("homecoming"): Sangh campaigns to reconvert Christians and Muslims to Hinduism.
Mission 1

Hindutva and secularisation

  • India is a secular state (Constitution 1950; "secular" added to the Preamble in 1976). Indian secularism means equal respect for all religions, not strict separation (see Mission 2).
  • Hindutva critics call this "pseudo-secularism": they argue it treats minorities better than Hindus (e.g. separate Muslim personal law, state control of Hindu temples).
  • Their response to secularisation (the weakening of religion in public life): reassert Hindu culture in public: temples, festivals, symbols, school history, the name Bharat.
  • So Hindutva is both a response to secularism (the state's policy) and to secularisation (social change).
Mission 1Diversity

Hindus on Hindutva

  • Supporters: Hindutva is cultural, not theocratic; it includes all Indic faiths; it corrects centuries of foreign rule and an unfair secularism. India's Supreme Court in 1995 described Hindutva as indicating a "way of life".
  • Gandhian and Neo-Vedantin critics: true Hinduism is tolerant and universal; Hindutva turns a spiritual tradition into a political identity.
  • Secular and liberal Hindus: Hindutva threatens minorities' equal citizenship; they point to violence in Ayodhya (1992) and Gujarat (2002).
  • Dalit critics: Hindutva speaks for upper-caste Hinduism and hides caste inequality.
  • Many Sikhs, Jains and Buddhists: reject being counted as "Hindu".
  • Scholars, e.g. Christophe Jaffrelot (The Hindu Nationalist Movement and Indian Politics, 1996), study it as ethnic nationalism; supporters reject this label.

Mission 1Evaluate

"Hindutva is the only effective Hindu response to secularisation."

  • For: it has mass support and electoral success; it gives a clear identity in a fast-changing society; it has brought Hindu symbols back into public life.
  • Against: other responses also work: Neo-Vedanta's universal spirituality; devotional movements (ISKCON, Swaminarayan) that grow without politics; reform movements serving the poor (Ramakrishna Mission).
  • Against: "effective" for whom? Critics say it secures power but divides society and narrows Hinduism.
  • Judge it by: what counts as "effective" (numbers, depth of faith, social harmony)? Is Hindutva mainly religious at all?
Mission check

?A Jain businessman and a Muslim teacher were both born in Mumbai and have never left India. On Savarkar's definition of Hindutva, how would they be classed?

Hindutva draws its line by holy land, not by belief: that is what makes it a cultural and political identity rather than a creed.

Make a guess: Four in five Indians are Hindu, yet India calls itself secular. What does secular mean there?

Mission 2

A different kind of secular

  • The Constitution (1950) has no state religion; "secular" was added to its Preamble in 1976.
  • Equality before the law and no discrimination on grounds of religion or caste (Articles 14–15).
  • Everyone may "profess, practise and propagate" religion (Article 25, paraphrase); religious groups may run their own affairs and schools (Articles 26, 30).
  • Not a wall of separation (as in the USA or France): the Indian state engages with all religions: it funds pilgrimages, manages many Hindu temples, and has reformed Hindu practice by law.
  • Political theorist Rajeev Bhargava calls this "principled distance": the state may treat religions differently, but for the sake of equality.
Mission 2

Diversity in fact

  • 2011 Census: Hindus 79.8%, Muslims 14.2%, Christians 2.3%, Sikhs 1.7%, Buddhists 0.7%, Jains 0.4%. (The next census is under way; its religion figures are not yet out.)
  • India has one of the world's largest Muslim populations.
  • Born in India (Hindu, Sikh, Jain, Buddhist): share ideas such as karma, rebirth, dharma.
  • Born outside (Islam, Christianity, Zoroastrianism, Judaism): sometimes seen by Hindu nationalists as foreign (see Mission 1).
  • Tensions: Partition (1947) and later riots; the 1984 anti-Sikh violence after Indira Gandhi's assassination.
  • Shared life too: Hindus visit Sufi shrines (dargahs); mixed neighbourhoods and festivals.
  • Your spec: "the reality of religious diversity".

Mission 2

Personal law

  • Personal law: family matters (marriage, divorce, inheritance, adoption) are governed by each religion's law.
  • Hindu Code (1955–56): Hindu Marriage Act, Hindu Succession Act and others; brought monogamy, divorce and inheritance for daughters (made equal in 2005).
  • It also covers Sikhs, Jains and Buddhists: the Hindu Code Acts define them as "Hindus" (e.g. Hindu Marriage Act, section 2), as Article 25 (Explanation II) also does for its reform clause. Many Sikhs object.
  • Muslim Personal Law (Shariat Application Act, 1937), and separate Christian and Parsi laws.
  • A secular option for anyone: the Special Marriage Act (1954), e.g. for interfaith couples.
  • Your spec: "family law". Note: Hindu law was reformed by the state; the minorities' laws largely were not.
Mission 2

Shah Bano and a Uniform Civil Code

  • The Constitution asks the state to "endeavour" to secure a Uniform Civil Code (UCC) (Article 44): one family law for all.
  • Shah Bano (1985): the Supreme Court granted a divorced Muslim woman maintenance; Parliament's 1986 Act was seen as reversing it after Muslim protests.
  • Shayara Bano (2017): the Supreme Court struck down instant triple talaq; a 2019 Act made it a criminal offence.
  • Uttarakhand passed India's first state UCC (2024).
  • The debate: gender equality and national unity vs minorities' religious freedom.
Mission 2Diversity

Hindu responses to personal law

  • Hindutva (BJP, RSS): a UCC now; separate Muslim law is "appeasement" and divides the nation.
  • Liberal and feminist Hindus: equal rights for women of every faith, but reform should not be imposed by a Hindu majority; some prefer reform within each community.
  • Traditional Hindus in the 1950s: opposed the Hindu Code as interfering with dharma and the shastras; the RSS and Hindu Mahasabha joined the protests. B. R. Ambedkar, its chief drafter, resigned in 1951 partly over delays.
  • Hindus who stress pluralism: different communities, different laws, is itself a Hindu way of living with diversity.

Mission 2

Scheduled castes and religion

  • Article 17 abolishes "untouchability"; laws punish caste discrimination and violence (e.g. 1955, 1989 Acts).
  • Scheduled Castes (SCs): castes listed for reservations in education, government jobs and legislatures.
  • The 1950 Order allowed only Hindus to be SCs; Sikhs were added (1956), then Buddhists (1990).
  • Dalit Christians and Dalit Muslims are excluded, though many face the same caste discrimination: a long-running court and political debate.
  • Many Dalits converted to escape caste: Ambedkar and hundreds of thousands of followers became Buddhist in 1956.
  • So caste reaches beyond Hinduism: it shapes Sikh, Christian and Muslim communities too (see Mission 3).
Mission 2

Secularisation in India

  • Signs: cities, education, the internet, consumer culture; more inter-caste marriage; decline of some rituals.
  • Counter-signs: festivals and pilgrimages are larger than ever; television epics; new gurus and temples; religion very visible in politics.
  • So India shows change in religion more than simple decline.
  • Orthodox view: history runs in fixed cycles; this is the Kali Yuga, an age of foretold decline in dharma.
  • So orthodox Hindus can accept some secularisation as expected, not as a defeat, while keeping their own dharma.
  • Hindu attempts to reform Hinduism (e.g. the Hindu Code; temple entry for Dalits) have often come through the secular state.
Quick check
?Why might an orthodox Hindu accept some secularisation calmly?
Mission 2Evaluate

"India is a genuinely secular state."

  • For: no state religion; equal rights in the Constitution; courts strike down religious discrimination; minorities have held the highest offices.
  • Against (Hindutva critics): the state favours minorities (separate personal law) and controls Hindu temples but not mosques or churches: "pseudo-secular".
  • Against (minority and liberal critics): in practice Hindu majoritarianism is growing: cow-protection and anti-conversion laws; communal violence; exclusion of Dalit Christians and Muslims from SC status.
  • Another view: "secular" in India never meant separation; judged by equal respect, the question is whether respect is in fact equal.
  • Judge it by: which definition of secular? Constitution or practice? Which period?

India's secularism lets each religion keep its own family law, and that is exactly why it is argued over.

Mission 3

Varna and jati: two different things

  • Varna ("colour", "class"): four classes named in scripture: Brahmins (priests, teachers), Kshatriyas (rulers, warriors), Vaishyas (merchants, farmers), Shudras (labourers, servants).
  • Earliest account: the Purusha Sukta (Rig Veda 10.90.12): the four come from the mouth, arms, thighs and feet of the cosmic person (paraphrase).
  • Jati ("birth"): thousands of local groups, each traditionally with its own occupation, marrying within the group (endogamy) and ranked by region.
  • "Caste" is a Portuguese word (casta) and is used for both: a source of confusion.
  • Overlap: jatis often claim a varna; varna gives the scriptural frame, jati the lived reality.
Mission 3Read the source

Varna by nature, devotion for all

Bhagavad Gita 18.41 (Edwin Arnold, 1885):

> "The work of Brahmans, Kshatriyas, Vaisyas, / And Sudras, O thou Slayer of thy Foes! / Is fixed by reason of the Qualities / Planted in each"

Bhagavad Gita 9.32 (Arnold):

> "whoso will turn to Me, / Though they be born from the very womb of Sin, / Woman or man; sprung of the Vaisya caste / Or lowly disregarded Sudra,--all / Plant foot upon the highest path"

Q1 In 18.41, what fixes each varna's work: birth or qualities?

Show answer

The text says "the Qualities" (gunas): reformers stress this; traditionalists say qualities come with birth.

Q2 What does 9.32 offer that varna rules did not?

Show answer

The highest path is open to all who turn to Krishna, whatever their birth or sex.

  • Arnold's word "caste" translates varna, not jati.
  • Commentators differ on whether "born from the very womb of Sin" describes the groups named after it or a separate group. Critics who read it as describing them say the verse opens the highest path to all while keeping the ranking.
Mission 3

Untouchability

  • Some groups were placed outside the four varnas (avarna): treated as permanently polluting.
  • The idea rests on ritual purity and pollution: work with death, leather, waste or cremation was seen as impure, and the impurity as inherited.
  • Practices: separate wells and settlements; no entry to many temples; no shared food; violence against those who broke the rules.
  • Texts used to support it: e.g. Manusmriti 10.51–56 sets the lowest groups to live outside the village (paraphrase).
  • India's Constitution (1950) bans untouchability (Article 17), yet the practices have not disappeared.
  • Social issues today: discrimination in jobs, housing and marriage; violence; manual scavenging (cleaning waste by hand), still done mostly by Dalits despite being banned.

Mission 3

Whose name? Dalit, Harijan, Scheduled Castes

  • Untouchable: the old outsider word; now offensive.
  • Harijan ("people of Hari", i.e. of God): Gandhi's term from the 1930s. Meant as honour; many Dalits reject it as patronising.
  • Dalit ("broken", "crushed"): a name chosen by the community itself; often traced to the 19th-century reformer Jyotirao Phule, used by Ambedkar, and made popular by the Dalit Panthers (1972).
  • Scheduled Castes (SC): the legal term, from the lists ("schedules") in the Constitution.
  • The choice of name is itself the question: who names whom?
Mission 3

Secular India and the law

  • India's Constitution (1950) is secular and guarantees equality. Article 17 abolishes untouchability and makes its practice an offence.
  • Reservations: quotas for Scheduled Castes in education, government jobs and legislatures (Articles 15(4), 16(4), 330, 332).
  • Laws: Untouchability (Offences) Act 1955 (renamed Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1976); SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act 1989.
  • Religion and the law: SC status is limited to Hindus, Sikhs (from 1956) and Buddhists (from 1990). Dalit Christians and Muslims are excluded: a live debate.
  • Temple entry campaigns: the Vaikom satyagraha (1924–25); Travancore's Temple Entry Proclamation (1936).
  • Family law and personal law.
Mission 3

Bhakti transforms the rules

  • Varnashrama-dharma ties duty to birth (varna) and life stage (ashrama).
  • Bhakti (loving devotion) asks only for love of God, not birth or Sanskrit learning.
  • Low-caste and Dalit saints were honoured: Nandanar (Tamil Shaiva, Dalit), Chokhamela (14th century, Maharashtra, Mahar), Ravidas (15th–16th century, a leatherworker; North India).
  • Saints sang in local languages, so devotion did not need Brahmin priests.
  • Limits: bhakti often changed who is close to God more than who marries whom: caste rules in society largely stayed. Chokhamela was still barred from the Pandharpur temple.
Mission 3

Brahminism and the status of jati today

  • Brahminism: critics' term for the ideology that places Brahmins and their purity rules at the top of society.
  • Louis Dumont (1911–1998), Homo Hierarchicus (1966): caste is ordered by the opposition of pure and impure.
  • Views on jati today:
  • Traditionalists: jati and its rules are part of dharma and keep social order.
  • Reformers (e.g. Vivekananda, Gandhi): varna as duty is valid; untouchability and hierarchy by birth are corruptions.
  • Abolitionists (e.g. Ambedkar): caste is rooted in the scriptures themselves and must go.
  • Modern life (cities, jobs, education) has weakened some rules; caste remains strong in marriage and politics.
Mission 3Diversity

Who speaks for Dalits?

  • Others speaking for them: Gandhi called himself a voice for "Harijans", and fasted to oppose separate electorates for them (1932).
  • Dalits speaking for themselves: B. R. Ambedkar (1891–1956); the Dalit Panthers; Dalit literature, e.g. Omprakash Valmiki, Joothan (1997); Bama, Karukku (1992).
  • Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak, "Can the Subaltern Speak?" (1988): the most oppressed are spoken about by others, so their own voice is hard to hear.
  • Many Dalit voices reject Hindu identity (Ambedkar became a Buddhist in 1956); others stay Hindu and reclaim saints such as Ravidas and Valmiki.

Mission 3Evaluate

Is caste part of Hindu teaching, or only culture?

  • Claim: Caste discrimination is a cultural corruption, not Hindu teaching.
  • For: the Gita links varna to qualities, not birth (18.41); bhakti saints taught equality before God (9.32).
  • For: many Hindu reformers (Vivekananda, Gandhi) and many Hindus today reject untouchability as against dharma.
  • Against: the Purusha Sukta and Manusmriti give caste and pollution rules scriptural support.
  • Against: Ambedkar: you cannot separate caste from the texts that justify it.
  • Against: critics note that even Gita 9.32 may keep the ranking while opening the path ("born from the very womb of Sin").
  • Judgement criteria: Which texts count as authoritative? Who interprets them? Does the view listen to Dalit voices themselves?
  • Discussion point: what is religious teaching, and what is interpretation, custom or wider culture?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • India is a holy land of tirthas and avatars; Hindutva turns that sacred land into a national identity.
  • Savarkar (1923): a Hindu treats India as both fatherland and holy land; the Sangh Parivar (RSS, VHP, BJP) builds on this.
  • Hindus are divided: supporters see cultural pride and fair treatment; critics see exclusion of minorities and a politicised Hinduism.

Mission 2

  • India's secularism is "principled distance": equal respect and state engagement, not separation.
  • Personal law: Hindu law (also for Sikhs, Jains, Buddhists) was reformed in 1955–56; the UCC debate divides Hindus and minorities.
  • Secularisation in India means change more than decline; orthodox Hindus may see it as part of the Kali Yuga.

Mission 3

  • Varna is the four-class scriptural frame; jati is the lived system of birth groups; Dalits were placed outside the four varnas and treated as "untouchable".
  • India's Constitution abolishes untouchability and reserves places for Scheduled Castes; bhakti opened devotion to all but left much of social caste in place.
  • The debate includes who speaks: others (Gandhi's "Harijans") or Dalits themselves (Ambedkar, Dalit writers).
