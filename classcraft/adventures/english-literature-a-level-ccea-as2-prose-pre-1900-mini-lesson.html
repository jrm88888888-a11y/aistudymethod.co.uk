AS 2: The Study of Prose Pre-1900

This mini-lesson trains the skills CCEA Unit AS 2 rewards for a pre-1900 novel — not a single set text. You will learn to read a novelist's narrative methods, recognise pre-1900 genre conventions, and use context and different interpretations to build an argument.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you are ready.

The unit at a glance

What AS 2 actually assesses

AS 2 is a 1-hour, closed-book paper: one essay on a single pre-1900 novel. Because you study one text, there is no AO4 (comparison). The objectives are:

  • AO1 — an informed, personal response in accurate critical language.
  • AO2 — analyse the writer's use of narrative methods such as structure, form and language.
  • AO3 — the significance and influence of the contexts in which the novel was written and received.
  • AO5 — explore the novel informed by different interpretations.

Key emphasis: "closed book" means you must know precise, short quotations and telling detail by heart to support every point.

Narrative method (AO2)

What "narrative methods" means

AO2 is about how the story is told, not what happens. The novelist's toolkit includes:

  • Narrative voice / point of view — first-person, third-person limited, or omniscient narration.
  • Structure — chronology or disruption, frame narratives, parallel plots, pace and the placing of climaxes.
  • Form — the kind of novel: epistolary, bildungsroman, Gothic, realist.
  • Language — diction, imagery, symbolism, dialogue and free indirect discourse.

Skill, not summary: retelling the plot scores nothing for AO2. Analyse the effect of a chosen method on the reader.

Narrative voice

Voice, focalisation and reliability

Narratology gives us precise terms for perspective:

  • Voice — "who speaks" (the narrator); focalisation — "who perceives" the events. The distinction is Gérard Genette's (Narrative Discourse).
  • Unreliable narrator — a narrator whose account the work quietly undercuts. The term is Wayne Booth's (The Rhetoric of Fiction, 1961), alongside his idea of the implied author.
  • Free indirect discourse — third-person narration that slips into a character's own idiom, blurring narrator and character.

Why it matters: a shift in focalisation, or a narrator we learn to doubt, is exactly the kind of method that shapes meaning for AO2.

Genre & form

Pre-1900 forms of the novel

Knowing a novel's form lets you read against its conventions and expectations:

  • Epistolary — told through letters and documents, giving multiple limited viewpoints and immediacy.
  • Bildungsroman — traces a protagonist's growth from youth to maturity and self-knowledge.
  • Gothic — terror, the sublime, the uncanny, transgression and imprisoning settings.
  • Realism — the ambition to represent ordinary social life plausibly and in detail, prominent in the Victorian novel.

Use it as method: when a novel fulfils or subverts its genre's conventions, that tension is rich material for analysis.

Context (AO3)

Context for a pre-1900 novel

AO3 rewards relevant contexts of production and reception, drawn from outside the text:

  • Social — class, money, work, and especially attitudes to gender and marriage.
  • Historical — industrialisation, empire, science, changing religious belief.
  • Literary — the traditions the novel belongs to (Gothic, Romantic, Victorian realism).
  • Reception — how the novel's meaning has shifted for later readers.

Discipline: context must be relevant and woven in. A history essay bolted on top of the analysis earns nothing.

Interpretations (AO5)

Reading through different lenses

AO5 credits exploring the novel "informed by different interpretations". Critical lenses open up alternative readings:

  • Feminist — how the text represents gender, power and the domestic.
  • Marxist — class, labour and economic forces shaping character and plot.
  • Psychoanalytic — desire, repression and the unconscious.
  • Postcolonial — empire, race and the "other".

AO5 is judgement, not name-dropping: set readings against one another and argue which the text best supports.

Genre spotlight (illustrative)

Gothic conventions

Several set pre-1900 novels draw on the Gothic mode, so its conventions are worth knowing as an illustrative frame:

  • Terror and the sublime — awe mixed with dread before the vast or overwhelming.
  • The uncanny — the strangely familiar; doubles, the return of the repressed.
  • Transgression — forbidden knowledge, desire or ambition, and its punishment.
  • Setting — ruins, castles, storms and claustrophobic, imprisoning spaces.

Illustrative only: these conventions describe the Gothic mode in general; check which apply to your own set novel rather than assuming all do.

Expression (AO1)

Building the essay

With one text and one question, AS 2 rewards a sustained, single argument:

  • Answer the question asked with a clear thesis, not a prepared plot summary.
  • Support every claim with precise, memorised textual detail (closed book).
  • Move from method (AO2) to interpretation (AO5) to context (AO3) within the same argument.
  • Use accurate terminology — focalisation, free indirect discourse, bildungsroman — with control.

Golden rule: analysis of the writer's craft, informed by context and interpretation, always beats retelling.

Recap

The big ideas to know

Shape of AS 2: one pre-1900 novel · one closed-book essay · AO1, AO2, AO3, AO5 (no AO4)

Narrative method (AO2): voice & focalisation (Genette) · structure · form · language & free indirect discourse

Reliability: the unreliable narrator and implied author (Booth)

Form: epistolary · bildungsroman · Gothic · realism

Context (AO3): social, historical, literary and reception — woven in

Interpretations (AO5): feminist, Marxist, psychoanalytic, postcolonial — weighed and judged
