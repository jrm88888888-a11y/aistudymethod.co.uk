Mission 1

Mission 1: Why will a shop swap a meal for a printed slip of paper but not for a bag of apples?

Make a guess: Why is a printed slip of paper accepted as payment?

Mission 1

Life without money: barter

  • Barter: swapping goods and services directly. Apples for bread, a haircut for a meal.
  • Problem 1: the double coincidence of wants. You need someone who has what you want and wants what you have.
  • Problem 2: no common measure. How many apples is a haircut worth? Every pair of goods needs its own price.
  • Problem 3: hard to save. Apples rot.
  • Problem 4: hard to split. Half a cow is not much use.
  • Money solves all four. It makes specialization and trade far easier.
Mission 1

The functions of money

  • Money: anything generally accepted as a means of payment.
  • Its functions:
    • Medium of exchange: accepted in payment for goods and services.
    • Unit of account (or measure of value): prices and incomes are stated in it, so values can be compared.
    • Store of value: holds purchasing power over time.
    • Standard of deferred payment: debts and future payments are stated in it.
  • The AP course lists three functions: medium of exchange, unit of account and store of value.
  • Say: "money is a medium of exchange" — not: "money is something you buy things with because it is valuable".
Mission 1

When money stops working: Zimbabwe

  • 2007–2008: prices in Zimbabwe rose faster and faster: hyperinflation.
  • Official annual inflation reached 231 million percent (July 2008).
  • In early 2009 the central bank issued a 100 trillion Zimbabwe dollar note.
  • What failed first: the store of value. Money lost value in days, so people spent it at once.
  • Then the unit of account: shops priced goods in US dollars or changed prices several times a day.
  • Then the medium of exchange: sellers refused Zimbabwe dollars.
  • In 2009 Zimbabwe allowed the US dollar and other currencies to be used instead.
Quick check
?In a country with very high inflation, a family is paid in the local currency. They change their wages into a foreign currency on payday and keep their savings in it. Which function of the local currency has broken down most clearly?
Mission 1

What makes good money?

  • Acceptable: people trust that others will take it.
  • Scarce / limited in supply: if anyone can make more, it loses value.
  • Durable: does not rot or wear out quickly.
  • Portable: easy to carry.
  • Divisible: can be split into small amounts.
  • Uniform: every unit is the same.
  • Hard to forge.
  • Gold scores well on most. Apples score badly on durability and uniformity.
Mission 1

Forms of money

  • Commodity money: has value itself. Gold, silver, salt.
  • Representative money: paper that could be swapped for gold (a gold standard).
  • Fiat money: notes and coins with little value as objects. Accepted because the law makes them legal tender and people trust they will be accepted.
  • Bank deposits: numbers in accounts, spent by card, transfer or check.
    • In the UK, bank deposits are about 97% of broad money; notes and coins only about 3% (Bank of England, 2014).
  • Cryptocurrencies (e.g. Bitcoin): a debated case.
    • Their prices swing a lot. Few shops price goods in them.
    • Most central banks say they are not money in the full sense.
Quick check
?A café accepts payment in a cryptocurrency. Its price in US dollars often moves by 10% or more in a week, so the café must rewrite its menu prices in the cryptocurrency every day. Which function of money is failing most directly here?
Mission 1

Liquidity

  • Liquidity: how quickly and cheaply an asset can be turned into spending power without losing value.
  • Most liquid to least: notes and coins → current account deposits → savings deposits → fixed-term deposits → bonds and shares → houses.
  • Money is the most liquid asset. Narrow and broad measures of money are drawn along this ladder.
  • Assets also differ in rate of return (what they earn) and risk (how far the return may vary).
  • Cash and demand deposits: most liquid, low return, low risk. Bonds and stocks: less liquid, usually higher return, more risk.
Mission 1

M1, M2 and the monetary base

  • M1: currency in circulation + checkable (demand) deposits.
  • M2: M1 + savings deposits + small time deposits (under $100,000) + retail money market funds.
  • Monetary base: currency in circulation + bank reserves.
  • Worked example (illustrative data, $bn):
    • Currency 200; checkable deposits 300; savings deposits 400.
    • Small time deposits 150; money market funds 100; bank reserves 50.
    • M1 = 200 + 300 = 500.
    • M2 = 500 + 400 + 150 + 100 = 1,150.
    • Monetary base = 200 + 50 = 250.
  • Since 2020, the Fed's published M1 also includes savings deposits. Use the definitions a question gives.
Quick check
?Illustrative data ($bn): currency in circulation 80; checkable deposits 120; savings deposits 200; small time deposits 60; money market funds 40; large time deposits 150; bank reserves 30. What is M2?
Mission check

?In a prisoner-of-war camp in the Second World War, cigarettes became money. Prisoners who did not smoke still accepted cigarettes in payment for food and clothes. What best explains why?

Money is whatever people generally accept in payment, because each person expects others to accept it too.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why would anyone sell a safe government bond for less than they paid for it?

Make a guess: Why might a safe bond be sold for less than it cost?

Mission 2

What financial markets do

  • Financial markets link savers (who have spare money) with borrowers (who need it).
  • Their roles:
    • Help people save: a safe place and a return.
    • Lend to households and firms: mortgages, business loans.
    • Make exchange easy: payments for goods and services.
    • Provide forward markets: fix a price today for a deal in the future.
    • Provide a market for equities (shares), so firms can raise money.
  • Benefit to the real economy: savings flow to investment, which raises productive capacity and growth.
  • Households choose between holding money and other financial assets, such as bonds and stocks.
Mission 2

Debt versus equity

  • Debt: borrowed money that must be repaid with interest.
    • Loans from banks.
    • Bonds: a borrower (government or firm) sells an IOU that pays fixed yearly interest and repays the face value at maturity.
  • Equity: shares give part-ownership of a firm.
    • Shareholders may get dividends if the firm makes a profit; nothing is guaranteed.
    • If a firm fails, lenders are paid before shareholders.
  • So: shares usually carry more risk but a higher expected return.
  • In the US, shares are usually called stocks.
  • Say: "a firm raises equity finance by selling shares" — not: "a firm borrows by selling shares".
Mission 2

Liquidity, return and risk

  • Every asset can be judged on three things:
    • Liquidity: how easily it turns into spending power.
    • Rate of return: what it earns (interest, dividends, price gains).
    • Risk: how far that return could vary, or be lost.
  • Cash and current account deposits: most liquid, little or no return, low risk.
  • Bonds: less liquid, fixed interest, prices can change.
  • Shares: higher expected return, more risk.
  • In AP terms: cash and demand deposits (checking accounts) are the most liquid assets; stocks are shares.
  • Opportunity cost of holding money = the interest forgone by not holding bonds or savings instead.
    • When interest rates rise, holding money costs more, so people hold less of it.
Mission 2

How bonds work

  • A bond has:
    • Face value: what is repaid at the end, e.g. 1,000.
    • Coupon: the fixed yearly interest, e.g. 5% of 1,000 = 50 a year.
    • Maturity: the date it is repaid, e.g. in 10 years.
  • After it is sold, a bond can be traded. Its market price can change; its coupon cannot.
  • Suppose new bonds now pay more interest.
    • The old bond still pays only 50 a year.
    • No one will pay 1,000 for it when a new 1,000 bond pays more.
    • Its price must fall until it offers a similar return.
  • Bond prices and market interest rates move in opposite directions.
  • Say: "bond prices and interest rates are inversely related".
Mission 2

Calculating the yield

  • Yield = coupon ÷ market price × 100.
  • Worked example (illustrative data): a bond pays a fixed 50 a year.
    • Bought at 1,000: yield = 50 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = 5%.
    • New bonds pay 6.25%. The old bond's price falls until its yield matches.
    • 50 ÷ price = 0.0625, so price = 800.
    • Check: 50 ÷ 800 × 100 = 6.25%.
  • Simplified: this ignores the repayment at maturity, which would hold the price a little higher.
  • Price falls → yield rises. Price rises → yield falls.
  • Say: "the coupon is fixed; the yield changes with the price" — not: "the interest on the bond went up".

Interest rates and bond prices

Illustrative data. An existing bond pays a fixed 50 a year (simplified: no repayment date).

  • Takeaway: when market rates rise, existing bonds fall in price; when rates fall, they rise.

?A pension fund owns an older government bond that pays a fixed 40 a year. It bought the bond for 1,000. Market interest rates then fall, and new government bonds offer only 2%. What happens to the market price of the older bond?

Make a guess: If your savings grow every year, how can you still end up able to buy less?

Mission 3

Nominal and real

  • You save 1,000 for one year at an interest rate of 5% (illustrative data).
  • After a year you have 1,050. That 5% is the nominal interest rate: the rate in money terms, not adjusted for inflation.
  • Over the same year, inflation is 3%. A basket that cost 1,000 now costs 1,030.
  • 1,050 ÷ 1,030 = 1.019. You can buy only about 2% more than a year ago.
  • That 2% is the real interest rate: the rise in what your savings can buy.
  • Say: "the real interest rate fell" — not: "interest rates fell", when the nominal rate stayed the same and only inflation rose.
Mission 3

The Fisher relation

  • Real interest rate ≈ nominal interest rate − inflation rate.
  • Named after the economist Irving Fisher.
  • Example: nominal 5%, inflation 3% → real ≈ 5 − 3 = 2%.
  • If inflation is higher than the nominal rate, the real rate is negative: savings buy less each year.
  • The exact answer here is 1.94%, not 2%. The subtraction is close when rates are low, and it is the form you use in calculations.
  • AP: nominal interest rate = real interest rate + expected inflation rate. Rearranged: real rate = nominal rate − inflation.
  • Say: "real interest rate = nominal rate minus inflation" — not: "nominal rate minus the interest".
Quick check
?A bank pays a nominal interest rate of 6% on savings. Inflation over the year is 8%. What is the real interest rate?
Mission 3

When real rates go negative

  • Real-world data, euro area, October 2022:
  • The European Central Bank's deposit rate was 0.75%. Euro area inflation was 10.6%.
  • Real rate on that deposit ≈ 0.75 − 10.6 = −9.85%.
  • US, June 2022: the Fed's target for the federal funds rate was at most 1.75%. CPI inflation was 9.1%. Real rate ≈ 1.75 − 9.1 = −7.35%.
  • Nominal rates had started to rise, but real rates were still deeply negative.
  • Borrowers care about the real rate too: a negative real rate means a debt shrinks in real terms.
  • So a rise in the nominal rate is not always "tighter" money: it depends on what happens to inflation.
Mission 3

Expected and actual inflation

  • Lenders set the nominal rate before they know the inflation rate.
  • A lender wants a real return of 3% and expects inflation of 2%. It charges 3 + 2 = 5% nominal (illustrative data).
  • Inflation turns out to be 6%.
  • Real rate actually earned = 5 − 6 = −1%, not the 3% planned.
  • Unexpected inflation helps borrowers and hurts lenders: the money repaid buys less than both expected.
  • Unexpectedly low inflation does the reverse.
  • AP: the ex ante (expected) real rate = nominal rate − expected inflation. The ex post real rate = nominal rate − actual inflation.
Quick check
?A firm borrows at a fixed nominal rate of 7% for one year. When the loan is agreed, both sides expect inflation of 3%. Actual inflation turns out to be 1%. Which statement is correct?
Mission check

?A central bank raises its nominal policy rate from 3% to 5%. Over the same period, inflation rises from 2% to 7%. What happened to the real policy rate?

A higher nominal rate can still mean a lower real rate, if inflation rises by more.

Mission 4

Mission 4: If money has a price, what makes that price go up or down?

Make a guess: If money has a price, what makes that price go up or down?

Mission 4

Holding money has a cost

  • You can keep your wealth as money (cash, current accounts) or as interest-bearing assets such as bonds.
  • Money is the most liquid asset: you can spend it at once.
  • But money pays little or no interest.
  • So the opportunity cost of holding money is the interest you give up.
  • The higher the nominal interest rate, the more it costs to hold money.
  • AP: the opportunity cost of holding money is the nominal interest rate forgone on bonds.
Mission 4

The demand for money

  • At a high nominal interest rate, people hold less money and more bonds.
  • At a low rate, holding money costs little, so they hold more.
  • So money demand (MD) slopes down against the nominal interest rate.
  • A change in the interest rate → a movement along MD.
  • MD shifts right when people need more money for spending: a higher price level or higher real output (more transactions).
Mission 4

Money supply and equilibrium

  • The money supply (MS) is set by the central bank. It does not depend on the interest rate.
  • So MS is a vertical line.
  • AP: the money supply is vertical at a quantity set by the Fed, given the monetary base.
  • Equilibrium: the nominal interest rate where the quantity of money demanded = the money supply.
  • Illustrative data: MS = 600 (billion); MD gives an equilibrium rate of 4%.
  • Say: "money demand shifted right" — not: "money supply increased", when people simply want to hold more money.
Mission 4

Getting back to equilibrium

  • Bond prices and interest rates move in opposite directions (see Mission 2).
  • Step 1: rate 5%, above equilibrium. People want to hold only 500 of the 600: a surplus of money of 100.
  • They use spare money to buy bonds → bond prices rise → interest rates fall back to 4%.
  • Step 2: rate below equilibrium → a shortage of money.
  • People sell bonds to get money → bond prices fall → interest rates rise.
  • The nominal rate adjusts; the money supply stays where the central bank put it.
Quick check
?In the money market, the nominal interest rate is below its equilibrium level. What happens next?
Mission 4Graph lab

Prices up, rate?

  • Takeaway: a higher price level raises money demand, so with a fixed money supply the nominal rate rises.
Mission 4

The central bank moves the money supply

  • To raise the money supply, the central bank buys government bonds (open market operations or quantitative easing). It pays with new money.
  • MS shifts right: MS1 → MS2. The nominal rate falls (illustrative data: 600 → 700, rate 4% → 3%).
  • To reduce the money supply, it sells bonds: MS shifts left, the rate rises.
  • AP: the Fed's tools in this model are open market operations, the discount rate and the required reserve ratio. Buying bonds increases the money supply.
  • Say: "an increase in the money supply lowers the nominal interest rate" — not: "lower interest rates increase the money supply" (wrong direction of cause).
Quick check
?Real output grows strongly, so people make more transactions. The central bank leaves the money supply unchanged. What happens in the money market?
Mission 4

From the money market to AD

  • Step 1: the central bank buys bonds. MS shifts right; the nominal rate falls.
  • Step 2: borrowing is cheaper, so investment and interest-sensitive consumption rise.
  • Step 3: AD shifts right: real output and the price level rise.
  • AP calculation (illustrative data): the rate falls and investment rises by $20 billion. MPC = 0.75, so the spending multiplier = 1 ÷ 0.25 = 4.
  • AD shifts right by up to 4 × 20 = $80 billion.
  • AP: draw the money market (nominal interest rate) and AD-AS side by side; the reserve market is in a later lesson.
Quick check
?To cut inflation, the central bank sells government bonds. Which chain is correct?
Mission 4

The Fed today

  • The Fed now runs an ample-reserves system: banks hold plenty of reserves.
  • It steers the federal funds rate mainly with administered rates, such as interest on reserve balances, not by small changes in the quantity of reserves.
  • The money market graph still shows the key idea: a lower policy rate means a lower nominal interest rate, and more interest-sensitive spending.
  • Reserve market and limited vs ample reserves.
Mission check

?After a wave of online payment fraud, people decide to hold more cash. Money demand rises by 50 billion at every interest rate. The central bank wants to keep the nominal interest rate unchanged. What should it do?

To hold the rate steady, a central bank must supply the extra money people want to hold.

Mission 5: When a bank lends you money, whose money are you actually borrowing?

Make a guess: When a bank lends someone 10,000, where does that money come from?

Mission 5

What banks do

  • Commercial (retail) banks:
    • Accept deposits and keep them safe.
    • Make loans to households and firms.
    • Run payments: cards, transfers.
  • They channel savings into investment: firms borrow to build and expand.
  • Borrowers pay interest: the price of credit. Banks charge more for riskier loans.
  • Collateral: an asset the bank can take if a loan is not repaid (e.g. a house for a mortgage).
Mission 5

A bank's balance sheet

  • Liabilities: what the bank owes.
    • Customers' deposits (the bank owes them on demand or at a set date).
    • Borrowing from other banks.
  • Assets: what the bank owns or is owed.
    • Reserves: cash and deposits at the central bank. Most liquid, earn least.
    • Liquid assets: e.g. short-term government bills.
    • Loans to customers. Least liquid, earn most.
    • Investments such as bonds.
  • Capital (equity) = assets − liabilities. It absorbs losses.
  • AP T-account: assets = required reserves + excess reserves + loans + securities; liabilities = demand deposits + owner's equity.
Mission 5

Loans create deposits

  • When a bank makes a loan, it credits the borrower's account with a new deposit.
  • That deposit is new money: no one else's balance went down.
  • Example (illustrative): a bank lends 10,000 to a café owner.
    • Assets: loans +10,000.
    • Liabilities: deposits +10,000.
    • Broad money rises by 10,000 at once.
  • When loans are repaid, deposits fall: money is destroyed.
  • What limits lending? Demand for loans, the risk of non-payment, the interest rate set by the central bank, and capital and liquidity rules.
  • Say: "banks create deposits when they lend" — not: "banks just lend out savers' money".
Mission 5

The money multiplier model

  • A textbook model of how deposits spread through the banking system.
  • Fractional reserve banking: banks keep only a fraction of deposits as reserves and lend the rest.
  • Worked example (illustrative): reserve ratio 10%; a new cash deposit of 1,000.
    • Round 1: keep 100; lend 900. The 900 is spent and deposited in another bank.
    • Round 2: keep 90; lend 810.
    • Round 3: keep 81; lend 729 … and so on.
  • The money multiplier is the ratio of the money supply to the monetary base. Its maximum value = 1 ÷ required reserve ratio = 10.
Mission 5Graph lab

Rounds of lending

Illustrative data. A new deposit of 1,000 enters the banking system.

  • Takeaway: a lower reserve ratio means more of each deposit is lent on, so deposits grow further.
Mission 5

Required reserves, excess reserves and where deposits come from

  • Required reserves = required reserve ratio × demand deposits.
  • Excess reserves = actual reserves − required reserves. Only excess reserves can be lent.
  • Illustrative data: required reserve ratio 10%; money multiplier 10.
  • Case 1: a person deposits $1,000 of cash from under their mattress.
    • Required reserves rise by $100; excess reserves by $900.
    • Maximum new loans = $900 × 10 = $9,000.
    • Maximum change in demand deposits = $10,000.
    • Maximum change in the money supply = $9,000: the $1,000 was already money (currency), it just changed form.
  • Case 2: the Fed buys $1,000 of bonds from a bank.
    • Excess reserves rise by $1,000.
    • Maximum change in the money supply = $1,000 × 10 = $10,000.
Quick check
?The required reserve ratio is 20%. A person deposits $1,000 of cash into a checking account. Assuming banks lend all excess reserves and no cash is held back, what is the maximum change in the money supply?
Mission 5

Why the multiplier overstates

  • The simple multiplier assumes banks lend every penny of excess reserves and every loan comes back as a deposit.
  • In reality:
    • Banks may keep excess reserves, e.g. when they fear losses.
    • People and firms hold some cash instead of deposits.
    • Lending depends on demand for loans and on capital rules.
  • Real world:
    • The Fed's required reserve ratio has been 0% since March 2020.
    • Central banks today mostly set interest rates and supply the reserves banks need.
  • So the multiplier is a maximum, not a forecast.
Quick check
?Two economies both have a reserve ratio of 10%. In each, the central bank buys government bonds from banks, adding 1,000 to the banks' reserves. In Economy A, banks lend all their excess reserves, and every loan comes back as a deposit. In Economy B, banks keep some extra reserves, and people keep part of their loans as cash. Using the multiplier model, which is correct?
Mission check

?A bank holds 1,000,000 of its customers' savings deposits. It then agrees a new loan of 50,000 to a firm and credits the firm's account. What happens at once to the savers' balances and to the money supply?

A bank loan is not someone else's savings passed on. It is a new deposit, so it is new money.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Money is anything generally accepted as payment. It removes the double coincidence of wants.
  • Functions: medium of exchange, unit of account, store of value.
  • M1 = currency + checkable deposits; M2 = M1 + savings, small time deposits and money market funds; monetary base = currency + reserves.

Mission 2

  • Financial markets link savers and borrowers; money markets are short-term, capital markets long-term.
  • Debt (loans, bonds) must be repaid with interest; equity (shares) gives ownership and uncertain dividends.
  • Bond prices and market interest rates move in opposite directions.
  • Yield = coupon ÷ market price × 100.

Mission 3

  • Real interest rate ≈ nominal interest rate − inflation; it can be negative.
  • Unexpected inflation helps borrowers and hurts lenders.
  • Nominal rate = expected real rate + expected inflation; ex post real rate = nominal − actual inflation.

Mission 4

  • The opportunity cost of holding money is the interest given up, so money demand slopes down.
  • Money supply is vertical, set by the central bank; the nominal interest rate is where they meet.
  • MD shifts with the price level and real output; MS shifts with central bank action.
  • A lower rate raises investment and consumption, so AD shifts right.

Mission 5

  • Banks create money when they lend: a loan creates a matching deposit.
  • The multiplier model: maximum deposits = new reserves × 1 ÷ reserve ratio. It overstates because of idle reserves and cash leakages.
