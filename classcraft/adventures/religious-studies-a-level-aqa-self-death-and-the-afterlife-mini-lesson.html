Mission 1

Mission 1: Are you a body that thinks, or a mind that happens to have a body?

Make a guess: Are you a body that thinks, or a mind that happens to have a body?

Mission 1

Three words, one problem

  • Body: the physical, living organism.
  • Mind: thinking, feeling, choosing, being conscious.
  • Soul: in religion and in Greek thought (psyche), the principle of life and of the self; in many traditions, what may survive death.
  • The mind–body problem: how does what thinks and feels relate to what is physical?
  • Dualism: mind (or soul) and body are two different kinds of thing.
  • Monism: there is only one kind of thing (more in Mission 2).
Mission 1

Plato: the soul is the real you

  • For Plato the soul is the essential self: immaterial, rational and immortal.
  • It is only temporarily united with the body, which distracts it with desires and senses: in the Phaedo the body is like a prison for the soul.
  • The soul existed before birth (it knew the Forms: recollection) and lives after death.
  • The Forms: for Plato, the perfect, unchanging realities known by reason, not the senses. Recollection: learning is remembering what the soul knew of them before birth.
Mission 1Read the source

Plato's Phaedo

Plato, Phaedo 80a–b (Jowett translation, 1871). Socrates sums up:

> "the soul is in the very likeness of the divine, and immortal, and intellectual, and uniform, and indissoluble, and unchangeable; and that the body is in the very likeness of the human, and mortal, and unintellectual, and multiform, and dissoluble, and changeable."

Q1 What kind of reality does the soul resemble?

Show answer

The world of the Forms: unchanging, divine, known by intellect.

Q2 Why does "indissoluble" matter for life after death?

Show answer

What has no parts cannot break up, so it cannot be destroyed when the body dies.

Mission 1

Aristotle: the soul is the form of the body

  • De Anima (On the Soul) II.1: the soul is the form of a living body: what makes it alive and the kind of thing it is.
  • His image (paraphrase): if an eye were a living creature, sight would be its soul.
  • The soul is what the body does: its capacities to live, sense and think.
  • Three levels: nutritive soul (plants: growth, nourishment), sensitive soul (animals: perception, movement), rational soul (humans: thought).
  • So the soul cannot be separated from the body, as the shape of a wax seal cannot be separated from the wax (II.1).
  • One possible exception: the active intellect (De Anima III.5), which he calls separable and immortal. Scholars debate what he meant.

Mission 1

Descartes: two substances

  • René Descartes (1596–1650), Meditations on First Philosophy (1641), Principles of Philosophy (1644).
  • Substance dualism: mind and body are distinct substances, each able to exist without the other.
  • Principles I.53: each substance has one principal attribute. Mind: thought (res cogitans, thinking thing). Body: extension in space (res extensa, extended thing).
  • The mind has no size, shape or location; the body cannot think.
  • For Descartes "mind" and "soul" are the same thing; it is the real I.
Mission 1

Descartes' arguments for the soul

  • 1. From doubt (Meditation II; Discourse on Method IV, 1637): I can doubt that my body exists (I might be dreaming or deceived). I cannot doubt that I exist while I think. So I am a thinking thing, distinct from the body.
  • 2. Clear and distinct conception (Meditation VI): I can clearly conceive my mind without my body; God can make real whatever I clearly conceive; so mind and body can exist apart.
  • 3. Divisibility (Meditation VI): the body has parts; the mind does not.
  • Doubt and divisibility use one principle: if two things differ in even one property, they are not identical. Conceivability relies instead on what God can make real.
Mission 1Read the source

The divisibility argument

Descartes, Meditation VI (Molyneux translation, 1680, spelling as printed):

> "my Body is of its Nature divisible, but my Mind indivisible; for while I consider my Mind or my self, as I am only a thinking Thing, I can distinguish no parts in Me"

He adds that losing a foot or an arm takes no part of the mind away.

Q1 What property does the body have that the mind lacks?

Show answer

Divisibility: it has parts.

Q2 Why does that show they are distinct?

Show answer

If they were one thing, they would share every property.

Mission 1

How do soul and body relate?

  • Plato: the soul uses and rules the body for a time, then leaves it.
  • Aristotle: soul and body are one living thing, as shape and wax are one seal.
  • Descartes: two substances that interact: the body sends sensations to the mind; the mind moves the body.
  • He placed the meeting point in the pineal gland in the brain (Passions of the Soul, 1649).
  • Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia (1618–1680), letter of 1643: how can an unextended soul, with no size or location, push a physical body?
  • This interaction problem is the main challenge to substance dualism (materialist answers in Mission 2).
Mission 1Diversity

Christian views of body and soul

  • Hebrew Bible: many scholars read it as seeing a person as a unity: "The LORD God formed man from the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul." (Genesis 2:7)
  • Platonic strand: Augustine and many later Christians: an immortal soul that survives death (Matthew 10:28).
  • Aristotelian strand: Aquinas (1225–1274): the soul is the form of the body (Summa Theologiae I.76.1), yet can survive death until the resurrection. The Catholic Church holds this today (Catechism 365).
  • Cartesian strand: many popular and some Protestant views of the soul as a separate mind.
  • Christian physicalists: some modern Christian thinkers hold that a person is a physical being and rely on resurrection, not a separate soul (see Mission 3).
Mission 1Evaluate

Does Descartes prove the soul exists?

  • Claim: Descartes' arguments show that the mind is a non-physical substance.
  • For: Thought seems private and unified in a way no physical thing is.
  • For: We can coherently imagine existing without a body (out-of-body stories, life after death).
  • Against: The masked man fallacy: I can doubt that the masked man is my father, yet he may still be my father. Doubting X but not Y does not show X ≠ Y.
  • Against: Antoine Arnauld (Fourth Objections, 1641): I can clearly conceive a right-angled triangle without knowing Pythagoras' theorem holds of it, but it still holds.
  • Against: Minds may be divisible after all: brain damage removes abilities; split-brain cases.
  • Against: The interaction problem.
  • Judgement criteria: Does "I can conceive it" show "it can exist"? Does the argument fit what neuroscience shows? Is dualism needed to explain consciousness?

Mission 2

Materialism

  • Materialism (physicalism): only physical things exist. Mind and consciousness can be fully explained by physical processes in the brain and body.
  • So there is no soul as a spiritual substance.
  • It is a form of monism: one kind of stuff.
  • Several materialist accounts: behaviourism, identity theory, and (on the borderline) epiphenomenalism.
  • Epiphenomenalism: mental events are real and caused by brain events, but cause nothing themselves.
Mission 2

Ryle: the ghost in the machine

  • Gilbert Ryle (1900–1976), The Concept of Mind (1949), chapter 1.
  • He calls Descartes' picture "the dogma of the Ghost in the Machine": a hidden mind steering a body.
  • Ryle says this rests on a category error (category mistake): treating the mind as a thing of the same type as the body, only invisible.
  • "Mind" is not an extra object: it is a way of talking about how a person thinks, acts and responds.
Mission 2Read the source

Where is the university?

Ryle, The Concept of Mind, chapter 1 (paraphrase):

  • A visitor is shown the colleges, libraries, sports fields, museums and offices of a university.
  • He then asks: "But where is the University?"
  • His mistake: the university is not another building beside those he has seen; it is the way they are organised.

Q1 What category does the visitor wrongly put "the university" in?

Show answer

The same category as the buildings: one more thing to visit.

Q2 What, for Ryle, is the parallel mistake about the mind?

Show answer

Looking for the mind as an extra thing alongside the body and its behaviour.

Mission 2

Philosophical behaviourism

  • Philosophical (logical) behaviourism: talk about the mind is really talk about behaviour and dispositions to behave.
  • "Anna is in pain" means she winces, cries out, rubs the injury, would take a painkiller if offered.
  • Ryle's image: "glass is brittle" means it would shatter if struck; it names no hidden inner quality.
  • Critique: a person can be in pain and hide it completely; an actor can show pain and feel none.
  • Critique: it ignores what pain feels like from the inside.
Mission 2

The identity thesis

  • Identity thesis (mind–brain identity theory): mental states are brain states. Pain just is a certain pattern of neurons firing.
  • U. T. Place, "Is Consciousness a Brain Process?" (1956); J. J. C. Smart, "Sensations and Brain Processes" (1959).
  • Like lightning = electrical discharge, or water = H₂O: one thing, discovered under two descriptions.
  • Critique (multiple realisability): Hilary Putnam (1926–2016) asked whether an octopus, or an alien, with a very different brain could not also feel pain. If so, pain is not one type of brain state.
  • Critique: a brain state has a location and size; does a thought?
Mission 2

Materialist critiques of dualism

  • Interaction: how does a non-physical soul move physical neurons? (Elisabeth's question.)
  • Dependence: brain injury, drugs, anaesthesia and dementia change memory, mood and personality. Mind seems to depend on brain.
  • Simplicity (Ockham's razor): if the brain explains everything, a soul is an unnecessary extra.
  • Evolution: humans developed gradually from other animals; when would a soul have been added?
  • Physics: the physical world seems causally closed; a soul acting on it would add energy from nowhere.
Mission 2

Dualist responses

  • Qualia: the felt quality of experience (the redness of red, the hurt of pain). Thomas Nagel, "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (1974): science describes from outside; experience is from inside.
  • Mary's room (Frank Jackson, 1982): a scientist who knows every physical fact about colour, but has seen only black and white, still learns something new when she first sees red.
  • The hard problem (David Chalmers, The Conscious Mind, 1996): explaining brain functions does not explain why they are felt.
  • Correlation is not identity: a damaged radio distorts music without making the music; a damaged brain may distort a mind it does not produce.
  • Richard Swinburne (b. 1934), The Evolution of the Soul (1986): defends substance dualism today.

Mission 2Evaluate

Can consciousness be fully explained physically?

  • Claim: Materialism can explain everything about the mind.
  • For: Neuroscience keeps mapping mental functions to brain activity.
  • For: Simpler; no interaction problem; fits evolution.
  • For: Ryle: dualism rests on a category error.
  • Against: Qualia and the hard problem: function is explained, feeling is not.
  • Against: Correlation shows dependence, not identity.
  • Against: Each materialist account has its own problems (hidden pain; multiple realisability; the idle mind of epiphenomenalism).
  • Judgement criteria: What counts as "explaining" consciousness? Is a simpler theory more likely true? Does the theory leave room for meaning, free choice or life after death, and should that matter?
Mission check

?A computer scientist builds a robot that says "ouch" and pulls away when its arm is damaged. She says: "It has pain, since that's all pain is: reacting this way." A critic replies: "It's all behaviour; nothing is felt." Which debate is this?

Materialists explain the mind through body and brain; dualists ask whether that leaves out what it is like to be you. Next: can a person survive death? (see Mission 3)

Mission 3

What makes you, you?

  • Personal identity: what makes a person at one time the same person as someone at another time?
  • Body (or brain): the same living organism, continuous in space and time.
  • Mind / memory: John Locke (1632–1704), Essay concerning Human Understanding (2nd edn, 1694), II.27: the same person is the same consciousness, stretching back by memory.
  • Locke's case (paraphrase): if a prince's consciousness entered a cobbler's body, the person would be the prince.
  • Soul: the same non-physical self, whatever happens to the body.
Mission 3

Why identity matters for life after death

  • Life after death is only my survival if the one who lives on is me, not just someone like me.
  • Each view of the afterlife needs something that carries identity across death.
  • Immortality of the soul: the soul carries it.
  • Resurrection: God raises the same person, body and all.
  • Replica: God re-creates the whole person (Hick).
  • Rebirth and reincarnation.
Mission 3

Immortality of the soul

  • The belief that the soul is non-physical and spiritual, and continues to exist after the death of the body.
  • Plato (Phaedo): the soul is simple, indissoluble and akin to the Forms, so it cannot die.
  • Descartes: the mind is a separate substance, so the body's death need not end it.
  • Many Christians hold that the soul goes to God at death: "today you will be with me in Paradise" (Luke 23:43).
  • Identity carrier: the soul itself.
  • Problem: can a soul without body, brain or senses think, remember or recognise anyone? Is a bodiless soul a whole person?
Mission 3

Resurrection

  • Resurrection: the belief that God will restore the dead in bodily form to eternal life.
  • Christianity: "I believe in … the resurrection of the body" (Apostles' Creed); Jesus' resurrection is the model.
  • Judaism: resurrection of the dead is one of Maimonides' Thirteen Principles; "many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake" (Daniel 12:2, JPS 1917).
  • Islam: God will raise the dead on the Day of Judgement: "Thinketh man that We shall not assemble his bones?" (Qur'an 75:3, Pickthall).
  • Identity carrier: God, who raises this person.
  • Problem: the body decays and its atoms become part of other things. What makes the raised body mine?
Mission 3Read the source

Paul on the resurrection body

Paul, 1 Corinthians 15:42–44 (WEBBE):

> "So also is the resurrection of the dead. The body is sown perishable; it is raised imperishable. It is sown in dishonour; it is raised in glory. It is sown in weakness; it is raised in power. It is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body."

  • Paul uses the image of a seed (15:37): what is sown is not the same as the plant, yet the plant comes from it.

Q1 Is the raised body the same physical body?

Show answer

Not simply: it is transformed, "imperishable", yet continuous with the one that died, like seed and plant.

Q2 Does a "spiritual body" mean no body at all?

Show answer

No: Paul still calls it a body; many Christians read "spiritual" as fully given life by God's Spirit.

Mission 3Diversity

Christians on soul and resurrection

  • Catholic and Orthodox: at death the soul is judged and lives on (heaven, purgatory or hell in Catholic teaching); at the end, the resurrection of the body reunites soul and body.
  • Aquinas: the separated soul is not the full person; we need the body to be whole.
  • Some Protestants ("soul sleep" or conditional immortality, e.g. Seventh-day Adventists): the dead are unconscious until the resurrection; no separate immortal soul.
  • Some liberal Christians: resurrection language expresses hope and God's faithfulness, not a literal bodily event.
Mission 3

Hick's replica theory

  • John Hick (1922–2012), Philosophy of Religion (1963) and Death and Eternal Life (1976).
  • Hick sees a person as a psycho-physical unity: no separable soul.
  • Stage 1: John Smith vanishes in the USA; at the same moment an exact replica, with his memories and character, appears in India. Hick: we would say it is John Smith.
  • Stage 2: John Smith dies in the USA, and the replica appears in India. Still John Smith, Hick argues.
  • Stage 3: John Smith dies, and God creates the replica in a resurrection world, a different space. It is John Smith.
  • So resurrection is possible without a soul: God re-creates the whole person. Hick links this to Paul's "spiritual body".
Quick check
?In Hick's replica theory, what makes the replica the same person as John Smith?
Mission 3Evaluate

Is the replica really you?

  • Claim: A replica created by God after death would be the same person.
  • For: We already judge identity by likeness and memory: Smith's friends would accept him.
  • For: Needs no soul, so fits materialist views of the person (see Mission 2), and fits belief in bodily resurrection.
  • Against: Duplication: God could make two replicas. Both cannot be Smith, so neither may be.
  • Against: A perfect copy is still a copy: if Smith is alive when the replica appears, there are two people.
  • Against: Without continuity of matter, place or soul, nothing links Smith to the replica except likeness.
  • Judgement criteria: Is likeness enough for identity? Does it matter if God guarantees there is only one? What would you need to survive to say "I survived"?
Mission check

Every view of survival must say what carries "you" across death: a soul, God raising the body, or an exact re-creation. Next: rebirth, reincarnation and the evidence debate (see Mission 4).

Mission 4

Reincarnation: the soul moves on

  • Reincarnation (transmigration of souls): an eternal soul passes from one body to another, life after life.
  • In Hinduism the soul is the atman, the true, unchanging self.
  • The cycle of birth, death and rebirth is samsara.
  • Karma: actions have consequences that shape future lives; good actions lead to better births.
  • The goal is moksha: release from samsara. For Advaita Vedanta, realising that atman is one with Brahman; for devotional traditions, eternal union with God.
  • Sikhs and Jains also hold beliefs in rebirth and karma, with their own goals.
Mission 4Read the source

The Bhagavad Gita

Bhagavad Gita 2.22 (Edwin Arnold translation, The Song Celestial, 1885):

> "Nay, but as when one layeth / His worn-out robes away, / And taking new ones, sayeth, / 'These will I wear to-day!' / So putteth by the spirit / Lightly its garb of flesh, / And passeth to inherit / A residence afresh."

Q1 In the image, what stays the same and what changes?

Show answer

The spirit, the atman, stays; the body, like clothing, is changed.

Q2 What does this suggest carries personal identity in reincarnation?

Show answer

The atman, not the body or memory.

Mission 4

Rebirth: no unchanging self

  • Buddhism teaches anatta (Sanskrit anatman): there is no unchanging soul or self.
  • A person is five changing khandhas (aggregates): body, feelings, perceptions, mental formations, consciousness.
  • "'All forms are unreal,' he who knows and sees this becomes passive in pain" (Dhammapada 279, Müller translation, 1881). Here "unreal" renders anatta, "not-self".
  • So what is reborn? A causal stream: the energy of karma (kamma, intentional action) shapes a new life.
  • The Questions of King Milinda (paraphrase): rebirth is like lighting one lamp from another. The new flame is neither the same nor another.
  • The goal is nibbana (nirvana): the end of craving and of rebirth.
  • Traditions differ: many Theravada teachers hold rebirth is immediate; Tibetan Buddhism teaches an intermediate state (bardo).
Quick check
?What is the key difference between Hindu reincarnation and Buddhist rebirth?
Mission 4

Who is reborn? The identity problem

  • Most people remember no past lives. If memory makes identity (Locke), the past person was not me.
  • Hindu reply: identity rests on the atman, not memory. The same self lives each life, as one actor plays many roles.
  • Buddhist reply: there is no fixed self to be "the same". Lives are linked by cause, like flames passed on. "Neither the same nor another."
  • Critique: if nothing carries over except karmic effects, is it me who is reborn, or someone affected by me?
  • Critique: why should I care about, or deserve, the results of actions I cannot remember?
Mission 4Evaluate

Are NDEs evidence of life after death?

  • A near-death experience (NDE) is reported by some people who come close to death, e.g. in cardiac arrest: peace, leaving the body, a bright light.
  • Claim: Near-death experiences show that the self can exist apart from the body.
  • For: Many, similar reports from people of different ages and cultures.
  • For: Some report accurate details from while they were unconscious.
  • For: Life-changing effects: many lose the fear of death.
  • Against: "Near" death is not death: the brain may still be active. Not one has returned from lasting death.
  • Against: Brain-based explanations: lack of oxygen, REM intrusion, drugs such as ketamine, temporal lobe activity (Susan Blackmore, Dying to Live, 1993: the "dying brain" hypothesis).
  • Against: Content varies with culture and religion; verifiable cases are few and hard to check.
  • Judgement criteria: What would count as evidence for life after death? Do brain explanations account for all features? Is testimony reliable here?
Quick check
?Ravi, a Hindu, says: "I may not remember my past lives, but I am the same self that lived them." What does he think carries his identity?
Mission check

?A river is never the same water from one day to the next, yet we call it the same river because each day's flow comes from the last. Which account of life after death does this picture best fit?

Reincarnation keeps an eternal self; rebirth keeps only a stream of causes. Whether either, or any afterlife, can be shown by evidence is still debated.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Plato: the soul is the immortal, immaterial true self, temporarily joined to the body.
  • Aristotle: the soul is the form of the body, its capacities to live, sense and think; not separable from it.
  • Descartes: mind and body are distinct substances (thought and extension); arguments from doubt, conceivability and divisibility; the interaction problem and the masked man fallacy are key criticisms.
  • Christians differ: a soul that survives death, a soul as the form of the body, or a physical person raised by God.

Mission 2

  • Materialism: the mind is fully explained physically; there is no soul as a spiritual substance. Ryle calls dualism a category error.
  • Behaviourism: mind = dispositions to behave. Identity thesis: mental states = brain states.
  • Critiques of dualism (interaction, dependence, simplicity) meet dualist replies (qualia, the hard problem, correlation is not identity).

Mission 3

  • Personal identity: the same body, the same consciousness and memory (Locke), or the same soul.
  • Immortality of the soul: a non-physical self lives on; resurrection: God restores the person in bodily form (1 Cor 15:42–44: a transformed "spiritual body").
  • Hick's replica theory: a person is a psycho-physical unity; God re-creates an exact replica in a resurrection world. Key objection: duplication.

Mission 4

  • Reincarnation: the atman passes from body to body (Gita 2.22); karma shapes each birth; goal moksha.
  • Rebirth: no unchanging self (anatta); a causal stream shaped by karma continues, "neither the same nor another"; goal nibbana.
