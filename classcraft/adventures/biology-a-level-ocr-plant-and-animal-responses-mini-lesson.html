Mission 1

A team of plant hormones

  • Plants use several hormones, not just auxin. They often act together.
  • Auxins: cell elongation.
  • Cytokinins: cell division.
  • Gibberellins (GA): stem elongation; seed germination.
  • Abscisic acid (ABA): stomatal closure; seed dormancy.
  • Ethene: fruit ripening; leaf fall.
Mission 1

Gibberellins make longer stems

  • Internode: the stretch of stem between two nodes (where leaves attach).
  • Gibberellins promote elongation of internodes: cells elongate (and divide).
  • Evidence 1: a fungus that makes GA causes "foolish seedling" disease: infected rice grows abnormally tall (Kurosawa, 1926).
  • Evidence 2: many dwarf varieties make little active GA. Spray them with GA → they grow tall.
  • Evidence 3: Mendel's dwarf peas carry the le allele. Its enzyme, which makes active GA, barely works.
  • Evidence 4: drugs that block GA synthesis → short plants. Add GA → normal height again.
Quick check
?Dwarf pea plants sprayed with gibberellin grew as tall as untreated tall plants. Tall plants sprayed with gibberellin grew only a little taller than untreated tall plants (illustrative data). Which conclusion do these results support?
Mission 1

Gibberellin wakes the seed

  • Cereal grain (e.g. barley): embryo + starch-filled endosperm, wrapped in an aleurone layer.
  • Seed absorbs water → embryo releases gibberellin.
  • GA diffuses to the aleurone layer → switches on transcription of the amylase gene.
  • Amylase hydrolyses starch → maltose → glucose.
  • Glucose → embryo: respiration (ATP) and building new cells.
  • Evidence: barley grains with the embryo removed make no amylase. Add GA → amylase is made (Paleg, 1960; Yomo, 1960).
  • Evidence: mutant seeds that cannot make GA do not germinate unless GA is supplied.
Mission 1Practical

Gibberellin and stem growth (OCR PAG11)

  • Dwarf pea seedlings, same variety and age.
  • Serial dilution of GA (e.g. 10× steps); control: water with the same wetting agent.
  • Spray equal volumes on each plant.
  • Same light, temperature and watering for all groups.
  • Measure height (or internode length) every few days for 2–3 weeks.
  • Calculate the mean and standard deviation for each concentration.
Quick check
?Barley grains were cut in half and placed on starch agar. Halves with the embryo produced clear zones. Halves without the embryo produced none, unless gibberellin had been added to the agar. What do the results show?
Mission 1

Closing stomata, dropping leaves

  • Drought → ABA made (in roots and leaves) → carried to leaves.
  • ABA binds receptors on guard cells → K⁺ ions leave.
  • Guard-cell water potential rises → water leaves by osmosis → guard cells lose turgor → stoma closes. Less water lost.
  • Leaf loss (deciduous trees, autumn): shorter days → leaf makes less auxin.
  • The abscission zone at the leaf base becomes sensitive to ethene → enzymes (e.g. cellulase) digest its cell walls → leaf falls.
  • A protective layer of cells seals the scar.
  • Say: "water potential of the guard cells rises" — not: "the guard cells run out of water".
Mission 1

Responses to stress and to herbivores

  • Abiotic stress (non-living): drought → ABA, stomata close; cold and short days → leaf loss.
  • Herbivory defences:
  • Tannins: bitter; bind proteins, so leaves are hard to digest.
  • Alkaloids (e.g. nicotine, caffeine): bitter and toxic.
  • Pheromones (OCR's term; strictly, airborne signal chemicals): maize attacked by caterpillars releases chemicals that attract parasitic wasps.
  • Folding: Mimosa pudica leaflets fold within seconds of touch; cells at the leaflet bases lose turgor.
  • Say: "Mimosa folding is a rapid turgor response" — not: "a tropism" (no directional growth).
Mission 1

Ethene ripens fruit, and fruit makes more ethene

  • Ethene is a gas, made by ripening fruit.
  • It triggers ripening: starch → sugars, cell walls softened, colour changes.
  • Ethene also triggers leaf abscission.
Mission 1

Hormones at work in farming

  • Ripening control: fruit (e.g. bananas) picked unripe; shipped cool and ventilated (ethene removed).
  • At the destination: ethene gas in ripening rooms → ripe fruit on demand.
  • Rooting powders: auxin on cut stems → roots form on cuttings.
  • Weedkillers: synthetic auxins (e.g. 2,4-D) → broad-leaved weeds grow fast and abnormally, then die.
  • Grasses and cereals are far less sensitive: the weedkiller is selective.
Quick check
?Unripe bananas are shipped in cool, well-ventilated holds. Why is the ventilation important?
Mission check

?To make malt, brewers soak barley grains in water so they start to germinate. Some add gibberellin to the water. Malting is complete when much of the grain's starch has been turned into sugar. Why does adding gibberellin speed up malting?

A plant's chemical signal can be borrowed: the same gibberellin that wakes a seed in soil drives a malting floor.

Mission 2

From stimulus to response

  • A stimulus is a change in the environment, inside or outside the body.
  • Receptor detects it → sensory neurone → CNS → motor neurone → effector responds.
  • CNS (central nervous system) = brain + spinal cord. It coordinates: it links input to output.
  • Effectors: muscles contract; glands secrete.
Mission 2

Two ways to divide the nervous system

  • Structural division:
    • CNS: brain and spinal cord.
    • PNS (peripheral nervous system): the nerves outside the CNS: cranial and spinal nerves.
  • Functional division of the PNS:
    • Somatic: motor neurones to skeletal muscle; mostly under conscious (voluntary) control.
    • Autonomic: motor neurones to cardiac muscle, smooth muscle and glands; involuntary.
  • The autonomic system has two branches, sympathetic and parasympathetic (see Mission 3).
Mission 2

The reflex arc

  • A reflex: a rapid, automatic response to a stimulus, with no conscious decision.
  • Frame 1 — Receptor detects the stimulus; sensory neurone carries impulses into the spinal cord.
  • Frame 2 — In the grey matter of the spinal cord, one relay neurone (interneuron) passes impulses on, across synapses.
  • Frame 3 — Motor neurone carries impulses out to the effector: a skeletal muscle, which contracts.
  • Three neurones, two synapses: a three-neurone simple reflex.
Quick check
?A person touches a hot pan and withdraws their hand before feeling any pain. Which sequence describes the reflex arc that produced the withdrawal?
Mission 2

Fast, automatic, protective

  • Fast: a short pathway with only two synapses.
  • Involuntary: the response is produced in the spinal cord. The brain does not decide.
  • Protective: the body part is removed before much damage is done.
  • The brain still finds out: other neurones carry impulses up the cord. Pain is felt after the movement.
  • Say: "the reflex is coordinated in the spinal cord" — not: "the brain decides very quickly".
Mission 2

Two reflexes to know

  • Knee jerk reflex:
    • Tap the tendon below the kneecap → the thigh muscle (quadriceps) is stretched.
    • Stretch receptors in the muscle → sensory neurone → spinal cord.
    • Sensory neurone synapses directly with the motor neurone: no relay neurone.
    • Quadriceps contracts → lower leg kicks.
    • Survival value: keeps the leg straight and the body balanced, with no conscious effort.
  • Blinking reflex:
    • Something touches the cornea → sensory neurone → relay neurone in the brain stem, not the spinal cord.
    • Motor neurones → eyelid muscles → both eyes close.
    • Survival value: protects the eye from damage.
Quick check

Mission check

?Months after an injury that cut through the spinal cord in the neck, a doctor pricks the sole of a patient's foot. The leg pulls the foot away, but the patient says they felt nothing. Which explanation is correct?

The movement and the feeling travel separate routes. The spinal cord moves you first; the brain finds out later.

Mission 3

Two ways to send a message

  • Nervous system:
    • Electrical impulses along neurones.
    • To specific cells. Fast (milliseconds). Short-lived.
  • Endocrine (hormonal) system:
    • Hormones secreted by glands into the blood.
    • Carried to every organ. Only target cells with matching receptors respond.
    • Slower to start. Longer-lasting.
  • The two often work together, e.g. fight or flight.
Quick check
?Which statement correctly distinguishes the roles of the nervous system and the endocrine system in sending messages between organs?
Mission 3

Five parts of the brain

  • Cerebrum: two cerebral hemispheres. Conscious thought, voluntary movement, interpreting senses, memory, learning, language.
  • Cerebellum: coordinates muscle contraction for smooth, accurate movement; balance and posture. Not under conscious control.
  • Medulla oblongata: controls involuntary processes: heart rate, breathing rate.
  • Hypothalamus: monitors the blood (e.g. temperature, water potential); controls the autonomic system and the pituitary gland.
  • Pituitary gland: secretes hormones that control other glands. Releases ADH (made in the hypothalamus).
Quick check
?Which statement describes the role of the cerebellum in the control of body movements?
Mission 3

The autonomic nervous system

  • Autonomic = involuntary. Motor neurones to cardiac muscle, smooth muscle and glands.
  • Two branches, usually acting on the same organs with opposite effects (antagonistic):

  • The balance between the two sets the level of activity.
  • Centres in the medulla and hypothalamus control both branches.
Mission 3

Fight or flight

  • Threat detected → brain (hypothalamus) → sympathetic neurones activated: a nervous response in milliseconds.
  • Sympathetic neurones also stimulate the adrenal glands to secrete adrenaline into the blood.
  • Adrenaline reaches every organ, extending and spreading the response:
    • Heart rate and stroke volume up.
    • Bronchioles widen; breathing deeper and faster.
    • Liver: glycogen → glucose; blood glucose up.
    • Blood diverted: arterioles to skeletal muscle widen; to gut and skin narrow.
    • Pupils dilate.
  • Result: more oxygen and glucose reach the muscles, ready for intense contraction.
  • Say: "adrenaline prepares the muscles; motor neurones make them contract" — not: "adrenaline makes the muscles contract".
Quick check
?A sprinter waits for the starting signal and epinephrine (adrenaline) is secreted into the blood. Which set of effects of epinephrine helps to facilitate intense contraction of the leg muscles during the race?
Mission 3

How adrenaline acts on a liver cell

  • Frame 1 — Adrenaline (the first messenger) binds to a specific receptor on the cell surface membrane. It does not enter the cell.
  • Frame 2 — The receptor activates (via a G protein) the enzyme adenylyl cyclase: ATP → cyclic AMP (cAMP).
  • Frame 3 — cAMP (the second messenger) activates protein kinases → an enzyme cascade → glycogen → glucose. Glucose leaves the cell into the blood.
  • Each step activates many molecules at the next: the signal is amplified.
  • Say: "cAMP is made inside the cell" — not: "adrenaline turns into cAMP".
Quick check
?A liver cell responds to adrenaline even though adrenaline never enters the cell. Which statement explains this?
Mission check

?After a heart transplant, the new heart has no nerve connections to the brain. During exercise its rate still rises, but more slowly than a normal heart's. Which explanation fits?

Nerves give speed; hormones give reach. Lose one route, and the other still works, more slowly.

Mission 4

From muscle to myofibril

  • Muscle → bundles → muscle fibres → myofibrils → sarcomeres.
  • A muscle fibre is very long and has many nuclei. It forms when many cells fuse.
  • Fibre membrane: sarcolemma. Fibre cytoplasm: sarcoplasm.
  • Inside each fibre:
    • Many myofibrils, packed side by side.
    • Many mitochondria: supply ATP.
    • Sarcoplasmic reticulum: stores calcium ions (Ca²⁺).
    • T-tubules: inward folds of the sarcolemma that carry impulses deep into the fibre.
Mission 4

The sarcomere

  • A sarcomere runs from one Z line to the next.
  • Thin actin filaments: attached to the Z lines.
  • Thick myosin filaments: in the middle, held at the M line.
  • Bands:
    • A band (dark): the full length of the myosin filaments, including overlap.
    • I band (light): actin only. Z line in its centre.
    • H zone: myosin only, in the centre of the A band.
  • Alternating dark and light bands: skeletal muscle is striated.
Mission 4

Sliding filaments

  • Frame 1 — Relaxed: little overlap; wide I band; wide H zone.
  • Frame 2 — Contracted: myosin heads pull actin towards the M line.
  • The filaments slide past each other. Neither gets shorter.
  • Z lines move closer → the sarcomere shortens → the whole muscle shortens.
  • I band narrows. H zone narrows, and may disappear. A band stays the same.
  • Say: "the filaments slide past each other" — not: "the filaments contract".
Quick check
?Electron micrographs of a sarcomere are taken before and after it contracts. Which set of changes is observed?
Mission 4

Read the data: overlap and force

  • Gordon, Huxley and Julian (1966) held single frog muscle fibres at fixed sarcomere lengths, stimulated them, and measured the force.
  • Maximum force: sarcomere length 2.0–2.25 µm.
  • Longer than 2.25 µm: force falls in a straight line, to zero at 3.65 µm.
  • Shorter than 2.0 µm: force also falls, as the filaments start to collide.
  • Why zero at 3.65 µm?
    • Myosin filament: 1.6 µm. Each actin filament (to the Z line): about 1.025 µm.
    • 1.6 + 1.025 + 1.025 = 3.65 µm: actin and myosin no longer overlap.
  • More overlap → more myosin heads reach actin → more force. Strong evidence for sliding filaments.
Quick check
?In the data on the earlier screen, a sarcomere held at 3.0 µm produces about 46% of the maximum force. Why is the force lower than at 2.2 µm?
Mission 4

The cross-bridge cycle

  • Frame 1 — An impulse spreads along the sarcolemma and down the T-tubules → the sarcoplasmic reticulum releases Ca²⁺ into the sarcoplasm.
  • Frame 2 — Ca²⁺ binds troponin; troponin changes shape and pulls tropomyosin off the binding sites on actin.
  • Frame 3 — A myosin head (carrying ADP + Pi) binds actin: a cross-bridge. The head pivots: the power stroke pulls actin towards the M line. ADP and Pi are released.
  • Frame 4 — A new ATP binds the head → the head detaches. ATPase on the head hydrolyses ATP → ADP + Pi; this re-cocks the head.
  • The cycle repeats while Ca²⁺ and ATP are present.
  • Relaxation: Ca²⁺ is actively transported back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum (uses ATP); binding sites are blocked again.
  • Say: "Ca²⁺ uncovers the binding sites; ATP detaches the head" — not: "Ca²⁺ powers the stroke".
Quick check
?A drug blocks the release of calcium ions from the sarcoplasmic reticulum of a skeletal muscle fibre. The motor neuron still stimulates the fibre normally. What is the effect on the fibre?
Mission check

?After death, cells stop making ATP. A few hours later the skeletal muscles become rigid and cannot be stretched (rigor mortis). Which explanation fits the sliding filament model?

The filaments slide; the heads must let go to slide again. No ATP, no letting go.

Mission 5

Three kinds of muscle

  • Skeletal muscle:
    • Striated. Long, unbranched fibres with many nuclei at the edge.
    • Voluntary: stimulated by motor neurones. Contracts fast; fatigues.
  • Cardiac muscle:
    • Striated. Short, branched cells, usually one central nucleus.
    • Joined end to end by intercalated discs: impulses pass from cell to cell.
    • Myogenic and involuntary. Does not fatigue.
  • Smooth (involuntary) muscle:
  • No striations. Spindle-shaped cells, one central nucleus.
  • Walls of gut, blood vessels, airways. Contracts slowly; sustained; autonomic control.
  • Why cardiac muscle does not fatigue: its cells are packed with mitochondria and have a dense capillary supply, so aerobic respiration keeps ATP coming.
Mission 5Practical

Identifying muscle under the microscope

  • PAG1 (microscopy): examine stained sections or photomicrographs of skeletal muscle.
  • Method:
    • Focus at low power first; then switch to high power and use the fine focus only.
    • Look for: striations; number and position of nuclei; branching; intercalated discs; cell shape.
    • Draw a few cells: clear single lines, no shading, labels, magnification stated.
    • Calibrate the eyepiece graticule against a stage micrometer for each objective lens.
  • Worked example (illustrative data), ×400:
    • Step 1: 100 µm on the stage micrometer = 40 eyepiece units → 1 unit = 100 ÷ 40 = 2.5 µm.
    • Step 2: a row of 10 sarcomeres (from one dark band to the 11th) spans 9 eyepiece units → 9 × 2.5 = 22.5 µm.
    • Step 3: one sarcomere = 22.5 ÷ 10 = 2.25 µm. Measure across many to reduce error.
Quick check
?A photomicrograph shows striated cells that branch. Each cell has one central nucleus, and dark lines cross the tissue where cells join end to end. Which tissue is it?
Mission 5

The neuromuscular junction

  • Where a motor neurone meets a muscle fibre.
  • Impulse arrives → Ca²⁺ enters the axon terminal → vesicles release acetylcholine (ACh) by exocytosis.
  • ACh diffuses across the cleft → binds receptors on the sarcolemma → Na⁺ enters → depolarisation.
  • An action potential spreads along the sarcolemma and down the T-tubules → Ca²⁺ released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum (see Mission 4).
  • Acetylcholinesterase in the cleft hydrolyses ACh, so each impulse gives one response.
  • The sarcolemma at the junction is folded: more area, more ACh receptors.
Quick check
?The drug curare binds to the ACh receptors on the sarcolemma without opening them. What happens when a motor neurone carries impulses to a muscle treated with curare?
Mission 5

Keeping ATP topped up

  • Myosin heads and Ca²⁺ pumps use ATP fast. A fibre holds only enough ATP for a few seconds of hard work.
  • Phosphocreatine (creatine phosphate) is the first back-up:
    • PCr + ADP → creatine + ATP. One step: fast; no oxygen needed.
    • Lasts a few more seconds of maximum effort.
    • Rebuilt from creatine when the muscle rests, using ATP from respiration.
  • Then respiration supplies ATP: aerobic if O2 is enough; anaerobic if not.
  • Say: "ATP is used as soon as it is made" — not: "muscles store ATP for later".
Quick check
?In the first few seconds of a sprint, muscle fibres use ATP faster than respiration can make it. What keeps ATP available?
Mission check

?A student examines a prepared slide of diaphragm muscle. The diaphragm contracts in every breath, usually without conscious thought. What will the student see?

Identify muscle by what you see, not by what it does: striations, branching and nuclei tell you the type.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Gibberellins lengthen internodes and trigger germination by switching on amylase.
  • Ethene ripens fruit and triggers leaf fall; ABA closes stomata; hormones are used to control ripening, root cuttings and kill weeds.

Mission 2

  • Receptor → sensory neurone → relay neurone in the spinal cord → motor neurone → effector: a fast, involuntary, protective reflex, with no decision by the brain.
  • CNS and PNS; somatic and autonomic. Knee jerk (no relay neurone) and blinking (brain stem) protect posture and the eye.

Mission 3

  • The autonomic system is involuntary: sympathetic and parasympathetic branches act antagonistically on the same organs, e.g. heart rate via the SAN.
  • Nerves are fast and targeted; hormones are slower, carried everywhere and act on cells with receptors. In fight or flight, adrenaline spreads and prolongs the sympathetic response.
  • Adrenaline is a first messenger: outside the cell, it activates adenylyl cyclase, and cAMP inside is the second messenger.

Mission 4

  • Sarcomeres shorten because actin slides over myosin: I band and H zone narrow, A band stays the same. More overlap, more force.
  • Ca²⁺ uncovers binding sites on actin; myosin heads bind, pull, and use ATP to detach and reset.

Mission 5

  • Phosphocreatine regenerates ATP from ADP in the first seconds of hard work.
  • Skeletal muscle (striated, multinucleate fibres) and cardiac muscle (striated, branched, intercalated discs) differ in structure and function.
  • Smooth muscle is unstriated, with spindle-shaped cells.
  • At the neuromuscular junction, ACh depolarises the sarcolemma.
