Mission 1

A king carved 'despair' on his statue. Who has the last word?

Make a guess: A king carved 'despair' on his statue. Who has the last word?

Power and Conflict · "Ozymandias"Screen 1

A pharaoh, a sonnet and a competition

  • Ozymandias is the Greek name for the Egyptian pharaoh Ramesses II, who ruled over 3,000 years ago.
  • Percy Bysshe Shelley wrote the sonnet around Christmas 1817, in a friendly contest with the poet Horace Smith to write on the same subject.
  • It was published in The Examiner in January 1818.
  • Shelley was a political radical who opposed tyranny and absolute monarchy. This helps explain why his poem cuts a mighty ruler down to size.
Extract Power and Conflict · "Ozymandias"Screen 2

"Ozymandias" (the whole sonnet)

Power and Conflict · "Ozymandias"Screen 3

Voices inside voices

  • The poem is a frame narrative: a speaker ("I") retells what a traveller told him.
  • Inside the traveller's story is the sculptor, who "well those passions read": he saw the king's pride and carved it.
  • Inside that are the king's own words on the pedestal.
  • Each layer is further from the king's power. His voice reaches us only second-hand, through people he never ruled.
  • Mocked has two meanings: the sculptor copied the king's face, and made fun of it.

Power and Conflict · "Ozymandias"Screen 5

A sonnet that breaks the rules

  • A sonnet has 14 lines. This one is in loose iambic pentameter.
  • Its rhyme scheme, ABABACDCEDEFEF, mixes the Petrarchan and Shakespearean patterns. Like the statue, the form is not quite whole.
  • The volta (turn) comes after the king's boast: "Nothing beside remains."
  • The full stop after "remains" is a caesura: the boast is cut off by silence.
  • The last lines use alliteration ("boundless and bare", "lone and level") to stretch the empty desert.

?A king carved 'despair' on his statue. Who has the last word?

The king's boast survives, but it now describes nothing: the last line belongs to the "lone and level sands". Human power is temporary. What lasts is time, nature and, ironically, art: the sculptor's picture of the king's pride, retold by a traveller and a poet.

Mission 2

Blake hears misery in every London voice. Whose fault is it?

Make a guess: Blake hears misery in every London voice. Whose fault is it?

Poetry · "London"Screen 1

Blake's London, 1794

  • William Blake published "London" in Songs of Experience (1794).
  • He wrote during the French Revolution, which he followed closely and saw as a revolt against oppressive rule.
  • In the poem a speaker walks the city and records what he sees and hears: chimney sweeps, a soldier, a young prostitute ("Harlot").
  • Charter'd: given out by a charter, a written grant of rights. In the poem even the streets and the river seem owned and controlled.
Extract Poetry · "London"Screen 2

Stanzas 1 and 2

Poetry · "London"Screen 3

A walking beat, a trapped pattern

  • Four quatrains, each rhymed ABAB: a tight, regular pattern.
  • Mostly iambic tetrameter, four beats a line, like steady footsteps: i WAN | der THRO' | each CHAR | ter'd STREET.
  • Repetition traps the reader as the people are trapped: "charter'd" twice, "mark" and "Marks", and anaphora: "In every" opens three lines, and "every" comes five times in stanza 2.
  • In stanza 3 the beat shifts: "How the Chimney-sweeper's cry" starts on a stress, as the cries begin.

?Blake hears misery in every London voice. Whose fault is it?

The poem points at the powerful: charters control the streets, the Church ignores the sweeps and the Palace is stained with soldiers' blood. But the "mind-forg'd manacles" suggest the chains are also in people's heads: misery continues because people accept it.

Mission 3

A boy steals a boat for fun. Why does a mountain haunt him?

Make a guess: A boy steals a boat for fun. Why does a mountain haunt him?

Poetry · The PreludeScreen 1

A boy, a boat, a mountain

  • The Prelude is Wordsworth's long autobiographical poem in blank verse (unrhymed iambic pentameter).
  • He began it in 1798 and revised it for the rest of his life. The final version was published in 1850, three months after his death.
  • In this episode, from Book I, he remembers a childhood summer evening in the Lake District: he takes a boat that isn't his and rows out across a lake.
  • Your extract is from the 1850 text.
Poetry · The PreludeScreen 2

Three moods in one episode

  • Pleasure and guilt: the boy pushes off proudly, but knows it is theft. "It was an act of stealth / And troubled pleasure"
  • Terror: as he rows, a huge peak seems to rise and stride after him, as if alive. He turns back.
  • Haunting: for days afterwards, strange, huge shapes fill his thoughts and dreams.
  • The turning point is the moment the peak appears: confidence becomes fear.

?A boy steals a boat for fun. Why does a mountain haunt him?

The theft begins as a thrill, but when the peak seems to rise and follow him, his pride turns to fear. Nature appears to judge his guilty act, and for days afterwards he senses a strange power in the world, bigger than himself. Many readers see this as a lesson in awe and conscience; others focus on the boy's own guilty imagination.

Mission 4

A duke shows a visitor a portrait. What is he really saying?

Make a guess: A duke shows a visitor a portrait. What is he really saying?

Poetry · "My Last Duchess"Screen 1

A duke, a portrait, a visitor

  • Browning published the poem in 1842. It is headed "Ferrara", a city in Italy.
  • The Duke is usually identified as Alfonso II d'Este, Duke of Ferrara. His first wife died in 1561, aged 16; poisoning was rumoured.
  • He then sought a new bride, negotiating through the Count of Tyrol.
  • In the poem the Duke shows the Count's envoy (messenger) a portrait of his "last Duchess" while they arrange his next marriage.
Extract Poetry · "My Last Duchess"Screen 2

The portrait behind the curtain (lines 1–10)

Poetry · "My Last Duchess"Screen 3

A dramatic monologue in disguised couplets

  • A dramatic monologue: one character speaks to a silent listener (the envoy), and gives away more than he means.
  • We only hear the Duke, so we judge him from his own words.
  • Rhyming couplets in iambic pentameter: that's MY | last DUCH | ess PAINT | ed ON | the WALL.
  • Heavy enjambment hides the rhymes ("I call / That piece a wonder"): the speech sounds casual, but the strict form shows control.

Mission checkScreen 9

?A duke shows a visitor a portrait. What is he really saying?

On the surface the Duke is showing off a painting. Underneath, he reveals his pride, his jealousy and his need to control, and hints that he had his wife silenced. Because he says this to the envoy arranging his next marriage, many readers hear a warning about how the next Duchess must behave.

Mission 5

600 men ride into a deadly blunder. Why does Tennyson say 'Honour'?

Make a guess: 600 men ride into a deadly blunder. Why does Tennyson say 'Honour'?

Poetry · "The Charge of the Light Brigade"Screen 1

An order goes wrong, 1854

When25 October 1854, the Battle of Balaclava, Crimean War
WhatA confused order sent the Light Brigade (cavalry, about 670 men) charging down a valley lined with Russian guns; 118 were killed and 127 wounded
The poemTennyson, Poet Laureate since 1850, read reports in The Times, one calling it "some hideous blunder". His poem appeared in The Examiner on 9 December 1854
  • In stanza 2 the soldiers know someone "blunder'd", yet they obey: "Their's not to reason why, / Their's but to do and die,".
Extract Poetry · "The Charge of the Light Brigade"Screen 2

Stanza 1

Poetry · "The Charge of the Light Brigade"Screen 3

A galloping beat

  • Dactylic dimeter: two beats a line, each a dactyl (DUM-da-da): HALF a league | HALF a league. Hooves at full gallop.
  • Short lines and repetition ("Cannon to right of them, / Cannon to left of them") drive the men on.
  • The refrain changes as men die: "Rode the six hundred" becomes "Not the six hundred", then "Left of six hundred". The last stanza commands: "Honour the Light Brigade,".
  • The order is direct speech, but the commander is never named.

?600 men ride into a deadly blunder. Why does Tennyson say 'Honour'?

Tennyson honours the soldiers' courage and their duty to obey, even into "the valley of Death". But by telling us that "Some one" had blundered, he lets readers see the waste too. Many readers hear celebration; others hear a quiet criticism of those in command.

Mission 6

No battle, no attack. So what is killing these soldiers?

Make a guess: No battle, no attack. So what is killing these soldiers?

Poetry · "Exposure"Screen 1

Owen's winter at the front

  • Wilfred Owen served in France with the Manchester Regiment from December 1916.
  • In the winter of 1917 he and his men lay out in the snow for two days and nights, unable to move. The final draft of "Exposure" is dated September 1918.
  • Owen was killed on 4 November 1918; his Poems came out in 1920.
  • His draft Preface says: "The subject of it is War, and the pity of War."
Extract Poetry · "Exposure"Screen 2

Stanzas 1 and 2

Poetry · "Exposure"Screen 3

A poem that goes nowhere

  • Eight stanzas, each of four long, slow lines and a short last line. Most last lines are the refrain "But nothing happens", or a question.
  • Half-rhyme (pararhyme): words share consonants but not vowels, as in "silent / salient" and "wire / war". The sounds almost match, never quite: like the men's hopes.
  • Ellipses (". . .") trail off: time drags.
  • The first person plural ("we", "our") speaks for all the men.

Mission checkScreen 9

?No battle, no attack. So what is killing these soldiers?

The weather kills them, slowly, while they wait. Owen makes the wind, snow and frost the attackers and shows the enemy's bullets as "Less deadly than the air". The refrain "But nothing happens" turns their deaths into something routine and pointless, which is the pity of war.

Poetry skills · comparing poemsScreen 1

One argument, two poems

  • A comparison is one argument about both poems, not two essays joined by "on the other hand".
  • Compare what the poems say (ideas, attitudes) and how they say it (language, form, structure).
  • Find a link first, then the differences within it.
  • Keep both poems in play in each paragraph, using whereas, similarly, in contrast, both.

Recap

Big ideas

  • "Ozymandias" (1818) uses a frame narrative and a broken sonnet to show a mighty ruler's power crumbling to nothing.
  • The volta at "Nothing beside remains" answers the boast with emptiness; the sculptor's art outlasts the king.
  • In Section B, compare it with poems about power, pride or nature's strength.
  • "London" (1794) walks through a controlled city and hears suffering in every voice.
  • Tight quatrains, a steady beat and heavy repetition make the city feel like a trap; the images link suffering to the Church and the Palace.
  • Compare with other poems about power and those it hurts.
  • The stolen-boat episode moves through three moods: guilty pleasure, terror as the peak "rises", and a haunting that lasts for days.
  • Personification ("Upreared its head", "Strode after me") makes the mountain seem alive; the ending replaces familiar shapes with "huge and mighty forms".
  • "My Last Duchess" (1842) is a dramatic monologue: the Duke speaks to a silent envoy and reveals more than he means.
  • Disguised couplets, enjambment and euphemism ("I gave commands") show a man whose calm speech hides total control.
  • Context: the poem draws on the Duke of Ferrara and his young first wife (died 1561).
  • The poem (1854) responds to news of the Light Brigade's charge at Balaclava, caused by a confused order.
  • Galloping dactyls, repetition and a changing refrain carry the men forward; personification makes the valley a devouring monster.
  • It honours the soldiers' courage while showing the blunder: strong answers weigh both.
  • "Exposure" draws on Owen's own winter at the front in 1917: soldiers die from the cold while waiting for an attack that never comes.
  • Slow stanzas, half-rhyme, ellipses and the refrain "But nothing happens" make time drag and hope fail.
  • The weather is personified as the enemy ("knife us", the frost that will "fasten" on them); the poem shows the pity of war, not its glory.
  • A comparison is one argument about both poems: find a link, then explore the differences within it.
  • Compare ideas and methods together (voice, metre, rhyme, imagery), using comparative connectives in every paragraph.
  • Your exam pairs a named poem with a second poem (one you choose, or an unseen one): know which, and write about both.

Key quotations to learn

  • sneer of cold command (line 5) The sculptor shows a ruler's contempt for his people.
  • The hand that mocked them, and the heart that fed: (line 8) Art copies and mocks the king's pride.
  • ‘My name is Ozymandias, king of kings: (line 10) The boast of total power.
  • Look on my works, ye Mighty, and despair!’ (line 11) The command that the poem turns into irony.
  • Nothing beside remains. (line 12) The volta: the boast meets emptiness.
  • The lone and level sands stretch far away. (line 14) Nature and time have the last word.
  • I wander thro' each charter'd street, (stanza 1) A city owned and controlled, even its streets.
  • Marks of weakness, marks of woe. (stanza 1) Suffering is visible on every face.
  • The mind-forg'd manacles I hear. (stanza 2) The chains are in people's minds too.
  • Every black'ning Church appalls; (stanza 3) The Church is stained and does nothing.
  • Runs in blood down Palace walls. (stanza 3) The rulers are marked with the soldier's blood.
  • And blights with plagues the Marriage hearse. (stanza 4) Oxymoron: love and death joined; misery passes to the next generation.
  • It was an act of stealth / And troubled pleasure (1850, lines 361–362) Pleasure mixed with guilt from the start (AQA, Edexcel text).
  • a huge peak, black and huge, (1850, line 378) The turning point: the threat appears.
  • Upreared its head. (1850, line 380) Personification: the mountain seems alive.
  • Strode after me. (1850, line 385) The peak seems to pursue him.
  • But huge and mighty forms, that do not live (1850, line 398) Nature's strange power stays in his mind.
  • By day, and were a trouble to my dreams. (1850, line 400) The lasting effect: the extract's last line.
  • That's my last Duchess painted on the wall, (line 1) "last": one of a series; she is now just a possession.
  • The curtain I have drawn for you, but I) (line 10) He controls who sees her.
  • She looked on, and her looks went everywhere. (line 24) His jealousy at her friendliness.
  • My gift of a nine-hundred-years-old name (line 33) Pride in rank and family.
  • This grew; I gave commands; / Then all smiles stopped together. (lines 45–46) Euphemism hiding what he ordered.
  • Taming a sea-horse, thought a rarity, (line 55) The final image of control.
  • Half a league, half a league, (stanza 1) Galloping dactylic rhythm.
  • Into the valley of Death (stanza 1) The outcome is clear from the start.
  • Their's not to reason why, (stanza 2) Duty without question.
  • Their's but to do and die, (stanza 2) Obedience, even to death.
  • Into the jaws of Death, (stanza 3) Personification: a valley that devours.
  • Honour the Light Brigade, (stanza 6) The final command to the reader.
  • merciless iced east winds that knife us (stanza 1, line 1) The weather as attacker (1920 text; some anthologies print "knive").
  • But nothing happens. (refrain (stanzas 1, 3, 4, 8)) Endless waiting; death becomes routine.
  • What are we doing here? (stanza 2) The soldiers question the war itself.
  • Less deadly than the air that shudders black with snow, (stanza 4) Nature is deadlier than the enemy.
  • frost will fasten on this mud and us, (stanza 8) The frost grips men and mud alike (1920 text "His frost"; most anthologies "this frost").
  • All their eyes are ice, (stanza 8) The dead frozen; the living numbed.
