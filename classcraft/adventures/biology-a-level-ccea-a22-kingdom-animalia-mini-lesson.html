Mission 1

Three questions for any body plan

  • To place an animal in a phylum, ask:
  • Symmetry: radial or bilateral?
  • Gut: one opening, or a mouth and an anus?
  • Support: water, fluid, body tissue or bone?
  • Plus: is the body segmented?
  • This mission: three phyla with no bones.
Mission 1

Radial or bilateral?

  • Radial symmetry: many planes through a central axis cut the body into matching halves. Like a cake.
  • Bilateral symmetry: only one plane gives mirror-image left and right halves.
  • Bilateral animals have a head end, a dorsal (upper) and a ventral (lower) surface.
  • Dorso-ventrally flattened: flattened from top to bottom.
Mission 1

Cnidaria: hydra and jellyfish

  • Multicellular, radially symmetrical.
  • Tentacles round one opening into a gut cavity: the enteron.
  • Support 1: the water around them (the aqueous medium). Out of water, a jellyfish collapses.
  • Support 2: a hydrostatic skeleton: the fluid-filled enteron.
  • Mouth closed, contractile cells squeeze the fluid. Water cannot be compressed, so the body changes shape: longer and thinner, or shorter and wider.
Quick check
?A hydra closes its mouth. Contractile cells running round its body then contract, and the hydra becomes longer and thinner. What makes this possible?
Mission 1

Platyhelminthes: planarian and liver fluke

  • Bilaterally symmetrical, flattened dorso-ventrally.
  • Flat: a short diffusion distance to every cell.
  • Single opening to the gut: the mouth. Food goes in and waste comes out the same way.
  • No specialised skeletal system: supported by body tissue.
  • Planarian: free-living in ponds and streams.
  • Liver fluke: a parasite in the bile ducts of sheep and cattle.
Quick check
?Which feature do planarians share with hydra, but not with earthworms?
Mission 1

Annelida: earthworm and lugworm

  • Bilaterally symmetrical, round in transverse section.
  • Metamerically segmented: the body is a row of repeated segments.
  • Gut with a mouth and an anus, showing regional specialisation:
  • mouth → pharynx (sucks) → crop (stores) → gizzard (grinds) → intestine (digests, absorbs) → anus.
  • Hydrostatic skeleton: fluid in the segmental body cavities, separated by walls.
  • Circular and longitudinal muscles act on the fluid, segment by segment: the worm lengthens and shortens to move.
Quick check
?An earthworm's gut has a mouth and an anus. What advantage does this give over a gut with one opening?
Mission 1Practical

Studying animal specimens

  • Living hydra, stereo microscope: count tentacles; touch gently and watch the body contract.
  • Living planarian: note the flat body, head end and ventral pharynx.
  • Living earthworm: see the segments; feel the bristles on the ventral surface. Handle gently; return it.
  • Prepared slide, transverse section of earthworm: round outline, gut, body cavity, muscle layers.
  • Photographs: jellyfish, liver fluke, lugworm.
  • Calculation: actual size = image size ÷ magnification. Example: a hydra 60 mm long in an image at ×20 is 60 ÷ 20 = 3 mm long.
Mission check

?A marine animal lives attached to rocks. Its body is radially symmetrical, with tentacles round a single opening into a fluid-filled gut cavity. When it closes the opening and contracts its body wall, it stretches upwards. Which phylum, and which means of support, fit this animal?

No bones needed: water and the enteron fluid hold up a cnidarian, body tissue holds up a flatworm, and segmental fluid holds up an earthworm.

Mission 2

Arthropoda: insect and spider

  • Bilaterally symmetrical.
  • Metamerically segmented, with a fixed number of segments in each region.
  • Insect: three regions: head, thorax, abdomen.
  • Spider: two regions.
  • Jointed limbs: hard sections linked by flexible joints.
  • A hard outer covering (exoskeleton) supports the body.
  • Gut with a mouth and an anus, showing regional specialisation.
Mission 2

One plan, many tools

  • Segments and jointed limbs are repeated units.
  • Over generations, units have been modified for different jobs:
  • head limbs → antennae and mouthparts (biting, sucking, piercing);
  • thorax: three pairs of legs (walking, jumping, swimming, digging);
  • thorax: wings in most insects.
  • Insects were the first animals to fly.

Mission 2

Why insects succeeded

  • The basic insect body plan (segments in regions + jointed limbs) could be modified in many directions.
  • So insects evolved rapidly into many forms: new mouthparts for new foods, legs for new habitats, wings for flight.
  • Flight: escape, reach new food and spread to new habitats.
  • Result: arthropods, especially insects, are the most successful animal group:
  • most species;
  • vast numbers of individuals.
  • Say: "the body plan was modified by natural selection" — not: "insects changed their bodies to survive".
Mission 2

Chordata: mammal and bird

  • Bilaterally symmetrical and segmented: repeated vertebrae, ribs and spinal nerves.
  • Gut with a mouth and an anus, showing regional specialisation (e.g. stomach, small intestine, large intestine).
  • Support: normally a spinal column of jointed, calcified bones inside the body.
  • Like arthropods, chordates have jointed limbs: but the joints are between internal bones.

Mission 2Practical

Studying animal specimens

  • Preserved insect, hand lens: count the body regions, the legs on each thoracic segment, and the wings.
  • Spider (specimen or photograph): two body regions, four pairs of legs.
  • Prepared slide, insect mouthparts: compare biting and sucking types.
  • Mammal or bird skeleton: find the repeated vertebrae and ribs, and the joints between bones.
  • Calculation: magnification = image size ÷ actual size. Example: a leg 1.5 mm long drawn 45 mm long: 45 ÷ 1.5 = ×30.
Mission check

?On an isolated island, biologists find about 40 species of one insect group, all descended from a single colonising species. Some feed on nectar, some on seeds, some on leaves and some on other insects; their mouthparts differ. Which feature of the insect body plan best explains how one ancestor gave rise to such variety?

Segments and jointed limbs are repeated units. Natural selection could reshape them in many directions, which is why insects are so diverse.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Cnidaria: radial; one opening; supported by water and the fluid-filled enteron. Platyhelminthes: bilateral, flattened dorso-ventrally; one opening; supported by body tissue.
  • Annelida: bilateral, round, metamerically segmented; mouth and anus with regional specialisation; hydrostatic skeleton of segmental body cavities.

Mission 2

  • Arthropoda: bilateral; fixed segments per region; jointed limbs; mouth and anus with regional specialisation. The insect plan was modified in many directions, including flight: the most species-rich animal group.
  • Chordata: bilateral, segmented; mouth and anus with regional specialisation; jointed spinal column of calcified bones.
