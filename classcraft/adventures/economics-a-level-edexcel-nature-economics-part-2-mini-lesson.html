Make a guess: How could ten workers make thousands of pins a day when one alone could barely make twenty?

Mission 1

What is specialisation?

  • Specialisation: concentrating on a limited range of tasks or products.
  • It happens at every level:
    • Individual: a dentist, an electrician, a coder.
    • Firm: a company that only makes bicycle brakes.
    • Region: a city known for finance or for films.
    • Country: one economy exports coffee, another aircraft parts.
  • Division of labour: splitting the making of one product into separate tasks, each done by a different worker.
  • Division of labour is specialisation inside a firm.
Mission 1

Adam Smith's pin factory

  • Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations (1776), described a small pin workshop.
  • Pin-making was split into about 18 distinct operations: one draws the wire, one straightens it, one cuts it, one sharpens the point…
  • 10 workers made upwards of 48,000 pins a day: about 4,800 each.
  • Working alone and untrained, Smith wrote, each "certainly could not" have made twenty pins a day.
  • Smith's three reasons output soared:
    • Dexterity: repeating one task builds skill and speed.
    • Time saved: no switching between tasks and tools.
    • Machinery: a narrow task is easier to mechanise, so machines get invented.
  • Real case, 1913: Ford's moving assembly line cut the time to assemble a car from about 12.5 hours to 93 minutes by early 1914.
Mission 1

Worked example: output and cost per unit

A sandwich bar has 4 workers, each paid 12 per hour (illustrative data).

  • Before: each worker makes whole sandwiches: 30 per hour each, so 120 per hour in total.
  • After: one slices, one fills, one wraps, one serves: 200 per hour in total.
  • Step 1: output per worker after = 200 ÷ 4 = 50 per hour.
  • Step 2: labour productivity rises from 30 to 50: (50 − 30) ÷ 30 × 100 = +66.7%.
  • Step 3: labour cost per sandwich before = 12 ÷ 30 = 0.40.
  • Step 4: labour cost per sandwich after = 12 ÷ 50 = 0.24: down 40%.
  • Same workers, same wage, same hours: more output, lower cost per unit.
Quick check
?A bakery has 6 workers, each paid 90 per day. When each worker bakes whole loaves, the bakery makes 120 loaves a day. After it divides the work into separate tasks, the same 6 workers make 180 loaves a day (illustrative data). What is the labour cost per loaf after the change?
Mission 1

Who gains?

  • Firms: higher labour productivity, lower unit costs, more output from the same inputs.
  • Workers: can build expert skills; easier to train for one task; possibly higher pay as productivity rises.
  • Consumers: lower prices, more output, often better quality from experts.
  • The economy: more output from scarce resources: higher living standards.
  • Countries: specialising and trading lets each focus on what it does best.
Mission 1

The downsides

  • Workers: repetitive work → boredom, lower motivation, more mistakes, absence and staff turnover.
  • Smith himself warned (Book V) that a lifetime of "a few simple operations" can leave a worker's mind dulled.
  • Narrow skills: if a task is automated or demand changes, workers face structural unemployment.
  • Interdependence: if one stage stops, the whole line stops.
  • Countries and regions: over-reliance on a few products; exposed to a shock in one market or supply chain.
  • Real case, 2021: a global shortage of computer chips forced car makers around the world to cut production.
  • Loss of variety: standardised products may cut consumer choice.
Quick check
?A car plant buys a key electronic part from a single specialist supplier abroad. A fire at that supplier's factory halts its output, and within a week the car plant stops its own production line. Which disadvantage of specialisation does this show best?
Mission 1

Evaluate: how far should division of labour go?

It depends on…

  • The size of the market. Smith: division of labour is "limited by the extent of the market". A village baker selling 50 loaves a day cannot keep 6 specialists busy.
  • The type of task. Routine, repeated tasks gain most. Creative or varied work (design, care work) gains less, and repetition can harm quality.
  • How jobs are designed. Job rotation and teamwork can keep productivity gains while cutting boredom and absence.
  • Time period. Gains appear quickly; the risks (deskilling, dependence on one supplier or one export) show up later.
  • Say: "labour productivity rose" (output per worker) — not: "production rose", which could just mean more workers.
Mission 1

Specialise, then exchange

  • A specialist makes far more of one thing than they need, and none of everything else.
  • So specialisation only works with exchange: selling what you make to buy what you need.
  • Individual: a nurse sells her labour for a wage, then buys food, rent and transport.
  • National: a country exports what it specialises in and imports the rest.
  • The more people specialise, the more they depend on trade, and on a cheap, trusted way to trade.
Mission 1

Barter and its problem

  • Barter: swapping goods or services directly, with no money.
  • Problem 1: it needs a double coincidence of wants: each side must want exactly what the other offers, at the same time.
  • Problem 2: no common measure: with 100 goods, every good needs a price in terms of the other 99 (4,950 exchange rates).
  • Problem 3: indivisibility: a cow cannot be split to buy one pair of shoes.
  • Money fixes this as a medium of exchange: anything widely accepted in payment. Sell to anyone for money, then buy from anyone.
  • So money makes specialisation and exchange far cheaper and quicker.
Mission 1

Four functions of money

  • Medium of exchange: accepted in payment for goods and services.
  • Measure of value (unit of account): prices in one unit, so values can be compared.
  • Store of value: holds purchasing power over time, unlike fish or milk. Inflation weakens it.
  • Method of deferred payment: debts and contracts can be set now and settled later.
  • Say: "money is a medium of exchange" — not: "money is valuable in itself"; a bank note is worth what it buys.
Quick check
?In a village with no money, a farmer has eggs and wants shoes. The shoemaker has shoes but wants a haircut, not eggs. What problem does this show, and how does money solve it?
Mission check

?A car-repair garage keeps the same 8 mechanics on the same hours. Instead of each mechanic doing whole jobs, each now does one type of job: tyres, brakes or servicing. Repairs completed per mechanic rise by a third. Which explanation fits the analysis of the division of labour?

Same workers, same hours, more output: the division of labour raises productivity.

Mission 2

Life without money: barter

  • Barter: swapping goods and services directly. Apples for bread, a haircut for a meal.
  • Problem 1: the double coincidence of wants. You need someone who has what you want and wants what you have.
  • Problem 2: no common measure. How many apples is a haircut worth? Every pair of goods needs its own price.
  • Problem 3: hard to save. Apples rot.
  • Problem 4: hard to split. Half a cow is not much use.
  • Money solves all four. It makes specialisation and trade far easier.
Mission 2

The functions of money

  • Money: anything generally accepted as a means of payment.
  • Its functions:
    • Medium of exchange: accepted in payment for goods and services.
    • Unit of account (or measure of value): prices and incomes are stated in it, so values can be compared.
    • Store of value: holds purchasing power over time.
    • Standard of deferred payment: debts and future payments are stated in it.
  • Say: "money is a medium of exchange" — not: "money is something you buy things with because it is valuable".
Mission 2

When money stops working: Zimbabwe

  • 2007–2008: prices in Zimbabwe rose faster and faster: hyperinflation.
  • Official annual inflation reached 231 million per cent (July 2008).
  • In early 2009 the central bank issued a 100 trillion Zimbabwe dollar note.
  • What failed first: the store of value. Money lost value in days, so people spent it at once.
  • Then the unit of account: shops priced goods in US dollars or changed prices several times a day.
  • Then the medium of exchange: sellers refused Zimbabwe dollars.
  • In 2009 Zimbabwe allowed the US dollar and other currencies to be used instead.
Quick check
?In a country with very high inflation, a family is paid in the local currency. They change their wages into a foreign currency on payday and keep their savings in it. Which function of the local currency has broken down most clearly?
Mission 2

What makes good money?

  • Acceptable: people trust that others will take it.
  • Scarce / limited in supply: if anyone can make more, it loses value.
  • Durable: does not rot or wear out quickly.
  • Portable: easy to carry.
  • Divisible: can be split into small amounts.
  • Uniform: every unit is the same.
  • Hard to forge.
  • Gold scores well on most. Apples score badly on durability and uniformity.
Mission 2

Forms of money

  • Commodity money: has value itself. Gold, silver, salt.
  • Representative money: paper that could be swapped for gold (a gold standard).
  • Fiat money: notes and coins with little value as objects. Accepted because the law makes them legal tender and people trust they will be accepted.
  • Bank deposits: numbers in accounts, spent by card, transfer or cheque.
    • In the UK, bank deposits are about 97% of broad money; notes and coins only about 3% (Bank of England, 2014).
  • Cryptocurrencies (e.g. Bitcoin): a debated case.
    • Their prices swing a lot. Few shops price goods in them.
    • Most central banks say they are not money in the full sense.
Quick check
?A café accepts payment in a cryptocurrency. Its price in US dollars often moves by 10% or more in a week, so the café must rewrite its menu prices in the cryptocurrency every day. Which function of money is failing most directly here?
Mission 2

Liquidity

  • Liquidity: how quickly and cheaply an asset can be turned into spending power without losing value.
  • Most liquid to least: notes and coins → current account deposits → savings deposits → fixed-term deposits → bonds and shares → houses.
  • Money is the most liquid asset. Narrow and broad measures of money are drawn along this ladder.
Mission check

?In a prisoner-of-war camp in the Second World War, cigarettes became money. Prisoners who did not smoke still accepted cigarettes in payment for food and clothes. What best explains why?

Money is whatever people generally accept in payment, because each person expects others to accept it too.

Make a guess: In a country of millions, who decides how many loaves of bread are baked tomorrow?

Mission 3

Three questions every economy must answer

  • Resources are scarce, so every society must decide:
  • What to produce (and how much)?
  • How to produce it (which resources, which methods)?
  • For whom to produce it (who gets the output)?
  • An economic system is the way a society answers these questions.
  • It has two parts: who owns the resources, and the coordinating mechanism that links millions of decisions.
Mission 3

Free market economy

  • Resources are privately owned.
  • The price mechanism coordinates: prices rise where goods are wanted and scarce, fall where they are not.
  • Firms seek profit; consumers choose what to buy. Spending acts like votes (consumer sovereignty).
  • The state's role is small: protect property rights, enforce contracts, keep law and order.
  • Strengths: incentives to work, invest and innovate; prices carry information quickly; wide choice.
  • Weaknesses: some goods under-provided (e.g. street lighting); pollution ignored; large inequality of income and wealth; booms and slumps.
Mission 3

Planned (command) economy

  • Most resources are owned by the state.
  • A central planning agency answers what, how and for whom: it sets output targets and fixes prices.
  • Strengths: resources can be directed to national priorities (e.g. heavy industry, defence, health); income can be kept more equal; full employment can be a target.
  • Weaknesses: planners lack the information to plan millions of goods; fixed prices bring shortages and queues or surpluses; weak incentives to cut costs or innovate; little consumer choice.
Quick check
?In a planned economy, a central agency sets each factory's output and fixes all prices. A shortage of winter coats appears. What is most likely to happen next under this system?
Mission 3

Mixed economy

  • Both the market (private sector) and the state (public sector) allocate resources.
  • Most goods are bought and sold in markets.
  • The state provides some goods (e.g. defence, roads, often schools and healthcare), taxes, regulates and redistributes income.
  • Every real economy is mixed. They differ in how much the state does.
  • Think of a spectrum: free market at one end, planned at the other. Countries sit in between, and move along it over time.
Mission 3

Real case: planning and reform

  • The Soviet Union ran its economy through central plans, with five-year plans from 1928 until the state broke up in 1991.
  • Planning built heavy industry fast, but consumer goods were often short and queues were common.
  • China began market reforms in 1978. Over the next few years farm households were allowed to keep or sell output above their state quota; private firms were later allowed.
  • The state still owns many large firms in China, alongside a large private sector: a mixed economy.
  • Both show economies moving along the spectrum, not fixed in one box.
Mission 3

Smith, Hayek and Marx

  • Adam Smith (The Wealth of Nations, 1776): a person seeking only their own gain in a competitive market is "led by an invisible hand" to serve society. The state should provide defence, justice and some public works.
  • Friedrich Hayek (The Road to Serfdom, 1944; "The Use of Knowledge in Society", 1945): knowledge is dispersed among millions of people. Prices pass it on. No central planner can gather it all, and planning concentrates power in the state.
  • Karl Marx (Capital, 1867; The Communist Manifesto, 1848, with Engels): capitalism exploits workers. Owners keep the value workers create beyond their wages. He predicted crises and the replacement of private ownership by collective ownership.
  • Smith and Hayek still saw roles for the state; Marx argued for replacing private ownership of factories, land and machines with collective ownership.
Quick check
?Which economist argued that no central planner could ever gather the knowledge spread across millions of people, and that prices pass it on?
Mission 3

The role of the state

  • Even market supporters give the state a role. Common roles:
  • Legal framework: property rights, contracts, competition law.
  • Public goods and services the market under-provides (e.g. defence, street lighting).
  • Correcting market failure: pollution, information gaps, monopoly.
  • Redistribution: taxes and benefits to reduce inequality.
  • Stabilising the economy in booms and slumps.
  • But governments can fail too: poor information, slow decisions, unintended effects (government failure).
Mission 3Evaluate

Which system allocates resources best?

  • It depends on information: prices pass on dispersed knowledge well; planners struggle with millions of goods.
  • It depends on goals: markets tend to score well on efficiency and choice; equality and national priorities may need the state.
  • It depends on market failures: where externalities or public goods matter, markets alone under- or over-provide.
  • It depends on the state's capacity: good intervention needs good information, honest institutions and incentives.
  • Economists disagree about where on the spectrum an economy should sit. Much of that is a value judgement.
Quick check
?In Country Y, most firms are privately owned and most prices are set in markets. The state funds schools and hospitals from taxes and sets a minimum wage. How is Country Y's economy best described?
Mission check

?A sudden frost destroys half of a country's coffee harvest. How would the response most likely differ between a free market economy and a planned economy with fixed prices?

Markets let a price change do the coordinating; plans need someone to notice and decide.

Mission 4

Ideas grow out of their time

  • Economic ideas respond to the problems of their age.
YearThinkerProblem of the time
1776Adam SmithTrade restrictions; early industry
1867Karl MarxPoverty and harsh conditions in industrial cities
1944Friedrich HayekRise of central planning
  • Each answers one big question differently: what role should the state play?
Mission 4

Adam Smith and laissez faire

  • The Wealth of Nations (1776).
  • People pursuing their self-interest in competitive markets are "led by an invisible hand" to serve society, though that is not their aim.
  • Division of labour raises productivity (the pin factory).
  • Laissez faire (French: "let do", that is, leave people free to act): keep government out of most market decisions.
  • Smith's state still has duties: defence, justice (law and property rights) and certain public works that markets will not provide.
  • He also warned that business owners often conspire to raise prices.
Mission 4

Karl Marx: the critique

  • Das Kapital (1867).
  • Workers create value, but owners of capital (the means of production) keep part of it as profit: exploitation.
  • Competition drives firms to cut wages and replace workers with machines, leading to recurring crises.
  • Class conflict between workers and owners would end capitalism.
  • Role of the state: in Marx's vision, collective ownership of the means of production and planning replace private ownership and markets.
Quick check
?Which statement matches a thinker with their view of the role of the state?
Mission 4

Hayek: knowledge and prices

  • Friedrich Hayek, The Road to Serfdom (1944) and "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (1945).
  • Knowledge about wants and resources is spread across millions of people.
  • Prices pull that knowledge together and signal what to produce. No central planner can collect it all.
  • Central planning risks shortages, waste and loss of freedom.
  • Role of the state: a firm rule of law, secure property rights and a limited role in the economy.
Quick check
?A government sets the price and output of bread for every bakery in the country. Soon some towns have shortages while others have unsold bread. Which explanation would Hayek give?
Mission 4Evaluate

Why the answers differ

  • Views on the state rest on beliefs about how well markets work:
    • Do markets serve everyone (Smith) or exploit workers (Marx)?
    • Do prices carry knowledge no planner could gather (Hayek)?
  • And on values: freedom, equality, security. These are normative judgements.
  • Market failure suggests a role for the state; government failure suggests caution.
  • Real economies are mixed: almost all combine markets with some state provision and regulation.
Mission check

?An economist argues: "Factory owners pay workers less than the value their work creates. Competition between owners will bring repeated crises. Ownership of industry should pass to society as a whole." Which thinker's ideas is this economist drawing on, and what role for the state follows?

Each thinker's role for the state follows from their picture of how markets work and whom they serve.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Specialisation and the division of labour raise labour productivity (Smith: dexterity, time saved, machinery) and cut unit costs.
  • Costs: monotony, narrow skills, interdependence and over-reliance; how far to go depends on market size and the task.
  • Specialists must exchange; money as a medium of exchange removes barter's need for a double coincidence of wants.

Mission 2

  • Money is anything generally accepted as payment. It removes the double coincidence of wants.
  • Functions: medium of exchange, unit of account, store of value.
  • A fourth: standard of deferred payment.

Mission 3

  • Every economy must answer what, how and for whom; systems differ in ownership and in their coordinating mechanism.
  • Free market: prices and private ownership. Planned: the state owns and plans. Mixed: both; every real economy is mixed.
  • Smith (invisible hand), Hayek (prices carry dispersed knowledge), Marx (exploitation; collective ownership).

Mission 4

  • Smith: self-interest plus competition serves society; a limited state.
  • Marx: capitalism exploits workers and brings crises; collective ownership and planning.
  • Hayek: prices coordinate dispersed knowledge; the state keeps the rule of law.
