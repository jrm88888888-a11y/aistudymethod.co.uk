Mission 1: Why does a passing siren drop in pitch, and what can that shift tell us about speed?

Make a guess: Why does a passing siren drop in pitch, and what can that shift tell us about speed?

Quick check
?Predict first. The diagram shows the crests of the sound waves from a source S that moves to the right at constant speed through still air. Listeners A and B are at rest. Listener A hears a higher frequency than listener B does. Which explanation, based on the diagram, is correct?
Mission 1

A moving source

  • A source emits wavefronts at a steady frequency. Each spreads out from where the source was when it was emitted.
  • Moving source: wavefronts bunched ahead (shorter λ), spread out behind (longer λ).
  • Wave speed through the air is unchanged. So ahead: higher f; behind: lower f.
  • Approaching: higher pitch. Receding: lower pitch. Constant velocity → constant (shifted) pitch.
  • Faster relative motion → bigger shift. Same velocity as the listener → no shift.
Mission 1

A moving observer

  • Source still: wavefronts are evenly spaced circles. λ unchanged.
  • Observer moving toward the source meets wavefronts more often → higher f.
  • Moving away → meets them less often → lower f.
  • What matters is the relative velocity of source and observer.
Quick check
?The diagram shows a fire truck T sounding its siren, whose rest frequency is f0, a car 1 and a person 2 beside a straight road; the velocity arrows are drawn to the same scale. The driver of car 1 hears frequency f1 and person 2 hears frequency f2. Which relationship is correct?
Mission check

?A car sounding its horn, whose rest frequency is f0, moves along a straight road near a microphone that is at rest. The graph shows the frequency f measured by the microphone as a function of time t. Which claim is supported by the graph?

Approaching: waves bunched, higher f; receding: stretched, lower f. The size of the shift grows with the relative speed, so measuring it measures the speed.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Two speakers play the same note. How can one step sideways make the sound almost vanish?

Make a guess: Two speakers play the same note. How can one step sideways make the sound almost vanish?

Mission 2

Superposition

  • When two waves or pulses overlap, they interfere.
  • Principle of superposition: the resultant displacement = the sum of the individual displacements.
  • Displacements have signs. Up (+) and down (−) can cancel.
  • Pulses pass through each other. Afterward each carries on unchanged, as if the other was never there.
Quick check
?Two pulses on a rope overlap at point X. At that instant, pulse 1 on its own would displace the rope at X by 2.0 mm downward, and pulse 2 on its own would displace it by 3.0 mm downward. Taking upward as positive, what is the displacement of the rope at X?
Mission 2

Adding waves on a graph

  • To add two waves, add their displacements point by point.
  • Same frequency, in phase (crest meets crest): amplitude A1 + A2. Constructive interference.
  • Same frequency, half a cycle out of phase (crest meets trough): amplitude |A1 − A2|. Destructive interference. Equal amplitudes cancel completely.
  • Anything in between gives an amplitude in between.
Quick check
?The graph shows the displacement y of a long rope against position x at time t = 0. Pulse A moves right and pulse B moves left, each at the speed shown. What is the displacement of the rope at x = 5.0 cm at time t = 1.25 s?
Mission 2

Two sources: path difference

  • Two sources, S1 and S2, emit waves in phase, with the same wavelength λ.
  • At a point P: path-length difference ΔD = |S2P − S1P|.
  • ΔD = mλ (0, λ, 2λ, …): crest meets crest. Constructive: loud, bright, large amplitude.
  • ΔD = (m + ½)λ (½λ, 1½λ, …): crest meets trough. Destructive: quiet, dark, small amplitude.
  • Result: a fixed pattern of maxima and minima. Energy is not destroyed at the minima; it is redistributed to the maxima.
Mission 2

Worked example

  • Two speakers, in phase, play 850 Hz. Speed of sound 340 m/s. λ = v/f = 340 ÷ 850 = 0.400 m.
  • Point P: 3.00 m from one speaker, 3.60 m from the other. Path difference = 0.60 m = 1.5λ → destructive: quiet.
  • Point Q: 3.00 m and 3.80 m. Path difference = 0.80 m = 2λ → constructive: loud.
  • Walk from Q to P: the sound fades from loud to quiet in a few tens of centimeters.
Quick check
?Two loudspeakers connected to the same signal generator emit sound in phase at a frequency of 500 Hz. The speed of sound in air is 340 m/s. At which of these path differences is the sound quiet because of destructive interference?
Mission 2

Beats

  • Two waves of slightly different frequency: they drift in and out of step.
  • In step: displacements add, loud. Out of step: they oppose, quiet. The loudness rises and falls: beats.
  • Beat frequency: fbeat = |f1 − f2|.
  • Tuning forks of 256 Hz and 260 Hz together: 4 beats per second.
  • Tuning an instrument: adjust it until the beats with a reference fork slow down and disappear.
Quick check
?A 440.0 Hz tuning fork and a second tuning fork are struck together, and a student counts 12 beats in 4.00 s. Which of the following could be the frequency of the second fork?
Mission check

?Two dippers vibrating in phase act as coherent sources S1 and S2 on a ripple tank, producing waves of wavelength 2.0 cm. Point P is 31.0 cm from S1 and 37.0 cm from S2. What kind of interference occurs at P?

Where the path difference is a whole number of wavelengths, crest meets crest: loud. Step to where it is a half-wavelength more, and crest meets trough: almost silent.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why does a flute or a guitar string sound only certain notes, never the ones in between?

Make a guess: Why does a flute or a guitar string sound only certain notes, never the ones in between?

Mission 3

Two waves, opposite directions

  • Two waves with the same frequency, wavelength and amplitude travel in opposite directions and superpose.
  • Usually a wave and its own reflection from a fixed end or the end of a pipe.
  • Add the displacements at each point at t = 0, T/4, T/2: some points never move; others swing the most.
  • The pattern does not travel. No energy is carried along it.
  • This is a standing wave.
Mission 3

Nodes and antinodes

  • Node: zero amplitude at all times. Antinode: maximum amplitude.
  • Node to next node: λ/2. Node to next antinode: λ/4.
  • Amplitude varies with position: zero at nodes, largest at antinodes.
  • Between two neighboring nodes, all points move in phase.
  • Points on opposite sides of a node are half a cycle out of phase (phase difference π rad).
Mission 3

Two kinds of wave compared

  • Energy: a traveling wave carries it along; a standing wave does not (stored, swapping between kinetic and potential).
  • Amplitude: traveling, the same everywhere (no losses); standing, varies from zero (node) to maximum (antinode).
  • Phase: traveling, changes steadily along the wave; standing, in phase within a loop, half a cycle out of phase across a node.
  • Wavelength: traveling, crest to crest; standing, twice the node-to-node distance.
Quick check
?A string fixed at both ends vibrates in a standing wave. In the figure, the dashed curves show the extreme positions of the string, and the solid line shows the string at an instant when it is straight. Points J, K, and M are on the string. Which ranking of the speeds vJ, vK, and vM of these points at that instant is correct?
Mission 3

Strings

  • A fixed end must be a node.
  • Fixed at both ends: a whole number of loops fits, L = nλ/2.
  • A free (loose) end, e.g. a ring sliding on a smooth rod, is an antinode.
  • Fixed–free: L = λ/4, 3λ/4, … → odd harmonics only, f = nv/4L (n = 1, 3, 5, …). Free–free: like fixed–fixed, L = nλ/2.
  • n = 1: the first harmonic (one loop, λ = 2L). Then n = 2, 3, …: second, third harmonics.
  • Wave speed v fixed by the string, so f = v/λ = nv/2L: only these frequencies "fit".
Mission 3

Air in pipes

  • Closed end: air cannot move → displacement node. Open end: displacement antinode.
  • Open at both ends: antinode–antinode, L = nλ/2, all harmonics: f = nv/2L.
  • Closed at one end: node–antinode, L = λ/4, 3λ/4, 5λ/4, …
  • Closed at one end: odd harmonics only, f = nv/4L, n = 1, 3, 5, …
Mission 3

Worked example

  • A pipe 0.50 m long; speed of sound 340 m/s.
  • Closed at one end: λ = 4L = 2.0 m → f = 340 ÷ 2.0 = 170 Hz.
  • Next: third harmonic, λ = 4L/3 → f = 3 × 170 = 510 Hz. No 340 Hz.
  • Open at both ends: λ = 2L = 1.0 m → 340 Hz, then 680 Hz, 1020 Hz, …
Quick check
?Pipe X is open at both ends. The frequencies at which standing sound waves form in it are 400 Hz, 800 Hz, 1200 Hz, and so on. One end of pipe X is then closed, with its length and the air inside unchanged. What is now the second-lowest frequency at which a standing wave can form in the pipe?
Quick check
?A string fixed at both ends vibrates in its fundamental mode. The tension in the string is then increased to four times its original value, with the length and the string unchanged. By what factor does the frequency of the fundamental change?
Mission check

?The diagram shows the envelope of a standing wave on a string of length L = 1.20 m that is fixed at both ends. What is the wavelength of the waves on the string?

The ends must be nodes, so only whole numbers of half-wavelengths fit. With v fixed, only the matching frequencies can build up: the instrument's notes.

Mission 4

Mission 4: You can hear someone around a corner but not see them. What decides how much a wave spreads?

Make a guess: You can hear someone around a corner but not see them. What decides how much a wave spreads?

Quick check
?Predict first. The diagram shows a top view of straight water waves of wavelength λ approaching two barriers, one with gap 1 and one with gap 2. The waves are identical in both cases. Which statement correctly compares the waves beyond the two gaps?
Mission 4

Diffraction

  • Diffraction: a wave spreads out as it passes through a gap or around the edge of an obstacle.
  • Speed, frequency and wavelength are unchanged. Only the direction of travel spreads.
  • Spreading becomes significant when the gap is comparable with λ. The narrower the gap, the more it spreads.
  • Gap about λ or smaller: nearly semicircular wavefronts, spreading through almost 180°.
  • Gap much wider than λ: very little spreading; a sharp-edged "shadow".
  • Speech: λ ≈ 0.3–3 m, like doorways. Light: λ ≈ 4–7 × 10⁻⁷ m, far smaller than any doorway.
Mission 4

Light through one slit

  • Laser light through one narrow slit gives bright and dark bands, not a slit-shaped patch.
  • Central maximum: brightest, and twice as wide as each side maximum.
  • Side maxima: much dimmer, fainter the more distant they are from the center.
  • Narrower slit → wider central maximum (and dimmer: less light gets through).
  • Longer λ (red rather than blue) → wider central maximum.
  • Cause: wavefronts from different parts of the one opening interfere.
Mission 4

Where the dark bands are

  • Split the slit into a top half and a bottom half. Pair each point in the top half with the point half a slit width below it.
  • At angle θ, the top edge and the middle differ in path by (a/2) sin θ.
  • If that is λ/2, every pair cancels → first minimum: a sin θ = λ.
  • Edge-to-edge path length difference ΔD = a sin θ, a = slit width.
  • Minima: a sin θ = mλ. Small angles (θ < 10°): a·ymin/L ≈ mλ, L = slit-to-screen distance.
Mission 4

Worked example

  • Laser, λ = 650 nm; slit width 0.10 mm; screen 2.0 m away.
  • sin θ ≈ λ/a = (650 × 10⁻⁹) ÷ (0.10 × 10⁻³) = 6.5 × 10⁻³ (θ ≈ 0.37°, well under 10°).
  • Center to first dark band: 2.0 × 6.5 × 10⁻³ = 1.3 × 10⁻² m (1.3 cm).
  • Central maximum width: 2 × 1.3 cm = 2.6 cm, about 260 times the slit width.
Quick check
?Light of wavelength 650 nm passes through a single slit onto a screen 1.2 m away. The graph shows the intensity of the light on the screen as a function of the position y measured from the middle of the pattern. What is the width of the slit?
Mission 4

Shape of the opening

  • The pattern depends on the shape of the opening.
  • Long, narrow vertical slit: light spreads horizontally, across the narrow width.
  • Small circular hole: a bright central disk surrounded by bright and dark rings.
  • Rule: the narrower the opening in a direction, the more the light spreads in that direction.
Mission check

?Sea waves reach a harbor wall with a 20 m wide entrance. A boat is moored behind the wall, well to one side of the entrance. On day 1 the waves have a wavelength of 20 m; on day 2, 2.0 m, with the same amplitude. On which day is the boat rocked more, and why?

Spreading depends on λ compared with the gap or obstacle. Sound's wavelengths, about the size of a doorway, bend around it; light's, a million times smaller, barely do.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Two slits, a laser and a ruler: how can stripes on a wall measure a wavelength far thinner than a hair?

Make a guess: Two slits, a laser and a ruler: how can stripes on a wall measure a wavelength far thinner than a hair?

Quick check
?Predict first. Monochromatic light passes through two very narrow slits and falls on a distant screen, producing a pattern of bright and dark bands. Which explanation accounts for this pattern?
Mission 5

Young's double slit

  • Monochromatic light (e.g. a laser) → two narrow slits, very close together → a screen far away.
  • Light diffracts (spreads out) from each slit. The two spreading waves overlap and interfere.
  • Both slits are lit by the same wavefronts, so they act as coherent sources.
  • Bright fringe: path-length difference mλ. Dark fringe: (m + ½)λ.
  • Result: equally spaced bright and dark fringes, with a bright fringe in the center.
  • Light added to light gives dark bands: light has wave properties. This was Young's experiment (early 1800s).
Mission 5

The fringe-spacing equation

  • Fringe spacing = λ × (slits-to-screen distance) ÷ (slit separation).
  • d(ymax/L) ≈ mλ: d = slit separation, L = slits-to-screen distance, ymax = distance from the center to the mth maximum. Neighboring maxima: Δy = λL/d.
  • Valid when the screen is far away compared with the slit separation (small angles).
Mission 5

Path difference and angle

  • Light leaving the two slits at angle θ to the normal: path-length difference ΔD = d sin θ.
  • Maxima: d sin θ = mλ (m = 0, 1, 2, …). Minima: d sin θ = (m + ½)λ.
  • Small angles (θ < 10°): sin θ ≈ tan θ = y/L. So d(ymax/L) ≈ mλ.
  • Each step of m moves the maximum by λL/d: the maxima are evenly spaced.
Mission 5

Worked example

  • Laser light of wavelength 633 nm. Slits 0.50 mm apart. Screen 2.00 m from the slits.
  • Δy = λL/d = (633 × 10⁻⁹ × 2.00)/(0.50 × 10⁻³) = 2.5 × 10⁻³ m
  • Fringes 2.5 mm apart: easy to measure. A wavelength of 0.000 000 633 m becomes millimeters on the screen.
  • Turn it around: measure the fringe spacing, slit separation and distance, and calculate λ.
Quick check
?In a double-slit experiment, light of wavelength 400 nm is replaced by light of wavelength 600 nm, and the pair of slits is replaced by a pair whose separation is half as large. The screen is not moved. Assume small angles. By what factor does the distance between neighboring bright bands on the screen change?
Mission 5

Real slits: the single-slit envelope

  • Each slit has a width. One slit alone gives a single-slit diffraction pattern: a broad central maximum and much weaker side maxima.
  • With two slits, the interference fringes lie inside this single-slit pattern, the envelope.
  • Fringe spacing is set by the slit separation. Envelope width is set by the slit width.
  • Fringe brightness follows the envelope. Where the envelope is zero, fringes are missing.
Quick check
?Two identical slits each have width a, and their centers are a distance d = 3a apart. Monochromatic light passes through them onto a distant screen. Counting outward from the central maximum (order 0), which is the first bright-fringe order that is missing from the pattern?
Mission check

?Monochromatic light passes through two slits 0.20 mm apart and falls on a screen 1.5 m away. The centers of the two third-order bright maxima, one on each side of the central maximum, are 18.0 mm apart. What is the wavelength of the light?

Fringe spacing = λ × distance to screen ÷ slit separation. A sub-micrometer λ, times meters, divided by a fraction of a millimeter, gives millimeters you can measure with a ruler.

Mission 6

Mission 6: How can a slide ruled with thousands of fine lines measure the wavelength of light?

Make a guess: How can a slide ruled with thousands of fine lines measure the wavelength of light?

Mission 6

What a grating is

  • A diffraction grating: a plate with many equally spaced, parallel slits (lines).
  • Slit spacing d = 1 ÷ (lines per meter). 300 lines per mm → d = 1/300 mm = 3.33 × 10⁻⁶ m.
  • Each slit diffracts, so light from all the slits overlaps and interferes.
  • Bright beams leave only at certain angles: where light from every slit arrives in phase.
  • The pattern combines the diffraction patterns of all the slits, superimposed.
Mission 6

Deriving the grating equation

  • Light hits the grating at normal incidence (straight on).
  • Look at light leaving two adjacent slits at angle θ. Extra path for the lower slit: d sin θ.
  • In phase when this path difference is a whole number of wavelengths:
  • ΔD = d sin θ = mλ, m = 0, 1, 2, … (the order).
  • If adjacent slits are in phase, all the slits are in phase.
  • m = 0: the zero order, straight through (θ = 0).
  • Highest order: sin θ cannot exceed 1, so m ≤ d/λ.
Mission 6

Worked example

  • 300 lines per mm, red laser λ = 633 nm.
  • d = 1 ÷ (300 × 10³) = 3.33 × 10⁻⁶ m.
  • First order: sin θ = λ/d = 633 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ 3.33 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.190 → θ = 10.9°.
  • Second order: sin θ = 2λ/d = 0.380 → θ = 22.3°.
  • Highest order: d/λ = 5.27 → m = 5 (at 71.7°). Total beams: 5 + 5 + 1 = 11.
  • No small-angle shortcut: angles are large. Use sin θ.
  • On a screen a distance L away, a spot y from the center is at tan θ = y/L. Find θ first, then use sin θ.
Quick check
?Light of wavelength 600 nm is incident normally on a diffraction grating that has 800 lines per millimeter. At what angle from the normal is the second-order maximum?
Quick check
?Predict first. Monochromatic light is incident normally first on a double slit and then on a diffraction grating whose adjacent slits have the same separation d as the double slit. Every slit is equally illuminated. Compared with the double-slit fringes, how do the grating's principal maxima appear?
Mission 6

Why use many slits?

  • Same d, same λ: grating maxima are at the same angles as double-slit fringes.
  • But with many slits, a tiny step away from those angles puts the slits out of step and they cancel.
  • So the maxima are very sharp and very bright, with darkness between. Angles can be measured precisely.
  • Smaller d (more lines per mm) → orders farther apart.
Mission 6

White light and spectra

  • Zero order is white: every wavelength has θ = 0 there.
  • Each other order is a spectrum: violet (short λ) nearest the center, red farthest, since sin θ ∝ λ.
  • Higher orders spread wider and can overlap: second-order red (2 × 700 = 1400 nm) lies beyond third-order violet (3 × 400 = 1200 nm).
Quick check
?A lamp emits only blue light of wavelength 450 nm and red light of wavelength 650 nm. The light passes through a diffraction grating. The figure shows the central maximum C and the three bright lines nearest to it on one side of the pattern, J, K and L. Which line is the first-order maximum of the red light?
Mission check

?A laser beam is directed normally at a grating with 500 lines per mm. On a screen 1.00 m from the grating, the two second-order spots are 1.50 m apart. What is the wavelength of the laser light?

A grating turns a tiny, unmeasurable spacing into large, sharp angles. Measure the angle, know d, and λ follows from the grating equation.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Soap and water are both colorless. So where do the swirling colors on a soap bubble come from?

Make a guess: Soap and water are both colorless. So where do the swirling colors on a soap bubble come from?

Mission 7

Light at a boundary

  • When light meets a boundary between two media, it is shared three ways:
    • reflected (some),
    • transmitted (most, for clear glass or water),
    • absorbed (a little).
  • Even clear glass reflects a small fraction: that is why you see faint reflections in a window.
  • A thin film has two boundaries, so it sends back two reflected waves.
Mission 7

Phase change on reflection

  • Reflecting off a medium of greater index n: 180° phase change (the wave flips).
  • Reflecting off a medium of smaller n: no phase change.
  • Light transmitted (refracted) into a new medium: no phase change.
  • Like a pulse on a string: it flips when it reflects from a slower medium. Light is slower where n is greater.
Quick check
?The diagram shows light in air striking a thin film of oil floating on water at nearly normal incidence (the angles are exaggerated). Ray 1 is reflected from the top surface of the oil and ray 2 from the bottom surface. Which statement correctly compares the phase changes of the two rays on reflection?
Mission 7

Two reflections interfere

Normal incidence, film of thickness t and index n.

  • Wave 2 travels an extra 2t inside the film.
  • Inside the film the wavelength is λfilm = λ/n (λ in air).
  • Count the 180° flips:
    • 0 or 2 flips: constructive when 2t = mλfilm; destructive when 2t = (m + ½)λfilm.
    • 1 flip: the conditions swap: constructive when 2t = (m + ½)λfilm.
  • m = 0, 1, 2, … The two waves add to a single reflected wave.
  • Film must be thin (t comparable to λ). A 3 mm window pane shows no colors.
Mission 7

Worked example

Soap film (n = 1.33) in air. Thinnest film that reflects 600 nm light (in air) most strongly?

  • Top surface: air → soap, greater n: flip. Bottom: soap → air, smaller n: no flip. 1 flip.
  • So constructive needs 2t = ½λfilm (m = 0).
  • λfilm = 600/1.33 = 451 nm.
  • t = λfilm/4 = 600/(4 × 1.33) = 113 nm.
  • Next thickness that works: 2t = 1½λfilm → t = 338 nm.
Quick check
?A soap film (n = 1.33) in air is 115 nm thick. Light strikes it at normal incidence. What is the longest wavelength, measured in air, for which the light reflected from the film's two surfaces interferes constructively?
Quick check
?Predict first. A vertical soap film in a wire loop is lit by white light. The diagram shows the film from the side and from the front, where horizontal bands of different colors are seen. Which explanation of the bands is supported by the diagram?
Mission 7

Colors in bubbles and oil films

  • White light = all visible wavelengths.
  • At each thickness, some wavelengths reflect constructively, others destructively.
  • The film's thickness varies from place to place, so the color varies: bands and swirls.
  • Change the viewing angle and the path difference changes, so the color shifts.
  • No dyes, no prism: just two reflected waves adding.
Mission 7

Antireflection coatings

  • Coating on a lens, with nair < ncoating < nglass.
  • Both reflections are off a greater n: 2 flips, which cancel out.
  • Destructive when 2t = ½λcoating, so t = λ/(4ncoating): a quarter-wavelength coating (normal incidence).
  • Example: ncoating = 1.38, λ = 550 nm → t = 550/(4 × 1.38) = 99.6 nm.
Quick check
?A camera lens has an antireflection coating designed for green light, so much less green light is reflected from it than from an uncoated lens. What happens to the energy of the green light that is no longer reflected?
Mission check

?A soap film (n = 1.33) in air is lit at normal incidence by green light of wavelength 532 nm in air. Two regions of the film have thicknesses of 100 nm and 200 nm. Which comparison of the light reflected from the two regions, with its reasoning, is correct?

The color at each spot is set by its thickness: there, one wavelength's two reflections arrive in step. A bubble's wall varies in thickness, so it shows many colors at once.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Approaching → higher f, shorter λ; receding → lower f, longer λ; bigger relative speed → bigger shift.

Mission 2

  • Superposition: add displacements. Waves and pulses pass through each other unchanged.
  • Sources in phase: ΔD = mλ → constructive; (m + ½)λ → destructive.
  • Beats: fbeat = |f1 − f2|.

Mission 3

  • Standing wave: two identical waves traveling opposite ways; nodes λ/2 apart; no energy carried along.
  • Fixed–fixed string or open–open pipe: L = nλ/2, all harmonics. Node at one end, antinode at the other: odd harmonics only.
  • Speed on a string rises with tension and falls with mass per unit length.

Mission 4

  • Diffraction is significant when the gap or obstacle is comparable with λ, and greater the narrower the gap; λ itself does not change.
  • Single slit: wide, bright central maximum; narrower slit or longer λ → wider pattern.
  • Minima: a·ymin/L ≈ mλ; a double slit's fringes sit inside the single-slit envelope.

Mission 5

  • Two coherent slits → equally spaced bright and dark fringes; bright where path-length difference = mλ.
  • d sin θ = mλ; small angles: Δy = λL/d.
  • The single-slit pattern of each slit forms the envelope of the double-slit fringes.

Mission 6

  • Grating: many evenly spaced slits; maxima where d sin θ = mλ; white light gives a white center and spectra with red farthest out.

Mission 7

  • Reflection off a greater n flips the phase by 180°; off a smaller n, or on transmission, no flip.
  • Thin film, normal incidence: extra path 2t, λfilm = λ/n; with 1 flip, constructive when 2t = (m + ½)λfilm. Quarter-wave coating: t = λ/(4ncoating), with n between air and glass.
