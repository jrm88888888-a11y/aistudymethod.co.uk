Mission 1

Mission 1: When oxygen runs out, muscles make lactate and yeast makes alcohol. What do they gain from it?

Make a guess: When oxygen runs out, muscles make lactate and yeast makes alcohol. What do they gain from it?

Mission 1

Glycolysis on its own

  • Without oxygen, the mitochondrial stages stop. Only glycolysis still makes ATP: 2 ATP per glucose (net).
  • Anaerobic respiration happens in the cytoplasm only.
  • Human muscle: glucose → lactate. No carbon dioxide.
  • Yeast and plants: glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide.
  • Aerobic respiration yields many times more ATP per glucose.
  • Say: "anaerobic respiration uses no oxygen" — not: "it uses a little oxygen".
Mission 1

Recycling NAD

  • Glycolysis needs NAD to accept hydrogen at its oxidation step.
  • No oxygen → the electron transport chain stops → reduced NAD is not re-oxidised. NAD would soon run out, and glycolysis would stop.
  • Solution: pyruvate accepts the hydrogen from reduced NAD.
  • Animals: pyruvate → lactate. Pyruvate is reduced; NAD is regenerated.
  • Yeast and plants: pyruvate loses CO2, then the 2-carbon product is reduced → ethanol. NAD is regenerated.
  • The pathway is the same as in humans up to pyruvate. Only this last step differs.
  • These steps make no ATP. They keep glycolysis running, making its 2 ATP.
  • Say: "lactate formation regenerates NAD" — not: "lactate formation makes extra ATP".
Quick check
?During intense exercise, muscle cells convert pyruvate to lactate. What is the purpose of this conversion?
Mission 1Practical

Rate of respiration in yeast

  • AQA required practical 9: the effect of a named variable on the rate of respiration of cultures of single-celled organisms.
  • Yeast + buffered glucose solution in a flask, in a water bath. Equilibrate 10 min.
  • Anaerobic: boil and cool the glucose solution (removes dissolved oxygen); cover with a layer of oil.
  • Aerobic: leave the flask open to air, or bubble air through it.
  • Measure CO2: volume in a gas syringe at 1-minute intervals.
  • Change one variable (e.g. temperature, glucose concentration); keep yeast mass, volume and pH constant.
  • Control: flask with no yeast.
  • Rate = volume of CO2 ÷ time.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Pyruvate enters the mitochondrion with three carbon atoms. Where do they go, and what does the cell gain?

Make a guess: Pyruvate enters the mitochondrion with three carbon atoms. Where do they go, and what does the cell gain?

Mission 2

Inside the mitochondrion

  • Outer membrane: smooth; encloses the organelle.
  • Inner membrane: folded into cristae. Holds the electron carriers and ATP synthase.
  • Intermembrane space: the narrow gap between the two membranes.
  • Matrix: the fluid inside the inner membrane.
  • The matrix holds the enzymes of the link reaction and Krebs cycle.
Mission 2

The link reaction

  • Pyruvate enters the mitochondrial matrix by active transport.
  • In the matrix, pyruvate (3C) is:
  • decarboxylated: loses CO2 → a 2C acetyl group.
  • oxidised: loses hydrogen to NAD → reduced NAD.
  • Pyruvate is oxidised to acetate.
  • The acetyl group joins coenzyme A → acetyl coenzyme A (acetyl CoA).
  • Also written acetylcoenzyme A.
  • Coenzyme A is a carrier, not an enzyme. It delivers acetyl groups to the Krebs cycle.
  • Twice per glucose (two pyruvate).
Quick check
?What happens to pyruvate in the link reaction of aerobic cell respiration?
Mission 2

The Krebs cycle

  • Acetyl (2C) joins oxaloacetate (4C) → citrate (6C). Coenzyme A is released, to collect more.
  • Acetylcoenzyme A reacts with a four-carbon molecule to give a six-carbon molecule.
  • Citrate is converted back to oxaloacetate in a series of steps, each with its own enzyme:
  • 2 decarboxylations: 2 CO2 released.
  • 4 dehydrogenations (oxidations): 3 reduced NAD and 1 reduced FAD.
  • 1 ATP, made directly by substrate-level phosphorylation.
  • Oxaloacetate is regenerated, ready for the next acetyl group.
  • Two turns per glucose.
  • Say: "CO2 comes from decarboxylation" — not: "oxygen combines with carbon".
Quick check
?One acetyl group enters the Krebs cycle and oxaloacetate is regenerated. Which account of one turn of the cycle is correct?

  • Carbon check: glucose has 6 C. 2 leave in the link reaction + 4 in the Krebs cycle = 6. Every carbon leaves as CO2.

Mission 2

Why the cycle needs oxygen without using it

  • NAD and FAD accept hydrogen; coenzyme A carries acetyl groups. Both kinds are coenzymes, recycled again and again.
  • Reduced NAD and FAD are re-oxidised only when they hand on their hydrogen to the electron transport chain, which needs oxygen.
  • No oxygen → no NAD for the dehydrogenations → the link reaction and Krebs cycle stop.
  • Say: "the Krebs cycle depends on oxygen" — not: "the Krebs cycle uses oxygen".

Mission 3

Mission 3: How can a bead of liquid creeping along a thin tube show how fast seeds respire, and which fuel they use?

Make a guess: How can a bead of liquid creeping along a thin tube show how fast seeds respire, and which fuel they use?

Mission 3

Other fuels

  • Glucose and fatty acids are the main respiratory substrates. Many other carbon compounds can be respired.
  • Fatty acids are cut into many 2-carbon acetyl groups → acetyl coenzyme A → Krebs cycle.
  • Lipids are first hydrolysed to glycerol + fatty acids. Glycerol → triose phosphate → glycolysis.
  • Amino acids (from protein) are deaminated: the amino group is removed (→ urea, in the liver). The rest enters as pyruvate, acetyl coenzyme A or a Krebs cycle intermediate.
  • So the breakdown products of lipids and amino acids feed the Krebs cycle.
Mission 3Practical

The respirometer

  • AQA required practical 9: effect of a named variable on the rate of respiration of single-celled organisms (e.g. yeast in a respirometer).
  • Organisms (germinating seeds, woodlice or yeast) sit in a sealed tube, above potassium hydroxide solution (or soda lime), which absorbs CO2.
  • O2 is used → gas volume falls → the liquid bead in the capillary moves towards the organisms.
  • O2 volume = distance moved × πr² (r = capillary radius).
  • Control tube: glass beads of the same volume. It corrects for temperature and pressure changes.
  • Constant-temperature water bath; let the tubes equilibrate first; reset the bead with the syringe.
  • Without the alkali, the bead shows O2 used − CO2 released (the net change).
  • Carbohydrate: CO2 released = O2 used, so no net change. Lipid: less CO2 than O2, so the bead still moves towards the organisms. The net change hints at the fuel.
Mission 3

Worked example: rate of oxygen uptake

Illustrative data: 2.0 g germinating seeds, 10 min, 25 °C, capillary diameter 1.0 mm. Bead moved 24 mm towards the seeds; the control's bead moved 2 mm the same way.

  • Step 1, correct with the control: 24 − 2 = 22 mm.
  • Step 2, volume: r = 0.50 mm, so π × 0.50² × 22 = 17.3 mm³ O2.
  • Step 3, rate: 17.3 ÷ 2.0 g ÷ 10 min = 0.86 mm³ g⁻¹ min⁻¹.
  • Always divide by time and by mass, so different samples can be compared.
Quick check

With the alkali, the bead measures oxygen uptake: the rate. Without it, it measures the balance of the two gases, and that balance points to the fuel.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Energy flows along every food chain but never comes back. So where does it start, and where does it go?

Make a guess: Energy flows along every food chain but never comes back. So where does it start, and where does it go?

Mission 4

Energy in, energy out

  • Sunlight is the source of energy for almost every ecosystem.
  • Producers (plants, algae) capture light and fix carbon dioxide from the air or water into organic compounds.
  • That energy is now chemical energy in carbon compounds.
  • It passes along food chains when one organism eats another.
  • At every step, respiration releases energy that leaves as heat. Heat cannot be captured again.
  • Decomposers feed on dead matter and faeces, and respire too.
  • So energy flows through an ecosystem, one way. It must be resupplied all the time.
  • Say: "energy is transferred, and lost as heat" — not: "energy is used up".
Mission 4

Food chains and food webs

  • Food chain: one feeding pathway, e.g. grass → rabbit → fox.
  • Each arrow points from the food to the feeder: the direction energy and biomass move.
  • Food web: many linked chains in one community.
  • Say: "the arrow means 'is eaten by'" — not: "the arrow means 'eats'".
Quick check
?What do the arrows in a food web represent?
Mission 4

Trophic levels

  • A trophic level is a feeding position in a food chain.
  • Level 1: producers. Level 2: primary consumers (herbivores). Level 3: secondary consumers. Level 4: tertiary consumers.
  • An animal with a varied diet sits at different levels in different chains.
  • Count from the producer: the producer is not a consumer.
Quick check
?In a garden, blackbirds eat berries from shrubs and also eat snails, which feed on the leaves of the same shrubs. Which trophic level or levels does the blackbird occupy?
Mission check

?In autumn, an oak tree drops its leaves. Over the next year, fungi and bacteria break the leaves down. What happens to the carbon and to the energy that were in the leaves?

Matter goes round; energy goes through. Sunlight in, heat out: that is why every food chain needs the sun, or another outside source, all the time.

Mission 5

Mission 5: A caterpillar eats a whole leaf. Why does so little of that leaf end up as caterpillar?

Make a guess: A caterpillar eats a whole leaf. Why does so little of that leaf end up as caterpillar?

Mission 5

Gross and net primary production

  • Producers use light energy to fix CO2 into sugars.
  • Most of the sugar is respired by the plant itself. The rest builds other molecules: the plant's biomass.
  • Gross primary production (GPP): the chemical energy stored by photosynthesis in plant biomass, in a given area or volume.
  • R: the energy the plants lose in respiration.
  • Net primary production: NPP = GPP − R.
  • NPP is what is left for plant growth and reproduction, and for consumers and decomposers.
  • Say: "producers capture and store energy" — not: "producers make energy".
Mission 5

Production is a rate

  • The biomass present now is a stock: e.g. g m⁻² or kJ m⁻².
  • Productivity is the rate of production: per area per unit time.
  • e.g. kJ ha⁻¹ year⁻¹ (1 ha = 10 000 m²).
  • Primary production: by producers. Secondary production: by consumers.
  • Say: "productivity is a rate" — not: "productivity is the biomass present".
Mission 5

Measuring biomass and its energy

  • Water content varies, so measure dry mass.
  • Dry samples at about 80 °C; reweigh until the mass is constant.
  • Biomass can also be given as mass of carbon.
  • Express per area: sample with quadrats, then scale up (g m⁻²).
  • Energy in dry biomass: calorimetry. Burn a sample; the energy released heats a known mass of water.
  • Energy released = mass of water × 4.18 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹ × temperature rise.
  • Drawbacks: organisms are killed; a few samples stand for a whole area.
Mission 5

Worked example: NPP

A grassland (illustrative data): GPP = 24 000 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹. Plant respiration, R = 14 400 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹.

  • Step 1: NPP = GPP − R = 24 000 − 14 400 = 9 600 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹.
  • Step 2: share of GPP left as NPP = 9 600 ÷ 24 000 × 100 = 40%. The plants respired 60%.
  • Step 3: per hectare: 9 600 × 10 000 = 9.6 × 10⁷ kJ ha⁻¹ year⁻¹.
  • Keep the units: per area, per year, at every step.

Mission 5

Where a consumer's food goes

  • Energy in the food eaten (ingested): I.
  • Part is never absorbed: lost in faeces (egestion).
  • Part of what is absorbed leaves in urine (excretion, e.g. urea).
  • Most of the absorbed energy is respired (R) and lost as heat.
  • Only the rest becomes new biomass: the consumer's net production.
  • N = I − (F + R), where F = energy lost in faeces and urine.
  • Example (illustrative): a bullock eats grass holding 100 kJ; 64 kJ is lost in faeces and urine; R = 32 kJ. Net production = 100 − (64 + 32) = 4 kJ.
Quick check
?A caterpillar ingests leaf material containing 100 kJ of energy. It egests 50 kJ in faeces, releases 35 kJ as heat through cell respiration and stores 15 kJ in new biomass. What happens to the energy in the faeces?
Mission 5

Why so much is lost

  • Not eaten: roots, wood, bones. Dead remains go to decomposers.
  • Not digested: lost in faeces. Cellulose is hard to digest, so herbivores lose much more this way than carnivores.
  • Excreted: nitrogenous waste such as urea, in urine.
  • Respired: every organism, plants included, respires; most absorbed energy ends up as heat.
  • Endotherms (mammals, birds) respire far more to keep warm, so less of their food becomes biomass.
  • Say: "energy is lost to the surroundings as heat" — not: "energy is used up".
Quick check
?Why is much less energy available to the secondary consumers in a food chain than to the primary consumers?

Mission check

Like the caterpillar, the mouse loses most of its food in waste and as heat from respiration. Only the small remainder becomes body, and only that can feed the next level.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Why are top predators so rare, and why does eating lower in a food chain feed more people?

Make a guess: Why are top predators so rare, and why does eating lower in a food chain feed more people?

Mission 6

Less at every level

  • Each trophic level passes on only a small share of its energy: often around 10%, measured from about 5% to 20%.
  • After 3 or 4 transfers (4 or 5 levels), too little is left to support another population.
  • So top predators are few, and each needs a large area to find enough food.
  • Eat or harvest lower in a chain and fewer transfers come between the producers and you: more of their energy reaches you as food.
  • Say: "less energy reaches each higher level" — not: "top predators hold the most energy".
Quick check
?Food chains in most ecosystems have no more than four or five trophic levels. What is the reason for this restriction?
Mission 6

Farming: send more energy to us

  • Farmers cannot make more sunlight fall on a field. They steer more of the energy their crops capture into human food.
  • Simplify the food web: less energy flows into chains that do not end in humans.
  • Herbicides kill weeds, which compete with the crop for light, water and ions.
  • Insecticides and fungicides kill pests and pathogens that would eat or infect the crop.
Mission 6

Farming: cut respiratory losses

  • Livestock respire much of their food away. Cut that loss, and more becomes meat, milk or eggs.
  • Confinement in pens or sheds: less movement, so less energy respired.
  • Warm housing: less energy spent keeping warm (they are endotherms).
  • Harvest young: growing animals convert more of their food into biomass.
  • These methods raise animal-welfare and disease-spread concerns; questions may ask you to weigh them against the gains.
  • Say: "less energy is lost as heat in respiration" — not: "the animals make more energy".
Quick check
?Young cattle are kept in warm sheds with little room to move. Why does this raise the share of their food energy that becomes meat?
Mission check

?In a lake, the main food chain is phytoplankton → zooplankton → small fish → pike. A fishery could harvest either the small fish or the pike. Which harvest can be the larger each year, and why?

Every step up a chain loses most of its energy, mainly as heat. That keeps top predators rare, and it is why shorter chains, and lower respiratory losses, give more food.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Nearly four-fifths of the air is nitrogen. So why do crops so often run short of it?

Make a guess: Nearly four-fifths of the air is nitrogen. So why do crops so often run short of it?

Mission 7

Atoms go round; energy flows through

  • Every element an organism needs is recycled: carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus and the rest.
  • Energy is not recycled. It leaves each trophic level as heat.
  • Decomposers break down dead matter and wastes. They return elements as simple inorganic ions that plants can absorb.
  • These decomposers are called saprobionts: mainly bacteria and fungi.
Mission 7

How decomposers feed

  • Saprobiotic nutrition: feeding on dead or waste organic matter.
  • Step 1: secrete enzymes onto the food.
  • Step 2: extracellular digestion: large molecules hydrolysed outside the cells.
  • Step 3: absorb the small, soluble products.
  • Fungal hyphae spread through the food: large surface area for secretion and absorption.
  • Proteins and other nitrogen compounds end up as ammonium; phosphates are released as phosphate ions.
Quick check
?Which statement about the recycling of chemical elements in ecosystems is correct?
Mission 7

Nitrogen cycle 1: getting nitrogen in

  • The air is about 78% N2. Its triple bond is very strong: plants cannot use N2.
  • Nitrogen fixation: bacteria reduce N2 to ammonia (NH3).
  • Ammonification: saprobionts turn the nitrogen in proteins, nucleic acids and urea of dead matter and wastes into ammonia / ammonium ions (NH4⁺).
Mission 7

Nitrogen cycle 2: nitrate, and back to the air

  • Nitrification: ammonium → nitrite (NO2⁻) → nitrate (NO3⁻). An oxidation: needs oxygen.
  • Plants absorb nitrate (and ammonium) by active transport, then make amino acids and nucleotides.
  • Denitrification: bacteria convert nitrate back to N2. Happens in anaerobic soil, e.g. waterlogged.
  • Say: "nitrifying bacteria make nitrate from ammonium" — not: "nitrifying bacteria fix nitrogen".
Quick check
?A field is flooded for several weeks and the soil becomes waterlogged. Afterwards, the nitrate content of the soil is much lower. Which process best explains the loss?
Mission 7

Partners that feed plants

  • Mutualism: a relationship between two species in which both benefit.
  • Root nodules of legumes (peas, beans, clover: the Fabaceae): Rhizobium bacteria fix N2. Plant gets ammonia / amino acids; bacteria get sugars and shelter.
  • Mycorrhizae: fungi living with plant roots. Hyphae spread far into the soil: a huge surface area for absorbing water and inorganic ions, especially phosphate. The plant supplies sugars.
  • Say: "bacteria in the nodules fix nitrogen" — not: "legumes fix nitrogen themselves".
Quick check
?What are the benefits to each partner in the relationship between a legume and the bacteria in its root nodules?
Mission 7

The phosphorus cycle

  • Phosphorus is in ATP, DNA, RNA and phospholipids.
  • Weathering of rocks releases phosphate ions (PO4³⁻) into soil and water.
  • Producers absorb phosphate (helped by mycorrhizae); consumers get it by eating.
  • Excretion and decomposition return phosphate to the soil.
  • Some is washed into the sea and forms sediments, which become rock over millions of years.
  • No major gas phase: unlike carbon and nitrogen, there is no atmospheric reservoir. Phosphate returns to soil only slowly.
Mission 7

Why farms need fertiliser

  • Harvesting crops and removing livestock removes the nitrogen and phosphorus in their biomass. It is not returned to the field by decomposers.
  • Fertilisers replace the lost nitrates and phosphates.
  • Natural (organic): manure, slurry, compost. Decomposers release ions slowly.
  • Artificial (inorganic): e.g. ammonium nitrate. Soluble, fast-acting, precise amounts.
  • More fertiliser gives higher yields only up to a point: above that, extra fertiliser is wasted or washed away.
Mission 7

Leaching and eutrophication

  • Leaching: rain washes soluble nitrate and phosphate out of the soil into rivers and lakes.
  • The nutrient no longer limits growth: algal bloom at the surface.
  • Algae block light; plants below die. Algae die too.
  • Aerobic bacteria (decomposers) multiply and decompose the dead matter.
  • Their respiration raises BOD (biochemical oxygen demand) and lowers dissolved oxygen.
  • Fish and other aerobic animals die.
  • Say: "BOD rises, so dissolved oxygen falls" — not: "BOD falls" or "the fertiliser poisons the fish".

Mission check

?A farmer grows clover, a legume, in a field for a year, then ploughs the clover into the soil. Wheat sown next needs much less nitrogen fertiliser than wheat sown after wheat. Which explanation is correct?

Air is full of nitrogen, but plants can use it only after microorganisms have converted it. That is why crops run short.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Without oxygen only glycolysis makes ATP (net 2 per glucose); lactate (animals) or ethanol and carbon dioxide (yeast, plants) are the products.
  • Making lactate or ethanol regenerates NAD from reduced NAD, so glycolysis can continue.

Mission 2

  • Link reaction: pyruvate is decarboxylated and oxidised to an acetyl group, carried by coenzyme A. Krebs cycle: acetyl + oxaloacetate → citrate; 2 CO2, 3 reduced NAD, 1 reduced FAD, 1 ATP per turn; oxaloacetate regenerated.

Mission 3

  • A respirometer with an alkali measures O2 uptake: rate = corrected volume ÷ mass ÷ time; without the alkali it shows O2 used − CO2 released.
  • Lipids and amino acids enter respiration mostly via acetyl coenzyme A and the Krebs cycle; lipids give about twice the energy per gram.

Mission 4

  • Energy enters as light (or, for chemoautotrophs, from oxidising inorganic substances), passes along food chains in carbon compounds, and leaves as heat; it is never recycled.
  • Trophic level is a feeding position; one species can occupy several. Decomposers feed on dead matter from every level.

Mission 5

  • NPP = GPP − R; productivity is a rate per area per time; efficiency = energy (or biomass) at a level ÷ that at the level below × 100.
  • Consumers lose energy in faeces, urine and, above all, as heat from respiration; only new biomass passes on.

Mission 6

  • Most energy is lost at each transfer, so higher levels hold less energy and biomass, and chains rarely exceed four or five levels. Harvesting lower in a chain gives more food energy.
  • Farming increases food output by simplifying food webs and cutting respiratory losses of livestock.

Mission 7

  • Elements are recycled by microorganisms; energy is not. Plants cannot use N2 until bacteria fix it.
  • Fixation (N2 → NH3/NH4⁺), ammonification (organic N → NH4⁺), nitrification (NH4⁺ → NO2⁻ → NO3⁻, aerobic), denitrification (NO3⁻ → N2, anaerobic).
  • Leached nutrients → algal bloom → decomposition → BOD up, oxygen down → fish die.
