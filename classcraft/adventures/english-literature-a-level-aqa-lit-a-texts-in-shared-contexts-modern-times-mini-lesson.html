Texts in shared contexts: Modern times

Paper 2: Texts in shared contexts — Option 2B, Modern times: literature from 1945 to the present day. The shared context is the post-war world: the welfare state and its retreat, the end of empire, class mobility and its costs, the transformation of gender and sexuality, migration, consumerism, and the fracturing of a shared national story.

  • Paper 2 structure: Section A — one essay on a set text (25). Section B — contextual linking: a compulsory question on an unseen extract (25) and an essay linking two texts (25).
  • Your texts: one prose, one poetry and one drama text, of which one must be written post-2000.
  • Remember the either/or: Paper 2 is Modern times OR WW1 and its aftermath. You sit one option, never both.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go and collect ⭐ stars. Named texts appear only as swappable exemplars — the skills are what the exam rewards, and set-text lists change. Press Start when you are ready.

The field · 1

The post-war settlement and its discontents

The shared context begins in 1945 with a promise: a welfare state, mass education, full employment, a Britain fairer than the one that went to war. Post-war literature is very largely the record of what happened to that promise.

  • The disappointed 1950s — the so-called Angry Young Men: a generation educated by the state into an articulacy it had nowhere to spend. The characteristic emotion is rage without a target, and the characteristic figure is a man who has escaped his class and despises everyone, including himself, for it.
  • The scholarship boy — a recurring and deeply examinable figure: education as a ladder that removes you from your family and does not admit you to the class above. Richard Hoggart's The Uses of Literacy (1957) named the type; poets and novelists have been writing it ever since.
  • Affluence and consumerism — the fridge, the car, the television, the supermarket. Prosperity presented as liberation and analysed, repeatedly, as a new kind of confinement.
  • Deindustrialisation and its wreckage — the closure of the pits, the mills and the yards; communities whose entire meaning was their work; the literature of what is left afterwards.
  • Post-1979 and after — the market as the organising fact; the retreat of the state; precarity, debt and insecure work in the most recent writing.

Exemplar, not syllabus: writers named here only illustrate the field. AQA revises its set-text lists — work from the texts your class studies. The paper examines your grasp of the shared context, not a fixed reading list.

The field · 2

Empire, migration and who counts as British

The other half of 1945 is the end of empire — and the arrival, in Britain, of the people the empire had ruled.

  • The Windrush generation and after — invited to fill labour shortages, and met with a hostility the invitation had not mentioned. The literature of arrival is a literature of double consciousness: to be legally British and socially foreign at the same time.
  • Writing back — a novel or poem that takes the centre's story and retells it from the margin. Much of the strongest post-war British writing is an argument with the version of Britain the reader was taught.
  • Language as territory — the use of Creole, patois, dialect and code-switching, not as local colour but as a claim: this is a literary language, and standard English is not the only one. When a poem refuses standard orthography, that is an argument about who owns the page.
  • The second and third generations — born here, belonging incompletely, negotiating identities the first generation did not have to name. Much post-2000 writing lives here.
  • Race, policing and the city — the urban novel as a record of who is stopped, who is watched, and who is at home.

The post-colonial insight that pays: in this period, the colony arrives at the metropolis. Post-colonial reading is therefore not an exotic add-on to modern British literature — it is a description of what modern Britain is.

The field · 3

Gender, sexuality and the private made political

  • The domestic as a battlefield — post-war women's writing takes the kitchen, the pregnancy, the marriage and the unpaid work and insists that they are political subjects, not the background to them.
  • Second-wave feminism — from the 1960s: contraception, abortion, equal pay, divorce reform, the naming of domestic violence. Literature both records these changes and is one of the places they were argued for.
  • Sexuality and the law — writing before, during and after decriminalisation. Before it, desire is written in code, and the code is analysable: what a text cannot say directly shapes its form.
  • The body — illness, ageing, abortion, appetite, labour. Modern writing is far more physically explicit than earlier literature, and the explicitness is itself a claim about what may be said.
  • Identity as constructed — the late-century recognition that class, gender, race and nation are things people perform and are assigned, not essences they contain. Much post-2000 writing takes this as its starting assumption.

The methodological point: in this option, private life is never merely private. A marriage in a 1960s novel is a legal and economic arrangement under active revision, and reading it as a purely emotional matter throws away the shared context you are being examined on.

AO2 · method

How modern literature works

The period's formal signature is fragmentation and self-consciousness. AO2 here means being precise about it.

  • The unreliable and the partial narrator — no authoritative voice; a world in which nobody has the whole story. Not a stylistic tic but the shared context expressed as form.
  • Multiple voices and polyphony — several narrators, several dialects, no arbitration between them. Formally democratic; often, on inspection, not quite.
  • Non-linear time — flashback, fracture, the withheld event. Trauma, memory and migration all produce it.
  • Metafiction — the text that admits it is a text. In post-2000 writing this often carries a moral argument: who has the right to tell this story?
  • Realism, and its return — do not assume experiment is the whole picture. Much of the period's most politically serious writing is plainly, deliberately realist, because realism makes a claim: this is happening, and you can see it.
  • Register and vernacular — the working-class voice, the migrant voice, the regional voice, admitted into literary language on their own terms rather than as comic relief.
  • Drama's specific tools — the fourth wall, direct address, the naturalist box set and its destruction, the shift from drawing-room to kitchen to bare stage. If one of your texts is a play, write about the stage, not just about the words.

AO5

Reading modern times through theory

  • Marxist — the most obviously productive lens here. Who works, what happens when the work disappears, and how does the text handle the difference between poverty and precarity? Also: what does the text think the working class is for — is it a subject, or a spectacle for a middle-class reader?
  • Feminist — the domestic as a site of labour; the distribution of interiority; the difference between a novel about a woman and a novel that thinks with her.
  • Post-colonial — the empire arriving home; hybridity and double consciousness; who is permitted to be the narrator of Britain.
  • Eco-critical — the post-industrial landscape, the poisoned river, the motorway, climate anxiety in the most recent writing. Late twentieth-century nature writing is not pastoral: it is an inventory of damage.
  • Postmodern — the loss of grand narratives; suspicion of any single story that claims to explain everything. Useful — but do not let it become an excuse for saying a text means nothing. It usually means something quite specific about why meaning has become hard.

The rule that never changes: one lens, one textual claim. Not a Marxist would focus on class, but the novel gives its factory forty pages and its closure a single paragraph — the prose has the same attention span as the economy it is describing.

Section B

The unseen extract: contextual linking

Section B begins with a compulsory question on an unseen extract, which you must relate to your understanding of the shared context of modern times.

  1. Triage (three minutes). When, roughly, does this feel like it was written — and, crucially, what is it taking for granted? A text that assumes full employment, or that assumes precarity, is dating itself for you.
  2. Locate the strand. Class and work? Empire and migration? Gender and sexuality? Consumerism? Most extracts touch more than one, and saying which are in tension is a strong thesis.
  3. Analyse the method — voice, register, form, structure, what the text can and cannot say — and connect each observation to the context it reveals.
  4. Link outward. The question is about the shared context: this extract's version of the post-war world sits alongside, or against, the versions in your set texts.
  5. Infer, never invent. Do not name an author or a date you cannot verify. Careful hedging is credited; false certainty is not.

The observation that almost always pays: listen to the register. Whose English is treated as normal in this extract, and whose is marked as different? That single question opens class, region, race and education at once.

Section B

The linking essay: two texts, one context

The second Section B task links two texts through the shared context. AO3 and AO4 must work together.

  1. Open on the context: The post-war settlement promised that class would stop determining a life. These two texts, forty years apart, are both about the discovery that it had merely changed the way it did so.
  2. Idea-paragraphs across both texts: the work; the family; the language; the ending's verdict on whether change is possible.
  3. Use the genre difference. A play stages class in bodies and space; a novel can go inside a head; a poem can compress an entire economy into an image. That difference is evidence, not an inconvenience.
  4. Use the post-2000 text properly. It is not simply the modern one — it is the text that knows how the story turned out, and its knowledge changes its method.

Time discipline: three tasks, 25 marks each. The unseen must not swallow the linking essay.
