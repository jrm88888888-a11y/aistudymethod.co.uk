What is a language discourse?

  • A discourse is a recurring way of talking about a topic, with its own words, images and assumptions.
  • Common discourses about language: decline (English is getting worse), golden age (it was better once), purity (outside influences spoil it), change as natural (the linguist's view).
  • AQA asks how such texts represent language, construct an identity for the writer, and position the reader.

Writing the linguist's reply

  • Write for non-specialists: explain any term in a phrase (descriptivists, linguists who study how people really speak).
  • Answer the column's claims with evidence: complaints about decline go back centuries (Swift proposed an academy to fix English in 1712), and meanings have always shifted (nice once meant "foolish").
  • Explain what the features do: abbreviations save time; like can introduce reported speech or soften a claim.
  • Keep the genre: headline, a hooking opening, a confident but fair tone.

How your exam asks this

  • Paper 2, Section B: two texts on a language topic. One question asks how they use language to present ideas, attitudes and opinions (40 marks); then directed writing on the same topic, e.g. an opinion article (30 marks).
  • Essay practice is for class; this mission builds the habits both tasks reward.

Reply to the column's claim that teenagers are ruining English. Write for readers with no linguistics training, and use at least one piece of evidence about language change.

Model answer

Every generation thinks the young are wrecking English. In 1712 Jonathan Swift wanted an academy to stop English changing, and it carried on changing anyway. Linguists, who study how people actually speak, see words like innit not as grunts but as tools: they help a speaker sound friendly and show which group they belong to. Language isn't crumbling. It's doing what it has always done: adapting to the people who use it.

How and why change happens

TheoryThe ideaExample
S-curvea change starts slowly, spreads rapidly, then slows as the last users adopt ita new word in a community
Random fluctuationchange has no clear cause, like fashionshifting slang
Functional theorylanguage changes to meet its users' needsnew words for new technology
Substratum theoryfeatures of a language people used to speak carry into their new oneIrish English I'm after eating ("I've just eaten"), modelled on Irish
  • Literally fits a regular pattern: intensifiers wear out and are replaced. Very once meant "true".

Aitchison's three metaphors

In her 1996 BBC Reith Lectures, The Language Web, Jean Aitchison named three common worries about change:

MetaphorThe worryHer reply, in outline
Damp spoon syndromechange comes from laziness, like leaving a damp spoon in the sugar bowlmany changes add effort or precision
Crumbling castleEnglish was once perfect and is now falling apartthere was never a finished golden age
Infectious diseasebad usage is caught from others and must be resistedpeople adopt new forms to fit in with a group

Mission checkScreen 9

?Is literally meaning "figuratively" a decline, or just change?

Linguists call it change. Literally has been used for emphasis since at least the 19th century, as Chopin's 1899 novel shows, and intensifiers often weaken this way. Calling it a decline is a prescriptive attitude; a descriptive answer looks at the evidence first.

Recap

Big ideas

  • A discourse is a recurring way of talking about language; decline, golden age and purity discourses are common in the media.
  • Analyse how a text represents language and its users, builds the writer's identity and positions the reader.
  • A linguist's reply is descriptive, evidence-based and written for non-specialists.
  • Prescriptivism judges usage against rules and sees change as decay; descriptivism records usage and sees change as natural.
  • Theories of change: the S-curve (how it spreads), random fluctuation, functional theory and substratum theory (why it happens).
  • Aitchison's metaphors (damp spoon, crumbling castle, infectious disease) name common worries, which she argued are mistaken.
