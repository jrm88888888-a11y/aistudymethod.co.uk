KQ1 · functionalism

Functionalism: education as the great sorter

  • Émile Durkheim — school creates social solidarity by transmitting the shared culture and history of society, and teaching children to co-operate with people who are not their kin. It is 'society in miniature': the child learns to obey impersonal rules, exactly as adult life will require. It also teaches the specialist skills a complex division of labour needs.
  • Talcott Parsons — school is the bridge between the family and wider society. The family judges the child by particularistic, ascribed standards; society judges by universalistic, achieved ones. School is where the switch happens, and it applies universal standards to everyone. It is therefore a meritocracy, and a mechanism of role allocation — sifting pupils and directing them towards appropriate jobs (compare Davis and Moore).

Evaluation — and this is the whole argument of the topic. Functionalism assumes meritocracy rather than proving it. If achievement is powerfully patterned by class, ethnicity and gender — and it is — then the sorting is not by merit alone. Marxists (Bowles and Gintis) argue meritocracy is a myth whose function is to make an unequal outcome look deserved. Functionalism also ignores the counter-school cultures Willis found, and cannot explain why a system supposedly transmitting shared values is so often rejected by those it fails.

KQ1 · Marxism

Marxism: reproducing class, disguising it as merit

  • Louis Althusser — education is the dominant Ideological State Apparatus in capitalist society. It does two jobs: it reproduces labour power (producing workers with the right skills), and it reproduces the ideology that makes the resulting inequality seem legitimate.
  • Bowles and Gintis (Schooling in Capitalist America, 1976) — the correspondence principle: the social relationships of school mirror those of the workplace. Hierarchy (head → teacher → pupil corresponds to boss → manager → worker); extrinsic rewards (grades for work you do not enjoy, exactly as wages are); fragmentation of knowledge; competition; and above all obedience. This is taught not in lessons but through the hidden curriculum — the rules, routines, bells and hierarchies. And they argue school promotes the myth of meritocracy: by persuading the failed that they failed fairly, it neutralises the resentment that inequality would otherwise produce.
  • Paul Willis (Learning to Labour, 1977) offers a neo-Marxist correction: the lads are not passively moulded. They see through the meritocratic promise and actively resist school — and, tragically, that resistance delivers them straight into the manual labour their fathers did. Reproduction happens through their agency, not despite it.

KQ1 · liberal, social democratic, New Right, feminist

Four more views of the role of education

  • Liberal. Education exists to develop the individual — to cultivate curiosity, autonomy, critical thought and personal potential, not merely to serve the economy. Child-centred, progressive teaching flows from this. Critique: optimistic and largely silent about the structures that constrain the individual it wishes to liberate.
  • Social democratic. Education should deliver equality of opportunity, and the state must actively intervene to make that real. This is the tradition behind the comprehensive system (abolishing selection at 11), and behind sociologists such as A. H. Halsey, whose research (Origins and Destinations, 1980, with Heath and Ridge) documented how powerfully class origin still shaped destination. Critique: Marxists argue reform within capitalism can never deliver equality; the New Right argues state provision is inefficient.
  • New Right. The state runs education badly: it is monopolistic, unresponsive and produces low standards. The solution is a market: give parents choice, force schools to compete, publish results, and let failing schools fail. Standards will rise. This is the ideology behind marketisation from 1988 onwards.
  • Feminism. Education has historically reproduced patriarchy — through the curriculum, through subject choice, through the hidden curriculum and through teacher expectations. Liberal feminists point to enormous progress (girls now outperform boys at GCSE); radical feminists reply that the deeper structures persist — male-dominated senior leadership, gendered subject choice channelling girls away from engineering and physics, and sexual harassment in school corridors.

KQ1 · education and work

Education, work and vocationalism

OCR asks specifically about the relationship between education and work, and about vocationalism post-1988.

  • Functionalism — education supplies the specialist skills a modern economy needs and allocates people to roles by merit. Vocational training is therefore a rational match of person to job.
  • Marxism — the correspondence principle. What education really supplies is not skills but attitudes: obedience, punctuality, acceptance of hierarchy. Dan Finn's critique of youth training schemes (1987) argued they served to provide employers with cheap labour, to keep young people off the unemployment register, and to instil discipline — rather than to deliver genuine skills or jobs.
  • New Right — schools have failed to produce work-ready young people, so education must be tied more tightly to the needs of employers. Hence the growth of vocational qualifications, apprenticeships and work-based training since 1988.
  • The critique from all sides: vocational routes have been persistently seen as lower status than academic ones, and are taken disproportionately by working-class pupils — so a system intended to widen opportunity can end up formalising a class-divided pathway. This is the 'parity of esteem' problem, and it has never been solved.
KQ2/3 · social class

Class: outside-school factors

The class gap in attainment is the most persistent finding in the sociology of education. OCR asks you to divide explanations into material and cultural, outside and inside school.

  • Material deprivation. Halsey, Heath and Ridge (1980) found material factors central. Poor housing (no quiet space, overcrowding, illness), poor diet, no computer or internet, no money for trips, uniform, tutoring or revision guides, and the pressure to take paid work. Smith and Noble (1995) describe these as barriers to learning. Note the honest complication: material and cultural factors are entangled, because poverty shapes what parents can do.
  • Cultural capital. Pierre Bourdieu — middle-class children arrive already fluent in the culture the school rewards: the vocabulary, the confidence, the museums, the books, the expectation of being listened to. Their habitus matches the school's. Working-class children face what Bourdieu calls symbolic violence — their own culture is treated as a deficiency. Crucially, the school does not create the advantage; it converts it into qualifications, and then calls the result merit.
  • Speech codes. Basil Bernstein distinguished the restricted code (context-bound, shorter, assumes shared understanding) from the elaborated code (context-free, explicit, elaborated). Schools and exams operate entirely in the elaborated code, which middle-class children already speak. Bernstein is careful — this is a mismatch, not a deficiency, and he blamed the school as much as the home.
  • Cultural deprivation theory. J. W. B. Douglas (1964) linked achievement to parental interest; Barry Sugarman (1970) claimed working-class subculture involved fatalism, collectivism, immediate gratification and present-time orientation. Handle with care — this tradition has been heavily criticised for blaming the victim, for confusing lack of resources with lack of interest, and for measuring 'parental interest' by attendance at parents' evenings, which working-class parents on shift work cannot easily attend.

KQ3 · inside-school processes

Inside school: labelling, setting and subcultures

  • Labelling. Howard Becker (1971), from interviews with Chicago teachers, found they judged pupils against an image of the 'ideal pupil' — defined by appearance, speech and conduct that were, in practice, middle-class. Working-class children were furthest from the ideal, and were labelled accordingly.
  • The self-fulfilling prophecy. Rosenthal and Jacobson's Pygmalion in the Classroom (1968) is the classic field experiment: they told teachers that certain randomly chosen pupils were 'spurters' likely to bloom. Those pupils subsequently made greater gains — even though the only thing that had changed was the teachers' expectations. The label became true because it was believed. (Methodological caveat: the study has been criticised on both its measures and its replicability — say so, and you have your AO3.)
  • Setting, streaming and polarisation. Colin Lacey (Hightown Grammar, 1970) described differentiation (teachers rank and categorise) leading to polarisation (pupils respond by splitting into a pro-school and an anti-school subculture). David Hargreaves (1967) and Stephen Ball (Beachside Comprehensive, 1981) found the same: those placed in low bands were taught less demanding material, given lower expectations, and formed counter-cultures that made a virtue of what they had been denied.
  • The A-to-C economy. Gillborn and Youdell (2000) identified educational triage: under league-table pressure, schools sort pupils into those who will pass anyway, those who are 'hopeless cases', and the borderline pupils on the C/D boundary — into whom resources are poured. Working-class and Black pupils were disproportionately consigned to the 'hopeless' category. Marketisation therefore does not simply fail to reduce inequality; it generates a new mechanism for producing it.

KQ2/3 · ethnicity

Ethnicity and achievement

The first rule: never generalise about 'ethnic minorities'. Attainment varies enormously between minority ethnic groups — Chinese and Indian pupils have long been among the highest attainers, while some other groups have persistently lagged — and within them, by class and gender. Any answer treating minorities as one bloc has already lost marks.

  • Outside school. Material deprivation is a major driver: some minority ethnic groups are disproportionately in poverty, so what looks like an ethnic effect is partly a class effect. Cultural explanations (family structure, parental attitudes) exist but must be handled sceptically — they slide easily into blaming the community rather than examining the school.
  • Inside school — institutional racism. Bernard Coard's pamphlet (1971) argued that the British school system was making West Indian children 'educationally subnormal' through a curriculum, expectations and assessment practices that devalued them. Decades later, David Gillborn argues that institutional racism persists: through the ethnocentric curriculum, through disproportionate exclusions, through low teacher expectations, and through setting decisions that place Black pupils in lower sets than their prior attainment warrants.
  • Pupil responses vary. Tony Sewell (1997) found Black boys responding to school in several distinct ways, from conformity to outright rebellion, and argued that a minority adopted an anti-school 'street' masculinity that teachers then read as a threat — a reading applied to all Black boys, including the conformists. Mary Fuller's study (1980) found Black girls responding to negative labels not by rejecting school but by working harder — proving that a label does not automatically produce a self-fulfilling prophecy. Heidi Safia Mirza (Young, Female and Black, 1992) likewise found young Black women with high aspirations, held back not by their attitudes but by the school's.

KQ2/3 · gender

Gender: girls' rise, and the boy problem

  • Why girls improved. The women's movement raised expectations; Sue Sharpe's comparison of London girls found their priorities shifting from 'love, marriage and children' to careers and independence; the growth of service-sector jobs created real opportunities; equal opportunities policies (GIST, WISE) challenged gendered subject choice; and coursework — introduced with GCSEs — was argued to suit girls' patterns of working. Girls now outperform boys at GCSE across nearly all subjects.
  • Why boys fell behind. Mitsos and Browne (1998) point to the collapse of traditional male manual work — a 'crisis of masculinity' that removed the point of qualifications for many working-class boys. Carolyn Jackson (2006) analyses laddish behaviour as a defensive strategy: appearing not to work protects a boy from the humiliation of trying and failing. Mac an Ghaill's 'macho lads' and Willis's lads are the same logic in earlier decades.
  • The vital qualification — do NOT stop at the headline. Class remains the strongest predictor of achievement, far stronger than gender: a middle-class boy still typically outperforms a working-class girl. The 'boys' underachievement' framing generated a moral panic that concentrated attention on gender while the much larger class gap continued unexamined. Becky Francis and others also stress that girls' success at school does not translate into equal outcomes at work — subject choice remains heavily gendered, and the pay gap persists. Educational success has not dismantled patriarchy.

KQ2 · global educational inequality

Education in a global context

  • Global 'gender apartheid' in education — OCR names this explicitly. In some societies girls are systematically excluded from schooling, whether by law, by poverty, by early marriage, by unsafe journeys to school, by the absence of sanitation, or by armed opposition to girls' education. The campaigner Malala Yousafzai was shot in Pakistan in 2012 for advocating girls' education — the starkest possible illustration of education as a contested political right, not a neutral service.
  • Disparity in provision. Educational provision is drastically unequal across the world: class sizes, teacher training, buildings, books, electricity, and access to secondary and higher education. UNESCO consistently reports that many millions of children — disproportionately girls, disabled children, and children in conflict zones — remain out of school.
  • Theoretical readings. Modernisation theory sees education as the engine of development. Dependency and Marxist theorists reply that education in poorer countries often serves the interests of global capital — training a workforce for foreign firms, and exporting the most highly educated in a brain drain. Feminists see the exclusion of girls as patriarchy operating at global scale, and note that girls' education is one of the strongest predictors of development outcomes — which makes the exclusion doubly costly.
KQ4 · policy from 1988

Marketisation: the 1988 Education Reform Act onwards

OCR anchors this key question on 1988. Know what the Act did and what it was for.

  • The 1988 Education Reform Act introduced the National Curriculum, national testing, league tables, open enrolment (parental choice), formula funding (money follows the pupil) and local management of schools. Together these create a market: schools compete for pupils, because pupils bring money.
  • The ideology. This is a New Right programme — competition, choice and diversity will raise standards. Phillip Brown called the resulting system a parentocracy: education now depends less on the ability of the child than on the wealth and wishes of the parent.
  • What actually happened. Ball, Bowe and Gewirtz (1995) studied parental choice and found that the market is not used equally. They identified privileged-skilled choosers (middle-class parents with the cultural capital, information, transport and confidence to work the system), semi-skilled choosers (ambitious but lacking the know-how) and disconnected-local choosers (working-class parents for whom the local school is the only realistic option). Meanwhile popular schools cream-skim the most able pupils and silt-shift the difficult ones elsewhere. Choice, in short, is a resource — and it is unequally distributed.
  • Since 1988: specialist schools, academies, free schools, tuition fees, the pupil premium (a social-democratic counterweight targeting funding at disadvantaged pupils), and the raising of the participation age. Diversity of provision has genuinely increased — the question is whether it has increased equality.

The judgement to reach: marketisation has raised measured standards in some respects and increased diversity of provision — but it has also produced league-table gaming (triage), selection by mortgage, and a system in which the parents best able to choose are those who already had the most. Equality of opportunity is not the same as equality of outcome, and the market has delivered neither reliably.
