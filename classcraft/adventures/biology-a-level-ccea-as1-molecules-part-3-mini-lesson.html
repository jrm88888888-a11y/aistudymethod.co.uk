Mission 1

Mission 1: Before a cell divides, it copies all of its DNA. How does the copy come out almost perfect?

Make a guess: Before a cell divides, it copies all of its DNA. How does the copy come out almost perfect?

Mission 1

Why copy DNA?

  • Every cell made by mitosis needs a complete copy of the DNA.
  • So DNA is copied before every mitosis: for growth, repair and asexual reproduction.
  • It is also copied once before meiosis, the division that makes gametes.
  • Copying happens in interphase, before the cell divides.
  • The copies are exact: identical base sequences.
Quick check
?A cut in the skin heals as new skin cells are produced by cell division. Why must DNA replication take place before each of these divisions?
Mission 1

Replication, step by step

  • Helicase unwinds the double helix and breaks the hydrogen bonds between complementary bases. The strands separate.
  • Each separated strand is a template.
  • Free DNA nucleotides are attracted to the exposed bases and pair by complementary base pairing: A with T, C with G. Hydrogen bonds hold each pair.
  • DNA polymerase joins adjacent nucleotides of the new strand in condensation reactions, building its sugar–phosphate backbone.
  • Result: two molecules, each with one original strand and one new strand. This is semi-conservative replication.
  • Say: "helicase breaks the hydrogen bonds between bases" — not: "helicase cuts the DNA".
  • Say: "DNA polymerase joins nucleotides to each other" — not: "DNA polymerase pairs the bases". Base pairs form on their own.
Quick check
?A drug inhibits helicase but has no effect on DNA polymerase. What will happen to DNA replication in a cell treated with the drug?
Mission 1

Worked example: complementary, not identical

One template strand reads G A T T C A.

  • Pair each base: G→C, A→T, T→A, T→A, C→G, A→T.
  • New strand: C T A A G T.
  • The new strand is complementary to its template.
  • It is identical to the strand that was paired with this template before.
  • The other template is copied the same way. Both daughter molecules match the parent.
  • Pairing is set by hydrogen bonding: each base can pair stably only with its complement.
Quick check
?During replication, a section of a template strand with the base sequence TACGGA is copied. Which statement describes the new strand built on this section?
Mission check

?A short section of a DNA molecule has one strand reading GATTC, paired base by base with a second strand reading CTAAG. The molecule is replicated once. Which statement describes the two DNA molecules produced?

Each old strand is the pattern for its new partner, and base pairing makes that pattern hard to misread. That is why the copy is almost perfect.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How did one experiment with heavy nitrogen show that every new DNA molecule keeps half of the old one?

Make a guess: How did one experiment with heavy nitrogen show that every new DNA molecule keeps half of the old one?

Mission 2

Three possible models

  • Conservative: the parent molecule stays whole; the copy is all new.
  • Semi-conservative: each new molecule has one parent strand + one new strand.
  • Dispersive: old and new DNA are mixed in pieces along every strand.
  • In 1958, Meselson and Stahl designed an experiment that gave each model a different prediction.
Mission 2

The experiment

  • Bases contain nitrogen. ¹⁵N is a heavy isotope; ¹⁴N is the normal, light one.
  • E. coli were grown for many generations on ¹⁵N: all their DNA became heavy.
  • They were moved to a ¹⁴N medium. Every new strand made after that is light.
  • Samples were taken after each generation (one round of replication).
  • DNA was extracted and spun in a caesium chloride density gradient.
  • DNA settles where its density matches the solution: heavier DNA settles lower.
Mission 2

Read the data

2two bands, equal amounts: intermediate and light
  • Intermediate (hybrid) DNA = one ¹⁵N strand + one ¹⁴N strand.
  • Light DNA = two ¹⁴N strands.
  • Read each row as a test. Which models survive it?
Quick check
?Bacteria were grown for many generations with only heavy nitrogen (¹⁵N), so all their DNA was heavy. They were then moved to a medium with only light nitrogen (¹⁴N). After one round of replication, all the DNA had a density midway between heavy and light. After a second round, half the DNA was of this intermediate density and half was light. What do these results show about replication?
Mission 2

Which model fails, and when

  • Conservative predicts generation 1: two bands, heavy and light. Seen: one intermediate band. Refuted at generation 1.
  • Dispersive predicts generation 1: one intermediate band. That fits.
  • But dispersive predicts generation 2: still one band, lighter than intermediate. Seen: two bands. Refuted at generation 2.
  • Semi-conservative predicts both results. It is supported.
  • Say: "the results support the semi-conservative model" — not: "the results prove it".
Mission 2

Worked example: after n generations

Start: one ¹⁵N molecule. Grow it in ¹⁴N.

  • Molecules after n generations = 2ⁿ.
  • The two original ¹⁵N strands never break up. Each ends up in one hybrid molecule.
  • So there are always exactly 2 hybrid molecules; all the rest are light.

Quick check
?A double-stranded DNA molecule in which both strands contain only heavy nitrogen (¹⁵N) is replicated three times in a medium that supplies only light nitrogen (¹⁴N), producing 8 DNA molecules. How many of the 8 DNA molecules contain any ¹⁵N?
Mission 2

Judging the evidence

  • Each model made a different, testable prediction before the results were known.
  • The bands were sharp and clearly separated.
  • Pure ¹⁵N and pure ¹⁴N DNA gave reference positions for heavy and light.
  • Generation 2 was essential: generation 1 alone could not rule out dispersive.
  • The method and results were published in full (1958), so others could check and repeat them.
  • A limit: it was done in one bacterium. Other studies were needed to extend it to eukaryotes.
Quick check
?In the Meselson–Stahl experiment, which result after one generation in ¹⁴N would have supported the conservative model?
Mission check

?The experiment is run in reverse. Bacteria are grown for many generations on light nitrogen (¹⁴N), then moved to a medium containing only heavy nitrogen (¹⁵N). Replication is semi-conservative. What will the DNA show after two generations on ¹⁵N?

Change the labels and the logic still works. Each old strand survives intact, paired with a new partner, in every generation.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Many foods hide sugar, starch, protein or fat. How can a few drops of reagent tell you which, and how much?

Make a guess: Many foods hide sugar, starch, protein or fat. How can a few drops of reagent tell you which, and how much?

Mission 3

Starch: the iodine test

  • Add a few drops of iodine in potassium iodide solution.
  • Starch present: orange-brown → blue-black.
  • Works on solids or solutions.
  • Iodine slots inside the coiled amylose chain: that is what gives the colour.
Mission 3

Reducing sugars: Benedict's test

  • Reducing sugars: all monosaccharides, and the disaccharides maltose and lactose.
  • (1) Add an equal volume of Benedict's reagent (blue: Cu²⁺ ions).
  • (2) Heat in a boiling water bath for about 5 minutes.
  • (3) Reducing sugar reduces Cu²⁺ to Cu⁺: a brick-red precipitate of copper(I) oxide forms.
  • The more reducing sugar, the further along: blue → green → yellow → orange → brick red.
  • So the colour is semi-quantitative. For a number, use a colorimeter.
Quick check
?Four solutions, W, X, Y and Z, were each heated with the same volume of Benedict's reagent for the same time. Final colours: W orange, X blue, Y brick red, Z green. Which list puts them in order of reducing sugar concentration, lowest first?
Mission 3

Proteins and lipids

  • Biuret test: add biuret reagent (sodium hydroxide, then dilute copper(II) sulphate).
  • Protein present: blue → lilac (purple). The reagent detects peptide bonds.
  • Free amino acids give no lilac: they have no peptide bonds.
Mission 3

Glucose-specific test strips

  • Benedict's detects any reducing sugar. A glucose test strip detects glucose only.
  • The pad holds the enzyme glucose oxidase, plus peroxidase and a dye.
  • Glucose oxidase acts only on glucose: hydrogen peroxide forms, and the dye changes colour.
  • Compare with the colour chart on the pot: an estimate of glucose concentration.
  • Used to check urine for glucose.
Quick check
?A urine sample gives a brick-red precipitate with Benedict's reagent, but a glucose test strip shows no glucose. What is the best conclusion?
Mission check

?A sample of milk is tested. Heated with Benedict's reagent: an orange-red precipitate. With biuret reagent: lilac. With iodine in potassium iodide: stays orange-brown. Which conclusion is supported?

Each reagent answers one question. Choose the tests, read every result, and claim no more than they show.

Mission 4

Mission 4: One spot of a mixture goes in; a ladder of spots comes out. How do you work out what each spot is?

Make a guess: One spot of a mixture goes in; a ladder of spots comes out. How do you work out what each spot is?

Mission 4

How chromatography separates

  • (1) Stationary phase: the paper, or a thin layer of silica gel on a plate (TLC). It stays put.
  • (2) Mobile phase: the solvent. It creeps up the stationary phase by capillary action, carrying the mixture.
  • (3) Each molecule spends time dissolved in the solvent and time attached to the stationary phase.
  • More soluble in the solvent, less attracted to the stationary phase: it travels further.
  • So molecules in a mixture separate into spots.
  • TLC runs faster and gives sharper spots than paper.
  • Say: "distance depends on solubility and attraction" — not: "on how much of it there is".
Mission 4

Running a chromatogram

  • Draw an origin line in pencil (ink would itself separate), about 1.5 cm from the bottom.
  • Put a small, concentrated spot of the sample on the origin. Dry it; add more; dry again.
  • Stand the paper in solvent with the origin above the solvent level, so the sample does not wash off into it.
  • Cover the container, so the air inside stays saturated with solvent vapour.
  • Take it out before the solvent reaches the top. Mark the solvent front in pencil at once.
  • Coloured spots show themselves. Colourless ones need a locating agent.
Mission 4

Worked example: Rf values

  • Rf = distance moved by the solute ÷ distance moved by the solvent front.
  • Measure both from the origin; measure to the centre of the spot.
  • Illustrative data: origin 20 mm from the paper's bottom edge; spot centre 56 mm; solvent front 80 mm.
  • Solute: 56 − 20 = 36 mm. Solvent: 80 − 20 = 60 mm.
  • Rf = 36 ÷ 60 = 0.60. Always between 0 and 1; no units.
  • Same solute, solvent and stationary phase: same Rf. Compare with reference values, or run standards beside the sample.
Quick check
?On a paper chromatogram of leaf pigments, the origin line was 20 mm from the bottom edge of the paper, the centre of the carotene spot was 65 mm from the bottom edge and the solvent front was 70 mm from the bottom edge. What is the Rf value of carotene?
Mission 4

Amino acids and other colourless molecules

  • Amino acids are colourless. After the run, dry the chromatogram and spray with ninhydrin; warm it.
  • Amino acids show up as purple spots. (Wear gloves: ninhydrin also stains skin.)
  • To identify a protein's amino acids, hydrolyse the protein first.
  • Run standards (known amino acids) beside the unknown. Matching Rf: same amino acid.
  • CCEA practical: prepare, run and develop the chromatogram, then calculate Rf values.
Quick checkRead the data
?Illustrative data. In the solvent used, the reference Rf values of five amino acids are P 0.26, Q 0.38, R 0.60, S 0.67 and T 0.80. A hydrolysed peptide is run. The origin is 15 mm from the bottom edge of the paper and the solvent front is 90 mm from it. After ninhydrin, two purple spots are centred 34.5 mm and 60.0 mm from the bottom edge. Which amino acids does the peptide contain?
Mission check

?Sugars in a fruit juice are separated by thin-layer chromatography, with glucose, fructose and sucrose standards run on the same plate. The juice gives two spots, level with the glucose and fructose standards; nothing is level with sucrose. On a second plate, with a different solvent, every spot (juice and standards) has a different Rf from the first plate. Which conclusion is correct?

A spot is named by where it lands compared with a known, under the same conditions. Not by its size, and not by guesswork.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Helicase unwinds DNA and breaks hydrogen bonds; free nucleotides pair with each exposed template; DNA polymerase joins them by condensation.
  • Each new molecule has one original and one new strand: semi-conservative replication, giving identical copies.

Mission 2

  • Meselson and Stahl (1958): heavy (¹⁵N) DNA, then growth on ¹⁴N; one intermediate band after 1 generation, intermediate + light after 2.
  • Generation 1 refutes conservative; generation 2 refutes dispersive; semi-conservative predicts both.

Mission 3

  • Iodine: blue-black = starch. Benedict's, heated: brick-red precipitate = reducing sugar. Biuret: lilac = protein.

Mission 4

  • Molecules separate by how they share time between the solvent and the stationary phase.
  • Rf = distance moved by the solute ÷ distance moved by the solvent front, both from the origin; compare with standards or reference values for the same solvent.
  • Colourless amino acids are revealed with ninhydrin and identified against standards.
