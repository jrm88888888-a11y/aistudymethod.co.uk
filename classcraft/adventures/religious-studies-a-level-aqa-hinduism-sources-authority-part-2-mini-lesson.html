Mission 1

Smriti in story form

  • Much Hindu smriti is narrative:
  • Itihasa ("so it was"): the two great epics, the Ramayana and the Mahabharata.
  • Puranas ("ancient lore"): stories of creation, the gods and the avatars.
  • They are known through recitation, festivals, temple carvings, dance and drama, comics, film and television (Ramanand Sagar's Ramayan, 1987–88; B. R. Chopra's Mahabharat, 1988–90).
  • Their aim: to show dharma (right conduct and duty) in action.
  • Status: smrti, "remembered", below shruti in authority, but central in daily life.
Mission 1

The Ramayana: the story

  • Attributed to the sage Valmiki: about 24,000 verses in seven books; composed over several centuries before c. 200 CE.
  • Prince Rama of Ayodhya is exiled for 14 years because of a promise his father made to a queen. His wife Sita and brother Lakshmana go with him.
  • The demon-king Ravana abducts Sita to Lanka. With the monkey Hanuman and an army, Rama defeats Ravana and rescues her.
  • Rama returns to rule Ayodhya: Rama-rajya, the ideal kingdom. Many Hindus light lamps at Diwali to welcome him home.
  • Many versions: Tulsidas's Hindi Ramcharitmanas (16th century) is the best known in north India; Tamil, Thai and other tellings differ.
Mission 1

What the Ramayana teaches

  • Rama: the ideal man (maryada purushottama), keeping dharma as son, brother, husband and king. Vaishnavas worship him as an avatar of Vishnu.
  • Sita: faithfulness, courage and strength in captivity; for many Hindus the ideal wife.
  • Lakshmana: loyalty and selfless caring for his brother.
  • Hanuman: devotion (bhakti), courage and service; a model of friendship with Rama.
  • Debates: Sita's trial by fire and later banishment are criticised by many, including feminist readers; some versions retell or omit them.

Mission 1

The Mahabharata

  • Attributed to the sage Vyasa: about 100,000 verses in 18 books, among the longest poems in the world. It calls itself a "fifth Veda".
  • Two sets of cousins: the five Pandavas and the hundred Kauravas, rivals for one kingdom.
  • The Pandavas lose everything in a rigged game of dice; their wife Draupadi is humiliated. After 13 years of exile, war follows at Kurukshetra (18 days).
  • Krishna guides the Pandavas; before the battle he teaches Arjuna: the Bhagavad Gita (book 6).
  • Key theme: doing one's duty (svadharma) when duties clash; "dharma is subtle". Good people make hard choices; victory brings grief.
Mission 1Read the source

Why the avatar comes

Bhagavad Gita 4:7–8 (Edwin Arnold's verse translation, 1885), Krishna speaks:

> "When Righteousness

> Declines, O Bharata! when Wickedness

> Is strong, I rise, from age to age, and take

> Visible shape, and move a man with men,

> Succouring the good, thrusting the evil back,

> And setting Virtue on her seat again."

Q1 When does the avatar come?

Show answer

Whenever righteousness, dharma, declines and wickedness grows strong.

Q2 What three things does the avatar do?

Show answer

Protects the good, pushes back evil, re-establishes dharma.

Mission check

?A Hindu nurse faces a hard choice between duty to her patients during a strike and loyalty to her colleagues. She looks to Hindu sacred stories for guidance. Which episode most directly fits her situation?

The epics teach dharma by showing it under pressure, where duties pull in different directions.

Mission 2

The Song of the Lord

  • Bhagavad Gita: "Song of the Lord". 700 verses in 18 chapters.
  • It sits in book 6 of the Mahabharata (see Mission 1), on the eve of the war at Kurukshetra.
  • Krishna, whom many Hindus worship as God (an avatar of Vishnu, or for some the Supreme himself), is Arjuna's charioteer.
  • Arjuna is the great archer of the five Pandava brothers.
  • Usually dated somewhere between c. 400 BCE and 200 CE; scholars disagree.
  • Status: smrti ("remembered"), yet treated by many Hindus with the reverence of shruti; often called the essence of the Upanishads.
Mission 2

Arjuna's crisis

  • Arjuna looks across the field and sees teachers, uncles, cousins and friends in the enemy army.
  • He drops his bow: killing kin would destroy family dharma and bring chaos and sin (chapter 1).
  • But he is a kshatriya (warrior); his duty is to fight a just war.
  • So his personal dharma (svadharma) as a warrior clashes with his dharma as a kinsman.
  • Krishna's answer fills the next 17 chapters: who you really are; how to act; how to love God.
Mission 2Read the source

Do your own duty

Bhagavad Gita 2:31 (Edwin Arnold's verse translation, 1885), Krishna to Arjuna:

> "Nought better can betide a martial soul

> Than lawful war"

Bhagavad Gita 18:47 (Arnold):

> "Better thine own work is, though done with fault,

> Than doing others' work, ev'n excellently."

Q1 What kind of war does 2:31 approve?

Show answer

A "lawful" war: a righteous war, fought according to dharma, not any war.

Q2 According to 18:47, why should Arjuna not leave the battle to become a peaceful hermit?

Show answer

His own duty, even done imperfectly, is better than another's duty done well.

Mission 2

Karma yoga: act without clinging

  • Karma yoga: the path of action, done as duty, without attachment to its results.
  • 3:5 (Arnold): "no jot of time, at any time, / Rests any actionless; his nature's law / Compels him, even unwilling, into act".
  • So giving up action is impossible: everyone acts. What matters is how.
  • 3:19 (Arnold): "thy task prescribed / With spirit unattached gladly perform".
  • Selfless action builds no binding karma, so it leads towards moksha (liberation).
  • Arjuna should fight, not for glory or gain, but because it is his duty, offered to God.
Mission 2

Bhakti yoga: love and surrender

  • Bhakti yoga: the path of loving devotion to a personal God.
  • 9:26 (Arnold): "Whoso shall offer Me in faith and love / A leaf, a flower, a fruit, water poured forth, / That offering I accept".
  • 9:27: whatever you do (eating, giving, fasting) do it as an offering to God.
  • 9:32 (Arnold): "Woman or man; sprung of the Vaisya caste / Or lowly disregarded Sudra,--all / Plant foot upon the highest path". Bhakti is open to everyone.
  • 11:54 (Arnold): "Only by fullest service, perfect faith, / And uttermost surrender am I known".
  • After Arjuna sees Krishna's universal form (chapter 11), many readers take the Gita to present devotion as the highest way to know God (11:54; 12:2).
  • A third path, jnana yoga, is the path of knowledge: seeing the eternal self as distinct from the body (2:20).
Mission check

?Priya, a Hindu software engineer, offers her daily work to Krishna and does not chase promotion or praise. Each morning she places a flower at her home shrine. A friend says: "You only follow bhakti yoga." Which response fits the Gita best?

The Gita does not ask Arjuna to leave the world: it asks him to act, know and love, all at once, with his eyes on God.

Make a guess: Hindus have no pope. So who has the authority to teach them, and why do people listen?

Mission 3

Living sources of authority

  • Hinduism has no single head, no central council and no one creed.
  • Alongside scripture, authority comes from people: those who live what the texts teach.
  • Four kinds of holy person:
  • Sadhu / sadhvi: an ascetic devoted wholly to the religious life.
  • Yogi / yogini: a practitioner of yoga disciplines.
  • Guru / swami: a spiritual teacher.
  • Sannyasi: a renunciate who has given up worldly life.
  • The labels overlap: one person can be all four.
Mission 3

The guru

  • Guru: a spiritual teacher. A popular derivation: gu (darkness) + ru (remover), "one who removes darkness".
  • The guru–shishya (teacher–disciple) relationship passes knowledge on in a parampara (lineage).
  • Diksha (OCR: diksa): initiation. The guru may give a new name and a personal mantra to repeat.
  • Disciples serve, listen, ask and obey; many touch the guru's feet as a sign of respect.
  • A swami ("master", often explained as "master of oneself") is usually a monk of an order; many swamis act as gurus.
  • Some traditions honour the guru as a form of God; Guru Purnima celebrates gurus every year.
Mission 3Read the source

Seek a teacher

Bhagavad Gita 4:34 (Edwin Arnold's verse translation, 1885), Krishna to Arjuna:

> "these are gained by reverence, by strong search,

> By humble heed of those who see the Truth

> And teach it."

  • The Mundaka Upanishad (1.2.12, paraphrase) says the seeker should go, fuel in hand, to a teacher who knows the Vedas and is established in Brahman.

Q1 According to the Gita, what three things lead to wisdom?

Show answer

Reverence, strong search and humbly listening to those who see and teach the truth.

Q2 What makes a teacher worth hearing in these texts?

Show answer

They have seen the truth themselves, not only studied it.

Mission 3

Swami Sivananda: a life

  • Swami Sivananda Saraswati (1887–1963), born in Pattamadai, Tamil Nadu.
  • Trained in medicine; worked as a doctor in Malaya (now Malaysia) for about ten years.
  • Gave up his career, wandered as a seeker, and took sannyasa in Rishikesh in 1924.
  • Founded the Divine Life Society (1936) and an ashram on the Ganges at Rishikesh.
  • Wrote over 200 books on yoga, Vedanta and devotion, many in plain English for a wide readership.
  • His disciples spread his teaching worldwide: e.g. Swami Vishnudevananda (Sivananda Yoga Vedanta Centres) and Swami Chidananda.
Mission 3

Sivananda's teaching

  • Yoga of Synthesis: combine all the paths: karma (service), bhakti (devotion), raja (meditation) and jnana (knowledge).
  • His motto: "Serve, Love, Give, Purify, Meditate, Realise."
  • Also: "Be good, do good."
  • Service of the sick and poor: he ran a free dispensary and later a hospital at the ashram.
  • Spiritual life is open to everyone, of any background or nation.
  • Authority for him came from realisation and example, rooted in Vedanta scripture.

Mission 3Diversity

Gurus' authority today

  • Sources of a guru's authority: personal realisation; a recognised lineage; faithfulness to scripture; the disciples' experience of change.
  • Global gurus: since the 20th century many gurus teach in the West and online; yoga and meditation centres spread their influence.
  • Different Hindu attitudes: some say a guru is essential for spiritual progress; others rely on family, scripture, temple priests or their own devotion.
  • Concerns: some gurus have faced scandals over money, abuse of power or sexual misconduct; critics warn against blind obedience.
  • Responses: test a guru by their life and by scripture; value lineages with accountability; follow the teaching rather than the person.
Mission 3Evaluate

Does a Hindu today need a guru?

  • Claim: A guru is essential for a Hindu's spiritual life today.
  • For: The Upanishads and the Gita (4:34) send the seeker to a teacher; spiritual knowledge is caught from a person, not only from books.
  • For: In a world of confusing information, a lineage gives tested guidance.
  • Against: Many Hindus live devout lives through family puja, temple worship and scripture without a personal guru.
  • Against: Scandals show the danger of placing too much authority in one person.
  • Against: Bhakti says God is reached by love, open to all; some say God is the true guru within.
  • Judgement criteria: Which kind of path (jnana, bhakti, karma)? Which sampradaya? How is a genuine guru recognised?
  • 15-mark (AO2) tip: weigh at least two views with reasons and reach a justified conclusion.
Mission check

?Meena, a British Hindu, joins an online meditation course run by a celebrity teacher who calls himself a guru. He gives no lineage, sells costly "secret" mantras and says students must never question him. Which response best reflects traditional Hindu teaching about gurus?

A guru's authority does not come from a title: it comes from a life that shows the truth and a teaching faithful to the tradition.

Make a guess: An ashram was once a sage's forest hut. How did it become a yoga school in Kerala and a temple in Wales?

Mission 4

Ashram and ashrama

  • Two words from the same root, shram: "effort", "striving".
  • Ashram: a place: a spiritual retreat where people live, study and practise under a teacher.
  • Ashrama: a stage of life: student, householder, forest-dweller, renunciate (see Mission 3).
  • They connect: students and forest-dwellers traditionally lived in ashrams; renunciates often found or lead them.
  • Say: "ashram" for the place; "ashrama" for the stage.
Mission 4

Ashrams before the 20th century

  • In the epics and Puranas, ashrams are forest hermitages of rishis (sages), far from cities.
  • Valmiki's ashram shelters Sita, where her sons Lava and Kusha are born and taught (Ramayana).
  • Krishna and his friend Sudama study at the ashram of the teacher Sandipani (Puranas).
  • Roles: gurukula (students live with the guru, serve and learn the Vedas); retreat for those in the vanaprastha stage; a place of austerity and meditation.
  • Alongside them grew mathas (monasteries), such as those traditionally founded by Shankara (c. 8th century) at Sringeri, Puri, Dwarka and Joshimath.
  • Usually small, simple, for a few disciples, and open mainly to men of the higher varnas.
Mission 4

What changed in the 20th century

  • Social and political ashrams: Gandhi's Sabarmati Ashram (Ahmedabad, 1917): simple living, spinning, work against untouchability; the Salt March began there (1930).
  • Ashrams around a realised teacher: Sri Aurobindo Ashram (Pondicherry, 1926); Ramana Maharshi's ashram (Tiruvannamalai).
  • Teaching ashrams for the world: Sivananda's Divine Life Society (Rishikesh, 1936) trained monks and welcomed visitors of any nation.
  • Western seekers came to India in large numbers from the 1960s; ashrams opened in Europe and America.
  • So ashrams became open to women, lay people and non-Hindus, often with set courses and a public timetable.

Mission 4

Types of ashram today

  • Monastic training: where monks and nuns are initiated and trained (e.g. Rishikesh ashrams).
  • Yoga and retreat: courses in yoga, meditation and Vedanta, often for international guests.
  • Devotional: built around temples and a guru's lineage, with daily puja and festivals.
  • Service: running schools, hospitals, hospices, food programmes and disaster relief.
  • Social reform: following Gandhi, simple living and work for justice.
  • Diaspora: ashrams outside India, serving local Hindus and seekers of every faith.
  • Many ashrams combine several types.
Mission 4

Sivananda Yoga Vedanta Dhanwantari Ashram, Kerala

  • At Neyyar Dam, near Thiruvananthapuram, Kerala.
  • Founded in 1978 by Swami Vishnudevananda, a disciple of Swami Sivananda (see Mission 3).
  • Named after Dhanwantari, the deity of healing and Ayurveda.
  • Runs residential yoga teacher training courses and retreats; guests come from all over the world.
  • A strict daily timetable: early morning satsang (meditation, chanting, a talk), yoga classes, karma yoga (selfless work around the ashram), lectures, evening satsang.
  • Teaches Vishnudevananda's five points of yoga: proper exercise, proper breathing, proper relaxation, proper diet (vegetarian), positive thinking and meditation.
  • Shows the modern yoga-and-retreat type, rooted in Sivananda's Yoga of Synthesis.
Mission 4

Skanda Vale, Wales

  • In the hills of Carmarthenshire, Wales; founded in 1973 by Guru Sri Subramanium.
  • A monastic community: monks and nuns living under vows, who call themselves the Community of the Many Names of God.
  • A multi-faith ashram: the main temples honour Murugan (Lord Subramanium) and the Divine Mother; people of every faith and none are welcome.
  • Daily puja and large festivals; visitors stay and eat free of charge, as offerings to God.
  • Ahimsa in practice: it cares for many animals and became widely known in 2007 when its temple bullock, Shambo, was removed and killed by the authorities after a positive TB test, despite protests.
  • It also runs a free hospice nearby.
  • Shows the devotional, service and diaspora types together.
Mission 4Read the source

A quiet place

Bhagavad Gita 6:10–11 (Edwin Arnold's verse translation, 1885), on the one who practises yoga:

> "Sequestered should he sit,

> Steadfastly meditating, solitary,"

> "In a fair, still spot

> Having his fixed abode"

Q1 What kind of place does the Gita recommend for meditation?

Show answer

A quiet, set-apart, steady place, away from distraction.

Q2 How might a modern ashram claim to keep this ideal, even with many visitors?

Show answer

By a quiet setting, silence at set times and a daily discipline of meditation.

Mission 4Evaluate

Have ashrams lost their purpose?

  • Claim: Modern ashrams have lost the spiritual purpose of ashrams before the 20th century.
  • For: Many offer paid courses to tourists; yoga is sometimes taught as fitness, not as a path to moksha.
  • For: Large numbers of short-stay guests change the quiet, intimate gurukula ideal (Gita 6:10–11).
  • Against: The core continues: a teacher, disciples, discipline, satsang and karma yoga, as at the Dhanwantari Ashram.
  • Against: Skanda Vale shows ashrams adding worship, hospitality and service, deepening their spiritual role.
  • Against: Opening ashrams to women and to all nations follows the Gita's teaching that the path is open to all (9:32).
  • Judgement criteria: What is the purpose of an ashram: moksha, teaching, service, community? Who judges: residents, Hindu visitors, tradition?
Mission check

?A new centre in Leicester calls itself an ashram. It runs weekend yoga retreats online and in person, has a café open to the public, and has a resident teacher whose students live with him, serve in the centre and study the Upanishads. Which feature most clearly continues the role of ashrams before the 20th century?

Ashrams have changed their audience and their activities, but at their heart is still a teacher and disciples living a disciplined spiritual life.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The epics (itihasa) and Puranas are smriti in story form, widely known among Hindus.
  • The Ramayana shows ideal dharma in Rama, Sita, Lakshmana and Hanuman; the Mahabharata shows duty in conflict, and contains the Gita.
  • The avatar descends "from age to age" when dharma declines (Gita 4:7–8).

Mission 2

  • The Gita is Krishna's teaching to Arjuna at Kurukshetra: do your own dharma (2:31, 18:47), even when duties clash.
  • Three paths: karma yoga (action without attachment, 3:19), bhakti yoga (devotion, 9:26, 11:54), jnana yoga (knowledge of the self).
  • A smrti text that many Hindus treat almost as shruti.

Mission 3

  • Hinduism has no single head: holy persons (sadhus, yogis, gurus, sannyasis) are living sources of authority, and the roles overlap.
  • A guru passes on knowledge through initiation (diksha) and mantra in a lineage; Swami Sivananda (1887–1963) taught the Yoga of Synthesis and service to all.
  • Today gurus teach worldwide; Hindus differ on whether a guru is essential, and test gurus by life, lineage and scripture.

Mission 4

  • An ashram is a place of spiritual effort; an ashrama is a stage of life.
  • Before the 20th century: small forest retreats and gurukulas for students and forest-dwellers; in the 20th century: social, teaching and global ashrams open to all.
  • The Dhanwantari Ashram (Kerala, 1978) is a yoga-and-Vedanta training centre; Skanda Vale (Wales, 1973) is a multi-faith monastic temple community offering free hospitality and care.
