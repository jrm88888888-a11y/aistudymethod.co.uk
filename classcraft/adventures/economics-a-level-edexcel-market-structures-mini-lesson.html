Mission 1

Barriers to entry

  • A barrier to entry is anything that makes it hard for new firms to enter a market.
TypeExamples
LegalPatents, licences, the sole right to run a service (e.g. a local water or power network)
CostEconomies of scale, high start-up costs, sunk costs (costs that cannot be recovered on leaving)
MarketBrand loyalty, heavy advertising, product differentiation
PhysicalControl of a key resource, the best locations, owning the network
  • Why they matter: without barriers, supernormal profit attracts entrants, whose extra supply competes it away. With high barriers, firms can keep supernormal profit in the long run.
Mission 1

Sunk costs and barriers to exit

  • A sunk cost cannot be recovered if the firm leaves the market: advertising, research, specialist equipment with no resale value.
  • High sunk costs deter entry: an entrant that fails loses them for good.
  • Barriers to exit: costs of leaving, e.g. sunk costs written off, redundancy pay, long contracts.
  • If sunk costs are low, a market is contestable: a new firm could enter, take profit and leave (hit-and-run entry).
  • So even a market with few firms may have prices close to average cost, because incumbents fear entry. (Contestable markets are covered fully in a later lesson.)

Mission 1

Concentration ratios

  • Market share = firm's sales ÷ total market sales × 100.
  • n-firm concentration ratio = combined market share of the n largest firms.
  • Great Britain grocery shares (Kantar, 12 weeks to 2 November 2025): Tesco 28.2%, Sainsbury's 15.7%, Asda 11.6%, Aldi 10.6%, Morrisons 8.3%.
  • 4-firm CR = 28.2 + 15.7 + 11.6 + 10.6 = 66.1%.
  • 5-firm CR = 66.1 + 8.3 = 74.4%.
  • A high ratio suggests oligopoly. There is no single official cut-off, so always state n.
  • Limits: the result depends on how the market is defined (all groceries or one product? national or local?). It does not show whether the top firms are equal in size or one dominates.

Mission 2: Can a firm make goods at the lowest possible cost and still waste resources?

Mission 2

Two questions about waste

  • Efficiency: getting the most out of scarce resources.
  • Ask two separate questions:
    • How is it made? At the lowest possible cost per unit? → productive efficiency
    • What is made, and how much? The goods society values most? → allocative efficiency
  • One can hold without the other.
  • Both are static efficiency: efficiency at a point in time, with today's technology.
  • Dynamic efficiency asks a third question: are costs and products improving over time?
Mission 2

Productive efficiency

  • Productive efficiency: output where average cost (AC) is at its minimum.
  • At that output, MC = AC: the MC curve cuts the AC curve at its lowest point.
  • Why? If the next unit costs less than the average (MC < AC), it pulls AC down. If it costs more (MC > AC), it pushes AC up.
  • Say: "productively efficient at minimum AC" — not: "at the lowest total cost" (that is zero output).

Mission 2

Allocative efficiency

  • Allocative efficiency: resources make the goods and services society values most, in the right amounts.
  • Condition: P = MC.
    • Price shows what buyers will pay for the last unit: its marginal benefit (MB) to them.
    • MC shows the cost of the resources used for it: the value of what else they could have made.
  • P > MC: buyers value one more unit above its cost → too little is made.
  • P < MC: the last unit costs more than it is worth to buyers → too much is made.
  • Say: "allocatively efficient where P = MC" — not: "where P = AC" (that is normal profit).

Mission 2

Dynamic efficiency

  • Dynamic efficiency: efficiency over time, through innovation.
    • Process innovation: new methods lower costs → the whole AC curve shifts down.
    • Product innovation: new and better products.
  • It needs investment, R&D and new technology.
  • Funding: retained supernormal profit is a major source.
  • Incentive: firms innovate to win or protect profit.
Mission 2

X-inefficiency

  • X-inefficiency: a firm produces at a cost above the lowest possible cost for its output.
  • On the diagram: a point above the AC curve, not on it.
  • Causes: organisational slack, overstaffing, overpaying suppliers, managers not pushed to cut costs.
  • Most likely where competition is weak, e.g. a protected monopoly.
  • Competition (or the threat of entry) removes it: high-cost firms lose sales.
  • Say: "X-inefficient" only for costs above the AC curve — not: for every firm away from minimum AC. A point on the curve, away from its minimum, is productively inefficient but not X-inefficient.

Mission 2

Case: patents

  • A patent gives an inventor the sole right to sell an invention for a set time.
  • WTO members must offer patent protection for at least 20 years from the filing date.
  • Static cost: a monopoly → P > MC → allocatively inefficient.
  • Dynamic gain: the expected profit pays for costly R&D (e.g. new medicines).
  • A deliberate trade: less static efficiency now for more dynamic efficiency later.
Mission 2Evaluate

Static or dynamic: which matters more?

  • It depends on whether profit is reinvested: supernormal profit may fund R&D, or be paid out to owners.
  • It depends on competitive pressure: a protected monopoly may stop innovating and let costs drift up.
  • It depends on economies of scale: one large firm's minimum AC may be far below that of many small firms.
  • It depends on time: static losses happen now; dynamic gains, if any, come later and are uncertain.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. A firm produces exactly at the lowest point of its AC curve. Its price is 12. At this output, its marginal cost is 7. Which statement is correct?

Low cost and the right amount are two different tests. A firm can pass the first and fail the second.

Mission 3

The assumptions

  • Perfect competition assumes:
    • many buyers and sellers, each too small to affect the price
    • identical (homogeneous) products: one seller's output is a perfect substitute for another's
    • perfect information: everyone knows every price
    • free entry and exit: no barriers
  • Also assumed: firms aim to maximise profit; factors of production are mobile.
  • Real markets come close, but never fully: wheat and other crops, some currency markets.
Mission 3

A price taker

  • Two diagrams:
    • Industry: market D and S set the price, Pe.
    • Firm: sells any amount it wants at Pe. Its demand curve is horizontal at Pe.
  • Charge above Pe? Buyers switch to identical products: the firm sells nothing.
  • Charge below Pe? Pointless: it can already sell all it wants at Pe.
  • So the firm is a price taker: every extra unit sells for Pe.
  • P = AR = MR = D, a horizontal line.
  • Say: "the firm's demand curve is perfectly elastic" — not: "market demand is perfectly elastic". Market demand slopes down as normal.
Quick check
?In a perfectly competitive market, why is each firm's demand curve horizontal at the market price?
Mission 3

Case: the world wheat price

  • Wheat (US hard red winter), world price, annual averages:
    • 2021: about 315 dollars per tonne
    • 2022: about 430 dollars per tonne
    • 2023: about 340 dollars per tonne
  • 2022 was the year Russia invaded Ukraine; both are major wheat exporters.
  • No single farmer caused these swings, and none could resist them.
  • Each farmer's only choice: how much to grow and sell at the world price.
Mission 3

How much to produce?

  • Profit is maximised where MC = MR.
  • For a price taker, MR = P. So produce where MC = P, on the rising part of MC.
  • Why:
    • MC below P: the next unit adds more to revenue than to cost → produce it.
    • MC above P: the unit adds more to cost than to revenue → don't produce it.
Mission 3

Profit, normal profit or loss

  • Find output where MC = MR. Then compare AR (= P) with AC at that output.
  • P > AC: supernormal profit = (P − AC) × Q, a rectangle.
  • P = AC: normal profit (break-even): just enough to keep the firm in this industry.
  • P < AC: loss = (AC − P) × Q.
  • Say: "supernormal (or abnormal) profit" — not: "a big profit". Normal profit is already counted in AC as a cost.

Mission 3

Shut down or carry on?

  • In the short run, fixed costs must be paid even at zero output.
  • Shut down → loss = fixed cost.
  • Carry on → loss = TC − TR.
  • Rule: carry on if TR ≥ TVC, i.e. P ≥ AVC. Revenue covers variable costs and pays part of the fixed cost.
  • Shut down if P < AVC at every output.
  • Shutdown price = minimum AVC.
  • The firm from an earlier screen: shutdown price = minimum AVC = 10.
Mission 3Graph lab

Same firm. Fixed cost 360. Shutdown loss = 360.

Mission 3

The firm's supply curve

  • At each price, the firm produces where P = MC.
  • So its MC curve shows how much it supplies at each price.
  • Below minimum AVC it supplies nothing.
  • Firm's short-run supply curve = its MC curve above minimum AVC.
  • Industry supply = the firms' supply curves added sideways (horizontally).
Mission check

?A firm sells standard cardboard boxes in a perfectly competitive market. The market price is 15 per box. At its current output, the marginal cost of its last box is 12. What should it do to maximise profit?

A price taker cannot choose its price. Its only lever is output: produce up to where MC equals the market price.

Mission 4

Mission 4: When a product is very profitable and anyone can start selling it, where do the profits go?

Make a guess: When a product is very profitable and anyone can start selling it, where do the profits go?

Mission 4

The long run

  • Long run: all factors can change. Firms can enter or leave the industry.
  • In perfect competition there are no barriers, so entry and exit are free.
  • Normal profit: the minimum return needed to keep the firm's resources in this industry. It is their opportunity cost.
  • Normal profit is counted inside AC. So at P = AC, the owners earn just enough to stay.
  • Say: "normal profit" — not: "no profit". The owners are still paid their opportunity cost.
Mission 4

Supernormal profit attracts entry

  • Build it in steps (two panels: industry and firm):
  • 1. Industry: D and S1 set price P1, above minimum AC. Firm: supernormal profit rectangle.
  • 2. Profit is a signal: new firms enter. Industry supply shifts right: S1 → S2.
  • 3. Price falls to P2 = minimum AC. Each firm cuts output back to the lowest point of AC.
  • 4. Supernormal profit is gone: normal profit only. Entry stops.
Quick check
?A perfectly competitive industry is earning supernormal profit. New firms enter until the long run is reached. What happens to output?
Mission 4

Losses drive exit

  • The mirror image:
  • 1. Price below minimum AC: firms make losses.
  • 2. In the long run, loss-making firms leave. Industry supply shifts left: S1 → S2.
  • 3. Price rises back to minimum AC.
  • 4. The firms that remain make normal profit. Exit stops.
Mission 4

Long-run equilibrium

  • Entry and exit stop only when price = minimum AC.
  • Firm in long-run equilibrium: P = AR = MR = MC = AC (at its minimum).
  • Each firm makes normal profit: no reason to enter or leave.
  • Industry: D and S set this price; the number of firms adjusts to match demand.
Mission 4Graph lab

A demand boom

Illustrative data. 240 identical firms, each making normal profit at a price of 26. Assume each firm's costs do not change as the industry grows.

  • Takeaway: entry continues until price is back at minimum AC (26). More firms; same output each.
Mission 4

Worked example: the boom, step by step

  • Illustrative data. Each firm: minimum AC = 26 at 30 units.
  • Start: market demand at 26 is 7,200 units → 7,200 ÷ 30 = 240 firms.
  • Short run: demand rises. Still 240 firms. Price jumps to 43; each firm makes 40 units.
    • Industry output: 240 × 40 = 9,600.
    • Profit per firm: (43 − 28) × 40 = 600 (supernormal profit). AC at 40 units = 28.
  • Long run: firms enter until price = 26 again.
    • Market demand at 26 is now 14,700 units → 14,700 ÷ 30 = 490 firms.
  • Industry output doubles and more; each firm is back to 30 units and normal profit.
Quick check

At a price of 4, market demand would be 60,000 units. Costs do not change as firms enter. How many firms will there be in the long run?
Mission 4

Why the long run is efficient

  • Allocative efficiency: P = MC. The value of the last unit to buyers equals its cost.
  • Productive efficiency: output at minimum AC, the lowest cost per unit.
  • Consumers pay the lowest price that firms can sustain: minimum AC.
Mission 4Evaluate

A benchmark, not a description

  • Few real markets meet every assumption: products differ, information is costly, entry takes time and money.
  • It depends on economies of scale: where minimum efficient scale is large, many small firms would each have high costs. A few big firms can produce more cheaply.
  • It depends on innovation: with only normal profit in the long run, and rivals able to copy any idea, firms have little to fund or reward R&D. Dynamic efficiency may be weak.
  • It depends on time: entry and exit can take years, so supernormal profits and losses can last.
  • Still useful: it is the benchmark against which monopoly and other structures are judged.
Mission check

?A city's market for bicycle repairs is perfectly competitive and in long-run equilibrium. The city then charges every repair firm a fixed yearly fee, whatever its output. Demand is unchanged. What happens in the long run?

Free entry and exit push price to minimum AC. Any change in costs or demand is eventually competed into the price.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Why can a popular new kind of café make big profits at first, then only just cover its costs later?

Make a guess: Why can a popular new kind of café make big profits at first, then only just cover its costs later?

Mission 5

Many firms, different products

  • Monopolistic competition: many firms, each selling a differentiated product.
  • Low barriers to entry and exit.
  • Each firm has a little price-setting power: its product is not identical to rivals'.
  • Examples: cafés, hairdressers, restaurants, takeaways, a new style of café such as bubble tea.
  • Firms are small, so each one acts independently: it does not plan around one rival's reaction.
Mission 5

Standing out: non-price competition

  • Product differentiation: real or perceived differences (quality, design, location, service, brand).
  • Non-price competition: competing on anything but price: branding, advertising, loyalty cards, opening hours, new menu items.
  • Aim 1: shift the firm's demand curve right (more customers at each price).
  • Aim 2: make demand less price elastic (customers less willing to switch).
  • Say: "the firm's demand curve slopes downward because its product is differentiated" — not: "because it has few rivals".
Quick check
?A hairdresser in a busy town centre raises its price slightly. It loses some customers but not all of them. Which best explains why?
Mission 5

The short run

  • Profit is maximised where MR = MC. Price is read up to the AR curve.
  • AR > AC: supernormal profit. AR < AC: a loss. AR = AC: normal profit.
  • In the short run, this looks just like the monopoly diagram. The difference is what happens next.
Mission 5

Worked example: short-run profit

  • Illustrative data. A new bubble tea shop.
  • Demand: AR = 30 − 0.2Q, so MR = 30 − 0.4Q.
  • Costs: MC = 0.2Q. AC = 360/Q + 0.1Q.
  • MR = MC: 30 − 0.4Q = 0.2Q, so Q = 50.
  • Price from AR: 30 − 0.2 × 50 = 20.
  • AC at Q = 50: 360/50 + 0.1 × 50 = 7.2 + 5 = 12.2.
  • Supernormal profit = (AR − AC) × Q = (20 − 12.2) × 50 = 390.
Mission 5

From short run to long run

  • Profit above normal is a signal. Barriers are low, so new firms enter.
  • Each firm loses some customers to the newcomers: its demand curve shifts left.
  • More close substitutes: demand becomes more price elastic.
  • Entry stops when AR just touches (is tangent to) AC: normal profit.
  • Losses work the other way: firms leave, and each remaining firm's demand shifts right until AR touches AC again.
  • Say: "each firm's demand curve shifts left as firms enter" — not: "the market demand curve falls".

Mission 5

Worked example: the long run

  • Illustrative data. After entry, each shop's demand: AR = 18 − 0.125Q, so MR = 18 − 0.25Q.
  • Costs are unchanged: MC = 0.2Q; AC = 360/Q + 0.1Q.
  • MR = MC: 18 − 0.25Q = 0.2Q, so Q = 40.
  • Price: 18 − 0.125 × 40 = 13.
  • AC at Q = 40: 360/40 + 0.1 × 40 = 9 + 4 = 13.
  • P = AC: profit per unit 0. Normal profit.
  • PED at the chosen price: SR −2; LR −2.6. Demand is more elastic after entry.
Mission 5

Excess capacity

  • The long-run tangency is on the falling part of AC.
  • So output is below the output at the lowest AC: excess capacity.
  • In the previous screen: output 40; lowest AC (12) at 60. Excess capacity = 60 − 40 = 20 units.
  • Not productively efficient: P = AC = 13 > lowest AC = 12.
  • Not allocatively efficient: P = 13 > MC = 8. Buyers value one more unit more than it costs to make.
  • Say: "excess capacity: output is below the level that minimises AC" — not: "the firm has too many workers".

Mission 5

Advertising

  • Advertising aims to shift the firm's demand right and make it less price elastic (brand loyalty).
  • It also raises costs: AC shifts up.
  • Short run: if extra sales outweigh the extra cost, profit rises.
  • Long run: rivals copy the idea, and new firms enter. Profit returns to normal.
  • Informative advertising (prices, opening hours, what is new) helps buyers choose.
  • Persuasive advertising (image, brand) may add to costs and prices without making the product better.
Quick check
?A nail salon launches a successful advertising campaign that makes its customers more loyal. In the short run, what happens to the salon's demand curve?
Mission 5Evaluate

Is excess capacity a waste?

  • It depends on how much buyers value variety: a slightly higher price than perfect competition may be the cost of choice.
  • It depends on how elastic demand is: with many close substitutes, demand is very elastic, the tangency is near the lowest AC, and the excess capacity is small.
  • It depends on the type of advertising: informative or persuasive.
  • It depends on dynamic efficiency: normal profit leaves little to fund research, but rivalry pushes firms to try new products.
  • It depends on how realistic the model is: some firms build brands, or win locations, that rivals cannot easily copy.
Mission check

?Illustrative case: a town has a few gyms making large profits. Opening a gym is easy, and each gym differs a little in equipment and classes. New gyms open until the market settles. Which describes a typical gym in the new long-run equilibrium?

Low barriers mean profit above normal attracts entry until each firm's AR just touches AC, on the falling part of AC.

Mission 6

A few firms dominate

  • Oligopoly: a market dominated by a few large firms.
  • High barriers to entry (economies of scale, brands, patents, high start-up costs).
  • Products may be identical (cement, crude oil) or differentiated (cars, smartphones, supermarkets).
  • Much non-price competition: advertising, branding, loyalty schemes.
Mission 6

Measuring concentration

  • Illustrative data: a country's cement market.

  • A high n-firm ratio suggests oligopoly. Always state n (see Mission 1).
Mission 6

Interdependence

  • Interdependence: each firm's best choice depends on what its rivals do.
  • So every decision (price, output, advertising) brings uncertainty: how will rivals react?
  • If one firm cuts its price, rivals may match the cut to protect their sales. All end up with lower prices and little extra market share.
  • If one firm raises its price, rivals may not follow, and it loses customers to them.
  • Matching cuts can spiral into a price war: each firm undercuts the others, and profits fall for all.
  • Say: "oligopolists are interdependent" — not: "oligopolists ignore their rivals" or "oligopolists always collude".
Mission 6

Compete or collude?

  • Non-collusive oligopoly: firms compete; each decides alone.
  • Collusive oligopoly: firms cooperate to limit competition, e.g. fixing prices or sharing out markets.
  • Overt (explicit) collusion: a formal agreement. A cartel is a group of firms that agree on prices or output.
  • Tacit collusion: no agreement, but firms behave as if there were one, e.g. following a price leader.
  • Why collude? Less uncertainty, higher joint profit, keeping new firms out.
  • Why not? Cartels are illegal in most countries, there may be fines, and members may cheat.
Mission 6

When does a cartel work?

  • A cartel is more likely to hold when there are:
    • few firms (easier to agree and to watch each other)
    • similar costs and similar products (easier to agree one price)
    • high barriers to entry (no newcomers to undercut the cartel)
    • easy monitoring of rivals' prices and output (cheating gets spotted)
    • stable demand (less pressure to break ranks)
    • weak enforcement of competition law
  • Consequences: higher prices, lower output, high profit for members, less choice; buyers lose surplus.
Quick check
?Four firms in a market are thinking of forming a cartel. Which set of conditions makes the cartel most likely to last?
Mission 6

A cartel acts like a monopoly

  • The members act as one firm: they face the industry demand curve.
  • Joint profit is greatest where industry MR = MC. Price is read up to AR.
  • Members then share the output out as quotas.
  • Illustrative data: AR = 100 − Q; MC = AC = 20 for every member.
    • Cartel: MR = 100 − 2Q = 20, so Q = 40, P = 60.
    • Competitive benchmark: P = MC = 20, Q = 80.
Mission 6

Worked example: the pull to cheat

  • Illustrative data: two firms, A and B. AR = 100 − Q. MC = AC = 20.
  • Cartel quotas: 20 each. Q = 40, P = 60. Profit each: (60 − 20) × 20 = 800.
  • A secretly produces 25. Q = 45, so P = 100 − 45 = 55.
    • A: (55 − 20) × 25 = 875. A gains.
    • B: (55 − 20) × 20 = 700. B loses.
  • If both produce 25: Q = 50, P = 50. Each earns (50 − 20) × 25 = 750.
  • Each firm gains by cheating, whatever the other does. Together they lose. (Payoff matrix in Mission 7.)

Mission 6

Price leadership and tacit collusion

  • Price leadership: one firm changes its price; the others follow.
    • Dominant-firm leadership: the largest firm leads.
    • Barometric leadership: a firm that reads market conditions well leads.
  • No meeting and no agreement: this is tacit collusion, which is hard to prove.
  • Result: prices that move together and stay stable for long periods.
Mission 6

Real cartels

  • OPEC: set up in 1960 by Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia and Venezuela. Members agree production targets to influence the world oil price.
  • Truck makers (EU): in 2016 the European Commission fined truck makers a total of €2.93 billion for colluding on prices from 1997 to 2011.
  • One member, MAN, revealed the cartel and was not fined: the law rewards the first to confess.
  • In the UK, cartels are illegal under the Competition Act 1998. The CMA investigates them.
  • OPEC is made up of governments, not private firms. Competition laws on cartels do not apply to it in the same way.
Mission 6Evaluate

Is oligopoly good or bad?

  • Costs: prices above MC (allocative inefficiency); collusion transfers surplus to firms; heavy advertising may add to costs.
  • Benefits: economies of scale can mean low average costs; supernormal profit can fund research and new products (dynamic efficiency); non-price competition can raise quality and choice.
  • It depends on conduct: fierce rivalry (even price wars) helps buyers; collusion harms them.
  • It depends on barriers: if entry is possible, the threat of new firms limits high prices.
  • It depends on regulation: the chance of being caught and fined.

Mission 7

A game between firms

  • Game theory studies decisions where your result depends on others' choices too.
  • Every game has:
    • players (here, two firms)
    • strategies (the choices open to each player, e.g. high price or low price)
    • payoffs (what each player gets, e.g. profit, for each pair of choices)
  • Oligopoly fits: firms are interdependent (see Mission 6).
  • Say: "a strategy is a choice of action" — not: "a strategy is a payoff".

Mission 7

Dominant strategy

  • A dominant strategy is best for a player whatever the other player does.
  • Method: fix the rival's choice, then compare your payoffs.
  • A's view:
    • If B keeps to quota: cheat 875 > keep 800.
    • If B cheats: cheat 750 > keep 700.
  • So cheating is A's dominant strategy. B's payoffs mirror A's, so B's dominant strategy is also to cheat.
  • Say: "dominant strategy: best whatever the rival does" — not: "the strategy with the biggest number in the matrix".

Mission 7

Nash equilibrium

  • Nash equilibrium: an outcome where no player can do better by changing only its own strategy, given the other's choice.
  • Check each cell: would either player want to switch?
  • In the cartel game: (cheat, cheat) → (750, 750).
    • A switching to "keep" gets 700 < 750. B switching gets 700 < 750. No one gains: Nash equilibrium.
  • (keep, keep) → (800, 800) is not a Nash equilibrium: each firm could earn 875 by cheating.
  • When both players have a dominant strategy, the Nash equilibrium is where they both play it.
  • Say: "no player has an incentive to change strategy alone" — not: "both players get their best possible payoff".
Mission 7

The prisoner's dilemma

  • The story: two suspects are questioned in separate rooms. Each can confess or stay silent.
  • Confessing is each one's dominant strategy. Both confess, yet both would get shorter sentences if both stayed silent.
  • A prisoner's dilemma: each player follows its own dominant strategy, and both end up worse off than if they had cooperated.
  • The cartel game is a prisoner's dilemma:
    • Nash equilibrium: (cheat, cheat): 750 each.
    • Cooperation: (keep, keep): 800 each.
  • Why cooperation fails: neither firm can trust the other, and a cartel agreement cannot be enforced in court.

Mission 7Evaluate

How useful is game theory?

  • It shows why oligopolists are interdependent, and why cartels are unstable.
  • It depends on whether the game is one-off or repeated: repeated play makes cooperation easier (match what the rival did last time).
  • It depends on information: real firms rarely know their rivals' payoffs.
  • It depends on the number of firms: real markets often have more than two.
  • It depends on rationality: firms may value trust, reputation or market share, not just this period's profit.
  • Policy use: leniency rules turn a cartel into a prisoner's dilemma. The first member to confess is not fined (as with MAN in Mission 6).

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Market structures range from perfect competition to monopoly, by number of firms, product differentiation, barriers to entry and control over price.
  • Barriers to entry (legal, cost, market, physical) let firms keep supernormal profit in the long run.
  • n-firm concentration ratio = combined market share of the n largest firms.

Mission 2

  • Productive efficiency: minimum AC (MC = AC).
  • Allocative efficiency: P = MC.
  • Dynamic efficiency: costs and products improve over time through R&D and investment.
  • X-inefficiency: costs above the AC curve, often where competition is weak.

Mission 3

  • A perfectly competitive firm is a price taker: P = AR = MR = D, a horizontal line.
  • It produces where MC = MR = P. Compare P with AC: supernormal profit, normal profit or loss.
  • In the short run it keeps producing while P ≥ AVC; its supply curve is MC above minimum AVC.

Mission 4

  • Supernormal profit attracts entry; losses cause exit. Both stop at price = minimum AC.
  • Long-run equilibrium: P = MR = MC = minimum AC; normal profit (zero economic profit).
  • It is allocatively and productively efficient.

Mission 5

  • Many firms, differentiated products, low barriers: each firm has a downward-sloping, fairly elastic demand curve.
  • Short run: MR = MC; profit above normal or a loss is possible. Long run: entry or exit leaves normal profit (AR tangent to AC).
  • Long run: excess capacity and P > MC, but buyers get variety.

Mission 6

  • Oligopoly: a few large firms, high barriers, interdependence and uncertainty.
  • Firms can compete or collude: overtly (cartels) or tacitly (price leadership). Cartels act like a monopoly but each member is tempted to cheat.

Mission 7

  • Payoff matrix: players, strategies, payoffs; each cell is (row player, column player).
  • Dominant strategy: best whatever the rival does. Nash equilibrium: no player gains by changing only its own strategy.
  • Prisoner's dilemma: each plays its dominant strategy, and both end up worse off than if they had cooperated. That is why cartels are unstable.
