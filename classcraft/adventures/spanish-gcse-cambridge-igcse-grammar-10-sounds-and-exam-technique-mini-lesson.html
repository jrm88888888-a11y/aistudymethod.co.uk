Exam skills & deducing meaning

Cambridge tells you plainly: its vocabulary list is "not prescriptive or exhaustive", and the exams deliberately include words you haven't been taught. So the real skill isn't memorising every word — it's working out what an unknown word means from the clues around it, and then attacking each paper with a clear plan.

Work through each screen and answer the questions as you go — some ask you to choose the best meaning, some ask you to type the word you've deduced. Collect ⭐ stars and press Start.

Strand A · Deducing meaning

You will meet words you don't know

That's by design. Cambridge won't test you on a fixed word-list, so panicking at an unknown word is the biggest time-waster in the exam. Instead, run through five clue-checks — one of them almost always cracks it:

  • 1. Cognates — does it look like an English word?
  • 2. Word families & roots — do you know a related Spanish word?
  • 3. Affixes — what do the prefix/suffix tell you?
  • 4. Context & logic — what would make sense here?
  • 5. Grammatical clues — is it a noun, a verb, an adjective?

Golden rule: never leave a gap blank because of one unknown word. A sensible guess from the clues is often exactly right — and always beats nothing.

Clue 1 · Cognates

Words that look English

Thousands of Spanish words are cognates — they share a root with English. Once you spot the pattern, the meaning jumps out:

  • la información, el hospital, importante — near-identical.
  • -ción ↔ English -tion: la nación (nation), la educación (education).
  • -dad ↔ English -ty: la ciudad (city), la universidad (university).

Watch the false friends. A few look English but aren't: éxito = success (not "exit"), sensible = sensitive, actualmente = currently. Let the sentence sanity-check your guess.

Clue 2 · Word families & roots

Pull on a word you already know

Spanish builds families around a shared root. If you know one member, you can often unlock the others:

you know…so you can guess…
trabajar (to work)el trabajo (work/job), el trabajador (worker)
cocinar (to cook)la cocina (kitchen), el cocinero (cook)
enseñar (to teach)el enseñante (teacher), la enseñanza (teaching)

How to use it: when a word looks unfamiliar, cover the ending and ask "what root do I recognise?" deportista hides deporte (sport) → "a sportsperson".

Clue 3 · Affixes

Prefixes & suffixes rewire meaning

The little bits on the front and back of a word change it in predictable ways. Learn these and you can decode words you've never seen:

  • -mente = English -ly (makes an adverb): rápida → rápidamente (quickly), fácil → fácilmente (easily).
  • -dad makes an abstract noun (English -ty): feliz → la felicidad (happiness), real → la realidad (reality).
  • des- reverses, like English un-/dis-: ordenado → desordenado (untidy), aparecer → desaparecer (disappear).
  • in-/im- = "not": útil → inútil (useless), posible → imposible (impossible).

Try it live: desafortunadamente looks long, but it's des- + afortunada (fortunate) + -mente → "unfortunately".

Clue 4 · Context & logic

What would make sense here?

Even with no root to grab, the rest of the sentence narrows the meaning down. Read around the gap and ask what fits:

Worked example

Cuando hace mucho calor, me pongo el bañador y voy a la piscina.

You may not know bañador — but it's hot, you're heading to the pool, and you "put it on"… so it's a swimming costume. Context did the work.

Signposts help too: pero (but) signals a contrast, porque (because) a reason, por ejemplo (for example) a clarification. They tell you what kind of idea comes next.

Use the context

?Read: No pude dormir porque los vecinos hacían mucho ruido. What does ruido most likely mean?
Clue 5 · Grammatical clues

Is it a noun, a verb, or an adjective?

Knowing a word's job narrows the meaning fast — and stops silly errors. The grammar around it gives it away:

  • After el / la / un / una → it's a noun (a thing): la huelga = a strike.
  • Ends in -ar / -er / -ir or has a person ending → it's a verb (an action): madrugar = to get up early.
  • Sits next to a noun and agrees with it → it's an adjective (a describer): un coche veloz = a fast car.

Why it matters: in a gap-fill, spotting that the missing word must be a verb (because a subject and an object are already there) instantly rules out half the options.

Strand B · Exam technique

Four papers, each worth 25%

Cambridge IGCSE Spanish (0530) is untiered and assessed by four equal papers. Every candidate sits all four:

paperskill · weight
Paper 1Listening · 25%
Paper 2Reading · 25%
Paper 3Speaking · 25%
Paper 4Writing · 25%

Not in this exam: there is no translation and no dictation in 0530. Don't waste revision time on those — put it into deducing meaning and the four skills below.

Papers 1 & 2 · Listening & Reading

Listening and Reading

Paper 1 — Listening. Multiple-choice and matching tasks. Crucially, every recording is played twice:

  • Use the reading time to underline key words in each question first.
  • First play: get the gist and mark obvious answers. Second play: confirm and fill the gaps.
  • Listen for distractors — a speaker may mention a wrong option before correcting it.

Paper 2 — Reading. Matching, multiple-choice, and short written answers in Spanish:

  • Scan for the answer, then read closely around it. Watch negatives (no, nunca) that flip meaning.
  • For short-answer questions, reply in Spanish — lift and adapt words from the text; you don't need full sentences.
  • Meet an unknown word? Run the five clue-checks from Strand A rather than stalling.

Paper 3 · Speaking

Role play + two conversations

The Speaking test has two parts:

  • Role play. You respond to five transactional prompts — practical situations like a shop, a station, or booking something. Give the information asked for; keep it clear and complete.
  • Two topic conversations. Longer, freer chats on prepared topic areas. This is where you show off: opinions, reasons, and more than one tense.

Score-lifters: add an opinion + reason (Me gusta… porque…), use past, present and future, and connect ideas with además, sin embargo, por eso. Don't answer a role-play prompt with a bare "sí".

Paper 4 · Writing

Three writing tasks

Paper 4 builds up in length. Know exactly what each task wants:

taskwhat to produce
Form-fillingshort details — name, age, likes; single words / short phrases
Directed writingabout 80–90 words covering set bullet points
Extended writingabout 130–140 words — choose an email/letter or an article/blog

Hit the marks: cover every bullet point, stay near the word count, and vary your tenses and connectives. In extended writing, an opinion with a reason and a future plan pushes you up the bands.

Recap

Your exam-skills toolkit

Deduce meaning (5 clue-checks): cognates & near-cognates; word families & roots; affixes (-mente, -dad, des-, in-); context & logic; grammatical clues (noun / verb / adjective).

Never blank a gap over one unknown word — Cambridge's list isn't exhaustive, so a reasoned guess is expected and often right.

Paper 1 Listening (25%): MCQ + matching; each recording heard twice — gist first, confirm second.

Paper 2 Reading (25%): matching + MCQ + short written answers in Spanish; watch negatives.

Paper 3 Speaking (25%): role play (5 transactional prompts) + 2 topic conversations; add opinions, reasons and tenses.

Paper 4 Writing (25%): form-filling; directed writing ~80–90 words; extended writing ~130–140 words (email/letter or article/blog).

Not in 0530: no translation, no dictation.
