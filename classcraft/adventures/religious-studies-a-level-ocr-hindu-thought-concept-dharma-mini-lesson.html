Mission 1

Dharma: what holds things up

  • Dharma comes from the root dhr: to hold, uphold, support.
  • English needs many words for it: duty, righteousness, law, virtue, religion, the right way.
  • It also means a thing's nature: the dharma of fire is to burn; of water, to flow.
  • So dharma is at once cosmic (how the world is ordered), social (how society works) and personal (how I should live).
  • A much-quoted saying: dharma protects those who protect it (Manusmriti 8.15, paraphrase).
  • Your spec asks for "different translations/interpretations of the term 'dharma'".
Mission 1Read the source

"Dharma upholds"

In the Mahabharata, two passages explain the word (paraphrase):

  • Karna Parva 69.58 (Krishna to Arjuna): it is called dharma because it upholds; dharma upholds all creatures. Whatever upholds is dharma.
  • Shanti Parva 109.9–11 (Bhishma to Yudhishthira): dharma was declared for the growth and welfare of all beings, and to stop beings harming one another; what does this is dharma.

Q1 Which test of dharma do both passages share?

Show answer

Whatever upholds and sustains beings is dharma.

Q2 What second test does Shanti Parva add?

Show answer

Dharma prevents harm: a link to ahimsa.

  • Both are taught in the epic as answers to hard cases, where truth-telling or a rule seems to cause harm.
Mission 1

Rta: the order before dharma

  • In the Rig Veda, rta is the true order of things: the sun's path, the seasons, the rivers' flow, the right performance of sacrifice, and truthful speech.
  • The gods, especially Varuna and Mitra, guard rta; anrta is falsehood and disorder.
  • In later texts the word dharma takes over much of this role: the order that sustains cosmos and society.
Mission 1

Sanatana dharma: the eternal way

  • Sanatana means eternal: without beginning or end.
  • Sanatana dharma has two common uses:
  • 1. A name for the religion. Many Hindus prefer it to "Hinduism" (a name from outside): their way is not founded at a point in time but is eternal truth.
  • 2. Universal dharma. Duties for everyone, whatever their class or stage of life (also called sadharana dharma, common dharma).
  • Manusmriti 10.63 (paraphrase) sums them up: non-violence, truthfulness, not stealing, purity, control of the senses.
Mission 1

Universal and personal dharma

  • Alongside universal dharma, each person has svadharma: their own dharma.
  • Traditionally this is fixed by varna (class) and ashrama (stage of life): varnashrama dharma.
  • Krishna to Arjuna:

> "this is better, that one do / His own task as he may, even though he fail, / Than take tasks not his own, though they seem good." (Gita 3:35, Arnold)

  • Tension: Arjuna's universal dharma says "do not harm"; his svadharma as a warrior says "fight a just war". The Gita is built on that conflict.

Mission 1Read the source

When dharma declines

  • Adharma: the opposite of dharma: disorder, injustice, unrighteousness. It harms the world, society and the self, and brings bad karma.

> "When Righteousness / Declines, O Bharata! when Wickedness / Is strong, I rise, from age to age, and take / Visible shape, and move a man with men, / Succouring the good, thrusting the evil back, / And setting Virtue on her seat again." (Gita 4:7–8, Arnold)

  • Arnold translates dharma as "Righteousness" and adharma as "Wickedness".
  • Tradition pictures dharma as a bull that loses a leg in each of four ages (yugas); many Hindus believe we live in the last, the Kali Yuga.

Q1 According to the passage, why does Krishna take visible form?

Show answer

To protect the good, destroy evil and re-establish dharma.

Q2 What does "from age to age" suggest about time?

Show answer

Dharma declines and is restored again and again: time is cyclical.

  • Your spec's passage: Gita IV.5–8.
Mission 1

Dharma wears away: the yugas

  • Time moves in great cycles. One mahayuga has four ages (yugas), each shorter and worse than the one before.
  • Krita (Satya) Yuga: dharma stands like a bull on four legs. Treta: three. Dvapara: two. Kali Yuga: one (Manusmriti 1.81–82, paraphrase).
  • Traditional lengths: 1,728,000, 1,296,000, 864,000 and 432,000 years: 4,320,000 in all.
  • Many Hindus believe we live in the Kali Yuga, traditionally dated from 3102 BCE.
  • At its end, tradition says, Kalki (the tenth avatar of Vishnu) ends the age and a new Krita Yuga begins: decay and restoration.
  • Not all Hindus read the figures literally; many take the scheme as a picture of moral decline and renewal.
Quick check
?In which yuga does dharma, pictured as a bull, stand on only one leg?
Mission 1

Who decides what dharma requires?

  • Sources (Manusmriti 2.6, 2.12, paraphrase): the Veda (shruti); smriti (remembered tradition); the conduct of good people; and what satisfies one's own conscience.
  • So the community matters: families, teachers, sampradayas (traditions) and gurus interpret dharma for new situations.
  • Dharma applies in many contexts: personal (honesty, self-control), family (care of parents), professional (a doctor's dharma to heal), societal (just rule, care for the poor).
  • Dharma is context-sensitive: the epic shows people weighing duties against each other.
  • Spec issues: the role of the Hindu community in shaping interpretations of dharma; the diversity of contexts.

Mission 1Evaluate

Universal or personal dharma first?

  • Claim: Universal (sanatana) dharma matters more than the dharma of one's own class and stage of life.
  • For: It binds everyone; non-violence and truth are the "summary" of dharma (Manusmriti 10.63).
  • For: Reformers appealed to universal values such as non-violence and equal dignity to attack untouchability (e.g. Gandhi).
  • Against: The Gita (3:35, 18:47) says doing your own dharma, even imperfectly, is better than another's.
  • Against: Society works only if each role is filled; svadharma gives the universal a concrete shape.
  • Judgement criteria: Which fits the texts? Which guides real choices? Which fits Hinduism in a modern, plural society?
  • Spec issue: dharma as a foundational principle of civilisation (sanatana dharma).

Mission 2

Four aims of life

  • The purusharthas: "aims of a person", four goals a full human life may pursue.
  • Dharma: right living, duty, virtue.
  • Artha: wealth, security, success, power.
  • Kama: pleasure, love, beauty, enjoyment of the senses.
  • Moksha: liberation from samsara.
  • All four are legitimate. The question is how they fit together.
Mission 2

Artha: wealth and power

  • Earning a living, owning property, supporting a family, building a business, and for rulers, governing well.
  • Without artha, a household cannot give to the poor, support temples or pay for ritual.
  • The Arthashastra (attributed to Kautilya, compiled c. 2nd century BCE–3rd century CE): a manual of statecraft and economics; wealth as the basis of a stable kingdom.
  • Artha is good when gained and used within dharma: honestly, generously, without harm.
Mission 2

Kama: pleasure and love

  • Delight in the senses, music, art, food, friendship, romantic and sexual love within marriage.
  • The Kama Sutra (Vatsyayana, c. 3rd century CE) is a guide to the art of pleasant living, not only to sex.
  • Kama shows that Hindu tradition does not reject pleasure: it is one of life's aims.

> "These am I, free from passion and desire; / Yet am I right desire in all who yearn" (Gita 7:11, Arnold)

  • Krishna is present in desire that is not contrary to dharma (the Sanskrit of 7:11): kama, too, needs dharma's limits.
Mission 2

How the four fit together

  • Trivarga: the first three, the "group of three" for life in the world.
  • Dharma frames artha and kama, like the banks of a river: they give the water its course.
  • Moksha goes beyond the three: it is freedom from the whole cycle in which they are pursued.
  • Two directions of life: pravritti (engagement with the world: dharma, artha, kama) and nivritti (withdrawal: moksha).
  • Most Hindus live engaged lives and turn more towards moksha with age.
Mission 2Read the source

Which aim is best?

Manusmriti 2.224 (paraphrase):

  • Some say the chief good is dharma and artha; others, kama and artha; others, dharma alone; others, artha alone.
  • The text's answer: the chief good is the three together (trivarga).

Q1 Which aim is missing from the debate in this verse?

Show answer

Moksha: the passage concerns life in the world.

Q2 What is Manu's own answer?

Show answer

All three in balance, not one alone.

Mission 2

The aims across the stages of life

  • Brahmacharya (student): learn dharma; self-discipline.
  • Grihastha (householder): pursue artha and kama within dharma; raise a family; support society.
  • Vanaprastha (retirement): loosen ties; growing vairagya (detachment, freedom from craving).
  • Sannyasa (renunciation): give up everything for moksha.
  • Vairagya is the bridge: detachment turns a person from the three worldly aims towards liberation.
Mission 2

Aims and varnashramadharma

  • Varnashramadharma: the dharma of one's class (varna) and stage of life (ashrama).
  • It sets how each person pursues the aims: a student does not seek kama; a sannyasi does not seek artha.
  • Traditional emphasis also differs by varna: teaching and ritual (brahmins), protection and rule (kshatriyas), trade and farming (vaishyas), service (shudras).
  • Many modern Hindus keep the aims and the stages but reject birth-based duties.
  • Your spec: the purusharthas' "relationship to varnasharamadharma" (spec spelling).
Mission 2

Punya: merit

  • Punya: merit or virtue, gained by acts done according to dharma: giving (dana), worship, pilgrimage, service, keeping vows.
  • Its opposite is papa: demerit from adharmic acts.
  • Hindus believe punya brings happiness and a good rebirth, even time in heaven; but it is still karma, and is held still to bind.

> "The mind of pure devotion--even here-- / Casts equally aside good deeds and bad, / Passing above them." (Gita 2:50, Arnold)

  • So the purusharthas lead somewhere: dharma earns punya; moksha goes beyond both punya and papa.

Mission 2Evaluate

Is moksha the most important aim?

  • Claim: Moksha is the most important of the four aims.
  • For: It alone ends samsara; the others are temporary and bind (even punya).
  • For: Upanishads and Vedanta make liberation the highest goal (parama purushartha).
  • Against: Dharma is the foundation: without it, no aim is pursued rightly, and moksha is not reached.
  • Against: For most Hindus the householder's three aims fill most of life; Manusmriti 2.224 praises the three together.
  • Against: For some bhakti traditions, loving God matters more than liberation itself.
  • Judgement criteria: Most important for whom, and at which stage of life? For the individual, or for society?

Mission 3

Varna + ashrama + dharma

  • Varnashramadharma: the dharma that fits your varna (class) and your ashrama (stage of life).
  • Its defenders present it as how universal order becomes social order: each person has a place and a duty, and society is held together as the cosmos is.
  • It is presented in the classical texts as an ideal social order: everyone's needs met by everyone's work. Critics see it as a justification of inequality.
Mission 3Read the source

The Purusha Sukta

Rig Veda 10.90 (paraphrase): the gods offer the cosmic Purusha (Person) in a great sacrifice. From his body the world is made. Verses 11–12:

  • From his mouth: the brahmana (priest).
  • From his arms: the rajanya (ruler, later called kshatriya).
  • From his thighs: the vaishya (merchant, farmer).
  • From his feet: the shudra (servant, labourer).
  • In the next verses the moon comes from his mind, the sun from his eye, the sky from his head.

Q1 What does the hymn suggest about society?

Show answer

Society as one body, each part with its own function; readers disagree whether head-to-foot order also implies a ranking.

Q2 What does it not mention?

Show answer

Jatis, untouchability, or rules about marriage and food.

Mission 3

Four varnas, four kinds of duty

  • As the classical texts describe them:
  • Brahmanas (brahmins): study and teach the Veda, perform ritual.
  • Kshatriyas: protect and rule; fight just wars.
  • Vaishyas: trade, farming, cattle.
  • Shudras: service of the other three.
  • The first three are dvija, "twice-born": the sacred-thread ceremony (upanayana) marks a second, spiritual birth.
  • The Gita lists each varna's qualities (Gita 18:41–44): serenity and learning for brahmins; courage for kshatriyas; trade and farming for vaishyas; service for shudras (paraphrase).

?In the Purusha Sukta, which varna comes from the cosmic Person's arms?
Mission 3Diversity

By birth or by nature?

Krishna in the Gita:

> "I am He / Made the Four Castes, and portioned them a place / After their qualities and gifts." (Gita 4:13, Arnold)

  • The Sanskrit says by guna (qualities) and karma (actions).
  • By birth: the Dharma Shastras (e.g. Manusmriti) assign varna by birth; duties are inherited.
  • By nature: many reformers read varna as a matter of qualities: Dayananda Saraswati (Arya Samaj); Vivekananda; today ISKCON and others.
Mission 3

Varna and jati

  • Varna: the four-fold scheme of the texts; ideal, all-India.
  • Jati: "birth group": thousands of local communities, often linked to an occupation, usually marrying within the group (endogamy), ranked by ideas of ritual purity.
  • Overlap: jatis claim or are given a place within a varna; that place can be disputed and has shifted over time.
  • Difference: the texts know four varnas; daily social life in India has been organised far more by jati.
  • English "caste" (from Portuguese casta) is used for both, which causes confusion.
  • Communities placed outside the four varnas were treated as "untouchable"; many now call themselves Dalits.
Quick check
?Which statement correctly contrasts varna and jati?
Mission 3

Jati in modern India: the debate

  • The Constitution of India (1950) abolished "untouchability" (Article 17) and banned discrimination by caste (Article 15); reserved places in education and jobs help disadvantaged groups.
  • Jati still shapes marriage, social networks and politics for many Indians; caste discrimination continues and is contested.
  • Views differ:
  • Reform: varna as an ideal of interdependent duties; jati ranking and untouchability as corruption (Gandhi's position for much of his life).
  • Abolition: the whole system is unjust and must go (B. R. Ambedkar, 1891–1956, who left Hinduism for Buddhism in 1956).
  • Identity: some value jati as community and heritage, without hierarchy.
Mission 3Evaluate

Does the Purusha Sukta make caste sacred?

  • Claim: The Purusha Sukta gives religious authority to the caste system.
  • For: It is shruti (revealed scripture), the highest authority, and it places the classes in a body from head to foot.
  • For: Later law books (Manusmriti) build birth-based rules on it.
  • Against: It is a late hymn (Book 10) and one image among many; it may describe the unity of society, not a ranking.
  • Against: It names four functions, not thousands of jatis or untouchability.
  • Against: Other teachings point another way: the wise look equally on a brahmin, a cow and an outcaste (Gita 5:18); varna by qualities (4:13).
  • Judgement criteria: What did the hymn mean then? How has it been used? What weight do other texts carry?
Quick check

Mission 3

Varnashramadharma and righteous living

  • Doing one's svadharma well is righteous living: it earns punya (merit) and good karma.
  • Good karma leads to a better rebirth; on the traditional view, a better birth may be into another varna (Chandogya V.10.7).
  • The purusharthas are pursued within varnashramadharma: what counts as right artha for a kshatriya is not the same as for a brahmin (see Mission 2).
  • The Gita adds: duty done without desire for its fruit leads beyond karma towards moksha.
  • Spec issues: the relationship between living virtuously and achieving liberation; virtues in a later lesson.

Mission 4

Four stages of life

  • Brahmacharya: the student. Learn from a guru; celibacy, discipline, study of dharma.
  • Grihastha: the householder. Marry, work, raise children, give, worship.
  • Vanaprastha: the "forest-dweller", retirement. Hand on responsibilities; turn to spiritual practice.
  • Sannyasa: the renouncer (a sannyasi). Give up home, property and name; seek moksha.
  • Traditionally for twice-born men, beginning with the sacred thread; often pictured as four spans of about 25 years.
Mission 4

The householder holds it all up

  • Manusmriti 3.77–78 (paraphrase): as all creatures live by the air, so all the other stages live by the householder; it is the most important stage.
  • Householders feed students, support renouncers, care for elders and children, and keep worship going.
  • Traditional five daily offerings (pancha mahayajna): to the gods, the sages (study), the ancestors, other people (hospitality) and all beings (food for animals).
  • So a full worldly life is not a lesser path: it is dharma.
Quick check
?Why does Manusmriti call the grihastha stage the most important?
Mission 4

The stages today

  • Few Hindus follow all four stages strictly. Student and householder stages are almost universal; vanaprastha is often a gradual shift in retirement towards prayer, pilgrimage and service.
  • Sannyasis are a small minority; some renounce young without marrying (Shankara, by tradition, as a boy).
  • Many orders once accepted men only; women renouncers exist (e.g. the Sri Sarada Math, a women's order founded 1954).
  • In Britain and elsewhere, the stages are often taught as an ideal of a balanced life rather than a rule.
Mission 4Read the source

Own duty and universal duty

> "Better thine own work is, though done with fault, / Than doing others' work, ev'n excellently." (Gita 18:47, Arnold)

  • Link: both are dharma; varnashrama dharma is how universal dharma becomes concrete for one person at one stage.
  • Difference: sanatana dharma (non-violence, truth, purity, self-control) binds everyone always; varnashrama dharma changes with class and stage.
  • Tension: a warrior's duty to fight against universal non-violence (Arjuna).

Show answer

Q2 Which kind of dharma is it talking about?

Show answer

Svadharma: varnashrama dharma.

Mission 4

Bhakti changes the rules

  • Bhakti: loving devotion to God. In bhakti, what counts most is love, not birth, stage or ritual rank.

> "whoso will turn to Me, / Though they be born from the very womb of Sin, / Woman or man; sprung of the Vaisya caste / Or lowly disregarded Sudra,--all / Plant foot upon the highest path" (Gita 9:32, Arnold)

  • Bhakti poet-saints came from every background, including communities treated as untouchable: Nandanar (Tamil Shaiva saint), Chokhamela (14th-century Maharashtra), Ravidas (15th–16th-century north India, from a leather-working community).
  • Chaitanya (1486–1534) shared the chanting of Krishna's names with all, regardless of caste; Basava (12th century) founded a Shaiva movement that rejected caste.
  • Bhakti often relaxed the rules in worship more than in marriage or daily life: the change was real but uneven.
Quick check
?How did bhakti movements most often change the rules of varnashramadharma?
Mission 4

Community and the meaning of life

  • Community of believers: family (where most practice happens), jati networks, sampradayas (traditions), gurus and satsang (gatherings of devotees), the mandir.
  • The community passes on and interprets dharma, supports each stage (feeding sannyasis, teaching students) and, in the diaspora, keeps identity alive.
  • Meaning and purpose of life: to fulfil one's dharma through every stage, pay the three debts (rinas) to the gods (worship), the sages (study) and the ancestors (children), and finally reach moksha.
Mission 4

Stages, aims, karma

  • Each ashrama gives a different weight to the four aims: dharma (student), artha and kama within dharma (householder), detachment (retiree), moksha (renouncer) (see Mission 2).
  • Hindus believe that doing each stage's duty well earns punya and good karma, leading to a better rebirth.
  • Renunciation aims beyond karma: the sannyasi seeks to act without desire and so to end samsara.
  • So varnashramadharma links social life to liberation: righteous living prepares the way.
  • Your spec: the relationship of varnashramadharma with the purusharthas, karma and samsara.
Mission 4Evaluate

Are the ashramas still relevant?

  • Claim: The four ashramas are no longer relevant to Hindu life today.
  • For: Few Hindus become forest-dwellers or renouncers; modern careers and pensions do not fit the scheme.
  • For: Traditionally the scheme was for twice-born men only; it leaves most people out.
  • Against: The ideal (learn, contribute, let go, seek God) fits any modern life and is widely taught.
  • Against: Vanaprastha is lived out as retirement given to prayer, pilgrimage and service; sannyasa still flourishes in orders and ashrams.
  • Judgement criteria: Relevant as a rule, or as an ideal? For whom: India or the diaspora; men or women?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Dharma (from dhr, "uphold"): the order that sustains the cosmos, society and the person; the successor to Vedic rta.
  • Sanatana dharma: the eternal way (a name for the religion) and the universal duties of all; svadharma: duties of one's own class and stage of life.
  • Adharma grows through the four yugas; God restores dharma "from age to age" (Gita 4:7–8).

Mission 2

  • Four purusharthas: dharma, artha, kama, moksha; dharma frames artha and kama; moksha goes beyond all three.
  • Relative importance is debated: Manusmriti 2.224 praises the three together; Vedanta makes moksha the highest aim.
  • Dharmic acts earn punya; moksha goes beyond punya and papa (Gita 2:50).

Mission 3

  • Varnashramadharma: duty by class (varna) and stage of life (ashrama), cosmic order as social order.
  • The Purusha Sukta (Rig Veda 10.90.11–12): brahmana from the mouth, rajanya from the arms, vaishya from the thighs, shudra from the feet.
  • Varna is a four-fold textual scheme; jatis are thousands of local birth groups; Hindus debate birth versus qualities (Gita 4:13) and the place of jati today.

Mission 4

  • Four ashramas: brahmacharya (student), grihastha (householder, the support of all), vanaprastha (retirement), sannyasa (renunciation).
  • Varnashrama dharma makes universal (sanatana) dharma concrete for each person; Gita 18:47 values one's own dharma over another's.
  • Bhakti made devotion, not birth, the measure in worship (Gita 9:32), though caste often persisted in social life.
