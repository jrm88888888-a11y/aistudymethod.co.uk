Mission 1

Every crime story asks 'whodunit'. What else is it asking?

Make a guess: Every crime story asks 'whodunit'. What else is it asking?

Crime writing · conventions and the detectiveScreen 1

Two stories in one

  • Tzvetan Todorov argued that the classic detective novel holds two stories: the story of the crime (in the past, hidden) and the story of the investigation (in the present, which we read).
  • A whodunit starts from consequences (a body, a theft, clues) and works back to the cause.
  • But crime texts ask more: why (motive), who suffers (victims), can order be restored, and what the crime reveals about the society around it.

Crime writing · conventions and the detectiveScreen 5

The crime behind the crime

  • The professional detective: Collins drew on Detective Inspector Jack Whicher and the 1860 Road Hill House murder. Yet Cuff doesn't reach the full solution.
  • A crime in the past: the Prologue tells how John Herncastle seized the diamond at the storming of Seringapatam (1799). His cousin writes "that crime brings its own fatality with it."
  • So the 1848 theft points back to an imperial crime. T. S. Eliot (1927) called the novel the first and greatest English detective novel.
Crime writing · conventions and the detectiveScreen 6

Elements in your set texts

  • Hamlet: a revenge tragedy with a detective plot. A ghost names the murderer; Hamlet tests the claim: "The play’s the thing / Wherein I’ll catch the conscience of the King." (Act 2 Scene 2).
  • Oliver Twist: a life lost to crime. After murdering Nancy, Sikes sees "Those widely staring eyes" everywhere (Chapter XLVIII): guilt as punishment before the law acts.
  • The Rime of the Ancient Mariner: a crime against nature, then penance and confession.
  • No detective solves these: ask what each text does instead.

Crime writing · crime in poetryScreen 5

Sympathy and judgement

  • A dramatic monologue has one speaker, a silent listener and a revealing moment. Dramatic irony: the speaker shows more than he means.
  • Robert Langbaum (1957) argued that we half-suspend moral judgement, drawn into the Duke's power and confidence.
  • Glenn Everett replies that we read from the envoy's side: we feel the rhetoric's pull but still judge.
  • In 'Porphyria's Lover' the killer waits for a verdict: "And yet God has not said a word!" The reader supplies it.

Crime writing · crime in poetryScreen 7

Narrators who judge, and judges who are judged

  • Coleridge: confession is the Mariner's penance, and it changes the listener: the Wedding-Guest leaves "A sadder and a wiser man," (Part VII).
  • Crabbe: the town hears an apprentice's cries and says "Grimes is at his exercise." Three boys die; banned from taking more, Grimes is haunted and confesses as he dies.
  • Wilde: a prisoner watches a soldier hanged in 1896 for killing his wife, then turns judgement outwards: "Yet each man kills the thing he loves".

Mission checkScreen 9

?A murderer speaks and we listen calmly. What does the poem make us do?

Crime poems hand the reader the role of judge. The speaker's calm, the listener's silence and the gaps in what is said make us notice what the speaker hides, while we feel the pull of his voice. Strong answers explore that tension between sympathy and judgement.

Mission 3

Two novels, 1818 and 1898, both about science. What connection scores?

Make a guess: Two novels, 1818 and 1898, both about science. What connection scores?

A-level skills · connections and comparison (AO4)Screen 1

What AO4 asks

  • Every board's AO4: explore connections across literary texts.
  • Connect through theme (what the texts are about), method (voice, imagery, structure), genre (Gothic, science fiction, tragedy) or context (the ideas each text answers).
  • Difference counts as much as likeness: the strongest connections show two texts treating one idea in different ways.
  • AQA: even a single-text answer can place the text among others of its period or genre.
A-level skills · connections and comparison (AO4)Screen 2

How your exam asks this

  • AQA Literature B: AO4 counts in every question (about 12%). Paper 1 Section C and Paper 2 Section C each set one essay connecting two texts (25 marks), through the genre you've studied.

A connection scores when it goes beyond the shared topic. Compare how each text treats the idea, using precise evidence and methods from both, and explain the differences, often through context.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Crime texts hold two stories: the crime (hidden, past) and the investigation (present). The best answers ask what the crime reveals, not just who did it.
  • In The Moonstone, Sergeant Cuff is an unreadable outsider, and the 1848 theft points back to plunder at Seringapatam in 1799.
  • In Hamlet, Oliver Twist and the Mariner, look for the elements AQA names: guilt, punishment, victims, order restored or not.
  • A dramatic monologue gives one speaker and a silent listener; dramatic irony lets us see more than the speaker means, so we judge from his own words.
  • Critics debate how far we sympathise (Langbaum) or stand with the listener and judge (Everett).
  • Coleridge, Crabbe and Wilde add confession, haunting and punishment: the tale changes the listener, and Wilde turns judgement back on society.
  • AO4 rewards connections of theme, method, genre and context, and difference counts as much as likeness.
  • Move from topic ("both about science") to a connection that compares how each text works and why they differ.
  • Shelley's scientist gazes down at his creation; Wells turns the microscope on humanity: one idea, opposite ends of the lens.
  • AQA marks AO4 in every question, as well as the linking essays.

Key quotations to learn

  • He might have been a parson, or an undertaker—or anything else you like, except what he really was. (The Moonstone, First Period, Chapter XII (Betteredge)) The detective as an unreadable outsider.
  • crime brings its own fatality with it (The Moonstone, Prologue (Herncastle's cousin)) The curse of the first, imperial crime.
  • detective-fever (The Moonstone, First Period, Chapter XV (Betteredge)) The reader's and narrator's hunger to solve.
  • links of iron in the wonderful chain forged by the science of the detective officer (Lady Audley's Secret, Chapter XV (Robert Audley)) Detection as a science of evidence.
  • The play’s the thing / Wherein I’ll catch the conscience of the King. (Hamlet, Act 2 Scene 2 (Hamlet)) The revenger turns detective.
  • Those widely staring eyes (Oliver Twist, Chapter XLVIII) Guilt punishes Sikes before the law does.
  • That's my last Duchess painted on the wall, ('My Last Duchess', line 1) The victim survives only as a possession.
  • This grew; I gave commands; / Then all smiles stopped together. ('My Last Duchess', lines 45–46) Murder hidden behind polite authority.
  • And yet God has not said a word! ('Porphyria's Lover', last line) The killer waits for a judgement the reader gives.
  • Which is the poison to poison her, prithee? ('The Laboratory', line 4) Jealous motive in a courtly, almost playful voice.
  • He holds him with his glittering eye— (Ancient Mariner, Part I) The listener is held: we can't choose but hear.
  • A sadder and a wiser man, (Ancient Mariner, Part VII) The tale of crime changes the one who hears it.
