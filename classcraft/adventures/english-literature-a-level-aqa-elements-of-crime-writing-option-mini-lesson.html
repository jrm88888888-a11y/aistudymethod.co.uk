Genre · first principles

What crime writing is for

Crime writing is not simply writing that contains a crime. It is the genre that stages a violation of the moral and social order, and then works out what — if anything — can restore it.

The genre's core question is not who did it but what does the crime reveal about the society that produced it? Whodunnit is the surface; the deep structure is guilt, punishment, justice and order.

  • Transgression — a boundary is crossed: legal, moral, sexual, social.
  • Detection — the attempt to know. Crime writing is an epistemological genre: it is about how truth is established from traces.
  • Punishment and justice — which may or may not coincide. The gap between the two is where the best essays live.
  • Restoration — order returns, or fails to. Conservative crime fiction restores it fully; noir and much literary crime writing refuses.

Genre · types

The sub-genres of crime writing

Identify what kind of crime text you have. Each has different rules and different politics:

  • Classic / Golden Age detective fiction — a puzzle, a closed circle of suspects, a brilliant detective, order fully restored. Ronald Knox's Ten Commandments (1929) and S. S. Van Dine's rules codified the fair-play contract with the reader.
  • Hard-boiled and noir — the city is corrupt, the detective is compromised, and solving the crime changes nothing. Raymond Chandler's essay The Simple Art of Murder attacked the artificiality of the country-house puzzle and demanded a fiction in which murder was given back to the people who actually commit it.
  • Crime from the criminal's side — the confession, the psychology of guilt, the reader made complicit.
  • Gothic and revenge crime — older forms: the avenger, the haunted conscience, the punishment that damns the punisher.
  • Literary crime — the crime as a device to examine class, empire, gender or memory; the solution deliberately withheld.

Exemplar, not syllabus: texts named here only illustrate the method. AQA rotates and revises its set-text lists — swap in whatever your class studies. The paper examines the genre, not a book.

Genre · roles

Criminal, detective, victim — and the reader

  • The criminal — the crucial question is whether the text makes the criminal an alien intruder (order is essentially sound; one bad person breached it) or a product of the society (order itself is criminal). This distinction predicts the whole politics of a crime text.
  • The detective — a figure of reason and of licensed intrusion. Notice how detectives are usually outsiders (foreign, amateur, disgraced, female in a male institution), which lets them see what the establishment cannot, or will not.
  • The victim — often the most ideologically loaded figure. Which victims does the genre grieve, and which does it use merely as a puzzle-piece? Whose body is the plot?
  • The reader as detective — you are given the same clues, invited to suspect, and often made complicit: crime writing lets us enjoy the transgression and then be reassured by its punishment.

W. H. Auden's essay The Guilty Vicarage (1948) argued that detective fiction appeals to readers who feel guilty: the genre stages an expulsion of guilt onto a single culprit, so that everyone else may be declared innocent. That is a genuinely useful AO5 lens for almost any classic detective text.

AO2 · structure

Structure: two stories, told backwards

The single most useful piece of narrative theory for this paper comes from Tzvetan Todorov, who argued that detective fiction contains two stories: the story of the crime (what happened, told in the past, absent) and the story of the investigation (how it is reconstructed, told in the present, on the page). The whole art of the genre is the relationship between them.

Consequences you can use in an essay:

  • The narrative is told backwards. The plot moves forward while the story it recovers moves back. That is why crime writing is obsessed with traces, testimony, and unreliable memory.
  • Withholding is the method. Suspense is manufactured by controlling what the reader is allowed to know and when. Ask: who narrates, and what can they not see?
  • The red herring is a structural device that makes the reader's own reasoning part of the text's subject.
  • The confession or reveal is the moment the two stories fuse. Test whether the reveal satisfies or disturbs — literary crime writing often makes the solution worse than the mystery.
  • Foreshadowing, retrospect and the framed narrative — a narrator writing after the event has already made a moral judgement, and the prose carries it.

AO3 / AO5

Context: crime, class, gender and empire

Crime writing is deeply historical. It emerges alongside the modern police force, the newspaper, the city and the science of evidence, and it carries the anxieties of each.

  • Class — a Marxist reading asks whose property the law protects, and notices that theft against the rich is a crime while destitution is not. The country-house mystery is an entire ideology in a floor-plan.
  • Gender — a feminist reading counts the female corpses. Crime writing has an old habit of building suspense out of violence against women while granting them little interiority; more recent crime writing frequently makes that habit its subject.
  • Empire and race — a post-colonial reading notices where the crime is said to have come from: the foreign, the colonial, the racialised outsider. It also notices who is presumed guilty by a text before any evidence appears.
  • Reception — a text once read as reassuring can now be read as complicit; the class assumptions that once passed unnoticed are now the loudest thing on the page. That change is AO3, and it is also AO5.

The high-mark synthesis: the sub-genre predicts the politics. A form that expels a single culprit and restores the house tends to argue that society is sound. A form that reveals corruption reaching the top tends to argue that it is not.

Section A

The unseen passage: a method that works

Section A gives you an unseen extract and asks how it presents an element of crime writing. You have never seen it, and you cannot revise it — so revise the method instead.

  1. Two minutes of triage. What kind of crime text is this — puzzle, noir, confession, gothic, literary? Who narrates, and can they be trusted? Where does it sit: before, during or after the crime?
  2. Fix a line of argument in one sentence, drawn from the genre: this passage generates suspense by making the reader's knowledge exceed the victim's, so that we become spectators of a death we cannot prevent.
  3. Analyse in four moves: narrative voice and perspective → structure and pacing (withholding, delay, the placement of the revelation) → setting and atmosphere → lexis and imagery (violence, decay, the law, the body).
  4. Every observation ends in the genre. Method → effect → element of crime writing.
  5. Do not invent context. If you do not know the author or the date, say what the passage itself implies, and say it tentatively. Confident invention is punished; careful inference is not.

Do not quote what you cannot see. With unseen material, quote briefly and accurately from the passage in front of you — and never import a half-remembered quotation from a set text.

AO4 · Section C

Connecting two texts

Section C connects two texts across the genre. Structure the essay by element, not by text:

  1. Thesis from the genre: Crime writing promises that guilt can be located in a person. Both texts test that promise, and only one of them still believes it.
  2. Element-paragraphs: the criminal; the investigator; the victim; the restoration (or refusal) of order — each drawing on both texts.
  3. Use the pre-1900 text to expose the modern one, and vice versa: what has changed is rarely the crime and almost always the confidence.
  4. Land the difference. AO4 marks reward the illuminating connection, not the tidy one.

Time discipline: three sections, 25 marks each. The unseen must not eat the comparison.

The big ideas to take into the exam

Core elements: transgression · detection · guilt · punishment vs justice · restoration of order (or its refusal)

Sub-genres: Golden Age puzzle (Knox's rules) · hard-boiled and noir (Chandler) · the criminal's confession · gothic and revenge · literary crime

Roles: criminal as intruder or as product of society · the detective as licensed outsider · the ideologically loaded victim · the complicit reader (Auden, The Guilty Vicarage)

Structure: Todorov's two stories — the crime and the investigation; withholding, red herrings, unreliable narration, the reveal
