Mission 1: How can you titrate a tablet that won't even dissolve in water?

Make a guess: How can you titrate a tablet that won't even dissolve in water?

Mission 1

When you can't titrate directly

  • Some samples can't go straight into a titration: an insoluble solid (CaCO3, MgO), a slow reaction, or no sharp end point.
  • Back titration: add a known excess of a standard acid. The solid reacts completely.
  • Then titrate the leftover acid with a standard alkali.
  • Acid that reacted = acid added − acid left over.
  • AP won't test the term "back titration": it is two titration stoichiometry steps chained together.
Mission 1

Worked example: an indigestion tablet

  • A 1.250 g tablet (active ingredient CaCO3) is added to 50.0 mL of 0.500 M HCl. The leftover acid needs 22.40 mL of 0.400 M NaOH.
  • CaCO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g). Assume nothing else in the tablet reacts with acid.
  • Acid added: 0.500 × 0.0500 = 0.02500 mol.
  • Acid left: n(NaOH) = 0.400 × 0.02240 = 0.00896 mol (HCl : NaOH = 1 : 1).
  • Acid reacted: 0.02500 − 0.00896 = 0.01604 mol.
  • n(CaCO3) = 0.01604 ÷ 2 = 0.00802 mol; mass = 0.00802 × 100.1 = 0.803 g = 64.2% of the tablet.
Quick check
?In a back titration, an insoluble carbonate is added to excess hydrochloric acid, and the leftover acid is titrated with sodium hydroxide. Which amount equals the moles of HCl that reacted with the carbonate?
Mission 1

Dilutions and portions

  • A solution is made up to 250.0 mL in a volumetric flask. A 25.00 mL portion (aliquot) is pipetted out and titrated.
  • The portion holds one tenth of the total moles. Scale back up: × 10.
  • Diluting changes molarity, not moles: M1V1 = M2V2.
  • Track moles through every step. Scale at the right moment, and say what each number is the moles of.
Quick check
?A 10.0 mL sample of vinegar is diluted to 100.0 mL in a volumetric flask. A 25.00 mL portion needs 20.75 mL of 0.100 M NaOH. CH3COOH + NaOH → CH3COONa + H2O. What was the molarity of acetic acid in the original vinegar?
Mission check

?An impure sample of magnesium oxide, an insoluble base, is analyzed by back titration. MgO(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2O(l). Assume the impurities do not react with acid.
  • A 0.400 g sample is added to 25.0 mL of 1.00 M HCl (excess). The leftover acid needs 14.30 mL of 0.500 M NaOH. What is the percentage by mass of MgO in the sample? (Molar masses: O 16.00 g/mol, Mg 24.31 g/mol)

You never have to dissolve the solid in water. Dissolve it in a measured excess of acid, titrate what is left, and the difference is what the solid used.

Mission 2

  • Oxidation and reduction always happen together: a redox reaction.
  • The electron and oxidation-number definitions work for every redox reaction.
Mission 2

Oxidation numbers: the rules

  • An uncombined element is 0: Fe, O2, Cl2.
  • A simple ion's oxidation number is its charge: Fe³⁺ is +3.
  • Usually O is −2 and H is +1. F is −1 in all its compounds.
  • In compounds, group 1 metals are +1; group 2 metals, +2.
  • The numbers add up to the charge on the species: 0 for a compound.
Mission 2

Worked examples

  • SO4²⁻: x + 4(−2) = −2, so S is +6.
  • H2S: 2(+1) + x = 0, so S is −2.
  • Cr2O7²⁻: 2x + 7(−2) = −2, so 2x = +12 and each Cr is +6.
  • Rust, Fe2O3: 2x + 3(−2) = 0, so each Fe is +3.
  • Write the sign first: +3, not 3+.
Mission 2

Rusting: where the electrons go

  • Simplified: 4Fe(s) + 3O2(g) → 2Fe2O3(s).
  • Fe: 0 → +3. Each Fe atom loses 3 electrons: oxidized.
  • O: 0 → −2. Each O atom gains 2 electrons: reduced.
  • Electrons move from the species oxidized to the species reduced.
Mission 2

Oxidizing and reducing agents

  • The oxidizing agent takes electrons, so it is itself reduced.
  • The reducing agent gives electrons, so it is itself oxidized.
  • Agents are reactants, never products.
  • Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu. Zn: 0 → +2, oxidized: the reducing agent. Cu²⁺: +2 → 0, reduced: the oxidizing agent.
  • AP questions ask which species is oxidized or reduced, not for these names.
Quick check
?A student places a copper wire in AgNO3(aq). Silver metal deposits on the wire, and the solution turns blue as copper ions form. The table shows the student's data. Which claim is best supported by the data?
Mission 2

Combustion is redox

  • Combustion: a species reacts with oxygen gas.
  • Complete combustion of a hydrocarbon gives CO2 and H2O.
  • CH4(g) + 2 O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2 H2O(g)
  • C: −4 → +4, oxidized. O: 0 → −2, reduced. H stays +1.
Mission 2

Half-equations

  • A half-equation shows the oxidation or the reduction alone, with the electrons written in.
  • AP calls these half-reactions.
  • Oxidation: electrons are products. Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻.
  • Reduction: electrons are reactants. Cl2 + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻.
  • Both atoms and charge must balance.
Mission 2

Balancing in acid: worked example

Cr2O7²⁻ → Cr³⁺ in acidic solution:

  • Balance Cr: Cr2O7²⁻ → 2Cr³⁺.
  • Balance O with H2O: add 7H2O on the right.
  • Balance H with H⁺: add 14H⁺ on the left.
  • Balance charge with e⁻: left +12, right +6, so add 6e⁻ on the left.
  • Cr2O7²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6e⁻ → 2Cr³⁺ + 7H2O. Check: 2 Cr each fall by 3 (+6 → +3), so 6e⁻.
  • Same steps: MnO4⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H2O (Mn +7 → +2).
Mission 2

Combining half-equations

  • Scale so the electrons lost equal the electrons gained, then add and cancel them.
  • Oxidation: Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + e⁻. Multiply by 6 to give 6e⁻.
  • Add to the dichromate(VI) half-equation from the earlier screen; cancel 6e⁻:
  • Cr2O7²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6Fe²⁺ → 2Cr³⁺ + 7H2O + 6Fe³⁺
  • Check charge: left −2 + 14 + 12 = +24; right +6 + 18 = +24.
  • This is the balanced net ionic equation.
Quick check
?In acidic solution, MnO4⁻(aq) oxidizes Fe²⁺(aq) to Fe³⁺(aq) and is itself reduced to Mn²⁺(aq). Which equation is the balanced net ionic equation for the reaction? (States are omitted; all ions are aqueous and H2O is liquid.)
Mission check

?In the thermite reaction, aluminum reacts with iron(III) oxide: Fe2O3(s) + 2Al(s) → Al2O3(s) + 2Fe(l). What happens to the iron?

Rusting: Fe 0 → +3, so iron loses electrons. In thermite, iron gains them back. Oxidation numbers tell you which way.

Mission 3: How can nitrogen be −3 in ammonia but +5 in nitric acid?

Make a guess: How can nitrogen be −3 in ammonia but +5 in nitric acid?

Mission 3

What an oxidation number is

  • The oxidation number is the charge an atom would have if all its bonds were ionic.
  • Shared electrons are given to the more electronegative atom.
  • So an atom's oxidation number depends on what it is bonded to.
  • Atoms in an element are 0: identical atoms, so neither takes the shared electrons.
  • Write the sign first: +2, −1. (Ion charges are written 2+, 1−.)
Mission 3

The rules

  • In a compound the oxidation numbers sum to zero; in an ion, to the ion's charge.
  • O is −2, except −1 in peroxides, such as H2O2 and Na2O2.
  • H is +1, except −1 in metal hydrides, such as NaH and CaH2.
  • Group 1 metals +1, group 2 metals +2, fluorine always −1.
  • Find the unknown atom last.
Mission 3

Worked examples

  • NH3: x + 3(+1) = 0, so N is −3. N is more electronegative than H.
  • HNO3: +1 + x + 3(−2) = 0, so N is +5. O is more electronegative than N.
  • SO4²⁻: x + 4(−2) = −2, so S is +6.
  • H2O2: 2(+1) + 2x = 0, so O is −1 (a peroxide).
  • CaH2: +2 + 2x = 0, so H is −1 (a metal hydride).
Quick check
?What is the oxidation number of carbon in the oxalate ion, C2O4²⁻?
Calculate
?What is the oxidation number of phosphorus in the diphosphate ion, P2O7⁴⁻?
Mission 3

Oxidation numbers in names

  • Background: some names show an oxidation number as a Roman numeral.
  • FeCl2 is iron(II) chloride; FeCl3 is iron(III) chloride.
  • KMnO4: +1 + x + 4(−2) = 0, so Mn is +7. It is called potassium permanganate, or potassium manganate(VII).
Mission check

?Chlorine is −1 in the chloride ion, Cl⁻, but +1 in the hypochlorite ion, ClO⁻ (in bleach). Which explains the +1?

Same element, different partners. Bonded to less electronegative atoms (N to H) it is negative; bonded to more electronegative ones (N to O) it is positive.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Is water an acid or a base?

Make a guess: Is water an acid or a base?

Mission 4

Acids give protons, bases take them

  • A Brønsted–Lowry acid is a proton (H⁺) donor.
  • A Brønsted–Lowry base is a proton acceptor. It uses a lone pair to take the H⁺.
  • HNO3 + NH3 → NH4⁺ + NO3⁻: HNO3 donates H⁺; NH3 accepts it.
  • An acid–base reaction is a proton transfer.
  • Hydronium, H3O⁺(aq), is preferred; H⁺(aq) is also accepted.
Mission 4

Base or alkali?

  • An alkali is a base that dissolves in water, releasing OH⁻(aq): NaOH, KOH, NH3(aq).
  • Every alkali is a base; not every base is an alkali.
  • Iron(III) oxide neutralizes acids, so it is a base. It is insoluble, so it is not an alkali.
  • A base need not contain OH: NH3, O²⁻ and CO3²⁻ all accept H⁺ with a lone pair.
  • Background: AP Chemistry uses "base" for all of these and does not use "alkali".
Quick check
?Each equation below represents a proton transfer read from left to right. In which one does H2O act as the Brønsted–Lowry acid?
Mission 4

Conjugate acid–base pairs

  • A conjugate pair differs by exactly one proton.
  • Conjugate base: remove one H⁺; the charge falls by one. HSO4⁻ → SO4²⁻.
  • Conjugate acid: add one H⁺; the charge rises by one. NH3 → NH4⁺.
  • Every proton transfer has two pairs: HNO3/NO3⁻ and NH4⁺/NH3.
  • The acid and base that react are not a pair: each one's partner is across the arrow.
  • Pairs can be compared: the stronger the acid, the weaker its conjugate base.
Quick check
?The reaction HSO4⁻(aq) + NH3(aq) → SO4²⁻(aq) + NH4⁺(aq) occurs when the two species are mixed. Which choice gives the Brønsted–Lowry acid in the forward reaction, followed by its conjugate base?
Mission 4

Amphiprotic species

  • Amphiprotic: can act as a Brønsted–Lowry acid or a base, depending on its partner.
  • It needs an H it can lose and a lone pair to accept H⁺.
  • Examples: H2O, HSO4⁻, H2PO4⁻, HPO4²⁻.
  • In any one reaction it plays one role only.
Mission 4

Water both ways: writing the equations

  • Water as a base: HNO3 + H2O → H3O⁺ + NO3⁻.
  • Water as an acid: NH3 + H2O ⇌ NH4⁺ + OH⁻.
  • HPO4²⁻ as a base: HPO4²⁻ + H3O⁺ ⇌ H2PO4⁻ + H2O.
  • HPO4²⁻ as an acid: HPO4²⁻ + OH⁻ ⇌ PO4³⁻ + H2O.
  • Check: as a base it gains one H; as an acid it loses one. Charges balance.
Quick check
?Which equation shows the hydrogensulfate ion, HSO4⁻, acting as a Brønsted–Lowry acid?
Mission check

?Water takes part in two reactions: (1) CH3NH2 + H2O ⇌ CH3NH3⁺ + OH⁻ and (2) HBr + H2O → H3O⁺ + Br⁻. What role does water play?

Water is both, but one at a time. Faced with an acid it acts as a base; faced with a base it acts as an acid.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Back titration: acid reacted = acid added − acid left over; then use the equation's ratio.
  • With dilutions and portions, track moles step by step and scale (× 10 for a 25 of 250 portion).

Mission 2

  • Oxidation = electron loss, oxidation number rises; reduction = electron gain, oxidation number falls.
  • Half-equations: balance atoms (H2O, H⁺ in acid), then charge with e⁻; scale so electrons cancel.

Mission 3

  • Oxidation number: the charge if all bonds were ionic, shared electrons to the more electronegative atom.
  • Sum to zero (compound) or to the charge (ion); O −1 in peroxides, H −1 in metal hydrides.

Mission 4

  • Acid = proton donor; base = proton acceptor (using a lone pair). Conjugate pairs differ by one H⁺.
  • Water, like HSO4⁻ and HPO4²⁻, is amphiprotic: its partner decides its role.
