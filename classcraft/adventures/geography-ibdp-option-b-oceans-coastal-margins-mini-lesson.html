Mission 1

Surface currents and gyres

  • Wind, tides, and differences in temperature and salinity drive ocean currents.
  • Earth's rotation deflects moving air and water to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern: the Coriolis effect.
  • Surface currents form gyres, large rotating systems. There are five major gyres: the North and South Pacific, the North and South Atlantic, and the Indian Ocean subtropical gyres.
Mission 1

Warm and cold currents

  • On the western side of each ocean basin, warm currents carry tropical water towards the poles (the Gulf Stream, the Kuroshio).
  • The Gulf Stream's extension, the North Atlantic Drift, carries warm water on towards north-west Europe.
  • On the eastern side, cold currents flow towards the equator (the Humboldt off Peru, the Canary, the Benguela).
  • Warm currents warm and moisten the air above them; cold currents cool it and often bring fog and aridity to the coast.

Mission 1

The global conveyor belt

  • Below the surface, the thermohaline circulation is driven by density, which depends on temperature (thermo) and salinity (haline).
  • When sea ice forms, the remaining seawater gets saltier and denser, and starts to sink. Water that loses heat to the atmosphere also becomes denser and sinks.
  • NOAA describes the global ocean conveyor belt as getting its "start" in the Norwegian Sea.
  • The Atlantic part is the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC).

Mission 1

Nutrient transfers: upwelling

  • Upwelling is a process in which deep, cold water rises towards the surface. Winds blowing along the shore push surface water away.
  • Upwelled water is typically colder and rich in nutrients, so good fishing grounds are found where upwelling is common.
  • Off Peru, upwelling in the Humboldt Current feeds the anchoveta fishery, which in recent years made up to 71% of catches in the South-east Pacific.

Mission 1

Energy transfers

  • Currents move heat from the tropics towards the poles. NOAA calls the Gulf Stream "the main conveyor of heat from south to north in the Atlantic".
  • This is an energy transfer: the ocean releases the heat to the air in higher latitudes.
Mission 1

Does the Gulf Stream warm Europe? (AO3)

  • Seager et al. (2002) modelled Europe's mild winters. They found "the principal cause" of the winter contrast with eastern North America "is advection by the mean winds".
  • Ocean heat transport warms winters on both sides of the North Atlantic "by a few degC", in an essentially uniform way, so it does not explain the contrast between them.
  • Exam move: an influential model study is evidence, not proof. Say what it found, and weigh it against other explanations.
Mission 1

A changing conveyor

  • IPCC (2021): the AMOC "is very likely to weaken over the 21st century for all emissions scenarios".
  • It has "medium confidence that there will not be an abrupt collapse before 2100".
  • If a collapse did occur, it would very likely bring abrupt shifts such as weaker African and Asian monsoons and drying in Europe.
  • Synthesis: a change in one part of the ocean system has effects far away.
Mission check

?Is the Gulf Stream why north-west Europe has mild winters?

Partly. Ocean heat transport, of which the Gulf Stream is a major part, warms winters on both sides of the North Atlantic by a few °C. But Seager et al. (2002) found that westerly winds carrying mild ocean air are the main cause of Europe's advantage over eastern North America. The deep conveyor belt links it all, and the IPCC expects it to weaken this century.

Mission 2

Normal conditions in the tropical Pacific

  • Trade winds blow west along the equator, taking warm water from South America towards Asia; cold water rises off South America to replace it.
  • Warm water piles up in the west, as the Pacific Warm Pool. Air rises over it and sinks over the cooler east.
  • This loop of rising air in the west and sinking air in the east is the Walker Circulation.
Mission 2

El Niño and La Niña

  • El Niño: above-average sea surface temperatures in the central and eastern tropical Pacific. The easterly trade winds weaken or even reverse.
  • Rising air and rain shift east with the warm water, beyond 180°: less rain over Indonesia, more over the central Pacific.
  • The thermocline in the east-central Pacific deepens to typically 150–175 m, and upwelling weakens or stops.
  • La Niña: below-average temperatures there, stronger trade winds, more upwelling, a thermocline of typically 50–100 m, and more rain over Indonesia.
Mission 2

A coupled system

  • ENSO is coupled: ocean and atmosphere change together.
  • The atmospheric half is the Southern Oscillation, a see-saw of air pressure between Tahiti and Darwin.
  • A strongly negative Southern Oscillation Index (SOI) goes with El Niño, and a strongly positive one with La Niña.
Mission 2

How often, and how it is measured

  • NOAA's Oceanic Niño Index (ONI) is the rolling 3-month average temperature anomaly in the east-central tropical Pacific. Values of +0.5 or higher indicate El Niño; −0.5 or lower, La Niña.
  • Episodes typically occur every 3–5 years, though the interval has varied from 2 to 7 years. They typically last nine to 12 months.
  • WMO: the 2023–24 El Niño peaked in December 2023 "as one of five strongest on record".

Mission 2

El Niño 2023–24: the Panama Canal

  • The Gatun and Alhajuela lakes store water for the canal's operation and for Panama's population. October 2023 was the driest October on record in the Canal watershed, and 2023 its second-driest year.
  • The Panama Canal Authority cut daily transits to 22, against 36 in what its table calls the "regular scenario", citing "the drought induced by the El Niño phenomenon". It called this "the first time the Canal has ever had to restrict transits".
Mission 2

El Niño 2023–24: southern Africa

  • In large parts of central and southern Africa, February 2024 was the driest on record, with a severe heatwave.
  • Seven countries declared national disasters (SADC's count; six of them for drought alone), including Malawi, Zambia and Zimbabwe. 71 million people were food insecure, 17.9% of the region's population.
  • SADC: El Niño was "not the sole driver" of food insecurity, but was "the primary driver of the significant increase" in 2024.
Mission 2

El Niño and Peru's fishery

  • In 2023, a coastal El Niño "disrupted the highly productive Humboldt Current upwelling system", and anchoveta catches were severely hit.
  • As the anchoveta recovered, Peru's catches rose from 3.5 million tonnes in 2023 to 5.7 million tonnes in 2024.
  • The chain: warmer water → weaker upwelling → fewer nutrients → fewer plankton → fewer fish.

Mission 2

La Niña 2020–23: the Horn of Africa

  • The tropical Pacific was in a La Niña state, with short interruptions, from September 2020: WMO called it the first "triple-dip" La Niña of the 21st century.
  • Southern Ethiopia, southern Somalia and eastern Kenya had five consecutive failed rainy seasons from October 2020.
  • Kenya's drought early warning bulletin projected that 4.35 million people in its drought-affected counties would need humanitarian assistance by October 2022 (cited by WWA).
Mission 2

Weighing La Niña and climate change (AO3)

  • Below-average short rains correlate highly with La Niña.
  • World Weather Attribution found that climate change has made such droughts "about 100 times more likely", mainly through higher temperatures raising evaporation.
  • Not all ENSO effects are harmful: El Niño "contributes to more eastern Pacific hurricanes and fewer Atlantic hurricanes", and La Niña's stronger upwelling supports more marine life off the Americas.
  • Exam move: name the phase, place and date, and weigh ENSO against other drivers such as warming and vulnerability.
Mission check

?What did a patch of warm Pacific Ocean have to do with the Panama Canal's restrictions?

El Niño warmed the eastern Pacific and shifted the Walker Circulation. Rainfall over Panama dropped, Gatun Lake ran low, and the Canal cut transits from 36 to 22 a day. The same event brought drought to southern Africa and hit Peru's anchoveta.

Mission 3

What a tropical cyclone is

  • A rapidly rotating storm that begins over tropical oceans; called a hurricane or typhoon, depending on the region.
  • Structure: a calm eye, a ring of towering thunderstorms in the eyewall, and spiral rain bands.
  • The Saffir–Simpson scale rates hurricanes 1 to 5 by maximum sustained wind: category 4 is 113–136 kt and category 5 is 137 kt or more. Categories 3–5 are major hurricanes.
  • The scale "does not take into account" storm surge, rainfall flooding or tornadoes.
Mission 3

How they form

  • Warm ocean water (about 80 °F, or 27 °C) at the surface, warm to a depth of about 150 ft (about 45 m).
  • An atmosphere that cools quickly with height, with moist air in the middle troposphere.
  • Enough spin from the Coriolis force, so at least 300 miles (about 480 km) from the equator, and low vertical wind shear.
  • A pre-existing disturbance to start from: in the Atlantic, about 85% of intense hurricanes begin as African Easterly Waves. Many disturbances with favourable conditions still do not develop.
Quick check

Mission 3

Where and when

  • Tropical cyclones form over tropical waters, between 25°S and 25°N.
  • Main seasons, "though there are differences from basin to basin": July–October in the Northern Hemisphere and December–March in the Southern. Globally, September is the most active month.
  • The North-west Pacific has cyclones all year round. Cool water, as off the US west coast, quickly weakens storms.
  • WMO counts, over "the past 50 years", 1,945 disasters attributed to tropical cyclones, with 779,324 deaths and US$1.4 trillion in losses.
Mission 3

Storm surge

  • Storm surge: an abnormal rise of water generated by a storm, over and above the predicted astronomical tide. Storm surge plus tide gives the storm tide.
  • It is caused mainly by strong onshore winds; in shallow water near the coast the water can only move up and inland.
  • Surge is higher on a wide, gently sloping continental shelf, and on a concave coastline.
Mission 3

Case study: Hurricane Ian, 2022

  • Ian intensified rapidly over waters of 30 °C and higher, with low wind shear.
  • 27 September: landfall near La Coloma, Pinar del Río, Cuba, as a 110-kt category 3 hurricane.
  • 28 September: peak of 140 kt (category 5), then landfall on Cayo Costa, Florida, at 130 kt (category 4).
  • The NHC links the devastation to the exact track, strong winds and "large storm size".
Mission 3

Ian: impacts on coastal places

  • Peak surge inundation was 10–15 ft (3–4.6 m) above ground at Fort Myers Beach. Portions of the Sanibel Causeway were washed away.
  • Lee County: at least 52,514 structures impacted, 5,369 of them destroyed.
  • On Florida's east coast, aerial imagery showed "extensive beach erosion and damage to piers" from large breaking waves.
Mission 3

Ian: deaths and damage

  • In the US, at least 156 deaths, 66 of them directly caused by the storm. Storm surge killed 41, freshwater flooding 12 and wind 4.
  • US damage: an estimated $112.9 billion (90% confidence interval $86.8–135.9 billion).
  • Cuba: 5 deaths reported (3 directly caused by Ian); more than 100,000 homes damaged or destroyed; the whole island reportedly lost power for a few days.

Warnings and forecasts

  • The storm surge watch and warning for Fort Myers Beach were issued 48 and 30 hours before tropical-storm-force winds arrived.
  • The peak surge forecast there rose from 4–7 ft to 8–12 ft on 27 September, and to a final 12–18 ft. NHC's later analysis found 10–15 ft above ground.
  • Intensity forecast errors were above average, largely because of the rapid intensification.
Mission 3

Recovery and evaluation (AO3)

  • By September 2024, federal support topped $10.2 billion, including $1.15 billion for 387,139 households and $4.67 billion of flood-insurance claims paid to about 47,000 policyholders.
  • The data are unequal too: the NHC gives no monetary damage estimate for Cuba, so losses in the two countries cannot be compared in dollars.
  • Exam move: compare impacts and responses at different scales and levels of development, and say where data are missing.

Mission 4

The ocean as a carbon store

  • The ocean holds about 50 times as much carbon as the atmosphere.
  • Carbon moves quickly between surface water and the air, but may be stored for centuries in the deep ocean.
  • A store holds carbon; a sink takes up more than it releases; a source releases more than it takes up.
Mission 4

The solubility pump

  • CO₂ is more than twice as soluble in cold polar water as in warm equatorial water.
  • Water cooled on its way poleward absorbs CO₂, becomes denser and sinks, taking the carbon to depth.
Mission 4

The biological pump

  • Phytoplankton turn CO₂ into organic matter. Perhaps 30% of it sinks into deeper water, and only about 0.1% reaches the seafloor sediments.
  • A 1995 model study (Sarmiento et al.), reported by Feely et al., estimated that the natural solubility pump causes about 20% of the vertical gradient in dissolved inorganic carbon, and the biological pump about 80%.

Mission 4

Sink and source

  • 2015–2024: the ocean took up 3.2 ± 0.4 billion tonnes of carbon (GtC) a year, 29% of total CO₂ emissions; the land took up 21% and the atmosphere kept 50%.
  • Natural CO₂ is released mostly in the tropics, especially where deep water upwells along the equator.
  • During large El Niño events, less CO₂ escapes from the tropical Pacific, so the sink is stronger; it is weaker during La Niña. Synthesis with Mission 2.
Mission 4

A changing role

  • The ocean sink has been "stagnant since 2016", partly because of climate variability and the ocean heatwave of 2023–24.
  • Warmer water dissolves less CO₂ (screen 4.2), so warming works against the solubility pump.
  • Models estimate that climate change cut the sink by 0.20 GtC a year in 2015–2024: 7.1% less than in a model run with rising CO₂ but no climate change.
  • IPCC: the proportion of emissions taken up by land and ocean decreases as cumulative emissions rise.
Mission 4

Ocean acidification

  • CO₂ dissolves in seawater to form carbonic acid, which releases hydrogen ions and bicarbonate. The ocean's average pH is now around 8.1.
  • Extra hydrogen ions bond with carbonate ions, leaving fewer for corals and shellfish to build calcium carbonate skeletons. If pH falls far enough, shells and skeletons can begin to dissolve.
  • IPCC: it is "virtually certain" that human CO₂ emissions are the main driver of current ocean acidification.

Mission 4

Coral reefs under two pressures

  • Bleaching is caused by heat stress. From early 2023 to mid-2025, bleaching-level heat stress hit 84% of the world's coral reef area; mass bleaching was documented in at least 83 countries and territories.
  • Acidification slows calcification and so reef growth. IPCC: acidification is projected to "amplify the adverse effects of warming".
  • Coral reefs are projected to decline by a further 70–90% at 1.5 °C of warming, and by more than 99% at 2 °C.
Mission 4

The Great Barrier Reef: recent data

  • Hard coral cover, northern GBR: 39.8% (2024) → 30.0% (2025), the largest annual decline recorded for the northern region since monitoring began. Southern GBR: 38.9% → 26.9%.
  • AIMS: "the mass coral bleaching event was the primary source of coral mortality".
  • 2026: northern cover recovered to 35.1%, helped by late-summer monsoon cloud and cooler water; southern cover was 26.4%.
  • Exam move: separate heat (bleaching) from chemistry (acidification) when you explain reef decline.
Mission check

?Why is the ocean's uptake of CO₂ bad news for coral reefs?

The ocean sink slows warming, but absorbed CO₂ forms carbonic acid. Surface pH has fallen by 0.1 units, leaving fewer carbonate ions for coral skeletons. Together with heat-driven bleaching, the IPCC projects a further 70–90% loss of reefs at 1.5 °C.

Mission 5

The coast as a system

  • Inputs: energy from waves, tides and wind; sediment from rivers, cliffs and the seabed.
  • Processes: erosion, transport, deposition and subaerial processes (weathering and mass movement on the cliff above the sea).
  • Outputs: landforms, and sediment carried along or offshore.
  • Coasts are divided into sediment cells: stretches within which sediment mostly circulates.
Mission 5

Waves

  • Waves are made by wind. Their energy grows with wind speed, wind duration and fetch (the distance of open water the wind blows over).
  • Destructive waves: high and steep, with a backwash stronger than the swash, so they remove material.
  • Constructive waves: low, with a strong swash, so they build beaches.
Mission 5

Tides

  • Tides: the rise and fall of sea level caused by the Moon and Sun. Britain has some of the largest tides in the world, with a range of up to 15 m.
  • The tidal range decides where waves attack: a small range concentrates wave attack at one level of the cliff; a large range spreads it.
Mission 5

Erosion and transport

  • Hydraulic action: the force of water, and air compressed into cracks, breaks rock apart.
  • Abrasion: waves throw sand and pebbles against the cliff, wearing it away.
  • Littoral (longshore) drift: waves arrive at an angle, so the swash moves sediment up the beach at that angle and the backwash drags it straight down; sediment zig-zags along the coast.
  • Currents deposit the sediment where they slow down. A beach that loses its sand is left more exposed to waves.

Mission 5

Lithology: weak till and hard chalk

  • Holderness cliffs are glacial till (boulder clay), mostly late Devensian (18,000–13,000 years old): the Skipsea Till and the overlying Withernsea Till.
  • At Aldbrough the cliffs are about 20 m high. At high tide, waves erode their lower and sometimes middle sections.
  • At Flamborough Head, to the north, the cliffs are chalk, capped by till. The chalk "is more resistant to erosion than the overlying till".
Mission 5

Subaerial processes and mass movement

  • Above the waves, the till cliffs fail by rotational slumping, with smaller topples and falls, and short-lived mudflows.
  • Increased rainfall could make the till more likely to fail.
  • Waves remove the slumped material from the cliff foot, so the cycle repeats.

Mission 5

Sediment supply (synthesis)

  • Holderness's erosion is also a sediment supply: estimates going back to 1786 put the loss from the cliffs at up to 2 million m³ a year.
  • Spurn Point, to the south, formed from "deposits moving along the coast" (Mission 6).
  • Exam move: one stretch of coast's loss is another's supply. Link erosion, transport and deposition.
Mission 5

Vegetation

  • Vegetation traps and binds sediment: marram on dunes (Mission 6), and saltmarsh, which damps wave action and acts as a natural defence.
  • Roots also bind cliff-top soil, but cannot stop slumping where the sea removes the cliff foot.
Mission check

?Why do Aldbrough's cliffs disappear so fast?

Lithology and process. Soft late-Devensian till is attacked by waves at high tide, slumps from above, and the debris is carried away along the coast. Retreat averaged 2.16 m a year between 1951 and 2004, while chalk at Flamborough Head resists erosion.

Mission 6

Cliffs and wave-cut platforms

  • Waves erode a wave-cut notch at the base of a cliff by hydraulic action and abrasion.
  • The overhang collapses, and the cliff retreats inland, leaving a gently sloping wave-cut platform, exposed at low tide.
  • As the platform widens, waves break further out and lose energy before reaching the cliff, so retreat slows: negative feedback.
Mission 6

Headlands: cave, arch, stack, stump

  • Waves exploit lines of weakness (joints and faults) in a headland: crack → cave → arch → stack → stump.
  • At Flamborough Head, BGS warns that more wave activity could attack the chalk at wave level, "creating undercutting and cavities".
  • Old Harry Rocks, Dorset: "towering sea stacks and cliffs topped by chalk downland".

Mission 6

Beaches and spits

  • Beaches form where deposition exceeds removal: sand on gentle slopes, shingle on steeper ones.
  • A spit forms where littoral drift carries sediment past a change in the direction of the coast; it grows out from the land, often with a recurved (hooked) end and saltmarsh behind it.
  • Spurn formed "as a long, narrow spit from deposits moving along the coast carried by currents". It is over three miles long, but in places only 50 m wide.
Mission 6

Spurn: a changing landform

  • Storms have breached Spurn many times. In December 2013 a tidal surge burst through its narrowest part and destroyed the road.
  • A 1 km "washover" section is sometimes covered at high tide, cutting the end off from the mainland.
  • BGS reports that sea-level rise "is predicted to completely cut off Spurn Head by 2050", according to modelled UKCP18 scenarios.
Mission 6

How sand dunes start

  • Onshore winds blow dry sand inland from a wide beach; it collects behind obstacles such as the strandline.
  • Embryonic dunes are the first, low dunes at the top of a beach, where pioneer plants such as sand couch and lyme-grass trap blowing sand. They are either replaced by marram-dominated dunes or washed away by storms.
  • Dunes depend on a continued supply of sand from the beach.
Mission 6

Dune succession

  • White (shifting) dunes, also called yellow dunes: sand-binding marram (Ammophila arenaria) usually dominates. The vegetation "will rapidly change and disappear if stability is imposed".
  • Grey (fixed) dunes: grassland replaces marram as the dune stabilises and the sand gains organic matter.
  • Dune slacks: low, seasonally flooded hollows with few nutrients.
  • Succession on sand is a psammosere: each stage changes conditions for the next.

Mission 6

Studland and Sefton: dunes that move

  • At Studland Dunes, Dorset, embryonic dunes "initiate the very clear successional sequence of dune communities".
  • In the 2014 winter storms, Studland lost up to 10 m of dunes in a single storm. The National Trust is now focusing on adaptation and softer defences.
  • The Sefton Coast, Merseyside, "displays both rapid erosion and active progradation": some parts retreat while others build seaward.
Mission check

?How did Spurn form across the mouth of the Humber?

Spurn is a spit. Sediment drifting along the coast is deposited where the coastline turns at the Humber mouth. Storms breach it, as in December 2013, so it keeps changing shape.

Mission 7

Eustatic change: the sea moves

  • Eustatic change is a worldwide change in sea level, as the volume of ocean water changes.
  • Global mean sea level rose by 0.20 m between 1901 and 2018. The rate rose from 1.3 mm a year (1901–71) to 1.9 (1971–2006) and 3.7 (2006–18).
  • Thermal expansion explained 50% of the rise in 1971–2018; glaciers 22%, ice sheets 20% and changes in water stored on land 8%.
Mission 7

Isostatic change: the land moves

  • Isostatic change is a vertical movement of the land. The weight of an ice sheet presses the crust down; when the ice melts, the land slowly rises.
  • On the High Coast (Sweden) and the Kvarken Archipelago (Finland), about 290 m of uplift has been recorded over the past 10,500 years.
  • The land is still rising, by around 0.9 m a century, and new islands keep emerging. At today's rate, 10,500 years would give only about 95 m, so uplift was far faster at first.

Mission 7

Landforms of a falling relative sea level

  • Raised beach: an old beach, with rounded pebbles and shells, now above the reach of the sea.
  • Relict cliff: a former sea cliff behind a raised beach, no longer attacked by waves and weathering into a gentler slope.

UNCLOS: the zones

  • The UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (1982) entered into force in 1994; it has 172 parties.

  • The seabed beyond national limits is the Area, managed by the International Seabed Authority (ISA). UNCLOS calls the Area and its resources "the common heritage of mankind".

  • Paper 1 is 35% of the grade at both SL and HL. SL answers two options in 1 hour 30 minutes; HL answers three in 2 hours 15 minutes: 45 minutes per option.
  • Each option has a structured question and one extended-answer question from a choice of two: 10 + 10 = 20 marks.
  • Questions may use topographic maps, which the guide flags as especially relevant to oceans and coastal margins.
Exam technique

Command terms for the extended answer

  • Evaluate: "Make an appraisal by weighing up the strengths and limitations".
  • To what extent: "Consider the merits or otherwise of an argument or concept", with conclusions "supported with empirical evidence and sound argument".
  • Discuss: "Offer a considered and balanced review that includes a range of arguments, factors or hypotheses".
Exam technique

What the top band rewards (9–10 marks)

  • "In-depth and question-specific (topic and command term)".
  • Examples, statistics and details are integrated and explained: name the place, the date and the scale.
  • Evaluation includes "cause and effect relations, other perspectives; strengths and weaknesses of evidence", as with Seager's model study or Medmerry's two cost figures.
  • A balanced conclusion aligned with the evidence; maps and diagrams annotated and explained.
Recap

The big ideas

  • Currents and the conveyor belt move heat and nutrients; ENSO shifts rainfall and fisheries across the globe (Panama, southern Africa, Peru, the Horn of Africa).
  • Hurricanes need warm water, spin, low shear and a starting disturbance; storm surge is often the deadliest hazard (Ian).
  • The ocean is a carbon sink, but its uptake acidifies seawater and adds to the heat stress on coral reefs.
  • Lithology, waves and littoral drift shape coasts (Holderness, Spurn); dunes build by succession; isostatic and eustatic change make coasts advance or retreat.
  • Management involves trade-offs between actors (Medmerry, Sizewell C, Mikoko Pamoja, the Great Barrier Reef).
  • The oceans are a global commons: resources, pollution, sovereignty and choke points are managed from local to global scales (FAO, MARPOL, UNCLOS, the Arctic, Hans Island).
