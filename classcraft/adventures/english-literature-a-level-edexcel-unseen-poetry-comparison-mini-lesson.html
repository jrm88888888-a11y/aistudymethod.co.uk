Unseen Poetry Comparison

One of the hardest exam skills is reading a poem you have never seen before under time pressure and comparing it with another. This lesson teaches a reliable method for approaching any unseen poem and building a fast, argued comparison.

  • No revision of content will save you — only a method you can trust applies here.
  • AO2 leads: you are judged on how you analyse poetic method and turn it into meaning.
  • Comparison is argued, not listed: the marks are in how the two poems illuminate each other.

You have the poems printed in front of you, so quote exactly and never approximate. Press Start.

Method · the first five minutes

How to read an unseen poem

Under time pressure, follow a fixed order so you never freeze:

  1. Read the title, form and shape first — before line 1, the title and the poem’s shape on the page already tell you something.
  2. Read the whole poem twice — once for sense, once for method.
  3. Find the volta or shift — where does the feeling or argument turn?
  4. Ask the big question: what is this poem doing — mourning, protesting, remembering, seducing?
  5. Only now annotate method that serves that answer.

Start from the whole, not the parts. The commonest failure is diving into device-spotting before you know what the poem is about.

Pitfall · feature-spotting

Why naming devices earns nothing

The single biggest trap in unseen analysis is feature-spotting: listing techniques without saying what they do.

  • Weak: “The poet uses enjambment, alliteration and a metaphor.”
  • Strong: “The enjambment hurries the reader past the full stop the speaker cannot bring herself to place, so the grief outruns her control.”

Every device must be converted: feature → effect → meaning. If you cannot say what a device does here, do not mention it.

AO2 is not a checklist. Three devices fully analysed beat ten devices merely named.

Toolkit · the vocabulary you carry in

Terms ready for any poem

Bring a small, sharp toolkit you can apply to any poem:

  • Voice — persona, tone, register, mode of address.
  • Form — stanza shape, line length, free verse or fixed form, the volta.
  • Movement — enjambment, end-stopping, caesura, pace.
  • Sound — rhythm, rhyme and half-rhyme, assonance, sibilance, plosives; sound echoing sense.
  • Image — metaphor, simile, symbol, personification, the concrete detail.

Sound and sense: harsh, clustered consonants can enact violence; long open vowels can slow a line into grief. Name it only when the poem is truly doing it.

Method · comparison

Making two unseen poems argue

When comparing, do not analyse poem 1 fully and then poem 2. Instead:

  1. Find the shared subject or feeling and open with a proposition about it.
  2. Interleave — each paragraph takes one idea (voice, form, ending) across both poems.
  3. Lead with difference — “whereas” and “by contrast” do more work than “similarly”.
  4. Compare the endings — how each poem chooses to close is often the richest point of contrast.

Balance: spend roughly equal time on both poems; a brilliant reading of one and a thin paragraph on the other loses comparison marks.

AO3 · light-touch context

Context from the poem itself

With an unseen poem you usually have little external context, so infer it from the text:

  • Internal evidence — diction, references and setting suggest a period, place or situation.
  • Situation, not biography — you cannot know the poet’s life; you can read the speaker’s circumstances.
  • Do not invent — a confident but unfounded historical claim is worse than a careful reading of the words on the page.

Honesty rule: never assert facts about the poet you cannot know; ground everything in the printed text.

AO1 · under the clock

Writing fast and well

Time discipline decides unseen marks as much as insight:

  • Thesis in the first sentence — state what both poems do; refine it as you go.
  • Embed exact quotation — short, precise, woven into the sentence.
  • One clear idea per paragraph, always comparative.
  • Save time for the endings — do not run out of clock before the poems’ most important lines.

Golden rule: a controlled, argued comparison of three shared ideas beats an exhaustive one-poem commentary every time.
