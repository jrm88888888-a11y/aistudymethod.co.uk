Mission 1

Mission 1: How can you measure the length of a cell you can only see through a microscope?

Make a guess: How can you measure the length of a cell you can only see through a microscope?

Mission 1

Cell theory

  • Cell theory: all living organisms are made of one or more cells.
  • The cell is the smallest unit that carries out all the processes of life.
  • New cells arise only from existing cells, by division.
  • Cell theory is a unifying concept: it links every area of biology.
  • Viruses are not cells and are not classed as living, so they are not exceptions.
Mission 1

Units for tiny things

  • 1 mm = 1000 µm (micrometres).
  • 1 µm = 1000 nm (nanometres).
  • mm → µm: × 1000. µm → nm: × 1000.
  • Whole cells: µm. Organelle detail, viruses, molecules: nm.
  • A red blood cell is about 7.5 µm across; a ribosome about 25 nm.
  • Say: "1 mm = 1000 µm" — not: "1 mm = 100 µm".
Mission 1

The magnification formula

  • magnification = image size ÷ actual size
  • Rearrange: actual size = image size ÷ magnification.
  • Rearrange: image size = actual size × magnification.
  • Put both sizes in the same unit first.
  • Magnification has no unit: write ×2000.
  • Your specification writes it: magnification = size of image / size of real object.
  • Check: the actual size must be smaller than the image. If yours is bigger, you multiplied.
Mission 1

Worked example: size and magnification

Actual size of a chloroplast in a micrograph at ×4000. The image is 24 mm long.

  • Convert: 24 mm = 24 000 µm.
  • Actual size = 24 000 ÷ 4000 = 6 µm.

Magnification of a mitochondrion drawn 45 mm long. Its actual length is 1.5 µm.

  • Convert: 45 mm = 45 000 µm.
  • Magnification = 45 000 ÷ 1.5 = ×30 000.
Quick check
?In a photomicrograph taken at a magnification of ×2000, a red blood cell measures 15 mm across. What is the actual width of the cell?
Mission 1

Scale bars

  • A scale bar is a line on an image labelled with the actual length it represents.
  • Magnification = measured bar length ÷ bar label (same units).
  • Example: a bar 20 mm long labelled 5 µm. 20 000 µm ÷ 5 µm = ×4000.
  • Then use the formula for anything else in that image.
  • To draw a bar: drawn length = label × magnification.
  • Say: "measure the bar with a ruler" — not: "the label is the bar's length".
Mission 1

Practical: calibrating an eyepiece graticule

  • Eyepiece graticule: a scale in the eyepiece. Its divisions have no fixed size.
  • Stage micrometer: a slide with a real scale, often 1 mm in 100 divisions of 10 µm.
  • Line up the two scales. Example at the ×10 objective: 25 graticule divisions = 250 µm. So 1 division = 10 µm.
  • Change the objective, recalibrate. At ×40: 10 divisions = 25 µm. So 1 division = 2.5 µm.
  • Measure: a cell spans 18 divisions at ×40. 18 × 2.5 µm = 45 µm.
  • AT d: light microscope at high and low power, with a graticule. RP2: actual size = size of image ÷ magnification.
Quick check
?With the ×40 objective lens in place, 40 divisions of an eyepiece graticule line up exactly with 100 µm on a stage micrometer. Using the same objective, a cell spans 44 graticule divisions. What is the actual length of the cell in millimetres?
Mission 1

Making and viewing a slide

  • Focus at low power first, with the coarse then the fine adjustment.
  • Switch to high power. Refocus with the fine adjustment only.
Mission check

?A micrograph shows a pollen grain 60 mm across. A scale bar on the micrograph is 20 mm long and is labelled 10 µm. What is the actual diameter of the pollen grain?

You never measure the cell directly. You measure the image, then use a calibrated scale (graticule or scale bar) to convert it.

Mission 2

Mission 2: If you can enlarge an image as much as you like, why do some things inside a cell stay blurred?

Make a guess: If you can enlarge an image as much as you like, why do some things inside a cell stay blurred?

Mission 2

Magnification is not resolution

  • Magnification: how many times larger the image is than the object.
  • Resolution: the smallest distance between two points that can still be seen as separate.
  • Resolution is limited by the wavelength used. Visible light (about 400–700 nm) gives a limit of about 200 nm.
  • Electrons have a far shorter wavelength, so electron microscopes resolve much finer detail.
  • Enlarging past the resolution limit gives a bigger blur, not more detail.
  • Say: "higher resolution shows more detail" — not: "higher magnification shows more detail".
Quick check
?Ribosomes are about 25 nm across. They cannot be seen with a light microscope but are clearly visible in electron micrographs. What is the reason?
Mission 2

Three microscopes, three ways to make an image

  • Light (optical) microscope: light passes through a thin specimen; glass lenses focus it.
  • Transmission electron microscope (TEM): a beam of electrons passes through a very thin section. Electromagnets focus it.
  • In a TEM, parts stained with heavy metals scatter electrons and look dark. The image is a flat slice of internal structure.
  • Scanning electron microscope (SEM): an electron beam is scanned across the surface of a specimen coated in metal.
  • In an SEM, electrons given off or scattered back from the surface build a 3D image of the surface only.
Mission 2

  • Electron microscopes work in a vacuum, so specimens must be dead.
  • Their preparation (fixing, staining, sectioning, coating) is complex and can create artefacts.
  • Colours on electron micrographs are false colours added by computer.
  • Electron microscopes are large and expensive; light microscopes are cheap and portable.
Quick check
?A student wants to watch living white blood cells engulfing bacteria about 2 µm long. Which choice of microscope, with its reason, is correct?
Mission 2

Reading micrographs

  • Light micrograph (photomicrograph): coloured by stains; whole cells and tissues. Only structures larger than about 0.2 µm, such as nuclei and chloroplasts.
  • TEM micrograph: a flat slice. Shows membranes inside organelles, e.g. the folds inside a mitochondrion.
  • SEM micrograph: a 3D-looking surface with shadows; no internal detail.
  • First find the scale bar or the magnification.
Mission 2

Organelle or artefact?

  • Artefact: something in an image produced by the preparation, not present in the living cell.
  • Golgi apparatus: described by Camillo Golgi in 1898 using a silver stain. For decades many thought it was an artefact. Electron microscopy in the 1950s showed it is real.
  • Mesosomes: folds of membrane seen in electron micrographs of bacteria from the 1950s. Later work showed they form during chemical fixation; they are absent from rapidly frozen cells. They are artefacts.
  • Scientists accept a structure as real when different preparation methods all show it.
Mission check

?A virus particle is about 100 nm across. A student photographs a stained sample through a light microscope at ×1500, then enlarges the photo 20 times: a total magnification of ×30 000. At this magnification a 100 nm particle would be 3 mm across. Which conclusion is correct?

Enlarging an image never adds detail. Detail is set by resolution, and resolution is set by the wavelength used.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Your pancreas makes enzymes that digest protein. How does it ship them out without digesting itself?

Make a guess: Your pancreas makes enzymes that digest protein. How does it ship them out without digesting itself?

Mission 3

The secretory pathway

  • Ribosomes on the rough ER make the protein. It passes into the space inside the RER.
  • The protein is folded, then carried in transport vesicles that bud off the RER.
  • The vesicles fuse with the Golgi apparatus, which modifies and packages the protein.
  • Secretory vesicles bud off the Golgi and move to the cell surface.
  • They fuse with the plasma membrane, releasing the protein outside: exocytosis.
Mission 3

Read the data: following a labelled protein

  • Pulse-chase: cells get radioactive amino acids for a few minutes (pulse), then unlabelled ones (chase).
  • Then: where is the labelled protein at each time? George Palade's group used this method on pancreas cells in the 1960s.

  • The label peaks in the RER first, then the Golgi, then the vesicles.
  • As it falls in one place, it rises in the next: the same labelled protein moves from one compartment to the next.
Quick check
?Pancreatic cells were supplied with radioactive amino acids for 3 minutes and then with non-radioactive amino acids. Samples of cells were taken at intervals and the location of radioactive protein was found. After 5 minutes it was mainly in the rough endoplasmic reticulum, after 20 minutes mainly in the Golgi apparatus, and after 60 minutes mainly in vesicles close to the plasma membrane. Which conclusion do these results support?
Mission 3

Finishing the protein

  • Many polypeptides do not work until they are modified.
  • Carbohydrate chains are added to many proteins, making glycoproteins. This starts in the RER and is finished in the Golgi.
  • The Golgi sorts proteins into vesicles by destination, e.g. secretion or the plasma membrane.
  • Some Golgi vesicles become lysosomes.
  • Say: "the Golgi modifies and packages proteins" — not: "the Golgi makes proteins".
Mission 3

Why build compartments?

  • Membranes keep enzymes and their substrates together in a small volume, so reactions run faster.
  • Membranes keep incompatible processes apart: hydrolytic enzymes stay inside lysosomes.
  • Each compartment can keep its own conditions: the inside of a lysosome is acidic (about pH 5).
  • Proteins made for export stay inside membranes until they leave the cell.
Mission check

?Plasma cells make antibodies, which are proteins they secrete into the blood. At which stage, if any, is a newly made antibody molecule free in the cell's cytoplasm?

Like the pancreas's enzymes, an exported protein is never loose in the cytoplasm. Membranes surround it from the RER until exocytosis.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Your nerve cells and muscle cells carry the same genes. So why are they so different?

Make a guess: Your nerve cells and muscle cells carry the same genes. So why are they so different?

Mission 4

One cell becomes hundreds of cell types

  • You began as one cell: a zygote.
  • Mitosis made many genetically identical, unspecialised cells.
  • Differentiation: each cell becomes specialised, with a structure suited to one function.
  • A nerve cell and a muscle cell keep the same genes.
  • They differ in which genes are switched on.
  • Visible differentiation comes from expressing genes for tissue-specific proteins.
  • Say: "different genes are expressed" — not: "different genes are kept".
Quick check
?In a human, a neuron and a liver cell are both descended by mitosis from the same zygote. What is the difference between them once they have differentiated?
Mission 4

Cells → tissues → organs → systems

  • Tissue: a group of specialised cells working together to perform the same function.
  • Organ: several tissues grouped together to perform an overall function.
  • Organ system: organs working together, e.g. the digestive system.
  • Stomach (organ): epithelium + muscle + connective tissue.
  • Leaf (organ): epidermis + mesophyll + xylem + phloem.
  • A tissue can hold more than one cell type, e.g. ciliated cells and goblet cells in airway epithelium.
Quick check
?The wall of the stomach contains epithelium, smooth muscle and connective tissue. Together they digest and churn food. Which statement is correct?
Mission check

?In a hypothetical species of plant, leaf cells make large amounts of a protein that binds chlorophyll, but the root cells of the same plant make none of it. Which statement best explains this difference?

Same genes in every cell; different genes switched on. That is what makes a leaf cell, a root cell, a nerve cell or a muscle cell.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Some of your cells have no nucleus and some have hundreds. How does a cell's build match its job?

Make a guess: Some of your cells have no nucleus and some have hundreds. How does a cell's build match its job?

Mission 5

Read a cell by its organelles

  • Many mitochondria → lots of ATP: for active transport or movement.
  • Lots of RER and Golgi → making and secreting proteins.
  • Microvilli or folded membranes → large surface area.
  • More carrier and channel proteins in the membrane → faster transport.
  • Many lysosomes → digesting engulfed material.
  • Many chloroplasts → photosynthesis.
  • Say: "mitochondria release energy in respiration, making ATP" — not: "mitochondria make energy".
Mission 5

Specialised animal cells

  • Erythrocyte: biconcave disc → large surface area for O2 diffusion. No nucleus → more room for haemoglobin. Flexible → squeezes through capillaries.
  • Neutrophil: lobed nucleus → squeezes between cells. Many lysosomes → digests pathogens it engulfs.
  • Squamous epithelial cell: flat and thin → short diffusion distance.
  • Ciliated epithelial cell: cilia beat to move mucus. Many mitochondria → ATP for beating.
  • Sperm: flagellum to swim. Mitochondria in midpiece. Acrosome enzymes digest a path into the egg.
  • Say: "the biconcave shape gives a large surface area" — not: "it holds more haemoglobin".
Mission 5

Specialised plant cells

  • Palisade cell: column-shaped, packed with chloroplasts, near the upper surface → absorbs most light.
  • Root hair cell: long hair → large surface area for water and mineral ion uptake. Many mitochondria → ATP for active transport of mineral ions.
  • Guard cells: a pair around each stoma. Take in water → swell → bend apart → stoma opens.
  • Guard cell wall is thicker on the pore side, so swelling bends the cells.
  • Water enters a root hair by osmosis, not active transport.
Quick check
?Root hair cells have no chloroplasts but contain many mitochondria. What is the best explanation for the many mitochondria?
Mission check

?Cells lining the first part of a kidney tubule take glucose back from the filtrate into the blood. Under the electron microscope they show a dense border of microvilli and very many mitochondria. What do these features suggest about how the cells work?

A cell's shape, size and parts are clues to its job.

Mission 6

Mission 6: A bacterium has no nucleus. So where does it keep its genes, and what else does it manage without?

Make a guess: A bacterium has no nucleus. So where does it keep its genes, and what else does it manage without?

Mission 6

What every cell has

  • Every cell has: a plasma membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes and DNA.
  • Two kinds of cell:
    • Prokaryotic (bacteria, archaea): no nucleus.
Mission 6

Inside a bacterium

  • No nucleus. One circular DNA molecule lies free in the cytoplasm, in the nucleoid region.
  • Prokaryotic DNA is short and not associated with proteins (no histones).
  • Ribosomes are smaller: 70S (eukaryotic cytoplasm: 80S).
  • No membrane-bound organelles: no mitochondria, no ER, no Golgi.
  • Cell wall of peptidoglycan, outside the plasma membrane.
  • AQA's name for peptidoglycan is murein: sugar chains cross-linked by short peptides.
  • Your spec words this as "murein, a glycoprotein". Murein is a peptidoglycan, not a glycoprotein.
  • Often present: plasmids (small extra DNA circles), a capsule, one or more flagella.
  • Say: "the DNA is in the nucleoid" — not: "the DNA is in a small nucleus".
Quick check
?Which description of the structure of a Bacillus cell is correct?

  • Ribosomes: 70S in prokaryotes; 80S in eukaryotic cytoplasm.
  • Shared by both: plasma membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes, DNA.
  • A cell wall does not tell you the cell type. Look for a nucleus.
Quick check
?A unicellular organism is found to have a cell wall, a plasma membrane and ribosomes, but no nucleus. Its DNA is a single circular molecule, not associated with histones, lying in the cytoplasm. A student concludes that the organism is a prokaryote. Which evaluation of this conclusion is correct?
Mission 6

Why a bacterium needs its wall

  • Bacterial cytoplasm is rich in solutes, so in dilute surroundings water enters by osmosis.
  • The wall is strong: it resists swelling and stops the cell bursting.
  • Water and small solutes pass through the wall; the plasma membrane, inside it, controls what enters the cell.
  • Strip the wall away (e.g. with the enzyme lysozyme) and a bacterium in water bursts.
Mission 6

Binary fission

  • Bacteria reproduce by binary fission: one cell → two.
  • (1) The circular DNA is replicated. Plasmids are replicated too.
  • (2) The cell grows; the two DNA copies move apart.
  • (3) The cytoplasm divides; new membrane and wall grow across the middle.
  • Each daughter cell: one copy of the circular DNA and a variable number of plasmids.
  • No nucleus, no spindle, no chromosomes condensing: binary fission is not mitosis.
  • Say: "binary fission" — not: "the bacterium divides by mitosis".
Quick check
?A bacterial cell contains one circular DNA molecule and six copies of a plasmid. It divides once by binary fission. Which statement about the two daughter cells is correct?
Mission check

?An electron micrograph shows a cell about 3 µm long. It has a cell wall, a paler fibrous region of DNA not enclosed by any membrane, many small dense granules, and no membrane-bound organelles. How should the cell be classified?

No nucleus, but not disorganised: the genes sit in the nucleoid, and the cell manages without membrane-bound organelles.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Magnification = image size ÷ actual size; same units first (1 mm = 1000 µm = 1 000 000 nm).
  • Calibrate the eyepiece graticule with a stage micrometer for every objective; a scale bar gives the magnification of a micrograph.

Mission 2

  • Magnification makes an image bigger; resolution decides how much detail it holds, and is limited by wavelength (light about 200 nm).
  • TEM: thin sections, internal detail, highest resolution; SEM: 3D surfaces; both need dead specimens in a vacuum.

Mission 3

  • Secreted proteins: made on RER → transport vesicles → Golgi (modified, packaged) → secretory vesicles → exocytosis.
  • Compartments concentrate enzymes and keep incompatible processes apart; exported or digestive enzymes stay behind membranes.

Mission 4

  • Differentiation: cells with the same genes become specialised by expressing different genes.
  • Specialised cells form tissues; tissues form organs; organs form systems.

Mission 5

  • A cell's shape, size and organelles match its function.
  • Many mitochondria → active transport or movement; microvilli → large surface area; chloroplasts → photosynthesis.

Mission 6

  • A prokaryote keeps one circular DNA molecule in the nucleoid, with no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles; it still has a membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes and (usually) a wall.
  • Binary fission: DNA and plasmids replicate, the cytoplasm divides; each daughter gets one circular DNA and a variable number of plasmids.
