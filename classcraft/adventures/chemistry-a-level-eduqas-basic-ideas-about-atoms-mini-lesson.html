Mission 1

Mission 1: How can atoms that decay at random be used to tell how old a bone is?

Make a guess: How can atoms that decay at random be used to tell how old a bone is?

Mission 1

Unstable nuclei

  • Some nuclei are unstable: the wrong balance of protons and neutrons, or too big.
  • They decay by emitting radiation. This is radioactive decay.
  • Decay is spontaneous: heat, pressure and chemical bonding have almost no effect on its rate.
  • Write nuclei as ᴬZ X: A = mass number (protons + neutrons), Z = atomic number (protons).
  • In a nuclear equation, A and Z both balance on each side.
Mission 1

Alpha, beta and gamma

  • α (alpha): a helium nucleus, ⁴2He. A − 4, Z − 2.
  • ²²⁶88Ra → ²²²86Rn + ⁴2He
  • β⁻ (beta): a fast electron, ⁰−1e, made when a neutron → proton + electron. A same, Z + 1.
  • ¹⁴6C → ¹⁴7N + ⁰−1e
  • γ (gamma): high-energy electromagnetic radiation. No change in A or Z; it often follows α or β decay as the nucleus loses extra energy.
Mission 1

Positron emission and electron capture

  • Both happen in nuclei with too many protons. Both turn a proton → neutron: A same, Z − 1.
  • Positron emission (β⁺): emits a positron, ⁰+1e (same mass as an electron, positive charge).
  • ¹⁸9F → ¹⁸8O + ⁰+1e
  • Electron capture: the nucleus captures one of its own inner electrons. The electron is on the left.
  • ⁷4Be + ⁰−1e → ⁷3Li
  • Same overall change in Z, but different equations.
Quick check
?Copper-64 is used in medical imaging. One way it decays is: ⁶⁴29Cu → ⁶⁴28Ni + X. What is X?
Mission 1

Penetrating power

  • α: stopped by a sheet of paper or a few cm of air. Most ionising.
  • β: passes through paper; stopped by a few mm of aluminium.
  • γ: passes through aluminium; reduced by several cm of lead or thick concrete. Least ionising.
  • The more strongly radiation ionises, the faster it loses energy, so the less it penetrates.
Mission 1

In electric and magnetic fields

  • Electric field (between charged plates):
  • α (2+) bends towards the negative plate.
  • β⁻ (1−) bends towards the positive plate, and much more than α: it is far lighter (about 1/7300 of the mass), even though its charge is only half as big.
  • γ is not deflected: it has no charge.
  • Magnetic field: α and β⁻ curve in opposite directions; β⁻ curves more tightly; γ goes straight.
  • A positron curves the same way as α (positive), but much more.
Quick check
?A beam containing α, β⁻ and γ radiation passes between two oppositely charged parallel plates. Which statement describes the β⁻ radiation?
Mission 1

Random decay, fixed half-life

  • You cannot predict when one nucleus will decay: decay is random.
  • But every nucleus of an isotope has the same chance of decaying each second.
  • With billions of nuclei, the same fraction decays in each time interval.
  • Half-life (t½): the time for half the radioactive nuclei in a sample to decay (so the activity also halves).
  • Each isotope has its own half-life, from fractions of a second to billions of years.
  • After n half-lives, the fraction left = (½)ⁿ.
Mission 1

Worked example: dating a bone

  • Living things take in carbon, including a little carbon-14 (t½ = 5730 years). The ¹⁴C level stays steady while alive.
  • After death, no new ¹⁴C is taken in; the ¹⁴C decays (β⁻).
  • An ancient bone has 25% of the ¹⁴C (per gram of carbon) of living bone.
  • 100% → 50% → 25%: 2 half-lives.
  • Age = 2 × 5730 = 11 460 years (about 11 500 years).
  • Assumes the ¹⁴C level in living things was the same then as now.
Calculate
?Technetium-99m (t½ = 6.0 hours) is injected into a patient for a scan. Its activity at injection is 400 MBq. What is its activity 24 hours later? Answer in MBq.

Mission 1

Radiation and living cells

  • Radiation ionises atoms and molecules in cells.
  • This can damage DNA: cells may die or mutate. Mutations can lead to cancer.
  • Large doses cause radiation sickness.
  • Outside the body: β and γ are most dangerous (they reach the organs); α is stopped by skin.
  • Inside the body (swallowed or breathed in): α is most dangerous: all its ionisation is in nearby cells.
  • Reduce exposure: less time, more distance, shielding.
Mission 1

Using radioisotopes

  • Medicine: ⁹⁹ᵐTc (γ, t½ 6.0 h) is a tracer: a γ camera outside the body images the organ; ¹⁸F (β⁺) in PET scans; ¹³¹I (β⁻) treats thyroid cancer; ⁶⁰Co (γ) in radiotherapy.
  • Health: ⁶⁰Co γ rays sterilise medical equipment and food.
  • Radio-dating: ¹⁴C for once-living material up to about 50 000 years; uranium-238 → lead-206 for rocks.
  • Industry: β sources gauge the thickness of paper or foil; γ tracers find leaks in buried pipes; ²⁴¹Am (α) in smoke detectors.
  • Analysis: compounds labelled with ¹⁴C or ³²P show where atoms go in a reaction or a living organism.
  • Match the isotope to the job: radiation type and half-life.
Quick check
?A hospital needs a radioisotope to inject into a patient so that a camera outside the body can image the kidneys. Which isotope is most suitable?
Mission check

?Phosphorus-32 (t½ = 14.3 days) is used to label DNA in biochemical analysis. A sample's count rate (corrected for background) falls from 1600 to 100 counts per minute. How long did this take?

Each nucleus decays at random, but billions of them halve on a fixed timetable. That timetable is the clock in a bone, a rock or a tracer.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why do fireworks come in different colours?

Make a guess: Why do fireworks come in different colours?

Mission 2

Wavelength, frequency, energy

  • Light travels as photons. f = c/λ and E = hf.
  • Shorter wavelength → higher frequency → more energy per photon.
  • Energy order: infrared < visible < ultraviolet. In visible light, red is lowest, violet highest.
  • Sodium's yellow light, λ = 589 nm = 5.89 × 10⁻⁷ m:
  • f = 3.00 × 10⁸ ÷ (5.89 × 10⁻⁷) = 5.093 × 10¹⁴ Hz. E = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 5.093 × 10¹⁴ = 3.38 × 10⁻¹⁹ J per photon.
Mission 2

How atoms give out light

  • Heat or electricity excites an electron to a higher energy level.
  • When it falls back, it emits a photon. Photon energy = the energy gap.
  • Only certain gaps exist, so only certain colours: an emission line spectrum, bright lines on a dark background.
  • Absorption spectrum: white light through the cool gas shows dark lines at the same frequencies, where electrons absorb photons and move up.
Quick check
?When sodium compounds are heated in a flame, the flame turns yellow. Which process in the sodium atoms produces this yellow light?
Mission 2

Flame colours

  • Each metal has its own energy gaps, so its compounds give their own flame colour.
  • Li crimson red · Na yellow-orange · K lilac.
  • Ca brick red · Sr red (crimson) · Ba pale green.
  • Fireworks: strontium salts for red, barium for green, sodium for yellow (and copper for blue-green).
Mission 2

What hydrogen's spectrum proves

  • Hydrogen gives lines, not a continuous band, so its electron has fixed energy levels.
  • Levels get closer together as n rises, so each set of lines converges at high frequency.
  • Falls to n = 1 emit ultraviolet; to n = 2, mainly visible; to n = 3, infrared.
  • Falls to n = 1 form the Lyman series. Its convergence limit is a fall from n = ∞ to n = 1: its frequency gives hydrogen's ionisation energy (see Mission 4).
  • One electron, many lines: different transitions happen in different atoms of the sample.
Quick check
?Which electron transition in a hydrogen atom emits the photon of highest frequency?
Quick check
?A red laser emits light of wavelength 650 nm. What is the energy of one photon? (h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s; c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹)
Mission 2

How many electrons a level holds

  • Main levels (shells) are numbered n = 1, 2, 3, 4 outwards.
  • Level n holds at most 2n² electrons: 2, 8, 18, 32.
  • The third level holds 18, not 8. "2, 8, 8" is how the first 20 elements fill, not a capacity.
Quick check
?What is the maximum total number of electrons that the first three main energy levels of an atom (n = 1, 2 and 3) can hold?
Mission check

?Lithium compounds give a crimson-red flame. Sodium compounds give a yellow-orange flame. Which statement explains the difference?

That's the firework: each metal has its own energy gaps, so its falling electrons emit their own photon energies, and so their own colours.

Mission 3

Mission 3: If an electron isn't in an orbit, where is it?

Make a guess: If an electron isn't in an orbit, where is it?

Mission 3

Shells split into sub-shells

  • Each shell (main energy level, n) splits into sub-shells: s, p, d, f.
  • n = 1: 1s. n = 2: 2s, 2p. n = 3: 3s, 3p, 3d. n = 4: 4s, 4p, 4d, 4f.
  • s, p, d, f have 1, 3, 5, 7 orbitals, so hold 2, 6, 10, 14 electrons.
  • So the first four shells hold 2, 8, 18, 32.
  • Up to Z = 36 no f orbitals are filled; 4f only matters for the 32.
Mission 3

Orbitals: where an electron is

  • An orbital is a region around the nucleus where an electron is likely to be found. Not a path.
  • Each orbital holds up to two electrons, with opposite spins.
  • s orbital: a sphere centred on the nucleus.
  • p orbitals: three dumbbells along the x, y and z axes, at 90° to each other.
Quick check
?What is the maximum number of electrons that a 3d sub-shell can hold, and why?
Mission 3

Three filling rules

  • Aufbau ("building up"): fill the lowest energy first: 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p.
  • Pauli: at most two electrons per orbital, with opposite spins.
  • Hund: in orbitals of equal energy, electrons go in singly, spins parallel, before pairing.
  • Electrons-in-boxes: one box per orbital; arrows ↑↓ for spins. Oxygen 2p⁴: [↑↓][↑ ][↑ ].
Mission 3

Writing configurations

  • Iron, Z = 26: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d⁶ 4s², shortened to [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s².
  • 4s fills before 3d, but either written order is fine: [Ar] 4s² 3d⁶ is also correct.
  • Two exceptions up to krypton: Cr [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ and Cu [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹.
Quick check
?Which full electron configuration of a ground-state atom is correct?
Quick check
?Which electrons-in-boxes diagram correctly shows the 3p sub-shell of a ground-state sulfur atom (… 3s² 3p⁴)?
Mission 3

Configurations of ions

  • s- and p-block ions: add or remove electrons in the outer sub-shell.
  • Na⁺: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶. S²⁻: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ (same as Ar).
  • d-block ions lose their 4s electrons first.
  • Fe²⁺: [Ar] 3d⁶. Fe³⁺: [Ar] 3d⁵. Cu²⁺ (from [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹): [Ar] 3d⁹.
  • 4s fills first but empties first too.
Quick check
?An iron atom (Z = 26) has the condensed electron configuration [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s². How is an Fe²⁺ ion formed from this atom?
Mission 3

Configurations and the periodic table

  • Blocks are named after the sub-shell being filled: s-block (groups 1–2), p-block (13–18), d-block.
  • Across Periods 2 and 3: two s electrons are added, then six p electrons.
  • Down a group the outer configuration repeats: Mg 3s², Ca 4s². So their chemistry is similar (both form 2+ ions).
Mission check

?A ground-state phosphorus atom is [Ne] 3s² 3p³. Where are its three 3p electrons?

An electron isn't on a track. It is in an orbital: a region where it is likely to be. The configuration tells you which orbitals are occupied.

Mission 4

Mission 4: How much energy does it take to pull an electron out of an atom?

Make a guess: How much energy does it take to pull an electron out of an atom?

Mission 4

From the convergence limit to kJ mol⁻¹

  • Hydrogen's lines for falls to n = 1 crowd together to a convergence limit: a fall from n = ∞ to n = 1.
  • This is the convergence limit of the Lyman series.
  • Run it backwards: the same energy removes the electron completely. That is ionisation.
  • f = 3.29 × 10¹⁵ Hz. E = hf = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.29 × 10¹⁵ = 2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ J per atom.
  • × 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ = 1.31 × 10⁶ J mol⁻¹ = 1310 kJ mol⁻¹ (measured: 1312).
Quick check
?In the emission spectrum of hydrogen, the set of lines produced by electrons falling to n = 1 has its lowest-frequency line at a wavelength of 121.6 nm and converges to a limit at a wavelength of 91.18 nm. What is the first ionisation energy of hydrogen? (h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s; c = 2.998 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹; NA = 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹; E = hf; c = λf)
Mission 4

Defining ionisation energy

  • First ionisation energy: the energy needed to remove one mole of electrons from one mole of gaseous atoms, forming one mole of gaseous 1+ ions.
  • Your spec calls it the standard molar ionisation energy of gaseous atoms.
  • Na(g) → Na⁺(g) + e⁻. Always gaseous; always endothermic (positive).
  • Second: Na⁺(g) → Na²⁺(g) + e⁻. Each further one is a successive ionisation energy.
Quick check
?Which equation represents the second ionisation energy of calcium?
Mission 4

What sets the size of an IE

  • The electron is held by its attraction to the nucleus. Stronger attraction → higher IE.
  • Nuclear charge: more protons → stronger attraction.
  • Distance (atomic radius): electron further out → weaker attraction.
  • Shielding: inner electrons repel the outer electron, so it feels less of the nuclear charge.
Mission 4

The pattern across the periodic table

  • Across a period: IE generally rises. Nuclear charge increases; electrons enter the same shell, with similar shielding; the radius shrinks.
  • Period 3: Na 496 → Ar 1521 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Down a group: IE falls. Each new shell puts the outer electron further away and more shielded; this outweighs the extra protons.
  • Peaks at the noble gases, sharp drops at group 1: the evidence for shells.
  • Two small dips in each period (e.g. Be → B, N → O) come from sub-shells: Mission 5.
Quick check
?How does the first ionisation energy generally change across Period 3 from sodium to argon, and why?
Mission 4

Successive IEs reveal shells and the group

  • Successive IEs always rise: each electron is pulled from an ion that is more positive than the last.
  • A big jump marks the first electron taken from a shell nearer the nucleus, less shielded.
  • Magnesium: 738, 1451, then 7733 kJ mol⁻¹. Jump after 2 → 2 outer electrons → Group 2.
  • Count the electrons before the first big jump = outer electrons.
  • Three outer electrons → aluminium's group (13 in 1–18 numbering; Group 3 or III on some tables).
Quick check
?The first four successive ionisation energies of an element X are 578, 1817, 2745 and 11 577 kJ mol⁻¹. How many electrons does an atom of X have in its outer shell?
Mission check

?Element X is in Period 3.
Its first six ionisation energies are 1012, 1907, 2914, 4964, 6274 and 21 268 kJ mol⁻¹. Which statement identifies X and explains the large sixth value?

How much energy? It depends on how strongly the nucleus attracts that electron: nuclear charge, distance and shielding. Removing an electron from an inner shell takes far more.

Mission 5

Mission 5: If nuclear charge rises across period 3, why does aluminium lose an electron more easily than magnesium?

Make a guess: If nuclear charge rises across period 3, why does aluminium lose an electron more easily than magnesium?

Mission 5

Two dips in period 3

  • First ionisation energy: energy to remove 1 mol of electrons from 1 mol of gaseous atoms. X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻.
  • Across period 3 it rises overall: more protons, same shell, similar shielding.
  • But it falls twice: Mg → Al (738 → 578) and P → S (1012 → 1000 kJ mol⁻¹).
  • After argon it falls steeply to potassium: a new shell. That is evidence for shells.
Mission 5

Why dips mean sub-shells

  • If every electron in shell 3 had the same energy, the rise would be smooth.
  • The dips show that shell 3 is split into sub-shells of different energy: 3s, then 3p.
  • To explain a dip, compare the electron removed from each atom: which sub-shell, which orbital.
Mission 5

Mg → Al: a new sub-shell

  • Mg: [Ne] 3s². Al: [Ne] 3s² 3p¹.
  • Al's electron is removed from 3p, which is higher in energy than 3s.
  • The 3p electron is also slightly further from the nucleus and partly shielded by the 3s electrons.
  • So less energy is needed, even though Al has one more proton.
Quick check
?After the dip, the rise resumes: silicon's first ionisation energy is 787 kJ mol⁻¹, higher than aluminium's 578 kJ mol⁻¹. Why?
Mission 5

P → S: p-orbital repulsion

  • P: [Ne] 3s² 3p³. Three 3p electrons, one in each orbital: [↑ ] [↑ ] [↑ ].
  • S: [Ne] 3s² 3p⁴. One 3p orbital now holds a pair: [↑↓] [↑ ] [↑ ].
  • The paired electrons repel, so one of them is easier to remove.
  • Both electrons come from 3p: this dip is about orbitals, not a new sub-shell.
Quick check
?The first ionisation energies of phosphorus and sulfur are 1012 kJ mol⁻¹ and 1000 kJ mol⁻¹. Which explanation of the lower value for sulfur is correct?
Mission 5

The same pattern in period 2

  • Be → B (900 → 801 kJ mol⁻¹): B's electron comes from 2p, higher in energy than Be's 2s.
  • N → O (1402 → 1314 kJ mol⁻¹): O's electron comes from a paired 2p orbital: p-orbital repulsion.
  • Period 3 repeats this one shell further out: Mg → Al and P → S.
Quick check
?A student sketches first ionisation energy from Na to Ar as a smooth, steady rise. Which correction does the sketch need?
Mission check

?The first ionisation energies of beryllium and boron are 900 kJ mol⁻¹ and 801 kJ mol⁻¹. Which explanation of the lower value for boron is correct?

Same reason as aluminium. Its outer electron is in 3p, higher in energy than magnesium's 3s, so it is easier to remove despite the extra proton.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • α: A − 4, Z − 2 · β⁻: Z + 1 · β⁺ and electron capture: Z − 1 · γ: no change.
  • Penetration: α < β < γ. In fields: α and β⁻ bend opposite ways, β⁻ more; γ not at all.
  • Half-life is fixed for an isotope: fraction left = (½)ⁿ after n half-lives.

Mission 2

  • An electron falling between levels emits a photon; photon energy = the gap. Fixed gaps → fixed colours.
  • f = c/λ, E = hf; IR < visible < UV. Lyman series: falls to n = 1.
  • Level n holds 2n²: 2, 8, 18, 32.

Mission 3

  • Sub-shells s, p, d, f hold 2, 6, 10, 14; orbitals hold two electrons of opposite spin.
  • Fill 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p; singly before pairing. Cr and Cu are exceptions; 4s empties first in ions.

Mission 4

  • First IE: 1 mol of electrons from 1 mol of gaseous atoms: X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻.
  • IE rises across a period, falls down a group. Electrons before the big jump in successive IEs = outer electrons.

Mission 5

  • First IE rises across a period, with dips where a new sub-shell starts (Be→B, Mg→Al) and where p electrons first pair (N→O, P→S).
  • The dips are evidence that shells are split into sub-shells.
