Mission 1

Mission 1: Why can a few minutes of weak ultraviolet damage skin cells' DNA when hours of bright red light can't?

Make a guess: Why can a few minutes of weak ultraviolet damage skin cells' DNA when hours of bright red light can't?

Mission 1

Light comes in packets: photons

  • Electromagnetic radiation is emitted and absorbed in discrete packets of energy: photons.
  • A photon is a quantum of electromagnetic energy.
  • Energy of one photon: E = hf = hc/λ.
  • h = Planck constant = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s.
  • Higher frequency (shorter wavelength) → more energy per photon.
  • A brighter beam of the same color has more photons, not bigger ones.
  • A photon is massless and electrically neutral; it travels in a straight line unless it interacts with matter.
  • Speed c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s in a vacuum; in a medium, v = c/n (inversely proportional to the index of refraction).
Mission 1

The electronvolt

  • Photon energies in joules are tiny. Use the electronvolt.
  • 1 eV: the energy gained by an electron accelerated through a potential difference of 1 V.
  • 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. J → eV: divide by 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹.
  • Example, blue light, λ = 450 nm:
    • E = hc/λ = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸)/(450 × 10⁻⁹) = 4.42 × 10⁻¹⁹ J
    • = 4.42 × 10⁻¹⁹/1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 2.76 eV.
  • Shortcut from the AP table: hc = 1240 eV·nm, so E = 1240/450 = 2.76 eV.
Quick check
?Light with a wavelength of 500 nm in air passes from the air into water (n = 1.33). What is the energy of each photon while it travels through the water? Use h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s and c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s, and treat the speed of light in air as c.
Mission 1

Why quantum theory was needed

  • Some observations could not be explained by classical physics: atomic line spectra, blackbody radiation, the photoelectric effect.
  • Blackbody: a hot object's spectrum depends only on its temperature. Classical models predicted far too much short-wavelength radiation.
  • Planck (1900) matched the measured curves by assuming energy passes between the hot object and its radiation only in packets of hf.
  • Einstein (1905) went further: light itself comes in quanta (photons).
  • Quantum theory is needed at atomic scales; there, light and matter show both wave-like and particle-like behavior.
Mission 1

The photoelectric effect

Shine electromagnetic radiation on a clean metal surface. Electrons may be emitted: photoelectrons.

  • Below a threshold frequency f0: no emission, however intense the light.
  • Above f0: emission immediately, even in very dim light.
  • Maximum kinetic energy of the electrons rises with frequency; it does not depend on intensity.
  • Number emitted per second is proportional to intensity (above f0).
  • f0 depends on the metal (e.g. zinc needs ultraviolet).
Mission 1

Einstein's explanation

  • One photon gives all its energy to one electron: a one-to-one interaction.
  • Work function ϕ: the minimum energy needed to remove an electron from the metal's surface.
  • If hf < ϕ: no electron can escape. Threshold: hf0 = ϕ.
  • If hf is bigger than the work function, the extra energy becomes kinetic energy. Electrons from just below the surface lose some on the way out, so this is a maximum.
  • Kmax = hf − ϕ
Mission 1

Why waves can't explain it

Wave theory: energy arrives spread over the wavefront and builds up with intensity and time. It predicts:

  • Any frequency should work, given bright enough light. ✗ Observed: a sharp threshold f0.
  • Dim light should cause a delay while energy builds up. ✗ Observed: instant emission.
  • Brighter light should give faster electrons. ✗ Observed: the maximum kinetic energy is set by f alone.
  • Photon model explains all three: energy comes in lumps of hf, one lump per electron.
  • So the photoelectric effect is evidence for the particle (photon) nature of light.
Quick check
?For a certain metal, the threshold frequency corresponds to light of wavelength 540 nm. Which of the following will eject electrons from the metal?
Mission 1

Worked example

Light of wavelength 400 nm falls on potassium, work function 2.30 eV.

  • Photon energy: hf = hc/λ = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸)/(400 × 10⁻⁹) = 4.97 × 10⁻¹⁹ J = 3.11 eV.
  • Maximum kinetic energy = 3.11 − 2.30 = 0.81 eV = 1.29 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
  • Threshold frequency: f0 = (work function)/h = (2.30 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹)/(6.63 × 10⁻³⁴) = 5.55 × 10¹⁴ Hz.
Mission 1

Stopping potential

  • Emitting plate and collecting plate in a vacuum tube; monochromatic light on the emitter.
  • Make the collector negative relative to the emitter: electrons are slowed as they cross.
  • Increase this reverse potential difference until the current just falls to zero: the stopping potential ΔVs.
  • Then even the fastest electrons are turned back: eΔVs = Kmax.
  • In the worked example: Kmax = 0.81 eV, so ΔVs = 0.81 V.
  • With Vs and the photon energy, the work function follows: ϕ = hf − eΔVs.
Quick check
?In a photoelectric experiment, monochromatic light whose photons each have energy 4.1 eV illuminates a metal plate in a vacuum tube. The graph shows the current I as a function of the potential difference ΔV of the collecting plate relative to the illuminated plate. What is the work function of the metal?
Quick check
?Light whose photons each have energy 4.0 eV shines on three metals: X, with work function 2.0 eV; Y, with work function 3.0 eV; and Z, with work function 4.5 eV. Which statement about the maximum kinetic energy of the electrons emitted from each metal is correct?
Mission check

?Ultraviolet light above the threshold frequency shines on a metal plate and electrons are emitted. The ultraviolet is replaced by ultraviolet of a shorter wavelength, with the same intensity (the same power per square meter). What happens?

Energy per photon is set by the frequency; the number of photons by the power. The same holds in skin: each ultraviolet photon can damage a molecule on its own; red photons cannot, however many arrive.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Electrons fired at a thin carbon film make rings, just as light does. How can particles diffract?

Make a guess: Electrons fired at a thin carbon film make rings, just as light does. How can particles diffract?

Mission 2

Wave or particle?

  • Light: diffraction and interference → wave. Photoelectric effect → photons (particles).
  • Electrons: definite mass and charge, single impacts → particle. Electron diffraction → wave.
  • Wave–particle duality: light and matter both need both models. The experiment decides which shows.
Mission 2

The de Broglie wavelength

  • Every moving particle has a wavelength.
  • λ = h/p, where p is the momentum.
  • More momentum → shorter wavelength.
  • h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s, so λ is tiny unless p is tiny.
Mission 2

Worked example

  • An electron is accelerated from rest through 150 V. Find λ.
  • Kinetic energy: K = eΔV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 150 = 2.40 × 10⁻¹⁷ J.
  • Momentum: K = p²/2m, so p = √(2mK) = √(2 × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × 2.40 × 10⁻¹⁷) = 6.61 × 10⁻²⁴ kg·m/s.
  • λ = h/p = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ ÷ 6.61 × 10⁻²⁴ = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁰ m.
  • About the spacing of atoms in a crystal.
Quick check
?An electron (mass 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg) in an electron microscope moves at 2.5 × 10⁷ m/s. Relativistic effects are negligible. What is its de Broglie wavelength? Use h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s.
Mission 2

Electron diffraction

  • Electron gun: a hot filament releases electrons; a few kV accelerates them.
  • They pass through a thin film of polycrystalline graphite, then hit a fluorescent screen.
  • The screen shows bright concentric rings: a diffraction pattern.
  • Electron λ (about 10⁻¹¹ m) is a few times smaller than the spacing between carbon atoms (about 10⁻¹⁰ m): close enough for the atomic layers to diffract them.
  • Many tiny crystals at random angles → rings, not spots.
  • Ring angles fit λ = h/p. Only a wave model explains this.
Mission 2

More momentum, less diffraction

  • Raise the accelerating potential difference → electrons faster → more momentum.
  • More momentum → shorter λ → smaller diffraction angles → smaller rings.
  • Lower potential difference → longer λ → larger rings.
  • From eΔV = p²/2m: λ ∝ 1/√ΔV. Four times the potential difference halves λ.
Quick check
?An electron's speed is reduced to one-third of its original value. Its speed is always much less than the speed of light. How does its de Broglie wavelength change?
Quick check
?Predict first. A low-intensity beam of electrons passes through two narrow slits and strikes a detector screen. The diagram shows the screen after 20, 100 and 400 electrons have arrived. Which conclusion is supported by the diagram?
Mission 2

One electron at a time

  • Electrons sent through a double slit so slowly that only one is in the apparatus at a time.
  • Each arrives as a single dot: particle-like.
  • After thousands of dots, interference fringes appear.
  • So each electron is detected at a point, but wave interference sets where it is likely to land.
Mission 2

When does the wave nature show?

  • Diffraction is noticeable only when λ is comparable to the gap or object size.
  • A baseball at 30 m/s: λ ≈ 10⁻³⁴ m. No gap is that small, so no diffraction is seen.
Mission check

?Electrons in a beam each have a de Broglie wavelength of 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁰ m. In which situation is quantum theory necessary to describe how the electrons behave?

Electrons have a wavelength λ = h/p. Quantum theory is necessary when λ is comparable to the size of the system, as with the spacing of atoms in a crystal.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why does a neon sign glow in a few sharp shades instead of a full rainbow?

Make a guess: Why does a neon sign glow in a few sharp shades instead of a full rainbow?

Mission 3

Energy levels

  • An electron bound in an atom can have only certain energies: energy levels.
  • Lowest level: the ground state (n = 1). Higher levels: excited states.
  • Zero energy = electron just free of the atom (ionized).
  • So every bound level is negative: energy must be supplied to free the electron.
  • Hydrogen: −13.6 eV, −3.40 eV, −1.51 eV, −0.85 eV, … crowding toward 0.
  • 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Levels may be given in eV or J.
  • The energy belongs to the atom: the electron–nucleus system.
Mission 3

Emitting a photon

  • An electron drops from a higher level to a lower one. The atom emits one photon.
  • Photon energy = energy lost: hf = ΔE.
  • With c = fλ: λ = hc/ΔE.
  • Bigger drop → higher frequency → shorter wavelength.
  • Each transition gives one frequency: one spectral line.
Mission 3

Worked example

  • Hydrogen: n = 2 (−3.40 eV) → n = 1 (−13.6 eV).
  • ΔE = −3.40 − (−13.6) = 10.2 eV.
  • In joules: 10.2 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 1.63 × 10⁻¹⁸ J.
  • f = ΔE/h = 1.632 × 10⁻¹⁸ ÷ 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ = 2.46 × 10¹⁵ Hz.
  • λ = c/f = 1.22 × 10⁻⁷ m (122 nm, ultraviolet).
  • Always use the difference between levels, never one level's value.
Quick check
?The energy-level diagram shows the four lowest energy levels of the hydrogen atom. A hydrogen atom in the n = 4 state emits a single photon and ends in the n = 2 state. What is the energy of the emitted photon?
Mission 3

How many lines?

  • An excited electron can drop straight down or in steps.
  • Every pair of levels gives one possible line.
  • 3 levels → 3 lines: 3→2, 3→1, 2→1.
  • Different gaps → different wavelengths. The largest gap gives the shortest wavelength.
Quick check
?The energy-level diagram shows the four lowest energy levels of a hypothetical single-electron atom. In a gas of these atoms, collisions put atoms into all four levels, and none of the atoms is ionized. How many different wavelengths of light can the gas emit in transitions among these four levels?
Mission 3

Absorbing a photon; ionization

  • A photon is absorbed only if its energy exactly equals a gap from the electron's level. All or nothing.
  • Any other photon passes straight through.
  • Ionization energy: energy to free the electron from the ground state = 0 − E1. Hydrogen: 13.6 eV (2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ J).
  • Any photon with at least this energy can ionize; the extra becomes kinetic energy of the freed electron.
  • From an excited level, less is needed: the ground state needs the most.
  • AP calls this the electron's binding energy. Not the same as nuclear binding energy.
Mission 3

Three kinds of spectrum

  • Continuous: all wavelengths. Hot, dense source: a lamp filament, a star's surface.
  • Emission line: bright lines on a dark background. Hot gas at low pressure.
  • Absorption line: a continuous spectrum crossed by dark lines. White light through a cooler gas.
  • The dark lines sit at the same wavelengths as that gas's emission lines.
  • They look dark because the absorbed energy is re-emitted in all directions, so little continues forward.
Mission 3

Evidence and fingerprints

  • Sharp lines, not a smear: atoms change energy only by fixed amounts. Evidence for discrete levels.
  • Each element has its own set of levels, so its own set of lines.
  • Match the lines with laboratory spectra → identify the element.
  • Works in a lamp, a gas cloud or a star's outer layers.
  • Helium was discovered in 1868 from a yellow line in the Sun's spectrum that matched no known element.
Quick check
?The diagram shows the emission spectra of elements A, B, and C and the emission spectrum of the light from a gas lamp. Which conclusion about the gas in the lamp is supported by the spectra?
Mission check

?The energy levels of a hypothetical single-electron atom are −6.0 eV, −5.5 eV, −2.0 eV, and −1.0 eV, and the atom is ionized when its energy reaches 0 eV. An atom in its ground state is struck by a photon of energy 4.5 eV. What happens?

An atom can only take in or give out energy equal to a gap between its levels. Neon's gaps are fixed, so its photons, and its colors, are too.

Mission 4: Blow an atom up to the size of a stadium. How big is the nucleus at its heart?

Make a guess: Blow an atom up to the size of a stadium. How big is the nucleus at its heart?

Quick check
?Predict first. A student claims that most of the mass of an atom is outside its nucleus, because the electrons occupy nearly all of the atom's volume. Which response to the student's claim is correct?
Mission 4

The nuclear atom

  • Nucleus: protons and neutrons. Positive. Tiny.
  • Electrons around it, held by the electric attraction of the nucleus.
  • A proton or neutron has about 1840 times the mass of an electron.
  • So over 99.9% of an atom's mass is in the nucleus.
  • (The neutron was discovered in 1932.)
Mission 4

How small is the nucleus?

  • Atom: about 10⁻¹⁰ m across.
  • Nucleus: about 10⁻¹⁵ to 10⁻¹⁴ m across.
  • Atom ÷ nucleus: roughly 10⁴ to 10⁵ times.
  • Stadium-sized atom (about 200 m) → nucleus a few millimeters: about a pea on the center spot.
  • Almost all the volume is empty; almost all the mass is in the pea.
Mission check

?A museum model of a hydrogen atom shows the nucleus as a ball 1 cm across, with the atom built to the same scale. About how wide must the model atom be?

The nucleus is about 10⁴ to 10⁵ times smaller than the atom: a pea in a stadium, holding nearly all the mass.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Two atoms of the same element can have different masses. What is different inside them?

Make a guess: Two atoms of the same element can have different masses. What is different inside them?

Mission 5

Inside the atom

  • A tiny, positively charged nucleus, with electrons around it.
  • Nucleus: protons (charge +e) and neutrons (no charge). Together: nucleons.
  • An ion is an atom with a nonzero net charge: electrons have been added or removed.
Mission 5

Nuclear notation

  • ᴬZ X. Example: ⁵⁶26Fe.
  • Z = proton number (atomic number): 26 protons.
  • A = nucleon number (mass number): 56 nucleons.
  • Neutrons: N = A − Z = 56 − 26 = 30.
  • Z fixes the element. Every iron nucleus has 26 protons.
Quick check
?A nucleus of potassium-40 is written in nuclear notation as ⁴⁰19K. Which statement about this nucleus is correct?
Quick check
?Predict first. Carbon-12 (¹²6C) and carbon-14 (¹⁴6C) are isotopes of carbon. Which statement about these two nuclides is correct?
Mission 5

Isotopes

  • Isotopes: same proton number, different neutron number.
  • Same element. Same number of electrons, so the same chemistry.
  • Different mass: extra neutrons add mass.
  • Carbon: ¹²6C (6 neutrons), ¹³6C (7), ¹⁴6C (8).
  • Some isotopes are unstable: carbon-14 is radioactive; carbon-12 is not.
Mission 5

What holds the nucleus together?

  • Protons repel electrically. Two protons 1 × 10⁻¹⁵ m apart: about 230 N.
  • Gravity between nucleons is negligible.
  • The strong nuclear force acts between all nucleons: p–p, n–n, p–n.
  • It is attractive at nuclear separations and stronger than the electric repulsion there.
  • At nuclear scales the strong force dominates the interactions of nucleons.
Quick check
?Predict first. Two protons are held 1 × 10⁻¹⁵ m apart, a typical separation of nucleons in a nucleus. They are then moved apart until they are 1 × 10⁻¹³ m apart, 100 times farther. How does the strong force between them at the larger separation compare with the strong force at the smaller separation?
Mission 5

A very short range

  • The strong force is very short-range: negligible beyond a few × 10⁻¹⁵ m.
  • Nuclei of neighboring atoms, about 10⁻¹⁰ m apart, do not feel it.
  • So it holds each nucleus together but does not bind one nucleus to the next.
Mission check

?Which pair are isotopes of the same element?

Same element means the same number of protons. Atoms of one element differ in mass because their nuclei hold different numbers of neutrons.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Photon energy E = hf = hc/λ; 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Intensity sets the number of photons, frequency the energy of each.
  • One photon frees at most one electron; threshold hf0 = ϕ; Kmax = hf − ϕ, independent of intensity. Evidence for photons.
  • Stopping potential: eVs = maximum kinetic energy.
  • Planck explained blackbody radiation: energy exchanged in packets of hf. Einstein: light itself comes in quanta.

Mission 2

  • Particles have a de Broglie wavelength λ = h/p; electron diffraction is the evidence.
  • More momentum → shorter λ → less diffraction (smaller rings).

Mission 3

  • Levels are negative (0 = ionized). A transition emits or absorbs one photon: hf = ΔE, λ = hc/ΔE.
  • Line spectra are evidence of discrete levels, and each element's lines identify it, in a lamp or a star.

Mission 4

  • The nucleus (protons and neutrons) holds over 99.9% of the mass and is 10⁴–10⁵ times smaller than the atom.

Mission 5

  • Nucleus: protons + neutrons (nucleons). ᴬZ X: Z protons, A nucleons, A − Z neutrons.
  • Isotopes: same Z, different numbers of neutrons; same chemistry, different mass.
  • The strong nuclear force: attractive between all nucleons, very short-range.
