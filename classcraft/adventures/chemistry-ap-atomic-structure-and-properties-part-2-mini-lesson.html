Mission 1

Mission 1: If an electron isn't in an orbit, where is it?

Make a guess: If an electron isn't in an orbit, where is it?

Mission 1

Shells split into subshells

  • Each shell (main energy level, n) splits into subshells: s, p, d, f.
  • AP calls these subshells (or sublevels).
  • n = 1: 1s. n = 2: 2s, 2p. n = 3: 3s, 3p, 3d. n = 4: 4s, 4p, 4d, 4f.
  • s, p, d, f have 1, 3, 5, 7 orbitals, so hold 2, 6, 10, 14 electrons.
  • So the first four shells hold 2, 8, 18, 32.
  • Up to Z = 36 no f orbitals are filled; 4f only matters for the 32.
Mission 1

Orbitals: where an electron is

  • An orbital is a region around the nucleus where an electron is likely to be found. Not a path.
  • Each orbital holds up to two electrons, with opposite spins.
  • s orbital: a sphere centered on the nucleus.
  • p orbitals: three dumbbells along the x, y and z axes, at 90° to each other.
  • Background: you won't be asked to draw these shapes. The key idea is a region, not an orbit.
Mission 1

Three filling rules

  • Aufbau ("building up"): fill the lowest energy first: 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p.
  • Pauli: at most two electrons per orbital, with opposite spins.
  • Hund: in orbitals of equal energy, electrons go in singly, spins parallel, before pairing.
  • You'll use Aufbau; Pauli and Hund are background for AP.
Mission 1

Writing configurations

  • Iron, Z = 26: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d⁶ 4s², shortened to [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s².
  • 4s fills before 3d, but either written order is fine: [Ar] 4s² 3d⁶ is also correct.
  • Two exceptions up to krypton: Cr [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ and Cu [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹.
  • Background: AP won't ask you to write the Cr and Cu exceptions.
  • Core electrons are the inner ones; valence electrons the outer ones. Mg, 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s²: 10 core, 2 valence.

Mission 1

Configurations of ions

  • s- and p-block ions: add or remove electrons in the outer subshell.
  • Na⁺: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶. S²⁻: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ (same as Ar).
  • d-block ions lose their 4s electrons first.
  • Fe²⁺: [Ar] 3d⁶. Fe³⁺: [Ar] 3d⁵. Cu²⁺ (from [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹): [Ar] 3d⁹.
  • 4s fills first but empties first too.
Quick check
?A ground-state iron atom has the electron configuration [Ar]4s²3d⁶. Which statement describes how a ground-state Fe²⁺ ion differs from the atom and gives its electron configuration?
Mission 1

Configurations and the periodic table

  • Blocks are named after the subshell being filled: s-block (groups 1–2), p-block (13–18), d-block.
  • Across Periods 2 and 3: two s electrons are added, then six p electrons.
  • Down a group the outer configuration repeats: Mg 3s², Ca 4s². So their chemistry is similar (both form 2+ ions).
  • So the periodic table's layout reflects recurring configurations.
  • In a photoelectron spectrum (PES), each subshell gives one peak: position = energy to remove an electron; height ∝ number of electrons.
Quick check
?An element has the ground-state electron configuration 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s². The chemical properties of this element are most similar to those of which of the following elements?
Mission check

?Which statement best describes the electrons in a ground-state magnesium atom, 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s²?

An electron isn't on a track. It is in an orbital: a region where it is likely to be. The configuration tells you which orbitals are occupied.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How much energy does it take to pull an electron out of an atom?

Make a guess: How much energy does it take to pull an electron out of an atom?

Mission 2

From the convergence limit to kJ/mol

  • Background: this shows where an IE value comes from. You won't be asked to calculate it.
  • Hydrogen's lines for falls to n = 1 crowd together to a convergence limit: a fall from n = ∞ to n = 1.
  • Run it backward: the same energy removes the electron completely. That is ionization.
  • ν = 3.29 × 10¹⁵ s⁻¹. E = hν = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.29 × 10¹⁵ = 2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ J per atom.
  • × 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ = 1.31 × 10⁶ J/mol = 1310 kJ/mol (measured: 1312).
Mission 2

Ionization energy and Coulomb's law

  • Ionization energy: the energy needed to remove an electron from a gaseous atom or ion.
  • First ionization energy: Mg(g) → Mg⁺(g) + e⁻, in kJ/mol.
  • Coulomb's law: attraction ∝ q1q2/r².
  • Electron closer to the nucleus, or feeling a larger effective nuclear charge → harder to remove → higher IE.
Quick check
?Which statement describes the process whose energy change is the first ionization energy of magnesium?
Mission 2

What sets the size of an IE

  • The electron is held by its attraction to the nucleus. Stronger attraction → higher IE.
  • Nuclear charge: more protons → stronger attraction.
  • Distance (atomic radius): electron further out → weaker attraction.
  • Shielding: inner electrons repel the outer electron, so it feels less of the nuclear charge.
  • Nuclear charge minus shielding is the effective nuclear charge.
Mission 2

The pattern across the periodic table

  • Across a period: IE generally rises. Nuclear charge increases; electrons enter the same shell, with similar shielding; the radius shrinks.
  • Period 3: Na 496 → Ar 1521 kJ/mol.
  • Down a group: IE falls. Each new shell puts the outer electron further away and more shielded; this outweighs the extra protons.
  • Peaks at the noble gases, sharp drops at group 1: the evidence for shells.
  • Two small dips in each period (e.g. Be → B, N → O) come from subshell energies and electron pairing; photoelectron spectroscopy shows the subshells.
Quick check
?Astatine, At, lies directly below iodine in group 17. All of its isotopes are highly radioactive and only tiny amounts can be made, so few of its properties have been measured. A team plans to measure one atomic property of astatine. Based on periodic trends, which result should the team predict?
Mission 2

Successive IEs reveal shells and the group

  • Successive IEs always rise: each electron is pulled from an ion that is more positive than the last.
  • A big jump marks the first electron taken from a shell nearer the nucleus, less shielded.
  • Magnesium: 738, 1451, then 7733 kJ/mol. Jump after 2 → 2 valence electrons → group 2.
  • Count the electrons before the first big jump = outer electrons.
  • Three valence electrons → group 13 (groups 13–18: valence electrons + 10).
Quick check
?The first four ionization energies of an element in period 3 are 496, 4562, 6910 and 9543 kJ/mol. Which element is it?
Mission check

?Element X is in Period 3.
Its first six ionization energies are 1012, 1907, 2914, 4964, 6274 and 21 268 kJ/mol. Which statement identifies X and explains the large sixth value?

How much energy? It depends on how strongly the nucleus attracts that electron: nuclear charge, distance and shielding. Removing an electron from an inner shell takes far more.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why is helium in group 18 when its atoms have only two electrons?

Make a guess: Why is helium in group 18 when its atoms have only two electrons?

Mission 3

Periods, groups and blocks

  • Elements are arranged in order of atomic number; the layout reflects recurring electron configurations.
  • A period is a row. Its number is the outer occupied shell (energy level).
  • A group is a column, numbered 1 to 18. Elements in a group have similar chemical properties.
  • Blocks are named after the subshell being filled: s-block groups 1–2; p-block groups 13–18; d-block groups 3–12.
  • f-block: the two rows usually drawn below the table.
Mission 3

Metals, metalloids, nonmetals

  • Metals fill the left and center. Non-metals sit at the upper right, plus hydrogen.
  • Metalloids lie along the stepped line: B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te.
  • Metalloids have in-between properties: silicon is shiny but only a semiconductor.
  • Aluminum touches the line but is a metal: shiny, malleable, a good conductor.
Mission 3

Valence electrons and the group

  • Valence electrons: the electrons in the outer shell.
  • Groups 1–2: valence electrons = group number.
  • Groups 13–18: valence electrons = group number − 10.
  • Group 16 atoms have 6 valence electrons, not 16. Group 13 atoms have 3.
  • Helium is the one exception. A later screen explains it.
Mission 3

From position to configuration

  • Period gives the outer shell, n. Block gives the subshell being filled.
  • Bromine: period 4, p-block, group 17 → 7 valence electrons, 4s² 4p⁵.
  • Bromine: [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s² 4p⁵. The 3d¹⁰ is inner: not valence.
  • Manganese: period 4, d-block, group 7 → [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s².
Mission 3

From configuration to position

  • Highest n → period. Subshell being filled → block.
  • [Ne] 3s² 3p¹: period 3, p-block, 3 outer electrons → group 13 (aluminum).
  • [Ar] 3d⁸ 4s²: period 4, d-block, 8 + 2 → group 10 (nickel).
  • Count the whole outer shell, not just the last subshell: 3s² 3p¹ is three.
Quick check
?Germanium is in period 4, group 14. Which is the full electron configuration of a germanium atom?
Quick check
?An atom has the configuration [Ne] 3s² 3p⁵. Where is the element in the periodic table?
Mission 3

Outer configuration decides the chemistry

  • Chemical reactions involve the outer electrons.
  • Same group → same outer configuration → similar chemical properties.
  • Group 2: outer ns². The highest-energy electrons are in an s subshell, so Group 2 is in the s-block.
  • Each Group 2 metal loses its two s electrons: Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺, Sr²⁺, Ba²⁺.
  • So elements in the same column form analogous compounds: MgCl2, CaCl2, SrCl2; H2O, H2S, H2Se.
Mission 3

Ionic charge from position

  • Metals lose their valence electrons: group 1 → +1, group 2 → +2, group 13 → +3.
  • Non-metals gain electrons to fill the outer shell: group 15 → −3, group 16 → −2, group 17 → −1.
  • Then balance the charges: Al³⁺ and O²⁻ → Al2O3; Mg²⁺ and N³⁻ → Mg3N2.
Quick check
?An element has the ground-state electron configuration 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁴. What is the typical charge of the ions of this element in its ionic compounds?
Mission 3

So where does helium go?

  • Helium is 1s². By configuration alone, its last electron is in an s subshell.
  • But shell n = 1 holds at most 2 electrons (2n² = 2). So 1s² is a full outer shell.
  • A full outer shell → very hard to remove or add electrons → unreactive, like Ne, Ar, Kr.
  • It does not behave like group 2: those metals readily lose their ns² electrons.
  • So helium is placed with the chemistry it shares: group 18.
Mission check

?Beryllium, 1s² 2s², and helium, 1s², both have two electrons in their outer s subshell. Why is beryllium placed in group 2, but helium in group 18?

Position in the table follows the outer shell and the chemistry it gives, not a simple count of electrons.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why is a chlorine atom smaller than a sodium atom, even though it has six more electrons?

Make a guess: Why is a chlorine atom smaller than a sodium atom, even though it has six more electrons?

Mission 4

Three factors explain every trend

  • Nuclear charge: more protons, a stronger pull on the outer electrons.
  • Distance: outer electrons in a higher shell are further away, so pulled less.
  • Shielding: inner electrons repel outer ones, reducing the pull they feel.
  • These are Coulomb's law in action: attraction rises with charge and falls with distance. Effective nuclear charge ≈ protons − inner electrons.
  • Across a period: nuclear charge rises, same shell, similar shielding → stronger pull.
  • Down a group: a new shell adds distance and shielding → weaker pull, despite more protons.
Mission 4

Atomic radius

  • Across a period, atomic radius decreases. Down a group, it increases.
  • Na: 11 protons. Cl: 17 protons. Both have 10 inner electrons and outer electrons in shell 3.
  • Chlorine's six extra electrons join the same shell. Electrons in one shell shield each other poorly.
  • So the outer shell feels a much larger net pull and is drawn closer: Cl is smaller.
Mission 4

Ionic radius

  • Positive ions are smaller than their atoms: Na → Na⁺ loses the whole outer shell.
  • Negative ions are larger than their atoms: Cl → Cl⁻ has the same 17 protons but more electron repulsion.
  • Down a group, ions with the same charge get larger: Li⁺ < Na⁺ < K⁺; F⁻ < Cl⁻ < Br⁻.
  • Isoelectronic ions share a configuration; more protons → smaller ion.
  • N³⁻ > O²⁻ > F⁻ > Na⁺ > Mg²⁺ > Al³⁺: all 1s² 2s² 2p⁶, with 7 to 13 protons.
Quick check
?Which statement correctly compares the radius of a chloride ion, Cl⁻, with the radius of a chlorine atom, Cl, and explains the difference?
Mission 4

First ionization energy

  • First ionization energy: the energy needed to remove the most loosely held electron from a gaseous atom.
  • Na(g) → Na⁺(g) + e⁻.
  • Across a period it generally increases: more protons, same shell, similar shielding.
  • Down a group it decreases: the outer electron is further away and more shielded.
  • Small dips (Mg → Al, P → S) are explained in another lesson.
Quick check
?The first ionization energy of magnesium is lower than that of beryllium. Which explains this?
Mission 4

Electron affinity

  • Electron affinity: the energy change when a gaseous atom gains one electron: X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g).
  • Energy is usually released: the nucleus attracts the incoming electron.
  • Across a period: more protons, same shell → more energy released.
  • Down a group: further away, more shielded → less energy released.
  • Many exceptions. Cl releases more energy (349 kJ/mol) than F (328 kJ/mol): fluorine's small, crowded atom repels the newcomer.
Quick check
?Which statement correctly compares the energy released when a gaseous S atom gains an electron with the energy released when a gaseous Cl atom gains an electron, and explains the difference?
Mission 4

Electronegativity

  • Electronegativity: the ability of an atom to attract the pair of electrons in a covalent bond.
  • Pauling scale, no units. Fluorine is highest, 4.0.
  • Increases across a period and decreases down a group: the same three factors.
  • Period 3: Na 0.9 → Cl 3.0.
  • Not electron affinity: that is measured on isolated gaseous atoms.
Quick check
?Fluorine is more electronegative than chlorine. Which explains this?
Mission 4

All the trends on one map

  • Across a period → radius decreases; IE and electronegativity increase.
  • Down a group → radius increases; IE and electronegativity decrease.
  • The pattern repeats in each period: this is periodicity.
  • Metallic character increases down a group and toward the left.
  • Metals react by losing electrons: reactivity rises down a group. Non-metals react by gaining electrons: reactivity rises up a group.
  • Use trends to estimate: Ca is below Mg (738 kJ/mol) and right of K (419 kJ/mol), so its first IE should lie between them (it is 590 kJ/mol).
Mission check

?An argon atom has seven more electrons than a sodium atom, yet argon's first ionization energy is about three times sodium's. Which explains this?

Same rule as chlorine vs sodium. Across a period, more protons pull the same shell in tighter.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Subshells s, p, d, f hold 2, 6, 10, 14; orbitals hold two electrons of opposite spin.
  • Fill 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p; singly before pairing. Cr and Cu are exceptions; 4s empties first in ions.
  • Core vs valence electrons; recurring valence configurations give the periodic table its shape.

Mission 2

  • Ionization energy rises with effective nuclear charge and falls with distance (Coulomb's law).
  • IE rises across a period, falls down a group. Electrons before the big jump in successive IEs = outer electrons.

Mission 3

  • Period = outer shell; group = similar outer configuration and chemistry; block = subshell being filled.
  • Same column → analogous compounds and typical ionic charges.
  • Helium's 1s² is a full shell, so it sits with the noble gases.

Mission 4

  • Nuclear charge, distance and shielding explain radius, ionization energy, electron affinity and electronegativity.
  • Across a period: more protons, same shell → smaller atoms that hold electrons more tightly.
