Comparative Study: American Literature 1880-1940

Component 02 is the Comparative and Contextual Study. Studying American Literature 1880–1940, you face an unseen prose passage and a comparative essay on two texts. This lesson teaches the period’s movements, themes and criticism so it works for any pairing.

  • Two tasks: close analysis of an unseen passage, and a comparison of two studied texts.
  • A period as a topic: realism to modernism, the American Dream, the Jazz Age, the Harlem Renaissance.
  • AO weighting: unseen rewards AO2; the essay rewards AO3 context, AO4 connection and AO5 interpretation.

Named authors are illustrative only; critical terms are accurately attributed and no quotations are invented. Press Start.

Movements · realism to modernism

How the writing changes

The period travels a great distance in style:

  • Realism — the ordinary, verifiable social world rendered plainly (associated with Howells and Henry James).
  • Naturalism — realism pushed toward determinism: character governed by heredity and environment, indebted to Zola (Crane, Norris, Dreiser).
  • Regionalism / local colour — the particular dialect and landscape of a region.
  • Modernism — fragmentation, experiment, stream of consciousness and disillusion after the First World War.

Comparative angle: two texts a few decades apart may treat the same subject — money, race, the city — through utterly different narrative methods.

Themes · the Dream and its shadow

What the period is about

Recurrent themes bind the topic together:

  • The American Dream — self-making and success, and the novels that expose its betrayal and cost.
  • The Gilded Age and the Jazz Age — wealth, spectacle, excess and moral hollowness.
  • The frontier and the city — the closing of the West and the rise of the modern metropolis.
  • The Great Depression — the Dust Bowl, poverty and a literature of social protest.

Read the Dream critically: the strongest essays show how a text both stages the Dream’s glamour and diagnoses its emptiness.

Voices · race, identity and the Harlem Renaissance

Who gets to speak

The period is decisive for questions of identity and voice:

  • The Harlem Renaissance — a flowering of African-American art in the 1920s. Alain Locke’s anthology The New Negro (1925) became its manifesto.
  • Double consciousness — W.E.B. Du Bois (The Souls of Black Folk, 1903) named the “sense of always looking at one’s self through the eyes of others”.
  • The Lost Generation — the disillusioned post-war expatriate writers (the phrase associated with Gertrude Stein).
  • Immigrant and regional voices — the many Americas competing for representation.

Attribution honesty: “double consciousness” is Du Bois’s term; “the New Negro” is Locke’s — accurate, high-value AO3/AO5 references.

Task 1 · the unseen passage

Reading American prose closely

The unseen passage asks you to analyse an extract from the period:

  • Identify the mode — realist plainness, naturalist determinism, modernist fragmentation, regional dialect.
  • Read the narrative voice — point of view, reliability, the use of vernacular.
  • Analyse method — diction, syntax, imagery, rhythm — and convert each to effect.
  • Relate to the period — how the passage embodies its moment’s style and concerns.

Vernacular is a choice: a text’s use of dialect or slang encodes region, class and race — never treat it as mere decoration.

AO4 · comparing two texts

The comparative essay

Compare two studied texts with AO4 governing structure:

  1. Lead with a period proposition — e.g. both texts expose the American Dream as a promise made only to be broken.
  2. Interleave in theme-paragraphs (the Dream; the city; identity) across both texts.
  3. Prize the productive difference — where one text mourns the Dream and the other was never offered it.
  4. Let position matter — whose America each text speaks from.

Stem: “Where one text records the disillusion of those the Dream courted, the other records the anger of those it excluded from the start.”

AO3 · context and AO5 · criticism

The history behind the writing

Context and criticism are inseparable for this period:

  • Context — industrial capitalism and the Gilded Age, mass immigration, the First World War, Prohibition and the Jazz Age, the Wall Street Crash and the Depression, and segregation and the Great Migration.
  • Criticism — Du Bois and Locke on Black identity and art; Marxist readings of class and the Dream; feminist readings of the period’s women.

Weld it: because one text is written in Jazz-Age affluence and the other in Depression hardship, one anatomises excess and the other bare survival — AO3 driving AO4.
