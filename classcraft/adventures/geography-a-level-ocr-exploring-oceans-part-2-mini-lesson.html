Mission 1: Tankers spilled about 95% less oil in the 2010s than in the 1970s. Why has plastic proved so much harder to stop?

Make a guess: Tankers spilled about 95% less oil in the 2010s than in the 1970s. Why has plastic proved so much harder to stop?

Specification: what this mission covers

Mission 1

Few sources or many?

  • Point sources: one identifiable outlet, such as a tanker, a pipe or a nuclear plant. They can be inspected and regulated.
  • Diffuse sources: many scattered sources, such as litter, farm runoff or exhaust gases.
  • Tanker spills of more than 7 tonnes have fallen by over 90% since the 1970s.
  • Pollutants from diffuse sources are much harder to cut.
Mission 1

Fossil-fuel CO₂

  • Burning fossil fuels releases CO₂; the ocean absorbs part of it.
  • 2015–2024: the ocean took up 3.2 billion tonnes of carbon a year, 29% of total CO₂ emissions.
  • Dissolved CO₂ forms carbonic acid: surface pH has fallen by 0.1 units, leaving fewer carbonate ions for shells and corals.
  • Source: billions of chimneys and exhausts, a diffuse source on a global scale.
Mission 1

Plastics

  • Global plastic consumption passed 320 million tonnes a year; about 60% of plastic produced is less dense than seawater, so it floats.
  • One estimate of plastic entering the ocean: 5.32–19.3 million tonnes a year.
  • Land-based sources: litter, rivers, poorly managed waste. Sea-based sources: lost or discarded fishing gear, called ghostnets.
Mission 1

Heavy metals: mercury

  • Human activity emits about 2,220 tonnes of mercury a year.
  • Largest sources: artisanal and small-scale gold mining (37.7%) and burning coal (21%).
  • Mercury "settles into water or onto land where it can be washed into water"; microorganisms turn it into methylmercury, which "builds up in fish, shellfish and animals that eat fish".
  • Concentrations rise up the food chain: biomagnification.
Mission 1

Nuclear waste

  • Under the 1972 London Convention, low-level radioactive waste "could be dumped at sea under certain conditions"; in 1993 dumping radioactive waste at sea was banned.
  • Under the 1996 London Protocol, "all dumping is prohibited" except wastes on a "reverse list".
  • Discharges continue under limits: at Sellafield, Cumbria, an ion-exchange plant removes caesium and strontium from used water before it reaches the sea.
  • Nuclear-sector discharges by OSPAR (north-east Atlantic) states fell 12-fold for total beta (excluding tritium) since 1995–2001.
Quick check
?Mercury from gold mining far inland ends up in tuna caught in the open ocean. What is the most likely route?
Mission 1

How currents spread plastic

  • Floating plastic is carried by surface currents and winds. It may be washed ashore, broken into smaller pieces, or sink.
  • Some is carried offshore into the gyres: large rotating current systems. There are five major subtropical gyres.
  • Winds and Earth's rotation push surface water towards the gyre's centre (Ekman transport), so floating debris converges there instead of dispersing.
Mission 1

Case study: the Great Pacific Garbage Patch

  • In the eastern North Pacific Subtropical Gyre, between California and Hawaii: "a gyre within a gyre".
  • Area: 1.6 million km². Mass: at least 79 thousand tonnes of floating plastic (range 45–129 thousand).
  • About 1.8 trillion pieces (range 1.1–3.6 trillion).
  • It is not "a literal island of trash" visible from satellites; most pieces are small and spread out.
Mission 1

Why plastic accumulates there

  • Circulation: the gyre's currents trap floating debris in its centre.
  • Persistence: only durable items stay afloat. Most collected objects were hard plastic and bundled fishing nets and ropes; thin films were rare.
  • Sources: fishing nets made up at least 46% of the mass; at least half of the collected plastic came from marine sources.
  • Events: the 2011 Tohoku tsunami may have supplied 10–20% of debris input after 2011. Plastic in the patch is increasing exponentially.
Quick check
?At least 79,000 tonnes of plastic float across 1.6 million km². What is the average concentration, in kg per km²?
Mission 1

Impacts on marine ecosystems

  • Ingestion: debris "can easily be ingested by marine species causing choking, starvation, and other impairments".
  • Entanglement: ghostnets harm marine habitats and the animals caught in them.
  • Rafting: 37 coastal invertebrate taxa were found on floating debris in the eastern North Pacific gyre, on 70.5% of items: coastal species now live in the open ocean.
  • Microplastics: 8% of the patch's mass but 94% of its pieces: small enough for plankton feeders to swallow.
Mission 1

How sure are we?

  • The estimate comes from a model calibrated with surveys by vessels and aircraft; the range (45–129 thousand tonnes) is wide.
  • Most of the authors work for The Ocean Cleanup, which also designs clean-up systems: read the methods, not just the headline.
  • Models predicted millions of tonnes in the region; surveys found tens of thousands. Most plastic may sink, break into tiny pieces or wash ashore.
Quick check
?Which finding best shows that gyre plastic changes marine ecosystems, not just how the sea looks?
Mission check

?A country cut oil spills by inspecting a few hundred tankers. Why would inspecting ships alone not clear plastic from the North Pacific gyre?

Tanker oil came from a limited number of ships that could be regulated. Plastic comes from countless land and sea sources, lasts for years, and gyre currents concentrate it far from where it entered: at least 79,000 tonnes in the Great Pacific Garbage Patch, rising.

Mission 2: When an oil well blew out under the sea, why did hotels far from any oil lose bookings?

Mission 2

The blowout

  • 20 April 2010: an explosion on BP's Deepwater Horizon drilling rig in the Gulf of Mexico killed 11 workers.
  • The Macondo well lay under about 1,520 m (5,000 ft) of water.
  • Oil flowed from the sea bed until a capping stack stopped it on 15 July 2010: 87 days.
  • About 134 million gallons of oil spilled.
Quick check
?About 134 million gallons spilled over 87 days. What was the average release per day?
Mission 2

Physical impacts

  • Oil spread at the surface and through the deep water column, from a source about 1.5 km down.
  • At least about 2,100 km (1,300 miles) of Gulf shoreline were exposed to oil as slicks came ashore.
  • Oil and dispersants spread "in a layer of contaminants that moved toward the shoreline".
  • Marshes, beaches and the deep sea floor were all affected, so impacts were three-dimensional, not just a surface slick.
Mission 2

Ecosystem impacts

  • Fish and invertebrates: "Trillions" of larvae killed in surface, deep and estuary waters.
  • Sea turtles: 4,900–7,600 large juveniles and adults and 56,000–166,000 small juveniles killed.
  • Dolphins: a 35% increase in deaths of bottlenose dolphins in Barataria Bay, Louisiana.
  • Whales: up to a 7% decline in endangered sperm whales; 48% of Bryde's whales affected.
  • Ranges show these are estimates from modelling and surveys, not counts of bodies.
Mission 2

Fishing

  • At its largest, about 229,000 km² (88,522 square miles) of the Gulf was closed to fishing.
  • By late September 2010, about 82,900 km² (nearly 32,000 square miles) was still closed.
  • Gulf commercial fishers had landed catches worth $659 million in 2008.
  • Even after waters reopened, buyers stayed wary: "the real impact here is the reputational damage to Gulf seafood as a safe brand".
  • BP gave Louisiana $48 million and Florida $20 million for seafood testing and marketing.
Quick check
?Fishing waters reopened as the oil cleared. Why did fishing businesses still suffer?
Mission 2

Tourism

  • The Gulf coast generates about $19.7 billion of tourism activity a year.
  • In Pinellas County, Florida, which was not oiled, a fall in hotel reservations through June 2010 cost about $70 million.
  • In a poll for Louisiana, 29% of people planning to visit said they were cancelling.
  • The Commission describes "an ill-founded stigma affecting everything related to the Gulf".
Quick check
?Pinellas County had no oil on its beaches but lost about $70 million in hotel bookings. What best explains this?
Mission 2

Management: the response

  • At its peak the response involved more than 45,000 people and thousands of vessels.
  • "Hundreds of miles of boom" were laid along the coast to hold oil back.
  • Dispersant on the surface rose from 14,654 gallons (20–26 April) to 168,988 gallons in the third week; the EPA limited sea-bed use to 15,000 gallons a day.
  • Dispersants break oil into droplets: less surface slick, but more oil spread through the water, and oil and dispersant moved as a layer towards the shore.
  • The leak was stopped only when the well was capped after 87 days.
Mission 2

Management: paying for damage

  • By 23 November 2010 the Gulf Coast Claims Facility had paid more than $2 billion to about 127,000 claimants.
  • In 2015 BP agreed civil settlements worth $20.8 billion, the US Justice Department's largest with a single entity.
  • They included a $5.5 billion Clean Water Act penalty, $8.1 billion for natural resource damages, $4.9 billion to the five Gulf states and up to $1 billion to local governments.
  • The natural resource money pays for long-term restoration of habitats and wildlife.
Mission 2

Management: changing the rules

  • The offshore regulator, the Minerals Management Service (MMS), was divided in May 2010.
  • From 1 October 2011, BSEE enforces safety and environmental rules, separate from BOEM, which manages resource development.
  • Evaluation: response and compensation were huge in scale, but the well flowed for 87 days, and ecological injuries (turtles, dolphins, whales) were long-lasting.
Quick check
?Which statement best evaluates the management of the Deepwater Horizon spill?
Mission check

?A tanker spill fouls 20 km of one bay in a region famous for beach holidays and seafood. Using evidence from Deepwater Horizon, which extra action would most limit losses for unoiled resorts and fishers elsewhere in the region?

An oil spill's economic footprint can be far larger than its physical footprint. Management must deal with perception (testing, marketing, compensation) as well as the oil itself.

Specification: what this mission covers

Mission 3

The ocean as a carbon store

  • The ocean holds about 50 times as much carbon as the atmosphere.
  • Carbon moves quickly between surface water and air, but may be stored for centuries in the deep ocean.
  • Store: holds carbon. Sink: takes up more than it releases. Source: releases more than it takes up.
Mission 3

A sink under strain

  • 2015–2024: the ocean took up 3.2 ± 0.4 billion tonnes of carbon a year, 29% of total CO₂ emissions; land took 21% and the atmosphere kept 50%.
  • The ocean sink has been "stagnant since 2016".
  • Models estimate climate change cut the sink by 0.20 GtC a year (−7.1%) in 2015–2024: warmer water dissolves less CO₂.
Mission 3

Ocean acidification

  • Dissolved CO₂ forms carbonic acid, releasing hydrogen ions. The ocean's average pH is now around 8.1; surface pH has fallen by 0.1 units over the industrial era.
  • Extra hydrogen ions bond with carbonate ions, leaving fewer carbonate ions for corals and shellfish to build skeletons and shells.
  • IPCC: it is "virtually certain" that human-caused CO₂ emissions, such as from burning fossil fuels, are the main driver of current surface ocean acidification.
Quick check
?The pH scale is logarithmic: a fall of 1 unit means 10 times as many hydrogen ions. Surface pH has fallen by 0.1. By about what percentage has hydrogen-ion concentration risen?
Mission 3

Acidification, shellfish and fish stocks

  • In 2007 a shellfish hatchery on the Oregon coast suffered "a massive, mysterious die-off of oyster larvae".
  • The cause was seawater with low pH and low carbonate saturation, which made "shell production too energetically costly".
  • Northwest hatchery failures were "largely due to ocean acidification", a concern for the "people, communities and industries that depend on these resources".
  • Shell-building plankton and larvae sit low in food webs; if they decline, fish that feed on them can decline too.
Mission 3

Coral reefs under two pressures

  • Bleaching is caused by heat stress: from early 2023 to mid-2025, bleaching-level heat stress hit 84% of the world's coral reef area; mass bleaching was recorded in at least 83 countries and territories.
  • Acidification slows reef growth, and is projected to "amplify the adverse effects of warming".
  • Critical threshold: reefs are projected to decline by a further 70–90% at 1.5 °C of warming, and by more than 99% at 2 °C.
Mission 3

The Great Barrier Reef: recent data

  • Hard coral cover, northern GBR: 39.8% (2024) → 30.0% (2025), the largest annual decline recorded there since monitoring began.
  • AIMS: "the mass coral bleaching event was the primary source of coral mortality".
  • 2026: northern cover recovered to 35.1%, helped by monsoon cloud and cooler water.
  • Coral loss means habitat loss: biodiversity falls as reef-dependent fish and invertebrates lose shelter and food.
Mission 3

Who depends on healthy oceans?

  • Food: aquatic foods gave about 17% of the world's animal protein in 2019, but 50% or more in Cambodia, Ghana, Mozambique, Bangladesh, Indonesia, Sierra Leone and some small island developing states.
  • Coastal protection: reefs reduce the wave energy reaching coastlines by 97%; 197 million people can receive risk-reduction benefits from reefs.
  • Tourism: reef-based tourism earns income in many coastal and island economies.
  • Threats to ocean health hit hardest where people have fewest alternatives: often developing regions.
Quick check
?Which community is most vulnerable to reef loss?
Mission check

?In 2007 an Oregon oyster hatchery lost huge numbers of larvae. The cause was seawater with low pH and low carbonate saturation. How is this linked to the ocean taking up CO₂?

Corals face the same chemistry. The ocean sink slows warming, but absorbed CO₂ forms carbonic acid: surface pH has fallen by 0.1, leaving fewer carbonate ions for skeletons. Add heat-driven bleaching, and the IPCC projects a further 70–90% loss of reefs at 1.5 °C.

Mission 4

Why does sea level rise?

  • Thermal expansion: ocean water expands as it warms, so the same mass takes up more volume.
  • Transfer of water from land: melting glaciers and ice sheets, and changes in water stored on land, add water to the ocean.
  • Global mean sea level rose 0.20 m between 1901 and 2018.
  • The rate has sped up: 1.3 mm a year (1901–1971), 1.9 (1971–2006), 3.7 (2006–2018).
Mission 4

Which cause matters most?

  • 1971–2018, IPCC shares of the rise:
  • Thermal expansion 50%.
  • Glaciers 22%, ice sheets 20%, land-water storage 8%: together, the transfer of water from land.
  • Sea ice is not on the list: it already floats, so melting it adds almost nothing to sea level.
Quick check
?Using the IPCC shares for 1971–2018 (thermal expansion 50%, glaciers 22%, ice sheets 20%, land-water storage 8%), what share came from the transfer of water from land?
Mission 4

Tuvalu, Funafuti: the place

  • A Pacific nation of nine coral islands; mean elevation 2 m above sea level.
  • Only about 25 km² of land in total; much of it less than 1 m above high spring tide.
  • Funafuti atoll holds the capital; its largest islet is home to more than half the nation's people.
  • No hills to retreat to: the island community is the coast.
Mission 4

Threats

  • Sea level nearly 15 cm higher than 30 years earlier; about 5 mm a year, 1.5 times the global average.
  • Projected: 20 cm or more by 2050, perhaps another 0.5–1 m by 2100.
  • Flooding comes from "small-scale ocean variability, storms, high tides and sea level rise" combining.
  • Some land is also sinking (subsidence), at variable rates.
  • Storms: intensifying cyclones plus higher seas amplify inundation and erosion.
Mission 4

Impacts on the community

  • 2015: Cyclone Pam displaced 45% of the population.
  • Houses, schools and hospitals stand on the most exposed, "high-value" coastline.
  • By 2050, much of the land and critical infrastructure is projected to sit below the average high tide.
  • An estimate: over half the capital would be flooded by tidal waters by 2050 without action.
Quick check
?NASA's sea-level team visited Tuvalu. Which statement matches what they found is causing flooding there now?
Mission 4

Short-term adaptation: holding the shore

  • Before 2017: "community-scale interventions that hardly withstand the current wave energy".
  • Engineered coastal protection covered only 570 m.
  • The Tuvalu Coastal Adaptation Project (TCAP), 2017–2026: beach nourishment, revetments, sea walls and ecosystem measures on Funafuti, Nanumea and Nanumaga.
  • Target: 2,780 m of high-value coastline protected.
  • Finance: US$36 million from the Green Climate Fund plus US$2.9 million from Tuvalu's government.
Mission 4

Long-term adaptation: higher land and a way to move

  • Raised land: 8 hectares of elevated land on Fogafale (2025), "safe space for homes and infrastructure".
  • Designed to stay above sea level "beyond 2100", with drainage; its buried mega-bags expected to last "well beyond 40 years".
  • Mobility: the Falepili Union with Australia (in force 28 August 2024) gives Tuvaluans "the choice to travel to Australia to live, work, and study"; first ballot up to 280 places.
  • Australia recognises that Tuvalu's statehood continues despite sea-level rise.
Quick check
?TCAP aims to raise protected coastline from 570 m to 2,780 m. How many times longer is the target?
Mission 4

Governments and communities

  • Government (with UNDP and the Green Climate Fund): engineered defences, land raising, coastal mapping by airborne LIDAR.
  • International: a treaty with Australia opens a mobility pathway.
  • Communities: small local defences, often too weak for today's waves.
  • Scale check (our calculation): 8 ha = 0.08 km², about 0.3% of Tuvalu's 25 km².
  • Exam move (OCR "Evaluate"): weigh how much each measure protects, for how long, and for whom.
Mission check

?A low-lying atoll community (not Tuvalu) floods at king tides today, and its main islet is projected to be largely below high tide by 2100. Which plan best fits what Tuvalu's case shows?

An island community needs both: short-term protection (revetments, sea walls, nourishment) for today's floods, and long-term adaptation (raised land, planned mobility) for the rise expected by 2100. Its causes, thermal expansion and water from land, lie far beyond its own control.

Mission 5

Shrinking summer sea ice

  • On 12 September 2026 Arctic sea ice reached its yearly minimum: 4.60 million km² (preliminary), tied for 10th-lowest in the satellite record.
  • The record low was 3.39 million km², in 2012.
  • The September minimum is shrinking by 11.8% per decade relative to the 1981–2010 average.
  • IPCC: late-summer Arctic sea ice was smaller in 2011–2020 than at any time in at least 1,000 years (medium confidence).
Quick check
?The 2026 minimum (4.60 million km²) was 1.62 million km² below the 1981–2010 average minimum. By about what percentage was it below average?
Mission 5

The ice–albedo feedback

  • Albedo: the share of sunlight a surface reflects. Snow-covered sea ice reflects up to 90%; bare ice about 50–70%; open ocean only 6%.
  • Warming melts ice → darker ocean is exposed → more sunlight absorbed → more warming and melting.
  • This is a positive feedback: it amplifies the original change. Melt ponds lower albedo further.
Mission 5

Arctic amplification

  • The Arctic warmed nearly four times as fast as the globe in 1979–2021.
  • Several feedbacks contribute, including surface albedo; IPCC says the causes of polar amplification "are well understood".
  • IPCC projects the Arctic is likely to be practically sea-ice-free in September at least once before 2050, under all five scenarios assessed.
Mission 5

Thresholds: is the loss irreversible?

  • A tipping point is "a critical threshold beyond which a system reorganizes, often abruptly and/or irreversibly".
  • IPCC: Arctic summer sea ice "varies approximately linearly with global surface temperature, implying that there is no tipping point"; losses are "potentially reversible" (high confidence).
  • Other ice is different: at sustained warming of 2–3 °C, the Greenland and West Antarctic ice sheets could be lost "almost completely and irreversibly over multiple millennia".
  • Carbon lost from thawing permafrost is irreversible over centuries (high confidence).
Quick check
?Which statement about thresholds is best supported by the IPCC?
Mission 5

Geopolitics: who owns the seabed?

  • Under the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea, a coastal state can claim seabed beyond 200 nautical miles if it shows the continental shelf extends that far, by submitting evidence to a UN commission.
  • Arctic Ocean submissions: Russia (2001; revised 2015), Norway (2006), Denmark for northern Greenland (2014), Canada (2019).
  • Claims rest on seabed geology, and submissions have a ten-year time limit: ice loss does not create the right.
  • But less ice makes surveying, shipping and drilling easier, so the rights become worth more.
  • The Arctic Council's mandate "explicitly excludes military security": security is handled elsewhere.
Mission 5

Opportunities: routes and resources

  • Routes: Rotterdam to Yokohama is about 11,200 nautical miles via Suez, about 6,500 across the top of the world.
  • Ships entering the Arctic Polar Code area rose 37% in 2013–2023; distance sailed rose 111%.
  • Seabed resources: USGS estimated about 90 billion barrels of undiscovered oil, 1,669 trillion cubic feet of gas and 44 billion barrels of gas liquids north of the Arctic Circle; about 84% offshore.
Mission 5

Threats

  • Sea ice will still be present for most of each year, so routes stay seasonal and hazardous.
  • More ships and drilling bring risks of spills in ice-covered, remote waters.
  • Burning newly reachable oil and gas would add to the warming that opened the ocean: a human feedback loop.
  • Resource estimates are of undiscovered oil and gas, not proven reserves.
Mission 5

Indigenous peoples

  • Six organisations of Arctic Indigenous Peoples, including the Inuit Circumpolar Council and the Saami Council, are Permanent Participants in the Arctic Council.
  • Shishmaref, a village on a barrier island in Alaska: summer sea ice has become "much more unpredictable" since the 1980s; about 7 m of it washes into the sea each year; residents have voted three times to relocate.
  • Swedish Saami reindeer herders report thawing ground and "rotten spring vegetation" in summer grazing areas since the mid-1990s.
Mission 5

International management

  • Arctic Council: set up on 19 September 1996 (Ottawa Declaration); eight Arctic states decide by consensus.
  • It "does not and cannot implement or enforce" its guidelines and recommendations: it is a forum.
  • UN Commission on the Limits of the Continental Shelf reviews seabed claims.
  • Shipping rules apply in a defined Polar Code area, which the Arctic Council's shipping group uses to track traffic.
  • Exam move (OCR "Assess"): weigh how far each organisation can actually change outcomes, given consensus and lack of enforcement.
Quick check
?What is a key limit of the Arctic Council as a manager of the changing Arctic?
Mission check

?A shipping company and an energy company both plan Arctic projects for the 2040s. Which reasoning best explains why the Arctic's seabed and sea routes are becoming worth claiming?

Warming, amplified by the ice–albedo feedback, is shrinking summer sea ice. That opens seasonal routes and access to a seabed estimated to hold large undiscovered oil and gas, so states file claims, while Indigenous communities bear the impacts and international bodies can coordinate but not enforce.

Mission 6

Oceans carry globalisation

  • UNCTAD: "Shipping is the backbone of globalized trade".
  • Maritime trade reached 12,720 million tons in 2024, up 2.2%.
  • A choke point is a narrow strait or canal that a large share of shipping must pass through.
  • Block a choke point and ships must take a longer route, or wait.
Mission 6

Principal shipping routes

  • The busiest container routes run East–West:
  • Trans-Pacific: East Asia ↔ North America.
  • East Asia–Europe: through the Strait of Malacca, the Red Sea and the Suez Canal.
  • Transatlantic: Europe ↔ North America.
  • In 2024, container flows grew 14.7% Trans-Pacific eastbound, 10.2% East Asia–Europe westbound and 5.2% transatlantic.
Mission 6

The changing scale of shipping

  • Container trade: 153.8 million TEU (twenty-foot equivalent units) in 2018; 170.5 million in 2024. That is a rise of about 11% (our calculation).
  • Average voyage length rose from 4,831 miles (2018) to 5,245 miles (2024).
  • More cargo, carried further: so a detour at a choke point now affects more trade.
Mission 6

Direction and type of trade

  • Type: containers carry mostly manufactured goods; dry bulk ships carry iron ore, coal and grain; tankers carry crude oil, products and gas.
  • Direction: East–West trade is "anchored by Asia's central role in global logistics".
  • Oil: in 2024, 20 million barrels a day passed through the Strait of Hormuz, about 20% of world petroleum liquids consumption.
  • 84% of the crude oil and condensate passing through it went to Asian markets.
Quick check
?Which statement best describes the direction of seaborne oil trade through the Strait of Hormuz in 2024?
Mission 6

When a choke point fails

  • About 10% of global trade by tonnage passes through the Suez Canal.
  • "Attacks on vessels in the Red Sea prompted most shipping lines to reroute around the Cape of Good Hope".
  • By June 2024, Suez transits were down 70% against mid-December 2023; arrivals at the Cape of Good Hope were up 89%.
  • Shenzhen to Rotterdam: about 10,000 nautical miles via Suez (about 31 days), 13,000 via the Cape (about 41 days).
Quick check
?Using the figures earlier in this mission, by what percentage did rerouting around the Cape increase the Shenzhen–Rotterdam distance?
Mission 6

Naval strongholds: the USA

  • Home ports: Naval Station Norfolk, Virginia, is "the largest naval complex in the world" and supports the US Atlantic Fleet. Joint Base Pearl Harbor-Hickam, Hawaii, is a Pacific homeport for destroyers.
  • Overseas ports: Fleet Activities Yokosuka, Japan; Naval Support Activity Bahrain, supporting "maritime operations throughout the USCENTCOM AOR" (the Middle East); Naval Station Rota, Spain, "the Gateway to the Mediterranean", with forward-deployed destroyers.
  • Pattern: overseas ports sit beside major routes and choke points: Hormuz (Bahrain), Gibraltar and the Mediterranean (Rota), the western Pacific (Yokosuka).
Quick check
?Why does the US Navy keep forward-deployed ships at overseas ports such as Rota and Bahrain, rather than only at home ports?
Mission 6

Who owns the sea? The rules

  • Under UNCLOS, a state's territorial sea extends up to 12 nautical miles, and its exclusive economic zone (EEZ) no further than 200 nautical miles.
  • In its EEZ a state controls fishing, oil and gas.
  • So owning even a small island can bring a large area of sea, if it generates an EEZ.
Mission 6

The South China Sea: a marine conflict zone

  • China claims historic rights within a "nine-dash line".
  • 2016: in a case brought by the Philippines, a tribunal under UNCLOS found "no legal basis for China to claim historic rights to resources" within the nine-dash line.
  • It ruled the high-tide features of the Spratly Islands are legally "rocks" that do not generate an EEZ.
  • China's position: it "will neither accept nor participate in the arbitration".
Mission 6

Implications for people and environments

  • Environment: the tribunal found that China's island-building had "caused severe harm to the coral reef environment".
  • People: whoever holds an EEZ decides who may fish and drill there, so livelihoods depend on the outcome.
  • Routes: the sea lies on the East Asia–Europe shipping route, which passes through the Strait of Malacca.
Mission check

?In 2023–24, drought cut the number of ships the Panama Canal could take each day. Some carriers rerouted via Suez, the Strait of Magellan or the Cape of Good Hope. Why would a carrier choose a longer route?

In 2024 the Red Sea route became unsafe after attacks on ships, so most lines went round the Cape: about 30% further. Choke points give the oceans strategic value; that is also why naval powers base ships beside them and why seas such as the South China Sea are contested.

Make a guess: Why are a few stretches of sea so dangerous, both for ships and for people seeking safety?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 3.3-5.c distribution and management of 21st-century piracy
  • 3.3-5.c oceans as migrant escape routes, e.g. South East Asia to Australia, North Africa to Europe
Mission 7

What counts as piracy?

  • In international law (UNCLOS Art. 101), piracy is violence, detention or robbery for private ends by one ship against another on the high seas, outside any state's jurisdiction.
  • On the high seas "every State may seize a pirate ship".
  • The same acts inside a state's waters are armed robbery against ships, a matter for that state.
  • The International Maritime Bureau (IMB) counts both together.
Mission 7

Where it happens now

  • 2025: 137 incidents worldwide, up from 116 in 2024.
  • Singapore Straits: 80 incidents, 58% of the world total.
  • Gulf of Guinea: 21 incidents (2025).
  • Off Somalia, few incidents in 2025, but two in November far from shore; in January–September 2026 Somali pirates carried out 8 hijackings and took 94% of all crew held hostage.
Quick check
?In 2025 the Singapore Straits recorded 80 of 137 incidents worldwide. What share is that?
Mission 7

Why there?

  • Chokepoints: narrow, crowded lanes such as the Singapore Straits, where ships slow down or anchor.
  • Weak control on land or at sea: a state with little capacity to patrol its waters or punish attackers.
  • Reward: valuable cargo, ships or crews to hold for ransom.
  • Distribution = opportunity (shipping density) × weak governance × reward.
Mission 7

Somalia: peak and response

  • At its peak, in January 2011, pirates held 736 hostages and 32 ships.
  • The EU's Operation Atalanta, the EU's first naval operation, launched in 2008.
  • Tasks: escort World Food Programme ships (a 100% protection record), deter and repress piracy, monitor fishing.
  • Its mandate was extended towards February 2027.
Mission 7

Asia: sharing information

  • ReCAAP, finalised on 11 November 2004, is the "first and only" government-to-government anti-piracy agreement in Asia.
  • Its Information Sharing Centre opened in Singapore in 2006.
  • Members grew from 14 Asian countries (2006) to 22.
  • Its tools: information sharing, capacity building, cooperation between states and the shipping industry.
Quick check
?Why can a navy seize a pirate ship far out at sea, but not chase armed robbers into another country's harbour?
Mission 7

Seas as escape routes

  • Some people cross seas to escape conflict, persecution or poverty, often in unsafe boats run by smugglers.
  • Sea routes to Europe (from North Africa, and also from Türkiye and West Africa): sea arrivals were 363,123 in 2016, 102,431 in 2019, 263,048 in 2023 and 144,854 in 2025.
  • The totals cover every sea route into Europe, not only crossings from North Africa, and do not separate refugees, asylum seekers and other migrants.
  • The routes are short on a map but dangerous: overcrowded boats, poor weather, long distances between rescue points.
Mission 7

The human cost

  • IOM's Missing Migrants Project records 30,522 migrants dead or missing in the Mediterranean since 2014.
  • That is the largest regional figure on its global total of 68,447.
  • Exam move: a count of deaths needs a date range and a source, and is a minimum (many deaths at sea go unrecorded).
Mission 7

South East Asia to Australia

  • The route: smugglers' boats from Indonesia towards Australian territory such as Christmas Island, a short crossing south of Java.
  • Regional: the Bali Process (established 2002), co-chaired by Australia and Indonesia, is a "non-binding" forum of 45 member states against people smuggling and trafficking.
  • National: Australia's Operation Sovereign Borders, "a military-led border security operation", established in 2013; government states "Australia's borders are closed to unauthorised maritime arrivals".
  • Management relies on cooperation between origin, transit and destination states.
Quick check
?Sea arrivals to Europe rose from 102,431 in 2019 to 263,048 in 2023. Which interpretation is best supported?
Mission 7

Managing routes: tensions

  • Destination states try to deter crossings (patrols, returns, agreements with transit states).
  • Humanitarian agencies focus on rescue and the right to seek asylum.
  • Transit states may lack capacity or incentive to stop departures.
  • These positions conflict; outcomes depend on cooperation across the whole route, as with piracy.
Mission check

?A busy, narrow strait separates a stable, well-policed state from a state whose coastguard has collapsed in a civil war. Which prediction fits the patterns in this mission?

Piracy clusters where busy, narrow lanes meet weak control and a reward; escape routes cluster where the shortest crossing links danger with safety. Both are managed by cooperation between states: navies and information sharing for piracy; regional forums and rescue for migration.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Major ocean pollutants: CO₂ (acidification), plastics, heavy metals such as mercury (biomagnification) and nuclear waste (now banned from dumping; discharges limited).
  • Gyres trap floating plastic: the Great Pacific Garbage Patch holds at least 79,000 tonnes over 1.6 million km², harming wildlife through ingestion, entanglement and rafting.

Mission 2

  • Deepwater Horizon, Gulf of Mexico, 20 April 2010: 11 killed; 87 days; about 134 million gallons; at least about 2,100 km (1,300 miles) of shoreline exposed; turtles, dolphins, whales and trillions of larvae harmed.
  • Fishing: up to 88,522 sq mi closed; "reputational damage" to seafood. Tourism: $19.7 bn a year at risk; unoiled Pinellas lost about $70 m. Management: 45,000 responders, boom, dispersants, capping; $20.8 bn settlement; regulator split (BSEE).

Mission 3

  • Absorbing CO₂ makes the ocean more acidic; with heat-driven bleaching, reefs near a critical threshold, threatening food, coastal protection and income, above all in developing regions.

Mission 4

  • Sea level rises through thermal expansion (half of the 1971–2018 rise) and the transfer of water from land (glaciers, ice sheets, land water).
  • Tuvalu: threats (faster local rise, storms, high tides, sinking land) → impacts (45% displaced by Cyclone Pam) → short-term defences (TCAP) and long-term raised land and mobility (Falepili Union).

Mission 5

  • Summer sea ice is shrinking (11.8% a decade); ice–albedo feedback amplifies warming, but IPCC finds no tipping point for summer sea ice, unlike ice sheets and permafrost carbon.
  • Ice loss raises the stakes over seabed rights, routes and resources; Indigenous peoples face direct impacts; the Arctic Council and UN bodies manage by consensus and evidence, not enforcement.

Mission 6

  • Most trade moves by sea along East–West routes through a few choke points; when one fails, ships take the long way.
  • The USA projects power from home ports (Norfolk, Pearl Harbor) and overseas ports beside key routes (Yokosuka, Bahrain, Rota); the South China Sea is a marine conflict zone over EEZs and islands.

Mission 7

  • Piracy (UNCLOS: high seas) and armed robbery (national waters): 137 incidents in 2025; Singapore Straits 58%; Somali hijackings returned in 2026. Managed by Operation Atalanta (2008) and ReCAAP (2004).
  • Escape routes: North Africa to Europe (sea arrivals 102,431 in 2019, 263,048 in 2023; 30,522 dead or missing in the Mediterranean since 2014); South East Asia to Australia (Bali Process, 2002).
