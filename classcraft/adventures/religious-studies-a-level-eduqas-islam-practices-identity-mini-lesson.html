Mission 1: The Prophet's mosque was mud and palm trunks. What was it for, and do today's mosques still do the same?

Make a guess: The Prophet's mosque was mud and palm trunks. What was it for, and do today's mosques still do the same?

Mission 1

Masjid: a place of prostration

  • Masjid (Arabic): "place of sujud", of prostration. The English word mosque comes from it.
  • So the core of a masjid is simple: a clean place where Muslims bow to the ground before God.
  • A hadith says the whole earth was made a masjid for the Prophet's community: Muslims may pray anywhere clean (Bukhari; paraphrase).
  • So a building is not required for salah. What, then, is a mosque for?
  • Jami' (gathering) mosque: a larger mosque where Friday prayer (Jumu'ah) is held.
  • Your spec: "the masjid as a place of prostration".
Mission 1

The first masjid in Madinah

  • 622 CE, after the Hijrah: first the mosque at Quba, then the Prophet's own mosque in Madinah.
  • Built by the community together: mud-brick walls, palm-trunk pillars, palm-leaf roof, an open courtyard.
  • One building, many roles:
  • Worship: the five prayers, Friday prayer and khutbah; Bilal's adhan called people in.
  • Teaching: the Qur'an recited and explained; the suffa (shaded platform) sheltered poor Companions who studied there.
  • Government: disputes judged, decisions taken, delegations from other tribes received.
  • Welfare: charity given out; the needy fed and housed.
  • Your spec: "the importance of the functions of the first masjid in Madinah".

Mosques in the Qur'an

> "There is a mosque whose foundation was laid from the first day on piety; it is more worthy of the standing forth (for prayer) therein." (Qur'an 9:108, Yusuf Ali)

> "The mosques of Allah shall be visited and maintained by such as believe in Allah and the Last Day, establish regular prayers, and practise regular charity" (Qur'an 9:18, Yusuf Ali)

> "And the places of worship are only for Allah, so pray not unto anyone along with Allah." (Qur'an 72:18, Pickthall)

  • Many commentators link 9:108 to the mosque at Quba; others to the Prophet's mosque.

Mission 1

Design and features

  • Prayer hall: an open carpeted space with no seats, often with lines to keep rows straight. All stand shoulder to shoulder: equality before God.
  • Qiblah wall and mihrab: the niche showing the direction of the Ka'bah in Makkah, so all face one centre.
  • Minbar: stepped pulpit for the khutbah (the Prophet's had three steps).
  • Washing facilities: for wudu before prayer; purity of body points to purity of heart.
  • Dome: often said to echo the heavens; helps carry sound. Not required: many mosques have none.
  • Minaret: the tower from which the adhan is called; a landmark that announces the mosque.
  • Decoration: no pictures of people or animals; calligraphy of Qur'an verses and geometric patterns point beyond the visible to the infinite God.
  • Also: a space for women (separate hall, gallery or area behind), a shoe rack, often classrooms and offices.

Mission 1

Mosques in Britain: from house to centre

  • 1889: Britain's first purpose-built mosque, the Shah Jahan Mosque in Woking; the same year, the Liverpool Muslim Institute opened in converted terraced houses.
  • 1950s–1970s: migrant workers, mostly men, opened house mosques in rooms and terraces: prayer first.
  • As families arrived, mosques added madrasah classes (Qur'an and Arabic for children, often after school).
  • From the 1970s–80s, more purpose-built mosques with domes and minarets.
  • Today: many are community centres: youth clubs, food banks, advice, sport, marriage and funerals, open days and interfaith visits.
  • Debates: space and roles for women; the language of the khutbah; how imams are trained (abroad or in the UK).
Mission 1

The London Central Mosque: its work today

  • Worship: five daily prayers; Friday prayer for thousands; Eid prayers in several sittings.
  • Life events: Islamic marriages (nikah), funeral prayers, guidance for new Muslims taking the shahadah.
  • Education: Qur'an and Arabic classes, lectures, a library and bookshop.
  • Visits and dialogue: school groups, tours, interfaith events; a public face of Islam in London.
  • Advice and welfare: religious and family guidance; support for those in need.
  • The work has grown from prayer for diplomats and visitors to a national centre serving a diverse London ummah.
Mission 1Diversity

Roles of the masjid in the UK today

  • Religious: salah, Jumu'ah, Ramadan tarawih prayers, Eid, Qur'an recitation, nikah, funerals.
  • Educational: madrasah classes, adult study circles, talks.
  • Social: community meals (e.g. iftar), food banks, youth and sports clubs, care for the elderly.
  • Political (civic): voter registration, meetings with MPs and police, statements after local events.
  • Women: some mosques have full facilities, women trustees and classes; others little or no space. Muslim women's groups campaign for access, recalling that women prayed in the Prophet's mosque.
  • Shi'a centres (imambargah, husayniyyah) hold majlis gatherings, especially in Muharram (see Mission 4).
  • Supported by the community: donations, sadaqah, volunteers, committees (9:18).
  • Your spec: "the masjid as the hub of the Ummah … for men and women".

Mission 1Evaluate

Has the mosque kept its first purpose?

  • Claim: Today's mosques have kept the function of the first masjid in Madinah.
  • For: prayer and prostration remain central; many UK mosques again teach, feed and advise, as in Madinah.
  • For: the London Central Mosque's growth from a prayer room to a national centre mirrors that breadth.
  • Against: some mosques are prayer halls only, run by small committees; few have political or judicial roles.
  • Against: in Madinah women prayed and learned in the mosque; in some UK mosques women have little space.
  • Prostration first? Yes: the word masjid means place of sujud, and the earth itself is a masjid. Or no: the Prophet's mosque shows the community, not only the prayer, as its purpose.
  • Criteria: What counts as the mosque's "function": its acts, or its role as hub of the ummah? Should a minority community's mosque match a city-state's?
  • Spec issues: prostration as most important purpose; whether the masjid has maintained the first masjid's function.

Mission 2

Sawm: the duty

  • Sawm (fasting): the fourth pillar, kept through the month of Ramadan, the ninth month of the lunar calendar.
  • "O ye who believe! Fasting is prescribed to you as it was prescribed to those before you, that ye may (learn) self-restraint" (Qur'an 2:183, Yusuf Ali).
  • From dawn to sunset: no food, drink or sexual relations (2:187: "until the white thread of dawn appear to you distinct from its black thread").
  • Niyyah (intention) for the fast; suhur (pre-dawn meal); iftar (breaking the fast), often with dates and water, as the Prophet did.
  • Origins: prescribed in Madinah in 2 AH (624).
  • Because the lunar year is shorter, Ramadan moves about 11 days earlier each year: sometimes winter, sometimes summer.
Mission 2Read the source

Why Ramadan?

> "Ramadhan is the (month) in which was sent down the Qur'an, as a guide to mankind, also clear (Signs) for guidance and judgment" (Qur'an 2:185, Yusuf Ali)

> "Allah intends every facility for you; He does not want to put to difficulties." (Qur'an 2:185, Yusuf Ali)

Q1 Why is Ramadan the month of fasting, according to 2:185?

Show answer

The Qur'an was first sent down in it.

Q2 What does the second sentence suggest about those who cannot fast?

Show answer

God wants ease, not hardship: there are exemptions, the later screen.

Mission 2

Who fasts?

  • Required of adult Muslims who are sane, healthy and not travelling.
  • Make up later: the sick and travellers (2:184–185); women who are menstruating or recovering from childbirth (from hadith).
  • Fidya: those who can never fast (e.g. long-term illness, frailty in old age) feed a needy person for each day missed (2:184).
  • Pregnant and breastfeeding women may not fast if they fear harm; the schools differ on whether they make up, pay fidya, or both.
  • Children often practise with part-days.
  • Fasting that harms health is not required: the rule is God's ease (2:185).

Mission 2

The nights of Ramadan

  • Many Muslims recite the whole Qur'an during the month.
  • Laylat al-Qadr (the Night of Power), when the Qur'an's revelation began: "The Night of Power is better than a thousand months." (Qur'an 97:3, Yusuf Ali). Sought in the last ten nights.
  • Sunnis often mark the 27th night; Shi'a give special weight to the 19th, 21st and 23rd, which also recall Ali's wounding and death in Ramadan (40 AH).
  • Tarawih: Sunnis pray these extra night prayers in congregation at the mosque.
  • Twelver Shi'a pray extra Ramadan night prayers individually, regarding congregational tarawih as a later practice (introduced under the caliph Umar).
  • I'tikaf: retreat in the mosque in the last ten days.
Mission 2

What the fast is for

  • Relationship to God: "Fasting is for Me, and I will reward it" (hadith qudsi, Bukhari; Muslim; paraphrase). Only God sees a fast kept in secret.
  • Spirituality: taqwa (God-consciousness, 2:183) and control of the self (nafs).
  • Al-Ghazali (1058–1111), Ihya Ulum al-Din, Book 6: three levels of fasting: the stomach and body; the eyes, tongue, hands and feet kept from sin; the heart kept from everything but God (paraphrase).
  • Morality: whoever does not give up false speech and acting on it, God has no need of their giving up food and drink (Bukhari; paraphrase).
  • Community: shared iftars; empathy with the hungry; more giving (Ramadan is a common time to pay zakah).
  • Eduqas: relationship to God; development of Muslim spirituality; the importance of morality in Islam.
Mission 2

Eid-ul-Fitr

  • 1 Shawwal: the "festival of breaking the fast". Fasting on Eid day is forbidden.
  • Zakat al-fitr paid before the Eid prayer: a purification for the one who fasted and food for the poor (Abu Dawud; paraphrase).
  • Eid prayer in congregation, often in a park or large hall: two rak'ahs and a sermon; takbir on the way.
  • New clothes, gifts for children, visiting family, graves and neighbours; "Eid Mubarak".
  • Social importance: the poor are included; unity; brotherhood of the ummah.
  • Eduqas spelling: Id-ul-Fitr.
  • Spec issue: whether Id-ul-Fitr is treated as a religious celebration or a social occasion.

Mission 2Evaluate

Who benefits more from fasting?

  • Claim: Fasting benefits the individual more than the community.
  • Individual: taqwa and self-control (2:183); the hadith qudsi makes the fast a secret between the person and God.
  • Individual: Al-Ghazali's levels are all about the inner life.
  • Community: shared iftars, tarawih and Eid build unity; zakat al-fitr and Ramadan giving feed the poor.
  • Community: a person who fasts learns empathy, and empathy is for others.
  • Criteria: Can the two be separated? Which benefits does the Qur'an name? Is a fast without changed conduct of any benefit?
  • Spec issue: the extent to which fasting benefits the individual more than the community.

Mission 3

The party of Ali

  • Shi'a comes from Shi'at Ali: "the party (followers) of Ali".
  • A minority of Muslims worldwide; the majority in Iran, Iraq, Azerbaijan and Bahrain; large communities in Lebanon, Pakistan, India and Britain.
  • Shared with Sunnis: one God, the Qur'an, the Prophet as the final messenger, salah, fasting, Hajj, the Day of Judgement.
  • The distinctive belief: Imamah (the Imamate): after the Prophet, God appointed Imams from his family to guide the community.
  • Origins: Ali's designation at Ghadir Khumm (as Shi'a understand it); the martyrdom of Husain at Karbala in 680 (see Mission 4).
  • At Ghadir Khumm (632), on the way back from his last pilgrimage, the Prophet said: "For whomever I am his mawla, Ali is his mawla" (paraphrase). Shi'a read mawla as master or guardian, so Ali was named leader; Sunnis read it as friend or ally.
  • Eduqas spelling: Hussein.
Mission 3

Who is an Imam?

  • Designated (nass): each Imam is named by the Prophet or the Imam before him, by God's command.
  • Ma'sum (infallible): protected by God from sin and error ('ismah).
  • Knowledge ('ilm): the Imam knows the outer and inner meaning of the Qur'an and the Prophet's teaching.
  • Hujjah (proof of God): the earth is never without one (al-Kulayni, al-Kafi; paraphrase).
  • So the Imams' words and actions are an authoritative source beside the Qur'an and the Prophet's Sunnah. Their reported sayings fill Shi'a hadith collections such as al-Kafi (10th century).
  • The Imam is not a prophet: revelation ended with Muhammad. He explains and guards it.
Mission 3Read the source

Verses Shi'a scholars cite

> "He said: 'I will make thee an Imam to the Nations.' He pleaded: 'And also (Imams) from my offspring!' He answered: 'But My Promise is not within the reach of evil-doers.'" (Qur'an 2:124, Yusuf Ali)

> "Obey Allah, and obey the Messenger, and those charged with authority among you." (Qur'an 4:59, Yusuf Ali)

  • 2:124: God makes Abraham an Imam after testing him. Shi'a reading: Imamate is God's appointment, and no wrongdoer can hold it; so the Imam must be sinless.
  • 4:59: Shi'a reading: "those charged with authority" are the infallible Imams, since God would not command obedience to someone who might lead into error. Sunni reading: rulers and scholars, obeyed within the limits of God's law.
  • With 33:33 (which speaks of God purifying the Prophet's Family; Shi'a apply it to the Prophet, Ali, Fatimah, Hasan and Husain), these verses ground belief in the Imams.

Q1 In 2:124, who cannot receive God's promise of leadership?

Show answer

Evil-doers, wrongdoers.

Q2 Why do Sunnis and Shi'a read 4:59 differently?

Show answer

They disagree on who holds authority: the Imams, or rulers and scholars.

Mission 3

The Twelve Imams

  • 1 Ali (d. 661, Najaf) · 2 Hasan (d. 670, Madinah) · 3 Husain (d. 680, Karbala)
  • 4 Ali Zayn al-Abidin · 5 Muhammad al-Baqir · 6 Ja'far al-Sadiq (d. 765): great teacher of law; Shi'a law is called Ja'fari
  • 7 Musa al-Kazim · 8 Ali al-Rida (d. 818; shrine in Mashhad, Iran) · 9 Muhammad al-Jawad
  • 10 Ali al-Hadi · 11 Hasan al-Askari (d. 874, Samarra) · 12 Muhammad al-Mahdi: believed by Twelvers to be in occultation
  • Shi'a accounts hold that most Imams were killed or poisoned by rulers: a line of martyrs for justice.
  • Their tombs are major places of ziyarah (visiting pilgrimage).

Mission 3

Occultation and the Mahdi

  • 874: Twelvers believe the twelfth Imam, Muhammad al-Mahdi, a child, withdrew from view: ghaybah (occultation).
  • Lesser occultation (874–941): he is said to have guided the community through four deputies in turn.
  • Greater occultation (from 941): no named deputy. Twelvers believe he is alive, hidden by God.
  • They expect him to return as the Mahdi ("rightly guided one") to fill the earth with justice as it is now filled with injustice (hadith; paraphrase), with Jesus (Isa) at his side.
  • Intizar (waiting) is active: living justly, praying for his return; his birthday (15 Sha'ban) is celebrated.
  • Sunnis also expect a Mahdi from the Prophet's family before the end, but most do not identify him with the hidden twelfth Imam.
Mission 3Diversity

Twelvers, Seveners and others

  • Shi'a groups split over which son carried the Imamate.
  • Twelvers (Ithna 'Ashariyyah): the great majority of Shi'a; twelve Imams; the last in occultation.
  • Isma'ilis ("Seveners"): after Ja'far al-Sadiq (d. 765), they followed his son Isma'il and his line, not Musa al-Kazim. Strictly, "Seveners" were those who held the line ended with the seventh Imam. The Fatimid caliphs were Isma'ili.
  • Isma'ilis today: Nizaris, led by a living, present Imam, the Aga Khan; Bohras, whose Imam is hidden and who are led by a Da'i.
  • Zaydis ("Fivers"): follow Zayd, son of the fourth Imam; any learned descendant of Hasan or Husain who rises against injustice may be Imam; mainly in Yemen.
Mission 3

Authority while the Imam is hidden

  • In the greater occultation, Twelvers hold that qualified jurists (mujtahids) represent the Imam's guidance in law.
  • Each believer follows (taqlid) a living marja' al-taqlid ("source of imitation"), e.g. Grand Ayatollah Ali al-Sistani (b. 1930) in Najaf; the marja' receives part of the khums.
  • Wilayat al-faqih (guardianship of the jurist): Ayatollah Khomeini (1902–1989), lectures of 1970: a just jurist should also hold political rule. This became the basis of the Islamic Republic of Iran (1979).
  • Many Shi'a jurists, including al-Sistani, give jurists a narrower role in politics.
  • So authority flows: God → Prophet → Imams → (in occultation) jurists on the Imam's behalf, never replacing him.

Mission 3

Distinctive practices

  • The Five Pillars, Shi'a view: the practices are counted among ten Furu' al-Din (branches), beside khums, jihad, enjoining good and forbidding evil; many add "Ali is the wali (friend) of God" after the shahadah in the adhan, though not as part of the obligatory words; the Ramadan fast ends a little later, when the redness of sunset has gone.
  • Salat and wudu: prayers often combined into three times; forehead on a turbah; feet wiped, not washed; adhan adds "Come to the best of deeds".
  • Khums: a fifth of surplus income each year, half for the Imam's share (given through a marja'), half for needy descendants of the Prophet.
  • Pilgrimage: Hajj, and ziyarah to the Imams' shrines: Najaf, Karbala, Kadhimiya, Samarra, Mashhad. Millions walk to Karbala for Arba'een (see Mission 4).
  • Muta (temporary marriage): a marriage contract for a fixed period with a dowry; Twelvers hold it lawful, citing 4:24; Sunnis hold the Prophet forbade it. Many Shi'a discourage it in practice.
  • Taqiyya (precautionary concealment): hiding one's faith when life is in danger, as Shi'a did under hostile rulers. Cited: 16:106, 3:28. Sunnis also allow concealment under compulsion. Not a general licence to deceive.
  • Your spec: "muta (temporary marriage) and taqiyya (concealing belief)".
Mission 3Evaluate

More alike or more different?

  • Claim: The differences between Sunni and Shi'a Islam matter more than what they share.
  • Background: in Shi'a teaching Imamah is a root of faith (one of the five Usul al-Din). The Amman Message (2004–2005) is a statement in which hundreds of Sunni and Shi'a scholars recognised each other's schools as Muslim.
  • For: Imamah is a root of faith for Shi'a; it changes who holds religious authority, which hadith count, and how law is made.
  • For: distinct practices (khums, muta, ziyarah, Muharram) mark separate identities; politics has deepened the division.
  • Against: the shahadah, Qur'an, salah, sawm, zakah and Hajj are shared; the Amman Message recognises both.
  • Against: Sunnis also love the ahl al-bayt and expect a Mahdi; Sufi orders honour Ali.
  • Criteria: Are differences of authority deeper than differences of practice? Should we judge by doctrine, daily life, or history?

Mission 4

680: a refusal

  • Husain (Hussein): son of Ali and Fatimah, grandson of the Prophet; for Shi'a Muslims the third Imam.
  • 680: Mu'awiya dies; his son Yazid demands an oath of allegiance (bay'ah). Husain refuses: Yazid's rule is unjust and hereditary.
  • Letters from Kufa in Iraq invite Husain to lead them. He sets out from Makkah with his family and a small band of companions.
  • Husain's stated aim (Shi'a sources, paraphrase): not pride or power, but to reform the community of his grandfather, to enjoin good and forbid evil.
  • Yazid's governor in Kufa crushes support; the Kufans do not come.
  • Eduqas spelling: Hussein; Ashura.
Mission 4

Ten days at Karbala

  • 2 Muharram 61 AH: Husain's party is forced to camp at Karbala, by the Euphrates.
  • 7 Muharram: the army cuts them off from the river. Days of thirst follow.
  • 10 Muharram, Ashura (10 October 680): Husain's companions and family fall one by one.
  • Abbas, his half-brother, is killed trying to bring water; his infant son Ali al-Asghar is struck by an arrow.
  • Husain is killed; his head is taken to Kufa and Damascus.
  • The women and children, led by his sister Zaynab, are taken captive. Zaynab speaks out boldly in Yazid's court. His son Ali Zayn al-Abidin, too ill to fight, survives as the fourth Imam.
Mission 4Read the source

Not dead, but living

> "And say not of those who are slain in the way of Allah: 'They are dead.' Nay, they are living, though ye perceive (it) not." (Qur'an 2:154, Yusuf Ali)

> "Who say, when afflicted with calamity: 'To Allah We belong, and to Him is our return'" (Qur'an 2:156, Yusuf Ali)

  • Shahid: martyr, "witness": one who dies for God's cause bears witness to the truth with his life.
  • Shi'a Muslims read these verses of Husain: his death was victory, not defeat; he lives with God.
  • 2:156 is said at every loss; mourners recite it for Husain.
  • A hadith: "Husain is from me, and I am from Husain" (Tirmidhi; paraphrase).

Q1 How do these verses change the meaning of Husain's death?

Show answer

The martyr is alive with God: his death is witness, not defeat.

Q2 Why might 2:156 be recited in Muharram?

Show answer

It is the believer's response to calamity: all belongs to God.

Mission 4

What Karbala means

  • Martyrdom: for Shi'a Muslims, Husain chose death over approving injustice; they believe his sacrifice kept true Islam from being corrupted by power.
  • Justice against tyranny: a well-known Shi'a saying: "Every day is Ashura, every land is Karbala": each age has its Yazid and its Husain.
  • Persecution: the Imams after Husain lived under hostile rulers; Shi'a history is remembered as a history of the oppressed.
  • Grief and love: sayings of the Imams promise reward for tears shed for Husain (Shi'a hadith; paraphrase). Mourning shows love for the ahl al-bayt.
  • Intercession: many Shi'a hope for Husain's intercession on the Day of Judgement.
  • Guilt and loyalty: Kufa failed Husain. Mourning asks: would I have stood with him?
  • Your spec: "persecution, suffering".
Mission 4

Muharram rituals

  • The first ten days of Muharram build up to Ashura. Black clothes; no weddings or celebrations.
  • Majlis (pl. majalis): gatherings in a husayniyyah or imambargah where a speaker recounts the events, day by day (rawda).
  • Marthiya and nawha: elegies and laments, sung by the gathering.
  • Matam: rhythmic beating of the chest in grief.
  • Processions: banners (alam), a replica of Husain's tomb (South Asia), a riderless horse for Husain's horse; in Iran, ta'ziyeh passion plays.
  • Sabil: free water and drinks given to passers-by, remembering the thirst at Karbala.
Mission 4Diversity

Self-mortification: a debate

  • Some mourners go beyond matam: zanjir (striking the back with chains, sometimes with blades) or tatbir (cutting the scalp with a blade) to share Husain's suffering.
  • Supporters: a sign of love and grief; a pledge of readiness to suffer for justice; an old custom.
  • Critics within Shi'a Islam: Islam forbids harming the body; it gives outsiders a false picture of the faith. Many leading jurists forbid tatbir (e.g. Iran's Ayatollah Khamenei, 1994); others permit it only if it causes no serious harm.
  • Alternatives: many communities, including in Britain, hold blood donation drives at Ashura: giving blood to save life.
  • Most Shi'a Muslims mourn with majlis and matam; self-cutting is a minority practice.
  • Spec issue: "self-harm as excessive religious devotion".
Mission 4

Pilgrimage to Karbala

  • Husain's shrine, and Abbas's beside it, stand in Karbala, Iraq: among the holiest places for Shi'a Muslims.
  • Pilgrims visit (ziyarah) on Ashura and through the year; they recite Ziyarat Ashura, a greeting and lament for Husain.
  • Arba'een: 40 days after Ashura (20 Safar), millions walk to Karbala, many from Najaf, about 80 km; local people give free food, water and rest on the way.
  • Under Saddam Hussein these gatherings were restricted; after 2003 they grew hugely.
  • Pilgrimage brings Husain's story into the body: thirst, walking, sacrifice.

Mission 4

Ashura and Shi'a identity

  • Identity: Ashura is where Shi'a Muslims most visibly say who they are: lovers of the ahl al-bayt, people who refuse injustice.
  • Community: local associations (anjuman) organise processions; families sponsor majalis and food as a vow (nazr); children learn the nawhas.
  • In Britain: processions in London and other cities; open majalis and blood drives invite neighbours in.
  • Sunni Muslims also mark 10 Muharram: the Prophet fasted that day, remembering God's saving of Moses (Bukhari; paraphrase). Many Sunnis, especially in South Asia, also honour Husain.
  • Your spec: "the role of the community of believers in ensuring the traditions of the festival are maintained".
Mission 4Evaluate

Does a focus on suffering misrepresent Shi'a identity?

  • Claim: Ashura's focus on suffering gives a false picture of Shi'a identity.
  • For: outsiders see blood and weeping, not belief; self-harm images are used to mock or fear Shi'a Muslims.
  • For: the meaning of Karbala has shifted over time (penitence, public mourning, protest), so suffering is not the only possible centre.
  • Against: suffering is not the goal; it is witness to justice and love for the Prophet's family.
  • Against: grief binds the community and keeps the memory of persecution alive, which is part of its identity.
  • Criteria: Who decides what represents a tradition: insiders or observers? Is suffering the message, or the way the message is told?
  • Spec issue: "whether a focus on suffering during Ashura misrepresents Shi'a identity".
Mission check

Karbala is remembered as a stand for justice; the rituals keep the story alive and keep asking what loyalty to Husain means today. Next: the ummah, the whole community of Muslims.

Mission 5: Why would a Muslim who already prays five times a day choose a teacher and a path of training?

Mission 5

Ihsan: worship as if you see God

  • The Gabriel hadith (Sahih Muslim, Book of Faith, from 'Umar; also Sahih al-Bukhari, Book of Faith): a stranger in white questions the Prophet; the Prophet later says it was the angel Gabriel, come to teach the religion.
  • Three levels (paraphrase):
  • Islam: the five pillars (outward practice).
  • Iman: faith in God, angels, books, messengers, the Last Day and God's decree.
  • Ihsan: "to worship God as though you see Him; and if you do not see Him, He sees you."
  • Sufis take ihsan (doing what is beautiful; spiritual excellence) as their goal: the same religion, lived with full awareness of God.
Mission 5

Purifying the self

  • Tazkiyat al-nafs: purifying (and growing) the self (nafs), the part of us that wants and grasps.
  • Sufis map three states of the nafs from Qur'anic phrases:
  • The soul that commands evil (al-nafs al-ammara, 12:53).
  • The self-reproaching soul (al-nafs al-lawwama, 75:2): conscience awake.
  • The soul at peace (al-nafs al-mutma'inna, 89:27): "But ah! thou soul at peace!" (Qur'an 89:27, Pickthall)
  • The struggle against the ego is often called the greater jihad (a saying attributed to the Prophet; many hadith scholars judge its chain weak).
Mission 5

The role of asceticism

  • Renunciation (zuhd): letting go of attachment to wealth, status and comfort, not of the world itself.
  • Practices: extra fasting, night vigils, simple food and clothes, retreat (khalwa; the Chishti forty-day chilla).
  • Poverty (faqr): Sufis are often called fuqara' (the poor); the aim is to need God alone.
  • Limits: most Sufis reject lifelong celibacy and withdrawal; the Qur'an says monasticism was "invented", not ordained (57:27). The Prophet married, traded and led a community.
  • So asceticism is a training stage: it weakens the grasping nafs so the heart can turn to God.
  • Spec: "the role of asceticism".

> "And do thou (O Muhammad) remember thy Lord within thyself humbly and with awe, below thy breath, at morn and evening." (Qur'an 7:205, Pickthall)

Mission 5

Dhikr and muraqaba

  • Dhikr (remembrance): repeating God's names or short formulas, e.g. la ilaha illa'llah (no god but God) or Allah, often counted on prayer beads.
  • Aloud (jahri), often in a group circle (Qadiriyya); or silent (khafi), in the heart (Naqshbandiyya).
  • A litany (wird) is a daily set of prayers and formulas given by the shaykh.
  • Muraqaba (Sufi meditation; "watchfulness"): sitting in silence, aware that God watches you; one of God's names is the Watcher (al-Raqib, 4:1). It lives out the second half of ihsan: "He sees you".
  • Personal mystical union: many Sufis hope this remembrance leads to fana' ("passing away"), in which the self passes away so that only God is in view. Mainstream Sufis teach that this is a union of awareness, not of essence: the soul does not become God.
  • Goal: to move remembrance from the tongue to the heart, until the whole person is aware of God.
Quick check
?A Naqshbandi Sufi sits quietly after the dawn prayer, repeating "Allah" in her heart without moving her lips. What is she practising?
Mission 5

Master and disciple

  • The shaykh (also pir, murshid: guide) trains the disciple (murid: one who wills, seeks).
  • The disciple gives an oath of allegiance (bay'a), often by clasping hands, and joins the shaykh's chain (silsila) back to the Prophet.
  • The shaykh knows the soul's illnesses and prescribes: fasting, a litany, service, silence. Teaching comes through companionship (suhba) as much as words.
  • Qur'anic model: Moses asks a servant of God (named Khidr in tradition) to teach him: "May I follow thee, to the end that thou mayst teach me right conduct of that which thou hast been taught?" (Qur'an 18:66, Pickthall). The lesson is patience with what one does not yet understand.
  • Parables: teachers use stories. Rumi's elephant in the dark (Masnavi III): people feel one part each and argue; each grasps only a part of the truth.
  • The Qutb ("pole" or "axis"): in Sufi teaching, the highest friend of God of each age, around whom a hidden order of saints turns. The title is also given to great shaykhs.
Mission 5

The tariqa: path and order

  • Tariqa means path: the inner way to God, beside the shari'a (the broad road of law).
  • A classic image: the shari'a is the boat, the tariqa the sea, and haqiqa (the Reality) the pearl. Without the boat, no one reaches the pearl.
  • Tariqa also means an order: a community sharing a founder, a silsila, a litany and meetings (e.g. a weekly dhikr circle).
  • The path is described as stations to be earned (repentance, renunciation, patience, trust, contentment) and states given by God.

Mission 5

Sama': spiritual listening

  • Sama' ("listening"): hearing poetry, chant or music to stir love and longing for God.
  • Mevlevi ceremony (the "whirling dervishes", Konya): the reed flute (ney) plays; dervishes turn, right palm up to receive from God, left palm down to pass it to the earth.
  • Their tall hat stands for a tombstone, the white skirt for a shroud: turning is dying to self.
  • Chishti gatherings in South Asia use qawwali (sung devotional poetry).
  • Listening can bring wajd (ecstasy), a state in which the listener is overcome.
  • Debate: many jurists forbid musical instruments; the Naqshbandiyya avoid music; al-Ghazali allowed sama' with conditions of time, place and company (Ihya', Book of Listening).
Mission 5Read the source

Rumi's Song of the Reed

Jalal al-Din Rumi (1207–1273), Masnavi, Book I, opening lines (paraphrase):

  • Listen to the reed flute, telling of separation.
  • Since I was cut from the reed-bed, my cry has made men and women weep.
  • Whoever is far from their source longs to return to the time of union.
  • Its sound is fire, not wind: the fire of love.
  • Meaning (as Sufis read it): the reed is the soul; the reed-bed is its origin with God; the lament is longing; the path is the journey home.

Show answer

It has been cut off from the reed-bed: separation from its source.

Q2 What does the reed say its sound really is?

Show answer

Fire, not wind: the fire of love.

Mission 5Evaluate

Are Sufi devotional practices religiously valid?

  • Claim: Sufi practices such as group dhikr, muraqaba and sama' are valid Islamic worship.
  • For: dhikr and meditation on God are commanded or praised in the Qur'an (33:41, 7:205, 3:191) and the Gabriel hadith.
  • For: great Sunni scholars such as al-Ghazali practised and defended them; they bear fruit in character.
  • Against: Salafi critics: forms not done by the Prophet (set litanies, dancing, music) are bid'a (innovation).
  • Against: ecstasy can be faked or confused with emotion; devotion to shaykhs can slide towards excess.
  • Judgement criteria: Must a practice copy the Prophet's form, or only serve his aims? Is the test scripture, scholarly consensus or spiritual fruit?

Mission 6

Justice and the aims of punishment

  • "Allah commands justice, the doing of good" (Qur'an 16:90, Yusuf Ali); "when ye judge between man and man, that ye judge with justice" (Qur'an 4:58, Yusuf Ali).
  • Muslims believe crimes wrong God, the victim and society.
  • Aims of punishment in fiqh: retribution (justice for the wronged), deterrence, protection of society, reform and repentance.
  • Final judgement belongs to God; earthly punishment is limited and needs strict proof.
Mission 6

Hudud: fixed limits

  • Hudud (singular hadd, "limit"): penalties fixed by Qur'an or Sunna, seen as God's right; a judge may not change them.
  • Examples: theft (5:38); unlawful sexual relations (24:2); false accusation of unchastity (24:4); armed robbery and spreading terror (hiraba, 5:33); drinking alcohol (from the Sunna).
  • Strict proof: e.g. four eyewitnesses for adultery (24:4); confession can be withdrawn; theft only above a minimum value and not from need.
  • Legal maxim: "avert the hudud by doubts" (Tirmidhi; paraphrase). Caliph Umar is reported to have suspended the theft penalty in a famine.
  • Today: few states apply hudud; many Muslims and scholars question whether they can be applied justly now.
Mission 6

Qisas and diya

  • Qisas: equal retribution for murder or deliberate injury: "life for life, eye for eye" (5:45). It limits revenge: one life for one life, never more (17:33).
  • The victim's family chooses: qisas; diya (compensation, "blood money"); or forgiveness.
  • Diya is also due for accidental killing (4:92), with an act of expiation.
  • A court must try the case and carry out any sentence: never private revenge.
  • Forgiveness is praised: "if a person forgives and makes reconciliation, his reward is due from Allah" (Qur'an 42:40, Yusuf Ali).

Mission 6

Ta'zir and maslaha

  • Ta'zir: discretionary punishments set by a judge or the state for offences without a fixed penalty: e.g. fraud, bribery, drug dealing, cases where hadd proof fails.
  • Can be fines, prison, community penalties; aimed at reform and deterrence.
  • Guided by maslaha (public interest): laws should protect the five essentials named by al-Ghazali (1058–1111): religion, life, intellect, lineage, property.
  • So the state can make new laws (traffic, cyber-crime) that serve the shari'a's purposes.
  • Eduqas spells it tazir; the three categories: qisas, hudud, tazir.

Mission 6

The death penalty: a traditionalist case

  • In classical fiqh, death may be a penalty for: murder (qisas, unless the family forgives); hiraba (5:33); adultery by a married person (from the Sunna); apostasy (disputed today).
  • Sheikh Ahmad Ash-Sharabasi (1918–1980), a scholar of al-Azhar, Cairo: a traditionalist defence:
  • The penalty is God's law, not a human invention; Muslims may not set it aside.
  • It saves life: "in the Law of Equality there is (saving of) Life" (2:179) by deterring murder.
  • It is just: the killer takes a life; justice demands the like.
  • It is merciful within limits: strict proof, and the family's right to forgive.
Mission 6

Modern voices: limit or pause

  • Naima Asif, a barrister, gives a modern legal response. Compare it with Ash-Sharabasi on: who decides, how proof is tested, and whether forgiveness and mercy should come first.
  • Many Muslims in Britain and elsewhere argue that the Qur'an's preference for forgiveness (2:178, 42:40) points away from executions; others say this sets aside God's law.
Mission 6

James Rachels: a non-Islamic response

  • James Rachels (1941–2003), American moral philosopher, The Elements of Moral Philosophy (1986).
  • Divine command: if a penalty is right only because God commands it, morality looks arbitrary; if God commands it because it is right, then the reasons must stand on their own.
  • So the death penalty must be judged by reasons open to all: does it deter? Is it deserved? Is it applied fairly?
  • Problems he and others raise: the evidence for deterrence is unclear; innocent people can be executed; sentences fall unequally on the poor.
  • A Muslim reply: God's wisdom includes reasons (2:179 gives one), and strict proof and forgiveness answer many of the objections.
Mission 6Evaluate

Can Islamic teachings on punishment be applied today?

  • Claim: Islamic teachings on punishment can be fully applied today.
  • For: they are revealed law (5:38, 24:2, 2:178); strict proof and forgiveness make them more merciful than they look.
  • For: qisas with family choice and diya puts victims at the centre, something many modern systems lack.
  • Against: many Muslims argue that the Qur'an's preference for forgiveness (2:178, 42:40) points away from executions; and errors cannot be undone.
  • Against: Rachels: penalties must be justified by shared reasons; deterrence is unproven.
  • Judge it by: Who should interpret: classical jurists or modern scholars? Is maslaha a reason to adapt? Which aims matter most: retribution, deterrence or reform?
  • Eduqas issue: the extent to which Islamic teachings on punishment can be applied today.
Mission check

?A driver, keeping to the speed limit, accidentally hits and kills a pedestrian who steps into the road. What would classical Islamic law require?

Islamic criminal law ties punishment to intention and to the victim's rights: retribution for the deliberate, compensation for the accidental, mercy for those who forgive.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Masjid means place of prostration; the first masjid in Madinah was also school, court, welfare centre and meeting place.
  • Features (prayer hall, qiblah wall and mihrab, minbar, wudu area, dome, minaret, calligraphy) serve salah and point to God's oneness.

Mission 2

  • Sawm: dawn to sunset in Ramadan, for taqwa (2:183), with exemptions for illness, travel and frailty.
  • Ramadan's benefits: nearness to God, spiritual discipline, moral change, unity and care for the poor.
  • Eid-ul-Fitr ends the fast with zakat al-fitr and shared joy.

Mission 3

  • Shi'a Muslims believe God appointed twelve (or, for Isma'ilis, a different line of) Imams from the Prophet's family: designated, infallible, sources of guidance.
  • Twelvers believe the twelfth Imam has been in occultation since 874/941 and will return as the Mahdi; jurists (marja') act for him meanwhile.
  • Distinctive practices: combined prayers, turbah and wudu, khums, ziyarah, muta, taqiyya.

Mission 4

  • 10 Muharram 61 AH (680 CE): Husain, refusing to approve Yazid's unjust rule, was killed at Karbala with his family and companions.
  • Muharram rites (majlis, rawda, nawha, matam, processions, ziyarah, Arba'een) mourn Husain and renew loyalty to justice and to the ahl al-bayt.
  • Self-mortification is a minority practice, debated among Shi'a jurists.

Mission 5

  • Ihsan (worship as if you see God) is the goal; tazkiyat al-nafs (purifying the self) and remembrance (dhikr) are the means.
  • A shaykh trains a disciple within a tariqa (path and order), through bay'a, companionship, litanies and, for some orders, sama'.
  • Rumi's reed: the soul cut from its source longs to return; love drives the journey.
  • The Qutb is the "pole" of the saints; muraqaba is watchful meditation; asceticism is training, not an end.

Mission 6

  • Three categories: hudud (fixed, strict proof), qisas (retribution, with diya or forgiveness by the family), ta'zir (discretion, guided by maslaha).
  • Death penalty: a traditionalist defence (Ash-Sharabasi); many Muslims stress forgiveness instead.
  • James Rachels: penalties must be justified by reasons open to all.
