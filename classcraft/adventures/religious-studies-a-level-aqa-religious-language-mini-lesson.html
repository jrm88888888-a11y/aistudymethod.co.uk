Mission 1

Mission 1: Can you describe God by saying only what God is not?

Make a guess: Can you describe God by saying only what God is not?

Mission 1

Why talking about God is hard

  • We learn words from finite things we can see and share: "wise" from wise people, "father" from fathers.
  • Classical theists believe God is infinite, timeless, not a physical object, and not one thing among others: this is God's transcendence.
  • So the words may not fit: can a word learned in time describe a God outside time?
  • The question for this unit: can talk about God be meaningful, and if so, how?
Mission 1

Cognitive and non-cognitive

  • Cognitive language makes factual claims: it says how things are, so it is true or false. "God exists." "Jesus rose from the dead."
  • Non-cognitive language does something else: it expresses feelings or commitments, commands, praises or shapes a way of life. It is not true or false as a report of fact. "Lord, have mercy." "Hallelujah."
  • Most believers take central claims ("God created the world") as cognitive: they mean them as true.
  • Hare's bliks, and some readings of Wittgenstein, treat religious language as mainly non-cognitive (–f).
  • The difference matters: a challenge such as verification (see Mission 4) is aimed at cognitive claims.

Mission 1

Univocal and equivocal

  • Univocal: a word means exactly the same in both uses. "The cat is black"; "the car is black".
  • If "wise" is univocal in "Sophie is wise" and "God is wise", God's wisdom is just like ours, only bigger. The danger: anthropomorphism, making God in human form (a God who learns, forgets, changes mind).
  • Equivocal: the same word with completely different meanings. A cricket bat; a bat in a cave.
  • If "wise" is equivocal, it tells us nothing about God. Why then call God wise rather than foolish? The danger: we can know nothing about God at all.
  • Medieval thinkers asked: is there a path between these two?
Mission 1Read the source

Aquinas: neither the same nor different

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274), Summa Theologiae I, question 13, article 5 (c. 1268):

> "Univocal predication is impossible between God and creatures."

> "Neither, on the other hand, are names applied to God and creatures in a purely equivocal sense, as some have said."

  • His reason against equivocal names: then "from creatures nothing could be known or demonstrated about God at all".

Q1 Why does Aquinas reject univocal language?

Show answer

God is not wise in the limited, separate way a human is wise; the word cannot mean the same.

Q2 What would follow if all words about God were equivocal?

Show answer

We could know or prove nothing about God from the world.

Mission 1

The via negativa

  • Via negativa ("the negative way"), also called the apophatic way (Greek apophasis, denial): speak of God only by saying what God is not.
  • God is not finite, not in time (eternal), not made of parts (simple), not changing (immutable), not a body.
  • Each denial removes an error and brings us closer to God's real otherness.
  • Pseudo-Dionysius (an unknown Christian writer, c. 500), The Mystical Theology: God is beyond every affirmation and every denial; the soul rises to union with God in a "darkness" beyond knowing (paraphrase).
  • Your spec: "the Via Negativa".
Mission 1Read the source

Into the thick darkness

> "The people stayed at a distance, and Moses came near to the thick darkness where God was." (Exodus 20:21, WEBBE)

> "dwelling in unapproachable light, whom no man has seen nor can see" (1 Timothy 6:16, WEBBE)

  • Pseudo-Dionysius and Gregory of Nyssa read Moses entering the darkness as the soul going beyond what it can know.
  • Aquinas himself begins his study of God this way (Summa Theologiae I, question 3, introduction): "we cannot know what God is, but rather what He is not".

Q1 Why is "darkness" a fitting image for the apophatic way?

Show answer

God is present but cannot be grasped or seen by the mind's ordinary light.

Q2 Does Aquinas reject the via negativa?

Show answer

No: he starts with it, but adds positive language by analogy.

Mission 1

Maimonides' ship

  • Moses Maimonides (1138–1204), Jewish philosopher, The Guide for the Perplexed (c. 1190), Part I, chapters 58–60.
  • Positive attributes risk dividing God into parts, or comparing God with creatures. So only negative attributes describe God truly.
  • His example (paraphrase, Guide I.60): someone knows a "ship" exists but not what it is. Told it is not an animal, not a plant, not solid all through, not flat, and so on, he comes close to a true idea of a ship by denials alone.
  • Even "God lives" means only: God is not lifeless.
  • The more you deny, the nearer you come; but you never grasp God's essence.
  • Aquinas' objection (ST I.13.2): when believers say "God lives" they mean more than "God is not lifeless".
Mission 1Diversity

Saying "not" across traditions

  • Orthodox Christianity: a strong apophatic tradition. Gregory Palamas (1296–1359): God's essence is unknowable; God is known through his energies (his acts and presence).
  • Judaism: Maimonides' negative attributes; later Kabbalah calls God in himself Ein Sof, "without end".
  • Islam: tanzih, God's being beyond comparison. "There is nothing whatever like unto Him, and He is the One that hears and sees (all things)." (Qur'an 42:11, Yusuf Ali) The same verse denies and affirms.
  • Hinduism: neti, neti, "not this, not this", describes Brahman (Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 2.3.6, paraphrase); Advaita Vedanta speaks of Brahman without qualities (nirguna).
  • Most traditions combine denial with positive names (the 99 names of God in Islam; "God is love" in 1 John 4:8).

Mission 1Evaluate

Does the via negativa work?

  • Claim: The via negativa is the best way to speak about God.
  • For: It respects God's transcendence: nothing finite is said of an infinite God.
  • For: It avoids anthropomorphism and idolatry, a danger of univocal language.
  • For: It fits mystics' reports that God is ineffable, beyond words, and the silence of much worship.
  • Against: Denials alone may say nothing: "not in time, not physical, not changing" is also true of the number seven.
  • Against: Maimonides' ship works only because we already know positive facts about ships; we have no such knowledge of God.
  • Against: Believers speak positively ("God is love"); worship and scripture need it (Aquinas, ST I.13.2).
  • Against: Some say a God described only by negation is hard to love, trust or pray to.
  • Judgement criteria: Does it say anything? Does it fit scripture and worship? Is it better on its own, or as a first step before analogy?
Mission check

?A hymn calls God "our faithful friend". Three listeners react. Rae: "God is a friend just as my best mate is, only bigger." Sam: "'Friend' about God has nothing in common with 'friend' about people." Tom: "We can only say God is not unfaithful and not distant." Which description is correct?

Same meaning, wholly different meaning, or only denial: each path has a cost. Next: Aquinas' middle path, analogy.

Mission 2

Analogy: the middle path

  • Recap (see Mission 1): univocal language makes God human-like; equivocal language tells us nothing.
  • Analogy: a word used of God and of creatures with a meaning that is related but not identical: partly the same, partly different.
  • Aquinas: analogy is "a mean between pure equivocation and simple univocation" (Summa Theologiae I.13.5).
  • Why it is possible: God is the cause of creatures, so creatures carry some likeness of God, however faint.
Mission 2Read the source

Aquinas on "healthy"

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274), Summa Theologiae I, question 13, article 5:

> "Therefore it must be said that these names are said of God and creatures in an analogous sense, i.e. according to proportion."

  • His example (paraphrase): "healthy" is said first of an animal. Medicine is called healthy because it causes health; urine is called healthy because it is a sign of health. One word, related meanings, all pointing to one thing.
  • He adds (I.13.6): goodness and wisdom are "applied primarily to God rather than to creatures", because they flow from God; but we learn the words from creatures first.

Q1 Why is "healthy" neither univocal nor equivocal here?

Show answer

The meanings differ but all relate to the health of the animal.

Q2 According to I.13.6, where does goodness exist most fully?

Show answer

In God; creatures have it by sharing in it.

Mission 2

Analogy of attribution

  • A quality is attributed to something because of its relation to the thing that has it properly, usually as cause or effect.
  • Aquinas' case: medicine is "healthy" because it causes health in the animal.
  • A common textbook case: we call bread "good", meaning the baker is skilled; the goodness of the bread comes from the baker.
  • Applied to God: God is the cause of all goodness and wisdom in the world, so the words can be used of God.
  • But Aquinas insists on more than "God causes good things": God is good, in a higher way (I.13.2, I.13.6).
Mission 2

Analogy of proper proportion

  • A quality belongs to each thing in proportion to its nature.
  • A good dog: loyal, obedient, healthy. A good person: honest, kind, wise. Same word, fitted to each kind of being.
  • So God's goodness is goodness in proportion to God's infinite nature: real goodness, but far beyond ours.
  • Like a scale: dog's goodness : dog's nature = human goodness : human nature = God's goodness : God's nature.
Quick check
?"A good knife cuts well; a good doctor heals; so God's goodness is goodness fitting God's own perfect nature." Which approach is this?
Mission 2

Cognitive or non-cognitive?

  • For Aquinas, "God is good" is true: it says something real about God, though we cannot fully grasp how. A cognitive, realist view.
  • Analogy gives real but limited knowledge: we know that God is good, not fully what God's goodness is like.
Mission 2Diversity

Who uses analogy today?

  • Catholic teaching: the Fourth Lateran Council (1215): between Creator and creature no likeness can be noted without a greater unlikeness (paraphrase). Erich Przywara (1889–1972), Analogia Entis (1932), defended the analogy of being.
  • Reformed Protestant: Karl Barth (1886–1968) called the analogy of being "the invention of Antichrist" (Church Dogmatics I/1, 1932, preface). For Barth, likeness to God is known only through God's revelation in Christ (analogy of faith).
  • Medieval critic: John Duns Scotus (c. 1266–1308): some words, such as "being", must be univocal, or we could not reason about God at all.
  • Modern philosophy: Richard Swinburne (b. 1934), The Coherence of Theism (1977), argues many words about God are used univocally, with care.
Mission 2Evaluate

Does analogy work?

  • Claim: Analogy lets believers speak meaningfully about God.
  • For: It avoids both errors: anthropomorphism (univocal) and emptiness (equivocal).
  • For: It fits how believers speak: "Father", "shepherd", "king" are meant as true, yet not literal.
  • For: It is grounded in creation: "the invisible things of him … are clearly seen, being perceived through the things that are made" (Romans 1:20, WEBBE), a verse Aquinas cites in I.13.5.
  • Against: It assumes God is the cause of creatures; a sceptic who rejects that has no reason to accept the analogy.
  • Against: Proportion with an unknown: if we do not know God's nature, we do not know what God's goodness amounts to.
  • Against: Suffering strains the analogy: what kind of goodness allows what we see?
  • Against: Attribution alone says only that God causes good, not that God is good, as Aquinas himself admits.
  • Judgement criteria: Does it tell us anything? Does it rest on contested premises? Does it fit worship and scripture?
Mission 2Worked answer

Comparing usefulness

Task: evaluate whether analogy is more useful than the via negativa.

  • Point: Analogy says more: "God is good" gives real, if limited, content; denials alone may say nothing.
  • Counter-point: The via negativa runs less risk: analogy may smuggle in human ideas of goodness.
  • Link to a scholar: Aquinas uses both: he begins with what God is not (ST I.3) and then speaks by analogy (ST I.13).
  • Judgement step: decide which criterion matters most (content, or safety from error), and argue from it.

Mission 3

Signs and symbols

  • A sign points to something by convention: a road sign, a red traffic light, "&". It can be swapped for another without loss.
  • A symbol also points beyond itself, but participates in what it points to.
  • Tillich's own example: a flag shares in the power and dignity of the nation. Insult the flag and people feel the nation is insulted.
  • Nobody feels a road authority is dishonoured when a sign is replaced.
Mission 3

Tillich: what symbols do

Paul Tillich (1886–1965), German-American Lutheran theologian, Dynamics of Faith (1957), chapter 3 "Symbols of Faith" (paraphrase):

  • Symbols point beyond themselves (like signs).
  • They participate in the reality they point to.
  • They open up levels of reality otherwise closed to us (as art does).
  • They unlock dimensions of the soul that answer to those levels.
  • They cannot be invented on purpose: they grow from a group's shared unconscious life.
  • They grow and die: when a situation changes, a symbol may lose its power.
Quick check
?On Tillich's account, which is a symbol rather than a sign?
Mission 3

Ultimate concern and being-itself

  • For Tillich, faith is "the state of being ultimately concerned" (Dynamics of Faith, ch. 1): concern for what matters unconditionally.
  • God is that which concerns us ultimately: not a being among beings, but being-itself, the ground of being (Systematic Theology, vol. 1, 1951).
  • So nearly everything said about God is symbolic: "Father", "King", "Lord", even "God is personal".
  • Tillich says that even the word "God" is a symbol for God.
Mission 3Read the source

My rock and my fortress

> "The LORD is my rock, my fortress, and my deliverer; my God, my rock, in whom I take refuge" (Psalm 18:2, WEBBE)

  • Read literally, the verse says God is a stone and a building. Nobody reads it that way.
  • For Tillich, "rock" and "fortress" are symbols: they open up the experience of safety in God and take part in it.

Q1 Which of Tillich's characteristics fits "fortress" best?

Show answer

It opens up a level of reality: God as the one in whom we are safe, which plain words cannot fully express.

Q2 How would a defender of analogy (see Mission 2) read "rock" differently?

Show answer

Aquinas would call "rock" a metaphor, since it implies a body (ST I.13.3); it rests on a literal truth that analogy can state: God is faithful, in a way proportioned to God's nature. Tillich says both are symbols.

Mission 3Diversity

Symbols across traditions

  • Christianity: the cross; bread and wine. Catholic and Orthodox Christians believe Christ is really present in the Eucharist, not only symbolised; many Protestants see a memorial and sign.
  • Judaism: the seven-branched menorah (the Temple lamp); the Shabbat candles.
  • Islam: calligraphy of God's name and Qur'an verses; most Muslims avoid images of God or prophets, and many do not treat the crescent as a religious symbol.
  • Hinduism: Om (Aum), a sacred sound symbol of Brahman.
  • Buddhism: the Dharma wheel: eight spokes for the Noble Eightfold Path.
  • Sikhism: the Khanda: double-edged sword, chakkar (circle) and two kirpans.
Mission 3Evaluate

Does symbol help us speak of God?

  • For: It explains how believers really use language: "rock", "light", "Father" are not literal, yet matter deeply.
  • For: Symbols move people and communicate across time; they avoid literalism and anthropomorphism.
  • For: Participation explains why symbols cannot simply be swapped, and why religious language is not mere decoration.
  • Against: If symbols are not literally true, what do they claim? Paul Edwards ("Professor Tillich's Confusions", Mind, 1965) argued they convey no checkable content.
  • Against: "Participates" is unclear: in what sense does a flag share in a nation's power?
  • Against: Symbols change and die: the svastika, an ancient sign of good fortune in Hindu, Buddhist and Jain traditions, took on a different meaning in Europe after the Nazis used it.
  • Against: Tillich's "being-itself" is not the personal God most believers worship.
  • Judgement criteria: content gained · fit with worship · clarity · whether believers mean more than symbol.
Mission 3

Three ways compared

  • Negation (via negativa): safest from error; may say nothing positive.
  • Analogy (Aquinas): gives real but limited content; rests on God as cause of the world.
  • Symbol (Tillich): fits the power of religious language in worship; its truth-content is unclear.
  • Use one criterion at a time: content, safety from error, fit with believers' practice, cognitive status.
  • Many theologians combine them: Aquinas used negation then analogy; Tillich allowed one non-symbolic statement.

Mission 4

Logical positivism

  • 1920s–30s: the Vienna Circle, a group of philosophers and scientists led by Moritz Schlick (1882–1936), with Rudolf Carnap and others.
  • Inspired by the success of science and by early Wittgenstein.
  • Their aim: a test for which statements are meaningful (tell us something about the world) and which are nonsense.
  • A. J. Ayer (1910–1989) attended the Circle's meetings in 1932–33 and brought its ideas to Britain in Language, Truth and Logic (1936).
Mission 4Read the source

Hume's fork

David Hume (1711–1776), An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding (1748), Section XII, Part III, on any book of theology or metaphysics:

> "Does it contain any abstract reasoning concerning quantity or number? No. Does it contain any experimental reasoning concerning matter of fact and existence? No. Commit it then to the flames: for it can contain nothing but sophistry and illusion."

  • The positivists took Hume's two prongs as the only two kinds of meaningful statement.

Q1 What two kinds of reasoning does Hume accept?

Show answer

Abstract reasoning about quantity and number, such as maths; and experimental reasoning about matters of fact.

Q2 Why would a positivist say a sermon on "God's love" fails Hume's test?

Show answer

It is not maths, and no experiment or observation could settle it.

Mission 4

Analytic and synthetic

  • Analytic statements are true (or false) by definition: "All bachelors are unmarried"; "2 + 2 = 4". Checking the meaning is enough. They tell us nothing new about the world.
  • Synthetic statements are true or false because of how the world is: "It is raining in Leeds"; "Water boils at 100 °C at sea level". You check them by experience.
  • Logical positivists: every meaningful statement is either analytic or empirically verifiable.
  • "God exists" is not true by definition (for them), and no observation could verify it.
Mission 4

Ayer's verification principle

  • Language, Truth and Logic (1936), ch. 1 (paraphrase): a statement is factually meaningful only if it is analytic, or if we know what observations would lead us to accept it as true or reject it as false.
  • Strong verification: the statement can be conclusively proved by experience. Ayer thought this too strict.
  • Weak verification (Ayer's choice): experience can make it probable.
  • In principle, not just in practice: Ayer's example, "there are mountains on the far side of the moon", was meaningful in 1936 because we knew what would verify it.
  • Result (ch. 6): talk about a transcendent God is literally meaningless: not false, but saying nothing. So "there is no God" is meaningless too; agnosticism, which treats the question as open, is also ruled out.
Quick check

Mission 4

Problems for the principle

  • Self-defeating: the principle is neither analytic nor verifiable by observation, so by its own test it is meaningless. (Ayer's later reply: it is a proposal about how to use "meaningful", not a fact.)
  • Universal laws: "All water boils at 100 °C at sea level" can never be conclusively verified: we cannot test every case. Strong verification rules out science itself.
  • History: statements about the past ("Julius Caesar crossed the Rubicon") cannot be observed now.
  • Too generous: Ayer's revised version (2nd edition, 1946: "direct" and "indirect" verification) was shown by critics to let in almost any statement.
  • Ayer later said, in a BBC interview with Bryan Magee (1978), that much of the book's view was mistaken (paraphrase).
Mission 4

Hick: eschatological verification

  • John Hick (1922–2012), Faith and Knowledge (1957); "Theology and Verification" (1960).
  • Parable of the Celestial City (paraphrase): two travellers walk one road. One believes it leads to the Celestial City; the other believes it leads nowhere. They meet the same good and bad things on the way. Only at the final corner will one of them be shown to be right.
  • Christian claims are verifiable in principle, after death (eschaton, "the end"): if they are true, believers will experience it.
  • Asymmetry: verifiable if true, but not falsifiable if false (no one would be there to find out).
  • So, even on Ayer's weak principle, "God exists" is meaningful: we know what experience would verify it.
Mission 4Read the source

Face to face

> "For now we see in a mirror, dimly, but then face to face. Now I know in part, but then I will know fully, even as I was also fully known." (1 Corinthians 13:12, WEBBE)

  • Paul expects knowledge of God that is partial now and full later.
  • Hick's travellers live in the "now": the road is ambiguous. The "then" is the final corner.

Q1 How does this verse fit Hick's argument?

Show answer

It expects a future experience in which what believers hope for will be known directly.

Q2 Why would Ayer not be satisfied?

Show answer

The verifying experience depends on life after death, which is itself unverifiable now and contested.

Quick check
?Hick says Christian belief is verifiable but not falsifiable. Why not falsifiable?
Mission 4Evaluate

Does verification make religious language meaningless?

  • Claim: The verification principle shows that religious language is meaningless.
  • For: It demands what science demands: a clear link between words and experience.
  • For: Much God-talk is compatible with any experience; it is hard to see what it claims.
  • Against: The principle fails its own test; strong verification also rules out universal laws and history.
  • Against: Hick: religious claims are verifiable in principle, after death.
  • Against: Much religious language is not meant as empirical report; a test for science may not fit worship.
  • Against: Some claims have empirical content: e.g. that Jesus lived and died under Pontius Pilate.
  • Judgement criteria: Is the principle itself justified? Does any version avoid its problems? Does it fit what believers mean?
Mission 4Worked answer

Building a judgement

Task: evaluate the claim that Hick's eschatological verification answers Ayer.

  • Point: It meets Ayer's weak, in-principle test: we know what experience would verify the claim.
  • Counter-point: It assumes personal survival after death, which is just as unverifiable now.
  • Response: Hick says the test asks only what would verify a claim, not whether it can be done today, as with mountains on the moon in 1936.
  • Judgement: Weigh which matters more: the logic of "in principle", or the fact that the verifying experience depends on another disputed claim.
  • AO2 tip: end each paragraph with a mini-judgement that links back to the question.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Words learned from finite things struggle to describe an infinite, timeless God; cognitive language makes factual claims, non-cognitive language expresses, praises or guides.
  • Univocal language risks anthropomorphism; equivocal language risks emptiness; Aquinas rejects both (ST I.13.5).
  • The via negativa (Pseudo-Dionysius, Maimonides) speaks of God only by denial: it protects transcendence but may say nothing positive.

Mission 2

  • Analogy is a middle path between univocal and equivocal language (Aquinas, ST I.13.5): words used of God are related but not identical.
  • Attribution names God through cause and effect; proper proportion fits each quality to each nature; both give real but limited knowledge.

Mission 3

  • A sign points by convention; a symbol points and participates (Tillich, Dynamics of Faith, 1957): it opens up reality, cannot be invented and can die.
  • For Tillich, God is being-itself and our ultimate concern; almost all talk of God is symbolic.

Mission 4

  • Logical positivists (Vienna Circle; Ayer, Language, Truth and Logic, 1936): a statement is meaningful only if analytic or empirically verifiable; God-talk, atheism and ethics are meaningless.
  • Criticisms: the principle fails its own test and excludes universal laws and history; Hick replies that religious claims are verifiable (not falsifiable) after death, so meaningful.
