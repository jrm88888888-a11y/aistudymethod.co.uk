Mission 1

Mission 1: How does a plant send sugar up to its buds and down to its roots at the same time?

Make a guess: How does a plant send sugar up to its buds and down to its roots at the same time?

Mission 1

Sources and sinks

  • Phloem carries organic substances, mainly sucrose and amino acids: translocation.
  • Source: where sugars enter the phloem. A photosynthesising leaf; a storage root in spring.
  • Sink: where sugars leave the phloem to be used or stored. Roots, meristems, fruits, growing storage organs.
  • Sap flows from source to sink: up or down.
  • Different sieve tubes can carry sap in opposite directions at the same time.
  • At the source, companion cells use ATP to load sucrose into the sieve tubes.
  • Say: "from source to sink" — not: "always down from the leaves".
Mission 1

Sieve tubes and companion cells

  • Sieve tube elements: living cells, end to end, forming sieve tubes.
  • End walls are sieve plates, full of pores: sap flows from cell to cell.
  • No nucleus, few organelles, thin layer of cytoplasm: little blocks the flow.
  • Companion cells: nucleus, dense cytoplasm, many mitochondria: they make the ATP for loading.
  • Many plasmodesmata link each companion cell to its sieve tube element.
  • Walls of cellulose, not lignified.
  • Say: "sieve tube elements are living cells without a nucleus" — not: "dead like xylem".

Make a guess: Why can a shortage of one simple charged particle stop a cell, or a whole plant, from working properly?

Mission 2

Ions: small, charged, specific

  • An ion is an atom, or a group of atoms, with an electric charge.
  • Cation: positive, e.g. Na⁺. Anion: negative, e.g. Cl⁻.
  • Inorganic ions dissolve in water: cytoplasm, blood, tissue fluid, plant sap.
  • Each type of ion has a specific role, set by its properties: its charge, its size, what it binds to.
  • One ion cannot stand in for another.
Mission 2

What plants need from the soil

  • Roots absorb water and ions from the soil through root hairs.
  • Nitrate (NO3⁻): nitrogen for amino acids (→ proteins), nucleotides (→ DNA, RNA, ATP) and chlorophyll.
  • Magnesium (Mg²⁺): at the centre of every chlorophyll molecule.
  • Calcium (Ca²⁺): forms calcium pectate in the middle lamella, holding cell walls together.
  • Water: solvent and transport medium for these ions; keeps cells turgid; a reactant in photosynthesis.
  • Lack nitrate: stunted growth; older leaves turn yellow (chlorosis).
  • Lack magnesium: yellowing between the veins, older leaves first.
  • Lack calcium: growing tips and young leaves are weak and die back.
  • Say: "ions are raw materials" — not: "plant food" (they supply no energy).
Mission 2Practical

Mineral deficiency

  • Core practical 7: investigate plant mineral deficiencies.
  • Grow equal starting numbers of duckweed (Lemna) plants, or seedlings, in culture solutions:
  • complete (the control) · lacking nitrate · lacking magnesium
  • … and lacking calcium.
  • Keep volume, light, temperature and starting size the same. Cover the solutions to stop algae.
  • Over 2–3 weeks: count fronds (or measure mass, height, leaf number). Record leaf colour.
  • Calculate: mean per solution; each mean as a percentage of the control mean.

Mission 3

Mission 3: A family remedy helped some patients and poisoned others. How do we now test whether a medicine works?

Make a guess: A family remedy helped some patients and poisoned others. How do we now test whether a medicine works?

Mission 3

Withering and the foxglove

  • 1775: doctor William Withering is asked about a Shropshire family remedy for dropsy (fluid build-up: swollen legs and abdomen, breathlessness).
  • The remedy, a herbal brew or "digitalis soup", had twenty or more herbs.
  • He reasoned the active herb was foxglove (Digitalis purpurea).
  • He gave dried foxglove leaf to patients and kept case notes: 163 cases, published in 1785.
  • Doses that were too high caused nausea, vomiting and a very slow pulse.
  • So he started low and increased the dose slowly, stopping at the first sign of these effects.
  • Today we know foxglove compounds make a failing heart beat more strongly and more slowly.
Mission 3

What Withering could not show

  • Strengths: he identified one active plant, standardised the preparation (dried, powdered leaf) and found a safe dose range.
  • But every patient got foxglove. There was no comparison group of similar patients without it.
  • Some patients recover anyway. Without a comparison, you cannot tell how many improved because of the drug.
  • He chose the patients and judged the results himself: risk of bias.
  • Leaf strength varied with plant and season, so the dose of active compound varied.
  • Modern protocols fix each of these weaknesses.
Quick check
?Withering recorded 163 patients treated with foxglove. Which is the main reason his records could not show, by modern standards, that foxglove was an effective treatment?
Mission 3

Before any human: preclinical testing

  • A new drug is first tested without people.
  • Cells and tissues in the lab: does it act on its target? Is it toxic to cells?
  • Computer models: predict how the molecule will behave.
  • Animals: is it toxic in a whole body? How is it absorbed, broken down and excreted?
  • Most candidate drugs fail here and never reach a human.
  • Only then can a drug enter clinical trials on people.
Mission 3

Three-phased testing

  • Phase 1: a small group (about 20–100) of healthy volunteers.
    • Is it safe? What are the side effects? How does the body handle it? What dose is tolerated?
  • Phase 2: a few hundred patients with the condition.
    • Does it work? Which dose works best? Side effects?
  • Phase 3: hundreds to thousands of patients, often in many hospitals.
    • Compared with a placebo or the existing best treatment.
    • Confirms it works; finds rarer side effects.
  • Pass all three, and a regulator may license the drug.
  • Each phase starts only if the last one was safe.
Quick check
?A new antidepressant has passed preclinical tests. Which group receives it in phase 1 of its clinical trials?
Mission 3

The placebo

  • Placebo: a dummy treatment with no active drug, made to look, taste and be taken exactly like the real one.
  • Patients often improve on a placebo: the placebo effect. Expecting to get better can change symptoms such as pain.
  • Some also improve because illness varies naturally.
  • Placebo group improvement = improvement not caused by the drug.
  • Drug effect = drug group result minus placebo group result.
  • Ethics: if an effective treatment already exists, giving a placebo would deny it. The new drug is compared with the existing treatment instead.
  • All patients give informed consent: they know they may receive a placebo.
Mission 3

Randomised and double-blind

  • Randomised: a random process, not a doctor, decides who gets drug or placebo.
    • Groups are then similar in age, sex and severity: a fair comparison.
  • Single-blind: patients do not know which they receive.
  • Double-blind: neither the patients nor the doctors and researchers assessing them know.
    • Removes bias in how patients report symptoms and how researchers judge results.
    • Each patient has a code; the code is broken only when the trial ends.
  • Say: "double-blind: neither patients nor researchers know who receives the drug" — not: "the patients are not told".
Quick check
?A trial of a new pain-relief drug is described as double-blind. Who knows, during the trial, which patients are receiving the drug and which the placebo?

Mission check

Mission 4

Mission 4: How do you grow bacteria on purpose without growing anything else, or infecting yourself?

Make a guess: How do you grow bacteria on purpose without growing anything else, or infecting yourself?

Mission 4

What bacteria need

  • Nutrients: a carbon source (e.g. glucose) for respiration and for building molecules.
  • A nitrogen source (e.g. amino acids, nitrate) for proteins and nucleic acids; plus minerals.
  • Water: for reactions and transport into the cell.
  • A suitable temperature: growth depends on enzymes, each with an optimum.
  • A suitable pH: most bacteria grow best near neutral.
  • Oxygen: obligate aerobes need it; facultative anaerobes grow with or without it; obligate anaerobes are harmed by it.
Mission 4

Agar or broth

  • Nutrient broth: liquid medium. Growth shows as cloudiness (turbidity).
  • Nutrient agar: broth set with agar, a seaweed polysaccharide most bacteria cannot digest.
  • Agar melts near 85 °C and sets below about 40 °C: poured hot, solid at incubation temperature.
  • On agar, one cell (or a small clump) grows into a visible colony.
  • Spread culture evenly over the agar: colonies merge into a lawn.
Mission 4

Aseptic technique

  • Two aims: keep unwanted microbes out of the culture; keep the culture away from people.
  • Before: wash hands; disinfect the bench; sterilise media and glassware in an autoclave (121 °C, 15 min).
  • During: work beside a lit Bunsen burner: its updraught carries airborne microbes up and away.
  • Flame the inoculating loop until red hot; cool it before touching the culture.
  • Flame the neck of each bottle as you open and close it. Lift the dish lid only slightly, at an angle.
  • After: secure the lid with two short strips of tape, not all round: air can still enter, which discourages anaerobic bacteria (including many pathogens).
  • Label the base. Incubate upside down (condensation cannot drip onto the agar) at 25 °C. School labs avoid 30–42 °C: temperatures near body heat (37 °C) favour human pathogens.
  • Never open a plate after incubation. Autoclave cultures before disposal.
Mission 4

Serial dilution

  • Transfer 1.0 cm³ into 9.0 cm³ of sterile water; mix. Total 10 cm³: a 1 in 10 dilution (10⁻¹).
  • Repeat from the new tube: 10⁻², 10⁻³, 10⁻⁴ …
  • Dilution factor = total volume ÷ volume transferred = 10 ÷ 1 = 10.
  • After k tenfold steps, concentration = original × 10⁻ᵏ. Factors multiply; they never add.
  • A fresh sterile pipette for each transfer: no carry-over between tubes.
  • Uses here: a range of antimicrobial concentrations.
Quick check
?A student adds 1.0 cm³ of a bacterial culture to 9.0 cm³ of sterile water and mixes. She transfers 1.0 cm³ of this into a second 9.0 cm³ of sterile water, and repeats until she has made four dilutions. What fraction of the original concentration of bacteria is in the fourth dilution?
Mission 4Practical

Testing antimicrobials

  • Core practical 9: antimicrobial properties of plants. Core practical 15: effect of different antibiotics on bacteria.
  • Spread a known volume of broth culture over agar with a sterile spreader: a lawn.
  • Add sterile paper discs soaked in each antibiotic or plant extract, or fill wells cut with a sterile cork borer.
  • Extract in ethanol? Let the solvent evaporate first: ethanol itself kills bacteria.
  • Control: a disc or well with solvent only.
  • Incubate. Measure the clear zone (zone of inhibition) through the base: two diameters at right angles, take the mean.
Quick check
?A student soaks paper discs in garlic, ginger and mint extracts, all made in ethanol, and places them on a lawn of bacteria. Another disc is soaked in ethanol only. Which of these is a controlled variable in her investigation?
Mission 4

Worked example: clear area around a well

  • Clear zone diameter 21 mm; well diameter 6 mm (illustrative data).
  • Step 1: radii. Zone 21 ÷ 2 = 10.5 mm. Well 6 ÷ 2 = 3.0 mm.
  • Step 2: zone area = π × 10.5² = 346 mm².
  • Step 3: well area = π × 3.0² = 28.3 mm².
  • Step 4: clear agar = 346 − 28.3 = 318 mm².
  • Subtract areas, never diameters. Halve before squaring.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Bacteria can go from a handful to billions overnight. How do you count them, and how do you graph that?

Make a guess: Bacteria can go from a handful to billions overnight. How do you count them, and how do you graph that?

Mission 5

Growth on a log scale

  • To count them, take a tiny sample: dilute the culture in tenfold steps, count the cells or colonies in a small volume, then scale up by the dilution factor.
  • Microbe numbers span many orders of magnitude: 10² to 10⁹ per cm³.
  • On an ordinary axis, the early points squash flat against zero.
  • On a log10 scale, each equal step is × 10. log10(10⁶) = 6.
  • Closed culture phases: lag, log (exponential), stationary, death (decline).
  • Exponential growth plots as a straight line on a log scale.
  • Say: "exponential: multiplied by the same factor each interval" — not: "the same number added each interval".
Mission 5

Worked example: doublings and logs

  • N = N0 × 2ⁿ. N0 = starting number; n = number of generations.
  • N0 = 2.0 × 10³ cells per cm³; one generation every 25 min; 5 h of log phase.
  • Step 1: n = 300 min ÷ 25 min = 12.
  • Step 2: 2¹² = 4096 (calculator: 2 xʸ 12).
  • Step 3: N = 2.0 × 10³ × 4096 = 8.2 × 10⁶ cells per cm³.
  • On a log10 axis: 3.30 → 6.91. Each generation adds log102 = 0.301; 12 × 0.301 = 3.61. ✓
Quick check
?A student records the size of a duckweed population every two days and plots it on a logarithmic vertical axis against time on a non-logarithmic horizontal axis. Which observation would show that growth was exponential?
Mission check

?A bacterial culture is in its log phase. Plotted on a log10 scale, its count rises in a straight line from 10³ to 10⁶ cells per cm³ over 3 hours. Approximately how long does one generation take?

Huge numbers become manageable twice over: count a tiny sample and scale it up, then plot the logs so that doublings line up.

Mission 6

Mission 6: How can people keep taking food and materials from nature without wrecking it?

Make a guess: How can people keep taking food and materials from nature without wrecking it?

Mission 6

Take less than grows back

  • Sustainable: meets today's needs without reducing what future generations can take.
  • Harvesting is sustainable only if the rate of harvesting is below the rate of replacement.
  • Net replacement = growth + new young (recruitment) − natural deaths.
  • Renewable does not mean it cannot run out.
Quick check
?A stock of Atlantic cod has a biomass of 200 000 tonnes. Each year, growth of the fish and recruitment of young fish add 15% to the biomass, while natural mortality removes 5% of it. What is the annual rate of replacement of the stock, below which the catch must stay for harvesting to be sustainable?
Mission 6

Timber and forest products

  • Coppicing: cut a tree near the ground; many new stems regrow; cut again years later.
  • Rotational felling: fell one area at a time; replant; return only when it has regrown.
  • Selective felling: take only some mature trees; the forest structure and habitats remain.
  • Large-scale clear-felling exposes soil to erosion and leaching and destroys habitats.
Mission 6

Using the carbon cycle

  • Reforestation: growing trees take in CO2 by photosynthesis and store carbon in wood and soil.
  • Biofuels (e.g. bioethanol, biodiesel): burning releases CO2 that the crop recently took from the air. Fossil fuels release carbon locked away for millions of years.
  • But growing, fertilising, processing and transporting biofuel crops release CO2 too, and the crops can take land from food or forest.
  • Plant fibres (e.g. hemp, flax) and starch-based plastics replace oil-based materials; they are renewable and many are biodegradable.
Quick check
?A power station switches from burning coal to burning wood pellets from forests that are replanted as they are harvested. Which statement about the carbon dioxide released is correct?
Mission check

?Illustrative data.
A seaweed bed with a biomass of 5000 tonnes is harvested for food. Each year, growth adds 30% to the biomass and natural losses remove 10%. The current harvest is 1400 tonnes per year. Which conclusion is correct?

Fish, timber or seaweed: take no more than the net amount that grows back, and nature can keep supplying.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Phloem sieve tubes (living, no nucleus, sieve plates) and companion cells (many mitochondria) translocate sucrose from sources to sinks, up or down.

Mission 2

  • Each inorganic ion has a specific role that no other ion can fill.
  • Plants need nitrate for proteins, nucleic acids and chlorophyll, and magnesium for chlorophyll.
  • Calcium ions form calcium pectate between plant cell walls.

Mission 3

  • Withering (1785) found foxglove as the active herb in a "digitalis soup" for dropsy and worked out a safe dose, but had no comparison group.
  • Modern drugs pass preclinical tests, then phase 1 (healthy volunteers: safety), phase 2 (patients: effect and dose) and phase 3 (many patients: randomised, double-blind, against a placebo or the existing treatment).

Mission 4

  • Bacteria need nutrients, water, a suitable temperature and pH; aseptic technique keeps contaminants out and the culture contained.
  • Serial dilution multiplies the dilution factor at each step: four tenfold steps give 10⁻⁴.
  • Antimicrobial tests compare clear zones against a solvent-only control; clear area = zone area − well (or disc) area.

Mission 5

  • On a log10 scale, exponential growth is a straight line; each generation adds 0.301 (N = N0 × 2ⁿ).

Mission 6

  • Harvesting is sustainable only if it stays below net replacement (growth + recruitment − natural deaths).
