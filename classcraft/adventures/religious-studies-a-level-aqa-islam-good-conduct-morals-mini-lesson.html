Mission 1

Mission 1: Islam has five verdicts for any action, not two. Why would a religion need a "disliked" category?

Make a guess: Islam has five verdicts for any action, not two. Why would a religion need a "disliked" category?

Mission 1

Why five categories?

  • Muslims believe the shari'a covers all of life: worship, family, trade, food, manners.
  • As fiqh developed, jurists needed a clear way to say how strongly God wants or forbids an act.
  • Fiqh: scholars' understanding of God's law. The schools of law are the Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i and Hanbali (Sunni) and the Ja'fari (Twelver Shi'a).
  • The answer: al-ahkam al-khamsa, the five rulings, a scale from "must" to "must not".
  • The scale guides intention and reward, not only law courts.
Mission 1

The scale of five

  • Fard (also wajib): obligatory. Doing it is rewarded; leaving it is a sin.
  • Mustahab (also mandub, sunna): recommended. Doing it is rewarded; leaving it is no sin.
  • Mubah: permitted and neutral. No reward, no sin.
  • Makruh: disliked. Avoiding it is rewarded; doing it is no sin, but it is discouraged.
  • Haram: forbidden. Doing it is a sin; avoiding it for God's sake is rewarded.
  • Halal means "permitted": in a broad sense everything not haram; Eduqas uses halal for the middle category.
Mission 1Read the source

Do not invent the haram

> "O ye who believe! make not unlawful the good things which Allah hath made lawful for you, but commit no excess" (Qur'an 5:87, Yusuf Ali)

> "But say not - for any false thing that your tongues may put forth,- 'This is lawful, and this is forbidden,' so as to ascribe false things to Allah." (Qur'an 16:116, Yusuf Ali)

  • Jurists drew a principle: in everyday matters, the default is permission; a ban needs evidence.

Q1 What two errors does 5:87 warn against?

Show answer

Forbidding what God allowed; and excess.

Q2 Why might 16:116 make jurists slow to call something haram?

Show answer

Declaring a ban without proof is attributing a lie to God.

Quick check
?In the five categories, what is the difference between a mustahab act and a mubah act?
Mission 1

Fard and mustahab: examples

  • Fard: the five daily salah; fasting in Ramadan; zakah; Hajj if able; honesty; caring for parents.
  • Fard 'ayn: on each person (salah). Fard kifaya: on the community; if some do it, the rest are free (funeral prayer, training doctors).
  • Mustahab: extra (sunna) prayers; fasting on Mondays and Thursdays; sadaqah; saying bismillah before eating; using the miswak (tooth-stick).
  • Greeting with salam is mustahab; replying to it is obligatory.
  • These categories show a Muslim where the floor is and how to go above it.
Mission 1

Mubah, makruh, haram: examples

  • Mubah: most daily choices: which halal food, which job, what colour to wear.
  • Makruh: wasting water in wudu; eating raw garlic or onion before going to the mosque (Bukhari; paraphrase); many classical jurists put smoking here.
  • Divorce is lawful but discouraged: reported as the most hated of permitted things to God (Abu Dawud; paraphrase; chain debated).
  • Haram: alcohol and gambling (5:90); pork, blood and carrion (2:173); theft; riba; lying; murder.
Mission 1Diversity

Same scale, different placements

  • Hanafi jurists split the top: fard (proved by certain evidence) and wajib (by less certain evidence), e.g. the witr prayer is wajib for Hanafis, strongly recommended for other schools.
  • Hanafis also split makruh tahrimi (close to haram) and makruh tanzihi (mildly disliked).
  • Twelver Shi'a jurists use the same five: wajib, mustahabb, mubah, makruh, haram.
  • The same act may sit in different places: e.g. prawns are halal for most schools, makruh or forbidden for many Hanafis.
  • Each believer follows their school or, for Twelver Shi'a, a senior scholar (marja') (taqlid: following a qualified scholar or school).
Quick check
?Which pairing of an action with its category do most Sunni jurists accept?
Mission 1

Freedom and judgement

  • The mubah zone is vast: most of life is a matter of free, responsible choice.
  • Necessity (darura) can lift a ban: "if one is forced by necessity, without wilful disobedience, nor transgressing due limits,- then is he guiltless" (Qur'an 2:173, Yusuf Ali).
  • Intention (niyya) can turn a mubah act into worship: eating to have strength to serve God.
  • Makruh and mustahab call for judgement, not just obedience.
  • AQA: the role of personal freedom and judgement in the Shari'ah, through the five-fold classification.
  • Some Muslims stress obedience: the categories are God's, not ours; others stress conscience within them.
Mission 1Evaluate

Clarity or confusion?

  • Claim: The five categories are, in practice, a recipe for confusion rather than clarity.
  • For: schools place the same act differently (witr, prawns, smoking).
  • For: the middle categories need scholarly advice; ordinary believers may not know where a new act belongs.
  • Against: a two-way halal/haram split would be cruder; five levels can capture degrees of moral weight.
  • Against: the core is shared: salah fard, alcohol haram; disagreement sits at the edges.
  • Judge it by: Is diversity confusion or mercy? Do the categories work for new questions (IVF, crypto-currency)? Do they guide inner life as well as outward acts?
Mission 1Worked answer

Linking category, source, life

Task: Explain how the five categories guide Muslim life.

  • Point: They show how strongly God wills an act. Source: 5:87 and 16:116 forbid inventing bans. Development: so most of life is mubah, a space of freedom.
  • Point: They join law to reward. Example: sunna prayers are mustahab: leaving them is no sin, doing them is rewarded. Development: believers aim above the minimum.
  • Diversity: Hanafi fard/wajib; same scale in Shi'a fiqh.
  • AO1 tip: give an example for each category you name, and one source.
Mission check

?Yusuf oversleeps and has time only for the two fard rak'ahs of Fajr, so he misses the sunna prayer before them. How do most jurists judge this?

The five categories let Muslims tell a duty from a recommendation, and a sin from a dislike, so they can aim higher without fear of losing everything.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why do Muslims call the fight against your own selfishness the "greater" jihad?

Make a guess: Why do Muslims call the fight against your own selfishness the "greater" jihad?

Mission 2

Jihad means striving

  • Jihad: from j-h-d, "to strive, to make an effort". Not the word for "holy war".
  • "And those who strive in Our (cause),- We will certainly guide them to our Paths" (Qur'an 29:69, Yusuf Ali).
  • Forms of jihad: of the self (nafs); of the tongue and pen (speaking truth, teaching); of wealth (giving); and armed struggle.
  • "Strive in His cause as ye ought to strive" (Qur'an 22:78, Yusuf Ali).
  • Tradition: greater jihad (al-jihad al-akbar): struggle with the self; lesser jihad (al-jihad al-asghar): armed struggle.
  • AQA recognises Jihad as a technical term.
Mission 2

Greater jihad: the nafs

  • Nafs: the self, the ego with its desires.
  • Three states named from the Qur'an: the soul inciting to evil (12:53); the self-reproaching soul, conscience (75:2); the soul at peace (89:27).
  • Greater jihad: lifelong effort to move the self towards peace with God: prayer, fasting, honesty, controlling anger and greed.
  • The Prophet is reported to have said that the true striver (mujahid) is the one who strives against his own self for God (Tirmidhi; paraphrase).
  • A popular report calls returning from battle a return "from the lesser jihad to the greater" (al-Bayhaqi; paraphrase; many scholars grade it weak).
Mission 2Read the source

Purifying the self

> "Truly he succeeds that purifies it, And he fails that corrupts it!" (Qur'an 91:9–10, Yusuf Ali)

> "And for such as had entertained the fear of standing before their Lord's (tribunal) and had restrained (their) soul from lower desires" (Qur'an 79:40, Yusuf Ali)

Q1 What decides success in 91:9–10?

Show answer

Whether a person purifies or corrupts the soul.

Q2 What motive for self-control does 79:40 give?

Show answer

Awareness of standing before God at Judgement.

  • AQA: greater jihad as part of good conduct and obedience to God.

Mission 2

Lesser jihad in the Prophet's time

  • Makkah (610–622): persecution, but no fighting; patience and preaching.
  • 622: the hijrah to Madinah. Permission to fight comes because the Muslims "are wronged" (22:39).
  • Battles: Badr (624), Uhud (625), the Trench (627).
  • 628: Treaty of Hudaybiyyah: a peace accepted on hard terms.
  • 630: Makkah taken almost without fighting; a general amnesty.
  • Jurists made lesser jihad a communal duty of the ummah (fard kifaya), led by legitimate authority; it becomes an individual duty if the land is invaded.
Mission 2Read the source

Close reading

> "To those against whom war is made, permission is given (to fight), because they are wronged" (Qur'an 22:39, Yusuf Ali)

> "Fight in the cause of Allah those who fight you, but do not transgress limits; for Allah loveth not transgressors." (Qur'an 2:190, Yusuf Ali)

> "But if the enemy incline towards peace, do thou (also) incline towards peace, and trust in Allah" (Qur'an 8:61, Yusuf Ali)

  • Verses often quoted alone, e.g. 9:5 ("slay the Pagans wherever ye find them"), are read by most scholars in context: Arab polytheists who broke treaties (9:4 exempts those who kept them; 9:13 names those who "took the aggressive"); 9:6 orders asylum for any who ask.

Q1 In 22:39, what is the reason fighting is permitted?

Show answer

The believers have been wronged and attacked.

Q2 Which two limits do 2:190 and 8:61 set?

Show answer

No transgression; accept peace when the enemy offers it.

Mission 2

Conditions of lesser jihad

  • Just cause: defence, or ending oppression of the weak (4:75), never forcing faith: "no compulsion in religion" (2:256).
  • Legitimate authority: declared by a recognised leader, not by individuals.
  • Right intention: for God and justice, not gain or revenge.
  • Last resort and proportion: "do not transgress limits" (2:190).
  • Protect non-combatants: the Prophet forbade killing women and children (Bukhari, Muslim; paraphrase); Abu Bakr told his commanders not to kill the old or children, cut fruit trees or destroy buildings (Malik, Muwatta; paraphrase).
  • Stop when the enemy seeks peace (8:61); treat prisoners humanely.
Quick check
?A militia leader with no state behind him declares a "jihad" and attacks a market full of civilians. Which two classical conditions does this most clearly break?
Mission 2

Modern warfare and WMD

  • Nuclear, chemical and biological weapons cannot tell soldiers from civilians: they break non-combatant protection.
  • They destroy crops, animals and land for generations: compare the condemnation of one who would "destroy crops and cattle" (2:205).
  • So most scholars hold their use haram; some allow possession only as a deterrent.
  • Iran's Supreme Leader is reported to have issued a fatwa forbidding nuclear weapons; Pakistan holds them for deterrence.
  • Other problems today: who is the "legitimate authority" when Muslims live in many nation-states? Drones and remote war? Wars between Muslim states?
  • AQA: debates about lesser jihad today, including WMD.
Mission 2

Terrorism is not jihad

  • Terrorism: violence against civilians to spread fear for political goals.
  • Mainstream scholars across Sunni and Shi'a Islam reject calling it jihad: no legitimate authority, civilians targeted, suicide forbidden (4:29).
  • "If any one slew a person - unless it be for murder or for spreading mischief in the land - it would be as if he slew the whole people" (Qur'an 5:32, Yusuf Ali).
  • Examples: the Amman Message (2004–05); Tahir-ul-Qadri's Fatwa on Terrorism and Suicide Bombings (2010); scholars' open letter to the leader of ISIS (2014).
  • Groups that use the word for terror are a small minority, condemned by most Muslim bodies; many of their victims are Muslims.
Quick check
?Why do most Muslim scholars hold that using a nuclear weapon could not be a lawful lesser jihad?
Mission 2Evaluate

Is lesser jihad still relevant?

  • Claim: The teachings on lesser jihad have no relevance today.
  • For: modern weapons make the conditions almost impossible to meet.
  • For: the misuse of the word by terrorists harms Muslims; some argue the inner jihad should be stressed instead.
  • Against: the conditions are a just-war ethic that can limit modern war: protect civilians, accept peace, use proportion.
  • Against: Muslims still face invasion and oppression; the right of defence (22:39) still matters.
  • Judge it by: Are the principles separable from the 7th-century setting? Who has authority now? Does stressing the greater jihad answer the misuse?
Mission check

Jihad is striving in God's way. The greater jihad, with the self, never ends; the lesser, armed jihad is a limited, conditional duty.

Mission 3

Mission 3: If life belongs to God, can a Muslim ever end a pregnancy, use IVF or switch off a ventilator?

Make a guess: If life belongs to God, can a Muslim ever end a pregnancy, use IVF or switch off a ventilator?

Mission 3

Life as God's gift and trust

  • Sanctity of life: for Muslims, life is sacred because God gives it and God alone decides its term.
  • "Nor can a soul die except by Allah's leave, the term being fixed as by writing" (Qur'an 3:145, Yusuf Ali).
  • Humans are khalifah (stewards, 2:30): life is held in trust (amanah), not owned.
  • Life is a test: God "created Death and Life, that He may try which of you is best in deed" (Qur'an 67:2, Yusuf Ali).
  • So suffering may have meaning, and patience (sabr) is a virtue (2:155–156).
  • At the end of life, most Muslim scholars hold active euthanasia and assisted suicide haram; many Muslim bodies accept brain death (e.g. the Islamic Fiqh Academy, 1986), so life support may then be switched off.
Mission 3Read the source

As if he slew the whole people

> "if any one slew a person - unless it be for murder or for spreading mischief in the land - it would be as if he slew the whole people: and if any one saved a life, it would be as if he saved the life of the whole people." (Qur'an 5:32, Yusuf Ali)

> "take not life, which Allah hath made sacred, except by way of justice and law" (Qur'an 6:151, Yusuf Ali)

Q1 What value does 5:32 give to a single life?

Show answer

The value of all humanity.

Q2 What exception does 6:151 allow?

Show answer

Taking life "by way of justice and law": e.g. a court's lawful sentence.

  • So in Islam, sanctity of life is very strong but not stated without exception.
Mission 3

When does a person begin?

  • The Qur'an describes stages in the womb: drop, clinging clot, lump of flesh, bones clothed with flesh, "then we developed out of it another creature" (23:12–14).
  • Hadith (Ibn Mas'ud): each stage lasts forty days; then an angel breathes in the soul (ruh) (Bukhari, Muslim; paraphrase).
  • Most jurists: ensoulment at about 120 days (four months).
  • Others, from another hadith (Muslim), place a key change at about 40 days.
  • Before ensoulment the embryo is not yet a full person, but it is life with sanctity, to be protected.
  • After ensoulment, harming it is like harming a living person.
Quick check
?Why do many Muslim jurists treat abortion at 16 weeks differently from abortion at 20 weeks?
Mission 3

Abortion: what is agreed

  • "Kill not your children for fear of want: We shall provide sustenance for them as well as for you" (Qur'an 17:31, Yusuf Ali). Many apply this to abortion for economic reasons.
  • After ensoulment (c. 120 days): all schools forbid abortion.
  • Exception widely accepted by modern scholars: when the mother's life is in real danger: she is an established life with duties to others, and the greater harm is avoided.
  • Abortion is never just a personal choice: the life is God's, and the father and family have a stake.
Mission 3Diversity

Before 120 days

  • Hanafi: many permit with a serious reason (e.g. health of mother or child) before 120 days; some call it makruh.
  • Maliki: most forbid from conception.
  • Shafi'i: views differ; some permit before 40 or 80 days; al-Ghazali (1058–1111) forbade it from conception, the wrong growing graver at each stage.
  • Hanbali: many permit in the first 40 days.
  • Twelver Shi'a: generally forbidden from conception, except to save the mother's life; Iran's law (2005) allows abortion before four months for severe fetal abnormality.
  • Modern rulings: the Islamic Fiqh Council in Makkah (1990) allowed abortion before 120 days for grave, confirmed fetal abnormality.
Mission 3

The embryo in the laboratory

  • IVF often makes spare embryos. May they be used for research (e.g. stem cells)?
  • Many Sunni bodies (e.g. the Islamic Fiqh Council in Makkah, 2003) and many Shi'a jurists in Iran: yes, for surplus embryos, donated with consent, before implantation, for healing.
  • Reasons: the embryo outside the womb is not ensouled and cannot develop alone; research serves maslaha (public good) and saving life (5:32).
  • Against: creating embryos for research, cloning humans, and selling embryos are widely forbidden.
  • Some scholars reject all destructive embryo research: the embryo is potential human life from conception.
  • AQA: the sanctity of life applied to the embryo and the unborn child.
Mission 3

Assisted conception: Sunni and Shi'a

  • Children are a blessing; seeking a cure for infertility is encouraged. "He leaves barren whom He will" (42:50) is read as a test, not a ban on treatment.
  • Sunni consensus (e.g. al-Azhar, 1980): IVF is permitted for a married couple, using their own egg and sperm, during the marriage.
  • Forbidden for Sunnis: donor sperm, donor eggs, donor embryos and surrogacy: they mix lineage (nasab), which the shari'a protects, and resemble adultery.
  • Twelver Shi'a: some maraji', including Ayatollah Khamenei, allow donor eggs and, with conditions, donor sperm; other maraji' are more restrictive.
  • So Iran offers treatments that most Sunni countries ban.
Quick check
?A married Sunni couple cannot conceive because the wife has no viable eggs. A clinic offers IVF with eggs from an anonymous donor. What would most Sunni scholars say?
Mission 3Evaluate

Is the sanctity of life absolute in Islam?

  • Claim: In Islam, the sanctity of life is absolute.
  • For: life and death belong to God (3:145, 67:2); killing one is like killing all (5:32).
  • For: euthanasia, assisted suicide and abortion after ensoulment (unless the mother's life is at risk) are forbidden by all schools.
  • Against: the Qur'an itself allows life to be taken "by way of justice and law" (6:151): qisas, just war.
  • Against: many permit early abortion for serious reasons, abortion to save the mother, research on spare embryos and switching off life support after brain death.
  • Judge it by: Is sanctity the same as "never end life"? Does a gradual view of the embryo weaken or refine it? Who decides: jurist, doctor or family?
  • AQA: different views about the application of sanctity of life to the embryo and the unborn child.
Mission check

?After successful IVF, a married Sunni couple in Belfast have three spare frozen embryos. Which option would many Sunni scholarly bodies accept?

Muslims hold life sacred as God's trust; jurists draw careful lines: ensoulment, lineage, futility of treatment, to protect it.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Fard, mustahab, mubah (halal), makruh, haram: a scale of how strongly God wills an act, linked to reward and sin.
  • Most of life is permitted; bans need evidence (5:87, 16:116); necessity and intention matter.
  • Schools differ at the edges (Hanafi fard/wajib; prawns; smoking); Shi'a fiqh uses the same five.

Mission 2

  • Jihad = striving: greater (against the nafs) and lesser (armed, under conditions).
  • Lesser jihad in the Prophet's time was a response to persecution and attack, limited by the Qur'an (22:39, 2:190, 8:61) and the Prophet's rules.
  • Most scholars hold WMD and terrorism break the conditions; whether lesser jihad is relevant today is debated.

Mission 3

  • Life is God's gift and trust (3:145, 5:32, 67:2); only "justice and law" may take it (6:151).
  • Ensoulment at about 120 days (hadith); abortion after it forbidden; before it, schools differ; the mother's life is a widely accepted exception.
  • IVF within marriage is permitted for Sunnis; donor gametes and surrogacy forbidden (lineage); some Shi'a maraji' allow donors; many allow research on spare embryos.
