Mission 1

Mission 1: Can a country buy lower unemployment by accepting a little more inflation?

Make a guess: Can a country buy lower unemployment by accepting a little more inflation?

Mission 1

The original Phillips curve

  • In 1958, the economist A. W. Phillips plotted UK data from 1861 to 1957.
  • When unemployment was low, money wages rose fast. When unemployment was high, wages rose slowly.
  • Faster wage growth feeds into faster price rises, so the idea became a link between inflation and unemployment.
  • The curve slopes down: lower unemployment goes with higher inflation.
  • It is now called the short-run Phillips curve (SRPC).
  • Axes: inflation rate (%) up the side, unemployment rate (%) along the bottom.
Mission 1

Why a trade-off? Back to AD/AS

  • Step 1: AD rises. The economy moves up along SRAS.
  • Real output rises, so firms hire: unemployment falls.
  • The price level rises faster: inflation rises.
  • Step 2: the same change is a movement up along the SRPC.
  • A fall in AD does the reverse: down along SRAS, down along the SRPC.
  • Say: "a movement along the SRPC" — not: "a shift of the SRPC", when AD changes.

Mission 1

Real world: the trade-off holds, then breaks

  • 1950s–60s: UK data fitted the curve well. Many thought governments could choose a point on it.
  • 1970s: UK inflation averaged about 24% in 1975 (RPI), while unemployment also rose.
  • High inflation and high unemployment: stagflation.
  • Points no longer sat on one curve: the SRPC had shifted outwards.
  • Two reasons: oil-price supply shocks, and workers and firms coming to expect high inflation.
Mission 1

Expectations shift the short-run curve

  • Milton Friedman (1968) and Edmund Phelps (1967): the trade-off is only temporary.
  • Adaptive expectations: people expect inflation to be what it was recently.
  • (1) Point A: unemployment at its natural rate, inflation 2%, expected inflation 2%.
  • (2) AD rises. Workers' money wages rise, but prices rise faster than they expected. Real wages fall, firms hire: move along SRPC1 to B (lower unemployment, higher inflation).
  • (3) Workers now expect the higher inflation and bargain for higher money wages. Real wages recover, firms stop the extra hiring: unemployment returns to the natural rate at C. SRPC1 → SRPC2: each SRPC is drawn for one expected inflation rate.
  • Result: same unemployment, higher inflation.
Mission 1

The long-run Phillips curve

  • Join points A and C: the long-run Phillips curve (LRPC) is vertical at the natural rate of unemployment.
  • In the long run there is no trade-off: pushing unemployment below the natural rate only raises inflation.
  • The natural rate of unemployment: unemployment when the labour market is in equilibrium. It is frictional plus structural unemployment.
  • NAIRU: the non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment. Below it, inflation keeps rising. It is often treated as the same as the natural rate.
  • Vertical LRPC = vertical LRAS: the classical (neo-classical) view.
Mission 1

Worked example: chasing low unemployment

  • Illustrative data. Natural rate = 5%. Expected inflation starts at 2%.
  • Short-run rule: inflation = expected inflation + (5 − unemployment rate).
  • Year 1: government holds unemployment at 3%. Inflation = 2 + 2 = 4%.
  • Year 2: people now expect 4%. Inflation = 4 + 2 = 6%.
  • Year 3: expect 6%. Inflation = 6 + 2 = 8%.
  • Each year the SRPC shifts up. Holding unemployment below the NAIRU makes inflation accelerate.
  • Stop pushing: unemployment returns to 5% with inflation stuck at 8%.
  • Bringing inflation back down needs unemployment above 5% for a time: at 7%, inflation = 8 − 2 = 6%.

Mission 1

Along the curve, or a shift?

  • Change in AD (demand shock): movement along the SRPC.
  • Supply shock (e.g. a sharp rise in oil or energy prices): SRAS shifts left, so the SRPC shifts right (up). Higher inflation at every unemployment rate.
  • A favourable supply shock (cheaper energy, faster productivity growth) shifts the SRPC left (down).
  • A change in expected inflation shifts the SRPC.
  • A change in the natural rate shifts the LRPC. Better skills matching, job search help or more flexible labour markets move it left. Long spells of unemployment that erode skills (hysteresis) can move it right.
Quick check
?A country invests heavily in solar and wind power. Over several years, firms' energy costs fall sharply. Other things equal, what happens on a Phillips curve diagram?
Mission 1Evaluate

How useful is the Phillips curve?

It depends on:

  • Time horizon: a short-run trade-off may exist.
  • In the long run, if expectations adapt, it disappears.
  • Supply shocks: they shift the SRPC, so past data may not predict the future.
  • Measurement: the NAIRU cannot be observed. It is estimated, and it changes over time.
  • Credible inflation targets: if people trust the central bank, expectations stay near target. The curve can look flat.
  • UK evidence: in 2019 unemployment was 3.8%, yet CPI inflation was only 1.8%.
  • Global factors (cheap imports, energy prices) also drive inflation, not just domestic unemployment.
Mission check

?A sudden rise in world energy prices hits an economy. Over a year, inflation rises from 2% to 6% and unemployment rises from 4% to 5%. Which best describes this on a Phillips curve diagram?

A lower-unemployment, higher-inflation deal exists only along one short-run curve. Supply shocks can shift that curve.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why are some people out of work even when the economy is booming?

Make a guess: Why are some people out of work even when the economy is booming?

Mission 2

Between jobs

  • Frictional unemployment: people between jobs, searching for a suitable one.
  • Examples: a school leaver's first search, someone who quit to find better work, a worker who moved town.
  • Cause: imperfect information. Matching workers to vacancies takes time.
  • Usually short-term. Some is unavoidable, even useful: a good match raises productivity.
  • Seasonal unemployment: jobs that disappear at certain times of year (farm harvests, beach resorts, ski resorts, winter construction).
  • Seasonal unemployment is predictable and recurs every year.
Mission 2

The wrong skills, the wrong place

  • Structural unemployment: a mismatch between the skills or locations of the jobless and the jobs on offer.
  • Cause: the pattern of demand and production changes: industries decline, new ones grow.
  • Occupational immobility: workers lack the skills for new jobs (a laid-off steelworker cannot become a nurse overnight).
  • Geographical immobility: jobs are elsewhere, but moving is costly (house prices, family ties).
  • Technological unemployment: machines or software replace a skill, a type of structural unemployment.
  • Often long-term, and concentrated in particular regions.
Mission 2

Not enough demand

  • Cyclical unemployment: caused by too little aggregate demand, in a downturn or recession.
  • Also called demand-deficient unemployment.
  • AD falls → firms sell less → they need fewer workers (labour is a derived demand).
  • Real national output falls below full-employment output: a negative output gap.
  • It affects many industries and regions at once.
  • Real case: UK unemployment rose from about 5% in early 2008 to 8.4% in late 2011 (ONS, Labour Force Survey).
Quick check
?A car factory installs robots to do its welding. The welders it lays off have no skills that other employers in the area want. Which type of unemployment is this?
Mission 2

Wages stuck above equilibrium

  • If wages are held above the market-clearing level, more people want to work than firms want to hire.
  • The excess supply of labour is unemployment.
  • This is real-wage unemployment (also called classical unemployment).
  • Causes: a minimum wage set above equilibrium; trade union bargaining; wages that do not fall when demand for labour falls.
  • Debate: whether minimum wages cost jobs in practice depends on the level set and the market. Studies disagree; the evidence is in an earlier lesson.
  • Say: "an excess supply of labour at the minimum wage" — not: "a shortage of workers".

Mission 2

The natural rate

  • Even at full-employment output, some people are unemployed. Full employment does not mean zero unemployment.
  • Natural rate of unemployment: the unemployment rate when output is at its full-employment level.
  • It is the unemployment that remains when the labour market is in equilibrium: frictional, structural and seasonal.
  • So: cyclical unemployment = actual rate − natural rate.
  • The natural rate changes slowly: with skills, mobility, benefit rules, demographics and technology.
  • NAIRU: the unemployment rate at which inflation does not accelerate. It is often treated as the natural rate (Phillips curve in Mission 1).
Mission 2

Worked example: splitting the rate

  • Illustrative data. Actual unemployment rate 7%. Frictional 1.5%, structural 3.5%.
  • Seasonal 0.5%.
  • Natural rate = 1.5 + 3.5 + 0.5 = 5.5%.
  • Cyclical = 7 − 5.5 = 1.5 percentage points.
  • Cyclical unemployment above zero means output is below full employment.
  • Say: "1.5 percentage points above the natural rate" — not: "1.5% above".
Quick check
?Illustrative data: a country's unemployment rate is 6.5%. Its natural rate is estimated at 4.5%. Which statement is correct?
Mission 2

Lasting damage, and matching the cure

  • Match the policy to the cause:
TypeMain policies
CyclicalDemand-side: lower interest rates, more government spending, tax cuts
FrictionalBetter job information and matching (job centres, online vacancy services)
StructuralSupply-side: training, help to move, more housing where the jobs are
Real-wageDebated: labour market reforms; effects on pay and fairness disputed
Mission 2

The costs of unemployment

  • Personal: lost income; skills fade; stress and worse mental and physical health.
  • Social: links with crime and family strain; whole communities decline when a big employer closes; inequality rises.
  • Economic:
    • lost output that can never be recovered: the economy is inside its PPC
    • lower tax revenue and higher benefit spending, so a larger budget deficit
    • long spells reduce skills, so potential output may fall too
  • Say: "benefits are a transfer" — not: "benefits are lost output". The lost output is what the unemployed could have produced.
Quick check
?A deep recession keeps unemployment high for three years. Which effect could reduce the economy's potential output, not just its actual output?
Mission 2Evaluate

How serious is a rise in unemployment?

It depends on:

  • The type and length: short frictional spells can improve job matches; long spells cut skills and can raise the natural rate.
  • Who and where: losses concentrated in one region or group cause deeper social costs than the same rise spread thinly.
  • Support and policy response: benefits, retraining and quick action on demand reduce the lasting damage.
  • What else is happening: rising unemployment with falling inflation leaves room for demand-side policy; with high inflation, the choice is harder.
Mission check

?Four people are unemployed in the same country. Aggregate demand then rises, and output returns to its full-employment level. Whose unemployment is most likely to end because of the rise in demand?

Extra demand cures cyclical unemployment. Frictional and structural unemployment remain even at full employment: they are part of the natural rate.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why can't a government get fast growth, plenty of jobs and steady prices all at once?

Make a guess: Why can't a government get fast growth, plenty of jobs and steady prices all at once?

Mission 3

The big four objectives

ObjectiveWhat "success" looks likeWhy it matters
Economic growthSteady, sustainable rise in real GDPHigher living standards; more tax revenue
Low unemploymentFew people who want work are without itLost output; lost income; poverty and poor health
Price stabilityLow and stable inflationUncertainty and lost spending power when inflation is high or erratic
Balance of paymentsA sustainable current account positionA large deficit relies on borrowing from, or selling assets to, the rest of the world
  • UK: the Bank of England's inflation target is 2% (CPI), set by the government.
  • Say: "low and stable inflation" — not: "falling prices". Deflation is not the goal.
Mission 3

More objectives

  • Balanced budget: government borrowing and debt at levels that can be sustained.
  • Environment / sustainability: growth that does not run down natural resources or harm future generations.
  • Greater equality: a fairer distribution of income and wealth (redistribution).
  • Objectives can support each other: growth creates jobs and raises tax revenue.
  • But they can also conflict: a trade-off means getting more of one costs some of another.
Quick check
?Illustrative data for one economy this year: real GDP growth 2.1%, unemployment 4.0%, inflation 6.5% (target 2%), current account deficit 1% of GDP. Which objective is furthest from being met?
Mission 3

Conflict: low unemployment vs low inflation

  • (1) AD1 → AD2 in a slump: real output rises a lot, the price level a little. Unemployment falls.
  • (2) AD2 → AD3 near full capacity: real output rises a little, the price level a lot.
  • Near capacity, firms compete for scarce workers: wages and prices rise.
  • So pushing unemployment ever lower tends to raise inflation. Cooling inflation tends to raise unemployment.
  • This is the short-run trade-off shown by the Phillips curve (see Mission 1).
Mission 3

Conflict: growth vs inflation and the current account

  • Demand-led growth faster than potential output opens a positive output gap: inflation rises.
  • Supply-led growth (LRAS shifts right) raises output without adding to inflation.
  • Faster growth raises incomes, and part of that income is spent on imports: the current account can worsen.
  • Higher inflation than in trading partners makes exports less competitive: another drag on the current account.
  • Raising interest rates to curb inflation may strengthen the currency, making exports dearer.
  • Budget vs growth: cutting a deficit by cutting spending or raising taxes reduces AD in the short run. Supporters say it lowers future borrowing costs and builds confidence; critics say it slows growth. How much depends on the size of the multiplier.

Mission 3

Conflict: growth vs the environment

  • More output usually means more energy and raw materials, more emissions and more waste.
  • These are negative externalities: the costs fall on third parties and future generations.
  • But the link can weaken: UK territorial greenhouse gas emissions fell by about 50% between 1990 and 2022, while real GDP grew.
  • Caution: part of that fall came from making less at home and importing more. Emissions measured by what the UK consumes fell by less.
  • Green technology, carbon taxes and regulation aim to decouple growth from emissions. How far that can go is debated.
Mission 3

Conflict: growth vs equity

  • Growth may reward some groups more: owners of capital, highly skilled workers, booming regions.
  • So inequality can widen even as average incomes rise.
  • Redistribution (progressive taxes, benefits) can narrow the gap.
  • One view: high taxes and generous benefits reduce incentives to work and invest, so equity costs some efficiency (Okun, 1975).
  • Another view: IMF research (Ostry, Berg and Tsangarides, 2014) found lower inequality was linked to faster and more durable growth, and redistribution was generally not harmful to growth except in extreme cases.
  • Which effect dominates depends on the policy design and the country.
Mission 3

Can conflicts be reconciled?

  • Supply-side policies (education, training, infrastructure, competition) shift LRAS right.
  • Then growth and falling unemployment come with less inflation.
  • But supply-side policies take years to work.
  • Policy mix: use different tools for different goals, e.g. interest rates for inflation, supply-side policies for growth.
  • Prioritise: tackle the most pressing problem first, accepting a cost elsewhere for a time.
Quick check
?A government wants faster growth without higher inflation. Which policy is most likely to achieve both over time?
Mission 3Evaluate

Which objective should come first?

It depends on:

  • Where the economy is: a deep slump makes jobs the priority; a positive output gap makes inflation the priority.
  • The cause: a demand shock and a supply shock call for different responses.
  • Time horizon: short-run trade-offs may ease in the long run if capacity grows.
  • Value judgements: how much inflation is worth a lower jobless rate, or how much growth is worth more emissions, is normative. Economists can show the trade-off; voters and governments choose.
Mission check

?An economy is already close to full capacity. The government cuts income tax to give growth a boost. Which outcome is most likely in the short run?

Pushing demand when capacity is nearly used up trades one objective (growth) against another (low inflation).

Mission 4

Mission 4: If a country's output is worth more money this year, has it really produced more?

Make a guess: If a country's output is worth more money this year, has it really produced more?

Mission 4

What GDP counts

  • Gross domestic product (GDP): the total value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a period (usually a year or a quarter).
  • Final goods: bought by the end user. Intermediate goods (steel for a car, flour for a baker) are not added separately: their value is already in the final price.
  • Within a country: a foreign-owned factory's output counts where the factory is.
  • Not counted:
    • second-hand sales (the car was counted when it was new)
    • transfer payments (pensions, unemployment benefits): money moves, nothing is produced
    • buying shares or bonds: a change of ownership, not production
  • Say: "final goods and services" — not: "everything that is sold".
Quick check
?Which of these is counted in a country's GDP for this year?
Mission 4

Nominal and real

  • Nominal GDP: output valued at current prices. It rises if output rises or if prices rise.
  • Real GDP: output valued at constant prices. Only changes in the quantity of goods and services move it.
  • Real GDP strips out inflation, so it is the measure of economic growth.
  • Rough rule: real growth ≈ nominal growth − inflation (exact method next).
  • Say: "real GDP" when you mean output — not: "GDP went up, so output went up".
Mission 4

Worked example: the GDP deflator

  • The GDP deflator is a price index for everything in GDP. Base year = 100.
  • Real GDP = nominal GDP × 100 ÷ deflator.
  • Illustrative data (billions):
Nominal GDPDeflatorReal GDP (Year 1 prices)
Year 1800100800
Year 2880105838.1
  • Year 2 real GDP = 880 × 100 ÷ 105 = 838.1.
  • Nominal growth = (880 − 800) ÷ 800 × 100 = 10%.
  • Real growth = (838.1 − 800) ÷ 800 × 100 = 4.76%: about half the headline rise was higher prices.

Mission 4

Per person, per worker, per year

  • Rate of economic growth = (real GDP this year − real GDP last year) ÷ real GDP last year × 100.
  • Always use real figures, and divide by the earlier year.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A country's real GDP was 500 billion last year and 520 billion this year. What is its rate of economic growth?
Mission check

?Illustrative data. A country's nominal GDP rose from 2,000 billion to 2,120 billion. Over the same year, its GDP deflator rose from 100 to 108. What happened to real GDP?

A bigger money figure is not more output. Remove the rise in prices first.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Can an economy keep growing just by people spending more?

Make a guess: Can an economy keep growing just by people spending more?

Mission 5

What counts as growth

  • Economic growth: an increase in real GDP (real output) over time.
  • Rate of growth = % change in real GDP from one year to the next.
  • Nominal GDP is measured at current prices, so it rises with inflation. Real GDP removes price changes.
  • Total real GDP shows the size of the economy. Real GDP per capita (per person) is closer to average living standards.
  • Say: "real GDP rose by 2%" — not: "GDP rose by 2%", when you mean output.
Mission 5

Worked example: growth rates

  • Illustrative data.
Year 1Year 2
Nominal GDP500 bn540 bn
Real GDP (year 1 prices)500 bn515 bn
Population50 m51 m
  • Real growth = (515 − 500) / 500 × 100 = 3.0%.
  • Nominal growth = (540 − 500) / 500 × 100 = 8.0%. Most of that is higher prices.
  • Real GDP per capita: 500 bn / 50 m = 10,000 → 515 bn / 51 m = 10,098.
  • Growth per capita = (10,098 − 10,000) / 10,000 × 100 = 0.98%.
  • Population grew too, so output per person grew much less than total output.
  • Small rates compound. Real GDP per capita doubles in about 35 years at 2% a year, about 10 years at 7%, about 8 years at 9%.
  • After 35 years, 2% a year makes output per person about 2 times as big; 7% a year makes it about 10.7 times as big.
Quick check
?Illustrative data: a country's real GDP rises from 800 bn to 820 bn in a year. Its population rises from 40 million to 41 million. What is the growth rate of real GDP per capita?
Mission 5

Actual and potential growth on a PPC

  • Actual growth: a rise in the output the economy actually produces (real GDP).
  • Potential growth: a rise in the economy's productive capacity: the most it could produce with all resources fully and efficiently used.
  • On a PPC:
    • point A inside the curve: unemployed resources (spare capacity)
    • A → B, onto the curve: actual growth, using spare capacity
    • PPC1 → PPC2, shifting outwards: potential growth
  • Once the economy is on its PPC, output can only keep rising if the PPC shifts out.
  • Say: "potential growth: productive capacity increases" — not: "the economy produces more".
Mission 5

Short-run growth: AD rises

  • Start with spare capacity: real output Y1 is below full-employment output Yf.
  • AD rises (more consumption, investment, government spending or net exports): AD1 → AD2.
  • Real output rises Y1 → Y2; firms use idle workers and machines. The price level rises a little.
  • This is short-run (actual) growth: the economy moves towards its capacity. LRAS has not moved.
  • Close to Yf, more AD raises the price level more and real output less.
Mission 5

Long-run growth: LRAS shifts right

  • Long-run growth: productive capacity rises, so LRAS shifts right: LRAS1 → LRAS2.
  • Full-employment output rises: Yf1 → Yf2. This is the same event as the PPC shifting out.
  • With AD unchanged, real output rises and the price level falls.
  • On a Keynesian AS curve, the vertical section moves right: the economy's maximum output rises.
  • Long-run growth is what lets output keep rising year after year without rising inflation.

Mission 5

Causes of growth

  • Short run (actual growth): a rise in AD, when there is spare capacity: C, I, G or (X − M).
  • Long run (potential growth): more or better factors of production.
    • Quantity: a bigger labour force (migration, more people in work); more capital (investment).
    • Quality: education and training (human capital); new technology.
    • Institutions: property rights, rule of law, stable government; good infrastructure.
  • Investment does both: it raises AD now and capacity later.
Mission 5

Benefits and costs of growth

BenefitsCosts
Higher real incomes and living standardsPollution, emissions, depletion of resources
More jobs; less cyclical unemploymentGains can be unequal: income distribution may widen
More tax revenue for health, education, welfareInflation, if AD grows faster than capacity
Firms' profits fund more investmentMore imports: current account can worsen
Less absolute povertyInvestment now means less consumption now
  • Real case: since 1978, China's GDP growth averaged over 9% a year, lifting almost 800 million people out of extreme poverty (World Bank).
Mission 5

Sustainable and inclusive growth

  • Sustainable growth: growth that meets today's needs without reducing future generations' ability to meet theirs (idea from the Brundtland Report, 1987).
  • It needs growth that does not use up natural resources faster than they renew or degrade the environment for the future.
  • Say: "sustainable growth" — not: "slow growth". It is about whether growth can continue, not how fast it is.
Quick check
?Illustrative data: over ten years a country's real GDP grows by 6% a year. Over the same years the real incomes of its poorest 40% of households hardly change. Which description fits this best?
Mission 5Evaluate

Is economic growth always good?

It depends on:

  • The source: growth from productivity and investment can last; growth from AD alone with no spare capacity mainly raises prices.
  • Who gains: if most gains go to a few, average figures overstate how much most people's living standards rise.
  • The environment: growth with high emissions can lower future living standards; cleaner technology and carbon pricing can reduce the conflict.
  • The level of development: an extra 2% matters more for health and poverty in a low-income country than in a rich one.
Mission check

?An economy is at full capacity: unemployment is at its natural rate and firms are working at full capacity. The government wants output to keep rising over the next ten years. Which policy is most likely to achieve this?

Spending can only use the capacity an economy has. Lasting growth needs that capacity to grow.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The short-run Phillips curve slopes down: a rise in AD lowers unemployment and raises inflation.
  • Supply shocks shift the SRPC; changes in AD move the economy along it.
  • With adaptive expectations, the SRPC shifts up whenever unemployment is held below the natural rate. The LRPC is vertical at the natural rate (NAIRU).

Mission 2

  • Frictional: between jobs. Structural: skills or location mismatch. Cyclical: too little aggregate demand.
  • Seasonal: jobs that vanish at certain times of year.
  • Real-wage: wages held above equilibrium.
  • Costs are personal, social and economic; lost output is never recovered.

Mission 3

  • Main objectives: growth, low unemployment, low and stable inflation, a sustainable current account; also budget, environment and equality.
  • Near full capacity, pushing unemployment down tends to push inflation up.
  • Demand-led growth risks inflation; growth may harm the environment and widen inequality.
  • Supply-side policies can ease conflicts, but slowly. Choosing priorities involves value judgements.

Mission 4

  • GDP: the value of final goods and services produced within a country in a period.
  • Real GDP = nominal GDP × 100 ÷ deflator. Only real GDP measures growth in output.
  • Growth rate = change in real GDP ÷ earlier year × 100; per capita = ÷ population.

Mission 5

  • Economic growth is an increase in real GDP.
  • Actual growth uses spare capacity (AD); potential growth raises capacity (PPC shifts out, LRAS shifts right).
  • Long-run growth comes from more or better factors: labour, capital, human capital, technology, institutions.
  • Growth raises living standards but can harm the environment, widen inequality and add to inflation.
