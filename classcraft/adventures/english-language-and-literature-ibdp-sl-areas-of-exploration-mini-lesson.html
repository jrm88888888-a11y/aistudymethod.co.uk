Mission 1

Does a text mean what the writer meant, or what the reader finds?

Make a guess: Does a text mean what the writer meant, or what the reader finds?

IB areas of exploration · readers, writers and textsScreen 1

One text, three corners

  • Writer: makes choices of words, form and structure, in a context.
  • Text: the choices themselves, on the page. This is your evidence.
  • Reader: brings experience, values and context, and interprets.
  • This area asks how meaning is constructed by a writer and interpreted by readers, and how texts affect us.
  • The same triangle works for an advert or a speech: creator, text, audience.
IB areas of exploration · readers, writers and textsScreen 2

The writer's intention: evidence, not a verdict

  • You rarely know what a writer meant. Even a writer's own comment is another text to read.
  • Frankenstein's 1818 preface says the hero's views are "by no means to be conceived as existing always in my own conviction". In 1831 Mary Shelley wrote that, as far as she could recall, her husband had written that preface.
  • W. K. Wimsatt and Monroe Beardsley (1946) argued that intention outside the text shouldn't decide its meaning; Roland Barthes (1967) turned attention to the reader.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Does a text mean what the writer meant, or what the reader finds?

Neither alone. A writer makes choices; readers interpret them, from their own context. The strongest IB answers argue from the text's choices (Chopin's narration lets us hear…) and show how they shape a reader's response, rather than guessing what the writer meant.

Mission 2

Would this work mean the same if written somewhere else, or now?

Make a guess: Would this work mean the same if written somewhere else, or now?

IB areas of exploration · time and spaceScreen 1

Two contexts

  • Context of production: when, where, by whom and for whom a text was made.
  • Context of reception: when, where and by whom it is read, and what those readers bring.
  • Space means place and culture, not just date: a work read far from where it was written meets new expectations.
  • The area asks how important context is to a text's making and reading, and how its meaning and impact change over time.

Pride and Prejudice (Jane Austen, 1813)

IB areas of exploration · time and spaceScreen 3

Context that does work

  • An entail kept an estate in the male line. Mr Bennet's passes to a male cousin, Mr Collins, so his five daughters will lose their home when he dies.
  • For women like them, marriage was the main route to security.
  • That context sharpens the opening: the "truth" is less about what rich men want than about what families with daughters need.
  • Context helps explain a choice; it isn't a history lesson added on.

IB areas of exploration · time and spaceScreen 5

Read from another time and place

  • A reader in 2026, in Lagos or Toronto, may not know what an entail is, and may read the marriage plot as romance, as social satire, or both.
  • Adaptations move the story: Gurinder Chadha's film Bride and Prejudice (2004) sets it partly in Amritsar.
  • The words stay the same; their impact changes. Your argument should hold both contexts.
  • Non-literary texts date fast: an invented 1950s-style advert promising a wife "a happier husband" now reads very differently.

How your exam asks this

  • No exam question names the area, but every component rewards context that serves analysis.
  • Your literary works span at least 2 periods and 2 places on 2 continents (SL).
  • Paper 2 (25 marks) asks you to compare two works; different contexts often explain why they treat an issue differently.
  • Individual Oral: one global issue across texts from different times or places.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Would this work mean the same if written somewhere else, or now?

Probably not. A work's meaning is shaped by when and where it was made (the entail behind Austen's opening "truth") and by when and where it is read. The words stay the same; their impact changes, and strong IB answers use context to explain how the text's choices work.

Mission 3

Every story borrows from an older one. Is that theft or conversation?

Make a guess: Every story borrows from an older one. Is that theft or conversation?

IB areas of exploration · intertextualityScreen 1

Ways texts talk to each other

  • Allusion and quotation: a text names or echoes another.
  • Epigraph: a borrowed line placed before a text, framing how we read it.
  • Rewriting and parody: a new text retells or mocks an old one.
  • Conventions: every text follows or breaks the habits of its form or genre.
  • The area asks how texts follow and break conventions and how comparing them can change our reading.
  • Across text types too: an advert can echo a famous painting; a speech, an older speech.

IB areas of exploration · intertextualityScreen 4

Following and breaking conventions

  • Renaissance love sonnets, after Petrarch, praised a beloved with fixed comparisons: eyes like the sun, lips like coral, cheeks like roses.
  • Shakespeare's Sonnet 130 uses that set of comparisons to refuse it, one by one.
  • Its final couplet turns the joke into a claim about true love.
  • Intertextuality here is a conversation with a whole tradition, not one source.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Every story borrows from an older one. Is that theft or conversation?

Conversation. Writers borrow to answer: Shelley gives her creature Adam's question to God, and Shakespeare quotes the love-poem tradition in order to refuse it. In an IB essay, the connection earns credit when you show what the new text does with the old.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Writer, text and reader: you analyse the text's choices and how they shape a reader's response.
  • A writer's intention is rarely knowable; treat a writer's comments as another text, not a final answer.
  • Paper 1 tests this on unseen non-literary texts.
  • Context of production (when, where, for whom it was made) and context of reception (who reads it, when and where).
  • Context earns its place when it explains a choice in the text, like the entail behind Austen's ironic opening.
  • Your works span at least 2 periods and 2 places at SL, 3 and 3 at HL.
  • Intertextuality covers allusion, quotation, epigraphs, rewriting, parody and the conventions of a form.
  • A connection earns credit when you say what the borrowing changes in how we read.
  • Paper 2 rewards comparison.
  • The IO links a literary work and a body of work.

Key quotations to learn

  • Free! Body and soul free! ("The Story of an Hour") Her private joy, which only the reader hears.
  • joy that kills ("The Story of an Hour", last line) The doctors' reading; the irony is completed by the reader.
  • I bid my hideous progeny go forth and prosper (Frankenstein, 1831 introduction) A writer sending her text out to readers.
  • by no means to be conceived as existing always in my own conviction (Frankenstein, 1818 preface) The author is not the hero: don't confuse a character's views with the writer's.
  • It is a truth universally acknowledged (Pride and Prejudice, Ch. 1) The ironic "truth": certain in tone, local in fact.
  • must be in want of a wife (Pride and Prejudice, Ch. 1) Whose want? The families', as the context shows.
  • unfortunately for his daughters, was entailed (Pride and Prejudice, Ch. 7) The legal context behind the marriage plot.
  • Did I request thee, Maker, from my clay / To mould me man? (Frankenstein (1818), epigraph; Paradise Lost X) Adam's protest becomes the creature's question.
  • Like Adam, I was created apparently united by no link to any other being (Frankenstein, Vol. II Ch. VII) The creature reads himself through Milton.
  • Satan as the fitter emblem of my condition (Frankenstein, Vol. II Ch. VII) He turns the comparison: wronged, and dangerous.
  • My mistress’ eyes are nothing like the sun (Sonnet 130, line 1) A convention refused in the first line.
  • As any she belied with false compare (Sonnet 130, line 14) Names the tradition being answered.
