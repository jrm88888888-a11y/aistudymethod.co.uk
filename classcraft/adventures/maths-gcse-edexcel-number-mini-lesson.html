Number · place value and the order of operations

Place value and ordering numbers

  • Each column is worth 10 times the one to its right. ×10× 10, ×100× 100, ×1000× 1000 move digits 1, 2, 3 places left: 3.7×100=3703.7 × 100 = 370, 52÷1000=0.05252 ÷ 1000 = 0.052.
  • Compare decimals column by column from the left. On a number line, further right is bigger: −7<−2−7 < −2.
  • Symbols: ==, =≠ (not equal to), <<, >>, ≤≤ (less than or equal to), ≥≥ (greater than or equal to).

Number · place value and the order of operations

Negative numbers and reciprocals

  • Adding a negative is the same as subtracting: −6+(−2)=−8−6 + (−2) = −8. Subtracting a negative is the same as adding: 5−(−3)=85 − (−3) = 8.
  • Multiplying or dividing: same signs give a positive, different signs give a negative. (−4)×(−5)=20(−4) × (−5) = 20, −12÷3=−4−12 ÷ 3 = −4.
  • The reciprocal of a number is 1 divided by it: the reciprocal of 4 is 41​1/4, the reciprocal of 32​2/3 is 23​3/2.
  • A number times its reciprocal is 1. Zero has no reciprocal.

Number · place value and the order of operations

The order of operations

  • Order: Brackets, Indices (powers, roots), Division and Multiplication, Addition and Subtraction.
  • ×× and ÷÷ have equal priority: work them left to right. So do ++ and −−.
  • A number next to a bracket means multiply: 3(4+1)=3×53(4 + 1) = 3 × 5. A fraction line and a square root sign group like brackets.
  • Inverse operations undo each other (++ and −−, ×× and ÷÷, squaring and square-rooting). Check 223−98=125223 − 98 = 125 with 125+98=223125 + 98 = 223.

Number · factors, multiples and primes

Factors, multiples and primes

  • A factor (divisor) of a number divides into it exactly. Factors come in pairs: 36=1×36=2×18=3×12=4×9=6×636 = 1 × 36 = 2 × 18 = 3 × 12 = 4 × 9 = 6 × 6.
  • A multiple of a number is in its times table: the multiples of 12 are 12, 24, 36, 48, …
  • A prime number has exactly two factors, 1 and itself. 1 is not prime (it has only one factor), and 2 is the only even prime.
  • Common factors are shared factors; common multiples appear in both times tables.

Number · factors, multiples and primes

Prime factor decomposition

  • Every whole number above 1 can be written as a product of prime factors in exactly one way (the unique factorisation theorem).
  • Use a factor tree or repeated division by primes, and stop when every branch ends in a prime.
  • Write the answer in index form, smallest prime first: 360=2×2×2×3×3×5=23×32×5360 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 5 = 2³ × 3² × 5.
  • Check by multiplying back: 8×9×5=3608 × 9 × 5 = 360.

Number · factors, multiples and primes

HCF and LCM

  • The highest common factor (HCF) is the biggest number that divides into both. The lowest common multiple (LCM) is the smallest number both divide into.
  • Listing: write out factors (HCF) or multiples (LCM) of each number until they match.
  • Prime factor method: put the prime factors in a Venn diagram, with shared primes in the overlap. HCF = product of the overlap; LCM = product of everything in the diagram.

Number · fractions

Equivalent fractions and mixed numbers

  • Equivalent fractions have the same value: multiply or divide the top and bottom by the same number. 32​=128​2/3 = 8/12.
  • Simplest form: divide top and bottom by their HCF. 2418​=43​18/24 = 3/4 (divide by 6).
  • A mixed number has a whole part and a fraction part. Improper to mixed: 411​=243​11/4 = 23/4 (4 goes into 11 twice, remainder 3).
  • Mixed to improper: whole × denominator, plus numerator, over the same denominator.

Number · fractions

Fractions of amounts

  • To find a fraction of an amount, divide by the denominator, multiply by the numerator: 52​2/5 of 35 is 35÷5×2=1435 ÷ 5 × 2 = 14.
  • "Of" means multiply: 52​2/5 of 35 is 52​×352/5 × 35.
  • To write one quantity as a fraction of another, put the first over the second (same units), then simplify: 45 minutes out of an hour is 6045​=43​45/60 = 3/4.
  • A fraction can be greater than 1: 90 minutes as a fraction of an hour is 6090​=23​90/60 = 3/2.

Number · fractions

Adding and subtracting fractions

  • You can only add or subtract fractions when the pieces are the same size: rewrite them with a common denominator (the LCM of the denominators is the neatest).
  • Then add or subtract the numerators; the denominator stays the same. 41​+61​=123​+122​=125​1/4 + 1/6 = 3/12 + 2/12 = 5/12.
  • Never add the denominators.
  • With mixed numbers, the safest route is to change to improper fractions first. Negative fractions follow the usual sign rules: −21​+31​=−63​+62​=−61​−1/2 + 1/3 = −3/6 + 2/6 = −1/6.

Multiplying and dividing fractions

  • To multiply, multiply the numerators and multiply the denominators, cancelling first if you can: 32​×109​=3018​=53​2/3 × 9/10 = 18/30 = 3/5.

Number · fractions, decimals and percentages

Converting between fractions, decimals and percentages

  • Percent means "number of parts per hundred": 60%=10060​=0.660% = 60/100 = 0.6.
  • Fraction to decimal: divide the numerator by the denominator. 53​=3÷5=0.63/5 = 3 ÷ 5 = 0.6.
  • Decimal to fraction: use place value, then simplify. 0.35=10035​=207​0.35 = 35/100 = 7/20.
  • Decimal to percentage: multiply by 100. 47​=1.75=175%7/4 = 1.75 = 175%.
  • To order a mix of forms, change them all to decimals first.

Number · fractions, decimals and percentages

Terminating and recurring decimals

  • A terminating decimal stops: 403​=0.0753/40 = 0.075. A recurring decimal repeats for ever: 31​=0.333…1/3 = 0.333…, 61​=0.1666…1/6 = 0.1666…, 115​=0.4545…5/11 = 0.4545…
  • A fraction in its simplest form terminates exactly when its denominator has no prime factors other than 2 and 5 (because 10=2×510 = 2 × 5). Otherwise it recurs.
  • Every recurring decimal is an exact fraction, even though its digits never end.

Recurring decimals to fractions (Higher)

  • Dot notation: a dot over a digit shows it repeats. 0.4˙=0.444…0.4 = 0.444…, 0.17˙=0.1777…0.17 = 0.1777…, 0.1˙2˙=0.121212…0.12 = 0.121212…, 0.1˙23˙=0.123123…0.123 = 0.123123… (dots on the first and last digit of the repeating block).

Number · powers and indices

Powers, roots and repeated doubling

  • 53=5×5×5=1255³ = 5 × 5 × 5 = 125: the base is 5 and the index (power) is 3.
  • Learn the squares to 152=22515² = 225, the cubes of 1 to 5 and 10, and 25=322⁵ = 32, 34=813⁴ = 81, 10610⁶ (a million).
  • A root undoes a power: 169​=13√169 = 13, 364​=4∛64 = 4. A positive number has two square roots, e.g. ±13± 13.
  • Each fold doubles the thickness, so nn folds multiply it by 2n2ⁿ.
  • Higher: estimate roots from nearby squares: 51​√51 is just over 7, as 72=497² = 49.
Number · powers and indices

The three laws of indices

  • Multiplying powers of the same base: add the indices. am×an=am+na^m × aⁿ = a^(m+n)
  • Dividing: subtract the indices. am÷an=am−na^m ÷ aⁿ = a^(m−n)
  • A power of a power: multiply the indices. (am)n=amn(a^m)ⁿ = a^mn
  • The laws only work when the bases are the same: 23×522³ × 5² cannot be combined.
  • Shortcut: 210=1024≈10002¹⁰ = 1024 ≈ 1000, so 240=(210)4≈10122⁴⁰ = (2¹⁰)⁴ ≈ 10¹².

Number · accuracy and bounds

Decimal places and significant figures

  • Decimal places (d.p.) count digits after the decimal point: 3.14159 to 2 d.p. is 3.14.
  • Significant figures (s.f.) count from the first non-zero digit: 0.047 38 to 2 s.f. is 0.047.
  • Look at the next digit: 5 or more rounds up. Keep place-value zeros: 52,861 to 2 s.f. is 53,000.
  • Never round in the middle of a calculation: keep full calculator values and round only the final answer.

Estimating by rounding

  • To estimate, round every number to 1 significant figure, then work it out by hand.

  • An estimate checks a calculator answer: the exact value here is about 15,400, so a result of 1540 would be wrong.

Number · accuracy and bounds

The bounds of a rounded measurement

  • 80 kg to the nearest 10 kg means anything from 75 kg up to, but not including, 85 kg.
  • Go half a unit either way: lower bound 75 kg, upper bound 85 kg.
  • As an inequality: 75≤m<8575 ≤ m < 85. 85 is excluded because it rounds up to 90.
  • This is the error interval. A truncated value is cut off: 2.1 truncated to 1 d.p. gives 2.1≤x<2.22.1 ≤ x < 2.2.

Number · accuracy and bounds

Bounds in calculations (Higher)

  • To find the biggest or smallest possible result, choose the right bound for each value.
  • Add: upper + upper gives the upper bound. Multiply: upper × upper.
  • Subtract: upper − lower gives the upper bound (take away as little as possible).
  • Divide: upper ÷ lower gives the upper bound (divide by as little as possible).
  • For lower bounds, swap every choice. Then round only at the end, to a sensible accuracy.

Make a guess: Halve an A4 sheet and it keeps its shape. Why must that be 2​√2?

Number · surds

What a surd is (Higher)

  • A surd is a root that cannot be simplified to a whole number or fraction, such as 2​√2 or 7​√7. It is irrational, so leaving it as 2​√2 keeps an answer exact.
  • 9​=3√9 = 3 is not a surd.
  • Multiply and divide inside one root: a​×b​=ab​√a × √b = √ab and b​a​​=ba​​(√a)/(√b) = √(a/b)
  • So a​×a​=a√a × √a = a: 2​×2​=2√2 × √2 = 2.
  • You cannot add inside a root: 9​+16​=7√9 + √16 = 7, but 25​=5√25 = 5.
Number · surds

Simplifying surds (Higher)

  • Split off the largest square factor: 12​=4×3​=4​×3​=23​√12 = √(4 × 3) = √4 × √3 = 2√3
  • Useful squares to look for: 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 100.
  • Simplify first, then collect like surds as you would like terms:
200​−32​=102​−42​=62​√200 − √32 = 10√2 − 4√2 = 6√2
  • Different surds such as 2​√2 and 3​√3 cannot be combined by adding.

Mission 9

A 4-digit PIN: how many guesses to be certain of getting it?

Make a guess: A 4-digit PIN: how many guesses to be certain of getting it?

Number · counting strategies

Listing systematically

  • Systematic listing means following a fixed order so that nothing is missed and nothing is repeated.
  • Fix the first choice, run through every option for the second, then move the first choice on.
  • A table or grid works well for two choices: rows for one, columns for the other.
  • Look for a structure you can count without writing everything out: the 2-digit codes 00, 01, …, 99 go from 0 to 99, so there are 100 of them.

Number · counting strategies

The product rule for counting (Higher)

  • If there are mm ways to do one thing and, for each of those, nn ways to do another, there are m×nm × n ways to do both.
  • It extends to more stages: multiply the number of choices at each stage. 3 starters, 4 mains and 2 desserts give 3×4×2=243 × 4 × 2 = 24 meals.
  • If an item can't be repeated, each stage has one fewer choice than the one before.
  • Deal with any restricted position first (for example, "the first digit can't be 0").

Mission check

?A 4-digit PIN uses the digits 0–9, and digits can repeat. How many guesses make you certain to get it?

Listed in order, the PINs run 0000, 0001, …, 9999, which is 10,000 codes (just as the 2-digit codes 00 to 99 make 100). With the product rule: 10 choices for each of 4 positions, 104=1000010⁴ = 10 000. To be certain you would need 10,000 guesses in the worst case; if no digit could repeat it would drop to 10×9×8×7=504010 × 9 × 8 × 7 = 5040.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Place value: each column is 10 times the next; compare decimals column by column, and on a number line further right is bigger.
  • Negatives: same signs multiply or divide to a positive; subtracting a negative adds.
  • Brackets, then powers and roots, then ×× and ÷÷ left to right, then ++ and −− left to right.
  • Factors divide into a number; multiples are in its times table; a prime has exactly two factors (so 1 is not prime).
  • Every whole number above 1 has one prime factor decomposition; write it in index form.
  • HCF: shared primes (the Venn overlap). LCM: all the primes in the Venn diagram. "Next time together" problems need the LCM; "biggest equal groups" problems need the HCF.
  • Equivalent fractions: multiply or divide top and bottom by the same number; simplify using the HCF.
  • Add and subtract with a common denominator; never add the denominators.
  • Multiply tops and bottoms; to divide, multiply by the reciprocal. Change mixed numbers to improper fractions first.
  • Fraction to decimal: divide. Decimal to percentage: multiply by 100. To order, change everything to decimals.
  • A fraction in simplest form terminates only if its denominator's prime factors are 2s and 5s; otherwise it recurs. Every recurring decimal is an exact fraction.
  • Higher: recurring decimal to fraction by multiplying by a power of 10 and subtracting.
  • An index counts repeated multiplication; a root undoes a power.
  • Same base: multiply → add indices, divide → subtract, power of a power → multiply. a0=1a⁰ = 1 and a−n=an1​a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ.
  • Higher: anm​=(na​)ma^(m/n) = (n√a)^m. Take the root first.
  • Standard form is A×10nA × 10ⁿ with 1≤A<101 ≤ A < 10; the power counts how far the point moves.
  • Multiply: multiply the numbers, add the powers. Divide: divide, subtract. Add or subtract: match the powers first.
  • Always finish by adjusting AA back into the range 1≤A<101 ≤ A < 10.
  • Round to d.p. or s.f. only at the end; estimate by rounding every number to 1 s.f.
  • A rounded value stands for a range: half a unit below (included) up to half a unit above (not included).
  • Higher: for the largest result, pick upper bounds for what you add or multiply and lower bounds for what you subtract or divide by.
  • A surd such as 2​√2 is an exact irrational root. a​×b​=ab​√a × √b = √ab, but a​+b​=a+b​√a + √b ≠ √(a+b).
  • Simplify by taking out the largest square factor, then collect like surds.
  • Rationalise a​k​k/(√a) by multiplying by a​a​​(√a)/(√a); for a+b​a + √b, multiply by a−b​a − √b.
  • List systematically: fix one choice, run through the others, then move on; use a table or grid for two choices.
  • Higher: product rule: multiply the number of choices at each stage; reduce the choices when items can't repeat, and do restricted positions first.
  • When order doesn't matter (A v B is the same match as B v A), divide out the double counting.
