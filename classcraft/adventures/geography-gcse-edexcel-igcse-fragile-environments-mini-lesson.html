Mission 1

Mission 1: Between 11,000 and 5,000 years ago, the Sahara had permanent lakes, plants and people. Today it is desert. Did people cause the change?

Make a guess: Between 11,000 and 5,000 years ago, the Sahara had permanent lakes, plants and people. Today it is desert. Did people cause the change?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 7.1a distribution and characteristics of the world's fragile environments
  • 7.1b causes of desertification: drought, population pressure, fuel supply, overgrazing, migration
  • 7.2a social, economic and environmental impacts of desertification: reduced output, malnutrition, famine, migration
Mission 1

What desertification is

  • "Desertification is land degradation in arid, semi-arid, and dry sub-humid areas", caused by many factors, "including human activities and climatic variations" (IPCC).
  • It means dry land losing its productivity: soil, plants and water. It is not simply sand dunes advancing.
  • Its range and intensity have increased in some dryland areas over recent decades (high confidence).
Mission 1

Fragile environments: where they are

  • A fragile environment is easily damaged and slow to recover.
  • Examples: hot deserts and semi-arid lands, tropical rainforests, polar and mountain areas, and coral reefs.
  • Drylands cover about 46% of the world's land and are home to about 3 billion people (IPCC; low confidence in the area figure).
  • Desertification threatens the semi-arid margins of deserts, such as the Sahel, south of the Sahara.
Mission 1

The Green Sahara: a natural change

  • From 11,000 to 5,000 years ago, the Sahara had high rainfall, plants, permanent lakes and people.
  • Slow changes in Earth's orbit made northern summers sunnier, which strengthened the African monsoon and brought rain north.
  • As that orbital effect faded, the monsoon weakened. Losing plants and gaining dust made it drier still (feedbacks).
  • So the Sahara became desert mainly through natural climate change, not people.
Mission 1

The Sahel: drought

  • In the Sahel, summer rainfall was high in the 1950s and 1960s, fell sharply in the 1970s and 1980s (driest year 1984), and has partly recovered since.
  • A cooler north Atlantic is linked to a drier Sahel.
  • Drought dries soils and kills plants, leaving ground bare.
Quick check
?What best explains why the Green Sahara became desert about 5,000 years ago?
Mission 1

How people cause desertification

  • Population growth raises demand for food, fuel and water. In the West African Sahel, cropland doubled after 1975 and settlement area grew by about 150%.
  • Over-cultivation: farming the same land without rest exhausts the soil.
  • Overgrazing: too many animals strip the plants. In the Sahel it reduced the store of seeds in the soil, so plants struggle to return.
  • Fuel wood removal: most people in developing countries still rely on biomass, mostly fuelwood, for a large share of their energy, with the highest dependence in Sub-Saharan Africa. It is linked to tree loss in some drylands, but not in others.
Mission 1

Soil erosion: the downward spiral

  • Bare soil has no roots to hold it and no plants to shade it.
  • Wind blows the fine, fertile topsoil away; intense rain washes it off.
  • With thinner soil, less water soaks in, so fewer plants grow and more soil is lost: a positive feedback.
  • Migration: when people and herds move into an area, for example fleeing drought elsewhere, pressure on its land and water rises.
Quick check
?A village doubles its goats and cattle on the same grazing land. Which cause of desertification is this?
Mission 1

Impacts of desertification

  • Economic: desertification has reduced farm output and incomes (IPCC, high confidence).
  • Social: the Sahel droughts of the 1970s–80s contributed to "hunger, out-migration and suffering for millions of pastoralists". Long hunger causes malnutrition; when food runs out across a region, famine.
  • Migration: in some places desertification adds an incentive to migrate (medium confidence), though strong effects are disputed.
  • Environmental: soil loss, fewer plants and animals, and dust storms.
Mission check

?In a semi-arid district, rainfall has not changed for 30 years. But herds have doubled, and trees are cut for cooking fuel. The land is degrading. What is the main cause?

The Green Sahara ended about 5,000 years ago mainly through natural change: a weaker monsoon, made worse by feedbacks. Today, in the Sahel, climate and people both drive desertification, in proportions that vary from place to place (IPCC, high confidence).

Mission 2

Mission 2: The world's biggest rainforest is still being cleared. Who is doing it, and why does it matter beyond Brazil?

Make a guess: The world's biggest rainforest is still being cleared. Who is doing it, and why does it matter beyond Brazil?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 7.1b causes: commercial timber, agriculture, mining, transport, settlement, HEP
  • 7.2b impacts: biodiversity loss, climate change contribution, economic development, soil erosion
Mission 2

The Amazon rainforest

  • The largest tropical rainforest on Earth. Most of it is in Brazil; it also covers parts of eight other South American countries and territories.
  • About 16,000 tree species.
  • Amazon trees store more carbon than any other ecosystem on Earth.
  • This mission uses the Brazilian Amazon as the case study.
Mission 2

Changing rates of deforestation

  • From 2004 to 2012 the rate of clearing in the Brazilian Amazon fell by 84%.
  • 2012: 4,571 km² cleared, the lowest on record until 2026.
  • The rate then rose again: 5,731 km² in August 2024–July 2025.
  • August 2025–July 2026: an estimated 3,335 km², the lowest since records began in 1988.
Quick check
?Using the figures just taught: By what percentage did clearing fall from 2024–25 (5,731 km²) to 2025–26 (3,335 km²)?
Mission 2

Cause: farming

  • Across the tropics, 90–99% of deforestation in 2011–15 was driven by agriculture.
  • In the Amazon, most cleared land first becomes cattle pasture (commercial farming).
  • Soy then spreads, mostly onto pastures first cleared for cattle.
  • Much soy is exported: in 2025 China imported 82.3 million tonnes of soybeans from Brazil, 74% of its soybean imports.
Mission 2

Cause: logging

  • Selective logging takes valuable trees and leaves the rest standing.
  • 1999–2002, in Brazil's top five timber states, 12,075–19,823 km² were logged each year: 60–123% of the area deforested.
  • Logging roads open up the forest to farmers and settlers.
  • Edexcel calls this commercial timber extraction.
Mission 2

Cause: roads, mining and energy

  • Roads: nearly 95% of deforestation happened within 5.5 km of a road or 1 km of a river. Over 190,000 km of the roads are unofficial.
  • Mining: small-scale gold mining (garimpo) in Brazil grew from about 218 km² (1985) to about 2,627 km² (2022). More than 91% is in the Amazon, and at least 77% of 2022 sites showed signs of illegality.
  • Energy (HEP): the Belo Monte dam on the Xingu River flooded land and forced people in Altamira to move.
Mission 2

Cause: settlement and population growth

  • New roads bring settlers looking for land.
  • Growing towns and villages need land for homes, food and fuel.
Quick check
?Which cause most often opens the forest up for the others?
Mission 2

Impact: economic development

  • Benefits: farm exports (beef, soy), mining income and jobs, and electricity from HEP.
  • Costs: a 2025 study found mining's economic benefits for Brazilian towns were limited and tied to global mineral prices.
  • A study in Altamira found Belo Monte undermined the livelihoods of fishing households.
  • Gains often go to companies and cities; costs fall on forest communities.
Mission 2

Impact: soils and nutrients

  • Most nutrients in a rainforest are stored in plants, not the soil. Rainforest soils are thin and poor.
  • Clearing breaks the nutrient cycle: no more leaf litter feeds the soil.
  • Heavy rain hits bare ground: soil erosion washes topsoil away and leaching removes nutrients.
  • Pasture and crops can fail within a few years, so more forest is cleared.
Mission 2

Impact: rainfall and regional climate

  • Trees pump water vapour into the air (transpiration), which falls again as rain downwind.
  • Over more than 60% of tropical land, air that has passed over forest gives at least twice as much rain as air that has passed over little vegetation.
  • Replacing forest with monoculture crops or pasture means less moisture is recycled.
  • If clearing trends continued, Amazon rainfall could fall by 12% (wet season) and 21% (dry season) by 2050.
Mission 2

Impact: climate change

  • Trees store carbon. Clearing and burning release it as carbon dioxide.
  • Aircraft measurements in 2010–2018 found south-eastern Amazonia had become a net carbon source.
  • The causes: more deforestation and longer, drier dry seasons, bringing more fires.
Mission 2

Impact: biodiversity

  • Habitat loss shrinks each species' range.
  • Fragmentation: in 1999–2002, deforestation and logging created about 32,000 km and 38,000 km of new forest edge each year.
  • Small, separate fragments leave isolated populations that cannot mix or move.
  • Species found only in cleared areas can die out locally, or globally: extinction.
Quick check
?Which impact of clearing works at a regional scale, across much of the Amazon basin?
Mission check

?In September–October 2015, forest and peatland fires across maritime south-east Asia, mostly in Indonesia, released more CO₂ per day than all the fossil fuels burned in the EU. Which conclusion best fits?

In the Amazon, farming (cattle, then soy) is the main cause, helped by roads, logging, mining and HEP. The impacts reach far beyond Brazil: rainfall, carbon and biodiversity.

Mission 3

Mission 3: In 1991 one volcano in the Philippines cooled the whole planet. How?

Make a guess: In 1991 one volcano in the Philippines cooled the whole planet. How?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 7.1c natural causes: Milankovitch cycles, solar variation, volcanism
Mission 3

Three natural causes

  • Earth's climate has always changed, through glacial and interglacial periods.
  • Three natural causes:
    • orbital changes (Milankovitch cycles)
    • solar output (sunspots and the solar cycle)
    • volcanic eruptions.
Mission 3

Milankovitch cycles

  • Eccentricity: Earth's orbit changes from more circular to more oval, in a cycle of about 100,000 years.
  • Axial tilt (obliquity): the tilt of Earth's axis changes, in about 41,000 years.
  • Precession: the axis wobbles, changing which season is nearest the Sun, about 23,000 years on average.
  • Together they change how much sunlight each latitude and season gets, pacing glacials and interglacials.
Quick check
?Why can Milankovitch cycles not explain the warming of the last 100 years?
Mission 3

Sunspots and solar output

  • Sunspots are "cooler", "darker" areas on the Sun's surface.
  • Their number rises and falls in a solar cycle of about 11 years. More sunspots go with a more active Sun.
  • Over a cycle the Sun's energy output varies by only about 0.1%.
  • The Maunder Minimum (1645–1715), "when there were few sunspots", fell within the cooler Little Ice Age, but that began centuries earlier.
  • But warming from human greenhouse gases since 1750 is "over 270 times greater" than extra warming from the Sun.
Mission 3

Volcanic eruptions: Pinatubo, 1991

  • In June 1991 Mount Pinatubo "injected about 15 million tons of sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere".
  • The gas formed tiny droplets (aerosols) that "scatter and absorb incoming sunlight".
  • High winds spread the haze around the world, so less sunlight reached the surface.
Mission 3

How big, and how long?

  • Global average temperature fell by about 0.6 °C over the next 15 months.
  • There was "a measurable cooling of the Earth's surface for a period of almost two years".
  • The droplets slowly fall out of the stratosphere, so the cooling fades.
  • Ash falls out within days; it is the sulfur haze that cools the whole planet.
Quick check
?Why did Pinatubo's cooling last only about two years?
Mission check

?Imagine a huge eruption injects sulfur gas high into the stratosphere. What would happen to global temperature?

Pinatubo "injected about 15 million tons of sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere". The haze scattered sunlight and cooled the planet by about 0.6 °C for almost two years.

Mission 4

Mission 4: The UK's emissions fell by 54% between 1990 and 2025. Has the UK really halved its part in climate change?

Make a guess: The UK's emissions fell by 54% between 1990 and 2025. Has the UK really halved its part in climate change?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 7.1c how industry, transport, energy and farming cause the enhanced greenhouse effect
Mission 4

The natural greenhouse effect

  • Sunlight (short-wave) passes through the atmosphere and warms the ground.
  • The warm ground gives off heat (long-wave radiation).
  • Greenhouse gases (water vapour, carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide) absorb some of it and give out heat in all directions, including back down.
  • Without them, Earth's surface would be about −19 °C; it averages about 14 °C.
Mission 4

The enhanced greenhouse effect

  • People add greenhouse gases faster than nature removes them.
  • More gas traps more long-wave heat, so the planet warms: the enhanced greenhouse effect.
  • 2024 levels: carbon dioxide 423.9 ppm, methane 1,942 ppb, nitrous oxide 338.0 ppb.
  • That is 52%, 166% and 25% above pre-industrial levels (before 1750).
Quick check
?What makes the greenhouse effect enhanced?
Mission 4

Cause 1: burning fossil fuels

  • Coal, oil and gas release carbon dioxide when burned.
  • In 2025 burning fossil fuels released about 38.1 billion tonnes of CO₂ (projection).
  • Energy: power stations burning coal and gas for electricity and heat.
  • Transport: petrol, diesel and jet fuel. In the UK, domestic transport made 31% of net emissions in 2025.
  • Industry: fuel for factories, plus processes such as making cement and steel.
Mission 4

Cause 2: farming

  • Cattle and sheep release methane as they digest grass. Flooded rice fields and manure release methane too.
  • Fertilisers release nitrous oxide.
  • Farming and waste together released about 228 million tonnes of methane a year (2010–2019), against 115 million tonnes from fossil fuels.
  • Methane is the second most important human-influenced greenhouse gas, after CO₂.
Quick check
?In 2025, fossil fuels released about 38.1 billion tonnes of CO₂ and land-use change (such as deforestation) about 4.1 billion tonnes. What percentage of this total came from fossil fuels, to the nearest whole number?
Mission 4

What is a carbon footprint?

  • A carbon footprint is the total greenhouse gas emitted to supply what a person, organisation or country uses, measured in CO₂-equivalent.
  • A country's footprint counts emissions from making its imports, wherever they happen.
  • Its territorial emissions count only what is released inside its borders.
Mission 4

The UK's footprint

  • The UK's carbon footprint was about 699 million tonnes CO₂-equivalent in 2023.
  • Emissions from making its imports rose from 31% of the footprint in 1996 to 53% in 2023.
  • Imports from China make up 25% of those import emissions.
Mission 4

Two measures, two stories

  • Since 1996, the UK's territorial emissions fell by 50%; its carbon footprint by only 15%.
  • Defra links this to "a shift from manufacturing towards a service-based economy, alongside an increased reliance on imported goods and services".
  • Some cuts were real: the UK's last coal power station closed in September 2024.
Quick check
?Why has the UK's carbon footprint fallen much less than its territorial emissions?
Mission check

?A country's territorial emissions fell by 40% over 20 years. In the same years it closed its steelworks and began importing most of its steel and electronics. Has its part in climate change fallen by 40%?

The UK's territorial emissions halved, but its carbon footprint fell by only 15% (1996–2023), because 53% of the footprint now comes from imports. Part of the cut is real; part has moved abroad.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Global sea level has risen about 10 cm since 1993, and the yearly rise has more than doubled. Where is the extra water coming from?

Make a guess: Global sea level has risen about 10 cm since 1993, and the yearly rise has more than doubled. Where is the extra water coming from?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 7.2c negative effects on fragile environments and people: sea-level rise, hazards, ecosystems, jobs, settlement, health, food
Mission 5

The sea is rising faster

  • Global sea level rose 0.20 m between 1901 and 2018.
  • The yearly rise sped up: 1.3 mm a year (1901–1971), 1.9 mm (1971–2006), 3.7 mm (2006–2018).
  • Satellites: about 10 cm since 1993.
Mission 5

Where the extra water comes from

  • Thermal expansion: seawater takes up more space as it warms.
  • Melting land ice: water from glaciers and ice sheets runs into the sea.
  • Shares of the rise, 1971–2018: thermal expansion 50%, glaciers 22%, ice sheets 20%, other land water 8%.
  • Melting sea ice adds almost nothing: it is already floating, so it already displaces its own weight of water.
Quick check
?Which of these adds least to sea-level rise?
Mission 5

Retreating glaciers

  • Reference glaciers worldwide lost on average 214 mm of water-equivalent thickness per decade in the 1980s, and 896 mm per decade in the 2010s.
  • Glacier National Park, USA: about 80 glaciers larger than 0.1 km² around 1850; 32 in 2005.
  • People: in the upper Indus basin, glacier melt made up 40.6% of river runoff (1998–2007). Shrinking glaciers threaten that water supply in the long term.
Mission 5

Effects on the environment

  • About half of the species studied worldwide have moved towards the poles or uphill.
  • From early 2023 to mid-2025, heat stress able to bleach coral hit 84% of the world's reef area.
  • Climate zones are shifting: dry (arid) zones are expanding and polar zones shrinking.
Mission 5

Effects on people

  • Health: heat-related deaths average 546,000 a year, a rate 23% higher than in the 1990s.
  • Diseases carried by mosquitoes and ticks have spread to new areas.
  • Displacement: storms forced 17.9 million movements in 2025. Not every disaster is caused by climate change; scientists test each event.
  • About 3.3 to 3.6 billion people live in places highly vulnerable to climate change.
Quick check
?Which is an effect of climate change on people, rather than on the environment?
Mission 5

Food: changing crop yields

  • Farm output keeps growing, but climate change "has slowed this growth over the past 50 years globally".
  • Losers: yields of some crops, such as maize and wheat, have fallen in many lower-latitude (tropical) regions.
  • Winners: some crops (maize, wheat, sugar beet) have gained in many higher-latitude regions.
  • In Africa, livestock on pasture grow more slowly.
Mission 5

Effects on the economy, jobs and settlements

  • Jobs: heat cost 640 billion potential working hours in 2024, especially in outdoor work such as farming and building.
  • Food prices (projection): climate change could raise cereal prices by a median 7.6% by 2050 (range 1–23%).
  • Settlements: by 2050, many low-lying megacities and small islands are projected to face, at least once a year, extreme sea levels that used to come once a century.
Mission 5

More extreme weather

  • Since the 1950s, heatwaves have become more frequent and more intense over most land regions.
  • Heavy rainfall has become more frequent and more intense over most land since the 1950s.
  • A heat extreme that used to come once in 10 years now comes 2.8 times per decade; at 2 °C of warming, 5.6 times (projection).
Mission 5

Predicted sea level

  • By 2100, compared with 1995–2014: a likely rise of 0.28–0.55 m if emissions are very low, and 0.63–1.01 m if they are very high.
  • These are projections: the range depends on how much people emit.
Quick check
?Which statement about climate change and crop yields is best supported?
Mission check

?Imagine two summers. In summer 1, 100 billion tonnes of floating Arctic sea ice melts. In summer 2, 100 billion tonnes of ice melts from Greenland's ice sheet on land, and the upper ocean warms. Which summer raises sea level more?

The extra water comes from melting land ice (glaciers and ice sheets) and from thermal expansion of warming seawater, which explained half the rise in 1971–2018. Rising seas are one of many effects on people, places and the economy.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Deserts get more sunshine than they can use, and many lie beside the sea. Can technology turn sun and seawater into reliable power and water?

Make a guess: Deserts get more sunshine than they can use, and many lie beside the sea. Can technology turn sun and seawater into reliable power and water?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 7.3a how technology can resolve water shortages in environments threatened by desertification
Mission 6

Why drylands run short of water

  • Rain is low and unreliable, and hot, sunny weather evaporates much of it.
  • Drylands are home to about 3 billion people, and demand for water grows with population, farming and industry.
  • Technology can add new supplies (desalination) or make each drop go further (drip irrigation, water harvesting).
Mission 6

How desalination works

  • Desalination removes salt from sea water or salty groundwater.
  • Distillation: boil the water, capture the steam and condense it back into fresh water.
  • Reverse osmosis: force salty water through a semi-permeable membrane (a very fine filter). Water passes through; the dissolved salt is held back.
  • Both are "expensive, energy-intensive, and involve large-scale facilities" (USGS).
Mission 6

Desalination worldwide

  • A 2019 global study counted 15,906 working desalination plants, producing about 95 million m³ of fresh water a day.
  • 48% of it was produced in the Middle East and North Africa, where deserts meet the sea.
Mission 6

Case: Carlsbad, California

  • Southern California is dry and often in drought. The Carlsbad plant began commercial operations in 2015.
  • It makes more than 50 million gallons of drinking water a day, enough for about 400,000 people: about 10% of the San Diego region's demand.
  • It uses reverse osmosis. Energy-recovery devices cut the energy the process uses by 46%.
  • Its water is "more expensive than the region's traditional imported water sources", but it is drought-proof.
Quick check
?How does reverse osmosis make fresh water?
Mission 6

The salty leftovers: brine

  • For every plant, the salt has to go somewhere: a very salty liquid called brine.
  • The 2019 study estimated brine output at about 142 million m³ a day, 55% of it from Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Kuwait and Qatar.
  • Disposal is "both costly and associated with negative environmental impacts".
  • At Carlsbad the brine has about twice the salt of sea water; it is diluted with sea water before release.
Quick check
?The world's plants make about 95 million m³ of fresh water and about 142 million m³ of brine a day. About how many m³ of brine is made for each m³ of fresh water?
Mission 6

Can the sun power it?

  • Deserts get strong sunshine: Coober Pedy, Australia, averages 28.3 MJ of solar energy per m² a day in January.
  • Morocco's Noor-Ouarzazate concentrated solar power complex: the World Bank helped finance a first 160 MW phase of a 500 MW plant (2011), then two more phases (2014), to raise output "especially during peak hours".
  • The project needed money to cover the gap between the price paid for the power and the price it was sold at.
  • Stronger sand storms and moving dunes damage solar and wind infrastructure (IPCC, high confidence).
Mission 6

Making less water go further

  • Drip irrigation delivers water to plant roots through pipes. In India it cut the water used to grow sugarcane by 44% and cotton by 45%, and raised yields by up to 29%.
  • Rainwater harvesting: zaï pits and stone bunds catch runoff from rare storms, raising crop yields in drylands.
  • Solar-powered drip irrigation raised farm incomes in Benin. But solar pumps can over-abstract groundwater unless use is limited.
Quick check
?A farmer in a dry region wants to grow the same crop with less water. Which technology fits best?
Mission 6

Does technology solve water shortages?

  • For: desalination gives a supply that does not depend on rain, and solar power can run it.
  • Against: high cost and energy use; about 1.5 m³ of brine for each m³ of water.
  • Who benefits? Rich coastal cities. "Many oases are located far from water sources that are suitable for desalination".
  • Exam move: judge each technology by cost, energy, side effects, scale and who benefits.
Mission check

?A dry, wealthy coastal city plans a large solar-powered desalination plant. Which prediction is most accurate?

Yes, with costs. Solar power and desalination already supply power and water in drylands, but solar needed price support at Noor-Ouarzazate, and desalination leaves about 1.5 m³ of brine for each m³ of fresh water. Technology helps most where people can afford it and live near the sea.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Some villagers in Guatemala make a living by cutting down rainforest trees. Can logging help save a forest?

Make a guess: Some villagers in Guatemala make a living by cutting down rainforest trees. Can logging help save a forest?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 7.3b approaches to sustainable use and management of a rainforest in a named region to limit deforestation
Mission 7

What makes management sustainable?

  • Sustainable management meets people's needs today without destroying the forest for the future.
  • Test any plan with three questions:
  • Environment: does forest cover and wildlife stay?
  • People: do local people earn a living from it?
  • Long term: will it still work in 20 years, with rules that are enforced?
Mission 7

The toolkit: using the timber

  • Selective logging: cut only some mature trees, leave the rest standing.
  • Replanting: replace trees that are felled; native species keep habitat for wildlife.
  • Certification: the Forest Stewardship Council (FSC) sets standards for responsible forest management; independent bodies check forests against them.
  • International hardwood agreements: the International Tropical Timber Agreement has governed trade in tropical timber since the 1980s; today's version, of 2006, came into force in 2011.
Mission 7

The toolkit: protecting and paying

  • Conservation: protected areas such as national parks ban or limit clearing.
  • Education: teaching local people and buyers why the forest matters, and how to use it without clearing it.
  • Ecotourism: small-scale tourism that pays local people to keep the forest standing.
  • Debt reduction (debt-for-nature swaps): part of a country's debt is cancelled in return for spending on conservation. The first was signed by Bolivia and Conservation International in 1987.
Quick check
?A forest company wants to keep earning from timber but do the least damage to the forest. Which strategy fits best?
Mission 7

The case: the Maya Biosphere Reserve, Guatemala

  • Petén, northern Guatemala: the largest expanse of continuous rainforest in Central America.
  • Guatemala's protected areas cover 32% of its land, yet only Nicaragua has lost forest faster in Latin America and the Caribbean since 2000.
  • The reserve is shared out in zones, each with different rules.
  • Guatemala has a medium HDI (0.662, 2023): an emerging country in Edexcel's terms.
Mission 7

Zoning

  • Core zone: strictly protected; no logging. Its national parks include Tikal and Mirador–Río Azul.
  • Multiple-use zone: forest used under rules. Rights to timber and other forest products were given to 12 community groups and 2 timber companies.
  • Buffer zone: along the southern edge; farming and settlement, with some clearing allowed "under certain circumstances".
  • UNESCO's model: core = strict protection; buffer = activities compatible with conservation; transition = where communities live and work.
Mission 7

Local scale: Uaxactún's community forest

  • A village of about 800 people that manages 835 km² of forest in the multiple-use zone.
  • Selective logging since 2000; certified by the FSC since 2001.
  • Residents agreed hunting rules: no hunting at waterholes, no selling meat to outsiders.
  • Result: camera-trap surveys estimated 25 species of large ground animals in Uaxactún's forest and 24 in the strictly protected Mirador–Río Azul area next door.
  • But heavily hunted species, such as the great curassow, were scarcer near the village.
Mission 7

Does it work?

  • Success: Tikal and Mirador–Río Azul national parks stayed over 99% stable forest, 2000–2017.
  • Mixed: some concessions manage forests well; others "have had limited success or failed completely".
  • Failure: the buffer zone lost 1.4% of its forest a year; by 2017 only 17% was undisturbed forest.
  • Pressure on the core: Laguna del Tigre National Park, beside the buffer zone, was heavily cleared despite its protection.
Quick check
?Laguna del Tigre National Park, beside the buffer zone, was heavily cleared, while Mirador–Río Azul National Park, in the far north, stayed almost untouched. Both are in the same strictly protected core zone. What does this suggest?
Mission check

?A government wants to cut deforestation in a rainforest region where many villagers depend on the forest for income. Which plan is most likely to be sustainable?

In the Maya Biosphere Reserve, a certified community forest at Uaxactún kept as many large animal species as the strictly protected forest next door. Where rules were not enforced, as in the buffer zone, the forest kept shrinking.

Mission 8

Mission 8: The least developed countries emitted less than 0.4% of all historical fossil CO₂, yet face some of the worst impacts. Who should cut emissions, and who should pay?

Make a guess: The least developed countries emitted less than 0.4% of all historical fossil CO₂, yet face some of the worst impacts. Who should cut emissions, and who should pay?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 7.3c responses to climate change by individuals, organisations and governments in a named developed and a named emerging or developing country
Mission 8

Two kinds of response

  • Mitigation: reducing the causes of climate change, by cutting emissions or removing greenhouse gases (renewable energy, planting forests).
  • Adaptation: adjusting to the effects of climate change to reduce harm (sea walls, heat alerts, drought-tolerant crops).
  • Responses happen at international, national and local scales, and by individuals.
Mission 8

The Paris Agreement, 2015

  • Adopted on 12 December 2015; in force from 4 November 2016.
  • Aim: hold warming "well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels", and try to limit it to 1.5 °C.
  • Every country sets its own target, a nationally determined contribution (NDC), and updates it every five years.
  • 194 Parties. The United States' withdrawal took effect on 27 January 2026.
Mission 8

Who cuts, and who pays?

  • Principle: "common but differentiated responsibilities"; developed countries "should take the lead".
  • The least developed countries produced less than 0.4% of historical CO₂ from fossil fuels and industry (1850–2019).
  • Finance: rich countries' goal of US$100 billion a year was first met in 2022 (US$115.9 billion). In 2024 a new goal was set: US$300 billion a year by 2035.
  • A Fund for responding to Loss and Damage helps developing countries that are particularly vulnerable.
Quick check
?What is a nationally determined contribution (NDC)?
Mission 8

Is Paris working?

  • Strengths: almost every country has joined; every Party must set and update targets.
  • Limits: targets are self-chosen. UNEP (2025): if all NDCs are met, warming this century of 2.3–2.5 °C; current policies alone, up to 2.8 °C.
  • 2024 was likely the first calendar year above 1.5 °C (1.55 ± 0.13 °C). The Paris goal is judged over decades, not one year.
  • The remaining carbon budget for 1.5 °C is 170 billion tonnes of CO₂; 2025 emissions were about 38.1 billion tonnes from fossil fuels plus 4.1 billion from land-use change.
Quick check
?The remaining carbon budget for 1.5 °C is 170 billion tonnes of CO₂. In 2025, about 38.1 billion tonnes came from fossil fuels and 4.1 billion from land-use change. At 2025 levels, about how many years would the budget last?
Mission 8

A developed country: the UK

  • Government: the Climate Change Act 2008 made cuts a legal duty: at least 80% by 2050 (from 1990), raised to 100% (net zero) in 2019.
  • It set five-year carbon budgets and an independent Committee on Climate Change.
  • Results: 2025 emissions were 54% below 1990 (provisional); the last coal power station closed in September 2024.
  • Hardest to cut: domestic transport, 31% of net emissions in 2025.
Mission 8

An emerging country: India

  • India's updated NDC (2022): cut the emissions intensity of its GDP by 45% by 2030 from 2005.
  • About 50% of its installed electricity capacity to come from non-fossil sources by 2030; net zero by 2070.
  • It promotes "LIFE – 'Lifestyle for Environment'", a mass movement on individual lifestyles.
  • Emissions per person, 2025: India 3.11 t, UK 5.45 t CO₂-equivalent.
  • Evaluate: an intensity target cuts emissions per unit of GDP, so total emissions can still grow as the economy grows.
Mission 8

Local action, individuals and technology

  • Technology: from 2010 to 2019, the cost of solar energy fell by 85%, wind by 55% and lithium-ion batteries by 85%.
  • Local: in Germany, 220,000 members of 951 energy cooperatives have invested about €3.6 billion in renewable energy.
  • Individuals: choices about travel, home heating, diet and what to buy change a person's carbon footprint.
  • UK organisations and individuals: the Energy Saving Trust advises householders to insulate, because "about a third of all the heat lost in an uninsulated home escapes through the walls".
  • Government sets the rules that make the low-carbon choice easier: standards, taxes and grants.
Quick check
?Which is a local response to climate change?
Mission check

?Country X is a low-income country with tiny historical emissions and worsening coastal floods. Under the Paris Agreement, what is expected of it, and from others?

The agreements' answer is common but differentiated responsibilities: every Party cuts through its NDC, developed countries take the lead, and finance (US$300 billion a year by 2035; the Loss and Damage Fund) flows to the most vulnerable. Pledges still fall short: UNEP projects 2.3–2.8 °C.

Mission 9

Mission 9: About a quarter of the Netherlands lies below sea level. Tuvalu's land averages about 2 m above it. Which is more vulnerable, and why?

Make a guess: About a quarter of the Netherlands lies below sea level. Tuvalu's land averages about 2 m above it. Which is more vulnerable, and why?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 7.3c responses to climate change by individuals, organisations and governments in a named developed and a named emerging or developing country
Mission 9

Hazard, exposure, vulnerability

  • Hazard: the physical threat, such as sea-level rise or a storm surge.
  • Exposure: people and property in places that could be harmed.
  • Vulnerability: how likely people are to be harmed, and how able they are to cope and adapt.
  • Risk comes from the three together. The same hazard can bring very different risk.
Mission 9

The Netherlands: highly exposed

  • 26% of the Netherlands lies below sea level; with land at risk from rivers, 55% is at risk of flooding.
  • It is a high-income country: income (GNI) per person US$68,530 in 2025.
  • A named developed country.
Mission 9

The Netherlands: the government responds

  • Since 2017, flood-protection standards have been written into the Water Act.
  • Target: by 2050, a yearly chance of dying in a flood of no more than 1 in 100,000 for everyone behind the main defences.
  • In the first check (2021), about 67% of dike sections met the standard.
  • The Delta Programme has a budget of about €29 billion for 2015–2050; costs are estimated at about €38 billion: a funding gap.
Quick check
?Which best explains why the Netherlands is less vulnerable than its height suggests?
Mission 9

Tuvalu: highly exposed

  • Tuvalu is a Pacific atoll nation; its land averages 2 m above sea level.
  • 9,492 people (2025); more than half live on the largest islet, in Funafuti atoll.
  • Sea level there was nearly 15 cm higher in 2023 than 30 years earlier.
  • By 2050, much of its land and key infrastructure is projected to sit below the average high-tide level.
  • The UN lists Tuvalu as a Small Island Developing State.
Mission 9

Tuvalu: responses

  • Raising land: a project funded by the Green Climate Fund created 8 hectares of raised land on Fogafale, Funafuti's main islet (2025).
  • Mobility: the Falepili Union treaty with Australia (in force since 28 August 2024) lets Tuvaluans choose to live, work and study in Australia; the 2025 ballot offered up to 280 visa places.
  • Australia recognises that Tuvalu stays a state "notwithstanding the impact of climate-change related sea-level rise".
  • Individuals apply in the ballot; organisations (the Green Climate Fund) fund projects; governments (Tuvalu, Australia) sign treaties.
Quick check
?In 2025, income per person was US$68,530 in the Netherlands and US$9,780 in Tuvalu. How many times Tuvalu's is the Dutch figure, to 1 decimal place?
Mission 9

Governments at different levels of development

  • Netherlands (high income): pays for its own defences, sets legal standards, plans to 2050.
  • Tuvalu (small island developing state): relies on international funds for raised land and a partner country for migration.
  • Trade-off: under the treaty, Tuvalu must agree with Australia any security or defence deal with another state.
Mission check

?Two coastal towns face the same rise in sea level. Town P is 1 m above sea level, with high incomes, sea walls built to a legal standard and a funded 30-year plan. Town Q is 3 m above sea level on a small island, with low incomes, no defences and no higher ground. Which is more vulnerable, and why?

Both the Netherlands and Tuvalu are exposed. The Netherlands has about seven times Tuvalu's income per person, a €29 billion programme and legal flood standards. Tuvalu has little land and no high ground, so it relies on raised land and mobility to Australia. Tuvalu is more vulnerable.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Desertification is drylands losing productivity. Causes: drought and climate change, plus population pressure, over-cultivation, overgrazing and fuel wood removal; soil erosion speeds it up.
  • Impacts: lower output, hunger, malnutrition, famine and migration.

Mission 2

  • Amazon clearing fell sharply after 2004, rose again after 2012, and in 2025–26 reached its lowest estimate since 1988.
  • Causes: farming (cattle, soy), logging, roads, mining, HEP, settlement.
  • Impacts: economic gains, soil erosion, less rainfall, carbon release and biodiversity loss.

Mission 3

  • Natural causes: Milankovitch cycles (100,000, 41,000, 23,000 years), sunspots (11-year cycle, ~0.1% change), volcanoes (Pinatubo: −0.6 °C, about two years).

Mission 4

  • Fossil fuels, farming and deforestation add CO₂, methane and nitrous oxide faster than nature removes them: the enhanced greenhouse effect.
  • A carbon footprint counts emissions from everything consumed, including imports. The UK's fell far less than its territorial emissions.
  • Link each sector to its gas: energy, transport and industry (CO₂); farming (methane, nitrous oxide).

Mission 5

  • Sea level rises because seawater expands as it warms and land ice melts.
  • Effects reach the environment (species, coral, glaciers) and people (heat, disease, displacement).

Mission 6

  • Desalination (distillation or reverse osmosis) turns sea water into fresh water: reliable, but costly, energy-hungry and brine-producing.
  • Drip irrigation and rainwater harvesting make scarce water go further, and suit poorer, inland drylands.

Mission 7

  • Zoning (strict core, managed multiple-use, buffer) works where people benefit and rules are enforced: Uaxactún kept its wildlife; the buffer zone lost most of its forest.

Mission 8

  • Mitigation cuts causes; adaptation reduces harm.
  • Paris (2015): every Party sets an NDC; aim well below 2 °C, ideally 1.5 °C. Strength: nearly universal. Weakness: self-chosen targets that fall short.
  • UK: legal net-zero target; India: intensity target, 50% non-fossil capacity by 2030, net zero 2070.

Mission 9

  • Risk = hazard + exposure + vulnerability. Money, land, plans and partners change vulnerability.
  • Netherlands: protect (dikes, standards). Tuvalu: raise land and plan for migration.
