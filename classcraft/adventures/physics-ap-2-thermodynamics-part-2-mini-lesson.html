Mission 1

Mission 1: Squash a gas and it warms up, though nothing hot touched it. Where did the energy come from?

Make a guess: Squash a gas and it warms up, though nothing hot touched it. Where did the energy come from?

Mission 1

Two ways to change internal energy

  • Internal energy U: random kinetic + potential energy of the particles. A property of the gas's state.
  • Heating: energy crosses the boundary because of a temperature difference.
  • Work: energy crosses the boundary because a force moves it (a piston).
  • Heat and work are energy in transit. A gas does not "contain" heat or work; it contains internal energy.
  • Isolated system: no energy in or out, so its total energy is constant.
Mission 1

The first law

  • Conservation of energy for a gas: change in U = energy in by heating + work done on it.
  • ΔU = Q + W. Q: energy transferred into the gas by heating. W: work done on the gas.
Quick check
?A gas in a cylinder is compressed by a piston. During the compression, 250 J of work is done on the gas, and 400 J of energy is transferred from the gas to its surroundings by cooling. What is the change in the internal energy of the gas?
Mission 1

Work done by a gas

  • Gas at constant pressure pushes a piston of area A a distance Δx.
  • Work = force × distance = PA × Δx = PΔV (P = pressure).
  • Work done on the gas: W = −PΔV. Expansion: W < 0. Compression: W > 0.
  • Units: Pa × m³ = N/m² × m³ = J.
Mission 1

Work as an area

  • On a pressure–volume graph, the work done = area under the graph, between the start and end volumes.
  • Constant pressure: a rectangle, pressure × ΔV.
  • Changing pressure: count squares, or use a trapezoid for a straight line.
  • Expansion (moving right): work done by the gas. Compression (moving left): work done on the gas.
  • |W| = area under the curve. Sign from the direction: expansion, W < 0.
  • Curves of constant temperature are isotherms (PV = constant).
Mission 1

Worked example: heating at constant pressure

  • A monatomic ideal gas at 2.0 × 10⁵ Pa expands from 1.0 × 10⁻³ m³ to 2.5 × 10⁻³ m³. Pressure stays constant.
  • PΔV = 2.0 × 10⁵ × 1.5 × 10⁻³ = 300 J of work done by the gas.
  • nRΔT = Δ(PV) = PΔV = 300 J, so ΔU = (3/2)nRΔT = 450 J.
  • W = −PΔV = −300 J. ΔU = Q + W: 450 = Q − 300 → Q = 750 J.
Quick check
?The P–V diagram shows a process in which a gas expands from state 1 to state 2 along a straight line. Which expression gives the work W done on the gas during the process?
Mission 1

Reading the signs

  • Negative Q: the gas loses energy by heating its surroundings.
  • Negative ΔU: internal energy falls, so the temperature of an ideal gas falls.
  • Negative W: the gas does work on its surroundings (it expands).
  • Constant volume: no work, so all the heat supplied goes into U.
Quick check
?A sample of 8.0 g of helium, a monatomic ideal gas of molar mass 4.0 g/mol, is heated at constant pressure from 20 °C to 80 °C. The thermal energy supplied is 2.49 × 10³ J. R = 8.31 J/(mol·K). What is the change in internal energy ΔU of the helium?
Mission check

?An ideal gas is in a cylinder whose walls and piston are perfectly insulated. The gas expands, pushing the piston outward. What happens to the temperature of the gas?

Energy in = energy out + change stored. Push a piston in and you do work on the gas: its internal energy, and so its temperature, rises. Let it push out with no heating, and it cools.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Let a gas expand slowly or suddenly to the same volume. Which ends up colder, and why?

Make a guess: Let a gas expand slowly or suddenly to the same volume. Which ends up colder, and why?

Mission 2

Four special processes

  • Keep one thing fixed:
  • Isovolumetric: volume constant. A vertical line on a P–V diagram.
  • Isobaric: pressure constant. A horizontal line.
  • Isothermal: temperature constant. A curve, PV = constant (an isotherm). Needs a slow change with good thermal contact.
  • Adiabatic: no energy transferred by heating (Q = 0). Perfect insulation, or a change so fast that heating has no time to happen.
Quick check
?An ideal gas in a cylinder is compressed slowly, so that its temperature stays constant. The work done on the gas during the compression is W, which is positive. Which statement about the energy transferred by heating or cooling is correct?
Mission 2

The first law in each process

  • Recall: ΔU = Q + W, W done on the gas = −PΔV.
  • Isovolumetric: W = 0, so all heating changes U.
  • Isobaric: work and a change in U together.
  • Isothermal (ideal gas): T fixed, so ΔU = 0.
  • Isothermal: Q = −W. Energy in by heating = work done by the gas on its surroundings.
  • Adiabatic: Q = 0.
  • Adiabatic: ΔU = W. Compression (W > 0): U and T rise. Expansion: T falls.
Quick check
?The pressure–volume diagram shows two processes in which a sample of ideal gas expands from the same initial state i to the same final volume Vf. Process 1 is isothermal and process 2 is adiabatic. Which claim is supported by the diagram and the first law of thermodynamics?
Mission 2

Isotherms and adiabats

  • Isotherms: PV = constant curves. Hotter isotherms lie further from the origin.
  • An adiabat through the same point is steeper than the isotherm.
  • Adiabatic expansion: P falls faster, because T falls too. The gas ends on a cooler isotherm.
  • Adiabatic compression: the gas ends on a hotter isotherm.
  • Work = area under the curve. From the same start to the same final volume, the isothermal expansion does more work (bigger area).
Quick check
?The pressure–volume diagram shows a sample of ideal gas taken from state X to state Y along the path X → Z → Y. The dashed curve is an isotherm. Which claim about the temperatures TX and TY, with its evidence, is correct?
Mission check

?Two identical samples of ideal gas start in the same state. Each is compressed to half its volume: one slowly, in good thermal contact with a water bath (isothermal); the other quickly, in an insulated cylinder (adiabatic). Which statement is correct?

In an adiabatic change no energy is exchanged by heating, so the work changes U. A sudden expansion is nearly adiabatic: the work done by the gas comes only from U, so it cools. A slow one is topped up by heating (isothermal).

Mission 3

Mission 3: Under the same Sun, beach sand gets scorching while the sea stays cool. Why?

Make a guess: Under the same Sun, beach sand gets scorching while the sea stays cool. Why?

Mission 3

Specific heat

  • Specific heat, c: energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg by 1 K. Unit: J/(kg·K).
  • Q = mcΔT
  • Water: c ≈ 4200 J/(kg·K). Dry sand: about 800. Same energy per kg → water warms about one fifth as much.
  • ΔT in °C = ΔT in K. No conversion needed.
Mission 3

Worked example

  • 0.500 kg aluminum block, 20 °C → 45 °C. c = 900 J/(kg·K).
  • Temperature rise: 45 − 20 = 25 K.
  • Energy: 0.500 × 900 × 25 = 1.1 × 10⁴ J (11 250 J).
  • With a 50 W heater and no losses: t = 11 250 ÷ 50 = 225 s.
Quick check
?An electric kettle heats 250 g of water from 18 °C to 90 °C. The specific heat of water is 4.18 × 10³ J/(kg·K). How much energy is transferred to the water? Ignore energy transferred to the kettle and surroundings.
Mission 3

Why c differs between materials

  • Specific heat is an intrinsic property: it depends on the material, not on the size of the sample.
  • It is set by how the atoms are arranged and how they interact: the ways the atoms can store energy.
  • Lighter atoms → more atoms per kilogram → usually a larger specific heat.
  • Same molecules, different arrangement, different c: liquid water ≈ 4200 J/(kg·K), ice ≈ 2100 J/(kg·K).
Mission check

?0.20 kg of water and 0.20 kg of cooking oil, both at 20 °C, are each supplied with 12 kJ of energy. Specific heat capacities: water 4180 J/(kg·K), oil 2000 J/(kg·K). Assume no energy is lost. Which statement about their final temperatures is correct?

Same energy, same mass: the lower c, the bigger temperature rise. Sand's c is about a fifth of water's, so in the same sunshine each kilogram of sand warms about five times as much. The sea also spreads its energy through meters of mixing water and loses some by evaporation; dry sand keeps it in a thin top layer.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Water freezes into a neat crystal. How is that allowed if disorder can never decrease overall?

Make a guess: Water freezes into a neat crystal. How is that allowed if disorder can never decrease overall?

Quick check
?A moving car brakes to a stop. Treat the car, its brakes, the road and the air as an isolated system. Which statement correctly compares the system after the car stops with the system before?
Mission 4

What entropy measures

  • Entropy, S: describes how spread out a system's energy is. The more spread out, the less of it is available to do work.
  • More ways to arrange the particles and share out their energy → higher entropy.
  • Solid → liquid → gas: entropy rises.
  • Localized energy tends to spread out: a hot spot in a metal bar warms its surroundings, never the reverse.
  • Unit: J/K. Entropy is not conserved.
Quick check
?A sealed, insulated box is divided by a partition. One side contains a gas, and the other side is evacuated. The partition is removed, and the gas spreads through the box. At which of the following times is the entropy of the gas greatest?
Mission 4

The second law

  • Isolated system: no matter and no energy cross its boundary.
  • Second law: the total entropy of an isolated system never decreases.
  • Reversible process (an ideal limit): entropy stays constant.
  • Irreversible process (every real one): entropy increases.
  • An isolated system moves toward thermodynamic equilibrium, where its entropy is a maximum.
  • Why heat flows hot → cold: energy spreads from the hot body into the cold one, so the total entropy rises. The reverse would concentrate energy: never seen.
Quick check
?Two identical samples of ideal gas start in the same state. Sample 1 expands slowly at constant temperature, pushing a piston, until its volume has doubled. Sample 2 is in an insulated container and expands freely into an evacuated region until its volume has doubled; its temperature does not change. How do the entropy changes of the two samples compare?
Mission 4

Entropy is a state function

  • Entropy depends only on the current state (P, V, T) of a system, not on how it got there.
  • Same start and same end → same ΔS, by any route.
  • A gas taken round a full cycle back to its start: ΔS = 0 for the gas.
Mission 4

Local decreases are allowed

  • A system that exchanges energy with its surroundings is not isolated. Its entropy can decrease.
  • Freezing water: energy leaves the water, so the water's entropy falls.
  • The freezer passes that energy, plus the work done by its motor, to the kitchen.
  • The kitchen's entropy rises by at least as much as the water's falls. Total: never down.
  • Living things build ordered molecules in the same way: they pass energy and entropy to their surroundings.
Quick check
?A covered cup of hot coffee is left on a table and cools to room temperature. Taking the coffee alone as the system, which numbered graph could show the entropy S of the coffee as a function of time t?
Mission check

?A gas in a cylinder is in good thermal contact with a large water bath. The gas is compressed very slowly, so its temperature stays constant. Which statement about the entropy changes is correct?

Only an isolated system's entropy can never fall. Freezing water gives energy to its surroundings, and their entropy rises by at least as much as the water's falls.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Heat and work are energy in transit; the first law is conservation of energy.
  • ΔU = Q + W, W done on the gas = −PΔV; |W| = area under the P–V curve.

Mission 2

  • Isovolumetric (V fixed), isobaric (P fixed), isothermal (T fixed, ΔU = 0), adiabatic (Q = 0).
  • Adiabatic compression warms a gas; adiabatic expansion cools it. Adiabats are steeper than isotherms.

Mission 3

  • Q = mcΔT. Same energy and mass: lower c, bigger temperature rise.

Mission 4

  • Entropy measures how spread out energy is; it is a state function, greatest at equilibrium.
  • The entropy of an isolated system never decreases: constant if reversible, rising if irreversible. A system that exchanges energy can lose entropy if its surroundings gain at least as much.
