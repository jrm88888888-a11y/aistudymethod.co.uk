Mission 1

Mission 1: Guyana's income per person is more than three and a half times Sri Lanka's. Why do they have exactly the same HDI?

Make a guess: Guyana's income per person is more than three and a half times Sri Lanka's. Why do they have exactly the same HDI?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA GCSE Geography

  • 2C.1 describe MEDC–LEDC differences using social and economic indicators
  • 2C.1 evaluate social and economic indicators; advantages of the HDI
Mission 1

What is development?

  • Development: improvement in people's lives and in a country's economy.
  • Economic view: how much wealth and income a country has.
  • Social view: health, education, housing, food and water.
  • Political view: freedom, rights and how honestly a country is governed.
Mission 1

Classifying countries

  • The World Bank groups countries by GNI per head (2025 data, used from July 2026):
    • low income: $1,175 or less · lower-middle: $1,176–$4,635 · upper-middle: $4,636–$14,375 · high: more than $14,375
  • The UK is high income, India lower-middle income and Rwanda low income.
  • CCEA uses MEDCs (more economically developed) and LEDCs (less economically developed).
Mission 1

Economic measures

  • GDP (gross domestic product): the value of everything produced inside a country in a year.
  • GNI (gross national income): GDP plus income earned abroad by its people and firms, minus income sent out by foreign firms.
  • Divide by population to get per head (per capita) figures.
  • GNI per head, 2025: UK $54,550, India $2,760, Rwanda $1,150.

Mission 1

The Human Development Index (HDI)

  • The HDI combines three dimensions into one score from 0 to 1:
    • a long and healthy life (life expectancy)
    • knowledge (years of schooling)
    • a decent standard of living (GNI per head)
  • 2023: UK 0.946 (13th), India 0.685 (130th), Rwanda 0.578 (159th) of 193 countries.
  • Advantages of the HDI: it combines social and economic measures; one number is easy to compare; nearly every country has a value.

Mission 1

Why income cannot buy a high HDI

  • In the HDI, each extra dollar counts for less and less: income is scored on a logarithmic scale, and any income above $75,000 counts the same as $75,000.
  • The three dimension scores are multiplied together (then the cube root is taken), not simply averaged. A weak score pulls the total down, and a strong one cannot fully make up for it.
  • So Guyana's high income is held back by its lower health and education scores.
Mission 1

Limitations of measures

  • Averages hide inequality: GNI per head says nothing about how income is shared.
  • Money is not well-being: Guyana's real GDP grew 43.8% in 2024 after oil and gas production began; health and schooling change much more slowly.
  • Data quality: the World Bank lists India's adult literacy as 81.7% for 2023 but 78.2% for 2024; different surveys can give different answers.
  • Missing parts: no single measure covers rights, safety or the environment; even the HDI leaves them out.
  • Single measures (GNI) are simple; composite ones (HDI) are broader but still incomplete.
Mission 1

Exam move: evaluating a measure

  • Say what it measures, give a strength and a limitation, and use data from named countries.
  • Finish with a judgement: use more than one measure, because each shows only part of development.
  • For the HDI: broader than GNI alone, but still an average that leaves out inequality and rights.
Mission check

?Country A's income per head is four times Country B's, yet Country A has a lower HDI. What is the most likely reason?

Same HDI, different lives. Sri Lanka: life expectancy 77.5, mean schooling 10.8 years. Guyana: 70.2 and 8.7, but income of $46,959 against $12,616. Because extra income counts for less and less and the three scores are multiplied, one strong dimension cannot buy a high HDI.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why does a baby born in Norway expect to live about 25 years longer than one born in Somalia?

Make a guess: Why does a baby born in Norway expect to live about 25 years longer than one born in Somalia?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA GCSE Geography

  • 2C.1 factors hindering LEDC development: historical, environmental, dependence on primary activities, debt
Mission 2

The development gap

  • The development gap is the difference in wealth and wellbeing between the richest and poorest countries.
  • UNDP's Human Development Index (2023): Iceland is top at 0.972; South Sudan is bottom of 193 countries at 0.388.
  • Life expectancy at birth: Norway 83.3 years, Somalia 58.8, a gap of about 24.5 years (our calculation).
  • CCEA uses MEDC and LEDC.
Mission 2

Physical factors

  • Your specification calls these environmental factors.
  • Landlocked: no coast, so trade goes through neighbours. Landlocked developing countries paid $3,204 to export a container against $1,268 for transit countries (World Bank data, 2014).
  • The UN estimates their development is "about 20 percent lower than it would be were they not landlocked".
  • Disease: in 2024 the WHO African Region had 95% of the world's malaria cases and deaths. Illness keeps children out of school and adults out of work.
  • Hazards and climate: drought, floods and tropical storms destroy crops, homes and roads again and again.
Mission 2

Historical factors

  • Colonialism: from the 1500s to the mid-1900s, European powers ruled much of Africa, Asia and the Americas.
  • Colonies were organised to export raw materials (cotton, rubber, minerals) to the ruling country, which made the profitable goods.
  • Railways and ports often ran from mines and plantations to the coast, not between towns.
  • Many countries became independent with few factories, few trained officials and borders drawn by others.
Quick check
?Which is a physical (environmental) factor that holds back development?
Mission 2

Economic: relying on primary products

  • CCEA: dependence on primary activities.
  • Primary products: raw materials such as crops, minerals and oil, sold unprocessed.
  • UNCTAD counts a country as commodity-dependent when commodities make up more than 60% of its goods exports: 95 of 142 developing countries were, in 2019–2021. In Africa it was 45 of 53.
  • Raw materials earn less than processed goods, and their prices rise and fall sharply, so income is hard to plan.
Mission 2

Debt

  • Poor countries borrow from banks, governments and international lenders to build roads, dams and schools.
  • If export earnings fall, repayments and interest take money that could pay for health and education.
  • The HIPC debt-relief scheme: of 39 eligible countries, 36 had completed it by February 2023; Somalia became the 37th in December 2023.
  • Debt can trap a country in poverty (a poverty trap), because each year's repayments leave less to invest.
Quick check
?Which sequence best describes a poverty trap?
Mission check

?A landlocked country earns most of its export income from one crop, coffee. The world coffee price halves, and the country owes large debts in US dollars. What is the most likely result?

Uneven development has many linked causes: physical (landlocked, disease, hazards), historical (colonialism) and economic (primary products, debt). They add up, which is why a baby in Somalia expects to live about 24.5 years less than one in Norway.

Mission 3

Mission 3: In 1798, Thomas Malthus argued that population would outrun food. Since 1961, the world's population has grown about 2.6 times. Has food per person risen or fallen?

Make a guess: In 1798, Thomas Malthus argued that population would outrun food. Since 1961, the world's population has grown about 2.6 times. Has food per person risen or fallen?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA GCSE Geography

  • 2C.2 how any three Sustainable Development Goals attempt to reduce the development gap
Mission 3

The numbers since 1961

  • World population: 3.06 billion (1961) → 8.06 billion (2023).
  • World cereal yield: 1,422 → 4,229 kg per hectare.
  • Food supply per person: 2,196 kcal a day (1961) → 2,884 kcal a day (2013).
  • Yet in 2025, 645 million people were still chronically undernourished.
Quick check
?Food supply rose from 2,196 to 2,884 kcal per person per day (1961–2013). What was the percentage increase?
Mission 3

What is sustainable development?

  • The Brundtland Report (UN, 1987): development that "meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs".
  • Three parts must work together: social (people), economic (jobs and income) and environmental (nature).
  • Example: more food today is not sustainable if it ruins the soil for tomorrow.
Mission 3

The Sustainable Development Goals

  • In 2015, UN member states agreed 17 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), with 169 targets, for 2030.
  • They apply to every country, but many targets focus on the poorest people and countries.
  • They try to close the development gap with shared targets, data to track progress, and international cooperation and funding.
Mission 3

SDG 2: Zero hunger

  • Goal: "End hunger, achieve food security and improved nutrition and promote sustainable agriculture".
  • Target 2.1: by 2030, ensure access "by all people, in particular the poor" to "safe, nutritious and sufficient food all year round".
  • Target 2.3: "double the agricultural productivity and incomes of small-scale food producers".
  • The gap: 645 million people chronically undernourished in 2025.
Mission 3

SDG 6: Clean water and sanitation

  • Goal: "Ensure availability and sustainable management of water and sanitation for all".
  • Target 6.1: "universal and equitable access to safe and affordable drinking water for all" by 2030.
  • Target 6.a: more international cooperation and support for developing countries.
  • The gap: about 2.1 billion people lack safely managed drinking water.
Mission 3

SDG 7: Affordable and clean energy

  • Goal: "Ensure access to affordable, reliable, sustainable and modern energy for all".
  • Target 7.1: "universal access to affordable, reliable and modern energy services" by 2030.
  • Target 7.b: expand energy infrastructure, "in particular least developed countries".
  • The gap: 655 million people had no electricity in 2024; 86% of them lived in sub-Saharan Africa.
Quick check
?How does SDG 7 try to reduce the development gap?
Mission 3

Are the SDGs working?

  • Of 139 targets with enough data (2026): 15% on track, 21% moderate progress, 49% too slow, 15% worse than in 2015.
  • Aid from rich countries "collapsed by a record 23 per cent in 2025".
  • Strength: shared targets and data. Weakness: goals are not binding, and funding is falling.
Mission check

?In an imaginary country, population doubled between 1990 and 2020, while food production tripled. What happened to food per person?

Since 1961, population grew about 2.6 times, but yields roughly tripled, so food per person rose (2,196 → 2,884 kcal a day, 1961–2013). Yet 645 million people are still undernourished: enough food on average does not mean food for all.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Women in rural Senegal got ovens that cook with free sunshine. Why did most still cook on wood fires?

Make a guess: Women in rural Senegal got ovens that cook with free sunshine. Why did most still cook on wood fires?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA GCSE Geography

  • 2C.2 define appropriate technology
  • 2C.2 describe and evaluate one appropriate technology product (e.g. solar cooker, Hippo Water Roller)
Mission 4

The problem

  • Around 2.1 billion people cook on open fires or inefficient stoves burning wood, dung, crop waste, kerosene or coal.
  • The smoke causes household air pollution: an estimated 2.9 million deaths in 2021.
  • Women and children "bear the greatest health burden", and collecting fuel takes "significant time".
Mission 4

What is appropriate technology?

  • Appropriate technology: technology suited to the needs, skills, money and resources of the people who will use it.
  • The idea comes from the economist E.F. Schumacher, who believed in "solutions suited to context". In 1966 he co-founded the Intermediate Technology Development Group, now Practical Action.
  • It is usually small-scale, affordable and easy to repair with local skills and materials.
Mission 4

How to test if a technology is appropriate

  • Need: does it solve a problem people actually have?
  • Cost: can local people afford to buy and run it?
  • Skills and materials: can it be made, used and repaired locally?
  • Fit: does it suit how people already live and work?
  • Environment: does it cut pollution or resource use?
Quick check
?A village needs clean water. Which choice is the most appropriate technology?
Mission 4

The Hippo Water Roller

  • A barrel with a handle that is rolled along the ground instead of carried.
  • It holds 90 litres at a time; the alternative is "heavy 25-litre buckets", often carried several times a day.
  • The maker reports 65,000 rollers in 56 countries: the organisation's own figures.
  • Strengths: more water per trip, less weight on the head and neck. Limits: harder to use on steep, rough or sandy paths, and it carries water but does not make it safe to drink.
Mission 4

The solar cooker

  • A box or panel that reflects and traps sunlight to heat a cooking pot.
  • No fuel to buy or collect, and no smoke.
  • On paper it meets the need, cost and environment tests. The open question is fit: will families actually use it?
Mission 4

A fair test in Senegal

  • Researchers ran a randomised controlled trial among women in rural Senegal who were interested in buying a solar oven.
  • 465 households got an oven first (the treatment group); 325 got theirs 6 months later (the control group).
  • Because the groups were chosen at random, differences between them show the oven's real effect.
Mission 4

What happened

  • Families with an oven used it on only 19% of days measured.
  • They did not use significantly less fuel or spend less time collecting it.
  • There was no evidence of less carbon monoxide exposure or fewer coughs and sore throats.
  • One exception: households cooking for 7–12 people cut their wood use for cooking by 14%.
Quick check
?The ovens were used on 19% of days measured. What does this mean?
Mission 4

Why was it so little used?

  • 80% of the women usually cooked for more people than the oven could hold, so they kept using their old stove too.
  • A one-pot oven could not replace the three-stone fire for lunch, which involved several pots, cooks and burners.
  • The technology met a real need but did not fit how families cook.
Mission 4

Was the trial a failure?

  • The authors call the evaluation "a policy success": its results halted a planned nationwide rollout of the oven.
  • That avoided spending on a stove that could not cut indoor air pollution or fuel use much.
  • Their advice for designers: stoves should be affordable, durable, locally appropriate, suit current cooking (e.g. two burners) and be large enough for big households.
Quick check
?Which change would most likely make families in the trial use a solar cooker more often?
Mission 4

Evaluating an appropriate technology

  • Successes: no fuel, no smoke; bigger households used less wood.
  • Failures: used on only 19% of days; no significant cut in fuel use or air pollution exposure.
  • Judgement: a technology is only appropriate if it fits local needs and ways of life, not just local sunshine.
  • Exam move: "evaluate" means weigh success against failure with evidence, then give a judgement.
Mission check

?A charity gives a small one-pot solar cooker to families who cook their main meal after dark, in several large pots, for ten people. What is the most likely result?

In Senegal the ovens were used on only 19% of days: 80% of the women cooked for more people than the oven could hold, and one pot could not replace a three-stone fire with several pots. A technology is appropriate only when it fits local needs, skills, money and ways of life.

Mission 5: In 2006 a bank for poor villagers won the Nobel Peace Prize. Do small loans and fairer prices really lift people out of poverty?

Make a guess: In 2006 a bank for poor villagers won the Nobel Peace Prize. Do small loans and fairer prices really lift people out of poverty?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA GCSE Geography

  • 2C.2 fair trade and the advantages it brings to LEDCs
Mission 5

Microfinance

  • In 2006 Muhammad Yunus and Grameen Bank (Bangladesh) won the Nobel Peace Prize "for their efforts to create economic and social development from below".
  • Microfinance: small loans, savings and insurance for people that ordinary banks will not serve.
  • In 2018, microfinance lenders had 139.9 million borrowers: 80% women, 65% in rural areas.
Mission 5

Does microfinance work?

  • Six randomised trials in six countries on four continents found "a consistent pattern of modestly positive, but not transformative, effects".
  • In Andhra Pradesh, India, rapid lending left many households over-indebted: by 2010, 83% had loans from more than one source.
  • So small loans help some families run businesses and manage money, but do not on their own lift large numbers out of poverty.
Quick check
?What do the six randomised trials suggest about microfinance?
Mission 5

Fair trade: how it works

  • Fair trade: a system that gives producers in poorer countries a fairer, more stable deal.
  • Minimum price: a floor paid when world prices fall. For washed Arabica coffee it rose from $1.40 to $1.80 per pound for contracts from 1 August 2023.
  • Fairtrade Premium: an extra sum that producer groups decide together how to spend.
  • In 2024 Fairtrade covered 1.87 million farmers and workers and paid €201.6 million in Premium.
Mission 5

The benefits for producers

  • A safety net: the minimum price protects income when world prices crash.
  • Investment: in 2024 small-scale producer groups spent 35% of their Premium on improving production and farming, and 19% on payments to farmers.
  • Communities: workers on certified plantations put 67% of their Premium into social investments.
  • Reach: 75% of Fairtrade farmers and workers were in Africa and the Middle East.
  • Exam move: name the advantages to LEDC producers: stable prices, the Premium, and investment in farms and communities.
Mission 5

The limits

  • In 2024 only about 27% of reported certified coffee production was sold on Fairtrade terms; the rest was sold at ordinary prices.
  • Fair Trade coffee was 1.8% of world coffee exports in 2009: a small share of trade.
  • A study in Ethiopia and Uganda found that, in some cases, hired workers in areas with Fairtrade groups were "significantly worse paid" than elsewhere.
  • Fairtrade itself says its Premium data do not show "outcomes or impacts".
Quick check
?Which is a benefit of fair trade for producers?
Mission check

?A charity starts offering small loans and a guaranteed minimum price to cocoa farmers in a new region. What is the most realistic expectation?

Modestly. Microcredit's effects are "modestly positive, but not transformative", and too much lending caused a crisis in Andhra Pradesh. Fairtrade sets a floor price and pays a Premium, but only about 27% of certified coffee was sold on Fairtrade terms in 2024. Both are tools, not cures.

Mission 6

Mission 6: The Netherlands tops the KOF Globalisation Index. The USA, the world's largest economy at market exchange rates, ranks 28th. How can that be?

Make a guess: The Netherlands tops the KOF Globalisation Index. The USA, the world's largest economy at market exchange rates, ranks 28th. How can that be?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA GCSE Geography

  • 2C.3 meaning of the term globalisation
Mission 6

What is globalisation?

  • Globalisation: the growing connections and interdependence between places, through flows of:
    • goods and services (trade)
    • money (investment, loans, aid)
    • people (migrants, tourists, students)
    • ideas and information (internet, media, culture).
  • Places become linked so that events in one place affect others far away.
Mission 6

Measuring it: the KOF index

  • The KOF Globalisation Index (ETH Zurich) scores 195 countries using 42 variables.
  • Three dimensions: economic (trade, investment), social (people, information, culture) and political (embassies, treaties, international organisations).
  • 2025 ranking: 1st Netherlands, 2nd Switzerland, 3rd Belgium, 4th UK. Singapore 17th, USA 28th, China 82nd, India 94th.
Quick check
?The KOF index counts the number of McDonald's restaurants per person in each country. Which dimension of globalisation does this measure?
Mission 6

Why size matters

  • Most KOF variables are ratios: trade is measured as exports and imports "(% of GDP)".
  • A small, open economy, such as the Netherlands, trades a lot compared with its size.
  • The USA trades a huge amount in total, but its "large internal markets reduce the relative share of international flows".
  • So the index measures how connected a country is, not how big it is.
Mission 6

Key features of globalisation

  • Global trade in goods and services keeps growing.
  • Transnational corporations (TNCs) produce and sell in many countries; foreign direct investment reached $1.6 trillion in 2025.
  • Fast, cheap transport and instant communications.
  • Migration and tourism move people; global brands and media spread culture.
  • International organisations set rules, e.g. for trade.
Mission check

?Belgium ranks 3rd on the KOF index; China, a far bigger economy, ranks 82nd. Which is the best explanation?

KOF mostly measures flows relative to size (% of GDP). The Netherlands' trade is huge for its size; the USA's is huge in total but small compared with its vast home market. Globalisation is about connection and interdependence, not size.

Mission 7

Mission 7: India now has more people than any other country. Why is it still only a lower-middle-income country?

Make a guess: India now has more people than any other country. Why is it still only a lower-middle-income country?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA GCSE Geography

  • 2C.3 how globalisation both helps and hinders development, one BRICS country case study
Mission 7

Where is India?

  • In South Asia: the Himalayas to the north, the Indian Ocean to the south, between the Arabian Sea and the Bay of Bengal.
  • 1.46 billion people in 2025, more than China's 1.41 billion.
  • India has about 87% of South Asia's people and 86% of its GDP (our calculation).
Mission 7

Why India matters globally

  • The world's 6th-largest economy in 2025 at market exchange rates: about US$3.9 trillion (World Bank US$3.96 trillion; IMF US$3.92 trillion), about 3% of world GDP (our calculation).
  • A member of BRICS, which it chairs in 2026.
  • Mumbai is a global financial centre: home of the Reserve Bank of India since 1937.
  • But income per person (GNI) is only US$2,760 (2025): "lower middle income".
  • So India is a BRICS country: the type of case study CCEA asks for.
Quick check
?India has the world's 6th-largest economy but a GNI per person of only US$2,760. Which statement explains both facts?
Mission 7

Political context

  • A federal country: 36 states and union territories and 707 districts.
  • Central and state governments share power, so the same policy can work differently in different states.
  • National programmes set by the central government: e.g. Make in India (2014) to grow manufacturing.
  • Member of BRICS: links to Brazil, China, Russia, South Africa and others.
Mission 7

Social and cultural context

  • Young: 24% of people were under 15 in 2025.
  • Smaller families: about 2.0 births per woman (2024), down from 3.7 in 1995.
  • Still mostly rural: 36% lived in towns and cities in 2025.
  • Education: 76% of adults could read in 2022.
  • Culture: a mix of influences, e.g. Mumbai's World Heritage buildings "blend Indian design with Art Deco imagery".
Mission 7

Environmental context

  • Monsoon: most rain falls in a few months. At Patna, 76% of the Ganges' yearly flow came in the monsoon months (1980).
  • Farming needs water: 90% of India's water withdrawals go to agriculture (2022).
  • Cyclones hit the coasts; October–December is the main cyclone season.
Quick check
?Which is part of India's environmental context?
Mission 7

Globalisation helps

  • Investment: foreign direct investment rose from US$3.6bn (2000) to US$40.8bn (2025).
  • Trade: exports rose from 7% of GDP (1990) to 22% (2025).
  • Jobs and skills: Apple's partners make products in India.
  • Poverty: people below US$3 a day fell from 27% (2011) to 3% (2023) (globalisation is one of several causes).
Mission 7

Globalisation hinders

  • Unstable flows: FDI was US$64.4bn in 2020 but US$27.1bn in 2024.
  • Uneven gains: services make 49% of output but give 33% of jobs; 42% of workers stay in farming, producing only 16% of output.
  • Inequality: one study finds the top 1% received 22.6% of national income in 2022–23, the highest in its series since 1922.
  • Regional gaps: multidimensional poverty is 33.76% in Bihar but 7.81% in Maharashtra: gains have not spread evenly.
  • Exam move (CCEA "Explain"): give one way globalisation helps and one way it hinders, each with a fact.
Mission check

?Country V has the world's 10th-largest economy but is still middle income. Its population is very large, and 45% of its workers farm small plots that produce 15% of output. Which change would do most to raise V's income per person?

India is the 6th-largest economy but lower-middle income: 1.46 billion people share the output, and 42% of workers are in farming, producing 16% of output.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Development is economic, social and political; measures include GNI per head, birth and death rates, infant mortality, life expectancy, doctors, literacy, water and the HDI.
  • Every measure has limits (averages, data quality, missing dimensions), so use several.

Mission 2

  • Causes: physical/environmental, historical (colonialism) and economic (primary products, debt).

Mission 3

  • Food per person has risen since 1961, but hunger remains.
  • SDGs 2, 6 and 7 set targets for food, water and energy for all, with help for the poorest countries.

Mission 4

  • Appropriate technology suits the needs, skills, money and resources of local people: small-scale, affordable, repairable.
  • Evaluation needs evidence: in a randomised trial in Senegal, solar ovens were used on 19% of days and did not significantly cut fuel use, because they did not fit how families cook.

Mission 5

  • Microfinance and fair trade bring modest, real benefits (stable prices, Premium, small loans for women), but reach only part of trade and need careful lending.

Mission 6

  • Globalisation: growing connections and interdependence through flows of goods, money, people and ideas.
  • Indices such as KOF measure it with economic, social and political variables, mostly relative to size.

Mission 7

  • India: 1.46 billion people (about 87% of South Asia's), 6th-largest economy, but GNI per person US$2,760.
  • Context: federal (36 states/UTs); young; mostly rural; monsoon-dependent farming.
  • Globalisation brought investment and exports but unstable flows and uneven gains.
