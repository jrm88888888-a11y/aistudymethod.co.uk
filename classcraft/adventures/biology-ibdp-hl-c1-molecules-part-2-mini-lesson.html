Mission 1

Mission 1: Burning sugar releases all its energy in one flash. How do your cells release it slowly enough to use?

Make a guess: Burning sugar releases all its energy in one flash. How do your cells release it slowly enough to use?

Mission 1

Why every cell respires

  • Respiration releases energy from carbon compounds and transfers it to ATP.
  • It runs in every living cell, day and night: animals, plants, fungi, bacteria.
  • Autotrophs respire too. Photosynthesis makes their sugar; respiration releases its energy.
  • Main substrates: glucose and fatty acids. Many other carbon compounds can be used.
  • Not the same as gas exchange. Breathing moves gases; respiration is a chemical process inside cells.
  • Say: "respiration releases energy" — not: "respiration produces energy".
Quick check
?Which organisms release energy by the oxidation of carbon compounds in cell respiration?
Mission 1

Small steps, not one flash

  • Overall (aerobic): glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water.
  • Burning releases the energy all at once, as heat and light.
  • Respiration releases the same total energy, but in many small steps.
  • Each step is catalysed by its own specific enzyme, inside the cell.
  • At some steps, part of the energy is captured in ATP. The rest is released as heat.
  • No energy transfer is 100% efficient, so heat is inevitable. Mammals and birds use it to keep a constant body temperature.
  • Say: "energy is transferred to ATP" — not: "glucose is turned into energy".
Mission 1

Two routes from glucose

  • First stage, glycolysis: glucose → 2 pyruvate, in the cytoplasm. No oxygen needed.
  • With oxygen: pyruvate enters the mitochondria and is oxidised completely to carbon dioxide and water. Far more ATP.
  • The mitochondrial stages: link reaction and Krebs cycle (in the matrix), then oxidative phosphorylation (on the inner membrane).
  • Without oxygen: pyruvate stays in the cytoplasm. In human muscle it becomes lactate.
  • In yeast it becomes ethanol and carbon dioxide.

Mission 1

Glycolysis in four steps

  • (1) Phosphorylation: 2 ATP add two phosphate groups to glucose. The sugar becomes more reactive.
  • (2) Lysis: the 6-carbon sugar splits into two 3-carbon triose phosphate molecules.
  • (3) Oxidation: hydrogen is removed from each triose phosphate. NAD accepts it and becomes reduced NAD.
  • (4) ATP formation: each 3-carbon molecule becomes pyruvate, forming 2 ATP.
  • Net per glucose: 4 ATP made − 2 used = 2 ATP, plus 2 reduced NAD and 2 pyruvate.
  • Each step has its own enzyme. All in the cytoplasm; no oxygen used.
  • Say: "phosphorylating glucose uses ATP" — not: "it makes ATP".
Mission 1

NAD carries hydrogen

  • Oxidation = loss of electrons. Reduction = gain of electrons.
  • Removing hydrogen (with its electron) is dehydrogenation. It oxidises the substrate, with no oxygen involved.
  • NAD accepts the hydrogen, so NAD is reduced. Both happen together: a redox reaction.
  • With oxygen, the hydrogen (its electrons) from reduced NAD is passed into the mitochondria, where it is used to make far more ATP.
  • Say: "NAD is reduced when it gains hydrogen" — not: "NAD is oxidised when it gains hydrogen".
Quick check
?During glycolysis of one glucose molecule, two ATP are used in phosphorylation and four ATP are formed later. What is the net yield per glucose?
Quick check
?In one reaction of the Krebs cycle, two hydrogen atoms (each with an electron) are removed from a substrate and accepted by NAD. Which statement describes this reaction?
Mission check

?Aerobic respiration of one glucose molecule yields many times more ATP (roughly 30) than anaerobic respiration (2). Which statement explains the difference?

Energy comes out of glucose a step at a time. Stop after glycolysis and most of it is still locked in the product.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How can a drug that once cured an infection stop working against the very same germ?

Make a guess: How can a drug that once cured an infection stop working against the very same germ?

Mission 2

Hit what only bacteria have

  • An antibiotic: a chemical that kills bacteria or stops them growing.
  • It blocks a process found in bacteria but not in eukaryotic cells.
  • Penicillin: blocks the enzymes that cross-link peptidoglycan in the bacterial cell wall.
  • Tetracycline: binds bacterial 70S ribosomes; protein synthesis stops.
  • Human cells: no peptidoglycan wall; 80S ribosomes in the cytoplasm.
  • So the drug has a target in the bacterium and none in our cells: selective toxicity.
Mission 2

Why viruses escape antibiotics

  • A virus has no cell wall, no ribosomes, no metabolism of its own.
  • It replicates using the host cell's processes.
  • Antibiotics block none of these, at any dose.
  • Antibiotics for a cold or flu: no benefit, but still selection for resistant bacteria.
  • Say: "viruses have no bacterial processes for the drug to block" — not: "viruses are too small for the drug".
Quick check
?Why do antibiotics fail to control infections caused by viruses?
Mission 2

Penicillin and tetracycline up close

  • Penicillin acts on growing bacteria, as they build new wall.
  • It binds the transpeptidase active site irreversibly and changes it chemically: mechanism-based inhibition.
  • Resistance: a mutated transpeptidase that still cross-links peptidoglycan but no longer binds penicillin.
Quick check
?Penicillin inhibits transpeptidases, the bacterial enzymes that cross-link peptidoglycan in the cell wall. Which statement correctly describes both the action of penicillin and how bacteria become resistant to it?
Mission 2

How resistance spreads

  • A random mutation, or a gene gained from another bacterium on a plasmid, gives resistance before any drug is used.
  • The antibiotic kills susceptible bacteria. Resistant ones survive, reproduce, pass the allele on.
  • Resistance to each drug usually needs its own gene. Strains can collect several: multiresistant.
  • More antibiotic use, more selection: careful use slows resistance.
  • Say: "resistant bacteria were selected" — not: "the bacteria got used to the drug".
Quick check
?In a weed population of a hypothetical plant species, an allele gives resistance to herbicide H. Where the herbicide is not used, resistant plants produce fewer seeds than susceptible plants. After years of spraying with H, most plants in a field carry the allele. The farmer then stops using H. Which prediction about the resistance allele over the following generations is best supported?
Mission 2

Finding new antibiotics

  • Few new classes of antibiotic since the late 1980s. Resistance keeps rising.
  • Chemical libraries: thousands of compounds screened, now also by computer models. A new technique opens a new avenue of research.
  • E.g. halicin: found by a machine-learning screen; kills several resistant bacteria in the lab.
  • Careful use is still needed: a new antibiotic will select for resistance too.
Quick check
?Researchers screened a chemical library of thousands of compounds using a computer model trained to predict antibacterial activity. They identified a compound, structurally unlike any existing antibiotic, that killed several multiresistant strains of bacteria in the laboratory. Which statement is the best evaluation of this work?
Mission check

?Tuberculosis is treated with four antibiotics at once for the first two months. Each drug acts on a different target in the bacterium. Why is a combination used rather than a single drug?

The drug never changed. The population of bacteria did, because the drug selected the few that could already survive it.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Your cells already get energy from sugar. Why do they spend it through a different molecule first?

Make a guess: Your cells already get energy from sugar. Why do they spend it through a different molecule first?

Mission 3

Meet ATP

  • ATP: adenosine triphosphate.
  • Adenine (a base) + ribose (a pentose) + three phosphate groups.
  • Adenine + ribose = adenosine.
  • ATP is a nucleotide.
  • ADP: adenosine diphosphate. Two phosphate groups.
  • Same base and sugar as an RNA nucleotide carrying adenine.
Quick check
?Which statement about ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is correct?
Mission 3

Hydrolysis releases energy

  • ATP + H2O → ADP + Pi (inorganic phosphate).
  • Energy is released: enough for many single tasks in the cell.
  • Breaking any bond takes energy. Energy is released overall because ADP and Pi are more stable than ATP.
  • Say: "energy is released when ATP is hydrolysed" — not: "energy is stored in a high-energy bond".
Mission 3

Coupling: putting the energy to work

  • ATP hydrolysis is coupled to an energy-requiring process: one enzyme links them, so they happen together.
  • What ATP powers:
  • active transport of ions and molecules across membranes
  • synthesis of macromolecules (anabolism), e.g. proteins, DNA
  • movement: muscle contraction, cilia, chromosomes in cell division
  • Say: "active transport uses ATP" — not: "diffusion and osmosis use ATP".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly describes a life process that ATP supplies with energy?
Mission 3

Remaking ATP: the ATP–ADP cycle

  • ADP + Pi → ATP + H2O: a condensation reaction.
  • Energy comes from respiration (every cell) and photosynthesis (in chloroplasts).
  • ATP is not stockpiled. A cell keeps a small pool and recycles it, second by second.
  • A resting adult turns over roughly their own body mass of ATP each day.
Quick check
?During a sprint, the small store of ATP in muscle fibres is hydrolysed and then re-formed several times over. Which statement about the energy transfers is correct?
Mission 3

Why not spend glucose directly?

  • Glucose releases its energy in many enzyme-controlled steps. ATP: one step, one enzyme.
  • One glucose holds far more energy than one reaction needs. ATP comes in small packets.
  • Released immediately, right where it is needed.
  • Soluble: moves freely within the cell.
  • Quickly remade from ADP + Pi.
  • So ATP is the molecule that distributes energy within cells.
Quick check
?A cell needs an energy currency that can supply many different reactions in the cytoplasm. Which property makes ATP suitable for this role?
Mission check

?A muscle fibre holds only enough ATP for a few seconds of hard contraction. Yet a marathon runner's leg muscles keep contracting for hours. Which statement explains this?

A small, fast-recycled pool of ATP, refilled by respiration, beats spending glucose directly.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Pyruvate enters the mitochondrion with three carbon atoms. Where do they go, and what does the cell gain?

Make a guess: Pyruvate enters the mitochondrion with three carbon atoms. Where do they go, and what does the cell gain?

Mission 4

Inside the mitochondrion

  • Outer membrane: smooth; encloses the organelle.
  • Inner membrane: folded into cristae. Holds the electron carriers and ATP synthase.
  • Intermembrane space: the narrow gap between the two membranes.
  • Matrix: the fluid inside the inner membrane.
  • The matrix holds the enzymes of the link reaction and Krebs cycle.
Mission 4

The link reaction

  • In the matrix, pyruvate (3C) is:
  • decarboxylated: loses CO2 → a 2C acetyl group.
  • oxidised: loses hydrogen to NAD → reduced NAD.
  • The acetyl group joins coenzyme A → acetyl coenzyme A (acetyl CoA).
  • Coenzyme A is a carrier, not an enzyme. It delivers acetyl groups to the Krebs cycle.
  • Twice per glucose (two pyruvate).
Quick check
?What happens to pyruvate in the link reaction of aerobic cell respiration?
Mission 4

The Krebs cycle

  • Acetyl (2C) joins oxaloacetate (4C) → citrate (6C). Coenzyme A is released, to collect more.
  • Citrate is converted back to oxaloacetate in a series of steps, each with its own enzyme:
  • 2 decarboxylations: 2 CO2 released.
  • 4 dehydrogenations (oxidations): 3 reduced NAD and 1 reduced FAD.
  • 1 ATP, made directly by substrate-level phosphorylation.
  • Oxaloacetate is regenerated, ready for the next acetyl group.
  • Two turns per glucose.
  • Say: "CO2 comes from decarboxylation" — not: "oxygen combines with carbon".
Quick check
?One acetyl group enters the Krebs cycle and oxaloacetate is regenerated. Which account of one turn of the cycle is correct?

  • Carbon check: glucose has 6 C. 2 leave in the link reaction + 4 in the Krebs cycle = 6. Every carbon leaves as CO2.
  • The energy is now mostly in the 8 reduced NAD and 2 reduced FAD, not in ATP.
Mission 4

Why the cycle needs oxygen without using it

  • NAD and FAD accept hydrogen; coenzyme A carries acetyl groups. Both kinds are coenzymes, recycled again and again.
  • Reduced NAD and FAD are re-oxidised only when they hand on their hydrogen to the electron transport chain, which needs oxygen.
  • No oxygen → no NAD for the dehydrogenations → the link reaction and Krebs cycle stop.
  • Say: "the Krebs cycle depends on oxygen" — not: "the Krebs cycle uses oxygen".

Mission 5

Mission 5: How can a bead of liquid creeping along a thin tube show how fast seeds respire, and which fuel they use?

Make a guess: How can a bead of liquid creeping along a thin tube show how fast seeds respire, and which fuel they use?

Mission 5

Other fuels

  • Glucose and fatty acids are the main respiratory substrates. Many other carbon compounds can be respired.
  • Fatty acids are cut into many 2-carbon acetyl groups → acetyl coenzyme A → Krebs cycle.
  • Fatty acids skip glycolysis, so they can be respired only aerobically. Anaerobic respiration needs carbohydrate.
Mission 5

Energy per gram

SubstrateTypical energy value (kJ g⁻¹)
Carbohydrate16
Protein17
Lipid39
  • Lipid gives about twice the energy per gram of carbohydrate.
  • Why: lipids hold more hydrogen and less oxygen per gram.
  • More hydrogen → more reduced NAD → more ATP by oxidative phosphorylation.
  • Say: "lipids have more oxidisable hydrogen" — not: "lipids contain more oxygen".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly compares lipids and carbohydrates as respiratory substrates?
Mission 5Practical

The respirometer

  • Organisms (germinating seeds, woodlice or yeast) sit in a sealed tube, above potassium hydroxide solution (or soda lime), which absorbs CO2.
  • O2 is used → gas volume falls → the liquid bead in the capillary moves towards the organisms.
  • O2 volume = distance moved × πr² (r = capillary radius).
  • Control tube: glass beads of the same volume. It corrects for temperature and pressure changes.
  • Constant-temperature water bath; let the tubes equilibrate first; reset the bead with the syringe.
  • Without the alkali, the bead shows O2 used − CO2 released (the net change).
  • Carbohydrate: CO2 released = O2 used, so no net change. Lipid: less CO2 than O2, so the bead still moves towards the organisms. The net change hints at the fuel.
Mission 5

Worked example: rate of oxygen uptake

Illustrative data: 2.0 g germinating seeds, 10 min, 25 °C, capillary diameter 1.0 mm. Bead moved 24 mm towards the seeds; the control's bead moved 2 mm the same way.

  • Step 1, correct with the control: 24 − 2 = 22 mm.
  • Step 2, volume: r = 0.50 mm, so π × 0.50² × 22 = 17.3 mm³ O2.
  • Step 3, rate: 17.3 ÷ 2.0 g ÷ 10 min = 0.86 mm³ g⁻¹ min⁻¹.
  • Always divide by time and by mass, so different samples can be compared.
Quick check
?In a respirometer, carbon dioxide is absorbed by potassium hydroxide. With 5.0 g of germinating seeds, the gas volume in the apparatus fell by 1.2 cm³ in 10 minutes. What is the rate of respiration per gram?
Quick checkRead the data

Mission check

?A student set up a respirometer with blowfly larvae above potassium hydroxide solution, and the bead moved steadily towards the larvae. In a second run, identical except that the potassium hydroxide was replaced with water, the bead hardly moved. Which explanation is best?

With the alkali, the bead measures oxygen uptake: the rate. Without it, it measures the balance of the two gases, and that balance points to the fuel.

Mission 6

Mission 6: A leaf splits water to get electrons. Where do those electrons end up, and what do they make on the way?

Make a guess: A leaf splits water to get electrons. Where do those electrons end up, and what do they make on the way?

Mission 6

Photosystems catch light

  • Thylakoid membranes hold two photosystems: photosystem II (PSII) and photosystem I (PSI).
  • Each is an array of chlorophyll and accessory pigments, with a special chlorophyll a at the reaction centre.
  • Absorbed energy is passed to the reaction centre.
  • There an electron is raised to a higher energy level and emitted to an electron acceptor.
  • Photosystems are always in membranes: thylakoids in chloroplasts; internal membranes in cyanobacteria.
  • Say: "light excites an electron, which the chlorophyll loses" — not: "chlorophyll makes electrons".
Mission 6

The electron path: PSII → PSI → NADP

1. Light excites PSII. Its electron passes to an electron transport chain (ETC).

2. Carrier to carrier: each step is a redox reaction that releases energy.

3. The electron reaches PSI and is excited again by light.

4. It passes to NADP in the stroma.

  • This one-way flow is non-cyclic photophosphorylation. (Cyclic.)
Mission 6

Photolysis refills PSII

  • PSII has lost electrons. It must get them back.
  • Water is split: 2H2O → 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ + O2. This is photolysis.
  • Electrons replace those lost from PSII.
  • Protons (H⁺) stay inside the thylakoid: they add to the proton gradient.
  • Oxygen is a by-product (waste): it diffuses out of the leaf.
  • Say: "splitting water supplies electrons" — not: "splitting water supplies the energy". The energy comes from light.
Quick check
?Which statement about the photolysis of water in photosystem II is correct?
Mission 6

Reducing NADP

  • At the end of the chain, PSI passes two electrons to NADP, on the stroma side.
  • NADP also takes a hydrogen ion from the stroma: NADP is reduced to reduced NADP.
  • Reduced NADP and ATP carry the energy to the light-independent stage.
Mission 6

Chemiosmosis makes ATP

  • Energy released along the ETC pumps H⁺ from the stroma into the thylakoid space.
  • Photolysis adds more H⁺ inside; NADP reduction removes H⁺ outside.
  • Result: high [H⁺] inside, low outside: an electrochemical gradient.
  • H⁺ flow back to the stroma through ATP synthase. This drives ADP + Pᵢ → ATP, released into the stroma.
  • ATP made this way: photophosphorylation. The mechanism: chemiosmosis.
  • Say: "ATP synthase is driven by H⁺ flowing down their gradient" — not: "the carriers make ATP".
Quick check
?Which processes in an illuminated chloroplast build up the proton gradient across the thylakoid membrane?
Quick checkRead the data
?In illuminated chloroplasts of a hypothetical alga, the pH of the thylakoid space was 5.0 and the pH of the stroma was 8.0. What is the H⁺ concentration in the thylakoid space as a multiple of the H⁺ concentration in the stroma?
Mission check

?A chemical is added that takes electrons from photosystem I in place of NADP. The chloroplasts are illuminated with a supply of water, ADP, phosphate and NADP. Which prediction is correct?

Electrons from water normally end on NADP; on the way, their energy builds the proton gradient that makes ATP. Swap the final acceptor, and only the reduced NADP is lost.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Why would a chloroplast send its electrons round in a loop that makes no oxygen at all?

Make a guess: Why would a chloroplast send its electrons round in a loop that makes no oxygen at all?

Mission 7

PSII and PSI: an array each

  • Both photosystems sit in the thylakoid membranes.
  • Each: hundreds of pigment molecules (chlorophyll a, b, carotenoids) around one reaction centre.
  • PSII: linked to water splitting; passes electrons to the ETC.
  • PSI: passes electrons to NADP, or back to the ETC.
  • Why an array? A lone chlorophyll absorbs photons rarely, over a narrow range.
  • The array absorbs many wavelengths and funnels the energy to one reaction centre, which is excited many times a second.
  • A single pigment molecule could not perform any part of photosynthesis.
Quick check
?Why is a structured array of many different pigment molecules, rather than a single chlorophyll molecule, needed to carry out the first step of photosynthesis?
Mission 7

Cyclic photophosphorylation

1. Light excites PSI. Its electron passes to the ETC.

2. The electron flows along the carriers, pumping H⁺ into the thylakoid space.

3. It returns to PSI: a closed loop.

  • H⁺ flow through ATP synthase: ATP is made by chemiosmosis.
  • PSI only. No water split, no O2, no reduced NADP.
  • Say: "cyclic flow uses PSI only and makes only ATP" — not: "cyclic flow also makes reduced NADP and O2".
Mission 7

Why run both?

  • The Calvin cycle uses 3 ATP for every 2 reduced NADP.
  • Non-cyclic flow alone makes too little ATP for that ratio.
  • Cyclic flow tops up the ATP.
Quick check
?A herbicide blocks the flow of electrons out of photosystem II. Chloroplasts treated with it are kept in bright light with a supply of ADP, phosphate and NADP. Which process can continue?
Mission 7

Evidence: ATP in the dark

  • Chemiosmotic theory (Mitchell, 1961): a proton gradient drives ATP synthase.
  • Test (Jagendorf and Uribe, 1966): thylakoids soaked in the dark at pH 4, so the inside became pH 4.
  • Then moved, still in the dark, to pH 8 with ADP and Pᵢ: ATP formed briefly.
  • No pH difference: no ATP.
  • So the gradient alone, without light or electron flow, can drive ATP synthesis.
Quick checkRead the data
?Isolated thylakoids were soaked in the dark in an acidic solution (pH 4) until the thylakoid space also reached pH 4. Still in the dark, they were then moved to a solution at pH 8 containing ADP and inorganic phosphate, and ATP formed for a short time. Thylakoids treated the same way but moved to a pH 4 solution, and thylakoids kept at pH 8 throughout, formed no ATP. Which claim is best supported by these results?
Mission check

?Chloroplasts are lit only with far-red light (above about 690 nm). This light excites PSI strongly but PSII hardly at all. ADP, phosphate and NADP are supplied. What is predicted?

The loop runs PSI alone. It adds ATP without using water, so it makes no O2 and no reduced NADP.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Respiration releases the energy in carbon compounds in many small, enzyme-catalysed steps; part is captured in ATP, the rest is heat.
  • Glycolysis: phosphorylation (2 ATP used), lysis to two triose phosphate, oxidation (NAD reduced), ATP formation: net 2 ATP, 2 reduced NAD, 2 pyruvate.

Mission 2

  • Antibiotics block processes found only in bacteria, so they do not harm our cells and cannot touch viruses.
  • Resistance evolves by selection: random variants that survive the drug reproduce, so every use of an antibiotic must be justified.

Mission 3

  • ATP = adenine + ribose + three phosphates. Hydrolysis to ADP + Pi releases energy in a small, immediate packet; respiration supplies the energy to remake it.

Mission 4

  • Link reaction: pyruvate is decarboxylated and oxidised to an acetyl group, carried by coenzyme A. Krebs cycle: acetyl + oxaloacetate → citrate; 2 CO2, 3 reduced NAD, 1 reduced FAD, 1 ATP per turn; oxaloacetate regenerated.

Mission 5

  • A respirometer with an alkali measures O2 uptake: rate = corrected volume ÷ mass ÷ time; without the alkali it shows O2 used − CO2 released.
  • Lipids and amino acids enter respiration mostly via acetyl coenzyme A and the Krebs cycle; lipids give about twice the energy per gram.

Mission 6

  • Light excites electrons in PSII and PSI; they pass along an electron transport chain to NADP. Water splitting replaces PSII's electrons and releases O2.
  • Energy from the chain pumps protons into the thylakoid space; their flow back through ATP synthase makes ATP (chemiosmosis). Products: ATP and reduced NADP.

Mission 7

  • Non-cyclic flow (PSII → PSI → NADP) makes ATP, reduced NADP and O2. Cyclic flow (PSI → carriers → PSI) makes only ATP, topping up the ATP the Calvin cycle needs.
  • A proton gradient drives ATP synthase, even in the dark (Jagendorf and Uribe, 1966).
