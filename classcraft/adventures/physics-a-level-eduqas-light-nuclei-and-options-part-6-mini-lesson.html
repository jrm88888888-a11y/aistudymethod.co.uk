Mission 1

Mission 1: Switch on a current in a wire just above a compass. Which way does the needle turn, and why?

Make a guess: Switch on a current in a wire just above a compass. Which way does the needle turn, and why?

Mission 1

Where magnetic fields come from

  • A magnetic field is produced by moving charges (an electric current) and by permanent magnets.
  • It exerts forces on moving charges, currents and magnetic materials (iron, steel).
  • A charge at rest feels no magnetic force.
Mission 1

Field lines

  • Direction of a field line: the way the north end of a compass needle points.
  • Closer lines = stronger field. Lines never cross.
  • Field lines are closed loops: outside a magnet they run N → S; inside, S → N.
Mission 1

Field of a long straight wire

  • Field lines are concentric circles centred on the wire, in planes at right angles to it.
  • Right-hand grip rule: thumb along the current; curled fingers give the field direction.
  • Lines spread out with distance: the field gets weaker away from the wire.
  • End-on views: ⊙ current out of the page → field anticlockwise; ⊗ into the page → clockwise.
Quick check
?A long, straight, vertical wire carries a steady current upwards. Point P is 5.0 cm due east of the wire, level with it. What is the direction of the magnetic field of the wire at P?
Mission 1

Flat coil and solenoid

  • Flat coil: the circles around each part of the wire combine. At the centre the field is along the axis.
  • Solenoid (long coil): inside, straight, parallel, evenly spaced lines (uniform field). Outside, the pattern of a bar magnet.
  • Direction: curl the fingers of your right hand the way the current flows around the turns; the thumb points along the field inside, towards the coil's N end.
  • An iron core makes the solenoid field many times stronger: the iron becomes magnetised and its field adds.
Quick check
?You look at the left end of a long solenoid. Seen from there, the current in its turns flows clockwise. Which end of the solenoid is its north end?
Mission check

?A flat horizontal circular coil carries a steady current that flows clockwise when viewed from above. What is the direction of the magnetic field at the centre of the coil?

Same rule, new shape. Around a straight wire the field circles the current, so a compass needle just below a wire laid along it swings towards lying across the wire, tangent to a circle centred on it: fully across if the wire's field is much stronger than Earth's. Reverse the current and it swings the other way.

Mission 2

Mission 2: A wire carrying a current lies between the poles of a magnet. Which way is it pushed?

Make a guess: A wire carrying a current lies between the poles of a magnet. Which way is it pushed?

Mission 2

The motor effect

  • A current-carrying wire in a magnetic field feels a force.
  • The force is at right angles to both the current and the field.
  • No force when the current is parallel to the field.
  • Reverse the current or the field: the force reverses.
Mission 2

Which way?

  • Fleming's left-hand rule: First finger = Field (N → S); seCond finger = Current (+ → −); thuMb = Motion (force).
Quick check
?A straight wire lies in the plane of the page and carries a current towards the right of the page. A uniform magnetic field in the plane of the page is directed towards the top of the page. What is the direction of the force on the wire?
Mission 2

How big?

  • F = BIl sin θ
  • θ = angle between the current and the field. Largest at 90°, zero at 0°.
  • The length is the length inside the field.
  • B is in tesla (T).
Mission 2

Magnetic flux density

  • B is the magnetic flux density.
  • Rearranged (current ⟂ field): B = F/(Il): force per unit current per unit length.
  • 1 tesla: the field in which a wire carrying 1 A at right angles to the field feels 1 N on each metre.
  • 1 T = 1 N A⁻¹ m⁻¹.
Mission 2

Worked example

  • A 4.0 cm length of wire carries 2.5 A at right angles to a 0.15 T field.
  • Force = 0.15 × 2.5 × 0.040 = 0.015 N.
  • Turn the wire to 30° to the field: F = 0.015 × sin 30° = 0.0075 N.
  • Backwards: 0.012 N on a 5.0 cm wire carrying 3.0 A at right angles. B = force ÷ (current × length) = 0.012 ÷ (3.0 × 0.050) = 0.080 T.
Quick check
?A straight wire 0.40 m long carries a current of 3.0 A. Only 0.25 m of the wire lies inside a uniform magnetic field of strength 0.080 T, and the wire makes an angle of 30° with the direction of the field. What is the magnitude of the force on the wire?
Mission 2

Measuring B with a balance

  • A U-shaped magnet sits on a top-pan (digital) balance. A stiff wire is clamped across the gap, at right angles to the field, not touching the magnet.
  • Current on: the wire is pushed one way, so (Newton's third law) the magnet is pushed the other way. The reading changes by Δm.
  • F = Δm × g.
  • Vary I: plot F against I. Straight line through the origin; gradient = Bl, so B = gradient ÷ l.
  • Zero the balance with the current off; reverse the current and average to cancel stray effects.
Quick check
?In a current-balance experiment, 5.0 cm of a horizontal wire lies at right angles to the field between the poles of a magnet resting on a balance. With no current the balance reads 152.46 g. With a current of 3.0 A it reads 152.10 g. Take g = 9.81 N kg⁻¹. What is the magnetic flux density between the poles?
Mission check

?A long straight wire carries a current vertically upwards. It is in a uniform horizontal magnetic field that points due north. In which direction is the magnetic force on the wire?

The force is at right angles to both the current and the field, and its size is B × current × length when they are perpendicular: so a wire between the poles is pushed sideways, out of the gap or into it, never towards a pole.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Two parallel wires carry currents in the same direction. Do they attract or repel, and why?

Make a guess: Two parallel wires carry currents in the same direction. Do they attract or repel, and why?

Mission 3

Field of a long straight wire

  • B = μ0I/(2πa), a = distance from the wire.
  • μ0 = 4π × 10⁻⁷ T m A⁻¹: the permeability of free space, a constant.
  • B ∝ I and B ∝ 1/distance: twice as far, half the field.
  • Direction: right-hand grip rule. The field is tangent to circles around the wire.
Mission 3

Worked example

  • A long wire carries 15 A. Find B 3.0 cm from it.
  • B = μ0I/(2π × 0.030) = (4π × 10⁻⁷ × 15) ÷ (2π × 0.030).
  • Shortcut: μ0/2π = 2 × 10⁻⁷ T m A⁻¹.
  • B = 2 × 10⁻⁷ × 15 ÷ 0.030 = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ T.
  • About twice Earth's field at the surface (roughly 5 × 10⁻⁵ T): enough to swing a compass.
Quick check
?A long straight wire of diameter 0.010 m carries a current of 12 A. Point P is 0.045 m from the surface of the wire. What is the magnitude of the magnetic field produced by the wire at P? (Use μ0 = 4π × 10⁻⁷ T m A⁻¹.)
Mission 3

Field inside a long solenoid

  • B = μ0nI, with n = number of turns per metre (N/L).
  • Uniform inside, away from the ends. It does not depend on the solenoid's radius.
  • Example: 500 turns on 0.25 m, so n = 2000 turns per metre; I = 1.5 A. B = 4π × 10⁻⁷ × 2000 × 1.5 = 3.8 × 10⁻³ T.
  • An iron core multiplies B many times: the iron's aligned dipoles add their field.
Quick check
?A long solenoid with N turns and length L has a field of magnitude B0 inside it when the current is I. A second long solenoid has the same number of turns N spread over a length 2L and twice the radius, and it carries the same current I. What is the magnitude of the field inside the second solenoid?
Mission 3

Force between parallel wires

  • Wire 1 makes a field at wire 2: B1 = μ0I1/2πa, ⟂ to wire 2.
  • Wire 2 carries I2 in that field, so it feels F = B1I2l.
  • Same direction → attract. Opposite directions → repel.
Mission 3

Worked example

  • Two long parallel wires 5.0 cm apart each carry 10 A in the same direction.
  • Field of wire 1 at wire 2: B = 2 × 10⁻⁷ × 10 ÷ 0.050 = 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ T. Force per metre: BI = 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ × 10 = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ N.
  • On 2.0 m of wire: 8.0 × 10⁻⁴ N, towards the other wire.
Quick check
?Two long straight parallel wires in air are 4.0 cm apart and carry currents of 5.0 A and 8.0 A. What is the magnitude of the magnetic force on a 1.5 m length of the wire carrying 8.0 A? Take μ0 = 4π × 10⁻⁷ T m A⁻¹ for air.
Quick check
?Two long straight parallel wires, X and Y, are 5.0 cm apart in air. X carries 2.0 A and Y carries 6.0 A, in the same direction. What is the magnetic force on a 1.0 m length of each wire?
Mission check

?A light, loosely wound coil of wire, like a soft spring, hangs vertically. A large steady current is passed through it. What happens to the length of the coil?

Parallel currents in the same direction attract: each wire sits in the other's field, and the force on it points towards the other wire. Opposite currents repel.

Mission 4

Mission 4: An electron and a proton feel the same force between charged plates. Which swerves more, and by how much?

Make a guess: An electron and a proton feel the same force between charged plates. Which swerves more, and by how much?

Mission 4

The force on a charge in a field

  • F = qE, the same everywhere in a uniform field.
  • Positive charge: force along the field (towards the negative plate).
  • Negative charge: force against the field (towards the positive plate).
  • Between parallel plates: E = V/d. 400 V across 2.0 cm → E = 2.0 × 10⁴ V m⁻¹.
  • Electron or proton there: |F| = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 2.0 × 10⁴ = 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ N. Same size, opposite directions.
Mission 4

Same force, different acceleration

  • Constant force → constant acceleration: a = F/m.
  • Electron: a = 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ / 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ = 3.5 × 10¹⁵ m s⁻².
  • Proton: a = 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ / 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ = 1.9 × 10¹² m s⁻².
  • The electron's mass is about 1/1800 of the proton's, so its acceleration is about 1800 times greater.
  • Gravity is negligible: the electron's weight is about 10⁻²⁹ N, some 10¹⁴ times smaller than 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ N.
Quick check
?Two horizontal parallel plates, 2.5 cm apart in a vacuum, have a potential difference of 150 V between them. What is the magnitude of the acceleration of an electron between the plates? Gravity is negligible. Take e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C and me = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg.
Mission 4

Accelerating through a potential difference

  • Work done by the field = qV = gain in kinetic energy.
  • From rest: ½mv² = eV (electron or proton).
  • Worked example: electron through 2000 V.
  • v = √(2eV/m) = √(2 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 2000 / 9.11 × 10⁻³¹) = 2.65 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹.
  • That is about 9% of the speed of light, so ½mv² is still accurate.
Quick check
?Two parallel plates, 2.0 cm apart in a vacuum, have a potential difference of 500 V between them. An electron is released from rest at the negative plate and accelerates across the gap. What is its speed when it reaches the positive plate? Take e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C and me = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg.
Quick check
?An electron moves horizontally into the region between two horizontal parallel plates, midway between them and at right angles to the uniform electric field between the plates. It is deflected towards the positive plate. Gravity is negligible. Which describes the motion of the electron while it is between the plates?
Mission 4

Deflection: a projectile in an electric field

  • Particle enters at right angles to the field (parallel to the plates).
  • Along the plates: no force → constant velocity v.
  • Across the plates: constant force → constant acceleration a = F/m.
  • Path between the plates: a parabola, like a ball thrown horizontally.
  • Time between plates: t = L/v. Sideways shift: y = ½at².
  • After the plates: no force → straight line.
Mission 4

Worked example: deflection

  • Electron at 2.65 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹ enters midway between plates 5.0 cm long, 2.0 cm apart, 400 V.
  • a = eV/(md) = 3.5 × 10¹⁵ m s⁻² (as for the electron on the earlier screen).
  • t = L/v = 0.050 / 2.65 × 10⁷ = 1.9 × 10⁻⁹ s.
  • y = ½at² = ½ × 3.5 × 10¹⁵ × (1.9 × 10⁻⁹)² = 6.3 mm. Less than 10 mm, so it misses the plate.
  • Sideways velocity at exit: vy = at = 6.6 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹.
  • A proton at the same speed: about 1800 times less deflection.
Mission check

?A proton and an alpha particle are placed, one at a time, at the same point in a uniform electric field. The alpha particle has twice the charge of the proton and about four times its mass. Gravity is negligible. What is the ratio (acceleration of the alpha particle) / (acceleration of the proton)?

Same field, acceleration = charge × E ÷ mass. The electron and proton feel equal forces, but the electron has about 1/1800 of the mass: about 1800 times the acceleration, so it swerves far more.

Mission 5

Mission 5: A magnetic field can steer a beam of charged particles. Can it also make them go faster?

Make a guess: A magnetic field can steer a beam of charged particles. Can it also make them go faster?

Mission 5

Force on a moving charge

  • A current is moving charge, so a single moving charge in a field also feels a force.
  • F = Bqv sin θ
  • θ = angle between v and B.
  • Zero force on a charge at rest, or moving along the field.
  • Example: an electron at 3.0 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹ at right angles to a 0.20 T field: F = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 3.0 × 10⁶ × 0.20 = 9.6 × 10⁻¹⁴ N.
Mission 5

Which way?

  • Fleming's left-hand rule, with the second finger along the conventional current: the direction a positive charge moves.
  • For an electron, point the second finger opposite to its motion.
  • The force is always ⟂ to the velocity, so it does no work: the speed and kinetic energy never change.
Quick check
?An electron moves towards the right of the page, in the plane of the page, through a uniform magnetic field directed into the page. What is the direction of the magnetic force on the electron at that instant?
Mission 5

Circular paths

  • Only the direction changes. Uniform field, v ⟂ B: a circle.
  • The magnetic force is the centripetal force: magnetic force = mv²/r.
  • r = mv/(Bq)
  • Faster or heavier → bigger circle. Stronger field or more charge → tighter circle.
  • Time for one orbit: T = 2πr/v = 2πm/(Bq).
Mission 5

Worked example

  • A proton (m = 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, q = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C) at 2.0 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹, at right angles to a 0.50 T field.
  • Radius: r = mv/(Bq) = (1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ × 2.0 × 10⁶) ÷ (0.50 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹) = 0.042 m.
  • Period: T = 2πm/(Bq) = 1.3 × 10⁻⁷ s.
  • Double the field: r halves to 0.021 m, and T halves too, to 6.6 × 10⁻⁸ s.
Quick check
?A proton moves in a circle in a uniform magnetic field that is perpendicular to its velocity. A second proton moves at twice the speed of the first, perpendicular to the same field. Compared with the first proton, what are the radius of the second proton's circle and the time it takes to complete one revolution?
Quick check
?In a velocity selector, uniform electric and magnetic fields act at right angles to each other and to the velocity of the particles, so that the electric and magnetic forces on a proton are in opposite directions. Protons moving at speed v0 pass straight through undeflected. Gravity is negligible. Which statement about this velocity selector is correct?
Mission 5

Crossed fields

  • In both E and B fields, a charge feels two independent forces: qE (any speed) and Bqv (depends on speed).
  • E and B at right angles, both ⟂ to the beam, forces opposite. Straight through only if qE = Bqv: v = E/B. This is a velocity selector.
  • Faster particles: magnetic force wins. Slower: electric force wins.
  • Example: plates 1.0 cm apart, 400 V, B = 0.050 T. E = 4.0 × 10⁴ V m⁻¹; v = E/B = 8.0 × 10⁵ m s⁻¹.
  • Mass spectrometer: a selector fixes v; then B alone bends ions into circles with r = mv/(Bq). Ions of the same charge separate by mass.
Mission 5

The Hall effect

  • Current flows along a thin slab in a field B ⟂ to its face. The moving charge carriers are pushed to one edge.
  • Charge builds up on opposite edges: a transverse pd, the Hall voltage VH.
  • It stops growing when the electric force balances the magnetic force: qE = Bqv.
  • With width d across which VH is measured: VH = Bvd. A constant current fixes the carriers' drift speed v, so VH ∝ B.
  • A Hall probe (a semiconductor slab, few carriers, so large v and VH) measures B. Turn it until the reading is largest: then the slab's face is ⟂ to B. Calibrate in a known field.
Quick check
?The figure shows a metal strip, in the plane of the page, carrying a current I in a uniform magnetic field B. The charge carriers in the metal are electrons. As the magnetic field pushes on the moving electrons, charge builds up on two opposite surfaces of the strip. Which surface becomes negatively charged?
Mission check

?Two protons enter two separate regions at the same speed. Region 1 contains only a uniform electric field; region 2 contains only a uniform magnetic field. Each proton enters at right angles to the field lines. Gravity is negligible. A short time later, which statement is correct?

The magnetic force is always at right angles to the velocity. It changes direction, never speed, so a magnetic field alone can bend a beam but never accelerate it to a higher energy. That is why particle accelerators use electric fields to speed particles up; magnetic fields only steer them.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Why are the most powerful particle accelerators built as rings many kilometres around?

Make a guess: Why are the most powerful particle accelerators built as rings many kilometres around?

Mission 6

Electric fields push, magnetic fields steer

  • Electric field: force along the field, so it does work on a charge. Energy gained crossing a pd V: charge × V.
  • Recap, magnetic field: No work done, so the speed stays the same; only the direction changes. A uniform B field bends the path into a circle, r = mv/(Bq): more momentum, bigger circle.
Mission 6

The linear accelerator (linac)

  • A line of drift tubes in a vacuum, joined alternately to an alternating pd.
  • Inside a tube: no electric field. The particle drifts at constant speed.
  • In each gap: a field pushes the particle forward. It gains charge × V at every gap.
  • The pd reverses while the particle is inside a tube, so the next gap is ready to push it again.
  • Tubes get longer along the line: the particle is faster, but must spend the same time (half a cycle) in each tube.
Quick check
?A proton crosses 20 gaps in a linac. The pd across each gap is 50 kV as the proton crosses it. How much kinetic energy does the proton gain?
Mission 6

The cyclotron

  • Two hollow D-shaped electrodes ("dees") in a vacuum, between the poles of a magnet.
  • B field (perpendicular to the plane of the dees): bends the particle into a semicircle inside each dee.
  • Alternating pd across the gap: accelerates the particle each time it crosses.
  • Faster particle → bigger radius: it spirals outwards, then leaves at the edge.
  • Time for a half-turn: πr/v = πm/(Bq): it does not depend on the speed.
  • So a pd of fixed frequency stays in step: f = Bq/(2πm). (For B = 1.5 T, protons: about 23 MHz.)
Quick check
?A cyclotron accelerates protons with a fixed magnetic field. It is now used for alpha particles: twice the charge, about four times the mass. What must happen to the frequency of the alternating pd?
Mission 6

The synchrotron

  • A ring of fixed radius: an evacuated beam pipe.
  • Bending magnets keep the particles on the ring. Focusing magnets keep the beam narrow.
  • Accelerating cavities: an alternating electric field gives the particles energy each lap.
  • As momentum grows, the B field is increased in step, so the radius stays the same.
  • The frequency of the accelerating field is matched to the particles' lap time.
  • Two beams can travel in opposite directions and collide head-on.
Mission check

?A ring's bending magnets already run at their maximum field. Engineers want protons with three times the momentum. What must they do?

Magnets have a maximum field. At the highest energies, the only way to keep the bending radius manageable is a bigger ring: the Large Hadron Collider's ring is about 27 km round.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Magnetic fields come from moving charges and magnets; field lines are closed loops, closer lines mean a stronger field.
  • Straight wire: circles, direction by the right-hand grip rule. Solenoid: uniform inside, bar-magnet pattern outside.

Mission 2

  • Force on a current in a field: at right angles to both; zero when parallel.
  • Direction: Fleming's left-hand rule.
  • F = BIl sin θ.
  • 1 T: 1 N on 1 m of wire carrying 1 A at right angles to the field.

Mission 3

  • Long straight wire: B = μ0I/2πa; μ0 = 4π × 10⁻⁷ T m A⁻¹.
  • Long solenoid: B = μ0nI, uniform inside, independent of radius.
  • Find B from one wire, then F = BIl on the other.
  • Parallel wires: same direction attract, opposite repel; equal and opposite forces.

Mission 4

  • In a uniform field F = qE (positive charges along E, negative against it), so a = qE/m is constant; light particles accelerate most.
  • Through a potential difference V, a charge gains kinetic energy qV.
  • Entering at right angles: constant velocity along the plates, constant acceleration across them, so a parabola.

Mission 5

  • F = Bqv sin θ.
  • The force is ⟂ to v, so speed and kinetic energy never change.
  • v ⟂ B gives a circle: r = mv/(Bq) = p/(Bq); the period does not depend on speed.
  • Crossed fields pass undeflected only particles with v = E/B.

Mission 6

  • Electric fields give charged particles energy; magnetic fields bend them in circles of radius set by momentum.
  • Linac: drift tubes lengthen. Cyclotron: spiral outwards at a fixed frequency.
  • Synchrotron: fixed radius, so B rises as the momentum rises.
