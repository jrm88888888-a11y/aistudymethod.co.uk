Paper 1: Fiction & Imaginative Writing

This mini-lesson trains the skills Edexcel 1EN0 Paper 1 tests: reading an unseen 19th-century fiction extract, analysing language and structure, evaluating a writer's craft, and producing your own imaginative writing.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go (spot the device, pick the best comment, match method to effect) and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you're ready.

Know the paper

What Paper 1 asks of you

Section A gives you one unseen 19th-century fiction extract (around 650 words). You answer a ladder of reading questions worth 24 marks:

  • Short retrieval — find and identify information (AO1).
  • Language & structure — analyse how the writer uses words, phrases and structure for effect (AO2).
  • Evaluation — evaluate how the writer creates a particular effect, supported by examples (AO4).

Section B — Imaginative Writing (40 marks): choose one task (often prompted by images or a title) and write imaginatively. Assessed on communication & organisation (AO5) and technical accuracy (AO6).

19th-century fiction can feel dense — the language is older. Read slowly, work out what's happening, then focus on how the writer builds it.

Reading skill

Read 19th-century fiction like a detective

Older fiction rewards patient reading. Before you analyse, annotate:

  • Get the gist — who, where, what's happening, in modern terms.
  • Underline striking verbs, adjectives and images.
  • Bracket structural moves — a shift of focus, a change of pace, a short sentence.
  • Note the mood in the margin (tense? gloomy? tender?).

Don't panic at old words. You don't need every word — you need enough to comment on the writer's methods and their effect.

Toolkit · AO2

Language techniques & their effects

Naming a device earns almost nothing. The marks are in the effect — what the word makes the reader feel or picture.

  • Metaphor / simile — paint a vivid picture; make the abstract concrete.
  • Personification — gives objects or nature feelings, creating mood.
  • Powerful verbs — a precise verb controls the whole image.
  • Sensory imagery — sight, sound, touch pull the reader into the scene.
  • Sentence forms — a short sentence after long ones lands like a shock.
  • Pathetic fallacy — weather/nature reflecting a character's mood.

The golden rule: device → quotation → effect on the reader. Miss the effect and you're only "feature-spotting".

Analysis skill · AO2

Comment on the effect, not the label

Compare two comments on the same line:

"The mist clawed at the windows of the silent hall." ▸ verb "clawed" — personification / powerful verb
Weak (feature-spotting)

"The writer uses personification here. This is effective and makes it interesting."

Strong (device → effect)

"The verb 'clawed' makes the mist seem like a predator trying to break in, so the reader feels the hall is under siege by something threatening and alive."

Misconception: the marks are not for spotting personification — they're for explaining why 'clawed' works and what it does to the reader.

Structure · AO2

Structure — the shape of the whole extract

AO2 rewards language and structure. Structure is how the writer orders and shapes the extract to guide the reader:

  • The opening — where does the writer focus your attention first?
  • Shifts — zooming from a wide view to a tiny detail; outside to inside.
  • Perspective — following a character's eyes, or a narrator pulling back.
  • The ending — a cliffhanger, a return to the opening image, a revelation.
The evaluation question · AO4

How to structure an evaluation answer

The evaluation question asks you to judge how successfully the writer creates an effect, backed by examples. Use a What → How → Why chain (PEE with a verdict):

Evaluation = analysis + verdict. Always end with a judgement: "This is highly effective because…", "This works well, though…".

Section B · Writing

Imaginative writing

Section B asks you to write imaginatively — often prompted by a picture or a title. It can be descriptive or narrative. Pick the option you can do best and plan it:

  • Descriptive — a vivid picture of a place, moment or person; rich imagery.
  • Narrative — a story with a clear shape; keep it small and controlled.

AO5 rewards craft, not length. A tightly-controlled, vividly-described single scene scores higher than a sprawling plot with weak description.

Writing craft · AO5

Show, don't tell

  • Use the senses — sight, sound, smell, touch, taste build a real place.
  • Choose precise verbs and nouns — "sprinted", not "went fast".
  • Zoom like a camera — wide shot, then a close-up on one telling detail.
  • Vary sentence length for pace and control.
  • Use a motif — a repeated image ties the piece together.

Plan first. A few minutes planning a shape saves the whole answer and boosts AO5 organisation.

Worked model

Imaginative writing in action

A short model opening describing a deserted fairground, with techniques labelled:

"The carousel slept, its painted horses frozen mid-gallop. Rust wept down the ticket booth. Wind rattled a loose chain — once, twice — then nothing. The fair was a graveyard of joy." ▸ personification ("slept", "wept") · sensory sound ("rattled") · a rhythmic, listed pair ("once, twice") building pace before the silence · metaphor ("graveyard of joy")

Each device does a job: eerie stillness, decay, then a final image that lingers. That is craft, not decoration.

Writing craft · AO5/AO6

Control your sentences

  • Long, flowing sentences — build atmosphere and detail.
  • Short, blunt sentences — create shock, tension or drama.
  • Openers — start some sentences with a verb or adverb for variety.
  • Punctuation for effect — a dash, colon or ellipsis used deliberately.

Read it in your head. If every sentence is the same length, vary it — rhythm is part of AO5.

Technical accuracy · AO6

AO6 — spelling, punctuation, grammar & vocabulary

A large slice of your Section B marks is for technical accuracy and ambitious vocabulary. Easy to gain, easy to throw away.

  • Punctuate accurately — full stops, commas, and the occasional colon, semicolon or dash used correctly.
  • Reach for precise vocabulary — "desolate" not "empty"; "menacing" not "scary".
  • Vary sentence structures securely, using a range of punctuation.
  • Proofread — leave 3–4 minutes to catch slips.

Ambition with control beats safety with errors. Only use words you can spell and punctuate correctly.

Exam-day plan

Working the paper for marks

  • Watch the mark tariff — a short retrieval takes a moment; the evaluation and Section B need the most time.
  • Analyse the whole extract for the AO2/AO4 questions — don't stall on the first line.
  • Quote briefly and precisely — short, well-chosen quotations beat long copied chunks.
  • Section B is worth 40 marks — plan first, then write. Give it real time.
  • Save time to proofread for AO6.

Time by marks: Reading is 24 marks, Writing is 40 — weight your time towards the imaginative writing.

Recap

The skills to take into the exam

Read & annotate: get the gist of the 19th-century extract, then mark methods.

AO2 analysis: device → quotation → effect on the reader (never just name it).

Structure: track the reader's journey across the whole extract.

AO4 evaluation: analysis + a clear judgement of how successful it is.

Section B imaginative (AO5): show don't tell; senses, precise verbs, varied sentences.

AO6: punctuate accurately, ambitious vocabulary, proofread.
