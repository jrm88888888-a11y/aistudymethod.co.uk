Mission 1

Mission 1: Cut open an organ or slice it thin: how do you know what you are looking at?

Make a guess: Cut open an organ or slice it thin: how do you know what you are looking at?

Mission 1

Before you cut

  • Tools: scalpel, scissors, forceps, blunt seeker, mounted needle, dissecting board.
  • Cut away from yourself and your fingers, on the board.
  • Keep blades sharp: a blunt blade needs more force and slips.
  • Gloves on; wash hands afterwards; disinfect the board.
  • Blades into a sharps bin; tissue disposed of as instructed.
  • Ethics: organs usually come from a butcher, so no animal is killed for the lesson.
  • Anyone who objects can use models, photographs or video for the same observations.
Mission 1

Find your way round an organ

  • Orient first. Heart: the coronary artery runs diagonally across the front (ventral) surface.
  • Identify by structure, position and connections: not by how dark something looks.
  • Push a blunt seeker into each vessel. Which chamber does it lead to?
  • Artery: thick, elastic wall that stays open. Vein: thin wall that collapses flat.
  • Record: labelled drawing or photo with a ruler in view. Measure, e.g. wall thickness.
  • Say: "the left ventricle wall is thicker because it generates higher pressure to pump blood round the whole body" — not: "because it holds more blood".
Mission 1Practical

Dissections each board names

  • Heart structure investigated practically, e.g. by dissection.
  • Same routine every time: orient → identify → record → measure.
  • Organ detail: heart in an earlier lesson; gills and tracheae in another lesson; kidney in another lesson.
Quick check
?A student dissects a sheep's heart and finds that the wall of the left ventricle is much thicker than the wall of the right ventricle. Which explanation is correct?
Mission check

?A student dissects a set of sheep's lungs. Near the lungs she finds two tubes side by side. Tube P has C-shaped rings of cartilage in its wall and stays open when empty. Tube Q has a thin, soft wall with no cartilage and collapses flat. Which identification do these observations best support?

Structure, position and connections, never shade or size alone: that is how you name what you find, whether cut open or sliced thin.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How can a fatty streak inside an artery wall end in a heart attack years later?

Make a guess: How can a fatty streak inside an artery wall end in a heart attack years later?

Mission 2

The heart feeds itself

  • Heart muscle needs a constant supply of oxygen and glucose.
  • It gets them from the coronary arteries, which branch from the aorta.
  • Blood inside the chambers does not supply the heart wall.
  • Coronary heart disease (CHD): coronary arteries narrowed or blocked.
  • Say: "occlusion starves the heart muscle of oxygen" — not: "blood can no longer pass through the chambers".
Mission 2

Atherosclerosis, step by step

  • (1) Endothelial dysfunction: the inner lining is damaged, e.g. by high blood pressure, smoking or high LDL cholesterol.
  • (2) Inflammatory response: white blood cells enter the wall and take up LDL cholesterol.
  • (3) Plaque (atheroma) builds inside the wall: cholesterol, white cells, fibrous tissue, calcium salts. The lumen narrows; the wall stiffens.
  • (4) Narrower, stiffer arteries raise blood pressure, which damages more endothelium.
  • Atherosclerosis: hardening and narrowing of arteries by atheroma.
  • Say: "atheroma forms within the artery wall" — not: "fat sticks to the inside like grease in a drainpipe".
Mission 2

From plaque to blockage

  • A plaque can rupture, exposing the tissue beneath.
  • Platelets and damaged tissue release thromboplastin.
  • Thromboplastin (with calcium ions) converts prothrombin into thrombin.
  • Thrombin converts soluble fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin: a mesh that traps cells.
  • A clot (thrombus) forms in the artery: thrombosis.
  • Plaque plus clot can occlude (block) the artery. Or the clot breaks off and blocks a smaller artery downstream.

Mission 2

What blockage does

  • Narrowed coronary artery: too little oxygen during exercise → chest pain (angina).
  • Blocked coronary artery: muscle beyond dies → myocardial infarction (heart attack).
  • Dead muscle is replaced by scar tissue, which does not contract.
  • A clot in an artery to the brain → stroke.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Countries that eat more fat have more heart disease. Does that prove the fat is the cause?

Make a guess: Countries that eat more fat have more heart disease. Does that prove the fat is the cause?

Mission 3

Correlation or cause?

  • Correlation: two variables change together.
  • Causal relationship: a change in one produces a change in the other.
  • A correlation can arise from a confounding variable (a third factor linked to both), from chance, or from cause running the other way.
  • Stronger evidence of cause: a mechanism; a controlled trial; more exposure, more disease (dose–response); many studies agreeing.
  • Say: "is correlated with" — not: "causes", until the evidence shows it.
Mission 3

Correlation coefficients

  • A correlation coefficient runs from −1 to +1.
  • The sign gives direction: + both rise together; − one rises as the other falls.
  • The size gives strength: near ±1 strong; near 0 little or none.
  • Sample size changes how far you can trust a coefficient, not how strong it is.
  • A statistical test tells you whether a coefficient is significant (see Mission 5).

Mission 3

Conflicting evidence: the HRT story

  • HRT (hormone replacement therapy): hormones taken to ease symptoms of the menopause.
  • Early observational studies: women taking HRT had less coronary heart disease. This was read as cause and effect.
  • Later randomised controlled trials: HRT caused a small increase in CHD risk.
  • Why? Women who chose HRT had higher socioeconomic status, which itself lowers CHD risk: a confounding variable.
  • Random allocation spreads confounders evenly between groups, so a trial isolates the treatment's own effect.
  • Say: "the correlation was real but not causal" — not: "the early studies got the numbers wrong".
Quick check
?Early epidemiological studies found that women taking HRT had a lower incidence of coronary heart disease (CHD). Later randomised controlled trials found that HRT slightly increased CHD risk. Which conclusion is best supported?
Mission 3

Judging a study

  • Sample selection: representative of the population; chosen at random; controls matched for age, sex and other factors.
  • Sample size: large, so chance differences matter less.
  • Validity: measures what it claims to; confounding variables controlled or allowed for.
  • Reliability: repeat studies find similar results.
  • Study types: cohort (follow people over time), case–control (compare people with and without the disease), randomised controlled trial.
  • Also ask: peer reviewed? Who funded it?
Mission 3

Feeling the risk vs the real risk

  • Risk: the probability that harm happens over a stated time.
  • People often overestimate risks that are rare, dramatic, unfamiliar or imposed on them.
  • They often underestimate risks that are familiar, chosen and slow: diet, inactivity, smoking and heart disease.
  • Other reasons: "it won't happen to me" (optimism), and news coverage of rare events.
  • So people may worry about the wrong things and ignore the big, controllable risks.
Mission 3

Cholesterol: from data to cause

  • Data: higher total and LDL cholesterol go with higher CVD rates; higher HDL goes with lower rates.
  • Correlation alone is not enough. The case for cause:
  • Mechanism: LDL cholesterol is found inside atheroma.
  • Genes: familial hypercholesterolaemia gives very high LDL from birth and early CHD.
  • Trials: lowering LDL with statins lowers events. Each 1 mmol dm⁻³ fall in LDL cuts major vascular events by about one fifth (real data: trial meta-analysis).
  • Dose–response: the higher the LDL, the higher the risk.
Mission 3

Worked example: BMI and waist-to-hip ratio

  • BMI = mass in kg ÷ (height in m)².
  • 85 kg, 1.75 m: 85 ÷ 1.75² = 85 ÷ 3.0625 = 27.8 kg m⁻².
  • Adult bands: below 18.5 underweight; 18.5–24.9 healthy; 25.0–29.9 overweight; 30 and above obese.
  • Waist-to-hip ratio = waist ÷ hip. 96 cm ÷ 104 cm = 0.92.
  • Fat stored round the abdomen carries more CVD risk. 0.90 or above (men) or 0.85 or above (women) signals raised risk.
  • People use these measures, with exercise, diet and not smoking, to reduce their CHD risk.

Mission 3

Exercise: too little and too much

  • Too little: higher risk of obesity, CVD and type 2 diabetes.
  • Too much: wear and tear on joints; suppression of the immune system (more infections after long, intense exercise).
  • Many studies find the lowest risk at moderate levels.
  • Most of these data are correlations. Active people may differ in diet, income or health to begin with.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. A study of 2000 adults found a correlation coefficient of −0.40 between hours of golf played per week and the incidence of coronary heart disease. Which conclusion is best supported?

Fat and heart disease, HRT and heart disease, golf and heart disease: a correlation is where the question starts, not where it ends.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Blood must flow freely through every vessel, yet seal a cut within minutes. How does it do both?

Make a guess: Blood must flow freely through every vessel, yet seal a cut within minutes. How does it do both?

Mission 4

Sealing a cut

  • Clotting is a non-specific defence: it closes the skin barrier, so blood stays in and pathogens stay out.
  • (1) At the cut, platelets stick to the damaged vessel and to each other: a plug. They release clotting factors.
  • (2) A cascade of enzyme reactions produces the enzyme thrombin.
  • (3) Thrombin converts soluble fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin threads.
  • (4) The fibrin mesh traps red blood cells: a clot.
  • Say: "thrombin converts fibrinogen to fibrin" — not: "fibrin threads in the blood tangle together".
Mission 4

The cascade by name

  • Damaged tissue and platelets release thromboplastin.
  • With calcium ions, this starts a chain that converts prothrombin (inactive, in plasma) into thrombin.
  • Thrombin: fibrinogen → fibrin.
Quick check
?Fibrinogen circulates in the blood all the time. Why does a clot form at a cut, but not throughout the blood vessels?
Mission 4

Why a cascade?

  • Each activated enzyme activates many molecules of the next.
  • The signal is amplified: a small injury makes a lot of fibrin, fast.
  • The cascade starts only where clotting factors are released: the clot stays at the wound.
  • Fibrinogen circulates all the time, but stays soluble until thrombin acts on it.
Quick check
?Fibrinogen circulates in the plasma of healthy people all the time, yet clots form only where a blood vessel has been damaged. Which statement best explains this?
Mission 4

A clot in the wrong place

  • An atheroma (plaque) in an artery wall can rupture.
  • The exposed plaque triggers the cascade: a clot (thrombus) forms inside the artery.
  • It can block the artery there, or break off and block a smaller one downstream.
  • Coronary artery blocked: heart muscle is starved of oxygen: myocardial infarction (heart attack).
  • Artery supplying the brain blocked: stroke.
  • Say: "a clot blocks the artery" — not: "the plaque itself always blocks the artery".
Quick check
?The drug warfarin blocks the use of vitamin K in the liver. The liver needs vitamin K to make prothrombin. What happens to the blood of a person taking warfarin?
Mission check

?People with haemophilia A lack Factor VIII, one of the clotting factors in the cascade that produces thrombin. Their platelets and fibrinogen are normal. What happens when such a person cuts their skin?

Fibrinogen flows everywhere, harmless. Only at a wound does a cascade make thrombin to turn it into fibrin. Break the cascade and the cut keeps bleeding.

Mission 5

Mission 5: A difference between two groups could be real or just luck. How do biologists decide which?

Make a guess: A difference between two groups could be real or just luck. How do biologists decide which?

Mission 5

Could it just be chance?

  • Samples vary, so two groups almost never match exactly.
  • Start with the null hypothesis: there is no difference (or no association, or no correlation).
  • A statistical test turns the data into one number, the test statistic.
  • Compare it with the critical value at p = 0.05.
  • Above the critical value: if the null hypothesis were true, a result this extreme would happen by chance less than 5% of the time. The result is significant: reject the null hypothesis.
  • Below it: not significant. The difference could be due to chance.
  • Then accept the null hypothesis.
  • Significant does not mean proved; not significant does not prove "no effect".
Mission 5

Which test?

  • Chi-squared (χ²): counts in categories, compared with expected counts.
  • Student's t-test: two means of continuous data (e.g. lengths, masses).
  • Correlation coefficient: is there a relationship between two variables measured on the same individuals or sites?
  • Ask: Counts or measurements? Difference or relationship?
  • t-tests and correlation coefficients are worked through in Mission 6.
Quick check
?A student measures the lengths of 20 leaves from plants growing in the sun and 20 from plants growing in the shade. She wants to know whether mean leaf length differs between the two. Which statistical test should she use?

Worked example: chi-squared beyond genetics

Daisies counted in two areas of a field of equal size, one trampled and one not (illustrative data). Null hypothesis: no difference, so equal numbers are expected.

Mission 5

Correlation

  • Plot two measured variables on a scatter graph (scattergram).
  • Positive correlation: both rise together. Negative: one rises as the other falls.
  • A correlation coefficient runs from −1 to +1.
  • The sign gives the direction; the size gives the strength. 0 means no correlation.
  • −0.85 is as strong as +0.85.
Mission 5

Correlation is not cause

  • A correlation, even a strong one, can arise from:
    • chance (small samples);
    • a third variable linked to both (a confounding variable);
    • cause running the other way.
  • Evidence for cause: a mechanism; controlled experiments or randomised controlled trials; the effect grows with the dose; many studies agree.
  • Smoking and lung cancer: a strong correlation, plus a mechanism (carcinogens in tar damage DNA) and animal experiments. That combination is why it is accepted as causal.
  • Climate records: CO2 concentration and temperature correlate; the known mechanism (CO2 absorbs infrared radiation) is part of the evidence for cause.
  • Say: "there is a positive correlation between X and Y" — not: "X causes Y", unless there is evidence for cause.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Leaves in the shade look bigger than leaves in the sun. How big must the gap be before you believe it?

Make a guess: Leaves in the shade look bigger than leaves in the sun. How big must the gap be before you believe it?

Mission 6

Student's t-test

  • Use it to compare two means of continuous data that are roughly normally distributed.
  • Null hypothesis: there is no significant difference between the two means.
  • t gets bigger when the means are further apart, the SDs are smaller, or the samples are larger.
  • t = |x̄1 − x̄2| ÷ √(s1²/n1 + s2²/n2)
  • Degrees of freedom = n1 + n2 − 2.
  • t ≥ critical value at p = 0.05: reject the null hypothesis. The difference is significant.
Mission 6

Worked example: t-test

Limpet shell height on an exposed and a sheltered shore (illustrative data), 10 limpets each.

ShoreMean height, x̄ / mmSD, s / mmn
Exposed18.22.110
Sheltered15.62.410
  • Difference in means = 18.2 − 15.6 = 2.6 mm.
  • s1²/n1 = 2.1² ÷ 10 = 0.441. s2²/n2 = 2.4² ÷ 10 = 0.576.
  • √(0.441 + 0.576) = √1.017 = 1.008.
  • t = 2.6 ÷ 1.008 = 2.58.
  • Degrees of freedom = 10 + 10 − 2 = 18. Critical value at p = 0.05: 2.10.
  • 2.58 > 2.10: reject the null hypothesis. Shell height differs significantly between the shores.
  • Why it differs (wave action? predators?) needs a separate hypothesis and investigation.

Mission 6

Spearman's rank correlation

  • Tests whether two variables measured on the same individuals or sites are correlated.
  • Your board asks for "a correlation coefficient": Spearman's rank is one, and works for data that are not normally distributed.
  • Null hypothesis: there is no correlation between the two variables.
  • Rank each variable separately (smallest = 1). Tied values share the mean of their ranks.
  • d = difference between the two ranks for each pair.
  • rₛ = 1 − 6Σd² ÷ n(n² − 1).
  • Compare |rₛ| with the critical value for n pairs. Equal or above: significant correlation.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Dissect safely (cut away, sharp blade, board, gloves) and identify by structure, position and connections; record and measure.

Mission 2

  • Atheroma builds inside a damaged artery wall; it narrows the lumen and raises blood pressure.
  • A ruptured plaque triggers a clot; occlusion of a coronary artery starves heart muscle of oxygen.

Mission 3

  • A correlation coefficient gives direction (sign) and strength (size); even a strong correlation does not prove cause.
  • Confounding variables explain conflicting evidence, as with HRT; randomised trials test cause.
  • Risk can be estimated (BMI, waist-to-hip, cholesterol), misperceived, and reduced by lifestyle or treatment, each with benefits and risks.

Mission 4

  • Platelets at a wound release clotting factors; a cascade makes thrombin; thrombin turns soluble fibrinogen into a fibrin mesh that traps red blood cells.
  • Thromboplastin and calcium ions convert prothrombin to thrombin.

Mission 5

  • Null hypothesis → test statistic → compare with the critical value at p = 0.05 → significant or not. Neither outcome is proof.
  • χ² compares counts with expected counts; degrees of freedom = categories − 1.
  • A correlation coefficient measures direction and strength (−1 to +1); correlation alone never shows cause.

Mission 6

  • t-test: two means; t = |x̄1 − x̄2| ÷ √(s1²/n1 + s2²/n2); df = n1 + n2 − 2.
  • Spearman's rank: rₛ = 1 − 6Σd² ÷ n(n² − 1); compare |rₛ| with the critical value for n pairs.
  • At or above the critical value at p = 0.05: significant. Never "proved".
