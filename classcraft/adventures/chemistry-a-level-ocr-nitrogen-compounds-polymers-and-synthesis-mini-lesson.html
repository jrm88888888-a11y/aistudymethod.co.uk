Mission 1

Mission 1: Fish smells fishy because of amines. Why does a squeeze of lemon juice take the smell away?

Make a guess: Fish smells fishy because of amines. Why does a squeeze of lemon juice take the smell away?

Mission 1

What is an amine?

  • Take ammonia, NH3. Replace H atoms with alkyl or aryl groups: an amine.
  • Primary: one C on the N, e.g. butylamine, CH3CH2CH2CH2NH2, and phenylamine, C6H5NH2.
  • Secondary: two C on the N, e.g. (CH3)2NH. Tertiary: three, e.g. (CH3)3N.
  • Every amine N has a lone pair. That drives all its chemistry.
  • Amide, not amine: N bonded to a C=O carbon (–CONH2, –CONHR).
Quick check
?Which statement about the compound (CH3)3CNH2 is correct?
Mission 1

Amines are bases

  • The N lone pair accepts H⁺: amines are bases, like ammonia.
  • In water, a weak base: CH3CH2CH2CH2NH2 + H2O ⇌ CH3CH2CH2CH2NH3⁺ + OH⁻. The solution is alkaline.
  • With acids, salts: CH3CH2CH2CH2NH2 + HCl → CH3CH2CH2CH2NH3⁺Cl⁻ (butylammonium chloride). No water forms.
  • Add NaOH to the salt and the free amine comes back: RNH3⁺ + OH⁻ → RNH2 + H2O.
  • Back to the opening question: fishy-smelling amines are volatile. Lemon's acid turns them into ionic salts, which don't evaporate.
Mission 1

Making aliphatic amines

  • Route 1: haloalkane + excess ammonia in ethanol, heated in a sealed tube.
  • CH3CH2CH2CH2Br + 2NH3 → CH3CH2CH2CH2NH2 + NH4Br
  • Excess NH3 makes the primary amine the main product, but some 2° and 3° amine still forms.
  • Route 2: reduce a nitrile: CH3CH2CH2CN + 4[H] → CH3CH2CH2CH2NH2. Only the primary amine forms.
  • Reducing agent: H2 with a nickel catalyst.
Mission 1

Amines attack haloalkanes too

  • The amine product also has a lone pair: it is a nucleophile.
  • Primary amine + haloalkane → secondary amine; then → tertiary amine.
  • Use this to make secondary and tertiary amines from amines.
Mission 1

Making aromatic amines

  • Reduce nitrobenzene with tin and concentrated HCl, heated under reflux.
  • C6H5NO2 + 6[H] → C6H5NH2 + 2H2O
  • In all that acid, the product is the phenylammonium ion, C6H5NH3⁺: a salt.
  • Add NaOH(aq) to free phenylamine: C6H5NH3⁺ + OH⁻ → C6H5NH2 + H2O.
  • Nitrobenzene comes from nitrating benzene.
Quick check
?Nitrobenzene is heated under reflux with tin and concentrated hydrochloric acid. Excess sodium hydroxide solution is then added before the phenylamine is separated. Why is the NaOH needed?
Mission 1

Amines + acyl chlorides: amides

  • The N lone pair attacks the δ+ carbonyl carbon of an acyl chloride.
  • Ammonia gives a primary amide, RCONH2. A primary amine gives a secondary (N-substituted) amide, RCONHR′.
  • CH3COCl + 2CH3CH2CH2CH2NH2 → CH3CONHCH2CH2CH2CH3 + CH3CH2CH2CH2NH3⁺Cl⁻
  • Product: N-butylethanamide. Adding CH3CO– to N is ethanoylation.
Mission check

?A reaction mixture contains phenylamine and some unreacted nitrobenzene, dissolved in an organic solvent. It is shaken with dilute hydrochloric acid in a separating funnel. Which compound moves into the water layer, and why?

Same chemistry as lemon on fish. An acid turns an amine into a salt, which stays in water instead of escaping.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How can a chemist make a molecule's carbon chain exactly one atom longer?

Make a guess: How can a chemist make a molecule's carbon chain exactly one atom longer?

Mission 2

Building the skeleton

  • Most organic reactions only swap functional groups. The carbon chain stays the same length.
  • To make a longer chain, you need a reaction that forms a new C–C bond.
  • The trick: a carbon nucleophile, a carbon atom with a lone pair or a δ− charge.
  • Cyanide, ⁻:C≡N, is the simplest: it adds one carbon.
  • Friedel–Crafts reactions join a carbon chain to a benzene ring (later in this mission).
Mission 2

Cyanide + haloalkane

  • Reagent: KCN in ethanol, heat under reflux.
  • CH3CH2Br + CN⁻ → CH3CH2CN + Br⁻
  • Product: propanenitrile. The chain grows from 2 C to 3 C.
  • Naming: count the C≡N carbon in the chain. CH3CH2CN is propanenitrile.
  • Mechanism: nucleophilic substitution. Lone pair on the C of CN⁻ attacks the δ+ C; the C–Br electrons go to Br.
  • KCN is highly toxic. Acid turns cyanide into HCN gas, so cyanide and acid only ever meet in a fume cupboard, in small amounts, under controlled conditions.
Mission 2

Cyanide + carbonyl

  • Aldehydes and ketones add cyanide across C=O: hydroxynitriles.
  • Reagent: NaCN(aq) / H⁺(aq), a source of HCN.
  • CH3CHO → CH3CH(OH)CN, 2-hydroxypropanenitrile. Chain: 2 C → 3 C.
  • Mechanism: nucleophilic addition. Product is racemic when the new C is chiral.
  • Hazards: HCN is a very toxic gas, and cyanide salts are toxic too. Fume cupboard, gloves, small amounts.
Quick check
?1-Bromobutane is heated under reflux with potassium cyanide in ethanol. What is the organic product?
Mission 2

Nitrile → carboxylic acid

  • Acid hydrolysis: heat under reflux with dilute HCl.
  • CH3CH2CN + 2H2O + HCl → CH3CH2COOH + NH4Cl
  • The C≡N carbon becomes the –COOH carbon.
  • From a hydroxynitrile: CH3CH(OH)CN → CH3CH(OH)COOH, 2-hydroxypropanoic acid (lactic acid).
Mission 2

Nitrile → amine

  • Reduction with H2 and a nickel catalyst: CH3CH2CN + 2H2 → CH3CH2CH2NH2
  • Product: propylamine (propan-1-amine), one carbon longer than bromoethane.
  • Unlike ammonia + haloalkane, this gives only the primary amine: no further substitution.
Quick check
?2-Hydroxypropanenitrile, CH3CH(OH)CN, is heated under reflux with dilute hydrochloric acid. What is the organic product?
Mission 2

Friedel–Crafts: C–C to a ring

  • Benzene + a haloalkane or acyl chloride, with a halogen carrier such as AlCl3.
  • Alkylation: C6H6 + CH3CH2Cl → C6H5CH2CH3 + HCl (ethylbenzene).
  • Acylation: C6H6 + CH3COCl → C6H5COCH3 + HCl (phenylethanone).
  • AlCl3 makes the electrophile (CH3CH2⁺ or CH3CO⁺); then electrophilic substitution.
  • Why it matters: it is a way to attach carbon chains to an aromatic ring.
Mission check

?Bromomethane, CH3Br, has one carbon. Which route turns it into ethylamine, CH3CH2NH2?

Cyanide adds the extra carbon, then you choose what the C≡N becomes: an amine, or (by hydrolysis) an acid.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why does making a polyester give off water, when making some other plastics loses no atoms at all?

Make a guess: Why does making a polyester give off water, when making some other plastics loses no atoms at all?

Mission 3

Two ways to build a polymer

  • Addition polymerisation: monomers with a C=C join up. The polymer is the only product: no atoms lost.
  • Condensation polymerisation: each monomer has two functional groups. Two groups react to make a link.
  • Every link releases a small molecule: usually H2O.
  • With an acyl chloride as monomer, the small molecule is HCl.
  • So a condensation polymer has fewer atoms than the monomers it came from.
Mission 3

Making a polyester

  • Diol + dicarboxylic acid → polyester + water.
  • –OH reacts with –COOH: an ester link, –COO–, plus H2O.
  • Each monomer has a group at both ends, so the chain grows in both directions.
  • Example: ethane-1,2-diol + benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid → PET.
  • A dicarbonyl dichloride reacts with a diol in the same way, releasing HCl.
Mission 3

Drawing the repeat unit

  • Draw the two monomers end to end.
  • Remove H from each –OH and OH from each –COOH.
  • Join the fragments with ester links. Add open bonds at both ends, brackets and n.
  • The repeat unit holds one residue of each monomer: two links, two H2O.
  • PET: –[–OCH2CH2OOC–C6H4–CO–]n–
Quick check
?Ethane-1,2-diol, HOCH2CH2OH, and hexanedioic acid, HOOC(CH2)4COOH, react to form a polyester. Which option gives the repeat unit and describes it correctly?
Mission 3

Polyamides

  • Diamine + dicarboxylic acid → polyamide + water.
  • –NH2 reacts with –COOH: an amide link, –CONH–, plus H2O.
  • You won't need to recall named polymers: work from the monomers you are given.
  • A dicarbonyl dichloride (ClOC–R–COCl) reacts faster, releasing HCl instead of H2O.
Quick check
?Hexane-1,6-diamine, H2N(CH2)6NH2, and decanedioic acid, HOOC(CH2)8COOH, react to form the polyamide nylon 6,10. Which option gives the repeat unit and describes it correctly?
Mission 3

Working backwards: chain → monomers

  • Find the links in the chain: –COO– (ester) or –CONH– (amide).
  • Break each link at the C–O or C–N single bond.
  • Add OH back to the C=O carbon; add H back to the O or N.
  • Diacid + diol, or diacid + diamine, or one amino acid: read off the monomers.
  • Which type? Main chain all carbon, no links → addition. Ester or amide links in the chain → condensation.
Mission 3

Hydrolysis breaks the links

  • Water reacts with each ester or amide link: the reverse of condensation.
  • Hot acid or hot alkali speeds it up.
  • Polyester + acid → diol + dicarboxylic acid.
  • Polyester + NaOH → diol + dicarboxylate salt (–COO⁻Na⁺).
  • Polyamide + acid → dicarboxylic acid + diammonium salt (–NH3⁺Cl⁻ with HCl).
  • Polyamide + NaOH → diamine + dicarboxylate salt.
Quick check
?A sample of nylon 6,6, made from hexane-1,6-diamine and hexanedioic acid, is boiled with excess sodium hydroxide solution until it is fully hydrolysed. Which pair of products forms?
Mission check

?Lactic acid, CH3CH(OH)COOH, is made from plant sugars. Its molecules join into poly(lactic acid), PLA, used for compostable cups. Which statement describes how PLA forms?

Same reason as for PET. Two groups react to make each link, and the leftover H and OH leave as water.

Mission 4: How can one small molecule react with both acids and bases?

Make a guess: How can one small molecule react with both acids and bases?

Mission 4

Amino acids

  • An α-amino acid (2-amino acid): RCH(NH2)COOH.
  • Both groups sit on one carbon, the α-carbon. R is the side chain.
  • Glycine: R = H, H2NCH2COOH. Alanine: R = CH3, H2NCH(CH3)COOH.
  • About 20 amino acids build proteins. They differ only in R.
Mission 4

Two groups, two jobs

  • –COOH is an acid group. –NH2 is a base group, like ammonia.
  • With alkali: H2NCH2COOH + NaOH → H2NCH2COO⁻Na⁺ + H2O.
  • With acid: H2NCH2COOH + HCl → ⁺H3NCH2COOH Cl⁻.
  • Each group reacts with its opposite: the molecule is amphoteric.
Mission 4

Making an ester

  • The –COOH reacts with alcohols like any carboxylic acid.
  • Warm with methanol and a little concentrated H2SO4 (catalyst).
  • H2NCH(R)COOH + CH3OH ⇌ H2NCH(R)COOCH3 + H2O.
  • The ester has no –COOH, but the –NH2 is still there to react with acids.
Quick check
?Glycine, H2NCH2COOH, is added to excess dilute hydrochloric acid at room temperature. Which species of glycine is present?
Quick check
?Alanine is added to excess sodium hydroxide solution. Which species of alanine is present?
Mission check

?2-Aminobutanoic acid, CH3CH2CH(NH2)COOH, is an α-amino acid not found in proteins. Which equation shows a correct reaction?

Any α-amino acid has an acid group and a base group, so it reacts with acids and with alkalis.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Why do chemists plan the route to a new molecule by starting from the finished product?

Make a guess: Why do chemists plan the route to a new molecule by starting from the finished product?

Mission 5

Work backwards

  • Start from the target. Name its functional groups and count its carbons.
  • Ask: which one known reaction makes this group? That gives the compound one step back.
  • Repeat until you reach the starting material you were given.
  • Carbon count goes up? You need a C–C bond-forming step (see Mission 2).
  • Then write the route forwards, with reagents and conditions for every step.
Mission 5

Your reaction map

  • Alkene → haloalkane (HBr) · → alcohol (steam, acid catalyst).
  • Haloalkane → alcohol (NaOH(aq)) · → amine (NH3) · → nitrile (KCN, +1 C) · → alkene (KOH in ethanol).
  • Primary alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid (oxidation). Secondary alcohol → ketone. Acid + alcohol → ester.
  • Aldehyde or ketone → hydroxynitrile (cyanide, +1 C) · → alcohol (reduction).
  • Nitrile → amine (reduction).
  • Nitrile → carboxylic acid (hydrolysis).
Mission 5

Worked example: three steps

  • Target: propylamine, CH3CH2CH2NH2 (3 C). Start: ethene (2 C).
  • Back 1: an amine from a nitrile by reduction → propanenitrile, CH3CH2CN.
  • Back 2: a nitrile from a haloalkane, adding 1 C → bromoethane, CH3CH2Br.
  • Back 3: bromoethane from ethene + HBr. Reached the start.
  • Forwards: (1) HBr, room temperature. (2) KCN in ethanol, reflux. (3) Reduce the nitrile.
  • Step 3 reagent: H2/Ni.
Quick check
?Which compound reacts with KCN in ethanol to give butanenitrile, CH3CH2CH2CN?
Mission 5

Molecules with several groups

  • In a multifunctional molecule, each group reacts as it normally would.
  • Example: HOCH2CH=CHCHO has a primary alcohol, an alkene and an aldehyde.
  • Bromine water: adds across the C=C. Decolourised.
  • Tollens' reagent: oxidises the aldehyde only. Silver mirror.
  • Acidified dichromate(VI), reflux: oxidises both –CH2OH and –CHO to –COOH.
  • Exam questions may give you extra reactions: use them as new steps.
Mission 5

On a ring, order matters

  • Groups already on a benzene ring decide where the next one goes.
  • –NO2: 3-directing. –NH2 and –OH: 2- and 4-directing.
  • Target: 3-bromophenylamine from benzene.
  • (1) Nitrate (conc HNO3 + conc H2SO4) → nitrobenzene.
  • (2) Brominate (Br2 + halogen carrier) → 1-bromo-3-nitrobenzene: –NO2 sends Br to position 3.
  • (3) Reduce –NO2 (Sn + conc HCl, then NaOH) → 3-bromophenylamine.
  • Reduce first, and –NH2 directs Br to 2, 4 and 6 instead.
Quick check
?A chemist wants to make 1-chloro-3-nitrobenzene from benzene. Cl on a ring is 2- and 4-directing. Which plan works?
Mission check

?A chemist needs 2-hydroxybutanenitrile, CH3CH2CH(OH)CN, and has propan-1-ol, CH3CH2CH2OH. Which two-step plan works?

Start from the target, find the one reaction that makes it, then step back again. The route writes itself.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Amines: the N lone pair makes them bases (salts with acids, freed by alkali) and nucleophiles.
  • Make them: haloalkane + excess NH3; nitrile reduction; nitroarene + Sn / conc. HCl, then NaOH.
  • Amines + acyl chlorides → N-substituted amides.

Mission 2

  • CN⁻ adds one carbon: with haloalkanes (substitution) or carbonyls (addition).
  • Nitriles can be reduced to amines.
  • Nitriles can be hydrolysed to carboxylic acids.

Mission 3

  • Addition: C=C monomers, no atoms lost. Condensation: two groups react at each link, losing H2O (or HCl).
  • Polyester: –COO– links from diol + diacid. Polyamide: –CONH– links from diamine + diacid, or from amino acids.
  • Ester and amide links are hydrolysed by hot acid or alkali.

Mission 4

  • An amino acid has an acid group (–COOH) and a base group (–NH2), so it reacts with both acids and bases.

Mission 5

  • Work backwards from the target, one known reaction per step; count carbons to spot a C–C step.
  • On a ring, add groups in the order that puts each one where its director sends it.
