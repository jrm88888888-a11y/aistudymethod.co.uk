Mission 1

Mission 1: How does water reach the top of the tallest tree with no pump and no energy spent by the tree?

Make a guess: How does water reach the top of the tallest tree with no pump and no energy spent by the tree?

Mission 1

Transpiration: the price of carbon dioxide

  • Stomata open to let carbon dioxide in for photosynthesis.
  • Water evaporates from the walls of mesophyll cells into the air spaces.
  • Water vapour diffuses out through the open stomata: transpiration.
  • Transpiration is an unavoidable consequence of gas exchange.
  • Say: "water vapour diffuses out while stomata are open" — not: "the plant opens stomata to lose water".
Quick check
?Why is transpiration described as a consequence of gas exchange rather than as a process the leaf carries out for its own sake?
Mission 1

Xylem vessels: built for flow

  • Xylem carries water and mineral ions from roots to leaves.
  • Dead cells, no contents: nothing obstructs the flow.
  • End walls lost: cells join into one continuous tube.
  • Lignified walls: rigid, so the vessel does not collapse under tension; also waterproof, and support the plant.
  • Pits: gaps in the lignin. Water moves sideways between vessels and out to leaf cells.
  • Say: "lignin stops the vessel collapsing under tension" — not: "lignin is only for support".
Mission 1

Seeing the lignin

  • Light microscope, L.S.: lignin as rings, spirals or a net; T.S.: wide, empty, thick-walled cells.
  • Electron microscope: thick, layered secondary wall; pits where only the thin primary wall remains; no cytoplasm.
Quick check
?Why are the walls of xylem vessels thickened with lignin?
Mission 1

Where the xylem is

  • Stem: vascular bundles in a ring near the outside. Pith in the centre.
  • In each bundle: xylem inside, phloem outside, cambium between.
  • Cortex between the ring and the epidermis.
Quick check
?A student is drawing a plan diagram from a micrograph of a transverse section of a dicotyledonous stem. Which description matches what should be drawn?
Mission 1

Cohesion–tension: pulled from the top

  • (1) Water evaporates from leaf cell walls. Water is drawn out of the xylem, through the cell walls, to replace it.
  • (2) This puts the xylem water under tension.
  • (3) Cohesion: hydrogen bonds hold water molecules together, so the whole column is pulled up unbroken.
  • Adhesion: water is attracted to the hydrophilic vessel walls, which helps it creep up narrow spaces.
  • The flow from roots to leaves is the transpiration stream.
  • Passive: no ATP. The energy comes from the Sun evaporating water.
  • Evidence: tree trunks shrink slightly in diameter at midday; cut xylem draws air in, not water out.
  • Say: "water is pulled up under tension" — not: "the roots pump water up".
Quick check
?What generates the force that draws water up the xylem from the roots to the leaves?
Mission check

?Florists cut the stems of fresh flowers under water. A stem cut in air and then put in a vase often wilts, even though its cut end is in water. Which explanation is correct?

The tree and the vase work the same way: evaporation pulls, cohesion passes the pull down, and the column must stay unbroken.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How does water get from the soil into a root, and how do desert plants keep hold of it?

Make a guess: How does water get from the soil into a root, and how do desert plants keep hold of it?

Mission 2

Inside a root

  • Epidermis with root hairs: a huge surface for uptake.
  • Cortex: loosely packed cells; storage.
  • Endodermis: one ring of cells round the centre.
  • Central vascular cylinder (stele): xylem forms a star or X; phloem lies between its arms.
  • Root: xylem in the centre. Stem: xylem in a ring of bundles.
Quick check
?In a transverse section of a dicotyledonous root, where are the xylem and the phloem found?
Mission 2Practical

Drawing a root section

  • Examine prepared slides of T.S. dicotyledon root (e.g. Ranunculus).
  • Low power first: find the epidermis, cortex, endodermis and central cylinder.
  • Plan diagram: tissue outlines only, true proportions, no shading, ruled labels.
  • Check: xylem star in the centre, phloem between its arms. If you see a ring of bundles, it is a stem.
Mission 2

Into the root hair

  • Root hair cells take in mineral ions by active transport: carrier proteins, ATP.
  • This lowers the water potential of the cell.
  • Soil water has a higher water potential. Water enters by osmosis.
  • Root hairs: long and thin, a large surface; many mitochondria for ATP.
  • Say: "water moves down a water potential gradient" — not: "water is actively pumped in".
Mission 2

Across the cortex

  • Apoplast: through cell walls and the spaces between cells. No membranes crossed. Fast; cohesion pulls water along.
  • Symplast: through the cytoplasm, from cell to cell via plasmodesmata.
  • Vacuolar: through cytoplasm and vacuoles, crossing the tonoplast too.
Mission 2

The endodermis: a gatekeeper

  • Endodermis cell walls carry the Casparian strip: a waterproof band of suberin.
  • It blocks the apoplast. Water and ions must cross a membrane into the symplast.
  • So the root controls which ions reach the xylem.
  • Endodermal cells actively transport ions into the xylem. The water potential of the xylem sap falls; water follows by osmosis.
  • Say: "the Casparian strip blocks the apoplast pathway" — not: "it blocks all water".
Quick check
?A mutant plant forms no Casparian strip in its endodermis. Which effect is most likely?
Mission 2

Root pressure: a push from below

  • Cells around the xylem actively transport mineral ions into it.
  • Water follows by osmosis. A positive pressure builds and pushes water up: root pressure.
  • Evidence: sap oozes from cut stems; guttation (drops at leaf tips); both stop with respiratory inhibitors.
  • It matters when transpiration is low: humid air, or spring before deciduous leaves open.
  • Too small to lift water up a tall tree. Cohesion–tension does that.
  • Say: "ions are pumped; water follows by osmosis" — not: "root cells pump water".
Quick check
?A cut stem of a well-watered plant exudes sap from the xylem for several hours. When the roots are treated with a respiratory inhibitor, the exudation stops. What does this show about how the sap was being pushed out?
Mission 2

Xerophytes: the compromise

  • Xerophytes: plants adapted to dry habitats.
  • They must open stomata for carbon dioxide, so they cannot stop water loss. They reduce it.
  • Smaller gradient: sunken stomata, hairs, rolled or curved leaves trap humid air.
  • Less surface: reduced leaf area, spines, fewer stomata, a thick waxy cuticle.
  • More water in: deep or widespread roots; succulent tissue stores it.
Mission 2

Marram grass and cacti

  • Marram grass (sand dunes): in dry wind, hinge cells lose water and the leaf rolls up.
  • Its stomata lie on the inner surface, in pits between ridges, among hairs. Thick cuticle outside.
  • On a T.S. marram slide find: rolled shape, ridges and grooves, hairs, hinge cells, thick outer cuticle.
  • Cacti: leaves reduced to spines; a green, fleshy stem photosynthesises.
  • Thick cuticle; succulent tissue stores water; widespread shallow roots catch brief rain.
Mission 2

Hydrophytes: the water lily

  • Hydrophytes: plants adapted to living in water.
  • Floating leaves: stomata on the upper surface, open to the air.
  • Aerenchyma: large air spaces keep leaves afloat and carry oxygen to submerged parts.
  • Thin cuticle and little lignified tissue: water loss is no threat, and water supports the plant.
  • On a T.S. water lily slide find: upper-surface stomata, large air spaces, thin cuticle.
Quick check
?A cactus grows where rain falls only a few times a year. Which explanation of one of its features is correct?
Mission check

?A gardener scatters a large amount of fertiliser on dry soil around a plant. Within a day the plant wilts, although the soil has not dried further. Which explanation is correct?

Ions in, water follows: roots take up water only while the soil has a higher water potential than the root.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why does a plant lose water fastest on a warm, dry, windy day, and how can you measure it?

Make a guess: Why does a plant lose water fastest on a warm, dry, windy day, and how can you measure it?

Mission 3

Four outside factors

  • Transpiration rate depends on the water-vapour gradient from air spaces to air, and on how open the stomata are.
Factor risesRateWhy
Light intensity↑stomata open wider
Temperature↑faster evaporation and diffusion; warm air holds more vapour, so the gradient is steeper
Humidity↓less steep gradient
Air movement↑humid air next to the leaf is blown away; gradient steeper
  • Say: "humid air reduces the water-vapour gradient" — not: "humid air makes the leaf wetter, so it loses more".
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A potometer was used to estimate transpiration by a leafy shoot at 20 °C. Water uptake in still air was 0.8 cm³ per hour; with a fan blowing air across the leaves, 1.9 cm³ per hour; in still air with a clear plastic bag enclosing the shoot, 0.3 cm³ per hour. Which conclusion is supported by these results?
Mission 3Practical

The potometer

  • Specified practical: investigation into transpiration using a simple potometer.
  • Cut the shoot under water, at a slant. Fit it to the potometer under water: no air gaps.
  • Seal joints with petroleum jelly. Dry the leaves. Let the shoot settle for 5 minutes.
  • Let in one air bubble. Time how far it moves along the scale. Reset it from the reservoir. Repeat; take a mean.
  • Change one factor (e.g. fan, lamp distance). Keep the others, and the same shoot, constant.
  • Say: "a potometer measures water uptake" — not: "water loss". A little water is used in photosynthesis or kept in cells.
Mission 3

Worked example: rate from the bubble

Illustrative data: capillary radius 0.50 mm; bubble moves 45 mm in 5.0 min.

  • Water taken up = volume of a cylinder = πr²l.
  • = π × (0.50 mm)² × 45 mm = 35 mm³.
  • Rate = 35 mm³ ÷ 5.0 min = 7.1 mm³ min⁻¹.
  • To compare shoots, divide by leaf area (trace leaves on squared paper).
Quick check
?In a potometer, the capillary tube has a radius of 0.40 mm. The air bubble moves 30 mm in 10 minutes. What is the rate of water uptake?
Mission 3Practical

Looking inside a stem

  • Examine slides of T.S. dicotyledon primary stem.
  • Stain: toluidine blue turns lignified walls (xylem, sclerenchyma) blue-green and other walls purple. Phloroglucinol with acid turns lignin red.
  • Thin sections let light through: cut with a sharp blade, keep sections wet.
Mission 3

Drawing a plan diagram

  • A plan diagram shows tissue outlines only: no individual cells.
  • Sharp pencil, clear continuous lines, no shading.
  • Keep the proportions of the tissues true.
  • Label with ruled lines that do not cross.
Quick check
?A celery stalk stood for two hours in water containing a red dye. In a transverse section, red spots form a ring near the outside of the stalk. Under the microscope, each red spot is the inner part of a vascular bundle. Which tissue has carried the dye?
Mission check

?A grower's greenhouse plants wilt on hot afternoons. The grower wants to slow their transpiration. Which change would do this?

Warm, dry, moving air steepens the gradient; humid, still air flattens it. The plant does not decide: diffusion does.

Mission 4

Mission 4: How does a plant send sugar up to its buds and down to its roots at the same time?

Make a guess: How does a plant send sugar up to its buds and down to its roots at the same time?

Mission 4

Sources and sinks

  • Phloem carries organic substances, mainly sucrose and amino acids: translocation.
  • Source: where sugars enter the phloem. A photosynthesising leaf; a storage root in spring.
  • Sink: where sugars leave the phloem to be used or stored. Roots, meristems, fruits, growing storage organs.
  • Sap flows from source to sink: up or down.
  • Different sieve tubes can carry sap in opposite directions at the same time.
  • Say: "from source to sink" — not: "always down from the leaves".
Mission 4

Sieve tubes and companion cells

  • Sieve tube elements: living cells, end to end, forming sieve tubes.
  • End walls are sieve plates, full of pores: sap flows from cell to cell.
  • No nucleus, few organelles, thin layer of cytoplasm: little blocks the flow.
  • Companion cells: nucleus, dense cytoplasm, many mitochondria: they make the ATP for loading.
  • Many plasmodesmata link each companion cell to its sieve tube element.
  • Walls of cellulose, not lignified.
  • Say: "sieve tube elements are living cells without a nucleus" — not: "dead like xylem".
Mission 4

Phloem under the microscope

  • Light microscope, L.S.: long sieve tube elements; sieve plates as cross-walls; narrow companion cells alongside.
  • Light microscope, T.S.: a sieve plate looks like a sieve; companion cells small and densely stained.
  • Electron microscope: sieve pores; a thin layer of cytoplasm against the wall, with smooth ER, a few mitochondria and phloem protein.
  • Electron microscope, companion cell: nucleus, many mitochondria and ribosomes, plasmodesmata to the sieve tube element.
Quick check
?Which feature of a sieve tube element helps sap to flow along the phloem?
Mission 4

Loading at the source

  • (1) The companion cell uses ATP to pump H⁺ out: active transport.
  • (2) H⁺ diffuses back in through a co-transporter protein, carrying sucrose in with it.
  • (3) Sucrose passes into the sieve tube element through plasmodesmata.
  • At the sink, sucrose is unloaded and used in respiration or converted to starch.
Mission 4

Mass flow

  • (4) At the source, sucrose lowers the water potential of the sieve tube.
  • (5) Water enters from the xylem by osmosis. Hydrostatic pressure rises.
  • (6) At the sink, sucrose leaves; water potential rises; water leaves; pressure falls.
  • (7) Sap flows down the pressure gradient, from source to sink.
  • ATP is spent on loading, not on pushing the sap.
Quick check
?In spring, sugars stored in the roots of a deciduous tree are converted to sucrose and carried in the phloem to the opening buds. Which statement about this transport is correct?
Mission 4

The evidence

  • Ringing: remove a ring of bark, and with it the phloem, from a woody stem. The xylem is left. Sugars pile up above the ring; the bark swells. Leaves stay turgid.
  • Tracers: give a leaf ¹⁴CO2. It makes ¹⁴C-sucrose. An autoradiograph (photographic film laid on a stem section) shows ¹⁴C in the phloem, then in the sinks.
  • Aphids: the stylet enters one sieve tube. Cut the aphid away: sap keeps oozing from the stylet, under pressure. It is rich in sucrose.
  • Energy: respiratory inhibitors, lack of oxygen or cold slow translocation. Companion cells are packed with mitochondria.
  • Two-way flow: ¹⁴C given to one leaf moves both up and down the stem.
Mission 4

Weighing up mass flow

  • For: sap in sieve tubes is under pressure (stylets ooze); sucrose concentration is higher at sources than at sinks; blocking ATP production stops translocation.
  • Against: sieve plates should slow mass flow, so why keep them?; different solutes may move at different speeds, which one bulk flow would not predict.
  • Diffusion: far too slow. Sucrose would take about 30 years to diffuse 1 m; translocation moves sap about 1 m per hour.
  • Cytoplasmic strands: strands through the sieve pores might carry solutes, both ways. Mature sieve tubes show little organised cytoplasm, so most evidence favours mass flow.

Mission check

?A young leaf near a shoot tip is still expanding. It imports sucrose through the phloem. Weeks later, fully grown, the same leaf exports sucrose. Which explanation is correct?

Up to the buds, down to the roots, in and then out of the same leaf: sap always flows from where sugar is loaded to where it is used.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Xylem vessels are dead, hollow, lignified tubes with pits: a continuous path that does not collapse under tension.
  • Evaporation from leaves puts the xylem under tension; cohesion pulls the whole column up. No ATP is spent.

Mission 2

  • Roots pump in mineral ions; water follows by osmosis.
  • The Casparian strip forces water and ions through the symplast at the endodermis.
  • Xerophytes cannot stop water loss while gas exchange goes on; they reduce the gradient, the area and the route.

Mission 3

  • Light, temperature and air movement raise transpiration; humidity lowers it. All act through stomatal opening or the water-vapour gradient.
  • A potometer measures water uptake: volume = πr²l, then divide by time.
  • Lignin stains identify xylem (and sclerenchyma); plan diagrams show tissue outlines only.

Mission 4

  • Phloem sieve tubes (living, no nucleus, sieve plates) and companion cells (many mitochondria) translocate sucrose from sources to sinks, up or down.
  • Active loading lowers water potential at the source; water follows; pressure drives mass flow to the sink.
  • Ringing, tracers and aphid stylets show sugars move in the phloem, need energy, and flow both ways.
