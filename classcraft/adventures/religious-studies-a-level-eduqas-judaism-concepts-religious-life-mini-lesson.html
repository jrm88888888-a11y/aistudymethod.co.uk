Mission 1

One God, no other

  • Judaism is absolute monotheism: one God, with no partners, rivals or parts.
  • Jews say it twice a day in the Shema: God is one (Hebrew echad).
  • God alone is worthy of worship: no intermediaries, no idols (Exodus 20:3–5).
  • Ethical monotheism: the one God is also the source of justice and goodness, and demands them from people. Belief and conduct cannot be separated.
  • The phrase became central in 19th-century Jewish thought (e.g. Hermann Cohen, 1842–1918), but the idea runs through the prophets.

Mission 1

Creator, sustainer, judge

  • Jewish teaching describes God in these roles:
  • Creator: "IN THE beginning God created the heaven and the earth." (Genesis 1:1, JPS 1917) Most Jewish thinkers: creation from nothing.
  • Sustainer / controller: creation is not a one-off. The morning prayer praises God who "in his goodness renews every day, continually, the work of creation" (paraphrase).
  • Eternal: "from everlasting to everlasting, Thou art God" (Psalm 90:2). God does not change (Malachi 3:6).
  • Judge: God rewards and punishes: "shall not the judge of all the earth do justly?" (Genesis 18:25, JPS 1917).
  • Judgement is just and merciful; Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur are the "days of judgement".
Mission 1

No body, no likeness

  • Jews believe God is incorporeal: no body, no shape, no physical comparison.
  • At Sinai (Horeb) "ye saw no manner of form" (Deuteronomy 4:15, JPS 1917): so no image of God may be made.
  • Bible phrases like God's "hand" or "voice" are metaphors: "the Torah speaks in human language" (Talmud, Berakhot 31b, paraphrase), a principle Maimonides uses.
  • God is neither male nor female. Hebrew grammar uses "he", but this says nothing about God's nature.

Mission 1

What God is like: attributes

  • Jewish teaching describes God as:
  • Omnipotent: all-powerful; nothing is beyond God.
  • Omniscient: all-knowing, including human thoughts and the future.
  • Omnibenevolent / merciful: "merciful and gracious, long-suffering, and abundant in goodness and truth" (Exodus 34:6, JPS 1917): the start of the Thirteen Attributes of Mercy, recited on fast days and festivals.
  • Holy: "Holy, holy, holy, is the LORD of hosts" (Isaiah 6:3), set apart and pure.
  • Just and perfect: God's acts are right and complete.
  • Your spec lists: omnipotent, omniscient, omnibenevolent, Holy, Just, Perfect, Merciful.
  • Many Jewish thinkers (e.g. Maimonides) say such words describe what God does, not what God is in essence.
Mission 1

Maimonides' Thirteen Principles

  • Moses Maimonides (Rambam, 1138–1204), Commentary on the Mishnah, Sanhedrin chapter 10 (c. 1168).
  • About God (1–5): (1) God exists and is the cause of all; (2) God is one; (3) God has no body; (4) God is eternal (first and last); (5) only God may be worshipped.
  • Revelation (6–9): prophecy; Moses is the greatest prophet; the Torah is from God; it will not be changed.
  • Reward and the end (10–13): God knows human deeds; rewards and punishes; the Messiah will come; the dead will be raised.
  • Sung in Yigdal and recited as Ani Ma'amin ("I believe") in many prayer books.
  • Critics: Hasdai Crescas (c. 1340–1410/11) and Joseph Albo (Sefer ha-Ikkarim, c. 1425) counted fewer principles (Albo: three). Some argue Judaism stresses practice more than creed.
Mission 1

Maimonides: saying what God is not

  • The Guide for the Perplexed (c. 1190), Book I, chapters 50–60.
  • Human words describe things with bodies and limits; God has neither. So positive attributes risk limiting God.
  • Negative attributes (via negativa): we know God best by saying what God is not. "God is living" means "God is not lifeless"; "God is one" means "God has no parts and no equal".
  • Each denial brings us closer to the truth, like narrowing down what an unseen object is.
  • Attributes of action: words like "merciful" (Exodus 34:6) describe what God does, not God's essence.
  • Spec issue: whether it is possible to know God.

Mission 1

Kavod and shekinah: God's presence

  • How can a God beyond the world be present in it?
  • Kavod ("glory", literally "weight"): God's visible presence. "The glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle" (Exodus 40:34, JPS 1917). Moses asks, "Show me, I pray Thee, Thy glory" (Exodus 33:18, JPS 1917), but cannot see God's face (33:20).
  • Shekinah (often spelled Shekhinah; from shakhan, "to dwell"): a rabbinic word for God's indwelling presence among people. Root in Exodus 25:8: "that I may dwell among them".
  • Rabbis teach that when ten pray together (Talmud, Berakhot 6a), or even two study Torah together (Mishnah Avot 3), the shekinah is among them (paraphrase). The shekinah went with Israel into exile.
  • In Kabbalah, the Shekhinah is the feminine aspect of God's presence.
  • Together: transcendent (beyond) yet immanent (present).
Mission 1Diversity

One God, many understandings

  • Orthodox: God as in Maimonides' principles: personal, revealing the Torah, rewarding and punishing.
  • Kabbalah and Hasidism: God is Ein Sof ("without end"), beyond all thought, revealed through ten sefirot; some Hasidic teachers say nothing exists apart from God.
  • Reform: ethical monotheism is the essence of Judaism; God known through conscience and history more than through law.
  • Reconstructionist: Mordecai Kaplan (1881–1983) described God as "the power that makes for salvation": a force in nature and people, not a supernatural person.
  • Humanistic Judaism: Jewish identity without belief in God.
  • Orthodox, Reform and Reconstructionist Jews all recite the Shema; they read "one" differently.
Mission 1Evaluate

Is it possible to know God?

  • Claim: If God is incorporeal and beyond human words, humans cannot know God.
  • For: Maimonides: positive statements mislead; we know only what God is not.
  • For: "man shall not see Me and live" (Exodus 33:20).
  • Against: God is known through revelation (Torah at Sinai) and through actions in history (the Exodus).
  • Against: God's presence (kavod, shekinah) is experienced in worship and community.
  • Against: Relationship, not description, may be the way of knowing (Buber).
  • Second issue: are God's characteristics meaningful today?
  • For: mercy and justice still shape Jewish prayer (the Thirteen Attributes of Mercy) and Jewish ethics and social action.
  • Against: after the Holocaust some Jewish thinkers question whether "omnipotent" can still be used of God; feminist critics question images such as "King" and "Father".
  • Judgement criteria: What counts as "knowing": describing, or relating? Is negative knowledge real knowledge? Are God's characteristics still meaningful today?

Mission 2

Maimonides writes a list

  • Moses ben Maimon, called Maimonides or Rambam (1138–1204): rabbi, legal scholar, philosopher, physician.
  • Born in Córdoba, Spain; his family fled persecution; he settled in Fustat (Old Cairo), Egypt.
  • In his Commentary on the Mishnah (Arabic, completed c. 1168) he reached Mishnah Sanhedrin 10:1: "All Israel have a share in the world to come" (paraphrase).
  • Who counts as "Israel" here? His answer: anyone who holds thirteen foundations (Hebrew yesodot) of faith.
  • They are known as the Thirteen Principles of Faith.
Mission 2

Thirteen principles, three groups

  • About God (1–5): God exists and causes all · is one · has no body · is eternal · alone is to be worshipped.
  • About revelation (6–9): prophecy · Moses the greatest prophet · the Torah from heaven · the Torah unchanging.
  • About God and humanity (10–13): God knows our deeds · reward and punishment · the Messiah · resurrection of the dead.
  • Principles 6–13: a later mission.
Mission 2

Principles 1 and 2: God exists; God is one

  • 1. Existence: God exists, is perfect, and is the cause of everything else that exists. Everything depends on God; God depends on nothing.
  • Root: "I am the LORD thy God" (Exodus 20:2), which Maimonides counts as the first commandment: to know that God exists.
  • 2. Unity: God is one, but not one as a pair has one member, nor one as a species, nor one as a body made of parts (paraphrase).
  • God's unity is unique: nothing else is one in this way.
  • Root: "HEAR, O ISRAEL: THE LORD OUR GOD, THE LORD IS ONE." (Deuteronomy 6:4, JPS 1917)
Mission 2Read the source

Principle 3: no body

> "for ye saw no manner of form on the day that the LORD spoke unto you in Horeb out of the midst of the fire" (Deuteronomy 4:15, JPS 1917)

  • 3. Incorporeality: God has no body, no shape, and nothing that happens to bodies (moving, resting, being in a place).
  • So what about God's "hand" or "eyes"? Maimonides uses the Talmud's rule: "The Torah speaks in the language of human beings" (paraphrase).
  • These words are figurative: "hand" means power; "eyes" means knowledge.

Q1 What did the Israelites not see at Horeb (Sinai)?

Show answer

Any form: they heard a voice but saw no shape.

Q2 Why does the Torah still use body words for God?

Show answer

To speak in a way ordinary people understand.

Mission 2

Principles 4 and 5: eternal; worship God alone

  • 4. Eternity: God is first: before everything, without beginning. Later versions add "and last".
  • Roots: "The eternal God is a dwelling-place" (Deuteronomy 33:27, JPS 1917); "I am the first, and I am the last" (Isaiah 44:6, JPS 1917).
  • 5. Worship God alone: prayer and praise go to God only.
  • Not to angels, stars or forces of nature, and not through them as go-betweens.
  • Root: "Thou shalt have no other gods before Me." (Exodus 20:3, JPS 1917)
  • Principle 5 is about practice: what a Jew may do in prayer, not only what a Jew thinks.
Mission 2

Speaking about God: negative attributes

  • In the Guide for the Perplexed (Arabic, c. 1190), Part I, chapters 50–60, Maimonides asks what words can truly say about God.
  • Positive attributes ("God is wise", "God is powerful") would divide God into parts, against principle 2.
  • So we can say only what God is not: "God exists" means God is not non-existent; "God is powerful" means God is not weak (paraphrase).
  • Each negation removes a wrong idea, and brings us a little closer.
  • Attributes of action: "merciful" and "gracious" (Exodus 34:6) describe what God does, not what God is.
  • The highest praise is silence: he reads Psalm 65 as "to Thee silence is praise" (Psalm 65:2 in Hebrew numbering, paraphrase).
  • He reads the kavod (glory) of God in some verses as a created light, not God's own being (Guide I.64, paraphrase).

Mission 2Evaluate

Can a God known only by negation be worshipped?

  • Claim: Maimonides' negative way leaves Jews with a God they cannot love or pray to.
  • For: Prayer addresses God as "Thou", "our Father, our King"; denial after denial seems to leave nothing to address.
  • For: The Torah itself speaks of a God who speaks, acts and cares (God as personal).
  • Against: Maimonides keeps attributes of action, so Jews can praise what God does in the world.
  • Against: Removing false pictures protects worship from idolatry (worshipping a false picture of God); awe before what cannot be grasped is itself worship.
  • Judgement criteria: Does the view fit the Tanakh's language? Does it fit how Jews actually pray? Does it protect God's unity? Can a believer live by it?
  • Eduqas issue: whether it is possible to know God.

Mission 3

Made in the image of God

  • "And God created man in His own image, in the image of God created He him; male and female created He them." (Genesis 1:27, JPS 1917)
  • Tzelem Elohim ("image of God"): not a body shape (God has none) but human reason, moral freedom, creativity and the ability to relate to God.
  • Every person has equal and infinite worth: humanity began with one person, so "whoever saves a single life saves a whole world" (Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5, paraphrase).
  • So how a person treats others is part of how they honour God.
Mission 3

Nefesh and pikuach nefesh

  • "the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul." (Genesis 2:7, JPS 1917)
  • Nefesh ("life", "living being"): life is a gift from God, held in trust.
  • Pikuach nefesh ("saving a life"): saving a life overrides almost every other mitzvah, including Shabbat and Yom Kippur.
  • Basis: "which if a man do, he shall live by them" (Leviticus 18:5, JPS 1917): the rabbis add, "and not die by them" (Talmud, Yoma 85b, paraphrase).
  • Three exceptions: a Jew must accept death rather than commit idolatry, murder or forbidden sexual relations (Talmud, Sanhedrin 74a).
  • Examples: driving a sick person to hospital on Shabbat; eating on Yom Kippur when ill.
Mission 3

Two inclinations

  • Every person has a yetzer hatov (good inclination) and a yetzer hara (evil, or selfish, inclination).
  • The Torah: "the imagination of man's heart is evil from his youth" (Genesis 8:21, JPS 1917). Hebrew yetzer = "imagination", "inclination".
  • The yetzer hara is not evil in itself: a midrash says without it no one would build a house, marry or have children (Genesis Rabbah 9:7).
  • The task is to direct it, not destroy it. "Love the LORD thy God with all thy heart": with both inclinations (Mishnah Berakhot 9:5).
  • Torah and the mitzvot train the yetzer hatov; it is said to mature at bar/bat mitzvah age.
  • No original sin: each person is born free to choose. "Therefore choose life" (Deuteronomy 30:19).

Mission 3

Purpose 1: obedience

  • "what doth the LORD thy God require of thee, but to fear the LORD thy God, to walk in all His ways, and to love Him, and to serve the LORD thy God with all thy heart and with all thy soul" (Deuteronomy 10:12, JPS 1917).
  • "fear God, and keep His commandments; for this is the whole man." (Ecclesiastes 12:13, JPS 1917)
  • Obedience means keeping the 613 mitzvot of the covenant: ritual and ethical.
  • The mitzvot make ordinary life holy: eating, working, resting, family, business.
  • Obedience is an act of love, not just duty: Jews bless God "who has made us holy with his commandments".
Mission 3

Purpose 2: repairing the world

  • Tikkun olam: "repair of the world".
  • In prayer: the Aleinu hopes "to perfect the world under the kingdom of the Almighty" (paraphrase).
  • In Mishnah law: some rulings are made "for the sake of tikkun ha-olam" (social order) (Mishnah Gittin 4).
  • In Kabbalah: Isaac Luria (1534–1572): at creation, divine light was scattered in sparks; each mitzvah helps gather them, repairing the world.
  • Today: especially in Reform and Liberal Judaism, social justice and environmental work.
  • Humans, made in God's image, are partners with God in completing creation.

The Shema

> "HEAR, O ISRAEL: THE LORD OUR GOD, THE LORD IS ONE. And thou shalt love the LORD thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy might." (Deuteronomy 6:4–5, JPS 1917)

> "and thou shalt teach them diligently unto thy children, and shalt talk of them when thou sittest in thy house, and when thou walkest by the way, and when thou liest down, and when thou risest up." (Deuteronomy 6:7, JPS 1917)

  • Three paragraphs: (1) Deuteronomy 6:4–9: one God, love, teaching, tefillin, mezuzah. (2) Deuteronomy 11:13–21: obedience brings rain and blessing; disobedience brings loss. (3) Numbers 15:37–41: tzitzit "that ye may look upon it, and remember all the commandments" (15:39); the Exodus recalled.

Mission 3

Why the Shema matters

  • Aid to faith and remembering: said twice daily, the first prayer taught to children, the last words before death (Rabbi Akiva died saying "echad", Berakhot 61b).
  • Objects that remind: mezuzah on the door, tefillin on arm and head, tzitzit on garments.
  • Covenant: reciting it is "accepting the yoke of the kingdom of heaven", then the "yoke of the commandments" (Mishnah Berakhot 2:2, paraphrase).
  • Purpose of life: to love God with heart, soul and might, and to pass this on.
  • Pikuach nefesh and the Shema together: love of God expressed in protecting life.

Mission 3Evaluate

Does the Shema contain the most important beliefs?

  • Claim: The Shema contains the most important beliefs in Judaism.
  • For: One God; love of God; Torah taught and remembered; reward and punishment; the Exodus.
  • For: Twice-daily use makes it the centre of Jewish faith and identity.
  • Against: No mention of the Messiah, life after death, or the image of God; Maimonides needed 13 principles.
  • Against: Practice matters more than a creed in Judaism: Shabbat, kashrut and ethics are only implied.
  • Precise enough? "These words" are not defined; the rabbis needed oral Torah to say what tefillin and mezuzah are.
  • Judgement criteria: Does "most important" mean most central, or most complete? Is a short statement meant to be precise?

Mission 4

Mashiach: the anointed one

  • Messiah comes from Hebrew mashiach, "anointed": a person marked with oil for a task God gives.
  • In the Tanakh, kings and priests were anointed: "Samuel took the horn of oil, and anointed him" (1 Samuel 16:13, JPS 1917), of David.
  • Even the Persian king Cyrus is called "His anointed" (Isaiah 45:1, JPS 1917) for freeing the exiles.
  • Later hope focused on one future king from David's line: the King Messiah (Melekh ha-Mashiach).
  • He is a human being, not divine; Jews pray for him, they do not pray to him.
  • Your spec writes G-d, a form many Jews use out of respect.

Swords into plowshares

> "they shall beat their swords into plowshares, and their spears into pruninghooks; nation shall not lift up sword against nation, neither shall they learn war any more." (Micah 4:3, JPS 1917; almost the same in Isaiah 2:4)

> "But they shall sit every man under his vine and under his fig-tree; and none shall make them afraid" (Micah 4:4, JPS 1917)

  • Isaiah 2:2–4 and Micah 4:1–3 share one vision: "in the end of days" all nations stream to "the mountain of the LORD'S house" in Jerusalem to learn God's ways.
  • "For out of Zion shall go forth the law" (Isaiah 2:3, JPS 1917).

Mission 4

The Messianic Age in the prophets

  • A king of David's line: "a shoot out of the stock of Jesse" (Isaiah 11:1, JPS 1917), filled with God's spirit.
  • Judge: God "shall judge between the nations" (Isaiah 2:4); the king judges the poor "with righteousness" (Isaiah 11:4).
  • Ingathering: God "will assemble the dispersed of Israel" (Isaiah 11:12, JPS 1917).
  • Temple rebuilt; Jerusalem a centre of worship for all nations.
  • Knowledge of God: "the earth shall be full of the knowledge of the LORD, as the waters cover the sea" (Isaiah 11:9, JPS 1917).
  • Herald: Elijah, before "the great and terrible day of the LORD" (Malachi 4:5, JPS 1917; 3:23 in Hebrew).
  • Your spec's summary: the Anointed One and Judge who brings peace; a new world order.

Mission 4

Maimonides on the Messiah

  • Mishneh Torah, Laws of Kings, chapters 11–12 (paraphrase):
  • Who: a king from the house of David, who studies Torah, keeps the mitzvot and leads Israel to keep them.
  • Test: if he rebuilds the Temple and gathers the exiles, he is certainly the Messiah. If he fails or is killed, he was not.
  • He need not work miracles.
  • Nature unchanged: "the world follows its normal course"; the wolf lying with the lamb (Isaiah 11:6) is a metaphor for peace among nations.
  • Purpose: not power or pleasure, but freedom to study Torah and know God.
  • Do not calculate the date. (Principle 12: wait for him.)

Mission 4

Bringing the Messiah: a purpose of life

  • AQA's three purposes: obedience to God's commandments; bringing the Messiah; repairing the world (tikkun olam).
  • The Talmud links them: asked when he will come, the Messiah answers "To-day, if ye would but hearken to His voice!" (Psalm 95:7, JPS 1917, cited in Sanhedrin 98a).
  • So keeping mitzvot is active waiting: each good deed brings redemption nearer.
  • Tikkun olam: working for justice and peace prepares the world for the Messianic Age.
  • Relative importance: many Orthodox Jews put obedience first (the Messiah comes through Torah); many Progressive Jews put tikkun olam first (people build the Messianic Age).
Mission 4Diversity

Orthodox and Progressive today

  • Orthodox: a personal Messiah of David's line; the Amidah prays daily for "the sprout of David"; principle 12 in Ani Ma'amin.
  • Chabad Hasidim: campaign to hasten the Messiah through mitzvot.
  • Religious Zionists (Rav Kook, 1865–1935): the return to the Land is "the beginning of redemption".
  • Some Haredi groups (e.g. Satmar): a human-made state forces the end; only the Messiah should restore Israel.
  • Reform: the Pittsburgh Platform (1885) spoke of "the establishment of the kingdom of truth, justice, and peace among all men": a Messianic Age, not a person. Reform prayer books replaced "redeemer" with "redemption".
  • Masorti / Conservative: the Messianic hope affirmed; whether a person or a symbol is left open.
Mission 4Evaluate

Is the Messiah a serious belief for all Jews today?

  • Claim: The Messiah is a serious religious belief for all Jews today.
  • For: It is in the daily Amidah, in Ani Ma'amin, and at every seder (Elijah's cup).
  • For: Reform kept the hope while changing its form: a Messianic Age of justice.
  • Against: Reform and Liberal Jews mostly reject a personal Messiah.
  • Against: Many secular Jews see the Messiah as a symbol, not a belief.
  • Judgement criteria: What counts as "serious": a literal belief, or a hope that shapes action? Does "all Jews" include secular Jews?

Mission 5

She'ol: the place of the dead

  • In most of the Tenakh, the dead go down to She'ol: a dark, silent place under the earth.
  • Everyone goes there, righteous and wicked alike: Jacob says he will "go down to the grave to my son mourning" (Genesis 37:35, JPS 1917; Hebrew She'ol).
  • It is a place of shades, cut off from praise: "For in death there is no remembrance of Thee; in the nether-world who will give Thee thanks?" (Psalm 6:5, JPS 1917)
  • No reward, no punishment: "there is no work, nor device, nor knowledge, nor wisdom, in the grave" (Ecclesiastes 9:10, JPS 1917).
  • So the early focus is this life: blessing, long life, children, land.
Mission 5

Resurrection of the dead

> "And many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to reproaches and everlasting abhorrence." (Daniel 12:2, JPS 1917)

  • Also: "Thy dead shall live, my dead bodies shall arise" (Isaiah 26:19, JPS 1917); Ezekiel's dry bones (Ezekiel 37), first about the nation's revival.
  • The Pharisees taught resurrection; the Sadducees denied it.
  • The Mishnah: whoever says resurrection is not taught in the Torah has no share in the world to come (Sanhedrin 10:1, paraphrase).
  • Daily Amidah: God "revives the dead" (mechayeh ha-metim).
  • Thirteen Principles: principle 13, resurrection of the flesh.
  • Modern attitudes: Orthodox Jews affirm a literal, bodily resurrection (one reason for burial, not cremation); many Progressive Jews do not.
Mission 5

Judgement, olam ha-ba, Gan Eden, Gehinnom

  • Judgement: each soul is judged after death; the final judgement follows the resurrection.
  • Olam ha-ba: "the world to come": the realm of souls after death, or the renewed world after resurrection (sources use both).
  • "This world is like a vestibule before the world to come" (Pirkei Avot 4:16, paraphrase): life now prepares for it.
  • Gan Eden (paradise): the reward of the righteous.
  • Gehinnom (Gehenna): named after the valley of Hinnom, outside Jerusalem (Jeremiah 7:31); a place of purification, mostly for at most twelve months (Mishnah Eduyot 2:10).
  • So mourners say Kaddish for eleven months, not twelve.
  • "The righteous of all nations have a share in the world to come" (Tosefta Sanhedrin 13:2, paraphrase).

Mission 5

Immortality of the soul and the Pittsburgh Platform

  • Later Jewish thought stressed the immortal soul: medieval philosophers (Saadia, Maimonides) and Moses Mendelssohn, whose Phaedon (1767) argued for immortality as endless moral growth.
  • Pittsburgh Platform (American Reform, 1885), paragraph 7:
  • "We reassert the doctrine of Judaism that the soul is immortal" (Pittsburgh Platform, 1885).
  • It rejects "the beliefs both in bodily resurrection and in Gehenna and Eden (Hell and Paradise) as abodes for everlasting punishment and reward" (Pittsburgh Platform, 1885).
  • Reform prayer books changed "who revives the dead" to "who gives life to all" (mechayeh ha-kol); recent ones offer both.
  • Today: Orthodox Jews hold resurrection and an immortal soul; many Reform and Liberal Jews hold immortality only, or leave the question open.
Mission 5Evaluate

Are beliefs about the afterlife still relevant for Jews today?

  • Claim: Beliefs about judgement and the afterlife remain relevant for Jews today.
  • For: They are in the daily liturgy (Amidah, morning blessings) and in mourning (Kaddish, burial).
  • For: They answer the problem of God's justice when the righteous suffer.
  • Against: The Tenakh is mostly silent; Judaism stresses this world and the mitzvot.
  • Against: Pittsburgh and many Progressive Jews reject bodily resurrection and literal Gehenna and Eden.
  • Judgement criteria: Relevant to belief or to practice? To which Jews? Does "relevant" require being literal?
  • Eduqas issue: whether Jewish beliefs about judgement and the afterlife are relevant for Jews today.

Mission 6

Mission 6: If the Torah holds 613 commandments, why did only ten go on the stone tablets?

Make a guess: If the Torah holds 613 commandments, why did only ten go on the stone tablets?

Mission 6

A mitzvah is a commandment

  • Mitzvah (plural mitzvot) means commandment: something God commands, not just a kind act.
  • The Talmud counts 613 mitzvot in the Torah: 248 positive ("do") and 365 negative ("do not") (Makkot 23b).
  • Many apply only in the Land of Israel, in Temple times or to priests; fewer than 300 can be kept by most Jews today.
  • A Jew becomes bar or bat mitzvah, "son" or "daughter of the commandment": now responsible for keeping them.
  • Keeping mitzvot is how Jews live out the covenant made at Sinai.
  • Your spec writes G-d, a form many Jews use out of respect.
Mission 6

Towards God, towards people

  • The rabbis sort mitzvot by direction:
  • Bein adam la-Makom: between a person and God (e.g. Shabbat, prayer, kashrut).
  • Bein adam la-chavero: between a person and another person (e.g. honesty, charity, not gossiping).
  • Both kinds are commands of God: wronging a person is also a sin against God.
  • Mishnah Yoma 8:9 (paraphrase): Yom Kippur atones for sins against God; for sins against another person, you must first put things right with that person.
Mission 6Read the source

"Even the ten words"

> "I am the LORD thy God, who brought thee out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage." (Exodus 20:2, JPS 1917)

> "And He declared unto you His covenant, which He commanded you to perform, even the ten words; and He wrote them upon two tables of stone." (Deuteronomy 4:13, JPS 1917)

  • The Torah calls them the ten words (Hebrew aseret ha-devarim); the rabbis call them Aseret ha-Dibrot, the Ten Sayings.

Mission 6

Two tablets

  • The usual Jewish count:
  • Tablet 1: (1) I am the LORD your God · (2) no other gods, no idols · (3) do not take God's name in vain · (4) keep Shabbat · (5) honour father and mother.
  • Tablet 2: (6) do not murder · (7) do not commit adultery · (8) do not steal (the Talmud reads this as kidnapping) · (9) no false witness · (10) do not covet.
  • Common reading: tablet 1 is between people and God, tablet 2 between people.
  • Why are parents on tablet 1? The Talmud calls God, father and mother partners in making a person (Kiddushin 30b).
  • Christians number them differently (most start with "no other gods"): do not mix the two systems.

Mission 6

Ten headings for 613

  • Rabbinic tradition reads the Ten Sayings as ten categories that hold all 613 mitzvot.
  • Saadia Gaon (882–942) wrote liturgical poems (Azharot) arranging all 613 under the ten.
  • Rashi (1040–1105), on Exodus 24:12, repeats it (paraphrase): all 613 are contained in the Ten; Saadia showed which belong to each.
  • Examples: "keep Shabbat" heads all the festival laws; "do not steal" heads honest weights and fair trade.
  • Earlier, Philo of Alexandria (c. 20 BCE–c. 50 CE) also called the ten "headings" of the special laws.
  • So the Ten are a summary and a doorway, not a replacement for the 613.
Mission 6Diversity

Orthodox, Reform, Hasidic

  • Orthodox: all 613 come from God (Torah min ha-shamayim, "Torah from heaven"); every mitzvah that can be kept today is binding; the Oral Torah fixes how.
  • Reform: the Torah was written by inspired humans; the ethical mitzvot remain central; ritual mitzvot are chosen by informed choice. Pittsburgh Platform (1885): "we accept as binding only its moral laws". Later Reform statements (Pittsburgh 1999) encourage more ritual practice.
  • Hasidic: fully Orthodox in practice, often stricter (hiddur mitzvah, beautifying the mitzvah); stress joy, kavanah (intention) and devekut (clinging to God) while doing each mitzvah.
  • Hasidic teaching: each mitzvah raises hidden holy sparks; Chabad-Lubavitch "mitzvah campaigns" (from 1967) invite other Jews to do single mitzvot, e.g. tefillin, Shabbat candles.
Mission 6

Literal or not? Today?

  • Interpretation: no Jewish group reads every mitzvah literally. "Eye for eye" (Exodus 21:24) is read in the Oral Torah as money compensation (Bava Kamma 84a).
  • Application today: Orthodox rabbis apply old rules to new cases (e.g. switching on electricity on Shabbat counts as forbidden work). Reform Jews decide which practices "elevate and sanctify" life (Pittsburgh 1885).
  • Importance today: for Orthodox and Hasidic Jews, mitzvot are the core of a Jewish life; for many Reform Jews, ethical mitzvot (justice, tzedakah) matter most; for Hasidim, each mitzvah is a meeting with God.
  • Spirituality? For: with kavanah, a mitzvah becomes an act of closeness to God (Hasidic devekut). Against: mitzvot can become habit without feeling; some Reform Jews seek spirituality beyond ritual law.
  • Spec issue: "whether mitzvot contribute effectively to spirituality in Judaism".

Mission 6Evaluate

Do mitzvot divide Jews?

  • Claim: The concept of mitzvot divides Judaism.
  • For: Different views on authority (God-given or human-inspired) lead to different practice: some Jews cannot eat in other Jews' homes or pray in each other's synagogues.
  • For: Disputes over Shabbat, kashrut and conversion standards split communities and institutions.
  • Against: All groups share the same Torah, the same 613 count and the core ethical mitzvot.
  • Against: Mitzvot shape shared moments: Pesach, Yom Kippur and Shabbat are kept in some form by most Jews.
  • Judgement criteria: Is the division about mitzvot or about authority? Does shared practice outweigh different standards? Is diversity a sign of division or of life?
  • Spec issue: "the extent to which the concept of mitzvot is divisive within Judaism".
Mission 6Evaluate

Are ten enough?

  • Claim: The Ten Sayings adequately summarise Jewish belief and practice.
  • For: They begin with God's identity and the Exodus, cover worship, Shabbat, family and justice; Saadia and Rashi make them headings for all 613.
  • For: Their ethical sayings (murder, theft, false witness) are a clear guide that many traditions share.
  • Against: They omit major beliefs and practices: the Messiah, life after death, prayer, kashrut, tzedakah.
  • Against: The rabbis stopped reciting them daily (Berakhot 12a) so that no one would claim only these came from Sinai.
  • Judgement criteria: Summary of belief or of ethics? Headings or complete rules? Do they guide new issues (e.g. medical ethics)?
  • Spec issues: whether the Ten Sayings are "an effective guide for ethical living"; how far they "adequately summarise religious belief".

Mission 7

Tefillah: judging yourself

  • Tefillah = prayer. The verb hitpallel ("to pray") is reflexive: often explained as "to judge oneself".
  • So prayer is spiritual self-reflection before God, not only asking for things.
  • The rabbis call it "service of the heart": Deuteronomy 11:13 commands serving God "with all your heart"; the Talmud asks what that service is and answers: prayer (Ta'anit 2a).
  • Kavanah: inner intention and focus; prayer without it is "a body without a soul" (a later saying, often quoted).
  • Your spec writes G-d, a form many Jews use out of respect.
Mission 7

Why pray?

  • Praise, requests and thanks: the three movements of Jewish prayer.
  • Instead of sacrifices: since the Temple fell (70 CE), prayer takes their place: "so will we render for bullocks the offering of our lips" (Hosea 14:2 [Heb. 14:3], JPS 1917).
  • Following the patriarchs: Abraham prayed in the morning, Isaac in the afternoon, Jacob at night (Berakhot 26b).
  • Relationship: prayer speaks to a personal God who listens.
  • Community: Jews mostly pray in the plural ("our God", "heal us"), for the whole people.
Mission 7

The Amidah: three parts

  • Amidah = "standing" prayer; also Shemoneh Esrei ("eighteen") or simply ha-Tefillah, "the Prayer".
  • Originally 18 blessings; a 19th was added at Yavneh (c. 1st century CE).
  • 3 praise: God of the patriarchs; God's power (giving rain, reviving the dead); God's holiness.
  • 13 requests: knowledge, repentance, forgiveness, redemption, healing, good harvest, gathering of exiles, justice, against slanderers, for the righteous, rebuilding Jerusalem, the Messiah, hearing prayer.
  • 3 thanks: for worship, for daily miracles, for peace.
  • On Shabbat and festivals the 13 requests are replaced by one blessing for the day (7 in all): no weekday requests on a day of rest.
Mission 7Read the source

How the Amidah begins

> "O Lord, open Thou my lips; and my mouth shall declare Thy praise." (Psalm 51:15 [Heb. 51:17], JPS 1917)

> "Now Hannah, she spoke in her heart; only her lips moved, but her voice could not be heard …" (1 Samuel 1:13, JPS 1917)

  • The Amidah opens with Psalm 51's verse; it is said silently, lips moving, as Hannah prayed (Berakhot 31a).
  • Jews stand with feet together, face Jerusalem, take three steps forward at the start and three back at the end, and bow at set points.
  • The Amidah is said three times a day: morning, afternoon and evening.

Mission 7

Three times a day, ten together

  • The Amidah is the centre of all three daily services: Shacharit (morning), Minchah (afternoon), Ma'ariv (evening).
  • With a minyan, the leader repeats it aloud after the silent prayer, adding the Kedushah ("Holy, holy, holy", Isaiah 6:3).
  • Minyan: at least ten adult Jews. The rabbis derive ten from the word "congregation" used for the ten spies (Numbers 14:27) (Megillah 23b).
  • Needed for: the Amidah repetition, Kaddish, Barechu, Kedushah and the public Torah reading.
  • Orthodox: ten men over 13. Masorti, Reform, Liberal: women count equally.
  • A minyan expresses that Jews pray as a community, not only as individuals.
Mission 7Diversity

How Jews pray

  • Orthodox: Hebrew siddur; set times; men and women seated apart (mechitzah); men lead.
  • Masorti: traditional Hebrew liturgy; mostly egalitarian.
  • Reform / Liberal: more prayer in the vernacular; music and instruments; mixed seating; some texts changed (e.g. the matriarchs added to the first blessing; "who gives life to all" offered beside "who revives the dead").
  • Hasidic: joyful, intense prayer; wordless melodies (niggunim); swaying; Breslov Hasidim also practise hitbodedut, private spontaneous prayer.
  • Women's prayer: Orthodox women's groups; progressive women rabbis and cantors.
Mission 7

Fixed words, living intention

  • Keva = fixed (set words and times); kavanah = intention.
  • Rabbi Eliezer (Mishnah Berakhot 4:4, paraphrase): if prayer is a fixed routine, it is not true supplication.
  • Fixed words give structure: they teach what to ask for and carry a person when their own words fail.
  • Risk: routine can become empty ritual.
  • Responses: personal additions in the Amidah (e.g. in "hear our prayer"); Hasidic stress on kavanah; Reform creative liturgy.
  • Spec issue: "whether prayer has become a spiritually ineffective ritual".

Mission 7

Tzedakah is justice

  • Tzedakah comes from tzedek, "justice" or "righteousness": giving is a duty, not optional charity.
  • "Thou shalt not harden thy heart, nor shut thy hand from thy needy brother" (Deuteronomy 15:7, JPS 1917).
  • The rich hold wealth in trust from God; the poor have a right to help.
  • Even a person who lives on tzedakah must give some (Gittin 7b).
  • Custom: give a tenth (ma'aser) of income; the rabbis set a maximum of a fifth (Ketubot 50a), so the giver is not made poor.
  • Homes and schools keep a pushke (collection box); many give before Shabbat.
  • "Righteousness delivereth from death" (Proverbs 10:2, JPS 1917): the Hebrew word is tzedakah.
Mission 7

Maimonides' ladder

  • Maimonides (1138–1204), Mishneh Torah, Laws of Gifts to the Poor 10:7–14: eight levels, highest first.
  • 1. Helping someone become self-sufficient: a gift, a loan, a partnership or a job.
  • 2. Giver and receiver do not know each other.
  • 3. Giver knows; receiver does not.
  • 4. Receiver knows; giver does not.
  • 5. Giving before being asked.
  • 6. Giving after being asked.
  • 7. Giving less than is fitting, but gladly.
  • 8. Giving grudgingly.
  • Two values rise up the ladder: the receiver's dignity and independence.
  • Spec wording: "the 'ladder of tzedekah'".

Mission 7Evaluate

Is the Amidah Judaism in miniature?

  • Claim: The Amidah encapsulates the most important beliefs, values and teachings of Judaism.
  • For: It covers the covenant (God of the patriarchs), God's power and holiness, repentance and forgiveness, justice, Jerusalem, the Messiah, resurrection and peace.
  • For: Said three times a day, it shapes a Jew's whole outlook; its structure (praise, request, thanks) teaches how to relate to God.
  • Against: It leaves out central elements: God's oneness is in the Shema (the declaration "Hear, O Israel … the LORD is one", Deuteronomy 6:4, JPS 1917, said morning and evening); the mitzvot towards others, such as tzedakah, are not its theme.
  • Against: Reform changes (resurrection, Temple worship, the Messiah) show Jews disagree about some of its beliefs.
  • Judgement criteria: Summary of beliefs, or of a relationship? Which Judaism (Orthodox or progressive)? Does a prayer need to be complete to be central?
  • Spec issue: "the extent to which the Amidah is an encapsulation of the most important beliefs, values and teachings of Judaism".

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Judaism is absolute monotheism: one, eternal, incorporeal God, creator, sustainer and judge, who alone is worshipped and who demands justice (ethical monotheism).
  • Maimonides' Thirteen Principles summarise belief; Jewish traditions interpret the one God differently.
  • Maimonides teaches that we know God best by saying what God is not.
  • God's presence is kavod (glory) and shekinah (indwelling).

Mission 2

  • Maimonides (1138–1204) set out Thirteen Principles in his Commentary on the Mishnah; principles 1–5 are about God: exists, one, without body, eternal, alone to be worshipped.
  • Body words for God are figurative: "the Torah speaks in the language of human beings".
  • In the Guide, positive words about God are read as negations or as attributes of action.

Mission 3

  • Humans are made in God's image, with infinite worth, free will and two inclinations.
  • Life (nefesh) is God's gift, so saving it (pikuach nefesh) overrides almost every law.
  • Purposes of life include obedience to the mitzvot and tikkun olam; Jews weigh them differently.
  • The Shema (Deuteronomy 6:4–9; 11:13–21; Numbers 15:37–41) states one God, love, remembering and the covenant.

Mission 4

  • Messiah (mashiach, "anointed") is a human king of David's line; the prophets picture an age of peace, judgement, ingathering and knowledge of God (Isaiah 2, Micah 4, Isaiah 11).
  • Maimonides: the Messiah is known by rebuilding the Temple and gathering the exiles; nature is unchanged.
  • Orthodox Jews await a personal Messiah; Reform Jews look to a Messianic Age that people help to build.

Mission 5

  • The Tenakh's She'ol became, in later Judaism, judgement, olam ha-ba, Gan Eden, Gehinnom and the resurrection of the dead (Daniel 12:2).
  • Orthodox Jews affirm bodily resurrection (principle 13); the Pittsburgh Platform (1885) kept only the immortal soul.

Mission 6

  • A mitzvah is a commandment from God; the Talmud counts 613, between people and God and between people.
  • The Ten Sayings (Aseret ha-Dibrot) begin with God's self-introduction; rabbinic tradition (Saadia, Rashi) reads them as ten categories of the 613.
  • Orthodox Jews hold all 613 binding; Reform Jews treat ethical mitzvot as binding and ritual as informed choice; Hasidic Jews keep Orthodox practice with stress on joy and intention.

Mission 7

  • Tefillah is self-reflection before God, the "service of the heart"; the Amidah (19 blessings: praise, requests, thanks) is said silently, standing, three times a day.
  • A minyan of ten is needed for public prayers such as Kedushah and Kaddish; Orthodox, Reform and Hasidic Jews pray in different ways.
  • Tzedakah is justice, a duty; Maimonides ranks gifts by dignity and independence, with self-sufficiency at the top.
