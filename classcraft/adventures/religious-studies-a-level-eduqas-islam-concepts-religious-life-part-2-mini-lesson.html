Mission 1

Akhirah: one life, then the return

  • Akhirah (the hereafter, "the last"): one of the six articles of faith for Sunnis; for Twelver Shi'a, ma'ad (the return) is a root of faith.
  • Muslims believe each person lives one earthly life; there is no rebirth.
  • "Every soul shall have a taste of death: And only on the Day of Judgment shall you be paid your full recompense." (Qur'an 3:185, Yusuf Ali)
  • The stages: death → Barzakh (waiting) → the trumpet and resurrection → judgement → Jannah (paradise) or Jahannam (hell).
  • Eduqas: the Day of Final Judgement (Akhirah) shows the meaning of life: submission and reward.
Mission 1

Death and Barzakh

  • At death the person asks to return, but: "Before them is a Partition till the Day they are raised up." (Qur'an 23:100, Yusuf Ali)
  • Barzakh (barrier) is the state between death and resurrection.
  • Hadith describe two angels, Munkar and Nakir, questioning the dead: Who is your Lord? What is your religion? Who is your prophet? (Tirmidhi; paraphrase)
  • The grave becomes a foretaste: "a garden of paradise or a pit of hell" (Tirmidhi; paraphrase).
  • Martyrs are "living" with their Lord (3:169).
  • Views differ on how conscious the dead are; most Sunni and Shi'a scholars hold the dead are aware.

The trumpet and the resurrection

> "The Trumpet will (just) be sounded, when all that are in the heavens and on earth will swoon … Then will a second one be sounded, when, behold, they will be standing and looking on!" (Qur'an 39:68, Yusuf Ali)

> "He says, 'Who can give life to (dry) bones and decomposed ones (at that)?' Say, 'He will give them life Who created them for the first time!'" (Qur'an 36:78–79, Yusuf Ali)

  • In tradition the angel Israfil blows the trumpet (named in hadith, not in the Qur'an).

Q1 How many trumpet blasts does 39:68 describe, and what follows each?

Show answer

Two: all swoon; then all stand.

Q2 What argument does 36:79 give for resurrection?

Show answer

The God who created once can create again.

  • Eduqas: "events from the last trumpet onward".
Mission 1

The Day of Judgement

  • God is "Master of the Day of Judgment" (Qur'an 1:4, Yusuf Ali).
  • Each person receives their record of deeds: in the right hand (saved) or the left (lost) (69:19–25).
  • Deeds are weighed on the balance (mizan): "if there be (no more than) the weight of a mustard seed, We will bring it (to account)" (Qur'an 21:47, Yusuf Ali).
  • Intercession (shafa'a), above all by the Prophet, only with God's permission (2:255).
  • Hadith describe a bridge (sirat) over hell to paradise (Bukhari; Muslim).
  • Mercy: "My mercy prevails over My wrath" (hadith qudsi, Bukhari; Muslim; paraphrase).

Paradise and hell

> "(Here is) a Parable of the Garden which the righteous are promised: in it are rivers of water incorruptible; rivers of milk of which the taste never changes; rivers of wine, a joy to those who drink; and rivers of honey pure and clear." (Qur'an 47:15, Yusuf Ali)

> "Every time a Group is cast therein, its Keepers will ask, 'Did no Warner come to you?' They will say: 'Yes indeed; a Warner did come to us, but we rejected him'" (Qur'an 67:8–9, Yusuf Ali)

  • 47:15 contrasts the Garden with those who drink "boiling water". 67:7–10 describe hell drawing in breath and "bursting with fury"; the damned admit, "Had we but listened or used our intelligence" (67:10).

Q1 What word does 47:15 use to introduce the Garden?

Show answer

A "parable" or "similitude", Arabic * mathal *.

Q2 According to 67:8–10, why are people in hell?

Show answer

They were warned and rejected the warning.

  • Eduqas names Sura 47:15 and Sura 67:7–10.
Mission 1

Jannah and Jahannam

  • Jannah (the Garden): gardens, rivers, peace, companionship; ranks of paradise; above all, nearness to God.
  • Sunni theologians (Ash'ari, Maturidi, Hanbali) hold the righteous will see God: faces "Looking towards their Lord" (Qur'an 75:23, Yusuf Ali). Mu'tazila and Twelver Shi'a read this as spiritual vision, not sight.
  • Jahannam (hell): fire, boiling water, regret; also separation from God.
  • Most scholars teach that those who rejected faith remain in hell; believers punished for sins may be brought out by mercy and intercession (Bukhari; Muslim).
  • A minority discussed whether hell's punishment ends (e.g. Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya, 1292–1350).
Mission 1

Why the Akhirah matters now

  • Purpose: life is a test; every act counts (99:7–8).
  • Accountability: no one escapes justice; wrongs not righted on earth will be righted.
  • Hope and fear together (khawf and raja'): fear keeps a believer from sin; hope stops despair (39:53).
  • Comfort in grief: death is a return to God (2:156).
  • Submission and reward: Eduqas frames Akhirah as underlining submission (Islam) and its reward.
Mission 1Evaluate

Does the Day of Judgement inspire fear or faith?

  • Claim: Belief in the Day of Judgement inspires fear more than faith.
  • Fear: vivid descriptions of hell (67:7–10; 4:56) are meant to warn.
  • Fear: a believer can never be certain of their own outcome.
  • Faith: the warnings serve mercy: a warning given is a chance to change (67:8).
  • Faith: "Despair not of the Mercy of Allah" (Qur'an 39:53, Yusuf Ali); good deeds are multiplied.
  • Criteria: Is fear itself a form of faith (taqwa, God-consciousness)? Does the effect differ between believers? Do paradise texts outnumber hell texts? What does God's mercy mean for judgement?
  • Spec issue: whether the Day of Final Judgement inspires fear or faith.

Mission 2: Muslims pray at set times, in set words, facing one place. Is there room left for the heart?

Make a guess: Muslims pray at set times, in set words, facing one place. Is there room left for the heart?

Mission 2

Salah within ibadah

  • Ibadah (worship, service): any lawful act done for God's sake. "Truly, my prayer and my service of sacrifice, my life and my death, are (all) for Allah" (Qur'an 6:162, Yusuf Ali).
  • Salah: the fixed ritual prayer, five times a day; the second pillar.
  • Other prayer surrounds it: nafila (extra), tahajjud (night prayer), du'a (personal calling on God), tasbih (glorifying), wird (Sufi litany), dhikr (remembrance).
  • Eduqas spellings: salah, niyat, Jummah.
Mission 2

Preparing to pray

  • Wudu (washing): "wash your faces, and your hands (and arms) to the elbows; Rub your heads (with water); and (wash) your feet to the ankles" (Qur'an 5:6, Yusuf Ali).
  • Ghusl (full washing) after major impurity; tayammum (dry earth) when there is no water (5:6).
  • Niyyah (intention) in the heart: this prayer, for God.
  • Face the qiblah, the Ka'bah in Makkah (2:144); a clean place; modest covering.
  • The adhan (call) and iqamah (call to stand) begin congregational prayer.
  • Purity of body points to purity of heart.
Mission 2Read the source

Fixed times

> "set up Regular Prayers: For such prayers are enjoined on believers at stated times." (Qur'an 4:103, Yusuf Ali)

> "Establish regular prayers - at the sun's decline till the darkness of the night, and the morning prayer and reading" (Qur'an 17:78, Yusuf Ali)

  • The Qur'an gives times in outline (also 11:114; 20:130). The number five, and their forms, come from the Sunnah: hadith say they were fixed on the Night Journey (Mi'raj).
  • Fajr (dawn, 2 rak'ahs) · Zuhr (after midday, 4) · Asr (afternoon, 4) · Maghrib (after sunset, 3) · Isha (night, 4).

Q1 What does 4:103 say about the timing of prayer?

Show answer

It is enjoined at stated times.

Q2 Where do the exact number and form of prayers come from?

Show answer

The Prophet's example, the Sunnah.

Mission 2

One rak'ah

  • Rak'ah: one cycle of set words and positions.
  • Takbir ("Allahu akbar": God is greatest) opens the prayer.
  • Qiyam (standing): al-Fatiha in Arabic; in the first two rak'ahs, another passage of the Qur'an follows.
  • Ruku' (bowing), then standing again.
  • Sujud (prostration) twice: "bow down in adoration, and bring thyself the closer (to Allah)!" (Qur'an 96:19, Yusuf Ali). A hadith says a servant is closest to God in prostration (Muslim; paraphrase).
  • Tashahhud (sitting, with the shahadah) and taslim ("peace be on you") to right and left end the prayer.

Mission 2Diversity

Sunni and Shi'a salah

  • Same pillar, same five prayers, same core words and movements.
  • Times: many Shi'a combine Zuhr with Asr and Maghrib with Isha, praying at three times a day; Sunnis combine mainly when travelling.
  • Hands: most Sunnis fold them (Hanafi, Shafi'i, Hanbali); Shi'a, and many Maliki Sunnis, pray with arms at their sides.
  • Prostration: Shi'a place the forehead on earth or something natural, often a small clay turbah (frequently from Karbala). It is prostrated on, not to.
  • Wudu: Shi'a wipe the feet; Sunnis wash them. Both cite 5:6.
  • Adhan: Shi'a add "Come to the best of deeds" and often a witness to Ali.
Mission 2

Jumu'ah, the Friday prayer

> "O ye who believe! When the call is proclaimed to prayer on Friday (the Day of Assembly), hasten earnestly to the Remembrance of Allah, and leave off business (and traffic)" (Qur'an 62:9, Yusuf Ali)

  • Replaces Zuhr on Friday: a khutbah (sermon, in two parts) and two rak'ahs in congregation.
  • After it: "disperse through the land, and seek of the Bounty of Allah" (62:10): Friday is not a day of rest.
  • Sunni law: a duty for adult men who are resident and well; women may attend.
  • Twelver Shi'a: in the Imam's absence (occultation), many jurists hold Friday prayer may be prayed instead of Zuhr rather than required; Iran revived it publicly after 1979.
  • Purpose: unity of the ummah, teaching, news of the community.
Mission 2

The imam

  • The imam leads congregational prayer: chosen for knowledge of the Qur'an and good character (a hadith in Muslim gives this order).
  • Any qualified Muslim may lead; women may lead other women (the majority view).
  • Not a priest: no sacraments, no go-between. Every Muslim prays directly to God.
  • In Britain many mosques employ an imam to lead prayers, give the khutbah, teach, conduct marriages and funerals, and act as chaplain.
  • Persian- and Urdu-speaking Shi'a often call a prayer leader pish-namaz; Imam with a capital (the twelve Imams) means something different.
Mission 2

Other prayer

  • Du'a: "When My servants ask thee concerning Me, I am indeed close (to them): I listen to the prayer of every suppliant when he calleth on Me" (Qur'an 2:186, Yusuf Ali). Any language, any time.
  • Tahajjud: voluntary night prayer, "an additional prayer (or spiritual profit) for thee" (Qur'an 17:79, Yusuf Ali).
  • Nafila: extra prayers, many linked to the five (sunnah prayers before or after them).
  • Tasbih: glorifying God, e.g. after salah, often with prayer beads: SubhanAllah, Alhamdulillah, Allahu akbar (Shi'a call it the tasbih of Fatimah).
  • Wird: a daily litany given by a Sufi shaykh to his followers.
  • Eduqas: du'a ("cry out"), tasbih ("glorify").

Mission 2

Outward act, inward state

  • "The believers must (eventually) win through,- Those who humble themselves in their prayers" (Qur'an 23:1–2, Yusuf Ali). Khushu': humble attention.
  • Warning: "So woe to the worshippers Who are neglectful of their prayers" (Qur'an 107:4–5, Yusuf Ali).
  • Ihsan: worship God as if you see Him; if you do not see Him, He sees you (Hadith of Jibril, Muslim; paraphrase).
  • Effect on life: "Prayer restrains from shameful and unjust deeds" (Qur'an 29:45, Yusuf Ali).
  • The set form supports the heart: the body bows so the self learns to bow.
Mission 2Evaluate

Ritual, personal, collective?

  • Claim: Salah is simply a ritualistic act of piety.
  • For: fixed words in Arabic, many worshippers do not understand them; the form can become habit.
  • For: Muslims who pray mechanically are warned in 107:4–5, which shows the risk is real.
  • Against: niyyah and khushu' are required; the form trains attention.
  • Against: salah changes conduct (29:45); du'a and tahajjud give room for personal words.
  • Personal or collective? Prayer in congregation is worth many times prayer alone (Bukhari; Muslim; paraphrase); yet du'a and night prayer are deeply personal.
  • Criteria: What makes an act "only" ritual? Is collective worship the setting for personal experience or a substitute for it?
  • Spec issues: prayer as ritualistic piety; whether Jummah is the most important prayer.

Mission 3

Wealth as a trust

  • All things belong to God; humans hold wealth on trust. "Believe in Allah and His messenger, and spend of that whereof He hath made you trustees" (Qur'an 57:7, Pickthall).
  • Wealth is good when used well: "Wealth and sons are allurements of the life of this world: But the things that endure, good deeds, are best" (Qur'an 18:46, Yusuf Ali).
  • It is also a test (64:15).
  • Hoarding is condemned (9:34); so is waste: "eat and drink: But waste not by excess" (Qur'an 7:31, Yusuf Ali).
  • Riba (interest, usury) is forbidden: "Allah hath permitted trade and forbidden usury" (Qur'an 2:275, Yusuf Ali).
Mission 3

Zakah: the pillar of giving

  • Zakah: "purification" and "growth". Giving purifies the rest of one's wealth and the giver's heart.
  • "Of their goods, take alms, that so thou mightest purify and sanctify them" (Qur'an 9:103, Yusuf Ali).
  • 2.5% of savings and trade goods held for a lunar year above the nisab (minimum; usually the value of 85 g of gold or 595 g of silver). Crops and livestock have their own rates.
  • Origins: commanded with prayer in the Qur'an (2:43); organised and collected under the Prophet in Madinah.
  • Zakat al-fitr: a small gift of food per person at the end of Ramadan, so the poor can celebrate Eid.
  • Spelling: zakah.
Mission 3Read the source

Who receives zakah?

> "Alms are for the poor and the needy, and those employed to administer the (funds); for those whose hearts have been (recently) reconciled (to Truth); for those in bondage and in debt; in the cause of Allah; and for the wayfarer" (Qur'an 9:60, Yusuf Ali)

  • Eight categories: the poor, the needy, collectors, new Muslims (or those whose hearts are to be won), freeing captives, debtors, "the cause of Allah", travellers in need.

Q1 Name two groups who receive zakah who are not simply "poor".

Show answer

E.g. debtors; collectors; travellers; captives.

Q2 Why might "those employed to administer" be included?

Show answer

Collecting and distributing zakah is itself a service to the ummah.

Mission 3

Sadaqah

  • Sadaqah (from "truth, sincerity"): voluntary giving, any amount, at any time, to anyone in need.
  • Hadith widen it beyond money: a smile, removing harm from a path, a kind word (Tirmidhi; Bukhari; paraphrase).
  • Sadaqah jariyah (ongoing charity), e.g. a well: its good continues after death. A hadith names it with useful knowledge and a righteous child as deeds that go on benefiting the dead (Muslim; paraphrase).
  • Giving in secret is praised: "if ye conceal them, and make them reach those (really) in need, that is best for you" (Qur'an 2:271, Yusuf Ali).
  • Never spoiled by boasting: "cancel not your charity by reminders of your generosity or by injury" (Qur'an 2:264, Yusuf Ali).
  • Eduqas: sadaqat (voluntary giving).
Mission 3

Khums

  • Khums: "a fifth". "know that out of all the booty that ye may acquire (in war), a fifth share is assigned to Allah,- and to the Messenger, and to near relatives, orphans, the needy, and the wayfarer" (Qur'an 8:41, Yusuf Ali).
  • Sunni reading: khums applies to spoils of war and buried treasure.
  • Twelver Shi'a reading: the word for "acquire" covers all gain; so 20% of each year's surplus (what is left after the year's living costs).
  • Half, the share of the Imam, goes to a senior scholar (marja') for religious and charitable work; half, the share of the sayyids, to needy descendants of the Prophet.
  • Twelver Shi'a also pay zakah, on nine named kinds of wealth (crops, livestock, gold and silver coin).

Mission 3

Why give?

  • Obedience: zakah is commanded with prayer (2:43).
  • Compassion: "they feed, for the love of Allah, the indigent, the orphan, and the captive" (Qur'an 76:8, Yusuf Ali).
  • Personal sacrifice: "By no means shall ye attain righteousness unless ye give (freely) of that which ye love" (Qur'an 3:92, Yusuf Ali).
  • Benefits gained: purification, reward multiplied like a grain growing "seven ears" (2:261), protection from greed.
  • Impact on the ummah: support for the poor, debtors and travellers; less envy; bonds of brotherhood.
  • Liberationist thinking: e.g. Asghar Ali Engineer (1939–2013), Islam and Liberation Theology (1990), reads the Qur'an as siding with the oppressed (mustad'afin, 28:5) and calls for structural justice, not only charity (paraphrase). More in Mission 5.
Mission 3Evaluate

Is Islam based on support for the poor?

  • Claim: Islam is based on support for the poor.
  • For: zakah is a pillar; 9:60 names the poor first; 2:177 joins faith with giving.
  • For: liberationist readings put the oppressed at the centre of the Qur'an's message.
  • Against: Islam is based on tawhid: giving is worship of God, and its first purpose is purification.
  • Against: zakah's eight categories go beyond the poor; giving is one pillar of five.
  • Criteria: Does "based on" mean foundation or outcome? How do sadaqah and khums change the picture? Is charity enough without justice?
  • Spec issue: the extent to which Islam is based on support for the poor.

Mission 4

The fifth pillar

  • Hajj: pilgrimage to Makkah, 8–13 Dhu'l-Hijjah, the last month of the lunar year.
  • "Pilgrimage thereto is a duty men owe to Allah,- those who can afford the journey" (Qur'an 3:97, Yusuf Ali).
  • Once in a lifetime, for adult Muslims who are able: health, money (debts paid, family provided for) and a safe route.
  • Origins: Ibrahim and Isma'il raised the foundations of the Ka'bah (2:127); God told Ibrahim to "proclaim the Pilgrimage among men" (22:27).
  • The Prophet made one Hajj, the Farewell Hajj (10 AH, 632). Its rites are the model.
  • Umrah: the "lesser pilgrimage", at any time, with fewer rites; it does not replace Hajj.
Mission 4

Ihram: the state of consecration

  • At the miqat (boundary points) pilgrims enter ihram with an intention.
  • Men wear two plain white unstitched cloths; women wear simple modest clothes, face and hands uncovered.
  • Forbidden in ihram: perfume, cutting hair or nails, hunting, sexual relations, quarrelling (2:197: "no obscenity, nor wickedness, nor wrangling in the Hajj").
  • The talbiyah: "Here I am, O God, here I am; You have no partner; praise, blessing and sovereignty are Yours."
  • Meaning: rich and poor look the same; ihram is often compared to the burial shroud: the pilgrim leaves the world behind.
Mission 4

The rites, day by day

  • Arrival: tawaf, seven circuits of the Ka'bah anticlockwise; sa'y, seven times between the hills of Safa and Marwah.
  • 8th: to Mina, the tent city.
  • 9th: 'Arafat: wuquf (standing) in prayer from midday to sunset; the Prophet's Farewell Sermon was given here. Night at Muzdalifah, gathering pebbles.
  • The standing at 'Arafat is often seen as a rehearsal for the Day of Judgement: all stand equal before God, asking forgiveness.
  • 10th (Eid-ul-Adha): stoning the largest pillar (Jamrat al-Aqabah); sacrifice; men shave or trim, women cut a lock; tawaf al-ifadah and sa'y.
  • 11th–13th: at Mina, stoning all three jamarat each day.
  • Farewell tawaf before leaving. Many also visit the Prophet's Mosque in Madinah (not part of Hajj).

Mission 4

What Hajj means

  • Tawhid: the whole journey answers God's call ("Here I am").
  • Ibrahim's faith: his family's obedience is re-lived at the Ka'bah, Safa and Marwah, the jamarat and in the sacrifice.
  • Equality: "the most honoured of you in the sight of Allah is (he who is) the most righteous of you" (Qur'an 49:13, Yusuf Ali).
  • Unity of the ummah: Muslims of every nation, in one place, one dress, one prayer.
  • Eduqas: Hajj's role in uniting the Ummah.
  • Since the mid-20th century most pilgrims have come by air, not on months-long journeys by land and sea, so far more Muslims from every nation now meet at Hajj; Saudi Arabia limits numbers with country quotas.
  • Judgement and forgiveness: a Hajj done without wrongdoing returns the pilgrim "as on the day his mother bore him" (Bukhari; paraphrase).
  • Malcolm X (1925–1965), on Hajj in 1964, wrote that seeing pilgrims of every colour as brothers changed his view of race (letter from Makkah; paraphrase).
Mission 4Diversity

Sunni and Shi'a pilgrims

  • Sunni and Shi'a pilgrims perform the same Hajj together, at the same places and times.
  • Twelver Shi'a add tawaf al-nisa', a further full tawaf (seven circuits, with its two-rak'ah prayer) needed before marital relations are lawful again.
  • Small differences in prayer (see Mission 2) continue during Hajj.
  • Shi'a also value ziyarah (visiting) to the shrines of the Prophet's family: Karbala, Najaf, Mashhad. These are highly honoured but never replace Hajj.
  • Politics have touched Hajj: e.g. Iran's boycott after 1987; disputes over the treatment of Shi'a pilgrims and holy sites.
Mission 4Evaluate

More than a personal journey?

  • Claim: Hajj is above all a personal spiritual journey.
  • Personal: intention, repentance and forgiveness are individual; each pilgrim stands alone at 'Arafat.
  • Personal: many describe it as the high point of their own faith.
  • More than personal: it unites the ummah across nations (49:13); millions act as one.
  • More than personal: it re-enacts a shared history (Ibrahim, Hajar, the Prophet) and shapes Muslim identity worldwide.
  • Criteria: Can the personal and communal be separated? What does the Qur'an stress (22:27–28)? Has modern mass travel made Hajj more communal or more of an individual experience?
  • Spec issue: whether the Hajj is more than just a personal journey of religious enquiry.

Mission 5

Justice at the heart of revelation

  • 'Adl and qist: justice, fairness, putting things in their right place.
  • Muslims believe God sent prophets with scripture and "the Balance (of Right and Wrong), that men may stand forth in justice" (Qur'an 57:25, Yusuf Ali).
  • "Allah commands justice, the doing of good, and liberality to kith and kin" (Qur'an 16:90, Yusuf Ali).
  • The poor: zakah is their right, not a favour (9:60;). Surah 107 links denying the Judgement with failing to feed the poor.
  • The oppressed (mustad'afun): the Qur'an asks why believers do not act for "those who, being weak, are ill-treated (and oppressed)" (Qur'an 4:75, Yusuf Ali).
  • Your spec: "attitudes towards the poor".

Mission 5

Liberationist approaches

  • Liberationist: reading the Qur'an from the side of the poor and oppressed, and acting to change unjust structures, not only to give charity.
  • Farid Esack (b. 1959), South Africa: fought apartheid with the Call of Islam movement, alongside Christians and others; Qur'an, Liberation and Pluralism (1997): God sides with the oppressed (paraphrase).
  • Asghar Ali Engineer (1939–2013), India: Islam and Liberation Theology (1990): Islam began as a movement for the weak against the rich of Makkah (paraphrase).
  • The other side: traditionalist Sufis such as the Cambridge scholar Abdal Hakim Murad (b. 1960) argue that change must begin with purifying the heart (tazkiyah; 13:11): "activism within" (paraphrase).

"The struggle for justice must begin within the self."

  • Against: 4:75 demands action for the oppressed now; liberationists such as Esack: waiting for inner perfection serves the oppressor.

Mission 6

Why five categories?

  • Muslims believe the shari'a covers all of life: worship, family, trade, food, manners.
  • As fiqh developed, jurists needed a clear way to say how strongly God wants or forbids an act.
  • The answer: al-ahkam al-khamsa, the five rulings, a scale from "must" to "must not".
  • The scale guides intention and reward, not only law courts.
  • Eduqas: the need for guidance arising from the development of shari'a.
Mission 6

The scale of five

  • Fard (also wajib): obligatory. Doing it is rewarded; leaving it is a sin.
  • Mustahab (also mandub, sunna): recommended. Doing it is rewarded; leaving it is no sin.
  • Mubah: permitted and neutral. No reward, no sin.
  • Makruh: disliked. Avoiding it is rewarded; doing it is no sin, but it is discouraged.
  • Haram: forbidden. Doing it is a sin; avoiding it for God's sake is rewarded.
  • Halal means "permitted": in a broad sense everything not haram; Eduqas uses halal for the middle category.
  • Eduqas spelling: shari'a; middle category halal (blessed, allowed).
Mission 6Read the source

Do not invent the haram

> "O ye who believe! make not unlawful the good things which Allah hath made lawful for you, but commit no excess" (Qur'an 5:87, Yusuf Ali)

> "But say not - for any false thing that your tongues may put forth,- 'This is lawful, and this is forbidden,' so as to ascribe false things to Allah." (Qur'an 16:116, Yusuf Ali)

  • Jurists drew a principle: in everyday matters, the default is permission; a ban needs evidence.

Q1 What two errors does 5:87 warn against?

Show answer

Forbidding what God allowed; and excess.

Q2 Why might 16:116 make jurists slow to call something haram?

Show answer

Declaring a ban without proof is attributing a lie to God.

Mission 6

Fard and mustahab: examples

  • Fard: the five daily salah; fasting in Ramadan; zakah; Hajj if able; honesty; caring for parents.
  • Fard 'ayn: on each person (salah). Fard kifaya: on the community; if some do it, the rest are free (funeral prayer, training doctors).
  • Mustahab: extra (sunna) prayers; fasting on Mondays and Thursdays; sadaqah; saying bismillah before eating; using the miswak (tooth-stick).
  • Greeting with salam is mustahab; replying to it is obligatory.
  • These categories show a Muslim where the floor is and how to go above it.
Mission 6

Mubah, makruh, haram: examples

  • Mubah: most daily choices: which halal food, which job, what colour to wear.
  • Makruh: wasting water in wudu; eating raw garlic or onion before going to the mosque (Bukhari; paraphrase); many classical jurists put smoking here.
  • Divorce is lawful but discouraged: reported as the most hated of permitted things to God (Abu Dawud; paraphrase; chain debated).
  • Haram: alcohol and gambling (5:90); pork, blood and carrion (2:173); theft; riba; lying; murder.
Mission 6Diversity

Same scale, different placements

  • Hanafi jurists split the top: fard (proved by certain evidence) and wajib (by less certain evidence), e.g. the witr prayer is wajib for Hanafis, strongly recommended for other schools.
  • Hanafis also split makruh tahrimi (close to haram) and makruh tanzihi (mildly disliked).
  • Twelver Shi'a jurists use the same five: wajib, mustahabb, mubah, makruh, haram.
  • The same act may sit in different places: e.g. prawns are halal for most schools, makruh or forbidden for many Hanafis.
  • Each believer follows their school or, for Twelver Shi'a, a senior scholar (marja') (taqlid: following a qualified scholar or school).

Mission 6

Freedom and judgement

  • The mubah zone is vast: most of life is a matter of free, responsible choice.
  • Necessity (darura) can lift a ban: "if one is forced by necessity, without wilful disobedience, nor transgressing due limits,- then is he guiltless" (Qur'an 2:173, Yusuf Ali).
  • Intention (niyya) can turn a mubah act into worship: eating to have strength to serve God.
  • Makruh and mustahab call for judgement, not just obedience.
  • Some Muslims stress obedience: the categories are God's, not ours; others stress conscience within them.
Mission 6Evaluate

Clarity or confusion?

  • Claim: The five categories are, in practice, a recipe for confusion rather than clarity.
  • For: schools place the same act differently (witr, prawns, smoking).
  • For: the middle categories need scholarly advice; ordinary believers may not know where a new act belongs.
  • Against: a two-way halal/haram split would be cruder; five levels can capture degrees of moral weight.
  • Against: the core is shared: salah fard, alcohol haram; disagreement sits at the edges.
  • Judge it by: Is diversity confusion or mercy? Do the categories work for new questions (IVF, crypto-currency)? Do they guide inner life as well as outward acts?
  • Eduqas issue: the success of the five categories as a guide for Islamic living today.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Death → Barzakh → trumpet and resurrection → judgement (record, balance, intercession) → paradise or hell.
  • The Qur'an describes paradise and hell vividly (47:15; 67:7–10); Muslims read the images literally "without asking how", or as signs of realities beyond imagining.

Mission 2

  • Salah: five fixed prayers, with wudu, niyyah and qiblah; each rak'ah moves from standing to prostration.
  • Sunni and Shi'a Muslims share the pillar but differ in timing, hand position, prostration surface and wudu.
  • Other prayer (du'a, tahajjud, nafila, tasbih, wird) gives room for personal devotion beside salah and Jumu'ah.

Mission 3

  • Wealth belongs to God and is held on trust; hoarding, waste and riba are condemned.
  • Zakah (2.5% above the nisab, eight groups), sadaqah (voluntary, any good deed) and, for Twelver Shi'a, khums (a fifth of yearly surplus).
  • Reasons to give: obedience, compassion, sacrifice, benefits; liberationist readings stress justice for the poor.

Mission 4

  • Hajj: a once-in-a-lifetime duty for those able (3:97), retracing Ibrahim, Hajar and Isma'il and the Prophet's Farewell Hajj.
  • Ihram, tawaf, sa'y, 'Arafat, Muzdalifah, stoning, sacrifice: equality, obedience and a foretaste of Judgement.

Mission 5

  • Justice ('adl) is central: 57:25, 4:135, 5:8; the poor have a right to zakah; the Qur'an calls for action for the oppressed (4:75).

Mission 6

  • Fard, mustahab, mubah (halal), makruh, haram: a scale of how strongly God wills an act, linked to reward and sin.
  • Most of life is permitted; bans need evidence (5:87, 16:116); necessity and intention matter.
  • Schools differ at the edges (Hanafi fard/wajib; prawns; smoking); Shi'a fiqh uses the same five.
