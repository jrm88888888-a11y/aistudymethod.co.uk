Mission 1: The Qur'an says "pray", but not exactly how. Where do Muslims learn the rest?

Make a guess: The Qur'an says "pray", but not exactly how. Where do Muslims learn the rest?

Mission 1

Sunnah and Hadith

  • Sunnah ("path" or "custom"): the Prophet Muhammad's way of life: what he said, what he did, and what he approved by staying silent.
  • Hadith ("report", "news"; plural ahadith): a report, passed on orally and later in writing, of something in the Sunnah.
  • So the Sunnah is the example; the Hadith are how Muslims know it.
  • The Qur'an is God's word; a hadith records the Prophet's words and actions. Muslims keep them clearly apart.
Mission 1Read the source

Why follow the Prophet's example?

> "Verily in the messenger of Allah ye have a good example for him who looketh unto Allah and the Last Day" (Surah 33:21, Pickthall)

> "And whatsoever the messenger giveth you, take it. And whatsoever he forbiddeth, abstain (from it)." (Surah 59:7, Pickthall)

  • God sent the Qur'an to the Prophet "that thou mayst explain to mankind that which hath been revealed for them" (Surah 16:44, Pickthall).
  • "Obey Allah, and obey the messenger" (Surah 4:59, Pickthall).

Q1 According to 16:44, what was one task of the Prophet?

Show answer

To explain the revelation to people.

Q2 Why do these verses give the Sunnah authority?

Show answer

The Qur'an itself tells Muslims to obey and copy the Prophet.

Mission 1

Inside a hadith: isnad and matn

  • Every hadith has two parts:
  • Isnad ("support"): the chain of narrators, from the collector back to the Prophet.
  • Matn ("text"): the content: what the Prophet said or did.
  • Example (the first hadith in Sahih al-Bukhari):
  • Isnad: al-Bukhari ← al-Humaydi ← Sufyan ← Yahya ibn Sa'id ← Muhammad ibn Ibrahim ← Alqamah ← the caliph Umar ← the Prophet.
  • Matn: deeds are judged by their intentions, and each person will have what they intended (paraphrase).
  • A sound chain is unbroken, and every narrator in it is honest and has a good memory.

Mission 1

Testing and collecting

  • In the Prophet's lifetime, companions memorised his words; some wrote them down.
  • Later, false reports spread, so scholars of hadith (muhaddithun) travelled widely to collect and test them.
  • Testing the isnad: Did each narrator meet the next? Were they honest, careful, of good memory?
  • Testing the matn: Does it contradict the Qur'an, or better-known hadith?
  • Grades (Sunni scholars): sahih (sound) · hasan (good) · da'if (weak) · mawdu' (fabricated).
  • The great collections were made mainly in the 9th and 10th centuries CE.
Mission 1

Sunni collections

  • Sahih al-Bukhari: compiled by Muhammad al-Bukhari (810–870). He is said to have examined hundreds of thousands of reports and kept only those meeting his strictest tests.
  • Sahih Muslim: compiled by Muslim ibn al-Hajjaj (d. 875).
  • Together, "the two Sahihs": for Sunni Muslims, the most trusted books after the Qur'an.
  • With four more (Abu Dawud, al-Tirmidhi, al-Nasa'i, Ibn Majah) they form the Six Books.
  • Most Sunni hadith come through the companions (sahabah), such as Abu Hurayrah, Aisha and Umar.
Mission 1

Shi'a hadith: the Four Books

  • Shi'a Muslims follow the Prophet's Sunnah too, but trust it most when it comes through his family, the Ahl al-Bayt.
  • The sayings of the twelve Imams also carry authority: Shi'a Muslims believe the Imams were protected from error and handed down the Prophet's knowledge.
  • The Four Books:
  • al-Kafi, by al-Kulayni (d. 941)
  • Man la yahduruhu al-faqih, by al-Shaykh al-Saduq (d. 991)
  • Tahdhib al-ahkam and al-Istibsar, both by al-Shaykh al-Tusi (d. 1067)
  • Most Shi'a scholars hold that no collection is entirely sound: each report is still judged one by one.

Mission 1

The Sunnah in daily life

  • Worship: the Qur'an says "establish worship"; the Sunnah shows how: wudu, the times, the number of rak'ahs, the words. The Prophet told Muslims to pray as they had seen him pray (hadith in Sahih al-Bukhari, paraphrase).
  • Pillars: the 2.5% rate of zakah and the steps of Hajj come from the Sunnah.
  • Manners: saying "Bismillah" before eating, eating with the right hand, greeting with "As-salamu alaykum".
  • Character: none of you truly believes until he wants for his brother what he wants for himself (paraphrase of a hadith in Bukhari and Muslim).
  • Extra acts: voluntary prayers and fasts that the Prophet kept.
  • Law: the Sunnah, after the Qur'an, is a main source of Shari'ah (see Mission 2).
Mission 1Diversity

How much authority do the Hadith have?

  • Most Sunni and Shi'a Muslims: the Sunnah is the second source after the Qur'an and is needed to understand it.
  • Sunni: the two Sahihs are highly trusted; scholars still discuss individual reports in other books.
  • Shi'a: the Imams' teaching is part of the Sunnah; even the Four Books are checked report by report.
  • Qur'anists (a small minority): follow the Qur'an alone, doubting the Hadith's reliability. Most Muslims reject this: without the Sunnah, they say, salah could not be performed.
  • Some modern scholars: re-examine weaker hadith, e.g. on women's roles, while keeping the Sunnah's authority.

Mission 1Evaluate

"Muslims could not practise Islam without the Hadith."

  • For: the Qur'an tells Muslims to obey and follow the Prophet (4:59, 59:7, 33:21). Salah, zakah and Hajj depend on details found only in the Sunnah. The Prophet's role was to explain the Qur'an (16:44).
  • Against: the Qur'an is God's complete word; it calls itself clear guidance. Hadith were collected about two centuries later, and some reports are weak or fabricated. Qur'anists practise using the Qur'an alone.
  • Another view: the Sunnah is essential, but it was also passed on by practice, generation to generation, not only by written Hadith.
  • Judge it by: What does the Qur'an itself say about the Prophet's authority? How reliable is the testing of hadith? Is "practising Islam" the same as following every detail?

Mission 2: The Qur'an never mentions cocaine or cigarettes. How do Muslim scholars decide what Islam says?

Make a guess: The Qur'an never mentions cocaine or cigarettes. How do Muslim scholars decide what Islam says?

Mission 2

Shari'ah: the straight path

  • Shari'ah means a "path", originally the path to water: the straight path God sets for life.
  • "And now have We set thee (O Muhammad) on a clear road of (Our) commandment; so follow it" (Surah 45:18, Pickthall).
  • It covers everything: worship, food, family, business, crime and manners.
  • It is both a code of behaviour and ethics and, where applied, a legal system.
  • Muslims believe Shari'ah is God's law; fiqh ("understanding") is the scholars' work of working out what it requires. Fiqh is human, so scholars can differ.
  • Purpose: to please God and to protect what matters: religion, life, mind, family and property.
Mission 2

Where the law comes from

  • 1. Qur'an: for Muslims, God's word, the first and highest source.
  • 2. Sunnah: the Prophet's example, recorded in the Hadith (see Mission 1).
  • 3. Ijma: consensus: the agreement of qualified scholars.
  • 4. Qiyas: analogy: applying a known ruling to a new case with the same cause.
  • Ijtihad: the scholar's careful effort of reasoning that uses these sources.
  • Shi'a (Ja'fari) law: Qur'an; Sunnah of the Prophet and the Imams; ijma, valued when it includes the Imam's view; and 'aql (reason) in place of qiyas.

Mission 2Read the source

Consensus: Surah 4:115

> "And whoso opposeth the messenger after the guidance (of Allah) hath been manifested unto him, and followeth other than the believer's way, We appoint for him that unto which he himself hath turned" (Surah 4:115, Pickthall)

  • Sunni reading: the scholar al-Shafi'i (767–820) argued that "the believers' way" means the agreed way of the community: so ijma binds Muslims. A hadith adds that the community will not agree on an error (paraphrase).
  • Shi'a reading: consensus counts because, and when, it includes the view of the infallible Imam; agreement alone is not enough.
  • Example of ijma: the companions agreed to gather the Qur'an into one book.

Q1 What two things does the verse warn against?

Show answer

Opposing the messenger; following a way other than the believers'.

Q2 Why might Shi'a Muslims hesitate to make "the believers' way" mean any majority view?

Show answer

They hold that the majority went wrong over the succession after the Prophet.

Mission 2

Analogy (qiyas): a worked example

  • Qiyas applies an existing ruling to a new case that shares the same cause.
  • Original case: wine (khamr) is forbidden: "Strong drink and games of chance… are only an infamy of Satan's handiwork. Leave it aside" (Surah 5:90, Pickthall).
  • Cause: it intoxicates, clouding the mind. The Prophet said every intoxicant counts as khamr (paraphrase of a hadith in Sahih Muslim).
  • New case: cocaine, cannabis, whisky: none named in the Qur'an, but each intoxicates.
  • Ruling: haram (forbidden), by analogy.
  • This shows the chain: Qur'an → Hadith → Shari'ah ruling.
Mission 2

Ijtihad: reasoning out new answers

  • Ijtihad ("effort", "intellectual struggle"): a qualified scholar (mujtahid) reasons from the sources to a ruling.
  • When the Prophet sent Mu'adh ibn Jabal to Yemen as a judge, Mu'adh said he would judge by the Qur'an, then the Sunnah, then his own reasoning; the Prophet approved (paraphrase of a hadith in Abu Dawud).
  • Sunni: from about the 10th century many said the "gate of ijtihad" was closed and followed their school (taqlid: following). Many modern scholars use ijtihad again for new questions.
  • Shi'a: ijtihad never closed. Each Twelver Shi'a follows a living senior scholar, a marja' al-taqlid ("source to follow").

Mission 2

The schools of law

  • A madhhab is a school of fiqh, named after its founder. The four Sunni schools accept one another as valid.
  • Hanafi (Abu Hanifa, d. 767): the largest; Turkey, South and Central Asia, many British Muslims.
  • Maliki (Malik ibn Anas, d. 795): North and West Africa.
  • Shafi'i (al-Shafi'i, d. 820): Egypt, East Africa, South-East Asia.
  • Hanbali (Ahmad ibn Hanbal, d. 855): Saudi Arabia, Qatar; known for keeping closely to the texts of the Qur'an and Hadith.
  • Ja'fari (Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq, d. 765): Twelver Shi'a; Iran, Iraq, Lebanon, Bahrain.
  • Differences are mostly in detail: e.g. where the hands rest in salah; Shi'a Muslims often combine the five prayers into three times of day.
Mission 2

Ulema and qadis

  • Ulema (also spelt 'ulama'; singular 'alim, "one who knows"): scholars trained in Arabic, Qur'an, Hadith and fiqh. They teach, and a mufti among them gives a fatwa (a reasoned ruling).
  • Qadi: a judge who applies Shari'ah in a court. The Prophet and the early caliphs appointed judges (e.g. Mu'adh in Yemen).
  • Sunni: there are no priests; authority comes from learning. Muslims may ask different scholars.
  • Shi'a: the marja' has wide authority. In Iran, a senior jurist leads the state (wilayat al-faqih); many Shi'a scholars reject this political role.
  • Examples of rulings: many scholars, including a UK Muslim law council (1995), have ruled that organ donation is permitted to save life; others forbid it. Many recent fatwas declare smoking haram.
Mission 2

Halal and haram: a scale

  • Halal: permitted. Haram: forbidden.
  • Scholars place actions on a five-point scale:
  • Fard / wajib (obligatory): e.g. salah, fasting in Ramadan.
  • Mustahabb (recommended): e.g. extra prayers, smiling at others.
  • Mubah (neutral, simply allowed): e.g. what colour to wear.
  • Makruh (disliked): e.g. wasting water, even during wudu.
  • Haram (forbidden): e.g. pork, alcohol, theft, lying.
  • Principles: in daily matters, everything is halal unless forbidden. In real necessity the forbidden may be allowed: "he who is driven by necessity… it is no sin for him" (Surah 2:173, Pickthall).

Mission 2Diversity

Shari'ah in different communities

  • For most Muslims, mainly personal: prayer, fasting, food, modesty, honest business, family life.
  • Muslim-majority countries differ: many base family law (marriage, divorce, inheritance) on Shari'ah; a few, such as Saudi Arabia and Iran, also apply Shari'ah in criminal law; some, such as Turkey, have secular law.
  • In the UK: Muslims keep UK law; many scholars teach that Muslims should keep their agreements with the country they live in. Islamic finance avoids interest; Shari'ah councils give religious rulings, mostly on Islamic divorce, which are not binding in UK law.
  • Debates among Muslims: how far ijtihad should go; whether old rulings fit modern society; the role of Shari'ah councils.
Mission 2Evaluate

"Shari'ah can guide Muslims today as well as it did in the Prophet's time."

  • For: its sources are God's word and the Prophet's example, believed to be for all times. Qiyas and ijtihad answer new questions (drugs, smoking, organ donation). Millions find it guides daily life.
  • Against: much fiqh was worked out in medieval societies. Scholars and schools disagree, so there is often no single answer. Muslims in non-Muslim countries live mainly under secular law.
  • Another view: the purposes of Shari'ah (protecting religion, life, mind, family and property) are timeless; some detailed rulings may need fresh ijtihad.
  • Judge it by: Is Shari'ah mainly personal ethics or state law? Does disagreement weaken it or show it is flexible? Who has authority to decide: ulema, marja' or individual conscience?
Mission check

Shari'ah reaches new questions by reasoning from the Qur'an and Sunnah: that is how a modern drug can be haram without being named.

Mission 3: Shi'a Muslims follow twelve Imams, and every mosque has an imam. Is that the same word for the same job?

Make a guess: Shi'a Muslims follow twelve Imams, and every mosque has an imam. Is that the same word for the same job?

Mission 3

One word, two meanings

  • Imam (Arabic) means "one who stands in front": a leader.
  • Every mosque has an imam: the person who leads the prayer and serves the community. Sunni and Shi'a mosques both have one.
  • For Shi'a Muslims, the Imams (capital I) are something more: leaders chosen by Allah from the Prophet's family to guide the ummah after him.
  • Belief in these Imams, Imamah (the imamate), is one of the Shi'a five roots of faith.
  • So the same word can mean a local prayer leader or a divinely chosen guide.
Mission 3

The imamate: who guides after the Prophet?

  • Shi'a Muslims believe Allah would not leave the community without a guide after the Seal of the Prophets.
  • No new prophet comes, but each age needs a guide to explain the Qur'an without error.
  • So Allah appointed Imams from the Ahl al-Bayt (the Prophet's household), beginning with Ali, his cousin and son-in-law.
  • Role: to lead, to interpret the Qur'an and Sunnah, and to be a perfect example.
  • Significance: the imamate continues the guidance of prophethood. Sunni Muslims see leadership as chosen by the community, not a root of faith.
Mission 3Read the source

Ghadir Khumm and "leaders who guide"

  • In 632, returning from his Farewell Pilgrimage, the Prophet stopped at the pool (Ghadir) of Khumm. He took Ali's hand and said: "Of whomever I am mawla, Ali is his mawla" (Hadith of the pond of Khumm, paraphrase).
  • Shi'a reading: mawla means master and guardian; this was Ali's public appointment as leader.
  • Sunni reading: most Sunni scholars accept the hadith but read mawla as friend and supporter: an honour for Ali, not a succession.
  • Allah told Ibrahim: "I will make thee an Imam to the Nations." (Qur'an 2:124, Yusuf Ali) Ibrahim asked for Imams from his offspring too; Allah replied that His promise does not reach wrongdoers (paraphrase).

Q1 In 2:124, who makes someone an Imam?

Show answer

Allah: Shi'a Muslims see the imamate as a divine appointment.

Q2 Why do Sunni and Shi'a Muslims read Ghadir Khumm differently?

Show answer

They understand "mawla" differently: guardian and leader, or friend.

Mission 3

What makes an Imam

  • Nass (designation): each Imam is named by the one before, by Allah's command. The community does not elect him.
  • Inerrancy ('ismah; OCR: infallible; EDXI: sinless): Allah protects the Imams from sin and from error in teaching. Shi'a scholars cite Qur'an 33:33: Allah wishes to remove all abomination from "ye members of the Family" (Yusuf Ali), read as the Ahl al-Bayt.
  • Knowledge: the Imams inherit the Prophet's knowledge and are the true interpreters of the Qur'an.
  • Intercession (shafa'ah): Shi'a Muslims believe the Prophet and the Imams will plead for believers on the Day of Judgement, by Allah's permission ("Who is he that intercedeth with Him save by His leave?", Qur'an 2:255, Pickthall).

Mission 3

The Twelve Imams and the Hidden Imam

  • Ali (d. 661): the first Imam; his sermons and letters are collected in Nahj al-Balagha.
  • Husayn (3rd), killed at Karbala in 680: his stand against injustice is remembered at Ashura.
  • Ja'far al-Sadiq (6th, d. 765): a great teacher; Twelver law is called the Ja'fari school after him.
  • Ali al-Rida (8th, d. 818): his shrine at Mashhad, Iran, is a major place of pilgrimage.
  • Muhammad al-Mahdi (12th), born 869: Twelvers believe Allah hid him (the occultation), that he is alive and that he will return with Isa (Jesus) to fill the earth with justice.
Mission 3

Twelvers and Seveners today

  • Twelvers (the large majority of Shi'a): while the Imam is hidden, senior scholars (mujtahids; the most senior, marja', "source to follow") guide believers in his place.
  • Scholars disagree how far this reaches: some say into government (Iran's system of rule by a scholar); others say mainly religious guidance (many scholars in Najaf, Iraq).

Mission 3

The imam in Sunni Islam

  • Origins: the Prophet led the prayer himself and told others who should lead in his absence.
  • Chosen by the mosque community for knowledge of the Qur'an and good character, often after years of study.
  • Not a priest: no special holiness, no ordination; any adult Muslim man with knowledge can lead the congregation; most schools allow a woman to lead other women. Only Allah forgives sins.
  • Prayer: stands in front, facing the qiblah; the congregation follows his movements.
  • Community: gives the Friday khutbah (sermon), teaches the Qur'an, counsels families, conducts marriages (nikah) and funerals.
  • In Britain many imams also work as chaplains in hospitals, prisons and universities.
Mission 3Read the source

Sahih al-Bukhari 73:34–37

  • 73:34: the Prophet held up two fingers together: he and the one who looks after an orphan will be as close as this in Paradise (paraphrase).
  • 73:35–36: one who works for widows and the poor is like a fighter in Allah's cause, or one who prays all night and fasts all day (paraphrase).
  • 73:37: young men stayed with the Prophet for twenty nights. He sent them home to teach their families, saying: pray as you have seen me pray; when prayer is due, one of you should call the adhan, and the eldest should lead (paraphrase).

Mission 3Diversity

imam and Imam side by side

  • Sunni imam: chosen by the community · leads prayer, preaches, teaches · an ordinary believer with knowledge · many in every generation.
  • Shi'a prayer leader: the same role in Shi'a mosques, usually a trained scholar.
  • Shi'a Imam: appointed by Allah through nass (designation by the Imam before) · sinless and without error · the true interpreter of the Qur'an · twelve for most Shi'a (Twelvers), the last alive but hidden; a living Imam for most Isma'ilis (a smaller Shi'a group).
  • Different: for Shi'a Muslims, leadership of the ummah is a matter of faith (a root); for Sunni Muslims, a matter of practice and community choice.
Mission 3Evaluate

"The Imams are the most important source of guidance for Shi'a Muslims today."

  • For: Imamah is a root of faith. The Imams' sayings fill Shi'a hadith collections and shape Ja'fari law. Twelvers believe the Hidden Imam is alive and watches over the community. For most Isma'ilis the living Imam guides each age.
  • Against: the Qur'an is the word of Allah and comes first for all Muslims. The Prophet's Sunnah comes before the Imams' teaching. For Twelvers, day-to-day guidance now comes from scholars, because the Imam is hidden.
  • Another view: for Shi'a Muslims the sources do not compete: the Imams are the guardians of the Qur'an's meaning (the Hadith al-Thaqalayn joins "the Book" and "the family").
  • Judge it by: Which source settles disagreements? Does "guidance" mean law, example or spiritual help? Do Twelvers and Isma'ilis answer differently?

Mission 4

Sufism: the inner path

  • Sufism (Arabic tasawwuf) is the inner, mystical dimension of Islam: seeking to know and love Allah directly, with a purified heart.
  • It is not a separate branch like Sunni or Shi'a: most Sufis are Sunni; some are Shi'a.
  • Sufis keep the Shari'ah (the Five Pillars and Islamic law) and add inner disciplines.
  • Their goal is ihsan, described by the Prophet in the Hadith of Jibril: to worship Allah as if you see Him, and if you do not see Him, to know that He sees you (paraphrase).
  • Sufis often describe it as Shari'ah (the road) and tariqah (the path) leading to haqiqah (the truth: nearness to Allah).
Mission 4

Origins

  • Sufis trace their path to the Prophet: his nights of prayer, his retreats in the cave of Hira, his simple life.
  • 8th century: as the Muslim empire grew rich, devout people in Iraq turned away from luxury to prayer and self-denial, e.g. Hasan al-Basri (d. 728).
  • Name: probably from suf (wool): the rough woollen cloaks worn by early ascetics.
  • 11th century: al-Ghazali showed that Sufism fits within mainstream Sunni teaching.

Hearts at rest

"Those who believe, and whose hearts find satisfaction in the remembrance of Allah: for without doubt in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find satisfaction." (Qur'an 13:28, Yusuf Ali)

  • Remembrance = dhikr. Sufis take this verse as a central reason for their practice.

Show answer

Mission 4

Al-Ghazali (1058–1111)

  • Leading Sunni scholar, professor at the Nizamiyya college in Baghdad.
  • 1095: a crisis of faith; he could no longer speak or teach. He left his post, lived as a Sufi for about ten years, and later explained why in Deliverance from Error.
  • Revival of the Religious Sciences (Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din): 40 books on worship, daily life, the vices that destroy and the virtues that save.
  • Teaching: the Shari'ah and the inner life belong together; outward acts without a sincere heart are empty; inner experience without the Shari'ah goes astray.
  • Qur'anic basis: he grounds Sufism in the Qur'an and Sunnah, e.g. dhikr (13:28) and purifying the soul: "He is indeed successful who causeth it to grow" (Qur'an 91:9, Pickthall).
  • Relevance today: called Hujjat al-Islam ("Proof of Islam"); still taught in mosques and universities; his balance of law and spirituality guides many Muslims, Sufi and not.

Mission 4Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain two reasons why al-Ghazali is important for Muslims today.

  • Reason 1: he united Shari'ah and Sufism. Development: in the Revival of the Religious Sciences he taught that outward worship needs a sincere heart, so Muslims today pray not only correctly but attentively.
  • Reason 2: he grounded the inner life in the Qur'an. Source: "in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find satisfaction" (13:28). Development: so dhikr is shown to be Qur'anic, and many Muslims practise it daily.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The Sunnah is the Prophet's example (words, actions, approvals); a hadith is a report of it, made of an isnad (chain) and a matn (content).
  • For Muslims, the Sunnah is the second source of guidance and law: it shows how to pray, give and live.

Mission 2

  • For Muslims, Shari'ah ("straight path") is God's law for all of life; fiqh is the scholars' understanding of it.
  • Sunni sources: Qur'an, Sunnah, ijma (consensus), qiyas (analogy); Shi'a: Qur'an, Sunnah of the Prophet and Imams, ijma with the Imam, 'aql. Actions run from fard to haram.
  • Ulema and qadis interpret and apply it; schools and communities differ in detail and in how far it is state law.

Mission 3

  • Every mosque has an imam: a prayer leader chosen for knowledge and character, with no priestly status.
  • Shi'a Muslims believe in the Imamate: Imams appointed by Allah (nass), free from sin and error, beginning with Ali at Ghadir Khumm; for Twelvers the twelfth is hidden and will return.

Mission 4

  • Sufism (tasawwuf) is the inner dimension of Islam, practised by Sunni and some Shi'a Muslims alongside the Shari'ah; its aim is ihsan.
  • Al-Ghazali (1058–1111) united Shari'ah and Sufism in the Revival of the Religious Sciences; Ibn al-'Arabi and Rabi'a are other great teachers.
