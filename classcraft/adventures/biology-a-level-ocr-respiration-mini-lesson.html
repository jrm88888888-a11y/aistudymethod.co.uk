Mission 1

Mission 1: Pyruvate enters the mitochondrion with three carbon atoms. Where do they go, and what does the cell gain?

Make a guess: Pyruvate enters the mitochondrion with three carbon atoms. Where do they go, and what does the cell gain?

Mission 1

Inside the mitochondrion

  • Outer membrane: smooth; encloses the organelle.
  • Inner membrane: folded into cristae. Holds the electron carriers and ATP synthase (see Mission 2).
  • Intermembrane space: the narrow gap between the two membranes.
  • Matrix: the fluid inside the inner membrane.
  • The matrix holds the enzymes of the link reaction and Krebs cycle.
  • The matrix also contains mitochondrial DNA and ribosomes: the mitochondrion makes some of its own proteins.
Mission 1

The link reaction

  • In the matrix, pyruvate (3C) is:
  • decarboxylated: loses CO2 → a 2C acetyl group.
  • oxidised: loses hydrogen to NAD → reduced NAD.
  • The acetyl group joins coenzyme A → acetyl coenzyme A (acetyl CoA).
  • Coenzyme A is a carrier, not an enzyme. It delivers acetyl groups to the Krebs cycle.
  • Twice per glucose (two pyruvate).
Quick check

Mission 1

The Krebs cycle

  • Acetyl (2C) joins oxaloacetate (4C) → citrate (6C). Coenzyme A is released, to collect more.
  • Citrate is converted back to oxaloacetate in a series of steps, each with its own enzyme:
  • 2 decarboxylations: 2 CO2 released.
  • 4 dehydrogenations (oxidations): 3 reduced NAD and 1 reduced FAD.
  • 1 ATP, made directly by substrate-level phosphorylation.
  • Oxaloacetate is regenerated, ready for the next acetyl group.
  • Two turns per glucose.
  • Say: "CO2 comes from decarboxylation" — not: "oxygen combines with carbon".

?One acetyl group enters the Krebs cycle and oxaloacetate is regenerated. Which account of one turn of the cycle is correct?

Mission 1

Why the cycle needs oxygen without using it

  • NAD and FAD accept hydrogen; coenzyme A carries acetyl groups. Both kinds are coenzymes, recycled again and again.
  • Reduced NAD and FAD are re-oxidised only when they hand on their hydrogen to the electron transport chain, which needs oxygen.
  • No oxygen → no NAD for the dehydrogenations → the link reaction and Krebs cycle stop.
  • Say: "the Krebs cycle depends on oxygen" — not: "the Krebs cycle uses oxygen".

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why would a cell pump protons uphill across a membrane, only to let them flood straight back?

Make a guess: Why would a cell pump protons uphill across a membrane, only to let them flood straight back?

Mission 2

Handing on electrons

  • Reduced NAD and reduced FAD arrive from glycolysis, the link reaction and the Krebs cycle.
  • They deliver electrons (and protons) to a chain of carriers in the inner membrane, on the cristae.
  • Reduced NAD passes a pair of electrons to the first carrier. It becomes NAD again, free to collect more hydrogen.
  • Electrons pass from carrier to carrier in redox reactions: each carrier is reduced, then oxidised.
  • Each carrier is at a lower energy level than the last. Energy is released at each transfer.
Mission 2

Pumping protons

  • The energy released by electron flow pumps protons (H⁺) from the matrix into the intermembrane space.
  • The inner membrane is impermeable to H⁺, so they build up.
  • Result: a proton gradient across the inner membrane. It is electrochemical: a concentration difference and a charge difference.
  • More H⁺ = lower pH. The intermembrane space has a lower pH than the matrix.
  • Say: "protons are pumped across the membrane" — not: "electrons are pumped across".
Quick check
?How is the proton gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane generated?
Mission 2

ATP synthase: chemiosmosis

  • H⁺ return to the matrix mainly through ATP synthase, a channel protein in the inner membrane.
  • H⁺ flow down their gradient. The flow turns part of the enzyme.
  • That energy joins ADP + Pᵢ → ATP, on the matrix side.
  • Making ATP using energy from a proton gradient = chemiosmosis.
  • The explanation is called the chemiosmotic theory.
  • The whole process, electron flow + chemiosmosis = oxidative phosphorylation. Most of the cell's ATP is made this way.
  • Say: "protons flow through ATP synthase" — not: "water moves by osmosis".
Quick check
?How does ATP synthase couple the proton gradient to the synthesis of ATP?
Mission 2

Oxygen: the last acceptor

  • Oxygen accepts electrons from the last carrier, and protons from the matrix.
  • Product: water (metabolic water).
  • This keeps the last carrier oxidised, so electrons keep flowing.
  • No oxygen → the last carrier stays reduced → every carrier backs up → flow and proton pumping stop.
  • Then reduced NAD cannot be re-oxidised, so the Krebs cycle and link reaction stop too.
  • Say: "oxygen becomes water" — not: "oxygen becomes carbon dioxide".

Mission 3

Mission 3: Mitochondria, chloroplasts and bacteria all make ATP with the same machine. What does that tell us?

Make a guess: Mitochondria, chloroplasts and bacteria all make ATP with the same machine. What does that tell us?

Mission 3

Built for the job: the mitochondrion

  • Cristae: the folded inner membrane gives a large surface area for electron carriers and ATP synthase. More cristae → more ATP per second.
  • Very active cells (e.g. heart and flight muscle) have mitochondria packed with cristae.
  • Small intermembrane space: a few pumped protons raise the concentration fast → a steep gradient forms quickly.
  • Matrix: Krebs cycle enzymes and substrates are concentrated together → fast reactions; reduced NAD is made right beside the inner membrane.
  • Mitochondrial DNA and ribosomes: some mitochondrial proteins are made on site.
  • Say: "cristae increase the area for ATP synthase and carriers" — not: "for absorbing oxygen or glucose".

Mission 3

The same machine in the chloroplast

  • In the thylakoid membrane, light excites electrons in chlorophyll (photosystems).
  • Excited electrons pass along a chain of carriers. The energy released pumps H⁺ from the stroma into the thylakoid space (lumen).
  • H⁺ flow back out through ATP synthase → ATP made in the stroma.
  • ATP made this way in light = photophosphorylation.

  • In 1966, André Jagendorf and Ernest Uribe tested the chemiosmotic theory with thylakoids from spinach chloroplasts.
  • They soaked thylakoids in an acid buffer (pH 4) in the dark, so H⁺ filled the thylakoid space.

  • Their finding: ATP was made in the dark, with no light and no electron flow.
Mission check

Same machine, same weak point: in a chloroplast as in a mitochondrion, no gradient means no ATP.

Mission 4

Mission 4: When oxygen runs out, muscles make lactate and yeast makes alcohol. What do they gain from it?

Make a guess: When oxygen runs out, muscles make lactate and yeast makes alcohol. What do they gain from it?

Mission 4

Glycolysis on its own

  • Without oxygen, the mitochondrial stages stop. Only glycolysis still makes ATP: 2 ATP per glucose (net).
  • Anaerobic respiration happens in the cytoplasm only.
  • Human muscle: glucose → lactate. No carbon dioxide.
  • Yeast and plants: glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide.
  • Aerobic respiration yields many times more ATP per glucose.
  • Say: "anaerobic respiration uses no oxygen" — not: "it uses a little oxygen".
Mission 4

Recycling NAD

  • Glycolysis needs NAD to accept hydrogen at its oxidation step.
  • No oxygen → the electron transport chain stops → reduced NAD is not re-oxidised. NAD would soon run out, and glycolysis would stop.
  • Solution: pyruvate accepts the hydrogen from reduced NAD.
  • Animals: pyruvate → lactate. Pyruvate is reduced; NAD is regenerated.
  • Yeast and plants: pyruvate loses CO2, then the 2-carbon product is reduced → ethanol. NAD is regenerated.
  • The pathway is the same as in humans up to pyruvate. Only this last step differs.
  • These steps make no ATP. They keep glycolysis running, making its 2 ATP.
  • Say: "lactate formation regenerates NAD" — not: "lactate formation makes extra ATP".
Quick check
?During intense exercise, muscle cells convert pyruvate to lactate. What is the purpose of this conversion?
Mission 4

Why respire without oxygen?

  • Benefit: ATP can still be made when oxygen is short or absent.
  • Animals: a burst of extra ATP, fast, on top of aerobic respiration, for short intense activity. The cost: an oxygen debt (the earlier for CCEA).
  • Plants and fungi: survival for some time without oxygen, e.g. roots in waterlogged soil.
  • Lactate can be converted back later; ethanol and carbon dioxide are lost from the cell.

Mission 4Practical

Rate of respiration in yeast

  • OCR: respiration rates in yeast, under aerobic and anaerobic conditions.
  • Yeast + buffered glucose solution in a flask, in a water bath. Equilibrate 10 min.
  • Anaerobic: boil and cool the glucose solution (removes dissolved oxygen); cover with a layer of oil.
  • Aerobic: leave the flask open to air, or bubble air through it.
  • Measure CO2: volume in a gas syringe at 1-minute intervals.
  • Change one variable (e.g. temperature, glucose concentration); keep yeast mass, volume and pH constant.
  • Control: flask with no yeast.
  • Rate = volume of CO2 ÷ time.

Mission 5: How can a bead of liquid creeping along a thin tube show how fast seeds respire, and which fuel they use?

Make a guess: How can a bead of liquid creeping along a thin tube show how fast seeds respire, and which fuel they use?

Mission 5

Other fuels

  • Glucose and fatty acids are the main respiratory substrates. Many other carbon compounds can be respired.
  • Fatty acids are cut into many 2-carbon acetyl groups → acetyl coenzyme A → Krebs cycle.
  • Lipids are first hydrolysed to glycerol + fatty acids. Glycerol → triose phosphate → glycolysis.
  • Amino acids (from protein) are deaminated: the amino group is removed (→ urea, in the liver). The rest enters as pyruvate, acetyl coenzyme A or a Krebs cycle intermediate.
Mission 5

Energy per gram

SubstrateTypical energy value (kJ g⁻¹)
Carbohydrate16
Protein17

  • Lipid gives about twice the energy per gram of carbohydrate.
  • Why: lipids hold more hydrogen and less oxygen per gram.
  • More hydrogen → more reduced NAD → more ATP by oxidative phosphorylation.
  • Protein is similar to carbohydrate, but it is not normally a main fuel.
  • Say: "lipids have more oxidisable hydrogen" — not: "lipids contain more oxygen".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly compares lipids and carbohydrates as respiratory substrates?
Mission 5

The respiratory quotient

  • RQ = CO2 produced ÷ O2 consumed (same time; volumes or moles).
  • Glucose: C6H12O6 + 6O2 → 6CO2 + 6H2O. RQ = 6 ÷ 6 = 1.0.
  • A lipid (tripalmitin): C51H98O6 + 72.5O2 → 51CO2 + 49H2O. RQ = 51 ÷ 72.5 = 0.70.
  • Lipids need extra O2 to oxidise their extra hydrogen, so their RQ is lower.
  • Typical RQ: carbohydrate 1.0; protein about 0.9; lipid about 0.7.
  • A mixed fuel gives a value in between (resting humans: about 0.8).
  • RQ above 1: CO2 is released with no matching O2 uptake, so some anaerobic respiration (e.g. yeast making ethanol).
Mission 5Practical

The respirometer

  • OCR: effects of temperature, substrate concentration and different substrates on respiration rate, using respirometers.
  • Organisms (germinating seeds, woodlice or yeast) sit in a sealed tube, above potassium hydroxide solution (or soda lime), which absorbs CO2.
  • O2 is used → gas volume falls → the liquid bead in the capillary moves towards the organisms.
  • O2 volume = distance moved × πr² (r = capillary radius).
  • Control tube: glass beads of the same volume. It corrects for temperature and pressure changes.
  • Constant-temperature water bath; let the tubes equilibrate first; reset the bead with the syringe.
  • Without the alkali, the bead shows O2 used − CO2 released (the net change).
  • Carbohydrate: CO2 released = O2 used, so no net change. Lipid: less CO2 than O2, so the bead still moves towards the organisms. The net change hints at the fuel.
Mission 5

Worked example: rate of oxygen uptake

Illustrative data: 2.0 g germinating seeds, 10 min, 25 °C, capillary diameter 1.0 mm. Bead moved 24 mm towards the seeds; the control's bead moved 2 mm the same way.

  • Step 1, correct with the control: 24 − 2 = 22 mm.
  • Step 2, volume: r = 0.50 mm, so π × 0.50² × 22 = 17.3 mm³ O2.
  • Step 3, rate: 17.3 ÷ 2.0 g ÷ 10 min = 0.86 mm³ g⁻¹ min⁻¹.
  • Always divide by time and by mass, so different samples can be compared.
Quick check
?In a respirometer, carbon dioxide is absorbed by potassium hydroxide. With 5.0 g of germinating seeds, the gas volume in the apparatus fell by 1.2 cm³ in 10 minutes. What is the rate of respiration per gram?
Mission 5

Worked example: RQ from a respirometer

Illustrative data: the same seeds, two 10-minute runs.

  • Run 1, with KOH: bead moves towards the seeds; gas volume falls by 30 mm³ → O2 used = 30 mm³.
  • Run 2, without KOH: bead moves towards the seeds; gas volume falls by 6 mm³ → O2 used − CO2 released = 6 mm³.
  • CO2 released = 30 − 6 = 24 mm³.
  • RQ = 24 ÷ 30 = 0.80: a mixture of substrates, including some lipid or protein.
  • If the bead moves away in run 2, CO2 released > O2 used: add the movement, and RQ > 1.

?A student set up a respirometer with blowfly larvae above potassium hydroxide solution, and the bead moved steadily towards the larvae. In a second run, identical except that the potassium hydroxide was replaced with water, the bead hardly moved. Which explanation is best?

With the alkali, the bead measures oxygen uptake: the rate. Without it, it measures the balance of the two gases, and that balance points to the fuel.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Link reaction: pyruvate is decarboxylated and oxidised to an acetyl group, carried by coenzyme A. Krebs cycle: acetyl + oxaloacetate → citrate; 2 CO2, 3 reduced NAD, 1 reduced FAD, 1 ATP per turn; oxaloacetate regenerated.

Mission 2

  • Electrons from reduced NAD and FAD flow along carriers in the inner membrane; the energy released pumps H⁺ into the intermembrane space; H⁺ flows back through ATP synthase, making ATP (chemiosmosis); oxygen accepts the electrons and forms water.

Mission 3

  • Chemiosmosis works the same way in mitochondria and chloroplasts: carriers pump H⁺ across a membrane and ATP synthase uses the flow back to make ATP; only the energy source, the compartments and the last acceptor differ.
  • Mitochondria are adapted for ATP production: large cristae area, small intermembrane space, Krebs enzymes concentrated in the matrix.

Mission 4

  • Without oxygen only glycolysis makes ATP (net 2 per glucose); lactate (animals) or ethanol and carbon dioxide (yeast, plants) are the products.
  • Making lactate or ethanol regenerates NAD from reduced NAD, so glycolysis can continue.

Mission 5

  • A respirometer with an alkali measures O2 uptake: rate = corrected volume ÷ mass ÷ time; without the alkali it shows O2 used − CO2 released.
  • RQ = CO2 produced ÷ O2 consumed: about 1.0 carbohydrate, 0.9 protein, 0.7 lipid; above 1 means some anaerobic respiration.
  • Lipids and amino acids enter respiration mostly via acetyl coenzyme A and the Krebs cycle; lipids give about twice the energy per gram.
