Mission 1

Mission 1: How can a metal salt with no hydrogen in its formula give away hydrogen ions?

Make a guess: How can a metal salt with no hydrogen in its formula give away hydrogen ions?

Mission 1

Metal-aqua ions

  • Dissolve a metal salt in water: each metal ion is surrounded by six water ligands.
  • Each H2O donates a lone pair from O to the metal ion: a dative (coordinate) bond.
  • Formula: [M(H2O)6]ⁿ⁺, an octahedral metal-aqua ion.
  • The ion keeps the metal's charge: water ligands are neutral.
  • You need: [Fe(H2O)6]²⁺, [Cu(H2O)6]²⁺, [Al(H2O)6]³⁺, [Fe(H2O)6]³⁺.
Mission 1

Colours of aqua ions

  • [Cu(H2O)6]²⁺: blue.
  • [Fe(H2O)6]²⁺: pale green.
  • [Fe(H2O)6]³⁺: pale violet, but its solutions usually look yellow-brown.
  • The yellow-brown comes from [Fe(H2O)5(OH)]²⁺ and similar ions, formed by hydrolysis (see "Hydrolysis: aqua ions are acids", below).
  • [Al(H2O)6]³⁺: colourless (Al³⁺ has no d electrons).
Quick check
?Which list gives the usual colours of solutions of Fe²⁺(aq), Fe³⁺(aq) and Cu²⁺(aq), in that order?
Mission 1

Pulled water ligands

  • The positive metal ion attracts the O lone pair strongly.
  • Electron density is drawn from the O–H bonds towards O and the metal.
  • So the O–H bonds become more polar and weaker.
  • A water ligand can now lose an H⁺, leaving an OH⁻ ligand on the metal.
  • The "hydrogen ions" in the opening question come from the water ligands, not the salt's formula.
Mission 1

Hydrolysis: aqua ions are acids

  • A water molecule from the solvent takes H⁺ from a water ligand:
  • [Fe(H2O)6]³⁺(aq) + H2O(l) ⇌ [Fe(H2O)5(OH)]²⁺(aq) + H3O⁺(aq)
  • This is hydrolysis: the aqua ion acts as a Brønsted–Lowry acid.
  • 2+ ions do the same, much less: [Fe(H2O)6]²⁺(aq) + H2O(l) ⇌ [Fe(H2O)5(OH)]⁺(aq) + H3O⁺(aq)
  • The 3+ ions can lose further H⁺ in later steps.
Mission 1

Why 3+ beats 2+: charge/size ratio

  • Fe³⁺: higher charge and smaller (radius ≈ 65 pm) than Fe²⁺ (≈ 78 pm).
  • So Fe³⁺ has a higher charge/size ratio (charge density).
  • It polarises its water ligands more: O–H bonds weaker, H⁺ released more easily.
  • More H3O⁺ forms: [M(H2O)6]³⁺ solutions are more acidic.
  • At 0.1 mol dm⁻³: [Fe(H2O)6]³⁺ about pH 2; [Fe(H2O)6]²⁺ about pH 5.
  • Al³⁺ (≈ 54 pm, 3+) is also acidic for the same reason.
Quick check
?Why is a solution of [Al(H2O)6]³⁺ more acidic than a solution of [Cu(H2O)6]²⁺ of the same concentration?
Mission 1

Bases pull H⁺ off: a neutral precipitate

  • Add a few drops of OH⁻(aq): it removes H⁺ from water ligands (H⁺ + OH⁻ → H2O).
  • Remove as many H⁺ as the ion's charge: the complex becomes neutral.
  • A neutral complex is insoluble: it appears as a precipitate.
  • [Cu(H2O)6]²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → [Cu(H2O)4(OH)2](s) + 2H2O(l)
  • Blue solution → pale blue precipitate.
  • This is the same acid behaviour as in hydrolysis, pushed further by a strong base.
Mission 1

Worked example: iron(III) with hydroxide

  • Start: [Fe(H2O)6]³⁺. Charge 3+, so remove 3 H⁺.
  • Product: [Fe(H2O)3(OH)3]. Check: 6 ligands; charge 3+ − 3 = 0.
  • Each removed H⁺ meets an OH⁻: 3H2O formed.
  • [Fe(H2O)6]³⁺(aq) + 3OH⁻(aq) → [Fe(H2O)3(OH)3](s) + 3H2O(l)
  • Observation: yellow-brown solution → orange-brown precipitate.
  • Give the complex formulae, as above.
Quick check
?A few drops of NaOH(aq) are added to a solution of [Fe(H2O)6]²⁺. Which species is the precipitate?
Mission check

?Gallium is below aluminium in Group 13 and forms the aqua ion [Ga(H2O)6]³⁺. A few drops of NaOH(aq) are added, and a white precipitate forms. Which species is the precipitate?

No hydrogen in the salt's formula, but plenty in its six water ligands. A base pulls off H⁺ until the complex is neutral, and the solid drops out.

Mission 2: Why does extra alkali make some metal precipitates vanish but leave others untouched?

Make a guess: Why does extra alkali make some metal precipitates vanish but leave others untouched?

Mission 2

A little base: the same first step

  • NaOH(aq) supplies OH⁻, which removes H⁺ from water ligands (see Mission 1).
  • A few drops: the neutral hydroxide precipitates.
  • NH3(aq) is a weak base: NH3 + H2O ⇌ NH4⁺ + OH⁻.
  • So a little NH3 gives the same precipitate as a little NaOH.
  • [Cu(H2O)6]²⁺(aq) + 2NH3(aq) → [Cu(H2O)4(OH)2](s) + 2NH4⁺(aq)
  • The interesting part comes next: add the reagent in excess.
Mission 2

Precipitate colours

  • Cu²⁺: blue solution → pale blue precipitate.
  • Fe²⁺: pale green → green precipitate; turns brown at the surface on standing (air oxidises Fe(II) to Fe(III)).
  • Fe³⁺: yellow-brown → orange-brown precipitate.
  • Al³⁺: colourless → white precipitate.
Quick check
?A few drops of NaOH(aq) are added to a pale green solution. A green precipitate forms, and its surface turns brown on standing in air. Which ion was in the solution?
Mission 2

Excess NaOH: amphoteric hydroxides

  • An amphoteric hydroxide dissolves in both acids and bases.
  • Al: [Al(H2O)3(OH)3](s) + OH⁻(aq) → [Al(OH)4]⁻(aq) + 3H2O(l). White precipitate → colourless solution.
  • With acid: [Al(H2O)3(OH)3](s) + 3H⁺(aq) → [Al(H2O)6]³⁺(aq). Precipitate dissolves: colourless solution.
  • Cu, Fe(II) and Fe(III) hydroxides: no change in excess NaOH.
Mission 2

Excess ammonia: ligand exchange

  • NH3 is a ligand as well as a base. In excess it replaces H2O and OH⁻ ligands, giving a soluble complex.
  • Cu: [Cu(H2O)4(OH)2](s) + 4NH3(aq) → [Cu(NH3)4(H2O)2]²⁺(aq) + 2H2O(l) + 2OH⁻(aq). Pale blue precipitate → deep blue solution.
  • Al and Fe hydroxides: no change in excess NH3.
Quick check
?Which precipitate dissolves in excess NH3(aq) but not in excess NaOH(aq)?
Mission 2

Carbonate ions: 2+ and 3+ differ

  • 2+ ions: the metal carbonate precipitates.
  • [Fe(H2O)6]²⁺(aq) + CO3²⁻(aq) → FeCO3(s) + 6H2O(l): green precipitate.
  • [Cu(H2O)6]²⁺(aq) + CO3²⁻(aq) → CuCO3(s) + 6H2O(l): blue-green precipitate.
  • 3+ ions are more acidic (see Mission 1): they give H⁺ to CO3²⁻, which leaves as CO2.
  • 2[Fe(H2O)6]³⁺(aq) + 3CO3²⁻(aq) → 2[Fe(H2O)3(OH)3](s) + 3CO2(g) + 3H2O(l): orange-brown precipitate and effervescence.
  • Al³⁺: the same, giving a white precipitate and effervescence.
Quick check
?Na2CO3(aq) is added to a yellow-brown solution of iron(III) ions. An orange-brown precipitate forms and bubbles of gas are seen. Which statement explains the bubbles?
Mission 2

Running the tests

  • Wear eye protection: NaOH(aq) is corrosive.
  • A few cm³ of the unknown solution in a test tube. Add NaOH(aq) dropwise: note the precipitate colour.
  • Keep adding until in excess, shaking: does the precipitate dissolve?
  • Repeat with NH3(aq) on a fresh sample, dropwise then in excess.
  • Repeat with Na2CO3(aq): look for a precipitate and effervescence.
  • Leave the precipitates to stand: some change colour in air (oxidation).
  • This is Required practical 11.
Mission check

?Zinc ions are not in your table of tests. A few drops of NaOH(aq) added to Zn²⁺(aq) give a white precipitate. With excess NaOH(aq), the precipitate dissolves to give a colourless solution. Which equation shows what the excess NaOH(aq) does?

The precipitates that vanish turn into charged complex ions.

They do it by losing more H⁺ (amphoteric) or by swapping in new ligands. The rest stay neutral and stay solid.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • [M(H2O)6]ⁿ⁺: the metal ion polarises its water ligands, so they can lose H⁺.
  • OH⁻ removes as many H⁺ as the charge: the neutral complex precipitates.
  • 3+ aqua ions are more acidic than 2+: higher charge/size ratio, more polarising.

Mission 2

  • A little OH⁻ (or NH3): neutral hydroxide precipitate. Its colour identifies the ion.
  • Excess NaOH dissolves amphoteric hydroxides; excess NH3 dissolves some by ligand exchange.
  • Carbonate: 2+ gives MCO3; 3+ gives the hydroxide and CO2 (more acidic).
