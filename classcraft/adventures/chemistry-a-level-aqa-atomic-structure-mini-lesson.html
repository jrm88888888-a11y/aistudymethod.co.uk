Mission 1

How the model of the atom changed

  • Dalton (early 1800s): atoms are tiny solid spheres that can't be divided.
  • Thomson: discovered the electron (1897). His "plum pudding" model (1904): electrons in a sphere of positive charge.
  • Rutherford (1911): most alpha particles passed through gold foil; a few bounced back. So: a tiny, dense, positive nucleus, mostly empty space.
  • Bohr (1913): electrons in fixed energy levels (shells).
  • Chadwick (1932): discovered the neutron.
  • Each model was replaced when new evidence didn't fit it.
Mission 1

The nuclear atom

  • Almost all the mass is in a tiny, dense nucleus of protons and neutrons.
  • Electrons surround the nucleus. The atom is about 100 000 times wider than its nucleus.

Mission 1

Reading the nuclear symbol

  • In ᴬZ X, Z = atomic (proton) number = number of protons. It fixes the element.
  • A = mass number = protons + neutrons. So neutrons = A − Z.
  • Neutral atom: electrons = Z. Positive ion: Z − charge. Negative ion: Z + charge.
  • ⁵⁶26Fe³⁺: 26 protons, 30 neutrons, 23 electrons.
  • ¹⁶8O²⁻: 8 protons, 8 neutrons, 10 electrons.
  • Ions form by losing or gaining electrons. The number of protons never changes.

Mission 1

Isotopes

  • Isotopes: atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons, so different masses.
  • Chlorine-35: 17 p, 18 n. Chlorine-37: 17 p, 20 n.
  • Same electron configuration → same chemical properties.
  • Different mass → different physical properties: density, rate of diffusion.
Quick check
?Chlorine (atomic number 17) has isotopes with mass numbers 35 and 37. Pure samples of ³⁵Cl2 gas and ³⁷Cl2 gas are compared at the same temperature and pressure. Which statement is correct?
Mission 1

Masses on the carbon-12 scale

  • Atoms are too light to weigh in grams, so we compare them with ¹/12 of the mass of one carbon-12 atom.
  • Relative atomic mass, Aᵣ = average mass of one atom of the element ÷ (¹/12 × mass of one atom of ¹²C).
  • On this scale ¹²C is exactly 12. Relative masses have no units.
Mission 1

A weighted mean

  • Aᵣ = Σ(relative isotopic mass × % abundance) ÷ 100.
  • Boron: ¹⁰B 19.9% (10.01), ¹¹B 80.1% (11.01). (10.01 × 19.9 + 11.01 × 80.1) ÷ 100 = (199.2 + 881.9) ÷ 100 = 10.81.
  • Chlorine: ³⁵Cl 75.8% (34.97), ³⁷Cl 24.2% (36.97). (34.97 × 75.8 + 36.97 × 24.2) ÷ 100 = 35.45.
  • So 35.45 is an average: no single atom has it. It sits nearer 35 because ³⁵Cl is about 3 times as common.
Calculate
?Lithium is 7.59% ⁶Li (relative isotopic mass 6.015) and 92.41% ⁷Li (7.016).
  • Calculate the relative atomic mass of lithium to three significant figures.
Mission 1

Where the abundances come from

  • A mass spectrometer gives the data. For an element, each peak from a 1+ ion is one isotope.
  • x-axis: m/z = the isotope's relative mass. Peak height = its abundance.
  • Neon: m/z 20 (90.48%), 21 (0.27%), 22 (9.25%).
  • Aᵣ = (19.992 × 90.48 + 20.994 × 0.27 + 21.991 × 9.25) ÷ 100 = 20.18.
  • The tallest peak is the most common isotope, not the Aᵣ.
Mission check

?Bromine's relative atomic mass is 79.90, almost exactly halfway between 79 and 81. What does this show?

Same reason as chlorine: isotopes. Aᵣ is a weighted mean of whole-ish isotopic masses, so it is rarely a whole number.

Mission 2

What a mass spectrometer tells you

  • It turns particles into positive ions and sorts them by mass-to-charge ratio, m/z.
  • For a 1+ ion, m/z = the ion's relative mass.
  • For an element: one peak per isotope. Peak height = relative abundance.
  • So it gives each isotope's mass and abundance, and from these the element's mean atomic mass.
  • The isotope pattern also identifies an element, and molecular ions give Mᵣ.
Mission 2

Time of flight 1: ionisation

  • Electron impact: the vaporised sample is hit by high-energy electrons from an electron gun, which knock one electron off: X(g) + e⁻ → X⁺(g) + 2e⁻.
  • Used for elements and low-Mᵣ compounds. It often breaks molecules into fragments.
  • Electrospray: the sample, dissolved in a volatile solvent, is sprayed from a fine needle at high voltage. Each molecule gains a proton: X + H⁺ → XH⁺.
  • Used for large molecules (e.g. proteins): little fragmentation.
  • Either way the ions are 1+.
Mission 2

Acceleration, drift, detection

  • Acceleration: an electric field gives every ion the same kinetic energy.
  • Ion drift: through a field-free flight tube. KE = ½mv², so lighter ions move faster and arrive first.
  • Detection: ions hit a negatively charged plate and gain electrons. The current is proportional to the number of ions.
  • Data analysis: a computer turns flight times into m/z and currents into abundances.

Mission 2

Aᵣ from relative peak heights

  • Heights are often scaled so the tallest peak is 100. Then they don't add up to 100.
  • Aᵣ = Σ(m/z × height) ÷ Σ(heights).
  • Boron: m/z 10 (height 24.8), m/z 11 (height 100).
  • (10 × 24.8 + 11 × 100) ÷ (24.8 + 100) = 1348 ÷ 124.8 = 10.8.
  • Sense check: the answer lies between the smallest and largest m/z, nearer the tallest peak.
Quick check
?The mass spectrum of element E shows three peaks from singly charged atomic ions: m/z 24 (relative abundance 100), m/z 25 (relative abundance 12.7) and m/z 26 (relative abundance 13.9). What is the relative atomic mass of E, calculated from these data?
Mission 2

Molecules: the molecular ion

  • A molecule that loses one electron is the molecular ion, M⁺.
  • The M⁺ peak is at the highest m/z (ignore a tiny peak one unit above it). Its m/z = Mᵣ.
  • Electron impact gives M⁺, so m/z = Mᵣ. Electrospray gives MH⁺, so m/z = Mᵣ + 1.
  • The other peaks are fragments (organic analysis lesson).
Quick check
?The electron-impact mass spectrum of a liquid has peaks at m/z 15, 29, 31, 45 and 46, plus a very small peak at 47. Which compound could it be? (Aᵣ: H 1.0, C 12.0, O 16.0)
Mission 2

Precise masses give the formula

  • A high-resolution spectrometer measures m/z to 4 or more decimal places.
  • Precise masses: ¹H 1.00783 · ¹²C 12.00000 · ¹⁴N 14.00307 · ¹⁶O 15.99491.
  • Mᵣ ≈ 28 could be CO (27.9949), N2 (28.0061) or C2H4 (28.0313).
  • A molecular ion at m/z 28.0313 must be C2H4.
  • Add up the precise masses for each candidate; pick the one that matches.
Quick check
?A compound's molecular ion has a precise m/z of 44.0262. Which is its molecular formula? (¹H 1.00783, ¹²C 12.00000, ¹⁴N 14.00307, ¹⁶O 15.99491)
Mission check

?The mass spectrum of gallium shows two peaks from 1+ ions: m/z 69 (height 100) and m/z 71 (height 66.4).
What is the relative atomic mass of gallium given by these data?

You never weigh one atom. The spectrometer sorts ions by m/z and counts them; masses × abundances give the average. (The data-table value, 69.72, uses precise isotopic masses rather than whole-number m/z.)

Mission 3

Shells split into sub-shells

  • Each shell (main energy level, n) splits into sub-shells: s, p, d, f.
  • n = 1: 1s. n = 2: 2s, 2p. n = 3: 3s, 3p, 3d. n = 4: 4s, 4p, 4d, 4f.
  • s, p, d, f have 1, 3, 5, 7 orbitals, so hold 2, 6, 10, 14 electrons.
  • So the first four shells hold 2, 8, 18, 32.
  • Up to Z = 36 no f orbitals are filled; 4f only matters for the 32.
Mission 3

Orbitals: where an electron is

  • An orbital is a region around the nucleus where an electron is likely to be found. Not a path.
  • Each orbital holds up to two electrons, with opposite spins.
  • s orbital: a sphere centred on the nucleus.
  • p orbitals: three dumbbells along the x, y and z axes, at 90° to each other.
  • Background: you won't be asked to draw these shapes. The key idea is a region, not an orbit.
Quick check
?What is the maximum number of electrons that a 3d sub-shell can hold, and why?
Mission 3

Three filling rules

  • Aufbau ("building up"): fill the lowest energy first: 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p.
  • Pauli: at most two electrons per orbital, with opposite spins.
  • Hund: in orbitals of equal energy, electrons go in singly, spins parallel, before pairing.
  • Electrons-in-boxes: one box per orbital; arrows ↑↓ for spins. Oxygen 2p⁴: [↑↓][↑ ][↑ ].
Mission 3

Writing configurations

  • Iron, Z = 26: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d⁶ 4s², shortened to [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s².
  • 4s fills before 3d, but either written order is fine: [Ar] 4s² 3d⁶ is also correct.
  • Two exceptions up to krypton: Cr [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ and Cu [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹.
Quick check
?Which full electron configuration of a ground-state atom is correct?
Quick check
?Which electrons-in-boxes diagram correctly shows the 3p sub-shell of a ground-state sulfur atom (… 3s² 3p⁴)?
Mission 3

Configurations of ions

  • s- and p-block ions: add or remove electrons in the outer sub-shell.
  • Na⁺: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶. S²⁻: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ (same as Ar).
  • d-block ions lose their 4s electrons first.
  • Fe²⁺: [Ar] 3d⁶. Fe³⁺: [Ar] 3d⁵. Cu²⁺ (from [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹): [Ar] 3d⁹.
  • 4s fills first but empties first too.
Quick check
?An iron atom (Z = 26) has the condensed electron configuration [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s². How is an Fe²⁺ ion formed from this atom?
Mission 3

Configurations and the periodic table

  • Blocks are named after the sub-shell being filled: s-block (groups 1–2), p-block (13–18), d-block.
  • Across Periods 2 and 3: two s electrons are added, then six p electrons.
  • Down a group the outer configuration repeats: Mg 3s², Ca 4s². So their chemistry is similar (both form 2+ ions).
Mission check

?A ground-state phosphorus atom is [Ne] 3s² 3p³. Where are its three 3p electrons?

An electron isn't on a track. It is in an orbital: a region where it is likely to be. The configuration tells you which orbitals are occupied.

Mission 4: How much energy does it take to pull an electron out of an atom?

Make a guess: How much energy does it take to pull an electron out of an atom?

Mission 4

From the convergence limit to kJ mol⁻¹

  • Background: this shows where an IE value comes from. You won't be asked to calculate it.
  • Hydrogen's lines for falls to n = 1 crowd together to a convergence limit: a fall from n = ∞ to n = 1.
  • Run it backwards: the same energy removes the electron completely. That is ionisation.
  • f = 3.29 × 10¹⁵ Hz. E = hf = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.29 × 10¹⁵ = 2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ J per atom.
  • × 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ = 1.31 × 10⁶ J mol⁻¹ = 1310 kJ mol⁻¹ (measured: 1312).
Mission 4

Defining ionisation energy

  • First ionisation energy: the energy needed to remove one mole of electrons from one mole of gaseous atoms, forming one mole of gaseous 1+ ions.
  • Na(g) → Na⁺(g) + e⁻. Always gaseous; always endothermic (positive).
  • Second: Na⁺(g) → Na²⁺(g) + e⁻. Each further one is a successive ionisation energy.
  • You define only the first, but write equations for the second and later ones.
Quick check
?Which equation represents the second ionisation energy of calcium?
Mission 4

What sets the size of an IE

  • The electron is held by its attraction to the nucleus. Stronger attraction → higher IE.
  • Nuclear charge: more protons → stronger attraction.
  • Distance (atomic radius): electron further out → weaker attraction.
  • Shielding: inner electrons repel the outer electron, so it feels less of the nuclear charge.
Mission 4

The pattern across the periodic table

  • Across a period: IE generally rises. Nuclear charge increases; electrons enter the same shell, with similar shielding; the radius shrinks.
  • Period 3: Na 496 → Ar 1521 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Down a group: IE falls. Each new shell puts the outer electron further away and more shielded; this outweighs the extra protons.
  • Peaks at the noble gases, sharp drops at group 1: the evidence for shells.
  • Two small dips in each period (e.g. Be → B, N → O) come from sub-shells: Mission 5.
Quick check
?How does the first ionisation energy generally change across Period 3 from sodium to argon, and why?
Mission 4

Group 2: IE and reactivity

  • First IE falls down Group 2: Be 900 · Mg 738 · Ca 590 · Sr 550 · Ba 503 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Each step down adds a shell: the outer s electrons are further out and more shielded. Evidence for shells.
  • Group 2 metals react by losing two electrons, so 1st + 2nd IE matter.
  • 1st + 2nd: Mg 2188 · Ca 1735 · Sr 1614 · Ba 1468 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Less energy needed to form M²⁺ → reactivity increases down the group.
Mission 4

Successive IEs reveal shells and the group

  • Successive IEs always rise: each electron is pulled from an ion that is more positive than the last.
  • A big jump marks the first electron taken from a shell nearer the nucleus, less shielded.
  • Magnesium: 738, 1451, then 7733 kJ mol⁻¹. Jump after 2 → 2 outer electrons → Group 2.
  • Count the electrons before the first big jump = outer electrons.
  • Three outer electrons → aluminium's group (13 in 1–18 numbering; Group 3 or III on some tables).
Quick check
?The first four successive ionisation energies of an element X are 578, 1817, 2745 and 11 577 kJ mol⁻¹. How many electrons does an atom of X have in its outer shell?
Mission check

?Element X is in Period 3.
Its first six ionisation energies are 1012, 1907, 2914, 4964, 6274 and 21 268 kJ mol⁻¹. Which statement identifies X and explains the large sixth value?

How much energy? It depends on how strongly the nucleus attracts that electron: nuclear charge, distance and shielding. Removing an electron from an inner shell takes far more.

Mission 5

Mission 5: If nuclear charge rises across period 3, why does aluminium lose an electron more easily than magnesium?

Make a guess: If nuclear charge rises across period 3, why does aluminium lose an electron more easily than magnesium?

Mission 5

Two dips in period 3

  • First ionisation energy: energy to remove 1 mol of electrons from 1 mol of gaseous atoms. X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻.
  • Across period 3 it rises overall: more protons, same shell, similar shielding.
  • But it falls twice: Mg → Al (738 → 578) and P → S (1012 → 1000 kJ mol⁻¹).
  • After argon it falls steeply to potassium: a new shell. That is evidence for shells.
Mission 5

Why dips mean sub-shells

  • If every electron in shell 3 had the same energy, the rise would be smooth.
  • The dips show that shell 3 is split into sub-shells of different energy: 3s, then 3p.
  • To explain a dip, compare the electron removed from each atom: which sub-shell, which orbital.
Mission 5

Mg → Al: a new sub-shell

  • Mg: [Ne] 3s². Al: [Ne] 3s² 3p¹.
  • Al's electron is removed from 3p, which is higher in energy than 3s.
  • The 3p electron is also slightly further from the nucleus and partly shielded by the 3s electrons.
  • So less energy is needed, even though Al has one more proton.
Quick check
?After the dip, the rise resumes: silicon's first ionisation energy is 787 kJ mol⁻¹, higher than aluminium's 578 kJ mol⁻¹. Why?
Mission 5

P → S: p-orbital repulsion

  • P: [Ne] 3s² 3p³. Three 3p electrons, one in each orbital: [↑ ] [↑ ] [↑ ].
  • S: [Ne] 3s² 3p⁴. One 3p orbital now holds a pair: [↑↓] [↑ ] [↑ ].
  • The paired electrons repel, so one of them is easier to remove.
  • Both electrons come from 3p: this dip is about orbitals, not a new sub-shell.
Quick check
?The first ionisation energies of phosphorus and sulfur are 1012 kJ mol⁻¹ and 1000 kJ mol⁻¹. Which explanation of the lower value for sulfur is correct?
Mission 5

The same pattern in period 2

  • Be → B (900 → 801 kJ mol⁻¹): B's electron comes from 2p, higher in energy than Be's 2s.
  • N → O (1402 → 1314 kJ mol⁻¹): O's electron comes from a paired 2p orbital: p-orbital repulsion.
  • Period 3 repeats this one shell further out: Mg → Al and P → S.
Quick check
?A student sketches first ionisation energy from Na to Ar as a smooth, steady rise. Which correction does the sketch need?
Mission check

?The first ionisation energies of beryllium and boron are 900 kJ mol⁻¹ and 801 kJ mol⁻¹. Which explanation of the lower value for boron is correct?

Same reason as aluminium. Its outer electron is in 3p, higher in energy than magnesium's 3s, so it is easier to remove despite the extra proton.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Z = protons (fixes the element); A = protons + neutrons; ions differ only in electrons.
  • Isotopes: same protons, different neutrons. Aᵣ is their abundance-weighted mean, so it is rarely a whole number.

Mission 2

  • A mass spectrum of an element: one peak per isotope; m/z = isotopic mass; height = abundance.
  • Mean mass = Σ(m/z × height) ÷ Σ(heights).
  • Molecules: M⁺ (highest m/z) gives Mᵣ.

Mission 3

  • Sub-shells s, p, d, f hold 2, 6, 10, 14; orbitals hold two electrons of opposite spin.
  • Fill 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p; singly before pairing. Cr and Cu are exceptions; 4s empties first in ions.

Mission 4

  • First IE: 1 mol of electrons from 1 mol of gaseous atoms: X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻.
  • IE rises across a period, falls down a group. Electrons before the big jump in successive IEs = outer electrons.

Mission 5

  • First IE rises across a period, with dips where a new sub-shell starts (Be→B, Mg→Al) and where p electrons first pair (N→O, P→S).
  • The dips are evidence that shells are split into sub-shells.
