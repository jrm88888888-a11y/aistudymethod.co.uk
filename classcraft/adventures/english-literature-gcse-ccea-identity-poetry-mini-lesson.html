Mission 1

From cursing his fate to scorning kings. What changes his mind?

Make a guess: From cursing his fate to scorning kings. What changes his mind?

Identity poetry · Sonnet 29Screen 1

The sonnet in context

  • Shakespeare's 154 sonnets were printed in 1609 by Thomas Thorpe.
  • Sonnets 1–126 are usually read as addressed to a young man, often called the Fair Youth; Sonnet 29 is one of them.
  • Fortune here means luck and status, and men's eyes means reputation: how others see you.
  • That makes the poem about identity: is he who others think he is, or who he is to one person?

Identity poetry · Sonnet 29Screen 5

Form: one sentence that turns

  • A Shakespearean sonnet: three quatrains and a rhyming couplet, rhymed abab cdcd efef gg, in iambic pentameter (ten syllables, five stresses).
  • The volta (turn) comes at line 9 ("Yet") and line 10 ("Haply"): despair turns to joy.
  • The whole poem is one long sentence, so the change happens inside a single train of thought.
  • State ends lines 2 and 10 and returns in line 14: his "outcast state" becomes a state he won't trade with kings.

Mission checkScreen 8

?From cursing his fate to scorning kings. What changes his mind?

Nothing in the speaker's life changes. At his lowest he happens to think of the beloved, and remembering that love makes him feel richer than a king. The sonnet's turn at lines 9–10 shows identity resting on one person's love rather than on fortune and "men's eyes".

Mission 2

A calm sea on a moonlit night. Why does he hear only sadness?

Make a guess: A calm sea on a moonlit night. Why does he hear only sadness?

Identity poetry · Dover BeachScreen 1

The poem in context

  • Matthew Arnold probably wrote "Dover Beach" in 1851, on his honeymoon; it was published in New Poems in 1867.
  • The speaker looks across the Strait of Dover towards France.
  • Many Victorians felt religious faith weakening as new science, such as geology and later Darwin's On the Origin of Species (1859), challenged literal readings of the Bible.
  • This helps explain why a lovely view leads the speaker to doubt and loss.

Four stanzas, one movement

Mission checkScreen 9

?A calm sea on a moonlit night. Why does he hear only sadness?

The tide draws back over the pebbles, and the speaker hears it as faith withdrawing from the world. Without that certainty the world has "neither joy, nor love, nor light", so he turns to the one thing left: being "true / To one another".

Mission 3

In a hospital bed, he calls himself master of his fate. How?

Make a guess: In a hospital bed, he calls himself master of his fate. How?

Identity poetry · InvictusScreen 1

The poem in context

  • W. E. Henley had tuberculosis of the bone. As a teenager he lost his left leg below the knee.
  • In 1873 he went to the infirmary in Edinburgh, where the surgeon Joseph Lister saved his other leg. He wrote the poem there; his Poems date it 1875.
  • It was first printed, untitled, in A Book of Verses (1888). An editor, Arthur Quiller-Couch, gave it the title Invictus (Latin: unconquered) in The Oxford Book of English Verse (1900).

Identity poetry · InvictusScreen 3

Four stanzas, one stance

StanzaWhat the speaker says
1In total darkness he thanks "whatever gods may be" for a soul that can't be conquered.
2Gripped and beaten by bad luck, he hasn't flinched or cried out: bloody but unbowed.
3Ahead lies only death ("the shade"), yet the years' threats find him unafraid.
4However hard the judgement, he rules his fate and steers his soul.
  • Every stanza sets something terrible against his refusal to give in.

Identity poetry · InvictusScreen 5

Form: a steady beat

  • Four quatrains, rhymed abab, in iambic tetrameter: eight syllables, four stresses (da-DUM × 4).
  • The first two lines open on a stress ("OUT of the NIGHT", "BLACK as the PIT") before the iambic beat takes over, like a jolt then control.
  • Most lines are end-stopped, so each statement lands firmly.
  • The last two lines repeat "I am the" (anaphora), ending on two firm claims.

Identity poetry · InvictusScreen 8

Links in your cluster

  • Unit 2 Section B: compare two poems on a key term, often a named poem and one you choose. Henley's illness is useful context (AO4) if you link it to the poem.
  • Sonnet 29 (Shakespeare): another speaker cursing his "fate"; love, not his own will, rescues him.
  • "Dover Beach" (Arnold): faith withdraws; Arnold turns to love, Henley to the self.
  • 'Prayer Before Birth' (MacNeice, in copyright): a voice that feels powerless before the world, a contrast to Henley's defiance.
Mission checkScreen 9

?In a hospital bed, he calls himself master of his fate. How?

Henley can't control his illness or death, and the poem admits it ("My head is bloody"). What he claims to rule is his response: his soul. Whether that sounds heroic or defiant to the point of pride is a fair debate, but the poem's "fate" is the fate of his spirit, not his body.

Mission 4

A woman sings at a piano. Why does a grown man end up in tears?

Make a guess: A woman sings at a piano. Why does a grown man end up in tears?

Anthology poetry · PianoScreen 1

The poem in context

  • D. H. Lawrence (1885–1930) grew up in Eastwood, Nottinghamshire, the son of a miner.
  • He was very close to his mother, Lydia, who died in 1910.
  • "Piano" was published in New Poems (1918). An earlier draft of the poem was written before his mother's death.
  • This helps explain why a memory of a mother at the piano carries such strong feeling (AO4).

Anthology poetry · PianoScreen 5

Form: couplets and long lines

  • Three quatrains, each made of two rhyming couplets (aabb): me / see, strings / sings, song / belong.
  • The lines are long and loose, with no regular metre, like memories flowing on.
  • Paired rhymes link present and past: clamour / glamour sets the new singer's loudness against the spell of childhood.
  • Enjambment in stanza 3 ("my manhood is cast / Down") makes the line drop, as his adult self does.

?A woman sings at a piano. Why does a grown man end up in tears?

The singer's song pulls the speaker back, against his will, to Sunday evenings as a child at his mother's feet. The memory is so vivid that his "manhood is cast / Down", and he weeps for a past he can't return to.

Mission 5

Two war poems, a century apart. How do you compare, not list?

Make a guess: Two war poems, a century apart. How do you compare, not list?

Poetry skills · comparing poemsScreen 1

One argument, two poems

  • A comparison is one argument about both poems, not two essays joined by "on the other hand".
  • Compare what the poems say (ideas, attitudes) and how they say it (language, form, structure).
  • Find a link first, then the differences within it.
  • Keep both poems in play in each paragraph, using whereas, similarly, in contrast, both.

Mission checkScreen 9

Start from a link (here: both armies are destroyed by something other than battle), then let the differences drive the argument: Byron's purposeful, galloping destruction against Owen's slow, pointless waiting. Keep both poems in every paragraph, comparing ideas and methods together.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Sonnet 29 (printed 1609) moves from despair (lines 1–8) to joy (lines 9–14) in one sentence, turning at "Yet" and "Haply".
  • The change is inward: remembering love makes him "scorn to change my state with kings".
  • Key methods: the Shakespearean sonnet's volta, the repeated word "state", and the lark simile that rises from "sullen earth" to "heaven's gate".
  • "Dover Beach" (probably 1851, published 1867) moves from a calm view to a sad sound, then to Sophocles, the "sea of faith", and a final appeal to love.
  • The extended sea image links the ebbing tide to the Victorian sense that religious faith was weakening.
  • The irregular stanzas and the dark final simile ("ignorant armies clash by night") leave the reader with uncertainty, not comfort.
  • Written in an Edinburgh infirmary (dated 1875), printed untitled in 1888, titled "Invictus" (unconquered) in 1900.
  • Each stanza sets suffering ("bludgeonings of chance") against refusal ("unbowed", "unafraid").
  • Firm quatrains in iambic tetrameter and the repeated metaphors "master" and "captain" make the ending a statement of control over the self.
  • "Piano" (New Poems, 1918) moves from a present-day song to a childhood memory, three times, until the past takes over.
  • The speaker resists ("In spite of myself", "insidious", "Betrays") but is overwhelmed; the simile "like a child" undoes his adult self.
  • Long, flowing lines in rhyming couplets and sound words (boom, tingling, tinkling, clamour) make the memory physical.
  • A comparison is one argument about both poems: find a link, then explore the differences within it.
  • Compare ideas and methods together (voice, metre, rhyme, imagery), using comparative connectives in every paragraph.
  • Your exam pairs a named poem with a second poem (one you choose, or an unseen one): know which, and write about both.

Key quotations to learn

  • When in disgrace with fortune and men’s eyes (line 1) Identity measured by luck and reputation.
  • I all alone beweep my outcast state, (line 2) Isolation; "state" begins its journey.
  • And trouble deaf heaven with my bootless cries, (line 3) Even heaven seems to reject him.
  • Haply I think on thee, and then my state, (line 10) The turn: a chance thought of the beloved.
  • Like to the lark at break of day arising (line 11) Simile: his mood rises like a lark at dawn.
  • That then I scorn to change my state with kings. (line 14) Love outweighs status: the final reversal.
  • The sea is calm to-night. (stanza 1, line 1) The calm the poem then undoes.
  • The eternal note of sadness in. (stanza 1, line 14) The sound becomes a timeless sadness.
  • The sea of faith (stanza 3, line 21) The central metaphor.
  • Its melancholy, long, withdrawing roar, (stanza 3, line 25) Faith ebbing slowly; long vowels drag out the loss.
  • Ah, love, let us be true / To one another! (stanza 4, lines 29–30) Love as the only answer left.
  • Where ignorant armies clash by night. (stanza 4, line 37) A confused, faithless world.
  • Out of the night that covers me, (stanza 1, line 1) Darkness: the suffering he starts from.
  • I thank whatever gods may be (stanza 1, line 3) Uncertain faith; strength comes from within.
  • For my unconquerable soul. (stanza 1, line 4) Links to the title: unconquered.
  • My head is bloody, but unbowed. (stanza 2, line 4) Pain admitted, defeat refused.
  • Finds, and shall find, me unafraid. (stanza 3, line 4) Defiance of the future and death.
  • I am the master of my fate: (stanza 4, line 3) Metaphor of control over his response.
  • Softly, in the dusk, a woman is singing to me; (stanza 1, line 1) The gentle present that triggers memory.
  • A child sitting under the piano, in the boom of the tingling strings (stanza 1, line 3) Vivid, physical memory.
  • In spite of myself, the insidious mastery of song (stanza 2, line 1) Memory as a sly power he resists.
  • And hymns in the cosy parlour, the tinkling piano our guide. (stanza 2, line 4) Warm, safe childhood home.
  • my manhood is cast / Down in the flood of remembrance (stanza 3, lines 3–4) The adult self overwhelmed.
  • I weep like a child for the past. (stanza 3, line 4) The simile that ends the poem.
