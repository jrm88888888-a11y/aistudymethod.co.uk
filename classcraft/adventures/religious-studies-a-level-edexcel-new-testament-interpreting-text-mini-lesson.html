Mission 1

What is the Synoptic Problem?

  • Matthew, Mark and Luke are the Synoptic Gospels: they can be "seen together" (Greek synopsis) in parallel columns.
  • They share much of the same content, often in the same order, sometimes in the same wording in Greek.
  • Yet each also differs: material found in only one, changes of wording, order and emphasis.
  • The Synoptic Problem: what literary relationship explains both the agreements and the differences?
  • Source criticism studies the written sources behind a text.
  • John is not counted: it has a different order, style and much different content.
Mission 1Read the source

An aside in three Gospels

> "But that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins"—he said to the paralytic— (Mark 2:10, WEBBE)

> "But that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins—" (then he said to the paralytic) (Matthew 9:6, WEBBE)

> "But that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins," he said to the paralysed man (Luke 5:24, WEBBE)

  • All three break off Jesus' sentence at the same point with the same narrator's aside.

Q1 Why is the shared aside hard to explain by shared memory alone?

Show answer

An aside is the writer's own construction; independent writers would hardly break the sentence identically.

Q2 What does it suggest?

Show answer

A literary link: one writer copied another, or all used a common written source.

Mission 1

Triple, double, special

  • Triple tradition: in all three (e.g. the healing of the paralysed man, the feeding of the five thousand). Streeter: about 90% of Mark's content is in Matthew, over half in Luke.
  • Double tradition: in Matthew and Luke but not Mark: about 220–235 verses, mostly sayings (the Lord's Prayer, the Beatitudes, John the Baptist's preaching).
  • Special material: only in one:
  • M (Matthew): the magi, parable of the sheep and goats.
  • L (Luke): the shepherds, the Good Samaritan, the Lost Son.
  • Mark has very little of his own (e.g. the blind man of Bethsaida, 8:22–26).

Mission 1

Why Mark first?

  • Content: almost all of Mark is in Matthew or Luke. Why would a later writer shorten Matthew and leave out the birth, the Sermon on the Mount and the Lord's Prayer?
  • Order: Matthew and Luke follow Mark's order; where one departs, the other usually keeps it. They rarely agree together against Mark.
  • Wording: Matthew and Luke often agree with Mark, seldom with each other against him.
  • Style: Mark's rougher Greek and Aramaic words are smoothed or dropped by the others.
  • Harder readings: "He could do no mighty work there" (Mark 6:5) becomes "He didn't do many mighty works there because of their unbelief" (Matthew 13:58). A writer is more likely to soften a difficult phrase than to create one.
  • Markan priority is held by most scholars today.
Mission 1

Q: the lost sayings source

  • If Matthew and Luke did not copy each other, the double tradition needs a source: Q (German Quelle, "source").
  • Mostly sayings, little narrative; no passion story.
  • Read the source:

> "You offspring of vipers, who warned you to flee from the wrath to come?" (Matthew 3:7, WEBBE)

> "You offspring of vipers, who warned you to flee from the wrath to come?" (Luke 3:7, WEBBE)

  • Identical words, absent from Mark.
  • No manuscript of Q survives. But the Gospel of Thomas (found at Nag Hammadi, 1945) shows that sayings collections existed.
  • Matthew and Luke place Q sayings differently (Matthew gathers them into sermons; Luke scatters them), which suggests independent use.
Mission 1

Two sources, then four

  • Two-source hypothesis: Matthew and Luke each used Mark and Q, independently. Proposed by C. H. Weisse (1838) and H. J. Holtzmann (1863).
  • Four-source hypothesis: B. H. Streeter (1874–1937), The Four Gospels: A Study of Origins (1924): Mark, Q, M and L.
  • Streeter linked each to a church centre: Mark with Rome, Q with Antioch, M with Jerusalem, L with Caesarea.
  • He treated M and L as sources, not just leftover material; many scholars now see some of M and L as the evangelists' own writing or oral tradition.
Mission 1

Proto-Gospels

  • A proto-Gospel is an earlier version or draft behind a Gospel we have.
  • Proto-Luke (Streeter, 1924): Luke first combined Q and L, then inserted blocks of Mark. Evidence: Luke uses Mark in blocks, and his passion narrative often differs from Mark's.
  • Ur-Markus: an earlier form of Mark, suggested to explain the minor agreements (below).
  • Aramaic Matthew: Papias (c. 125), quoted by Eusebius (Church History III.39), says Matthew collected the sayings "in the Hebrew language". Most scholars think our Greek Matthew is not a translation.
  • G. E. Lessing (1778) proposed a single lost Aramaic original gospel behind all three.
Mission 1

Other solutions

  • Augustinian: Matthew, then Mark, then Luke (Augustine, Harmony of the Gospels, c. 400): the traditional order.
  • Griesbach / Two-Gospel hypothesis: Matthew first, Luke used Matthew, Mark combined both (J. J. Griesbach, 1789; revived by W. R. Farmer, The Synoptic Problem, 1964).
  • Farrer hypothesis: Mark first, Matthew used Mark, Luke used both: no Q needed (Austin Farrer, "On Dispensing with Q", 1955; Mark Goodacre, The Case Against Q, 2002).
  • The Farrer case rests on the minor agreements: places where Matthew and Luke agree against Mark in triple-tradition passages, e.g. both add "Who … struck you?" to the mocking (Matthew 26:68; Luke 22:64; not in Mark 14:65).

Mission 1Evaluate

Is the two-source hypothesis the best solution?

  • Claim: The two-source hypothesis is the best explanation of the Synoptic Problem.
  • For: Explains Markan order and wording, Mark's harder readings, and the double tradition.
  • For: Matthew and Luke use Q sayings in different places and forms, which fits independent use.
  • Against: Q is hypothetical: no manuscript and no ancient mention.
  • Against: The minor agreements suggest Luke knew Matthew (Farrer, Goodacre).
  • Against: Literary models may underrate oral tradition: memory and performance could explain some agreements and differences (e.g. James Dunn, Jesus Remembered, 2003).
  • Judgement criteria: Simplicity (fewer lost sources) or fit with all the data? How much weight do the minor agreements bear? Does the answer change how we read each Gospel?
  • Spec link 3-3.1c: the significance of these findings for understanding the texts.

Mission 2

Between Jesus and the Gospels

  • c. AD 30: Jesus' ministry, in Aramaic.
  • c. 30–65: stories and sayings passed on orally in preaching, worship, teaching and debate; perhaps some small written collections.
  • c. 65–90: the Gospels written in Greek, using sources (see Mission 1).
  • Each stage could change the material: selection, shaping, translation, editing.
  • Form criticism studies the oral stage; redaction criticism studies the final editing.
Mission 2

Form criticism

  • German Formgeschichte, "history of forms".
  • K. L. Schmidt (1919): Mark's framework of times and places is mostly editorial; the Gospel is made of separate units (pericopae) strung together.
  • Martin Dibelius, Die Formgeschichte des Evangeliums (1919), and Rudolf Bultmann (1884–1976), The History of the Synoptic Tradition (1921), classified the units by form:
  • Pronouncement stories (Bultmann: apophthegms; Dibelius: paradigms): a short scene leading to a saying.
  • Miracle stories: problem → Jesus acts → proof → reaction.
  • Sayings and parables; legends and historical stories.
  • Each form had a Sitz im Leben, a "setting in life" in the early Church: preaching, worship, controversy, teaching.
  • Bultmann judged that many units were shaped or created by the communities. Vincent Taylor (1933) and others thought eyewitnesses limited this.
Mission 2Read the source

A pronouncement story

> The Pharisees: "Behold, why do they do that which is not lawful on the Sabbath day?" (Mark 2:24, WEBBE)

> "The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath." (Mark 2:27, WEBBE)

  • Mark 2:23–28: disciples pick grain on the Sabbath; Pharisees object; Jesus cites David; the scene ends with the saying.

Q1 Which part of the unit would a form critic see as its point?

Show answer

The final pronouncement on the Sabbath: the narrative exists to frame it.

Q2 What setting in the early Church might have kept this unit alive?

Show answer

Debates with other Jews about keeping the Sabbath.

Mission 2

Translated, edited, transmitted

  • Translated: Jesus taught mainly in Aramaic; the Gospels are Greek. Mark keeps some Aramaic: Talitha cumi (5:41), Abba (14:36). Translation always involves choices.
  • Edited: writers selected, arranged and changed units. Matthew and Luke both drop Mark's mention of Abiathar (Mark 2:26), perhaps because 1 Samuel 21 names Ahimelech as priest.
  • Transmitted: copied by hand for 1,400 years; scribes made variants. Example: Mark's earliest manuscripts end at 16:8 ("they were afraid"); verses 9–20 are a later addition in the view of most scholars.
  • Textual criticism compares manuscripts to reach the earliest wording.

Mission 2

Redaction criticism

  • German Redaktionsgeschichte: the evangelists as editors and theologians, not just collectors.
  • Method: compare a Gospel with its source (usually Mark), and ask why each change was made.
  • Günther Bornkamm, "The Stilling of the Storm in Matthew" (1948): Matthew puts the storm after sayings on following Jesus; "his disciples followed him" into the boat (8:23); Mark's "Teacher, don't you care that we are dying?" (Mark 4:38) becomes a prayer: "Save us, Lord!" (Matthew 8:25). The boat becomes a picture of the Church.
  • Hans Conzelmann, The Theology of St Luke (1954): Luke's Salvation History.
  • Willi Marxsen, Mark the Evangelist (1956), named the method.
  • Limits: depends on knowing the sources (see Mission 1); may read too much into small changes.
  • A third approach, narrative criticism, reads a Gospel in its final form as one story, with plot, characters and setting.
Mission 2Read the source

Peter's confession, three ways

  • Jesus asks, "But who do you say that I am?" (Mark 8:29, WEBBE). Peter answers:
  • Mark 8:29: "You are the Christ."
  • Luke 9:20: "The Christ of God."

> "You are the Christ, the Son of the living God." (Matthew 16:16, WEBBE)

  • Matthew adds "Son of the living God" and Jesus' blessing: "on this rock I will build my assembly" (Matthew 16:18). Focus: Jesus' divine sonship and the Church.
  • Mark follows with Peter rebuking Jesus and being called "Satan" (8:32–33); Luke leaves the rebuke out.

Q1 What does Matthew's addition suggest about his concerns?

Show answer

Jesus' identity as Son of God, and Peter's role in the Church.

Q2 What might Luke's omission of the rebuke show?

Show answer

A gentler portrait of the apostles, who lead the Church in Acts.

Mission 2Diversity

How Christians respond to criticism

  • Catholic: Pius XII, Divino Afflante Spiritu (1943), encouraged study of literary forms; Vatican II, Dei Verbum 12 (1965), asks readers to attend to the writers' "literary forms"; the Pontifical Biblical Commission (1993) called the historical-critical method indispensable, while warning against reducing faith to it.
  • Mainline and liberal Protestants: largely accept source, form and redaction criticism.
  • Conservative evangelicals: use the methods with caution; stress eyewitness memory (e.g. Richard Bauckham, Jesus and the Eyewitnesses, 2006; Birger Gerhardsson, Memory and Manuscript, 1961).
  • Fundamentalists: often reject critical methods as threatening the Bible's inerrancy (see Mission 4).
  • Narrative criticism is widely welcomed across these groups because it reads the text as given.
Mission 2Evaluate

How much do these methods help?

  • Claim: Biblical criticism is essential for understanding the Synoptic Gospels.
  • For: Shows each evangelist's theology (redaction); the life of the early Church (form); the text's history (textual criticism).
  • For: Narrative criticism helps readers see each Gospel's story and its effect, as first hearers would.
  • Against: Form criticism's assumption of creative communities ignores eyewitnesses and the short time span; its "laws" of tradition have been questioned (E. P. Sanders, The Tendencies of the Synoptic Tradition, 1969).
  • Against: Methods can fragment the text and seem to leave little for faith; narrative criticism may ignore history altogether.
  • Judgement criteria: Understanding for whom: historian, preacher, believer? Do the methods complement each other? Can critical study and faith in inspiration coexist?
  • Spec: "the significance of this for understanding the texts" (3-3.1c).

Mission 3: Who wrote the Fourth Gospel, and why does it tell Jesus' story so differently?

Mission 3

A different kind of Gospel

  • Compared with Matthew, Mark and Luke, John has:
  • Long discourses and "I am" sayings, but no parables and no exorcisms.
  • Three Passovers (2:13; 6:4; 11:55), so a ministry of two to three years; much of it in Jerusalem.
  • The Temple cleansing at the start (2:13–22), not in the last week.
  • Seven signs; the raising of Lazarus triggers the plot to kill Jesus.
  • At the Last Supper, foot-washing (13:1–17) instead of words over bread and cup.
  • The crucifixion on the Preparation Day of the Passover (19:14), when the lambs were killed.
  • So: who wrote it, and why?
Mission 3Read the source

The stated purpose

> "but these are written that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that believing you may have life in his name." (John 20:31, WEBBE)

  • Three aims in one verse: Jesus is the Christ; Jesus is the Son of God; life in his name.
  • Ancient manuscripts differ on one letter of "believe":
  • pisteusēte ("come to believe"): written to convert outsiders.
  • pisteuēte ("go on believing"): written to strengthen believers.

Q1 Which version suits the view that John is a missionary Gospel?

Show answer

Pisteusēte, "come to believe".

Q2 Why can the verse not settle the debate alone?

Show answer

The manuscripts differ, and the same words could encourage either kind of reader.

Mission 3

To convert Jews and Gentiles?

  • For Jews: the Gospel argues from Moses and the scriptures (5:46); it shows Jesus as the Messiah (1:41) and the fulfilment of Israel's feasts (Sabbath, Passover, Booths, Dedication).
  • For Gentiles: Jewish words are translated: "'Rabbi' (which is to say, being interpreted, Teacher)" (1:38); "Messiah … Christ" (1:41); customs are explained (4:9).
  • Greeks come asking to see Jesus (12:20–21); "I … will draw all people to myself" (12:32); Jesus dies to gather "the children of God who are scattered abroad" (11:52).
  • Dodd (The Interpretation of the Fourth Gospel, 1953), paraphrase: written for thoughtful non-Christians in a Hellenistic city such as Ephesus: people seeking eternal life and knowledge of God.
  • But much of the Gospel assumes readers who already know the story and the terms (e.g. 11:2 refers to an anointing not yet told). This suits believers.
Mission 3

A "spiritual Gospel" fulfilling scripture

  • Clement of Alexandria (c. 150–c. 215), quoted by Eusebius (Ecclesiastical History 6.14.7, c. 325), paraphrase: John wrote last; knowing the others had recorded the outward facts, he composed, moved by the Spirit, "a spiritual Gospel".
  • Meaning: John draws out the inner meaning of Jesus' words and works, not that he invented them.
  • Fulfilment of scripture runs through the Gospel: "that the Scripture might be fulfilled" (13:18; 15:25; 19:24, 28, 36).
  • Example: "A bone of him will not be broken" (John 19:36): Jesus dies as the true Passover lamb (Exodus 12:46), as the Baptist said: "Behold, the Lamb of God" (1:29).
  • Purpose: to show that Jesus completes what Israel's scriptures promised.

Mission 3

Other purposes scholars detect

  • R. E. Brown (1928–1998), The Gospel according to John (1966–70), paraphrase: the Gospel has several purposes, reflecting its community's history:
  • The synagogue: believers are "put out of the synagogue" (9:22; 12:42; 16:2): written for Jewish Christians in conflict with synagogue leaders, and to urge secret believers to declare themselves.
  • Followers of John the Baptist: the Baptist insists, "I am not the Christ" (1:20) and "He must increase, but I must decrease" (3:30).
  • False teaching: against those who denied Jesus was truly human: "The Word became flesh" (1:14); blood and water from his side (19:34).
  • Encouragement: to strengthen believers facing hostility (the farewell discourses, 14–17).
  • J. Louis Martyn (1968) read John as a "two-level drama": Jesus' story and the community's story told together.
Mission 3

Clues inside the text

  • The author is never named. The Gospel points to "the disciple whom Jesus loved", the Beloved Disciple:
  • Leans on Jesus' chest at the supper (13:23); at the cross with Jesus' mother (19:26); first to believe at the empty tomb (20:2–8); recognises the risen Lord (21:7).
  • "He who has seen has testified, and his testimony is true" (19:35): an eyewitness.
  • "This is the disciple who testifies about these things, and wrote these things. We know that his witness is true." (21:24)
  • Note the "we": others vouch for him. Chapter 21 looks like an addition by his community after his death (21:22–23).
  • B. F. Westcott (1825–1901), The Gospel according to St John (1881): the author was a Jew, of Palestine, an eyewitness, an apostle, and so John son of Zebedee.
  • But John son of Zebedee is never named in this Gospel (only "the sons of Zebedee", 21:2).
Mission 3

Clues outside the text

  • Irenaeus (c. 130–c. 202), Against Heresies 3.1.1 (c. 180), paraphrase: John, the disciple of the Lord who had leaned on his breast, published his Gospel while living at Ephesus.
  • Irenaeus said he had heard Polycarp, who had known John: a chain of memory.
  • Papias (c. 60–c. 130), quoted by Eusebius (Ecclesiastical History 3.39.4): he lists "John" among the apostles and also "the elder John". Eusebius concluded there were two Johns.
  • Papias is known only in fragments, and Irenaeus wrote about a century after the events.
  • Date: most scholars date the Gospel c. 90–110. A papyrus fragment of John 18 (P52, Manchester) is usually dated to the 2nd century, showing the Gospel circulated in Egypt early.
Mission 3Diversity

Four views of authorship

  • 1. John son of Zebedee, the apostle: church tradition (Irenaeus), Westcott; the Beloved Disciple is John.
  • 2. Another eyewitness disciple: the Beloved Disciple was not one of the Twelve. Suggestions: John the Elder (Papias); Lazarus, the one Jesus "loved" (11:3, 5); the unnamed disciple "known to the high priest" (18:15).
  • 3. A community over time: Brown first (1966) linked the Beloved Disciple with John son of Zebedee, but later (The Community of the Beloved Disciple, 1979) argued he was a disciple from outside the Twelve. The Gospel grew in stages: his witness, an evangelist, a final editor.
  • 4. Independent early tradition, author uncertain: C. H. Dodd, Historical Tradition in the Fourth Gospel (1963): John uses early tradition independent of the Synoptics, with accurate knowledge of Palestine; Dodd left the author's name open.
  • Most scholars today hold some form of view 3, with the Beloved Disciple as the source of the tradition.

Mission 3Evaluate

Was the author John son of Zebedee?

  • Claim: The Fourth Gospel was written by John son of Zebedee.
  • For: Early and consistent tradition (Irenaeus, via Polycarp); the Gospel claims eyewitness testimony (19:35; 21:24).
  • For: Accurate detail of Jerusalem and Jewish feasts; Westcott's argument from the inside out.
  • Against: He is never named; the Beloved Disciple first appears late in the story (ch. 13), mostly in Jerusalem, while John son of Zebedee was a Galilean fisherman.
  • Against: Key Synoptic scenes with John (the Transfiguration; Jairus' daughter; Gethsemane's inner circle) are missing.
  • Against: 21:24 implies others completed the work; Papias suggests a second John.
  • Judgement criteria: How much weight should 2nd-century tradition carry? Can internal clues identify a person? Does authorship matter for the Gospel's authority?
Mission 3Evaluate

One purpose or several?

  • Claim: The Fourth Gospel was written mainly to convert unbelievers.
  • For: 20:31 ("that you may believe", in the reading pisteusēte); explanations of Jewish words for outsiders; Dodd's Hellenistic readers.
  • Against: The farewell discourses (14–17) are addressed to disciples; much assumes insider knowledge.
  • Against: Brown: several purposes at once (synagogue conflict, Baptist followers, false teaching, encouragement).
  • Spiritual Gospel vs history: Clement's label could suggest a theological rather than historical aim; but Dodd showed much reliable tradition, and John claims eyewitness truth.
  • Judgement criteria: What do the text's addressees suggest? How strong is a purpose read from a single verse? Can a Gospel be both evangelistic and pastoral?
Mission 3

Why it matters for readers

  • Individuals: if John is an eyewitness account, readers may trust its details; if it is a spiritual Gospel, they read it for meaning and encounter with Christ. Many Christians hold both.
  • John is the Gospel most read for personal faith: "that believing you may have life" (20:31); "God so loved the world" (3:16).
  • Communities: churches use John for doctrine (incarnation, Trinity) and for worship (Holy Week readings; the Prologue at Christmas).
  • The synagogue conflict matters today: if harsh words about "the Jews" reflect a 1st-century family quarrel, readers must not use them against Jewish people now.
  • For historians, authorship affects how the Gospel is weighed alongside the Synoptics in reconstructing Jesus' life.

The Fourth Gospel claims eyewitness roots and a clear aim, but scholars still weigh who shaped it and for whom. A later mission: the conflict that led to Jesus' death.

Mission 4

Inspired, but still to be interpreted

  • "Every Scripture is God-breathed and profitable for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for instruction in righteousness" (2 Timothy 3:16).
  • Christians agree scripture is inspired; they differ on how God inspired it and what it means.
  • Why interpretation is needed: many genres (law, poetry, history, parable, letter, apocalypse); ancient languages; distance in time and culture.
  • Hermeneutics: the study of interpretation.
  • Every reading answers three questions: what did it mean then? What does it mean now? Who has authority to decide (the reader, the Church, the scholar)?
Mission 4

Literal, allegorical, moral

  • Literal sense: what the words and their human author mean, including metaphor and poetry. Literal is not the same as literalistic.
  • Allegorical sense: persons and events stand for deeper truths, often about Christ and the Church. Paul says of Hagar and Sarah, "These things contain an allegory" (Galatians 4:24).
  • Origen of Alexandria (c. 185–c. 253), On First Principles IV: scripture has a body, soul and spirit, like a human being.
  • Moral (tropological) sense: what the text teaches about how to live.
  • A fourth, the anagogical sense (about the life to come), became standard in medieval teaching. John Cassian (c. 360–435) illustrated all four with Jerusalem: a city (literal); the Church (allegorical); the soul (moral); the heavenly city (anagogical).
  • Context: the Alexandrian school favoured allegory; the Antiochene school stressed the literal-historical sense. At the Reformation, Luther and Calvin pressed for the plain sense.
Mission 4Read the source

Aquinas on the senses

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274), Summa Theologica I, q. 1, a. 10 (English Dominican translation, 1920s):

> "That signification whereby things signified by words have themselves also a signification is called the spiritual sense, which is based on the literal, and presupposes it."

  • Aquinas divides the spiritual sense into allegorical, moral and anagogical.

Q1 Which sense does Aquinas make the foundation of the others?

Show answer

The literal sense: the spiritual senses are "based on" it.

Q2 What is the strength of this rule?

Show answer

It limits fanciful allegory: Aquinas adds that arguments can be drawn from the literal sense alone, Reply Obj. 1.

Mission 4

Barth: the Word of God

  • Karl Barth (1886–1968), Swiss Reformed theologian; The Epistle to the Romans (1919; 2nd ed. 1922); Church Dogmatics I/1 (1932).
  • Context: a reaction against liberal theology after liberal leaders backed the First World War. Barth's theme: God speaks; humans do not reach God by their own reason or experience.
  • The Word of God in threefold form:
  • Revealed: Jesus Christ, the Word himself.
  • Written: scripture, the witness to that revelation.
  • Proclaimed: the Church's preaching.
  • The Bible is a human witness that becomes the Word of God when God speaks through it.
  • Strengths: keeps Christ at the centre; accepts biblical criticism without losing authority.
  • Weaknesses: How do we know when the Bible "becomes" God's Word? (subjectivity); fundamentalists say it is God's Word; liberals find it too dogmatic.
Mission 4

Bultmann: demythologising

  • Rudolf Bultmann (1884–1976), "New Testament and Mythology" (1941).
  • The New Testament's world-picture is mythical: a three-storey universe, demons, miracles. Modern people, Bultmann argued, cannot honestly hold it (paraphrase).
  • Demythologising does not delete the myths: it interprets them to find the kerygma (proclamation) inside.
  • He used existentialist philosophy (Martin Heidegger, Being and Time, 1927): the gospel calls each person to authentic existence, in faith and openness to God's future.
  • Example: the resurrection expresses the rise of Easter faith and the meaning of the cross, not an event a historian could check.
  • Strengths: makes the message heard by modern people; takes science and history seriously.
  • Weaknesses: assumes the modern worldview is final; loses the history that faith claims (N. T. Wright); Barth thought it reduced theology to human self-understanding.

Mission 4

Four modern approaches

  • After the Enlightenment (17th–18th centuries), scholars read the Bible "like any other book":
  • Rational: reason judges what is credible. Spinoza, Theological-Political Treatise (1670); D. F. Strauss, The Life of Jesus (1835): gospel stories as myth. Strength: honest questions. Weakness: may rule out the supernatural in advance.
  • Historical: what the text meant in its original setting; Albert Schweitzer, The Quest of the Historical Jesus (1906). Strength: guards against reading our ideas into texts. Weakness: leaves the text in the past; results often uncertain.
  • Sociological: the social world of texts and communities. Gerd Theissen, Sociology of Early Palestinian Christianity (1977): wandering charismatic preachers. Also liberation and feminist readings. Strength: hears overlooked voices. Weakness: may reduce faith to social forces.
  • Literary: the text as literature: genre, story, rhetoric. Hans Frei, The Eclipse of Biblical Narrative (1974); narrative criticism (see Mission 2). Strength: reads the text as a whole, as given. Weakness: may bracket the question of truth.
Mission 4Diversity

Fundamentalism and liberalism

  • Fundamentalism: from The Fundamentals (1910–15), a reaction to liberal theology and criticism.
  • Scripture is inerrant (without error) in all it affirms, in the original manuscripts (Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy, 1978); many read history and creation accounts literally.
  • Strength: clear authority; takes the text as God's word. Weakness: hard to square with differences between texts (e.g. the birth narratives) and with science.
  • Liberalism: Friedrich Schleiermacher (1768–1834), On Religion (1799) and The Christian Faith (1821–22): religion rests on the feeling of absolute dependence on God; Adolf von Harnack, What Is Christianity? (1900): the kernel of Jesus' teaching within the husk of ancient forms.
  • The Bible is a human witness to God, culturally shaped; its message is reinterpreted for each age.
  • Strength: open to science and criticism. Weakness: who decides which parts are "husk"?
  • Between: Catholic teaching (Dei Verbum 11, 1965) holds that scripture teaches without error the truth God wished recorded for our salvation; many evangelicals hold infallibility in faith and morals; Barth.

Mission 4Evaluate

Which model of interpretation is best?

  • Claim: A historical-critical approach is the best model for interpreting the Bible today.
  • For: Grounds meaning in what authors intended; limits arbitrary readings; open to all, believers or not.
  • For: Accepted in principle by Catholic and mainline Protestant teaching.
  • Against: Pre-modern senses (allegorical, moral) let the Church read scripture as a whole and for life; Aquinas kept them on a literal base.
  • Against: Barth: the Bible's subject is God speaking, which history alone cannot reach; fundamentalists: criticism undermines authority.
  • Against: Literary and sociological readings show what historical study misses: story, power, the poor.
  • Judgement criteria: Best for what purpose (scholarship, worship, ethics)? Does a model respect both the text's human history and believers' claim that God speaks through it? Can models be combined?
  • Spec: "contexts, strengths and weaknesses" and "relevance for the modern world" (3-4.1b); "models of interpretation" (4B-2.1c).
Mission 4Evaluate

Sacred text in any age

  • Claim: The Bible remains a living authority for believers in every age.
  • For: Believers experience scripture as God's address: "Your word is a lamp to my feet, and a light for my path." (Psalm 119:105) It shapes worship, prayer, ethics.
  • For: Interpretation lets a fixed text speak to new questions (e.g. medical ethics, ecology).
  • Against: Ancient texts carry ancient views (e.g. on slavery, women's roles) that many believers now reject; selective reading may undermine authority.
  • Against: In a secular, multi-faith society the Bible is no longer a shared reference; many read it as literature or history.
  • Judgement criteria: Does authority depend on agreement about interpretation? Is a text "living" if it must be reinterpreted? Authority for whom: Church, individual, society?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The Synoptic Problem asks what literary relationship explains the agreements and differences of Matthew, Mark and Luke; source criticism studies their sources.
  • Most scholars hold Markan priority; the two-source hypothesis adds Q, and Streeter's four-source hypothesis adds M and L (with proto-Luke).
  • Alternatives: Augustinian, Griesbach (Two-Gospel) and Farrer (no Q, based on the minor agreements).

Mission 2

  • Form criticism (Schmidt, Dibelius, Bultmann) classifies oral units (pronouncement stories, miracle stories, sayings, parables, legends) and their Sitz im Leben; texts were also translated from Aramaic, edited and copied with variants.
  • Redaction criticism (Bornkamm, Conzelmann, Marxsen) treats the evangelists as theologians, shown by how they change their sources.

Mission 3

  • Stated purpose (20:31): that readers believe Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and have life in his name; manuscripts differ on "come to believe" or "go on believing".
  • Other purposes: a "spiritual Gospel" (Clement), fulfilment of scripture, outreach to Jews and Gentiles (Dodd's Hellenistic readers), and Brown's several community purposes.
  • Authorship: tradition says John son of Zebedee (Irenaeus, Westcott); the text points to the Beloved Disciple; Brown's community view; Dodd's independent early tradition.

Mission 4

  • Christians agree scripture is inspired but differ on how to read it: literal, allegorical and moral senses (Origen, Cassian, Aquinas: the literal as foundation).
  • Barth: the Bible is a human witness that becomes the Word of God; Bultmann: demythologise to hear the kerygma; modern rational, historical, sociological and literary approaches each have strengths and weaknesses.
  • Fundamentalism (inerrancy) and liberalism (human, culturally shaped witness) mark the ends of a range; the role of sacred text in any age is debated.
