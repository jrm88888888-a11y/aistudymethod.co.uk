Mission 1

Mission 1: Nobody designs a drug-resistant germ, so how do whole populations of them appear so quickly?

Make a guess: Nobody designs a drug-resistant germ, so how do whole populations of them appear so quickly?

Mission 1

How a resistant population arises

  • (1) A random mutation gives a few bacteria an allele for resistance, before any antibiotic is used. Bacteria can also gain resistance genes on plasmids from other bacteria.
  • (2) The antibiotic kills susceptible bacteria. Resistant ones survive.
  • (3) Survivors reproduce fast (binary fission) and pass on the resistance allele.
  • (4) Over generations the frequency of the allele rises: the population is now mostly resistant.
  • One extreme is favoured: directional selection (see Mission 2).
  • Say: "the antibiotic selected resistant bacteria" — not: "the bacteria became resistant".
Mission 1

Read the data: was resistance there first?

Lederberg and Lederberg (1952): replica plating with the bacterium E. coli.

  • (1) Bacteria spread on a master plate with no antibiotic. Each cell grows into a colony.
  • (2) A sterile velvet pad picks up cells from every colony and presses them onto plates with the antibiotic, keeping their positions.
  • (3) A few colonies grew on the antibiotic plates, at the same positions on every replica.
  • Cells taken from those positions on the master plate, never exposed to the drug, already included resistant cells.
  • So resistance arose before exposure. The drug selected it; it did not cause it.
Quick check
?In a replica-plating test, colonies grow on the antibiotic plate only at the positions of colonies 4 and 11 on the master plate, which has no antibiotic. Cells are then taken from colony 4 on the master plate and placed in a broth containing the antibiotic. What result is expected, and why?
Mission 1

Many drugs, many pests

  • Resistance to a drug usually depends on a specific gene or mutation. Strains can accumulate several: multiresistant bacteria, e.g. MRSA.
  • Every use of an antibiotic is a selection pressure. More use, more selection.
Quick check
?A hospital ward uses one particular antibiotic heavily. Over several years, an increasing proportion of the bacteria isolated from patients on the ward are found to be resistant to it. Which statement correctly explains this change?
Mission 1

Read the data: owls and snow

Tawny owls (Strix aluco) in Finland, studied 1981–2008 (Karell and colleagues, 2011).

  • Two colour forms: grey and brown. Colour is strongly inherited (about 80% heritable).
  • In winters with deep snow, brown owls survived less well than grey owls.
  • Over the study, snowy winters became less common as the climate warmed.
  • The proportion of brown owls rose.
  • Reading: less snow → brown owls' fitness rose → alleles for brown plumage rose in frequency. Evolution driven by climate change.
Quick check
?In Finland, tawny owls (Strix aluco) occur as a grey morph and a brown morph, and the colour is inherited. Brown owls survived snowy winters less well than grey owls. Between 1981 and 2008 winters became milder with less snow, and the proportion of brown owls rose markedly. What best explains the change?
Mission check

?A patient is given an antibiotic. Within a few days, the bacteria in the patient's infection change from almost all susceptible to almost all resistant. Which statement best explains how this happened so quickly?

Germs, insects, owls: the same rule. The allele was there first; the environment decided who left offspring.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why doesn't selection always push a population in the same direction?

Make a guess: Why doesn't selection always push a population in the same direction?

Mission 2

Three shapes of selection

  • Directional: one extreme is favoured. The mean shifts.
  • Stabilising: the intermediate (modal) phenotype is favoured; both extremes are selected against. The mean stays; the range narrows.
  • Disruptive: both extremes are favoured over the intermediate. The distribution can split into two peaks.
  • All of them change allele frequencies. Stabilising selection removes alleles for extreme phenotypes.
  • Say: "stabilising selection still changes allele frequencies" — not: "no change in the mean, so no evolution".
Mission 2

Named examples

  • Directional: antibiotic resistance in bacteria (see Mission 1).
  • Stabilising: human birth mass. In a large study of births at a London hospital, mortality was lowest at intermediate birth masses and higher for very light and very heavy babies (Karn and Penrose, 1951).
  • Disruptive: black-bellied seedcracker finches in Cameroon. Large-billed birds crack hard seeds; small-billed birds handle soft seeds; intermediates do worse at both (Smith, 1993).
  • Disruptive is not "selection after a disruption". It is defined by which phenotypes are favoured.
Quick check
?In a study of human births in the mid-twentieth century, babies of intermediate birth mass had the highest survival, while both very light and very heavy babies had higher mortality. Which type of selection does this show, and why?
Quick check
?Which statement about directional, disruptive and stabilising selection is correct?
Mission check

?In a hypothetical songbird, clutch size (number of eggs) is heritable. Parents with very large clutches cannot feed all their chicks, and many starve; parents with very small clutches raise few young. Over many generations, the mean clutch size stays at 4 eggs, but nests with 2 or 6 eggs become rarer. Which statement is correct?

The mean stayed put, yet the population still evolved. Selection can hold the middle as well as push an extreme.

Mission 3

Mission 3: A desert fox has enormous ears and almost never drinks. How did it end up so well suited to the desert?

Make a guess: A desert fox has enormous ears and almost never drinks. How did it end up so well suited to the desert?

Mission 3

What is an adaptation?

  • An adaptation is an inherited feature that increases an organism's chance of surviving and reproducing in its environment.
  • It arises by natural selection: random variation exists; individuals with a useful allele survive and breed more; the allele becomes more common over generations.
  • Individuals do not adapt in their lifetime. Populations change over generations.
  • An adaptation helps in one environment; the same trait may be useless or harmful in another.
  • Adaptation and selection are major factors in evolution and in the diversity of life.
  • Say: "individuals with the allele survived and bred more" — not: "the fox adapted because it needed to".
Mission 3

Three kinds of adaptation

  • Anatomical: body structure. Fennec fox: huge ears with blood vessels near the surface radiate heat.
  • Physiological: body processes. Fennec fox: kidneys conserve water; it gets most of its water from food.
  • Behavioural: what it does. Fennec fox: active at night; shelters in a burrow by day.
  • Most organisms show all three, each meeting the same challenge.
Quick check
?Which adaptation is classified correctly?
Quick check
?A hypothetical grass species grows both on soil contaminated with zinc around an old mine and in nearby uncontaminated fields. When grown on zinc soil, plants from the mine site survive, but plants from the fields die. When grown on normal soil, plants from the mine site grow more slowly than plants from the fields. Which reasoning best explains why zinc tolerance is common at the mine site but rare in the fields?
Mission check

?Arctic foxes have much smaller ears than fennec foxes. Which statement best explains how small ears became typical of Arctic foxes?

The fennec's big ears and the Arctic fox's small ones arose the same way: selection acting on inherited variation, in different environments.

Mission 4

Mission 4: How do you grow bacteria on purpose without growing anything else, or infecting yourself?

Make a guess: How do you grow bacteria on purpose without growing anything else, or infecting yourself?

Mission 4

What bacteria need

  • Nutrients: a carbon source (e.g. glucose) for respiration and for building molecules.
  • A nitrogen source (e.g. amino acids, nitrate) for proteins and nucleic acids; plus minerals.
  • Water: for reactions and transport into the cell.
  • A suitable temperature: growth depends on enzymes, each with an optimum.
  • A suitable pH: most bacteria grow best near neutral.
Mission 4

Agar or broth

  • Nutrient broth: liquid medium. Growth shows as cloudiness (turbidity).
  • Nutrient agar: broth set with agar, a seaweed polysaccharide most bacteria cannot digest.
  • Agar melts near 85 °C and sets below about 40 °C: poured hot, solid at incubation temperature.
  • On agar, one cell (or a small clump) grows into a visible colony.
  • Spread culture evenly over the agar: colonies merge into a lawn.
Mission 4

Aseptic technique

  • Two aims: keep unwanted microbes out of the culture; keep the culture away from people.
  • Before: wash hands; disinfect the bench; sterilise media and glassware in an autoclave (121 °C, 15 min).
  • During: work beside a lit Bunsen burner: its updraught carries airborne microbes up and away.
  • Flame the inoculating loop until red hot; cool it before touching the culture.
  • Flame the neck of each bottle as you open and close it. Lift the dish lid only slightly, at an angle.
  • After: secure the lid with two short strips of tape, not all round: air can still enter, which discourages anaerobic bacteria (including many pathogens).
  • Label the base. Incubate upside down (condensation cannot drip onto the agar) at 25 °C. School labs avoid 30–42 °C: temperatures near body heat (37 °C) favour human pathogens.
  • Never open a plate after incubation. Autoclave cultures before disposal.
Mission 4

Serial dilution

  • Transfer 1.0 cm³ into 9.0 cm³ of sterile water; mix. Total 10 cm³: a 1 in 10 dilution (10⁻¹).
  • Repeat from the new tube: 10⁻², 10⁻³, 10⁻⁴ …
  • Dilution factor = total volume ÷ volume transferred = 10 ÷ 1 = 10.
  • After k tenfold steps, concentration = original × 10⁻ᵏ. Factors multiply; they never add.
  • A fresh sterile pipette for each transfer: no carry-over between tubes.
  • Uses here: a range of antimicrobial concentrations.
Quick check
?A student adds 1.0 cm³ of a bacterial culture to 9.0 cm³ of sterile water and mixes. She transfers 1.0 cm³ of this into a second 9.0 cm³ of sterile water, and repeats until she has made four dilutions. What fraction of the original concentration of bacteria is in the fourth dilution?
Mission 4Practical

Testing antimicrobials

  • Required practical 6: use of aseptic techniques to investigate the effect of antimicrobial substances on microbial growth.
  • Spread a known volume of broth culture over agar with a sterile spreader: a lawn.
  • Add sterile paper discs soaked in each antibiotic or plant extract, or fill wells cut with a sterile cork borer.
  • Extract in ethanol? Let the solvent evaporate first: ethanol itself kills bacteria.
  • Control: a disc or well with solvent only.
  • Incubate. Measure the clear zone (zone of inhibition) through the base: two diameters at right angles, take the mean.
Quick check
?A student soaks paper discs in garlic, ginger and mint extracts, all made in ethanol, and places them on a lawn of bacteria. Another disc is soaked in ethanol only. Which of these is a controlled variable in her investigation?
Mission 4

Worked example: clear area around a well

  • Clear zone diameter 21 mm; well diameter 6 mm (illustrative data).
  • Step 1: radii. Zone 21 ÷ 2 = 10.5 mm. Well 6 ÷ 2 = 3.0 mm.
  • Step 2: zone area = π × 10.5² = 346 mm².
  • Step 3: well area = π × 3.0² = 28.3 mm².
  • Step 4: clear agar = 346 − 28.3 = 318 mm².
  • Subtract areas, never diameters. Halve before squaring.

?Use the mean diameter for garlic in the table (23 mm; well 6 mm). What area of clear agar surrounds the well, to 3 significant figures?
Mission check

?A student plans to test three disinfectants on a lawn of bacteria in Petri dishes. Which plan for incubating the dishes follows safe aseptic practice?

Aseptic technique works both ways: nothing gets into the culture, and nothing gets out of it.

Mission 5: If two groups of the same animal never meet again, how do they end up as different species?

Make a guess: If two groups of the same animal never meet again, how do they end up as different species?

Mission 5

What counts as a species?

  • Biological species concept: a group of organisms that can interbreed and produce fertile offspring.
  • Horse × donkey → mule. Offspring, yes; fertile, no. So horse and donkey are two species.
  • Say: "can produce fertile offspring" — not: "can produce offspring".
Quick check
?According to the biological species concept, what is a species?
Mission 5

Where the definition struggles

  • Often it cannot be tested:
    • fossils cannot be crossed;
    • populations that never meet cannot show it in the wild.
  • It does not fit organisms that reproduce asexually.
  • Some pairs of species still produce some fertile hybrids (common in plants).
  • So competing species concepts exist: shared traits, DNA, ancestry. None fits every case.
Mission 5

Speciation means splitting

  • Speciation: one species splits into two or more.
  • The key step: populations become reproductively isolated. Gene flow between them stops.
  • Their gene pools then accumulate differences, until members can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring.
  • Usually gradual. Part-way through, "one species or two?" can be an arbitrary call.
  • A lineage that changes over time without splitting has not speciated.
Mission 5

Allopatric speciation, step by step

  • (1) One population. Gene flow keeps its gene pool mixed.
  • (2) A geographical barrier (river, sea, mountains) splits it. Gene flow stops. This is allopatric speciation.
  • (3) Each gene pool now changes on its own: new mutations, different selection pressures, and genetic drift in small populations. Allele frequencies diverge.
  • (4) In time, the two can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring: two species.
  • Say: "isolation stopped gene flow; selection changed allele frequencies" — not: "the river caused mutations" or "they changed in order to adapt".

Mission 5

What keeps species apart

  • Isolating mechanisms stop two gene pools mixing.
  • Before fertilisation (pre-zygotic):
    • geographical: they live in different places;
    • seasonal (temporal): they breed at different times;
    • behavioural: courtship signals differ;
    • morphological (mechanical): reproductive parts or flowers do not fit;
    • gametic: sperm cannot fertilise the other species' eggs.
  • After fertilisation (post-zygotic):
    • the hybrid dies before it can breed;
    • hybrid sterility: the hybrid lives but cannot breed. Mule: 32 horse + 31 donkey chromosomes = 63; the unmatched sets cannot pair properly in meiosis.
Mission 5

Courtship: the right reply or no mating

  • Courtship behaviour is a necessary step before successful mating.
  • It lets an animal:
    • recognise its own species;
    • recognise a mate of the opposite sex that is ready to breed;
    • synchronise mating, so gametes meet;
    • choose a strong, healthy mate.
  • Signals are species-specific: songs, displays, flashes, scents.
  • Example: male fireflies of each Photinus species flash their own pattern; females answer only their own.
  • Wrong signal, no reply, no mating: behavioural isolation.
Quick check
?Two closely related bird species live in the same forest. The males of each species sing a different song, and females respond only to the song of their own species. What is the significance of this for the two species?
Mission check

?A population of lizards was split when rising sea level cut off an island from the mainland. Many generations later, island and mainland lizards can no longer produce fertile offspring together. Which statement about this speciation is correct?

The barrier only stops gene flow. Mutation, selection and drift do the rest, over many generations.

Mission 6: Can a new species appear right beside its parents, or even in a single generation?

Mission 6

Speciation without a barrier

  • Sympatric speciation: populations in the same area become reproductively isolated.
  • No geographical barrier. Gene flow is cut by:
    • timing: they breed at different times (temporal isolation);
    • behaviour: different courtship or mate choice;
    • ecology: they use different foods, hosts or soils;
    • chromosomes: a sudden change in chromosome number.
  • Example: two Howea palm species on tiny Lord Howe Island. They grow on different soils and flower at different times.
Mission 6

Allopatric and sympatric side by side

  • Both need reproductive isolation: gene flow must stop.
  • Both then need the gene pools to diverge until fertile interbreeding fails.
  • Allopatric: a geographical barrier; populations live apart.
  • Sympatric: same area; isolation by timing, behaviour, ecology or chromosomes.
  • Say: "sympatric means without a geographical barrier" — not: "a smaller barrier".
Quick check
?What do allopatric speciation and sympatric speciation have in common?
Mission 6

A new species in one generation

  • (1) Species P (2n = 14) × species Q (2n = 18). Hybrid: 7 + 9 = 16 chromosomes, none with a partner. Sterile.
  • (2) The chromosome number doubles (e.g. a failed cell division): 32. Every chromosome now has a partner. Meiosis works: fertile.
  • (3) Polyploid × P: offspring get 16 + 7 = 23, unpaired. Sterile. The polyploid is isolated from both parents: a new species, formed sympatrically, at once.
  • Polyploid: more than two complete sets of chromosomes. Common in plants (bread wheat has six sets).
  • Illustrative chromosome numbers.
Quick check
?Suppose one Persicaria species has 20 chromosomes in each diploid cell and another has 24. A hybrid forms between them, and in one hybrid plant the chromosome number doubles. Which statement is correct?
Mission 6

One ancestor, many species

  • Adaptive radiation: one ancestral species gives rise to many species, each adapted to a different niche.
  • Fastest when new habitats or vacant niches appear: new islands, or after a mass extinction.
  • Example: Hawaiian honeycreepers. One finch-like colonist gave rise to dozens of species, with beaks for nectar, seeds, insects and grubs.
  • Repeated over long periods, speciation has produced today's great diversity of species.
Quick check
?A single species of finch colonised a remote group of islands that had no other land birds. Its descendants now form many species, each with a beak suited to a different food. Which statement explains this?
Mission check

?Two populations of a fly species live in the same orchard but lay their eggs on different fruit species, which ripen at different times, so the flies rarely mate with each other. Which type of speciation could this lead to, and why?

No mountain or sea is needed. Anything that stops gene flow, even a calendar or a doubled set of chromosomes, can start a new species.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Resistance alleles arise by random mutation (or gene transfer) before exposure; the drug or pesticide kills susceptible individuals, so resistant ones reproduce and the allele's frequency rises.

Mission 2

  • Directional selection favours one extreme and shifts the mean; stabilising selection favours the intermediate and narrows the range; both change allele frequencies.
  • Disruptive selection favours both extremes.

Mission 3

  • An adaptation is an inherited feature, favoured by natural selection, that improves survival and reproduction in a particular environment.
  • Adaptations are anatomical (or morphological), physiological or behavioural.

Mission 4

  • Bacteria need nutrients, water, a suitable temperature and pH; aseptic technique keeps contaminants out and the culture contained.
  • Serial dilution multiplies the dilution factor at each step: four tenfold steps give 10⁻⁴.
  • Antimicrobial tests compare clear zones against a solvent-only control; clear area = zone area − well (or disc) area.

Mission 5

  • A species can interbreed to produce fertile offspring; speciation is one species splitting into two once gene flow between populations stops and their gene pools diverge.
  • Isolating mechanisms (geographical, seasonal, behavioural, morphological, hybrid sterility) keep species' gene pools apart.

Mission 6

  • Sympatric speciation happens in one area when timing, behaviour, ecology or chromosomes stop gene flow; allopatric needs a geographical barrier; both need reproductive isolation, then divergence.
  • Hybridisation plus doubling of the chromosome number can make a fertile, isolated plant species in one generation.
