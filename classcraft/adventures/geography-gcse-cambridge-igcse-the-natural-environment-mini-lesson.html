Mission 1

The drainage basin

  • A drainage basin is the area of land drained by a river and its tributaries.
  • Watershed: the boundary of a basin, usually along high ground. It separates one basin from the next.
  • Source: where the river starts. Mouth: where it ends, in the sea or a lake.
  • Confluence: where two rivers join. A tributary is a smaller river that joins a larger one.
  • Channel: the bed and banks that the river flows between.
Quick check
?Two streams meet and flow on as one river. What is the meeting point called?
Mission 1

The water cycle and the basin

  • The hydrological (water) cycle is a closed system: Earth's water is not gained or lost, only moved between stores.
  • Main stores: oceans hold about 96.5% of Earth's water; ice, groundwater, lakes, soil, the atmosphere and rivers hold the rest.
  • Main transfers: evaporation, condensation, precipitation, and flows over and under the land back to the sea.
  • A drainage basin is an open system inside this cycle: water enters as precipitation and leaves as river flow and evapotranspiration.
Mission 1

Inputs, stores and outputs

  • Input: precipitation (rain, snow, hail).
  • Stores: interception (on leaves), surface water (puddles, lakes), soil moisture, groundwater (in rock), the river channel.
  • Outputs: river discharge at the mouth, and evapotranspiration.
  • Evapotranspiration = evaporation from surfaces + transpiration from plants.
Mission 1

Transfers (flows)

  • Infiltration: water soaks into the soil.
  • Overland flow (surface runoff): water flows over the ground.
  • Throughflow: water flows downslope through the soil.
  • Percolation: water seeps down from the soil into the rock.
  • Groundwater flow: water moves slowly through rock to the river.
  • Channel flow: water flows along the river.
Quick check
?Which transfer usually takes longest to carry rainwater to the river?
Mission 1

How the flows depend on each other

  • Interception slows rain down, so more of it can infiltrate.
  • If rain falls faster than the soil can absorb it, the extra becomes overland flow.
  • If the soil is already full (saturated), new rain cannot infiltrate: it runs off as overland flow.
  • Water that does infiltrate moves as throughflow, or percolates deeper.
  • So: less interception or infiltration → more overland flow → water reaches the river faster.
Quick check
?Which conditions would produce the most overland flow?
Mission 1

Where the fresh water is

  • Only about 2.5% of Earth's water is fresh.
  • Of that fresh water: 68.7% is ice and snow; 30.1% is groundwater.
  • Rivers hold about 0.006%: a tiny store, refilled by much bigger, slower ones.
  • In the upper Indus basin, glacier melt gave 40.6% of total river flow (1998–2007).
Mission 1

Groundwater keeps rivers flowing

  • In a USGS study of 54 US streams, groundwater gave on average 52% of the flow.
  • UK basin: the River Lambourn, Berkshire, drains chalk, a rock that stores water.
  • About 97% of its long-term flow comes from stored water such as groundwater (base flow index 0.97).
  • So in dry weeks, groundwater flow keeps the river running.
Quick check
?River Lambourn, 1962–2019: rainfall averaged 768 mm a year. River flow equalled 235 mm a year spread over the basin. About what share of the rain left as river flow?
Mission check

?In a long dry summer, a water company pumps much more water out of the rock beneath a river basin. What is most likely to happen to the river?

A river is the smallest, fastest store. Between rains it is fed by slow stores: groundwater flow (52% of flow on average in 54 US streams) and, in mountains, meltwater.

Mission 2

Long profile and cross profile

  • Long profile: the river's height from source to mouth. Steep near the source, gentle near the mouth.
  • Cross profile: a slice across the valley and channel.
  • Upper course: steep, narrow V-shaped valley; small, shallow channel.
  • Lower course: wide, flat valley floor; wide, deep channel.

Mission 2

What changes downstream

  • A river's load is the rock and soil it carries.
  • Increase: discharge, width, depth, cross-sectional area, velocity, load amount.
  • Decrease: gradient, bed roughness, load size. Stones become smaller and rounder.
  • Upper Tees: "gravel and rock bed on steep gradient".
  • This pattern is the Bradshaw model. It is a general model: tributaries, rock type and dams break the pattern.
  • Wetted perimeter: the length of bed and banks in contact with the water. Downstream it grows, but cross-sectional area grows faster, so a smaller share of the water touches the bed: less friction, faster flow.
  • Real rivers: velocity "generally tends to increase downstream", with many exceptions.
Quick check
?Use the Tees table. Middleton is 211.2 m high and 37.72 km from the furthest source; Barnard Castle is 133.0 m high and 54.95 km from it. What is the average gradient between them, in metres of drop per km?
Mission 2

Erosion

  • Hydraulic action: the force of water, pushing air into cracks in the bed and banks.
  • Abrasion: the load scrapes and grinds the bed and banks.
  • Attrition: stones hit each other, becoming smaller and rounder.
  • Solution: water dissolves soluble rock, such as limestone.
  • Vertical erosion deepens the channel; lateral erosion widens it.
  • Vertical erosion in the upper course cuts V-shaped valleys and interlocking spurs; pebbles swirled by the water drill potholes in the bed.
Mission 2

Transportation

  • Traction: large boulders roll along the bed.
  • Saltation: smaller stones and sand bounce along the bed.
  • Suspension: fine silt and clay are carried in the water, making it look muddy.
  • Solution: dissolved minerals are carried, invisible.
  • Big boulders "may only be moved during rapid flows under flood conditions".
Quick check
?A river carries sand grains that bounce along its bed. Which process is this?
Mission 2

Why rivers deposit

A river deposits (drops) its load when it loses energy:

  • velocity falls, e.g. on the inside of a bend or where it enters a lake or the sea;
  • discharge falls after a flood or in a dry spell;
  • the water becomes shallow;
  • it has more load than it can carry.
  • The largest, heaviest particles are dropped first.
Mission 2

Why fine grains are hard to pick up

  • One textbook reading of a velocity graph: a 1 mm sand grain is picked up at about 20 cm/s; a 0.01 mm silt grain needs about 60 cm/s. Versions of the graph differ.
  • Fine grains, especially clay, "stick together": they are cohesive.
  • They also lie in a thin layer of slow water against the bed, which shelters them.
  • Once lifted, fine grains stay in suspension even in slow water, so they are deposited last.
Quick check
?A river slows from 30 cm/s to 5 cm/s as it enters a lake. What is most likely?
Mission 2

Processes change downstream

  • Upper course: steep gradient, large angular load: vertical erosion dominates.
  • Middle course: more discharge: lateral erosion on the outside of bends, deposition on the inside.
  • Lower course: most discharge, fine load: deposition dominates, especially in floods and near the mouth.
Quick check
?Which describes the upper course of a river such as the Tees near Middleton?
Mission check

?In a flood, water runs at 30 cm/s past a sandy bar and a bank of firm clay. The sand is being picked up, but the clay bank is barely eroded. Why?

Fine grains are cohesive and sheltered at the bed, so silt needs about 60 cm/s to be picked up, against about 20 cm/s for 1 mm sand. Once lifted, they are the last to be deposited.

Mission 3

V-shaped valleys and interlocking spurs

  • In the upper course the river cuts down: vertical erosion.
  • Weathering and mass movement wear the valley sides back, giving a V-shaped valley.
  • The river winds around ridges of harder rock. Seen from upstream, the ridges overlap: interlocking spurs.
Mission 3

How a waterfall forms

  • A band of hard (resistant) rock lies over soft rock.
  • The soft rock erodes faster, so the hard rock is left overhanging.
  • Falling water and swirling rocks erode a deep plunge pool by hydraulic action and abrasion.
  • The soft rock is undercut; the unsupported overhang collapses.
  • Repeated collapse moves the waterfall back upstream, leaving a steep-sided gorge.
Mission 3

Niagara Falls

  • Hard dolostones and limestones lie over weaker shales and limestones.
  • About 12,000 years ago the river took its present course; since then the falls have moved about 11 km (7 miles) upstream.
  • Recent retreat: about 0.9–1.5 m (3–5 feet) a year over the last 500+ years; now 0.3 m (1 foot) a year, largely because water is taken for hydroelectric power.
Quick check
?Niagara has retreated about 7 miles in about 12,000 years. 1 mile = 1.609 km. What was its average rate of retreat?
Mission 3

A UK waterfall: High Force

  • High Force, on the River Tees, "plunges over the Whin Sill", a hard rock called dolerite.
  • "Below the Whin Sill you can see layers of sandstone and limestone": softer rocks that are undercut.
  • "Torrential meltwaters carved gorges like those at Low Force and High Force".
Mission 3

Rapids and potholes

  • Rapids: where hard and soft bands of rock alternate across the bed, the soft bands erode faster, making a series of small steps with fast, turbulent water.
  • Potholes: round hollows in the bed. Pebbles caught in swirling eddies drill into the rock by abrasion.
  • Both are features of the upper course, where vertical erosion dominates.
Mission 3

Meanders

  • On a bend the fastest water swings to the outside.
  • Outside bank: lateral erosion cuts a steep river cliff.
  • Inside bank: slow water deposits sand and gravel: a gentle slip-off slope, built of a point bar.
  • In cross-section the channel is deep on the outside and shallow on the inside.
Mission 3

Oxbow lakes

  • Erosion on the outside of two bends narrows the neck of land between them.
  • In a flood the river cuts through the neck and takes the shorter route.
  • Deposition seals off the old bend, leaving an oxbow lake.
Quick check
?In a cross-section across a meander, where is the river deepest?
Mission 3

Floodplains and levees

  • Floodplain: the wide, flat valley floor that floods. It is widened as meanders migrate and built up by layers of alluvium (silt) left by floods.
  • When the river floods, water slows sharply as it leaves the channel, so the coarsest sediment is dropped first, beside the channel.
  • Flood after flood, this builds raised banks: natural levees.
  • Finer silt and clay settle further out on the floodplain.
Quick check
?A river floods and spreads across its floodplain. Where is the coarsest sediment deposited?
Mission 3

Deltas

  • A delta forms where a river enters a sea or lake, slows, and deposits sediment faster than waves and tides remove it.
  • "The formation of a delta depends on the interaction between the river flow and sediment supply".
  • The river splits into smaller channels, distributaries, and the land builds outwards.
  • Example: the Mississippi's "bird foot" delta.
Mission check

?An upland river flows over a band of hard sandstone lying on top of soft shale. Over thousands of years its waterfall moves upstream. Which explanation is correct?

Hard rock over soft rock, undercutting in the plunge pool, and collapse move a waterfall upstream. Niagara has moved about 11 km in about 12,000 years, leaving a gorge.

Mission 4

Living near a river

  • Opportunities: water for homes, farms and industry; fertile floodplain soils (silt left by floods); flat land to build on; transport; fishing; hydroelectric power.
  • Hazards: flooding, bank erosion, polluted water and water-borne disease.
  • So people choose to live near rivers, and that puts them in the way of floods.
Mission 4

Why rivers flood: physical causes

  • A flood happens when a river's discharge is more than its channel can hold, so water spills onto land that is usually dry.
  • Intense rain: falls faster than the ground can absorb. Long rain: soaks the ground until it is saturated.
  • Relief: steep slopes send water to the river quickly.
  • Impermeable rock (e.g. clay) stops water soaking in.
Quick check
?Which situation is most likely to cause a river flood without any human cause?
Mission 4

Urbanisation

  • Impermeable surfaces (roofs, roads, car parks) stop rain infiltrating, so more becomes surface runoff.
  • Drains and sewers carry the water straight to the river.
  • Result: shorter lag time, steeper rising limb, higher peak discharge.
  • Building on floodplains also puts more people and property in the way.
Mission 4

Houston, 2017

  • Hurricane Harvey stalled over or near the Texas coast for four days; the highest storm rainfall, in south-east Texas, was about 1,540 mm (60.58 inches).
  • A study in Nature found that the city's growth made the flood response and the rainfall worse.
  • It estimated that urbanisation made such extreme floods in the basins studied about 21 times as likely (25–30 August 2017).
  • One study of one event: strong evidence, but not proof for every city.
Quick check
?A new housing estate is planned upstream of a town. Which design would best stop it raising flood peaks downstream?
Mission 4

Farming, forests and industry

  • Deforestation: fewer leaves to intercept rain and fewer roots to open the soil, so more runoff and soil erosion. Eroded soil can fill river channels.
  • A review by FAO and CIFOR found that forests can reduce flooding from "frequent, low-intensity, short-duration storms".
  • But "forests cannot stop catastrophic large-scale floods": in long rain, forest soils become saturated too.
  • Agriculture: heavy machinery and grazing compact soil; ploughing downslope and field drains speed runoff.
Quick check
?Which statement about forests and floods is best supported by the evidence on the last screen?
Mission 4

Impacts at different scales: Harvey

  • Local, social: 36 direct deaths in Harris County (Houston), out of at least 68 across Texas.
  • Regional, economic: over 300,000 structures flooded across south-eastern Texas.
  • National, economic: damage estimated at $125 billion; flooded oil refineries went offline for days and US petrol prices rose to their highest level in two years.
  • Environmental: floodwater spreads sediment and pollution, and erodes banks and habitats.
Quick check
?Which Harvey impact is national in scale?
Mission check

?Fields upstream of a town are replaced by houses, roads and storm drains that empty into a stream. The same storm now gives a bigger flood. How has the stream's hydrograph changed, and why?

Impermeable surfaces and drains cut infiltration and lag time and raise peak discharge. In Houston, urbanisation also increased the rainfall, making Harvey-scale floods about 21 times as likely.

Mission 5

Two ways to manage floods

  • Hard engineering: building structures to control the river.
  • Soft engineering: working with natural processes, or changing how people use the land and prepare.
  • Hard schemes usually cost more to build and maintain; soft schemes usually cost less but may protect less.
  • A strategy is the overall plan (for example, "give the river more space"). A technique is a method used within it (for example, moving an embankment back).
Mission 5

Hard engineering

  • Dams and reservoirs: store floodwater upstream and release it slowly. Cost: very expensive; they flood land and trap sediment.
  • Straightening (channelisation): water leaves faster. Cost: it can send the flood downstream sooner and harms habitats.
  • Embankments or artificial levees (raised banks) and flood walls: hold more water in the channel. Cost: if they fail or are overtopped, flooding is sudden.
  • Deepening (dredging): more water fits in the channel, but sediment soon refills it.
  • Flood relief channels: carry extra water round a town. Cost: expensive to dig, and they protect only the town they bypass.
Mission 5

A UK flood relief channel: the Jubilee River

  • Built by the Environment Agency and opened in 2002: an 11.6 km man-made channel taking Thames floodwater round Maidenhead, Windsor, Eton and Cookham.
  • Benefit: it reduces flood risk to about 3,000 properties.
  • Limit: it "is not designed to reduce flood risk to communities upstream or downstream".
Quick check
?A council straightens a meandering river through its town. What is the most likely cost?
Mission 5

Soft engineering

  • Flood warnings and preparation (flood plans, flood doors, moving valuables upstairs): cheap; they save lives and some property, but the water still comes.
  • Flood plain zoning (land-use zoning): keep houses off the riskiest land; use it for fields, parks or playing fields. Hard where towns are already built.
  • Planting trees (afforestation): trees intercept rain and soils absorb more. Cost: they take years to grow and the land is lost to farming. Most useful for smaller floods: forests "cannot stop catastrophic large-scale floods".
  • Washlands: parts of the floodplain left to flood on purpose, storing water. Cost: that land floods, so it cannot be built on or farmed all year.
  • River restoration: return a straightened river to a natural course, so it floods safe land and slows down. Cost: land beside the river floods more often.
Mission 5

Flood warnings in England

  • Three levels from the Environment Agency:
  • Flood alert: "flooding is possible so you should prepare now".
  • Flood warning: "flooding is expected so you should act now".
  • Severe flood warning: flooding "could cause danger to life".
  • People can sign up to get warnings by phone call, text or email.
Mission 5

Predicting floods

  • Prediction says when and where a flood will come; prevention stops or reduces it.
  • Weather forecasts 3–10 days ahead have improved by "about one day per decade".
  • Rain forecasts feed river models, so better forecasts give earlier warnings.
  • In 2024, scientists reported that an AI model could forecast extreme floods in rivers with no gauges up to five days ahead; it was used in warnings in over 80 countries.
Mission 5

A UK soft scheme: Slowing the Flow, Pickering

  • In Pickering, North Yorkshire, the scheme planted 40,000 trees, restored heather moorland and built over 300 leaky dams in streams and drains.
  • It also built an upstream flood storage area (a small reservoir).
  • Over Christmas 2015 it cut the flood flow by 15–20%, about half from the land management and half from the storage area.
  • It "prevented the flooding of a number" of homes and the town's museum.
Quick check
?Which measure is soft engineering?
Mission 5

The Dutch answer: Room for the River

  • The floods of 1993 and 1995, when more than 250,000 people had to leave, were "the direct reason" for the Room for the River programme.
  • Aim: "give rivers space again", not only raise dikes.
  • Works at 34 places along the Rhine branches; total cost €2.3 billion.
  • Methods: move dikes back, lower floodplains, dig high-water side channels, return reclaimed land to the river.
Mission 5

Nijmegen: moving a dike back

  • At Lent, the dike was moved 350 m inland to make a side channel 3 km long.
  • Flood levels fell by 35 cm, beating the 27 cm target. Cost: about €351 million; finished 2016.
  • The new island became an urban river park.
  • Cost to people: about 50 houses in Lent were demolished and their families moved.
Mission 5

Is it sustainable?

  • Environment: more floodplain and wetland habitat; the river can flood safely.
  • Economy: €2.3 billion is costly, but it protects towns and farmland.
  • Society: safer homes and new parks; some families lost their homes.
  • Future: room to spread can cope with bigger floods better than walls built to one height.
Quick check
?Which is the strongest reason to call Room for the River sustainable?
Mission check

?A town's river floods every few years. Planners want lower flood levels, no extra flooding downstream and more wildlife habitat. Which plan matches the Dutch approach?

The Dutch gave the rivers room. Dikes were moved back, floodplains lowered and side channels dug at 34 places. At Nijmegen, flood levels fell by 35 cm.

Mission 6

Where pollution comes from

  • Point source: one outlet you can find and measure, such as a factory pipe or a sewage works.
  • Non-point (diffuse) source: pollution spread across a large area, such as runoff from thousands of fields or streets.
  • Main human causes: farming, sewage, industry, mining and litter, including plastics.
Mission 6

Farming and eutrophication

  • Fertiliser and manure wash nitrogen and phosphorus off fields into rivers.
  • The extra nutrients "can fuel the growth of algae blooms".
  • Dead algae are "decomposed by bacteria that consume oxygen until there is little or none left".
  • Water with less than 2 mg/L of dissolved oxygen is a dead zone: fish and shellfish die or leave.
  • This chain is eutrophication.
Mission 6

Chesapeake Bay, USA

  • The bay's watershed spans six states and Washington, DC.
  • Agriculture is "the single largest source of nitrogen pollution entering the Chesapeake Bay".
  • 2024 modelling: farms supplied about 46% of the nitrogen and 29% of the phosphorus.
Quick check
?Why is farm pollution harder to cut than pollution from one factory?
Mission 6

Sewage

  • Untreated sewage carries germs, and its organic matter uses up oxygen as it rots.
  • In England, storm overflows release sewage mixed with rainwater; they may be used legally only "during times of rainfall and snowmelt".
  • In 2024, each overflow spilled 31.8 times on average.
  • London's Victorian sewers were built for 4 million people; they now serve over 9 million.
Mission 6

Industry and mining

  • Industry: chemicals, heavy metals, oil and warm cooling water can enter rivers from pipes or accidents.
  • Mining: water draining old mines can be acidic and full of metals.
  • Example: on 5 August 2015, a team investigating the Gold King Mine, Colorado, USA, released about three million gallons of mine water. It reached the Animas and San Juan rivers.
  • Water intakes on the Animas were closed, and the US EPA supplied drinking and irrigation water to users.
Mission 6

Plastics

  • Rivers carry plastic litter to the sea.
  • A 2021 study estimated that rivers carry 0.8–2.7 million tonnes of plastic to the ocean each year.
  • More than 1,000 rivers carry 80% of it, and small urban rivers are among the most polluting.
Mission 6

Impacts of river pollution

  • Ecosystems: dead zones, fish kills, loss of plants and insects.
  • Health: unsafe drinking water causes about 505,000 diarrhoeal deaths a year.
  • Economy: fishing suffers; water firms must treat water more, or shut intakes as on the Animas.
  • People: rivers become unsafe for swimming, farming and drinking.
Quick check
?Which pairs a pollution source with its most direct impact?
Mission 6

Managing pollution: techniques

  • Treat it: upgrade sewage works; build storage. London's 25 km Thames Tideway Tunnel stores sewage that used to spill into the Thames; its builders say it now stops 95% of those spills.
  • Stop it at source: farmers plant cover crops and forest buffer strips, fence livestock out of streams and follow nutrient plans.
  • Regulate it: in 2010 the US EPA set a "pollution diet" for Chesapeake Bay, with measures due by 2025.
  • Collect it: litter traps and clean-ups on rivers.
Mission 6

Which works best?

  • Chesapeake: by 2023, measures in place had achieved 57% of the nitrogen and 67% of the phosphorus cuts needed; the 2025 goal "will not be met on time".
  • Tunnels and treatment work fast at one place but cost a lot.
  • Sustainable strategies tackle the source and last: buffer strips and wetlands trap nutrients and add habitat.
  • Exam move: judge each method on effectiveness, cost, time and who must change.
Quick check
?Which is the most sustainable way to cut nutrient pollution from farms?
Mission check

?In a hot summer, a lake surrounded by farmland turns bright green, then many fish die. What is the most likely explanation?

In Chesapeake Bay, farms supply about 46% of the nitrogen. Nutrients feed algae; bacteria breaking down dead algae use up the oxygen, leaving summer dead zones.

Mission 7

Mission 7: For weeks in 2014, some Somerset villages could only be reached by boat. Why did the floods last so long?

Make a guess: For weeks in 2014, some Somerset villages could only be reached by boat. Why did the floods last so long?

Specification: what this mission covers

Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460)

  • 1.3.8 causes and impacts of a flood on a named river
  • 1.3.8 strategies and techniques used to manage that river's flooding, including sustainable
Mission 7

Where: the Somerset Levels and Moors

  • South-west England. Rivers Parrett and Tone flow across it to the Bristol Channel.
  • Over 60,000 hectares, "very flat", and 18% of Somerset lies below average tide level.
  • Records of flooding go back to the 1600s. Much of the land was drained long ago for farming.
  • When: flooding began in mid-December 2013 and lasted into spring 2014.
Mission 7

Physical cause 1: a winter of storms

  • Mid-December 2013 to mid-February 2014: at least 12 major storms, the stormiest period for at least 20 years.
  • The UK's winter was the wettest on record, with 165% of average rainfall.
  • Each storm alone was not exceptional: "it was the overall totals accumulated … that led to the extensive flooding".
  • So the ground was saturated and rivers stayed full for weeks.
Mission 7

Physical cause 2: flat, low land and the tide

  • On flat land, water flows away slowly.
  • Water leaves through sluices into the tidal river. At high tide the sluice gates must close: water is tide-locked and cannot drain out.
  • Tides also carry silt from the sea up the Parrett and Tone.
  • Pumps cannot help when the river is already full: "Pumping into a full river will cause overtopping".
Quick check
?Why could floodwater on the Levels not simply drain away to the sea at every moment?
Mission 7

Human causes

  • Drained wetland: people farm and live on land that was once marsh.
  • Smaller river channels: silt had built up. Dredging "can restore the system to the channel capacity put in place in the 1960s".
  • Land use upstream: farming affects how fast water and soil reach rivers, though changed land management "would not have lessened the impact" of floods on this scale.
  • Hard surfaces: in towns, rain on roads and roofs "runs off fast and cannot infiltrate".
Mission 7

Impacts on people

  • 172 properties flooded; about 100 million m³ of water covered 65 km².
  • Over 200 homes were cut off, some for more than two months. Residents of Northmoor had to leave their homes.
  • Villages reached only by boat; the South West main line railway was flooded.
  • Transport disruption: impacts estimated "in the region of £100m".
  • Loss of life: no deaths were linked directly to flooding in England's winter 2013–14 floods; storms and high seas did cost lives on the coast.
  • Each flooded property in Somerset cost about £100,000, compared with £23,000 on average nationally, because floods lasted so long.
Mission 7

Impacts on the environment

  • 11,000 hectares of farmland still under water in early 2014, when the Flood Action Plan was written. In Somerset, 57% of the flooded farmland was under water for more than 15 days.
  • Wildlife sites: some 7,000 hectares of wildlife sites in Somerset were flooded or had high water levels.
  • Livestock had to be moved; crops were under water.
  • Pollution risk: sewage could not be treated in some cut-off communities.
Quick check
?Which impact of the 2014 Somerset floods is environmental?
Mission 7

Managing the Parrett and Tone: engineering

  • Dredging: 8 km of the Parrett and Tone dredged; about 130,000 m³ of silt removed by 31 October 2014.
  • Pumping: in 2014, more than 100 pumps, including 18 from the Netherlands. The EA now runs 21 permanent pumping stations and adds temporary pumps when set trigger points are met.
  • Raising roads, new control structures at Beer Wall, new flood schemes at Westonzoyland and Aller; over £250 million invested since 2014.
  • Bridgwater Tidal Barrier: gates across the Parrett to stop high tides travelling upstream; to protect at least 11,300 homes and 1,500 businesses.
Mission 7

Managing the floods: sustainable approaches

  • A 20-year Flood Action Plan (2014) for the whole catchment, not just the rivers.
  • Somerset Rivers Authority (from 31 January 2015), funded by just over £3 million a year of local council tax.
  • Hills to Levels: working with farmers to "slow the flow of water" upstream, e.g. leaky woody dams.
  • The vision: a "thriving, nature-rich wetland landscape" with grassland farming.
  • Honest limit: "We will never be able to stop flooding completely".
Quick check
?Which strategy is the most sustainable way to reduce flooding on the Levels in the long term?
Mission check

?In 2014 more than 100 pumps worked on the Levels, yet some homes were cut off for over two months. Why could pumping not clear the water faster?

The floods lasted so long because a record wet winter fell on flat, low land. Water drains out slowly through silted rivers whose sluices are tide-locked twice a day, so even 100 pumps had to wait for room in full rivers.

Mission 8

Mission 8: Why can a heavy shower in London send raw sewage into the River Thames?

Make a guess: Why can a heavy shower in London send raw sewage into the River Thames?

Specification: what this mission covers

Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460)

  • 1.3.9 detailed example — causes and impacts of pollution in a named river
  • 1.3.9 detailed example — strategies and techniques used to manage pollution levels in that river, including sustainable
Mission 8

Meet the river

  • The tidal Thames runs from Teddington through London to the sea.
  • Pollution here does not simply flow away: it moves up and down with the tide.
  • Waste can take about 1 month in winter, and up to 3 months in summer, to reach the sea from Teddington.
Mission 8

One pipe for two jobs

  • Most of London has combined sewers: one pipe carries sewage from homes and rainwater from streets and roofs.
  • When the pipes are full, Combined Sewer Overflows (CSOs) release the excess into the river.
  • Why? Otherwise sewage would back up and flood buildings and roads.
Mission 8

Cause 1: an old system for a bigger city

  • London's main sewers were designed for a 19th-century city of 4 million people.
  • Today they serve over 9 million.
  • In dry weather they run at 80% or more of capacity, so even light rain can trigger spills.
Mission 8

Cause 2: hard surfaces

  • More houses and offices replace green space, so less rain soaks into the ground.
  • More rain runs off roofs and roads into the sewers, which overload faster.
  • Climate change may bring warmer water and lower summer flows, which lower oxygen in the river.
Quick check
?Why can a heavy shower send raw sewage into the Thames?
Mission 8

How big is the problem?

  • On average, 39 million tonnes of untreated waste water overflowed into the Thames in London each year, 50–60 times a year.
  • In 2014, central London CSOs released 16 million tonnes.
  • Just three CSOs (Hammersmith, Lots Road, Western Pumping Stations) released 11 million tonnes of that.
Quick check
?In 2014, three CSOs released 11 of the 16 million tonnes from central London. What share is that?
Mission 8

Impacts on wildlife

  • Sewage is broken down by bacteria that use up dissolved oxygen.
  • In extreme events this kills fish (especially young fish) and other wildlife, sometimes in large numbers.
  • Dissolved oxygen below 45% can kill fish.
  • Nitrate is slowly rising; sewage effluent is a main source.
Mission 8

Impacts on people

  • Health risks for people who row, swim or work on the river.
  • Sewage litter: wipes, sanitary products and needles form floating slicks that wash up on the foreshore.
  • One study valued what people would be willing to pay for a cleaner Thames at £7.4–12.7 billion (2014 prices): a measure of how much they value it.
Mission 8

Quick fixes that were not enough

  • Bubbler boats pump oxygen into the river after spills.
  • Skimmer boats collect floating sewage litter.
  • Upgrading sewage treatment works cleans more of the waste that reaches them.
  • Problem: bubblers and skimmers treat the damage but "would not prevent pollution entering the river".
Mission 8

The Thames Tideway Tunnel

  • A 25 km tunnel, 7.2 m wide and 31–66 m deep, under the river.
  • It catches spills from the CSOs and stores them: about 1.6 million m³.
  • It links to the Lee Tunnel (open since 2016), which carries the waste to treatment at Beckton.
  • Fully connected in 2025; its builders say 95% of spills are now stopped. Cost: about £4.6 billion.
Quick check
?Which technique reduces the amount of rainwater entering the sewers in the first place?
Mission 8

Is it sustainable?

  • SuDS (permeable surfaces, storage ponds) cut runoff at source; but a 2013 review found SuDS alone would not cut spills enough for the Thames.
  • So London uses a mix: tunnel + treatment upgrades + SuDS.
  • Signs of progress: phosphorus has fallen since the 1990s; long-term improvement after five treatment works were upgraded.
  • Still a problem: nitrate rising.
  • Exam move: "evaluate" = weigh what improved against what did not, with figures, then judge.
Mission check

?Another city also has combined sewers. Which change would make its sewage overflows into the river more frequent?

In London, sewers built for 4 million people now serve over 9 million, and paved surfaces send more rain into them, so even light rain can trigger spills. The Tideway Tunnel now stores that overflow; SuDS reduce it at source.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A basin is an open system: precipitation in; stores and transfers; discharge and evapotranspiration out.
  • Slow stores (groundwater, ice) keep rivers flowing when it does not rain.

Mission 2

  • Downstream: discharge, width and depth rise; gradient and load size fall (Tees: 211 m to 12 m high; 9.1 to 21.0 m³/s).
  • Four erosion and four transport processes; rivers deposit when they lose energy; fine cohesive grains resist erosion.

Mission 3

  • Erosion landforms: V-shaped valleys, interlocking spurs, waterfalls, gorges; erosion and deposition: meanders and oxbow lakes; deposition: floodplains and levees.
  • Rapids and potholes (upper course); deltas (mouth).

Mission 4

  • Physical causes: intense or long rain, saturated ground, steep relief, impermeable rock.
  • Human causes: urbanisation, deforestation, farming; they shorten lag time and raise peaks.

Mission 5

  • Hard engineering controls the river with structures; soft engineering works with nature and changes land use, warnings and preparation.
  • Room for the River (Netherlands) gave the Rhine space.

Mission 6

  • Rivers are polluted by farming (eutrophication), sewage, industry, mining and plastics; point sources are easier to control than non-point ones.
  • Sustainable management stops pollution at source.

Mission 7

  • Somerset Levels, winter 2013–14 (Rivers Parrett and Tone). Physical: at least 12 storms, wettest winter on record, flat land below tide level, tide-locking. Human: drained wetland, silted channels, land use upstream.
  • Impacts: 172 properties flooded, over 200 homes cut off for weeks, railway and roads closed, about £100,000 per flooded property; farmland and 7,000 ha of wildlife sites under water; sewage risk.
  • Management: dredging, pumping, road raising, Bridgwater Tidal Barrier; sustainable: Somerset Rivers Authority, Hills to Levels, wetland vision.

Mission 8

  • Causes (Thames): combined sewers built for 4 million people, a city of over 9 million, more paved surfaces; rain → CSO spills (average 39 million tonnes a year).
  • Impacts and management: low oxygen and fish deaths, sewage litter, health risks; managed by treatment upgrades, bubbler and skimmer boats, the 25 km Tideway Tunnel (95% of spills stopped, its builders say) and sustainable drainage (SuDS).
