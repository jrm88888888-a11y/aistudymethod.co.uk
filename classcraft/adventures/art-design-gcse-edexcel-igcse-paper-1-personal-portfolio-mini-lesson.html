Paper 1: Personal Portfolio

This mini-lesson shows you how to build a strong Personal Portfolio for the Edexcel International GCSE (9-1) Art & Design — the coursework component that is internally set and worth 50% of your grade.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go (some match evidence to objectives, some ask you to pick the best next step) and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you're ready.

The component

What the Personal Portfolio is

The Edexcel International GCSE Art & Design has two components:

  • Component 1 — Personal Portfolio (Paper 1). Coursework: internally set by your school, marked by your teacher and moderated by Pearson. 50% of the qualification.
  • Component 2 — Externally Set Assignment. Based on a Pearson theme, with preparation followed by a supervised period. Also 50%.

A portfolio is not a folder of finished pictures. It is the evidence of a creative journey: your research, your experiments, your recording and your development, ending in a personal outcome.

Endorsed titles: you may work in a chosen discipline such as Fine Art, Graphic Communication, Textile Design, Three-Dimensional Design, or Photography — but the four assessment objectives are the same whichever discipline you take.

How you are marked

The four Assessment Objectives

Your whole portfolio is judged against four Assessment Objectives (AOs). Each is worth 25% — they are equally weighted, so you cannot ignore one.

Remember: Develop · Refine · Record · Present. Every strong portfolio touches all four in an integrated, holistic way.

AO1 · Develop

AO1 — Develop ideas through investigation

AO1 asks you to develop ideas through investigations informed by contextual and other sources, demonstrating critical understanding.

Evidence to include:

  • Research into artists, designers, cultures or movements linked to your theme.
  • Artist analysis: not just biography — what they did, how, and why, and what you take from it.
  • A mood board or mind map that shows where your ideas are coming from.
  • First-hand and second-hand contextual sources (a gallery visit, a website, a book).

"Critical understanding" means judging and connecting — say what works, what you'd borrow, and how it feeds your intentions. Copying an image without comment does not score AO1.

AO2 · Refine

AO2 — Refine by experimenting with media

AO2 asks you to refine work by exploring ideas, and by selecting and experimenting with appropriate media, materials, techniques and processes.

Evidence to include:

  • Trying different media: pencil, ink, watercolour, collage, print, clay, digital — whatever suits your theme.
  • Media samples / experiments that you then evaluate ("the ink bled too much, so I switched to fine liner").
  • Refining: keeping what works, dropping what doesn't, and improving an idea across attempts.
  • Choosing media deliberately to match the effect you want.

Key idea: AO2 is about experimenting and refining, not about producing one perfect piece. Show the choices you made and why.

AO3 · Record

AO3 — Record ideas, observations & insights

AO3 asks you to record ideas, observations and insights relevant to intentions as work progresses.

Evidence to include:

  • Observational drawings from first-hand study (from life, not just from photos).
  • Your own photographs taken as reference or as artwork.
  • Annotations that record your thinking, decisions and reflections.
  • Studies that stay relevant to your intentions — recording linked to where the project is going.

"As work progresses" is the clue: AO3 is a running record. Date your pages and let the sketchbook tell the story of your thinking.

AO4 · Present

AO4 — Present a personal response

AO4 asks you to present a personal and meaningful response that realises intentions and, where appropriate, makes connections between visual and other elements.

Evidence to include:

  • A final outcome (or outcomes) that clearly grows out of your earlier pages.
  • A response that is personal and meaningful — yours, not a copy.
  • Evidence that it realises your intentions — it delivers what your journey set out to do.
  • Connections between visual elements (and, where relevant, ideas, sources or context).

Realises intentions is the phrase examiners look for: the final piece should feel like the destination of the journey the rest of the portfolio described.

The visual language

The formal elements

The formal elements are the building blocks of all art. You use them to describe artwork (in your analysis) and to make your own:

Tip: Shape is flat (2D); form is three-dimensional (has depth). Tone is how light or dark something is, independent of colour.

Colour theory

The colour wheel

Colour is a formal element you should be able to talk about. The colour wheel organises hues so you can describe relationships:

Warm colours (reds, oranges, yellows) feel bold and advance; cool colours (blues, greens, purples) feel calm and recede. Naming these in your annotation strengthens AO1 and AO3.

Skill · annotation

Annotate the thinking

Annotation is writing on your pages that explains your thinking. Good annotation is not a label ("this is my drawing") — it records decisions, judgements and plans.

  • Describe using formal elements ("I used cool blues and heavy tone…").
  • Evaluate ("…but the ink smudged, so next I'll try fine liner").
  • Connect ("…this echoes Hokusai's flat wave shapes I researched").
  • Plan ("next I want to test collage on a larger scale").

Weak vs strong: "My painting of the sea." → weak. "I layered turquoise over grey to capture cold, choppy water — inspired by Turner's loose brushwork." → strong: it describes, connects and shows intention.

Your turn · write

Upgrade the annotation

✍️A weak caption says: "This is my drawing of a leaf." Write one stronger sentence that describes (formal elements), evaluates and hints at your next step.

The graphite felt a little flat, so next I'll experiment with watercolour washes to bring in the autumn colours I researched." — it describes with formal elements, evaluates and plans a next step.

Skill · artist analysis

Analyse an artist (not just copy)

An artist study should do more than reproduce a picture. A useful structure:

  • Who & what — the artist, the work, and its context (brief).
  • How — the media, techniques and formal elements they use.
  • Why / effect — the mood or meaning it creates, and whether it works.
  • So what for me — what you will borrow and how it feeds your intentions.

That final step — the personal connection — is what turns a copy into AO1 critical understanding.

Do include a study in the style of the artist (media experiment = AO2), but always add the analysis that says why and what next.

Skill · presentation

Presentation & sketchbook practice

Presentation is how your journey reads. It supports AO4 and makes your other AOs easy for the examiner to see.

  • Layout: arrange each page so the eye flows — research, studies, experiments and notes working together.
  • Sketchbook: treat it as a working document — messy thinking is fine, but keep it relevant and dated.
  • Photograph 3D or large work and mount the photos so the evidence is in the portfolio.
  • Quality of presentation should serve the ideas — don't let decoration bury the artwork.

Selection matters: you don't submit everything. Choose pages that show the strongest evidence of all four AOs and a clear journey to your outcome.

Common mistakes

Myth-busting the portfolio

Myth 1: "The portfolio is just my best finished pieces." → No. It must show the whole journey: research, experiments, recording and development, not only outcomes.

Myth 2: "Annotation is just labelling what things are." → No. Annotate the thinking — describe, evaluate, connect and plan.

Myth 3: "AO2 means making one polished piece." → No. AO2 is experimenting and refining with media — trying options and improving, showing your choices.

Myth 4: "One weak AO won't matter." → No. The four AOs are equally weighted (25% each) — a gap in any one caps your grade.
