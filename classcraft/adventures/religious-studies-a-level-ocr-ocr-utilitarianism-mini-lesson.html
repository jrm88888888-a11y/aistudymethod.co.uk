Utilitarianism

Utilitarianism is a teleological, consequentialist theory: the right action is the one that produces the best consequences — the greatest good for the greatest number. We compare Bentham (act) and Mill (rule).

Bentham

Bentham: the principle of utility

Jeremy Bentham was a hedonist: pleasure is the only intrinsic good and pain the only evil. His principle of utility says the right act is the one that produces the greatest happiness for the greatest number.

  • He is an act utilitarian: judge each individual act by how much pleasure over pain it produces.
  • Everyone counts equally — 'each to count for one, none for more than one'.

Hedonic calculus

The hedonic calculus

To weigh the pleasure of an act, Bentham gives the hedonic (felicific) calculus — seven factors:

  • Intensity — how strong is the pleasure?
  • Duration — how long does it last?
  • Certainty — how likely is it?
  • Propinquity — how soon (near) is it?
  • Fecundity — will it lead to more pleasure?
  • Purity — how free is it from pain?
  • Extent — how many people does it affect?

Mill

Mill: higher and lower pleasures

John Stuart Mill refined the theory. Worried it was a doctrine 'fit for swine', he argued that pleasures differ in quality, not just quantity:

  • Higher pleasures (intellectual, aesthetic, moral) are superior to lower pleasures (bodily).
  • 'It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied.'
  • Mill leans toward rule utilitarianism: follow the rules that generally tend to produce the greatest happiness, protecting justice and rights.

Act, rule, preference

Act, rule and preference utilitarianism

Three forms to distinguish:

  • Act utilitarianism (Bentham): judge each act individually by its utility. Flexible, but can justify almost anything.
  • Rule utilitarianism (Mill): keep rules that generally maximise happiness. More stable, protects rights, but can become rigid.
  • Preference utilitarianism (Peter Singer): maximise the satisfaction of people preferences, not just pleasure — taking each person interests into account.
Evaluation

Strengths and criticisms

Strengths: democratic, impartial, focused on real-world welfare, and easy to grasp.

Criticisms:

  • Consequences are hard to measure and predict.
  • It can justify injustice — harming a minority for the majority (the 'tyranny of the majority').
  • It ignores intentions, promises and special relationships (e.g. to family).
  • Whose pleasure counts, and can pleasures really be added up like this?
