Mission 1: Could right and wrong be worked out like a sum of pleasures and pains?

Mission 1

Judging by results

  • Teleological (from telos, end or goal): an act is judged by the goal it achieves.
  • Consequentialist: what makes an act right or wrong is its consequences, not the act in itself or the motive.
  • Utilitarianism: the right act is the one with the best consequences for happiness: the most pleasure and least pain overall.
  • Founded in its classic form by Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832), An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (printed 1780, published 1789).
  • Contrast: deontological ethics (Kant, natural law) judge the act itself, by duty or rule.
Mission 1

Bentham's world

  • Enlightenment: morality and law should rest on reason and evidence, not tradition or religious authority.
  • Empiricism: Hume had grounded morals in human feelings of pleasure and approval.
  • The phrase "greatest happiness for the greatest numbers" had already appeared in Francis Hutcheson (1725); Bentham also credited Joseph Priestley and the Italian penal reformer Cesare Beccaria (1764).
  • Industrial Revolution Britain: growing cities, harsh punishments, a chaotic legal system that Bentham attacked in A Fragment on Government (1776).
  • Bentham wanted a science of morals and law that anyone could check.
Mission 1Read the source

Two sovereign masters

Bentham, An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789), chapter I, paragraph 1:

> "Nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure. It is for them alone to point out what we ought to do, as well as to determine what we shall do."

  • Two claims: pleasure and pain determine what we do (a claim about human psychology), and point out what we ought to do (a moral claim).

Mission 1

The principle of utility

  • Utility: the tendency of an act to produce pleasure, benefit or happiness, or to prevent pain.
  • Principle of utility: approve an act if it tends to increase the happiness of those affected; disapprove if it tends to decrease it.
  • "The greatest happiness of the greatest number" (A Fragment on Government, 1776): the measure of right and wrong.
  • Hedonism: pleasure is the only thing good in itself; pain the only thing bad in itself. Happiness = pleasure and the absence of pain.
  • Equality: "everybody to count for one, nobody for more than one" (Bentham's rule, as Mill reports it).
  • Quantity only: kinds of pleasure do not differ in value. Bentham: push-pin (a children's game) is as valuable as poetry, if it gives as much pleasure.

Mission 1

The hedonic calculus

Bentham's way of measuring the pleasure or pain an act will cause (IPML, chapter IV), seven factors:

  • Intensity: how strong? · Duration: how long?
  • Certainty: how likely? · Propinquity (remoteness): how soon?
  • Fecundity: will it lead to more pleasure? · Purity: is it free from pain afterwards?
  • Extent: how many people are affected?
  • Bentham's own memory verse: "Intense, long, certain, speedy, fruitful, pure— / Such marks in pleasures and in pains endure."
Quick check
?Reading for pleasure tends to make further pleasures possible: new interests, friendships and skills. Which factor of the hedonic calculus does this describe?
Mission 1

Act utilitarianism

  • Act utilitarianism: judge each act, in its own situation, by its consequences. No act is right or wrong in itself.
  • So lying, or breaking a promise, can be right if it produces the greatest balance of pleasure over pain here.
  • Relativist: the right act is relative to the situation and its consequences; only the principle of utility is fixed.
  • Bentham applied it above all to law and government: a law is good if it increases the community's happiness.
  • Spec: utility in teleological and relativist approaches to ethics.
Mission 1Evaluate

Is Bentham's act utilitarianism a good guide?

  • Claim: Bentham's act utilitarianism is a good way to decide what is right.
  • For: Simple and practical; based on something everyone recognises (pleasure and pain); every person counts equally.
  • For: Looks at real consequences; secular, so usable by anyone; inspired real reforms (and includes animals).
  • Against: Can justify injustice: harming an innocent minority if the majority gain enough pleasure.
  • Against: Consequences are hard to predict; pleasures are hard to measure and compare; motives and rights seem to count for nothing.
  • Judgement criteria: Can pleasure be measured? Does justice for individuals matter more than totals? Is pleasure really the only good?
Mission 1Worked answer

Using the calculus

Task: explain how Bentham's hedonic calculus would decide whether a town should replace a park with a car park.

  • Point: Bentham would compare the pleasure and pain each option produces for everyone affected.
  • Apply the factors: extent (many shoppers vs local families), duration (permanent loss of green space), fecundity (exercise and community events lead to further pleasures), purity (pollution afterwards).
  • Develop: The option with the greater balance of pleasure over pain is right. No act is right in itself: this is act utilitarianism.
  • Link: The answer could change with the facts (no act is right or wrong in itself), which is both a strength (flexibility) and a weakness (uncertainty).

Mission 2

Mill and the "pig philosophy" objection

  • John Stuart Mill (1806–1873), raised by his father James Mill as a utilitarian; Bentham was a family friend.
  • Works: On Liberty (1859); Utilitarianism (magazine 1861, book 1863); The Subjection of Women (1869).
  • Critics mocked Bentham's theory as "a doctrine worthy only of swine": if all pleasures are equal, a pig's contentment is as good as a human life.
  • Mill keeps the greatest happiness principle, but argues that pleasures differ in quality, not only quantity.
Mission 2

Higher and lower pleasures

  • Higher pleasures: of the mind: intellect, imagination, feelings, moral sentiments (learning, art, friendship, doing good).
  • Lower pleasures: of the body: food, drink, sex, rest.
  • Higher pleasures are worth more in kind: a small amount can outweigh a large amount of lower pleasure.
  • Competent judges: the test is what people who have experienced both prefer.
  • Mill does not say lower pleasures are bad. They matter; they are simply not the best.
Mission 2Read the source

Socrates dissatisfied

Mill, Utilitarianism (1863), chapter 2:

> "It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied."

  • Mill adds (paraphrase): if the fool or the pig thinks otherwise, it is because they know only their own side of the question.

Mission 2

The harm principle

On Liberty (1859), chapter 1:

> "…the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilised community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others."

  • Society may not compel you for your own good: it may advise, persuade and warn.
  • Liberty of thought and speech, of tastes and pursuits, and of association.
  • Mill's example (paraphrase): you may stop someone stepping onto an unsafe bridge if there is no time to warn them; if they know the risk, the choice is theirs.
  • Why it is utilitarian: free people develop themselves and discover new truths, which increases happiness in the long run.

Mission 2

Rules from experience

  • Objection to Bentham: there is no time to calculate before every act.
  • Mill (paraphrase): humanity has been learning the effects of actions throughout its history. Those lessons are stored in secondary principles (rules such as "do not lie", "keep promises").
  • Sailors do not calculate the stars before every voyage; they take the Nautical Almanac, already calculated (Utilitarianism, chapter 2).
  • So in normal life we follow the rules; the principle of utility settles conflicts between them.
Mission 2

Rule utilitarianism: strong and weak

  • Rule utilitarianism: an act is right if it follows a rule which, if generally followed, produces the greatest happiness.
  • Spec: following accepted laws that lead to the greatest overall balance of good over evil, and promote the common good.
  • Strong rule utilitarianism: never break a well-founded rule, even when breaking it would bring more happiness this time.
  • Weak rule utilitarianism: follow the rules normally, but break one in an exceptional case where obeying would clearly cause more harm.
  • Example: "do not lie" maximises trust in general. Strong: keep it always. Weak: may lie to save a life.
Quick check
?A doctor's rule is to keep patients' information confidential. Which describes a weak rule utilitarian doctor?
Mission 2

Was Mill a rule utilitarian?

  • Mill never uses the term. "Rule utilitarianism" was named in the 20th century; J. O. Urmson (1953) argued Mill held a version of it.
  • Others read Mill as an act utilitarian who used rules as rules of thumb.
  • Either way, Mill gives rules a far bigger place than Bentham.
Mission 2Evaluate

Is rule utilitarianism better than act utilitarianism?

  • Claim: Rule utilitarianism is a better basis for moral decisions than act utilitarianism.
  • For: Protects justice and rights (no punishing the innocent for the majority's pleasure); quicker; predictable; promotes trust and the common good.
  • Against: J. J. C. Smart (1956): if a rule should be broken whenever breaking it produces more happiness, rule utilitarianism collapses into act utilitarianism; if not, it is irrational "rule worship".
  • Against (higher pleasures): who decides what is "higher"? The ranking can look elitist and culturally biased.
  • Judgement criteria: Can rules be justified only by consequences? What about exceptional cases? Does it promote justice better?

Mission 3

Preference utilitarianism

  • Maximise the satisfaction of preferences (or interests), not pleasure: what people would choose, on reflection.
  • R. M. Hare (1919–2002), Moral Thinking (1981): put yourself in the place of everyone affected.
  • Peter Singer (b. 1946), Animal Liberation (1975), Practical Ethics (1979; 3rd edition 2011): equal consideration of interests.
  • Any being that can suffer has interests, so animals count. Favouring humans simply for being human is speciesism.
  • Respects choices that do not maximise pleasure (e.g. a person who prefers truth to comfort).
  • Singer is one of your spec's suggested scholars.

Mission 3

Can pleasure be measured?

  • Problems: pleasures are subjective; different kinds cannot be put on one scale; consequences are hard to predict; how far ahead should we count?
  • Problems: who counts? Future generations, animals, people far away?
  • Replies: rough estimates are enough for most choices; rules store past experience (Mill's almanac); preferences can be asked about and compared.
  • Practice: modern health systems measure "quality-adjusted life years" to compare treatments: a utilitarian kind of measure.
  • Spec: whether it is possible to measure good or pleasure and then reach a moral decision.

Mission 3Evaluate

Is utilitarianism a helpful method?

  • Claim: Utilitarianism provides a helpful method of moral decision-making.
  • For: Clear principle; impartial ("everybody to count for one"); takes real consequences seriously; usable by believers and non-believers alike; adaptable (rule, preference).
  • For: Has driven reforms widely seen as progress; extends concern to animals and distant strangers.
  • Against: Can justify injustice to minorities; ignores motives, rights and special duties (to family, promises).
  • Against: Measurement and prediction problems; no intrinsic wrongs: no act is wrong in itself, so any act could be permitted if the consequences are good enough; may encourage immoral acts "for the greater good".
  • Judgement criteria: Is pleasure (or preference) the only intrinsic good? Does it promote justice? Can it work in contemporary society? Is it compatible with religious ethics?
Mission 3Worked answer

Evaluating with a scholar

Task: "Utilitarianism cannot protect justice." Evaluate.

  • Line of argument: the claim is strong against act utilitarianism, weaker against rule utilitarianism.
  • Support: Bentham's calculus could approve punishing an innocent person if enough people gain.
  • Counter: Mill's rules (e.g. fair trials) maximise happiness generally; a weak rule utilitarian keeps them in almost all cases.
  • Critique of the counter: Smart: then either rules collapse into act utilitarianism, or they become "rule worship".
  • Judgement: decided by your own criteria (e.g. can rules be justified only by consequences?), stated and defended.

Mission 4

What the words mean

  • Euthanasia (Greek eu "good" + thanatos "death"): deliberately ending a person's life, or allowing it to end, for that person's sake.
  • Active euthanasia: someone does something that causes death (e.g. a doctor gives a lethal injection).
  • Passive euthanasia: treatment that would keep the person alive is withheld or withdrawn, so that they die.
  • Assisted suicide: the person takes the final act themselves, with means supplied by someone else. Physician-assisted suicide: the helper is a doctor.
  • Assisted dying: the term used in many current laws and bills for physician-assisted suicide for terminally ill adults.
  • Not euthanasia: stopping treatment that no longer helps, where the aim is not death (see Mission 5).
Mission 4

Whose choice? Three types

  • Voluntary: the life is ended at the person's request or with their consent. Typical case: an incurable or terminal illness.
  • Non-voluntary: the person cannot consent (e.g. a newborn, or someone in a persistent vegetative state, PVS). Someone representing their interests decides.
  • Involuntary: the person could consent but is not asked, or refuses. Treated as murder in every legal system; almost no one defends it.
  • OCR's wording: non-voluntary euthanasia is ending a life "without their consent but with the consent of someone representing their interests", as for a patient in PVS.
  • PVS: the body is kept alive (breathing, sleep–wake cycles) but there are no signs of awareness, for months or years.
  • The two distinctions combine: e.g. voluntary active (lethal injection on request), non-voluntary passive (feeding withdrawn in PVS).
Mission 4Read the source

Sanctity of life

  • Sanctity of life: human life is sacred, holy, because it is given by God and bears God's image. Its religious origin is in the Hebrew Bible.

> "God created man in his own image. In God's image he created him; male and female he created them." (Genesis 1:27, WEBBE)

> "You shall not murder." (Exodus 20:13, WEBBE)

> "The LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away. Blessed be the LORD's name." (Job 1:21, WEBBE)

Q1 What reason does Genesis give for treating every human life as sacred?

Show answer

Show answer

Mission 4

Sanctity of life in Christian teaching

  • Strong sanctity of life: innocent human life must never be deliberately ended, whatever its quality. Its value does not depend on health, ability or awareness.
  • Christians also cite the body as a "temple of the Holy Spirit" (1 Corinthians 6:19) and Jesus' care for the sick.
  • Declaration on Euthanasia (Sacred Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith, 5 May 1980, paraphrase): euthanasia, by act or omission, intended to end suffering by causing death, is a grave wrong; but no one is obliged to use treatments out of proportion to the expected result, and pain relief may be given even if it may shorten life.
  • Catholic teaching today: Catechism of the Catholic Church 2276–2279; John Paul II, Evangelium Vitae (1995), §65.
  • Weak sanctity of life (held by some Christians): life is precious and God-given, but not of absolute value; love may sometimes allow death to come or be hastened.
  • Sanctity of life is not vitalism (keeping alive at all costs): most sanctity views allow burdensome treatment to stop.

Mission 4

Quality of life

  • Quality of life: a life has value because of what it contains or makes possible, not just because it is human.
  • OCR: its origins are secular: life must have certain attributes (e.g. awareness, relationships, freedom from unbearable pain) to have value.
  • Used in medicine to weigh treatments (e.g. the QALY, a "quality-adjusted life year", used in UK health planning).
  • Jonathan Glover (b. 1941), Causing Death and Saving Life (1977): what matters is not being alive but having a life worth living, chiefly conscious life.
  • Peter Singer (b. 1946), Rethinking Life and Death (1994): the traditional sanctity-of-life ethic is collapsing; the worth of a human life varies (paraphrase).
  • Critics: who judges a life "not worth living"? Disability-rights campaigners warn the idea devalues disabled lives.
  • Some religious believers also use quality-of-life reasoning, alongside sanctity, when deciding about treatment.

Mission 4

Autonomy and rights

  • Autonomy (Greek "self-law"): the right of a competent person to make decisions about their own life and body.
  • Right to die arguments: if I have a right to life, I may waive it; refusing treatment is already lawful, so why not ask for help to die?
  • Critics: Mill himself said one may not sell oneself into slavery, since that ends future freedom; does a choice to die do the same?
  • Is a request to die ever fully free? Pressure from depression, pain, cost or a sense of being a burden.
  • Christian reply: life is held in trust (stewardship), not owned; freedom is for living well with others.
Mission 4

The law: a snapshot

  • England and Wales: Suicide Act 1961: suicide is no longer a crime, but encouraging or assisting suicide is (up to 14 years). Active euthanasia is murder or manslaughter.
  • DPP policy (2010, after the Purdy case, 2009): lists factors that make prosecution for assisting suicide more or less likely, such as a wholly compassionate motive.
  • Airedale NHS Trust v Bland (House of Lords, 1993): feeding could be withdrawn from Tony Bland, in PVS after the Hillsborough disaster; the court treated it as withdrawing treatment no longer in his interests, not as killing.
  • Elsewhere: Netherlands and Belgium (both 2002): euthanasia on request under strict conditions. Oregon (1997): assisted suicide for terminally ill adults. Canada (2016): medical assistance in dying. Switzerland: assisting suicide is not a crime unless done from selfish motives.
  • Recent bills for terminally ill adults: the Scottish Parliament rejected one (March 2026); one for England and Wales passed the Commons (2025) but ran out of time in the Lords (April 2026).
Mission 4

Palliative care

  • Palliative care: care that relieves pain and distress (physical, emotional, spiritual) when a cure is no longer possible. It neither hastens nor postpones death.
  • Cicely Saunders (1918–2005), a Christian nurse and doctor, founded St Christopher's Hospice, London (1967), the first modern hospice. Her idea of "total pain": suffering has physical, emotional, social and spiritual sides.
  • Many opponents of euthanasia argue: good palliative care removes most reasons to ask for it.
  • Supporters reply: some suffering cannot be relieved, and some people value control over the manner and time of death.
Mission 4Diversity

Religious and secular views

  • Catholic and Orthodox Christians: euthanasia and assisted suicide are always wrong; refusing disproportionate treatment and pain relief that may shorten life are allowed.
  • Church of England: has opposed changing the law, stressing palliative care and protection of the vulnerable. But individual Anglicans differ: e.g. Desmond Tutu (1931–2021) publicly supported assisted dying (2014).
  • Liberal Christian voices: e.g. Hans Küng (1928–2021), a dissenting Catholic theologian, argued for a responsible choice to die in A Dignified Dying (1995, with Walter Jens).
  • Judaism: actively hastening death is forbidden, but most authorities allow removing what only prolongs dying. Islam: life belongs to God; most scholars forbid euthanasia, citing e.g. Qur'an 4:29 ("Nor kill (or destroy) yourselves", Yusuf Ali).
  • Secular supporters: Humanists UK and Dignity in Dying back a change in the law, on grounds of autonomy and compassion.
  • Secular opponents: some disability-rights campaigners (e.g. Not Dead Yet UK) and many palliative-care doctors fear pressure on vulnerable people.
  • So the divide is not simply "religious against secular".

Mission 5

Natural law applied

  • Primary precept: preservation of life. A secondary precept follows: never directly kill an innocent human being.
  • Aquinas on suicide (Summa Theologiae II-II q. 64 a. 5, paraphrase): it goes against natural self-love, injures the community, and takes what belongs to God.
  • So voluntary euthanasia and assisted suicide are wrong: the person's consent does not change what the act is.
  • Also against ordered society: doctors would become agents of death.
  • Natural law is absolutist here: no exceptions for hard cases.
Mission 5

Double effect and proportionate means

  • Double effect: an act with a good and a bad effect may be done if the act is not wrong in itself, the bad effect is not intended or the means to the good, and there is a proportionate reason.
  • Pain relief: strong painkillers given to relieve pain may be right even if they may shorten life, as long as death is foreseen, not intended (Pius XII, 1957; Catechism 2279).
  • Proportionate means (1980 Declaration, paraphrase): no one must accept treatment that is too burdensome or out of proportion to its expected benefit. Refusing it is accepting death, not causing it.
  • (Older terms: ordinary and extraordinary means.)
  • So natural law separates euthanasia from good end-of-life care by intention and proportion, not by whether the doctor acts or omits.
  • Critics: intention is hidden; a large dose "to relieve pain" might be euthanasia in disguise.

Mission 5

Situation ethics applied

  • Joseph Fletcher argued for voluntary euthanasia years before Situation Ethics (1966): Morals and Medicine (1954), chapter "Euthanasia: Our Right to Die".
  • Agape: the only intrinsic good. If ending a life on request is the most loving act in the situation, it is right.
  • Personalism: the person in pain matters more than the rule "never kill".
  • Pragmatism: does a rule against euthanasia actually help this patient?
  • Relativism: the answer depends on the case: love might also refuse a request made under family pressure.
  • Fletcher also used personhood ("indicators of humanhood") to argue that ending life where no person remains need not be wrong.
  • Critics: one person's calculation of love could justify non-voluntary euthanasia; a society may need firm rules to protect the vulnerable.
Mission 5

Virtue ethics and utilitarianism

  • Peter Singer (preference utilitarian): if a competent person prefers to die, respecting that preference is right; "respect a person's desire to live or die" is one of his new commandments (Rethinking Life and Death, 1994).
Mission 5

Acts and omissions

  • Acts and omissions doctrine: actively killing is morally worse than letting someone die, even if the result is the same.
  • Defenders: in letting die, the disease causes death; the doctor stops fighting it. UK law follows this line (Bland, 1993).
  • James Rachels (1941–2003), "Active and Passive Euthanasia" (New England Journal of Medicine, 1975): Smith drowns his young cousin for an inheritance; Jones means to, but watches the boy slip and drown. Neither is less guilty (paraphrase).
  • So, Rachels argues, if letting die is allowed, a quicker, kinder lethal injection may be better: passive euthanasia can mean a slower death.
  • Glover (Causing Death and Saving Life, 1977) also denies that the difference between act and omission has moral weight of its own; what matters is the outcome and the reasons.
  • OCR's question: is there a moral difference between intervention and non-intervention to end a patient's life?

Mission 5

The doctor–patient relationship and trust

  • The Hippocratic Oath (Greek, c. 4th century BCE) includes a promise not to give a deadly drug to anyone who asks (paraphrase).
  • Trust argument against: if doctors may end lives, patients (especially the old, disabled or poor) may fear them; medicine's purpose of healing is blurred.
  • Doctors' consciences: should any doctor be required to take part? Bills and laws normally include a right to opt out.
  • Trust argument for: patients may trust doctors more if they know their wishes at the end will be honoured, openly rather than in secret.
  • Professional bodies differ: the British Medical Association moved to a neutral position in 2021; many palliative-care doctors remain opposed.
  • Patients' trust also depends on doctors being honest about prognosis and options.
Mission 5

The slippery slope

  • Slippery slope: allowing voluntary euthanasia in limited cases will lead, step by step, to cases now thought wrong.
  • Logical form: if autonomy is the reason, why limit it to the terminally ill? If relief of suffering is the reason, why limit it to those who can ask?
  • Empirical form: points to change where laws exist, e.g. the Netherlands' Groningen Protocol for newborns (2005); Belgium's removal of an age limit for children (2014); Canada extending medical assistance in dying to people whose death is not foreseeable (2021).
  • Replies: each change was debated and voted on, not inevitable; strict safeguards and reporting can hold a line; the evidence is read differently by each side.
  • Opponents add: pressure on people who feel a burden, and on disabled people.
  • Glover takes the side-effects seriously but argues they call for safeguards, not a ban (paraphrase).
Mission 5Evaluate

Can a person have complete autonomy over their own life?

  • Claim: A competent adult should have complete autonomy over their own life, including how and when it ends.
  • For: Mill's harm principle: over one's own body the individual is sovereign. Refusing treatment is already allowed.
  • For: Singer and Glover: respecting a person's considered wish to die respects them as a person.
  • Against: No one chooses alone: choices are shaped by pain, depression, money and fear of being a burden.
  • Against: Others must act (doctors, nurses), so it is not purely self-regarding; natural law and many Christians: life is held in trust from God.
  • Against: some argue autonomy dominates only because shared values have thinned; it may not be enough on its own.
  • Judgement criteria: Is the choice truly free and informed? Who else must act? Can safeguards protect the vulnerable? Is life a gift, a possession, or both?

  • Point: Situation ethics responds to the actual patient: if a competent, terminally ill woman in unrelievable pain asks to die, agape and personalism may require helping her.
  • Source: Fletcher, Morals and Medicine (1954): a "right to die" in such cases.
  • Counter: Natural law protects the vulnerable with a fixed rule, and double effect already allows strong pain relief.
  • Develop: The test is whether a case-by-case approach can be safe across a whole health system, or only at one bedside.
  • Mini-judgement: It answers the single case well, but must meet the slippery slope and trust objections to answer the question about law.
  • AO2 tip: end each paragraph with a mini-judgement that answers the issue set, not just a summary.

For natural law the line is drawn by intention, not by whether the doctor acts or stands back; some philosophers argue that neither line has moral weight on its own.

Mission 6

Shareholders and stakeholders

  • Shareholders: the owners of a company; they invest to receive a share of the profits.
  • Stakeholders: everyone who affects or is affected by a business: employees, customers, suppliers, the local community, the country as a whole, governments and the environment.
  • Shareholder view: managers work for the owners; their duty is to maximise returns within the law.
  • Stakeholder view: R. Edward Freeman (b. 1951), Strategic Management: A Stakeholder Approach (1984): managers must balance the interests of all stakeholders.
  • Business ethics asks when these interests clash, whose come first.
Mission 6

Corporate social responsibility (CSR)

  • CSR: the idea that a business has responsibility towards the community and the environment, not only to its shareholders.
  • Archie B. Carroll, "The Pyramid of Corporate Social Responsibility" (1991), four layers from the base up:
  • Economic (be profitable) → legal (obey the law) → ethical (do what is right and fair beyond the law) → philanthropic (give back to the community).
  • Applied to stakeholders: fair pay and safe conditions (employees); honest, safe products (customers); jobs, low pollution, local giving (local community); paying taxes fairly (the country, governments).
  • Andrew Crane and Dirk Matten, Business Ethics (2003): CSR is now expected of companies in a global economy; firms take on roles once left to governments (corporate citizenship).
Mission 6Read the source

Friedman on profit

Milton Friedman (1912–2006), economist, "The Social Responsibility of Business is to Increase its Profits", The New York Times Magazine, 13 September 1970 (paraphrase, with one short quotation):

  • A business has one social responsibility: to increase its profits, "so long as it stays within the rules of the game", that is, open competition without deception or fraud.
  • A manager is an employee of the owners. Spending their money on social causes they did not choose is like taxing them without their consent.
  • Social problems belong to governments, elected to solve them, not to unelected business managers.

Q1 What limits does Friedman place on the pursuit of profit?

Show answer

The rules of the game: the law, open and free competition, no deception or fraud.

Q2 Why does Friedman think CSR is unfair to shareholders?

Show answer

Managers would spend the owners' money on causes the owners did not choose, like a tax imposed without consent.

Quick check
?A supermarket's managers decide to pay every worker above the legal minimum wage, cutting the dividend paid to shareholders. How would Friedman judge this?
Mission 6

Whistle-blowing

  • Whistle-blowing: an employee discloses wrongdoing by the employer, to the employer itself (internal) or to the public, regulators or media (external).
  • The contract between employee and employer includes loyalty and confidentiality; whistle-blowing seems to break both.
  • But the employee is also a citizen, with duties to the public who may be harmed.
  • UK law: the Public Interest Disclosure Act 1998 protects workers from dismissal for disclosures in the public interest (e.g. crime, danger to health and safety, environmental damage, cover-ups).
  • Example: in 2001 Sherron Watkins, an executive at the US energy company Enron, warned its chairman of accounting fraud shortly before the company collapsed.
  • Costs to whistle-blowers can be severe: loss of career, isolation, legal battles.
Mission 6

When is whistle-blowing justified?

  • Richard De George (b. 1933), Business Ethics (1982 and later editions), gives conditions (paraphrase).
  • Permissible if: (1) the harm to the public is serious; (2) the employee has reported it to a superior; (3) internal routes have been tried and have failed.
  • Obligatory if also: (4) there is documented evidence that would convince a reasonable observer; (5) there is good reason to think going public will bring about change.
  • Ronald Duska, "Whistleblowing and Employee Loyalty" (1983): a company is not the kind of thing one owes loyalty to; it exists for profit. So whistle-blowing is not disloyal.
  • Critics of De George: insisting on internal routes first may let harm continue or expose the employee to reprisal.
Quick check
?An engineer finds that her company has hidden a safety fault in its cars. She reports it to her manager, who ignores her. She has test records. According to De George, what follows?
Mission 6

Good ethics is good business

  • The idea: good business decisions are good ethical decisions; ethical conduct brings trust, loyal customers, motivated staff and lower risk, and so profit.
  • Applied to shareholders: ethics protects long-term value; scandals destroy it.
  • Example: in 1982 Johnson & Johnson recalled all Tylenol capsules in the USA after poisonings by a third party; the costly recall is often credited with restoring trust in the brand.
  • Ethical investment: the FTSE4Good index series (from 2001) lists companies that meet environmental, social and governance (ESG) standards.
  • Questions: Do unethical firms sometimes profit for years? If ethics is chosen only because it pays, is it ethics or just prudence?
Mission 6

Globalisation and stakeholders

  • Globalisation: economies, industries, markets, cultures and policy-making around the world becoming integrated.
  • Customers: cheaper goods and wider choice.
  • Workers in poorer countries: new jobs and income; but also low pay, long hours and unsafe factories (sweatshops). The Rana Plaza factory collapse in Bangladesh (2013) killed more than 1,100 garment workers.
  • Workers and communities in richer countries: jobs moved abroad (offshoring).
  • Governments: compete for investment, with a risk of a "race to the bottom" in tax, wages and regulation; some multinationals are richer than many states.
  • After Rana Plaza, brands signed a binding safety Accord (2013): global pressure can also raise standards.

Biblical voices on business

> "You shall not oppress your neighbour, nor rob him. The wages of a hired servant shall not remain with you all night until the morning." (Leviticus 19:13, WEBBE)

> "Behold, the wages of the labourers who mowed your fields, which you have kept back by fraud, cry out" (James 5:4, WEBBE)

> "A false balance is an abomination to the LORD, but accurate weights are his delight." (Proverbs 11:1, WEBBE)

  • Catholic social teaching (Leo XIII, Rerum Novarum, 1891): workers are owed a just wage, enough to live in reasonable comfort (paraphrase).
  • Quaker firms (e.g. Cadbury's Bournville village, 1890s) built housing and schools for workers.

Show answer

Employees: they must be paid fairly and promptly.

Q2 Which business practice does Proverbs 11:1 condemn?

Show answer

Cheating customers with false measures: dishonest trading.

Mission 6Evaluate

Does globalisation encourage good ethics as good business?

  • Claim: Globalisation encourages the pursuit of good ethics as the foundation of good business.
  • For: Global media and campaigns expose abuses fast; reputation is global, so ethics pays (Rana Plaza Accord; FTSE4Good).
  • For: Trade has brought jobs and income to many poorer countries; global standards spread.
  • Against: Firms can move to where laws are weakest (race to the bottom); long supply chains hide abuses.
  • Against: Consumers want cheap goods; companies too large for any one government to control (Crane and Matten).
  • Judgement criteria: Who gains and who loses among stakeholders? Does global pressure change conduct or only image? Can national laws govern global firms?

Mission 7

Kant applied: the universal law test

  • Categorical imperative, first formulation: act only on a maxim you could will to be a universal law.
  • The lying promise (Kant's own example, Groundwork, 1785): borrowing with a promise you mean to break. Universalised, no one would trust promises; the maxim contradicts itself.
  • Business applications: false advertising, hidden fees, cooking the books, breaking contracts. All depend on others being honest; universalised, trade would collapse.
  • So business needs honesty and keeping contracts as perfect duties, whatever the profit.
  • Kant on motive: an act has moral worth only if done from duty, not just in line with duty.
Mission 7Read the source

Kant's honest dealer

Immanuel Kant (1724–1804), Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785), First Section (Abbott translation, 1873):

> "it is always a matter of duty that a dealer should not over charge an inexperienced purchaser; and wherever there is much commerce the prudent tradesman does not overcharge, but keeps a fixed price for everyone, so that a child buys of him as well as any other."

> "Men are thus honestly served; but this is not enough to make us believe that the tradesman has so acted from duty and from principles of honesty: his own advantage required it."

Q1 Does the dealer do the right thing?

Show answer

Yes: his action accords with duty; customers are honestly served.

Q2 Why does Kant deny that this act has moral worth?

Show answer

It was done from self-interest, not from duty: the dealer is honest only because honesty pays.

  • Link: "good ethics is good business" (see Mission 6) is the dealer's reasoning.
Mission 7

Kant applied: people as ends

  • Humanity formula: treat humanity, in yourself or others, "never as means only" (Abbott translation).
  • Employees: not just costs or tools; owed fair pay, safe work, honest dealing and respect for their autonomy.
  • Customers: not to be manipulated (e.g. advertising that exploits fears); informed consent to what they buy.
  • Kingdom of ends: act as a member of a community where each makes the rules for all; stakeholders as co-makers of the rules.
  • Norman Bowie, Business Ethics: A Kantian Perspective (1999): a Kantian firm offers meaningful work and treats itself as a moral community.
  • Problems: duties can clash (honesty to customers vs confidentiality to employer); Kant gives no way to rank them; profit itself is not a moral motive.
Quick check
?A firm keeps its prices fair only because it fears losing customers to rivals. What would Kant say?
Mission 7

Utilitarianism applied

  • Act utilitarianism (Bentham): weigh the pleasures and pains of all stakeholders affected (extent). Cost–benefit analysis is a business form of the hedonic calculus.
  • Profit can serve utility: jobs, useful products, pensions funded by shares.
  • Rule utilitarianism (Mill): rules such as honesty, keeping contracts, safety standards and whistle-blower protection maximise happiness when generally kept; trust is a huge public good.
  • Problems: could justify harming a minority if the majority gain (cheap goods made in unsafe factories); short-term pleasures vs long-term harms; costs to the environment and future people are hard to measure.
  • Notorious example: the Ford Pinto case (1970s): a Ford cost–benefit study putting a money value on deaths and injuries was widely reported as the reason a fuel-tank fix was not made (historians dispute the link).
  • Strength: utilitarianism and CSR both look at the consequences for every stakeholder.
Quick check
?A firm can save money by moving production to a factory where workers are paid very little. Customers would pay less. Which reasoning is distinctively rule utilitarian?
Mission 7

Is CSR hypocritical window-dressing?

  • The charge: CSR is "hypocritical window-dressing" covering the greed of a business intent on profit. The phrase is Friedman's own (1970): he called profit-serving acts labelled "social responsibility" hypocritical window-dressing.
  • Greenwashing: presenting a business as greener than it is. Example: Volkswagen advertised "clean diesel" while using software to cheat emissions tests (exposed 2015).
  • For the charge: CSR reports are written by the companies themselves; spending on image can exceed real change; Friedman: "the cloak of social responsibility" dresses up profit-seeking.
  • Against: much CSR brings real change (safer supply chains, cuts in emissions); independent standards (FTSE4Good) check claims.
  • Kant: CSR done only for reputation lacks moral worth (the dealer); deception in it is plainly wrong.
  • Utilitarian: motive does not matter if the outcome is good; hypocritical CSR that helps people still increases happiness.
Mission 7

Capitalism: freedom and prosperity

  • Capitalism: private ownership of businesses, markets and the pursuit of profit.
  • Adam Smith (1723–1790), The Wealth of Nations (1776): by pursuing their own interest, traders are led as if by an "invisible hand" to benefit society (paraphrase). He also wrote of sympathy and moral sense (The Theory of Moral Sentiments, 1759).
  • Friedman, Capitalism and Freedom (1962): economic freedom is a condition of political freedom.
  • For flourishing: capitalism has lifted many out of poverty; it rewards creativity and gives choice.
  • Against: inequality; insecurity of work; markets put a price on things (health, nature) that may not belong in markets.
Mission 7

Consumerism and flourishing

  • Consumerism: a culture in which buying and owning goods becomes central to identity and happiness.
  • Aristotle (Nicomachean Ethics I.5, Chase translation): "the life of money-making, it is one of constraint, and wealth manifestly is not the good we are seeking, because it is for use". Eudaimonia (flourishing) lies in virtuous activity, not possessions.
  • Karl Marx (1818–1883), Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts (1844): under capitalism workers are alienated from their work, its products and each other.
  • Erich Fromm (1900–1980), To Have or to Be? (1976): consumer society trains people to find identity in having rather than being.
  • Christian voices: "You can't serve both God and Mammon" (Matthew 6:24, WEBBE); "the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil" (1 Timothy 6:10, WEBBE). Catholic teaching (John Paul II, Centesimus Annus, 1991) accepts a free economy within law and solidarity but warns against consumerism (paraphrase).
Mission 7Evaluate

Can humans flourish under capitalism and consumerism?

  • Claim: Human beings can flourish in the context of capitalism and consumerism.
  • For: Prosperity, health and education have grown in market economies; choice expresses freedom (Friedman); trade needs and rewards virtues such as honesty and trust (Smith).
  • For: Kant and rule utilitarians can be built into business (Bowie; ethical rules, CSR).
  • Against: Alienating work (Marx); identity reduced to having (Fromm); wealth is only a means (Aristotle).
  • Against: Consumer demand drives environmental damage and exploitation in supply chains (see Mission 6).
  • Judgement criteria: What is flourishing: wealth and choice, or virtue and relationships? Is the problem capitalism itself or its abuses? Can ethics be built in, or only bolted on?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Utilitarianism is teleological and consequentialist: the right act produces the greatest happiness of the greatest number. For Bentham, pleasure is the only good, measured by quantity.
  • The hedonic calculus: intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity, extent. Act utilitarianism judges each act afresh (relativist).

Mission 2

  • Mill: pleasures differ in quality; higher (mental) pleasures outweigh lower (bodily) ones, by the verdict of competent judges.
  • Harm principle: power over a person is justified only to prevent harm to others. Rule utilitarianism (strong and weak) follows rules that maximise happiness; critics say it collapses into act utilitarianism or becomes rule worship.

Mission 3

  • Preference utilitarianism (Hare, Singer) satisfies interests, and animals count. Evaluation: measurement, prediction, justice for minorities, no act wrong in itself; rule and preference forms reply in part.

Mission 4

  • Euthanasia can be active or passive, and voluntary, non-voluntary or involuntary; in assisted suicide the person takes the last act.
  • Sanctity of life (religious: life is God-given and bears God's image) and quality of life (secular: life needs certain attributes to have value) give different answers; autonomy adds the person's own choice.

Mission 5

  • Natural law forbids directly ending innocent life but, through double effect and proportionate means, allows strong pain relief and refusal of burdensome treatment.
  • Situation ethics (Fletcher) can support voluntary euthanasia out of love; Rachels and Glover reject the moral weight of acts versus omissions; slippery-slope and trust arguments concern the law, not only the single case.

Mission 6

  • Shareholders own a company; stakeholders (employees, customers, community, country, governments) are affected by it. CSR says a business owes responsibility to the community and environment; Friedman says its one responsibility is profit within the rules.
  • Whistle-blowing tests the contract of loyalty; De George sets conditions for when it is permissible and obligatory.
  • "Good ethics is good business" says ethics pays; globalisation can spread both abuses and higher standards.

Mission 7

  • Kant: business needs honesty and promise-keeping (universal law) and respect for people as ends; acts done only because they pay lack moral worth (the dealer).
  • Utilitarianism weighs consequences for all stakeholders; rule utilitarianism supports trust-building rules; both can allow harm to minorities.
  • Debates: CSR as window-dressing (motive vs outcome); flourishing under capitalism (Smith, Friedman) or alienation and having (Marx, Fromm, Aristotle, Christian teaching).
