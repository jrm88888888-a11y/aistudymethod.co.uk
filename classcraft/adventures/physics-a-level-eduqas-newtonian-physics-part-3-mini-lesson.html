Mission 1

Mission 1: Hold a heavy bag still for ten minutes and your arms ache. How much work have you done on the bag?

Make a guess: Hold a heavy bag still for ten minutes and your arms ache. How much work have you done on the bag?

Mission 1

Work is energy transferred

  • A force does work when its point of application moves in the direction of the force.
  • Work done = force × distance moved in the direction of the force.
  • Work done = energy transferred. Both in joules.
  • 1 J = the work done when a force of 1 N moves its point of application 1 m in its own direction.
Mission 1

A force at an angle

  • Only the component along the displacement does work.
  • W = Fx cos θ, θ = angle between the force and the displacement.
  • θ = 0: W = Fx. θ = 90°: W = 0. The force changes only the direction of motion.
Quick check
?A child pulls a sledge 25 m along level snow using a rope held at 30° above the horizontal. The tension in the rope is a constant 80 N. How much work does the tension do on the sledge?
Mission 1

Positive, negative or zero

  • Work is a scalar. Its sign shows which way energy goes.
  • θ < 90°: positive work. The force transfers energy to the object.
  • θ > 90°: negative work. The force takes energy away: friction, braking.
  • θ = 90°: zero. A crate slides along a level floor: its weight and the normal force do no work.
  • No displacement: zero. Holding a load still does no work on it.
Quick check
?A worker uses a rope to lower a box straight down at constant speed. Which statement about the work done on the box during the descent is correct?
Mission 1

Worked example: pushing a crate

  • A 150 N push at 40° below the horizontal moves a crate 6.0 m along a level floor. Friction is 90 N.
  • Push: W = 150 × 6.0 × cos 40° = +690 J.
  • Friction (θ = 180°): W = 90 × 6.0 × cos 180° = −540 J.
  • Weight and normal force (θ = 90°): 0 J each.
  • Each force's work is found separately. Use the angle between that force and the displacement.
Mission check

?A student carries a bag of weight 15 N up a flight of stairs at constant velocity. The bag moves 4.0 m horizontally and rises 3.0 m, so it travels 5.0 m along the stairs. How much work does the student's upward force do on the bag?

Work needs a displacement along the force. The held bag does not move, so you do no work on it. Your arms ache because your muscles use energy internally to keep exerting the force.

Mission 2: Two smooth slides drop the same height: one steep, one gentle. Which gives you more speed at the bottom?

Make a guess: Two smooth slides drop the same height: one steep, one gentle. Which gives you more speed at the bottom?

Mission 2

Kinetic energy

  • Kinetic energy: the energy an object has because it is moving.
  • E = ½mv² (kinetic energy, KE)
  • A scalar: never negative; no direction.
  • Double the mass → double the kinetic energy. Double the speed → four times the kinetic energy.
Mission 2

Gravitational potential energy

  • Gravitational potential energy: energy due to position in a gravitational field.
  • ΔE = mgΔh (change in gravitational potential energy)
  • Valid near the surface, where g is roughly constant.
Quick check
?Object A has mass m and moves at speed 2v. Object B has mass 2m and moves at speed v. What is the kinetic energy of object A divided by the kinetic energy of object B?
Mission 2

Elastic potential energy

  • A stretched or compressed spring stores elastic potential energy.
  • Hooke's law: F = kx, where k is the spring constant (force per unit extension).
  • E = ½kx² (elastic PE), x = extension or compression.
  • It is the area under the force–extension line (a triangle), while the line stays straight (up to the limit of proportionality).
Mission 2

Conservation of energy

  • Energy cannot be created or destroyed.
  • It is transferred between stores.
  • Stores include kinetic, gravitational, elastic, internal (thermal), chemical and nuclear.
  • No friction or drag: mechanical energy stays constant. Loss of potential energy = gain of kinetic energy.
  • With friction or drag: some becomes internal energy and sound. The total is still conserved.
Mission 2

Worked example: a falling ball

  • A ball is dropped from 1.8 m. Air resistance is negligible.
  • Loss of GPE = gain of KE: mgh = ½mv². m cancels: v = √(2gh).
  • v = √(2 × 9.81 × 1.8) = 5.9 m s⁻¹.
  • Only the vertical drop matters. A smooth slide of any shape, same drop: the same speed.
Quick check
?A child slides from rest down a straight slide with negligible friction and reaches a speed v at the bottom. A second straight slide has the same steepness, but its top is four times as high above its bottom. What speed does the child reach at the bottom of the second slide?
Mission 2

Worked example: a spring launcher

  • A spring (k = 400 N m⁻¹) is compressed 0.050 m, then fires a 0.020 kg ball along a smooth, level track.
  • Energy stored = ½ × 400 × 0.050² = 0.50 J.
  • All of it becomes kinetic energy: ½ × 0.020 × v² = 0.50 → v = 7.1 m s⁻¹.
  • Fired straight up instead: elastic PE → KE → GPE. At the top, ½kx² = mgΔh.
Quick check
?A spring of spring constant 250 N m⁻¹ is compressed by 0.080 m and then released. It launches a block of mass 0.40 kg along a smooth horizontal track. All the elastic potential energy becomes the block's kinetic energy. What is the speed of the block?
Mission check

?Three identical stones are thrown from the top of a sea cliff with the same speed: one straight up, one horizontally and one straight down. Air resistance is negligible. Which stone hits the sea with the greatest speed?

Only the drop in height matters, not the path. Two smooth slides of the same height give the same speed at the bottom; the gentle one just takes longer.

Mission 3

Momentum

  • Momentum p = mv: mass × velocity.
  • Unit: kg m s⁻¹ (the same as N s).
  • A vector: same direction as the velocity. Choose a positive direction; the other way is negative.
  • A 0.20 kg ball hits a wall at 5.0 m s⁻¹ and bounces straight back at 5.0 m s⁻¹. Its momentum changes, from +1.0 to −1.0 kg m s⁻¹, though its speed does not.
Quick check
?A cricket ball of mass m moving horizontally with speed v is struck by a bat. It leaves the bat moving horizontally with speed 3v/2 in the opposite direction. Taking the ball's initial direction of motion as positive, what is the change in the ball's momentum?
Mission 3

Conservation of momentum

  • The total momentum of a system stays constant unless a resultant external force acts on it.
  • Why: in a collision, A pushes B and B pushes A with equal and opposite forces (Newton's third law), for the same time.
  • So A's change in momentum is equal and opposite to B's. The total does not change.
  • Total momentum before = total momentum after. Add the momenta with signs.
Mission 3

Worked example: a collision

  • Glider A, 1.5 kg, moves at 4.0 m s⁻¹ along an air track into glider B, 0.50 kg, at rest. They stick together.
  • Before: p = 1.5 × 4.0 + 0.50 × 0 = 6.0 kg m s⁻¹.
  • After: (1.5 + 0.50) × v = 2.0v.
  • 2.0v = 6.0, so v = 3.0 m s⁻¹, in A's original direction.
Mission 3

Explosions

  • Everything starts at rest: total momentum = 0. So it is still 0 afterwards.
  • Two pieces: they move off in opposite directions, with equal and opposite momenta.
  • 2.0 kg and 3.0 kg blocks pushed apart by a spring. The 3.0 kg block moves at 1.2 m s⁻¹.
  • 2.0 × v = 3.0 × 1.2 = 3.6, so v = 1.8 m s⁻¹ the other way. Lighter piece: faster.
  • Kinetic energy increases (0 → 5.4 J): it comes from energy stored in the compressed spring (or in the explosive, for a cannon).
Quick check
?Two blocks, of masses 1.0 kg and 4.0 kg, are held at rest on a horizontal surface with negligible friction, with a compressed spring between them. When they are released, the 1.0 kg block moves off at 2.0 m s⁻¹. Which statement about the 4.0 kg block is correct?
Mission 3

Elastic or inelastic?

  • Kinetic energy Eₖ = ½mv².
  • Momentum is conserved in every collision (no resultant external force). Kinetic energy is not always.
  • Elastic: total kinetic energy unchanged.
  • Inelastic: total kinetic energy decreases. It becomes internal (thermal) energy, sound, and energy of permanent deformation.
  • Perfectly inelastic: the bodies stick together and share one velocity. Biggest loss of kinetic energy.
  • Total energy is always conserved; only the kinetic part changes.
  • Test it with the glider collision: kinetic energy before = ½ × 1.5 × 4.0² = 12 J; after = ½ × 2.0 × 3.0² = 9.0 J. 3.0 J transformed into other forms: inelastic.
Quick check
?A 0.50 kg trolley moving at 5.0 m s⁻¹ collides with a 3.0 kg trolley at rest on a level track with negligible friction. After the collision, the 0.50 kg trolley moves backwards at 1.0 m s⁻¹ and the 3.0 kg trolley moves forwards at 1.0 m s⁻¹. How much of the system's kinetic energy is transformed into other forms of energy?
Mission check

?A person of mass 60 kg stands in a rowing boat of mass 120 kg. Both are at rest, and water resistance is negligible. The person jumps horizontally from the boat onto a jetty, leaving at 1.0 m s⁻¹. Which statement about the boat immediately afterwards is correct?

Total momentum starts at zero, so it stays zero: equal and opposite momenta. The cannon recoils for the same reason: same mv as the ball, much bigger m, so a much smaller v.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why can a stunt performer walk away from a fall onto an airbag that would kill on concrete?

Make a guess: Why can a stunt performer walk away from a fall onto an airbag that would kill on concrete?

Mission 4

Force is the rate of change of momentum

  • F = Δp/Δt: resultant force = rate of change of momentum.
  • Constant mass: Δp/Δt = mΔv/Δt = ma. F = ma is the special case.
Mission 4

Impulse

  • From F = Δp/Δt: Ft = mv − mu (constant mass).
  • Unit: N s, the same as kg m s⁻¹.
  • A vector: in the direction of the resultant force, which need not be the direction of motion.
Mission 4

Worked example: a rebound

  • A 0.060 kg ball hits a wall at 25 m s⁻¹ and rebounds at 15 m s⁻¹. Contact time 0.020 s.
  • Take away from the wall as positive.
  • u = −25 m s⁻¹, v = +15 m s⁻¹.
  • Δp = m(v − u) = 0.060 × (15 − (−25)) = 2.4 N s, away from the wall.
  • Average force = 2.4 ÷ 0.020 = 120 N, away from the wall.
  • Trap: 0.060 × (15 − 25) = −0.60 N s. Velocity is a vector; the direction reverses.
Mission 4

Longer contact, smaller force

  • Stopping: the change in momentum is fixed by the mass and the speed.
  • Longer stopping time → smaller average force.
  • A 70 kg occupant stops from 14 m s⁻¹: Δp = 980 N s.
  • Seat belt and crumple zone, 0.12 s: F ≈ 8.2 kN. Hard dashboard, 0.012 s: F ≈ 82 kN.
  • Same idea: airbags, bicycle helmets, packaging foam, bending your knees on landing.
Mission check

?Two identical 0.50 kg gliders on an air track each move at 2.0 m s⁻¹ towards an obstacle. Glider 1 hits a steel buffer and bounces straight back at 2.0 m s⁻¹; the contact lasts 0.010 s. Glider 2 hits a foam pad and stops; the contact lasts 0.040 s. How many times larger is the average force on glider 1 than the average force on glider 2?

Same change in momentum, longer time, smaller force. The airbag stops the performer over a much longer time than concrete, so the average force is many times smaller.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Double a car's speed. How much longer is its braking distance?

Make a guess: Double a car's speed. How much longer is its braking distance?

Mission 5

Net work changes kinetic energy

  • The work done by the resultant force = the change in kinetic energy.
  • Fx = ½mv² − ½mu², F the resultant force.
  • Net work positive: speeds up. Negative: slows down. Zero: constant speed.
Mission 5

Worked example: braking

  • A 1200 kg car at 20 m s⁻¹ brakes with a constant 8000 N resultant force.
  • Final KE = 0, so F × x = ½mu².
  • x = ½ × 1200 × 20² ÷ 8000 = 30 m.
  • At 40 m s⁻¹: x = ½ × 1200 × 40² ÷ 8000 = 120 m.
  • Braking distance ∝ u²: double the speed, four times the distance.
Quick check
?A child on a toboggan slides across level snow. The child and toboggan have a total mass of 50 kg and slow down from 8.0 m s⁻¹ to 6.0 m s⁻¹. What is the magnitude of the net work done on the child–toboggan system during this time?
Mission 5

Friction and drag dissipate energy

  • Friction and drag do negative work on a moving object.
  • That mechanical energy becomes internal (thermal) energy and sound. The total is still conserved.
  • Loss of GPE = gain of KE + work done against resistive forces.
  • Dissipative forces reduce efficiency: less of the input ends up as useful output.
Mission 5

Worked example: a toboggan run

  • A 50 kg toboggan and rider start from rest. They descend 12 m vertically along a 40 m slope and reach 12 m s⁻¹.
  • GPE lost = 50 × 9.81 × 12 = 5890 J. KE gained = ½ × 50 × 12² = 3600 J.
  • Work done against resistive forces = 5890 − 3600 ≈ 2.3 kJ. Average resistive force = 2290 ÷ 40 ≈ 57 N.
Quick check
?A skier of mass 60 kg starts from rest at the top of a slope and descends through a vertical height of 20 m. At the bottom her speed is 16 m s⁻¹. Take g = 9.81 m s⁻². What is the total work done on the skier by friction and air resistance during the descent?
Mission check

?A block on a level, frictionless surface is sped up by a constant horizontal force. The work needed to speed the block up from rest to speed v is W. How many times W is needed to speed the block up from v to 3v?

Work changes kinetic energy, and kinetic energy ∝ v². Braking from twice the speed removes four times the KE, so the same braking force needs four times the distance.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Identical twins climb the same stairs, one running, one walking. Who does more work? Who is more powerful?

Make a guess: Identical twins climb the same stairs, one running, one walking. Who does more work? Who is more powerful?

Mission 6

Power

  • Power = rate of doing work = rate of energy transfer.
  • P = W/t
  • Unit: the watt. 1 W = 1 J s⁻¹.
  • Same work in half the time: twice the power.
Mission 6

Worked example: climbing stairs

  • A 60 kg student climbs stairs 3.0 m high in 4.0 s.
  • Work done = mgΔh = 60 × 9.81 × 3.0 = 1.8 kJ (1766 J).
  • Power = 1766 ÷ 4.0 = 440 W.
  • Walk up in 8.0 s: the same work, half the power.
Mission 6

Efficiency

  • Efficiency = useful energy output ÷ total energy input.
  • Or: useful power output ÷ total power input.
  • Multiply by 100 for a percentage.
  • Always less than 100%. The rest is wasted, mostly as thermal energy in the surroundings.
Mission 6

Worked example: a motor

  • An electric motor takes 200 W. It lifts a 20 kg load 3.0 m in 5.0 s.
  • Useful power = mgΔh/t = 20 × 9.81 × 3.0 ÷ 5.0 = 118 W.
  • Efficiency = 118 ÷ 200 = 0.59, or 59%.
  • Wasted power = 200 − 118 = 82 W, mostly heating the motor and its surroundings.
Quick check
?A Sankey diagram for a car engine has an input arrow representing 200 kJ of chemical energy from the fuel. It divides into four output arrows: 60 kJ of useful mechanical work, 100 kJ of thermal energy carried away by the exhaust gases, 30 kJ of thermal energy removed by the cooling system, and 10 kJ of sound. What is the efficiency of the engine?
Mission check

?Motor A raises a 200 N load through 3.0 m in 6.0 s. Motor B raises a 100 N load through 6.0 m in 4.0 s. Both lift at constant speed. Which statement is correct?

Work depends on force and distance, not time. The runner and the walker do the same work against gravity; the runner does it faster, so develops more power.

Mission 7

Mission 7: A car rounds a bend at a steady speed. Is it accelerating, and if so, how much?

Make a guess: A car rounds a bend at a steady speed. Is it accelerating, and if so, how much?

Mission 7

The radian

  • Angle in radians: θ = arc length ÷ radius = s/r.
  • 1 radian: the angle at the centre when the arc length equals the radius.
  • Full circle: s = 2πr, so 2π rad = 360°. 1 rad ≈ 57.3°.
  • Convert: degrees × π/180 → radians.
Mission 7

Period, frequency, speed

  • Period T: time for one full revolution (s).
  • Frequency f: revolutions per second (Hz). f = 1/T.
  • One revolution = one circumference, 2πr. Constant speed: v = 2πr/T.
Mission 7

Angular velocity

  • Angular velocity ω: angle swept per second, in rad s⁻¹.
  • One revolution sweeps 2π rad in time T: ω = 2π/T = 2πf.
  • v = ωr.
  • Every point on a rigid turning object has the same ω. Points further out have larger v.
  • ω is also used for simple harmonic motion (2πf there too).
Quick check
?A bicycle wheel of diameter 0.700 m rotates at a steady rate about a fixed axle, making one complete revolution every 0.250 s. What is the linear speed of a point on the rim of the wheel?
Mission 7

Steady speed, changing velocity

  • Velocity is a vector. Going around a circle, its direction keeps changing.
  • Changing velocity = acceleration, even at constant speed.
  • Draw v1 and v2 at two nearby points. The change, Δv = v2 − v1, points towards the centre.
  • So the acceleration points to the centre: centripetal acceleration.
  • It needs a resultant force towards the centre.
Mission 7

Centripetal acceleration

  • a = v²/r = ω²r, towards the centre.
  • Example: a child on a spinning playground ride, 1.5 m from the centre, one revolution every 4.0 s.
  • v = 2πr/T = 2π × 1.5 ÷ 4.0 = 2.4 m s⁻¹.
  • ω = 2π/T = 2π ÷ 4.0 = 1.6 rad s⁻¹.
  • a = v²/r = 2.36² ÷ 1.5 = 3.7 m s⁻², towards the centre.
  • Check: ω²r = 1.57² × 1.5 = 3.7 m s⁻².
Quick check
?Car X rounds a curve of radius 50 m and car Y rounds a curve of radius 100 m. Both cars move at the same constant speed. How does the magnitude of car X's centripetal acceleration compare with that of car Y?
Mission 7

Recap: three ways to write it

  • Given v and r: a = v²/r.
  • Given ω (or f) and r: a = ω²r, with ω = 2πf.
  • Given T and r: ω = 2π/T, so a = 4π²r/T².
  • Always use the radius, not the diameter. ω in rad s⁻¹, not degrees per second.
Mission check

?The drum of a washing machine has a diameter of 0.50 m. It rotates about a horizontal axis at constant speed, with a time period of 0.050 s. What is the centripetal acceleration of a sock pressed against the inside of the drum?

Constant speed is not constant velocity. The sock's direction keeps changing, so it accelerates towards the centre of the drum, with a = v²/r.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • W = Fx cos θ: only the component along the displacement does work; work done = energy transferred.

Mission 2

  • E = ½mv² (kinetic energy); ΔE = mgΔh (gravitational PE). Without resistive forces, PE lost = KE gained: only the height dropped matters.
  • Elastic PE = ½kx² joins the energy total.

Mission 3

  • p = mv, a vector. With no resultant external force, total momentum is conserved in every collision and explosion.
  • Elastic: total Eₖ unchanged. Inelastic: total Eₖ decreases. Explosions: Eₖ increases from a stored energy.

Mission 4

  • F = Δp/Δt: for the same change in momentum, a longer contact time means a smaller average force.

Mission 5

  • Fx = ½mv² − ½mu²; dissipative forces reduce efficiency.
  • Friction and drag turn mechanical energy into internal energy; total energy is conserved.

Mission 6

  • P = W/t; efficiency = useful output ÷ total input (energy or power).

Mission 7

  • ω = 2π/T = 2πf (rad s⁻¹); v = ωr.
  • Constant speed in a circle means acceleration towards the centre: a = v²/r.
  • Also a = ω²r.
