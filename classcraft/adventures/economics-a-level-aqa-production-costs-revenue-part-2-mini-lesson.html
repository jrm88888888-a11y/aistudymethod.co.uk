Mission 1

Barriers to entry

  • A barrier to entry is anything that makes it hard for new firms to enter a market.
TypeExamples
LegalPatents, licences, the sole right to run a service (e.g. a local water or power network)
CostEconomies of scale, high start-up costs, sunk costs (costs that cannot be recovered on leaving)

Quick check
?Building a new steel works costs billions. The two existing producers each make so much steel that their average cost is far below what any new, smaller rival could achieve. Which barrier to entry does this describe?
Mission 1

Minimum efficient scale and the number of firms

  • Minimum efficient scale (MES): the lowest output at which long-run average cost reaches its minimum.
  • Many industries have an L-shaped LRAC: cost falls until MES, then stays flat, so firms of any size above MES are equally efficient.
  • Compare MES with the size of the market:
  • MES small relative to market demand → room for many firms → competitive structure.
  • MES large → room for only a few firms → oligopoly.
  • MES close to the whole market → one firm can supply it most cheaply → natural monopoly.

Mission 1

Concentration ratios

  • Market share = firm's sales ÷ total market sales × 100.
  • n-firm concentration ratio = combined market share of the n largest firms.
  • Great Britain grocery shares (Kantar, 12 weeks to 2 November 2025): Tesco 28.2%, Sainsbury's 15.7%, Asda 11.6%, Aldi 10.6%, Morrisons 8.3%.
  • 4-firm CR = 28.2 + 15.7 + 11.6 + 10.6 = 66.1%.
  • 5-firm CR = 66.1 + 8.3 = 74.4%.
  • A high ratio suggests oligopoly. There is no single official cut-off, so always state n.
  • Limits: the result depends on how the market is defined (all groceries or one product? national or local?). It does not show whether the top firms are equal in size or one dominates.

Mission 1

Structure is not conduct

  • Market structure: the number and size of firms, measured by the concentration ratio.
  • Market conduct: how firms actually behave: price wars, collusion, advertising, innovation.
  • Two markets with the same concentration ratio can behave very differently: one fiercely competitive, one quietly colluding.
  • Interdependence: in an oligopoly each firm's best price or output depends on how rivals react, so firms face uncertainty.
  • Say: "this market is highly concentrated" — not: "this market is uncompetitive", until you have evidence on conduct.
Mission check

?In which market are existing firms most likely to keep earning more than normal profit in the long run?

Profit above normal survives only where something stops new firms from entering.

Mission 2

Mission 2: If a seller must cut its price to sell one more, why does that extra sale bring in less than the price?

Mission 2

Three measures of revenue

  • Total revenue (TR) = price × quantity sold.
  • Average revenue (AR) = TR ÷ quantity. With one price for all units, AR = price.
  • Marginal revenue (MR) = the change in TR from selling one more unit: MR = ΔTR ÷ ΔQ.
  • Say: "marginal revenue is the extra revenue from one more unit" — not: "the price of one more unit".

Mission 2

Why MR is below price

  • To sell a 3rd unit, the stall cuts its price from 8 to 6, for every buyer.
  • Gain: the 3rd unit sells for +6.
  • Loss: the first 2 units now earn 2 less each: 2 × 2 = −4.
  • MR = 6 − 4 = 2, below the price of 6.
  • So when a firm must lower its price to sell more, MR < AR for every unit after the first.
  • Say: "AR is the demand curve; MR lies below it" — not: "MR equals the price".
Quick check
?A firm charges one price to all buyers. At a price of 15 it sells 20 units a week. At a price of 14 it sells 22 units (illustrative data). What is the marginal revenue per extra unit?
Mission 2

A price taker

  • In a perfectly competitive market, a firm is a price taker: it can sell as much as it likes at the market price.
  • It never has to cut its price to sell more, so each extra unit adds exactly the price to TR.
  • So P = AR = MR, all one horizontal line.
  • TR is a straight line from the origin, rising by the price with each unit.
  • Illustrative data: price 5 → TR 5, 10, 15, 20 …; MR = 5 every time.
Mission 2

Drawing the curves

  • AR = D: the downward-sloping demand curve.
  • With a straight-line demand curve, MR starts at the same point on the price axis and is twice as steep.
  • MR meets the quantity axis halfway to where AR does.
  • TR is an arch: it rises while MR > 0, peaks where MR = 0, and falls once MR < 0.
  • Say: "TR is maximised where MR = 0" — not: "where AR = 0", where TR is zero.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why might a business that brings in money every year still be making no real profit?

Make a guess: Why might a business that brings in money every year still be making no real profit?

Mission 3

What profit is

  • Profit = total revenue − total costs.
  • Profit = TR − TC.
  • Total costs means the opportunity cost of every resource the firm uses.
  • Some costs are paid out in money. Some are not. Both count.
Mission 3

Explicit and implicit costs

  • Explicit costs: money actually paid out. Wages, rent, materials, energy, interest on loans.
  • Implicit costs: the value of the owner's own resources used in the business. No payment is made.
  • Examples: the salary the owner gives up by not working elsewhere; the interest her own savings could have earned; the rent her own building could have earned.
  • Accounting profit = TR − explicit costs.
  • Economic profit = TR − explicit costs − implicit costs.
  • Economists treat normal profit as a cost: the reward the owner must get to keep her resources in this business.
  • Say: "accounting profit ignores implicit costs" — not: "accounting profit is wrong". It answers a different question.
Mission 3

Worked example: the café

  • Illustrative data. A café, one year. Figures per year.
  • Total revenue: 120,000.
  • Explicit costs (food, staff, rent, energy): 85,000.
  • Accounting profit = 120,000 − 85,000 = 35,000.
  • Implicit costs: the owner gives up a salary of 30,000 as a manager elsewhere. She put 100,000 of her savings in; at 5% interest they would earn 100,000 × 0.05 = 5,000.
  • Implicit costs = 30,000 + 5,000 = 35,000.
  • Economic profit = 35,000 − 35,000 = 0.
  • The accounts show a profit. Yet she would do just as well elsewhere. That is normal profit.
Mission 3

Normal, supernormal and subnormal profit

  • Normal profit: the minimum profit needed to keep the firm in its current use. TR = TC, so AR = AC.
  • Supernormal profit: profit above normal profit. TR > TC, so AR > AC.
  • Subnormal profit (a loss): profit below normal profit. TR < TC, so AR < AC.
  • Here costs include normal profit. So TR = TC is not "no money": it is exactly enough.
  • Say: "the firm earns normal profit" — not: "the firm makes no profit".
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A designer runs her own studio. Last year her revenue was £200,000 and her explicit costs were £150,000. She gave up a salary of £45,000 to run it, and she put £80,000 of her savings into the business, which could have earned 5% interest. What kind of profit did the studio earn?
Mission 3

Profit on a cost diagram

  • Profit per unit = AR − AC.
  • Total profit = (AR − AC) × Q: a rectangle.
  • Illustrative data, Q = 100, AC = 9:
  • AR = 12: profit = (12 − 9) × 100 = 300. Supernormal.
  • AR = 9: profit = 0. Normal profit.
  • AR = 8: profit = (8 − 9) × 100 = −100. A loss.
  • (Which output a firm picks comes in a later lesson.)

Quick check
?Illustrative data. A diagram shows a firm producing 50 units. At this output its average revenue is 20 and its average cost is 16. What is the area of the profit rectangle?
Mission 3

Why profit matters in a market economy

  • Signal: supernormal profit shows buyers want more. Resources move in. Losses show they want less. Resources move out.
  • Firms enter in search of supernormal profit and leave when they make subnormal profit.
  • Incentive: profit rewards risk-taking, effort and new ideas.
  • Source of finance: retained profit pays for investment and research.
  • Normal profit is the minimum that keeps resources in their current use.
Mission 3Evaluate

Is supernormal profit good or bad?

It depends on …

  • Barriers to entry: with free entry, new firms compete it away. Behind high barriers it can last, with higher prices for buyers.
  • Where it comes from: a better product or lower costs, or market power?
  • How it is used: reinvested in research and new products (dynamic efficiency), or paid out while prices stay high.
  • Time: a short-run reward for risk is different from a profit that lasts for decades.
  • Judge: supernormal profit is a useful signal and reward, but it also needs watching when it persists.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. A software engineer leaves a job paying 60,000 a year to build her own app business. In its first year the app earns revenue of 90,000. She pays 50,000 for servers, design and marketing. She has no other costs. Which statement is correct?

Revenue has to cover the owner's best alternative too. Until it does, the business is not earning a real profit.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Can a new idea that wipes out whole industries still leave an economy better off?

Make a guess: A new technology wipes out an old industry. What happens to the economy as a whole?

Mission 4

Invention and innovation

  • Invention: creating a new idea, product or process, often protected by a patent.
  • Innovation: turning an invention into something used commercially: a product people buy, or a method firms adopt.
  • Many inventions are never innovated; the gain to the economy comes from innovation.
  • Research and development (R&D): spending on finding and developing new products and processes.
  • Example: a Kodak engineer built the first digital camera in 1975, but the firm's main business stayed camera film for decades.
  • Say: "invention creates the idea; innovation brings it to market" — not: using the two words as if they mean the same.
Mission 4

Product and process innovation

  • Product innovation: a new or improved good or service (e.g. the smartphone, streaming services).
  • Process innovation: a new or better way of producing, which raises productivity (output per worker, or per hour worked) and cuts average cost.
  • Example: on 1 December 1913 Henry Ford installed a moving assembly line. The time to build a car fell from more than 12 hours to 1 hour 33 minutes.
  • Technological change shifts the LRAC curve down: lower cost at every output.
  • Say: "technological change shifts the LRAC downwards" — not: "it is an economy of scale", which is a movement along the LRAC as output grows.
Mission 4

Worked example: productivity and cost

A bakery employs 20 workers, each paid 100 a day (illustrative data).

  • Before: 400 loaves a day. Productivity = 400 ÷ 20 = 20 loaves per worker.
  • Labour cost per loaf = (20 × 100) ÷ 400 = 2,000 ÷ 400 = 5.
  • A new oven (process innovation) lets the same 20 workers make 500 loaves.
  • Productivity = 500 ÷ 20 = 25 loaves per worker: up (25 − 20) ÷ 20 × 100 = 25%.
  • Labour cost per loaf = 2,000 ÷ 500 = 4: down (4 − 5) ÷ 5 × 100 = −20%.
  • The firm can cut its price, raise its profit margin, or both.

Quick check
?A university lab develops a new battery that stores twice as much energy. Five years later a car maker starts selling cars that use it. Which statement is correct?
Mission 4

Creative destruction

  • Creative destruction (Joseph Schumpeter, 1942): innovation creates new products, firms and markets and destroys old ones.
  • Example: digital photography and then smartphone cameras replaced film. Kodak, long the giant of camera film, filed for bankruptcy protection in January 2012.
  • Effects on market structure:
  • It can break monopoly power: a newcomer with a better product can overtake a dominant firm that seemed safe behind its barriers to entry.
  • It rewards the innovator with temporary supernormal profit, until others copy or leapfrog it.
  • It can also create new dominant firms, especially in digital markets.
  • Dynamics of competition: in the short run, rivalry brings lower prices and better products; in the long run, the threat of being replaced drives firms to keep innovating, often through non-price competition (new features, quality, design).
Quick check
?Streaming services spread quickly. Many shops renting out films on disc closed, while new firms making shows for streaming grew fast. How is this best described?
Mission 4

Does market power help innovation?

  • For (Schumpeter): firms with market power earn supernormal (abnormal) profit, which can finance R&D. Large firms can also bear the risk of failed projects.
  • Patents give an innovator a temporary monopoly (usually up to 20 years), so it can earn the profit that rewards its R&D.
  • Against: a monopolist that already earns high profit may feel little pressure to innovate. In competitive markets, firms must innovate or lose customers.
  • Oligopolies often compete through innovation and other non-price competition. Perfectly competitive firms earn only normal profit in the long run and new ideas are copied at once, so they have little means or reason to do R&D.
  • Static efficiency: allocative and productive efficiency at a point in time. Dynamic efficiency: improvements over time from R&D, investment and new technology. A market can be statically inefficient but dynamically efficient, or the reverse.
Quick check
?Which statement best supports the view that large firms with market power can benefit consumers through innovation?
Mission 4Evaluate

Is creative destruction worth it?

  • Consumers gain new and cheaper products; displaced workers and owners lose jobs and capital.
  • It depends on time: job losses come first; higher productivity and new jobs may take years.
  • It depends on mobility: workers whose skills fit the old industry may stay unemployed (structural unemployment) unless they retrain or move.
  • It depends on what follows: if new dominant firms build high barriers, competition may weaken until the next wave.
  • It depends on policy: patent length, competition policy and support for retraining shape both the gains and the losses.
Mission check

?Banking apps spread through a country. Banks close most of their branches and many branch staff lose their jobs, while new firms that build payment software grow fast. Which judgement of the effect on the economy is best supported?

Creative destruction can raise output over time, but its gains and its losses fall on different people.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Market structures range from perfect competition to monopoly, by number of firms, product differentiation, barriers to entry and control over price.
  • Barriers to entry (legal, cost, market, physical) let firms keep supernormal profit in the long run.
  • n-firm concentration ratio = combined market share of the n largest firms.
  • The larger the minimum efficient scale relative to the market, the fewer firms it can hold.

Mission 2

  • TR = P × Q; AR = TR ÷ Q = price, so the AR curve is the demand curve; MR = ΔTR ÷ ΔQ.
  • When a firm must cut its price to sell more, MR is below AR; a price taker has P = AR = MR.
  • TR is highest where MR = 0, at unit elasticity; MR is positive where demand is elastic and negative where it is inelastic.

Mission 3

  • Accounting profit = TR − explicit costs; economic profit also subtracts implicit costs (opportunity costs of the owner's own resources).
  • Normal profit: AR = AC. Supernormal (abnormal) profit: AR > AC. Subnormal profit (loss): AR < AC.

Mission 4

  • Invention creates an idea; innovation brings it into use. Creative destruction: new products and firms replace old ones.
  • Process innovation raises productivity and shifts cost curves down.
  • Supernormal profit from market power can finance R&D; competition can also force innovation.
