Mission 1

Bhakti marga: the way of loving devotion

  • Bhakti (from bhaj, "to share in", "to worship"): loving, personal devotion to God.
  • Bhakti marga: the way of devotion, one of the paths to moksha.
  • God is approached as saguna, with qualities: Vishnu (often as Krishna or Rama), Shiva, or the Goddess.
  • The devotee relates to God as servant, friend, parent, child or beloved.
  • The heart of it: love and surrender; liberation through God's grace.
  • Liberation is often seen as eternal loving closeness to God, not loss of self.

Q1 According to 9:27–28, how does devotion free a person from karma?

Show answer

By doing every action as an offering to God, not for oneself.

Q2 Who can reach "the highest path" in 9:32?

Show answer

  • Also set: Gita 9:26 and 16:1–3 (the divine qualities of the devotee: fearlessness, purity, non-harming, truthfulness).
Mission 1

Origins: the Tamil saints

  • The movement began in South India, in Tamil, c. 6th–9th centuries CE.
  • Alvars: 12 Vaishnava poet-saints devoted to Vishnu (e.g. Nammalvar; Andal, a woman). Their hymns were gathered as the Four Thousand Divine Hymns.
  • Nayanars: 63 Shaiva saints devoted to Shiva (e.g. Appar, Sambandar, Sundarar; Karaikkal Ammaiyar, a woman). Their hymns form the Tevaram.
  • They sang in Tamil, not Sanskrit, travelling from temple to temple.
  • Background: the Gita's teaching on devotion; temple worship growing; competition with Jain and Buddhist communities in South India.
Mission 1

The movement spreads north

  • Ramanuja (trad. 1017–1137): gives bhakti a philosophical basis.
  • Karnataka, 12th century: Basava and the Lingayats (Shaiva); Akka Mahadevi.
  • Maharashtra: Jnaneshvar (13th century), Namdev, Chokhamela (a Mahar, "untouchable"), Tukaram (17th century): devotees of Vithoba at Pandharpur.
  • North India, 15th–17th centuries: Kabir; Ravidas (a leatherworker); Mirabai (a Rajput princess devoted to Krishna); Surdas; Tulsidas (Ramcharitmanas, Rama's story in Hindi).
  • Bengal: Chaitanya (1486–1534): ecstatic chanting of Krishna's names (kirtan), later the root of ISKCON.
  • Common features: local languages, song, saints from every caste, and women.

Mission 1

Vaishnava bhakti

  • Loving devotion to Vishnu and his avatars, especially Krishna and Rama.
  • Krishna as child, cowherd and lover; the love of the gopis (cowherd women) in the Bhagavata Purana, and of Radha in later poetry such as Jayadeva's Gita Govinda (12th century), as the model of the soul's longing for God.
  • Rama as the ideal king and the object of devotion (Tulsidas; Hanuman as the perfect devotee).
  • Key ideas: prapatti (surrender) and God's grace.
  • Today: ISKCON, Swaminarayan and many other movements.
Mission 1

Shaiva bhakti

  • Loving devotion to Shiva, often worshipped as the lingam.
  • Shiva is both fearsome ascetic and loving lord who chooses his devotees and saves them by grace.
  • The Nayanars' hymns (Tevaram) are still sung in Tamil Shiva temples; they shaped Shaiva Siddhanta theology.
  • Lingayats (Virashaivas), from Basava: each devotee wears a small lingam on the body, rejects temple priests and caste ranking, and worships Shiva directly.
  • Shaiva bhakti shows the same pattern as Vaishnava: local language, personal love, grace.
Mission 1

Yatra: pilgrimage as devotion

  • Yatra (pilgrimage): a journey to a holy place to receive darshan (sight) of the deity.
  • The saints themselves were pilgrims, singing at temple after temple.
  • Pandharpur wari: each year Varkari devotees walk about 250 km from Alandi and Dehu to Vithoba's temple, singing the hymns of Jnaneshvar and Tukaram, arriving for Ashadhi Ekadashi.
  • Vaishnava sites: Vrindavan and Mathura (Krishna), Ayodhya (Rama). Shaiva sites: Varanasi, the twelve jyotirlingas.
  • For the devotee, the hardship of the journey is itself an offering of love.
Mission 1

Bhakti and the rules of caste

  • Varna: the four ideal classes of scripture. Jati: the thousands of birth-based groups people actually belong to, often tied to occupation and marriage.
  • Bhakti transforms varnashrama dharma: devotion, not birth, makes a person dear to God.
  • Saints came from low jatis: Nandanar (a Nayanar of an "untouchable" community), Tiruppan Alvar, Chokhamela, Ravidas.
  • Women saints (Andal, Akka Mahadevi, Mirabai) defied family and social roles.
  • Songs in local languages took religion out of the hands of Sanskrit-trained priests.
  • But: in many stories low-caste saints still face exclusion (Nandanar must pass through fire before entering the temple). Spiritual equality did not always end social caste.
Mission 1Evaluate

Did bhakti make Hinduism open to all?

  • Claim: The bhakti movement made Hindu religious life open to everyone.
  • For: Gita 9:32 and the saints: women and low-caste devotees reach God directly.
  • For: Worship moved into local languages, homes, songs and pilgrim roads, needing no priest.
  • Against: Caste ranking survived; temples often stayed closed to Dalits until 20th-century laws and campaigns.
  • Against: Devotion was often made "inner": equal before God, but not equal in society.
  • Judgement criteria: Open spiritually or socially? Which region and century? Whose voices tell the story: saints, priests or later reformers?

Mission 2

What puja is

  • Puja: worship that honours a deity with offerings, as a beloved and honoured guest.
  • Offerings engage every sense: flowers, incense, a lamp, food, water, sandalwood paste, bells and song.
  • A short form has five offerings (panchopachara): scent, flower, incense, lamp, food. Fuller temple puja has sixteen (shodashopachara).
  • The worshipper often chants mantras and makes pranam (bowing, palms joined).
  • Aim: loving relationship with the deity (bhakti), and the deity's blessing.
Mission 2

Murti and darshan

  • A murti is a sacred image in which the deity is believed to be present, not an "idol" worshipped as mere stone or metal.
  • In a temple, a ceremony called prana pratishtha invites the deity's presence into the murti.
  • Darshan: "seeing". The devotee sees the deity and is seen by it; this exchange of sight is itself a blessing.
  • That is why the murti's eyes are prominent, and why curtains open for darshan at set times.
  • Scholar Diana Eck, Darśan: Seeing the Divine Image in India (1981), paraphrase: in Hindu worship, seeing is a way of touching and knowing the divine; darshan flows both ways.
Mission 2

Puja at home

  • Most Hindu homes have a shrine: a shelf, cabinet or small room, with murtis or pictures of the family's chosen deity (ishta-devata).
  • Daily: wash first; light a diya (lamp) and incense; offer flowers, water and food; ring a small bell; say mantras (e.g. the Gayatri mantra); perform a short arti.
  • Often led by women of the household; no priest needed.
  • Food cooked at home may be offered first, then eaten as blessed.
  • Personal and family: worship fits the rhythm of the day and the family's own tradition.
Mission 2

Puja in the mandir

  • A mandir is the home of the deity. Worshippers remove shoes, may ring the bell on entering, and approach the shrine.
  • The garbhagriha ("womb-house") holds the main murti, under the tower (shikhara); the hall (mandapa) is where people gather.
  • Pujaris (priests) care for the deity through the day: waking, bathing (abhisheka), dressing, offering food, arti, and putting to rest at night.
  • Worshippers bring offerings, receive darshan, join arti and bhajans (devotional songs).
  • Many British mandirs are also community centres: classes, weddings, meals, festivals.
Mission 2

Arti and prashad

  • Arti: a tray with a lamp of ghee wicks is waved in circles before the murti, with bells and a hymn (e.g. "Om Jai Jagdish Hare").
  • Worshippers pass their palms over the flame, then over their eyes and head: receiving the deity's light and blessing.
  • Prashad ("grace"): food offered to the deity is returned to the worshippers as blessed. Accepted with the right hand, and never thrown away.
  • Sharing prashad shares the deity's grace and binds the community.
  • Your spec writes prashad.
Mission 2

Circumambulation

  • Pradakshina or parikrama: walking clockwise around the shrine or the murti, keeping the deity on one's right.
  • Meaning: the deity is the centre of life; the devotee's life revolves around God.
  • Done inside the temple's walkway around the garbhagriha, around sacred trees (the tulsi plant, the pipal) and around whole holy towns on pilgrimage.
  • Some devotees circle a set number of times, or prostrate at each step as a vow.

Mission 2Diversity

Not all Hindus worship the same way

  • Shaiva: abhisheka of the Shiva lingam with water and milk; bilva leaves; ash marks.
  • Vaishnava: the tulsi plant honoured; murtis of Krishna, Rama, Vishnu richly dressed; ISKCON's daily cycle of arti.
  • Shakta: the Goddess honoured with red cloth and flowers; in some places, animal offerings (opposed by many Hindus).
  • Arya Samaj (founded 1875): rejects murti worship as un-Vedic and performs havan (fire ritual) instead.
  • Some Advaitins see the murti as a support for those who still need form, on the way to formless Brahman.
Mission 2Evaluate

Is personal worship more important than congregational?

  • Claim: For Hindus, personal worship matters more than congregational worship.
  • For: Hinduism has no weekly obligation to attend the mandir; the home shrine is central to daily devotion.
  • For: Bhakti is a personal relationship with a chosen deity (Gita 9:26: anyone may offer).
  • Against: The consecrated murti and pujaris' rites are in the mandir; festivals and arti are shared.
  • Against: In the diaspora, mandirs carry language, identity and community; congregational gatherings have grown.
  • Judgement criteria: Importance for whom (a priest, a family, a migrant)? Measured by frequency, sacredness or identity? Are the two really separable?

Mission 3

Holi: the spring full moon

  • Holi falls on the full moon of the month Phalguna (February or March): the end of winter and the start of the spring harvest.
  • Night one: Holika Dahan. A bonfire is lit; grains, coconuts and new wheat are offered to the fire.
  • Day two: the festival of colours. People throw coloured powder (gulal) and coloured water at friends, family and strangers.
  • Rules of status relax for the day: old and young, rich and poor play together; quarrels are made up.
  • Two different stories explain Holi in different traditions.
Mission 3

Holika and Prahlada

  • Hiranyakashipu, a demon king, had a boon that made him almost impossible to kill. He demanded that everyone worship him.
  • His son Prahlada would worship only Vishnu. His father tried again and again to kill him; each time Vishnu protected him.
  • The king's sister Holika could not be harmed by fire. She sat in a fire holding Prahlada on her lap.
  • Holika burned; Prahlada was unharmed, because of his devotion to Vishnu.
  • Vishnu then appeared as Narasimha (man-lion avatar) and killed Hiranyakashipu in a way his boon had not foreseen.
  • The bonfire recalls Holika's burning: good defeats evil, and God protects the devotee.
Mission 3

Krishna and Radha

  • In Braj (the region of Mathura and Vrindavan), Holi recalls the young Krishna and Radha.
  • In a well-known story, Krishna, dark-skinned, worried that fair Radha would not love him; his mother said he could colour Radha's face. He and the gopis (cowherd girls) played with colour.
  • The colours celebrate divine love and the playful joy (lila) of Krishna with his devotees.
  • In Barsana (Radha's village) and Vrindavan, Holi lasts for days, with songs, flowers and colour.
  • For Vaishnava devotees, Radha's love for Krishna is the model of the soul's love for God.

Mission 3

What Holi means

  • Agni's blessing: in some regions, parents carry babies around the Holika fire so that Agni, god of fire, will bless and protect them.
  • Love: colour play recalls Krishna's love; people forgive and renew friendships.
  • Faith confirmed: Prahlada shows that steady devotion is protected. Devotees are encouraged to trust God.
  • Good over evil: Holika burns; Hiranyakashipu's pride is defeated.
  • The gods' benevolence: Vishnu saves his devotee; the harvest is a gift to give thanks for.
  • Aims and duties: Holi reminds devotees to help others: food is shared, and social barriers fall for a day.
Mission 3

Durga and the buffalo demon

  • Durga is a form of the Goddess (Devi, Shakti: divine power).
  • The Devi Mahatmya (a text within the Markandeya Purana) tells the story (paraphrase):
  • The demon Mahishasura, who could take the form of a buffalo, had a boon that no man or god could kill him. He drove the gods from heaven.
  • The gods' combined energies took form as Durga. Each god gave her a weapon: Shiva's trident, Vishnu's discus. She rode a lion.
  • After a long battle, she killed Mahishasura: good defeats evil, through the power of the Mother.
  • Many Hindus read the demon also as inner evil: pride, anger, ignorance.
Mission 3Read the source

Rama's autumn worship and the avatars

  • In a Bengali retelling of the Ramayana (Krittivasa's), Rama, about to fight Ravana to rescue Sita, worships Durga in the autumn, out of her usual season (akal bodhan, "untimely awakening").
  • He must offer 108 blue lotuses; one is missing, so he prepares to offer his own eye, called "lotus-eyed". Durga stops him and blesses him; he wins the battle.
  • Rama is an avatar of Vishnu. Krishna explains why avatars come:

> "When Righteousness / Declines, O Bharata! when Wickedness / Is strong, I rise, from age to age, and take / Visible shape, and move a man with men, / Succouring the good, thrusting the evil back" (Bhagavad Gita 4:7–8, Arnold)

Q1 What does the verse say an avatar comes to do?

Show answer

To protect the good, push back evil and restore righteousness, or dharma.

Mission 3

Durga Puja: the festival days

  • Held in the month Ashvin (September or October), at the same time as Navaratri ("nine nights") elsewhere in India; greatest in Bengal, Assam, Odisha and Tripura.
  • Mahalaya: Durga is invoked; many also make offerings to their ancestors.
  • Shashthi (day 6): the Goddess is welcomed and awakened in a clay murti in a pandal (temporary shrine). She stands with her children Lakshmi, Sarasvati, Ganesha and Kartikeya, with Mahishasura at her feet.
  • Saptami to Navami (days 7–9): puja, mantras, offerings, arti; the Sandhi Puja with 108 lamps and 108 lotuses recalls Rama.
  • Vijaya Dashami (day 10): married women smear red sindoor on the Goddess and each other; the murti is immersed in a river (visarjan).
  • In Bengali tradition, Durga is a daughter coming home to her parents for these days, then returning to Shiva.
Mission 3

What Durga Puja means

  • Durga as divine mother: fierce to demons, loving to her children; devotees call her Ma.
  • Thanksgiving: for her protection and for the harvest; Rama's thanks after victory.
  • Impurity and sin: devotees pray to be freed from inner demons; many fast and keep vows of purity.
  • Worship and prayer: reciting the Devi Mahatmya, puja, arti; faith confirmed and strengthened.
  • Good over evil: Mahishasura's defeat; Rama's victory over Ravana.
  • Aims and duties: to live free from impurity, sin and weakness; to help others: food (bhog) is shared with all visitors.

Mission 3Diversity

One season, many festivals

  • Bengal: Durga Puja; community pandals and the homecoming of the Goddess.
  • Gujarat: Navaratri with garba and dandiya dances around a lamp for the Goddess.
  • North India: Ramlila plays and Dussehra, burning effigies of Ravana: focus on Rama's victory.
  • South India: Golu displays of dolls; Sarasvati and Lakshmi honoured too.
  • Holi too differs: Braj colour play for Krishna; the Holika bonfire for Prahlada; in parts of South India the focus is the god of love, Kama, burnt by Shiva.
  • Community: neighbourhood committees fund and run sarbojanin (public) pujas; families, priests, artists and volunteers keep each tradition alive. In 2021 UNESCO listed Durga Puja in Kolkata as intangible cultural heritage.
Mission 3Evaluate

Are festivals necessary to Hindu identity?

  • Claim: Festivals are a necessary expression of Hindu identity.
  • For: They pass stories and values to children in a way texts cannot.
  • For: They gather the whole community, especially as a minority outside India.
  • Against: Many Hindus see moksha as the goal; jnana, daily puja and right action matter more than annual events.
  • Against: Festivals can become cultural or commercial: colour runs and parties without the stories.
  • Against: Hindu practice is so diverse that no single festival is shared by all Hindus.
  • Judgement criteria: Is identity about belief, practice or community? Does it matter if the stories are mythical rather than historical? Which Hindus are you thinking of?

Mission 4

Tirtha: a crossing place

  • A tirtha is a holy place. The word means a ford: a place to cross a river.
  • Hindus speak of tirthas as places where it is easier to cross over from this world towards the divine, and from samsara towards moksha (liberation).
  • Many tirthas are on rivers, coasts or mountains; many are linked to a story of a deity or a saint.
  • Yatra (or tirtha-yatra): a pilgrimage, a journey to a tirtha.
  • Pilgrims go for many reasons: to fulfil a vow, to ask for help, to give thanks, to remove the effects of wrongdoing, to perform rites for the dead, or simply for darshan.
Mission 4

Darshan: seeing and being seen

  • Darshan means seeing: the devotee sees the deity, and is seen by the deity.
  • At a shrine or mandir, the murti (sacred image) is not "just a statue": many Hindus believe the deity is present in it, especially after its consecration.
  • Darshan is a moment of blessing, not mainly an act of looking at art. Many murtis have large, open eyes for this reason.
  • Darshan can also be received from a holy person (a guru or saint) or from a sacred place: the river Ganges itself is "seen" as a goddess.
  • On pilgrimage, the long journey leads to darshan at the end: at a temple, a river or a mountain.
Mission 4

Ganga Ma: the river as goddess

  • Hindus call the Ganges Ganga or Ganga Ma (Mother Ganga).
  • The story (told in the Ramayana and the Puranas, paraphrase): King Bhagiratha practised long austerities so that Ganga would come down from heaven to purify the ashes of his ancestors. Shiva caught her in his matted hair, so that her fall would not shatter the earth.
  • So the river is believed to flow from the divine world into this one: a tirtha along its whole length.
  • Bathing in Ganga is believed to purify: to wash away the effects of wrongdoing (papa).
  • Key places: Haridwar (where the river leaves the mountains), Prayagraj (meeting of Ganga, Yamuna and the unseen Sarasvati), Varanasi (Kashi, city of Shiva).
  • Kumbh Mela: a great bathing festival that rotates between Prayagraj, Haridwar, Ujjain and Nashik.
Mission 4

Death and the river

  • Water from the Ganges (Ganga jal) is kept in many Hindu homes, and a few drops may be placed in the mouth of a dying person.
  • Varanasi: many Hindus believe that to die in Kashi brings moksha, because Shiva himself whispers the words of liberation to the dying.
  • Cremation takes place on the ghats (riverside steps), e.g. Manikarnika Ghat.
  • After cremation, the ashes are immersed in the Ganges (asthi visarjan) if possible; families living elsewhere may carry or send the ashes to India.
  • Rites for ancestors (shraddha) are often performed at tirthas such as Gaya and Varanasi.
  • Not all Hindus share every belief: many see bathing as a symbol of inner purification, which still needs right action and devotion.

Yatra as devotion

Krishna speaks in the Bhagavad Gita:

> "Whoso shall offer Me in faith and love / A leaf, a flower, a fruit, water poured forth, / That offering I accept, lovingly made / With pious will." (Bhagavad Gita 9:26, Arnold)

  • In bhakti (loving devotion) what matters is love, not the cost of the gift. A pilgrimage can be such an offering: a journey made for God.
  • Vaishnava example: the Pandharpur Wari in Maharashtra. Varkari devotees walk for weeks, singing, to Vithoba (a form of Vishnu/Krishna), carrying the sandals of the saints Jnaneshwar and Tukaram.
  • Shaiva example: the Kanwar Yatra. Devotees carry Ganga water from Haridwar and other places to pour over the linga of Shiva.

Mission 5

Samkhya: counting what is real

  • Hindu thought has six classical darshanas ("views": schools of philosophy) that accept the authority of the Vedas.
  • Samkhya ("enumeration", counting) is one of the oldest. It lists the basic principles (tattvas) of reality: 25 in all.
  • Its legendary founder is the sage Kapila. Its classic text is the Samkhya Karika of Ishvarakrishna (c. 4th–5th century CE).
  • Samkhya ideas appear earlier in the Upanishads and the Bhagavad Gita, often in a form that includes God.
  • Yoga (Patanjali) is its sister school: Samkhya gives the theory, yoga the practice.
Mission 5

Two realities: purusha and prakriti

  • Samkhya is dualist: reality has two eternal, separate kinds.
  • Purusha: pure consciousness. It only witnesses; it does not act or change. There are many purushas, one for each living being.
  • Prakriti: nature or matter. It is one, unconscious, always active and changing.
  • Key point: the mind, ego and intellect belong to prakriti, not to purusha. Thinking is a material process; awareness of it is purusha.
  • Suffering begins when purusha mistakes the activity of prakriti for itself: "I am angry", "I am this body".
Mission 5

The three gunas

  • Prakriti is made of three gunas ("strands", qualities), mixed in different proportions in everything:
  • Sattva: light, purity, calm, goodness.
  • Rajas: energy, passion, restlessness, desire.
  • Tamas: heaviness, darkness, inertia, dullness.
  • Before creation the gunas are in balance; when the balance is disturbed, the world unfolds.

> "Sattwan, Rajas, and Tamas, so are named / The qualities of Nature, "Soothfastness," / "Passion," and "Ignorance." These three bind down / The changeless Spirit in the changeful flesh." (Bhagavad Gita 14:5, Arnold)

  • Hindus apply the gunas to food, character and actions: e.g. sattvic food is fresh and vegetarian; many aim to increase sattva.
Mission 5

How the world unfolds

  • From prakriti, the world evolves step by step (the effect already exists in its cause):
  • Buddhi (intellect) → ahamkara (ego, "I-maker") → manas (mind), the 5 sense capacities, the 5 action capacities, and the 5 subtle elements → the 5 gross elements (space, air, fire, water, earth).
  • That makes 24 tattvas of prakriti, plus purusha: 25.
  • The whole unfolding exists for the sake of purusha: to give it experience, and then release.
  • In the Samkhya Karika's images (paraphrase): prakriti is like a dancer who stops once she has been fully seen; purusha and prakriti work together like a lame man carried by a blind man.

The chariot, read by Samkhya

Katha Upanishad, Part 3, Paramananda translation (1919):

> "Know the Atman (Self) as the lord of the chariot, and the body as the chariot. Know also the intellect to be the driver and mind the reins." (Katha 3:3)

> "The senses are called the horses; the sense objects are the roads" (Katha 3:4)

  • A Samkhya reading: the lord of the chariot is purusha, who rides but does not drive. Body, intellect, mind and senses are all prakriti.
  • An Advaita reading: the lord of the chariot is the atman, which is one with Brahman.

Show answer

Purusha is present and aware but does not itself act.

Mission 5

Liberation: kaivalya

  • The goal is kaivalya: "aloneness" or isolation of purusha from prakriti.
  • It comes through viveka: discriminating knowledge that "I am not this body, mind or ego".
  • When purusha is no longer confused with prakriti, the dance of prakriti stops for it. No more rebirth.
  • Liberation is not union with God: each liberated purusha remains itself, separate and pure.
  • Classical Samkhya is non-theistic: it does not need Ishvara (a Lord) to explain the world. Many Hindus who use its ideas do believe in God.
Mission 5Read the source

Samkhya in the Upanishads

  • The Shvetashvatara Upanishad uses Samkhya words inside a belief in God (paraphrase):
  • 1:9 and 1:12: three unborn realities, the Lord (ishvara), nature (prakriti) and the individual self (atman), together make up the threefold Brahman.
  • 5:7: the individual soul, bound by the three gunas, acts, enjoys the fruits of action and wanders through rebirth by its deeds.
  • 5:12–13: the embodied self takes many forms through its qualities; one who knows the one Creator, without beginning or end, is freed from all fetters.
  • Maitri Upanishad 6:18–19 (paraphrase): a six-limbed yoga: breath control, withdrawal of the senses, meditation, concentration, reflection and absorption (samadhi), by which the mind is drawn back from objects to the Self.

Q1 How does the Shvetashvatara differ from classical Samkhya?

Show answer

It places nature and souls within Brahman and under a Lord; classical Samkhya has no God.

Mission 5

Patanjali and the eight limbs

  • Patanjali, Yoga Sutras (date disputed, often placed c. 2nd century BCE–5th century CE).
  • He accepts Samkhya's purusha, prakriti and gunas. Yoga is the stilling of the movements of the mind (Yoga Sutra 1:2, paraphrase).
  • Ashtanga (eight-limbed) yoga: yama (restraints, e.g. ahimsa), niyama (observances), asana (posture), pranayama (breath), pratyahara (withdrawal of senses), dharana (concentration), dhyana (meditation), samadhi (absorption).
  • Patanjali adds Ishvara: a special purusha, untouched by karma; devotion to Ishvara is one way to samadhi.
  • The goal is still kaivalya: the seer resting in its own nature.

Mission 5Evaluate

Which explains God and humanity better?

  • Claim: Samkhya explains the relationship between God and humanity better than Advaita Vedanta.
  • For Samkhya: it takes the world and individual persons as real; many selves explain why liberation is personal.
  • For Samkhya: its gunas give a working account of character and change, still used in yoga and Ayurveda.
  • Against Samkhya: classical Samkhya leaves out God, so it explains humanity without a relationship with God.
  • Against Samkhya: how can unconscious prakriti act "for the sake of" purusha, or two unrelated realities interact?
  • For Advaita: it fits the Upanishads' teaching that atman is Brahman; against: it must explain why a world that seems real is appearance.
  • Judgement criteria: fit with shruti; coherence; whether God is needed; which Hindus find each view true.

Mission 6

Why children matter

  • Marriage opens the grihastha (householder) stage; having children is one of its duties.
  • The three debts (rinas): to the gods (paid by ritual), to the sages (by study) and to the ancestors (by having children).
  • Sons traditionally perform the funeral rites (antyeshti) and ancestral offerings (shraddha).
  • So infertility can bring grief, family pressure and stigma, often falling most on the wife.
  • Hindus have long responded with prayer, vows (vratas), pilgrimage, Ayurvedic treatment, and adoption.
  • Modern assisted reproductive technology (ART) adds new options, and new questions.
Mission 6

What ART involves

  • IVF (in vitro fertilisation): eggs and sperm are joined in a laboratory; an embryo is placed in the womb.
  • Sperm donation: sperm from a donor, not the husband (AID); with the husband's own sperm it is AIH.
  • Embryonic transfer: moving an embryo into a womb: the mother's own, or a surrogate's.
  • Spare embryos: IVF often creates more embryos than are used; they may be frozen, donated, used in research, or allowed to perish.
  • Each step raises questions for Hindu ethics: lineage, purity, ahimsa, and the duties of marriage.
Mission 6

"Magical" births in the epics

  • Niyoga: a childless wife or widow conceives by an appointed man (often the husband's brother) to continue the husband's line.
  • Dhritarashtra and Pandu were born by niyoga, fathered by the sage Vyasa (Mahabharata).
  • Kunti and Madri conceive the Pandavas by calling on gods with a mantra.
  • Gandhari's hundred sons grow from a mass of flesh divided into pots of ghee.
  • Balarama: the embryo is moved from Devaki's womb to Rohini's (Bhagavata Purana 10.2): often compared with embryo transfer.
  • These are sacred narratives, not medical records; some Hindus read them as precedents, others as signs of divine power only.
Mission 6Read the source

Niyoga in the Manusmriti

Manusmriti 9.59–61 (paraphrase): if there are no children, a wife who has been authorised may obtain a son by her husband's brother or another kinsman; he must act without desire, and only to beget one son (some allow two).

Manusmriti 9.64–68 (paraphrase): the next verses condemn niyoga among the twice-born as fit only for animals.

Q1 What was niyoga for?

Show answer

Continuing the husband's family line when he could not father a child.

Q2 Why is it hard to cite Manu as clear support for donor conception?

Show answer

The same text permits niyoga and then condemns it.

Mission 6

Key concepts: ahimsa, karma, dharma

  • Ahimsa (non-harm): many Hindus hold that the atman enters at conception; destroying spare embryos may then be harm to a life.
  • Karma: some see infertility as the result of past karma; others reply that seeking a cure is also right action, as with any illness.
  • Dharma: parenthood is a duty of the householder; ART can help fulfil it.
  • Purity and lineage: donor gametes raise questions of whose line the child belongs to, and in a caste setting, whose jati.
  • Adultery: some Hindus think donor sperm comes close to it, even without sexual contact; others disagree because there is no act of desire.

Mission 6

Swasti Bhattacharyya: narrative bioethics

  • Swasti Bhattacharyya, Magical Progeny, Modern Technology: A Hindu Bioethics of Assisted Reproductive Technology (2006).
  • Method: build a Hindu bioethics from Hindu sources, especially the birth stories of the Pandavas and Kauravas in the Mahabharata, not only from Western principles (paraphrase).
  • She applies the principles she finds to a California court case about who a child's parents are, and compares Hindu and Roman Catholic views.
  • The stories show a tradition that already imagined children born apart from ordinary intercourse: niyoga, divine conception, embryo transfer.
  • So ART is not foreign to Hindu thought; it can be judged within dharma, the duty to have children, and care for all involved (paraphrase).
  • She still asks about harm, consent and the welfare of the child and the women involved.
Mission 6

S. Cromwell Crawford: contextual dharma

  • S. Cromwell Crawford, Hindu Bioethics for the Twenty-First Century (2003).
  • Hindu ethics is contextual: dharma takes account of place, time and circumstance (desha, kala, patra); it is not one fixed code (paraphrase).
  • So Hindu ethics can adapt to new technology rather than reject it.
  • IVF: no great difficulty on harm and benefit; in ordinary cases he would limit it to married couples using their own egg and sperm (paraphrase).
  • Donor sperm: he notes the tradition makes provision for it, with niyoga as precedent; others raise lineage, caste and fidelity concerns (paraphrase).
  • Surrogacy and spare embryos raise concerns about exploitation and ahimsa (paraphrase).

Mission 6Diversity

Hindu views on donors and surrogacy

  • More accepting: IVF and donors help fulfil the duty of parenthood; the epics show many ways of birth; compassion for the childless.
  • More cautious: AIH acceptable, donors not (lineage, caste, fidelity); spare embryos break ahimsa.
  • Many families prefer adoption within the family or wider kin, recognised by the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act (1956).
  • Indian law now regulates ART clinics (ART Act 2021) and bans commercial surrogacy (Surrogacy Act 2021): concern for poor women used as surrogates.
  • Views differ by tradition, guru, region and generation; there is no single Hindu ruling.
Mission 6Evaluate

To what extent is IVF compatible with Hindu teaching?

  • Claim: IVF is fully compatible with Hindu teaching.
  • For: having children is a householder's dharma and a debt to the ancestors; IVF helps fulfil it.
  • For: the epics show children born in unusual ways (Bhattacharyya); dharma adapts to context (Crawford).
  • Against: spare embryos may be destroyed: a conflict with ahimsa if the atman is present at conception.
  • Against: donor gametes raise questions of lineage, caste and fidelity; Manu condemns niyoga.
  • Judgement criteria: which form of IVF (AIH, donor, surrogacy)? Are the stories precedents or miracles? Whose interests count: couple, child, donor, surrogate?
  • Spec issue: "the extent to which IVF is compatible with Hindu teaching".

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Bhakti is loving devotion to a personal God; liberation comes by grace and surrender.
  • It began with the Tamil Alvars (Vishnu) and Nayanars (Shiva), c. 6th–9th centuries, and spread north through saints of every caste, singing in local languages.
  • Bhakti made devotion, not birth, the measure of a devotee, though social caste often survived.

Mission 2

  • Puja honours a deity with offerings to the senses; darshan is the two-way sight between devotee and deity, believed present in the murti.
  • In the mandir: garbhagriha, pujaris, arti, prashad and clockwise circumambulation; at home: a family shrine, often led by women.
  • Home and mandir share one core; they differ in leaders, setting and community.

Mission 3

  • Holi (Phalguna full moon): the Holika bonfire recalls Prahlada saved by Vishnu; the colours recall Krishna and Radha's love; both teach that good overcomes evil.
  • Durga Puja (Ashvin): Durga, the divine mother made from the gods' energies, defeats Mahishasura; Rama worshipped her before fighting Ravana.
  • Both festivals confirm faith, give thanks, call devotees to help others and depend on the community to keep them alive.

Mission 4

  • A tirtha is a "crossing place"; on yatra, pilgrims seek darshan (seeing and being seen by the deity), purification and help for the dead.
  • Ganga is a goddess believed to have come down from heaven; bathing purifies, and ashes are immersed in her waters.
  • Yatra is part of bhakti: a journey offered in love (Gita 9:26), as in the Pandharpur Wari or the Kanwar Yatra.

Mission 5

  • Samkhya is dualist: many purushas (pure consciousness) and one prakriti (nature, made of sattva, rajas and tamas); mind and intellect belong to prakriti.
  • Liberation (kaivalya) comes through knowing the self is not prakriti; classical Samkhya needs no God, but Patanjali adds Ishvara and the eight limbs of yoga.
  • Samkhya and Advaita agree that the self is not body or mind; they disagree on whether there are many selves or one Brahman.

Mission 6

  • Children fulfil a householder's dharma and the debt to the ancestors, so infertility matters deeply; ART offers new ways to meet that duty.
  • Ahimsa (spare embryos), lineage and fidelity (donor gametes) and exploitation (surrogacy) are the main Hindu concerns.
  • Bhattacharyya builds a Hindu bioethics from the epics' "magical" births; Crawford stresses dharma adapting to place, time and circumstance.
