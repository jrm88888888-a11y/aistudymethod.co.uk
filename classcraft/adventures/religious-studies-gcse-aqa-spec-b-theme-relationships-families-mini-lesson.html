Mission 1

Mission 1: If you don't believe in God, where do your morals come from, and what happens when you die?

Make a guess: If you don't believe in God, where do your morals come from, and what happens when you die?

Mission 1

Theist, atheist, agnostic

  • Theist: believes in God (or gods).
  • Atheist: believes there is no God (some atheists say simply that they lack belief in God).
  • Agnostic: believes we cannot know whether God exists, or has not decided. The word was coined by T. H. Huxley (1825–1895) in 1869.
  • These are answers to one question: does God exist?
  • Humanism goes further: a whole non-religious approach to life. Most Humanists are atheists or agnostics.
Quick check
?Sam says: "There might be a God, there might not. I don't think anyone can know for sure." Which term best describes Sam?
Mission 1

What Humanists believe

  • Humanists see no good evidence for God or the supernatural; they understand the world through reason, evidence and science.
  • One life: there is no afterlife, so this life matters all the more.
  • Meaning is made, not given: people create purpose through relationships, work, learning and helping others.
  • Morality comes from people: reason, empathy and concern for human flourishing (wellbeing).
  • Humanists support human rights, equality and freedom of belief for everyone.
  • Groups: Humanists UK (Britain); Humanists International (Amsterdam Declaration, 2002).
Mission 1

How Humanists decide what is right

  • Ask: What are the consequences? Who is helped, who is harmed?
  • Empathy: treat others as you would want to be treated (the Golden Rule, found in many cultures).
  • Respect for choice: people should run their own lives if they harm no one else.
  • Examples: many Humanists support legal assisted dying, a woman's right to choose on abortion, and equal marriage.
  • Humanists hold non-religious ceremonies: weddings, naming ceremonies, funerals.
  • Humanists do not all agree: each person must think the issue through.
Quick check
?A Humanist is deciding whether to give a kidney to a stranger. Which line of reasoning is most typically Humanist?
Mission 1

Soul and afterlife: non-religious views

  • Most atheists and Humanists see the mind as the activity of the brain. No separate soul survives death.
  • Death is the end of personal existence. Evidence: damage to the brain changes memory and personality.
  • People live on in other ways: memories, children, work and the good they have done; the body's matter returns to nature.
  • The ancient Greek Epicurus (341–270 BCE): death is nothing to us, because when death comes, we are no longer there to experience it (paraphrase).
  • Humanist funerals celebrate the person's life, without prayers or talk of an afterlife.
Mission 1Diversity

Shared values and disagreements

  • Shared: human dignity and rights; care for the vulnerable; honesty; justice; freedom of belief.
  • Disagree about: where morality comes from (God or humans); life after death; assisted dying and abortion; faith schools; bishops in the House of Lords.
  • Dialogue means understanding each other's reasons, not winning.
Mission 1Evaluate

"You can be a good person without believing in God."

  • Reasons for: many atheists and Humanists live honest, generous lives; empathy and reason can guide action; Paul says Gentiles without the Jewish law can show "the work of the law written in their hearts" (Romans 2:15), and some Christians apply this to non-believers.
  • Reasons against: many believers argue that without God there is no objective standard of goodness (one that holds whatever anyone thinks); religion gives motivation, community and forgiveness; Catholic teaching sees moral law as rooted in God, even when people do not know it.
  • Another view: many religious people agree non-believers can act well, but say true goodness includes loving God.
  • Judge it by: What does "good" mean: acting well, or a relationship with God? Must a standard be objective to be real?
Mission check

?At a hospital ethics meeting, a Humanist and a Catholic both oppose a plan to cut care for elderly patients. Which statement best describes their positions?

Non-religious people build ethics on reason and empathy; they often reach the same actions as believers, for different reasons.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Fewer than half of people in England and Wales now say they are Christian. Is Britain still Christian?

Make a guess: Fewer than half of people in England and Wales now say they are Christian. Is Britain still Christian?

Mission 2

A Christian heritage

  • Christianity reached Britain under Roman rule. In 597, Augustine of Canterbury began a mission to the English.
  • For over 1,400 years Christianity shaped Britain's laws, calendar, language, art and buildings.
  • Parish churches and cathedrals stand in almost every town; many schools and hospitals were founded by churches.
Mission 2

Many traditions, one country

  • Christian traditions: Church of England (Anglican), Catholic, Methodist, Baptist, United Reformed, Pentecostal and black-majority churches, Orthodox, Quakers, Salvation Army.
  • Other religions: Islam, Hinduism, Sikhism, Judaism, Buddhism, and smaller groups.
  • Non-religious beliefs: atheism (the belief that there is no God) and Humanism (a non-religious approach to life based on reason and care for people).
  • Britain is mainly Christian in heritage and diverse in belief today.

Mission 2Diversity

Responding to a changing Britain

  • Catholic Church: a call to evangelise: share the Gospel by word and by service ("Go and make disciples of all nations", Matthew 28:19). Falling numbers are seen as a challenge and an opportunity.
  • Church of England: serves every parish, believers or not; its leaders generally support remaining established so faith keeps a voice in public life.
  • Evangelical and Pentecostal churches: active mission, church planting, Alpha courses.
Mission 2Evaluate

"Britain is no longer a Christian country."

  • Reasons for: fewer than half chose "Christian" in 2021; church attendance is low; many laws now differ from church teaching; Britain is diverse in religion and belief.
  • Reasons against: Christianity is still the largest group; laws, holidays and the calendar are rooted in Christianity; many people still believe without attending church.
  • Another view: "Christian" may describe heritage and culture rather than belief; Britain may be best described as diverse or plural (many faiths and beliefs side by side).
  • Judge it by: Does "Christian country" mean belief, practice, law or history? Which evidence counts most: census, attendance or public institutions?
Mission check

?A new town council plans its calendar. The council gives staff the Christmas Day bank holiday, opens with a prayer by the Anglican vicar, and also holds a Diwali lights event and an Eid reception. Which description of Britain does this best illustrate?

Britain's laws and calendar are rooted in Christianity, but its people are increasingly diverse, and many are non-religious.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why do so many religions make marriage a promise before God, not just a legal contract?

Make a guess: Why do so many religions make marriage a promise before God, not just a legal contract?

Mission 3

What is marriage?

  • Marriage: a legal union of two people, publicly promising to share their lives.
  • Religious view: also a sacred commitment: a covenant (a binding promise before God), a sacrament (Catholic, Orthodox) or a contract blessed by God (Islam).
  • UK law: a wedding can be civil (register office, approved venue) or religious.
  • Same-sex couples have been able to marry in Great Britain since 2014 (more in Mission 5).
  • Civil partnership: a legal union with almost the same rights, without the word "marriage".
Mission 3

Christian purposes of marriage

  • Lifelong, faithful union: "a man will leave his father and his mother, and will join with his wife, and they will be one flesh." (Genesis 2:24)
  • Purposes: mutual love, help and comfort; a faithful sexual relationship; having and bringing up children; a stable base for family and society.
  • It reflects God's love: "Husbands, love your wives, even as Christ also loved the assembly and gave himself up for her" (Ephesians 5:25).
  • Catholic: marriage is a sacrament, unitive (binding the couple in love) and procreative (open to children); permanent and exclusive.
Mission 3Read the source

Mark 10

"But from the beginning of the creation, God made them male and female. For this cause a man will leave his father and mother, and will join to his wife, and the two will become one flesh, so that they are no longer two, but one flesh. Q1 Which earlier scripture is Jesus quoting?

Show answer

Genesis 2:24.

Q2 What does "God has joined together" show about marriage?

Show answer

God is involved in it, so it is meant to be permanent.

Mission 3

A valid Catholic marriage

  • Free consent: each freely chooses to marry; no force or deception.
  • Capacity: both are free to marry: not already married, of legal age, not closely related, not bound by religious vows.
  • Right intention: to accept marriage as permanent, faithful and open to children.
  • Canonical form: consent exchanged before a priest or deacon and two witnesses.
  • Between two baptised people, marriage is a sacrament; a Catholic marrying a non-Catholic needs the Church's permission.
  • The vows: to be faithful in good times and bad, in sickness and health, to love and honour each other for life. The couple give the sacrament to each other; the priest witnesses.
Quick check

Mission 3Diversity

Cohabitation and civil partnership

  • Cohabitation: a couple living together in a sexual relationship without marrying.
  • Catholic teaching: not acceptable; sex belongs within marriage.
  • Many Protestants: prefer marriage, but some accept committed cohabitation, often as a step towards it.
  • Muslims, Orthodox Jews, most Hindus and Sikhs: not acceptable.
  • Buddhists: no fixed rule; what matters is avoiding harm (the Third Precept).
  • Humanists: a personal choice; commitment, honesty and care matter more than the legal form.
  • Civil partnership: since 2005 for same-sex couples; since 2019 for opposite-sex couples too (England and Wales). Some churches allow ceremonies; many do not.
Quick check
?Ali and Beth have lived together for six years and say a wedding certificate would change nothing. Which group would most likely agree with them?
Mission 3Evaluate

"Marriage is no longer important today."

  • Reasons for: cohabitation is common and widely accepted; many marriages end in divorce; commitment does not need a certificate; Humanists see it as one choice among several.
  • Reasons against: marriage is a public, lifelong promise; legal protection for partners and children; for many believers it is a sacrament or covenant before God; many non-religious people still choose to marry.
  • Judge it by: Important to whom: the couple, children, society, God? Does a public promise change a relationship? Are religious and legal importance different?
Mission check

?Sam and Jo married at a register office. A year later, as Christians, they ask their Anglican parish church for a service of prayer and dedication. In Christian belief, what does the service add?

For many believers, marriage is more than a legal contract: a lifelong promise made before God, who joins the couple.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Many religions call sex a gift from God. So why do they say it belongs only in marriage?

Make a guess: Many religions call sex a gift from God. So why do they say it belongs only in marriage?

Mission 4

Made sexual, male and female

  • "God created man in his own image. In God's image he created him; male and female he created them." (Genesis 1:27)
  • In Genesis, God blesses them: "Be fruitful, multiply" (Genesis 1:28). Jews and Christians read this as showing that sexuality is part of God's good creation.
  • Genesis 2: the man and woman become "one flesh" (2:24); they "were both naked, and they were not ashamed" (2:25).
  • Human sexuality: our sexual identity, attraction and relationships. Religions see it as a gift with a purpose.
Mission 4

Marital, unitive, procreative

  • Traditional Christian teaching, set out most fully by the Catholic Church, gives sex three purposes:
  • Marital: sex belongs within marriage, the lifelong, faithful commitment it expresses.
  • Unitive: sex unites the couple in love: body and person.
  • Procreative: sex is open to new life.
  • Catholic teaching (Catechism 2360–2365): sexual love is "noble and honourable" within marriage; it has a twofold purpose, the good of the spouses and the passing on of life; it requires fidelity.
  • Many Protestants stress the unitive purpose more, and accept that not every act must be open to children.
Mission 4

The Theology of the Body

  • Pope John Paul II (1920–2005): 129 Wednesday talks (1979–1984), published as Man and Woman He Created Them.
  • The body reveals the person: we do not just have bodies; we are bodily persons.
  • The nuptial meaning of the body: men and women are made to give themselves in love.
  • Original solitude, unity, nakedness: Genesis 2 shows humans alone without a partner, then joined as one flesh, unashamed (2:25).
  • Lust treats a person as an object; Jesus warns against looking "to lust" (Matthew 5:28).
  • Married sex is a "language of the body": it should say what the vows say: free, total, faithful, fruitful love, an image of God's self-giving love.

Mission 4Diversity

Christian and non-religious views on sex before marriage

  • Catholic and Orthodox: sex before marriage is wrong; all are called to chastity.
  • Evangelical Protestants: sex is for marriage only (Hebrews 13:4: "Let marriage be held in honour amongst all, and let the bed be undefiled").
  • Church of England: official teaching places sex within marriage; many Anglicans accept sex in a committed, loving relationship.
  • Liberal Christians: what matters is love, commitment and no exploitation (situation ethics).
  • Humanists: sex is a personal choice between consenting adults; what matters is consent, honesty, respect and responsibility (e.g. avoiding pregnancy and infection when not wanted).
  • Catholic response: love is shown by commitment first; sex without marriage cannot express total self-gift.
Mission 4

Adultery

  • Adultery: sex between a married person and someone who is not their spouse.
  • Condemned in every tradition here: "You shall not commit adultery." (Exodus 20:14)
  • Jesus goes further: lust in the heart is already adultery (Matthew 5:28).
  • Islam: zina (17:32). Buddhism: the Third Precept. Hinduism and Sikhism: breaks the marriage promise.
  • Why it is seen as wrong: it breaks vows made before God and partner, betrays trust, harms children and families.
  • Humanists also see it as wrong because it breaks trust and causes harm, though most say it is a private matter, not for the law.
Mission 4Evaluate

"Sex should only take place within marriage."

  • Reasons for: Genesis 2:24 and 1 Corinthians 6: sex expresses a lifelong union; marriage gives security for any children; Catholic teaching: sex should be unitive and procreative within marriage; Islam forbids zina.
  • Reasons against: many loving, committed couples are not married; liberal Christians and Humanists focus on love, consent and responsibility; Buddhist ethics asks about harm, not marital status.
  • Judge it by: Is marriage the only setting for total commitment? Should religious teaching or personal choice decide? What best protects people from harm?
Mission check

?A charity runs a sex-education talk. Which statement could be agreed by Catholics, Muslims, Orthodox Jews and Humanists?

Religions disagree about where sex belongs, but share values such as faithfulness, respect and consent.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Two people who love each other can marry in law. Why do believers reading the same texts still disagree?

Make a guess: Two people who love each other can marry in law. Why do believers reading the same texts still disagree?

Mission 5

Words and the law

  • Sexuality / sexual orientation: who a person is attracted to. Heterosexual: the opposite sex. Homosexual (gay, lesbian): the same sex. Bisexual: both.
  • Civil partnership: legal union, not called marriage. UK-wide since 2005 (Civil Partnership Act 2004).
  • Same-sex marriage: England and Wales from 2014 (Marriage (Same Sex Couples) Act 2013); Scotland 2014; Northern Ireland 2020.
  • No religious group is forced to hold same-sex weddings. Groups may opt in; the Church of England and Church in Wales are barred by the Act unless the law changes.
  • Cohabitation: a couple living together without marrying.
Mission 5

The traditional Christian view

  • Marriage is a lifelong union of one man and one woman: "they will be one flesh" (Genesis 2:24); Jesus repeats it (Mark 10:6–8).
  • Sex belongs only within that marriage, where it unites the couple and can create life.
  • Texts read as forbidding same-sex acts: Leviticus 18:22; Romans 1:26–27; 1 Corinthians 6:9–10; 1 Timothy 1:10.
  • Most who hold this view separate orientation (not a sin) from sexual acts (sinful outside man–woman marriage).
  • So: same-sex attracted Christians are called to celibacy; same-sex marriage is not a true marriage in God's eyes.
Mission 5

Catholic teaching

  • The Catechism (CCC 2357–2359, paraphrase): same-sex sexual acts cannot be approved; people with same-sex attraction must be treated with respect, compassion and sensitivity; unjust discrimination must be avoided; all are called to chastity.
  • Marriage can only be between a man and a woman, open to children.
  • The reason: sex is marital, unitive and procreative (CCC 2360–2365). Same-sex acts cannot be procreative.
  • 2023, Fiducia Supplicans: priests may give a short, informal blessing to couples in "irregular" situations, including same-sex couples, but never in a form like a wedding, and without approving the union.
Mission 5Read the source

Two ways of reading

"You shall not lie with a man as with a woman. That is detestable." (Leviticus 18:22, WEBBE)

  • Traditional reading: a moral law, repeated in the New Testament (1 Corinthians 6:9–10), so it still applies.
  • Liberal reading: part of a holiness code that also bans wearing two kinds of material (Leviticus 19:19); it addresses acts in an ancient setting, not loving, faithful partnerships.

Q1 Which other verse might a liberal Christian use to argue Leviticus should not be applied word for word?

Show answer

Leviticus 19:19: Christians don't keep that law either.

Q2 How would a traditionalist reply?

Show answer

The moral ban is repeated in the New Testament; food and fabric laws are not.

Mission 5Diversity

The range of Christian views

  • Catholic and Orthodox: acts sinful; persons respected; no same-sex marriage.
  • Many evangelicals: same view, from the authority of the Bible's plain sense.
  • Church of England: does not marry same-sex couples; since 2023 its Prayers of Love and Faith can be used to pray God's blessing on them in ordinary services.
  • Have opted in to same-sex weddings: Quakers, United Reformed Church (2016), Scottish Episcopal Church (2017), Methodist Church (2021, where a local church agrees), Church of Scotland (2022, ministers who opt in).
  • Liberal Christians: "God is love" (1 John 4:8); God made people as they are; faithful, loving relationships reflect God whatever the couple's sex.
Quick check
?A Christian says: "Being gay is not a sin, but sex outside marriage between a man and a woman is." Which group's teaching does this most closely match?
Mission 5

Non-religious views

  • Humanists: morality is about wellbeing and consent, not divine commands. A loving relationship between consenting adults harms no one.
  • Equality: the same rights for all couples. Humanists UK campaigned for the 2013 Act.
  • Atheists hold a range of views, but there is no religious reason against same-sex marriage.
  • Religious response: the state may change the law, but it cannot change what marriage is in God's design; the Act protects freedom of religion by letting faiths opt out.
  • Catholic response: respect every person and oppose unjust discrimination, but marriage is by nature man–woman and open to life.
Mission 5Evaluate

"Religious believers should accept same-sex marriage."

  • Reasons for: God is love (1 John 4:8); everyone is made in God's image (Genesis 1:27); faithful love is a good that churches already praise; the law treats all couples equally.
  • Reasons against: scripture defines marriage as man and woman (Genesis 2:24, Mark 10:6–8); texts forbid same-sex acts; marriage is open to children; churches must follow God, not changing culture.
  • Another view: accept civil same-sex marriage in law, but not as religious marriage.
  • Judge it by: How should ancient texts apply today? What is marriage for? Does respecting people mean approving every relationship?
Mission check

?A same-sex couple in England asks four places of worship to hold their wedding. Which could legally marry them there?

Law and religion now differ: the state allows same-sex marriage, and each faith decides whether to hold weddings, guided by how it reads its texts.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Can a promise made "till death us do part" ever be undone, and who gets to decide?

Make a guess: Can a promise made "till death us do part" ever be undone, and who gets to decide?

Mission 6

Ending a marriage

  • Divorce: the legal ending of a marriage. Separation: living apart while still married.
  • Annulment: a declaration that a marriage was never valid in the first place.
  • Remarriage: marrying again while a former spouse is still alive.
  • Law in England and Wales: since April 2022, no-fault divorce (Divorce, Dissolution and Separation Act 2020). The only ground: the marriage has broken down irretrievably; no one has to be blamed.
  • Common reasons: adultery, abuse, addiction, money worries, growing apart.
Mission 6

What Jesus taught

  • Marriage is God's work: "the two will become one flesh" (Mark 10:8; Genesis 2:24).
  • Matthew 19:1–12: Pharisees ask whether divorce is allowed. Jesus says Moses allowed it "because of the hardness of your hearts" (Matthew 19:8), but it was not God's plan.
  • In Matthew, one exception: sexual immorality (Matthew 19:9; 5:32). Mark has no exception (Mark 10:11–12).
Mission 6Read the source

Matthew 19

"What therefore God has joined together, don't let man tear apart." (Matthew 19:6)

"I tell you that whoever divorces his wife, except for sexual immorality, and marries another, commits adultery." (Matthew 19:9)

Q1 Who joins a couple in marriage, according to Matthew 19:6?

Show answer

God; so humans should not separate them.

Q2 What does Matthew 19:9 say remarriage after divorce usually is?

Show answer

Adultery, except where the divorce followed sexual immorality.

Mission 6

Catholic teaching: annulment, not divorce

  • A valid, consummated sacramental marriage cannot be dissolved by any human power (CCC 2382, paraphrase).
  • Divorce is a grave offence against the natural law (CCC 2384, paraphrase), but civil divorce may be tolerated to protect legal rights or children (CCC 2383); separation is allowed.
  • A spouse unjustly abandoned is not to blame (CCC 2386).
  • Remarriage while a former spouse lives is not accepted; the person cannot usually receive Holy Communion.
  • Annulment (declaration of nullity): a Church tribunal finds the marriage was never valid, e.g. no free consent, force or fear, no intention of a lifelong faithful union open to children. Both may then marry in church.
  • Pope Francis, Amoris Laetitia (2016): accompany divorced and remarried Catholics with care and discernment; some bishops allow Communion case by case.
Quick check
?Maria married at 18 because her family forced her. Years later a Catholic tribunal grants her an annulment. What does this mean?
Mission 6Diversity

Other Christians

  • Orthodox: marriage is meant for life, but out of mercy (economy) the Church allows divorce and up to three marriages; a second wedding has a penitential tone.
  • Church of England: since 2002 (General Synod), divorced people may remarry in church at the priest's discretion.
  • Methodists and many Protestants: divorce is a sad last resort; God forgives; remarriage can be a fresh start.
  • Some evangelicals: divorce only for adultery (Matthew 19:9) or desertion by an unbelieving spouse (1 Corinthians 7:15).
Mission 6

Situation ethics and non-religious views

  • Situation ethics (Joseph Fletcher, Situation Ethics, 1966): do the most loving (agape) thing. If staying together causes lasting harm, divorce may be the loving act.
  • Humanists and atheists: marriage is a human commitment; if it has failed, divorce can be best for adults and children; remarriage is fine.
  • They support no-fault divorce: less conflict, less blame.
  • Catholic response: compassion is right, but love also means keeping promises; separation can protect people without denying the marriage.
Mission 6Evaluate

Sanctity of vows or compassion?

  • Sanctity of vows: the promise was made before God for life; Jesus taught against divorce (Matthew 19:6); stability for children; easy divorce weakens commitment.
  • Compassion: no one should stay in abuse or misery; God forgives and offers new starts; children can suffer more from conflict; Moses allowed divorce because of human weakness (Matthew 19:8).
  • Another view: both matter: work hard to save the marriage, but allow separation or divorce when it truly fails.
  • Judge it by: Is a vow unbreakable or a commitment to try? Whose welfare counts most? Does allowing divorce show mercy or weaken marriage?
Mission 6Worked answer

Vows and compassion

Question: "Religious people should never divorce." Evaluate this statement.

  • For: Catholics believe a valid marriage cannot be dissolved (CCC 2382); Jesus said "What therefore God has joined together, don't let man tear apart" (Matthew 19:6). Vows before God are sacred.
  • Against: Muslims, Jews and most Protestants allow divorce as a last resort; staying in abuse is not loving (situation ethics); God forgives.
  • Weighing: the Catholic view rests on the sacrament being permanent; the other view rests on compassion for real suffering. Which is stronger depends on whether a vow can be broken by the other partner's behaviour.
Mission check

?Two Christians each divorce after their spouses leave them. One is Catholic, one is an Anglican in England. Both want to marry again in church. What is most likely?

Christians share Jesus' teaching on lifelong marriage, but differ on how to apply it: annulment, mercy, or pastoral discretion.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Theist: believes in God; atheist: believes there is no God; agnostic: cannot know.
  • Humanists: no God, one life, ethics from reason, empathy and human flourishing.

Mission 2

  • Christianity is Britain's main religious tradition in history and culture; Britain is diverse in religion and non-religious belief.
  • Census (England and Wales): Christian 72% (2001) → 59% (2011) → 46% (2021); no religion 15% → 25% → 37%.

Mission 3

  • Christians see marriage as a lifelong union before God (Genesis 2:24; Mark 10:6–9); Catholics as a sacrament, unitive and procreative.
  • Other faiths: nikah contract (Islam), kiddushin (Judaism), samskara (Hinduism), Anand Karaj (Sikhism), social contract (Buddhism).
  • Cohabitation and civil partnership: accepted by some, rejected by others; free consent is essential to any marriage.

Mission 4

  • Many Christians, especially Catholics, see sexuality as God's good gift, meant for marriage: marital, unitive and procreative.
  • Theology of the Body: the body reveals the person and is made for self-giving love.
  • Views on sex before marriage range from forbidden (Catholic, Islam, Orthodox Judaism) to accepted when loving and responsible (liberal believers, Humanists); adultery is condemned across traditions.

Mission 5

  • UK law allows civil partnership (2005) and same-sex marriage (2014; NI 2020); faiths can opt in or out.
  • Traditional Christians, Catholics, Orthodox Jews and most Muslims forbid same-sex acts while calling for respect; liberal Christians, Reform Jews, many Western Buddhists and Humanists accept same-sex relationships.

Mission 6

  • Divorce ends a marriage in law; annulment declares it was never valid. Catholics accept annulment, not remarriage after divorce; most other Christians allow remarriage.
  • The key debate: sanctity of vows (Matthew 19:6) vs compassion (Matthew 19:8).
  • Situation ethics and Humanists put compassion and the welfare of those involved first.
