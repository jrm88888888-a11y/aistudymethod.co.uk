Mission 1

Mission 1: A reaction leaves a cloudy mix of product, leftovers and water. How do chemists get the product out pure?

Make a guess: A reaction leaves a cloudy mix of product, leftovers and water. How do chemists get the product out pure?

Mission 1

Heating under reflux

  • Use it for slow reactions with volatile liquids: heat for a long time, lose nothing.
  • Quickfit glassware: ground-glass joints fit together without bungs.
  • Round-bottomed flask + anti-bumping granules (smooth boiling).
  • Condenser vertical: vapour condenses and drips back into the flask.
  • Water in at the bottom, out at the top: the jacket stays full.
  • Top left open. Never heat a sealed apparatus.
  • Heat with an electric heater, water bath or sand bath: organic liquids are flammable.
Mission 1

Distillation

  • Separates liquids by boiling point.
  • Flask + still head + thermometer bulb level with the side arm: it reads the vapour that is distilling.
  • Condenser sloping down, water in at the lower end.
  • Collect the fraction that distils within a degree or two of the product's boiling point.
  • Cool the receiver in ice for a very volatile product.
Mission 1

Reflux or distil? Oxidising ethanol

  • Ethanol + acidified potassium dichromate(VI). Boiling points: ethanal 20 °C, ethanol 78 °C, ethanoic acid 118 °C.
  • Want the aldehyde? Add the oxidant slowly to warm ethanol and distil off ethanal as it forms.
  • It leaves before it can be oxidised further.
  • Want the carboxylic acid? Heat under reflux with excess oxidant. Ethanal can't escape, so it is oxidised to the acid.
  • Then distil to collect the acid.
Quick check
?A student wants to make propanal from propan-1-ol using acidified potassium dichromate(VI). Which method gives the most propanal?
Mission 1

Separating funnel and washing

  • Two liquids that don't mix form layers. The denser layer is at the bottom.
  • Not sure which is which? Add a little water and see which layer grows.
  • Washing: shake the crude product with an aqueous solution, then separate.
  • Water removes water-soluble impurities. Sodium hydrogencarbonate solution removes acid.
  • Stopper, invert, open the tap often to release pressure (NaHCO3 + acid gives CO2).
  • Run off the lower layer through the tap. Keep the layer that holds your product.
  • Solvent extraction: shake an aqueous mixture with an immiscible organic solvent. The organic product moves into the solvent layer.
  • Separate, extract again with fresh solvent, combine the extracts, then distil off the solvent.
Mission 1

Drying and redistillation

  • The washed organic layer is cloudy: it holds tiny drops of water.
  • Add an anhydrous salt: MgSO4, CaCl2 or Na2SO4. Swirl.
  • Keep adding until the salt no longer clumps and the liquid is clear.
  • Decant or filter off the drying agent.
  • Redistil, collecting only near the product's boiling point.
  • A boiling temperature close to the data value is a check of purity.
Mission 1

Worked example: 2-chloro-2-methylpropane

  • Shake 2-methylpropan-2-ol with concentrated HCl in a separating funnel (fast at room temperature: no reflux).
  • Two layers form. The product (density 0.84 g cm⁻³) is the upper layer. Discard the lower aqueous layer.
  • Wash with NaHCO3(aq), venting often, to remove acid. Then wash with water.
  • Dry with anhydrous CaCl2 or Na2SO4 until clear.
  • Distil, collecting at about 50–52 °C (bp 51 °C).
  • This is Core practical 6.
Quick check
?A student shakes a crude organic product with sodium hydrogencarbonate solution in a separating funnel. Why must the tap be opened often?
Mission 1

Purifying a solid: recrystallisation

  • Choose a solvent the product dissolves in well when hot, poorly when cold.
  • Dissolve the crude solid in the minimum volume of hot solvent.
  • Filter while hot to remove insoluble impurities.
  • Cool slowly: the product crystallises; soluble impurities, present in small amounts, stay in solution.
  • Filter under reduced pressure (Büchner funnel), wash with a little cold solvent, dry.
Mission 1

Melting point: is it pure?

  • Melting point apparatus: a little solid in a capillary tube, heated slowly near the melting point.
  • Pure: melts sharply (within about 1 °C), at the data-book value. Aspirin: 136 °C.
  • Impure: melts lower and over a wider range.
Quick check
?Pure aspirin melts at 136 °C. A student's recrystallised aspirin melts over the range 126–131 °C. What does this show?
Mission 1

Steam distillation

  • For a compound that is volatile, doesn't mix with water and might decompose at its boiling point.
  • Pass steam through the mixture: the compound distils with the water below 100 °C.
  • Collect the milky distillate; separate the oily product from the water.
  • Example: limonene (bp 176 °C) from orange peel.
Mission 1

Hazards and control measures

  • Read the hazard data, then choose a control measure to match.
  • Flammable (ethoxyethane, ethanol): no flames; electric heater or water bath.
  • Corrosive (conc. H2SO4, conc. HCl): gloves, goggles; add slowly.
  • Toxic or harmful vapour (bromine, ethanoyl chloride): fume cupboard.
  • Justify each step: say what it does and why it is needed.
Mission check

?A student makes 1-bromobutane (bp 102 °C, density 1.27 g cm⁻³) from butan-1-ol (bp 118 °C). The crude product contains water, acid and some unreacted alcohol. Which purification is best?

Each step uses a different property: density (separate), reactivity (wash), water uptake (dry), boiling point (distil). Then a melting or boiling point proves purity.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Benzene is drawn with three double bonds. So why doesn't it react like an alkene?

Make a guess: Benzene is drawn with three double bonds. So why doesn't it react like an alkene?

Mission 2

Three ideas from the video

  • σ bond: head-on overlap of orbitals; density on the line between the nuclei.
  • π bond: sideways overlap of p orbitals; density above and below that line. C=C = σ + π.
  • Beyond your course, as background: a ring carbon with three σ bonds at 120° is called sp². It keeps one p orbital for π bonding. Each H bonds with its 1s orbital.
  • Background: resonance structures are alternative drawings (linked by ↔) of one real molecule. It never flips between them.
  • You won't be asked to use the last two terms. The delocalised model below says the same thing.
Mission 2

Kekulé's model

  • Kekulé (1865): a ring of six carbons, alternating C–C and C=C bonds.
  • It predicts two bond lengths: short C=C and long C–C.
  • It predicts alkene reactions: rapid addition of bromine.
  • It predicts 3 × the hydrogenation enthalpy of one C=C.
  • All three predictions fail.
Mission 2

The delocalised model

  • Each C forms σ bonds to two C and one H, all in one plane, at 120°.
  • Each C has one electron left in a p orbital, above and below the plane.
  • The six p orbitals overlap sideways all the way round the ring.
  • Result: six π electrons delocalised in rings of density above and below the plane.
  • Drawn as a hexagon with a circle inside.
  • You may draw benzene either way (circle or Kekulé) in equations and mechanisms.
Quick check
?Which statement describes the bonding in a benzene molecule?
Mission 2

Evidence 1: bond lengths

  • X-ray diffraction: benzene is planar, with all bond angles 120°.
  • All six C–C bonds are the same length: 0.139 nm.
  • Compare C–C 0.154 nm and C=C 0.134 nm.
  • Kekulé predicts two lengths. One length, in between, fits delocalisation.
Mission 2

Evidence 2: enthalpy of hydrogenation

  • Cyclohexene + H2 → cyclohexane: ΔH = −120 kJ mol⁻¹ (one C=C).
  • Kekulé triene, three C=C: expect 3 × −120 = −360 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Benzene + 3H2 → cyclohexane: measured −208 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • 152 kJ mol⁻¹ less released, to make the same product.
  • So benzene starts 152 kJ mol⁻¹ lower in energy: delocalisation makes it more stable.
Quick check
?The enthalpy change of hydrogenation of cyclohexene (one C=C bond) is about −120 kJ mol⁻¹. For benzene, forming cyclohexane, it is about −208 kJ mol⁻¹, much less exothermic than the −360 kJ mol⁻¹ predicted for a structure with three localised C=C bonds. Which statement explains the difference?
Mission 2

Evidence 3: resistance to reaction

  • Cyclohexene decolourises bromine at room temperature, in the dark: addition.
  • Benzene does not. It reacts only with a catalyst (halogen carrier), and then by substitution.
  • Alkene: π electrons localised between two carbons. High electron density polarises Br2 and attracts it.
  • Benzene: π electrons spread over six carbons. Electron density between any two is lower.
  • Too low to polarise Br2 enough: no reaction without a catalyst.
Mission 2

Why substitution, not addition

  • Adding across the ring would destroy the delocalised π system.
  • That would throw away about 150 kJ mol⁻¹ of extra stability.
  • Substitution swaps an H atom for another group. The delocalised ring survives.
  • So benzene reacts by electrophilic substitution, with strong electrophiles (see Mission 3).
  • Answer to the opening question: benzene has no localised C=C bonds, so it doesn't behave like an alkene.
Quick check
?Cyclohexene decolourises bromine at room temperature, but benzene does not react with bromine unless a catalyst is added. Which statement explains this?
Mission check

?Phenylethene, C6H5CH=CH2, has a benzene ring and a C=C in its side chain. It is shaken with a solution of bromine in hexane, in the dark, at room temperature. What happens?

One molecule, two kinds of π electrons. Localised ones add bromine like any alkene; delocalised ones in the ring hold on to their extra stability.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Benzene does react with bromine, given a catalyst. Why does a hydrogen get swapped rather than bromine adding?

Make a guess: Benzene does react with bromine, given a catalyst. Why does a hydrogen get swapped rather than bromine adding?

Mission 3

Why the ring attracts electrophiles

  • Benzene's six delocalised π electrons make the ring electron-rich, like an alkene's C=C.
  • So it attracts electrophiles: electron-pair acceptors, written E⁺.
  • But the density is spread over six carbons (see Mission 2). It can't polarise Br2 or attack weak electrophiles.
  • So benzene needs a strong, positive electrophile, made with a catalyst.
Mission 3

Substitution, not addition

  • Addition would destroy the delocalised ring and its extra stability.
  • Substitution swaps one H for E. The ring is restored.
  • Overall: C6H6 + E⁺ → C6H5E + H⁺.
  • Benzene also burns in air with a very smoky flame: its high C : H ratio leaves unburnt carbon (soot).
Mission 3

Making the electrophile: the nitronium ion

  • Reagents: concentrated nitric acid + concentrated sulfuric acid (the nitrating mixture).
  • Keep at about 50 °C (below 55 °C). Hotter: more than one NO2 group goes on.
  • Electrophile: the nitronium ion, NO2⁺.
  • HNO3 + 2H2SO4 → NO2⁺ + H3O⁺ + 2HSO4⁻
  • Also written: HNO3 + H2SO4 → NO2⁺ + HSO4⁻ + H2O.
  • Sulfuric acid is the stronger acid: it protonates nitric acid, which then loses water.
Mission 3

The mechanism, arrow by arrow

  • Arrow 1: from the delocalised ring (inside the circle) to the N of NO2⁺. A C–N bond forms.
  • Intermediate: the attacked carbon now holds both H and NO2. Draw a horseshoe over the other five carbons, gap facing that carbon, + inside.
  • Arrow 2: from the C–H bond into the ring. The ring is restored; H⁺ leaves.
  • H⁺ + HSO4⁻ → H2SO4: the catalyst is regenerated.
  • Overall: C6H6 + NO2⁺ → C6H5NO2 + H⁺. Product: nitrobenzene.
  • Back to the opening question: bromine with a catalyst gives Br⁺, which reacts by these same two arrows. Losing H⁺ gets the stable ring back; adding Br⁻ would not.
Quick check
?Benzene reacts with a charged electrophile, E⁺. Which sequence describes the mechanism?
Quick check
?The diagram shows the intermediate formed when benzene reacts with an electrophile, E⁺, together with the curly arrow for the step that follows. What happens in the step shown?
Mission 3

Halogenation

  • Benzene + bromine + a halogen carrier, at room temperature, dry.
  • Halogen carriers: iron, FeBr3, or AlBr3. Iron filings make FeBr3 from the bromine.
  • Electrophile: Br⁺, made by the carrier: Br2 + FeBr3 → Br⁺ + FeBr4⁻.
  • Same two arrows: ring → Br⁺; then C–H bond → ring, losing H⁺.
  • H⁺ + FeBr4⁻ → FeBr3 + HBr. Product: bromobenzene, C6H5Br.
  • Chlorination works the same way: Cl2 with AlCl3 or FeCl3 gives Cl⁺.
Mission 3

Friedel–Crafts alkylation

  • Benzene + halogenoalkane + AlCl3 catalyst, anhydrous (water destroys AlCl3).
  • CH3CH2Cl + AlCl3 → CH3CH2⁺ + AlCl4⁻
  • Ring → C⁺ of the carbocation; then C–H bond → ring, losing H⁺.
  • H⁺ + AlCl4⁻ → AlCl3 + HCl. Product: ethylbenzene, C6H5CH2CH3.
  • A new C–C bond to the ring: useful in synthesis.
Mission 3

Friedel–Crafts acylation

  • Benzene + acyl chloride + AlCl3, anhydrous, heated under reflux.
  • CH3COCl + AlCl3 → CH3CO⁺ + AlCl4⁻ (the acylium ion)
  • Arrow 1: from the ring to the C of CH3CO⁺. Intermediate: horseshoe, + inside.
  • Arrow 2: from the C–H bond into the ring; H⁺ is lost.
  • H⁺ + AlCl4⁻ → AlCl3 + HCl. Product: phenylethanone, C6H5COCH3, an aromatic ketone.
Quick check
?Benzene is refluxed with propanoyl chloride, CH3CH2COCl, and anhydrous AlCl3. Which is the electrophile, and which is the organic product?
Mission check

?Benzene reacts with chlorine when AlCl3 is present. After Cl⁺ bonds to the ring, the positive intermediate could, in principle, gain a Cl⁻ ion, as the carbocation from an alkene does. Instead it loses H⁺. Why?

Whatever the electrophile, the second arrow is the same. Losing H⁺ puts the delocalised ring back, which is why benzene substitutes.

Make a guess: Fish smells fishy because of amines. Why does a squeeze of lemon juice take the smell away?

Mission 4

What is an amine?

  • Take ammonia, NH3. Replace H atoms with alkyl or aryl groups: an amine.
  • Primary: one C on the N, e.g. butylamine, CH3CH2CH2CH2NH2, and phenylamine, C6H5NH2.
  • Secondary: two C on the N, e.g. (CH3)2NH. Tertiary: three, e.g. (CH3)3N.
  • Every amine N has a lone pair. That drives all its chemistry.
  • Amide, not amine: N bonded to a C=O carbon (–CONH2, –CONHR).
Quick check
?Which statement about the compound (CH3)3CNH2 is correct?
Mission 4

Amines are bases

  • The N lone pair accepts H⁺: amines are bases, like ammonia.
  • In water, a weak base: CH3CH2CH2CH2NH2 + H2O ⇌ CH3CH2CH2CH2NH3⁺ + OH⁻. The solution is alkaline.
  • With acids, salts: CH3CH2CH2CH2NH2 + HCl → CH3CH2CH2CH2NH3⁺Cl⁻ (butylammonium chloride). No water forms.
  • Add NaOH to the salt and the free amine comes back: RNH3⁺ + OH⁻ → RNH2 + H2O.
  • Back to the opening question: fishy-smelling amines are volatile. Lemon's acid turns them into ionic salts, which don't evaporate.
Mission 4

Which is the strongest base?

  • Base strength depends on how available the N lone pair is to accept H⁺.
  • Alkyl groups push electrons towards N (positive inductive effect). Lone pair more available: stronger base than NH3.
  • Phenyl: the lone pair is partly delocalised into the ring. Less available: much weaker base than NH3.
  • Order: primary aliphatic amine > ammonia > phenylamine.
  • Data, pKa of the conjugate acid (higher = stronger base): CH3CH2NH3⁺ 10.7 · NH4⁺ 9.3 · C6H5NH3⁺ 4.6.
Quick check
?Which lists ethylamine, ammonia and phenylamine in order of base strength, strongest first?
Mission 4

Making aliphatic amines

  • Route 1: halogenoalkane + excess ammonia in ethanol, heated in a sealed tube.
  • CH3CH2CH2CH2Br + 2NH3 → CH3CH2CH2CH2NH2 + NH4Br
  • Excess NH3 makes the primary amine the main product, but some 2° and 3° amine still forms.
  • Route 2: reduce a nitrile: CH3CH2CH2CN + 4[H] → CH3CH2CH2CH2NH2. Only the primary amine forms.
  • Reducing agent: LiAlH4 in dry ether, or H2 with a nickel catalyst.
Mission 4

Amines attack halogenoalkanes too

  • The amine product also has a lone pair: it is a nucleophile.
  • Primary amine + halogenoalkane → secondary amine; then → tertiary amine.
  • e.g. butylamine + bromoethane → CH3CH2CH2CH2NHCH2CH3 + HBr (taken up as a salt by more amine).
Mission 4

Making aromatic amines

  • Reduce nitrobenzene with tin and concentrated HCl, heated under reflux.
  • C6H5NO2 + 6[H] → C6H5NH2 + 2H2O
  • In all that acid, the product is the phenylammonium ion, C6H5NH3⁺: a salt.
  • Add NaOH(aq) to free phenylamine: C6H5NH3⁺ + OH⁻ → C6H5NH2 + H2O.
  • Nitrobenzene comes from nitrating benzene (see Mission 3).
Quick check
?Nitrobenzene is heated under reflux with tin and concentrated hydrochloric acid. Excess sodium hydroxide solution is then added before the phenylamine is separated. Why is the NaOH needed?
Mission 4

Amines + acyl chlorides: amides

  • The N lone pair attacks the δ+ carbonyl carbon of an acyl chloride.
  • Ammonia gives a primary amide, RCONH2. A primary amine gives a secondary (N-substituted) amide, RCONHR′.
  • CH3COCl + 2CH3CH2CH2CH2NH2 → CH3CONHCH2CH2CH2CH3 + CH3CH2CH2CH2NH3⁺Cl⁻
  • Product: N-butylethanamide. Adding CH3CO– to N is ethanoylation.
Mission 4

Butylamine on the bench

  • Smell: fishy, like ammonia. Damp red litmus turns blue.
  • Add to Cu²⁺(aq): a pale blue precipitate of copper(II) hydroxide forms (OH⁻ from the amine solution).
  • Excess butylamine: the precipitate dissolves to a deep blue solution, [Cu(CH3CH2CH2CH2NH2)4(H2O)2]²⁺.
  • Just like ammonia: the N lone pair forms a dative bond to Cu²⁺ (ligand substitution).
  • In CP15, these tests point to an amine in an unknown.
Mission check

?A reaction mixture contains phenylamine and some unreacted nitrobenzene, dissolved in an organic solvent. It is shaken with dilute hydrochloric acid in a separating funnel. Which compound moves into the water layer, and why?

Same chemistry as lemon on fish. An acid turns an amine into a salt, which stays in water instead of escaping.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Why does making a polyester give off water, when making some other plastics loses no atoms at all?

Make a guess: Why does making a polyester give off water, when making some other plastics loses no atoms at all?

Mission 5

Two ways to build a polymer

  • Addition polymerisation: monomers with a C=C join up. The polymer is the only product: no atoms lost.
  • Condensation polymerisation: each monomer has two functional groups. Two groups react to make a link.
  • Every link releases a small molecule: usually H2O.
  • So a condensation polymer has fewer atoms than the monomers it came from.
Mission 5

Making a polyester

  • Diol + dicarboxylic acid → polyester + water.
  • –OH reacts with –COOH: an ester link, –COO–, plus H2O.
  • Each monomer has a group at both ends, so the chain grows in both directions.
  • Example: ethane-1,2-diol + benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid → PET.
Mission 5

Drawing the repeat unit

  • Draw the two monomers end to end.
  • Remove H from each –OH and OH from each –COOH.
  • Join the fragments with ester links. Add open bonds at both ends, brackets and n.
  • The repeat unit holds one residue of each monomer: two links, two H2O.
  • PET: –[–OCH2CH2OOC–C6H4–CO–]n–
Quick check
?Ethane-1,2-diol, HOCH2CH2OH, and hexanedioic acid, HOOC(CH2)4COOH, react to form a polyester. Which option gives the repeat unit and describes it correctly?
Mission 5

Polyamides

  • Diamine + dicarboxylic acid → polyamide + water.
  • –NH2 reacts with –COOH: an amide link, –CONH–, plus H2O.
  • Nylon 6,6: hexane-1,6-diamine + hexanedioic acid → –[–NH(CH2)6NHCO(CH2)4CO–]n–
Mission 5

Amino acids make polyamides too

  • An amino acid has –NH2 and –COOH on the same molecule.
  • So one kind of monomer is enough: –NH2 of one reacts with –COOH of the next.
  • The link is an amide link. In proteins it is called a peptide link.
  • Repeat unit for one amino acid, H2NCH(R)COOH: –[–NHCH(R)CO–]n–
  • Chains of many amino acids are polypeptides; proteins are polypeptides.
Quick check
?Hexane-1,6-diamine, H2N(CH2)6NH2, and decanedioic acid, HOOC(CH2)8COOH, react to form the polyamide nylon 6,10. Which option gives the repeat unit and describes it correctly?
Mission 5

Working backwards: chain → monomers

  • Find the links in the chain: –COO– (ester) or –CONH– (amide).
  • Break each link at the C–O or C–N single bond.
  • Add OH back to the C=O carbon; add H back to the O or N.
  • Diacid + diol, or diacid + diamine, or one amino acid: read off the monomers.
Mission check

?Lactic acid, CH3CH(OH)COOH, is made from plant sugars. Its molecules join into poly(lactic acid), PLA, used for compostable cups. Which statement describes how PLA forms?

Same reason as for PET. Two groups react to make each link, and the leftover H and OH leave as water.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Reflux: heat without loss. Distil: separate by boiling point. Separating funnel: by density. Recrystallise: by solubility, hot v cold.
  • Pure solid: sharp melting point at the data value. Impure: lower and broader.
  • Your core practicals: CP5, CP6, CP16 (aspirin); CP12 uses the same filtering and recrystallising.

Mission 2

  • Benzene: planar ring; six p electrons delocalised above and below; all C–C 0.139 nm.
  • Evidence against Kekulé: one bond length, 152 kJ mol⁻¹ extra stability, no ready addition of bromine.
  • Delocalised density is too low to polarise Br2, and addition would destroy the ring: so benzene substitutes.

Mission 3

  • Ring π electrons attack a strong electrophile; the positive intermediate loses H⁺ to restore the ring.
  • Nitration: conc. HNO3 + conc. H2SO4, about 50 °C, electrophile NO2⁺.
  • Halogenation: Br2 + halogen carrier (Br⁺). Alkylation: RCl + AlCl3 (R⁺).
  • Acylation: RCOCl + AlCl3 (RCO⁺) gives an aromatic ketone.

Mission 4

  • Amines: the N lone pair makes them bases (salts with acids, freed by alkali) and nucleophiles.
  • Make them: halogenoalkane + excess NH3; nitrile reduction; nitroarene + Sn / conc. HCl, then NaOH.
  • Base strength: aliphatic amine > NH3 > phenylamine (lone-pair availability).
  • Amines + acyl chlorides → N-substituted amides.

Mission 5

  • Addition: C=C monomers, no atoms lost. Condensation: two groups react at each link, losing H2O (or HCl).
  • Polyester: –COO– links from diol + diacid. Polyamide: –CONH– links from diamine + diacid, or from amino acids.
