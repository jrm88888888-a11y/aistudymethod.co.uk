Mission 1

What pacifism is

  • Pacifism: the belief that war and violence are wrong; disputes should be settled peacefully.
  • Absolute pacifism: violence is never right, not even in self-defence or to protect others.
  • Conditional (relative) pacifism: violence is almost always wrong, but may be the lesser evil in extreme cases; or opposition to some kinds of war (e.g. nuclear war).
  • Conscientious objector: someone who refuses to fight on grounds of conscience. Many have served in non-combat roles such as ambulance work.
  • Passive resistance / non-violent direct action: opposing injustice without violence: marches, boycotts, sit-ins, refusing to obey unjust laws.
  • Pacifism is not doing nothing: pacifists actively work for peace (see Mission 2).

Mission 1

Quakers and the peace churches

  • The Religious Society of Friends (Quakers), founded in 17th-century England, holds a Peace Testimony.
  • Their declaration to King Charles II (1660): "We utterly deny all outward wars and strife and fightings with outward weapons, for any end or under any pretence whatsoever."
  • Reason: "that of God" is in every person, so no one may be killed.
  • Many Quakers were conscientious objectors in the World Wars; some served in the Friends Ambulance Unit.
  • Other historic peace churches: Mennonites, Church of the Brethren.

Mission 1

Catholic teaching and the popes

  • The Catholic Church is not absolutely pacifist: states may use force in legitimate defence under strict conditions (CCC 2308–2309).
  • But it honours those who renounce violence, as long as others' rights are not harmed (CCC 2306, paraphrase), and supports conscientious objectors (CCC 2311).
  • A developing theme in papal teaching:
  • John XXIII, Pacem in Terris (1963): in the atomic age, war can hardly be a way to restore justice (paraphrase).
  • Paul VI at the United Nations (1965): "No more war, war never again!"
  • Francis, World Day of Peace message (2017): "Nonviolence: A Style of Politics for Peace". Fratelli Tutti (2020) repeats "Never again war!"
  • Some Catholics (e.g. members of Pax Christi) now ask the Church to move further towards non-violence; others defend the just war tradition.
Mission 1

Non-violent resistance in action

  • Mohandas (Mahatma) Gandhi (1869–1948): satyagraha ("truth-force"): resisting injustice by non-violence and willingness to suffer. The Salt March (1930) helped win Indian independence (1947).
  • Martin Luther King Jr (1929–1968), Baptist minister: led the Montgomery bus boycott (1955–56) and the civil rights movement. Inspired by the Sermon on the Mount and by Gandhi.
  • Desmond Tutu (1931–2021), Anglican archbishop: non-violent opposition to apartheid in South Africa; Nobel Peace Prize 1984.

Mission 1Diversity

Other religions

  • Islam: not pacifist overall (defensive jihad is allowed), but peace is the goal: "if they incline to peace, incline thou also to it" (Qur'an 8:61, Pickthall).
  • Abdul Ghaffar Khan (1890–1988) led the Muslim non-violent movement Khudai Khidmatgar ("Servants of God") against British rule.
  • Judaism: self-defence is allowed, so most Jews are not pacifists; peace is the hope of the prophets (Micah 4:1–6; Isaiah 2:4).
  • Buddhism: many Buddhists are pacifists: the First Precept is to refrain from harming living beings.
  • The Upayakausalya Sutra tells of the Buddha in a past life killing one man to save many: some read it as allowing conditional pacifism ("skilful means").
  • Hinduism: ahimsa (non-harming); Gandhi's satyagraha.
  • Sikhism: not absolutely pacifist (Dharam Yudh), but peace is the goal.

Mission 1

Criticisms and non-religious views

  • Criticism: pacifism may let aggressors win. Could Hitler have been stopped without war?
  • Dietrich Bonhoeffer (1906–1945), a German Lutheran pastor drawn to pacifism, joined resistance circles plotting against Hitler, believing it was his duty; he was executed in 1945.
  • Criticism: there is a duty to protect the weak, and doing nothing can also cause death.
  • Responses: non-violence has worked (India 1947; US civil rights; the largely peaceful revolutions of 1989); violence breeds more violence.
  • Non-religious views: Humanists differ. Many are conditional pacifists who judge each war by its consequences and human rights. Bertrand Russell (1872–1970) was imprisoned in 1918 for anti-war writing and became first president of CND (1958).
Mission 1Evaluate

"Religious believers should always be pacifists."

  • Reasons for: Jesus taught "turn … the other also" and "love your enemies"; Quakers, many Buddhists and Gandhi show non-violence can work; modern war kills mainly civilians.
  • Reasons against: most Christians accept just war as a last resort (CCC 2309); Islam, Judaism, Sikhism and Hinduism allow defensive war; failing to protect the innocent may be the greater evil (Bonhoeffer).
  • Another view: conditional pacifism: work for peace always, but accept force as a last resort.
  • Judge it by: Is non-violence a rule or a goal? Is a refusal to protect others itself a choice to let harm happen? Has non-violence succeeded against brutal regimes?
Mission 1Worked answer

Two contrasting beliefs

Question: Explain two contrasting beliefs in contemporary British society about pacifism.

  • Belief 1: Most Quakers are absolute pacifists: all war is wrong. Development: "that of God" is in every person, and Jesus taught "love your enemies" (Matthew 5:44), so no one should be killed.
  • Belief 2: Most Christians (e.g. Catholics, Anglicans) are not pacifists: war can be justified as a last resort. Development: governments have a duty to protect citizens; the Catechism sets strict conditions for legitimate defence (CCC 2309).

Mission 2

More than "no war"

  • Peacemaking: actively working to end conflict and build a peace that lasts.
  • Negative peace: no fighting. Positive peace: justice, fairness and trust, so that fighting does not return.
  • Peace and justice go together: "The work of righteousness will be peace" (Isaiah 32:17).
  • Peacemaking includes: dialogue, mediation (a neutral helper), justice for grievances, forgiveness and reconciliation (restoring relationships; see Mission 3).
  • Pope Paul VI (World Day of Peace, 1972): "If you want peace, work for justice."

Mission 2

Christian peacemakers

  • St Oscar Romero (1917–1980), Archbishop of San Salvador: spoke out against violence and injustice towards the poor as El Salvador slid into civil war; he was shot while celebrating Mass (1980) and canonised in 2018.
  • Dorothy Day (1897–1980): co-founded the Catholic Worker movement (1933): houses of hospitality for the poor; a committed pacifist who opposed war, even the Second World War.
  • Desmond Tutu (1931–2021), Anglican archbishop: led non-violent opposition to apartheid; chaired South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission (1996–98).
  • Martin Luther King Jr (1929–1968): non-violent civil rights campaigner (see Mission 1).
  • Each was motivated by the Gospel's link between peace and justice.

Mission 2

Christian organisations for peace

  • Pax Christi (founded 1945 in France): international Catholic peace movement. Works for reconciliation, disarmament and peace education; promotes non-violence.
  • Community of Sant'Egidio (Rome, 1968): Catholic lay community. Mediated the talks that ended Mozambique's civil war (Rome peace accord, 1992); runs dialogue between enemies and help for refugees.
  • Corrymeela Community (Northern Ireland, 1965): Christian community bringing Catholics and Protestants together for reconciliation.
  • Christian CND (CCND): campaigns for nuclear disarmament on Christian grounds.
Mission 2

The United Nations

  • Founded in 1945, after the Second World War, "to save succeeding generations from the scourge of war" (UN Charter, Preamble).
  • Security Council: can order sanctions or approve the use of force to restore peace.
  • Peacekeeping (since 1948): "blue helmet" troops monitor ceasefires and protect civilians; awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 1988.
  • Promotes human rights (Universal Declaration of Human Rights, 1948), development and humanitarian aid.
  • Many religious leaders support its work; popes have addressed the General Assembly since 1965.
  • Criticisms: permanent members can veto action; peacekeepers have sometimes failed to protect civilians.
Mission 2Diversity

Peacemaking in other religions

  • Islam: salam (peace) is a name of God and the Muslim greeting. "Repel the evil deed with one which is better, then lo! he, between whom and thee there was enmity (will become) as though he was a bosom friend." (Qur'an 41:34, Pickthall)
  • Muslim peace groups (e.g. the Muslim Peace Fellowship, 1994) work for reconciliation.
  • Judaism: "seek peace, and pursue it" (Psalm 34:14, JPS 1917; 34:15 in Hebrew numbering).
  • Buddhism: "hatred does not cease by hatred at any time: hatred ceases by love" (Dhammapada 5, Müller). The Buddhist Peace Fellowship (1978) and Engaged Buddhism (Thich Nhat Hanh, 1926–2022) apply mindfulness and compassion to conflict.
  • Hinduism: Gandhi's satyagraha; prayers end "Om shanti, shanti, shanti" (peace).
  • Sikhism: service (sewa) for all; Khalsa Aid (1999) brings relief to people in war zones whatever their faith.

Mission 2

How conflicts are resolved

  • Steps: ceasefire → talks (often with a mediator) → agreement on justice and power-sharing → reconciliation over years.
  • Northern Ireland: churches and individuals (e.g. Fr Alec Reid, a Redemptorist priest) helped open talks that led to the Good Friday Agreement (1998).
  • South Africa: the Truth and Reconciliation Commission let victims tell their stories and offered amnesty for full confession.
  • Non-religious views: Humanists work for peace through human rights, democracy, education and international law. Peacemaking does not need religion, but many religious people are motivated by it.

Mission 2Evaluate

"Working for peace is the most important thing a religious believer can do."

  • Reasons for: "Blessed are the peacemakers" (Matthew 5:9); every faith values peace (salam, shalom, shanti); without peace, nothing else (worship, education, care for the poor) is secure.
  • Reasons against: many believers say worship of God, or following God's will, comes first; justice may come before peace (a false peace can hide oppression); caring for the poor and sick is equally commanded (Matthew 25).
  • Another view: peace, justice and worship are inseparable: Romero and King saw peacemaking as part of following God.
  • Judge it by: Can there be peace without justice? What does each religion say is the most important duty? Is peacemaking only for leaders, or for everyone?

Mission 3

Four key words

  • Peace: not just the absence of war or conflict, but harmony, security and justice. Hebrew shalom and Arabic salaam mean wholeness and well-being.
  • Justice: fairness; giving each person what is due to them; putting right what is wrong.
  • Forgiveness: letting go of resentment and the wish for revenge towards someone who has wronged you.
  • Reconciliation: restoring a broken relationship, so that former enemies can live or work together again.
  • They link: justice is often needed for lasting peace; forgiveness opens the way to reconciliation.

Christian teaching on forgiveness

  • In the Lord's Prayer: "Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors." (Matthew 6:12, WEBBE)
  • "If you forgive men their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you." (Matthew 6:14, WEBBE)
  • Peter asks how often to forgive: "until seventy times seven" (Matthew 18:22, WEBBE): without limit.
  • Jesus on the cross: "Father, forgive them, for they don't know what they are doing." (Luke 23:34, WEBBE)
  • God has given Christians "the ministry of reconciliation" (2 Corinthians 5:18, WEBBE). Catholics receive forgiveness in the sacrament of Reconciliation.
  • Forgiveness does not cancel justice: most Christians say a forgiven offender may still rightly face punishment.

Justice and human dignity

  • "Let justice roll on like rivers, and righteousness like a mighty stream." (Amos 5:24, WEBBE)
  • Just before, God says "I hate, I despise your feasts" (Amos 5:21, WEBBE): worship without justice is empty.

Peace and justice in other traditions

  • Islam: "Be ye staunch in justice, witnesses for Allah, even though it be against yourselves" (Surah 4:135, Pickthall). Believers answer the foolish with "Peace" (Surah 25:63, Pickthall).
  • Judaism: justice must be proportionate: "eye for eye, tooth for tooth" (Deuteronomy 19:21, JPS 1917), applied by courts; the rabbis read it as fair compensation, not literal injury. "Though your sins be as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow" (Isaiah 1:18, JPS 1917).
  • At Moses' plea, God says: "I have pardoned according to thy word" (Numbers 14:20, JPS 1917).
  • Buddhism: "hatred does not cease by hatred at any time: hatred ceases by love" (Dhammapada 5, Müller).
  • Hinduism: ahimsa (non-harm) and shanti (peace); Gandhi's non-violent struggle for justice.
  • Sikhism: sarbat da bhala (the welfare of all); defend the oppressed; justice for all, regardless of caste or religion.

Mission 3

The Truth and Reconciliation Commission

  • South Africa: under apartheid (1948–1994) the white minority government denied rights to the Black majority; violence came from the state and from some opponents.
  • After the first free elections (1994), the TRC was set up (1995), chaired by Archbishop Desmond Tutu (1931–2021).
  • Victims told their stories in public hearings. Those who fully confessed politically motivated crimes could receive amnesty.
  • Aim: restorative justice: truth and healing, rather than revenge. Tutu, a Christian, argued there is no future without forgiveness.
  • Criticisms: some victims felt justice was denied; reparations were slow; some offenders never came forward.
Mission 3Evaluate

"Forgiveness should always be offered, whatever the wrong."

  • Reasons for: Jesus teaches forgiveness without limit (Matthew 18:22) and forgives from the cross; forgiveness frees the victim from bitterness; it can break cycles of revenge.
  • Also for: Buddhists teach that "hatred ceases by love" (Dhammapada 5).
  • Also for: the TRC shows it can help a nation.
  • Reasons against: some wrongs, such as genocide, seem unforgivable; many Jews teach that only the victim can forgive a wrong done to them, so no one can forgive on behalf of the murdered; forgiveness offered too easily can excuse wrong or deny victims justice.
  • Another view: forgiveness may be a goal, but it cannot be forced; it is different from excusing, forgetting or letting off punishment.
  • Humanists: forgiveness is often good for everyone, but it is a free choice, not a duty.
  • Judge it by: Is forgiveness possible for every wrong? Who has the right to forgive? Can forgiveness and justice go together?

Mission 4

Forgiveness, repentance, reconciliation

  • Forgiveness: choosing to let go of anger and the wish for revenge against someone who has wronged you.
  • It is not pretending the wrong did not happen, and not saying it was acceptable.
  • Repentance: being truly sorry for wrongdoing and turning away from it (metanoia in Christianity; teshuvah in Judaism; tawbah in Islam).
  • Reconciliation: restoring a broken relationship, so both sides can live in peace again.

Mission 4

Forgiveness and punishment

  • The tension: if Christians must forgive without limit, should offenders be punished at all?
  • Most Christians answer: forgiveness is personal; justice is the state's duty (Romans 13:4). A victim can forgive and still want a fair sentence.
  • Punishment can itself serve the offender's good: it helps them face the wrong and change.
  • Example: Pope John Paul II was shot in 1981 by Mehmet Ali Ağca. In 1983 he visited Ağca in prison and forgave him, yet Ağca stayed in prison.
  • Some argue the other way: punishing someone you have forgiven still looks like holding on to the wrong.

Mission 4Diversity

Islam and Judaism

  • Islam: Allah is al-Ghafur (the Forgiving) and ar-Rahim (the Merciful); forgiveness is offered to all who sincerely repent (tawbah).
  • "And if ye efface and overlook and forgive, then lo! Allah is Forgiving, Merciful." (Qur'an 64:14, Pickthall)
  • "The guerdon of an ill-deed is an ill the like thereof. But whosoever pardoneth and amendeth, his wage is the affair of Allah." (Qur'an 42:40, Pickthall) The Prophet forgave many who had wronged him.
  • In qisas, the victim's family may forgive the offender (Qur'an 2:178): forgiveness and justice together.
  • Judaism: God is merciful to those who return (teshuvah). "Who is a God like unto Thee, that pardoneth the iniquity … because He delighteth in mercy." (Micah 7:18, JPS 1917)
  • Numbers 14:19–20: Moses asks God to pardon the people, and God answers, "I have pardoned according to thy word" (Numbers 14:20, JPS 1917). "Though your sins be as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow" (Isaiah 1:18, JPS 1917).
  • Wrongs against people must be put right with them first: Yom Kippur does not atone for these until the person wronged is reconciled (Mishnah Yoma 8:9, paraphrase).
Mission 4Diversity

Buddhism, Hinduism, Sikhism

  • Buddhism: holding on to anger harms yourself; forgiveness grows from metta (loving-kindness) and karuna (compassion).
  • "For hatred does not cease by hatred at any time: hatred ceases by love, this is an old rule." (Dhammapada 5, Müller)
  • Dhammapada 3–4: in those who keep thinking "He abused me, he beat me, he defeated me, he robbed me", hatred never ceases; in those who let go, it ceases (Müller).
  • Hinduism: kshama (forgiveness, patience) is a virtue of dharma.
  • Mahabharata 3.29 (paraphrase): Yudhishthira tells Draupadi that forgiveness is virtue and sacrifice, and that the world is held together by forgiveness.
  • Sikhism: forgiveness (khima) is a mark of a God-centred (gurmukh) person; anger is one of the Five Thieves.

Mission 4

Examples of forgiveness

  • Gordon Wilson (Methodist): his daughter Marie was killed in the Enniskillen bombing in 1987. He said he bore the bombers no ill will and prayed for them; he later worked for peace.
  • Gee Walker (Christian): her son Anthony was murdered in a racist attack in Liverpool in 2005. She said she forgave his killers, because of her faith.
  • Azim Khamisa (Muslim): his son Tariq was shot by a 14-year-old in 1995. He forgave the boy and set up a foundation with the boy's grandfather to stop youth violence.
  • The Truth and Reconciliation Commission of South Africa (1996–1998), chaired by Archbishop Desmond Tutu: people who fully confessed politically motivated crimes could receive amnesty, so the nation could heal after apartheid.
  • Each acted from personal belief; none said the crime did not matter.
Mission 4Evaluate

"Some actions are unforgivable."

  • Reasons for: some crimes (murder, genocide) cause harm that can never be repaired. Forgiving may seem to disrespect victims. Some offenders show no repentance. Humanists: forgiveness is a choice, not a duty.
  • Reasons against: Jesus taught "seventy times seven" and forgave on the cross. Examples like Gordon Wilson and Gee Walker show it is possible.
  • Muslims believe Allah is al-Ghafur (the Forgiving); Buddhists teach that "hatred ceases by love" (Dhammapada 5), so forgiving frees the victim from hatred.
  • Another view: forgiving the person is possible, while the act stays wrong and justice is still done.
  • Judge it by: Who has the right to forgive? Must the offender repent? Does forgiveness cancel punishment?

Mission 5

Wealth: gift or danger?

  • Wealth: money, property and possessions.
  • Most religions see wealth as a gift that can do great good: "The rich and the poor have this in common: The LORD is the maker of them all." (Proverbs 22:2)
  • Many believers see themselves as stewards (trustees): wealth is lent by God, to be used well.
  • The danger is not money but the attitude to it:
  • Greed: wanting more than you need. Materialism: valuing possessions above people and God.
  • Valuing people by what they own goes against teaching that all have equal worth.
  • Key question: how is wealth gained, and how is it used?

Mission 5

Social justice

  • Social justice: a fair share of wealth, opportunities and rights for everyone in society.
  • "But let justice roll on like rivers, and righteousness like a mighty stream." (Amos 5:24).
  • Amos says God rejects worship from people who ignore injustice (Amos 5:21–24).
  • Catholic social teaching: the common good. Churches back the Living Wage, food banks and Church Action on Poverty.
Mission 5

Social justice across religions

  • Islam: zakah redistributes wealth from rich to poor every year.
  • Judaism: tikkun olam: repairing the world.
  • Hinduism: seva (service) and dana (giving) are part of dharma.
  • Sikhism: sewa and langar serve everyone.
  • Buddhism: compassion (karuna) for all who suffer.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Absolute pacifists reject all violence; conditional pacifists accept it as a last resort; passive resistance opposes injustice without violence.
  • Quakers, many Buddhists and Gandhi are pacifists; most Christians, Muslims, Jews, Sikhs and Hindus accept defensive war, while valuing peace.
  • The Catholic Church is not pacifist, but recent popes have moved strongly towards non-violence ("War never again!").

Mission 2

  • Peacemaking aims at positive peace: justice, reconciliation and trust, not only an end to fighting.
  • Christian peacemakers (Romero, Day, Tutu, King) and organisations (Pax Christi, Sant'Egidio, Corrymeela) act on Matthew 5:9; the UN keeps and builds peace internationally.
  • Other faiths have their own peacemakers and sources: Qur'an 41:34, Psalm 34:14, Dhammapada 5, Gandhi, Khalsa Aid.

Mission 3

  • Peace (harmony, shalom, salaam), justice (fairness), forgiveness (letting go of revenge) and reconciliation (restoring relationships) are linked but different.
  • Christians are taught to forgive without limit (Matthew 6:12–14, 18:21–35) and to act justly (Micah 6:8, Amos 5:24); human dignity rests on the image of God; forgiveness does not cancel justice.
  • South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission chose truth and amnesty over revenge, and was criticised for denying some victims justice.

Mission 4

  • Forgiveness: letting go of anger and revenge; repentance: turning from wrong; reconciliation: restoring the relationship.
  • Jesus: forgive "seventy times seven" (Matthew 18:22).
  • Muslims: Allah is al-Ghafur (64:14); Jews: teshuvah (Micah 7:18); Buddhists: "hatred ceases by love" (Dhammapada 5).
  • Forgiving the person can go with justice for the act.

Mission 5

  • Wealth is a gift and a trust; the danger is greed and "the love of money" (1 Timothy 6:10).
  • Social justice means a fair share of wealth, opportunity and rights.
  • Amos calls for justice to "roll on like rivers" (Amos 5:24).
