Mission 1

Mission 1: Hormones reach almost every cell in the body. So why does each one affect only a few?

Make a guess: Hormones reach almost every cell in the body. So why does each one affect only a few?

Mission 1

Two ways to send a message

  • Nervous system: electrical impulses along neurones to specific cells. Fast (milliseconds), short-lived, localised.
  • Endocrine system: hormones secreted into the blood. Slower to start, longer-lasting, often widespread.
  • The two systems work together, e.g. in the "fight or flight" response.
Mission 1

Glands, blood, target cells

  • Endocrine glands are ductless: they secrete hormones directly into the blood.
  • The blood carries each hormone to almost every cell.
  • Only target cells respond: they have receptors with a shape complementary to that hormone.
  • Cells without the receptor are unaffected, however much hormone reaches them.
  • Say: "the hormone binds to complementary receptors on target cells" — not: "the blood takes the hormone to its target".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly distinguishes the roles of the nervous system and the endocrine system in sending messages between organs?
Mission 1

The adrenal glands

  • Two adrenal glands, one on top of each kidney.
  • Two layers, two jobs.
  • Cortex (outer): steroid hormones made from cholesterol.
  • Cortisol (a glucocorticoid): raises blood glucose; helps the body respond to long-term stress.
  • Aldosterone (a mineralocorticoid): increases Na⁺ reabsorption in the kidney, so blood volume and pressure rise.
  • Medulla (inner): secretes adrenaline (and noradrenaline) when stimulated by sympathetic nerves.
Mission 1

Adrenaline: ready for action

  • Released in seconds when danger or effort is sensed: fight or flight.
  • Heart rate and stroke volume rise.
  • Bronchioles widen: easier ventilation.
  • Arterioles to skeletal muscle dilate; arterioles to gut and skin constrict.
  • Liver: glycogen → glucose, so blood glucose rises.
  • Net effect: more oxygen and glucose delivered to muscles for intense contraction.
  • Say: "adrenaline prepares the muscles" — not: "adrenaline gives muscles energy".
Quick check

Mission check

Every hormone is broadcast to the whole body; the receptor decides who listens.

Mission 2

Mission 2: A few hormone molecules can make a cell release thousands of sugar molecules. How?

Make a guess: A few hormone molecules can make a cell release thousands of sugar molecules. How?

Mission 2

Where is the receptor?

  • A hormone acts only by binding a receptor with a complementary shape.
  • Polar hormones (proteins, peptides, adrenaline) cannot cross the phospholipid bilayer.
  • So their receptors are on the cell-surface membrane. The hormone stays outside.
Mission 2

The second messenger model

  • Adrenaline is the first messenger. It binds a receptor on the surface of a liver cell.
  • This activates the enzyme adenylyl cyclase.
  • The enzyme converts ATP → cyclic AMP (cAMP): the second messenger.
  • cAMP activates protein kinase, which phosphorylates enzymes, switching on glycogen → glucose.
  • Each step activates many molecules of the next: the signal is amplified.
Quick check
?Which sequence correctly describes the events after adrenaline binds to its receptor on a liver cell?
Mission check

?Inside liver cells, the enzyme phosphodiesterase breaks cAMP down to an inactive product. Caffeine inhibits phosphodiesterase. Liver cells are given caffeine and then a low dose of adrenaline. Compared with cells given adrenaline alone, what is predicted?

The second messenger is the amplifier: anything that keeps cAMP around turns the volume up.

Mission 3

Mission 3: You eat a sugary snack, yet your blood sugar barely changes for long. What pulls it back?

Make a guess: You eat a sugary snack, yet your blood sugar barely changes for long. What pulls it back?

Mission 3

Glucose in, glucose out

  • Cells need a steady supply of glucose as respiratory substrate. The brain depends on it most.
  • Raises blood glucose: digesting and absorbing carbohydrate; the liver releasing glucose from glycogen or making new glucose.
  • Lowers blood glucose: respiration (e.g. exercise); cells storing glucose as glycogen or fat.
  • Control: two pancreatic hormones, insulin and glucagon, by negative feedback.
Mission 3

The pancreas: two tissues

  • Most of the pancreas is exocrine: clusters of cells (acini) secrete digestive enzymes into a duct.
  • Scattered among them: the islets of Langerhans, the endocrine tissue.
  • β (beta) cells secrete insulin. α (alpha) cells secrete glucagon.
  • Islet cells secrete straight into capillaries: no duct.
  • The islet cells are both the receptors and the source of the hormones: no signal from the brain is needed.
Mission 3Practical

Drawing a pancreas section (PAG1)

  • View a stained section at low then high power. Find an islet: a rounded, paler-staining cluster of cells with capillaries.
  • Around it: darker-staining acini, often around small ducts.
  • Draw a low-power plan: sharp pencil, continuous lines, no shading, ruled label lines, title and magnification.
  • Show tissue boundaries, not every cell. α and β cells look alike in a standard stain.
  • Calculation: actual size = image size ÷ magnification.
  • Worked (illustrative data): islet 18 mm across in a ×100 drawing → 18 ÷ 100 = 0.18 mm = 180 µm.

Mission 3

Three liver words

  • Glycogenesis: glucose → glycogen. ("genesis" = making)
  • Glycogenolysis: glycogen → glucose. ("lysis" = splitting)
  • Gluconeogenesis: new glucose from non-carbohydrates: glycerol and amino acids.
  • All three happen in the liver. Muscle also stores glycogen, but for its own use.
  • Say: "glycogen is the store; glucagon is the hormone" — never mix them.
Mission 3

Glucose too high: insulin

  • β cells detect the rise and secrete insulin into the blood.
  • Insulin binds receptors on target cells: liver, muscle and adipose (fat) cells.
  • In muscle and adipose cells, more glucose transporter channel proteins are put into the cell-surface membrane, so they take up more glucose.
  • Insulin activates enzymes that convert glucose to glycogen (glycogenesis) and to fat.
  • Cells respire more glucose.
  • Blood glucose falls → β cells secrete less insulin: negative feedback.
  • Say: "insulin makes cells take up glucose" — not: "insulin breaks down glucose".
Mission 3

How a β cell senses glucose

  • At rest, K⁺ channels are open: K⁺ diffuses out; the inside is negative (about −70 mV).
  • Glucose enters the β cell and is respired: ATP rises.
  • ATP closes the K⁺ channels. K⁺ builds up inside: the membrane depolarises.
  • Voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open; Ca²⁺ diffuses in.
  • Ca²⁺ makes vesicles of insulin move to the membrane and release it by exocytosis.
Quick check
?Some drugs used to treat type 2 diabetes close the ATP-sensitive K⁺ channels in β cells. What is the effect on a β cell?
Mission 3

Glucose too low: glucagon

  • α cells detect the fall and secrete glucagon.
  • Glucagon binds receptors on liver cells.
  • It activates enzymes that convert glycogen → glucose.
  • It also activates gluconeogenesis: glucose from glycerol and amino acids.
  • Glucose leaves the liver cells; blood glucose rises → α cells secrete less glucagon.
Quick check
?Which option correctly pairs a hormone with its effect on target cells?
Mission check

?In a rare inherited condition, liver cells cannot break down glycogen. Their glucagon receptors work normally. Which prediction for a person with this condition after an overnight fast is best supported?

Feedback needs every link: sensor, hormone, receptor and effector. Break the effector and the hormone shouts into silence.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Two people both have high blood sugar, but only one lacks insulin. How can that be?

Make a guess: Two people both have high blood sugar, but only one lacks insulin. How can that be?

Mission 4

Two types of diabetes

  • Diabetes mellitus: blood glucose stays too high because the insulin loop fails.
  • Type 1: the immune system destroys the β cells (autoimmune). Little or no insulin. Often starts in childhood; onset is usually quick.
  • Type 2: insulin is made, but target cells respond less to it: insulin resistance. Later, β cells may secrete less. Usually develops slowly in adults.
  • Type 2 risk factors: obesity (especially around the abdomen), physical inactivity, a high-sugar, energy-dense diet, increasing age, family history.
  • Type 1 is not caused by diet or lifestyle.
  • Say: "in type 2, target cells respond less to insulin" — not: "type 2 is a milder type 1".
Quick check
?A lean 12-year-old develops very high blood glucose within a few weeks; antibodies against pancreatic islet cells are found in the blood and blood insulin is very low. A 55-year-old with obesity has had slowly rising fasting blood glucose for years, with a high blood insulin concentration. Which interpretation is best supported?
Mission 4

Treatment and prevention

  • Type 1: insulin by injection or pump, matched to carbohydrate eaten and exercise. Regular blood glucose monitoring.
  • Type 2: first diet (less sugar and refined carbohydrate), exercise and weight loss: these improve insulin sensitivity.
  • Then drugs, e.g. metformin (the liver releases less glucose; cells respond better). Insulin is added later if needed.
  • Prevention of type 2: healthy body mass, regular activity, a balanced diet. Type 1 cannot be prevented this way.
Mission 4

New insulin, new cells

  • Insulin was once extracted from pig and cow pancreases.
  • Since 1982: human insulin from genetically modified bacteria. The human insulin gene is inserted into a plasmid, then into E. coli, grown in fermenters.
  • Advantages: identical to human insulin, so fewer immune reactions; made in large amounts; no animal source needed.
  • Stem cells: could be grown into new β cells and transplanted. Potential: no daily injections.
  • Problems: rejection (and in type 1 the autoimmune attack continues); immunosuppressant drugs; risk of uncontrolled division. Still in clinical trials.
Quick check
?Why might β cells grown from stem cells and transplanted into a person with type 1 diabetes stop working, even if they are a perfect tissue match?
Mission check

?In a strain of mice, the liver, muscle and adipose cells have no working insulin receptors. Their β cells are normal. Which prediction after a meal is best supported?

High glucose with high insulin means the message is sent but not received: the signature of insulin resistance.

Mission 5

Mission 5: How can a bacterium be made to produce a human hormone it has never made before?

Make a guess: How can a bacterium be made to produce a human hormone it has never made before?

Mission 5

One gene, a new host

  • Recombinant DNA: DNA joined from two sources, e.g. a human gene inside a bacterial plasmid.
  • An organism carrying a gene from another species is transgenic: a genetically modified organism (GMO).
  • The genetic code is universal. Transcription and translation work the same way in all cells.
  • So the host reads the human gene and makes the same polypeptide.
  • Five stages: get the gene → put it in a vector → transform host cells → find the transformed cells → grow them.
Mission 5

Get the gene

  • Reverse transcriptase: make DNA from the gene's mRNA. This DNA is cDNA (complementary DNA).
    • Cells making the protein are full of its mRNA.
    • mRNA has had its introns removed, so cDNA has no introns. Bacteria cannot remove introns.
  • Restriction enzymes cut the gene out of a cell's DNA (next screen).
Mission 5

Cutting: restriction enzymes and sticky ends

  • Restriction enzymes (restriction endonucleases) cut DNA at one specific base sequence: the recognition site.
  • Each enzyme's active site is complementary to its own sequence. EcoRI cuts at GAATTC.
  • Many sites read the same on both strands, 5′ to 3′: they are palindromic.
  • A staggered cut leaves sticky ends: short single-stranded overhangs, here AATT.
  • Cut the gene and the plasmid with the same enzyme: their sticky ends are complementary.
  • Say: "complementary sticky ends" — not: "matching" or "identical sticky ends".
Quick check
?A human gene and a plasmid are both cut with EcoRI, then mixed. Which statement describes how the recombinant plasmid forms?
Mission 5

Into a vector, into a host

  • A vector carries the gene into the host: usually a plasmid.
  • DNA ligase joins the gene into the cut plasmid: phosphodiester bonds in the backbone. Result: a recombinant plasmid.
  • Transformation: host cells take up the plasmid.
    • Calcium ions plus a brief heat shock make bacterial membranes more permeable.
    • Electroporation: a short electric pulse opens pores in the membrane.
  • Only a small fraction of cells take up a plasmid.
Mission 5

Marker genes: which cells took it up?

  • The plasmid also carries a marker gene.
  • Antibiotic resistance marker: spread the cells on agar with the antibiotic. Only cells with the plasmid grow.
  • Fluorescent marker (e.g. the jellyfish GFP gene): transformed cells glow under UV or blue light.
  • If the gene is inserted inside a second marker gene, that marker stops working. Cells with the recombinant plasmid lose that marker: an easy test.

Mission 5

Grow them: one cell becomes a factory

  • Transformed cells divide. Every new cell copies the plasmid and the gene.
  • Human insulin: DNA coding for human insulin (no introns) put into E. coli; grown in large fermenters; insulin extracted and purified. Approved for use in 1982.
  • Compared with insulin from pigs or cattle: identical to human insulin; large, reliable supply; no dependence on animal sources.
Mission 5

Why the gene still works

  • The bacterium's RNA polymerase transcribes the human gene. Its ribosomes translate the mRNA.
  • Each codon codes for the same amino acid as in a human cell.
  • So the amino acid sequence, and the protein, are the same.
  • This is why cDNA is used: bacteria cannot remove introns, so a gene with introns would give the wrong polypeptide.
  • Say: "the genetic code is universal" — not: "all organisms have the same genes".
Mission check

?In 1986, researchers inserted the firefly luciferase gene, with a promoter that works in plant cells, into tobacco cells and grew whole plants from them. When supplied with luciferin, the plants glowed. Why could tobacco cells make active firefly luciferase?

Bacterium, plant or animal: any cell can read a gene from any species. That is what makes recombinant DNA possible.

Make a guess: If a goat can be made to produce a human medicine in its milk, what else could we change, and should we?

Mission 6

GM microorganisms

  • Bacteria and yeasts grow fast in fermenters. Many medicines are made this way:
    • insulin and human growth hormone from GM bacteria
    • the hepatitis B vaccine protein from GM yeast (since 1986)
  • GM pathogens for research: e.g. knock out a virulence gene to find its role, or add a marker gene to track infection.
Mission 6

GM plants

  • Insect resistance: a gene from the bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis makes a Bt toxin that kills certain caterpillars.
  • Example: insect-resistant GM soya.
  • Herbicide tolerance: e.g. GM soya survives a herbicide that kills weeds.
  • Nutrition: Golden Rice makes β-carotene (the body converts it to vitamin A) in its grain. Aimed at vitamin A deficiency.
Mission 6

Transgenic animals

  • Pharming: animals that make a human protein in their milk.
    • Example: human antithrombin (stops blood clotting) from GM goats' milk; approved 2006 (EU) and 2009 (US).
    • The gene is joined to the promoter of a milk-protein gene and put into an early embryo.
    • So every cell carries the gene, but only mammary gland cells express it.
  • Why not bacteria? Some human proteins need changes after translation, such as added sugar groups. Bacteria cannot make them.
Quick check
?Why are some human proteins for medicines made in transgenic animals or plants rather than in GM bacteria?
Mission 6

Risks, and how they are reduced

  • Documented risks:
    • Resistance: pests exposed to Bt toxin year after year can evolve resistance. Pink bollworm in India survives some Bt cotton.
    • Gene flow: a GM herbicide-tolerant grass escaped from field trials and spread in the wild (USA, 2000s).
    • Escape of GM microorganisms from a laboratory or factory.
  • Safety precautions:
    • Use host strains that survive poorly in the human body (e.g. lab strains of E. coli).
    • "Suicide genes": cells die outside the conditions they are given.
    • Containment: sealed fermenters; restricted labs; separation distances for field trials.
  • Products are tested before approval; rules differ between countries.
Quick check
?Bt cotton made a toxin that killed pink bollworm caterpillars. After several years of growing it in parts of India, many caterpillars survived it. Which explanation is correct?
Mission 6

Weighing the arguments

  • Arguments for:
    • Humanitarian: more food; fewer deficiency diseases; safer medicines in larger supply.
    • Less insecticide sprayed on insect-resistant crops.
  • Arguments against or concerns:
    • Environmental: resistance in pests and weeds; gene flow; effects on other species.
    • Ownership: GM seeds and genes are patented. Farmers may have to buy new seed each season. Large companies control supply.
    • Technology transfer: should GM seed be given cheaply to poor farmers? Patents fund research but raise prices.
    • GM pathogens: research aids vaccines, but a pathogen could escape or be misused.
    • Animal welfare: in pharming and in disease-model animals.
  • Exam answers: give both sides, then weigh them using the evidence in the question.
Mission check

?A company plans to make a human enzyme in the egg white of GM hens. The human gene is joined to the promoter of a gene for an egg-white protein and inserted into early embryos. The enzyme is found in egg white but not in the hens' blood or muscle. Why?

One inserted gene, one chosen tissue: that control is what makes pharming work. Whether to use it is a separate question, answered by weighing evidence and values.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Hormones are secreted into the blood and reach almost every cell; only target cells with complementary receptors respond.
  • The adrenal medulla's adrenaline prepares the body for vigorous activity.

Mission 2

  • Polar hormones bind cell-surface receptors and stay outside the cell.
  • Adrenaline's message is relayed and amplified inside by cAMP and protein kinase.

Mission 3

  • High glucose → β cells → insulin → cells take up glucose and make glycogen; low glucose → α cells → glucagon → liver breaks down glycogen. Both are negative feedback.
  • Liver: glycogenesis, glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis.

Mission 4

  • Type 1: autoimmune loss of β cells, no insulin, treated with insulin. Type 2: target cells respond less to insulin, treated first by diet, exercise and weight loss.

Mission 5

  • A gene from one species is expressed in another because the genetic code, transcription and translation are universal.
  • Get the gene, insert it into a vector, transform host cells, select them with marker genes, then grow them.

Mission 6

  • GM microorganisms, plants and animals make medicines, food traits and research models; tissue-specific promoters target where a gene is expressed.
  • Risks (resistance, gene flow, escape) are documented and reduced by safety precautions; decisions weigh benefits against environmental, ownership and welfare concerns.
