Mission 1

Mission 1: A wire carrying a current lies between the poles of a magnet. Which way is it pushed?

Make a guess: A wire carrying a current lies between the poles of a magnet. Which way is it pushed?

Mission 1

The motor effect

  • A current-carrying wire in a magnetic field feels a force.
  • The force is at right angles to both the current and the field.
  • No force when the current is parallel to the field.
  • Reverse the current or the field: the force reverses.
Mission 1

Which way?

  • Fleming's left-hand rule: First finger = Field (N → S); seCond finger = Current (+ → −); thuMb = Motion (force).
Quick check
?A straight wire lies in the plane of the page and carries a current towards the right of the page. A uniform magnetic field in the plane of the page is directed towards the top of the page. What is the direction of the force on the wire?
Mission 1

How big?

  • F = BIl sin θ
  • θ = angle between the current and the field. Largest at 90°, zero at 0°.
  • The length is the length inside the field.
  • B is in tesla (T).
Mission 1

Magnetic flux density

  • B is the magnetic flux density.
  • Rearranged (current ⟂ field): B = F/(Il): force per unit current per unit length.
  • 1 tesla: the field in which a wire carrying 1 A at right angles to the field feels 1 N on each metre.
  • 1 T = 1 N A⁻¹ m⁻¹.
Mission 1

Worked example

  • A 4.0 cm length of wire carries 2.5 A at right angles to a 0.15 T field.
  • Force = 0.15 × 2.5 × 0.040 = 0.015 N.
  • Turn the wire to 30° to the field: F = 0.015 × sin 30° = 0.0075 N.
  • Backwards: 0.012 N on a 5.0 cm wire carrying 3.0 A at right angles. B = force ÷ (current × length) = 0.012 ÷ (3.0 × 0.050) = 0.080 T.
Quick check
?A straight wire 0.40 m long carries a current of 3.0 A. Only 0.25 m of the wire lies inside a uniform magnetic field of strength 0.080 T, and the wire makes an angle of 30° with the direction of the field. What is the magnitude of the force on the wire?
Mission check

?A long straight wire carries a current vertically upwards. It is in a uniform horizontal magnetic field that points due north. In which direction is the magnetic force on the wire?

The force is at right angles to both the current and the field, and its size is B × current × length when they are perpendicular: so a wire between the poles is pushed sideways, out of the gap or into it, never towards a pole.

Mission 2

Mission 2: A magnetic field can steer a beam of charged particles. Can it also make them go faster?

Make a guess: A magnetic field can steer a beam of charged particles. Can it also make them go faster?

Mission 2

Force on a moving charge

  • A current is moving charge, so a single moving charge in a field also feels a force.
  • F = Bqv sin θ
  • θ = angle between v and B.
  • Zero force on a charge at rest, or moving along the field.
  • Example: an electron at 3.0 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹ at right angles to a 0.20 T field: F = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 3.0 × 10⁶ × 0.20 = 9.6 × 10⁻¹⁴ N.
Mission 2

Which way?

  • Fleming's left-hand rule, with the second finger along the conventional current: the direction a positive charge moves.
  • For an electron, point the second finger opposite to its motion.
  • The force is always ⟂ to the velocity, so it does no work: the speed and kinetic energy never change.
Quick check
?An electron moves towards the right of the page, in the plane of the page, through a uniform magnetic field directed into the page. What is the direction of the magnetic force on the electron at that instant?
Mission 2

Circular paths

  • Only the direction changes. Uniform field, v ⟂ B: a circle.
  • The magnetic force is the centripetal force: magnetic force = mv²/r.
  • r = p/(BQ), with p = mv.
  • Faster or heavier → bigger circle. Stronger field or more charge → tighter circle.
  • Time for one orbit: T = 2πr/v = 2πm/(Bq).
Mission 2

Worked example

  • A proton (m = 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, q = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C) at 2.0 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹, at right angles to a 0.50 T field.
  • Radius: r = mv/(Bq) = (1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ × 2.0 × 10⁶) ÷ (0.50 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹) = 0.042 m.
  • Period: T = 2πm/(Bq) = 1.3 × 10⁻⁷ s.
  • Double the field: r halves to 0.021 m, and T halves too, to 6.6 × 10⁻⁸ s.
Quick check
?A proton moves in a circle in a uniform magnetic field that is perpendicular to its velocity. A second proton moves at twice the speed of the first, perpendicular to the same field. Compared with the first proton, what are the radius of the second proton's circle and the time it takes to complete one revolution?
Mission check

?Two protons enter two separate regions at the same speed. Region 1 contains only a uniform electric field; region 2 contains only a uniform magnetic field. Each proton enters at right angles to the field lines. Gravity is negligible. A short time later, which statement is correct?

The magnetic force is always at right angles to the velocity. It changes direction, never speed, so a magnetic field alone can bend a beam but never accelerate it to a higher energy. That is why particle accelerators use electric fields to speed particles up; magnetic fields only steer them.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Drop a strong magnet down a copper pipe and it falls in slow motion. Why?

Make a guess: Drop a strong magnet down a copper pipe and it falls in slow motion. Why?

Mission 3

Magnetic flux

  • Magnetic flux: how much field passes through an area. Think: number of field lines through it.
  • φ = BA, with B normal (⟂) to the area A.
  • Unit: weber. 1 Wb = 1 T m².
  • Plane of the loop parallel to the field: zero flux.
Mission 3

Flux linkage

  • A coil of N turns: the same flux passes through every turn.
  • Flux linkage = Nφ.
Quick check
?A flat coil of 150 turns, each of area 20 cm², is placed with its plane at right angles to a uniform magnetic field of 0.40 T. What is the flux linkage of the coil?
Mission 3

Faraday's law

  • An emf is induced whenever the flux linkage changes: move a magnet, move the coil, change B, change the area, or turn the coil.
  • Steady flux, however large: no emf.
  • Size: emf = rate of change of flux linkage.
  • ℰ = −d(Nφ)/dt
  • On a flux linkage–time graph, size of the emf = gradient. On a graph of the flux through one turn, size of the emf = N × gradient.
Mission 3

Worked example

  • A coil of 200 turns, area 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ m², is ⟂ to a 0.50 T field. The field falls steadily to zero in 0.10 s.
  • Change in flux per turn = 0.50 × 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ Wb. Change in flux linkage = 200 × 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ = 0.040 Wb.
  • emf = 0.040 ÷ 0.10 = 0.40 V (while the field is falling).
  • Same change in 0.050 s: twice the emf. Once the field stops changing: zero.
Quick check
?The graph shows how the magnetic flux through each turn of a coil of 200 turns varies with time t. The coil is held at rest. What is the magnitude of the emf induced in the coil between t = 0.50 s and t = 0.70 s?
  • (The graph labels the flux Φ; your data sheet writes it φ.)
Mission 3

Lenz's law

  • The induced current flows in the direction that opposes the change that produces it.
  • N pole pushed in: the near end of the coil becomes an N pole, repelling it.
  • N pole pulled out: the near end becomes an S pole, attracting it back.
  • The minus sign in Faraday's law expresses this.
Mission 3

Lenz's law is energy conservation

  • Suppose the induced current helped the change: the magnet would be pulled in faster, the current would grow, and so on. Energy from nothing.
  • So the induced current must oppose the motion. You must do work against that force.
  • That work becomes electrical energy, then thermal energy in the circuit.
  • Open circuit: an emf, but no current, so no opposing force.
  • A solid metal sheet or pipe acts like many loops: changing flux drives eddy currents around inside it, and they oppose the motion too.
Quick check
?The south pole of a bar magnet is pulled away from one end of a coil whose ends are joined. Which statement is correct?
Mission check

?A thick aluminium plate swings as a pendulum through the gap between the poles of a strong magnet, and stops after a few swings. An identical plate with many deep slots cut into it swings for much longer. Which explains the difference?

The falling magnet changes the flux through each ring of the pipe, so currents flow around the pipe. By Lenz's law their field opposes the magnet's motion, slowing it; its lost energy heats the copper.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Switch on a current in one coil. Why does a meter on a separate coil flick, then fall back to zero?

Make a guess: Switch on a current in one coil. Why does a meter on a separate coil flick, then fall back to zero?

Mission 4

Seeing induction: magnet and coil

  • Coil joined to a centre-zero galvanometer (or data logger). Push a magnet in: the needle flicks.
  • Magnet at rest, even deep inside: no reading.
  • Pull it out, or use the other pole: the needle flicks the other way.
  • Bigger emf with: faster motion, stronger magnet, more turns.
Mission 4

Linked coils

  • Two coils on one iron core. Coil 1 has a switch and a cell; coil 2 has a meter.
  • Switch on: the current in coil 1 grows, its flux grows, and coil 2's flux linkage changes: a flick.
  • Steady current: steady flux, zero emf. Switch off: a flick the other way.
  • Bigger emf with: faster change of current, more turns on coil 2, an iron core (more of coil 1's flux links coil 2).
  • An alternating current in coil 1 gives a continuous alternating emf in coil 2: the principle of the transformer.
Quick check
?According to Faraday's law of induction, the magnitude of the emf induced in a coil with a fixed number of turns is proportional to which quantity?
Mission 4

Demonstrating Lenz's law

  • Hanging aluminium ring. Push a magnet towards it: the ring moves away. Pull the magnet back: the ring follows.
  • Ring with a slit: no complete circuit, no current, no movement. The emf alone exerts no force.
  • Galvanometer and coil: find which way current makes an N pole at the coil's end (test with a cell). Push an N pole in: the coil's near end becomes N.
  • Each time, the induced current opposes the change. To keep the magnet moving you must do work: that is where the electrical energy comes from.
Quick check
?Some trains brake with strong magnets held just above the steel rail. The magnets move along the rail with the train and never touch it. Where does the train's kinetic energy go, and how does the braking force change as the train slows down?
Mission 4

Reading flux-linkage graphs

  • Size of emf = gradient of the flux linkage–time graph. The induced emf opposes the change, so the graph draws it as minus the gradient.
  • Example: flux linkage rises steadily from 0 to 0.060 Wb in 0.20 s: emf = 0.060 ÷ 0.20 = 0.30 V.
  • Flux linkage then stays at 0.060 Wb: emf = 0.
  • Flux linkage falls to 0 in 0.10 s: emf = 0.060 ÷ 0.10 = 0.60 V, in the opposite direction.
  • Steeper gradient → bigger emf. Flat → none, whatever the value.
Mission check

?Two coils share an iron core. The current in coil 1 rises steadily from 0 to 2.0 A in 0.40 s, stays at 2.0 A for a while, then falls steadily to zero in 0.10 s. How does the emf induced in coil 2 compare in the three intervals?

An emf appears only while flux linkage is changing. Switching on coil 1 changes coil 2's flux linkage for an instant: a flick. Once the current is steady, nothing changes, and the reading falls back to zero.

Mission 5

Mission 5: An oscilloscope draws a voltage wave. How do you read its size and its frequency off the screen?

Make a guess: An oscilloscope draws a voltage wave. How do you read its size and its frequency off the screen?

Mission 5

Alternating voltage

  • a.c.: the current reverses direction again and again. Here (mains, generators) the pd follows a sine curve.
  • Peak value V0: from the centre line to a crest.
  • Peak-to-peak: crest to trough = 2V0.
  • Period T: time for one cycle. Frequency f = 1/T, in Hz.
  • UK mains: f = 50 Hz, so T = 20 ms.
Mission 5

Reading an oscilloscope

  • The oscilloscope draws pd (up) against time (across) on a grid of divisions.
  • Y-gain (volts per division): pd = number of divisions × V/div.
  • Time-base (time per division): time = number of divisions × time/div.
  • Measure peak-to-peak and halve it: the centre line is hard to judge.
  • Measure several cycles and divide: smaller percentage uncertainty.
  • dc input: a flat line, shifted up or down by the pd.
Mission 5

Worked example

  • Y-gain 2.0 V/div. Time-base 5.0 ms/div.
  • Trace: 6.0 divisions peak-to-peak; one cycle is 4.0 divisions wide.
  • Peak-to-peak = 6.0 × 2.0 = 12 V, so V0 = 6.0 V.
  • T = 4.0 × 5.0 = 20 ms, so f = 1/0.020 = 50 Hz.
Quick check
?An alternating pd is displayed with the time-base set to 2.0 ms per division. Exactly three complete cycles span 6.0 divisions. What is the frequency of the pd?
Mission 5

Root mean square

  • Power in a resistor depends on V², which keeps changing.
  • rms value: the steady (dc) value giving the same mean power in a resistor.
  • Square → take the mean → take the square root. For a sine wave the mean of V² is V0²/2.
  • Vrms = V0/√2 and Irms = I0/√2 (sine waves only).
  • Mean power = Irms × Vrms = ½I0V0.
  • a.c. meters and supply labels give rms values.
  • Example: the worked example's 6.0 V peak gives Vrms = 6.0/√2 = 4.2 V.
Mission 5

Mains values

  • UK mains is labelled 230 V: that is the rms value.
  • Peak: V0 = 230 × √2 = 325 V.
  • Peak-to-peak: 2 × 325 = 651 V (about 650 V).
  • So the insulation must withstand 325 V, not just 230 V.
Quick check
?An alternating supply with a peak pd of 10 V is connected across a 20 Ω resistor. What is the mean power dissipated in the resistor?
Quick check
?An alternating pd is displayed with the Y-gain set to 0.50 V per division. The trace measures 6.4 divisions from crest to trough. What is the peak pd?
Mission check

?A signal generator gives a sine wave of peak pd 1.5 V and frequency 500 Hz. It is displayed with the Y-gain at 0.50 V per division and the time-base at 0.50 ms per division. How tall is the trace from crest to trough, and how wide is one cycle?

Divisions × Y-gain gives pd. Divisions for one cycle × time-base gives T, and f = 1/T.

Labels and meters give rms values: Vrms = V0/√2.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Force on a current in a field: at right angles to both; zero when parallel.
  • Direction: Fleming's left-hand rule.
  • F = BIl sin θ.
  • 1 T: 1 N on 1 m of wire carrying 1 A at right angles to the field.

Mission 2

  • F = Bqv sin θ.
  • The force is ⟂ to v, so speed and kinetic energy never change.
  • v ⟂ B gives a circle: r = mv/(Bq) = p/(Bq); the period does not depend on speed.

Mission 3

  • Flux φ = BA with B ⟂ A; flux linkage Nφ. Unit: Wb = T m².
  • Faraday: emf = rate of change of flux linkage; no change, no emf.
  • Lenz: the induced current opposes the change; otherwise energy would be created.

Mission 4

  • Induced emf only while flux linkage changes: faster change, stronger field or more turns give more emf.
  • Lenz's law in practice: the induced current opposes the change, so work must be done; a cut ring has emf but no current and no force.

Mission 5

  • Read a trace: pd = divisions × V/div; T = divisions × time/div; f = 1/T. Measure peak-to-peak and several cycles.
  • Vrms = V0/√2: the dc value giving the same mean power. Mains: 230 V rms, 325 V peak, 651 V peak-to-peak.
