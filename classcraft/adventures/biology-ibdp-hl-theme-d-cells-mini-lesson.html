Mission 1

Mission 1: Each parent passes on chromosomes, yet the number never doubles. How is it halved so exactly?

Make a guess: Each parent passes on chromosomes, yet the number never doubles. How is it halved so exactly?

Mission 1

Why halve?

  • Body cells are diploid (2n): two sets of chromosomes, one set from each parent. Human 2n = 46.
  • Gametes are haploid (n): one set. Human n = 23.
  • Fertilisation fuses two gametes: n + n = 2n.
  • Without halving, the number would double every generation.
  • Meiosis is the reduction division: one diploid cell → four haploid cells.
  • Say: "a haploid cell has one of every chromosome" — not: "half of every chromosome".
Mission 1

Homologous pairs

  • Homologous chromosomes: a matching pair, one maternal, one paternal.
  • Same size and shape; the same genes at the same loci.
  • They may carry different alleles of those genes.
  • After replication, each homologue has two identical sister chromatids.
  • Sister chromatids are copies. Homologues are not.
  • Say: "the two chromatids of one chromosome are identical copies" — not: "one chromatid came from each parent".

Mission 1

Meiosis I: the pairs separate

  • DNA is replicated once, before meiosis I. Then come two divisions.
  • Prophase I: chromosomes condense. Homologues pair up, forming a bivalent.
  • Non-sister chromatids cross over at points called chiasmata. The nuclear envelope breaks down; the spindle forms.
  • Metaphase I: pairs line up on the equator. Each pair faces the poles independently of the others.
  • Anaphase I: homologues are pulled to opposite poles. Sister chromatids stay joined.
  • Telophase I: two cells, each haploid. Each chromosome still has two chromatids.
Mission 1

Meiosis II: the chromatids separate

  • No DNA replication between the two divisions.
  • Prophase II: a new spindle forms in each cell.
  • Metaphase II: chromosomes line up singly on the equator.
  • Anaphase II: centromeres divide; sister chromatids move to opposite poles.
  • Telophase II: nuclear envelopes form. Four haploid cells, each chromosome a single chromatid.
  • Two rounds of segregation: homologues in meiosis I, sister chromatids in meiosis II.

Mission 1

Find meiosis in any life cycle

  • Find where the chromosome number goes 2n → n. That is meiosis.
  • Where n + n → 2n, that is fertilisation.
  • Mitosis can happen in haploid or diploid cells. It keeps the number.
  • In animals, meiosis makes gametes.
  • In many plants and fungi, meiosis makes spores. Haploid organisms then make gametes by mitosis.
Mission check

Find where 2n becomes n, and you have found meiosis, in a moss, a fern or a human.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Brothers and sisters share both parents, yet each gets a different genetic mix. Where does it come from?

Make a guess: Brothers and sisters share both parents, yet each gets a different genetic mix. Where does it come from?

Mission 2

Three shuffles

  • Every gamete a parent makes carries a new combination of alleles.
  • Shuffle 1: how each homologous pair lines up at metaphase I.
  • Shuffle 2: crossing over between homologues in prophase I.
  • Shuffle 3: random fertilisation: which gamete meets which.
  • Say: "meiosis makes new combinations of alleles" — not: "meiosis makes new alleles".
Mission 2

Shuffle 1: independent assortment

  • At metaphase I, each homologous pair faces the poles at random.
  • Each pair's orientation is independent of every other pair's.
  • So each gamete gets a random mix of maternal and paternal chromosomes.
  • The term is random orientation of bivalents.
  • Say: "each pair lines up independently" — not: "all the maternal chromosomes go to one pole".
Mission 2

Worked example: how many combinations?

  • Let n = number of homologous pairs.
  • 1 pair: 2 orientations → 2 kinds of gamete.
  • 2 pairs: 2 × 2 = 4 kinds. 3 pairs: 2 × 2 × 2 = 8.
  • Rule: combinations = 2ⁿ.
  • Human, n = 23: 2²³ = 8 388 608 kinds of gamete from each parent.
  • Random fertilisation: 2²³ × 2²³ = 2⁴⁶ ≈ 7.0 × 10¹³ possible combinations in a zygote. Before any crossing over.
  • Say: "multiply the orientations: 2ⁿ" — not: "add them: 2 × n".
Quick check
?Garden peas have a diploid number of 14. Ignoring crossing over, how many different combinations of maternal and paternal chromosomes can random orientation of bivalents produce in the gametes?
Mission 2

Shuffle 2: crossing over

  • In prophase I, homologues pair up (a bivalent).
  • Non-sister chromatids (one from each homologue) break and rejoin at chiasmata.
  • They exchange equivalent sections, so alleles swap between homologues.
  • The result: recombinant chromatids, carrying new combinations of alleles.
  • So even the four cells from one meiosis can all differ.
  • Say: "non-sister chromatids exchange sections" — not: "sister chromatids swap sections".
Quick check
?Which statement describes crossing over during meiosis?
Mission 2

Shuffle 3, and why sex pays

  • Any sperm can fertilise any egg: random fertilisation. Each zygote is a new combination from two parents.
  • Meiosis breaks up parental combinations of alleles; fertilisation makes new ones.
  • Only mutation makes new alleles. Sexual reproduction makes new combinations.
  • Asexual reproduction: identical offspring. Good when a parent is already adapted to an unchanging environment.
  • Sexual reproduction: varied offspring. Variation needed for adaptation when the environment changes.
  • Say: "variation arises at random; selection acts on it afterwards" — not: "offspring get the variation they need".
Quick check
?Aphids reproduce asexually through the summer, when conditions are stable and food is plentiful, and switch to sexual reproduction in autumn before conditions change. Which statement best explains this pattern?
Mission 2

Two genes, two pairs

  • Genes A and B on different chromosomes are unlinked.
  • A cell is AaBb. Pair 1 carries A and a; pair 2 carries B and b.
  • Segregation: A and a separate at anaphase I; so do B and b.
  • Orientation 1 at metaphase I → gametes AB and ab.
  • Orientation 2 → gametes Ab and aB.
  • Both orientations are equally likely, so AB : Ab : aB : ab = 1 : 1 : 1 : 1. This is independent assortment, the basis of dihybrid ratios.
Quick check
?A plant is heterozygous for two genes on different chromosomes (AaBb). Which event in meiosis determines which allele of the first gene ends up in a gamete together with which allele of the second gene?
Mission 2

When separation fails: non-disjunction

  • Non-disjunction: a homologous pair (anaphase I) or two sister chromatids (anaphase II) fail to separate.
  • One gamete gets an extra chromosome (n + 1); another lacks it (n − 1).
  • Fertilised by a normal gamete → a zygote with 2n + 1 (three copies: trisomy) or 2n − 1.
  • Mitosis copies this into every body cell.
  • Down syndrome: usually three copies of chromosome 21, so 47 chromosomes.
  • The chance of non-disjunction in eggs rises with the mother's age.
  • Say: "the extra chromosome comes from non-disjunction in meiosis, before fertilisation" — not: "it is made at fertilisation".
Quick check
?In one meiosis in a parent, the first division is normal. In the second division, the two chromatids of chromosome 21 fail to separate in one of the two cells; the other cell divides normally. How many chromosomes are in each of the four gametes from this meiosis?
Mission check

?A mutation in a hypothetical species of animal prevents crossing over during meiosis, but meiosis is otherwise normal. Compared with the gametes of a normal individual, which prediction about the gametes of an individual with this mutation is correct?

Take one shuffle away and the others still work. Siblings differ because meiosis shuffles twice and fertilisation deals once more.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Bacteria make the enzymes for milk sugar only when it is there. How do they know when to start?

Make a guess: Bacteria make the enzymes for milk sugar only when it is there. How do they know when to start?

Mission 3

Enzymes made only when needed

  • The bacterium Escherichia coli can feed on the sugar lactose.
  • β-galactosidase hydrolyses lactose → glucose + galactose.
  • No lactose: the cell makes almost none of these enzymes. Lactose added: they appear within minutes.
  • Making them only when lactose is present saves energy and amino acids.
  • An external chemical, lactose, changes the pattern of gene expression.
Mission 3

The lac operon

  • Operon: a group of genes transcribed together, under the control of one promoter and one operator.
  • Promoter: where RNA polymerase binds.
  • Operator: where a repressor protein binds.
Mission 3

Lactose lifts the block

  • (1) No lactose: the repressor binds the operator. RNA polymerase cannot transcribe the genes.
  • (2) Lactose present: lactose (as allolactose) binds the repressor. Its shape changes; it falls off the operator.
  • (3) RNA polymerase binds the promoter and transcribes the genes → enzymes made → lactose used.
  • Lactose used up → the repressor binds the operator again → genes off.
  • Say: "lactose binds the repressor" — not: "lactose binds the DNA".

Mission check

?A mutant strain of E. coli makes a lac repressor that still binds the operator normally, but can no longer bind lactose. What happens when these bacteria are given lactose?

The switch is the repressor's shape. A repressor that cannot bind lactose never lets go, so the genes stay locked off.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Meiosis is a reduction division: one DNA replication, two divisions, four haploid cells, so fertilisation restores the diploid number.
  • Meiosis I separates homologous pairs (the halving); meiosis II separates sister chromatids.

Mission 2

  • Independent assortment (orientation of pairs at metaphase I) and crossing over (prophase I) give every gamete a new combination of alleles.
  • Random fertilisation multiplies the combinations again.
  • Independent assortment alone gives 2ⁿ kinds of gamete per parent (n = number of homologous pairs).
  • Non-disjunction gives gametes n + 1 or n − 1; Down syndrome is trisomy 21.

Mission 3

  • Lac operon: with no lactose, the repressor sits on the operator and blocks transcription; lactose binds the repressor, it lets go, and the genes for lactose use are transcribed.
