Mission 1

Mission 1: Benzene is drawn with three double bonds. So why doesn't it react like an alkene?

Make a guess: Benzene is drawn with three double bonds. So why doesn't it react like an alkene?

Mission 1

Three ideas from the video

  • σ bond: head-on overlap of orbitals; density on the line between the nuclei.
  • π bond: sideways overlap of p orbitals; density above and below that line. C=C = σ + π.
  • Beyond your course, as background: a ring carbon with three σ bonds at 120° is called sp². It keeps one p orbital for π bonding. Each H bonds with its 1s orbital.
  • Background: resonance structures are alternative drawings (linked by ↔) of one real molecule. It never flips between them.
  • You won't be asked to use the last two terms. The delocalised model below says the same thing.
Mission 1

Kekulé's model

  • Kekulé (1865): a ring of six carbons, alternating C–C and C=C bonds.
  • It predicts two bond lengths: short C=C and long C–C.
  • It predicts alkene reactions: rapid addition of bromine.
  • It predicts 3 × the hydrogenation enthalpy of one C=C.
  • All three predictions fail.
Mission 1

The delocalised model

  • Each C forms σ bonds to two C and one H, all in one plane, at 120°.
  • Each C has one electron left in a p orbital, above and below the plane.
  • The six p orbitals overlap sideways all the way round the ring.
  • Result: six π electrons delocalised in rings of density above and below the plane.
  • Drawn as a hexagon with a circle inside.
  • You may draw benzene either way (circle or Kekulé) in equations and mechanisms.
Quick check
?Which statement describes the bonding in a benzene molecule?
Mission 1

Evidence 1: bond lengths

  • X-ray diffraction: benzene is planar, with all bond angles 120°.
  • All six C–C bonds are the same length: 0.139 nm.
  • Compare C–C 0.154 nm and C=C 0.134 nm.
  • Kekulé predicts two lengths. One length, in between, fits delocalisation.
Mission 1

Evidence 2: enthalpy of hydrogenation

  • Cyclohexene + H2 → cyclohexane: ΔH = −120 kJ mol⁻¹ (one C=C).
  • Kekulé triene, three C=C: expect 3 × −120 = −360 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Benzene + 3H2 → cyclohexane: measured −208 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • 152 kJ mol⁻¹ less released, to make the same product.
  • So benzene starts 152 kJ mol⁻¹ lower in energy: delocalisation makes it more stable.
Quick check
?The enthalpy change of hydrogenation of cyclohexene (one C=C bond) is about −120 kJ mol⁻¹. For benzene, forming cyclohexane, it is about −208 kJ mol⁻¹, much less exothermic than the −360 kJ mol⁻¹ predicted for a structure with three localised C=C bonds. Which statement explains the difference?
Mission 1

Evidence 3: resistance to reaction

  • Cyclohexene decolourises bromine at room temperature, in the dark: addition.
  • Benzene does not. It reacts only with a catalyst (halogen carrier), and then by substitution.
  • Alkene: π electrons localised between two carbons. High electron density polarises Br2 and attracts it.
  • Benzene: π electrons spread over six carbons. Electron density between any two is lower.
  • Too low to polarise Br2 enough: no reaction without a catalyst.
Mission 1

Why substitution, not addition

  • Adding across the ring would destroy the delocalised π system.
  • That would throw away about 150 kJ mol⁻¹ of extra stability.
  • Substitution swaps an H atom for another group. The delocalised ring survives.
  • So benzene reacts by electrophilic substitution, with strong electrophiles (see Mission 2).
  • Answer to the opening question: benzene has no localised C=C bonds, so it doesn't behave like an alkene.
Quick check
?Cyclohexene decolourises bromine at room temperature, but benzene does not react with bromine unless a catalyst is added. Which statement explains this?
Mission 1

Naming aromatic compounds

  • Benzene ring as the parent: chlorobenzene, nitrobenzene, methylbenzene.
  • Number the ring to give the lowest locants: 1,3-dinitrobenzene.
  • With a named parent, its group is on C1: 2,4-dinitromethylbenzene.
  • Some parents have their own names: phenol C6H5OH, benzoic acid C6H5COOH, phenylamine C6H5NH2.
  • C6H5– as a substituent is phenyl: phenylethanone C6H5COCH3, phenylethene C6H5CH=CH2.
  • Different substituents: alphabetical order, e.g. 1-bromo-3-nitrobenzene.
Quick check
?A phenol molecule has two bromine atoms on the ring: one on a carbon next to the C–OH carbon, and one on the carbon directly opposite the C–OH carbon. What is its name?
Mission check

?Phenylethene, C6H5CH=CH2, has a benzene ring and a C=C in its side chain. It is shaken with a solution of bromine in hexane, in the dark, at room temperature. What happens?

One molecule, two kinds of π electrons. Localised ones add bromine like any alkene; delocalised ones in the ring hold on to their extra stability.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Benzene does react with bromine, given a catalyst. Why does a hydrogen get swapped rather than bromine adding?

Make a guess: Benzene does react with bromine, given a catalyst. Why does a hydrogen get swapped rather than bromine adding?

Mission 2

Why the ring attracts electrophiles

  • Benzene's six delocalised π electrons make the ring electron-rich, like an alkene's C=C.
  • So it attracts electrophiles: electron-pair acceptors, written E⁺.
  • But the density is spread over six carbons (see Mission 1). It can't polarise Br2 or attack weak electrophiles.
  • So benzene needs a strong, positive electrophile, made with a catalyst.
Mission 2

Substitution, not addition

  • Addition would destroy the delocalised ring and its extra stability.
  • Substitution swaps one H for E. The ring is restored.
  • Overall: C6H6 + E⁺ → C6H5E + H⁺.
Mission 2

Making the electrophile: the nitronium ion

  • Reagents: concentrated nitric acid + concentrated sulfuric acid (the nitrating mixture).
  • Keep at about 50 °C (below 55 °C). Hotter: more than one NO2 group goes on.
  • Electrophile: the nitronium ion, NO2⁺.
  • HNO3 + 2H2SO4 → NO2⁺ + H3O⁺ + 2HSO4⁻
  • Also written: HNO3 + H2SO4 → NO2⁺ + HSO4⁻ + H2O.
  • Sulfuric acid is the stronger acid: it protonates nitric acid, which then loses water.
Mission 2

The mechanism, arrow by arrow

  • Arrow 1: from the delocalised ring (inside the circle) to the N of NO2⁺. A C–N bond forms.
  • Intermediate: the attacked carbon now holds both H and NO2. Draw a horseshoe over the other five carbons, gap facing that carbon, + inside.
  • Arrow 2: from the C–H bond into the ring. The ring is restored; H⁺ leaves.
  • H⁺ + HSO4⁻ → H2SO4: the catalyst is regenerated.
  • Overall: C6H6 + NO2⁺ → C6H5NO2 + H⁺. Product: nitrobenzene.
  • Back to the opening question: bromine with a catalyst gives Br⁺, which reacts by these same two arrows. Losing H⁺ gets the stable ring back; adding Br⁻ would not.
Quick check
?Benzene reacts with a charged electrophile, E⁺. Which sequence describes the mechanism?
Quick check

Mission 2

Halogenation

  • Benzene + bromine + a halogen carrier, at room temperature, dry.
  • Halogen carriers: iron, FeBr3, or AlBr3. Iron filings make FeBr3 from the bromine.
  • Electrophile: Br⁺, made by the carrier: Br2 + FeBr3 → Br⁺ + FeBr4⁻.
  • In the mechanism you can take the electrophile simply as X⁺.
  • Same two arrows: ring → Br⁺; then C–H bond → ring, losing H⁺.
  • H⁺ + FeBr4⁻ → FeBr3 + HBr. Product: bromobenzene, C6H5Br.
  • Chlorination works the same way: Cl2 with AlCl3 or FeCl3 gives Cl⁺.
Mission 2

Friedel–Crafts alkylation

  • Benzene + haloalkane + AlCl3 catalyst, anhydrous (water destroys AlCl3).
  • CH3CH2Cl + AlCl3 → CH3CH2⁺ + AlCl4⁻
  • Ring → C⁺ of the carbocation; then C–H bond → ring, losing H⁺.
  • H⁺ + AlCl4⁻ → AlCl3 + HCl. Product: ethylbenzene, C6H5CH2CH3.
  • A new C–C bond to the ring: useful in synthesis.
Mission 2

Friedel–Crafts acylation

  • Benzene + acyl chloride + AlCl3, anhydrous, heated under reflux.
  • CH3COCl + AlCl3 → CH3CO⁺ + AlCl4⁻ (the acylium ion)
  • Arrow 1: from the ring to the C of CH3CO⁺. Intermediate: horseshoe, + inside.
  • Arrow 2: from the C–H bond into the ring; H⁺ is lost.
  • H⁺ + AlCl4⁻ → AlCl3 + HCl. Product: phenylethanone, C6H5COCH3, an aromatic ketone.
  • Important in synthesis: it joins a carbon chain to the ring (a new C–C bond).
Quick check
?Benzene is refluxed with propanoyl chloride, CH3CH2COCl, and anhydrous AlCl3. Which is the electrophile, and which is the organic product?
Mission 2

Where the second group goes

  • A group already on the ring decides where the next one goes.
  • –OH and –NH2 (electron-donating): 2,4-directing. Phenol + Br2(aq) → 2,4,6-tribromophenol (see Mission 3).
  • –NO2 (electron-withdrawing): 3-directing. Nitrobenzene + Br2/FeBr3 → 1-bromo-3-nitrobenzene.
  • Other groups: the exam will give you the data.
  • In synthesis, order matters: put on first the group that directs the second where you want it.
  • Unfamiliar reaction? Find E⁺, then use the same two arrows: ring → E⁺; C–H → ring.
Quick check
?You want to make 1-bromo-3-nitrobenzene from benzene. Data: –Br is 2,4-directing; –NO2 is 3-directing. Which route works?
Mission check

?Benzene reacts with chlorine when AlCl3 is present. After Cl⁺ bonds to the ring, the positive intermediate could, in principle, gain a Cl⁻ ion, as the carbocation from an alkene does. Instead it loses H⁺. Why?

Whatever the electrophile, the second arrow is the same. Losing H⁺ puts the delocalised ring back, which is why benzene substitutes.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Benzene needs a catalyst to react with bromine. Why does phenol react at once, with no catalyst at all?

Make a guess: Benzene needs a catalyst to react with bromine. Why does phenol react at once, with no catalyst at all?

Mission 3

Phenol: an OH on the ring

  • Phenol, C6H5OH: an –OH bonded directly to a benzene ring.
  • The O atom has lone pairs. One is in a p orbital, parallel to the ring's p orbitals.
  • It overlaps with the ring's π system: that lone pair is partly delocalised into the ring.
  • This changes the ring: it gains electron density.
  • It also changes the O–H: phenol is a weak acid.
Mission 3

A weak acid

  • C6H5OH ⇌ C6H5O⁻ + H⁺ (the phenoxide ion)
  • In phenoxide, the negative charge is spread into the ring: the ion is stabilised.
  • So phenol gives up H⁺ more readily than ethanol or water.
  • Still weak: Ka ≈ 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ mol dm⁻³, against 1.7 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ for ethanoic acid.
Mission 3

NaOH: yes. Carbonate: no.

  • With sodium hydroxide: C6H5OH + NaOH → C6H5O⁻Na⁺ + H2O. Phenol dissolves as sodium phenoxide.
  • With sodium carbonate: no fizzing. Phenol is too weak an acid to release CO2.
  • Carboxylic acids do fizz with carbonates.
  • Test: acidic with NaOH, but no CO2 with carbonate → a phenol.
Quick check
?Compound X reacts with sodium hydroxide solution to form a salt, but gives no gas with sodium carbonate solution. Which could X be?
Mission 3

Phenol and bromine water

  • Shake phenol solution with bromine water at room temperature. No catalyst.
  • The bromine is decolourised (orange → colourless). A white precipitate forms.
  • C6H5OH + 3Br2 → C6H2Br3OH + 3HBr
  • Product: 2,4,6-tribromophenol. Three H atoms are substituted at once.
  • Benzene, by contrast, needs pure bromine and a halogen carrier, and swaps just one H.
  • –OH is 2,4-directing: positions 2, 4 and 6 (6 is the second "2").
Mission 3

Why phenol reacts so readily

  • The O lone pair, from its p orbital, is donated into the π system.
  • The ring's π electron density increases, especially at C2, C4 and C6.
  • That denser ring polarises Br2 strongly enough on its own.
  • So the ring is far more open to attack by electrophiles than benzene's.
  • Explain it this way: the ring's electron density. Not the stability of the intermediate.
  • Back to the opening question: phenol's ring has extra electrons, supplied by the oxygen.
Quick check
?Phenol reacts with bromine water at room temperature, but benzene reacts with bromine only when a halogen carrier is added. Which explains the difference?
Mission 3

Nitration of phenol

  • Phenol reacts with dilute nitric acid at room temperature.
  • No concentrated HNO3 and no concentrated H2SO4: compare benzene (see Mission 2).
  • C6H5OH + HNO3 → C6H4(OH)NO2 + H2O
  • Product: a mixture of 2-nitrophenol and 4-nitrophenol: –OH is 2,4-directing.
  • Same reason as bromination: the ring is activated by the O lone pair.
Quick check
?0.470 g of phenol reacts completely with excess bromine water. What is the maximum mass of 2,4,6-tribromophenol, C6H2Br3OH, that can form? (Ar: H 1.0, C 12.0, O 16.0, Br 79.9)
Mission check

?Phenylamine, C6H5NH2, is shaken with bromine water at room temperature. The bromine is decolourised at once and a white precipitate forms. No catalyst is needed. Which explains this?

An O or N lone pair next to the ring feeds it electrons. A richer ring reacts with bromine with no catalyst.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why does cyanide add across a carbonyl double bond but leave an alkene double bond alone?

Make a guess: Why does cyanide add across a carbonyl double bond but leave an alkene double bond alone?

Mission 4

The carbonyl group

  • Carbonyl: C=O.
  • Aldehyde: C=O at the end of the chain, with an H on it. R–CHO. Name ends -al: ethanal, CH3CHO.
  • Ketone: C=O inside the chain. R–CO–R′. Name ends -one: propanone, CH3COCH3.
  • The C=O carbon is trigonal planar (bond angles about 120°).
Mission 4

Why nucleophiles choose C=O

  • O is more electronegative: Cδ+=Oδ−.
  • A nucleophile (lone-pair donor) is attracted to the δ+ carbon.
  • Alkene C=C: non-polar and electron-rich. It repels nucleophiles and attracts electrophiles.
  • So C=O undergoes nucleophilic addition: nucleophile to C, then H to O.
Mission 4

Reduction with NaBH4

  • NaBH4 in water (or aqueous methanol), warm.
  • Aldehyde → primary alcohol: CH3CHO + 2[H] → CH3CH2OH
  • Ketone → secondary alcohol: CH3COCH3 + 2[H] → CH3CH(OH)CH3
  • [H] stands for the reducing agent.
  • The nucleophile is the hydride ion, H⁻.
Mission 4

Mechanism: NaBH4

  • Arrow 1: from the lone pair of H⁻ to the carbonyl C.
  • Arrow 2: from the C=O π bond to the O. An alkoxide ion, C–O⁻, forms.
  • Arrow 3: from a lone pair on O⁻ to an H of H2O.
  • Arrow 4: from that O–H bond to its O. OH⁻ is released.
Quick check
?Butanone, CH3COCH2CH3, is reduced.
What is the organic product?
  • Reagent: NaBH4(aq).
Mission 4

Adding cyanide: hydroxynitriles

  • Ethanal + HCN → 2-hydroxypropanenitrile, CH3CH(OH)CN
  • Propanone + HCN → 2-hydroxy-2-methylpropanenitrile, (CH3)2C(OH)CN
  • The chain gains one carbon.
  • Reagent: NaCN(aq) / H⁺(aq).
  • HCN is a weak acid: little CN⁻ on its own. The cyanide salt supplies the CN⁻ nucleophile.
Mission 4

Mechanism: cyanide

  • Show Cδ+=Oδ− dipole.
  • Arrow 1: from the lone pair on the C of CN⁻ to the carbonyl C.
  • Arrow 2: from the C=O π bond to the O. An alkoxide intermediate, C–O⁻, forms.
  • Arrow 3: from a lone pair on O⁻ to H⁺, or to an H of H2O.
Quick check
?In the mechanism for the reaction of propanone with cyanide, where does the first curly arrow start and end?
Mission check

?Hex-5-en-2-one, CH2=CHCH2CH2COCH3, has a C=C bond and a C=O bond.
What is the organic product?
  • It is warmed with NaBH4(aq).

Nucleophiles (H⁻, CN⁻) are drawn to the δ+ carbon of C=O. The C=C bond has no δ+ end, so they pass it by.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Aldehydes and ketones look alike. Why does only one of them turn a test tube into a mirror?

Make a guess: Aldehydes and ketones look alike. Why does only one of them turn a test tube into a mirror?

Mission 5

Why aldehydes are easy to oxidise

  • Aldehyde: an H on the carbonyl carbon. Ketone: two carbons on it.
  • Mild oxidising agents turn that C–H into C–OH: aldehyde → carboxylic acid.
  • RCHO + [O] → RCOOH
  • Ketones would need a C–C bond broken: mild oxidising agents cannot do it.
  • The oxidising agent is reduced, and changes colour. That is the test.
Mission 5

Tollens' reagent: the silver mirror

  • Tollens' reagent: ammoniacal silver nitrate.
  • Make it fresh: AgNO3(aq) + a drop of NaOH(aq) → brown precipitate; add dilute NH3(aq) until it just dissolves. This gives [Ag(NH3)2]⁺.
  • Warm in a water bath, not over a flame.
  • Aldehyde: silver mirror (or grey-black precipitate). Ketone: no change.
  • Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag: silver ions are reduced; the aldehyde is oxidised.
  • Pour away straight after use: stored reagent can form explosive solids.
Mission 5

Acidified dichromate

  • K2Cr2O7 with dilute H2SO4, warm.
  • Aldehyde: orange → green (Cr2O7²⁻ reduced to Cr³⁺).
  • Ketone: stays orange.
  • Primary and secondary alcohols also turn it green, so it is not a test for aldehydes alone.
Quick check
?Propanal is warmed with Tollens' reagent and a silver mirror forms. Which statement is correct?
Mission 5

2,4-DNPH: is there a C=O?

  • 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (2,4-DNPH; OCR writes 2,4-DNP). Also called Brady's reagent.
  • Add a few drops of the compound: yellow / orange precipitate with aldehydes and ketones.
  • No precipitate with the C=O of carboxylic acids or esters.
  • It tells you C=O is present, not which kind.
Mission 5

Which carbonyl? Melting point of the derivative

  • 1. Filter off the precipitate.
  • 2. Recrystallise: dissolve in the minimum hot solvent (e.g. ethanol), cool, filter, wash with a little cold solvent, dry.
  • 3. Measure the melting point.
  • 4. Compare with a data table of derivative melting points.
  • Impurities lower the melting point and make it melt over a wider range.
Quick check
?A compound gives an orange precipitate with 2,4-DNPH. After recrystallising, the derivative melts at 154–156 °C. The compound gives no silver mirror with Tollens' reagent. Melting points of 2,4-DNPH derivatives: propanal 155 °C · propanone 126 °C · butanal 123 °C · pentan-3-one 156 °C. What is the compound?
Mission 5

Planning a test sequence

  • Step 1: 2,4-DNPH. Precipitate → aldehyde or ketone.
  • Step 2: Tollens'. Silver mirror → aldehyde; none → ketone.
  • Step 3: melting point of the 2,4-DNPH derivative → which compound.
  • One test rarely identifies a compound. Each test narrows the list.
Mission check

?4-Oxopentanal, CH3COCH2CH2CHO, has an aldehyde group and a ketone group. It is warmed with Tollens' reagent. What happens?

The H on an aldehyde's carbonyl carbon is what makes it easy to oxidise. That is why only aldehydes give a silver mirror.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Swap one hydrogen for a carbon chain: why does a sour acid become a sweet-smelling liquid?

Make a guess: Swap one hydrogen for a carbon chain: why does a sour acid become a sweet-smelling liquid?

Mission 6

Acids and esters: the structures

  • Carboxylic acid: –COOH. Ethanoic acid, CH3COOH.
  • Ester: –COO– with a carbon on both sides. RCOOR′.
  • An ester is an acid with the O–H hydrogen replaced by an alkyl group.
  • Name: alkyl (from the alcohol) + -oate (from the acid).
  • Methyl ethanoate, CH3COOCH3. Ethyl propanoate, CH3CH2COOCH2CH3.
Mission 6

Hydrogen bonding: boiling point and solubility

  • Acids have O–H and C=O: strong hydrogen bonds between molecules.
  • Boiling points (Mr 60): ethanoic acid 118 °C · propan-1-ol 97 °C · methyl methanoate 32 °C.
  • Small acids mix with water: they hydrogen bond to water molecules.
  • Solubility falls as the hydrocarbon chain grows.
Mission 6

Weak acids that still make salts

  • CH3COOH ⇌ CH3COO⁻ + H⁺: only partly dissociates. A weak acid.
  • Alkali: CH3COOH + NaOH → CH3COONa + H2O
  • Carbonate: 2CH3COOH + Na2CO3 → 2CH3COONa + CO2 + H2O
  • Metal: 2CH3COOH + Mg → (CH3COO)2Mg + H2
  • Metal oxide: 2CH3COOH + MgO → (CH3COO)2Mg + H2O
  • Salts are named -oate: sodium ethanoate.
Mission 6

The carbonate test

  • Add sodium carbonate: effervescence.
  • The gas turns limewater milky: CO2.
  • Alcohols, aldehydes, ketones and esters: no fizzing.
Quick check
?0.010 mol of ethanoic acid reacts with excess sodium carbonate. What amount of carbon dioxide forms?
Mission 6

Esterification

  • Carboxylic acid + alcohol ⇌ ester + water
  • CH3COOH + CH3CH2OH ⇌ CH3COOCH2CH3 + H2O
  • Catalyst: concentrated H2SO4. Warm or reflux.
  • Reversible: an excess of one reactant gives a higher yield.
  • The water forms from the acid's –OH and the alcohol's H.
Quick check
?Propanoic acid is warmed with methanol and a few drops of concentrated sulfuric acid. What ester forms?
Mission 6

Hydrolysis: splitting the ester

  • OCR: hot aqueous acid or hot aqueous alkali.
  • Acid: reflux with dilute H2SO4(aq). Reversible; does not go to completion.
  • CH3COOCH2CH3 + H2O ⇌ CH3COOH + CH3CH2OH
  • Alkali: reflux with NaOH(aq). Goes to completion.
  • CH3COOCH2CH3 + NaOH → CH3COONa + CH3CH2OH
  • The carboxylate ion cannot react with the alcohol, so it does not reverse.
  • Add strong acid afterwards to get the free carboxylic acid.
Quick check
?Methyl propanoate, CH3CH2COOCH3, is heated under reflux with sodium hydroxide solution. What are the products?
Mission check

?Two bottles, X and Y, each hold a liquid of formula C3H6O2. One is propanoic acid; the other is methyl ethanoate.
X: boils at 141 °C, fizzes with sodium carbonate.
Y: boils at 57 °C, smells fruity, no fizzing.
Which statement is correct?

Replacing the acid's O–H hydrogen with a carbon group removes both its acidity and its hydrogen bonding. What is left is a volatile, fruity ester.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Why does ethanoyl chloride fume in moist air, when ethanoic acid just sits in the bottle?

Make a guess: Why does ethanoyl chloride fume in moist air, when ethanoic acid just sits in the bottle?

Mission 7

Meet the acid derivatives

  • Swap the –OH of a carboxylic acid for another group: an acid derivative.
  • Acyl chloride: –COCl. Ethanoyl chloride, CH3COCl. Name ends -oyl chloride.
  • Acid anhydride: two acyl groups joined by O. Ethanoic anhydride, (CH3CO)2O.
  • Amide: –CONH2. Ethanamide, CH3CONH2 (a primary amide).
  • Secondary (N-substituted) amide: –CONHR, e.g. N-methylethanamide, CH3CONHCH3.
Mission 7

Making an acyl chloride

  • Start from the carboxylic acid. Dry conditions: water destroys the product.
  • Thionyl chloride: CH3COOH + SOCl2 → CH3COCl + SO2 + HCl
  • Both by-products are gases, so the acyl chloride is easy to separate.
Mission 7

Why so reactive?

  • The carbonyl carbon is bonded to two electronegative atoms: O and Cl.
  • So it is strongly δ+: a target for any nucleophile with a lone pair.
  • Chlorine then leaves easily as Cl⁻: a good leaving group.
  • So acyl chlorides react at room temperature, often violently. No catalyst.
  • In a carboxylic acid the C is less δ+, and –OH is a poor leaving group.
  • So, the opening question: water vapour in the air hydrolyses it. The "fumes" are HCl.
Quick check
?Ethanoyl chloride reacts with cold water within seconds. Ethanoic acid can stay in water for years without changing. Which explains the difference?
Mission 7

Four nucleophiles, one pattern

  • The nucleophile swaps in for Cl. HCl is the other product.
  • Water → carboxylic acid: CH3COCl + H2O → CH3COOH + HCl
  • Alcohol → ester: CH3COCl + CH3CH2OH → CH3COOCH2CH3 + HCl. Fast, and not reversible.
  • Ammonia → primary amide:
  • CH3COCl + 2NH3 → CH3CONH2 + NH4Cl. The second NH3 takes up the HCl.
  • Primary amine → N-substituted amide: CH3COCl + 2CH3NH2 → CH3CONHCH3 + CH3NH3⁺Cl⁻
  • Phenol → ester: CH3COCl + C6H5OH → CH3COOC6H5 + HCl (phenyl ethanoate). Carboxylic acids barely esterify phenol.
Quick check

Mission 7

Acid anhydrides: the gentler cousin

  • Same reactions, but the leaving group is ethanoate, not Cl⁻.
  • By-product: ethanoic acid, not HCl. Slower: warming is usually needed.
  • Alcohol → ester: (CH3CO)2O + CH3OH → CH3COOCH3 + CH3COOH
Mission 7

Making aspirin

  • Salicylic acid (2-hydroxybenzoic acid) has a phenolic –OH and a –COOH.
  • Ethanoic anhydride ethanoylates the –OH, making an ester: aspirin.
  • C7H6O3 + (CH3CO)2O → C9H8O4 + CH3COOH
  • The phenol –OH is esterified by the anhydride, which is far quicker than by a carboxylic acid.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Benzene: planar ring; six p electrons delocalised above and below; all C–C 0.139 nm.
  • Evidence against Kekulé: one bond length, 152 kJ mol⁻¹ extra stability, no ready addition of bromine.
  • Delocalised density is too low to polarise Br2, and addition would destroy the ring: so benzene substitutes.

Mission 2

  • Ring π electrons attack a strong electrophile; the positive intermediate loses H⁺ to restore the ring.
  • Nitration: conc. HNO3 + conc. H2SO4, about 50 °C, electrophile NO2⁺.
  • Halogenation: Br2 + halogen carrier (Br⁺). Alkylation: RCl + AlCl3 (R⁺).
  • Acylation: RCOCl + AlCl3 (RCO⁺) gives an aromatic ketone.

Mission 3

  • Phenol's O lone pair is partly delocalised into the ring: the ring's electron density rises.
  • Bromine water, no catalyst: 2,4,6-tribromophenol (white precipitate) + 3HBr.
  • Weak acid: reacts with NaOH, no CO2 with carbonates.
  • Dilute HNO3 gives 2- and 4-nitrophenol.

Mission 4

  • C=O is polar: nucleophiles add to the δ+ carbon, then the O gains H. C=C is not polar: no nucleophilic addition.

Mission 5

  • Aldehydes (H on the C=O carbon) are oxidised to carboxylic acids; ketones are not. The reagent is reduced and changes colour.
  • 2,4-DNPH shows a C=O is present; the derivative's melting point identifies the compound.

Mission 6

  • Acid → ester: the O–H hydrogen is replaced by an alkyl group. No acidity, no hydrogen bonding between molecules.
  • Esters form with an acid catalyst (reversible) and hydrolyse with acid (reversible) or alkali (complete, gives the salt).

Mission 7

  • Acyl chlorides: δ+ carbonyl C and Cl⁻ leaving group. Water → acid, alcohol → ester, NH3 → amide, amine → N-substituted amide; HCl formed.
  • Ethanoic anhydride reacts the same way, more gently, giving ethanoic acid: used to make aspirin.
