Mission 1

Mission 1: Why can't you open an account at a central bank, and what does it do instead?

Make a guess: Why can't you open an account at a central bank, and what does it do instead?

Mission 1

The bank behind the banks

  • A central bank is the bank at the centre of a country's money system. Examples:
  • Bank of England (founded 1694); US Federal Reserve (1913); European Central Bank (1998), for the countries that use the euro.
  • Its customers are the government and commercial banks, not households or firms.
  • Commercial banks hold accounts there called reserves.
  • When customers of one bank pay customers of another, the two banks settle by moving reserves between their accounts at the central bank.
  • It is usually not run for profit: its aim is a stable economy.
Mission 1

What a central bank does

  • Monetary policy: sets the policy interest rate to keep inflation low and stable.
  • Issues banknotes.
  • Banker to the government: holds its main account and helps manage its borrowing.
  • Banker to the banks: holds their reserves and runs the system they use to pay each other.
  • Lender of last resort: lends to banks that run short of cash.
  • Financial stability: watches for risks to the whole system and, in many countries, supervises banks.
Mission 1

The goal: stable prices

  • Most central banks aim for low, stable inflation, often a numerical target.
  • UK: the government sets an inflation target of 2% (CPI). The Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee (MPC), nine members, sets Bank Rate to hit it.
  • The UK target is symmetric: inflation below 2% is treated as seriously as inflation above it.
  • Stable prices help firms and households plan, borrow and invest.
Mission 1

The tools

  • Policy interest rate: the rate at which the central bank lends to, or pays on reserves of, commercial banks. Other rates tend to follow.
  • UK: Bank Rate (often called the base rate), set by the MPC; it feeds through to loan and savings rates (the transmission mechanism).
  • Open market operations: buying or selling government bonds to change bank reserves and the money supply.
  • Quantitative easing (QE): buying bonds on a large scale with newly created money, to push down long-term rates.
  • The Bank of England sets no minimum reserve ratio for monetary policy.

Mission 1

Lender of last resort

  • A bank can be solvent (assets worth more than it owes) but illiquid: it cannot turn its loans into cash fast enough.
  • If depositors panic and withdraw at once (a bank run), even a sound bank can fail.
  • The central bank can lend it cash against good collateral: it is the lender of last resort.
  • Classic rule (Walter Bagehot, 1873): lend freely, against good collateral, at a high rate.
  • Real-world: on 14 September 2007 the Bank of England gave emergency support to Northern Rock. Queues of savers still formed outside its branches.
  • Risk: moral hazard. If banks expect rescue, they may take bigger risks.
Quick check
?A bank's loans are worth more than it owes, but depositors suddenly withdraw more cash than it holds. What can the central bank do as lender of last resort?
Mission 1

Central banks, banks and the money supply

  • Most money is bank deposits, created when commercial banks lend.
  • The central bank shapes that lending:
  • Policy rate: a higher rate makes loans dearer, so banks lend less and deposits grow more slowly.
  • QE: buying bonds creates reserves for banks and deposits for the sellers, so broad money rises directly.
  • Regulation: rules on capital and liquidity limit how far banks can expand lending.
  • So the central bank influences the money supply and the banking sector, but does not fix the quantity of money directly.
Mission 1

Keeping the system safe

  • Banks fail in ways that can spread to the whole economy. Central banks often help regulate them.
  • UK (since 2013): the Bank of England's Financial Policy Committee (FPC) looks at risks to the whole system.
  • The Prudential Regulation Authority (PRA), part of the Bank of England, supervises individual banks and insurers.
  • The Financial Conduct Authority (FCA), separate from the Bank, regulates how firms treat customers.
  • More on regulation and crises in a later lesson.
Mission 1Evaluate

Should central banks be independent?

  • UK: the Bank of England has set interest rates independently since 1997. The ECB's independence is written into EU treaties.
  • For: rate-setters cannot be pushed to cut rates before an election, so promises of low inflation are more credible. Inflation expectations may stay anchored.
  • Against: unelected officials make decisions that affect jobs, house prices and savers; accountability is weaker.
  • Against: monetary and fiscal policy may pull in opposite directions without coordination.
  • It depends on: how the target is set and reviewed, how open the central bank is, and how far it strays into areas such as QE that change who gains and loses.

Make a guess: How can one interest rate, set by a handful of people, slow down inflation across a whole country?

Mission 2

What monetary policy is

  • Monetary policy: the central bank's use of interest rates and the money supply to influence demand and inflation.
  • UK: the Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee (MPC), 9 members, sets Bank Rate.
  • The Bank has had operational independence since 1997: the government sets the target; the MPC chooses the rate.
  • The central bank is also banker to the government and to the banks (see Mission 1).
  • Expansionary policy: lower rates, more money. Contractionary: higher rates, less money.
Mission 2

Goals: an inflation target

  • Main goal: price stability, which means low and stable inflation.
  • UK: 2% CPI inflation, set by the government. The target is symmetric: undershooting is treated as seriously as overshooting.
  • If inflation is more than 1 percentage point away from 2%, the Governor writes an open letter to the Chancellor explaining why.
  • Why a target: it anchors expectations. If people expect 2%, wages and prices are set to match.
Mission 2

The central bank's tools

  • Policy rate (base rate, Bank Rate, discount rate): the rate the central bank pays or charges banks.
  • Open market operations: buying or selling government bonds. Buying adds money to the banking system; selling drains it.
  • Minimum reserve requirements: the share of deposits banks must hold as reserves.
  • Quantitative easing (QE): large-scale bond buying when rates are near zero (see Mission 3).
Mission 2

The transmission mechanism

  • How a rise in the policy rate reaches inflation:
    • Market rates rise: mortgages, loans, savings. Borrowing costs more; saving pays more. Consumption (C) and investment (I) fall.
    • Asset prices fall (houses, shares, bonds). Households feel less wealthy and spend less.
    • Confidence and expectations: a signal that the central bank is serious about inflation.
    • Exchange rate rises: exports more expensive abroad, imports cheaper. Net exports fall; import prices fall.
  • Result: AD grows more slowly. Less pressure on firms' capacity, wages and prices. Inflation slows.
  • A cut works the other way.
  • Say: "Bank Rate" (UK) — not: "the interest rate", when you mean the Bank of England's policy rate.
Mission 2

Monetary policy on AD/AS

  • Contractionary (rate rise): AD shifts left, AD1 → AD2.
    • Real output falls. The price level is lower than it would have been.
    • Closes an inflationary (positive) gap.
  • Expansionary (rate cut): AD shifts right.
    • Real output and the price level rise. Unemployment falls.
    • Closes a deflationary or recessionary (negative) gap.
  • Say: "inflation slows" (disinflation) — not: "prices fall", unless the price level actually falls (deflation).

Mission 2

What the committee weighs

  • The MPC forecasts inflation about two years ahead, because rate changes take time to work.
  • It looks at: spare capacity and unemployment, wage growth, inflation expectations, the exchange rate, house prices and credit, and world energy and commodity prices.
Mission 2

Real case: the rate rises after the pandemic

  • UK: Bank Rate was 0.1% (March 2020). The MPC raised it from December 2021 to 5.25% (August 2023).
  • CPI inflation peaked at 11.1% (October 2022) and was back at 2.0% in May 2024 (ONS).
  • Inflation then fell, but economists debate how much was due to higher rates and how much to falling energy prices.
Mission 2

Strengths and limits

  • Strengths:
    • Flexible: rates can change in small steps, several times a year.
    • Reversible, and quick to decide: no new law needed.
    • An independent central bank is credible: a promise to hit the target shapes expectations.
  • Limits:
    • Time lags: up to about two years for the full effect on inflation (Bank of England).
    • Supply shocks: rate rises can lower inflation, but at the cost of lower output and jobs.
    • Low confidence: firms and households may not borrow, however cheap credit is.
    • One rate for everyone: regions, and borrowers and savers, are hit differently.
Mission 2

The zero lower bound

  • Rates cannot be cut far below zero: people could hold cash instead.
  • Zero lower bound: once rates are near zero, conventional policy has little room left.
  • Real cases: Bank Rate 0.1% (March 2020). The European Central Bank's deposit rate reached −0.5% (2019); the Bank of Japan's short-term rate was −0.1% from 2016 to March 2024.
  • Liquidity trap (Keynes): rates are so low that more money does not raise spending; people just hold it.
  • Central banks then turn to QE and forward guidance (see Mission 3).
Quick check
?A recession has cut confidence. The central bank's policy rate is already 0.25%, and banks are unwilling to lend. Why might a further rate cut do little to raise AD?
Mission 2Evaluate

How well can interest rates steer the economy?

It depends on:

  • The cause of inflation: demand-pull responds well; a supply shock forces a choice between inflation and output.
  • Time lags: a rate change made now acts on demand up to two years later, when conditions may have changed.
  • Confidence and debt: with many variable-rate borrowers, rates bite fast; when confidence is low, cuts may not work.
  • How low rates already are: near the zero lower bound, other tools are needed.
Mission check

?A sharp rise in world oil prices pushes a country's inflation well above target, while real output falls. The central bank raises its policy rate. What is the most likely result in the short run?

Interest rates work through demand. After a supply shock, slowing inflation comes at the cost of lower output and jobs in the short run.

Mission 3

Mission 3: When interest rates can go no lower, how can a central bank still get people spending?

Make a guess: When interest rates can go no lower, how can a central bank still get people spending?

Mission 3

When the usual tool runs out

  • The usual tool: cut the policy interest rate to make borrowing cheaper.
  • In the UK: the MPC cut Bank Rate from 5.75% (2007) to 0.5% (March 2009).
  • Rates cannot go far below zero: people could hold cash at 0% instead. This floor is the zero (or effective) lower bound.
  • With the economy still weak, central banks turned to unconventional tools.
  • Main one: quantitative easing (QE).
Mission 3

What QE is

  • QE: the central bank creates new central bank reserves (electronic money) and uses them to buy assets, mainly government bonds.
  • UK government bonds are called gilts.
  • It buys from investors such as pension funds and insurers, not straight from the government.
  • The seller's bank account is credited: broad money (bank deposits) rises; banks' reserves rise.
  • So QE increases the money supply while the policy rate is stuck near zero.
  • Say: "the central bank creates reserves to buy bonds" — not: "prints banknotes and gives them away".
Quick check
?Which statement best describes quantitative easing?
Mission 3

How QE reaches spending

  • 1. Bond prices up, yields down. Big purchases raise bond prices. A bond's yield (its return) moves the opposite way to its price.
  • 2. Lower long-term interest rates. Government bond yields are a benchmark, so mortgage and business loan rates tend to fall.
  • 3. Portfolio rebalancing. Sellers use their new money to buy other assets (shares, company bonds): their prices rise, firms can borrow more cheaply.
  • 4. Wealth effect. Higher asset prices make owners feel richer: more consumption.
  • 5. Exchange rate. Lower yields can make the currency less attractive: it tends to fall, exports become cheaper.
  • 6. Confidence. It signals the central bank will act.
  • Result: AD shifts right, raising real output and the price level towards the inflation target.
Mission 3

Worked example: price up, yield down

  • Illustrative data. A bond pays a fixed 3 a year, for ever.
  • Yield = annual payment ÷ price × 100.
  • Price 100: 3 ÷ 100 × 100 = 3%.
  • QE buying pushes the price to 120: 3 ÷ 120 × 100 = 2.5%.
  • Price 150: 3 ÷ 150 × 100 = 2%.
  • The payment is fixed, so paying more for it means a lower return.
  • Say: "higher bond prices mean lower yields" — not: "bond prices and interest rates rise together".

Mission 3

Reversing QE

  • Quantitative tightening (QT): the central bank shrinks its bond holdings.
  • Two ways: stop reinvesting as bonds mature, or sell bonds back to investors.
  • Reserves and deposits shrink: the money supply contracts.
  • The chain runs in reverse: bond prices fall, yields rise, borrowing costs rise: AD tends to shift left.
  • UK: the MPC decided to begin reducing its stock of bonds in February 2022.
  • Central banks try to reverse QE gradually and predictably, so markets are not shocked.
Mission 3

Real world: QE in practice

  • The Bank of England began QE in March 2009, with Bank Rate at 0.5%.
  • In total it bought £895 billion of bonds: £875 billion of gilts and £20 billion of company bonds.
  • The last increase was announced in November 2020, in the pandemic.
  • Question to weigh: why did central banks buy on this scale, and what happens when they stop?
Mission 3Evaluate

Does QE work?

It depends on:

  • Confidence. If households and firms want to save and banks will not lend, new reserves sit idle: "pushing on a string".
  • Who gains. Higher bond, share and house prices help people who own them, which may widen wealth inequality. Supporters reply that QE protected jobs and incomes.
  • Timing and exit. Too much for too long risks inflation; unwinding risks bond-market disruption and losses for the central bank.
  • In the UK, those losses are covered by HM Treasury, so they fall on taxpayers.
  • Evidence. Most studies find early QE lowered long-term yields. How much it raised output and inflation is still debated.
Mission check

?A central bank bought large amounts of government bonds during a slump. Years later, with inflation above target, it starts selling some of the bonds back to investors. Other things equal, what is the most likely effect?

Buying bonds pushes long-term rates down; selling them pushes rates up. The same chain works in both directions.

Mission 4

Direct and indirect taxes

  • Direct tax: a tax on income or wealth, paid straight to the government by the person or firm it is levied on.
    • Income tax, corporation tax (on company profits), taxes on wealth.
  • UK direct taxes also include National Insurance contributions, capital gains tax and inheritance tax.
  • Indirect tax: a tax on spending on goods and services. The seller pays it to the government, but can pass some or all of it on to buyers in higher prices.
    • VAT (a sales tax), excise duties on fuel, alcohol and tobacco, tariffs on imports.
  • How much of an indirect tax buyers bear depends on elasticity.
  • Say: "VAT is an indirect tax" — not: "VAT is a direct tax because shoppers pay it directly at the till".
Mission 4

Why tax, and what makes a good tax

  • Reasons to tax:
    • raise revenue for public spending
    • redistribute income and wealth
    • correct market failure (taxes on pollution, tobacco)
    • manage aggregate demand (fiscal policy)
  • Adam Smith's canons of a good tax (1776):
    • equity: in line with ability to pay
    • certainty: clear what, when and how much
    • convenience: easy to pay
    • economy: cheap to collect
  • Modern additions: efficiency (few distortions to choices) and flexibility (easy to change).
Quick check
?Which of these is an indirect tax?
Mission 4

Progressive, proportional, regressive

  • Judge a tax by the share of income it takes, not the amount.
  • Progressive: the share of income paid in tax rises as income rises.
  • Proportional: the share stays the same at every income.
  • Regressive: the share falls as income rises.
  • A regressive tax can still take more money from richer people.
  • Say: "a regressive tax takes a larger proportion of a low income" — not: "poor people pay more money".
Mission 4

Average and marginal tax rates

  • Average tax rate (ART) = total tax ÷ total income × 100.
  • Marginal tax rate (MRT) = change in tax ÷ change in income × 100: the tax on the next unit of income.
  • Progressive: MRT > ART, so ART rises with income.
  • Proportional: MRT = ART.
  • Regressive: ART falls as income rises.
  • Moving into a higher band raises the rate only on income inside that band.

  • With an allowance, a flat tax is mildly progressive. With no allowance, it is proportional.
  • Real case: Estonia introduced a flat income tax in 1994; several central and eastern European countries followed.
  • Supporters: simple, cheap to collect, keeps the reward for earning more. Critics: less redistribution than several rising bands.
Mission 4

The Laffer curve

  • Tax revenue = tax rate × tax base (the income or spending being taxed).
  • At a 0% rate, revenue is zero. At 100%, there is little point earning declared income, so revenue is close to zero.
  • In between, revenue reaches a maximum at some rate, t*.
  • Below t*: a higher rate raises revenue.
  • Above t*: the tax base shrinks so much that a higher rate lowers revenue. A cut could raise revenue.
  • The base shrinks through less work and investment, more avoidance and evasion, and people or firms moving abroad.
  • Nobody knows exactly where t* is. Estimates differ, and most economists think most tax rates are below it.
  • Real case: in 2012 HMRC estimated that cutting the UK top income tax rate from 50% to 45% would cost only about £100 million a year, because high earners respond strongly to the top rate. HMRC stressed the estimate was very uncertain.

Mission 4

How taxes change the economy

  • Incentives: higher income tax may reduce work effort (substitution effect), or make people work more to keep their income (income effect). Corporation tax affects the reward for investing.
  • Revenue: depends on the rate and on how the tax base responds (Laffer curve).
  • Distribution: progressive taxes narrow the gap between incomes after tax; regressive taxes widen it.
  • Output: higher taxes reduce AD; lower taxes on work and profit may raise LRAS over time.
  • Prices: a rise in an indirect tax raises the price level (a one-off rise in costs).
  • Trade: tariffs raise import prices; taxes on firms affect the cost of exports.
  • FDI: low corporation tax can attract foreign firms. Real case: Ireland's 12.5% rate on trading profits. Since 2024, large multinational groups face a 15% minimum (OECD agreement).
Mission 4Evaluate

Should a country make its taxes more progressive?

It depends on:

  • How people respond: if high earners cut work, move or avoid tax, revenue may rise less than planned.
  • Value judgements about equity: how much inequality is acceptable is a normative question.
  • Which taxes change: raising income tax rates at the top differs from cutting regressive taxes on spending.
  • Administration: complex systems cost more to run and leave more room for avoidance.

Mission 5

What supply-side policies aim to do

  • Supply-side policies: government policies that aim to raise the economy's productive capacity.
  • They work on the quantity and quality of factors of production and on how efficiently markets work.
  • Goals:
    • long-term growth: LRAS shifts right
    • a lower natural rate of unemployment
    • growth with less inflationary pressure
    • better international competitiveness
Mission 5

On the AD/AS diagram

  • Capacity rises: LRAS shifts right.
  • Productive capacity rises from Yfe1 to Yfe2.
  • With AD unchanged: real output rises, the price level falls.
  • Lower costs per unit often shift SRAS right too.
  • On the Keynesian AS, the vertical section shifts right.
  • Employment can rise at a lower natural rate of unemployment.
Quick check
?Which policy is most clearly aimed at the supply side of the economy?
Mission 5

Worked example: per person, what can policy change?

  • Illustrative data. Real GDP per person = output per hour × hours per worker × workers ÷ population.
  • Population 10 million; workers 5 million; 1,600 hours each a year; output per hour 50.
  • Real GDP = 50 × 1,600 × 5 million = 400 billion. Per person: 40,000.
  • Policy 1: childcare and job support bring 0.5 million more people into work. Real GDP = 440 billion; per person 44,000 (+10%).
  • Policy 2: training and better equipment raise output per hour to 55. Per person also 44,000 (+10%).
  • Both together: 48,400 per person: +21%, not +20%, because the effects multiply.
  • Labour productivity (output per worker or per hour) depends on capital per worker, skills, health, technology and management.

Mission 5

Market-based policies: how they work

  • Lower income tax raises the reward for work: some work more hours or join the labour force.
  • Lower profit tax raises the return on investment: more capital, more productivity.
  • Benefit reform: a bigger gap between income in and out of work makes taking a job more attractive. Voluntary unemployment falls: a lower natural rate.
  • Deregulation and privatisation: fewer rules and a profit motive push firms to cut costs and innovate.
  • Competition policy and trade liberalisation: rivals force firms to be efficient.
  • Labour market reform: weaker union power and more flexible contracts let wages and jobs adjust faster.
  • But: some of these may widen inequality or weaken job security.
Mission 5

Interventionist policies: how they work

  • Education and training raise human capital: more output per worker. Fewer skill gaps: lower structural unemployment.
  • Health care: fewer days lost to illness; longer working lives.
  • R&D: new ideas spill over to other firms (a positive externality), so markets under-invest. Subsidies or tax credits raise R&D.
  • Infrastructure: roads, rail and broadband cut firms' costs and link workers to jobs.
  • Industrial policy: support for strategic industries.
  • But: costly, slow, and the government may "pick losers" (government failure).
Quick check
?A government sells its state-owned rail company to private investors and lets rival firms bid to run routes. How is this policy meant to raise LRAS?
Mission 5

Two effects at different speeds

  • Many supply-side policies also change spending:
    • building roads or schools is government spending: AD shifts right at once
    • a tax cut raises disposable income: AD shifts right
  • The supply-side effect comes later, once the road is built or the trained workers arrive: LRAS shifts right.
  • So fiscal policy can have supply-side effects (e.g. tax incentives), and supply-side policies can have demand-side effects.

Mission 5

Real world: cutting taxes on company profits

  • The US cut its main federal corporate tax rate from 35% to 21% from 2018.
  • Aim: more investment in capital, so higher productivity and pay.
  • One 2024 study estimated investment rose by about 11% at firms given the average tax cut, compared with firms given none, but wages and growth rose by less than supporters had claimed.
  • The UK cut its main corporation tax rate from 28% (2010) to 19% (2017), then raised it to 25% in 2023 for profits over £250,000.
  • Question to weigh: how would you judge whether a tax cut on profits raised capacity, and over how many years?
Mission 5Evaluate

How well do supply-side policies work?

It depends on:

  • Time lags. Education, health and infrastructure take years, even decades, to raise capacity.
  • Response to incentives. Tax cuts may change effort or investment only a little; profits may go to shareholders instead.
  • Cost and trade-offs. Interventionist policies need spending (opportunity cost); market-based ones may widen inequality.
  • The output gap. With weak AD, extra capacity sits unused; Keynesians would boost demand first.
  • Government failure. Poor information can lead to wasted subsidies or badly designed rules.
  • Strength: if they work, growth with lower inflation and a lower natural rate of unemployment.
Mission check

?A government expands free childcare and job training, paid for by cutting other spending, so AD does not change. Over several years, more parents take jobs and workers become more skilled. According to the AD/AS model, what happens in the long run?

Supply-side policies work on capacity, not spending, and capacity takes years to build.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Why can the right cure for one slump be the wrong cure for the next?

Make a guess: When an economy slumps, what should a government do first?

Mission 6

The toolkit and the policy mix

  • Three sets of tools:
    • Fiscal policy: government spending and taxes.
    • Monetary policy: interest rates, the money supply, QE.
    • Supply-side policies: raise productive capacity (education, incentives, competition).
  • Policy mix: how these are combined at one time.
  • Fiscal and monetary policy can push the same way (both expansionary) or opposite ways (one expansionary, one contractionary).
  • Policies that shift AD are demand-side; policies that shift LRAS are supply-side.
Mission 6

Combining fiscal and monetary policy

  • Step 1: start with real output below full employment.
  • This is a negative output gap.
  • Step 2: both expansionary: AD shifts right for two reasons. Real output and the price level both rise.
  • Step 3: the interest rate:
    • Fiscal expansion raises incomes and spending, so demand for money rises: rates are pushed up.
    • Monetary expansion raises the money supply: rates are pushed down.
    • Overall change: indeterminate. It depends on which effect is bigger.
  • Opposite directions flip this: the interest-rate change is certain, the AD change is not.
    • Expansionary fiscal + contractionary monetary: interest rate up; AD uncertain.
    • Contractionary fiscal + expansionary monetary: interest rate down; AD uncertain.
  • Say: "the change in the interest rate is indeterminate" — not: "it stays the same".

Mix the policies

Illustrative data. Start: real output 960, full employment at 1,000, interest rate 5%.

  • Takeaway: two pushes the same way make the AD change certain; two pushes in opposite ways make the interest-rate change certain.
Quick check
?A government cuts its spending to reduce a budget deficit. At the same time, the central bank buys government bonds to increase the money supply. Which outcome is certain?
Mission 6

Demand shocks and supply shocks

  • An external shock starts outside the economy and outside the government's control.
  • Demand shock: e.g. a world recession cuts demand for exports.
    • AD shifts left. Real output and the price level fall.
    • Expansionary policy can push both back up: no conflict.
  • Supply shock: e.g. world energy prices jump.
    • SRAS shifts left. Real output falls and the price level rises: stagflation.
    • Raising AD helps jobs but adds to inflation. Cutting AD slows inflation but deepens the slump: a policy dilemma.
  • First step in any crisis: diagnose the shock.
  • Say: "a cost-push supply shock" — not: "inflation caused by too much spending".
Mission 6

Conflicts and trade-offs

  • Hitting one objective can make another harder:
    • Inflation vs unemployment: in the short run, along the SRPC, lower inflation may mean higher unemployment.
    • Growth vs the current account: faster growth raises imports.
    • Growth vs the environment: more output can mean more pollution and resource use.
    • Growth vs equality: gains may go mostly to some groups.
  • Unintended consequences: e.g. very low interest rates for years can push up house and share prices.
  • Macroeconomic perspectives differ:
    • Keynesian: markets can stay in a slump for a long time; demand-side policy helps.
    • Classical / monetarist: markets self-correct; policy should keep inflation low and boost supply.
  • Say: "a trade-off" when one objective is given up for another — not: "the policy failed".
Quick check
?A country that imports most of its energy is hit by a sharp rise in world energy prices. Unemployment and inflation both rise. Which statement is most accurate?
Mission 6

The Great Depression

  • USA, 1929–33: real GDP fell by about a quarter; unemployment reached about 25% in 1933.
  • Thousands of banks failed. The money supply fell by about a third.
    • The Federal Reserve did little to stop it.
    • The Smoot–Hawley tariffs (1930) raised taxes on imports; other countries hit back.
  • Recovery began after 1933: the US left the gold standard; the New Deal brought public works and bank reform.
  • UK: governments aimed to balance the budget and cut spending in 1931.
    • The UK left the gold standard in 1931. Bank Rate was cut to 2% in 1932: "cheap money".
  • Keynes's General Theory (1936) argued that governments should raise spending in a slump.
  • Two readings, both still argued:
    • Keynesian: a collapse in spending that budget-balancing made worse.
    • Monetarist: a collapse in money that the central bank allowed.
Mission 6

The 1970s: when the old cure stopped working

  • After 1945, full employment was the top priority. Governments managed AD to keep unemployment low.
  • 1973–74: oil prices roughly quadrupled. A supply shock.
  • Result: stagflation. UK inflation (RPI) peaked at about 27% in 1975.
  • Raising AD to cut unemployment now pushed inflation even higher.
  • Direct controls on inflation were tried:
    • Incomes policies: legal or agreed limits on pay rises (UK, 1970s).
    • Price controls: the US froze wages and prices for 90 days in 1971.
  • Problems with direct controls:
    • They treat symptoms, not causes.
    • Shortages and black markets where prices are held below equilibrium.
    • Hard to enforce; pent-up pay and price rises when they end.
Mission 6

Monetarism and new priorities

  • Late 1970s: inflation became the top priority.
  • USA: from 1979, the Fed (chair Paul Volcker) cut money growth. The federal funds rate reached nearly 20% in 1980–81.
    • Inflation fell from 13.5% (1980) to 3.2% (1983).
    • The cost: a deep recession; unemployment hit 10.8% in 1982.
  • UK: from 1980, the government set targets for money supply growth (monetarism).
    • Inflation fell, but unemployment rose above 3 million in the 1980s.
    • Money targets proved hard to hit and were dropped.
  • 1992: the UK set an inflation target. 1997: the Bank of England was given control of interest rates.
  • Since 2008, financial stability has moved up the list.
Mission 6

2008: the response

  • The global financial crisis began in the banks: lending froze, AD collapsed.
  • UK:
    • Bank Rate cut from 5% (2008) to 0.5% (March 2009).
    • Quantitative easing: the Bank of England bought £200bn of assets, mainly government bonds, by early 2010.
    • VAT cut from 17.5% to 15% for 13 months.
    • Government stakes in failing banks (RBS, Lloyds).
  • USA:
    • Federal funds rate cut to 0–0.25% (December 2008); large-scale asset purchases (QE).
    • $700bn fund to rescue banks (TARP, 2008).
    • $787bn stimulus package (2009).
  • Lessons drawn from the 1930s: stop the money supply and banks collapsing; support AD.
Mission 6

After 2008: the austerity debate

  • From 2010, the UK and many other countries cut deficits: fiscal consolidation ("austerity").
  • Arguments for:
    • High debt can raise borrowing costs. In 2010, Greece's borrowing costs soared.
    • A credible plan may boost confidence and keep interest rates low.
    • Smaller deficits leave room to respond to the next shock.
  • Arguments against:
    • Cutting spending in a weak economy reduces AD and growth.
    • A study by IMF economists (2013) found the multiplier in the crisis was larger than forecasters assumed, so cuts hit output harder.
    • With interest rates near zero, monetary policy could not offset the cuts.
  • Your judgement depends on: the size of the multiplier, the level of debt, and market confidence.
Quick check
?In the USA from 1929 to 1933, the money supply fell by about a third as thousands of banks failed. Which response in 2008 most clearly reflects that lesson?
Mission 6

Problems and constraints for policymakers

  • Time lags: spotting a problem, acting and the effect can take 18 months or more.
  • Poor data and forecasts: GDP figures are revised; the size of the output gap is uncertain.
  • The zero lower bound: rates near 0% leave little room to cut.
  • High public debt limits fiscal room.
  • A global economy:
    • External shocks (energy prices, world recessions, pandemics) are outside any one government's control.
    • Capital flows and exchange rates can undo interest-rate changes.
    • Low-income countries face extra limits: poverty, weak tax bases, costly borrowing.
    • Competitiveness depends on supply-side factors that change slowly.
  • Expectations and politics: short-termism before elections; rising inflation expectations.
  • Result: government failure in macro policy is possible: a policy that arrives late or is the wrong size can destabilise the economy.
Mission 6Evaluate

Which policy works best?

  • Criteria for success: are the targets met (e.g. 2% inflation)? At what cost? How fast? With what side-effects? Who gains and who loses?
  • Relative effectiveness:
    • Monetary: quick to change, but weak near 0% rates and slow to work (about 18–24 months).
    • Fiscal: strong in a deep slump, but slow to agree and limited by debt.
    • Supply-side: lasting gains in capacity, but they take years.
  • It depends on:
    • The cause of the problem: demand shock or supply shock?
    • Spare capacity: a big gap favours stimulus; little spare capacity means inflation.
    • The state of public finances and how open the economy is.
Mission check

?Two economies both face rising unemployment. In Economy X, demand for its exports has collapsed and inflation is falling below target. In Economy Y, the price of imported food and fuel has doubled and inflation is rising above target. Which judgement is best?

Diagnose before you prescribe: a demand shock calls for stimulus, a supply shock forces a choice.

Mission 7: Can a country buy lower unemployment by accepting a little more inflation?

Make a guess: Can a country buy lower unemployment by accepting a little more inflation?

Mission 7

The original Phillips curve

  • In 1958, the economist A. W. Phillips plotted UK data from 1861 to 1957.
  • When unemployment was low, money wages rose fast. When unemployment was high, wages rose slowly.
  • Faster wage growth feeds into faster price rises, so the idea became a link between inflation and unemployment.
  • The curve slopes down: lower unemployment goes with higher inflation.
  • It is now called the short-run Phillips curve (SRPC).
  • Axes: inflation rate (%) up the side, unemployment rate (%) along the bottom.
Mission 7

Why a trade-off? Back to AD/AS

  • Step 1: AD rises. The economy moves up along SRAS.
  • Real output rises, so firms hire: unemployment falls.
  • The price level rises faster: inflation rises.
  • Step 2: the same change is a movement up along the SRPC.
  • A fall in AD does the reverse: down along SRAS, down along the SRPC.
  • Say: "a movement along the SRPC" — not: "a shift of the SRPC", when AD changes.

Mission 7

Real world: the trade-off holds, then breaks

  • 1950s–60s: UK data fitted the curve well. Many thought governments could choose a point on it.
  • 1970s: UK inflation averaged about 24% in 1975 (RPI), while unemployment also rose.
  • High inflation and high unemployment: stagflation.
  • Points no longer sat on one curve: the SRPC had shifted outwards.
  • One big reason: oil-price supply shocks raised costs.
Mission 7

Along the curve, or a shift?

  • Change in AD (demand shock): movement along the SRPC.
  • Supply shock (e.g. a sharp rise in oil or energy prices): SRAS shifts left, so the SRPC shifts right (up). Higher inflation at every unemployment rate.
  • A favourable supply shock (cheaper energy, faster productivity growth) shifts the SRPC left (down).
Quick check
?A country invests heavily in solar and wind power. Over several years, firms' energy costs fall sharply. Other things equal, what happens on a Phillips curve diagram?
Mission 7Evaluate

How useful is the Phillips curve?

It depends on:

  • Time horizon: a short-run trade-off may exist.
  • Supply shocks: they shift the SRPC, so past data may not predict the future.
  • Credible inflation targets: if people trust the central bank, expectations stay near target. The curve can look flat.
  • UK evidence: in 2019 unemployment was 3.8%, yet CPI inflation was only 1.8%.
  • Global factors (cheap imports, energy prices) also drive inflation, not just domestic unemployment.
Mission check

?A sudden rise in world energy prices hits an economy. Over a year, inflation rises from 2% to 6% and unemployment rises from 4% to 5%. Which best describes this on a Phillips curve diagram?

A lower-unemployment, higher-inflation deal exists only along one short-run curve. Supply shocks can shift that curve.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A central bank is banker to the government and to the banks; it issues notes and runs monetary policy.
  • Its main goal is low, stable inflation; its tools include the policy rate, open market operations and QE.
  • It is lender of last resort, helps keep the financial system safe, and is often independent of government.

Mission 2

  • The central bank changes the policy rate to steer AD and keep inflation near its target.
  • Higher rates: borrowing costs more, asset prices lower, currency stronger. AD shifts left; inflation slows.
  • Lags, supply shocks and low confidence limit what interest rates can do.
  • Near the zero lower bound, rate cuts lose their power.

Mission 3

  • At the zero lower bound, QE creates reserves to buy bonds: bond prices rise, yields fall, borrowing gets cheaper, AD shifts right.
  • Reversing QE (QT) contracts the money supply and pushes yields up.

Mission 4

  • Direct taxes fall on income and wealth. Indirect taxes fall on spending.
  • Progressive, proportional or regressive: judged by the share of income paid (ART), not the amount.
  • ART = total tax ÷ income. MRT = tax on the next unit of income.
  • Laffer curve: above the revenue-maximising rate, a lower rate can raise revenue.

Mission 5

  • Supply-side policies aim to raise capacity: LRAS shifts right, raising real output without adding to inflation.
  • Market-based policies use incentives and competition; interventionist policies fix under-provision of skills, health, R&D and infrastructure.
  • Effects take years and are uncertain in size.

Mission 6

  • The policy mix combines fiscal, monetary and supply-side tools. When two policies push the same way, the AD change is certain and the interest-rate change is not, and the reverse.
  • Diagnose the shock first: demand shocks allow stimulus without conflict; supply shocks force a trade-off.
  • Priorities shifted: full employment after 1945, inflation from the late 1970s, financial stability after 2008. Each crisis was fought with lessons from the last.

Mission 7

  • The short-run Phillips curve slopes down: a rise in AD lowers unemployment and raises inflation.
  • Supply shocks shift the SRPC; changes in AD move the economy along it.
