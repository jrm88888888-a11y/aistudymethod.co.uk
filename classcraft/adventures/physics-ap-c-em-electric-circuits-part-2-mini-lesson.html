Mission 1

Mission 1: Go once round any loop of a circuit. Why do the rises and drops in potential always cancel?

Make a guess: Go once round any loop of a circuit. Why do the rises and drops in potential always cancel?

Mission 1

Potential round a loop

  • Set V = 0 at the battery's negative terminal.
  • Through the battery, − to +: V rises by ε.
  • Through each resistor, along the current: V falls by IR.
  • Along an ideal wire: V stays constant.
  • Back at the start: same V. So ΣΔV = 0: a charge returns with the same potential energy.
Quick check
?A battery and three resistors, R1, R2 and R3, form a single loop. The graph shows the electric potential V as a function of position around the loop, starting at the battery's negative terminal; the position axis shows only the order of the elements. Which ranking of the resistances is correct?
Mission 1

Several loops

  • One element can belong to more than one loop.
  • The loop rule holds for every closed loop, with or without a battery in it.
  • Signs: resistor crossed along the current: −IR. Against it: +IR.
  • Battery crossed − to +: +ε. + to −: −ε.
  • Use the junction rule to cut down the unknown currents.
Mission 1

Worked example

  • Ideal 12 V battery. R1 = 2.0 Ω in series with R2 = 3.0 Ω and R3 = 6.0 Ω in parallel.
  • Junction: I1 = I2 + I3.
  • Loop through the battery, R1 and R2: 12 − 2.0I1 − 3.0I2 = 0.
  • Loop through R2 and R3 only (no battery): 3.0I2 − 6.0I3 = 0, so I2 = 2I3.
  • Solve: I1 = 3.0 A, I2 = 2.0 A, I3 = 1.0 A.
  • Check: 12 − 2.0(3.0) − 3.0(2.0) = 0.
Mission 1

Two batteries

  • Left branch: an ideal battery ε1 = 9.0 V in series with a 3.0 Ω resistor. Right branch: an ideal battery ε2 = 6.0 V in series with a 3.0 Ω resistor. Middle branch: a 1.0 Ω resistor. Both + terminals face the top junction.
  • Junction: middle current = I1 + I2.
  • Left loop: 9.0 − 3.0I1 − 1.0(I1 + I2) = 0.
  • Right loop: 6.0 − 3.0I2 − 1.0(I1 + I2) = 0.
  • Solve: I1 = 2.0 A, I2 = 1.0 A, middle 3.0 A.
  • A current that comes out negative was just guessed the wrong way.
Quick check
?The circuit shown contains two ideal batteries and two resistors in a single loop. In terms of ε and R, what is the magnitude of the current in the loop?
Mission 1

Short circuits and real meters

  • Wire across an element: its ends are at the same potential. ΔV = 0, so no current in it.
  • The rest of the circuit now has a smaller Req, so the battery current rises.
  • A real ammeter has some resistance: it lowers the current it measures.
  • A real voltmeter has finite resistance: it draws current and lowers the ΔV it measures.
  • To calculate: treat the meter as one more resistor in the circuit.
Quick check
?Two resistors, each of resistance R, are connected in series with an ideal battery of emf ε. A nonideal voltmeter, whose resistance is 4R, is connected across one of the resistors. What does the voltmeter read?
Mission check

?A student builds a single loop from a battery, two resistors R1 and R2, a switch and connecting wires. Using an ideal voltmeter, the student measures 6.0 V across the battery's terminals, 2.5 V across R1 and 2.5 V across R2. Which conclusion is best supported by these measurements?

Energy is conserved: a charge gains qε in the battery and loses it all in the elements of the loop. So the ΔVs round any closed loop add to zero; a "missing" 1.0 V must be across something in the loop.

Mission 2

Mission 2: A kettle's element gets very hot, yet the cable carrying the same current stays cool. Why?

Make a guess: A kettle's element gets very hot, yet the cable carrying the same current stays cool. Why?

Mission 2

Power: energy per second

  • Power = rate of energy transfer. 1 W = 1 J/s.
  • Potential difference = joules per coulomb. Current = coulombs per second.
  • Multiply: joules per second.
  • P = IΔV.
Mission 2

Three forms of one law

  • Use ΔV = IR: P = IΔV = I²R = ΔV²/R.
  • Same current (in series): use I²R. Larger R → more power.
  • Same ΔV (in parallel): use ΔV²/R. Smaller R → more power.
Quick check
?The potential difference across an ohmic resistor is increased so that the current in the resistor becomes three times as large. What is the ratio of the new power dissipated in the resistor, P2, to the original power, P1?
Mission 2

Energy transferred

  • ΔE = PΔt = IΔVΔt.
  • A heater on 12 V takes 3.0 A for 5.0 minutes.
  • P = 12 × 3.0 = 36 W. t = 5.0 × 60 = 300 s.
  • Energy = 36 × 300 = 1.1 × 10⁴ J.
  • Check: R = 12 ÷ 3.0 = 4.0 Ω; I²R = 3.0² × 4.0 = 36 W.
Quick check
?A steady current of 0.25 A flows in a 24 Ω resistor for 2.0 minutes. How much energy is dissipated in the resistor?
Mission 2

The heating effect

  • Electrons collide with the lattice ions and pass on energy. The ions vibrate more: the resistor heats up.
  • Kettle element and its cable are in series: the same current.
  • P = I²R: the element's resistance is far larger, so nearly all the power is dissipated there.
  • The cable's resistance is tiny: very little heating.
  • Wanted heating: kettles, heaters. Unwanted: cables, chargers.
Mission 2

Brightness of bulbs

  • A bulb's brightness increases with its power.
  • Compare bulbs with P = IΔV.
  • Identical bulbs: the one with the larger current is brighter.
  • Bulbs in series (same I): the larger R is brighter. In parallel (same ΔV): the smaller R is brighter.
Quick check
?In the circuit shown, the current in each of the lightbulbs X, Y and Z and the potential difference across it are labeled beside it. Which ranking of the brightnesses of the bulbs is correct?
Mission check

?Two identical heating coils, each of resistance R, are connected to the same ideal battery: first in series, then in parallel. What is the total power in parallel divided by the total power in series?

Same potential difference: the smaller resistance takes more power. Same current, as in the kettle and its cable: the larger resistance takes more power (P = I²R).

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why do a car's headlights dim while its starter motor is turning?

Make a guess: Why do a car's headlights dim while its starter motor is turning?

Mission 3

emf and internal resistance

  • emf: energy transferred to each coulomb of charge by the source.
  • emf = the ΔV across the terminals when there is no current in the battery.
  • A real cell has internal resistance r. Model: an ideal cell in series with r.
  • With a current, some energy per coulomb is dissipated inside the battery: a potential difference Ir across r.
  • Terminal potential difference = emf − Ir.
Mission 3

Terminal potential difference

  • ΔVterminal = ε − Ir.
  • No current: terminal potential difference = emf.
Quick check
?A battery with an internal resistance of 0.50 Ω drives a current of 0.60 A through a lamp, and an ideal voltmeter across the battery's terminals reads 4.2 V. The lamp is then disconnected, so that only the voltmeter remains connected across the battery. What does the voltmeter now read?
Mission 3

Worked example

  • Cell: emf 1.5 V, internal resistance 0.50 Ω. External resistor: 2.5 Ω.
  • Current = emf ÷ total resistance = 1.5 ÷ (2.5 + 0.50) = 0.50 A.
  • Potential difference across r: Ir = 0.50 × 0.50 = 0.25 V.
  • Terminal potential difference = 1.5 − 0.25 = 1.25 V.
  • Check: IR = 0.50 × 2.5 = 1.25 V.
Quick check
?A battery of emf ε and internal resistance r is connected to an external resistor of resistance R. What is the potential difference across the terminals of the battery?
Mission 3

Consequences

  • More current → larger Ir → lower terminal potential difference.
  • A starter motor draws a very large current. The battery's terminal potential difference drops, so the headlights dim.
  • Short circuit (R = 0): current = emf ÷ r. Small r → a very large current; the cell heats up.
Quick check
?In the circuit shown, the battery inside the dashed box has an emf ε and an internal resistance r that is much smaller than the resistance of either bulb. Bulbs A and B are identical, and the voltmeter is ideal. Switch S is initially open. When S is closed, what happens to the voltmeter reading?
Mission check

?A battery of internal resistance r is connected to an external resistor whose resistance equals r. The external resistor is then replaced by one of resistance 2r. How does the potential difference across the terminals of the battery change?

Larger external resistance → smaller current → smaller Ir inside the battery → higher terminal potential difference. The starter motor is the reverse: tiny resistance, huge current, so the terminal potential difference drops and the headlights dim.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Join two different capacitors in series to a battery. Which one ends up holding more charge?

Make a guess: Join two different capacitors in series to a battery. Which one ends up holding more charge?

Mission 4

Capacitors in parallel and in series

  • Parallel: same potential difference across each; the charges add, Q = Q1 + Q2.
  • Ceq,p = ΣCi
  • Series: the same charge Q on every capacitor. The two joined inner plates form an isolated conductor: they hold −Q and +Q, and charge is conserved.
  • The potential differences add, ΔV = ΔV1 + ΔV2.
  • 1/Ceq,s = Σ(1/Ci)
  • Series total is less than the smallest; parallel total is more than the largest.
Mission 4

Worked example

  • 3.0 μF and 6.0 μF in series across 12 V.
  • 1/C = 1/3.0 + 1/6.0 = 1/2.0, so C = 2.0 μF.
  • Q = 2.0 μF × 12 V = 24 μC on each capacitor.
  • Potential differences: 24 ÷ 3.0 = 8.0 V and 24 ÷ 6.0 = 4.0 V. The smaller capacitor takes the larger potential difference.
  • The same pair in parallel: C = 9.0 μF, total charge 9.0 × 12 = 108 μC (1.1 × 10² μC).
Quick check
?Two capacitors, of capacitance C and 3C, are connected in series with an ideal battery of emf ε. After they have been charging for a long time, what is the potential difference across the capacitor C?
Quick check
?Four identical capacitors, each of capacitance C, are first connected all in parallel, giving an equivalent capacitance CX. They are then reconnected all in series, giving an equivalent capacitance CY. How does CX compare with CY?
Quick check
?A 4.7 μF capacitor is connected to a 9.0 V battery. Without changing the battery, which change doubles the total charge taken from the battery?
Mission check

?The circuit shown contains a battery and three capacitors, initially uncharged. After the capacitors have been charging for a long time, which capacitor has the largest potential difference across it?

The plates joined between two series capacitors form an isolated conductor that starts neutral: if one plate holds −Q, the other holds +Q. So every capacitor in a series chain carries the same charge, whatever its capacitance.

Mission 5

Mission 5: A discharging capacitor never quite empties. So what sets how fast it gets close?

Make a guess: A discharging capacitor never quite empties. So what sets how fast it gets close?

Mission 5

Charging, step by step

  • Battery (emf ε), resistor R and uncharged capacitor C in series. Close the switch.
  • At the start: ΔVC = 0, so the whole emf is across R. The current is largest: I0 = ε/R.
  • An uncharged capacitor acts like a wire at the instant it is connected.
  • Electrons pile onto one plate and leave the other. ΔVC rises.
  • Potential difference across R = ε − ΔVC falls, so the current falls, so charging slows.
  • After a long time: ΔVC = ε, current zero. Fully charged.
Mission 5

Discharging

  • Now connect the charged capacitor across R alone.
  • The capacitor's potential difference drives the current: I = ΔVC/R. Electrons flow back to the plate they left. The current is reversed compared with charging.
  • Charge leaves → ΔVC falls → current falls → charge leaves more slowly.
  • The rate of loss is proportional to the charge left. That is exponential decay.
  • It gets ever closer to zero but, in this model, never reaches it.
Mission 5

The graphs

  • Charging: Q and ΔVC rise steeply, then level off at the final value. Current starts at I0 and decays to zero.
  • Discharging: Q, ΔVC and I all decay with the same shape.
  • Q and ΔVC always have the same shape, because Q = CΔVC.
  • Slope of a Q–t graph = current.
  • Area under an I–t graph = charge that has flowed.
Quick check
?The graph shows the potential difference ΔVC across a capacitor as a function of time t while it charges through a resistor from an ideal battery of emf ε. Points J, K and L are marked on the curve, and IJ, IK and IL are the currents in the resistor at those times. Which ranking of the currents is correct?
Mission 5

The time constant

  • Time constant τ = Req Ceq. Unit: Ω × F = s.
  • Discharging: after one τ, 37% of the starting charge is left.
  • Charging: after one τ, the charge has reached 63% of its final value.
  • After about 5τ: over 99% of the way. Treat as finished.
  • Bigger R → smaller current → slower. Bigger C → more charge to move → slower.
  • The starting potential difference does not change τ.
Quick check
?An uncharged capacitor is charged through a 3.0 kΩ resistor by an ideal battery of emf 9.0 V, all connected in series. What is the current in the resistor at the instant when the potential difference across the capacitor is 6.0 V?
Mission 5

After a long time

  • Just after switching on: an uncharged capacitor behaves like a wire (potential difference zero).
  • After a long time (≫ τ): no current in the capacitor's branch. It behaves like a break in the circuit.
  • Its potential difference then equals the potential difference across whatever it is connected in parallel with.
  • Example: 9.0 V battery, 2.0 kΩ and 1.0 kΩ in series, capacitor across the 1.0 kΩ. Long after: I = 9.0 ÷ 3.0 kΩ = 3.0 mA; ΔVC = 3.0 mA × 1.0 kΩ = 3.0 V.
Quick check
?In the circuit shown, bulbs X and Y are identical, the battery is ideal and capacitor C is initially uncharged. Switch S is closed and left closed for a long time. How do the bulbs then compare?
Mission check

?An uncharged capacitor is charged through a resistor by a battery. Its charge takes a time t to reach 63% of its final value. The capacitor is emptied. The battery is replaced by one with twice the emf, and a second, identical resistor is added in series with the first. How long does the charge now take to reach 63% of its new final value?

The pace is set by RC alone: the time constant. Every RC, the charge falls to 37% of what it was, so it gets close to empty but never quite there.

Mission 6

Mission 6: After one time constant, a charging capacitor is about two-thirds full. Where does that fraction come from?

Make a guess: After one time constant, a charging capacitor is about two-thirds full. Where does that fraction come from?

Mission 6

From the loop rule to an equation

  • Series loop: battery ε, resistor R, capacitor C. Switch closed at t = 0, capacitor uncharged.
  • Loop rule: ε − IR − q/C = 0.
  • The current is the rate at which charge arrives on the plate: I = dq/dt.
  • So ε = R dq/dt + q/C: a first-order differential equation for q(t).
  • Read it before solving: as q grows, q/C grows, so dq/dt must fall.
Quick check
?An uncharged capacitor of capacitance C is connected in series with a resistor of resistance R, a switch and an ideal battery of emf ε, and the switch is closed at t = 0. Let q be the charge on the capacitor. At the instant when q = Cε/4, what is dq/dt?
Mission 6

Solving it

  • Rearrange: R dq/dt = (Cε − q)/C.
  • Separate: dq/(Cε − q) = dt/(RC).
  • Integrate, q from 0 to q, t from 0 to t:
  • −ln[(Cε − q)/(Cε)] = t/(RC).
  • q = Cε(1 − e−t/RC).
  • Check: t = 0 gives q = 0 ✓. Substituting back gives ε ✓.
Mission 6

What the solution says

  • I = dq/dt = (ε/R)e−t/RC. Starts at ε/R: the empty capacitor acts like a wire.
  • ΔVC = q/C = ε(1 − e−t/RC); ΔVR = εe−t/RC. They always add to ε.
  • τ = RC comes out of the solution. Units: Ω × F = s.
  • At t = RC: q = Cε(1 − e⁻¹) = 0.63Cε, about two-thirds full, whatever ε, R and C. The fraction comes from e.
  • e−t/RC is never zero, so q never quite reaches Cε. After 5τ it is within 1%.
  • The initial slope ε/R, kept up, would fill the capacitor in exactly t = RC.
Quick check
?An uncharged capacitor of capacitance C, a resistor of resistance R and an ideal battery of emf ε are connected in series, and the circuit is completed at t = 0. By solving the loop-rule equation ε = (dq/dt)R + q/C, find the time t at which the current in the resistor is half its initial value.
Mission 6

Discharging

  • Remove the battery; the charged capacitor (Q0) drives current through R.
  • Loop rule: q/C − IR = 0. Now the plate loses charge: I = −dq/dt.
  • R dq/dt + q/C = 0: the same equation with ε = 0.
  • Separate: dq/q = −dt/(RC) → q = Q0e−t/RC.
  • I = −dq/dt = (Q0/RC)e−t/RC = (ΔV0/R)e−t/RC.
  • After each τ, the charge, potential difference and current are all multiplied by e⁻¹ = 0.37.
Mission 6

Worked example

  • ε = 12 V, R = 15 kΩ, C = 200 μF, uncharged. When does ΔVC reach 9.0 V?
  • τ = RC = 15 × 10³ × 200 × 10⁻⁶ = 3.0 s.
  • 9.0 = 12(1 − e−t/3.0) → e−t/3.0 = 0.25 → t = 3.0 ln 4 = 4.2 s.
  • Current then: I = (12 ÷ 15 × 10³)(0.25) = 0.20 mA.
  • Charge then: q = CΔVC = 200 × 10⁻⁶ × 9.0 = 1.8 mC (of a final 2.4 mC).
Quick check
?An uncharged 50 μF capacitor is connected in series with a 20 kΩ resistor, an open switch and an ideal 9.0 V battery. The switch is closed at t = 0. What is the current in the resistor at t = 2.0 s?
Mission check

?A capacitor of capacitance C is charged, disconnected from the battery and then, at t = 0, connected across a resistor of resistance R. At what time t is the energy stored in the capacitor half of its value at t = 0?

The loop rule makes the rate of change proportional to what is left to change, so the solution is exponential. After one RC the gap left is e⁻¹ = 0.37 of the start, so the capacitor has 1 − e⁻¹ = 0.63 of its final charge (roughly two-thirds), for any battery, resistor or capacitor.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Potential against position round a loop: rises across the battery, falls by IR across each resistor, flat along wires; ΣΔV = 0.
  • Multi-loop circuits: junction rule at junctions, loop rule round as many loops as there are unknowns.
  • Short circuit: ΔV = 0 across the bypassed element. Real meters change the circuit: voltmeters should have large R, ammeters small R.

Mission 2

  • P = IΔV = I²R = ΔV²/R; brighter bulb = larger power.

Mission 3

  • ΔVterminal = ε − Ir; emf = terminal ΔV with no current.

Mission 4

  • Parallel: Ceq,p = ΣCi; series: 1/Ceq,s = Σ(1/Ci), same charge on each.

Mission 5

  • Charging: Q and ΔVC rise to a limit while I decays; uncharged it acts like a wire, after a long time like a break.
  • τ = Req Ceq: 37% left after one τ (discharging), 63% reached (charging).

Mission 6

  • Loop rule with I = dq/dt: ε = R dq/dt + q/C. Separate and integrate: q = Cε(1 − e−t/RC), I = (ε/R)e−t/RC.
  • Discharging: R dq/dt + q/C = 0 gives q = Q0e−t/RC; τ = RC appears in the solution.
