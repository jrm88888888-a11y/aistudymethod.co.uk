Component 1: 20th Century Literature Reading & Creative Prose Writing

This mini-lesson trains the skills Eduqas Component 1 tests: reading an unseen 20th century literary prose extract, analysing how language and structure create effects, and writing one strong creative prose task — a narrative or recount.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go (spot the device, pick the best comment, match method to effect) and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you're ready.

Know the component

What Component 1 asks of you

Section A gives you one unseen literary prose extract from the 20th century (roughly 60–100 lines). You answer a ladder of structured questions worth 40 marks that get harder as they go:

  • List / retrieval — find explicit details from a short section (tests AO1).
  • Language analysis — how the writer uses language to create an effect (AO2).
  • Structure analysis — how the extract is shaped and ordered for the reader (AO2).
  • Evaluation — a critical judgement on a statement about the extract (AO4).

Section B is creative prose writing (40 marks): you choose one of four titles and write a narrative or recount — assessed on communication & organisation (AO5) and technical accuracy (AO6).

Unseen extract: unlike Literature, you don't study these texts in advance — the extract is unseen. So the skill you practise is reading any 20th century prose closely, fast.

Reading skill

Read like a detective — annotate

Marks are lost by readers who skim. Before you write a word, annotate the extract: mark it up so your analysis is built on evidence, not vibes.

  • Character & mood — who is here, and what feeling is created?
  • Setting — where and when; how the place shapes the mood.
  • Underline striking words and phrases (verbs, adjectives, imagery).
  • Bracket structural moves — where the focus shifts, short punchy sentences, a change of tone.
  • Tone — jot the mood in the margin (tense? calm? menacing?).

60-second scan first: get the gist of the whole extract before answering, so your language and structure comments fit the bigger picture.

Toolkit · AO2

Language methods & their effects

Naming a device earns almost nothing. The marks are in the effect — what it makes the reader feel or picture.

  • Metaphor / simile — paint a vivid picture; make the abstract concrete.
  • Personification — gives objects/nature feelings, life or menace.
  • Powerful verbs — carry action and mood in a single word.
  • Adjectives / sensory detail — build atmosphere and let the reader "see".
  • Simile of the senses — sound, sight, touch that immerse the reader.
  • Pathetic fallacy — weather/nature mirrors a character's feelings.
  • Contrast — light vs dark, calm vs chaos, to sharpen a mood.

The golden rule: method → quotation → effect on the reader. Miss the effect and you're only "feature-spotting".

Analysis skill · AO2

Comment on the effect, not the label

Compare two comments on the same line:

"The old house watched them from the hill." ▸ verb "watched" — personification
Weak (feature-spotting)

"The writer uses personification here. This is effective and makes it interesting."

Strong (method → effect)

"The verb 'watched' gives the house a silent, human presence, making it feel sinister and alive — so the reader senses the characters are being observed and grows uneasy."

Misconception: the marks are not for spotting the personification — they're for explaining why 'watched' works and what it does to the reader.

Toolkit · AO2

Structure — the shape of the whole extract

Eduqas has a separate structure question. Structure is how the writer orders and shapes the extract to guide the reader:

  • The opening — where the writer chooses to begin (a shock, a calm, an image).
  • Shifts in focus — zooming from wide to close, outside to inside, past to present.
  • Sentence length — a short, blunt sentence lands like a punch after long ones.
  • The ending — a cliff-hanger, a return to the opening, a lasting image.
The evaluation question · AO4

How to structure an evaluation answer

The final reading question gives you a statement (e.g. "the writer makes the reader feel the character's fear") and asks how far you agree. You judge the effect and back it with evidence. Use a What → How → Why chain (this is PEE with a judgement on top):

Evaluation = analysis + verdict. Always give your own critical view: "I strongly agree, because…", "This works well, though…".

Section B · Writing

Creative prose: choosing & planning your task

You get four titles — a mix of prompts (a title, a first line, or a picture-style scenario). Pick one and write a narrative or recount:

  • Narrative — tells a story with characters, tension and a shape (beginning, turning point, ending).
  • Recount — retells an experience or event, often first-person, in vivid detail.
  • Plan first — jot a quick arc so your story has direction, not just description.

Watch the form: the mark scheme rewards narrative / recount. Writing a poem, a play script, or pure description with no story caps your marks — so keep a clear plot even in a short piece.

Writing craft

Show, don't tell

Great creative writing shows feeling through detail rather than stating it. It's how you turn "he was scared" into something the reader can feel:

Telling vs showing

❌ "He was very frightened as he walked down the corridor."

✔ "His breath came in short bursts; each footstep echoed too loudly, and the corridor seemed to stretch away, longer than before."

  • Sensory detail — sound, sight, touch, smell put the reader inside the moment.
  • Precise verbs & nouns — "sprinted", "flinched"; not "moved", "went".
  • Varied sentences — a short one lands the tension after a longer build.

Craft over quantity: a controlled, vivid page beats a rushed, flat two pages. Quality of writing is what AO5 rewards.

Structure your story

Give your narrative a shape

Even a short story needs a structure so it feels complete, not like a description that stops. A reliable arc:

Keep the plot small and focused — one moment done vividly beats a whole life-story rushed. A clear arc is a big slice of AO5.

Worked model

Craft in action

Here is a short model opening for a narrative, with the craft moves labelled:

"The lift shuddered, then stopped. For a long second nothing moved. Somewhere above me, a cable groaned — a slow, complaining sound that seemed to come from the building itself. I pressed the alarm. Nothing. Just the dark, and my own breath, too loud." ▸ powerful verbs ("shuddered", "groaned") · personification ("the building itself") · short sentence for tension ("Nothing.") · sensory detail (sound, dark, breath)

Notice it shows fear rather than saying "I was scared" — through sound, silence and rhythm. That is what lifts AO5.

Technical accuracy · AO6

AO6 — spelling, punctuation, grammar & vocabulary

Around a fifth of the whole GCSE is for technical accuracy and ambitious vocabulary — much of it earned in your writing. Easy marks to gain, or throw away.

  • Vary your sentences — mix short and long for control and effect.
  • Punctuate accurately — full stops, commas, and the occasional colon, semicolon or dash used correctly.
  • Reach for precise vocabulary — "shattered" not "very broken"; "gaunt" not "thin and ill".
  • Proofread — leave 3–4 minutes to catch slips.

Ambition with control beats safety with errors. Aim high on vocabulary, but only use words you can spell and punctuate correctly.

Exam-day plan

Working the component for marks

  • Watch the mark tariff — a short retrieval needs a moment; a 10-mark answer needs range and depth.
  • Analyse the section named — Eduqas often points you to specific lines; stay in that zone.
  • Quote briefly and precisely — short, well-chosen quotations beat long copied chunks.
  • In Section B, plan first — 5 minutes on your story's arc saves the whole piece.
  • Save time to proofread for AO6.

Split your time by marks: Section A and Section B are worth 40 marks each — give each roughly half the paper (about 50 minutes on each).
