Mission 1: A small country tops a world wellbeing league, while far richer nations rank low. So what should development mean?

Mission 1

The traditional measure: GDP growth

  • GDP: the value of all goods and services produced in a country in a year; GDP per capita divides by population.
  • Traditional view: development = GDP growth as an end in itself.
  • Strength: easy to measure and compare.
  • Weakness: ignores inequality, health, happiness and environmental cost. The HPI's authors: GDP growth "on its own does not mean a better life for everyone".
Mission 1

The Happy Planet Index (HPI)

  • HPI ≈ (life expectancy × wellbeing) ÷ Ecological Footprint.
  • Life expectancy × wellbeing = "Happy Life Years".
  • It measures how efficiently a country turns resource use into long, happy lives.
  • 2025 edition: 134 countries.

Mission 1

A contested model: Bolivia

  • Bolivia's 2009 Constitution adopts suma qamaña ("live well") as a principle of the state.
  • It describes a state "where the search for a good life predominates".
  • Hydrocarbons are "the inalienable and unlimited property of the Bolivian people": state control of resources, not a free-market model.
  • Bolivia under Evo Morales is the specification's example of a model that contests western growth-first thinking.
  • Data: Gini 58.5 (2005) → 47.8 (2014); GDP growth averaged about 5% a year, 2006–14.
Mission 1

A contested model: Sharia-based finance

  • Islamic finance means financial activity "conforming to Islamic Law (Shari'ah)".
  • A core principle: "prohibition of the payment and the receipt of ribā (interest)".
  • Debt is replaced by asset-based finance and partnerships that share risk.
  • A World Bank volume argues that risk sharing "promotes social solidarity".
  • It contests a western model of development built on interest-bearing loans.
Mission 1

Goals vs means (Rosling)

  • The specification attributes to Hans Rosling the view that improvements in environment, health, life expectancy and human rights are the more significant goals.
  • Economic growth is then the means of delivering them, not the goal itself.
  • Test any policy: does growth here buy longer, healthier, freer lives?

Mission 1

Education as human capital

  • Human capital: the knowledge, skills and health that make people productive.
  • The World Bank's Human Capital Index "measures the human capital that a child born... today can expect to acquire".
  • Review of 1,120 estimates in 139 countries: each extra year of schooling raises earnings by about 9% a year.
  • Returns are higher in low-income countries, and women's average returns are higher than men's.
  • Education also lets people understand and assert their rights.
Mission 1

Unequal access and achievement

  • 64 million primary-age children are out of school; 53% are girls.
  • Girls in conflict-affected countries are "more than twice as likely to be out of school".
  • Barriers include poverty, child marriage, and families who "favour boys when investing in education".
  • Afghanistan: about 3.7 million children out of school, 60% of them girls (UNICEF).
  • Attitudes to gender equality in education differ: the human-capital view is not universally shared.

Mission 2

Measuring health: mortality and morbidity

  • Mortality: death (rates of dying). Morbidity: illness and disability (rates of being unwell).
  • Healthy life expectancy (HALE) combines both: years a newborn can expect in full health. World, 2023: 62.8.
  • HALE by WHO region, 2023: Western Pacific 67.3, Europe 66.7, Americas 64.4, South-East Asia 61.3, Eastern Mediterranean 60.4, Africa 56.2.
Mission 2

Infant and maternal mortality

  • IMR: deaths before age 1 per 1,000 live births. 2024: world 27.7; low-income countries 46.7; high-income 4.2.
  • MMR: maternal deaths per 100,000 live births. 2023: about 260,000 deaths, an MMR of 197.
  • Sub-Saharan Africa had around 70% of maternal deaths. Nigeria's MMR was 993; Japan's was 3.
Mission 2

Basic needs: food, water and sanitation

  • 2024: 3.4 billion people lacked safely managed sanitation; 354 million practised open defecation.
  • Safely managed sanitation: WHO African Region 27%, Europe 80%.
  • 23.2% of children under five were stunted in 2024.
  • Without sanitation, faeces reach water; diarrhoea and undernutrition then feed each other and kill infants. Skilled care in childbirth cuts maternal deaths.

Mission 2

The epidemiological transition (Omran, 1971)

  • Omran's three stages: "The Age of Pestilence and Famine"; "The Age of Receding Pandemics"; "The Age of Degenerative and Man-Made Diseases".
  • Over time "pandemics of infection are gradually displaced by degenerative and man-made diseases".
  • It runs alongside the demographic transition: death rates fall first, then people live longer.
  • Driver: rising living standards (more food, clean water, sanitation) cut susceptibility to infection, so more people survive childhood.
Mission 2

The evidence

  • NCDs caused 58% of deaths worldwide in 2000 and 74% in 2023.
  • Eight of the ten leading causes of death in 2023 were NCDs.
  • In 2023, for the first time, communicable diseases caused less than half of all deaths in low-income countries.
  • Life expectancy 73.3 years, but healthy life expectancy (HALE) 62.8 (2023): many extra years are lived in poor health.
Quick check

Mission 2

Developed world: deprivation, lifestyle and care

  • England, 2018–2020: males in the most deprived areas lived 9.7 years fewer than those in the least deprived; females 7.9 years fewer.
  • Healthy life expectancy gaps were wider: 18.6 years (males) and 19.3 years (females).
  • Causes: lifestyles (smoking, diet, activity), deprivation (income, housing, work), and the availability, cost and effectiveness of medical care.
  • Exam move: use "largely a function of" carefully: show how deprivation shapes lifestyle and access to care.
Mission 2

Ethnicity and income: Indigenous Australians

  • Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander life expectancy at birth, 2020–2022: 71.9 years (males), 75.6 (females).
  • Gap with non-Indigenous Australians: 8.8 years (males), 8.1 years (females).
  • In Remote and Very Remote Areas the gap is 12.4 years for both sexes; in Major Cities 8.5 (males) and 7.5 (females).
  • Income, remoteness from services and lifestyle risks overlap; the data show where gaps are largest, not one single cause.
Quick check
?Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander females had a life expectancy of 76.5 years in Major Cities and 71.3 years in Remote and Very Remote Areas (2020–2022). How much lower is it in remote areas?
Mission 2

Limits of the model

  • Omran's classical model is based on Western Europe (England and Wales, Sweden); he added an accelerated model (Japan) and a delayed model (Chile, Ceylon).
  • Many poorer countries now face infections and NCDs together: a double burden of disease.
  • Olshansky and Ault (1986) added a fourth stage: "the age of delayed degenerative diseases".
  • New and re-emerging infections (HIV, COVID-19) break the one-way pattern.
  • Within countries, deprived and Indigenous groups lag behind the national transition.
Mission check

?Over 40 years in one middle-income country, child deaths from diarrhoea fell sharply, life expectancy rose, and the share of deaths from heart disease, diabetes and dementia doubled. Good news or bad news?

Both. The world's rise from 58% to 74% is Omran's epidemiological transition: infections recede as living standards rise, people live longer, and NCDs take over, while the poorest countries and groups carry both burdens at once.

Mission 3

Agencies built after 1945

  • IMF: established in 1944 by 44 countries to promote financial stability; 191 members today.
  • World Bank: development finance; its IDA arm gives "grants and concessional loans" to low-income countries.
  • United Nations: its Charter dates from June 1945; its agencies work on peace, development and rights.
  • Their aim: growth and stability. The question: for whom?
Mission 3

Who decides at the IMF?

  • The IMF lends to countries that cannot pay for imports or debts, with conditions.
  • Each member's quota sets its voting power.
  • The biggest decisions, such as changing quotas, need 85% of votes.
  • USA 16.49% of votes; China 6.08%. The USA alone can block a quota change.
Mission 3

Sri Lanka's crisis

  • The economy shrank by 7.3% in 2022 and 2.0% in 2023.
  • The IMF describes "the external default in 2022".
  • Inflation peaked at 70% in September 2022.
  • Cause, in the IMF's words: "Public debt increased drastically in 2020-22 due to exchange rate depreciation, large fiscal deficits, SOE borrowing" (SOE: state-owned enterprise).
  • March 2023: a 48-month IMF loan of SDR 2.286 billion (about US$3 billion).
Mission 3

Conditions and results

  • Conditions included higher taxes and cost-recovery energy pricing (charging what energy really costs).
  • Debts were restructured in talks with governments, Chinese state banks and private bondholders.
  • Results: tax revenue rose from 7.3% to 9.8% of GDP in 2023; inflation fell "from 70 percent to around 4 percent".
  • Reserves reached US$5.5 billion by end-April 2024; the economy grew by 5.0% in 2024.

Mission 3

Stability for whom?

  • Growth and stability: inflation fell, reserves were rebuilt and growth returned within two years.
  • Inequality: higher taxes and energy prices raise household costs. The IMF itself called for social safety net reforms "to protect the poor and the vulnerable".
  • Injustice: rules are set by votes that follow quotas, so the largest shareholders weigh most.
Quick check
?Which evidence best supports the view that global agencies can exacerbate injustice?
Mission 3

Governments choose social spending

  • Economic growth does not automatically bring social progress: governments decide how much to spend on health, education and welfare.
  • Welfare states: public social spending was just over 30% of GDP in Austria, Finland and France in 2024.
  • In Chile, Colombia, Costa Rica, Ireland, Mexico and Türkiye it was 15% of GDP or less.
  • At the far end of the spectrum, totalitarian regimes run by elites spend little on health and education.
  • Spending also depends on revenue: Sri Lanka's tax revenue was only 7.3% of GDP in 2022.
Mission 3

The neo-liberal model, and newer programmes

  • Neo-liberal model: free trade, privatisation and deregulated financial markets, often required as loan conditions.
  • Debt relief came with a reform track record: under the HIPC Initiative (1996), countries needed IMF- and World Bank-supported reforms and a poverty reduction strategy.
  • Newer aims: in 2005 the MDRI added 100% relief on eligible debts; Somalia became the 37th of 39 eligible countries to complete HIPC in December 2023.
  • Programmes now include social safety nets and IDA grants for health and education.
Mission 3

From MDGs to SDGs

  • MDGs: eight goals "to reduce extreme poverty by 2015".
  • SDGs: 17 goals, "adopted by all United Nations Member States in 2015".
  • The post-2015 agenda widens the focus: from poverty in developing countries to sustainable development everywhere, including climate, oceans and inequality.
Mission 3

Mixed progress

  • UN, 2025: 139 of the 169 targets could be assessed.
  • "only 35 per cent show adequate progress – 18 per cent are on track and 17 per cent are making moderate progress".
  • 31% show only marginal gains, 17% no progress, and "18 per cent of targets have regressed below 2015 baseline levels".
  • An estimated 808 million people will live in extreme poverty in 2025: about 1 in 10 people.
Quick check
?In 2025, 18% of the 139 assessed SDG targets had regressed below their 2015 baseline. About how many targets is that?
Mission 3

An alternative lender: the NDB

  • The New Development Bank was agreed by Brazil, Russia, India, China and South Africa in 2014 at Fortaleza; it is based in Shanghai.
  • 10 members by 2026. The founders' combined votes cannot fall below 55%; no new member may exceed 7%.
  • It funds projects such as power and transport: 139 projects worth US$42.9 billion (website, accessed October 2026).
  • Compared with the IMF (191 members), it is small, and it is not a crisis lender for countries that cannot pay their debts.
Mission check

?A member of the NDB can no longer pay for imports and asks for an emergency loan. Which statement best describes how the terms would be set?

Rescue terms are set by the borrowing government and the IMF, whose board votes by quota, so the largest shareholders weigh most; creditors may also be asked to restructure debts. The NDB gives emerging economies more control, but it funds projects, not rescues.

Mission 4

What are human rights?

  • Human rights: rights every person holds because they are human, not because a state grants them.
  • Civil and political: life, no torture, fair trial, free speech, vote.
  • Economic, social and cultural: health, education, work.
  • A right is a claim against power: usually the state must respect, protect and fulfil it.
Mission 4

The UDHR, 1948

  • Adopted on 10 December 1948 as General Assembly Resolution 217(III): 48 in favour, 8 abstaining.
  • Described in the drafting debate as "a common standard of achievement for all peoples and all nations".
  • The General Assembly "may make recommendations" (UN Charter, Art. 10): a resolution is not a treaty.
  • Its rights were "later enshrined in a legally binding international agreement" (Amnesty International, on torture).
  • So the UDHR is a statement of intent, and it was never "signed": states voted, and 8 did not vote for it.
Quick check
?Why is the UDHR described as a statement of intent rather than international law?
Mission 4

A framework for foreign policy

  • The EU's treaty says its action on the international scene is guided by "the universality and indivisibility of human rights" and "respect for the principles of the United Nations Charter" (TEU Art. 21).
  • So the UDHR's standards shape aid, trade and diplomacy, not only law.
Mission 4

The ECHR and the Human Rights Act 1998

  • ECHR: signed in Rome on 4 November 1950 by Council of Europe members, "Considering the Universal Declaration".
  • Example right: "No one shall be subjected to torture" (Art. 3).
  • States "undertake to abide by the final judgment of the Court" in Strasbourg (Art. 46).
  • Human Rights Act 1998: gives "further effect" to Convention rights in UK law; in force 2 October 2000.
  • Public authorities must not act incompatibly with a Convention right (s. 6); UK courts must "take into account" Strasbourg judgments (s. 2).
Mission 4

The sovereignty controversy

  • Under s. 4, a court may issue a declaration of incompatibility, but it "does not affect the validity" of the Act. Parliament keeps the last word.
  • The Safety of Rwanda (Asylum and Immigration) Act 2024 stated: "the Parliament of the United Kingdom is sovereign" and "the validity of an Act is unaffected by international law".
  • Its s. 3 disapplied HRA ss. 2, 3 and 6–9 for that Act. The whole Act was repealed on 2 December 2025.
  • The debate: one position holds that elected Parliament should decide; the other that rights must bind governments, including through a European court.

Mission 4

Geneva Conventions and war crimes

  • The 1949 Geneva Conventions protect "people who do not take part in the fighting (civilians, medics, aid workers)".
  • "Those who commit grave breaches must be pursued and tried or extradited, whatever their nationality".
  • Torture is a war crime under all four Conventions, "ratified by every single state in the world".
  • ICC: tries genocide, war crimes, crimes against humanity and aggression; 125 states parties.
  • Few trials: 34 cases and 68 arrest warrants, but only 15 convictions and 4 acquittals; 34 people remain at large.
  • Widespread torture: Amnesty received reports of torture or ill-treatment by state officials in 141 countries (Jan 2009–May 2013).
Quick check
?Which pair of facts best shows the gap between law and practice?
Mission 4

Norms

  • A norm is a shared expectation of how states should behave. It can exist before, or without, a binding law.
  • Responsibility to Protect (R2P): "unanimously adopted in 2005 at the UN World Summit".
  • Para. 138: "Each individual State has the responsibility to protect its populations from genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing and crimes against humanity".
  • Norms change the question from "may outsiders act?" to "has this state failed its people?"
Mission check

?In 2007 the General Assembly adopted the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples; four states voted against: Australia, Canada, New Zealand and the United States. What did that vote do?

The UDHR bound no one in 1948. It works as a norm and a foreign-policy framework; binding force comes from treaties such as the ECHR and the Geneva Conventions, and even those depend on enforcement.

Political controls: shutting the internet down

  • The #KeepItOn coalition recorded at least 313 internet shutdowns in 52 countries in 2025, up from 304 in 2024.
  • Myanmar had at least 95, the most of any country.
  • "For the third year, conflict was the lead trigger": 125 conflict-related shutdowns in 14 countries.

Mission 5

Example 1: Nigeria suspends Twitter, 2021

  • On 4 June 2021 Nigeria suspended Twitter, days after Twitter deleted a post from President Buhari's account.
  • The government argued that freedom of expression "is not absolute", and "threatened to prosecute Nigerians accessing Twitter through circumvention tools".
  • Access returned in January 2022; the authorities said Twitter had agreed to conditions, which the company "has not acknowledged".
Mission 5

Nigeria: the court

  • SERAP, a Nigerian NGO, and others took Nigeria to the ECOWAS Court of Justice, the regional court of the Economic Community of West African States.
  • On 14 July 2022 the court declared the suspension "unlawful and inconsistent with the provisions of Article 9 of the ACHPR and Article 19 of the ICCPR".
  • It ordered Nigeria to lift the suspension and "guarantee a non-repetition".
Mission 5

Example 2: Turkey blocks Wikipedia, 2017–2020

  • On 29 April 2017 Turkey's internet regulator blocked all language versions of Wikipedia.
  • The stated grounds were "maintaining national security and public order".
  • On 26 December 2019 Turkey's Constitutional Court found a violation of freedom of expression; on 15 January 2020 an Ankara court ruled to lift the ban.

Mission 5

Different views of human rights

  • States differ in which rights they put first, and they say so.
  • China's government: "The rights to subsistence and development are the primary, basic human rights" (white paper, December 2016).
  • Freedom House describes South Africa as "a constitutional democracy" that, since the end of apartheid in 1994, "has been regarded globally as a proponent of human rights".
  • Exam move: attribute each position to the state or organisation that holds it.
Mission 5

An authoritarian and a democratic system compared

  • Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2025 (scores out of 100):
    • South Africa: 81, "Free"; political rights 34/40, civil liberties 47/60.
    • China: 9, "Not Free"; political rights −2/40, civil liberties 11/60.
  • Freedom House: the Chinese Communist Party "maintains tight control over all aspects of life and governance, including … the media, online speech".
  • South Africa moved to democracy in 1994: an emerging power that made a democratic transition.
Mission 5

Measuring corruption

  • Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index (CPI) scores 182 countries and territories on perceived public-sector corruption, from 0 (highly corrupt) to 100 (very clean).
  • 2025: global average 42; "more than two-thirds of countries score below 50".
  • Denmark 89 (1st); China 43 (76th); South Africa 41 (81st).
  • Corruption threatens rights when officials, police or courts can be bought: the rule of law is subverted.
Quick check

Mission check

?In a fictional country a citizens' group wins a court ruling that the government's block on a social-media platform is unlawful. Based on the Nigeria and Turkey cases, what does the ruling achieve?

Sometimes, and slowly, mainly through courts. In Nigeria, SERAP won a ruling that the 2021 suspension was unlawful, although access had already returned in January 2022. In Turkey, the Constitutional Court found the Wikipedia block violated freedom of expression; access returned in January 2020, more than two and a half years after the block began.

Edexcel A-level Geography (9GE0)

Mission 7

A spectrum of intervention

  • Geopolitical intervention: action by outside actors to change what happens inside another state.
  • From least to most forceful: development aid → sanctions and embargoes → military aid (arms, training) → indirect military action (supporting one side) → direct military action.
  • National sovereignty: a state's right to run its own affairs. The UN Charter rests on "the sovereign equality of all its Members".
  • Its limit: non-intervention "shall not prejudice the application of enforcement measures under Chapter VII".
Mission 7

Aid and embargoes

  • Development aid: OECD donors gave $174.3 billion in 2025; aid "fell in 2025 by 23.1% in real terms".
  • Embargoes: bans on trade in some or all goods. UN sanctions on North Korea, begun under resolution 1718 (2006) and widened in 2016–2017, include an arms embargo, a "luxury goods" ban and bans on its exports of coal, iron, textiles and seafood.
  • Embargoes aim to change behaviour without force, but can also hurt ordinary people.
Quick check
?A government sends weapons and military trainers to an allied state fighting rebels, but sends no troops. What kind of intervention is this?
Mission 7

Who promotes intervention?

  • IGOs: the UN Security Council can authorise sanctions or force under Chapter VII.
  • Governments: act alone or in alliances, using aid, trade and arms.
  • NGOs: Amnesty International, "a global movement of more than 10 million people", says it is "independent of any political ideology, economic interest or religion".
  • Human Rights Watch works "in close to 100 countries", refuses government funding, and uses "satellite imagery to track the destruction of villages and city blocks".
  • NGOs document violations and lobby; they do not use force.
Mission 7

Libya 2011: intervention without consensus

  • 2005: UN members endorsed the responsibility to protect populations "from genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing and crimes against humanity".
  • 17 March 2011: Security Council Resolution 1973 authorised states "to take all necessary measures" to protect civilians in Libya, and set a no-fly zone.
  • Vote: 10 for, 0 against, 5 abstentions (China, Russia, Brazil, Germany, India); it excluded "an occupation force".
  • NATO's Operation Unified Protector enforced it until 31 October 2011.
Quick check
?What does the Resolution 1973 vote show about intervention?
Mission 7

Conditions on aid, trade and arms

  • Military aid conditions: the US Leahy Law bars funds "for assistance to units of foreign security forces" where there is "credible information" of "gross violations of human rights".
  • It names "torture, extrajudicial killing, enforced disappearance, and rape under color of law".
  • Trade conditions: the EU's Everything But Arms scheme, adopted in 2001 for the 49 least developed countries, gives duty-free, quota-free access.
  • In 2020 the EU partly withdrew Cambodia's access "due to serious and systematic concerns related to human rights"; about 20% of its exports to the EU, such as garments and footwear, lost duty-free access.
  • Sovereignty debate: supporters see conditions as defending universal rights; critics see outside states shaping domestic policy.
Quick check
?Why can the EU's partial withdrawal of Cambodia's trade preferences be said to challenge national sovereignty?
Mission check

?A government's forces attack civilians. NGOs publish evidence, but a Security Council resolution authorising force fails. Which response can still go ahead without Council authorisation?

Intervention is a spectrum, from aid and conditions to force, promoted by IGOs, governments and NGOs. Consensus is rare, and every step tests the balance between human rights and national sovereignty.

Mission 8: After its 2010 earthquake, Haiti's aid nearly tripled in a year. Why is it still the region's poorest country?

Make a guess: After its 2010 earthquake, Haiti's aid nearly tripled in a year. Why is it still the region's poorest country?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel A-level Geography (9GE0)

  • 8A.8a forms of development aid: charitable, NGO (Oxfam, Christian Aid), government, IGO loans (Haiti)
  • 8A.8b contested impact of aid: successes (malaria, gender equality) vs dependency, corruption, elites
  • 8A.11a aid has mixed success: Botswana or Ebola in West Africa vs Haiti, Iraq
  • 8A.11b inequality in aid recipients may rise or fall, affecting health and life expectancy
  • 8A.11c superpowers use aid as foreign policy, judging success by resources, IGO votes, alliances
Mission 8

Who gives aid?

  • Charitable / NGO aid: from non-governmental organisations funded by donations (and often by governments).
    • Oxfam: a confederation of "over 20 member organizations", working "in more than 70 countries".
    • Christian Aid: works with "our most marginalised global neighbours, of all faiths and none"; aims "to eradicate extreme poverty by tackling its root causes".
  • Government (bilateral) aid: one state to another.
  • IGO aid (multilateral): from bodies such as the World Bank and IMF — as grants or loans.
Mission 8

Haiti: emergency aid and IGO loans

  • 12 January 2010: M7.0 earthquake, 10 km SE of Léogâne.
  • Net aid received: $0.99bn (2009) → $2.78bn (2010) → $1.51bn (2011).
  • Haiti's debt to the World Bank's IDA: $507m (2008) → $38.5m (2009) → $0 (2010), under the World Bank–IMF debt-relief scheme for Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC).
  • Loans must be repaid: IGO lending can build debt in a poor country; cancelling it frees money for recovery.
Quick check
?Haiti's debt to the World Bank's IDA fell from $507m (2008) to $0 (2010) under HIPC debt relief. What does this show about IGO aid?
Mission 8

A success: malaria

  • WHO: since 2000, 2.3 billion malaria cases and 14 million deaths averted worldwide.
  • The Global Fund (a public–private aid fund) provides 57% of all international financing for malaria programmes.
  • It reports 196 million insecticide-treated nets distributed in 2025.
  • Aid paid for the tools: nets, drugs, tests.
Mission 8

A success: gender equality in schooling

  • Gender parity index (GPI) = girls' enrolment ÷ boys' (1 = equal).
  • Sub-Saharan Africa, primary: 0.851 (2000) → 0.958 (2020).
  • Secondary: 0.802 (2000) → 0.900 (2020).
  • Aid-funded schooling is one contributor among several (government spending, attitudes, urbanisation).
Mission 8

The critics: dependency, corruption, elites

  • Dependency: when aid is a large share of income, governments may rely on donors rather than taxes.
  • Elite capture: a World Bank study of the 22 most aid-dependent countries found aid disbursements "coincide with sharp increases in bank deposits in offshore financial centers".
  • Implied leakage ≈ 7.5% of aid, higher where aid is a larger share of GDP.
  • Minorities and rights: if elites control aid, the poorest and minority groups may gain least.
Quick check
?The World Bank study estimated a leakage rate of about 7.5%. What is the most accurate conclusion?
Mission 8

Relative success: Botswana and Ebola

  • Botswana: life expectancy 52.4 (2002) → 69.3 (2024).
  • Botswana is among the countries that reached the UNAIDS 95-95-95 targets.
  • But aid was small: net aid was under 0.8% of GNI in most years 2000–16, so the gains cannot be credited mainly to aid.
  • Ebola, West Africa 2014–16: more cases and deaths than all earlier outbreaks combined; it reached 7 further countries.
  • More than 28,600 infected and 11,325 died; declared over in June 2016.
  • Aid helped end it: a WHO-led vaccine trial in Guinea, part-funded by the UK's aid department (DFID), found no cases among 5,837 vaccinated people from 10 days after vaccination.
Mission 8

Relative failure: Haiti (and Iraq)

  • World Bank (2026): Haiti "remains the poorest country in Latin America and the Caribbean".
  • Real GDP has fallen for an eighth consecutive year (2026).
  • Over 5.83 million people faced high acute food insecurity (March–June 2026).
  • Barriers named by the World Bank: "political instability, increased violence, and unprecedented levels of insecurity".
  • Iraq: net aid peaked at $22.1bn in 2005, during conflict. Large volumes do not guarantee results where security is poor.
Mission 8

Aid and inequality

  • Gini index (0 = equal, 100 = one person has everything).
  • Botswana: Gini 61.5 (2002) → 54.9 (2015): inequality fell as life expectancy rose.
  • Zambia: Gini 52.0 (2010) → 55.8 (2015): inequality rose while aid was only about 4% of GNI.
  • Unequal gains can leave the poorest with worse health even as averages improve.
  • Gini values come from household surveys in different years, so treat single-country changes with caution.
Mission 8

Aid as foreign policy

  • Superpowers may judge aid by national interest: access to resources, votes in IGOs (e.g. the UN), and alliances.
  • AidData: in the Belt and Road era, China spent about $85bn a year on overseas development finance, compared with $37bn by the USA.
  • Much of China's finance is loans for infrastructure; AidData links it to rising "hidden debt".
  • US official aid fell from $65.5bn (2024) to $29.0bn (2025): aid budgets shift with government priorities.

?Which would be evidence that a donor judges aid success by foreign-policy criteria rather than development outcomes?
Mission check

?A minister says: "Aid works if enough money is sent." Which response is best supported by the evidence in this mission?

Aid's record is mixed. Targeted aid can save lives (malaria, Ebola, HIV), but outcomes depend on governance, security, inequality and donors' motives, not just the amount.

Mission 9: About half of the 800-mile Trans-Alaska Pipeline runs above the ground. Why not bury all of it?

Make a guess: About half of the 800-mile Trans-Alaska Pipeline runs above the ground. Why not bury all of it?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel A-level Geography (9GE0)

  • 8A.8c economic development by superpowers and TNCs harming minority groups' land and culture
Mission 9

Case study: Alaska's North Slope

  • Oil was discovered at Prudhoe Bay, on Alaska's Arctic coast, in 1968.
  • The Trans-Alaska Pipeline System (TAPS) runs about 1,290 km (800 miles) from Deadhorse to the port of Valdez. It was completed in 1977 and has carried more than 18 billion barrels.
  • It is raised on supports for 420 miles and buried for 380.
  • Owners include the TNCs ConocoPhillips and ExxonMobil.
Mission 9

Warm oil, frozen ground

  • About 85% of Alaska has some form of permafrost; in places it is up to 80% ice.
  • Oil was expected to flow at about 70–80 °C (158–176 °F); buried, it "would thaw a cylindrical area 20 to 30 feet in diameter within a decade".
  • Thawed ice-rich ground loses volume, so the pipe could sink and break.
  • So the pipe is raised where permafrost is unstable, and buried in thawed soils, bedrock or thaw-stable ground.
Quick check
?Why is TAPS buried along some stretches but raised along others?
Mission 9

Niger Delta: oil and Ogoniland

  • UNEP assessed pollution from "more than 50 years of oil operations" in Ogoniland, Nigeria, home to some 1 million people.
  • It examined more than 200 locations and 122 km of pipeline routes.
  • In Nisisioken Ogale, families drink water with benzene, a known carcinogen, over 900 times the WHO guideline.
  • Mangroves, "nurseries for fish", were badly damaged; clean-up could take 25–30 years, with $1 billion for the first five years.
Mission 9

Land, culture and rights

  • Pollution reaches the soil, creeks, mangroves and drinking water around Ogoni communities; UNEP found they are "exposed to hydrocarbons every day".
  • The study was funded by Shell's Nigerian subsidiary, under the polluter-pays principle.
  • Alaska shows a different route: the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act (1971) settled Native land claims before the pipeline was built.
  • Exam move (Edexcel): name the actor (TNC, state), the minority group, the right affected (land, water, culture) and the evidence.
Quick check
?What makes the Ogoniland case evidence of harm to a minority group's land and culture, rather than only an oil spill?
Mission check

?Engineers plan a pipeline to carry warm oil across ice-rich permafrost in Siberia. They propose raising long stretches on supports. Why?

Buried warm oil would thaw ice-rich permafrost; the ground would collapse into thermokarst ("irregular surfaces of marshy bowls") and take the pipe with it. So pipelines are raised over unstable permafrost and buried only where thaw does no harm.

Mission 10: States promise never to use force against each other. So why do they still intervene, and at what cost?

Make a guess: States promise never to use force against each other. So why do they still intervene, and at what cost?

Edexcel A-level Geography (9GE0)

Mission 10

The rule and its exceptions

  • UN Charter, Art. 2(4): states must not use force "against the territorial integrity or political independence of any state".
  • Exception 1: the Security Council may authorise force under Chapter VII.
  • Exception 2: "the inherent right of individual or collective self-defence if an armed attack occurs" (Art. 51).
  • A newer norm: in 2005 UN members endorsed the responsibility to protect people "from genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing and crimes against humanity".
Mission 10

Strategic interests and human-rights justifications

  • US National Security Strategy, 2002: "America must stand firmly for the nonnegotiable demands of human dignity".
  • After 11 September 2001 and Afghanistan, the US turned to "regime change in Iraq as part of the second phase of what it called the Global War on Terror".
  • The Iraq Inquiry (UK, 2016): the UK chose to "invade and occupy a sovereign nation, without the support of a Security Council resolution explicitly authorising the use of force".
  • It was later found that Iraq's weapons programmes "had been dismantled". Motives mixed security, alliance and rights claims.
Quick check
?The Iraq Inquiry found that "diplomatic options had not at that stage been exhausted. Military action was therefore not a last resort". What does this most directly challenge?
Mission 10

Military aid to states with poor rights records

  • Military aid: arms sales, training and equipment for another state's forces.
  • Saudi Arabia was the world's 3rd largest importer of major arms in 2021–25 (6.8% of the world total); 77% came from the USA.
  • Saudi Arabia was the largest recipient of US arms exports (12%).
  • Freedom House rates Saudi Arabia Not Free (9/100): its monarchy "restricts almost all political rights and civil liberties".
  • US law bars aid to foreign security units with credible evidence of "gross violations of human rights".
Quick check
?Why might a government sell arms to a state it rates poorly on human rights?
Mission 10

The war on terror and torture

  • The war on terror was framed as defending freedom and human dignity.
  • The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948): "No one shall be subjected to torture".
  • US Senate Intelligence Committee (2014): CIA interrogations "were brutal and far worse than the CIA represented".
  • Its finding #1: the techniques were "not an effective means of acquiring intelligence".
  • The report was approved by a 9–6 vote; minority views were filed.
  • 2009: Executive Order 13491 limited US interrogations to techniques in the Army Field Manual.
  • Exam move (Edexcel "Evaluate"): weigh the rights justification against actions that violated rights.
Mission 10

Costs: short-term gains, long-term costs

  • Short term: the Coalition achieved "the removal of a brutal regime".
  • Long term: it "failed to achieve the goals it had set for a new Iraq"; "serious disorder … aggravated by sectarian differences".
  • People: refugees from Iraq rose from 0.37 million (2003) to 2.31 million (2007).
  • Sovereignty: Iraq was occupied; rights: detainees were abused in the war on terror.
Quick check
?Refugees from Iraq: 368,577 in 2003 and 2,309,246 in 2007. How many times larger was the 2007 figure?
Mission check

?A powerful state arms an ally with a poor rights record. Years later, it invades a neighbouring state without Security Council approval, citing human rights. Which evaluation is best supported?

Military interventions are usually justified by rights but driven partly by strategic interests; they operate inside a system of laws and norms that protects states; and short-term gains often bring long-term costs to sovereignty, rights and people.

Mission 11

What counts as success?

  • An intervention can be judged by many indicators:
  • Health (child mortality), life expectancy, education (enrolment);
  • Gender equality (women in decision-making), free speech (media freedom);
  • Refugees (numbers fleeing or hosted), GDP per head.
  • Different actors pick different indicators, so they reach different verdicts.
Mission 11

Two views of success

  • Rights and democracy first: the EU's external action is guided by "democracy, the rule of law, the universality and indivisibility of human rights" (TEU Art. 21).
  • Development first: China's 1991 white paper says "priority should be given to the safeguarding of the right of the people of the developing countries to subsistence and development".
  • It also opposes "interfering in other countries' internal affairs on the pretext of human rights".
  • So the same outcome can be called success or failure.

Rwanda: the LIDC case

  • Rwanda is a low-income country (World Bank): an LIDC in OCR's terms.
  • 1994, the year of the genocide: life expectancy at birth 12.2 years; under-5 mortality 389 per 1,000.

Rwanda: strategy through law

Rwanda: inequality and injustice

  • Freedom House (an NGO) rates Rwanda Not Free: 21/100 (political rights 7/40; civil liberties 14/60).
  • It reports self-censorship by journalists, and that 2018 penal-code changes criminalised cartoons and writings that "humiliate" leaders.
  • It finds women legislators have "little practical ability to engage in politics outside the RPF".

  • Opportunity and injustice sit side by side.
Mission 11

Non-military interventions: UN Women and UNICEF

  • UN Women: created by the General Assembly in July 2010 to drive the UN's work on gender equality.
  • UNICEF is a partner in the Measles & Rubella Partnership with WHO and others.
  • Measles vaccination "averted nearly 59 million deaths between 2000 and 2024".
  • Yet 95,000 died of measles in 2024, and about 29 million infants were under-protected in 2025.
  • Non-military aid can save lives at scale, but attributing results to one agency is hard.

The cost of not acting

  • Rwanda 1994: when protection failed, life expectancy fell to 12.2 years in the genocide year.
  • Syria: since 2011 Russia cast 14 Security Council vetoes on Syria; China 8.
  • By the end of 2025, 4.87 million Syrians were refugees abroad.
  • Inaction exports consequences: refugee flows, regional instability, and lost development.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • GDP growth vs wellbeing: the HPI (life expectancy × wellbeing ÷ footprint) ranks Costa Rica 1st and the USA 105th of 134 (2025); Bolivia's suma qamaña and Sharia-based finance contest western growth models.
  • Rosling: health, environment, life expectancy and rights are the goals, growth the means; education builds human capital (≈9% higher earnings per extra year) and rights, but access is unequal (64 million out of primary school, 53% girls).

Mission 2

  • Health is measured by mortality (IMR, MMR) and morbidity; HALE combines them.
  • As living standards rise, infections recede and NCDs dominate: the epidemiological transition.
  • Within countries, deprivation and ethnicity (Indigenous Australians) still mean gaps of 8–19 years.

Mission 3

  • Post-1945 agencies such as the IMF can restore growth and stability (Sri Lanka 2023–24), but conditions raise costs for households and votes follow quotas.
  • Social progress depends on government spending choices; IGOs moved from a neo-liberal model towards poverty, health and rights goals; SDG progress is mixed (35% of assessed targets adequate in 2025).

Mission 4

  • Human rights belong to people as humans; the UDHR (1948, 48 for, 8 abstaining) set a standard but is a statement of intent, not law.
  • The ECHR and HRA 1998 make rights binding in the UK, but Parliament stays sovereign (s. 4; the 2024 Rwanda Act); Geneva law is universal, yet trials are few and torture widespread.

Mission 5

  • Citizens can win back access through courts and campaigns, but slowly. States define and protect rights differently; Freedom House scores and the CPI measure freedom and perceived corruption, and they do not always agree.

Mission 6

  • Rights gaps by gender and ethnicity show up in health and education: First Nations in Canada; Australia and Bolivia moving forward at different rates; Afghanistan reversing.

Mission 7

  • Geopolitical intervention ranges from development aid and embargoes to military aid and direct military action; IGOs, governments and NGOs (Amnesty, HRW) promote it, but rarely agree.
  • Western governments attach human rights conditions to military aid (Leahy Law) and trade (EU EBA, Cambodia 2020), which challenges national sovereignty.

Mission 8

  • Forms: NGO/charitable (Oxfam, Christian Aid), bilateral government aid, IGO grants and loans (Haiti's IDA debt cancelled in 2010).
  • Contested: successes (malaria, girls' schooling) vs dependency and elite capture; Botswana and the Ebola response vs Haiti and Iraq; inequality can fall (Botswana) or rise (Zambia, 2010–15); superpowers may judge aid by resources, votes and alliances.

Mission 9

  • TAPS is raised for 420 of its 800 miles because warm oil would thaw ice-rich permafrost.
  • In Ogoniland, decades of oil pollution harmed water, mangroves and livelihoods (UNEP, 2011).

Mission 10

  • The UN Charter bans force against states except under Chapter VII or in self-defence; the responsibility to protect adds a contested norm.
  • Arms to Saudi Arabia, the war on terror's use of torture, and Iraq 2003 show rights justifications mixed with strategic interest, and short-term gains followed by long-term costs.

Mission 11

  • Success can be measured by health, life expectancy, education, gender equality, free speech, refugees or GDP. The EU puts democracy and rights first, while China's stated position puts subsistence and development first.
  • Rwanda (LIDC): life expectancy 12.2 (1994) → 68.0 (2024), women 63.8% of deputies under a 30% quota, yet "Not Free" (21/100). Non-military aid (measles: ~59 million deaths averted) can achieve much; inaction (Syria) has global costs.
