Health, Human Rights and Intervention

EQ1 · what is development?

Development is more than income

The starting move of this topic is to break "development" apart. Edexcel wants three dimensions:

  • Economic — GDP and GNI per capita, ideally adjusted for purchasing power parity (PPP) so that a unit of currency buys a comparable basket of goods in every country. Without PPP, cross-country income comparisons are distorted by exchange rates and price levels.
  • Social — education (years of schooling, literacy), health (life expectancy, child mortality), access to clean water and sanitation.
  • Political — freedom of expression and assembly, the right to vote, the rule of law, freedom from arbitrary detention. This is the dimension income statistics are blindest to.

The central critique: GDP per capita is a mean. It says nothing about distribution, nothing about whether the income was earned sustainably, and nothing about whether people are free. A resource-rich state can have a high mean income and dismal health, education and political freedom — the pattern often called the resource curse. Naming that gap between economic and social/political development is the beginning of every good answer here.

EQ1 · composite indices

Composite indices — and what each one hides

Because no single number captures development, statisticians build composite indices. Each is a choice about what matters, and each is therefore arguable.

  • HDI (Human Development Index) — combines health (life expectancy at birth), education (mean and expected years of schooling) and income (GNI per capita, PPP) into a single figure between 0 and 1. Strength: it forced income out of the driving seat. Limits: it is still a national average that ignores inequality, and it says nothing about political freedom, human rights or the environment.
  • Gender Inequality Index — captures loss of achievement from inequality between women and men across reproductive health, empowerment and the labour market. Strength: reveals disparities the HDI averages away. Limit: data on informal work and unpaid care are weak, so it can understate the gap.
  • Corruption Perceptions Index — ranks countries by perceived public-sector corruption. Strength: it gets at governance, which almost nothing else measures. Limit: the clue is in the name — it measures perception, drawn largely from business and expert surveys, so it can reflect reputation as much as reality.
  • Happy Planet Index — combines wellbeing and life expectancy with ecological footprint, so it rewards delivering good lives at low environmental cost. Strength: the only one of the four that treats sustainability as part of development. Limit: wellbeing is self-reported and culturally variable, and a very poor country can score well simply by consuming little.

Examiner's eye: whenever you are handed an index in an exam, say what it includes, what it excludes, and at what scale it is measured. Every index is an argument in disguise.

EQ1 · variations in health

Why health varies — within countries, not just between them

Health outcomes vary enormously between countries. What earns the marks is showing that they vary just as sharply within them — between regions, between urban and rural areas, and between neighbourhoods a few kilometres apart. The causes are structured, not random:

  • Environmental determinants — air quality, water quality and sanitation, exposure to vectors and to hazards, housing quality, workplace risk.
  • Social determinants — income, education (especially maternal education, which is strongly associated with child health), housing, and physical and financial access to healthcare.
  • The distance-decay of healthcare — even where a service exists, distance, cost and time ration access. Rural and peripheral populations pay the highest access cost.
  • Epidemiological shift — as countries develop, the burden of disease tends to shift from infectious disease and maternal/child mortality towards non-communicable disease. Many countries now face both at once — the "double burden".

EQ2 · human rights

The architecture of human rights

Rights are claims that are supposed to hold simply because you are human. The legal scaffolding is worth knowing precisely, because vague answers here lose easy marks.

  • The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR), 1948 — adopted by the UN General Assembly in the aftermath of the Second World War. It is a declaration, not a treaty: it sets out rights that are universal and inalienable, but it is not in itself directly enforceable in national courts. Its power is normative — it created the standard against which states are now judged.
  • The European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR) — a regional treaty which, unlike the UDHR, created a court. This is the crucial difference: a right with a court behind it is enforceable; a right without one depends on persuasion.
  • The Geneva Conventions — the laws of armed conflict: protection of civilians, the wounded, and prisoners of war. They do not stop war; they regulate it.
  • Civil and political rights (free speech, fair trial, the vote, freedom from torture) versus social and economic rights (health, education, work, an adequate standard of living). The first group mostly requires a state to refrain; the second requires it to provide — which is why the second is far more contested, and far more dependent on a country's resources.

The universality debate: some governments argue that rights are culturally relative and that the UDHR encodes a particular Western liberal tradition. Others reply that this argument is most often made by exactly the states that would like to violate them. Both positions belong in a serious answer — the mark is for handling the tension, not for picking a side quickly.

EQ2 · who protects rights

Who actually enforces a right?

A right is only as strong as the body willing to defend it. Edexcel wants the players — and an honest assessment of what each can and cannot do.

  • National law and national courts — in practice the primary protector, and by far the most effective where the state is willing. The whole problem is that the state is often the violator.
  • IGOs — the UN and its human-rights machinery can monitor, report and condemn. Their weakness is structural: enforcement requires the Security Council, where the veto can block action.
  • The International Criminal Court (ICC) — prosecutes individuals for genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes and aggression. It is a court of last resort, acting only where a state is unwilling or unable to prosecute. Its limits are real: it depends on state cooperation to arrest suspects, several major powers are not parties to it, and it is criticised both for being too slow and for its geographical pattern of cases.
  • NGOs and civil society — monitor, document, litigate, and above all publicise. Their power is informational: they raise the reputational cost of abuse. They cannot compel.
  • The media — can force an issue onto the agenda, but coverage is uneven and short-lived, and attention does not track severity.

Minorities are the test case. Rights regimes are judged not by how they treat the majority but by how they treat minorities — ethnic, religious, linguistic, indigenous, LGBT+ — because majorities can usually protect themselves through the ballot box. Where domestic norms and domestic law diverge from international standards, you get precisely the variations in rights the specification asks you to explain.

EQ3 · the spectrum of intervention

Intervention is a spectrum, not an event

Most students think "intervention" means troops. It does not. Edexcel wants the whole ladder — and the recognition that the rungs at the bottom are used vastly more often than the one at the top.

The players: the UN and its agencies; regional bodies and alliances such as NATO; individual national governments; NGOs delivering aid and documenting abuse; the media shaping which crises are seen; and civil society within the affected state — whose consent or resistance ultimately determines whether anything sticks.

EQ3 · R2P vs sovereignty

The Responsibility to Protect — and the sovereignty problem

Sovereignty is the founding principle of the international order: a state has supreme authority within its own borders, and other states may not interfere. It is what makes the UN system possible — and it is also what makes atrocity inside a state so hard to stop from outside.

The Responsibility to Protect (R2P) doctrine, endorsed at the UN in 2005, is the attempted resolution. It holds that:

  • Each state has the primary responsibility to protect its own population from genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing and crimes against humanity.
  • The international community should assist states to do so.
  • If a state is manifestly failing, the international community should respond collectively — through the UN, and with force only as a last resort.

The unresolved tension. R2P reframes sovereignty as a responsibility rather than a shield. But it does not solve the two hardest problems. First, who decides? Collective action runs through the Security Council, and therefore through the veto — so R2P is invoked where the great powers agree and ignored where they do not. Second, mixed motives. Interventions rarely have a single cause; humanitarian, strategic, resource and geopolitical motives arrive together. A powerful essay does not claim to know which motive was "really" decisive — it shows that the selectivity of intervention (why here and not there?) is itself the strongest evidence that motives are mixed.

EQ3 · motives

Why do interventions actually happen?

Four families of motive, which in reality overlap:

  • Humanitarian — to halt mass atrocity, relieve famine, respond to disaster. Often genuine, and often insufficient on its own to generate political will.
  • Strategic — to stabilise a region, protect an ally, deny a rival a foothold, control a chokepoint or a sea lane.
  • Resource — access to energy, minerals, water or agricultural land, or protection of the supply chains that carry them.
  • Geopolitical and domestic — signalling resolve to rivals, honouring alliance commitments, and (not to be ignored) responding to domestic public opinion after media coverage of a crisis.

The test of selectivity. If humanitarian need alone drove intervention, we would expect intervention to track need. It does not. Comparable crises attract wildly different responses depending on media attention, strategic value, and whether a permanent member has an interest. That mismatch is your evidence — and it can be argued without asserting any single actor's private motives.

Argue like a geographer, not a pundit: "Intervention was motivated by oil" is an assertion. "The pattern of intervention correlates more closely with strategic and resource significance than with the scale of humanitarian need, which suggests that humanitarian justification is necessary but not sufficient" is an argument — and it is markable.

EQ4 · does it work?

Measuring the success of intervention

This is the topic's culminating question, and it is genuinely hard — because "success" depends entirely on whose goals, on what timescale, and measured how.

  • Short-term outcomes — lives saved, violence halted, aid delivered, an election held. Measurable, visible, and the basis of most political claims of success.
  • Long-term outcomes — the ones the specification really cares about: changes in HDI, life expectancy, literacy and school enrolment; the Gender Inequality Index; corruption and governance measures; and whether rights are actually protected once the intervening actors leave.
  • Unintended consequences — state fragility where institutions are destroyed faster than they are rebuilt; displacement of populations, internally and across borders; aid dependency, where local production and local government are undercut by the aid economy itself; and aid without accountability, where money flows through channels no local citizen can question.
  • The measurement problem itself — data in post-conflict states are weak; the counterfactual (what would have happened without intervention) is unknowable; and the actors reporting success are frequently the actors being judged.

Exam technique · the 20-marker

Writing the evaluative essay

Edexcel's 20-markers reward a sustained line of argument with a genuine, conditional judgement. This topic is unusually well suited to it, because almost every claim in it has a serious counter-claim.

  • Define the contested term. "Development", "success", "human rights" — none is neutral. Say which meaning you are using and why, in the introduction.
  • Disaggregate. Separate economic from social from political development; short-term from long-term outcomes; the intention of an intervention from its effect. Most marks are hiding in these distinctions.
  • Use indices as evidence, then criticise them. Citing an HDI change is AO1. Explaining what the HDI cannot see is AO2.
  • Weigh the players. Who acted, what power did they hold, and whose interests were served? This is also exactly the Paper 3 "players" framework.
  • Conclude conditionally. "Intervention improves welfare where local institutions are strengthened rather than displaced, and where success is judged over decades rather than months — but the record on both conditions is poor." That is a judgement with its conditions stated.

Try it: "Evaluate the view that military intervention rarely improves human welfare in the long term." Blocks: (1) the case for — atrocity halted, lives saved, an immediate humanitarian gain that is real and should not be minimised; (2) the case against — fragility, displacement, dependency, and the collapse of institutions; (3) the decisive move — the outcome depends less on the intervention itself than on whether the rebuilding phase transfers capacity to local institutions. Judgement: the record is poor, but the failure is usually in the aftermath rather than in the act.

Where this topic connects

Recap

The big ideas to know

Development: economic (GDP/GNI per capita, PPP) · social (health, education) · political (freedom, voice) — GDP is a mean and hides distribution

Indices: HDI (health + education + income, but no rights and no inequality) · Gender Inequality Index · Corruption Perceptions Index (perception, not fact) · Happy Planet Index (adds ecological footprint)

Dimension index: (actual − min) ÷ (max − min)

Health: varies within countries as well as between; environmental and social determinants set the choices available; distance-decay of healthcare access; the double burden of disease

Rights: UDHR 1948 (a declaration — normative, not directly enforceable) · ECHR (a treaty with a court) · Geneva Conventions (laws of armed conflict); civil/political vs social/economic rights

Protection: national law and courts · IGOs (blocked by the veto) · the ICC (last resort, dependent on cooperation) · NGOs and media (informational power only)

Intervention spectrum: aid and trade → diplomacy → sanctions → military aid → direct military intervention — every rung has costs

R2P vs sovereignty: protection is the state's own primary responsibility; collective action is a last resort — but it runs through the Security Council, so it is selective

Motives: humanitarian · strategic · resource · geopolitical — selectivity is the evidence that they are mixed

Success: short-term relief vs long-term HDI, literacy, life expectancy and rights; unintended consequences — fragility, displacement, dependency, aid without accountability; and the counterfactual problem
