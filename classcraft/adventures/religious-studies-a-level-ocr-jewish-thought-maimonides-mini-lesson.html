Mission 1

Maimonides writes a list

  • Moses ben Maimon, called Maimonides or Rambam (1138–1204): rabbi, legal scholar, philosopher, physician.
  • Born in Córdoba, Spain; his family fled persecution; he settled in Fustat (Old Cairo), Egypt.
  • In his Commentary on the Mishnah (Arabic, completed c. 1168) he reached Mishnah Sanhedrin 10:1: "All Israel have a share in the world to come" (paraphrase).
  • Who counts as "Israel" here? His answer: anyone who holds thirteen foundations (Hebrew yesodot) of faith.
  • They are known as the Thirteen Principles of Faith.
  • Your spec writes G-d, a form many Jews use out of respect.
Mission 1

Thirteen principles, three groups

  • About God (1–5): God exists and causes all · is one · has no body · is eternal · alone is to be worshipped.
  • About revelation (6–9): prophecy · Moses the greatest prophet · the Torah from heaven · the Torah unchanging.
  • About God and humanity (10–13): God knows our deeds · reward and punishment · the Messiah · resurrection of the dead.
  • Principles 6–13: a later mission (see Mission 2).
Mission 1

Principles 1 and 2: God exists; God is one

  • 1. Existence: God exists, is perfect, and is the cause of everything else that exists. Everything depends on God; God depends on nothing.
  • Root: "I am the LORD thy God" (Exodus 20:2), which Maimonides counts as the first commandment: to know that God exists.
  • 2. Unity: God is one, but not one as a pair has one member, nor one as a species, nor one as a body made of parts (paraphrase).
  • God's unity is unique: nothing else is one in this way.
  • Root: "HEAR, O ISRAEL: THE LORD OUR GOD, THE LORD IS ONE." (Deuteronomy 6:4, JPS 1917)
Mission 1Read the source

Principle 3: no body

> "for ye saw no manner of form on the day that the LORD spoke unto you in Horeb out of the midst of the fire" (Deuteronomy 4:15, JPS 1917)

  • 3. Incorporeality: God has no body, no shape, and nothing that happens to bodies (moving, resting, being in a place).
  • So what about God's "hand" or "eyes"? Maimonides uses the Talmud's rule: "The Torah speaks in the language of human beings" (paraphrase).
  • These words are figurative: "hand" means power; "eyes" means knowledge.

Q2 Why does the Torah still use body words for God?

Mission 1

Principles 4 and 5: eternal; worship God alone

  • 4. Eternity: God is first: before everything, without beginning. Later versions add "and last".
  • Roots: "The eternal God is a dwelling-place" (Deuteronomy 33:27, JPS 1917); "I am the first, and I am the last" (Isaiah 44:6, JPS 1917).
  • 5. Worship God alone: prayer and praise go to God only.
  • Not to angels, stars or forces of nature, and not through them as go-betweens.
  • Root: "Thou shalt have no other gods before Me." (Exodus 20:3, JPS 1917)
  • Principle 5 is about practice: what a Jew may do in prayer, not only what a Jew thinks.
Mission 1

Speaking about God: negative attributes

  • In the Guide for the Perplexed (Arabic, c. 1190), Part I, chapters 50–60, Maimonides asks what words can truly say about God.
  • Positive attributes ("God is wise", "God is powerful") would divide God into parts, against principle 2.
  • So we can say only what God is not: "God exists" means God is not non-existent; "God is powerful" means God is not weak (paraphrase).
  • Each negation removes a wrong idea, and brings us a little closer.
  • Attributes of action: "merciful" and "gracious" (Exodus 34:6) describe what God does, not what God is.
  • The highest praise is silence: he reads Psalm 65 as "to Thee silence is praise" (Psalm 65:2 in Hebrew numbering, paraphrase).

Mission 1

Roots, development, living Judaism

  • Biblical roots: Exodus 20:2–3; Deuteronomy 4:15, 6:4, 33:27; Isaiah 44:6.
  • Maimonides' development: turns these into defined beliefs that mark who is inside Judaism; adds philosophical precision (unique unity; no body at all; negative attributes).
  • Living Judaism: the hymn Yigdal (c. 14th century) sings all thirteen at the start or end of services in many synagogues.
  • Ani Ma'amin ("I believe with perfect faith…"), printed in many prayer books after the morning service, turns each principle into a sentence of faith.
  • Principles 1–5 are the least disputed today: almost all Jewish movements affirm one God, beyond the world, to be worshipped alone.
Mission 1Diversity

Who disagreed about principles 1–5?

  • Raavad (Abraham ben David of Posquières, c. 1125–1198) glossed Maimonides' code: people "greater and better" than Maimonides had read verses literally and thought God has a body; why call them heretics? (paraphrase). Raavad himself did not hold that God has a body.
  • So the dispute was about the status of the belief: a wrong idea, or heresy?
  • Kabbalah (from the late 12th and 13th centuries) speaks of the sefirot, ten emanations of God; critics said this risks dividing God. Kabbalists answered that God (Ein Sof) stays one.
  • Progressive thinkers: Mordecai Kaplan (1881–1983) re-described God as the power in the world that makes for human fulfilment, not a supernatural person.
Mission 1Evaluate

Can a God known only by negation be worshipped?

  • Claim: Maimonides' negative way leaves Jews with a God they cannot love or pray to.
  • For: Prayer addresses God as "Thou", "our Father, our King"; denial after denial seems to leave nothing to address.
  • For: The Torah itself speaks of a God who speaks, acts and cares (God as personal).
  • Against: Maimonides keeps attributes of action, so Jews can praise what God does in the world.
  • Against: Removing false pictures protects worship from idolatry (worshipping a false picture of God); awe before what cannot be grasped is itself worship.
  • Judgement criteria: Does the view fit the Tanakh's language? Does it fit how Jews actually pray? Does it protect God's unity? Can a believer live by it?

Mission 2

Principle 6: prophecy

  • 6. Prophecy: God communicates with chosen human beings, the prophets.
  • Root: "the Lord GOD will do nothing, but He revealeth His counsel unto His servants the prophets" (Amos 3:7, JPS 1917).
  • In the Guide (II.32–48) Maimonides explains prophecy as the highest human perfection: God's influence reaches a person's intellect and then their imagination.
  • A prophet must be wise and morally perfected; even then, God may withhold prophecy.
Mission 2Read the source

Principle 7: Moses

> "if there be a prophet among you, I the LORD do make Myself known unto him in a vision, I do speak with him in a dream. My servant Moses is not so; … with him do I speak mouth to mouth" (Numbers 12:6–8, JPS 1917)

  • 7. Moses is the greatest of all prophets, before or after him.
  • Maimonides lists four differences (paraphrase): Moses received prophecy awake, not in a dream; directly, not through an angel; without terror; and whenever he chose.
  • "And there hath not arisen a prophet since in Israel like unto Moses" (Deuteronomy 34:10, JPS 1917).

Mission 2

Principles 8 and 9: the Torah from heaven, unchanging

  • 8. Torah from heaven (Torah min ha-shamayim): the whole Torah we now have was given by God to Moses.
  • Every verse is equally divine: a list of Esau's descendants (Genesis 36) as much as "Hear, O Israel" (Maimonides' own example, paraphrase).
  • The Oral Torah, the explanation of the commandments, also comes from God.
  • 9. Unchanging: the Torah will not be replaced, and nothing will be added or taken away. Root: Deuteronomy 12:32 (13:1 in Hebrew numbering).
  • Context: many Christian and Muslim teachers of his time held that a later revelation completed or replaced the Torah; principle 9 denies this.

Mission 2

Principles 10 and 11: God knows; reward and punishment

  • 10. God knows human deeds and thoughts: "He that fashioneth the hearts of them all, that considereth all their doings" (Psalm 33:15, JPS 1917).
  • 11. Reward and punishment: God rewards those who keep the commandments and punishes those who break them.
  • The greatest reward is the world to come (olam ha-ba); the greatest punishment is for the soul to be cut off (karet).
  • But Maimonides adds: serve God out of love, not for reward. He cites Antigonus: be not like servants who serve their master for a reward (Pirkei Avot 1:3, paraphrase).
  • His image: a child is given sweets to learn; an adult learns for the sake of truth (paraphrase).
Mission 2

Principles 12 and 13: the Messiah and resurrection

  • 12. The Messiah will come: "though it tarry, wait for it" (Habakkuk 2:3, JPS 1917). Do not calculate the date. (Full picture in Mission 3.)
  • 13. Resurrection of the dead (techiyat ha-metim): "many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake" (Daniel 12:2, JPS 1917).
  • Maimonides stressed a spiritual world to come for souls, so some accused him of denying bodily resurrection.
  • His reply, the Treatise on Resurrection (1191): resurrection is real, a miracle, a foundation of the Torah; the risen will later die, and the final reward is for the soul (paraphrase).
  • Living Judaism: the song "Ani ma'amin… I believe in the coming of the Messiah" (principle 12) was sung by many Jews in the Nazi camps.

Mission 2

How his contemporaries received the principles

  • Raavad (c. 1125–1198) criticised Maimonides' code for calling people heretics over beliefs (e.g. a bodily God) held by sincere, learned Jews (see Mission 1).
  • Critics such as Samuel ben Ali, head of the academy in Baghdad, and Meir Abulafia of Toledo questioned his view of resurrection.
  • After his death, the Maimonidean controversy (1230s): some rabbis in Provence banned the Guide and the first book of his code; copies were burned in Montpellier (c. 1233).
  • Others defended him fiercely; Nachmanides (1194–1270) tried to calm the dispute.
  • So the principles were admired and contested from the start.
Mission 2

Later critics: Crescas, Albo, Abarbanel

  • Hasdai Crescas (c. 1340–1411), Or Adonai (1410): you cannot be commanded to believe that God exists; it is the presupposition of every command. He also sorted beliefs into different kinds.
  • Joseph Albo (c. 1380–1444), Sefer ha-Ikkarim (1425): only three roots — God's existence, revelation, reward and punishment. The Messiah is a belief, not a root.
  • Isaac Abarbanel (1437–1508), Rosh Amanah: defended Maimonides, but concluded that every teaching of the Torah is equally binding, so none is a "principle" above the rest (paraphrase).
  • The debate: is Judaism defined by beliefs, or by a Torah to be lived?
Mission 2Diversity

The principles today

  • Orthodox: the principles mark the boundary of Orthodox belief; Yigdal and Ani Ma'amin are in Orthodox prayer books. Some Orthodox scholars note that respected authorities disputed individual principles in the past.
  • Masorti / Conservative: God, Torah and Israel affirmed; revelation includes a human element, so principle 8 is reinterpreted.
  • Reform: the Pittsburgh Platform (1885) rejected bodily resurrection and a personal Messiah (a Messianic Age instead), and saw the Torah as inspired but written by people. Reform versions of Yigdal change the resurrection line.
  • Reconstructionist: Kaplan rejected a supernatural revelation altogether.
  • Principles 1–5 unite most Jews; 8, 12 and 13 divide them most.

Mission 2Evaluate

Does Judaism need a creed?

  • Claim: Maimonides was right to define Judaism by a list of beliefs.
  • For: A clear list protects Jews from error (e.g. a bodily God) and gives them unity.
  • For: Mishnah Sanhedrin 10:1 already names beliefs (resurrection, Torah from heaven); Maimonides only made them explicit.
  • Against: Biblical and rabbinic Judaism stresses practice and covenant; emunah means trust more than assent (a case argued by Menachem Kellner, Must a Jew Believe Anything?, 1999, paraphrase).
  • Against: Crescas, Albo and Abarbanel disagreed about the list; Moses Mendelssohn (1729–1786) held that Judaism reveals laws, not dogmas.
  • Judgement criteria: Does it fit the Tanakh and the rabbis? Does it unite or divide Jews today? Does it explain why Jews keep the mitzvot?

Mission 3

Mashiach: the anointed one

  • Messiah comes from Hebrew mashiach, "anointed": a person marked with oil for a task God gives.
  • In the Tanakh, kings and priests were anointed: "Samuel took the horn of oil, and anointed him" (1 Samuel 16:13, JPS 1917), of David.
  • Even the Persian king Cyrus is called "His anointed" (Isaiah 45:1, JPS 1917) for freeing the exiles.
  • Later hope focused on one future king from David's line: the King Messiah (Melekh ha-Mashiach).
  • He is a human being, not divine; Jews pray for him, they do not pray to him.
  • Your spec writes G-d, a form many Jews use out of respect.

Swords into plowshares

> "they shall beat their swords into plowshares, and their spears into pruninghooks; nation shall not lift up sword against nation, neither shall they learn war any more." (Micah 4:3, JPS 1917; almost the same in Isaiah 2:4)

> "But they shall sit every man under his vine and under his fig-tree; and none shall make them afraid" (Micah 4:4, JPS 1917)

  • Isaiah 2:2–4 and Micah 4:1–3 share one vision: "in the end of days" all nations stream to "the mountain of the LORD'S house" in Jerusalem to learn God's ways.
  • "For out of Zion shall go forth the law" (Isaiah 2:3, JPS 1917).

Mission 3

The Messianic Age in the prophets

  • A king of David's line: "a shoot out of the stock of Jesse" (Isaiah 11:1, JPS 1917), filled with God's spirit.
  • Judge: God "shall judge between the nations" (Isaiah 2:4); the king judges the poor "with righteousness" (Isaiah 11:4).
  • Ingathering: God "will assemble the dispersed of Israel" (Isaiah 11:12, JPS 1917).
  • Temple rebuilt; Jerusalem a centre of worship for all nations.
  • Knowledge of God: "the earth shall be full of the knowledge of the LORD, as the waters cover the sea" (Isaiah 11:9, JPS 1917).
  • Herald: Elijah, before "the great and terrible day of the LORD" (Malachi 4:5, JPS 1917; 3:23 in Hebrew).

Mission 3

Maimonides on the Messiah

  • Mishneh Torah, Laws of Kings, chapters 11–12 (paraphrase):
  • Who: a king from the house of David, who studies Torah, keeps the mitzvot and leads Israel to keep them.
  • Test: if he rebuilds the Temple and gathers the exiles, he is certainly the Messiah. If he fails or is killed, he was not.
  • He need not work miracles.
  • Nature unchanged: "the world follows its normal course"; the wolf lying with the lamb (Isaiah 11:6) is a metaphor for peace among nations.
  • Purpose: not power or pleasure, but freedom to study Torah and know God.
  • Do not calculate the date. (Principle 12: wait for him.)

Mission 3

Bringing the Messiah: a purpose of life

  • AQA's three purposes: obedience to God's commandments; bringing the Messiah; repairing the world (tikkun olam).
  • The Talmud links them: asked when he will come, the Messiah answers "To-day, if ye would but hearken to His voice!" (Psalm 95:7, JPS 1917, cited in Sanhedrin 98a).
  • So keeping mitzvot is active waiting: each good deed brings redemption nearer.
  • Tikkun olam: working for justice and peace prepares the world for the Messianic Age.
  • Relative importance: many Orthodox Jews put obedience first (the Messiah comes through Torah); many Progressive Jews put tikkun olam first (people build the Messianic Age).
Mission 3Diversity

Orthodox and Progressive today

  • Orthodox: a personal Messiah of David's line; the Amidah prays daily for "the sprout of David"; principle 12 in Ani Ma'amin.
  • Chabad Hasidim: campaign to hasten the Messiah through mitzvot.
  • Religious Zionists (Rav Kook, 1865–1935): the return to the Land is "the beginning of redemption".
  • Some Haredi groups (e.g. Satmar): a human-made state forces the end; only the Messiah should restore Israel.
  • Reform: the Pittsburgh Platform (1885) spoke of "the establishment of the kingdom of truth, justice, and peace among all men": a Messianic Age, not a person. Reform prayer books replaced "redeemer" with "redemption".
  • Masorti / Conservative: the Messianic hope affirmed; whether a person or a symbol is left open.
Mission 3Evaluate

Does messianic hope answer suffering?

  • Claim: Messianic hope answers the question of suffering.
  • For: Suffering is not the last word: history ends in peace and justice (Micah 4).
  • For: It sustained Jews through exile and persecution; Ani Ma'amin was sung in the camps.
  • Against: It does not explain why the innocent suffer now (Job).
  • Against: False messiahs (Bar Kokhba, 132–135 CE; Shabbetai Tzvi, 1665–66) brought more suffering.
  • Against: For Maimonides the Messianic Age leaves nature unchanged: illness and death go on.
  • Judgement criteria: Is "answer" an explanation or a hope? Does it fit Orthodox and Progressive views alike? Does it help the person suffering now?

Mission 4

The book of Job

  • Job is in the Ketuvim (Writings), among the wisdom books.
  • Shape: a prose frame (chapters 1–2 and 42:7–17) around long poetry (3:1–42:6).
  • Prose: the heavenly court, the test, the restoration. Poetry: Job's lament, the friends' speeches, God's speeches, Job's reply.
  • The question: why do the righteous suffer? Is suffering always punishment?
  • Your study: chapters 1–4 (test, lament, first friend), 38 (God speaks) and 42 (ending).
  • Your spec writes G-d, a form many Jews use out of respect.
Mission 4Read the source

"Doth Job fear God for nought?"

> "that man was whole-hearted and upright, and one that feared God, and shunned evil." (Job 1:1, JPS 1917)

> "Then Satan answered the LORD, and said: 'Doth Job fear God for nought?'" (Job 1:9, JPS 1917)

  • Satan here is Hebrew ha-satan, "the adversary": a member of God's court who tests, not the devil of later thought.
  • His charge: Job is good only because he is rewarded. Take the reward away, and Job will curse God.
  • So the test is about disinterested faith: serving God for nothing.

Mission 4

The test: Job 1–2

  • Job loses his oxen, sheep, camels, servants and all ten children in one day.
  • His response: "the LORD gave, and the LORD hath taken away; blessed be the name of the LORD" (Job 1:21, JPS 1917).
  • God says the adversary moved Him against Job "to destroy him without cause" (Job 2:3, JPS 1917): the suffering is undeserved.
  • Then Job is struck with sores. His wife: "blaspheme God, and die" (Job 2:9, JPS 1917).
  • Job: "shall we receive good at the hand of God, and shall we not receive evil?" (Job 2:10, JPS 1917).
  • Three friends come and sit with him seven days, silent, "for they saw that his grief was very great" (Job 2:13, JPS 1917).

Mission 4

Job 3: the lament

  • After seven days, Job "opened … his mouth, and cursed his day" (Job 3:1, JPS 1917).
  • "Let the day perish wherein I was born" (Job 3:3, JPS 1917).
  • "Why died I not from the womb?" (Job 3:11, JPS 1917).
  • He curses his birth, not God: the adversary's prediction does not come true.
  • The lament gives permission to protest: Job speaks honestly to and about God.
  • The prose Job (patient) and the poetic Job (protesting) are both part of Jewish tradition.
Mission 4

Job 4: Eliphaz and suffering as punishment

  • Eliphaz the Temanite, the first friend, speaks:
  • "who ever perished, being innocent? Or where were the upright cut off?" (Job 4:7, JPS 1917).
  • "they that plow iniquity, and sow mischief, reap the same" (Job 4:8, JPS 1917).
  • Retribution: suffering is punishment for sin; the innocent do not perish.
  • And: "Shall mortal man be just before God?" (Job 4:17, JPS 1917): no human is wholly pure, so Job must have done something.
  • This view has roots in the Torah's covenant blessings and curses (Deuteronomy 11:13–17).
  • The reader knows Eliphaz is wrong about Job (Job 2:3).

God answers out of the whirlwind

> "Then the LORD answered Job out of the whirlwind, and said: Who is this that darkeneth counsel by words without knowledge?" (Job 38:1–2, JPS 1917)

> "Where wast thou when I laid the foundations of the earth? Declare, if thou hast the understanding." (Job 38:4, JPS 1917)

  • But God asks questions: about the sea, the stars ("Canst thou bind the chains of the Pleiades?", 38:31), the raven's young (38:41).
  • God never mentions the wager with the adversary, or Job's sin.

Mission 4

Job 42: reply, rebuke, restoration

  • Job: "I had heard of Thee by the hearing of the ear; but now mine eye seeth Thee" (Job 42:5, JPS 1917).
  • "Wherefore I abhor my words, and repent, seeing I am dust and ashes" (Job 42:6, JPS 1917). Translators disagree on this verse: "abhor myself", "retract", or "am comforted".
  • God to Eliphaz: "ye have not spoken of Me the thing that is right, as My servant Job hath" (Job 42:7, JPS 1917).
  • So the friends' theory is rejected; Job's honest protest is approved.
  • "the LORD gave Job twice as much as he had before" (Job 42:10, JPS 1917), after Job prays for his friends.
  • Problem: does restoration bring back the reward theory? His first children are not brought back.
Mission 4

The apparent hiddenness of God

  • In the poetry Job seeks God and cannot find Him: "Behold, I go forward, but He is not there" (Job 23:8, JPS 1917).
  • "Wherefore hidest Thou Thy face?" (Job 13:24, JPS 1917).
  • Torah background: hester panim, "hiding of the face": "I will surely hide My face" (Deuteronomy 31:18, JPS 1917), there as a response to sin.
  • Job's case is different: God seems hidden from an innocent man.
  • In chapters 38–42, God is no longer hidden but still unexplained.
  • Trust: faith in Job is not having answers; it is staying in relationship with God through protest.
Mission 4Diversity

Jewish readings of Job

  • Talmud (Bava Batra 15a): one rabbi says Job never existed but was a parable; others place him in history (paraphrase).
  • Maimonides (Guide III.22–23): Job was righteous but lacked wisdom; when he came to know God truly, he understood that true happiness is knowledge of God, not wealth or health (paraphrase).
  • Rabbinic debate: some sages praise Job's piety; others say his protest went too far (Bava Batra 16a, paraphrase).
  • After the Shoah: many Jews read Job as the voice of the innocent who protest to God yet hold on; some, like Elie Wiesel, put God "on trial" in the tradition of Job (paraphrase).
  • Living Judaism: "the LORD gave, and the LORD hath taken away" is said in the burial service (Tziduk ha-Din) in many communities; on hearing of a death Jews bless "the true Judge" (Dayan ha-emet), and mourners tear their garment.

Does Job answer the problem of undeserved suffering?

  • Claim: The book of Job answers the problem of undeserved suffering.
  • For: It rejects the view that suffering is always punishment (42:7).
  • For: It shows that God's wisdom is beyond human grasp (Job 38), so trust is reasonable.
  • For: God appears to Job; presence, not explanation, answers his deepest need.
  • Against: God never tells Job why; the wager in chapters 1–2 makes God seem to allow suffering for a test.
  • Against: The restoration (42:10) cannot replace Job's dead children.
  • Judgement criteria: "Answer" as explanation or as response? Does it help believers trust God (OCR issue)? How does it fit Messianic hope (see Mission 3)?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Maimonides (1138–1204) set out Thirteen Principles in his Commentary on the Mishnah; principles 1–5 are about God: exists, one, without body, eternal, alone to be worshipped.
  • Body words for God are figurative: "the Torah speaks in the language of human beings".
  • In the Guide, positive words about God are read as negations or as attributes of action.

Mission 2

  • Principles 6–9 are about revelation (prophecy, Moses, Torah from heaven, Torah unchanging); 10–13 about God and humanity (knowledge, reward and punishment, Messiah, resurrection).
  • Contemporaries and later thinkers (Raavad, Crescas, Albo, Abarbanel) disputed the list; the debate is about belief versus practice.
  • Today Orthodox Jews affirm all thirteen; Progressive Jews reinterpret especially 8, 12 and 13.

Mission 3

  • Messiah (mashiach, "anointed") is a human king of David's line; the prophets picture an age of peace, judgement, ingathering and knowledge of God (Isaiah 2, Micah 4, Isaiah 11).
  • Maimonides: the Messiah is known by rebuilding the Temple and gathering the exiles; nature is unchanged.
  • Orthodox Jews await a personal Messiah; Reform Jews look to a Messianic Age that people help to build.

Mission 4

  • Job's suffering is undeserved (2:3); Eliphaz's view that suffering is punishment (4:7–8) is rejected by God (42:7).
  • God answers from the whirlwind (38) with questions about creation, not reasons; God is present but not explained.
  • Jewish readings range from parable (Bava Batra 15a) and Maimonides' knowledge of God to protest after the Shoah.
