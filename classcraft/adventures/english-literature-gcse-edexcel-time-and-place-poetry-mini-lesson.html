Mission 1

Autumn means things ending. Why does Keats make it feel so full?

Make a guess: Autumn means things ending. Why does Keats make it feel so full?

Time and Place poetry · "To Autumn"Screen 1

A walk in September 1819

  • Keats wrote the poem on 19 September 1819, after a Sunday walk near Winchester.
  • Two days later he wrote to a friend: "I never liked stubble-fields so much as now".
  • It is an ode: a poem addressed to its subject, here the season itself ("thee").
  • Keats had nursed his brother Tom, who died of tuberculosis in 1818; Keats died of it in 1821, aged 25.

Time and Place poetry · "To Autumn"Screen 4

A day and a season in three stanzas

  • Three 11-line stanzas in iambic pentameter, each rhymed ABAB, then a longer seven-line pattern.
  • Stanza 1: ripening, the fruit swelling.
  • Stanza 2: harvest, with autumn personified as a worker who dozes on "a half-reap'd furrow".
  • Stanza 3: the end of the day and the year, told through sounds.
  • The structure moves from morning mist to the fading light of evening, from full to finished.

?Autumn means things ending. Why does Keats make it feel so full?

Keats fills the season with ripeness, gives it a human presence and answers "Where are the songs of Spring?" with autumn's own music. The ending of the day and the year is there ("soft-dying", "mourn"), but it is held inside richness and song. Readers debate how much sadness sits under the calm: that is a good argument to make.

Mission 2

London, 1802: smoky, crowded, loud. Why does Wordsworth call it fair?

Make a guess: London, 1802: smoky, crowded, loud. Why does Wordsworth call it fair?

Time and Place poetry · "Composed upon Westminster Bridge"Screen 1

A city seen at dawn

  • William Wordsworth (1770–1850) was a Romantic poet, best known for poems of nature and the Lake District.
  • On 31 July 1802 he and his sister Dorothy crossed Westminster Bridge by coach early in the morning, on the way to France.
  • Dorothy's journal describes the same view: St Paul's, the river, boats, and houses free of their usual cloud of smoke.
  • Published 1807 in Poems, in Two Volumes.
Extract Time and Place poetry · "Composed upon Westminster Bridge"Screen 2

The whole sonnet

Time and Place poetry · "Composed upon Westminster Bridge"Screen 3

A sonnet that turns

  • A Petrarchan sonnet: 14 lines of iambic pentameter, rhymed ABBAABBA CDCDCD.
  • The octave (lines 1–8) shows the view: the city "like a garment" in the morning light.
  • The volta (turn) comes at line 9: the sestet moves from what he sees to what he feels, "a calm so deep!".
  • Exclamations build to the last line: a strict form holding strong feeling.

Stillness, and what it hides

  • A list, "Ships, towers, domes, theatres, and temples", gathers trade, religion and entertainment in one glance.

  • The final image, "lying still", is read by some as deep peace, by others as a stillness close to death.

?London, 1802: smoky, crowded, loud. Why does Wordsworth call it fair?

Wordsworth catches London at one unusual moment, early morning, with no smoke and no crowds, and sees it with the eye he usually turns on nature: valleys, fields, sky. The beauty belongs to that hour ("now", "asleep", "lying still"), which is why some readers sense the busy city waiting just behind the calm.

Mission 3

Blake hears misery in every London voice. Whose fault is it?

Make a guess: Blake hears misery in every London voice. Whose fault is it?

Poetry · "London"Screen 1

Blake's London, 1794

  • William Blake published "London" in Songs of Experience (1794).
  • He wrote during the French Revolution, which he followed closely and saw as a revolt against oppressive rule.
  • In the poem a speaker walks the city and records what he sees and hears: chimney sweeps, a soldier, a young prostitute ("Harlot").
  • Charter'd: given out by a charter, a written grant of rights. In the poem even the streets and the river seem owned and controlled.
Extract Poetry · "London"Screen 2

Stanzas 1 and 2

Poetry · "London"Screen 3

A walking beat, a trapped pattern

  • Four quatrains, each rhymed ABAB: a tight, regular pattern.
  • Mostly iambic tetrameter, four beats a line, like steady footsteps: i WAN | der THRO' | each CHAR | ter'd STREET.
  • Repetition traps the reader as the people are trapped: "charter'd" twice, "mark" and "Marks", and anaphora: "In every" opens three lines, and "every" comes five times in stanza 2.
  • In stanza 3 the beat shifts: "How the Chimney-sweeper's cry" starts on a stress, as the cries begin.

The poem points at the powerful: charters control the streets, the Church ignores the sweeps and the Palace is stained with soldiers' blood. But the "mind-forg'd manacles" suggest the chains are also in people's heads: misery continues because people accept it.

Mission 4

She takes her dog to the sea. Why does she end up running?

Make a guess: She takes her dog to the sea. Why does she end up running?

Time and Place poetry · "I started Early – Took my Dog"Screen 1

Which text you are reading

  • Emily Dickinson (1830–1886) lived in Amherst, Massachusetts. Almost none of her poems were printed in her lifetime.
  • This poem, written in the early 1860s, was first printed in 1891 as "By the Sea", edited by Mabel Loomis Todd and T. W. Higginson; they swapped many of her dashes for commas.
  • Your anthology prints a later edition from her manuscript, with dashes and capitals. The words are almost the same.
  • This lesson quotes the 1891 text: learn your quotations from your anthology.

Time and Place poetry · "I started Early – Took my Dog"Screen 3

A hymn tune for a strange walk

  • Six quatrains in common metre, the metre of many hymns: lines of eight and six syllables, four beats then three.
  • Lines 2 and 4 rhyme, often as half-rhymes (heel/pearl, know/withdrew).
  • The narrative has three movements: a playful visit, the rising tide, the chase and the sea's retreat.
  • In your anthology's version, the many dashes break the lines into breathless pieces.

?She takes her dog to the sea. Why does she end up running?

The sea starts as a playful house full of mermaids and turns into "he", a tide that climbs her clothes, threatens to "eat me up" and follows her until the town stops him. Readers argue about what he stands for (nature's power, desire, the unknown, danger), and the strongest answers weigh more than one reading from the same images.

Mission 5

A burnt circle on a hill. Why does it matter so much to him?

Make a guess: A burnt circle on a hill. Why does it matter so much to him?

Time and Place poetry · "Where the Picnic Was"Screen 1

Coming back to a place

  • Thomas Hardy (1840–1928) wrote novels first; after 1895 he wrote mainly poetry.
  • The poem appeared in 1914 in Satires of Circumstance. His wife Emma had died in November 1912.
  • The same book holds "Poems of 1912–13", his poems of grief for Emma.
  • The speaker climbs a hill above the sea in winter, to the spot where four friends had a picnic "last year".

Time and Place poetry · "Where the Picnic Was"Screen 3

Short lines, a stanza that grows

  • Short lines, mostly of two beats, give a slow, halting pace, like a man climbing.
  • Rhyme is close and frequent but the pattern shifts in every stanza, so the poem never settles.
  • The first two stanzas have nine lines; the last has twelve. Stanza 3 opens with three rhyming couplets.
  • Stanza 1 is one long sentence: the "summer time" memory comes first, before "I slowly climb" through "winter mire".

Mission 6

A train stops for one minute. Why does a poem remember it?

Make a guess: A train stops for one minute. Why does a poem remember it?

Time and Place · "Adlestrop"Screen 1

Context and first reading

  • 24 June 1914: Edward Thomas's train stops at Adlestrop, a tiny Cotswold station. He begins the poem in January 1915.
  • War comes that August. Thomas enlists in 1915 and is killed at Arras on 9 April 1917.
  • The poem is published after his death, in 1917.
  • First reading: a remembered minute. The train halts, nobody moves, and the speaker notices the place around him.

Time and Place · "Adlestrop"Screen 5

Form and structure

  • Four quatrains rhyming ABCB (afternoon / June, came / name), in mostly eight-syllable, four-beat lines: plain, like speech.
  • Stanzas 1–2 use short sentences and caesuras: "The steam hissed. Someone cleared his throat." Time almost stops.
  • Enjambment carries the sentence across the stanza break ("only the name / And willows"), and the list of plants (repeated "And…") opens out.
  • The focus moves from train to nature and from near to far.

?A train stops for one minute. Why does a poem remember it?

The minute matters because it is empty. With nothing happening, the speaker notices plants, sky and birdsong spreading "Farther and farther" across the landscape. Time and Place · "Home-Thoughts, from Abroad"Screen 1

Context and first reading

  • Robert Browning published the poem in 1845, in Dramatic Romances and Lyrics. He had visited Italy in 1838 and again in 1844.
  • In 1846 he married Elizabeth Barrett and settled in Italy.
  • First reading: a speaker far from home pictures England in April and May, bird by bird and branch by branch. A warm-country flower appears only in the last line.
  • Context link: the poem is about distance, so the England it shows is a memory, not an eyewitness view.

?Why does a poet abroad make England sound like paradise?

Browning's speaker is abroad, so England exists only in his imagination "now". Longing makes him zoom in on small, ordinary things (a chaffinch, a pear-tree, buttercups) and treasure them. The last line brings back where he really is and shows how homesickness shapes what he values.

Mission 8

A telegraph wire hums over the marsh. Why put it in the poem?

Make a guess: A telegraph wire hums over the marsh. Why put it in the poem?

Time and Place · "In Romney Marsh"Screen 1

Context

  • John Davidson (1857–1909) was a Scottish poet and former teacher who moved to London in 1889.
  • The poem is from Ballads and Songs (1894), his most popular book.
  • Romney Marsh is low, flat land on the Kent coast. Much of it lies near or below sea level, so sea walls such as the Dymchurch Wall hold back the sea.
  • The telegraph was Victorian high technology. This lesson describes the poem rather than quoting it: read it in your anthology alongside.
Time and Place · "In Romney Marsh"Screen 2

What happens

The speaker walks to the sea wall and back, from late afternoon into night:

StanzaWhat the speaker sees and hears
1The speaker walks to the Dymchurch Wall. A south wind blows across the land; low sunlight falls on the mounds where old Norman churches are built.
2The telegraph wire between Romney and Hythe rings in the wind.
3Mist over the Straits of Dover; a deep blue sky; the colours of sunset.
4Ships' masts rock offshore; waves break and spray flies over the beach; the noise of the sea goes on.
5The speaker leaves the Wall; the sunset fades over the low hills inland.
6Night: the stars appear, and the wire between Hythe and Romney sounds again.
7Spray flies in the dark; the noise of the beach is compared to an organ; the sound of the surf lingers.

Time and Place · "In Romney Marsh"Screen 4

Form and structure

  • Seven quatrains rhyming ABAB, in iambic tetrameter: the steady four-beat line of a ballad, fitting a book called Ballads and Songs.
  • Structure: out to the sea wall in sunset (stanzas 1–4), back as night falls (5–7).
  • Colour words (yellow, purple, sapphire, rose, red) and sound (wind, wire, waves, organ) build an overwhelming scene.
  • Human traces (wall, churches, wire, ships) appear, but no other people.

Mission checkScreen 8

?A telegraph wire hums over the marsh. Why put it in the poem?

The wire is part of a pattern: the Dymchurch Wall, the Norman churches, the ships and the telegraph all show people's mark on Romney Marsh, but each is set inside overwhelming wind, colour and sea. The wire sounds only because the wind plays it: many readers see human works dwarfed by the place, while others hear the wire joining the landscape's music. Either way, it is part of a pattern, not a stray detail.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Written on 19 September 1819 after a walk near Winchester; an ode in three 11-line stanzas that move from ripening to harvest to the end of the day and season.
  • Personification ("bosom-friend of the maturing sun"), crowded images of ripeness and the sounds of stanza 3 make an ending feel full.
  • For AO3, link the poem to its moment (a September walk, a poet who knew illness and loss) only where it explains an idea in the lines.
  • Written on 31 July 1802 from a coach crossing Westminster Bridge at dawn; a Petrarchan sonnet whose octave shows the view and whose sestet turns to feeling.
  • The city "like a garment" wears beauty for the morning; comparisons with "valley, rock, or hill" let a Romantic nature poet praise a city.
  • For AO3, use the Romantic love of nature and the usual smoke of London to explain why this moment surprises him.
  • "London" (1794) walks through a controlled city and hears suffering in every voice.
  • Tight quatrains, a steady beat and heavy repetition make the city feel like a trap; the images link suffering to the Church and the Palace.
  • Compare with another poem about a place; Wordsworth's calm London at dawn makes a sharp contrast.
  • First printed in 1891 as "By the Sea"; your anthology prints Dickinson's dashes and capitals from her manuscript.
  • A hymn-like common metre carries a strange story: the sea as a house, then as "he", a tide that rises and pursues her to the "solid town".
  • For AO3, use the 1890s editing and Dickinson's private, unpublished writing only where they explain the form, and treat readings of the sea as interpretations.
  • Published 1914, two years after Emma Hardy's death; a speaker returns in winter to the spot of a summer picnic for four.
  • Short lines, shifting rhyme and a final stanza that grows carry the contrast of then and now; pathetic fallacy and the euphemism "has shut her eyes / For evermore" carry the grief.
  • For AO3, use Hardy's loss as a possible reading of "her", never as a fact the poem states.
  • Thomas's train stopped at Adlestrop on 24 June 1914; he wrote the poem in 1915, and it was published in 1917, after he was killed at Arras.
  • Four quatrains (ABCB) move from short, still sentences on a "bare platform" to a long, flowing list of nature, and from near to far.
  • For Part 1, link "Adlestrop" to another poem through a still moment, memory or the sense of a place; use the war as context only where it explains the poem's mood.
  • Published 1845, after Browning's visits to Italy: a speaker abroad imagines England in April and May.
  • Exclamations, dashes, the repeated "now" and run-on pairs of rhymes make it sound like excited, homesick speech.
  • The final contrast ("this gaudy melon-flower") is a strong link to any poem about seeing a place from outside, or about memory and longing.
  • Davidson's poem (Ballads and Songs, 1894) follows a walk to the Dymchurch Wall and back, from sunset into night.
  • Seven ABAB ballad quatrains pile up colour and sound; the wire, the Wall and the churches are human traces in a landscape stronger than them.
  • Link it with poems where a place overwhelms the speaker, or where human works meet nature.

Key quotations to learn

  • Season of mists and mellow fruitfulness, (Stanza 1, line 1) The ode's opening address: soft sounds, ripeness.
  • Close bosom-friend of the maturing sun; (Stanza 1, line 2) Personification: the season as a warm partner.
  • And fill all fruit with ripeness to the core; (Stanza 1, line 6) Abundance taken as far as it can go.
  • Thee sitting careless on a granary floor, (Stanza 2, line 3) Autumn personified as a relaxed harvest worker.
  • Where are the songs of Spring? Ay, where are they? (Stanza 3, line 1) A question about loss, answered with autumn's own music.
  • And gathering swallows twitter in the skies. (Stanza 3, line 11) The last image: birds about to leave.
  • Earth has not anything to show more fair: (Line 1) A bold opening claim, made about a city.
  • This City now doth like a garment wear (Line 4) Simile: beauty worn for the moment ("now").
  • All bright and glittering in the smokeless air. (Line 8) The unusual clean air that makes the view possible.
  • Ne'er saw I, never felt, a calm so deep! (Line 11) After the volta: from seeing to feeling.
  • And all that mighty heart is lying still! (Line 14) Personification: the city as a body at rest.
  • I wander thro' each charter'd street, (stanza 1) A city owned and controlled, even its streets.
  • Marks of weakness, marks of woe. (stanza 1) Suffering is visible on every face.
  • The mind-forg'd manacles I hear. (stanza 2) The chains are in people's minds too.
  • Every black'ning Church appalls; (stanza 3) The Church is stained and does nothing.
  • Runs in blood down Palace walls. (stanza 3) The rulers are marked with the soldier's blood.
  • And blights with plagues the Marriage hearse. (stanza 4) Oxymoron: love and death joined; misery passes to the next generation.
  • I started early, took my dog, (Stanza 1, line 1) A calm, ordinary start in hymn metre.
  • The mermaids in the basement (Stanza 1, line 3) The sea as a house: playful fancy.
  • But no man moved me till the tide (Stanza 3, line 1) The turn: the tide begins to move.
  • And made as he would eat me up (Stanza 4, line 1) Personification at its most threatening.
  • And he — he followed close behind; (Stanza 5, line 1) The repeated "he": the chase.
  • Until we met the solid town, (Stanza 6, line 1) The town as safety: the sea withdraws.
  • In the summer time, (Stanza 1, line 2) The past, set against "winter mire".
  • The forsaken place (Stanza 1, line 8) The place feels abandoned, as he does.
  • As a burnt circle—aye, (Stanza 2, line 4) The one trace the picnic left.
  • Last relic of the band (Stanza 2, line 8) He is what survives of the group.
  • As when we four came. (Stanza 3, line 6) The place is "the same"; the four are not.
  • And one—has shut her eyes / For evermore. (Stanza 3, lines 11–12) Euphemism for death, after a hesitant dash.
  • Yes. I remember Adlestrop— (Line 1) The poem is a memory: it opens with a conversational, emphatic "Yes".
  • Unwontedly. It was late June. (Line 4) The unusual stop; caesura and short sentence slow time down.
  • No one left and no one came (Line 6) Balanced halves show the stillness and emptiness.
  • Was Adlestrop—only the name (Line 8) The place is at first just a sign on a platform.
  • And for that minute a blackbird sang (Line 13) One bird; one minute: the moment of noticing.
  • Farther and farther, all the birds (Line 15) Repetition widens the scene to the whole region.
  • Oh, to be in England (Line 1) An exclamatory wish: the poem starts in longing.
  • Now that April's there, (Line 2) He pictures England in the present, from far away.
  • In England—now! (Line 8) "Now" frames the stanza; the dash and exclamation stress the distance.
  • That's the wise thrush; he sings each song twice over, (Line 14) The repeated song mirrors the poem's wish to relive joy.
  • The first fine careless rapture! (Line 16) Joy that can't quite be recaptured: like memory.
  • Far brighter than this gaudy melon-flower! (Line 20) The real place breaks in and is judged.
