No critics? Three other guides

The set poems are in copyright, so this lesson doesn't quote them: learn your lines from your own copy. For a recent poem you have three guides that never let you down:

  • Tradition: what older form or poem is it answering?
  • Voice: who speaks, to whom, and how reliable are they?
  • The other poem: comparison shows what is distinctive.
Modern poetry · approachesScreen 2

How your exam asks this

  • AS 1 Section A (open book): one question on your two poets, such as Boland and Bleakney: methods, context and connections between poems (AO1–AO4). Critics (AO5) aren't assessed here either.
  • Essay practice is for class.
Modern poetry · approachesScreen 3

Form as a conversation

  • Modern poets often rework a traditional form: the sonnet, the ode, the dramatic monologue, the villanelle.
  • Ask what the poem keeps (fourteen lines, a turn, an address) and what it breaks (rhyme, metre, decorum).
  • Example: Tim Turnbull's 'Ode on a Grayson Perry Urn' (in Poems of the Decade) takes its title from Keats's "Ode on a Grecian Urn" (1820), which begins "Thou still unravish'd bride of quietness,". Reading the two together is a critic-free way into the new poem.

Modern poetry · approachesScreen 7

Voice, and readings you can defend

  • Persona: the speaker is not the poet. Many contemporary poems are dramatic monologues, a Victorian form (Browning's "My Last Duchess" opens "That's my last Duchess painted on the wall,").
  • The poem's world: brand names, technology, place and dialect are choices: ask what they do.
  • Tentative readings: might suggest, can be read as; weigh two readings and choose with evidence.
  • For CCEA, context outside the poem (AO3) is assessed: link it to what the poem does.

?A poem from this century, no critics yet: how do you write about it?

You don't need critics. Read the poem's form against tradition, listen to its voice (speaker, not poet), notice the world it builds, and compare it with the other poem. Then argue a tentative, evidenced reading: that is what these sections reward.

Why does The Merchant of Venice play differently now than in 1600?

Make a guess: Why does The Merchant of Venice play differently now than in 1600?

A-level skills · contexts of production and reception (AO3)Screen 1

Two kinds of context

  • Context of production: when, where and how the text was written: its society, beliefs, genre conventions, theatre or publishing, the writer's situation.
  • Context of reception: how readers and audiences have received it since, from first reviews to modern productions and criticism.
  • The words on the page stay the same; what audiences notice, fear and sympathise with changes.
  • Context earns marks when it explains something in the text: ‘this helps explain why…’.

How your exam asks this

  • CCEA: AS 1, AS 2, A2 1, A2 2 Section A and the A2 3 essay ask you to discuss context, using information from outside the text.

Jane Eyre's restlessness

A-level skills · contexts of production and reception (AO3)Screen 7

Jane Eyre's first readers and later ones

  • 1847: published under the name Currer Bell.
  • 1848: Elizabeth Rigby, in the Quarterly Review (December), condemned the novel as irreligious and linked its spirit to the revolutions abroad and Chartism at home, the unrest of that year.
  • 1966: Jean Rhys's novel Wide Sargasso Sea retells Bertha's story from the Caribbean.
  • 1979: Sandra Gilbert and Susan Gubar read Bertha as Jane's dark double, her suppressed anger.
  • 1985: Gayatri Spivak argued that Jane's freedom is bought at the colonised Bertha's expense.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Why does The Merchant of Venice play differently now than in 1600?

The text hasn't changed; its audiences have. Written in a society that had expelled its Jews and was gripped by the Lopez affair, the play is now received after the Holocaust, so many audiences hear Shylock's speech as a plea for shared humanity. The strongest answers use both contexts to explain what the text does.

Recap

Big ideas

  • No critics needed: tradition (form), voice (persona) and the other poem are your guides; readings should be tentative and evidenced.
  • Contemporary poets rework sonnets, odes and dramatic monologues: ask what they keep and what they break.
  • AS 1 Section A also rewards context (AO3) and connections between your two poets' poems.
  • Context of production: when, where and how a text was written. Context of reception: how it has been read and staged since.
  • The Merchant of Venice came from an England without a Jewish community and fresh from the Lopez affair; after the Holocaust, its antisemitism is staged and read very differently.
  • Jane Eyre was attacked in 1848 as irreligious and rebellious; feminist and post-colonial critics since 1966 have reread it, including through Bertha.
  • Context earns marks only when it explains something in the text.
