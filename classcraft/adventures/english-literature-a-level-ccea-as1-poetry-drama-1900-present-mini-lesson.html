AS 1: Poetry & Drama 1900–Present

This mini-lesson trains the skills CCEA Unit AS 1 rewards — not a single set text. You will practise Section A (analysing and comparing modern poetry) and Section B (analysing a modern play), and learn exactly what each Assessment Objective is testing.

The unit at a glance

What AS 1 actually assesses

AS 1 is a 2-hour written paper in two halves. You write one essay on poetry (Section A, open book) and one on a modern play (Section B, closed book).

  • Section A — Poetry 1900–Present: you study two poets and learn to analyse, evaluate, and compare and contrast. Assessed on AO1, AO2, AO3, AO4.
  • Section B — Drama 1900–Present: you study a play by a modern dramatist. Assessed on AO1, AO2, AO3, AO5.

The five AOs (CCEA): AO1 informed, personal response in accurate critical language; AO2 analyse how meaning is shaped; AO3 contexts of writing and reception; AO4 connections across texts; AO5 different interpretations. Notice Section A tests AO4 (comparison); Section B tests AO5 (interpretations) instead.

Section A · poetic method (AO2)

Reading a poem's method

AO2 asks you to analyse the poet's use of poetic methods such as form, structure, language and tone. Think in four layers:

  • Form — the kind of poem and its shape: sonnet, ode, elegy, dramatic monologue, free verse, blank verse.
  • Structure — how the poem unfolds: stanza pattern, enjambment, caesura, the volta or turn, refrain, and where emphasis falls.
  • Sound — metre (e.g. iambic pentameter), rhyme and half-rhyme, alliteration, assonance, sibilance.
  • Language & tone — diction, imagery (metaphor, simile, personification, symbolism) and the poem's shifting attitude or mood.

Method, not label-spotting: naming a device earns little. The marks are for showing how the method shapes meaning and effect on the reader.

Section A · form & structure

Form and structure in detail

Two of the most productive things to analyse are line endings and the movement of the whole poem:

  • Enjambment — a sentence spills over the line-break, creating momentum, surprise or a sense of the uncontainable.
  • Caesura — a strong pause mid-line (often a full stop or dash) that halts the rhythm and isolates a phrase.
  • Volta — the turn where a poem changes direction, from problem to resolution, question to answer, or one mood to another.
  • Metre — the underlying beat; departures from a regular metre (a stressed word out of place) are where meaning often concentrates.

Illustrative (not from a set text): a line broken as "and still it / falls" places weight on falls through enjambment. Always tie the technique to the specific effect.

Section A · comparison (AO4)

Comparing two poets

Section A rewards AO4 — connections across texts. Comparison is not two separate summaries stuck together; it is an integrated argument that moves between the poets.

  • Compare method (how each handles form or imagery), theme and attitude, and the effect on the reader.
  • Use comparative connectives — similarly, by contrast, whereas, in the same way — to signal genuine connection.
  • Value difference as much as similarity: precise contrasts often make the sharpest points.

Structure tip: build each paragraph around an idea and bring both poets to it, rather than writing all of poet A then all of poet B.

Section A & B · context (AO3)

Using context well

AO3 asks you to show the significance and influence of the contexts in which texts are written and received, drawing on information from outside the text.

  • Context of production — the historical, social, literary or biographical moment of writing.
  • Context of reception — how meaning shifts for different readers and audiences over time.
  • Context should illuminate the reading, woven into analysis — never a free-standing history lecture.

Examiner's warning: irrelevant biography or dates score nothing. Only context that changes how we read the words on the page earns AO3.

Section B · dramatic method (AO2)

Reading a modern play

For the play, AO2 asks you to analyse the dramatist's use of dramatic methods such as characterisation, structure, language and staging. The extra dimension beyond poetry is performance.

  • Characterisation — what characters say and do, and how they change.
  • Structure — act and scene shape, exposition, climax, and dramatic irony.
  • Language — dialogue, register, subtext and silence.
  • Staging — set, lighting, movement, props and stage directions.

Traditions to recognise (illustrative frames): naturalism (life rendered realistically); Brechtian epic theatre (breaking illusion to keep the audience critical); and the Theatre of the Absurd — Martin Esslin's 1961 term for drama of stalled action and futility. Modern set plays draw on such traditions.

Section B · interpretations (AO5)

Different interpretations

The drama section assesses AO5 — explore a play informed by different interpretations. A play lives in performance and in criticism, so its meaning is genuinely contested.

  • Weigh alternative critical readings (for example feminist, political or psychoanalytic approaches).
  • Consider how staging choices in different productions change a scene's meaning.
  • Reach a considered personal view — AO5 is not "list what critics said", but judge between readings.

Balance: AO5 works best fused with AO2 — use the text's methods as the evidence for why one interpretation is more persuasive.

Both sections · expression (AO1)

Writing the essay (AO1)

AO1 rewards an informed, personal and creative response in accurate critical language and coherent, well-structured writing.

  • Open with a clear thesis — a line of argument the essay will prove.
  • Use precise terminology (enjambment, volta, dramatic irony) accurately, not for show.
  • Choose short, apt quotations and analyse the words closely; embed them in your sentences.
  • Keep a through-line — every paragraph should advance the argument.

Golden rule: argument first, evidence second, feature-spotting never. Examiners reward a persuasive case built on close reading.

The big ideas to know

Shape of AS 1: Section A poetry (compare two poets, open book) + Section B modern drama (closed book)

Poetic method (AO2): form · structure (enjambment, caesura, volta) · sound (metre, rhyme) · imagery & tone

Comparison (AO4): integrate two poets around ideas; value contrast as well as likeness

Dramatic method (AO2): characterisation · structure · language · staging (performance is key)

Context (AO3): production and reception, woven in — never free-standing history

Interpretations (AO5): weigh rival critical and performance readings; reach a judgement
