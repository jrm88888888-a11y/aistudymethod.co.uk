Mission 1

Mission 1: If a firm sells thousands of items, how can it still be losing money?

Make a guess: If a firm sells thousands of items, how can it still be losing money?

Mission 1

Fixed and variable costs

  • Fixed costs (FC): do not change with output in the short run. E.g. rent, insurance, loan repayments, salaries.
  • Variable costs (VC): change with output. E.g. raw materials, packaging, energy used by machines.
  • If output is zero, fixed costs must still be paid. Variable costs are zero.
Mission 1

Total and average cost

  • Total cost: TC = FC + VC
  • Average cost: AC = TC ÷ output. This is the cost per unit.
  • Say: "average cost fell" — not: "costs fell" — when total cost rose but cost per unit fell.
Mission 1

Revenue

  • Total revenue: TR = price × quantity sold
  • Average revenue: AR = TR ÷ quantity sold. If every unit sells at the same price, AR = price.
  • Profit = TR − TC. If TC is bigger than TR, the firm makes a loss.
  • Revenue is not profit: it is all the money coming in, before costs are paid.
Mission 1

Worked example

Illustrative data. A bakery: fixed costs £300 a day; cakes sell at £5.50 each.

  • At 50 cakes: it sells 50 cakes but makes a loss of £175: revenue does not cover the fixed costs.
  • At 100 cakes: TR = TC. Profit is zero (break-even).
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A firm sells 400 units at £6 each. Its fixed costs are £800 and its variable cost is £3 per unit. What is its profit?

Spreading the fixed costs

  • Takeaway: fixed cost per unit keeps falling as output rises; average cost falls at first, then can rise again as variable cost per unit climbs.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A firm's total cost is £1,000 when it makes 100 units, and £1,600 when it makes 200 units. What happens to its average cost?
Mission 1

What do firms aim for?

  • Profit maximisation: making the biggest possible gap between TR and TC.
  • Growth: getting bigger, e.g. by selling more or opening new sites.
  • Sales growth and market share: selling more, or a bigger share of all sales in the market, even if profit per unit is lower.
Mission 1

Profit as a signal, and ethics

  • Higher prices → more profit per unit → firms have an incentive to expand output, and new firms are drawn in.
  • That is why supply curves slope upwards.
  • Motives vs ethics: firms aim for profit, but choices can clash with ethical standards.
  • E.g. paying suppliers a fair price, safe working conditions, or cutting pollution can raise costs and lower profit in the short run.
  • Some firms gain customers and loyal workers by acting ethically; others may cut corners. Consumers, laws and regulators can change these choices.
Quick check
?A firm cuts its prices below its rivals' prices. Its profit per unit falls, but it wins many customers from rivals and becomes the biggest seller in the market. Which objective is it most likely pursuing?
Mission check

?Illustrative data. A phone-repair shop has fixed costs of £1,500 a month. Each repair costs £20 in parts and is charged at £35. Last month it did 80 repairs. What was its profit or loss?

Each repair earns £15 more than its parts cost, but 80 repairs bring in only £1,200 of that. The shop needs more repairs to cover its fixed costs.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How can the same workers make more without working longer hours, and why does it matter?

Make a guess: How can the same workers make more without working longer hours, and why does it matter?

Mission 2

Production vs productivity

  • Production: the total output of goods and services. E.g. 1,000 chairs a week.
  • Productivity: output per unit of input, over a period. E.g. chairs per worker per week.
  • Labour productivity = total output ÷ number of workers (or ÷ hours worked).
  • Production can rise just by hiring more workers. Productivity rises only if each input produces more.
  • Say: "productivity rose" only when output per worker (or per hour) rose — not when total output rose.
Mission 2

Worked example

  • Illustrative data. Two furniture factories, one week.
  • Factory A: 20 workers make 1,000 chairs. Productivity = 1,000 ÷ 20 = 50 chairs per worker.
  • Factory B: 10 workers make 600 chairs. Productivity = 600 ÷ 10 = 60 chairs per worker.
  • A has the higher production. B has the higher productivity.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A bakery employs 10 workers who make 500 loaves a day. It hires 2 more workers, and output rises to 540 loaves a day. What has happened?
Mission 2

What raises productivity?

  • Education and training: skills make each worker produce more. Skills, knowledge and experience are called human capital.
  • Investment in capital: new machines, computers and buildings. Workers with better tools produce more per hour.
  • Technology: better methods, e.g. robots on a production line, or software that speeds up paperwork.
  • Management and organisation: e.g. specialisation and division of labour, better layout, less waste.
  • Motivation: better pay, conditions and recognition can raise effort and cut absence.
Mission 2Graph lab

Higher wages, lower costs?

Illustrative data. Each worker is paid £100 a day and makes 20 units a day.

  • Takeaway: labour cost per unit = wage ÷ output per worker. If productivity rises faster than wages, cost per unit falls.
Mission 2

Why higher productivity matters

  • Firms: lower average cost → more profit, or lower prices to win customers. More competitive, including against firms abroad.
  • Workers: firms can afford higher wages without raising cost per unit.
  • Consumers: lower prices, better quality.
  • The economy: more output from the same resources → economic growth and higher living standards.
  • But: if machines replace workers, some may lose their jobs and need to retrain.
Quick check
?Which change is most likely to raise labour productivity in a car factory?
Mission check

?Illustrative data. Last year, a call centre had 50 staff who handled 20,000 calls a month. It bought new software. This year, 40 staff handle 20,000 calls a month. Which statement is correct?

Investment in new technology let fewer workers produce the same output: productivity rose even though production did not.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why can a giant firm make things more cheaply than a small one, and when does size start to hurt?

Make a guess: Why can a giant firm make things more cheaply than a small one, and when does size start to hurt?

Mission 3

Cost per unit as a firm grows

  • Economies of scale: as a firm grows, its average cost (cost per unit) falls.
  • Diseconomies of scale: as a firm grows too big, its average cost rises.
  • These happen in the long run, when the firm can grow all its inputs: bigger factories, more machines, more staff.
  • On the long-run average cost (LRAC) curve: falling part = economies of scale; lowest point = lowest cost per unit; rising part = diseconomies of scale.
  • Say: "economies of scale lower average cost" — not: "lower costs". Total cost still rises as output grows.
Mission 3

Internal economies of scale

  • Internal economies come from the firm's own growth.
  • Technical: bigger, specialised machines and mass production. Bigger containers cost less per unit of space.
  • Purchasing: buying in bulk earns discounts on materials.
  • Financial: banks see big firms as less risky, so they lend more cheaply.
  • Managerial: big firms can hire specialist managers (finance, marketing, production).
  • Risk-bearing: selling many products in many markets, so a fall in one is offset by others.
Mission 3

Worked example: why bigger ships carry more cheaply

  • Think of a ship's cargo hold as a cube (a simplified model).
  • Make each side twice as long, 1 → 2.
  • Surface (steel needed to build it): 6 → 24 squares. ×4.
  • Volume (cargo it can carry): 1 → 8 cubes. ×8.
  • Steel per unit of cargo: 6 ÷ 1 = 6 → 24 ÷ 8 = 3. It halves. This is a technical economy of scale.
  • A bigger ship also needs only a slightly bigger crew.
  • Real data: the container ship MSC Irina, delivered in 2023, carries 24,346 twenty-foot containers.
Quick check
?An airline orders 100 new planes at once and gets a much lower price per plane than a small airline buying 2. Which economy of scale is this?
Mission 3

Diseconomies of scale

  • When a firm grows very large, average cost can rise. Why?
  • Communication: more layers of managers; messages are slow or get distorted.
  • Control and coordination: hard to keep many sites and departments working well together.
  • Motivation: workers feel like a small part of a huge machine; absence and staff turnover rise.
  • On the LRAC curve: the rising part, after the lowest point.
Quick check
?A restaurant chain grows fast and opens in 15 countries. Instructions from head office take weeks to reach managers, and staff say nobody listens to them. Its average cost per meal starts to rise. What is the best explanation?
Mission 3

Growth: who gains?

  • Economies of scale give firms a reason to grow: by expanding, or by joining with other firms.
  • Lower average cost lets a big firm cut prices, win market share, or earn more profit.
  • Small new firms with higher costs find it hard to compete: economies of scale can act as a barrier to entry.
  • Benefits of growth: lower costs and prices; money for new products; more secure jobs.
  • Costs of growth: diseconomies of scale; less competition can mean higher prices and less choice; less personal service.
  • Whether consumers gain depends on whether the cost savings are passed on in lower prices.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. A delivery firm grows over several years. At 1,000 parcels a day its total cost is £6,000 a day. At 5,000 parcels it is £20,000. At 20,000 parcels it is £100,000. What do these figures show?

Growth can cut cost per unit, but only up to a point. Judge it by average cost, not total cost.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why do a few firms grow huge while most firms stay small all their lives?

Make a guess: Why do most firms stay small?

Mission 4

How big is a firm?

  • Ways to measure firm size:
    • number of employees (workers)
    • sales revenue (value of sales)
    • market share (its % of all sales in the market)
    • capital employed (money invested in the business)
  • A common size rule: small = fewer than 50 employees; medium = 50–249; large = 250 or more.
  • UK, start of 2024: 5.5 million private sector businesses.
    • 99.2% were small (0 to 49 employees).
    • 74% employed no one except the owner(s).
  • One firm can look big on one measure and small on another (a few staff, but huge sales).
Mission 4

Small firms: strengths and weaknesses

Strengths of small firmsWeaknesses of small firms
Personal service: they know their customersHigher average costs: few economies of scale
Flexible: quick decisions, quick to changeHarder to borrow, often at higher interest rates
Can serve a niche (a small, specialist market)Owner may lack skills in every area
Owner keeps controlOne lost customer or bad year can close the firm
  • Large firms are the mirror image: lower average costs, easier finance, wider range, money for research. But slower decisions and harder to manage.
Mission 4

Why most firms stay small

  • Small market: a village bakery or a local plumber only has so many customers.
  • Niche or personal service: customers want a specialist or a personal touch (a tailor, a hairdresser).
  • Owner's choice: owners want control, less risk or less stress, not maximum size.
  • Finance: banks see small firms as risky, so borrowing to grow is hard.
  • Few economies of scale in some industries: a bigger salon is not much cheaper per haircut.
  • Say: "the firm chose to stay small" — not: "the firm failed to grow". Staying small can be a sensible choice.
Quick check
?A family runs one bakery in a small village. It has made a profit for 20 years and has never grown. Which is the most likely reason it has stayed small?
Mission 4

Two ways to grow

  • Internal (organic) growth: the firm expands itself. More branches, more machines, new products, new markets. Slower, but lower risk and the owners keep control.
  • External growth: the firm joins with another firm.
    • Merger: two firms agree to combine into one.
    • Takeover: one firm buys another.
  • External growth is faster, but costly, and joining two businesses can go wrong (different ways of working).
  • Firms grow to gain economies of scale, a bigger market share and more profit.
Mission 4

Bigger and cheaper? Worked example

  • Illustrative data. A bakery grows:
Loaves a dayTotal costAverage cost (TC ÷ output)
Before1,000£800£0.80
After5,000£2,500£0.50
  • Economies of scale (see Mission 3) cut the average cost per loaf by £0.30.
  • At a price of £1.00, profit per loaf rises from £0.20 to £0.50.
  • How growth can change what a firm does:
    • Keep the price and earn more profit per item.
    • Cut the price (say to £0.80, still £0.30 profit per loaf) and win customers from rivals.
    • Spend profits on new products, research or more growth.
  • Too big, and diseconomies of scale can push average cost back up.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A furniture firm makes 2,000 chairs a year at a total cost of £80,000. After growing, it makes 5,000 chairs at a total cost of £150,000. What is its new average cost per chair?
Mission 4

Growth: who gains, who loses?

Possible benefitsPossible costs
The firmLower average costs; more profit; less riskDiseconomies of scale; harder to manage
ConsumersLower prices; wider range; better productsFewer rivals, so higher prices or less choice
WorkersTraining; promotion; job securityJob cuts after a merger; feeling less valued
  • Which side wins depends on whether lower costs are passed on in lower prices, and on how much competition is left.
  • Governments can block mergers that would cut competition too far (Sainsbury's and Asda, 2019).
Quick check
?The two biggest food-delivery apps in a country agree to merge. Customers have few other apps to choose from. What is the main risk for customers?
Mission check

?A hairdresser has run one profitable salon for 15 years and has turned down chances to open more. In the same country, a car maker has merged with a rival and now sells all over the world. Which best explains the difference?

Firms grow huge where size cuts average cost and the market is big; where it does not, staying small makes sense.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • TC = FC + VC; average cost = TC ÷ output. TR = price × quantity; AR = TR ÷ quantity.
  • Profit = TR − TC: a firm can sell a lot and still make a loss if its costs are higher.
  • Firms may aim for profit, growth, sales, survival or wider goals, not only profit.

Mission 2

  • Production = total output. Productivity = output per unit of input (e.g. per worker or per hour).
  • Training (human capital), investment, technology, good management and motivation raise productivity.
  • Higher productivity lowers cost per unit, which can mean higher wages, lower prices and economic growth.

Mission 3

  • Economies of scale: average cost falls as a firm grows. Diseconomies of scale: it rises.
  • Internal economies (technical, purchasing, financial, managerial, risk-bearing, marketing) come from the firm's own growth.

Mission 4

  • Most firms are small: small markets, owners' choices and finance hold them back.
  • Firms grow internally or by mergers and takeovers; growth can lower average costs.
