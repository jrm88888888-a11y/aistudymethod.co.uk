Mission 1

Mission 1: No one can pull a salt crystal apart into gaseous ions and measure the energy. So how do we know the lattice enthalpy of NaCl?

Make a guess: No one can pull a salt crystal apart into gaseous ions and measure the energy. So how do we know the lattice enthalpy of NaCl?

Mission 1

Making gaseous atoms

  • Every ionic solid can be traced back to its elements in their standard states.
  • Enthalpy change of atomisation, ΔatH⦵: the enthalpy change when 1 mol of gaseous atoms is formed from the element in its standard state.
  • Metal: Na(s) → Na(g), +107 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Chlorine: ½Cl2(g) → Cl(g), +121 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Bond enthalpy: the enthalpy change to break 1 mol of a covalent bond, all species gaseous. Cl–Cl is +242, so ΔatH⦵(Cl) = ½ × 242 = +121.
  • Atomisation is always endothermic.
Mission 1

Making gaseous ions

  • First ionisation energy: the enthalpy change when 1 mol of gaseous atoms each lose one electron to form 1 mol of gaseous 1+ ions. Na(g) → Na⁺(g) + e⁻, +496. Endothermic.
  • First electron affinity: the enthalpy change when 1 mol of gaseous atoms each gain one electron to form 1 mol of gaseous 1− ions. Cl(g) + e⁻ → Cl⁻(g), −349. Exothermic.
  • Second electron affinity: O⁻(g) + e⁻ → O²⁻(g). Endothermic: the negative ion repels the incoming electron.
  • Units: kJ mol⁻¹. All values here are standard enthalpy changes.
Mission 1

Lattice enthalpy: two directions

  • Enthalpy of lattice formation: the enthalpy change when 1 mol of an ionic solid is formed from its gaseous ions. Na⁺(g) + Cl⁻(g) → NaCl(s). Negative.
  • Enthalpy of lattice dissociation (breaking): 1 mol of an ionic solid separated into its gaseous ions. NaCl(s) → Na⁺(g) + Cl⁻(g). Positive.
  • Same size, opposite sign.
  • The video uses dissociation: +790 for NaCl.
  • AQA accepts either definition. The question tells you which.

Mission 1

Reading a Born–Haber cycle

  • A Born–Haber cycle is Hess's law for an ionic solid.
  • Enthalpy change of formation, ΔfH⦵: the enthalpy change when 1 mol of a compound forms from its elements in their standard states, under standard conditions.
  • Two routes from the elements to the solid: directly (ΔfH⦵), or through gaseous atoms and ions.
  • Draw it as an energy-level diagram: up = endothermic, down = exothermic.
  • Elements in standard states sit at zero.
  • Both routes give the same total, so one unknown step can be found.
  • You must be able to construct the cycle: steps in order, with correct species and state symbols at each level.
Mission 1

Worked example: lattice enthalpy of NaCl

  • Data / kJ mol⁻¹: ΔfH⦵(NaCl) −411 · ΔatH⦵ Na +107, Cl +121 · IE1(Na) +496 · EA1(Cl) −349.
  • Route through ions: 107 + 121 + 496 − 349 = +375 (Na⁺(g) + Cl⁻(g) level).
  • Hess's law: +375 + (lattice formation) = −411.
  • Lattice formation = −411 − 375 = −786 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • So lattice dissociation = +786 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Data books give about 787–790: step values come from different data sets.
  • Only the lattice step releases enough energy to make ΔfH⦵ negative.

Mission 1

Two charges: CaCl2 and oxides

  • Ca²⁺ needs IE1 + IE2. CaCl2 has two Cl⁻, so double the chlorine steps.
  • Data / kJ mol⁻¹: ΔfH⦵(CaCl2) −796 · ΔatH⦵ Ca +178, Cl +121 · IE1 +590, IE2 +1145 · EA1(Cl) −349.
  • Route to ions: 178 + 590 + 1145 + 2(121) + 2(−349) = +1457.
  • Lattice formation = −796 − 1457 = −2253 kJ mol⁻¹: almost three times NaCl.
  • Lattice dissociation = +2253 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Oxides (e.g. MgO, CaO, Na2O): atomise oxygen (½O2 → O), then add EA1 (exothermic) and EA2 (endothermic).

Mission 1

Theory vs experiment

  • The perfect ionic model calculates lattice enthalpy from charges and distances, assuming spherical ions with no covalent character.
  • The Born–Haber value is experimental.
  • NaCl: Born–Haber −787 (our cycle gave −786; data sets differ by 1–2), theory −766 kJ mol⁻¹. Close: NaCl is almost purely ionic.
  • AgCl: Born–Haber −918, theory −833 kJ mol⁻¹. The experimental value is more exothermic.
  • A big gap = covalent character: the extra bonding makes the lattice stronger than ions alone predict.
  • Cause: a small, highly charged cation distorts (polarises) the electron cloud of a large anion, so electron density is shared between the ions.
Quick check

Mission check

?Calcium oxide, CaO, is quicklime. Which expression gives the enthalpy change for Ca²⁺(g) + O²⁻(g) → CaO(s)? (ΔatH⦵ = enthalpy change of atomisation; IE = ionisation energy of Ca; EA = electron affinity of O)

No one measures the lattice step. Every other step is measured (or found from another cycle), so Hess's law round the Born–Haber cycle gives the one unknown. That is how the NaCl value is known.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why do some salts make water colder as they dissolve, while others warm it up?

Make a guess: Why do some salts make water colder as they dissolve, while others warm it up?

Mission 2

Dissolving in two steps

  • Step 1: break the lattice. Separate the ions into gaseous ions: energy absorbed.
  • Step 2: hydrate the ions. Water molecules surround each ion: energy released.
  • Water's δ− O faces cations; its δ+ H faces anions.
  • Absorbed > released: endothermic. Released > absorbed: exothermic.
Mission 2

What the thermometer tells you

  • In an insulated cup, the water is the surroundings; the dissolving salt is the system.
  • Temperature falls: energy flowed from the water into the dissolving salt. Endothermic.
  • Temperature rises: energy flowed from the dissolving salt into the water. Exothermic.
  • Energy transferred: q = mcΔT, then ΔsolH = −q ÷ n (n = moles dissolved), as in an earlier lesson. A temperature fall gives a positive ΔsolH.
Mission 2

Two definitions

  • Enthalpy change of solution, ΔsolH⦵: the enthalpy change when 1 mol of a solute dissolves in water.
  • e.g. NaCl(s) + aq → Na⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq).
  • Enough water is used that the ions no longer interact with each other.
  • Enthalpy change of hydration, ΔhydH⦵: the enthalpy change when 1 mol of gaseous ions dissolves in water.
  • e.g. Na⁺(g) + aq → Na⁺(aq). Always exothermic.
  • One value per ion, not per compound.
Quick check
?Which equation represents the enthalpy change of hydration of magnesium ions?
Mission 2

The solution cycle

  • Solid → gaseous ions → aqueous ions, or solid → aqueous ions directly.
  • Hess's law: ΔsolH = lattice dissociation enthalpy + ΣΔhydH.
  • If you are given the lattice formation value, change its sign first.
  • Multiply each ΔhydH by the number of those ions in the formula.
Mission 2

Worked example: sodium chloride

  • Data / kJ mol⁻¹: lattice dissociation enthalpy of NaCl +787.
  • ΔhydH⦵: Na⁺ −406, Cl⁻ −378.
  • ΔsolH⦵ = +787 + (−406) + (−378) = +3 kJ mol⁻¹. Slightly endothermic.
  • Measured: about +4 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • A small difference between two big numbers: a 1 % change in either value can flip the sign.
Quick check
?Calculate ΔsolH⦵ of magnesium chloride, MgCl2. Data / kJ mol⁻¹: ΔhydH⦵ Mg²⁺ −1921; ΔhydH⦵ Cl⁻ −378.
  • Lattice dissociation enthalpy of MgCl2: +2526.
Mission check

?A student stirs potassium nitrate, KNO3, into water in an insulated cup. The temperature falls from 21.0 °C to 14.5 °C. Which statement explains the fall?

Every salt pays to pull its ions apart and is repaid as water surrounds them. Which is bigger decides cold (KNO3, NH4NO3) or warm (MgCl2, CaCl2).

Mission 3

Enthalpy alone can't decide

  • Most reactions that happen by themselves are exothermic. Not all.
  • This reaction is endothermic, yet happens at room temperature:
  • Ethanoic acid + ammonium carbonate: 2CH3COOH + (NH4)2CO3 → 2CH3COONH4 + H2O + CO2. Fizzes and cools.
  • So ΔH alone cannot predict feasibility. The missing factor: entropy.
Mission 3

What entropy measures

  • Entropy, S: a measure of the disorder of a system.
  • More ways to arrange the particles and share out their energy: higher entropy.
  • Same conditions: gas > liquid > solid.
Mission 3

Predicting the sign of ΔS

  • Look first at moles of gas: more gas formed → ΔS positive; less gas → negative.
  • Melting, boiling, subliming: ΔS positive. Freezing, condensing: negative.
  • Dissolving an ionic solid: usually positive, as the ions spread through the water. Dissolving a gas: negative.
Quick check
?Which change has a negative entropy change, ΔS, for the system?
Quick check
?Consider the four changes W, X, Y and Z below at 298 K. Which change results in an increase in the entropy of the system? W: 2H2S(g) + O2(g) → 2S(s) + 2H2O(l) (exothermic) · X: NH3(g) → NH3(aq) (exothermic) · Y: 2C(s) + O2(g) → 2CO(g) (exothermic) · Z: N2(g) + 2O2(g) → 2NO2(g) (endothermic)
Mission 3

Calculating ΔS⦵

  • Standard entropies, S⦵, are absolute values: positive for every substance, elements included.
  • ΔS = ΣS(products) − ΣS(reactants), each times its coefficient.
  • Units: J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ (joules, not kJ).
  • Worked: CH4(g) + 2O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2O(l). S / J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹: CH4 186.3, O2 205.2, CO2 213.8, H2O(l) 70.0.
  • ΔS = [213.8 + 2(70.0)] − [186.3 + 2(205.2)] = 353.8 − 596.7 = −242.9 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. As predicted: 3 mol of gas → 1 mol.

Mission check

?Ammonium nitrate, NH4NO3, dissolves in water at room temperature, and the solution gets colder. Which statement best explains why this endothermic change still happens?

Perfume molecules or dissolved ions: spread out, they are far more disordered. So entropy increases, and gathering back is overwhelmingly unlikely.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why does limestone need a kiln at about 900 °C to turn into lime?

Make a guess: Why does limestone need a kiln at about 900 °C to turn into lime?

Mission 4

Two drives: enthalpy and entropy

  • Feasibility depends on ΔH and on TΔS: the entropy change times the temperature.
  • Water freezing: exothermic, but entropy falls. It happens only below 0 °C.
  • Ammonium chloride dissolving: endothermic, but entropy rises. It happens at room temperature.
  • The balance between entropy and enthalpy decides feasibility.
Mission 4

The surroundings count too (background)

  • A reaction also changes the entropy of its surroundings.
  • Heat released spreads into the surroundings: their entropy rises. Heat absorbed: it falls.
  • The same heat matters more at low temperature.
  • You won't calculate this. Gibbs energy (next) includes it for you, using only the system's ΔH and ΔS.
Mission 4

Gibbs energy

  • ΔG = ΔH − TΔS combines both drives in one value.
  • Feasible when ΔG is zero or negative.
  • ΔG says nothing about rate: a feasible reaction can be immeasurably slow.
Mission 4

Worked example: limestone at two temperatures

  • CaCO3(s) → CaO(s) + CO2(g). ΔH = +179.2 kJ mol⁻¹; ΔS = +160.2 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ (see Mission 3).
  • Units first: ΔS = +0.1602 kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. T in kelvin.
  • 298 K: ΔG = 179.2 − 298(0.1602) = 179.2 − 47.7 = +131.5 kJ mol⁻¹. Not feasible.
  • 1200 K: ΔG = 179.2 − 1200(0.1602) = 179.2 − 192.2 = −13.0 kJ mol⁻¹. Feasible.
  • Assumes ΔH and ΔS stay the same at every temperature.
Quick check
?For C2H4(g) + H2(g) → C2H6(g), calculate ΔG at 298 K.
  • ΔH = −136 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS = −120.7 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Answers in kJ mol⁻¹.
Mission 4

When ΔG changes sign

ΔHΔSΔG negative
−+at all temperatures
+−at no temperature
++above T = ΔH/ΔS
−−below T = ΔH/ΔS
  • At the switch-over ΔG = 0, so T = ΔH/ΔS (ΔS in kJ).
  • Limestone: T = 179.2 ÷ 0.1602 = 1118.6 K, about 1119 K (845 °C).
  • Rearrange for any unknown: ΔH = ΔG + TΔS; ΔS = (ΔH − ΔG)/T.
Quick check
?For a reaction, ΔH⦵ is negative and ΔS⦵ is negative. Assuming that neither value changes with temperature, which statement about the reaction is correct?
Mission 4

Reading a graph of ΔG against T

  • ΔG = ΔH − TΔS has the form y = c + mx, with ΔG on y and T on x.
  • Intercept (at T = 0) = ΔH.
  • Gradient = −ΔS.
  • Where the line crosses ΔG = 0: the temperature at which the reaction becomes feasible.
  • Limestone: intercept +179.2 kJ mol⁻¹; gradient −0.1602 kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, so ΔS = +160.2 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
  • A change of state makes the line bend: ΔS changes.
Mission check

?Magnesium carbonate decomposes when heated: MgCO3(s) → MgO(s) + CO2(g). Why must it be heated, and above roughly what temperature does the decomposition become feasible?
  • ΔH = +100.7 kJ mol⁻¹; ΔS = +175.1 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.

Like limestone, the decomposition is entropy-driven: a gas forms from a solid. Heat until TΔS beats ΔH: about 575 K for MgCO3, about 1119 K for CaCO3. These are estimates (constant ΔH and ΔS assumed); kilns run hotter, which also makes the reaction fast.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Born–Haber cycle: elements → gaseous atoms → gaseous ions → solid, equals ΔfH⦵. One unknown, found by Hess's law.
  • Lattice formation is negative; lattice dissociation (breaking) is the same size, positive. State which you mean.

Mission 2

  • Dissolving = break the lattice (energy in) + hydrate the ions (energy out).
  • ΔsolH = lattice dissociation (breaking) enthalpy + ΣΔhydH.

Mission 3

  • Entropy: gas > liquid > solid. More moles of gas → ΔS positive.
  • ΔS = ΣS(products) − ΣS(reactants); S of an element is not zero.
  • Units J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹: convert to kJ before combining with ΔH.

Mission 4

  • ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, ΔS in kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, T in K. ΔG negative: feasible.
  • AQA: feasible when ΔG is zero or negative.
  • Same signs of ΔH and ΔS: temperature decides, switching at T = ΔH/ΔS.
