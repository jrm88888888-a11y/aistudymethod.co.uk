Mission 1

Every reading has an uncertainty

  • No measurement is exact: the uncertainty is the range either side of the reading where the true value probably lies.
  • Record every certain digit plus one estimated digit. These are the significant figures: they show the precision of the instrument.
  • A balance that reads to 0.01 g gives 5.00 g (3 sig figs) or 0.10 g (2 sig figs).
Mission 1

Relative uncertainty

  • Relative uncertainty = uncertainty ÷ measured value (often shown as a %).
  • A mass found by difference uses two readings: about ±0.01 g.
  • 5.00 g: 0.01 ÷ 5.00 = 0.2%. 0.10 g: 0.01 ÷ 0.10 = 10%.
  • Same balance, same ±0.01 g: a smaller quantity has a much bigger relative uncertainty, and fewer significant figures.
Mission 1

Accuracy, precision and errors

  • Accurate: close to the true value.
  • Precise: repeat readings close to each other.
  • Random error: readings scatter either way. Repeats and a mean reduce its effect.
  • Systematic error: every reading shifted the same way (e.g. heat lost to the air, reading the top of the meniscus). Repeats do not remove it: change the method.
Mission 1

How far from the true value?

  • Compare your result with the accepted (data-book) value.
  • Percent error = |experimental − accepted| ÷ accepted × 100.
  • Example: ΔH°comb of ethanol measured as −1020 kJ/mol; accepted value −1367 kJ/mol: 25% out.
  • The apparatus uncertainties add to only about 3%, so most of the gap is systematic: heat lost to the air, incomplete combustion.
Mission 1

Significant figures

  • × and ÷: give the answer to the same number of significant figures as the data value with the fewest.
  • Keep every digit until the final step: round only once.
  • Adding or subtracting: keep the fewest decimal places: 25.0 mL + 1.27 mL = 26.3 mL.
  • Too many figures claims precision you don't have; too few throws precision away.
Quick check
?A student dissolves 2.5 g of NaCl (molar mass 58.5 g/mol) in water and makes the solution up to 250.0 mL. Which value of the molarity, in M, is given to the appropriate number of significant figures?
Mission 1

Drawing and reading graphs

  • Independent variable on the x-axis; label each axis with quantity and unit.
  • Label style: quantity (unit), e.g. volume (mL).
  • Scales: use most of the grid, in easy steps.
  • Line of best fit: straight or a smooth curve, points scattered either side; ignore anomalies.
  • Slope = Δy ÷ Δx from a large triangle, with units. Intercept: where the line crosses the y-axis.
  • Calibration curve (Beer's law): absorbance against concentration of standards gives a straight line; read the unknown's concentration from its absorbance.
Quick check
?A student plots the volume of gas collected against time. The best-fit line is straight, but does not pass through the origin. It passes through (10 s, 14 mL) and (50 s, 62 mL). What is the slope, in mL/s?
Mission check

?A student mixes two solutions in a polystyrene cup to find an enthalpy of reaction. The thermometer has an uncertainty of ±0.5 °C per reading, and the temperature rises by only about 4 °C. Which change would most reduce the relative uncertainty in the temperature change, ΔT?

Same balance or thermometer, same absolute uncertainty. Make the quantity you measure bigger, and the uncertainty becomes a smaller share of it.

Mission 2

Choosing glassware

  • Volumetric pipet (one mark): delivers one fixed volume with a very small uncertainty.
  • Volumetric flask (one mark): contains one exact volume: for making solutions.
  • Buret (marked all the way): delivers any volume, read to small divisions.
  • Graduated cylinder: quick and approximate.
  • One mark = one job, done precisely. Many marks = flexible, less precise per reading.
  • Typical uncertainties: 25 mL pipet ±0.06 mL; 250 mL flask ±0.3 mL; 100 mL graduated cylinder ±1.0 mL (class B).
Mission 2

Titration technique

  • Rinse the pipet and buret with the solutions they will hold (not water).
  • Pipet: use a pipet bulb; fill until the bottom of the meniscus sits on the mark, at eye level. Let it drain; touch the tip to the liquid surface; don't blow out the last drop.
  • Buret: remove the funnel; run liquid through the tap to clear air bubbles; read the bottom of the meniscus at eye level.
  • Erlenmeyer flask on a white tile; add a few drops of indicator; swirl.
  • Add dropwise near the end point. Do a rough titration, then repeat until titers are concordant.
  • Read the buret to 0.01 mL, estimating between the 0.1 mL marks.
Mission 2

Making a standard solution

  • Weigh the solid by difference: weigh the container full, tip the solid into a beaker, reweigh the container.
  • Dissolve in a little distilled water in the beaker.
  • Pour into a volumetric flask through a funnel; rinse the beaker and funnel into the flask.
  • Add distilled water until the bottom of the meniscus is on the mark: the last part dropwise, at eye level.
  • Stopper and invert several times to mix.
Quick check
?A student needs to add exactly the same volume of sodium hydroxide solution to each of four Erlenmeyer flasks, ready for titration. Which piece of apparatus should the student use?
Mission 2

Measuring gas, mass, time and temperature

  • Gas syringe: collects gas directly; good for gases that dissolve in water.
  • Over water: gas pushes water out of an upturned buret or graduated cylinder. Not for very soluble gases (e.g. NH3, HCl).
  • Mass loss: flask on a balance; the gas escapes; a cotton plug stops spray.
  • Time with a stopwatch; temperature with a thermometer or a temperature probe.
  • Gas collected over water also contains water vapor: subtract the water's vapor pressure to get the gas's partial pressure.
Mission 2

Hazards and control measures

  • Hazard: what could cause harm. Risk: the chance of harm, given how you use it.
  • Corrosive / irritant: eye protection, gloves; clean up spills at once.
  • Flammable: no naked flames; water bath or electric heater.
  • Toxic (or harmful vapor): smallest amounts, fume hood.
Quick check
?A student must heat a reaction mixture containing ethanol (highly flammable, boiling point 78 °C) for 30 minutes. Which plan best controls the risk?
Mission 2

Planning an investigation

  • Independent variable: what you change. Dependent variable: what you measure.
  • Control variables: what you keep the same, and how (e.g. a water bath for temperature).
  • Choose a sensible range and number of values; plan repeats.
  • Write numbered steps, with apparatus chosen for the precision needed.
  • Say how a change to the procedure would change the measured result, and why.
Mission 2

Evaluating a method

  • Find the limitation: which step causes error, and in which direction?
  • Systematic? Fix the method; repeats won't help.
  • Random? Repeat and average, or use more precise apparatus.
  • Gas lost before the bung goes in → keep the reactants apart until sealed (e.g. a small tube of acid inside the flask, then tip).
  • Heat lost from a cup → lid and insulation, or extrapolate a cooling curve.
  • Small readings → measure larger quantities, to cut % uncertainty (see Mission 1).
  • Each improvement must match a specific limitation.
Mission 2

Gravimetric analysis

  • Dissolve the sample; add excess of a reagent that precipitates only the target ion (e.g. AgNO3 for Cl⁻ → AgCl(s)).
  • Filter; wash the precipitate with distilled water; dry to constant mass; weigh.
  • Example: 0.500 g sample → 1.150 g AgCl (143.32 g/mol) → 0.008024 mol Cl⁻ → 0.2845 g Cl → 56.9% Cl by mass.
  • Precipitate still wet → mass too high → % Cl too high.
  • Some precipitate lost through the filter, or slightly soluble → % Cl too low.
Quick check
?A student reacts pure calcium carbonate chips with excess hydrochloric acid and collects the carbon dioxide over water in an upturned graduated cylinder. The acid is tipped in from a small tube only after the stopper is fitted. In every repeat, the volume of gas is lower than calculated. Which improvement deals with the most likely cause?
Mission check

?Before a redox titration, a student must add about 10 mL of dilute sulfuric acid to each flask. The acid is in excess, and its exact volume does not affect the result. Which apparatus is the best choice?

Match the apparatus to the job. One-mark glassware for exact volumes that matter; a cylinder when "about" is enough.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Relative uncertainty = uncertainty ÷ value; significant figures show precision.
  • Repeats reduce random error; only a better method removes systematic error.

Mission 2

  • Volumetric pipet and flask: one exact volume. Buret: precise variable volume. Cylinder: quick and approximate.
  • Control each hazard with a measure that fits it; fix systematic errors by changing the method.
