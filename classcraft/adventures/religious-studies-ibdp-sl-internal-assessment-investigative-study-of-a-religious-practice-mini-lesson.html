Mission 1

The task

  • Part 3 of the course: an investigative study of a religious practice, experience or belief, of a group or of individual believers.
  • About 20 teaching hours; worth 25% of the final grade.
  • Written up as a written analysis of 1,500–1,800 words. Only the first 1,800 words are assessed.
  • Title, citations, references and appendices do not count towards the words.
  • A coversheet gives the title and the word count.
Mission 1

Five headings, five criteria

  • The written analysis uses five headings; each matches one assessment criterion:
  • A. Rationale and preliminary research: why this topic; what you read first.
  • B. Plan for study: your focused question, scope and method.
  • C. Summary of significant findings: what you found, by observation, interview and/or participation.
  • D. Critical reflection and evaluation: what the findings mean; limits of your study.
  • E. References and compliance with format: consistent referencing; word limit; headings used.
  • Use the headings exactly: a marker looks under each one for that criterion.
Mission 1

From topic to question

  • Start broad, then narrow: religion → practice → one community → one question.
  • Example: Sikhism → langar → the langar at one gurdwara → "How do volunteers at one gurdwara understand sewa when they serve in the langar?"
  • A good question is:
  • Focused: one practice, one group, manageable in 1,800 words.
  • Researchable: you can reach people, places and sources.
  • About meaning, not only facts: it invites explanation of what the practice means to believers.
  • Open: you do not already know the answer.

Mission 1

Criterion A: rationale and preliminary research

  • Rationale: why the topic matters and why it interests you (for example, a practice you have seen but not understood).
  • Preliminary research: what you read before fieldwork, to understand the practice and its background.
  • Use a wide range of sources: scholarly works and, where relevant, scripture.
  • Justify your sources: say why each is useful and how far it can be trusted.
  • Preliminary research also tests whether the topic is workable; your teacher approves the topic.
Mission 1

Primary and secondary sources

  • Primary: first-hand evidence you gather: interviews, your own observation, participation (where welcome), questionnaires, photographs (with permission), the community's own materials.
  • Secondary: other people's study of the practice: academic books, journal articles, scholarly encyclopaedias, documentaries.
  • Scripture is primary evidence of a tradition's teaching, but believers may read it in many ways.
  • Use both: primary sources give the insiders' voice; secondary sources give context and a check.
  • Judge each source: who made it, for whom, and from inside or outside the tradition?
Quick check
?A student is studying how one mosque's members experience Ramadan. Which of these is a primary source for the study?
Mission 1

Criterion B: plan for study

  • State the research question clearly and narrowly.
  • Outline the scope: which community, which practice, what is left out.
  • Outline the method: whom you will interview, what you will observe or take part in, which sources you will read, and in what order.
  • Show how the plan answers the question: each step should gather evidence for it.
  • Keep the plan realistic: a few good interviews beat a large careless survey.
Mission 1

Fieldwork with respect

  • Permission: contact the community leader first; explain your project.
  • Informed consent: interviewees know what the study is for and that they may stop at any time. Under-18s need a parent's or guardian's consent.
  • Customs: dress, head covering, removing shoes, when to be silent; ask before taking photographs or recording.
  • Privacy: use first names or pseudonyms; store notes safely.
  • Accuracy: report views fairly; check quotations with the speaker if you can.
  • Safety: tell your teacher and family where you are going; do not go alone to meet strangers.
Mission 1

The method: study fairly

  • Use the course's phenomenological approach:
  • Epoché (bracketing): set aside your own judgements while you gather evidence.
  • Empathy: try to see the practice as it means to the believer.
  • Bracketing is not agreeing: it is describing first, then analysing.
  • Prepare open questions ("What does this mean to you?") rather than leading ones ("Don't you find this restrictive?").
  • Ninian Smart's dimensions (ritual, narrative, experiential, doctrinal, ethical, social, material) can help you notice what to ask about.

Mission 2

Criterion C: significant findings

  • Findings come from observation, interview and/or participation.
  • Significant means: it helps answer your question. Not everything you saw or heard is significant.
  • Summarise and organise by theme, not in the order things happened.
  • Use short, accurate quotations from interviewees, with their (pseudonymous) name and role.
  • Link each finding back to your rationale and plan: show the plan produced it.
Mission 2

From notes to themes

  • Read through all your notes; mark recurring ideas.
  • Group them into two to four themes.
  • Example (langar question): theme 1 "serving as worship"; theme 2 "equality: everyone sits together"; theme 3 "learning by doing".
  • Under each theme: the evidence (quotation or observation), then what it shows.
  • Note disagreements: two volunteers who see sewa differently are a finding, not a problem.
Quick check
?A student's notes from a gurdwara visit include: the time she arrived; the colour of the carpets; a volunteer saying "I serve because it removes my ego"; and the menu. Her question is about how volunteers understand sewa. Which is the most significant finding?
Mission 2

Linking findings to concepts

  • Analysis connects what believers say and do to the concepts of their tradition.
  • Example: "removes my ego" → haumai; sewa as part of the Sikh path to mukti.
  • Compare with secondary sources: does the community's practice match the textbook account? If not, why?
  • Avoid two errors: pure description (no concepts) and pure textbook (no findings).
  • Each paragraph: finding → concept → what it shows about the question.
Mission 2

Criterion D: critical reflection

  • Reflect on the findings in relation to the question: what is your answer, and how strong is the evidence?
  • Show how the study deepens understanding of the practice, experience or belief.
  • Identify misconceptions: ideas you (or textbooks, or the public) held that the findings corrected.
  • Identify inconsistencies: where sources disagree, or where your plan or materials let you down.
  • Judge the study's success: did the method answer the question?
  • Suggest further research: what would you ask next, or of whom?
Mission 2

Judging your evidence

  • Sample: how many people, and how typical? Three volunteers at one gurdwara cannot speak for all Sikhs.
  • Reliability: did interviewees give the answer they thought you wanted? Did a leader choose whom you met?
  • Your position: insider or outsider? Each sees some things more clearly and misses others.
  • Bias: did your own expectations shape what you noticed, despite bracketing?
  • Secondary sources: scholarly or popular? From inside or outside the tradition?
  • Recognising limits is a strength: it shows you know the difference between evidence and proof.
Quick check
?Which sentence shows the strongest critical reflection?
Mission 2

Criterion E: references and format

  • Use one standard referencing style (for example, author–date) and keep it consistent.
  • Reference every source you use, including interviews ("Interview with volunteer A, gurdwara, March 2026").
  • Stay within the word limit: anything beyond 1,800 words is not assessed, and the format criterion is affected.
  • Use the five headings exactly.
  • Appendices (interview questions, consent forms) do not count towards the words, but markers are not obliged to read them: put what matters in the text.
Mission 2

Drafting and honesty

  • Your teacher may comment on one full draft, but will not correct or edit it heavily.
  • After that, the final version is yours alone.
  • Academic honesty: every quotation, idea and image from someone else is acknowledged.
  • Do not invent or tidy interview quotations; quote what people said, or paraphrase fairly.
  • Respect: thank the community; offer them a copy if they would like one.
Mission 2Worked answer

One strong paragraph

Question: "How do volunteers at one gurdwara understand sewa when they serve in the langar?"

  • Finding: two of three volunteers described serving as "removing my ego"; the third stressed "feeding everyone equally".
  • Concept: links to haumai (ego), which Sikhs believe separates them from Waheguru, and to the equality of the langar.
  • Secondary check: matches textbook accounts of sewa as selfless service; the equality theme was stronger than expected.
  • Reflection: a small sample, chosen by one committee member; interviewing younger volunteers would test whether the emphasis differs by age.
Mission check

?A student studying what Shabbat means to one Jewish family finds that the parents describe it as "a gift of rest", while the teenage son says it is "mostly rules I have to keep". What should she do with this in her write-up?

Findings become analysis when they are selected for the question, linked to concepts and judged honestly, limits included.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The investigative study: one practice, experience or belief; 1,500–1,800 words; five headings (A rationale and preliminary research, B plan, C findings, D reflection and evaluation, E references and format); 25%.
  • A good question is focused, researchable, about meaning and open.
  • Plan primary and secondary sources, fieldwork with consent and respect, and a fair, phenomenological method.

Mission 2

  • Criterion C: select significant findings, organise them by theme, quote accurately, and link them to the question and plan.
  • Criterion D: reflect on what the findings show, name misconceptions and inconsistencies, judge reliability and success, and suggest further research.
  • Criterion E: consistent references (interviews included), the five headings, and no more than 1,800 words.
