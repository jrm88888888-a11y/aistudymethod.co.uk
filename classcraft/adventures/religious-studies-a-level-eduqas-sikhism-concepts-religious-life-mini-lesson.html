Mission 1

Nam: the Name

  • Nam (EDQ: Naam) = the Name of God; more than a word: God's presence and revealed nature in all creation.
  • All creatures, worlds and realms are upheld by the Name (Sukhmani, Guru Granth Sahib 284, paraphrase).
  • So your spec calls Naam a controlling force: the power that orders and sustains everything.
  • To remember the Name is to become aware of God everywhere.
  • Forgetting the Name is the root of the human problem.
Mission 1

The names of God

  • Waheguru (Wonderful Lord): the Gurmantar.
  • Satnam (True Name), Akal Purakh (Timeless Being), Nirankar (Formless), Kartar (Creator).
  • The Gurus also use Hari, Ram, Gobind, Allah, Khuda: names from Hindu and Muslim use, for the one God of all.
  • Guru Arjan: the tongue speaks names drawn from God's deeds; Satnam is God's primal name (Guru Granth Sahib 1083, paraphrase).
  • Philosophical point: every name is a window on God, but no name contains God.
Mission 1

Naam Japo and the 5 Shabads

  • Naam Japo: remembering God by repeating and focusing the mind on the Name: simran, kirtan, Nitnem.
  • Your spec: the "quintessence of Sikh spirituality".
  • The 5 Shabads: scripture speaks of five "sounds" (panch shabad) of a heavenly, unstruck (anhad) music heard within when the mind is absorbed in God.
  • Sikhs read this as God as Word: the divine is experienced as sound and Word, not as an image.
  • IB link: this is Sikh religious experience: inward, disciplined, often musical.
Mission 1

Hukam: God's will and order

  • Hukam = command, order, will.
  • Everything comes to be by hukam; no one is outside it (Japji pauri 2, paraphrase).
  • The way to God is to walk in hukam (Japji pauri 1, paraphrase).
  • Understanding hukam ends haumai: you stop seeing yourself as the centre.
  • Accepting God's will (bhana) is not fatalism: Sikhs still work, choose and resist injustice.
Mission 1

Nadar: grace

  • Nadar = God's gracious glance; also called kirpa and, in the Mool Mantar, Gurprasad.
  • By karma comes the body (this birth); by grace comes the gate of liberation (Japji pauri 4, paraphrase).
  • Grace is a gift: it cannot be earned or bought by ritual.
  • But Sikhs are not passive: they meditate, serve and live honestly, and pray for grace.

Mission 1

The five khands

Japji pauris 34–37 (Guru Granth Sahib 7–8, paraphrase): five realms of the soul's progress.

  • Dharam Khand: realm of duty: the earth as a place to act rightly; every deed is judged.
  • Gian Khand: realm of knowledge: awe at the vastness of creation.
  • Saram Khand: realm of spiritual endeavour (or beauty): the mind and understanding are reshaped.
  • Karam Khand: realm of grace: power, filled with God.
  • Sach Khand: realm of truth, where the Formless One dwells.
Mission 1Read the source

Saram Khand and Karam Khand

Japji pauris 36–37 (Guru Granth Sahib 7–8, paraphrase):

  • Saram Khand: its language is beauty; there, something matchless is fashioned: intuition, intellect and understanding are shaped. It cannot be described.
  • Karam Khand: its language is power; only the spiritually brave dwell there, filled with God; they do not die and are not deceived.

Q1 In Saram Khand, what is reshaped?

Show answer

The person's inner life: intuition, intellect, understanding.

Q2 Why is Karam Khand called the realm of grace?

Show answer

Mission 1

Stages or dimensions?

  • Ladder reading: five stages climbed in order, duty first, truth last.
  • Dimensions reading: five aspects of the spiritual life, present together and deepening.
  • Both readings agree: grace is at work throughout; no stage is earned by ritual.
  • Sach Khand is not a place in the sky: it is union with the Formless One.
  • Spec focus: Saram Khand (endeavour) and Karam Khand (grace) as the hinge of the path.
Mission 1Evaluate

"Karam Khand is the most important of the five stages."

  • For: without grace no one is liberated; Japji: by grace comes the gate of liberation (pauri 4, paraphrase).
  • For: Gurprasad in the Mool Mantar puts grace at the start of Sikh belief.
  • Against: Sach Khand, union itself, is the goal; the others serve it.
  • Against: Dharam Khand grounds everything in right action; without duty the path never begins.
  • Against: if the khands are dimensions, not a ladder, ranking them misreads Japji.
  • Judge it by: ladder or dimensions; the balance of effort and grace; what most shapes Sikh practice.
Mission check

Effort and grace are partners: the Sikh walks in hukam and remembers the Name, and receives union as a gift.

Mission 2

The soul: a spark of the divine

  • Sikhs believe each person has a soul (atma, or jot: light).
  • The soul is a spark of Waheguru's light: O my mind, you are the embodiment of divine light; know your origin (Guru Granth Sahib 441, paraphrase).
  • Your spec: the soul is ethereal and non-material: it is not the body and does not die with it.
  • Every person, of any religion, caste or sex, carries the same light.
  • The soul's goal: to return to and unite with its source.
Mission 2

Haumai: the self at the centre

  • Haumai = "I, I": ego, self-centredness.
  • It makes the self feel separate from God and others, and hides the divine light.
  • Scripture calls haumai a chronic disease, but says its cure lies within it too (Guru Granth Sahib 466, paraphrase): the same self that turns inward can turn to God.
  • Haumai is the root of the five thieves.
Mission 2

Maya: the passing show

  • Maya = attachment to the world as if it were the final reality.
  • Mainstream Sikh teaching does not call the world unreal or evil: it is God's creation and good.
  • When scripture calls the world "false" or a dream, it means passing, not non-existent.
  • The problem is clinging: to wealth, status, family, pleasure, as though they last.
  • Maya feeds haumai; haumai clings to maya.
  • Sikh image: the world is like a dream or a passing shade; enjoy it, but do not be owned by it.
Mission 2

Manmukh: facing the self

  • Manmukh = "facing one's own mind": self-willed, led by haumai.
  • Marked by the five thieves (panj chor): kaam (lust), krodh (anger), lobh (greed), moh (attachment), ahankar (pride).
  • The manmukh forgets the Name and stays in the cycle of rebirth (see Mission 3).
  • Not a fixed class of people: anyone can live as a manmukh, and anyone can turn.
Mission 2

Gurmukh: facing the Guru

  • Gurmukh = "facing the Guru": God-centred, led by the Guru's word.
  • Marked by the five virtues: sat (truth), santokh (contentment), daya (compassion), nimrata (humility), pyaar (love).
  • Lives in the world: family, work, service, remembering the Name.
  • The goal of Sikh life is to move from manmukh to gurmukh, by effort and grace.

Mission 2

God and the soul: monist or monotheistic?

  • Monist reading: ultimately only God is real; the soul is God's light, like a drop in the ocean or a ray of the sun. Union = merging.
  • Monotheistic reading: God is the one Creator, distinct from creatures; the soul is created. Union = loving closeness, like bride and Beloved.
  • Evidence for monism: images of light in all, drop and ocean.
  • Evidence for monotheism: God as personal, Creator, Judge; scripture addresses God as "you"; grace given by one to another.
  • Many Sikhs say the Gurus hold both: one reality, yet a relationship of love.
Mission 2Evaluate

"The relationship between God and the soul in Sikhism is monist."

  • For: the soul is a spark of the divine light; liberation is described as merging, light into light.
  • For: Ik Onkar: nothing exists outside the One.
  • Against: scripture's personal language (father, mother, husband) needs two parties.
  • Against: grace, prayer and judgement assume a God distinct from the soul.
  • Judge it by: which images dominate; whether the distinction is ultimate or temporary; whether "monist" and "monotheistic" are Western categories that do not fit Sikh thought.

Mission 3

Mission 3

The journey of the soul

  • Samsara: the cycle of birth, death and rebirth (Sikh phrase: aavan jaan, coming and going).
  • The soul passes through many life forms: traditionally 8.4 million (chaurasi lakh joon).
  • Human birth is rare and precious: the one chance to meet God.
  • You have been given this human body; this is your turn to meet the Lord of the universe (Guru Granth Sahib 12, paraphrase).
  • Your spec: the transmigration of the soul.
Mission 3

Karma

  • Karma = action and its consequences, carried from life to life.
  • By karma comes the body, this birth (Japji pauri 4, paraphrase).
  • Deeds, good and bad, are "read out" before Dharam, the righteous judge (Japji closing verse, paraphrase).
  • Karma is not fate: present choices matter, and a person can turn from manmukh to gurmukh.
  • Karma works within hukam, God's order.
Mission 3

Grace beyond karma

  • By karma comes the body; by grace (nadar), the gate of liberation (Japji pauri 4, paraphrase).
  • Karma explains where a soul is; it cannot by itself set the soul free.
  • Liberation is God's gift: Gurprasad.
  • Your spec: ignorance (forgetting God, haumai) is replaced by spiritual enlightenment, brought about by God's grace.
  • So good deeds are not a payment for liberation; they are the life of someone open to grace.
Quick check
?A Sikh is born into a poor family. A classmate says: "That is his karma, so he must accept that his life is fixed." Which response best reflects Sikh teaching?
Mission 3

Mukti: liberation

  • Mukti = liberation from the cycle of rebirth.
  • For Sikhs it is union with God: the soul's light merging with the divine light, or dwelling in Sach Khand.
  • Jivan mukti: liberation while still alive: the jivan mukt lives in the world, free of haumai, absorbed in the Name.
  • Heaven and hell are not the goal: many Sikhs see them as states of separation or nearness to God.
  • Your spec: union with God is the meaning and purpose of Sikh life.
Mission 3Read the source

The purpose of a human life

Guru Granth Sahib 12 (Rehras, Guru Arjan, paraphrase):

  • You have been given this human body: this is your chance to meet God.
  • Other works are of no use; join the company of the holy (sadh sangat) and meditate only on the Name.
  • Your spec also lists Adi Granth 1127, 905 and 275 for union with God as life's purpose.

Q1 Why is human birth described as a "chance"?

Show answer

Only in a human life can the soul meet God and be liberated.

Q2 What two things does the passage say to do?

Show answer

Join the holy congregation; meditate on the Name.

Mission 3Diversity

How Sikhs read rebirth today

  • Most Sikhs and the mainstream tradition take rebirth literally: the soul really transmigrates.
  • Some modern Sikh writers read rebirth language mainly as states of life now: a manmukh life is "death"; nearness to God is "life".
  • Many hold both: rebirth is real, and the cycle is also felt in this life.
  • All agree the present life is where the change happens.
  • Comparison: Hindu traditions also seek moksha, by several paths (knowledge, action, devotion); the Sikh path centres on the Name and grace and rejects renunciation for the householder life.

Mission 3

How belief shapes a Sikh's life

  • Honesty and service: actions have consequences; Sikhs work honestly and serve others (seva).
  • Urgency without fear: human life is precious, so the Name is remembered now, not later.
  • Equality: birth is not a rank of worth; Sikhs reject caste hierarchy.
  • Humility: liberation is grace, so no one can boast of earning it.
  • Hope: no one is beyond God's grace.
Mission 3Evaluate

"Beliefs about rebirth and mukti are no longer relevant for Sikhs today."

  • For: many young Sikhs, especially in the diaspora, focus on identity, seva and justice more than on rebirth.
  • For: a symbolic reading lets the teaching work without belief in literal transmigration.
  • Against: mukti is the stated purpose of Sikh life; Nitnem, amrit and nam simran all point to it.
  • Against: the belief shapes ethics now: honesty, humility, equality.
  • Judge it by: what "relevant" means (believed, practised, or shaping action); diaspora vs Punjab; literal vs symbolic readings.
Mission check

?Two Sikh brothers, Harpreet and Jasvir, argue about a teacher who is honest, humble and devoted to the Name. Harpreet says: "He has earned liberation by his good karma." Jasvir says: "He may already be liberated, by God's grace, even while he lives among us." Which is closer to Sikh teaching?

Karma explains where the soul is; grace sets it free, and Sikhs believe that freedom can begin in this life.

Mission 4

Three pillars

  • Nam japna: remember God's Name.
  • Kirat karni: earn an honest living by your own work.
  • Vand chhakna: share what you have with others.
  • Rooted in Guru Nanak's community at Kartarpur: prayer, farming and shared meals.
  • Your spec spells them Naam Japo, Kirat Karo, Vand Chhako.
Mission 4

Nam japna: remembering the Name

  • Keeping God in mind all the time, not only at set prayers.
  • Forms: simran (repeating "Waheguru"), Nitnem (daily prayers), kirtan (sung hymns), amrit vela (early-morning meditation).
  • Purpose: to overcome haumai and become gurmukh (see Mission 2).
  • The other two pillars come from this remembrance: work and sharing done with God in mind.
Mission 4

Kirat karni: honest work

  • Earn by honest effort: no theft, cheating, bribery or exploiting others.
  • Guru Nanak rejected begging and renunciation as a path: the Sikh is a householder (grihasti).
  • Janamsakhi story: Guru Nanak eats with Bhai Lalo, a poor carpenter, rather than at the feast of Malik Bhago, a rich official. Squeezed, Lalo's bread gives milk; Bhago's gives blood: wealth gained by oppression.
  • Work is not a distraction from God; done honestly, it is worship.
Mission 4Read the source

Earn, give, remember

Guru Granth Sahib (paraphrase):

  • One who works for what he eats, and gives some of what he has: Nanak says, he knows the path (GGS 1245, Guru Nanak).
  • Make the effort and earn, and enjoy it; meditate, and meet God, and anxiety ends (GGS 522, Guru Arjan).

Q1 Which two pillars appear in the first verse?

Show answer

Kirat karni, earning by work; vand chhakna, giving.

Q2 Which pillar does the second verse add to effort?

Show answer

Nam japna: meditating on God.

Mission 4

Vand chhakna: sharing

  • Share wealth, food, time and skill, especially with those in need.
  • Dasvandh: giving a tenth of income (Guru Arjan organised it; the Rehat Maryada commends it).
  • Langar: free food for all, of any faith or caste: sharing made visible.
  • Sarbat da bhala: "the welfare of all", prayed for at the end of every Ardas.
  • Sharing cuts lobh (greed) and moh (attachment), two of the five thieves.

Mission 4

How the three fit together

  • Kirat in relation to Naam: work is done while remembering God, so it is free of greed and dishonesty.
  • Kirat in relation to Vand: only what is honestly earned can truly be shared.
  • Karma: honest work and sharing are good actions; but liberation is not bought by them.
  • Grace: the three pillars are a life open to nadar, not a wage for it.
  • Sangat: shared earnings fund the gurdwara and langar; the community lives from its members' kirat.
  • Your spec links Kirat Karo to Guru Gobind Singh: the Khalsa discipline kept honest work and dasvandh as duties (rooted in Guru Nanak's teaching).
Mission 4Diversity

Different emphases

  • Some Sikhs, often in devotional dera traditions, put nam japna first: long hours of meditation and kirtan.
  • Others put social action first: campaigns for justice, relief work, langar on the streets.
  • Diaspora pressures: long working hours, secular workplaces, debt and consumer culture make balance harder.
  • Debate: is giving a tenth still realistic? Many give time and skill as well as money.
  • All agree the three belong together; they differ on balance, not on the principle.
Quick check
?Why does Sikh teaching say the three pillars cannot "earn" liberation?
Mission 4Evaluate

"Nam japna is more important than kirat karni and vand chhakna."

  • For: remembrance of the Name is the root: without it, work and sharing can feed haumai (pride in giving).
  • For: the five khands and mukti centre on the Name and grace.
  • Against: Guru Nanak's own life at Kartarpur joined all three; scripture links them (GGS 1245, 522).
  • Against: remembrance that never reaches work or sharing is the yogis' withdrawal Guru Nanak rejected.
  • Judge it by: whether the three can be ranked or are one way of life; the relation of spiritual and material values; what Sikhs today actually do.
Mission check

?A young Sikh software engineer is offered a high salary by a company that designs online gambling apps. She turns it down for a lower-paid job, keeps up her morning simran, and volunteers at a weekend langar. Which teaching most directly explains turning down the job?

Remember, earn honestly, share: for Sikhs, everyday working life is where the path to God is walked.

Mission 5

What sewa means

  • Sewa (also spelled seva): selfless service, to God and to others.
  • Your spec spells it sewa.
  • Sikhs see no line between the two: God (Waheguru) is present in every person, so serving people is serving God.
  • Sewa is done freely, with no wish for reward, praise or return.
  • It belongs with the three pillars (see Mission 4): remember God, work honestly, share.
  • Your spec: Naam Japo, Kirat Karo, Vand Chhako.
Mission 5

Sewa in the Guru Granth Sahib

  • Guru Nanak: serve in this world, and you will be given a seat of honour in God's court (Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 26, Sri Rag; paraphrase).
  • Guru Arjan, in the Sukhmani: the one who serves without desire for reward (nishkam) reaches the Lord (Ang 286; paraphrase).
  • So sewa is not a side-activity: it is part of the path to mukti (liberation).
  • But Sikhs hold that liberation comes finally by God's grace (nadar): sewa prepares the self; it does not buy salvation.
Mission 5

Three forms of sewa

  • Tan (body): physical service. Cooking and serving langar, washing dishes, cleaning the gurdwara, caring for the sick, wiping pilgrims' shoes.
  • Man (mind): mental service. Teaching, sharing knowledge, reciting and explaining gurbani, counselling, offering skills.
  • Dhan (wealth): material service. Giving money and goods; many Sikhs aim to give dasvandh, a tenth of their income.
  • Sikh tradition often ranks tan sewa highly: it is personal, cannot be paid for by someone else, and is the most humbling.
  • Spec wording: physical service, mental service, material service.
Mission 5

The state of mind: sewa against haumai

  • Haumai: the ego, "I-am-ness"; self-centredness that keeps the soul apart from God.
  • A manmukh (self-willed person) serves to be seen. A gurmukh (God-centred person) serves because self has given way to God.
  • So the intention matters more than the act: selfless and desire-less action.
  • Sewa done for status or reputation feeds haumai; done humbly, it loosens it.

Bhai Gurdas and the serving Sikh

  • Bhai Gurdas (c. 1551–1636): scribe of the first Adi Granth (1604); his Vars (ballads) are approved for recital in the gurdwara and called "the key to the Guru Granth Sahib".
  • Varan 6:12 (paraphrase): the Gursikh is praised for humble service of the sangat with his own hands, done in love and humility rather than pride.
  • Spec link: sewa is learned and practised in the community of believers.

Q1 Why does the Var stress the Sikh's own hands?

Show answer

Tan sewa is personal and humbling; it cannot be handed to others or bought.

Q2 Which Sikh virtue does the Var set against pride?

Show answer

Humility: sewa is a remedy for haumai.

Mission 5

Bhai Ghanaiya: service without barriers

  • Bhai Ghanaiya (d. 1718), a Sikh of Guru Gobind Singh, carried water to the wounded at Anandpur, Sikh and Mughal alike.
  • Accused of helping the enemy, he said he saw no friend or foe: he saw the Guru in every face (tradition).
  • The Guru praised him and gave him ointment to dress wounds as well.
  • Sewa recognises no barriers of religion, caste or race.
  • Ghanaiya is remembered as founder of the Sewapanthi order; Sikh first-aid and relief groups today often name him.
  • Modern examples: langar open to all; Sikh relief charities serving disaster areas regardless of faith.
Mission 5

Sarbat da bhala: sewa for everyone

  • Every Ardas (the congregational prayer) ends asking for sarbat da bhala: "the welfare of all".
  • Sewa is the practical side of that prayer: not only for Sikhs.
  • Langar shows it: anyone may eat, sitting together, whatever their religion, caste, gender or wealth.
  • So sewa shapes personal morality: humility, equality, generosity, compassion.
Quick check

Mission 5Evaluate

"Sewa is the most important Sikh teaching."

  • For: it puts every other belief into action: one God in all, equality, sarbat da bhala.
  • For: it is the visible mark of Sikh identity: langar and Sikh relief work are what many non-Sikhs know of Sikhism.
  • For: it attacks haumai directly, the main obstacle to mukti.
  • Against: without nam simran (remembering God's Name in meditation), sewa becomes social work; Gurbani puts remembering the Name at the centre.
  • Against: liberation is by grace (nadar); no practice, sewa included, earns it.
  • Against: sewa depends on kirat karni (honest work): you can only share what you have honestly earned.
  • Practicality today: busy working lives and distance from a gurdwara limit tan sewa; but man and dhan sewa, online teaching and relief charities widen it.
  • Judgement criteria: what Gurbani stresses most; whether one teaching makes the others possible; what shapes Sikh identity in practice.

Mission 6

Miri and piri

  • Piri: spiritual authority, from pir (a holy teacher).
  • Miri: temporal (worldly, political) authority, from mir (a ruler).
  • Miri-piri: the two belong together. Faith is not only private prayer; it must also act in the world, defending justice.
  • Spec wording: miri (temporal) and piri (spiritual).
Mission 6

Guru Hargobind's two swords

  • 1606: Guru Arjan, the fifth Guru, dies in Mughal custody in Lahore.
  • His son Guru Hargobind (1595–1644) becomes the sixth Guru.
  • At his installation he wears two swords: one of miri, one of piri.
  • He builds the Akal Takht ("Throne of the Timeless One") facing the Harmandir Sahib: a seat of temporal authority beside the seat of worship.
  • He keeps horses and armed followers; the Sikhs fight defensive battles against Mughal forces.
  • Many Sikhs see this as Arjan's own wish: the community must be able to protect itself.
Mission 6

Original or new?

  • Continuity view: miri-piri was present from Guru Nanak, who criticised unjust rulers and taught a faith lived in society. Hargobind made visible what was there.
  • Development view: Nanak's path was peaceful and devotional; armed resistance arose from historical pressure after Arjan's death.
  • Both views agree the Gurus never taught a purely private religion.
Mission 6

The saint-soldier: Sant Sipahi

  • Guru Gobind Singh (1666–1708), tenth Guru, founds the Khalsa in 1699.
  • Ideal: the sant sipahi, the saint-soldier.
  • Sant duties: nam simran, sewa, honest work, self-discipline, humility.
  • Sipahi duties: courage, readiness to defend the weak and the faith, discipline in arms.
  • The saint comes first: the soldier is only legitimate when controlled by a saintly mind free of anger, greed and ego.
  • Spec wording: Sant Sipahi (saint-soldier).

Mission 6

Dharam and the Khalsa's rules

  • Dharam: righteousness, moral and religious duty; the right order God wills.
  • The sant sipahi fights only for dharam: to defend it, never for gain, revenge or land.
  • Khalsa discipline restrains the soldier: the Khalsa vows, no harm to the innocent, respect for women and prisoners, no looting.
  • These rules apply the general principle of Dharam Yudh ("war for righteousness"): criteria in Mission 7.
  • So the Khalsa's arms are bound by the same dharam as its prayer.
Mission 6Read the source

The Zafarnama

  • 1705: Guru Gobind Singh writes the Zafarnama ("Letter of Victory"), in Persian, to the emperor Aurangzeb, after Mughal commanders broke sworn oaths of safe passage at Anandpur.
  • Verse 22 (paraphrase): when every other means has failed, it is right to take up the sword.

Mission 6

Teg and deg

  • Teg: the sword: protecting the poor and oppressed.
  • Deg: the cooking pot: feeding the hungry (langar).
  • Together: Sikhs meet material and spiritual needs: food for the body, protection for the vulnerable, the Name for the soul.
  • The phrase "Deg Teg Fateh" ("victory to the pot and sword") appears on the seal of Banda Singh Bahadur (from 1710) and on Sikh coins from 1764.
  • Spec link: Sikh liberationist thinking: faith means defending the poor and oppressed.
  • Today: langar and Sikh relief work are deg; standing up for others' rights is teg.
Mission 6

The kirpan

  • One of the 5Ks worn by amritdhari (initiated) Sikhs.
  • A common explanation of the word: kirpa (mercy, grace) + aan (honour, dignity).
  • It is a religious article of faith, not a weapon for attack: a reminder of the duty to defend justice and the weak.
  • Sizes vary: from a full sword in ceremonies to a small blade worn under clothes.
  • Many Sikhs treat it with great respect and do not draw it in anger.

Mission 6

The kirpan in Britain

  • A religious and ethnic minority must negotiate how to live its faith under national law.
  • In England and Wales, carrying a blade in public is an offence, but the law allows a defence for religious reasons (Criminal Justice Act 1988, s.139).
  • Issues Sikhs have faced: kirpans in schools, airports, courts and workplaces; some institutions allow a small or sheathed kirpan, some do not.
  • Sikh responses differ: some accept a smaller or symbolic kirpan; others insist it must be a real, usable blade.
  • Spec issue: the relationship between miri and piri in Sikh daily life.
Mission 6Evaluate

"It is impossible to combine spirituality with the use of force."

  • For: force tends to feed anger and ego (haumai), the very thing spiritual life overcomes.
  • For: many spiritual traditions (e.g. Jain ahimsa, Christian pacifists, Gandhi) hold that true spirituality renounces force.
  • For: in many traditions, "righteous war" claims have been made for causes others reject.
  • Against: the sant sipahi ideal subordinates force to the saint: only to protect others, as a last resort.
  • Against: refusing to defend the oppressed may itself be a moral failure; Guru Tegh Bahadur died defending others' freedom of religion (see Mission 7).
  • Against: the Khalsa's rules and Dharam Yudh's criteria limit force in a way pure "warrior" cultures do not.
  • Judgement criteria: is force truly a last resort? who decides when dharam is threatened? can inner detachment really survive battle?

Mission 7

Peace comes first

  • Sikh teaching begins with peace: one God (Waheguru) is present in every person.
  • Guru Arjan: no one is my enemy, and no one is a stranger; I get along with all (Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1299; paraphrase).
  • The goal is sarbat da bhala, the welfare of all, prayed for in every Ardas.
  • Inner peace (freedom from anger and ego, haumai) comes before outer peace.
  • So war is never good in itself: at most it can be necessary.
Mission 7

Dharam yudh: war for righteousness

  • Dharam yudh: a war fought for dharam (righteousness, justice), never for gain.
  • Conditions usually given (drawn from the Gurus' example and the Zafarnama):
  • Last resort: every peaceful means has failed.
  • Just cause: to defend the oppressed or freedom of faith; never revenge, conquest or land.
  • Minimum force; fighting stops when the enemy stops.
  • No harm to civilians; no looting; property taken is returned.
  • Disciplined soldiers: fighters must be sant sipahi, acting without hatred.
  • Spec wording: Dharam Yudh (just war); the Khalsa's rules apply this general principle.

Mission 7

Guru Tegh Bahadur: dying for others' freedom

  • 1675, Delhi: the ninth Guru is executed under the emperor Aurangzeb.
  • Kashmiri Hindu Brahmins (Pandits), facing forced conversion, had asked for his help.
  • He died defending another community's right to its own faith.
  • Sikhs call him Hind di Chadar, "the shield of India"; Gurdwara Sis Ganj in Delhi marks the place.
  • Significance: Sikh martyrdom is about freedom of conscience for all, not only for Sikhs.
Mission 7Evaluate

"Dharam yudh can still justify Sikhs using force today."

  • For: the principle is timeless: oppression and persecution still exist, and the sant sipahi must protect the weak.
  • For: the criteria (last resort, just cause, minimum force) are strict enough to prevent misuse.
  • Against: modern war cannot meet the criteria: weapons cannot protect civilians or be limited.
  • Against: in a democracy, non-violent action, law and courts are always "other means".
  • Against: who may apply the label is disputed: in the 1980s Punjab conflict, Sikhs disagreed sharply over whether armed militancy met the criteria.
  • Judgement criteria: who has authority to declare dharam yudh? can the criteria be met by a modern state army or only by individuals? what do the Gurus' examples actually show?
  • Spec issue: whether it is possible to combine spirituality with the use of force (see Mission 6).

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Nam is God's Name and presence; hukam is God's will and order; nadar is grace, God's gift.
  • The five khands of Japji (duty, knowledge, endeavour, grace, truth) map the soul's progress; Saram Khand and Karam Khand pair effort with grace.
  • Naam Japo, the names of God and the five shabads express God as Name and Word.

Mission 2

  • The soul is a spark of Waheguru's light; haumai (ego) and maya (clinging) hide it.
  • Manmukh: self-facing, ruled by the five thieves. Gurmukh: Guru-facing, shaped by the five virtues, living in the world.
  • Sikhs debate whether God and the soul are one (monist) or distinct in love (monotheistic).

Mission 3

  • The soul passes through many lives (samsara); human birth is the precious chance to meet God.
  • Karma shapes birth, but liberation (mukti), union with God, comes by grace (nadar), and can begin in this life (jivan mukti).
  • Belief in karma and mukti shapes Sikh ethics; Sikhs differ on literal and symbolic readings of rebirth.

Mission 4

  • Nam japna (remember the Name), kirat karni (earn honestly) and vand chhakna (share) form one gurmukh way of life, rooted in Guru Nanak's Kartarpur.
  • The pillars are good karma and a life open to grace, but grace is not earned; they sustain the sangat.

Mission 5

  • Sewa (seva) is selfless service to God and to others; Sikhs see no line between the two.
  • Three forms: tan (physical), man (mental), dhan (material); intention matters most: desire-less action that loosens haumai.
  • Sewa knows no barriers of religion or caste (Bhai Ghanaiya; sarbat da bhala) and is central to Sikh identity.

Mission 6

  • Miri (temporal) and piri (spiritual) belong together: Guru Hargobind's two swords and the Akal Takht facing the Harmandir Sahib.
  • Guru Gobind Singh's Khalsa lives the ideal of the sant sipahi (saint-soldier): force only for dharam, as a last resort, under saintly control.
  • Teg and deg: protect the oppressed and feed the hungry; the kirpan is an article of faith reminding Sikhs of that duty.

Mission 7

  • Peace and sarbat da bhala come first; dharam yudh allows force only as a last resort, for justice, with minimum force and no harm to civilians.
