Henry VIII & his Ministers 1509–40

This mini-lesson walks you through the Edexcel Henry VIII and his ministers, 1509–40 depth study: Henry's early reign, the rise and fall of Cardinal Wolsey, the King's Great Matter, the work of Thomas Cromwell, the Break with Rome, and the Dissolution of the Monasteries with the rebellion it sparked.

The early reign · 1509

A new young king

In 1509 the 17-year-old Henry VIII became King of England. Soon after, he married Catherine of Aragon — the widow of his elder brother Arthur, who had died in 1502.

The young Henry wanted glory and reputation. His early reign was marked by:

  • a glittering Renaissance court of music, learning and display;
  • a wish to be a great warrior king — leading to war with France;
  • a hunger for a strong male heir to secure the Tudor dynasty.

Key idea: Henry loved glory but had little patience for daily government. He relied on able ministers — first Wolsey, then Cromwell — to run the country for him.

Cardinal Wolsey

The rise of Wolsey

Thomas Wolsey rose from humble origins to become the most powerful man in England after the king. He was made Lord Chancellor in 1515 and a Cardinal of the Church.

Wolsey ran both domestic and foreign policy. At home he reformed the legal system, using the Court of Chancery and the Star Chamber to give cheaper, fairer justice — especially to the poor against powerful nobles.

Wolsey · foreign policy

Diplomacy and display

Wolsey wanted to make Henry a leading figure in Europe:

  • Treaty of London (1518) — a grand peace treaty between the major European powers, arranged by Wolsey to put England at the centre of diplomacy.
  • Field of the Cloth of Gold (1520) — a spectacularly lavish meeting between Henry VIII and Francis I of France, full of feasting, jousting and display of wealth.

Watch out: the Field of the Cloth of Gold looked magnificent but achieved little lasting alliance — soon afterwards England was at odds with France again. It was more about show than substance.

Wolsey · failures

Where Wolsey went wrong

For all his power, Wolsey made serious mistakes:

  • The Amicable Grant (1525) — a tax Wolsey tried to raise (without Parliament) to pay for war. It caused protests and a revolt, and had to be abandoned.
  • Above all, Wolsey failed to secure the king's annulment — the "King's Great Matter". This was the failure that destroyed him.

Remember: the Amicable Grant damaged Wolsey's reputation, but it was the failure over the annulment that actually brought him down.

The King's Great Matter

The annulment crisis

By the late 1520s Henry was desperate. His marriage to Catherine of Aragon had produced only a daughter, Mary — no surviving son. Henry feared for the Tudor dynasty, and he had fallen in love with Anne Boleyn.

Henry wanted the Pope to grant an annulment — a ruling that his marriage to Catherine had never been valid — so he could marry Anne. This became known as the "King's Great Matter".

Why the Pope said no

The Pope refuses

The Pope was Clement VII. Normally a king could hope for an annulment — but this time the Pope refused. The key reason was politics, not just religion.

  • Catherine of Aragon's nephew was Charles V, the powerful Holy Roman Emperor.
  • In 1527 Charles V's troops had sacked Rome — the Pope was effectively under Charles's control.
  • The Pope dared not insult Charles by declaring his aunt's marriage invalid.

Key link: the annulment failed not because Henry's case was hopeless, but because Charles V controlled the Pope and Catherine was Charles's aunt.

Wolsey's fall · 1529–30

The fall of Wolsey

Because he could not deliver the annulment, Wolsey lost the king's favour. In 1529 he was stripped of his position as Lord Chancellor and fell from power.

  • He was later arrested for treason.
  • On his way to face the king in London, Wolsey died in 1530.

Cause and effect: Wolsey's whole career rested on serving the king's wishes. The one wish he could not grant — the annulment — was the one that ended him.

Thomas Cromwell

The rise of Cromwell

After Wolsey's fall, Thomas Cromwell rose to become Henry's chief minister. Where Wolsey had failed by relying on the Pope, Cromwell had a bold new plan: get the annulment through Parliament and English law, not Rome.

Cromwell became the architect of the break with Rome — the man who turned Henry's problem into a revolution in Church and state.

Key contrast: Wolsey tried to win the annulment from the Pope; Cromwell got it by cutting England off from the Pope altogether.

The Break with Rome · 1533–34

Breaking from the Pope

Cromwell used the Reformation Parliament (from 1529) to pass laws that broke England away from the authority of Rome:

  • Act in Restraint of Appeals (1533) — stopped legal appeals going to the Pope in Rome, so English courts had the final say.
  • Thomas Cranmer, the new Archbishop of Canterbury, declared Henry's marriage to Catherine annulled in 1533. Henry married Anne Boleyn the same year.
  • Act of Supremacy (1534) — made Henry Supreme Head of the Church of England, completely breaking from the Pope.

Thomas More, Henry's former Lord Chancellor, refused to accept the king as head of the Church and was executed in 1535.

Dissolution of the Monasteries · 1536–40

Closing the monasteries

Now Supreme Head of the Church, Henry — through Cromwell — turned on the monasteries. Between 1536 and 1540 Cromwell organised the Dissolution of the Monasteries: closing them down and seizing their land and wealth for the Crown.

  • Monasteries were rich and owned much land — a huge prize for a king short of money.
  • Their treasures, buildings and estates passed to the Crown, which sold much of it on.

Why it mattered: the Dissolution made Henry far wealthier, wiped out centres of loyalty to Rome — but caused deep anger, especially in the more traditional North.

The Pilgrimage of Grace · 1536

Rebellion in the North

The religious changes and the Dissolution sparked the largest rebellion of Henry's reign. In 1536 tens of thousands rose up in the North of England in the Pilgrimage of Grace, led by the lawyer Robert Aske.

  • The rebels wanted the monasteries restored and the religious changes reversed.
  • Henry made promises to buy time, then crushed the rebellion. Robert Aske and other leaders were executed.

Significance: the Pilgrimage of Grace showed how unpopular the changes were — but its failure showed the Crown was now too strong to be forced to back down.

Cromwell's fall · 1540

The fall of Cromwell

Like Wolsey before him, Cromwell fell when he displeased the king. He arranged Henry's marriage to Anne of Cleves in 1540 — a match Henry disliked almost at once.

  • The failed marriage, plus enemies at court, turned Henry against him.
  • Cromwell was arrested and executed in 1540.
  • (Anne Boleyn had already been executed in 1536, before Cromwell's own fall.)

Pattern to remember: both of Henry's great ministers, Wolsey (1529–30) and Cromwell (1540), rose very high — and both fell suddenly when they lost the king's favour.

Exam skill

Sources and interpretations

In the exam you handle sources and interpretations about Henry's reign. Two quick habits win marks:

  • Ask who made it and why (provenance). A source written by a supporter of the Pope and one written for the king will judge the Break with Rome very differently.
  • Use your own knowledge to judge it. Support or challenge a source with facts you know (dates, ministers, events).

Interpretations differ: some historians see Cromwell as a brilliant, principled reformer; others as a ruthless operator. Being aware that interpretations vary — and why — is exactly what the exam rewards.

Recap

The essentials to remember

Early reign: Henry becomes king 1509; marries Catherine of Aragon; wants glory, war with France, a Renaissance court and a male heir.

Wolsey: Lord Chancellor 1515 & Cardinal; legal reform (Chancery, Star Chamber); Treaty of London 1518; Field of the Cloth of Gold 1520; Amicable Grant 1525; falls 1529, dies 1530 — for failing the annulment.

The Great Matter: Henry wants Catherine annulled to marry Anne Boleyn; Pope Clement VII refuses (Charles V controls Rome).

Cromwell & the Break with Rome: Reformation Parliament; Act in Restraint of Appeals 1533; Cranmer annuls the marriage 1533; Act of Supremacy 1534; Thomas More executed 1535.

Dissolution & rebellion: Dissolution of the Monasteries 1536–40; Pilgrimage of Grace 1536 (Robert Aske). Cromwell executed 1540.
