Mission 1

Three words: Sunnah, Hadith, Sira

  • Sunnah ("trodden path", custom): the Prophet's example: what he said, did, and silently approved.
  • Hadith ("report", plural ahadith): a single report of one saying or action. Hadiths are how the Sunnah is known.
  • Sira ("way of going", biography): the story of the Prophet's life, told in order.
  • Muslims see the Qur'an as God's word; the Sunnah as the Prophet's lived explanation of it.
  • Eduqas wording: sunna (clear trodden path), hadith (report).

Mission 1

Anatomy of a hadith

  • Every hadith has two parts.
  • Isnad (chain, "support"): the names of those who passed the report on, back to the Prophet: "A told me that B told him that C heard the Prophet say…".
  • Matn (text): the words or action reported.
  • Scholars test both: is every link in the chain known, trustworthy and able to have met the next? Does the text fit the Qur'an and well-established Sunnah?
  • The study of narrators ('ilm al-rijal, "knowledge of the men") produced huge biographical dictionaries.
  • Hadith qudsi ("sacred report"): God's words in the Prophet's phrasing. It is not Qur'an: it is not recited in salah as Qur'an.

Mission 1

Grading a hadith

  • Sahih (sound): unbroken chain of trustworthy, accurate narrators; no hidden defect; not contradicting stronger reports.
  • Hasan (good): as sahih, but a narrator's memory is slightly less exact. Still used in law.
  • Da'if (weak): a break in the chain or an unreliable narrator. Many allow it only for encouraging good deeds, not for law.
  • Mawdu' (fabricated): invented. Rejected.
  • By number of chains: mutawatir (mass-transmitted, by so many that error is ruled out) and ahad (single or few chains: most hadiths).
  • Your spec: categories "in relation to the overall reliability of text and chain of transmission".
Mission 1

From memory to collections

  • 632 CE: the Prophet dies. Companions remember and teach his words; some write notes.
  • 8th century: reports are gathered; fabrication (for politics, sects, piety) makes scholars insist on chains.
  • 9th century: the great Sunni collections. Muhammad al-Bukhari (810–870) chose only reports he judged sound: Sahih al-Bukhari. With Sahih Muslim (Muslim ibn al-Hajjaj, d. 875) it is the most trusted Sunni book after the Qur'an.
  • Sunni Six Books add Abu Dawud, al-Tirmidhi, al-Nasa'i and Ibn Majah.
  • Shi'a Islam accepts reports from the Prophet and from the Imams of his family, mainly through narrators loyal to them. Its Four Books begin with al-Kafi of al-Kulayni (d. 941).

Mission 1

The Sunnah in daily life and law

  • The Qur'an commands salah; the Sunnah shows how: times, number of units, words, movements. Likewise zakah rates and Hajj rites.
  • So most Muslims treat the Qur'an and Sunnah as one guidance: the Qur'an supreme, the Sunnah explaining and applying it.
  • Everyday Sunnah: eating with the right hand, greeting with "salam", the call to prayer at a baby's birth, kindness to neighbours.
  • Al-Shafi'i (767–820), al-Risala: the "wisdom" taught alongside the Book (2:151) is the Sunnah; a sound hadith binds, even if it has a single chain. This made the Sunnah the second source of law.

Mission 2

Shari'a and fiqh

  • Shari'a: literally "the path to the water": God's way for human life.
  • "Then We put thee on the (right) Way of Religion: so follow thou that (Way), and follow not the desires of those who know not." (Qur'an 45:18, Yusuf Ali) The Arabic word is shari'ah.
  • It covers worship (prayer, fasting) and dealings (family, trade, food, crime): much more than criminal law.
  • Fiqh ("deep understanding"): the human effort to understand and apply the shari'a. Scholars can err; the shari'a, as God's will, cannot.
  • So Muslims distinguish the ideal (God's shari'a) from law in practice (the jurists' fiqh).
Mission 2

Scriptural sources: Qur'an and Sunnah

  • Qur'an: the first source, God's word. Some rulings are clear (no pork, 5:3; fasting in Ramadan, 2:185); many verses give principles (justice, 4:58; consultation, 42:38).
  • Sunnah: the Prophet's example, known through hadith. It explains and adds detail: the Qur'an commands prayer; the Sunnah shows how to pray.
  • "So take what the Messenger assigns to you, and deny yourselves that which he withholds from you." (Qur'an 59:7, Yusuf Ali)
  • Hadith are graded by chain and text: sahih (sound), hasan (good), da'if (weak) (see Mission 1).
  • Shi'a Muslims include the teachings of the Imams within the Sunnah.
  • Eduqas spellings: sunna (clear trodden path), hadith (report).
Mission 2

Non-scriptural sources

  • Ijma (consensus): agreement of the qualified scholars of an age on a ruling. Once reached, Sunnis treat it as binding ("my community will not agree on error", Ibn Majah; paraphrase).
  • Qiyas (analogy): extending a ruling from a case in the texts to a new case that shares its cause ('illah).
  • 'Urf (custom): local practice accepted where it does not contradict the texts (e.g. customary amounts of dowry). Malik also gave weight to the practice ('amal) of Madinah.
  • Other tools: istihsan (juristic preference, Hanafi), maslahah (public interest, Maliki).
  • Twelver Shi'a jurists: Qur'an, Sunnah (with the Imams), ijma (valid if it reflects the Imam's view) and 'aql (reason); they reject qiyas.

Mission 2

Al-Shafi'i: a method for law

  • Muhammad ibn Idris al-Shafi'i (767–820), al-Risala ("The Epistle").
  • Before him, regional scholars (Kufa, Madinah) mixed hadith, local practice and personal opinion (ra'y) in different ways.
  • He set out an order of sources: Qur'an → Sunnah → ijma → qiyas.
  • A sound hadith binds, even if reported by a single chain, because the Sunnah explains the Qur'an (2:151: "Scripture and Wisdom", Yusuf Ali).
  • He limited free opinion and istihsan: reasoning must be tied to a text through qiyas.
  • So he is called the founder of usul al-fiqh (the roots, or method, of jurisprudence).
  • Your spec: "the work of Al-Shafi in devising a methodology for fiqh (deep understanding) of shari'a".
Mission 2

The law schools (madhhabs)

  • Hanafi: Abu Hanifa (699–767), Kufa; wide use of reasoning and istihsan; Turkey, Central and South Asia (most British Muslims of South Asian heritage).
  • Maliki: Malik ibn Anas (c. 711–795), Madinah; al-Muwatta; the practice of Madinah; North and West Africa.
  • Shafi'i: al-Shafi'i; Egypt, East Africa, South-East Asia.
  • Hanbali: Ahmad ibn Hanbal (780–855), Baghdad; text first, cautious about reasoning; Saudi Arabia, Qatar.
  • Ja'fari: Ja'far al-Sadiq (d. 765), the sixth Imam; Twelver Shi'a; Iran, Iraq.
  • They agree on essentials; they differ in detail (e.g. hand position in prayer). Each recognises the others as legitimate.
  • Taqlid: ordinary Muslims follow a school or scholar; ijtihad: qualified jurists derive rulings within its framework.
Mission 2

The five rulings (ahkam)

  • Fard / wajib (obligatory): rewarded if done, sin if left: salah, fasting in Ramadan.
  • Mustahabb / mandub (recommended): rewarded if done, no sin if left: extra prayers, sadaqah.
  • Mubah (permitted): neutral: most foods, ordinary work.
  • Makruh (disliked): better avoided, no sin if done: e.g. wasting water in wudu; coming to the mosque after eating raw garlic.
  • Haram (forbidden): sin if done: alcohol and gambling (5:90), pork (5:3).
  • Halal: lawful, everything not haram. Modesty: lower the gaze and guard modesty (24:30–31); dress rules derive from these verses and the Sunnah.
  • Killing and eating animals: halal meat is slaughtered with God's name (6:121), a swift cut and draining of blood; scholars differ on pre-stunning.
  • Makruh applied: smoking was long called makruh; as its harm became known, many scholars ruled it haram (harm to self is forbidden).

Mission 2

Interpretation and authority beyond the texts

  • Ulama (scholars), muftis issuing fatwas (non-binding legal opinions), and the schools carry authority beyond the texts.
  • Was the "gate of ijtihad" closed after about the 10th century? A common Sunni view said yes; Wael Hallaq (b. 1955) argues jurists never stopped independent reasoning. Twelver Shi'a always kept ijtihad open.
Mission 2Evaluate

Divine law or human interpretation?

  • Claim: Shari'a as Muslims practise it is more human interpretation than divine law.
  • For: fiqh is human; the schools disagree; rulings change (smoking); applying old rulings to new cases needs interpretation.
  • For: ijma and qiyas are human methods; states have codified and reshaped the law.
  • Against: the sources and principles are divine; scholars discover rulings, they do not invent them.
  • Against: the core (prayer, fasting, the main prohibitions) is agreed across all schools.
  • A further view: the limits of human understanding can lead to tolerance of diversity within Muslim practice.
  • Criteria: Where is the line between shari'a and fiqh? Does diversity weaken authority or show its depth?

Mission 3

Shari'a and fiqh

  • Shari'a: literally "the path to the water"; for Muslims, God's will for human life, perfect and unchanging.
  • Fiqh: literally "understanding"; the human effort to know and apply the shari'a. Fiqh is written by scholars and can be mistaken.
  • Many Muslims hold that the shari'a is the ideal; fiqh is the law in practice.
  • So two scholars can disagree on a ruling without either denying the shari'a.
Mission 3

Ijtihad: effort to find the ruling

  • Ijtihad: from the root j-h-d, "to strive" (the same root as jihad). The jurist's full effort to derive a ruling from the sources.
  • Mujtahid: a scholar qualified to do this: Arabic, Qur'an, Sunna and its chains, earlier consensus, legal method.
  • A widely cited report: the Prophet asked Mu'adh ibn Jabal how he would judge in Yemen. Qur'an, then Sunna, and if neither answered, he would strive to form his own judgement (Abu Dawud; paraphrase; its chain is debated).
  • A judge who strives and is right has two rewards; one who strives and errs has one reward (Bukhari, Muslim; paraphrase).
  • So honest effort is rewarded; error made in good faith is not sin.
Mission 3

Taqlid and the madhhab

  • Taqlid: following a qualified scholar's ruling without examining all the evidence yourself (literally "imitation").
  • Most Muslims are not mujtahids, so most practise taqlid, usually of a madhhab (school of law).
  • Sunni schools: Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, Hanbali. Twelver Shi'a: the Ja'fari school.
  • Defenders: taqlid is like trusting a doctor; it protects ordinary believers from amateur rulings.
  • Critics: rigid taqlid can freeze the law and repeat past mistakes (more in Mission 4).

Mission 3

Three roles of shari'a

  • Civil law: in some states, parts of shari'a are law (e.g. family law in many Muslim-majority countries).
  • Religious law: rules of worship (prayer, fasting, zakah) that Muslims keep wherever they live.
  • Moral guide: a way of life and conscience, kept voluntarily, e.g. by Muslims in Britain under British law.
  • The mujtahid gives rulings; most believers follow them by taqlid.
  • Eduqas issue: is "law" a misleading word for shari'a, if it is also worship and ethics?

Mission 3

Drugs by qiyas

  • Qiyas: analogy. Four parts:
  • Original case (asl): wine, forbidden by text.
  • New case (far'): e.g. cannabis, heroin, cocaine.
  • Effective cause ('illa): intoxication, which clouds the mind and keeps people from remembering God.
  • Ruling (hukm): the new case shares the cause, so it shares the ruling: haram.
  • Medical use differs: many jurists permit anaesthetics and pain relief, as necessity (2:173) and not for intoxication.

Mission 3

Usury and banking

  • The Qur'an forbids riba (usury): "Allah hath permitted trade and forbidden usury" (Qur'an 2:275, Yusuf Ali).
  • "Devour not usury, doubled and multiplied" (Qur'an 3:130, Yusuf Ali).
  • Ijtihad question: is all bank interest riba, or only exploitative, multiplying interest?
  • Majority view: any fixed, guaranteed return on a loan is riba (major fiqh councils in the 20th century).
  • Minority view: some scholars allowed fixed returns on modest savings, or defined riba as exploitation of the needy.
  • Response: Islamic banking shares profit and risk instead of charging interest, e.g. murabaha (cost-plus sale), ijara (leasing), mudaraba (profit-sharing).
Mission 3

Tobacco: from dispute to consensus

  • Tobacco reached Muslim lands around 1600. No text names it.
  • Early jurists disagreed: haram (it harms, wastes money, may intoxicate); makruh (it smells, like raw garlic); mubah (no proof of harm).
  • 20th century: scientific findings showed smoking causes cancer, heart disease and addiction.
  • Principle: there is to be no harm and no returning harm (Ibn Majah; Malik; paraphrase); "make not your own hands contribute to (your) destruction" (Qur'an 2:195, Yusuf Ali).
  • So a growing consensus: many fatwa bodies now call smoking haram; others still say makruh.
  • The texts did not change; new knowledge changed the ijtihad.
Mission 3Evaluate

Does human interpretation get in the way?

  • Claim: Human interpretation impairs understanding of the shari'a.
  • For: fiqh is fallible; scholars of one era carry its customs and blind spots into rulings.
  • For: disagreement between schools can confuse believers about what God wants.
  • Against: without ijtihad, the shari'a could not speak to heroin, banks or tobacco at all.
  • Against: the limits of interpretation make room for tolerance of diversity: the reward for honest error (Bukhari, Muslim).
  • Judge it by: Is fiqh a barrier to shari'a or the only way to it? Who is qualified to interpret? Do new sciences make ijtihad more or less reliable?
  • Eduqas issue: the extent to which human interpretation may impair understanding of shari'a.

Mission 4

Sunni Islam: the "gate of ijtihad"

  • By about the 10th century the four Sunni schools were settled.
  • Many later jurists said that absolute ijtihad (founding a new method from the sources) had ended: the "closing of the gate of ijtihad".
  • Scholars after that were ranked: absolute mujtahid · mujtahid within a school · jurist who rules on cases · muqallid (follower by taqlid).
  • But ijtihad within the schools went on: new fatwas on trade, new lands, tobacco.
  • Some scholars always rejected rigid taqlid, e.g. Ibn Taymiyya (1263–1328).
  • Wael Hallaq argued in 1984 that the gate was never closed in theory or practice ("Was the Gate of Ijtihad Closed?").
Mission 4

Shi'a Islam: ijtihad never closed

  • Twelver Shi'a hold that during the Occultation of the twelfth Imam, qualified jurists act as his general deputies.
  • So ijtihad is a continuing duty. The Usuli school, dominant since the late 18th century, insists on it.
  • Every believer who is not a mujtahid must follow a living mujtahid by taqlid: a marja' al-taqlid ("source of imitation"), a grand ayatollah, e.g. Ali al-Sistani in Najaf.
  • Each marja' publishes a book of rulings; believers may pay khums through his office.
  • The Akhbari minority distrusted ijtihad and relied on the reports (akhbar) of the Imams.
  • Shi'a jurists use reason ('aql) as a source in place of open-ended qiyas.

Mission 4Diversity

Sunni and Shi'a compared

  • Who may do ijtihad? Sunni: qualified scholars; many classical jurists said only within a school. Shi'a: qualified mujtahids, always.
  • Whom do lay believers follow? Sunni: a madhhab, often through a local scholar or mufti. Shi'a: one living marja'.
  • Dead scholars? Sunni: founders long dead are still followed. Shi'a (Usuli): most say a believer may not begin taqlid of a dead mujtahid.
  • Methods: Sunni: Qur'an, Sunna, ijma, qiyas. Shi'a: Qur'an, Sunna (including the Imams), ijma, 'aql.
  • Shared: both forbid unqualified people from ruling; both accept honest disagreement.
Mission 4

Rashid Rida: back to the sources

  • Muhammad Rashid Rida (1865–1935), born in Ottoman Syria, worked in Cairo; student of Muhammad Abduh; editor of the journal al-Manar (1898–1935).
  • A Salafi reformer: return to the Qur'an, the authentic Sunna and the salaf (the first generations).
  • Attacked blind taqlid and loyalty to one madhhab, which he blamed for Muslim decline.
  • Distinguished worship (fixed by text) from social dealings (mu'amalat), where ijtihad based on public interest (maslaha) may make new rules.
  • Such rules should be made by qualified scholars and leaders in consultation (shura).
  • Wanted a renewed caliphate and shari'a as the law of Muslim states.
Mission 4

An-Na'im: reform and a secular state

  • Abdullahi Ahmed An-Na'im (b. 1946), Sudanese-born professor of law at Emory University.
  • Toward an Islamic Reformation (1990): building on his teacher Mahmoud Mohamed Taha, he argues the universal message of the Makkan Qur'an should guide law today, rather than Madinan rules suited to the 7th century.
  • So ijtihad must reach even areas with clear texts (classical fiqh said: no ijtihad where a clear text exists).
  • Aim: equality of women and non-Muslims, and human rights, from within Islam.
  • Islam and the Secular State (2008): if the state enforces shari'a, obedience is no longer free and religious; the state should be neutral, and Muslims follow shari'a by conviction.

Mission 4Evaluate

Are the doors of ijtihad closed?

  • Claim: The doors of ijtihad are closed.
  • For: the four schools already cover almost every question; repeating them protects unity.
  • For: few scholars today master all the sciences a classical mujtahid needed.
  • Against: Shi'a jurists have practised ijtihad without break; Sunni fatwas on IVF, banking and tobacco are ijtihad in all but name.
  • Against: reformers from Ibn Taymiyya to Rida and An-Na'im argue Islam requires it; Hallaq argues the gate never closed.
  • Judge it by: What counts as ijtihad: founding a method, or applying one? Who is qualified? Is "closed" a historical claim or a recommendation?

Mission 5

Jihad means striving

  • Jihad: from j-h-d, "to strive, to make an effort". Not the word for "holy war".
  • "And those who strive in Our (cause),- We will certainly guide them to our Paths" (Qur'an 29:69, Yusuf Ali).
  • Forms of jihad: of the self (nafs); of the tongue and pen (speaking truth, teaching); of wealth (giving); and armed struggle.
  • "Strive in His cause as ye ought to strive" (Qur'an 22:78, Yusuf Ali).
  • Tradition: greater jihad (al-jihad al-akbar): struggle with the self; lesser jihad (al-jihad al-asghar): armed struggle.
Mission 5

Greater jihad: the nafs

  • Nafs: the self, the ego with its desires.
  • Three states named from the Qur'an: the soul inciting to evil (12:53); the self-reproaching soul, conscience (75:2); the soul at peace (89:27).
  • Greater jihad: lifelong effort to move the self towards peace with God: prayer, fasting, honesty, controlling anger and greed.
  • The Prophet is reported to have said that the true striver (mujahid) is the one who strives against his own self for God (Tirmidhi; paraphrase).
  • A popular report calls returning from battle a return "from the lesser jihad to the greater" (al-Bayhaqi; paraphrase; many scholars grade it weak).

Mission 5

Lesser jihad in the Prophet's time

  • Makkah (610–622): persecution, but no fighting; patience and preaching.
  • 622: the hijrah to Madinah. Permission to fight comes because the Muslims "are wronged" (22:39).
  • Battles: Badr (624), Uhud (625), the Trench (627).
  • 628: Treaty of Hudaybiyyah: a peace accepted on hard terms.
  • 630: Makkah taken almost without fighting; a general amnesty.
  • Jurists made lesser jihad a communal duty of the ummah (fard kifaya), led by legitimate authority; it becomes an individual duty if the land is invaded.

Mission 5

Conditions of lesser jihad

  • Just cause: defence, or ending oppression of the weak (4:75), never forcing faith: "no compulsion in religion" (2:256).
  • Legitimate authority: declared by a recognised leader, not by individuals.
  • Right intention: for God and justice, not gain or revenge.
  • Last resort and proportion: "do not transgress limits" (2:190).
  • Protect non-combatants: the Prophet forbade killing women and children (Bukhari, Muslim; paraphrase); Abu Bakr told his commanders not to kill the old or children, cut fruit trees or destroy buildings (Malik, Muwatta; paraphrase).
  • Stop when the enemy seeks peace (8:61); treat prisoners humanely.

Mission 5Diversity

Defensive, expansionist, suspended

  • Classical Sunni jurists (c. 8th–12th centuries) divided the world into dar al-Islam and dar al-harb and allowed wars of expansion led by the caliph, alongside truces.
  • Many modern Sunni scholars read the Qur'an as permitting defensive war only; expansion reflected the empires of its time.
  • Twelver Shi'a: most jurists hold that offensive jihad needs the presence of the infallible Imam, so it is suspended during the Occultation; defensive jihad remains.
  • Sufi teachers stress the inner jihad above all.
  • Ahmadiyya Muslims teach that armed jihad for religion is not permitted today; jihad of the pen is the duty of the age.
  • Eduqas: the historical context of lesser jihad shapes how it is applied now.
Mission 5

Modern warfare and WMD

  • Nuclear, chemical and biological weapons cannot tell soldiers from civilians: they break non-combatant protection.
  • They destroy crops, animals and land for generations: compare the condemnation of one who would "destroy crops and cattle" (2:205).
  • So most scholars hold their use haram; some allow possession only as a deterrent.
  • Iran's Supreme Leader is reported to have issued a fatwa forbidding nuclear weapons; Pakistan holds them for deterrence.
  • Other problems today: who is the "legitimate authority" when Muslims live in many nation-states? Drones and remote war? Wars between Muslim states?
Mission 5

Terrorism is not jihad

  • Terrorism: violence against civilians to spread fear for political goals.
  • Mainstream scholars across Sunni and Shi'a Islam reject calling it jihad: no legitimate authority, civilians targeted, suicide forbidden (4:29).
  • "If any one slew a person - unless it be for murder or for spreading mischief in the land - it would be as if he slew the whole people" (Qur'an 5:32, Yusuf Ali).
  • Examples: the Amman Message (2004–05); Tahir-ul-Qadri's Fatwa on Terrorism and Suicide Bombings (2010); scholars' open letter to the leader of ISIS (2014).
  • Groups that use the word for terror are a small minority, condemned by most Muslim bodies; many of their victims are Muslims.

Mission 5Evaluate

Is lesser jihad still relevant?

  • Claim: The teachings on lesser jihad have no relevance today.
  • For: modern weapons make the conditions almost impossible to meet.
  • For: the misuse of the word by terrorists harms Muslims; some argue the inner jihad should be stressed instead.
  • Against: the conditions are a just-war ethic that can limit modern war: protect civilians, accept peace, use proportion.
  • Against: Muslims still face invasion and oppression; the right of defence (22:39) still matters.
  • Judge it by: Are the principles separable from the 7th-century setting? Who has authority now? Does stressing the greater jihad answer the misuse?
  • Eduqas issues: misunderstandings of the term jihad; the relevance today of the teachings about lesser jihad.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Sunnah is the Prophet's example; Hadith are the reports that carry it, each with an isnad (chain) and matn (text); Sira is his biography.
  • Scholars grade hadiths (sahih, hasan, da'if, mawdu'); al-Bukhari and Muslim compiled the most trusted Sunni collections; Shi'a Muslims have their own Four Books.

Mission 2

  • Shari'a is God's way; fiqh is the human understanding of it. Sources: Qur'an and Sunnah, then ijma, qiyas and custom (Twelvers: reason instead of qiyas).
  • Al-Shafi'i (767–820) set the order of sources; five schools (Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, Hanbali, Ja'fari) apply them.
  • Every act falls under five rulings: obligatory, recommended, permitted, disliked (makruh), forbidden (haram).

Mission 3

  • For Muslims, shari'a is God's ideal; fiqh is human understanding of it, reached by ijtihad and followed by taqlid within a madhhab.
  • Ijtihad in practice: qiyas extends the wine ban to drugs; scholars debate whether bank interest is riba; science moved the ruling on tobacco.
  • Shari'a works as civil law, religious law and moral guide; the mujtahid interprets, most believers follow by taqlid.

Mission 4

  • Classical Sunni jurists spoke of a closed gate of absolute ijtihad; ijtihad within schools continued, and Hallaq argues the gate never closed.
  • Usuli Shi'a: ijtihad is ongoing; believers follow a living marja' al-taqlid.
  • Rida: ijtihad by returning to the salaf, shari'a as state law; An-Na'im: Makkan principles and a neutral state.

Mission 5

  • Jihad = striving: greater (against the nafs) and lesser (armed, under conditions).
  • Lesser jihad in the Prophet's time was a response to persecution and attack, limited by the Qur'an (22:39, 2:190, 8:61) and the Prophet's rules.
  • Most scholars hold WMD and terrorism break the conditions; whether lesser jihad is relevant today is debated.
