Mission 1

Mission 1: Why can a heater burning in a closed tent kill, even with fuel to spare?

Make a guess: Why can a heater burning in a closed tent kill, even with fuel to spare?

Mission 1

Alkanes as fuels: complete combustion

  • Alkanes burn in oxygen, releasing a lot of energy: they are fuels.
  • Complete combustion, in plenty of O2: all C → CO2, all H → H2O.
  • Balance C, then H, then O last.
  • Propane: C3H8(g) + 5O2(g) → 3CO2(g) + 4H2O(l).
  • Odd number of O atoms? Use ½O2, or double everything.
Mission 1

Background: elements burn too

  • Combustion is not only for fuels. Many elements burn in oxygen.
  • Metals form ionic oxides: 2Mg(s) + O2(g) → 2MgO(s); 4Al(s) + 3O2(g) → 2Al2O3(s).
  • Non-metals form covalent oxides: C(s) + O2(g) → CO2(g); S(s) + O2(g) → SO2(g).
  • Always write oxygen as O2.
  • Sulfur in a fuel burns to SO2, and in a hot engine N2 + O2 → 2NO. You'll meet these as pollutants.
Quick check
?When the equation for the complete combustion of octane, C8H18, is balanced with a coefficient of 2 for octane, what is the coefficient of O2?
Mission 1

Burning alcohols

  • Alcohols burn completely to CO2 and H2O, just like alkanes.
  • An alcohol already contains one O atom: subtract it before finding the O2.
  • Ethanol: C2H5OH(l) + 3O2(g) → 2CO2(g) + 3H2O(l).
  • Butan-1-ol: C4H9OH(l) + 6O2(g) → 4CO2(g) + 5H2O(l). Products have 13 O; 1 comes from the alcohol.
Quick check
?What amount of oxygen, O2, is needed to burn 0.500 mol of propan-1-ol, C3H7OH, completely?
Mission 1

Incomplete combustion

  • In limited O2, some carbon forms carbon monoxide, CO, or carbon (soot), C. Hydrogen still forms H2O.
  • To CO: 2C3H8(g) + 7O2(g) → 6CO(g) + 8H2O(l). To C: C3H8(g) + 2O2(g) → 3C(s) + 4H2O(l).
  • Less energy is released than in complete combustion.
  • Glowing soot particles make a yellow, smoky flame. Plenty of air: a blue flame.
  • The higher a fuel's carbon content, the smokier its flame: methane (75% C by mass) burns cleaner than octane (84% C).
Mission 1

Why carbon monoxide kills

  • CO is colourless, odourless and toxic.
  • It binds to haemoglobin in red blood cells far more strongly than O2 does.
  • So the blood carries less oxygen to the body's tissues.
  • You cannot see or smell it: that is why CO alarms save lives.
  • Soot (carbon particulates) also harms: it damages the lungs.
Quick check
?Which equation represents an incomplete combustion of ethanol?
Mission 1

Other pollutants from burning fuels

  • Carbon monoxide and carbon particulates: from incomplete combustion.
  • Unburned hydrocarbons: fuel that escapes burning.
  • Oxides of nitrogen, NOx: N2 and O2 from the air react at the high temperature in an engine. N2 + O2 → 2NO; then 2NO + O2 → 2NO2.
  • Sulfur dioxide, SO2: from sulfur impurities in the fuel. S + O2 → SO2.
  • NOx and SO2 are acidic gases: they dissolve in rain to form acid rain.
Mission 1

Biofuels: the carbon-neutral claim

  • A biofuel is a fuel made from recently living material (biomass).
  • Bioethanol: sugars are fermented by yeast, then the ethanol is separated by fractional distillation.
  • Biodiesel is made from plant oils.
  • Photosynthesis: 6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2.
  • Fermentation: C6H12O6 → 2C2H5OH + 2CO2.
  • Combustion: 2C2H5OH + 6O2 → 4CO2 + 6H2O.
  • 6CO2 taken in; 2 + 4 = 6CO2 given out. So the claim: carbon neutral.
Mission 1

Why the claim fails; other issues

  • Not carbon neutral: fossil fuels power the tractors, make the fertiliser, transport the crop and heat the distillation.
  • For: renewable, as crops regrow in months; their carbon came recently from the air. Fossil fuels are finite.
  • Against: land for fuel crops cannot grow food: higher food prices, an ethical issue.
  • Against: clearing forest for crops releases stored carbon and destroys habitats.
  • Ethanol releases less energy per gram than petrol; engines may need changes for high blends.
Quick check
?Bioethanol is made by fermenting sugar from sugar cane and is burned as a fuel for vehicles. Which statement about bioethanol as a fuel is correct?
Mission check

?After cooking outdoors, campers carry a still-glowing charcoal barbecue into their closed tent for warmth. Charcoal is almost pure carbon. Why is this deadly, even though there is no flame?

Same chemistry as the heater. Too little O2 turns carbon into CO, and CO stops your blood carrying oxygen.

Mission 2

Make a guess: Why does bromine in hexane stay orange in the dark but lose its colour in sunlight?

Mission 2

Radicals

  • A radical is a species with an unpaired electron.
  • A dot marks the unpaired electron: Cl•, CH3•.
  • The dot is not a charge. These radicals are neutral.
  • Odd number of outer electrons → one is unpaired. CH3•: 4 + 3 = 7.
  • Radicals are highly reactive: the unpaired electron pairs up by forming a new bond.
Quick check
?Which statement about the chlorine radical, Cl•, is correct?
Mission 2

Two ways a bond can break

  • Homolytic fission: one electron to each atom → two radicals. Cl–Cl → Cl• + Cl•
  • Heterolytic fission: both electrons to one atom → two ions. H–Cl → H⁺ + Cl⁻
  • The atom that takes the pair becomes negative.
  • Radical reactions use homolytic fission. (Heterolytic fission comes up again in a later mission.)
Mission 2

Background: fish-hook arrows

  • A fish-hook (half) arrow has one barb: it moves one electron.
  • A full curly arrow (two barbs) moves an electron pair.
  • Cl2 splitting: two fish-hooks, each from the Cl–Cl bond to a different Cl atom.
  • Your exam does not require half-arrows here. Write each step as an equation with dots.
Mission 2

Initiation

  • Methane and chlorine don't react in the dark. In UV light they do.
  • Initiation: UV light splits the halogen homolytically: Cl2 → 2Cl•
  • Cl–Cl breaks because it is the weakest bond present: Cl–Cl 243, C–H 413 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Br–Br is weaker still (193 kJ mol⁻¹): even sunlight splits it.
  • Initiation makes radicals.
Quick check
?Methane reacts with bromine in ultraviolet (UV) light. Which equation represents the initiation step?
Mission 2

Propagation: the chain

  • Propagation: radical + molecule → new molecule + new radical.
  • Step 1: CH4 + Cl• → CH3• + HCl (Cl• removes an H atom)
  • Step 2: CH3• + Cl2 → CH3Cl + Cl•
  • Cl• is regenerated, so steps 1 and 2 repeat: a chain reaction.
  • Add the two steps: CH4 + Cl2 → CH3Cl + HCl.
  • No H• ever forms.
Mission 2

Termination

  • Termination: two radicals combine. Radicals are removed.
  • Cl• + Cl• → Cl2
  • CH3• + Cl• → CH3Cl
  • CH3• + CH3• → C2H6 (so a trace of ethane appears)
  • Radicals are scarce, so termination is rare: most product forms in propagation.
Quick check
?2,2-dimethylpropane, C5H12, reacts with chlorine in UV light. Four steps of the mechanism are:
P: C5H11• + Cl2 → C5H11Cl + Cl•
Q: Cl2 → 2Cl•
R: C5H11• + Cl• → C5H11Cl
S: C5H12 + Cl• → C5H11• + HCl
Which sequence gives initiation, then the two propagation steps in the order they happen, then termination?
Mission 2

A mixture of products

  • Cl• attacks CH3Cl too: CH3Cl + Cl• → CH2Cl• + HCl, then CH2Cl• + Cl2 → CH2Cl2 + Cl•.
  • Further substitution goes on to CHCl3 and CCl4.
  • In longer chains, Cl• can remove an H from different positions, giving isomers.
  • So radical substitution is a poor way to make one pure product: a limitation in synthesis.
Mission 2

Back to the hook: bromine in hexane

  • In the dark: no light to split Br2, so no radicals. No reaction; it stays orange.
  • In sunlight: Br2 → 2Br•.
  • C6H14 + Br• → C6H13• + HBr, then C6H13• + Br2 → C6H13Br + Br•.
  • Overall: C6H14 + Br2 → C6H13Br + HBr. Br2 is used up, so the orange fades.
  • Br• can take an H from C1, C2 or C3: 1-, 2- and 3-bromohexane all form.
Quick check
?The products of the reaction of methane with chlorine in UV light include CH2Cl2, CHCl3 and CCl4. How are most of these more highly substituted products formed?
Mission 2

Why alkanes are so unreactive

  • C–C and C–H bonds are strong: breaking them needs a lot of energy.
  • C and H have similar electronegativities: the bonds are almost non-polar.
  • No δ+ or δ− sites, so polar reagents are not attracted.
  • Only very reactive species, such as radicals, attack alkanes.
  • That's why the bromine needs light: light makes the radicals.
Mission check

?A mixture of methane and chlorine does not react in the dark. A single brief flash of UV light splits a few Cl2 molecules, yet many CH3Cl molecules form for each Cl2 split. Which explains this?

Light is needed only to start the chain by splitting the halogen. That's why bromine in hexane fades only in sunlight.

Mission 3: Why does an alkene take bromine's colour away in the dark, when an alkane needs light?

Make a guess: Why does an alkene take bromine's colour away in the dark, when an alkane needs light?

Mission 3

Electrophiles

  • An electrophile is an electron pair acceptor.
  • It is electron-deficient: H⁺, or the δ+ atom of a polar molecule.
  • No charge needed: HBr (through Hδ+) and Br2 act as electrophiles.
  • Heterolytic fission: a bond breaks and both electrons go to one atom, forming two ions.
  • Radical reactions (see Mission 2) use homolytic fission instead: one electron to each atom.
Quick check
?Which set contains only species that can act as electrophiles?
Mission 3

Why alkenes attract electrophiles

  • Alkenes are unsaturated: they contain a C=C bond, so they can add more atoms.
  • Cycloalkenes, such as cyclohexene, are unsaturated too.
  • The C=C bond is a region of high electron density.
  • It attracts electrophiles and donates an electron pair to them.
  • Alkanes have only C–C and C–H σ bonds: no electron-rich site. They react with bromine only when light makes Br• radicals.
  • The reagent adds across C=C. Double becomes single; nothing is lost: electrophilic addition.
Mission 3

The test for C=C

  • Shake with bromine water, at room temperature, in the dark.
  • Alkene: orange → colourless. Alkane: stays orange.
  • With Br2 in an organic solvent: CH2=CH2 + Br2 → CH2BrCH2Br.
  • In bromine water, water also adds and much of the product is BrCH2CH2OH. The colour change is the same.
Quick check
?Ethene is bubbled through a solution of bromine in an organic solvent at room temperature, in the dark. Which statement is correct?
Mission 3

Adding halogens and hydrogen halides

  • Room temperature. Each reagent adds one part to each C=C carbon.
  • CH2=CH2 + Br2 → CH2BrCH2Br, 1,2-dibromoethane.
  • CH2=CH2 + Cl2 → CH2ClCH2Cl, 1,2-dichloroethane.
  • CH2=CH2 + HBr → CH3CH2Br, bromoethane.
  • CH2=CH2 + HCl → CH3CH2Cl, chloroethane.
  • Cyclohexene + HBr → bromocyclohexane.
  • Unsymmetrical alkenes, such as propene, can give two products: a later lesson.
Mission 3

Adding water

  • Steam + an acid catalyst (e.g. H3PO4) adds H to one carbon and OH to the other.
  • CH2=CH2 + H2O → CH3CH2OH, ethanol. This is how ethanol is made industrially.
  • Cyclohexene + H2O → cyclohexanol.
Mission 3

Adding hydrogen

  • Hydrogenation: H2 adds across C=C, with a nickel catalyst.
  • CH2=CH2 + H2 → CH3CH3. Typical conditions: about 150 °C.
  • Margarine: vegetable oils have many C=C bonds. Hydrogenating some of them raises the melting point, so the oil becomes a soft solid.
Mission 3

Oxidising C=C with manganate(VII)

  • Shake an alkene with acidified potassium manganate(VII).
  • Purple → colourless: a second test for C=C.
  • An OH adds to each C=C carbon: a diol.
  • CH2=CH2 + [O] + H2O → HOCH2CH2OH, ethane-1,2-diol. [O] stands for the oxidising agent.
Quick check
?2,3-dimethylbut-2-ene, (CH3)2C=C(CH3)2, reacts with hydrogen bromide. What is the product?
Mission 3

Mechanism: alkene + HBr

  • H–Br is already polar: Hδ+–Brδ−.
  • Arrow 1: from the C=C bond to the Hδ+.
  • Arrow 2: from the H–Br bond to Br. Heterolytic fission: Br leaves as Br⁻.
  • A carbocation forms: a C⁺ with only three bonds.
  • Arrow 3: from a lone pair on Br⁻ to the C⁺. Product: bromoethane.
  • HCl reacts in the same way.
Mission 3

Mechanism: alkene + bromine

  • The electron-rich C=C repels the Br–Br electrons: an induced dipole, Brδ+–Brδ−.
  • Arrow 1: from the C=C bond to Brδ+.
  • Arrow 2: from the Br–Br bond to Brδ−, which leaves as Br⁻.
  • A carbocation forms. Arrow 3: from a lone pair on Br⁻ to the C⁺. Product: CH2BrCH2Br.
  • No light and no radicals: the C=C itself splits Br2.
  • Cl2 and other given binary compounds (e.g. ICl) add the same way: the δ+ atom bonds first.
Quick check
?Ethene reacts with hydrogen bromide by electrophilic addition. Which describes the curly arrows in the first step of the mechanism?
Mission check

?In the dark, at room temperature, but-2-ene quickly decolourises a solution of iodine monochloride, ICl. Butane does not react with it. The product is CH3CHICHClCH3. Which statement explains why but-2-ene reacts without light?

The electron-rich C=C pulls an electrophile towards it and splits it into ions. So bromine (or ICl) adds in the dark. An alkane has no such site: it waits for light to make Br• radicals.

Mission 4

Mission 4: What does acid do to an alcohol that lets its OH group break away?

Make a guess: What does acid do to an alcohol that lets its OH group break away?

Mission 4

Alcohols from alkenes and steam

  • Hydration: water adds across C=C.
  • C2H4(g) + H2O(g) ⇌ C2H5OH(g)
  • Catalyst: phosphoric acid, H3PO4, on a solid support.
  • Typical industrial conditions: about 300 °C, about 6–7 MPa.
  • Only about 5% converts per pass; unreacted ethene is recycled.
  • Unsymmetrical alkenes give two alcohols: propene → mainly propan-2-ol.
  • Continuous process; very pure ethanol; but ethene comes from crude oil (finite).
Mission 4

Biofuels and the carbon-neutral claim

  • Biofuel: a fuel made from biological material that has grown recently (biomass).
  • Bioethanol comes from fermenting crop sugars; biodiesel from plant oils.
  • Photosynthesis: 6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2 (6CO2 taken in)
  • Fermentation: C6H12O6 → 2C2H5OH + 2CO2 (2CO2 out)
  • Combustion: 2C2H5OH + 6O2 → 4CO2 + 6H2O (4CO2 out)
  • On paper: 6CO2 in, 6CO2 out. So it is called carbon neutral.
Mission 4

Why the claim fails

  • Fossil fuels are burned to farm, make fertiliser, transport and distil the ethanol.
  • So net CO2 is lower than for petrol, but not zero.
  • Land for fuel crops can push up food prices (food vs fuel).
  • Deforestation for crops releases stored carbon and destroys habitats.
  • vs fossil fuels: biofuels are renewable; fossil fuels are finite.
  • Ethanol gives less energy per gram than petrol.
Quick check
?A fuel company says its bioethanol, made by fermenting sugar from sugar cane, is "carbon neutral". Which statement best evaluates the claim?
Mission 4

Why acid lets the OH go

  • OH⁻ is a strong base: it is a poor leaving group. The C–O bond will not break on its own.
  • H⁺ bonds to a lone pair on the O: –OH becomes –OH2⁺.
  • Now the C–O bond breaks and a neutral, stable H2O molecule leaves.
  • Then either:
  • a neighbouring C loses H⁺ → alkene (elimination)
  • a halide ion bonds to the C → halogenoalkane (substitution)
Mission 4

Dehydration: alcohol → alkene

  • Heat the alcohol with concentrated H3PO4 (or conc H2SO4).
  • C2H5OH → CH2=CH2 + H2O
  • Cyclohexanol → cyclohexene + H2O: the alkene distils off as it forms.
  • If the OH carbon has different neighbours, several alkenes can form, including E/Z isomers.
Quick check
?Butan-2-ol, CH3CH(OH)CH2CH3, is heated with concentrated phosphoric acid. How many different alkenes can form?
Mission 4

OH → halogen: halide ions plus acid

  • Alcohol + hydrogen halide → halogenoalkane + water.
  • CH3CH2OH + HBr → CH3CH2Br + H2O
  • HBr is made in the flask: KBr + H2SO4 → KHSO4 + HBr.
  • Edexcel: KBr with 50% concentrated H2SO4. Diluting cuts oxidation of HBr to Br2.
  • CP6: tertiary alcohols react with concentrated HCl at room temperature.
Mission 4

Phosphorus halides

  • PCl5, room temperature: CH3CH2OH + PCl5 → CH3CH2Cl + POCl3 + HCl
  • Steamy fumes of HCl: a test for an O–H group (alcohols, acids, water all give it).
  • Red phosphorus + iodine, warm: forms PI3 in the flask.
  • 3CH3CH2OH + PI3 → 3CH3CH2I + H3PO3
Mission 4

Making and purifying a halogenoalkane

  • 1–2. CP6: shake 2-methylpropan-2-ol with conc HCl in a separating funnel, room temperature. Release the pressure often.
  • 3. Separate in a separating funnel. Check which layer is organic: 1-bromobutane is below the water; 2-chloro-2-methylpropane floats above the acid.
  • 4. Wash with NaHCO3(aq) to remove acid. Vent: CO2 forms.
  • 5. Dry with an anhydrous salt (e.g. Na2SO4) until the liquid is clear.
  • 6. Distil, collecting near the boiling point (1-bromobutane 102 °C; 2-chloro-2-methylpropane 51 °C).
Quick check
?Propan-2-ol is warmed with potassium bromide and sulfuric acid. What is the main organic product?
Quick check
?A few drops of an unknown colourless liquid are added to solid PCl5. Steamy fumes appear. What can you conclude?
Mission check

?Cyclopentanol is treated in two ways:
(1) heated with concentrated phosphoric acid
(2) warmed with potassium bromide and sulfuric acid
What are the main organic products?

Acid lets the OH leave as water. Whether a halide ion is present decides between alkene and halogenoalkane.

Mission 5

Mission 5: A double bond shares twice as many electrons as a single bond. So why isn't it twice as strong?

Make a guess: A double bond shares twice as many electrons as a single bond. So why isn't it twice as strong?

Mission 5

Sigma bonds

  • σ bond: direct (head-on) overlap of orbitals between two nuclei.
  • Electron density lies on the axis between the nuclei.
  • Every single bond is a σ bond: all C–C and C–H bonds in alkanes.
  • Groups can rotate freely about a σ bond (free rotation).
Mission 5

Pi bonds

  • π bond: sideways overlap of two p orbitals, one on each atom.
  • Electron density lies above and below the axis, not on it.
  • Double bond = 1σ + 1π. Triple bond = 1σ + 2π.
  • The π bond stops rotation: groups on each C are fixed in place.
  • Restricted rotation about C=C gives E/Z (cis/trans) isomers when each C carries two different groups.
  • Each C of C=C: three bonding regions, trigonal planar, about 120°.
Quick check
?The displayed formula of propenenitrile (acrylonitrile), C3H3N, is shown: CH2=CH–C≡N. How many σ bonds and how many π bonds are in one molecule?
Mission 5

Why C=C is shorter and stronger, but not twice as strong

  • C–C 154 pm, 347 kJ mol⁻¹. C=C 134 pm, 612 kJ mol⁻¹ (mean, or average, bond enthalpies).
  • Two shared pairs pull the nuclei closer: C=C is shorter and stronger than C–C.
  • But not twice as strong: the π bond is weaker than the σ bond (612 − 347 ≈ 265).
  • Sideways overlap of p orbitals is less effective than head-on overlap.
  • π electrons are exposed above and below the bond: C=C is a region of high electron density. Electrophiles attack it.
  • So alkenes are far more reactive than alkanes.
Quick check
?A student suggests that a π bond between two carbon atoms prevents rotation about the bond that joins them. Which observation does this hypothesis predict?
Mission 5

π overlap round a ring: benzene

  • Kekulé model: a ring of alternating C–C and C=C bonds.
  • Delocalised model: each C uses one p orbital. The six p orbitals overlap sideways all round the ring.
  • This forms a ring of π electron density above and below the plane of the ring: six delocalised electrons.
  • The evidence for this model, and why benzene resists addition, are in a later lesson.
Mission check

?A C≡C bond is only about 2.4 times as strong as a C–C bond, not 3 times. Which statement explains this?
  • Mean (average) bond enthalpies: C–C 347 kJ mol⁻¹ · C=C 612 kJ mol⁻¹ · C≡C 838 kJ mol⁻¹.

The first bond between two atoms is a σ bond. Every extra bond is a π bond, from weaker sideways overlap, so each one adds less strength than the σ bond.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Plenty of O2: C → CO2, H → H2O. Limited O2: CO or C (soot), and a yellow, smoky flame.
  • CO is toxic: it binds to haemoglobin, so blood carries less O2.
  • Biofuels are renewable, but not carbon neutral: making them burns fossil fuels.

Mission 2

  • Light splits Cl2 or Br2 homolytically into radicals (initiation).
  • Propagation: radical + molecule → molecule + radical, so the chain repeats; termination: two radicals combine.
  • Further substitution and attack at different positions give a mixture: a limitation in synthesis.

Mission 3

  • C=C is electron-rich, so alkenes undergo electrophilic addition with Br2, HX and H2O (acid catalyst).
  • Mechanism: C=C → δ+ atom; bond → δ− atom; lone pair of the anion → C⁺.
  • Bromine water: orange → colourless with C=C.
  • H2 adds over a nickel catalyst.

Mission 4

  • Acid protonates –OH; water leaves; then lose H⁺ (alkene) or add a halide (halogenoalkane).

Mission 5

  • σ: head-on overlap, on the axis. π: sideways p overlap, above and below; weaker; stops rotation. Double = σ + π; triple = σ + 2π.
  • Benzene: six p orbitals overlap sideways to give a delocalised π ring (evidence in a later lesson).
