Mission 1

State, nation, nation state

  • State: a political unit with sovereign control of a territory. The 1933 Montevideo Convention lists four tests: "a permanent population; a defined territory; government; and capacity to enter into relations with the other states".
  • Nation: a large group of people who feel bound by shared culture, language, history or identity.
  • Nation state: where the borders of a state and a nation roughly match.
  • In reality, most states hold more than one nation or ethnic group.
Mission 1

Sovereignty and territorial integrity

  • Sovereignty: a state's supreme authority over its own territory and affairs. The UN "is based on the principle of the sovereign equality of all its Members".
  • Territorial integrity: a state's right to keep its borders intact. Members "shall refrain … from the threat or use of force against the territorial integrity or political independence of any state".
  • Montevideo adds: "The political existence of the state is independent of recognition by the other states". In practice, recognition decides membership of the UN and much of the world's trade and diplomacy.
Mission 1

The world political map changes

  • UN members: 51 in 1945 → 99 in 1960 → 193 in 2011.
  • In 1960 alone, 17 states joined, most of them newly independent African states such as Nigeria, Senegal and Mali.
  • The newest member, South Sudan, joined in 2011.
  • The map is not fixed: borders are made, split and argued over.

?A territory has a permanent population, clear borders and its own government, but it has no formal diplomatic relations with any other state. Which Montevideo test is most in doubt?
Mission 1

A contested border: Ukraine and Russia

  • 2014: after a referendum in Crimea, the UN General Assembly called on states "not to recognize any change in the status of Crimea" (100 for, 11 against, 58 abstentions).
  • 2022: the Assembly condemned the "attempted illegal annexation" of Donetsk, Kherson, Luhansk and Zaporizhzhia (143 for, 5 against, 35 abstentions).
  • Russia voted against both; it treats the areas as Russian, while most UN members treat them as Ukrainian.
  • Movement: refugees from Ukraine rose from about 28,000 in 2021 to 5.2 million in 2025, with 3.7 million displaced inside Ukraine (UNHCR).
Mission 1

An unrecognised state: Taiwan

  • Taiwan (the Republic of China) has its own elected government, but in 1971 UN Resolution 2758, "Restoration of the lawful rights of the People's Republic of China in the United Nations", gave China's UN seat to the People's Republic.
  • The People's Republic of China regards Taiwan as part of China; Taiwan's government runs its own affairs.
  • Only 12 states have formal diplomatic relations with Taiwan (its foreign ministry lists Belize, Eswatini, Guatemala, Haiti, the Holy See, the Marshall Islands, Palau, Paraguay, St Kitts and Nevis, St Lucia, St Vincent and the Grenadines, and Tuvalu).
  • So Taiwan meets three Montevideo tests easily; its recognition is what is limited.
Quick check
?Which statement best explains why Taiwan has no UN seat?
Mission 1

Norms, intervention and geopolitics

  • Norms: shared rules of behaviour between states, such as non-intervention: the UN Charter does not authorise the UN "to intervene in matters which are essentially within the domestic jurisdiction of any state".
  • Norms change: in 2005 UN members accepted a responsibility to protect populations "from genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing and crimes against humanity".
  • Intervention: outside action inside another state, from sanctions to force; it tests sovereignty.
  • Geopolitics: how power, territory and location shape relations between states, e.g. great powers backing rival claims.
  • Together these show sovereignty is conditional and contested, not absolute.
Mission 1

Boundaries that challenge sovereignty

  • Maritime boundaries: in the South China Sea arbitration (Philippines v. China), the tribunal issued its final award on 12 July 2016 on "the role of historic rights and the source of maritime entitlements". China did not take part and does not accept the arbitration.
  • Annexation: Crimea (2014) and four regions of Ukraine (2022), rejected by large UN majorities.
  • Internal boundaries: Iraq's federal Kurdistan Region keeps its own laws and authorities within Iraq.
  • Each case pits territorial integrity against another claim: history, security or self-determination.
Quick check
?A state seizes islands claimed by a neighbour, and an international tribunal rules against the seizure, but the state ignores the ruling. What does this show?
Mission check

?A territory has a permanent population, a defined territory and its own elected government. A neighbouring state claims it, and most UN members do not recognise it. Which change would most strengthen its claim to full statehood?

Statehood is partly legal and partly political. Borders shaped by empires and wars can leave groups divided and sovereignty contested, and recognition by other states decides who sits at the UN.

Mission 2

Sovereignty and the TNC

  • Sovereignty: a state's authority over its territory, laws and taxes.
  • A TNC operates in many states at once and can choose where to place assets, staff and profits. Each state taxes only what is recorded in its territory.
  • Profit repatriation: UNCTAD estimated FDI income of $1.5 trillion in 2011 on a stock of $21 trillion; nearly one-third was retained in host economies and two-thirds repatriated.
Mission 2

Avoidance or evasion?

  • Tax evasion is illegal: hiding income or wealth from the tax authority.
  • Tax avoidance uses the rules to reduce tax. The OECD defines base erosion and profit shifting (BEPS) as "tax planning strategies used by multinational enterprises that exploit gaps and mismatches in tax rules to avoid paying tax".
  • Avoidance is lawful, but rules change and arrangements can be challenged.
Mission 2

How profit shifting works

  • A TNC places intangible assets (brands, patents, software) in a subsidiary in a low-tax jurisdiction.
  • Subsidiaries in high-tax countries pay it royalties or fees, which cut their taxable profit.
  • Transfer pricing: the prices set for trade between parts of the same TNC decide where profit is recorded.
Mission 2

The scale

  • Tørsløv, Wier and Zucman estimate that close to 40% of multinational profits, close to $1 trillion, were shifted to tax havens in 2019.
  • They estimate this cut corporate tax revenue by more than $200 billion, about 10% of global corporate tax receipts.
  • These are model estimates from national accounts, not audited counts.
Mission 2

Case study: Apple and Ireland, 2003–2014

  • In August 2016 the European Commission decided that Ireland had given Apple illegal tax benefits of up to €13 billion for 2003–2014.
  • The effective tax rate of Apple Sales International "declined from 1% in 2003 to 0.005% in 2014".
  • Under Irish tax rulings, most profit was allocated to "head offices" that "existed only on paper"; those profits "were not subject to tax in any country".
Mission 2

The outcome

  • Ireland and Apple appealed separately. In 2020 the EU General Court annulled the decision.
  • On 10 September 2024 the Court of Justice set that judgment aside and confirmed the Commission's 2016 decision.
  • Apple had paid €14.3 billion into an escrow account in 2018; by May 2025 it had passed to the Irish state.
  • It was Ireland's tax rulings, not a crime by Apple, that were found to be illegal state aid.
Quick check
?A subsidiary records a profit of €10 billion. How much tax is due at an effective rate of 0.005%?
Mission 2

Governments and IGOs: accept, then regulate

  • Most governments and IGOs have not abolished tax havens; they set shared rules instead.
  • OECD/G20 Two-Pillar solution (8 October 2021): agreed by 139 member jurisdictions by 9 June 2023.
  • Ireland: from 31 December 2023, groups with global turnover over €750 million pay a minimum effective rate of 15%; other businesses keep 12.5%.
  • A shared minimum limits every state's freedom to set its own rate: states pool some sovereignty to protect their tax base from TNCs.
Mission 2

Exam move: whose sovereignty?

  • Ireland's view: low corporation tax attracts FDI and jobs; on the 15% rate, its finance minister spoke of "safeguarding our future competitiveness".
  • The Commission's view: tax benefits for selected companies are illegal state aid.
  • Other states: profits booked elsewhere shrink their tax base.
  • TNCs challenge sovereignty because they can choose jurisdictions; states compete for them, and can respond only by cooperating, which limits each state's own choices.
Quick check
?Which is an example of tax avoidance rather than tax evasion?
Mission check

?A TNC sells software across Europe. Its subsidiaries pay large royalties to a sister company that owns the code and is based where corporate tax is 0%. Who loses, and why?

Profit shifting records profit where little tax is due. Apple's subsidiary paid 0.005% in 2014 because Irish rulings left most profit untaxed; the EU ruled this illegal state aid. States where sales and staff are located lose tax base, and a global minimum of 15% is the shared response.

Mission 3

Globalisation's rise and stall

  • World trade (exports plus imports) was 25.8% of world GDP in 1970 and 60.3% in 2008.
  • It fell to 51.9% in 2009, then moved between about 52% and 62%; it was 56.7% in 2024.
  • The long rise stopped after 2008. This slowdown is sometimes called "slowbalisation".
Quick check
?World trade was 25.8% of world GDP in 1970 and 60.3% in 2008. How many times as high was the share in 2008?
Mission 3

Backlash: nationalism and separatism

  • Nationalism: putting national identity, control and interests first, e.g. tariffs or tighter borders.
  • Separatism: a region seeking to leave a state and become independent.
  • A correlation between globalisation and nationalism needs evidence of a mechanism before it can be called a cause.
Mission 3

The UK's vote to leave the EU

  • On 23 June 2016 the UK voted to leave the European Union: Leave won 51.9%.
  • Leave won 53.4% in England and 52.5% in Wales, but 44.2% in Northern Ireland and 38.0% in Scotland.
  • A national choice to leave a trading bloc, with sharp differences between the UK's nations.
Mission 3

Brexit and the "China shock"

  • Colantone and Stanig (2018) found support for Leave "was systematically higher in regions hit harder" by globalisation.
  • Exposure was measured by "the shock of surging imports from China over the past three decades".
  • They link the effect to workers displaced by import competition "in the absence of effective compensation"; their design (an instrumental variable) "supports a causal interpretation".
Quick check
?What does the Colantone and Stanig study show?
Mission 3

Trading blocs and sovereignty

  • A supranational institution makes rules that bind member states. The EU's single market rests on "free movement of goods, services, capital and persons".
  • Members gain market access but give up control of tariffs, product rules and (within the EU) migration from other members.
  • To leave, the UK repealed the European Communities Act 1972, and provided that "the principle of the supremacy of EU law does not apply" to UK laws passed after exit day.
  • Regained control has costs: the OBR assumes UK exports and imports will be about 15% lower in the long run than if it had stayed.
Quick check
?How does a regional trading bloc such as the EU challenge a member's sovereignty?
Mission 3

Synthesis: weighing globalisation

  • Advantages: wider markets, cheaper goods, export jobs, flows of investment and ideas.
  • Risks: losers concentrated in places, fragile supply chains, emissions, political backlash.
  • Scale: gains often national and thin; losses often local and deep. That geography helps explain the backlash.
  • Exam move: make the judgement conditional: for whom, where and when do the gains outweigh the costs?
Quick check
?Which conclusion would earn most credit in an essay on globalisation?
Mission check

?By early May 2025, average US tariffs on Chinese goods had reached 126.1%, and China's on US goods peaked at 147.6% in mid-April; by 22 August 2026 they stood at 35.2% and 31.9%. Is this evidence that globalisation creates its own backlash?

The backlash is real but partial: UK regions more exposed to Chinese imports backed Leave more, and US–China tariffs rose to 126.1% before falling back. Globalisation's gains and losses fall on different places, which is why the judgement must be conditional.

Mission 4

What is ethnic conflict?

  • Ethnic conflict: conflict between groups who see themselves as different by nationality, language, religion or race, where that identity becomes the line of division.
  • Conflict ranges from protest to war. It is usually about power, territory and rights, not identity alone.
  • Most cases have several causes acting together.
Mission 4

When one ethnic group dominates the state

  • Political dominance: one group controls government, the army and resources; others feel excluded.
  • Rwanda: by 1994 Hutu were about 85%, Tutsi 14%, Twa 1% of the population. A Hutu-dominated government faced the Rwandan Patriotic Front (RPF), mainly Tutsi exiles, which invaded from Uganda in 1990 demanding return of refugees and power sharing.
  • Bosnia and Herzegovina: the post-war constitution names Bosniacs, Croats and Serbs as "constituent peoples" and reserves seats for each: the House of Peoples has 5 Croats, 5 Bosniacs, 5 Serbs.
  • Iraq: the 2005 constitution recognises the region of Kurdistan, with its existing authorities, as a federal region.
  • Outcomes differ: the Dayton Agreement, signed 14 December 1995, ended the 3½-year Bosnian War; in Cyprus, the UN has kept a buffer zone between the two communities since the hostilities of 1974.
Quick check
?Bosnia and Herzegovina's constitution reserves seats in the House of Peoples for Bosniacs, Croats and Serbs. What is the main aim of this arrangement?
Mission 4

How dominance challenges sovereignty

  • Sovereignty: a state's authority over its territory. Territorial integrity: its borders stay whole.
  • An excluded group may seek:
    • Secession: a separate state (loss of territory).
    • Autonomy: self-government inside the state (central power shrinks), e.g. Iraq's Kurdistan Region.
    • Armed takeover: e.g. the RPF took military control of all Rwanda by 4 July 1994.
  • International intervention can override a state's control: the UN Security Council set up the International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda on 8 November 1994.
Quick check
?Which situation is the clearest challenge to a state's territorial integrity?
Mission check

?Two countries each have one ethnic majority. In Country A, the minority has guaranteed seats and regional self-government. In Country B, the minority is excluded from power, and its armed group holds part of the territory. Which outcome is most likely?

Outcomes depend less on how old the differences are than on how power is shared. Where groups are excluded, protest can escalate into civil war, terrorism or ethnic cleansing, ending in division or intervention. Where power is shared, through autonomy or peace processes, states more often stay whole.

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

Mission 5

Sovereignty and territorial integrity

  • Sovereignty: a state's supreme authority over its territory and people.
  • Territorial integrity: the right of a state to keep its borders intact.
  • The UN Charter: members "shall refrain … from the threat or use of force against the territorial integrity or political independence of any state".
  • Challenge to sovereignty: when another actor takes or contests control of a state's land or decisions.
Mission 5

Cause: boundaries and population mix

  • Ukraine's 2001 census: Ukrainians 77.8%, Russians 17.3% of the population.
  • The mix varied by region: Autonomous Republic of Crimea 58.3% Russian, 24.3% Ukrainian, 12.0% Crimean Tatar.
  • Donetsk 38.2% and Luhansk 39.0% Russian; Lviv only 3.6%.
  • Where a region's majority differs from the national majority, the boundary can be contested: a factor OCR names ("political dominance of ethnic groups").
  • Census data show composition, not political loyalty or cause.
Mission 5

2014: Crimea

  • A referendum in Crimea on 16 March 2014.
  • UN General Assembly resolution 68/262, "Territorial integrity of Ukraine" (27 March 2014): 100 for, 11 against, 58 abstentions; it underscored "the invalidity of the 16 March referendum".
  • Ukraine's government requested an OSCE monitoring mission to report on security; all 57 OSCE states agreed, and it ran from 21 March 2014 to 31 March 2022.
Quick check
?In 2001, 58.3% of Crimea's people identified as Russian, compared with 17.3% across Ukraine. How should a geographer use this?
Mission 5

2022: full-scale invasion

  • February 2022: a draft UN Security Council resolution demanding Russia stop its attack failed: 11 for, 3 abstentions, and Russia, a permanent member, voted against (a veto).
  • 2 March 2022: the General Assembly resolution "Aggression against Ukraine" passed 141–5, 35 abstaining.
  • It demanded Russia "unconditionally withdraw all of its military forces".
Mission 5

Annexation declared, then rejected

  • In 2022 Russia declared it had annexed four Ukrainian regions: Donetsk, Kherson, Luhansk and Zaporizhzhia.
  • The General Assembly (October 2022) declared this attempted annexation invalid: 143 for, 5 against, 35 abstentions.
  • The Charter allows the Assembly to "make recommendations"; it has no force of its own to impose them.
Quick check
?Why did the General Assembly votes not restore Ukraine's control of its territory?
Mission 5

Challenges to the government

  • Territory: the Crimea referendum (2014) and Russia's declared annexation of four regions (2022), both rejected by the UN Assembly.
  • Economy: GDP fell 10.1% (2014), 9.8% (2015) and 28.8% (2022).
  • Monitoring and diplomacy: the OSCE mission ended on 31 March 2022.
  • Reconstruction: recovery needs over the next decade estimated at almost $588 billion (assessment covering Feb 2022 – Dec 2025).
Mission 5

Impacts on people

  • Refugees from Ukraine (UNHCR): 237,617 (2014) → 5,684,177 (2022) → 5,224,131 (2025).
  • Internally displaced people: 1.6 million (2015) → 5.9 million (2022) → 3.7 million (2025).
  • Displacement split families and labour markets inside and outside Ukraine.
Quick check
?UNHCR counted 5,224,131 refugees from Ukraine and 3,712,000 internally displaced people at the end of 2025. About what share of the displaced total were refugees?
Mission 5

Impacts on places

  • Direct damage: over $195 billion (Feb 2022 – Dec 2025).
  • 14% of housing damaged or destroyed, affecting over 3 million households.
  • Highest recovery needs: transport (over $96 bn), energy (nearly $91 bn), housing (almost $90 bn).
  • Assessment by the Government of Ukraine, World Bank, European Commission and UN.
Mission check

?Country P has a border region where most people share an identity with a larger neighbour. The neighbour sends troops and holds a vote there; the UN General Assembly declares the vote invalid by a large majority, but a permanent Security Council member blocks Council action. Which outcome is most likely, based on the Ukraine case?

Ukraine shows that territorial integrity is protected in law and by large UN majorities, but the General Assembly can only recommend and a veto can block the Security Council. Control on the ground, and the costs to people and places, follow force, not votes.

Mission 6

The rule and its exceptions

  • UN Charter, Art. 2(4): states must not use force "against the territorial integrity or political independence of any state".
  • Exception 1: the Security Council may authorise force under Chapter VII.
  • Exception 2: "the inherent right of individual or collective self-defence if an armed attack occurs" (Art. 51).
  • A newer norm: in 2005 UN members endorsed the responsibility to protect people "from genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing and crimes against humanity".
Mission 6

Sovereignty challenged: Kuwait 1990

  • 2 August 1990: Iraq invaded Kuwait. Resolution 660 condemned it and demanded Iraq "withdraw immediately and unconditionally".
  • Resolution 678 (29 November 1990) authorised states working with Kuwait's government "to use all necessary means" if Iraq had not complied by 15 January 1991.
  • Vote: 12 for, 2 against (Cuba, Yemen), 1 abstention (China).
  • Resources: in the South China Sea, a 2016 tribunal found "no legal basis for China to claim historic rights to resources" inside its "nine-dash line"; China would "neither accept nor participate".
Quick check
?After 1990–91, Kuwait existed again as a sovereign state within its old borders. What does this show about international law and institutions?
Mission 6

Laws and norms that regulate conflict

  • Geneva Conventions (1949): protect "people who do not take part in the fighting"; grave breaches "must be pursued and tried or extradited".
  • International Criminal Court: 125 states are parties to the Rome Statute.
  • Norms: non-intervention, self-defence, the responsibility to protect.
  • These rules treat states as the units of world politics, so even interventions usually aim to rebuild states, not replace the system: they reproduce the state system.
Mission 6

Strategic interests and human-rights justifications

  • US National Security Strategy, 2002: "America must stand firmly for the nonnegotiable demands of human dignity".
  • After 11 September 2001 and Afghanistan, the US turned to "regime change in Iraq as part of the second phase of what it called the Global War on Terror".
  • The Iraq Inquiry (UK, 2016): the UK chose to "invade and occupy a sovereign nation, without the support of a Security Council resolution explicitly authorising the use of force".
  • It was later found that Iraq's weapons programmes "had been dismantled". Motives mixed security, alliance and rights claims.
Quick check
?The Iraq Inquiry found that "diplomatic options had not at that stage been exhausted. Military action was therefore not a last resort". What does this most directly challenge?
Mission 6

Flows in geopolitical intervention

  • People: troops in (Afghanistan's ISAF had "more than 130,000" troops from 51 nations); refugees out (Iraq, 2.31 million by 2007).
  • Money: arms exports and military aid (the USA supplied 42% of world arms exports, 2021–25).
  • Ideas: human dignity, democracy, the responsibility to protect.
  • Technology: weapons systems transferred between states.
Quick check
?A state supplies an ally with aircraft and trains its pilots, citing the "responsibility to protect". Which flows are involved?
Mission check

?A powerful state arms an ally with a poor rights record. Years later, it invades a neighbouring state without Security Council approval, citing human rights. Which evaluation is best supported?

Military interventions are usually justified by rights but driven partly by strategic interests; they operate inside a system of laws and norms that protects states; and short-term gains often bring long-term costs to sovereignty, rights and people.

Mission 7

Governance at many scales

  • Global governance: rules and organisations that manage issues crossing or within borders.
  • United Nations (global): the Security Council sets peacekeeping mandates.
  • National government (state): holds sovereignty and runs public services.
  • NGOs (non-governmental organisations), from international to local: deliver aid and services, independent of governments.
  • In a conflict area these scales interact: they cooperate, depend on each other and sometimes clash.
Mission 7

The conflict area: South Sudan

  • South Sudan became independent on 9 July 2011.
  • On 15 December 2013, violence broke out in Juba and spread across the country.
  • Almost 500,000 people were displaced within the country in the first month; 74,300 fled to neighbouring countries.
  • By the end of February 2014, 900,000 people were displaced; people in acute or emergency food insecurity rose from 1.1 million to 3.2 million.
Mission 7

The UN steps in

  • The UN Mission in South Sudan (UNMISS) was set up by Security Council resolution 1996 on 8 July 2011.
  • In December 2013, tens of thousands of civilians fled to UNMISS compounds in Juba, Bor, Akobo, Bentiu, Malakal and Melut.
  • On 27 May 2014, resolution 2155 refocused UNMISS on protection of civilians, human-rights monitoring and humanitarian support.
  • The bases became Protection of Civilians (PoC) sites.
Quick check
?What did Security Council resolution 2155 (2014) change?
Mission 7

The UN mandate today

  • UNMISS now works under resolution 2779 (2025).
  • Four task groups: protect civilians; create conditions for humanitarian aid; support the peace agreement and elections; monitor human rights.
  • Methods include "targeted and active patrolling" and "identifying and deterring threats and attacks against civilians".
  • Exam move (OCR "Examine"): link each UN task to a local outcome, then weigh its limits.
Mission 7

The national government

  • Parties to the conflict signed a peace agreement in August 2015; fighting resumed in July 2016.
  • On 12 September 2018 they signed the Revitalized Agreement, and a Revitalized Transitional Government of National Unity was formed.
  • Bentiu PoC site was redesignated a conventional displacement camp under government authority in 2021.
  • Malakal was formally handed over in February 2026; its health services went, in theory, to local health authorities, "short of supplies and staff".
Mission 7

NGOs on the ground

  • Médecins Sans Frontières (MSF) has worked in the country since 1983.
  • In 2025 MSF had 3,418 staff and gave 663,900 outpatient consultations, in six of South Sudan's ten states.
  • MSF recorded nine attacks on its staff and facilities in 2025.
  • In Malakal, the NGO International Medical Corps ended its services, including outpatient consultations.
  • The International Organization for Migration (IOM) is now the only water and sanitation provider in both camps.

Mission 7

Consequences for local communities

  • About 150,000 displaced people still live in the Bentiu and Malakal camps, set up in 2013.
  • Bentiu holds about 111,000 people; about 60 people share each working latrine; 109 handwashing stations serve the whole camp.
  • Rubkona County, home to Bentiu, had nearly one-third of South Sudan's cholera cases from January to September 2026.
  • Nationally since 2024: nearly 110,000 cholera cases and 1,970 deaths.
Mission 7

Short term vs long term

  • Short term: UN bases gave refuge when violence broke out in 2013.
  • Long term: 13 years later the camps still exist, and people depend on outside services.
  • Transition: handing camps to the state ends the emergency model, but local authorities lack supplies and staff.
  • Funding: IOM's water and sanitation funding in Bentiu runs only to March 2027; MSF calls on donors to reverse the cuts.

Mission check

In South Sudan the UN (UNMISS) gave first refuge and still has a protection mandate; the national government now has authority over the camps; NGOs such as MSF supply health care, though International Medical Corps has withdrawn from Malakal. The consequence for camp residents is long-term dependence on services that are stretched.

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 2.2.4-4.a short- and long-term consequences of governing sovereignty issues (humanitarian aid, regime change)
  • 2.2.4-4.a short- and long-term consequences of governing territorial integrity issues (peacekeeping, trade relationships)
  • 2.2.4-4.a case study of sovereignty or territorial integrity governance in one LIDC: issue, strategies, opportunities, injustices
Mission 8

Governing sovereignty and territory

  • Sovereignty issue: who holds authority over a state (e.g. a coup, a rebel movement, a collapsed government).
  • Territorial integrity issue: who controls the state's land (e.g. rebels holding regions or resources).
  • Global governance responds through the UN, regional bodies (e.g. ECOWAS), states and NGOs.
  • Consequences differ by timescale:
    • Short term: humanitarian aid; peacekeeping.
    • Long term: changes of regime; new trade relationships.
Mission 8

Consequences of governing sovereignty

  • Short term: humanitarian aid keeps people alive: food, shelter, health care, return of refugees.
    • Risk: aid can bypass a weak state, or be seized by armed groups.
  • Long term: regime change: an illegal regime removed, an elected government restored, new elections held.
    • Risk: a new regime may lack legitimacy, or depend on outside support.
Mission 8

Consequences of governing territorial integrity

  • Short term: peacekeeping separates forces, monitors ceasefires and protects civilians, so the state's territory stays whole.
    • Risk: peacekeepers may be too few, or attacked.
  • Long term: trade relationships: sanctions lifted, resource trade regulated and investment returns, giving the state income across its whole territory.
    • Risk: income from resources may stay with a few people.
Quick check
?Which is a long-term consequence of governing a territorial integrity issue?
Mission 8

Sierra Leone: the issue

  • LIDC: World Bank "Low income"; HDI 0.467, rank 185 of 193 (2023).
  • March 1991: the Revolutionary United Front (RUF) launched a war from the east, near Liberia, "to overthrow the government".
  • Sovereignty: the army overthrew the government in 1992 and again in May 1997, when it joined the RUF in a junta; the elected president went into exile in Guinea.
  • Territorial integrity: the rebel alliance gained control of "more than half the country" and in January 1999 overran most of Freetown.
  • Rebels bought weapons with conflict diamonds.
Mission 8

Strategies: from regional force to UN mission

  • 1997: UN Security Council oil and arms embargo; ECOWAS's force ECOMOG enforces it.
  • 1998: ECOMOG expels the junta; the president returns (10 March).
  • 1999: Lomé peace agreement (7 July); UN mission UNAMSIL set up (22 October), up to 6,000 military personnel.
  • 2000: RUF seizes hundreds of peacekeepers; sanctions on RUF sponsors; a UK force restores peace and later restructures the army.
  • 2001: UNAMSIL authorised to 17,500 military personnel; peak deployment 17,368 (March 2002).
  • Cost: about US$2.8 billion (October 1999 to December 2005).
Quick check
?UNAMSIL's authorised military strength rose from 6,000 (October 1999) to 17,500 (March 2001). By what factor did it grow?
Mission 8

Short-term consequences

  • Peacekeeping: by early 2002, more than 75,000 ex-fighters disarmed, including child soldiers; the government declared the war over.
  • Humanitarian: voluntary return of more than half a million refugees and displaced people.
  • Troops rebuilt schools and clinics, funded farm projects and ran free medical clinics.
  • Government authority and services restored in former rebel areas: territorial integrity regained.
  • Cost to peacekeepers: 192 UNAMSIL fatalities.
Mission 8

Long term: regime and stability

  • 2002: Sierra Leone's "first ever free and fair" presidential and parliamentary elections, with UN logistics.
  • Justice: the UN helped set up the Special Court for Sierra Leone and a Truth and Reconciliation Commission.
  • UNAMSIL ended on 31 December 2005; a smaller UN office followed to support the 2007 elections.
  • 2023: the sixth general election since the war.
  • Exam move (OCR "Evaluate"): separate short-term success (fighting stopped) from long-term success (stable, legitimate government), and judge each.
Mission 8

Long term: trade and growth

  • Kimberley Process, launched 2003: rough diamonds must be certified, and participants trade them only with other participants.
  • The UN helped the government "stop illicit trading in diamonds and regulate the industry".
  • Exports of goods and services: US$93 million (1999) → US$454 million (2005) → US$1,554 million (2014) → US$1,204 million (2024).
  • Resources include diamonds, gold, bauxite and rutile; real GDP grew 4.8% in 2025.
Mission 8

Inequality and injustice

  • In 2005 the UN reported that "a disproportionate share of income from diamond mining still found its way into private hands, rather than Government coffers".
  • Thousands of ex-combatants and young people, many never schooled, were unemployed.
  • The economy was "heavily dependent on donor funds": net aid was 29.3% of national income in 2000, 7.8% in 2023.
  • The Special Court was set up to try only "those most responsible" for war crimes, not every perpetrator.
  • In 2023 Sierra Leone still ranked 185 of 193 on the HDI.
Quick check
?Which evidence best shows a continuing challenge of inequality after the war?
Mission check

?Ten years after a UN mission ends a civil war in a low-income country, elections have changed the ruling party peacefully, mining exports have tripled, but most mining income goes to a few firms and officials, and youth unemployment is high. Which evaluation is best supported?

In Sierra Leone, regional and UN governance (ECOMOG, UNAMSIL, sanctions, the Kimberley Process) ended the war, restored territorial control and led to repeated elections. The opportunities for growth were real, but unequal sharing of resource income and poverty remained.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A state has territory, population, government and the capacity for relations; a nation is a people; few states are true nation states. Sovereignty and territorial integrity are protected in the UN Charter but are contested in practice.
  • Norms, intervention and geopolitics explain why sovereignty is conditional; maritime claims, annexation and internal boundaries challenge territorial integrity.

Mission 2

  • TNCs challenge sovereignty by choosing where profit is recorded; states respond by cooperating (OECD 15% minimum), which limits their own choices.

Mission 3

  • World trade's share of GDP more than doubled to 2008, then stalled; regions that lost from import competition backed nationalist choices more.
  • Trading blocs pool sovereignty: their rules can override national law, and leaving restores control at a cost.

Mission 4

  • When one ethnic group dominates the state, excluded groups may seek secession, autonomy or takeover, challenging sovereignty and territorial integrity.

Mission 5

  • Causes: contested boundaries and regional identity mix (2001 census), then the use of force (2014, 2022) and declared annexations.
  • Challenges and impacts: lost territory, GDP down 28.8% (2022), 5.2 million refugees and 3.7 million IDPs (2025), $195 bn direct damage, $588 bn recovery needs.

Mission 6

  • The UN Charter bans force against states except under Chapter VII or in self-defence; the responsibility to protect adds a contested norm. Kuwait 1990 and the South China Sea show sovereignty and resources driving conflict and institutions defending the state system.
  • Intervention moves people, money, ideas and technology across borders.

Mission 7

  • One conflict area, three scales: UN (UNMISS, resolutions 1996 and 2155), national government (2018 Revitalized Agreement; authority over camps from 2021/2026), NGOs (MSF, International Medical Corps).
  • Local consequences: refuge in 2013, but in 2026 about 150,000 people still in camps with crowded sanitation, cholera and funding gaps.

Mission 8

  • Consequences: short term (humanitarian aid, peacekeeping) vs long term (regime change, trade relationships); each brings risks as well as gains.
  • Sierra Leone (LIDC): issue: RUF war from 1991, coups, rebels holding over half the country, conflict diamonds. Strategies: embargo, ECOMOG, Lomé, UNAMSIL (up to 17,500 troops, ~US$2.8 bn), UK force, Kimberley Process. Opportunities: >75,000 disarmed, elections since 2002, exports up. Injustices: diamond income in private hands, youth unemployment, aid dependence, HDI rank 185.
