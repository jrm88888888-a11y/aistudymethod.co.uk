Mission 1

Mission 1: How can you measure the length of a cell you can only see through a microscope?

Make a guess: How can you measure the length of a cell you can only see through a microscope?

Mission 1

Cell theory

  • Cell theory: all living organisms are made of one or more cells.
  • The cell is the smallest unit that carries out all the processes of life.
  • New cells arise only from existing cells, by division.
Mission 1

Units for tiny things

  • 1 mm = 1000 µm (micrometres).
  • 1 µm = 1000 nm (nanometres).
  • mm → µm: × 1000. µm → nm: × 1000.
  • Whole cells: µm. Organelle detail, viruses, molecules: nm.
  • A red blood cell is about 7.5 µm across; a ribosome about 25 nm.
  • Say: "1 mm = 1000 µm" — not: "1 mm = 100 µm".
Mission 1

The magnification formula

  • magnification = image size ÷ actual size
  • Rearrange: actual size = image size ÷ magnification.
  • Rearrange: image size = actual size × magnification.
  • Put both sizes in the same unit first.
  • Magnification has no unit: write ×2000.
  • CCEA calls the actual size the true size, usually in µm.
  • Check: the actual size must be smaller than the image. If yours is bigger, you multiplied.
Mission 1

Worked example: size and magnification

Actual size of a chloroplast in a micrograph at ×4000. The image is 24 mm long.

  • Convert: 24 mm = 24 000 µm.
  • Actual size = 24 000 ÷ 4000 = 6 µm.

Magnification of a mitochondrion drawn 45 mm long. Its actual length is 1.5 µm.

  • Convert: 45 mm = 45 000 µm.
  • Magnification = 45 000 ÷ 1.5 = ×30 000.
Quick check
?In a photomicrograph taken at a magnification of ×2000, a red blood cell measures 15 mm across. What is the actual width of the cell?
Mission 1

Scale bars

  • A scale bar is a line on an image labelled with the actual length it represents.
  • Magnification = measured bar length ÷ bar label (same units).
  • Example: a bar 20 mm long labelled 5 µm. 20 000 µm ÷ 5 µm = ×4000.
  • Then use the formula for anything else in that image.
  • To draw a bar: drawn length = label × magnification.
  • Say: "measure the bar with a ruler" — not: "the label is the bar's length".
Mission 1

Practical: calibrating an eyepiece graticule

  • Eyepiece graticule: a scale in the eyepiece. Its divisions have no fixed size.
  • Stage micrometer: a slide with a real scale, often 1 mm in 100 divisions of 10 µm.
  • Line up the two scales. Example at the ×10 objective: 25 graticule divisions = 250 µm. So 1 division = 10 µm.
  • Change the objective, recalibrate. At ×40: 10 divisions = 25 µm. So 1 division = 2.5 µm.
  • Measure: a cell spans 18 divisions at ×40. 18 × 2.5 µm = 45 µm.
  • Assessed practical: graticule and stage micrometer at low and high power.
Quick check
?With the ×40 objective lens in place, 40 divisions of an eyepiece graticule line up exactly with 100 µm on a stage micrometer. Using the same objective, a cell spans 44 graticule divisions. What is the actual length of the cell in millimetres?
Mission 1

Making and viewing a slide

  • Temporary mount: a thin layer of cells (e.g. onion epidermis) in a drop of water or stain; lower a coverslip at an angle to avoid air bubbles.
  • Stains add contrast, not detail. Iodine stains starch blue-black; methylene blue stains cells blue, nuclei most strongly.
  • Focus at low power first, with the coarse then the fine adjustment.
  • Switch to high power. Refocus with the fine adjustment only.
  • Say: "the stain increases contrast" — not: "the stain makes the image sharper".
Mission 1

Scientific drawings and low-power plans

  • Sharp pencil; single, continuous lines; no shading or colouring.
  • Draw what you see, in correct proportions; large enough to fill half the page.
  • Label lines ruled, not crossing; labels outside the drawing. Annotations add notes on function.
  • Low-power plan: tissue outlines only, no individual cells.
  • Drawing magnification = drawing size ÷ actual size. Example: a privet leaf 300 µm thick (30 divisions × 10 µm) drawn 90 mm thick: 90 000 ÷ 300 = ×300.
Mission check

?A micrograph shows a pollen grain 60 mm across. A scale bar on the micrograph is 20 mm long and is labelled 10 µm. What is the actual diameter of the pollen grain?

You never measure the cell directly. You measure the image, then use a calibrated scale (graticule or scale bar) to convert it.

Mission 2

Magnification is not resolution

  • Magnification: how many times larger the image is than the object.
  • Resolution: the smallest distance between two points that can still be seen as separate.
  • Resolution is limited by the wavelength used. Visible light (about 400–700 nm) gives a limit of about 200 nm.
  • Electrons have a far shorter wavelength, so electron microscopes resolve much finer detail.
  • Enlarging past the resolution limit gives a bigger blur, not more detail.
  • Say: "higher resolution shows more detail" — not: "higher magnification shows more detail".
Quick check
?Ribosomes are about 25 nm across. They cannot be seen with a light microscope but are clearly visible in electron micrographs. What is the reason?
Mission 2

Three microscopes, three ways to make an image

  • Light (optical) microscope: light passes through a thin specimen; glass lenses focus it.
  • Transmission electron microscope (TEM): a beam of electrons passes through a very thin section. Electromagnets focus it.
  • In a TEM, parts stained with heavy metals scatter electrons and look dark. The image is a flat slice of internal structure.
  • Scanning electron microscope (SEM): an electron beam is scanned across the surface of a specimen coated in metal.
  • In an SEM, electrons given off or scattered back from the surface build a 3D image of the surface only.

  • Colours on electron micrographs are false colours added by computer.
  • Electron microscopes are large and expensive; light microscopes are cheap and portable.
Quick check
?A student wants to watch living white blood cells engulfing bacteria about 2 µm long. Which choice of microscope, with its reason, is correct?
Mission 2

Reading micrographs

  • Light micrograph (photomicrograph): coloured by stains; whole cells and tissues. Only structures larger than about 0.2 µm, such as nuclei and chloroplasts.
  • TEM micrograph: a flat slice. Shows membranes inside organelles, e.g. the folds inside a mitochondrion.
  • SEM micrograph: a 3D-looking surface with shadows; no internal detail.
  • First find the scale bar or the magnification.
  • Next missions: naming the organelles you see.
Mission check

?A virus particle is about 100 nm across. A student photographs a stained sample through a light microscope at ×1500, then enlarges the photo 20 times: a total magnification of ×30 000. At this magnification a 100 nm particle would be 3 mm across. Which conclusion is correct?

Enlarging an image never adds detail. Detail is set by resolution, and resolution is set by the wavelength used.

Mission 3

Why take cells apart?

  • A microscope shows what an organelle looks like. To find out what it does, you need it on its own, in quantity.
  • Cell fractionation: break cells open, then separate their organelles into fractions.
  • Isolated organelles keep working: e.g. isolated mitochondria use oxygen and make ATP.
Mission 3

Step 1: homogenise in a cold, isotonic, buffered solution

  • Chop the tissue; put it in solution; break the cells in a homogeniser (a blender).
  • Cold (ice-cold): reduces enzyme activity, so enzymes released from the cell (e.g. from lysosomes) cannot digest the organelles.
  • Isotonic: same water potential as the tissue, so organelles neither gain nor lose water by osmosis: they do not burst or shrivel.
  • Buffered: keeps the pH constant, so the organelles' proteins are not denatured.
  • Filter the homogenate: removes unbroken cells and large debris.
  • Say: "cold reduces enzyme activity" — not: "cold denatures the enzymes".
  • Say: "stops the organelles bursting" — not: "stops the cells bursting" (the cells are broken on purpose).
Quick check
?Liver tissue is homogenised in an ice-cold, isotonic, buffered solution. Why must the solution be isotonic?
Mission 3

Step 2: ultracentrifugation

  • Spin the filtered homogenate in an ultracentrifuge.
  • The largest, densest organelles settle first, into a pellet at the bottom.
  • The liquid above, the supernatant, is poured into a fresh tube and spun faster and longer.
  • Repeat. Each spin brings down smaller, less dense organelles.
  • This is differential centrifugation.
  • Usual order: nuclei → (plant tissue: chloroplasts) → mitochondria → lysosomes → ER fragments → ribosomes.
  • Typical (illustrative) conditions: about 1 000 × g for nuclei; about 10 000–20 000 × g for mitochondria; about 100 000 × g and above for ER and ribosomes.
Quick check
?A homogenate of leaf cells is filtered and then spun at increasing speeds. In which order do these organelles form the main part of each pellet?

Mission 3Practical

Homogenisation and centrifugation

CCEA: "the use of homogenisation and centrifugation". Example: chloroplasts from spinach leaves.

  • Cut leaves (stalks and midribs removed) into ice-cold, isotonic, buffered solution; blend in short bursts.
  • Filter through layers of muslin into a chilled centrifuge tube.
  • Spin at low speed briefly; discard the pellet of debris and unbroken cells.
  • Spin the supernatant faster: the green pellet is mostly chloroplasts. Resuspend in cold buffer.
  • Check: a drop under the light microscope shows many green chloroplasts and few whole cells.
  • Keep everything cold throughout: tubes and solutions on ice. Balance the centrifuge tubes before spinning.
Mission check

?Liver cells were broken open in a cold, isotonic, buffered solution and the homogenate was spun in an ultracentrifuge at a series of increasing speeds. Only the pellet formed at the second speed consumed oxygen and produced ATP when supplied with substrates. What does this investigation illustrate about how progress in science is made?

Keep the organelles cold, isotonic and buffered, then spin at rising speeds: each fraction can then be tested on its own.

Mission 4

Rules for a scientific drawing

  • Sharp pencil. Clear, continuous single lines. No shading or colouring.
  • Draw only what you see, in the right proportions. Use most of the space.
  • Label lines: ruled, no arrowheads, touching the structure, never crossing.
  • Labels outside the drawing, aligned neatly.
  • Annotation = label + a short note: what the structure does, or a feature you can see.
  • Title: specimen, section (TS or LS), magnification or a scale bar.
Mission 4

Plan diagram or cell drawing?

  • Plan diagram (low power): outlines of tissue regions only. No individual cells.
  • CCEA calls this a block diagram: it shows where tissues are, not individual cells.
  • Draw each tissue layer in the right proportion of the whole.
  • High-power drawing: a few representative cells, with the detail you see (walls, nuclei, chloroplasts).
  • Leaf TS, top to bottom: upper epidermis (cuticle) → palisade mesophyll → spongy mesophyll (air spaces) → lower epidermis (stomata).
  • In each vein: xylem towards the upper surface, phloem towards the lower.
  • Ileum TS, inside out: mucosa (villi) → submucosa → muscle (circular, then longitudinal) → serosa.
Quick check
?Which statement about a plan diagram of a transverse section of a dicotyledonous leaf is correct?
Mission 4

Stem and root in section

  • Stem TS: epidermis → cortex → a ring of vascular bundles → pith in the centre.
  • In each bundle: phloem on the outside, xylem on the inside.
Quick check
?A student is drawing a plan diagram from a micrograph of a transverse section of a dicotyledonous stem. Which description matches what should be drawn?
Mission 4

Drawing from an electron micrograph

  • Trace the outline of each structure you can identify. Double membranes as two lines.
  • Rough ER: flattened sacs studded with ribosomes (dots). Golgi: a stack of curved sacs with vesicles at the edges.
  • Mitochondrion: double membrane; inner one folded into cristae.
  • Annotate each with its function.
  • Say: "ribosomes on the rough ER make proteins; the Golgi modifies and packages them" — not: "the Golgi makes proteins".
Quick check
?A student draws a cell from an electron micrograph of a gland that secretes enzymes, and must annotate each structure with its function. Which annotation is correct?
Mission 4

Worked example: magnification of a drawing

  • Magnification = size of drawing ÷ actual size, both in the same units.
  • A cell spans 12 eyepiece graticule divisions. At this objective, 1 division = 2.5 µm.
  • Step 1: actual length = 12 × 2.5 = 30 µm.
  • Step 2: the drawing is 60 mm long = 60 000 µm.
  • Step 3: magnification = 60 000 ÷ 30 = × 2000.
  • Scale bar instead: a 10 µm bar is drawn 10 × 2000 = 20 000 µm = 20 mm long.
  • Say: "draw a 10 µm scale bar at its magnified length" — not: "draw it 10 mm long".
Mission check

?A student is asked for a plan diagram of a transverse section of an artery seen at low power. The wall has three layers: a thin inner layer, a thick middle layer of muscle and elastic tissue, and a thinner outer layer. Which drawing meets the brief?

Leave out the cells, keep the proportions, and label with care: that is what turns a sketch into evidence.

Mission 5

Before you cut

  • Tools: scalpel, scissors, forceps, blunt seeker, mounted needle, dissecting board.
  • Cut away from yourself and your fingers, on the board.
  • Keep blades sharp: a blunt blade needs more force and slips.
  • Gloves on; wash hands afterwards; disinfect the board.
  • Blades into a sharps bin; tissue disposed of as instructed.
  • Ethics: organs usually come from a butcher, so no animal is killed for the lesson.
  • Anyone who objects can use models, photographs or video for the same observations.
Mission 5

Find your way round an organ

  • Orient first. Heart: the coronary artery runs diagonally across the front (ventral) surface.
  • Identify by structure, position and connections: not by how dark something looks.
  • Push a blunt seeker into each vessel. Which chamber does it lead to?
  • Artery: thick, elastic wall that stays open. Vein: thin wall that collapses flat.
  • Record: labelled drawing (see Mission 4) or photo with a ruler in view. Measure, e.g. wall thickness.
  • Say: "the left ventricle wall is thicker because it generates higher pressure to pump blood round the whole body" — not: "because it holds more blood".
Mission 5Practical

Dissections each board names

  • Assessed tasks: dissect a mammalian heart (AS); a complex dissection (A2).
  • Same routine every time: orient → identify → record → measure.
  • Organ detail: heart in a later lesson; gills and tracheae in another lesson; kidney in a later lesson.
Quick check
?A student dissects a sheep's heart and finds that the wall of the left ventricle is much thicker than the wall of the right ventricle. Which explanation is correct?
Mission 5Practical

A complex dissection

  • Assessed task: a complex dissection, e.g. insect mouthparts or leaf epidermis.
  • Work under a stereo microscope with fine forceps and mounted needles.
  • Insect mouthparts: remove each part in turn; mount them in order; draw at a stated magnification.
  • Leaf epidermis: peel the lower epidermis; mount in water; find guard cells and stomata.
  • Specimens (plant and animal groups): study living and preserved specimens, slides and photographs. Record the visible features that place each in its group.
Mission 5

Reading a stained section

  • A section is a thin slice. A tube cut across (TS) looks like a ring; cut along (LS), two parallel strips (its walls).
  • A common stain, H&E: haematoxylin colours nuclei blue-purple; eosin colours cytoplasm and fibres pink.
  • Identify tissue by cell shape, the number and position of nuclei, arrangement and striations.
  • Stain colour alone does not identify a tissue.
  • Draw a plan first (see Mission 4); then a few cells at high power.
Mission 5

Three kinds of muscle

  • Skeletal: long, unbranched fibres; striated; many nuclei at the edge of each fibre.
  • Cardiac: branched cells joined by intercalated discs; striated; one or two central nuclei.
  • Smooth: spindle-shaped cells; no striations; one central nucleus.
Quick check
?A photomicrograph shows long, unbranched, cylindrical fibres with regular cross-striations. Each fibre has many nuclei, lying just beneath its surface. Which tissue is this?
Mission 5

Blood films

  • Practical: identify blood cells and platelets in stained blood films.
  • Red blood cells: most numerous; no nucleus; pale centre (biconcave).
  • Neutrophil (polymorph): nucleus with 2–5 lobes.
  • Lymphocyte: a large round nucleus filling most of the cell.
  • Monocyte: the largest white cell; kidney-shaped nucleus.
  • Platelets: tiny cell fragments, no nucleus.
  • Draw a few of each type at high power, to scale with the red cells.
Mission 5

Sections each board names

  • Vessels in section: artery, thick muscle and elastic layer, narrow round lumen; vein, thin wall, wide lumen, often squashed.
  • Eye: conjunctiva, cornea, iris, pupil, ciliary body, suspensory ligaments, aqueous and vitreous humours, retina (rods, cones), choroid, sclera, blind spot, optic nerve.
Mission check

?A student dissects a set of sheep's lungs. Near the lungs she finds two tubes side by side. Tube P has C-shaped rings of cartilage in its wall and stays open when empty. Tube Q has a thin, soft wall with no cartilage and collapses flat. Which identification do these observations best support?

Structure, position and connections, never shade or size alone: that is how you name what you find, whether cut open or sliced thin.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Magnification = image size ÷ actual size; same units first (1 mm = 1000 µm = 1 000 000 nm).
  • Calibrate the eyepiece graticule with a stage micrometer for every objective; a scale bar gives the magnification of a micrograph.

Mission 2

  • Magnification makes an image bigger; resolution decides how much detail it holds, and is limited by wavelength (light about 200 nm).
  • TEM: thin sections, internal detail, highest resolution; SEM: 3D surfaces; both need dead specimens in a vacuum.

Mission 3

  • Homogenise in ice-cold (less enzyme activity), isotonic (no osmosis into or out of organelles), buffered (no pH change) solution; filter; spin at increasing speeds: nuclei, then mitochondria, then ER and ribosomes.
  • Isolating one type of organelle lets its function be tested directly: a new technique opened a new field.

Mission 4

  • Scientific drawings: clear continuous lines, no shading, ruled label lines, true proportions; annotations add function or features.
  • Plan (block) diagrams show tissue regions, not cells: leaf, stem with a ring of bundles (xylem inside, phloem outside).
  • Magnification = size of drawing ÷ actual size; scale bars are drawn at their magnified length.

Mission 5

  • Dissect safely (cut away, sharp blade, board, gloves) and identify by structure, position and connections; record and measure.
  • In stained sections, identify tissue by cell shape, nuclei, arrangement and striations, not stain colour.
