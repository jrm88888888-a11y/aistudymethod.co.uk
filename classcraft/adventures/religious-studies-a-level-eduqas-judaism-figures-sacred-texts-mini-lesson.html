Mission 1: God promised Abraham a land and a nation. What did Abraham have to do in return?

Make a guess: God promised Abraham a land and a nation. What did Abraham have to do in return?

Mission 1

A covenant: a binding bond

  • A covenant (Hebrew berit) is a solemn, binding agreement between two parties, sealed by an oath or a sign.
  • In the Torah, God makes covenants with people: Noah, Abraham, then all Israel at Sinai.
  • Abraham (first called Abram) leaves Haran for Canaan at God's call (Genesis 12).
  • Jews call him Avraham Avinu, "Abraham our father": the first patriarch, father of the Jewish people.
  • Jewish tradition sees Abraham as the one who recognised one God, creator of heaven and earth. He swears by "the LORD, God Most High, Maker of heaven and earth" (Genesis 14:22, JPS 1917). A well-known midrash (Genesis Rabbah 38) tells of the young Abram breaking his father's idols.

Mission 1

Land, offspring, a chosen people

  • Land: "Unto thy seed will I give this land" (Genesis 12:7, JPS 1917). The land is Canaan, later the Land of Israel.
  • Genesis 13:14–17: Abram looks north, south, east and west; all he sees is given "to thy seed for ever", and his offspring will be like "the dust of the earth" in number.
  • Offspring: a great nation, from a childless old couple (Abram and Sarai). The covenant passes to Isaac; Ishmael is blessed too, but the covenant is with Isaac (Genesis 17:19–21).
  • Chosen people: God chooses Abraham's family for a relationship and a task: to be a blessing to "all the families of the earth".

Mission 1

Brit milah: the sign in the flesh

  • Genesis 17: God renames Abram Abraham ("father of a multitude") and promises an "everlasting covenant" (17:7).
  • The sign: "every male among you shall be circumcised … it shall be a token of a covenant betwixt Me and you" (Genesis 17:10–11, JPS 1917).
  • Brit milah ("covenant of circumcision"): on the eighth day after birth (17:12), even on Shabbat or Yom Kippur, unless the baby's health requires delay.
  • Done by a mohel; the baby is named; the father blesses God, who commanded "to bring him into the covenant of Abraham our father".
  • Meaning: a Jewish boy's link to the covenant is marked on the body, generation after generation.
  • Diversity: Orthodox, Conservative and most Reform Jews keep brit milah; some Reform and secular parents hold a non-cutting ceremony (brit shalom). Girls are welcomed with naming ceremonies, e.g. simchat bat.
Mission 1

Genesis 22: the test

  • "God did prove Abraham" (Genesis 22:1, JPS 1917): take Isaac, "thine only son, whom thou lovest", and offer him on one of the mountains in the land of Moriah (22:2).
  • Abraham goes; at the last moment an angel stops him: "now I know that thou art a God-fearing man" (22:12). A ram is offered instead.
  • God repeats the promise "because thou hast hearkened to My voice" (22:18).
  • Jews call it the Akedah ("binding"). It is read on Rosh Hashanah; the shofar (ram's horn) recalls the ram.
  • Significance: faith as complete obedience and trust; Jews ask God to remember the merit of the Akedah.
  • Readings differ: many see it as rejecting child sacrifice; some modern Jewish thinkers question whether Abraham should have argued, as he did for Sodom (Genesis 18:25).

Mission 1Evaluate

Is the covenant a privilege or a responsibility?

  • Claim: The covenant with Abraham is mainly a privilege for one people.
  • For: God gives land, offspring and a special relationship to Abraham's line, through Isaac.
  • For: the promises of Genesis 12–13 come with no law code attached.
  • Against: Genesis 17 asks for a sign kept in every generation.
  • Against: Genesis 22 asks for total obedience.
  • Against: The blessing is for "all the families of the earth" (12:3): the chosen line serves others.
  • Judgement criteria: Which chapters carry most weight? Is a gift that brings duties still a privilege? How do Jews today understand it?
  • Spec issues: "covenant as a privilege or a responsibility"; "the extent of covenant's universality".

Mission 2

From slavery to Sinai

  • The book of Exodus tells that, after the Exodus from Egypt, Israel camps at Mount Sinai "in the third month" (Exodus 19:1).
  • Moses goes up the mountain; God sends a message to the whole people.
  • God first recalls what he has done: "how I bore you on eagles' wings" (19:4). Rescue comes before law.
  • The people prepare for three days: washing clothes, keeping a boundary round the mountain (19:10–15).
  • Thunder, fire, smoke and a shofar: God speaks the Ten Commandments to the whole people (Exodus 20).

Mission 2

The Ten Commandments

  • Hebrew: Aseret ha-Dibrot, "the ten words" or "ten statements" (compare 34:28, "the ten words").
  • Two stone tablets, traditionally five statements on each: the first five (God, no idols, God's name, Shabbat, honouring parents) are linked with duties to God, the second five (murder, adultery, theft, false witness, coveting) with duties to people.
  • Jewish count: the first statement is "I am the LORD thy God, who brought thee out of the land of Egypt" (Exodus 20:2, JPS 1917). Belief in God comes first.
  • Most Christians count differently: none counts "I am the LORD thy God" as a separate statement. Catholics and Lutherans take it with "no other gods" and "no idols" as the first, and split coveting in two; most Protestants and the Orthodox make "no other gods" the first and "no idols" the second.
  • They are 10 of the 613 mitzvot. Medieval rabbis (e.g. Saadia Gaon) saw all 613 as grouped under the ten.
  • The rabbis removed the daily reading of the Ten Commandments in the synagogue so that no one would claim only these ten came from Sinai (Talmud, Berakhot 12a).

Mission 2

The status of Moses

  • Moshe Rabbenu, "Moses our teacher": the greatest prophet, but never worshipped.
  • "There hath not arisen a prophet since in Israel like unto Moses, whom the LORD knew face to face" (Deuteronomy 34:10, JPS 1917).
  • God speaks with him "mouth to mouth" (Numbers 12:8); he is "very meek" (Numbers 12:3).
  • Maimonides' 7th Principle: Moses' prophecy is above that of every other prophet. 8th: the whole Torah now in our hands was given to Moses.
  • Moses has no tomb: "no man knoweth of his sepulchre" (Deuteronomy 34:6), often read as preventing a cult around him.
Mission 2

Written, broken, renewed

  • Recorded: "Moses wrote all the words of the LORD" (Exodus 24:4, JPS 1917), built an altar and 12 pillars for the 12 tribes.
  • Ratified: he read the "book of the covenant"; the people said "will we do, and obey" (24:7: na'aseh ve-nishma).
  • Sign of blood: "Behold the blood of the covenant, which the LORD hath made with you" (Exodus 24:8, JPS 1917): half the blood on the altar, half on the people. Both parties bound.
  • Failure: the golden calf (Exodus 32). Moses breaks the tablets (32:19).
  • Renewed: "Hew thee two tables of stone like unto the first" (34:1); God tells Moses, "Write thou these words" (34:27); the tablets bear "the words of the covenant, the ten words" (34:28).
  • Protection for obedience: "before all thy people I will do marvels" (34:10); "Observe thou that which I am commanding thee this day; behold, I am driving out before thee the Amorite …" (Exodus 34:11, JPS 1917).

Mission 2Diversity

Sinai today

  • Orthodox: the covenant is binding law (halakhah): all applicable mitzvot, as interpreted by the oral Torah. Revelation at Sinai is historical.
  • Conservative (Masorti): halakhah binding, but it develops; Sinai as an ongoing revelation through interpretation.
  • Reform / Liberal: the moral law (e.g. the Ten Commandments) binds; ritual laws are chosen where they bring meaning. Classical Reform's Pittsburgh Platform (1885) accepted "only the moral laws" as binding; later Reform statements (1999) value more ritual practice.
  • Shavuot celebrates the giving of the Torah; the Ten Commandments are read in the synagogue, and the congregation stands.
  • Significance at the time of Moses: a freed people becomes a nation under God's law. Today: identity, ethics and practice for Jews everywhere.
Mission 2Evaluate

Is the Sinai covenant mainly a legal contract?

  • Claim: The Sinai covenant is above all a legal agreement.
  • For: It has stated requirements, a written record and sanctions, like a legal agreement.
  • For: It is the source of halakhah: Jewish law still applies it in courts (beit din) and daily life.
  • Against: It begins with rescue and love ("eagles' wings"), not a contract; obedience answers grace.
  • Against: Prophets later describe the covenant as a marriage (Hosea 2:21–22 in Jewish numbering).
  • Against: Some say covenant could become a tool of religious control (law enforced by priests); others answer that the people freely consent (24:7).
  • Judgement criteria: What does "legal" mean: binding, or impersonal? Do Orthodox and Reform Jews experience it the same way?
  • Spec issues: "how far covenant is of legal value"; "whether covenant is a method of religious control".

Mission 3

Three parts: TaNaKh

  • Tenakh is an acronym: Torah + Nevi'im + Ketuvim. 24 books in the Jewish count, mostly in Hebrew.
  • Torah (teaching, instruction): Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy. The heart of the Tenakh.
  • Nevi'im (Prophets): Joshua to Kings (history); Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel and the Twelve.
  • Ketuvim (Writings): Psalms, Proverbs, Job, the five Megillot (scrolls, e.g. Esther, Ruth), Daniel, Ezra–Nehemiah, Chronicles.
  • "Torah" can also mean all Jewish teaching, written and oral.
  • Your spec spells it Tanakh.
Mission 3

Given at Sinai

  • The Torah's account: after the Exodus, Israel camps at Mount Sinai (Exodus 19). God makes a covenant with the people.
  • God speaks the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20); Moses receives the rest of the teaching.
  • The people promise to do all God has said (Exodus 24:7); the rabbis read the Hebrew order, "we will do" before "we will hear", as accepting before fully understanding.
  • Moses writes the Torah and gives it to the priests and elders (Deuteronomy 31:9).
  • Revelation is to a whole people, not one individual: the root of Jewish identity.
  • 613 mitzvot (commandments) are counted in the Torah.

Mission 3Diversity

Word of God or inspired word?

  • Orthodox: Torah min ha-shamayim ("Torah from heaven"). God gave the whole Torah to Moses; every letter is divine, complete, unchangeable. Maimonides' 8th Principle.
  • "Ye shall not add unto the word which I command you, neither shall ye diminish from it" (Deuteronomy 4:2, JPS 1917).
  • Reform and Liberal: the Torah is inspired by God but written by humans over centuries; it records Israel's encounter with God. Ethical teaching binds; ritual laws may change.
  • Modern scholarship (e.g. the theory of several sources behind the Torah) shaped this view.
  • Masorti (Conservative): divine in origin, passed on and shaped by humans; law still binding but developing.
  • All treat the Torah as holy and central.
Mission 3

A Torah before the world

  • Pre-existent: a midrash says God looked into the Torah and created the world, like an architect using plans (Genesis Rabbah 1:1, paraphrase).
  • Linked to Wisdom in Proverbs: "The LORD made me as the beginning of His way" (Proverbs 8:22, JPS 1917). The rabbis read "Wisdom" as Torah.
  • Eternal relevance: "It is not in heaven" (Deuteronomy 30:12, JPS 1917): the Torah is given to humans to live by, in every age.
  • Orthodox: eternal and unchanging, so every generation is bound.
  • Reform: eternally relevant in its values, applied freshly in each age.

Mission 3

The Tenakh in worship and life

  • Synagogue: the Torah is read on Shabbat, Mondays, Thursdays and festivals; the yearly cycle ends and restarts at Simchat Torah. Public reading needs a minyan.
  • A haftarah from the Nevi'im follows the Shabbat reading; the Megillot are read at festivals (Esther at Purim, Ruth at Shavuot).
  • Psalms fill daily prayer; the Shema (Deuteronomy 6:4–9) is in the mezuzah and tefillin.
  • Home and life: study, Shabbat table discussion of the week's portion; guidance for ethics and law.
  • Beliefs shape use: Orthodox Jews follow the laws as divine command; some Reform and Masorti communities read a third of each portion a week (a three-year cycle) and choose which practices to keep.
Mission 3

The Sefer Torah: care and study

  • Sefer Torah: handwritten in Hebrew by a trained scribe (sofer) on parchment, with a quill; one error makes it unfit until corrected.
  • Kept in the Aron Hakodesh (ark), facing Jerusalem; dressed in a mantle; read with a yad (pointer) so hands do not touch the text.
  • People stand when the ark is opened; it is lifted and shown to the congregation. On return: "She is a tree of life to them that lay hold upon her" (Proverbs 3:18, JPS 1917).
  • A worn-out scroll is not thrown away but buried in a Jewish cemetery.
  • Yeshiva: a place of study of Tenakh and Talmud, often in pairs (chavruta), where the oral Torah opens the written one.
Mission 3Evaluate

Is the Torah still the main authority?

  • Claim: The Torah remains the main authority in Judaism today.
  • For: it is read in every synagogue, holds the 613 mitzvot and is treated with unique honour.
  • For: both Orthodox and Reform Jews call it holy and base their identity on Sinai.
  • Against: in practice the Talmud and later codes decide how laws apply (see Mission 4); rabbis interpret.
  • Against: many secular and Progressive Jews give individual conscience and modern ethics the final say.
  • Judge it by: Does "authority" mean honour or decision-making? Can the written Torah be separated from its interpretation? Which Jews?
  • Spec issue: "whether the Torah has become a religious icon".

Mission 4

A text that needs explaining

  • The written Torah gives commands without details:
  • "thou shalt not do any manner of work" on the Sabbath (Exodus 20:10, JPS 1917): what counts as work? (The Mishnah later lists 39 categories of work, such as kindling a fire, writing and building: Shabbat 7:2.)
  • "thou shalt bind them for a sign upon thy hand" (Deuteronomy 6:8, JPS 1917): how? (the answer: tefillin).
  • Slaughter "as I have commanded thee" (Deuteronomy 12:21, JPS 1917): where was that commanded? Nowhere in writing.
  • Rabbinic Judaism teaches that God gave Moses an oral Torah with the written one: explanations, methods and laws passed on by word of mouth.
  • Together: Torah she-bikhtav (written) and Torah she-be'al peh (oral).
Mission 4Read the source

The chain of transmission

> "Write thou these words, for after the tenor of these words I have made a covenant with thee and with Israel." (Exodus 34:27, JPS 1917)

  • The Hebrew for "after the tenor of" is literally "by the mouth of": the Talmud reads this as a hint of an oral covenant too (Gittin 60b).
  • Pirkei Avot 1:1 (paraphrase): Moses received Torah from Sinai and handed it to Joshua; Joshua to the elders; the elders to the prophets; the prophets to the Men of the Great Assembly.
  • Then pairs of sages, down to Hillel and Shammai (1st century BCE): order gives authority.

Mission 4

The Mishnah

  • Why written down: after the Temple's destruction (70 CE) and the failed Bar Kokhba revolt (132–135), sages feared the oral teaching would be lost.
  • Edited c. 200 CE by Rabbi Judah ha-Nasi in the land of Israel, in Hebrew.
  • Content: six orders (Shas): Zera'im (seeds, farming), Mo'ed (festivals), Nashim (women, marriage), Nezikin (damages, civil law), Kodashim (holy things), Tohorot (purity); 63 tractates.
  • Style: short, arranged by topic not by verse; records disputes and minority opinions by name.
  • The teachers of the Mishnah are the Tannaim.
  • Importance: the first authoritative code of the oral Torah; studied as the base text of the Talmud.
Mission 4

The Gemara

  • Gemara: the discussion of the Mishnah by later sages, the Amoraim (c. 200–500 CE), mostly in Aramaic.
  • Style: a debate on paper: question, answer, objection, proof from scripture, digressions. Often no final ruling.
  • Content: halakhah (law) and aggadah (stories, ethics, theology, history).
  • Talmud = Mishnah + Gemara.
  • A printed page: Mishnah and Gemara in the centre; Rashi's commentary on the inner side; Tosafot on the outer side.
  • Importance: it teaches how to reason about law, not only what the law is.

Mission 4

Two Talmuds

  • Jerusalem Talmud (Yerushalmi): from the academies of Galilee (e.g. Tiberias); completed c. 400 CE; Western Aramaic; shorter and less edited; covers the farming laws of the land of Israel.
  • Babylonian Talmud (Bavli): from the academies of Sura and Pumbedita; main editing c. 500 CE (Rav Ashi and Ravina), with later polishing; Eastern Aramaic; much longer, fuller aggadah.
  • The Bavli became the Talmud: Babylonia's academies led the Jewish world for centuries; later authorities ruled it decisive where the two disagree.

"It is not in heaven"

> "It is not in heaven, that thou shouldest say: 'Who shall go up for us to heaven, and bring it unto us'" (Deuteronomy 30:12, JPS 1917)

  • Talmud, Bava Metzia 59b (paraphrase): Rabbi Eliezer proves his view with miracles and a heavenly voice. Rabbi Joshua answers: "It is not in heaven." The majority of the sages decides.
  • The story ends with God smiling: "My children have defeated Me."

Mission 4Diversity

Whose authority?

  • Orthodox: the oral Torah is divine revelation given at Sinai with the written Torah; the Talmud is binding as rabbis apply it. Many study a page a day (daf yomi), finishing the Talmud every seven and a half years.
  • Masorti (Conservative): authoritative, but developed over history; law can change by rabbinic process.
  • Reform and Liberal: human wisdom from inspired teachers; valuable, not binding; individual informed choice.
  • Karaites (from the 8th century): reject the oral Torah altogether, following the written text only.
  • Challenges: historians trace the Talmud's growth over centuries; the Talmud itself records disagreement. Orthodox reply: disagreement belongs inside one revealed tradition.
Mission 4Evaluate

Is the Mishnah the most important part of the Talmud?

  • Claim: The Mishnah is the most important element of the Talmud.
  • For: it is the base text; earlier, closer to the chain of transmission; Hebrew; concise statement of the law.
  • For: without it the Gemara has nothing to discuss.
  • Against: the Gemara explains, tests and applies the Mishnah; most halakhic rulings come from it.
  • Against: the Gemara teaches the reasoning method and holds most of the aggadah.
  • Judge it by: Importance for what: law, study, or ethics? Can either part stand alone?

Mission 5

Searching the text

  • Midrash comes from darash, "to seek" or "to inquire": searching the Tanakh for meaning.
  • It is both a method of reading and the collections of rabbinic interpretation that resulted.
  • Purposes: fill gaps in the story; explain repetitions or contradictions; draw out laws; apply old words to new times; comfort and teach.
  • Rabbis treat every word, letter and repetition as meaningful, since the text is from God.
  • Main collections: Mekhilta (on Exodus), Sifra (Leviticus), Sifre (Numbers, Deuteronomy), Genesis Rabbah (c. 5th century) and the wider Midrash Rabbah.
Mission 5

Halakhah and Aggadah

  • Midrash halakhah: interpretation that produces law: what Jews must do.
  • Midrash aggadah: interpretation that tells stories, teaches ethics and theology: why and how to live.
  • Same texts, different questions: "What must I do?" and "What does this mean about God and us?"
  • Mekhilta, Sifra and Sifre are mainly halakhic; Genesis Rabbah is mainly aggadic.
  • The Talmud mixes both on almost every page.

Mission 5

PaRDeS: four levels

  • Peshat: plain, literal, in context.
  • Remez: hint or allusion, e.g. a pattern in letters or numbers.
  • Derash: homily: the interpretive, preached meaning.
  • Sod: hidden, mystical meaning (Kabbalah).
  • Together: PaRDeS, "orchard" (the word behind "paradise").
  • The four-level scheme was set out in medieval Spain (13th century); the methods are older.
  • Rule: "a verse never leaves its plain meaning" (Talmud, Shabbat 63a, paraphrase): deeper levels add to peshat, they do not cancel it.
Mission 5Read the source

Genesis 1:1 four ways

> "In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth." (Genesis 1:1, JPS 1917)

  • Peshat: God is the creator of everything; the world had a beginning.
  • Remez: the Torah's first letter is bet (ב), closed on three sides and open forward: do not speculate about what was before creation, only what follows (Genesis Rabbah 1:10, paraphrase).
  • Derash: Rashi asks why the Torah starts with creation and not the first law (Exodus 12:2). Answer: to show the whole earth belongs to God, who gives the land to whom He wills (paraphrase).
  • Sod: kabbalists read "beginning" as the first hidden emanation of divine Wisdom.

Mission 5

Making law from a repeated verse

> "Thou shalt not seethe a kid in its mother's milk." (Exodus 23:19, JPS 1917)

  • The same words appear three times (Exodus 23:19; 34:26; Deuteronomy 14:21).
  • The rabbis ask why the Torah repeats itself, and answer: three separate laws: do not cook meat with milk, do not eat it, do not benefit from it (Talmud, Chullin 115b, paraphrase).
  • From this comes the separation of meat and dairy in kashrut.
  • This is midrash halakhah: close reading of the text produces practice.
Mission 5

613 mitzvot: God's will?

  • The Talmud counts 613 mitzvot: 248 positive ("do") and 365 negative ("do not") (Makkot 23b).
  • Halakhah ("the way to walk"): the whole body of Jewish law drawn from the mitzvot, the oral Torah and later rulings.
  • Some mitzvot cannot be kept today (Temple sacrifices); some apply only to priests, or to the land of Israel.
  • Covers worship, food, Shabbat, family, business, ethics: every part of life.
Mission 5Diversity

Orthodox and Reform on Halakhah

  • Orthodox: Halakhah is the revealed will of God, from the written and oral Torah given at Sinai; binding on every Jew; it changes only through recognised rabbinic process.
  • Reform: the Pittsburgh Platform (1885) accepted only the moral laws as binding, rejecting many ritual laws as unsuited to modern life.
  • Reform Judaism today: Halakhah is a valued guide; each Jew makes informed choices; many Reform Jews have taken up practices again (e.g. Shabbat candles, kashrut in some form).
  • Masorti: Halakhah binding, but developing with history.
  • The difference rests on the Torah's origin (see Mission 3): dictated, or inspired and humanly written.
Mission 5

Why Aggadah matters

  • Purpose: to teach values, theology and hope through story, parable and sermon.
  • Example: "whoever destroys one life is as if he destroyed a whole world; whoever saves one life, as if he saved a whole world" (Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5, paraphrase).
  • Example: as the Egyptians drown, God rebukes the angels who want to sing: "My creatures are drowning, and you sing?" (Megillah 10b, paraphrase).
  • Role: gives the reasons behind laws; comforted Jews after the Temple's destruction and in exile; shapes festival teaching (e.g. the Passover Haggadah).
  • Bialik (1873–1934), "Halakhah and Aggadah" (1917), paraphrase: the two belong together; Aggadah without Halakhah loses its way, Halakhah without Aggadah becomes dry.
Mission 5Evaluate

Is midrash an imprecise science?

  • Claim: Midrash is an imprecise science.
  • For: different rabbis draw very different meanings from one verse; remez and sod can seem to read in anything.
  • For: modern critical scholars say midrash often ignores historical context.
  • Against: it follows rules (e.g. Hillel's seven and Rabbi Ishmael's thirteen principles of interpretation) and must not leave the peshat.
  • Against: its aim is not one "correct" answer but many meanings: "seventy faces to the Torah" (a later rabbinic saying).
  • Judge it by: Is "science" the right test for religious reading? Do the rules limit interpretation enough? Does variety weaken or enrich authority?
Mission 5Evaluate

Halakhah or Aggadah: which matters more?

  • Claim: Halakhah is more important than Aggadah for Judaism.
  • For: Judaism is lived through practice (mitzvot); Halakhah tells Jews what to do; Orthodox identity centres on it.
  • For: the rabbis decide law from Halakhah, not from stories.
  • Against: Aggadah gives the reasons and the spirit; without it law loses meaning (Bialik).
  • Against: for Reform and many secular Jews, the ethical and narrative heritage matters more than ritual law.
  • Judge it by: Which Jews? What is Judaism mainly: law, story, or both? Can the two be separated?

Mission 6

Rashi of Troyes

  • Rashi = Rabbi Shlomo Yitzchaki (1040–1105), of Troyes, in Champagne, northern France.
  • Studied in the great Rhineland academies of Mainz and Worms, then led his own school in Troyes.
  • Wrote commentaries on almost the whole Tanakh and almost the whole Babylonian Talmud.
  • Context: in 1096 crusaders massacred the Jewish communities of Speyer, Worms and Mainz, where he had studied.
  • His comments at times answer Christian readings of scripture or reflect Israel's suffering among the nations.
Mission 6

Peshat: Rashi's method

  • Rashi's goal: the plain sense (peshat) of each verse, in its context.
  • In his own words (on Genesis 3:8): he has come only for the plain meaning, and for such aggadah as "settles" the words of the verse (paraphrase).
  • Close reading: he notices a strange word, a repetition, a gap, and asks the question a careful reader would ask.
  • Forensic exegesis: grammar, context and parallels as evidence.
  • He uses midrash selectively: only where it explains the text.
  • He glosses hard words in Old French, written in Hebrew letters (la'azim).
  • Eduqas: clarity through peshat; compare the four PaRDeS levels (see Mission 5).
Mission 6Read the source

Rashi on Genesis 1:1

> "IN THE beginning God created the heaven and the earth." (Genesis 1:1, JPS 1917)

  • Why begin with creation? Rashi asks: a book of law could have begun with the first commandment to Israel (Exodus 12:2).
  • Answer (paraphrase): so that if the nations call Israel robbers for taking the land, Israel can reply that the whole earth belongs to God, who gives it to whom He wills.
  • What does the grammar say? The verse does not teach the order of creation: water is already there in verse 2.
  • Read it as: "In the beginning of God's creating the heaven and the earth, when the earth was unformed… God said: Let there be light" (paraphrase).

Mission 6

Rashi and the Talmud page

  • Rashi's Talmud commentary goes word by word: he explains terms, supplies missing steps and shows where each argument begins and ends.
  • Without it, few could study the Aramaic Talmud alone; with it, the Talmud became open to every student.
  • Where he died before finishing (e.g. tractate Makkot), his pupils completed the work.
  • Printed editions place Rashi in the inner column beside the text; the complete Venice edition of Daniel Bomberg (1520–23) fixed this layout.
  • The first dated printed Hebrew book was Rashi's Torah commentary (Reggio di Calabria, 1475).
  • Eduqas: this is Rashi's importance in the history of Talmudic study.
Mission 6

His pupils and the tosafot

  • Rashi had no sons; his daughters married scholars, and his grandsons led the next generation.
  • Rashbam (Samuel ben Meir, c. 1085–c. 1158) pressed peshat further, sometimes against his grandfather's readings.
  • Rabbeinu Tam (Jacob ben Meir, c. 1100–1171) led the tosafists.
  • Tosafot ("additions"): 12th–14th-century comments from France and Germany, printed in the outer column.
  • Method: compare passages across the whole Talmud, find contradictions, resolve them by fine distinctions; often question Rashi.
  • A practical trace: Rashi and Rabbeinu Tam disagreed on the order of passages in tefillin; some Jews put on a second pair, "Rabbeinu Tam tefillin".

Mission 6Evaluate

Is Rashi the most important medieval Jewish commentator?

  • Claim: Rashi is the most important medieval Jewish commentator.
  • For: Universal use: printed with every Chumash and Talmud; first commentary taught to children.
  • For: His clarity made the Talmud open to all, and the tosafot built on his work.
  • Against: Maimonides shaped law and philosophy more deeply (see Mission 7); Ibn Ezra and Rashbam were stricter about peshat.
  • Against: Rashi explains texts; others (Maimonides) asked why to believe at all.
  • Judgement criteria: Importance for whom (students, legal scholars, philosophers)? Breadth of influence or depth of ideas? Then or now?
  • Eduqas issue: the relative importance of Rashi and Maimonides for understanding Hebrew scriptures.

Mission 7

Three great works

  • Maimonides (Rambam, 1138–1204): rabbi, philosopher and court physician in Fustat (Old Cairo).
  • Commentary on the Mishnah (Arabic, c. 1168): explains each Mishnah; includes the Thirteen Principles.
  • Mishneh Torah (Hebrew, c. 1180): a complete code of Jewish law, in 14 books.
  • Guide for the Perplexed (Arabic, c. 1190): philosophy for a Jew torn between Torah and reason.
  • One aim runs through all three: to know God truly, and to live by the Torah with understanding.
Mission 7

The Mishneh Torah

  • Means "Repetition of the Torah"; also called Yad ha-Chazakah, "the Strong Hand" (yad = 14 in Hebrew letters: 14 books).
  • Covers all of Jewish law, including Temple laws not practised since 70 CE.
  • Clear Mishnaic Hebrew; gives rulings without sources or debates.
  • His aim (paraphrase): read the Written Torah, then this book, and you know the whole Oral Torah.
  • Book 1, the Book of Knowledge, opens with God, the universe and how to love God.
  • Critics: Raavad and others feared readers would skip the Talmud and could not check his rulings.
  • Influence: a foundation of Joseph Karo's Shulchan Arukh (1565).
Mission 7

The Guide for the Perplexed

  • Written as letters to a student, Joseph ben Judah, perplexed because Torah and philosophy seemed to clash.
  • Arabic, Hebrew letters; quickly translated into Hebrew (Samuel ibn Tibbon, 1204) and Latin.
  • Method: reason and Torah cannot truly conflict, since both come from God.
  • Where a verse seems to clash with what is proved, read the verse figuratively (e.g. God's "hand").
  • Approach to midrash: philosophy and reason; many midrashim are parables that hide deeper truths.
  • Written deliberately in hints: deep matters are for the prepared, not for everyone.
Mission 7

Creation: where Maimonides rejects Aristotle

  • Guide II.13: three views of the world's origin:
  • Torah of Moses: God brought the world, and time itself, into being from nothing.
  • Plato: God shaped the world from eternal matter.
  • Aristotle: the world is eternal and flows from God by necessity.
  • Maimonides: Aristotle never proved eternity; the arguments on both sides are not conclusive.
  • If eternity had been proved, he would read Genesis figuratively, as he does God's body (Guide II.25, paraphrase).
  • Since it has not, he accepts creation: a God who wills can give a Law and work miracles; an eternal, necessary world leaves no room for either.

Mission 7Read the source

Nature reveals God's glory

Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Foundations of the Torah 2:2 (paraphrase):

  • How does a person come to love and fear God?
  • By contemplating God's great and wonderful works and creatures, and seeing in them God's wisdom, which has no limit.
  • At once the person loves, praises and longs to know God, as David said:

> "My soul thirsteth for God, for the living God" (Psalm 42:2, JPS 1917)

  • Then, reflecting more, the person draws back in awe, knowing how small and dark they are before God's knowledge:

> "When I behold Thy heavens, the work of Thy fingers, the moon and the stars, which Thou hast established; What is man, that Thou art mindful of him?" (Psalm 8:3–4, JPS 1917)

Mission 7

Genesis 1: Rashi and Maimonides compared

  • Rashi (see Mission 6): asks what the words plainly mean. "In the beginning" is "in the beginning of God's creating"; the verse does not teach the order of creation. The Torah starts with creation to show the earth is God's to give.
  • Maimonides: Genesis 1 is the Account of Creation: physics in the form of a parable, for the many to read and the few to understand (Guide II.29–30, paraphrase).
  • "Beginning" cannot be the first moment in time, since time itself was created.
  • The rabbis taught that heaven and earth were created together, then separated: the days give an order of nature, not only a sequence of 24-hour periods (paraphrase).
  • Shared: God is the creator; the world is not eternal by necessity; the Torah is true.
  • Different: Rashi aims at clarity for every reader; Maimonides at hidden philosophical truth for the prepared.
Mission 7Evaluate

Is Maimonides the most complete Jewish scholar?

  • Claim: Maimonides is the most complete Jewish scholar.
  • For: He mastered every field: Mishnah, Talmudic law, philosophy, science, medicine.
  • For: His code still guides halakhah; his principles are in the prayer book.
  • Against: Rashi is more widely studied: every child meets Rashi first (see Mission 6).
  • Against: He neglected mysticism (Kabbalah) and poetry (religious verse).
  • Against: His philosophy depends on Aristotle's science, now abandoned.
  • Judgement criteria: "Complete" in breadth or in influence? Complete for which Jews (Orthodox, Progressive, Hasidic)? Then or now?
  • Eduqas issues: the relative importance of Rashi and Maimonides; whether Maimonides is the most complete Jewish scholar.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • In Genesis 12, 15 and 17 God promises Abraham land, offspring and a blessing for all families of the earth; Abraham responds with trust and the sign of circumcision.
  • Brit milah on the eighth day keeps the sign; Genesis 22 shows faith as obedience.

Mission 2

  • In the Exodus account, God, who had rescued Israel, offers a covenant at Sinai: "if" Israel keeps it, they will be a treasured, holy nation; the people agree (Exodus 19:8).
  • The Ten Commandments begin, in Jewish counting, with "I am the LORD thy God".
  • Moses wrote the covenant down, the people agreed again (24:7), and it was renewed after the golden calf (Exodus 24; 32; 34).

Mission 3

  • The Tenakh = Torah, Nevi'im, Ketuvim; the Torah tells of its own giving at Sinai through Moses, within a covenant.
  • Orthodox Jews hold the Torah is the complete, unchangeable Word of God (Torah min ha-shamayim); Reform and Liberal Jews see it as inspired but humanly written.
  • The Sefer Torah is read publicly, studied, and treated with great care: ark, mantle, yad, burial.

Mission 4

  • The oral Torah explains the written Torah; Pirkei Avot 1:1 traces its chain from Moses to the Men of the Great Assembly.
  • Mishnah (Hebrew, c. 200 CE, six orders) + Gemara (Aramaic discussion) = Talmud; the Babylonian Talmud became more authoritative than the Jerusalem Talmud.

Mission 5

  • Midrash ("seeking") interprets the Tanakh to draw out law (Halakhah) and story, ethics and theology (Aggadah).
  • PaRDeS: peshat (plain), remez (hint), derash (homily), sod (hidden); deeper readings never cancel the plain meaning.
  • Orthodox Jews see Halakhah as God's revealed, binding will; Reform Jews treat it as a guide for informed choice.

Mission 6

  • Rashi (1040–1105) explained the Tanakh and Talmud through peshat, close reading and selected midrash; his pupils and the tosafot built on and challenged him.
  • Rashi's clarity made him central to Talmud study; compare him with Maimonides in Mission 7.

Mission 7

  • Maimonides wrote the Commentary on the Mishnah, the Mishneh Torah (a code without sources) and the Guide (philosophy); reason and Torah cannot truly conflict.
  • He proved God on Aristotle's premises but rejected Aristotle's eternal world as unproved; Genesis 1 for him is physics in a parable, unlike Rashi's plain reading.
  • Contemplating nature reveals God's wisdom and glory, leading to love and then awe.
