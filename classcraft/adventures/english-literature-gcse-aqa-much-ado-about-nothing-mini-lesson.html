Mission 1

Two love stories, one play. Why does Shakespeare need both?

Make a guess: Two love stories, one play. Why does Shakespeare need both?

Much Ado About Nothing · two plots, one playScreen 1

The play in five acts

Much Ado About Nothing was probably written in 1598–99 and was first printed in 1600. It is set in Messina, Sicily, as soldiers return from a war.

ActWhat happens
1Don Pedro's army arrives at Leonato's house. Claudio falls for Hero; Don Pedro offers to woo her for him. Beatrice and Benedick trade insults. Don John looks for mischief.
2At a masked dance Don Pedro wins Hero for Claudio; the wedding is set. Don Pedro plans to make Beatrice and Benedick fall in love. Borachio plots against Hero. Benedick overhears he is loved.
3Beatrice overhears she is loved. Don John tells Claudio that Hero is "disloyal"; that night (offstage) Claudio sees a man at her window. The Watch overhear Borachio boasting and arrest him.
4Claudio shames Hero at the altar. The Friar has her reported dead. Beatrice and Benedick confess their love; she asks him to challenge Claudio.
5Borachio confesses. Claudio mourns at Hero's tomb and agrees to marry Leonato's "niece", who is Hero. Beatrice and Benedick agree to marry.

How your exam asks this

  • AQA Paper 1 Section A (closed book): one question on an extract, then the play as a whole. Usually 30 marks plus 4 for spelling, punctuation and grammar (AO4).
  • Essay practice is for class. These missions build what every answer needs: knowing the play, its methods and its key lines.
Much Ado About Nothing · two plots, one playScreen 3

Two plots and a third

  • Main plot: Hero and Claudio. A quick match, arranged by Don Pedro and Leonato, wrecked by Don John's lie. The story comes from Ariosto and Bandello.
  • Subplot: Beatrice and Benedick. Two sharp talkers who claim to hate love. Largely Shakespeare's own invention.
  • Comic plot: Dogberry and the Watch. Bumbling night-watchmen who stumble on the truth.
  • A subplot is a second story that runs beside the main one and comments on it.

Much Ado About Nothing · two plots, one playScreen 5

Where the plots cross

  • The plots are parallel: two couples, two tricks by overhearing, two weddings in Act 5.
  • They meet in Act 4 Scene 1. In the scene that breaks Hero and Claudio, Beatrice and Benedick confess their love.
  • The comic plot holds the answer early: the Watch arrest Borachio in Act 3, but Dogberry is too long-winded to tell Leonato before the wedding. The audience knows; the characters don't (dramatic irony).

The two love stories are built to be compared. Claudio falls for Hero at a glance and trusts what he sees; Beatrice and Benedick argue their way to love and must learn to trust each other. The plots cross in the church, where the comic couple has to deal with the tragic one.

Mission 2

They trade insults at every meeting. Why do they end up married?

Make a guess: They trade insults at every meeting. Why do they end up married?

Much Ado About Nothing · Beatrice and BenedickScreen 1

The "merry war"

  • Leonato says of them: "they never meet but there’s a skirmish of wit between them." (1.1)
  • Beatrice's first question in the play is about Benedick, using a mocking fencing nickname, "Signior Mountanto".
  • Both swear off marriage. Benedick: "I will live a bachelor." Beatrice (2.1): she could not "endure a husband with a beard on his face".
  • They speak quick prose in repartee: rapid replies that pick up and twist each other's words.

Much Ado About Nothing · Beatrice and BenedickScreen 4

Hurt beneath the wit

  • At the masked dance (2.1) Beatrice calls Benedick "the Prince's jester". Stung, he tells Don Pedro: "She speaks poniards, and every word stabs".
  • She says Benedick once lent her his heart, "and I gave him use for it, a double heart for a single one" and that he won it "with false dice".
  • Many readers hear a past romance that ended badly; others, only a joke. Either way, insults can work as armour: strike first, so as not to be hurt.

?They trade insults at every meeting. Why do they end up married?

Their insults are a sign of interest, and perhaps of an old hurt, as much as dislike. The overhearing tricks don't create love from nothing; they let two proud people admit it. The church scene then tests that love with action, not words.

Mission 3

Claudio shames Hero at the altar. Does he deserve her at the end?

Make a guess: Claudio shames Hero at the altar. Does he deserve her at the end?

Much Ado About Nothing · Hero and ClaudioScreen 1

A match made by others

  • Claudio falls for Hero's looks: "Can the world buy such a jewel?" Then he asks Don Pedro, "Hath Leonato any son, my lord?" She is the "only heir".
  • Don Pedro woos Hero for him, masked; Leonato hands her over "and with her my fortunes". Hero speaks no reply.
  • Many readers see money in Claudio's question; others see what a careful suitor of the time would ask. Among the gentry, fathers largely controlled their daughters' marriages.

Much Ado About Nothing · Hero and ClaudioScreen 5

Hero's voice

  • In public Hero says little. In the church she answers plainly: "I talk’d with no man at that hour, my lord."
  • With women she is lively: she runs the 3.1 trick on Beatrice, ending with a couplet: "Some Cupid kills with arrows, some with traps."
  • Revived after fainting, she swears her innocence; then she is silent until Act 5, when she unmasks: "One Hero died defil’d, but I do live,". She never says whether she forgives Claudio.
Much Ado About Nothing · Hero and ClaudioScreen 6

Is Claudio redeemable?

  • For him: the window scene was staged to fool his eyes, and the Prince was fooled too. On hearing the truth he says "I have drunk poison whiles he utter’d it." He mourns at her tomb.
  • Against him: he plans the public shaming before seeing anything ("there will I shame her", 3.2). He jokes soon after hearing she is dead. He says "yet sinn’d I not / But in mistaking".
  • Readers and productions disagree. The play gives evidence both ways.

There's no single answer, and the play seems to want us uneasy. Claudio was fooled by a scene staged for his eyes and he does public penance, but he planned Hero's humiliation and is quick to joke. Hero's silence in Act 5 leaves the audience to decide.

Mission 4

Two tricks by overhearing: one makes love, one nearly kills. Why?

Make a guess: Two tricks by overhearing: one makes love, one nearly kills. Why?

Much Ado About Nothing · deception and 'noting'Screen 1

"Nothing" and "noting"

  • In Shakespeare's day "nothing" and "noting" probably sounded almost the same.
  • Noting meant observing, overhearing, gossiping, and also musical notes. So the title can mean ‘a great fuss about overhearing’. ("Nothing" was also a rude slang word.)
  • The pun is played on stage. Balthasar: "There’s not a note of mine that’s worth the noting." Don Pedro: "Notes, notes, forsooth, and nothing!" (2.3)

Much Ado About Nothing · deception and 'noting'Screen 5

The same trick, used to harm

  • Borachio plans to let Claudio see him court Margaret, called by Hero's name, at Hero's window, so that "jealousy shall be called assurance" (2.2).
  • Don John taunts the doubting men: "If you dare not trust that you see, confess not that you know." (3.2)
  • Shakespeare keeps the window scene offstage: we hear of it only when Borachio boasts to Conrade (3.3). So the audience watches the wedding with dramatic irony: we know what Claudio doesn't.

Much Ado About Nothing · deception and 'noting'Screen 7

Deception that heals

  • The Friar's plan: report Hero dead, so that grief will "Change slander to remorse" (4.1). He tells her: "Come, lady, die to live".
  • Leonato's "niece" in Act 5 is another kindly trick: Claudio must marry without seeing her face.
  • Deception in Messina is a tool. The same method can wound or heal; the motive decides.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Two tricks by overhearing: one makes love, one nearly kills. Why?

Both tricks work the same way: people are made to "note" a staged scene. One is told with a loving motive and tells proud lovers what they half want to hear; the other is told with malice and feeds Claudio's fear. The title's pun on "noting" points to how easily seeing and hearing can mislead.

Mission 5

One rumour, and Hero's own father wishes her dead. Why?

Make a guess: One rumour, and Hero's own father wishes her dead. Why?

Much Ado About Nothing · honour, gender and reputationScreen 1

Honour in Messina

  • For men, honour meant reputation: courage, keeping your word, not being laughed at.
  • For women, honour meant chastity: virginity before marriage and faithfulness after. A daughter's chastity was part of her family's honour too.
  • The men's endless jokes about horns (the sign of a cuckold, a man whose wife is unfaithful) show their fear of being fooled by women.
  • So a rumour about Hero threatens her father, her groom and the Prince.

?One rumour, and Hero's own father wishes her dead. Why?

Leonato's horror makes sense in a world where a daughter's chastity is her family's honour: the princes' word ruins her, and so him. Shakespeare lets us see the cost of that code, through a father who believes rumour over his child and a Beatrice who can only wish she were a man.

Mission 6

The play opens as soldiers come home. Why start a comedy with a war?

Make a guess: The play opens as soldiers come home. Why start a comedy with a war?

Much Ado About Nothing · war, courtship and the first audienceScreen 1

Messina after the war

  • The play opens with a letter: Don Pedro, Prince of Arragon, has won his war, losing, the Messenger says, "But few of any sort, and none of name."
  • His half-brother Don John has "of late stood out against" him (1.3), and is now "reconciled" but bitter.
  • Messina is in Sicily, ruled by Aragon in the play. The soldiers have time on their hands: they will "stay here at the least a month".
  • Comedies traditionally end in marriage; this one begins with soldiers at leisure.

Much Ado About Nothing · war, courtship and the first audienceScreen 6

Courtship for Shakespeare's audience

  • Among the gentry, fathers largely controlled their daughters' marriages. A friend or patron could woo for a suitor, as Don Pedro does.
  • Shakespeare may have known Castiglione's The Courtier (translated by Hoby, 1561), a book about life at court; it may lie behind Beatrice and Benedick.
  • Masked dances (revels) let people flirt behind visors, as in 2.1.
Much Ado About Nothing · war, courtship and the first audienceScreen 7

From war to dance

  • The first audiences had already seen it acted publicly several times before it was printed in 1600. Will Kemp, the company's star clown, played Dogberry.
  • The play's shape runs from war to peace: a victory, revels, a near-tragedy, then weddings and a dance.
  • But the soldiers' code nearly wins: loyalty to comrades and honour make the men believe Don John. Benedick must choose Beatrice over his brothers-in-arms.
  • The war ends twice: Don John is "ta’en in flight" in the last scene.

Mission checkScreen 9

?The play opens as soldiers come home. Why start a comedy with a war?

The war is over, but the soldiers bring its habits home: loyalty to each other, quickness to attack, and honour that must be defended. The play tests those habits against love. The men side with each other against Hero, until Benedick chooses Beatrice.

Mission 7

Most of the play is prose. So why does Beatrice switch to rhyme?

Make a guess: Most of the play is prose. So why does Beatrice switch to rhyme?

Much Ado About Nothing · wit, prose and verseScreen 1

Prose and verse

  • Blank verse: unrhymed lines of iambic pentameter: ten syllables, five stresses, each beat da-DUM.
  • Prose: ordinary sentences, with no fixed line length or rhythm.
  • Most of Much Ado is prose: Beatrice and Benedick's wit, Dogberry's muddles. Verse comes with romance and crisis: Claudio's love, the gulling of Beatrice, the church scene.
  • It isn't a status rule: princes speak prose too.
Much Ado About Nothing · wit, prose and verseScreen 2

Much Ado About Nothing · wit, prose and verseScreen 6

Why the switch matters

  • Beatrice's speech rhymes ABAB CDCD EE: the pattern of a sonnet, a love poem, with one quatrain missing. Many readers hear it as love poetry she has no time to finish.
  • Benedick's matching soliloquy (2.3) stays in comic prose: "the world must be peopled."
  • So the form marks the change: alone and unwatched, Beatrice drops the weapon of wit. In 5.4 both are exposed by love poems in their own hands.

?Most of the play is prose. So why does Beatrice switch to rhyme?

In a play of prose wit, the switch is the point. Alone in the bower, Beatrice speaks in rhymed verse shaped like a sonnet, the form of love poetry, so we hear a feeling she can't joke about. Benedick's matching speech stays in prose, which keeps him comic.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Much Ado About Nothing (c.1598–99, printed 1600) has a main plot (Hero and Claudio), a subplot (Beatrice and Benedick) and a comic plot (Dogberry and the Watch).
  • The plots run in parallel (two tricks by overhearing, two weddings) and collide in Act 4 Scene 1, when Beatrice says "Kill Claudio."
  • The Watch discover the truth in Act 3, so the audience watches the church scene knowing Hero is innocent.
  • Leonato calls their relationship "a kind of merry war": quick prose repartee in which each twists the other's words.
  • Hints of a shared past ("I know you of old", a heart won "with false dice") let many readers see their wit as armour against being hurt.
  • They change through the overhearing tricks (2.3, 3.1), the confession and challenge (4.1) and the love poems (5.4).
  • The match is arranged by others: Don Pedro woos, Leonato hands over Hero "and with her my fortunes", and Hero barely speaks.
  • In 4.1 Claudio's verse turns Hero into a "rotten orange" and her blush into "guiltiness".
  • Is Claudio redeemable? For: deceived, remorseful, mourns. Against: planned public shame, flippancy, "sinn’d I not / But in mistaking".
  • "Nothing" and "noting" probably sounded alike: the play is full of noting (overhearing), and much of it is false.
  • The same method, staged overhearing, makes love in 2.3 and 3.1 and nearly destroys Hero in 3.2–4.1.
  • The window scene is offstage, so the audience watches the wedding with dramatic irony; the Friar then uses deception to heal ("Change slander to remorse").
  • Honour was gendered: for men, reputation and courage; for women, chastity, which also carried the family's honour.
  • Leonato's repeated "mine" and his image of "a pit of ink" show shame treated as a permanent, public stain.
  • Beatrice exposes the code: only men can fight for honour, and "manhood is melted into curtsies".
  • Context marks come from linking these ideas to a moment in the play, not from listing facts.
  • The play opens as Don Pedro's soldiers return from a war won, with Don John, who had stood out against his brother, newly reconciled; Messina gives them a month of leisure and revels.
  • War language runs through the love plots ("merry war", "take her hearing prisoner", "Kill Claudio.").
  • Courtship context: fathers and friends arranged matches; Castiglione's The Courtier may lie behind the witty pair; Will Kemp played Dogberry for the first audiences.
  • Link each context point to a moment in the play; a fact on its own earns little.
  • Most of Much Ado is prose; verse marks romance and crisis. Form is not a simple status rule.
  • Wit tools: pun ("civil as an orange"), antithesis, repartee, Dogberry's malapropisms.
  • Beatrice's 3.1 soliloquy is rhymed verse shaped like a sonnet; Benedick's (2.3) is prose. The switch shows her guard coming down.

Key quotations to learn

  • There is a kind of merry war betwixt Signior Benedick and her (Act 1 Scene 1 (Leonato)) Sets up the subplot's sparring.
  • In mine eye she is the sweetest lady that ever I looked on. (Act 1 Scene 1 (Claudio)) The main plot: love at first sight, based on looks.
  • I love you with so much of my heart that none is left to protest. (Act 4 Scene 1 (Beatrice)) The subplot's confession, in the main plot's worst scene.
  • Kill Claudio. (Act 4 Scene 1 (Beatrice)) Where the two plots collide.
  • What your wisdoms could not discover, these shallow fools have brought to light (Act 5 Scene 1 (Borachio)) The comic plot solves the main plot.
  • What! my dear Lady Disdain, are you yet living? (Act 1 Scene 1 (Benedick)) Mock-affection: the opening shot of the merry war.
  • I had rather hear my dog bark at a crow than a man swear he loves me. (Act 1 Scene 1 (Beatrice)) Her scorn for romantic vows.
  • She speaks poniards, and every word stabs (Act 2 Scene 1 (Benedick)) Words as weapons; his hurt pride.
  • he lent it me awhile; and I gave him use for it, a double heart for a single one (Act 2 Scene 1 (Beatrice; many editions: "his single one")) A hint that she was hurt before.
  • the world must be peopled (Act 2 Scene 3 (Benedick)) A comic excuse for changing his mind.
  • here’s our own hands against our hearts (Act 5 Scene 4 (Benedick)) Their written poems betray what their words denied.
  • Can the world buy such a jewel? (Act 1 Scene 1 (Claudio)) Love as admiring, and perhaps owning, a precious object.
  • Give not this rotten orange to your friend; (Act 4 Scene 1 (Claudio)) Hero as goods handed between men.
  • Her blush is guiltiness, not modesty. (Act 4 Scene 1 (Claudio)) He misreads the evidence of her innocence.
  • I talk’d with no man at that hour, my lord. (Act 4 Scene 1 (Hero)) Her plain, truthful defence.
  • yet sinn’d I not / But in mistaking. (Act 5 Scene 1 (Claudio)) Apology, or excuse? Key to the redemption debate.
  • One Hero died defil’d, but I do live, (Act 5 Scene 4 (Hero)) Her reputation "died"; she returns cleared.
  • There’s not a note of mine that’s worth the noting. (Act 2 Scene 3 (Balthasar)) The noting/nothing pun on stage.
  • jealousy shall be called assurance (Act 2 Scene 2 (Borachio)) The cruel trick: suspicion dressed as proof.
  • Of the false sweet bait that we lay for it. (Act 3 Scene 1 (Hero)) The kindly trick: a lie meant lovingly.
  • If you dare not trust that you see, confess not that you know. (Act 3 Scene 2 (Don John)) Seeing is made to seem the same as knowing.
  • Change slander to remorse (Act 4 Scene 1 (Friar)) Deception used to heal.
  • Come, lady, die to live (Act 4 Scene 1 (Friar)) The paradox of the fake death.
  • Death is the fairest cover for her shame (Act 4 Scene 1 (Leonato)) A father prefers his daughter dead to dishonoured.
  • Hath drops too few to wash her clean again, (Act 4 Scene 1 (Leonato)) Shame as a stain that can never be removed.
  • I stand dishonour’d, that have gone about (Act 4 Scene 1 (Don Pedro)) The Prince's first thought is his own honour.
  • O God, that I were a man! I would eat his heart in the market-place. (Act 4 Scene 1 (Beatrice)) Female rage against a code only men can act on.
  • But manhood is melted into curtsies, valour into compliment (Act 4 Scene 1 (Beatrice)) She mocks men's honour as empty talk.
  • Done to death by slanderous tongues (Act 5 Scene 3 (Claudio's epitaph)) Reputation can kill; Hero is cleared in public.
  • A victory is twice itself when the achiever brings home full numbers. (Act 1 Scene 1 (Leonato)) The play opens with a war just won.
  • I looked upon her with a soldier’s eye, (Act 1 Scene 1 (Claudio)) Love seen through a soldier's habits.
  • And take her hearing prisoner with the force (Act 1 Scene 1 (Don Pedro)) Wooing as military capture.
  • how many hath he killed and eaten in these wars? (Act 1 Scene 1 (Beatrice)) Beatrice mocks the soldier's glory.
  • In a false quarrel there is no true valour. (Act 5 Scene 1 (Benedick)) Benedick rejects his comrades' code.
  • Strike up, pipers! (Act 5 Scene 4 (Benedick)) War turned to dance in the last line.
  • civil as an orange (Act 2 Scene 1 (Beatrice)) A pun: civil / Seville, the bitter orange.
  • He that hath a beard is more than a youth, and he that hath no beard is less than a man (Act 2 Scene 1 (Beatrice)) Antithesis: no man is quite right for her.
  • Contempt, farewell! and maiden pride, adieu! (Act 3 Scene 1 (Beatrice)) Verse vow: she drops her guard.
  • Taming my wild heart to thy loving hand: (Act 3 Scene 1 (Beatrice)) The hawk tamed by choice.
  • our watch, sir, have indeed comprehended two aspicious persons (Act 3 Scene 5 (Dogberry)) Malapropism: comic, but he holds the truth.
  • Thou and I are too wise to woo peaceably. (Act 5 Scene 2 (Benedick)) Their wit stays, even in love.
