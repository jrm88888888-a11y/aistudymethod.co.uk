Cognitive approach to behaviour

The cognitive approach treats the mind as an information processor. This mini-lesson covers models of memory (multi-store and working memory), schema theory, thinking and decision-making, the reliability of cognitive processes, emotion and cognition, and cognition and technology — all anchored to real studies.

Approach · assumptions

The mind as an information processor

  • Mental processes — perception, attention, memory, thinking — can be studied scientifically.
  • Information is encoded, stored and retrieved, and represented through models and schemas.
  • Memory is reconstructive, not a perfect recording — a key theme of the whole approach.
Models of memory

The multi-store model

The multi-store model (Atkinson and Shiffrin, 1968) describes memory as a flow through three stores:

sensory memory → short-term memory → long-term memoryattention moves information in; rehearsal transfers it to LTM

Evidence: patient HM (Milner) could hold information briefly but not transfer it to LTM, and the serial position effect suggests separate short- and long-term stores.

Limitation: the model treats STM as a single passive store — too simple, which motivated the working memory model.

Models of memory

The working memory model

Baddeley and Hitch (1974) argued short-term memory is an active system, "working memory", with separate parts:

  • Central executive — directs attention and coordinates the subsystems.
  • Phonological loop — handles sound and language.
  • Visuospatial sketchpad — handles visual and spatial information.
  • Episodic buffer (added by Baddeley, 2000) — integrates information across the subsystems.

Strength: explains why we can do a visual and a verbal task at once, but not two verbal tasks — they compete for the same subsystem.

Schema theory

Schema theory and reconstructive memory

A schema is a mental framework of prior knowledge that guides how we interpret and remember information.

  • Bartlett (1932) had English participants repeatedly recall the Native American folk tale "The War of the Ghosts". Through serial reproduction, the story became shorter and more conventional — people unconsciously altered it to fit their own cultural schemas, showing memory is reconstructive.
  • Brewer and Treyens (1981) found participants left in an "office" later recalled objects that fit an office schema (e.g. books) that were never there.

Thinking & decision-making

Dual-process thinking

Kahneman's dual-process model describes two systems of thinking:

  • System 1 — fast, automatic, intuitive and effortless (but prone to bias).
  • System 2 — slow, effortful, logical and deliberate.

Tversky and Kahneman (1981) showed framing effects: in the "Asian disease" problem, people chose the safe option when outcomes were framed as lives saved but the risky option when framed as lives lost — the same facts, different decisions.

Reliability of cognition

The reliability of memory

Because memory is reconstructive, it can be distorted — important for eyewitness testimony.

  • Loftus and Palmer (1974) showed a car-crash film, then asked how fast the cars were going when they "smashed" versus "hit". The word "smashed" produced higher speed estimates, and a week later those participants were more likely to falsely recall broken glass — leading questions reshape memory.
  • Loftus and Pickrell (1995) implanted a false childhood memory of being "lost in a shopping mall" in a quarter of participants.

Emotion & cognition

Emotion and cognition

Emotion influences how memories are formed and recalled.

  • Brown and Kulik (1977) proposed flashbulb memories — vivid, detailed memories of shocking events.
  • Neisser and Harsch (1992) found people were confident but often inaccurate about hearing of the Challenger disaster — flashbulb memories are not immune to distortion.
  • Sharot et al. (2007) linked vivid 9/11 memories to greater amygdala activity in those near the event.

Cognition & technology

Cognition and technology

Digital tools change how we use cognition.

  • Sparrow, Liu and Wegner (2011) found people who expected information to be saved on a computer recalled it less well but remembered where to find it — the internet acts as an external "transactive memory".
Recap

The big ideas to know

Models of memory: Atkinson & Shiffrin (multi-store); Baddeley & Hitch (working memory)

Schema theory: Bartlett (War of the Ghosts); Brewer & Treyens (office schema)

Thinking: Kahneman (dual-process); Tversky & Kahneman (framing)

Reliability: Loftus & Palmer (leading questions); Loftus & Pickrell (false memory)

Emotion & technology: Brown & Kulik / Neisser & Harsch (flashbulb); Sparrow et al. (transactive memory)
