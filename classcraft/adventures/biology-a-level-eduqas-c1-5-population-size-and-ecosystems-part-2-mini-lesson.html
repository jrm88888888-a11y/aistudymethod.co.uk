Mission 1

Mission 1: A caterpillar eats a whole leaf. Why does so little of that leaf end up as caterpillar?

Make a guess: A caterpillar eats a whole leaf. Why does so little of that leaf end up as caterpillar?

Mission 1

Gross and net primary production

  • Producers use light energy to fix CO2 into sugars.
  • Most of the sugar is respired by the plant itself. The rest builds other molecules: the plant's biomass.
  • Gross primary production (GPP): the chemical energy stored by photosynthesis in plant biomass, in a given area or volume.
  • R: the energy the plants lose in respiration.
  • Net primary production: NPP = GPP − R.
  • NPP is what is left for plant growth and reproduction, and for consumers and decomposers.
  • Say: "producers capture and store energy" — not: "producers make energy".
Mission 1

Production is a rate

  • The biomass present now is a stock: e.g. g m⁻² or kJ m⁻².
  • Productivity is the rate of production: per area per unit time.
  • e.g. kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹.
  • Primary production: by producers. Secondary production: by consumers.
  • Say: "productivity is a rate" — not: "productivity is the biomass present".
Mission 1

Worked example: NPP

A grassland (illustrative data): GPP = 24 000 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹. Plant respiration, R = 14 400 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹.

  • Step 1: NPP = GPP − R = 24 000 − 14 400 = 9 600 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹.
  • Step 2: share of GPP left as NPP = 9 600 ÷ 24 000 × 100 = 40%. The plants respired 60%.
  • Keep the units: per area, per year, at every step.

Mission 1

Where a consumer's food goes

  • Energy in the food eaten (ingested): I.
  • Part is never absorbed: lost in faeces (egestion).
  • Part of what is absorbed leaves in urine (excretion, e.g. urea).
  • Most of the absorbed energy is respired (R) and lost as heat.
  • Only the rest becomes new biomass: the consumer's net production.
  • Example (illustrative): a bullock eats grass holding 100 kJ; 64 kJ is lost in faeces and urine; R = 32 kJ. Net production = 100 − (64 + 32) = 4 kJ.
Quick check
?A caterpillar ingests leaf material containing 100 kJ of energy. It egests 50 kJ in faeces, releases 35 kJ as heat through cell respiration and stores 15 kJ in new biomass. What happens to the energy in the faeces?
Mission 1

Why so much is lost

  • Not eaten: roots, wood, bones. Dead remains go to decomposers.
  • Not digested: lost in faeces. Cellulose is hard to digest, so herbivores lose much more this way than carnivores.
  • Excreted: nitrogenous waste such as urea, in urine.
  • Respired: every organism, plants included, respires; most absorbed energy ends up as heat.
  • Endotherms (mammals, birds) respire far more to keep warm, so less of their food becomes biomass.
  • Say: "energy is lost to the surroundings as heat" — not: "energy is used up".
Quick check
?Why is much less energy available to the secondary consumers in a food chain than to the primary consumers?

Mission check

?A wood mouse eats seeds containing 500 kJ of energy. 100 kJ is lost in faeces and urine, and 380 kJ is released in respiration. What percentage of the energy it ate is available to an owl that eats the mouse?

Like the caterpillar, the mouse loses most of its food in waste and as heat from respiration. Only the small remainder becomes body, and only that can feed the next level.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why are top predators so rare, and why does eating lower in a food chain feed more people?

Make a guess: Why are top predators so rare, and why does eating lower in a food chain feed more people?

Mission 2

Less at every level

  • Each trophic level passes on only a small share of its energy: often around 10%, measured from about 5% to 20%.
  • After 3 or 4 transfers (4 or 5 levels), too little is left to support another population.
  • So top predators are few, and each needs a large area to find enough food.
  • Eat or harvest lower in a chain and fewer transfers come between the producers and you: more of their energy reaches you as food.
  • Say: "less energy reaches each higher level" — not: "top predators hold the most energy".
Quick check
?Food chains in most ecosystems have no more than four or five trophic levels. What is the reason for this restriction?
Mission 2

Three kinds of pyramid

  • Pyramid of numbers: organisms per area at each level. Can be inverted: one oak tree feeds thousands of caterpillars.
  • Pyramid of biomass: dry mass per area (g m⁻²) at one moment. Usually upright. In some seas it is inverted: a small, fast-dividing stock of phytoplankton feeds a larger stock of zooplankton.
  • Pyramid of energy: energy flow per area per year (kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹). Never inverted: each level receives less than the level below.
  • Decomposers are not drawn as a bar.
Quick check
?In a model grassland ecosystem the dry biomass per square metre is: producers 800 g, primary consumers 100 g, secondary consumers 15 g, tertiary consumers 2 g. Samples of dry biomass from each level release similar amounts of energy per gram when burned. Which conclusion is supported?
Mission check

?In a lake, the main food chain is phytoplankton → zooplankton → small fish → pike. A fishery could harvest either the small fish or the pike. Which harvest can be the larger each year, and why?

Every step up a chain loses most of its energy, mainly as heat. That keeps top predators rare, and it is why shorter chains, and lower respiratory losses, give more food.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Bare rock can turn into forest without anyone planting a single tree. How does that happen?

Make a guess: Bare rock can turn into forest without anyone planting a single tree. How does that happen?

Mission 3

What succession is

  • Succession: change in the species making up a community, over time.
  • Primary succession: starts on bare ground with no soil: rock, cooled lava, sand, land left by a melting glacier.
  • Secondary succession: starts where soil remains after the community is destroyed: fire, flood, wind damage, felling, abandoned farmland.
Mission 3

Pioneers on bare rock

  • Pioneer species colonise first: e.g. lichens, then mosses.
  • Their features: spores spread by wind; survive drought, heat and cold; need almost no soil.
  • Lichens help weather the rock surface.
  • When pioneers die, decomposers turn them into humus.
  • Rock particles + humus = the first thin soil.
  • Each stage is a seral stage; the whole sequence is a sere.
Mission 3

Each stage paves the way, then gives way

  • Deeper soil holds more water and nutrients: the environment is less hostile.
  • New species can now establish: grasses and ferns, then shrubs, then trees.
  • Taller plants shade out the earlier ones.
  • Each stage changes the environment so it suits other species better, and often suits itself less.
  • Say: "the pioneers changed conditions, which allowed other species to establish" — not: "pioneers prepare the ground for the next species".
Quick check
?Alder trees colonising ground exposed by a retreating glacier have nitrogen-fixing bacteria in their root nodules. Within decades the soil nitrogen content rises and spruce trees, which cannot grow on the bare ground, replace the alders. Which statement about the cause of this change is correct?
Mission 3

What rises as succession proceeds

  • Soil depth, humus, water and mineral content.
  • Plant size: mosses → shrubs → trees.
  • Primary production (biomass made per m² per year).
  • Species diversity and food-web complexity: more niches.
Quick checkRead the data

Soil depth and organic content increased throughout. Which conclusion do the data support?
Mission 3

The climax community

  • Climax community: the final, stable community, in equilibrium with its environment.
  • It persists until a disturbance; its populations still fluctuate.
  • Usually dominated by a few large species. In most of lowland Britain and Ireland: deciduous woodland (e.g. oak).
Mission check

?On a coastal sand-dune system, marram grass covers the young dunes nearest the sea. On older dunes further inland, marram is scarce and the dunes are covered by mosses, low shrubs and other grasses, growing in a thin layer of soil. Which explanation is correct?

On rock or on sand, pioneers change the ground they live on, and their changes let others take over. That is how bare ground becomes forest.

Mission 4

Mission 4: The carbon atoms in your body were once in the air. How do they keep being recycled?

Make a guess: The carbon atoms in your body were once in the air. How do they keep being recycled?

Mission 4

Where carbon is stored

  • Carbon sits in stores (reservoirs):
  • the atmosphere, as CO2;
  • the oceans, as dissolved CO2 and hydrogencarbonate ions;
  • living organisms, as carbon compounds;
  • dead organic matter and soil;
  • fossil fuels, peat and rocks such as limestone.
  • Carbon atoms move between stores. None is made or destroyed.
Mission 4

The processes that move it

  • Photosynthesis removes CO2 from air or water and fixes it in producers.
  • Feeding passes carbon compounds along food chains.
  • Respiration in every organism, producers included, returns CO2.
  • Decomposition: decomposers respire the carbon in dead matter and faeces, returning CO2.
  • Combustion of fuels and biomass releases CO2.
  • Say: "plants respire all the time" — not: "plants photosynthesise instead of respiring".
Quick check
?A student draws a carbon cycle diagram for a pond. Which set of labelled arrows is correct?
Mission 4

Decomposers unlock the dead

  • Dead organisms and faeces lock carbon and other elements inside large organic molecules.
  • Decomposers (saprotrophic fungi and bacteria) secrete enzymes, digest the matter outside their cells, absorb the products and respire them: CO2 is released.
  • They also release mineral ions, such as ammonium and phosphate, that plants can absorb.
  • So all the elements living things need are recycled, not just carbon.
  • No decomposers, no recycling: carbon builds up in dead matter.
  • Example: waterlogged soil lacks O2, so decay is very slow and peat forms.
  • Say: "decomposers return carbon to the air as CO2" — not: "decomposition puts the carbon into the soil".
Quick check
?In an experiment, all the decomposers in a woodland soil are killed, but the trees keep growing for a while. Which change would follow over the next few years?
Mission 4

Slow stores: oceans, rocks and fuels

  • CO2 dissolves in the oceans. Marine organisms build calcium carbonate shells; their remains form limestone.
  • Where decomposition was incomplete (waterlogged, little O2), plant remains became peat and, over millions of years, coal.
  • Oil and natural gas formed from marine plankton buried in sediments with little O2.
  • Combustion returns that stored carbon to the air as CO2 very quickly.
  • Say: "fossil-fuel carbon was fixed by photosynthesis" — not: "fossil fuels are just minerals".
Quick check
?Which statement about the combustion of biomass, peat, coal, oil and natural gas is correct?
Mission check

?A peat bog has stored carbon for thousands of years in waterlogged soil. It is drained for farmland, and the peat dries out. What happens to much of the carbon in the peat?

Carbon only keeps moving while something moves it: photosynthesis takes it in; respiration, decomposition and combustion send it back.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Nearly four-fifths of the air is nitrogen. So why do crops so often run short of it?

Make a guess: Nearly four-fifths of the air is nitrogen. So why do crops so often run short of it?

Mission 5

Atoms go round; energy flows through

  • Every element an organism needs is recycled: carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus and the rest.
  • Energy is not recycled. It leaves each trophic level as heat.
  • Decomposers break down dead matter and wastes. They return elements as simple inorganic ions that plants can absorb.
  • Nitrates are needed to make proteins (amino acids) and nucleic acids (bases).
Mission 5

How decomposers feed

  • Saprobiotic nutrition: feeding on dead or waste organic matter.
  • Also called saprotrophic nutrition, e.g. fungi.
  • Step 1: secrete enzymes onto the food.
  • Step 2: extracellular digestion: large molecules hydrolysed outside the cells.
  • Step 3: absorb the small, soluble products.
  • Fungal hyphae spread through the food: large surface area for secretion and absorption.
  • Proteins and other nitrogen compounds end up as ammonium; phosphates are released as phosphate ions.
Quick check
?Which statement about the recycling of chemical elements in ecosystems is correct?
Mission 5

Nitrogen cycle 1: getting nitrogen in

  • The air is about 78% N2. Its triple bond is very strong: plants cannot use N2.
  • Nitrogen fixation: bacteria reduce N2 to ammonia (NH3).
  • Ammonification: saprobionts turn the nitrogen in proteins, nucleic acids and urea of dead matter and wastes into ammonia / ammonium ions (NH4⁺).
Mission 5

Nitrogen cycle 2: nitrate, and back to the air

  • Nitrification: ammonium → nitrite (NO2⁻) → nitrate (NO3⁻). An oxidation: needs oxygen.
  • Plants absorb nitrate (and ammonium) by active transport, then make amino acids and nucleotides.
  • Denitrification: bacteria convert nitrate back to N2. Happens in anaerobic soil, e.g. waterlogged.
  • Say: "nitrifying bacteria make nitrate from ammonium" — not: "nitrifying bacteria fix nitrogen".
Quick check
?A field is flooded for several weeks and the soil becomes waterlogged. Afterwards, the nitrate content of the soil is much lower. Which process best explains the loss?
Mission 5

Ploughing, drainage and food

  • Nitrifying bacteria need oxygen. So do the roots that absorb nitrate.
  • Ploughing breaks up and aerates soil. Drainage lets air back into waterlogged soil.
  • Both raise nitrification and cut denitrification: more nitrate stays for crops.
  • Economic importance: more nitrate means higher yields and less money spent on fertiliser.
  • But drainage destroys wetland habitats (marshes, bogs, wet meadows) and the species that depend on them.
Mission 5

Partners that feed plants

  • Mutualism: a relationship between two species in which both benefit.
  • Root nodules of legumes (peas, beans, clover: the Fabaceae): Rhizobium bacteria fix N2. Plant gets ammonia / amino acids; bacteria get sugars and shelter.
  • Say: "bacteria in the nodules fix nitrogen" — not: "legumes fix nitrogen themselves".
Quick check
?What are the benefits to each partner in the relationship between a legume and the bacteria in its root nodules?
Mission 5

Why farms need fertiliser

  • Harvesting crops and removing livestock removes the nitrogen and phosphorus in their biomass. It is not returned to the field by decomposers.
  • Fertilisers replace the lost nitrates and phosphates.
  • Natural (organic): manure, slurry, compost. Decomposers release ions slowly.
  • Artificial (inorganic): e.g. ammonium nitrate. Soluble, fast-acting, precise amounts.
  • More fertiliser gives higher yields only up to a point: above that, extra fertiliser is wasted or washed away.
Mission 5

Leaching and eutrophication

  • Leaching: rain washes soluble nitrate and phosphate out of the soil into rivers and lakes.
  • The nutrient no longer limits growth: algal bloom at the surface.
  • Algae block light; plants below die. Algae die too.
  • Aerobic bacteria (decomposers) multiply and decompose the dead matter.
  • Their respiration raises BOD (biochemical oxygen demand) and lowers dissolved oxygen.
  • Fish and other aerobic animals die.
  • Say: "BOD rises, so dissolved oxygen falls" — not: "BOD falls" or "the fertiliser poisons the fish".

Mission check

?A farmer grows clover, a legume, in a field for a year, then ploughs the clover into the soil. Wheat sown next needs much less nitrogen fertiliser than wheat sown after wheat. Which explanation is correct?

Air is full of nitrogen, but plants can use it only after microorganisms have converted it. That is why crops run short.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • NPP = GPP − R; productivity is a rate per area per time; efficiency = energy (or biomass) at a level ÷ that at the level below × 100.
  • Consumers lose energy in faeces, urine and, above all, as heat from respiration; only new biomass passes on.

Mission 2

  • Most energy is lost at each transfer, so higher levels hold less energy and biomass, and chains rarely exceed four or five levels. Harvesting lower in a chain gives more food energy.
  • Pyramids of numbers and biomass can be inverted; pyramids of energy never are.

Mission 3

  • Pioneers colonise bare ground and change it; each stage makes the environment suit other species, until a stable climax community forms.

Mission 4

  • Carbon moves between stores by photosynthesis (removes CO2), feeding, and respiration, decomposition and combustion (all add CO2).
  • Decomposers recycle carbon and every other element; without them, carbon piles up in dead matter such as peat.

Mission 5

  • Elements are recycled by microorganisms; energy is not. Plants cannot use N2 until bacteria fix it.
  • Fixation (N2 → NH3/NH4⁺), ammonification (organic N → NH4⁺), nitrification (NH4⁺ → NO2⁻ → NO3⁻, aerobic), denitrification (NO3⁻ → N2, anaerobic).
  • Leached nutrients → algal bloom → decomposition → BOD up, oxygen down → fish die.
