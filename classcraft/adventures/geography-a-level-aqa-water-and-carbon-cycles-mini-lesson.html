Mission 1

What is a system?

  • A system is a set of linked parts that work together.
  • Stores (stocks): where matter is held, e.g. the ocean, the atmosphere, vegetation.
  • Flows (transfers, fluxes): movements between stores, e.g. evaporation, photosynthesis.
  • Inputs enter the system; outputs leave it.
  • Energy drives the flows.
Mission 1

Open and closed systems

  • Open system: both matter and energy cross the boundary.
    • A drainage basin is open: precipitation in; river discharge, evaporation and groundwater flow out.
  • Closed system: energy crosses the boundary, but matter does not.
    • The global water cycle and the global carbon cycle are treated as closed: Earth gains and loses almost no water or carbon to space, but sunlight enters and heat leaves.
  • The scale you choose decides the boundary, and so which kind of system it is.
Quick check
?A student models one forest's carbon. Sunlight enters, CO₂ is taken from the air, and logs are trucked away. Which description fits?
Mission 1

Energy drives the flows

  • About 30% of sunlight reaching the top of the atmosphere is reflected straight back to space.
  • Earth's surface and atmosphere absorb about 240 W m⁻², and Earth radiates about the same amount back to space.
  • Water cycle: evaporation needs heat energy; water evaporates "much more slowly" near freezing. Gravity then drives precipitation, runoff and infiltration.
  • Carbon cycle: photosynthesis uses solar energy to fix CO₂ into sugars; respiration releases that energy and returns the carbon to the air.
Mission 1

Stores and flows in numbers

  • Water: oceans hold 96.54% of Earth's water; the atmosphere only 12,900 km³ (0.001%).
  • Yet global precipitation is about 486,900 km³ a year.
  • Carbon: the atmosphere held 828 PgC in 2011.
  • Photosynthesis on land takes about 123 PgC a year.
  • A small store with a big flow through it turns over fast. Divide a store by its flow to get the residence time.
Quick check
?The atmosphere holds about 12,900 km³ of water. Global precipitation is about 486,900 km³ a year. Roughly how long does a water molecule stay in the air?
Mission 1

Negative feedback

  • Negative feedback: a change sets off effects that reduce the change. It stabilises the system.
  • Carbon example: more CO₂ in the air → plants and ocean take up more.
  • Of the CO₂ people emitted in 2010–2019, the IPCC estimates about 23% was taken up by the ocean and 31% stored by land vegetation.
  • Since the 1980s, carbon fertilisation from rising CO₂ has "increased the strength of the net land CO₂ sink".
  • Limit: as emissions grow, these sinks take up a smaller proportion.
Mission 1

Positive feedback

  • Positive feedback: a change sets off effects that amplify the change. It moves the system away from its old state.
  • Ice–albedo: snow-covered sea ice reflects up to 90% of sunlight; open ocean only 6%. Warming → less ice → more absorbed heat → more warming.
  • Permafrost carbon: Arctic and boreal permafrost holds 1,460–1,600 Gt of organic carbon, "almost twice the carbon in the atmosphere". Thaw releases carbon, which adds to warming.
Quick check
?Warmer air holds more water vapour, and water vapour absorbs outgoing heat. What kind of feedback is this?
Mission 1

Dynamic equilibrium

  • Dynamic equilibrium: flows keep running, but inputs and outputs balance over time, so the stores stay about the same size.
  • Short term: CO₂ at Mauna Loa falls each northern summer (growth) and rises each winter (decay). The store wobbles around a steady average.
  • Long term: before the Industrial Revolution CO₂ was "280 ppm or less"; through the ice-age cycles of the past million years or so it was never above 300 ppm.
  • Disturbed: in 2024 Mauna Loa's annual average was 424.61 ppm, about 1.5 times 280. Fossil fuels add a flow that the sinks do not fully balance.

Mission check

?A glacier gains ice from snowfall high up and loses it by melting lower down. For decades the two balance. Then warmer summers melt the snow cover, exposing darker ice, which melts faster. Which statement is best supported?

The air's carbon stayed level for millennia because uptake by photosynthesis was balanced by respiration and decay: dynamic equilibrium. Feedbacks decide whether a disturbed system returns to balance (negative) or runs away from it (positive).

Mission 2

Four stores

  • Hydrosphere: liquid water at the surface: oceans, seas, lakes, rivers, swamps.
  • Cryosphere: frozen water: ice sheets, glaciers, snow, ground ice and permafrost.
  • Lithosphere: water in rocks and soil: groundwater and soil moisture.
  • Atmosphere: water vapour, cloud droplets and ice crystals.
  • Globally the water cycle is a closed system: the total stays the same; water moves between stores.
Mission 2

How big is each store?

  • Oceans: 96.54% of all Earth's water.
  • Ice caps, glaciers and permanent snow: 1.74%.
  • Groundwater: 1.69%, of which more than half is saline.
  • Atmosphere: 0.001%.
  • By sphere (calculated from the same table): hydrosphere ≈ 96.55%, cryosphere ≈ 1.76%, lithosphere ≈ 1.69%, atmosphere ≈ 0.001%.
Mission 2

Where the fresh water is

  • Of Earth's fresh water, 68.7% is ice and permanent snow and 30.1% is fresh groundwater.
  • Rivers hold only 0.006% of fresh water.
  • Most ice is in just two ice sheets, Antarctica and Greenland; ice sheets hold over 68% of Earth's fresh water.
  • Distribution is uneven: ice is concentrated at high latitudes and altitudes; groundwater lies under the continents.
Quick check
?Which store holds most of Earth's fresh water?
Mission 2

Evaporation: filling the atmosphere

  • Evaporation: liquid water turns to vapour, using heat energy.
  • About 90% of atmospheric moisture comes from oceans, seas, lakes and rivers; the rest from plant transpiration and a very small amount from sublimation.
  • Faster when it is warm, windy and the air is dry; slow near freezing.
  • Once evaporated, a water molecule spends about 10 days in the air: a small store, refilled fast.
Mission 2

Condensation and cloud formation

  • Air rises, expands and cools.
  • At the dew point the air is saturated; vapour condenses into droplets.
  • Droplets form on tiny particles (condensation nuclei: dust, salt, smoke).
  • Billions of droplets or ice crystals make a cloud.
  • So the key question is: what makes air rise?
Mission 2

Three causes of precipitation

  • Convectional (Met Office: "convective"): the Sun heats the ground and a shallow layer of air above it; warm air rises. Typical of hot afternoons and the tropics.
  • Frontal: cold air meets warm air at a front; the warm air is forced to rise over the cold.
  • Orographic (relief): wind pushes air towards a hill and it is forced upwards; the lee side gets less (rain shadow).
  • Cloud droplets then grow (by colliding and merging, or via ice crystals) until heavy enough to fall.
Quick check
?Moist westerly winds reach the mountains of western Scotland, and it rains far more there than on the east coast. Which cause of precipitation explains the west?
Mission 2

Cryospheric processes

  • Accumulation: snow falls in the accumulation area and builds up ice.
  • Ablation: lower down, "most of the melting and evaporation occur"; ice can also sublimate straight to vapour or calve into the sea.
  • Accumulation > ablation: the ice store grows; ablation > accumulation: it shrinks and releases water.
  • Ground ice and permafrost store water too; when it thaws, that water joins soil and rivers.
  • Over long periods the change is huge: glacial ice covers 10% of land today but about 32% at the last glacial maximum.
Mission 2

Scales and timescales

  • Hillslope (minutes to days): rain soaks into soil, flows over or through it; soil moisture rises and falls.
  • Drainage basin (days to seasons): snow stored in winter melts in spring; groundwater refills in wet seasons.
  • Global (years to millennia): ice ages move water from oceans to ice sheets and back again.
  • Stores turn over at different speeds: about 10 days in the air, far longer in ice sheets and deep groundwater.
Quick check
?Which change to a water store happens over the longest timescale?
Mission check

?At the last glacial maximum, glaciers covered about 32% of land, against 10% today. Which chain of processes moved the water into the ice?

The atmosphere is tiny but refilled constantly: water stays about 10 days before falling again. Over thousands of years, the same evaporation–snowfall chain can shift huge volumes from the oceans into the ice.

Specification: what this mission covers

Mission 3

Basin inputs and outputs

  • Input: precipitation. Its type (rain or snow), intensity and duration decide what happens next.
  • Outputs: evaporation and transpiration (together evapotranspiration), and channel flow (runoff) leaving at the mouth.
Mission 3

Stores

  • Interception: water held on leaves and stems.
  • Surface storage: puddles, ponds and lakes. Soil water: water held in the soil.
  • Groundwater: water in permeable rock (aquifers). Channel storage: water in the river itself.
  • Stores delay water on its way to the river.
Mission 3

Flows

  • Throughfall (drips through the canopy) and stemflow (runs down stems and trunks) carry intercepted water to the ground.
  • Infiltration: water enters the soil. Percolation: it moves down into the rock.
  • Overland flow: infiltration-excess flow when rain is more intense than the soil can absorb; saturated overland flow when the soil is already full.
  • Throughflow: downslope through the soil. Groundwater flow: slowly through rock to the channel (base flow). Channel flow: in the river.
Quick check
?Which route usually takes longest to carry rainwater to the river?
Mission 3

The water balance

  • P = Q + E ± ΔS: precipitation = runoff + evapotranspiration ± change in storage.
  • Over many years storage changes little, so E ≈ P − Q.
  • River Lambourn (234.1 km², on chalk): mean catchment rainfall 768 mm a year, 1962–2019.
  • Its mean flow, 1.743 m³/s, equals 235 mm of runoff a year spread over the catchment (our calculation).
Quick check
?Use the figures earlier in this mission. How much water does the Lambourn lose to evapotranspiration each year?
Mission 3

Groundwater feeds rivers

  • In a USGS study of 54 US streams, groundwater supplied on average 52% of streamflow, from 14% to 90%.
  • The Lambourn's chalk aquifer keeps it flowing: its flow is at least 0.77 m³/s for 95% of the time. For the Beult, a clay river in Kent, it is only 0.057 m³/s.
  • So how a basin stores water decides how its river behaves in dry spells.
Mission 3

Ice feeds rivers

  • In the upper Indus basin, glacier melt supplied 40.6% of total runoff (1998–2007); in the upper Ganges, 11.5%.
  • Meltwater arrives in the warm season, often when rain is scarce.
  • The river is the smallest, fastest store. Groundwater and ice are the big, slow stores that refill it.
Mission check

?After a dry month with almost no rain, the River Lambourn is still flowing. Which explanation is best?

The river is a tiny, fast store. It keeps flowing because big, slow stores drain into it: groundwater as base flow (on average 52% of flow in 54 US streams) and meltwater from ice and snow (40.6% of runoff in the upper Indus).

Mission 4

The storm hydrograph

  • A storm hydrograph plots discharge against time after a rainfall event; rainfall is shown as bars.
  • Rising limb, peak discharge, falling (recession) limb.
  • Lag time: from peak rainfall to peak discharge.
  • Base flow (slow groundwater) lies beneath; storm flow (quickflow) sits on top.
Quick check
?What is lag time?
Mission 4

Two rivers, one storm

  • Beult, Kent: 277.1 km². Lambourn, Berkshire: 234.1 km².
  • 23 December 2013 catchment rainfall: Beult 42.6 mm; Lambourn 44.9 mm.
  • Beult: daily mean flow rose from 32.0 to 79.4 m³/s on 24 December, the highest daily mean in its record from 1958 (the record has gaps).
  • Lambourn: from 1.42 to 2.39 m³/s. It peaked only on 15 February 2014, at 9.53 m³/s, after a very wet January and February.
Mission 4

Chalk and clay

  • The Lambourn drains the Chalk: about 97% of its bedrock is highly permeable. Rain infiltrates and percolates, then reaches the river slowly as base flow.
  • The Beult drains a "predominantly clay catchment": about 74% of its bedrock has low permeability. Rain runs off quickly as overland flow and throughflow.
  • Drainage density (NRFA index): Beult 1.11, Lambourn 0.17. More channels carry water away faster.
  • Base flow index: Lambourn 0.97, Beult 0.23.
Quick check
?The Beult's mean flow is 2.126 m³/s. How many times its mean flow was its peak of 79.4 m³/s?
Mission 4

Physical factors

  • Basin size: large basins have longer lag times. Shape: in a round basin water from all parts arrives together, giving a higher, earlier peak.
  • Relief: steep slopes speed runoff. Soil and geology: clay and impermeable rock shed water; sand, chalk and limestone absorb it.
  • Drainage density: more channels per km² means a faster, higher peak.
  • Precipitation: intense rain exceeds infiltration capacity; snow is stored until it melts. Antecedent conditions: the Beult was already high after rain on 16–22 December.
  • Vegetation: interception and transpiration reduce and delay runoff.
Quick check
?Which basin would have the shortest lag time and highest peak after the same storm?
Mission 4

Annual regimes: seasonal change

  • A river regime is the pattern of discharge through an average year.
  • Monthly mean flows (calculated from NRFA daily data): the Beult peaks in January (5.53 m³/s) and is lowest in July (0.22). The Lambourn peaks in March (2.59) and is lowest in September–October (1.11, 1.10).
  • Monthly rainfall varies much less: 47–84 mm on the Beult, 53–79 mm on the Lambourn.
Mission 4

Why regimes differ

  • Summer flows fall mainly because evapotranspiration is higher, not because rain stops.
  • Clay (Beult): little storage, so flow follows the season closely.
  • Chalk (Lambourn): the aquifer fills in winter and drains slowly, so the peak comes late, in March, and summer flow stays high.
  • In cold climates, snowmelt gives a spring or summer peak, and frozen ground acts like an impermeable surface.
Mission check

?Two basins of similar size receive the same 40 mm storm. Basin X is on permeable limestone (base flow index 0.9); Basin Y is on clay (0.3). Which pair of hydrographs is most likely?

Geology decided it. The Beult's clay (base flow index 0.23) sent the rain straight to the river: 79.4 m³/s, about 37 times its mean flow. The Lambourn's chalk (0.97) soaked it up; its groundwater kept rising through a wet winter and it peaked on 15 February 2014. That February peak is a seasonal groundwater peak, not a storm lag time.

Mission 5

Urbanisation

  • Impermeable surfaces (roofs, roads, car parks) cut infiltration and increase overland flow.
  • Drains and sewers carry water to rivers quickly.
  • Result: shorter lag time, steeper rising limb, higher peak discharge.
  • Building on floodplains also puts more people and property in the way (exposure).
Mission 5

Hurricane Harvey, Houston, 2017

  • Harvey stalled over or near the Texas coast "for four days"; the highest storm total was about 1,540 mm (60.58 inches), near Nederland, Texas.
  • A study in Nature found urbanisation "exacerbated not only the flood response but also the storm total rainfall".
  • It estimated that urbanisation made such extreme floods in the basins studied about 21 times as likely, on average (25–30 August 2017).
  • One modelling study of one event: strong evidence, but test it against others.
Quick check
?A housing estate is planned upstream of a town. Which design would best stop it raising flood peaks downstream?
Mission 5

Farming practices

  • Ploughing leaves bare soil; ploughing up and down slopes creates channels for runoff.
  • Heavy machinery and livestock compact the soil, lowering its infiltration capacity.
  • Field drains and ditches move water to rivers faster.
  • Some practices slow water down: contour ploughing, cover crops, hedgerows and buffer strips.
Mission 5

Abstraction and reservoirs

  • Abstraction: pumping water from rivers and aquifers lowers river flow and the water table.
  • Beijing: heavy groundwater pumping lowered the mean groundwater level in the Beijing Plain from 15 m below the surface in 2000 to 26 m in 2014, about 0.7 m a year.
  • Reservoirs create new stores and change the timing of flow downstream. Danjiangkou Reservoir, on the Han River, holds 29 km³.
  • Since 2014 water transferred from it has helped Beijing's groundwater recover.
Mission 5

Deforestation in Amazonia

  • Trees intercept rain, roots open up the soil, and transpiration returns water to the air.
  • Tocantins River, south-eastern Amazonia (about 175,000 km² above Porto Nacional): farmland grew from about 30% of the basin (1960) to about 49% (1995).
  • Annual mean discharge rose by 24% and high-flow season discharge by 28%, while precipitation did not change significantly.
  • Less transpiration also means less water vapour for rain downwind.
Quick check
?On the Tocantins, discharge rose by about a quarter while rainfall did not change significantly. Which explanation fits best?
Mission 5

Natural variation: storms and seasons

  • Storm events: a single storm can multiply flow many times. The Beult in Kent rose from 32.0 to 79.4 m³/s in a day in December 2013.
  • Seasons: its mean monthly flow is 5.53 m³/s in January but 0.22 in July, mainly because evapotranspiration is higher in summer (calculated from NRFA data).
  • Human impacts are layered on top of this natural variation, so compare before and after with long records.
Mission check

?Over 20 years, a small rural catchment becomes 40% covered by housing and roads with conventional storm drains. Rainfall does not change. What happens to its storm hydrograph, and why?

Impermeable surfaces and drains cut infiltration and lag time and raise the peak. In Houston, urbanisation also increased storm rainfall, making Harvey-scale flooding about 21 times as likely in the basins studied.

Mission 6

Carbon: the building block of life

  • "Carbon is the chemical backbone of life on Earth": every sugar, protein and cell is built on carbon chains.
  • Natural world: plants fix carbon into sugars; animals eat it and breathe it out; shells and skeletons store it as calcium carbonate.
  • Humans: fossil fuels "provide energy that fuels our global economy"; limestone is used to make concrete.
  • Carbon is never made or destroyed here: it moves between stores by fluxes.
Mission 6

Where the carbon is

  • Unit: 1 petagram (Pg) = 1 gigatonne (Gt) = 10¹⁵ g.
  • Atmosphere: about 589 PgC before industry; 828 PgC in 2011.
  • Vegetation: 450–650 PgC; litter and soils: 1,500–2,400 PgC; permafrost: about 1,700 PgC.
  • Ocean: dissolved inorganic carbon about 38,000 PgC; marine life only about 3 PgC. The ocean holds "about 50 times more carbon than the atmosphere".
  • Rocks: "Most of Earth's carbon" is stored in rocks.
  • AQA's spheres: lithosphere (rocks, fossil fuels), hydrosphere (ocean), cryosphere (permafrost), biosphere (vegetation, soils), atmosphere.
Mission 6

Geological carbon

  • "Most of Earth's carbon" is geological, stored in rocks: in carbonate rocks (limestone, chalk) built from shells, and as organic carbon from buried plants and plankton in shale and coal.
  • Fossil fuel reserves (AR5): gas 383–1,135, oil 173–264, coal 446–541 PgC.
  • These are slow-domain stores: turnover times of "10,000 years or longer".
  • Natural exchange with the fast cycle is "relatively small (<0.3 PgC yr–1)".
Quick check
?In 2025 atmospheric CO₂ was projected to average 425.7 ppm. One ppm of CO₂ holds 2.124 GtC. About how much carbon was in the atmosphere?
Mission 6

Photosynthesis and respiration

  • Photosynthesis: plants "move carbon from the atmosphere into the biosphere". Gross primary production is about 123 ± 8 PgC a year.
  • Respiration (plants, animals, microbes) returns much of it to the air.
  • The fast cycle moves 1,000 to 100,000 million tonnes of carbon a year.
  • Evidence: CO₂ at Mauna Loa falls each northern summer and rises each winter, "driven by Northern Hemisphere summer vegetation growth … and winter decay".
Mission 6

Decomposition and soil carbon

  • Dead leaves, roots and animals become litter and soil organic matter.
  • Decomposition by microbes releases carbon "back into the atmosphere or soil".
  • Cold or waterlogged ground slows decomposition, so carbon builds up as peat and in permafrost.
  • Arctic and boreal permafrost holds 1,460–1,600 Gt of organic carbon, "almost twice the carbon in the atmosphere".
Mission 6

Combustion

  • Combustion oxidises carbon to CO₂ quickly.
  • Natural: wildfires burn vegetation and peat.
  • Human: burning fossil fuels; projected at 38.1 billion tonnes of CO₂ in 2025.
  • Fossil burning takes carbon from a slow store and adds it to the fast cycle within hours.
Mission 6

Sequestration in the oceans and sediments

  • Solubility pump: CO₂ "is more than twice as soluble in cold polar waters than in warm equatorial waters"; cold, dense water sinks and carries it to depth.
  • Biological pump: plankton fix carbon near the surface; "perhaps 30%" of it sinks into deeper waters, and "only about 0.1% reaches the seafloor".
  • Calcifying plankton build calcium carbonate shells; these sink, but "below about 4 km" virtually none accumulates on the seafloor.
  • What reaches the seafloor can be buried in sediments and, over millions of years, become rock.
Quick check
?Why does much ocean uptake of CO₂ happen in cold high-latitude seas?
Mission 6

The slow cycle: weathering and outgassing

  • CO₂ dissolves in rain to form carbonic acid, which dissolves rock (chemical weathering) and releases calcium and other ions.
  • Rivers carry the ions to the ocean, where calcium combines with bicarbonate to form calcium carbonate: shells, then limestone.
  • Volcanic outgassing returns rock carbon to the air: volcanoes emit 130–380 million tonnes of CO₂ a year.

Stores that keep Earth habitable

Four links between the cycles

  • Ocean: took up 23% of human CO₂ in 2010–2019 and more than 90% of the excess heat since 1970.
  • Cryosphere: Arctic and boreal permafrost holds 1,460–1,600 billion tonnes of organic carbon, almost twice the carbon in the atmosphere.
  • Vegetation: land plants stored 31% of human CO₂ in 2010–2019.

Mission 8

Feedback loops

  • A feedback loop: a change triggers effects that alter the original change.
  • Positive feedback amplifies it; negative feedback dampens it.
  • Negative example: a warmer surface emits more longwave radiation to space.
  • Water vapour feedback: warmer air holds more vapour, a greenhouse gas. IPCC: "This is a positive feedback".
  • Global water vapour in the air has very likely increased since the 1980s.
Mission 8

Albedo

  • Albedo: the fraction of sunlight a surface reflects (0 = none, 1 = all).

  • Open ocean: about 0.06.

  • Area: measure width and depths across the channel. Velocity: time a float over a set distance, or use a flow meter.
  • Infiltration: time how fast a set volume of water sinks into the soil inside a ring, under forest and under grass.
  • Rainfall: simple rain gauges in the open and under a canopy show interception directly.
  • Repeat after dry and wet spells to see how precipitation changes stores and transfers.

Mission check

?A water company's very wet upland reservoir catchment (rain on most days) is 70% covered by mature conifers. Which change would most likely raise reservoir yield over the next decade?

A mature conifer canopy intercepts rain and evaporates it back, so less reaches the river: at Plynlimon, the 70%-forested catchment lost roughly 150–250 mm a year more than the grassland. Felling and replanting shrink that gap.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Systems language: inputs, outputs, stores, flows, energy; open (matter and energy cross) vs closed (energy only); the global water and carbon cycles are closed, a drainage basin or a forest is open.
  • Feedback and balance: negative feedback stabilises; positive feedback amplifies; dynamic equilibrium = flows continue but balance, as in pre-industrial CO₂ (280 ppm or less), now disturbed (424.61 ppm in 2024).

Mission 2

  • Oceans hold 96.54% of Earth's water; ice holds 68.7% of fresh water; the atmosphere holds just 0.001% but turns over in about 10 days.
  • Evaporation, condensation, cloud formation, precipitation (convectional, frontal, orographic) and accumulation/ablation move water between stores at hillslope, basin and global scales, over minutes to millennia.

Mission 3

  • A drainage basin is an open system of inputs, stores, flows and outputs inside the closed global cycle; P = Q + E ± ΔS.

Mission 4

  • Hydrograph shape depends on basin size, shape, relief, soil, geology, drainage density, precipitation, antecedent conditions and land use; the Beult (clay) and Lambourn (chalk) show geology at work.

Mission 5

  • Urbanisation, deforestation, farming, abstraction and reservoirs change stores and flows; most speed runoff and raise peaks.

Mission 6

  • Stores (PgC): ocean ~38,000 ≫ soils 1,500–2,400 and permafrost ~1,700 > atmosphere ~828 (2011) > vegetation 450–650; most carbon of all is in rock.
  • Fast cycle: photosynthesis, respiration, decomposition, combustion, ocean exchange (≈123 PgC a year by photosynthesis). Slow cycle: weathering, carbonate formation, burial, outgassing (fractions of a PgC a year, over millions of years).

Mission 7

  • Natural change (volcanoes, wildfires) is small or cyclical; human change (fossil carbon, deforestation, farming) adds new carbon to the fast cycle.
  • Budget 2015–2024: about half the extra CO₂ stays in the air, 29% goes to the ocean, 21% to land; impacts are warming, acidification and weakening sinks.

Mission 8

  • Water and carbon stores regulate climate; they link through the atmosphere (vapour, CO₂), ocean, cryosphere and vegetation.
  • Arctic warming is amplified by positive feedbacks (ice–albedo, water vapour, permafrost carbon); some dampen it (radiation to space, CO₂ fertilisation), but sinks become less effective.

Mission 9

  • Interventions either cut emissions (efficiency, fuel shifts, carbon pricing) or store carbon (forests, CCS, DAC, wetlands, soils). Capture is tiny today: about 50 Mt a year against 38.1 Gt emitted.
  • Trading and credits work only with falling caps and real, permanent cuts.

Mission 10

  • Amazonia stores 150–200 Pg C and recycles much of its own rain; clearing for food, minerals and energy turns local gains in streamflow into basin-wide losses of rainfall and carbon.
  • Droughts (2005, 2010, 2023) show the store is vulnerable; restoration, better farming and protection help both cycles, but at small scale so far.

Mission 11

  • At Plynlimon, nearly identical rainfall gives different runoff: the forested Severn loses about 240 mm a year more than the grassland Wye (calculated from NRFA data), mainly through interception.
  • Impermeable rock, peat and steep slopes make both rivers flashy; supply planning must allow for forest water use, which changes with forest age.
