Mission 1

The littoral zone

  • Backshore: from the beach face up to the dune foot, cliff base or sea wall. Dry in normal conditions; reached by waves only in extreme events (high tide plus storm surge).
  • Foreshore (beach face): from mean low water up to the beach berm; wetted daily by tides and swash.
  • Nearshore: from the foreshore out across the shallow sea floor where waves shoal and break, often to depths of about 20 m.
  • Offshore: beyond that, where waves have little effect on moving sediment.
  • In a three-zone scheme (backshore, nearshore, offshore), the foreshore is the strip where backshore meets nearshore.
Mission 1

A dynamic zone of rapid change

  • Holderness, East Yorkshire: cliffs retreat 1–4.5 m a year; about 2 km lost in 1,000 years, with 26 villages.
  • Start Bay, Devon: occasional storms from the east and south-east can strip beach material from long stretches of shoreline in a short time.
  • The littoral zone includes many coastal types: cliffs, shingle barriers, spits, estuaries, lagoons.

Mission 1

Two ways to classify coasts

Longer-term criteria

  • Geology: rocky coasts vs coastal plain coasts.
  • Sea-level change: emergent vs submergent. The River Dart, Devon, enters the sea through a ria-type estuary between hard-rock headlands.

Shorter-term processes

  • Waves: high-energy vs low-energy coasts.
  • Tides, by tidal range: microtidal under 2 m; mesotidal 2–4 m; macrotidal over 4 m.
  • Rivers: where rivers deliver sediment, deltas form; they are classed as river-, wave- or tide-dominated.
Quick check

Mission 1

Rocky coasts

  • Form where geology resists erosion by sea, rain and wind, often in high-energy settings.
  • High relief: at Start Point, Devon, the beach disappears and cliffs plunge directly into deep water.
  • Between Tinsey Head and Start Point the cliffs are very resistant and have stayed relatively stable.
  • Rocky coasts can also be low relief: rocky platforms and low cliffs where resistant rock lies near sea level.
Mission 1

Coastal plain coasts

  • Found at low relief, often low-energy; built from sediment supplied from land or offshore.
  • Sandy: the Curonian Spit (Lithuania and Russia) is a 98 km dune peninsula formed from sand carried by currents.
  • Offshore supply: as sea level rose after about 10,000 years ago, sediment was swept landwards to build Start Bay's shingle barrier beaches.
  • Estuarine: in the Exe estuary, Devon, fine sediment settled inside the estuary; coarser sand and shingle built spits (Dawlish Warren), tidal deltas and sandbanks at its mouth.

Mission 1

Concordant and discordant coasts

  • Concordant: rock beds run parallel to the coastline.
  • Discordant: bands of different rock run perpendicular to the coast.
  • At Durlston, Dorset, both meet: the south-facing coast is concordant (hard limestone as a barrier); the east side is discordant.
  • On the discordant coast, differences in resistance formed Durlston Head, Swanage Bay, Ballard Point and Studland Bay: headlands of resistant rock, bays in weaker rock.
Mission 1

Dalmatian and Haff coasts

  • Dalmatian (Croatia): mountain ridges and valleys run parallel to the coast, NW–SE.
  • Rising sea level flooded the valleys (synclines) into bays and channels; the ridges (anticlines) were left as long islands: some 1,200 islands and islets.
  • Haff (Baltic): long sand spits run parallel to the coast and cut off lagoons. The Curonian Spit separates the Baltic Sea from the Curonian Lagoon; it is 98 km long and 0.4–3.8 km wide, formed about 5,000 years ago.
  • Both are concordant in plan: the main features run parallel to the coast. Dalmatian coasts follow rock structure; Haff coasts are built from deposited sediment.
Quick check
?Why does a Dalmatian coast have long islands parallel to the shore?
Mission check

?Read this description of an unnamed coast: "Bands of sandstone and clay run at right angles to the shore. The sandstone forms headlands; the clay has been worn into bays. A river enters one bay through a drowned valley. Tidal range: 5.5 m." Which classification is correct?

Coasts can be classified over long timescales by geology (rocky vs coastal plain; concordant vs discordant) and sea-level change, and over short timescales by waves, tides and rivers. Structure explains shape: parallel ridges drown into Dalmatian islands, parallel spits make Haff lagoons, perpendicular bands make headlands and bays.

Mission 2

How waves form and break

  • Wind blowing over water transfers energy to it. Wave energy grows with wind speed, duration and fetch (the distance of open water).
  • In deep water, water particles move in near-circular orbits; the energy moves forward, not the water.
  • In shallow water, friction with the bed slows the base of the wave: wavelength shortens, height rises, and the crest topples forward: the wave breaks.
  • Water rushes up the beach as swash and drains back as backwash.
Mission 2

Wave refraction

  • Waves slow down in shallow water, so a wave crest approaching an uneven coast bends: this is refraction.
  • Off a headland, water is shallow sooner; the crest slows there and wraps round it, so energy converges on the headland.
  • In a bay, the crest is still in deep water and diverges: energy is spread out, so deposition is more likely.
  • Result: headlands are eroded, bays fill with beaches.
Mission 2

Tides and currents

  • Tides raise and lower the level at which waves attack. Britain has some of the largest tides in the world, with a range of up to 15 m.
  • At Aldbrough, it is at high tide that waves erode the lower and sometimes middle cliff.
  • Currents (tidal, longshore and ocean currents) carry sediment and drop it "wherever the current slows down".
Mission 2

Lithology: rock type controls recession

  • Lithology is what the rock is made of. Igneous and many metamorphic rocks resist erosion; some sedimentary rocks, such as clays and shales, are weak.
  • Holderness cliffs are unconsolidated glacial till (boulder clay); at Flamborough Head, the cliffs are chalk, "more resistant to erosion than the overlying till".
  • Aldbrough retreated 1.16 m a year in 1852–1951 and 2.16 m a year in 1951–2004.
Mission 2

Geological structure

  • Structure is how rocks are arranged: bedding planes (layers), joints (cracks), faults (fractures with movement), folds, and the dip (tilt) of the beds.
  • Joints and faults are lines of weakness: waves widen them into caves and geos.
  • Dip: beds dipping seaward can slide along bedding planes, giving lower, sloping cliffs; beds dipping landward or horizontal can stand as steep faces.
  • Structure therefore shapes cliff profiles, erosion rates and micro-features.
Mission 2

The Glamorgan Heritage Coast

  • Its cliffs are alternating limestones and shales of the Lower Jurassic Porthkerry Formation.
  • These "thinly interbedded limestones and shales" form "the spectacular cliffline of the Glamorgan Heritage Coast".
  • Faults cut the cliffs, and toppled blocks lie on the foreshore and wave-cut platform.
Mission 2

Differential erosion

  • Where resistant and less resistant strata alternate, the weaker beds erode faster.
  • The stronger beds are left overhanging; they crack along joints and fall as blocks.
  • The result is a stepped, complex cliff profile, retreating unevenly.
  • Permeable over impermeable beds: water drains down to the impermeable layer, which becomes a slip surface for landslides.
Quick check
?Two cliffs are made of the same limestone. One retreats faster. Which difference in structure could explain it?
Mission 2

A sediment cell: Portland Bill to Selsey Bill

  • England and Wales's coast "has been divided into 11 main 'cells'"; cell 5 runs from Selsey Bill to Portland Bill, in seven sub-cells.
  • Within a cell: sources (cliffs, rivers, sea bed) → transfers (longshore drift) → sinks (beaches, spits, offshore banks).
  • Dynamic equilibrium: inputs and outputs balance over time.
  • Negative feedback restores balance (a bar forms and absorbs wave energy); positive feedback amplifies change: a beach "washed away is left more vulnerable to wave action".
Mission 2

Why recession rates are not constant

  • Short term: storms and passing depressions, onshore wind direction and long fetch, high spring tides, and wet seasons that soak cliffs.
  • Studland lost up to 10 m of dunes in a single storm in 2014.
  • Longer term: sea-level change and changes in sediment supply alter how much energy reaches the cliff.
  • At Aldbrough the average rate itself changed: 1.16 m a year (1852–1951) to 2.16 m a year (1951–2004).

Mission 3

Estuaries: where rivers meet the tide

  • An estuary is the downstream part of a river valley, subject to the tide, with a salinity gradient from fresh to marine water.
  • River sediment, shelter from waves and low current flows produce extensive intertidal flats.
  • Most UK estuaries by area are coastal plain estuaries: valleys flooded at the end of the last glaciation.
Mission 3

Mudflats

  • Intertidal flats grade from clean sands (exposed, high energy) to muds (sheltered, low energy).
  • Mudflats "form in the most sheltered areas of the coast, usually where large quantities of silt derived from rivers are deposited in estuaries".
  • Rich in worms, bivalves and mud-snails, they feed waders and wildfowl such as knot and dunlin.
Mission 3

Processes: how fine sediment settles

  • At slack water (high and low tide) currents almost stop, so fine particles can settle.
  • Fine particles flocculate (clump into flocs) and settle faster; this depends on suspended concentration and organic content.
  • Biofilms on the mud enhance flocculation.
  • Once plants grow, they attenuate flow and promote deposition from suspension.
  • Saltmarsh "helps to attenuate the wave action, forming a natural defence against the sea" (Spurn, Humber).
Quick check

Mission 3

The halosere begins: pioneers

  • A halosere is a succession that begins in salt water.
  • Pioneers: glasswort Salicornia and annual seablite colonise mud "in areas protected from strong wave action".
  • They live at the lower marsh, "frequently flooded by the tide": halophytes (salt-tolerant plants).
  • They are "an important precursor to the development of more stable saltmarsh vegetation".
  • Plants slow the water; more sediment settles; the surface rises.
Mission 3

Cord-grass: a human twist

  • The UK's only native cord-grass is Spartina maritima.
  • S. alterniflora arrived accidentally in the 1820s in ships' ballast; crossing produced a fertile hybrid, common cord-grass S. anglica .
  • S. anglica was "extensively planted … as an aid to stabilisation of intertidal mudflats" and spread rapidly.
  • Dense swards have "little intrinsic value to wildlife" and can take over mudflats where waders feed.
Mission 3

Middle and upper marsh

  • Salt meadows form the middle and upper marsh, "where tidal inundation still occurs but with decreasing frequency and duration".
  • Low marsh: open common saltmarsh-grass Puccinellia; higher up: more herbs and more species.
  • Grazing by livestock shapes the upper marsh.
  • Carmarthen Bay (south Wales) shows "a complete sequence … from pioneer vegetation through to upper saltmarsh transitions".
Quick check

Mission 3

Factors at different scales

  • Sediment supply: pioneer saltmarsh is scarce in Scotland and Northern Ireland "because of a lack of new sediment".
  • Shelter: UK salt meadows (over 29,000 ha) lie mostly in "large, sheltered estuaries" of England and south Wales.
  • Beyond the UK: the Wadden Sea (Netherlands, Germany, Denmark) is "the largest unbroken system of intertidal sand and mud flats in the world"; its salt marshes host about 2,300 species.
  • Worldwide, 5.5 million ha of saltmarsh have been mapped across 43 countries.
Mission 3

The psammosere: dune succession

  • Embryo dunes: sea rocket, sand couch and lyme-grass at the top of the beach; need a "continued supply of new sand".
  • Yellow (white) dunes: marram dominates where sand is moving; "will rapidly change and disappear if stability is imposed".
  • Grey (fixed) dunes: replace marram "as the dune stabilises and the organic content of the sand increases".
  • Slacks: damp hollows, seasonally flooded.
  • At Murlough (Co. Down) one transect runs from foredunes, through yellow and grey dunes, to dune heath, scrub, "and then to saltmarsh".
Mission 3

Succession stabilises landforms

  • Spits: saltmarsh behind Spurn's curve damps waves.
  • Bars and tombolos: Chesil Beach shelters the Fleet lagoon, "extremely sheltered from wave action".
  • Managed realignment: Medmerry (West Sussex, completed 2014) let the sea in behind new sea embankments, creating intertidal habitat.
  • Vegetation binds sediment and slows water; but marram dunes need mobility, so over-stabilising can harm them.

Mission 4

Waves shape beach profiles

  • Constructive waves (low, long, strong swash) push sediment up the beach, building berms and a steeper profile.
  • Destructive waves (high, steep, strong backwash) drag sediment down and offshore, flattening the beach and building offshore bars.
  • Profiles change over days (each storm), seasons (calmer summers, stormier winters) and years.
Mission 4

Erosion processes and their controls

  • Hydraulic action: the force of water, and air compressed into cracks. Abrasion: the load is thrown at the rock. Attrition: pebbles collide and round. Corrosion: seawater dissolves soluble rock.
  • Wave type and size: steep, high storm waves do most erosion.
  • Lithology: at Flamborough the chalk "is more resistant to erosion than the overlying till"; chalk is also open to corrosion.
Mission 4

Notches, platforms and cliffs

  • Waves erode a wave-cut notch at the cliff base by hydraulic action and abrasion.
  • The overhang collapses and the cliff retreats, leaving a gently sloping wave-cut platform, exposed at low tide.
  • As the platform widens, waves break further out and lose energy before reaching the cliff, so retreat slows: negative feedback.
Mission 4

Headlands: cave, arch, stack, stump

  • Headlands and bays form where bands of resistant and weaker rock meet the coast: weaker rock erodes back into bays; resistant rock is left standing out as headlands.
  • Waves attack lines of weakness (joints, faults) in a headland: crack → cave → arch → stack → stump.
  • BGS warns that more wave activity could attack Flamborough's chalk at wave level, "creating undercutting and cavities".
  • Old Harry Rocks, Dorset: "towering sea stacks and cliffs topped by chalk downland".
Mission 4

Outside the UK: the Twelve Apostles

  • Victoria, Australia: limestone, formed from shells and skeletons on an ancient sea floor.
  • Caves "eventually became arches, which have collapsed and left impressive towering rock stacks".
  • There were nine stacks until 2005, when one collapsed; another fell in 2009. Seven remain.

Mission 4

Moving sediment along the coast

  • Angle of wave attack: waves arriving at an angle carry sediment up the beach at that angle; backwash returns it straight down.
  • Longshore drift: the repeated zig-zag moves sediment along the coast in the direction of the prevailing waves.
  • Tides and currents move finer sediment and deposit it "wherever the current slows down".
Mission 4

Beaches

  • Beaches form where deposition exceeds removal: sand on gentle slopes, shingle on steeper ones.
  • Beach material is sorted: coarser shingle near the top, finer sand lower down, as swash loses energy.
  • In bays, wave refraction spreads energy, so beaches build up there.
Mission 4

Spits

  • A spit forms where longshore drift carries sediment past a change in the direction of the coast; it grows out into open water.
  • Waves refracting round its tip curl it into a recurved end; saltmarsh grows in the sheltered water behind.
  • Spurn formed "as a long, narrow spit from deposits moving along the coast carried by currents". It is over three miles long and in places only 50 m wide.
  • Outside the UK: Farewell Spit, New Zealand, stretches 30 km eastward into the Tasman Sea.
  • A double spit forms where drift builds spits from both sides of an estuary or bay mouth.
Mission 4

Bars, barrier beaches and tombolos

  • A bar is a ridge of sediment across a bay, enclosing a lagoon. It forms as a spit grows right across, or as a shingle ridge migrates onshore while sea level rises.
  • Slapton bar, Devon, a shingle bay-bar, "is attached to headlands at both ends".
  • A tombolo joins an island to the mainland: Chesil Beach "stands separately as a tombolo joining the Isle of Portland to the mainland".
  • A barrier beach is a ridge of sand or shingle running parallel to the coast, separated from it by a lagoon or marsh; Chesil Beach is classed as one.
  • Offshore bars are submerged ridges built by destructive waves.
Mission 4

Cuspate forelands

  • A cuspate foreland is a triangular point of shingle or sand built out where two directions of drift meet.
  • Dungeness, Kent, is "the largest cuspate foreland in Britain", formed almost entirely of flint shingle, with about 2,158 ha of exposed shingle.
  • Its southern beach faces SSW; its eastern beach "has gradually migrated eastwards as the ness has grown".
  • Inland lies a sequence of old shingle ridges, each a former shoreline.

Mission 4

Sand dunes and plant succession

  • Onshore winds blow dry sand inland from wide beaches; it collects behind obstacles such as the strandline.
  • Embryonic dunes: pioneer plants such as sand couch trap sand at the top of the beach.
  • White (yellow) dunes: sand-binding marram dominates; this vegetation "will rapidly change and disappear if stability is imposed".
  • Grey (fixed) dunes: grassland replaces marram as the sand gains organic matter; dune slacks are seasonally flooded hollows.
  • Succession on sand (a psammosere) stabilises the landform.

Mission 5

Subaerial processes

  • Subaerial processes act on the cliff face and top, above the reach of the sea.
  • Weathering: rocks breaking down in place.
  • Mass movement: rock, earth or debris moving down a slope under gravity, suddenly or slowly.
  • Both feed loose sediment to the foot of the cliff, where waves can remove it.
Mission 5

Mechanical weathering

  • Freeze–thaw: water in cracks freezes and expands, pushing the cracks apart; repeated cycles split the rock.
  • Other physical stresses, such as changes in temperature, also break rock apart in place.
  • The broken fragments supply sediment, and the cracks weaken the cliff.
  • Freeze–thaw also reduces cohesion between grains, making landslides more likely.
Mission 5

Chemical and biological weathering

  • Chemical: CO₂ dissolves in rainwater, making it slightly acidic; it reacts with minerals in the rock. Limestones and chalk are especially open to this.
  • Biological: plant roots grow in cracks and widen them; burrowing animals enlarge cracks.
  • But plants can also protect: on the Port Campbell cliffs (Australia), roots help hold the cliff top together, and trampling increases erosion.
  • Weathering lowers rock strength, a key trigger for slope failure.

Mission 5

Why cliffs fail

  • A slope fails when the force of gravity acting on it exceeds the resisting forces.
  • Water adds weight, lowers strength and reduces friction.
  • Toe erosion by the sea steepens the slope until it can no longer hold itself up.
  • Geology matters: rock type, joints, bedding orientation and the arrangement of layers.
  • Vegetation binds material; removing it raises the risk.
Mission 5

Types of mass movement

  • Falls and blockfall: blocks drop from steep faces, often along joints. On southern Portland the vertical limestone cliffs fail by infrequent rock falls controlled by jointing.
  • Outside the UK: at Port Campbell, Australia, a 45 m limestone stack suddenly collapsed in 2005.
  • Rotational slumping: a block slides on a curved surface and tilts back. It is the principal mode on Holderness's glacial till, with topples, falls and minor mudflows.
  • Landslides on complex geology: Dorset cliffs of clays interbedded with permeable strata suffer large, episodic rotational landslides.
  • Flows: saturated material flows as mudflows or mudslides.

  • Rotational scar (back scar): the steep, curved face left at the top after a block slips away.
  • Talus scree: angular debris piled at the cliff foot after falls. On Portland, cliff bases are strewn with rock debris and scree slopes, though much there is old quarry waste.

  • Tension cracks above the cliff top
  • Rotational landslides, slumps and gullying at the top of the slope

Mission 6

Eustatic change: the sea moves

  • Eustatic change: a worldwide change in sea level as the volume of ocean water (or the shape of ocean basins) changes.
  • Cold periods: water is locked in ice sheets on land, so sea level falls (glacio-eustatic fall). Warm periods: ice melts and water expands, so it rises.
  • Global mean sea level rose 0.20 m between 1901 and 2018; the rate rose from 1.3 mm a year (1901–71) to 3.7 mm a year (2006–18).
  • Thermal expansion explained 50% of the rise in 1971–2018.
Mission 6

Isostatic change: the land moves

  • Isostatic change: vertical movement of the land. An ice sheet's weight presses the crust down; when it melts, the land slowly rises (isostatic recovery, or rebound).
  • On the High Coast (Sweden) and Kvarken (Finland), about 290 m of uplift has been recorded over the past 10,500 years.
  • The land still rises by about 0.9 m a century, and new islands keep emerging.
  • Land can also sink: beyond the old ice margin, and where thick sediments compact.
Mission 6

Tectonic change

  • Plate movement can raise or lower a coast, gradually or in seconds.
  • On 13 November 2016 a magnitude 7.8 earthquake lifted the seabed by 0.5–2 m along 20 km of the Kaikōura coast, New Zealand, and by 5.5 m in one area.
  • Rocks, seaweed and sea creatures were lifted above the water line; GNS geologist Kelvin Berryman described the newly exposed "uplifted platform".
  • In 1931 the Hawke's Bay earthquake raised land around Napier by 1–2 m; a former estuary now holds Napier's airport.

The last 10,000 years

  • Marine platform (marine terrace): a raised shore platform. Kaikōura's "uplifted platform" was created in one earthquake.
  • At Arbroath, Scotland, the Needle E'e arch, "originally formed through coastal erosion", now lies "several metres above current sea level".

Mission 6

Coasts of submergence: rias and fjords

  • Where the sea rises faster than the land, it floods low ground.
  • Rias are drowned river valleys: V-shaped in cross-section, deepening seaward, with branching creeks. JNCC: "Rias are drowned river valleys, characteristically found in south-west Britain". The Fal and Helford, Cornwall, form a "ria system".

Mission 6

Dalmatian coasts

  • A Dalmatian coast forms where ridges and valleys run parallel to the coast and rising sea level drowns the valleys.
  • Croatia's coast: ridges and valleys aligned north-west to south-east; drowning left the hill tops "above modern sea level as an archipelago of some 1200 islands and islets".
  • Ridges (anticlines) became islands; valleys (synclines) became channels and bays. This coast "has given its name to this type of coast".
  • Contrast: rias drown valleys at right angles to the coast.

Mission 6

Present and future change

  • IPCC: global sea level is likely to rise by 0.28–0.55 m by 2100 under very low emissions, and 0.63–1.01 m under very high emissions (relative to 1995–2014).
  • Emergent coasts can switch: where uplift is slower than future sea-level rise, raised features stay raised but the shoreline starts to retreat.
  • Low, soft coasts face more flooding and breaching: modelled UKCP18 scenarios suggest sea-level rise could completely cut off Spurn Head by 2050.

  • Global warming: slow, continuous rise; risk grows each decade for low, soft coasts and estuaries.

Mission 10

Hard engineering

  • Sea wall: concrete wall at the back of the beach; reflects wave energy. Reflected waves can scour the beach in front.
  • Revetment: sloping structure that absorbs some wave energy and protects the cliff or bank behind.
  • Rip rap (rock armour): large boulders at the cliff foot that break waves up.
  • Groynes: walls across the beach that trap littoral drift and widen the beach updrift, but starve beaches downdrift.
  • Offshore breakwaters: barriers offshore that make waves break before reaching the shore; sediment collects behind them.
  • All are costly to build and maintain, and they directly alter physical processes.
Mission 10

  • Beach nourishment: adding sand or shingle to a beach. On the Lincolnshire coast, the Lincshore scheme has nourished beaches since 1994, adding about 350,000 m³ a year from Mablethorpe to Skegness.

  • Dune stabilisation: fencing, boardwalks and planting marram to trap and hold sand.
  • Managed realignment: let the sea in behind a new, landward defence.

Case study: Mappleton, Holderness

  • Holderness's till cliffs retreat on average 1–4.5 m a year. Net drift is southward, about 200,000–350,000 m³ a year.
  • Strategy: in 1991, two rock groynes and a rock revetment were built "to prevent cliff recession" at Mappleton, with a design life of 50 years.
  • Intended effects: hold the cliff line in front of the village; trap drift to build a beach that absorbs wave energy.
  • The SMP keeps hold the line at Mappleton, though it finds only a "marginal economic case" for doing so.
Mission 10

Effects on the sediment budget and landforms

  • Mappleton is becoming a headland: undefended cliffs either side keep retreating, so the defended stretch sticks out.
  • Sediment budget: holding the line means "some interruption to the sediment supplied to downdrift coastlines", and beach narrowing is possible.
  • Landforms: the SMP expects "beach and platform lowering in front of the coastal defences" and a bay developing between Hornsea and Mappleton.
  • South of Mappleton, shore-platform lowering of up to 3.0 m in a year was measured in 1992–93, an extreme value.
  • Exam move: go beyond the choice of strategy; trace the effect on inputs, stores and outputs of sediment, then on landforms and the wider landscape.

Mission 10

Medmerry: managed realignment

  • A shingle bank west of Selsey protected 300 properties, large caravan sites and 650 ha. It had been breached many times in 15 years and needed extensive winter repairs.
  • Holding the line was judged "not practical"; inland banks "will not need to be as large", though "a relatively large area of land" would flood.
  • The government also had to create new intertidal habitat to compensate for losses elsewhere.
  • Medmerry (2007 estimates, present value): no active intervention £8–10 million; hold the line £80 million; managed realignment £10 million.
  • Completed in 2014 with 7 km of new embankments, protecting key infrastructure for Selsey. At the time, the £28 million scheme was "the largest open-coast scheme in Europe".
Mission 10

Sustainable management, local conflict

  • Sustainable plans aim to cope with rising seas and stronger storms, but someone usually loses.
  • Medmerry: farmland flooded so that Selsey stayed protected and habitat was created.
  • Holderness: owners of cliff-top homes and farmland between the defended towns get no defences, so that the beaches downdrift keep their supply.
  • Exam move (Edexcel 2B.11c): name the players, what each gains or loses, and why the plan is still called "sustainable".

Giving land to the sea can be the sustainable choice: at Medmerry, realignment was far cheaper than holding a failing bank and created habitat; at Holderness, letting cliffs erode keeps sediment flowing along the cell. Hard defences protect a point but change the coast around it.

Mission 11

Littoral cells

  • A littoral (sediment) cell is a stretch of coast within which sediment moves largely as a closed system: sources (eroding cliffs, rivers), transfers (littoral drift) and sinks (spits, beaches, offshore banks).
  • Cells are split into sub-cells. Shoreline Management Plans (SMPs) follow them: the Kelling Hard to Lowestoft SMP's area boundaries "have been set based on analysis of coastal processes".
  • Managing by cell matters because an action in one place changes sediment supply elsewhere in the cell.
Mission 11

ICZM and SMPs

  • Integrated coastal zone management (ICZM) is holistic: it plans a whole stretch of coast across all its uses (homes, industry, energy, farming, tourism, conservation) and all its players, over the long term.
  • England's 20 SMPs plan flood and erosion risk management for the whole coast to 2105.
  • SMPs are adopted by local authorities and used in the planning system to designate Coastal Change Management Areas: coastal-defence policy is tied to land-use planning, as ICZM requires.
Mission 11

Four policy options

  • Hold the line (HTL): "maintain or upgrade protection … by holding the shoreline in broadly the same position".
  • Advance the line (ATL): "actively move shoreline defences significantly seawards".
  • Managed (strategic) realignment: "change the position of the shoreline in a controlled way, such as by slowing erosion or creating areas of habitat".
  • No active intervention (NAI): "maintain or encourage a more natural coastline", which "may involve discussing adaptation".
  • SMPs set a policy for each policy unit in three epochs: to about 2025, 2055 and 2105.

?A council lets a low cliff erode but builds a new bank 200 m inland to protect a village from flooding. Which SMP policy is this?
Mission 11

How decisions are made

  • Decisions weigh engineering feasibility, environmental sensitivity, land value and political and social factors.
  • Cost-benefit analysis (CBA): compare the cost of each option with the value of what it protects. For Bangladesh's coastal embankment project, the World Bank's review put the benefit-cost ratio at 2.9 to 5.1 at project closing.
  • Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA): predicts effects on habitats, water and people before approval. The same project financed EIAs for all its remaining polders.
  • SMPs also carry strategic environmental and habitats assessments: the Kelling Hard to Lowestoft SMP lists updates to both, including effects on SSSIs and coastal processes.
Mission 11

Suffolk: who commented, who decided

  • Sizewell C, a proposed nuclear power station of about 3,340 MW, received 1,282 relevant representations.
  • Players: the developer, East Suffolk Council, Natural England, the RSPB and Suffolk Wildlife Trust, the National Trust, the water company, local action groups and residents.
  • The Examining Authority recommended against consent unless the water supply strategy was resolved and the Habitats Regulations information was provided.
  • On 20 July 2022 the Secretary of State granted consent: "the very substantial and urgent need for the proposal outweighs the harms". The Examining Authority found that its hard sea defence breached the SMP's "hold the line" policy for that unit, but judged it "as landward as possible".

Mission 11

Happisburgh, Norfolk: the policy

  • Happisburgh lies on soft cliffs on the north-east Norfolk coast, in the Kelling Hard to Lowestoft SMP.
  • Policy for the Ostend to Eccles unit: managed realignment in all three epochs to 2105. The Bacton Gas Terminal, just to the north, is hold the line.
  • The SMP's reason: this coast's erosion supplies "the sediment needed to maintain beaches throughout much of the SMP area including Great Yarmouth".
  • Defending any one point would form "an artificial promontory" that "would deflect much of the active sediment offshore".
Mission 11

Happisburgh: winners and losers

  • Winners: communities downdrift whose beaches depend on the sediment, including Great Yarmouth; the gas terminal, which is defended and has had a beach recharge; the cliffs' wildlife and geology, protected as an SSSI.
  • Losers: owners of cliff-top homes and land, who are not defended.
  • Players: homeowners; North Norfolk District Council, which leads actions to "develop transition/adaptation objectives" with affected communities; the Environment Agency; conservation bodies.
  • Exam move: a "sustainable" policy for the whole cell can still be a loss for one place. Say whose perspective each argument reflects.
Mission 11

Bangladesh: protecting a delta coast

  • Bangladesh's coastal zone was home to nearly 42 million people in 2010, estimated to grow to 61 million by 2050.
  • The government has built over 2,130 multipurpose cyclone shelters, 139 polders (areas enclosed by embankments) and 2,900 water control structures; after Cyclone Sidr (2007), agencies and donors prepared a long-term disaster risk reduction programme.
  • The World Bank's Coastal Embankment Improvement Project (approved 26 June 2013, US$375 million) aimed to protect polders from tidal flooding and storm surges and to reduce saline intrusion.
  • An integrated design: embankments, drainage, afforestation, water management organisations run by local people, and coastal monitoring.
Mission 11

Bangladesh: winners and losers

  • Rising costs cut the scope from 17 polders to 10, and the area protected from 100,800 ha to 66,012 ha: communities in the dropped polders lost out.
  • Land acquisition costs rose by 300% after a 2017 law; acquisition typically took 12–15 months, delayed by unclear land records. People whose land was taken waited for compensation.
  • Six cyclones during the project period interrupted works.
  • Players: the Bangladesh Water Development Board (builder), the World Bank (funder), landowners and polder communities. Complaints went to 36 grievance redress committees, which had received and resolved 269 by 31 December 2023.
  • By June 2024, 94% of land-acquisition compensation had been paid; some claims were stuck on missing records or disputed ownership.
  • Intended winners: households in the 10 rehabilitated polders, which the project aimed to protect from tidal flooding and salinity.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Littoral zone: backshore (storm-only), nearshore (where waves shoal and break), offshore (little wave effect on sediment); a zone of rapid change.
  • Classification: long-term (geology; sea level) vs short-term (waves; micro/meso/macrotidal; river/wave/tide-dominated deltas). Concordant (Dalmatian, Haff) vs discordant (headlands and bays: Swanage).

Mission 2

  • Waves form from wind (speed, duration, fetch) and break in shallow water.
  • Lithology and structure set how fast cliffs retreat: weak till at Holderness, alternating limestone and shale at Glamorgan.
  • Sediment cells (cell 5: Portland Bill to Selsey Bill) balance sources, transfers and sinks; recession rates vary with storms, tides, seasons and supply.

Mission 3

  • Factors: shelter (low energy), river and marine sediment, tides (slack water), salinity; processes: flocculation, settling, plant trapping and flow attenuation.
  • Halosere: bare mudflat → pioneers (Salicornia) → cord-grass/low marsh → middle and upper salt meadow (less frequent flooding; grazing). Spartina anglica shows human-assisted spread.
  • Psammosere: embryo → yellow (marram) → grey → slack/heath; succession stabilises spits, bars, tombolos and realigned coasts.

Mission 4

  • High-energy coasts: waves exploit weaknesses to form notches, platforms, cliffs and the cave–arch–stack–stump sequence (Old Harry Rocks; the Twelve Apostles).
  • Low-energy coasts: longshore drift and deposition build beaches, spits (Spurn), bars, tombolos (Chesil Beach) and cuspate forelands (Dungeness); plant succession stabilises dunes.

Mission 5

  • Weathering (mechanical, chemical, biological) breaks rock in place, supplies sediment and lowers strength; mass movement (falls, rotational slumps, landslides, flows) moves it downslope.
  • Landforms: rotational scars, talus scree, terraced profiles (undercliffs). Subaerial and marine processes together make recession episodic, not steady.

Mission 6

  • Eustatic change moves the sea worldwide; isostatic and tectonic change move the land locally. What matters at a coast is the relative change.
  • Emergence leaves raised beaches, relict (fossil) cliffs and marine platforms; submergence makes rias, fjords and Dalmatian coasts.
  • Future sea-level rise will push many emergent and submergent coasts towards retreat.

Mission 7

  • Rapid recession needs weak material and strong marine processes; human actions (dams, canals, dredging, defences) cut sediment supply or move erosion along the coast.
  • Losses are largest on densely developed coasts: the Nile Delta's people and farmland, California's homes and beaches.

Mission 8

  • Risk = hazard × exposure × vulnerability; the Netherlands is more exposed, Tuvalu more vulnerable.
  • Local factors (polder subsidence) raise relative sea level; the pace of global rise is uncertain (ice sheets); some people may become environmental refugees.

Mission 9

  • Storm surge, from tropical cyclones or depressions (such as the North Sea storm of 1953), is often the deadliest hazard; Ian's surge flooded both Florida and Cuba, but losses are recorded very differently.

Mission 10

  • Hard engineering fixes a line but alters processes and costs more over time; soft engineering works with processes but must be repeated.
  • SMPs set policies by sediment cell.

Mission 11

  • Littoral cells set the scale for management; ICZM plans the whole coast and all its uses; SMPs choose HTL, ATL, managed realignment or NAI for each unit.
  • CBA and EIA inform decisions, but conflicts remain: at Happisburgh (developed) cliff-top owners lose so that the cell keeps its sediment; in Bangladesh's polders (developing) money, cyclones and land acquisition decided who was protected.
