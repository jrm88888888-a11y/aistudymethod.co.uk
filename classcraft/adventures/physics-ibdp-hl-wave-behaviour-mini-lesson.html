Mission 1

Mission 1: Why does light bend as it enters water, but not when it hits the surface head-on?

Make a guess: Why does light bend as it enters water, but not when it hits the surface head-on?

Quick check
?Circular ripples spread out across a ripple tank from a vibrating dipper. A student sketches a wavefront–ray diagram of the waves. Which statement correctly describes the rays on the diagram?
Mission 1

Wavefronts and rays

  • Wavefront: a line (or surface) joining points that are in phase, e.g. every crest.
  • Neighbouring wavefronts are one wavelength apart.
  • Ray: a line perpendicular to the wavefronts, showing the direction the wave's energy travels.
  • Point source: circular wavefronts, rays spreading out.
  • Distant source or laser: straight (plane) wavefronts, parallel rays.
Mission 1

Reflection

  • The normal: a line at 90° to the surface where the ray hits it.
  • Measure every angle from the normal, not from the surface.
  • Law of reflection: angle of incidence = angle of reflection.
  • Incident ray, reflected ray and normal lie in the same plane.
  • Reflection changes direction only: speed, frequency and wavelength stay the same.
Mission 1

Why refraction happens

  • At a boundary, part of a wave is reflected and part is transmitted.
  • The transmitted wave changes speed. Its frequency stays the same (set by the source), so its wavelength changes too: wavelength = speed ÷ frequency.
  • Hitting at an angle, one end of each wavefront reaches the boundary first and slows first. The wavefront turns, so the ray changes direction: refraction.
  • Into a slower medium: wavefronts closer together, ray bends towards the normal.
  • Into a faster medium: ray bends away from the normal.
  • Along the normal: the whole wavefront slows at once. Speed and wavelength change; the direction does not.
  • Water waves travel more slowly in shallower water. In a ripple tank, a shallow region refracts waves as glass refracts light.
Mission 1

Refractive index

  • n = c/v: speed of light in a vacuum ÷ speed in the material.
  • No unit. Vacuum: n = 1 exactly. Air: n ≈ 1.00 (1.0003).
  • Water 1.33 · typical glass about 1.5 · diamond 2.42.
  • Larger n, slower light.
Quick check
?The refractive index of a certain plastic is 1.5. Which statement correctly describes what this value means?
Mission 1

Snell's law

  • n1/n2 = sin θ2/sin θ1 = v2/v1, with θ the angle between the ray and the normal.
  • Into larger n: smaller angle (towards the normal). Into smaller n: larger angle (away).
  • θ1 = 0 gives θ2 = 0: no bending along the normal.
Mission 1

Worked example

  • Light of wavelength 589 nm in air enters water (n = 1.33) at 60.0° to the normal.
  • sin θ2 = (n1/n2) sin θ1 = (1.00/1.33) × sin 60.0° = 0.651 → θ2 = 40.6°.
  • Speed in water = c/n = 3.00 × 10⁸ ÷ 1.33 = 2.26 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.
  • Same frequency, so the wavelength falls by the same factor: 589 ÷ 1.33 = 443 nm.
  • Check: 40.6° < 60.0°, so the ray bent towards the normal, as it should entering a slower medium.
Quick check
?A ray of light passes from air into a transparent liquid. The incident ray makes an angle of 40.0° with the surface of the liquid and the refracted ray makes an angle of 55.0° with the surface. The speed of light in air is 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. What is the speed of light in the liquid?
Mission check

?The diagram shows straight water waves in a ripple tank crossing the boundary between region X and region Y. The wavefronts, a ray and the normal at the point where the ray crosses the boundary are drawn. Which statement is correct?

Refraction happens because the speed changes at the boundary. Head-on, the whole wavefront slows at once, so the direction does not change.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How can light stay trapped inside a thin glass strand, even when the strand bends?

Make a guess: How can light stay trapped inside a thin glass strand, even when the strand bends?

Mission 2

Heading into a lower n

  • Light going from glass into air (larger n to smaller n) bends away from the normal.
  • Increase the angle of incidence: the refracted ray swings towards the surface.
  • At the critical angle, the refracted ray runs along the boundary (angle of refraction 90°).
  • Beyond the critical angle no light leaves: all of it reflects back. This is total internal reflection (TIR).
  • Below the critical angle there is also a partial reflection, growing stronger as the angle nears the critical angle.
  • TIR obeys the law of reflection.
Mission 2

The critical angle

  • Put θ2 = 90° (sin 90° = 1) into Snell's law.
  • n1/n2 = sin 90°/sin θc, so sin θc = n2/n1.
  • Glass (1.50) to air: 41.8°. Water (1.33) to air: 48.8°.
  • TIR needs both: light heading into a medium of lower n, and angle of incidence greater than the critical angle.
Quick check
?Light can cross a flat boundary between water (n = 1.33) and glass (n = 1.50) in either direction. Which statement about total internal reflection at this boundary is correct?
Mission 2

Worked example: will it reflect?

  • A ray in water (n = 1.33) meets the water surface. Critical angle: sin⁻¹(1/1.33) = 48.8°.
  • At 50.0° to the normal: 50.0° > 48.8°, so TIR. No light leaves.
  • At 45.0°: 45.0° < 48.8°, so it refracts out. 1.33 × sin 45.0° = 1.00 × sin θ2 → sin θ2 = 0.940 → θ2 = 70.1°.
  • Always compare the angle from the normal with the critical angle.
  • An optical fibre uses this: light meets the wall at more than the critical angle at every bounce, even round gentle bends.
Quick check
?A glass block of refractive index 1.52 is under water of refractive index 1.33. A ray inside the glass meets the glass–water boundary at an angle of incidence of 55.0°. What happens to the ray?
Mission check

?An optical fibre has a glass core of refractive index 1.50, surrounded by glass of refractive index 1.40. On a straight stretch, a ray meets the core's wall at 75° to the normal. Where the fibre bends, the same ray meets the wall at 66° to the normal. What happens at each point?

In a fibre, light heads towards lower n and meets the wall at more than the critical angle, so it is totally internally reflected at every bounce, round gentle bends too.

Mission 3

Mission 3: A wave reaches the boundary between two materials. Does it carry on, bounce back, or both?

Make a guess: A wave reaches the boundary between two materials. Does it carry on, bounce back, or both?

Mission 3

Reflected and transmitted

  • Wherever the wave speed changes, a boundary splits a wave in two:
    • a reflected wave, back into the first medium;
    • a transmitted wave, on into the second.
  • The energy is shared between them (some may also be absorbed).
  • Bigger change in the medium → more reflected.
    • Sound from air into water: most is reflected.
Quick check
?Predict first. Sound from a loudspeaker in air reaches the surface of a swimming pool, and sound travels on into the water. Which property of the sound is the same in the water as in the air?
Mission 3

What changes and what doesn't

  • Frequency: unchanged. Each oscillation arriving at the boundary drives one oscillation beyond it.
  • Speed: set by the medium, so it changes.
  • Wavelength: λ = v/f, so it changes in proportion to v.
  • Reflected wave: same medium, so same speed and same wavelength as the incident wave.
Quick check
?Predict first. A light string is tied to a heavy string under the same tension, so waves travel more slowly on the heavy string. An upright pulse (displaced upward) travels along the light string towards the knot. Which claim about the reflected pulse, with its reasoning, is correct?
Mission 3

Upright or upside down?

  • Fixed end (wall): the reflected pulse is inverted (phase change of π, or 180°).
  • Free end (ring on a smooth pole): reflected pulse upright.
  • Join between two strings at the same tension:
    • into a slower (heavier) string: reflected pulse inverted;
    • into a faster (lighter) string: reflected pulse upright.
  • The transmitted pulse is always upright.
Mission 3

When pulses meet

  • Principle of superposition: where two waves or pulses overlap, the resultant displacement is the sum of their displacements, with signs.
  • +2 cm and +1 cm → +3 cm. +2 cm and −3 cm → −1 cm.
  • Pulses do not bounce off each other: they pass through and carry on unchanged.
Quick check
?Two pulses of the same shape and width travel towards each other along a stretched string. Pulse P has a maximum displacement of +3.0 cm and pulse Q a maximum displacement of −1.0 cm. Which statement about the pulses is correct?
Mission check

?At night, a person in a brightly lit room sees a faint reflection of the room in the window, while people outside can see into the room through the same window. What does this show?

Both. Wherever the speed changes, part of a wave is reflected and part transmitted; the frequency is kept, so the wavelength follows the speed.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Two speakers play the same note. How can one step sideways make the sound almost vanish?

Make a guess: Two speakers play the same note. How can one step sideways make the sound almost vanish?

Quick check
?Two pulses of identical shape and size but opposite displacement travel towards each other along a stretched string. At one instant they overlap exactly and the whole string is momentarily straight. Which statement about this instant, and what follows it, is correct?
Mission 4

Adding waves on a graph

  • To add two waves, add their displacements point by point.
  • Same frequency, in phase (crest meets crest): amplitude A1 + A2. Constructive interference.
  • Same frequency, in antiphase (crest meets trough): amplitude |A1 − A2|. Destructive interference. Equal amplitudes cancel completely.
  • Anything in between gives an amplitude in between.
Quick check
?The graph shows the displacement y of a stretched string against position x at time t = 0. Pulse A is travelling to the right and pulse B to the left, each at a speed of 1.0 cm s⁻¹. What is the displacement of the string at x = 6.0 cm when t = 3.0 s?
Mission 4

Two sources: path difference

  • Two sources, S1 and S2, emit waves in phase, with the same wavelength λ.
  • At a point P: path difference = |S2P − S1P|.
  • Path difference = nλ (0, λ, 2λ, …): crest meets crest. Constructive: loud, bright, large amplitude.
  • Path difference = (n + ½)λ (½λ, 1½λ, …): crest meets trough. Destructive: quiet, dark, small amplitude.
  • Result: a fixed pattern of maxima and minima. Energy is not destroyed at the minima; it is redistributed to the maxima.
Mission 4

Coherence

  • A steady pattern needs coherent sources: same frequency and a constant phase difference.
  • Two separate lamps are not coherent: their phase difference changes randomly, far too often to follow (light is emitted in short, unrelated bursts). The pattern shifts too fast to see.
  • Coherent sources come from one source: two speakers on one signal generator; two slits lit by the same light.
Mission 4

Worked example

  • Two speakers, in phase, play 850 Hz. Speed of sound 340 m s⁻¹. λ = v/f = 340 ÷ 850 = 0.400 m.
  • Point P: 3.00 m from one speaker, 3.60 m from the other. Path difference = 0.60 m = 1.5λ → destructive: quiet.
  • Point Q: 3.00 m and 3.80 m. Path difference = 0.80 m = 2λ → constructive: loud.
  • Walk from Q to P: the sound fades from loud to quiet in a few tens of centimetres.
Quick check
?Two loudspeakers connected to the same signal generator emit sound in phase at a frequency of 500 Hz. The speed of sound in air is 340 m s⁻¹. At which of these path differences is the sound quiet because of destructive interference?
Quick check
?Two loudspeakers side by side are driven by two separate signal generators, each set to 500 Hz. A listener walking across the room hears no steady pattern of loud and quiet places. Which statement explains this?
Mission check

?Two dippers vibrating in phase act as coherent sources S1 and S2 on a ripple tank, producing waves of wavelength 2.0 cm. Point P is 31.0 cm from S1 and 37.0 cm from S2. What kind of interference occurs at P?

Where the path difference is a whole number of wavelengths, crest meets crest: loud. Step to where it is a half-wavelength more, and crest meets trough: almost silent.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Two slits, a laser and a ruler: how can stripes on a wall measure a wavelength far thinner than a hair?

Make a guess: Two slits, a laser and a ruler: how can stripes on a wall measure a wavelength far thinner than a hair?

Quick check
?Predict first. Monochromatic light passes through two very narrow slits and falls on a distant screen, producing a pattern of bright and dark bands. Which explanation accounts for this pattern?
Mission 5

Young's double slit

  • Monochromatic light (e.g. a laser) → two narrow slits, very close together → a screen far away.
  • Light diffracts (spreads out) from each slit. The two spreading waves overlap and interfere.
  • Both slits are lit by the same wavefronts, so they act as coherent sources.
  • Bright fringe: path difference nλ. Dark fringe: path difference (n + ½)λ.
  • Result: equally spaced bright and dark fringes, with a bright fringe in the centre.
Mission 5

The fringe-spacing equation

  • Fringe spacing = λ × (slits-to-screen distance) ÷ (slit separation).
  • s = λD/d: s = fringe separation, d = slit separation, D = slits-to-screen distance.
  • Valid when the screen is far away compared with the slit separation (small angles).
Mission 5

Worked example

  • Laser light of wavelength 633 nm. Slits 0.50 mm apart. Screen 2.00 m from the slits.
  • s = λD/d = (633 × 10⁻⁹ × 2.00)/(0.50 × 10⁻³) = 2.5 × 10⁻³ m
  • Fringes 2.5 mm apart: easy to measure. A wavelength of 0.000 000 633 m becomes millimetres on the screen.
  • Turn it around: measure the fringe spacing, slit separation and distance, and calculate λ.
Quick check
?In a Young's double-slit experiment, monochromatic red light is incident normally on the slits and the fringe separation on the screen is 2.0 mm. Which single change would make the fringe separation 4.0 mm?
Mission check

?In a Young's double-slit experiment, monochromatic light is incident normally on two slits whose centres are 0.40 mm apart; each slit is 0.030 mm wide. The screen is 1.60 m from the slits. The distance from the centre of the 1st bright fringe to the centre of the 11th bright fringe is 24.0 mm. What is the wavelength of the light?

Fringe spacing = λ × distance to screen ÷ slit separation. A sub-micrometre λ, times metres, divided by a fraction of a millimetre, gives millimetres you can measure with a ruler.

Mission 6

Mission 6: You can hear someone around a corner but not see them. What decides how much a wave spreads?

Make a guess: You can hear someone around a corner but not see them. What decides how much a wave spreads?

Quick check
?Predict first. A wavefront diagram shows straight water waves of wavelength 2.0 cm, with wavefronts parallel to a barrier, passing through a gap 6.0 cm wide in the barrier; beyond the gap the wavefronts are straight in the middle and curved only at the edges. The gap is narrowed to 0.40 cm. How do the wavefronts beyond the gap change?
Mission 6

Diffraction

  • Diffraction: a wave spreads out as it passes through a gap or around the edge of an obstacle.
  • Speed, frequency and wavelength are unchanged. Only the direction of travel spreads.
  • Spreading becomes significant when the gap is comparable with λ. The narrower the gap, the more it spreads.
  • Gap about λ or smaller: nearly semicircular wavefronts, spreading through almost 180°.
  • Gap much wider than λ: very little spreading; a sharp-edged "shadow".
  • Speech: λ ≈ 0.3–3 m, like doorways. Light: λ ≈ 4–7 × 10⁻⁷ m, far smaller than any doorway.
Mission 6

Huygens and wavefront diagrams

  • Each point on a wavefront can be treated as a source of secondary wavelets; the new wavefront joins them.
  • At a gap, only part of the wavefront gets through. Wavelets from its edges spread into the "shadow".
  • Wavefront diagram: rays at 90° to wavefronts. Wavefront spacing (λ) stays the same beyond the gap.
  • Wide gap: straight in the middle, curved at the edges. Narrow gap: nearly semicircular.
Mission 6

Light through one slit

  • Laser light through one narrow slit gives bright and dark bands, not a slit-shaped patch.
  • Central maximum: brightest, and twice as wide as each side maximum.
  • Side maxima: much dimmer, fainter the more distant they are from the centre.
  • Narrower slit → wider central maximum (and dimmer: less light gets through).
  • Longer λ (red rather than blue) → wider central maximum.
Mission 6

Where the dark bands are

  • Split the slit into a top half and a bottom half. Pair each point in the top half with the point half a slit width below it.
  • At angle θ, the top edge and the middle differ in path by (b/2) sin θ.
  • If that is λ/2, every pair cancels → first minimum: b sin θ = λ.
  • Small angles (θ in radians): θ = λ/b, b = slit width. Central maximum spans 2θ.
Mission 6

Worked example

  • Laser, λ = 650 nm; slit width 0.10 mm; screen 2.0 m away.
  • θ = λ/b = (650 × 10⁻⁹) ÷ (0.10 × 10⁻³) = 6.5 × 10⁻³ rad.
  • Centre to first dark band: 2.0 × 6.5 × 10⁻³ = 1.3 × 10⁻² m (1.3 cm).
  • Central maximum width: 2 × 1.3 cm = 2.6 cm, about 260 times the slit width.
Quick check
?Monochromatic light of wavelength 600 nm is incident normally on a single rectangular slit of width 0.12 mm. A screen is 2.0 m from the slit. What is the width of the central maximum on the screen, measured between the first minima on either side?
Mission 6

Two slits, each with a width

  • Real double slits: each slit has width b and diffracts. Centres are d apart.
  • Interference fringes: equally spaced, set by d.
  • Their brightness follows the single-slit envelope, set by b.
  • Fringe spacing s = λD/d; envelope's first minima at θ = λ/b.
  • Where a fringe falls on an envelope minimum, it is missing. Cover one slit: the fringes vanish and only the envelope is left.
Quick check
?A double slit has slits of width b = 0.10 mm whose centres are d = 0.30 mm apart. It is illuminated normally with monochromatic light. How many bright interference fringes lie within the central maximum of the single-slit diffraction envelope?
Mission check

?Sea waves reach a harbour wall with a 20 m wide entrance. A boat is moored behind the wall, well to one side of the entrance. On day 1 the waves have a wavelength of 20 m; on day 2, 2.0 m, with the same amplitude. On which day is the boat rocked more, and why?

Spreading depends on λ compared with the gap or obstacle. Sound's wavelengths, about the size of a doorway, bend around it; light's, a million times smaller, barely do.

Mission 7

Mission 7: How can a slide ruled with thousands of fine lines measure the wavelength of light?

Make a guess: How can a slide ruled with thousands of fine lines measure the wavelength of light?

Mission 7

What a grating is

  • A diffraction grating: a plate with many equally spaced, parallel slits (lines).
  • Slit spacing d = 1 ÷ (lines per metre). 300 lines per mm → d = 1/300 mm = 3.33 × 10⁻⁶ m.
  • Each slit diffracts, so light from all the slits overlaps and interferes.
  • Bright beams leave only at certain angles: where light from every slit arrives in phase.
Mission 7

Deriving the grating equation

  • Light hits the grating at normal incidence (straight on).
  • Look at light leaving two adjacent slits at angle θ. Extra path for the lower slit: d sin θ.
  • In phase when this path difference is a whole number of wavelengths:
  • d sin θ = nλ, n = 0, 1, 2, … (the order).
  • If adjacent slits are in phase, all the slits are in phase.
  • n = 0: the zero order, straight through (θ = 0).
  • Highest order: sin θ cannot exceed 1, so n ≤ d/λ.
Mission 7

Worked example

  • 300 lines per mm, red laser λ = 633 nm.
  • d = 1 ÷ (300 × 10³) = 3.33 × 10⁻⁶ m.
  • First order: sin θ = λ/d = 633 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ 3.33 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.190 → θ = 10.9°.
  • Second order: sin θ = 2λ/d = 0.380 → θ = 22.3°.
  • Highest order: d/λ = 5.27 → n = 5 (at 71.7°). Total beams: 5 + 5 + 1 = 11.
  • No small-angle shortcut: angles are large. Use sin θ.
  • On a screen a distance D away, a spot x from the centre is at tan θ = x/D. Find θ first, then use sin θ.
Quick check
?Light of wavelength 650 nm is incident normally on a diffraction grating that has 600 lines per millimetre. At what angle to the straight-through direction is the second-order maximum observed?
Quick check
?Predict first. Monochromatic light is incident normally first on a double slit and then on a diffraction grating whose adjacent slits have the same separation d as the double slit. Every slit is equally illuminated. Compared with the double-slit fringes, how do the grating's principal maxima appear?
Mission 7

Why use many slits?

  • Same d, same λ: grating maxima are at the same angles as double-slit fringes.
  • But with many slits, a tiny step away from those angles puts the slits out of step and they cancel.
  • So the maxima are very sharp and very bright, with darkness between. Angles can be measured precisely.
  • Smaller d (more lines per mm) → orders farther apart.
Quick check
?Predict first. White light is incident normally on a diffraction grating. Which describes the pattern seen on a screen?
Mission 7

White light and spectra

  • Zero order is white: every wavelength has θ = 0 there.
  • Each other order is a spectrum: violet (short λ) nearest the centre, red farthest, since sin θ ∝ λ.
  • Higher orders spread wider and can overlap: second-order red (2 × 700 = 1400 nm) lies beyond third-order violet (3 × 400 = 1200 nm).
  • A gas-discharge lamp gives a line spectrum: separate bright coloured lines in each order, one per wavelength.
Mission check

?A laser beam is directed normally at a grating with 500 lines per mm. On a screen 1.00 m from the grating, the two second-order spots are 1.50 m apart. What is the wavelength of the laser light?

A grating turns a tiny, unmeasurable spacing into large, sharp angles. Measure the angle, know d, and λ follows from the grating equation.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Rays are perpendicular to wavefronts. Reflection: angle of incidence = angle of reflection, measured from the normal.
  • n = c/v. Refraction is caused by a change of speed; frequency is unchanged, wavelength changes.
  • n1/n2 = sin θ2/sin θ1 = v2/v1: towards the normal into larger n.

Mission 2

  • TIR: light heading into lower n at more than the critical angle is all reflected.
  • sin θc = n2/n1.

Mission 3

  • At a boundary where the wave speed changes, a wave is partly reflected and partly transmitted; frequency is unchanged, so λ changes with v.
  • Reflection from a fixed end, or into a slower medium, inverts the pulse; from a free end, or into a faster medium, it does not.
  • Overlapping pulses: displacements add with signs; pulses pass through each other.

Mission 4

  • Superposition: add displacements. Waves and pulses pass through each other unchanged.
  • Sources in phase: path difference nλ → constructive; (n + ½)λ → destructive.
  • A steady interference pattern needs coherent sources: same frequency, constant phase difference.

Mission 5

  • Two coherent slits → equally spaced bright and dark fringes; bright where path difference = nλ.
  • s = λD/d.

Mission 6

  • Diffraction is significant when the gap or obstacle is comparable with λ, and greater the narrower the gap; λ itself does not change.
  • Single slit: wide, bright central maximum; narrower slit or longer λ → wider pattern.
  • First minimum at θ = λ/b; in a double slit, the single-slit envelope modulates the fringes.

Mission 7

  • Grating at normal incidence: d sin θ = nλ, d = 1/(lines per metre); n ≤ d/λ.
  • Many slits → same angles as a double slit, but much sharper, brighter maxima.
