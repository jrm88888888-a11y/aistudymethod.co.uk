The Making of Modern Britain 1951-2007

This is an optional route. AQA A-level History is option-based. 2S The Making of Modern Britain is one of nineteen Component 2 depth studies (2A–2T). It is a British option, so pair it with a non-British Component 1 breadth study, plus the Component 3 Historical Investigation.

This mini-lesson covers both parts of 2S: building a new Britain, 1951–1979 and modern Britain, 1979–2007. The organising questions are whether there was ever a post-war consensus, whether Britain really declined, and how far 1979 was a genuine break.

1951–1964 · affluence and its anxieties

Conservative Britain: never had it so good?

Thirteen years of Conservative government coincided with rising living standards — and a mounting sense of national decline.

  • The Conservatives accepted the post-war settlement: the NHS, full employment, the mixed economy, most nationalisation. Only iron, steel and road haulage were denationalised.
  • Affluence: real wages rose, home ownership and consumer durables spread. Harold Macmillan's speech at Bedford in July 1957 — that most of our people had 'never had it so good' — captured and mocked the mood in equal measure.
  • Suez, 1956: the Anglo-French-Israeli operation against Nasser collapsed under American financial pressure. It was the moment Britain's post-imperial dependence became undeniable, and it destroyed Anthony Eden.
  • Decolonisation accelerated — Macmillan's 'wind of change' speech in Cape Town (February 1960) — while the first EEC application was vetoed by de Gaulle in 1963.
  • Decay: the Profumo affair (1963) and satire punctured Conservative authority. Macmillan resigned; Alec Douglas-Home's brief premiership ended in defeat in 1964.

Immigration: the Empire Windrush had docked in 1948, and Commonwealth migration grew through the 1950s. The Commonwealth Immigrants Act (1962) was the first major restriction — the beginning of a politics that runs the length of this option.

1964–1970 · Wilson and the permissive society

White heat, devaluation and social liberalisation

Harold Wilson won in 1964 promising to modernise Britain in the 'white heat' of technological revolution — a phrase from his 1963 conference speech.

  • Economic failure: the National Plan collapsed; defence of an overvalued pound ended in devaluation in November 1967. Wilson's authority never recovered.
  • Social reform was the lasting achievement, much of it through private members' bills supported by Home Secretary Roy Jenkins: the Abortion Act (1967), the Sexual Offences Act (1967) partially decriminalising male homosexuality, the abolition of theatre censorship (1968), the Divorce Reform Act (1969) and the Equal Pay Act (1970). The Race Relations Acts (1965 and 1968) outlawed discrimination.
  • Backlash: Enoch Powell's speech of April 1968 — the so-called 'Rivers of Blood' speech — got him sacked from the shadow cabinet and made him, briefly, the most popular politician in Britain. The Commonwealth Immigrants Act (1968) restricted entry further.

Interpretation: was the 'permissive society' a genuine liberation, or a change confined to legislation and metropolitan elites, leaving most people's lives and attitudes largely untouched until much later? The evidence on both sides is real. Take a side.

1970–1979 · the crisis decade

Heath, the unions, the IMF and the Winter of Discontent

The 1970s destroyed the post-war economic settlement.

  • Edward Heath (1970–74) took Britain into the EEC on 1 January 1973 — his greatest achievement — but was broken by inflation and industrial conflict. After the 1973 oil shock and the miners' overtime ban came the three-day week and the February 1974 election, fought on 'who governs Britain?'. The answer was: not Heath.
  • Labour, 1974–79: the 1975 referendum confirmed EEC membership by a large majority. But inflation peaked above 20% in 1975, and in 1976 Chancellor Healey was forced to seek an IMF loan with spending cuts attached — a humiliation that, for many historians, marks the real death of Keynesian Britain.
  • The Winter of Discontent (1978–79): public-sector strikes against pay restraint produced the images — uncollected rubbish, unburied dead — that destroyed Labour's claim to manage the unions. Callaghan lost a confidence vote by one and the election that followed.

Causation for the essay: Thatcherism is unintelligible without the 1970s. She did not create the crisis of confidence in the post-war model — she offered an answer to a crisis that was already there. Establish that and your 1979 essay writes itself.

1979–1990 · Thatcherism

How much really changed?

Margaret Thatcher won in 1979 and governed until November 1990.

  • Economy: monetarism and high interest rates squeezed inflation at the cost of a savage recession — unemployment passed three million by 1982, and manufacturing was hit hardest. Then privatisation (British Telecom 1984, British Gas 1986), council house sales, and financial deregulation (the 'Big Bang', 1986).
  • Confrontation: the Falklands War (1982) transformed her standing; the defeat of the miners' strike (March 1984 – March 1985) broke the industrial power of the unions.
  • Fall: the poll tax (community charge) provoked mass non-payment and riots; Cabinet and party turned on her in November 1990.
  • John Major (1990–97): won unexpectedly in 1992, then lost economic credibility on Black Wednesday (16 September 1992) when sterling crashed out of the ERM. Europe split the Conservative party — as it had split it before, and would again.

The Thatcherism question. Was it a coherent ideological project, or a series of improvisations rationalised afterwards? Note what she did not do: the NHS survived, welfare spending did not fall in real terms, and comprehensive schools remained. The strong answer distinguishes rhetoric, policy and outcome — they are not the same thing.

1997–2007 · New Labour

Continuity, constitution, and Iraq

Tony Blair's landslide in May 1997 ended eighteen years of Conservative rule.

  • Economic continuity: Labour accepted Conservative spending plans for two years, made the Bank of England independent (1997), and did not renationalise. But it introduced the National Minimum Wage (1998) and tax credits, and raised NHS and school spending sharply after 2000.
  • Constitutional change was the real radicalism: devolution for Scotland and Wales (referendums 1997; parliament and assembly 1999); the Good Friday Agreement (10 April 1998); the Human Rights Act (1998); removal of most hereditary peers (1999); the Freedom of Information Act (2000).
  • Iraq (2003): the decision to join the US invasion, and the failure to find weapons of mass destruction, destroyed public trust in Blair and defines his premiership's historical reputation. He left office in June 2007.

The 2S judgement: did New Labour represent the consolidation of Thatcherism (a new consensus around markets, deregulation and a lightly redistributive state), or a genuine social-democratic project working within its constraints? Both cases can be made from the evidence — which is precisely why AQA sets it.

Interpretations · consensus and decline

The two debates that structure 2S

Almost every extract in this option belongs to one of two arguments. Learn to place them instantly.

1. Was there a post-war consensus? Paul Addison, The Road to 1945 (1975), argued that the wartime coalition forged a genuine cross-party settlement — full employment, welfare, the mixed economy — that governed British politics until the 1970s. Ben Pimlott replied that consensus was largely a mirage: politics was bitterly adversarial at the time, and the appearance of agreement is a trick of hindsight. Test it: the Conservatives kept the NHS, but fought Labour ferociously on steel, housing and rents.
2. Did Britain decline? Correlli Barnett (The Audit of War, 1986) argued that post-war Britain squandered its industrial future on a welfare state it could not afford — the declinist case, which was politically useful to the Thatcherite right. Revisionists disagree sharply: Jim Tomlinson argues that 'decline' is a political construct, and David Edgerton (The Rise and Fall of the British Nation, 2018) shows that Britain's post-war economy and technological capacity were far stronger than the declinist story allows. Britain grew — just more slowly than others rebuilding from a lower base.

Exam gold: 'relative decline' and 'decline' are not the same claim. British living standards rose throughout this period. What fell was Britain's share of world output relative to fast-recovering competitors. Make that distinction and you are immediately arguing above the crowd.

Exam technique · Component 2

The depth paper, and the recent-history trap

AQA Component 2: 80 marks, 2 hours 30 minutes, 40% — one compulsory three-extract question (30 marks) plus two essays from three (25 marks each).

  • The 2S trap is opinion. This is history within living memory and students smuggle in politics. Examiners can tell. Anchor every claim in evidence and date: not 'Thatcher destroyed communities', but 'the closure programme after the 1984–85 strike removed the economic base of coalfield towns, and unemployment in those areas remained above the national average for a decade'.
  • Use the historiography as a structure. An essay on 1979 that is organised around the consensus debate and the declinist debate will automatically be analytical rather than narrative.

Key question · society and culture

How much did Britain really change?

2S is not only a political option. Social change is examined, and it is where the most interesting judgements live.

  • Affluence: car and television ownership, foreign holidays, home ownership. The Right to Buy after 1980 turned millions of council tenants into owners — and permanently reduced the social housing stock.
  • The permissive legislation of 1967–70 — abortion, homosexuality, divorce, equal pay — changed the law far in advance of general public attitudes. Whether it changed lives as quickly is a genuine question, and the evidence is mixed.
  • Race: from the Windrush (1948) to the Race Relations Acts (1965, 1968, 1976), and the 1981 riots in Brixton and Toxteth, whose causes the Scarman Report examined.
  • Class: the collapse of heavy industry after 1979 destroyed the occupational basis of the old industrial working class — arguably the deepest social change of the whole period.

The judgement to argue: Britain in 2007 was richer, more diverse, more socially liberal and far less industrial than in 1951 — but inequality had widened sharply since the late 1970s. 'Progress' is not a verdict; for whom, and measured how is.
