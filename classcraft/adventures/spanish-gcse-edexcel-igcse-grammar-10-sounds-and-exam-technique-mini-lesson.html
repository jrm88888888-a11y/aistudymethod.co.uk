Exam skills & deducing meaning

The last cluster isn't new grammar — it's how to use what you know under exam conditions. 4SP1 sets a "Minimum core" vocabulary list, and its tasks "may include some unfamiliar vocabulary". So half the battle is working out words you've never been taught. This lesson trains two things: deducing meaning from context, and exam technique for the three 4SP1 papers.

Work through each screen and answer the questions as you go — some ask you to recognise the right meaning, some are type-it tasks where you supply the correct word form. Collect ⭐ stars and press Start.

Deducing meaning · why it matters

You will meet unknown words — on purpose

4SP1's vocabulary is a "Minimum core" — the words you're guaranteed to be tested on. But three things mean you must handle words beyond it:

  • Tasks "may include some unfamiliar vocabulary" — unknown words are built in.
  • Cognates (words that look like English) are deliberately left off the list — the exam expects you to spot them yourself.
  • Glossing is minimised — few words are explained for you at the bottom of the text.

The mindset: never freeze at a word you don't know. Squeeze meaning out of four sources: what it looks like (cognate), the word family it belongs to, its affixes, and the context & grammar around it. The next screens drill each one.

Clue 1 · cognates

Cognates & near-cognates

Thousands of Spanish words are cognates — near-identical to English — or near-cognates with a predictable twist. Learn the common spelling swaps and you unlock them on sight:

Spanish patternEnglish & example
-ción-tion — nación, educación
-dad / -tad-ty — universidad, libertad
-mente-ly — rápidamente = rapidly
-oso / -osa-ous — famoso, peligroso
es- + consonants- — estudiar, especial = study, special

Watch false friends. A few look English but aren't: sensible = "sensitive" (not sensible), éxito = "success" (not exit), actualmente = "currently" (not actually), embarazada = "pregnant". Let context confirm the cognate before you commit.

Clue 2 · word families

One root, a whole family

If you know one word, you often know its relatives. Spot the shared root and the meaning follows:

you know…so you can guess…
trabajar = to worktrabajo (job), trabajador (worker / hard-working)
cocinar = to cookcocina (kitchen), cocinero (cook / chef)
enseñar = to teachenseñanza (teaching), enseñante (teacher)

The trick: when a word looks new, strip it to its stem and ask "what verb or noun do I already know that starts like this?" deportista → deporte (sport) → "a sportsperson".

Clue 3 · prefixes & suffixes

Affixes bend the meaning

Small prefixes and suffixes shift a word predictably. Learn the frequent ones:

  • Negating prefixes: in-/im- = "un-/not" — imposible, increíble; des- = "un-/dis-" — desordenado (untidy), desempleo (unemployment).
  • Again: re- — reciclar (recycle), rehacer (redo).
  • -mente makes an adverb ("-ly"): fácil → fácilmente (easily).
  • -ería = a shop/place: pan → panadería (bakery), libro → librería (bookshop).
  • -ito / -ita = small/affectionate: perro → perrito (little dog), casa → casita.

Put it together: desempleo = des- (un-) + empleo (employment) = unemployment. Each piece you recognise narrows the meaning.

Clue 4 · context & grammar

Let the sentence tell you

Even with no spelling clue, the grammar around a word tells you a lot about it — and the topic tells you the likely field of meaning:

  • Is it a noun, verb or adjective? An article (el/la/un) before it → a noun. A subject + it + object → a verb. Sitting after a noun and agreeing → an adjective.
  • Verb endings date the action. -é / -ó = past; -ré / -rá = future; -ando / -iendo = "-ing".
  • Connectives steer meaning. pero (but) signals a contrast coming; porque (because) a reason; sin embargo (however) a turn.
  • Topic frames the guess. An unknown noun in a health text is probably about the body, illness or lifestyle — pick the meaning that fits the topic.

Worked example

"No fui al colegio porque estaba enfermo."

Clues: porque gives a reason; the topic is school/absence; estaba + adjective describes a state. A reason to miss school + "I was ___" → enfermo = ill. You never needed it pre-taught.

Exam technique · Paper 1

Paper 1 — Listening (25%)

Every clip is heard twice. The question types are all recognition- or short-answer-based:

  • Multiple choice — pick the right option; watch for distractors that mention the wrong detail.
  • Multiple matching — match speakers/statements to options; cross off as you go.
  • Note-taking — jot key facts (who, what, when, how much).
  • Table completion & gap-fill — write the missing word; numbers, dates and times come up a lot.

Two-hearings plan: 1st listen — get the gist and pencil in easy answers. 2nd listen — confirm and fill gaps. Use the question wording to predict what to listen for, and don't be thrown by an unfamiliar word — catch the numbers and names around it.

Exam technique · Paper 2

Paper 2 — Reading & Writing (50%)

The biggest paper, in two sections:

  • Section A — Reading comprehension. Texts with questions in Spanish/English. This is where your deducing-meaning skills earn marks — infer unknown words, don't leave blanks.
  • Section B — Writing. Two tasks plus a word-form task:
    • A 60–75 word task that must incorporate 4 given words — tick each one off as you use it.
    • A 130–150 word task on 4 bullet points — cover every bullet and show a range of tenses.
    • Q8 — the word-form task (next screen): change a bracketed word into its correct form.

No translation, no dictation in 4SP1. Don't waste time preparing those — this course doesn't set them. Do bank the two word counts and the "4 given words / 4 bullets" rules.

Paper 2 · the Q8 skill

Q8 — the word-form task

Q8 gives a sentence with a word in brackets in its base form. You must write the correct form for the sentence — usually a conjugated verb or an adjective made to agree. Nothing else changes.

Worked examples

Mis hermanas son muy (alto). → subject is feminine plural, so alto → altas.

Ayer yo (comer) paella. → "yesterday" + yo → preterite → comer → comí.

Ella (vivir) en Madrid ahora. → present, ella → vivir → vive.

Method: ask (1) what part of speech is the bracketed word, (2) who/what does it refer to (subject or noun), and (3) which tense/agreement the sentence needs. Then change only the ending.

Exam technique · Paper 3

Paper 3 — Speaking (25%)

Two parts, and — importantly — no role-play in 4SP1:

  • Picture-based discussion. You choose a picture in advance and discuss it. Describe it with En la foto hay… (in the photo there is/are…), Veo… (I see…), A la izquierda / a la derecha… (on the left / right), then give opinions and reasons (Me parece… porque…).
  • Two spontaneous topic conversations. Unprepared chats on two topics — answer in full sentences, add a reason, and use more than one tense to lift your marks.

No role-play. Unlike some other boards, 4SP1 Speaking is a picture discussion + two conversations — there are no scripted transactional role-plays to rehearse.

Recap

Your exam-skills toolkit

Deduce meaning: cognates (-ción→-tion, -dad→-ty, -mente→-ly), word families (shared roots), affixes (des-, re-, -ería, -ito), and context/grammar clues. Watch false friends (éxito, sensible, actualmente).

Paper 1 · Listening (25%): heard twice; MCQ, multiple-matching, note-taking, table-completion, gap-fill.

Paper 2 · Reading & Writing (50%): Reading comprehension (infer unknowns); Writing = 60–75 words using 4 given words, 130–150 words on 4 bullets, and Q8 word-form.

Paper 3 · Speaking (25%): picture-based discussion + two spontaneous topic conversations. No role-play.

Not in 4SP1: no translation, no dictation.
