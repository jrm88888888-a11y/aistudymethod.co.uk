Mission 1: Is switching off a machine morally different from giving a lethal injection?

Mission 1

Natural law applied

  • Primary precept: preservation of life. A secondary precept follows: never directly kill an innocent human being.
  • Aquinas on suicide (Summa Theologiae II-II q. 64 a. 5, paraphrase): it goes against natural self-love, injures the community, and takes what belongs to God.
  • So voluntary euthanasia and assisted suicide are wrong: the person's consent does not change what the act is.
  • Also against ordered society: doctors would become agents of death.
  • Natural law is absolutist here: no exceptions for hard cases.
Mission 1

Double effect and proportionate means

  • Double effect: an act with a good and a bad effect may be done if the act is not wrong in itself, the bad effect is not intended or the means to the good, and there is a proportionate reason.
  • Pain relief: strong painkillers given to relieve pain may be right even if they may shorten life, as long as death is foreseen, not intended (Pius XII, 1957; Catechism 2279).
  • Proportionate means (1980 Declaration, paraphrase): no one must accept treatment that is too burdensome or out of proportion to its expected benefit. Refusing it is accepting death, not causing it.
  • (Older terms: ordinary and extraordinary means.)
  • So natural law separates euthanasia from good end-of-life care by intention and proportion, not by whether the doctor acts or omits.
  • Critics: intention is hidden; a large dose "to relieve pain" might be euthanasia in disguise.

Mission 1

Situation ethics applied

  • Joseph Fletcher argued for voluntary euthanasia years before Situation Ethics (1966): Morals and Medicine (1954), chapter "Euthanasia: Our Right to Die".
  • Agape: the only intrinsic good. If ending a life on request is the most loving act in the situation, it is right.
  • Personalism: the person in pain matters more than the rule "never kill".
  • Pragmatism: does a rule against euthanasia actually help this patient?
  • Relativism: the answer depends on the case: love might also refuse a request made under family pressure.
  • Fletcher also used personhood ("indicators of humanhood") to argue that ending life where no person remains need not be wrong.
  • Critics: one person's calculation of love could justify non-voluntary euthanasia; a society may need firm rules to protect the vulnerable.
Mission 1

Virtue ethics and utilitarianism

  • Virtue ethics asks: what would a compassionate, just and practically wise doctor (or patient) do?
  • Philippa Foot (1920–2010), "Euthanasia" (Philosophy & Public Affairs, 1977): euthanasia must be for the good of the one who dies (charity) and must not violate their rights (justice). A competent person can waive the right not to be killed, but she doubted that a law allowing active euthanasia could be safely framed (paraphrase).
  • Other virtue ethicists: courage and hope in dying, and the doctor's role as healer, count against it.
Mission 1

Acts and omissions

  • Acts and omissions doctrine: actively killing is morally worse than letting someone die, even if the result is the same.
  • Defenders: in letting die, the disease causes death; the doctor stops fighting it. UK law follows this line (Bland, 1993).
  • James Rachels (1941–2003), "Active and Passive Euthanasia" (New England Journal of Medicine, 1975): Smith drowns his young cousin for an inheritance; Jones means to, but watches the boy slip and drown. Neither is less guilty (paraphrase).
  • So, Rachels argues, if letting die is allowed, a quicker, kinder lethal injection may be better: passive euthanasia can mean a slower death.
  • Glover (Causing Death and Saving Life, 1977) also denies that the difference between act and omission has moral weight of its own; what matters is the outcome and the reasons.

Mission 1

The doctor–patient relationship and trust

  • The Hippocratic Oath (Greek, c. 4th century BCE) includes a promise not to give a deadly drug to anyone who asks (paraphrase).
  • Trust argument against: if doctors may end lives, patients (especially the old, disabled or poor) may fear them; medicine's purpose of healing is blurred.
  • Doctors' consciences: should any doctor be required to take part? Bills and laws normally include a right to opt out.
  • Trust argument for: patients may trust doctors more if they know their wishes at the end will be honoured, openly rather than in secret.
  • Professional bodies differ: the British Medical Association moved to a neutral position in 2021; many palliative-care doctors remain opposed.
  • Patients' trust also depends on doctors being honest about prognosis and options.
Mission 1

The slippery slope

  • Slippery slope: allowing voluntary euthanasia in limited cases will lead, step by step, to cases now thought wrong.
  • Logical form: if autonomy is the reason, why limit it to the terminally ill? If relief of suffering is the reason, why limit it to those who can ask?
  • Empirical form: points to change where laws exist, e.g. the Netherlands' Groningen Protocol for newborns (2005); Belgium's removal of an age limit for children (2014); Canada extending medical assistance in dying to people whose death is not foreseeable (2021).
  • Replies: each change was debated and voted on, not inevitable; strict safeguards and reporting can hold a line; the evidence is read differently by each side.
  • Opponents add: pressure on people who feel a burden, and on disabled people.
Mission 1Evaluate

Can a person have complete autonomy over their own life?

  • Claim: A competent adult should have complete autonomy over their own life, including how and when it ends.
  • For: Mill's harm principle: over one's own body the individual is sovereign. Refusing treatment is already allowed.
  • For: Singer and Glover: respecting a person's considered wish to die respects them as a person.
  • Against: No one chooses alone: choices are shaped by pain, depression, money and fear of being a burden.
  • Against: Others must act (doctors, nurses), so it is not purely self-regarding; natural law and many Christians: life is held in trust from God.
  • Against: some argue autonomy dominates only because shared values have thinned; it may not be enough on its own.
  • Judgement criteria: Is the choice truly free and informed? Who else must act? Can safeguards protect the vulnerable? Is life a gift, a possession, or both?

Mission 1Worked answer

Applying a theory

Issue: "Situation ethics is a better approach to voluntary euthanasia than natural law."

  • Point: Situation ethics responds to the actual patient: if a competent, terminally ill woman in unrelievable pain asks to die, agape and personalism may require helping her.
  • Source: Fletcher, Morals and Medicine (1954): a "right to die" in such cases.
  • Counter: Natural law protects the vulnerable with a fixed rule, and double effect already allows strong pain relief.
  • Develop: The test is whether a case-by-case approach can be safe across a whole health system, or only at one bedside.
  • Mini-judgement: It answers the single case well, but must meet the slippery slope and trust objections to answer the question about law.
  • AO2 tip: end each paragraph with a mini-judgement that answers the issue set, not just a summary.

Mission 2

Five purposes of punishment

  • Retribution: the offender gets what they deserve; punishment fits the crime. Looks back.
  • Deterrence: discourage this offender (individual) and others (general) from crime. Looks forward.
  • Protection: keep the public safe from a dangerous offender.
  • Rehabilitation (reform): help the offender change and return to society.
  • Vindication: uphold the authority of the law and publicly affirm the rights that were violated.
  • Retributive theories (e.g. Kant) look back to guilt; utilitarian theories (e.g. Bentham) look forward to consequences.

Mission 2

Moral responsibility and diminished responsibility

  • Punishment assumes moral responsibility: the offender acted freely and knew right from wrong.
  • Libertarian: free choice makes retribution deserved. Hard determinist: no one truly deserves punishment, though deterrence and reform may still be useful. Compatibilist: punish uncoerced acts.
  • Diminished responsibility (Homicide Act 1957, s. 2, in England and Wales; Criminal Justice Act (Northern Ireland) 1966, s. 5): an abnormality of mental functioning that substantially impairs a person's responsibility reduces murder to manslaughter.
  • Also: insanity (the M'Naghten Rules, 1843); the age of criminal responsibility (10 in England, Wales and Northern Ireland).
  • Christian view: guilt depends on knowledge and freedom; the Catechism (§1735) says responsibility can be diminished by ignorance, fear, habit and other factors.
  • Spec: "the relevance of moral responsibility to reward and punishment".
Mission 2

Capital punishment: the debate

  • UK: last executions 1964; abolished for murder 1965 (made permanent 1969; Northern Ireland 1973); abolished for all crimes 1998.
  • For: retribution (Kant: justice requires the murderer's death, Metaphysics of Morals, 1797); deterrence; permanent protection; justice for victims' families.
  • For: John Stuart Mill (1868 speech in Parliament) supported it for the worst murders as more humane than lifelong imprisonment.
  • Against: irreversible errors (Timothy Evans, hanged 1950, pardoned 1966; Derek Bentley, hanged 1953, conviction quashed 1998).
  • Against: deterrent effect disputed; ends any chance of reform; risk of unequal application; the sanctity and dignity of every life.
Mission 2

Natural moral law applied

  • Aquinas (Summa Theologiae II-II, q. 64, a. 2): a dangerous offender is like a diseased limb: "it is praiseworthy and advantageous that he be killed in order to safeguard the common good". Only public authority may do this.
  • Primary precepts: preserve life and live in an ordered society: Aquinas saw the death penalty as serving both.
  • Development: John Paul II, Evangelium Vitae (1995) §56: cases where execution is truly necessary are "very rare, if not practically non-existent".
  • Catechism §2267 (revised 2018): the death penalty is inadmissible, because it attacks the dignity of the person; modern prisons can protect society.
  • Debate: does this develop natural law (the common good now met without killing), or contradict Aquinas?
  • Ask how the secondary precept follows from the primary precept in today's conditions.
Mission 2

Situation ethics and virtue ethics applied

  • Situation ethics: is execution the most loving act for everyone: offender, victims, society? Personalism favours the person over the law.
  • Most situationists oppose it: it ends any chance of repentance, and errors cannot be undone. But relativism means no absolute rule: an extreme case is not ruled out in principle.
  • Virtue ethics: what would a just and merciful person do? Aristotle: justice gives each their due; equity corrects the law where its general rule fits a case badly (Nicomachean Ethics V.10).
  • Vengeance driven by anger is a vice (excess); indifference to victims is a deficiency.
  • Virtue ethicists divide: some see execution as just desert; many see it as failing in mercy and practical wisdom.

Mission 2Evaluate

Can capital punishment ever be justified?

  • Claim: Capital punishment can be morally justified for the worst crimes.
  • For: Retribution: some crimes deserve death (Kant); Aquinas allowed it for the common good.
  • For: It may deter and permanently protects; Mill thought it more humane than life imprisonment.
  • Against: Irreversible errors (Evans, Bentley); deterrence unproven.
  • Against: Human dignity (Catechism §2267, 2018); Finnis: intending death is always wrong; no room for reform or mercy.
  • Judgement criteria: Is punishment mainly backward- or forward-looking? Can the state be trusted with irreversible power? Does dignity belong to offenders too?
  • Weigh how natural law, situation ethics and virtue ethics answer differently.

Mission 3

Who counts morally?

  • Every answer about animals rests on one question: do animals have moral status (do they matter for their own sake)?
  • No direct status: René Descartes (1596–1650), Discourse on the Method (1637), Part V: animals are like machines, without minds.
  • Indirect duties only: Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274) and Immanuel Kant (1724–1804, Lectures on Ethics): animals are for human use; cruelty is wrong mainly because it makes people cruel to humans.
  • Direct status through sentience: Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832), An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789), ch. XVII:

> "The question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?"

  • Sentience = the capacity to feel pain and pleasure.
Mission 3Read the source

Dominion and stewardship

> "Be fruitful, multiply, fill the earth, and subdue it. Have dominion over the fish of the sea, over the birds of the sky, and over every living thing that moves on the earth." (Genesis 1:28, WEBBE)

> "The LORD God took the man, and put him into the garden of Eden to cultivate and keep it." (Genesis 2:15, WEBBE)

> "A righteous man respects the life of his animal, but the tender mercies of the wicked are cruel." (Proverbs 12:10, WEBBE)

  • After the Flood, animals are given as food: "Every moving thing that lives will be food for you." (Genesis 9:3)
  • Dominion: humans rule over animals. Stewardship: for many Christians, that rule is held in trust from God, who owns creation.

Mission 3

Welfare or rights?

  • Animal welfare: animals may be used, but suffering must be kept to a minimum. Most UK law takes this view.
  • Peter Singer (b. 1946), Animal Liberation (1975), a preference utilitarian: equal interests deserve equal consideration, whatever the species. Favouring humans just for being human is speciesism (term coined by Richard Ryder, 1970). Singer does not rest his case on rights.
  • Tom Regan (1938–2017), The Case for Animal Rights (1983): every subject-of-a-life (a being with beliefs, desires, memory and a welfare of its own) has inherent value and rights. Rights cannot be traded for benefits: he calls for abolition of animal farming, hunting and experiments, not reform.
  • Andrew Linzey (b. 1952), Christianity and the Rights of Animals (1987): theos-rights. Animals have rights because God, their creator, has rights in them.

Mission 3

Animals as food and intensive farming

  • Intensive (factory) farming: large numbers kept indoors at high density to produce cheap meat, eggs and milk (e.g. broiler chickens, enriched cages for hens, indoor pig units).
  • For: cheap protein for many people; efficient use of land per unit of food; disease controlled indoors.
  • Against: restricted natural behaviour and suffering; heavy antibiotic use; pollution and greenhouse emissions.
  • Reform, not abolition, is the UK pattern: conventional battery cages (EU, 2012) and sow stalls (UK, 1999) are banned.
  • Alternatives: free-range and organic farming; vegetarian and vegan diets; eating less meat.
Mission 3Diversity

Religious and secular views on eating animals

  • Catholic: animals may be used for food and clothing; causing them to suffer or die needlessly is contrary to human dignity (Catechism 2417–2418, paraphrase).
  • Other Christians: Orthodox Christians abstain from meat on fast days; Seventh-day Adventists encourage a vegetarian diet; Linzey argues for vegetarianism as a Christian ideal.
  • Judaism: meat is permitted (kosher, with shechitah, slaughter by one swift cut); tza'ar ba'alei chayim forbids causing animals needless pain.
  • Islam: meat is permitted if halal: the animal is slaughtered with Allah's name pronounced over it; Muslims cite the Prophet's teaching on mercy to animals.
  • Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism: ahimsa (non-harm) leads many to vegetarianism; Theravada monks may accept meat offered to them if the animal was not killed for them.
  • Secular: utilitarians (Singer) argue against factory farming; many humanists support welfare reform and eating less meat.
Mission 3

Blood sports

  • Blood sports: killing or harming animals for sport, e.g. fox hunting with hounds, hare coursing, bullfighting; field sports such as shooting and angling are often included.
  • Law: the Hunting Act 2004 bans hunting wild mammals with dogs in England and Wales (trail hunting is still legal, though the UK government consulted on banning it in 2026); shooting and fishing remain legal.
  • For: pest control; rural jobs and community; tradition; hunted animals live free lives (Roger Scruton, 1944–2020, Animal Rights and Wrongs, 1996).
  • Against: suffering in the chase; killing for pleasure, not need; other pest-control methods exist.
  • Many Christians distinguish hunting for food or control (permitted by dominion) from killing for amusement (needless suffering).
Mission 3

Natural moral law applied

  • Aquinas (Summa Theologiae II-II q.64 a.1): the less perfect exists for the more perfect, so using and killing animals for food is lawful. The primary precepts protect human goods.
  • Animals have no direct rights; but God forbade some cruel practices "so as to be less inclined to be cruel to other men, through being used to be kind to beasts" (ST I-II q.102 a.6, English Dominican translation).
  • Food: eating meat is permitted. Intensive farming can be judged by its real goods: does it serve health and life, or greed?
  • Blood sports: killing for food or pest control fits human ends; killing for pleasure in suffering forms cruelty, a vice that harms human relationships.
  • Criticism: animals matter only indirectly; critics say this misses the wrong done to the animal itself.
Mission 3

Situation ethics and virtue ethics applied

  • Situation ethics (Joseph Fletcher, 1905–1991, Situation Ethics, 1966): agape for persons (personalism). Animals count through their effect on people.
  • So: intensive farming may be loving if it feeds the poor; wrong if it harms human health or the planet people depend on. Blood sports judged by human outcomes (jobs, community, cruelty's effect on character).
  • Critics: personalism leaves animal suffering itself out of the calculation.
  • Virtue ethics (Aristotle, 384–322 BCE): Aristotle held that animals exist for human use (Politics I.8).
  • Modern virtue ethicists ask what the practice shows about character: Rosalind Hursthouse (Ethics, Humans and Other Animals, 2000) argues that factory farming shows callousness and self-indulgence.
  • Blood sports: critics see cruelty and delight in suffering; defenders (Scruton) point to courage, skill and community.

Mission 3Evaluate

Should animals have rights, or is welfare enough?

  • Claim: Animals should have rights, not just welfare protection.
  • For: Sentient animals have interests of their own (Bentham); subjects-of-a-life have inherent value (Regan).
  • For: Welfare law still permits killing and confinement; only rights would stop it. Linzey: God's rights in creatures demand respect.
  • Against: Contract thinkers argue that rights need reason and reciprocity; animals cannot bear duties.
  • Scruton argues this too: rights belong to beings who can also bear duties.
  • Against: Equal consideration of interests can protect animals without rights language (Singer); rights would forbid even life-saving research. Catholic teaching gives humans duties of care, not animal rights.
  • Judgement criteria: What grounds moral status (reason, sentience, being a subject-of-a-life, God's creation)? Must rights be reciprocal? Which approach protects animals better in practice?

Mission 4

Animals in science

  • Animal experimentation: using live animals in research, mainly mice, rats and fish; also used to test medicines for safety before human trials.
  • Purposes: understanding disease; developing medicines and vaccines; safety testing; producing genetically altered animals for research.
  • UK law: the Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act 1986. Every project needs a Home Office licence, granted only after a harm–benefit analysis.
  • The 3Rs (Russell and Burch, 1959): Replace animals where possible (cell cultures, computer models); Reduce the numbers used; Refine procedures to cause less suffering.
  • Testing cosmetics on animals is banned in the UK (since 1998) and across the EU (sale of newly tested products, 2013).
Mission 4

Bentham's act utilitarianism applied

  • Act utilitarianism: judge each act by its consequences. Bentham's hedonic calculus weighs pleasure and pain.
  • Animals count. Bentham (1789): "The question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?"
  • Apply the calculus to one experiment:
  • Intensity, duration of the animals' pain against the extent (how many patients helped) and certainty of the benefit.
  • Fecundity: will the result lead to further cures? Purity: will it bring side effects or later suffering?
  • Result: some experiments pass (a serious disease, a good chance of success); many fail (trivial aims, uncertain results).
  • Bentham himself approved painful experiments with a definite, useful object and a fair prospect of success, but not cruelty without purpose (paraphrase, 1825 letter).
Mission 4Read the source

Mill on the whole sentient creation

John Stuart Mill (1806–1873), Utilitarianism (1863), ch. 2, defines the standard of morality as:

> "the rules and precepts for human conduct, by the observance of which an existence such as has been described might be, to the greatest extent possible, secured to all mankind; and not to them only, but, so far as the nature of things admits, to the whole sentient creation."

Q1 Who must the rules of morality benefit, according to Mill?

Show answer

All humans and, as far as possible, all sentient beings, including animals.

Q2 Which word shows this is a rule approach?

Show answer

"rules and precepts": morality is the set of rules whose general observance gives the greatest happiness.

Mission 4

Mill's rule utilitarianism applied

  • Rule utilitarianism: follow the rules that, if generally kept, maximise happiness.
  • Likely rules: experiment only for serious medical good; use no animal where an alternative works; keep suffering to the minimum (the 3Rs as rules).
  • Rules give predictability and trust: public confidence in research and in animal care.
  • Higher pleasures: Mill ranks the pleasures of the intellect above bodily pleasures: "better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied" (ch. 2). Some infer that human interests may weigh more.
  • Critics (Singer): equal suffering deserves equal weight; Mill's ranking may hide bias by species.
  • Weak rule utilitarians may break a rule when keeping it would clearly cause more suffering; strong rule utilitarians keep it.

Mission 4

Cloning animals

  • Cloning: producing a genetically identical copy of an organism.
  • Dolly the sheep (born 1996, Roslin Institute, Scotland): the first mammal cloned from an adult body cell, by somatic cell nuclear transfer (a body-cell nucleus placed in an egg with its nucleus removed).
  • Uses: research models of disease; animals that produce medicines; conserving endangered species; copying prize livestock; cloning pets.
  • For: medical and conservation benefits; preserves valuable genetic lines.
  • Against: low success rates, so many failed embryos and pregnancies; health problems in some clones; loss of genetic diversity; treating animals as products.
  • Many fear a slippery slope from animal to human reproductive cloning.
Mission 4

Animals as a source of organs

  • Xenotransplantation: transplanting animal cells, tissues or organs into humans. Pigs are used because their organs are similar in size.
  • Pig heart valves have been used in surgery for decades. Gene-edited pig hearts (2022) and kidneys (2024) have been transplanted into living patients in the USA as experimental treatments.
  • For: organ shortage; people die waiting for transplants; animals bred for the purpose.
  • Against: rejection; risk of passing animal viruses to humans; animals bred and killed as spare parts; changing animals' genes.
  • Religious views: many Jewish and Muslim authorities permit pig organs to save a life (pikuach nefesh, saving life, in Judaism; necessity in Islamic law). The Pontifical Academy for Life (2001) judged xenotransplantation acceptable in principle, with strict safeguards.
Mission 4

Natural moral law applied

  • Aquinas: animals exist for human good (ST II-II q.64 a.1). The primary precept to preserve (human) life supports research and transplants that save lives.
  • Catholic teaching: medical experiments on animals are acceptable "within reasonable limits" if they help to care for or save human lives (Catechism 2417, short phrase).
  • Cloning animals: the precept about reproduction concerns humans; animal cloning is judged by its real goods and its suffering. Some natural lawyers worry it treats God's creatures as products.
  • Xenotransplantation: serves life; but the real vs apparent good test asks whether risks (new viruses) outweigh the hoped-for good.
  • Cruelty without purpose remains wrong: it trains people in cruelty (ST I-II q.102 a.6).
Mission 4

Situation ethics and virtue ethics applied

  • Situation ethics (Fletcher): personalism puts people first; a life-saving experiment or pig transplant can be the most loving act for persons in the situation.
  • Pragmatism asks: will it work? Trivial or cosmetic testing fails the test of love.
  • Critics: animals' suffering hardly enters Fletcher's calculation.
  • Virtue ethics: what would a person of practical wisdom (phronesis) and compassion do?
  • Such a person might accept necessary research but refuse callous or careless use; virtue lies in a mean between indifference to animals and sentimentality that ignores human need.
  • Aristotle himself saw animals as for human use; modern virtue ethicists (Hursthouse) stress compassion and argue for strict limits.

Mission 4Evaluate

Can utilitarianism justify animal experiments?

  • Claim: Utilitarianism provides a sound basis for deciding about animal experiments.
  • For: Counts animal suffering directly; distinguishes serious research from trivial testing; matches the law's harm–benefit test.
  • For: Usable by religious believers and non-believers alike; rule forms (the 3Rs) give clear limits.
  • Against: Benefits are hard to predict; animal pain is hard to measure against human health.
  • Against: It could justify great suffering for large benefits, or, applied consistently, experiments on humans with similar capacities (Singer's challenge). Rights theorists (Regan) say no total justifies using a subject-of-a-life.
  • Judgement criteria: Is pleasure (or freedom from pain) the only intrinsic good? Does utilitarianism give justice to the weak? Does it work in contemporary society?
Mission 4Worked answer

Act and rule compared

Task: "Rule utilitarianism gives a better basis than act utilitarianism for decisions about animal experiments." Evaluate.

  • Line of argument: rule utilitarianism is better here, because research needs consistent standards.
  • Support: Bentham's case-by-case calculus is slow and open to bias by researchers; Mill's rules (the 3Rs) protect animals predictably and keep public trust.
  • Counter: a rule may forbid a single experiment that would save many lives; act utilitarianism allows for this.
  • Critique of the counter: a weak rule utilitarian can break a rule in a clear emergency, so most of this flexibility is kept.
  • Judgement: decided by your own criteria (e.g. predictability vs best outcome in each case), stated and defended.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Natural law forbids directly ending innocent life but, through double effect and proportionate means, allows strong pain relief and refusal of burdensome treatment.
  • Situation ethics (Fletcher) can support voluntary euthanasia out of love; Rachels and Glover reject the moral weight of acts versus omissions; slippery-slope and trust arguments concern the law, not only the single case.

Mission 2

  • Five purposes: retribution, deterrence, protection, rehabilitation, vindication; responsibility (and diminished responsibility) shapes what is deserved.
  • Capital punishment: Aquinas allowed it for the common good; Catholic teaching now calls it inadmissible; SE and VE ask about love, justice and mercy; Bentham and Mill disagreed.

Mission 3

  • Views on animals depend on moral status: none (Descartes), indirect (Aquinas, Kant), sentience (Bentham, Singer), rights as subjects-of-a-life (Regan) or theos-rights (Linzey).
  • Applied to food, intensive farming and blood sports: natural law permits use but warns against cruelty; situation ethics puts persons first; virtue ethics asks what a practice shows about character.

Mission 4

  • Experiments are regulated by harm–benefit analysis and the 3Rs: replace, reduce, refine.
  • Act utilitarianism (Bentham) weighs each experiment; rule utilitarianism (Mill) sets rules for "the whole sentient creation"; Singer challenges bias by species.
  • Cloning (Dolly, 1996) and xenotransplantation: natural law supports life-saving uses but forbids pointless cruelty; situation ethics puts persons first; virtue ethics asks for practical wisdom and compassion.
