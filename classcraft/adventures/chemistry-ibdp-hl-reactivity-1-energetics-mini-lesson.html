Mission 1

System and surroundings

  • The system is the reacting chemicals: reactants and products.
  • The surroundings are everything else: water, container, thermometer, air.
  • Energy moves between them, but the total is conserved.
  • A thermometer in a solution reads the surroundings, not the chemicals.
Mission 1

Heat is not temperature

  • Heat: energy transferred because of a temperature difference, in joules.
  • Temperature: in kelvin, proportional to the average kinetic energy of the particles.
  • A bath and a cup of water at 40 °C have the same temperature.
  • The bath can transfer far more heat as it cools: it has many more particles.
Quick check
?Which statement correctly describes the temperature of a substance?
Mission 1

Exothermic and endothermic

  • Exothermic: energy passes from system to surroundings. Surroundings warm. ΔH is negative.
  • Endothermic: energy passes from surroundings to system. Surroundings cool. ΔH is positive.
  • ΔH describes the system. Always write its sign: −184 or +178 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Exothermic: combustion, neutralization. Endothermic: thermal decomposition of CaCO₃, photosynthesis.
Quick check
?A mixture of iron and sulfur must be heated strongly before it reacts. Once started, it glows red-hot with no further heating. What does this show?
Mission 1

Energy profiles

  • Axes: potential energy (y) against reaction coordinate (x). Not time.
  • Activation energy, Ea: from the reactant level up to the top of the barrier.
  • ΔH: from the reactant level to the product level.
  • Products lower → exothermic. Products higher → endothermic.
  • Both kinds of reaction have an activation-energy barrier.
Mission 1

Stability decides the direction

  • Lower potential energy means more stable.
  • Exothermic: the products are more stable than the reactants.
  • Endothermic: the products are less stable; the system must take in energy.

Mission 2

The standard enthalpy change, ΔH⦵

  • ΔH: the heat exchanged with the surroundings when the pressure is kept constant.
  • ΔH⦵: measured at standard pressure, 100 kPa, with every substance in its standard state. Usually 298 K.
  • Units: kJ mol⁻¹, for the amounts in the equation.
  • States matter: forming H₂O(l) releases more energy than forming H₂O(g).
Quick check
?Which statement about the standard enthalpy change of a reaction, ΔH⦵, is correct?
Mission 2

Q = mcΔT

  • Q = mcΔT (data booklet). Q is in J.
  • m: mass of the water or solution whose temperature changes, in g. Not the reactant's mass.
  • c: specific heat capacity of water, 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ (data booklet).
  • ΔT: the temperature change. Same in K and °C; never add 273.
  • For dilute solutions, take density 1.00 g cm⁻³ and c of water.
Mission 2

ΔH = −Q/n

  • ΔH = −Q/n. n is the amount of the limiting reactant, in mol.
  • Divide Q by 1000 to get kJ.
  • The minus sign: the surroundings gain Q, so the system loses it.
  • A temperature rise gives a negative ΔH; a fall gives a positive ΔH.
Mission 2

Finding the true ΔT

  • Heat is exchanged with the air while the reaction is still going.
  • So the highest (or lowest) reading falls short of the change with no heat exchange.
  • Plot temperature against time. Extrapolate the line after the highest (or lowest) reading back to the time of mixing.
  • ΔT = extrapolated value − starting temperature.
Quick check
?Why is the line after the highest reading extrapolated back to the time of mixing?
Mission 2

Worked example

  • Excess zinc powder is added to 50.0 cm³ of 0.200 mol dm⁻³ CuSO₄(aq): Zn(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + Cu(s).
  • From the graph: ΔT = 31.00 − 21.40 = 9.60 K.
  • Q = 50.0 × 4.18 × 9.60 = 2006 J = 2.006 kJ.
  • n(CuSO₄) = 0.0500 dm³ × 0.200 mol dm⁻³ = 0.0100 mol. Zinc is in excess, so CuSO₄ is limiting.
  • ΔH = −2.006 ÷ 0.0100 = −201 kJ mol⁻¹.
Calculate
11.00 g of iron powder is added to 50.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ CuSO₄(aq), an excess, in a polystyrene cup. Fe(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → FeSO₄(aq) + Cu(s). The corrected temperature rise is 12.5 K. Calculate ΔH, in kJ mol⁻¹. (Ar Fe = 55.85; c = 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹; density of solution 1.00 g cm⁻³.)
Mission 2

Why measured values fall short

  • Heat is lost to the air and apparatus, so measured ΔH is less exothermic than the accepted value.
  • Burning a fuel under a can loses most: much of the heat warms the air and the can.
  • Fuels may also burn incompletely, and conditions are not standard.
  • Reduce losses with a lid, insulation, and the burner close to the can.
Mission check

?A spirit burner containing ethanol, C₂H₅OH, is used to heat 150.0 g of water in a copper can. Burning 0.920 g of ethanol raises the temperature of the water from 19.0 °C to 40.5 °C. Use c(water) = 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ and Ar values H 1.01, C 12.01, O 16.00. What value of ΔH for the combustion of ethanol do these results give?

The accepted value is −1367 kJ mol⁻¹ (data booklet). The missing half was not destroyed: it heated the air and the can, not the water. Some ethanol may also have burned incompletely.

Mission 3: If breaking bonds takes in energy, how can burning a fuel give energy out?

Make a guess: If breaking bonds takes in energy, how can burning a fuel give energy out?

Mission 3

Breaking takes in, forming gives out

  • Breaking a bond is endothermic: energy is absorbed.
  • Forming the same bond releases exactly the same energy: exothermic.
  • A reaction is exothermic when the bonds formed are stronger in total than those broken.
  • Bonds never release energy as they break.
Mission 3

Bond enthalpy

  • Bond enthalpy: energy needed to break 1 mol of a bond, with all species gaseous.
  • Always positive. Average values are in the data booklet.
  • ΔH = Σ(bonds broken) − Σ(bonds formed).
  • Draw the structures first, and count every bond broken and formed.
Mission 3

Worked example

  • H₂(g) + Cl₂(g) → 2HCl(g).
  • Data booklet: H–H 436, Cl–Cl 242, H–Cl 431 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Broken: 436 + 242 = 678 kJ. Formed: 2 × 431 = 862 kJ.
  • ΔH = 678 − 862 = −184 kJ mol⁻¹.
Mission 3

Why it is only an estimate

  • Bond enthalpies are averages over many compounds; a real bond depends on its molecule.
  • They apply only to gases: energy for changes of state is left out.
  • So a value from bond enthalpies can differ from an experimental one.
Quick check
?Why is the C–H bond enthalpy in a table of bond enthalpies described as an average value?
Calculate

Make a guess: Carbon always burns partly to CO₂. So how do we know ΔH for making carbon monoxide?

Mission 4

Hess's law

  • Hess's law: ΔH is the same whatever route links the same initial and final states.
  • It follows from conservation of energy.
  • So ΔH for a reaction that is hard to measure = the sum of ΔH along another route.
Mission 4

Three moves

  • Reverse an equation: change the sign of ΔH.
  • Multiply an equation: multiply ΔH by the same number.
  • Add equations: add their ΔH values. Species on both sides cancel.
  • Arrange the given equations so they add up to the target equation.
Mission 4

Worked example

  • Target: C(s) + ½O₂(g) → CO(g).
  • (1) C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g), ΔH = −393.5 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • (2) CO(g) + ½O₂(g) → CO₂(g), ΔH = −283.0 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Keep (1). Reverse (2): CO₂(g) → CO(g) + ½O₂(g), ΔH = +283.0.
  • Add: CO₂ and ½O₂ cancel. ΔH = −393.5 + 283.0 = −110.5 kJ mol⁻¹.
Quick check
?An exothermic reaction P → Q takes place in a single step. With a catalyst, the same reaction takes place by a different route of three steps with a lower activation energy, and is much faster. Both routes start from the same reactants and give the same products under the same conditions. How does ΔH of the catalysed reaction compare with ΔH of the uncatalysed reaction?
Calculate
1Nitrogen monoxide from car exhausts is oxidized in the air: 2NO(g) + O₂(g) → 2NO₂(g). Calculate ΔH for this reaction, in kJ mol⁻¹, from: (1) ½N₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → NO(g), ΔH = +90.3 kJ mol⁻¹ (2) ½N₂(g) + O₂(g) → NO₂(g), ΔH = +33.2 kJ mol⁻¹
Mission check

?Use reactions (1) and (2) from the worked example to find ΔH for 2C(s) + O₂(g) → 2CO(g).

ΔH depends only on start and finish (Hess's law). The two reactions that make CO₂ can be measured, so they give ΔH for making CO, which cannot be measured directly.

Mission 5

Combustion: reaction with oxygen

  • Combustion: an exothermic reaction with oxygen when a substance is heated in it.
  • Reactive metals form ionic oxides: 2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s).
  • Non-metals form covalent oxides: S(s) + O₂(g) → SO₂(g); C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g).
  • Write oxygen as O₂. Work out ionic oxide formulas from the ion charges: Al₂O₃.
Quick check
?Which equation correctly represents the combustion of an element in excess oxygen?
Mission 5

Burning hydrocarbons and alcohols completely

  • Complete combustion, in plenty of O₂: all C → CO₂, all H → H₂O.
  • Balance C, then H, then O last.
  • Propane: C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O.
  • Alcohols already contain an O atom: subtract it before finding the O₂.
  • Butan-1-ol: C₄H₉OH + 6O₂ → 4CO₂ + 5H₂O (13 O needed, 1 from the alcohol).
Quick check
?Ethanol, C₂H₅OH, undergoes complete combustion in oxygen. What amount of oxygen, O₂, reacts with 1.00 mol of ethanol?
Quick check
?What amount of oxygen, O₂, is needed to burn 0.500 mol of propan-1-ol, C₃H₇OH, completely?
Mission 5

Incomplete combustion

  • In limited O₂, some carbon forms carbon monoxide, CO, or carbon (soot), C.
  • Hydrogen still forms H₂O.
  • To CO: 2C₃H₈ + 7O₂ → 6CO + 8H₂O. To C: C₃H₈ + 2O₂ → 3C + 4H₂O.
  • Less energy is released than in complete combustion.
  • "Incomplete" means different products, not fuel left over.
Mission 5

Why CO and soot are harmful

  • Carbon monoxide is colourless, odourless and toxic.
  • It binds to haemoglobin more strongly than oxygen does, so blood carries less O₂.
  • Soot particles damage the lungs and blacken surfaces; they give a smoky yellow flame.

Mission 6

Fossil fuels

  • Fossil fuels: coal, crude oil and natural gas. They formed from the remains of organisms over millions of years.
  • Non-renewable: we use them far faster than they form.
  • Burning them releases carbon locked away long ago, adding CO₂ to the atmosphere.
Mission 6

Energy per gram, CO₂ per kilojoule

  • Specific energy: energy released per gram = energy per mol ÷ molar mass.
  • Propane releases 2219 kJ mol⁻¹ (data booklet); M = 44.11 g mol⁻¹ → 50.3 kJ g⁻¹.
  • C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O: 3 × 44.01 = 132.03 g CO₂ per 2219 kJ.
  • That is 59.5 g of CO₂ per 1000 kJ.
  • More hydrogen per carbon: more energy comes from forming water, so less CO₂ per kJ.
Calculate
aHexane, C₆H₁₄, releases 4163 kJ when 1 mol burns completely (data booklet). Calculate its specific energy, in kJ g⁻¹. (Ar: H 1.01, C 12.01, O 16.00)
bWhen 1 mol burns completely, methanol, CH₃OH, releases 726 kJ and ethanol, C₂H₅OH, releases 1367 kJ (data booklet). Work out the mass of CO₂ released per kJ for each. Which fuel releases less CO₂ per kJ of heat?
Mission 6

The greenhouse effect

  • Sunlight, mostly visible, passes through the atmosphere and warms the Earth's surface.
  • The warm surface emits infrared radiation.
  • CO₂, H₂O and CH₄ molecules absorb infrared, then re-emit it in all directions, partly back down.
  • More CO₂ → more infrared absorbed → the enhanced greenhouse effect: a warmer surface.
Quick check

Mission 6

Biofuels

  • Renewable: replenished naturally at least as fast as it is used.
  • Biofuels come from recent biomass; plants fixed the carbon by photosynthesis within months or years.
  • Photosynthesis: 6CO₂(g) + 6H₂O(l) → C₆H₁₂O₆(aq) + 6O₂(g), using light energy.
  • Examples: bioethanol from fermented sugars, biodiesel from plant oils, biogas from waste.
Quick check
?Plants such as sugar cane, grown to make bioethanol, carry out photosynthesis. Which statement gives the reactants and products of photosynthesis?
Mission 6

Biofuels: for and against

  • For: renewable, and the CO₂ released was recently taken from the air.
  • For: can be made locally, some from waste.
  • Against: not carbon neutral. Farming, fertilizer, transport and processing burn fossil fuels.
  • Against: crops compete with food for land and water; clearing land releases stored carbon.
  • Against: often a lower energy per gram than the fossil fuel replaced.

Mission 7

What a fuel cell does

  • A fuel cell turns a fuel's chemical energy directly into electrical energy.
  • Fuel and oxygen are fed in continuously, to separate electrodes. There is no flame.
  • Anode (negative): the fuel is oxidized, losing electrons.
  • Cathode (positive): oxygen is reduced, gaining electrons.
  • Electrons flow through the external circuit from anode to cathode.
Mission 7

The hydrogen fuel cell, acidic electrolyte

  • Anode: H₂ → 2H⁺ + 2e⁻.
  • Cathode: O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O.
  • H⁺ ions move through the electrolyte to the cathode.
  • Double the anode equation and add: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. The only product is water.
Quick check
?In a hydrogen fuel cell with an acidic electrolyte, hydrogen gas is supplied to the anode. Which half-equation represents the reaction at the anode?
Quick check

Mission 7

Half-equations for carbon fuels

  • The methanol fuel cell: overall CH₃OH + 1½O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. It releases CO₂.
  • Its cathode reaction is the same as in the hydrogen cell.
  • At the anode, the fuel → CO₂. Balance: C atoms; O with H₂O; H with H⁺; charge with e⁻.
  • Worked, ethanol: C₂H₅OH + 3H₂O → 2CO₂ + 12H⁺ + 12e⁻.
  • Check: 4 O, 12 H and zero charge on each side.
Quick check

Recap

The big ideas

  • Energy moves between system and surroundings; the total is conserved. Heat is not temperature.
  • Exothermic: surroundings warm, ΔH negative. Endothermic: surroundings cool, ΔH positive.
  • Energy profile: potential energy against reaction coordinate. ΔH = products − reactants; Ea is the barrier.
  • Q = mcΔT, with m the mass of water or solution; ΔH = −Q/n, with n for the limiting reactant.
  • ΔH = Σ(broken) − Σ(formed) gives an estimate. Hess's law: reverse → change the sign; multiply; add.
  • Complete combustion gives CO₂ + H₂O; limited O₂ gives CO or C. Compare fuels by kJ per gram and CO₂ per kJ.
  • Biofuels are renewable but not carbon neutral. In a fuel cell, the fuel is oxidized at the anode.
