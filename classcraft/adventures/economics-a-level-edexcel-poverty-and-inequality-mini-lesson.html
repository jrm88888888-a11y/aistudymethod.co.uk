Mission 1

Two meanings of poverty

  • Absolute poverty: income too low to pay for basic needs: food, clean water, shelter, clothing.
    • Measured against a line that is fixed in real terms (it rises only with prices).
  • Relative poverty: income far below the typical income of the same society.
    • Measured against a line that moves with the median income, e.g. 60% of median household income.
  • Median: the middle income when all households are lined up from lowest to highest.
  • Absolute poverty asks: can you survive? Relative poverty asks: can you take part in normal life where you live?
  • Say: "relative poverty is low income compared with the median" — not: "a milder form of absolute poverty".
Mission 1

Worked example: the line moves

  • Illustrative data. Nine households, weekly income:
    • 150 · 220 · 280 · 340 · 400 · 460 · 520 · 650 · 900
  • Median = 400. Relative line = 0.6 × 400 = 240.
  • Below 240: two households (150, 220). Relative poverty rate = 2/9 = 22%.
  • Ten years later, in real terms: the bottom three gain 2%; everyone else gains 20%.
    • New median = 400 × 1.2 = 480. New line = 0.6 × 480 = 288.
    • Bottom three now: 153 · 224.4 · 285.6. All below 288.
    • Relative poverty rate = 3/9 = 33%.
  • Absolute line fixed at 200 (real terms): one household below it before (150), one after (153).
  • Everyone got richer. Relative poverty rose; absolute poverty did not.
Quick check
?Over 20 years, the real income of every household in a country doubles, including the median household's. The poverty lines are 60% of the median (relative) and a fixed basket of basic needs (absolute). What happens?
Mission 1

Poverty lines in practice

  • International poverty line (World Bank): $3.00 a day per person, at 2021 prices, adjusted for purchasing power parity (PPP).
    • PPP: adjusts for what money buys in each country, so $3.00 means the same basket everywhere.
    • It is an absolute line. It replaced $2.15 a day (2017 prices) in June 2025.
    • Higher lines for richer countries: $4.20 and $8.30 a day.
  • UK: relative low income = below 60% of median household income, before or after housing costs (BHC/AHC).
  • The UK also uses an "absolute low income" line: 60% of the 2010/11 median, held fixed in real terms.
Mission 1

Case: the numbers

  • World, below the international line:
    • 1990: around 2.3 billion people.
    • 2025: around 831 million: about 1 in 10 people.
  • UK, 2023/24, median income £650 a week (BHC). Line: 0.6 × 650 = £390 a week.
    • Relative low income: 17% of people BHC; 21% AHC.
    • Children: 31% AHC. Pensioners: 16% AHC.
  • These figures are for a couple with no children. So the UK relative line is about £28 a day each (390 ÷ 7 ÷ 2): far above the World Bank's $3.00 a day.
  • One country can cut absolute poverty fast while relative poverty barely moves.
Mission 1

Minimum income standards

  • A minimum income standard (MIS) asks: what income does a household need for an acceptable standard of living?
  • Built from a basket of goods and services: food, housing, clothing, transport, a phone, a modest social life.
  • UK MIS (Joseph Rowntree Foundation, Loughborough University): members of the public agree the basket in group discussions.
  • UK 2025: a single person needs to earn about £30,500 a year; a couple with two children about £74,000 between them.
  • Strength: based on real costs and needs, not just a % of the median.
  • Weakness: what counts as "acceptable" is a judgement, and baskets differ by family type and place.
Mission 1

The Multidimensional Poverty Index

  • Income is not the only thing that matters. The MPI (UNDP and Oxford's OPHI) counts deprivations.
  • 3 dimensions, 10 indicators:
    • Health (1/3): nutrition, child mortality. Each 1/6.
    • Education (1/3): years of schooling, school attendance. Each 1/6.
    • Living standards (1/3): cooking fuel, sanitation, drinking water, electricity, housing, assets. Each 1/18.
  • A person is multidimensionally poor if their weighted deprivations add up to 1/3 or more.
  • MPI = H × A
    • H = share of people who are poor (headcount ratio).
    • A = average share of weighted deprivations among the poor (intensity).
  • Illustrative data: H = 0.30, A = 0.45 → MPI = 0.30 × 0.45 = 0.135.
  • 2025 Global MPI: 1.1 billion of 6.3 billion people in 109 countries are multidimensionally poor.
Quick check
?A household is deprived in five MPI indicators: cooking fuel, sanitation, drinking water, housing and assets. It has no health or education deprivations. Using the MPI weights, is it multidimensionally poor?
Mission 1

Why poverty is hard to measure

  • Where to draw the line: 60% or 50% of the median? $3.00 or $4.20? Each choice changes the count.
  • Surveys miss people: homeless people, refugees, people in conflict zones.
  • Households, not individuals: income may not be shared fairly within a household.
  • Housing costs: two families with the same income can have very different amounts left (BHC vs AHC).
  • Income vs consumption: poor countries often measure spending; incomes can swing month to month.
  • PPP and prices: estimates change when price data are updated (the $2.15 → $3.00 change).
  • MPI weights are a judgement: is sanitation worth less than schooling?
  • Snapshots: a one-year count does not show who is poor for many years.
Mission 1

Causes of poverty

  • Individuals and households:
    • unemployment, low pay, part-time or insecure work
    • low human capital: few skills or qualifications
    • sickness, disability, caring duties, old age
    • large families or one earner (e.g. lone parents)
    • discrimination (gender, ethnicity, disability) and unequal opportunity
    • owning little wealth or property: no income from rent, interest or profit
  • Economy and government:
    • recession, rising prices of essentials (food, energy, rents)
    • changes to taxes and benefits
    • conflict, weak institutions, climate shocks (low-income countries)
Mission 1

What changes the poverty count?

  • Absolute poverty falls when the real incomes of the poor rise.
  • Relative poverty falls only when the poor's incomes rise faster than the median.
  • So a recession can cut relative poverty: the median falls while benefits and pensions hold steady.
  • And fast growth can raise relative poverty, if gains go mostly to middle and high earners.
  • Recent UK causes of change: benefit uprating, housing costs, energy and food prices, employment.
  • Say: "relative poverty fell because the median fell" — not: "the poor became better off".
Mission 1

Effects of poverty and inequality

  • Individuals: worse health and shorter lives, poorer diet, stress, lower school results, debt.
  • Firms: lower demand for most goods; workers with less training and lower productivity.
  • Economy:
    • low human capital → lower productivity and growth
    • higher spending on benefits and health care; less tax paid
  • Counter-argument: some inequality gives incentives to work, train and take risks.
Mission 1Evaluate

Can growth alone end poverty?

  • Growth helps: much of the fall in world absolute poverty since 1990 came in fast-growing economies such as China and India.
  • It depends on:
    • the measure: growth cuts absolute poverty more reliably than relative poverty
    • who gains: "inclusive" growth raises low incomes; growth in a few high-pay sectors may not
    • prices of essentials: rising rents or food prices can absorb income gains
    • public services: health, schooling and clean water reach people that higher average incomes may not
  • Policy limits: governments with deficits and debt have less room for transfers; external shocks (energy prices, global recession) can raise poverty whatever national policy does.
Quick check
?During a recession, median household income falls by 5% in real terms. State pensions are held constant in real terms, and most pensioners' income comes from the state pension. What is most likely to happen to measured relative poverty among pensioners?
Mission check

?Over ten years, the real incomes of the poorest fifth of a country's households rise by 15%. Median real household income rises by 40%. Which outcome is most likely?

Absolute poverty tracks the real incomes of the poor. Relative poverty tracks the gap between the poor and the median, so it can rise while almost everyone gets richer.

Mission 2

Growth is not the same as development

  • Economic growth: an increase in real GDP (output) over time.
  • Economic development: an improvement in people's well-being and living standards. It is multidimensional:
    • higher incomes and less poverty
    • better health and longer lives
    • more education and skills
    • more freedom and choice (Amartya Sen: development as expanding what people are able to do and be)
  • Growth can happen without much development: the gains may go to a few, or come with pollution.
  • Development can happen with slow growth: cheap public health and schooling can lengthen and improve lives.
  • Say: "growth is a rise in real output; development is a rise in well-being" — not: "development means growth".
Mission 2

Monetary indicators

  • GDP per capita: output per person. GNI per capita adds net income from abroad.
  • Compare countries at purchasing power parity (PPP): it corrects for differences in price levels (prices tend to be lower in poorer countries).
Mission 2

Non-monetary indicators

  • Health: life expectancy at birth, infant mortality rate, maternal mortality.
  • Education: adult literacy rate, years of schooling.
  • Inequality: Gini coefficient, income share of the richest 10%.
  • One indicator alone gives only part of the picture.
Quick check
?A government wants one indicator to track whether its new clinics are reducing deaths among babies. Which is best?
Mission 2

The Human Development Index (HDI)

  • Published by the UN Development Programme (UNDP) since 1990.
  • Three dimensions, four indicators:
    • a long and healthy life: life expectancy at birth
    • knowledge (education): expected years of schooling for children; mean years of schooling for adults
    • a decent standard of living: GNI per capita at PPP
  • Each dimension is scored from 0 to 1, then combined. The HDI runs from 0 to 1.
  • UNDP groups: very high (0.800 and above), high (0.700–0.799), medium (0.550–0.699), low (below 0.550).
  • Its three dimensions: health, education, living standards.
  • Say: "HDI measures health, education and income" — not: "HDI measures income".
Mission 2

Worked example: same income, different HDI

  • Illustrative data. Countries P and Q both have GNI per capita of 10,000 international dollars (PPP).
Life expectancyExpected schoolingMean schooling
P72 years13.5 years9 years
Q59 years12 years6 years
  • Each dimension is scaled between a minimum and a maximum (life expectancy: 20 to 85 years):
    • Health: P (72 − 20) ÷ 65 = 0.800; Q (59 − 20) ÷ 65 = 0.600
    • Education: P 0.675; Q 0.533
    • Income: both 0.696
  • HDI: P = 0.722 (high); Q = 0.606 (medium).
  • Same income, but P's people live longer and learn more.
  • Two design features:
    • Income enters as a logarithm: doubling P's income to 20,000 raises HDI only to 0.756. An extra dollar matters more to the poor.
    • The three scores are combined by a geometric mean, so a very low score in one dimension pulls the HDI down more than a simple average would.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. Country S has a GNI per capita at PPP far higher than Country T's, but T has the higher HDI. What best explains this?
Mission 2

Adjusting HDI: inequality, gender and the planet

  • Inequality-adjusted HDI (IHDI): HDI discounted for inequality in each dimension.
    • With perfect equality, IHDI = HDI. The bigger the gap between them, the more unequal the country.
  • Gender Inequality Index (GII): from 0 (equality) to 1. The higher the GII, the worse the inequality. Three dimensions:
    • reproductive health: maternal mortality, births to teenage mothers
    • empowerment: women's share of seats in parliament; secondary education by gender
    • labour market: labour force participation by gender
Mission 2

Poverty and welfare: MPI and MEW

  • Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI), UNDP and Oxford University, since 2010.
  • Ten indicators in the same three dimensions as HDI:
    • health: nutrition, child mortality
    • education: years of schooling, school attendance
    • living standards: cooking fuel, sanitation, drinking water, electricity, housing, assets
  • A household is multidimensionally poor if it is deprived in at least one third of the weighted indicators.
  • It shows who is poor and in what way, not just average income.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. Countries V and W have the same HDI of 0.75. V's IHDI is 0.70; W's IHDI is 0.55. What can you conclude?
Mission 2

Why development is hard to measure

  • Data quality: large informal and subsistence sectors; surveys are costly and infrequent.
  • Averages hide distribution: a rising mean can leave the poor behind.
  • Value judgements: which dimensions count, and how to weight them?
  • Missing dimensions: freedom, security, culture and the environment are hard to put in numbers.
  • PPP estimates depend on price surveys that may be out of date.
Mission 2

How growth and development are linked

  • Growth can fund development:
    • higher incomes for families
    • more tax revenue for schools, clinics, water and roads
  • Development can fuel growth: healthier, better-educated workers are more productive.
  • But growth may not bring development if:
    • the gains go to a few (rising inequality)
    • growth comes from exporting a resource that employs few people
    • it damages health and the environment
  • So the link is likely but not automatic: it depends on how the gains are shared and spent.
Mission 2Evaluate

Is HDI a better measure of development than GNI per capita?

It depends on:

  • The purpose: HDI captures health and education; GNI per capita at PPP better shows material living standards.
  • Distribution: both are averages.
  • The IHDI adjusts HDI for inequality.
  • What is left out: HDI has no measure of inequality, freedom, the environment or happiness.
  • Weights: HDI weights its three dimensions equally. That is a judgement, not a fact.
  • So: use HDI as a better starting point than income alone, alongside other indicators.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. Over 20 years, Country X's GNI per capita at PPP doubled, mainly from oil exports owned by a small group. Life expectancy and years of schooling barely changed. Which conclusion is best supported?

Income is one part of development, so the same money can buy very different lives.

Mission 3

The poverty cycle

  • A poverty cycle (poverty trap): low income causes the things that keep income low.
  • Low income → low saving → low investment → little capital per worker → low productivity → low income.
  • A second loop: low income → poor health and education → low human capital → low productivity → low income.
  • Each arrow is a link. Break one link, and the cycle can turn into a growth path.
Quick check
?In a poverty cycle, low incomes lead to low saving. Why does low saving keep incomes low in the future?
Mission 3

The savings gap: Harrod-Domar

  • Harrod-Domar model: growth depends on saving and on how productive new capital is.
  • Growth rate = savings ratio ÷ capital-output ratio.
  • Capital-output ratio: units of capital needed for 1 more unit of output a year.
  • Worked example (illustrative data): savings ratio 12%, capital-output ratio 4.
  • Growth = 12 ÷ 4 = 3% a year.
  • Target growth 6%: savings needed = 6 × 4 = 24% of income.
  • Savings gap = 24% − 12% = 12% of income. Aid, FDI or borrowing abroad may fill it.
  • With population growth of 3%, growth of 3% leaves income per head flat.
  • Income from oil, copper or cocoa raises growth only if it is saved and invested productively at home.
  • Limits: saving may not become productive investment; the model ignores technology, human capital and institutions; the capital-output ratio is not fixed.
Quick check
?A country saves 20% of its income. Its capital-output ratio is 5. According to the Harrod-Domar model, what is its growth rate?
Mission 3

Dependence on primary products

  • Primary products: crops, minerals, fuels, sold mostly unprocessed.
  • Commodity-dependent: commodities make up over 60% of a country's merchandise exports.
  • In 2019–2021, 95 of 142 developing countries were commodity-dependent (UNCTAD).
  • Problems:
  • Volatile prices → volatile export earnings, tax revenue and incomes.
  • Low income elasticity of demand for many foods: as world incomes rise, demand grows slowly.
  • Few links to the rest of the economy: little processing, few skilled jobs.
Mission 3Graph lab

Why commodity prices swing

A crop market starts at Price 100, Quantity 100 (illustrative data). A world recession shifts demand left by 10 at every price.

  • Takeaway: when supply and demand are both price inelastic, small shifts cause large price swings.
Mission 3

Volatility in practice

  • Supply of crops and minerals is price inelastic in the short run: trees take years to grow; mines take years to open.
  • Demand is often price inelastic too.
  • So shifts in supply (weather, new mines) or demand (world recession) cause big price changes.
  • Real example: Brent crude oil averaged $111.80 a barrel in June 2014 and $30.70 in January 2016: a fall of about 73% (EIA, via FRED).
  • Oil exporters' export earnings and tax revenue fell sharply.
  • Harder to plan: investment projects are started in boom years and cut in slumps.
Quick check
?In a country, copper makes up 70% of export revenue (illustrative data). The world copper price falls by 40%. The volume of copper exported, and all other exports, stay the same. By how much does total export revenue fall?
Mission 3

Money that leaks away

  • Foreign currency gap: export earnings are too small to pay for the imports that development needs (machinery, fuel, medicines).
  • Illustrative data: needed imports 18 billion; export earnings 14 billion; gap 4 billion.
  • Capital flight: savers and firms move money abroad, legally or illegally. Causes: fear of devaluation, instability, weak property rights, corruption.
  • Capital flight shrinks the funds left for domestic investment.
  • Debt: past borrowing in foreign currency must be repaid in foreign currency.
  • Illustrative data: debt service 3 billion a year; export earnings 12 billion. Debt service ratio = 3 ÷ 12 = 25% of export earnings.
  • Money spent on interest cannot pay for schools or roads. A depreciation raises the debt burden in local currency.
  • Debt relief is in a later lesson.
Mission 3

People and places

  • Human capital: the skills, knowledge and health of workers. Poor schooling and ill health cut productivity.
  • Infrastructure: roads, ports, power, water, telecoms. Without them, firms face high costs and cannot reach markets.
  • Geography: a landlocked country pays more to move goods to a port. Climate and natural disasters matter too.
  • Population growth: fast growth means GDP must rise faster just to keep income per head constant. A high share of children raises the dependency ratio.
Mission 3

Institutions and corruption

  • Institutions: the rules of the game: courts, laws, tax systems, banks.
  • Property rights: secure, legal ownership. Without them, people invest less, and land cannot be used as collateral for a loan.
  • Weak governance: unstable or ineffective government deters investment.
  • Corruption: bribes raise costs, divert public money (including income from oil or mining) and reward connections over productivity.
  • Weak credit and banking systems: savers have nowhere safe to save; firms cannot borrow.
Quick check
?In one region, families have farmed the same land for generations but have no legal title to it. Which effect on development is most likely?
Mission 3Evaluate

Which barrier matters most?

It depends on:

  • The binding constraint: a new port does little if the roads to it are unsafe. The barrier that binds first matters most.
  • Interactions: corruption worsens capital flight and debt; ill health weakens education.
  • The country: a landlocked mineral exporter faces different barriers from a coastal farming economy.
  • Time scale: education and health raise growth only over decades.
  • Evidence: some resource-rich countries grew fast, e.g. Botswana with diamonds, often linked to strong institutions. Resource wealth does not have to hold a country back.
Mission check

?A government gives farmers in a remote region free high-yield seed. Harvests rise by 40%. A year later, farm incomes have barely risen. Which is the most likely explanation?

Removing one barrier may not help if another, such as poor infrastructure, still binds.

Mission 4

Why crop prices jump

  • Primary commodities: raw materials and crops, e.g. cocoa, coffee, wheat, copper, oil.
  • Their supply shifts often and without warning: weather, pests, disease, strikes at mines.
  • Once a crop is harvested, supply is almost fixed for the season: a near-vertical short-run supply curve.
  • Demand is price inelastic: buyers cut back little when price rises, so the demand curve is drawn steep.
  • Inelastic demand + supply shifts = large price swings for small changes in quantity.
  • Say: "a bumper harvest shifts supply to the right; price falls and there is an extension of demand" — not: "demand increases".
Mission 4

Why both curves are so steep

Primary commoditiesManufactured products
PEDLow: necessities (food, fuel, materials) with few substitutesHigher: many close substitutes (brands, models)
PESLow: crops take months or years to grow; new mines take years to open; land and machinery are hard to switch to other uses; perishable crops cannot be stored for longHigher: factories add shifts, use spare capacity and hold stocks of finished goods
  • Example: a new cocoa tree takes several years to bear its first pods, so a high cocoa price cannot quickly raise output.
  • Both PED and PES are usually less than 1 in the short run (ignoring the minus sign for PED).
  • In the long run both become more elastic: farmers replant, buyers find substitutes.
Mission 4

Worked example: a bigger harvest, less money

  • Illustrative data: a world market for a crop. Quantity in thousand tonnes; price in $ per tonne.
  • Normal harvest: 90 sold at $40. Revenue = 40 × 90 = $3,600 thousand.
  • Bumper harvest: 100. Buyers take the extra only if the price falls to $20.
  • Revenue = 20 × 100 = $2,000 thousand.
  • %ΔQ = (100 − 90) ÷ 90 × 100 = +11.1%. %ΔP = (20 − 40) ÷ 40 × 100 = −50%.
  • PED = 11.1 ÷ −50 = −0.22: price inelastic.
  • Poor harvest: 80, price rises to $60. Revenue = 60 × 80 = $4,800 thousand.
  • Inelastic demand: price changes by a bigger percentage than quantity, so revenue moves the same way as price and the opposite way to the harvest. A bigger crop earns less; a smaller crop earns more.
  • Shortcut: %ΔP ≈ %ΔQ ÷ PED. With PED −0.22, an 11% rise in quantity needs about a 50% price fall.
Quick check
?Illustrative data: a frost cuts the world coffee harvest by 5%. Short-run PED for coffee is −0.25. Roughly how does the world price of coffee change, so that buyers' quantity demanded falls by 5%?
Mission 4

Why volatile prices matter

  • Producers: farm incomes rise and crash from year to year; planning and borrowing become risky, so investment falls.
  • Signals: planting decisions are made on last year's price, so a high price can lead to over-planting and a later crash.
  • Countries: many low-income countries earn most export revenue from one or two commodities: primary product dependency.
  • UNCTAD calls a country commodity-dependent when commodities make up more than 60% of its merchandise exports.
  • Export earnings, tax revenue and the exchange rate all swing with the commodity price.
  • Case: in 2024 cocoa futures passed $10,000 per tonne for the first time, after poor harvests in West Africa. Many cocoa farmers in Ghana and Côte d'Ivoire sell at prices set in advance by their governments, so they gained much less than the world price rise.
Quick check
?A world price rise for copper ore and a world price rise for smartphones are each the same percentage. Which best explains why the quantity of copper ore supplied rises much less in the next 6 months?
Mission 4

The buffer stock scheme

  • Buffer stock scheme: an agency holds a stock of a storable commodity and buys or sells it to keep the price inside a band.
  • Floor: the minimum price. Ceiling: the maximum price.
  • Step 1: normal harvest. Price is inside the band. The agency does nothing.
  • Step 2: bumper harvest. The free-market price would fall below the floor. The agency buys the excess supply at the floor price and adds it to the stock.
  • Step 3: poor harvest. The free-market price would rise above the ceiling. The agency sells from its stock at the ceiling price.
  • Needs: a commodity that can be stored, money to buy in good years, and a stock to sell in bad years.
Mission 4Graph lab

Hold the band

Illustrative data: the crop from an earlier screen. Floor $30, ceiling $50. Normal harvest 90, price $40.

  • Takeaway: the agency buys or sells only the gap between harvest and quantity demanded at the floor or ceiling price.
Mission 4

Worked example: three harvests

  • Illustrative data: demand from an earlier screen; band $30–$50.
  • Normal (90): free price $40, inside the band. No action.
  • Bumper (100): free price $20. At the floor of $30, buyers take 95. Agency buys 100 − 95 = 5, costing 5 × 30 = $150 thousand.
  • Poor (80): free price $60. At the ceiling of $50, buyers want 85. Agency sells 85 − 80 = 5, earning 5 × 50 = $250 thousand.
  • Farm revenue without the scheme: $2,000–$4,800 thousand.
  • With the scheme: bumper 30 × 100 = $3,000 thousand; poor 50 × 80 = $4,000 thousand.
  • Incomes are more stable. Here the stock is back where it started, because good and bad years balanced out.

Mission 4Evaluate

Why many schemes collapsed

  • It depends on where the band is set. Nobody knows the long-run equilibrium price. A band set too high means the agency buys year after year: the stock and the bill keep growing.
  • It depends on whether a change is temporary or permanent. A scheme can smooth good and bad years, but not a lasting fall in demand or rise in supply.
  • It depends on storage. Coffee and metals store; fresh fruit does not. Storage costs money every year.
  • It depends on funding. The agency must have money to buy in a run of bumper years.
  • Case: the International Tin Council ran out of funds defending the tin price in October 1985; tin trading on the London Metal Exchange was suspended and the price collapsed.
  • Case: Australia's wool reserve price scheme was suspended in February 1991 with a stockpile of 4.7 million bales, which took until 2001 to sell.
  • Supporters reply: with a band near the long-run average and enough funds, a scheme can reduce income swings for poor producers.
Quick check
?Illustrative data: a coffee buffer stock agency sets a band of $3.00–$3.60 per kg. Over five years the free-market price would have been $2.70, $2.80, $3.20, $2.60 and $2.90. What is the most likely outcome if the band is kept unchanged?
Mission check

?Illustrative data: a drought cuts a tea-exporting country's harvest by 10%. Harvests fall by a similar amount across the region, and the world price of tea rises by 40%. What happens to the country's tea export revenue, and why?

When demand is inelastic, a smaller harvest raises the price by a bigger percentage than the fall in quantity, so revenue rises. A bumper harvest does the reverse.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Absolute poverty: below a fixed real line for basic needs. Relative poverty: below a share (often 60%) of the median.
  • Growth cuts absolute poverty more reliably than relative poverty; a recession can even cut relative poverty.
  • Measures: poverty lines (World Bank $3.00 a day), minimum income standards, the MPI (H × A). All involve judgements.

Mission 2

  • Growth is a rise in real output; development is a rise in well-being on many dimensions.
  • HDI combines health, education and income (GNI per capita at PPP) into one index from 0 to 1.
  • IHDI and GII adjust for inequality and for gender gaps.
  • The MPI counts households deprived in health, education and living standards.
  • No single number captures development: use several indicators.

Mission 3

  • A poverty cycle: low income → low saving and human capital → low investment and productivity → low income.
  • Harrod-Domar: growth = savings ratio ÷ capital-output ratio; a savings gap limits growth.
  • Primary product dependency, price volatility, capital flight and debt drain the funds for development.
  • Weak institutions, insecure property rights, corruption and poor infrastructure deter investment; which barrier matters most depends on the country.

Mission 4

  • Primary commodity prices are volatile: supply shifts often, and both PED and PES are low.
  • With inelastic demand, a bigger harvest can mean lower revenue.
  • A buffer stock buys at the floor in good years and sells at the ceiling in bad years; it fails if the band is set above the long-run price or funds run out.
