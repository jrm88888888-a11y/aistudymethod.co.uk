Mission 1

A religion without a starting point

  • Hinduism has no founder, no single moment of origin and no single founding text.
  • Many Hindus call it sanatana dharma: the "eternal" way, without beginning.
  • Historians look at two kinds of evidence:
  • Archaeology: the cities of the Indus Valley civilisation.
  • Texts: the Rig Veda, the oldest Hindu scripture, composed orally in an early form of Sanskrit.
  • The hard question: how, if at all, do the two connect?
Mission 1

The Indus Valley civilisation

  • Flourished along the Indus and the Ghaggar-Hakra rivers (today Pakistan and north-west India); mature phase c. 2600–1900 BCE.
  • Rediscovered in the 1920s: Harappa (excavated 1921) and Mohenjo-daro (1922); surveys led by John Marshall of the Archaeological Survey of India.
  • Other sites: Dholavira, Lothal, Kalibangan, Rakhigarhi.
  • Features: planned streets on a grid, covered drains, standard-sized bricks and weights, granaries or large halls, trade with Mesopotamia.
  • A script on thousands of small stamp seals: still undeciphered.
  • No palaces or obvious temples have been found.
Mission 1

Evidence of religion

Each find is real; each interpretation is debated.

  • The Great Bath (Mohenjo-daro): a watertight brick pool. Perhaps ritual bathing for purity, as at later temple tanks.
  • The "Pashupati" seal: a horned figure seated cross-legged, surrounded by animals. Marshall called it a "proto-Shiva", as lord of animals and a yogi.
  • Female figurines: terracotta figures, often read as mother goddesses, linked to later Devi worship.
  • Fire altars (Kalibangan, Lothal): pits with ash, perhaps for offerings.
  • Other symbols: the svastika, pipal leaves, conical stones once read as lingams.
Mission 1

Decline

  • From c. 1900 BCE the great cities shrank; people moved to smaller villages east and south.
  • Likely causes, debated: rivers shifting or drying (the Ghaggar-Hakra), a weaker monsoon, declining trade.
  • Mortimer Wheeler (1890–1976) once blamed invading Aryans for a massacre at Mohenjo-daro.
  • Later study showed the skeletons were buried at different times, not killed in one attack. Most archaeologists now reject the massacre idea.
  • Decline was gradual: much village culture, crafts and perhaps beliefs continued.

Mission 1

Who were the "Aryans"?

  • Arya ("noble") is what the people of the Rig Veda called themselves.
  • It names a language and culture, not a race. They spoke Vedic Sanskrit, related to Old Persian, Greek and Latin (the Indo-European family).
  • Their culture, as the hymns describe it: cattle herding, horses and chariots, fire sacrifice (yajna), the sacred drink soma, gods such as Agni and Indra.
  • The Rig Veda is usually dated c. 1500–1200 BCE: after the Indus cities declined.
  • 19th- and 20th-century writers misused "Aryan" as a racial label; scholars reject that use.

Rig Veda 10.75.5 (paraphrase): a hymn to the rivers names, in order, the Ganga, Yamuna, Sarasvati and others.

Q1 Why do continuity supporters stress these verses?

Show answer

Q2 What is the main objection?

Show answer

Mission 1

Problems with the evidence

  • Undeciphered script: we have Indus writing but cannot read it.
  • Reading backwards: seeing a "Shiva" or a "goddess" may project later Hinduism onto older objects.
  • Different kinds of evidence: archaeology without texts (Indus) versus texts without much archaeology (the Vedas).
  • Dating oral texts: the Rig Veda was memorised for centuries before being written; its date is estimated from language.
  • Genes and language: ancient DNA shows people moving, not what they believed or spoke.
  • Politics: national and religious identity is at stake, so evidence is read with strong motives.

Mission 1Evaluate

Did Hinduism begin in the Indus Valley?

  • Claim: Modern Hinduism has its roots in the Indus Valley civilisation.
  • For: Possible links: ritual bathing, a yogi-like figure, goddess figurines, fire altars, the svastika.
  • For: Hinduism absorbed many local traditions; Indus village culture continued after the cities fell.
  • Against: Without the script, every link is a guess; similar objects need not mean similar beliefs.
  • Against: The Vedas, which most Hindus treat as the highest authority, come from Indo-Aryan culture.
  • Judgement criteria: What counts as evidence of belief? Does "roots" mean one source or many? How far does inconclusive evidence matter to the many Hindus whose faith rests on the Vedas, not on archaeology?

Mission 2

The four Vedas

  • Veda means "knowledge". There are four:
  • Rig Veda: 1,028 hymns of praise in 10 books (mandalas); the oldest, c. 1500–1200 BCE.
  • Sama Veda: verses set to chant. Yajur Veda: formulas for performing sacrifice. Atharva Veda: hymns and spells for healing, protection and daily life.
  • Each Veda grew in layers: hymns (Samhitas), ritual texts (Brahmanas), "forest" texts (Aranyakas), and the philosophical Upanishads.
Mission 2

Yajna: the sacrifice

  • Yajna: offerings (ghee, grain, milk, soma, and in some rites animals) poured into a sacred fire.
  • Specialist priests: one recites hymns, one performs the actions, one chants, one oversees.
  • Purpose: to praise and feed the gods (devas), who give in return: rain, cattle, sons, long life, victory.
  • Sacrifice keeps rta, the cosmic order that holds nature and society together.
  • Vedic society: semi-nomadic, cattle-herding tribes led by a chief (rajan), with tribal assemblies. Priests and warriors held high status; the four-fold varna scheme appears only in a late hymn (Rig Veda 10.90; see a later lesson).
  • Household rites (daily fire offerings) and great public rites (lasting days) both existed.
Mission 2

Agni: the fire

  • Agni is fire, and the god in the fire. About 200 hymns are addressed to him; the Rig Veda opens with a hymn to Agni (1.1).
  • Messenger: Agni carries offerings up to the gods and brings the gods down to the sacrifice.
  • Priest of the gods: he is called the household priest and the "mouth" of the gods.
  • Present in three places: the hearth on earth, lightning in the air, the sun in the sky.
  • Every yajna depends on Agni: without fire, there is no offering.
Mission 2

Indra: king of the gods

  • Indra: the warrior god of storm and thunder, armed with the vajra (thunderbolt). About a quarter of the Rig Veda's hymns are to him.
  • Great deed (Rig Veda 1.32, paraphrase): Indra kills the serpent Vritra, who had held back the waters, and releases the rivers.
  • He leads the Aryas in battle and is strengthened by drinking soma.
  • Worshippers ask Indra for victory, rain and cattle.
  • He is a model of the Vedic warrior-chief: generous, powerful, sometimes reckless.
Mission 2

Soma: the drink and the god

  • Soma is a plant, the drink pressed from it, and a god. Book 9 of the Rig Veda (114 hymns) is wholly to Soma.
  • In the rite, stalks are pressed, the juice is filtered through wool, mixed with milk and offered; priests drink it too.
  • Effects described: inspiration, strength, visions, a sense of immortality (Rig Veda 8.48.3, paraphrase: the worshippers have drunk soma, become immortal and reached the light).
  • The plant is unknown today: suggestions include ephedra and a mushroom; no theory is agreed.
  • Later, Soma is identified with the moon, and substitute plants are used in rites.

Mission 3

Where the word came from

  • Sindhu is the Sanskrit name of the Indus river.
  • Persians said Hindu: the land and people beyond the Indus. It was a geographical name, not a religious one.
  • Muslim rulers in India later used "Hindu" for Indians who were not Muslim.
  • "Hinduism" (early spelling "Hindooism") came into English around 1800, used by British writers and also by Indian reformers.
  • Before that, people named their own traditions: devotees of Vishnu or Shiva, followers of a teacher, the Vedic way.
  • So the word groups together traditions that did not always see themselves as one.
Mission 3

Sanatana and sadharana dharma

  • Dharma: the order that upholds the universe, and the duty and right conduct that follow from it.
  • Sanatana dharma: the eternal dharma. Many Hindus prefer it to "Hinduism": it has no founder and no beginning.
  • Sadharana dharma: universal duties for everyone: non-violence, truthfulness, not stealing, purity, self-control (e.g. Manusmriti 10.63, paraphrase).
  • These contrast with duties that depend on one's place in society and stage of life (varnashrama dharma).

Mission 3Diversity

One religion or many?

What differs:

  • Deities: Vaishnavas (Vishnu and his avatars), Shaivas (Shiva), Shaktas (the Goddess), Smartas (several deities as forms of one Brahman).
  • Philosophy: non-dualist (Advaita), qualified non-dualist, dualist (Dvaita).
  • Practice: regional festivals, caste customs, temple or home worship, ascetic or family life.
  • No single founder, creed, scripture or authority.

What is shared (by most):

  • Respect for the Vedas; ideas of karma, samsara, moksha, dharma; puja, pilgrimage, samskaras.
  • Images used by scholars: a family of traditions; a banyan tree with many trunks (Julius Lipner).
Mission 3Evaluate

Is Hinduism one religion?

  • Claim: "Hinduism" names a single religion.
  • For: Shared ideas (karma, samsara, dharma, moksha) and respect for the Vedas link almost all Hindus.
  • For: Hindus themselves use the name; the idea of sanatana dharma gives an inner unity.
  • Against: The word came from outsiders; deities, philosophies and goals differ greatly.
  • Against: "Religion" assumes a founder, creed and canon that Hinduism lacks.
  • Judgement criteria: What makes a religion "one": a founder, a creed, shared practices, or self-identity? Who decides: scholars, reformers or ordinary Hindus?

Mission 4

The Song of the Lord

  • Bhagavad Gita: "Song of the Lord". 700 verses in 18 chapters.
  • It sits in book 6 of the Mahabharata, on the eve of the war at Kurukshetra.
  • Krishna, whom many Hindus worship as God (an avatar of Vishnu, or for some the Supreme himself), is Arjuna's charioteer.
  • Arjuna is the great archer of the five Pandava brothers.
  • Usually dated somewhere between c. 400 BCE and 200 CE; scholars disagree.
Mission 4

Arjuna's crisis

  • Arjuna looks across the field and sees teachers, uncles, cousins and friends in the enemy army.
  • He drops his bow: killing kin would destroy family dharma and bring chaos and sin (chapter 1).
  • But he is a kshatriya (warrior); his duty is to fight a just war.
  • So his personal dharma (svadharma) as a warrior clashes with his dharma as a kinsman.
  • Your spec: "the conflict which can arise from following personal dharma".
  • Krishna's answer fills the next 17 chapters: who you really are; how to act; how to love God.
Mission 4Read the source

Do your own duty

Bhagavad Gita 2:31 (Edwin Arnold's verse translation, 1885), Krishna to Arjuna:

> "Nought better can betide a martial soul

> Than lawful war"

Bhagavad Gita 18:47 (Arnold):

> "Better thine own work is, though done with fault,

> Than doing others' work, ev'n excellently."

Q1 What kind of war does 2:31 approve?

Show answer

A "lawful" war: a righteous war, fought according to dharma, not any war.

Q2 According to 18:47, why should Arjuna not leave the battle to become a peaceful hermit?

Show answer

His own duty, even done imperfectly, is better than another's duty done well.

  • Also 3:35 says the same in almost the same words.
Mission 4

Dharma and varnadharma

  • Dharma: duty, right order, the way things should be.
  • Varnadharma: the duty of one's varna (social class). The Gita (18:41–44) lists four:
  • Brahmins: calm, self-control, learning. Kshatriyas: courage, leadership. Vaishyas: farming, cattle, trade. Shudras: service.
  • The Gita says the varnas are set by gunas (qualities) and karma (actions) (4:13).
  • 18:46: a person reaches perfection by worshipping God through their own work.
  • Debate: varna by birth (traditional view, linked to caste) or by qualities and conduct (many reformers, e.g. Dayananda Saraswati of the Arya Samaj, Vivekananda)? Caste discrimination is illegal in India (Constitution, 1950).

Mission 4

The wisdom of Samkhya

  • Samkhya ("discrimination", "enumeration"): an ancient school that separates two realities:
  • Purusha: the conscious self, eternal and unchanging. Prakriti: matter and nature, always changing, including body and mind.
  • Prakriti works through three gunas (qualities): sattva (light, goodness), rajas (passion, activity), tamas (darkness, inertia).
  • Krishna first teaches Arjuna this "wisdom of Samkhya" (2:39): the self does not die when the body dies.
  • Gita 2:20 (Arnold): "Never the spirit was born; the spirit shall cease to be never".
  • So Arjuna's grief is misplaced: bodies die; the atman (self) is reborn (2:22) until liberation.
  • The Gita uses Samkhya terms but adds what classical Samkhya lacks: Krishna as Lord over purusha and prakriti.
Mission 4

Karma yoga: act without clinging

  • Karma yoga: the path of action, done as duty, without attachment to its results.
  • 3:5 (Arnold): "no jot of time, at any time, / Rests any actionless; his nature's law / Compels him, even unwilling, into act".
  • So giving up action is impossible: everyone acts. What matters is how.
  • 3:19 (Arnold): "thy task prescribed / With spirit unattached gladly perform".
  • Selfless action builds no binding karma, so it leads towards moksha (liberation).
  • Arjuna should fight, not for glory or gain, but because it is his duty, offered to God.
Mission 4

Bhakti yoga: love and surrender

  • Bhakti yoga: the path of loving devotion to a personal God.
  • 9:26 (Arnold): "Whoso shall offer Me in faith and love / A leaf, a flower, a fruit, water poured forth, / That offering I accept".
  • 9:27: whatever you do (eating, giving, fasting) do it as an offering to God.
  • 9:32 (Arnold): "Woman or man; sprung of the Vaisya caste / Or lowly disregarded Sudra,--all / Plant foot upon the highest path". Bhakti is open to everyone.
  • 11:54 (Arnold): "Only by fullest service, perfect faith, / And uttermost surrender am I known".
  • After Arjuna sees Krishna's universal form (chapter 11), many readers take the Gita to present devotion as the highest way to know God (11:54; 12:2).
Mission 4

Jnana yoga and going beyond the gunas

  • Jnana yoga: the path of knowledge: seeing the eternal self as distinct from body and gunas.
  • 14:23–26: one who is untroubled by the gunas, equal in pleasure and pain, praise and blame, "the clod, the marble, and the gold are one" (14:24, Arnold), has gone beyond the gunas.
  • 14:26 links this to devotion: such a person, "With single, fervent faith adoring Me", becomes fit for Brahman.
  • 9:23: even those who worship other gods with faith worship Krishna, "though they pray wrongfully" (Arnold).
  • So the Gita joins the paths: knowledge, action and devotion support one another.
Mission 4Read the source

Signs of a godly person

Bhagavad Gita 16:1 (Arnold), part of the "divine qualities":

> "Fearlessness, singleness of soul, the will

> Always to strive for wisdom; opened hand

> And governed appetites; and piety,"

  • The list goes on: humility, uprightness, non-injury (ahimsa: "heed to injure nought which lives"), truthfulness, calm, charity, compassion, patience, purity (16:2–3).
  • Opposite qualities (16:4): deceit, arrogance, anger, harsh speech, ignorance.

Q1 Which practices in the list are about the inner life rather than outward ritual?

Show answer

Singleness of soul, striving for wisdom, governed appetites, calm.

Q2 How does this list show the "nature of religious practices" in the Gita?

Show answer

Religion is moral character and devotion in daily life, not only sacrifice and ritual.

Mission 4Diversity

One text, many readings

  • Shankara (c. 8th century), Advaita: the Gita's goal is jnana, knowledge that atman is Brahman; action prepares the mind.
  • Ramanuja (trad. 1017–1137), Vishishtadvaita: the goal is bhakti, loving surrender to a personal God.
  • B. G. Tilak (1856–1920), Gita Rahasya (1915): the Gita teaches karma yoga, active duty in the world (including the struggle for Indian independence).
  • M. K. Gandhi (1869–1948): the battle is an allegory of the inner war between good and evil; the Gita teaches selfless action and, for him, non-violence.
  • ISKCON: A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada, Bhagavad-gita As It Is (1968): Krishna is the Supreme Person; bhakti is the goal.
  • In daily life: the Gita is recited, sung, studied in groups, and celebrated at Gita Jayanti.
Mission 4Evaluate

Which path matters most?

  • Claim: In the Gita, bhakti yoga is more important than karma or jnana yoga.
  • For: Chapters 9–12 and 18:65–66 call for surrender to Krishna; 11:54 says only devotion lets God be known.
  • For: Bhakti is open to all (9:32), whatever their learning or varna.
  • Against: Arjuna's question is about action; Krishna's practical answer is to fight without attachment (karma yoga).
  • Against: Advaita readers rank jnana highest; Shankara read the Gita that way.
  • Against: The paths overlap: devotion is shown in action; knowledge deepens devotion.
  • Judgement criteria: Which verses are central? Whose interpretation carries authority (gurus, schools, the community)? Does the Gita rank the paths or join them?
  • Also weigh: how important each path is for liberation; whether the bond between Krishna and Arjuna is a central feature of Hinduism.

Mission 5

What "yoga" means

  • Yoga from Sanskrit yuj: "to yoke", "to join". A discipline that unites the self with the ultimate.
  • Also called a marga (path): a way to moksha, liberation from samsara (the cycle of rebirth).
  • The Bhagavad Gita teaches several yogas side by side; each chapter is titled a yoga.
  • Not mainly postures: the postures belong to hatha yoga (see Mission 6).
  • Key belief shared by all paths: karma and ignorance bind the atman; the right discipline frees it.
Mission 5

Different paths for different people

  • Hindus believe people differ in temperament, so different paths suit different characters:
  • Jnana yoga: path of knowledge, for the reflective mind.
  • Karma yoga: path of action, for the active person.
  • Bhakti yoga: path of devotion, for the emotional heart.
  • Raja yoga: path of meditation, for the contemplative (see Mission 6).
  • Swami Vivekananda (1863–1902) popularised this fourfold scheme in his lectures Karma Yoga, Bhakti Yoga, Jnana Yoga and Raja Yoga (1896). The Gita itself mainly names three.
Mission 5

Karma yoga: selfless action

  • Karma yoga: doing one's dharma (duty) without attachment to results (nishkama karma).
  • Action itself does not bind; desire for its fruits does.
  • Offer every action to God; this purifies the mind (chitta-shuddhi) of selfish desire.
  • Aim: identification with the transcendent, the self no longer acting for itself.

> "Therefore, thy task prescribed / With spirit unattached gladly perform, / Since in performance of plain duty man / Mounts to his highest bliss." (Bhagavad Gita 3:19, Arnold)

Mission 5Read the source

> "No man shall 'scape from act / By shunning action; nay, and none shall come / By mere renouncements unto perfectness. / Nay, and no jot of time, at any time, / Rests any actionless; his nature's law / Compels him, even unwilling, into act" (Bhagavad Gita 3:4–5, Arnold)

Mission 5

Jnana yoga: the path of wisdom

  • Jnana: knowledge, not facts but direct realisation that atman is Brahman.
  • Method: viveka (discrimination between real and unreal), vairagya (renunciation), study of the Upanishads with a guru: hearing, reflecting, meditating.
  • Aim: identification with Brahman; ignorance (avidya) ends, and with it rebirth.
  • Classically linked to Advaita Vedanta and Shankara.

Mission 5

Bhakti yoga: the path of devotion

  • Bhakti: loving devotion to a personal God, often Krishna, Rama, Shiva or the Goddess.
  • Open to everyone, whatever caste or learning (Gita 9:32;).
  • Practices: puja, chanting the divine name, singing (kirtan, bhajan), pilgrimage.
  • Liberation is often by God's grace, and may be eternal loving union rather than merging (Ramanuja).

> "Only by fullest service, perfect faith, / And uttermost surrender am I known / And seen, and entered into, Indian Prince!" (Bhagavad Gita 11:54, Arnold)

  • Set verse for your spec: Gita 11:54.
Mission 5

The Samkhya link

  • Samkhya: an ancient dualist school. Two realities: purusha (pure consciousness) and prakriti (matter and mind, made of the three gunas).
  • Bondage: purusha wrongly identifies with prakriti. Liberation: kaivalya ("isolation"), knowing the difference.
  • The Gita uses Samkhya ideas: chapter 2's "wisdom of Samkhya" teaches that the atman is eternal and the body passes (2:11–30).

Mission 6

Raja yoga: the path of meditation

  • Raja yoga, the "royal" path: control of the mind through meditation.
  • Main text: the Yoga Sutras of Patanjali (about 195 short sutras; dating debated, many scholars place them around 400 CE).
  • Definition in Yoga Sutra 1.2 (paraphrase): yoga is the stilling of the movements of the mind (chitta vritti nirodha).
  • When the mind is still, the self rests in its own true nature (1.3).
  • The name "raja yoga" for Patanjali's system was popularised by Vivekananda, Raja Yoga (1896).
  • Links: jnana yoga needs meditation to turn knowledge into realisation (see Mission 5).
Mission 6

The eight limbs (ashtanga)

Yoga Sutra 2.29 lists eight limbs, from outer to inner:

  • 1 Yama: restraints (ahimsa, truthfulness, non-stealing, brahmacharya, non-possessiveness).
  • 2 Niyama: observances (purity, contentment, austerity, self-study, surrender to Ishvara).
  • 3 Asana: a steady, comfortable seat (2.46). 4 Pranayama: breath control.
  • 5 Pratyahara: withdrawing the senses. 6 Dharana: concentration. 7 Dhyana: meditation.
  • 8 Samadhi: absorption.
  • Goal: kaivalya, the self freed from entanglement with matter.
  • Ethics first: postures and breath come only after yama and niyama.
Mission 6

Samkhya beneath the Yoga Sutras

  • Patanjali builds on Samkhya dualism: purusha (consciousness) and prakriti (matter and mind, made of the three gunas).
  • Suffering comes from confusing the seer with what is seen (Yoga Sutra 2.17, paraphrase).
  • Difference: Samkhya does not need God; Patanjali adds Ishvara, a special purusha untouched by karma, as a focus for devotion (1.23–24).
  • Your spec calls this "the wisdom of Samkhya" (Gita 2).

Mission 6

Hatha yoga

  • Developed c. 11th–15th centuries, especially among the Nath yogis (traditionally founded by Gorakshanath).
  • Key text: Hatha Yoga Pradipika (Svatmarama, 15th century).
  • Practices: asanas, pranayama, cleansing techniques (shatkarma), locks (bandha) and seals (mudra).
  • Aim: purify the body and its subtle channels, awaken kundalini energy, and realise the body's divine nature; also strength and health.
  • Hatha means "force": a forceful, physical method.
  • The Hatha Yoga Pradipika presents hatha as a stairway to raja yoga (1.1–2, paraphrase).
Mission 6

How yoga came West

  • 1893: Vivekananda speaks at the World's Parliament of Religions, Chicago; Raja Yoga (1896) presents yoga as meditation.
  • 1920: Paramahansa Yogananda arrives in the USA; Autobiography of a Yogi (1946).
  • 1930s–60s: T. Krishnamacharya (1888–1989) in Mysore develops dynamic posture sequences; students B. K. S. Iyengar (Light on Yoga, 1966), K. Pattabhi Jois, Indra Devi.
  • 1936: Swami Sivananda founds the Divine Life Society, Rishikesh; Swami Vishnudevananda opens Sivananda centres in the West from 1959.
  • 1960s–70s: Maharishi Mahesh Yogi; the Beatles in Rishikesh (1968).
  • 2015: first International Day of Yoga (21 June), set by the UN in 2014.
Mission 6

New purposes

  • Much Western yoga focuses on fitness, flexibility and stress relief, often with no religious aim.
  • Scholar Mark Singleton, Yoga Body (2010), paraphrase: modern posture yoga blends hatha with Indian and Western physical culture (gymnastics, body-building) of the early 20th century.
  • Some Western teachers keep mantra, meditation and ethics; others teach postures alone.
  • Back in India, posture yoga is now also promoted for health and as national heritage.
  • Purpose changes significance: for liberation (moksha), health, or both.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Hinduism has no founder or start date; historians use Indus archaeology and the Rig Veda.
  • Indus finds (Great Bath, "Pashupati" seal, figurines, fire altars) suggest religion, but the script is unread and every link is an interpretation.
  • Invasion is largely abandoned; migration is the majority academic view; continuity is held by some Indian scholars and many Hindus.

Mission 2

  • The four Vedas (Rig, Sama, Yajur, Atharva) were memorised and recited long before they were written.
  • Yajna (fire sacrifice) kept rta; Agni carried offerings, Indra won battles and released the waters, soma inspired.

Mission 3

  • "Hindu" began as a Persian geographical name; "Hinduism" entered English around 1800. Many Hindus prefer sanatana dharma.
  • Hinduism has no single founder, creed or scripture; scholars speak of a family of traditions with shared ideas.

Mission 4

  • The Gita is Krishna's teaching to Arjuna at Kurukshetra: do your own dharma (2:31, 18:47), even when duties clash.
  • Three paths: karma yoga (action without attachment, 3:19), bhakti yoga (devotion, 9:26, 11:54), jnana yoga (knowledge of the self).
  • Samkhya: the eternal self (purusha) and changing nature (prakriti) with its three gunas; going beyond the gunas (14:23–26); divine qualities (16:1–3); work as worship (18:46).

Mission 5

  • Yoga ("joining") is a disciplined path (marga) to moksha; different paths suit different temperaments.
  • Karma yoga: duty without attachment; jnana yoga: knowledge that atman is Brahman; bhakti yoga: loving devotion to a personal God.

Mission 6

  • Raja yoga (Patanjali) stills the mind through eight limbs, built on Samkhya dualism, aiming at kaivalya.
  • Hatha yoga purifies the body as a stairway to raja yoga; modern posture yoga grew from it and from physical culture.
