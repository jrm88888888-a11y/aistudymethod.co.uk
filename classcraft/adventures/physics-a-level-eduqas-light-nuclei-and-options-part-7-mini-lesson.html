Mission 1

Mission 1: Drop a strong magnet down a copper pipe and it falls in slow motion. Why?

Make a guess: Drop a strong magnet down a copper pipe and it falls in slow motion. Why?

Mission 1

Magnetic flux

  • Magnetic flux: how much field passes through an area. Think: number of field lines through it.
  • Φ = AB cos θ, θ = angle between B and the normal.
  • Unit: weber. 1 Wb = 1 T m².
  • Plane of the loop parallel to the field: zero flux.
Mission 1

Flux linkage

  • A coil of N turns: the same flux passes through every turn.
  • Flux linkage = NΦ.
Quick check
?A flat rectangular loop measures 6.0 cm by 5.0 cm. It is in a uniform magnetic field of 0.25 T, with the plane of the loop at 20° to the field lines. What is the magnetic flux through the loop?
Mission 1

Faraday's law

  • An emf is induced whenever the flux linkage changes: move a magnet, move the coil, change B, change the area, or turn the coil.
  • Steady flux, however large: no emf.
  • Size: emf = rate of change of flux linkage.
  • emf = −Δ(NΦ)/Δt
  • On a flux linkage–time graph, size of the emf = gradient. On a graph of the flux through one turn, size of the emf = N × gradient.
Mission 1

Worked example

  • A coil of 200 turns, area 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ m², is ⟂ to a 0.50 T field. The field falls steadily to zero in 0.10 s.
  • Change in flux per turn = 0.50 × 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ Wb. Change in flux linkage = 200 × 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ = 0.040 Wb.
  • emf = 0.040 ÷ 0.10 = 0.40 V (while the field is falling).
  • Same change in 0.050 s: twice the emf. Once the field stops changing: zero.
Quick check
?The graph shows how the magnetic flux through each turn of a coil of 200 turns varies with time t. The coil is held at rest. What is the magnitude of the emf induced in the coil between t = 0.50 s and t = 0.70 s?
Mission 1

Lenz's law

  • The induced current flows in the direction that opposes the change that produces it.
  • N pole pushed in: the near end of the coil becomes an N pole, repelling it.
  • N pole pulled out: the near end becomes an S pole, attracting it back.
  • The minus sign in Faraday's law expresses this.
Mission 1

Lenz's law is energy conservation

  • Suppose the induced current helped the change: the magnet would be pulled in faster, the current would grow, and so on. Energy from nothing.
  • So the induced current must oppose the motion. You must do work against that force.
  • That work becomes electrical energy, then thermal energy in the circuit.
  • Open circuit: an emf, but no current, so no opposing force.
  • A solid metal sheet or pipe acts like many loops: changing flux drives eddy currents around inside it, and they oppose the motion too.
Quick check
?The south pole of a bar magnet is pulled away from one end of a coil whose ends are joined. Which statement is correct?
Mission check

?A thick aluminium plate swings as a pendulum through the gap between the poles of a strong magnet, and stops after a few swings. An identical plate with many deep slots cut into it swings for much longer. Which explains the difference?

The falling magnet changes the flux through each ring of the pipe, so currents flow around the pipe. By Lenz's law their field opposes the magnet's motion, slowing it; its lost energy heats the copper.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Switch on a current in one coil. Why does a meter on a separate coil flick, then fall back to zero?

Make a guess: Switch on a current in one coil. Why does a meter on a separate coil flick, then fall back to zero?

Mission 2

Seeing induction: magnet and coil

  • Coil joined to a centre-zero galvanometer (or data logger). Push a magnet in: the needle flicks.
  • Magnet at rest, even deep inside: no reading.
  • Pull it out, or use the other pole: the needle flicks the other way.
  • Bigger emf with: faster motion, stronger magnet, more turns.
Mission 2

Linked coils

  • Two coils on one iron core. Coil 1 has a switch and a cell; coil 2 has a meter.
  • Switch on: the current in coil 1 grows, its flux grows, and coil 2's flux linkage changes: a flick.
  • Steady current: steady flux, zero emf. Switch off: a flick the other way.
  • Bigger emf with: faster change of current, more turns on coil 2, an iron core (more of coil 1's flux links coil 2).
  • An alternating current in coil 1 gives a continuous alternating emf in coil 2: the principle of the transformer.
Quick check
?According to Faraday's law of induction, the magnitude of the emf induced in a coil with a fixed number of turns is proportional to which quantity?
Mission 2

Demonstrating Lenz's law

  • Hanging aluminium ring. Push a magnet towards it: the ring moves away. Pull the magnet back: the ring follows.
  • Ring with a slit: no complete circuit, no current, no movement. The emf alone exerts no force.
  • Galvanometer and coil: find which way current makes an N pole at the coil's end (test with a cell). Push an N pole in: the coil's near end becomes N.
  • Each time, the induced current opposes the change. To keep the magnet moving you must do work: that is where the electrical energy comes from.
Quick check
?Some trains brake with strong magnets held just above the steel rail. The magnets move along the rail with the train and never touch it. Where does the train's kinetic energy go, and how does the braking force change as the train slows down?
Mission 2

A coil turning in a field

  • Rectangular coil, N turns, area A, in a uniform field B. θ = angle between B and the normal to the coil.
  • Φ = AB cos θ per turn, so flux linkage NΦ = NAB cos θ.
  • Coil face-on to the field (θ = 0): flux linkage largest, BAN. Edge-on (θ = 90°): zero.
  • Example: N = 50, A = 3.0 × 10⁻³ m², B = 0.20 T. Largest flux linkage = 0.030 Wb. At θ = 60°: 0.030 × cos 60° = 0.015 Wb.
Quick check
?A flat rectangular coil of 100 turns measures 4.0 cm by 5.0 cm. It starts with its plane perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field of strength 0.40 T and is then rotated through 60° in 0.050 s, about an axis perpendicular to the field. What is the magnitude of the average emf induced during the rotation?
Mission 2

Reading flux-linkage graphs

  • Size of emf = gradient of the flux linkage–time graph. The induced emf opposes the change, so the graph draws it as minus the gradient.
  • Example: flux linkage rises steadily from 0 to 0.060 Wb in 0.20 s: emf = 0.060 ÷ 0.20 = 0.30 V.
  • Flux linkage then stays at 0.060 Wb: emf = 0.
  • Flux linkage falls to 0 in 0.10 s: emf = 0.060 ÷ 0.10 = 0.60 V, in the opposite direction.
  • Steeper gradient → bigger emf. Flat → none, whatever the value.
Mission check

?Two coils share an iron core. The current in coil 1 rises steadily from 0 to 2.0 A in 0.40 s, stays at 2.0 A for a while, then falls steadily to zero in 0.10 s. How does the emf induced in coil 2 compare in the three intervals?

An emf appears only while flux linkage is changing. Switching on coil 1 changes coil 2's flux linkage for an instant: a flick. Once the current is steady, nothing changes, and the reading falls back to zero.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Move a metal rod or spin a coil near a magnet, and a voltage appears. What decides how big it is?

Make a guess: Move a metal rod or spin a coil near a magnet, and a voltage appears. What decides how big it is?

Mission 3

A moving rod makes an emf

  • A rod moves at right angles to a uniform field. Its free electrons move with it, so the magnetic force pushes them along the rod.
  • Charge piles up at one end: an emf across the rod.
  • Flux view: in time Δt the rod sweeps area (length × vΔt), so the flux it cuts is B × length × vΔt.
  • emf = Blv (B, l and v all at right angles; l = rod length).
  • Moving along the field, or along its own length: no emf.
Mission 3

Worked example: rod on rails

  • Rod 0.20 m long slides at 3.0 m s⁻¹ on rails joined by a 1.5 Ω resistor. B = 0.50 T, at right angles to the rails' plane.
  • emf: 0.50 × 0.20 × 3.0 = 0.30 V.
  • Current: 0.30 ÷ 1.5 = 0.20 A (rod and rails of negligible resistance).
  • Force on the rod: B × I × length = 0.50 × 0.20 × 0.20 = 0.020 N, opposing its motion.
Quick check
?A metal rod 0.40 m long rests across two parallel horizontal rails 0.40 m apart, which are joined at one end by a resistor. A uniform vertical magnetic field of strength 150 mT acts over the whole arrangement. The rod moves along the rails at constant velocity, away from the resistor, so that it travels 1.0 m in the next 0.50 s. At the instant considered it is 0.15 m from the resistor. What is the emf induced in the rod?
Mission 3

Why the rod is braked

  • The induced current is in the field, so the rod feels a force B × I × length.
  • Lenz's law: that force opposes the motion. Let go, and the rod slows down.
  • Constant speed needs a push equal to the magnetic force: F = BIl = B²l²v/R.
  • Energy: work done by the push = thermal energy in the resistor.
  • Example, the rod on rails above: 0.020 N × 3.0 m s⁻¹ = 0.060 W = I²R = 0.20² × 1.5.
Mission 3

The a.c. generator

  • A coil turns at a steady rate in a uniform field. Slip rings and brushes connect it to the circuit.
  • As it turns, the flux linkage keeps changing, so an emf is induced (Faraday). It reverses every half turn: alternating.
  • Coil plane perpendicular to the field: flux linkage greatest, momentarily not changing. emf = 0.
  • Coil plane parallel to the field: flux linkage zero but changing fastest. emf greatest.
  • Larger B, coil area or number of turns: larger peak emf.
  • Turn it twice as fast: flux linkage changes twice as fast. Peak emf doubles; period halves.
Quick check
?A coil turns at a steady rate in a uniform magnetic field, making one full turn in a time T. At t = 0 the plane of the coil is perpendicular to the field. At which of these times is the size of the induced emf greatest?
Quick check
?A rectangular coil rotates at a constant frequency in a uniform magnetic field. A graph of the induced emf against time is a sine curve that is zero at t = 0, 10 ms, 20 ms, 30 ms and 40 ms, with maxima of +4.0 V at 5 ms and 25 ms and minima of −4.0 V at 15 ms and 35 ms. The coil is then made to rotate at 40 revolutions per second in the same field. What is the new peak emf?
Mission check

?An aircraft with a wingspan of 40 m flies horizontally at 250 m s⁻¹. The vertical component of the Earth's magnetic field there is 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ T. What emf is induced between the wingtips?

The emf is the rate of cutting flux: emf = B × length × v. Stronger field, longer conductor or faster motion all give more.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Φ = BA cos θ; unit Wb = T m².
  • Faraday: emf = rate of change of flux linkage; no change, no emf.
  • Lenz: the induced current opposes the change; otherwise energy would be created.

Mission 2

  • Induced emf only while flux linkage changes: faster change, stronger field or more turns give more emf.
  • Lenz's law in practice: the induced current opposes the change, so work must be done; a cut ring has emf but no current and no force.
  • Turning coil: NΦ = NAB cos θ, largest face-on, zero edge-on.

Mission 3

  • A conductor moving at right angles to a field: emf = Blv. The induced current is braked by a force F = BIl.
  • Rotating coil: emf greatest when the coil plane is parallel to the field; bigger B, area, turns or speed give a bigger peak.
