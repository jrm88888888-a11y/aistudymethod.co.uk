The Christian Church: Catholic & Protestant

This mini-lesson walks you through the whole of CCEA Unit 1: the birth, development and beliefs of the church (Pentecost, St Paul, the Early Church, the Apostles' Creed, St Patrick and the Reformation); leadership, worship and sacraments in the Catholic Church and in one Protestant Church; and the role of the church in contemporary society.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go and collect ⭐ stars. Watch for the Set passage and Evaluation (AO2) flags — CCEA awards marks for knowledge and understanding (AO1) and for analysis and evaluation (AO2). Press Start when you are ready.

The birth of the church · set passage: Acts 2: 1–8

Pentecost — the birth of the church

CCEA starts Unit 1 where the church itself starts: Pentecost. Fifty days after Passover, Jesus' followers were gathered together in Jerusalem when, in the words of the set passage, there came a sound like a rushing wind, what looked like tongues of fire rested on each of them, and they were filled with the Holy Spirit and began to speak in other languages.

  • Jerusalem was crowded with Jewish pilgrims from many nations for the festival of Shavuot (Weeks). Each heard the disciples speaking in their own language (Acts 2: 5–8).
  • The meaning: the Spirit Jesus promised has been given; the message is for every nation, not one; frightened disciples become bold witnesses.
  • Christians call Pentecost the birthday of the church — the moment the group of disciples becomes a preaching, growing community.

Exam habit: when CCEA names a passage, learn what happens and what it means. AO1 marks come from the detail (wind, fire, languages); AO2 marks come from explaining why it matters — for example, that Pentecost reverses division and makes the church universal.

Set passage: 1 Cor. 12: 1–11, 14–22, 25–27

St Paul's teaching on the church

Paul writes to a divided congregation in Corinth and gives Christians their most famous picture of the church: the body of Christ.

  • Many gifts, one Spirit (12: 1–11) — wisdom, knowledge, faith, healing, prophecy, tongues and their interpretation are all given by the same Spirit for the common good.
  • Many parts, one body (12: 14–22) — a body is not one member but many. The eye cannot say to the hand, "I have no need of you." The parts that seem weaker are indispensable.
  • Mutual care (12: 25–27) — there should be no division in the body; if one part suffers, all suffer; if one is honoured, all rejoice. "Now you are the body of Christ, and individually members of it."

Why CCEA sets it: this passage is the theological argument against a church of star performers and for a church where every member matters. It is used in both Catholic and Protestant teaching about ministry.

Set passages: Matt. 10: 1, 5–14 · Acts 2: 42, 44–47 · 1 Cor. 11: 23–25 · James 2: 14–17 · Col. 3: 12–17

Characteristics of the Early Church

CCEA lists six characteristics: preaching, teaching, prayer, miracles, hymn-singing and sharing. Each is anchored in a set passage.

  • Preaching & miracles — Jesus gives the Twelve authority over unclean spirits and to heal, and sends them out to proclaim the kingdom without payment (Matt. 10: 1, 5–14).
  • Teaching, prayer, fellowship & breaking of bread — the first believers "devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching and fellowship, to the breaking of bread and the prayers" (Acts 2: 42).
  • Sharing — they held things in common, sold possessions and gave to any who had need, and ate together with glad and generous hearts (Acts 2: 44–47).
  • The Lord's Supper — Paul hands on what he received: on the night he was betrayed Jesus took bread, gave thanks, broke it, and said, "This is my body... do this in remembrance of me" (1 Cor. 11: 23–25).
  • Faith in action — "faith without works is dead": kind words do not feed a hungry neighbour (James 2: 14–17).
  • Character & hymn-singing — clothe yourselves with compassion, kindness, humility, patience and forgiveness; sing psalms, hymns and spiritual songs (Col. 3: 12–17).

Belief · the Apostles' Creed

The Apostles' Creed as a summary of belief

A creed (from the Latin credo, "I believe") is a short, agreed summary of faith. The Apostles' Creed is used across Catholic and Protestant churches, especially at baptism, and it is structured in three parts:

  • God the Father — almighty, creator of heaven and earth.
  • Jesus Christ — his only Son, conceived by the Holy Spirit, born of the Virgin Mary, suffered under Pontius Pilate, crucified, died and was buried; rose again on the third day; ascended; will come to judge the living and the dead.
  • The Holy Spirit — and then the church: the holy catholic (meaning universal) church, the communion of saints, the forgiveness of sins, the resurrection of the body and life everlasting.

Truth and meaning: the creed anchors belief in history ("under Pontius Pilate") and in hope ("life everlasting"). Reciting it together is an act of unity — but Christians differ over how literally some clauses should be read, and that is exactly the kind of debate CCEA rewards you for handling in an AO2 answer.

Christianity in Ireland · St Patrick

How Christianity came to Ireland

CCEA asks you to study the life and work of St Patrick (5th century). Unusually for the period, we have two documents written by Patrick himself: the Confessio (his spiritual autobiography and defence) and the Letter to the Soldiers of Coroticus.

  • Born in Roman Britain to a Christian family; by his own account he was not a serious believer as a boy.
  • Captured by raiders around the age of sixteen and taken to Ireland as a slave, working as a herdsman. In captivity he turned to prayer.
  • Escaped, returned home, trained for ministry, and then went back to Ireland as a missionary bishop — to the people who had enslaved him.
  • Work: baptising, ordaining clergy, encouraging monastic life, and challenging violence against Christians (the Coroticus letter). Armagh became the centre associated with him.

Handle the legends carefully. The shamrock explaining the Trinity, and driving the snakes out of Ireland, come from much later tradition, not from Patrick's own writings. A strong candidate distinguishes evidence from legend — that is the historian's skill, and CCEA examiners notice it.

The Reformation

How Protestantism emerged

For centuries the Western church was one visible institution. The Reformation of the 16th century split it.

  • 1517 — Martin Luther, a German monk and professor, published Ninety-Five Theses attacking the sale of indulgences (payments linked to the forgiveness of punishment for sin).
  • The Reformation slogans: sola scriptura (Scripture alone is the final authority), sola fide (justification by faith alone), sola gratia (by grace alone). Reformers argued salvation is God's gift, not something the church can sell.
  • Spread: the printing press put Bibles and pamphlets in ordinary hands; Zwingli in Zurich and Calvin in Geneva developed Reformed theology; in England the break with Rome came in the 1530s under Henry VIII.
  • Ireland: Presbyterianism grew strongly in Ulster through 17th-century settlement from Scotland; Methodism emerged later, from the 18th-century revival led by John Wesley.

Fairness matters. The Catholic Church also reformed itself in this period (the Council of Trent). GCSE answers that caricature either side lose AO2 marks; answers that explain what each side believed was at stake gain them.

Key leadership roles

Church structure, ordination and ministry

CCEA wants the Catholic Church and one Protestant church compared. We will use the Presbyterian Church as the worked Protestant example — your teacher may use another (Church of Ireland, Methodist, Baptist).

Structure

Catholic: a hierarchy — the Pope (Bishop of Rome), cardinals, archbishops and bishops (each leading a diocese), then parish priests and deacons. Authority runs downward and is understood as apostolic succession.

Presbyterian: government by elders — the congregation elects elders who, with the minister, form the Kirk Session; sessions send representatives to a Presbytery, and up to the General Assembly, which elects a Moderator for one year. There is no single head for life.

  • Ordination (Catholic) — the sacrament of Holy Orders. A bishop lays hands on the candidate; the priest is understood to act in persona Christi, especially at Mass and in absolution. In the Latin Church priests are celibate and are men.
  • Ordination (Protestant) — a solemn setting apart for ministry of Word and sacrament, usually by prayer and the laying on of hands by other ministers and elders. Most Protestants do not count it as a sacrament; ministers may marry, and many Protestant churches now ordain women.
  • The priest celebrates Mass, hears confession, anoints the sick, baptises, marries and buries, and leads the parish.
  • The minister or pastor preaches and teaches, leads worship and Communion, provides pastoral care, and shares oversight with elders — under the principle of the priesthood of all believers.

Christian worship

The Mass and a Protestant order of service

The Mass (Catholic). Its shape is fixed: Introductory Rites (greeting, penitential act, Gloria) → Liturgy of the Word (Old Testament reading, psalm, New Testament reading, Gospel, homily, Creed, intercessions) → Liturgy of the Eucharist (offering of gifts, Eucharistic Prayer, Lord's Prayer, sign of peace, Communion) → Concluding Rite (blessing and dismissal).

A Protestant order of service varies by denomination, but a typical Presbyterian or Methodist Sunday service runs: call to worship → hymn → prayer of approach and confession → Bible readings → hymn → sermon → prayers of intercession → offering → hymn → benediction. Communion is celebrated less frequently (often monthly or quarterly) and the sermon usually occupies the central place.

Styles of worship — the four CCEA bullets

Singing and music: Catholic worship uses hymns, chant and choirs; Protestant traditions range from metrical psalms and hymns to full contemporary worship bands.

Liturgical vs non-liturgical: liturgical worship follows a set written order (the Mass; the Book of Common Prayer); non-liturgical worship follows a flexible pattern chosen by the leader. Neither is "less serious" — they express different convictions about order and freedom.

Preaching: central in most Protestant worship (the pulpit is often physically central); present but shorter in the Mass, where the Eucharist is the climax.

The Bible in worship: read publicly in both; Catholics follow a set lectionary cycle; Protestant emphasis on sola scriptura gives the sermon its authority as exposition of the text.

Prayer

Types of prayer and their purpose

Christians describe prayer as communication with God. CCEA expects you to know types and purpose:

  • Adoration — praising God for who God is.
  • Confession — admitting sin and asking forgiveness.
  • Thanksgiving — gratitude for what has been given.
  • Supplication / petition — asking for one's own needs.
  • Intercession — praying for others: the sick, the bereaved, the world.

Prayer may be formal (set words such as the Lord's Prayer, or the Rosary in Catholic devotion) or extempore (spoken freely, a strong tradition in Protestant churches); public (led in worship) or private; spoken, silent or contemplative.

Purpose (AO2): believers say prayer is not a vending machine. Its purposes include relationship with God, formation of the person praying, and solidarity with those prayed for. A common objection — "unanswered prayer disproves God" — assumes prayer is mainly about getting things, which is precisely what most Christian teaching denies.

Architecture and furniture

Church buildings and what the furniture means

Buildings preach. Where a church puts its furniture tells you what it believes matters most.

  • Pulpit — the raised platform for preaching. Central and elevated in many Protestant churches: the Word is proclaimed.
  • Altar (Catholic) or communion table (Protestant) — where the Eucharist / Communion is celebrated. The word chosen is itself a theological claim: altar suggests sacrifice; table suggests a shared meal.
  • Lectern — the stand from which the Bible is read (often eagle-shaped, symbolising the gospel carried to the world).
  • Tabernacle — in a Catholic church, the locked, often veiled box where consecrated hosts are reserved; a lamp burns beside it. Catholics genuflect towards it because they believe Christ is truly present.
  • Baptismal font — a basin, usually near the entrance, for baptism by pouring, including infant baptism. A baptismal pool allows full immersion, used in churches that baptise believers on profession of faith (for example Baptist churches).

Compare: a Catholic church draws the eye to the altar and tabernacle; many Protestant churches draw it to the pulpit and open Bible. Same faith, different centre of gravity — and that is a ready-made evaluation point.

The church year

Festivals: meaning and purpose

The Christian year retells the life of Christ. CCEA names these:

  • Advent — four weeks of preparation and expectation before Christmas.
  • Christmas — the incarnation: God born as a human being in Jesus.
  • Epiphany — the visit of the wise men; Christ revealed to the nations.
  • Lent — forty days of self-examination, fasting and almsgiving before Easter.
  • Holy Week and Easter — the death and resurrection of Jesus; the heart of the whole year.
  • Ascension — Jesus taken up to heaven, forty days after Easter.
  • Pentecost — the gift of the Holy Spirit; the birthday of the church.
  • Saints' Days — remembering Christians whose lives are held up as examples (emphasised in Catholic practice).
  • Harvest — thanksgiving for the fruits of the earth, usually with gifts for those in need (widely kept in Protestant churches).

Purpose: festivals teach (they carry doctrine in story form), unite (the whole church keeps them together) and shape time (they mark the year by the gospel rather than by the calendar of work).

Sacraments and ordinances

Baptism and the Eucharist or Communion

A sacrament is usually defined as an outward, visible sign of an inward, invisible grace. The Catholic Church recognises seven; most Protestant churches recognise two (baptism and Communion) because they hold that only these two were plainly instituted by Christ — some prefer the word ordinance to stress obedience to Christ's command rather than a channel of grace.

Baptism

Catholic: infants are usually baptised. Water is poured three times with the words "I baptise you in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit." Symbols: water (cleansing and new life), white garment (purity), candle lit from the paschal candle (the light of Christ), oil (strengthening). Godparents promise to help raise the child in the faith.

Protestant: Presbyterians, Methodists and Anglicans also baptise infants, with parents professing faith on their behalf. Baptists and Pentecostals baptise believers only, by full immersion, as a public declaration of a personal decision — arguing that the New Testament shows only believers being baptised.

Eucharist / Communion

All trace it to 1 Cor. 11: 23–25 and the Last Supper. The difference is what the bread and wine are:

Catholic: transubstantiation — the substance truly becomes the body and blood of Christ, though the appearances of bread and wine remain. The Mass is a re-presentation of Christ's one sacrifice.

Reformed / Presbyterian: the elements remain bread and wine, but Christ is truly received spiritually by faith. Baptist and many evangelical churches: the bread and wine are a memorial, obeying "do this in remembrance of me". Lutherans hold Christ is present in, with and under the bread and wine.

The church in contemporary society

Community, cohesion and challenge

Contribution to the local community: churches run food banks, debt advice, youth clubs, parent and toddler groups, night shelters, addiction support and lunch clubs for older people; church schools educate; clergy conduct the rites that mark birth, marriage and death. In Northern Ireland, cross-community initiatives — shared worship, joint youth work, mediation and peace-building — have been a deliberate contribution to community cohesion.

The challenge of changing moral and social trends: churches face falling attendance and a more secular culture; deep internal disagreement over sexuality, marriage and the role of women; the collapse of trust caused by abuse scandals and their cover-up; and the question of how to speak into a society that no longer assumes Christian belief.

AO2 — how examiners want the argument built

One view: the church must adapt — if it does not change, it will lose the next generation; the church has changed before (on slavery, on the vernacular Bible) and was right to.

Another view: the church must hold firm — a church that simply follows public opinion has nothing distinctive to offer, and its authority is Scripture and tradition, not the opinion poll.

A third view: the real test is not doctrine but integrity — the church regains a hearing by being honest about failure and generous in service.

Your job: state the views, weigh them with reasons and evidence, and reach a justified conclusion. That structure is worth more than any amount of assertion.
