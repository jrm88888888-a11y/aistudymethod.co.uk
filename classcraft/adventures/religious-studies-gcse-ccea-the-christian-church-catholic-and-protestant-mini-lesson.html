Mission 1

Who is the Holy Spirit?

  • Christians believe the Holy Spirit is the third Person of the Trinity: fully God, with the Father and the Son.
  • Christians see the Spirit at work in the Old Testament: "God's Spirit" hovers over the waters at creation (Genesis 1:2) and inspires prophets.
Mission 1Read the source

Pentecost (Acts 2:1–8)

  • Fifty days after Passover, the apostles are together in one house in Jerusalem (Acts 2:1).
  • "Suddenly there came from the sky a sound like the rushing of a mighty wind, and it filled all the house where they were sitting." (Acts 2:2)
  • "Tongues like fire appeared and were distributed to them, and one sat on each of them." (Acts 2:3)
  • They are filled with the Holy Spirit and begin to speak in other languages (Acts 2:4).
  • Jews from many nations, in Jerusalem for the festival, each hear them in their own language, and are amazed (Acts 2:5–8, paraphrase).

Q1 What two signs come before the apostles speak?

Show answer

A sound like a mighty wind; tongues like fire.

Q2 Why are the crowds amazed?

Show answer

Each hears the apostles speaking in his own native language.

Mission 1

What Pentecost means

  • Christians draw these meanings from Pentecost:
  • The promise kept: the Spirit Jesus promised has come.
  • Wind: in Hebrew and Greek, one word means both "wind/breath" and "spirit". God's breath gives life (Genesis 2:7).
  • Fire: God's presence (the burning bush, Exodus 3:2); purifying and giving energy.
  • Languages: the good news is for every nation; many see the confusion of languages at Babel (Genesis 11) undone.
  • Courage: the frightened disciples now preach in public. Peter's sermon leads about 3,000 to be baptised (Acts 2:41).
  • The birthday of the Church: the Church begins its mission.
  • The Jewish festival of Shavuot (Weeks), 50 days after Passover, is the setting; the Greek name "Pentecost" means "fiftieth".

Mission 1

God's power given to believers

  • Christians believe the Spirit lives in every believer, not only in the apostles.
  • The Spirit guides into truth (John 16:13), helps in prayer (Romans 8:26) and gives courage to witness.
  • Significance: Christians pray "Come, Holy Spirit" before decisions, worship and study.
Mission 1

The gifts of the Spirit

  • Gifts: abilities the Spirit gives for serving others and building up the Church.
  • "But to each one is given the manifestation of the Spirit for the profit of all." (1 Corinthians 12:7)
  • 1 Corinthians 12:8–10: wisdom, knowledge, faith, healing, miracles, prophecy, telling spirits apart, speaking in tongues, interpreting tongues.
  • Speaking in tongues (glossolalia): praying or praising in a language the speaker has not learned. Paul says it needs interpreting in public worship (1 Corinthians 14:27–28).
  • Fruit = character; gifts = abilities for service.
Mission 1Diversity

The Spirit in worship

  • Pentecostal and charismatic Evangelical churches: lively, free worship; raised hands; spontaneous prayer; speaking in tongues, prophecy and prayer for healing. Modern Pentecostalism grew from the Azusa Street revival (Los Angeles, 1906).
  • Some other Protestants (cessationists) believe the sign-gifts such as tongues ended with the apostles. Paul: "Let all things be done decently and in order" (1 Corinthians 14:40).
  • Catholic, Orthodox and Anglican liturgy: the Spirit is invoked in set prayers, for example over the bread and wine. The Catholic Charismatic Renewal (from 1967) brought charismatic worship into some Catholic parishes.
  • Quakers: sit in silence until the Spirit moves someone to speak.
  • Tongues may be used by a whole congregation together, or by one person followed by an interpretation.

Mission 1Evaluate

"Speaking in tongues is the clearest sign of the Holy Spirit."

  • Reasons for: at Pentecost, tongues were the visible sign of the Spirit (Acts 2:4; also Acts 10:46, 19:6). Many Pentecostal churches teach tongues as the first evidence of baptism in the Spirit. It is a gift people can see and hear.
  • Reasons against: Paul asks "Do all speak with various languages?" and expects the answer no (1 Corinthians 12:30). Love is greater than tongues (1 Corinthians 13:1). Jesus: "By their fruits you will know them" (Matthew 7:20): character, not experience. Cessationists hold that the gift has ended.
  • Another view: Catholic and Orthodox Christians see the Spirit most clearly in the sacraments and in holy lives (saints).
  • Judge it by: Does Acts describe what happened then, or set a pattern for always? Is a sign the same as proof? Which matters more: gifts or fruit?

Pentecost turned frightened followers into bold witnesses. Christians believe the same Spirit gives power, gifts and a changed character today.

Mission 2

The Church is people

  • In the New Testament, "church" translates the Greek ekklesia: an assembly, people called together.
  • The first Christians had no church buildings. They met in homes and in the Temple courts (Acts 2:46).
  • So, for Christians, the Church (capital C) is the community of everyone who follows Jesus.
  • A church (small c) is a building, or one local congregation.
  • Two pictures from the Bible describe this community: the Body of Christ and the People of God.
Mission 2Read the source

One body, many members

"For as the body is one and has many members, and all the members of the body, being many, are one body; so also is Christ." (1 Corinthians 12:12)

  • Paul wrote to Christians in Corinth who were divided and competing over whose gifts mattered most.
  • The foot cannot say it is not part of the body because it is not a hand; the ear cannot leave because it is not an eye (12:15–16, paraphrase).
  • "The eye can't tell the hand, 'I have no need for you'" (12:21). The weaker-looking parts are necessary (12:22).
  • "When one member suffers, all the members suffer with it. When one member is honoured, all the members rejoice with it." (12:26)
  • "Now you are the body of Christ, and members individually." (12:27)

Q1 What two mistakes does Paul warn against?

Show answer

Thinking you don't belong; thinking others aren't needed.

Q2 What should happen when one member suffers?

Show answer

All share it, and care for that member: 12:25–26.

Mission 2

Many gifts, one Spirit

  • 1 Corinthians 12:1–11: the Holy Spirit gives each Christian gifts (Greek charismata).
  • Paul's list: wisdom, knowledge, faith, healing, miracles, prophecy, telling spirits apart, tongues and their interpretation.
  • Each gift is given "for the profit of all" (12:7): for building up the community, not for showing off.
  • The same Spirit gives them all (12:4, 11). No gift makes someone a better Christian.
  • So the body works because members are different, not in spite of it.
  • Paul's point to Corinth: unity is not sameness; division and pride damage the whole body.

Mission 2

The body in action: Acts 2

  • After Pentecost, the first Christians "continued steadfastly in the apostles' teaching and fellowship, in the breaking of bread, and prayer." (Acts 2:42)
  • "All who believed were together, and had all things in common." (Acts 2:44)
  • They sold possessions and shared "according as anyone had need" (2:45).
  • They met daily, ate together "with gladness", and praised God (2:46–47).
  • Here the body is visible: teaching, fellowship, the Lord's Supper, prayer, and sharing. (The six characteristics of the early Church: a later mission.)
Mission 2Diversity

What is "the Body"?

  • All Christians: Christ is the head; believers are the members; the Spirit unites them.
  • Catholic: the Church is a visible, ordered community; the one Church of Christ "subsists in" the Catholic Church, led by the pope and bishops (Lumen Gentium 8, paraphrase). Elements of holiness are found in other Churches too.
  • Orthodox: the Church is most fully the Body when it gathers for the Divine Liturgy around its bishop.
  • Many Protestants: the true Body is all genuine believers everywhere, known fully to God (the invisible Church), seen in local congregations.

Mission 2Evaluate

"The Church is the people, not the building."

  • Reasons for: ekklesia means assembly; the early Church met in homes (Acts 2:46); "where two or three are gathered together in my name, there I am" (Matthew 18:20); the Body of Christ is made of people (1 Corinthians 12:27).
  • Reasons against (the building matters too): it is the centre of worship and of the local community; Catholics and Orthodox believe Christ is present in the Eucharist and the tabernacle there; consecrated buildings, icons and altars help people pray; a visible building witnesses to the area.
  • Another view: "people and building": the building serves the people and expresses their faith.
  • Judge it by: Could the Church survive without buildings (history says it once did)? What is lost without a sacred space? Does the answer differ between denominations?

Mission 3

Six marks of the first Christians

  • After Pentecost (Acts 2), the first followers of Jesus formed communities in Jerusalem and beyond.
  • The New Testament shows six characteristics of this early Church:
  • Preaching · teaching · prayer · miracles · hymn-singing · sharing.
  • Each one has a source in the New Testament (next screens).
  • Many Christians still look to these six as a pattern for church life today.
Mission 3

Preaching and miracles: Matthew 10

  • Jesus sends out the twelve apostles and gives them authority over unclean spirits and to heal every illness (Matthew 10:1, paraphrase).
  • Preaching: "As you go, preach, saying, 'The Kingdom of Heaven is at hand!'" (Matthew 10:7)
  • Miracles: they are to heal the sick and cast out demons (10:8, paraphrase).
  • "Freely you received, so freely give." (10:8) They take no money and travel light (10:9–10).
  • If a town will not listen, they shake its dust off their feet and move on (10:14).
  • After Pentecost, Peter preaches to the crowd (Acts 2:14–41) and "many wonders and signs were done through the apostles" (Acts 2:43).
Mission 3Read the source

Acts 2:42–47

"They continued steadfastly in the apostles' teaching and fellowship, in the breaking of bread, and prayer." (Acts 2:42)

  • "All who believed were together, and had all things in common." (2:44)
  • They sold possessions and gave "according as anyone had need" (2:45).
  • They met every day in the Temple, broke bread in their homes, and ate "with gladness and singleness of heart" (2:46).
  • They were "praising God", and "the Lord added to the assembly day by day those who were being saved" (2:47).

Q1 Find three of the six characteristics in these verses.

Show answer

Teaching, prayer, sharing; praising God points to hymn-singing.

Q2 What happened to the size of the community?

Show answer

It grew every day.

Mission 3

Breaking bread, faith in action, songs

  • Breaking of bread: Paul hands on what Jesus did at the Last Supper: bread and cup, "Do this in memory of me" (1 Corinthians 11:23–25). Sharing this meal marked the early Church.
  • Sharing as faith in action: if a brother or sister has no clothes or food, warm words are not enough; "faith, if it has no works, is dead" (James 2:14–17).
  • Hymn-singing and teaching: Christians are to teach and encourage each other, with "psalms, hymns, and spiritual songs", "singing with grace in your heart to the Lord" (Colossians 3:16).
  • Living it out: put on compassion, kindness, humility, patience; forgive as the Lord forgave you; above all, love (Colossians 3:12–14, paraphrase).

Mission 3

Before Patrick

  • Christianity spread across the Roman Empire, including Britain, by the 4th century.
  • Ireland was never part of the Empire, but there was trade and raiding across the Irish Sea.
  • Some Irish people were already Christian before Patrick.
  • In 431, Pope Celestine I sent a bishop, Palladius, "to the Irish believing in Christ" (Prosper of Aquitaine, a chronicler of the time).
  • So Patrick did not bring the very first Christians to Ireland, but his mission spread the faith widely.
Mission 3

Patrick: from captive to bishop

  • Born in Roman Britain in the 5th century; his father was a deacon and his grandfather a priest.
  • At about 16, he was captured by Irish raiders and taken to Ireland as a slave, herding animals for about six years.
  • In captivity he turned to God and prayed many times a day and night.
  • A voice in a dream told him a ship was ready. He escaped, travelled a long way to the coast, and got home.
  • Later he dreamed of the Irish calling him: "we ask you, holy boy, to come and walk among us again" (Confession 23, paraphrase).
  • He was ordained, and later returned to Ireland as a bishop.
Mission 3Read the source

Patrick's own words

Patrick wrote two short Latin works: the Confession and the Letter to the Soldiers of Coroticus. In paraphrase:

  • He calls himself a sinner, unlearned, "the least of all the faithful".
  • He gives thanks that God watched over him in slavery and called him back to the people who had enslaved him.
  • He says many thousands were baptised and he ordained clergy for them.
  • He refused gifts from converts, so no one could say he preached for money.
  • In the Letter, he condemns British soldiers who killed and enslaved newly baptised Irish Christians.

Q1 Why did Patrick refuse gifts?

Show answer

So his mission could not be accused of greed; compare Matthew 10:8.

Q2 What did he do for the people he baptised?

Show answer

Ordained clergy to lead and care for them.

Mission 3

Patrick's work

  • Preaching widely, even in remote places, often in danger; he was seized and held captive more than once.
  • Baptising thousands and ordaining priests, so the Church could continue after him.
  • Working through local kings and chiefs: their support opened whole regions.
  • Encouraging monastic life: sons and daughters of chiefs became monks and nuns.
  • Tradition links him with Saul (his first church, in a barn given by the chief Dichu), Armagh (his main church) and Downpatrick (his burial place), all in the north.
  • Legacy: within two centuries Ireland's monasteries were sending missionaries to Scotland, England and Europe (e.g. Columba to Iona, 563).

Mission 3Diversity

History, legend and a shared saint

  • History: Patrick's own two writings are the most reliable sources. Dates are uncertain (tradition: arrival 432; death 461 or 493).
  • Later stories: Lives written about 200 years later (Muirchú, Tírechán) add the Paschal fire at Slane defying King Laoghaire.
  • Legends: the shamrock to teach the Trinity (first recorded much later) and driving out snakes (Ireland had none after the Ice Age).
  • Shared saint: Catholics and Protestants both honour Patrick. Armagh has two St Patrick's cathedrals: one Catholic, one Church of Ireland.
  • Catholics stress continuity with Rome and Patrick as a bishop; many Protestants stress his love of Scripture and personal faith, seen in the Confession.
  • His feast day, 17 March, is kept by Christians of many traditions.
Mission 3Evaluate

"The Church today should live like the early Church in Acts 2."

  • Reasons for: the apostles were closest to Jesus; Acts 2 shows teaching, prayer, worship and sharing in balance; James 2: faith must be lived; Patrick's mission followed the same pattern and the Church grew.
  • Reasons against: having "all things in common" suited a small community, not a worldwide Church; churches today do share through charities and welfare; the early Church had its own quarrels (1 Corinthians 1:10–12); later practices like creeds and church buildings also serve the faith.
  • Another view: copy the spirit (devotion, generosity, joy), not every detail.
  • Judge it by: Which of the six characteristics are practical today? Is the pattern a command or an example? What do different denominations take from Acts 2?

Mission 4

What a creed is

  • A creed is a short statement of shared belief. From Latin credo: "I believe".
  • It sums up what a church teaches about God, Jesus and salvation.
  • Said aloud together, it shows who belongs: "this is what we believe".
  • The two most used: the Apostles' Creed and the Nicene Creed.
  • Both are built in three parts: Father, Son, Holy Spirit.
Mission 4

Where the Apostles' Creed came from

  • It grew from the questions asked of new Christians at baptism in Rome: "Do you believe in God the Father…?"
  • An early Roman form was written down by about 340.
  • Its final form was settled in Western Europe by about the 8th century.
  • A legend says each of the twelve apostles wrote one line. Historians see it as a summary of the apostles' teaching, not their words.
  • It is used mainly in Western churches: Catholic, Anglican, Lutheran, Methodist, Reformed.
Mission 4Read the source

The Apostles' Creed

"I believe in God the Father Almighty, Maker of heaven and earth:

And in Jesus Christ his only Son our Lord, Who was conceived by the Holy Ghost, Born of the Virgin Mary, Suffered under Pontius Pilate, Was crucified, dead, and buried, He descended into hell; The third day he rose again from the dead, He ascended into heaven, And sitteth on the right hand of God the Father Almighty; From thence he shall come to judge the quick and the dead.

I believe in the Holy Ghost; The holy catholic Church; The Communion of Saints; The Forgiveness of sins; The Resurrection of the body, And the life everlasting. Amen." (Book of Common Prayer, 1662)

  • Old words: Ghost = Spirit; quick = living; sitteth = sits.

Show answer

Q2 Which line ties the Creed to a real moment in history?

Show answer

Mission 4

What the Creed affirms

  • Father: Almighty Creator of everything.
  • Son: Jesus is God's Son and Lord; born as a human (Incarnation); died, rose, ascended; will return as judge.
  • "Descended into hell": went to the place of the dead, between his death and resurrection.
  • Holy Spirit: God at work now.
  • "Holy catholic Church": catholic means universal: all Christians everywhere, not one denomination.
  • "Communion of saints": all believers, living and dead, united in Christ.
  • Ends with hope: forgiveness, resurrection of the body, life everlasting.

Mission 4

Creeds in Christian life

  • Baptism and confirmation: candidates (or parents and godparents) answer questions drawn from the Apostles' Creed.
  • Sunday worship: the Nicene Creed at Mass or Eucharist; the Apostles' Creed at Anglican Morning and Evening Prayer.
  • Catholic prayer: the Rosary begins with the Apostles' Creed.
  • Unity: Christians of different churches can say the same words together.
  • Daily life: "forgiveness of sins" → forgive others; "Maker of heaven and earth" → care for creation; "he shall come to judge" → live responsibly; "resurrection of the body" → hope at funerals.
  • Truth and meaning: believers see the Creed as stating what is true, and as giving life its purpose.
Mission 4Diversity

Different views on creeds

  • Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican, Lutheran, Methodist: creeds are central, trusted summaries of the faith.
  • Orthodox: use the Nicene Creed only, without the Western words "and the Son" about the Spirit.
  • Many Baptist, Pentecostal and independent evangelical churches: hold the same core beliefs, but stress the Bible itself; many rarely recite creeds.
  • Quakers: no creeds; they stress the inward experience of God and fear set words can replace living faith.
  • Unitarians: no creeds; they reject the Trinity.
  • Some liberal Christians: say the creeds, but read some lines (e.g. the virgin birth) as symbols.

Mission 4Evaluate

"The Apostles' Creed is a complete summary of Christian belief."

  • Reasons for: covers God as Trinity, Jesus' birth, death, resurrection, ascension and return, the Church, forgiveness and eternal life. Shared by most Western churches for centuries. Short enough to learn by heart.
  • Reasons against: jumps from "born" to "suffered": nothing on Jesus' teaching, miracles or love of neighbour. Says little about how to live. No mention of the Bible or the Eucharist. Does not say how the three Persons are one (the Nicene Creed goes further). Orthodox and Quakers do not use it.
  • Judge it by: Is a summary meant to be complete, or only the essentials? Can belief be separated from how Christians live? Should "Christian belief" mean what all Christians agree on, or what most do?
Mission 4Worked answer

Explaining a creed's importance

Question: Explain why the Apostles' Creed is important for Christians.

  • Point 1: It sums up core beliefs in one short text. Development: a new Christian at baptism can say, in a few lines, what they believe about the Father, Son and Spirit.
  • Point 2: It unites Christians. Development: Catholics, Anglicans and Methodists can say the same words together, showing one faith across different churches.
  • Point 3: It guides daily life. Development: "the Forgiveness of sins" reminds believers that they are forgiven and must forgive others.

A creed is a shared, three-part map of belief; that is why the same old words still unite Christians today.

Mission 5

One Church, many branches

  • For about 1,000 years most Christians belonged to one Church, though with growing differences between East and West.
  • 1054: the Great Schism splits it into the Orthodox Church (East) and the Catholic Church (West).
  • 1517 onwards: the Reformation splits the Western Church: Protestant Churches form.
  • 1906 onwards: Pentecostal Churches grow fast, and charismatic renewal spreads through older Churches from the 1960s.
  • Each family of Churches is called a denomination (or group of denominations).
  • Nearly all still believe in one God as Trinity, in Jesus as Son of God and Saviour, and in the Bible.
Mission 5

Why the Reformation happened

  • Abuses: some clergy were poorly educated or corrupt; Church posts were bought and sold.
  • Indulgences: the Church taught they reduced the punishment still due for sins already forgiven (including in purgatory); in 1517 they were being sold to raise money for St Peter's in Rome.
  • Printing press (Gutenberg, about 1450): new ideas and Bibles spread fast and cheaply.
  • Renaissance scholars went back to the Bible in its original languages (Erasmus' Greek New Testament, 1516).
  • Rulers wanted more control over the Church in their lands.
  • Earlier reformers had been silenced: John Wycliffe in England; Jan Hus, burned in 1415.
Mission 5

Martin Luther

  • Martin Luther (1483–1546): German monk and professor of the Bible at Wittenberg.
  • Studying Romans, he concluded that God makes sinners right with him as a free gift, received by faith (Romans 1:17).
  • 31 October 1517: his 95 Theses challenge the sale of indulgences.
  • 1521: excommunicated by the pope; at the Diet of Worms he refuses to take back his writings unless shown wrong from Scripture.
  • Translates the New Testament into German (1522), so ordinary people can read it.
  • Marries a former nun, Katharina von Bora (1525); writes hymns and catechisms for families.
Mission 5Read the source

What Luther taught

Three "alones" sum up the Reformers' teaching (paraphrase):

  • Scripture alone (sola scriptura): the Bible is the final authority, above popes and councils.
  • Grace alone (sola gratia): salvation is God's free gift, not earned.
  • Faith alone (sola fide): people are made right with God by trusting Christ, not by good works or indulgences. "The righteous shall live by faith" (Romans 1:17).
  • Priesthood of all believers: every Christian can come to God directly through Christ (1 Peter 2:9).
  • Most Reformers taught that Christ instituted only two sacraments: baptism and the Lord's Supper.

Q1 Why did "faith alone" make selling indulgences pointless?

Show answer

If people are put right with God by faith as a free gift, nothing paid for can add to it.

Q2 Why did "Scripture alone" lead Luther to translate the Bible?

Show answer

If the Bible is the final authority, everyone should be able to read it.

Mission 5

Protestantism spreads

  • Switzerland: Zwingli in Zurich; John Calvin in Geneva (Institutes, 1536): God's sovereignty; churches led by ministers and elders.
  • England: Henry VIII breaks with Rome over his marriage (Act of Supremacy, 1534); the Church of England becomes Protestant under Edward VI and Elizabeth I, with Cranmer's Book of Common Prayer.
  • Scotland: John Knox; the Presbyterian Church of Scotland (1560).
  • Ireland: Henry VIII is made head of the Church in Ireland (1530s); the state Church of Ireland becomes Protestant, though most people stay Catholic; Scottish settlers bring Presbyterianism to Ulster in the 1600s.
  • Later: Baptists (1600s), Methodists (John Wesley, 1700s).
  • Catholic response: the Council of Trent (1545–1563) reforms abuses, ends the sale of indulgences, and reaffirms seven sacraments and Scripture with Tradition.

Mission 5Evaluate

"Luther's Reformation still matters to Christians today."

  • Reasons for: Bibles in everyday languages; "faith alone" is central to Protestant preaching worldwide; the priesthood of all believers shaped lay leadership; his hymns and catechism are still used; Protestant and Pentecostal Churches worldwide grew from the Reformation.
  • Reasons against: the sale of indulgences ended long ago (Council of Trent); in 1999 Catholics and Lutherans signed a Joint Declaration on Justification, agreeing on its basics; many Lutheran Churches have formally rejected his later writings against Jews.
  • Another view: his questions still matter (authority, grace, the Bible), even where his answers or his tone do not.
  • Judge it by: Relevant to which Christians? The man or the ideas? Could reform have happened without division?

Mission 6

Laity and clergy

  • Laity (from Greek laos, "people"): all Christians who are not ordained. Nearly every Christian is a lay person.
  • Clergy: Christians set apart, usually by ordination, to lead, teach and care for others.
  • Titles vary: priest (Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican), vicar or rector (Anglican parish), minister (Presbyterian, Methodist), pastor (Baptist, Pentecostal).
  • Some Churches also have bishops; the Catholic Church has a pope.
  • Christians disagree about what ordination is and whether it is needed at all.
Mission 6

The Catholic hierarchy

Catholics believe:

  • Pope: the Bishop of Rome, successor of St Peter (Matthew 16:18–19); visible head of the Church on earth; final teaching authority.
  • Cardinals: senior clergy who advise the pope and elect a new one.
  • Bishops (archbishops lead groups of dioceses): successors of the apostles; each leads a diocese; together with the pope they form the college of bishops and teach with authority (the magisterium).
  • Priests: lead parishes on the bishop's behalf.
  • Deacons: serve, preach, baptise, witness marriages and lead funerals; permanent deacons may be married men.
  • Laity: the People of God in the world (Lumen Gentium chapter 4).
  • Apostolic succession: the Catholic belief (shared by the Orthodox and many Anglicans) that bishops stand in an unbroken line from the apostles, passed on by ordination.
Mission 6

Catholic ordination and the priest

  • Holy Orders is one of the seven sacraments, in three degrees: deacon, priest, bishop.
  • Only a bishop ordains, by the laying on of hands and a prayer of consecration; a new priest's hands are anointed with chrism.
  • Catholics believe ordination leaves a permanent spiritual mark: "once a priest, always a priest".
  • The priest acts in the person of Christ (in persona Christi) when he celebrates the sacraments.
  • Role: celebrate Mass, hear confessions, baptise, marry, anoint the sick, preach, care for the parish.
  • In the Latin (Western) Catholic Church, priests are normally celibate; Catholic priests are men.

Mission 6

Protestant structures

  • Presbyterian (e.g. Presbyterian Church in Ireland): no bishops. Each congregation is led by a minister and elders (the Kirk Session); congregations form a Presbytery; the General Assembly meets each year, chaired by a Moderator elected for one year.
  • Anglican (Church of Ireland, Church of England): bishops, priests and deacons; archbishops (Armagh and Dublin in the Church of Ireland; Canterbury and York in England); a synod of bishops, clergy and laity.
  • Methodist: an annual Conference with an elected President; circuits of churches, with ministers and lay local preachers.
  • Baptist: each congregation governs itself and calls its own pastor.
Mission 6

Protestant ordination and the minister

  • Ordination means being set apart by the Church for ministry, after a call from God and from a congregation.
  • Presbyterian: after training, a minister is ordained by the Presbytery with prayer and the laying on of hands.
  • For most Protestants ordination is not a sacrament: there are two sacraments (baptism and the Lord's Supper).
  • The minister is not a different kind of Christian; he or she is a servant (Latin minister) of the congregation.
  • Role: preach and teach the Bible (central), lead worship, lead baptism and the Lord's Supper, pastoral care, weddings and funerals, lead with the elders.
  • Many Protestant Churches ordain women as ministers.
Mission 6

The priesthood of all believers

  • Taught by Martin Luther (To the Christian Nobility of the German Nation, 1520).
  • "You are a chosen race, a royal priesthood" (1 Peter 2:9): this is said of all believers.
  • Jesus is the one great high priest (Hebrews 4:14–16), so every Christian can come to God directly, without a human go-between.
  • Every Christian can pray, read the Bible, confess sins to God and serve; every job can be a calling.
  • Ministers are still needed for order and teaching, not because they are closer to God.
  • Catholics and Orthodox also pray directly to God; the priest's distinctive role is in the sacraments.
  • Catholic teaching also speaks of a "common priesthood" of all the baptised, but says it differs in kind from the ordained priesthood (Lumen Gentium 10).

Mission 6

Churches without clergy

  • Quakers (Religious Society of Friends): no clergy; meetings for worship in silence; anyone may speak if moved by the Spirit.
  • Brethren assemblies: no ordained ministers; members share preaching and leading.
  • Reasons:
  • Priesthood of all believers: every Christian has direct access to God.
  • Gifts of the Spirit are given to all (1 Corinthians 12; 14:26: "each one of you has a psalm, has a teaching…").
  • Jesus is the one mediator between God and people (1 Timothy 2:5).
  • "Where two or three are gathered together in my name, there I am" (Matthew 18:20): no priest needed for Christ to be present.
  • Concern that a paid, separate clergy can create a hierarchy Jesus did not intend.
Quick check
?Why do Quakers have no ordained clergy?
Mission 6Evaluate

"Christians need ordained leaders."

  • Reasons for: Christ gives leaders to the Church (Ephesians 4:11–12); for Catholics and Orthodox only a priest can celebrate the Eucharist and absolve sins; trained leaders guard against false teaching; worship should be "decently and in order" (1 Corinthians 14:40).
  • Reasons against: the priesthood of all believers (1 Peter 2:9); Jesus the one mediator (1 Timothy 2:5); Quakers and Brethren (Churches with no ordained clergy) have worshipped for centuries without clergy; the Spirit gives gifts to everyone (1 Corinthians 12); clergy can be set too far above the laity.
  • Another view: leaders are needed, but as servants of the whole body (Mark 10:43–45), not as a separate class.
  • Judge it by: Needed for what: sacraments, teaching or organisation? Is Bible or Tradition the authority here? Does the answer change between denominations?
Mission check

?A group of Christians from different Churches spends six months at a remote research station with no priest or minister. Which statement about their Sunday worship is most accurate?

Catholics believe ordained priests are needed for the sacraments; Protestants stress the priesthood of all believers; Quakers and Brethren have no ordained clergy.

Mission 7

Scripture, Tradition, Magisterium

  • Scripture: the Bible, the written Word of God.
  • Tradition: the faith handed on by the apostles in the Church's life, creeds, worship and teaching: "hold the traditions which you were taught by us, whether by word or by letter" (2 Thessalonians 2:15).
  • Magisterium (Latin magister, teacher): the Church's living teaching office.
  • Catholic teaching: the three are so joined that none stands without the others (Dei Verbum 10, paraphrase).
  • The Magisterium is not above the Word of God; it serves it.
Mission 7

The living teaching office

  • Who: the pope and the bishops in communion with him (CCC 100, paraphrase).
  • Function: to interpret Scripture and Tradition authentically, and apply them to new questions.
  • Why "living": new questions (medicine, technology, the economy) need answers faithful to the old truths.
  • Importance: it keeps Catholics united in one faith across the world and the centuries.
  • Catholics believe the Holy Spirit guides it: "he will guide you into all truth" (John 16:13).
Mission 7

Apostolic succession

  • Jesus to Peter: "you are Peter, and on this rock I will build my assembly" (Matthew 16:18). "Feed my sheep." (John 21:17)
  • To the apostles: "Whoever listens to you listens to me" (Luke 10:16).
  • Catholics believe the apostles appointed successors by the laying on of hands: "commit the same things to faithful men who will be able to teach others also" (2 Timothy 2:2).
  • Catholics believe bishops are the successors of the apostles, and the pope, Bishop of Rome, is the successor of Peter.
  • So, Catholics believe, their teaching authority comes from Christ, through an unbroken line.
Mission 7

The hierarchy and how it consults

  • Pope: Bishop of Rome; head of the College of Bishops; chief teacher and pastor.
  • Cardinals: senior clergy who advise the pope; those under 80 elect a new pope in a conclave.
  • Bishops lead dioceses; archbishops lead groups of dioceses (in Ireland, the Archbishop of Armagh is Primate of All Ireland).
  • Priests lead parishes, helped by deacons; the laity are all the baptised who are not ordained.
  • Consultative: bishops share responsibility with the pope (collegiality, Vatican II); the Synod of Bishops (since 1965); national bishops' conferences; diocesan and parish councils with lay members.
  • The Synod on Synodality (2021–2024) consulted parishes worldwide, and lay men and women voted in its assemblies.

Mission 7

Conciliar and pontifical

  • Conciliar: teaching given by the bishops of the world gathered in an ecumenical council with the pope.
  • Model: the Council of Jerusalem: "It seemed good to the Holy Spirit, and to us" (Acts 15:28).
  • The Catholic Church counts 21 ecumenical councils, from Nicaea (325) to the Second Vatican Council (1962–65).
  • Pontifical (from pontifex, a title of the pope): teaching given by the pope himself.
  • Forms: encyclicals (letters to the whole Church, e.g. Laudato Si', 2015, on care for creation), apostolic exhortations (e.g. Evangelii Gaudium, 2013).
  • Catholics believe both carry authority because both are the voice of the successors of the apostles.
Quick check
?In 1965, bishops from around the world, meeting with the pope at the Second Vatican Council, approved a document on the Church in the modern world. Which kind of magisterium is this?
Mission 7Evaluate

"The Magisterium is the most important source of authority for Catholics."

  • For: CCC 100 gives it alone the task of authentic interpretation. Without it, people read the Bible in conflicting ways. Its authority comes from Christ through the apostles (Luke 10:16). It answers new questions Scripture does not address directly.
  • Against: it is not above the Word of God but serves it (Dei Verbum 10): Scripture is primary. Conscience is where God's voice echoes within each person (Gaudium et Spes 16, paraphrase). The three sources depend on each other, so none is "most" important.
  • Other Christians: most Protestants hold Scripture alone as the final authority (Luther, 1521: unless convinced by Scripture and plain reason). The Orthodox accept councils and Tradition but not the pope's universal authority.
  • Judge it by: Can authority be ranked if the sources depend on each other? What prevents division? What role is left for conscience?
Mission check

?A new technology raises a moral question that the Bible never mentions. According to Catholic teaching, how would the Church give authoritative guidance on it?

For Catholics, authority is not one person's opinion: the pope and bishops, as successors of the apostles, serve and interpret the Word handed on to them.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Christians believe the Holy Spirit is the third Person of the Trinity, God present and active on Earth, promised by Jesus.
  • Acts 2 tells how, at Pentecost, wind, fire and many languages marked the Spirit's coming and the start of the Church's mission.
  • Christians differ on how the Spirit shows itself: tongues and gifts in Pentecostal worship, sacraments and liturgy in Catholic and Orthodox worship, silence for Quakers.

Mission 2

  • The Church (ekklesia) is the community of Christians; a church building is where they gather.
  • 1 Corinthians 12: one body, many members, many gifts from one Spirit; every member needed, all share suffering and joy.

Mission 3

  • The early Church was marked by preaching, teaching, prayer, miracles, hymn-singing and sharing (Matthew 10; Acts 2:42–47; 1 Cor 11:23–25; James 2:14–17; Colossians 3:12–17).
  • Patrick, a Briton enslaved in Ireland as a teenager, returned as a bishop, preached, baptised and ordained clergy; his own writings are the best sources.
  • Catholics and Protestants both honour Patrick; later legends (shamrock, snakes) must be told apart from history.

Mission 4

  • A creed is a short statement of shared belief; the Apostles' Creed grew from baptism questions in Rome and has three parts: Father, Son, Holy Spirit.
  • Creeds are used at baptism, in worship and prayer; most churches value them, while Quakers and Unitarians do not use them.

Mission 5

  • 1517 onwards: Luther's Reformation (Scripture alone, grace alone, faith alone, priesthood of all believers) gave rise to Protestant Churches.
  • Catholics answered with reform at the Council of Trent (1545–1563).

Mission 6

  • Catholic hierarchy: pope, bishops, priests, deacons, laity; Catholics believe ordination is a sacrament given by a bishop; the priest celebrates Mass and the sacraments.
  • Protestant Churches are organised differently (e.g. Presbyterian: minister and elders, Presbytery, General Assembly); ordination sets ministers apart to preach and lead but is not a sacrament.
  • Protestants teach the priesthood of all believers: every Christian can come to God directly.
  • Quakers and Brethren have no ordained clergy.

Mission 7

  • Scripture, Tradition and the Magisterium (pope and bishops in communion with him) belong together; the Magisterium serves the Word of God (CCC 100).
  • Apostolic succession: Catholics believe bishops succeed the apostles and the pope succeeds Peter.
  • Magisterium is conciliar (councils, e.g. Nicaea 325, Vatican II) or pontifical (popes, e.g. encyclicals); it can be ordinary (everyday) or extraordinary (solemn definitions).
