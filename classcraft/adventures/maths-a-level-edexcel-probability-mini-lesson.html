Probability · Venn diagrams and independence

Events and Venn diagrams

  • P(A′)P(A') is the probability that AA does not happen: P(A′)=1−P(A)P(A') = 1 − P(A).
  • P(A∩B)P(A ∩ B) means "AA and BB"; P(A∪B)P(A ∪ B) means "AA or BB or both".
  • In a Venn diagram each region holds a frequency or a probability. Fill the overlap first, then the rest of each circle, then the outside. All the regions add up to 1 (or the total).
Worked example Probability · Venn diagrams and independence

Completing a Venn diagram

Question
P(A)=0.5P(A) = 0.5, P(B)=0.35P(B) = 0.35 and P(A∩B)=0.15P(A ∩ B) = 0.15. Find P(A∪B)P(A ∪ B) and the probability that exactly one of AA and BB happens.
  1. 1
    Overlap first: A∩BA ∩ B region =0.15= 0.15
  2. 2
    AA only: 0.5−0.15=0.350.5 − 0.15 = 0.35; BB only: 0.35−0.15=0.20.35 − 0.15 = 0.2
  3. 3
    Outside both: 1−(0.35+0.15+0.2)=0.31 − (0.35 + 0.15 + 0.2) = 0.3
  4. 4
    Read off: P(A∪B)=0.35+0.15+0.2=0.7P(A ∪ B) = 0.35 + 0.15 + 0.2 = 0.7; exactly one =0.35+0.2=0.55= 0.35 + 0.2 = 0.55
Answer
P(A∪B)=0.7P(A ∪ B) = 0.7 and P(exactly one)=0.55P(exactly one) = 0.55.
⚠️ Watch out
P(A)+P(B)=0.85P(A) + P(B) = 0.85 counts the overlap twice. Subtract it once.

Probability · Venn diagrams and independence

Mutually exclusive or independent?

  • Mutually exclusive: the events cannot both happen. P(A∩B)=0P(A ∩ B) = 0, so P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B). The circles do not overlap.
  • Independent: one event happening does not change the probability of the other.
A,B independent⟺P(A∩B)=P(A)×P(B)A, B independent ⇔ P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B)
  • To test for independence, work out both sides and compare.
  • Two events with non-zero probabilities that are mutually exclusive can never be independent: if one happens, the other cannot.
Worked example Probability · Venn diagrams and independence

Testing for independence with a two-way table

Question
Of 200 commuters, 80 cycle to work, 50 are under 30, and 20 are under-30 cyclists. Are the events CC "cycles" and UU "under 30" independent?
  1. 1
    Convert to probabilities: P(C)=20080​=0.4P(C) = 80/200 = 0.4, P(U)=20050​=0.25P(U) = 50/200 = 0.25
  2. 2
    Joint probability: P(C∩U)=20020​=0.1P(C ∩ U) = 20/200 = 0.1
  3. 3
    Multiply: P(C)×P(U)=0.4×0.25=0.1P(C) × P(U) = 0.4 × 0.25 = 0.1
  4. 4
    Compare: P(C∩U)=P(C)×P(U)P(C ∩ U) = P(C) × P(U)
Answer
yes, CC and UU are independent.
⚠️ Watch out
always state the comparison and conclude in words. "0.1 = 0.1" alone is not a conclusion.

Probability · Venn diagrams and independence

Sample spaces, trees and three events

  • A sample space diagram lists every equally likely outcome; count the outcomes in an event and divide by the total.
  • On a tree diagram, multiply along the branches for "this then that", and add the probabilities of separate paths.
  • For independent events the branch probabilities stay the same on every stage.
  • With three events, list every path that fits (for "exactly two", there are three) before adding.

Probability · conditional probability

Conditional probability (A-level only)

  • P(A∣B)P(A | B) means "the probability of AA given that BB has happened". You restrict attention to BB only.
P(A∣B)=P(B)P(A∩B)​,P(A∩B)=P(A)×P(B∣A)P(A | B) = (P(A ∩ B))/(P(B)), P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B | A)
  • From a two-way table: divide by the total of the row or column you are given, not the grand total.
  • P(A∣B)P(A | B) and P(B∣A)P(B | A) are usually different: check which event is "given".
  • Addition law: P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B)P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B). Independence means P(A∣B)=P(A)P(A | B) = P(A).
Worked example Probability · conditional probability

Two-way table: which way round?

Question
A sixth form has 70 Year 12 students (7 drive) and 50 Year 13 students (18 drive). One student is chosen at random. Find P(drives∣Year 13)P(drives | Year 13) and P(Year 13∣drives)P(Year 13 | drives).
  1. 1
    Given Year 13, the total is 50: P(drives∣Y13)=5018​=0.36P(drives | Y13) = 18/50 = 0.36
  2. 2
    Given a driver, the total is 7+18=257 + 18 = 25: P(Y13∣drives)=2518​=0.72P(Y13 | drives) = 18/25 = 0.72
  3. 3
    Check with the formula: 25/12018/120​=2518​(18/120)/(25/120) = 18/25
Answer
P(drives∣Y13)=0.36P(drives | Y13) = 0.36 and P(Y13∣drives)=0.72P(Y13 | drives) = 0.72.
⚠️ Watch out
12018​=0.1518/120 = 0.15 is P(drives∩Y13)P(drives ∩ Y13), not a conditional probability.

Probability · conditional probability

Conditional probability on tree diagrams

  • On a tree, the second-stage branches are conditional: they are P(B∣A)P(B | A) and P(B∣A′)P(B | A').
  • Multiply along a path: P(A∩B)=P(A)×P(B∣A)P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B | A).
  • To find P(B)P(B), add every path that ends in BB.
  • For "given BB, find the chance of AA": the path through AA and BB, divided by the total of all paths ending in BB.
Worked example Probability · conditional probability

Working backwards on a tree

Question
On any school day P(rain)=0.3P(rain) = 0.3. Sam is late with probability 0.2 if it rains and 0.05 if it is dry. Given that Sam is late, find the probability that it rained.
  1. 1
    Path rain then late: 0.3×0.2=0.060.3 × 0.2 = 0.06
  2. 2
    Path dry then late: 0.7×0.05=0.0350.7 × 0.05 = 0.035
  3. 3
    Add the late paths: P(late)=0.06+0.035=0.095P(late) = 0.06 + 0.035 = 0.095
  4. 4
    Restrict to "late": P(rain∣late)=0.0950.06​=1912​P(rain | late) = 0.06/0.095 = 12/19
Answer
P(rain∣late)=1912​≈0.632P(rain | late) = 12/19 ≈ 0.632.
⚠️ Watch out
0.2 is P(late∣rain)P(late | rain), the other way round. The question gives "late" as the condition.

Probability · conditional probability

Modelling with probability

  • A probability model rests on assumptions: outcomes equally likely (a fair coin or die), trials independent, probabilities constant, or values estimated from past data.
  • Critique each assumption in context: is it realistic here?
  • Say how a more realistic assumption would change the answer. If wet days tend to follow wet days, the chance of two wet days in a row is higher than the independent model gives.
  • Probabilities from past data are only estimates; bigger samples give better estimates.

Mission check

?A 95% accurate test says you have a rare disease. How likely is it?

1% have the disease; the test is positive for 95% of people with it and negative for 95% of people without it.

picture 10 000 people. 100 have the disease and 95 of them test positive; 9 900 don't, but 5% of them (495) also test positive. So P(disease∣positive)=95+49595​=59095​≈0.16P(disease | positive) = 95/(95 + 495) = 95/590 ≈ 0.16. Because the disease is rare, false alarms outnumber true positives.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Venn diagrams: fill the overlap first; P(A′)=1−P(A)P(A') = 1 − P(A).
  • Mutually exclusive: P(A∩B)=0P(A ∩ B) = 0. Independent: P(A∩B)=P(A)×P(B)P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B). Test by computing both sides.
  • Trees and sample spaces: multiply along paths, add separate paths, list every path that fits.
  • P(A∣B)=P(B)P(A∩B)​P(A | B) = (P(A ∩ B))/(P(B)): restrict to the "given" event and divide by its total.
  • On a tree, second branches are conditional; to reverse a condition, divide one path by the sum of all paths ending in the given event.
  • Every probability model rests on assumptions (fair, independent, constant): critique them in context and say how the answer would change.
