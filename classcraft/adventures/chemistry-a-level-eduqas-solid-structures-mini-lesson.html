Mission 1

Mission 1: Salt is made of charged particles. So why doesn't a salt crystal conduct electricity?

Make a guess: Salt is made of charged particles. So why doesn't a salt crystal conduct electricity?

Mission 1

Which ions atoms form

  • Metal atoms lose electrons: positive ions, cations.
  • Non-metal atoms gain electrons: negative ions, anions.
  • Groups 1, 2 and 13 (3 in older numbering) lose all outer electrons: 1+, 2+, 3+.
  • Groups 15, 16 and 17 (5, 6, 7) gain enough to fill the outer shell: 3−, 2−, 1−.
  • Mg (2,8,2) loses two: Mg²⁺. N (2,5) gains three: N³⁻. Both now have neon's arrangement.
  • Charge = protons − electrons. The number of protons never changes.

Mission 1

Ionic bonds and formulae

  • Ionic bonding: strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.
  • It acts in all directions: each ion attracts every oppositely charged neighbour.
  • Formula: total charge zero. Mg²⁺ and N³⁻ → Mg3N2.
  • Name: cation first, then the anion ending -ide: magnesium nitride.
  • Roman numerals give a metal's charge: Fe³⁺ with O²⁻ is iron(III) oxide, Fe2O3.

Mission 1

The giant ionic lattice

  • Ionic compounds form a giant lattice: a regular 3-D array of alternating cations and anions.
  • The array maximises attractions between opposite charges and minimises repulsions between like ones.
  • There are no molecules. Al2O3 means 2 Al³⁺ for every 3 O²⁻.
  • In sodium chloride each Na⁺ is surrounded by 6 Cl⁻, and each Cl⁻ by 6 Na⁺.
Mission 1

Sodium chloride and caesium chloride

  • Both are 1 : 1, but the lattices differ.
  • NaCl: each ion has 6 nearest neighbours of opposite charge (6 : 6).
  • CsCl: each Cs⁺ sits at the centre of a cube of 8 Cl⁻, and each Cl⁻ in a cube of 8 Cs⁺ (8 : 8).
  • Why: Cs⁺ (about 170 pm) is much larger than Na⁺ (102 pm), so more Cl⁻ (181 pm) fit around it.
  • More oppositely charged neighbours, each touching: the ions pack to maximise attraction.
Quick check
?Sodium chloride and caesium chloride both have a 1 : 1 formula. Which statement about their lattices is correct?
Mission 1

Properties from the lattice

  • High melting point, very low volatility: every ion is held by many strong attractions.
  • Brittle: a blow shifts a layer, like charges meet, they repel, and the crystal splits.
  • Conducts only when molten or in solution: then the ions are free to move and carry charge.
  • Solid: no conduction. The ions are fixed in the lattice, and there are no free electrons.
  • Often soluble in water: polar water molecules attract the ions out of the lattice.
Quick check
?Sodium chloride has a very low volatility: it does not evaporate at room temperature and melts only at 801 °C. Which explanation is correct?
Mission 1

How strongly a lattice holds together

  • The attraction is stronger when ions have larger charges and smaller radii (centres closer).
  • Same charges, size decides: NaF melts at 993 °C, NaCl 801 °C, KCl 770 °C.
  • Double both charges, with ions about the size of NaF's: MgO (Mg²⁺, O²⁻) melts at about 2850 °C.
Quick check
?Ionic radii (pm): Na⁺ 102, K⁺ 138, F⁻ 133, Cl⁻ 181. Which shows the strength of the ionic bonding in NaF, NaCl and KCl in decreasing order?
Mission check

?In a kettle's heating element, the hot metal wire is packed in powdered magnesium oxide, MgO, inside a metal tube. MgO is ionic, yet it is used here as an electrical insulator. Why doesn't it conduct?

Salt is the same. Its Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions can't move in the crystal, so no charge flows. Melt it or dissolve it, the ions move, and the bulb lights.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Diamond and graphite are both pure carbon. Why does only graphite conduct electricity?

Make a guess: Diamond and graphite are both pure carbon. Why does only graphite conduct electricity?

Mission 2

Diamond: a giant covalent lattice

  • Covalent bonds run through the whole solid. There are no separate molecules.
  • Name: giant covalent (giant molecular) structure.
  • Diamond: each C bonded to four others, tetrahedrally (109.5°), in 3D.
  • Very hard, very high melting point: melting breaks strong covalent bonds.
  • No conduction: all four outer electrons of each C are in localised bonds.
Mission 2

Graphite

  • Flat layers of hexagons. Each C bonded to three others (120°).
  • Each C's fourth outer electron is delocalised across its layer.
  • Delocalised electrons move when a voltage is applied: graphite conducts.
  • Weak forces between the layers. Layers slide, so graphite is soft.
  • Still a very high melting point: strong covalent bonds within layers must break.
Quick check
?The diagram represents parts of the structures of diamond and graphite, two forms of carbon. Which statement best explains why graphite is soft and diamond is hard?
Mission 2

Graphene and fullerenes

  • Background from the video: graphene and fullerenes are not examined on your course.
  • Graphene: a single layer of graphite, one atom thick.
  • Each C bonded to three others, with delocalised electrons: it conducts.
  • Very strong for its mass: strong covalent bonds run across the whole sheet.
  • Fullerenes, e.g. C60: closed cages of carbon atoms. These are separate molecules.
  • Only weak forces act between C60 molecules: far lower sublimation temperature than diamond.
  • Diamond, graphite, graphene and fullerenes are allotropes: one element, different structures.
Mission 2

Silicon and silicon dioxide

  • Background from the video: silicon and SiO2 are not examined on your course.
  • Silicon: the diamond structure. Each Si bonded to four Si, tetrahedrally.
  • Si–Si bonds are longer and weaker than C–C: silicon is less hard than diamond and melts lower (1414 °C).
  • Silicon conducts only slightly: it is a semiconductor, not a metal.
  • Silicon dioxide, SiO2: each Si bonded to four O; each O bonded to two Si.
  • SiO2 is a giant lattice, so its formula is just the ratio Si : O = 1 : 2.
  • Melts near 1700 °C. Hard, insoluble, non-conducting.

Mission 2

Boron nitride: two structures

  • B (3 outer e⁻) + N (5) = 8, the same as C + C. So BN forms carbon-like structures.
  • Hexagonal BN: flat hexagonal layers of alternating B and N, each atom bonded to three. Like graphite.
  • Layers slide: soft, slippery, high melting. Used as a high-temperature lubricant and for crucibles.
  • But it is an electrical insulator: N is more electronegative, so the spare electrons stay on N, not delocalised.
  • Cubic BN: diamond-like 3D lattice, each atom bonded tetrahedrally to four. Made from hexagonal BN at high pressure and temperature.
  • Almost as hard as diamond. Used for cutting tools and abrasives, especially for steel, which attacks diamond at high temperature.
Quick check
?Hexagonal boron nitride has a layered structure like graphite, but it does not conduct electricity. Which statement explains this?
Mission check

?Graphite conducts electricity far better along its layers than at right angles to them. Which statement explains this?

Conduction needs mobile charged particles. Graphite has delocalised electrons within each layer; diamond uses all four outer electrons in localised bonds, so it has none.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Tin conducts electricity. So why doesn't liquid tin(IV) chloride conduct at all?

Make a guess: Tin conducts electricity. So why doesn't liquid tin(IV) chloride conduct at all?

Mission 3

The metallic bond

  • Metal atoms release their outer electrons into a shared pool of delocalised electrons.
  • Metallic bonding: strong electrostatic attraction between positive ions (cations) and delocalised electrons.
  • This is the 'electron sea' model.
  • The ions form a regular giant metallic lattice.
  • Bonding is non-directional: each ion attracts the electrons all around it.
Mission 3

Why metals conduct and bend

  • Electrical conductivity: delocalised electrons drift when a voltage is applied. The ions stay put.
  • Thermal conductivity: delocalised electrons carry kinetic energy quickly through the metal.
  • Malleable (hammered into shape) and ductile (drawn into wires): layers of ions slide.
  • After sliding, every ion is still surrounded by delocalised electrons: the bonding is not broken.
  • An ionic lattice is different: a shift brings like charges together. They repel and the crystal shatters.
Quick check
?Aluminium is rolled into kitchen foil less than 0.02 mm thick without cracking. Which statement explains this property of aluminium?
Mission 3

What makes metallic bonding stronger

  • Stronger with a higher ion charge and a smaller ion.
  • More delocalised electrons per ion, packed closer to each ion's centre: stronger attraction.
  • Na⁺ → Mg²⁺ → Al³⁺: melting point 98 °C → 650 °C → 660 °C.
  • Down group 1 the ions get larger: Li 181 °C, Na 98 °C, K 63 °C.
  • Mass does not decide: heavier potassium melts lower than lithium.
Mission 3

Alloys

  • Background from the video: alloys are not examined on your course.
  • An alloy is a mixture of a metal with other elements: brass (copper, zinc), steel (iron, carbon).
  • Metallic bonding is non-directional, so other atoms fit into the lattice. The alloy still conducts.
  • Atoms of a different size disrupt the layers, so they slide less easily.
  • So alloys are usually harder and stronger than the pure metal.
Mission 3

Four types of structure

  • Giant ionic (e.g. NaCl): ions held by electrostatic attraction in all directions.
  • Giant metallic (e.g. magnesium): ions in a sea of delocalised electrons.
  • Giant covalent (e.g. diamond, graphite): atoms joined by covalent bonds throughout.
  • Simple molecular (e.g. iodine, ice): small molecules held by weak intermolecular forces.
  • Identify the particles and the forces between them. The properties follow.
Mission 3

Simple molecular: iodine and ice

  • Iodine: I2 molecules in a regular lattice, held by weak London (van der Waals) forces.
  • Low melting point (114 °C); iodine sublimes on gentle warming. The I–I bonds do not break.
  • Ice: each H2O hydrogen-bonded to four others, tetrahedrally. An open lattice.
  • The open lattice makes ice less dense than liquid water, so ice floats.
  • Hydrogen bonds are stronger than London forces, but far weaker than covalent bonds.

Mission 3

Bonding is a continuum

  • Ionic, covalent and metallic are models. Real bonds usually lie in between.
  • Bigger electronegativity difference: more ionic character. Smaller difference: more covalent.
  • A small, highly charged cation polarises an anion: ionic bonding with some covalent character.
  • Background (not examined on any course here): the bonding triangle plots average electronegativity (x) against difference (y). Corners: metallic, ionic, covalent.
  • NaCl: average 2.05, difference 2.3: near the ionic corner. High melting point, conducts when molten.
  • SnCl4: average 2.6, difference 1.2: polar covalent. Small molecules: liquid, no ions, no conduction.
Mission check

?Each liquid is tested with two electrodes and a bulb. Which liquid will not conduct electricity?

Tin metal has delocalised electrons. SnCl4, like TiCl4, is simple molecular: electrons are held in bonds inside molecules and there are no ions, so neither liquid conducts.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Ions form by losing or gaining electrons; charges balance to zero in the formula.
  • A giant lattice of fixed ions: high melting point, brittle, conducts only when molten or dissolved.
  • Higher charges and smaller ions → stronger attraction.

Mission 2

  • Giant covalent: strong covalent bonds throughout, so very high melting point and insoluble.
  • Diamond: 4 bonds per C, all electrons localised, hard, no conduction. Graphite: 3 bonds per C, one delocalised electron per C, conducts along layers, layers slide.

Mission 3

  • Metallic bonding: attraction between cations and delocalised electrons; non-directional, so metals conduct, are malleable and (usually) melt high.
  • To conduct, a substance needs mobile charged particles. Decide the structure, then explain the property.
