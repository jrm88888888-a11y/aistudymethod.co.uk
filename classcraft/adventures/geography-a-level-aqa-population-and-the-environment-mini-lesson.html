Mission 1: Why do more than 9 in 10 people in China live on one side of a single line?

Specification: what this mission covers

Mission 1

Four population parameters

  • Numbers: how many people live in an area.
  • Distribution: where they live, the pattern on the map.
  • Density: people per km² (population ÷ area).
  • Change: how numbers grow or shrink: natural change (births − deaths) plus net migration.
Mission 1

Global numbers

  • World population: about 2.49 billion in 1950 and 8.16 billion in 2024.
  • Asia held 4.81 billion people in 2024: 58.9% of the world (our calculation).
  • Africa held 1.52 billion: 18.6% (our calculation).
Mission 1

Global densities

  • World average: about 63 people per km² (2024).
  • Asia about 153; Africa about 51; Europe about 34; Oceania about 5.
  • National extremes: Bangladesh about 1,333 per km²; Mongolia about 2.
  • An average hides the pattern: most of Oceania's people live in a few coastal cities. In 2001, 85% of Australians lived within 50 km of the coast.
Quick check
?Egypt's density is about 117 people per km² (2024). What does this tell you about where Egyptians live?
Mission 1

Global change rates

  • World growth rate: 0.86% a year in 2024.
  • Africa 2.27% a year; Asia 0.60%; Northern America 0.60%; Europe −0.08% (falling).
  • Change has two parts. High-income countries had slightly more deaths than births in 2024, but grew through net migration.
Mission 1

The role of development

  • Low-income countries grew by 2.68% a year in 2024; high-income countries by 0.25%.
  • As development raises incomes, health care and girls' schooling, death rates fall first, then birth rates.
  • So development processes shape the change rate, and over time the distribution of the world's people.
Mission 1

Climate

  • Attract: warmth, a long growing season and reliable rainfall support farming and dense settlement.
  • Repel: extreme cold (polar, high mountains), extreme heat with drought (hot deserts), and very short growing seasons.
  • Climate sets broad limits: the empty areas of the world map are mostly too dry, too cold or too high.
Mission 1

Soils

  • Alluvial soils (river deposits on floodplains and deltas) are deep and fertile: they support the densest farming populations.
  • Volcanic soils can also be very fertile.
  • Thin, leached or frozen soils (e.g. in permafrost regions) support few people.
  • Soils interact with climate: fertile soil is little use without water.
Mission 1

Resources and water supply

  • Water supply: rivers, lakes and aquifers for drinking and irrigation.
  • In dry regions, settlement clusters where water is reliable: along rivers, around oases and above usable aquifers.
  • Other resources: minerals and energy can draw people to harsh places (mining towns), but often in small numbers.
Mission 1

China: the Hu Line

  • The Hu Huanyong Line runs from Heihe (Heilongjiang) in the north-east to Tengchong (Yunnan) in the south-west.
  • South-east of the line: about 43% of the land and about 94% of the people.
  • Hu drew the line in 1935. Researchers find the split "remains at roughly 94:6".
Mission 1

Why the line holds

  • East: fertile plains and river valleys, with monsoon rain: good for farming.
  • West: mountains, plateaux and deserts, with arid and semi-arid climates and little rainfall.
  • Human factors reinforce it: ports, industry and cities grew in the east (for example the Special Economic Zones at Shantou, Shenzhen and Zhuhai), and migrants followed the jobs.
Quick check
?Which statement best explains why the Hu Line split stays at about 94:6?
Mission 1

Development can loosen the limits

  • Technology: irrigation, desalination and air conditioning let people live where conditions are harsh.
  • Economic growth concentrates people further in cities and ports, even within fertile regions.
  • Exam move: in AQA essays, link each physical factor to a population parameter (distribution, density, numbers, change) and judge how far development changes its influence.
Mission check

?Egypt's national density is about 117 people per km², yet about 95% of its people live in the Nile Valley and Delta. Which explanation is best?

In China and Egypt, physical factors (rainfall, relief, water, soils) set the limits of where people live, and human factors reinforce them. That is why the Hu Line split "remains at roughly 94:6".

What food security means

How much food is available per person

  • Dietary energy supply (DES): food available per person per day, in kilocalories (kcal).

  • 2023: Europe 3,490 · Americas 3,438 · Oceania 3,137 · Asia 2,991 · Africa 2,556 kcal.

Mission 2

Production is not consumption

  • Trade moves food from surplus to deficit regions: Singapore imports over 90% of its food, with about 1% of its land available for food production.
  • Much production feeds animals or fuel, not people directly.
  • DES measures supply, not intake: food later wasted in shops and homes still counts.
  • So high production or supply does not mean everyone eats enough.
Mission 2

Hunger: a shifting pattern

  • Prevalence of undernourishment (PoU): the share of people whose calorie intake is insufficient.
  • 2025: 7.8% of the world, about 645 million people, down from 8.1% in 2024.
  • Africa: 20.0%, about 309 million people, now more than Asia's 292 million.
  • Asia's rate fell from 7.9% (2022) to 6.7% (2024). The centre of world hunger is moving to Africa.
Mission 2

Exam move: describing patterns

  • Describe production and consumption with figures and regions: "Asia grows half the world's cereals, yet Africa's supply per person is the lowest."
  • Explain with access as well as supply: income, prices, trade and conflict decide who eats.
Mission check

The same logic answers the hook. The world's 3,016 kcal per person is an average of supply; around 645 million people still went hungry in 2025 because access is unequal, and 20.0% of Africans were undernourished.

Mission 3

Farming is a system

  • Inputs: physical (sunlight, rain, temperature, soil, slope) and human (labour, seed, fertiliser, machinery, money).
  • Processes: ploughing, sowing, spraying, harvesting; rearing, milking.
  • Outputs: crops, meat, milk, wool, income; also unwanted outputs such as soil loss and polluted runoff.
  • Feedback: profit or loss changes next year's inputs.
Mission 3

Types of agricultural system

  • Arable (crops), pastoral (livestock), mixed (both).
  • Commercial (sold for profit) or subsistence (eaten by the farm household).
  • Intensive (high inputs of labour or capital per hectare) or extensive (low inputs spread over large areas).
  • Scale: more than a third of the world's land surface is used for crops or livestock; about 12% of ice-free land for crops and 25% for grazing.
Quick check
?A sheep station in a dry region grazes a few animals on each hectare of a very large property and sells the wool. How is it best classified?
Mission 3

Measuring productivity

  • Land productivity: output per hectare (yield, tonnes per hectare).
  • Labour productivity: output per worker or per hour.
  • Yield = production ÷ area harvested.
  • UK wheat, 2025: 12 million tonnes from 1.7 million hectares; yield 7.2 t/ha.
  • A farm can score high on one measure and low on another: big machinery raises output per worker without raising yield.
Quick check
?In 2025 the UK produced 963 thousand tonnes of oats from 198 thousand hectares. What was the oat yield?
Mission 3

Yields differ between places and years

  • Wheat yield, 2024 (t/ha): UK 7.28; world 3.64; Australia 2.61; Kazakhstan 1.42.
  • The same country varies by year: UK 8.59 in 2022 and 7.28 in 2024.
  • DEFRA, 2025: wet September weather hindered the harvest, giving "considerable variability in yield between regions".
  • So yield reflects both the place (climate, soils) and the year (weather), as well as farmers' inputs.
Mission 3

Climate sets the limits

  • Temperature: crops need a long enough growing season above their minimum temperature; frost and heat stress cut yields.
  • Water: rainfall must meet crop needs when crops grow; too little causes drought stress, too much waterlogs fields and delays harvest.
  • Sunshine drives photosynthesis; extremes (storms, drought) cause sudden losses.
  • England and Wales grade farmland by "a combination of climate, topography and soil characteristics".
  • That grading notes arable land that is "very dry because of drought" in a low grade, and that "in moist climates, yields of grass may be moderate to high".
Mission 3

Soils set the limits

  • Soil depth, texture, drainage, nutrients, pH and stoniness control root growth, water supply and when machines can work the land.
  • The Agricultural Land Classification (ALC) grades land from 1 (excellent) to 5 (very poor).
  • Grade 1: "a very wide range" of crops; yields "high and less variable".
  • Grade 5: "very severe limitations that restrict use to permanent pasture or rough grazing".
  • The best and most versatile land is grades 1 to 3a.
Quick check
?Land has severe limitations. It is mainly used for grass, with an occasional cereal crop whose yields vary. Which ALC grade fits best?
Mission 3

People push the limits, at a cost

  • Irrigation adds water; drainage removes it; fertiliser and lime fix nutrients and pH; glasshouses control temperature; plant breeding raises potential yield.
  • Since 1970 the value of agricultural crop production has risen by about 300%.
  • But productivity has fallen on 23% of land areas because of land degradation.
  • So climate and soil set the starting limits; inputs move those limits only while they are affordable and the land is not degraded.
  • Exam move (AQA "Assess"): separate physical controls (climate, soils) from human ones (inputs, prices, technology), then judge which matters most for a named place.
Mission check

?Kazakhstan's average wheat yield was 0.92 t/ha in 2023 and 1.42 t/ha in 2024. What is the most likely main cause of such a large change in one year?

Yield depends on the place's climate and soils, on weather in that year, and on the inputs farmers can add. Where rain or temperature limits growth, yields swing from year to year.

Mission 4: Deserts cover vast areas, yet few people live in them. Does climate decide where people live and farm?

Make a guess: Deserts cover vast areas, yet few people live in them. Does climate decide where people live and farm?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

Mission 4

People cluster in a narrow band of climates

  • A study by Xu et al. (2020) found that for thousands of years, people have lived in a narrow part of Earth's range of climates.
  • The main peak is at a mean annual temperature of about 11–15 °C; a smaller peak at about 20–25 °C is mostly the Indian Monsoon region.
  • Production of crops and livestock is largely limited to the same conditions.
  • People use most of the range of rainfall, except the driest end, which stays almost empty.
Mission 4

Climate type 1: hot arid

  • Characteristics: rainfall far below what evaporation and plants could use. Dryness is measured by the aridity index: precipitation ÷ potential evapotranspiration.
  • Hyper-arid areas have an aridity index below 0.05.
  • Distribution: hot deserts lie along about 30° N and S: the American Southwest and Mexico, northern Africa and Australia. High pressure there brings dry weather.
  • Mean annual temperatures above 29 °C occur on only 0.8% of land today, mostly in the Sahara.
  • Drylands as a whole (arid, semi-arid and dry sub-humid) cover about 46.2% of land.
Quick check
?A desert station gets 50 mm of rain a year; potential evapotranspiration is 2,000 mm. What is its aridity index, and is it hyper-arid?
Mission 4

Hot arid: activities and numbers

  • The hottest, driest cores hold very few people: the driest end of the climate range is almost unoccupied.
  • Drylands overall are home to about 3 billion people (about 38% of the world), most in South Asia, then sub-Saharan Africa and Latin America.
  • Pastoralism is a key dryland activity: about 100–200 million people, of whom 30–63 million are nomadic.
  • Pastoral systems are an adaptation to unreliable rain and low plant growth; herds need large areas and move seasonally.
  • Where grazing land is converted to crops, mobile herding is squeezed and conflicts can follow.
Mission 4

Climate type 2: tropical monsoon

  • Characteristics: a strong seasonal switch from dry to wet. The IPCC defines the monsoon area as where summer rainfall exceeds winter rainfall by more than 2.5 mm a day.
  • Temperatures are warm all year: the 20–25 °C population peak lies here.
  • Distribution: the IPCC's monsoon regions include South and South East Asia, West Africa and parts of the Americas. The tropical monsoon type is best developed in South and South East Asia.
  • The wet season can bring floods; a weak or late monsoon can bring drought.
Mission 4

Tropical monsoon: activities and numbers

  • The Indian Monsoon region forms the world's second population peak by temperature.
  • Farming here depends on the timing and amount of wet-season rain: planting follows the rains, and the dry season needs stored water.
  • Very wet and very dry seasons bring flood and drought risk, set partly by how the monsoon behaves.
  • Contrast with hot deserts: here seasonal rain, not year-round drought, sets the farming year, and populations are dense.
Quick check
?Xu et al. found that people use most of the world's range of rainfall at their preferred temperatures. Which part of the range is almost empty of people?
Mission 4

Climate sets limits; it does not decide everything

  • Soil fertility does not seem to be a major driver of where people live, at the global scale.
  • Water from rivers and irrigation can let people farm in dry climates; technology and history shape numbers inside the climate limits.
  • Xu et al. stress that many other factors affect decisions to move.
  • Exam move ("To what extent"): show the climate pattern, then the exceptions, then judge: climate sets broad limits; water, technology and development decide numbers within them.
Mission 4

Climate change and agriculture: the global picture

  • Agricultural productivity has grown, but climate change has slowed that growth over the past 50 years.
  • Negative impacts were mainly in mid- and low-latitude regions; some high-latitude regions gained.
  • More frequent and intense droughts, floods and heatwaves, and sea-level rise, will increase risks to food security.
  • With no or little adaptation, heat and drought losses will raise food prices and malnutrition risk, especially in the tropics.
Mission 4

Climate change in each climate type

  • Hot arid / drylands: extra CO₂ boosts plant growth, but falling water availability has a larger effect in many dryland areas.
  • Aridity is projected to increase in some places; salinisation risk and water erosion are projected to grow.
  • Tropical monsoon: monsoon rainfall is projected to increase, especially over South and South East Asia.
  • The monsoon season is projected to start later over North and South America and West Africa.
  • Wet and dry extremes are projected to intensify, raising flood and drought risk to crops.
Quick check
?Higher CO₂ makes many plants grow faster. Why does the IPCC still expect many dryland farms to struggle?
Mission check

?By 2070, under high emissions, mean annual temperatures of at least 29 °C could spread from 0.8% to 19% of Earth's land, home to 3.5 billion people if no one moves. Which conclusion best fits what you have learned?

Climate sets broad limits: people and farming cluster in mild climates and the Indian Monsoon region, and avoid the hottest, driest cores. Climate change shifts those limits, mostly harming low-latitude farming through heat, drought and water stress.

Mission 5

What makes a soil "zonal"?

  • A soil reflects five soil-forming factors: climate; vegetation, fauna and people; topography; parent material; and time.
  • Zonal soils occur mainly in particular climate zones: climate is the dominant factor.
  • Where another factor dominates locally (e.g. waterlogging, parent rock), the soil is intrazonal; soils too young to show local conditions are azonal, e.g. young river alluvium.
  • Zonal soils therefore map onto the world's climate and vegetation belts. This mission uses two: Chernozems (mid-latitude grassland) and Ferralsols (humid tropics).
Mission 5

Zonal soil 1: Chernozems (black earths)

  • Characteristics: a thick, black, organic-rich surface horizon (Ah), from about 20 cm to more than 2 m deep, over a brownish subsoil with calcium carbonate accumulation.
  • Surface layers hold 10–16% organic matter, are neutral (pH about 7.0) and are rich in bases (nutrient cations).
  • Climate and vegetation: continental (cold winters, hot summers), with tall-grass steppe. Deep Chernozems form where annual precipitation is about equal to evaporation.
  • Distribution: about 230 million hectares, mainly the mid-latitude steppes of Eurasia and North America.
  • FAO calls these black earths "some of the best soils of the world"; the name, from Russian words for black and earth, was coined by Dokuchaev in 1883.
Mission 5

Why are Chernozems so fertile?

  • Tall grasses put most of their biomass into the soil: about 1–1.5 t/ha of dry matter above ground, but 4–6 t/ha of roots already in the soil.
  • 80% of roots sit in the top 30–40 cm, so organic matter builds up near the surface.
  • Worms and burrowing animals mix the soil (bioturbation); some Chernozems are mostly worm casts.
  • Rain roughly balances evaporation, so leaching is moderate: soluble salts are flushed out, but bases stay.
Quick check
?Which statement best explains the thick, humus-rich topsoil of a Chernozem?
Mission 5

Chernozems and agriculture

  • High natural fertility and flat to undulating plains allow arable farming and cattle ranging.
  • Main crops: wheat, barley and maize; P (phosphate) fertiliser is needed for high yields.
  • Dry summers: supplemental irrigation; maize output tends to stagnate in drier years unless irrigated.
  • Less than half of Eurasian Chernozems are used for arable crops: a large resource for the future.
  • Risk: structure must be protected by timely cultivation and careful irrigation at low watering rates to prevent erosion.
Mission 5

Zonal soil 2: Ferralsols (latosols)

  • Also called latosols (Brazil) and Oxisols (USA).
  • Characteristics: deep (usually several metres), red or yellow, intensely weathered; rich in iron and aluminium oxides (sesquioxides); clay is mainly kaolinite.
  • Formation: high soil temperatures and intense percolation dissolve and remove weatherable minerals over long periods on stable, old land surfaces.
  • Distribution: about 750 million hectares, almost all in the humid tropics, mainly the continental shields of South America (Brazil) and Africa.
  • They have good physical properties (deep, permeable, friable) but are chemically poor.
Mission 5

Ferralsols and agriculture

  • Under rainforest, most nutrients are held in the biomass; leaves and debris return them to the top 10–50 cm of soil (nutrient cycling).
  • Kaolinite holds few nutrient cations, and Ferralsols "fix" (lock up) phosphate.
  • Break the cycle (e.g. low-input farming every year) and the root zone is rapidly depleted.
  • Traditional use: shifting cultivation with long enough fallows.
  • Sedentary farming needs liming (normally 0.5–2 t/ha) and full fertilisation, plus manuring, mulching and control of surface erosion.
  • Exam move: link soil property → farming system → problem, e.g. low CEC → nutrients lost after clearance → shifting cultivation or lime and fertiliser.
Quick check
?A patch of rainforest on Ferralsols is cleared and planted with the same crop every year, with no fertiliser. What is most likely?
Mission 5

Soil problem 1: soil erosion

  • Soil erosion: removal of soil by water, wind or tillage; natural, but greatly accelerated by human activity.
  • Global median yield loss from erosion is about 0.3% a year; continued, that is a 10% loss of potential yield by 2050.
  • Management: conservation tillage and no-till; keep a protective cover of cover crops and crop residues; soil conservation on slopes.
  • In North America, reduced tillage, plus much less summer fallow on the Great Plains of Canada, cut both wind and water erosion.
  • On Chernozems: timely cultivation and careful irrigation; on Ferralsols: prevent surface erosion after clearance.
Quick check
?On US cropland, average water erosion fell from 10.8 to 7.4 tonnes per hectare per year between 1982 and 2007. By roughly what percentage did it fall?
Mission 5

Soil problems 2 and 3: waterlogging and salinisation

  • Waterlogging: soil so wet that pores lack the oxygen roots need; CO₂ and ethylene build up.
  • Salinisation: build-up of salts. Secondary (human-induced) salinisation comes from poor irrigation, e.g. salty water or too little drainage.
  • The largest cause of human-made salinisation is ill-designed, large-scale irrigation.
  • Case — Indus Basin Irrigation System, Pakistan: canal seepage raised the water table, causing waterlogging and salinisation in the 1950s–60s.
  • Today waterlogging still affects 35% of the canal command area, mostly in Sindh; irrigation adds about 16 million tons of salt a year, threatening up to 43% of the irrigated area.
Mission 5

Managing waterlogging and salinisation

  • Lower the water table: Pakistan encouraged groundwater pumping; this reduced waterlogging in most of Punjab.
  • But unregulated pumping now depletes groundwater in parts of Punjab: one fix created a new problem.
  • Reclaim salty soils: leach salts with water plus drainage, use salt-tolerant crops, and add chemical or organic amendments.
  • Leaching needs extra irrigation water, which can worsen water stress.
  • Prevent it: cut system losses (leaking canals) and evaporation to raise water-use efficiency.
Mission 5

Soil problem 4: structural deterioration

  • Compaction: pressure at the surface raises density and cuts macropores; roots, water and air struggle to enter.
  • Main cause: heavy machinery; overgrazing and spreading mechanised farming add to it.
  • Some cultivated Ferralsols low in iron or organic matter suffer surface sealing and compaction.
  • Management: minimise disturbance (conservation tillage, no-till), keep residues and cover crops, and use crop rotations; these also cut erosion and organic-carbon loss.
  • Exam move ("Assess"): one practice can manage several problems at once; judge which problem limits farming most in your chosen soil.
Mission check

?Two schemes are planned: (1) continuous maize on newly cleared Ferralsols in Central Africa, with no inputs; (2) heavily watered wheat on Chernozems in the dry southern steppe, with no drainage. Which forecast is best?

Grass roots build organic matter inside Chernozems, so the soil stores the nutrients; rainforest keeps its nutrients in the biomass above weathered, chemically poor Ferralsols. Good soils can still be degraded by careless irrigation and tillage.

Mission 6

A spectrum of strategies

  • By time scale: short-term relief (food now) → capacity building (skills, incomes, institutions) → long-term system redesign (laws, markets, how food is produced and shared).
  • By scale of technique: large-scale technology (new crop varieties, indoor farms) → small-scale, bottom-up, appropriate approaches (led by communities, cheap, locally maintained).
  • By pillar: raise availability, improve access, improve use.
Mission 6

Short-term relief

  • Relief feeds people in a crisis, but does not remove the cause.
  • UK: food banks in the Trussell community gave out more than 2.6 million emergency food parcels in 2025.
  • Trussell itself says: "We need long-term solutions to ensure no one in the UK needs to turn to a food bank to get by".
Mission 6

Capacity building from the bottom up

  • Farmer Field Schools (FAO): groups of farmers, herders or fisherfolk learn by doing, through "Practical field exercises using direct observation, discussion and decision making".
  • FAO says they have "empowered more than 10 million farmers globally".
  • Strengths: cheap, locally owned, adapted to local conditions. Limits: slow to scale, and evidence is often reported by the programme itself.
Quick check
?Which is an example of capacity building rather than short-term relief?
Mission 6

Case 1: Brazil (upper-middle income)

  • Brazil is upper-middle income.
  • The Zero Hunger (Fome Zero) project, launched in October 2001, became the government's main strategy from 2003.
  • Bolsa Família gives cash to poor families who keep their children in school and attend health check-ups.
  • By 2013 it reached nearly 14 million households, about a quarter of the population, at around 0.5–0.6% of GDP.
Mission 6

Brazil: school meals and family farms

  • A 2009 law: at least 30% of federal school-meal funds must buy food directly from family farms.
  • A 2025 law raised the minimum to 45%.
  • One budget does two jobs: it feeds pupils (access) and gives small farmers a steady market (availability and incomes).
Mission 6

Brazil: results and setbacks

  • Brazil left the Hunger Map in 2014: undernourishment below 2.5%.
  • It returned in 2019–2022, after an economic crisis and COVID-19.
  • It left again in 2025: undernourishment averaged below 2.5% in 2022–2024.
Mission 6

Brazil on the spectrum

  • Relief: cash meets poor families' immediate needs.
  • Capacity building: school attendance and health check-ups are conditions of the cash.
  • System redesign: laws tie public food buying to family farms.
  • Exam move: weigh two exits (2014, 2025) against one return: success depended on sustained policy and incomes.
Mission 6

Large-scale technology: GM crops

  • US National Academies (2016): "no substantiated evidence of a difference in risks to human health" from GM crops then on sale; but "no evidence that GE crops had changed the rate of increase in yields".
  • Golden Rice carries extra beta-carotene against vitamin A deficiency, which affects more than 15% of Filipino children under six.
  • The Philippines approved it for commercial propagation in July 2021, the first country to do so; in April 2024 a court revoked the permit.
  • Technology can raise supply or nutrition, but law, politics and public trust decide whether it spreads.
Mission 6

Case 2: Singapore (high income)

  • Singapore is high income. It imports over 90% of its food, with about 1% of its land available for food production.
  • "Three food baskets": diversify import sources (about 170 countries and regions), grow locally, and grow overseas.
  • In November 2025 it set new 2035 targets, replacing its earlier "30-by-30" goal: local farms to supply 20% of fibre and 30% of protein eaten.
Mission 6

Singapore: high-tech farming

  • Vertical farming: crops grown indoors in stacked layers under artificial light.
  • Lettuce from an indoor plant factory needs 247 kWh of purchased energy per kg dry weight; a Dutch greenhouse 70 kWh.
  • But plant factories use water, CO₂ and land more productively.
  • Singapore approved cell-based meat for sale in 2020, the first country to do so. Its minister notes alternative proteins faced "higher production costs and weaker than expected consumer acceptance globally".

Mission 6

Cutting loss and waste

  • Food loss: over 13% of food is lost after harvest and before retail.
  • Food waste: 1.05 billion tonnes in 2022, 132 kg per person; households 60%.
  • SDG 12.3: halve per capita food waste at retail and consumer levels by 2030.
  • France's 2016 law bans destroying unsold edible food and makes large supermarkets seek a food-aid charity partner. The source does not measure its effect.
Mission check

?Singapore has little hunger, yet imports over 90% of its food. What best keeps it food-secure over the long term?

The hook's answer is the same idea in a different place. Brazil tackled access with cash transfers and school meals bought from family farms. Hunger fell below 2.5% by 2014, returned in 2019–2022, and fell again by 2025: ending hunger takes sustained policy, not just more food.

Mission 7

Mission 7: In 2000, non-communicable diseases caused 58% of the world's deaths. In 2023 they caused 74%. Good news or bad news?

Make a guess: In 2000, non-communicable diseases caused 58% of the world's deaths. In 2023 they caused 74%. Good news or bad news?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.2.4.3 global patterns of health, mortality and morbidity; economic and social development and the epidemiological transition
Mission 7

Measuring health: mortality and morbidity

  • Mortality: death (rates of dying). Morbidity: illness and disability (rates of being unwell).
  • Healthy life expectancy (HALE) combines both: years a newborn can expect in full health. World, 2023: 62.8.
  • HALE by WHO region, 2023: Western Pacific 67.3, Europe 66.7, Americas 64.4, South-East Asia 61.3, Eastern Mediterranean 60.4, Africa 56.2.
Mission 7

Infant and maternal mortality

  • IMR: deaths before age 1 per 1,000 live births. 2024: world 27.7; low-income countries 46.7; high-income 4.2.
  • MMR: maternal deaths per 100,000 live births. 2023: about 260,000 deaths, an MMR of 197.
  • Sub-Saharan Africa had around 70% of maternal deaths. Nigeria's MMR was 993; Japan's was 3.
Mission 7

Basic needs: food, water and sanitation

  • 2024: 3.4 billion people lacked safely managed sanitation; 354 million practised open defecation.
  • Safely managed sanitation: WHO African Region 27%, Europe 80%.
  • 23.2% of children under five were stunted in 2024.
  • Without sanitation, faeces reach water; diarrhoea and undernutrition then feed each other and kill infants. Skilled care in childbirth cuts maternal deaths.
Quick check
?A low-income country wants the fastest fall in infant mortality. Which change would do most?
Mission 7

The epidemiological transition (Omran, 1971)

  • Omran's three stages: "The Age of Pestilence and Famine"; "The Age of Receding Pandemics"; "The Age of Degenerative and Man-Made Diseases".
  • Over time "pandemics of infection are gradually displaced by degenerative and man-made diseases".
  • It runs alongside the demographic transition: death rates fall first, then people live longer.
  • Driver: rising living standards (more food, clean water, sanitation) cut susceptibility to infection, so more people survive childhood.
Mission 7

The evidence

  • NCDs caused 58% of deaths worldwide in 2000 and 74% in 2023.
  • Eight of the ten leading causes of death in 2023 were NCDs.
  • In 2023, for the first time, communicable diseases caused less than half of all deaths in low-income countries.
  • Life expectancy 73.3 years, but healthy life expectancy (HALE) 62.8 (2023): many extra years are lived in poor health.
Mission 7

Diseases of poverty and of affluence

  • Diseases of poverty (mostly communicable): unsafe water and sanitation, crowded housing, undernutrition, warm climates suiting vectors, young populations, weak health care.
  • Diseases of affluence (NCDs): older populations, tobacco, physical inactivity, harmful use of alcohol and unhealthy diets.
  • The label misleads: 82% of the 18 million NCD deaths before age 70 (2021) were in low- and middle-income countries.
Quick check
?In a country, the risk of dying of heart disease at each age has not changed, yet heart disease's share of all deaths has doubled. What best explains this?
Mission 7

Limits of the model

  • Omran's classical model is based on Western Europe (England and Wales, Sweden); he added an accelerated model (Japan) and a delayed model (Chile, Ceylon).
  • Many poorer countries now face infections and NCDs together: a double burden of disease.
  • Olshansky and Ault (1986) added a fourth stage: "the age of delayed degenerative diseases".
  • New and re-emerging infections (HIV, COVID-19) break the one-way pattern.
Mission check

?Over 40 years in one middle-income country, child deaths from diarrhoea fell sharply, life expectancy rose, and the share of deaths from heart disease, diabetes and dementia doubled. Good news or bad news?

Both. The world's rise from 58% to 74% is Omran's epidemiological transition: infections recede as living standards rise, people live longer, and NCDs take over, while the poorest countries and groups carry both burdens at once.

Mission 8: Two villages share a hillside in Kenya's highlands. Why might one have far more malaria than the other?

Make a guess: Two villages share a hillside in Kenya's highlands. Why might one have far more malaria than the other?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.2.4.3 relationship between environmental variables (e.g. climate, topography/drainage) and incidence of disease; air quality and health; water quality and health
Mission 8

How environment affects disease

  • Environment can affect the pathogen (bacteria, virus, parasite), the vector that carries it (e.g. mosquitoes), or people's exposure (air, water).
  • Four environmental variables in this mission:
    • Climate: temperature, rainfall, humidity.
    • Topography and drainage: altitude, slope, where water collects.
    • Air quality and water quality.
Mission 8

Climate: temperature and malaria

  • Malaria spreads through the bites of some infected female Anopheles mosquitoes; in 2024 there were an estimated 282 million cases and 610,000 deaths.
  • A temperature model predicts optimal malaria transmission at 25 °C, decreasing dramatically above 28 °C.
  • In the highlands of Ethiopia and Colombia, malaria reached higher altitudes in warmer years.
  • So climate sets where and when transmission is possible; rainy seasons add breeding sites.
Mission 8

Topography and drainage

  • Altitude: transmission generally falls with height, partly because lower temperatures slow both parasite and mosquito development.
  • Drainage: the shape of the land decides where water accumulates, and so where Anopheles can find breeding habitat.
  • Western Kenyan highlands: malaria case households were on average 280 m closer to very wet ground than households without malaria.
  • Distance to wet ground still predicted risk after controlling for elevation.
Quick check
?In the Kenyan highland study, which explanation best fits the finding that households near very wet ground had more malaria?
Mission 8

Air quality and health

  • Outdoor (ambient) air pollution caused an estimated 4.2 million premature deaths in 2019.
  • 99% of the world's population lived where WHO air quality guideline levels were not met (2019).
  • 89% of those deaths were in low- and middle-income countries.
  • Causes of these deaths: 68% heart disease and stroke, 14% chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, 14% acute lower respiratory infections, 4% lung cancer.
  • Household air pollution (cooking on open fires or inefficient stoves): about 2.9 million deaths in 2021; about 2.1 billion people cook this way.
Mission 8

When climate and topography trap pollution

  • A temperature inversion: a layer of warm air sits above cold air near the ground, so polluted air cannot rise and disperse.
  • Inversions are most likely on calm, clear, cold nights, especially in valleys and basins where cold air drains and pools.
  • London, 1952: smog with SO₂ from coal burning contributed to thousands of respiratory-related deaths.
Quick check
?Which conditions are most likely to produce dangerous air pollution in a city that burns coal for heating?
Mission 8

Water quality and health

  • In 2022, at least 1.7 billion people used a drinking-water source contaminated with faeces.
  • Microbially contaminated water can transmit diarrhoea, cholera, dysentery, typhoid and polio; it causes about 505,000 diarrhoeal deaths a year.
  • Chemical quality matters too: natural arsenic and fluoride in groundwater, and lead from pipes.
  • About 251.4 million people needed preventive treatment for schistosomiasis (caught through contact with infested water) in 2021.
Mission 8

Cholera: a water-quality disease

  • Cholera is an acute diarrhoeal infection caught by consuming food or water contaminated with Vibrio cholerae.
  • In 2025, 47 countries reported 451,499 cases and 7,870 deaths.
  • Risk is highest where sanitation and safe water are lacking, so it follows poverty and disruption as well as environment.
Quick check
?In 2022, 2.2 billion people lacked safely managed drinking water. Which group faced the greatest risk of water-borne disease?
Mission 8

Environment and development together

  • The same environment can cause very different disease burdens: 89% of outdoor air pollution deaths are in low- and middle-income countries.
  • Drainage, water treatment, clean cooking fuels and housing can break the link between environment and disease.
  • Exam move (AQA "Assess"): name the environmental variable, the mechanism (pathogen, vector or exposure), then show how human factors strengthen or weaken it.
Mission check

?A city lies in a highland basin, about 2,500 m above sea level. Winters are cold and calm, and many homes burn coal or wood. Which environment–disease link is likely to be strongest?

Disease risk depends on how the environment acts on pathogens, vectors and exposure: temperature and drainage for malaria, inversions for air pollution, contaminated water for diarrhoeal disease. In Kenya's highlands, homes near ground where water collects had more malaria, even at similar heights.

Mission 9

Mission 9: Malaria killed an estimated 610,000 people in 2024. Why does it kill mostly in one continent, and mostly young children?

Make a guess: Malaria killed an estimated 610,000 people in 2024. Why does it kill mostly in one continent, and mostly young children?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.2.4.3 one biologically transmitted disease (malaria): global prevalence, distribution, seasonal incidence; links to physical and socio-economic environments incl. environmental variables and vectors; impact on health and well-being; management and mitigation
  • 3.2.4.3 role of international agencies and NGOs in promoting health and combating disease
Mission 9

A biologically transmitted disease

  • Malaria is caused by Plasmodium parasites; P. falciparum and P. vivax are the most dangerous.
  • It spreads through bites of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes: the vector. Symptoms usually start 10–15 days after the bite.
  • Communicable, but not contagious: it needs the mosquito.
Mission 9

Global prevalence and distribution

  • 2024: an estimated 282 million cases and 610,000 deaths in 80 countries.
  • WHO African Region: 95% of cases and 95% of deaths.
  • Shares of the Region's malaria deaths: Nigeria 31.9%, DR Congo 11.7%, Niger 6.1%.
  • Prevalence: the share of people infected at one time. Incidence: new cases in a period.
Mission 9

Physical factors: heat, water and height

  • Temperature: a model validated with African data puts optimal transmission at 25 °C, falling sharply above 28 °C.
  • Water: Anopheles lay eggs on water "in marshy areas or near the banks of shallow creeks and streams"; egg to adult takes 10–14 days.
  • Relief: cool highlands limit transmission; in warmer years malaria reached higher altitudes in Ethiopia and Colombia.
  • So risk peaks in warm, humid lowlands close to still or slow water.
Mission 9

Seasonal incidence

  • Risk varies with the season, "being highest during the rainy season in tropical countries": rain creates pools for breeding.
  • WHO's seasonal malaria chemoprevention gives drugs "at designated time points during the period of greatest malarial risk".
  • Exam move: seasonal incidence means a pattern over the year, not just a total; link it to rainfall and temperature.
Quick check
?Where would malaria transmission be most intense?
Mission 9

Socio-economic environment

  • Housing and poverty: open eaves and unscreened windows let mosquitoes in; nets and treatment cost money.
  • Health systems and conflict: testing and treatment need clinics; instability disrupts services.
  • Funding: US$3.9 billion was invested in 2024, less than half the US$9.3 billion target for 2025.
  • Urban spread: An. stephensi "thrives in urban settings, endures high temperatures and is resistant to many of the insecticides"; it has invaded 9 African countries.
Mission 9

Nigeria: prevalence and pattern

  • Malaria Indicator Survey 2021: 22.3% of children aged 6–59 months tested positive by microscopy; 39.6% by rapid diagnostic test (RDT).
  • Microscopy by zone: highest North West 29.8%, lowest South West 16.2% (by RDT the North West was 51.6%).
  • Trend: prevalence "fell from 42% in 2010 to 22% in 2021". Nigeria had 31.9% of the WHO African Region's malaria deaths in 2024.
  • Evaluate: always name the test and the survey dates (12 October to 4 December 2021); methods and seasons give different rates.
Quick check
?Using microscopy (2021), North West Nigeria had 29.8% prevalence in young children and South West had 16.2%. How many times higher was the North West?
Mission 9

Impact on health and well-being

  • Children under 5: about 76% of malaria deaths in the WHO African Region.
  • Infants, young children, pregnant women and people with HIV/AIDS are at particular risk; "partial immunity reduces the risk that malaria infection will cause severe disease". Young children have not yet built it.
  • Socio-economic: Gallup and Sachs (2001) found incomes in intensive-malaria countries were a third of those without, and growth 1.3% lower per person per year (1965–1990).
  • Evaluate: a cross-country correlation; poverty also limits protection, so causation runs both ways.
Mission 9

Direct strategies

  • Vector control: "The 2 core interventions are insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) and indoor residual spraying (IRS)".
  • Treatment: artemisinin-based combination therapies. Prevention: seasonal chemoprevention for young children.
  • Vaccines: RTS,S (2021) and R21 (2023). Nigeria introduced R21 in Kebbi and Bayelsa states in December 2024: 1 million doses, 846,200 from Gavi and 153,800 bought by the government.
Mission 9

International agencies and NGOs

  • WHO (Global Malaria Programme): sets "evidence based global guidance", monitors trends and threats, and shapes research.
  • The Global Fund (a financing partnership): "57% of all international financing for malaria programs"; 196 million nets distributed in 2025.
  • Gavi (vaccine alliance): co-funded Nigeria's R21 doses.
  • NGOs: e.g. the Against Malaria Foundation: "100% of public donations buys long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs)".
  • Together these tools prevented an estimated 170 million cases and 1 million deaths in 2024.
Quick check
?Which role is played mainly by WHO rather than by a funding body such as the Global Fund?
Mission 9

Threats to progress

  • Drug resistance: partial resistance to artemisinin is confirmed in Eritrea, Rwanda, Uganda and Tanzania, and confirmed or suspected in at least 8 African countries.
  • Vector spread: An. stephensi, native to parts of South Asia and the Arabian Peninsula, has invaded 9 African countries.
  • Funding gap: investment below half the target.
Mission check

?Two children aged 2 have caught malaria for the first time. One lives in a cool highland town at 2,800 m; the other in a hot, humid lowland village beside a marsh, where few homes have nets. Which statement best explains which child faces the greater risk over the coming years?

Warm, wet lowlands and rainy seasons suit the parasite and Anopheles; poverty, weak services and funding gaps limit nets and treatment. So 95% of deaths are in the WHO African Region, and about three in four are children under 5, who have little partial immunity.

Mission 10: In 1961 heart and circulatory disease caused over half of all UK deaths. Why has its death rate fallen so far?

Make a guess: In 1961 heart and circulatory disease caused over half of all UK deaths. Why has its death rate fallen so far?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.2.4.3 one specified non-communicable disease (here cardiovascular disease, including coronary heart disease): global prevalence and distribution, links to physical and socio-economic environment and lifestyles, impacts on health and well-being, management and mitigation
  • 3.2.4.3 role of international agencies and NGOs in promoting health and combating disease at the global scale
Mission 10

Cardiovascular disease

  • Non-communicable disease (NCD): not passed from person to person; usually long-lasting.
  • Cardiovascular disease (CVD): "an umbrella term for a range of conditions affecting the heart and blood vessels", including coronary heart disease, heart failure and stroke.
  • Coronary heart disease (CHD): arteries narrowed by "a build-up of atheroma, a fatty material within their walls"; a blockage can cause a heart attack.
  • CHD is "the leading cause of premature death in the UK (before the age of 75)".
Mission 10

Global prevalence and distribution

  • CVDs are "the leading cause of death globally": about 19.8 million deaths in 2022, about 32% of all deaths.
  • 85% of these deaths were due to heart attack and stroke.
  • "Over three quarters of CVD deaths take place in low- and middle-income countries".
  • CVDs caused at least 38% of the 18 million premature NCD deaths (under 70) in 2021.
  • Not just a "disease of affluence": the burden is now greatest where health systems are weakest.
Mission 10

Prevalence and incidence in the UK

  • Prevalence: how many people have the disease at one time. UK: over 8 million people living with CVD.
  • Incidence: how many new cases occur in a period. UK: around 100,000 hospital admissions each year are due to heart attacks.
  • Deaths: about 170,000 a year, 26% of all UK deaths; about 47,000 under age 75.
  • In 1961, CVD caused more than half of all UK deaths (320,000).
Mission 10

Patterns in the UK

  • Early (under 75) CVD death rates are "highest in the north of England, central Scotland and the south of Wales, and lowest in the south of England".
  • Highest local rates (2022–24, per 100,000): Blackpool 136.1, Glasgow City 132.7, Kingston upon Hull 125.3.
  • Blackpool's rate was "more than three times" Wealden's (43).
  • Rates are age-standardised, so the gap is not just older populations.
Mission 10

Lifestyle and environment links

  • About 70% of the UK's CVD burden is linked to modifiable risk factors.
  • High blood pressure: about 30% of UK adults (16 million); linked to about 80,000 CVD deaths a year.
  • Smoking: about 1 in 8 adults; up to 18,000 CVD deaths a year.
  • Obesity: about 30% of adults; 32% of adults are not active enough.
  • Environment: at least 9,000 CVD deaths a year are attributed to particulate air pollution. WHO also lists air pollution as "an important factor".
Mission 10

Social, economic and cultural causes

  • Social and economic: early CVD deaths are highest in places with high deprivation. Blackpool has the highest rate in the UK, and "Seven neighbourhoods in Blackpool rank amongst the top 10 most deprived" in England.
  • Deprivation links to CVD through risk factors such as smoking, diet, inactivity and late diagnosis, which are not spread evenly.
  • Cultural: food habits, alcohol and attitudes to smoking and exercise vary between groups and places; only about 3 in 10 UK adults eat five portions of fruit and vegetables a day.
  • BHF also lists ageing, sex, ethnicity and family history as risk factors.
Quick check
?Blackpool's early CVD death rate is age-standardised and over three times Wealden's. What best explains the gap?
Mission 10

Impacts

  • Health and well-being: around 80% of people with CVD have at least one other health condition.
  • Health system: NHS costs of about £12 billion a year.
  • Economy: about £29 billion a year, including premature death, long-term care, disability and informal care.
  • Uneven impacts: the burden of early death falls hardest on the places with the highest rates, such as Blackpool.
Mission 10

Managing CVD in the UK

  • Direct (screening): the NHS Health Check, for people "aged 40 to 74" without existing heart conditions, offered "every 5 years".
  • Indirect (prevention): the Soft Drinks Industry Levy (from 6 April 2018) on drinks with at least 5 g of sugar per 100 ml; a higher rate applies at 8 g or more.
  • Treatment: high-intensity statins "can reduce cholesterol levels by half".
  • Result over time: the UK's age-standardised CVD death rate has "declined by three quarters" since 1961.
Mission 10

International agencies and NGOs

  • WHO: in 2013 member states agreed a global NCD action plan; in 2016 WHO launched the Global HEARTS Initiative to strengthen CVD prevention "through primary health care, particularly in low- and middle-income countries".
  • UN SDG 3.4: "By 2030, reduce by one third premature mortality from non-communicable diseases".
  • NGOs: the British Heart Foundation funds research and publishes data; since it was established, UK CVD deaths have "fallen by nearly half".
  • Agencies set targets, guidance and data; NGOs fund research, campaign and support patients.
Quick check
?Which is an indirect strategy to reduce CVD?
Mission check

?An emerging economy's CVD deaths are rising fastest in poorer urban districts, where smoking, high blood pressure and air pollution are common. Which plan best fits the evidence from the UK and WHO?

CVD is the world's biggest killer. Its patterns follow risk factors that are shaped by income, lifestyle and environment, so the most effective plans combine screening and treatment with population-wide prevention, backed by agencies and NGOs.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Population numbers, distribution, density and change reflect climate, soils, water and other resources; development shapes change rates.
  • Physical factors set broad limits (China's Hu Line; Egypt's Nile); human factors and development reinforce or loosen them.

Mission 2

  • Asia produces about half the world's cereals; Europe and the Americas have the highest supply per person; Africa the lowest, with the fastest-growing hunger.

Mission 3

  • An agricultural system turns physical and human inputs into outputs; systems are arable, pastoral or mixed, commercial or subsistence, intensive or extensive.
  • Productivity (yield per hectare, output per worker) is limited by climate and soils, as the ALC grades show; inputs can raise the limits, at a cost.

Mission 4

  • Hot arid: aridity index low (hyper-arid below 0.05), near 30° N/S; few people in the cores, pastoralism on the margins. Tropical monsoon: strong wet–dry seasons; dense farming populations in South Asia.
  • Climate change: slowed farm productivity growth, worse in low latitudes; drylands lose more from water stress than they gain from CO₂; monsoon rain increases in South Asia but extremes intensify.

Mission 5

  • Chernozems: black, 10–16% organic matter, neutral, mid-latitude steppes; wheat, barley, maize. Ferralsols: deep, red/yellow, weathered, chemically poor, humid tropics; shifting cultivation or lime and fertiliser.
  • Soil erosion → conservation tillage and cover; waterlogging and salinisation → drainage, lower water table, careful irrigation; structural deterioration → less heavy traffic and tillage, residues and rotations.

Mission 6

  • Strategies run from short-term relief to capacity building and system redesign, and from large-scale technology to small-scale, bottom-up approaches.
  • Brazil (upper-middle income) fixed access through cash and school meals; Singapore (high income) manages import dependence through diverse sources and technology. Both show that results last only if policy lasts.

Mission 7

  • Health is measured by mortality (IMR, MMR) and morbidity; HALE combines them.
  • As living standards rise, infections recede and NCDs dominate: the epidemiological transition.

Mission 8

  • Climate and topography/drainage control vectors such as Anopheles (optimum about 25 °C; more malaria near wet ground in the Kenyan highlands).
  • Air quality: 4.2 million outdoor and about 2.9 million household air pollution deaths; inversions trap pollution. Water quality: 1.7 billion use faecally contaminated sources; about 505,000 diarrhoeal deaths a year.

Mission 9

  • Malaria is vector-borne: temperature near 25 °C, breeding water, low altitude and the rainy season raise transmission.
  • Poverty, housing, health systems, conflict and funding shape who is protected.
  • Nigeria: high prevalence (highest in the North West); direct strategies (nets, IRS, drugs, R21), backed by WHO, the Global Fund, Gavi and NGOs.

Mission 10

  • CVD (including CHD) causes about 32% of global deaths, over three quarters in low- and middle-income countries; in the UK over 8 million live with it and about 170,000 die each year, with early deaths highest in poorer northern areas.
  • Causes are mostly modifiable (blood pressure, smoking, obesity, inactivity, air pollution); impacts include £12 billion NHS and £29 billion economic costs.
  • Management: NHS Health Check (direct), Soft Drinks Industry Levy (indirect), statins; WHO HEARTS, SDG 3.4 and the BHF act at wider scales.
