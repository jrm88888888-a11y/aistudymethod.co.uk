Mission 1

Mission 1: Christ isn't Jesus' surname. So what were his followers claiming when they called him Christ?

Make a guess: Christ isn't Jesus' surname. So what were his followers claiming when they called him Christ?

Mission 1

Why titles matter

  • A title says who someone is and what they are for: "Doctor", "Captain", "King".
  • Jesus' first followers were Jews. They used titles from the Jewish scriptures (the Christian Old Testament) to say who Jesus was.
  • Main titles: Messiah (Christ), Son of God, Son of Man.
  • Each title is a claim: Christians say Jesus fulfils it; others disagree.
Mission 1

Messiah, Christ, Son of David

  • Messiah (Hebrew mashiach) = "anointed one". Kings of Israel were anointed with oil.
  • Christ (Greek Christos) means the same. "Jesus Christ" = "Jesus the Messiah".
  • Many Jews hoped for a Messiah from King David's family who would free Israel and bring God's peace.
  • Christians believe Jesus is the Messiah, but a servant who suffers, not a warrior king. Jews do not accept Jesus as the Messiah: many look for a Messiah, or a messianic age, still to come, bringing peace to the whole world.
Mission 1

Son of God, Son of Man

  • Son of God: the title says Jesus has a unique relationship with God the Father. Christians believe he is the eternal Son, the second Person of the Trinity.
  • Son of Man: Jesus' favourite way of speaking about himself in the Gospels.
  • It stresses his humanity, and that he will suffer (Mark 8:31).
  • It also recalls Daniel 7:13–14: "one like a son of man" who comes with the clouds and is given everlasting authority.
  • So the two titles together express what Christians believe: Jesus is fully human and fully God.

Mission 1Read the source

"You are the Christ"

  • Near Caesarea Philippi, Jesus asks: "Who do you say that I am?"
  • In Mark, Peter says "You are the Christ" (Mark 8:29). Then Jesus teaches "that the Son of Man must suffer many things… and be killed, and after three days rise again" (8:31). Peter rebukes him (8:32–33).

Q1 Which title does Peter use?

Show answer

Christ: the Messiah.

Mission 1Read the source

Before the high priest

At Jesus' trial, "the high priest asked him, 'Are you the Christ, the Son of the Blessed?' Jesus said, 'I am. You will see the Son of Man sitting at the right hand of Power, and coming with the clouds of the sky.'" (Mark 14:61–62)

  • "The Blessed" is a respectful way of saying "God".
  • The high priest tears his clothes and calls it blasphemy (14:63–64).

Q1 Which three titles appear in these two verses?

Show answer

Christ, Son of the Blessed = Son of God, Son of Man.

Q2 Why did this answer lead to his condemnation?

Show answer

He claimed divine authority: to sit at God's right hand.

Quick check
?Soon after Peter calls Jesus the Christ, Jesus says the Son of Man must suffer and be killed (Mark 8:31; Matthew 16:21). Why did Peter object?
Mission 1Evaluate

"Son of God is the most important title for Jesus."

  • Reasons for: it expresses his divinity, central to the Trinity and the creeds.
  • It is the charge at his trial (Mark 14:61–62).
  • Reasons against: "Son of Man" is the title Jesus himself uses most. "Christ" became his name for all Christians.
  • For many, "Saviour" (one who rescues) says most about what he does.
  • Another view: each title needs the others: one says who he is, another what he does.
  • Judge it by: Which title did Jesus use? Which do the creeds stress? Which matters most to Christians today?

Every title is a claim about who Jesus is: Christ (Messiah), Son of God, Son of Man, Lord, Saviour.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Jesus said he came to fulfil the law, then made it harder. What did he expect of his followers?

Make a guess: Jesus said he came to fulfil the law, then made it harder. What did he expect of his followers?

Mission 2

Why Jesus has moral authority

  • Christians believe Jesus is the Son of God and the Word of God made flesh (John 1:14): his teaching is God's teaching.
  • The crowds noticed: "he taught them with authority, and not like the scribes" (Matthew 7:29).
  • He teaches by words (sermons, parables) and by example (how he lived and died).
  • Catholics see Jesus as the model of the virtues: he shows what a fully good human life looks like.
Mission 2Read the source

Fulfilling the law (Matthew 5:17–24)

  • "Don't think that I came to destroy the law or the prophets. I didn't come to destroy, but to fulfil." (Matthew 5:17)
  • Fulfil: to bring the law to its full meaning, and to complete what it pointed towards.
  • Then Jesus goes deeper (5:21–22, paraphrase): the law says "Do not murder"; Jesus says even anger and insults put a person in danger of judgement.
  • Before offering a gift at the altar, first go and make peace with your brother (5:23–24, paraphrase).

Q1 Did Jesus come to abolish the law?

Show answer

No: to fulfil it.

Q2 How does Jesus go beyond "Do not murder"?

Show answer

He forbids the anger that leads to it, and asks for reconciliation first.

Mission 2

The Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5–7)

  • Beatitudes (5:3–12): who is truly blessed.
  • Salt and light (5:13–16): followers should make a difference.
  • Go deeper than the law (5:17–48): anger, adultery, oaths, revenge; "love your enemies" (5:44).
  • Real religion, not show (6:1–18): give, pray (the Lord's Prayer) and fast in secret.
  • Trust, not wealth (6:19–34): "You can't serve both God and Mammon (money)" (6:24).
  • Do not judge; the Golden Rule (7:1–12): treat others as you want to be treated (7:12).
  • Wise and foolish builders (7:24–27): hear these words and do them.
Mission 2Read the source

The Beatitudes (Matthew 5:3–12)

  • Beatitude: from Latin beatus, "blessed" or "truly happy".
  • "Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the Kingdom of Heaven." (5:3)
  • Also blessed (5:4–10, paraphrase): those who mourn; the gentle; those who hunger and thirst for righteousness; the merciful; the pure in heart; the peacemakers; those persecuted for doing right.
  • "Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called children of God." (5:9)
  • They describe virtues: humility, mercy, purity of heart, love of peace.
  • Q: Which Beatitude would encourage a Christian to help settle a quarrel? (Blessed are the peacemakers.)
Quick check
?What did Jesus mean when he said he came to fulfil the law (Matthew 5:17)?
Mission 2Read the source

The sheep and the goats (Matthew 25:31–46)

  • At the final judgement, the King separates people "as a shepherd separates the sheep from the goats" (25:32).
  • The sheep fed the hungry, gave drink to the thirsty, welcomed the stranger, clothed the naked, cared for the sick, visited the prisoner.
  • "Most certainly I tell you, because you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me." (Matthew 25:40)
  • Catholics base the corporal works of mercy on this passage (adding: bury the dead).
  • Q: According to the King, whom were the sheep really serving? (Christ himself, in the people they helped.)
Quick check
?A Christian nurse volunteers at a prison on her day off. Which teaching of Jesus most directly supports what she does?
Mission 2Diversity

How literally should Christians follow it?

  • Literal ideal for all: some (e.g. Quakers, Mennonites) refuse oaths and violence because of Matthew 5:34 and 5:39.
  • Catholic: the commands bind every Christian; some (monks, nuns) also follow "counsels of perfection": poverty, chastity, obedience.
  • Lutheran tradition: the Sermon shows how far everyone falls short, so all need God's grace; it is still a guide for living.
  • Liberal Protestant: principles of love, not fixed rules; apply agape to each situation.
Quick check
?A Quaker refuses to swear an oath in court and instead makes a solemn promise to tell the truth. Which part of Jesus' teaching is she following?
Mission 2Evaluate

"Jesus' moral teaching is too demanding to follow today."

  • Reasons for: Jesus' commands in the Sermon on the Mount, "love your enemies" (Matthew 5:44) and "don't resist him who is evil" (5:39), seem impossible in a violent world. Even anger is condemned (5:22). Lutheran Christians say no one keeps it perfectly.
  • Reasons against: many Christians do live it: e.g. forgiving a family member's killer, or giving up wealth. Jesus promises help: the Spirit and grace (–). The Beatitudes describe God's Kingdom, which Christians grow towards. Ordinary acts of love, such as caring for a stranger in need, count too.
  • Another view: it is a demanding ideal to aim at, not a set of rules to tick off; failure is met with forgiveness.
  • Judge it by: Is it meant as rules or ideals? Does "too demanding" mean impossible or just hard? Do real examples show it can be lived?
Mission check

?A company's code says: "Don't steal from the firm." A new manager tells staff: "Not stealing isn't enough. I want you to want the firm to do well." How does this mirror Jesus' approach to the law?

Christians believe Jesus fulfils the law by taking it to the heart. Christians follow both his words and his example of agape.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Catholics say God reaches people through water, oil and bread. How can ordinary things carry grace?

Make a guess: Catholics say God reaches people through water, oil and bread. How can ordinary things carry grace?

Mission 3

A world that shows God

  • Sacramental nature of reality: Catholics believe the whole of creation can show the presence of God.
  • "The heavens declare the glory of God." (Psalm 19:1)
  • Things that can be seen point to the God who cannot be seen.
  • It flows from the Incarnation: "The Word became flesh and lived amongst us" (John 1:14). God used a human body to show himself.
  • Jesus healed with touch, mud and words, and gave himself in bread and wine. So matter can carry grace.
  • Grace: God's free gift of his own life and help.
  • Catholics therefore see God at work in nature, people and events, and above all in the seven sacraments.
Mission 3

What a sacrament is

  • Sacrament: an outward, visible sign, given by Christ to the Church, that gives the inward grace it signifies (CCC 1131, paraphrase).
  • A long-standing summary: "a visible sign of invisible grace".
  • Each has matter (the sign: water, oil, bread) and form (the words that go with it).
  • Catholics believe Christ himself acts in every sacrament. It works by Christ's power, not the minister's holiness.
  • The fruit depends on the openness of the person receiving it.
  • So sacraments communicate grace: they are not only reminders, they do what they show.
Mission 3Read the source

CCC 1210–1211

Catechism of the Catholic Church 1210–1211 (paraphrase):

  • Christ gave the Church seven sacraments: baptism, confirmation, Eucharist, penance (reconciliation), anointing of the sick, holy orders and matrimony.
  • They touch every stage and key moment of life: they give birth and growth, healing and mission to the life of faith.
  • This mirrors natural life: being born, growing up, eating, being healed, serving others.
  • So there are three groups: initiation, healing, and sacraments at the service of communion (holy orders, matrimony).
  • The Eucharist has a unique place: the "sacrament of sacraments"; all the others lead to it.

Q1 Which three sacraments are sacraments of initiation?

Show answer

Baptism, confirmation, Eucharist.

Q2 Why does the Catechism compare the sacraments to natural life?

Show answer

Each matches a stage: birth, growth, food, healing, service.

Mission 3

Seven sacraments, seven effects

  • Baptism: new birth; original sin and all sins washed away; member of the Church.
  • Confirmation: strengthened by the Holy Spirit to live and witness to the faith.
  • Eucharist: receiving Christ himself; food for the journey; unity with the Church.
  • Reconciliation: forgiveness of sins committed after baptism.
  • Anointing of the sick: strength, peace and healing in serious illness.
  • Matrimony: grace for a faithful, lifelong love; holy orders: a deacon, priest or bishop given to serve the Church.
  • Sanctification: each sacrament makes a stage of life holy, from birth to death.

?Which pair are both sacraments of healing?
Mission 3

Baptism: the ceremony

  • Usually for babies, at the font; parents and godparents present the child.
  • Sign of the cross traced on the forehead: the child belongs to Christ.
  • Anointing with oil of catechumens: strength against evil.
  • Parents and godparents reject sin and profess the faith for the child.
  • Water poured three times (or immersion): "N., I baptise you in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit" (from Matthew 28:19).
  • Anointing with chrism on the head: sharing Christ's role as priest, prophet and king.
  • White garment: a new life, "clothed in Christ". Candle lit from the Easter (Paschal) candle: the light of the risen Christ.
Mission 3

Baptism: what it does

  • Catholics believe that baptism:
  • Washes away original sin and all sin.
  • New birth as a child of God; a member of the Church, the Body of Christ: "in one Spirit we were all baptised into one body" (1 Corinthians 12:13).
  • Gives sanctifying grace; leaves a permanent spiritual mark, so it is never repeated.
  • Water means both death and life: "buried… with him through baptism into death… so we also might walk in newness of life" (Romans 6:4).
  • Into the life of the Trinity: baptised "in the name" of Father, Son and Spirit, a person is drawn into God's own life.
  • Adopted by the Father; joined to the Son; the Spirit lives in them: "God sent out the Spirit of his Son into your hearts, crying, 'Abba, Father!'" (Galatians 4:6)
Mission 3

Confirmation

  • Completes the grace of baptism. Usually received in the teenage years; in Ireland, often near the end of primary school.
  • The bishop is the usual minister. Candidates renew their baptismal promises; a sponsor stands with each one.
  • The bishop extends his hands over them and prays for the seven gifts of the Holy Spirit: wisdom, understanding, counsel, fortitude, knowledge, piety, fear of the Lord (from Isaiah 11:2).
  • Anointing with chrism on the forehead: "Be sealed with the Gift of the Holy Spirit."
  • Roots: "Then they laid their hands on them, and they received the Holy Spirit." (Acts 8:17)
  • Meaning: a closer bond with Christ and the Church; strength to witness; a permanent mark, received once. Many take a saint's name.
Mission 3

The Eucharist completes initiation

  • First Holy Communion, usually around age 7–8, after first reconciliation.
  • Baptism gives new life; confirmation strengthens it; the Eucharist feeds it, again and again.
  • Catholics believe they receive Christ himself under the appearances of bread and wine.
  • Effects: closer union with Christ, forgiveness of lesser (venial) sins, unity with the whole Church.
  • Adults joining the Church usually receive all three together at the Easter Vigil.
Quick check
?At a Catholic baptism, a lighted candle, lit from the Easter (Paschal) candle, is given to the family. What does it mean?
Mission 3Evaluate

"Catholics need the sacraments to receive God's grace."

  • For: Christ gave the sacraments himself: "baptising them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 28:19). The Church teaches that for believers they are necessary for salvation (CCC 1129, paraphrase). Humans are bodily: visible signs meet them where they are.
  • Against: the sacramental view of reality says God's grace is found everywhere: in creation, prayer, Scripture and other people. The Catechism says God has bound salvation to baptism but is not himself bound by his sacraments (CCC 1257, paraphrase).
  • Other Christians: most Protestants stress grace received through faith and the Word; Quakers through inward experience.
  • Judge it by: Is "need" the same as "only"? What did Christ command? Can grace be found outside the sacraments and still lead to them?
Mission check

?At the Easter Vigil, Tom, an adult, is baptised, confirmed and receives his first Holy Communion in one service. How does Catholic teaching understand this?

For Catholics, ordinary things (water, oil, bread) become signs through which Christ really gives his grace.

Mission 4

Mission 4: If only God can forgive sins, why do Catholics confess them to a priest?

Make a guess: If only God can forgive sins, why do Catholics confess them to a priest?

Mission 4

Sacraments of healing and service

  • Healing: Jesus forgave sins and healed the sick. Catholics believe he continues both through two sacraments.
  • Reconciliation heals the soul from sin; anointing of the sick strengthens the seriously ill.
  • At the service of communion: two sacraments given for building up others.
  • Matrimony builds a family; holy orders gives the Church its deacons, priests and bishops.
  • Sanctification: each one makes a moment of life holy: failure, illness, love, service.
Mission 4

Reconciliation: the rite

  • Also called confession or penance. For sins committed after baptism.
  • Four parts:
  • Contrition: real sorrow for sin and a firm intention not to sin again.
  • Confession: telling one's sins to a priest, in a confessional or reconciliation room.
  • Penance (satisfaction): a prayer or action the priest gives, to help repair the harm.
  • Absolution: the priest says, "I absolve you from your sins in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit."
  • The seal of confession: a priest may never reveal what he hears, for any reason.
  • Catholics must confess grave (mortal) sins at least once a year; many go far more often, especially in Advent and Lent.
Mission 4

Why a priest?

  • Catholics agree: "Who can forgive sins but God alone?" (Mark 2:7). The priest does not forgive by his own power.
  • The risen Jesus gave the apostles this task: "If you forgive anyone's sins, they have been forgiven them." (John 20:23)
  • Catholics believe bishops and priests share it through ordination; the priest acts in the person of Christ.
  • Sin also harms the Church, so the penitent is reconciled with God and the community.
  • People need to name their sins and hear that they are forgiven.
  • Effects: forgiveness, peace of conscience, restored friendship with God, strength to resist sin.
Quick check
?During confession, a priest asks a girl who stole from a friend to pray a decade of the Rosary and to pay the money back. What is this part of the sacrament called?
Mission 4

Anointing of the sick

  • "Is any amongst you sick? Let him call for the elders of the assembly, and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord" (James 5:14)
  • For: anyone seriously ill, frail through old age, or facing major surgery. It can be repeated if the illness gets worse.
  • Rite: the priest lays his hands on the person in silence, then anoints the forehead and hands with the Oil of the Sick, blessed by the bishop in Holy Week.
  • Effects: strength, peace and courage; union with Christ's own suffering; forgiveness of sins; healing of body, if God wills.
  • "The Lord will raise him up. If he has committed sins, he will be forgiven." (James 5:15)
  • Once called "extreme unction"; since the Second Vatican Council it is not only for the dying.
Mission 4

The last rites

  • For a Catholic near death, the priest offers the last rites, ideally in this order:
  • Reconciliation: confession and absolution, if the person is able: peace with God before death.
  • Anointing of the sick: healing: strength, peace and courage, even when the body will not recover.
  • Viaticum (Latin: "provisions for the journey"): a last Holy Communion, food for the journey through death.
  • Hope: "He who eats my flesh and drinks my blood has eternal life, and I will raise him up at the last day." (John 6:54)
  • Family often pray the prayers for the dying with the priest. The funeral rite follows.

Mission 4

Matrimony

  • In the Western (Latin) Catholic Church, the bride and groom are the ministers: they give the sacrament to each other by their free consent.
  • The priest or deacon receives their consent for the Church; two witnesses are present.
  • Rite: questions (freely? faithfully? open to children?), vows, blessing and exchange of rings, nuptial blessing; often within Mass.
  • "The two will become one flesh… What therefore God has joined together, let no man separate." (Mark 10:8–9)
  • A sign of Christ's love: "Husbands, love your wives, even as Christ also loved the assembly and gave himself up for her" (Ephesians 5:25).
  • Effects: a lifelong, exclusive bond; grace to love faithfully; the family as a "domestic church".
Mission 4

Holy orders and the role of the priest

  • Three degrees: deacon, priest, bishop. Only a bishop ordains.
  • Ordination: the bishop lays hands on the candidate's head in silence and says the prayer of consecration. A new priest's hands are anointed with chrism; he receives the chalice and paten.
  • "Stir up the gift of God which is in you through the laying on of my hands." (2 Timothy 1:6)
  • It leaves a permanent mark: once a priest, always a priest. The Church ordains only baptised men; in the Latin Church priests promise celibacy.
  • Role of the priest: celebrates Mass ("Do this in memory of me", Luke 22:19), hears confessions, anoints the sick, baptises, marries, buries; preaches; cares for the parish.
  • He acts in the person of Christ and links the parish to the bishop and, through him, to the apostles.
Mission 4

Vocation: called by God

  • Vocation (Latin vocare, "to call"): God's call to each person. Every baptised Catholic is called to holiness.
  • Kingdom values (justice, peace, love, service) shape each path: "Let your Kingdom come" (Matthew 6:10).
  • Priesthood: serving God's people through the sacraments and preaching.
  • Family: marriage and parenthood; the home as a "domestic church" where faith is first learned.
  • Religious life: vows of poverty, chastity and obedience; contemplative (e.g. Carmelites) or active (e.g. Sisters of Mercy).
  • Community: single life, parish ministries, groups such as the St Vincent de Paul Society, serving the poor.
  • Jesus' rule for all of them: "whoever wants to become great amongst you shall be your servant" (Mark 10:43).

Mission 4Evaluate

"Catholics should not need a priest to be forgiven."

  • Claim: confession to a priest is unnecessary, because God forgives directly.
  • For: "Who can forgive sins but God alone?" (Mark 2:7). "There is one God and one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus" (1 Timothy 2:5). Most Protestants confess directly to God (1 John 1:9). Catholic teaching itself says perfect sorrow, out of love for God, brings forgiveness, with the intention to confess (CCC 1452, paraphrase).
  • Against: Jesus gave the apostles authority to forgive (John 20:23). Sin harms the Church, so reconciliation with the Church matters. Hearing the words of absolution brings assurance. The priest acts for Christ, not instead of him.
  • Judge it by: Who does the forgiving? Is sin only private? What helps a person really change?
Mission check

?At a school retreat, a priest hears confessions and says to each person, "I absolve you from your sins…". A Protestant friend asks how a priest can forgive sins. Which answer fits Catholic teaching?

For Catholics, the priest is the instrument and God the forgiver: confession is meeting Christ through his Church.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Christ = Messiah = "anointed one": the king Jews hoped for from David's family.
  • Son of God expresses Jesus' unique relationship with the Father; Son of Man his humanity, suffering and future glory.

Mission 2

  • Christians see Jesus as the Word of God: his teaching and example carry God's authority.
  • In Matthew 5:17–24 Jesus says he came to fulfil the law, and he goes deeper, to attitudes such as anger.
  • The Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5–7) sets out the way of life he expected; Christians differ on how literally to follow it.
  • The Beatitudes describe who is truly blessed.
  • The sheep and the goats (Matthew 25): serving people in need is serving Christ.

Mission 3

  • Catholics believe all creation can show God (sacramental reality), and the seven sacraments are visible signs that really give grace.
  • CCC 1210–1211: seven sacraments in three groups: initiation, healing, service of communion; the Eucharist at the centre.
  • Baptism gives new birth and membership of the Church; confirmation seals with the Spirit; the Eucharist completes initiation.

Mission 4

  • Reconciliation (contrition, confession, penance, absolution) and anointing of the sick are sacraments of healing; Catholics believe Christ forgives and strengthens through the priest.
  • Matrimony (the couple are the ministers) and holy orders (deacon, priest, bishop) are sacraments at the service of communion.
  • The last rites (reconciliation, anointing, viaticum) express reconciliation, healing and hope; every Catholic has a vocation shaped by Kingdom values.
