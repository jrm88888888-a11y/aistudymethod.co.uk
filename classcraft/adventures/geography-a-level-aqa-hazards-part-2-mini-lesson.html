Mission 1: Hurricane Ian hit Florida with 130-knot winds in 2022. Why was water, not wind, its deadliest hazard?

Make a guess: Hurricane Ian hit Florida with 130-knot winds in 2022. Why was water, not wind, its deadliest hazard?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.1.5.5 nature and causes of tropical storms; hazards: high winds, storm surges, coastal flooding, river flooding, landslides; distribution, magnitude, frequency, regularity, predictability
  • 3.1.5.5 impacts and responses of two recent tropical storms in contrasting areas (Ian here)
Mission 1

What a tropical storm is

  • A tropical cyclone is "a rapidly rotating storm that begins over tropical oceans"; it is called a hurricane or typhoon depending on the region.
  • The Saffir–Simpson scale rates hurricanes 1 to 5 by maximum sustained wind only. Category 4 is 113–136 kt; categories 3–5 are major hurricanes.
  • The scale "does not take into account" storm surge, rainfall flooding or tornadoes.
Mission 1

Structure

  • Eye: a "nearly cloud-free area of light winds", generally 20–50 km across.
  • Eyewall: a ring of towering thunderstorms around the eye, where warm air turns upward. "Usually, the strongest winds and heaviest precipitation are found in this area".
  • Rain bands: spiral inwards towards the eyewall, bringing heavy rain and wind, occasionally tornadoes, with rain-free gaps between them.
Mission 1

How they form

  • Ocean water of about 27 °C (80 °F) at the surface, warm to about 45 m (150 ft) depth.
  • An atmosphere that cools quickly with height and is moist in the middle troposphere; low vertical wind shear.
  • Enough Coriolis spin: at least about 480 km (300 miles) from the equator.
  • A pre-existing disturbance: about 85% of intense Atlantic hurricanes begin as African Easterly Waves. The conditions are "necessary but not sufficient".
Quick check
?Why do tropical cyclones almost never form on the equator, even over warm sea?
Mission 1

Distribution, frequency and magnitude

  • Distribution: tropical cyclones form over tropical waters between 25°S and 25°N.
  • Regularity: seasons run July–October in the Northern Hemisphere and December–March in the Southern, with differences by basin; globally, September is the most active month. The north-west Pacific has them all year.
  • Frequency: an average Atlantic season (1991–2020) has 14 named storms, 7 hurricanes and 3 major hurricanes.
  • Magnitude: measured by wind (Saffir–Simpson), but impacts also depend on size, surge and rain.
  • WMO: over the past 50 years, 1,945 disasters attributed to tropical cyclones killed 779,324 people.
Mission 1

Storm surge

  • Storm surge: "an abnormal water level rise generated by a storm over and above the predicted astronomical tide".
  • Mainly driven by strong onshore winds; low air pressure adds a little more.
  • Higher on a wide, gently sloping continental shelf, and on a concave coast.
  • Surge plus high tide gives the storm tide, which floods low coasts in hours.
Mission 1

Case study: Hurricane Ian, 2022

  • Ian intensified rapidly over waters of 30 °C and higher, with low wind shear.
  • 27 September: landfall near La Coloma, Pinar del Río, Cuba, as a 110-kt category 3 hurricane.
  • 28 September: peak of 140 kt (category 5), then landfall on Cayo Costa, Florida, at 130 kt (category 4).
Mission 1

Ian's surge on two coasts

  • Florida (developed): peak surge inundation of 3–4.6 m (10–15 ft) above ground at Fort Myers Beach; parts of the Sanibel Causeway washed away.
  • Cuba (developing): at La Coloma the sea reached 2.5–3 m and penetrated 300 m inland; at Majana and Cajío, up to 1,500 m inland.
  • Florida's coast is low and its shelf wide and gentle, which favours a high surge.
Mission 1

Rain, rivers and floods inland

  • Ian's rain caused "considerable and deadly freshwater flooding" in Florida; the highest total was about 685 mm (26.95 inches) at Grove City.
  • The Peace, Myakka and Alafia Rivers and Horse Creek "crested to record levels".
  • So a tropical storm brings coastal flooding (surge) and river flooding (rain), often far apart.
Mission 1

Landslides

  • On steep, saturated slopes, heavy rain triggers landslides.
  • Hurricane Maria hit Puerto Rico on 20 September 2017 and "triggered more than 40,000 landslides in at least three-fourths of Puerto Rico's 78 municipalities".
  • Ian struck a mostly flat peninsula, so its hazards were surge, wind and floods instead.
Mission 1

Impacts: developed and developing

  • USA: at least 156 deaths, 66 directly caused by the storm: surge 41, freshwater flooding 12, wind 4.
  • US damage: an estimated $112.9 billion (90% confidence interval $86.8–135.9 billion). In Lee County, 52,514 structures impacted, 5,369 destroyed.
  • Cuba: 5 deaths (3 direct); more than 100,000 homes damaged or destroyed; the whole island reportedly lost power for a few days.
  • There is "no monetary damage estimate available for Cuba", so losses cannot be compared in dollars.
Quick check
?What percentage of Ian's direct deaths in the USA did storm surge cause?
Mission 1

Predictability and responses

  • Tracks are forecast days ahead. The storm surge watch and warning for Fort Myers Beach came 48 and 30 hours before tropical-storm-force winds.
  • The surge forecast there rose from 1.2–2.1 m (4–7 ft) to 2.4–3.7 m (8–12 ft), then 3.7–5.5 m (12–18 ft); intensity errors were above average because of the rapid intensification.
  • Response: by September 2024, US federal support topped $10.2 billion, including $1.15 billion for 387,139 households.
  • Exam move: compare your two storms by hazard, development level and response timescale (short term: rescue and aid; long term: rebuilding and insurance).
Mission check

?In 1953 a North Sea storm, not a hurricane, killed 307 people in England and 1,836 in the Netherlands. Why can a storm's water kill more people than its wind?

Ian's onshore winds drove a 3–4.6 m (10–15 ft) surge over low islands at Fort Myers Beach; surge caused 41 of the 66 direct US deaths (62%). The Saffir–Simpson scale measures wind, but on low, gently shelving coasts the sea is the main killer.

Mission 2

Two storms, two places

  • Hurricane Ian, 2022: landfall in western Cuba on 27 September as a 110-knot category 3; then Cayo Costa, Florida, on 28 September at 130 knots.
  • Cyclone Idai, 2019: landfall near Beira, Mozambique, at 23:30 GMT on 14 March, with winds above 150 km/h. Official death toll in Mozambique: 602 by 7 April 2019.
  • The United States is a high-income economy; Mozambique is low-income (World Bank, FY2027).
  • Two recent storms in contrasting areas: North America and southern Africa; high income and low income.
Mission 2

Sorting impacts

  • Primary impacts: caused directly by wind, rain and storm surge (drowning, destroyed homes).
  • Secondary impacts: follow later from the primary ones (disease, power cuts, lost harvests, lost jobs).
  • Sort by type too: environmental, social, economic, political.
  • A strong answer links them: surge (primary) → destroyed crops (economic) → food insecurity (social) → aid appeals (political).
Mission 2

Ian in Florida: impacts

  • At least 156 deaths, 66 caused directly by the storm; storm surge caused 41, with 36 of these in Lee County.
  • Surge reached 3–4.6 m (10–15 ft) above ground level at Fort Myers Beach.
  • In Lee County, 52,514 structures were impacted and 5,369 destroyed.
  • Parts of the Sanibel causeway were washed away; Sanibel and Pine Islands could be reached only by boat or helicopter.
  • 4.45 million customers (about 9.62 million people) lost power.
  • Leading cause of 90 indirect deaths: lack of timely medical care (18).
  • Damage in the US: $112.9 billion.
Quick check
?After Ian, storm water carried fertiliser and debris into the Gulf of Mexico, followed by a harmful algal bloom. How is this impact best classified?
Mission 2

Ian: responses

  • Short-term: over 250 water rescues in central and eastern Florida.
  • Long-term: federal support topped $10.2 billion in two years, including $1.15 billion to 387,139 households.
  • Flood insurance (NFIP) paid $4.67 billion to about 47,000 policyholders.
  • Cuba (contrast): 76,221 people evacuated under Civil Defence guidelines; 5 deaths; over 100,000 homes damaged or destroyed; the whole island lost power for a few days.
  • In Cuba, losses in tobacco, a key export, were described as "disastrous". The IFRC notes Cuba's exclusion from major international financial institutions "complicates its disaster response".
Mission 2

Management in place: forecasts and warnings

  • The National Hurricane Center (NHC) began talking to emergency managers on 23 September, four days before Cuba's landfall.
  • At Fort Myers Beach, the surge watch and warning came 48 h and 30 h before tropical-storm-force winds.
  • The forecast cone contained the landfall point in every advisory.
  • But on the morning of 27 September the surge forecast rose from 1.2–2.1 m (4–7 ft) to 2.4–3.7 m (8–12 ft), and finally to 3.7–5.5 m (12–18 ft).
  • The NHC could not predict Ian's second rapid intensification to 140 knots.
Quick check
?Which evidence best supports the judgement that Florida's warnings were only partly successful?
Mission 2

Idai in Mozambique: impacts

  • Official death toll 602 by 7 April 2019.
  • 239,682 houses destroyed or damaged; at least 715,378 ha of farmland damaged.
  • 3,359 classrooms destroyed; the electricity grid in Beira was not working.
  • Cholera followed: 3,161 cases registered by 7 April.
  • About 3 million people affected across Malawi, Mozambique and Zimbabwe.
  • Political and economic reach: the storm damaged the transport corridor linking Beira with Malawi, Zambia and Zimbabwe.
Mission 2

Idai: responses

  • Before: the regional forecasting centre (RSMC La Réunion) called it "extremely dangerous", especially if surge met high tide.
  • Short-term: 160,927 people sheltering in 164 sites; 756,200 received food assistance; 593,000 given oral cholera vaccine (73% of target).
  • The UN-coordinated response plan sought US$337.2 million but was just over 20% funded by 7 April.
  • Long-term: recovery costs estimated at over US$2.0 billion across the three countries.
  • Governments and partners committed to climate-resilient schools, roads and power; better regional forecasting; crisis preparedness; disaster risk financing.
Quick check
?Which of these was a long-term response to Cyclone Idai?
Mission 2

Why such contrasting outcomes?

  • Exposure and value: Florida's coast held costly, insured property, so the economic loss was huge.
  • Vulnerability: in Mozambique, homes, farmland and schools were damaged on a vast scale, and disease spread after the flood.
  • Capacity to respond: Ian's recovery drew on federal funds and insurance; Idai's relied on an appeal only about 20% funded.
  • Caution: Ian's $112.9 billion is a damage estimate; Idai's $2 billion is a recovery cost estimate. They are not like-for-like.
  • Exam move ("Assess"): compare the same categories for both storms, then weigh wealth against physical factors (surge height, landfall near a city).
Mission check

?Two cyclones of similar strength strike two coasts. Country X (high income): $40 billion damage, 20 deaths. Country Y (low income): $1 billion damage, 900 deaths and a cholera outbreak. Which explanation best fits?

Wealth changes what a storm costs. Rich places lose more money because their exposed property is worth more, but they can warn, shelter, insure and rebuild. Poorer places lose more lives and livelihoods, and secondary impacts such as disease and food insecurity last longer.

Mission 3

The nature of wildfires

  • A wildfire is an unplanned fire burning in vegetation.
  • Fire needs fuel, heat and oxygen.
  • Types: ground fires (smouldering in peat and roots), surface fires (grass, litter, shrubs), crown fires (through tree canopies).
  • Scale: in 2023, fires worldwide released an estimated 2,100 megatonnes of carbon; Canada almost 480.
Mission 3

Vegetation and fuel characteristics

  • Moisture: "Moisture level is the most important consideration". Dead, dry fuel ignites first.
  • Size: fine fuels (grass, needles, twigs) dry and ignite faster than logs.
  • Load: the amount of fuel per area sets how much heat a fire can release.
  • Continuity: unbroken fuel carries fire sideways; ladder fuels (shrubs, low branches) carry it into the crowns. Invasive grasses increase fuel continuity in desert scrub.
Mission 3

Fuel-limited and drought-limited places

  • Fuel-limited (deserts, some shrublands): too little fuel most years. "Anomalously wet periods can promote vegetation growth, temporarily increasing fire risk".
  • Hydroclimatic rebound: wet periods followed by intense drying create "highly flammable fuel".
  • Fuel-rich forests: there is always fuel; extreme fires follow prolonged drought, typically up to 11 months before the fire.
  • So the "right" weather for a big fire depends on the vegetation type.
Quick check
?In a semi-desert, a very wet winter is followed by a hot, dry summer. Why might the fire risk be higher than after a dry winter?
Mission 3

Climate and recent weather

  • Climate sets the fire season: for example, wet winters and dry summers in Mediterranean climates.
  • Fire weather: hot, dry and windy conditions together.
  • Human influence has likely increased fire weather "in some regions of all inhabited continents (medium confidence)".
  • Recent weather: in California's shrublands, extreme fires follow wet spells 6–27 months earlier, then a "drying cascade" in the 6 months before ignition. Before the Eaton fire (January 2025), drying began about 6 months before ignition.
Mission 3

Fire behaviour

  • The fire behaviour triangle: "fuels, weather, and topography".
  • Slope: fire "will typically move more quickly uphill"; flames preheat the fuel above.
  • Wind brings "a fresh supply of oxygen" and drives flames and embers forward.
  • Santa Ana winds: "strong, dry, downslope winds" in Southern California; in January 2025 they reached 60–80 mph, gusting to 100 mph.
Quick check
?Which set of conditions gives the most intense, fastest-spreading fire?
Mission 3

Natural causes

  • Lightning is the main natural ignition.
  • In the US (1992–2012), lightning fires were concentrated in a shorter season; the human-caused fire season was three times longer.
  • Other natural ignitions (for example, volcanic activity) are rare.
  • Some ecosystems are adapted to regular natural fire.
Mission 3

Human causes

  • US records, 1992–2012: human-started fires were 84% of all wildfires and 44% of the area burned.
  • People "added an average of 40,000 wildfires per year" and extended burning into spring, autumn and winter.
  • Sources include debris burning, equipment, power lines, discarded cigarettes and arson.
  • Human ignitions happen where people are: often in the wildland–urban interface.
Quick check
?In the US (1992–2012), human-started fires were 84% of fires and 44% of the area burned; lightning fires were 16% and 56%. On average, a lightning fire burned about how many times the area of a human-started fire?
Mission 3

When the factors line up

  • Intense fires need several things at once:
    • plenty of dry, continuous fuel (vegetation type, recent weather);
    • fire weather (heat, low humidity, strong wind);
    • topography that speeds spread;
    • an ignition, natural or human.
  • Ignition decides when and where a fire starts; fuel, weather and slope decide how big and fast it gets.
Mission check

?In a Mediterranean region, a very wet spring is followed by a record-hot, dry summer. A fire starts from a spark from farm machinery on a windy hillside. A news report says: "The farmer caused this disaster." Which judgement is best supported?

Wildfires need fuel, heat and oxygen. Intense fires occur when vegetation and recent weather create dry, continuous fuel, fire weather is hot, dry and windy, and slope speeds spread. Natural (lightning) and human ignitions start fires; most recorded US fires are human-started.

Mission 4

The event: Los Angeles, January 2025

  • 7 January 2025: the Palisades fire started at 10:30 am; the Eaton fire at 6:18 pm.
  • Santa Ana winds of 60–80 mph, gusts to 100 mph, and dry weather drove both fires.
  • Area: 23,448 acres (Palisades) + 14,021 acres (Eaton) ≈ 152 km² (our calculation).
  • Both were contained on 31 January, after 24 days.
Mission 4

Primary impacts

  • Structures destroyed: 6,845 (Palisades) + 9,419 (Eaton) = 16,264 (our calculation).
  • Damaged: 975 + 1,076 = 2,051 (our calculation).
  • Deaths: 12 + 19 = 31 confirmed civilian fatalities.
  • Causes listed by CAL FIRE: Palisades "Arson"; Eaton "Electrical".
Mission 4

Secondary impacts

  • Social: over 180,000 residents displaced; more than 150,000 under evacuation notice a week after the fires began. Evacuation brings stress and disrupts work, school and health care.
  • Health: wildfire-related emergency visits rose eightfold as a share of all visits.
  • Environmental: about 28% of structural-ash samples exceeded the US EPA's 200 ppm lead screening level.
  • Economic: losses estimated at $76–131 billion.
  • Political: pressure on state-backed insurance. The fires signalled "major disruption to wildfire risk transfer markets"; California's FAIR Plan (insurer of last resort) had already grown from $115 billion to $433 billion of exposure in 2020–2024.
Quick check
?Which is a secondary, environmental impact of the fires?
Mission 4

Same fire, unequal impacts

  • Median per-capita income: Palisades area $140,933; Eaton area $70,253.
  • Both areas were mostly single-family homes.
  • Lower incomes and savings can mean less insurance cover and slower rebuilding.
  • Vulnerability differs inside one disaster, even in a high-income country.
Mission 4

Short-term responses

  • Evacuation and firefighting during the 24 days of burning.
  • Federal disaster declaration: 8 January 2025.
  • FEMA's Individuals and Households Program: $140.9 million approved; 34,708 applications approved.
  • Clean-up: US EPA removed household hazardous materials (Phase 1); the US Army Corps of Engineers removed debris and 15 cm of soil from ash footprints (Phase 2). In total about 2.5 million tonnes of hazardous materials, ash and debris.
Quick check
?FEMA approved $140,882,476.67 for 34,708 approved applications. What was the average per approved application?
Mission 4

Risk management: four approaches

  • Prevention: reduce ignitions. In US records (1992–2012), 84% of wildfires were human-started; power lines, equipment and arson are targets.
  • Mitigation: reduce damage, for example through defensible space and ember-resistant building.
  • Preparedness: forecasts, warnings, evacuation plans. Including recent fuel conditions improved fire forecasts for the 2025 fires.
  • Adaptation: change how and where people live: building rules, land-use planning, insurance.
Mission 4

Defensible space (mitigation)

  • California law requires 100 feet of defensible space (Public Resources Code 4291).
  • Zone 0 (0–5 ft): "The first five feet from your home is the most important"; non-combustible surfaces.
  • Zone 1 (to 30 ft): "Lean, Clean and Green" — dead plants and leaves removed.
  • Zone 2 (to 100 ft): grass mown to a "maximum height of four inches"; spacing between shrubs and trees.
Quick check
?Which measure is adaptation rather than mitigation, prevention or preparedness?
Mission 4

Long-term responses: evaluation

  • Rebuilding: rebuild where homes stood, or change where people live?
  • Insurance: FAIR plans fill a gap but may do "little to discourage development in high-risk areas".
  • Health: emergency data "likely underestimate" smoke-related illness; after clean-up, under 5% of scraped-area samples exceeded 200 ppm lead.
  • Equity: recovery speed depends on income, insurance and savings.
Mission 4

Exam move: using a recent wildfire

  • Name the event, date, place and scale (area, structures, deaths).
  • Sort impacts by primary/secondary and by social, economic, environmental, political.
  • Sort responses by short/long-term and by prevention, mitigation, preparedness, adaptation.
  • Evaluate: what worked, for whom, and what risk remains.
Mission check

?A town in a fire-prone shrubland region has just lost 300 homes to a wind-driven fire. Which recovery plan best reduces future impacts?

The January 2025 Los Angeles fires show primary impacts (31 deaths, over 16,000 structures destroyed) and secondary impacts (displacement, health, toxic ash, $76–131 billion losses, insurance pressures). Responses ranged from evacuation and federal aid to clean-up and rebuilding rules; long-term risk management combines prevention, mitigation, preparedness and adaptation.

Mission 5

A multi-hazardous environment

  • Japan lies on the Circum-Pacific Volcanic Belt ("Ring of Fire"), where several plates meet.
  • It has "roughly a tenth of all earthquakes" in the world and about 10% of the world's active volcanoes: 111 active volcanoes.
  • It also suffers typhoons, torrential rain and heavy snow.
  • Multi-hazardous: tectonic, atmospheric and hydrological hazards in one place.
Mission 5

Many hazards, many disasters

  • Dead and missing, selected disasters:
    • 1959 Typhoon Vera (Ise-wan): 5,098
    • 1995 Great Hanshin-Awaji Earthquake: 6,437
    • 2011 Great East Japan Earthquake (Mw 9.0): 22,332
    • 2018 July heavy rain: 271 · 2019 Typhoon Hagibis: 108
    • 2024 Noto Peninsula Earthquake: 551
  • Yet 123.8 million people live there (2024).
Mission 5

The risk ahead: the Nankai Trough

  • An 80% likelihood of a magnitude 8–9 earthquake along the Nankai Trough within 30 years (as of January 2025).
  • Worst-case estimate: up to 298,000 deaths, 215,000 of them from the tsunami.
  • One event, several hazards: shaking, tsunami, fire and building collapse.
  • Risk = hazard × exposure × vulnerability: Japan's coasts are where its people and industry are.
Quick check
?Which best explains why Japan is a multi-hazardous environment?
Mission 5

Four ways to live with hazards

  • Mitigation: reduce the impact of an event (e.g. strengthen buildings, levees).
  • Adaptation: change where and how people live (e.g. land-use limits, evacuation routes).
  • Management: plan and organise before, during and after (laws, warnings, drills).
  • Resilience: the ability to absorb, recover and learn from an event.
  • Occupation is sustained when all four work together.
Mission 5

Management: the Basic Act

  • Typhoon Ise-wan (1959) caused immense damage and "led to the enactment of the Basic Act on Disaster Management in 1961".
  • The Act sets a "comprehensive and strategic disaster management system".
  • It has been revised "following the lessons learned from large-scale disasters".
  • After the 2014 Mount Ontake eruption (58 deaths), the Act on Special Measures for Active Volcanoes was amended.
Mission 5

Prediction and warning

  • Seismometers at about 1,800 locations locate earthquakes; about 4,400 measure shaking.
  • Earthquake Early Warning: the Japan Meteorological Agency analyses the first P-waves near the source and sends alerts by TV, radio and mobile phones.
  • 50 volcanoes are watched 24 hours a day.
  • Five volcanic alert levels, each tied to an action: "Evacuate; Prepare to Evacuate; Entry Restricted".
Quick check
?A phone alarm sounds a few seconds before strong shaking, and people take cover. Which response type is this mainly?
Mission 5

Mitigation: the Nankai plan

  • Goal for the next 10 years: cut estimated deaths of about 298,000 by about 80%, and building collapses of about 2.35 million by about 50%.
  • Retrofit buildings: collapses from shaking fall from about 1,279,000 (90% of buildings earthquake-resistant) to about 359,000 (100%).
  • Fix furniture, fit earthquake-sensitive circuit breakers, strengthen coastal levees.
Quick check
?Estimated tsunami deaths: about 215,000 if few evacuate early, about 73,000 if everyone starts within 10 minutes. By what percentage would prompt evacuation cut deaths?
Mission 5

Adaptation: hazard maps and land use

  • Flood hazard maps exist in about 99% of municipalities beside rivers with flood warnings, and about 67% beside small and medium rivers (July 2025).
  • Volcano hazard maps cover 50 volcanoes; sediment (landslide) maps cover 1,602 municipalities.
  • Since 2020 the law promotes "Suppression of building new property in disaster hazard areas" and "moving out from disaster hazard areas".
  • Tsunami evacuation towers and slopes on coastal embankments shorten the route to safety.
Mission 5

Resilience, and its limits

  • Each large disaster has led to new laws or plans (1959 → 1961; 2011 → Nankai plan built for the "largest scientifically conceivable" earthquake and tsunami).
  • Limits: 22,332 dead and missing in 2011; 551 in Noto, 2024. Defences can be exceeded.
  • Occupation continues because losses are reduced and recovery is organised, not because risk is removed.
  • Exam move (AQA "Assess"): weigh each response type with evidence, then judge how far, together, they sustain occupation.
Mission check

?A coastal city lies above a subduction zone and in a typhoon track. Which strategy would best sustain its occupation?

Japan shows that a multi-hazardous environment can stay densely occupied when mitigation, adaptation, management and resilience work together, though large events can still exceed them.

Mission 6

The setting: a ridge on land

  • Grindavík is a coastal town on Iceland's Reykjanes Peninsula, "where the Mid-Atlantic Ridge rises above sea level".
  • A rift zone on thin oceanic crust: the plates pull apart and magma rises.
  • Magma gathered beneath the Svartsengi volcanic system; on 10 November 2023 a large dike (a blade of magma) cut under the Sundhnúkur crater row and the town.
Mission 6

The hazards

  • Ground rupture and subsidence: a rift valley formed; "half the town has dropped more than a meter".
  • Eruptions: from 18 December 2023, then in January and February 2024; five eruptive episodes by June 2024.
  • Short warning: on 8 February 2024, only 35 minutes from first warning signs to eruption.
  • Sea flooding: "increased risk of sea flooding due to lowered elevation of Grindavík".
Quick check
?Which hazard to Grindavík comes from the town sinking, not from lava?
Mission 6

Economic character: living off the heat

  • The Svartsengi geothermal plant, near the town: heat use began in 1976, electricity two years later.
  • Licensed for up to 85 MW of electricity; hot water pumped to consumers across Reykjanes.
  • Its waste brine formed a pond that became the foundation of the Blue Lagoon tourist spa.
  • The same rift that makes the heat now threatens it: seismicity endangers "the Svartsengi Power Plant and Blue Lagoon".
Mission 6

Response 1: holding back the lava

  • From November 2023, barriers of earth and rock were built round the town and plant.
  • 14 January 2024: the barriers diverted the main lava flow west, but a second fissure opened outside the barrier, at the edge of town, and its lava burned three homes.
  • At an earlier eruption site (Fagradalsfjall), similar dams delayed lava by up to 16 days.
  • Mitigation: barriers can protect assets, but not stop a fissure opening on the town side of them.
Mission 6

Social character: a community dispersed

  • 10 November 2023: the town, about 3,700 people, was evacuated the same day.
  • An evacuated town cannot work as a community in place: residents live, work and study elsewhere.
  • Whether and when anyone returns depends on safety decisions taken by the state.
Mission 6

Political character: the state steps in

  • The National Police Commissioner ordered the evacuation.
  • Parliament passed Act 16/2024 on the purchase of residential housing in Grindavík.
  • A property company, Þórkatla, buys, manages and disposes of the homes: 961 properties bought; 969 applications since March 2024.
  • Former owners are to be offered the chance to buy back their homes.
  • Iceland's Natural Catastrophe Insurance (NTÍ) covers buildings damaged by eruptions and earthquakes.

Mission 6

Resilience or retreat?

  • Resilience: barriers, monitoring and national insurance keep the plant and parts of town usable.
  • Managed retreat: the state buy-out lets owners leave without losing their equity.
  • Adaptation: buy-back offers keep a path open to return if the area becomes safe.
  • Exam move (AQA "Analyse"): link each response to the economic, social or political character it reflects.
Mission check

?On the Icelandic island of Heimaey, workers "sprayed millions of tons of seawater on a lava flow's edge to cool and slow it, preventing it from destroying a harbor". What does this suggest about how a hazard response reflects a community's character?

In a hazardous setting, a community's economy may depend on the hazard (geothermal heat, harbours), its society can be scattered by one event, and its politics decide who stays: at Grindavík, barriers defended the plant, and a national law bought out the town.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Tropical cyclones form over warm oceans away from the equator; the eye, eyewall and rain bands organise their winds and rain.
  • Storm surge, from tropical cyclones or depressions (such as the North Sea storm of 1953), is often the deadliest hazard; Ian's surge flooded both Florida and Cuba, but losses are recorded very differently.
  • Rain brings river flooding and landslides (Maria, 2017); forecasts give days of warning but rapid intensification remains hard to predict.

Mission 2

  • Ian (2022, Florida): 66 direct deaths, mostly from surge; $112.9 billion damage; warnings 30–48 h ahead, but a late surge upgrade; recovery funded by federal aid and insurance.
  • Idai (2019, Mozambique): 602 deaths, 239,682 houses and 715,378 ha of farmland damaged, cholera; appeal about 20% funded; long-term focus on resilient infrastructure and risk financing.

Mission 3

  • Nature: ground, surface and crown fires; conditions: fuel moisture, size, load and continuity; fuel-limited vs drought-limited vegetation; fire weather; the fire behaviour triangle (fuels, weather, topography).
  • Causes: lightning (natural) and people (84% of US fires, 1992–2012); ignition sets the start, conditions set the intensity.

Mission 4

  • Los Angeles, 7–31 January 2025: Palisades and Eaton fires, about 152 km², 16,264 structures destroyed, 31 deaths; secondary impacts on health, soil, the economy and insurance, falling unevenly by income.
  • Responses: evacuation, federal declaration and aid, two-phase clean-up; risk management through prevention, mitigation (defensible space), preparedness and adaptation.

Mission 5

  • Japan faces earthquakes, tsunamis, volcanoes, typhoons, heavy rain and snow; the Nankai Trough earthquake (80% likely within 30 years) is the largest risk.
  • Its responses (the 1961 Basic Act, Earthquake Early Warning, volcanic alert levels, retrofitting, hazard maps and land-use limits) reduce losses and sustain occupation, but cannot remove risk.

Mission 6

  • Grindavík sits on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge: dike intrusion, ground rupture, subsidence, lava, sea-flood risk.
  • Its geothermal economy comes from the same rift; the 2023–24 events led to evacuation, lava barriers, and a state buy-out under Act 16/2024 (961 homes).
