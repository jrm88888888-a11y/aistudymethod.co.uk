Mission 1

Mission 1: Why do some industries have thousands of small firms while others have only two or three?

Make a guess: Why do some industries have thousands of small firms while others have only two or three?

Mission 1

Short run and long run

  • Short run: at least one factor of production is fixed, usually capital (the size of the plant).
  • Long run: all factors can be changed. The firm can build a bigger plant or a smaller one.
  • So in the long run there are no fixed costs: all costs are variable.
  • The length of each period differs by industry: a café can add a coffee machine in a week; a steelworks takes years to build.
  • Diminishing returns are a short-run idea: they need a fixed factor.
  • Say: "the short run is the period when at least one factor is fixed" — not: "the short run is less than a year".
Mission 1

Returns to scale

  • Returns to scale: what happens to output when the firm changes all its inputs by the same proportion.
  • Double every input, then compare output:
  • Increasing returns to scale: output more than doubles.
  • Constant returns to scale: output exactly doubles.
  • Decreasing returns to scale: output less than doubles.
  • Returns to scale are long run: nothing is fixed.
  • Say: "decreasing returns to scale" for the long run — not: "diminishing returns", which is short run with one factor fixed.
Quick check
?A furniture maker increases its workers, machines and floor space by 50%. Its weekly output rises from 400 chairs to 560 chairs. What does this show?
Mission 1

Worked example: from returns to average cost

  • Illustrative data. A firm uses 10 workers and 2 machines. Inputs cost 1,000 a week. Output: 100 units.
  • Average cost = 1,000 ÷ 100 = 10 per unit.
  • It doubles all inputs: 20 workers, 4 machines. Input prices stay the same, so inputs cost 2,000.
Output after doublingReturns to scaleAverage cost
250Increasing2,000 ÷ 250 = 8 (falls)
200Constant2,000 ÷ 200 = 10 (same)

  • With input prices constant: increasing returns → LRAC falls; decreasing returns → LRAC rises.
Mission 1

Many short runs make one long run

  • Each plant size has its own U-shaped short-run average cost (SRAC) curve.
  • In the long run the firm picks the plant with the lowest average cost for the output it plans.
  • The long-run average total cost (LRATC) curve traces the lowest average cost at each output. It is the envelope of the short-run ATC curves.
  • Each SRAC touches the long-run curve at one output and lies above it everywhere else.
  • So short-run average cost can never be below long-run average cost at the same output.
  • Say: "the LRAC is the envelope of the SRAC curves" — not: "the LRAC joins the minimum points of the SRAC curves".

Mission 1

The shape of the long-run curve

  • LRATC falls: economies of scale. LRATC flat: constant returns to scale. LRATC rises: diseconomies of scale.
  • Textbook shape: U-shaped. The reasons for each part are in Mission 2.
  • Say: "economies of scale lower average cost" — not: "economies of scale lower total cost". Total cost still rises with output.
Mission 1

Minimum efficient scale

  • Minimum efficient scale (MES): the lowest output at which long-run average cost reaches its minimum.
  • On a U-shaped curve: the bottom of the U (the start of the flat part, if there is one).
  • A firm producing below MES has higher average cost than a rival at or above MES.
  • The steeper the curve below MES, the bigger that cost penalty, and the harder it is for small firms to survive.
  • Say: "MES is the smallest output at the lowest average cost" — not: "MES is the biggest a firm can grow".
Mission 1

How many firms can a market hold?

  • Compare MES with market demand at a price equal to the minimum average cost.
  • Room for firms at MES ≈ market quantity ÷ MES.
  • Illustrative data. Market demand: 1,000,000 units a year.
  • MES of 10,000 → room for up to 100 firms: many small firms can compete (low concentration).
  • MES of 400,000 → room for only 2 firms at lowest cost: a few big firms (high concentration, oligopoly).
  • MES at or above total market demand → room for one firm at lowest cost: a natural monopoly.
  • A natural monopoly has economies of scale over the entire range of market demand: LRATC is still falling where it meets demand.
Mission 1

Real world: large passenger jets

  • Designing a new airliner costs billions before the first plane is sold. Spreading that cost needs huge output.
  • Two firms make most of the world's large passenger jets.
  • Real data (company figures): in 2023 Airbus delivered 735 commercial aircraft and Boeing 528.
  • Compare hairdressers: low MES, small local markets, thousands of firms.
  • High MES relative to market demand is one reason a market ends up with few firms.
Quick check
?At a price equal to the lowest possible average cost, buyers want 600,000 units a year. The minimum efficient scale is 150,000 units a year (illustrative data). How many firms can the market hold, each producing at the lowest average cost?
Mission check

?A country buys 1.2 million washing machines a year. The minimum efficient scale is 0.8 million machines a year (illustrative data). The country then joins a free-trade area, and its producers can now sell in a market of 6 million machines a year. Which statement is correct?

The number of firms a market can hold at lowest cost depends on MES compared with the size of the market, so a bigger market can hold more of them.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why can a giant firm make things more cheaply than a small one, and when does size start to hurt?

Make a guess: Why can a giant firm make things more cheaply than a small one, and when does size start to hurt?

Mission 2

Cost per unit as a firm grows

  • On the LRATC curve: falling = economies of scale; flat = constant returns to scale; rising = diseconomies of scale.
  • Why it falls: big set-up costs are spread over more units, and large firms can use bigger, more specialized machines, buy inputs in bulk at a discount and borrow more cheaply.
  • They are long run: the firm grows by changing all its inputs, including plant size.
  • Say: "economies of scale lower average cost" — not: "lower costs". Total cost still rises with output.
Mission 2

Diseconomies of scale

  • As a firm grows very large, average cost can rise. Main causes:
  • Communication: more layers of managers; messages are slow and distorted.
  • Coordination and control: hard to keep many sites and departments working together; harder to check that staff do their jobs well.
  • Motivation: workers feel like a small part of a huge machine; absence and staff turnover rise.
Quick check
?A firm has grown to 40,000 employees in 30 countries. Decisions take months to pass through layers of managers, and staff surveys show falling motivation. Its cost per unit is now rising as output grows. What is the best explanation?
Mission 2

Natural monopoly

  • Some industries have economies of scale over the whole range of market demand: average cost is still falling where it meets demand.
  • Then one firm can supply the whole market at a lower average cost than two or more firms could.
  • Examples: water pipes, electricity grids, rail track. Building two networks to every home would duplicate a huge fixed cost.
  • Illustrative data. A grid costs 90 million a year to run, plus 30 per household supplied. The market is 3 million households.
  • One firm: 90 million ÷ 3 million + 30 = 60 per household.
  • Three firms, 1 million households each: 90 million ÷ 1 million + 30 = 120 per household.
  • On the graph: LRATC slopes down throughout the range where it crosses market demand.
Quick check
?A water network costs 60 million a year to maintain, plus 40 per household supplied. There are 2 million households (illustrative data). What is the average cost per household with one network, and with two rival networks that each supply half the households?
Mission check

?A food firm grows over several years (illustrative data). At 10,000 meals a week its total cost is 200,000. At 50,000 meals it is 600,000. At 120,000 meals it is 1,800,000. What do these figures show?

Growing can lower cost per unit, but only up to a point; judge it by average cost, not total cost.

Mission 3

The spectrum of competition

Perfect competitionMonopolistic competitionOligopolyMonopoly
Number of firmsVery manyManyA few dominateOne
ProductIdentical (homogeneous)DifferentiatedIdentical or differentiatedNo close substitutes
Barriers to entryNoneLowHighVery high
Control over priceNoneSomeConsiderableHigh
  • Example of monopolistic competition: hairdressers, cafés, pizza restaurants in a town.
  • Oligopoly: firms are interdependent: each must think about how rivals will react.
  • Monopsony: a single buyer in a factor market (e.g. the only large employer in a town). Monopoly, oligopoly and monopolistic competition are imperfect product markets.
Mission 3

Price takers and price makers

  • Market power: a firm's ability to influence the price of its product.
  • A perfectly competitive firm is a price taker: it can sell all it wants at the market price, and nothing above it.
  • Its demand curve is horizontal: P = MR = AR = D.
  • Every other firm is a price maker: it faces a downward-sloping demand curve, so it must lower its price to sell more. MR is below price.
  • Producing where MR = MC, a price maker sets P > MC: consumers value one more unit above its cost, so too little is produced. That is allocative inefficiency.
  • Say: "P > MC (or P > MB = MC) shows inefficiency" — not: "the firm's price equals MR".
Quick check
?A firm faces a downward-sloping demand curve and produces the output where MR = MC. What is true at that output?
Mission 3

Barriers to entry

  • A barrier to entry is anything that makes it hard for new firms to enter a market.
TypeExamples
LegalPatents, licenses, the sole right to run a service (e.g. a local water or power network)
CostEconomies of scale, high start-up costs, sunk costs (costs that cannot be recovered on leaving)
MarketBrand loyalty, heavy advertising, product differentiation
PhysicalControl of a key resource, the best locations, owning the network
  • Key examples: high start-up costs, legal barriers (patents, licenses, exclusive franchises) and ownership of a key resource. In most US towns one utility owns the local power lines, under a franchise from government.
  • Why they matter: without barriers, economic profit attracts entrants, whose extra supply competes it away. With high barriers, firms can keep economic profit in the long run.
  • AP calls economic profit positive economic profit.
Quick check
?Building a new steel works costs billions. The two existing producers each make so much steel that their average cost is far below what any new, smaller rival could achieve. Which barrier to entry does this describe?
Mission 3

Minimum efficient scale and the number of firms

  • Minimum efficient scale (MES): the lowest output at which long-run average cost reaches its minimum.
  • AP labels the long-run average cost curve LRATC (long-run average total cost).
  • Compare MES with the size of the market:
  • MES small relative to market demand → room for many firms → competitive structure.
  • MES large → room for only a few firms → oligopoly.
  • MES close to the whole market → one firm can supply it most cheaply → natural monopoly.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why might a business that brings in money every year still be making no real profit?

Make a guess: Why might a business that brings in money every year still be making no real profit?

Mission 4

What profit is

  • Profit = total revenue − total costs.
  • Profit = TR − TC. Economists count all costs, including the ones nobody sends a bill for.
  • Total costs means the opportunity cost of every resource the firm uses.
  • Some costs are paid out in money. Some are not. Both count.
Mission 4

Explicit and implicit costs

  • Explicit costs: money actually paid out. Wages, rent, materials, energy, interest on loans.
  • Implicit costs: the value of the owner's own resources used in the business. No payment is made.
  • Examples: the salary the owner gives up by not working elsewhere; the interest her own savings could have earned; the rent her own building could have earned.
  • Accounting profit = TR − explicit costs.
  • Economic profit = TR − explicit costs − implicit costs.
  • Say: "accounting profit ignores implicit costs" — not: "accounting profit is wrong". It answers a different question.
Mission 4

Worked example: the café

  • Illustrative data. A café, one year. Figures per year.
  • Total revenue: 120,000.
  • Explicit costs (food, staff, rent, energy): 85,000.
  • Accounting profit = 120,000 − 85,000 = 35,000.
  • Implicit costs: the owner gives up a salary of 30,000 as a manager elsewhere. She put 100,000 of her savings in; at 5% interest they would earn 100,000 × 0.05 = 5,000.
  • Implicit costs = 30,000 + 5,000 = 35,000.
  • Economic profit = 35,000 − 35,000 = 0.
  • The accounts show a profit. Yet she would do just as well elsewhere. That is normal profit.
Mission 4

Economic profit, zero economic profit and losses

  • Positive economic profit: TR > total cost (explicit + implicit). P > ATC.
  • Zero economic profit = normal profit: TR = total cost. P = ATC. Accounting profit is still positive: it just covers the implicit costs.
  • Economic loss: TR < total cost. P < ATC.
  • A firm with zero economic profit is doing as well as its owners could do in their next-best use.
  • Say: "zero economic profit" — not: "the firm earns nothing".
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A designer runs her own studio. Last year her revenue was $200,000 and her explicit costs were $150,000. She gave up a salary of $45,000 to run it, and she put $80,000 of her savings into the business, which could have earned 5% interest. Which statement is correct?
Mission 4

Profit on a cost diagram

  • Profit per unit = P − ATC. Here P is average revenue (AR) and ATC is average cost (ATC).
  • Total economic profit = (P − ATC) × Q: a rectangle.
  • Illustrative data, Q = 100, ATC = 9:
  • P = 12: economic profit = (12 − 9) × 100 = 300.
  • P = 9: economic profit = 0. Normal profit.
  • P = 8: economic profit = (8 − 9) × 100 = −100. An economic loss.
  • (Which output a firm picks comes in Mission 5.)
Mission 4Graph lab

Slide the price

  • Takeaway: P above ATC: positive economic profit. P = ATC: zero economic profit (normal profit). P below ATC: an economic loss.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A diagram shows a firm producing 50 units. At this output its average revenue is 20 and its average cost is 16. What is the area of the profit rectangle?
Mission 4

Why profit matters in a market economy

  • Signal: positive economic profit shows buyers want more. Resources move in. Economic losses show they want less. Resources move out.
  • Firms respond to economic profit or loss, not accounting profit. Positive economic profit attracts entry; economic losses lead to exit.
  • Incentive: profit rewards risk-taking, effort and new ideas.
  • Source of finance: retained profit pays for investment and research.
  • Normal profit is the minimum that keeps resources in their current use.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. A software engineer leaves a job paying 60,000 a year to build her own app business. In its first year the app earns revenue of 90,000. She pays 50,000 for servers, design and marketing. She has no other costs. Which statement is correct?

Revenue has to cover the owner's best alternative too. Until it does, the business is not earning a real profit.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Why would a business stop making more when each extra sale still brings in money?

Make a guess: Why would a business stop making more when each extra sale still brings in money?

Mission 5

Thinking at the margin

  • Profit = TR − TC.
  • Marginal revenue (MR): the extra revenue from selling one more unit.
  • Marginal cost (MC): the extra cost of making one more unit.
  • MR > MC: one more unit adds to profit. Make more.
  • MR < MC: one more unit cuts profit. Make less.
  • Profit is maximized where MC = MR.
  • The MR = MC rule: produce where MR = MC.
  • Say: "profit is maximized where MC = MR" — not: "where revenue is highest" or "where cost per unit is lowest".

Quick check
?Illustrative data. A bakery sells batches of bread at a fixed price of 5 per batch, however many it sells. Its fixed costs are 4. The marginal cost of the 1st to 5th batches is 2, 3, 4, 6 and 8. How many batches maximize its profit?
Mission 5

MC = MR on the diagram

  • Find Q where MC cuts MR from below. That is the profit-maximizing output.
  • Go up from that Q to the AR (demand) curve to read the price.
  • Go up to ATC at the same Q to read cost per unit.
  • Profit = (P − ATC) × Q: the rectangle between price and ATC.
  • If P < ATC at that output, MR = MC gives the smallest loss the firm can make while producing.
  • Say: "read the price off the demand (AR) curve" — not: "off the point where MC = MR".

  • Takeaway: profit climbs while MR > MC and falls once MC > MR, even though revenue is still rising. The peak is the MC = MR output.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. On a diagram, MC cuts MR from below at an output of 40, where MC = MR = 12. At an output of 40, average revenue is 25 and average cost is 18. What price does the profit-maximizing firm charge, and what is its profit?
Mission 5

Making a loss: carry on or shut down?

  • Short run: fixed costs must be paid even at zero output (rent, loan repayments).
  • Shut down: loss = fixed costs.
  • Carry on: loss = TC − TR.
  • So carry on if TR covers variable costs. Every unit sold then pays something toward fixed costs.
  • Rule: produce if TR ≥ TVC, i.e. P ≥ AVC. Shut down if P < AVC.
  • Shutdown point: the minimum of AVC.
  • Say: "shut down if price is below average variable cost" — not: "shut down as soon as the firm makes a loss".
Mission 5

Worked example: shut down or not?

  • Illustrative data, one week. Fixed costs 600. Best output 100 units. AVC = 8.
  • Price 10: TR = 1,000. TVC = 800. TC = 1,400.
  • Carry on: loss = 1,400 − 1,000 = 400. Shut down: loss = 600.
  • Carry on. Contribution = (10 − 8) × 100 = 200 toward fixed costs.
  • Price falls to 7: TR = 700, less than TVC of 800.
  • Carry on: loss = 1,400 − 700 = 700. Shut down: loss = 600.
  • Shut down: P (7) < AVC (8).
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A firm's fixed costs are 2,000 a month. At its best output of 500 units, its AVC is 6 and its ATC is 10. The price is 7. What should it do in the short run?
Mission 5

Break-even and the long run

  • Break-even point: P = minimum ATC. TR = TC: zero economic profit.
  • Price between min AVC and min ATC: a loss, but keep producing in the short run.
  • Long run: every cost is variable. A firm exits if it expects P < ATC to last.
  • With no barriers, firms enter a market with positive economic profit and exit when they expect long-run losses.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A firm's minimum AVC is 4 and its minimum average total cost (ATC) is 6. The market price falls from 8 to 5 and is expected to stay there. What should the firm do?
Mission check

?Illustrative data. A water park makes a large profit each year. It is thinking of opening for one extra hour every evening. The extra hour would bring in 900 of ticket and food sales. It would add 1,100 to costs (staff, lighting, heating the pools). What should a profit-maximizing water park do?

Profit rises only while each extra unit adds more to revenue than to cost. Past that point, more sales mean less profit.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • In the long run all factors and costs are variable; the LRAC is the envelope of the SRAC curves, and SRAC is never below it.
  • Increasing returns to scale (with constant input prices) make long-run average cost fall; decreasing returns make it rise.
  • Minimum efficient scale compared with market demand limits how many firms can produce at the lowest average cost.

Mission 2

  • Economies of scale: long-run average cost falls as output grows; diseconomies: it rises.

Mission 3

  • Market structures range from perfect competition to monopoly, by number of firms, product differentiation, barriers to entry and control over price.
  • Barriers to entry (legal, cost, market, physical) let firms keep economic profit in the long run.
  • The larger the minimum efficient scale relative to the market, the fewer firms it can hold.
  • Price makers must lower price to sell more; P > MC signals allocative inefficiency.

Mission 4

  • Accounting profit = TR − explicit costs; economic profit also subtracts implicit costs (opportunity costs of the owner's own resources).
  • Zero economic profit is normal profit. Firms enter and exit in response to economic profit, not accounting profit.

Mission 5

  • Profit is maximized where MC = MR (MC cutting MR from below); the price is read off the AR (demand) curve.
  • In the short run, keep producing if P ≥ AVC (TR ≥ TVC); shut down if P < AVC.
  • Break-even is at minimum ATC; in the long run firms exit if they expect P < ATC to last.
