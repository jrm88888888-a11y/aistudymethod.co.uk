Mission 1: Between 1951 and 2004 the cliffs at Aldbrough, on England's Holderness coast, retreated about 2 m a year. Why do they disappear so fast?

Make a guess: Between 1951 and 2004 the cliffs at Aldbrough, on England's Holderness coast, retreated about 2 m a year. Why do they disappear so fast?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • A2-1.C1.i coastal processes: wave action, refraction, erosion, transport; swash- and drift-aligned coasts
Mission 1

How waves form and break

  • Wind blowing over water transfers energy to it. Wave energy grows with wind speed, duration and fetch (the distance of open water).
  • In deep water, water particles move in near-circular orbits; the energy moves forward, not the water.
  • In shallow water, friction with the bed slows the base of the wave: wavelength shortens, height rises, and the crest topples forward: the wave breaks.
  • Water rushes up the beach as swash and drains back as backwash.
Mission 1

Wave type sets the work

  • Destructive waves: high, steep, frequent; strong backwash; they erode and remove sediment.
  • Constructive waves: low, long; strong swash; they build beaches.
  • Steep storm waves do most of the erosion on cliffed coasts.
Mission 1

Erosion and transport

  • Erosion: hydraulic action (water and trapped air force cracks open), abrasion (the load is thrown at the rock), attrition (pebbles collide and round), solution (soluble rock, such as chalk, dissolves).
  • Transport: traction (rolling), saltation (bouncing), suspension (fine particles carried in the water), solution (dissolved load).
  • Waves arriving at an angle also move sediment along the shore: longshore drift.
Mission 1

Wave refraction

  • Waves slow down in shallow water, so a wave crest approaching an uneven coast bends: this is refraction.
  • Off a headland, water is shallow sooner; the crest slows there and wraps round it, so energy converges on the headland.
  • In a bay, the crest is still in deep water and diverges: energy is spread out, so deposition is more likely.
  • Result: headlands are eroded, bays fill with beaches.
Mission 1

Swash-aligned and drift-aligned coasts

  • Swash-aligned: waves arrive roughly parallel to the shore; sediment moves up and down the beach with little net movement along it.
  • Drift-aligned: waves arrive at an angle; longshore drift moves sediment along the coast, often building spits.
  • Drift-aligned beaches "show a tendency to swash-alignment through time".
  • Porlock, Somerset, is a "retreating shingle barrier with both swash-aligned and drift-aligned longshore sections".

Mission 1

Tides and currents

  • Tides raise and lower the level at which waves attack. Britain has some of the largest tides in the world, with a range of up to 15 m.
  • At Aldbrough, it is at high tide that waves erode the lower and sometimes middle cliff.
  • Currents (tidal, longshore and ocean currents) carry sediment and drop it "wherever the current slows down".
Mission 1

Lithology: rock type controls recession

  • Lithology is what the rock is made of. Igneous and many metamorphic rocks resist erosion; some sedimentary rocks, such as clays and shales, are weak.
  • Holderness cliffs are unconsolidated glacial till (boulder clay); at Flamborough Head, the cliffs are chalk, "more resistant to erosion than the overlying till".
  • Aldbrough retreated 1.16 m a year in 1852–1951 and 2.16 m a year in 1951–2004.

Mission check

?On the Glamorgan coast, one mudstone-rich unit of the Lias cliffs "is easily eroded because of its high mudstone content". Why does it disappear faster than the limestone-rich beds around it?

Aldbrough is the extreme case. Its cliffs are unconsolidated till, cut by waves at high tide and slumping from above, so they retreated 2.16 m a year in 1951–2004, while chalk at Flamborough resists.

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • A2-1.C1.ii formation of landforms at high-energy coasts (headlands, cliffs, arches, stacks, stumps) and low-energy coasts (beaches and dunes, spits, tombolos, bars)
Mission 2

Notches, platforms and cliffs

  • Waves erode a wave-cut notch at the cliff base by hydraulic action and abrasion.
  • The overhang collapses and the cliff retreats, leaving a gently sloping wave-cut platform, exposed at low tide.
  • As the platform widens, waves break further out and lose energy before reaching the cliff, so retreat slows: negative feedback.
Mission 2

Headlands: cave, arch, stack, stump

  • Headlands and bays form where bands of resistant and weaker rock meet the coast: weaker rock erodes back into bays; resistant rock is left standing out as headlands.
  • Waves attack lines of weakness (joints, faults) in a headland: crack → cave → arch → stack → stump.
  • BGS warns that more wave activity could attack Flamborough's chalk at wave level, "creating undercutting and cavities".
  • Old Harry Rocks, Dorset: "towering sea stacks and cliffs topped by chalk downland".
Mission 2

Outside the UK: the Twelve Apostles

  • Victoria, Australia: limestone, formed from shells and skeletons on an ancient sea floor.
  • Caves "eventually became arches, which have collapsed and left impressive towering rock stacks".
  • There were nine stacks until 2005, when one collapsed; another fell in 2009. Seven remain.
Quick check
?Which sequence is correct for the erosion of a headland?
Mission 2

Moving sediment along the coast

  • Angle of wave attack: waves arriving at an angle carry sediment up the beach at that angle; backwash returns it straight down.
  • Longshore drift: the repeated zig-zag moves sediment along the coast in the direction of the prevailing waves.
  • Tides and currents move finer sediment and deposit it "wherever the current slows down".
Mission 2

Beaches

  • Beaches form where deposition exceeds removal: sand on gentle slopes, shingle on steeper ones.
  • In bays, wave refraction spreads energy, so beaches build up there.
Mission 2

Spits

  • A spit forms where longshore drift carries sediment past a change in the direction of the coast; it grows out into open water.
  • Waves refracting round its tip curl it into a recurved end; saltmarsh grows in the sheltered water behind.
  • Spurn formed "as a long, narrow spit from deposits moving along the coast carried by currents". It is over three miles long and in places only 50 m wide.
  • Outside the UK: Farewell Spit, New Zealand, stretches 30 km eastward into the Tasman Sea.
Mission 2

Bars, barrier beaches and tombolos

  • A bar is a ridge of sediment across a bay, enclosing a lagoon. It forms as a spit grows right across, or as a shingle ridge migrates onshore while sea level rises.
  • Slapton bar, Devon, a shingle bay-bar, "is attached to headlands at both ends".
  • A tombolo joins an island to the mainland: Chesil Beach "stands separately as a tombolo joining the Isle of Portland to the mainland".
Quick check
?A ridge of shingle links a rocky island to the mainland. What is it?
Mission 2

Sand dunes and plant succession

  • Onshore winds blow dry sand inland from wide beaches; it collects behind obstacles such as the strandline.
  • Embryonic dunes: pioneer plants such as sand couch trap sand at the top of the beach.
  • White (yellow) dunes: sand-binding marram dominates; this vegetation "will rapidly change and disappear if stability is imposed".
  • Grey (fixed) dunes: grassland replaces marram as the sand gains organic matter; dune slacks are seasonally flooded hollows.
Quick check
?What role does marram grass play in dune development?
Mission check

?At a river mouth the coastline turns sharply inland. Waves usually arrive at an angle from one direction. Over centuries a long, narrow ridge of sand grows out across the mouth. How?

Spurn is a spit. Sediment from the eroding Holderness cliffs drifts south along the coast and is deposited where the coastline turns at the Humber mouth. Storms breach it, as in December 2013, so it keeps changing shape.

Mission 3

Make a guess: On the coast of the Gulf of Bothnia, the land rises out of the sea by about 0.9 m a century. Elsewhere, coasts are drowning. How can both be true?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

Mission 3

Eustatic change: the sea moves

  • Eustatic change: a worldwide change in sea level as the volume of ocean water (or the shape of ocean basins) changes.
  • Cold periods: water is locked in ice sheets on land, so sea level falls (glacio-eustatic fall). Warm periods: ice melts and water expands, so it rises.
  • Global mean sea level rose 0.20 m between 1901 and 2018; the rate rose from 1.3 mm a year (1901–71) to 3.7 mm a year (2006–18).
  • Thermal expansion explained 50% of the rise in 1971–2018.
Mission 3

Isostatic change: the land moves

  • Isostatic change: vertical movement of the land. An ice sheet's weight presses the crust down; when it melts, the land slowly rises (isostatic recovery, or rebound).
  • On the High Coast (Sweden) and Kvarken (Finland), about 290 m of uplift has been recorded over the past 10,500 years.
  • The land still rises by about 0.9 m a century, and new islands keep emerging.
  • Land can also sink: beyond the old ice margin, and where thick sediments compact.

Quick check

Mission 3

The last 10,000 years

  • After the last glaciation, melting ice sheets raised sea level fast, then the rise slowed: the Holocene transgression.
  • In Start Bay, Devon, relative sea level is thought to have risen by 18 m in the past 9,000 years, with "a notable deceleration of the rate of rise during the past 5000 years".
  • Near the Solent, sea level "approached its present position" 4,000–5,000 years ago.
  • Meanwhile isostatic recovery continued where the ice had been thickest: hence about 290 m of uplift on the High Coast.
  • Off Victoria, Australia, researchers found drowned stacks 50 m under water, beside a submerged cliff about 6 km off the Twelve Apostles: sea level rose so fast that "the stacks were simply swamped in place".
Mission 3

Coasts of emergence

  • Where the land rises faster than the sea (or the sea falls), old shorelines are left high and dry.
  • Raised beach: a former beach (rounded pebbles, shells) now above the reach of the sea, often backed by a relict cliff that is no longer undercut and weathers to a gentler slope.
  • Marine platform (marine terrace): a raised shore platform. Kaikōura's "uplifted platform" was created in one earthquake.
  • At Arbroath, Scotland, the Needle E'e arch, "originally formed through coastal erosion", now lies "several metres above current sea level".
Quick check
?Which evidence would best show that a coast has emerged?
Mission 3

Coasts of submergence: rias and fjords

  • Where the sea rises faster than the land, it floods low ground.
  • Rias are drowned river valleys: V-shaped in cross-section, deepening seaward, with branching creeks. JNCC: "Rias are drowned river valleys, characteristically found in south-west Britain". The Fal and Helford, Cornwall, form a "ria system".
  • Fjords are glacial troughs drowned by the sea: a fjord forms "by submergence of a glaciated valley".
  • West Norwegian fjords: walls rise up to 1,400 m and extend 500 m below sea level, with deep basins and a shallower rock threshold near the mouth.
Quick check
?Why is a fjord usually deepest inland and shallower at its mouth?
Mission 3

Ria or fjord?

  • Ria: river valley; V-shaped; gets deeper and wider towards the sea; unglaciated (southern Britain).
  • Fjord: glacial trough; U-shaped; steep walls; deepest inland; threshold at the mouth; glaciated uplands (Norway, western Scotland).
  • Exam move: name the cross-section shape and the long profile; they identify the origin.
Mission 3

Present and future change

  • IPCC: global sea level is likely to rise by 0.28–0.55 m by 2100 under very low emissions, and 0.63–1.01 m under very high emissions (relative to 1995–2014).
  • Emergent coasts can switch: where uplift is slower than future sea-level rise, raised features stay raised but the shoreline starts to retreat.
  • Low, soft coasts face more flooding and breaching: modelled UKCP18 scenarios suggest sea-level rise could completely cut off Spurn Head by 2050.
  • Barriers such as Slapton try to roll landward; where a road blocks them (the A379), the beach steepens instead.
Mission check

?At Arbroath, Scotland, a wave-cut arch stands several metres above today's sea. In Cornwall, river valleys are flooded by the sea far inland. How can both be true in Britain today?

Relative sea level = eustatic change in the sea ± isostatic (or tectonic) movement of the land. Where uplift wins, coasts emerge (raised arches and beaches; the Gulf of Bothnia); where the sea wins, they submerge (rias, fjords).

Make a guess: About a quarter of the Netherlands lies below sea level. Tuvalu's land averages about 2 m above it. Which is more vulnerable, and why?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • A2-1.C2.ii threat of climate-driven sea-level rise to human and physical environments (LEDC national study: Tuvalu)
Mission 4

Risk

  • IPCC: climate risk results "from the interaction of climate related hazards with vulnerabilities of societies and systems exposed". Often written Risk = Hazard × Exposure × Vulnerability.
  • Hazard: a physical event or trend that may cause loss (storm surge, sea-level rise).
  • Exposure: people, livelihoods, ecosystems and assets in places that could be harmed.
  • Same hazard, different exposure and vulnerability → different risk.
Mission 4

Vulnerability

  • Vulnerability: "the propensity or predisposition to be adversely affected", including lack of capacity to cope and adapt.
  • Higher with poverty, governance challenges, limited basic services and climate-sensitive livelihoods; worsened by inequity linked to gender, ethnicity and low income.
  • Adaptation can lower exposure (moving people) or vulnerability (building capacity); it cannot change the hazard.
Mission 4

The Netherlands: high exposure

  • 26% of the land is below sea level; with river flooding, 55% is at risk of flooding.
  • The IPCC's 2007 report wrongly said 55% was below sea level; PBL, which supplied the text, published the correction.
  • Exam move: check the source behind a statistic, even an authoritative one.
Mission 4

The Netherlands: high capacity

  • Flood standards in the Water Act since 2017: by 2050, a yearly flood-death risk of no more than 1 in 100,000 behind primary defences.
  • First national assessment (2021): about 67% of dike sections met the standard.
  • Delta Programme budget about €29 billion (2015–2050) against costs of about €38 billion.
Mission 4

Tuvalu: high exposure

  • A Pacific atoll nation; mean elevation 2 m above sea level.
  • Population 9,492 (2025); more than half live on the largest islet, in Funafuti atoll.
  • Sea level nearly 15 cm higher in 2023 than 30 years before; by 2050 much land and critical infrastructure projected below average high tide.
  • No higher ground to retreat to.
Mission 4

Tuvalu: threats to the physical environment

  • Floods: more frequent and more severe as sea level rises.
  • IPCC, low-lying coasts: without more adaptation, erosion and land loss, flooding and salinisation are projected to increase this century under all emissions scenarios.
  • Coral reefs: almost all warm-water reefs are projected to suffer significant losses of area, even at 1.5 °C.
  • Some island nations are likely to become uninhabitable (medium confidence).
Mission 4

Tuvalu: adapting and moving

  • Raising land: the Green Climate Fund-financed project created 8 hectares of raised land on Fogafale (2025): a protected core, not the whole nation.
  • Mobility: the Falepili Union with Australia (in force 28 August 2024) lets Tuvaluans choose to live, work and study in Australia; first ballot (2025) up to 280 places.
  • Australia recognises Tuvalu's statehood continues "notwithstanding the impact of climate-change related sea-level rise".
  • Trade-off: Tuvalu must agree with Australia any security arrangement with another state.

Mission check

?South-west Bangladesh has embanked islands that sank 1.0–1.5 m after embankments blocked sediment; after a 2009 cyclone breached them, land stayed flooded for up to two years. Dutch polders also lie below sea level. Why is Bangladesh's coast more vulnerable?

Exposure is high in both the Netherlands and Tuvalu (and Bangladesh). Vulnerability differs: the Netherlands has about seven times Tuvalu's income per person, a €29 billion programme and legal flood standards; Tuvalu has little land and no high ground, so it raises land and opens a mobility pathway.

Make a guess: By 2014 the Environment Agency had built new banks inland at Medmerry, West Sussex, and let the sea flood the land between. Why give land to the sea?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • A2-1.C3.i role of EIA, cost-benefit analysis, sediment cells and Shoreline Management Plans
  • A2-1.C3.ii evaluate the impact and sustainability of hard and soft engineering (regional case using both)
Mission 5

Why intervene?

  • Coasts are managed to reduce two risks: erosion (land lost) and flooding (land submerged, often in storm surges).
  • Holderness, East Yorkshire: over the last 1,000 years the coast has retreated about 2 km, with the loss of 26 villages listed in the Domesday survey.
  • Every intervention changes the flows of energy and sediment, so it also changes other places along the coast.
Mission 5

Hard engineering

  • Sea wall: concrete wall at the back of the beach; reflects wave energy. Reflected waves can scour the beach in front.
  • Revetment: sloping structure that absorbs some wave energy and protects the cliff or bank behind.
  • Rip rap (rock armour): large boulders at the cliff foot that break waves up.
  • Groynes: walls across the beach that trap littoral drift and widen the beach updrift, but starve beaches downdrift.
  • Offshore breakwaters: barriers offshore that make waves break before reaching the shore; sediment collects behind them.
Mission 5

Soft engineering

  • Beach nourishment: adding sand or shingle to a beach. On the Lincolnshire coast, the Lincshore scheme has nourished beaches since 1994, adding about 350,000 m³ a year from Mablethorpe to Skegness.
  • Cliff regrading and drainage: lower the slope angle and drain water out to reduce landslides. It matters most where landslides threaten towns: Ventnor, Isle of Wight, "sits at the bottom of the largest urbanised landslide complex in north-west Europe".
  • Dune stabilisation: fencing, boardwalks and planting marram to trap and hold sand.
  • Managed realignment: let the sea in behind a new, landward defence.
  • They work with natural processes, but most need repeating.
Quick check
?Which strategy works with natural processes rather than against them?
Mission 5

Sediment cells and Shoreline Management Plans

  • A sediment cell (littoral cell) is a stretch of coast within which sediment largely circulates; sub-cells divide it further.
  • Shoreline Management Plans (SMPs) plan how flood and erosion risk will be managed around England to 2105; there are 20 SMPs for the English coast.
  • SMP areas' boundaries "have been set based on analysis of coastal processes".
  • Each stretch gets one of four approaches: hold the line, advance the line, managed realignment, no active intervention.
  • Why cells matter: "defending one place from erosion can interrupt the movement of sediment down the coast, starving other places of material for beaches".
Mission 5

Case study: Mappleton, Holderness

  • Holderness's till cliffs retreat on average 1–4.5 m a year. Net drift is southward, about 200,000–350,000 m³ a year.
  • Strategy: in 1991, two rock groynes and a rock revetment were built "to prevent cliff recession" at Mappleton, with a design life of 50 years.
  • Intended effects: hold the cliff line in front of the village; trap drift to build a beach that absorbs wave energy.
  • The SMP keeps hold the line at Mappleton, though it finds only a "marginal economic case" for doing so.
Mission 5

Effects on the sediment budget and landforms

  • Mappleton is becoming a headland: undefended cliffs either side keep retreating, so the defended stretch sticks out.
  • Sediment budget: holding the line means "some interruption to the sediment supplied to downdrift coastlines", and beach narrowing is possible.
  • Landforms: the SMP expects "beach and platform lowering in front of the coastal defences" and a bay developing between Hornsea and Mappleton.
  • South of Mappleton, shore-platform lowering of up to 3.0 m in a year was measured in 1992–93, an extreme value.
  • Exam move: go beyond the choice of strategy; trace the effect on inputs, stores and outputs of sediment, then on landforms and the wider landscape.
Quick check
?Groynes are built on a cliffed coast where littoral drift runs south. What is the most likely effect on the coast to the south of them?
Mission 5

Cost-benefit analysis and EIA

  • Cost-benefit analysis (CBA) compares the costs of each option with the value of what it protects over the scheme's life, as present values (future sums discounted).
  • Medmerry (2007 estimates, present value): no active intervention £8–10 million; hold the line £80 million; managed realignment £10 million.
  • At Mappleton, CBA gave only a "marginal economic case" for holding the line.
  • Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) predicts a scheme's effects on habitats, landscape, water and people before it is approved. At Mappleton the SMP's assessment warned of "beach narrowing and steepening" and harm to seabed habitats.
  • CCEA: name both tools and say what each adds: CBA weighs money; EIA weighs effects that money does not capture.
Mission 5

Medmerry: managed realignment

  • A shingle bank west of Selsey protected 300 properties, large caravan sites and 650 ha. It had been breached many times in 15 years and needed extensive winter repairs.
  • Holding the line was judged "not practical"; inland banks "will not need to be as large", though "a relatively large area of land" would flood.
  • The government also had to create new intertidal habitat to compensate for losses elsewhere.
  • Completed in 2014 with 7 km of new embankments, protecting key infrastructure for Selsey. At the time, the £28 million scheme was "the largest open-coast scheme in Europe".
Mission 5

ICZM: managing a whole coast

  • Integrated coastal zone management (ICZM) plans a whole coast (ideally a whole sediment cell) across all its uses: settlement, industry, farming, tourism, conservation.
  • The Flamborough Head to Gibraltar Point SMP shows this. Holderness's eroding cliffs supply sand and gravel to Spurn Head and fine sand to Lincolnshire: "The continuation of erosion along the Holderness coast is crucial for the downdrift frontages".
  • So it holds the line at Bridlington, Hornsea, Mappleton, Withernsea and the Easington gas terminals (hard), lets the cliffs between keep eroding, and nourishes Lincolnshire's beaches (soft).
  • A regional case using both hard and soft engineering: evaluate each part by cost, effect downdrift and lifespan.
Quick check
?Why might hard defences at one village be judged unsustainable in the long term?
Mission check

?The plan for Holderness defends its towns but lets the cliffs between them keep eroding, even though farms and some homes are lost. Why let that land go?

Giving land to the sea can be the sustainable choice: at Medmerry, realignment was far cheaper than holding a failing bank and created habitat; at Holderness, letting cliffs erode keeps sediment flowing along the cell. Hard defences protect a point but change the coast around it.

Mission 6

The region: Durlston Head to Rame Head

  • One Shoreline Management Plan (SMP2) covers the Dorset coast west of Poole Bay, all of South Devon and the south-east tip of Cornwall.
  • Completed in 2011, it sets policy for each stretch for three periods to 2105, allowing for climate change.
  • The coast includes the Jurassic Coast World Heritage Site, unstable landslide cliffs, shingle barriers and resort towns.
  • So the region needs different answers in different places.
Mission 6

Policies change over time

Of the plan's 193 policy units (counts calculated from the policy table):

  • 2005–2025: hold the line 84 · no active intervention 98 · managed realignment 10
  • 2055–2105: hold the line 77 · no active intervention 100 · managed realignment 14
  • Most of the coast is left to evolve naturally; defence is kept where people and property are concentrated.
Mission 6

Hard engineering at Lyme Regis

  • Sea wall along the foot of East Cliff and Church Cliff, built in 1957.
  • Before it, those cliffs eroded about 0.45 m a year (perhaps up to 1.3 m a year at Church Cliff).
  • The Cobb, a breakwater, acts as a terminal groyne: it blocks almost all longshore drift.
  • Recent works added rock groynes and breakwaters to hold recharged beaches, and cliff stabilisation against landslides; work began in 1995 in phases.
  • Policy: hold the line to 2105.
Mission 6

Hard engineering elsewhere in the region

  • Revetment: Beesands has had a rock revetment backed by a wave-return wall since 1992. A revetment at Blackpool Sands (1992) reduced cliff slips caused by toe erosion.
  • Rip-rap and gabions: Bowleaze Cove's cliffs are partly protected by rock armour and gabions, built in about 1984.
  • Rock revetment with soil nailing and drainage: Seatown, 1996; it has since been outflanked at its western end and had to be extended about 15 m.
  • Groynes: a terminal rock groyne at Preston Beach, Weymouth.
Quick check
?Sea walls at Lyme Regis stopped the cliffs behind them eroding. What effect does the SMP say this has on the wider physical environment?
Mission 6

Impacts of hard engineering

  • Human: the town is protected from erosion and landslides; tourism depends on resorts such as Lyme Regis.
  • Physical: defences stop sediment from cliff erosion reaching the beaches, here and further afield.
  • Monmouth Beach, west of The Cobb, has lost volume and steepened over 100–150 years, apart from accretion right next to The Cobb.
  • Sustainability: the landslide complexes east of Lyme Regis could outflank the defences; long-term defence depends on how they evolve.
Mission 6

Soft engineering: beach nourishment

  • Preston Beach, Weymouth (1995/96): a new sea wall plus 207,000 m³ of sand and shingle recharge and a terminal rock groyne.
  • Since then, sediment has been recycled and the beach re-profiled to keep the standard of protection; more recharge is likely to be needed.
  • Ringstead Bay (1996): a rock breakwater with about 25,000 m³ of beach recharge.
  • Lyme Regis: beaches recharged with sand and shingle, held by rock groynes.
Quick check
?Weymouth's 207,000 m³ beach recharge needs recycling, re-profiling and probably more recharge. What does this suggest about the sustainability of beach nourishment?
Mission 6

Managed retreat at Slapton Sands

  • Slapton Sands is a shingle barrier about three miles long, with the A379 road along its ridge and Slapton Ley, a freshwater lake and National Nature Reserve, behind.
  • The barrier is frequently damaged in storms. It has been migrating landwards as sea level rises.
  • Policy: managed realignment in all three periods to 2105. Keeping the road in its current form will become unsustainable.
  • The road has already had to be realigned.
Mission 6

Impacts of managed retreat

  • Physical: the barrier can roll back naturally, keeping the beach and the habitats it protects.
  • Human: the biggest cost is the future loss of road access across Slapton Sands; transport in the wider area must be replanned.
  • At Torcross and Beesands, holding the line will get harder as the ridges roll back; some realignment may be needed in the long term.
  • Dune regeneration: this cliffed, shingle-dominated stretch relies mainly on shingle recharge, not dunes; dune work suits sandy coasts.

Mission check

?A village on a shingle barrier in the same region has a small car park and a few houses. The barrier is migrating landwards and storms damage the road behind it. Using the regional evidence, which strategy is most sustainable?

Across South Devon and Dorset, hard engineering is kept where towns justify it (Lyme Regis, Weymouth) but starves beaches and needs upkeep; soft engineering (recharge) works with the sea but must be repeated; managed retreat (Slapton) accepts loss of a road to keep a natural barrier working.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Waves form from wind (speed, duration, fetch) and break in shallow water; refraction concentrates energy on headlands, and coasts are swash- or drift-aligned by the angle of wave approach.

Mission 2

  • High-energy coasts: waves exploit weaknesses to form notches, platforms, cliffs and the cave–arch–stack–stump sequence (Old Harry Rocks; the Twelve Apostles).
  • Low-energy coasts: longshore drift and deposition build beaches, spits (Spurn), bars, tombolos (Chesil Beach); plant succession stabilises dunes.

Mission 3

  • Eustatic change moves the sea worldwide; isostatic and tectonic change move the land locally. What matters at a coast is the relative change.
  • Emergence leaves raised beaches, relict cliffs and marine platforms; submergence makes rias and fjords.
  • Future sea-level rise will push many emergent and submergent coasts towards retreat.

Mission 4

  • Risk = hazard × exposure × vulnerability; the Netherlands is more exposed, Tuvalu more vulnerable.

Mission 5

  • Hard engineering fixes a line but alters processes and costs more over time; soft engineering works with processes but must be repeated.
  • SMPs set policies by sediment cell; CBA and EIA inform the choice, and ICZM plans the whole coast.

Mission 6

  • Regional SMP2 (Durlston Head–Rame Head): mostly no active intervention; hold the line at towns; managed realignment growing over time.
  • Hard (sea walls, revetments, rip-rap, gabions, groynes): protect assets but cut sediment supply and risk outflanking. Soft (nourishment, managed retreat): work with processes but need maintenance or acceptance of loss.
