Mission 1

Mission 1: Your cells already get energy from sugar. Why do they spend it through a different molecule first?

Make a guess: Your cells already get energy from sugar. Why do they spend it through a different molecule first?

Mission 1

Meet ATP

  • ATP: adenosine triphosphate.
  • Adenine (a base) + ribose (a pentose) + three phosphate groups.
  • Adenine + ribose = adenosine.
  • ADP: adenosine diphosphate. Two phosphate groups.
  • Same base and sugar as an RNA nucleotide carrying adenine.
Quick check
?Which statement about ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is correct?
Mission 1

Hydrolysis releases energy

  • ATP + H2O → ADP + Pi (inorganic phosphate).
  • Energy is released: enough for many single tasks in the cell.
  • Breaking any bond takes energy. Energy is released overall because ADP and Pi are more stable than ATP.
  • Say: "energy is released when ATP is hydrolysed" — not: "energy is stored in a high-energy bond".
Mission 1

Coupling: putting the energy to work

  • ATP hydrolysis is coupled to an energy-requiring process: one enzyme links them, so they happen together.
  • What ATP powers:
  • active transport of ions and molecules across membranes
  • synthesis of macromolecules (anabolism), e.g. proteins, DNA
  • movement: muscle contraction, cilia, chromosomes in cell division
  • Say: "active transport uses ATP" — not: "diffusion and osmosis use ATP".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly describes a life process that ATP supplies with energy?
Mission 1

Remaking ATP: the ATP–ADP cycle

  • ADP + Pi → ATP + H2O: a condensation reaction.
  • Energy comes from respiration (every cell) and photosynthesis (in chloroplasts).
  • The ATP/ADP cycle couples respiration with energy use.
  • ATP is not stockpiled. A cell keeps a small pool and recycles it, second by second.
  • A resting adult turns over roughly their own body mass of ATP each day.
Quick check
?During a sprint, the small store of ATP in muscle fibres is hydrolysed and then re-formed several times over. Which statement about the energy transfers is correct?
Mission 1

Why not spend glucose directly?

  • Glucose releases its energy in many enzyme-controlled steps. ATP: one step, one enzyme.
  • One glucose holds far more energy than one reaction needs. ATP comes in small packets.
  • About 30 kJ per mole of ATP hydrolysed; about 2870 kJ per mole of glucose fully oxidised.
  • Released immediately, right where it is needed.
  • Soluble: moves freely within the cell.
  • Quickly remade from ADP + Pi.
Quick check
?A cell needs an energy currency that can supply many different reactions in the cytoplasm. Which property makes ATP suitable for this role?
Mission check

?A muscle fibre holds only enough ATP for a few seconds of hard contraction. Yet a marathon runner's leg muscles keep contracting for hours. Which statement explains this?

A small, fast-recycled pool of ATP, refilled by respiration, beats spending glucose directly.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Burning sugar releases all its energy in one flash. How do your cells release it slowly enough to use?

Make a guess: Burning sugar releases all its energy in one flash. How do your cells release it slowly enough to use?

Mission 2

Why every cell respires

  • Respiration releases energy from carbon compounds and transfers it to ATP.
  • It runs in every living cell, day and night: animals, plants, fungi, bacteria.
  • Autotrophs respire too. Photosynthesis makes their sugar; respiration releases its energy.
  • Main substrates: glucose and fatty acids. Many other carbon compounds can be used.
  • Not the same as gas exchange. Breathing moves gases; respiration is a chemical process inside cells.
  • Say: "respiration releases energy" — not: "respiration produces energy".
Quick check
?Which organisms release energy by the oxidation of carbon compounds in cell respiration?
Mission 2

Small steps, not one flash

  • Overall (aerobic): glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water.
  • Burning releases the energy all at once, as heat and light.
  • Respiration releases the same total energy, but in many small steps.
  • Each step is catalysed by its own specific enzyme, inside the cell.
  • At some steps, part of the energy is captured in ATP. The rest is released as heat.
  • Say: "energy is transferred to ATP" — not: "glucose is turned into energy".
Mission 2

Two routes from glucose

  • First stage, glycolysis: glucose → 2 pyruvate, in the cytoplasm. No oxygen needed.
  • With oxygen: pyruvate enters the mitochondria and is oxidised completely to carbon dioxide and water. Far more ATP.
  • The mitochondrial stages: link reaction and Krebs cycle (in the matrix), then oxidative phosphorylation (on the inner membrane).
  • Krebs cycle in the mitochondrial matrix; electron transport on the cristae.
  • Without oxygen: pyruvate stays in the cytoplasm. In human muscle it becomes lactate.
  • In yeast it becomes ethanol and carbon dioxide (see Mission 5).
Mission 2

Glycolysis in four steps

  • (1) Phosphorylation: 2 ATP add two phosphate groups to glucose. The sugar becomes more reactive.
  • The product is fructose bisphosphate.
  • (2) Lysis: the 6-carbon sugar splits into two 3-carbon triose phosphate molecules.
  • (3) Oxidation: hydrogen is removed from each triose phosphate. NAD accepts it and becomes reduced NAD.
  • (4) ATP formation: each 3-carbon molecule becomes pyruvate, forming 2 ATP.
  • Net per glucose: 4 ATP made − 2 used = 2 ATP, plus 2 reduced NAD and 2 pyruvate.
  • Each step has its own enzyme. All in the cytoplasm; no oxygen used.
  • Say: "phosphorylating glucose uses ATP" — not: "it makes ATP".
Mission 2

NAD carries hydrogen

  • Oxidation = loss of electrons. Reduction = gain of electrons.
  • Removing hydrogen (with its electron) is dehydrogenation. It oxidises the substrate, with no oxygen involved.
  • NAD accepts the hydrogen, so NAD is reduced. Both happen together: a redox reaction.
  • With oxygen, the hydrogen (its electrons) from reduced NAD is passed into the mitochondria, where it is used to make far more ATP (see Mission 4).
  • Say: "NAD is reduced when it gains hydrogen" — not: "NAD is oxidised when it gains hydrogen".

Mission 2Practical

Dehydrogenase activity with a redox indicator

  • CCEA practical: the role of hydrogen acceptors, using a redox indicator.
  • Dehydrogenases remove hydrogen in respiration. An artificial hydrogen acceptor shows their activity.
  • Methylene blue: blue when oxidised → colourless when reduced.
  • Method: yeast suspension + glucose in a water bath → add methylene blue → time until colourless (match to a colour standard).
  • Do not shake: oxygen re-oxidises the dye and the blue returns.
  • Control: boiled yeast. Dye stays blue: the change needs active enzymes.
  • Rate = 1 ÷ time.

Mission check

Mission 3

Mission 3: Pyruvate enters the mitochondrion with three carbon atoms. Where do they go, and what does the cell gain?

Make a guess: Pyruvate enters the mitochondrion with three carbon atoms. Where do they go, and what does the cell gain?

Mission 3

Inside the mitochondrion

  • Outer membrane: smooth; encloses the organelle.
  • Inner membrane: folded into cristae. Holds the electron carriers and ATP synthase (see Mission 4).
  • Intermembrane space: the narrow gap between the two membranes.
  • Matrix: the fluid inside the inner membrane.
  • The matrix holds the enzymes of the link reaction and Krebs cycle.
Mission 3

The link reaction

  • In the matrix, pyruvate (3C) is:
  • decarboxylated: loses CO2 → a 2C acetyl group.
  • oxidised: loses hydrogen to NAD → reduced NAD.
  • The acetyl group joins coenzyme A → acetyl coenzyme A (acetyl CoA).
  • Together, this is the oxidative decarboxylation of pyruvate.
  • Coenzyme A is a carrier, not an enzyme. It delivers acetyl groups to the Krebs cycle.
  • Twice per glucose (two pyruvate).
Quick check
?What happens to pyruvate in the link reaction of aerobic cell respiration?
Mission 3

The Krebs cycle

  • Acetyl (2C) joins oxaloacetate (4C) → citrate (6C). Coenzyme A is released, to collect more.
  • A C4 acid + acetyl co-enzyme A → a C6 acid.
  • Citrate is converted back to oxaloacetate in a series of steps, each with its own enzyme:
  • 2 decarboxylations: 2 CO2 released.
  • 4 dehydrogenations (oxidations): 3 reduced NAD and 1 reduced FAD.
  • 1 ATP, made directly by substrate-level phosphorylation.
  • Oxaloacetate is regenerated, ready for the next acetyl group.
  • Two turns per glucose.
  • Say: "CO2 comes from decarboxylation" — not: "oxygen combines with carbon".
Quick check
?One acetyl group enters the Krebs cycle and oxaloacetate is regenerated. Which account of one turn of the cycle is correct?

Mission 3

Why the cycle needs oxygen without using it

  • NAD and FAD accept hydrogen; coenzyme A carries acetyl groups. Both kinds are coenzymes, recycled again and again.
  • Reduced NAD and FAD are re-oxidised only when they hand on their hydrogen to the electron transport chain, which needs oxygen.
  • No oxygen → no NAD for the dehydrogenations → the link reaction and Krebs cycle stop.
  • The Krebs cycle is a hub: fatty acids feed in as acetyl CoA, amino acids as cycle intermediates; intermediates also leave to make amino acids and other molecules.
  • Say: "the Krebs cycle depends on oxygen" — not: "the Krebs cycle uses oxygen".

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why would a cell pump protons uphill across a membrane, only to let them flood straight back?

Make a guess: Why would a cell pump protons uphill across a membrane, only to let them flood straight back?

Mission 4

Handing on electrons

  • Reduced NAD and reduced FAD arrive from glycolysis, the link reaction and the Krebs cycle.
  • They deliver electrons (and protons) to a chain of carriers in the inner membrane, on the cristae.
  • Reduced NAD passes a pair of electrons to the first carrier. It becomes NAD again, free to collect more hydrogen.
  • Electrons pass from carrier to carrier in redox reactions: each carrier is reduced, then oxidised.
  • Each carrier is at a lower energy level than the last. Energy is released at each transfer.
Mission 4

Pumping protons

  • The energy released by electron flow pumps protons (H⁺) from the matrix into the intermembrane space.
  • The inner membrane is impermeable to H⁺, so they build up.
  • Result: a proton gradient across the inner membrane. It is electrochemical: a concentration difference and a charge difference.
  • More H⁺ = lower pH. The intermembrane space has a lower pH than the matrix.
  • Say: "protons are pumped across the membrane" — not: "electrons are pumped across".
Quick check
?How is the proton gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane generated?
Mission 4

ATP synthase: chemiosmosis

  • H⁺ return to the matrix mainly through ATP synthase, a channel protein in the inner membrane.
  • H⁺ flow down their gradient. The flow turns part of the enzyme.
  • That energy joins ADP + Pᵢ → ATP, on the matrix side.
  • Making ATP using energy from a proton gradient = chemiosmosis.
  • The whole process, electron flow + chemiosmosis = oxidative phosphorylation. Most of the cell's ATP is made this way.
  • The ATP "points" of the chain are where carriers pump protons; ATP synthase turns that gradient into ATP.
  • Say: "protons flow through ATP synthase" — not: "water moves by osmosis".
Quick check
?How does ATP synthase couple the proton gradient to the synthesis of ATP?
Mission 4

Oxygen: the last acceptor

  • Oxygen accepts electrons from the last carrier, and protons from the matrix.
  • Product: water (metabolic water).
  • Oxygen is the ultimate hydrogen acceptor.
  • This keeps the last carrier oxidised, so electrons keep flowing.
  • No oxygen → the last carrier stays reduced → every carrier backs up → flow and proton pumping stop.
  • Then reduced NAD cannot be re-oxidised, so the Krebs cycle and link reaction stop too.
  • Say: "oxygen becomes water" — not: "oxygen becomes carbon dioxide".
Quick check
?A student claims: 'Without oxygen the electron transport chain keeps running; only the formation of water stops.' Which statement correctly evaluates this claim?
Mission 4

The carriers and the ATP points

  • The chain, in order: NAD → flavoprotein (FP) → co-enzyme Q → cytochromes → oxygen.
  • Each link is at a progressively lower energy level.
  • ATP is made at three points: NAD → FP; between cytochromes (b → c); last cytochrome → oxygen.
  • A hydrogen pair from reduced NAD passes all three points: 3 ATP.
  • A hydrogen pair from reduced flavoprotein (FADH, from the Krebs cycle) enters after the first point: 2 ATP.
  • Per glucose: 10 reduced NAD × 3 + 2 FADH × 2 + 4 ATP made directly = 38 ATP (theoretical maximum).
  • Newer research: the real yield is lower (about 30–32): ATP per hydrogen pair is not a whole number, some H⁺ are used to move ADP, Pᵢ and ATP across the membrane, and some leak back.

Mission 5

Mission 5: When oxygen runs out, muscles make lactate and yeast makes alcohol. What do they gain from it?

Make a guess: When oxygen runs out, muscles make lactate and yeast makes alcohol. What do they gain from it?

Mission 5

Glycolysis on its own

  • Without oxygen, the mitochondrial stages stop. Only glycolysis still makes ATP: 2 ATP per glucose (net).
  • Anaerobic respiration happens in the cytoplasm only.
  • Human muscle: glucose → lactate. No carbon dioxide.
  • Yeast and plants: glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide.
  • Aerobic respiration yields many times more ATP per glucose.
  • Say: "anaerobic respiration uses no oxygen" — not: "it uses a little oxygen".
Mission 5

Recycling NAD

  • Glycolysis needs NAD to accept hydrogen at its oxidation step.
  • No oxygen → the electron transport chain stops → reduced NAD is not re-oxidised. NAD would soon run out, and glycolysis would stop.
  • Solution: pyruvate accepts the hydrogen from reduced NAD.
  • Animals: pyruvate → lactate. Pyruvate is reduced; NAD is regenerated.
  • Yeast and plants: pyruvate loses CO2, then the 2-carbon product is reduced → ethanol. NAD is regenerated.
  • The pathway is the same as in humans up to pyruvate. Only this last step differs.
  • These steps make no ATP. They keep glycolysis running, making its 2 ATP.
  • Say: "lactate formation regenerates NAD" — not: "lactate formation makes extra ATP".
Quick check
?During intense exercise, muscle cells convert pyruvate to lactate. What is the purpose of this conversion?
Mission 5

Why respire without oxygen?

  • Benefit: ATP can still be made when oxygen is short or absent.
  • Animals: a burst of extra ATP, fast, on top of aerobic respiration, for short intense activity. The cost: an oxygen debt (the earlier for CCEA).
  • Plants and fungi: survival for some time without oxygen, e.g. roots in waterlogged soil.
  • Lactate can be converted back later; ethanol and carbon dioxide are lost from the cell.
Mission 5

After the sprint

  • Lactate passes from muscle into the blood.
  • In the liver, lactate is oxidised back to pyruvate (NAD is reduced).
  • Pyruvate is then respired aerobically, or converted to glucose and stored as glycogen.
  • So extra oxygen is used after exercise; breathing and heart rate stay high.
  • Oxygen debt: the additional oxygen needed to metabolise accumulated lactate and/or resynthesise depleted ATP.

Mission check

?Crucian carp can survive for months in ice-covered ponds with no oxygen. In these conditions their muscle cells convert pyruvate to ethanol and carbon dioxide, and the ethanol leaves through the gills. Which statement about this anaerobic respiration is correct?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • ATP = adenine + ribose + three phosphates. Hydrolysis to ADP + Pi releases energy in a small, immediate packet; respiration supplies the energy to remake it.

Mission 2

  • Respiration releases the energy in carbon compounds in many small, enzyme-catalysed steps; part is captured in ATP, the rest is heat.
  • Glycolysis: phosphorylation (2 ATP used), lysis to two triose phosphate, oxidation (NAD reduced), ATP formation: net 2 ATP, 2 reduced NAD, 2 pyruvate.

Mission 3

  • Link reaction: pyruvate is decarboxylated and oxidised to an acetyl group, carried by coenzyme A. Krebs cycle: acetyl + oxaloacetate → citrate; 2 CO2, 3 reduced NAD, 1 reduced FAD, 1 ATP per turn; oxaloacetate regenerated.

Mission 4

  • Electrons from reduced NAD and FAD flow along carriers in the inner membrane; the energy released pumps H⁺ into the intermembrane space; H⁺ flows back through ATP synthase, making ATP (chemiosmosis); oxygen accepts the electrons and forms water.
  • Carriers NAD → FP → co-enzyme Q → cytochromes → oxygen; ATP at three points: 3 ATP per hydrogen pair from reduced NAD, 2 from reduced flavoprotein.

Mission 5

  • Without oxygen only glycolysis makes ATP (net 2 per glucose); lactate (animals) or ethanol and carbon dioxide (yeast, plants) are the products.
  • Making lactate or ethanol regenerates NAD from reduced NAD, so glycolysis can continue.
