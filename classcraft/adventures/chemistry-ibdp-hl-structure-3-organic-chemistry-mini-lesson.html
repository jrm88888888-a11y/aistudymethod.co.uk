Mission 1

Five kinds of formula

  • Empirical: simplest whole-number ratio of atoms. Hexane: C₃H₇.
  • Molecular: actual number of each atom. Hexane: C₆H₁₄.
  • Full structural: every atom and bond. Condensed: CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₃, or CH₃(CH₂)₄CH₃.
  • Skeletal: a zig-zag of bonds; carbons and their hydrogens are implied.
  • Stereochemical: wedges and dashes show the 3D shape. Draw one only when asked.
  • Build it as a 3D model (real or virtual): each carbon is tetrahedral, so chains zig-zag.
Mission 1

Reading a skeletal formula

  • Every line end and corner is a carbon, unless a line ends in a label (OH, Cl): that end is the labelled atom.
  • A group drawn at a corner, or on a short line off it, is attached to that carbon.
  • H on carbon is not drawn: give each carbon enough H for four bonds. Other atoms, and H on them, are always drawn.
  • Lines are bonds: count the points, not the lines.
  • Worked example: five zig-zag lines, the last ending in OH. That is 5 C and 1 O.
  • H: 3 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 1 (on O) = 12. So C₅H₁₂O, CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₂OH.

Mission 1

Telling look-alikes apart

  • Carboxyl: the C=O carbon also carries –OH. It counts as one group.
  • Ester: the single-bonded O is bonded to a second carbon, not to H.
  • Amido: N bonded to the C=O carbon. Amino: N on a carbon with no C=O.
  • Alkoxy: C–O–C with no C=O.
  • Saturated: only single C–C bonds. Unsaturated: at least one C=C or C≡C.
  • Cyclohexane, C₆H₁₂, is saturated: its ring, not a double bond, lowers the H count.

Mission 2

Homologous series

  • A homologous series is a family with the same functional group.
  • Each member differs from the next by CH₂.
  • One general formula fits every member.
  • Members have similar chemical properties; physical properties change gradually.

Mission 2

Why boiling point rises along a series

  • Boiling separates whole molecules. No covalent bonds break.
  • Each extra CH₂ adds electrons and contact area: stronger London (dispersion) forces.
  • More energy is needed to separate the molecules, so the boiling point rises.
  • In the alcohols, each molecule still has one –OH: its hydrogen bonding stays about the same.
  • Melting points rise too, but less regularly: how molecules pack in the solid also matters.

Mission 3

The parts of a name

  • Stem = carbons in the parent chain: meth 1, eth 2, prop 3, but 4, pent 5, hex 6.
  • -an- only single C–C bonds; -en- one C=C; -yn- one C≡C.
  • Suffix for the main group: -ol (hydroxyl), -al (aldehyde), -one (ketone), -oic acid (carboxyl). Alkanes end in -e.
  • Prefixes for side groups: methyl, ethyl; fluoro, chloro, bromo, iodo.
  • Locants are carbon numbers: 2-methylpentan-3-ol.
Mission 3

Four steps to a name

  • 1. Find the longest chain that contains the functional-group carbon. It may bend through a branch.
  • 2. Number from the end that gives the suffix group the lowest locant.
  • 3. Then the lowest locant to the C=C or C≡C, then to the prefixes.
  • 4. Prefixes in alphabetical order. Repeats: di-, tri- (not used for alphabetical order). Commas between numbers; hyphens between numbers and letters.
Mission 3

Worked examples: prefixes only

  • CH₃CH₂CH(CH₃)CH(CH₃)CH₃: longest chain 5 → pentane.
  • From the right, methyls on C2 and C3; from the left, C3 and C4. Lower set wins.
  • Name: 2,3-dimethylpentane.
  • CH₃CH(CH₃)CHBrCH₂CH₃: from the left {2, 3}; from the right {3, 4}.
  • Name: 3-bromo-2-methylpentane. Prefixes go in alphabetical order (b before m), not number order.

Mission 3

Groups that take a suffix

  • -ol: CH₃CH₂CH₂CH(OH)CH₃ is pentan-2-ol, not pentan-4-ol.
  • -one: CH₃COCH₂CH₂CH₃ is pentan-2-one. Propanone needs no number.
  • -al and -oic acid: that carbon is always C1, so no number: CH₃CH₂CH₂COOH is butanoic acid.
  • With a branch: (CH₃)₂CHCOOH is 2-methylpropanoic acid.
  • -ene, -yne: number the multiple bond by its lower carbon: CH₂=CHCH₂CH₂CH₃ is pent-1-ene; CH₃C≡CCH₃ is but-2-yne.
Mission 3

A C=C plus a suffix group

  • The suffix group gets the lowest locant first; the C=C locant goes before -en-.
  • CH₂=CHCH₂CH(OH)CH₃: from the CH₃ end, OH is on C2 and the C=C starts at C4: pent-4-en-2-ol.
  • Not pent-1-en-4-ol: the suffix group outranks the double bond.
  • Branch plus suffix: CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₂OH is 3-methylbutan-1-ol.

Mission 4

Structural isomers

  • Structural isomers: same molecular formula, atoms connected differently.
  • Chain (branched-chain) isomers: different skeleton. Hexane and 2-methylpentane, both C₆H₁₄.
  • Position isomers: same group, different place. Propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol.
  • Functional group isomers: different series. Propanal and propanone, both C₃H₆O.
  • Rotating, flipping or bending a drawing does not make a new isomer.
Mission 4

Primary, secondary, tertiary

  • Alcohols and halogenoalkanes: count the carbons bonded to the carbon carrying –OH or –X.
  • One → primary; two → secondary; three → tertiary.
  • Propan-1-ol is primary, propan-2-ol secondary, 2-methylpropan-2-ol tertiary. (CH₃)₃CBr is a tertiary halogenoalkane.
  • Amines: count the carbons bonded to N. CH₃CH₂NH₂ 1°, (CH₃)₂NH 2°, (CH₃)₃N 3°.
  • The class is not the locant: hexan-3-ol is secondary.

Mission 5AHL

Cis–trans isomers

  • Stereoisomers: same atoms, same connections, different arrangement in space.
  • Cis–trans needs restricted rotation: a C=C (its π bond stops rotation) or a ring.
  • It also needs two different groups on each of the two carbons.
  • Cis: matching groups on the same side. Trans: on opposite sides.
  • 1,2-Dichloroethene, ClCH=CHCl, has both. Propene, CH₂=CHCH₃, does not: one carbon carries two H.
  • Rings: 1,2-dichlorocyclopropane has cis and trans forms. E–Z names are not needed.

Mission 5AHL

Chiral carbons and enantiomers

  • A chiral carbon is bonded to four different groups.
  • Its molecule exists as two non-superimposable mirror images: enantiomers.
  • 2-Hydroxypropanoic acid (lactic acid), CH₃CH(OH)COOH: C2 carries H, OH, CH₃ and COOH.
  • Wedge-dash drawing: two bonds in the plane, a tapered wedge towards you, a dash away. The shape is a tetrahedron (109.5°).
  • Draw the other enantiomer as the mirror image. In a 3D model, no rotation makes the two match.
Mission 5AHL

Spotting a chiral carbon

  • Only a carbon with four single bonds can be chiral: CH₃, CH₂ and C=C carbons never are.
  • Compare whole groups, not just the atom bonded to the carbon.
  • 1-Chloro-2-methylbutane, CH₂ClCH(CH₃)CH₂CH₃: C2 carries H, CH₃, C₂H₅ and CH₂Cl: chiral.
  • Pentan-3-ol, CH₃CH₂CH(OH)CH₂CH₃: C3 carries H, OH and two C₂H₅: not chiral.

Mission 5AHL

Optical activity

  • Each enantiomer rotates plane-polarized light: by equal angles, in opposite directions. This is optical activity.
  • A racemic mixture contains equal amounts of both enantiomers: the rotations cancel.
  • Enantiomers share melting point, boiling point and density.
  • They react in the same way, except in a chiral environment such as an enzyme or a receptor.

Mission 6AHL

Molecular ion and fragments

  • A molecule loses one electron to form the molecular ion, M⁺. Its m/z = Mr.
  • M⁺ is usually the peak at highest m/z (ignore tiny isotope peaks just above it). It can be small.
  • M⁺ can break into a fragment ion (detected) and a neutral piece (not detected).
  • The tallest peak, the base peak, is often a fragment ion.
  • Mass lost = Mr − fragment m/z. Data booklet (section 22): 15 CH₃, 17 OH, 18 H₂O, 28 C₂H₄ or CO, 29 C₂H₅ or CHO, 31 OCH₃, 45 COOH.
Mission 6AHL

Worked example: ethanol

  • Ethanol, CH₃CH₂OH: Mr = 46, so M⁺ at m/z 46.
  • Base peak at m/z 31: mass lost 46 − 31 = 15, a CH₃ (data booklet).
  • So the ion is CH₂OH⁺: the C–C bond broke.
  • m/z 29: mass lost 46 − 29 = 17, an OH, leaving C₂H₅⁺.

Mission 6AHL

Reading an IR spectrum

  • Bonds stretch and bend. A bond absorbs infrared at wavenumbers that match its vibrations.
  • Wavenumber (cm⁻¹) = 1 ÷ wavelength in cm. Higher wavenumber, higher energy.
  • The spectrum plots % transmittance against wavenumber, from 4000 cm⁻¹ on the left. Absorptions are dips.
  • Read the functional group region, above about 1500 cm⁻¹. Below it, the fingerprint region is too complex to assign band by band. Strong C–O bands (1050–1410) can still be picked out.

Worked example: alcohol or ether?

  • Propan-1-ol and methoxyethane are both C₃H₈O. Spectra X and Y are theirs.
  • X: a strong, broad dip centred near 3350 cm⁻¹: O–H in an alcohol (3200–3600). X is propan-1-ol.

Mission 6AHL

Greenhouse gases

  • The Earth's surface emits infrared. Greenhouse gases absorb it, then re-emit it in all directions.
  • A molecule absorbs IR only if a vibration changes its dipole moment.
  • CO₂ is non-polar, but its asymmetric stretch and bend change the dipole: it absorbs IR. Its symmetric stretch does not.
  • H₂O and CH₄ absorb IR too. N₂ and O₂ do not: stretching them never makes them polar.

Mission 7AHL

What ¹H NMR shows

  • In a strong magnetic field, ¹H nuclei absorb radio waves.
  • H atoms in different chemical environments absorb at different frequencies: one signal per environment.
  • Position: chemical shift, δ in ppm, measured from TMS at δ = 0.
  • δ depends on what is nearby: an O atom or a C=O moves a signal to higher δ.
  • The area under a signal (the integration trace) is proportional to the number of H in that environment.
Mission 7AHL

Worked example: ethanol, low resolution

  • CH₃CH₂OH has three H environments: three signals, areas 3 : 2 : 1.
  • δ 3.7, 2H: the CH₂ on the carbon bonded to O (data booklet R–O–CH₂, 3.3–3.7).
  • δ 2.6, 1H: the O–H (R–OH, 1.0–6.0; its position varies with conditions).
  • δ 1.2, 3H: the CH₃ (R–CH₃, 0.9–1.0; the O two carbons away moves it slightly higher).
Mission 7AHL

Counting environments

  • H atoms are equivalent if symmetry or free rotation makes them interchangeable. Equivalent H share one signal.
  • All three H on one CH₃ group are equivalent.
  • 1,2-Dichloroethane, ClCH₂CH₂Cl: one signal (4H).
  • 2-Methylpropane, (CH₃)₃CH: two signals, areas 9 : 1.

Mission 7AHL

Splitting: the n + 1 rule

  • In a high-resolution spectrum, a signal is split by H atoms on adjacent carbons.
  • n neighbouring H → n + 1 peaks.
  • 0 → singlet, 1 → doublet, 2 → triplet, 3 → quartet.
  • Equivalent H do not split each other. An O–H usually gives a singlet.
  • Splitting shows the neighbours' H; the group's own H count comes from the area.
Mission 7AHL

Worked example: ethanol, high resolution

  • δ 1.2, 3H, triplet: CH₃ next to CH₂ (2 neighbours → 3 peaks).
  • δ 3.7, 2H, quartet: CH₂ next to CH₃ (3 neighbours → 4 peaks).
  • δ 2.6, 1H, singlet: O–H.
  • A 3H triplet with a 2H quartet is the sign of an ethyl group, CH₃CH₂–.

A strategy

  • 1. Molecular formula: from composition; Mr from M⁺.
  • 2. IR: which bonds are present (O–H? C=O? both? neither?).
  • 3. MS fragments: mass lost → pieces such as CH₃, C₂H₅, OH.

  • 5. Propose a structure, then check it against every piece of data.

Recap

The big ideas

  • Formulas show more and more: empirical → molecular → structural → skeletal → stereochemical.
  • The functional group sets the class; a homologous series adds CH₂ each time.
  • Boiling points rise along a series: stronger London forces, same functional-group force.
  • IUPAC: longest chain with the group; lowest locant to the suffix; prefixes in alphabetical order.
  • Structural isomers: same formula, different connections. 1°, 2°, 3°: count carbons on that C (on N for amines).
  • AHLCis–trans needs restricted rotation and two different groups on each carbon; a chiral carbon gives enantiomers.
  • AHLMS gives Mr and mass lost; IR gives bonds; ¹H NMR gives environments, ratios and neighbours. A structure must fit them all.
