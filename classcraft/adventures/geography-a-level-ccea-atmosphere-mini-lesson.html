Mission 1

Mission 1: In 1991 one volcano in the Philippines cooled the whole planet. How?

Make a guess: In 1991 one volcano in the Philippines cooled the whole planet. How?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • AS1.3a.i global energy balance, including vertical and horizontal heat transfers in the atmosphere and the role of ocean currents
Mission 1

The atmosphere as an energy system

  • Energy arrives as shortwave radiation from the Sun and leaves as longwave (infrared) radiation from Earth.
  • IPCC global averages: 340 W/m² of sunlight arrives at the top of the atmosphere; 100 W/m² is reflected straight back to space.
  • Of the rest, 80 W/m² is absorbed by the atmosphere and 160 W/m² by the surface.
Mission 1

The outgoing side

  • The surface emits 398 W/m² of longwave radiation.
  • Greenhouse gases and clouds absorb much of it and send 342 W/m² back down (back radiation).
  • 239 W/m² of longwave leaves the top of the atmosphere.
  • Incoming slightly exceeds outgoing: an imbalance of 0.7 W/m², so the climate system is gaining heat.
Quick check
?340 W/m² of sunlight arrives and 100 W/m² is reflected. What is Earth's planetary albedo?
Mission 1

The natural greenhouse effect

  • Greenhouse gases let most sunlight through but absorb outgoing longwave radiation and re-emit it, partly back towards the surface.
  • "Water vapour is the most important greenhouse gas, and carbon dioxide (CO2) is the second-most important one".
  • To emit about 240 W/m², a surface would need to be about −19 °C; the actual global mean is about 14 °C.
  • The natural greenhouse effect adds about 33 °C (our calculation) and makes Earth habitable.
Mission 1

Vertical heat transfer

  • The surface passes heat up to the atmosphere in three ways (shares of incoming solar energy):
    • latent heat: about 25% leaves the surface through evaporation, released when vapour condenses;
    • convection (sensible heat): about 5%, as warm, buoyant air rises;
    • net longwave radiation: about 17%.
  • So the atmosphere is heated mainly from below, which is why air usually cools with height.
Mission 1

Horizontal heat transfer

  • The Sun heats the equator more than the poles.
  • In September 2008, areas near the equator absorbed about 200 W/m² more than they lost; areas near the poles lost about 200 W/m² more than they absorbed.
  • The tropics have a net surplus; the polar regions an annual deficit.
  • The atmosphere and ocean carry the surplus poleward: winds, storms and ocean currents act as Earth's "heat engine".
Mission 1

The role of ocean currents

  • Warm currents carry heat from the tropics towards the poles (for example the Gulf Stream and North Atlantic Drift).
  • Cold currents return cooler water towards the equator (for example the Canary and Benguela currents).
  • Warm currents raise winter temperatures on coasts they reach: north-west Europe is milder than places at the same latitude in eastern Canada.
Quick check
?Without poleward heat transfer by the atmosphere and oceans, what would happen?
Mission 1

Volcanic forcing: Pinatubo, 1991

  • In June 1991 Mount Pinatubo injected about 15 million tons of sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere.
  • The gas formed tiny droplets (aerosols) that "scatter and absorb incoming sunlight": less sunlight reached the surface.
  • Over the next 15 months, average global temperature fell by about 0.6 °C; measurable cooling lasted almost two years.
Mission check

?A large tropical eruption injects sulfur gas high into the stratosphere. What happens to the global energy balance, and for how long?

Pinatubo put about 15 million tons of sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere. Droplets scattered incoming sunlight, changing the incoming side of the energy balance, and global temperature fell by about 0.6 °C over 15 months. The droplets fell out within about two years, so the cooling was temporary.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why do so many of the world's hot deserts lie near the same two lines of latitude?

Make a guess: Why do so many of the world's hot deserts lie near the same two lines of latitude?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

Mission 2

Why the atmosphere circulates

  • The Sun heats the Earth unevenly: the equator gets more direct sunlight than the poles.
  • Circulation carries heat from the tropics towards the poles, like "a gigantic conveyor belt".
  • On a non-rotating Earth, one cell would form per hemisphere: air rising at the equator, sinking at the poles.
  • George Hadley proposed this single circulation in 1735. Earth's rotation breaks it into three cells in each hemisphere.
Mission 2

The forces on moving air

  • Pressure gradient force (PGF): pushes air from high to low pressure; the closer the isobars, the stronger the wind.
  • Coriolis force: from Earth's rotation; deflects moving air to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern.
  • Friction: near the ground it slows the wind, so air crosses the isobars, spiralling out of highs and into lows.
  • Higher up, with little friction, PGF and Coriolis balance, and the wind blows parallel to the isobars.
Quick check
?In the Northern Hemisphere, surface air flows from the subtropical high towards the equator. Which way is it deflected, and what wind results?
Mission 2

The tri-cellular model

  • Hadley cell: trade winds meet at the ITCZ, where air rises in thunderstorms; air flows poleward aloft, then sinks over the subtropics.
  • Ferrel cell: in mid-latitudes; surface air flows poleward and is deflected into westerlies. It "acts like a gear" between the other two cells.
  • Polar cell: air sinks over the poles, forming polar highs, and flows out as polar easterlies; the smallest and weakest cell.
  • Where Ferrel-cell air meets cold polar air, it rises at the polar front.
Mission 2

Surface pressure belts and winds

  • Low at the equator (rising air, ITCZ).
  • High at about 30° N/S (sinking air) and at the poles.
  • Low at about 50–60° N/S (rising air at the polar front).
  • Winds blow from the highs to the lows, turned by Coriolis: trade winds, westerlies (about 35–60°), polar easterlies.
  • High pressure brings dry weather; low pressure brings rain and storms.
  • Many hot deserts lie along about 30° N/S, including those of the American Southwest and Mexico, northern Africa and Australia.
Quick check
?Why are many hot deserts found at about 30° N and S?
Mission 2

Upper westerlies and Coriolis

  • At the equator Earth's surface moves east at over 1,600 km/h; at the poles, at zero.
  • Air moving poleward keeps its angular momentum (its eastward spin) while the ground beneath it moves east more slowly.
  • So, relative to the surface, the air moves increasingly west to east: this is the Coriolis effect.
  • Result: strong upper westerly winds in the middle and high troposphere of both hemispheres.
Mission 2

Jet streams

  • Jet streams: narrow bands of very strong wind high in the atmosphere, typically about 9,100 m up; they blow west to east.
  • Polar jet: about 50–60° latitude; subtropical jet: about 30°.
  • They form where temperature changes most sharply between air masses, so they are strongest in winter.
  • Speeds can exceed 442 km/h (275 mph).
  • They "follow the Sun", moving poleward in summer and equatorward in autumn and winter.
Quick check
?Why is the polar front jet stream strongest in winter?
Mission 2

Zonal and meridional flow

  • Zonal flow: the jet runs fairly straight west to east and pushes weather systems through quickly.
  • Meridional flow: the jet meanders north and south; weather systems can stall.
  • The polar front jet guides Atlantic depressions. In summer it usually lies north of the UK; in winter it moves south, raising the risk of storms.
  • During meridional flow, lows can sit over the UK, giving prolonged rain and wind.
Mission 2

Model vs reality

  • The three cells are an average over many years; day-to-day weather is messier.
  • The Sun's overhead position shifts with the seasons, so the belts shift too.
  • More land in the Northern Hemisphere creates semi-permanent highs and lows.
  • Sources differ on edges: the Hadley cell reaches about 30° (NOAA) or 30–40° (Met Office).
  • Exam move (CCEA "Explain"): draw the three cells with surface pressure belts and winds; then link each wind to PGF and Coriolis.
Mission check

?Places at 50–60° N, such as the British Isles, are much wetter than places at about 30° N on the west sides of continents. Which explanation fits the general circulation best?

The general circulation sets where air rises (equatorial and 50–60° lows: wet) and where it sinks (30° and polar highs: dry). The pressure gradient force drives the winds and Coriolis turns them into trades, westerlies and polar easterlies. Jet streams form where air masses contrast most.

Mission 3: The Gulf Stream carries more water than all the world's rivers combined. Is that why north-west Europe has mild winters?

Make a guess: The Gulf Stream carries more water than all the world's rivers combined. Is that why north-west Europe has mild winters?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • AS1.3a.i global energy balance, including vertical and horizontal heat transfers in the atmosphere and the role of ocean currents
  • AS1.3a.iii factors influencing air temperature: latitude, continentality, altitude, ocean currents, seasonality
Mission 3

Surface currents and gyres

  • Wind, tides and differences in temperature and salinity drive ocean currents.
  • The Coriolis effect deflects moving water to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern.
  • Surface currents form gyres: five major ones, the North and South Pacific, North and South Atlantic, and Indian Ocean subtropical gyres.
Mission 3

Warm and cold currents

  • Western sides of ocean basins: warm currents flow poleward (Gulf Stream, Kuroshio, Brazil, East Australian).
  • Eastern sides: cold currents flow towards the equator (California, Canary, Humboldt, Benguela).
  • Warm currents warm and moisten the air above them; cold currents cool it, often bringing fog and dry coasts.
  • Polar water flows south as cold currents too, e.g. the Labrador Current.
Quick check
?A current flows towards the equator along the west coast of southern Africa. What is it most likely to be?
Mission 3

The North Atlantic: surface currents

  • The Gulf Stream flows north-east from the Gulf of Mexico. NOAA: "the main conveyor of heat from south to north in the Atlantic".
  • It carries more water than all the world's rivers combined.
  • Its extension, the North Atlantic Drift, carries warm water towards north-west Europe.
  • The Canary Current returns water south along north-west Africa; the cold Labrador Current flows south past Canada.
Mission 3

The North Atlantic: deep currents

  • Below the surface, thermohaline circulation is driven by density, set by temperature and salinity.
  • When sea ice forms, the remaining seawater gets saltier and denser, and sinks. NOAA says the global ocean conveyor belt gets its "start" in the Norwegian Sea.
  • The Atlantic part is the AMOC (Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation): warm water north near the surface, cold, dense water south at depth.
Mission 3

Air temperature: latitude and seasonality

  • Latitude: sunlight is more concentrated where the Sun is high. Compared with the equator, the tropics receive about 90% of the energy, latitude 45° roughly 70% and the polar circles about 40%.
  • Seasonality: Earth's tilt changes sun angle and day length. In the summer hemisphere, a pole can receive more incoming sunlight than the tropics; in winter, none.
  • Snow and sea ice reflect much of that light, so polar summers stay cool.
Mission 3

Air temperature: altitude, sea and currents

  • Altitude: in the troposphere, temperature falls with height, from an average of about 17 °C at the surface to −51 °C at the tropopause.
  • Continentality: water heats and cools more slowly than land. Coasts have a small annual range (maritime); interiors have hot summers and cold winters (continental).
  • Ocean currents: warm currents raise coastal air temperatures; cold currents lower them.
Quick check
?Two towns lie at the same latitude and height. One is on a west-facing coast; the other is 2,000 km inland. Which has the larger annual temperature range, and why?
Mission 3

Does the Gulf Stream warm Europe?

  • Seager et al. (2002) modelled why north-west Europe's winters are milder than eastern North America's at the same latitude.
  • Ocean heat transport warms winters on both sides of the North Atlantic "by a few degC", in an essentially uniform way.
  • The contrast between the two sides is mainly due to "advection by the mean winds": westerly winds bring mild ocean air to Europe and cold continental air to eastern Canada.
  • Exam move: one model study is evidence, not proof. Say what it found, then weigh it.
Mission check

?A newspaper claims: "Without the Gulf Stream, Britain's winters would be as harsh as Labrador's." What does the evidence on these screens suggest?

Partly. The Gulf Stream and the rest of the ocean's heat transport warm winters on both sides of the North Atlantic by a few °C. Seager et al. (2002) found that the prevailing westerlies, bringing mild maritime air to Europe, explain most of its advantage.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Air holds water as an invisible gas. What finally squeezes it out as rain?

Make a guess: Air holds water as an invisible gas. What finally squeezes it out as rain?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • AS1.3b.i formation of orographic (relief), frontal and convectional precipitation
Mission 4

Saturation and dew point

  • Air always holds some water vapour: an invisible gas.
  • Cool the air and it can hold less. At the dew point it is saturated.
  • Cool it further and vapour condenses into droplets: cloud.
  • Met Office: rainfall "is caused by the condensation of the water in air that is being lifted and cooled below its dew point".
  • So every rain type needs the same thing: a reason for air to rise.
Mission 4

Adiabatic cooling

  • Rising air meets lower pressure, so it expands and cools.
  • No heat leaves the parcel: it "cannot exchange heat with its environment". This is adiabatic cooling.
  • Unsaturated rising air cools at the dry adiabatic lapse rate: about 9.8 °C per km.
  • Sinking air is compressed and warms at the same rate, so it dries out.
Mission 4

Above the cloud base

  • Once air is saturated, condensation releases latent heat: "The change of state from vapour to liquid water is accompanied by heating".
  • So saturated air cools more slowly as it keeps rising.
  • The saturated (moist) adiabatic lapse rate "is not a constant like the dry adiabatic lapse rate but is dependent on parcel temperature and pressure".
  • The height where rising air reaches its dew point is the cloud base (condensation level).
Quick check
?Air at 14 °C at sea level is forced upwards. It stays unsaturated until it reaches its dew point at 400 m. Using the dry adiabatic lapse rate of 9.8 °C per km, what is its temperature at 400 m?
Mission 4

Orographic (relief) precipitation

  • Wind pushes air at a hill: "it is forced upwards, as it cannot go inside the hill".
  • It cools adiabatically to its dew point; cloud forms on the windward slope and rain falls.
  • Northern Ireland: the wettest places are the Sperrin, Antrim and Mourne Mountains.
  • Around Killeter Forest, west Tyrone, the average reaches about 2,000 mm a year; near Strangford Lough and the east coast, just under 800 mm.
  • Descending air on the lee side warms and dries: a rain shadow.
Mission 4

Frontal precipitation

  • At a front, warm air meets cold air. The warm air is less dense, so it rises over the cold air.
  • As it rises it cools adiabatically, condenses and rains.
  • Fronts come with Atlantic depressions. In Northern Ireland these "bring most of the rain" in autumn and winter.
  • The wettest months everywhere in Northern Ireland are October to January.
  • Typical result: grey skies and "persistent rain through a large portion of the day".
Mission 4

Convectional precipitation

  • The Sun heats the ground; the ground heats a shallow layer of air, which rises as a thermal.
  • Uneven surfaces heat unevenly: tarmac heats air faster than grass or a lake.
  • If the rising air cools to its dew point, cumulus grows; strong uplift builds cumulonimbus, showers and thunder.
  • In Northern Ireland, summer convection "sometimes forms shower clouds".
  • Thunder at Armagh: fewer than 4 days a year, against 15 to 20 at many places in eastern England.
Quick check
?Which description is frontal precipitation?
Mission 4

One mechanism, three triggers

  • All three: air rises, expands, cools adiabatically to its dew point, condenses, and droplets grow until they fall.
  • What differs is the trigger for rising:
    • orographic: high ground forces air up;
    • frontal: a wedge of colder, denser air forces warm air up;
    • convectional: surface heating makes air buoyant.
  • Types combine: a front crossing the Mournes gives frontal and relief rain.
  • Exam move ("Explain the formation of …"): give the trigger, then the chain: rise → expansion → adiabatic cooling → dew point → condensation → precipitation.
Quick check
?Why do the uplands of west Tyrone get far more rain than the shores of Strangford Lough?
Mission check

?A forecaster says: "Clear skies this morning over the Lough Neagh lowlands, but towering cloud and heavy, scattered showers by mid-afternoon." What turns the vapour into rain here?

Whatever the trigger (a hill, a front or a hot surface), rain needs air to rise, expand, cool adiabatically to its dew point and condense. This afternoon's showers are convectional.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Weather in the British Isles can swing from gales to frost. What makes it so changeable?

Make a guess: Weather in the British Isles can swing from gales to frost. What makes it so changeable?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • AS1.3b.ii mid-latitude weather systems (depressions and anticyclones) and their formation, with reference to air masses and the Polar Front Jet Stream
Mission 5

Highs and lows

  • Air pressure is measured in hectopascals (hPa), the same as millibars; average sea-level pressure is about 1013 hPa.
  • Depression (low): air rises, cools and condenses into cloud and rain; unsettled, often with fronts.
  • Anticyclone (high): air sinks, which suppresses cloud; settled, light winds.
  • In the Northern Hemisphere, wind turns anticlockwise round lows and clockwise round highs.
Mission 5

Air masses

  • An air mass is a large body of air with fairly uniform temperature and humidity, set by its source region.
  • Neighbouring air masses meet at fronts.
  • Polar (P) air is cold; tropical (T) is warm. Maritime (m) crossed sea: moist; continental (c) crossed land: dry.
  • Six affect the British Isles; polar maritime is the most common.
Mission 5

Where the air comes from

  • Polar maritime (Pm): from northern Canada and Greenland; cool, showery.
  • Tropical maritime (Tm): from the Atlantic between the Azores and Bermuda; mild, moist, low cloud, drizzle, fog in exposed areas.
  • Polar continental (Pc): from eastern Europe and Russia, winter only; very cold and dry.
  • Tropical continental (Tc): from North Africa and the Sahara, mostly June–August; hot, dry.
  • Also Arctic maritime (colder than Pm) and returning polar maritime (longer sea track, milder).
Quick check
?In January, high pressure sits over Scandinavia and easterly winds cross a short stretch of sea to Britain. Which air mass arrives, and what weather does it bring?
Mission 5

The polar front and the jet stream

  • The polar front forms where cold polar air meets warmer tropical air.
  • High above it runs the Polar Front Jet Stream, around 50–60° latitude, strongest in winter.
  • The jet stream "helps to develop and steer areas of low pressure".
  • In summer it usually lies north of the UK; in winter it moves south, bringing more depressions.
Mission 5

How a depression forms

  • Birth: warm air (such as Tm) meets cooler air (such as Pm) along the polar front.
  • A kink develops; where it interacts with the jet stream it can rotate into a new low.
  • Maturity: warm air rises and spirals anticlockwise over sinking cold air, making a warm sector between a warm front and a cold front.
  • Occlusion: the faster cold front catches the warm front; the warm sector is lifted off the ground.
  • Death: the warm air has all risen and cooled; the occlusion fades.
Mission 5

Weather as a depression passes

  • Ahead of the warm front: high cirrus; pressure falls steadily.
  • Warm front: cloud thickens and lowers; drizzle then steady rain from altostratus and nimbostratus.
  • Warm sector: pressure steadies; rain stops; milder, often low cloud.
  • Cold front: cumulonimbus, heavy downpours, squalls; pressure rises, temperature drops.
  • Behind: fine and cool, with a few showers.
  • Extreme case: Storm Desmond brought "severe gales" and 341.4 mm of rain at Honister Pass, Cumbria, in 24 hours to 18:00 on 5 December 2015, a UK record.
Quick check
?At a weather station, steady rain has just stopped. Pressure has stopped falling, the air feels milder, and low cloud remains. Where is the depression relative to the station?
Mission 5

Anticyclones

  • Air sinks and warms, so clouds tend not to form; winds are light.
  • Summer: fine, warm, sunny and dry; heatwaves and drought are possible.
  • Winter: cold, dry days, severe overnight frost and fog; sometimes a sheet of stratocumulus cloud cuts the sunshine.
  • Under a high, air can stagnate near the ground.
Mission 5

A winter anticyclone: the Great Smog, 1952

  • From 5 to 9 December 1952, an anticyclone hung over London.
  • Clear skies let the ground cool; moist air cooled to its dew point: radiation fog; an inversion trapped it.
  • Smoke mixed with the fog. About 4,000 people were known to have died, "but it could be many more".
  • The Clean Air Acts of 1956 and 1968 followed.
Mission 5

Blocking highs

  • Blocks are highs that stay nearly stationary and divert the usual west-to-east march of weather systems.
  • Fronts skirt round the edges, so one area stays dry while another stays wet and windy.
  • A weak jet stream is one way a high becomes slow-moving.
  • Exceptional blocks lasted months: mid-summer 1976, mid-winter 1963.
Quick check
?Why can a blocking anticyclone keep one part of the British Isles dry for weeks?
Mission check

?In one week, Belfast has a mild, drizzly day with south-westerly winds, a sudden squally downpour, then three still days with hard frost and fog. Which explanation fits best?

The British Isles sit under the polar front, where different air masses meet. The jet stream develops and steers depressions through; anticyclones, sometimes blocking, bring the settled spells between.

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

Mission 6

What a synoptic chart shows

  • A synoptic chart summarises the weather over a wide area at one time.
  • The Met Office issues an analysis chart (the observed state) and forecast charts up to five days ahead, updated about every 12 hours.
  • Isobars join places of equal pressure; H and L mark highs and lows with their central pressure.
  • High pressure usually means settled weather; low pressure, unsettled and windier.
Mission 6

Reading isobars

  • Closely spaced isobars mean strong winds; widely spaced isobars, lighter winds.
  • Air moves from high to low pressure; the pressure gradient drives it.
  • In the Northern Hemisphere, air circulates clockwise round highs and anticlockwise round lows.
  • Near the ground the wind spirals outward from highs and inward to lows.
Quick check
?On a chart, isobars are packed tightly over north-west Scotland and spread far apart over south-east England. What does this show?
Mission 6

Front symbols

  • Warm front: red line with semicircles pointing the way it moves.
  • Cold front: blue line with triangles.
  • Occluded front: purple line with alternating triangles and semicircles, where a cold front has caught a warm front.
  • A front line broken by crosses is weakening.
  • Trough: a black line with no symbols; often brings showers, but is not an air-mass boundary.
Mission 6

Reading a depression on a chart

  • Find the L and the fronts trailing from it; the warm sector lies between warm and cold fronts.
  • The warm sector "often brings low cloud and patchy light rain".
  • A frontal wave appears as a small section of warm front on a long cold front; it can delay clearance and prolong rain.
  • Expect the weather sequence from the previous mission as the fronts pass.
Quick check
?On a chart, a purple line with alternating triangles and semicircles curls round a low centre. What is it?
Mission 6

Wind direction from the chart

  • Picture the wind blowing along the isobars, anticlockwise round a low (Northern Hemisphere).
  • Then turn it slightly inward towards the low, as friction does near the ground.
  • So, south of a low centre the wind blows from the west or south-west; north of it, from the east.
  • Around a high, reverse it: clockwise, spiralling outward.
Quick check
?A deep low is centred over the Hebrides, with tight isobars across Northern Ireland, which lies to its south. What wind should Belfast expect?
Mission 6

Satellite images: two kinds

  • Geostationary satellites orbit at 35,786 km and view the whole Earth disc all the time, with limited views of the poles.
  • Polar-orbiting satellites fly about 833 km up; they cannot see the whole surface at once and give about six or seven images a day.
  • Visible images show sunlight reflected by cloud; they "appear black at night".
  • Infrared images sense heat, so they work by day and night.
Mission 6

Reading infrared images

  • Air cools with height: about 2–3 °C per 300 m (1,000 ft).
  • So high cloud tops are colder than low cloud, land or sea.
  • IR images are usually displayed so the coldest tops look whitest: deep frontal cloud and cumulonimbus bright white; low cloud and fog grey.
  • Infrared bands help find night-time fog and low cloud.
Quick check
?At 03:00 on a winter night, a forecaster wants to see where cloud lies over the British Isles. Which image should she use?
Mission 6

Charts and images together

  • Depression: an L with fronts on the chart; on the image, long bands of thick, bright cloud along the fronts curling round the low.
  • Anticyclone: an H with widely spaced isobars; on the image, clear skies, or a flat sheet of stratocumulus in winter.
  • Storm Desmond, 5–6 December 2015: rain records in Cumbria and gusts up to about 130 km/h (81 mph).
  • Exam move (CCEA "Interpret"): name the system, quote the chart evidence (isobars, symbols), quote the image evidence, then state the weather.
Mission check

?In January, a chart shows a 1035 hPa high centred over the North Sea, widely spaced isobars across Northern Ireland and no fronts. A night-time infrared image shows few clouds over Ireland. What should Belfast expect by dawn?

On a synoptic chart, spacing of isobars gives wind strength, flow round H and L gives direction, and front symbols locate rain. Satellite images confirm the cloud: visible by day, infrared at any hour.

Mission 7

Mission 7: In December 2023 the Panama Canal let through 22 ships a day, against 36 in its "regular scenario". What did a patch of warm Pacific Ocean have to do with it?

Make a guess: In December 2023 the Panama Canal let through 22 ships a day, against 36 in its "regular scenario". What did a patch of warm Pacific Ocean have to do with it?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • AS1.3c.i changes to global weather patterns associated with ENSO and La Niña
Mission 7

Normal conditions in the tropical Pacific

  • Trade winds blow west along the equator, pushing warm surface water from South America towards Asia.
  • Cold, nutrient-rich water upwells off South America to replace it.
  • Warm water piles up in the west. Air rises over it and sinks over the cooler east: the Walker circulation.
Mission 7

El Niño and La Niña

  • El Niño: above-average sea surface temperatures in the central and eastern tropical Pacific; trade winds weaken or reverse.
  • The rising branch of the Walker circulation shifts east of 180°: rain falls over the central Pacific and decreases over Indonesia.
  • La Niña: cooler than average there, stronger trade winds, more upwelling, more rain over Indonesia.
  • The thermocline deepens in El Niño (typically 150–175 m) and shallows in La Niña (typically 50–100 m).
Mission 7

ENSO: one coupled system

  • ENSO (El Niño–Southern Oscillation) is coupled: ocean and atmosphere change together.
  • The Southern Oscillation is a see-saw of air pressure between Tahiti and Darwin. A strongly negative SOI goes with El Niño; strongly positive with La Niña.
  • NOAA's Oceanic Niño Index (ONI): +0.5 or higher means El Niño; −0.5 or lower, La Niña.
  • Episodes typically occur every 3–5 years (range 2–7) and last nine to 12 months.
Quick check
?For three months the east-central equatorial Pacific has been 1.4 °C above average, the SOI is strongly negative and trade winds are weak. Which phase is this?
Mission 7

Changes to global weather patterns

  • When the Walker circulation shifts, rainfall belts shift with it: drier in some regions, wetter in others.
  • El Niño "contributes to more eastern Pacific hurricanes and fewer Atlantic hurricanes"; La Niña does the reverse.
  • These distant links are called teleconnections.
  • Exam move: name the phase, the region, the change (wetter/drier, more/fewer storms) and the date of a real event.
Mission 7

El Niño 2023–24: Panama and southern Africa

  • WMO: the 2023–24 El Niño peaked in December 2023 "as one of five strongest on record".
  • Panama: October 2023 was the driest October on record in the Canal watershed. Its lakes store water "for meeting the needs of the population and ensuring the smooth operation of the canal".
  • Transits were cut to 22 a day, against 36 in the "regular scenario", due to "the drought induced by the El Niño phenomenon".
  • Southern Africa: February 2024 was the driest on record in large parts of the region; 71 million people were food insecure. SADC called El Niño "the primary driver" of the increase, though "not the sole driver".
Mission 7

La Niña 2020–23: the Horn of Africa

  • La Niña lasted, with short interruptions, from September 2020: WMO called it the first "triple-dip" La Niña of the 21st century.
  • Southern Ethiopia, southern Somalia and eastern Kenya had five failed rainy seasons in a row from late 2020.
  • "There is a high correlation between below-average short rains and La Niña".
  • World Weather Attribution: climate change made such droughts "about 100 times more likely", mainly through higher evaporation.
Quick check
?Panama's daily transits fell from 36 to 22. By what percentage were they cut?
Mission check

?Australia's Millennium Drought (1997–2009) ended in 2010–11, when two strong La Niña events brought heavy rain. What did the Pacific have to do with it?

The same Walker circulation explains Panama. In El Niño the warm water and rising air shifted east, rain over the Canal watershed failed, and the lakes ran too low for 36 ships a day. ENSO moves rain belts across the globe.

Mission 8: Hurricane Ian hit Florida with 130-knot winds in 2022. Why was water, not wind, its deadliest hazard?

Make a guess: Hurricane Ian hit Florida with 130-knot winds in 2022. Why was water, not wind, its deadliest hazard?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • AS1.3c.ii how hurricanes, tropical cyclones or typhoons form, and their structure
Mission 8

What a tropical storm is

  • A tropical cyclone is "a rapidly rotating storm that begins over tropical oceans"; it is called a hurricane or typhoon depending on the region.
  • The Saffir–Simpson scale rates hurricanes 1 to 5 by maximum sustained wind only. Category 4 is 113–136 kt; categories 3–5 are major hurricanes.
  • The scale "does not take into account" storm surge, rainfall flooding or tornadoes.
Mission 8

Structure

  • Eye: a "nearly cloud-free area of light winds", generally 20–50 km across.
  • Eyewall: a ring of towering thunderstorms around the eye, where warm air turns upward. "Usually, the strongest winds and heaviest precipitation are found in this area".
  • Rain bands: spiral inwards towards the eyewall, bringing heavy rain and wind, occasionally tornadoes, with rain-free gaps between them.
Mission 8

How they form

  • Ocean water of about 27 °C (80 °F) at the surface, warm to about 45 m (150 ft) depth.
  • An atmosphere that cools quickly with height and is moist in the middle troposphere; low vertical wind shear.
  • Enough Coriolis spin: at least about 480 km (300 miles) from the equator.
  • A pre-existing disturbance: about 85% of intense Atlantic hurricanes begin as African Easterly Waves. The conditions are "necessary but not sufficient".
Quick check
?Why do tropical cyclones almost never form on the equator, even over warm sea?
Mission 8

Distribution, frequency and magnitude

  • Distribution: tropical cyclones form over tropical waters between 25°S and 25°N.
  • Regularity: seasons run July–October in the Northern Hemisphere and December–March in the Southern, with differences by basin; globally, September is the most active month. The north-west Pacific has them all year.
  • Frequency: an average Atlantic season (1991–2020) has 14 named storms, 7 hurricanes and 3 major hurricanes.
  • Magnitude: measured by wind (Saffir–Simpson), but impacts also depend on size, surge and rain.
  • WMO: over the past 50 years, 1,945 disasters attributed to tropical cyclones killed 779,324 people.
Mission 8

Storm surge

  • Storm surge: "an abnormal water level rise generated by a storm over and above the predicted astronomical tide".
  • Mainly driven by strong onshore winds; low air pressure adds a little more.
  • Higher on a wide, gently sloping continental shelf, and on a concave coast.
  • Surge plus high tide gives the storm tide, which floods low coasts in hours.
Mission 8

Case study: Hurricane Ian, 2022

  • Ian intensified rapidly over waters of 30 °C and higher, with low wind shear.
  • 27 September: landfall near La Coloma, Pinar del Río, Cuba, as a 110-kt category 3 hurricane.
  • 28 September: peak of 140 kt (category 5), then landfall on Cayo Costa, Florida, at 130 kt (category 4).
Mission 8

Ian's surge on two coasts

  • Florida (developed): peak surge inundation of 3–4.6 m (10–15 ft) above ground at Fort Myers Beach; parts of the Sanibel Causeway washed away.
  • Cuba (developing): at La Coloma the sea reached 2.5–3 m and penetrated 300 m inland; at Majana and Cajío, up to 1,500 m inland.
  • Florida's coast is low and its shelf wide and gentle, which favours a high surge.
Mission 8

Impacts: developed and developing

  • USA: at least 156 deaths, 66 directly caused by the storm: surge 41, freshwater flooding 12, wind 4.
  • US damage: an estimated $112.9 billion (90% confidence interval $86.8–135.9 billion). In Lee County, 52,514 structures impacted, 5,369 destroyed.
  • Cuba: 5 deaths (3 direct); more than 100,000 homes damaged or destroyed; the whole island reportedly lost power for a few days.
  • There is "no monetary damage estimate available for Cuba", so losses cannot be compared in dollars.
Quick check
?What percentage of Ian's direct deaths in the USA did storm surge cause?
Mission check

?In 1953 a North Sea storm, not a hurricane, killed 307 people in England and 1,836 in the Netherlands. Why can a storm's water kill more people than its wind?

Ian's onshore winds drove a 3–4.6 m (10–15 ft) surge over low islands at Fort Myers Beach; surge caused 41 of the 66 direct US deaths (62%). The Saffir–Simpson scale measures wind, but on low, gently shelving coasts the sea is the main killer.

Mission 9: Why can a storm cost a rich country billions, yet kill far more people in a poor one?

Make a guess: Why can a storm cost a rich country billions, yet kill far more people in a poor one?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • AS1.3c.iii impacts of a recent tropical cyclone and evaluation of the management strategies in place (national/regional case study)
Mission 9

Two storms, two places

  • Hurricane Ian, 2022: landfall in western Cuba on 27 September as a 110-knot category 3; then Cayo Costa, Florida, on 28 September at 130 knots.
  • Cyclone Idai, 2019: landfall near Beira, Mozambique, at 23:30 GMT on 14 March, with winds above 150 km/h. Official death toll in Mozambique: 602 by 7 April 2019.
  • The United States is a high-income economy; Mozambique is low-income (World Bank, FY2027).
  • Your case study: Ian in Florida, with Cuba as a contrast.
Mission 9

Sorting impacts

  • Primary impacts: caused directly by wind, rain and storm surge (drowning, destroyed homes).
  • Secondary impacts: follow later from the primary ones (disease, power cuts, lost harvests, lost jobs).
Mission 9

Ian in Florida: impacts

  • At least 156 deaths, 66 caused directly by the storm; storm surge caused 41, with 36 of these in Lee County.
  • Surge reached 3–4.6 m (10–15 ft) above ground level at Fort Myers Beach.
  • In Lee County, 52,514 structures were impacted and 5,369 destroyed.
  • Parts of the Sanibel causeway were washed away; Sanibel and Pine Islands could be reached only by boat or helicopter.
  • 4.45 million customers (about 9.62 million people) lost power.
  • Leading cause of 90 indirect deaths: lack of timely medical care (18).
  • Damage in the US: $112.9 billion.
Quick check
?After Ian, storm water carried fertiliser and debris into the Gulf of Mexico, followed by a harmful algal bloom. How is this impact best classified?
Mission 9

Ian: responses

  • Short-term: over 250 water rescues in central and eastern Florida.
  • Long-term: federal support topped $10.2 billion in two years, including $1.15 billion to 387,139 households.
  • Flood insurance (NFIP) paid $4.67 billion to about 47,000 policyholders.
  • Cuba (contrast): 76,221 people evacuated under Civil Defence guidelines; 5 deaths; over 100,000 homes damaged or destroyed; the whole island lost power for a few days.
  • In Cuba, losses in tobacco, a key export, were described as "disastrous". The IFRC notes Cuba's exclusion from major international financial institutions "complicates its disaster response".
Mission 9

Management in place: forecasts and warnings

  • The National Hurricane Center (NHC) began talking to emergency managers on 23 September, four days before Cuba's landfall.
  • At Fort Myers Beach, the surge watch and warning came 48 h and 30 h before tropical-storm-force winds.
  • The forecast cone contained the landfall point in every advisory.
  • But on the morning of 27 September the surge forecast rose from 1.2–2.1 m (4–7 ft) to 2.4–3.7 m (8–12 ft), and finally to 3.7–5.5 m (12–18 ft).
  • The NHC could not predict Ian's second rapid intensification to 140 knots.
Mission 9

Evaluating Ian's management

  • Strengths: early, accurate track forecasts and long warning lead times; insurance and federal aid funded recovery at scale.
  • Weaknesses: surge caused 62% of direct deaths (41 of 66): warnings did not move everyone out of the surge zone. The late rise in the surge forecast may have contributed.
  • Power loss and blocked roads raised indirect deaths (lack of medical care).
  • Property: about 10% of impacted structures in Lee County were destroyed (5,369 of 52,514; our calculation).
  • Exam move ("Evaluate"): judge each strategy against evidence for people and for property, then reach a weighted conclusion.
Quick check
?Which evidence best supports the judgement that Florida's warnings were only partly successful?
Mission check

?Two cyclones of similar strength strike two coasts. Country X (high income): $40 billion damage, 20 deaths. Country Y (low income): $1 billion damage, 900 deaths and a cholera outbreak. Which explanation best fits?

Wealth changes what a storm costs. Rich places lose more money because their exposed property is worth more, but they can warn, shelter, insure and rebuild. Poorer places lose more lives and livelihoods, and secondary impacts such as disease and food insecurity last longer.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Earth's energy balance: 340 W/m² in, 100 reflected, 239 longwave out; the natural greenhouse effect adds about 33 °C.
  • Heat moves up from the surface (latent heat, convection, radiation) and poleward (winds and ocean currents).

Mission 2

  • Uneven heating drives three cells per hemisphere (Hadley, Ferrel, Polar), giving surface belts: low at the equator, high near 30°, low near 50–60°, high at the poles.
  • PGF drives the winds; Coriolis turns them (trades, westerlies, polar easterlies, upper westerlies); jet streams at about 30° and 50–60° steer weather systems, in zonal or meridional patterns.

Mission 3

  • Ocean currents are part of the horizontal heat transfer in the global energy balance: warm currents carry heat poleward and cold currents return cooler water.
  • Air temperature depends on latitude, seasonality, altitude, continentality and ocean currents.

Mission 4

  • Rising air expands and cools adiabatically (about 9.8 °C per km when unsaturated); at the dew point vapour condenses; latent heat makes saturated air cool more slowly.
  • Orographic (forced up hills), frontal (forced over colder air) and convectional (heated from below) differ only in the trigger.

Mission 5

  • Six air masses (Pm most common; Tm, Pc, Tc, Am, rPm) bring contrasting weather; they meet at the polar front, under the Polar Front Jet Stream.
  • Depressions: birth, maturity (warm sector between warm and cold fronts), occlusion, death; anticyclones: sinking air, settled; frost and fog in winter; blocks divert fronts.

Mission 6

  • Chart reading: H/L and central pressure; isobar spacing = wind strength; anticlockwise round lows, clockwise round highs (NH), spiralling in/out; red semicircles, blue triangles, purple alternating, crosses = weakening, black trough lines.
  • Satellite reading: geostationary vs polar-orbiting; visible (day only) vs infrared (day and night; cold, high tops brightest).

Mission 7

  • ENSO shifts the Walker circulation and with it rainfall belts and storm tracks: El Niño dried Panama and southern Africa (2023–24); La Niña dried East Africa (2020–23).

Mission 8

  • Tropical cyclones form over warm oceans away from the equator; the eye, eyewall and rain bands organise their winds and rain.
  • Storm surge, from tropical cyclones or depressions (such as the North Sea storm of 1953), is often the deadliest hazard; Ian's surge flooded both Florida and Cuba, but losses are recorded very differently.

Mission 9

  • Ian (2022, Florida): 66 direct deaths, mostly from surge; $112.9 billion damage; warnings 30–48 h ahead, but a late surge upgrade; recovery funded by federal aid and insurance.
  • Evaluate management by evidence for people and property: Ian's forecasts and recovery funding worked; evacuation from the surge zone did not fully succeed.
