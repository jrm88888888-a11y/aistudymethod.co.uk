Mission 1

Read the data before you use it

  • Before you calculate anything, check four things:
  • Units: %, billions, an index, per person? Money in which currency and which year's prices?
  • Level or rate of change? "GDP" is a level; "GDP growth" is a rate of change.
  • Time period: a month, a quarter, a year? Which years are compared?
  • Source: who collected it, and how? Is it a forecast or an outturn?
  • Then ask: nominal or real? Money values not adjusted for inflation are nominal.
  • Say: "the rate of inflation fell" — not: "prices fell", when inflation is still above zero.
Mission 1

Percentage change or percentage points?

  • Percentage change = (new value − old value) ÷ old value × 100.
  • When the data are already rates (%), the plain difference is in percentage points.
  • Illustrative data: an unemployment rate rises from 4.0% to 5.0%.
  • Difference: 5.0 − 4.0 = 1 percentage point.
  • Percentage change: 1 ÷ 4 × 100 = 25%.
  • Real case: UK Bank Rate was 0.1% before December 2021 and 5.25% from August 2023.
  • That is a rise of 5.15 percentage points, or a 5,150% rise. Both true; the second sounds far more dramatic.
  • Percentage changes do not cancel: 100 falls 20% to 80; 80 rises 20% to 96, not 100.
  • Say: "rose by 1 percentage point" — not: "rose by 1%".
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A country's unemployment rate falls from 8.0% to 6.0%. Which statement is correct?
Mission 1

Index numbers in a data extract

  • Index = (value ÷ value in base year) × 100 (method in an earlier lesson).
  • Index numbers compare growth of series in different units.
  • Illustrative data: house prices and earnings, both 100 in the base year. Ten years later: house prices 160, earnings 125.
  • 160 > 125 does not mean houses cost more than people earn. It means house prices grew faster since the base year.
  • House prices relative to earnings: 160 ÷ 125 = 1.28, so 28% higher than in the base year.
  • Rebasing: new index = old index ÷ old index in the new base year × 100.
  • Illustrative data: an index is 110 in 2020 and 132 in 2024. With 2020 = 100: 132 ÷ 110 × 100 = 120.
  • 110 → 132 is a rise of 22 points, but 20%.
  • Say: "the index rose by 22 points, a 20% rise" — not: "the index rose by 22%".
Mission 1

Worked example: nominal to real

  • Illustrative data. Average weekly pay: 600 in 2020, 690 in 2024.
  • Price index (2020 = 100): 120 in 2024.
  • Nominal pay rose: 690 ÷ 600 = 1.15, so +15%.
  • Real pay in 2024, at 2020 prices = 690 × (100 ÷ 120) = 575.
  • Real change: 575 ÷ 600 = 0.958, so −4.2%.
  • Shortcut: 15% − 20% = −5%. Close, but only an approximation; use the exact method for exam answers.
  • Say: "real pay fell by about 4%" — not: "pay fell".
Quick check
?Illustrative data. Government spending on health rises from 150 billion to 180 billion. Over the same period, a price index for health services rises from 100 to 125. What happened to real spending on health?
Mission 1

Graphs: a falling rate is not a falling level

  • On a graph of a rate of change (inflation, growth), a falling line above zero means the level is still rising, only more slowly.
  • Real case: UK CPI inflation reached 11.1% in October 2022, its highest rate in over 40 years.
  • It fell to 2.0% in May 2024.
  • Prices did not fall. They kept rising, more slowly: disinflation.
  • Also check: does the vertical axis start at zero? A cut-off axis makes small changes look large.
  • On a chart with two vertical axes, check which line belongs to which axis.
  • Say: "disinflation" — not: "deflation", unless the price level itself falls.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. An economy's quarterly real GDP growth rates are: Q1 0.8%, Q2 0.5%, Q3 0.2%, Q4 0.1%. Which statement is correct?
Mission 1

Build an evaluation chain

  • Point: answer the question in one line.
  • Analysis: a chain of reasoning, step by step: "because … which leads to … so …".
  • Evidence: quote the data with units and dates, and calculate where it helps.
  • Evaluation: "it depends on …": how big? how likely? short run or long run? who gains, who loses? how reliable is the data?
  • Judgement: weigh the factors and say which matters most, and why.
  • Say: "this depends on the size of the multiplier, because …" — not just: "it depends".
Mission 1Evaluate

Did the policy work?

  • Real case: the Bank of England's target is 2% CPI inflation.
  • Bank Rate rose 14 times, from 0.1% to 5.25% (December 2021 to August 2023).
  • CPI inflation fell from 11.1% (October 2022) to 2.0% (May 2024).
  • Criteria for success: was the target met? How quickly? At what cost to growth and jobs?
  • It depends on …
  • The counterfactual: what would inflation have done anyway? Other causes, such as world energy prices, may have changed too.
  • Time lags: interest rates affect spending and prices only after a delay, so the timing of any effect is uncertain.
  • Side effects: higher mortgage and loan costs, weaker spending and output.
  • Other policies: fiscal policy was acting at the same time.
  • Judge: a target being reached shows the outcome, not on its own what caused it.
Quick check
?In an essay on whether higher interest rates brought UK inflation down after 2022, which sentence is evaluation?

Mission 2

Multinationals and FDI

  • A multinational corporation (MNC): a firm that produces or provides services in more than one country.
  • Edexcel also says transnational corporation (TNC).
  • Foreign direct investment (FDI): a firm in one country invests in productive assets in another that it controls.
    • Greenfield: building a new factory, mine or office.
    • Brownfield / acquisition: buying or merging with a foreign firm.
  • Statistical benchmark: owning 10% or more of the voting shares counts as a lasting interest, so FDI.
  • Inward FDI: foreign firms investing here. Outward FDI: our firms investing abroad.
  • Say: "FDI" for investment by firms — not: aid, which is a transfer from governments or charities.

Mission 2

Why firms go multinational

  • Market access: produce inside a big market, avoiding tariffs and transport costs (e.g. inside a trading bloc).
  • Lower costs: cheaper labour, land or energy.
  • Resources: oil, minerals, farmland only found in certain places.
  • Economies of scale: spread research, brands and management over global sales.
  • Spreading risk across many economies.
  • Lower tax: some countries offer low tax rates or tax breaks to attract investment.
  • Host governments' incentives (grants, tax holidays, special economic zones) pull FDI too.
Mission 2

Impact on the host country

Possible benefits:

  • Jobs and higher wages than local alternatives.
  • Capital a low-saving economy could not raise itself.
  • Technology transfer and skills: training, management methods, new processes.
  • Linkages: local suppliers win orders and learn standards.
  • Tax revenue, exports and foreign currency.

Possible costs:

  • Profit repatriation: profits flow abroad; GNI rises less than GDP.
  • Low pay, poor conditions, pollution if rules are weak.
  • Local firms crowded out by stronger rivals.
  • Footloose: plants can relocate when costs rise.
  • Tax avoidance and political influence.
  • Real case: inward FDI into Vietnam rose from US$1.3bn in 2000 to US$20.2bn in 2024.
Mission 2

Impact on the home country

  • Profits flow home: income credits in the current account (primary income).
  • Cheaper goods for home consumers; firms more competitive worldwide.
  • Jobs may move abroad (offshoring): structural unemployment in home regions.
  • Home investment may fall if firms build abroad instead.
  • Home tax base shrinks if profits are booked abroad.

?A foreign-owned car plant in Country H produces output worth $500m a year (value added). It sends $200m of profit to its parent company abroad each year (illustrative data). Other things equal, how does the plant change Country H's GDP and GNI?
Mission 2

Transfer pricing

  • Transfer price: the price one part of an MNC charges another part for goods, services, brands or loans.
  • MNCs can set transfer prices to book profit where tax is low.
  • Worked example (illustrative data). Plant in Country H (profits tax 30%) makes a part for $40. A sister firm in Country L (tax 10%) sells the finished good for $100.

  • Country H loses tax revenue it could spend on public services.
  • Rule used by tax authorities: the arm's length principle: charge what two independent firms would charge.

Mission 2

Ethics and controlling MNCs

  • Ethical issues: pay and conditions, environmental damage, supply-chain safety, marketing (e.g. unhealthy products in poorer countries).
  • Real case: the Rana Plaza factory building in Bangladesh collapsed in April 2013, killing more than 1,100 people. Most were garment workers making clothes for international brands.

Ways to control MNCs:

  • Politics: governments set terms for entry, taxes and ownership.
  • Law: minimum wages, health and safety, environmental and competition law.
  • Tax rules: arm's length transfer pricing, sharing tax data between countries.
  • Real case: a global minimum tax rate of 15% on large MNCs, agreed through the OECD, now applies in many countries.
  • Pressure groups and social media: campaigns damage brands and push change.
  • Self-regulation: codes of conduct, supplier audits, CSR reports.
  • After Rana Plaza, brands and unions signed the Accord on Fire and Building Safety in Bangladesh (2013), with factory inspections.
Mission 2

Evaluate: can MNCs be controlled?

It depends on…

  • Bargaining power. A small host competing for jobs may not risk losing the investment; large markets can set tougher terms.
  • International co-operation. One country acting alone can be avoided by moving profits or plants; agreed rules (minimum tax, data sharing) are harder to dodge.
  • Information. Transfer prices for brands and know-how are hard to check: no market price exists to compare.
  • Enforcement. Laws matter only if inspectors and courts work.
  • Consumers. Pressure and self-regulation work best where buyers notice and care.
Mission 2

Evaluate: inward FDI as a development strategy

How governments attract FDI: tax breaks, special economic zones, infrastructure, education and stable rules.

Whether it works depends on…

  • Linkages. FDI that buys from local suppliers and trains local staff spreads skills and demand. An enclave (imported inputs, foreign managers) does little.
  • Type of FDI. Resource-seeking (mining) often creates few jobs; manufacturing and services usually create more.
  • Terms. Large tax breaks cost revenue; countries competing to offer them risk a "race to the bottom".
  • Profit outflows. Much of the income may leave: watch GNI, not just GDP.
  • Absorptive capacity. Skills and infrastructure let local firms learn from foreign ones.
  • Time. Jobs come at once; productivity spillovers build over years.

Mission 3

From no trade to free trade

  • Start with domestic demand and domestic supply only (no trade). Price settles at 7, quantity 50 (illustrative data).
  • Now open the border. A small country is a price taker: it can buy or sell any amount at the world price (Pw).
  • World price 4, below the domestic price:
    • consumers buy more: 80
    • domestic firms produce less: 20
    • the gap, 80 − 20 = 60, is imports.
  • Say: "domestic supply" — not just "supply": with trade, total supply at Pw is domestic output plus imports.
Quick check
?A country that did not trade in rice opens its market. The world price of rice is below its domestic no-trade price. Which outcome follows?
Mission 3

The protectionist toolbox

Protectionism: government measures that shield domestic producers from foreign competition.

  • Tariff: a tax on imports (per unit, or a percentage of value).
  • Quota: a legal limit on the quantity (or value) of a good that may be imported.
  • Subsidy: a payment to domestic producers, so they can compete with imports or sell more abroad (export subsidy).
  • Embargo: a ban on trade in a good, or with a country.
  • Administrative barriers (non-tariff barriers, "red tape"): standards, regulations, licences and slow customs checks that make importing harder.
Mission 3

The tariff diagram

Follow the diagram step by step (illustrative data):

1. Free trade at Pw = 4: domestic output 20, consumption 80, imports 60.

2. A tariff of 2 per unit: the price in the home market rises to Pw + tariff = 6.

3. Domestic output extends to 40; consumption contracts to 60; imports fall to 20.

4. Government revenue = tariff × imports after the tariff: the rectangle.

5. Two triangles: the deadweight (welfare) loss.

  • Producer side: high-cost domestic firms replace cheaper imports (wasted resources).
  • Consumer side: buyers who valued the good above the world price no longer buy it.
  • Say: "domestic supply extends" — not: "supply increases": the curve does not shift.
Mission 3

Worked example: counting a tariff's effects

Tariff of 2 per unit, Pw 4 → 6 (illustrative data):

  • The loss is the two triangles: ½ × 2 × 20 = 20 (production) + ½ × 2 × 20 = 20 (consumption).

Mission 3

Quotas

Same market (illustrative data): Pw 4, free-trade imports 60.

  • A quota of 20 units: once 20 units have come in, no more imports.
  • Supply in the home market = domestic supply + 20: draw S + quota, 20 units right of domestic supply, starting at Pw.
  • Price rises until demand meets S + quota: price 6, consumption 60, domestic output 40.
  • Same price and quantities as the tariff of 2, with one key difference:
    • the gap (6 − 4) × 20 = 40 is not government revenue.
    • It goes to whoever holds the import licences (domestic importers or foreign exporters): the quota rent.
    • If foreign exporters hold the licences, the 40 leaves the country: an extra loss to the home economy.
  • If demand grows, a quota lets the price rise further; a tariff lets imports grow at the same price.
Quick check
?A government replaces a tariff on imported shoes with an import quota. The quota is set so that the domestic price of shoes stays exactly the same. What changes?
Mission 3

Subsidies to domestic producers

Same market (illustrative data): Pw 4, imports 60. Subsidy of 2 per unit to domestic producers.

  • Domestic supply shifts right (down by 2): S → S + subsidy.
  • The price in the home market stays at Pw: consumers are unaffected (still buy 80).
  • Domestic output rises 20 → 40; imports fall 60 → 40.
  • Government pays 2 × 40 = 80: a cost to taxpayers (opportunity cost of other spending).
  • Harder to spot than a tariff, and it does not raise prices, but it must be paid for.
Mission 3

Embargoes, red tape and other barriers

  • Embargo: a total ban. Real case: in August 2014 Russia banned many food imports from the EU, USA and others.
  • Administrative barriers: product standards (safety, labelling, health) and regulations that imports must meet.
    • Real case: the EU bans beef from cattle given growth hormones, which shuts out much US and Canadian beef. The WTO ruled against the ban in 1998; the EU kept it.
  • Standards can protect health, safety and the environment. They become protectionism when they are designed to block imports rather than reduce real risks.
Quick check
?Country X requires all imported toys to be tested in its own laboratories, which takes six months. Domestic toys are tested within a week. There is no limit on numbers and no tax. Which tool of protection is this?
Mission 3

Real case: washing machines, 2018

  • In 2018 the USA put safeguard tariffs on imported large washing machines.
  • The tariff rate was higher once imports passed a set number of units each year.
  • Researchers (Flaaen, Hortaçsu and Tintelnot, 2020) found:
    • washer prices rose by nearly 12%
    • dryers, which were not taxed, rose by about the same amount: dryers are complements, often bought as a pair, and sellers raised both prices
  • Domestic brands raised their prices too. Counting washers and dryers together, buyers in the USA paid more than the tariff itself.
  • Lesson: a tariff's cost can spread into related markets.
Mission 3

Evaluate: how much harm does a tariff do?

It depends on…

  • Elasticities. The more price-elastic domestic demand and supply, the more imports fall and the larger the welfare-loss triangles.
  • Country size. A large importer can push the world price down, so foreign producers bear part of the tariff; a small country cannot.
  • Retaliation. Partners may answer with tariffs on this country's exports.
  • Time. Protected firms may invest and catch up, or grow complacent.
  • The tool. Quotas fix quantity, so rising demand pushes prices up; tariffs raise revenue; subsidies avoid higher prices but cost taxpayers.
  • Administration and evasion. Tariffs invite smuggling and lobbying for exemptions.
Mission check

?A small country puts a tariff on imported steel. It cannot affect the world price of steel. Which statement is correct?

In a small country, home buyers bear a tariff through higher prices; home firms and the government gain less than buyers lose.

Mission 4

What counts as aid

  • Aid: a voluntary transfer of resources to a developing country on better-than-market terms.
  • Humanitarian aid: short-term relief after a disaster or conflict: food, shelter, medicine.
  • Development aid: longer-term help to raise living standards: schools, clinics, roads, water.
  • Bilateral aid: from one government to another. Multilateral aid: through bodies such as the World Bank.
  • Forms: grants (never repaid), concessional (soft) loans (low interest, long repayment), technical assistance (experts, training).
  • Tied aid: must be spent on goods or services from the donor country.
Mission 4

Official development assistance (ODA)

  • ODA: aid from governments, given to promote the economic development and welfare of developing countries, as grants or concessional loans.
  • Measured by the OECD's Development Assistance Committee (DAC).
  • UN target: donors give 0.7% of their GNI as ODA.
  • 2024 (OECD, preliminary): DAC donors gave 212.1 billion US dollars, 0.33% of their combined GNI. Aid fell 7.1% in real terms on 2023.
  • Only 4 donors went above 0.7%: Denmark, Luxembourg, Norway and Sweden.
  • Illustrative data: a donor with GNI of 3,000 bn giving 0.3% gives 9 bn. At 0.7% it would give 21 bn: a gap of 12 bn.
Quick check
?After an earthquake, a donor government flies in tents and medicine. Separately, it lends a poor country money at 1% interest over 40 years to build a power line, on condition that the cables are bought from the donor's own firms. Which description is correct?
Mission 4

How aid could raise growth

  • Poor countries often face a savings gap: low incomes → low saving → low investment.
  • They may also face a foreign currency gap: too little export revenue to import machinery and fuel.
  • Aid can fill both gaps.
  • Short run: aid spent in the economy raises AD: AD1 → AD2.
  • Long run: aid spent on infrastructure, health and education raises productive capacity: LRAS shifts right.
  • Aid spent on consumption, or lost to corruption, may give only the short-run boost.

Mission 4

The case for and against aid

  • For:
    • fills the savings and foreign currency gaps
    • saves lives quickly in a disaster
    • funds merit goods (vaccination, schooling) that markets under-provide
  • Against:
    • dependency: governments may rely on aid rather than build a tax system
    • corruption and fungibility: aid for clinics can free other funds for less useful spending
    • tied aid can cost more than buying at world prices
    • food aid can lower prices for local farmers and cut their output
    • large inflows can push up the exchange rate, making exports less competitive
  • Economists disagree on aid's overall effect on growth. Evidence varies by country, period and type of aid.
Mission 4

Why external debt can become a trap

  • External debt: money owed to foreign lenders, often in a foreign currency such as US dollars.
  • Repayments need foreign currency, earned mainly from exports.
  • Worked example (illustrative data):

1. Debt: 2 bn US dollars. Exchange rate: 100 local units per dollar → debt = 200 bn local units.

2. The currency depreciates to 150 per dollar → debt = 2 × 150 = 300 bn local units: 50% more, with no new borrowing.

3. Yearly interest at 5% = 0.1 bn dollars: 10 bn local units before, 15 bn after.

  • Debt service: yearly interest plus repayments. Every dollar spent on it is a dollar not spent on health, education or imports of capital goods.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A country owes 500 million US dollars. Its currency falls from 10 to 12.5 local units per dollar. What happens to the debt measured in local currency?
Mission 4

Debt relief

  • Debt relief: cancelling, reducing or rescheduling a country's debt.
  • HIPC Initiative (Heavily Indebted Poor Countries), launched by the IMF and World Bank in 1996.
  • The Multilateral Debt Relief Initiative (MDRI), from 2005, added 100% relief on eligible debts to the IMF, World Bank and African Development Fund for countries completing HIPC.
  • Countries had to meet conditions, including a poverty reduction strategy. By February 2023, 36 countries had completed the process (IMF), most of them in Africa.
  • For: frees money for health, education and infrastructure; can restore access to credit.
  • Against: moral hazard (governments may borrow carelessly, expecting future relief); savings may not reach the poor; lenders may lend less in future.
  • Say: "debt relief cuts debt service payments" — not: "debt relief gives the country new money to spend".
Mission 4

The IMF and the World Bank

  • Both set up at Bretton Woods in 1944. Member countries own them; votes depend largely on each country's financial contribution (its quota at the IMF, its shares at the World Bank), so richer countries have more say.
  • IMF (International Monetary Fund):
    • watches members' economies and global financial stability (surveillance)
    • lends to countries with balance of payments problems, usually short to medium term
    • loans come with conditionality: e.g. cut the budget deficit, reform taxes or the exchange rate
  • World Bank:
    • finances long-term development projects: roads, power, water, schools
    • lends to middle-income countries (IBRD) and gives grants and very low-interest loans to the poorest countries (IDA)
  • Critics: in the 1980s and 1990s, structural adjustment conditions (spending cuts, privatisation, liberalisation) were blamed for hurting the poor. Supporters say they restored stability and growth. Conditions now place more weight on protecting social spending.
  • Other forums: the WTO sets and enforces trade rules; the G20 is a forum where the largest economies coordinate policy. Countries also sign bilateral trade agreements outside the WTO.
Quick check
?A country cannot pay for its imports. Its currency is falling fast and it has run out of foreign currency reserves. Which body is it most likely to turn to, and on what terms?
Mission 4

NGOs and remittances

  • NGOs (non-governmental organisations): charities and other non-profit groups, independent of governments.
    • Strengths: local knowledge; work directly with communities; can reach people a weak government cannot.
    • Limits: small scale; patchy coordination; can depend on donations that change with the news.
  • Remittances: money sent home by people working abroad.
    • 2023 (World Bank): about 656 billion US dollars went to low- and middle-income countries: more than FDI, and more than ODA, that year.
    • They go straight to households: they pay for food, school fees and small businesses.
    • But they can fall in a downturn abroad, and they do not directly build public infrastructure.
Quick check
?A government receives large aid grants for its health service. It then cuts its own health budget by the same amount and spends the money on new military aircraft. What problem does this show?
Mission 4Evaluate

Does aid help growth and development?

It depends on:

  • What it is spent on: infrastructure, health and education can shift LRAS; consumption gives a short-run boost at most.
  • Governance: corruption and weak institutions cut the share that reaches its target.
  • Type and terms: humanitarian vs development; tied vs untied; grants vs loans that add to debt.
  • Size and timing: large inflows can push up the exchange rate; aid that stops suddenly can stall projects.
  • Alternatives: trade access, FDI and remittances may matter more for some countries.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. Two countries each receive aid worth 3% of GDP a year for ten years. Country P spends it on rural roads and child vaccination, and audits show little is lost. In country Q, much of it is spent on imported consumer goods and some is lost to corruption. Which outcome is most likely?

Aid can help growth when it builds productive capacity and good institutions make sure it is used well.

Mission 5

Steps of economic integration

  • Countries can tie their economies together step by step.
  • Free trade area: no tariffs or quotas between members; each keeps its own tariffs on outsiders.
  • Customs union: free trade area + a common external tariff.
  • Common market: customs union + free movement of labour and capital.
  • Monetary union: members share a single currency, managed by one central bank that sets one monetary policy (one interest rate) for all.
  • No exchange rate between members, so no member can devalue against another.
  • Say: "a single currency and a single monetary policy" — not: "a single currency" alone.
Mission 5

The eurozone and the ECB

  • The euro began on 1 January 1999 (accounts and electronic payments); notes and coins followed in 2002.
  • In 2026, 21 of the 27 EU countries use the euro. Bulgaria joined most recently (2026).
  • The European Central Bank (ECB) sets monetary policy for the whole euro area. Its Governing Council includes the governors of every member's national central bank.
  • Primary objective: price stability, aiming for 2% inflation over the medium term.
  • One ECB interest rate applies in every member, whatever its own inflation or unemployment.
  • Fiscal policy (taxes, government spending) stays with each national government, within EU rules on deficits and debt.
Quick check
?Three neighbouring countries already have free trade, a common external tariff and free movement of workers and capital between them. Which extra step would turn them into a monetary union?
Mission 5

What a single currency gains

  • No transaction costs: no fees for changing currency when trading within the union.
  • No exchange-rate uncertainty within the union: firms can price and plan without currency risk.
  • So more trade and more investment (FDI) between members are likely.
  • Price transparency: prices in one currency are easy to compare, which increases competition.
  • Credibility: a country with a history of high inflation can "borrow" the low-inflation record of a strong central bank, so inflation expectations and interest rates may fall.
  • Illustrative data: a firm sells 10 million worth of goods a year to other members, and currency changing costs 1%. Single currency saves 10 m × 0.01 = 0.1 m a year.
Mission 5

What a member gives up

  • No independent monetary policy: the central bank sets one interest rate for the whole union.
  • No exchange rate of its own to fall when its exports become uncompetitive.
  • So it loses two tools for dealing with an asymmetric shock: a shock that hits one member but not others.
  • A rate that suits the average member may be too high for one in recession and too low for one that is booming: "one size fits all".
  • Fiscal rules limit how far a government can borrow to support demand.
  • Real data: in 2013, unemployment averaged 27.8% in Greece and 4.9% in Germany. Both had the same ECB interest rate.
  • Adjustment then falls on wages and prices: an internal devaluation, which is usually slow and painful.
Mission 5

Worked example: one interest rate, different real rates

  • Illustrative data. Union central bank rate = 2% for all members.
  • Real interest rate ≈ nominal rate − inflation rate.
  • Country X is booming; inflation 5%. Real rate ≈ 2 − 5 = −3%.
  • Country Y is in recession; inflation 1%. Real rate ≈ 2 − 1 = +1%.
  • Borrowing is cheapest in real terms where the economy is already overheating.
  • So one rate can make the boom bigger in X and the slump deeper in Y.
Mission 5Graph lab

An asymmetric shock

Illustrative data. Country B starts at full-employment output (500). World demand for its main export collapses: AD falls by 40. B is in a monetary union; its partners are unaffected.

  • Takeaway: outside the union, a fall in B's currency and interest rate cushions the shock. Inside, B must wait for internal devaluation.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. In a monetary union the central bank's interest rate is 1%. Inflation is 4% in Country P and 0% in Country Q. Which statement is correct?
Mission 5

When does sharing a currency work? The optimal currency area

  • Optimal currency area (Mundell, 1961): a region where the gains of one currency outweigh the costs of losing separate exchange rates.
  • Conditions that make a monetary union more likely to succeed:
    • similar economic cycles: shocks hit members at the same time (symmetric), so one interest rate suits them all
    • labour mobility: workers move from slumping to booming regions
    • flexible wages and prices: costs can fall quickly where demand is weak
    • fiscal transfers: a shared budget sends money to regions in recession
    • high trade between members: the savings on transaction costs and currency risk are larger
  • Compare: US states share one currency, with a large federal budget that pays more benefits and collects less tax in states with weak economies.
  • Each condition is a matter of degree. Economists disagree on how far the eurozone meets them.
Quick check
?Two countries are thinking of adopting a single currency. Which feature would make them most suitable for one?
Mission 5Evaluate

Is a single currency worth it?

  • It depends on:
    • how similar members' economies are: symmetric shocks make one interest rate suit everyone
    • how well other shock absorbers work: labour mobility, flexible wages, fiscal transfers
    • how much members trade with each other: bigger trade means bigger gains from one currency
    • credibility: a country with a record of high inflation may gain more than one with a trusted central bank
  • Time matters: gains (lower costs, more trade) build slowly; the cost of a bad shock can arrive suddenly.
Mission check

?Northland and Southland share one currency and one central bank. World demand for timber, Southland's main export, collapses. Northland sells no timber and is unaffected. Why might Southland's recession last longer than it would with its own floating currency?

Sharing a currency removes two shock absorbers, so a shock that hits one member alone must be absorbed by its wages, prices and jobs.

Mission 6

Why go global? Push and pull factors

  • Push factors (problems at home that push a firm abroad):
    • saturated markets: almost everyone who wants the product already has it
    • competition: rivals squeeze sales and margins at home
  • Pull factors (gains that pull a firm abroad):
    • economies of scale: more output, lower average cost
    • risk spreading: a slump in one country hurts less if sales come from many
  • Also: extending the product life cycle (selling a product in new markets as it declines at home) and raising capacity utilisation (using spare factory space).
Mission 6

Offshoring and outsourcing

  • Offshoring: moving a business activity to another country.
  • Outsourcing: paying another firm to carry out an activity instead of doing it in-house.
  • They can combine:
Done by the firm itselfDone by another firm
At homeIn-house, at homeOutsourcing at home
AbroadOffshoring (own factory abroad)Offshore outsourcing
  • Say: "offshoring is about where; outsourcing is about who" — not: "offshoring and outsourcing mean the same".
Quick check
?A UK clothing firm closes its Leicester factory and pays an independent factory in Vietnam to make its shirts. What has it done?
Mission 6

Which market should we sell in?

Factors in deciding whether to expand into a market:

  • Disposable income: its level and its growth — can buyers afford the product, now and soon?
  • Ease of doing business: how long and costly it is to register, get permits, enforce contracts.
  • Infrastructure: roads, ports, power, internet, to get goods to buyers.
  • Political stability: risk of sudden changes in law, tax or ownership.
  • Exchange rate: a weaker local currency makes the firm's imported product dearer for local buyers and shrinks profits once converted home.
  • Data on ease of doing business must be used with care: the World Bank stopped its Doing Business rankings in 2021 after data irregularities.
Mission 6

Where should we produce?

Factors in choosing a production site:

  • Costs of production: wages, rent, energy, transport to customers.
  • Skills and availability of the labour force.
  • Infrastructure and natural resources.
  • Location in a trade bloc: a factory inside a bloc sells to the whole bloc without tariffs.
  • Government incentives: grants, tax breaks.
  • Ease of doing business and political stability.
  • Likely return on investment: the summary test for the whole decision.
Mission 6

Worked example: cheap wages, cheap goods?

Labour cost per unit = wage per hour ÷ units made per hour (illustrative data).

  • UK factory: £18 an hour ÷ 24 units = £0.75 per unit.
  • Country X factory: £3 an hour ÷ 5 units = £0.60 per unit.
  • Add shipping and tariffs from Country X to UK buyers: £0.25 per unit → £0.85 per unit.
  • Wages in X are one-sixth of UK wages, yet each unit delivered costs £0.10 more than from the UK factory.
  • Say: "lower unit labour costs" — not: "lower wages" — when explaining a cost advantage.
Quick check
?A firm compares two sites for goods sold in the UK (illustrative data). UK: £18 an hour, 24 units an hour. Country Y: £4 an hour, 10 units an hour, plus £0.30 per unit to ship to the UK. Which is correct?
Mission 6

Global or local? Three approaches

  • Globalisation (of a product): one standard product and message sold worldwide; big economies of scale.
  • Glocalisation: "think global, act local": a global brand adapted to local tastes, laws and culture.
    • Example: McDonald's in India sells no beef burgers; its menu is built around chicken and vegetarian items.
  • Three approaches to global markets:
    • Domestic / ethnocentric: treat foreign markets like home; sell the home product unchanged.
    • International / polycentric: treat each country as different; adapt products and marketing to each.
    • Mixed / geocentric: one global strategy with local changes where they pay off.
  • Adapting costs money (lost economies of scale); not adapting can lose sales.
Mission 6

Culture, tastes and language

  • Cultural differences: religion, customs, attitudes to colour, gifts or family shape what sells.
  • Different tastes: sweetness, spice, portion size, pack size vary by country.
  • Language: brand names and slogans can carry unintended meanings or be inaccurately translated.
  • Firms respond by adapting price (to local incomes) and non-price features (product, packaging, promotion, brand image).
  • Adapting marketing for mass markets (one broad approach) or niche markets (a specialised group, e.g. expatriates or premium buyers).
Quick check
?A European chocolate maker sells the same recipe in every country, but uses a different pack size, brand slogan and price in each, set by a central team. Which approach is this closest to?
Mission 6

Ethical issues for multinationals

  • Stakeholder conflicts: shareholders want low costs; workers want fair pay; local communities want clean air.
  • Pay and working conditions in overseas factories and suppliers.
  • Environment: emissions and waste disposal.
    • 2015: Volkswagen admitted software in about 11 million diesel cars worldwide cheated emissions tests.
  • Supply chains: exploitation of labour and child labour.
    • 2013: the Rana Plaza building in Bangladesh, which housed garment factories supplying global brands, collapsed, killing 1,134 people. Brands and unions then signed a legally binding safety Accord.
  • Marketing: misleading labelling, inappropriate promotion.
    • Promotion of formula milk in poorer countries led to a boycott of Nestlé from 1977 and a WHO code on marketing breast-milk substitutes in 1981.
Mission 6Evaluate

Should a firm move production abroad?

  • It depends on unit costs, not wages: low wages with low productivity, high shipping costs or tariffs may not save money.
  • It depends on risk: long supply chains can break (political instability, shipping delays, exchange-rate swings); some firms reshore for control and speed.
  • It depends on reputation: ethical failures in a supply chain can cost sales and lead to boycotts; checking suppliers costs money.
  • It depends on stakeholders: jobs lost at home vs jobs and incomes created abroad.
Mission check

?A UK furniture firm finds its home market saturated. It plans to sell flat-pack desks in Brazil, Japan and Kenya. Which plan best fits the factors in this mission?

Firms go global to escape saturated markets and cut unit costs, but they must still meet each country's buyers, and their own ethics, on local terms.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Check units, level or rate, period, source, and nominal or real before you use any number.
  • Rates change in percentage points; a percentage change divides by the old value. A falling rate above zero is still a rise.
  • An evaluation chain runs Point → Analysis → Evidence → Evaluation → Judgement; a policy reaching its target does not on its own prove it caused the change.

Mission 2

  • MNCs produce in more than one country; FDI buys control of productive assets abroad.
  • FDI brings control and production; portfolio investment brings money only and can leave fast.
  • Host countries can gain jobs, capital, technology and tax, but face profit outflows, footloose plants and weak standards.
  • Transfer pricing shifts profit to low-tax countries; control needs law, tax co-operation and pressure from buyers.

Mission 3

  • Free trade at a world price below the domestic price: consumption rises, domestic output falls, imports fill the gap.
  • A tariff or quota raises the domestic price: home producers gain, consumers lose more, and there is a deadweight loss. Tariff revenue goes to the government; quota rent goes to licence holders.
  • Subsidies, embargoes, administrative barriers and (for some) currency manipulation are other tools, each with its own winners and losers.

Mission 4

  • Aid: humanitarian or development; bilateral or multilateral; grants, soft loans, tied or untied. ODA target 0.7% of GNI.
  • Aid raises AD now; it raises LRAS only when spent on productive capacity. Debt in foreign currency gets heavier when the currency falls; debt relief cuts debt service.
  • IMF: balance of payments lending with conditions. World Bank: long-term development finance. NGOs and remittances also matter.

Mission 5

  • A monetary union shares one currency and one central bank setting one interest rate.
  • Gains: no transaction costs or currency risk within the union, more trade and investment, price transparency. Costs: no own interest rate or exchange rate to deal with asymmetric shocks.
  • It works best in an optimal currency area: similar cycles, mobile labour, flexible wages, fiscal transfers, high trade.

Mission 6

  • Push (saturation, competition) and pull (economies of scale, risk spreading) factors drive firms abroad; offshoring is about where, outsourcing about who.
  • Market choice depends on income, infrastructure, stability, ease of business and exchange rates; production choice depends on unit costs, skills, incentives and return.
  • Glocalisation and ethnocentric, polycentric and geocentric approaches trade economies of scale against local fit; ethics in pay, environment, supply chains and marketing carry real costs.
