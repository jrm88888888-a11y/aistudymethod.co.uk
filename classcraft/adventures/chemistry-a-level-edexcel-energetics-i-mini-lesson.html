Mission 1

Mission 1: When a cold pack goes cold, has the chemical inside lost energy or gained it?

Make a guess: When a cold pack goes cold, has the chemical inside lost energy or gained it?

Mission 1

System and surroundings

  • The system: the reacting chemicals, reactants and products.
  • The surroundings: everything else, water, container, thermometer, air.
  • Energy moves between them; the total is conserved.
  • A thermometer in the solution reads the surroundings, not the chemicals.
Mission 1

Heat is not temperature

  • Heat: energy transferred because of a temperature difference, in joules.
  • Temperature: a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles (in kelvin, proportional to it).
  • A bath and a cup of water at 40 °C have the same temperature.
  • The bath can give out far more heat as it cools: many more particles.
Quick check
?Which statement correctly describes the temperature of a substance?
Mission 1

Exothermic and endothermic

  • Exothermic: energy passes from system to surroundings. Surroundings warm. ΔH negative.
  • Endothermic: energy passes from surroundings to system. Surroundings cool. ΔH positive.
  • ΔH describes the system. Always write its sign.
  • Enthalpy change, ΔH: the heat energy change measured at constant pressure.
  • A cold pack: the dissolving chemical gains energy from the water and your hand.
Mission 1

ΔH for an equation, and standard conditions

  • Enthalpy change of reaction, ΔrH: the enthalpy change for the amounts in a stated equation, in kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Standard conditions: 100 kPa and a stated temperature, usually 298 K.
  • Standard enthalpy change, ΔH⦵: measured under standard conditions, every substance in its standard state (its normal state under those conditions).
  • More on standard states and definitions in Mission 4.
Quick check
?A mixture of iron and sulfur must be heated strongly before it reacts. Once started, it glows red-hot with no further heating. What does this show?
Mission 1

Breaking takes in, making gives out

  • Breaking bonds takes in energy: endothermic.
  • Making bonds gives out energy: exothermic.
  • Bond making releases more than bond breaking takes in → exothermic overall. Less → endothermic.
  • The numbers come in Mission 5.
Mission 1

Enthalpy level diagrams

  • y-axis: enthalpy. Two levels: reactants and products, each labelled with formulae.
  • One vertical arrow, from reactants to products, labelled ΔH with its sign.
  • Products lower: exothermic, ΔH negative. Products higher: endothermic, ΔH positive.
  • An enthalpy level diagram does not show the activation energy.
Mission 1

Reaction profiles and activation energy

  • y-axis: enthalpy. x-axis: progress of reaction. Not time.
  • A hump between reactants and products: the energy barrier.
  • Activation energy, Ea: the minimum energy needed for reaction; arrow from the reactant level up to the top of the hump.
  • ΔH: from the reactant level to the product level.
  • Exothermic and endothermic reactions both have a barrier.
  • This is a reaction profile. A catalysed route has a lower hump; the reactant and product levels, and so ΔH, are unchanged.
Quick check
?An enthalpy profile for a reaction shows, relative to the same zero, the reactants at 150 kJ mol⁻¹, the top of the energy barrier at 280 kJ mol⁻¹ and the products at 40 kJ mol⁻¹. What is ΔH for the reaction?
Quick check
?Which sketch is a correctly labelled profile for an endothermic reaction?
Mission check

?Hydrated barium hydroxide and ammonium chloride, both solids, are mixed in a flask standing on a wet wooden block. Within a minute the water under the flask freezes, sticking the flask to the block. Which statement about the reacting chemicals is correct?

A cold pack works the same way. The chemical inside gains energy from the water and your hand, so they cool: endothermic, ΔH positive.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Where does half the energy go when you burn a fuel under a can of water?

Make a guess: Where does half the energy go when you burn a fuel under a can of water?

Mission 2

Measuring an enthalpy change

  • Solutions: react in a polystyrene cup with a lid, stirrer and thermometer.
  • Combustion: a spirit burner heats a known mass of water in a copper can.
  • Record the temperature before, then at regular intervals after.
  • Temperature rises: the process gave out energy, exothermic. Falls: it took energy in, endothermic.
  • Some ΔH values can't be measured in one step. Measure two reactions that can be, then combine them with Hess's law (see Mission 3).
Mission 2

q = mcΔT

  • q = mcΔT, with q in joules.
  • m: mass of the water or solution whose temperature changes, in g. Not the reactant's mass.
  • c: specific heat capacity; water 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ (given in questions).
  • ΔT: the temperature change. Same in K and °C; never add 273.
  • Dilute solutions: take density 1.00 g cm⁻³ and c of water.
Mission 2

From q to ΔH

  • ΔH = −q ÷ n. n: amount of the limiting reactant, in mol (for a 1 : 1 equation).
  • Divide q by 1000 to get kJ.
  • The minus sign: the surroundings gain q, so the system loses it.
  • Temperature rise → ΔH negative. Fall → ΔH positive.
  • Final answer: sign, value, units, e.g. −201 kJ mol⁻¹.
Quick check
?50.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ HCl(aq) is placed in a polystyrene cup and 25.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ NaOH(aq) is added. The temperature of the mixture rises by 4.50 °C. HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H2O(l). Assume that the solutions have a density of 1.00 g cm⁻³ and a specific heat capacity of 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹. What is ΔH for the reaction?
Mission 2

Worked example

  • Excess zinc powder is added to copper(II) sulfate solution: Zn(s) + CuSO4(aq) → ZnSO4(aq) + Cu(s).
  • 50.0 cm³ of 0.200 mol dm⁻³ CuSO4(aq).
  • 1. ΔT from the graph, corrected for heat loss (how: next screen): 31.00 − 21.40 = 9.60 °C (= 9.60 K).
  • 2. q = 50.0 × 4.18 × 9.60 = 2006 J = 2.006 kJ.
  • 3. n(CuSO4) = 0.0500 × 0.200 = 0.0100 mol. Zinc is in excess, so CuSO4 is limiting.
  • 4. ΔH = −2.006 ÷ 0.0100 = −201 kJ mol⁻¹.
Mission 2

Finding the true ΔT

  • Heat is exchanged with the air while the reaction is still going.
  • So the highest (or lowest) reading falls short of the true change.
  • Plot temperature against time. Extrapolate the line after the highest (or lowest) reading back to the time of mixing.
  • ΔT = extrapolated value − starting temperature.
Quick check
?Why is the line after the highest reading extrapolated back to the time of mixing?
Calculate
?1.00 g of iron powder is added to 50.0 g of copper(II) sulfate solution, an excess, in a polystyrene cup. Fe(s) + CuSO4(aq) → FeSO4(aq) + Cu(s). The corrected temperature rise is 12.5 °C. Calculate ΔH for the reaction.
  • Data: Ar Fe = 55.8; c = 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹. The CuSO4(aq) is 50.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³.
kJ mol⁻¹
Mission 2

Enthalpy change of neutralisation

  • ΔneutH⦵: the enthalpy change when 1 mol of water is formed by an acid and an alkali, under standard conditions.
  • Method: acid in the cup, record its temperature, add alkali, stir, record the highest temperature.
  • n = amount of water formed, not of acid or alkali.
  • Strong acid + strong alkali: nearly the same value every time. It is always H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H2O(l).
Quick check
?A spirit burner containing ethanol, C2H5OH, is used to heat 150.0 g of water in a copper can. Burning 0.920 g of ethanol raises the temperature of the water from 19.0 °C to 40.5 °C. Use c(water) = 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ and Ar values H 1.01, C 12.01, O 16.00. What value of ΔH for the combustion of ethanol do these results give?
Mission 2

Why the measured value falls short

  • Accepted value for ethanol: −1367 kJ mol⁻¹. The can gave −675: about half.
  • The missing energy was not destroyed. It heated the air and the can, not the water.
  • Some ethanol burns incompletely (soot on the can); some evaporates from the wick.
  • Assumptions: all the heat reaches the water; the apparatus absorbs none; solutions behave like water.
  • Reduce losses: draught shield, lid, insulation, burner close to the can.
  • Heat loss makes a measured exothermic ΔH less negative.
Mission check

?A student measures the enthalpy change when potassium chloride dissolves in water. The temperature falls. The student uses a thin glass beaker with no lid instead of an insulated cup. How does the student's value of ΔH compare with the accepted value?

Exchange with the room always shrinks the measured ΔT. Under the can, the "missing half" went into the air and the can, so the value fell short.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Carbon always burns partly to CO2. So how do we know ΔH for making carbon monoxide?

Make a guess: Carbon always burns partly to CO2. So how do we know ΔH for making carbon monoxide?

Mission 3

Hess's law

  • Hess's law: the enthalpy change of a reaction is independent of the route taken, provided the initial and final conditions are the same.
  • It follows from conservation of energy: no route can create or destroy energy.
  • So a ΔH you can't measure = the sum of ΔH values you can.
Mission 3

Three moves

  • Reverse an equation: change the sign of ΔH.
  • Multiply an equation: multiply ΔH by the same number.
  • Add equations: add their ΔH values. Species on both sides cancel.
  • Arrange the given equations so they add up to the target equation.
Mission 3

Worked example: carbon monoxide

  • Target: C(s) + ½O2(g) → CO(g).
  • (1) C(s) + O2(g) → CO2(g), ΔH = −393.5 kJ mol⁻¹ · (2) CO(g) + ½O2(g) → CO2(g), ΔH = −283.0 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Keep (1). Reverse (2): CO2(g) → CO(g) + ½O2(g), ΔH = +283.0.
  • Add: CO2 cancels, and ½O2 cancels from one side.
  • ΔH = −393.5 + 283.0 = −110.5 kJ mol⁻¹.
Quick check
?An exothermic reaction P → Q takes place in a single step. With a catalyst, the same reaction takes place by a different route of three steps with a lower activation energy, and is much faster. Both routes start from the same reactants and give the same products under the same conditions. How does ΔH of the catalysed reaction compare with ΔH of the uncatalysed reaction?
Mission 3

Drawing an enthalpy cycle

  • Write the target reaction along the top, with its arrow.
  • Below, write what both sides can be converted into (here, CO2).
  • Draw arrows for the known ΔH values, in the direction they are measured.
  • Go round the other way: with an arrow, add ΔH; against an arrow, subtract it.
  • CO: ΔH = ΔH1 − ΔH2 = −393.5 − (−283.0) = −110.5 kJ mol⁻¹.
Calculate
?Nitrogen monoxide from car exhausts is oxidised in the air: 2NO(g) + O2(g) → 2NO2(g). Calculate ΔH for this reaction from:
  • (1) ½N2(g) + ½O2(g) → NO(g), ΔH = +90.3 kJ mol⁻¹ · (2) ½N2(g) + O2(g) → NO2(g), ΔH = +33.2 kJ mol⁻¹. Answer in kJ mol⁻¹.
kJ mol⁻¹
Mission 3

Hess's law in the lab

  • Target: CuSO4(s) + 5H2O(l) → CuSO4·5H2O(s). You can't measure it in a cup: you can't add exactly 5H2O and stop at the solid.
  • Run 1: dissolve anhydrous CuSO4(s) in water. ΔH1 = −67.0 kJ mol⁻¹ (example result).
  • Run 2: dissolve CuSO4·5H2O(s) in water. ΔH2 = +11.0 kJ mol⁻¹ (example result).
  • Both end as the same solution, so ΔH = ΔH1 − ΔH2 = −67.0 − (+11.0) = −78.0 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Core practical 8 uses the same two-reaction method.
Quick check
?Use the enthalpy changes of reactions 1 and 2 to calculate ΔH for the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide: 2H2O2(l) → 2H2O(l) + O2(g). Reaction 1: H2(g) + O2(g) → H2O2(l), ΔH1 = −187.8 kJ mol⁻¹. Reaction 2: 2H2(g) + O2(g) → 2H2O(l), ΔH2 = −571.6 kJ mol⁻¹.
Mission check

?Graphite turns into diamond only at enormous pressures, so ΔH for C(s, graphite) → C(s, diamond) can't be measured directly. But both forms burn.
(1) C(s, graphite) + O2(g) → CO2(g), ΔH = −393.5 kJ mol⁻¹ · (2) C(s, diamond) + O2(g) → CO2(g), ΔH = −395.4 kJ mol⁻¹. What is ΔH for graphite → diamond?

Same trick as for CO. Combine reactions you can measure, and Hess's law gives the one you can't.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Carbon and hydrogen don't combine cleanly to make methane. So how does the data booklet know its enthalpy of formation?

Make a guess: Carbon and hydrogen don't combine cleanly to make methane. So how does the data booklet know its enthalpy of formation?

Mission 4

Standard conditions and standard states

  • ⦵ means standard conditions: 100 kPa and a stated temperature, usually 298 K. Solutions: 1 mol dm⁻³.
  • Standard state of a substance: its pure, normal form under those conditions.
  • Examples: C(s, graphite), O2(g), Br2(l), H2O(l), Na(s).
Mission 4

Standard enthalpy of formation

  • ΔfH⦵: the enthalpy change when 1 mol of a compound is formed from its elements, with all reactants and products in their standard states, under standard conditions.
  • An element in its standard state: ΔfH⦵ = 0.
  • Use fractions to make exactly 1 mol: 2Al(s) + 1½O2(g) → Al2O3(s).
  • Start from elements, never from gaseous atoms such as C(g) or H(g).
Quick check
?Which equation represents the standard enthalpy change of formation, ΔfH⦵, of methanol, CH3OH(l)?
Mission 4

Standard enthalpy of combustion

  • ΔcH⦵: the enthalpy change when 1 mol of a substance burns completely in oxygen, with all reactants and products in their standard states, under standard conditions.
  • Complete: carbon ends as CO2(g), hydrogen as H2O(l).
  • Always negative. Elements burn too: C(s) and H2(g) have values.
Mission 4

Other standard enthalpy changes

  • Enthalpy change of reaction, ΔrH⦵: for the amounts in the equation as written, under standard conditions.
  • Neutralisation: the enthalpy change when 1 mol of water forms from an acid and an alkali, under standard conditions. Method in Mission 2.
  • Strong acids are fully ionised, so strong acid + strong alkali gives nearly the same value each time.
  • A weak acid is only partly ionised. Energy goes into ionising it as it reacts, so ΔneutH is usually less exothermic.
Quick check
?Which equation represents the standard enthalpy change of combustion of ethanol?
Mission 4

Using formation data: limestone

  • Reactants and products can both be made from the same elements.
  • Hess's law then gives ΔH⦵ = ΣΔfH⦵(products) − ΣΔfH⦵(reactants).
  • Multiply each value by its coefficient. Elements in their standard states count as zero.
  • CaCO3(s) → CaO(s) + CO2(g)
  • ΔfH⦵ / kJ mol⁻¹: CaCO3(s) −1207.6, CaO(s) −634.9, CO2(g) −393.5.
  • ΔH⦵ = [−634.9 + (−393.5)] − [−1207.6] = −1028.4 + 1207.6 = +179.2 kJ mol⁻¹: endothermic.
Quick check
?Use the data to calculate the standard enthalpy change of combustion of ethane, C2H6(g). ΔfH⦵ / kJ mol⁻¹: C2H6(g) −84.0; CO2(g) −393.5; H2O(l) −285.8; H2O(g) −241.8.
Mission 4

Using combustion data

  • Reactants and products all burn to the same CO2 and H2O.
  • So the order flips: ΔH⦵ = ΣΔcH⦵(reactants) − ΣΔcH⦵(products).
  • Elements that burn, C and H2, are not zero here.
  • C2H4(g) + H2(g) → C2H6(g). ΔcH⦵ / kJ mol⁻¹: C2H4 −1411.0, H2 −285.8, C2H6 −1560.7.
  • ΔH⦵ = [−1411.0 + (−285.8)] − [−1560.7] = −136.1 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • This is how ΔfH⦵ of methane is found: burn C, H2 and CH4 separately.
Calculate
?Ethyne can be hydrogenated to ethane: C2H2(g) + 2H2(g) → C2H6(g). ΔcH⦵ / kJ mol⁻¹: C2H2(g) −1301.1; H2(g) −285.8; C2H6(g) −1560.7. Calculate ΔH⦵ for the reaction, in kJ mol⁻¹.
kJ mol⁻¹
Mission check

?Propane can't be made cleanly from carbon and hydrogen. These reactions can be measured in a calorimeter:
(1) C(s) + O2(g) → CO2(g), ΔH = −393.5 kJ mol⁻¹ · (2) H2(g) + ½O2(g) → H2O(l), ΔH = −285.8 kJ mol⁻¹ · (3) C3H8(g) + 5O2(g) → 3CO2(g) + 4H2O(l), ΔH = −2219.2 kJ mol⁻¹. What is ΔfH⦵ of propane, for 3C(s) + 4H2(g) → C3H8(g)?

That's how the booklet knows the enthalpy of formation of methane too. Carbon, hydrogen and methane are each burned separately, and Hess's law combines the three values.

Mission 5

Mission 5: If breaking bonds takes in energy, how can burning a fuel give energy out?

Make a guess: If breaking bonds takes in energy, how can burning a fuel give energy out?

Mission 5

Breaking takes in, forming gives out

  • Breaking a bond is endothermic: energy is absorbed.
  • Forming the same bond releases the same amount: exothermic.
  • Bonds never release energy as they break.
  • Exothermic overall: the bonds formed are stronger in total than those broken.
Mission 5

Bond enthalpy

  • Bond enthalpy: energy needed to break 1 mol of a bond, with all species gaseous. Always positive.
  • Strictly, bond enthalpy is for a bond in one particular molecule, e.g. H–Cl in HCl.
  • Mean bond enthalpy: the enthalpy change to break 1 mol of a given bond in gaseous molecules, averaged over many different compounds.
  • Larger value → stronger bond.
Mission 5

Worked example: burning methane

  • ΔH = Σ(bonds broken) − Σ(bonds formed). Draw the structures; count every bond.
  • CH4(g) + 2O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2O(g)
  • Values given / kJ mol⁻¹: C–H 414, O=O 498, C=O 804, O–H 463.
  • Broken: 4 C–H + 2 O=O = 1656 + 996 = 2652 kJ.
  • Formed: 2 C=O + 4 O–H = 1608 + 1852 = 3460 kJ.
  • ΔH = 2652 − 3460 = −808 kJ mol⁻¹. Forming gives out more than breaking takes in.
Quick check
?Why is the C–H bond enthalpy in a table of bond enthalpies described as an average value?
Calculate
?Calculate ΔH for N2(g) + 3H2(g) → 2NH3(g).
  • Bond enthalpies / kJ mol⁻¹: N≡N 945, H–H 436, N–H 391. Give ΔH in kJ mol⁻¹.
kJ mol⁻¹
Mission 5

Why it is only an estimate

  • Values are averages over many compounds. The real bond in your molecule may differ.
  • They apply only to gases. If a substance is liquid or solid, the energy to change state is left out.
  • Hess's law with ΔfH or ΔcH data uses measured values for the exact substances and states, so it is more accurate.
  • So a bond-enthalpy ΔH is an estimate.
  • Methane: −808 estimated; −890 kJ mol⁻¹ measured, with liquid water.
Quick check
?Using average bond enthalpies (C–H 414, O=O 498, C=O 804, O–H 463 kJ mol⁻¹), a student calculates ΔH = −808 kJ mol⁻¹ for CH4(g) + 2O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2O(g). The experimental enthalpy change for burning methane under standard conditions (100 kPa, 298 K) is −890 kJ mol⁻¹. Which statement explains why the experimental value is more exothermic than the calculated one?
Mission 5

Working backwards to a bond enthalpy

  • H2(g) + Br2(g) → 2HBr(g), ΔH = −103.5 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Given: H–H 436, Br–Br 193 kJ mol⁻¹. Find H–Br, x.
  • Broken − formed = ΔH: (436 + 193) − 2x = −103.5
  • 2x = 629 + 103.5 = 732.5
  • x = 366 kJ mol⁻¹. Two H–Br bonds form, so divide by 2.
Quick check
?CH4(g) + Cl2(g) → CH3Cl(g) + HCl(g), ΔH = −99 kJ mol⁻¹. Bond enthalpies / kJ mol⁻¹: C–H 413, Cl–Cl 243, H–Cl 432. What value for the C–Cl bond enthalpy do these data give?
Mission check

?Both these reactions are exothermic, by very different amounts: H2(g) + I2(g) → 2HI(g) and H2(g) + F2(g) → 2HF(g). Which statement explains why the fluorine reaction gives out far more energy?
  • Bond enthalpies / kJ mol⁻¹: H–H 436, I–I 151, F–F 158, H–I 299, H–F 568.

A fuel gives energy out for the same reason. Breaking its bonds takes energy in, but forming the strong bonds in CO2 and H2O gives out more.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Exothermic: system loses energy, surroundings warm, ΔH negative. Endothermic: system gains energy, surroundings cool, ΔH positive.
  • Profiles: ΔH from reactants to products; Ea from reactants to the top of the barrier.

Mission 2

  • q = mcΔT (m of the water or solution), then ΔH = −q ÷ n, with n for the limiting reactant.
  • Give ΔH with sign, in kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Correct for heat exchange by extrapolating the temperature–time graph to the time of mixing.

Mission 3

  • Hess's law: ΔH depends only on start and finish, so a hard-to-measure ΔH is the sum along another route.
  • Reverse → change the sign; multiply → multiply ΔH; add → add ΔH.
  • In a cycle: with an arrow add ΔH; against it subtract.

Mission 4

  • ΔfH⦵: 1 mol of compound from its elements in standard states (elements = 0). ΔH⦵ = ΣΔfH⦵(products) − ΣΔfH⦵(reactants).
  • ΔcH⦵: 1 mol burned completely; ΔH⦵ = ΣΔcH⦵(reactants) − ΣΔcH⦵(products). Standard: 100 kPa, usually 298 K.

Mission 5

  • ΔH = Σ(bonds broken) − Σ(bonds formed): exothermic when the bonds formed are stronger in total.
  • Mean (average) bond enthalpies give an estimate: values are averages, for gases only.
