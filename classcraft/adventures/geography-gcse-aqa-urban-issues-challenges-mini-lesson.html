Mission 1

Mission 1: In 1979 Shenzhen was what its government calls "a small backward border town". By 2024 it had 17.98 million residents. Where did they all come from?

Make a guess: In 1979 Shenzhen was what its government calls "a small backward border town". By 2024 it had 17.98 million residents. Where did they all come from?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA GCSE Geography (8035)

  • 3.2.1 global pattern of urban change
  • 3.2.1 urban trends in different parts of the world incl. HICs and LICs
  • 3.2.1 factors affecting the rate of urbanisation: migration (push–pull theory), natural increase
  • 3.2.1 the emergence of megacities
Mission 1

Urbanisation

  • Urbanisation: a rising share of a country's people living in towns and cities.
  • Urban growth: a rising number of urban people.
  • Cities grow in three ways:
    • natural increase: more births than deaths;
    • migration into the city;
    • reclassification: villages and towns grow big enough to be counted as part of a city.
Mission 1

The world since 1950

  • 1950: 20% of the world's 2.5 billion people lived in cities.
  • 2025: 45% of 8.2 billion lived in cities and 36% in towns.
  • Using each country's own definition of "urban", the world's urban share rose from 37.4% in 1974 to 57.6% in 2024.
Mission 1

Contrasting trends, 1974 to 2024

Urban share of the population, by income group:

  • High income: 69.2% → 80.1%
  • Upper-middle income: 28.8% → 67.8%
  • Lower-middle income: 22.9% → 41.6%
  • Low income: 18.6% → 34.7%
  • HICs are mostly urban already; LICs are still mostly rural but urbanising.
Mission 1

Different times, different speeds

  • High-income countries (HICs) urbanised first, with industrialisation. On the UN's new city definition, their city populations passed their rural populations by 1957.
  • LICs started later: on the UN definition they reached urban–rural parity only in 2017. (On national definitions, they are still mostly rural.)
  • Urban population growth in 2024:
    • low income 3.99% a year · lower-middle 2.27% · upper-middle 0.90% · high income 0.84%.
  • Fast growth comes from high birth rates and migration; slow growth where most people already live in cities.
Quick check
?From 1974 to 2024, the urban share rose by 10.9 percentage points in high-income countries but by 39.0 in upper-middle-income countries. Why the difference?
Mission 1

Why people move to cities

  • Push (rural): poverty, few jobs, mechanised farming, poor schools and clinics.
  • Pull (city): jobs, higher wages, education, health care, bright lights.
  • This is push–pull theory.
  • Natural increase: young people are the most likely to move to cities, so cities gain births as well as migrants.
Mission 1

Shenzhen

  • Established in March 1979; China's first special economic zone from August 1980.
  • 17.98 million permanent residents at the end of 2024.
  • Its government calls it "a young immigrant city": average age 32.5 at the seventh national census.
Mission 1

China: what drove city growth?

  • China's urban share (national definition): 20.6% in 1982 → 63.8% in 2020.
  • Of China's urban growth between the 2010 and 2020 censuses:
    • 56% rural-to-urban migration
    • 26% administrative changes (reclassification)
    • 18% natural increase.
  • Worldwide, natural increase and reclassification drive most city growth; migration matters most in Eastern and South-Eastern Asia and sub-Saharan Africa.
Quick check
?Use the figures earlier in this mission. What share of China's urban growth in 2010–2020 did not come from rural-to-urban migration?
Mission 1

Megacities

  • Megacity: a city of 10 million people or more.
  • 8 megacities in 1975 → 33 in 2025 → 37 projected by 2050.
  • 2025: 19 in Asia (India 5, China 4), 5 in Latin America, 4 in Africa, 3 in Europe, 2 in Northern America.
  • Largest in 2025: Jakarta (41.9 million), Dhaka (36.6 million), Tokyo (33.4 million).
  • They emerge where big countries urbanise fast: "Most megacities are located in the global South".
Mission check

?Between 1975 and 2025, Ghana's total population grew 3.5 times, but its city population grew 13.5 times, from just under 2 million to 27 million. Where did most of the extra city dwellers come from?

When cities grow much faster than the whole country, people must be moving in from rural areas and towns. In China, 56% of urban growth in 2010–2020 came from rural-to-urban migration; Shenzhen calls itself "a young immigrant city".

Mission 2

Mission 2: Mumbai keeps drawing people in from across India. Who really gains from its growth?

Make a guess: Mumbai keeps drawing people in from across India. Who really gains from its growth?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA GCSE Geography (8035)

  • 3.2.1 LIC/NEE city: location and importance regionally, nationally, internationally
  • 3.2.1 causes of growth (natural increase, migration)
  • 3.2.1 social and economic opportunities created by urban growth
Mission 2

Where is Mumbai?

  • Site: on India's west coast, on the Arabian Sea; built on a group of former islands, with a sheltered natural harbour.
  • Situation: capital of Maharashtra state; India's main gateway to the Arabian Sea and the Gulf.
  • India is lower-middle income.
  • India is an NEE (newly emerging economy).
Mission 2

Why Mumbai matters

  • Global: a world city at the Alpha level, linked to firms worldwide (GaWC, 2024). In the 19th century it became "a global trading centre".
  • National: home of India's central bank; the Reserve Bank's central office "permanently moved to Mumbai in 1937".
  • Regional: the main centre for jobs, ports, trade and services in Maharashtra and the wider city-region.
Mission 2

How big, and how fast?

  • UN, same boundary method each year:
    • 2000: 16.0 million · 2025: 20.2 million · 2050 (projected): 23.1 million.
    • 12th-largest city in the world in 2025; growth 0.94% a year (2000–25).
  • Census 2011, Greater Mumbai's municipal area: 12,442,373 people.
  • Different boundaries, different totals: always name the source.
  • It is the world's densest city of a million or more: 27,000 people per km².
Mission 2

Why it grows

  • Natural increase: births exceed deaths in a young population.
  • Rural–urban migration from across India:
    • Push: low farm incomes, few jobs, poor services in rural areas.
    • Pull: jobs, wages, schools, hospitals in the city.
  • Since the 1960s M/East ward "has given shelter to migrants from different parts of India".
Mission 2

A clue in the census

  • Greater Mumbai, 2011: 6,715,931 males and 5,726,442 females.
  • That is about 853 females per 1,000 males (our calculation).
  • Many migrants are young men who come alone to work, sending money home.
Quick check
?A young man leaves his village in Bihar because farm work pays too little, and takes a job on a Mumbai building site. Which factors are at work?
Mission 2

Opportunities

  • Economic: jobs in finance, trade, ports, film, manufacturing and services; urban industry stimulates growth across Maharashtra.
  • Social: more schools and hospitals than in most rural areas. In 2011, about 90% of Greater Mumbai's people aged 7+ could read and write (calculated from census).
  • Resources: piped water and electricity reach far more homes than in villages.
  • For migrants, wages sent home help rural families.
Mission check

?Who gains from Mumbai's growth? Which answer gives one economic and one social opportunity?

Migrants gain jobs and wages, their families gain from money sent home, and the city gains as a national and global hub. But growth this fast has a price. The next mission shows who pays it.

Mission 3

Mission 3: In 2011, 1 in 10 people in Mumbai's D ward lived in slums. In M/East ward, more than 3 in 4 did. Why such a gap inside one city?

Make a guess: In 2011, 1 in 10 people in Mumbai's D ward lived in slums. In M/East ward, more than 3 in 4 did. Why such a gap inside one city?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA GCSE Geography (8035)

  • 3.2.1 LIC/NEE city case study: challenges (slums, water/sanitation/energy, services, unemployment, crime, environmental issues)
  • 3.2.1 example of urban planning improving quality of life for the urban poor
Mission 3

Mumbai: a fast-growing city

  • Mumbai is on India's west coast, on the Arabian Sea.
  • Census 2011: 12,442,373 people in Greater Mumbai.
  • The UN, counting the whole built-up city, gives 15.95 million in 2000 and 20.20 million in 2025: a rise of about 4.25 million in 25 years (our calculation).
  • In 2025 it was the most densely populated city of a million or more in the world: about 27,000 people per km².
  • India is a newly emerging economy (NEE).
Mission 3

Why people move to Mumbai

  • Cities grow by natural increase (more births than deaths) and migration.
  • Push from rural areas: low farm incomes, few jobs, drought, poor services.
  • Pull to the city: jobs, wages, schools and hospitals.
  • Since the 1960s, M/East ward "has given shelter to migrants from different parts of India, as well as those who have been 'resettled' from slums in the inner recesses of the city".
Mission 3

Slums: unplanned housing

  • A slum is crowded housing that lacks basic services such as water, toilets and drains; much of it is built without permission.
  • Census 2011: 6,534,460 of Greater Mumbai's 12,442,373 people lived in slums: 52.5% (our calculation).
  • Praja Foundation, citing the same census, gives 41.84%.
  • AQA also uses squatter settlement: homes on land the residents do not own.
Mission 3

Where the slums are

  • Slums grow on land others do not want or cannot use: beside railways, on marshy or flood-prone ground, next to landfill, at the city edge.
  • D ward: 34,699 of 346,866 people in slums (10.0%).
  • M/East ward: 685,994 of 807,720 (84.9%).
  • M/East contains "Asia's second largest and oldest open land-fill".
  • Formal housing dominates where land is most valuable.
Quick check
?Two sources, both using the 2011 census, give Mumbai's slum share as 52.5% and 41.84%. What is the best explanation?
Mission 3

Life in M/East

Praja Foundation, citing a 2017 study by the charity Apnalaya:

  • The landfill still receives about 4,500 tonnes of garbage a day.
  • Infant mortality: 66 per 1,000 live births, against a national average of 41.
  • Average age at death: 39.
  • Air and groundwater are contaminated; basic amenities "are conspicuous by their absence".
  • Average family income in its slum clusters: Rs 7,802 a month.
Quick check
?Which M/East figure is the clearest sign of poor health and living conditions?
Mission 3

Informal work

  • Formal jobs: contract, regular pay, taxes paid, legal protection.
  • Informal jobs: no contract, sick pay or pension. Examples: street vending, waste picking, domestic work, small workshops, building-site day labour.
  • In 2024, informal jobs were 81.5% of all jobs in lower-middle-income countries such as India, against 12.9% in high-income countries.
  • Causes: migrants arrive faster than formal jobs are created; many lack qualifications; informal work is easy to start.
  • In M/East, "Every second male and six out of seven females are unemployed".
Quick check
?Which worker is in the informal sector?
Mission 3

Challenges of rapid growth

  • Housing: about half the city lived in slums at the 2011 census.
  • Water, sanitation and energy: in M/East, families won legal water and electricity connections only after organising; many homes share or lack toilets.
  • Waste and pollution: about 4,500 tonnes a day to M/East's open landfill; air and groundwater contaminated.
  • Services and transport: schools, clinics, roads and trains struggle to keep up with demand.
  • Jobs: high unemployment and informal work (jobs with no contract, sick pay or pension).
  • Crime: poverty and unemployment can increase some crime, but crime is not limited to slums.
Mission 3

Improving life for the urban poor

  • The UN calls for slums to be upgraded with basic services, "preventing displacement".
  • Planning: Maharashtra set up the Slum Rehabilitation Authority (SRA) in December 1995 as the planning authority for Mumbai's slum areas. Developers build flats given free to slum-dwellers, paid for by selling extra flats on the open market.
  • In M/East, citizen-led initiatives with the charity Apnalaya helped families get legal water connections, electricity connections, cooking gas and ration cards.
  • Mission 24: a civic initiative working with the city corporation (MCGM) to build basic amenities in M/East.
  • Evaluate: SRA flats give secure homes but depend on developer profit and can move people away from work; upgrading improves homes without moving people, but progress is street by street. M/East alone had 685,994 slum residents in 2011.
Quick check
?Which approach improves slum residents' lives while keeping them in their community?
Mission check

?In a made-up city, Ward A sits by the port and business district, where land is expensive. Ward B is at the edge, beside the city's landfill; people cleared from central slums were resettled there, and new migrants keep arriving. Which ward will have the higher slum share, and why?

Land values and history explain the gap: D ward 10.0% in slums, M/East 84.9%. Cheap marginal land beside a landfill, resettlement from inner-city slums and new migrants concentrated slums in M/East.

Mission 4

The UK's people

  • UK population: about 69.5 million (mid-2025).
  • Latest censuses: England 56.5 million and Wales 3.1 million (2021); Scotland 5.4 million (2022); Northern Ireland 1.9 million (2021).
  • London alone had 8.8 million: about 1 in 8 of the people counted (our calculation).
Mission 4

Dense and sparse

  • Population density = people per square kilometre (km²).
  • London: 5,598 per km², the most densely populated region.
  • England 434; Wales 150; South West England 239, the least dense English region.
  • Glasgow City: 3,555. Highland 9.2; Na h-Eileanan Siar (Outer Hebrides) 8.6, the lowest in Scotland.
  • Pattern: dense in the south-east, Midlands, north-west England and Scotland's Central Belt; sparse in upland Scotland, Wales and the Pennines.

Mission 4

An urban country

  • In England, an area is rural if it is outside settlements of more than 10,000 people.
  • In mid-2024, 17% of England's people lived in rural settlements.
  • So about 83% lived in urban areas (our calculation).
Mission 4

Physical factors

  • Uplands are often steep, wet and windy, with poor soils: few people live there.
  • The North Pennines has "a severe climate, with high rainfall, low temperatures and strong winds"; its population "is low, with only a few small market towns".
  • Lowlands, coasts and river estuaries are easier to farm, build on and trade from: most big cities grew there.
Mission 4

Human factors

  • Jobs and money: London had the highest GDP per head of any UK region in 2024, £72,201. Jobs draw people.
  • History: England and Wales grew fastest from 1801 to 1911, by 13.6% a decade on average, as industrial cities grew. Industry around the Tees estuary, for example, grew from "the abundance of mineral deposits" nearby.
  • Transport: ports, railways and motorways link the biggest cities.
Quick check
?Why is the North Pennines one of England's emptiest areas?
Mission check

?In Country Z, areas P and Q are both flat, low-lying and have the same mild climate. P is a port that has been a big city since the 1800s; Q is farmland far from main roads. Why is P far more densely populated?

The UK fits the same pattern: London is over 600 times as dense as the Highlands, because people cluster where jobs, transport and history meet on easy lowland, and stay sparse on harsh uplands.

Mission 5

Mission 5: London produces more per person than any other UK region. So why can so many Londoners not afford a home?

Make a guess: London produces more per person than any other UK region. So why can so many Londoners not afford a home?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA GCSE Geography (8035)

  • 3.2.1 location and importance in the UK and wider world
  • 3.2.1 impacts of national and international migration on growth and character
  • 3.2.1 opportunities (cultural mix, recreation, employment, integrated transport, urban greening)
  • 3.2.1 challenges (deprivation and inequality; dereliction, brownfield/greenfield building, waste disposal)
Mission 5

London: where and why it matters

  • The UK's capital, on the River Thames in south-east England. Population 8.80 million in 2021.
  • UK: the highest GDP per person of any UK region in 2024, £72,201: 2.4 times the North East's (our calculation).
  • World: one of only two Alpha++ world cities in the GaWC ranking, with New York; Heathrow is "Britain's aviation hub".
Mission 5

Growth: natural change and migration

  • London shrank to 6.61 million in 1981, then grew to 8.80 million by 2021. 2011–2021: up 7.7% (our calculation).
  • International migration: in 2021, 40.6% of Londoners were born outside the UK.
  • National migration: young adults move in for work and study; many families move out. 47.4% of Tower Hamlets residents were aged 20–39, against 26.3% across England (Census 2021).
  • In the year to mid-2025, London's population fell 0.29%.
Mission 5

How migration changed London's character

  • Ethnicity: 53.8% of Londoners were White in 2021, against 81.0% across England; 20.7% were Asian and 13.5% Black.
  • Neighbourhoods differ: in Tower Hamlets, 44.4% of residents were Asian; in Richmond upon Thames, 80.5% were White.
  • Language: London had the lowest share of people with English as their main language: 78.4%.
  • Migration brings new food, faith buildings, festivals, music and businesses to the city.
Quick check
?London's population fell 0.29% in the year to mid-2025, even though it had more births than deaths and more people arriving from abroad than leaving. What must explain the fall?
Mission 5

Opportunities

  • Cultural mix: 40.6% born abroad: food, music, festivals and faiths from around the world.
  • Recreation: the Olympic Park, museums, theatres and sport.
  • Employment: the City and Canary Wharf for finance; the Olympic Park's Legacy Area now has about 38,300 jobs.
  • Integrated transport: Tube, buses, trams and rail on one ticketing system; the Elizabeth line carries about 660,000 journeys a day.
  • Urban greening: new parks such as the Olympic Park, and Elephant Park, where nearly half the new development is public space.
Mission 5

Challenges: inequality and deprivation

  • Housing cost: an average London home cost 10.6 times average earnings in 2025, the highest of any region; the North East's ratio was 5.0.
  • In 19 of London's 33 boroughs, the ratio was above 12.
  • Deprivation is concentrated in "parts of east London".
  • Gaps between boroughs: in Tower Hamlets, 35.9% of households rent from a council or housing association; in Richmond upon Thames, 62.4% own their home.
Mission 5

Challenges: dereliction, brownfield and greenfield

  • Brownfield land has been built on before; greenfield land has not.
  • The Heygate estate (Southwark), completed in 1974 and housing about 3,000 people, was judged "structurally sound, but in need of complete refurbishment" and was demolished.
  • On its site, Elephant Park was approved in 2013 with 3,000 new homes; after renegotiation, the affordable share rose from 11% to 25%.
  • Building on brownfield land protects the green belt, but can push out the people who lived there.
Mission 5

Challenges: waste

  • In 2024/25 London sent only 28.8% of its council-collected waste to recycling: the lowest of any English region.
  • 65.2% was burned (incinerated), the highest share; only 0.1% went to landfill.
  • Recycling varies with "how heavily populated an area is, the kind of housing present": Tower Hamlets recycled 17.0% of household waste, Bromley 49.9%.
Quick check
?London has the UK's highest GDP per person, yet many Londoners struggle. Which statistic best shows why?
Mission check

?A fast-growing city has high wages and many new jobs, but homes cost more than ten times average earnings and some old estates are being demolished. Which outcome is most likely?

London produces £72,201 per person, the UK's highest, but an average home costs 10.6 times average earnings. High output does not reach everyone. Estates like the Heygate were replaced by new homes, only a quarter of them affordable.

Mission 6

Two kinds of building land

  • Brownfield site: land that has been built on before, e.g. old factories, gasworks, docks. It is often derelict (abandoned and decaying).
  • Greenfield site: land never built on, e.g. farmland or countryside, often at the edge of a city.
  • Dereliction: empty, run-down buildings and land, often left when industries close.
Mission 6

London builds on brownfield

  • In England in 2021–22, 54% of new residential addresses were on previously developed land.
  • London had the highest share: 88%.
  • Only 2% of new addresses in England were in the Green Belt.
Mission 6

The Green Belt

  • A Green Belt is a ring of open land kept free of most building. Its aim: "to prevent urban sprawl by keeping land permanently open".
  • England's Green Belt covers about 12.5% of its land (31 March 2024).
  • London's Metropolitan Green Belt is the largest: 31.1% of England's Green Belt area.
  • One purpose is to "assist urban regeneration by encouraging the recycling of derelict and other urban land".
Quick check
?Why does London build most new homes on brownfield land?
Mission 6

Brownfield: for and against

  • For: brings derelict land back into use and regenerates run-down areas.
  • For: roads, pipes, schools and transport are already nearby.
  • For: protects the countryside from sprawl.
  • Against: old industry may leave contaminated ground that is costly to clean.
  • Against: sites can be small and cramped, so homes are built densely.
Mission 6

Greenfield: for and against

  • For: open land is easier and quicker to build on: no demolition or clean-up.
  • For: room for larger homes, gardens and new roads.
  • Against: loses farmland and habitats. Worldwide, about 60% of land turned urban since 1970 was productive farmland.
  • Against: new estates far from jobs mean more car journeys, adding to sprawl.
Quick check
?Which is an advantage of a greenfield site for a developer?
Mission 6

The rural–urban fringe

  • The rural–urban fringe is where the city meets the countryside.
  • It attracts land uses that need space and road access: housing estates, retail parks, business and science parks, industrial estates.
  • Example: in 1970 Trinity College turned farmland outside Cambridge into the UK's first science park. It now covers 152 acres.
Mission 6

Sprawl and commuter settlements

  • Urban sprawl: the city spreading outwards onto greenfield land.
  • Commuter settlements: towns and villages beyond the city where people live but travel in to work.
  • Growth beyond the city edge is not always on fields: Ebbsfleet Garden City in north Kent is planned for up to 15,000 homes on previously used, brownfield land.
  • Impacts: busier roads and stations, rising house prices for local people, and quiet villages during the working day.
Quick check
?Which change is most typical of the rural–urban fringe?
Mission check

?A city with a green belt needs 2,000 new homes. It could use a derelict gasworks near the centre or farmland at its edge. What is the strongest argument for the gasworks?

London put 88% of new homes on previously developed land in 2021–22, because the Green Belt holds back sprawl. Brownfield reuse regenerates derelict land but can be costly; greenfield building is easier but loses countryside.

AQA GCSE Geography (8035)

Mission 7

Urban regeneration

  • Urban regeneration: improving a run-down urban area: new or improved homes, jobs, transport and environment.
  • Reasons: poor-quality housing, derelict land, few jobs, crime or a poor reputation, and rising land values nearby.
  • Decided by councils, developers and landowners, with residents consulted or not.
Mission 7

The Heygate estate, London

  • A council estate at Elephant and Castle in Southwark, completed in 1974, originally housing about 3,000 people.
  • 1,212 homes: 1,020 council-owned and 192 privately owned.
  • A late-1990s survey found the buildings "structurally sound, but in need of complete refurbishment".
  • In a 1998 consultation, 96% of responses favoured a major regeneration scheme.
Mission 7

Why regenerate? Two views

  • Southwark Council: "the combination of housing management challenges and regeneration opportunities" justified demolition.
  • Some residents: "releasing the very significant value of the land was the key factor".
  • The redevelopment "has been the subject of a two-decade long controversy".
Quick check
?A survey found the Heygate buildings "structurally sound, but in need of complete refurbishment". What does this suggest?
Mission 7

Main features: Elephant Park

  • Demolition of the Heygate was completed in July 2014.
  • The plan approved in January 2013 included 3,000 new homes, 50 retail spaces, offices and public spaces, and a new energy centre.
  • "Nearly half of the new development is public space, including one of central London's largest new parks".
  • Mature trees on the estate were kept where possible.
Mission 7

Housing: who gained?

  • In 2005, all 595 remaining council tenants were given top priority to move; 99% chose to stay in Southwark, and all were offered the right to return.
  • Leaseholders were offered compensation under the statutory code.
  • After the 2008 financial crisis, contracts were renegotiated in 2010: affordable housing rose from 11% to 25%.
  • Across London, 50 estates were regenerated in a decade: total homes almost doubled, but there was a net loss of some 8,000 social rented homes.
Quick check
?If 25% of Elephant Park's 3,000 new homes were affordable, how many is that, and how does it compare with the 1,020 council homes on the Heygate?
Mission 7

Employment

  • New jobs: 50 retail spaces and office space in the plan.
  • In 2016 the developer, Lendlease, opened a Construction Skills Centre at Elephant Park. It "aims to train 1,000 local residents annually".
  • Southwark Council says it "has already helped nearly a thousand people find work on the construction site".
  • Building jobs end when building ends; shop and office jobs last longer.
Mission 7

A contrast: Otodo-Gbame, Lagos

Amnesty International reports:

  • Over 30,000 residents of this waterfront community were forcibly evicted in three operations between November 2016 and April 2017.
  • All 75 evictees it interviewed said they had received no prior written notice.
  • The evictions went ahead despite court orders telling the state to wait.
  • A luxury housing estate is being built on part of the land and water area.
  • The government gave varying reasons, from denying responsibility to preventing crime.
Mission 7

So who decides?

  • Power over land: governments, landowners and developers make the key decisions.
  • Residents' influence depends on protections: secure tenure, rehousing rights, compensation, court orders that are obeyed.
  • Resident ballots: since 18 July 2018, landlords seeking Greater London Authority (GLA) funding for estate regeneration that demolishes social homes must win a residents' ballot.
Quick check
?What is the strongest contrast between the Heygate and Otodo-Gbame?
Mission check

?In a made-up city, a council and a developer plan to replace an old market and rented flats beside a new station. The tenants have short leases and there is no ballot. Who is most likely to decide what replaces the neighbourhood?

Power over land decides. At the Heygate, the council redeveloped amid two decades of controversy, with rehousing priority for tenants; in Lagos, Amnesty reports evictions of over 30,000 people without notice. Since 2018, London requires resident ballots for GLA-funded schemes that demolish social homes.

Mission 8

Mission 8: Urban areas use 75% of the world's energy. Vauban, a whole eco-district in Germany, is home to about 5,300 people. Can eco-cities and smart cities make a real difference?

Make a guess: Urban areas use 75% of the world's energy. Vauban, a whole eco-district in Germany, is home to about 5,300 people. Can eco-cities and smart cities make a real difference?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA GCSE Geography (8035)

  • 3.2.1 features of sustainable urban living: water and energy conservation, waste recycling, green space
Mission 8

Why city living matters

  • Urban areas use 75% of the world's energy and produce 75% of its greenhouse gas emissions (UN, citing the European Commission).
  • Sustainable urban living: meeting people's needs now without harming future generations.
  • Four features: water conservation, energy conservation, waste recycling, green space.
Mission 8

The ecological footprint

  • Ecological footprint: the area of productive land and sea a population needs to produce what it uses and to absorb its waste, especially carbon emissions.
  • It is measured in global hectares (gha).
  • A city's footprint is far bigger than the city itself: its food, energy and goods come from elsewhere.
  • Lower footprint per person = more sustainable living.
Mission 8

Vauban, Freiburg (Germany)

  • Built on a former French army barracks, left empty when the forces departed in 1992.
  • About 41 ha, with about 5,267 residents (December 2020).
  • A tram line has served the district since April 2006.
Mission 8

Energy conservation

  • Low-energy building is compulsory in Vauban (65 kWh per m²).
  • 277 homes are built to passive-house standard: at most 15 kWh per m² a year.
  • In the Solar Settlement's plus-energy houses, more energy is produced than used.
  • A woodchip-fired combined heat and power plant supplies heat, and electricity for about 700 households.
Mission 8

Water conservation

  • Vauban has no rainwater sewers: rain runs in open channels to trenches where it soaks into the ground.
  • Some builders also collect rainwater in tanks (cisterns) and reuse it.
  • Keeping rain on site refills groundwater and protects places downstream from floods.
  • Other measures in many cities: water meters, low-flush toilets, rainwater harvesting, reusing "grey" water from sinks and showers.
Mission 8

Fewer cars

  • Vauban is "not a car-free but a car-reduced district" (translated).
  • No public parking on residential streets: car owners must buy a space in one of two district garages, about 470 spaces.
  • In December 2020 there were 1,132 private cars for about 5,267 residents.
Quick check
?Use the figures earlier in this mission. How many cars were there per 1,000 Vauban residents?
Mission 8

Waste recycling and green space

  • Waste recycling: separate bins for paper, glass, plastics and food; composting; reuse; less sent to landfill.
  • Green space: parks, trees, green roofs and gardens cool the city, store floodwater, support wildlife and improve health.
  • Build on brownfield (used) land first: about 60% of land turned into urban space worldwide since 1970 was productive farmland.
Quick check
?Which Vauban feature is an example of water conservation?
Mission 8

Eco-cities, smart cities and retrofitting

  • Eco-city or eco-district: a whole area designed for a low footprint, like Vauban.
  • Smart city: uses digital technology and data to run services such as traffic and energy.
  • Retrofitting: adding these ideas to an existing city, where most people already live.
Mission check

?A made-up city of 3 million people builds one new eco-district for 5,000 people, with low-energy homes and few cars. What difference does this make?

Eco-districts like Vauban (about 215 cars per 1,000 residents, compulsory low-energy homes) show what works, but they house few people. Real difference comes when their ideas are retrofitted to existing cities, where most people live.

Mission 9

Mission 9: In 2003 London began charging drivers to enter its centre. By 2007 traffic was still lower, but congestion was back where it started. How?

Make a guess: In 2003 London began charging drivers to enter its centre. By 2007 traffic was still lower, but congestion was back where it started. How?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA GCSE Geography (8035)

  • 3.2.1 how urban transport strategies reduce traffic congestion
Mission 9

What is congestion?

  • Congestion: extra journey time caused by too much traffic for the road space.
  • Transport for London (TfL) measures excess delay: minutes per km above the free-flow time at night.
  • Congestion depends on traffic and on road capacity (the space available).
Mission 9

Why inner cities jam

  • Many journeys head for the centre at the same time: the morning and evening peaks.
  • Old inner-city streets are narrow; parked cars, deliveries and road works take space.
  • In London, TfL reported "a sustained rise in congestion in all parts of London during the years leading up to the pandemic".
Mission 9

Impacts: journey time and air quality

  • Journey time: London's average bus speed was 9.3 mph in 2023/24, 1% slower than the year before; TfL blamed "increasing traffic congestion" in part.
  • Air quality: road transport is the largest single source of fine particles (PM2.5) emitted within London, about 30%.
  • Stop-start traffic burns more fuel per km, so more pollution.
  • Over 600,000 Londoners live with a lung condition.
Quick check
?Traffic stays the same, but a lane is closed for road works. What happens to excess delay?
Mission 9

London's congestion charge

  • February 2003: a £5 daily charge to drive into central London.
  • July 2005: raised to £8. February 2007: the zone was extended westwards.
  • Aims: reduce congestion, improve buses, make car journey times more reliable, and help goods deliveries.
Mission 9

What happened?

  • Excess delay in the original zone (minutes per km) against a "representative" 2002 level of 2.3:
    • 2003: 1.6 · 2004: 1.6 · 2005: 1.8 · 2006: 2.1 · 2007: 2.3.
  • Traffic stayed lower: in 2007 there were 21% fewer vehicles with four or more wheels than in 2002.
Quick check
?By what percentage did excess delay fall from 2002 (2.3 minutes per km) to 2003 (1.6 minutes per km)?
Mission 9

Why the benefit faded

  • TfL: road space was given to "pedestrian, cyclist and bus priority measures" and to street improvement schemes.
  • Road works also rose sharply, mainly "infrastructure replacement by the utility companies".
  • So capacity for cars fell: less traffic, but less road to share.
  • Exam move: judge success by more than one measure: traffic (lasting fall) versus congestion (gain gone by 2007).
Mission 9

Other ways to beat congestion

  • Integrated transport: buses, trains, trams, cycling and walking linked by shared tickets and easy interchanges, so people can leave the car.
  • New rail capacity: the Elizabeth line opened in May 2022.
  • Bus and cycle lanes: give priority to space-efficient travel.
  • Ultra Low Emission Zone (ULEZ), mainly an air-quality scheme: from 8 April 2019 in central London, London-wide from 29 August 2023; older, more polluting vehicles pay a daily charge. In 2024 harmful roadside NO2 was estimated to be 27% lower across London than without it.
Quick check
?Which strategy works by giving drivers a good alternative to the car?
Mission check

?In a made-up city, a two-lane main road becomes one car lane plus a bus lane. Car traffic falls by a tenth, but car journeys now take longer than before. How?

Congestion is traffic relative to capacity. London's charge cut traffic for good (21% fewer four-wheeled vehicles in 2007), but road space went to buses, cyclists and pedestrians and road works rose, so by 2007 congestion was back to 2002 levels.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The world's urban share rose from 37% to 58% in 50 years.
  • HICs urbanised early and are now growing slowly; LICs and lower-middle-income countries are urbanising fastest, through migration and natural increase.
  • Megacities rose from 8 (1975) to 33 (2025), mostly in Asia.

Mission 2

  • Mumbai: west-coast port city, India's financial centre, an Alpha world city; 20.2 million (UN, 2025).
  • Growth: natural increase + rural–urban migration. Gains: jobs, services. The costs come in the next mission.

Mission 3

  • Mumbai grew fast through migration and natural increase. About half its people lived in slums in 2011, concentrated where land was cheap and marginal, as in M/East.
  • Rapid growth brings opportunities (jobs, services) and challenges (housing, water, waste, pollution, informal work). Upgrading with basic services, as in M/East, improves life without moving people.

Mission 4

  • About 69.5 million people; densest in London (5,598 per km²), the Midlands, northern English cities and Scotland's Central Belt; emptiest in the uplands (Highland 9.2 per km²).

Mission 5

  • London: the UK's capital and an Alpha++ world city; 8.80 million people (2021), 40.6% born abroad, with neighbourhoods that differ sharply.
  • Opportunities (jobs, culture, transport, parks) sit beside challenges (housing costs, deprivation in east London). London has the lowest recycling rate of any English region.

Mission 6

  • Brownfield = previously developed land; greenfield = never built on. Brownfield regenerates and protects countryside but can be costly; greenfield is easier but causes sprawl.
  • The rural–urban fringe attracts estates, retail parks and science parks; commuter settlements grow beyond the Green Belt.

Mission 7

  • Regeneration replaces run-down areas with new homes, jobs and spaces. The Heygate became Elephant Park: 3,000 homes, a large park and new shops, but fewer affordable homes than the council homes it replaced.
  • Those with power over land decide; residents' protections (tenure, rehousing rights, ballots) shape who benefits.

Mission 8

  • Sustainable urban living conserves water and energy, recycles waste and protects green space; Vauban does this through planning rules and design.

Mission 9

  • Congestion depends on traffic and road capacity. It lengthens journeys and worsens air quality.
  • London's congestion charge cut traffic, but congestion returned as road space was reallocated. Integrated transport, new rail lines and the ULEZ are further strategies.
