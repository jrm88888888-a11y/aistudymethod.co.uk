Mission 1

What is prayer?

  • Prayer: communicating with God. An old Christian definition calls it "the raising of the mind and heart to God" (St John Damascene, 8th century).
  • Christians pray in four main ways (ACTS): Adoration (praise), Confession (saying sorry), Thanksgiving, Supplication (asking, for yourself or others).
  • Set prayers, such as the Lord's Prayer (Matthew 6:9–13), are shared words. Informal (extempore) prayer is in a person's own words.
  • Prayer can be alone or together, spoken, sung or silent.
  • For believers it is an experience of God: a relationship, not only a request list.
Mission 1Read the source

Asking according to God's will

"This is the boldness which we have towards him, that if we ask anything according to his will, he listens to us. And if we know that he listens to us, whatever we ask, we know that we have the petitions which we have asked of him." (1 John 5:14–15, WEBBE)

  • The letter is written so that believers "may know that you have eternal life" (1 John 5:13, WEBBE).
  • Verses 16–17 (paraphrase): if you see a fellow believer committing a sin "not leading to death", pray for them, and God will give them life.

Q1 What condition does verse 14 attach to prayer being heard?

Show answer

Asking "according to his will".

Q2 Why might a Christian say verse 16 shows prayer is for others, not only for yourself?

Show answer

It asks believers to pray for a brother or sister who is sinning.

Mission 1

When prayer is answered

  • Jesus: "Ask, and it will be given you. Seek, and you will find." (Matthew 7:7, WEBBE)
  • Some prayers seem answered in the way the person expects: a recovery, a safe journey, a decision made clear.
  • To the believer this can feel like God acting personally: it strengthens faith, or begins it.
  • Others see God's answer in peace or strength to cope, even when events do not change.
  • Your spec's point: an answer that matches the request can lead someone to believe in God, because it seems too fitting to be chance.
Quick check
?According to 1 John 5:14–15, when can a Christian be confident that God hears a request?
Mission 1

When prayer seems unanswered

  • Many Christians say God answers every prayer: yes, no or not yet.
  • Jesus in Gethsemane: "Please remove this cup from me. However, not what I desire, but what you desire." (Mark 14:36, WEBBE) The cup was not removed.
  • Paul asked three times to be freed from a "thorn in the flesh"; the answer: "My grace is sufficient for you" (2 Corinthians 12:9, WEBBE).
  • Jesus teaches that God, like a good parent, gives "good things to those who ask him" (Matthew 7:11, WEBBE): not always the thing asked for.
  • When people do not know how to pray, "the Spirit himself makes intercession for us" (Romans 8:26, WEBBE).
Mission 1Diversity

Christians differ on what prayer does

  • Catholic and Orthodox: prayer is above all communion with God; saints are asked to pray for us; some healings are investigated and recognised (e.g. Lourdes).
  • Pentecostal and charismatic: expect God to heal and act today in answer to prayer (James 5:16).
  • Reformed (Calvinist): prayer does not change God's plan; God uses prayer as one of the ways his plan is carried out.
  • Liberal Protestant: prayer mainly changes the one who prays: their attitude, courage and love.
  • All agree prayer is not a slot machine: its heart is the relationship with God.
Mission 1

The atheist and Humanist challenge

  • Coincidence: with millions of prayers, some will match what happens by chance.
  • Confirmation bias: people remember the "hits" and forget the "misses".
  • Natural causes: many illnesses improve anyway; hope and calm can help recovery.
  • Tests: a large 2006 US study of prayer for heart-surgery patients found no benefit from being prayed for.
  • Unfairness: why would a loving God answer one person's prayer for a child's recovery and not another's? For some, unanswered prayer leads to loss of belief.
  • Humanists: quiet reflection and support from others can help, for natural reasons.
Quick check
?Joe prays for sunshine on five Saturdays. He says it worked twice, but has forgotten the three wet Saturdays. Which challenge to answered prayer fits Joe best?
Mission 1

Christian responses to the challenge

  • Not a test of God: Jesus refuses to put God to the test (Matthew 4:7); many Christians say an experiment cannot measure a relationship.
  • "No" is an answer: Gethsemane and Paul's thorn show faithful prayer that was not granted.
  • The answer may be strength: comfort, patience and courage are real changes.
  • Testimony: many believers report answers too specific to be chance.
  • Faith comes first: most Christians do not base belief on results; prayer is part of trusting God.
Mission 1

Hindu prayer

  • Hindus pray through puja (worship of the deity in the murti), japa (repeating a divine name) and mantras.
  • The Gayatri Mantra (Rig Veda 3.62.10), recited at dawn and dusk (paraphrase): "We meditate on the glorious light of the divine Savitr; may it inspire our minds."
  • Many Hindus see it as the greatest mantra: it asks not for things but for a mind awakened to the divine.
  • Answered prayer can deepen bhakti (loving devotion) to a chosen deity (ishta-devata) and lead to belief.
  • Unanswered prayer: many Hindus say outcomes follow karma; God gives what is truly good; the Gita teaches acting without clinging to results (Bhagavad Gita 2.47, paraphrase).
  • So for these Hindus, an unanswered request is no reason to give up belief: devotion is not a bargain.
Mission 1

Buddhist "prayers"

  • Most Buddhists do not pray to a creator God. "Prayer" means chanting, recitation and aspiration.
  • Om mani padme hum: the mantra of Avalokitesvara, the bodhisattva of compassion; recited and spun on prayer wheels, especially in Tibet.
  • Your spec prints it in short form as "Om padme hum".
  • Reasons Buddhists give: to develop metta (loving-kindness), to share merit with others (including the dead), to open the mind to awareness and calm.
  • Theravada: chanting recalls the Buddha's qualities; no one outside you can do the work (Dhammapada 165, paraphrase).
  • Mahayana: devotion to bodhisattvas; Pure Land Buddhists call on Amida Buddha ("Namu Amida Butsu") trusting his help.
  • Divergent: some Buddhists see such prayer as vital; others as a help for beginners, less important than meditation.
Quick check
?A Theravada Buddhist chants every morning: "May all beings be happy and free from suffering." What is the main purpose of this chant?
Mission 1Evaluate

"Answered prayer proves that God exists."

  • Reasons for: many detailed reports of answers; changed lives; prayer is experienced as a real relationship; 1 John 5:14–15 promises God listens.
  • Reasons against: coincidence and confirmation bias; natural recovery; tests show no effect; many prayers seem to go unanswered.
  • Another view: answered prayer may give the believer good reason to trust God, without proving anything to others.
  • Other traditions: for many Hindus and Buddhists the value of prayer lies in the change in the mind, not in proof.
  • Judge it by: Can a "no" ever count against God? Is personal experience enough? What would count as proof?
Mission check

?Ruth prays for her brother to get a job. He is turned down. A month later he finds a better job. Ruth's atheist friend says: "That was luck." Ruth replies: "God heard me. The first 'no' was part of a better answer." Which statement about this conversation is accurate?

The same event can be read as God's answer or as chance. For believers, prayer is a relationship with God, so a "no" is not the end of it.

Mission 2

Theist, atheist, agnostic

  • Theist: believes God exists.
  • Atheist: believes God does not exist, or has no belief in God.
  • Agnostic: thinks it is not possible, or not yet possible, to know whether God exists. Term coined by T. H. Huxley (1825–1895) in 1869.
  • Humanist: a non-religious person who lives by reason, empathy and concern for human flourishing; most Humanists are atheist or agnostic.
Mission 2

A religious upbringing

  • Christian: infant baptism (or dedication), prayers at home, church and Sunday school, Christmas and Easter, Christian schools, then confirmation: the young person's own commitment.
  • At baptism Catholic and many other parents promise to bring the child up in the faith.
  • Buddhist: visits to the vihara, offerings to monks, keeping the Five Precepts, meditation, festivals like Wesak. The aim is an understanding of reality, not belief in God.
  • Hindu: samskaras (life-cycle rites), home puja at the shrine, festivals, stories of Rama and Krishna, learning from a guru.
  • Sikh: a name chosen from the Guru Granth Sahib, gurdwara and langar, learning Gurmukhi and Gurbani; Amrit is taken later, by choice.
  • Why it may lead to belief: faith is learned from people you trust; practice becomes habit; a community supports it.
Mission 2Read the source

Teaching children

"Train up a child in the way he should go, and when he is old he will not depart from it." (Proverbs 22:6)

  • Christians read this as a duty to pass on faith, and a hope that it lasts. Parents are to "nurture them in the discipline and instruction of the Lord" without provoking them (Ephesians 6:4).
  • Sigalovada Sutta (Buddhist, paraphrase): parents should keep children from wrong, guide them to good, train them in a skill, help them find a good partner, and hand on the inheritance. Children support their parents and keep the family's good traditions.
  • Ramayan of Valmiki, Canto 19 (Hindu) is your spec's named source: Hindus point to the epic's princes, Rama and his brothers, raised in dharma, learning and respect for parents and gurus, as a model (paraphrase).
  • Guru Granth Sahib 1246 (Sikh) is your spec's named source: Sikhs teach that a child should be raised to remember God, work honestly and share with others (paraphrase).

Q1 What does Proverbs 22:6 say early training leads to?

Show answer

Lasting faith and values in later life.

Q2 Is the Sigalovada Sutta about believing in God?

Show answer

No: it is about duties, values and good conduct.

Mission 2

Upbringing: the debate

  • Non-religious arguments: children cannot yet judge; religion taught as certain fact is indoctrination.
  • Richard Dawkins (b. 1941), The God Delusion (2006): calling a child "a Christian child" is like calling a child "a Marxist child"; children are too young to have such views (paraphrase).
  • Strict or unanswered-questions upbringing can lead to rejecting God later.
  • Religious responses: all parents pass on values, including non-religious ones; faith is offered, not forced; young people make their own commitment (confirmation, bar/bat mitzvah, Amrit).
  • "There is no compulsion in religion." (Qur'an 2:256, Pickthall)
  • Buddhists say: test the teaching in your own experience, rather than believe because of tradition. Many Hindus say a religious upbringing is not required: every soul will find its way to the divine, in this life or another.
  • Humanists: bring children up to think for themselves and learn about many beliefs.

Mission 3

Personal God or ultimate reality?

  • Personal God: a being who creates, knows, loves, judges and answers prayer (Judaism, Christianity, Islam, Sikhism, many Hindus).
  • Ultimate reality: whatever is most real and final, the ground of everything. It need not be a person.
  • Non-theistic: a religion without a creator God at its centre, e.g. Buddhism.
  • Impersonal: ultimate reality is beyond personal qualities, e.g. nirguna Brahman in Hinduism.
  • Non-theistic does not mean non-spiritual: these traditions still have worship, ethics, and a final goal.
Mission 3

The Buddha and the creator question

  • The Buddha did not teach a creator God. He taught a path to end suffering (dukkha).
  • Asking who made the world does not help that goal.
  • The Poisoned Arrow (Cula-Malunkyovada Sutta): a man shot by a poisoned arrow refuses treatment until he knows who shot it, the bow, the feathers… He would die first. Pull out the arrow.
  • Some questions the Buddha left unanswered: is the world eternal? Is it infinite? What happens to an enlightened one after death? His Noble Silence: these questions do not lead to awakening.
Mission 3Read the source

The Devadaha Sutta and Dhammapada 188

  • Devadaha Sutta (paraphrase): Jain ascetics taught that pain could be burned away by harsh self-punishment. The Buddha asks: if beings' pleasure and pain were caused by a supreme creator, then these ascetics, who suffer so fiercely, must have been made by a cruel creator. Suffering comes from causes and conditions, above all craving, and is ended by the path, not by a creator's will.
  • Implications for Buddhist life: responsibility lies with each person; practice (ethics, meditation, wisdom) matters more than prayer to a creator; respect for devas without depending on them.

Q1 If a creator caused all pleasure and pain, what follows about the suffering ascetics, the Buddha argues?

Show answer

They must have been made by a cruel creator.

Q2 Where does suffering come from instead, and how is it ended?

Show answer

From causes and conditions, above all craving; it is ended by the path.

Mission 3

Gods, but not God: the devas

  • Devas: gods living in heavenly realms. Long-lived, powerful, happy.
  • But they are not creators, not all-powerful, and not eternal: they are still in samsara and will die and be reborn.
  • They cannot give liberation; they too need the Dhamma. The Buddha is called "teacher of gods and humans".
  • Personal God (Abrahamic) vs devas: eternal creator vs mortal beings within the cycle.
  • Śūraṅgama Sūtra, Section 9, Chapter 11 (paraphrase): the heavens of the gods are reached through virtue and meditation, but they are still levels of samsara, not liberation.
  • Wheel of Life: the realms held by Yama (death), with greed, hatred and ignorance at the hub; the Buddha points beyond it.
  • Theravada and Mahayana: the painted Wheel is used mostly in Tibetan (Mahayana/Vajrayana) Buddhism; many Theravada Buddhists teach the realms without it. Many modern Buddhists read the realms as states of mind as well as places.
Quick check
?How do the devas in Buddhism differ from God as Christians and Muslims understand God?
Mission 3

Brahman: with and without qualities

  • Brahman: Hindu ultimate reality: the one source, sustainer and goal of all.
  • Nirguna Brahman: "without qualities": beyond every description; impersonal. Upanishads describe it only as "not this, not this" (neti neti).
  • Saguna Brahman: "with qualities": Brahman known in personal forms, such as Vishnu, Shiva or Devi, who can be loved and worshipped.
  • Atman: the true self in each being. Many Hindus teach that atman and Brahman are one: "Tat twam asi" (That thou art), Chandogya Upanishad 6.8.7.
  • So many Hindus hold both: one impersonal reality, known through personal deities.
Mission 3

Shankara: beyond a personal God

  • Adi Shankara (traditionally 788–820 CE), teacher of Advaita Vedanta ("non-dual").
  • Only Brahman is ultimately real. The world of many separate things, and a personal Lord ruling it, belong to maya: how reality appears to the unenlightened.
  • Upadesa sahasri, Chapter 1 (paraphrase): a seeker asks the teacher how to escape samsara. The teacher shows that the true Self is not the body, caste or rituals but is Brahman itself; freedom comes through knowledge of this, not through actions.
  • Implications: knowledge (jnana yoga) and meditation; worship of deities is valued as a step, since they are saguna forms of Brahman.
  • Divergent: Ramanuja and Madhva, and many Vaishnavas today (e.g. ISKCON), teach that ultimate reality is personal: Vishnu or Krishna; loving devotion (bhakti) is the path.
Quick check
?A Hindu worships Krishna every day, yet says ultimate reality is beyond any form. Which idea makes sense of this?
Mission 3Evaluate

"Religion does not need a personal God."

  • Reasons for: Buddhism offers ethics, worship and a goal without a creator; some Hindus see personal deities as steps to an impersonal reality; avoids the problem of evil (no all-good creator to blame).
  • Also for: the Devadaha Sutta argument (a creator of all pain would be a cruel creator).
  • Reasons against: most religious people worship a personal God; love and prayer need someone to relate to; many Christians, Muslims and Jews: without God, morality and meaning have no final ground.
  • Also against: Hindu bhaktas following Ramanuja or Madhva turn to a personal, saving God.
  • Another view: "personal" and "impersonal" may describe two sides of one reality.
  • Judge it by: What makes something a religion? Does practice depend on belief in a creator? Which view better explains religious experience?
Mission check

?At a Buddhist temple open day, a visitor asks a monk: "So who created the universe?" The monk smiles and says that question will not help anyone end suffering. Which teaching is he following?

A religion can centre on liberation, Dhamma or Brahman rather than a personal creator.

Mission 4

Revelation and realisation

  • Revelation: God makes something known to people (scripture, prophets, visions).
  • Enlightenment: a person sees for themselves the truth about reality, through practice and insight.
  • "Buddha" means "awakened one": someone who has woken up to things as they really are.
  • Enlightenment ends ignorance; it is called knowing, not just believing.
  • In Hinduism and Sikhism the goal is liberation (moksha, mukti): freedom from rebirth, often with direct knowledge of the divine.
Mission 4

The Buddha's night of awakening

  • Siddhattha Gotama spent six years in extreme austerities: fasting until he was close to death. They brought no insight.
  • He remembered sitting as a child under a rose-apple tree, calm and joyful in the first jhana (meditative absorption). Could this be the way?
  • He ate food again (the Middle Way), sat under the Bodhi tree and passed through the four jhanas.
  • In the three watches of the night he knew: (1) his own past lives; (2) how beings die and are reborn according to their kamma; (3) the Four Noble Truths and the ending of the taints (craving, ignorance).
  • This account is in the Maha-Saccaka Sutta (Majjhima Nikaya 36).
Mission 4Read the source

Knowledge arose

The Buddha's words, in the Maha-Saccaka Sutta (MN 36, paraphrase): when my mind was concentrated, pure and steady, I directed it to knowing the ending of the taints. I knew as it really is: "This is suffering; this is its origin; this is its ending; this is the way." Ignorance was destroyed; knowledge arose; darkness was destroyed; light arose.

> "Without knowledge there is no meditation, without meditation there is no knowledge: he who has knowledge and meditation is near unto Nirvana." (Dhammapada 372, Müller)

Q1 What did the Buddha come to know in the third watch?

Show answer

The Four Noble Truths and the ending of the taints.

Q2 Why do Buddhists call this knowledge rather than faith?

Show answer

It is seen directly, "as it really is", not accepted from someone else.

Mission 4

The jhanas

  • Jhanas: deep states of meditative concentration.
  • Four form jhanas: (1) joy and happiness, with thought still present; (2) joy and happiness, thought falls away; (3) happiness with equanimity; (4) pure equanimity, beyond pleasure and pain.
  • Four formless jhanas: infinite space; infinite consciousness; nothingness; neither perception nor non-perception.
  • In the Ariyapariyesana Sutta (MN 26) the future Buddha learns the two highest formless states from two teachers, but leaves: they do not lead to nibbana (paraphrase).
  • MN 26:34–42 lists the jhanas and formless states, then the cessation of perception and feeling, where the monk, with taints destroyed by wisdom, has "blindfolded Mara" (paraphrase).
Quick check
?In MN 26 the future Buddha masters the formless states taught by his two teachers. Why does he leave them?
Mission 4

Theravada and Mahayana

  • Theravada: the goal is to become an arhat ("worthy one"), who has ended greed, hatred and delusion and will not be reborn.
  • The path: ethics, meditation and wisdom: seeing the three marks of existence: anicca (impermanence), dukkha (unsatisfactoriness), anatta (no fixed self).
  • Mahayana: the ideal is the bodhisattva, who seeks enlightenment in order to free all beings.
  • Many Mahayana Buddhists teach Buddha-nature: every being already has the potential to be a Buddha; enlightenment uncovers it.
  • Mahayana stresses sunyata (emptiness): nothing exists independently.
  • Both agree: enlightenment is nibbana (nirvana), the end of craving and suffering, not union with a creator God.
  • Today: the goal shapes daily practice: ethics, meditation, retreats and, for Mahayana Buddhists, the bodhisattva vow to help all beings.
Mission 4

Zen: kensho and satori

  • Zen: a Mahayana school of China and Japan.
  • Kensho: "seeing one's true nature": a flash of insight into Buddha-nature.
  • Satori: awakening; often used for a deeper, more lasting enlightenment.
  • Rinzai Zen uses koans (puzzles, e.g. "What is the sound of one hand clapping?") to break ordinary thinking. Soto Zen stresses zazen ("just sitting").
  • Many Zen teachers say kensho must be deepened by years of practice.

Mission 4

Sikhs: mukti by the Guru's grace

  • Sikh enlightenment is mukti: liberation, union with Waheguru ("Wonderful Lord"). A jivan mukt is liberated while still alive.
  • The barrier is haumai (ego); the way is naam simran (remembering God's name), seva and living as a gurmukh (God-centred person).
  • It comes by God's nadar (grace), not by human effort alone.
  • Vismad: wonder at God's creation, a numinous experience.
  • Non-religious examples: awe at a starry sky, a vast mountain or a newborn baby. Many Sikhs say such awe points to the Creator, so it can lead to belief in God.
  • Guru Granth Sahib 448 (Guru Ram Das, paraphrase): the arrow of the Lord's love has pierced my mind and body; through the Guru I have met the Lord.
  • Sikh tradition: Guru Nanak vanished into the River Vein for three days, met God and returned saying, "There is no Hindu, there is no Muslim" (janamsakhi tradition).
Mission 4

Non-religious explanations

  • Brain states: brain scans show meditation changes attention and emotion areas. Intense experiences may be brain events, nothing more.
  • Altered states: fasting, lack of sleep and long isolation can produce vivid experiences.
  • Illogical: atheists may say "knowing" by meditation cannot be tested; experiences of different faiths contradict each other.
  • Lying or mistaken: people may exaggerate, or misread a strong feeling as insight.
  • Humanists: value calm and mindfulness for their real benefits, without a supernatural or "ultimate" claim.
Mission 4

Religious responses

  • Buddhists: the Buddha told people to test teachings for themselves, not accept them on faith (Kalama Sutta, paraphrase). So enlightenment is a claim anyone can check by practice.
  • The Buddha himself rejected the extreme fasting that produces altered states (MN 36).
  • Many believers reply that brain activity during insight does not show the insight is false: seeing a tree also involves the brain.
  • Sikhs: mukti is shown in a transformed life of seva and humility, consistent with Gurbani.
  • Many traditions test claims by fruits: compassion, wisdom, freedom from greed.
Mission 4Evaluate

"Enlightenment gives real knowledge of ultimate reality."

  • Reasons for: direct seeing, not hearsay; practice can be repeated and taught; enlightened people show lasting compassion and calm; the Buddha invited testing.
  • Reasons against: cannot be checked from outside; brain-state explanations; experiences may be mistaken or exaggerated.
  • Problem of different ideas: Buddhists find no creator; Sikhs find a personal, loving Waheguru. Can both be knowledge?
  • Another view: traditions may describe one reality in different ways; or each tradition's own account may be the true one.
  • Judge it by: Is inner seeing a kind of knowledge? Do changed lives count as evidence? Do the differences weaken every claim?
Mission check

?Mei, a Zen Buddhist, says that during zazen she suddenly saw her true nature. Harpreet, a Sikh, says that through naam simran she felt the ego fall away and knew Waheguru's grace. Sam, an atheist, says both were having unusual brain states. Which statement is accurate?

Enlightenment is claimed as knowledge seen from the inside. Whether it shows ultimate reality, and which reality, is where traditions and critics disagree.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Prayer is communication with God (adoration, confession, thanksgiving, supplication); set and informal; for believers an experience of God.
  • Answered prayer can lead to belief; 1 John 5:14–15 ties it to God's will. Unanswered prayer is met with Gethsemane, Paul's thorn and "yes, no, not yet"; critics point to chance, bias and failed tests.
  • Hindus pray through puja, japa and the Gayatri Mantra (Rig Veda 3.62.10); Buddhists chant (Om mani padme hum) to grow metta, share merit and open the mind.

Mission 2

  • Theist, atheist, agnostic (Huxley, 1869), Humanist.
  • Religious upbringing may lead to belief (Proverbs 22:6); critics call it indoctrination; believers say faith is offered and later chosen.

Mission 3

  • Buddhism: the Buddha taught no creator God; the Poisoned Arrow: such questions do not help end suffering.
  • Devas are mortal, not creators; the Noble Silence.
  • Hinduism: Brahman is nirguna (beyond qualities) and saguna (personal forms).
  • Shankara's Advaita vs Ramanuja's and Madhva's personal God.

Mission 4

  • Enlightenment is seeing reality for oneself; the Buddha's awakening (Maha-Saccaka Sutta) came through the jhanas and insight into the Four Noble Truths. Theravada stresses the arhat; Mahayana the bodhisattva and Buddha-nature. Critics give brain-state and mistake explanations.
  • Jhanas are stages, not the goal; Zen speaks of kensho and satori.
  • Sikhs seek mukti, union with Waheguru by the Guru's grace.
