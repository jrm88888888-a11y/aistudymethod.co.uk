Mission 1

Mission 1: Why do some buyers barely react when a price jumps, while others switch away at once?

Make a guess: Why do some buyers barely react when a price jumps, while others switch away at once?

Mission 1

One ratio for "how strongly buyers react"

  • Price elasticity of demand (PED): how much quantity demanded responds to a change in price.
  • PED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price
  • % change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100.
  • Price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions, so PED is negative.
  • Judge the size (ignore the minus sign): bigger than 1 → elastic; smaller than 1 → inelastic.
Mission 1

Worked example: cinema tickets

  • Illustrative data: ticket price rises from 8 to 10. Tickets sold fall from 500 to 450 a week.
  • % change in price = (10 − 8) ÷ 8 × 100 = +25%
  • % change in quantity = (450 − 500) ÷ 500 × 100 = −10%
  • PED = −10 ÷ 25 = −0.4
  • Size 0.4 is less than 1 → demand is inelastic: quantity changes by a smaller % than price.
Quick check
?Illustrative data: a café cuts the price of a smoothie from 5 to 4. Daily sales rise from 200 to 260. What is the PED?

Mission 1

What makes demand elastic or inelastic?

  • Substitutes: many close substitutes → elastic. Buyers can switch.
  • Necessity or luxury: necessities (basic food, medicine) → inelastic.
  • Share of income: a small share of income (salt, matches) → inelastic.
  • Habit or addiction (cigarettes) → inelastic.
  • Time: buyers react more over a long period than straight away.
  • Brand or whole market: one brand of crisps has many close substitutes (other brands), so its demand is very elastic. If one brand puts up its price, its buyers can switch at once. Crisps as a whole have fewer close substitutes, so demand is less elastic.
Mission 1

Real evidence: US petrol

  • Real data (Hughes, Knittel and Sperling, 2008). Economists measured how US drivers reacted to petrol (gasoline) prices.
  • Short-run PED for petrol, 2001–2006: −0.034 to −0.077.
  • In 2001–2006, a 10% price rise cut petrol use by less than 1% in the short run.
  • For 1975–1980 they found −0.21 to −0.34. Inelastic in both periods, but even less elastic later.
  • Why so inelastic? For most journeys there is no close substitute for a car, and petrol is a necessity for many drivers.
Quick check
?Which of these is likely to have the most price elastic demand?
Mission 1

PED and total revenue

  • Total revenue (TR) = price × quantity sold. For buyers, the same amount is their total spending.
  • Inelastic demand: price rise → TR rises. Price fall → TR falls.
  • Elastic demand: price rise → TR falls. Price fall → TR rises.
  • Unitary elastic: TR stays the same.
  • Illustrative data: cinema tickets, price 8 → 10.

Quick check
?A mobile network finds that demand for its contracts has a PED of −2.5. It wants to raise its total revenue. What should it do to its price?
Mission 1

Why PED matters

  • Producers (firms): with inelastic demand, a firm can raise its price and raise revenue. With elastic demand, a price cut may win more revenue.
  • Consumers: when demand is inelastic (fuel, medicines), price rises are hard to avoid: buyers keep paying.
  • Government – taxes: a tax on a good with inelastic demand (tobacco, fuel) raises a lot of revenue, because sales fall little. It does less to cut use.
  • Government – subsidies: a subsidy that lowers the price raises consumption most when demand is elastic.
Quick check
?A government wants a new tax to raise as much revenue as possible with the smallest fall in sales. On which good should it put the tax?
Mission check

?Illustrative data: a bus company raises its fare from 2.00 to 2.20. Daily passengers fall from 10,000 to 9,700. Which statement is correct?

First turn both changes into percentages from the original values. Then the size of PED tells you which way total revenue moves.

Mission 2

Mission 2: When prices jump, why can some producers flood the market while others cannot grow more for years?

Make a guess: When prices jump, why can some producers flood the market while others cannot grow more for years?

Mission 2

What PES measures

  • Price elasticity of supply (PES): how strongly the quantity supplied responds to a change in price.
  • PES = % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price
  • Price up → firms supply more. So PES is normally a positive number.
  • Bigger number = stronger response.
  • Say: "the price rise caused an extension of supply" — not: "supply increased" (that means the whole curve shifts).
Mission 2

Five categories

PESNameWhat it means
0Perfectly inelasticQuantity does not change at all. Vertical curve
between 0 and 1InelasticQuantity changes by a smaller % than price
1Unitary elasticQuantity changes by the same % as price
greater than 1ElasticQuantity changes by a bigger % than price
infinitePerfectly elasticAny quantity at one price. Horizontal curve
  • The dividing line is 1.
Mission 2

Reading a straight supply curve

  • Look at where the line meets the axes, not just how steep it is.
  • Starts on the price axis → elastic (PES > 1) all along it.
  • Passes through the origin → unitary (PES = 1) all along it, however steep.
  • Starts on the quantity axis → inelastic (PES < 1) all along it.
Quick check
?A straight supply curve starts on the quantity axis, to the right of the origin, and slopes upwards. What can you say about its PES?
Mission 2

Worked example

  • Illustrative data. A strawberry farm sells punnets at £2.00. The price rises to £2.50.
  • Quantity supplied rises from 1,000 to 1,100 punnets a week.
  • % change in Qs = (1,100 − 1,000) ÷ 1,000 × 100 = +10%
  • % change in P = (2.50 − 2.00) ÷ 2.00 × 100 = +25%
  • PES = 10 ÷ 25 = 0.4 → inelastic.
  • Working backwards: PES = 1.5 and price rises 5% → % change in Qs = 1.5 × 5 = +7.5%. A firm supplying 800 now supplies 860.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. The price of a garden bench rises from £40 to £44. The quantity supplied rises from 500 to 600 a month. What is the PES?
Mission 2

What makes supply elastic?

  • Spare capacity: idle machines and staff → output can rise quickly.
  • Stocks: goods already made, or that can be stored, can be released fast.
  • Factors of production: if extra workers, machines and materials are easy to find, or can switch from other uses → more elastic.
  • Time: the longer firms have to respond, the more elastic supply becomes.
  • Production time: goods that take a long time to make or grow → less elastic.
Mission 2

Time changes everything

  • Very short run: output is fixed (e.g. fish already caught today). PES = 0.
  • Short run: firms use overtime, spare machines and stocks. Supply is inelastic.
  • Long run: firms build new capacity, and new firms join the market. Supply is more elastic.
  • So a rise in demand → short run: mostly a higher price; long run: more extra output.
Quick check
?Which change would make a firm's supply more price elastic?
Mission 2Graph lab

Same demand rise, different supply

  • Takeaway: the less elastic supply is, the more a rise in demand becomes a higher price, not more output.
Mission 2

Real world: cocoa, 2024

  • Cocoa beans (for chocolate) come mainly from West Africa.
  • Poor West African harvests in 2023–24 cut supply.
  • In March 2024, cocoa prices on the New York futures market (contracts to buy cocoa for later delivery) passed $10,000 a metric ton for the first time. Prices had roughly doubled since January.
  • Why could other growers not fill the gap? Newly planted cocoa trees take a few years to produce beans.
  • So in the short run, cocoa supply was very inelastic. Growers in other countries (e.g. Brazil, Ecuador) are working to expand output in the long run.
Mission 2

Primary vs manufactured products

  • Primary products (crops, metals, oil) usually have lower PES than manufactured products.
  • Primary: crops and trees take months or years to grow; land and mines are fixed in the short run; harvests depend on weather; many farm goods cannot be stored.
  • Manufactured: factories can add shifts, use spare machines and sell from stocks.
  • Exception: primary products that store well (grain, metals) can be released from stockpiles quickly.
  • Result: prices of primary products tend to swing more when demand changes.
Mission 2

Why PES matters

  • Producers: with elastic supply, a firm can sell much more when demand rises. Keeping stocks and spare capacity raises PES.
  • Consumers: when supply is inelastic, a rise in demand means a big price rise, with little extra to buy.
  • Government: a subsidy to raise output works best when supply is elastic. If supply is inelastic, output barely rises: most of the subsidy ends up as extra income for producers.
  • Taxes: a tax on producers cuts the quantity sold more when supply is elastic.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. The price of tomatoes rises by 20% and stays there. One week later, a greenhouse grower's quantity supplied is 4% higher than before. One year later, it is 30% higher than before. Which statement is correct?

The same product can have inelastic supply this week and elastic supply next year: time is the key factor.

Mission 3

Mission 3: When people get richer, why do they buy more of some things but less of others?

Make a guess: When people get richer, why do they buy more of some things but less of others?

Mission 3

What YED measures

  • Income elasticity of demand (YED): how strongly the quantity demanded responds to a change in income.
  • YED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in income
  • The price of the good stays the same. Income changes.
  • So a change in income shifts the demand curve. It is not a movement along it.
  • Say: "a rise in income shifted demand to the right" — not: "a rise in income caused an extension of demand".
Mission 3

Three kinds of goods

YEDType of goodWhen income rises…
positive (above 0)Normal gooddemand rises
greater than 1Luxury good (a normal good)demand rises by a bigger % than income
negative (below 0)Inferior gooddemand falls
  • The sign matters: + means normal, − means inferior.
  • The size matters: above 1 means luxury (income elastic).
  • A normal good with YED between 0 and 1 is often called a necessity: demand rises, but by a smaller % than income.
  • Inferior goods are usually cheaper versions that people switch away from as they get richer.
Mission 3

Worked example

  • Illustrative data. A household's income rises from £2,000 to £2,200 a month: +10%.
  • Restaurant meals: 8 → 10 a month = +25%. YED = 25 ÷ 10 = +2.5 → luxury.
  • Milk: 10 → 10.4 litres a week = +4%. YED = 4 ÷ 10 = +0.4 → normal (a necessity).
  • Bus journeys: 20 → 18 a month = −10%. YED = −10 ÷ 10 = −1 → inferior (the household now drives).
  • Always keep the sign: it tells you the type of good.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. Average incomes fall by 5%. Sales of a supermarket's cheapest own-brand pasta rise by 4%. What is the YED, and what type of good is it?
Mission 3Graph lab

Income rises: three markets

Three demand diagrams side by side: a luxury (holidays abroad), a necessity (milk), an inferior good (bus journeys).

  • Takeaway: income changes shift demand: right for normal goods (furthest for luxuries), left for inferior goods.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A study estimates YED for four goods. Which one is a luxury good?
Mission 3

Why YED matters to businesses

  • Planning output and stock: if incomes are expected to rise, sellers of luxury goods plan for much higher sales.
  • In a recession (incomes fall): sales of luxuries fall sharply; sales of inferior goods can rise.
  • Spreading risk: a firm that sells both luxury and inferior products (e.g. a premium range and a value range) is less hurt by booms and slumps.
  • Real pattern: as household income rises, the share of income spent on food falls, even though spending on food rises (Engel's law). Food is mostly a necessity.
Mission 3

Why YED matters to government

  • Tax revenue: as incomes grow, spending on luxuries grows faster than income. Taxes on luxuries raise more revenue over time.
  • In a recession: demand for inferior goods (e.g. cheap food, bus travel) may rise. The government may plan for this, e.g. when deciding on bus subsidies.
  • Income tax changes: a change in income tax changes people's disposable income. YED shows which markets will feel it most.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A country's incomes are forecast to fall next year. A firm sells three products: a luxury watch (YED +3), a family car (YED +1.2) and a budget mobile-phone plan (YED −0.5). What should it expect?
Mission check

?Illustrative data. A carmaker sells two models. Model X has YED = +2. Model Y has YED = −0.5. Average incomes are forecast to rise by 3%. Prices stay the same. What change in quantity demanded should the carmaker expect?

The sign of YED says which way demand moves when income changes; the size says how far.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Can a tax on sugary drinks make people healthier, or does it just raise money?

Make a guess: Can a tax on sugary drinks make people healthier, or does it just raise money?

Mission 4

Why governments step in

  • Market failure: too much of some goods (external costs), too little of others (external benefits).
  • This is the main reason for government intervention. Others: fairness, and protecting consumers from monopolies.
  • The government's toolkit:
    • taxes and subsidies: change the price
    • rules: regulation, legislation, fines
    • pollution permits
    • state provision and information
  • Environmental targets: air, water, noise and land pollution.
Mission 4

An indirect tax

  • Indirect tax: a tax on spending, paid to the government by the seller. Example: a tax per can of sugary drink.
  • The tax adds to firms' costs, so supply shifts left: S1 → S2.
  • The vertical gap between S1 and S2 is the tax per unit.
  • Price rises: P1 → P2. Quantity falls: Q1 → Q2.
  • The tax makes the price closer to the full cost to society: the polluter pays.
  • Pros: less of the harmful good; tax revenue for the government.
  • Cons: the external cost is hard to measure, so the tax may be too high or too low; it takes a bigger share of poorer people's income; a very high tax can lead to smuggling.
  • Say: "the supply curve shifts left (decrease in supply)" — not: "demand falls because of the tax".
Mission 4

Real world: the UK sugar tax

  • The UK Soft Drinks Industry Levy started on 6 April 2018.
  • It is paid by the makers of soft drinks with added sugar, per litre:
    • 5 g to under 8 g of sugar per 100 ml: 18p per litre
    • 8 g or more per 100 ml: 24p per litre
    • under 5 g: no levy
  • Many makers cut the sugar in their drinks to pay less or no levy. When the levy began, the UK government said over half of makers had already done so.
  • So a tax can work in two ways: a higher price for buyers, and a reason for firms to change the product.
  • Other countries tax sugary drinks too, e.g. Mexico (from 2014).
Mission 4Graph lab

Tax and elasticity

Illustrative data. A sugary drink sells at 1.50 a can; 200 thousand cans a week. The government adds a tax of 0.30 a can. Two markets: in one, demand is price inelastic (few substitutes); in the other, demand is price elastic (many substitutes).

  • Takeaway: inelastic demand: sales fall a little, the price rises more, and the tax raises more money. Elastic demand: sales fall a lot.
Quick check
?A government raises the tax on cigarettes, but the number of cigarettes smoked hardly falls. What is the most likely reason?
Mission 4

A subsidy

  • Subsidy: a payment from the government to producers, to lower their costs.
  • Used for goods with external benefits: public transport, vaccinations, solar panels.
  • Firms' costs fall, so supply shifts right: S1 → S2.
  • Price falls: P1 → P2. Quantity rises: Q1 → Q2.
  • Pros: more of a good that benefits society; cheaper for consumers.
  • Cons: opportunity cost: the money could be spent on hospitals or schools, or taxes could be lower. Firms may come to rely on it. The right size is hard to judge.
Quick check
?The government gives bus companies a subsidy for each journey they run. On a demand and supply diagram for bus journeys, what happens?
Mission 4

Rules, fines, permits and provision

  • Regulation and legislation: laws that limit harm, e.g. limits on factory emissions, bans on dangerous chemicals, safety standards.
    • Pro: clear and fast. Con: costly to enforce; a limit gives no reason to cut pollution below it.
  • Fines: penalties for breaking the rules. They only work if firms expect to be caught.
  • Pollution permits: the government sets a cap on total pollution and issues permits. Firms can buy and sell them.
    • A firm that cuts pollution cheaply sells spare permits; a firm that finds cutting costly buys them.
    • Real world: the EU Emissions Trading System, since 2005.
    • Pro: total pollution is capped. Con: setting the cap is hard; checking is costly.
  • State (direct) provision: the government supplies the good, e.g. vaccinations, schools. Con: opportunity cost.
  • Information provision: campaigns and labels, e.g. calorie labels on menus.
  • Protected areas: national parks stop land being built on.
Mission 4

Keeping markets competitive

  • A competition authority (in the UK, the Competition and Markets Authority) protects consumers from monopoly power.
  • Monopoly: it can investigate firms that abuse their power and fine them; some monopolies (water, energy networks) have their prices capped by a regulator.
  • Mergers: it can block a merger, or set conditions, if the merger would cut competition and raise prices.
  • Consumer protection: laws on safe products, honest labelling and the right to a refund for faulty goods.
  • Pros: lower prices, more choice, safer goods.
  • Cons: investigations are slow and costly; regulators may lack information; blocking a merger may stop economies of scale.
Quick check
?A government pays a subsidy to firms that make solar panels. Which of these is an opportunity cost of the policy?
Mission 4Evaluate

Does intervention work?

It depends on:

  • Information: can the government measure the external cost or benefit? Guess wrong, and the tax or subsidy is the wrong size.
  • Elasticity: with inelastic demand, a tax raises money but changes behaviour little.
  • Enforcement: rules and fines only work if they are checked; that costs money.
  • Unintended effects: illegal markets, smuggling, firms moving abroad, shortages under a maximum price.
  • Opportunity cost: every subsidy or state service uses money that could go elsewhere.
  • If intervention makes things worse, that is government failure.
Mission check

?Two cities of similar size each put the same charge on driving into the city centre, to cut traffic. City X has fast, cheap buses and trains to the centre. City Y has almost none. What is most likely?

Whether a tax changes behaviour or mainly raises money depends on how easily buyers can switch.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • PED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price; size above 1 = elastic, below 1 = inelastic.
  • Close substitutes make demand elastic; necessities, habits and small shares of income make it inelastic.
  • Inelastic: a price rise raises total revenue. Elastic: a price rise cuts it.

Mission 2

  • PES = % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price. Below 1: inelastic. Above 1: elastic.
  • Supply is more elastic with spare capacity, stocks, easy-to-find factors and, above all, more time.
  • Primary products usually have lower PES than manufactured products.

Mission 3

  • YED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in income.
  • Normal good: YED positive. Luxury: YED above 1. Inferior good: YED negative.
  • Firms and governments use YED to predict how a boom or recession changes demand and tax revenue.

Mission 4

  • Taxes shift supply left (price up, quantity down); subsidies shift supply right (price down, quantity up).
  • With inelastic demand, a tax raises revenue but cuts the quantity only a little.
  • Every policy has costs: information, enforcement, unintended effects and opportunity cost.
