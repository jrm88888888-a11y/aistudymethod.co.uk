Mission 1

What is development?

  • Development: improvement in people's lives and in a country's economy.
  • Economic view: how much wealth and income a country has.
  • Social view: health, education, housing, food and water.
  • Political view: freedom, rights and how honestly a country is governed.
  • Exam move: contrast a narrow economic definition with broader social and political ones.
Mission 1

Classifying countries

  • The World Bank groups countries by GNI per head (2025 data, used from July 2026):
    • low income: $1,175 or less · lower-middle: $1,176–$4,635 · upper-middle: $4,636–$14,375 · high: more than $14,375
  • The UK is high income, India lower-middle income and Rwanda low income.
  • Edexcel uses developed, emerging and developing countries.
Mission 1

Economic measures

  • GDP (gross domestic product): the value of everything produced inside a country in a year.
  • GNI (gross national income): GDP plus income earned abroad by its people and firms, minus income sent out by foreign firms.
  • Divide by population to get per head (per capita) figures.
  • GNI per head, 2025: UK $54,550, India $2,760, Rwanda $1,150.

Mission 1

The Human Development Index (HDI)

  • The HDI combines three dimensions into one score from 0 to 1:
    • a long and healthy life (life expectancy)
    • knowledge (years of schooling)
    • a decent standard of living (GNI per head)
  • 2023: UK 0.946 (13th), India 0.685 (130th), Rwanda 0.578 (159th) of 193 countries.

Mission 1

Why income cannot buy a high HDI

  • In the HDI, each extra dollar counts for less and less: income is scored on a logarithmic scale, and any income above $75,000 counts the same as $75,000.
  • The three dimension scores are multiplied together (then the cube root is taken), not simply averaged. A weak score pulls the total down, and a strong one cannot fully make up for it.
  • So Guyana's high income is held back by its lower health and education scores.
Mission 1

Limitations of measures

  • Averages hide inequality: GNI per head says nothing about how income is shared.
  • Money is not well-being: Guyana's real GDP grew 43.8% in 2024 after oil and gas production began; health and schooling change much more slowly.
  • Data quality: the World Bank lists India's adult literacy as 81.7% for 2023 but 78.2% for 2024; different surveys can give different answers.
  • Missing parts: no single measure covers rights, safety or the environment; even the HDI leaves them out.
  • Single measures (GNI) are simple; composite ones (HDI) are broader but still incomplete.
Mission 1

Inequality and corruption measures

  • Gini coefficient: how equally income (or spending) is shared, from 0 (everyone equal) to 100 (one person has everything). UK 32.4 (2021); Rwanda 39.4 (2023).
  • Corruption Perceptions Index (CPI): perceived public-sector corruption, from 0 (highly corrupt) to 100 (very clean).
  • CPI 2025: UK 70 (20th), Rwanda 58 (41st), India 39 (91st) of 182.
  • Rwanda has a lower income than India but a better CPI score: different measures rank countries differently.
Mission 1

What drives human development?

  • Economic: jobs and incomes pay for food, housing and schooling.
  • Social: access to health care and education.
  • Technological: e.g. electricity. In Rwanda, access rose from 6.2% of people in 2000 to 72% in 2024.
  • Cultural: e.g. attitudes to girls' schooling or women working.
  • Food and water security: in 2024 only 61.4% of people in Rwanda had at least basic drinking water.
Mission 1

Exam move: evaluating a measure

  • Say what it measures, give a strength and a limitation, and use data from named countries.
  • Finish with a judgement: use more than one measure, because each shows only part of development.
Mission check

?Country A's income per head is four times Country B's, yet Country A has a lower HDI. What is the most likely reason?

Same HDI, different lives. Sri Lanka: life expectancy 77.5, mean schooling 10.8 years. Guyana: 70.2 and 8.7, but income of $46,959 against $12,616. Because extra income counts for less and less and the three scores are multiplied, one strong dimension cannot buy a high HDI.

Edexcel GCSE Geography A (1GA0)

Mission 2

Income groups

  • The World Bank sorts countries by GNI per capita. For 2026–27:
    • low income: $1,175 or less
    • lower-middle income: $1,176–4,635
    • upper-middle income: $4,636–14,375
    • high income: more than $14,375
  • 25 low, 47 lower-middle, 59 upper-middle and 87 high-income economies.
  • GNI per capita is a national average: the country's income divided by its population. One label covers the whole country, so its regions can be far richer or poorer than the label suggests.
  • Edexcel's developed, emerging and developing countries are sorted by HDI, not income: very high HDI = developed; high or medium = emerging; low = developing.
Mission 2

The global pattern

  • Low income: 21 of the 25 are in sub-Saharan Africa (e.g. Niger, Rwanda).
  • High income: Europe and Central Asia has the most (40); others are in the Americas, East Asia and the Pacific, and the Gulf (e.g. the UK, Japan, Qatar).
  • Middle income covers most of Asia and Latin America: India is lower-middle, China upper-middle.
  • Pattern: a rich "North" and poorer "South", with many exceptions (Australia, Singapore).

Mission 2

Uneven within countries: core and periphery

  • A national average hides big differences between regions.
  • The core: the richest region, with most jobs, investment, transport links and power.
  • The periphery: poorer, remote regions with fewer jobs and services.
  • Young workers and money flow from periphery to core, which can widen the gap.
Mission 2

Uneven within the UK

  • Disposable income per person, 2024:
    • London £36,487 (highest region)
    • North East £20,562 (lowest)
    • UK average £25,965.
  • London's figure is about 1.8 times the North East's (our calculation).
  • Local areas range from £84,292 (Westminster and City of London) to £16,786 (Leicester).
Quick check

Mission check

?Country X is upper-middle income. Its capital region is far richer than its rural north. What does its income label tell you?

A label is an average. Inside one country, core regions can be far richer than the periphery. In the UK, London's disposable income per person is about 1.8 times the North East's.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why does a baby born in Norway expect to live about 25 years longer than one born in Somalia?

Make a guess: Why does a baby born in Norway expect to live about 25 years longer than one born in Somalia?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel GCSE Geography A (1GA0)

  • 5.2 physical, historic and economic factors causing spatial variation in development globally and within the UK
  • 5.3 impact of uneven development on quality of life: housing, health, education, jobs, technology, food and water security
Mission 3

The development gap

  • The development gap is the difference in wealth and wellbeing between the richest and poorest countries.
  • UNDP's Human Development Index (2023): Iceland is top at 0.972; South Sudan is bottom of 193 countries at 0.388.
  • Life expectancy at birth: Norway 83.3 years, Somalia 58.8, a gap of about 24.5 years (our calculation).
  • Edexcel uses developed, emerging and developing.
Mission 3

Physical factors

  • Landlocked: no coast, so trade goes through neighbours. Landlocked developing countries paid $3,204 to export a container against $1,268 for transit countries (World Bank data, 2014).
  • The UN estimates their development is "about 20 percent lower than it would be were they not landlocked".
  • Disease: in 2024 the WHO African Region had 95% of the world's malaria cases and deaths. Illness keeps children out of school and adults out of work.
  • Hazards and climate: drought, floods and tropical storms destroy crops, homes and roads again and again.
Mission 3

Historical factors

  • Colonialism: from the 1500s to the mid-1900s, European powers ruled much of Africa, Asia and the Americas.
  • Colonies were organised to export raw materials (cotton, rubber, minerals) to the ruling country, which made the profitable goods.
  • Railways and ports often ran from mines and plantations to the coast, not between towns.
  • Many countries became independent with few factories, few trained officials and borders drawn by others.
Quick check
?Which is a physical (environmental) factor that holds back development?
Mission 3

Economic: relying on primary products

  • Primary products: raw materials such as crops, minerals and oil, sold unprocessed.
  • UNCTAD counts a country as commodity-dependent when commodities make up more than 60% of its goods exports: 95 of 142 developing countries were, in 2019–2021. In Africa it was 45 of 53.
  • Raw materials earn less than processed goods, and their prices rise and fall sharply, so income is hard to plan.
Mission 3

Debt

  • Poor countries borrow from banks, governments and international lenders to build roads, dams and schools.
  • If export earnings fall, repayments and interest take money that could pay for health and education.
  • The HIPC debt-relief scheme: of 39 eligible countries, 36 had completed it by February 2023; Somalia became the 37th in December 2023.
Mission 3

Trade and geo-political relationships

  • Trade is buying (imports) and selling (exports) goods and services between countries.
  • Powerful countries and companies set many of the rules and prices of world trade.
  • Geo-political relationships: alliances, former colonial ties and aid agreements decide who can sell where, and on what terms.
Mission 3

Social, technological and cultural factors

  • Education: adults in Niger had on average 1.4 years of schooling in 2023; in the UK, 13.5. Fewer skills mean lower-paid jobs.
  • Technology: without reliable power, internet and machinery, businesses cannot grow.
  • Cultural: e.g. attitudes to girls' schooling or women working.
  • Food and water security: in 2025, 7.8% of the world's people faced hunger; in 2022, 2.2 billion people lacked safely managed drinking water. Hungry, ill people cannot learn or work well.
Mission 3

Uneven within the UK

  • Disposable income per person, 2024: London £36,487; North East £20,562; UK average £25,965.
  • Historic: the North East grew on coal, steel and shipbuilding; when they closed, many jobs went.
  • Economic: finance, government and head offices cluster in London, attracting investment and skilled workers.
  • Physical: remote upland regions, such as the Scottish Highlands, have steep land, poor soils and long journeys to markets, so fewer firms locate there.
Mission 3

Consequence: disparities in wealth and health

  • Income per person (2023, adjusted for prices): Norway $112,710, South Sudan $688, about 164 times more (our calculation).
  • Life expectancy: Norway 83.3, Somalia 58.8 years.
  • Hunger: Africa is home to about 309 million hungry people.
  • Wealth pays for doctors, clean water, vaccines and nutritious food, so the wealth gap becomes a health gap.
Mission 3

Consequence: quality of life

  • Housing: poor-quality homes and slums where incomes are low.
  • Health and water: 115 million people collected untreated surface water from lakes, ponds and rivers in 2022.
  • Education and jobs: fewer years of schooling lead to informal, low-paid work.
  • Technology: less access to electricity, phones and the internet.
  • Food security: hunger stunts children's growth and learning.
Mission 3

Consequence: international migration

  • People move from poorer to richer places for work and higher wages.
  • In 2024, 18.5 million people born in India lived abroad; about 45% of them in Western Asia, mostly Gulf states (our calculation).
  • Migrants send money home: remittances to India were US$150.7 billion in 2025.
  • Origin countries can lose skilled workers (brain drain); destination countries gain workers.
Quick check
?18.5 million people born in India live abroad, about 45% of them in Western Asia, mostly Gulf states. Why do so many workers make this move?
Mission check

?A landlocked country earns most of its export income from one crop, coffee. The world coffee price halves, and the country owes large debts in US dollars. What is the most likely result?

Uneven development has many linked causes: physical (landlocked, disease, hazards), historical (colonialism) and economic (primary products, debt, trade). They add up, which is why a baby in Somalia expects to live about 24.5 years less than one in Norway.

Mission 4

Closing the development gap

  • The development gap: the difference in wealth and well-being between richer and poorer countries.
  • Strategies to narrow it:
    • aid and debt relief
    • trade (including fairtrade) and investment
    • industrial development and tourism
    • small-scale tools: intermediate technology, microfinance.
Mission 4

Types of aid

  • Bilateral: from one government to another. Multilateral: through bodies such as the World Bank or UN.
  • Government aid (official development assistance) and NGO aid (charities such as Oxfam or Partners In Health).
  • Emergency aid: short-term help after a disaster: food, tents, medicine.
  • Long-term development aid: projects that build schools, clinics, water supplies and skills.
  • Grants are gifts; loans must be repaid.
Quick check
?Which is long-term development aid?
Mission 4

Development targets, and falling aid

  • Since a 1970 UN resolution, rich countries have been asked to give 0.7% of their national income (GNI) as aid.
  • The Sustainable Development Goals (2015) set development targets for 2030, such as ending hunger.
  • Aid from rich (OECD DAC) countries: $215.1 billion (2024) → $174.3 billion (2025, preliminary): 0.34% → 0.26% of GNI.
  • In 2025 only Norway, Luxembourg, Sweden and Denmark reached 0.7%.
Quick check
?Removing inflation, DAC aid fell from $215.14 billion (2024) to $165.51 billion (2025). What was the percentage fall?
Mission 4

Debt relief

  • Many poor countries owed more than they could repay. Repayments took money from schools and hospitals.
  • The IMF and World Bank started the Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) Initiative in 1996; in 2005 another scheme added 100% relief on some debts.
  • To qualify, countries need a track record of reform and a poverty reduction strategy carried out for at least a year.
  • Rwanda is one of the countries that completed HIPC; Somalia became the 37th in December 2023.
  • + frees money for services. − strict conditions; countries can fall into debt again.
Mission 4

Rescue loans: Sri Lanka

  • In 2022 Sri Lanka stopped paying its foreign debts: "the external default in 2022" (IMF).
  • Its economy shrank by 7.3% in 2022. Inflation peaked at 70% in September 2022.
  • March 2023: the IMF approved a four-year loan of about US$3 billion.
  • Conditions (conditionality) included higher taxes and cost-recovery energy pricing (charging what energy really costs).
  • Results: inflation fell "to around 4 percent in 2023", and the economy grew 5.0% in 2024.
Mission 4

Who sets the terms?

  • The government negotiates conditions with IMF staff; the IMF's Executive Board approves the loan.
  • Votes follow each country's quota, roughly its economic weight.
  • Big decisions, such as changing quotas, need 85% of the votes. The USA holds 16.49%, enough to block them.
  • Creditors also had to accept less: governments, Chinese state banks and private bondholders.
  • Alternatives: grants from donors, or loans from newer banks such as the BRICS countries' New Development Bank, which funds projects rather than rescues.
Mission 4

Inter-governmental agreements

  • Agreements between governments, often through the UN, IMF or World Bank:
    • the 0.7% aid target (UN resolution, 1970)
    • HIPC debt relief (1996, extended 2005)
    • the Sustainable Development Goals (2015).
  • Strength: shared rules and targets. Weakness: often not binding; in 2025 only four DAC donors met 0.7%.
Quick check
?What does conditionality mean for an IMF loan?
Mission check

?A low-income country asks to have its debts to the IMF and World Bank cancelled. Who sets the conditions it must meet?

Sri Lanka's government agreed conditions with the IMF; the IMF's Executive Board, where votes follow quotas, approved them, and creditors had to accept less. Alternatives include grants, debt relief and newer lenders such as the New Development Bank.

Mission 5: In 2006 a bank for poor villagers won the Nobel Peace Prize. Do small loans and fairer prices really lift people out of poverty?

Make a guess: In 2006 a bank for poor villagers won the Nobel Peace Prize. Do small loans and fairer prices really lift people out of poverty?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel GCSE Geography A (1GA0)

  • 5.4 top-down (government/TNC-led) vs bottom-up (community-led) projects: advantages and limitations
Mission 5

Top-down and bottom-up

  • Top-down: planned and run by governments, large companies or international bodies (such as the World Bank). Often large-scale.
    • Advantages: big scale, fast, can build national infrastructure.
    • Limitations: costly, may ignore local needs, local people have little say.
  • Bottom-up: run by and with local communities, often helped by charities. Usually small-scale.
    • Advantages: meets local needs, cheap, local people keep control.
    • Limitations: small reach, slow to spread, depends on funding and volunteers.
Mission 5

Microfinance

  • In 2006 Muhammad Yunus and Grameen Bank (Bangladesh) won the Nobel Peace Prize "for their efforts to create economic and social development from below".
  • Microfinance: small loans, savings and insurance for people that ordinary banks will not serve.
  • In 2018, microfinance lenders had 139.9 million borrowers: 80% women, 65% in rural areas.
Mission 5

Does microfinance work?

  • Six randomised trials in six countries on four continents found "a consistent pattern of modestly positive, but not transformative, effects".
  • In Andhra Pradesh, India, rapid lending left many households over-indebted: by 2010, 83% had loans from more than one source.
  • So small loans help some families run businesses and manage money, but do not on their own lift large numbers out of poverty.
Quick check
?What do the six randomised trials suggest about microfinance?
Mission 5

Fair trade: how it works

  • Fair trade: a system that gives producers in poorer countries a fairer, more stable deal.
  • Minimum price: a floor paid when world prices fall. For washed Arabica coffee it rose from $1.40 to $1.80 per pound for contracts from 1 August 2023.
  • Fairtrade Premium: an extra sum that producer groups decide together how to spend.
  • In 2024 Fairtrade covered 1.87 million farmers and workers and paid €201.6 million in Premium.
Mission 5

The benefits for producers

  • A safety net: the minimum price protects income when world prices crash.
  • Investment: in 2024 small-scale producer groups spent 35% of their Premium on improving production and farming, and 19% on payments to farmers.
  • Communities: workers on certified plantations put 67% of their Premium into social investments.
  • Reach: 75% of Fairtrade farmers and workers were in Africa and the Middle East.
Mission 5

The limits

  • In 2024 only about 27% of reported certified coffee production was sold on Fairtrade terms; the rest was sold at ordinary prices.
  • Fair Trade coffee was 1.8% of world coffee exports in 2009: a small share of trade.
  • A study in Ethiopia and Uganda found that, in some cases, hired workers in areas with Fairtrade groups were "significantly worse paid" than elsewhere.
  • Fairtrade itself says its Premium data do not show "outcomes or impacts".
Quick check
?Which is a benefit of fair trade for producers?
Mission 5

Top-down in Rwanda

  • Rwanda's Ministry of Education built more than 22,000 classrooms under a national project supported by the World Bank.
  • To staff them quickly, it relaxed hiring rules: about 25,000 graduates who had not been to a teacher training college became teachers.
  • From April 2024 a national programme trained and certified 24,000 of them in less than a year, in all 30 districts.
  • Advantages: national scale and speed. Limitations: thousands of pupils were taught at first by uncertified teachers; the project depends on outside funding.
Mission 5

Bottom-up compared

  • Microfinance (e.g. Grameen Bank, Bangladesh) and fair trade co-operatives are bottom-up: local people borrow, save, decide and run them.
  • Advantages: reach women and small farmers directly (80% of microfinance borrowers are women); local control.
  • Limitations: modest effects; small share of trade; risk of over-borrowing.
  • Compared: top-down reaches a whole country fast; bottom-up fits local needs but spreads slowly.
Quick check
?Which project is bottom-up?
Mission 5

Exam move: comparing strategies

  • Compare on scale, cost, speed, local control and evidence of success.
  • Use named places: Rwanda's classrooms and teachers (top-down) against microfinance and fair trade (bottom-up).
  • Finish with a judgement: the two often work best together.
Mission check

?A charity starts offering small loans and a guaranteed minimum price to cocoa farmers in a new region. What is the most realistic expectation?

Modestly. Microcredit's effects are "modestly positive, but not transformative", and too much lending caused a crisis in Andhra Pradesh. Fairtrade sets a floor price and pays a Premium, but only about 27% of certified coffee was sold on Fairtrade terms in 2024. Both are tools, not cures.

Mission 6

Where is India?

  • In South Asia: the Himalayas to the north, the Indian Ocean to the south, between the Arabian Sea and the Bay of Bengal.
  • 1.46 billion people in 2025, more than China's 1.41 billion.
  • India has about 87% of South Asia's people and 86% of its GDP (our calculation).
  • That makes India the regional power of South Asia.
Mission 6

Why India matters globally

  • The world's 6th-largest economy in 2025 at market exchange rates: about US$3.9 trillion (World Bank US$3.96 trillion; IMF US$3.92 trillion), about 3% of world GDP (our calculation).
  • A member of BRICS, which it chairs in 2026.
  • Mumbai is a global financial centre: home of the Reserve Bank of India since 1937.
  • But income per person (GNI) is only US$2,760 (2025): "lower middle income".

Mission 6

Political context

  • A federal country: 36 states and union territories and 707 districts.
  • Central and state governments share power, so the same policy can work differently in different states.
  • National programmes set by the central government: e.g. Make in India (2014) to grow manufacturing.
  • Member of BRICS: links to Brazil, China, Russia, South Africa and others.
Mission 6

Social and cultural context

  • Young: 24% of people were under 15 in 2025.
  • Smaller families: about 2.0 births per woman (2024), down from 3.7 in 1995.
  • Still mostly rural: 36% lived in towns and cities in 2025.
  • Education: 76% of adults could read in 2022.
  • Culture: a mix of influences, e.g. Mumbai's World Heritage buildings "blend Indian design with Art Deco imagery".
Mission 6

Environmental context

  • Monsoon: most rain falls in a few months. At Patna, 76% of the Ganges' yearly flow came in the monsoon months (1980).
  • Farming needs water: 90% of India's water withdrawals go to agriculture (2022).
  • Cyclones hit the coasts; October–December is the main cyclone season.
  • A monsoon that fails or floods can hit crops, farm incomes and food prices.

Mission 7

Transnational corporations

  • A transnational corporation (TNC) is a company that operates in more than one country.
  • Its headquarters is in its home country; a country where it sets up is a host country.
  • Foreign direct investment (FDI): a company builds or buys a business abroad and runs it.
  • Outsourcing: a company pays another firm to do part of its work, such as making its products.
Mission 7

How TNCs organise themselves

  • Headquarters, design and research usually stay in the home country: Apple is based in Cupertino, California.
  • Making the product can happen anywhere. Apple's manufacturing is mostly done by outsourcing partners in China mainland, India, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and Vietnam.
  • Toyota and its affiliates run more than 50 manufacturing operations in 27 countries and regions outside Japan.
Mission 7

Why locate in many countries?

  • Lower costs: Apple says outsourcing "can lower operating costs".
  • Near customers: in the year to March 2026 Toyota made 73.9% of the vehicles it sold outside Japan in factories outside Japan. Cars are big and heavy to ship.
  • Local supplies and money: buying parts locally matches local costs to local sales.
  • Avoiding trade barriers: Toyota notes a "significant increase" in US tariffs, including on cars, announced in 2025; making cars inside a market avoids them.
  • Government incentives such as tax breaks also attract TNCs.

Mission 7

Apple in India: outsourcing

  • India is one of the main countries where Apple's outsourcing partners make its products.
  • The partner firms own and run the factories and employ the workers; Apple designs the products and sells them worldwide.
  • Apple says outsourcing lowers costs but "reduce[s] the Company's direct control over production".
  • Because partners work in six countries, production can shift between them.
Mission 7

Toyota in India: FDI

  • Toyota Kirloskar Motor has made cars in India since December 1999.
  • Models include the Innova, Fortuner and Urban Cruiser Hyryder, some as hybrids.
  • A Toyota group company, Toyota Kirloskar Auto Parts, has made axles and transmissions in India since 2002.
  • Toyota invests in its own factories and in nearby suppliers: its Just in Time system needs parts to arrive as they are needed.
Mission 7

FDI into India

  • Foreign direct investment into India (World Bank):
    • 2000: $3.6 billion · 2020: $64.4 billion (peak) · 2024: $27.1 billion · 2025: $40.8 billion
  • FDI grew hugely after 2000, but it rises and falls from year to year.
Mission 7

Advantages for the host country

  • Jobs and wages in factories, and in partner and supplier firms.
  • Skills and technology: workers learn new methods (for example Toyota's production system).
  • Multiplier effect: suppliers (such as parts makers) and local services grow too.
  • Exports and taxes: goods made in India are sold abroad, and firms pay tax.
Mission 7

Disadvantages for the host country

  • Profits flow back to the home country.
  • Top jobs (design, research, management) often stay at headquarters.
  • Footloose: contracts and investment can move. FDI into India fell by about 58% from 2020 to 2024.
  • Competition: local firms may struggle against large TNCs.
  • Working conditions and the environment depend on how well laws are enforced.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Development is economic, social and political; measures include GNI per head, birth and death rates, infant mortality, life expectancy, doctors, literacy, water and the HDI.
  • Every measure has limits (averages, data quality, missing dimensions), so use several.
  • Add Gini (inequality) and the CPI (corruption).

Mission 2

  • Global pattern: low income mostly in sub-Saharan Africa; high income mostly in Europe, the Americas, East Asia and the Gulf.
  • Within countries: a national label is an average that hides richer and poorer regions. Core regions pull ahead of the periphery. UK: London £36,487 vs North East £20,562 (2024).

Mission 3

  • Causes: physical/environmental, historical (colonialism) and economic (primary products, debt, trade).
  • Social and technological factors matter too.
  • Consequences: gaps in wealth, health and quality of life, and international migration.
  • Within the UK, London's income per person is far above the North East's, for historic, economic and physical reasons.

Mission 4

  • Strategies to reduce the gap: aid, debt relief, trade, fairtrade, investment, industry, tourism, intermediate technology and microfinance, each with gains and limits.
  • Lenders (the IMF and World Bank) set the conditions for rescue loans and debt relief; voting follows quotas.

Mission 5

  • Microfinance and fair trade bring modest, real benefits (stable prices, Premium, small loans for women), but reach only part of trade and need careful lending.
  • Top-down (Rwanda's classrooms and teachers) is big and fast; bottom-up (savings groups, co-operatives) is local and slower.

Mission 6

  • India: 1.46 billion people (about 87% of South Asia's), 6th-largest economy, but GNI per person US$2,760.
  • Context: federal (36 states/UTs); young; mostly rural; monsoon-dependent farming.
  • Core (e.g. Maharashtra) and periphery (e.g. Bihar) differ sharply.

Mission 7

  • TNCs keep headquarters and design at home and spread production abroad, by outsourcing (Apple) or FDI (Toyota).
  • For India: jobs, skills, exports and suppliers; but profits leave, top jobs stay away, and FDI rises and falls.
  • India's jobs have shifted from farming to industry and services, and most investment is private.

Mission 8

  • Trade: top partners shifted (China, Russia, Gulf); exports 7% → 22% of GDP; UK–India deal ratified.
  • Geopolitics: BRICS, SCO, Quad; 5th-largest defence budget; Kashmir ceasefire observed by the UN since 1949; connectivity rising but rural areas lag.

Mission 9

  • Gains: life expectancy 61 → 72; under-5 deaths 110 → 27 per 1,000; electricity 99.9%; extreme poverty 27% → 3% (2011–23).
  • Costs: PM2.5 54 µg/m³ (above WHO's 35 interim target) and waste in cities. Slums and too few new jobs outside farming.
  • Population: narrower base, wider top. Inequality depends on the measure: spending Gini fell; top 1% income share at a record.
  • Management: toilets, clean cooking gas, community groups; global status through BRICS and defence.
