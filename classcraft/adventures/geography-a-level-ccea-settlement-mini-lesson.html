Mission 1

Mission 1: In Denmark, 200 people can make a place urban. In Japan it takes 50,000. So how urban is the world?

Make a guess: In Denmark, 200 people can make a place urban. In Japan it takes 50,000. So how urban is the world?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • AS2.2a.i distinguish rural and urban settlement with reference to the rural–urban continuum
Mission 1

Who counts as urban?

  • Countries define "urban" differently. Population thresholds range from 200 inhabitants in Denmark to 50,000 in Japan.
  • Some countries also use density, functions (e.g. share of non-farm jobs) or administrative status.
  • So a place of 5,000 people can be a town in one country and a village in another.
Mission 1

The rural–urban continuum

  • Settlements form a continuum, not two boxes: isolated dwelling → hamlet → village → town → city → conurbation.
  • Moving along it, population, density, range of functions, share of non-farm jobs and land use intensity all rise.
  • There is no sharp break: the rural–urban fringe and commuter villages share rural and urban traits.
  • Any line between "rural" and "urban" is drawn by people, for a purpose.
Quick check
?Under the UN's international rule, a town needs at least 5,000 people and 300 people per km². A settlement has 8,000 people living at 200 per km². What is it?
Mission 1

One world, two answers

  • The UN's Degree of Urbanisation applies one rule everywhere: city ≥50,000 people and ≥1,500 per km²; town ≥5,000 and ≥300 per km².
  • Adding countries' own definitions: 58% of the world was urban in 2025.
  • Using the single rule: 45% lived in cities and 36% in towns, 81% together.
  • The gap: many places that meet the town rule are classed as rural by their own countries, especially in Central and Southern Asia and sub-Saharan Africa.
Mission check

?Country Z classes every settlement under 20,000 people as rural. Many of its settlements have 5,000–15,000 people living at more than 300 per km². If Z switched to the UN's single rule, what would happen to its reported urban share?

"Urban" is a definition, not a fact of nature. Adding national definitions gives 58% urban; the UN's single rule puts 81% in cities or towns (2025). Settlements sit on a continuum.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Detroit had 1,849,568 people in 1950 and 639,111 in 2020. What emptied the Motor City?

Make a guess: Detroit had 1,849,568 people in 1950 and 639,111 in 2020. What emptied the Motor City?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • AS2.2a.ii rural–urban fringe issues: greenfield developments, suburbanisation, counterurbanisation
Mission 2

Moving out from the centre

  • Suburbanisation: people, then shops and offices, move from the inner city to the suburbs. Drivers: cars and commuter rail, cheaper land, more space.
  • Counterurbanisation: people move beyond the urban edge, to small towns and villages. Drivers: retirement, quality of life, remote working.
  • In parts of Europe, city populations have fallen with suburbanisation or counterurbanisation, and with boundary reclassification.
Mission 2

The rural–urban fringe

  • Rural–urban fringe: the zone where the built-up area meets the countryside.
  • Greenfield sites have never been built on; brownfield sites have been built on before.
  • Pressures: housing estates, retail and business parks, roads, against farmland and green space.
  • Worldwide, built-up area per person rose from 43 m² (1975) to 63 m² (2025).
  • About 60% of land converted to urban use since 1970 was productive farmland.
Quick check
?A retired couple sell their flat in central Leeds and buy a cottage in a village 50 km away. Which movement is this?
Mission 2

London: in and out at once

  • In the year to mid-2025 London had positive natural change and net international inward migration.
  • But "this was more than offset by net outward migration to other areas of the UK".
  • London's population fell by 0.29%, "the first decrease since the year to mid-2021".
  • A city can gain from births and arrivals while losing more people outwards.
Mission 2

Deindustrialisation and the rise of services

  • Deindustrialisation: a long-term fall in manufacturing jobs and output in a place.
  • Causes: the global shift of factories to lower-cost countries, automation, firms relocating, and recessions.
  • Service economy: UK employment in services rose from 66.8% (1991) to 83.0% (2024); industry fell from 30.8% to 16.1% (ILO modelled).
Mission 2

Detroit's population

  • US census counts for Detroit city:
    • 465,766 (1910); 1,849,568 (1950, the 5th largest US city); 1,027,974 (1990); 713,777 (2010); 639,111 (2020).
  • Most of the loss came early: 821,594 of the 1,210,457 people lost by 2020 had gone by 1990 (our calculation).
Mission 2

Detroit: the jobs left

  • Historian Thomas Sugrue told the Associated Press that Detroit lost 140,000 jobs between 1947 and 1963; "The postwar manufacturing boom was largely over by the mid-1950s" (reported by HNN).
  • Manufacturing jobs in the Detroit metro area: 380,900 (2000), 174,000 (2009).
  • People who could move for work, often younger and better paid, left first: selective out-migration.
Quick check
?By what percentage did Detroit city's population fall between 1950 (1,849,568) and 2020 (639,111)?
Mission check

?A northern English town loses its last large factory. Over 20 years its population falls, while commuter villages 20 km away grow. Its boundary and birth rate do not change. What best explains the town's decline?

Detroit lost about 65% of its people from 1950 to 2020, two-thirds of that loss before 1990, as factory jobs left from the 1950s and people moved out. Deindustrialisation and centrifugal movement (moving outwards from the centre) worked together.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why would a city build new homes on old, polluted factory land instead of the empty fields next door?

Make a guess: Why would a city build new homes on old, polluted factory land instead of the empty fields next door?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • AS2.2a.ii issues that impact the rural–urban fringe: greenfield developments, suburbanisation, counterurbanisation
  • A2-2.B2.iii greenfield and brownfield development in relation to sustainability (Option B, Planning for sustainable settlements)
Mission 3

Greenfield and brownfield

  • Greenfield site: land never built on, usually farmland or open land, often at the edge of a town.
  • Brownfield site (planning term: previously developed land): land "lawfully developed and is or was occupied by a permanent structure".
  • Not brownfield: land last used for farm or forestry buildings, and gardens, parks, recreation grounds and allotments in built-up areas.
  • Brownfield is often derelict: old factories, docks, gasworks, railway yards.
Mission 3

The rural–urban fringe

  • The rural–urban fringe is where the built-up area meets the countryside.
  • Pressures: housing estates, retail and business parks, ring roads, golf courses, waste sites.
  • Worldwide, about 60% of the land turned into urban space since 1970 was formerly productive farmland.
  • Built-up area per person rose from 43 to 63 square metres: cities are spreading outwards faster than they grow in population.
Mission 3

Why the fringe is under pressure

  • Suburbanisation: people (then shops and offices) move from the inner city to the suburbs at the city's edge.
  • Counterurbanisation: people move from the city to villages and small towns beyond it, often still commuting back.
  • Both create demand for new homes, roads and services on greenfield land.
  • Drivers: car ownership, cheaper land, more space, a wish for a "rural" lifestyle.
Quick check
?A family moves from a city flat to a village 25 km away, and one parent commutes daily to the city. What is this?
Mission 3

Protecting the fringe: green belts

  • Aim of Green Belt policy in England's planning rules (NPPF): "to prevent urban sprawl by keeping land permanently open".
  • Its purposes: check sprawl; stop neighbouring towns merging; protect the countryside from encroachment; preserve historic towns' setting; and "assist urban regeneration by encouraging the recycling of derelict and other urban land".
  • England: 1,632,730 hectares, about 12.5% of its land (31 March 2026).
  • It still changes: a net loss of 480 hectares in a year, as councils released land for housing in new local plans.
Mission 3

Where new homes go

  • England, 2021–22: 54% of new residential addresses were created on previously developed land.
  • Only 2% were within the Green Belt.
  • England's planning policy pushes development towards brownfield sites: even when Green Belt boundaries change, releases should prioritise previously developed land.
Quick check
?In England in 2021–22, 54% of new residential addresses were on previously developed land and 2% were within the Green Belt. What percentage were not on previously developed land?
Mission 3

Greenfield development: how sustainable?

  • Economic: often cheaper and quicker to build; no clean-up; large plots suit big estates and business parks.
  • Social: bigger homes and gardens; but new estates may lack shops, schools and buses at first.
  • Environmental: loses farmland and habitats; adds car journeys and sprawl; more roads and pipes to build.
  • Overall: meets demand fast, but tends to be the less sustainable option.
Mission 3

Brownfield development: how sustainable?

  • Environmental: reuses land and protects the countryside; homes sit near existing public transport and services.
  • Economic: can regenerate derelict areas; but contamination clean-up and old foundations raise costs.
  • Social: brings life back to run-down districts; but new homes may be expensive, and some brownfield sites are green spaces valued by locals or wildlife.
  • Example: Belfast's Titanic Quarter, around "the very place where Titanic was designed, built and launched", now home to the Titanic Belfast visitor attraction and a hotel.
Quick check
?Which is the strongest environmental argument for building on brownfield rather than greenfield land?
Mission check

?A town must find land for 500 homes. Site 1: a disused airfield 15 km from town, with no bus or rail, now rich in wildlife. Site 2: a field at the town's edge, beside the railway station and a school. Neither is in a green belt. Which is the more sustainable judgement?

Brownfield reuse is usually more sustainable because such sites are usually near services. Judge each site by location, transport and habitat, not by its label; greenfield building at the fringe spreads the city outward, the pressure suburbanisation and counterurbanisation create.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Northern Ireland has mountains and coasts worth protecting. Why does it still have no National Park?

Make a guess: Northern Ireland has mountains and coasts worth protecting. Why does it still have no National Park?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • AS2.2b.i why planning to protect the countryside matters; ASSIs/SSSIs, AONBs and National Parks as management measures
  • AS2.2b.ii evaluate the arguments for and against a National Park in Northern Ireland
Mission 4

Why plan the countryside?

  • NI's landscapes "underpin biodiversity, climate resilience, and community well-being, while supporting sustainable tourism and rural economies".
  • They "face mounting pressures from climate change, habitat fragmentation, pollution, and unsustainable land use".
  • Many users want the same land: farmers, residents, visitors, developers, conservation bodies.
  • Planning decides which uses are allowed where; designations add extra protection.
Mission 4

ASSIs (NI) and SSSIs (GB)

  • ASSI: Area of Special Scientific Interest, NI's protection for the best wildlife and geological sites.
  • Law: the Environment (Northern Ireland) Order 2002; NIEA "must declare land as an ASSI" if it is of special scientific interest.
  • Chosen by scientific survey.
  • Owners need consent for listed notifiable operations, e.g. changing grazing, or burning (Murlough ASSI list).
  • SSSI is the equivalent in Great Britain; in England, Natural England notifies the land and sets the rules.
Mission 4

Does designation work? ASSI condition data

  • 2025/26: 1,501 features listed on 394 terrestrial ASSIs.
  • Of features assessed favourable or unfavourable: 762 favourable, 607 unfavourable (56% favourable; our calculation).
  • Eastern Mournes ASSI: blanket bog, dry heath and wet heath all unfavourable.
  • DAERA: "unfavourable" does not mean a site is poorly managed; pressures can come from outside the boundary.
Quick check
?What do the ASSI condition data show about this type of designation?
Mission 4

AONBs: protected landscapes

  • Area of Outstanding Natural Beauty, designated under the Nature Conservation and Amenity Lands (NI) Order 1985.
  • Four purposes: conserve natural beauty; conserve wildlife and heritage; promote public enjoyment; provide public access.
  • Example: Causeway Coast AONB, designated 1989, about 30 km of the North Antrim coast.
  • Managed by a non-statutory forum and a Heritage Trust, grant-aided by NIEA.
  • NI's protected landscapes are AONBs, where Great Britain also has National Parks.
Mission 4

National Parks

  • IUCN Category II: "large natural or near natural areas set aside to protect large-scale ecological processes" that also provide for recreation and visitors.
  • The UK has 15, "across England, Scotland and Wales"; the Republic of Ireland had 6 in 2011.
  • England's 10 cover 9.3% of its land; Wales's 3 cover 19.9% (2011 figures).
  • NI law (1989) allows designation only, with "no accompanying statutory powers" to set up a management body such as a National Park Authority.
Mission 4

The Mournes proposal

  • 2002: the Department of the Environment announced it would process proposals for a National Park in the Mournes.
  • 2006–07: public consultation (31 Aug 2006 to 31 Jan 2007); a Working Party reported to the Minister in 2007.
  • Result: "the predominant view of those on the Mournes and Slieve Croob area who responded … is one of opposition".
  • Opposition was strongest among farming and landowning communities.
  • "Most Northern Ireland organisations, and many (but not all) business, tourism and environmental interests in the Mournes are in favour".
Mission 4

Arguments for

  • £2–4 million of extra funding for the area.
  • Up to 30 jobs in a National Park Authority.
  • Protection of landscape, built heritage and biodiversity.
  • More visitors and visitor spending; support for local services.
  • A National Park brand for local produce and attracting visitors.
Mission 4

Arguments against

  • More second homes; higher house prices, affordability problems and a change in social mix.
  • Visitor pressure on landscape and biodiversity unless carefully managed.
  • Conflicts between recreation and landowners over access.
  • More traffic congestion.
  • Tourism jobs that "tend to be lower paid and seasonal".
  • Key point: these impacts are "neither of which are inevitable"; they depend on the legislation and the National Park plan.
Quick check
?Which local group did the Working Party single out as most opposed to a Mournes National Park?
Quick check
?A farmer fears a National Park would bring rules on her land. Which point from the research paper best answers her worry?
Mission check

?A new National Park is proposed for another NI upland. A survey finds: residents worried about house prices; visitor numbers rising; heathland ASSI features unfavourable; no NI law yet for a Park Authority. Which evaluation is best supported?

NI has no National Park because local opposition (especially from farmers and landowners) met a law that allows designation but no management authority; any new proposal must be judged on its legislation and plan, not on the label.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Some of New York's poorest neighbourhoods lost a quarter of their people in the 1970s. Who moved back in?

Make a guess: Some of New York's poorest neighbourhoods lost a quarter of their people in the 1970s. Who moved back in?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • AS2.2c.i MEDC inner city: economic and social deprivation, re-urbanisation and gentrification (one inner-city case study)
Mission 5

Key terms

  • MEDC inner city: the old, dense ring around the central business district.
  • Economic deprivation: low incomes, unemployment, poverty. Social deprivation: poor housing, health, education, crime.
  • Re-urbanisation: people moving back into the city, reversing earlier decline.
  • Gentrification: higher-income people move into a low-income area, renovate, and push up housing costs.
Mission 5

Decline

  • New York City: 7,891,957 people (1950) → 7,071,639 (1980).
  • 1970–80: the whole city lost 10.4%; the low-income neighbourhoods that later gentrified lost 25.9%.
  • Why (process): suburbanisation and the loss of inner-city jobs and services left vacant homes behind.
Mission 5

Deprivation in 1990

  • In 1990, 22 of the city's 55 sub-borough areas were classed as low-income.
  • Poverty share: 32.9% of people in the later-gentrifying neighbourhoods v 19.4% citywide.
  • Central Harlem: average household income $39,650 v $78,500 citywide (1990, in 2015 dollars).
  • Social (education): adults 25+ with a college degree: 12.9% in the later-gentrifying neighbourhoods v 21.5% citywide (1990).
  • Use both kinds of indicator: economic (income, poverty) and social (education, housing, health).
Quick check
?In 1990, Central Harlem's average household income was $39,650 and the citywide average $78,500 (2015 dollars). About what share of the city average was Central Harlem's?
Mission 5

Re-urbanisation

  • City population rose again: 7,322,564 (1990) → 8,805,594 (April 2020 estimates base).
  • Later-gentrifying neighbourhoods grew +5.6% (1980s), +5.7% (1990s), +1.8% (2000s).
  • Yet in 2010 they were still 15.8% below their 1970 population.
  • Since 2020 the city fell to 8,478,072 (July 2024): re-urbanisation can stall.
Mission 5

Gentrification

  • Of the 22 low-income areas, 15 were "gentrifying": rents rose faster than the median area.
  • Average rent, 1990 → 2010–14: gentrifying +34.3%; citywide +22.1%; other low-income areas +13.2%.
  • Williamsburg/Greenpoint +78.7%; Central Harlem +53.2%.
  • Adults with a college degree in gentrifying areas: up about 15.6 percentage points (1990 to 2010–14).
Quick check
?Which evidence best shows that an area is gentrifying, rather than simply growing?
Mission 5

Who gains, who loses?

  • Poverty share in gentrifying areas: 32.9% (2000) → 29.7% (2010–14).
  • Rent burden (30%+ of income on rent), citywide: 40.7% (2000) → 51.7% (2010–14); gentrifying areas 52.9%.
  • Recently available units the median renter could afford: 20.2% → 13.3% citywide.
  • The Center warns: "We cannot directly observe if low-income residents are moving out".
Quick check
?The poverty share in gentrifying areas fell from 32.9% (2000) to 29.7% (2010–14). How should the change be described?
Mission 5

Exam move: evaluate the evidence

  • A falling poverty share can mean poor residents became better off or that poor residents were replaced.
  • The data cannot tell these apart: the Center says so.
  • Use several indicators: rents, rent burden, affordability, education, population.
  • Judge: gentrification brought investment and lower poverty shares, but higher housing costs for those who stayed.
Mission check

?In an inner-city district, population grows 6% in a decade, average rent rises 50%, the share of adults with degrees doubles, and the number of people in poverty stays the same. What is the best conclusion?

In New York the people who moved back into the poorest inner neighbourhoods were increasingly college-educated households, as rents rose faster than anywhere else. Poverty shares fell a little, but rent burdens rose: the inner city was re-urbanised and gentrified, unevenly.

Mission 6

Mission 6: In 2011, 1 in 10 people in Mumbai's D ward lived in slums. In M/East ward, more than 3 in 4 did. Why such a gap inside one city?

Make a guess: In 2011, 1 in 10 people in Mumbai's D ward lived in slums. In M/East ward, more than 3 in 4 did. Why such a gap inside one city?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • AS2.2c.i MEDC inner city: economic and social deprivation, re-urbanisation, gentrification (one inner-city case)
  • AS2.2c.ii LEDC city: informal settlement growth, service provision, economic activity (one urban case)
Mission 6

Key terms

  • Social inequality: unequal access to income, housing, health, education, work and services.
  • Deprivation: lacking what most people in a society have. Multiple deprivation: several lacks at once.
  • Social segregation: groups living apart, by income or by background.
  • Informal settlement: homes without legal title or planning approval, often without services.
  • CCEA calls the two contexts MEDC and LEDC cities.
Mission 6

Measuring inequality

  • England's Index of Multiple Deprivation 2025 ranks 33,755 small areas, of about 1,500 people each.
  • Seven weighted domains: income 22.5%; employment 22.5%; education 13.5%; health 13.5%; crime 9.3%; barriers to housing and services 9.3%; living environment 9.3%.
  • It is relative: it ranks areas, so some area is always "most deprived".
Quick check
?Residents of an area live far from a GP surgery and cannot afford housing nearby. Which IMD domain records this?
Mission 6

Between places: the global pattern

  • In 2022, 1.12 billion people lived in slums or informal settlements: 24.8% of the urban population.
  • Over 85% were in three regions: Eastern and South-Eastern Asia (362 million), Central and Southern Asia (334 million) and sub-Saharan Africa (265 million).
  • Informal jobs as a share of all jobs, 2024 (ILO modelled): low-income countries 88.3%; lower-middle 81.5%; upper-middle 50.8%; high-income 12.9%.
  • Inequality varies between places with development, and within every city.
Mission 6

Within one city: Mumbai

  • Greater Mumbai, Census 2011, in the city's own ward tables:
    • D ward: 34,699 of 346,866 people in slums (10.0%, calculated).
    • M/East ward: 685,994 of 807,720 (84.9%, calculated).
  • Praja Foundation, citing Census 2011, gives 77.5% for M/East.
  • The share depends on who defines and counts slums. Both sources put M/East above 3 in 4.
Mission 6

Daily life in M/East

Figures from Apnalaya, an NGO working in the ward, reported by Praja Foundation:

  • Health: infant mortality 66 per 1,000 live births, against India's 41. Average age at death: 39.
  • Employment: "Every second male and six out of seven females are unemployed."
  • Environment: a landfill receiving about 4,500 tonnes of waste a day; contaminated air and groundwater.
  • Services: the ward ranked last of Mumbai's 24 wards on human development (2009), and had the city's most complaints about water supply.
Mission 6

Why the gap?

  • Land values: formal housing takes the most valuable land; low-income households are pushed to cheaper, less serviced land.
  • Migration: since the 1960s M/East "has given shelter to migrants from different parts of India".
  • Policy: it also took people "'resettled' from slums in the inner recesses of the city".
  • Services: weaker water, health and schooling make it hard to escape low pay.
  • Exam move: name the factor, then the evidence, then the effect on daily life.
Quick check
?In the city's Census 2011 tables, 6,534,460 of Greater Mumbai's 12,442,373 people lived in slums. What percentage is that?
Mission 6

LEDC city: Mumbai's issues

  • Informal settlement growth: migrants since the 1960s and people resettled from inner-city slums filled M/East. Citywide, 52.5% (city tables) or 41.84% (Praja) lived in slums in 2011.
  • Service provision: M/East's 807,720 people had 77 schools and 10 hospitals and dispensaries, and the most water-supply complaints in the city.
  • Economic activity: India is a lower-middle-income country, where 81.5% of jobs are informal across that income group. Informal work means no contract, pension or sick pay.
Mission 6

MEDC inner city: New York

  • Deprivation: in 1990, 22 New York neighbourhoods were classed as low-income.
  • Re-urbanisation: the city's population fell from 7,891,957 (1950) to 7,071,639 (1980), then rose to 7,322,564 (1990).
  • Gentrification: 15 of the 22 were "gentrifying": average rents rose 34.3% (1990 to 2010–14), against 13.2% in the other low-income areas.
  • The share of college-educated adults in gentrifying areas rose by about 15.6 percentage points.
  • Limit: the Furman Center "cannot directly observe if low-income residents are moving out".
Mission 6

England: deprivation persists

  • The most deprived area is east of Jaywick, Clacton-on-Sea; seven of the ten most deprived are in Blackpool.
  • Deprivation clusters in large conurbations, former heavy-industry and mining areas, coastal towns and parts of east London.
  • 82% of the most deprived tenth of areas in 2025 were also in the most deprived tenth in 2019.
Mission 6

The cycle of deprivation

  • Low income → poor housing and diet → poorer health and schooling → fewer qualifications → low-paid or no work → low income for the next generation.
  • Area effects reinforce it: weak services, crime, and a poor reputation that deters investment.
  • Breaking it needs action at several points at once.
Mission check

?In England, 82% of the most deprived tenth of neighbourhoods in 2025 were also in the most deprived tenth in 2019. Seven of the ten most deprived are in Blackpool. Which explanation fits best?

The Mumbai gap and England's pattern share causes: land and housing markets, history (migration, resettlement, lost industry) and the cycle of deprivation. MCGM's tables give 10.0% (D) against 84.9% (M/East).

Mission 7

Mission 7: Dhaka grew from 3 million people in 1980 to over 18 million by 2017. Who gains and who loses when a city grows that fast?

Make a guess: Dhaka grew from 3 million people in 1980 to over 18 million by 2017. Who gains and who loses when a city grows that fast?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • AS2.2c.ii LEDC city: growth of informal settlements, service provision and economic activity (one urban case study)
Mission 7

How big is Dhaka? It depends on the boundary

  • UN, 2025: Dhaka had almost 37 million inhabitants, the world's second most populous city after Jakarta.
  • The UN now draws cities as continuous dense built-up areas, so it counts far more people than live inside Dhaka's two city corporations.
  • Always quote a city's population with its source and definition.
Mission 7

Why Dhaka grows

  • Natural increase plus rural–urban migration: push (low rural incomes, hazards) and pull (jobs, services).
  • Urban primacy: Bangladesh's largest city held 42.2% of the country's urban population in 2024.
  • In MEDC/LEDC terms, Dhaka is an LEDC city.
Mission 7

Economic activity: the formal economy

  • Ready-made garments (RMG) made up 80.6% of Bangladesh's exports in 2025–26.
  • Greater Dhaka hosts 80% of the country's export-oriented garment factories.
  • In the 1.4 million garment jobs covered by the ILO–IFC Better Work programme, 49% of workers are women.
  • Formal jobs bring regular wages, which migrants often send home to rural families.
Mission 7

Economic activity: the informal economy

  • Informal economy: work that is not registered, taxed or protected by labour law.
  • Examples: street vending, rickshaw pulling, domestic work, waste picking, small workshops.
  • It absorbs migrants who cannot find formal jobs, but pay is irregular, with no contract, sick pay or pension.
Quick check
?Which job is part of the informal economy?
Mission 7

Growth of informal settlements

  • Informal settlements (slums): housing built without permission or secure tenure, often lacking durable walls, space, safe water or sanitation.
  • In 2022, 51.5% of Bangladesh's urban population lived in slums (UN-Habitat estimate).
  • They grow because migration outpaces formal housing and services, and land near jobs is scarce.
Mission 7

Service provision

  • Water and sanitation: networks and drainage struggle to keep up with growth.
  • Transport: average driving speed fell from 21 km/h to less than 7 km/h in ten years (World Bank).
  • Congestion wasted about 3.2 million working hours a day.
  • Exam move: for "service provision", name the service, the shortfall and who bears it.
Quick check
?Which is a cost of rapid growth for the city as a whole, rather than for one household?
Mission 7

Safety at work

  • On 24 April 2013 the Rana Plaza building, home to garment factories in Greater Dhaka, collapsed.
  • It killed 1,138 workers and left more than 2,500 injured.
  • Jobs brought income, but weak building and safety enforcement put workers at risk.
Mission 7

Exam move: using the case study

  • Organise Dhaka under CCEA's three issues: informal settlement growth, service provision, economic activity.
  • Sort impacts by who (migrants, established residents, businesses, the state), and end with a judgement: who gains, who loses, and under what conditions.
Mission check

?A fast-growing city clears an informal settlement near its centre to build an expressway, and moves the residents to plots 30 km out. Who is most likely to lose most?

Rapid growth brings jobs and income, especially for women in garments, but the costs (informal housing, overstretched services, unsafe work, congestion) fall most on poorer residents, and even "improvements" can shift costs onto them. Who gains depends on who you are.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Settlements form a rural–urban continuum; "urban" thresholds range from 200 (Denmark) to 50,000 (Japan).

Mission 2

  • The rural–urban fringe is where greenfield development meets farmland.

Mission 3

  • Greenfield = never built on; brownfield = previously developed land. Suburbanisation and counterurbanisation push development onto the rural–urban fringe; green belts (12.5% of England) resist sprawl.
  • England built 54% of new addresses on previously developed land in 2021–22; brownfield is generally more sustainable but costlier to clean up.

Mission 4

  • Planning protects contested countryside; ASSIs/SSSIs protect sites (scientific), AONBs protect landscapes (four NCALO purposes), National Parks add a management body in GB.
  • Mournes proposal (2002–07): local opposition, especially farmers and landowners; for: funding, jobs, protection, tourism; against: house prices, second homes, access conflicts, traffic, seasonal jobs. Impacts depend on legislation and the Park plan.

Mission 5

  • New York's inner neighbourhoods lost people in the 1970s (−25.9% in areas that later gentrified) and were deprived in 1990 (32.9% in poverty v 19.4% citywide).
  • Re-urbanisation followed (city 7.07m in 1980 → 8.81m in 2020), with gentrification in 15 of 22 low-income areas: rents +34.3%, college share +15.6 points, rent burden up, poverty share down slightly.

Mission 6

  • Inequality is measured with several domains: income, employment, health, education, housing and access to services.
  • Land values, migration and resettlement concentrate deprivation in some wards; the cycle of deprivation keeps it there.
  • LEDC city: Mumbai (informal settlements, services, informal work). MEDC inner city: New York (deprivation, re-urbanisation, gentrification).

Mission 7

  • Dhaka's growth (almost 37 million in 2025 on the UN definition) is driven by natural increase and migration to garment and informal jobs; informal settlements and services lag behind.
