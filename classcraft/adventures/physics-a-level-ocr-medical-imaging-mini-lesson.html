Mission 1

Mission 1: How does slamming electrons into a lump of metal make X-rays?

Make a guess: How does slamming electrons into a lump of metal make X-rays?

Mission 1

The X-ray tube

  • Heater (cathode): a hot filament releases electrons by thermionic emission.
  • High-voltage supply: tens of kV to about 150 kV between cathode and anode.
  • Electrons accelerate across the vacuum and hit the target metal (usually tungsten) on the anode.
  • Each electron arrives with kinetic energy eV: at 100 kV, 100 keV.
  • A lead housing absorbs X-rays except through a small window.
Mission 1

Where the energy goes

  • Less than 1% of the electrons' energy becomes X-rays. The rest heats the target.
  • Tungsten: very high melting point (about 3400 °C) and high proton number (74), which makes X-ray production more efficient.
  • A rotating anode spreads the heat round a ring instead of one spot.
  • Oil around the tube carries heat away.
Quick check
?An X-ray tube runs at 100 kV with a beam current of 5.0 mA. About how much power heats the target?
Mission 1

Braking radiation: the continuous spectrum

  • An electron passing close to a nucleus is decelerated and emits an X-ray photon.
  • It can lose any amount of energy, up to all of it → photons of all energies up to a maximum: a continuous spectrum.
  • Maximum photon energy = eV (the electron's whole kinetic energy).
  • Minimum wavelength: λmin = hc/eV. At 100 kV, λmin = 1.2 × 10⁻¹¹ m.
  • Low-energy photons are mostly absorbed in the tube window and filter, so the curve falls to zero at low energy.
Mission 1

Characteristic lines: jumps between energy levels

  • A fast electron knocks an inner-shell electron out of a tungsten atom.
  • An electron from a higher energy level drops into the vacancy.
  • It emits one X-ray photon: hf = ΔE, the difference between the two levels.
  • The levels are fixed for tungsten → sharp lines at fixed energies on top of the continuous spectrum.
  • Tungsten's K lines: about 59 keV and 67 keV. They appear only if eV exceeds the K-shell binding energy (about 69.5 keV).
Quick check
?In a tungsten atom, an electron drops from an energy level at −11.5 keV into a vacancy in the level at −69.5 keV. What is the wavelength of the X-ray photon emitted? (h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹, e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C)
Mission 1

Controlling the beam

  • Tube current (more electrons per second) → more photons: higher intensity, same spectrum shape, same maximum energy.
  • Tube voltage higher → higher maximum photon energy, more penetrating ("harder") beam, and higher intensity.
  • An aluminium filter absorbs low-energy photons. They would be absorbed in the patient, adding dose, and never reach the detector.
  • A lead collimator narrows the beam to the area being imaged.
Quick check
?An X-ray tube operates at 60 kV. What is the maximum energy of an X-ray photon it produces? (e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C)
Mission check

?A tungsten-target X-ray tube is first run at 80 kV, then at 100 kV, with the same tube current. Which statement describes how the X-ray spectrum changes?

Two processes: electrons braked by nuclei give a continuous spread up to eV; electrons knocking out inner electrons give lines at energies set by the target's energy levels.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why does bone cast a sharp shadow on an X-ray image while most soft organs barely show?

Make a guess: Why does bone cast a sharp shadow on an X-ray image while most soft organs barely show?

Mission 2

A conventional X-ray image

  • An X-ray image is a shadow: X-rays pass through the patient onto a detector.
  • Tissue that attenuates (absorbs or scatters) more lets fewer X-rays through.
  • Fewer X-rays reach the detector → that area shows light. Bone looks white; air in the lungs looks dark.
  • Detector: a digital flat panel, or film behind an intensifying screen that turns X-rays into light.
  • It is 2D: structures at different depths overlap.
Mission 2

Four ways a photon is attenuated

  • Simple scatter (low energy, about 1–20 keV): photon bounces off an electron, same energy, new direction.
  • Photoelectric effect (main below about 30 keV in soft tissue; important up to ~100 keV, especially in heavy atoms): photon absorbed, inner electron ejected.
  • Compton effect (main in soft tissue from about 30 keV up to about 20 MeV): photon gives part of its energy to an electron and scatters with less energy.
  • Pair production (only above 1.02 MeV): photon near a nucleus becomes an electron and a positron.
  • Diagnostic X-rays (about 20–150 keV): photoelectric and Compton matter. Pair production never happens.
Quick check
?An 80 keV X-ray photon interacts in a patient. Leaving the point of interaction are a 70 keV X-ray photon, travelling in a new direction, and a fast electron. Which mechanism was this?
Mission 2

Why bone stands out

  • Photoelectric absorption rises steeply with proton number Z (roughly Z³) and falls as photon energy rises.
  • Bone contains calcium (Z = 20) and phosphorus (Z = 15). Soft tissue is mostly H, C, N, O (Z ≤ 8).
  • So bone absorbs far more strongly than soft tissue: high contrast.
  • Soft tissues are all similar in Z and density: low contrast between organs.
  • Compton scattering depends mainly on electron density, which is similar in all soft tissues. It adds little contrast, and scattered photons reaching the detector fog the image.
Mission 2

Exponential attenuation

  • I = I0e−μx: μ is the attenuation (absorption) coefficient of the material at that photon energy; x is thickness.
  • Each equal thickness removes the same fraction of the beam.
  • Half-value thickness: x½ = ln 2 / μ.
  • ln I against x: straight line, gradient −μ.
  • Example: μ = 0.20 cm⁻¹, x = 10 cm. I/I0 = e−0.20 × 10 = e−2.0 = 0.14. x½ = 0.693 ÷ 0.20 = 3.5 cm.
Quick check
?A narrow beam of X-rays passes through 4.0 mm of a material whose attenuation coefficient at that photon energy is 0.35 mm⁻¹. What fraction of the intensity is transmitted?
Mission 2

Contrast media

  • Soft organs can be made visible by filling them with a high-Z substance: a contrast medium.
  • Barium (Z = 56): barium sulfate swallowed (barium meal) or given as an enema. Outlines the stomach and intestines.
  • Barium sulfate is insoluble, so it passes through the gut without being absorbed.
  • Iodine (Z = 53): an iodine-based liquid injected into the blood. Outlines blood vessels and the kidneys.
  • Both absorb strongly by the photoelectric effect at diagnostic energies.
Quick check
?Suppose an iodine contrast medium raises the attenuation coefficient of the blood in a 0.50 cm wide blood vessel from 0.20 cm⁻¹ to 2.0 cm⁻¹. By what factor does the X-ray intensity transmitted across the vessel change?
Mission 2

The CT (CAT) scanner

  • An X-ray tube rotates around the patient, producing a thin fan-shaped beam.
  • A ring of detectors measures the transmitted intensity from every angle.
  • Computer software combines these measurements to reconstruct a cross-sectional slice.
  • The patient moves through on a table → many slices → a 3D image on a display.
Mission 2

CT scan or ordinary X-ray?

  • CT advantages: separates soft tissues with only small differences in attenuation.
  • No overlap: each slice shows structures at one depth, so a tumour's size and position can be measured accurately.
  • The 3D image can be viewed from any angle.
  • Disadvantages: much higher dose (dose to the body, in millisieverts, mSv). A chest CT gives about 6.6 mSv; a chest X-ray about 0.014 mSv: roughly 500 times more.
  • 6.6 mSv ≈ 3 years of average UK natural background radiation. CT is also much more expensive.
Quick check
?Why does the X-ray tube in a CT scanner rotate around the patient during a scan?
Mission check

?A child has swallowed a small metal coin and a plastic bead of the same size. On the X-ray image the coin shows clearly, but the bead can hardly be seen. Which is the best explanation?

Contrast comes from differences in attenuation. High-Z atoms (calcium in bone, metal, barium, iodine) absorb strongly by the photoelectric effect; soft tissues are alike, so they barely show.

Mission 3

Mission 3: How can a hospital picture what an organ is doing, not just what it looks like?

Make a guess: How can a hospital picture what an organ is doing, not just what it looks like?

Mission 3

Medical tracers

  • Tracer: a radioactive nuclide attached to a compound the body uses.
  • Injected, swallowed or inhaled. It collects where that compound is taken up: bone, kidney, heart, thyroid.
  • Its gamma photons leave the body and are detected outside it.
  • The image shows function (where the tracer goes), not just structure.
Mission 3

Technetium-99m

  • ⁹⁹ᵐ43Tc → ⁹⁹43Tc + γ. "m" = metastable: an excited nucleus.
  • Emits gamma only (140 keV): penetrates out of the body, little ionisation inside.
  • Half-life ≈ 6 hours: long enough to prepare and scan, short enough to keep the dose low.
  • Made in the hospital from molybdenum-99 (half-life ≈ 66 hours) in a generator.
  • Easily attached to many compounds, so it can target many organs.
Quick check
?A proposed tracer emits beta particles as well as gamma photons. Why is it a poorer choice than technetium-99m for a gamma-camera scan?
Mission 3

Worked example: activity

  • A dose of Tc-99m (half-life 6.0 h) has activity 800 MBq at 07:00.
  • Scan at 13:00: 6.0 h = 1 half-life → 400 MBq.
  • Next morning, 07:00: 24 h = 4 half-lives → 800 × (½)⁴ = 50 MBq.
  • Or use A = A0e−λt, with λ = ln 2 / t½.
Quick check
?A patient must receive 300 MBq of technetium-99m (half-life 6.0 h) at 15:00. The dose is prepared at 09:00. What activity must it have when it is prepared?
Mission 3

The gamma camera

  • Collimator: a thick lead plate full of long, parallel holes. Only photons travelling along the holes get through.
  • Scintillator: a large sodium iodide crystal. Each gamma photon makes a flash of thousands of visible-light photons.
  • Photomultiplier tubes: an array behind the crystal. Light frees electrons (photoelectric effect); the electrons are multiplied into a voltage pulse.
  • Computer: works out where each flash happened. Display: shows the image.
Mission 3

Forming the image

  • Collimator: a photon reaching a point on the crystal came from the body directly in front of it.
  • The tubes nearest a flash give the biggest pulses: the computer finds the flash position from the pattern.
  • Pulse size depends on photon energy: pulses from scattered photons (lower energy) are rejected.
  • Counts build up over minutes. More counts → brighter point → more tracer there.
  • Longer, narrower holes: sharper image but fewer counts (longer scan or more tracer).
Quick check
?A gamma camera is run with its collimator removed. What happens to the image?
Mission 3

Diagnosis with a gamma camera

  • Bone scan: tracer collects where bone is most active. Bright "hot spots": fractures healing, infection, cancer spread.
  • Kidneys: counts against time show how fast each kidney takes up and clears the tracer.
  • Heart: tracer in the blood supply to the heart muscle; a dark area means poor blood flow.
  • Thyroid: over- or under-active regions.
  • Fine detail is poorer than X-ray or CT (around a centimetre). Its strength: it shows function.
Mission check

?A patient is injected with technetium-99m attached to tiny particles. The blood carries them to the lungs, where they lodge in the smallest blood vessels. One region of a lung shows almost no counts. What is the most likely explanation?

A tracer goes where the body sends it, and its gamma photons are counted outside. The picture is a map of activity, which is why it shows function.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Matter and antimatter meet inside a patient. How does that help doctors find a tumour?

Make a guess: Matter and antimatter meet inside a patient. How does that help doctors find a tumour?

Mission 4

Fluorine-18

  • A positron (β⁺) emitter: ¹⁸9F → ¹⁸8O + ⁰₊1e + νₑ.
  • Half-life ≈ 110 minutes. Made in a cyclotron.
  • Attached to a glucose-like molecule: FDG (fluorodeoxyglucose).
  • FDG collects where cells use most glucose: many tumours, active brain regions, heart muscle.
Mission 4

Annihilation

  • The positron travels about a millimetre in tissue, then meets an electron.
  • They annihilate: their mass becomes two gamma photons.
  • Total momentum before ≈ 0 → the photons go in opposite directions.
  • Energy: each photon ≈ mₑc² = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × (3.00 × 10⁸)² = 8.2 × 10⁻¹⁴ J = 0.511 MeV.
Quick check
?Sometimes the positron–electron pair is still moving a little when it annihilates. How does this affect the two gamma photons?
Mission 4

The PET scanner

  • A ring of detectors around the patient: scintillator crystals + photomultiplier tubes.
  • Coincidence detection: two photons arriving within a few nanoseconds came from one annihilation.
  • The annihilation lies on the straight line between those two detectors.
  • Single photons with no partner are rejected.
  • Millions of lines → the computer reconstructs a 3D map of tracer concentration → display, in slices.
Mission 4

Worked example: where on the line?

  • Both photons travel at c. The one from nearer the detector arrives first.
  • Offset from the midpoint of the line: x = cΔt/2.
  • Δt = 0.40 ns: x = (3.00 × 10⁸ × 0.40 × 10⁻⁹) ÷ 2 = 0.060 m.
  • So the annihilation was 6.0 cm from the midpoint, towards the detector that fired first.
  • Why ÷ 2: moving the point by x shortens one path by x and lengthens the other by x.
Quick check
?In a PET scanner, detector A records its photon 0.20 ns before detector B, on the opposite side of the ring, records the other photon of the pair. Where did the annihilation occur?
Mission 4

Diagnosis using PET

  • FDG maps glucose use: bright areas use more.
  • Cancer: finding tumours and spread, and checking response to treatment.
  • Brain: areas of reduced activity, as in some dementias; the source of some seizures.
  • Heart: which muscle is still alive after a heart attack.
  • Often combined with CT (PET–CT): function from PET, anatomy from CT, in one image.
Mission 4

PET or gamma camera?

  • PET: 3D, sharper (a few millimetres), more sensitive; shows function.
  • PET: very expensive scanner and running costs. F-18's short half-life needs a cyclotron nearby or fast delivery.
  • Patient dose from the tracer (and from CT, if combined).
  • Gamma camera: cheaper, Tc-99m made on site from a generator, widely available; lower resolution.
  • Hospitals must decide: cost, how many patients benefit, travel to a regional centre.
Quick check
?A dose of fluorine-18 (half-life 110 min) takes 4.0 hours to reach a hospital from the cyclotron that made it. What fraction of its activity remains on arrival?
Mission check

?A patient is given FDG. Over 20 minutes the PET scanner records millions of coincident photon pairs. The lines joining each pair of detectors cross most often in one small region of the left lung. What does this show?

Each annihilation sends two photons back to back. Coincident pairs draw lines through the tracer, and where many lines cross, glucose use, and often a tumour, shows up.

Mission 5

Mission 5: How can a sound nobody can hear draw a picture of a baby before it is born?

Make a guess: How can a sound nobody can hear draw a picture of a baby before it is born?

Mission 5

What ultrasound is

  • Ultrasound: a longitudinal sound wave with frequency above 20 kHz, beyond human hearing.
  • Medical scanners use about 1–15 MHz.
  • Speed in soft tissue: about 1540 m s⁻¹ (scanners assume this value).
  • λ = c/f: at 5.0 MHz, λ = 1540 ÷ (5.0 × 10⁶) ≈ 0.31 mm.
  • Short wavelength is why it can show small structures.
Mission 5

The piezoelectric transducer

  • Piezoelectric effect: a p.d. across certain crystals (e.g. quartz, lead zirconate titanate) makes them change shape; squeezing them produces a p.d.
  • Emitting: an alternating p.d. at the crystal's resonant frequency makes it vibrate strongly → ultrasound.
  • Receiving: a returning echo squeezes and stretches the crystal → a small alternating p.d.
  • One transducer does both: it sends a short pulse, then listens for echoes.
  • A backing block damps the crystal so each pulse is only a few cycles long.
Quick check
?A 3.0 MHz ultrasound pulse is sent out. Its weak echo returns to the same crystal. What does the crystal produce?
Mission 5

Pulse-echo: finding a position

  • Send a short pulse. Time how long until the echo returns from a boundary.
  • The pulse travels there and back: distance to boundary d = ct/2.
  • Echoes happen where the wave is partly reflected, at boundaries between different media.
  • Example: an echo returns after 65 μs in soft tissue.
  • d = 1540 × 65 × 10⁻⁶ ÷ 2 = 0.050 m = 5.0 cm deep.
Quick check
?A ship sends a sound pulse straight down. The echo from the seabed returns 0.40 s later. The speed of sound in seawater is 1500 m s⁻¹. How deep is the seabed below the ship?
Mission 5

What limits the detail?

  • Wavelength: a structure much smaller than λ gives no clear echo (the wave diffracts round it). Detail finer than about λ is lost.
  • Higher frequency → shorter λ → finer detail.
  • But higher frequencies are absorbed more by tissue, so they cannot reach as deep.
  • Pulse duration τ: echoes from two boundaries closer than about cτ/2 overlap and merge into one.
  • Example: a 2-cycle pulse at 5.0 MHz lasts 0.40 μs → cτ/2 = 1540 × 0.40 × 10⁻⁶ ÷ 2 ≈ 0.3 mm.
  • So: short pulses and short wavelengths give more information.
Quick check
?A pulse-echo scanner shows a single echo where there are really two boundaries 0.2 mm apart. Which change is most likely to let it show two separate echoes?
Mission 5

A-scan

  • A-scan (A for amplitude): one beam, one direction.
  • Display: echo amplitude against time. Each spike = one boundary.
  • Spike's time → depth (d = ct/2). Gap between spikes → thickness of a layer.
  • Used to measure distances along one line, e.g. the length of the eyeball.
Mission 5

B-scan

  • B-scan (B for brightness): each echo becomes a dot on screen.
  • Dot position: from the beam's direction and the echo's time.
  • Dot brightness: from the echo's strength.
  • A probe with a row of transducers (or a swept beam) sends many lines → a 2D image of a slice.
  • Repeated many times a second: a real-time moving image.
Mission 5

Strengths and limits

  • Non-ionising: no X-rays. Considered safe at diagnostic intensities; used routinely in pregnancy.
  • Cheap and portable compared with CT or MRI. Real-time: shows moving organs and blood flow.
  • Cannot see through gas or bone: at a gas boundary about 99.9% of the ultrasound is reflected; bone reflects a large fraction and absorbs most of the rest.
  • Finer detail needs higher frequency, which reaches less deep.
  • Image quality depends on the operator's skill.
Quick check
?On a B-scan of a fetus, the skull shows as a bright curved line. The fluid around the fetus shows as a dark region. Why?
Mission check

?An A-scan through a soft-tissue layer shows an echo from its front surface and another from its back surface. The second echo arrives 13 μs after the first. The speed of ultrasound in the tissue is 1540 m s⁻¹. How thick is the layer?

Every echo's time gives a depth. A scanner sends thousands of pulses in different directions and plots each echo as a dot: that builds the picture of the baby.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Why does an ultrasound scan need a layer of gel between the probe and the skin?

Make a guess: Why does an ultrasound scan need a layer of gel between the probe and the skin?

Mission 6

Acoustic impedance

  • Acoustic impedance Z = ρc: density × speed of sound in the medium.
  • Unit: kg m⁻² s⁻¹.
  • Typical values (approximate):
  • Air: ~4 × 10² · Soft tissue: ~1.6 × 10⁶ · Bone: ~7.8 × 10⁶ kg m⁻² s⁻¹.
  • Air's Z is about 4000 times smaller than soft tissue's.
Quick check
?The lens on the front of some probes is made of silicone rubber. Its density is 1150 kg m⁻³ and the speed of ultrasound in it is 1020 m s⁻¹. What is its acoustic impedance in kg m⁻² s⁻¹?
Mission 6

Reflection at a boundary

  • At a boundary between media with impedances Z1 and Z2, a fraction is reflected:
  • Ir/I0 = (Z2 − Z1)² / (Z2 + Z1)²
  • Big difference in Z → big reflection. Equal Z → no reflection.
  • The rest is transmitted: It/I0 = 1 − Ir/I0.
  • Example, soft tissue → bone: (7.8 − 1.63)² / (7.8 + 1.63)² ≈ 0.43.
  • 43% reflected, 57% transmitted into the bone, which then absorbs strongly, so little reaches tissue behind it.
Quick check
?Fat has acoustic impedance 1.38 × 10⁶ kg m⁻² s⁻¹ and muscle has 1.70 × 10⁶ kg m⁻² s⁻¹. What percentage of the ultrasound intensity is reflected at a fat–muscle boundary?
Mission 6

Why the gel?

  • Without gel, a thin layer of air sits between probe and skin.
  • Air → skin: (1.6 × 10⁶ − 4 × 10²)² / (1.6 × 10⁶ + 4 × 10²)² ≈ 0.999.
  • 99.9% reflected at the skin. Almost nothing enters the body, and nothing useful returns.
  • Gel has Z close to skin's and pushes out the air → almost all the ultrasound is transmitted.
  • This is impedance (acoustic) matching.
Quick check
?A probe's crystal has an acoustic impedance of about 3.0 × 10⁷ kg m⁻² s⁻¹, far above skin's 1.6 × 10⁶. A thin layer is fixed to the front of the crystal to get more ultrasound into the body. Which acoustic impedance should the layer have?
Mission 6

Doppler ultrasound

  • Ultrasound reflected from moving red blood cells returns with a shifted frequency.
  • Δf/f = 2v cos θ / c
  • v: blood speed · θ: angle between beam and blood flow · c: speed of ultrasound in tissue.
  • Factor 2: the cells are moving receivers, then moving sources; the shift happens twice.
  • Towards the probe: frequency rises. Away: frequency falls.
  • At θ = 90°, cos θ = 0: no shift. The probe is angled along the vessel.
Mission 6

Worked example

  • f = 4.0 MHz, θ = 60°, measured Δf = 780 Hz, c = 1540 m s⁻¹. Find v.
  • Rearrange: v = cΔf / (2f cos θ)
  • v = 1540 × 780 ÷ (2 × 4.0 × 10⁶ × cos 60°)
  • v = 1540 × 780 ÷ (4.0 × 10⁶) = 0.30 m s⁻¹
  • Δf is in the audible range, so scanners often play it as a sound.
Quick check
?A 5.0 MHz Doppler probe makes an angle of 30° with an artery. The frequency shift is 2.6 kHz. The speed of ultrasound in tissue is 1540 m s⁻¹. What is the speed of the blood?
Mission check

?Ultrasound scans give almost no useful image of tissue inside healthy lungs. Which is the best explanation?

Big mismatch in Z, big reflection. Gel removes the air layer and matches skin, so the ultrasound gets in and the echoes get back out.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • X-ray tube: heated cathode, high p.d., tungsten target on the anode; under 1% of the energy becomes X-rays.
  • Continuous spectrum up to a maximum photon energy eV; characteristic lines at fixed energies from inner-shell transitions (hf = ΔE).
  • Current controls the number of photons; voltage controls their maximum energy (and the number).

Mission 2

  • X-ray image = shadow; high-Z, dense material (bone) absorbs more by the photoelectric effect and shows white.
  • Attenuation: simple scatter, photoelectric, Compton, pair production (> 1.02 MeV); I = I0e−μx; barium and iodine contrast media.
  • CT: rotating tube, fan beam, ring of detectors, computer reconstruction of slices; better soft-tissue detail, much higher dose.

Mission 3

  • Tracer: a gamma-emitting nuclide on a compound the body uses. Tc-99m: gamma only, half-life ≈ 6 h, made on site from Mo-99.
  • Gamma camera: collimator → scintillator → photomultiplier tubes → computer → display. Brightness shows where tracer collects.

Mission 4

  • F-18: β⁺ emitter, half-life ≈ 110 min, in FDG. Positron + electron → two 0.511 MeV gamma photons in opposite directions.
  • PET: ring of detectors, coincidence → line; time difference → position (x = cΔt/2); computer builds a 3D map of tracer.

Mission 5

  • Ultrasound: longitudinal, f > 20 kHz; pulse-echo gives depth d = ct/2.
  • Detail is limited by wavelength (higher f, finer detail, less depth) and by pulse duration (boundaries closer than about cτ/2 merge).
  • A piezoelectric transducer both emits and receives; A-scan = amplitude against time (one line), B-scan = brightness dots building a 2D image.

Mission 6

  • Z = ρc; Ir/I0 = (Z2 − Z1)²/(Z2 + Z1)². Large mismatch (tissue–air, tissue–bone) → strong reflection.
  • Gel matches the probe to skin and removes air: impedance matching.
  • Doppler: Δf/f = 2v cos θ/c gives the speed of blood.
