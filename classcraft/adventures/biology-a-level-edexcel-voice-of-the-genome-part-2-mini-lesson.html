Mission 1

Mission 1: Your body cells carry chromosomes in pairs. How can you tell which two belong together?

Make a guess: Your body cells carry chromosomes in pairs. How can you tell which two belong together?

Mission 1

Chromosomes come in pairs

  • Most body cells hold two sets of chromosomes: one set from each parent.
  • Two matching chromosomes form a homologous pair.
  • Homologues: same size, same genes at the same positions (loci).
  • They may carry different alleles (versions) of those genes.
  • Say: "homologous chromosomes carry the same genes" — not: "the same alleles".
Mission 1

A gene's address: the locus

  • Locus (plural loci): the location of a gene on a chromosome.
  • A gene's locus is the same in every member of the species.
  • Both alleles of a gene sit at the same locus: one on each homologue.
  • Example: the ABO blood group gene has its locus on chromosome 9. Both chromosome 9s carry it.
  • Say: "different alleles, same locus" — not: "each allele has its own locus".
Quick check
?A person has two different alleles of the ABO blood group gene, which has its locus on chromosome 9. Where are these two alleles in one of the person's body cells?
Mission check

?A genome database gives the locus of the CFTR gene, which codes for the protein affected in cystic fibrosis, as a position on chromosome 7. Which statement is correct?

A locus is a gene's fixed address. Homologues share every address; only the alleles living there can differ.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Your body makes about four million new cells a second. How does each one get a full set of chromosomes?

Make a guess: Your body makes about four million new cells a second. How does each one get a full set of chromosomes?

Mission 2

One cell becomes two

  • A parent cell (often called the mother cell) divides into two daughter cells.
  • The parent does not survive as a third cell: its contents are shared out.
  • First the nucleus divides: mitosis.
  • Then the cytoplasm divides: cytokinesis.
  • Not every cell keeps dividing: most mature nerve cells never do.
Mission 2

What mitosis is for

  • Growth: more cells.
  • Repair and replacement: cells lost by wounds or wear.
  • Asexual reproduction: the offspring are genetically identical to the parent.
  • Each daughter cell gets the same chromosome number and the same genes: genetic constancy.
  • Say: "growth needs cell division to add cells" — not: "organs grow just by their cells getting bigger".
Mission 2

Copy first: two chromatids

  • Before a cell divides, every DNA molecule is replicated.
  • This happens in S phase of interphase.
  • Each chromosome is then two identical sister chromatids, joined at the centromere.
  • They stay joined until anaphase.
  • One chromatid = one DNA molecule. Two joined chromatids = one chromosome.
  • Say: "replication gives each chromosome two sister chromatids" — not: "replication doubles the chromosome number".
Quick check
?A cell of an organism has 8 chromosomes before its DNA is replicated. How many chromosomes and how many DNA molecules are in its nucleus at metaphase of mitosis?
Mission 2

The cell cycle

  • Interphase (G1 → S → G2), then mitosis, then cytokinesis.
  • G1: the cell grows, makes proteins, duplicates organelles.
  • S: DNA is replicated.
  • G2: more growth and protein synthesis; the cell prepares to divide.
  • Interphase takes most of the cycle. It is not a resting stage.
  • Many cells leave the cycle and stop dividing; some can be stimulated to divide again.
Quick check
?Which statement correctly gives the sequence of phases in the cell cycle?
Mission 2

Mitosis in four stages

  • Prophase: chromosomes condense (shorter, thicker, visible). The nuclear envelope breaks down. The spindle forms.
  • Metaphase: chromosomes line up on the equator. Spindle fibres from both poles attach at each centromere.
  • Anaphase: centromeres divide. Sister chromatids are pulled to opposite poles; the spindle fibres shorten.
  • Telophase: chromatids reach the poles and uncoil. A nuclear envelope forms round each set. The spindle breaks down.
  • Say: "sister chromatids separate" — not: "homologous chromosomes separate".
Quick check
?A body cell has 4 chromosomes. It is in anaphase of mitosis. What is moving towards each pole of the cell?
Mission 2

Cytokinesis

  • Mitosis divides the nucleus. Cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm.
  • It usually begins during telophase.
  • Animal cell: the membrane is pulled in at the equator, forming a cleavage furrow that pinches the cell in two.
  • Plant cell: the rigid wall cannot be pinched. Vesicles gather at the equator and fuse into a cell plate.
  • The cell plate becomes new membranes and a new wall. It grows outwards until it joins the old wall.
  • Say: "spindle microtubules move chromosomes" — not: "the spindle pinches the cell in two".
Quick check
?A dividing cell is examined after its two new nuclei have formed. Many small vesicles are seen gathering and fusing in a line across the equator of the cell, midway between the nuclei. Which conclusion is justified?
Mission check

?A population of a hypothetical freshwater plant reproduces only by fragmentation: pieces break off a parent plant and grow into new plants by mitosis. Which claim about the new plants is best supported?

Copy every chromosome, then pull the copies apart: each new cell gets a full, identical set. Four million times a second, or once in a broken-off fragment.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Each parent passes on chromosomes, yet the number never doubles. How is it halved so exactly?

Make a guess: Each parent passes on chromosomes, yet the number never doubles. How is it halved so exactly?

Mission 3

Why halve?

  • Body cells are diploid (2n): two sets of chromosomes, one set from each parent. Human 2n = 46.
  • Gametes are haploid (n): one set. Human n = 23.
  • Fertilisation fuses two gametes: n + n = 2n.
  • Without halving, the number would double every generation.
  • Meiosis is the reduction division: one diploid cell → four haploid cells.
  • Say: "a haploid cell has one of every chromosome" — not: "half of every chromosome".
Mission 3

Homologous pairs

  • Homologous chromosomes: a matching pair, one maternal, one paternal.
  • Same size and shape; the same genes at the same loci.
  • They may carry different alleles of those genes.
  • After replication, each homologue has two identical sister chromatids.
  • Sister chromatids are copies. Homologues are not.
  • Say: "the two chromatids of one chromosome are identical copies" — not: "one chromatid came from each parent".
Quick check
?A diploid nucleus of an organism with 2n = 14 undergoes meiosis. What is produced?

?In a moss, the leafy green plant is haploid. It makes sperm and eggs by mitosis. After fertilisation, the zygote grows into a small diploid stalk with a capsule on top. The capsule releases haploid spores, each of which grows into a new leafy plant. Where in this life cycle does meiosis happen?

Find where 2n becomes n, and you have found meiosis, in a moss, a fern or a human.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Brothers and sisters share both parents, yet each gets a different genetic mix. Where does it come from?

Make a guess: Brothers and sisters share both parents, yet each gets a different genetic mix. Where does it come from?

Mission 4

Three shuffles

  • Every gamete a parent makes carries a new combination of alleles.
  • Shuffle 1: how each homologous pair lines up at metaphase I.
  • Shuffle 2: crossing over between homologues in prophase I.
  • Both shuffles happen in meiosis, so gametes are genetically different from one another.
  • Say: "meiosis makes new combinations of alleles" — not: "meiosis makes new alleles".
Mission 4

Shuffle 1: independent assortment

  • At metaphase I, each homologous pair faces the poles at random.
  • Each pair's orientation is independent of every other pair's.
  • So each gamete gets a random mix of maternal and paternal chromosomes.
  • The term is independent assortment.
  • Say: "each pair lines up independently" — not: "all the maternal chromosomes go to one pole".
Mission 4

Shuffle 2: crossing over

  • In prophase I, homologues pair up (a bivalent).
  • Non-sister chromatids (one from each homologue) break and rejoin at chiasmata.
  • They exchange equivalent sections, so alleles swap between homologues.
  • The result: recombinant chromatids, carrying new combinations of alleles.
  • So even the four cells from one meiosis can all differ.
  • Say: "non-sister chromatids exchange sections" — not: "sister chromatids swap sections".
Quick check
?Which statement describes crossing over during meiosis?
Mission check

?A mutation in a hypothetical species of animal prevents crossing over during meiosis, but meiosis is otherwise normal. Compared with the gametes of a normal individual, which prediction about the gametes of an individual with this mutation is correct?

Take one shuffle away and the others still work. Siblings differ because meiosis shuffles twice and fertilisation deals once more.

Mission 5

Mission 5: If two genes sit on the same chromosome, can they ever be separated?

Make a guess: If two genes sit on the same chromosome, can they ever be separated?

Mission 5

Linked genes

  • Genes whose loci are on the same chromosome are linked.
  • Their alleles travel together into gametes. They do not assort independently.
  • Genes on different chromosomes are not linked: in meiosis I each homologous pair lines up independently of the other, so their alleles mix freely.
  • But in prophase I, crossing over between non-sister chromatids can swap them.
  • A crossover between the two loci gives recombinant chromatids: new combinations of alleles.
  • The closer the loci, the less often a crossover falls between them.
  • Say: "autosomal linkage" — not "sex linkage". Linkage means the same chromosome, any chromosome.
  • So the answer to the mission's question: yes. Crossing over separates linked alleles, but only in some meioses.
Quick check
?In a fruit fly the loci for body colour and wing shape are on the same autosome. A fly inherited one chromosome carrying the alleles G and W and the homologous chromosome carrying g and w, shown as G W and g w beside two vertical lines. Why does this fly not produce GW, Gw, gW and gw gametes in equal numbers?

  • Parental classes are the large ones. They need not be "both dominant" and "both recessive".

Mission 6

Mission 6: A microscope slide freezes a root tip in one instant. How can counting its cells reveal time?

Make a guess: A microscope slide freezes a root tip in one instant. How can counting its cells reveal time?

Mission 6

Read the stage from the chromosomes

  • Interphase: intact nucleus; no separate chromosomes (DNA uncoiled). Most cells look like this.
  • Cells divide out of step, so the commonest stage is the one that lasts longest.
  • Prophase: thread-like chromosomes, scattered; nuclear envelope disappearing.
  • Metaphase: chromosomes in a single line across the equator.
  • Anaphase: two groups of V-shaped chromatids moving apart.
  • Telophase: two clusters at the poles; nuclear envelopes re-forming. In plants, a cell plate between them.
  • Why visible? Condensed chromosomes are short and thick, so they take up stain densely.
  • Say: "intact nucleus, no visible chromosomes = interphase" — not: "= prophase".
Quick check
?Two cells are seen in a stained micrograph of a root tip. Cell 1 has an intact nucleus in which no separate chromosomes can be distinguished. Cell 2 has no nuclear envelope, and its condensed chromosomes lie in a single line across the middle of the cell. Which identification is correct?
Mission 6Practical

Root-tip squash

  • Core practical 5.
  • Cut the last 5 mm of a growing root (onion or garlic). Dividing cells are in the meristem, near the tip.
  • Warm in hydrochloric acid (e.g. 1 mol dm⁻³, about 60 °C, a few minutes). This breaks the middle lamella, so cells separate, and stops division.
  • Rinse; add a stain that binds DNA (e.g. toluidine blue or acetic orcein).
  • Cover with a coverslip and squash with firm, vertical pressure: no sideways slide. Aim for one layer of cells.
  • Find the meristem at low power, then identify stages at high power (×400).
  • Key controls: use only the tip; same acid time and temperature for every root; count every cell in a field, not just the dividing ones.
Quick check
?In a stained root-tip squash, one cell shows two groups of V-shaped chromatids, their points facing opposite ends of the cell. Which explains this appearance?
Mission check

?A student counted 100 cells in a micrograph of a growing root tip. 85 cells had an intact nucleus with no visible chromosomes; 6 had condensed chromosomes scattered through the cell; 3 had chromosomes aligned across the equator; 4 had two groups of chromatids moving apart; 2 had two groups of chromosomes at the poles with nuclear envelopes forming. Which conclusion is correct?

More cells in a stage means more time spent in it. One frozen moment, counted carefully, is a clock.

Mission 7

Mission 7: One early embryo cell can grow into a whole baby. Why can't a cell from your bone marrow?

Make a guess: One early embryo cell can grow into a whole baby. Why can't a cell from your bone marrow?

Mission 7

What makes a stem cell?

  • A stem cell is undifferentiated. It can do two things:
  • (1) Divide again and again, renewing itself.
  • (2) Differentiate into specialised cell types.
  • Most specialised cells cannot do both.
  • Say: "able to divide endlessly" — not: "always dividing". Stem cells can rest between divisions.
Quick check
?Which pair of properties defines a stem cell?
Mission 7

Potency: how many cell types can it make?

  • Totipotent: any cell type, including the placenta and other extra-embryonic tissues. The zygote and the cells of the first few divisions.
  • Pluripotent: any body cell type, but not the placenta. Inner cell mass of the blastocyst: embryonic stem cells.
  • Adult tissues keep stem cells too, but these make only a limited range of types, e.g. bone marrow → blood cells.
  • Say: "totipotent includes the placenta; pluripotent does not" — not: "both mean any cell".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly distinguishes pluripotent stem cells from totipotent stem cells?
Mission check

?A cell is taken from adult bone marrow and grown in a dish. It divides many times. Some daughter cells stay like the parent; others become erythrocytes, neutrophils and other blood cells, but never nerve or muscle cells. Which statement is correct?

An early embryo cell can still become anything; a bone-marrow stem cell is limited to blood.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Homologous chromosomes carry the same genes at the same loci, one set from each parent; they may carry different alleles.

Mission 2

  • Every chromosome is replicated first (two sister chromatids); mitosis then sends one chromatid of each chromosome to each pole, so both daughter cells get an identical, complete set. Cytokinesis then divides the cytoplasm (cleavage furrow in animals, cell plate in plants).
  • The cell cycle: G1 → S (DNA replication) → G2 → mitosis (prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase) → cytokinesis.

Mission 3

  • Meiosis is a reduction division: one DNA replication, two divisions, four haploid cells, so fertilisation restores the diploid number.
  • Meiosis gives four genetically different haploid cells; mitosis gives two identical cells.

Mission 4

  • Independent assortment (orientation of pairs at metaphase I) and crossing over (prophase I) give every gamete a new combination of alleles.

Mission 5

  • Linked genes travel together; crossing over between the loci makes a minority of recombinants.

Mission 6

  • Stages are identified from the chromosomes: none visible (interphase), scattered (prophase), in a line (metaphase), V-shaped groups moving apart (anaphase), clusters at the poles (telophase).

Mission 7

  • A stem cell can divide repeatedly and differentiate into specialised cells.
  • Totipotent: any cell, including placenta. Pluripotent: any body cell.
