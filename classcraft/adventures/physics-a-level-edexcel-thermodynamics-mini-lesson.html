Mission 1

Mission 1: In a cold room, metal feels colder than wood. Is it really at a lower temperature?

Make a guess: In a cold room, metal feels colder than wood. Is it really at a lower temperature?

Quick check
?A 0.20 kg copper block at 80 °C is lowered into a large tank containing 200 kg of water at 20 °C. The water has far more internal energy than the block. Which statement describes the resultant thermal energy transfer?
Mission 1

Which way does energy flow?

  • Temperature decides the direction: net energy transfer is always from higher to lower temperature.
  • Not "more energy to less": a hot cup can lose energy to a huge cold bath.
  • Heat is energy in transit because of a temperature difference. A body does not "contain heat".
  • Thermal equilibrium: no net energy transfer between bodies in contact. They are at the same temperature.
  • If A and B are each in thermal equilibrium with C (a thermometer), A and B are at the same temperature. This is why thermometers work.
Mission 1

The Kelvin scale and absolute zero

  • Absolute zero, 0 K (−273.15 °C): the lowest possible temperature. Particles have their minimum internal energy.
  • The Kelvin (absolute, thermodynamic) scale starts at absolute zero. It does not depend on the property of any one substance.
  • T (K) = θ (°C) + 273.15, usually rounded to + 273.
  • No negative kelvin temperatures. 20 °C = 293 K.
Quick check
?Sample P is warmed from 10 °C to 30 °C. Sample Q is warmed from 290 K to 310 K. Which statement about the two temperature changes is correct?
Mission 1

Internal energy

  • Internal energy = total random kinetic energy + total potential energy of the particles.
  • KE: random motion (vibration in solids; moving about in liquids and gases).
  • PE: from the forces between particles. It depends on their separation.
  • The energies are randomly distributed: particles have a range of energies.
  • Not included: the KE of the whole object moving, or its gravitational PE.
  • Heat it → internal energy rises, and (unless it is changing phase) its temperature rises.
  • Temperature ≠ internal energy. A bath at 40 °C has far more internal energy than a cup at 40 °C: many more particles.
Quick check
?You have 1.0 kg of ice at 0 °C and 1.0 kg of liquid water at 0 °C. Which statement about their internal energies is correct?
Mission check

?A thermometer at room temperature, 20 °C, is placed in a cup of water at 70 °C. Its reading rises for a while and then stays steady. Why does the steady reading give the temperature of the water?

Metal and wood left in the same room reach thermal equilibrium with it: same temperature.

Metal feels colder because it conducts energy away from your warmer hand much faster.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Under the same Sun, beach sand gets scorching while the sea stays cool. Why?

Make a guess: Under the same Sun, beach sand gets scorching while the sea stays cool. Why?

Mission 2

Specific heat capacity

  • Specific heat capacity, c: energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg by 1 K. Unit: J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.
  • ΔE = mcΔθ
  • Water: c ≈ 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. Dry sand: about 800. Same energy per kg → water warms about one fifth as much.
  • A change of 1 °C is a change of 1 K: Δθ needs no conversion.
Mission 2

Worked example

  • 0.500 kg aluminium block, 20 °C → 45 °C. c = 900 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.
  • Temperature rise: 45 − 20 = 25 K.
  • Energy: 0.500 × 900 × 25 = 1.1 × 10⁴ J (11 250 J).
  • With a 50 W heater and no losses: t = 11 250 ÷ 50 = 225 s.
Quick check
?An electric kettle heats 250 g of water from 18 °C to 90 °C. The specific heat capacity of water is 4.18 × 10³ J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. How much energy is transferred to the water? Ignore energy transferred to the kettle and surroundings.
Mission 2

Phase change: the particle picture

  • Heat a solid steadily: its temperature rises, then stops rising while it melts. Same again while it boils.
  • During a phase change, energy goes into potential energy: work is done against the forces between particles.
  • Melting: the fixed arrangement breaks up; the spacing changes only slightly. Boiling: the particles separate completely.
  • Mean kinetic energy stays the same, so the temperature is constant. Internal energy still rises.
  • Boiling: throughout the liquid, at the boiling point. Evaporation: only at the surface, at any temperature.
  • Cooling reverses it: freezing and condensing release energy at constant temperature.
Mission 2

Specific latent heat

  • Specific latent heat: energy needed to change the phase of 1 kg with no temperature change. Unit: J kg⁻¹.
  • ΔE = LΔm: Δm is the mass that changes phase.
  • Water: Lf = 3.34 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹; Lv = 2.26 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹. Boiling separates the particles completely, so it takes far more.
  • 10 g of steam condensing on skin gives out 0.010 × 2.26 × 10⁶ = 2.3 × 10⁴ J. The same water cooling from 100 °C to 37 °C gives only 2.6 × 10³ J more.
Quick check
?0.40 kg of ice at 0 °C is melted and the water is then warmed to 20 °C. Specific latent heat of fusion of ice = 3.34 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹; specific latent heat of vaporisation of water = 2.26 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹; specific heat capacity of water = 4.18 × 10³ J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. What is the total energy that must be supplied?
Mission check

?0.20 kg of water and 0.20 kg of cooking oil, both at 20 °C, are each supplied with 12 kJ of energy. Specific heat capacities: water 4180 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, oil 2000 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. Assume no energy is lost. Which statement about their final temperatures is correct?

Same energy, same mass: the lower c, the bigger temperature rise. Sand's c is about a fifth of water's, so in the same sunshine each kilogram of sand warms about five times as much. The sea also spreads its energy through metres of mixing water and loses some by evaporation; dry sand keeps it in a thin top layer.

Mission 3

Mission 3: How do you measure the energy a material needs, when some always leaks away?

Make a guess: How do you measure the energy a material needs, when some always leaks away?

Mission 3

Electrical methods for latent heat

  • Fusion: crushed ice at 0 °C in two funnels over beakers. Heater in one; the other is a control with no heater.
  • Run for the same time. The control's meltwater comes from the room. Subtract it.
    • Lf = VIt ÷ (mheated − mcontrol)
  • Vaporisation: heater in water on a balance. Once boiling steadily, record the mass lost in time t.
    • Repeat at a second power. The rate of energy loss is the same at the same temperature (100 °C), so it cancels:
    • Lv = (P1 − P2)t ÷ (m1 − m2)
  • ΔE = LΔm.
Mission 3

Worked example: ice

  • Heater: 36.0 W for 300 s → E = 36.0 × 300 = 10 800 J.
  • Meltwater: heated funnel 41.5 g; control funnel 9.0 g.
  • Melted by the heater: 41.5 − 9.0 = 32.5 g = 0.0325 kg.
  • Lf = 10 800 ÷ 0.0325 = 3.32 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹ (data book: 3.34 × 10⁵).
Quick check
?A student finds the specific latent heat of fusion of ice with a heater in a funnel of crushed ice, but sets up no control funnel. The room is warmer than 0 °C. How does her value compare with the true value?
Mission check

?A student measures the specific latent heat of vaporisation of water. A heater keeps water boiling in a beaker on a balance. At 50.0 W, the mass falls by 6.0 g in 600 s. At 80.0 W, it falls by 14.0 g in 600 s. The rate of energy loss to the surroundings is the same in both runs. What is the specific latent heat of vaporisation?

Two ways to beat the leak: insulate to make it small, and design it out (a control, a second power, or a gradient instead of a single reading).

Mission 4

Mission 4: Keep cooling a gas in a sealed box. At what temperature would its pressure reach zero?

Make a guess: Keep cooling a gas in a sealed box. At what temperature would its pressure reach zero?

Mission 4

Pressure and the mole

  • p = F/A, F the force perpendicular to the surface. 1 Pa = 1 N m⁻².
  • A gas pushes perpendicular to every surface it touches.
Mission 4

Boyle's law and its experiment

  • Fixed mass of gas at constant temperature: pV = constant. Halve the volume, double the pressure.
  • Apparatus: air trapped above oil in a sealed tube; a pump pushes the oil up; Bourdon gauge reads p; scale reads V. (Or: syringe sealed to a pressure sensor.)
  • Change V slowly, then wait: compressing warms the gas, and T must return to room temperature.
  • Plot pressure against 1/V: a straight line through the origin.
  • Errors to name: leaks (mass not fixed); temperature not constant; volume of tubing not counted.
Quick check
?Predict first. Students measure the pressure of three samples of gas, each sealed in a rigid container, at several temperatures between 0 °C and 100 °C. The samples are of different gases and different amounts, and each behaves as an ideal gas. For each sample they plot pressure against temperature in °C and get a straight line. They then extend each best-fit line to zero pressure. What do they find?
Mission 4

Absolute zero by extrapolation

  • Fixed volume of gas: measure pressure at several temperatures, 0–100 °C.
  • Plot pressure against θ in °C: a straight line.
  • Extend the line to zero pressure: it meets the axis at about −273 °C.
  • Every gas, any amount: the same intercept.
  • This is absolute zero: 0 K = −273.15 °C.
  • T(K) = θ(°C) + 273.15 (often + 273).
Mission 4

The ideal gas equation

  • pV = NkT (N molecules)
  • k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹ (Boltzmann constant).
  • SI units only: pressure in Pa, V in m³, T in K.
Mission 4

Worked example: a gas cylinder

  • Rigid cylinder of helium: V = 0.020 m³, pressure 2.0 × 10⁵ Pa, θ = 20 °C → T = 293 K.
  • Molecules: N = pV/kT = (2.0 × 10⁵ × 0.020)/(1.38 × 10⁻²³ × 293) = 9.9 × 10²³.
  • Warmed to 60 °C (333 K), V fixed: pressure ∝ T, so the pressure = 2.0 × 10⁵ × 333/293 = 2.3 × 10⁵ Pa.
Quick check
?A flask of volume 250 cm³ contains an ideal gas at a pressure of 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa and a temperature of 300 K. k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹. How many molecules are in the flask?
Mission 4

Reading gas graphs

  • p against V, constant T: a curve (pV = constant). Hotter gas: curve further from the origin. Each curve is an isotherm.
  • pV against p, constant T: horizontal line.
  • V against T (constant p) and p against T (constant V): straight lines through 0 K.
  • On p–V axes, the product pV at a point is proportional to T (fixed amount of gas).
Quick check
?The pressure–volume diagram shows three states, A, B and C, of a fixed amount of ideal gas. Which ranks the temperatures of the gas in the three states, from highest to lowest?
Mission check

?A sealed, rigid container holds an ideal gas at 7 °C. The gas is heated to 287 °C. How does its new pressure compare with its starting pressure?

Pressure ∝ T in kelvin. Every gas line points to the same zero: 0 K = −273 °C, absolute zero.

Mission 5

Mission 5: How fast are the air molecules around you moving right now?

Make a guess: How fast are the air molecules around you moving right now?

Mission 5

The ideal gas model

  • A very large number of particles (atoms or molecules) in rapid, random motion.
  • Particle volume is negligible compared with the volume of the container.
  • Collisions with each other and the walls are perfectly elastic: no kinetic energy lost.
  • Collision time is negligible compared with the time between collisions.
  • No forces between particles except during collisions.
Quick check
?Careful measurements show that at very high pressures a real gas no longer obeys the ideal gas equation closely. Which reasoning, based on the assumptions of the ideal-gas model, best supports this observation?
Mission 5

Pressure from collisions

  • A molecule that rebounds from a wall changes momentum, so the wall pushes on it, and it pushes back on the wall (Newton's third law).
  • Many hits every second → an average force on the wall. Pressure = total perpendicular force ÷ area.
  • Smaller volume (T fixed): same speeds, but hits on each square metre are more frequent → pressure rises.
Quick check
?A sealed, rigid container of ideal gas is heated. What happens to the pressure of the gas, and why?
Mission 5

Temperature measures mean kinetic energy

  • ½m⟨c²⟩ = (3/2)kT, where ⟨c²⟩ is the mean square speed
  • Mean translational kinetic energy per molecule ∝ absolute temperature.
  • Translational: moving from place to place. Molecules such as N2 also spin; that energy is extra.
  • Same temperature → same mean translational kinetic energy, whatever the gas.
  • In this model, as T → 0 K this mean kinetic energy → 0: absolute zero.
  • k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹, the Boltzmann constant.
Mission 5

Worked example: root-mean-square speed

  • rms speed: square every speed, take the mean, then the square root.
  • Speeds 300, 400, 500 m s⁻¹: mean = 400; rms = √((300² + 400² + 500²)/3) = 408 m s⁻¹. Not the same.
  • Nitrogen, N2, at 300 K.
  • m(N2) = 4.65 × 10⁻²⁶ kg.
  • Mean translational kinetic energy = 1.5 × 1.38 × 10⁻²³ × 300 = 6.21 × 10⁻²¹ J.
  • rms speed = √(3kT/m) = √(3 × 1.38 × 10⁻²³ × 300 ÷ 4.65 × 10⁻²⁶) = 517 m s⁻¹.
Quick check
?A sealed container of gas is heated from 27 °C to 177 °C. By what factor does the mean kinetic energy of the gas molecules increase?
Mission 5

Internal energy of an ideal gas

  • Internal energy: total random kinetic energy of the particles + total potential energy from the forces between them.
  • Ideal gas: no forces between particles → no potential energy. U is all kinetic.
  • Monatomic: U = (3/2)NkT
  • U depends only on T (for a fixed amount). Same T → same U, whatever the volume.
Mission check

?A container holds a mixture of hydrogen (H2) and oxygen (O2) at one temperature. An oxygen molecule has 16 times the mass of a hydrogen molecule. What is (rms speed of the hydrogen molecules)/(rms speed of the oxygen molecules)?

Temperature fixes the mean translational kinetic energy. For air (mostly N2) at room temperature that means rms speeds near 500 m s⁻¹, faster than a passenger jet.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Molecules hit the walls at random. How can that add up to a pressure you can calculate exactly?

Make a guess: Molecules hit the walls at random. How can that add up to a pressure you can calculate exactly?

Mission 6

Derivation 1: one molecule

  • Cube of side L, volume V = L³. One molecule, mass m, velocity component u along x.
  • Hits the end wall and rebounds elastically: momentum change = −mu − (+mu) = −2mu.
  • Time to cross and return to the same wall: Δt = 2L/u.
  • Force on the wall = rate of change of momentum = 2mu ÷ (2L/u) = mu²/L.
  • Pressure on that wall = force ÷ area = mu²/L ÷ L² = mu²/V.
Mission 6

Derivation 2: all N molecules

  • Add all N molecules: p = m(u1² + u2² + … + uN²)/V = Nm⟨u²⟩/V.
  • For each molecule c² = u² + v² + w².
  • Random motion, large N: no direction is special, so ⟨u²⟩ = ⟨v²⟩ = ⟨w²⟩ = ⅓⟨c²⟩.
  • pV = ⅓Nm⟨c²⟩
  • Assumptions used: elastic collisions (2mu); negligible molecule size (each crosses distance L); no forces between collisions (constant speed across the box); negligible collision time; large N in random motion (averaging).
Mission 6

Using the kinetic theory equation

  • pV = ⅓Nm⟨c²⟩
  • Nm is the total mass of gas, so Nm/V = ρ and p = ⅓ρ × (mean square speed).
  • Air: p = 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa, ρ = 1.2 kg m⁻³.
  • Mean square speed = 3p/ρ = 2.5 × 10⁵ m² s⁻². rms speed = 500 m s⁻¹.
Quick check
?Nitrogen in a cylinder has a density of 3.40 kg m⁻³. The root-mean-square speed of its molecules is 517 m s⁻¹. Treat it as an ideal gas. What is its pressure?
Mission 6

Linking speed to temperature

  • Theory: pV = ⅓Nm⟨c²⟩. Experiment: pV = NkT.
  • Equate: ⅓Nm⟨c²⟩ = NkT, so m⟨c²⟩ = 3kT.
  • Halve both sides: ½m⟨c²⟩ = (3/2)kT. Mean translational KE ∝ T.
Quick check
?An ideal gas is heated from 27 °C to 927 °C. By what factor does the rms speed of its molecules increase?
Mission check

?Two sealed containers of equal volume are at the same temperature. Each holds the same number N of molecules of an ideal gas. Each molecule of gas Y has 4 times the mass of a molecule of gas X. What is (pressure of Y)/(pressure of X)?

About 10²⁷ collisions on each square metre every second average out. Momentum change per hit × hits per second gives a steady force: pV = ⅓Nm × (mean square speed), which agrees with pV = NkT.

Mission 7

Mission 7: How can we read the surface temperature of a star from its light alone?

Make a guess: How can we read the surface temperature of a star from its light alone?

Mission 7

A perfect absorber and emitter

  • All matter above absolute zero turns some of its internal energy into electromagnetic radiation.
  • A black body absorbs all the radiation that falls on it. None is reflected or transmitted.
  • It is also the best possible emitter at its temperature.
  • Its spectrum depends only on its temperature, not on what it is made of.
  • Stars are very good approximations to black bodies.
Quick check
?Predict first. A black body is heated to a higher temperature. How does its emission spectrum change?
Mission 7

Spectrum curves and temperature

  • Plot intensity per unit wavelength against wavelength: a smooth, continuous curve.
  • Hotter body: more at every wavelength. The peak is higher.
  • Hotter body: the peak moves to shorter wavelength.
  • The total power (area under the curve) rises steeply with temperature.
  • Colour shows this: red-hot (cooler) → yellow → white → bluish (hotter).
Mission 7

Wien's displacement law

  • Peak wavelength is inversely proportional to absolute temperature.
  • λmaxT = 2.898 × 10⁻³ m K
  • T must be in kelvin: T(K) = θ(°C) + 273.15, usually rounded to + 273.
  • The Sun: λmax ≈ 500 nm, so T ≈ 5800 K.
Mission 7

Worked example: a red star

  • A star's spectrum peaks at 850 nm (infrared, just beyond red light).
  • Use metres: 850 nm = 850 × 10⁻⁹ m.
  • T = 2.898 × 10⁻³ ÷ 850 × 10⁻⁹ = 3.4 × 10³ K
  • Longer peak wavelength → cooler surface. This star looks red.
Mission 7

Stefan–Boltzmann law

  • Total power radiated ∝ surface area × T⁴.
  • L = σAT⁴ (L: luminosity, the total power, in W)
  • For a star: L = 4πr²σT⁴
  • σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴ (Stefan constant).
  • T⁴ matters: double T → 16 × the power.
Quick check
?A small metal sphere of radius 5.0 cm is heated to 527 °C. Treat it as a black body and take σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴. What power does the sphere emit as thermal radiation?
Mission 7

Worked example: the size of a star

  • A star's spectrum peaks at 290 nm. Its luminosity is 1.0 × 10²⁸ W.
  • Step 1, temperature (Wien):
  • T = 2.898 × 10⁻³ ÷ 290 × 10⁻⁹ = 1.0 × 10⁴ K
  • Step 2, radius (Stefan): L = 4πr²σT⁴ → r = √(L / 4πσT⁴)
  • r = √(1.0 × 10²⁸ ÷ (4π × 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ × (1.0 × 10⁴)⁴)) = 1.2 × 10⁹ m, about 1.7 times the Sun's radius.
Quick check
?A red giant has a luminosity of 100 L⊙ and a surface temperature of 4000 K. A white dwarf has a luminosity of 0.010 L⊙ and a surface temperature of 16 000 K. Both are modelled as black bodies. What is (radius of the red giant)/(radius of the white dwarf)?
Mission check

?The graph shows the intensity per unit wavelength emitted by a black body as a function of wavelength λ. The absolute temperature of the black body is then doubled. At what wavelength does the spectrum of the heated black body peak?

Peak wavelength ∝ 1/T. So the colour of a star (where its spectrum peaks) gives its surface temperature: bluish stars are hotter than reddish ones.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Net energy transfer goes from higher to lower temperature until thermal equilibrium (same temperature, no net transfer).
  • Internal energy = random KE + intermolecular PE of the particles; absolute temperature measures the average random KE. T (K) ≈ temperature in °C + 273; temperature changes are the same in K and °C.

Mission 2

  • ΔE = mcΔθ. Same energy and mass: lower c, bigger temperature rise.
  • During a phase change, energy raises particle PE at constant temperature: ΔE = LΔm.

Mission 3

  • Latent heat: a control funnel (fusion) or two heater powers (vaporisation) cancel the energy exchanged with the surroundings.

Mission 4

  • Extrapolating pressure against θ (°C) to zero pressure gives absolute zero, −273 °C.
  • pV = NkT.

Mission 5

  • Ideal gas: many particles in random motion, negligible volume, elastic collisions, no forces except in collisions.
  • Pressure comes from momentum changes as particles hit a surface.
  • Mean translational KE per particle ∝ absolute temperature: (3/2)kT. Same T, same mean translational KE; lighter particles move faster.
  • Ideal gas internal energy is all kinetic: U = (3/2)NkT for a monatomic gas.

Mission 6

  • Derivation: Δp = 2mu, Δt = 2L/u, sum over N, ⟨u²⟩ = ⅓⟨c²⟩ → pV = ⅓Nm⟨c²⟩.
  • pV = ⅓Nm⟨c²⟩.
  • Combine with pV = NkT: ½m⟨c²⟩ = (3/2)kT.

Mission 7

  • A black body absorbs all incident radiation; its spectrum depends only on T. Hotter: more at every λ, peak at shorter λ.
  • Wien: λmaxT = 2.898 × 10⁻³ m K.
  • L = σAT⁴.
  • Star's radius: T from Wien, then r from Stefan.
