Mission 1: Why do some Christians baptise babies, while others wait until people can choose for themselves?

Make a guess: Why do some Christians baptise babies, while others wait until people can choose for themselves?

Mission 1

What baptism is

  • Baptism is the ceremony of initiation: Christians believe it makes a person a member of the Church, the Body of Christ.
  • It uses water, with the words "in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit".
  • Jesus was baptised by John in the River Jordan (Matthew 3:13–17), and he sent his followers out "baptising them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 28:19).
  • Most churches baptise babies (infant baptism). Some baptise only people old enough to say they believe (believers' baptism).
  • Most Christians call it a sacrament; Baptists and many Pentecostals call it an ordinance.
Mission 1Read the source

Born of water and Spirit

Jesus to Nicodemus, a Jewish teacher who came to him at night:

> "Most certainly I tell you, unless one is born of water and Spirit, he can't enter into God's Kingdom." (John 3:5)

> "We were buried therefore with him through baptism into death, that just as Christ was raised from the dead through the glory of the Father, so we also might walk in newness of life." (Romans 6:4)

Q1 According to John 3:5, what two things are needed to enter God's Kingdom?

Show answer

Being born of water and of the Spirit.

Q2 In Romans 6:4, what does going into and coming out of the water stand for?

Show answer

Dying and rising with Christ to a new life.

Mission 1

Into the life of the Trinity

  • Baptism is "in the name of" Father, Son and Holy Spirit: Christians believe the person is joined to God's own life.
  • Father: the baptised become God's adopted children.
  • Son: they share in Jesus' death and resurrection (Romans 6:4).
  • Holy Spirit: they receive the Spirit, who makes them holy.
  • Catholic teaching: baptism washes away original sin and all sin, gives grace, and marks the person for ever, so it is given only once.
  • It is the "door" to the other sacraments: no one receives Confirmation or the Eucharist without being baptised.
Mission 1

Infant baptism: the rite

  • Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican, Methodist and Presbyterian churches baptise babies; usually at a parish service.
  • Welcome: the priest or minister traces the sign of the cross on the baby's forehead.
  • Promises: parents and godparents reject evil and profess the faith for the child; the congregation promises support.
  • Water: poured three times over the head (or the baby is dipped), with the Trinity words: a sign of washing from sin and new birth.
  • Catholic rite: anointing with chrism (perfumed oil: sharing in Christ's role as priest, prophet and king), a white garment (clothed with Christ, cleansed from sin), and a candle lit from the Easter (Paschal) candle (the light of the risen Christ).

Mission 1

Why baptise babies?

  • Grace is a gift: God's love comes first; a baby cannot earn it and does not need to.
  • Original sin: Catholics believe baptism frees from original sin, so it should not be delayed (Catholic teaching also entrusts unbaptised children to God's mercy).
  • Whole households were baptised in the early Church (Acts 16:15, 16:33).
  • A covenant sign: Presbyterian and other Reformed Christians compare it to circumcision, given to Jewish baby boys (Colossians 2:11–12).
  • Jesus welcomed children and said the Kingdom belongs to such as these (Mark 10:14).
  • The child later makes the promises personally, at confirmation.
  • Article XXVII (Church of England): "The Baptism of young Children is in any wise to be retained in the Church, as most agreeable with the institution of Christ."
Mission 1

Believers' baptism

  • Practised by Baptists, most Pentecostals and many independent churches, for people old enough to profess faith (often teenagers or adults).
  • Testimony: the candidate tells the church how they came to faith in Jesus.
  • Immersion: the minister and candidate stand in a baptistery (pool in the church floor), river or sea; the candidate is lowered backwards under the water and raised up.
  • Symbolism: dying to the old life, buried with Christ, rising to new life (Romans 6:4).
  • Reasons: Peter said, "Repent and be baptised" (Acts 2:38): faith comes first; Jesus was baptised as an adult; the New Testament never clearly describes a baby being baptised.
  • For Baptists, baptism is an act of obedience and public witness; it does not itself save.
Mission 1Diversity

The debate over infant baptism

  • Catholic: baptism is necessary for salvation for those who know the Gospel, and frees from original sin; babies should be baptised soon after birth; unbaptised adults are baptised after preparation, usually at the Easter Vigil.
  • Orthodox: infants receive baptism, chrismation and Communion together: full members from the start.
  • Anglican, Methodist, Presbyterian: infant baptism is a sign of God's grace and covenant; adults who were not baptised as babies are baptised on profession of faith.
  • Baptist, most Pentecostal: only believers should be baptised; many Baptist churches baptise people who were baptised as babies, seeing infant baptism as not yet a baptism of faith.
  • Common ground: almost all churches accept each other's baptism if water and the Trinity words were used (Baptists are the main exception for infant baptism; some Orthodox churches also baptise converts afresh).

Mission 1Evaluate

"Babies should not be baptised."

  • Reasons for: faith should be a free personal choice. The New Testament pattern is "repent and be baptised" (Acts 2:38). Baptism by immersion pictures death and resurrection (Romans 6:4), which a baby cannot understand.
  • Reasons against: grace is God's gift, not a reward for understanding. Households were baptised (Acts 16:33). Original sin (Catholic view) means baptism should not wait. The child can confirm the promises later.
  • Judge it by: Is baptism mainly God's action or the believer's response? What does the New Testament show? Does the community's promise make up for the child's?
Mission 1Worked answer

Two different beliefs

Question: Explain two different beliefs about infant baptism.

  • Belief 1: Catholics believe babies should be baptised. Develop: baptism frees a person from original sin and gives God's grace, which a baby can receive without earning it.
  • Belief 2: Baptists believe only believers should be baptised. Develop: baptism is a public sign of a person's own faith, so it must follow repentance, as in Acts 2:38.
Mission check

?Mei, 30, has never been baptised and asks to join a Catholic parish. Josh, baptised as a baby in a Methodist church, joins a Baptist church at 25 and asks to be immersed. What would most likely happen?

Christians link baptism with belonging to Christ and the Church; the debate is whether God's gift or the person's faith should come first.

Mission 2: Is the bread at Communion really Jesus' body, a reminder of him, or something in between?

Make a guess: Is the bread at Communion really Jesus' body, a reminder of him, or something in between?

Mission 2

One meal, many names

  • Christians share bread and wine, as Jesus did at his Last Supper. Most churches call it a sacrament (Baptists: an ordinance).
  • Eucharist: Greek for "thanksgiving".
  • Mass: Catholic name (from the Latin dismissal, "Go, it is the sending").
  • Holy Communion: sharing with Christ and each other (Anglican, Methodist and many others).
  • Lord's Supper or Breaking of Bread: many Protestant churches.
  • Divine Liturgy: the Orthodox service in which the Eucharist is celebrated.
Mission 2Read the source

The Last Supper

At the Passover meal, the night before he died:

> "As they were eating, Jesus took bread, gave thanks for it, and broke it. He gave to the disciples and said, 'Take, eat; this is my body.'" (Matthew 26:26)

> "for this is my blood of the new covenant, which is poured out for many for the remission of sins." (Matthew 26:28)

  • Paul's earlier account adds Jesus' command: "Do this in memory of me." (1 Corinthians 11:24)

Q1 What did Jesus say the bread and the cup were?

Show answer

His body; his blood of the new covenant.

Q2 What reason for the blood does Matthew 26:28 give?

Show answer

It is poured out for the forgiveness of sins.

Mission 2

The Catholic Mass

  • Liturgy of the Word: readings from the Bible and a homily (sermon).
  • Liturgy of the Eucharist: the priest says Jesus' words over unleavened bread (hosts) and wine (the consecration).
  • Catholics believe the bread and wine become the body and blood of Christ: the Real Presence.
  • Transubstantiation: the substance (what it really is) changes; the appearances (taste, look) of bread and wine remain (Council of Trent, 1551).
  • The Mass makes present Christ's one sacrifice on the cross; it does not repeat it.
  • Catholics must attend Mass on Sundays; those prepared and free from serious sin receive Communion.
Quick check
?What do Catholics mean by transubstantiation?
Mission 2

Anglican and Lutheran Holy Communion

  • Church of England: Holy Communion is the main Sunday service in many parishes; people kneel or stand at the altar rail and receive both bread and wine.
  • Anglican beliefs vary: some (Anglo-Catholics) believe in a real, objective presence; many evangelical Anglicans stress a spiritual presence received by faith.
  • Article XXVIII: "The Body of Christ is given, taken, and eaten, in the Supper, only after an heavenly and spiritual manner." It also rejects transubstantiation.
  • Article XXX: lay people should receive the cup as well as the bread. Article XXXI: Christ's offering on the cross, made once, is complete, so the Lord's Supper does not offer him again (paraphrase).
  • Lutherans: Christ's body and blood are truly present "in, with and under" the bread and wine; the bread is not changed in substance.
Mission 2

Memorial and spiritual presence

  • Memorial view (Huldrych Zwingli, 1484–1531): "this is my body" means "this represents my body". The bread and wine are symbols that help believers remember Christ's death.
  • Baptist practice: the Lord's Supper, often monthly; bread and small individual cups of (often non-alcoholic) wine are passed along the rows; usually all believers are welcome.
  • Spiritual presence (John Calvin, 1509–1564; Presbyterian and Reformed): the bread stays bread, but by the Holy Spirit believers truly feed on Christ through faith.
  • All these churches agree: Jesus commanded it, it proclaims his death "until he comes" (1 Corinthians 11:26), and it unites believers.
Quick check
?In one church, elders pass bread and tiny cups along the rows. The pastor says, "This bread does not change: we eat it to remember that Jesus died for us." Which tradition fits best?
Mission 2Diversity

A spectrum of belief

  • Catholic: transubstantiation; Real Presence remains, so consecrated hosts are kept in the tabernacle and adored.
  • Orthodox: a real change, called a mystery, not defined in philosophical terms; leavened bread and wine given together on a spoon; babies receive too.
  • Lutheran: real presence in, with and under the bread and wine.
  • Anglican: a range, from real presence to spiritual presence.
  • Presbyterian (Reformed): spiritual presence received by faith.
  • Baptist and many evangelicals: memorial.
  • Quakers and The Salvation Army: no outward Communion; inward communion with Christ.
Mission 2

Why it matters

  • Unity with Christ: Catholics and Orthodox believe they receive Christ himself; others meet him spiritually or remember him.
  • Unity with each other: "we, who are many, are one body" (1 Corinthians 10:17).
  • Forgiveness and strength: grace to live as Christians in the week ahead.
  • Remembering and hoping: looking back to the cross and forward to Christ's return.
  • Sent out: the Mass ends by sending people to serve others.
  • A sadness: Christians disagree about who may receive, so many cannot share Communion together.
Mission 2Evaluate

"The Eucharist is the most important act of Christian worship."

  • Reasons for: Jesus commanded it (1 Corinthians 11:24). Catholics call it "source and summit"; Catholics and Orthodox believe Christ is truly present. It unites the Church across the world.
  • Reasons against: many Protestants put preaching the Bible at the centre, with Communion monthly. Quakers and The Salvation Army worship without it. Jesus also stressed love in action (Matthew 25:31–46).
  • Judge it by: What did Jesus command? Is Christ present in it in a special way? Which practice most changes how Christians live?
Mission 2Worked answer

Catholic and Protestant interpretations

Question: Explain Catholic and Protestant interpretations of the Eucharist.

  • Catholic: at the consecration, bread and wine become Christ's body and blood (transubstantiation). Source: "this is my body" (Matthew 26:26), taken literally. Develop: so hosts are reserved in the tabernacle and adored.
  • Protestant (Baptist): the Lord's Supper is a memorial. Source: "Do this in memory of me" (1 Corinthians 11:24). Develop: so the bread is shared as a symbol, and the focus is remembering and obeying.
  • Common ground: both obey Jesus' command and proclaim his death.

Mission 3

A different kind of pilgrimage

  • A pilgrimage is a journey made for a spiritual purpose.
  • Some pilgrims go to a holy site: a place of Jesus' life, a saint's tomb, a reported vision.
  • Others go to a living community: people who share prayer, work and meals.
  • Many of these communities are ecumenical: Christians of different denominations together.
  • Ecumenism: working for unity between Christian churches. It does not mean merging them into one.
Mission 3

Iona: Columba's island

  • Iona: a small island off the west coast of Scotland.
  • AD 563: the Irish monk Columba arrives with 12 companions and founds a monastery.
  • From Iona, monks took Christianity to the Picts and to northern England (Aidan founded Lindisfarne, 635).
  • Later a Benedictine abbey; ruined after the Scottish Reformation (1560).
  • Many call Iona a "thin place": a Celtic idea of a place where heaven and earth feel close.
Mission 3

The Iona Community today

  • Founded 1938 by George MacLeod, a Church of Scotland minister from Govan, Glasgow.
  • Ministers and unemployed craftsmen rebuilt the abbey together: faith joined to work and to the poor.
  • Today: an ecumenical community; members live all over Britain and beyond and keep a shared Rule:
    • daily prayer and Bible reading;
    • accounting for their use of money and time;
    • action for justice and peace.
  • Guests stay for a week: worship twice a day, shared meals and chores, talks on faith and justice.
  • A weekly pilgrimage walk round the island stops at places from Iona's history for prayer.
  • Significance: all of life (work, politics, the environment) is part of following Christ.

Mission 3

Taizé: a parable of community

  • Taizé: a village in Burgundy, eastern France.
  • 1940: Brother Roger (1915–2005), a Swiss Protestant, settles there and shelters refugees from the war, including Jews.
  • From 1949 brothers commit for life to celibacy, simplicity and community.
  • Today: about a hundred brothers from Catholic and Protestant backgrounds, from many countries.
  • They accept no gifts for themselves; they live by their own work.
  • Brother Roger called the community a "parable of community": a small sign that divided Christians can live as one.
Mission 3

A week at Taizé

  • Every week, thousands of pilgrims, mostly young people, arrive from all over the world.
  • Prayer three times a day in the Church of Reconciliation:
    • short chants, repeated many times, in many languages;
    • a Bible reading;
    • a long silence.
  • Bible introduction by a brother, then small-group sharing across languages.
  • Practical work: cooking, cleaning, washing up. Simple food and simple living.
  • Friday: prayer around the cross; Saturday: a candlelit vigil of the resurrection.
  • Significance: trust in God, reconciliation, and taking prayer home to ordinary life.

Mission 3Read the source

"Love one another"

> "A new commandment I give to you, that you love one another. Just as I have loved you, you also love one another. By this everyone will know that you are my disciples, if you have love for one another." (John 13:34–35)

  • At the Last Supper, Jesus also prays that his followers "may all be one", so that the world may believe (John 17:21, paraphrase).

Q1 According to Jesus, how will people recognise his disciples?

Show answer

By their love for one another.

Q2 Why might a divided Church weaken its witness?

Show answer

If Christians cannot love and live with each other, the sign Jesus gave is hidden.

Mission 3Diversity

Contrasting pilgrimages, differing views

  • Lourdes: Mary's reported visions; prayer for healing; processions, baths, Mass; mainly Catholic.
  • Iona: community life; justice and peace; simple shared worship; ecumenical.
  • Many Protestants who avoid shrines welcome communities like these: no relics or Marian devotion; Bible, prayer and service at the centre.
  • Catholics also go: popes have welcomed Taizé, and Catholic brothers belong to it.
  • Some Christians are wary:
    • some fear that a focus on unity blurs real differences in belief;
    • some evangelicals question repetitive chant (Matthew 6:7, paraphrase);
    • some think the Iona Community's political campaigning goes beyond the Church's role.
Quick check

Mission 3Evaluate

"A week in a Christian community is worth more than a visit to a holy site."

  • For: a week of shared life can change habits, not just feelings.
  • For: meeting Christians of other churches lives out John 13:34–35.
  • For: the focus stays on Christ and on service, not on a place or object.
  • Against: holy sites link pilgrims to real events of faith: Jesus' life, the apostles, the saints.
  • Against: shrines offer the sacraments and a welcome for the sick that communities may not.
  • Against: people need different things; both kinds of pilgrimage are prayer, so ranking them may miss the point.
  • Judge it by: What is pilgrimage for? Does the place matter, or the people? What lasts after the journey home?

Mission 4

The church year

  • Christians mark the life of Jesus in a yearly cycle: the church year (liturgical year).
  • In Western churches it begins with Advent, four Sundays before Christmas.
  • Order: Advent → Christmas → Epiphany → Lent → Holy Week → Easter → Ascension → Pentecost → Ordinary Time.
  • Fixed feasts keep their date (Christmas, 25 December); movable feasts depend on Easter.
  • Colours in church mark the season: purple for preparation, white or gold for celebration, red for the Spirit and martyrs, green for Ordinary Time.
  • Significance: the whole story of salvation is retold every year, so believers live it, not just learn it.
Mission 4

Advent: getting ready twice

  • Advent (Latin adventus, "coming"): the four weeks before Christmas.
  • Christians prepare for two comings of Jesus:
    • his birth in Bethlehem, celebrated at Christmas;
    • his Second Coming at the end of time, as judge and king.
  • Advent wreath: a candle lit each Sunday; in many churches a fifth, white candle for Christ on Christmas Day.
  • Advent services: carol services and an Advent procession moving from darkness to light.
  • Prayer, fasting and charity: Catholics are encouraged to go to confession; many churches collect food and gifts for those in need.
  • Advent calendars began as a way of counting the days in prayer.
Mission 4Read the source

Two birth accounts

  • Luke 2:1–20 (paraphrase): a census sends Joseph and Mary to Bethlehem; Jesus is born and laid in a manger; angels announce to shepherds a Saviour, "Christ the Lord", and sing "Glory to God in the highest".
  • Matthew 2:1–12 (paraphrase): wise men (Magi) from the east follow a star and ask in Jerusalem where the king of the Jews is born; King Herod is troubled; they find the child and bring gold, frankincense and myrrh.
  • Luke stresses the poor and lowly: shepherds are first to hear.
  • Matthew stresses Jesus as king, recognised by non-Jews from far away.

Q1 Which Gospel tells of the shepherds, and which of the Magi?

Show answer

Luke: shepherds. Matthew: Magi.

Q2 Does Matthew say how many Magi came?

Show answer

No. "Three" is a tradition based on the three gifts.

Mission 4

Christmas celebrates the Incarnation

  • Incarnation: Christians believe God the Son became a human being in Jesus. "The Word became flesh" (John 1:14).
  • Christmas celebrates that God came close to humanity, as a vulnerable baby.
  • The Bible gives no date for Jesus' birth. 25 December was chosen centuries later.
  • Significance:
    • God shares human life, so every human life has dignity;
    • joy and thanksgiving for God's gift;
    • giving to others, especially the poor, as Jesus was born poor.
  • Say: "Christmas celebrates the Incarnation" — not: "Christmas celebrates the resurrection".
Mission 4

How Christians celebrate Christmas

  • Midnight Mass (Catholic) or Midnight Communion (Anglican): the first celebration of Christmas, late on Christmas Eve.
  • Christmas Day services; Orthodox churches that follow the old (Julian) calendar celebrate on 7 January.
  • Carol services, often Nine Lessons and Carols: Bible readings from the Fall to the birth of Jesus.
  • Crib (nativity scene): begun by St Francis of Assisi in 1223.
  • Christingle: an orange (the world), a candle (Jesus, the Light of the World), a red ribbon (his blood), fruits (God's gifts).
  • Nativity plays, giving presents, family meals, and helping the lonely and homeless.

Mission 4

Where 25 December came from

  • The first record of Christians celebrating Jesus' birth on 25 December: Rome, AD 336.
  • Why that date? Historians give two main theories:
    • it replaced a Roman winter festival of the "Unconquered Sun";
    • it was calculated: Jesus was believed to have been conceived on 25 March, so born nine months later.
  • Many Eastern Christians first celebrated the birth on 6 January.
  • 1647: the Puritan Parliament banned Christmas in England as unbiblical; it returned in 1660.
  • 1840s: Christmas trees, cards and Dickens' A Christmas Carol (1843) shape the modern British Christmas.
Mission 4

Christmas in Britain today

  • A public holiday: 25 and 26 December. Many people who rarely attend church go at Christmas.
  • Schools hold nativity plays and carol services; the monarch's Christmas broadcast often speaks of faith.
  • Social significance: families gather, people give to charity, churches open their doors for lunches and night shelters.
  • Commercialisation: shopping, advertising and Father Christmas can take centre stage; many people celebrate without the religious meaning.
  • For Christians it remains a holy day: worship, the Incarnation, and generosity to others.
Mission 4Diversity

Different ways and different attitudes

  • Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican: the liturgical year in full; a night service (Midnight Mass for Catholics); a season of feasting from Christmas to Epiphany.
  • Many Protestant churches: carol services, nativity plays, preaching on the birth; less emphasis on the liturgical calendar.
  • Traditional Quakers: every day is equally holy, so no special festivals (many Quakers today mark Christmas informally).
  • Some Reformed churches keep only what the Bible commands, so they do not keep Christmas as a church feast.
  • Jehovah's Witnesses (who reject the Trinity) do not celebrate Christmas.
Quick check
?Which Christians have traditionally not celebrated Christmas as a special religious festival?
Mission 4Evaluate

"Christmas has lost its religious meaning in Britain today."

  • For: spending, adverts and Father Christmas dominate; most people do not go to church at Christmas.
  • For: many families celebrate with no mention of Jesus; for some, it is mainly a holiday.
  • Against: carol services and Midnight Masses are among the fullest services of the year.
  • Against: nativity plays, charity appeals and church lunches keep the story and its values public.
  • Against: for Christians the meaning is unchanged: the Incarnation is celebrated whatever the shops do.
  • Judge it by: Meaning for whom: Christians, or society as a whole? Can commercial and religious Christmas exist side by side? Are generosity and family gatherings themselves religious values?
Mission check

?In a country where 25 December is an ordinary working day, a small church holds its Christmas service on the nearest Sunday, with a crib and readings from Luke 2. Some members worry it is "on the wrong day". Which statement best explains why the service still keeps the meaning of Christmas?

Christmas is about the Incarnation, prepared for in Advent and shown to the world at Epiphany; the date is tradition, the meaning is the heart.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Baptism is the sacrament (or ordinance) of initiation, with water and the name of the Trinity.
  • Infant baptism stresses God's grace, original sin (Catholic) and the covenant; believers' baptism stresses personal faith and the dying-and-rising picture of immersion.
  • Godparents promise to support a baptised child.

Mission 2

  • The Eucharist (Mass, Holy Communion, Lord's Supper) goes back to Jesus' Last Supper: "this is my body… my blood of the new covenant".
  • Catholics: transubstantiation and the Real Presence, the "source and summit"; Orthodox: a real change, a mystery; Lutherans: real presence; Anglicans: a range; Reformed: spiritual presence; Baptists: memorial.
  • Article XXVIII: the Body of Christ is received "only after an heavenly and spiritual manner".

Mission 3

  • Iona: Columba's island (563); the ecumenical Iona Community (1938) joins prayer to justice and peace.
  • Taizé: Brother Roger's ecumenical community (1940); chants, silence, Bible sharing and work, mostly with young people.

Mission 4

  • Christmas celebrates the Incarnation: God the Son born as Jesus (Luke 2: shepherds; Matthew 2: Magi); the date 25 December is a 4th-century tradition.
  • Advent prepares for Jesus' birth and his Second Coming, with prayer, wreaths, services and charity.
