Your hand jerks off a hot pan before you decide to. What moved it?

Make a guess: Your hand jerks off a hot pan before you decide to. What moved it?

Brain and neuropsychology · the nervous system

Two main divisions

  • The central nervous system (CNS) is the brain and the spinal cord.
  • The peripheral nervous system (PNS) is all the nerves outside the CNS. It carries messages between the CNS and the rest of the body.
  • The PNS has two parts. The somatic nervous system controls voluntary movement and carries information from the senses.
  • The autonomic nervous system controls automatic functions, such as heart rate, breathing and digestion.
Brain and neuropsychology · the nervous system

What the CNS does

  • The CNS receives information from the senses, processes it and sends instructions to muscles and glands.
  • The brain is the control centre. It makes decisions and controls thinking, memory, emotion and voluntary movement.
  • The brain also keeps you alive: its lowest part, the brain stem, controls breathing and heartbeat.
Brain and neuropsychology · the nervous system

The spinal cord

  • The spinal cord runs down your back, protected by the spine.
  • It carries messages between the brain and the body: up from the senses, down to the muscles.
  • It can also control simple reflexes by itself. The message comes in and goes straight back out to the muscle, which is faster than waiting for the brain.
  • The brain finds out a moment later.

Brain and neuropsychology · the nervous system

The autonomic nervous system and fight or flight

  • The autonomic system has two branches: the sympathetic branch arouses the body; the parasympathetic branch calms it ("rest and digest").
  • Sensing a threat, the sympathetic branch triggers the fight or flight response and the adrenal glands release adrenaline.
  • Heart rate and breathing rise, pupils widen, you sweat and digestion slows, ready to fight or run.
  • When the threat passes, the parasympathetic branch returns the body to normal.
Brain and neuropsychology · the nervous system

The James-Lange theory of emotion

  • Common sense says: you feel afraid, so your heart races.
  • James-Lange says the order is the other way round: event → physical arousal → you notice the arousal → emotion.
  • The body reacts first (fight or flight); the emotion comes from interpreting that body change. "I'm trembling, so I must be afraid."
  • No physical change means no emotion.

Mission 2

Brain signals are electrical, until they hit a gap. How do they cross?

Make a guess: Brain signals are electrical, until they hit a gap. How do they cross?

Brain and neuropsychology · neurons and synapses

Inside a neuron

  • A neuron is a nerve cell that carries messages as electrical impulses.
  • Dendrites receive messages from other neurons. The cell body contains the nucleus.
  • The impulse travels along the axon, which is often wrapped in a fatty myelin sheath that speeds it up.
  • At the end of the axon are axon terminals, where the message is passed on to the next neuron.
Brain and neuropsychology · neurons and synapses

Three types of neuron

NeuronCarries messagesExample
Sensoryfrom sense receptors to the CNSheat on your skin
Relaybetween sensory and motor neurons, inside the CNSin the spinal cord or brain
Motorfrom the CNS to muscles and glandsmoving your hand
  • In a reflex, the message goes sensory → relay (spinal cord) → motor.
Brain and neuropsychology · neurons and synapses

Crossing the synapse

  • The tiny gap between two neurons is the synapse (synaptic gap). The impulse can't jump it.
  • When the impulse reaches the axon terminal, vesicles release a chemical, a neurotransmitter, into the gap: this is release.
  • The neurotransmitter crosses the gap and binds to receptors on the next neuron, passing the message on.
  • Leftover neurotransmitter is taken back into the first neuron to be reused: reuptake.
Brain and neuropsychology · neurons and synapses

Excitation and inhibition

  • A neurotransmitter's function is to carry the message across the synapse and affect the next neuron.
  • Excitatory neurotransmitters (e.g. glutamate) make the next neuron more likely to fire an impulse.
  • Inhibitory neurotransmitters (e.g. GABA) make it less likely to fire.
  • One neuron receives messages from many synapses. It adds up the excitatory and inhibitory signals and fires only if excitation is strong enough.

Brain and neuropsychology · neurons and synapses

Hebb's theory of learning and neuronal growth

  • Hebb said that when we learn, groups of neurons fire together. Each time they do, the synapses between them get stronger.
  • With practice, these neurons form a cell assembly: a network that fires easily, so the skill or memory becomes easier.
  • Neuronal growth: learning makes neurons grow new connections (synaptic knobs) and strengthens existing ones. Unused connections weaken.
  • So the brain is plastic: experience (nurture) changes its structure.

Mission check

?Brain signals are electrical, until they hit a gap. How do they cross?

They switch from electrical to chemical. At the axon terminal, vesicles release a neurotransmitter into the synapse; it crosses and binds to receptors on the next neuron, making it more (excitatory) or less (inhibitory) likely to fire. Leftover neurotransmitter is taken back by reuptake.

Mission 3

Your eyes are at the front. Which part of your brain does the seeing?

Make a guess: Your eyes are at the front. Which part of your brain does the seeing?

Brain and neuropsychology · brain structure

The lobes and the cerebellum

  • The wrinkled outer layer of the brain is the cerebral cortex. It has two halves (hemispheres), each divided into four lobes.
  • Frontal lobe: at the front, behind the forehead.
  • Parietal lobe: at the top, behind the frontal lobe.
  • Temporal lobe: at the side, near the ears.
  • Occipital lobe: at the back.
  • The cerebellum sits underneath, at the back, below the occipital lobe.
Brain and neuropsychology · brain structure

What each part does

StructureMain functions
Frontalplanning, decisions, personality, voluntary movement, speech
Parietaltouch, pain and temperature; sense of where the body is
Temporalhearing, memory, understanding language
Occipitalvision
Cerebellumbalance and coordination of movement
Brain and neuropsychology · brain structure

The cerebellum

  • The cerebellum ("little brain") sits at the back, underneath the occipital lobe.
  • It coordinates movement: balance, posture and smooth, accurate movements.
  • It doesn't start movements. It fine-tunes them, so you can walk in a straight line or reach for a cup without missing.
  • It helps you learn movement skills, such as riding a bike or typing, until they become automatic.
Brain and neuropsychology · brain structure

Localisation of function

  • Localisation of function means specific areas of the brain carry out specific jobs.

Penfield (1959)

Aim
To find out what happens when the temporal lobe of a conscious patient is electrically stimulated.
Method
Patients having surgery for severe epilepsy stayed awake under local anaesthetic. Penfield touched points on the exposed cortex with a weak electric current and recorded what they reported.
Results
Stimulating parts of the temporal lobe made some patients relive vivid past experiences, hear music, or feel déjà vu or fear.
Conclusion
The temporal lobe (the "interpretive cortex") is involved in memory and interpreting experience: evidence for localisation.
Strength
Direct stimulation of a living brain shows cause and effect.
Weakness
All patients had epilepsy, so their brains may not be typical.
Quick check

Mission 4

A rod went through his skull. He walked and talked. So what changed?

Make a guess: A rod went through his skull. He walked and talked. So what changed?

Brain and neuropsychology · neurological damage

What neurological damage is

  • Neurological damage is damage to the brain or nerves. Dead brain cells are not replaced.
  • A stroke happens when the blood supply to part of the brain is blocked or a vessel bursts, so cells there are starved of oxygen and die.
  • An injury is physical damage, e.g. a blow to the head.
  • Effects depend on where the damage is: one ability can be lost while others are untouched.
Brain and neuropsychology · neurological damage

Damage, movement and behaviour

  • Damage to the motor area in one hemisphere causes weakness or paralysis on the opposite side of the body, e.g. after a stroke.
  • Damage to the cerebellum causes clumsy, poorly coordinated movement and problems with balance.
  • Damage to Broca's area makes speaking slow and difficult.
  • Damage to the very front of the frontal lobe mainly changes behaviour rather than movement (next screen).
Brain and neuropsychology · neurological damage

The prefrontal cortex and behaviour

  • The prefrontal cortex is the very front of the frontal lobe.
  • It is involved in planning, decision-making, controlling impulses and controlling emotions and social behaviour.
  • Damage can leave movement, speech and memory working, yet the person becomes impulsive, takes risks, makes poor decisions and may seem to have a different personality.
Quick check
?After a car accident, Sam's memory, speech and movement test normally. But his family say he now makes rash decisions, swears at strangers and can't stick to a plan. Which area was most likely damaged?
Quick check
?Rhys has a stroke in the motor area of his left hemisphere. Which effect is most likely?
Mission check

?A rod went through his skull. He walked and talked. So what changed?

His personality changed. The rod damaged the prefrontal cortex at the front of his brain, which controls planning, decisions, impulses and emotions. He could still walk and talk, so the areas for movement and speech were not badly damaged, but he became impulsive and seemed a different person.

Mission 5

People once read minds from bumps on the skull. What changed?

Make a guess: People once read minds from bumps on the skull. What changed?

Brain and neuropsychology · studying the brain

From skull bumps to evidence

  • Around 1800, phrenology claimed each mental ability had its own brain area, shown by bumps on the skull.
  • The idea of areas with different jobs survived; skull-reading did not, as skull thickness varies.
  • 1800s: studying people with brain damage, e.g. Gage (1848) and Broca's patient who lost speech (1861).
  • 1900s: stimulating the brain during surgery; split-brain studies (1960s).
  • 1970s on: brain scanning shows the living brain.
Brain and neuropsychology · studying the brain

Cognitive neuroscience

  • Cognitive neuroscience studies how the structure and function of the brain relate to behaviour and cognition (thinking, memory, perception, language).
  • It asks: which brain areas are active when we remember, recognise a face or make a decision?
  • Its evidence comes from comparing people with brain damage, measuring what happens when the brain is stimulated, and scanning healthy brains while people do tasks.
Quick check
?A surgeon passes a weak electric current to an area of an awake patient's brain and asks what they experience. Which way of studying the brain is this?
Brain and neuropsychology · studying the brain

CT and PET scans

  • CT (computerised tomography): X-rays are taken from many angles and a computer builds up slice images. It shows structure, e.g. tumours, bleeding or stroke damage. Quick and widely available, but uses radiation and shows no activity.
  • PET (positron emission tomography): a radioactive tracer (often a form of glucose) is injected. Active areas use more glucose, so they show up. It shows function (activity), but is radioactive, expensive and less detailed.
Brain and neuropsychology · studying the brain

fMRI scans

  • fMRI (functional magnetic resonance imaging) uses a strong magnetic field to detect changes in blood oxygen. Active areas need more oxygen-rich blood.
  • It shows function: which areas are active during a task, with good detail.
  • No radiation, so it can be repeated. But it is expensive, noisy, needs the person to lie very still, and shows activity a few seconds after it happens.
Key study Brain and neuropsychology · studying the brain

Tulving (1989)

Aim
To find out whether episodic and semantic memories involve different areas of the brain.
Method
Six volunteers, including Tulving, were injected with a small amount of radioactive gold. They thought about personal events (episodic) or facts (semantic) on repeated trials while a scanner tracked blood flow (repeated measures).
Results
Only three of the six gave clear results: episodic memories increased blood flow to the frontal cortex; semantic memories to the back of the cortex.
Conclusion
Episodic and semantic memories use different brain areas, a biological basis for different types of long-term memory.
Strength
Scanning gives objective evidence that participants can't easily fake.
Weakness
Tiny sample with only three clear results; episodic and semantic memories overlap, so are hard to separate.

Mission check

?People once read minds from bumps on the skull. What changed?

Psychologists stopped guessing from the outside of the head and started gathering evidence from the brain itself: first from people with brain damage, then from stimulating the brain and from split-brain patients, and now from scans of living brains. The old idea that areas have different jobs survived; reading it from skull bumps did not.

Recap

Big ideas

  • The nervous system has two divisions: the CNS (brain and spinal cord) and the PNS (nerves linking the CNS to the body).
  • The CNS receives, processes and sends out information; the spinal cord carries messages and handles simple reflexes.
  • The autonomic nervous system controls automatic functions; its sympathetic branch produces fight or flight. James-Lange says emotion comes from noticing these body changes.
  • A neuron carries an electrical impulse along its axon; at the synapse, the message is carried across by a chemical neurotransmitter.
  • Neurotransmitters are released, bind to receptors and are removed by reuptake; excitatory ones make the next neuron more likely to fire, inhibitory ones less likely.
  • Sensory, relay and motor neurons carry messages to, within and out of the CNS; Hebb: neurons that fire together strengthen their connections, so learning physically changes the brain.
  • The cortex has four lobes in each hemisphere (frontal, parietal, temporal, occipital), with the cerebellum underneath at the back.
  • Each structure has main functions: frontal (planning, movement, speech), parietal (touch), temporal (hearing, memory, language), occipital (vision), cerebellum (balance and coordination).
  • Localisation of function: motor, somatosensory, visual, auditory and language (Broca's, Wernicke's) areas; Penfield's stimulation of the temporal lobe (interpretive cortex) is evidence for it.
  • Neurological damage (from a stroke or injury) affects whichever functions the damaged area carries out, leaving others intact.
  • Damage to the prefrontal cortex can change behaviour and personality: impulsive actions, poor decisions and less emotional control.
  • Damage to the motor area causes weakness or paralysis on the opposite side; cerebellum damage affects coordination and balance.
  • Understanding of the brain moved from phrenology to evidence: brain damage case studies, stimulation, split-brain research and brain scanning.
  • Cognitive neuroscience links brain structure and function to behaviour and cognition. CT shows structure; PET and fMRI show activity. Tulving's 'gold' study found episodic and semantic memories linked to different brain areas.
