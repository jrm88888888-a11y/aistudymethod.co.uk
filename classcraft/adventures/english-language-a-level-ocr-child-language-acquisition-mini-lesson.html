Child language · stages and early phonologyScreen 4

Hearing comes before saying

  • Children often perceive a sound contrast before they can produce it.
  • Berko and Brown (1960) reported a child who called his toy fish his 'fis'. When an adult asked 'Is this your fis?', he said no. Asked 'Is this your fish?', he said 'Yes, my fis'.
  • So the child had heard /ʃ/ correctly: the change happened in his own production.
  • Evaluate: it's a reported exchange with one child, not a large study.
Child language · stages and early phonologyScreen 5

Patterns in early pronunciation

  • Children's early pronunciations follow regular phonological processes: the same simplification appears across many words.
  • Data may use phonemic symbols: 'tat' is /tæt/ for /kæt/.
  • Most children grow out of these patterns in the preschool years.

Mission checkScreen 9

?A toddler says 'tat' for 'cat'. Mistake or system?

A system. 'Tat' for 'cat' is fronting: /k/, made at the back of the mouth, becomes /t/ at the front, and the same swap turns 'go' into 'do'. Children usually hear the difference (the 'fis' phenomenon), so their pronunciations are regular simplifications, not mishearing.

Mission 2

No one teaches 'add -ed', yet toddlers say 'I goed'. Whose rule is it?

Make a guess: No one teaches 'add -ed', yet toddlers say 'I goed'. Whose rule is it?

Child language · theories of acquisitionScreen 1

Imitation and reinforcement: Skinner

  • Behaviourism: in Verbal Behavior (1957), Skinner explained language as learned behaviour shaped by operant conditioning.
  • Children imitate what they hear; positive reinforcement (praise, getting the biscuit) strengthens a form, and unrewarded forms fade.
  • For: children pick up the words and accent around them.
  • Against: adults don't say 'goed', so it can't be copied. Brown and Hanlon (1970) found parents rarely corrected grammar.
Child language · theories of acquisitionScreen 2

An inborn capacity: Chomsky

  • Nativism: Chomsky reviewed Skinner's book critically (1959) and proposed a Language Acquisition Device (LAD, 1965), an inborn capacity for language, later developed as Universal Grammar.
  • Poverty of the stimulus: the speech children hear is messy and incomplete, yet they work out its rules.
  • For: overgeneralisations ('goed', 'mouses') show rule use; children learning many languages pass through similar stages.
  • Against: the LAD can't be observed directly, and it says little about interaction.
Child language · theories of acquisitionScreen 3

A critical period? The case of Genie

  • Lenneberg (1967) proposed a critical period: language is acquired most easily before about puberty.
  • Genie (a pseudonym) was found in Los Angeles in 1970, aged about 13, after years of severe isolation and neglect, with almost no language around her.
  • With care and teaching she learned many words, but her grammar stayed very limited.
  • Evaluate: her trauma means no single cause can be isolated, and the research raised serious ethical concerns. One case can't prove a theory.
Child language · theories of acquisitionScreen 4

Thought and language: Piaget and Vygotsky

  • Piaget (cognitive theory): language follows cognitive development. A child must grasp a concept first, e.g. that hidden objects still exist (object permanence), before words like 'gone' make sense.
  • Against: some children with learning difficulties develop strong language.
  • Vygotsky (Thought and Language, 1934): learning is social. A more knowledgeable other helps the child across the zone of proximal development, between what they can do alone and with help.
Child language · theories of acquisitionScreen 5

Learning through interaction: Bruner

  • Interactionism: in Child's Talk (1983), Bruner proposed a Language Acquisition Support System (LASS): caregivers' talk supports learning.
  • Child-directed speech: higher pitch, short sentences, repetition, questions, expansions.
  • Routines such as peekaboo and picture-book reading give a predictable frame for turn-taking.
  • Scaffolding (Wood, Bruner and Ross, 1976): support that is withdrawn as the child copes.
  • Against: child-directed speech isn't used the same way in every culture (Ochs, 1982), yet children everywhere learn to talk.

Mission checkScreen 9

?No one teaches 'add -ed', yet toddlers say 'I goed'. Whose rule is it?

The child's own. No adult says 'goed', so it can't simply be imitated or rewarded: the child has worked out 'add -ed for the past' and applied it too widely. Chomsky saw this as evidence for an inborn LAD, while interactionist and usage-based researchers argue rich input and general learning can do the same; the strongest answers weigh both.

Mission 3

A child calls all four-legged animals 'doggy'. What's she doing right?

Make a guess: A child calls all four-legged animals 'doggy'. What's she doing right?

Child language · vocabulary and grammarScreen 1

Building a first vocabulary

  • Most children have about 50 words somewhere between 1;6 and 2;0; word learning then speeds up.
  • Nelson (1973) studied 18 children's first 50 words: most were naming words (about 65%); others were action ('up', 'bye'), modifying ('hot', 'more') and personal-social words ('no', 'please').
  • Meanings don't arrive fully formed: children try out how far a word stretches.
Child language · vocabulary and grammarScreen 2

Stretching and shrinking meanings

  • Overextension: a word covers more than the adult meaning ('doggy' for all four-legged animals). Rescorla (1980) found about a third of the first 75 words of six children were overextended at some point.
  • Rescorla's types: categorical (a related category: 'doggy' for cows), analogical (a perceived likeness, e.g. shape: 'ball' for the moon), and predicate or mismatch statements ('doll!' at an empty doll's bed).
  • Underextension: a word covers less ('cat' only for the family cat).

Child language · vocabulary and grammarScreen 4

Errors that show a rule

  • A virtuous error follows a real rule of English but applies it too widely: 'sheeps', 'goed', 'runned'.
  • Berko (1958): children were shown a made-up creature, a 'wug', then two of them. Most said 'wugs', a plural for a word they'd never heard. So they had a rule, not a memorised list.
  • Cruttenden (1979): a U-shaped pattern. Correct 'went' (learned as one word) → 'goed' (rule overgeneralised) → 'went' again, now alongside the rule.
Child language · vocabulary and grammarScreen 5

The order of grammatical morphemes

  • Brown (1973) followed three children (Adam, Eve, Sarah) and found they mastered 14 grammatical morphemes in a similar order.
  • The first few, in order: -ing (no auxiliary), 'in', 'on', plural -s, irregular past ('went'), possessive 's.
  • Regular past -ed comes later (ninth): one reason irregular 'went' can come before 'goed'.
  • Evaluate: ages overlap widely; three children is a small sample, though later studies largely replicated the order.

Mission checkScreen 9

?A child calls all four-legged animals 'doggy'. What's she doing right?

She is categorising. Calling every four-legged animal 'doggy' is a categorical overextension: she has noticed features the animals share and is stretching her one word to fit them. About a third of early words are overextended at some point (Rescorla, 1980), and the meaning narrows as new words arrive.

Child language · learning to read and writeScreen 1

Learning to read

  • Phonics links letters (graphemes) to sounds (phonemes); children blend sounds to read and segment words to spell. In England, Year 1 pupils take a 40-word phonics screening check, including invented words, introduced in 2012.
  • Whole-word ('look and say') methods teach words as visual shapes.
  • Some common words, such as 'said', don't follow the usual patterns and must be learned.

From scribble to spelling

What spelling errors show

  • Many errors are logical: they show which strategy the child is using.
  • Gentry (1982) described a phonetic stage, when children spell every sound they hear, before they learn conventions.

Child language · learning to read and writeScreen 4

Stages and genres in children's writing

  • Kroll (1981) proposed four stages: preparation (to about 6: letter forms, basic spelling); consolidation (about 7–8: writing like speech, clauses chained with 'and'); differentiation (about 9–10: writing separates from speech, awareness of genre); integration (mid-teens: a personal style). Ages vary between sources.
  • Rothery (1984) described early school genres: observation/comment, recount (orientation, events, reorientation), report, narrative (orientation, complication, resolution).

Mission checkScreen 9

?Why does a six-year-old spell 'said' as 'sed', and is that progress?

Yes, it's progress. 'Sed' is a phonetic spelling: the child has segmented the word into its sounds and chosen a common letter for each one. 'Said' doesn't follow the usual pattern, so it has to be learned as an exception, which comes later, as stage models like Barclay's and Gentry's describe.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Children move from cooing and babbling through holophrastic, two-word and telegraphic stages to post-telegraphic speech; ages are approximate.
  • Early words already serve functions (Halliday): getting things, controlling others, finding out, pretending.
  • Children often hear more than they can say (Berko and Brown's 'fis'); their pronunciations follow regular phonological processes such as fronting, cluster reduction and reduplication.
  • Skinner (1957): imitation and reinforcement explain accent and vocabulary, but not forms like 'goed' that no adult says.
  • Chomsky (LAD, 1965): an inborn capacity explains rule-forming and shared stages; it is hard to test. Genie's case is often linked to Lenneberg's critical period, but one traumatic case can't prove it.
  • Piaget puts cognition first; Vygotsky and Bruner stress social support (ZPD, LASS, child-directed speech, scaffolding). Present theories as debated, and apply them to the data.
  • First words are mostly naming words (Nelson, 1973); children often overextend (Rescorla: categorical, analogical, predicate) or underextend meanings.
  • Virtuous errors ('sheeps', 'goed') show rules at work: Berko's wug test and Cruttenden's U-shaped curve.
  • Brown (1973) found a similar order of grammatical morphemes across children: -ing and 'in'/'on' early; regular past -ed later.
  • Reading: phonics (blend to read, segment to spell) and whole-word approaches; Chall's stages move from decoding to reading to learn.
  • Writing: Barclay's stages run from scribbling to correct spelling; Kroll's from preparation to integration; Rothery names early genres such as recount and narrative.
  • Spelling errors are evidence of strategy: phonetic spellings show sound-letter knowledge.
  • OCR examines spoken language only, so treat this as background.
