Mission 1

Mission 1: A fuel made only of carbon and hydrogen burns in air. Where do the poisonous gases in its exhaust come from?

Make a guess: A fuel made only of carbon and hydrogen burns in air. Where do the poisonous gases in its exhaust come from?

Mission 1

Alkanes: saturated hydrocarbons

  • Hydrocarbon: contains carbon and hydrogen only.
  • Saturated: only single bonds, C–C and C–H.
  • Alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons: general formula CnH2n+2.
  • Octane: n = 8, so C8H18.
Mission 1

σ-bonds and the tetrahedral carbon

  • Every C–C and C–H bond in an alkane is a σ-bond.
  • σ-bond: orbitals overlap directly between the two bonded atoms.
  • Groups can rotate freely about a σ-bond.
  • Each carbon has four bonding pairs. They repel equally and get as far apart as possible.
  • Shape: tetrahedral, bond angle 109.5°.
Mission 1

Boiling points

  • Alkane molecules are held together by London forces (induced dipole–dipole).
  • Longer chain → more electrons and more surface contact → stronger forces → higher boiling point.
  • More branching → molecules can't pack closely, less surface contact → weaker forces → lower boiling point.
  • C5H12: pentane 36 °C · 2-methylbutane 28 °C · 2,2-dimethylpropane 10 °C.
Quick check
?Which alkane has the highest boiling point?
Mission 1

Why alkanes react with so little

  • C–C and C–H bonds have high bond enthalpies (about 347 and 413 kJ mol⁻¹).
  • C and H have similar electronegativities: the σ-bonds have very low polarity.
  • No δ+ or δ− sites, so polar reagents (acids, alkalis, nucleophiles) are not attracted.
  • Alkanes do react with oxygen (combustion) and, in UV light, with halogens.
Mission 1

Complete and incomplete combustion

  • Alkanes are good fuels: combustion is very exothermic.
  • Complete (plenty of oxygen): CO2 and H2O.
  • C8H18 + 12½O2 → 8CO2 + 9H2O
  • Incomplete (limited oxygen): CO and/or C (soot), plus H2O.
  • C8H18 + 8½O2 → 8CO + 9H2O · C8H18 + 4½O2 → 8C + 9H2O
  • Carbon monoxide is toxic: it binds to haemoglobin more strongly than oxygen, so blood carries less oxygen.
  • CO is colourless and odourless: you can't tell it's there.
Quick check

Mission check

?A gas heater burns methane in a small room. The window is shut and the air vent is blocked. Why is this dangerous?

The fuel's own carbon becomes CO or soot when oxygen is short.

Mission 2

Alkanes as fuels: complete combustion

  • Alkanes burn in oxygen, releasing a lot of energy: they are fuels.
  • Complete combustion, in plenty of O2: all C → CO2, all H → H2O.
  • Balance C, then H, then O last.
  • Propane: C3H8(g) + 5O2(g) → 3CO2(g) + 4H2O(l).
  • Odd number of O atoms? Use ½O2, or double everything.
Mission 2

Background: elements burn too

  • Combustion is not only for fuels. Many elements burn in oxygen.
  • Metals form ionic oxides: 2Mg(s) + O2(g) → 2MgO(s); 4Al(s) + 3O2(g) → 2Al2O3(s).
  • Non-metals form covalent oxides: C(s) + O2(g) → CO2(g); S(s) + O2(g) → SO2(g).
  • Always write oxygen as O2.
Quick check
?When the equation for the complete combustion of octane, C8H18, is balanced with a coefficient of 2 for octane, what is the coefficient of O2?
Mission 2

Burning alcohols

  • Alcohols burn completely to CO2 and H2O, just like alkanes.
  • An alcohol already contains one O atom: subtract it before finding the O2.
  • Ethanol: C2H5OH(l) + 3O2(g) → 2CO2(g) + 3H2O(l).
  • Butan-1-ol: C4H9OH(l) + 6O2(g) → 4CO2(g) + 5H2O(l). Products have 13 O; 1 comes from the alcohol.
Quick check
?What amount of oxygen, O2, is needed to burn 0.500 mol of propan-1-ol, C3H7OH, completely?
Mission 2

Incomplete combustion

  • In limited O2, some carbon forms carbon monoxide, CO, or carbon (soot), C. Hydrogen still forms H2O.
  • To CO: 2C3H8(g) + 7O2(g) → 6CO(g) + 8H2O(l). To C: C3H8(g) + 2O2(g) → 3C(s) + 4H2O(l).
  • Less energy is released than in complete combustion.
  • Glowing soot particles make a yellow, smoky flame. Plenty of air: a blue flame.
  • The higher a fuel's carbon content, the smokier its flame: methane (75% C by mass) burns cleaner than octane (84% C).
Mission 2

Why carbon monoxide kills

  • CO is colourless, odourless and toxic.
  • It binds to haemoglobin in red blood cells far more strongly than O2 does.
  • So the blood carries less oxygen to the body's tissues.
  • You cannot see or smell it: that is why CO alarms save lives.
Quick check
?Which equation represents an incomplete combustion of ethanol?
Mission check

?After cooking outdoors, campers carry a still-glowing charcoal barbecue into their closed tent for warmth. Charcoal is almost pure carbon. Why is this deadly, even though there is no flame?

Same chemistry as the heater. Too little O2 turns carbon into CO, and CO stops your blood carrying oxygen.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Spearmint and caraway get their smells from mirror-image molecules. How can your nose tell them apart?

Make a guess: Spearmint and caraway get their smells from mirror-image molecules. How can your nose tell them apart?

Mission 3

Stereoisomers and restricted rotation

  • Stereoisomers: same structural formula, different arrangement of atoms in space.
  • A C=C is a σ bond plus a π bond. The π bond is a sideways overlap of p orbitals, above and below the plane.
  • Twisting about the C=C would break the π bond: a large energy barrier. So there is no rotation.
  • Groups are locked on the same side or on opposite sides of the C=C.
  • This needs two different groups on each C=C carbon. Propene, CH2=CHCH3, has none: one carbon carries two H.
Mission 3

Naming E and Z: CIP priority rules

  • On each C=C carbon, rank its two groups by the atomic number of the atom attached. Higher = priority.
  • A tie? Compare the atoms attached next: –CH2CH3 (C, H, H) beats –CH3 (H, H, H).
  • Z (zusammen, together): the two priority groups on the same side.
  • E (entgegen, opposite): on opposite sides.
  • ClCH=CHCl: Cl > H on each carbon. Both Cl on the same side: (Z)-1,2-dichloroethene.
  • BrClC=CHCH3: Br > Cl on C1; CH3 > H on C2. Br and CH3 on opposite sides: E.
Mission 3

cis–trans: a special case of E/Z

  • cis–trans applies when one group on each C=C carbon is the same.
  • With an H on each carbon: cis = Z, trans = E.
  • Without that H, they can differ. 2-Bromobut-2-ene, CH3CBr=CHCH3: methyls on the same side = cis.
  • But on C2, Br outranks CH3. So the priority groups (Br on C2, CH3 on C3) are opposite: E.
  • No shared group (BrClC=CHCH3)? Only E/Z can name it.
Quick check
?In one stereoisomer of 2-chlorobut-2-ene, CH3CCl=CHCH3, the Cl atom and the H atom are on the same side of the C=C. Which is correct?
Quick check
?Which compound can exist as E and Z stereoisomers about its C=C bond?
Mission 3

Chiral centres and enantiomers

  • A chiral centre (an asymmetric carbon) is bonded to four different groups.
  • It gives two optical isomers, or enantiomers: non-superimposable mirror images.
  • Example: 2-hydroxypropanoic acid (lactic acid), CH3CH(OH)COOH. C2 carries H, OH, CH3 and COOH.
  • Draw it in 3D: two bonds in the plane, a wedge towards you, a dash away (tetrahedral, 109.5°).
  • Draw the other enantiomer as the mirror image. No rotation makes the two match.
Mission 3

Spotting a chiral centre

  • Only a carbon with four single bonds can be chiral: CH3, CH2 and C=C carbons never are.
  • Compare whole groups, not just the atom attached.
  • 1-Chloro-2-methylbutane, CH2ClCH(CH3)CH2CH3: C2 carries H, CH3, C2H5 and CH2Cl: chiral.
  • Pentan-3-ol, CH3CH2CH(OH)CH2CH3: C3 carries two C2H5 groups: not chiral.
  • Mark a chiral centre with an asterisk: CH3C*H(OH)COOH.
Quick check
?Which carbon atom in 3-methylhexane, CH3CH2CH(CH3)CH2CH2CH3, is a chiral centre?
Mission 3

Optical activity and racemic mixtures

  • Each enantiomer rotates the plane of plane-polarised light: by equal angles, in opposite directions. It is optically active.
  • Background for the video: optical activity and racemates are not tested in your specification.
  • A racemic mixture (racemate): equal amounts of both enantiomers. The rotations cancel: optically inactive.
  • Enantiomers share melting point, boiling point, density and most reactions.
  • They behave differently only in a chiral environment: an enzyme, a receptor.
  • Your smell receptors are chiral. Each carvone enantiomer fits them differently: spearmint or caraway.
Mission 3

Where chirality matters

  • Optical isomers turn up in many natural compounds: amino acids, sugars, drugs.
  • Complexes show stereoisomerism too: cis–trans (cisplatin, Pt(NH3)2Cl2, is the cis isomer) and optical isomers with three bidentate ligands, e.g. [Ni(NH2CH2CH2NH2)3]²⁺. More in the complexes mission.
Mission check

?Butan-2-ol, CH3CH(OH)CH2CH3, exists as two stereoisomers with identical boiling points. Which explains why there are two?

Same as carvone. Mirror-image molecules share atoms, bonds and boiling point; only a chiral setting, like your nose, tells them apart.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Hydrogen bromide can add to propene two ways round, so why does one product win almost every time?

Make a guess: Hydrogen bromide can add to propene two ways round, so why does one product win almost every time?

Mission 4

Two ways round

  • Propene, CH3CH=CH2, is unsymmetrical: its two C=C carbons carry different groups.
  • H–Br can add two ways:
  • H to C1, Br to C2 → CH3CHBrCH3, 2-bromopropane.
  • H to C2, Br to C1 → CH3CH2CH2Br, 1-bromopropane.
  • The H⁺ adds first. Where it goes decides where the C⁺ ends up, and so where Br goes.
Mission 4

Carbocations: primary, secondary, tertiary

  • Count the carbon (alkyl) groups bonded to the C⁺.
  • Primary: one, e.g. CH3CH2CH2⁺. Secondary: two, e.g. CH3CH⁺CH3. Tertiary: three, e.g. (CH3)3C⁺.
  • Stability: tertiary > secondary > primary.
Quick check
?Which carbocation is the most stable?
Mission 4

The more stable carbocation wins

  • Route 1: H⁺ to C1 (the CH2 end) → secondary CH3CH⁺CH3.
  • Route 2: H⁺ to C2 → primary CH3CH2CH2⁺.
  • The route via the more stable carbocation has the lower activation energy, so it is much faster.
  • So 2-bromopropane is the major product; 1-bromopropane is the minor product.
  • Markownikoff's rule: H adds to the C=C carbon that already has more H atoms. Explain it by carbocation stability.
Mission 4

Drawing it

  • Draw the mechanism through the more stable carbocation for the major product.
  • Arrow 1: from the C=C bond to the Hδ+ of HBr. Arrow 2: from the H–Br bond to Br.
  • H bonds to the CH2 end; C⁺ is on the middle carbon (secondary).
  • Arrow 3: from a lone pair on Br⁻ to that C⁺ → 2-bromopropane.
  • The minor product forms by the same arrows, through the primary carbocation.
Quick check
?2-methylbut-1-ene, CH2=C(CH3)CH2CH3, reacts with hydrogen bromide. What is the major product, and why?
Mission 4

Same rule, other reagents

  • Water with an acid catalyst: propene → mainly propan-2-ol; minor propan-1-ol.
  • The electrophile (H⁺ or Hδ+) adds first. The nucleophile goes to the more substituted carbon.
  • Symmetrical alkenes (e.g. but-2-ene) give one product: no major or minor.
Quick check
?2-methylpropene, (CH3)2C=CH2, reacts with water in the presence of an acid catalyst. What is the major organic product?
Mission check

?1-methylcyclohexene is a six-membered ring with one C=C. One C=C carbon carries a CH3 group and no H; the other carries one H. It reacts with hydrogen bromide. What is the major product, and why?

The two directions give different carbocations. The route via the more stable one is faster, so its product is the major one. For propene + HBr, that is 2-bromopropane.

Mission 5

Mission 5: When a plastic softens in heat, what is breaking?

Make a guess: When a plastic softens in heat, what is breaking?

Mission 5

Polymers

  • A polymer is a very long molecule made of many small monomers joined by covalent bonds.
  • Neighbouring chains are held together only by intermolecular forces.
  • Heat a plastic such as poly(ethene): the chains gain energy, overcome the forces between them and slide past each other. It softens.
  • The covalent bonds along each chain stay intact.
Mission 5

Addition polymerisation

  • One bond of each monomer's C=C breaks. The carbons join neighbouring monomers by single bonds.
  • Nothing else is made: the polymer contains every atom of the monomers.
  • Ethene → poly(ethene): [–CH2–CH2–]n.
  • Propene → poly(propene): [–CH2–CH(CH3)–]n. The CH3 is a side group.
  • The repeating unit has the two former C=C carbons in the chain, with extension bonds at each end.
  • Substituted alkenes too: chloroethene, CH2=CHCl → poly(chloroethene), PVC.
Quick check
?Propene, CH2=CHCH3, forms the addition polymer poly(propene), written as [repeating unit]n. Which option gives the repeating unit and describes it correctly?
Mission 5

Back to the monomer

  • In a section of chain, find the repeating unit: two main-chain carbons for an addition polymer.
  • Keep each side group on the carbon it is attached to.
  • Remove the extension bonds and put a C=C between the two carbons.
  • –CH2–CHCl–CH2–CHCl– → repeating unit –CH2–CHCl– → monomer CH2=CHCl.
  • Check: the monomer has the same atoms as the repeating unit.
Quick check
?A section of an addition polymer is –CH2–C(CH3)2–CH2–C(CH3)2–CH2–C(CH3)2–. Which is the monomer?
Mission 5

Why polyalkenes last

  • A polyalkene is saturated: its chains have only strong C–C and C–H bonds.
  • These bonds are non-polar, so nucleophiles and electrophiles are not attracted.
  • So polyalkenes are unreactive: ideal for containers and pipes.
  • They are also non-biodegradable: microorganisms cannot break them down, so they persist for many years.
Mission 5

Landfill or incineration?

  • Landfill: cheap and simple, but polyalkenes persist, use land, and the material is lost.
  • Incineration (combustion): releases energy to generate heat or electricity, and cuts waste volume.
  • But it releases CO2, and can form toxic gases.
  • Burning PVC releases HCl, a toxic, acidic gas.
  • Remove it by passing the waste gases through a base (e.g. calcium oxide or carbonate): a scrubber.
Mission 5

Recycling

  • First, sort waste polymers into specific types.
  • Mechanical recycling: wash, shred, melt and remould into new products.
  • Saves crude oil and much of the energy used to make new polymer.
  • But: sorting and cleaning cost money; quality falls each time the polymer is reprocessed.
  • Feedstock recycling: heat or crack the polymer into small molecules, used to make new plastics and other chemicals.
Mission 5

Polymers that break down

  • Biodegradable polymers are broken down by microorganisms, e.g. poly(lactic acid), made from plant starch.
  • Often they need the right conditions, such as an industrial composter.
  • Photodegradable polymers contain bonds that absorb light, so the chains break into smaller pieces.
  • They need light, so they do not degrade when buried in landfill.
  • Benefits: less persistent plastic waste and, for plant-based polymers, less use of finite crude oil.
Quick check
?A council burns mixed plastic waste, including PVC, to generate electricity. Which extra step deals with the main problem caused by burning the PVC?
Mission 5

Addition vs condensation

  • Addition: monomers have a C=C. No other product. Main chain of carbon atoms only.
  • Condensation: each monomer has two functional groups (e.g. –OH, –COOH, –NH2).
  • A small molecule (H2O or HCl) is lost at each link. The chain contains ester or amide links.
  • Spot the type from the monomer: C=C → addition; two reacting groups → condensation.
Mission check

?Poly(propene) can be heated until soft, moulded into a new shape, and cooled. The same plastic can be remoulded many times, and its chemical properties do not change. Which statement explains this?

When a plastic softens, nothing covalent breaks. The forces between the chains are overcome, and the chains slide past each other.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Alkanes burn completely to CO2 and H2O; with limited oxygen they give toxic CO and soot.
  • Strong, almost non-polar σ-bonds make alkanes unreactive; longer chains boil higher, branched ones lower.

Mission 2

  • Plenty of O2: C → CO2, H → H2O. Limited O2: CO or C (soot), and a yellow, smoky flame.
  • CO is toxic: it binds to haemoglobin, so blood carries less O2.

Mission 3

  • E/Z: restricted rotation about C=C plus two different groups on each carbon; name by CIP priorities.
  • A chiral centre (four different groups) gives enantiomers: equal and opposite rotation of plane-polarised light; a racemate is inactive; they differ only in chiral environments.

Mission 4

  • Unsymmetrical alkene + HX: two carbocations possible. Stability: tertiary > secondary > primary.
  • The more stable carbocation forms faster, giving the major product; the other route gives the minor product.

Mission 5

  • Addition polymer: C=C opens; repeating unit = two main-chain carbons with their side groups; monomer has the same atoms.
  • Softening overcomes intermolecular forces between chains; covalent bonds stay intact.
  • Polyalkenes are unreactive and persist: landfill, incineration (remove HCl), recycling, or degradable alternatives.
