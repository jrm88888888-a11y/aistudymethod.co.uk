Mission 1

What demand means

  • Demand: the quantity of a good that buyers are willing and able to buy at each price, over a given time period.
  • Law of demand: as price rises, quantity demanded falls, ceteris paribus (other things equal).
  • A demand schedule lists the quantity demanded at each price. Illustrative data:

  • Demand is the whole curve, not one point on it.

Mission 1

Why the curve slopes down

  • Diminishing marginal utility: each extra unit consumed adds less satisfaction than the one before.
  • So a buyer is willing to pay less for the 4th cup than for the 1st. To sell more, the price must fall.
  • When the price of a good falls, two effects raise quantity demanded:
  • Substitution effect: the good is now cheaper relative to other goods, so buyers switch towards it.
  • Income effect: the same money income now buys more, so real income (purchasing power) rises and buyers can afford more.
  • Say: "the price fall raises real income" — not: "the price fall raises people's income".
Quick check
?The price of bus tickets falls while the price of train tickets is unchanged. Which describes the substitution effect for a commuter?
Mission 1

Movement or shift?

  • A change in the good's own price → a movement along D.
  • A change in anything else that affects buyers → a shift of the whole curve.
  • Step 1: price falls from P1 to P2 → slide along D from point a to point b.
  • Step 2: D1 → D2 to the right: more is demanded at every price.
  • Say: "a fall in price causes an extension of demand (a rise in quantity demanded)" — not: "demand increases".
  • A price rise → contraction of demand. A shift right → increase in demand; shift left → decrease.

Mission 2

What "rational" means

  • Economics models choices as rational: agents weigh benefits against costs and pick the option that best meets their objective.
  • Consumers aim to maximise utility (satisfaction).
  • Firms aim to maximise profit (total revenue − total cost).
  • Governments aim to maximise social welfare (the well-being of society as a whole).
  • Three assumptions: rationality, utility maximisation, perfect information (consumers know every option and its price).
Mission 2

Total and marginal utility

  • Total utility (TU): the whole satisfaction from all units consumed.
  • Marginal utility (MU): the change in TU from one more unit. MU = ΔTU ÷ ΔQ.
  • Slices of pizza at one meal (illustrative data, in "utils"):
SlicesTUMU

  • Law of diminishing marginal utility: as more units are consumed, each extra unit adds less utility.
  • TU still rises while MU is positive. TU is at its maximum where MU = 0.
  • Say: "marginal utility falls" — not: "total utility falls" (TU rises until MU turns negative).

Mission 2

From diminishing MU to the demand curve

  • A consumer buys one more unit only if its extra satisfaction is worth at least the price.
  • MU falls with each unit, so the most the consumer will pay for each extra unit falls too.
  • To persuade them to buy more, the price must be lower.
  • So quantity demanded rises as price falls: the demand curve slopes downward.
  • Say: "a fall in price causes an extension of demand" (a movement along D) — not: "demand increases".
Quick check
?Which statement best explains, using utility, why a demand curve usually slopes downward?
Mission 2

Decide at the margin: MB = MC

  • Marginal benefit (MB): extra benefit from one more unit of an activity. Marginal cost (MC): extra cost of that unit.
  • Rule: do one more unit while MB ≥ MC. Stop where MB = MC.
  • A café decides how many extra hours to open (illustrative data, money values):

  • Not where total benefit is biggest (5 hours), and not where MB − MC is biggest (hour 1).

Mission 2

Water and diamonds

  • The paradox of value: water is vital, diamonds are not, yet diamonds cost far more.
  • Water: plentiful. People already use a lot, so the MU of one more litre is tiny.
  • Diamonds: scarce. Few are owned, so the MU of one more diamond is high.
  • Price reflects marginal utility (what the next unit is worth), not total utility.
  • TU from water is enormous. MU of the last litre is small.
Mission 2Evaluate

How useful is the rational model?

  • Utility cannot be measured directly. "Utils" are a teaching device; economists infer preferences from choices.
  • It depends on information: real consumers rarely know every option and price.
  • It depends on effort and stakes: people use habits and shortcuts for small, frequent buys.
  • The model still predicts the direction of many responses well, e.g. buying less when price rises.
  • More on the limits: a later lesson.
Mission check

?A hiker has had no water all day on a hot trail and would pay a large amount for one bottle. At home, after drinking plenty, she would not pay even a small amount for one more bottle. Which explanation is best?

What we pay depends on how much one more unit adds, not on how much the good matters in total.

Mission 3

What supply means

  • Supply: the quantity that producers are willing and able to sell at each price, over a given time period.
  • Law of supply: as price rises, quantity supplied rises, ceteris paribus.
  • Why? The profit incentive: a higher price makes extra output more profitable, so firms produce more.
  • Illustrative data: a strawberry farm.

  • Plot it: the supply curve (S) slopes upwards.
Mission 3

Why supply slopes up: rising marginal cost

  • Marginal cost (MC): the extra cost of producing one more unit.
  • In the short run some inputs are fixed (land, machines). Add more workers and each adds less extra output: diminishing marginal returns.
  • So each extra unit costs more to make: MC rises.
  • A firm that is a price taker sells every unit at the market price. It produces each unit whose MC is no higher than the price.
  • Illustrative data:
Unit1st2nd3rd4th5th6th
MC2346811
  • Price 6 → produce 4 units (the 5th would cost 8). Price 8 → produce 5.
  • A higher price makes higher-cost units worth producing. So the supply curve traces the MC curve.

Mission 3

From one firm to the market

  • Individual supply: one producer's schedule. Market supply: all producers together.
  • At each price, add the quantities of every producer: a horizontal sum.
  • Illustrative data: at a price of 6, Farm A supplies 4 tonnes and Farm B supplies 3 tonnes → market supply 7 tonnes.
  • More firms → market supply rises at every price.
Quick check
?At a price of 5, three bakeries supply 40, 30 and 20 loaves a day. A fourth bakery opens and supplies 30 loaves at that price. What is market quantity supplied at a price of 5 now?
Mission 3

Movement or shift?

  • A change in the good's own price → a movement along S.
  • A change in anything else that affects producers → a shift of the whole curve.
  • Step 1: price rises P1 → P2 → slide up along S.
  • Step 2: S1 → S2 to the right = increase in supply: more offered at every price.
  • A shift left = decrease in supply.
  • Say: "an increase in supply shifts the curve right" — not: "the supply curve shifts up". A rightward shift looks lower on the graph.
  • Say: "a rise in price causes an extension of supply" — not: "supply increases".

  • The full list: costs of factors of production, prices of related goods, indirect taxes and subsidies, future price expectations, changes in technology, number of firms.

Mission 3

Real case: frost hits coffee

  • July 2021: frosts hit coffee-growing areas of Brazil, the world's largest coffee producer.
  • Damaged trees mean less coffee for sale at every price in later harvests.
  • That is a shock to supply: S shifts left, not a movement along it.
  • The price rise that followed is the result of the shift; it does not cause the shift.
Mission 3

Joint and competitive supply

  • Joint supply: producing one good automatically produces another. Cattle give beef and leather.
  • More beef produced → supply of leather increases.
  • Competitive supply: the same resources can make either good. A field can grow wheat or barley.
  • Barley price rises → farmers switch land → supply of wheat decreases.
  • Say: "a change in the price of a related good shifts supply" — not: "moves along it".
Quick check
?The price of oranges rises and orange growers sell more oranges. Nothing else changes. How should an economist describe this?
Mission check

?Which change causes a decrease in the supply of electric scooters?

Only the good's own price moves producers along S. A change in costs, technology, taxes, subsidies or the number of firms shifts it.

Mission 4

Short run and long run

  • Fixed factor: an input whose quantity cannot be changed quickly, e.g. a kitchen, ovens, a factory building.
  • Variable factor: an input that can be changed quickly, e.g. hours of work, ingredients, energy.
  • Short run: at least one factor of production is fixed.
  • Long run: all factors are variable. The firm can build a bigger kitchen.
  • The split is not a set number of months: a market stall can grow in weeks; a power station takes years.
Mission 4

Total, average and marginal product

  • Total product (TP): total output from a given number of workers.
  • Average product (AP): output per worker. AP = TP ÷ number of workers.
  • Marginal product (MP): the extra output from one more worker. MP = change in TP ÷ change in workers.
  • The fixed factor stays the same throughout.

Mission 4

The law of diminishing returns

  • Law of diminishing marginal returns: in the short run, as more of a variable factor is added to a fixed factor, the marginal product of the variable factor eventually falls.
  • Why: each extra cook has less of the fixed factor to work with: fewer ovens and less space per cook.
  • It is not because later cooks are worse: all cooks are assumed identical.
  • It applies only in the short run. In the long run nothing is fixed.
  • Diminishing marginal returns mean marginal costs rise as output rises, so firms need a higher price to supply more: this underlies the law of supply.
  • Say: "diminishing marginal returns: MP falls" — not: "total output falls".

Mission 4

From product to cost

  • Suppose each cook is paid the same wage per hour, and labour is the only variable cost.
  • Marginal cost (MC) = wage ÷ MP: the cost of one more meal.
  • Illustrative data: wage 60 per hour, same café kitchen.
    • 2nd cook: MP 14 → MC = 60 ÷ 14 ≈ 4.29.
    • 4th cook: MP 8 → MC = 60 ÷ 8 = 7.50.
    • 5th cook: MP 4 → MC = 60 ÷ 4 = 15.
  • Rising MP → falling MC. Falling MP → rising MC.
  • So diminishing marginal returns give the upward-sloping part of the MC curve (cost curves in Mission 5).
Quick check

Quick check
?A bakery has one oven. Illustrative data: with 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 bakers it makes 8, 20, 30, 36 and 38 trays a day. With which baker do diminishing marginal returns set in?
Mission check

?A call centre has 20 desks and phones, which it cannot add to this year. It hires more staff for each shift. Which outcome is most likely as staff are added?

With one input fixed, adding more of another eventually brings smaller and smaller gains.

Mission 5

Fixed and variable costs

  • Fixed costs (FC, or total fixed cost TFC): do not change with output in the short run. Rent, insurance, loan interest, salaried staff.
  • Fixed costs must be paid even at zero output.
  • Variable costs (VC, or total variable cost TVC): change with output. Ingredients, energy, packaging, hourly wages.
  • Total cost (TC) = fixed cost + variable cost.
Mission 5

Average and marginal cost

  • Average fixed cost: AFC = TFC ÷ Q.
  • Average variable cost: AVC = TVC ÷ Q.
  • Average total cost (often just average cost, AC): ATC = TC ÷ Q = AFC + AVC.
  • Marginal cost: the extra cost of producing one more unit. MC = change in TC ÷ change in Q.
  • Fixed costs do not change, so MC is also the change in variable cost.
  • Say: "marginal cost is the cost of the next unit" — not: "the average cost of all units".

Quick check
?A firm's fixed costs are 200 a week. When it produces 40 units, its total variable cost is 600. Illustrative data. What is its average total cost at 40 units?
Mission 5

Why the curves have their shapes

  • MC falls at first, then rises. Why: with the ovens fixed, diminishing marginal returns set in. Each extra worker adds less output, so each extra batch needs more labour (MC = wage ÷ MP).
  • AFC falls all the time: the same fixed cost spread over more units.
  • AVC and ATC are U-shaped:
    • below its minimum, MC pulls the average down
    • above it, MC pulls the average up
  • So MC cuts AVC and ATC at their lowest points (bakery: AVC lowest at 3 batches, ATC at 4).
  • The gap between ATC and AVC is AFC, so it narrows as output rises.
  • Rising marginal cost is why a firm needs a higher price to supply more: an assumption behind the law of supply.

Mission 5

What shifts the cost curves

  • Higher input prices (wages, energy, raw materials): AVC, ATC and MC shift up. AFC unchanged.
  • Higher fixed costs (rent, insurance, a licence fee): AFC and ATC shift up. AVC and MC unchanged.
  • Higher productivity (better technology, training, organisation): each worker makes more, so MC, AVC and ATC shift down.
  • Say: "a rise in fixed cost shifts ATC" — not: "it shifts MC".

Mission 6

Mission 6: How can a change in one market move prices in a completely different one?

Make a guess: The price of fuel jumps. What happens to sales of big cars that use a lot of fuel?

Mission 6

Joint supply: by-products

  • Joint supply: making one good automatically makes another.
  • Cattle → beef and leather. Crude oil refining → petrol, diesel and jet fuel.
  • Step 1: demand for beef rises → more cattle are reared and slaughtered.
  • Step 2: more hides → supply of leather shifts right.
  • Step 3: leather price falls, quantity rises.
  • The two goods move in opposite price directions here.
Mission 6

Case: carbon dioxide, 2021

  • In the UK, food-grade CO₂ is a by-product of making fertiliser from natural gas.
  • In 2021, gas prices nearly quadrupled. In September, the fertiliser firm CF Industries halted its two UK plants.
  • Those plants made about 60% of the UK's food-grade CO₂.
  • CO₂ supply fell sharply. Poultry processors (which use it to stun birds) and drinks makers (for the fizz) warned of disruption. The government told food firms to expect CO₂ prices to rise about 400%.
  • The government agreed short-term financial support to restart one plant, to give the market time to adjust.
  • A gas market shock cut the supply of CO₂ and raised its price, in a market that seems to have nothing to do with gas.
Mission 6

Competitive supply: one resource, two uses

  • Competitive supply: firms can make one good or another with the same resources.
  • A farmer's field: wheat or barley. A factory line: cars or vans.
  • Price of barley rises → farmers switch land to barley → supply of wheat shifts left.
  • Wheat price rises, quantity falls.
  • Joint and competitive supply are non-price determinants of supply: they shift the curve.

Quick check

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Demand is the whole schedule or curve; its own price moves you along it, every other factor shifts it.
  • Price fall: extension of demand; non-price change: increase or decrease in demand.
  • The curve slopes down because of diminishing marginal utility.
  • A price fall also works through the substitution effect and the income effect.

Mission 2

  • Marginal utility falls as consumption rises; this explains a downward-sloping demand curve.
  • Rational choice: keep going while MB ≥ MC; the best choice is where MB = MC.

Mission 3

  • Supply slopes up because a higher price makes extra output profitable; own price moves you along S, every other factor shifts it.
  • Rising marginal cost is why: a price taker supplies each unit whose MC is no higher than the price.
  • Increase in supply = shift right (it looks lower), decrease = shift left.

Mission 4

  • Short run: at least one factor fixed. Long run: all factors variable.
  • Law of diminishing marginal returns: adding a variable factor to a fixed factor eventually makes MP fall.
  • MC = wage ÷ MP, so falling MP means rising MC.

Mission 5

  • TC = FC + VC; fixed costs are paid even at zero output.
  • AFC, AVC, ATC are costs per unit; MC is the cost of the next unit.
  • Diminishing returns make MC rise; MC cuts AVC and ATC at their lowest points.
  • Input prices move AVC, ATC and MC; fixed costs move AFC and ATC; productivity gains shift costs down.

Mission 6

  • Joint supply: more of one product means more of its by-product.
  • Competitive supply: more of one product means less of the other.
