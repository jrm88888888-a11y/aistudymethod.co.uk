Mission 1: A desert fox has enormous ears and almost never drinks. How did it end up so well suited to the desert?

Make a guess: A desert fox has enormous ears and almost never drinks. How did it end up so well suited to the desert?

Mission 1

What is an adaptation?

  • An adaptation is an inherited feature that increases an organism's chance of surviving and reproducing in its environment.
  • It arises by natural selection: random variation exists; individuals with a useful allele survive and breed more; the allele becomes more common over generations.
  • Individuals do not adapt in their lifetime. Populations change over generations.
  • An adaptation helps in one environment; the same trait may be useless or harmful in another.
  • Say: "individuals with the allele survived and bred more" — not: "the fox adapted because it needed to".
Mission 1

Three kinds of adaptation

  • Anatomical: body structure. Fennec fox: huge ears with blood vessels near the surface radiate heat.
  • Physiological: body processes. Fennec fox: kidneys conserve water; it gets most of its water from food.
  • Behavioural: what it does. Fennec fox: active at night; shelters in a burrow by day.
  • Most organisms show all three, each meeting the same challenge.
Mission 1

Plants built for their habitat

  • Xerophytes live where water is scarce.
  • Example: marram grass (Ammophila arenaria) on sand dunes.
  • Leaf curvature (rolled leaves); sunken stomata; hairs: trap humid air near the stomata.
  • Thick cuticle; reduced surface area (e.g. spines instead of leaves).
  • Say: "trapped humid air lowers the water vapour gradient" — not: "rolled leaves collect water".
Quick check
?In dry, windy weather the leaves of marram grass roll inwards, so that the stomata, which lie in grooves on the inner surface, open into an enclosed space lined with hairs. How does this help marram grass to survive on a sand dune, where water drains away quickly?
Mission 1

Mangroves, deserts and rainforests

  • Mangrove trees (tidal mud, salty, almost no oxygen): Avicennia sends up pneumatophores (air roots) and secretes salt from salt glands on its leaves; Rhizophora has arching prop roots and its roots exclude most salt.
  • Hot desert, saguaro cactus (Carnegiea gigantea): spines instead of leaves; thick waxy cuticle; pleated stem swells to store water; shallow, wide roots catch brief rain; stomata open at night (CAM).
  • Hot desert, fennec fox (Vulpes zerda): ears radiate heat; nocturnal; rarely drinks.
  • Rainforest, kapok tree (Ceiba pentandra): buttress roots support a trunk up to 60 m tall in shallow soil; leaves with drip tips shed water.
  • Rainforest, black-handed spider monkey (Ateles geoffroyi): prehensile tail acts as a fifth limb in the canopy.
Quick check
?The grey mangrove (Avicennia marina) grows in tidal mud that is waterlogged and almost free of oxygen. From its horizontal roots it sends up pneumatophores, which project vertically out of the mud into the air. What is the function of the pneumatophores?
Mission 1

Eat or be eaten

  • Herbivores: leaf-eating insects have piercing mouthparts (aphids, into phloem sap) or chewing mouthparts (caterpillars).
  • Plants resist: thorns, spines and tough leaves; toxic secondary compounds in leaves and seeds.
  • Some herbivores detoxify them: koala liver enzymes break down the terpenes of eucalyptus leaves.
  • Predators: chemical (rattlesnake venom), physical (claws, teeth, a leopard's camouflage), behavioural (wolves hunting in packs).
  • Prey: chemical (skunk spray), physical (porcupine quills, camouflage), behavioural (zebras in herds, alarm calls).
Quick check
?Which statement correctly classifies an adaptation as chemical, physical or behavioural, and as belonging to a predator or a prey animal?
Mission 1

Reaching the light

  • In a forest, light is the limiting resource. Plants have different strategies:
  • Canopy trees: grow tall, fast, to reach full sun.
  • Lianas: rooted in the soil; climb up trees to the canopy without building a thick trunk.
  • Epiphytes: grow on branches, high in the light; not parasites; take water from rain and air.
  • Strangler epiphytes (strangler figs): germinate on a high branch, then send roots down to the soil.
  • Shade-tolerant shrubs and herbs: photosynthesise at the low light intensity of the forest floor.
Quick check
?Which pairing of a rainforest plant with its strategy for harvesting light is correct?
Mission 1

Same problem, same solution

  • Convergent evolution: unrelated groups evolve similar features because they face similar selection pressures.
  • The features are analogous: same function, different evolutionary origin.
  • Wings of birds and of insects: both for flight, built from completely different structures.
  • Biomes: similar climates on different continents select similar forms. American cacti and African euphorbias both have swollen, water-storing stems and spines.
  • Say: "similar because of similar selection" — not: "similar because closely related".
Quick check
?Cacti of the deserts of the Americas and euphorbias of the deserts of southern Africa both have swollen, water-storing stems and spines, yet they belong to different plant families. Which explanation is correct?
Mission check

?Arctic foxes have much smaller ears than fennec foxes. Which statement best explains how small ears became typical of Arctic foxes?

The fennec's big ears and the Arctic fox's small ones arose the same way: selection acting on inherited variation, in different environments.

Mission 2: One extra chromosome can change a whole body. So how do some plants thrive with doubled sets?

Make a guess: One extra chromosome can change a whole body. So how do some plants thrive with doubled sets?

Mission 2

Gene or chromosome mutation?

  • Gene mutation: a change in the bases of one gene.
  • Chromosome mutation: a change in the number or structure of whole chromosomes.
  • One chromosome carries hundreds of genes. Gain or lose one, and hundreds of genes change dose at once.
  • Development depends on genes being in the right balance.
  • A whole extra set keeps every gene in proportion. Many plants live well with one.
Mission 2

Non-disjunction in meiosis

  • Normal meiosis: each pair of homologous chromosomes separates (meiosis I); then each pair of chromatids separates (meiosis II).
  • Non-disjunction: a pair fails to separate. Both go to the same pole.
  • (1) In meiosis I: all four gametes are abnormal. Two have one extra chromosome (n + 1); two have one missing (n − 1).
  • (2) In meiosis II: two gametes are normal, one is n + 1, one is n − 1.
  • Fertilisation by a normal gamete gives a zygote with 2n + 1 or 2n − 1. Every cell inherits it by mitosis.
Mission 2

Worked example: counting chromosomes

  • Human body cells: 2n = 46. Normal gametes: n = 23.
  • Non-disjunction of one pair gives gametes with 24 or 22.
  • Zygote: 24 + 23 = 47 (one chromosome present three times).
  • Zygote: 22 + 23 = 45 (one chromosome present once).
  • Error in meiosis I: gametes 24, 24, 22, 22. Error in meiosis II: 24, 22, 23, 23.
Mission 2

Down syndrome

  • Down syndrome: three copies of chromosome 21 (trisomy 21). 47 chromosomes in each cell.
  • Most cases: non-disjunction of chromosome 21 in meiosis, usually in the egg-forming cells of the mother.
  • The chance rises with maternal age. One explanation: eggs pause part-way through meiosis I for decades, and the links holding chromosomes together weaken.
  • Effects vary widely between people; they include some degree of learning disability and a raised chance of heart defects.
  • The genes on chromosome 21 are normal. The problem is their dose.

Mission check

?A species has 2n = 8. In one cell undergoing meiosis, one pair of homologous chromosomes fails to separate in meiosis I; meiosis II is normal. How many chromosomes are in each of the four gametes?

A normal gamete (4) fertilising these gives 9 or 7: one chromosome too many or too few, which unbalances hundreds of genes. A doubled set keeps them balanced.

Mission 3

Extinction, then and now

  • Species have always gone extinct, at a slow background rate.
  • Today the rate is far higher. Vertebrate species lost since 1900 would have taken 800–10,000 years to disappear at the background rate (depending on the group).
  • This is the sixth mass extinction. The first five had natural causes; this one is caused by human activity.
  • Yet fossils suggest more species are alive today than at any time in the past. The concern is the rate of loss.

Quick check

Over 10 years, the number of records of a rare species rose from 40 to 300, while the number of participants rose from 5 000 to 60 000. Which statement about this evidence is correct?
Mission 3

What is driving the losses?

  • Overarching cause: human population growth. It drives every specific cause.
  • Habitat loss: deforestation, clearance for farming, urbanisation, drainage of wetlands.
  • Over-exploitation: hunting, overfishing, collecting.
  • Pollution: pesticides, fertiliser run-off, plastics.
  • Invasive species, pests and diseases, spread by global transport.
Quick check
?Which factor is the overarching cause of the current biodiversity crisis, driving each of the specific causes?
Mission 3

Three case studies

  • North Island giant moa (Dinornis novaezealandiae): flightless; slow to mature; few eggs. Extinct within about two centuries of human arrival (~1300 CE). Causes: hunting, egg-taking, forest burning.
  • Caribbean monk seal (Neomonachus tropicalis): killed for blubber oil from the 1490s; colonies disturbed; prey overfished. Last confirmed sighting 1952.
  • Mixed dipterocarp forest, Southeast Asia (an ecosystem): logged for hardwood, then cleared and burned for oil palm and pulpwood. Fires spread through logged, drained land.
  • A plantation keeps tree cover but is not the forest ecosystem.
  • Study one local extinction too: the IB asks for an example familiar to you.
Quick check
?Polynesian settlers reached New Zealand around 1300 CE. Within about two centuries the North Island giant moa (Dinornis novaezealandiae), a flightless bird that had evolved with no mammalian predators, matured slowly and laid few eggs, was extinct. Archaeological sites contain large numbers of butchered moa bones and moa eggshell. Which explanation of the extinction is best supported?
Quick check
?Mixed dipterocarp forest once covered the lowlands of Borneo, Sumatra and the Malay Peninsula. Which description of how this ecosystem has been lost is correct?
Mission 3

Invasive species

  • An invasive species is introduced by humans (deliberately or by accident) and spreads, harming native species.
  • In its new range it often has no predators, parasites or competitors that held it in check.
  • It can outcompete native species for food, light or space.
  • Example: feral goats on the Galápagos islands ate the vegetation that endemic giant tortoises feed on. Goats breed fast and had no predators there. Eradication programmes have removed them from several islands.
  • Endemic species, found only in one place, are especially vulnerable: they cannot be replaced from elsewhere.
Quick check
?Zebra mussels, small filter-feeding mussels native to Eurasia, were unintentionally introduced into North American lakes, where they had few predators. They filter large volumes of water, removing much of the plankton, and attach in dense clusters to hard surfaces, including the shells of native mussels. Predict the most likely effect on populations of native mussels in these lakes.
Mission check

?In the early 1800s the passenger pigeon was probably the most numerous bird in North America, with flocks of billions. Through the 1800s it was hunted on a commercial scale, and the eastern forests where it fed and nested were cleared for farming. The last known bird died in a zoo in 1914. Which explanation is best supported?

Extinction is natural; this pace is not. Human activity is removing species far faster than new ones arise.

Mission 4

Why conserve biodiversity?

  • Ecological: species depend on each other. Losing a keystone species changes the whole community.
  • Genetic resource: wild relatives of crops carry alleles for disease resistance and drought tolerance.
  • Economic: food, timber, medicines, tourism; healthy soils (continuous monoculture depletes them).
  • Aesthetic: landscapes and wildlife people value and enjoy.
Mission 4

In situ conservation

  • In situ: protecting a species in its natural habitat.
  • Nature reserves and wildlife reserves.
  • Reserves need active management: control invasive species and poaching; keep habitats suitable.
  • Advantage: the species keeps its habitat, its interactions and its natural selection pressures.
Mission 4

Ex situ conservation

  • Ex situ: conserving a species outside its natural habitat.
  • Zoos: captive breeding; studbooks pair unrelated animals to limit inbreeding; reintroduction.
  • Botanic gardens: living collections of rare plants; propagation; research.
  • Seed banks: seeds dried and frozen, then germinated and tested at intervals. Kew's Millennium Seed Bank holds seeds of over 40,000 species.
  • Tissue banks store germ plasm of species whose seeds die when dried or frozen.
Quick check
?Seeds of many tropical rainforest trees are killed by the drying and freezing used in seed banks. A conservation programme wants to conserve one such tree species, whose natural forest is being logged. Which approach to conserving this species is appropriate?
Mission 4

EDGE: which species first?

  • Money and effort are limited, so conservation must prioritise.
  • EDGE = Evolutionarily Distinct and Globally Endangered.
  • Evolutionarily distinct: few or no close relatives. Losing it loses a long branch of the tree of life.
  • Globally endangered: high risk of extinction (IUCN Red List category).
  • Highest priority: species that score high on both, e.g. the aye-aye of Madagascar.
  • Which species to save also has ethical, economic, cultural and political sides. These need debate.
Quick check
?The EDGE of Existence programme scores species for Evolutionary Distinctiveness (ED) and Global Endangerment (GE). Four species are assessed: W has high ED and is Critically Endangered; X has high ED and is of Least Concern; Y has low ED and is Critically Endangered; Z has low ED and is of Least Concern. Which species does the EDGE programme prioritise, and why?
Mission 4

Rewilding and restoring carbon stores

  • Rewilding restores natural processes, then lets them run:
  • reintroduce apex predators and other keystone species (e.g. wolves to Yellowstone, 1995);
  • re-establish connectivity between habitats over large areas;
  • minimise human impact, using ecological management only where needed.
  • Hinewai Reserve, New Zealand: livestock removed; introduced gorse left as a nurse canopy; native forest regenerated through it with minimal interference.
  • Carbon sequestration: afforestation, forest regeneration, and re-wetting peatlands (waterlogging slows decomposition, so carbon builds up as peat).
  • Debate: fast-growing non-native plantations vs native rewilding. The evidence does not yet settle which stores more carbon long term.
Quick check
?Which statement describes the approach used to restore native forest at Hinewai Reserve in New Zealand?
Quick check
?A government wants to sequester carbon on degraded land. Option A is a plantation of a fast-growing non-native conifer, harvested every 40 years. Option B is to allow native woodland to regenerate naturally. Scientists disagree about which is better. Which statement about this debate is justified?
Mission check

?A rare orchid survives in one wild meadow. Its seeds are now stored in a seed bank, and plants grow in a botanic garden. The meadow is due to be ploughed. Orchid seeds need a particular soil fungus to germinate. Which conclusion is best supported?

A species kept only in a zoo or a seed bank survives, but it is not truly saved. Conservation needs the habitat as well.

Mission 5

Mission 5: How can people keep taking food and materials from nature without wrecking it?

Make a guess: How can people keep taking food and materials from nature without wrecking it?

Mission 5

Take less than grows back

  • Sustainable: meets today's needs without reducing what future generations can take.
  • Harvesting is sustainable only if the rate of harvesting is below the rate of replacement.
  • Net replacement = growth + new young (recruitment) − natural deaths.
  • Renewable does not mean it cannot run out.
Quick check
?A stock of Atlantic cod has a biomass of 200 000 tonnes. Each year, growth of the fish and recruitment of young fish add 15% to the biomass, while natural mortality removes 5% of it. What is the annual rate of replacement of the stock, below which the catch must stay for harvesting to be sustainable?
Mission 5

Fishing without emptying the sea

  • Quotas: a maximum catch, set from estimates of the stock.
  • Minimum mesh size: young fish escape and live to breed.
  • Closed seasons and no-take zones: protect spawning fish and nursery areas.
  • Limits on fleet size or days at sea.
  • Warning signs: more effort for the same catch; the fish caught get younger and smaller.
  • Real case: Newfoundland's northern cod. Catches peaked at over 800 000 tonnes in 1968. By 1992 the spawning stock had fallen by about 99%, and the fishery was closed.
Quick checkRead the data
?Illustrative data.
Records for a cod fishery show that the annual catch stayed close to 250 000 tonnes for 20 years. Over the same period the number of fishing days needed to land that catch doubled, and the mean age of the cod caught fell from 8 years to 4 years. What do these data indicate?
Mission 5

Timber and forest products

  • Coppicing: cut a tree near the ground; many new stems regrow; cut again years later.
  • Rotational felling: fell one area at a time; replant; return only when it has regrown.
  • Selective felling: take only some mature trees; the forest structure and habitats remain.
  • Large-scale clear-felling exposes soil to erosion and leaching and destroys habitats.
  • Certification (e.g. FSC timber) lets buyers choose sustainably managed wood.
  • Terrestrial example: Brazil nuts, collected from wild Amazon trees. Where harvest is heavy, surveys find old trees but almost no young ones: replacement has failed.
Mission 5

Can farming last?

  • Soil erosion: bare soil washes or blows away faster than it forms.
  • Leaching of nutrients: lost to rivers (eutrophication).
  • Inputs: fertilisers, pesticides, water and fuel all have to be supplied and paid for.
  • Agrochemical pollution: pesticides harm non-target species and water supplies.
  • Carbon footprint: methane from cattle and rice paddies; nitrous oxide from fertiliser; CO2 from fuel and land clearance.
Quick check
?A farm sprays insecticide and herbicide several times each season. Downstream, insect and amphibian numbers have fallen and residues of the sprays are found in well water. Which factor affecting the sustainability of agriculture do these observations illustrate?
Mission 5

Biomagnification

  • Some pollutants are persistent (not broken down) and fat-soluble (stored in fat, not excreted).
  • A consumer eats many prey and retains the pollutant from all of them.
  • So the concentration rises at each trophic level: highest in top predators.
  • DDT (insecticide): thin eggshells in birds of prey; populations crashed in the 1950s–60s; banned in many countries from the 1970s.
  • Mercury: bacteria convert it to methylmercury; high in tuna and swordfish; mercury from a factory poisoned people at Minamata, Japan (1950s).
  • Say: "the concentration increases up the food chain" — not: "top predators take in more from the water".
Quick check
?Illustrative data, simplified from the pattern in a real estuary.
In a study of an estuary, DDT concentrations were measured in parts per million (ppm): water 0.00005, plankton 0.04, small fish 0.5, larger predatory fish 2, fish-eating birds 25. Which statement explains this pattern?
Mission 5

Plastic in the oceans

  • Most plastics are non-biodegradable: almost no decomposers have enzymes that digest them, so they persist for decades or longer.
  • Macroplastics (bags, nets, bottles): entangle seals and turtles; fill the stomachs of seabirds such as albatross chicks.
  • Sunlight and waves break them into microplastics (under 5 mm). Microbeads and clothing fibres add more.
  • Filter feeders and zooplankton eat microplastics; they carry other pollutants and pass along food chains.
  • Media coverage of research (e.g. Blue Planet II, 2017) changed public opinion; bans on microbeads and single-use bags followed.
Mission check

?Illustrative data.
A seaweed bed with a biomass of 5000 tonnes is harvested for food. Each year, growth adds 30% to the biomass and natural losses remove 10%. The current harvest is 1400 tonnes per year. Which conclusion is correct?

Fish, timber or seaweed: take no more than the net amount that grows back, and nature can keep supplying.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • An adaptation is an inherited feature, favoured by natural selection, that improves survival and reproduction in a particular environment.
  • Adaptations are anatomical (or morphological), physiological or behavioural.
  • Convergent evolution: similar selection pressures give similar (analogous) features in unrelated groups.

Mission 2

  • Non-disjunction in meiosis gives gametes with n + 1 or n − 1; fertilisation then gives 2n + 1 or 2n − 1 in every cell.
  • Down syndrome is trisomy 21, usually from non-disjunction in the mother's meiosis; its chance rises with maternal age.

Mission 3

  • Today's extinction rate is far above the background rate, driven by human activity: habitat loss, over-exploitation, pollution, invasive species and climate change.
  • Human population growth is the overarching cause.

Mission 4

  • In situ conservation keeps species and their interactions in the wild; ex situ (zoos, botanic gardens, seed and tissue banks) is insurance. No single approach is enough.
  • EDGE prioritises distinct, endangered species; rewilding restores natural processes.

Mission 5

  • Harvesting is sustainable only if it stays below net replacement (growth + recruitment − natural deaths).
  • Persistent, fat-soluble pollutants biomagnify: concentration rises at each trophic level.
