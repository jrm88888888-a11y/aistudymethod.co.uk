Mission 1

Mission 1: Was the Buddha a man who lived and died, or something that never dies?

Make a guess: Was the Buddha a man who lived and died, or something that never dies?

Mission 1

Theravada: the Buddha as teacher

  • Siddhartha Gautama was a human being who, by his own effort, became awakened: a sammasambuddha, who found the path alone and taught it.
  • He is unique in this age: there is one Buddha at a time. Earlier Buddhas came before him; Metteyya will come after.
  • He had extraordinary powers and qualities, but he was not a god and did not create the world.
  • At death he entered parinirvana: no further rebirth. He cannot be reached as a person now.
  • What remains is the Dhamma, his teaching, and the Sangha that keeps it.
Mission 1Read the source

"Only preachers"

> "You yourself must make an effort. The Tathagatas (Buddhas) are only preachers." (Dhammapada 276, Müller 1881)

  • In the Pali canon the Buddha tells the sick monk Vakkali, who longed to see him: whoever sees the Dhamma sees me (Samyutta Nikaya 22.87, paraphrase).

Q1 What does Dhammapada 276 say a Buddha does, and what must the follower do?

Show answer

The Buddha shows the way; the follower must make the effort.

Q2 What does the Vakkali story suggest the Buddha's true "body" is?

Show answer

His teaching, the Dhamma: a seed of the later idea of a Dharma body.

Mission 1

Mahayana: the Buddha extended

  • Many Buddhas: countless Buddhas teach now in countless world-systems, such as Amitabha in his Pure Land in the west.
  • The Buddha's life as a display: the Lotus Sutra says Shakyamuni became awakened countless ages ago; his birth and death were skilful means.
  • Earlier, a school called the Lokottaravada ("transcendentalists") already taught that the Buddha's life was supramundane: he only seemed to eat, sleep and fall ill.
  • So "Buddha" comes to mean more than one man: awakening itself, present everywhere.

Mission 1

The three bodies

  • Trikaya: "three bodies" of a Buddha. Developed in Mahayana, set out fully by the Yogacara school (c. 4th century CE).
  • Dharmakaya: the truth or Dharma body: awakening itself, formless, the same in all Buddhas.
  • Sambhogakaya: the enjoyment or bliss body: a glorious form in the Pure Lands, seen by advanced bodhisattvas.
  • Nirmanakaya: the emanation or transformation body: a form that appears in the world, such as Siddhartha.
  • A common teaching image: the moon in the sky (truth body), its light (enjoyment body), its reflections in many pools (emanation bodies).
Mission 1

Dharmakaya: the truth body

  • In Mahayana teaching, it is not a body in the ordinary sense: it is reality as a Buddha knows it: emptiness, "suchness" (tathata).
  • It does not begin or end; it has no shape, colour or place.
  • All Buddhas share the one Dharmakaya: they are awakened to the same truth.
  • Grows from the early idea that the Buddha's real body is the Dhamma (Vakkali, the earlier screen).
  • Significance for Mahayana Buddhists: the Buddha has not "gone": awakening is always present, and can be realised.
Mission 1

Sambhogakaya: the enjoyment body

  • In Mahayana teaching, a radiant, heavenly form, adorned with the marks of a Buddha.
  • Appears in Pure Lands (Buddha-fields) to bodhisattvas on the higher stages, teaching the Mahayana.
  • Example: Amitabha in his western Pure Land, Sukhavati.
  • Called "enjoyment" because it enjoys and shares the fruits of the Buddha's vast merit.
  • Significance for Mahayana Buddhists: a Buddha can be met in visualisation, devotion and the hope of rebirth in a Pure Land.
Mission 1

Nirmanakaya: the emanation body

  • In Mahayana teaching, a body projected into the world out of compassion, suited to the beings there.
  • Example: Shakyamuni, the Buddha of our age, who seemed to be born, awaken and die.
  • So, for Mahayana Buddhists, the historical Buddha is real, but he is one appearance of something greater.
  • In Tibet, the word for this body, tulku, names recognised teachers believed to be reborn by choice, such as the Dalai Lama.
  • Significance: Mahayana Buddhists hold that compassion never stops sending forms to teach.

Mission 1Diversity

Two concepts of Buddha

  • Who: Theravada — Gautama, a human being who awakened · Mahayana — awakening itself, appearing as many Buddhas.
  • How many now: Theravada — one per age · Mahayana — countless, in many worlds.
  • After death: Theravada — parinirvana; not reachable as a person · Mahayana — still present through the three bodies.
  • Relationship: Theravada — follow his teaching and example · Mahayana — also devotion, visualisation, Pure Land hope.
  • Shared: the Buddha is not a creator god; his awakening and his Dhamma are the heart of practice.
Mission 1Evaluate

Development or departure?

  • Claim: The trikaya doctrine departs from the Buddha's own teaching.
  • For: The Pali texts present a human teacher who is gone at death; heavenly Buddha-bodies are new.
  • For: The doctrine can make the Buddha look like a god, against his teaching of self-effort (Dhammapada 276).
  • Against: It develops seeds in the Pali texts: the Dhamma as the Buddha's body (Vakkali); the Buddha's marvellous powers.
  • Against: It answers a real question: how can a Buddha's compassion simply end at death?
  • Judgement criteria: Faithfulness to early texts; coherence; what the doctrine does for practice.

Mission 2

The "Great Vehicle"

  • Mahayana: "Great Vehicle". Its followers aim at full Buddhahood for the sake of all beings, through the bodhisattva path.
  • Some Mahayana texts called other paths Hinayana, "lesser vehicle". This is a polemical term: do not use it for Theravada today.
  • Today Mahayana is the largest Buddhist tradition, across China, Taiwan, Korea, Japan, Vietnam, Tibet and Mongolia (the Tibetan forms are often called Vajrayana).
  • Mahayana was a movement, not at first a separate school: Mahayana monks lived under the same monastic rules (Vinaya) as others, sometimes in the same monasteries.
  • Mahayana Buddhists accepted the new sutras as the Buddha's word (buddhavacana). Traditional accounts say they were taught by the Buddha but kept until people were ready, or revealed in meditation by Buddhas now teaching in other worlds; some also held that whatever agrees with the Dhamma is the Buddha's word.
Mission 2

Key Mahayana ideas

  • Bodhisattva ideal for all: aim at full Buddhahood out of compassion, not only personal liberation.
  • Emptiness (shunyata): all things lack fixed own-being; developed in the Perfection of Wisdom sutras and by Nagarjuna (–b).
  • Skilful means (upaya): the Buddha adapts his teaching to his hearers.
  • Many Buddhas in many worlds, now: e.g. Amitabha, and great bodhisattvas such as Avalokiteshvara who can be called on for help.
  • Buddha nature: every being can become a Buddha.
  • Merit transfer: merit can be shared for the good of all.
Mission 2

A bigger idea of the Buddha

  • Theravada: Gautama was a human being who found the path and taught it. After his parinibbana he is beyond reach; Buddhists honour him as teacher and example. One Buddha per age.
  • Mahayana: there are countless Buddhas in countless worlds, active now; the Buddha's life is far longer than it appeared (Lotus Sutra ch. 16).
  • The trikaya ("three bodies") later explains this: the Dharma-body (ultimate reality), the enjoyment-body (Buddhas in pure lands) and the emanation-body (Gautama's earthly life) (see Mission 1).
  • Gautama is still central in both traditions: his images and stupas are honoured everywhere, but in Mahayana he is one among many Buddhas.
  • Your spec: key differences between Theravada and Mahayana concepts of Buddha; Trikaya doctrine in Mission 1.

Mission 3

From anicca to emptiness

  • Anicca: impermanence. Every conditioned thing arises, changes and passes away.
  • Early teaching: because things change and depend on conditions, there is no fixed self in a person (anatta).
  • Early analysis (Abhidharma) broke the world into tiny elements, dharmas, each with its own fixed nature.
  • Mahayana takes the next step: the dharmas too are empty. Nothing at all has a fixed, independent nature.
  • Sunyata: emptiness. Not "nothing exists" but "nothing exists by itself".
Mission 3Read the source

Impermanent, not-self

> "'All created things perish,' he who knows and sees this becomes passive in pain; this is the way to purity." (Dhammapada 277, Müller 1881)

> "'All forms are unreal,' he who knows and sees this becomes passive in pain; this is the way that leads to purity." (Dhammapada 279, Müller 1881)

  • Müller's "forms are unreal" renders sabbe dhamma anatta: all things are not-self.

Q1 Which mark of existence does verse 277 teach?

Show answer

Anicca, impermanence.

Q2 How could verse 279 be read as a seed of emptiness?

Show answer

If all things, not just persons, lack self, nothing has a fixed nature of its own.

Mission 3

The Perfection of Wisdom sutras

  • Prajnaparamita: "perfection of wisdom": the sixth paramita, and a family of Mahayana sutras.
  • Earliest: the Perfection of Wisdom in 8,000 Lines, from about the 1st century BCE–1st century CE.
  • Longer versions (25,000; 100,000 lines) and very short ones: the Diamond Sutra and the Heart Sutra.
  • A copy of the Diamond Sutra from Dunhuang, printed in 868 CE, is the world's oldest dated printed book.
  • Their message: the bodhisattva sees all things as empty, and so acts with compassion free of clinging.

Mission 3Read the source

Form and emptiness

Heart Sutra (paraphrase of the core lines):

  • Form is emptiness; emptiness is form.
  • Emptiness is not other than form; form is not other than emptiness.
  • The same is true of feeling, perception, mental formations and consciousness.

Q1 Why does the sutra say both "form is emptiness" and "emptiness is form"?

Show answer

Form lacks fixed nature; and emptiness is not a separate thing behind forms, it is how forms are.

Q2 Which earlier teaching do the five listed items come from?

Show answer

The five skandhas, the aggregates that make up a person.

Mission 3

Empty of what?

  • Empty of svabhava: own-being, a nature that exists by itself, unchanged and independent.
  • A cup is empty of own-being: it depends on clay, a potter, heat, and a use; break it and "cup" is gone.
  • But the cup still holds tea: emptiness does not deny that things appear and work.
  • So form and emptiness are not two things: emptiness is just the way forms exist.
  • The development: anicca (things change) → anatta (persons lack self) → sunyata (nothing has own-being).
  • Even the teachings lack own-being: so the Heart Sutra, chanted daily, can say "no suffering, no path" (paraphrase). They are tools, not things to cling to.

Mission 3Evaluate

Do the Perfection of Wisdom teachings represent reality?

  • Claim: The Mahayana teaching of emptiness represents reality as it truly is.
  • For: It follows from anicca and dependent arising: if all things depend on conditions, none can have own-being.
  • For: It frees from clinging, which early Buddhism names as the cause of suffering.
  • Against: Theravadins do not accept these sutras as the Buddha's word; the Pali canon does not deny the dharmas so radically.
  • Against: "No path, no attainment" may sound like nihilism and confuse practitioners.
  • Judgement criteria: Continuity with early teaching? Coherence? Does it help practice?

Mission 4

Blowing out

  • Nibbana (Pali; Sanskrit nirvana) means "blowing out" or "extinguishing", as a flame goes out.
  • What goes out are the three fires of greed, hatred and delusion. Samyutta Nikaya 38.1 (paraphrase): the destruction of greed, hatred and delusion: this is called nibbana.
  • It is the third noble truth, nirodha: craving "cut off" through detachment, letting go of what changes.
  • It is not the destruction of a self: by anatta, there was no lasting self to destroy.
  • Nirvana is a technical term on your spec.
Mission 4

In this life and after death

  • Nibbana with remainder (sa-upadisesa): reached in this life. The fires are out, but the "remainder", the five khandhas, continues: the awakened one still has a body and feelings.
  • Nibbana without remainder (anupadisesa): at the death of an awakened one. The khandhas end and there is no rebirth.
  • Source: Itivuttaka 44 (paraphrase) names these two "elements" of nibbana.
  • The Buddha: awakening at Bodh Gaya (about age 35) = nibbana with remainder; death at Kusinara (about age 80) = parinibbana, final nibbana (Digha Nikaya 16).
  • Your spec: "Nirvana in this life and after death".

Mission 4

The unconditioned

  • Everything in samsara is conditioned: it arises from causes and so changes.
  • Nibbana is unconditioned (asankhata): not produced by causes, not subject to arising and passing.
  • Udana 8.3 (paraphrase): there is an unborn, unbecome, unmade, unconditioned; if there were not, there would be no escape from the born, become, made and conditioned.
  • So the path does not manufacture nibbana; it removes what obscures it, and nibbana is realised.
Mission 4

Where does the fire go?

  • In Majjhima Nikaya 72 (paraphrase), the wanderer Vacchagotta asks where an awakened one goes after death: is he reborn? not reborn? both? neither? To each, the Buddha says the question does not apply.
  • He asks: when a fire goes out, which direction did it go: east, west, north or south? Vacchagotta: the question does not fit; the fire went out because its fuel ran out.
  • So too the awakened one: no longer reckoned by the khandhas, "deep, immeasurable, hard to fathom, like the ocean" (paraphrase).
  • Scripture speaks of nibbana in three ways:
  • negatively: the end of craving, the unborn, cessation;
  • positively: peace, the highest happiness (Dhammapada 204, Müller), the cool;
  • by images: the further shore, an island, a refuge.
Mission 4Read the source

Milinda's 79th and 80th dilemmas

The Questions of King Milinda, Book IV (paraphrase):

  • 79th: Is nibbana pure bliss, or mixed with pain? Milinda: those seeking it torment body and mind, restrain the senses and give up pleasures, so it seems painful. Nagasena: the pain belongs to the seeking, not to nibbana. Kings endure hardship to win a kingdom; students struggle to learn a craft. The kingdom itself is not the hardship.
  • 80th: Can the form, shape, age or size of nibbana be shown by simile, reason or cause? Nagasena: no: it has no equal, as the water of the great ocean cannot be measured.
  • But its qualities can be shown by similes: like the lotus, untouched by defilements; like water, cooling the fever of defilement and quenching the thirst of craving; like medicine, ending suffering; like space, unborn and without limit; like a mountain peak, lofty and unshakeable; and more.

Q1 Why does Nagasena move from "form" to "qualities"?

Show answer

Nibbana is unconditioned, so it has no form to describe; but what it is like for the one who realises it can be pointed to.

Q2 One strength and one weakness of the similes?

Show answer

Strength: they show nibbana is positive peace, not nothing. Weakness: each is partial, and they risk making it sound like a place or a feeling.

  • Your spec names the 80th dilemma; the 79th is useful context.

Mission 4

Theravada and Mahayana emphases

  • Theravada: nibbana is the unconditioned, realised by the arhat; after parinibbana the awakened one is neither "somewhere" nor simply annihilated: the question does not apply.
  • Mahayana: a Buddha does not rest in a nirvana apart from the world but keeps helping beings: non-abiding nirvana (apratisthita-nirvana).
  • Nagarjuna (c. 2nd century CE): there is not the slightest difference between samsara and nirvana when both are seen as empty (Mulamadhyamakakarika 25, paraphrase;).
  • The Lotus Sutra treats the arhat's nirvana as a resting place on the way to Buddhahood (the "magic city").
Mission 4Evaluate

Can the goal of Buddhism be understood?

  • Claim: Nibbana cannot be understood by anyone who has not realised it.
  • For: Language is built for conditioned things; nibbana is unconditioned (Udana 8.3).
  • For: The Buddha says questions about the awakened one after death "do not apply" (MN 72).
  • Against: Its qualities can be shown (Milinda's similes), and the third truth defines it clearly: the end of craving.
  • Against: Its effects are visible: arhats and teachers living without greed or hatred show what it means in this life.
  • Against: Partial understanding is enough to begin, as a traveller trusts a destination they have not seen.
  • Judgement criteria: What counts as "understanding": a definition, a description, or direct experience? Is a goal that cannot be described still rational to pursue?
  • Your spec: "attempts in scripture to describe it and their strengths and weaknesses".

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Theravada: Gautama was a human being who found the path, taught it, and entered parinirvana; one Buddha per age.
  • Mahayana: countless Buddhas; the trikaya: Dharmakaya (truth body), Sambhogakaya (enjoyment or heavenly body), Nirmanakaya (emanation or earthly body).

Mission 2

  • Mahayana ("Great Vehicle") emerged gradually in India, c. 2nd century BCE to 1st century CE, through new sutras, devotion and the bodhisattva ideal; scholars debate why.
  • Mahayana widens the concept of Buddha: many Buddhas active now; Gautama one among them (trikaya in Mission 1).

Mission 3

  • Anicca (impermanence) and anatta (not-self) develop in Mahayana into sunyata: nothing has own-being (svabhava).
  • The Perfection of Wisdom sutras teach emptiness; the Heart Sutra: "form is emptiness, emptiness is form" (paraphrase).

Mission 4

  • In Buddhist teaching, nibbana ("blowing out") is the end of greed, hatred and delusion: with remainder in this life, without remainder (parinibbana) at death.
  • It is unconditioned; questions about where an awakened one "goes" do not apply. Mahayana stresses non-abiding nirvana.
  • Milinda's 79th dilemma: the pain is in the seeking, not in nibbana; 80th: its form cannot be shown, its qualities can.
