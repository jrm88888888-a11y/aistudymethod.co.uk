Mission 1

Mission 1: Every time your cells build a big molecule, they make water. Why?

Make a guess: Every time your cells build a big molecule, they make water. Why?

Mission 1

Monomers and polymers

  • Monomer: a small unit from which larger molecules are made.
  • Polymer: a large molecule made of many monomers joined together.
MonomerPolymer
Monosaccharide (e.g. glucose)Polysaccharide (starch, glycogen, cellulose)
Amino acidPolypeptide (protein)
NucleotidePolynucleotide (DNA, RNA)
  • Triglycerides are built the same way, but are not polymers: one glycerol + three fatty acids, not a repeating chain.
Mission 1

Condensation: building

  • Condensation: joins two molecules with a new covalent bond and eliminates a molecule of water.
  • One monomer loses an –OH, the other an –H. Together: H2O.
  • One water per bond formed.
  • Repeat: monomer → dimer → polymer.
  • Cells use enzymes for each joining reaction.
Mission 1

Hydrolysis: breaking

  • Hydrolysis: breaks a covalent bond between two molecules and uses a water molecule.
  • The water is split: –H goes to one product, –OH to the other.
  • One water per bond broken.
  • Digestion is hydrolysis. Enzymes make it fast; polymers in plain water break down extremely slowly.
  • Say: "condensation releases water; hydrolysis uses water" — not: the other way round.
Quick check
?Two monosaccharides are joined by a condensation reaction to form a disaccharide. What happens in this reaction?
Mission 1

Worked example: counting the water

  • Bonds sit between monomers. An unbranched chain of n monomers has n − 1 bonds.
  • So building it releases n − 1 water molecules; hydrolysing it uses n − 1.
  • Example: 5 glucose molecules joined into one unbranched chain.
  • Glucose Mr = 180; water Mr = 18.
  • Bonds: 5 − 1 = 4. Water released: 4.
  • Mr of chain = 5 × 180 − 4 × 18 = 900 − 72 = 828.
  • Hydrolysis reverses it exactly: 828 + 4 × 18 = 900.
Quick check
?A polypeptide made of 100 amino acids is completely hydrolysed into free amino acids. How many water molecules are used in the process?

Mission 2

What makes a lipid

  • Lipids dissolve in non-polar solvents but only sparingly in water.
  • Mostly non-polar C–H bonds: few δ+ or δ− regions to attract water. They are hydrophobic.
  • Lipids include fats, oils, waxes, phospholipids and steroids.
  • Say: "insoluble because non-polar" — not: "because the molecules are large" or "because they are lighter than water".
Mission 2

Building a triglyceride

  • (1) Glycerol has three –OH groups. A fatty acid is a hydrocarbon chain (R) with a –COOH group: RCOOH.
  • (2) Condensation: the –COOH of a fatty acid reacts with an –OH of glycerol. An ester bond forms; water is released.
  • (3) Three times: one glycerol + three fatty acids → one triglyceride + 3 H2O.
  • Hydrolysis breaks the ester bonds, releasing glycerol and three fatty acids (e.g. lipase in digestion).
  • A triglyceride is not a polymer.
  • Say: "one glycerol and three fatty acids" — not: "three glycerols and one fatty acid".
Quick check
?One glycerol molecule and three fatty acid molecules react by condensation to form one triglyceride. How much water is produced?
Mission 2

Saturated and unsaturated

  • Saturated: only single C–C bonds. Every carbon holds as many H atoms as it can: saturated with hydrogen.
  • Unsaturated: at least one C=C double bond.
  • In natural fats, each C=C puts a kink in the chain. Saturated chains are straight.
  • Only C=C in the chain counts. The C=O in –COOH does not make a fatty acid unsaturated.
  • Say: "saturated with hydrogen" — not: "full of double bonds".
Mission 2

Kinks and melting points

  • Straight saturated chains pack closely: strong attractions between molecules → higher melting point.
  • Kinked unsaturated chains cannot pack closely: weaker attractions → lower melting point.
  • Rich in saturated fatty acids: fat, solid at room temperature (butter).
  • Rich in unsaturated fatty acids: oil, liquid at room temperature (olive oil).
  • Melting breaks no covalent bonds. Molecules just move apart.
Quick checkRead the data
?The graph shows the approximate melting points of four fatty acids. Each has a chain of 18 carbon atoms; they differ only in the number of double bonds between carbon atoms in the chain. Which statement is best supported by the data?
Mission 2

Saturated fat and health: the evidence

  • Controlled feeding trials: replacing saturated fat with unsaturated fat lowers blood LDL cholesterol.
  • Raised LDL cholesterol is a risk factor for atherosclerosis and cardiovascular disease (CVD).
  • Randomised trials (Cochrane review, 2020; 15 trials, about 57 000 people, at least 2 years each):
  • Cutting saturated fat: about 17% fewer combined cardiovascular events.
  • Little or no difference in deaths from all causes or from CVD.
  • Replacing saturated fat with polyunsaturated fat or with carbohydrate both appeared useful.
  • Observational studies alone can show only a correlation; randomised trials test cause.
Quick check
?In the trials on the last screen, people who cut saturated fat had about 17% fewer cardiovascular events, but little or no difference in deaths from all causes. Which conclusion is best supported?
Mission check

?To make a spread, manufacturers react a liquid vegetable oil with hydrogen, using a catalyst. Some C=C double bonds in its fatty acid chains become C–C single bonds. How does the oil change?

Same molecule type, different tails: straight tails pack into a solid; kinked tails slide past each other as a liquid.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Oily molecules avoid water, yet they wrap every cell in a thin film. How does that film build itself?

Make a guess: Oily molecules avoid water, yet they wrap every cell in a thin film. How does that film build itself?

Mission 3

Lipids are more than fat

  • Lipids dissolve in non-polar solvents but only sparingly in water: they are hydrophobic.
  • Also cholesterol, a steroid.
  • Not every lipid is an energy store.
Mission 3

Amphipathic: two personalities

  • Head: phosphate group, charged. It is hydrophilic: it interacts with water.
  • Tails: hydrocarbon chains, non-polar. They are hydrophobic.
  • This is called amphipathic.
  • Say: "the tails are excluded from water" — not: "the tails repel water".
Mission 3

The bilayer builds itself

  • (1) On water, phospholipids form a single layer: heads in the water, tails in the air.
  • (2) Under water, tails cannot stay in the air. They hide by pairing up tail to tail.
  • (3) Result: a phospholipid bilayer. Heads face the water on both sides; tails form a non-polar core.
  • No enzymes, no energy from the cell: water molecules hydrogen-bond to each other and exclude the tails.
  • A sheet's edges still expose tails, so it closes into a sphere.
  • The molecules are not covalently bonded to each other, so the bilayer is fluid.
Mission 3

Steroids and cholesterol

  • Cholesterol is a steroid lipid.
  • Cholesterol is insoluble in water, so blood must carry it in a package.
Quick check
?Cholesterol and other steroids do not contain fatty acids, yet they are classified as lipids along with fats and phospholipids. Which property shared by these molecules is the basis for grouping them together?
Mission 3

Cholesterol in the blood: HDLs and LDLs

  • Cholesterol and triglycerides travel in blood inside lipoproteins.
  • A lipoprotein: an oily core (cholesterol, triglyceride) inside a coat of one layer of phospholipids, with proteins.
  • LDLs (low-density lipoproteins): carry cholesterol from the liver to body cells. High LDL levels are linked to atheroma in artery walls.
  • HDLs (high-density lipoproteins): carry cholesterol from the tissues back to the liver. High HDL levels are linked to lower CVD risk.
  • More protein, less lipid makes a lipoprotein denser: HDLs are the denser.
  • Say: "LDL cholesterol" or "HDL cholesterol" — not: "two kinds of cholesterol". The cholesterol is the same; the carriers differ.
Quick checkRead the data

Drugs that raise HDL have been tested in large trials and did not cut heart attacks. Correlation is a clue, not a cause.

Mission check

?Lipoproteins carry cholesterol and triglycerides in blood plasma, which is mostly water. Each lipoprotein has an oily core of cholesterol and triglyceride, surrounded by a single layer of phospholipids (with some proteins). How are the phospholipids in this layer most likely arranged?

One rule builds membranes, vesicles and lipoproteins: heads to the water, tails away from it.

Mission 4

The heart starts its own beat

  • Cardiac muscle is myogenic: it contracts without any nerve impulse.
  • The sinoatrial node (SAN), in the wall of the right atrium, is the pacemaker.
  • It sends out a wave of electrical excitation at regular intervals. Each wave starts one heartbeat.
  • Nerves and hormones only change the rate of the SAN. They do not start the beats.
  • Say: "the SAN initiates each beat" — not: "the brain sends each heartbeat".
Mission 4

Spreading the wave

  • (1) The wave from the SAN spreads across both atria → atrial systole.
  • (2) A layer of non-conducting tissue between atria and ventricles blocks it. The only way through is the atrioventricular node (AVN).
  • (3) The AVN delays the wave (about 0.1 s): the atria finish emptying first.
  • (4) The wave passes down the bundle of His in the septum to the apex, then up through the ventricle walls.
  • Ventricles contract from the apex upwards, squeezing blood up into the arteries.
  • Names: SAN, AVN, bundle of His, Purkyne fibres (also spelled Purkinje).
Quick check
?Suppose the AVN passed the wave of excitation on with no delay. What would be the main effect?
Mission 4

The ECG

  • An electrocardiogram (ECG): electrodes on the skin record the heart's electrical activity.
  • P wave: excitation spreading over the atria → atrial systole.
  • QRS complex: excitation spreading through the ventricles → ventricular systole.
  • T wave: ventricles recovering (repolarising) → diastole.
  • P to the start of QRS includes the AVN delay.
  • Heart rate (beats min⁻¹) = 60 ÷ time between two R peaks (s).
Mission 4

Worked example: heart rate from an ECG

  • ECG paper runs at 25 mm s⁻¹. One large square = 5 mm = 0.20 s.
  • R peak to R peak = 4 large squares = 4 × 0.20 = 0.80 s.
  • Heart rate = 60 ÷ 0.80 = 75 beats min⁻¹.
Mission 4

Abnormal traces

  • Tachycardia: resting rate above 100 beats min⁻¹. R peaks close together.
  • Bradycardia: resting rate below 60 beats min⁻¹. R peaks far apart. Normal in some trained athletes.
  • Fibrillation: uncoordinated, irregular excitation. Atrial: no clear P waves, irregular QRS. Ventricular: no QRS at all; the heart cannot pump.
  • Ectopic heartbeat: an extra beat that comes early, followed by a longer gap.
  • After a heart attack (myocardial infarction), damaged muscle conducts abnormally. The trace changes shape, e.g. a raised ST segment (between S and T).
  • So ECGs help diagnose CVD and other heart conditions.
Quick check
?On an ECG recorded at 25 mm s⁻¹, the distance between two R peaks is 30 mm. What is the heart rate in beats per minute?
Mission 4

Nerves and hormones adjust the rate

  • A centre in the medulla oblongata controls heart rate.
  • Edexcel calls it the cardiovascular control centre.
  • Sympathetic nerve to the SAN → SAN fires faster → heart rate up; contractions stronger, so stroke volume up.
  • Parasympathetic nerve (the vagus) to the SAN → SAN fires more slowly → heart rate down.
  • Say: "the medulla changes the rate of the SAN" — not: "the medulla makes the heart beat".
Mission 4

What the medulla senses

  • Baroreceptors (pressure receptors): stretch receptors in the walls of the aorta and carotid arteries. They monitor blood pressure.
  • Chemoreceptors: in the aorta and carotid arteries, and in the medulla. They monitor blood pH, CO2 and O2.
  • Blood pressure high → more baroreceptor impulses → medulla → more vagus impulses → rate falls.
  • Blood pressure low → fewer baroreceptor impulses → medulla → more sympathetic impulses → rate rises.
  • CO2 up, pH down (e.g. exercise) → chemoreceptors → medulla → sympathetic → rate rises → CO2 removed faster.
  • Each response reverses the change: negative feedback.
Quick check
?A person stands up quickly and their blood pressure falls for a few seconds. Which sequence describes the feedback response that restores blood pressure?
Mission 4

Exercise: heart and lungs together

  • Working muscles respire faster: more CO2, blood pH falls.
  • Chemoreceptors → cardiovascular control centre → sympathetic impulses → heart rate and stroke volume up → cardiac output up.
  • Chemoreceptors → ventilation centre → faster, deeper breathing.
  • Adrenaline, released even before exercise starts, also raises heart rate.
  • Result: O2 delivered to muscles and CO2 removed faster.
Mission 4Practical

Caffeine and Daphnia heart rate

  • Core practical 1: investigate the effect of caffeine on heart rate in Daphnia.
  • Place one Daphnia in a drop of pond water on a cavity slide; low power; let it settle for a few minutes.
  • Count heartbeats for 15 s with a tally counter; repeat 3 times; × 4 for beats min⁻¹.
  • Replace the water with a caffeine solution; settle; count again. Repeat for a range of concentrations.
  • Control: temperature (a lamp heats the slide), settling time, size of Daphnia.
  • Ethics, arguments for: invertebrates, a simple nervous system, brief use, returned to pond water. Arguments against: whether they feel pain is uncertain; caffeine may harm them; a human pulse study could replace them.
Mission check

?In a heart transplant, the nerves linking the donor heart to the medulla are cut, and they do not regrow for many months. Which statement about the transplanted heart is correct?

The heart makes its own beat; the nerves are the volume control, turning the rate up or down to match what the body needs.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Many foods hide sugar, starch, protein or fat. How can a few drops of reagent tell you which, and how much?

Make a guess: Many foods hide sugar, starch, protein or fat. How can a few drops of reagent tell you which, and how much?

Mission 5

Starch: the iodine test

  • Add a few drops of iodine in potassium iodide solution.
  • Starch present: orange-brown → blue-black.
  • Works on solids or solutions.
  • Iodine slots inside the coiled amylose chain: that is what gives the colour.
Mission 5

Reducing sugars: Benedict's test

  • Reducing sugars: all monosaccharides, and the disaccharides maltose and lactose.
  • (1) Add an equal volume of Benedict's reagent (blue: Cu²⁺ ions).
  • (2) Heat in a boiling water bath for about 5 minutes.
  • (3) Reducing sugar reduces Cu²⁺ to Cu⁺: a brick-red precipitate of copper(I) oxide forms.
  • The more reducing sugar, the further along: blue → green → yellow → orange → brick red.
  • So the colour is semi-quantitative. For a number, use a colorimeter.
Mission 5

Non-reducing sugars

  • Sucrose is a non-reducing sugar: Benedict's stays blue.
  • (1) Take a fresh sample. Boil with dilute hydrochloric acid: this hydrolyses the glycosidic bond.
  • (2) Neutralise with sodium hydrogencarbonate (Benedict's works only in alkaline conditions). Check with pH paper.
  • (3) Benedict's test again. Brick red now: a non-reducing sugar was present.
  • This only works if the first Benedict's test was negative, or gave less precipitate.
Quick check
?A solution gives a negative result with iodine. With Benedict's reagent, heated, it stays blue. A fresh sample is boiled with dilute hydrochloric acid, neutralised, and tested with Benedict's again: a brick-red precipitate forms. What does the solution contain?
Mission 5

Proteins and lipids

  • Biuret test: add biuret reagent (sodium hydroxide, then dilute copper(II) sulphate).
  • Protein present: blue → lilac (purple). The reagent detects peptide bonds.
  • Free amino acids give no lilac: they have no peptide bonds.
  • Emulsion test: shake the sample with ethanol; any lipid dissolves.
  • Pour the ethanol into water. Lipid present: a milky-white emulsion.
  • The lipid comes out of solution as tiny droplets that scatter light.
Quick check
?In the emulsion test, a sample is shaken with ethanol and the ethanol is then poured into water. A milky-white emulsion forms. Why?
Mission 5

Core practical 2: vitamin C

  • DCPIP is a blue dye. Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) reduces it: blue → colourless.
  • Put a fixed volume of DCPIP (e.g. 1.0 cm³) in a tube.
  • Add the juice from a burette, swirling, until the blue just disappears. Record the volume.
  • Calibrate: repeat with a vitamin C solution of known concentration.
  • Keep the DCPIP volume and concentration the same; repeat for a mean.
  • Illustrative data: standard (1.0 mg cm⁻³) needs 0.80 cm³, so the DCPIP took 0.80 mg of vitamin C.
  • Juice needs 2.0 cm³: 0.80 mg ÷ 2.0 cm³ = 0.40 mg cm⁻³ (40 mg per 100 cm³).
  • More juice needed means less vitamin C in it.
Quick check
?1.0 cm³ of DCPIP is decolourised by 0.50 cm³ of a 1.0 mg cm⁻³ vitamin C solution. The same volume of DCPIP is decolourised by 2.5 cm³ of a fruit drink. What is the vitamin C concentration of the drink?
Mission check

?A sample of milk is tested. Heated with Benedict's reagent: an orange-red precipitate. With biuret reagent: lilac. With iodine in potassium iodide: stays orange-brown. Which conclusion is supported?

Each reagent answers one question. Choose the tests, read every result, and claim no more than they show.

Mission 6

Mission 6: One spot of a mixture goes in; a ladder of spots comes out. How do you work out what each spot is?

Make a guess: One spot of a mixture goes in; a ladder of spots comes out. How do you work out what each spot is?

Mission 6

How chromatography separates

  • (1) Stationary phase: the paper, or a thin layer of silica gel on a plate (TLC). It stays put.
  • (2) Mobile phase: the solvent. It creeps up the stationary phase by capillary action, carrying the mixture.
  • (3) Each molecule spends time dissolved in the solvent and time attached to the stationary phase.
  • More soluble in the solvent, less attracted to the stationary phase: it travels further.
  • So molecules in a mixture separate into spots.
  • TLC runs faster and gives sharper spots than paper.
  • Say: "distance depends on solubility and attraction" — not: "on how much of it there is".
Mission 6

Running a chromatogram

  • Draw an origin line in pencil (ink would itself separate), about 1.5 cm from the bottom.
  • Put a small, concentrated spot of the sample on the origin. Dry it; add more; dry again.
  • Stand the paper in solvent with the origin above the solvent level, so the sample does not wash off into it.
  • Cover the container, so the air inside stays saturated with solvent vapour.
  • Take it out before the solvent reaches the top. Mark the solvent front in pencil at once.
  • Coloured spots show themselves. Colourless ones need a locating agent.
Mission 6

Worked example: Rf values

  • Rf = distance moved by the solute ÷ distance moved by the solvent front.
  • Measure both from the origin; measure to the centre of the spot.
  • Illustrative data: origin 20 mm from the paper's bottom edge; spot centre 56 mm; solvent front 80 mm.
  • Solute: 56 − 20 = 36 mm. Solvent: 80 − 20 = 60 mm.
  • Rf = 36 ÷ 60 = 0.60. Always between 0 and 1; no units.
  • Same solute, solvent and stationary phase: same Rf. Compare with reference values, or run standards beside the sample.
Quick check
?On a paper chromatogram of leaf pigments, the origin line was 20 mm from the bottom edge of the paper, the centre of the carotene spot was 65 mm from the bottom edge and the solvent front was 70 mm from the bottom edge. What is the Rf value of carotene?
Mission 6

Amino acids and other colourless molecules

  • Amino acids are colourless. After the run, dry the chromatogram and spray with ninhydrin; warm it.
  • Amino acids show up as purple spots. (Wear gloves: ninhydrin also stains skin.)
  • To identify a protein's amino acids, hydrolyse the protein first.
  • Run standards (known amino acids) beside the unknown. Matching Rf: same amino acid.
  • Appendix 5c: separate biological compounds by thin-layer or paper chromatography.
Quick checkRead the data
?Illustrative data. In the solvent used, the reference Rf values of five amino acids are P 0.26, Q 0.38, R 0.60, S 0.67 and T 0.80. A hydrolysed peptide is run. The origin is 15 mm from the bottom edge of the paper and the solvent front is 90 mm from it. After ninhydrin, two purple spots are centred 34.5 mm and 60.0 mm from the bottom edge. Mission check

?Sugars in a fruit juice are separated by thin-layer chromatography, with glucose, fructose and sucrose standards run on the same plate. The juice gives two spots, level with the glucose and fructose standards; nothing is level with sucrose. On a second plate, with a different solvent, every spot (juice and standards) has a different Rf from the first plate. Which conclusion is correct?

A spot is named by where it lands compared with a known, under the same conditions. Not by its size, and not by guesswork.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Monomers join by condensation (one water released per bond) and polymers are split by hydrolysis (one water used per bond).

Mission 2

  • A triglyceride is one glycerol + three fatty acids joined by ester bonds (three condensations); hydrophobic, energy-dense store.
  • Saturated chains are straight and pack closely (fats, higher melting point); C=C kinks unsaturated chains (oils, lower melting point).

Mission 3

  • Phospholipids are amphipathic: a hydrophilic phosphate head and two hydrophobic tails; in water they form bilayers by themselves.
  • Cholesterol travels in lipoproteins; high LDL and low HDL are linked to CVD, but correlation is not cause.

Mission 4

  • Cardiac muscle is myogenic: the SAN starts each beat; the AVN delays the wave; the bundle of His and Purkyne tissue carry it to the apex.
  • Baroreceptors and chemoreceptors → medulla → sympathetic (faster) or vagus (slower) nerves to the SAN.
  • ECG: P (atria), QRS (ventricles), T (recovery); rate = 60 ÷ R–R time.

Mission 5

  • Iodine: blue-black = starch. Benedict's, heated: brick-red precipitate = reducing sugar. Biuret: lilac = protein.
  • Non-reducing sugar: acid hydrolysis, neutralise, Benedict's again. Emulsion test: milky = lipid.
  • DCPIP: the less juice needed to decolourise it, the more vitamin C.

Mission 6

  • Molecules separate by how they share time between the solvent and the stationary phase.
  • Rf = distance moved by the solute ÷ distance moved by the solvent front, both from the origin; compare with standards or reference values for the same solvent.
  • Colourless amino acids are revealed with ninhydrin and identified against standards.
