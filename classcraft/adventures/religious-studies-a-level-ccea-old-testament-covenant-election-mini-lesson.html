Mission 1

A covenant: a binding bond

  • A covenant (Hebrew berit) is a solemn, binding agreement between two parties, sealed by an oath or a sign.
  • In the Torah, God makes covenants with people: Noah, Abraham, then all Israel at Sinai.
  • Abraham (first called Abram) leaves Haran for Canaan at God's call (Genesis 12).
  • Jews call him Avraham Avinu, "Abraham our father": the first patriarch, father of the Jewish people.
  • Your course reads these chapters as the Christian Old Testament: covenant as God's election and call.

Mission 1

Land, offspring, a chosen people

  • Land: "Unto thy seed will I give this land" (Genesis 12:7, JPS 1917). The land is Canaan, later the Land of Israel.
  • Offspring: a great nation, from a childless old couple (Abram and Sarai). The covenant passes to Isaac; Ishmael is blessed too, but the covenant is with Isaac (Genesis 17:19–21).
  • Chosen people: God chooses Abraham's family for a relationship and a task: to be a blessing to "all the families of the earth".
  • Christians read the promise as reaching all nations through faith: "those who are of faith are children of Abraham" (Galatians 3:7, WEBBE).

Mission 1

Brit milah: the sign in the flesh

  • Genesis 17: God renames Abram Abraham ("father of a multitude") and promises an "everlasting covenant" (17:7).
  • The sign: "every male among you shall be circumcised … it shall be a token of a covenant betwixt Me and you" (Genesis 17:10–11, JPS 1917).
  • Brit milah ("covenant of circumcision"): on the eighth day after birth (17:12), even on Shabbat or Yom Kippur, unless the baby's health requires delay.
  • Done by a mohel; the baby is named; the father blesses God, who commanded "to bring him into the covenant of Abraham our father".
  • In the New Testament, Paul argues that Gentile Christians need not be circumcised (Galatians 5:2–6).

Mission 2

From slavery to Sinai

  • The book of Exodus tells that, after the Exodus from Egypt, Israel camps at Mount Sinai "in the third month" (Exodus 19:1).
  • Moses goes up the mountain; God sends a message to the whole people.
  • God first recalls what he has done: "how I bore you on eagles' wings" (19:4). Rescue comes before law.
  • The people prepare for three days: washing clothes, keeping a boundary round the mountain (19:10–15).
  • Thunder, fire, smoke and a shofar: God speaks the Ten Commandments to the whole people (Exodus 20).
  • Read as the Christian Old Testament: God's election and call of a "chosen people", as with Noah and Abraham.

Mission 2

The Ten Commandments

  • Hebrew: Aseret ha-Dibrot, "the ten words" or "ten statements" (compare 34:28, "the ten words").
  • Two stone tablets, traditionally five statements on each: the first five (God, no idols, God's name, Shabbat, honouring parents) are linked with duties to God, the second five (murder, adultery, theft, false witness, coveting) with duties to people.
  • Jewish count: the first statement is "I am the LORD thy God, who brought thee out of the land of Egypt" (Exodus 20:2, JPS 1917). Belief in God comes first.
  • Most Christians count differently: none counts "I am the LORD thy God" as a separate statement. Catholics and Lutherans take it with "no other gods" and "no idols" as the first, and split coveting in two; most Protestants and the Orthodox make "no other gods" the first and "no idols" the second.
  • They are 10 of the 613 mitzvot. Medieval rabbis (e.g. Saadia Gaon) saw all 613 as grouped under the ten.
  • The rabbis removed the daily reading of the Ten Commandments in the synagogue so that no one would claim only these ten came from Sinai (Talmud, Berakhot 12a).

Mission 2

Written, broken, renewed

  • Recorded: "Moses wrote all the words of the LORD" (Exodus 24:4, JPS 1917), built an altar and 12 pillars for the 12 tribes.
  • Ratified: he read the "book of the covenant"; the people said "will we do, and obey" (24:7: na'aseh ve-nishma).
  • Sign of blood: "Behold the blood of the covenant, which the LORD hath made with you" (Exodus 24:8, JPS 1917): half the blood on the altar, half on the people. Both parties bound.
  • Failure: the golden calf (Exodus 32). Moses breaks the tablets (32:19).
  • Renewed: "Hew thee two tables of stone like unto the first" (34:1); God tells Moses, "Write thou these words" (34:27); the tablets bear "the words of the covenant, the ten words" (34:28).

Mission 3

Covenant: gift and obligation

  • A covenant (Hebrew berit) is a binding relationship between God and people, sealed by promises and signs.
  • Noahic (Genesis 9) and Abrahamic (Genesis 12; 15; 17): God's promises, largely unconditional.
  • Mosaic (Exodus 19–20): God has already rescued the people (Exodus 20:2); now they must keep his law.
  • The condition: "if you will indeed obey my voice and keep my covenant, then you shall be my own possession" (Exodus 19:5).
  • The people agree: "We will do all that the LORD has said, and be obedient." (Exodus 24:7)
  • Deuteronomy sets out blessings for obedience and curses for disobedience (Deuteronomy 28).
Mission 3

Failure: the pattern

  • At Sinai itself the people make the golden calf (Exodus 32).
  • In Judges, a repeated cycle: unfaithfulness → oppression → crying out → a rescuer (Judges 2:11–19).
  • The prophets' two charges: idolatry (worshipping other gods) and injustice (exploiting the poor, corrupt courts).
  • For the prophets the two are one failure: forsaking God shows itself in how the weak are treated.
  • The Deuteronomistic writers later explain the fall of Samaria (722 BCE) and of Jerusalem (587 BCE) as the covenant curses coming true (2 Kings 17:7–23).

Mission 3

Isaiah's world

  • Isaiah son of Amoz, eighth century BCE, prophet in Jerusalem under Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz and Hezekiah (Isaiah 1:1).
  • Prosperity for some: large estates, luxury, feasting; debt and land-loss for others.
  • The threat from Assyria: the northern kingdom (Israel) falls in 722 BCE; Jerusalem is besieged by Sennacherib in 701 BCE.
  • Pre-exilic: Isaiah speaks before the exile of 587 BCE, warning of judgement still to come.
  • Most scholars read chapters 1–39 as the core of Isaiah's own oracles; chapters 40–66 come from the time of the exile and after (see Mission 4).
Mission 3

Isaiah 5:8–30: six woes

  • Just before: the Song of the Vineyard ends "He looked for justice, but behold, oppression" (Isaiah 5:7).
  • Woe (Hebrew hoy): the cry of mourning at a funeral; the prophet mourns the living as already dead.
  • (1) "Woe to those who join house to house, who lay field to field" (5:8): land-grabbing.
  • (2) Drinking from morning to night, blind to "the work of the LORD" (5:11–12).
  • (3) Mocking God's plan (5:18–19). (4) "Woe to those who call evil good, and good evil" (5:20).
  • (5) Wise in their own eyes (5:21). (6) Heroes at drinking who acquit the guilty for a bribe (5:22–23).
  • Sentence: exile (5:13); God "will whistle" for a far nation, swift and unstoppable (5:26–30): Assyria.
Mission 3

The vision in the Temple

  • "In the year that King Uzziah died" (6:1), c. 740 BCE: Isaiah sees the Lord enthroned, "high and lifted up".
  • Seraphim (fiery beings) call: "Holy, holy, holy, is the LORD of Armies! The whole earth is full of his glory!" (Isaiah 6:3)
  • Holiness: God's otherness and moral purity; the title "the Holy One of Israel" runs through Isaiah.
  • Isaiah's response: "Woe is me! For I am undone, because I am a man of unclean lips" (Isaiah 6:5).
  • A seraph touches his lips with a live coal from the altar: guilt taken away (6:6–7).
  • The pattern: vision of God → sense of sin → cleansing → commission.

> "Make the heart of this people fat. Make their ears heavy, and shut their eyes; lest they see with their eyes, hear with their ears, understand with their heart, and turn again, and be healed." (Isaiah 6:10, WEBBE)

Mission 3

The rib: God takes Israel to court

  • Rib (Hebrew rîb): a dispute or lawsuit. The prophets borrow the scene of a court case.
  • Typical parts: (1) summons to witnesses; (2) indictment (the charge); (3) the accused's defences rejected; (4) verdict and sentence, often with a last appeal.
  • Witnesses: heavens and earth. Ancient treaties called on the gods as witnesses; Israel calls on creation (Deuteronomy 30:19).
  • Other examples: Hosea 4:1; Micah 6:1–8.
  • Debate: G. E. Wright (1909–1974; study of Deuteronomy 32, 1962) and H. B. Huffmon ("The Covenant Lawsuit in the Prophets", 1959) saw a fixed covenant lawsuit form. Later critics doubt a single fixed genre: rîb covers many kinds of dispute, and the court image is a literary device used freely.

Isaiah 1 and the Song of Moses

> "Hear, heavens, and listen, earth; for the LORD has spoken: 'I have nourished and brought up children and they have rebelled against me.'" (Isaiah 1:2, WEBBE)

> "Give ear, you heavens, and I will speak. Let the earth hear the words of my mouth." (Deuteronomy 32:1, WEBBE)

  • Charge: even the ox knows its owner, "but Israel doesn't know" (1:3). Deuteronomy 32:6: "Is this the way you repay the LORD?"
  • Defence rejected: sacrifices, festivals and prayers are worthless while "your hands are full of blood" (1:11–15).
  • Appeal: "Seek justice. Relieve the oppressed. Defend the fatherless. Plead for the widow." (Isaiah 1:17)
  • Choice: "Though your sins are as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow" (1:18); obey and eat the good of the land, refuse and face the sword (1:19–20).
  • Lament and hope: the faithful city has become a prostitute (1:21); "Zion shall be redeemed with justice" (1:27).

Mission 3Evaluate

Was the covenant conditional?

  • Claim: Israel's covenant with God depended on Israel's obedience.
  • For: "if you will indeed obey" (Exodus 19:5); blessings and curses (Deuteronomy 28); the rîb assumes binding obligations broken; the prophets and the Deuteronomistic History read the exile as the penalty.
  • Against: God acts first: rescue before law (Exodus 20:2); the Noahic and Abrahamic promises are unconditional; even in judgement a remnant and a "holy seed" survive (Isaiah 6:13); God offers cleansing, not only sentence (Isaiah 1:18).
  • Scholarship: G. E. Mendenhall (1916–2016, Law and Covenant in Israel and the Ancient Near East, 1955) compared Sinai with Hittite vassal treaties: the overlord's past kindness, then stipulations, witnesses, blessings and curses.
  • Jewish and Christian readings: Jews see the covenant as still in force, with teshuvah (repentance) always possible; many Christians read the prophets' failure as pointing to a "new covenant" (Jeremiah 31:31).
  • Judge it by: Can a covenant be both a gift and a binding agreement? Does judgement end the relationship or restore it?

Mission 4

Coming home to a ruin

  • 587 BCE: Babylon destroys Jerusalem and the Temple; leading families are taken into exile.
  • 539 BCE: Cyrus of Persia takes Babylon; he lets exiles return and rebuild the Temple (Ezra 1:1–4).
  • The Second Temple is completed c. 515 BCE. Judah is now Yehud, a small province of the Persian Empire, without a king.
  • Ezra, priest and scribe, brings "the law of Moses" from Persia (Ezra 7), traditionally dated 458 BCE.
  • Nehemiah, governor, rebuilds Jerusalem's walls from 445 BCE, against opposition from Sanballat of Samaria and Tobiah the Ammonite.
  • The question of the age: with no king and no independence, what makes someone a member of God's people?
Mission 4

Ezra 9–10: the marriages crisis

  • Leaders report that the people "have not separated themselves from the peoples of the lands" (Ezra 9:1).
  • The charge: "the holy offspring have mixed themselves with the peoples of the lands" (Ezra 9:2).
  • Ezra tears his clothes and prays a long confession: "our iniquities have increased over our head" (9:6).
  • Shecaniah proposes a covenant "to put away all the wives and those who are born of them" (Ezra 10:3).
  • Ezra: "Separate yourselves from the peoples of the land and from the foreign women." (Ezra 10:11)
  • An assembly in the winter rain; a commission investigates case by case (10:9–17). The list ends: "Some of them had wives by whom they had children." (10:44)
Mission 4

Nehemiah 10 and 13: pledge and purge

  • Nehemiah 10:28–39: the people sign a pledge to keep the Law of Moses.
  • No marriages with "the peoples of the land" (10:30); no buying on the Sabbath; the seventh-year release of debts (10:31).
  • Tithes, first fruits and offerings for the Temple: "We will not forsake the house of our God." (10:39)
  • Nehemiah 13: Deuteronomy 23:3 is read aloud (no Ammonite or Moabite in the assembly), and "they separated all the mixed multitude from Israel" (13:3).
  • Nehemiah throws Tobiah's goods out of a Temple room (13:4–9), closes the gates on the Sabbath (13:15–22).
  • Finding children who "could not speak in the Jews' language" (13:24), he confronts the men who had married foreign women: he curses them, strikes some of them and makes them swear (13:25).
Mission 4

Why exclude?

  • The Law: Deuteronomy forbids marriage with the nations of Canaan (Deuteronomy 7:3) and excludes Ammonites and Moabites (23:3).
  • History: "Didn't Solomon king of Israel sin by these things?" (Nehemiah 13:26). Foreign wives had led even Solomon to other gods (1 Kings 11).
  • Survival: a tiny community, no king, surrounded by larger peoples. Language, Sabbath and marriage kept a distinct identity.
  • Holiness: Israel as a "holy seed", set apart for God. The exile was read as punishment; repeating old sins risked a new disaster.
  • Many scholars stress that the concern is religious and communal identity, not race in the modern sense: the lists of peoples (Ezra 9:1) echo the old Canaanite nations. Others describe the policy as ethnic.
  • Critics within the Bible and since: the measures broke up families and seem to contradict Ruth the Moabite, ancestor of David.

A house of prayer for all peoples

> "Let no foreigner who has joined himself to the LORD speak, saying, 'The LORD will surely separate me from his people.' Do not let the eunuch say, 'Behold, I am a dry tree.'" (Isaiah 56:3, WEBBE)

> "I will bring these to my holy mountain, and make them joyful in my house of prayer. Their burnt offerings and their sacrifices will be accepted on my altar; for my house will be called a house of prayer for all peoples." (Isaiah 56:7, WEBBE)

  • Isaiah 56–66 is usually dated after the return, in the same era as Ezra and Nehemiah ("Third Isaiah").
  • The condition is covenant faithfulness, not birth: keeping the Sabbath, holding fast to the covenant (56:4, 6).
  • It is widely read as reversing Deuteronomy 23:1–3, which shut eunuchs and some foreigners out of the assembly.
  • Jesus quotes 56:7 when he clears the Temple (Mark 11:17).

Mission 4

Egypt my people: Isaiah 19 and Amos 9

  • Isaiah 19:18–25: five cities in Egypt speak "the language of Canaan"; "an altar to the LORD in the middle of the land of Egypt" (19:19).
  • The climax: "Blessed be Egypt my people, Assyria the work of my hands, and Israel my inheritance." (Isaiah 19:25). Israel's two great oppressors share its titles.
  • Amos 9:7: "Are you not like the children of the Ethiopians to me, children of Israel?" God also led the Philistines from Caphtor and the Syrians from Kir.
  • Amos's point: election is not a monopoly on God's care: the Exodus is one rescue among others.
  • Amos 9:11–15: hope beyond judgement: David's fallen "tent" raised up; "the nations who are called by my name" (9:12); exiles return to plant vineyards (9:14). Many scholars see these verses as a later, post-exilic ending.
  • Acts 15:16–17 uses Amos 9:11–12 to support welcoming Gentiles into the Church.
Mission 4Read the source

Psalm 96: the LORD reigns

> "Sing to the LORD a new song! Sing to the LORD, all the earth." (Psalm 96:1, WEBBE)

> "Say amongst the nations, 'The LORD reigns.' The world is also established. It can't be moved. He will judge the peoples with equity." (Psalm 96:10, WEBBE)

  • An enthronement psalm (with Psalms 93, 95–99): God is king of the whole earth.
  • Universal: Israel is to "declare his glory amongst the nations" (96:3).
  • Yet clear lines: "all the gods of the peoples are idols, but the LORD made the heavens" (96:5).
  • Judgement is good news: creation rejoices because God "comes to judge the earth… with righteousness" (96:13).
  • Sigmund Mowinckel (1884–1965, Psalmenstudien II, 1922) linked these psalms to an autumn festival celebrating God's kingship.
  • The psalm reappears in 1 Chronicles 16:23–33, a post-exilic history, at the ark's arrival in Jerusalem.

Mission 4Diversity

Two voices in one canon

  • Exclusion voice: Ezra 9–10; Nehemiah 10, 13; drawing on Deuteronomy 7 and 23. Purity, separation, the Law.
  • Inclusion voice: Isaiah 56; 19:18–25; Amos 9:7; also the books of Ruth (a Moabite who joins Israel: "your God my God", Ruth 1:16) and Jonah (God's mercy for Nineveh), which many scholars date to this period.
  • Paul D. Hanson (The Dawn of Apocalyptic, 1975) argued for rival parties in the restored community: priestly leaders in control of the Temple versus visionary prophetic groups behind Isaiah 56–66.
  • Jewish tradition holds both: Israel keeps a distinct covenant identity, and a sincere convert is welcomed fully (Ruth is the model); the righteous of all nations have a share in the world to come.
  • Christian readings: many see the inclusion voice fulfilled when the Church admits Gentiles (Acts 15; Ephesians 2:14). Others warn against using Ezra and Nehemiah to caricature Judaism as narrow.
Mission 4Evaluate

When opposing groups both claim God's will

  • The problem (OAa): Ezra and the author of Isaiah 56 both speak for the same God, in the same community, probably within a century of each other, and reach opposite policies.
  • Test 1, scripture: Ezra cites Deuteronomy; Isaiah 56 is often read as revising it. Scripture can be cited on both sides.
  • Test 2, the character of God: which policy better fits a God of justice and righteousness (Isaiah 56:1)? But each side defines God's holiness differently.
  • Test 3, consequences: separation is credited by many with preserving Jewish identity through later empires; inclusion opened the community to converts and later to the Gentile Church.
  • Test 4, community and time: both voices are kept in one canon, so the community itself refused to silence either.
  • Today: denominations, and groups within one faith, still disagree on marriage, membership and worship, each citing scripture.
  • Judge it by: Can a later revelation correct an earlier law? Is one test enough? Can both be God's will for different times?
Mission 4Evaluate

Exclusivity and inclusivity

  • Alan Race (Christians and Religious Pluralism, 1983) gave three labels now widely used:
  • Exclusivism: truth and salvation through one faith only (e.g. Hendrik Kraemer, 1888–1965, The Christian Message in a Non-Christian World, 1938).
  • Inclusivism: one faith is fully true, but others share in its truth or salvation (e.g. Karl Rahner, 1904–1984: "anonymous Christians").
  • Pluralism: many faiths are valid responses to one ultimate Reality (John Hick, 1922–2012, God and the Universe of Faiths, 1973).
  • For exclusivity: a faith must have boundaries to have an identity; truth claims cannot all be true; the Ezra community survived.
  • For inclusivity: God's care is universal (Amos 9:7; Psalm 96); excluding families caused real harm; Isaiah 56 welcomes the outsider.
  • Caution: Ezra's question is who belongs to the community, not who can be saved; the modern labels fit it only loosely.
  • Judge it by: Is identity possible without exclusion? Does inclusivism respect other faiths, or absorb them?

Mission 5

The story and its storytellers

  • David: traditionally king c. 1010–970 BCE, first of Judah, then of all Israel, ruling from Jerusalem.
  • His story fills 1 Samuel 16 to 1 Kings 2: part of the Deuteronomistic History (Deuteronomy to 2 Kings).
  • Martin Noth (1902–1968, Überlieferungsgeschichtliche Studien, 1943) argued one school of writers shaped this history, probably during the exile, to explain Israel's fall in the light of Deuteronomy.
  • They used older sources: stories of David's rise and of the struggle for his throne.
  • The portrait is not flattering throughout: courage and piety alongside adultery, murder and family chaos.
  • Compare 1 and 2 Chronicles (later): they retell David's reign but leave out the Bathsheba and Uriah affair.
Mission 5

Giant-killer and rival (1 Samuel 17:1–18:16)

  • Goliath of Gath challenges Israel; Saul's army is afraid (17:4–11).
  • David, a shepherd boy, refuses Saul's armour: "I come to you in the name of the LORD of Armies" (1 Samuel 17:45). "The battle is the LORD's" (17:47).
  • Theme: God saves through the unlikely; victory is God's, not weapons'.
  • Jonathan, Saul's son, makes a covenant of friendship with David (18:1–4).
  • The women sing: "Saul has slain his thousands, and David his ten thousands." (1 Samuel 18:7)
  • Saul's jealousy grows: "What can he have more but the kingdom?" (18:8). He hurls a spear at David (18:10–11).
  • "All Israel and Judah loved David" (18:16): the rise is God's doing and the people's choice.

In the cave at En-gedi (1 Samuel 24)

> "He said to his men, 'The LORD forbid that I should do this thing to my lord, the LORD's anointed, to stretch out my hand against him, since he is the LORD's anointed.'" (1 Samuel 24:6, WEBBE)

> "He said to David, 'You are more righteous than I; for you have done good to me, whereas I have done evil to you.'" (1 Samuel 24:17, WEBBE)

  • Saul, hunting David, enters the cave where David hides. David only cuts off the corner of Saul's robe, and then regrets even that (24:4–5).
  • Saul admits: "I know that you will surely be king" (24:20).

Q1 Why does David refuse to kill Saul?

Show answer

Saul is "the LORD's anointed": the king's life belongs to God.

Q2 What does Saul's own speech add to the portrait of David?

Show answer

The rival king declares David righteous and the future king.

Mission 5

Saul's end: Endor and Gilboa (1 Samuel 28, 31)

  • Facing the Philistines, Saul gets no answer from God "by prophets, or by dreams" (28:15).
  • He consults a medium at Endor, though he had banned mediums himself (28:3, 7).
  • The dead Samuel's message: "The LORD has torn the kingdom out of your hand and given it to your neighbour, even to David." (28:17)
  • Meanwhile David is serving the Philistine king Achish (28:1–2), but is sent away before the battle (chapter 29).
  • On Mount Gilboa, Saul's sons, including Jonathan, die; wounded, Saul falls on his own sword (31:4).
  • The men of Jabesh-gilead rescue the bodies and bury them (31:11–13): loyalty to Saul lives on.

Mission 5

From Hebron to Jerusalem (2 Samuel 2:1–3:1; 5:17–8:1)

  • David asks the LORD, goes up to Hebron, and is anointed king over Judah (2:1–4).
  • Abner makes Saul's son Ishbosheth king over Israel (2:8–10); at the pool of Gibeon the two sides fight; Abner kills Asahel (2:12–23).
  • "There was a long war between Saul's house and David's house" (3:1). David grows stronger.
  • King over all Israel, David takes Jerusalem, "David's city" (5:7), and defeats the Philistines (5:17–25; 8:1).
  • He brings the ark to Jerusalem: Uzzah dies touching it (6:6–7); David dances "with all his might" (6:14); Michal despises him (6:16, 20–23).
  • Jerusalem becomes both the political and the religious centre of the kingdom.

The Davidic covenant (2 Samuel 7)

  • David wants to build God a house (a temple) (7:2). God replies he will build David a house (a dynasty) (7:11).

> "He will build a house for my name, and I will establish the throne of his kingdom forever. I will be his father, and he will be my son. If he commits iniquity, I will chasten him with the rod of men and with the stripes of the children of men; but my loving kindness will not depart from him, as I took it from Saul, whom I put away before you." (2 Samuel 7:13–15, WEBBE)

  • "Your house and your kingdom will be made sure forever" (7:16).
  • Unconditional: sin will be punished, but the promise will not be withdrawn, unlike Saul's.
  • Later hope for a Messiah ("anointed one") from David's line grows from this promise.

Q1 What are the two meanings of "house" here?

Show answer

A temple for God; a dynasty for David.

Q2 What happens if David's heir sins?

Show answer

Discipline, but God's loving kindness is not withdrawn.

Mission 5

Bathsheba, Uriah and Nathan (2 Samuel 11–13)

  • "At the time when kings go out" to war, "David stayed at Jerusalem" (11:1). He takes Bathsheba, wife of Uriah the Hittite, who becomes pregnant.
  • He tries to cover it up, then sends Uriah to his death in battle (11:14–17). "The thing that David had done displeased the LORD" (11:27).
  • Nathan's parable: a rich man takes a poor man's only lamb (12:1–4). "You are the man!" (12:7).
  • The sentence: "the sword will never depart from your house" (12:10). David confesses: "I have sinned against the LORD." (12:13). The child dies; Solomon is born (12:24).
  • The sword arrives: Amnon rapes his half-sister Tamar; David is angry but does nothing (13:21); Absalom has Amnon killed and flees (13:1–38).
  • The narrative's point: a prophet can confront the king; the king is under God's law.

Mission 5

The king under the law (Deuteronomy 17:14–20)

  • The "law of the king": he must be chosen by God and be an Israelite (17:15).
  • He must not multiply horses, wives or silver and gold (17:16–17).
  • He must write a copy of the law and read it "all the days of his life" (17:18–19).
  • So "that his heart not be lifted up above his brothers" (Deuteronomy 17:20): the king is a brother under the law, not above it.
  • The Decalogue applies to the king too: in 2 Samuel 11 David breaks the commandments against coveting, adultery and murder (Exodus 20:13–14, 17).
  • Many scholars date this law to the late monarchy (Josiah's reforms); it reads like a critique of Solomon's horses, wives and gold (1 Kings 10–11).
Mission 5

How David is remembered

  • Psalm 78:67–72: God rejected Ephraim, "chose the tribe of Judah, Mount Zion" (78:68); took David "from the sheepfolds" (78:70) to shepherd his people "according to the integrity of his heart" (78:72).
  • 1 Kings 15:1–5 (King Abijam of Judah): his heart was not like David's; "Nevertheless for David's sake" God kept a lamp in Jerusalem (15:4).
  • David "did that which was right in the LORD's eyes… all the days of his life, except only in the matter of Uriah the Hittite." (1 Kings 15:5)
  • David becomes the measuring rod: later kings are judged "as David his father" did.
  • The measure is loyal worship of the LORD alone (no other gods), not moral perfection.
Mission 5Evaluate

An unconditional promise? Psalm 89

  • Claim: The Davidic covenant is an unconditional promise.
  • For: 2 Samuel 7:15–16: punishment, but no withdrawal; Psalm 89 itself first affirms it: "I will not break my covenant, nor alter what my lips have uttered." (Psalm 89:34)
  • Against: Psalm 89 then laments: "You have renounced the covenant of your servant. You have defiled his crown in the dust." (Psalm 89:39). The monarchy fell in 587 BCE.
  • Against: other texts make the dynasty conditional: "If your children will keep my covenant" (Psalm 132:12); 1 Kings 9:4–5.
  • Responses: the promise was suspended, not cancelled (hope for a future son of David); Jewish messianic hope; Christians see it fulfilled in Jesus, given "the throne of his father David" (Luke 1:32).
  • Judge it by: Can a promise be unconditional if its benefits depend on obedience? Does Psalm 89 reject the promise, or hold God to it ("where are your former loving kindnesses?", 89:49)?
Mission 5Evaluate

David: an ideal king?

  • For: chosen by God (1 Samuel 16); courage and trust in God (17:45); refuses to kill the LORD's anointed (24:6); unites Israel; makes Jerusalem the place of the ark; receives the covenant; confesses at once (2 Samuel 12:13); remembered as faithful in worship (1 Kings 15:5).
  • Against: adultery and murder (2 Samuel 11); multiplies wives (2 Samuel 3:2–5; 5:13), against Deuteronomy 17:17; serves the Philistines (1 Samuel 27–28); fails to act when Tamar is wronged (13:21); his house is torn by violence.
  • A different view: the narratives are not meant to show an ideal king but a real one: God's choice rests on grace, and repentance matters more than perfection.
  • Judge it by: Ideal by whose standard: Deuteronomy's law, loyalty to the LORD, or political success? Do the narratives praise David, or warn later kings?
Mission check

?More than two centuries after David, King Hezekiah is described: "He did that which was right in the LORD's eyes, according to all that David his father had done." (2 Kings 18:3). What does this formula show about how the Deuteronomistic writers remember David?

David is remembered as the model king not because he never sinned, but because he never turned to other gods, and because God's promise to his house stood.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • In Genesis 12, 15 and 17 God promises Abraham land, offspring and a blessing for all families of the earth; Abraham responds with trust and the sign of circumcision.

Mission 2

  • In the Exodus account, God, who had rescued Israel, offers a covenant at Sinai: "if" Israel keeps it, they will be a treasured, holy nation; the people agree (Exodus 19:8).
  • The Ten Commandments begin, in Jewish counting, with "I am the LORD thy God".
  • Moses wrote the covenant down, the people agreed again (24:7), and it was renewed after the golden calf (Exodus 24; 32; 34).

Mission 3

  • The Mosaic covenant binds Israel to God's law; the prophets charge the people with idolatry and injustice, and later writers read the exile as the covenant curses fulfilled.
  • Isaiah of Jerusalem (eighth century BCE) pronounces six woes (5:8–30) and is commissioned in a vision of God's holiness (6:1–13), with hope in a remnant.
  • The rîb (Isaiah 1:2–31, echoing Deuteronomy 32; 3:13–26) stages God's court case: witnesses, charge, rejected defence, verdict and appeal. Whether covenant is mainly conditional is debated.

Mission 4

  • After the return from exile, Ezra and Nehemiah protected a small community's identity by separation: dissolving mixed marriages, a pledge to keep the Law, and purges in Nehemiah 13.
  • Other voices, several of them post-exilic, include outsiders: foreigners and eunuchs in God's house (Isaiah 56), Egypt and Assyria blessed (Isaiah 19), God's care for other nations (Amos 9:7); Psalm 96 proclaims God's universal reign and fair judgement.
  • Opposing groups claiming God's will, and exclusivity versus inclusivity, are evaluated by scripture, God's character, consequences and the canon's choice to keep both.

Mission 5

  • 1–2 Samuel present David's rise as God's choice: Goliath, En-gedi, Saul's fall at Gilboa, kingship at Hebron and then Jerusalem, the ark brought to Zion.
  • The Davidic covenant (2 Samuel 7) promises a lasting dynasty; Psalm 89 challenges it after the fall of the monarchy. The king stands under the law (Deuteronomy 17:14–20); Bathsheba and Uriah show David breaking it and being judged.
  • The Deuteronomistic writers make David the standard for later kings (1 Kings 15:1–5), "except… Uriah"; whether he is an ideal king is debated.
