Mission 1

Vital rates

  • Crude birth rate (CBR): live births per 1,000 people per year.
  • Crude death rate (CDR): deaths per 1,000 people per year.
  • Natural increase rate (% a year) = (CBR − CDR) ÷ 10. Negative = natural decrease.
  • "Crude" means per 1,000 of the whole population, whatever its age.
Mission 1

Fertility and infant mortality

  • Total fertility rate (TFR): the average number of children a woman would have at today's age-specific birth rates.
  • Replacement level is about 2.1: "the level required for a population to maintain a constant size in the long run without migration".
  • Infant mortality rate (IMR): deaths of infants under 1 year per 1,000 live births. It is a sensitive measure of health care, nutrition and clean water.
  • World TFR in 2024: 2.25.

Mission 1

Age structure drives the crude rates

  • Japan's CDR (12.4) is higher than Niger's (8.7), although Japanese people live 23 years longer.
  • The reason is age structure: a population with many old people has many deaths each year, even with excellent health care.
  • A young population has few deaths and many future parents. Births stay high for decades even after TFR falls: population momentum.
Mission 1

The demographic transition model

  • Stage 1: high, fluctuating birth and death rates; slow growth.
  • Stage 2: death rates fall (food supply, clean water, sanitation, vaccination); birth rates stay high; rapid growth.
  • Stage 3: birth rates fall (lower infant mortality, urbanisation, girls' education, contraception); growth slows.
  • Stage 4: low birth and death rates; little growth.
  • Stage 5 (added later): births below deaths; decline.
Mission 1

Applying the DTM

  • Niger: CDR already low (8.7) but CBR very high (41.4): late stage 2.
  • Japan: deaths exceed births (CBR 6.1, CDR 12.4); population falling by 0.51% a year in 2024: stage 5.
Mission 1

Limits of the DTM

  • Based on the history of Western Europe and North America.
  • In many lower-income countries death rates fell faster, using imported medicine and public health, before industrialisation.
  • It ignores migration, culture and policy (such as China's birth limits).
  • It does not say when, or whether, a country moves on.
Mission 1

Contrasting physical and human settings

  • Niger: landlocked, in the Sahel; much of the north is Sahara desert. Infant mortality is still 63 per 1,000.
  • Japan: mountainous islands; people live mainly on coastal plains. Infant mortality is under 2 per 1,000.
  • The DTM describes both, but its causes differ by setting: health systems, schooling, women's work and culture, not the model itself, set the pace.
Mission 1

Age–sex composition

  • A population pyramid shows the % of people in each 5-year age group, males left, females right.
  • Niger: 46.6% are under 15; 2.6% are 65 or over (2024).
  • Japan: 11.4% are under 15; 29.8% are 65 or over (2024).
  • Dependency ratio = (aged 0–14 + aged 65+) ÷ aged 15–64 × 100.
Quick check
?In 2024, 11.4% of Japan's people were under 15, 29.8% were 65 or over and 58.8% were 15–64. What was its dependency ratio?
Mission 1

Cultural controls

  • Cultural controls are norms that shape births: age at marriage, ideal family size, religion, son preference.
  • Niger (2012 survey): women's ideal number of children was 9.2; 76.3% of women aged 20–24 had married by 18.
  • Son preference: China's sex ratio at birth was about 111 boys per 100 girls in 2020, against about 105 normally.
  • Cultural controls change too: they respond to schooling, urbanisation and law.
Quick check
?Which is a cultural control on fertility?
Mission check

?In 2024 Bangladesh's TFR was 2.14, close to replacement, yet its CBR (20.0) was four times its CDR (5.1). Why is it still growing by natural increase?

Natural change depends on fertility and age structure. Niger has high fertility and a very young population, so births far exceed deaths (natural increase 3.27% a year: doubling in about 21 years). Japan has low fertility and an old population, so deaths exceed births.

Mission 2

The demographic dividend

  • When fertility falls fast, people of working age are "temporarily overrepresented" relative to children and older people.
  • The UN calls this a "window of opportunity" to accelerate development by redirecting resources freed by having fewer children.
  • Reaping it "requires sufficient improvements in education, health, gender equality and gainful employment".
Mission 2

How the dividend works

  • Fertility falls → fewer children per worker → the dependency ratio falls.
  • More workers per dependant can mean more saving and investment, more women in paid work, and more spending on each child's health and schooling.
  • Without jobs and skills, a large young workforce means unemployment, not growth.
  • The window closes as the large working-age cohorts grow old and dependency rises again.
Quick check
?Why is the demographic dividend called a "window"?
Mission 2

Case study: Bangladesh's population change

  • Scale: 41.2 million (1950) → 173.6 million (2024).
  • Character: one of the world's most densely populated countries, about 1,333 people per km² (2024).
  • Pattern: still growing, by 1.2% a year in 2024; projected 214.7 million by 2050, then a slight decline to 208.6 million by 2100.
  • Net migration is outward: more people leave than arrive.
Mission 2

From high to low fertility

  • TFR: 6.82 children per woman in 1972; 2.14 in 2024.
  • Infant mortality: 149 per 1,000 live births in 1972; 17 in 2024.
  • Births still exceed deaths (CBR 20.0, CDR 5.1 in 2024) because the population is young.
Mission 2

Socio-economic factors

  • Family-size norms: women's mean ideal number of children was 2.3 in 2022.
  • Later marriage: the share of women aged 20–24 married by 18 fell from 73.3% (1994) to 50.7% (2022).
  • Women's work: ready-made garments made up 80.6% of exports in 2025–26; in the 1.4 million jobs covered by the ILO–IFC Better Work programme, 49% of workers are women.
  • Remittances from workers abroad equalled 7.4% of GDP in 2025.
Mission 2

Environmental factors

  • Delta setting: flat, fertile alluvial land on the Ganges–Brahmaputra–Meghna delta has long supported dense farming populations.
  • Intensification: irrigation "facilitated double and triple cropping" as population grew.
  • Food from water: in Bangladesh, aquatic foods supply 50% or more of animal protein intake.
  • Hazards: floods, cyclones and salinity threaten land and homes. World Bank projections give up to 40 million internal climate migrants in South Asia by 2050.
Quick check
?In 2024, 28.0% of Bangladesh's people were aged 0–14, 6.5% were 65 or over, and 65.5% were 15–64. What was the dependency ratio?
Mission 2

Implications: the window

  • The working-age share rose from 51.8% (1980) to 65.5% (2024).
  • UN projections put its peak at about 67.9% in 2045. After that, ageing raises dependency again.
  • The UN lists Bangladesh among countries projected to shrink in the second half of the century.
Mission 2

Implications: economy, cities and environment

  • Economy: the dividend depends on productive jobs; heavy reliance on one export industry is a risk.
  • Cities: young workers move to cities for jobs, adding to urban growth and informal housing.
  • Environment: a dense population on a low delta faces flood and cyclone risk; more people and more consumption add pressure on land and water.
Quick check
?Fertility in a country falls quickly, but youth unemployment is high and schooling is poor. What is most likely?
Mission check

?Three countries have similar falls in fertility. Which is most likely to turn its demographic dividend into higher income per person?

Yes, for a time. Falling fertility raises the working-age share (Bangladesh: 51.8% in 1980, 65.5% in 2024), which can raise income per person if there are jobs, health and education. It is a window, not a guarantee.

Mission 3

Voluntary and forced migration

  • Voluntary migration: people choose to move, usually for work, study or family.
  • Forced migration: people are compelled to move by threats to life or livelihood: conflict, persecution, disaster.
  • In practice it is a continuum: many moves mix choice and compulsion.
Mission 3

Who is who

  • Refugee: outside their country "owing to well-founded fear of being persecuted for reasons of race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group or political opinion" (1951 Convention).
  • Asylum seeker: has asked for protection; the claim is not yet decided.
  • Internally displaced person (IDP): forced to flee but "not crossed an internationally recognized State border".
  • Economic migrant: moves by choice, mainly for work and higher income.
Quick check

Mission 3

Global numbers

  • End of 2025: 117.8 million people were forcibly displaced worldwide.
  • They include 41.6 million refugees, 9 million asylum seekers and 68.7 million people displaced inside their own country by conflict or violence.
  • Most displaced people never cross a border.
Mission 3

Conflict and persecution have raised refugee numbers

  • Refugees under UNHCR's mandate (this series leaves out Palestine refugees under UNRWA and others in need of international protection, so it is lower than the 41.6 million total): 16.1 million at the end of 2015; 31.6 million at the end of 2023, almost double (our calculation).
  • The largest one-year rise came in 2022: up 8.1 million (our calculation). Refugees from Ukraine rose from about 28,000 to 5.7 million that year, as war began.
  • Largest origins at the end of 2025: Ukraine 5.2 million, Syria 4.9 million, Sudan 2.8 million, Afghanistan 2.7 million.
  • Numbers fell to 28.5 million by the end of 2025: trends can reverse when people return.

Mission 3

Political push: the Rohingya, 2017

  • On 25 August 2017 the Arakan Rohingya Salvation Army (ARSA) attacked a military base and up to 30 security-force outposts in northern Rakhine State.
  • The authorities called their response "clearance operations".
  • A UN fact-finding mission found the response "immediate, brutal and grossly disproportionate".
  • By mid-August 2018, nearly 725,000 Rohingya had fled to Bangladesh.
Mission 3

Root causes and the trigger

  • The mission found most Rohingya "de facto stateless, arbitrarily deprived of nationality".
  • At least 392 villages, 40% of all settlements in northern Rakhine, were partly or totally destroyed.
  • Long-term causes (statelessness, restrictions) made the group vulnerable; the 2017 operations were the trigger, so people fled at once.
  • On 31 October 2026, Bangladesh hosted 1,208,018 Rohingya refugees.
Mission 3

Migration processes

  • Stages: decision to move, the journey, arrival and settlement. Moves may happen in steps (step migration) or follow earlier migrants (chain migration).
  • Documented routes: visas and work permits, family reunion, refugee resettlement, an asylum claim at a border.
  • Undocumented migrants: cross borders without permission, often paying smugglers, or enter legally and overstay a visa.
  • Undocumented journeys carry high risks: dangerous routes, debt to smugglers and no legal protection at work.
Quick check
?Which person is an undocumented migrant?
Mission 3

Environmental push

  • Sudden onset: Pakistan's 2022 floods affected 33 million people, killed more than 1,730 and displaced more than 8 million. Those displaced within Pakistan are IDPs, not refugees.
  • Slow onset: drought, salinity and sea-level rise push people gradually; such moves often look like economic migration.
  • World Bank projections: climate change could force 216 million people in six regions to move within their own countries by 2050; strong emissions cuts could reduce this by as much as 80%. These are projections, not counts.
Mission 3

Economic migrants

  • Socio-economic causes: wage gaps, unemployment and labour demand abroad (for example construction and domestic work in the Gulf).
  • Processes are often organised: recruitment agencies, fixed contracts, chain migration through relatives.
  • Money sent home matters: personal remittances equalled 7.4% of Bangladesh's GDP in 2025.
Mission 3

Implications: demographic, social, economic

  • Demographic: migration is age- and sex-selective. In Qatar, which relies on migrant workers, there were about 248 males per 100 females in 2024. Source areas lose young adults and age.
  • Economic: remittances raise household incomes at origin; in Cox's Bazar, wages for unskilled work fell as refugees competed for jobs.
  • Social: host and migrant communities may cooperate or clash; family members are separated.
Mission 3

Implications: environmental, health, political

  • Environmental: about 4,818 acres of forest reserve around the Cox's Bazar camps were destroyed (Forest Department estimate).
  • Health: the Rohingya live in "33 highly congested camps"; crowding, shared water and sanitation raise the risk of infectious disease.
  • Political: the UN mission said returns must be "safe, voluntary and dignified" and that "such returns are not possible", so hosting becomes long-term.
Quick check
?Qatar's population has about 248 males per 100 females. Which implication of migration is this?
Mission check

?In 2022 the number of refugees under UNHCR's mandate rose by about 8 million in one year. Which explanation and consequence fit best?

People flee at once when a trigger (here, the 2017 "clearance operations") hits a group already made vulnerable (statelessness). Where they arrive, camps grow fast: Bangladesh hosted 1,208,018 Rohingya refugees in 2026, with long-term pressure on land, jobs, services and politics.

Mission 4

Two shapes of growth

  • Exponential (J-curve): grows by a constant rate, so the numbers added get bigger and bigger.
  • Logistic (S-curve): looks exponential at first, then "levels off" as the population nears a ceiling.
  • That ceiling is the carrying capacity, written K.
  • Lab example: a Paramecium culture grew fast, then levelled off at about 300 cells per cc.
Mission 4

Human growth: J or S?

  • World population: 2.49 billion (1950) → 8.16 billion (2024).
  • Growth rate peaked at 2.28% a year in 1963; 0.86% in 2024.
  • UN projection: a peak of about 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s.
  • The curve is bending, but mainly because fertility fell, not because food or space ran out. Human "K" is not a fixed number.
Quick check
?Niger's population grew 3.26% a year in 2024. Using doubling time ≈ 70 ÷ growth rate (%), about how long would it take to double at that rate?
Mission 4

Over-, under- and optimum population

  • Overpopulation: too many people for the resources and technology available; living standards fall.
  • Underpopulation: too few people to use the resources fully; more people would raise living standards.
  • Optimum population: the size that gives the highest living standard per person.
  • The optimum moves: technology and trade can raise it; depletion and pollution can lower it.
Mission 4

Carrying capacity for humans

  • In ecology: the largest population an environment can sustain (K).
  • For people, K depends on:
    • technology (yields, energy, water treatment);
    • trade (importing food and resources);
    • consumption per person.
  • So the same land can carry many people living simply, or fewer living with high consumption.
Mission 4

Ecological footprint

  • Ecological footprint: "how much area of biologically productive land and water an individual, population or activity requires".
  • Biocapacity: what those surfaces can regenerate. Unit: global hectares (gha).
  • Overshoot: demand exceeds the biosphere's regenerative capacity.
  • 2026: humanity is using nature 73% faster than ecosystems regenerate: 1.73 Earths.
  • Carbon from burning fossil fuels is 61% of humanity's footprint.
Mission 4

Countries in overshoot

Country Overshoot Day 2026: the date when the world would run out of a year's resources if everyone lived like that country.

  • Qatar 4 Feb; USA 14 Mar; UK 22 May; China 27 May.
  • Switzerland: footprint 4.15 gha per person vs world biocapacity 1.48 gha per person.
  • An early date means high demand per person: consumption, not only numbers, drives overshoot.
Quick check
?Switzerland's footprint is 4.15 gha per person; world biocapacity is 1.48 gha per person. How many Earths would humanity need if everyone lived like the Swiss?

Mission 4

The population, resources and pollution model

A systems model (as in The Limits to Growth, 1972):

  • "Population cannot grow without food,"
  • "food production is increased by growth of capital,"
  • "more capital requires more resources,"
  • "discarded resources become pollution,"
  • "pollution interferes with the growth of both population and food."
  • Each part feeds back on the others.
Mission 4

Positive and negative feedback

  • Positive feedback: a change sets off changes that make the first change bigger ("a vicious circle").
    • More people → more births → more people.
  • Negative feedback: a change sets off changes that oppose it, like a thermostat.
    • More people → more deaths at a given mortality; more pollution → less food and health → slower growth.
  • Positive loops drive runaway growth; negative loops pull a system back towards stability.
Quick check
?A city grows; more cars raise air pollution; worse health raises the death rate; growth slows. What kind of loop is this?
Mission 4

Limits of these ideas

  • "Overpopulated" is a judgement: it depends on resources, technology, trade and the living standard assumed.
  • The footprint "is not a full assessment of sustainability" and "does not evaluate the level of degradation".
  • Carrying capacity for people changes with technology and trade; it is not a fixed line.
  • Contrasting views (Malthus, Boserup, Simon) build on these concepts: see the next mission.
Mission check

?Two hypothetical countries. P: footprint 1.0 gha per person, its own biocapacity 0.5 gha per person, population growing fast. Q: footprint 5.0 gha per person, biocapacity 4.0 gha per person, population stable. Which are in overshoot of their own biocapacity, and why?

How many people a place can support depends on numbers × consumption per person set against its biocapacity, which technology and trade can shift; feedback loops can amplify or check growth.

Mission 5

Malthus: the pessimistic view

  • Malthus (1798): "Population, when unchecked, increases in a geometrical ratio. Subsistence increases only in an arithmetical ratio".
  • Population outgrows food until checks act:
    • preventive checks lower births (for example, later marriage)
    • positive checks raise deaths (for example, famine and disease).
Mission 5

Neo-Malthusians: limits to growth

  • Neo-Malthusians extend Malthus's logic to all resources and to pollution.
  • The Limits to Growth (Meadows et al., 1972): if trends in "population, industrialization, pollution, food production, and resource depletion continue unchanged, the limits to growth on this planet will be reached sometime within the next one hundred years".
  • Its second conclusion: "It is possible to alter these growth trends".
Mission 5

Boserup: the optimistic view

  • Ester Boserup (The Conditions of Agricultural Growth, 1965): in Turner and Ali's summary, "population growth is the central source of demand driving the intensification of cultivation".
  • Pressure on land drives new methods, such as irrigation for double and triple cropping and "green revolution" inputs.
  • In short: necessity is the mother of invention.
Quick check
?As population density rose, farmers shortened fallow periods and built terraces to grow more crops on the same land. Which view does this support?
Mission 5

Simon: people as the resource

  • Julian Simon (The Ultimate Resource, second edition) argued that "natural resources are not finite in any meaningful economic sense".
  • His evidence: copper and other minerals "have been getting less scarce, rather than more scarce", measured by price relative to wages.
  • People are producers as well as consumers: the number of people alive "to consume, but also to produce goods and knowledge" drives progress.
  • A cornucopian view: more people bring more ideas, and higher prices trigger substitution and invention.
Mission 5

Testing the views with data

  • World population: 3.06 billion (1961), 8.06 billion (2023).
  • World cereal yield: 1,422 kg per hectare (1961), 4,229 kg per hectare (2023).
  • World food supply: 2,196 kcal per person per day (1961), 2,884 (2013), on FAO's older series.
  • Yields grew about 3.0 times while population grew about 2.6 times (our calculation).
Quick check
?World food supply was 2,196 kcal per person per day in 1961 and 2,884 in 2013. By what percentage did it rise?
Mission 5

Evaluating the views

  • Against Malthus: food per person rose 31% (1961–2013), as yields tripled; he did not foresee the Green Revolution or large-scale trade in food.
  • For a Malthusian caution: 645 million people were still undernourished in 2025; six of nine planetary boundaries (Earth-system limits that mark the "safe operating space for humanity") were transgressed in 2023, seven by 2025.
  • Limits of Boserup: she assumed gradual growth and time to adapt; rapid growth or degraded soils can outpace intensification.
  • Limits of Simon: falling prices show markets adapting, but say little about pollution and planetary limits.
  • Exam move: say which view the evidence supports, at what scale, and for whom.
Mission check

?A study of Bangladeshi villages (1950–1986) found that small farmers "kept pace with the demands on production", but the share of landless households increased. Which conclusion is best supported?

Globally, food per person rose, from 2,196 to 2,884 kcal a day (1961–2013), because cereal yields roughly tripled while population grew 2.6 times. That fits Boserup (and Simon) better than Malthus, but hunger and planetary limits remain, and the answer depends on scale.

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

Mission 6

The ozone shield

  • The ozone layer lies about 15–40 km up, in the stratosphere.
  • Ozone fully absorbs UV-C; it "mostly" absorbs UV-B, though some reaches the ground; it does not absorb UV-A.
  • Chlorine and bromine from ozone-depleting substances (such as CFCs) destroy ozone. One chlorine atom "can destroy over 100,000 ozone molecules".
  • Less ozone → more UV-B at the surface.
Mission 6

UV-B and health

  • UV-B "is particularly effective at damaging DNA".
  • Skin cancer: UV-B causes non-melanoma skin cancer and "plays a major role" in melanoma.
  • Cataracts: UV-B "has been linked to the development of cataracts, a clouding of the eye's lens".
  • It can also suppress the immune system.
Mission 6

How big a burden?

  • Excess UV caused about 1.2 million non-melanoma skin cancers and 325,000 melanomas in 2020.
  • About 15 million people are blind from cataracts; "some 10%" may be due to UV.
  • Skin cancers are caused mainly by UV from the sun or from sunbeds. Ozone loss raises the UV level; behaviour sets each person's dose.
Quick check
?About 15 million people are blind from cataracts, and some 10% of cases may be due to UV. About how many people is that?
Mission 6

A success story: the Montreal Protocol

  • Under the Montreal Protocol (1987), "nearly 99%" of banned ozone-depleting substances have been phased out.
  • The ozone layer should recover to 1980 values by about 2066 over Antarctica, 2045 over the Arctic, 2040 elsewhere.
  • For people in the USA born 1890–2100, the Protocol is expected to prevent about 443 million skin cancers and 63 million cataracts.
Mission 6

Heat stress

  • The body must shed heat. In high temperature, high humidity and low wind it cannot, so risk of heat exhaustion and heatstroke rises.
  • This is heat stress (AQA: thermal stress).
  • Heat also worsens heart disease, diabetes and asthma.
  • About 489,000 heat-related deaths occurred each year in 2000–2019; 45% in Asia, 36% in Europe.
  • Heat-related deaths in people over 65 rose about 85% from 2000–04 to 2017–21.
Mission 6

Heatwaves and attribution

  • Europe's summer of 2003: about 70,000 deaths. Europe's summer of 2022: an estimated 61,672 heat-related excess deaths.
  • Research attributes 37% of heat-related deaths to human-induced climate change.
  • Most at risk: older people, people with chronic illness, outdoor workers, people without cooling.
Quick check
?Why are humid heatwaves more dangerous than dry ones at the same air temperature?
Mission 6

Vector-borne disease: malaria

  • Malaria parasites and mosquitoes need warmth. One model "predicts optimal malaria transmission at 25 °C", with transmission falling sharply above 28 °C.
  • So warming can add malaria where it is cool, and reduce it where it is already very hot.
  • In Ethiopia and Colombia, malaria reached higher altitudes in warmer years.
  • Risk: more malaria in the densely populated highlands of Africa and South America.
Mission 6

Vector-borne disease: dengue

  • Dengue is spread by Aedes mosquitoes; "about half of the world's population is now at risk".
  • Cases reported to WHO rose from 505,430 (2000) to 14.6 million (2024).
  • WHO lists climate change among the drivers, alongside spreading vectors, overburdened health systems and limits in surveillance and reporting.
  • So the rise in reported cases cannot all be put down to climate.
Quick check
?Reported dengue cases rose from 505,430 in 2000 to 14.6 million in 2024. Which statement is best supported?
Mission 6

Nutrition

  • Heat, drought and floods cut harvests. In 2020, 98 million more people faced food insecurity than the 1981–2010 average.
  • Climate change has slowed the growth of farm productivity over the past 50 years.
  • Higher CO₂ lowers zinc and iron in wheat, rice and legumes. About 2 billion people already lack these nutrients.
  • WHO projects about 250,000 extra deaths a year in 2030–2050 from undernutrition, malaria, diarrhoea and heat stress alone.
Mission 6

Who is most at risk?

  • WHO: 3.6 billion people live in areas "highly susceptible to climate change".
  • Low-income countries and small island states "endure the harshest" health impacts despite contributing least to emissions.
  • Risk = hazard × exposure × vulnerability: age, poverty, housing, health care.
Mission check

?Two places in the same country both warm by 2 °C. A highland town has a mean of 18 °C; a lowland town has a mean of 29 °C. Using the temperature model for malaria, what is the likely change in transmission?

Warming does not simply spread malaria: it pushes it upslope into cool highlands and can reduce it where it is already very hot. Ozone loss, separately, lets more UV-B through, raising skin cancer and cataract risk.

Mission 7

Mission 7: From 1980 China limited most couples to one child. By 2021 it allowed three. Why the U-turn, and can governments change how many children people have?

Make a guess: From 1980 China limited most couples to one child. By 2021 it allowed three. Why the U-turn, and can governments change how many children people have?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.2.4.6 prospects for the global population; projected distributions; critical appraisal of future population–environment relationships
Mission 7

Prospects for the global population

  • 8.2 billion in 2024; projected peak of about 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s; about 10.2 billion by 2100.
  • The UN puts the chance of a peak this century at 80%. In 2013 it was about 30%.
  • The earlier peak is partly due to lower-than-expected fertility in some of the largest countries, "particularly China".
Mission 7

Projected distributions

  • Africa: 1.52 billion (2024) → 2.47 billion (2050) → 3.81 billion (2100).
  • Asia: 4.81 billion (2024) → 5.28 billion (2050) → 4.61 billion (2100).
  • Europe: 745 million (2024) → 592 million (2100).
  • 63 countries, with 28% of the world's people, have already peaked, including China and Japan. In nine countries, including Niger, populations may double between 2024 and 2054.
Quick check
?Africa had 1.52 billion people in 2024 and is projected to have 3.81 billion in 2100. By how much is it projected to grow?
Mission 7

How certain are projections?

  • Projections assume future fertility, mortality and migration; the UN publishes ranges, not one answer.
  • The UN says population trends are far more predictable than the economy or technology in the short and medium term.
  • Its 2100 projection is now about 700 million lower than a decade ago: assumptions change as fertility data arrive.
Mission 7

Population and environment: a critical appraisal

  • UN: population growth "tends to amplify" environmental pressures by adding to total demand.
  • But "a sustainable future for all hinges more on human behaviours than on human numbers".
  • Consumption is unequal (IPCC): the 10% of households with the highest emissions produce 34–45% of household emissions; the bottom 50%, 13–15%.
  • Fastest-growing countries are mostly low income and have so far produced only a small share of resource use and emissions.
Quick check
?Which conclusion best fits the UN and IPCC evidence on population, consumption and the environment?
Mission 7

Why fertility policies?

  • Anti-natalist policy aims to reduce births: used where rapid growth outruns food, jobs, schools or resources (a population–resource imbalance).
  • Pro-natalist policy aims to raise births: used where fertility is far below replacement, the workforce shrinks and the population ages.
  • Examples of need: Niger's TFR is 5.94; Japan's 1.22 (2024).
Quick check
?Which is a pro-natalist measure?
Mission 7

China's one-child policy, 1980

  • Its rationale was "to reduce the growth rate of China's enormous population": a response to a population–resource imbalance.
  • 25 September 1980 is often cited as its official start.
  • Some exceptions: some ethnic minority groups, and parents whose first child was disabled.
  • Enforcement: economic and other sanctions, and in places "forced abortions and sterilizations".
Mission 7

Evaluation 1: did the policy cause the fall?

  • China's TFR had already fallen from 6.09 (1970) to 2.75 (1979), before the policy.
  • The 1970s "Longer, Later, Fewer" (wan xi shao) campaign came first. Researchers estimate it explains 27% of China's modern fertility decline.
  • So the one-child policy added to a fall already under way, alongside economic change and education.
Mission 7

Evaluation 2: the costs

  • Sex ratio at birth: about 117 boys per 100 girls in 2000, against about 105 normally, reflecting son preference under birth limits.
  • Ageing: 23.0% of China's people were aged 60 or over at the end of 2025.
  • Human costs: coercive enforcement, including forced abortions and sterilisations in places. Researchers call Chinese population policy "extreme in global experience".
Quick check
?Which statement is the strongest evaluation of the one-child policy?
Mission 7

The U-turn

  • 2016: two children allowed for all couples, after the law was revised in 2015.
  • 2021: three children; the 2020 fertility rate "dropped to 1.3 children per woman", and the shift "addresses the challenges of a graying populace".
  • 2025: 7.92 million births, 11.31 million deaths; population 1,404.89 million, 3.39 million fewer than a year earlier.
  • The imbalance had reversed: from too many births for resources to too few workers for an ageing population.
Mission 7

Can governments raise fertility?

  • UN review: child and family allowances "appear to have a positive but weak effect on fertility".
  • Short-term baby bonuses have "a limited effect, mostly affecting the timing of childbearing".
  • Raising births is harder than lowering them: the costs of raising children, housing and work still decide.
Mission check

?A country's TFR has fallen to about 1.0. Its government offers a one-off cash bonus for each new baby. What does the evidence predict?

China switched from anti-natalist to pro-natalist policy because fertility fell far below replacement (1.3 in 2020) and the population began to age and shrink: in 2025 deaths exceeded births by 3.39 million. Policy helped push births down; raising them again is much harder.

Mission 8

Mission 8: The coast should be good for health, yet men in one seaside town can expect to live years less than England's average. How can a place harm health?

Make a guess: The coast should be good for health, yet men in one seaside town can expect to live years less than England's average. How can a place harm health?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.2.4.7 case study of a specified local area showing the relationship between place and health, related to its physical environment, socio-economic character, and the experience and attitudes of its populations
Mission 8

The place: Blackpool

  • A resort town on the Lancashire coast: "a major centre for tourism in England with 18 million visitors annually".
  • In England's Indices of Deprivation 2025 it ranks 1st of 296 districts (most deprived on average).
  • 40% of its small areas are in England's most deprived tenth.
Mission 8

Health outcomes

  • Life expectancy at birth, 2021–23:
  • Men: 73.1 years (England 79.1). Women: 78.9 (England 83.1).
  • Census 2021, age-standardised: 6.7% in bad health and 2.1% in very bad health, v England 4.1% and 1.2%.
Quick check
?Male life expectancy, 2021–23: Blackpool 73.14 years, England 79.11. What is the gap?
Mission 8

Physical environment: coast and housing

  • The coast itself is healthy: the CMO notes coastal areas' outdoor space, social contact and lower air pollution.
  • But an "oversupply of guest housing has led to houses in multiple occupation (HMOs)", concentrating deprivation and ill health.
  • Blackpool: 31.8% of households rent privately, v 20.5% in England (2021).
  • Blackpool's Director of Public Health: poor-quality private rented housing is "a significant contributor to poor health in the town".
Mission 8

Socio-economic character

Census 2021, Blackpool v England:

  • Long-term sick or disabled: 8.2% v 4.1% of residents aged 16+.
  • No qualifications: 24.6% v 18.1%.
  • The CMO: the sea is "a benefit but also a barrier": limited transport, "in turn limiting job opportunities".
Mission 8

Selective migration

  • Cheap HMOs encourage "the migration of vulnerable people from elsewhere in the UK, often with multiple and complex health needs".
  • Older retired people, with more health problems, also settle on the coast.
  • So the place partly collects ill health, as well as causing it.
  • Blackpool has more in common with Hastings, Skegness or Torbay than with Preston, 18 miles inland.
Mission 8

Experience and attitudes

  • Smoking, adults, 2023–25: 18.7% in Blackpool (95% CI 16.3–21.1) v 10.3% in England.
  • Self-rated health: the Census asks people to judge their own health; Blackpool's residents rate it worse than England's.
  • Behaviours such as smoking cluster where stress, low income and poor housing cluster.
  • Caution: area averages describe places, not every person in them.
Mission 8

Access to care

  • Coastal communities have 14.6% fewer postgraduate medical trainees, 15% fewer consultants and 7.4% fewer nurses per patient than the national average.
  • Attracting NHS and care staff to "peripheral areas" is harder.
  • Health need is higher, but services are thinner: an inverse care pattern.
Quick check
?Which explanation of Blackpool's poor health does the Chief Medical Officer's report stress that a study of air quality alone would miss?
Quick check
?Adult smoking, 2023–25: Blackpool 18.7%, England 10.3%. Roughly how many times the England rate is Blackpool's?
Mission 8

Linking place and health

  • Physical: healthy coast, but old guest houses turned into poor HMOs.
  • Socio-economic: deprivation, worklessness, low qualifications, limited transport.
  • Experience and attitudes: higher smoking, worse self-rated health.
  • Services: fewer staff per patient.
  • Outcome: life expectancy about 6 years below England's for men.
  • Response: the CMO called for a cross-government strategy including housing, environment, education, employment and transport.
Mission check

?In an inland ex-mining town, life expectancy is lower than in a commuter village 10 km away. A student says: "The town's air must be worse." Using Blackpool's case, which reply is best?

A place shapes health through its physical environment (here, cheap converted housing more than sea air), its socio-economic character (deprivation, worklessness), and its people's experience (smoking, self-rated health), and it can attract people already in poor health.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • CBR, CDR, TFR and IMR measure natural change; age structure decides the crude rates. The DTM describes the sequence; its pace differs between settings.
  • Pyramids, dependency ratios and cultural controls (marriage age, ideal family size, son preference) explain the contrast.

Mission 2

  • The demographic dividend is a time-limited potential created by falling fertility; it needs jobs, health, education and gender equality.
  • Bangladesh: population up from 41.2 million (1950) to 173.6 million (2024), fertility down from 6.82 to 2.14, a dense delta society now in its window, with decline projected later this century.

Mission 3

  • Forced migration (refugees, asylum seekers, IDPs) and voluntary economic migration have political, environmental and socio-economic causes; conflict and persecution have raised refugee numbers since 2015.
  • Implications span demographic, environmental, social, economic, health and political effects.

Mission 4

  • Exponential (J) vs logistic (S) growth; carrying capacity K; world growth rate peaked in 1963 and the UN projects a peak around 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s.
  • Over-, under- and optimum population depend on resources and technology; the optimum moves.
  • Footprint vs biocapacity: humanity at 1.73 Earths (2026); overshoot is driven by consumption as well as numbers.
  • Population, resources and pollution model: positive feedback amplifies, negative feedback checks.

Mission 5

  • Malthus: population outruns food until checks act; neo-Malthusians extend limits to all resources and pollution.
  • Boserup: population pressure drives intensification; Simon: people create resources through knowledge.
  • Since 1961 food per person has risen globally, but hunger, inequality and environmental limits keep the debate open.

Mission 6

  • Ozone depletion → more UV-B → skin cancer, cataracts, weaker immunity; the Montreal Protocol is reversing it.
  • Climate change → heat stress, shifting vector-borne disease (malaria upslope, dengue spreading) and poorer nutrition.
  • Effects are unequal: the old, the poor and low-income countries face most risk.

Mission 7

  • The world is projected to peak at about 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s, with growth concentrated in Africa; environmental impact depends more on consumption than on numbers.
  • Fertility policy responds to population–resource imbalances. China's one-child policy accelerated an existing fall at high cost; its U-turn shows how hard it is to raise fertility.

Mission 8

  • Blackpool: men's life expectancy 73.1 v 79.1 in England (2021–23); most deprived district in England (IoD 2025).
  • Causes link housing (HMOs, private renting), deprivation, behaviours (smoking 18.7%), thin services and selective migration, despite a healthy coastal setting.
