Mission 1: Some of your cells have no nucleus and some have hundreds. How does a cell's build match its job?

Make a guess: Some of your cells have no nucleus and some have hundreds. How does a cell's build match its job?

Mission 1

No nucleus, one, or hundreds

  • A typical eukaryotic cell has one nucleus.
  • Red blood cell (mammal): no nucleus. Lost as it matures → more room for haemoglobin.
  • Phloem sieve tube element: no nucleus, but alive. A companion cell supports it.
  • Skeletal muscle fibre: many nuclei. Formed by fusion of many cells.
  • Aseptate fungal hypha: many nuclei in one continuous cytoplasm. No cross-walls.
  • Say: "sieve tube elements are living cells without a nucleus" — not: "dead like xylem".
Mission 1

Size fits the job

  • Sperm head: about 5 µm. Tiny, little cytoplasm → streamlined.
  • Red blood cell: 7–8 µm. Fits through capillaries.
  • White blood cells: mostly 10–20 µm.
  • Egg: about 100 µm, one of the largest human cells. Packed with cytoplasm.
  • Motor neuron: axon over 1 m long, micrometres wide → signals over distance.
  • Striated muscle fibre: can be centimetres long.
  • Egg diameter ≈ 20 × sperm head length.

Mission 1

Two cell types in one alveolus

  • The alveolar epithelium needs two jobs done, so it has two cell types.
  • Type I pneumocytes: extremely thin → very short diffusion distance for O2 and CO2.
  • Type II pneumocytes: thicker, with many lamellar bodies (secretory vesicles).
  • Lamellar bodies discharge surfactant into the alveolar lumen.
  • Say: "type II cells secrete surfactant" — not: "mucus".
Mission 1

Cardiac cells and striated fibres

  • Both contain myofibrils that contract.
  • Striated (skeletal) fibre: unbranched, very long, many nuclei.
  • Cardiac muscle cell: short, branched, usually one nucleus. Joined to neighbours where branches meet.
  • Hypothesis: branching lets the signal to contract spread in all directions → the heart wall contracts as a unit.
  • Is a striated fibre a cell? For: one membrane, one cytoplasm, works as a unit. Against: hundreds of nuclei; formed by fusion of many cells.

Mission 1

Egg and sperm

  • Egg: large; cytoplasm with nutrient stores for the first divisions of the embryo.
  • Egg: zona pellucida, a glycoprotein layer that sperm bind to. Not a cell wall.
  • Egg: cortical granules release enzymes after fertilisation → zona hardens → no more sperm enter.
  • Sperm: small, streamlined, almost no cytoplasm.
  • Sperm: acrosome enzymes digest through the zona. Midpiece mitochondria power the flagellum.
  • Both: haploid nucleus.

Mission 2

Rules for a scientific drawing

  • Sharp pencil. Clear, continuous single lines. No shading or colouring.
  • Draw only what you see, in the right proportions. Use most of the space.
  • Label lines: ruled, no arrowheads, touching the structure, never crossing.
  • Labels outside the drawing, aligned neatly.
  • Annotation = label + a short note: what the structure does, or a feature you can see.
  • Title: specimen, section (TS or LS), magnification or a scale bar.
Mission 2

Plan diagram or cell drawing?

  • Plan diagram (low power): outlines of tissue regions only. No individual cells.
  • Draw each tissue layer in the right proportion of the whole.
  • High-power drawing: a few representative cells, with the detail you see (walls, nuclei, chloroplasts).
  • Leaf TS, top to bottom: upper epidermis (cuticle) → palisade mesophyll → spongy mesophyll (air spaces) → lower epidermis (stomata).
  • In each vein: xylem towards the upper surface, phloem towards the lower.

Mission 2

Stem and root in section

  • Stem TS: epidermis → cortex → a ring of vascular bundles → pith in the centre.
  • In each bundle: phloem on the outside, xylem on the inside.
  • Root TS: epidermis (root hairs) → wide cortex → a central vascular cylinder.
  • In the root, xylem forms a central star; phloem lies between its arms.
  • Annotate functions: xylem carries water and minerals; phloem carries sugars; cortex stores and packs; epidermis protects.

Drawing from an electron micrograph

  • Say: "ribosomes on the rough ER make proteins; the Golgi modifies and packages them" — not: "the Golgi makes proteins".

Mission 3

Mission 3: Nearly every cell in your body carries the same genes. So why doesn't a skin cell act like a nerve cell?

Make a guess: Nearly every cell in your body carries the same genes. So why doesn't a skin cell act like a nerve cell?

Mission 3

One genome, many cell types

  • Almost every cell in a body carries the same genome.
  • Each cell uses only some of its genes. Which ones decides what the cell becomes.
  • Gene expression: transcription → translation → the protein does its job.
  • Transcription is the main switch. No transcription: no mRNA, no protein.
  • Differentiation = different patterns of gene expression, often triggered by the cell's environment.
Quick check
?Developing red blood cells produce large amounts of the beta chain of haemoglobin, but liver cells produce none, although both cell types contain the gene. Which statement explains the difference?
Mission 3

Transcription factors: the switches

  • Transcription factors: proteins that bind specific base sequences in DNA and control transcription.
  • In eukaryotes, RNA polymerase cannot start alone. Transcription factors bind the promoter first; then RNA polymerase binds.
  • Activators stimulate transcription. Repressors inhibit it.
  • Activators also bind enhancers: sequences that may lie thousands of base pairs away, upstream or downstream of the gene.
  • Each cell type has its own mix of transcription factors, so its own set of genes is on.
  • Say: "the promoter is a DNA sequence; transcription factors are proteins that bind it" — not: "the promoter is a protein".
Quick check
?In a eukaryotic cell, a non-coding base sequence located 20 000 base pairs upstream of a gene is deleted. The promoter of the gene is intact, but transcription of the gene falls to a much lower rate. What is the best explanation?
Mission 3

Oestrogen switches genes on

  • Oestrogen is a steroid hormone: lipid-soluble, so it diffuses through the phospholipid bilayer.
  • (1) Inside the cell it binds a receptor protein.
  • (2) In the nucleus, the hormone–receptor complex binds a specific base sequence near its target genes.
  • (3) The complex acts as a transcription factor: RNA polymerase binds; the target genes are transcribed.
  • Oestradiol (the main oestrogen), progesterone and testosterone all act through intracellular receptors like this.
  • Say: "the oestrogen–receptor complex binds the DNA" — not: "oestrogen binds the DNA".
Quick check
?Oestrogen is a steroid hormone that passes through the plasma membrane of its target cells. How does oestrogen alter the pattern of gene expression in these cells?
Mission 3

Same genes, different traits

  • Phenotypic plasticity: one genotype develops different traits in different environments.
  • It works by changing patterns of gene expression. The genotype does not change.
  • Example: a person moves to high altitude. Low oxygen increases expression of the gene for the hormone EPO → more red blood cells within weeks.
  • Back at sea level, expression falls and the red cell count returns towards normal: the change is reversible.
  • Say: "the environment changed which genes are expressed" — not: "the environment changed the genes".
Quick check
?Water fleas (Daphnia) reproduce asexually, so the members of one clone are genetically identical. Young from one clone grow a tall, protective helmet when they develop in water containing chemicals released by their predators, but not in water without these chemicals. Which statement explains this?
Mission 3

Epigenetics: switching without rewriting

  • Epigenetics: heritable changes in gene expression without any change to the base sequence of DNA.
  • Chemical tags on the DNA, and on the histone proteins it wraps around, turn genes down or up.
  • IB calls these epigenetic tags.
  • Tags can stay in place through mitosis: daughter cells keep the same genes switched off.
  • The environment can change the tags (more in the next mission).
Mission 3

Methyl groups and histones

  • (1) DNA methylation: methyl groups (–CH3) are added to cytosine bases, often in a gene's promoter.
  • Methylated promoter → transcription repressed → gene silenced.
  • (2) Histone methylation: methyl groups on amino acids in histones can repress or activate transcription, depending on the tag.
  • Say: "methylation silences the gene; its base sequence is unchanged" — not: "methylation mutates the gene".
Quick check

Quick check

Mission check

Mission 4

Genome, transcriptome, proteome

  • Genome: all the DNA of a cell. The same in almost every cell of the body.
  • Transcriptome: all the mRNA a cell is making at one moment.
  • Proteome: all the proteins in the cell at one moment.
  • No cell expresses all its genes. Its pattern of expression decides how it differentiates.
  • Epigenesis: cells developing different patterns of differentiation. Phenotype changes; genotype does not.

Mission 4

The environment changes the tags

  • Diet, stress, chemicals and pollution can add or remove epigenetic tags.
  • Changed tags → changed gene expression → changed phenotype. The base sequence stays the same.
  • IB example: people exposed to traffic air pollution showed altered DNA methylation in their blood cells within days.
  • Say: "the environment altered gene expression" — not: "the environment mutated the genes".
Quick check

Mission 4

Twins: one genome, two lives

  • Monozygotic (identical) twins come from one zygote: the same genome.
  • So a difference between them in epigenetic tags cannot be caused by their genes.
  • Researchers compare tags in young and old pairs, and in pairs who live together or apart.
  • Twin studies here investigate the environment's effect on gene expression.

Mission 4

Tags at fertilisation: most wiped, some kept

  • In the making of sperm and ova, most epigenetic tags are removed.
  • So the embryo starts fresh and its cells can build new patterns of differentiation.
  • But some tags survive meiosis. A gene's expression can then depend on which parent it came from.
  • Tags kept in gametes are one route of epigenetic inheritance: a phenotype passed on with no change in DNA sequence.
  • Lion × tiger hybrids show it: liger (lion father) grows huge; tigon (tiger father) does not.

Mission 5

The HIV particle

  • HIV (human immunodeficiency virus) is a retrovirus.
  • Genome: two copies of single-stranded RNA.
  • Enzymes packed inside: reverse transcriptase (and others).
  • Capsid: a protein coat around the RNA.
  • Matrix: a protein layer under the envelope.
  • Envelope: a lipid bilayer, taken from the host cell membrane.
  • Attachment proteins (glycoproteins) stick out of the envelope.
  • Contrast: phage lambda has double-stranded DNA and no envelope; coronaviruses have single-stranded RNA and an envelope.
Mission 5

Inside a helper T cell

  • (1) The attachment protein binds a CD4 receptor on a helper T cell.
  • (2) The envelope fuses with the cell membrane. Capsid enters, then releases RNA and enzymes.
  • (3) Reverse transcriptase copies the viral RNA into DNA.
  • (4) The viral DNA enters the nucleus and is inserted into the host cell's DNA.
  • (5) The host cell transcribes and translates it: viral RNA and proteins.
  • (6) New particles assemble and bud out, wrapped in host membrane.
  • The inserted DNA can stay inactive for years, then switch on.
Mission 5

How HIV spreads

  • HIV is carried in body fluids: blood, semen, vaginal fluid, breast milk.
  • Unprotected sex.
  • Shared needles, e.g. among people who inject drugs.
  • Unscreened blood or blood products in a transfusion.
  • Mother to child: across the placenta, during birth, or in breast milk.
  • Not spread by coughing, touching, sharing cutlery or insect bites.
  • A person can pass HIV on for years before AIDS develops.

Mission 5

Read the data: from HIV to AIDS

  • The graph: a typical untreated infection (schematic).
  • Weeks 2–6: virus multiplies fast. Helper T cells fall. Often flu-like illness.
  • Years: few or no symptoms. Helper T cells fall slowly.
  • AIDS: helper T cells below about 200 per mm³, or an AIDS-defining illness.
  • Few helper T cells: B cells are not activated (few antibodies); killer T cells are not stimulated.
  • So opportunistic infections and some cancers take hold: e.g. TB, pneumonia, Kaposi's sarcoma.
  • These, not HIV itself, cause most symptoms and deaths.
  • Say: "HIV destroys helper T cells" — not: "HIV destroys the immune system".
Quick check

Mission 5

The evolutionary race

  • HIV: reverse transcriptase does not proofread. High mutation rate; billions of new virions a day.
  • HIV: surface antigens keep changing, so antibodies and memory cells fall behind.
  • Influenza: point mutations change surface antigens: new vaccine most years.
  • Treatment: drug-resistant HIV variants arise in one patient, so drugs are given in combination.
  • Say: "random mutations; variants that escape are selected" — not: "the virus changes to escape".
Quick check
?Why do influenza viruses and HIV evolve so rapidly?
Mission 5

Diseases that jump from animals

  • A zoonosis: an infectious disease that can pass from another species to humans.
  • Common: most known human pathogens are zoonotic.
  • TB: Mycobacterium bovis from cattle, e.g. in unpasteurised milk.
  • Rabies: a virus passed in the bite of an infected dog or bat.
  • Japanese encephalitis: a virus in pigs and wading birds; mosquitoes carry it to people.
  • COVID-19: SARS-CoV-2's closest known relatives are bat viruses; once in humans it spread person to person.
  • HIV itself came from related viruses in chimpanzees and other primates.
Quick check
?Which statement about COVID-19 is correct?
Mission 5

Read the data: pandemic numbers

  • Percentage change: one quantity over time. Divide by the initial value.
  • % change = (final − initial) ÷ initial × 100.
  • Example (ONS): deaths in England and Wales rose from 530 841 (2019) to 607 922 (2020).
  • (607 922 − 530 841) ÷ 530 841 × 100 = +14.5%.
  • Percentage difference: two groups, neither "first". Divide by their mean.
  • % difference = difference ÷ mean × 100.
  • Example (ONS, 2020, males): death rates of 1231.1 (England) and 1297.3 (Wales) per 100 000, adjusted for age. Difference 66.2; mean 1264.2.
  • 66.2 ÷ 1264.2 × 100 = 5.2%.
  • Compare rates (per 100 000 or per million), not raw totals, when populations differ.

Mission 6: If no animal can change its own genes, how can a whole population change over time?

Make a guess: If no animal can change its own genes, how can a whole population change over time?

Mission 6

Where variation comes from

  • Individuals in a population differ in phenotype.
  • Causes: genes, environment, and both together.
  • Mutation is the original source of all new alleles. It is random: it does not happen because an allele is needed.
  • Meiosis and random fertilisation make new combinations of existing alleles. They make no new alleles.
  • Say: "a mutation arose by chance" — not: "the organism mutated to survive".
Quick check
?A population of a plant species has two alleles of a flower-colour gene. A third allele, never seen before, appears in one seedling. Which process could have produced it?
Mission 6

More young than the habitat can support

  • Populations overproduce: far more offspring than can survive.
  • Resources are limited: food, water, nest sites, territory, light.
  • So members of the same species compete (intraspecific competition).
  • Selection pressures decide who survives and breeds:
    • biotic: predation, disease, competition;
    • abiotic: e.g. extreme cold, drought.
  • Extreme temperature is density-independent: it kills a similar proportion whether the population is crowded or sparse.
Mission 6

Natural selection, step by step

  • (1) Variation: individuals differ in a heritable trait.
  • (2) Selection pressure: e.g. a predator that finds pale beetles more easily on dark bark.
  • (3) Differential survival and reproduction: individuals with the advantageous phenotype are more likely to survive and leave more offspring.
  • (4) Inheritance: they pass on the advantageous allele. Over many generations its frequency rises.
  • No individual changed. The proportions in the population changed.
Mission 6

Fitness

  • Fitness is about reproductive success: surviving offspring that go on to breed.
  • Not size, strength or speed. Those count only if they lead to more offspring.
  • Fitness: the survival value and reproductive potential of a genotype.
  • A phenotype that raises fitness in one environment may lower it in another.
  • Say: "leaves more surviving offspring" — not: "is the fittest because strongest".
Quick check
?In a population of red deer, stag X is the largest and strongest male but leaves two offspring that survive to breed. Stag Y is smaller and weaker but leaves six offspring that survive to breed. Which stag has the higher fitness, and why?
Mission 6

Only heritable traits evolve

  • Selection changes a population only if the trait is heritable: coded in the base sequence of DNA and passed on in gametes.
  • Acquired characteristics are not inherited: trained muscles, an injury, a learned skill.
  • Lamarck proposed that acquired characteristics are inherited. Darwin's natural selection replaced this: a paradigm shift, a change in the framework of ideas scientists use.
  • In Darwin's time many already accepted that species change. What was new was a convincing mechanism.
Quick check
?A woman develops large, strong arm muscles through years of rowing training. Will her children inherit larger arm muscles as a result of her training?
Mission 6

Evolution: a change in allele frequency

  • Evolution = change in the heritable characteristics of a population. Non-genetic changes are not evolution.
  • Gene pool: all the genes, and all their alleles, in a population.
  • Neo-Darwinism: Darwin's natural selection integrated with genetics. Selection changes allele frequencies in the gene pool.
  • Over long periods, adaptation and selection (with isolation) produce new species and the diversity of life.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Nobody designs a drug-resistant germ, so how do whole populations of them appear so quickly?

Make a guess: Nobody designs a drug-resistant germ, so how do whole populations of them appear so quickly?

Mission 7

How a resistant population arises

  • (1) A random mutation gives a few bacteria an allele for resistance, before any antibiotic is used. Bacteria can also gain resistance genes on plasmids from other bacteria.
  • (2) The antibiotic kills susceptible bacteria. Resistant ones survive.
  • (3) Survivors reproduce fast (binary fission) and pass on the resistance allele.
  • (4) Over generations the frequency of the allele rises: the population is now mostly resistant.
  • Nobody chose which bacteria bred, so this is natural selection, not artificial selection.
  • Say: "the antibiotic selected resistant bacteria" — not: "the bacteria became resistant".
Mission 7

Read the data: was resistance there first?

Lederberg and Lederberg (1952): replica plating with the bacterium E. coli.

  • (1) Bacteria spread on a master plate with no antibiotic. Each cell grows into a colony.
  • (2) A sterile velvet pad picks up cells from every colony and presses them onto plates with the antibiotic, keeping their positions.
  • (3) A few colonies grew on the antibiotic plates, at the same positions on every replica.
  • Cells taken from those positions on the master plate, never exposed to the drug, already included resistant cells.
  • So resistance arose before exposure. The drug selected it; it did not cause it.
Quick check
?In a replica-plating test, colonies grow on the antibiotic plate only at the positions of colonies 4 and 11 on the master plate, which has no antibiotic. Cells are then taken from colony 4 on the master plate and placed in a broth containing the antibiotic. What result is expected, and why?
Mission 7

Many drugs, many pests

  • Resistance to a drug usually depends on a specific gene or mutation. Strains can accumulate several: multiresistant bacteria, e.g. MRSA.
  • Every use of an antibiotic is a selection pressure. More use, more selection.
  • Careful use slows resistance: prescribe only when needed, the right drug, good infection control.
  • New techniques open new research: searching chemical libraries by computer is yielding new antibiotic candidates.
Quick check
?A hospital ward uses one particular antibiotic heavily. Over several years, an increasing proportion of the bacteria isolated from patients on the ward are found to be resistant to it. Which statement correctly explains this change?
Mission 7

The fastest evolvers

  • Some viruses evolve very fast:
    • their copying enzymes (RNA polymerase in influenza, reverse transcriptase in HIV) do not proofread, so the mutation rate is high;
    • huge numbers of virions, short generation times.
  • Influenza: point mutations change surface antigens year to year, so the vaccine is reformulated each year.
  • Related viruses infecting the same host cell can swap genetic material. In influenza, swapped genome segments can give a new strain that causes a pandemic.
  • HIV: resistance to any one drug evolves readily, so patients take a combination of drugs with different targets.
Quick check
?HIV infection is treated with a combination of three antiretroviral drugs that act on different viral enzymes, rather than with a single drug. Why is a combination used?
Mission 7

Read the data: owls and snow

Tawny owls (Strix aluco) in Finland, studied 1981–2008 (Karell and colleagues, 2011).

  • Two colour forms: grey and brown. Colour is strongly inherited (about 80% heritable).
  • In winters with deep snow, brown owls survived less well than grey owls.
  • Over the study, snowy winters became less common as the climate warmed.
  • The proportion of brown owls rose.
  • Reading: less snow → brown owls' fitness rose → alleles for brown plumage rose in frequency. Evolution driven by climate change.
Quick check
?In Finland, tawny owls (Strix aluco) occur as a grey morph and a brown morph, and the colour is inherited. Brown owls survived snowy winters less well than grey owls. Between 1981 and 2008 winters became milder with less snow, and the proportion of brown owls rose markedly. What best explains the change?
Mission check

?A patient is given an antibiotic. Within a few days, the bacteria in the patient's infection change from almost all susceptible to almost all resistant. Which statement best explains how this happened so quickly?

Germs, insects, owls: the same rule. The allele was there first; the environment decided who left offspring.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A cell's shape, size and organelles match its function.
  • Atypical cells: red blood cells and sieve tube elements have no nucleus; muscle fibres and aseptate hyphae have many.

Mission 2

  • Scientific drawings: clear continuous lines, no shading, ruled label lines, true proportions; annotations add function or features.
  • Plan (block) diagrams show tissue regions, not cells: leaf, stem with a ring of bundles (xylem inside, phloem outside).
  • In a root, xylem forms a central star with phloem between its arms.

Mission 3

  • Every cell carries the same genome; cell types differ in which genes are transcribed.
  • Transcription factors bind specific DNA sequences to switch genes on or off.
  • Oestrogen works through a receptor that becomes a transcription factor.
  • Epigenetic tags (DNA methylation, histone modification) change expression without changing the sequence, and pass through mitosis.
  • Phenotypic plasticity: one genotype, different traits, by varying gene expression.

Mission 4

  • The environment can change epigenetic tags, so genetically identical cells or organisms can express different genes; the base sequence is unchanged.
  • Most tags are wiped from gametes so the embryo starts afresh; the few that survive explain parent-of-origin effects such as ligers and tigons.

Mission 5

  • HIV binds helper T cells, copies its RNA into DNA, inserts it into host DNA and is made by the cell; losing helper T cells leads to AIDS and opportunistic infections.
  • Zoonoses pass from other species to humans; bats are major virus reservoirs.

Mission 6

  • Random mutation supplies new alleles; selection pressures make individuals with advantageous heritable phenotypes leave more offspring, so those alleles rise in frequency.
  • Evolution is a change in the heritable characteristics of a population; acquired characteristics are not inherited.

Mission 7

  • Resistance alleles arise by random mutation (or gene transfer) before exposure; the drug or pesticide kills susceptible individuals, so resistant ones reproduce and the allele's frequency rises.
  • Viruses evolve fastest: error-prone copying, huge numbers, and swapping of genetic material between related strains.
