Mission 1

Mission 1: Move a metal rod or spin a coil near a magnet, and a voltage appears. What decides how big it is?

Make a guess: Move a metal rod or spin a coil near a magnet, and a voltage appears. What decides how big it is?

Mission 1

A moving rod makes an emf

  • A rod moves at right angles to a uniform field. Its free electrons move with it, so the magnetic force pushes them along the rod.
  • Charge piles up at one end: an emf across the rod.
  • Flux view: in time Δt the rod sweeps area (length × vΔt), so the flux it cuts is B × length × vΔt.
  • ε = Blv (B, l and v all at right angles; l = rod length).
  • Moving along the field, or along its own length: no emf.
Mission 1

Worked example: rod on rails

  • Rod 0.20 m long slides at 3.0 m s⁻¹ on rails joined by a 1.5 Ω resistor. B = 0.50 T, at right angles to the rails' plane.
  • emf: 0.50 × 0.20 × 3.0 = 0.30 V.
  • Current: 0.30 ÷ 1.5 = 0.20 A (rod and rails of negligible resistance).
  • Force on the rod: B × I × length = 0.50 × 0.20 × 0.20 = 0.020 N, opposing its motion.
Quick check
?A metal rod 0.40 m long rests across two parallel horizontal rails 0.40 m apart, which are joined at one end by a resistor. A uniform vertical magnetic field of strength 150 mT acts over the whole arrangement. The rod moves along the rails at constant velocity, away from the resistor, so that it travels 1.0 m in the next 0.50 s. At the instant considered it is 0.15 m from the resistor. What is the emf induced in the rod?
Mission 1

Why the rod is braked

  • The induced current is in the field, so the rod feels a force B × I × length.
  • Lenz's law: that force opposes the motion. Let go, and the rod slows down.
  • Constant speed needs a push equal to the magnetic force: F = BIl = B²l²v/R.
  • Energy: work done by the push = thermal energy in the resistor.
  • Example, the rod on rails above: 0.020 N × 3.0 m s⁻¹ = 0.060 W = I²R = 0.20² × 1.5.
Mission 1

The a.c. generator

  • A coil turns at a steady rate in a uniform field. Slip rings and brushes connect it to the circuit.
  • As it turns, the flux linkage keeps changing, so an emf is induced (Faraday). It reverses every half turn: alternating.
  • Coil plane perpendicular to the field: flux linkage greatest, momentarily not changing. emf = 0.
  • Coil plane parallel to the field: flux linkage zero but changing fastest. emf greatest.
  • Larger B, coil area or number of turns: larger peak emf.
  • Turn it twice as fast: flux linkage changes twice as fast. Peak emf doubles; period halves.
Mission 1

The emf of a rotating coil

  • Angle between field and coil normal: θ = ωt, where ω = 2πf.
  • Flux linkage: NΦ = BAN cos ωt.
  • emf = −(gradient of the flux-linkage graph). The gradient of BAN cos ωt is −BANω sin ωt, so the emf is +BANω sin ωt.
  • ε = BANω sin ωt. Peak emf ε0 = BANω.
  • Example: 200 turns, area 4.0 × 10⁻³ m², B = 0.050 T, 50 revolutions per second. ω = 2π × 50 = 314 rad s⁻¹; peak = 0.050 × 4.0 × 10⁻³ × 200 × 314 = 13 V.
Quick check
?A coil turns at a steady rate in a uniform magnetic field, making one full turn in a time T. At t = 0 the plane of the coil is perpendicular to the field. At which of these times is the size of the induced emf greatest?
Quick check
?A rectangular coil of 40 turns measures 6.0 cm by 4.0 cm. It rotates at a constant 25 revolutions per second about an axis through its centre, parallel to its 6.0 cm sides and perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field of strength 0.10 T. What is the peak emf induced in the coil?
Quick check
?A rectangular coil rotates at a constant frequency in a uniform magnetic field. A graph of the induced emf against time is a sine curve that is zero at t = 0, 10 ms, 20 ms, 30 ms and 40 ms, with maxima of +4.0 V at 5 ms and 25 ms and minima of −4.0 V at 15 ms and 35 ms. The coil is then made to rotate at 40 revolutions per second in the same field. What is the new peak emf?
Mission check

?An aircraft with a wingspan of 40 m flies horizontally at 250 m s⁻¹. The vertical component of the Earth's magnetic field there is 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ T. What emf is induced between the wingtips?

The emf is the rate of cutting flux: emf = B × length × v. Stronger field, longer conductor or faster motion all give more.

Mission 2

Mission 2: An oscilloscope draws a voltage wave. How do you read its size and its frequency off the screen?

Make a guess: An oscilloscope draws a voltage wave. How do you read its size and its frequency off the screen?

Mission 2

Alternating voltage

  • a.c.: the current reverses direction again and again. Here (mains, generators) the pd follows a sine curve.
  • Peak value V0: from the centre line to a crest.
  • Peak-to-peak: crest to trough = 2V0.
  • Period T: time for one cycle. Frequency f = 1/T, in Hz.
  • UK mains: f = 50 Hz, so T = 20 ms.
Mission 2

Reading an oscilloscope

  • The oscilloscope draws pd (up) against time (across) on a grid of divisions.
  • Y-gain (volts per division): pd = number of divisions × V/div.
  • Time-base (time per division): time = number of divisions × time/div.
  • Measure peak-to-peak and halve it: the centre line is hard to judge.
  • Measure several cycles and divide: smaller percentage uncertainty.
  • dc input: a flat line, shifted up or down by the pd.
  • Time-base off: a dc pd moves the spot up or down by (pd ÷ V/div): a dc voltmeter. An a.c. pd draws a vertical line 2V0 long.
Mission 2

Worked example

  • Y-gain 2.0 V/div. Time-base 5.0 ms/div.
  • Trace: 6.0 divisions peak-to-peak; one cycle is 4.0 divisions wide.
  • Peak-to-peak = 6.0 × 2.0 = 12 V, so V0 = 6.0 V.
  • T = 4.0 × 5.0 = 20 ms, so f = 1/0.020 = 50 Hz.
Quick check
?An alternating pd is displayed with the time-base set to 2.0 ms per division. Exactly three complete cycles span 6.0 divisions. What is the frequency of the pd?
Mission 2

Root mean square

  • Power in a resistor depends on V², which keeps changing.
  • rms value: the steady (dc) value giving the same mean power in a resistor.
  • Square → take the mean → take the square root. For a sine wave the mean of V² is V0²/2.
  • Vrms = V0/√2 and Irms = I0/√2 (sine waves only).
  • Mean power = Irms × Vrms = ½I0V0.
  • a.c. meters and supply labels give rms values.
  • Example: the worked example's 6.0 V peak gives Vrms = 6.0/√2 = 4.2 V.
Mission 2

Mains values

  • UK mains is labelled 230 V: that is the rms value.
  • Peak: V0 = 230 × √2 = 325 V.
  • Peak-to-peak: 2 × 325 = 651 V (about 650 V).
  • So the insulation must withstand 325 V, not just 230 V.
Quick check
?An alternating supply with a peak pd of 10 V is connected across a 20 Ω resistor. What is the mean power dissipated in the resistor?
Quick check
?A laboratory a.c. supply is labelled 12 V rms. What is its peak-to-peak pd?
Quick check
?An alternating pd is displayed with the Y-gain set to 0.50 V per division. The trace measures 6.4 divisions from crest to trough. What is the peak pd?
Mission check

?A signal generator gives a sine wave of peak pd 1.5 V and frequency 500 Hz. It is displayed with the Y-gain at 0.50 V per division and the time-base at 0.50 ms per division. How tall is the trace from crest to trough, and how wide is one cycle?

Divisions × Y-gain gives pd. Divisions for one cycle × time-base gives T, and f = 1/T.

Labels and meters give rms values: Vrms = V0/√2.

Mission 3

Mission 3: A transformer changes the voltage of a supply with no moving parts. How does it do it?

Make a guess: A transformer changes the voltage of a supply with no moving parts. How does it do it?

Mission 3

How a transformer works

  • Two coils, primary and secondary, wound on one soft iron core. No wire joins them.
  • a.c. in the primary makes an alternating magnetic flux in the core.
  • The core carries that flux through the secondary.
  • Changing flux linkage in the secondary induces an alternating emf (Faraday's law).
  • Steady dc: steady flux, no output (except briefly at switch-on and switch-off).
Mission 3

The turns ratio

  • Same flux in both coils, so the same emf per turn.
  • Vs/Vp = Ns/Np.
  • Step-up: Ns > Np, so Vs > Vp. Step-down: Ns < Np, so Vs < Vp.
  • Ideal transformer (100 % efficient): IsVs = IpVp. Step the pd up and the current steps down.
Mission 3

Worked example

  • 230 V to 12 V. Primary: 2300 turns.
  • Ns = Np × Vs/Vp = 2300 × 12/230 = 120 turns.
  • Ideal; output current 2.0 A. Output power = 12 × 2.0 = 24 W.
  • Input current = 24/230 = 0.10 A.
Mission 3

Investigating a transformer

  • Two coils on a C-core, low-voltage a.c. supply on the primary.
  • Keep Np fixed; change Ns. Measure Vs with an a.c. voltmeter (or an oscilloscope).
  • Plot Vs against Ns: a straight line through the origin, gradient Vp/Np.
  • Add a load; measure Ip and Is too, to compare IsVs with IpVp.
Quick check
?A transformer has 400 turns on its primary coil and 1600 turns on its secondary coil. The primary is connected to a 12 V a.c. supply. What is the pd across the secondary?
Mission 3

Why real transformers waste energy

  • Efficiency = IsVs/IpVp (output power ÷ input power).
  • Coil resistance: I²R heating in the windings. Use thick copper wire.
  • Eddy currents: the changing flux induces currents in the iron core, which heat it. Make the core from thin laminations, insulated from each other.
  • Magnetising losses: energy to magnetise and demagnetise the core each cycle. Use soft iron.
  • Flux leakage: not all the flux links the secondary. Wind the coils close together, on the same core.
Quick check
?A transformer takes 0.50 A from a 230 V supply. Its output is 9.0 A at 12 V. What is its efficiency?
Quick check
?A transformer's laminated core is replaced by a solid iron core of the same shape and size. What is the main effect?
Mission 3

Sending power at high voltage

  • Power sent: P = IV. For a fixed P, a higher V means a smaller current I.
  • Power lost in the cables: Ploss = I²R.
  • Raise V by a factor k: I falls by k, so the loss falls by k².
  • Example: 20 MW through cables of total resistance 5.0 Ω.
  • At 25 kV: I = 800 A; loss = 800² × 5.0 = 3.2 MW (16 %).
  • At 400 kV: I = 50 A; loss = 50² × 5.0 = 12.5 kW (0.06 %).
  • Step-up transformers at the power station; step-down near homes. This needs a.c.
Quick check
?A power station sends a fixed power along a transmission line. The engineers want to cut the power wasted in the cables to one quarter of its present value. What must they do to the transmission pd?
Mission check

?A transformer has 2000 turns on its primary coil and 100 turns on its secondary coil. What is the secondary pd (i) when the primary is on a 230 V a.c. supply, and (ii) a few seconds after the primary is switched to a 230 V battery?

An alternating current makes a changing flux in the core, which induces an emf in the secondary. The turns ratio sets the size: Vs/Vp = Ns/Np.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A conductor moving at right angles to a field: ε = Blv. The induced current is braked by a force F = BIl.
  • Rotating coil: emf = BANω sin ωt; greatest when the coil plane is parallel to the field.

Mission 2

  • Read a trace: pd = divisions × V/div; T = divisions × time/div; f = 1/T. Measure peak-to-peak and several cycles.
  • Vrms = V0/√2: the dc value giving the same mean power. Mains: 230 V rms, 325 V peak, 651 V peak-to-peak.

Mission 3

  • A transformer needs a changing flux: a.c. in, a.c. out. Vs/Vp = Ns/Np; ideal: IsVs = IpVp.
  • Losses: coil heating, eddy currents (laminated core), magnetising losses and flux leakage.
  • High-voltage transmission: same power at a higher pd needs less current, and the cable loss I²R falls with the square of the current.
