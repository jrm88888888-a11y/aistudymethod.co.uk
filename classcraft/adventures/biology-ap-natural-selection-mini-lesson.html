Mission 1

Mission 1: Why doesn't selection always push a population in the same direction?

Make a guess: Why doesn't selection always push a population in the same direction?

Mission 1

When the heterozygote wins

  • Heterozygote advantage: the heterozygote has higher fitness than either homozygote.
  • Example: the sickle-cell allele where malaria is common.
    • Homozygous for sickle-cell allele: sickle-cell disease, often severe.
    • Homozygous for the normal allele: more likely to die of severe malaria.
    • Heterozygote: more resistant to severe malaria, and no severe sickle-cell disease.
  • So selection keeps both alleles in the population.
  • Where malaria is absent, the heterozygote has no advantage, and the sickle-cell allele is selected against.
Quick check
?In some regions where malaria is common, the allele for sickle-cell hemoglobin occurs at a relatively high frequency, although people homozygous for this allele have sickle-cell disease, which can be severe. People heterozygous for the allele are more resistant to severe malaria than people homozygous for the normal hemoglobin allele. Which statement best explains why the sickle-cell allele remains common in these regions?
Mission 1

Selection that changes direction

  • Biotic and abiotic environments fluctuate. That changes the rate and direction of evolution.
  • Different variants can be favored in different generations.
  • Medium ground finches, Daphne Major, Galápagos (Grant and Grant):
    • after the 1977 drought, mostly large, hard seeds were left: birds with deeper beaks survived better;
    • after the very wet years of 1982–83, small, soft seeds became common: smaller beaks were favored.
  • A change that later reverses is still evolution: allele frequencies changed each time.
Quick check
?In a population of a hypothetical marine snail species, shell thickness is heritable. For many generations predatory crabs were common, thick-shelled snails survived crab attacks more often than thin-shelled snails, and the proportion of thick-shelled snails increased. Building a thick shell uses resources: where there are no crabs, thick-shelled snails leave fewer surviving offspring than thin-shelled snails. A disease then eliminates the crabs from the area, and both shell types remain in the population. Which prediction about the following generations is best supported?
Mission check

?In a hypothetical meadow plant, flowering date is heritable. In years with late spring frosts, early-flowering plants lose their flowers and late-flowering plants set more seed. In years with no late frost, early-flowering plants set more seed. Over ten years of alternating conditions, the mean flowering date moves later, then earlier, and ends close to where it began. Which conclusion is best supported?

The environment changed, so the winners changed. Selection follows the conditions, not a fixed goal.

Mission 2

Mission 2: A desert fox has enormous ears and almost never drinks. How did it end up so well suited to the desert?

Make a guess: A desert fox has enormous ears and almost never drinks. How did it end up so well suited to the desert?

Mission 2

What is an adaptation?

  • An adaptation is an inherited feature that increases an organism's chance of surviving and reproducing in its environment.
  • It arises by natural selection: random variation exists; individuals with a useful allele survive and breed more; the allele becomes more common over generations.
  • Individuals do not adapt in their lifetime. Populations change over generations.
  • An adaptation helps in one environment; the same trait may be useless or harmful in another.
  • Say: "individuals with the allele survived and bred more" — not: "the fox adapted because it needed to".
Quick check
?A hypothetical grass species grows both on soil contaminated with zinc around an old mine and in nearby uncontaminated fields. When grown on zinc soil, plants from the mine site survive, but plants from the fields die. When grown on normal soil, plants from the mine site grow more slowly than plants from the fields. Which reasoning best explains why zinc tolerance is common at the mine site but rare in the fields?
Mission 2

Same problem, same solution

  • Convergent evolution: unrelated groups evolve similar features because they face similar selection pressures.
  • Similar selective pressures can produce similar adaptations in distantly related lineages.
  • Say: "similar because of similar selection" — not: "similar because closely related".
Quick check
?Two distantly related hypothetical species of ground beetle live on two different islands. In each species, body color ranges from pale to dark and is inherited, and birds that hunt by sight eat the beetles. A volcanic eruption covers large areas of both islands with black rock. Which prediction about the beetles over many generations is best supported?
Mission check

?Arctic foxes have much smaller ears than fennec foxes. Which statement best explains how small ears became typical of Arctic foxes?

The fennec's big ears and the Arctic fox's small ones arose the same way: selection acting on inherited variation, in different environments.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why do small, isolated populations end up so genetically different from everyone else?

Make a guess: Why do small, isolated populations end up so genetically different from everyone else?

Mission 3

Chance in the gene pool

  • Gene pool: all the alleles of all the genes in a population.
  • Each generation's alleles are a sample of the last generation's.
  • Which individuals happen to breed, and which alleles go into the gametes, is partly chance.
  • Genetic drift: a random change in allele frequencies from one generation to the next, unrelated to whether the alleles help survival.
  • Drift can make an allele fixed (frequency 1) or lost (frequency 0), even a harmful or useful one.
  • Mutation is also random: it adds new alleles. Drift is a nonselective process.
  • Say: "drift: chance change in allele frequency" — not: "drift: alleles drifting between populations".

Which statement best explains why the allele frequencies in the small population are more likely to change from one generation to the next?
Mission 3

Bottlenecks and founders

  • Genetic bottleneck: a population is cut to a few individuals for at least one generation.
    • The survivors carry a chance sample of alleles. Many alleles are lost.
    • Even after numbers recover, genetic diversity stays low.
    • Northern elephant seals: hunting left perhaps 20–100 by the 1890s. Now there are well over 100,000, with very little genetic variation.
  • Founder effect: a few individuals start a new, isolated population.
    • Allele frequencies reflect the founders, not the source population.
    • Ellis–van Creveld syndrome (a rare inherited condition) is far more common among the Old Order Amish of Lancaster County, Pennsylvania, than elsewhere; it has been traced to one founding couple.
  • Say: "survivors of a bottleneck are a chance sample" — not: "the survivors were the fittest".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly describes the role of random occurrences in the evolution of a population?
Mission 3

Gene flow

  • Gene flow: alleles moving between populations when individuals (or their pollen, seeds or gametes) arrive and breed.
  • AP calls this migration: it can add or remove alleles.
  • Gene flow keeps populations' allele frequencies similar. It prevents divergence.
  • Isolation (a river, mountains, distance, or differences in breeding) reduces gene flow.
  • Then mutation, selection and chance change each gene pool separately: the populations diverge.
  • Drift lets a small population diverge from other populations of its species.
Quick check
?Which event would result in gene flow into a population of a hypothetical wildflower species that grows in a meadow?
Quick check
?Two populations of a hypothetical frog species live in neighboring ponds connected by a stream, and frogs regularly move between the ponds and breed in both. The ponds have similar conditions. A dam is then built that stops all movement of frogs between the ponds. Which prediction about the two populations over many generations is best supported?
Mission check

?A storm carries six birds of a mainland species to a distant island whose habitat is very like the mainland's. A hundred generations later, the island population is still small, and it differs greatly from the mainland in the frequencies of several alleles that have no known effect on survival. Which explanation is best supported?

Few individuals, little gene flow: chance alone can make a population's gene pool unlike any other.

Mission 4: If two groups of the same animal never meet again, how do they end up as different species?

Make a guess: If two groups of the same animal never meet again, how do they end up as different species?

Mission 4

What counts as a species?

  • Biological species concept: a group of organisms that can interbreed and produce fertile offspring.
  • AP wording: a group capable of interbreeding and exchanging genetic information to produce viable, fertile offspring.
  • Horse × donkey → mule. Offspring, yes; fertile, no. So horse and donkey are two species.
  • Say: "can produce fertile offspring" — not: "can produce offspring".
Quick check
?According to the biological species concept, what is a species?
Mission 4

Where the definition struggles

  • Often it cannot be tested:
    • fossils cannot be crossed;
    • populations that never meet cannot show it in the wild.
  • It does not fit organisms that reproduce asexually.
  • Some pairs of species still produce some fertile hybrids (common in plants).
  • So competing species concepts exist: shared traits, DNA, ancestry. None fits every case.
Mission 4

Speciation means splitting

  • Speciation: one species splits into two or more.
  • The key step: populations become reproductively isolated. Gene flow between them stops.
  • Their gene pools then accumulate differences, until members can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring.
  • Usually gradual. Part-way through, "one species or two?" can be an arbitrary call.
  • A lineage that changes over time without splitting has not speciated.
Mission 4

Allopatric speciation, step by step

  • (1) One population. Gene flow keeps its gene pool mixed.
  • (2) A geographical barrier (river, sea, mountains) splits it. Gene flow stops. This is allopatric speciation.
  • (3) Each gene pool now changes on its own: new mutations, different selection pressures, and genetic drift in small populations. Allele frequencies diverge.
  • (4) In time, the two can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring: two species.
  • Gene flow prevents divergence. Without it, genetic drift alone can make a small population diverge.
  • Say: "isolation stopped gene flow; selection changed allele frequencies" — not: "the river caused mutations" or "they changed in order to adapt".

Mission 4

What keeps species apart

  • Isolating mechanisms stop two gene pools mixing.
  • Before fertilization (pre-zygotic):
    • geographical: they live in different places;
    • seasonal (temporal): they breed at different times;
    • behavioral: courtship signals differ;
    • morphological (mechanical): reproductive parts or flowers do not fit;
    • gametic: sperm cannot fertilize the other species' eggs.
  • After fertilization (post-zygotic):
    • the hybrid dies before it can breed;
    • hybrid sterility: the hybrid lives but cannot breed. Mule: 32 horse + 31 donkey chromosomes = 63; the unmatched sets cannot pair properly in meiosis.
  • Pre-zygotic and post-zygotic mechanisms maintain reproductive isolation and prevent gene flow.
Quick check
?Two hypothetical species of toad meet in a narrow zone where their ranges overlap. Where they meet, they sometimes mate, and the hybrid toads survive to adulthood but are sterile. Which prediction about gene flow between the two species is best supported?
Mission check

?A population of lizards was split when rising sea level cut off an island from the mainland. Many generations later, island and mainland lizards can no longer produce fertile offspring together. Which statement about this speciation is correct?

The barrier only stops gene flow. Mutation, selection and drift do the rest, over many generations.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Can a new species appear right beside its parents, or even in a single generation?

Make a guess: Can a new species appear right beside its parents, or even in a single generation?

Mission 5

Speciation without a barrier

  • Sympatric speciation: populations in the same area become reproductively isolated.
  • No geographical barrier. Gene flow is cut by:
    • timing: they breed at different times (temporal isolation);
    • behavior: different courtship or mate choice;
    • ecology: they use different foods, hosts or soils;
    • chromosomes: in plants, a doubling of the chromosome number (polyploidy) can isolate offspring from both parents in a single generation.
  • Example: two Howea palm species on tiny Lord Howe Island. They grow on different soils and flower at different times.
Mission 5

Allopatric and sympatric side by side

  • Both need reproductive isolation: gene flow must stop.
  • Both then need the gene pools to diverge until fertile interbreeding fails.
  • Allopatric: a geographical barrier; populations live apart.
  • Sympatric: same area; isolation by timing, behavior, ecology or chromosomes.
  • Say: "sympatric means without a geographical barrier" — not: "a smaller barrier".
Quick check
?A population of a hypothetical species of land snail lives on both sides of a valley. A river changes course and divides the population into two groups that cannot cross it. The habitats on the two sides differ in vegetation and predators. Which prediction about the two groups over many generations is best supported?
Mission 5

One ancestor, many species

  • Adaptive radiation: one ancestral species gives rise to many species, each adapted to a different niche.
  • Fastest when new habitats or vacant niches appear: new islands, or after a mass extinction.
  • Example: Hawaiian honeycreepers. One finch-like colonist gave rise to dozens of species, with beaks for nectar, seeds, insects and grubs.
  • This is divergent evolution: adaptation to new habitats → phenotypic diversification. Other examples: Hawaiian Drosophila, Caribbean Anolis lizards.
Quick check
?A single species of hypothetical seed-eating bird colonizes a chain of islands. Over many generations, populations on islands with mostly large, hard seeds come to have deep, strong beaks, while populations on islands with mostly small seeds come to have narrow beaks. Which term and explanation best describe this pattern?
Mission 5

Different ancestors, similar answers

  • Convergent evolution: similar selective pressures produce similar adaptations in distantly related lineages.
  • Sharks (fish) and dolphins (mammals): both streamlined, with fins, for fast swimming.
  • Similar traits made this way do not show close relationship.
  • Divergent: relatives become different. Convergent: non-relatives become alike.
Mission 5

Slow and steady, or long waits and bursts?

  • Gradualism: change happens slowly, over hundreds of thousands or millions of years.
  • Punctuated equilibrium: long periods of stasis, then rapid change.
  • "Rapid" is on a geological scale: still many thousands of generations.
  • Fossil records show both patterns in different lineages.
  • Say: "rapid compared with geological time" — not: "in one generation".
Quick check
?The graph shows the mean shell length of fossils of a hypothetical lineage of marine snails in rock layers spanning 10 million years. Which description of the pattern, and of what it shows, is best supported by the graph?
Mission check

?Apple maggot flies lay their eggs in fruit, and adults usually mate on the kind of fruit they grew up in. In parts of North America, some populations of these flies shifted from hawthorn fruit to apples after apple trees were introduced. Apple and hawthorn trees grow in the same areas, but apples ripen several weeks earlier. Which statement best describes this case?

No barrier is needed. Different timing on different hosts is enough to start cutting gene flow.

Mission 6

Mission 6: A harmful allele can hide in healthy carriers. How can you work out how many people carry it?

Make a guess: A harmful allele can hide in healthy carriers. How can you work out how many people carry it?

Mission 6

Populations and gene pools

  • Population: organisms of one species, in the same place at the same time, that can interbreed.
  • Gene pool: all the genes, and all their alleles, in a population.
  • Allele frequency: the fraction of all copies of a gene that are one allele.
  • Two alleles: frequency of A = p, frequency of a = q.
  • Each diploid has 2 copies of each gene. 500 individuals → 1000 copies.
  • Say: "allele frequency counts copies" — not: "the fraction of individuals that carry it".
Mission 6Worked example

Count the copies

A population of 400 snails: 196 AA, 168 Aa, 36 aa.

  • Step 1: total copies = 2 × 400 = 800.
  • Step 2: copies of A = (2 × 196) + 168 = 392 + 168 = 560.
  • Step 3: p = 560 ÷ 800 = 0.70.
  • Step 4: q = 1 − 0.70 = 0.30. Check: (2 × 36 + 168) ÷ 800 = 240 ÷ 800 = 0.30.
  • AA individuals give 2 copies; Aa give 1. Never count individuals.

Mission 6

The Hardy–Weinberg equations

  • p + q = 1 (all copies are A or a).
  • p² + 2pq + q² = 1 (all individuals are AA, Aa or aa).
  • p² = AA. 2pq = Aa. q² = aa.
  • Why 2pq? Aa forms two ways: A egg + a sperm, or a egg + A sperm.
  • Dominant phenotype = p² + 2pq = 1 − q². Recessive phenotype = q².
  • Prediction: allele frequencies stay the same from generation to generation.
Mission 6Worked example

Find the hidden carriers

Real figure: in the UK, about 1 in 2,500 babies has cystic fibrosis, a recessive condition.

  • Step 1: affected = aa = q². So q² = 1 ÷ 2,500 = 0.0004.
  • Step 2: q = √0.0004 = 0.02.
  • Step 3: p = 1 − 0.02 = 0.98.
  • Step 4: carriers = Aa = 2pq = 2 × 0.98 × 0.02 = 0.039.
  • So about 1 in 25 people is a carrier: about 98 carriers for every affected person.
  • Start from q², the only phenotype that shows one genotype.
Quick check
?In a large population, 16% of individuals show the phenotype of the homozygous recessive genotype for a gene with two alleles. Assuming Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, what is the frequency of the homozygous dominant genotype?
Mission 6

When the prediction holds

The allele frequencies stay constant only if:

  • the population is large (no chance changes: genetic drift),
  • mating is random,
  • there are no new mutations,
  • there is no migration in or out,
  • there is no natural selection: every genotype survives and reproduces equally.
  • These conditions are never all met, so the model is a null hypothesis: what to expect if the population is not evolving.
  • Say: "equilibrium: frequencies stay the same" — not: "equilibrium: p = q = 0.5".
Mission 6

Read the data: MN blood groups

Real data: MN blood group genotypes in 6,129 people in the USA (a classic dataset). M and N are codominant, so each blood type shows its genotype.

Mission 6

When frequencies change

  • Evolution: a change in allele frequencies in a population over generations.
  • Changing allele frequencies are evidence that a population is evolving.
  • Selection, drift, migration and mutation change allele frequencies.
  • Say: "populations evolve" — not: "individuals evolve".
Mission 6

Recessive alleles hide

  • A harmful recessive allele is selected against only in aa individuals.
  • Most copies sit in healthy Aa carriers (cystic fibrosis: about 98 carriers per affected person).
  • So selection lowers q slowly, and ever more slowly as q falls.
  • Being dominant does not make an allele spread.
Quick check

Mission check

?In a large, randomly mating population of a hypothetical moth species, pale wings are caused by a recessive allele. 9% of the moths have pale wings. Assuming Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, what proportion of the moths are heterozygous?

Count the ones you can see (q²), take the square root, and the hidden carriers appear: 2pq.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Heterozygote advantage keeps both alleles in a population.
  • When the environment fluctuates, the direction of selection can change.

Mission 2

  • An adaptation is an inherited feature, favored by natural selection, that improves survival and reproduction in a particular environment.
  • Convergent evolution: similar selection pressures give similar (analogous) features in unrelated groups.

Mission 3

  • Genetic drift is a random change in allele frequencies; it matters most in small populations, where chance sampling has large effects.
  • Bottlenecks and founder events leave a small, chance sample of alleles.
  • Gene flow keeps populations alike; isolation stops it, so allele frequencies diverge.

Mission 4

  • A species can interbreed to produce fertile offspring; speciation is one species splitting into two once gene flow between populations stops and their gene pools diverge.
  • Isolating mechanisms (geographical, seasonal, behavioral, morphological, hybrid sterility) keep species' gene pools apart.

Mission 5

  • Sympatric speciation happens in one area when timing, behavior, ecology or chromosomes stop gene flow; allopatric needs a geographical barrier; both need reproductive isolation, then divergence.
  • Adaptive radiation is divergent evolution into new habitats; convergent evolution makes distant lineages alike; tempo can be gradual or punctuated.

Mission 6

  • p + q = 1 and p² + 2pq + q² = 1: from the recessive phenotype (q²) you can find every allele and genotype frequency.
  • Allele frequencies stay constant only in a large, randomly mating population with no mutation, migration or selection; a change in allele frequencies is evolution.
  • The Hardy–Weinberg model is a null hypothesis for a population that is not evolving.
