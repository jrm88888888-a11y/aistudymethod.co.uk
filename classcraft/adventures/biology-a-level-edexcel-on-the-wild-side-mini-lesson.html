Mission 1

Mission 1: Why does a population that doubles again and again eventually stop growing?

Make a guess: Why does a population that doubles again and again eventually stop growing?

Mission 1

Four ways numbers change

  • Births and immigration add individuals. Deaths and emigration remove them.
  • Change in population = (births + immigrants) − (deaths + emigrants).
Mission 1

Sigmoid growth and carrying capacity

  • Real populations slow down and level off: an S-shaped (sigmoid) curve.
  • Carrying capacity: the largest population the environment's resources can support sustainably.
  • Limiting resources: food, water, light, space, nest sites, mineral ions.
  • As numbers rise, resources per individual fall. Births fall; deaths rise. At the plateau, births ≈ deaths.
  • Births and deaths do not stop at the plateau: they balance.
  • Carrying capacity varies with abiotic factors, competition and predation.
Mission 1

Density-dependent and density-independent factors

  • Density-dependent: affects a larger proportion of the population as it gets denser.
    • Competition for limited resources, predation, disease and parasites spread faster when crowded.
  • Density-independent: affects a similar proportion whatever the density.
    • Frost, drought, flood, fire, storms.
  • Biotic factors are usually density-dependent; abiotic factors such as weather are usually density-independent.
  • Say: "a storm kills the same proportion" — not: "density-dependent because it killed so many".
Quick check
?A storm kills 30% of a seabird colony. Over the following years, the colony returns to its former size. Which explanation is correct?
Mission check

?In 1911, 25 reindeer were introduced to St Paul Island, Alaska, where they had almost no predators. By 1938 there were about 2000; by 1950 only 8 remained, after the lichens they ate in winter had been destroyed. How should this be interpreted in relation to the sigmoid growth model?

Growth stops because each individual's share of the resources shrinks. Destroy the resources and the carrying capacity itself falls.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How can you know how many snails live in a whole wood without finding every single one?

Make a guess: How can you know how many snails live in a whole wood without finding every single one?

Mission 2

Why sample, and why at random

  • Counting every individual is usually impossible: too slow, organisms hidden or moving, the habitat trampled.
  • So count a sample and scale up.
  • Random sampling: every point has an equal chance of being chosen. This avoids bias.
  • The estimate still differs from the true size. That difference is sampling error. More samples reduce it; only a full count removes it.
  • Say: "random placement avoids bias" — not: "choose typical spots".
Mission 2

Random quadrats

  • For sessile or slow-moving organisms: plants, limpets, barnacles.
  • Lay two tape measures at right angles. Use random numbers as coordinates for each quadrat.
  • Measures of abundance:
    • Density: number of individuals per m².
    • Frequency: % of quadrats in which the species occurs.
    • Percentage cover: % of the quadrat area it covers. Use where individuals can't be counted, e.g. grass.
Mission 2

Worked example: from quadrats to a population

  • Plantain plants in a 1200 m² field. Ten random 0.5 m × 0.5 m quadrats.
  • Counts: 3, 0, 5, 2, 4, 1, 6, 3, 2, 4. Total 30.
  • Mean per quadrat = 30 ÷ 10 = 3.0.
  • Quadrat area = 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25 m². Density = 3.0 ÷ 0.25 = 12 per m².
  • Estimate = 12 × 1200 = 14 400 plants (14 000 to 2 significant figures).
Quick check
?A student places ten 0.5 m × 0.5 m quadrats at random in a 250 m² meadow and counts 20 orchids in total. What is the estimated population of orchids in the meadow?
Mission 2

Transects: sampling along a change

  • Use a transect where conditions change across the site: shore, woodland edge, sand dune, path.
  • Line transect: record every species touching a line at regular intervals.
  • Belt transect: place quadrats along the line, continuously or at regular intervals (interrupted).
  • At each quadrat, measure the abiotic factor: light intensity, soil moisture, soil pH, salinity, temperature. Sensors and data loggers help.
  • Pair each abundance value with its abiotic value. Then look for a correlation.
  • A correlation is not proof of cause. Up a shore, several factors change together.
Mission 2Practical

Distribution and an abiotic factor

  • Core practical 10: quadrats and transects for distribution and abundance; abiotic factors.
  • Method: lay a transect across the gradient; quadrat at fixed intervals; record % cover or density; measure the factor at each quadrat.
  • Control: same quadrat size, same observer, same time of day; repeat transects.
  • Analysis: plot abundance against the factor; test the correlation (e.g. Spearman's rank; choosing tests in another lesson).

Mission 3

Mission 3: Bare rock can turn into forest without anyone planting a single tree. How does that happen?

Make a guess: Bare rock can turn into forest without anyone planting a single tree. How does that happen?

Mission 3

What succession is

  • Succession: change in the species making up a community, over time.
  • Primary succession: starts on bare ground with no soil: rock, cooled lava, sand, land left by a melting glacier.
Mission 3

Pioneers on bare rock

  • Pioneer species colonise first: e.g. lichens, then mosses.
  • Their features: spores spread by wind; survive drought, heat and cold; need almost no soil.
  • Lichens help weather the rock surface.
  • When pioneers die, decomposers turn them into humus.
  • Rock particles + humus = the first thin soil.
Mission 3

Each stage paves the way, then gives way

  • Deeper soil holds more water and nutrients: the environment is less hostile.
  • New species can now establish: grasses and ferns, then shrubs, then trees.
  • Taller plants shade out the earlier ones.
  • Each stage changes the environment so it suits other species better, and often suits itself less.
  • Say: "the pioneers changed conditions, which allowed other species to establish" — not: "pioneers prepare the ground for the next species".
Quick check
?Alder trees colonising ground exposed by a retreating glacier have nitrogen-fixing bacteria in their root nodules. Within decades the soil nitrogen content rises and spruce trees, which cannot grow on the bare ground, replace the alders. Which statement about the cause of this change is correct?
Mission 3

What rises as succession proceeds

  • Soil depth, humus, water and mineral content.
  • Plant size: mosses → shrubs → trees.
  • Primary production (biomass made per m² per year).
  • Species diversity and food-web complexity: more niches.
Quick checkRead the data

Mission 3

The climax community

  • Climax community: the final, stable community, in equilibrium with its environment.
  • It persists until a disturbance; its populations still fluctuate.
  • Usually dominated by a few large species. In most of lowland Britain and Ireland: deciduous woodland (e.g. oak).
Mission check

?On a coastal sand-dune system, marram grass covers the young dunes nearest the sea. On older dunes further inland, marram is scarce and the dunes are covered by mosses, low shrubs and other grasses, growing in a thin layer of soil. Which explanation is correct?

On rock or on sand, pioneers change the ground they live on, and their changes let others take over. That is how bare ground becomes forest.

Mission 4

Mission 4: A plant takes in carbon dioxide and water and gives out oxygen. Which of the two did the oxygen come from?

Make a guess: A plant takes in carbon dioxide and water and gives out oxygen. Which of the two did the oxygen come from?

Mission 4

Photosynthesis in one line

  • carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen
  • 6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2 (light energy, absorbed by chlorophyll)
  • Light energy becomes chemical energy in the carbon compounds made.
  • Energy is transformed, not created.
  • Say: "photosynthesis transforms light energy" — not: "plants make energy".
Mission 4

Water is split for its hydrogen

  • CO2 has no hydrogen. Glucose has 12 H atoms. They come from water.
  • Light energy is used to split water.
  • Its hydrogen (as protons and electrons) reduces CO2 to glucose.
  • The oxygen left over is a by-product: it is released.
  • Light energy breaks the strong bonds in water; the hydrogen is stored in a fuel (glucose) by combining it with CO2.
  • CO2's carbon and oxygen end up in glucose and in some new water, not in the O2.
  • Plants, algae and cyanobacteria (prokaryotes) all split water and release O2.
  • Say: "the oxygen released comes from water" — not: "the plant keeps the carbon of CO2 and releases its oxygen".

Mission 4

Inside a chloroplast

  • Envelope: outer and inner membrane.
  • Thylakoids: flattened membrane sacs. A stack is a granum (plural grana).
  • Lamellae (intergranal thylakoids) link the grana.
  • Stroma: the fluid around the thylakoids, inside the inner membrane.
  • Thylakoid membranes hold chlorophyll and electron carriers.
  • The chlorophyll is organised into two photosystems.
  • Light-dependent stage: thylakoid membranes. Light-independent stage: stroma.
  • Stroma also holds circular DNA, 70S ribosomes, starch grains and lipid droplets.
Mission 4

Built for the job

  • Huge thylakoid membrane area: room for many photosystems, carriers and ATP synthase.
  • Small volume inside each thylakoid: protons pumped in build a steep gradient fast.
  • Stroma: Calvin cycle enzymes and substrates concentrated together, right next to the thylakoids that supply ATP and reduced NADP.
  • DNA and ribosomes: the chloroplast makes some of its own proteins.
  • Starch grains: sugar stored where it is made.
Quick check
?Which statement correctly matches an adaptation of the chloroplast to its function in photosynthesis?
Mission check

?Chloroplasts from a hypothetical alga are broken open and separated into two fractions: the thylakoid membranes, and the stroma with no thylakoids. Each fraction is placed in the light with water, ¹⁴C-labelled CO2, ADP, inorganic phosphate, and a dye that accepts electrons in place of NADP. No ATP or reduced NADP (NADPH) is added. The O2 comes from water split in the thylakoid membranes; the carbon from CO2 is built into sugar in the stroma.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Your cells already get energy from sugar. Why do they spend it through a different molecule first?

Make a guess: Your cells already get energy from sugar. Why do they spend it through a different molecule first?

Mission 5

Hydrolysis releases energy

  • ATP + H2O → ADP + Pi (inorganic phosphate).
  • Energy is released: enough for many single tasks in the cell.
  • Hydrolysis of ATP provides an immediate supply of energy.
  • Breaking any bond takes energy. Energy is released overall because ADP and Pi are more stable than ATP.
  • Say: "energy is released when ATP is hydrolysed" — not: "energy is stored in a high-energy bond".
Mission 5

Coupling: putting the energy to work

  • ATP hydrolysis is coupled to an energy-requiring process: one enzyme links them, so they happen together.
  • What ATP powers:
  • active transport of ions and molecules across membranes
  • synthesis of macromolecules (anabolism), e.g. proteins, DNA
  • movement: muscle contraction, cilia, chromosomes in cell division
  • Say: "active transport uses ATP" — not: "diffusion and osmosis use ATP".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly describes a life process that ATP supplies with energy?
Mission 5

Remaking ATP: the ATP–ADP cycle

  • ADP + Pi → ATP + H2O: a condensation reaction.
  • This phosphorylation of ADP requires energy.
  • Energy comes from respiration (every cell) and photosynthesis (in chloroplasts).
  • ATP is not stockpiled. A cell keeps a small pool and recycles it, second by second.
  • A resting adult turns over roughly their own body mass of ATP each day.
Quick check
?During a sprint, the small store of ATP in muscle fibres is hydrolysed and then re-formed several times over. Which statement about the energy transfers is correct?
Mission 5

Why not spend glucose directly?

  • Glucose releases its energy in many enzyme-controlled steps. ATP: one step, one enzyme.
  • One glucose holds far more energy than one reaction needs. ATP comes in small packets.
  • About 30 kJ per mole of ATP hydrolysed; about 2870 kJ per mole of glucose fully oxidised.
  • Released immediately, right where it is needed.
  • Soluble: moves freely within the cell.
  • Quickly remade from ADP + Pi.
Quick check
?A cell needs an energy currency that can supply many different reactions in the cytoplasm. Which property makes ATP suitable for this role?
Mission check

?A muscle fibre holds only enough ATP for a few seconds of hard contraction. Yet a marathon runner's leg muscles keep contracting for hours. Which statement explains this?

A small, fast-recycled pool of ATP, refilled by respiration, beats spending glucose directly.

Mission 6

Mission 6: A leaf splits water to get electrons. Where do those electrons end up, and what do they make on the way?

Make a guess: A leaf splits water to get electrons. Where do those electrons end up, and what do they make on the way?

Mission 6

Photosystems catch light

  • Thylakoid membranes hold two photosystems: photosystem II (PSII) and photosystem I (PSI).
  • Each is an array of chlorophyll and accessory pigments, with a special chlorophyll a at the reaction centre.
  • Absorbed energy is passed to the reaction centre.
  • There an electron is raised to a higher energy level and emitted to an electron acceptor.
  • Say: "light excites an electron, which the chlorophyll loses" — not: "chlorophyll makes electrons".
Mission 6

The electron path: PSII → PSI → NADP

1. Light excites PSII. Its electron passes to an electron transport chain (ETC).

2. Carrier to carrier: each step is a redox reaction that releases energy.

3. The electron reaches PSI and is excited again by light.

4. It passes to NADP in the stroma.

Mission 6

Photolysis refills PSII

  • PSII has lost electrons. It must get them back.
  • Water is split: 2H2O → 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ + O2. This is photolysis.
  • Electrons replace those lost from PSII.
  • Protons (H⁺) stay inside the thylakoid: they add to the proton gradient.
  • Oxygen is a by-product (waste): it diffuses out of the leaf.
  • Say: "splitting water supplies electrons" — not: "splitting water supplies the energy". The energy comes from light.
Quick check
?Which statement about the photolysis of water in photosystem II is correct?
Mission 6

Reducing NADP

  • At the end of the chain, PSI passes two electrons to NADP, on the stroma side.
  • NADP also takes a hydrogen ion from the stroma: NADP is reduced to reduced NADP.
  • Reduced NADP and ATP carry the energy to the light-independent stage (see Mission 7).
Mission 6

Chemiosmosis makes ATP

  • Energy released along the ETC pumps H⁺ from the stroma into the thylakoid space.
  • Photolysis adds more H⁺ inside; NADP reduction removes H⁺ outside.
  • Result: high [H⁺] inside, low outside: an electrochemical gradient.
  • H⁺ flow back to the stroma through ATP synthase. This drives ADP + Pᵢ → ATP, released into the stroma.
  • ATP made this way: photophosphorylation. The mechanism: chemiosmosis.
  • Say: "ATP synthase is driven by H⁺ flowing down their gradient" — not: "the carriers make ATP".
Quick check
?Which processes in an illuminated chloroplast build up the proton gradient across the thylakoid membrane?
Quick checkRead the data

Mission 6

Practical: chloroplasts and a blue dye

  • Core practical 11: investigate photosynthesis using isolated chloroplasts (the Hill reaction).
  • Blend leaves in ice-cold, isotonic buffer; filter; centrifuge; resuspend the chloroplasts. Keep cold and dim.
  • Add blue DCPIP. In light, it accepts electrons (as hydrogen) in place of NADP: blue → colourless.
  • Measure time to colourless, or absorbance in a colorimeter (red filter) at intervals.
  • Controls: chloroplasts in the dark; boiled chloroplasts in light; DCPIP with buffer only.
  • Named factor, e.g. light intensity (lamp distance), temperature or a herbicide concentration.
  • Rate = 1 ÷ time, or fall in absorbance per minute.

Mission check

?A chemical is added that takes electrons from photosystem I in place of NADP. The chloroplasts are illuminated with a supply of water, ADP, phosphate and NADP. Which prediction is correct?

Electrons from water normally end on NADP; on the way, their energy builds the proton gradient that makes ATP. Swap the final acceptor, and only the reduced NADP is lost.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Switch off the light and a leaf stops making sugar within seconds, yet that stage needs no light. Why?

Make a guess: Switch off the light and a leaf stops making sugar within seconds, yet that stage needs no light. Why?

Mission 7

The light-independent stage

  • Happens in the stroma.
  • Uses ATP and reduced NADP from the light-dependent stage.
  • Builds CO2 into sugar in a cycle: the Calvin cycle.
  • Three steps: fixation, reduction, regeneration.
  • It needs no light directly, but stops soon after the light does.
  • Say: "light-independent reactions" — not: "dark reactions that happen at night".
Quick check
?A student is drawing a model of how the two stages of photosynthesis in a chloroplast are connected. Which set of arrows between the light-dependent stage and the Calvin cycle should the model show?
Mission 7

Fixation: rubisco captures CO2

  • CO2 combines with ribulose bisphosphate (RuBP, 5C).
  • Enzyme: rubisco.
  • The 6C product is unstable: it splits at once into two glycerate 3-phosphate (GP, 3C).
  • No ATP is used in this step.
  • The enzyme is written RUBISCO.
Mission 7

Reduction: GP to triose phosphate

  • Each GP is converted to triose phosphate (TP, 3C).
  • ATP supplies energy (and phosphate).
  • Reduced NADP supplies hydrogen: it reduces GP.
  • ADP, Pᵢ and NADP go back to the thylakoids.
  • Triose phosphate is called GALP (glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate).
  • Say: "reduced NADP reduces GP" — not: "rubisco reduces CO2".
Quick check
?What is required to convert glycerate 3-phosphate into triose phosphate in the Calvin cycle?
Mission 7

Regeneration: keeping the cycle turning

  • Most TP is used to regenerate RuBP, using ATP (no reduced NADP).
  • 5 TP (15 C) → 3 RuBP (15 C).
  • Worked count for one glucose (6 C):

1. Fix 6 CO2 → 12 GP → 12 TP (36 C).

2. 10 TP (30 C) → 6 RuBP (30 C): the cycle is ready again.

3. 2 TP (6 C) leave → one glucose.

  • So five-sixths of TP is recycled; one-sixth is the product.
  • Cost per glucose: 18 ATP (12 for reduction, 6 for regeneration) and 12 reduced NADP.
Quick check
?In a Calvin cycle that produces one molecule of glucose, how many molecules of triose phosphate are made in total, and how many of them are used to regenerate RuBP?
Mission 7

What TP becomes

  • Carbohydrates: glucose → sucrose (transport), starch (store), cellulose (cell walls).
  • Lipids: glycerol and fatty acids.
  • Amino acids: carbon skeletons from the cycle, plus nitrogen from nitrate or ammonium ions taken up by roots.
  • Also nucleic acids. The sugars are used in respiration by plants, then by animals and other organisms.

Mission check

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Plentiful resources: exponential growth. As resources per individual shrink, births fall and deaths rise until they balance at the carrying capacity.
  • Density-dependent factors (competition, predation, disease) push numbers back towards carrying capacity; density-independent factors (weather) knock them about.

Mission 2

  • Sample at random and scale up: quadrats for sessile organisms, transects where conditions change.

Mission 3

  • Pioneers colonise bare ground and change it; each stage makes the environment suit other species, until a stable climax community forms.

Mission 4

  • Photosynthesis transforms light energy into chemical energy: water is split, its hydrogen reduces CO2 to sugar, and its oxygen is released.
  • Light-dependent stage (light reactions): thylakoid membranes. Light-independent stage (Calvin cycle): stroma.

Mission 5

  • ATP = adenine + ribose + three phosphates. Hydrolysis to ADP + Pi releases energy in a small, immediate packet; respiration supplies the energy to remake it.

Mission 6

  • Light excites electrons in PSII and PSI; they pass along an electron transport chain to NADP. Water splitting replaces PSII's electrons and releases O2.
  • Energy from the chain pumps protons into the thylakoid space; their flow back through ATP synthase makes ATP (chemiosmosis). Products: ATP and reduced NADP.

Mission 7

  • Rubisco fixes CO2 with RuBP → 2 GP; ATP and reduced NADP reduce GP → TP; five-sixths of TP regenerates RuBP (using ATP); one-sixth makes sugars, lipids and amino acids.
  • Light off: GP rises, RuBP falls. CO2 down: RuBP rises, GP falls.
