Mission 1

What is ordination?

  • Ordination: the setting apart of a person for ministry, by prayer and the laying on of hands.
  • Three orders in Catholic, Orthodox and Anglican churches: deacon, priest (presbyter), bishop.
  • For Catholics and Orthodox, ordination (Holy Orders) is a sacrament: the priest acts in persona Christi (in the person of Christ) at the Eucharist.
  • Many Protestant churches speak of ministers or pastors, called and recognised by the church.
  • Three broad positions: all orders open to women; some orders open; none.
Mission 1

1994 and after

  • 12 March 1994: 32 women ordained priest in Bristol Cathedral.
  • Some clergy and lay people left; several hundred clergy, with many lay people, were received into the Catholic Church.
  • 2012: a measure for women bishops fails in the House of Laity. 2014: it passes, with Five Guiding Principles promising "mutual flourishing" for those who disagree.
  • 2015: Libby Lane, first woman bishop (Stockport). 2018: Sarah Mullally, Bishop of London.
  • 2026: Sarah Mullally installed as Archbishop of Canterbury (25 March), the first woman to lead the Church of England.
Mission 1

Arguments against ordaining women

  • Jesus chose twelve men as apostles, though he had women disciples; the Church follows his choice.
  • Tradition: the Church has ordained only men for almost 2,000 years.
  • Sacramental sign: the priest represents Christ, the Bridegroom of the Church; a man represents him more naturally (Catholic: Inter Insigniores, 1976).
  • Headship: some evangelicals hold that 1 Timothy 2:12 and 1 Corinthians 14:34 reserve teaching authority over men to men.
  • Unity: ordaining women divides churches and harms relations with the Catholic and Orthodox Churches.
Mission 1

Arguments for ordaining women

  • Equal image of God (Genesis 1:27) and equality in Christ (Galatians 3:28).
  • Jesus' practice: women followed and funded his ministry; Mary Magdalene was the first witness of the resurrection, sent to tell the disciples (John 20:17–18): called "apostle to the apostles".
  • Early church: Phoebe a deacon; Junia "notable amongst the apostles"; the Spirit poured out so that "your sons and your daughters will prophesy" (Acts 2:17).
  • The Twelve were Jewish men representing Israel's twelve tribes; their maleness was symbolic of Israel, not a rule for priesthood.
  • Vocation and justice: women report a genuine call from God; refusing it seems unjust, and gifts are wasted.
Mission 1Diversity

Catholic and Orthodox positions

  • Catholic: Ordinatio Sacerdotalis (John Paul II, 22 May 1994): "the Church has no authority whatsoever to confer priestly ordination on women", a judgement to be held definitively.
  • This is presented as a matter of faith, not of discipline: the Church cannot change it, rather than will not.
  • Catholic teaching insists this is not a judgement on women's dignity: Mary, the greatest of the saints, was not an apostle.
  • Women deacons: studied by papal commissions (2016, 2020); no change to date.
  • Orthodox: priesthood for men only, following tradition and the icon of Christ; some Orthodox theologians (e.g. Elisabeth Behr-Sigel, 1907–2005) argued the question is open; the historic order of deaconesses is being discussed.

Mission 1

What the debates mean for individuals and communities

  • Individuals: women can now answer a call to priesthood in many churches; others, in Catholic and Orthodox Churches, live with a call they believe cannot be fulfilled. Some opponents left their churches.
  • Communities: parishes choose whether to accept a woman priest or bishop; separate oversight (e.g. The Society) keeps some within the Church of England.
  • Ecumenism: women bishops are an obstacle in Anglican–Catholic dialogue.
  • Equality in society: the Equality Act 2010 allows organised religions an exemption to limit some roles to one sex for doctrinal reasons. Is that freedom of religion or discrimination?

Mission 2

Secular humanism and two kinds of secularism

  • Secular humanism: a non-religious worldview: humans can live good, meaningful lives through reason, evidence and compassion, without belief in God (Humanists UK; the Amsterdam Declaration).
  • Its public claim (OCR): religious belief is personal; the state, schools and law should be neutral, giving no faith special privilege.
  • Secularisation: a process in which religious thinking, practice and institutions lose social significance.
  • Rowan Williams (Archbishop of Canterbury 2002–12) distinguished two secularisms (Rome lecture, 2006):
  • Procedural: the state is neutral and referees fairly between many voices, religious ones included. Many Christians welcome this.
  • Programmatic: religion should be pushed out of public life altogether. Many Christians resist this.
  • Contrast: France's laïcité (1905 law separating churches and state) keeps religion out of state institutions.
Mission 2

Christianity in UK public life today

  • Established church: the Church of England is the established church in England; the monarch is its Supreme Governor; 26 bishops sit in the House of Lords (Lords Spiritual).
  • The Church of Scotland is a national church but self-governing; Wales (1920) and Ireland (1871) have no established church.
  • Schools: RE is compulsory; in England, daily collective worship must be "wholly or mainly of a broadly Christian character" in schools without a religious character.
  • State occasions: coronation oath (2023), Remembrance, prayers before sittings of Parliament.
  • Humanist critique: these privileges are unfair in a diverse, largely non-practising society; they want an end to the Lords Spiritual, compulsory worship and state-funded faith schools.
Mission 2Diversity

Faith schools: valuable or divisive?

  • About a third of state-funded schools in England have a religious character, most of them Church of England or Catholic.
  • For (many Christians and others):
  • Churches founded mass education long before the state (National Society, 1811).
  • Parents' right to education in line with their beliefs (European Convention on Human Rights, Protocol 1, Article 2).
  • A clear ethos of values and community; popular with many families, religious or not.
  • Against (humanists, some Christians):
  • Admissions by faith can select and segregate by religion, and often by class.
  • Risk of indoctrination: children should learn to think for themselves (e.g. Richard Dawkins).
  • Public money should not fund religious bodies.
  • Your spec names "the value of Christian faith schools" as a point of conflicting views.

Mission 2

A "Christian country"?

  • Yes, in some senses: established church; Christian heritage in law, calendar, holidays, art and language; the largest single religious group (2021 Census, England and Wales: 46% Christian).
  • No, in other senses: under half identify as Christian, and far fewer attend church; 37% "no religion"; many faiths are present.
  • Public debate: in 2014 Prime Minister David Cameron wrote that Britain should be "more confident about our status as a Christian country"; over 50 public figures replied in a letter that Britain is a plural, largely non-religious society.
  • The same year, Rowan Williams called Britain "post-Christian": no longer a nation of believers, though Christian in heritage.
  • Meaning matters: heritage? belief? practice? law?

Mission 2

When beliefs conflict with the law

  • Law protects freedom of religion (European Convention, Article 9; Human Rights Act 1998) and bans discrimination on grounds of religion and of sexual orientation (Equality Act 2010). These can clash.
  • Eweida and Others v UK (European Court of Human Rights, 2013): four Christians.
  • Nadia Eweida (British Airways; wearing a cross): won.
  • Shirley Chaplin (nurse; cross, health and safety): lost.
  • Lillian Ladele (registrar; civil partnerships) and Gary McFarlane (counsellor; same-sex couples): lost.
  • Lee v Ashers Baking Co (UK Supreme Court, 2018): a Northern Ireland bakery could not be forced to decorate a cake with a message supporting same-sex marriage; the refusal was about the message, not the customer.
  • Conscientious objection to taking part in abortion is protected in law (Abortion Act 1967), though narrowly.
Mission 2

Perceived challenges to Christianity

  • Decline of role and status: fewer members; less influence on national moral debate.
  • Reduced impact in public life: laws passed despite church opposition (e.g. Sunday trading, 1994; same-sex marriage in England and Wales, 2013).
  • Restricted religious liberty: some groups (e.g. Christian Concern) argue Christians are marginalised at work and in public speech; recent debates include prayer near abortion clinics in "safe access zones" (from 2024).
  • Other Christians and commentators reply: Christianity is still privileged (establishment, bishops, faith schools); most cases show a balance between rights, not persecution; the Church can serve better as a minority voice.
  • Your spec frames these as perceived challenges: both readings should be weighed.

Mission 2Evaluate

Should Christianity play a part in public life?

  • Claim: Christian belief is personal and should play no part in public life.
  • For (secular humanists): a diverse society needs a neutral state; privileges for one church are unfair to others and to non-believers.
  • For: religious claims cannot be tested by public reason, so they should not shape law.
  • Against: Christians are citizens; excluding faith-based reasons is itself a programmatic secularism, not neutrality.
  • Against: Christianity shaped British law, welfare and education, and still provides schools, foodbanks and chaplaincies.
  • Against: freedom of religion includes public expression, not just private belief.
  • Judgement criteria: Procedural or programmatic secularism? What counts as a "public reason"? Heritage, belief or practice?

Mission 3

Four ways to relate science and religion

  • Ian Barbour (1923–2013), Religion in an Age of Science (1990), sorted views into four:
  • Conflict: one must win (Dawkins; also some creationists).
  • Independence: separate questions, no overlap (Stephen Jay Gould, "non-overlapping magisteria", 1997).
  • Dialogue: each raises questions for the other (McGrath).
  • Integration: science and theology form one account of reality (John Polkinghorne, the physicist who became an Anglican priest, leans here).
  • Keep asking: which model does each thinker hold?
Mission 3Read the source

  • Christians who see science as compatible with faith draw on texts like these: studying nature is studying God's work.

Mission 3

Richard Dawkins: conflict

  • Richard Dawkins (b. 1941), evolutionary biologist: The Selfish Gene (1976), The God Delusion (2006).
  • The existence of God is a scientific hypothesis about the universe, testable like any other.
  • Faith is "blind trust, in the absence of evidence, even in the teeth of evidence" (The Selfish Gene, 1976).
  • Natural selection explains design without a designer. A God able to design a universe would be more complex, so even less probable (the "Ultimate Boeing 747" argument, The God Delusion, ch. 4).
  • Science is the best (and only reliable) route to knowledge; religion stops people asking questions.
Mission 3

Alister McGrath: dialogue

  • Alister McGrath (b. 1953): doctorate in molecular biophysics, then theologian at Oxford; once an atheist. Dawkins' God (2004); with Joanna Collicutt McGrath, The Dawkins Delusion? (2007).
  • Dawkins' definition of faith is not the Christian one: for Christians, faith is a reasoned commitment that goes beyond but does not go against evidence.
  • Science's method is neutral about God: it can neither prove nor disprove God. Many scientists are believers.
  • Christianity is an inference to the best explanation: it makes sense of the world as a whole (its order, its intelligibility), as scientific theories do.
  • Science and faith both rely on things that cannot be proved, e.g. that the universe is orderly.
Mission 3Diversity

The nature of proof

  • Proof in maths and logic: certain; conclusion follows necessarily.
  • Evidence in science: theories are well supported, never final; open to revision (an unexpected observation can overturn them).
  • Dawkins: belief should be proportioned to evidence; there is no good evidence for God, so belief in God is irrational.
  • McGrath: no worldview, atheism included, can be proved; all involve faith in something; the question is which best explains the evidence.
  • Agreement: neither claims a mathematical proof. Disagreement: what counts as evidence, and whether religious belief is a hypothesis at all.
Mission 3

Limits of science and the God of the gaps

  • Limits of science: science deals with what can be measured and tested. Questions about purpose, value, meaning, and why anything exists may lie outside it (Peter Medawar, The Limits of Science, 1984, called them "ultimate questions").
  • Dawkins replies: some such questions may be empty; if science can't answer them, religion can't either.
  • God of the gaps: using God to explain what science cannot yet explain; as science advances, God is squeezed out. Example: Newton thought God corrected the planets' orbits; later physics showed this was unnecessary.
  • Christian critics of the idea: Dietrich Bonhoeffer (prison letters, 1944) said God should be found in what we know, not in what we don't know; C. A. Coulson (Science and Christian Belief, 1955) made the phrase well known.
  • Both McGrath and Polkinghorne reject God-of-the-gaps thinking: God is the ground of the whole order, not a stopgap.
  • Dawkins argues that religion historically was a gap-filler, so as science grows, religion shrinks.
  • Spec issue: "The extent to which a scientist must be an atheist."

Must a scientist be an atheist?

  • Claim: A consistent scientist must be an atheist.
  • For (Dawkins): science requires evidence; there is no evidence for God; faith is the opposite of scientific thinking.
  • For: natural selection removes the need for a designer.
  • Against (McGrath): scientific method cannot test God; it is neutral, not atheist.
  • Against: atheism is itself a belief beyond what science can prove.
  • Judgement criteria: What counts as evidence? Is God a scientific hypothesis? Is "many scientists believe" relevant, or only the arguments?

Mission 4

Three answers to one question

  • The question: can people of other faiths, or none, be saved (brought into a right relationship with God, now and for ever)?
  • Christian theology of religions usually gives three answers (Alan Race, Christians and Religious Pluralism, 1983):
  • Exclusivism: salvation only through explicit faith in Christ.
  • Inclusivism: salvation is only through Christ, but Christ's grace can reach people who do not know him.
  • Pluralism: the great religions are different, valid paths to salvation (see Mission 5).
  • Context: the end of the colonial missionary age, the Holocaust and migration made the question urgent for twentieth-century churches.
Mission 4Read the source

Exclusivism in the Hebrew Bible

> "You shall love the LORD your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your might." (Deuteronomy 6:5, WEBBE)

  • Joshua 23:16: if Israel goes to "serve other gods", the LORD's anger will be kindled and they will perish from the land.
  • Both demand exclusive loyalty to one God within the covenant: other gods are not to be served.
  • But both are addressed to Israel; they do not say what happens to other nations. Elsewhere outsiders (Ruth, Naaman, Nineveh in Jonah) are accepted by God.
  • Q: Does Deuteronomy 6:5 teach exclusivism about salvation? (It demands exclusive worship from Israel; whether that implies others cannot be saved is an interpretation.)
Mission 4

Exclusivism developed

  • "No salvation outside the Church" (extra ecclesiam nulla salus): Cyprian (d. 258); defined strongly at the Council of Florence (1442).
  • Karl Barth (1886–1968), Church Dogmatics I/2, §17 (1938): all religion, Christianity included, is human striving and "unbelief"; only God's revelation in Christ saves, by grace. True religion exists only as a sinner is justified.
  • Evangelical exclusivism: the Lausanne Covenant (1974) rejects any dialogue that implies Christ speaks equally through all religions (paraphrase).
  • OCR wording: only Christianity fully offers the means of salvation.
  • Strengths: takes John 14:6 and Acts 4:12 at face value; gives urgency to mission; keeps Christ unique.
  • Problem: what of those who never hear the gospel through no fault of their own (the unevangelised)? Does that fit a God who "desires all people to be saved" (1 Timothy 2:4, WEBBE)?

Mission 4

Strengths and limits

  • Restrictivism (strict exclusivism): only those who hear and believe in this life are saved.
  • Some exclusivists leave the fate of the unevangelised to God's mercy without claiming to know it (agnostic exclusivism).
  • Critics: strict exclusivism seems to make salvation depend on where and when a person is born.
  • Defenders reply: truth is not decided by fairness as we judge it; God's justice is greater than ours; and the remedy is mission, not changing doctrine.

Texts that point wider

> "Truly I perceive that God doesn't show favouritism; but in every nation he who fears him and works righteousness is acceptable to him." (Acts 10:34–35, WEBBE)

> "The true light that enlightens everyone was coming into the world." (John 1:9, WEBBE)

  • Also cited: Acts 17:23 (Paul in Athens: "TO AN UNKNOWN GOD"); Romans 2:14–15 (Gentiles who do "by nature the things of the law").
  • Q: Is Acts 10:34–35 inclusivist or pluralist? (Usually read as inclusivist: Peter goes on to preach Christ to Cornelius, so the acceptance leads to Christ, not past him.)
Mission 4

Rahner: the anonymous Christian

  • Karl Rahner (1904–1984), Jesuit; "Christianity and the Non-Christian Religions" (lecture, 1961; Theological Investigations vol. 5).
  • Every person is created open to God and offered grace: the supernatural existential.
  • Until the gospel truly reaches a person, their religion can be a lawful religion: a means through which Christ's grace works.
  • Someone who responds to that grace in conscience and love is an anonymous Christian: saved through Christ without knowing his name.
  • The Church is the explicit expression of what is hidden elsewhere. Mission still matters: explicit faith is a fuller, clearer form of salvation.
  • Rahner is the named scholar for inclusivism on your spec.
Mission 4

The Catholic Church after Vatican II

  • Lumen Gentium 16 (1964): those who, through no fault of their own, do not know Christ but seek God sincerely and try to do his will "may achieve eternal salvation" (paraphrase).
  • Nostra Aetate 2 (1965): the Church "rejects nothing that is true and holy" in other religions.
  • Dominus Iesus (2000): Christ is the one saviour; followers of other religions are, objectively, in a "gravely deficient situation" compared with those in the Church.
  • So official Catholic teaching is inclusivist, while insisting on the uniqueness of Christ and the Church.
  • Criticisms of "anonymous Christian": patronising (it labels people with a name they reject); it may weaken mission; Hans Küng thought it smuggled others into the Church without asking them.

Mission 4Evaluate

Does the Bible teach exclusivism?

  • Claim: The Christian Bible teaches that only those with explicit faith in Christ can be saved.
  • For: John 14:6 and Acts 4:12 are direct and unqualified; the Great Commission assumes people need the gospel (Matthew 28:19).
  • For: Deuteronomy 6:5 and Joshua 23:16 forbid the worship of other gods.
  • Against: The texts say salvation is through Christ; they do not say it requires knowing him (inclusivist reading).
  • Against: Acts 10:34–35, John 1:9 and 1 Timothy 2:4 (God "desires all people to be saved") point to God's will to save all.
  • Judgement criteria: context and audience of each text; how to treat texts in tension; the character of God; the fate of those who never hear.
  • Spec issue: "the extent to which the Christian Bible promotes exclusivism".

Mission 5

Diversity is a fact; pluralism is a claim

  • Religious diversity (sociological): many faiths live side by side, as in modern Britain. That is a fact.
  • Tolerance: accepting people's right to hold other beliefs, while still thinking one's own beliefs true.
  • Religious pluralism (philosophical, or theological): the claim that the great religions are equally valid routes to the same ultimate reality.
  • A Christian can welcome diversity and practise tolerance without being a pluralist.
  • Your spec asks for this difference: religious pluralism vs tolerance of religious diversity.
Mission 5

Hick's Copernican revolution

  • John Hick (1922–2012), philosopher of religion; an evangelical convert as a student. Working in multi-faith Birmingham from 1967, he met devout Muslims, Sikhs, Hindus and Jews whose lives seemed as holy as Christians'.
  • God and the Universe of Faiths (1973): theology needs a "Copernican revolution".
  • Old picture (Ptolemaic): Christianity at the centre; other faiths circle it.
  • New picture (Copernican): God at the centre; all religions, Christianity included, circle God.
  • Hick calls inclusivism an epicycle: a patch that keeps the old picture going, as astronomers added epicycles to save the earth-centred model.
Mission 5

The Real

  • An Interpretation of Religion (1989; Gifford Lectures 1986–87).
  • The Real in itself (an sich) is beyond all human concepts: neither personal nor impersonal.
  • Borrowing Kant's distinction between things in themselves and things as they appear: we know the Real only as humanly experienced, through a culture's concepts.
  • Personae (the Real experienced as personal): the Holy Trinity, Allah, the LORD of Israel, Vishnu.
  • Impersonae (the Real experienced as impersonal): Brahman, Nirvana, the Tao.
  • Hick uses the old parable of blind men and an elephant: each touches one part and describes the whole wrongly.
Mission 5

Salvation as transformation

  • For Hick, salvation (or liberation) is the transformation of human life from self-centredness to Reality-centredness.
  • Test (soteriological criterion): look at the fruits. Love, compassion and holiness (saints) appear in all the great traditions, as far as we can tell, to a roughly similar degree.
  • So no single religion can claim to be uniquely saving.
  • Doctrines that clash (one life or many; a personal God or not) are, for Hick, human responses to the Real, not literal descriptions of it.

Mission 5

Rethinking the incarnation

  • Pluralism faces a problem: if Jesus is God incarnate, Christianity seems uniquely founded by God.
  • Hick edited The Myth of God Incarnate (1977) and wrote The Metaphor of God Incarnate (1993; 2nd ed. 2005).
  • Incarnation is a metaphor: Jesus was so open to God that God's love was embodied in his life, as it can be in other lives.
  • Jesus did not claim to be God; the doctrine developed later in the Church (Hick's reading).
Mission 5

Criticisms of pluralism

  • It contradicts central Christian beliefs: Incarnation, Trinity, the resurrection and John 14:6 must all be reinterpreted; critics such as Alister McGrath (Christian Theology, ch. 17) say the result is no longer recognisable Christianity.
  • It is a hidden exclusivism: Gavin D'Costa (b. 1958) argues that pluralism judges every religion by its own standard (Hick's Real), so it is one more exclusive position, not a neutral one ("The Impossibility of a Pluralist View of Religions", 1996).
  • The Real is empty: if nothing can be said of the Real, why call it the source of all religions?
  • Religions do not see themselves this way: most believers think their tradition's claims are literally true; pluralism reinterprets them without their consent.
  • Conflicting truths: a personal God and an impersonal Brahman cannot both be literally the ultimate reality.
  • Hick's reply: these are mythological truths about our response, not literal descriptions of the Real.
Mission 5Read the source

Will all be saved?

> "For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive." (1 Corinthians 15:22, WEBBE)

> "and through him to reconcile all things to himself by him" (Colossians 1:20, WEBBE)

  • Christian universalism: in the end all will be saved, through Christ. Origen (c. 185–c. 254) hoped for the restoration of all things (apokatastasis); Barth spoke of all humanity elected in Christ, though he refused to call himself a universalist.
  • Pluralistic universalism: all will be saved, through many paths, with no single saviour. Hick held this (Death and Eternal Life, 1976).
  • OCR issues: Would a loving God deny anyone salvation? Will all good people be saved?
  • Q: Which of the two kinds of universalism does 1 Corinthians 15:22 support, if either? (Christian universalism at most: it says "in Christ".)

Hick and Rahner compared

  • Centre: Rahner, Christ; Hick, the Real.
  • Other religions: Rahner, lawful paths of Christ's grace until the gospel arrives; Hick, valid responses to the Real in their own right.
  • Mission: Rahner, still needed for explicit, fuller faith; Hick, dialogue rather than conversion.
  • Incarnation: Rahner, literal and unique; Hick, metaphor.
  • Each one's critique: Hick, Rahner's view is a patronising epicycle; Rahner's followers, Hick's view gives up the uniqueness of Christ.

Can a committed Christian be a pluralist?

  • Claim: It is possible to be both a committed Christian and a religious pluralist.
  • For: Hick himself remained a Christian (an ordained Presbyterian minister).
  • For: God's love for all (1 Timothy 2:4) suggests many ways; pluralism respects the holiness found in other faiths.
  • Against: Pluralism reinterprets the Incarnation, the Trinity and John 14:6 as metaphor.
  • Against: D'Costa: pluralism is itself an exclusive standpoint.
  • Judgement criteria: What is essential to being Christian? Is a metaphorical incarnation enough? Does the soteriological criterion (Hick's test of changed lives) work? Can there be neutral ground?
  • Spec issue: "the extent to which it is possible to be both a committed Christian and a religious pluralist".

Pluralism claims to stand above the religions; its critics say no one can stand there. That is the heart of the debate.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Church of England: women deacons 1987, priests 1994, bishops 2014–15; a woman Archbishop of Canterbury from 2026; "mutual flourishing" for those who disagree.
  • Against: Christ's choice of twelve men, tradition, sacramental sign, headship. For: equal image of God, Galatians 3:28, women in Jesus' ministry and the early church, vocation.
  • Catholic teaching (Ordinatio Sacerdotalis, 1994): the Church has no authority to ordain women priests; Orthodox likewise; Protestant practice varies.

Mission 2

  • Secular humanists hold that belief is personal and the state, schools and law should be neutral; Rowan Williams distinguishes procedural from programmatic secularism.
  • The UK keeps Christian features (establishment in England, Lords Spiritual, collective worship, faith schools), but under half now identify as Christian.
  • Clashes between belief and law (Eweida, Ashers) and "perceived" challenges are read by some as marginalisation and by others as a fair balance of rights.

Mission 3

  • Polkinghorne: science and theology are cousins seeking truth about one reality; science asks how, theology why; intelligibility and fine-tuning point beyond science.
  • Dawkins treats God as a failed scientific hypothesis; McGrath replies that science is neutral on God and Christian faith is reasoned, not blind.

Mission 4

  • Exclusivism (particularism): salvation only through explicit faith in Christ (John 14:6; Acts 4:12; Barth).
  • Inclusivism: salvation only through Christ, but Christ's grace can reach those who do not know him (Rahner's anonymous Christians; Vatican II).

Mission 5

  • Diversity is a fact; tolerance is an attitude; pluralism is a claim that religions are equally valid routes to the Real.
  • Hick: a Copernican revolution; the Real beyond all concepts; salvation as transformation; the Incarnation as metaphor.
  • Critics: it rewrites central Christian beliefs; it is itself exclusive (D'Costa); the Real is empty.
  • Christian universalism (all saved through Christ) differs from pluralistic universalism (all saved by many paths).
