Coursework: Historical Enquiry

This is the non-examined component. Edexcel Component 4 (9HI0/04): Coursework is worth 20% of the A-level. It is internally marked and externally moderated - not sat as an exam - and it is where you become a working historian.

You produce a single, independent historical enquiry of 3,000-4,000 words on a question that has generated genuine debate. You analyse and evaluate three works by historians, deploy your own knowledge, and reach a supported judgement. This lesson teaches the skills, not a period.

Answer the questions and collect stars. Look for the Interpretations flags - they are the heart of coursework. Press Start.

What the coursework is

An independent enquiry

The coursework asks you to investigate a historical problem, question or issue and reach a critical view based on your own reading:

  • You must engage with three main works by historians whose interpretations together offer a range of views or emphases.
  • You analyse, explain and evaluate those interpretations - and, crucially, explain why historians have arrived at different views.
  • You support your argument with your own knowledge and with primary (contemporary) source material, and you set out your reading in a bibliography with proper citations.

The big idea: coursework is about the nature and purpose of the work of the historian. You are not just learning history - you are studying how history is written and argued.

Choosing a question

A question worth arguing about

A strong enquiry begins with a question that historians genuinely disagree about - one with a real debate to weigh, not a settled matter of fact.

  • Good enquiries sit on a live controversy, for example: Why did the English Civil War break out? Was the New Deal a success? Why did the Weimar Republic collapse? How central was Martin Luther King to the civil rights movement?
  • Each of these has an established historiography - clashing schools whose works you can set against one another.
  • A weak question ('What happened in the New Deal?') only invites description; a strong one ('How far did the New Deal solve the Depression?') demands judgement.

Interpretations - the core skill

Why historians disagree

The heart of the coursework (AO3) is explaining why historians reach different interpretations. It is rarely simple 'bias'. Historians differ because of:

  • The evidence available: new archives can transform a debate - as the opening of Soviet or Chinese records reshaped histories of Russia and Mao's China.
  • The questions they ask: a historian studying elites will see a period differently from one studying ordinary people ('history from below').
  • Method and approach: Marxist, revisionist, social or political frameworks lead to different readings of the same events.
  • The time and place of writing: a historian's own context and values shape the questions and conclusions.

Key move: to evaluate an interpretation you test its argument against the evidence and understand the lens it is written through - not merely label it 'biased'.

Evaluating a work

How to weigh an interpretation

When you evaluate one of your three works, judge it as a piece of argument and evidence:

  • What is its core argument? Summarise the historian's thesis in a sentence.
  • How well does the evidence support it? Test the claim against what you know of the period and against your primary sources.
  • What questions and methods shape it? Recognise the school or approach - and what it illuminates or leaves out.
  • How convincing is it, overall? Reach a judgement, and use the tension between your three works to build your own answer.

Avoid the trap: calling a historian 'biased' explains nothing. Explaining why they argue as they do, and testing it against evidence, is the mark of real historical thinking.

Structuring the essay

Building the argument

A strong enquiry reads as a sustained argument, not a book review of each work in turn:

  • Introduction: set out the question, the debate, and the line you will argue.
  • Main body: work thematically, weaving your three works together, testing each interpretation against the evidence and your primary sources as you go.
  • Judgement: reach a clear, supported conclusion that engages the debate - it should follow from the analysis, not appear from nowhere.
  • Apparatus: reference quotations and citations accurately, and include a full bibliography.
How the coursework is assessed

The assessment objectives

Edexcel Component 4 (9HI0/04): 20% of the A-level, 3,000-4,000 words, internally marked and externally moderated.

  • AO1: deploy accurate, relevant own knowledge of the period to support your argument.
  • AO2: analyse and evaluate primary (contemporary) source material.
  • AO3: analyse and evaluate the interpretations of the three works, and explain why historians differ - the central skill of the coursework.

Note: this is the one component where you choose the question and control the whole enquiry. Careful planning, wide reading and honest evaluation are rewarded far more than length.
