Mission 1: Why are the world's rainforests and hottest deserts found in bands either side of the equator?

Make a guess: Why are the world's rainforests and hottest deserts found in bands either side of the equator?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR GCSE Geography B (J384)

  • 1.1a global circulation; high and low pressure belts creating climatic zones
  • 1.1a how global circulation causes weather extremes
  • 1.1a extremes of wind, temperature and precipitation in contrasting countries
  • 1.1a distribution and frequency of tropical storms and drought, and change over time
Mission 1

Uneven heating

  • The Sun heats the Earth unevenly. Near the equator sunlight is more direct; near the poles it is spread out.
  • This sets up a temperature difference between the equator and the poles.
  • Global atmospheric circulation: the large-scale movement of air that redistributes heat and moisture around the planet.
  • Warm air moves towards the poles; cold air moves towards the equator.
Mission 1

Rising air, sinking air

  • Rising air → low pressure at the surface. It cools, clouds form, rain falls.
  • Sinking air → high pressure at the surface. It warms, clouds clear, the weather is dry.
  • At the surface, wind blows from high pressure towards low pressure.
Mission 1

The three-cell model

  • In each hemisphere, air circulates in three cells in the lowest layer of the atmosphere (up to 10–15 km high):
    • Hadley cell: equator to about 30–40°
    • Ferrel cell: about 30–40° to 60–70°
    • Polar cell: about 60–70° to the pole
  • Together they work like a "global conveyor belt" carrying energy from the tropics to the poles.
Mission 1

The pressure belts

  • Equator: low pressure. Trade winds meet and air rises in the Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ), forming thunderstorms.
  • About 30°: high pressure. Air from the Hadley cell sinks over the subtropics, over oceans and deserts such as the Sahara.
  • About 60°: low pressure. Warm air meets cold polar air at the polar front, "a hotspot for unsettled weather", including over the UK.
  • Poles: high pressure. Cold air sinks at the poles.
Quick check
?Why is the Sahara, at about 20–30°N, so dry?
Mission 1

Surface winds

  • Surface winds blow from the high-pressure belts to the low-pressure belts.
  • Trade winds: blow from about 30° towards the equator.
  • Westerlies: in the Ferrel cell, from the west or south-west in the northern hemisphere. They bring Atlantic weather systems to the UK.
  • Polar easterlies: blow out from the polar highs towards about 60°.
  • Coriolis effect: Earth's rotation deflects moving air to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern hemisphere. So winds do not blow straight north or south.
Mission 1

Climatic zones

  • Equator (low pressure): hot, wet all year → tropical rainforests.
  • About 30° (high pressure): hot, dry → hot deserts.
  • About 60° (low pressure): mild, wet, changeable → e.g. the UK.
  • Poles (high pressure): cold and dry.
  • The circulation "shapes prevailing winds and the distribution of deserts and rainforests around the world".
  • Exam move: link each zone to its pressure belt and say whether air is rising or sinking.
Quick check
?A ship sails from the Tropic of Cancer (about 23°N) towards the equator. Which surface wind is it most likely to meet?
Mission 1

Tropical storms and the circulation

  • Tropical storms form over warm oceans: NOAA gives a sea surface of about 27 °C (80 °F) or more.
  • They need to be at least about 480 km (300 miles) from the equator, where the Coriolis effect is strong enough to make them spin.
  • They occur at about 5°–30° north and south: the zone of the ITCZ, rising air and warm seas.
  • They first move westward with the trade winds, then many turn eastward when they reach the westerlies.
Mission 1

Pressure belts bring hazards

  • Low pressure (ITCZ, polar front): rising air → heavy rain, floods, thunderstorms, tropical storms and mid-latitude storms.
  • In winter the jet stream shifts south over the UK, "increasing the risk of storms and even snow".
  • High pressure (about 30°): sinking air → little rain → drought and heatwaves.
  • Arica, Chile, on the edge of the subtropical high-pressure belt, went 172 months without rain (October 1903 to January 1918), a world record.
Mission 1

Extremes around the world

WMO world records:

  • Hottest: 56.7 °C, Furnace Creek, Death Valley, USA, 10 July 1913.
  • Coldest: −89.2 °C, Vostok, Antarctica, 21 July 1983: in the dark polar winter, under polar high pressure.
  • Wettest 12 months: 26.47 m, Cherrapunji, India, August 1860–July 1861.
  • Strongest gust: 113.2 m/s (253 mph), Barrow Island, Australia, 10 April 1996, during a tropical cyclone.
  • Longest dry spell: 172 months, Arica, Chile: subtropical high pressure.
Quick check
?Which of these world records is best explained by sinking air in the subtropical high-pressure belt?
Mission 1

Where and when tropical storms occur

  • Distribution: about 5°–30° north and south of the equator.
  • Northern hemisphere season: June to November, peaking in September. Southern hemisphere: November to April, with fewer storms.
  • The Atlantic usually has less than 15% of global tropical cyclone activity.
  • Change over time: the IPCC has "low confidence" in long-term trends in the number of tropical cyclones, but says the global proportion of major (Category 3–5) cyclones has "likely" increased over the last four decades.
  • In the north-west Pacific, the latitude where storms reach their peak has "very likely" moved north: a change in distribution.
Mission 1

Drought: where and changing?

  • Distribution: drought is most common under the subtropical high-pressure belts (about 20–30°), where sinking air brings little rain, and where seasonal rains fail.
  • Change over time: the IPCC finds that human-induced climate change "has contributed to increases in agricultural and ecological droughts in some regions".
  • "In some regions": the change is not the same everywhere.
  • Exam move: separate distribution (where) from frequency (how often), and say what the evidence shows about change.
Quick check
?What does the IPCC conclude about tropical storms over recent decades?
Mission check

?The Amazon and Congo rainforests lie close to the equator. The Kalahari and the great Australian deserts lie close to the Tropic of Capricorn. Which explanation fits this pattern best?

Uneven heating drives the three-cell circulation. Air rises at the equator (low pressure, rain, rainforest) and sinks at about 30° (high pressure, dry, desert), so both form bands that circle the Earth.

Mission 2: Hurricane Ian hit Florida with 130-knot winds in 2022. Why was water, not wind, its deadliest hazard?

Specification: what this mission covers

Mission 2

One storm, three names

  • A tropical cyclone is "a rapidly rotating storm that begins over tropical oceans".
  • It is called a hurricane in the Atlantic and north-east Pacific, a typhoon in the north-west Pacific, and a cyclone in the Indian Ocean and South Pacific.
  • Your specification calls them tropical storms.
Mission 2

Where and when

  • They form over tropical oceans, between about 25°S and 25°N.
  • Sources draw the belt slightly differently. NOAA's 25°S–25°N is where storms form; the Met Office gives about 5°–30° north and south for where they occur.
  • They do not form within about 480 km (300 miles) of the equator: there is too little spin there.
  • Main seasons: July–October in the Northern Hemisphere, December–March in the Southern; globally, September is the most active month.
  • These are NOAA's main months. The Met Office gives the full seasons: June to November in the north and November to April in the south.
  • The north-west Pacific has them all year round. Over cool water or land, they weaken.
Mission 2

Linked to global circulation

  • Near the equator, the trade winds of the two Hadley cells meet. Warm air rises: a belt of low pressure and thunderstorms.
  • Tropical storms grow from disturbances in this rising air over warm oceans: the source areas.
  • Trade winds then steer most storms westwards; many later curve towards the poles.
Quick check
?Why do tropical storms almost never form right on the equator, even where the sea is warm?
Mission 2

The ingredients

  • Warm ocean: about 27 °C (80 °F) at the surface, and warm down to about 46 m (150 feet).
  • Moist air high up, and air that cools quickly with height.
  • Low wind shear: winds at different heights must be similar, so the storm is not torn apart.
  • Spin from the Coriolis force, so at least about 480 km (300 miles) from the equator.
  • A pre-existing disturbance: an area of low pressure with thunderstorms. Even with all of these, many disturbances never develop.
  • Your specification quotes 26.5 °C. 80 °F is about 26.7 °C, so the figures agree.
Mission 2

How one forms: the sequence

  • 1. Warm sea heats the air above it, and lots of water evaporates.
  • 2. Moist air rises, cools and condenses into huge clouds. Condensation releases latent heat, which warms the air so it rises faster.
  • 3. Air pressure at the surface falls; more air rushes in to replace the rising air.
  • 4. The Coriolis force makes the in-rushing air spiral; thunderstorms organise into a rotating storm.
  • 5. Over land or cold water, the supply of warm, moist air is cut off and the storm weakens.
Mission 2

Structure

  • Eye: the calm centre, with the lowest pressure; air sinks, so skies are often clear.
  • Eyewall: a ring of towering clouds round the eye, with the strongest winds and heaviest rain.
  • Rain bands: spiral bands of cloud and rain further out.
  • Air spirals in near the surface, rises in the eyewall and flows out at the top.
Quick check
?Where does a tropical storm get most of the energy that keeps it going?
Mission 2

Hurricane Ian, 2022: Florida

  • Ian intensified rapidly over water of 30 °C and above, with low wind shear (winds at different heights were similar).
  • 28 September: it peaked at 140 kt (category 5), then hit Cayo Costa, Florida, at 130 kt (category 4).
  • Storm surge (sea water piled up by onshore winds) flooded Fort Myers Beach to 3–4.6 m (10–15 feet) above ground.
  • In Lee County, at least 52,514 buildings were damaged, 5,369 of them destroyed.
  • At least 156 people died in the US, 66 directly from the storm: surge killed 41, freshwater flooding 12 and wind 4.
Quick check
?Of Ian's 66 direct deaths in the US, 41 were caused by storm surge. What percentage is that, to the nearest whole number?
Mission check

?A category 3 hurricane is heading for a low-lying coast with a wide, gently sloping sea floor and a bay that narrows inland. Which hazard is likely to kill most people?

Ian's onshore winds drove a storm surge 3–4.6 m (10–15 feet) above ground at Fort Myers Beach. Surge caused 41 of the 66 direct deaths in the US (62%); wind caused 4.

Specification: what this mission covers

Mission 3

Normal conditions in the Pacific

  • Trade winds blow west along the equator, pushing warm surface water from South America towards Asia.
  • Cold water rises from the deep off South America to replace it: upwelling.
  • Warm, moist air rises over the warm west and sinks over the cool east. This loop is the Walker circulation.
  • Rising air brings rain to Indonesia and northern Australia; sinking air keeps coastal Peru dry.
Mission 3

El Niño

  • The central and eastern tropical Pacific becomes warmer than average.
  • The trade winds weaken, or even reverse.
  • The warm water, rising air and rain shift east, into the central Pacific.
  • Over Indonesia, rainfall "tends to become reduced"; off South America, upwelling weakens or stops.
Mission 3

La Niña

  • The opposite: the central and eastern Pacific becomes cooler than average.
  • Trade winds blow more strongly, and upwelling strengthens.
  • Rain increases over Indonesia.
  • Ocean and air change together: El Niño and La Niña are two phases of the El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO).
Quick check
?Trade winds over the Pacific have weakened, and the sea off Peru is much warmer than usual. Which phase is this?
Mission 3

How often, and how it is measured

  • El Niño and La Niña "typically occur every 3–5 years", but the gap has ranged from 2 to 7 years.
  • Episodes typically last nine to 12 months.
  • NOAA's Oceanic Niño Index averages sea temperature in the east-central tropical Pacific over three months: +0.5 °C or more above normal means El Niño; −0.5 °C or less means La Niña.
Mission 3

Why drought follows

  • Rain falls where air rises, cools and condenses. Where air sinks, it warms and dries: high pressure, clear skies, little rain.
  • When ENSO moves the rising part of the Walker circulation, some places lose their usual rising air.
  • Weeks or months of sinking air bring drought: low rainfall, dry soils, low rivers and reservoirs.
  • Exam move: link the chain: sea temperature → winds → where air rises or sinks → rainfall → drought.
Mission 3

El Niño 2023–24: the Panama Canal

  • The canal's locks are filled from lakes that also supply Panama's people.
  • October 2023 was the driest October on record in the canal's watershed, and 2023 its second-driest year.
  • The Canal Authority cut transits to 22 a day, against 36 in its "regular scenario", citing "the drought induced by the El Niño phenomenon".
  • WMO: this El Niño peaked in December 2023 "as one of five strongest on record".
Quick check
?Daily transits fell from 36 to 22. What percentage cut is that, to the nearest whole number?
Mission 3

La Niña 2020–23: the Horn of Africa

  • The tropical Pacific was in a La Niña state, with short breaks, from September 2020: the first "triple-dip" La Niña of this century.
  • Southern Ethiopia, southern Somalia and eastern Kenya had five failed rainy seasons in a row from October 2020.
  • Scientists found "a high correlation between below-average short rains and La Niña".
  • They also found that climate change had made such droughts about 100 times more likely, mainly by raising evaporation.
Quick check
?Which statement about the Horn of Africa drought is best supported?
Mission check

?In an El Niño year, farmers in Indonesia face drought. Which chain explains it?

El Niño warms the eastern Pacific and shifts the tropical rain belts. Some places, such as Indonesia and Panama, get much less rain than usual: in 2023 Panama's canal lakes ran low, and transits were cut from 36 to 22 a day.

Mission 4

Coverack, 18 July 2017

  • A coastal village on the east side of the Lizard peninsula, Cornwall.
  • Three small streams drain the land above it and flow through the village to the sea.
  • A flash flood: a flood that arrives within hours of heavy rain, with little warning.
  • This is your UK flash flood. Your non-UK heat wave is the Pacific Northwest heatwave, June 2021.
Mission 4

The extreme weather

  • A thunderstorm with hail "as large as fifty pence pieces".
  • 165–201 mm of rain in 3 hours: three times July's monthly average.
  • The upper value, 201 mm, broke the UK record for the highest three-hour total, according to Met Office records.
  • The storm "centred and stalled over the catchment". An EA rain gauge just 8 km away recorded no rain.
Mission 4

Why it flooded so fast: place-specific causes

  • Small, steep catchment: the South Coverack Stream drains just 2.2 km²; the land is steep with hard rock.
  • Soils: wet, slowly permeable; topsoils "quickly become saturated", and dry peaty soils can repel water.
  • So little rain soaked in: rapid overland flow into the streams.
  • Debris: wood, boulders and household materials blocked channels and culverts.
  • Buildings stand close to the stream banks.
  • Result: a "wall of water" down the main road 1.5–2 hours after the rain began.
Quick check
?Which combination best explains why Coverack flooded within two hours?
Mission 4

Consequences

  • Social: no lives lost; one family was rescued by coastguard helicopter. 44 properties flooded inside in Coverack, 10 in St Keverne, 1 in Porthallow; some to 1.5–2 m deep.
  • Economic: the B3294 road, car park and public toilets were badly damaged; 300 m of road had to be completely rebuilt. Homes and businesses face repair costs.
  • Environmental: erosion of land and river channels; pollution from damaged sewers and domestic oil; a landslip closed the South West Coast Path. The stream doubled in width in places.
Mission 4

Responses: the first weeks

  • A major incident was declared; the coastguard helicopter rescued people trapped on a roof.
  • The Environment Agency cleared a blocked culvert and removed about 100 tonnes of silt from the river bed and 50 tonnes of debris from the beach.
  • Cornwall Council rebuilt the road; it reopened on 24 July 2017.
  • South West Water repaired a sewer burst open by boulders.
Mission 4

Responses: reducing future risk

  • No flood scheme: "no flood alleviation scheme that could be reasonably practicable".
  • Warnings are hard: the local flood alert was triggered by the River Fal, so Coverack "would be unlikely to receive a timely warning".
  • Plans and people: help to write a Community Emergency Plan, plus flood warden training.
  • Better forecasting: use rainfall radar forecasts; review the national list of Rapid Response Catchments.
  • Land: promote natural flood management and good soil care, though it "would not have prevented" this flood.
Quick check
?The investigation found no flood defence scheme was practicable for Coverack. Which response best reduces the risk to life in a future flash flood?
Mission check

?Two villages get the same 100 mm of rain in three hours. Village P sits where a 2 km² steep valley meets the sea. Village Q sits beside a large, gently sloping river basin with deep, dry, sandy soils. Which village floods faster, and why?

Coverack flooded so fast because a stalled thunderstorm dropped 165–201 mm on a 2.2 km² steep catchment whose soils could not soak it up. A "wall of water" reached the village in under two hours.

Mission 5

Heatwaves are changing

  • IPCC: it is "virtually certain" that heatwaves have become more frequent and more intense over most land regions since the 1950s.
  • A heat extreme that used to come once in 10 years now comes 2.8 times per decade.
  • At 2 °C of warming it is projected to come 5.6 times per decade.
Mission 5

Case study: the Pacific Northwest heatwave, June 2021

  • Where: western Canada (British Columbia) and the north-west USA.
  • Cause: "a slow-moving strong high pressure system", sometimes called a heat dome, which reached record strength.
  • Sinking air and winds blowing down the mountainsides heated the air further.
  • In British Columbia temperatures rose from 24 June to a peak on 28–29 June, passing 40 °C in many places.
  • Lytton set Canada's all-time record: 49.6 °C.
Mission 5

Consequences

  • Deaths: 619 deaths in British Columbia between 25 June and 1 July were identified as heat-related.
  • 98% died indoors, 67% were aged 70 or over, and 56% lived alone. Most were in homes without adequate cooling.
  • Services: 911 emergency calls doubled at the peak; some callers waited or were told no ambulance was available.
  • Property and environment: shortly after its record, Lytton was largely destroyed in a wildfire.
Quick check
?619 deaths in British Columbia were heat-related, and 67% of those who died were aged 70 or over. About how many people aged 70 or over died?
Mission 5

Responses

  • Short term: heat alerts were issued, but there was a lag before people responded.
  • Long term: a coroners' review panel recommended:
    • a co-ordinated provincial heat alert and response system
    • identifying and supporting the people most at risk
    • prevention and long-term risk reduction.
  • Exam move: for a case study, give the place-specific cause (the heat dome), then consequences, then responses, each with a figure.
Mission 5

Could it happen without climate change?

  • Attribution science compares how likely an event is today with a modelled world without human-caused warming.
  • World Weather Attribution: the heatwave was "virtually impossible without human-caused climate change".
  • An event this rare would have been at least 150 times rarer without it, and the heat was about 2 °C hotter.
  • The heat dome itself is weather. Climate change made its heat more extreme.
Mission check

?A city has its hottest day ever, during a stalled high-pressure system. Which statement could an attribution study support?

For Lytton, World Weather Attribution found the heat "virtually impossible without human-caused climate change": about 2 °C hotter, and at least 150 times more likely. The heat dome was weather; warming made it deadlier.

Mission 6

Inside the Earth

  • Crust: thin, solid, rigid rock. About 5 km thick under oceans; about 30 km on average under continents.
  • Mantle: hot, dense rock, about 2,900 km thick. It is solid, but over millions of years it can flow very slowly.
  • Outer core: liquid, about 2,200 km thick.
  • Inner core: solid, about 1,250 km thick.
Mission 6

Plates

  • The crust and the top of the mantle form a rigid layer: the lithosphere, at least 80 km thick over much of Earth.
  • The lithosphere is broken into tectonic plates. They move slowly over a weaker, mobile layer of the mantle, the asthenosphere.
  • Main plates: Pacific, North American, South American, Eurasian, African, Indo-Australian, Antarctic, and the smaller Nazca plate.
  • Plate tectonics theory: the moving plates explain where earthquakes, volcanoes and mountains are found.
Quick check
?Which layer of the Earth is liquid?
Mission 6

Heat and convection currents

  • Heat inside Earth comes from radioactive decay and heat left over from Earth's formation.
  • Hot, less dense mantle rock rises; cooler, denser rock sinks. This slow loop is a convection current.
  • Where currents spread out under the plates, they may help move plates apart; where they sink, plates move together. Scientists think gravity at the plates' edges also matters a lot.
  • Convection takes millions of years: plates move only centimetres a year.
Mission 6

Ridge push and slab pull

  • Newer research adds two forces driven by gravity.
  • Ridge push: new plate at a mid-ocean ridge is hot and stands high, so it slides downhill away from the ridge.
  • Slab pull: at a subduction zone, the cold, dense edge of an oceanic plate sinks and drags the rest of the plate behind it.
  • Plates attached to sinking slabs, such as the Pacific and Nazca plates, move fastest. So many scientists think slab pull is the strongest force.
  • No single mechanism explains all plate movement.
Quick check
?Plates with a sinking edge at a subduction zone move fastest. Which force does this support?
Mission 6

Constructive margins

  • Plates move apart (divergence). Magma rises from the mantle to fill the gap and forms new crust.
  • Under the ocean this builds a mid-ocean ridge. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge spreads about 2.5 cm a year.
  • On land, stretching crust cracks and sinks into a rift valley, as in the East African Rift.
  • Earthquakes: shallow and usually small. Volcanoes: runny lava and fairly gentle eruptions. Iceland sits on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge.
  • Your specification also calls this a divergent boundary.
Mission 6

Destructive margins

  • Plates move together (convergence). The denser oceanic plate sinks under the lighter continental plate: subduction.
  • Where it bends down, an ocean trench forms: the deepest parts of the ocean floor.
  • Example: the Nazca Plate sinks under South America, pushing up the Andes.
  • Friction as the plate sinks causes powerful earthquakes. Water released from the sinking plate makes the mantle above it melt; this magma rises to form explosive composite volcanoes.
Mission 6

Collision margins

  • Two continental plates move together. Continental rock is light, so neither plate sinks.
  • The crust crumples and folds upwards: fold mountains.
  • Example: India collided with Asia about 50 million years ago, building the Himalayas.
  • Earthquakes: frequent and can be large. Volcanoes: few or none, because no plate sinks into the mantle, so no magma forms.
Mission 6

Conservative margins

  • Plates slide past each other. Crust is neither made nor destroyed.
  • The plates lock together. Stress builds up, then is released suddenly as an earthquake.
  • Example: the San Andreas Fault, California: the Pacific Plate moves past the North American Plate at about 5 cm a year.
  • Landform: a fault line. No volcanoes: no magma is produced.
Quick check
?An oceanic plate sinks beneath a continental plate. Which landform forms where it starts to sink?
Mission 6

Hotspots

  • A hotspot is fed by a mantle plume: a column of very hot rock rising under a plate.
  • Hawaii is more than 3,200 km from the nearest plate boundary.
  • The plate moves over the hotspot, so older islands are carried away: Big Island rocks are under 0.7 million years old; Kauai's are about 5.5 million.
  • Hawaii's volcanoes, such as Mauna Loa, are shield volcanoes built from runny lava.
Mission 6

Where earthquakes and volcanoes are

  • They form narrow belts along plate margins, not an even spread.
  • About 81% of the world's largest earthquakes happen around the Pacific (the Ring of Fire), and about 17% in a belt from Java through the Himalaya to the Mediterranean.
  • About 1,350 potentially active volcanoes, many around the Pacific Ring of Fire.
  • Exceptions: hotspots such as Hawaii, far from any margin.
Quick check
?Which statement best describes where earthquakes and volcanoes occur?
Mission check

?The Nazca Plate moves east and sinks beneath South America. What moves it?

The same forces widen the Atlantic. Internal heat drives convection currents; gravity adds slab pull (sinking edges) and ridge push (sliding off ridges).

Mission 7

Mission 7: A giant earthquake was felt from Tokyo to Moscow. Why can a quake that big do less damage than others of the same size?

Make a guess: A giant earthquake was felt from Tokyo to Moscow. Why can a quake that big do less damage than others of the same size?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR GCSE Geography B (J384)

  • 1.2a how plate movement causes earthquakes (shallow and deep focus)
Mission 7

Why earthquakes happen

  • Plates move a few centimetres a year. Where they meet, they lock together.
  • Stress builds up in the rock, then is released suddenly as the rock breaks along a fault.
  • The energy spreads out from the break as seismic waves: the shaking we feel.
Mission 7

Earthquakes at each type of margin

  • Constructive: plates pull apart. Earthquakes are shallow and mostly small; Iceland's have reached at least magnitude 6.9.
  • Destructive: one plate sinks. Where the plates lock near the surface, friction causes the largest earthquakes. Deeper earthquakes happen inside the sinking plate, down to about 700 km.
  • Collision: continents crush together. Shallow, sometimes very large: Nepal 2015, magnitude 7.8, about 8 km deep.
  • Conservative: plates slide past each other. Shallow earthquakes, such as on the San Andreas Fault.
Mission 7

Where earthquakes happen

  • Earthquakes form narrow belts along plate margins.
  • About 81% of the world's largest earthquakes happen round the Pacific, the Ring of Fire.
  • About 17% happen in a belt from Java through the Himalaya to the Mediterranean.
  • Few happen in the middle of plates.
Mission 7

Focus and epicentre

  • Focus (hypocentre): the point underground where the rock first breaks.
  • Epicentre: the point on the surface directly above the focus.
  • Shaking is usually strongest near the epicentre and weakens with distance from it.
Quick check
?What is the epicentre of an earthquake?
Mission 7

Shallow or deep focus?

  • Shallow-focus earthquakes: 0–70 km deep. Deep-focus: 300–700 km deep.
  • Every earthquake deeper than 70 km happens inside a plate sinking into the mantle. So deep-focus earthquakes occur only at destructive margins.
  • Constructive, collision and conservative margins have shallow earthquakes.
  • Deep-focus earthquakes cause less damage at the surface than shallow ones of the same magnitude: the waves weaken on their long journey up.
  • Example: the Sea of Okhotsk earthquake on 24 May 2013 (magnitude 8.3) started about 600 km down. It was felt all over Asia, as far away as Moscow, and in Tokyo.
  • Exam move: link shallow and deep focus to the boundary type.
Quick check
?At which type of plate margin can an earthquake start 500 km below the surface?
Mission check

?In 1994 a magnitude 8.2 earthquake started about 630 km below Bolivia, inside the Nazca Plate. Why would it do less damage at the surface than a magnitude 8.2 earthquake 20 km down?

The Sea of Okhotsk earthquake (2013, magnitude 8.3) started about 600 km down, inside the sinking Pacific Plate. It was felt across Asia, but its deep focus meant the waves were much weaker by the time they reached the surface.

Mission 8

Mission 8: In 1985 a volcano 45 km away killed about 23,000 people in Armero, two hours after it erupted. How?

Make a guess: In 1985 a volcano 45 km away killed about 23,000 people in Armero, two hours after it erupted. How?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR GCSE Geography B (J384)

  • 1.2a how plate movement causes volcanoes (shield and composite)
Mission 8

Runny or sticky magma

  • Viscosity = how much magma resists flowing.
  • Silica affects viscosity most. Most volcanic rocks are about 45–78% silica.
  • Low-silica (runny) lava flows easily: at Kīlauea, Hawaii, flow fronts can move up to 10 km/h.
  • High-silica (sticky) magma traps gas, so eruptions tend to be explosive.
Mission 8

Shield volcanoes

  • Built almost entirely from runny lava flows that spread a long way.
  • Shape: a broad cone with gentle slopes, like a warrior's shield.
  • Eruptions are mostly gentle (effusive): lava pours out rather than exploding.
  • Mauna Loa, Hawaii, is the largest: about 4,170 m (13,677 ft) above sea level.
  • Where: constructive margins (Iceland) and hotspots (Hawaii), where runny, low-silica magma rises from the mantle.
Mission 8

Composite volcanoes

  • Steep-sided cones built from alternating layers of lava and ash.
  • They can rise up to about 2,400 m (8,000 ft) above their base.
  • Sticky magma makes many eruptions explosive.
  • Where: destructive margins. Water released from the sinking oceanic plate makes the mantle above it melt; the magma that rises through the crust is sticky and gas-rich.
  • Examples: Nevado del Ruiz (Colombia) and Fuego (Guatemala).
Quick check
?A volcano has broad, gentle slopes built from thousands of thin lava flows. Which type is it?
Mission 8

Hazards: pyroclastic flows and lava flows

  • Pyroclastic flows: avalanches of hot rock fragments and gas. They usually travel at more than 80 km/h, at 200–700 °C.
  • They knock down, burn or bury almost everything in their path.
  • Lava flows: runny lava can move up to 10 km/h. Sticky lava moves far more slowly. Lava flows mainly destroy buildings, roads and farmland.
Mission 8

Hazards: lahars

  • A lahar is a volcanic mudflow: ash and rock mixed with water, like wet concrete.
  • The water comes from melting snow and ice, heavy rain, or lakes and rivers.
  • Lahars follow river valleys. They can grow to more than 10 times their first size and exceed 200 km/h on steep slopes.
Quick check
?What makes a lahar different from a lava flow?
Mission 8

Armero, 1985

  • Nevado del Ruiz, Colombia: a composite volcano 5,389 m high, topped by glaciers.
  • On 13 November 1985 an eruption melted ice. Lahars raced down the river valleys.
  • Armero, 45 km away and built on old lahar deposits, was hit about two hours after the eruption. About 23,000 people died.
  • Scientists lacked equipment to monitor the volcano, and residents had been told they were safe for the night.
Mission check

?Volcano A is a broad shield of runny lava in a warm climate. Volcano B is a steep, ice-capped composite volcano. A town lies 40 km down a river valley from each. Which town faces the greater danger, and why?

At Armero, an explosive eruption of an ice-capped composite volcano melted ice. Lahars, a secondary hazard set off by the eruption, followed the valleys 45 km to the town in about two hours.

Mission 9

Mission 9: Nepal 2015 and Kaikōura 2016 were both magnitude 7.8. One killed more than 8,790 people; the other killed 2. Why?

Make a guess: Nepal 2015 and Kaikōura 2016 were both magnitude 7.8. One killed more than 8,790 people; the other killed 2. Why?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR GCSE Geography B (J384)

  • 1.2b case study of a hazardous tectonic event: causes, consequences, responses
Mission 9

Two earthquakes, two levels of wealth

  • In 2015, income per person (GNI per capita) was US$870 in Nepal and US$40,660 in New Zealand.
  • That is about 47 times as much in New Zealand.
  • In OCR's terms: Nepal is a low-income developing country (LIDC); New Zealand is an advanced country (AC).
Mission 9

Nepal, 25 April 2015: cause

  • Magnitude 7.8, on Saturday 25 April at 11:56 local time.
  • Plates: the Indian Plate moves north into the Eurasian Plate at about 45 mm a year, pushing up the Himalaya.
  • The rock broke along a thrust fault about 8 km deep: a shallow focus.
  • Epicentre near Barpak, Gorkha district, about 76 km north-west of Kathmandu. The break spread east, towards Kathmandu.
Mission 9

Nepal: primary effects

  • Over 8,790 deaths and 22,300 injuries by June 2015.
  • Over half a million houses destroyed.
  • Nearly 7,000 schools completely or badly damaged.
  • The lives of about 8 million people, almost a third of Nepal's population, were affected.
Mission 9

Nepal: secondary effects

  • At least 25,000 landslides on steep Himalayan slopes; an avalanche killed 20 people at Everest Base Camp.
  • Road damage cut off rural villages.
  • Damaged slopes became more likely to slide or flood in the monsoon.
  • Economic: about US$7 billion of damage and losses, about one-third of Nepal's GDP. At least 700,000 more people expected to fall into poverty.
Quick check
?Which of these was a secondary effect of the Nepal earthquake?
Mission 9

Nepal: immediate responses

  • The government asked for international help within hours.
  • Search and rescue: 134 international teams from 34 countries. Helicopters made 4,236 flights; 7,558 people were rescued by air and 4,689 by land.
  • Shelter: transit shelters were set up in Kathmandu, but there were not enough tarpaulins.
  • Maps: over 6,000 volunteers added data to OpenStreetMap, used by Nepal's army and the Red Cross.
Mission 9

Nepal: long-term responses

  • Over 700,000 earthquake-resistant houses were built under the government's programme.
  • Families got grants of NPR 300,000 (about US$3,000) in three parts. Later parts were paid only after engineers inspected the building.
  • Masons and engineers were trained. More than 18,000 vulnerable people who had not finished rebuilding were found and given extra help.
Mission 9

Kaikōura, New Zealand, 14 November 2016

  • Magnitude 7.8 at 12:02 a.m. on Monday 14 November; focus about 15 km deep.
  • Plates: the Pacific and Australian plates, moving about 40 mm a year relative to each other. More than 20 faults broke, a world record.
  • Effects: 2 deaths. Over 10,000 landslides; the coast rose by up to 6.5 m; a tsunami ran 6.9 m up the shore at Goose Bay.
  • Landslides cut road and rail links, stranding many visitors in Kaikōura.
Mission 9

Kaikōura: responses

  • Immediate: road access to Kaikōura was restored before Christmas 2016.
  • Long-term: the railway reopened in September 2017 and the main coast road (State Highway 1) in December 2017.
  • About 40,000 insurance claims went to the state Earthquake Commission, which pays for natural-hazard damage.

Mission check

?Nepal wants fewer deaths when the next large earthquake strikes. Which action targets the main cause of the 2015 death toll?

The hazard was similar (magnitude 7.8, shallow). Vulnerability was not: Nepal's poorer, rural communities lived in houses with no earthquake-resistant features, while New Zealand builds to a seismic code and insures homes. Wealth shaped both the effects and the responses.

Mission 10

Mission 10: About 800 million people live near active volcanoes. Why don't they move away?

Make a guess: About 800 million people live near active volcanoes. Why don't they move away?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR GCSE Geography B (J384)

  • 1.2c how technology (building design, prediction, early warning) can reduce the effects of a tectonic hazard
Mission 10

Why people stay

  • About 800 million people live within 100 km of an active volcano.
  • Fertile soil: volcanic ash weathers into rich farmland.
  • Energy: about 90% of the energy for home heating in Iceland comes from geothermal sources.
  • Jobs: tourism, mining and quarrying.
  • Home: family, friends and work are there; moving costs money that many people do not have.
  • Rare events: big eruptions and earthquakes may not happen in a lifetime, and the risk can be reduced.
Quick check
?A family grows coffee on the rich soil of a volcano's lower slopes. Which reason for staying does this show?
Mission 10

Monitoring

  • Monitoring means watching for signs of change.
  • Volcanoes: GPS stations measure the ground's position every day, usually to better than 1 cm. Rising ground shows magma moving in. At Svartsengi, Iceland, the ground rose 35 cm between November and mid-December 2023.
  • Seismometers pick up the small earthquakes made by rising magma and show where they are.
  • Gas measurements can show fresh magma near the surface.
  • Tsunamis: DART stations, a pressure sensor on the seafloor linked to a surface buoy, detect a passing tsunami early and relay the data by satellite to warning centres.
Mission 10

Prediction

  • Volcanoes can sometimes be forecast. In 1991, scientists' warnings before Mount Pinatubo (Philippines) erupted saved at least 5,000 lives.
  • Where monitoring is missing, warnings fail: at Armero, Colombia, in 1985, scientists lacked the equipment to monitor the volcano.
  • Earthquakes cannot be predicted: no scientist has ever predicted a major earthquake.
Mission 10

Early warning technology

  • Earthquake early warning detects the fast, weaker P waves and sends alerts seconds to minutes before the strong shaking arrives.
  • Japan built a system after the 1995 Kobe earthquake. In 2011, no trains derailed.
  • Android phones now act as a network: alerts have been sent in 98 countries.
  • It is a warning, not a prediction: the earthquake has already started.
Quick check
?Why is an earthquake early-warning alert not a prediction?
Mission 10

Protection: buildings that resist shaking

  • Cross-bracing (steel braces) and shear walls stiffen a building against sideways shaking.
  • Base isolation: rubber or sliding bearings between the building and its foundations, so the ground moves more than the building.
  • In Mashiki, Japan, in 2016, wooden houses built before 1981 collapsed far more often than newer ones, so retrofitting older buildings matters.
  • New Zealand's Building Code requires buildings to withstand earthquakes.
Mission 10

Protection: sea walls and lava barriers

  • Tsunami walls: Japan, 2011: the tsunami overtopped sea walls mostly 5–10 m high, and more than 15,000 people died.
  • Japan now plans walls for frequent tsunamis, and evacuation for the largest ones.
  • Lava barriers: earth-and-rock walls diverted lava from Grindavík, Iceland. But in January 2024 a crack opened outside the barrier and lava burned three homes.
Quick check
?What does base isolation do?
Mission 10

Planning

  • Hazard mapping: maps show where shaking, lava, lahars or tsunamis are likely. Risk assessment combines them with where people live.
  • Land-use planning: California bans homes and offices over active faults; they must usually be set back 15 m (50 ft).
  • Emergency plans and drills: in 2015, more than 40 million people in 60 countries registered for "ShakeOut" drills: Drop, Cover and Hold On.
  • Evacuation: Japan's plans for the largest tsunamis centre on evacuation and hazard maps.
Mission 10

Is it worth it? An evaluation

  • For: a US study found that adopting model building codes saves about $11 for every $1 spent, and mitigation grants about $6.
  • Limits: defences can be beaten by rare, extreme events (Japan's walls, 2011). Early warning gives only seconds to minutes. Earthquakes cannot be predicted.
  • Cost: strong buildings and monitoring cost money, so poorer countries can do less; low-income countries suffer the most deaths per disaster.
  • Exam move: weigh each method's cost, who it protects, and what it cannot do.
Mission check

?Many cities, such as Tokyo and San Francisco, stand in earthquake zones. Which answer best explains why millions of people stay?

People stay near volcanoes and faults because of benefits (soil, energy, jobs, home) and because the risk can be cut by monitoring, prediction (for volcanoes), protection and planning. None of these removes the risk completely.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Uneven heating → three cells (Hadley, Ferrel, Polar) → belts of low pressure (equator, about 60°) and high pressure (about 30°, poles) → surface winds (trade winds, westerlies) bent by the Coriolis effect.
  • Low pressure brings rain, storms and floods; high pressure brings drought and heatwaves.
  • World extremes and the distribution of tropical storms and drought follow the same pattern.

Mission 2

  • Tropical storms form over oceans warmer than about 27 °C, away from the equator, where low pressure, low wind shear and Coriolis spin let thunderstorms organise. Latent heat drives them.
  • On low coasts, storm surge is usually the deadliest hazard: in Ian, 41 of 66 direct US deaths.

Mission 3

  • Normally, trade winds keep warm water and rising air in the west Pacific. El Niño weakens them and shifts rain east; La Niña strengthens them.
  • Drought follows where the shift leaves sinking, dry air: Panama in 2023 (El Niño), the Horn of Africa in 2020–23 (La Niña).

Mission 4

  • Coverack, 18 July 2017: 165–201 mm in 3 hours from a stalled storm; small, steep catchment, saturated soils and debris; 44 homes flooded in the village, road destroyed, pollution and landslip; no lives lost.
  • Responses: rescue and clean-up; no scheme practicable, so community plans, flood wardens and better radar forecasting.

Mission 5

  • Sinking air in high pressure brings clear skies and little rain: heatwaves and droughts.
  • Pacific Northwest, June 2021: a heat dome; 619 deaths in British Columbia. Warming made it about 2 °C hotter. A heat alert and response system was recommended.

Mission 6

  • Heat from inside Earth drives convection in the mantle; gravity adds slab pull and ridge push. Together they move the plates.
  • Plates move apart (constructive) or together (destructive, collision).
  • Plates also slide past each other at conservative margins.
  • Each margin has its own landforms, earthquakes and volcanoes.

Mission 7

  • Earthquakes happen where locked plates suddenly slip, in narrow belts along plate margins.
  • The focus is underground; the epicentre is above it. Shallow-focus earthquakes (0–70 km) do the most damage; deep-focus ones happen only in sinking plates at destructive margins.

Mission 8

  • Runny, low-silica lava builds gentle shield volcanoes; sticky, gas-rich magma builds steep, explosive composite volcanoes.
  • Hazards differ in speed and spread: pyroclastic flows are fast near the volcano; lahars follow valleys far from it.

Mission 9

  • Primary effects come from the shaking; secondary effects follow (landslides, tsunami, poverty).
  • Immediate responses save lives (rescue, shelter); long-term responses rebuild. Wealth and building standards explain why two magnitude 7.8 earthquakes had such different outcomes.

Mission 10

  • People live with tectonic hazards for economic and social reasons, and because events are rare.
  • Monitoring, prediction, protection (building design, defences) and planning (maps, zoning, drills) reduce risk.
