Mission 1

Mission 1: Benzene is drawn with three double bonds. So why doesn't it react like an alkene?

Make a guess: Benzene is drawn with three double bonds. So why doesn't it react like an alkene?

Mission 1

Three ideas from the video

  • σ bond: head-on overlap of orbitals; density on the line between the nuclei.
  • π bond: sideways overlap of p orbitals; density above and below that line. C=C = σ + π.
  • Beyond your course, as background: a ring carbon with three σ bonds at 120° is called sp². It keeps one p orbital for π bonding. Each H bonds with its 1s orbital.
  • Background: resonance structures are alternative drawings (linked by ↔) of one real molecule. It never flips between them.
  • You won't be asked to use the last two terms. The delocalised model below says the same thing.
Mission 1

Kekulé's model

  • Kekulé (1865): a ring of six carbons, alternating C–C and C=C bonds.
  • It predicts two bond lengths: short C=C and long C–C.
  • It predicts alkene reactions: rapid addition of bromine.
  • It predicts 3 × the hydrogenation enthalpy of one C=C.
  • All three predictions fail.
Mission 1

The delocalised model

  • Each C forms σ bonds to two C and one H, all in one plane, at 120°.
  • Each C has one electron left in a p orbital, above and below the plane.
  • The six p orbitals overlap sideways all the way round the ring.
  • Result: six π electrons delocalised in rings of density above and below the plane.
  • Drawn as a hexagon with a circle inside.
  • Other arenes (e.g. methylbenzene, naphthalene) have delocalised rings too.

Mission 1

Evidence 1: bond lengths

  • X-ray diffraction: benzene is planar, with all bond angles 120°.
  • All six C–C bonds are the same length: 0.139 nm.
  • Compare C–C 0.154 nm and C=C 0.134 nm.
  • Kekulé predicts two lengths. One length, in between, fits delocalisation.
Mission 1

Evidence 2: enthalpy of hydrogenation

  • Cyclohexene + H2 → cyclohexane: ΔH = −120 kJ mol⁻¹ (one C=C).
  • Kekulé triene, three C=C: expect 3 × −120 = −360 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Benzene + 3H2 → cyclohexane: measured −208 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • 152 kJ mol⁻¹ less released, to make the same product.
  • So benzene starts 152 kJ mol⁻¹ lower in energy: delocalisation makes it more stable.
Quick check
?The enthalpy change of hydrogenation of cyclohexene (one C=C bond) is about −120 kJ mol⁻¹. For benzene, forming cyclohexane, it is about −208 kJ mol⁻¹, much less exothermic than the −360 kJ mol⁻¹ predicted for a structure with three localised C=C bonds. Which statement explains the difference?
Mission 1

Evidence 3: resistance to reaction

  • Cyclohexene decolourises bromine at room temperature, in the dark: addition.
  • Benzene does not. It reacts only with a catalyst (halogen carrier), and then by substitution.
  • Alkene: π electrons localised between two carbons. High electron density polarises Br2 and attracts it.
  • Benzene: π electrons spread over six carbons. Electron density between any two is lower.
  • Too low to polarise Br2 enough: no reaction without a catalyst.
Mission 1

Why substitution, not addition

  • Adding across the ring would destroy the delocalised π system.
  • That would throw away about 150 kJ mol⁻¹ of extra stability.
  • Substitution swaps an H atom for another group. The delocalised ring survives.
  • So benzene reacts by electrophilic substitution, with strong electrophiles (see Mission 2).
  • Answer to the opening question: benzene has no localised C=C bonds, so it doesn't behave like an alkene.
Quick check
?Cyclohexene decolourises bromine at room temperature, but benzene does not react with bromine unless a catalyst is added. Which statement explains this?
Mission 1

Ring and substituent: chlorobenzene

  • A lone pair on Cl, in a p orbital, overlaps with the ring's π system.
  • So the C–Cl bond gains some double-bond character: shorter and stronger than in a chloroalkane.
  • Result: chlorobenzene is not hydrolysed by warm aqueous NaOH. 1-Chlorobutane is.
  • The ring changes the substituent, and the substituent changes the ring.
Mission check

?Phenylethene, C6H5CH=CH2, has a benzene ring and a C=C in its side chain. It is shaken with a solution of bromine in hexane, in the dark, at room temperature. What happens?

One molecule, two kinds of π electrons. Localised ones add bromine like any alkene; delocalised ones in the ring hold on to their extra stability.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Benzene does react with bromine, given a catalyst. Why does a hydrogen get swapped rather than bromine adding?

Make a guess: Benzene does react with bromine, given a catalyst. Why does a hydrogen get swapped rather than bromine adding?

Mission 2

Why the ring attracts electrophiles

  • Benzene's six delocalised π electrons make the ring electron-rich, like an alkene's C=C.
  • So it attracts electrophiles: electron-pair acceptors, written E⁺.
  • But the density is spread over six carbons (see Mission 1). It can't polarise Br2 or attack weak electrophiles.
  • So benzene needs a strong, positive electrophile, made with a catalyst.
Mission 2

Substitution, not addition

  • Addition would destroy the delocalised ring and its extra stability.
  • Substitution swaps one H for E. The ring is restored.
  • Overall: C6H6 + E⁺ → C6H5E + H⁺.
Mission 2

Making the electrophile: the nitronium ion

  • Reagents: concentrated nitric acid + concentrated sulfuric acid (the nitrating mixture).
  • Keep at about 50 °C (below 55 °C). Hotter: more than one NO2 group goes on.
  • Electrophile: the nitronium ion, NO2⁺.
  • HNO3 + 2H2SO4 → NO2⁺ + H3O⁺ + 2HSO4⁻
  • Sulfuric acid is the stronger acid: it protonates nitric acid, which then loses water.
Mission 2

The mechanism, arrow by arrow

  • Arrow 1: from the delocalised ring (inside the circle) to the N of NO2⁺. A C–N bond forms.
  • Intermediate: the attacked carbon now holds both H and NO2. Draw a horseshoe over the other five carbons, gap facing that carbon, + inside.
  • Arrow 2: from the C–H bond into the ring. The ring is restored; H⁺ leaves.
  • H⁺ + HSO4⁻ → H2SO4: the catalyst is regenerated.
  • Overall: C6H6 + NO2⁺ → C6H5NO2 + H⁺. Product: nitrobenzene.
  • Back to the opening question: bromine with a catalyst gives Br⁺, which reacts by these same two arrows. Losing H⁺ gets the stable ring back; adding Br⁻ would not.

Mission 2

Halogenation

  • Benzene + bromine + a halogen carrier, at room temperature, dry.
  • Halogen carriers: iron, FeBr3, or AlBr3. Iron filings make FeBr3 from the bromine.
  • Electrophile: Br⁺, made by the carrier: Br2 + FeBr3 → Br⁺ + FeBr4⁻.
  • Same two arrows: ring → Br⁺; then C–H bond → ring, losing H⁺.
  • H⁺ + FeBr4⁻ → FeBr3 + HBr. Product: bromobenzene, C6H5Br.
  • Chlorination works the same way: Cl2 with AlCl3 or FeCl3 gives Cl⁺.
Mission 2

Friedel–Crafts alkylation

  • Benzene + halogenoalkane + AlCl3 catalyst, anhydrous (water destroys AlCl3).
  • CH3CH2Cl + AlCl3 → CH3CH2⁺ + AlCl4⁻
  • Ring → C⁺ of the carbocation; then C–H bond → ring, losing H⁺.
  • H⁺ + AlCl4⁻ → AlCl3 + HCl. Product: ethylbenzene, C6H5CH2CH3.
  • A new C–C bond to the ring: useful in synthesis.
  • Recap: reflux a methyl side-chain with alkaline KMnO4, then acidify, to make an aromatic acid, e.g. methylbenzene → benzoic acid.
Mission check

?Benzene reacts with chlorine when AlCl3 is present. After Cl⁺ bonds to the ring, the positive intermediate could, in principle, gain a Cl⁻ ion, as the carbocation from an alkene does. Instead it loses H⁺. Why?

Whatever the electrophile, the second arrow is the same. Losing H⁺ puts the delocalised ring back, which is why benzene substitutes.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Benzene: planar ring; six p electrons delocalised above and below; all C–C 0.139 nm.
  • Evidence against Kekulé: one bond length, 152 kJ mol⁻¹ extra stability, no ready addition of bromine.
  • Delocalised density is too low to polarise Br2, and addition would destroy the ring: so benzene substitutes.

Mission 2

  • Ring π electrons attack a strong electrophile; the positive intermediate loses H⁺ to restore the ring.
  • Nitration: conc. HNO3 + conc. H2SO4, about 50 °C, electrophile NO2⁺.
  • Halogenation: Br2 + halogen carrier (Br⁺). Alkylation: RCl + AlCl3 (R⁺).
