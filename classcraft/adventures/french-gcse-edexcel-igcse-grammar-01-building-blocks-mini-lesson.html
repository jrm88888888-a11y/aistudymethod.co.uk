Mini-Lesson

The building blocks

Before verbs and tenses, French sentences are built from nouns and adjectives that must agree. This lesson lays the foundation: gender and plurals, the articles le / la / les and un / une / des, the partitive du / de la / des, adjective agreement and position, and the possessives mon / ma / mes.

Edexcel iGCSE topics. Every example is drawn from the five topic areas — la maison and le train (home & abroad), l'école (education & employment), ma sœur (personal life & relationships), le journal (the world around us) and du pain (social activities, fitness & health).

Gender & the definite article

Every noun is le or la

French nouns are either masculine or feminine. The word for "the" tells you which: le for masculine, la for feminine, and l' before a vowel:

  • le livre — the book (masculine)
  • la maison — the house (feminine)
  • l'école — the school (before a vowel, l' hides the gender)

Learn the gender with the noun. There is no reliable rule, so store le livre and la maison as whole units — the article is part of the word.

Making nouns plural

The plural: les and a silent -s

In the plural, both le and la become les. Most nouns just add -s (which is silent). A few endings behave differently:

singularplural
le chat (add -s)les chats
le cheval (-al → -aux)les chevaux
l'animal (-al → -aux)les animaux
le château (-eau → -eaux)les châteaux

Watch out: the -s is silent, so you never hear the plural on the noun — you hear it on les instead. And nouns ending -al or -eau break the rule: cheval → chevaux, château → châteaux.

The indefinite article

un, une and des = a / some

Where English says "a" or "an", French uses un (masculine) or une (feminine). In the plural it becomes des ("some"):

genderform
masculineun livre — a book
feminineune maison — a house
pluraldes livres — some books

English drops "some" — French does not. "I have books" is j'ai des livres, never j'ai livres. The little word des is compulsory.

The partitive · "some / any"

du, de la, de l', des

When you mean some or any of something (often food), French uses the partitive article. It matches the gender of the noun:

gender"some / any"
masculinedu pain — some bread
femininede la viande — some meat
before a vowelde l'eau — some water
pluraldes pommes — some apples

Two big traps: after a negative, the partitive collapses to just de — je ne mange pas de viande, never pas du viande. And after a quantity word it is also de: beaucoup de pain (lots of bread).

Adjective agreement

Adjectives change to match the noun

A French adjective must agree with its noun: add -e for feminine, -s for plural (both -es for feminine plural). Adjectives already ending in -e (like rouge) do not change in the feminine:

patternmasculine → feminine
most: add -epetit → petite
ends -e: no changerouge → rouge
-eux → -euseheureux → heureuse
-f → -vesportif → sportive
-er → -èrecher → chère

Doublers and irregulars: bon → bonne, gros → grosse, gentil → gentille; and learn these by heart — beau → belle, nouveau → nouvelle, vieux → vieille, blanc → blanche, long → longue.

Adjective position

Before or after the noun?

Most French adjectives go after the noun — the opposite of English: une maison blanche (a white house). But a small set of very common ones go before. Remember them with BAGS:

B·A·G·S go BEFOREBeauty · Age · Goodness · Size
  • Beauty: beau, joli · Age: jeune, nouveau, vieux
  • Goodness: bon, mauvais · Size: grand, petit, gros
  • un petit chien — a small dog (size → before)
  • une voiture rouge — a red car; a colour goes after

Default is after. Only BAGS adjectives (beauty, age, goodness, size) jump in front. Colours, nationalities and most descriptions stay behind the noun: une maison blanche.

After a negative or a quantity

When "some" shrinks to de

Two situations flatten the whole partitive family (du / de la / de l' / des) down to a plain de:

  • After a negative: je mange du pain → je ne mange pas de pain. Also je ne mange pas de viande.
  • After a quantity: beaucoup de pain (lots of bread), un peu de lait (a little milk).

Never write je ne mange pas du viande. After ne…pas the article is simply de: je ne mange pas de viande.

Possessive adjectives

mon, ma, mes — my / your / his / her

"My", "your", "his/her" also agree with the noun owned (not the owner). Each has a masculine, feminine and plural form:

meaningm · f · pl
mymon · ma · mes
your (tu)ton · ta · tes
his / herson · sa · ses
ournotre · notre · nos
your (vous)votre · votre · vos
theirleur · leur · leurs

Two quirks: before a vowel, ma switches to mon for sound — mon amie (my female friend), not ma amie. And son / sa / ses means his OR her — the form follows the noun, so son livre can be "his book" or "her book".

Putting the articles together

The three article families

You have now met all three sets of little words that sit in front of a noun. Keep them straight:

  • Definite ("the"): le / la / l' / les — a specific thing. la maison.
  • Indefinite ("a / some"): un / une / des — one, or some countable things. un livre, des livres.
  • Partitive ("some / any"): du / de la / de l' / des — an amount of something. du pain; but de after a negative or a quantity.

Golden habit: learn every noun with its article and gender, and let your adjectives copy that gender. Do that and agreement becomes automatic.

Recap

Your building-blocks toolkit

Gender: every noun is le (m) or la (f); l' before a vowel; plural les. Learn the article with the noun.

Plurals: add silent -s; -al → -aux (cheval → chevaux); -eau → -eaux (château → châteaux).

Articles: definite le/la/les · indefinite un/une/des · partitive du/de la/de l'/des — but de after a negative or a quantity.

Agreement: +e feminine, +s plural; rouge unchanged; heureux→heureuse, sportif→sportive, cher→chère; irregulars beau/belle, blanc/blanche, vieux/vieille.

Position: most adjectives go after; BAGS (beauty, age, goodness, size) go before — un petit chien.

Possessives: mon/ma/mes … leur/leurs; ma→mon before a vowel; son/sa = his or her.
