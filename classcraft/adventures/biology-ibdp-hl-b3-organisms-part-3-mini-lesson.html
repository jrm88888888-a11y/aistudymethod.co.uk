Mission 1

Mission 1: How does water get from the soil into a root, and how do desert plants keep hold of it?

Make a guess: How does water get from the soil into a root, and how do desert plants keep hold of it?

Mission 1

Inside a root

  • Epidermis with root hairs: a huge surface for uptake.
  • Cortex: loosely packed cells; storage.
  • Endodermis: one ring of cells round the centre.
  • Central vascular cylinder (stele): xylem forms a star or X; phloem lies between its arms.
  • Root: xylem in the centre. Stem: xylem in a ring of bundles.
  • Water from the soil enters the root hairs by osmosis.
  • Desert plants (xerophytes) keep hold of water with deep or widespread roots, a thick waxy cuticle, sunken stomata, and rolled leaves or spines.
Quick check
?In a transverse section of a dicotyledonous root, where are the xylem and the phloem found?
Mission 1Practical

Drawing a root section

  • Draw a diagram from a micrograph.
  • Low power first: find the epidermis, cortex, endodermis and central cylinder.
  • Plan diagram: tissue outlines only, true proportions, no shading, ruled labels.
  • Check: xylem star in the centre, phloem between its arms. If you see a ring of bundles, it is a stem.
Mission 1

Into the root hair

  • Root hair cells take in mineral ions by active transport: carrier proteins, ATP.
  • This lowers the water potential of the cell.
  • Soil water has a higher water potential. Water enters by osmosis.
  • Root hairs: long and thin, a large surface; many mitochondria for ATP.
  • Say: "water moves down a water potential gradient" — not: "water is actively pumped in".
Mission 1

Root pressure: a push from below

  • Cells around the xylem actively transport mineral ions into it.
  • Water follows by osmosis. A positive pressure builds and pushes water up: root pressure.
  • Evidence: sap oozes from cut stems; guttation (drops at leaf tips); both stop with respiratory inhibitors.
  • It matters when transpiration is low: humid air, or spring before deciduous leaves open.
  • Too small to lift water up a tall tree. Cohesion–tension does that.
  • Root pressure is a positive pressure potential; transpiration creates a negative one.
  • Say: "ions are pumped; water follows by osmosis" — not: "root cells pump water".
Quick check
?A cut stem of a well-watered plant exudes sap from the xylem for several hours. When the roots are treated with a respiratory inhibitor, the exudation stops. What does this show about how the sap was being pushed out?
Quick check
?Droplets of water appear at the leaf tips of a grass seedling overnight, when the air is saturated with water vapour, but not during a dry, sunny day. Which explanation is correct?
Mission check

?A micrograph shows a transverse section of an organ. It has an epidermis with long outgrowths, a wide cortex, and one central cylinder. In the cylinder, xylem forms a four-armed star with phloem between the arms. Which identification is correct?

Centre or ring: the pattern of the vascular tissue tells a root from a stem.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Your blood vessels leak fluid all day long. So why don't your tissues flood?

Make a guess: Your blood vessels leak fluid all day long. So why don't your tissues flood?

Mission 2

Three fluids

  • Plasma: the liquid part of blood. Water carrying dissolved substances and plasma proteins (e.g. albumin, fibrinogen).
  • Tissue fluid: bathes every cell in a tissue. Like plasma, but with very little protein and no red cells.
  • Lymph: tissue fluid that has drained into lymph vessels. Contains lymphocytes.
  • Cells exchange substances with tissue fluid, not directly with blood.
Mission 2

Out and back: the big picture

  • At the arteriole end of a capillary bed, blood pressure squeezes fluid out through the capillary wall (pressure filtration).
  • That fluid is tissue fluid. It bathes the cells.
  • At the venule end, most of it returns to the blood.
  • The excess drains into lymph vessels as lymph.
  • Lymph returns to the blood in veins near the heart.
  • Out, around the cells, back: the tissues stay supplied but never flood.
Mission 2

The pressures

  • (1) Hydrostatic pressure (blood pressure) pushes fluid out. High at the arteriole end.
  • Water and small solutes (glucose, amino acids, ions, O2) are forced through gaps in the wall: pressure filtration.
  • Cells and plasma proteins are too large. They stay in the blood.
  • (2) Hydrostatic pressure falls along the capillary, as fluid leaves and blood meets resistance.
  • (3) The proteins left behind pull water in by osmosis.
  • Arteriole end: push out > pull in → net outflow. Venule end: pull in > push out → net return.
  • Say: "tissue fluid forms by pressure filtration" — not: "plasma diffuses out".
  • Newer research: in most tissues, little fluid is actually reabsorbed at the venule end; most of it returns through the lymph (Levick and Michel, 2010). Exams still use the model above.
Mission 2

Worked example: net pressure

Illustrative values for one capillary (tissue-fluid pressures ignored):

Arteriole endVenule end
Hydrostatic pressure (out) / kPa4.71.3
Oncotic pressure (in) / kPa3.33.3
  • Net = out − in.
  • Arteriole end: 4.7 − 3.3 = +1.4 kPa: fluid moves out.
  • Venule end: 1.3 − 3.3 = −2.0 kPa: fluid moves in.
Quick check
?Blood pressure in a capillary is about 4.7 kPa at the arteriole end and about 1.3 kPa at the venule end. How do these values explain the release and reuptake of tissue fluid?
Mission 2

Tissue fluid: the link to every cell

  • Tissue fluid carries O2, glucose, amino acids, fatty acids, ions from blood to cells.
  • Cells take them up by diffusion, facilitated diffusion or active transport.
  • Cells release CO2, urea and other wastes into tissue fluid. These enter the blood at the venule end.
  • Each cell is a short diffusion distance from tissue fluid, and tissue fluid from a capillary.
Quick check
?(Illustrative data.) Samples of blood plasma and of tissue fluid from the same tissue were analysed. Both contained about 5 mmol per litre of glucose and similar concentrations of sodium ions, but plasma contained about 70 grams per litre of protein while tissue fluid contained only a small fraction of that amount. Which explanation of these results is correct?
Mission 2

Lymph: draining the excess

  • Not all tissue fluid returns to the capillaries. The excess enters lymph vessels.
  • Lymph capillaries are blind-ended, with thin walls and gaps that let fluid in.
  • Valves stop lymph flowing backwards.
  • Lymph is moved along as nearby skeletal muscles squeeze the vessels. Larger lymph vessels also contract rhythmically.
  • Lymph returns to the blood, entering veins near the heart.
  • Say: "lymph rejoins the blood" — not: "lymph circulates separately".
Quick check
?Which statement describes how excess tissue fluid is drained and what happens to it?
Mission check

?In lymphatic filariasis, parasitic worms live in lymph vessels and can block them. A person with blocked lymph vessels in one leg develops a badly swollen leg. Which explanation is correct?

Fluid leaks out at one end and returns at the other; lymph vessels carry off the rest. Block the drain and the tissues do flood.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Cut every nerve to a heart and it keeps on beating. So what are those nerves for?

Make a guess: Cut every nerve to a heart and it keeps on beating. So what are those nerves for?

Mission 3

The heart starts its own beat

  • Cardiac muscle is myogenic: it contracts without any nerve impulse.
  • The sinoatrial node (SAN), in the wall of the right atrium, is the pacemaker.
  • It sends out a wave of electrical excitation at regular intervals. Each wave starts one heartbeat.
  • The wave spreads from the SAN across both atria, then on to the ventricles, so the chambers contract in order.
  • Nerves and hormones only change the rate of the SAN. They do not start the beats.
  • Say: "the SAN initiates each beat" — not: "the brain sends each heartbeat".
Mission 3

Nerves and hormones adjust the rate

  • A centre in the medulla oblongata controls heart rate.
  • Sympathetic nerve to the SAN → SAN fires faster → heart rate up; contractions stronger, so stroke volume up.
  • Parasympathetic nerve (the vagus) to the SAN → SAN fires more slowly → heart rate down.
  • The medulla sends impulses to the heart to change heart rate and stroke volume.
  • Say: "the medulla changes the rate of the SAN" — not: "the medulla makes the heart beat".
Mission 3

What the medulla senses

  • Baroreceptors (pressure receptors): stretch receptors in the walls of the aorta and carotid arteries. They monitor blood pressure.
  • Chemoreceptors: in the aorta and carotid arteries, and in the medulla. They monitor blood pH, CO2 and O2.
  • Blood pressure high → more baroreceptor impulses → medulla → more vagus impulses → rate falls.
  • Blood pressure low → fewer baroreceptor impulses → medulla → more sympathetic impulses → rate rises.
  • CO2 up, pH down (e.g. exercise) → chemoreceptors → medulla → sympathetic → rate rises → CO2 removed faster.
  • Each response reverses the change: negative feedback.
Quick check
?A person stands up quickly and their blood pressure falls for a few seconds. Which sequence describes the feedback response that restores blood pressure?
Quick check
?Which statement gives the location and the function of the receptors that provide sensory input for the control of heart rate?
Mission check

?In a heart transplant, the nerves linking the donor heart to the medulla are cut, and they do not regrow for many months. Which statement about the transplanted heart is correct?

The heart makes its own beat; the nerves are the volume control, turning the rate up or down to match what the body needs.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Your hip and your knee both carry your weight. Why can only one of them swing your leg in a circle?

Make a guess: Your hip and your knee both carry your weight. Why can only one of them swing your leg in a circle?

Mission 4

Joints: where bones meet

  • Hinge: movement in one plane, like a door. Elbow, knee, finger joints.
  • Ball and socket: a rounded head in a cup. Movement in every direction, including rotation. Hip, shoulder.
Mission 4

Inside a synovial joint

  • Articular cartilage (hyaline): smooth caps on the bone ends. Cuts friction; absorbs shock.
  • Joint capsule: a tough sleeve enclosing the joint.
  • Synovial membrane: lines the capsule; secretes synovial fluid.
  • Synovial fluid: lubricates the joint; nourishes the cartilage.
  • Ligaments: join bone to bone. Hold the joint together; limit movement to a safe range.
  • Muscles pull on the bones through tendons to move the joint.
  • Say: "ligaments join bone to bone; tendons join muscle to bone" — not the other way round.
Mission 4

The hip joint

  • A ball-and-socket joint.
  • Ball: the rounded head of the femur.
  • Socket: a deep cup in the pelvis.
  • Cartilage covers both surfaces; synovial fluid fills the space between.
  • Strong ligaments hold the head of the femur in the socket.
  • Muscles, attached by tendons to the femur and pelvis, swing the thigh in any direction.
  • A deep socket: stable enough to carry body weight.
Quick check
?Which statement correctly describes the roles of structures in the human hip joint?
Mission 4

Range of motion

  • Range of motion: the angle a joint moves through, from one extreme to the other.
  • Measured in each dimension (plane) separately:
  • flexion–extension (bend / straighten)
  • abduction–adduction (away from / towards the midline)
  • rotation (turn about the bone's long axis)
  • Hip (ball and socket): useful ranges in all three. Knee (hinge): mainly one.
  • Measure with a goniometer (a hinged protractor) or by computer analysis of photos or video.
  • Centre it on the joint; one arm along each body part.
  • Measure both extremes from the neutral position (0°).
Mission 4

Worked example: shoulder

Illustrative typical values for a shoulder, measured from the arm hanging at the side (0°):

  • Flexion (arm forwards and up): 180°.
  • Extension (arm backwards): 60°.
  • The extremes lie on opposite sides of 0°. So add:
  • Range of motion = 180° + 60° = 240°.
  • Two readings on the same side of 0° (e.g. 30° and 90°) would be subtracted: 60°.

Mission check

?The shoulder is a ball-and-socket joint. Its socket is shallow, covering only part of the head of the humerus, and its capsule is loose. Which prediction about the shoulder, compared with the hip, is correct?

Same type of joint, different build: the deep hip socket carries weight; the shallow shoulder socket reaches in every direction.

Mission 5

Mission 5: A muscle can only pull. So how does a bent arm ever straighten again?

Make a guess: A muscle can only pull. So how does a bent arm ever straighten again?

Mission 5

Muscles need something to pull on

  • Muscle exerts force only by contracting: it pulls, never pushes.
  • Each end is anchored to a rigid part, across a joint.
  • The pull moves one part relative to the other: a lever.
  • Fulcrum = the joint. Effort = the muscle's pull. Load = the weight of the part moved, plus anything it carries.
  • Endoskeleton (vertebrates): muscles attach by tendons to the outside of bones.
  • Exoskeleton (arthropods): muscles attach to the inside of the hard cuticle, either side of a flexible joint.
Quick check
?A grasshopper's hind leg and a human leg both act as levers moved by muscles. Which comparison is correct?
Mission 5

Antagonistic pairs

  • A muscle cannot lengthen itself. So muscles work in antagonistic pairs.
  • Bend (flex) the elbow: biceps contracts, triceps relaxes and is stretched.
  • Straighten (extend) the elbow: triceps contracts, biceps relaxes and is stretched.
  • One muscle's contraction lengthens the other.
  • Tendons: cords of dense collagen joining muscle to bone. Strong and almost inextensible, so the muscle's pull reaches the bone.
  • Say: "the triceps pulls the arm straight" — not: "the biceps pushes the arm straight".
Mission 5

Titin: the sarcomere's spring

  • Titin: a giant elastic protein. It runs from the Z line to the centre of the sarcomere, anchoring the myosin filaments.
  • When the antagonist stretches a relaxed muscle, titin is extended and stores potential energy.
  • It then recoils, helping the sarcomere return to resting length.
  • Near full stretch it stiffens: it prevents overstretching, so actin and myosin still overlap.
  • Titin cannot lengthen a contracted sarcomere: there it is slack. That job needs the antagonist.
Quick check
?The biceps contracts and shortens to flex the elbow. When the arm is then straightened, how is the biceps returned to its longer, resting length?
Quick check
?What are the roles of the protein titin in a sarcomere?
Mission 5

Every organism moves

  • Motile: moves from place to place (locomotion). Example: a dolphin.
  • Sessile: fixed in one place. Example: an adult barnacle, cemented to a rock.
  • But the barnacle still moves parts of its body:
  • muscles beat its feathery cirri to sweep food from the water;
  • muscles close its shell plates when it is disturbed.
  • Plants move too: shoots bend towards light; guard cells open and close stomata.
  • Movement ≠ locomotion. Adaptations for movement are universal.
Quick check
?A student says: 'Adult barnacles are cemented to rocks and are sessile, so they are an exception to the idea that adaptations for movement are a universal feature of living organisms.' Which is the best response?
Mission 5

Why travel?

  • Foraging for food: a honeybee flying from flower to flower for nectar and pollen.
  • Escaping from danger: a gazelle sprinting from a cheetah.
  • Searching for a mate: a male emperor moth flying upwind along a female's scent (pheromone).
  • Migration: regular, usually seasonal, journeys between regions and back. The Arctic tern, between Arctic and Antarctic each year.
  • Say: "migration is a regular two-way journey" — not: "any move away from home".
Quick check
?Which example of locomotion is correctly matched with its reason?
Mission 5

Built for swimming: whales and dolphins

  • Streamlining: a smooth, tapered (spindle-shaped) body. No hind limbs, no external ears, almost no hair; blubber smooths the outline. Less drag.
  • Flippers: modified forelimbs, with the same bones as your arm. Used to steer and stabilise.
  • Fluke: a horizontal tail blade beaten up and down. This gives the thrust.
  • Airways: nostrils moved to the top of the head as a blowhole; sealed during a dive; airway separate from the mouth.
  • Periodic breathing: a few rapid breaths at the surface, then a long dive.
Quick check
?How does the way a dolphin propels itself differ from the way a bony fish such as a tuna does?
Mission check

?A locust's hind leg has a large muscle that straightens the leg for a jump (the extensor) and a smaller muscle that bends it (the flexor). Both are attached inside the leg's hard outer skeleton, either side of the knee. If the flexor muscle were cut, what would happen?

Locust leg or human arm, the rule is the same: muscles only pull, so every movement needs a partner to pull it back.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Moving costs energy. So what makes it worth an animal's while to move at all?

Make a guess: Moving costs energy. So what makes it worth an animal's while to move at all?

Mission 6

Responding pays

  • Environments change: light, humidity, temperature, food, predators.
  • Organisms that respond to change increase their chance of survival and of reproducing.
  • Locomotion: moving the whole body from place to place. It costs energy.
  • It pays when the move brings a benefit: food, safety, a mate or better conditions.
Mission 6

Four reasons to move

  • Foraging for food: honeybee workers fly from the hive to flowers and back, collecting nectar and pollen.
  • Escaping from danger: a scallop swims away by clapping its shell valves when a starfish touches it.
  • Searching for a mate: male mosquitoes fly towards the hum of a female's wingbeats.
  • Migration: Arctic terns breed in the Arctic, then fly to the Antarctic for the southern summer. A round trip, every year.
  • Say: "migration is a regular, usually seasonal, round trip between regions" — not: "any move away from home".
Quick check
?Each autumn, monarch butterflies fly thousands of kilometres south from Canada to Mexico, and their descendants fly north again in spring. Which reason for locomotion does this show?
Mission check

?Which example of locomotion is correctly matched with its reason?

Every journey has to pay its way: in food, safety, a mate or a better place to breed.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Roots pump in mineral ions; water follows by osmosis.
  • A dicot root has a central xylem star with phloem between the arms.

Mission 2

  • Tissue fluid is squeezed out at the arteriole end; most returns at the venule end; the excess drains as lymph back into the blood.
  • Hydrostatic pressure falls along the capillary; the plasma proteins left behind draw water back in.

Mission 3

  • Cardiac muscle is myogenic: the SAN starts each beat; the AVN delays the wave; the bundle of His and Purkyne tissue carry it to the apex.
  • Baroreceptors and chemoreceptors → medulla → sympathetic (faster) or vagus (slower) nerves to the SAN.

Mission 4

  • Hip: head of femur in a socket of the pelvis; ligaments bone to bone, tendons muscle to bone. Range of motion: measure both extremes in each dimension; add across the neutral position.

Mission 5

  • Muscles only pull: anchored to a skeleton, they act on levers (joint = fulcrum) and work in antagonistic pairs; tendons pass the pull to the bone.
  • Titin recoils after stretch and prevents overstretching. Locomotion: food, escape, mates, migration. Marine mammals: streamlining, flippers, up-and-down fluke, blowhole.

Mission 6

  • Animals move to forage, escape danger, find a mate and migrate.
