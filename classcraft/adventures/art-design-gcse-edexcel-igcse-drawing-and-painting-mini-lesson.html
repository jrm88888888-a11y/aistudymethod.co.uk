The four Assessment Objectives

What you are marked on

Every piece of Edexcel International GCSE Art & Design is judged against four Assessment Objectives, each worth 25%. They run across both components — the Personal Portfolio and the Externally Set Assignment.

  • AO1 — Develop: investigate ideas, showing critical understanding of sources (e.g. artists).
  • AO2 — Refine & experiment: explore and experiment with media, materials and techniques.
  • AO3 — Record: record ideas, observations and insights, e.g. by drawing from life.
  • AO4 — Present: present a personal, meaningful response using visual language.

Drawing and Painting is an endorsed Fine Art title within Edexcel International GCSE Art & Design — it uses the same four AOs and the same two components as every other title. The skills below are how you actually hit them.

The formal elements

The building blocks of art

The formal elements are the visual ingredients every drawing and painting is made from. Examiners want to see you use and talk about them:

  • Line — the mark that describes edges, contours and movement.
  • Tone — the lightness or darkness that makes things look 3-D.
  • Shape — a flat, 2-D enclosed area.
  • Form — the illusion of a solid, 3-D object (shape + tone).
  • Colour — hue, and whether it is warm or cool, bright or dull.
  • Texture — the surface quality (rough, smooth), real or implied.
  • Composition — how everything is arranged in the frame.

Watch out: shape is flat (2-D); form is a shape given depth by tone. A circle is a shape — add shading and it becomes a sphere (a form).

Observational drawing (AO3)

Drawing what you SEE

Observational drawing means recording an object in front of you — measuring with your eyes, not drawing from memory. Core techniques:

  • Mark-making — the range of marks you make (dots, dashes, scribbles) to suggest surface and tone.
  • Shading — building tone smoothly to describe light.
  • Cross-hatching — layered sets of lines; more layers = darker tone.
  • Proportion — getting relative sizes right (compare widths and heights).
  • Perspective — making things appear to recede into space.

Misconception — draw what you SEE, not what you know. You "know" a table top is a rectangle, but from where you sit its far edge looks narrower. Trust your eyes and the angles you actually observe.

Colours also split by temperature. This affects mood and depth — warm colours seem to advance, cool colours recede.

Painting media (AO2)

Watercolour, acrylic & gouache

Experimenting with different paints is exactly what AO2 rewards. Each behaves differently:

  • Watercolour — thin and translucent; you build up transparent washes and let the white paper shine through for highlights.
  • Acrylic — versatile and quick-drying; can be thin like watercolour or thick and opaque; great for layering.
  • Gouache — like watercolour but opaque and matte; good for flat, solid areas of colour.

Key painting techniques you should be able to name and use:

  • Wash — a thin, even, translucent layer of colour (classic watercolour).
  • Layering / glazing — building thin coats so colours show through each other.
  • Impasto — thick, textured paint that stands off the surface and catches light.

Watercolour has no white paint in the traditional sense — the lightest tone is the untouched white paper, so plan your highlights before you start.

Composition

Arranging the frame

Composition is how you arrange everything within the picture. Two ideas do a lot of the work:

  • Rule of thirds — divide the frame into a 3×3 grid; place key subjects on the lines or where they cross for a balanced, dynamic image.
  • Focal point — the main area you want the viewer's eye to land on, often placed on a third rather than dead centre.

Putting it together

Turning skills into AO marks

A strong Drawing and Painting project weaves all four AOs together. Here's how these skills map on:

  • AO3 — Record: observational drawings from life (tone, proportion, perspective).
  • AO2 — Refine & experiment: try watercolour washes, acrylic layering and impasto; test warm vs cool palettes.
  • AO1 — Develop: study an artist's use of colour or mark-making and develop ideas from them.
  • AO4 — Present: pull it together into a resolved final piece with a strong composition and personal meaning.

Exam tip: annotate your sketchbook. Naming the formal elements, techniques and artists you've used is direct evidence for AO1 and AO2.

Recap

The essentials to know

Formal elements: line, tone, shape, form, colour, texture, composition

Shape vs form: form = shape + tone (a light source)

Mark-making: hatching, cross-hatching, stippling, blending

Colour: primaries R/Y/B → secondaries; complementaries are opposite; warm advances, cool recedes

Painting: watercolour washes, acrylic layering, gouache opacity, impasto

Composition: rule of thirds & focal point

AOs: AO1 develop · AO2 refine/experiment · AO3 record · AO4 present
