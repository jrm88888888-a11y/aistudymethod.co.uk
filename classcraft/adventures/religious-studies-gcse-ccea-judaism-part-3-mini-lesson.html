Mission 1

From slavery to freedom

  • Pesach (Passover) remembers the Exodus: the Israelites leaving slavery in Egypt, led by Moses (Exodus 1–15).
  • Pharaoh refused to free the slaves; God sent ten plagues (blood, frogs … darkness).
  • The tenth plague: the death of the Egyptian firstborn. Israelites marked their doorposts with a lamb's blood, and God passed over their houses (Exodus 12:27).
  • They left in haste: no time for the dough to rise (Exodus 12:39).
  • At the Sea of Reeds (Red Sea), the waters parted; the Israelites crossed (Exodus 14).
  • For Jews, this is God's great act of redemption, which leads to Sinai and the covenant.

Mission 1

Chametz out

  • Chametz: leavened food from wheat, barley, spelt, rye or oats that has risen (bread, cakes, pasta, beer).
  • Jews may not eat or own chametz during Pesach (Exodus 12:15; 13:7).
  • Weeks of cleaning: cupboards, pockets, cars; special Pesach dishes or kashered ones.
  • The search (bedikat chametz): on the night before, by candle or torch, often with a feather and wooden spoon; ten wrapped pieces are hidden so children can find them.
  • Next morning, the chametz found is burned (biur chametz) and a formula declares any missed crumbs ownerless.
  • Large stocks can be sold to a non-Jew through a rabbi and bought back after.
  • Instead, matzah: flour and water, baked within 18 minutes so it cannot rise.
  • Meanings: the haste of the Exodus; the "bread of affliction" of slaves; and removing inner "puffed-up" pride.

Mission 1

The Seder plate

  • Seder means "order": a set meal on the first night (two nights for many outside Israel).
  • Zeroa: a roasted shank bone: the Passover lamb, no longer sacrificed since the Temple fell.
  • Beitzah: a roasted egg: the festival offering in the Temple; also mourning for the Temple.
  • Maror: bitter herbs (horseradish or bitter lettuce): the bitterness of slavery.
  • Charoset: apples, nuts, wine, cinnamon: the mortar slaves used for bricks.
  • Karpas: a green vegetable dipped in salt water: spring, and the slaves' tears.
  • Chazeret: a second bitter herb, used in a "sandwich" with matzah.
  • Beside the plate: three matzot, salt water, and wine for each person.
Mission 1

The Haggadah and four questions

  • Haggadah ("telling"): the book read at the Seder, from "thou shalt tell thy son in that day" (Exodus 13:8, JPS 1917).
  • The youngest child asks the four questions (Mah nishtanah: "Why is this night different?"):
  • Why only matzah? Why bitter herbs? Why dip twice? Why recline?
  • The answer is the story: "We were slaves to Pharaoh in Egypt…"
  • Four cups of wine: the four promises of Exodus 6:6–7 (bring out, deliver, redeem, take you as a people).
  • A drop of wine is spilled for each plague: joy is lessened because Egyptians suffered too.
  • Afikoman (hidden matzah) keeps children alert; Elijah's cup and the door opened express hope for the Messiah.
  • It ends: "Next year in Jerusalem!"

Mission 1

A pilgrim festival

  • Pesach is one of the three pilgrim festivals (shalosh regalim) with Shavuot and Sukkot: "Three times in a year shall all thy males appear before the LORD thy God" (Deuteronomy 16:16, JPS 1917).
  • In Temple times, families went up to Jerusalem to sacrifice the Passover lamb.
  • Since the Temple's destruction (70 CE) there is no sacrifice: the shank bone and the Haggadah take its place.
  • Pesach lasts seven days in Israel and for Reform Jews; eight days for most Orthodox Jews outside Israel. The first and last days are holy days.
  • On the second night, Jews begin counting the Omer: 49 days to Shavuot, linking freedom to the gift of the Torah (see Mission 2).
  • It is also a spring festival, in the month of Nisan.
Mission 1Diversity

Different ways of keeping Pesach

  • Orthodox: strict removal of all chametz; two Seders outside Israel; a fully traditional Haggadah in Hebrew.
  • Ashkenazi and Sephardi: many Ashkenazi Jews also avoid kitniyot (rice, beans, maize); most Sephardi Jews eat them.
  • Reform and Liberal: seven days; one Seder; Haggadot in English with new readings, e.g. on modern slavery or refugees.
  • Some families add Miriam's cup (water, for Moses' sister) to honour women in the story.
  • Many Jews who keep few other practices still hold or attend a Seder.
  • Understandings differ: many Orthodox Jews read the Exodus as history; some Reform and secular Jews read it mainly as a story that shapes Jewish identity and values.
Mission 1

Why Pesach matters today

  • Religious: it obeys the Torah's command "for ever" (Exodus 12:14) and thanks God as redeemer.
  • Memory: the Mishnah teaches that in every generation each Jew should see themselves as if they personally came out of Egypt (Pesachim 10:5, paraphrase).
  • Teaching: the whole Seder is built around children's questions.
  • Social: family and community gather; the Seder joins generations.
  • Values: freedom, and remembering "you were strangers in Egypt": a reason to care for the oppressed today.
  • Hope: Elijah's cup and "next year in Jerusalem".
Mission 1Evaluate

"Pesach is the most important Jewish festival."

  • For: it remembers the founding event of the Jewish people; the Torah commands it "for ever"; the Exodus is recalled daily in prayer and in the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20:2); it is very widely kept, even by non-observant Jews.
  • Against: Yom Kippur is the holiest day; Shabbat, kept weekly, is in the Ten Commandments and comes first in the Torah's list of holy days (Leviticus 23:3).
  • Against: Shavuot (the gift of the Torah) gives freedom its purpose.
  • Another view: festivals have different jobs (memory, atonement, joy); ranking them may miss the point.
  • Judge it by: What does the Torah stress? Which shapes Jewish identity most? Is "most kept" the same as "most important"?

Mission 2

Sukkot: harvest and wilderness

  • Sukkot ("booths" or "tabernacles"): seven days from 15 Tishri, five days after Yom Kippur.
  • Two origins in the Torah:
  • Harvest: the "feast of ingathering, at the end of the year" (Exodus 23:16, JPS 1917), kept once the grain and grapes are gathered in (Deuteronomy 16:13).
  • Wilderness: God sheltered the Israelites in temporary huts for forty years after the Exodus (Leviticus 23:43).
  • A pilgrim festival: in Temple times, Jews went up to Jerusalem (Deuteronomy 16:16).
  • Called "the season of our joy": "thou shalt be altogether joyful" (Deuteronomy 16:15, JPS 1917).
  • Straight after come Shemini Atzeret and Simchat Torah ("rejoicing in the Torah"; one day in Israel): the yearly reading of the Torah ends and begins again, with dancing around the synagogue holding the scrolls.

Mission 2

The sukkah

  • A sukkah is a temporary hut built outside, in a garden, on a balcony open to the sky, or at the synagogue.
  • At least three walls; the roof (schach) is of cut plant material: branches, bamboo or palm leaves.
  • More shade than sun, but gaps to see the stars.
  • Decorated with fruit, paper chains and children's pictures.
  • Jews eat their meals in it; some sleep in it. Heavy rain excuses them.
  • Many invite guests, and by custom symbolic "guests" (ushpizin): Abraham, Isaac, Jacob and others.
  • Meanings: life is fragile; trust God, not walls; remember those without a secure home; give thanks for the harvest.
Mission 2

The four species

  • Lulav: a closed palm branch. Etrog: a citron, like a large lemon. Hadassim: three myrtle twigs. Aravot: two willow twigs.
  • Lulav, myrtle and willow are bound together; the etrog is held alongside.
  • They are waved in six directions (east, south, west, north, up, down): God is everywhere. Not on Shabbat.
  • In synagogue, worshippers carry them around the bimah in processions (hoshanot), praying for rain and blessing.
  • A midrash: each plant is a kind of Jew. The etrog has taste and scent (Torah learning and good deeds); the willow has neither. Bound together, the community is complete (Leviticus Rabbah 30:12, paraphrase).

Mission 2Diversity

Different ways, and changes

  • Orthodox: Sukkot with all the laws of sukkah and four species, an extra festival day outside Israel; Simchat Torah dancing by men with the scrolls.
  • Reform and Liberal: one festival day; many families share a communal sukkah at the synagogue; women and men dance with the Torah together.
  • Changes: ready-made sukkah kits; sukkot on flat balconies, at schools and universities; etrogim imported from abroad; some link Sukkot to homelessness and the environment.

Mission 2Evaluate

"Sukkot still matters for Jews living in modern cities."

  • For: the Torah commands it "for ever"; the sukkah teaches trust in God and that wealth and walls are fragile; it remembers the Exodus; the joy and guests build community.
  • For: it raises care for the homeless and for creation: themes that matter in cities.
  • Against: few city Jews farm, so the harvest meaning may feel distant; flats and British weather make sukkah living hard.
  • Different Jews: Orthodox Jews keep it as binding law whatever the setting; Reform Jews may stress its ethical meaning and keep it communally.
  • Judge it by: Does a festival's meaning depend on farming? Can a communal sukkah carry the same meaning? What does "matters" mean: law, memory or values?

Mission 3

Brit Milah: the covenant in the body

  • A rite of passage is a ceremony that marks a move from one stage of life to the next: birth, coming of age, marriage, death.
  • Brit Milah means "covenant of circumcision": the removal of the foreskin of a Jewish baby boy.
  • It goes back to God's covenant with Abraham: "every male among you shall be circumcised" (Genesis 17:10).
  • It takes place on the eighth day after birth (Genesis 17:12; Leviticus 12:3), even on Shabbat or Yom Kippur.
  • If the baby is unwell it is delayed: protecting life comes first.
  • Meaning: the boy enters the covenant; it is a sign of Jewish identity carried for life.
  • It is a mitzvah for the father, who appoints a trained mohel to carry it out for him.
Mission 3

The ceremony

  • Held at home or in the synagogue, usually in the morning, with a minyan where possible.
  • The baby is carried in by a couple chosen as godparents; guests say "Blessed is he who comes".
  • He is placed briefly on the Chair of Elijah, the prophet believed to be present at every brit.
  • The sandek (often a grandfather) holds the baby on his knees: a great honour.
  • The mohel performs the circumcision and says a blessing; the father says a blessing for the mitzvah of bringing his son "into the covenant of Abraham our father".
  • Everyone answers: may he grow into Torah, marriage and good deeds.
  • The baby's Hebrew name is announced; a festive meal (seudat mitzvah) follows.
  • Significance for the community: a new member is welcomed into the people of the covenant.

Mission 3

Welcoming a baby girl

  • There is no circumcision for girls: Jewish girls are part of the covenant from birth.
  • Orthodox (Ashkenazi): the father is called up to the Torah in the synagogue soon after the birth; a prayer is said for mother and baby, and the girl's Hebrew name is announced; then a kiddush.
  • Sephardi Jews hold a zeved habat ("gift of a daughter") with blessings and verses from the Song of Songs.
  • Simchat bat ("joy of a daughter"): a newer home or synagogue celebration, now held in many communities, Orthodox and Progressive.
  • Reform and Liberal: both parents stand on the bimah for a baby blessing, for girls and boys alike; some hold a covenant ceremony for girls.
  • Significance: the community welcomes the girl, and she receives the name she will be called up by all her life.
Mission 3

Bar Mitzvah: son of the commandment

  • Bar Mitzvah means "son of the commandment". At 13, a boy becomes an adult in Jewish law.
  • This happens automatically on his 13th birthday (by the Jewish calendar), whether or not there is a ceremony.
  • New responsibilities: keeping all the mitzvot; fasting on Yom Kippur; wearing tefillin; being counted in a minyan; answering for his own actions.
  • Pirkei Avot teaches: at five for Scripture, at ten for Mishnah, at thirteen for the commandments (5:21, paraphrase).
  • In Orthodox communities the father says a blessing that he is no longer answerable for his son's deeds.
Mission 3

Preparing and the service

  • Lessons for a year or more: reading Hebrew, chanting the Torah portion to its traditional tune, learning the prayers and their meaning.
  • Many also take on a mitzvah project: volunteering or raising money for charity.
  • The service: on the Shabbat after his 13th birthday he is called up to the Torah (aliyah) for the first time, and reads from the scroll and often the haftarah.
  • He may give a short talk (d'var Torah) on the portion; the rabbi blesses and addresses him.
  • In some communities sweets are thrown, wishing him a sweet life; a kiddush or party follows.
  • Afterwards: what matters is the life that follows: daily prayer, tefillin, study and keeping the mitzvot. Some Progressive communities add a later ceremony at 15 or 16.

Mission 3

Bat Mitzvah and Bat Chayil

  • Bat Mitzvah: "daughter of the commandment". In Orthodox law a girl comes of age at 12.
  • Reform and Liberal: girls celebrate at 12 or 13 exactly as boys do: reading from the Torah, leading prayers, giving a talk. The first public Bat Mitzvah in the US was in New York in 1922.
  • Orthodox: girls do not read from the Torah in a mixed service. Many give a talk, hold a celebration, or read in a women's service.
  • Bat Chayil ("daughter of valour"), from Proverbs 31: "A woman of valour who can find? for her price is far above rubies." (Proverbs 31:10)
  • It is a group ceremony in some British Orthodox synagogues, often on a Sunday: girls who have studied together present projects and read prayers and psalms.
  • Some Orthodox rabbis welcome these ceremonies; others prefer no formal ceremony for girls, seeing it as a recent change.
Mission 3Diversity

Orthodox and Reform compared

  • Brit Milah: kept by Orthodox, Reform and Liberal Jews alike. A small number of Jews, and some outside critics, question infant circumcision; the main movements uphold it as a central mitzvah.
  • Naming girls: Orthodox: the father's aliyah and a prayer; Reform: both parents on the bimah.
  • Bar Mitzvah: 13 in every movement.
  • Bat Mitzvah: Orthodox: 12, no Torah reading in a mixed service, sometimes Bat Chayil. Reform and Liberal: 12 or 13, the same service as boys.
  • Why: Orthodox Jews follow halakhah on men's and women's roles in public worship; Reform and Liberal Jews stress equality of men and women.

Mission 3Evaluate

"The responsibilities matter more than the ceremony."

  • For: in Jewish law a boy is Bar Mitzvah at 13 and a girl Bat Mitzvah at 12 with or without a ceremony; the aim is a life of mitzvot; lavish parties can overshadow the meaning.
  • Against: the ceremony motivates years of study; it welcomes the young person publicly into the community; the father's blessing and the first aliyah are meaningful acts in themselves.
  • Different views: Orthodox Jews stress the new legal duties; Reform and Liberal Jews stress personal commitment and equality, and some add a later ceremony when commitment is more mature.
  • Another view: the ceremony and the responsibilities belong together: a public promise makes a private duty real.
  • Judge it by: What changes at 13 if there is no ceremony? What do young Jews themselves remember? Does the community need the public event?

Mission 4

Marriage: making something holy

  • Jews see marriage as a mitzvah (commandment) and the normal path for adult life.
  • The Hebrew name for the ceremony is kiddushin, from kadosh, holy: two people are set apart for each other.
  • Reasons in the Torah: companionship ("It is not good that the man should be alone", Genesis 2:18) and children (Genesis 1:28).
  • The home a couple builds is a mikdash me'at, a "small sanctuary": Shabbat, festivals and teaching children happen there.
  • Once there were two separate stages: kiddushin or erusin (betrothal) and, about a year later, nissuin (the wedding itself). Since the Middle Ages both happen in one ceremony.
Mission 4Read the source

One flesh

> "Therefore shall a man leave his father and his mother, and shall cleave unto his wife, and they shall be one flesh." (Genesis 2:24, JPS 1917)

  • Jews read this as the Torah's picture of marriage: a new unit, bound closer than to parents.
  • "Cleave" means hold fast: marriage is meant to be loyal and lasting.
  • "One flesh" covers body, home and life together; sexual love within marriage is seen as good and holy.

Mission 4

Before the chuppah

  • Many couples fast on the wedding day until the ceremony: a personal day of atonement and a fresh start.
  • The ketubah (marriage contract, in Aramaic) is signed by two witnesses.
  • It sets out the husband's duties to his wife: to provide food, clothing and marital love, and a payment for her if they divorce or he dies.
  • It protected women when they had few legal rights. The bride keeps it; many are beautifully decorated.
  • Bedeken (veiling): the groom lowers the veil over the bride's face, recalling Rebecca veiling herself when she met Isaac (Genesis 24:65).
Mission 4

Under the chuppah

  • The chuppah is a canopy held up on four poles, open at the sides: the couple's new home, open to guests like Abraham's tent.
  • In many Orthodox weddings the bride circles the groom (often seven times).
  • Betrothal: a blessing over wine; both drink. The groom places a plain ring on the bride's finger and says: "Behold, you are consecrated to me with this ring, according to the law of Moses and Israel."
  • The ketubah is read aloud.
  • Seven blessings (sheva brachot) over a second cup: God as Creator, joy of the couple, hope for Jerusalem.
  • The groom breaks a glass underfoot: even at the happiest moment, Jews remember the destroyed Temple (Psalm 137:5–6). Guests shout "Mazel tov!"
  • Yichud: the couple spend a few minutes alone together.

Mission 4

When a marriage ends: the get

  • Judaism permits divorce (Deuteronomy 24:1), but sees it as sad: "For I hate putting away, saith the LORD" (Malachi 2:16, JPS 1917).
  • The Talmud says that when a couple divorce, even the altar sheds tears (Gittin 90b, paraphrase).
  • A Jewish religious divorce needs a get: a document written by a scribe for this couple, which the husband gives to the wife before a beth din (rabbinic court).
  • A civil divorce alone does not end a marriage in Orthodox Jewish law.
  • If a husband refuses a get, the wife is an agunah ("chained woman"): she cannot remarry in an Orthodox synagogue.
  • Responses: prenuptial agreements; in the UK, the Divorce (Religious Marriages) Act 2002 lets a court delay a civil divorce until a religious one is given.
  • Reform and Liberal communities usually accept a civil divorce or their own beth din's ruling, so one partner cannot block the other.
Mission 4Diversity

Orthodox and Reform weddings

  • Both: chuppah, wine, ring, ketubah, seven blessings, breaking the glass.
  • Orthodox: only the groom gives a ring and speaks the betrothal words; the ketubah sets out the husband's duties; both partners must be Jewish.
  • Reform and Liberal: often a double-ring ceremony; both partners speak; an egalitarian ketubah with promises from each.
  • Many Reform and Liberal rabbis bless same-sex marriages; Orthodox Judaism does not.
  • Some Reform and Liberal rabbis take part in weddings where one partner is not Jewish; Orthodox rabbis do not.
Mission 4

From death to burial

  • At death, the eyes are closed, a candle is lit near the head ("The spirit of man is the lamp of the LORD", Proverbs 20:27, JPS 1917), and the body is never left alone.
  • Onen: a close relative between the death and the burial; the period is called aninut. An onen is freed from many mitzvot, such as daily prayer, to arrange the funeral. Comforting waits until after burial.
  • The Chevra Kaddisha ("holy society"): volunteers who wash the body (taharah) and dress it in plain white shrouds. Rich and poor are buried alike.
  • Their work is called true kindness: the dead can never repay it.
  • Keriah (tearing of clothes): mourners tear a garment and say "Blessed is the true Judge."
  • Why tear? It lets grief show, as Jacob "rent his garments" for Joseph (Genesis 37:34, JPS 1917); the blessing accepts God's judgement even in loss.
  • Burial soon, ideally within a day (based on Deuteronomy 21:23): a plain coffin, psalms, a eulogy; mourners help fill the grave with earth.
  • Orthodox Jews require burial ("for dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return", Genesis 3:19, JPS 1917); Reform and Liberal Jews also permit cremation, and some keep a shorter shiva.
Mission 4

The Kaddish

  • The Kaddish is a prayer in Aramaic said by mourners at the graveside and in services.
  • It opens: "Magnified and sanctified be his great name in the world which he hath created according to his will" (Singer's Authorised Daily Prayer Book, 1890).
  • It never mentions death. The mourner praises God at the moment of loss.
  • It needs a minyan (ten adults; in Orthodox practice, ten men), so mourners are drawn back into the community.
  • For a parent, many say it daily for eleven months, then on each anniversary.
  • Many Jews also believe saying Kaddish brings merit to the soul of the one who has died.
Mission 4

Mourning in stages

  • Shiva ("seven"): seven days from the burial. Mourners stay home on low chairs; mirrors are covered; no work, shaving or leather shoes; a candle burns; visitors bring food and form a minyan.
  • Sheloshim ("thirty"): to day 30. Mourners return to work but avoid parties and music.
  • Avelut (mourning): for a parent, it lasts twelve months. Many set the gravestone during this year.
  • Yahrzeit (anniversary): on the Hebrew date of the death each year: a 24-hour candle, Kaddish, often tzedakah (charity) or a visit to the grave.
  • The shape is gradual: grief is given room, then the mourner is led back into daily life.

Mission 4Evaluate

"Jewish mourning rituals are more for the living than for the dead."

  • Claim: the rituals mainly help mourners, not the person who has died.
  • For: shiva surrounds mourners with community and food; the stages lead them back to normal life step by step; Kaddish lets a grieving person affirm faith in God.
  • Against: the Chevra Kaddisha, taharah and never leaving the body alone show respect for the dead (kevod ha-met); quick burial honours the body; many Jews believe Kaddish benefits the soul.
  • Another view: Jewish law names two separate duties, honouring the dead and comforting mourners (nichum avelim); the rituals serve both.
  • Different Jews: Orthodox Jews stress the full laws for both; Reform Jews often explain the rituals mainly through their help to mourners.
  • Judge it by: What does each ritual say it is for? Would it make sense if there were no mourners? How do Orthodox and Reform Jews explain it?

Mission 5

Women in Jewish teaching

  • "And God created man in His own image, in the image of God created He him; male and female created He them." (Genesis 1:27, JPS 1917): men and women have equal worth before God.
  • Great women of the Tenakh: the matriarchs Sarah, Rebecca, Rachel and Leah; Miriam; Deborah the judge; Ruth; Esther.
  • On Friday night many husbands sing Eshet Chayil to their wives: "A woman of valour who can find? for her price is far above rubies." (Proverbs 31:10, JPS 1917)
  • In Orthodox Judaism, Jewish identity passes through the mother.
  • The home is a centre of Jewish life, and women have often led it: Shabbat, festivals, kashrut, teaching children.
  • Jews disagree about what equal worth means for roles in worship and leadership.
Mission 5

Orthodox Judaism: different roles

  • Women are exempt from most positive mitzvot tied to a set time, such as wearing tallit and tefillin or praying with a minyan (Mishnah Kiddushin 1:7, paraphrase).
  • Reasons given: family duties take priority; or women are thought not to need these reminders.
  • Mitzvot linked especially to women: lighting Shabbat candles, separating challah, and the laws of family purity (mikveh).
  • In the synagogue, women sit separately behind a mechitzah (screen) or in a gallery; they are not counted in the minyan and do not lead services for men.
  • Orthodox women do not usually serve as rabbis; many now teach Torah and Talmud at a high level and advise on Jewish law.
  • Many Orthodox women describe their role as different but equal; some Orthodox women campaign for more roles.
Mission 5

Reform, Liberal and Masorti: equal roles

  • Reform and Liberal Judaism: men and women sit together, count equally in the minyan, read from the Torah and lead services.
  • Women serve as rabbis: Regina Jonas (1902–1944), ordained in Germany in 1935, was the first; she was murdered at Auschwitz.
  • In Britain, Jackie Tabick became the first woman rabbi in 1975; Julia Neuberger followed in 1977 and later sat in the House of Lords.
  • Girls celebrate Bat Mitzvah in the same way as boys' Bar Mitzvah.
  • Many Masorti communities are also egalitarian.
  • Reasons: Genesis 1:27; changing roles of women in society; the belief that Jewish law can develop.

Mission 5

Orthodox dress

  • The guiding idea is tzniut (modesty): dress that shows respect for God, oneself and others.
  • Men: a kippah (skullcap): a reminder of God above. Tzitzit: fringes on a small four-cornered garment under the shirt: "that ye may look upon it, and remember all the commandments of the LORD" (Numbers 15:39, JPS 1917).
  • Haredi men often wear black suits and hats; Hasidic men may wear long black coats, a fur hat (shtreimel) on Shabbat, beards and peyot (sidelocks: "Ye shall not round the corners of your heads", Leviticus 19:27, JPS 1917).
  • Women: elbows, knees and collarbone covered; many wear skirts rather than trousers.
  • Married women cover their hair: a scarf (tichel), a hat, or a wig (sheitel).
  • Reform and Liberal Jews have no special dress code; many wear a kippah and tallit only for prayer, and women may wear them too.
  • Dress can make Orthodox Jews visible: a sign of identity, and sometimes a target for abuse.
Mission 5

A long history of persecution

  • Antisemitism: hostility to Jews as Jews. The word dates from 1879, the hatred from much earlier.
  • Some medieval Christians blamed all Jews for Jesus' death. The Catholic Church rejected this charge in Nostra Aetate (1965).
  • England: the false blood libel first appeared at Norwich (1144); the massacre at York (1190); all Jews expelled in 1290, readmitted from 1656.
  • Europe: expulsion from Spain (1492); ghettos; pogroms (violent riots) in the Russian Empire (1881–1906).
  • The Shoah (1933–1945): Nazi Germany stripped Jews of rights, forced them into ghettos and murdered about six million in mass shootings and death camps such as Auschwitz-Birkenau and Treblinka.
Mission 5

Faith after the Shoah

  • Jews have responded to the Shoah in different ways; none is the single Jewish view.
  • Eliezer Berkovits (1908–1992), Faith after the Holocaust (1973): God "hides his face" (hester panim) so that humans have real free will; the evil was human evil.
  • Emil Fackenheim (1916–2003): a "614th commandment": Jews must not hand Hitler a posthumous victory by giving up Judaism or the Jewish people (paraphrase).
  • Elie Wiesel (1928–2016), Night (1956): a survivor's account that protests to God and refuses easy answers.
  • Some Jews lost their faith; many kept it, praying in secret even in the camps.
  • Remembrance: Yom HaShoah in spring; in the UK, Holocaust Memorial Day on 27 January.
Mission 5

Prejudice today, and Jewish responses

  • Antisemitism continues: abuse in the street and online, attacks on synagogues, and conspiracy theories.
  • Many synagogues and Jewish schools in Britain need security; the Community Security Trust (CST, 1994) protects them and records incidents.
  • Teaching: "The stranger that sojourneth with you shall be unto you as the home-born among you, and thou shalt love him as thyself; for ye were strangers in the land of Egypt" (Leviticus 19:34, JPS 1917).
  • Responses: education (Holocaust survivors speaking in schools), interfaith work such as the Council of Christians and Jews (1942), and campaigns against all racism.
  • Jews also act for others who face prejudice: remembering their own history leads many to help refugees (tikkun olam).

Mission 5

An influential Jew: Jonathan Sacks

  • Rabbi Lord Jonathan Sacks (1948–2020): Chief Rabbi of the United Hebrew Congregations of the Commonwealth, 1991–2013; made a life peer in 2009.
  • Teaching: Judaism is a covenant of responsibility; faith and reason work together; each person, made in God's image, has dignity.
  • The Dignity of Difference (2002): God made people different, and difference between faiths and cultures can be a blessing, not a threat (paraphrase).
  • To Heal a Fractured World (2005): Jewish ethics of responsibility and tikkun olam.
  • Contribution to Judaism: a translation and commentary for the Koren Sacks Siddur (2009); weekly Torah essays, Covenant & Conversation; many books read by Jews of all kinds.
  • Contribution to society: talks on BBC Radio 4's Thought for the Day; work with Christian and Muslim leaders; the Templeton Prize (2016).
Mission 5

Other figures you could study

  • Hillel (1st century BCE): taught that the heart of the Torah is not to do to others what is hateful to you; "the rest is commentary" (Talmud Shabbat 31a, paraphrase).
  • Rashi (Rabbi Shlomo Yitzchaki, 1040–1105): commentaries on the Torah and Talmud, still printed on every page of the Talmud.
  • Regina Jonas (1902–1944): the first woman rabbi.
  • Julia Neuberger (born 1950): rabbi and member of the House of Lords; work on health care, care of the elderly and refugees.
  • Whichever figure you choose, show: what they taught, what they did, and why it matters now.
Mission 5Evaluate

"Orthodox Judaism treats women as equal but different."

  • For: Genesis 1:27 gives equal worth; women hold central mitzvot and lead the home; exemptions are not bans; Orthodox women's learning and leadership are growing.
  • Against: women are not counted in the minyan, cannot usually be rabbis and sit apart in synagogue; an agunah (see Mission 4) depends on her husband for a get.
  • Another view: "equal" may not mean "the same": roles can differ and still be fair, as many Orthodox women argue; Reform Jews reply that equal worth should mean equal roles.
  • Judge it by: What do the sources say? Who decides what equality means? What do Orthodox women themselves say?

Mission 6

Kosher means "fit"

  • Kashrut: the Jewish food laws. Food that meets them is kosher ("fit", "proper").
  • Food that does not is treifah: literally "torn", from the Torah's ban on meat "torn of beasts in the field" (Exodus 22:31). It now means any forbidden food.
  • Your spec spells it terefah.
  • The source: the Torah (Leviticus 11; Deuteronomy 14), explained in the Talmud.
  • Many food laws are chukim: laws given without a stated reason. Jews keep them because God commanded them.
  • The reason the Torah does give: holiness. "Ye shall therefore be holy, for I am holy." (Leviticus 11:45, JPS 1917)
Mission 6Read the source

Hoofs, cud, fins and scales

> "And every beast that parteth the hoof, and hath the hoof wholly cloven in two, and cheweth the cud, among the beasts, that ye may eat." (Deuteronomy 14:6, JPS 1917)

> "These ye may eat of all that are in the waters: whatsoever hath fins and scales may ye eat;" (Deuteronomy 14:9, JPS 1917)

Q1 What two signs make a land animal kosher?

Show answer

A fully split hoof and chewing the cud.

Q2 Why is a pig not kosher, though it has split hoofs?

Show answer

It does not chew the cud, Deuteronomy 14:8.

Mission 6

Which creatures?

  • Mammals: both signs needed. Kosher: cattle, sheep, goats, deer. Not kosher: pig, camel, hare, horse.
  • Fish: fins and scales. Kosher: salmon, cod, tuna, herring. Not kosher: shellfish (prawns, lobster), eels, sharks.
  • Birds: the Torah lists forbidden birds, mostly birds of prey. Jews eat only birds with a long tradition of being kosher: chicken, turkey, duck, goose.
  • Insects: almost all forbidden. Lev 11:22 allows some locusts; only a few communities (e.g. some Yemenite Jews) keep a tradition of which ones.
  • Fruit and vegetables: all kosher; but leafy vegetables are checked for tiny insects.
  • Products of a creature follow it: eggs of kosher birds and milk of kosher animals are kosher; pork gelatine is not.
Mission 6

Shechitah: how meat is made kosher

  • Shechitah: slaughter by a shochet, a trained, observant Jew licensed by a beth din.
  • A razor-sharp, perfectly smooth knife; one swift cut across the throat. Jewish authorities hold that blood pressure to the brain falls at once, so the animal quickly loses consciousness.
  • The animal is inspected; certain defects make it treifah. Lungs with no defects are glatt ("smooth").
  • No blood: "ye shall eat no manner of blood, whether it be of fowl or of beast" (Leviticus 7:26, JPS 1917). Meat is soaked and salted to draw it out.
  • Certain fats and the sciatic nerve are removed (Genesis 32:32), so some cuts are rarely sold as kosher in Britain.
  • An animal that dies by itself is not kosher (Deuteronomy 14:21).
  • Debate: UK law lets Jews and Muslims slaughter without pre-stunning. Some animal-welfare groups want this ended; Jewish bodies argue shechitah is humane and that a ban would restrict religious freedom.

Mission 6

Milk and meat; parev

  • Three times the Torah says: "Thou shalt not seethe a kid in its mother's milk." (Deuteronomy 14:21; Exodus 23:19; 34:26, JPS 1917)
  • The Talmud explains: never cook, eat, or benefit from meat and milk together.
  • Poultry counts as meat; so a chicken sandwich with butter is not kosher.
  • Jews wait between meat and dairy: commonly six hours; some communities three, or one. After dairy, a much shorter wait or none.
  • Parev (or pareve): neither meat nor dairy. Fish, eggs, fruit, vegetables, grains: eaten with either.
  • Many Jews also avoid cooking fish and meat together, by custom.
  • So a cheeseburger is forbidden even when the beef and the cheese are each kosher.
Mission 6

The kosher kitchen

  • Two sets of pans, plates, cutlery and utensils: one for meat, one for dairy; often colour-coded (e.g. red for meat, blue for dairy).
  • Separate sinks or washing-up bowls, tea towels, and work surfaces or boards; some homes have two ovens or dishwashers.
  • Parev foods may be prepared with either set.
  • Packaged foods carry a hechsher: a mark from a kosher authority (in Britain e.g. KLBD, the London Beth Din's kosher division, or the Manchester Beth Din).
  • A mashgiach (supervisor) checks kosher restaurants, caterers and factories.
  • At Pesach, many families use a third set again, kept free of leaven (see Mission 1).

Mission 6Diversity

Different ways of keeping kosher

  • Haredi (strictly Orthodox): extra stringencies, e.g. only glatt meat; milk produced under Jewish supervision; some foods cooked only with a Jew's involvement.
  • Modern Orthodox / United Synagogue: kashrut as binding law, at home and outside; eat out only where supervised.
  • Masorti: kashrut as binding, with some different rulings.
  • Reform: an informed personal choice. Many keep some rules (no pork or shellfish; no meat with milk at home); some keep fully.
  • Liberal: the ritual laws are not binding; ethical eating, e.g. vegetarianism or eco-kashrut, may matter more.
  • Many Jews who are not observant still avoid pork as a sign of identity.
Mission 6

Keeping kosher in Britain

  • Benefits: daily obedience to God; every meal becomes a reminder of holiness; a strong sense of Jewish identity; family and community meals; self-discipline; respect for animal life.
  • Challenges: kosher shops and restaurants are mostly in cities with large Jewish communities (e.g. north London, Manchester, Leeds, Gateshead); kosher meat costs more; school dinners, work events, university and travel need planning.
  • Help available: hechsher lists, kosher sections in some supermarkets, kosher school meals in Jewish schools, online delivery.
  • Pressure points: campaigns to ban slaughter without stunning; eating with non-Jewish friends.
Mission 6Evaluate

"The food laws are still important for Jews today."

  • For: they are mitzvot in the Torah, given by God, so they do not expire; they make daily life holy (Leviticus 11:45); they keep Jewish identity strong in a non-Jewish society; they teach self-control.
  • Against (some Jews): Reform and Liberal Jews see many ritual laws as belonging to an earlier time; ethics, such as how animals and workers are treated, may matter more than which animal is eaten.
  • Against (practical): cost and access make full observance hard for some British Jews.
  • Another view: a law can keep its importance even when its reason is not stated: obedience itself is the point (chukim).
  • Judge it by: Is the Torah's command binding for all time? Does the law still build holiness and identity? Is a personal-choice approach still "keeping" the law?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Pesach remembers the Exodus: slavery, plagues, the Passover, the Red Sea; "a memorial … for ever" (Exodus 12:14).
  • Chametz is cleared, searched for and burned; matzah is eaten; the Seder plate, Haggadah and four questions retell the story.
  • A pilgrim festival in Temple times; today kept by Orthodox, Reform and secular Jews in different ways.

Mission 2

  • Sukkot: harvest and the wilderness huts (Leviticus 23:42–43); the sukkah and the four species (Leviticus 23:40); Simchat Torah.

Mission 3

  • Brit Milah on the eighth day continues God's covenant with Abraham (Genesis 17); the mohel performs it, the sandek holds the baby, and the child is named.
  • Girls are named in the synagogue or at a simchat bat; Bar Mitzvah (13) and Bat Mitzvah (12 or 13) mark adulthood in Jewish law.
  • Orthodox girls may have a Bat Chayil and do not read from the Torah in mixed services; Reform and Liberal ceremonies are the same for girls and boys.

Mission 4

  • A Jewish wedding: ketubah, chuppah, wine, ring (kiddushin), seven blessings, breaking the glass; Genesis 2:24 pictures marriage as "one flesh".
  • Mourning moves in stages: aninut and burial, shiva, sheloshim, the year (avelut), then yahrzeit each year; the Chevra Kaddisha prepares the body and Kaddish praises God.
  • Divorce is allowed but needs a get; without it, an Orthodox wife is an agunah.

Mission 5

  • Orthodox Judaism gives women different roles, which many see as equal but different; Reform and Liberal Judaism give equal roles, including as rabbis.
  • Orthodox dress follows tzniut (modesty): kippah, tzitzit, covered hair for married women; Haredi and Hasidic dress is more distinctive.
  • Jews have faced persecution from the Middle Ages to the Shoah and today; responses include remembrance, education and interfaith work.
  • Jonathan Sacks (1948–2020) taught covenant, responsibility and the dignity of difference, and contributed to Judaism and to wider society.

Mission 6

  • Kosher = fit; treifah = forbidden. Land animals need split hoofs and cud-chewing; fish need fins and scales (Deuteronomy 14; Leviticus 11).
  • Meat comes from shechitah with the blood removed; meat and milk are never mixed; parev foods go with either; a kosher kitchen keeps two sets.
  • Orthodox Jews see kashrut as binding; Reform Jews as informed choice; in Britain it brings identity and holiness but also cost and planning.
