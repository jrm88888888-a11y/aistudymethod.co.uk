Mission 1

What GDP counts

  • Gross domestic product (GDP): the total value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a period (usually a year or a quarter).
  • Final goods: bought by the end user. Intermediate goods (steel for a car, flour for a baker) are not added separately: their value is already in the final price.
  • Within a country: a foreign-owned factory's output counts where the factory is.
  • Not counted:
    • second-hand sales (the car was counted when it was new)
    • transfer payments (pensions, unemployment benefits): money moves, nothing is produced
    • buying shares or bonds: a change of ownership, not production
  • Say: "final goods and services" — not: "everything that is sold".
Quick check
?Which of these is counted in a country's GDP for this year?
Mission 1

Three measures, one total

  • The circular flow: every unit spent on output becomes someone's income.
  • So national output, income and expenditure measure the same flow:
    • Output method: add the value added by every industry.
    • Income method: add factor incomes: wages, rent, interest, profit.
    • Expenditure method: add spending on final output: C + I + G + (X − M).
  • In practice the data come from different surveys, so the totals differ slightly: a statistical discrepancy.
  • Say: "national output = national income = national expenditure" — not: three different sizes of economy.

Mission 1

GDP, GNI and NNI

  • GDP measures output produced in the country, whoever owns it.
  • Gross national income (GNI) = GDP + net income from abroad.
    • Net income from abroad = income residents earn abroad − income foreign owners earn here.
  • Ireland, 2017: GDP 294.1 billion euros; GNI 234.2 billion euros. Much of the gap is profit earned by foreign-owned firms in Ireland and paid out to their owners abroad.
  • So GNI can be the better guide to the income of a country's residents.
Quick check
?Illustrative data (billions). A country's GDP is 500. Its residents earn 30 from investments and work abroad. Foreign owners earn 45 from firms in the country. What is the country's GNI?
Mission 1

Nominal and real

  • Nominal GDP: output valued at current prices. It rises if output rises or if prices rise.
  • Real GDP: output valued at constant prices. Only changes in the quantity of goods and services move it.
  • Real GDP strips out inflation, so it is the measure of economic growth.
  • Rough rule: real growth ≈ nominal growth − inflation (exact method next).
  • Say: "real GDP" when you mean output — not: "GDP went up, so output went up".
Mission 1

Worked example: the GDP deflator

  • The GDP deflator is a price index for everything in GDP. Base year = 100.
  • Real GDP = nominal GDP × 100 ÷ deflator.
  • Illustrative data (billions):
Nominal GDPDeflatorReal GDP (Year 1 prices)
Year 1800100800
Year 2880105838.1
  • Year 2 real GDP = 880 × 100 ÷ 105 = 838.1.
  • Nominal growth = (880 − 800) ÷ 800 × 100 = 10%.
  • Real growth = (838.1 − 800) ÷ 800 × 100 = 4.76%: about half the headline rise was higher prices.
  • Real GNI is found the same way: nominal GNI × 100 ÷ deflator.
Mission 1Graph lab

Money up, output up?

Illustrative data. The money flow around the economy = price level × volume of output.

  • Takeaway: nominal GDP can rise with no extra output at all; only real GDP tracks the quantity produced.
Mission 1

Per person, per worker, per year

  • Real GDP per capita = real GDP ÷ population. A bigger total can hide a smaller share per person.
  • Illustrative data, from the deflator example: Year 1, 800 billion ÷ 40 million people = 20,000 per person. Year 2, 838.1 billion ÷ 41 million = 20,441: up only 2.2%.
  • The same applies to real GNI per capita.
  • Rate of economic growth = (real GDP this year − real GDP last year) ÷ real GDP last year × 100.
  • Always use real figures, and divide by the earlier year.
Mission 1

Purchasing power parity (PPP)

  • Comparing countries needs one currency. Market exchange rates ignore that prices differ between countries.
  • PPP exchange rate: the rate at which the same basket of goods and services costs the same in both countries.
  • Illustrative data. A basket costs 100 US dollars in the US and 3,000 crowns in Country B.
  • PPP rate = 3,000 ÷ 100 = 30 crowns per dollar. The market rate is 80 crowns per dollar.
  • Country B's GDP per capita: 240,000 crowns.
    • At the market rate: 240,000 ÷ 80 = 3,000 dollars.
    • At PPP: 240,000 ÷ 30 = 8,000 international dollars.
  • Prices in B are lower, so B's income buys more than the market rate suggests.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. Country P's GDP per capita is 45,000 pesos. The market exchange rate is 25 pesos per US dollar. The PPP rate is 15 pesos per international dollar. What is P's GDP per capita at PPP?
Mission check

?Illustrative data. A country's nominal GDP rose from 2,000 billion to 2,120 billion. Over the same year, its GDP deflator rose from 100 to 108. What happened to real GDP?

A bigger money figure is not more output. Remove the rise in prices first.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How can one number tell you how much prices have risen for a whole country of shoppers?

Make a guess: How can one number tell you how much prices have risen for a whole country of shoppers?

Mission 2

Inflation, deflation, disinflation

  • Inflation: a sustained rise in the general price level. The inflation rate is its % change, usually over 12 months.
  • Deflation: a sustained fall in the general price level. The inflation rate is negative.
  • Disinflation: the inflation rate falls but stays positive. Prices still rise, just more slowly.
  • Real data: UK CPI inflation was 11.1% in October 2022 and 2.0% in May 2024. That fall was disinflation: prices kept rising.
  • Say: "inflation fell, so prices rose more slowly" — not: "inflation fell, so prices fell".
Mission 2

Index numbers and the base year

  • An index number shows a value relative to a base year, which is set to 100.
  • Index = (value ÷ value in base year) × 100.
  • Illustrative data: a price is 2.00 in the base year, 2.50 a year later, 2.60 the year after.
  • Index: 100 → 125 → 130.
  • Change from the base year: read it straight off: 125 means 25% higher.
  • Change between two later years: % change, not points. 125 → 130 is a 5-point rise but (130 − 125) ÷ 125 × 100 = 4%.
  • Index numbers have no units, so series in different units or currencies can be compared.
  • Say: "the index rose by 5 points, a 4% rise" — not: "the index rose by 5%".
Mission 2

Measuring inflation with a basket

  • Statisticians pick a basket of goods and services that typical households buy.
  • They record the prices of the basket's items every month, in many shops and places.
  • A household spending survey shows how much households spend on each item. These shares become the weights.
  • Big spending shares (e.g. housing, food) get big weights: their price changes move the index more.
  • The consumer price index (CPI) tracks the cost of this basket over time.
  • The CPI is the most common measure of inflation around the world; each country's statistics office runs its own basket.
Mission 2

Worked example: the cost of a fixed basket

  • Illustrative data. A basket of three items; the quantities stay fixed. Year 1 is the base year.
ItemQuantityPrice Y1Price Y2Price Y3

  • CPI: Y1 = 100; Y2 = 270 ÷ 250 × 100 = 108; Y3 = 283.5 ÷ 250 × 100 = 113.4.
  • Inflation Y2: (108 − 100) ÷ 100 × 100 = 8%.
  • Inflation Y3: (113.4 − 108) ÷ 108 × 100 = 5%.
  • The quantities purchased are the weights: each price change counts in proportion to spending on that item. The phone plan is 48% of base-year spending (120 ÷ 250), so its 5% rise adds 2.4 points to the index in year 2.

Mission 2

Real versus nominal

  • Nominal (money) value: measured in the prices of the time (current prices).
  • Real value: adjusted for inflation, measured in the prices of one year (constant prices).
  • Real value = nominal value × (price index in base year ÷ price index in current year).
  • Illustrative data: weekly pay rises from 400 to 520 while the CPI rises from 100 to 135.
  • Real pay now, in base-year prices: 520 × 100 ÷ 135 = 385.19.
  • Nominal pay is up 30%. Real pay is down 3.7%: prices rose faster than pay.
  • Shortcut: real growth ≈ nominal growth − inflation. It works well only for small changes (here: 30 − 35 = −5, against an exact −3.7).
  • Say: "real pay fell" — not: "pay fell", when nominal pay rose.
Mission 2

Real GDP and the GDP deflator

  • The GDP deflator is a price index for all goods and services in GDP, not just a consumer basket.
  • Real GDP = nominal GDP ÷ GDP deflator × 100.
  • Illustrative data: nominal GDP = 2,200 billion; deflator = 110 → real GDP = 2,200 ÷ 110 × 100 = 2,000 billion.
  • The same method turns nominal GNI into real GNI.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A worker's weekly pay rises from 500 to 540. Over the same period, the consumer price index rises from 120 to 132. What happened to the worker's real pay?
Mission 2

Limits of the CPI

  • Substitution bias: when one item's price jumps, people switch to cheaper alternatives. A fixed basket misses this, so it overstates the rise in the cost of living.
  • New products: new goods enter the basket only after a delay.
  • Quality changes: a dearer phone may also be a much better phone. Not all of the price rise is inflation.
  • Not your basket: the CPI tracks an average household. Students, pensioners and renters spend differently, so face different inflation rates.
  • Weights lag behind: they come from past spending surveys and are updated only from time to time (often yearly).
  • Real data: in 1996 the US Boskin Commission estimated that the CPI overstated the rise in the cost of living by about 1.1 percentage points a year; about 0.4 points of this was substitution bias.
  • Say: "the CPI measures changes in the price of a basket" — not: "the CPI measures each person's cost of living".
Quick check
?The price of beef rises by 30% while the price of chicken does not change. Many households buy less beef and more chicken. The consumer price index uses a fixed basket bought in the base year. What is the most likely effect on the measured inflation rate?
Mission check

?Illustrative data. In one year, food prices rise by 10% and all other prices stay the same. Household X spends 40% of its budget on food. Household Y spends 10% on food. In the country's consumer price index, food has a weight of 20%. Which set of inflation rates is correct?

A price index is only as good as its weights. Each household has its own inflation rate, and the CPI is the average one.

Mission 3: Can a country's income tell you how well its people really live?

Make a guess: Can a country's income tell you how well its people really live?

Mission 3

Income per person

  • Living standards: how well people live.
    • Material: the goods and services they can consume.
    • Non-material: health, education, safety, free time, a clean environment.
  • Total GDP shows the size of an economy, not how well-off its people are.
  • GDP per capita = GDP ÷ population: average output, and income, per person.
  • GNI per capita adds net income from abroad, so it can be a better guide to residents' income.
Mission 3

Comparing over time

  • Use real GDP per capita: "real" removes inflation; "per capita" removes population growth.
  • Illustrative data:
Real GDPPopulationReal GDP per capita
Year 1600 billion30 million20,000
Year 2612 billion30.3 million20,198
  • Real GDP grew 2%, but output per person grew only about 1%.
  • Over long periods, take care:
    • quality improves: a phone today does far more than one 20 years ago, so GDP may understate gains.
    • new products and changes in working hours are not fully captured.
Mission 3

Comparing countries: PPP

  • To compare, convert to one currency (usually US dollars).
  • Market exchange rates move with trade and capital flows, and ignore that prices differ between countries.
  • Purchasing power parity (PPP): an exchange rate at which the same basket of goods and services costs the same in both countries.
  • Illustrative data. A basket costs 50 US dollars in the US and 1,000 zeds in Country Z. PPP rate = 20 zeds per dollar. Market rate = 50 zeds per dollar.
  • Z's GDP per capita is 150,000 zeds:
    • at the market rate: 3,000 dollars
    • at PPP: 7,500 international dollars
  • Services such as haircuts and rent are usually cheaper in lower-income countries, so PPP figures raise their incomes.
  • Say: "GDP per capita at PPP" for living standards — not: at market exchange rates.
Quick check
?Country K's GDP per capita is 2,000 US dollars at market exchange rates and 6,000 international dollars at PPP. What best explains the gap?
Mission 3

What GDP misses

  • Nonmarket output: cooking, cleaning, caring for children or relatives, home repairs, volunteering. It is produced but not sold, so it is not in GDP.
  • Hidden economy: work not reported, to avoid tax or rules.
  • Leisure: GDP can be the same whether people work 35 or 50 hours a week.
  • Environment: pollution and lost resources are not subtracted; spending to clean up adds to GDP.
  • Health, education, safety and freedom are not measured directly.
  • Distribution: per capita is an average; it does not show who gets the income.
  • Say: "GDP measures market output" — not: "GDP measures welfare".
Quick check
?A parent leaves a paid job to look after their own child at home. The family stops paying for childcare. Nothing else changes. What happens to measured GDP?
Mission 3

Average or typical? Mean and median

  • GDP per capita is a mean: total ÷ number of people.
  • The median is the middle income when incomes are ranked: the typical person.
  • Illustrative data. Five incomes (thousands): 10, 15, 20, 25, 80.
    • Mean = 30. Median = 20.
  • Next year only the top income rises, to 130.
    • Mean = 40 (up 33%). Median = 20 (unchanged).
  • So GDP per capita can rise while the typical person is no better off.
Mission 3

Measuring well-being directly

  • OECD Better Life Index: 11 dimensions (housing, income, jobs, community, education, environment, civic engagement, health, life satisfaction, safety, work-life balance). Users choose the weights.
  • World Happiness Report (Happiness Index), annual since 2012: countries ranked by people's own rating of their lives from 0 (worst possible) to 10 (best possible), from the Gallup World Poll.
  • Happy Planet Index: combines well-being and life expectancy, then divides by ecological footprint. It rewards long, happy lives with low resource use.
  • Limits: self-reported answers vary with culture, language and mood; choosing weights is a judgement.
Mission 3

Does more income buy more happiness?

  • Richard Easterlin (1974): within a country, richer people report being happier on average.
  • But as average US income rose after the Second World War, average reported happiness barely changed. This is the Easterlin paradox.
  • Possible reasons:
    • relative income: people compare themselves with others
    • adaptation: people get used to higher incomes
  • The other side: Betsey Stevenson and Justin Wolfers (2008) found that life satisfaction rises with income, between countries and over time.
  • The debate continues. Most economists agree income matters, but it is not all that matters.
Quick check
?A government wants a headline measure that rewards long, happy lives achieved with low use of the planet's resources. Which measure fits this aim best?
Mission 3Evaluate

How useful is GDP per capita for comparing living standards?

It depends on:

  • What you compare: between countries, use PPP; over long periods, quality and new products are hard to capture.
  • The nonmarket and hidden economy: larger in some countries, so their output is understated more.
  • Distribution: the same mean can hide very different median incomes.
  • What else differs: working hours, health, environment, freedom.
  • But: GDP data are regular, follow international rules (the UN System of National Accounts), and tend to go with better health and education.
  • So: use GDP per capita alongside other indicators, not alone.
Mission check

?Real GDP per capita in Country P is 20% higher than in Country Q, measured in the same prices. Which finding would most weaken the claim that people in P have a higher material living standard?

GDP counts only what is bought and sold, so compare like with like before judging who lives better.

Mission 4: Why does an economy grow in bursts and slumps instead of at a steady pace?

Make a guess: Why does an economy grow in bursts and slumps instead of at a steady pace?

Mission 4

The trend and the cycle

  • Plot real GDP against time.
  • The trend line shows potential output: it rises steadily as productive capacity grows (long-term growth).
  • Actual real GDP wobbles above and below the trend: short-term fluctuations.
  • These ups and downs are the business cycle.
  • Say: "actual output fell below potential" — not: "potential output fell", when the economy has spare capacity.
Mission 4

The phases of the cycle

  • Boom: fast growth, actual output above trend. Peak: the top.
  • Downturn (slowdown): growth falls. Recession: real GDP falls.
  • Trough: the bottom. Recovery (upturn): growth returns.
  • Common definition of a recession: real GDP falls for two consecutive quarters.
  • Say: "a slowdown: growth falls but stays positive" — not: "a recession".

Mission 4

Output gaps

  • Output gap = actual output − potential output.
  • Deflationary (recessionary) gap: actual below potential.
  • Spare capacity, idle machines, cyclical unemployment. Downward pressure on inflation.
  • Inflationary gap: actual above potential.
  • Overtime, shortages of workers, capacity stretched. Upward pressure on wages and prices. It cannot last.
Mission 4

Worked example: the size of the gap

  • Illustrative data.
  • Potential output = 2,000 bn. Actual real GDP = 1,950 bn.
  • Gap = 1,950 − 2,000 = −50 bn.
  • As % of potential: −50 / 2,000 × 100 = −2.5%.
  • A deflationary gap of 2.5% of potential output.
  • Two years later, actual = 2,030 bn and potential = 2,000 bn: gap = +30 bn = +1.5% of potential.
Mission 4

Why output swings: shocks and instability

  • Demand shocks shift AD: a collapse in confidence, a financial crisis, falling export demand, a big change in government spending.
  • Supply shocks shift SRAS: a sharp rise in oil prices, a pandemic, a harvest failure.
  • Real case: UK real GDP fell for five quarters in a row and shrank by more than 6% between early 2008 and mid-2009. It took five years to regain its pre-recession size (ONS, 2018).
Quick check
?Illustrative data: potential output is estimated at 800 bn. Actual real GDP is 776 bn. Which statement is correct?
Mission check

?A surge in confidence and borrowing has kept a country's actual output above its potential output for two years. Unemployment is below its natural rate. Which is most likely to happen next?

When demand pushes output above what the economy can sustain, inflation tends to rise. When it falls below, workers and machines sit idle.

Make a guess: Two countries earn the same income per person. How can one be far more developed?

Mission 5

Growth is not the same as development

  • Economic growth: an increase in real GDP (output) over time.
  • Economic development: an improvement in people's well-being and living standards. It is multidimensional:
    • higher incomes and less poverty
    • better health and longer lives
    • more education and skills
    • more freedom and choice (Amartya Sen: development as expanding what people are able to do and be)
  • Growth can happen without much development: the gains may go to a few, or come with pollution.
  • Development can happen with slow growth: cheap public health and schooling can lengthen and improve lives.
  • Say: "growth is a rise in real output; development is a rise in well-being" — not: "development means growth".
Mission 5

Monetary indicators

  • GDP per capita: output per person. GNI per capita adds net income from abroad.
  • Compare countries at purchasing power parity (PPP): it corrects for differences in price levels (prices tend to be lower in poorer countries).
Mission 5

Non-monetary indicators

  • Health: life expectancy at birth, infant mortality rate, maternal mortality.
  • Education: adult literacy rate, years of schooling.
  • Inequality: Gini coefficient, income share of the richest 10%.
  • Energy: share of people with access to electricity, energy use per person.
  • Environment: carbon dioxide emissions per person, access to clean water and sanitation.
  • One indicator alone gives only part of the picture.
Quick check
?A government wants one indicator to track whether its new clinics are reducing deaths among babies. Which is best?
Mission 5

The Human Development Index (HDI)

  • Published by the UN Development Programme (UNDP) since 1990.
  • Three dimensions, four indicators:
    • a long and healthy life: life expectancy at birth
    • knowledge (education): expected years of schooling for children; mean years of schooling for adults
    • a decent standard of living: GNI per capita at PPP
  • Each dimension is scored from 0 to 1, then combined. The HDI runs from 0 to 1.
  • UNDP groups: very high (0.800 and above), high (0.700–0.799), medium (0.550–0.699), low (below 0.550).
  • Say: "HDI measures health, education and income" — not: "HDI measures income".
Mission 5

Worked example: same income, different HDI

  • Illustrative data. Countries P and Q both have GNI per capita of 10,000 international dollars (PPP).
Life expectancyExpected schoolingMean schooling
P72 years13.5 years9 years
Q59 years12 years6 years
  • Each dimension is scaled between a minimum and a maximum (life expectancy: 20 to 85 years):
    • Health: P (72 − 20) ÷ 65 = 0.800; Q (59 − 20) ÷ 65 = 0.600
    • Education: P 0.675; Q 0.533
    • Income: both 0.696
  • HDI: P = 0.722 (high); Q = 0.606 (medium).
  • Same income, but P's people live longer and learn more.
  • Two design features:
    • Income enters as a logarithm: doubling P's income to 20,000 raises HDI only to 0.756. An extra dollar matters more to the poor.
    • The three scores are combined by a geometric mean, so a very low score in one dimension pulls the HDI down more than a simple average would.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. Country S has a GNI per capita at PPP far higher than Country T's, but T has the higher HDI. What best explains this?
Mission 5

Adjusting HDI: inequality, gender and the planet

  • Inequality-adjusted HDI (IHDI): HDI discounted for inequality in each dimension.
    • With perfect equality, IHDI = HDI. The bigger the gap between them, the more unequal the country.
  • Gender Inequality Index (GII): from 0 (equality) to 1. The higher the GII, the worse the inequality. Three dimensions:
    • reproductive health: maternal mortality, births to teenage mothers
    • empowerment: women's share of seats in parliament; secondary education by gender
    • labour market: labour force participation by gender
  • Happy Planet Index: well-being and life expectancy, divided by ecological footprint. It rewards long, happy lives with low resource use.
  • The OECD Better Life Index and the World Happiness Report also measure well-being directly (see Mission 3).
Quick check
?Illustrative data. Countries V and W have the same HDI of 0.75. V's IHDI is 0.70; W's IHDI is 0.55. What can you conclude?
Mission 5

Why development is hard to measure

  • Data quality: large informal and subsistence sectors; surveys are costly and infrequent.
  • Averages hide distribution: a rising mean can leave the poor behind.
  • Value judgements: which dimensions count, and how to weight them?
  • Missing dimensions: freedom, security, culture and the environment are hard to put in numbers.
  • PPP estimates depend on price surveys that may be out of date.
Mission 5

How growth and development are linked

  • Growth can fund development:
    • higher incomes for families
    • more tax revenue for schools, clinics, water and roads
  • Development can fuel growth: healthier, better-educated workers are more productive.
  • But growth may not bring development if:
    • the gains go to a few (rising inequality)
    • growth comes from exporting a resource that employs few people
    • it damages health and the environment
  • So the link is likely but not automatic: it depends on how the gains are shared and spent.
Mission 5Evaluate

Is HDI a better measure of development than GNI per capita?

It depends on:

  • The purpose: HDI captures health and education; GNI per capita at PPP better shows material living standards.
  • Distribution: both are averages.
  • The IHDI adjusts HDI for inequality.
  • What is left out: HDI has no measure of inequality, freedom, the environment or happiness.
  • Weights: HDI weights its three dimensions equally. That is a judgement, not a fact.
  • So: use HDI as a better starting point than income alone, alongside other indicators.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. Over 20 years, Country X's GNI per capita at PPP doubled, mainly from oil exports owned by a small group. Life expectancy and years of schooling barely changed. Which conclusion is best supported?

Income is one part of development, so the same money can buy very different lives.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • GDP: the value of final goods and services produced within a country in a period.
  • Output, income and expenditure measure the same flow; value added avoids double counting.
  • GNI (GNP) = GDP + net income from abroad.
  • Real GDP = nominal GDP × 100 ÷ deflator. Only real GDP measures growth in output.
  • Growth rate = change in real GDP ÷ earlier year × 100; per capita = ÷ population.

Mission 2

  • Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level; disinflation is a fall in the inflation rate while prices still rise; deflation is a falling price level.
  • A price index compares the cost of a weighted basket with its cost in a base year (= 100). Inflation is the % change in the index, not the change in points.
  • Real values remove inflation: real = nominal × (base-year index ÷ current index). The CPI has limits, including substitution bias, quality change and its average-household basket.

Mission 3

  • Real GDP per capita is the usual starting point for comparing living standards.
  • Between countries, convert at PPP, not market exchange rates.
  • GDP leaves out nonmarket output.
  • It also misses the hidden economy, leisure, the environment and distribution.
  • Well-being measures and the Easterlin paradox ask whether more income means happier lives.

Mission 4

  • Actual real GDP fluctuates around a rising trend (potential output): the business cycle.
  • Phases: boom, peak, downturn, recession, trough, recovery.
  • Output gap = actual − potential. Below potential: spare capacity. Above potential: inflationary pressure.
  • Demand and supply shocks cause the swings.

Mission 5

  • Growth is a rise in real output; development is a rise in well-being on many dimensions.
  • HDI combines health, education and income (GNI per capita at PPP) into one index from 0 to 1.
  • IHDI and GII adjust for inequality and for gender gaps.
  • No single number captures development: use several indicators.
