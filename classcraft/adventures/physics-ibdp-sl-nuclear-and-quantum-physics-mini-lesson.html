Mission 1

Mission 1: Can a nucleus decay three times and end up as the same element it started as?

Make a guess: Can a nucleus decay three times and end up as the same element it started as?

Mission 1

Unstable nuclei

  • Some nuclei are unstable. They decay: they emit radiation and become more stable.
  • The emitted particle or photon is the radiation: α, β or γ.
  • The type of decay is set by the isotope: carbon-14 decays by β⁻; carbon-11 by β⁺.
Mission 1

Meet the radiations

  • α particle: a helium-4 nucleus. 2 protons + 2 neutrons. Charge +2e. Symbol ⁴2He or ⁴2α.
  • β⁻ particle: a fast electron from the nucleus. Charge −e. Symbol ⁰₋1e or ⁰₋1β.
  • β⁺ particle: a fast positron, the electron's antiparticle. Charge +e. Symbol ⁰₊1e or ⁰₊1β.
  • γ ray: a high-energy photon. No charge, no mass.
Mission 1

Balancing decay equations

  • Top numbers (nucleon number, A) add up the same on both sides.
  • Bottom numbers (proton number or charge, Z) add up the same on both sides.
  • An electron counts as ⁰₋1e: no nucleons, charge −1.
Mission 1

Worked example: α and β⁻

  • α decay: ²³⁸92U → ²³⁴90Th + ⁴2He
  • A: 238 = 234 + 4 ✓. Z: 92 = 90 + 2 ✓.
  • New element: Z falls by 2 (uranium → thorium). A falls by 4.
  • β⁻ decay: ¹⁴6C → ¹⁴7N + ⁰₋1e + ν̄
  • ν̄ is an antineutrino: uncharged, almost no mass, emitted with the β⁻ particle.
  • A: 14 = 14 + 0 ✓. Z: 6 = 7 + (−1) ✓.
  • Inside: a neutron → proton. Z rises by 1; A unchanged.
Quick check
?A nucleus has nucleon number A and proton number Z. It undergoes an alpha decay, and the nucleus formed then undergoes a beta-minus decay. Which expression gives the number of neutrons in the final nucleus?
Mission 1

β⁺ and γ decay

  • β⁺ decay: ¹⁸9F → ¹⁸8O + ⁰₊1e + ν
  • Inside: a proton → neutron. Z falls by 1; A unchanged.
  • γ decay: after α or β decay the new nucleus is often left excited. It drops to a lower energy state by emitting a γ photon.
  • ⁶⁰28Ni* → ⁶⁰28Ni + γ. A and Z do not change. Same nuclide, less energy.
Mission 1

Neutrinos

  • β⁻ decay emits an antineutrino ν̄; β⁺ decay emits a neutrino ν.
  • Both are uncharged, have tiny mass, and very rarely interact with matter.
Quick check
?Sodium-22 (²²11Na) decays by β⁺ emission. The daughter nucleus is left in an excited state and then emits a γ photon. What is the final nucleus?

A detector moved along the surface above the pipe records a much higher count rate above one point. Which statement explains this reading?
Quick check
?Predict first. With no radioactive source near it, a detector still records a count rate. Which statement about this background count rate is correct?
Mission 1

Background radiation

  • A detector counts even with no source present: the background count.
  • Natural: radon gas from rocks and soil, rocks and building materials, cosmic rays, food and drink (e.g. potassium-40).
  • Artificial (smaller): medical uses, past weapons tests, the nuclear industry.
  • Correction: measure the background (long count, no source). Subtract it from every reading.
  • Corrected count rate = measured count rate − background count rate.
Mission check

?Thorium-232 (²³²90Th) decays through a chain of α and β⁻ decays to stable lead-208 (²⁰⁸82Pb). How many α decays and how many β⁻ decays are there in the chain?

An α decay lowers Z by 2; two β⁻ decays raise it by 2. So α, β⁻, β⁻ returns a nucleus to the same element: an isotope with 4 fewer nucleons.

Mission 2

Mission 2: A helium nucleus is lighter than the protons and neutrons that make it. Where did the missing mass go?

Make a guess: A helium nucleus is lighter than the protons and neutrons that make it. Where did the missing mass go?

Mission 2

Mass is a form of energy

  • E = mc²: every mass m has a rest energy mc².
  • c² is huge: 1 kg ↔ 9.0 × 10¹⁶ J.
  • Every energy change changes mass, chemical ones too; there Δm is far too small to measure.
  • Nuclear reactions: Δm is big enough to measure. The energy released leaves as kinetic energy of the products and as photons.
Mission 2

Units for tiny masses

  • Atomic mass unit: 1 u = 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom.
  • 1 u = 1.661 × 10⁻²⁷ kg.
  • 1 MeV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ J.
  • 1 u ↔ 931.5 MeV: so 1 u = 931.5 MeV c⁻².
  • A mass in MeV c⁻²: multiply by c² and you have the energy in MeV. No c needed.
Quick check
?Radium-226 decays by α emission: ²²⁶88Ra → ²²²86Rn + ⁴2α. The atomic masses are 226.025410 u for radium-226, 222.017578 u for radon-222 and 4.002603 u for helium-4 (the electron masses cancel). Take 1 u = 1.661 × 10⁻²⁷ kg = 931.5 MeV c⁻², 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J and c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. How much energy is released in the decay of one radium-226 nucleus?
Quick check
?Predict first. Which statement correctly describes the binding energy of a nucleus?
Mission 2

Missing mass and binding energy

  • A nucleus has less mass than its separate protons and neutrons.
  • Mass defect: Δm = (total mass of separate nucleons) − (mass of nucleus).
  • Binding energy = Δm c²: the energy needed to separate the nucleus completely into its nucleons.
  • The same energy was released when the nucleus formed. That is where the "missing" mass went.
  • Binding energy is not stored in the nucleus. More binding = less mass.
Mission 2

Worked example: helium-4

  • Data: mp = 1.007276 u, mn = 1.008665 u, helium-4 nucleus 4.001506 u.
  • Nucleons: 2(1.007276) + 2(1.008665) = 4.031882 u.
  • Δm = 4.031882 − 4.001506 = 0.030376 u.
  • Binding energy = 0.030376 × 931.5 = 28.30 MeV.
  • Per nucleon: 28.30 ÷ 4 = 7.07 MeV.
  • Use nuclear masses (or atomic masses on both sides, so the electrons cancel).
Mission 2

The binding energy per nucleon curve

  • Binding energy per nucleon = binding energy ÷ nucleon number A. Higher = more tightly bound = more stable.
  • Rises steeply for light nuclei (²H: 1.1 MeV), with a spike at ⁴He (7.1 MeV).
  • Peak near A ≈ 56–62 (iron, nickel): about 8.8 MeV.
  • Falls slowly for heavy nuclei: ²³⁸U about 7.6 MeV.
  • Energy is released when the products have more total binding energy than the reactants:
  • Fusion: light nuclei (left of the peak) join.
  • Fission: heavy nuclei (right of the peak) split.
  • Energy released = (total binding energy after) − (total binding energy before). Total = A × (per nucleon value).
Quick check
?Approximate binding energies per nucleon are: ²³⁶92U 7.59 MeV; ⁹²36Kr 8.51 MeV; ¹⁴¹56Ba 8.33 MeV. A ²³⁶92U nucleus splits into a ⁹²36Kr nucleus, a ¹⁴¹56Ba nucleus and three free neutrons. What is the energy change?
Mission check

?In a fusion reaction, a helium-3 nucleus and a deuterium nucleus fuse: ³2He + ²1H → ⁴2He + ¹1H. The atomic masses are ³He: 3.016029 u, ²H: 2.014102 u, ⁴He: 4.002603 u and ¹H: 1.007825 u (the electron masses on the two sides cancel). How much energy is released in one reaction? Use 1 u = 1.661 × 10⁻²⁷ kg and c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.

The missing mass left as energy when the nucleus formed. For helium-4, 0.0304 u went out as 28.3 MeV, and the same 28.3 MeV is needed to pull it apart again.

Mission 3

Mission 3: If half a radioactive sample decays in an hour, why doesn't the rest decay in the next hour?

Make a guess: If half a radioactive sample decays in an hour, why doesn't the rest decay in the next hour?

Quick check
?Predict first. Radioactive decay is described as random and spontaneous. Which statement correctly describes what this means?
Mission 3

Random and spontaneous

  • Random: you cannot predict which nucleus decays next, or when.
  • Spontaneous: nothing outside triggers it. Temperature, pressure and chemistry make no difference.
  • Every undecayed nucleus of an isotope has the same, constant chance of decaying per second. A nucleus does not "age".
  • With huge numbers of nuclei, the fraction that decays in a given time is predictable.
Mission 3

Half-life

  • Half-life: the time for the number of undecayed nuclei of an isotope to halve.
  • Same half-life whatever the starting amount: each nucleus has the same chance.
  • After n half-lives, the fraction left is (½)ⁿ: ½, ¼, ⅛, 1/16 …
  • The decay curve never quite reaches zero.
  • Half-lives range from far less than a second (polonium-214: 164 μs) to billions of years (uranium-238: 4.5 × 10⁹ years).
Mission 3

Worked example: whole half-lives

  • A sample has 6.4 × 10⁶ undecayed nuclei. Half-life 2.0 hours.
  • After 8.0 hours: 8.0 ÷ 2.0 = 4 half-lives.
  • Left: 6.4 × 10⁶ × (½)⁴ = 6.4 × 10⁶ ÷ 16 = 4.0 × 10⁵.
  • Decayed: 6.4 × 10⁶ − 4.0 × 10⁵ = 6.0 × 10⁶.
  • Read the question: "left" or "decayed"?
  • Dating: wood with ⅛ of the living carbon-14 level is 3 half-lives old: 3 × 5730 = 17 190 years.
Quick check
?A hospital prepares a dose of technetium-99m (half-life 6.0 h) that has an activity of 800 MBq at 08:00. What will its activity be at 14:00 the next day?
Mission 3

Activity and count rate

  • Activity A: the number of nuclei that decay per second. Unit: becquerel, 1 Bq = 1 decay per second.
  • Count rate: the number of decays a detector records per second (or minute).
  • Count rate < activity: the detector catches only some of the radiation.
  • Both are proportional to the number of undecayed nuclei, so both halve every half-life.
Mission 3

Measuring a half-life

  • Measure the background count rate first.
  • Record the count in equal time intervals. Subtract the background from each: corrected count rate.
  • Plot corrected count rate against time.
  • Read the time to fall from any value to half of it. Repeat from several starting points; average.
  • If you forget to subtract the background, the curve levels off at the background, not zero, and the half-life you read off is too long.
Quick check
?The background count rate in a laboratory is 20 counts min⁻¹. A detector near a source records 340 counts min⁻¹ at t = 0 and 100 counts min⁻¹ at t = 6.0 h, background included both times. What is the half-life of the source?
Quick check
?Carbon-14 has a half-life of 5730 years. Equal masses of carbon from living wood and from charcoal at an ancient campsite give background-corrected count rates of 80 and 5.0 counts per minute. Assuming that the proportion of carbon-14 in living wood has not changed, how old is the charcoal?
Mission check

?A sample of radon-222 is sealed in a tube. What is the probability that a given nucleus in the original sample has decayed after three half-lives?

Each surviving nucleus keeps the same chance of decaying. So in the next hour, half of what is left decays: a quarter of the original sample, not the other half.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • α: ⁴2He, A − 4, Z − 2. β⁻: neutron → proton, Z + 1. γ: no change to A or Z.
  • β⁺: proton → neutron, Z − 1. β⁻ emits an antineutrino, β⁺ a neutrino.
  • α: most ionising, least penetrating; γ: least ionising, most penetrating.
  • Subtract the background from every count.

Mission 2

  • Every energy change changes mass: E = mc². Nuclear reactions release energy because the products have less mass.
  • Binding energy = mass defect × c², the energy needed to separate a nucleus into its nucleons; more binding means less mass.
  • Binding energy per nucleon peaks near iron: fusing light nuclei and splitting heavy nuclei both release energy.

Mission 3

  • Decay is random and spontaneous; each nucleus has a constant chance of decaying.
  • Half-life: the time for N to halve. After n half-lives, (½)ⁿ is left.
  • Activity (Bq) and count rate halve each half-life; subtract the background before reading a half-life.
