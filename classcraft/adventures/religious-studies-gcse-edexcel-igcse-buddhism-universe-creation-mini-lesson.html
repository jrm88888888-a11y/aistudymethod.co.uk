Mission 1

Kamma is intention

  • Kamma (Sanskrit karma): "action". For Buddhists it means intentional action, of body, speech or mind.
  • The Buddha (paraphrase): it is intention that I call kamma.
  • Actions are like seeds; their results (vipaka) are the fruit, in this life or later ones.
  • Buddhists teach that no god rewards or punishes: kamma works as a natural law of cause and effect.
  • Not everything that happens is kamma: the Buddha also named illness, weather and other people's actions as causes.
  • Say: kamma is action and its results — not: kamma is fate.
Mission 1

Skilful and unskilful

  • Buddhists judge actions as skilful (kusala) or unskilful (akusala), not as obeying or breaking a god's command.
  • Unskilful actions come from the three poisons: greed, hatred, ignorance (delusion).
  • Skilful actions come from their opposites: generosity, loving-kindness, wisdom.
  • Skilful action leads towards happiness and the path; unskilful action to suffering.
  • Avijja: ignorance, not seeing things as they are; the root of the other poisons.
  • Kilesa: the "defilements" (greed, hatred, delusion and their offshoots) that cloud the mind. Selfishness grows from them.
  • "Sin": Buddhists rarely speak of sin against God; wrong action is unskilful, rooted in ignorance, and can be changed.

Mission 1

Rebirth and samsara

  • Samsara: the endless cycle of birth, death and rebirth.
  • Buddhists believe kamma shapes the circumstances of rebirth, and of this life too.
  • The Wheel of Life shows six realms: gods, jealous gods, humans, animals, hungry ghosts, hell beings. None is permanent.
  • Not a soul moving house (anatta): like a flame lit from another, the new life is connected to the old but not identical.
  • Buddhists teach that ending craving ends rebirth: nibbana.
Mission 1

Merit

  • Merit (punna): the good kamma made by skilful action.
  • Three bases of merit: giving (dana), ethics (sila), meditation (bhavana).
  • Lay Buddhists make merit by giving food to monks and nuns, keeping the precepts, chanting and meditating.
  • Transfer of merit: many Buddhists share merit with others, for example with relatives who have died.

Mission 1

Free, but not unlimited

  • Kamma conditions a person; it does not fix them.
  • Freedom: each moment brings a new intention. This is why the path is possible.
  • Limits: past kamma, the body, upbringing, and avijja (ignorance) shape what we see as options.
  • Craving makes us less free; wisdom and meditation make us more free.
  • Nibbana is described as complete freedom: from craving, and from samsara.
Mission 1

Karuna: compassion

  • Karuna: compassion: feeling others' suffering as real and wishing, and working, to relieve it.
  • The Buddha taught for 45 years out of compassion for all beings.
  • Karuna and wisdom are like the two wings of a bird: both are needed to fly.
  • Mahayana places karuna at the heart of the bodhisattva ideal.
  • All living beings share dukkha and want happiness: this commonality is a reason for compassion towards animals too.
Mission 1

Metta: loving-kindness

  • Metta (Sanskrit maitri): loving-kindness, warm goodwill wishing all beings to be happy, enemies included.
  • > "For hatred does not cease by hatred at any time: hatred ceases by love, this is an old rule." (Dhammapada 5, Müller 1881)
  • Metta and karuna are two of the four "divine abodes" (brahmaviharas), with sympathetic joy and equanimity.
  • Practised in metta bhavana meditation (see the Meditation mission).

Mission 1

An impermanent world, shared

  • The universe is impermanent: always changing, arising and passing, with no fixed beginning taught.
  • All living beings are in the same cycle: in samsara, any being may once have been your mother or child.
  • So Buddhists feel responsibility for the planet, based on:
  • compassion: harm to the environment causes suffering to countless beings;
  • wisdom: everything is interdependent; greed for resources is a poison, not a solution.
  • Examples: Buddhist environmental projects, such as monks in Thailand "ordaining" trees to protect forests.
Mission 1Diversity

Kamma and compassion in different traditions

  • Theravada: strong emphasis on merit-making, especially giving to the monastic Sangha, and transferring merit to the dead.
  • Mahayana: bodhisattvas dedicate all their merit to the awakening of all beings.
  • Pure Land: Amida's merit, not one's own, brings birth in the Pure Land.
  • Tibetan: compassion trained deliberately, e.g. seeing every being as having once been one's mother.
  • Some Western and secular Buddhists (e.g. Stephen Batchelor, Buddhism Without Beliefs, 1997) stay agnostic about rebirth, while keeping kamma as a guide to how actions shape character now.
Mission 1Evaluate

"Buddhists should be good out of compassion, not to gain good kamma."

  • Reasons for: merit-seeking can become a subtle form of craving for oneself. The bodhisattva gives without thought of reward. Karuna aims at others' good.
  • Reasons against: the Buddha encouraged making merit; it supports lay practice and the Sangha. Kamma is simply how actions work, not a bribe. Wanting a good rebirth to keep practising is itself wise.
  • Another view: the motives can mix: an act done from compassion is skilful kamma.
  • Judge it by: Does motive matter more than outcome in Buddhism? Theravada or Mahayana? Monastic or lay life?

Mission 2

What does "Dhamma" mean?

  • Dhamma is Pali; Dharma is Sanskrit. Buddhists use it in three main ways.
  • 1. The Buddha's teaching: the Four Noble Truths, the Eightfold Path and all he taught.
  • 2. The truth about how things are: a natural law the Buddha discovered, not one he invented. It would be true even if no Buddha taught it.
  • 3. dhammas (small d): the passing events of body and mind that make up experience.
  • The Buddha compared his teaching to a raft: something to cross the river with, not to carry on your head afterwards (Majjhima Nikaya 22, paraphrase).

Mission 2

Dependent arising

  • Spelling: paticca-samuppada; also called dependent origination or conditionality.
  • The idea: everything arises from conditions, and ceases when those conditions cease.
  • The Buddha's short formula (e.g. Majjhima Nikaya 79, paraphrase): when this is, that is; when this arises, that arises. When this is not, that is not; when this ceases, that ceases.
  • Example: a flame burns only while there is fuel and air. Take one away and it goes out.
  • So nothing exists on its own, unchanging and independent.
Quick check

Mission 2

Why it matters: samsara and kamma

  • Samsara: the endless cycle of birth, death and rebirth, driven by craving and ignorance.
  • Kamma (karma): intentional actions of body, speech and mind. They shape a being's circumstances in this life and future lives.
  • Rebirth is a chain of conditions, not a soul moving house: like one candle lighting another (Milinda Panha, paraphrase).
  • Suffering has conditions. Remove the conditions (craving, ignorance) and suffering ceases: nibbana.
  • So dependent arising explains both how suffering keeps going and how it can end.
  • The Buddha also set dependent arising out as a chain of twelve links (nidanas), from ignorance to ageing and death.
  • "Whoever sees dependent arising sees the Dhamma" (Majjhima Nikaya 28, paraphrase).
Mission 2

The Wheel of Life

  • The Wheel of Life (Sanskrit bhavacakra) is a picture of samsara, painted at the entrance of many Tibetan monasteries.
  • Held by Yama, a fearsome figure for death and impermanence: nothing in the wheel escapes change.
  • Hub: three animals for the Three Poisons (see Mission 6).
  • Second ring: beings rising (light half) and falling (dark half) according to their kamma.
  • Third ring: the six realms of rebirth: gods, demi-gods, humans, animals, hungry ghosts, hell beings.
  • Rim: twelve pictures of the twelve nidanas (e.g. a blind person for ignorance, a potter for formations).
  • Outside: the Buddha points to the moon: nibbana, the way out.
  • The gods in the wheel are also inside samsara: they are not creators and they too will die and be reborn.

Mission 2

Ultimate reality and the question of God

  • The Buddha did not teach a creator God. Dependent arising needs no first cause.
  • He left some questions unanswered: whether the world is eternal, whether it has an end, what happens to a Buddha after death.
  • The poisoned arrow (Majjhima Nikaya 63, paraphrase): a man shot by an arrow refuses treatment until he knows who shot it and what the bow was made of. He would die first. Such questions do not help end suffering.
  • So many Buddhists are agnostic or uninterested in whether God exists; what matters is the path.
  • Ultimate reality for Buddhists: nibbana, the unconditioned, beyond change. Mahayana Buddhists also speak of emptiness (sunyata) as the true nature of all things.
Mission 2Diversity

Different understandings

  • Theravada: the Dhamma is the teaching preserved in the Pali Canon. The twelve links are usually read over three lifetimes (past, present and future: Buddhaghosa, Visuddhimagga, 5th century CE).
  • Mahayana: Nagarjuna (c. 150–250 CE), Mulamadhyamakakarika 24.18: whatever arises dependently is empty (sunyata) of fixed existence (paraphrase).
  • Many Tibetan and modern teachers also read the twelve links moment by moment: one angry thought can run round the whole chain.
  • Zen: the Dhamma is realised directly in experience, "outside the scriptures", through meditation.
Mission 2Evaluate

"Dependent arising is the key to all the Buddha's teaching."

  • Reasons for: it explains suffering (conditions), rebirth (no soul needed) and impermanence (everything changes). Majjhima Nikaya 28 links seeing dependent arising with seeing the Dhamma. Nagarjuna builds Mahayana thought on it.
  • Reasons against: the Buddha's first sermon taught the Four Noble Truths, not the twelve links. The Dhamma is a raft for practice: meditation and ethics may matter more than theory. Pure Land Buddhists stress faith in Amitabha Buddha.
  • Another view: the Four Noble Truths are dependent arising applied to suffering, so the two cannot be separated.
  • Judge it by: Which teaching explains the most? Which changes how Buddhists live? Do ordinary lay Buddhists need the theory?

Mission 3

Three marks of everything

  • The Buddha taught that everything conditioned has three marks (Pali tilakkhana):
  • anicca: impermanence;
  • dukkha: unsatisfactoriness, suffering;
  • anatta: no fixed self.
  • They follow from dependent arising (see Mission 2): what depends on conditions must change, cannot fully satisfy, and has no independent core.
Mission 3

Anicca: everything changes

  • Anicca: nothing stays the same. Things arise, change and pass away.
  • Big changes: seasons, ageing, death, mountains wearing away.
  • Small changes: your body's cells, your thoughts and moods, moment by moment.
  • Even things that seem solid are processes.
  • The universe itself is in a constant process of change. Because all beings share this condition, Buddhists stress the commonality of all living beings and our responsibility, out of compassion and wisdom, to care for the planet.
Mission 3

Dukkha: nothing fully satisfies

  • Dukkha is often translated "suffering", but it is wider: unsatisfactoriness.
  • Pain is dukkha. But so is a happy moment, because it will end.
  • Because everything is impermanent (anicca), nothing can give lasting satisfaction.
  • This is not saying "life is miserable". It is saying: do not expect permanent happiness from things that change.
Mission 3

Anatta: no fixed self

  • Anatta: there is no fixed, permanent self or soul at the core of a person.
  • It does not mean you do not exist. You exist as a changing process, like a river or a flame.
  • Many religions of the Buddha's time taught an eternal soul (atman). The Buddha rejected this.
  • Buddhists still believe in rebirth: what continues, they say, is a stream of causes and effects, not a soul (see Mission 2).

Mission 3

The five khandhas

  • If there is no fixed self, what is a person? The Buddha's answer: five khandhas ("heaps", "aggregates") working together.
  • 1. rupa: form, the body;
  • 2. vedana: feelings, pleasant, unpleasant or neutral;
  • 3. sanna: perception, recognising things;
  • 4. sankhara: mental formations, such as will and motivation;
  • 5. vinnana: consciousness, awareness.
  • Your spec's names: body, feelings, recognition, motivation, conscious awareness.
  • All five are always changing. None of them is a fixed self.
Mission 3Read the source

Nagasena and the chariot

From the Milinda Panha ("Questions of King Milinda"), Book II, chapter 1 (paraphrase). King Milinda, a Greek ruler in north-west India, asks the monk Nagasena his name.

  • Nagasena: "I am called Nagasena, but that is only a name. No permanent person is found here."
  • He asks the king how he came. "By chariot."
  • "Is the pole the chariot? The axle? The wheels? The frame? The reins?" No, says the king, to each.
  • "Is the chariot something apart from these parts?" No.
  • The king explains: "chariot" is a name used for these parts put together.
  • Nagasena: just so, "Nagasena" is a name for the khandhas put together. Beyond them there is no fixed self.

Mission 3Diversity

Theravada and Mahayana

  • All Buddhists accept the three marks. They develop them differently.
  • Theravada: anatta is seen by analysing a person into the khandhas and seeing each one change. Meditation (vipassana, insight) looks at the marks directly.
  • Mahayana: extends no-self to everything: all things are empty (sunyata) of fixed existence.
  • Many Mahayana schools also teach Buddha-nature: every being can become a Buddha. Most Mahayana teachers say this is a potential, not a hidden self.
  • Both speak of two levels of truth: in everyday speech "I" and "you" are fine; in ultimate terms there is no fixed self.
Mission 3

Why the marks matter

  • Letting go: if everything changes, clinging only brings disappointment. Buddhists try to enjoy things without grasping.
  • Facing loss: at many Theravada funerals monks chant that all conditioned things are impermanent. Death is part of anicca.
  • Compassion: if there is no fixed self to protect, there is less reason for selfishness; others' suffering matters as much as one's own.
  • Practice: insight meditation contemplates the marks, to weaken craving.
  • Responsibility: all living beings share impermanence and suffering, so Buddhists care for animals and the environment.
Mission 3Evaluate

"If there is no fixed self, there is no reason to be good."

  • Reasons for: if no self continues, who receives the results of kamma? King Milinda himself asks this. Some argue moral responsibility needs a lasting person.
  • Reasons against: a process continues, like a flame passed between candles; kamma shapes that stream. Seeing no-self weakens selfishness, which supports kindness. Suffering is real whoever feels it, so it still matters to reduce it.
  • Another view: Buddhists use "self" in everyday truth: people are responsible for their actions, even if no self exists ultimately.
  • Judge it by: Does moral responsibility need an unchanging self? Does belief in no-self change behaviour? Do the two levels of truth answer the objection?

Mission 4

Five heaps

  • The Buddha described a person as five khandhas ("heaps", aggregates). Sanskrit: skandhas.
  • Rupa: form, the body and the senses.
  • Vedana: feeling or sensation: pleasant, unpleasant or neutral.
  • Sanna: perception: recognising and labelling.
  • Sankhara: mental formations: intentions, habits, choices. This is where kamma is made.
  • Vinnana: consciousness: awareness through each sense.
  • Your spec's names: body, feelings, recognition, motivation, conscious awareness.
Mission 4

The heaps at work

  • A dog barks behind you.
  • Form: ears and sound waves.
  • Feeling: unpleasant (a jolt).
  • Perception: "That's a dog."
  • Mental formations: fear, the urge to step away, an old habit of disliking dogs.
  • Consciousness: awareness of the hearing.
  • All five arise and pass in moments. Nowhere in the list is a sixth thing, an "owner".

Heaps, marks and no owner

  • Anatta: none of them, alone or together, is a fixed, lasting self. Like Nagasena's chariot: a name for parts working together.
  • Dukkha: clinging to changing heaps as "me" leads to dissatisfaction.

Mission 5

Four truths, like a doctor

  • The Buddha's first sermon, at Sarnath, taught the Four Noble Truths (Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta, Samyutta Nikaya 56.11):
  • 1. dukkha: there is suffering;
  • 2. samudaya: suffering has a cause (craving, tanha);
  • 3. nirodha: suffering can end;
  • 4. magga: there is a path to its end, the Eightfold Path.
  • Buddhists often compare the Buddha to a doctor: diagnosis (the illness), cause, prognosis (it can be cured), treatment.
  • This is the core of the Buddha's teaching, given first and repeated for 45 years.
Mission 5Read the source

The First Truth

Samyutta Nikaya 56.11, Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta (paraphrase):

  • Birth is dukkha; ageing is dukkha; sickness is dukkha; death is dukkha.
  • Sorrow, grief, pain and despair are dukkha.
  • Being with what you dislike is dukkha; being parted from what you love is dukkha.
  • Not getting what you want is dukkha.
  • In short, the five khandhas that we cling to are dukkha.

Q1 Which items on the list match the Four Sights?

Show answer

Ageing, sickness, death.

Q2 Which item covers even a happy life?

Show answer

The five khandhas we cling to: everything we are is impermanent.

Mission 5

What "dukkha" means

  • Dukkha has no single English word. Translations include suffering, unsatisfactoriness, stress, dis-ease.
  • It covers pain and grief, but also the restlessness of never being fully satisfied.
  • The First Truth is a fact to understand, not a feeling to wallow in.
  • Buddhists believe it applies to all beings in samsara, not just humans: animals, ghosts and even gods.
Mission 5

Three types of dukkha

  • 1. Dukkha-dukkha: ordinary pain, of body and mind: a broken arm, grief, fear.
  • 2. Viparinama-dukkha: the suffering of change: pleasant things end, so even happiness carries loss.
  • 3. Sankhara-dukkha: the suffering of conditioned existence: everything is made of changing parts (khandhas), so there is a deep, built-in unsatisfactoriness.

Mission 5

Realistic, not pessimistic

  • Buddhists say the First Truth is realistic: it names what everyone faces.
  • The Truths do not stop at suffering: the Third says it can end; the Fourth shows how.
  • Understanding dukkha is the first step to compassion (karuna): all beings suffer, just as I do.
  • Meaning and purpose of life: to understand dukkha and find release from it, for oneself and others.
  • Buddhists do not face the problem of reconciling suffering with an all-loving creator God. Suffering arises from conditions: craving and ignorance.
Mission 5Diversity

Can every being be freed?

  • All Buddhists agree that dukkha can end. They differ in emphasis.
  • Theravada: each person must walk the path; the goal is to become an arahant, freed from suffering. The Buddha shows the way; he cannot walk it for you.
  • Mahayana: all sentient beings have Buddha-nature and can be liberated. The Bodhisattva vows to work until all beings are freed.
  • Pure Land: the Buddha Amitabha vowed to bring all who call on him in faith to his Pure Land, where awakening is easier.

Mission 5Evaluate

"Buddhism is a pessimistic religion."

  • Reasons for: the first teaching is about suffering. Even happiness is called dukkha. Birth itself is on the list.
  • Reasons against: the First Truth is a diagnosis; a doctor who names an illness is not pessimistic. The Third Truth says suffering can end; the Fourth gives a practical path. The Dhammapada calls nibbana "the highest happiness" (verse 204).
  • Another view: Buddhists call their outlook realistic: neither ignoring suffering nor despairing of it.
  • Judge it by: Does naming suffering make a view pessimistic? Do Buddhists live with hope? Which of the Four Truths is the "last word"?

Mission 6

The Second Truth: samudaya

  • Samudaya means "arising" or "origin": suffering has a cause.
  • The Buddha named the cause tanha: literally "thirst", usually translated craving.
  • Craving is wanting things to be other than they are: holding on to what changes, pushing away what is unpleasant.
  • Because everything is impermanent (anicca), craving can never be fully satisfied. So it keeps producing dukkha.
  • Craving also keeps beings in samsara: it drives rebirth.
Mission 6

Three kinds of craving

  • 1. Kama-tanha: craving for sense pleasures: tastes, sights, sounds, comfort.
  • 2. Bhava-tanha: craving to be or become: to exist, to be someone, to be admired.
  • 3. Vibhava-tanha: craving not to be: to get rid of something, to escape, to disappear.
  • Even wanting something to stop can be craving.
  • Selfishness grows from craving: putting "me" first, as if there were a fixed self to protect.

Mission 6Read the source

The Sermon at Benares

The first sermon was given in the Deer Park near Benares (Varanasi): the Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta, Samyutta Nikaya 56.11. Its Second Truth (paraphrase):

  • The origin of suffering is craving, which leads to renewed existence (rebirth).
  • It comes with delight and passion, seeking pleasure now here, now there.
  • It is craving for sense pleasures, craving for existence, craving for non-existence.

Q1 Name the three kinds of craving in the sutta.

Show answer

Sense pleasures; existence; non-existence.

Q2 What does craving lead to, beyond suffering in this life?

Show answer

Renewed existence: rebirth.

Mission 6

The Three Poisons

  • Craving grows from three roots of unskilful action, called the Three Poisons (or three fires):
  • lobha: greed, attachment, grasping;
  • dosa: hatred, aversion, anger;
  • moha: ignorance, delusion, confusion.
  • Ignorance is the deepest: not seeing the three marks; treating what changes as permanent, and a changing process as a fixed self.
  • From ignorance come greed (grabbing what we think will last) and hatred (pushing away what threatens "me").
Mission 6

The poisons in the Wheel of Life

  • At the very hub of the Wheel of Life (see Mission 2) are three animals:
  • a pig: ignorance (moha);
  • a rooster (cockerel): greed (lobha);
  • a snake: hatred (dosa).
  • In many paintings each animal bites the tail of the next: the poisons feed each other in an endless circle.
  • At the hub, they drive the whole wheel: kamma, the six realms and the twelve links all turn around them.
  • Message: to stop the wheel, deal with the poisons at the centre.

Mission 6

Avijja, kilesa and kamma

  • Avijja: ignorance; the first of the twelve links (see Mission 2). Not lack of facts, but not seeing things as they really are.
  • Kilesa: the defilements: greed, hatred and delusion and the states that grow from them (pride, envy, laziness). They cloud the mind like dirt in water.
  • Kamma: intentional actions rooted in the kilesa lead to suffering, now and in future lives.
  • Sin: most Buddhists do not speak of sin against God. They speak of unskilful (akusala) actions, which harm self and others.
  • Selfishness, greed and ignorance are therefore not punished by anyone; they bring their own results.

Mission 6

Different readings, and the antidotes

  • Each poison has a positive opposite:
  • greed → generosity (dana) and letting go;
  • hatred → loving-kindness (metta) and compassion (karuna);
  • ignorance → wisdom (panna, Sanskrit prajna).
  • Buddhists train in these: giving to monks and charities, metta bhavana meditation, study and insight meditation.
Mission 6Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain two Buddhist teachings about the causes of suffering.

  • Teaching 1: suffering is caused by tanha (craving). Source: the Buddha's first sermon (SN 56.11) says craving leads to renewed existence. Development: because everything is impermanent, craving can never be satisfied, so Buddhists try to let go.
  • Teaching 2: the Three Poisons, greed, hatred and ignorance, are the roots of unskilful action. Source: "hatred ceases by love" (Dhammapada 5). Development: so Buddhists practise metta to replace hatred with loving-kindness.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • In Buddhist teaching, kamma is intentional action; skilful actions (rooted in generosity, love, wisdom) and unskilful ones (rooted in greed, hatred, ignorance) shape this life and rebirth in samsara.
  • Merit comes from giving, ethics and meditation; Buddhists teach that nibbana ends rebirth altogether.
  • Karuna (compassion) and metta (loving-kindness) are central virtues; compassion needs wisdom.

Mission 2

  • Dhamma means the Buddha's teaching and the truth about reality; dependent arising teaches that everything arises from conditions and ceases when they cease.
  • Samsara is kept turning by craving and ignorance; kamma shapes rebirth; ending the conditions ends suffering (nibbana).

Mission 3

  • The three marks: anicca (impermanence), dukkha (unsatisfactoriness), anatta (no fixed self).
  • A person is five changing khandhas; Nagasena's chariot shows that "self" is a name for parts working together.
  • Theravada analyses the khandhas; Mahayana extends no-self to emptiness (sunyata); the marks lead to letting go and compassion.

Mission 4

  • A person is five changing khandhas: form, feeling, perception, mental formations, consciousness; none is a fixed self (anatta), all change (anicca), and clinging to them brings dukkha.
  • Your names for the khandhas: body, feelings, recognition, motivation, conscious awareness.

Mission 5

  • The First Noble Truth: there is dukkha (suffering, unsatisfactoriness), taught in the first sermon (SN 56.11).
  • Three types: dukkha-dukkha (pain), viparinama-dukkha (change), sankhara-dukkha (conditioned existence; Edexcel "fabrication").
  • Buddhists see the truth as realistic, not pessimistic; Theravada and Mahayana differ on emphasis, e.g. the Bodhisattva vow to free all beings.

Mission 6

  • The Second Noble Truth (samudaya): suffering is caused by tanha (craving) for sense pleasures, for existence and for non-existence.
  • The Three Poisons: greed (lobha), hatred (dosa) and ignorance (moha); ignorance is the deepest root.
