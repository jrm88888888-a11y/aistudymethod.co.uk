Mission 1

Mission 1: How can a fatty streak inside an artery wall end in a heart attack years later?

Make a guess: How can a fatty streak inside an artery wall end in a heart attack years later?

Mission 1

The heart feeds itself

  • Heart muscle needs a constant supply of oxygen and glucose.
  • It gets them from the coronary arteries, which branch from the aorta.
  • Blood inside the chambers does not supply the heart wall.
  • Coronary heart disease (CHD): coronary arteries narrowed or blocked.
  • Say: "occlusion starves the heart muscle of oxygen" — not: "blood can no longer pass through the chambers".
Mission 1

Atherosclerosis, step by step

  • (1) Endothelial dysfunction: the inner lining is damaged, e.g. by high blood pressure, smoking or high LDL cholesterol.
  • (2) Inflammatory response: white blood cells enter the wall and take up LDL cholesterol.
  • (3) Plaque (atheroma) builds inside the wall: cholesterol, white cells, fibrous tissue, calcium salts. The lumen narrows; the wall stiffens.
  • (4) Narrower, stiffer arteries raise blood pressure, which damages more endothelium.
  • Atherosclerosis: hardening and narrowing of arteries by atheroma.
  • Years later, a plaque in a coronary artery can rupture; a clot forms and blocks the artery. Heart muscle beyond it is starved of oxygen and dies: a heart attack.
  • Say: "atheroma forms within the artery wall" — not: "fat sticks to the inside like grease in a drainpipe".
Mission 1

What blockage does

  • Narrowed coronary artery: too little oxygen during exercise → chest pain (angina).
  • Blocked coronary artery: muscle beyond dies → myocardial infarction (heart attack).
  • Dead muscle is replaced by scar tissue, which does not contract.
  • A clot in an artery to the brain → stroke.
Mission 1

Fats, cholesterol and arteries

  • Saturated fats: no C=C double bonds; mostly solid (butter, lard). Unsaturated: one or more C=C; mostly liquid (olive, sunflower oil).
  • Diets high in saturated fat raise blood LDL cholesterol.
  • LDL carries cholesterol to tissues; high LDL feeds atheroma in artery walls.
  • HDL carries cholesterol back to the liver; high HDL goes with lower risk.
  • Replacing saturated with unsaturated (especially polyunsaturated) fat lowers LDL and the rate of cardiovascular events.
  • Cholesterol is still needed: membranes and steroid hormones are made from it.
Quick check
?A person replaces butter with sunflower oil, keeping total fat intake the same. Why is this expected to lower their risk of cardiovascular disease?
Mission check

?In familial hypercholesterolaemia, an inherited condition, blood LDL cholesterol is very high from birth, even on a low-fat diet. Many people with it develop coronary heart disease young. What does this suggest?

Whether from diet or genes, high LDL in the blood feeds atheroma inside the artery wall.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Every time your cells build a big molecule, they make water. Why?

Make a guess: Every time your cells build a big molecule, they make water. Why?

Mission 2

Monomers and polymers

  • Monomer: a small unit from which larger molecules are made.
  • Polymer: a large molecule made of many monomers joined together.
MonomerPolymer
Monosaccharide (e.g. glucose)Polysaccharide (starch, glycogen, cellulose)
Amino acidPolypeptide (protein)
NucleotidePolynucleotide (DNA, RNA)
  • Triglycerides are built the same way, but are not polymers: one glycerol + three fatty acids, not a repeating chain.
Mission 2

Condensation: building

  • Condensation: joins two molecules with a new covalent bond and eliminates a molecule of water.
  • One monomer loses an –OH, the other an –H. Together: H2O.
  • One water per bond formed.
  • Repeat: monomer → dimer → polymer.
  • Cells use enzymes for each joining reaction.
Mission 2

Hydrolysis: breaking

  • Hydrolysis: breaks a covalent bond between two molecules and uses a water molecule.
  • The water is split: –H goes to one product, –OH to the other.
  • One water per bond broken.
  • Digestion is hydrolysis. Enzymes make it fast; polymers in plain water break down extremely slowly.
  • Say: "condensation releases water; hydrolysis uses water" — not: the other way round.
Quick check
?Two monosaccharides are joined by a condensation reaction to form a disaccharide. What happens in this reaction?
Mission 2

Worked example: counting the water

  • Bonds sit between monomers. An unbranched chain of n monomers has n − 1 bonds.
  • So building it releases n − 1 water molecules; hydrolysing it uses n − 1.
  • Example: 5 glucose molecules joined into one unbranched chain.
  • Glucose Mr = 180; water Mr = 18.
  • Bonds: 5 − 1 = 4. Water released: 4.
  • Mr of chain = 5 × 180 − 4 × 18 = 900 − 72 = 828.
  • Hydrolysis reverses it exactly: 828 + 4 × 18 = 900.
Quick check
?A polypeptide made of 100 amino acids is completely hydrolysed into free amino acids. How many water molecules are used in the process?
Mission 2

One reaction, four kinds of bond

Molecules joinedBond formed by condensationMission
MonosaccharidesGlycosidic bond
Glycerol + fatty acidEster bond
Amino acidsPeptide bond
NucleotidesPhosphodiester bond
  • Every one forms by condensation and breaks by hydrolysis.
  • In the exam you are given structural formulae. Circle the –OH and –H that leave as water, then draw the new bond. You need not draw them from memory.
Mission check

?Seeds store starch: unbranched and branched chains of thousands of glucose units. During germination, enzymes hydrolyse the starch completely to glucose. Starting from 1.00 g of starch, about what mass of glucose forms?
  • Glucose Mr = 180; water Mr = 18.

Building releases one water per link, so breaking down has to put each one back.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Every protein in your body is built from just twenty building blocks. How can so few make so many?

Make a guess: Every protein in your body is built from just twenty building blocks. How can so few make so many?

Mission 3

One plan, twenty versions

  • Amino acids are the monomers of proteins.
  • A central carbon atom carries four groups:
  • Amine group (–NH2), carboxyl group (–COOH), a hydrogen atom, and an R group (side chain).
  • The twenty amino acids in proteins differ only in their R group.
  • The simplest R group is a single hydrogen atom.
  • Say: "amine group, –NH2" — not: "–NH3". Nitrogen's third bond is to the central carbon.
Quick check
?Which feature is shared by all 20 of the amino acids that are coded for in the genetic code?
Mission 3

The peptide bond

  • (1) Line up two amino acids: the carboxyl group of one beside the amine group of the next.
  • (2) –OH from the carboxyl and –H from the amine leave as one water molecule.
  • (3) C joins N: a peptide bond, –CO–NH–. A covalent bond. Two amino acids make a dipeptide.
  • This is a condensation reaction.
  • Hydrolysis reverses it: adding water breaks the peptide bond.
  • Given two formulae: circle the –OH of one –COOH and an H of the other's –NH2. Join C to N.
  • The R groups take no part.
Quick check
?Two amino acids react to form a dipeptide. Which statement describes this reaction?
Mission 3

Worked example: counting bonds and water

  • Many amino acids joined in a chain: a polypeptide.
  • A chain has two ends: a free amine group at one, a free carboxyl group at the other.
  • n amino acids → n − 1 peptide bonds → n − 1 water molecules released.
  • 3 amino acids: 2 bonds, 2 H2O.
  • Masses: glycine (Mr 75) + alanine (Mr 89) → dipeptide.
  • Mr = 75 + 89 − 18 = 146. Subtract one water per bond.
  • Hydrolysis of the chain uses the same number of water molecules.
  • Any of the twenty can sit at any position, repeats allowed, in any order, in chains of any length. So the number of possible sequences is effectively unlimited.
Quick check
?A polypeptide of 150 amino acids is assembled by condensation reactions. How does the number of water molecules change as the whole chain is formed?
Mission check

?Two short polypeptides, P and Q, are each made of the same five amino acids, but the amino acids are joined in a different order in P and in Q. Which statement about P and Q is correct?

Twenty kinds, joined in any order, any number of times, to any length. That is how so few make so many.

Mission 4

Mission 4: A protein is only a chain of amino acids. So how does it fold into the exact shape its job needs?

Make a guess: A protein is only a chain of amino acids. So how does it fold into the exact shape its job needs?

Mission 4

Sequence, shape, job

  • A polypeptide folds into a precise three-dimensional shape.
  • Bonds between parts of the chain hold that shape. Most are weak; together they are strong.
  • The shape decides the function: enzymes, hormones, antibodies, transport, support.
  • The same sequence folds the same way, every time.
  • Different sequences fold differently. So endless sequences give endless proteins.
  • Four levels describe the shape: primary, secondary, tertiary, quaternary.
Mission 4

Primary structure

  • Primary structure: the sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide.
  • Held by peptide bonds. Set by the gene.
  • The sequence, with the exact position of each amino acid, determines every later level.
  • Change one amino acid, anywhere, and the shape can change.
  • Say: "primary structure = the order of amino acids" — not: "which amino acids are present".
Mission 4

Secondary structure

  • The backbone carries polar C=O and N–H groups.
  • Hydrogen bonds form between the C=O of one peptide bond and the N–H of another, at regular positions.
  • α-helix: the chain coils; each turn is held to the next.
  • β-pleated sheet: lengths of chain lie side by side, folded like pleats, held to each other.
  • R groups point away from the backbone and take no part in these bonds, so helices and sheets form in many sequences.
  • Say: "backbone hydrogen bonds" — not: "bonds between R groups".
Quick check
?What stabilises the alpha helices and beta-pleated sheets of a protein's secondary structure?
Mission 4

Tertiary structure: R groups lock the fold

  • The chain, with its helices and sheets, folds further into its overall 3D shape: tertiary structure.
  • Held by bonds between R groups:
  • Hydrogen bonds: between polar R groups. Weak, but many.
  • Ionic bonds: between a –COO⁻ and an –NH3⁺ R group. pH-sensitive.
  • Disulfide bridges: covalent S–S bonds between two cysteines. Strong.
  • Hydrophobic interactions: non-polar R groups cluster together, away from water.
Mission 4

Oily inside, watery outside

  • In a water-soluble protein: non-polar R groups cluster in the core.
  • Polar and charged R groups sit on the surface, in contact with water. So the protein dissolves.
  • Why: water molecules hydrogen-bond to each other and exclude non-polar groups.
  • Say: "non-polar R groups are excluded from water" — not: "they repel water".
Quick check
?In one region of a polypeptide there are two cysteines, an amino acid whose R-group contains a carboxyl group and an amino acid whose R-group contains an amine group. Which statement about the bonds these could form in the tertiary structure is correct?
Mission 4

Quaternary structure

  • Some proteins are built from more than one polypeptide: quaternary structure.
  • The polypeptides are held together by the same kinds of bond as tertiary structure.
  • Example: haemoglobin, four polypeptides (see Mission 5).
  • All four levels together decide what the protein does.
Mission 4

Denaturation

  • (1) A folded protein: its shape held by many weak bonds.
  • (2) High temperature: more vibration breaks hydrogen bonds and ionic bonds. The chain unfolds.
  • (3) pH change: gaining or losing H⁺ changes the charges on R groups. Ionic and hydrogen bonds break.
  • This loss of shape is denaturation. The protein can no longer do its job.
  • Peptide bonds stay intact: the sequence is unchanged.
  • Usually permanent: the chains do not refold correctly. A cooked egg white stays white.
  • Cold does not denature: it slows molecules down, and activity returns on warming.
  • Say: "the enzyme is denatured" — not: "the enzyme is killed".
Quick check
?When egg white is heated, its soluble proteins turn into a white solid. What has happened to the protein molecules?
Mission 4

Enzymes: globular proteins with an active site

  • Enzymes are globular proteins: compact, folded, usually soluble.
  • The active site is a small pocket made of only a few amino acids.
  • But the rest of the folded chain holds those few in exactly the right places.
  • So the active site's shape and properties come from the enzyme's tertiary structure.
  • The substrate binds to the active site, forming an enzyme–substrate complex.
  • Denature the enzyme: the active site changes and the substrate no longer binds.
Quick checkRead the data
?(Illustrative data.) An enzyme's activity at its optimum pH of 7 was 100 units. One sample was placed at pH 2 for 10 minutes, then returned to pH 7: its activity was 4 units after 1 hour and 4 units after 24 hours, and its polypeptide chains were found to be intact. A second sample was cooled to 5 °C for 10 minutes, then returned to 37 °C: its activity was 100 units. What do these results show?
Mission check

?A mutation replaces one amino acid in an enzyme at a position on the surface far from the active site in the folded protein. The enzyme loses most of its activity. What is the best explanation?

Sequence sets shape, and shape sets function. One amino acid in the wrong place can be enough.

Mission 5

Two body plans: globular and fibrous

GlobularFibrous
Shapecompact, roughly sphericallong, parallel chains
In watersoluble: polar R groups on the outsideinsoluble
Jobsenzymes, hormones, transportstrength and support
Exampleshaemoglobin, insulin, enzymescollagen, keratin, elastin
  • Both have a precise, repeatable 3D structure.
  • Say: "fibrous proteins have an elongated structure" — not: "fibrous proteins are unfolded".
Mission 5

Haemoglobin

  • A globular protein of four polypeptides: two α and two β. So it has quaternary structure.
  • Each polypeptide holds one haem group: a ring with an Fe²⁺ ion at its centre.
  • Each haem binds one O2. One haemoglobin carries up to four O2.
  • Haem is not made of amino acids. It is a prosthetic group.
  • Polar R groups on the surface keep it soluble inside red blood cells.
Mission 5

Conjugated and modified proteins

  • Conjugated protein: protein + prosthetic group (a non-protein part).
  • Haemoglobin: prosthetic group haem.
  • Glycoprotein: carbohydrate chains attached, e.g. many cell-surface receptors and the mucins in mucus.
  • After translation, some polypeptides are modified: e.g. carbohydrate added in the Golgi apparatus.
  • Some are combined: several polypeptides join, as in haemoglobin.
  • Some are cut: insulin is made as one longer chain, then cut to leave two.
  • Say: "conjugated = has a prosthetic group" — not: "conjugated = has more than one polypeptide".
Quick check
?A haemoglobin molecule is made of four polypeptides, each holding one haem group that contains an Fe²⁺ ion. Which statement about haemoglobin is correct?
Mission 5

Collagen: built to take a pull

  • Three polypeptides wound round each other: a triple helix.
  • Every third amino acid is glycine, the smallest. So the three chains pack tightly.
  • Hydrogen bonds hold the three chains together. Covalent cross-links join neighbouring molecules into fibrils, then fibres.
  • Long, insoluble, with very high tensile strength.
  • Found in tendons, ligaments, skin, bone, cartilage and artery walls.
Quick check
?Protein X is compact and roughly spherical, dissolves in blood plasma and binds to a specific receptor on target cells. Protein Y forms long insoluble fibres with high tensile strength in tendons. Which statement correctly relates the form of each protein to its function?
Mission check

?Spider dragline silk is a protein. Its long polypeptide chains lie parallel to one another, with many stretches folded into β-pleated sheets that are stacked and held together by hydrogen bonds. It does not dissolve in water. Which statement best explains why it suits the frame of a web?

Long and cross-linked for strength, compact and soluble for work in solution. Shape fits job.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Many foods hide sugar, starch, protein or fat. How can a few drops of reagent tell you which, and how much?

Make a guess: Many foods hide sugar, starch, protein or fat. How can a few drops of reagent tell you which, and how much?

Mission 6

Starch: the iodine test

  • Add a few drops of iodine in potassium iodide solution.
  • Starch present: orange-brown → blue-black.
  • Works on solids or solutions.
  • Eduqas calls it the iodine-potassium iodide test.
  • Iodine slots inside the coiled amylose chain: that is what gives the colour.
Mission 6

Reducing sugars: Benedict's test

  • Reducing sugars: all monosaccharides, and the disaccharides maltose and lactose.
  • (1) Add an equal volume of Benedict's reagent (blue: Cu²⁺ ions).
  • (2) Heat in a boiling water bath for about 5 minutes.
  • (3) Reducing sugar reduces Cu²⁺ to Cu⁺: a brick-red precipitate of copper(I) oxide forms.
  • The more reducing sugar, the further along: blue → green → yellow → orange → brick red.
  • So the colour is semi-quantitative. For a number, use a colorimeter.
Mission 6

Non-reducing sugars

  • Sucrose is a non-reducing sugar: Benedict's stays blue.
  • (1) Take a fresh sample. Boil with dilute hydrochloric acid: this hydrolyses the glycosidic bond.
  • (2) Neutralise with sodium hydrogencarbonate (Benedict's works only in alkaline conditions). Check with pH paper.
  • (3) Benedict's test again. Brick red now: a non-reducing sugar was present.
  • This only works if the first Benedict's test was negative, or gave less precipitate.
Quick check
?A solution gives a negative result with iodine. With Benedict's reagent, heated, it stays blue. A fresh sample is boiled with dilute hydrochloric acid, neutralised, and tested with Benedict's again: a brick-red precipitate forms. What does the solution contain?
Mission 6

Proteins and lipids

  • Biuret test: add biuret reagent (sodium hydroxide, then dilute copper(II) sulphate).
  • Protein present: blue → lilac (purple). The reagent detects peptide bonds.
  • Free amino acids give no lilac: they have no peptide bonds.
  • Emulsion test: shake the sample with ethanol; any lipid dissolves.
  • Pour the ethanol into water. Lipid present: a milky-white emulsion.
  • The lipid comes out of solution as tiny droplets that scatter light.
Quick check
?In the emulsion test, a sample is shaken with ethanol and the ethanol is then poured into water. A milky-white emulsion forms. Why?
Mission check

?A sample of milk is tested. Heated with Benedict's reagent: an orange-red precipitate. With biuret reagent: lilac. With iodine in potassium iodide: stays orange-brown. Which conclusion is supported?

Each reagent answers one question. Choose the tests, read every result, and claim no more than they show.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Atheroma builds inside a damaged artery wall; it narrows the lumen and raises blood pressure.
  • Saturated fat raises LDL cholesterol; replacing it with unsaturated fat lowers LDL.

Mission 2

  • Monomers join by condensation (one water released per bond) and polymers are split by hydrolysis (one water used per bond).

Mission 3

  • Every amino acid: central carbon + amine group + carboxyl group + H + R group; only R differs.
  • Condensation joins carboxyl to amine: a peptide bond and one water per bond; hydrolysis reverses it.

Mission 4

  • The amino acid sequence decides how a chain folds; bonds between parts of the chain hold the shape; the shape decides the job.
  • Primary (sequence) → secondary (backbone H bonds: α-helix, β-pleated sheet) → tertiary (R-group bonds) → quaternary (more than one polypeptide).
  • Heat and pH changes break the weak bonds: the protein is denatured, though its peptide bonds survive.

Mission 5

  • Globular proteins (haemoglobin, insulin, enzymes) are compact and soluble; fibrous proteins (collagen) are long, insoluble and strong.
  • Haemoglobin: four polypeptides, each with a haem prosthetic group holding Fe²⁺; one O2 per haem.

Mission 6

  • Iodine: blue-black = starch. Benedict's, heated: brick-red precipitate = reducing sugar. Biuret: lilac = protein.
  • Non-reducing sugar: acid hydrolysis, neutralise, Benedict's again. Emulsion test: milky = lipid.
