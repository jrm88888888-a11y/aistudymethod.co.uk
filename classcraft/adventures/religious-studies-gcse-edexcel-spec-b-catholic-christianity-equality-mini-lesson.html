Mission 1

Mission 1: Why should every person have rights, and what do those rights ask of you in return?

Make a guess: Why should every person have rights, and what do those rights ask of you in return?

Mission 1

What are human rights?

  • Human rights: basic rights and freedoms that belong to every person simply because they are human.
  • The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR) was adopted by the United Nations on 10 December 1948, after the horrors of the Second World War.
  • "All human beings are born free and equal in dignity and rights." (UDHR, Article 1)
  • Examples: life; liberty; equality before the law and a fair trial; freedom of thought, conscience and religion; freedom of opinion and speech; education; work.
  • In the UK, the Human Rights Act 1998 makes the European Convention on Human Rights part of UK law.
Mission 1

Rights bring responsibilities

  • Every right brings responsibilities: if I have a right to free speech, I must respect your right not to be threatened or slandered.
  • "Everyone has duties to the community" (UDHR, Article 29).
  • Most rights can be limited when they clash with others' rights or public safety: e.g. free speech does not cover inciting violence.
  • Believers add duties to God and neighbour: protect the rights of others, especially the weak.
  • Catholic teaching: rights and duties go together; every right is matched by a duty to respect it in others.

Mission 1Read the source

Made in God's image

"God created man in his own image. In God's image he created him; male and female he created them." (Genesis 1:27)

"you shall love your neighbour as yourself" (Leviticus 19:18)

  • "He who oppresses the poor shows contempt for his Maker, but he who is kind to the needy honours him." (Proverbs 14:31)
  • Imago Dei (image of God): Christians and Jews believe every person, of any race, sex, ability or belief, has equal and God-given dignity.
  • Jesus' parable of the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25–37): your "neighbour" is anyone in need, even an enemy.

Mission 1

Catholic teaching: Gaudium et Spes

  • Gaudium et Spes ("Joy and Hope"), Second Vatican Council, 1965.
  • GS 26 (paraphrase): human rights and duties are universal and inviolable. Everyone must have what a truly human life needs: food, clothing, shelter, the right to choose a state of life and found a family, education, work, a good name, respect, information, the right to act by conscience, privacy, and religious freedom.
  • GS 29 (paraphrase): all people share the same nature and origin, so every kind of discrimination (by sex, race, colour, social condition, language or religion) goes against God's plan and must be overcome.
  • The Common Good and the Catholic Church's Social Teaching (Bishops of England and Wales, 1996), 36–37 (paraphrase): human rights come from the dignity of the person, and the Church supports them.
  • Catholic Social Teaching builds on this: the common good, solidarity, and the preferential option for the poor.
Mission 1Diversity

Christians in action, and different views

  • Examples: Christian Aid and CAFOD campaign for justice for the poor; Martin Luther King (civil rights, USA); Desmond Tutu (against apartheid, South Africa).
  • Situation ethics: if the most loving thing (agape) is the test, protecting people's rights is usually what love requires.
  • Most Christians strongly support human rights.
  • Divergent views: some rights claimed today (e.g. to abortion or assisted dying) are rejected by many Christians as against the sanctity of life; Christians differ among themselves on such claims.

Mission 1Diversity

Islam and Judaism

  • Islam: "Verily we have honoured the Children of Adam." (Qur'an 17:70, Pickthall): every human has dignity from Allah.
  • "let not hatred of any people seduce you that ye deal not justly. Deal justly, that is nearer to your duty." (Qur'an 5:8, Pickthall)
  • Divergent views: many Muslims support human rights; some hold that rights must fit within Shari'ah (e.g. the Cairo Declaration on Human Rights in Islam, 1990), so some rights (e.g. on changing religion) are debated.
  • Judaism: every person is made "in the image of God" (Genesis 1:27, JPS 1917), so every life is of infinite worth.
  • Proverbs 14:31: "he that is gracious unto the needy honoureth Him" (JPS 1917).
  • René Cassin (1887–1976), a French Jewish lawyer, was a principal drafter of the UDHR.
Mission 1Diversity

Hinduism, Sikhism, Buddhism

  • Hinduism: the same atman (self) is in every being, one with Brahman, so all deserve respect.
  • Mahabharata 5.39 (paraphrase): Vidura teaches that one should never do to others what one would find hurtful to oneself. Supporting rights is dharmic duty.
  • A problem some Hindus raise: rights language stresses what I can claim, while dharma stresses what I owe; some traditional duties (e.g. in family roles) can clash with individual rights.
  • Sikhism: one Creator, present in all; all are equal.
  • Guru Granth Sahib 300 is your spec's named Sikh source on human rights. A problem rights can cause: free speech may clash with respect for faith, e.g. Sikh protests in 2004 led a Birmingham theatre to cancel the play Behzti.
  • Buddhism: teaching focuses on duties and compassion rather than "rights", but leads to the same care.
  • Upekkha (equanimity): equal regard for all beings. The International Network of Engaged Buddhists' declaration (1998) links Buddhist non-violence to human rights. Some Buddhists worry rights language can feed self-centredness.
Mission 1

Non-religious views

  • Humanists strongly support human rights: they rest on our shared humanity, reason and the wish for everyone to flourish, not on God.
  • The UDHR itself does not mention God, so people of all beliefs can agree on it.
  • Many atheists and Humanists campaign for rights, e.g. freedom of belief, equality.
  • Religious responses: many welcome the agreement; some argue that without God (imago Dei) human dignity has no firm foundation.
  • Some non-religious people criticise religions where they see teachings that limit rights (e.g. on equality); believers reply that their faiths have also driven rights forward.
Quick check
?Which teaching would a Muslim most likely use to explain why everyone deserves fair treatment?
Mission 1Evaluate

"Human rights only make sense if people are made by God."

  • Reasons for: imago Dei gives every person equal worth that no government can remove (Genesis 1:27). Rights came largely from religious ideas. Without God, rights might seem just a human agreement that can change.
  • Reasons against: Humanists ground rights in shared humanity, reason and wellbeing. The UDHR does not mention God and is accepted across beliefs. Many non-religious people defend rights strongly. Religions have sometimes resisted rights.
  • Another view: people may agree on rights for different reasons; what matters is that they are protected.
  • Judge it by: Where does human worth come from? Can agreement without a shared foundation last?
Mission check

?A café owner refuses to serve a customer because of his religion and says: "It's my right to choose who I serve." Which response best links rights and responsibilities?

Rights belong to everyone because everyone shares the same worth, and that is exactly why they bring a duty to respect them in others.

Mission 2: If religions teach that everyone is equal, why do some still give women and men different roles?

Mission 2

Prejudice and discrimination

  • Prejudice: judging a person or group unfairly, usually before knowing them. It is an attitude (in the mind).
  • Discrimination: treating a person or group unfairly because of a prejudice. It is an action.
  • Equality: every person has the same worth and should have the same rights and chances.
  • Common types: race, religion, gender, sexuality, disability, age, class.
  • UK law: the Equality Act 2010 makes discrimination unlawful on grounds such as race, religion or belief, sex, sexual orientation and disability.
  • Most religions teach that every person has equal worth. Believers disagree over whether equal worth means identical roles, for example in who may lead worship.

"There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free man, there is neither male nor female; for you are all one in Christ Jesus." (Galatians 3:28)

  • Galatians 3:23–29: Paul says that through faith in Christ all are children of God, not through birth or the Jewish law.

Mission 2

Christian teaching and the Churches

  • "God doesn't show favouritism" (Acts 10:34). Showing partiality is a sin (James 2:9).
  • The Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25–37): a despised outsider is the true neighbour.
  • "Love one another. Just as I have loved you" (John 13:34): love that excludes no one.
  • Galatians 2:1–10: the Jerusalem apostles accept Paul's mission to non-Jews without demanding that they become Jews. Christians take this to mean no one should be treated as lesser because of their religious background.
  • Catechism 1934–1935: all people share one nature and one origin, so all have equal dignity; discrimination on grounds of sex, race, colour, social condition, language or religion goes against God's plan (paraphrase).

Mission 2

Equality in Islam and Judaism

  • Islam: "O mankind! Lo! We have created you male and female, and have made you nations and tribes that ye may know one another. Lo! the noblest of you, in the sight of Allah, is the best in conduct." (Surah 49:13, Pickthall)
  • Sahih al-Bukhari 56:681: when a noblewoman stole, the Prophet refused special treatment, saying even his own daughter Fatimah would face the same law (paraphrase). Rich and poor are equal before God's law.
  • Judaism: every person is made in God's image (Genesis 1:27).
  • Ahavat ha-beriot: love of all God's creatures. Leviticus 19:13–18: pay workers on time, do not put a stumbling block before the blind, judge fairly, "love your neighbour as yourself" (19:18).
  • "To show partiality in judgement is not good." (Proverbs 24:23). Jews know the cost of religious prejudice: centuries of antisemitism, which led to the Holocaust (Shoah) and still exists today.
Mission 2

Equality in Buddhism, Hinduism and Sikhism

  • Buddhism: all beings can become enlightened. The Buddha taught that a person is noble or low by their deeds, not their birth (Vasala Sutta, Sutta Nipata 1.7, paraphrase).
  • Lotus Sutra 12: the dragon king's young daughter reaches enlightenment. In the story she first changes into a male form; many Mahayana Buddhists read it as showing that gender and birth are no final barrier, while others see the change as reflecting old limits.
  • The Sutta Nipata warns against clinging to one's own views and looking down on others' (paraphrase): contempt for another religion is a form of attachment.
  • Hinduism: the same atman (eternal self) is in every being.
  • Rig Veda 10.90 (the Purusha Sukta): the four varnas come from one cosmic being. Some read this as a ranking; many Hindus read it as one body whose parts all need each other.
  • Bhagavad Gita 4.9–11: Krishna receives people by whatever path they come to him (paraphrase), so many Hindus say prejudice against other religions is wrong.
  • Sikhism: Guru Nanak taught: recognise God's light in everyone and do not ask about caste (Guru Granth Sahib 349, paraphrase). Langar: all sit and eat together; women may lead any part of worship.

?In a gurdwara's langar, everyone sits on the floor and eats the same food, whatever their wealth, caste or religion. Which Sikh teaching does this show most directly?
Mission 2

The status of women

  • Most religions teach men and women have equal worth. They differ on whether that means the same roles.
  • Catholic and Orthodox Churches: only men are ordained priests, because Jesus chose twelve men as apostles. Women lead in many other roles (religious sisters, teachers, theologians).
  • Church of England: women priests since 1994; women bishops allowed since 2014.
Mission 2Diversity

Difference or discrimination?

  • "Equal but different" (complementarian): men and women have equal worth but different God-given roles. Supporters say this is faithfulness to scripture and tradition, not prejudice.
  • "Equal and the same" (egalitarian): Galatians 3:28 means no role should be closed by gender. Supporters say limits on roles are discrimination.
  • On sexuality: some say the Church can welcome people while teaching that same-sex sexual acts are wrong; others say this still harms people.
  • Non-religious view: Humanists judge rules by their effect on human well-being and equal rights.
Mission 2Evaluate

"Religion does more to fight prejudice than to cause it."

  • Reasons for: core teachings of equal worth (Genesis 1:27; Surah 49:13; Guru Granth Sahib 349); believers led campaigns against slavery, racism and caste; churches, mosques and gurdwaras serve everyone.
  • Reasons against: scripture has been used to justify slavery, caste and unequal treatment of women; some religious groups exclude women from leadership or reject same-sex relationships; religious prejudice (antisemitism, Islamophobia, sectarianism) still causes harm.
  • Situation ethics (Joseph Fletcher, 1905–1991, Situation Ethics, 1966): the only rule is agape (selfless love), so any rule that discriminates should give way.
  • Another view: prejudice comes from people, not from religion; the same is true of non-religious ideologies.
  • Judge it by: Should we judge a religion by its teachings or by its followers' actions? Is "different roles" a form of prejudice?
Mission check

?A religious community teaches that women and men have equal worth, but only men may lead its main act of worship. Two members disagree about whether this is discrimination. What is their disagreement mainly about?

Prejudice is an attitude; discrimination is an action. Religions teach equal worth, but they differ on whether equal worth means identical roles.

Mission 3

What freedom of religion and belief means

  • Freedom of religion and belief: the right to hold any religion or none, to change it, and to practise it.
  • "Everyone has the right to freedom of thought, conscience and religion; this right includes freedom to change his religion or belief" (UDHR, Article 18, 1948).
  • It covers religious expression: worship, teaching, wearing symbols and clothing, keeping festivals and food laws, alone or with others, in private or in public.
  • It protects non-belief too: atheists and Humanists have the same freedom.
  • UK law: the Human Rights Act 1998 (Article 9) and the Equality Act 2010 protect "religion or belief" from discrimination.
Mission 3

Limits on religious expression

  • Article 9 allows limits only where the law sets them and they are necessary: for public safety, order, health, or the rights and freedoms of others.
  • Nadia Eweida (British Airways) won her case to wear a small cross at work (European Court of Human Rights, 2013).
  • Shirley Chaplin, a nurse, lost a similar case: the court accepted a hospital's health-and-safety reasons.
  • Sikhs may carry a kirpan (a small sword, an article of faith) where a "religious reasons" defence applies in law.
  • Freedom of expression also limits religion: in England and Wales, blasphemy is no longer a crime (abolished 2008); using threatening words or behaviour intended to stir up hatred against people because of their religion is (Racial and Religious Hatred Act 2006).
Quick check
?Why did Shirley Chaplin lose her case to wear a cross on a chain at work, when Nadia Eweida won hers?
Mission 3

Catholic teaching on religious freedom

  • Second Vatican Council, Dignitatis Humanae (1965, paraphrase): every person has a right to religious freedom, rooted in human dignity. No one may be forced to act against conscience, or stopped from acting by it, within just limits.
  • Catechism 1738 (paraphrase): the right to exercise freedom, especially in moral and religious matters, is an inalienable requirement of human dignity; the state must respect it within the limits of the common good and public order.
  • Catechism 1747 (paraphrase): freedom belongs to everyone because it is inseparable from human dignity, so it must be respected, especially in religion and morals.
  • Nostra Aetate (1965, paraphrase): the Church rejects nothing that is true and holy in other religions; Catholics should enter dialogue with respect.
  • Catholics also believe the fullness of truth is found in Christ and the Church, so they share their faith, but never by force.
Mission 3Diversity

Islam and Judaism

  • Islam: "There is no compulsion in religion. The right direction is henceforth distinct from error." (Qur'an 2:256, Pickthall)
  • "Unto you your religion, and unto me my religion." (Qur'an 109:6, Pickthall)
  • Surah 2:255–257 sets 2:256 between the Throne Verse (Allah's eternal power over all) and "Allah is the Protecting Guardian of those who believe" (2:257, Pickthall): faith must be freely chosen.
  • Divergent views: many Muslims defend freedom of belief; classical law punished leaving Islam, and some countries still do, while many modern scholars argue there should be no worldly penalty. Muslims differ on whether insulting the Prophet should be limited by law; almost all reject violence.
  • Judaism: Judaism does not actively seek converts; good people of all nations have a share in the world to come (keeping the Noahide laws).
  • Esther: Haman plots against the Jews because "their laws are diverse from those of every people" (Esther 3:8, JPS 1917). Esther risks her life to save them: "if I perish, I perish" (Esther 4:16, JPS 1917). Remembered at Purim.
  • Long experience of persecution leads many Jews to defend freedom of religion for all.
Mission 3Diversity

Hinduism, Sikhism, Buddhism

  • Hinduism: many paths can lead to the divine, so most Hindus accept other faiths.
  • Rig Veda 1.164.46 (paraphrase): truth is one; the wise call it by many names. Bhagavad Gita 4.11 (paraphrase): Krishna says that in whatever way people come to him, he receives them.
  • Sikhism: Guru Tegh Bahadur, the ninth Guru, was executed in Delhi in 1675 after defending the right of Kashmiri Hindus to practise their own faith.
  • Guru Gobind Singh, Dasam Granth 51 (paraphrase): his father gave his head to protect others' right to their own religious marks and practice.
  • Buddhism: the emperor Ashoka (3rd century BCE) ordered respect for all religious groups (Rock Edict XII).
  • The Dalai Lama, Toward a True Kinship of Faiths (2010), ch. 9 (paraphrase): followers of each religion can hold their own as true while respecting others, moving from tolerance to real acceptance.
  • Some Buddhist-majority countries have seen intolerance towards religious minorities; many Buddhists condemn it.
Quick check
?Many Sikhs say Guru Tegh Bahadur's death in 1675 shows Sikh support for religious freedom. Why?
Mission 3Diversity

Living in a multi-faith society

  • Multi-faith society: people of many religions and none living side by side, as in the UK today.
  • Benefits: learning from others; working together (interfaith groups, joint charity work); greater understanding and less prejudice; a richer culture.
  • Challenges: religions make different truth claims; questions about mixed-faith marriages and raising children; tension between sharing your faith (evangelism) and respecting others; prejudice and extremism.
  • Contrasting Christian views: exclusivists believe salvation comes only through Christ and share their faith actively; inclusivists (including Catholic teaching) see truth in other faiths; pluralists see many paths to God.
  • Contrasting views on expression: some Christians and Muslims feel public expression of faith is increasingly restricted; others think current limits are fair.
Mission 3

Non-religious arguments

  • Humanists defend freedom of religion and belief for everyone, including the freedom to criticise or leave religion.
  • They argue against some religious privileges: state-funded faith schools selecting pupils by religion; compulsory collective worship in schools; bishops sitting in the House of Lords; religious exemptions from equality law; blasphemy laws in other countries.
  • Religious responses: faith schools are popular and successful; Christianity shaped British law and culture; freedom of religion includes freedom to run institutions by faith; many believers agree that blasphemy laws are wrong.
  • Both sides often agree: no one should be harmed for their belief, or for having none.
Quick check
?Before 1965 some Catholic writers argued that only the true faith had a right to public expression. What did Dignitatis Humanae teach instead?
Mission 3Evaluate

"Freedom of religious expression should have no limits."

  • Reasons for: belief is central to a person's identity and dignity (Dignitatis Humanae; UDHR Article 18). "No compulsion in religion" (Qur'an 2:256). Limits are often used to persecute minorities (Esther; Guru Tegh Bahadur).
  • Reasons against: some expression harms others (stirring up hatred; extremism). Health and safety matter (Chaplin). Others' rights, including non-believers', must be protected. Humanists: religious freedom should not become privilege.
  • Another view: limits should be as few as possible, set by law, and applied equally to all beliefs.
  • Judge it by: Does the expression harm anyone? Are limits applied fairly to every belief? Who decides what is "necessary"?
Mission check

?A school bans all jewellery. A Sikh pupil asks to wear her kara (a steel bangle worn as an article of faith). Under UK human rights law, when may the school refuse?

Freedom of religion and belief is a basic right for everyone, believers and non-believers alike; it may be limited only when truly necessary to protect others.

Mission 4

Mission 4: If you believe your religion is true, can you also believe other religions are true?

Make a guess: If you believe your religion is true, can you also believe other religions are true?

Mission 4

Three answers

  • Exclusivism: only one religion is true and leads to salvation. Many evangelical Christians cite "I am the way, the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father, except through me." (John 14:6)
  • Inclusivism: one religion holds the fullest truth, but people of other faiths may share in it. The Catholic Church "rejects nothing that is true and holy" in other religions (Nostra Aetate 2, 1965).
  • Pluralism: many religions are valid paths to the same ultimate reality. Philosopher John Hick (1922–2012) argued this in God and the Universe of Faiths (1973).
  • Each religion contains people who hold each view.
Mission 4Read the source

Other faiths in scripture

"There is no compulsion in religion." (Surah 2:256, Pickthall)

"Lo! those who believe, and those who are Jews, and Sabaeans, and Christians - Whosoever believeth in Allah and the Last Day and doeth right - there shall no fear come upon them neither shall they grieve." (Surah 5:69, Pickthall)

  • Rig Veda 1.164.46: "Truth is one; the wise call it by many names" (paraphrase).
  • Bhagavad Gita 4.11: Krishna says he receives people by whatever path they come, for "all men everywhere / Shall fall into my path" (Arnold).
  • Surah 2:255–257: Allah alone is God; no one is to be forced into faith.

Mission 4

How each tradition sees other faiths

  • Christianity: all three views are held. Most agree all people must be loved (Matthew 22:37–40).
  • Islam: Jews and Christians are "People of the Book". The Constitution of Madinah (622) made Muslims, Jews and others of Madinah one community for defence, each keeping its religion.
  • Judaism: non-Jews need not convert: those who keep the seven Noachide laws are righteous. Reconstructionist Judaism rejects the idea that Jews are chosen above others.
  • The story of Esther: a Jewish queen saves her people from a decree to destroy them: a story of survival as a religious minority.
  • Buddhism: the Dhamma is tested by experience; the Dalai Lama argues for real respect between faiths while each keeps its own (Towards the True Kinship of Faiths, 2010).
  • Hinduism: many Hindus are pluralist or inclusivist (Rig Veda 1.164.46); some stress that only true knowledge (vidya) frees from samsara.
  • Sikhism: Guru Nanak taught there is one God of all.
  • Guru Tegh Bahadur gave his life (1675) defending Hindus' freedom to practise their faith (Dasam Granth 51, paraphrase).
Quick check

Mission 4

Inter-faith dialogue in Britain

  • Inter-faith dialogue: people of different religions meeting to understand, respect and work with each other.
  • Four forms: dialogue of life (neighbours), of action (working together), of theological exchange, of religious experience (sharing prayer and silence).
  • Examples: Council of Christians and Jews (1942); Christian Muslim Forum (2006); Hindu Christian Forum UK; local inter-faith councils; Inter Faith Week each November.
  • Why it matters in 21st-century Britain: a multi-faith society; fighting prejudice and extremism; joint work on poverty and the environment; responding together after attacks or disasters.
  • Benefits of a multi-faith society: learning, friendship, freedom to choose. Challenges: mixed marriages, raising children, pressure to blend beliefs, prejudice.
Mission 4

The Catholic Church and dialogue

  • Nostra Aetate (1965): Catholics should respect other religions and work with their followers. Dignitatis Humanae (1965): every person has a right to religious freedom.
  • Catechism 1738 and 1747: the right to freedom, especially in religious and moral matters, comes from human dignity; it is not a right to say or do anything at all (paraphrase).
  • Pope John Paul II invited leaders of many religions to pray for peace at Assisi (1986). Pope Francis and the Grand Imam of al-Azhar signed the Document on Human Fraternity (2019).
  • Catholics hold dialogue and proclamation together: respect for others, while still sharing the Gospel.
Quick check

Mission check

?At an inter-faith week event, a Hindu speaker says: "God is one, and the different religions are like rivers flowing into the same sea." Which view does this express?

Believers answer the question in different ways: exclusivist, inclusivist or pluralist, and all three views are found in most religions.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Human rights belong to every person because they are human (UDHR 1948; Human Rights Act 1998); every right brings responsibilities.
  • Religious basis: for Christians and Jews, imago Dei (Genesis 1:27) and love of neighbour.
  • Muslims: Qur'an 17:70; Hindus: atman in all; Sikhs: one Creator in all; Buddhists: duties and compassion.
  • Catholic teaching: Gaudium et Spes 26 (rights and duties), 29 (no discrimination), 78 (peace as the work of justice).

Mission 2

  • Prejudice is an unfair attitude; discrimination is an unfair action. Religions teach the equal worth of all (imago Dei; Surah 49:13; God's light in all).
  • Religions differ over women's roles and same-sex relationships: "equal but different" or "equal and the same".
  • Know each named source for your religion: what it says and how it is applied today.

Mission 3

  • Freedom of religion and belief: to hold, change and practise any religion or none (UDHR Article 18; Article 9; Equality Act 2010).
  • Limits only when necessary for safety or others' rights (Eweida won; Chaplin lost). Censorship, extremism and Islamophobia threaten freedom from different sides.
  • Teachings: Qur'an 2:256; Esther; Rig Veda 1.164.46; Guru Tegh Bahadur; Ashoka; Catholic teaching in Dignitatis Humanae.

Mission 4

  • Exclusivism: one true religion. Inclusivism: one fullest truth others can share. Pluralism: many paths to one reality.
