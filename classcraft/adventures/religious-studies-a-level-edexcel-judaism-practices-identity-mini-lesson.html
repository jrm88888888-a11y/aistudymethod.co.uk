Mission 1

What "Orthodox" means

  • Orthodox Jews see themselves as continuing traditional, rabbinic Judaism without break.
  • The label is modern: it came into use in early 19th-century Germany, first by reformers describing traditionalists. Many Orthodox Jews prefer "Torah-observant".
  • Three core claims: the written and oral Torah come from God; halakhah (Jewish law) binds every Jew; it changes only by its own rules.
  • Internal diversity:
  • Modern Orthodox: full observance plus engagement with secular learning (Samson Raphael Hirsch, 1808–1888: Torah im derekh eretz, "Torah with the way of the land").
  • Haredi ("those who tremble" at God's word, Isaiah 66:5): stricter separation from secular culture (the Hatam Sofer, 1762–1839: "the new is forbidden by the Torah").
  • Hasidic groups are part of the Haredi world.
  • B Brown (Benjamin Brown, Hebrew University of Jerusalem), paraphrase: Orthodoxy is itself a modern movement, shaped by its response to Haskalah and Reform, even as it claims unbroken continuity.
Mission 1Read the source

Two Torahs at Sinai

> "And the LORD said unto Moses: 'Come up to Me into the mount and be there; and I will give thee the tables of stone, and the law and the commandment, which I have written, that thou mayest teach them.'" (Exodus 24:12, JPS 1917)

  • The Talmud (Berakhot 5a, paraphrase) reads each phrase: tables = the Ten Commandments; law = the Five Books; commandment = the Mishnah; which I have written = the Prophets and Writings; that thou mayest teach them = the Gemara.

Q1 What does this reading claim about the oral Torah?

Show answer

That it was given to Moses at Sinai together with the written Torah.

Q2 Why does this matter for Orthodox authority?

Show answer

Rabbinic law is then not a human addition: it unfolds what God gave.

Mission 1

Torah from heaven

  • Torah min ha-shamayim: "Torah from heaven". The core Orthodox belief.
  • Maimonides' 8th principle (paraphrase): the whole Torah we have is the one given to Moses. 9th: it will not be exchanged for another.

> "Ye shall not add unto the word which I command you, neither shall ye diminish from it" (Deuteronomy 4:2, JPS 1917)

  • The written Torah needs the oral to be kept: "frontlets between thine eyes" (Deuteronomy 6:8) does not say what tefillin are; the oral Torah does.
  • Spelling: Tenakh (Torah, Nevi'im, Ketuvim); oral Torah written down in Mishnah and Talmud.
Mission 1

Halakhah: the way to walk

  • Halakhah from halakh, "to walk": the path a Jew walks every day.
  • Chain of authority: written Torah → Mishnah (c. 200 CE) → Talmud (Babylonian, c. 500–600 CE) → Mishneh Torah (Maimonides, c. 1180) → Shulchan Arukh (Joseph Karo, 1565, with Moses Isserles' Ashkenazi glosses) → responsa (rabbis' written answers to new questions).
  • Authority of the rabbis rests on Deuteronomy 17:11: do "according to the law which they shall teach thee".
  • New questions (electricity, IVF, organ transplants) are answered by applying old principles, not by voting or personal preference.
  • Orthodoxy stresses practice: what you do matters as much as what you believe.

Mission 1

Revelation before theology

  • Revelation: what God gave at Sinai (Torah and mitzvot). Theology: human reasoning about God.
  • In Orthodoxy revelation has priority: a Jew is bound by the commandments whatever theory of God they hold.
  • Judaism has few binding creeds. Maimonides' Thirteen Principles are widely sung in Orthodox prayer (Yigdal, Ani Ma'amin), but their status was debated: Isaac Abravanel (1437–1508) argued every part of the Torah is equally binding, so no 13 can be singled out.
  • Yet Orthodoxy does insist on one belief: Torah from heaven. Without it, the law loses its authority.
Mission 1Diversity

A literal Torah or a philosophy of history?

  • Literal view (common in Haredi teaching): every word dictated by God; the narratives are exact history; modern biblical criticism rejected.
  • Torah as a philosophy of history: the Torah also reveals the meaning of Jewish history: covenant, sin, exile and return (Deuteronomy 28–30). Exile, destruction and survival are read as part of a divine plan.
  • Some Modern Orthodox thinkers hold Torah from heaven while reading parts (e.g. the six days of creation) non-literally; Maimonides already read creation partly as allegory (Guide for the Perplexed, c. 1190).
  • Both are Orthodox: they agree the Torah is from God; they differ over how to read it.
  • B Brown (paraphrase): Orthodox groups differ less on the law than on how far to engage with modern thought.
Mission 1

The place of the Temple

  • First Temple destroyed 586 BCE; Second Temple destroyed by Rome 70 CE. Sacrifice stopped.
  • Prayer took its place: "so will we render for bullocks the offering of our lips" (Hosea 14:2, JPS 1917; 14:3 in JPS numbering).
  • The three daily services match the daily offerings (Talmud, Berakhot 26b): Shacharit (morning), Minchah (afternoon), Ma'ariv (evening).
  • Orthodox prayer still asks for the Temple to be rebuilt and its service restored (Amidah; Musaf on Shabbat and festivals). Reform removed these prayers (see Mission 6).
  • Synagogues face Jerusalem; the fast of Tisha B'Av mourns both Temples.
  • The synagogue is a "little sanctuary" (mikdash me'at), from Ezekiel 11:16.
  • Most Orthodox Jews expect the Temple to be rebuilt in the messianic age; many rabbis rule that Jews should not enter the Temple Mount today because of ritual purity, though some disagree.
Mission 1

An Orthodox synagogue

  • Mechitzah: a partition or gallery separating men and women in prayer.
  • Bimah (reading platform), often central; Aron Hakodesh (ark) on the Jerusalem wall; ner tamid (eternal light) recalls the Temple menorah.
  • Prayers in Hebrew; a minyan of ten men needed for public prayer; in most Orthodox synagogues only men lead and read from the Torah.
  • No musical instruments on Shabbat (Shabbat law; also mourning for the Temple).
  • G Robinson (George Robinson, Essential Judaism, 2000), paraphrase: Orthodox practice is marked by keeping the full traditional liturgy and halakhah; the layout of the synagogue expresses that continuity.
Mission 1

An Orthodox day

  • On waking: Modeh Ani (thanks for life restored).
  • Shacharit: men wear tallit and tefillin on weekdays.
  • Shema morning and evening:

> "and shalt talk of them when thou sittest in thy house, and when thou walkest by the way, and when thou liest down, and when thou risest up." (Deuteronomy 6:7, JPS 1917)

  • Berakhot (blessings) before and after food; kashrut; mezuzah on doorposts (Deuteronomy 6:9); tzitzit; modest dress (tzniut); daily Torah study.
  • Women are exempt from most time-bound positive commands (in Orthodox halakhah), e.g. tefillin; many pray daily. The roles are seen as different, not lesser; critics see inequality.

Mission 1Evaluate

Can Orthodoxy be unchanging in a changing world?

  • Claim: Orthodox Judaism preserves Judaism unchanged.
  • For: halakhah is binding and its core (Shabbat, kashrut, Shema, prayer times) is practised as it was centuries ago.
  • For: Deuteronomy 4:2 forbids adding or taking away; Torah from heaven cannot be revised by human vote.
  • Against: halakhah develops: responsa apply it to electricity, IVF and medicine. Development by its own rules is still change.
  • Against: Brown: the "Orthodox" movement itself formed in the 19th century in reaction to Reform; its many forms (Modern Orthodox, Haredi, Hasidic) differ.
  • Against: practice has shifted, e.g. formal Torah schooling for girls (Bais Yaakov, from 1917) and advanced women's learning in Modern Orthodoxy.
  • Judgement criteria: Is "change" a change in the law or in its application? Does continuity of practice matter more than continuity of institutions? Whose Orthodoxy is the test case?

Mission 2

Shabbat: creation and freedom

  • Shabbat (rest): from just before sunset on Friday until nightfall on Saturday, about 25 hours.
  • Creation: God "rested on the seventh day from all His work which He had made" (Genesis 2:2, JPS 1917). Resting imitates God.
  • Freedom: Deuteronomy's version of the commandment links rest to the Exodus: "thou was a servant in the land of Egypt" (Deuteronomy 5:15, JPS 1917). Slaves cannot rest; free people can.
  • Covenant: Shabbat is "a perpetual covenant" between God and Israel (Exodus 31:16, JPS 1917).
  • The rabbis read "Remember" (Exodus 20:8) and "Observe" (Deuteronomy 5:12) as two sides: positive celebration and refraining from work.
Mission 2Read the source

Exodus 20 and Isaiah 58

> "Remember the sabbath day, to keep it holy." (Exodus 20:8, JPS 1917)

> "in it thou shalt not do any manner of work, thou, nor thy son, nor thy daughter, nor thy man-servant, nor thy maid-servant, nor thy cattle, nor thy stranger that is within thy gates" (Exodus 20:10, JPS 1917)

> "and call the sabbath a delight, and the holy of the LORD honourable" (Isaiah 58:13, JPS 1917)

Q1 Who must rest, according to Exodus 20:10?

Show answer

Everyone in the household: children, servants, animals and resident foreigners.

Q2 What does Isaiah add to "not working"?

Show answer

Shabbat should be a "delight" (oneg Shabbat): joy and honour, not only restriction.

Mission 2

Shabbat at home

  • Friday evening: candles lit before sunset, traditionally by the woman of the house, with a blessing.
  • Kiddush ("sanctification") over wine: recalls creation and the Exodus.
  • Two challah loaves: a reminder of the double portion of manna before the Sabbath in the wilderness (Exodus 16:22).
  • Family meals, songs, guests; synagogue services Friday night and Saturday morning, with the weekly Torah portion.
  • Havdalah ("separation") on Saturday night: wine, sweet spices, a plaited candle; Shabbat is divided from the week.
Mission 2

What counts as "work"?

  • The Hebrew word is melachah: creative work, not effort.
  • The Mishnah lists 39 categories (Shabbat 7:2, paraphrase), based on the work of building the Mishkan (Tabernacle), because the Torah places the Shabbat command beside it (Exodus 31, 35).
  • Examples: sowing, cooking, writing, kindling a fire, building, carrying between private and public space.
  • Applied today: Orthodox authorities treat switching on lights and driving as forbidden; timers and pre-cooked food are used. An eruv (boundary) lets people carry within an area.
  • Pikuach nephesh (saving a life) overrides Shabbat completely (Talmud Yoma 85b): a doctor treats a patient; a sick person is driven to hospital.

Mission 2Diversity

Keeping Shabbat differently

  • Orthodox (shomer Shabbat, "Shabbat-keeping"): the 39 melachot are binding halakhah; no driving, phones, cooking or writing; walk to synagogue.
  • Masorti (Conservative): halakhic in principle; in 1950 American Conservative rabbis allowed driving to synagogue and using electricity on Shabbat. Practice among members varies.
  • Reform and Liberal: Shabbat as rest and renewal; individuals decide which practices make it holy (e.g. candles and family meal, but driving to synagogue).
  • Secular Jews: many keep a Friday-night family dinner as a cultural tradition.
  • All see Shabbat as a sign of Jewish identity; they differ on whether the rabbinic definitions of work are binding.
Mission 2

The daily rhythm

  • On waking, Modeh Ani ("I thank you"); blessings (brachot) before and after food and for many daily events.
  • Three daily services: Shacharit (morning), Minchah (afternoon), Ma'ariv (evening); the Shema morning and evening.
  • Mezuzah on the doorposts (Deuteronomy 6:9): touched on entering and leaving.
  • Daily Torah study, alone or with a partner.
  • The Temple: the rabbis linked the three prayer times to the daily Temple offerings (Talmud Berakhot 26b); after 70 CE prayer replaced sacrifice, "the offering of our lips" (Hosea 14:3, paraphrase).
  • B Brown stresses that Orthodoxy is defined by commitment to halakhah in every area of daily life; G Robinson (Essential Judaism, 2000) shows practice structured by the cycles of day, week and year (paraphrase).

Mission 3

Exile: worship without the Temple

  • 586 BCE: the Babylonians destroy the First Temple in Jerusalem; many Judeans are taken to Babylon.
  • "By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down, yea, we wept, when we remembered Zion." (Psalm 137:1, JPS 1917)
  • Sacrifice could happen only at the Temple. So how could exiles worship?
  • Jewish tradition answers: they gathered to pray, read Torah and study: the beginnings of the synagogue (Greek: "gathering together").
  • The Second Temple was rebuilt (516 BCE) and destroyed by Rome (70 CE). After 70 CE the synagogue became the centre of Jewish worship everywhere.
Mission 3

What historians say; after 70 CE

  • The Babylonian origin is the traditional view. Direct evidence from the exile itself has not been found.
  • The earliest firm evidence: inscriptions about Jewish "prayer houses" in Egypt (3rd century BCE) and synagogue buildings in the Land of Israel (1st century BCE–1st century CE).
  • After the Romans destroyed the Second Temple (70 CE), rabbis at Yavneh rebuilt Judaism around Torah study, prayer and the synagogue.
  • Prayer took the place of sacrifice: the three daily services match the Temple's daily offerings (Talmud Berakhot 26b).
  • With no Temple or priesthood, rabbis, as teachers of halakhah, became Judaism's leaders; in Orthodox Judaism their rulings guide daily life.
  • Orthodox Judaism still prays for the Temple to be rebuilt, and recalls it in prayers and synagogue design.
Mission 3

Three houses in one

  • Beit Knesset (house of meeting/assembly): the community gathers for social life, decisions, celebrations and help.
  • Beit Midrash (house of study): Torah reading and learning; the Yiddish name shul comes from the German for "school".
  • Beit Tefillah (house of prayer): daily and Shabbat services, festivals.
  • A synagogue is defined by these functions, not by its size: any room with a Torah scroll, an ark and a minyan can serve.
Mission 3

Inside: the ark and the Temple

  • Aron Hakodesh (holy ark): a cupboard holding the Sefer Torah scrolls, on the wall facing Jerusalem. It recalls the Ark of the Covenant: "thou shalt put into the ark the testimony which I shall give thee" (Exodus 25:16, JPS 1917).
  • Parochet (curtain) before the ark: recalls the veil before the Holy of Holies (Exodus 26:33).
  • Ner Tamid (eternal light) above it: recalls the lamp that burned "continually" in the Sanctuary (Exodus 27:20, JPS 1917).
  • Bimah: the raised platform from which the Torah is read.
  • Often the Ten Commandments above the ark.
  • So the synagogue recalls the Temple, but is not a replacement Temple: no altar, no sacrifice, no priests leading worship.

Mission 3Diversity

Synagogue worship

  • Orthodox: prayer mostly in Hebrew; men and women seated apart (mechitzah: a screen or gallery); a minyan of ten men for public prayer; no instruments or microphones on Shabbat; the bimah often in the centre; men lead services.
  • Masorti: traditional liturgy in Hebrew; many communities seat men and women together and count women in the minyan.
  • Reform and Liberal: mixed seating; women and men count equally and lead services; more prayers in English; some use instruments; the bimah usually at the front; women rabbis.
  • Hasidic shtiebels: small, informal prayer rooms, often tied to a rebbe's following.
  • G Robinson describes these Orthodox practices as continuous with rabbinic law; B Brown sees Orthodoxy marked by halakhic commitment more than by creed (paraphrase).

Mission 4

A Jew in Enlightenment Berlin

  • Moses Mendelssohn (1729–1786), born in Dessau, son of a Torah scribe.
  • 1743: follows his teacher, Rabbi David Fränkel, to Berlin. Teaches himself German, Latin and philosophy.
  • Berlin is a centre of the Enlightenment (German Aufklärung): reason, tolerance, "natural religion" open to all.
  • Jews in Prussia had limited rights: residence only by royal privilege, taxes, restricted trades.
  • Friend of the playwright Lessing; wins the Berlin Academy prize (1763) ahead of Kant; called "the German Socrates".
  • He stays an observant Jew all his life.
Mission 4

Truths that reason can reach

  • Mendelssohn's eternal truths: God exists; God's providence; the soul is immortal.
  • These are open to every human being by reason. A good God would not leave most of humanity without the truths needed for happiness.
  • Existence of God: he argued it by reason, not by appeal to scripture (prize essay, 1763; Morning Hours, 1785).
  • Freewill: by the same rational method he argued humans are free and so morally responsible.
  • So for Mendelssohn there is harmony between reason and Judaism: Judaism demands no belief that reason contradicts.
Mission 4Read the source

Jerusalem (1783)

Mendelssohn, Jerusalem, or on Religious Power and Judaism (1783), Part II (paraphrase):

  • Judaism is not a revealed religion in the Christian sense: it does not reveal saving doctrines that reason could not reach.
  • What Israel received at Sinai was revealed legislation: laws and commandments for the Jewish people.
  • The eternal truths are taught alongside the laws, but believed because reason shows them, not because they are commanded.

Q1 What, for Mendelssohn, is unique to Judaism?

Show answer

Its divinely revealed law, not special doctrines.

Q2 Why could he think this defends Judaism?

Show answer

Judaism asks no one to believe against reason, so an Enlightenment critic cannot call it irrational.

Mission 4

Why keep the commandments?

  • The ceremonial law binds Jews because God gave it at Sinai. Only God can release them from it, as publicly as it was given.
  • The commandments are a "living script" (paraphrase): actions that keep the eternal truths alive and point to them, unlike written creeds that can harden into idols.
  • They bind Jews together and sustain Israel's loyalty to God.
  • No coercion: neither state nor religious authority may force belief. He opposed the rabbis' power of excommunication (herem).
  • So Mendelssohn defends observance of Halakhah and rationalism together.

Mission 4

The Biur: Torah in German

  • Sefer Netivot ha-Shalom (1780–1783): the Five Books of Moses translated into German, printed in Hebrew letters, with a Hebrew commentary, the Biur ("explanation"), written with collaborators.
  • Aims: teach Jews pure German in place of Yiddish; teach Hebrew grammar and the plain sense (peshat) of the text.
  • 1783: translation of the Psalms into German.
  • Effect: a bridge from the traditional study house into German culture: the start of modernising Jewish education.
  • Some rabbis objected that boys would study German instead of Talmud; the work was banned in some communities.
Mission 4

Immortality as endless progress

  • Phaedon (1767): a reworking of Plato's Phaedo in modern form.
  • Simplicity: the soul is simple (not made of parts), so it cannot fall apart as bodies do.
  • God's goodness: a wise, good God would not create beings who long for perfection and then end them.
  • So immortality is unending moral progress: the soul keeps growing towards perfection after death.
  • Critics: Kant (Critique of Pure Reason, 2nd edition, 1787) argued the simplicity argument fails: a simple soul could still fade away by degrees.
  • Note the method: a rational proof, not a proof from scripture.

Mission 4Diversity

Hero or danger?

  • Critics: the Hatam Sofer (Moses Sofer, 1762–1839, Pressburg) urged his followers not to read Mendelssohn's books: openness to German culture would lead Jews away from Torah.
  • Assimilation: several of Mendelssohn's children and most of his grandchildren became Christians (e.g. the composer Felix Mendelssohn Bartholdy). Critics see the fruit of his approach; defenders say he kept the law and cannot be blamed for others' choices.
  • Samson Raphael Hirsch (Orthodox) praised his learning but said he did not build a Jewish philosophy from within Judaism.
  • Reform later claimed him as a founder, though he never questioned the law's binding force.
  • D Cohn-Sherbok (b. 1945), paraphrase: Mendelssohn opened the way for Jews to enter European culture, while insisting on observance.
  • G Robinson (Essential Judaism, 2000), paraphrase: the Haskalah he inspired began modern Jewish diversity.
Mission 4Evaluate

Did Mendelssohn transform Jewish life and thought?

  • Claim: Mendelssohn transformed Jewish life and thought.
  • For: the Biur moved Jews into German language and culture; modern Jewish schools followed (Berlin Freischule, 1778, founded by his circle).
  • For: he showed a Jew could be a leading European philosopher; Lessing's Nathan the Wise (1779) was modelled on him.
  • For: his separation of religion and state helped the case for emancipation (equal civil rights for Jews;).
  • Against: his own theology was traditional: reason plus revealed law, much like medieval Jewish philosophy.
  • Against: the Maskilim and Reformers went where he did not: he never gave up observance.
  • Against: Eastern European Jewry, the majority, was barely touched in his lifetime.
  • Judgement criteria: transform life (language, school, culture) or thought (beliefs)? His intentions or his influence? Which Jews?
Mission 4

Maimonides and Mendelssohn compared

  • Context: Maimonides (1138–1204) in Islamic Spain and Egypt, using Aristotle; Mendelssohn (1729–1786) in Christian Prussia, using Leibniz and Wolff and the Enlightenment.
  • Shared: both harmonise reason and revelation; both argue for God's existence by reason; both fully observant.
  • Difference: Maimonides gave Judaism Thirteen Principles of faith to be held; Mendelssohn said Judaism has no dogmas imposed on belief, only laws for action.
  • Legacy: Maimonides codified the law (Mishneh Torah); Mendelssohn translated the Torah into a European language.
  • Strengths/weaknesses within Judaism: both admired; both accused by critics of bringing outside philosophy into Torah.

Mission 5

The Haskalah

  • Haskalah: Hebrew for "enlightenment" (from sekhel, reason). Its followers: Maskilim ("enlightened ones").
  • Began in Berlin in the 1770s–80s around Mendelssohn (see Mission 4); spread to Galicia, then Russia, in the 19th century, until about the 1880s.
  • Core idea: Jews should join European learning and culture and remain Jews.
  • Tools: reason, secular study, European languages, a revived Hebrew.
  • It challenged the traditional world in which Torah and Talmud study was the only learning that counted.
Mission 5

The Maskilim's programme

  • Schools: the Jewish Free School in Berlin (1778) taught German, maths and trades alongside Hebrew.
  • Naphtali Herz Wessely (1725–1805), Words of Peace and Truth (1782): Jews need "the teaching of man" (general knowledge) as well as "the teaching of God".
  • Ha-Me'assef ("The Gatherer", from 1783–84): first modern Hebrew journal: science, poetry, history.
  • In the East: Isaac Baer Levinsohn (1788–1860) urged secular study within Torah; Joseph Perl (1773–1839) satirised the Hasidim.
  • Judah Leib Gordon (1830–1892), poem "Awake, my people!" (1863), paraphrase: be a person in the street and a Jew at home.
  • Maskilim opposed Hasidism and attacked Yiddish as a "jargon"; they promoted Hebrew and the local language.
Mission 5

Secular Jewish culture

  • From the Haskalah came a Jewish identity not built on religious practice.
  • History: the "Science of Judaism" (Wissenschaft des Judentums, from 1819; Leopold Zunz, 1794–1886) studied Jewish texts as history; Heinrich Graetz wrote a History of the Jews (1853–1876).
  • Language and literature: Hebrew novels and poetry; later Yiddish literature.
  • Politics: later movements built on this identity: cultural Zionism (Ahad Ha'am) and Jewish socialism (the Bund, 1897).
  • Result: many Jews defined themselves by history and identity rather than by observance.

Mission 5Diversity

From Haskalah to Reform and Progressive

  • Haskalah ideas Reform drew on: reason (keep what reason and ethics support); history (Judaism has always developed: Wissenschaft); aesthetics (decorum, sermons, the vernacular).
  • Reform (Germany): Seesen temple (1810), Hamburg Temple (1818), Abraham Geiger (see Mission 6).
  • Progressive in Britain: West London Synagogue (1840); Liberal Judaism (Jewish Religious Union, 1902; Claude Montefiore and Lily Montagu).
  • Not all Maskilim became Reform: in the East many became secular nationalists or writers; some stayed observant.
  • Orthodoxy also answered the Haskalah: Hirsch's neo-Orthodoxy took secular study on board while holding all of halakhah.
  • Context: emancipation offered Jews rights as individual citizens, not as a nation; many German Reformers answered by defining Jews as a religious community only ("citizens of the Jewish faith").
  • Your spec: the influence of Haskalah, "enlightenment and rationalism", on Reform.

Mission 5Evaluate

Did the Haskalah strengthen Judaism?

  • Claim: The Haskalah strengthened Judaism.
  • For: it gave Jews tools to live as equals in modern Europe: education, languages, professions.
  • For: it revived Hebrew and created modern Jewish scholarship, literature and history.
  • For: it produced new forms (Reform, neo-Orthodoxy, cultural Zionism): pluralism let Jews choose how to be Jewish.
  • Against: it weakened observance and community; for many, acculturation led to assimilation (losing a distinct Jewish identity) and conversion.
  • Against: science and rational thought challenged belief in God and revealed law; some Jews left religion.
  • Against: it divided Jews, and it did not stop modern antisemitism.
  • Judgement criteria: strengthen Judaism (the religion) or Jews (the people)? Short term or long term? Germany or Eastern Europe? Is diversity a strength or a weakness?
Mission check

?In 1880s Odessa, a writer publishes Hebrew novels, studies Jewish history and calls himself proudly Jewish, though he rarely prays or keeps the law. What does he best represent?

Emancipation offered Jews rights as individuals; the Haskalah gave them new ways to be Jewish. Together they made modern Jewish diversity.

Mission 6

Why Reform began

  • 19th-century Germany: emancipation was opening citizenship (see Mission 5); many young Jews were drifting away or converting.
  • Reformers wanted a Judaism modern Jews could stay in.
  • Israel Jacobson opens a temple in Seesen (1810); the Hamburg Temple follows (1818).
  • Changes: sermons in German, an organ and choir, shorter services, decorum, some prayers in German.
  • The Hamburg prayer book altered prayers for a return to Zion and the restoration of sacrifice.
  • Reformers drew on the Haskalah: reason and the historical study of Judaism (see Mission 5).
Mission 6

Progressive revelation

  • Abraham Geiger (1810–1874): Judaism has always developed; each age expresses its eternal core in new forms.
  • Progressive revelation: God's revelation continues through history; the Torah is inspired by God but written by humans in their time.
  • The ethical heart (the prophets' justice and compassion) is lasting; many ritual laws belonged to an earlier age.
  • Today Reform speaks of informed choice: Jews study the tradition and decide which mitzvot to keep.
  • Contrast: Orthodox Torah from heaven, binding as given (see Mission 1).
  • M Meyer (Michael A. Meyer, Response to Modernity, 1988), paraphrase: Reform is best seen as a set of responses to modernity that kept seeking continuity with the Jewish past.
Mission 6Diversity

Germany, America, Jerusalem

  • Germany: moderate. Reform worked inside united communities beside traditional Jews. Much Hebrew kept; kashrut and Shabbat largely kept. Rabbinical conferences (Brunswick 1844, Frankfurt 1845, Breslau 1846). A radical exception: Samuel Holdheim's Berlin congregation (Sunday services).
  • America: radical. Free of state-recognised communities. Isaac Mayer Wise (1819–1900) builds institutions (Union of American Hebrew Congregations 1873; Hebrew Union College 1875). The Pittsburgh Platform (1885) makes only moral laws binding; many pray bareheaded; some hold Sunday services.
  • Jerusalem (Israel): radical in another way. Hebrew Union College opens a Jerusalem campus (1963); the World Union for Progressive Judaism moves its headquarters there (1973); the Israel Movement for Progressive Judaism grows. Worship is in Hebrew and fairly traditional, but it challenges the Orthodox monopoly over marriage, conversion and prayer at the Western Wall.
  • D E Kaplan (Dana Evan Kaplan, American Reform Judaism: An Introduction, 2003), paraphrase: American Reform stresses personal autonomy, and in recent decades has moved back towards ritual and Hebrew.
Mission 6

Worship, observance and gender

  • Synagogue: mixed seating, organ and choir, prayers in Hebrew and the vernacular, often called a "temple".
  • Observance: kashrut, Shabbat rules, tefillin are matters of informed choice; many Reform Jews keep some.
  • Gender: women count in the minyan, read Torah, lead services.
  • Regina Jonas (1902–1944), Berlin, first woman ordained rabbi (1935); murdered at Auschwitz.
  • Sally Priesand: first woman ordained by an American seminary (Hebrew Union College, 1972); in Britain, Jackie Tabick (1975).
  • Prayer books add the matriarchs to the Amidah; Reform affirms LGBTQ+ inclusion.
  • American Reform (1983) accepts Jewish status through either parent if the child is raised Jewish.

Mission 6Evaluate

Development or break?

  • Claim: Reform Judaism is a legitimate development of Judaism, not a break from it.
  • For: Judaism has always changed: prayer replaced sacrifice; the rabbis adapted law (Geiger, Meyer).
  • For: Reform keeps the ethical core of the prophets and has drawn more Jews to stay Jewish in modern societies.
  • Against: Orthodox critics: if the Torah is not from heaven and halakhah not binding, the basis of Judaism has changed.
  • Against: classical Reform dropped hope of return to Zion and much ritual; later Reform restored much of it, suggesting the first changes went too far.
  • Judgement criteria: what is Judaism's "core"? Who decides what is legitimate? Development by halakhic process or by community choice?
  • Spec focus: the "significance of beliefs and practices changing over time".

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Orthodox Judaism holds that written and oral Torah are from God (Torah min ha-shamayim) and that halakhah binds every Jew; it ranges from Modern Orthodox to Haredi and Hasidic.
  • For Orthodox Jews, revelation has priority over theology; they differ over reading the Torah literally or as the meaning of Jewish history.
  • Prayer replaces the Temple offerings and asks for the Temple's rebuilding; synagogue layout (mechitzah, Hebrew, minyan) and the daily cycle (Shema, tefillin, berakhot, kashrut) express continuity.

Mission 2

  • Shabbat recalls creation (Genesis 2:2) and freedom from slavery (Deuteronomy 5:15): from Friday sunset to Saturday nightfall.
  • Candles, Kiddush, challah and Havdalah frame the day; Orthodox Jews keep the 39 melachot, Progressive Jews adapt.

Mission 3

  • Tradition traces the synagogue to the Babylonian exile ("a little sanctuary", Ezekiel 11:16); after 70 CE it replaced the Temple as the centre of worship.
  • Beit knesset, beit midrash, beit tefillah: meeting, study, prayer; the ark, parochet and ner tamid recall the Temple.

Mission 4

  • Mendelssohn (1729–1786): eternal truths (God, providence, immortality) by reason; Judaism is revealed legislation, binding on Jews, with no coercion of belief.
  • The Biur (1780–1783) and Psalms translation brought Jews into German language and culture; Phaedon (1767) argued for immortality as unending moral progress.
  • Critics (Hatam Sofer) feared assimilation; scholars debate whether he transformed Jewish thought or only Jewish life.
  • With Maimonides: both harmonise reason and revelation; Maimonides sets principles of faith, Mendelssohn denies imposed dogma.

Mission 5

  • The Haskalah (from the 1770s) joined Jewish life to reason, secular study and European languages; Maskilim revived Hebrew and created a secular Jewish culture of history and identity.
  • Haskalah rationalism and historical study fed Reform and Progressive Judaism; Orthodoxy and secular nationalism were other answers.

Mission 6

  • Reform began in early 19th-century Germany (Seesen 1810, Hamburg 1818), shaped by emancipation and the Haskalah; Geiger's progressive revelation sees Judaism developing with an ethical core.
  • German Reform was moderate; American Reform (Pittsburgh 1885) radical; Reform in Israel challenges the Orthodox monopoly. Changes include mixed seating, vernacular, informed choice and women rabbis.
