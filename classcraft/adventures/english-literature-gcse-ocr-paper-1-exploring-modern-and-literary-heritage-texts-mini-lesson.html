Component 01: Modern & Literary Heritage Texts

This mini-lesson teaches the skills and structure of OCR GCSE English Literature Component 01 (J352/01): how to analyse a printed extract from your modern text and link it to the whole text, how to do the same for your 19th-century (literary heritage) novel, and how to hit the Assessment Objectives AO1, AO2, AO3 and AO4. It drills terminology, quotations and technique — not the finished essay.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go (some ask you to pick the strongest point, some to name a method) and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you're ready.

The shape of the paper

Two sections, both two-part & extract-based

Component 01 is a 2 hour exam worth 80 marks — 40% of the whole GCSE. It is closed book — you get no copy of your texts except the printed extract. You answer two questions, and each has two parts:

  • Section A — Modern prose or drama (~40 marks): one two-part question on your set modern text. Part (a) is extract-based (analyse a printed passage); part (b) links out to the whole text as a discursive essay. Rewards AO1, AO2, AO4 (SPaG).
  • Section B — 19th-century prose (~40 marks): one two-part question on your set literary-heritage novel. Part (a) is extract-based; part (b) is a whole-text essay. Rewards AO1, AO2, AO3 (context).

Set texts differ by school: your modern text might be Anita and Me, An Inspector Calls, Animal Farm, Never Let Me Go, Leave Taking, DNA or My Mother Said I Never Should; your 19th-century novel might be Great Expectations, Jane Eyre, A Christmas Carol, Jekyll and Hyde, Pride and Prejudice or The War of the Worlds. The skills here work for any of them.

The Assessment Objectives

What the examiner rewards

Every mark is tied to an Assessment Objective (AO). Component 01 rewards AO1 and AO2 across both sections, plus AO4 (SPaG) on the modern text and AO3 (context) on the 19th-century prose:

Key split: AO1 and AO2 are rewarded in both sections. AO4 (SPaG) is assessed only on the modern text; AO3 (context) is assessed only on the 19th-century prose and is not foregrounded on the modern text.

The heart of AO2

Method → effect, every time

Naming a technique earns almost nothing. The marks come from linking the method to the effect it creates on the reader or audience:

method + evidence → effect on reader/audiencee.g. "the imperative 'Look' (method) forces the reader to share the character's shock (effect)"

Useful methods to spot in prose & drama: metaphor, simile, personification, imagery, symbolism, pathetic fallacy, semantic field, dramatic irony, foreshadowing, tone, narrative voice, first-person narrator, stage directions, structure and form.

Golden rule: never stop at "this is a metaphor." Always finish the sentence: "…which suggests / creates / emphasises / makes the reader feel …".

Worked model · annotation

Annotating an extract

Here is a short, made-up prose line (not a set text) annotated the way you'd mark up a real extract in part (a) of the exam:

The grey street held its breath as I passed,
and every window watched me like a stranger;
the silence pressed as heavy as a stone.
  • "held its breath" — personification: the street feels tense and alive, mirroring the narrator's unease.
  • "grey" — colour imagery / pathetic fallacy: a bleak, drained mood settles over the scene.
  • "watched me like a stranger" — simile: the narrator feels alienated and observed, an outsider.
  • "heavy as a stone" — simile: the silence becomes oppressive and physical, weighing on the reader.

Notice the pattern: each note is quote → method (with terminology) → effect. That is exactly the sentence shape your essay paragraphs need.

Section B skill · AO3

Context: the world behind the text

On the 19th-century prose (Section B), AO3 asks you to show you understand the context a text was written in — but only where it illuminates a choice in the text. Useful contexts include:

  • Class & poverty: Victorian social divides, the workhouse, wealth and status (e.g. A Christmas Carol, Great Expectations).
  • Science, duality & morality: Victorian anxieties about respectability, reputation and the double self (e.g. Jekyll and Hyde); the role of women (Jane Eyre, Pride and Prejudice).

Reminder: for the modern text (Section A) it is AO4 (SPaG) — not AO3 — that is assessed. Save context for Section B, and even there keep it woven in, not a history essay.

Argument skill

Answer with a thesis, not a plot summary

Open with a thesis — a clear line of argument that answers the exact question about the writer's method and purpose. Everything after it is proof.

Model opening (generic)

Question: "How does the writer present belonging in this extract and the whole text?"

Thesis: "The writer presents belonging as something the narrator must fight for, using first-person narrative voice and imagery of division to make the reader share the pain of feeling like an outsider."

Why it works: it takes a position (belonging must be fought for), names methods (narrative voice, imagery) and points to a purpose/effect (shared feeling). Now every paragraph proves one part of it.

Evidence done well

Embed quotes — keep them short

Because Component 01 is closed book, learn a bank of short, flexible quotations. Weave them into your own sentence rather than dropping in a whole line:

Clumsy vs embedded

❌ "Scrooge is mean. The quote is: 'a squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching, covetous old sinner.'"

✔ "Dickens's cluster of harsh verbs — 'wrenching', 'grasping', 'clutching' — makes Scrooge's greed feel physically violent."

Misconception: long quotations don't earn more marks — they waste time and bury your analysis. Quote the few words you'll actually analyse.

Context & AO3 (Section B)

Context serves the analysis

On Section B, context can enrich your points — but only if it explains a choice in the text. It must be woven in, not bolted on as a history lecture.

Bolted-on vs woven-in

❌ "Victorian London was very poor and dirty. Many children worked. Now back to the novel."

✔ "Against a backdrop of Victorian poverty, the boy's hunger becomes an indictment of a society that lets its children starve — sharpening Dickens's protest."

Misconception: context is not a paragraph you paste in. One relevant clause that illuminates a method or theme beats a page of dates.

Exam technique

Planning under the clock

Split your 2 hours: roughly 1 hour on each section (modern text + 19th-century prose). Spend the first 5 minutes of each planning:

  • Underline the key words in the question (the focus + the command word "how").
  • Read the extract twice, annotating 3–4 methods and their effects for part (a).
  • Jot 3 points for part (b) — each ranging from the extract to the whole text.
  • Write a one-line thesis, then write. On Section A, leave a minute to check SPaG (AO4).

Closed book: only the extract is printed — the rest of the text is not. Learn a bank of short, flexible quotations in advance.

Worked model · Section B

A paragraph in action

Watch how one paragraph moves from the extract to the whole text, with method, effect and a touch of context (Section B):

Model paragraph (generic)

"In the extract, the simile 'silent as a tomb' makes the house feel deathly and abandoned, the hard consonants mirroring its cold stillness. This atmosphere of decay develops across the novel: what begins as a single lonely room comes to stand for a whole life stopped in its tracks. To a Victorian reader anxious about respectability and social ruin, this frozen isolation would seem a fitting warning — deepening the writer's critique of a society obsessed with reputation."

Spot the moves: extract analysed (method + effect), linked to the whole text, context woven in to explain the effect — AO1, AO2 and AO3 together.

Avoid these traps

The five marks-killers

  • Feature-spotting: naming techniques without explaining their effect.
  • Extract-only: never linking part (a) out to the whole text in part (b) (you cap your marks).
  • Bolted-on context: a history block that doesn't serve a point (and on the modern text, remember it's AO4 SPaG, not AO3).
  • Fat quotes: long copied lines instead of short, embedded ones.
  • Plot retelling: narrating the story instead of arguing an answer.

Fix them all with one habit: every sentence should either make a point, prove it with a short quote, or explain a method's effect — and keep linking the extract to the whole text.

Recap

The skills to take into the exam

Component 01: A Modern prose/drama · B 19th-century prose — both two-part, extract-based, closed book (2h, 80 marks, 40%)

AO1: argue a thesis, support with short quotes (both sections)

AO2: method + terminology + evidence → effect on reader (both sections)

AO3: weave in context that explains the writer's choices (Section B only)

AO4: accurate SPaG + varied vocabulary and sentences (Section A only)

Structure: analyse the extract in (a), then range across the whole text in (b)
