Mission 1

He tells his sister to leave her book. Is Wordsworth anti-learning?

Make a guess: He tells his sister to leave her book. Is Wordsworth anti-learning?

Belonging · "To My Sister"Screen 1

Context and the title

  • Written in spring 1798 at Alfoxden, Somerset, where William lived with his sister Dorothy, near Coleridge. Printed that year in Lyrical Ballads.
  • 1798 title: "Lines written at a small distance from my House, and sent by my little Boy to the Person to whom they are addressed". The "little Boy" (Edward in the poem) was Basil Montagu, a child the Wordsworths fostered.
  • "To My Sister" is his revised 1845 title; your anthology prints that final text, quoted here.

Belonging · "To My Sister"Screen 5

Form and structure

  • Ten quatrains rhyming ABAB, mostly in eight-syllable lines, each stanza closing on a shorter line ("That stands beside our door."). It sounds like a simple song or ballad.
  • Lyrical Ballads called its poems "experiments" in the language of everyday conversation: here, a note to a sister.
  • Circular structure: stanzas 1–4 invite, 5–9 explain, and stanza 10 repeats the invitation of stanza 4.
  • Belonging: "our door", "we", "my friend": a household that belongs together outdoors.

Mission checkScreen 9

?He tells his sister to leave her book. Is Wordsworth anti-learning?

Wordsworth isn't rejecting learning outright. For "this one day" he argues that feeling in nature, the "hour of feeling", may give more than "years of toiling reason". Strong answers show how the poem limits and justifies that claim, and how the household belongs together in nature.

Mission 2

Boys, a pond and a raft of rushes. Where's the belonging?

Make a guess: Boys, a pond and a raft of rushes. Where's the belonging?

Belonging · "The Sunday Dip"Screen 1

Context

  • John Clare (1793–1864) was born in Helpston, Northamptonshire, the son of a farm labourer. His first book, Poems Descriptive of Rural Life and Scenery (1820), made him known as the "Northamptonshire Peasant Poet".
  • He wrote about the fields, birds and village life he knew first-hand. Much of his work was printed only after his death.
  • "Sunday Dip" was one of these: it was first printed in 1920, from Clare's manuscript.
Extract Belonging · "The Sunday Dip"Screen 2

"Sunday Dip"

Belonging · "The Sunday Dip"Screen 3

What happens

The poem follows one Sunday morning, in order. Use the extract on screen 2.

LinesWhat happens
1–2Boys crowd the morning road, heading for the water: "their Sunday joys".
3–4At a "shallow pit" they wade and "dance about the water in the shade".
5–6The boldest jumps in first; the rest follow until the water is up to their chins.
7–8They duck under, trying "to lose their fears", and laugh at "the thunder in their ears".
9–12They bundle rushes into a raft and try to float it across "the deepest place", under willows; it barely holds them.
13–14The others float without the rushes' help and "play about the water half the day".

Belonging · "The Sunday Dip"Screen 5

Form, structure and language

  • 14 lines: a sonnet's length, but seven rhyming couplets (AABB…) in regular iambic pentameter; "stoop"/"up" is a half-rhyme.
  • Each couplet is a new stage; lines opening "And" pile up actions, so the poem moves briskly.
  • Chronological structure: "The morning road" to "half the day".
  • The boys stay unnamed, "they" and "others": what matters is what they share.
  • The metaphor "thunder" makes the noise frightening, yet they "laugh".

?Boys, a pond and a raft of rushes. Where's the belonging?

Clare's belonging is shared and physical: a group of boys following the boldest into the pond, fighting their fear together, building a raft and spending half the day in the water. Brisk couplets carry the morning along, and the place itself, a pond with rushes and willows, is part of what they belong to.

Mission 3

Mist, rain and wet grass. Why do they take her back home?

Make a guess: Mist, rain and wet grass. Why do they take her back home?

Belonging · "Mild the Mist Upon the Hill"Screen 1

Context and first reading

  • Emily Brontë (1818–1848) grew up at Haworth parsonage in the Yorkshire Pennines, where her father Patrick was perpetual curate. Her mother died in 1821.
  • She taught for about six months at Law Hill School, Halifax (1837–38, or 1838–39 in Juliet Barker's dating). The poem is dated 27 July 1839.
  • It has no title (it is named by its first line) and was printed long after her death.
  • First reading: a calm evening after rain sends the speaker back to childhood.

Stanzas 3–4

Belonging · "Mild the Mist Upon the Hill"Screen 5

Form and structure

  • Four quatrains rhyming ABAB (hill / fill, more / door).
  • Stanza 1 has a falling beat (MILD the MIST up-ON the HILL), soft rhymes (to-morrow / sorrow) and pathetic fallacy: the day "has wept its fill".
  • From stanza 2 the lines alternate longer and shorter (about 8 then 6 syllables), like a hymn: the rhythm changes as she goes back.
  • The present tense throughout lets childhood and now overlap.
  • Stanzas 3–4 move through the senses: sight, damp, scent.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Mist, rain and wet grass. Why do they take her back home?

After rain, the mild evening's mists, wet grass and "dreamy scents" make the speaker feel "a child once more" under her "father's sheltering roof". Brontë presents belonging as something recovered through the senses and memory, and the weeping images keep a note of loss in it.

Mission 4

Why does a poem about a famous explorer end in a ploughed garden?

Make a guess: Why does a poem about a famous explorer end in a ploughed garden?

Belonging poetry · Captain Cook (To My Brother)Screen 1

Context in brief

  • Letitia Elizabeth Landon ("L.E.L.", 1802–1838) was a hugely popular poet. From 1832 she wrote most of the poems for Fisher's Drawing Room Scrap Book, an annual of engravings with verses.
  • "Captain Cook" appeared there in 1838, beside an engraving of Cook, dedicated to her brother Whittington (born 1804).
  • Captain James Cook made three voyages to the Pacific and was killed in Hawaii in 1779.
  • The family lived in the countryside from 1809 and moved back to London in 1815.

Lines 23–32: the garden now

Belonging poetry · Captain Cook (To My Brother)Screen 6

Form and structure

  • 32 lines in rhyming couplets (ago / know, more / yore). The steady chime suits a fond, storytelling voice.
  • Long lines, each split by a mid-line pause (caesura): "Ah! we both of us are altered, | and now we talk no more".
  • A frame structure: the present (lines 1–4, 23–32) surrounds the remembered past.
  • "Ah!" marks both returns to the present (lines 3 and 23). "Yet" (line 31) turns back to Cook at the very end.

?Why does a poem about a famous explorer end in a ploughed garden?

Cook is the doorway into memory. The poem is addressed to Landon's brother and recalls how a book of Cook's Voyages turned their garden pond into the South Seas. The ploughed garden shows that the place, like childhood, has gone; only the captain's name brings it back.

Mission 5

Bridges called this poem 'Elegy'. But who has died?

Make a guess: Bridges called this poem 'Elegy'. But who has died?

Belonging poetry · Clear and Gentle StreamScreen 1

Context in brief

  • Robert Bridges (1844–1930) grew up in Kent and was Poet Laureate from 1913 to 1930.
  • The poem first appeared in his first collection, Poems (1873). In his collected Shorter Poems it is titled "Elegy"; your anthology uses its first line.
  • An elegy is a poem of mourning. Keep the title in mind as you read: what is being mourned?

Stanza 1

Belonging poetry · Clear and Gentle StreamScreen 3

First reading

The speaker talks directly to the stream (apostrophe: addressing something that can't reply).

StanzaWhat happens
1He greets the stream he loved as a boy and sings his "old lament" to it again.
2He sits in his old seat under the branches; leaves, swans and fish.
3He remembers summer afternoons dreaming there until the bell and the evening came.
4Before leaving again, he asks the stream to hear his youthful song once more.
  • Even as a boy he had a "lament": the sadness isn't new, it is part of what he remembers.

Belonging poetry · Clear and Gentle StreamScreen 5

Form and structure

  • Four 12-line stanzas of very short lines, mostly five syllables: CLEAR and | GEN-tle | STREAM. Most lines start and end on a stress.
  • Rhymes come quickly (stanza 1: stream / long / song / dream…), which makes it sound like the "song" it describes.
  • The refrain "Clear and gentle stream" opens and closes stanzas 1 and 4: a circular structure, like a return visit.
  • Stanza 1 ends with "!", the final stanza with a full stop: excitement settles into calm.

?Bridges called this poem 'Elegy'. But who has died?

No one has died. "Elegy" mourns the speaker's own boyhood. Returning to the stream and repeating his "old lament" lets him be that boy for a moment before he goes away again.

Mission 6

Why would a happy childhood memory make him wish he'd died?

Make a guess: Why would a happy childhood memory make him wish he'd died?

Belonging poetry · I Remember, I RememberScreen 1

Context in brief

  • Thomas Hood (1799–1845) was born in the Poultry, in the City of London.
  • He was famous for comic verse and puns, but also wrote serious poems such as "The Song of the Shirt" (1843).
  • He first published this poem in Friendship's Offering for 1826, a gift-book annual that went on sale late in 1825, when he was 26 and already often ill.
  • The Golden Treasury (1861) printed it as "Past and Present", a title that sums up its method.

Belonging poetry · I Remember, I RememberScreen 5

Form and structure

  • Four 8-line stanzas in a ballad-like common metre: lines of eight then six syllables (I re-mem-ber, I re-mem-ber / The house where I was born).
  • Lines 2, 4, 6 and 8 rhyme (born / morn): simple, song-like, almost a nursery rhyme.
  • Refrain and anaphora: every stanza opens "I remember, I remember". The repetition sounds like someone lingering on memories.
  • The child-like music contrasts with the adult sadness at the end of each stanza.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Why would a happy childhood memory make him wish he'd died?

The happiness of the memories is what hurts. Every stanza moves from a bright childhood scene to a heavier adult present, so the contrast makes the present feel darker. The final stanza sums it up: he knows more now, but feels "farther off from Heaven".

Recap

Big ideas

  • Written at Alfoxden in 1798 and printed in Lyrical Ballads under a long title naming the "little Boy" who carried it; "To My Sister" is Wordsworth's later (1845) title.
  • Simple ABAB quatrains and conversational language carry a Romantic idea: feeling in nature can teach the heart.
  • Link it with poems about belonging to a place, a family or a moment of freedom; use the 1798 context (the "experiments") to explain its plain style.
  • Clare, the Northamptonshire "Peasant Poet", writes about a group of village boys' Sunday morning at a pond.
  • Fourteen lines of rhyming couplets follow the morning in order, from eager hurry to daring, fear, laughter and lost time.
  • Belonging here is shared freedom in a familiar place: link it with poems about childhood, play and the outdoors.
  • Dated 27 July 1839, after Emily Brontë's time teaching away from Haworth; untitled, and printed long after her death.
  • Pathetic fallacy ("the day has wept its fill"), a change of rhythm at "Oh, I'm gone back", the present tense and the senses carry the speaker into childhood.
  • Link it with poems about home, childhood and memory; contrast how far each speaker can really go back.
  • Published in 1838 and dedicated to her brother, "Captain Cook" recalls the siblings' childhood games inspired by Cook's Voyages.
  • A frame of present-day regret surrounds the remembered past; rhyming couplets give a fond, storytelling voice.
  • The destroyed garden works as a symbol of lost childhood; Cook's name is what still connects brother and sister to it.
  • First published in 1873 and later titled "Elegy", the poem mourns lost boyhood, not a death.
  • The speaker addresses the stream (apostrophe); short, quickly rhyming lines and the refrain make it a song.
  • A circular structure, from "Clear and gentle stream!" to "Clear and gentle stream.", mirrors his return visit.
  • Published in 1826 by a poet famous for comedy, the poem is serious: four memories, four turns to a sadder present.
  • Common metre, simple rhyme and the refrain "I remember, I remember" give a child-like music to adult sadness.
  • Juxtaposition of then and now is the key method; the final stanza links growing up with losing innocence.

Key quotations to learn

  • It is the first mild day of March: (Line 1) A precise moment: spring is just beginning.
  • There is a blessing in the air, (Line 5) Nature gives out feeling.
  • Come forth and feel the sun. (Line 12) The invitation: feeling, not thinking.
  • And bring no book: for this one day (Line 15) The claim is limited to one day.
  • It is the hour of feeling. (Line 24) The poem's central idea in one line.
  • Than years of toiling reason: (Line 26) One moment of feeling outweighs long, laboured thinking.
  • The morning road is thronged with merry boys (line 1) A crowd, not one boy: the group and its excitement from the start.
  • The boldest ventures first and dashes in (line 5) The turn from caution to daring; the group follows one leader.
  • And others go and follow to the chin (line 6) Belonging as following: the boys move as one.
  • And laugh to hear the thunder in their ears (line 8) The metaphor turns fear into shared fun.
  • The awkward load will scarcely bear them up (line 12) The raft of rushes: the riskiest, most daring moment.
  • And play about the water half the day (line 14) Time stops mattering: freedom in a place they know.
  • Mild the mist upon the hill, (Line 1) The calm, soft evening that starts the memory.
  • No, the day has wept its fill, (Line 3) Pathetic fallacy: sorrow is over, for now.
  • I am a child once more, (Line 6) Present tense: she is a child again.
  • And 'neath my father's sheltering roof (Line 7) Belonging as safety and shelter.
  • As thick as morning's tears; (Line 14) Simile and personification: beauty mixed with sadness.
  • That breathe of other years. (Line 16) Scent carries the past into the present.
  • Do you recall the fancies of many years ago, (Line 1) Direct address to the brother: shared memory.
  • We lived again its pages, we were its chiefs and kings, (Line 7) Books felt more real than life.
  • And the pond amid the willows the ocean seemed to be. (Line 14) Imagination transforms the garden.
  • We leave, in leaving childhood, life’s fairy land behind. (Line 24) The poem's statement of loss.
  • They have ploughed its long green grasses, and cut down the lime-tree bower. (Line 26) The lost garden as a symbol.
  • How much we loved his dangers, and how we mourned his fall. (Line 32) Cook's name still recalls their childhood.
  • Known and loved so long, (Stanza 1) Long attachment to one place.
  • Of my boyish day; (Stanza 1) What he really mourns.
  • In the selfsame strain (Stanza 1) He repeats the song of his boyhood.
  • And then evening comes, / Creeping up the glade, (Stanza 3) Personified evening: time passing gently.
  • That familiar strain / Silent now so long: (Stanza 4) His youthful song, unsung for years.
  • With my old lament / And my idle dream, (Stanzas 1 and 4) Sadness and dreaming, then and now.
  • I remember, I remember / The house where I was born, (Stanza 1) Refrain; home as the root of belonging.
  • But now, I often wish the night / Had borne my breath away (Stanza 1) The first, shocking turn to the present.
  • The tree is living yet! (Stanza 2) The tree outlasts his childhood.
  • My spirit flew in feathers then / That is so heavy now, (Stanza 3) Light past, heavy present.
  • To know I'm farther off from Heaven / Than when I was a boy. (Stanza 4) Knowledge gained, innocence lost.
