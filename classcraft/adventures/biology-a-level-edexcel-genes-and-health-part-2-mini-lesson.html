Mission 1

Mission 1: How can you show that the molecules in a membrane never stop moving?

Make a guess: How can you show that the molecules in a membrane never stop moving?

Mission 1

The cell-fusion test

  • Label the surface proteins of a mouse cell with one fluorescent dye, and a human cell's with another.
  • Fuse the two cells into one.
  • At first, each colour covers its own half.
  • At 37 °C, within about 40 minutes the colours are mixed all over.
  • The proteins moved sideways through the bilayer. The membrane is fluid.
  • This was Frye and Edidin's experiment (1970).
  • When they cooled the cells to about 15 °C or below, the colours did not mix within 40 minutes: in a cold membrane, molecules move far more slowly.
Mission 1Read the data

Temperature and mixing

Mission 1

How the model was built

  • Each model was an interpretation of the data available at the time.
  • 1925: lipid from red blood cells spread out to cover about twice the cells' surface area. A bilayer.
  • 1935, Davson–Danielli: a lipid bilayer sandwiched between two continuous layers of protein.
  • Then new data did not fit:
    • Freeze-fracture electron microscopy split membranes down the middle and showed particles (proteins) inside the bilayer.
    • Many membrane proteins have hydrophobic regions, so they must sit in the core, not on the watery surface.
    • Cell fusion showed proteins move.
  • 1972, Singer–Nicolson: the fluid mosaic model. It explains all of these.
Quick check
?Which observation was evidence against the Davson–Danielli model?
Mission 1

Cholesterol steadies the membrane

  • Cholesterol sits between the phospholipids: its –OH by the heads, its rings among the tails.
  • It restricts the movement of the other molecules in the membrane.
  • This makes the membrane more stable and less fluid, and less leaky to water-soluble substances.
Quick check
?Which statement describes cholesterol in the plasma membrane of an animal cell?
Mission check

?A student claims that proteins in the plasma membrane can move sideways within the membrane. Which observation would best support this claim?

Mixing after fusion and a bleached patch filling in point the same way: membrane proteins drift sideways through a fluid bilayer.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why does beetroot bleed its red pigment into hot water but not into cold?

Make a guess: Why does beetroot bleed its red pigment into hot water but not into cold?

Mission 2

Why the pigment stays in

  • Beetroot's red pigment (betalain) is stored in the cell vacuole.
  • To leave, it must cross two membranes: the tonoplast and the cell-surface membrane.
  • The cell wall is fully permeable: it does not hold the pigment in.
  • Betalain is large and polar. It cannot cross an intact phospholipid bilayer.
  • So pigment in the water = damaged membranes. More pigment = more permeable.
Mission 2

Temperature

  • Warmer: phospholipids gain kinetic energy and move more. The bilayer becomes more fluid; permeability rises slowly.
  • Above about 40 °C: membrane proteins denature. Their shape is lost, leaving gaps in the bilayer.
  • Permeability then rises steeply: pigment pours out.
  • Below 0 °C: ice crystals can pierce membranes; thawed tissue leaks.
  • Say: "membrane proteins denature" — not: "heat breaks the bonds in the fatty acid tails".
Mission 2

Solvents

  • Organic solvents such as ethanol (alcohol) dissolve in the bilayer and disrupt it: lipids dissolve in them.
  • They also denature membrane proteins.
  • Higher solvent concentration → more disruption → more pigment leaks.
  • Core practical 3 names alcohol concentration as one variable.
Quick check
?Beetroot discs held at 70 °C release far more pigment than discs held at 30 °C. Which explanation is correct?
Mission 2Practical

Beetroot and membrane permeability

  • Core practical 3: the effect of alcohol concentration or temperature on membrane permeability.
  • Cut cylinders with a cork borer; trim to equal length. Same surface area each.
  • Rinse in distilled water until it stays clear: removes pigment from cells cut open.
  • Put each in the same volume of water at one temperature (or ethanol concentration) for the same time.
  • Remove the beetroot, shake the tube, measure the liquid's absorbance in a colorimeter.
  • Control: same beetroot, size, volume and time. Only the independent variable changes.
Mission 2

Using a colorimeter

  • A colorimeter shines light of one colour through a sample in a cuvette.
  • Absorbance: how much light the sample absorbs. More pigment → higher absorbance.
  • Choose a green or blue-green filter: the red pigment absorbs these colours most.
  • Zero the colorimeter with a blank (distilled water) first.
  • Same filter and clean cuvette for every reading; light passes through the clear sides.
  • Some colorimeters give % transmission: more pigment → lower transmission.
Mission 2

Worked example: serial dilution and calibration

To turn absorbance into a pigment concentration, make a calibration curve.

  • Start with a pigment extract: 100%.
  • 1-in-2 dilution: 5.0 cm³ of extract + 5.0 cm³ distilled water → 50%.
  • Repeat from each new tube: 25%, 12.5%, 6.25%. Each step halves the concentration.
  • Measure each absorbance (illustrative data):

  • Plot absorbance against concentration; draw the best-fit line.
  • A sample reads 0.36: between 0.25 (25%) and 0.49 (50%).
  • Read across to the line, then down: about 38% of the extract's concentration.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why would a drip of pure water into a patient's vein do harm?

Make a guess: Why would a drip of pure water into a patient's vein do harm?

Mission 3

Osmosis

  • Osmosis: the net movement of water across a partially permeable membrane.
  • The membrane lets water through but not the solute.
  • Water molecules cross randomly both ways; more cross towards the more concentrated solution.
  • Solute molecules attract water molecules, so fewer water molecules are free to move. Free water molecules move from the dilute side to the concentrated side.
Quick check
?Two sucrose solutions, 0.2 mol dm⁻³ and 0.6 mol dm⁻³, are separated by a membrane that is permeable to water but not to sucrose. Which statement is correct?
Mission 3

Cells without a wall

  • Outside more dilute than the cytoplasm: water enters. The cell swells and can burst: lysis.
  • Red blood cells bursting: haemolysis.
  • Outside more concentrated: water leaves. The cell shrinks and its surface puckers: crenation.
  • Equal concentrations: water still crosses both ways, with no net movement.
Mission 3

Cells with a wall

  • Outside more dilute: water enters. The cell contents push on the wall: turgor pressure. The cell is turgid.
  • The wall resists, so the cell does not burst.
  • Outside more concentrated: water leaves; the contents shrink; the membrane pulls away from the wall: plasmolysis.
  • The wall is freely permeable: the gap fills with the outside solution.
  • No turgor pressure: the cell is flaccid.
  • Say: "the wall is freely permeable; the membrane is partially permeable" — not: "the wall keeps water out".
Quick check
?A strip of onion epidermis is mounted in a concentrated sucrose solution and viewed under a microscope. Which statement describes what happens to its cells?
Mission 3

Isotonic fluids in medicine

  • A common drip fluid has about the same solute concentration as blood plasma: 0.9% sodium chloride.
  • Pure water would enter red blood cells by osmosis. They would swell and burst.
  • A concentrated solution would draw water out of them. They would shrink.
Mission check

?Jam contains so much sugar that most bacteria landing on it cannot grow. Which statement explains this?

Water follows solute, whether it is entering red blood cells from pure water or leaving bacteria for the jam.

Mission 4

Mission 4: A cell keeps taking in a substance it already holds more of than its surroundings. How is that possible?

Make a guess: A cell keeps taking in a substance it already holds more of than its surroundings. How is that possible?

Mission 4

Active transport

  • Active transport moves particles against a concentration gradient: from low to high concentration.
  • It needs energy from ATP, made in respiration.
  • It needs a membrane protein that binds the particle.
  • The proteins are carrier proteins. Channel proteins cannot do it.
  • ATP is the immediate source of energy.
  • Each carrier binds only specific particles: active transport is selective.
  • Say: "active transport moves particles against their gradient" — not: "active transport is faster diffusion".
Mission 4

How a carrier pumps

  • (1) The particle binds to a specific site on the carrier, on one side of the membrane.
  • (2) ATP is hydrolysed to ADP + Pᵢ. The phosphate attaches to the carrier, which changes shape.
  • (3) The particle is released on the other side. The phosphate leaves; the carrier returns to its first shape.
  • No ATP, no shape change, no transport.
Quick check
?Root cells absorb nitrate ions from soil water in which the nitrate concentration is much lower than in the cell. When the roots are deprived of oxygen, nitrate uptake falls sharply. What explains these observations?
Mission 4

How do you know it is active?

  • Sign 1: the substance builds up above the outside concentration.
  • Sign 2: uptake falls sharply when the cell's ATP supply falls: no oxygen, or a respiratory inhibitor.
  • Diffusion is not affected by respiratory inhibitors. That is the test.
  • Ions leak back down their gradient. So a gradient needs a continuing energy supply, not a one-off.
Mission 4Read the data

Mission 4

Bulk transport: vesicles

  • Large molecules and particles cannot cross the bilayer. They move in vesicles.
  • Endocytosis (in): the membrane folds inwards around the material and pinches off a vesicle.
  • Exocytosis (out): a vesicle moves to the membrane, fuses with it and releases its contents outside. E.g. digestive enzymes, hormones, neurotransmitters.
  • Both need ATP: to reshape membrane and move vesicles.
  • Say: "endocytosis and exocytosis need ATP" — not: "they are passive because no pump is involved".
Quick check
?Cells of a hypothetical gland make a digestive enzyme, a large protein, and package it into vesicles inside the cell. A chemical is added that blocks exocytosis in these cells but does not affect how the enzyme is made or packaged. Which prediction about the cells is best supported?
Mission check

?Red blood cells hold K⁺ at a much higher concentration than the plasma around them. In donated blood stored at 4 °C, the cells slowly lose K⁺. When the cells are warmed to 37 °C with glucose, they take K⁺ back up to the original high level. Which explanation fits these observations?

A gradient against the odds is never free. Stop the energy supply and it runs down; restore it and the pumps rebuild it.

Mission 5

Mission 5: DNA's two strands are held together only by weak bonds. So why doesn't the molecule fall apart?

Make a guess: DNA's two strands are held together only by weak bonds. So why doesn't the molecule fall apart?

Mission 5

The nucleotide

  • DNA and RNA are polymers. Their monomers are nucleotides.
  • Edexcel calls a single nucleotide a mononucleotide.
  • Each nucleotide has three parts:
  • a pentose (5-carbon sugar) in the middle
  • a phosphate group, bonded to the sugar
  • a nitrogen-containing base, also bonded to the sugar
  • DNA nucleotide: deoxyribose + phosphate + one base: Adenine, Cytosine, Guanine or Thymine.
  • RNA nucleotide: ribose + phosphate + one base: A, C, G or Uracil.
  • Say: "a nucleotide contains a base" — not: "a nucleotide is a base".
Quick check
?Which is a correct description of a single nucleotide?
Mission 5

Nucleotides join into a strand

  • Condensation: the phosphate of one nucleotide bonds to the sugar of the next. Water is released.
  • The bond is a phosphodiester bond.
  • Repeated, this builds a sugar–phosphate backbone: one continuous chain of covalent bonds. Strong.
  • The bases stick out from the backbone. Each is bonded only to its own sugar.
  • n nucleotides in a chain: n − 1 bonds, n − 1 waters released.
  • Hydrolysis breaks phosphodiester bonds, using water, releasing nucleotides.
  • Say: "the backbone is sugar–phosphate–sugar" — not: "the bases are joined into a chain".
Mission 5

Two strands: the double helix

  • DNA has two polynucleotide strands.
  • Bases on opposite strands pair by hydrogen bonds.
  • Pairing is complementary: A with T, G with C. Never A–G or C–T.
  • The strands are antiparallel: they run in opposite directions.
  • The paired strands twist into a double helix.
  • One hydrogen bond is weak. Thousands along a molecule hold the strands firmly together.
  • Yet each can break: the strands can be separated without breaking the backbone.
  • Say: "complementary bases" — not: "matching" or "identical bases".
Quick check

Quick check

Mission 6

Mission 6: Your DNA never leaves the nucleus. So how do its instructions reach the place where proteins are made?

Make a guess: Your DNA never leaves the nucleus. So how do its instructions reach the place where proteins are made?

Mission 6

Transcription comes first

  • Transcription: making an RNA copy of a gene, using one DNA strand as a template.
  • It is the first stage of gene expression. Translation comes next.
  • In eukaryotes, the mRNA leaves the nucleus through a nuclear pore. The DNA stays behind.
Mission 6

Transcription, step by step

  • RNA polymerase binds at the start of a gene. The DNA unwinds; hydrogen bonds between bases break.
  • Only one strand, the template strand, is read.
  • The template strand is also called the antisense strand. The other is the coding (sense) strand.
  • Free RNA nucleotides pair with exposed template bases: A–U, T–A, C–G, G–C. Hydrogen bonds hold each pair.
  • RNA polymerase joins adjacent RNA nucleotides with phosphodiester bonds, building the sugar–phosphate backbone.
  • The RNA separates from the template. The DNA rewinds.
  • Say: "RNA polymerase joins adjacent RNA nucleotides" — not: "RNA polymerase pairs the bases". Base pairs form by hydrogen bonding.
Quick check
?A section of the DNA template strand of a gene has the base sequence TACGCAT. What is the base sequence of the RNA transcribed from this section?
Mission 6

Worked example: three sequences

  • Template strand: T A C G G A T C
  • mRNA (complementary to the template): A U G C C U A G
  • Coding strand (paired with the template): A T G C C T A G
  • So mRNA = the coding strand, with U in place of T.
  • mRNA is a single strand. Its sugar is ribose. It is a copy of one gene, so it is short.
  • Its bases are read in triplets: codons.
  • Each codon is complementary to a triplet on the template strand.
Quick check
?During transcription, what holds each RNA nucleotide in position against the DNA template strand while it is being added to the growing RNA?
Mission check

?α-Amanitin, a toxin from the death cap mushroom, blocks the RNA polymerase that makes mRNA in eukaryotic cells. It has no effect on DNA polymerase or on ribosomes. Which prediction about liver cells exposed to it is correct?

The DNA stays put. RNA polymerase makes a temporary, complementary copy of one gene, and that copy carries the message out. Stop RNA polymerase and the message stops.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Labelled proteins mix after cell fusion and refill a bleached patch: membrane components move sideways, faster when warmer.
  • Cholesterol sits among the phospholipids and restricts their movement, stabilising the membrane.

Mission 2

  • Pigment leaks only when membranes are damaged: heat (more fluid phospholipids, then denatured proteins above about 40 °C) and organic solvents such as ethanol both raise permeability.
  • Measure leakage with a colorimeter (blank first, green filter); convert absorbance to concentration with a calibration curve from a serial dilution.

Mission 3

  • Osmosis: net movement of water across a partially permeable membrane into the more concentrated solution; cells without walls burst or shrink, cells with walls become turgid or plasmolysed.

Mission 4

  • Active transport uses carrier (pump) proteins and ATP to move specific particles against their concentration gradient; it stops when the ATP supply stops.
  • Endocytosis and exocytosis move large substances in vesicles and need energy.

Mission 5

  • Nucleotide = pentose + phosphate + base. Condensation joins nucleotides into a strand with a covalent sugar–phosphate backbone.
  • DNA: two strands held by hydrogen bonds between complementary bases (A–T, G–C), twisted into a double helix. RNA: ribose, uracil, one strand.
  • The two strands are antiparallel.

Mission 6

  • RNA polymerase reads one DNA strand (the template) and joins complementary RNA nucleotides (A–U, C–G) to make mRNA.
  • mRNA has the coding strand's sequence with U for T; the DNA template is unchanged.
