A-level skills · contexts of production and reception (AO3)Screen 1

Two kinds of context

  • Context of production: when, where and how the text was written: its society, beliefs, genre conventions, theatre or publishing, the writer's situation.
  • Context of reception: how readers and audiences have received it since, from first reviews to modern productions and criticism.
  • The words on the page stay the same; what audiences notice, fear and sympathise with changes.
  • Context earns marks when it explains something in the text: ‘this helps explain why…’.

Jane Eyre's restlessness

A-level skills · contexts of production and reception (AO3)Screen 7

Jane Eyre's first readers and later ones

  • 1847: published under the name Currer Bell.
  • 1848: Elizabeth Rigby, in the Quarterly Review (December), condemned the novel as irreligious and linked its spirit to the revolutions abroad and Chartism at home, the unrest of that year.
  • 1966: Jean Rhys's novel Wide Sargasso Sea retells Bertha's story from the Caribbean.
  • 1979: Sandra Gilbert and Susan Gubar read Bertha as Jane's dark double, her suppressed anger.
  • 1985: Gayatri Spivak argued that Jane's freedom is bought at the colonised Bertha's expense.

?Why does The Merchant of Venice play differently now than in 1600?

The text hasn't changed; its audiences have. Written in a society that had expelled its Jews and was gripped by the Lopez affair, the play is now received after the Holocaust, so many audiences hear Shylock's speech as a plea for shared humanity. The strongest answers use both contexts to explain what the text does.

A shared pattern

Bradley (1904) found a common shape in Shakespeare's major tragedies: a hero "in high estate", suffering that ends in death, and a struggle both outside and inside the hero.

Shakespearean tragedy · hero and worldScreen 7

The critics divide

  • The hero: Bradley sees tragedy as "action issuing from character". He rejects the old view that Hamlet is "the tragedy of reflection"; Hamlet's delay comes from "a state of profound melancholy".
  • The world: Coleridge saw in Iago "the motive-hunting of a motiveless malignity". Gloucester's "As flies to wanton boys are we to the gods," blames the gods.
  • The audience: Johnson (1765) was "so shocked by Cordelia's death" he could hardly reread the ending.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Othello, Lear, Hamlet: is the flaw in the hero or the world?

Both. Shakespeare gives each hero a weakness (Lear's rashness, Othello's credulity, Hamlet's delay) and sets it in a world that exploits it (Iago, Goneril and Regan, Claudius's crime). The debate is about the balance, and critics from Coleridge to Bradley disagree, so argue it play by play.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Context of production: when, where and how a text was written. Context of reception: how it has been read and staged since.
  • The Merchant of Venice came from an England without a Jewish community and fresh from the Lopez affair; after the Holocaust, its antisemitism is staged and read very differently.
  • Jane Eyre was attacked in 1848 as irreligious and rebellious; feminist and post-colonial critics since 1966 have reread it, including through Bertha.
  • Context earns marks only when it explains something in the text.
  • In Component 02, read the unseen extract and your set texts in the context of your topic.
  • Shakespeare's tragedies share a pattern: a hero of high status, a flaw or error, a hostile world or villain, a fall that harms many, and order restored by survivors.
  • Hamlet's "The time is out of joint" puts the fault in the world and the burden on the hero; "the pale cast of thought" lets readers find a flaw in him too.
  • Critics divide: Bradley (character, melancholy), Coleridge (Iago's "motiveless malignity"), Johnson (shock at Cordelia's death). Treat each as a view to test.
  • Part (b) of the Section 1 question is a proposition: "flaw in the hero or the world?" is exactly that kind of debate.

Key quotations to learn

  • The time is out of joint. O cursed spite, (Hamlet, Act 1 Scene 5 (Hamlet)) The world is broken, and the task falls on him.
  • Is sicklied o’er with the pale cast of thought, (Hamlet, Act 3 Scene 1 (Hamlet)) Thought weakening action: a flaw in the hero?
  • More sinn’d against than sinning. (King Lear, Act 3 Scene 2 (Lear)) Lear's claim that others are more to blame.
  • As flies to wanton boys are we to the gods, / They kill us for their sport. (King Lear, Act 4 Scene 1 (Gloucester)) A world ruled by cruel gods.
  • Of one not easily jealous, but being wrought, (Othello, Act 5 Scene 2 (Othello)) Othello's own case: worked on from outside.
  • the motive-hunting of a motiveless malignity (Coleridge, note on Othello 1.3 (c.1818–19; Literary Remains, pg8533)) Evil located outside the hero.
