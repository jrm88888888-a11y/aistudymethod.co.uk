Traditions · tragedy before 1900

Revenge, verse and the fall

Pre-1900 tragic drama has deep roots:

  • Senecan revenge tragedy — inherited from Roman drama: a ghost demanding vengeance, feigned or real madness, a play-within-a-play, a bloody catastrophe (the tradition Kyd helped establish in English).
  • Renaissance and Jacobean tragedy — the fall of a great figure, the malcontent, the corrupt court, and Aristotle’s tragic pattern of hamartia and catastrophe.
  • Verse drama — blank verse as the medium of high feeling, and its collapse into prose as a mark of disorder.

Comparative angle: a tragic play and a tragic or elegiac poem may share a subject — death, revenge, ambition — but handle it through utterly different forms.

Traditions · comedy before 1900

Manners, wit and the blocking figure

Pre-1900 comic drama has its own conventions:

  • Comedy of manners — from Restoration comedy onward: witty, epigrammatic satire of a fashionable society and its marriage market.
  • The blocking figure — a father, guardian or law obstructing young love, overcome by the comic resolution.
  • Disguise and mistaken identity — engines of comic confusion and of gender play.
  • The reconciling ending — marriage and renewed community, following the comic pattern Northrop Frye described.

Dark edge: the strongest reading notices whom the happy ending excludes or silences.

AO2 · dramatic method

Reading the play as a play

Analyse the pre-1900 drama text as a score for performance:

  • Soliloquy and aside — access to a mind that may be plotting or self-deceiving.
  • Blank verse and prose — the shift between them marks status, sanity and register.
  • Dramatic irony — the audience knowing what a character does not.
  • Stagecraft — entrances, exits, off-stage violence, the play-within-a-play.

Test: could your point be made only about a play? If it works unchanged for the poem, you have not yet analysed drama.

AO4 · comparing drama and poetry

How the two forms meet

The essay compares a play with a poem, so you must compare across forms:

  1. Lead with the shared theme — ambition, grief, love, power — not with either text.
  2. Compare how form handles it: a soliloquy and a lyric both voice interiority, but a soliloquy is public, spoken on a stage, while a lyric is overheard.
  3. Interleave in theme-paragraphs across both texts.
  4. Prize the productive difference the two forms create.

Stem: “Where the play must externalise inner conflict into speech an audience can hear, the poem can keep it silent and interior.”

AO3 · theatrical context

The stage before 1900

Context for pre-1900 drama is often theatrical:

  • Playhouse conditions — the open-air Renaissance stage, boy actors, then the Restoration indoor theatre and the first actresses.
  • Beliefs — the ordered cosmos, kingship, honour, the marriage market, shifting across the centuries.
  • Reception — how the play’s meaning changes for later audiences.

Weld it: because the role was written for a boy actor, the play’s games with gender and disguise carry a theatrical charge a modern casting cannot reproduce.

AO5 · interpretation

Reading pre-1900 drama through theory

Bring a lens that yields a textual claim:

  • Feminist — who is granted stage-agency; how disguise both frees and contains women.
  • Marxist — how rank and money structure who acts and who is acted upon.
  • Performance — how staging choices reinterpret the text for a new audience.

Discipline: not “a Marxist would note class”, but “the play gives servants only prose and interruptions, so the stage itself is organised by rank.”
