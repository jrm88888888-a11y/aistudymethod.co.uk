Mission 1

The "omni" words

  • Omnipotent: all-powerful.
  • Omniscient: all-knowing: past, present, future, and every thought.
  • Omnipresent: present everywhere at once. "If I ascend up into heaven, you are there." (Psalm 139:8)
  • Omnibenevolent: all-loving and all-good.
  • These are ways of saying God has no limits.
Mission 1

Beyond the world, and within it

  • Transcendent: beyond and outside the universe, space and time; greater than anything humans can imagine. "My thoughts are not your thoughts" (Isaiah 55:8).
  • Immanent: present and active within the world and in people's lives.
  • A transcendent God inspires awe and worship; an immanent God is close enough to pray to.
  • Most Jews, Christians and Muslims hold both together: God is not part of the world, yet is never absent from it.
  • The Qur'an: "We are nearer to him than his jugular vein" (Qur'an 50:16, Pickthall), yet "Naught is as His likeness" (Qur'an 42:11, Pickthall).
  • Catholic teaching: God is utterly beyond creation, yet keeps every creature in being at every moment.

Mission 1

A good God and a good creation

  • "God saw everything that he had made, and, behold, it was very good." (Genesis 1:31)
  • Catholic teaching: God is perfect goodness, and creates out of love, not need.
  • So everything God makes is good and reflects God's goodness, as a work shows its maker.
  • Matter and the body are good, not evil: they will share in God's final glory.
  • Evil is not something God created; it is a lack or twisting of good (St Augustine).

Mission 2

Two kinds of evil

  • Evil: whatever is bad, harmful or wrong. Suffering: the pain, physical or mental, that it causes.
  • Moral evil: caused by human choices: murder, cruelty, war, theft, bullying.
  • Natural evil (natural suffering): caused by nature, not by humans: earthquakes, floods, disease, famine from drought.
  • Some cases are mixed: a quake kills more people where buildings were badly made.
  • Moral evil raises the question: why did God make people free to do this? Natural evil: why did God make a world that works like this?

Mission 2

The inconsistent triad

  • Theists (Christians, Jews, Muslims, Sikhs and many Hindus) believe God is omnipotent (all-powerful) and omnibenevolent (all-loving).
  • Most also say God is omniscient (all-knowing): God knows about every evil.
  • But evil exists.
  • The problem: these three statements seem not to fit together. If God knew, could stop it and wanted to, why does it happen?
  • This is the inconsistent triad. Many versions add omniscience, giving four statements; the name inconsistent triad is kept. Philosophers call it the logical problem of evil.
  • The evidential problem: even if some evil is needed, why so much, and why such pointless suffering?
Mission 2Read the source

Epicurus' old questions

David Hume (1711–1776), Dialogues concerning Natural Religion (1779), Part X, has a character repeat questions credited to the Greek philosopher Epicurus (341–270 BCE):

> "Is he willing to prevent evil, but not able? then is he impotent. Is he able, but not willing? then is he malevolent. Is he both able and willing? whence then is evil?"

Q1 Which quality of God does the first question challenge?

Show answer

Omnipotence: "not able" means "impotent", powerless.

Q2 Which does the second challenge?

Show answer

Omnibenevolence: "not willing" means "malevolent", ill-willed.

Mission 2

Innocent suffering and providence

  • Providence: God's loving care for the world, guiding it towards good.
  • Innocent suffering is the hardest case: babies with painful illnesses, children in war, animals in fires. They have done nothing to deserve it.
  • In Fyodor Dostoevsky's novel The Brothers Karamazov (1880), Ivan refuses any future harmony bought with the tears of a tortured child (paraphrase).
  • Even believers cry out: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Psalm 22:1, WEBBE). Christians recall that Jesus prayed these words on the cross (Mark 15:34).
  • So the question is not only "Does God exist?" but "Can God's providence be trusted?"
Mission 2

Reject, question or examine

  • Some reject belief: atheists argue that the triad shows an all-powerful, all-loving God does not exist.
  • Humanists: suffering has natural causes (evolution, plate movement, disease) and human ones; no God is needed to explain it; people should reduce it themselves.
  • Some question: personal tragedy can shake faith; doubt and lament are found in scripture (Psalm 22, Job).
  • Some examine: believers think harder about what God's power and love mean, and find answers (see Mission 3).

Mission 2Diversity

Where evil comes from: Abrahamic faiths

  • Christian: humans misused free will in the Fall (Genesis 3); "sin entered into the world through one man, and death through sin" (Romans 5:12, WEBBE). Original Sin: a damaged nature passed to all.
  • Catholic: God's creation is good: "God saw everything that he had made, and, behold, it was very good" (Genesis 1:31, WEBBE). God made no evil; evil spoils good things.
  • Jewish: humans have a yetzer ha-tov (good inclination) and a yetzer ha-ra (inclination to evil) and free choice: "I have set before thee life and death, the blessing and the curse; therefore choose life" (Deuteronomy 30:19, JPS 1917).
  • Muslim: Iblis refused to bow to Adam "through pride" (Surah 2:34, Pickthall) and now tempts humans; hardship is a test: "We shall try you with something of fear and hunger" (Surah 2:155, Pickthall).
Mission 2Evaluate

"Evil and suffering prove that God does not exist."

  • Reasons for: the triad seems logically inconsistent; the amount of suffering seems pointless (evidential problem); innocent suffering seems unfair; natural causes explain suffering without God.
  • Reasons against: God may have good reasons we cannot see (Isaiah 45:9); free will needs the possibility of evil; suffering may have a purpose (see Mission 3); many who suffer most keep their faith.
  • Another view: the problem may show that God's power or love must be understood differently, not that God does not exist.
  • Judge it by: Is "prove" too strong a word? Does free will explain natural evil? Can a finite mind judge God's reasons?

Mission 3

Three kinds of answer

  • A theodicy ("justifying God") tries to show why a good, all-powerful God allows evil. The word was coined by Gottfried Leibniz (1710).
  • Philosophical answers: free will, soul-making.
  • Scriptural answers: Job, the Psalms, life as a test, the suffering of Christ.
  • Practical answers: prayer, charity, care for those who suffer.
  • Most believers use all three. Many say: we may not fully explain suffering, but we can trust God and act.
Mission 3

Augustine: free will and privation

  • St Augustine of Hippo (354–430), Confessions, City of God, Enchiridion.
  • Augustine teaches that God made a good world. Evil is not a thing God made: "evil has no positive nature; but the loss of good has received the name 'evil.'" (Augustine, City of God XI.9, Dods)
  • Enchiridion 3.11 (paraphrase): in a body, disease and wounds are simply the absence of health. When healed, they do not go somewhere else; they cease to exist. Evil is a privation (lack) of good.
  • Angels and humans misused free will: the fall of the angels and of Adam and Eve.
  • Original Sin: all humans inherit a damaged nature; natural evil follows the disorder sin brought into creation.
  • So evil comes from creatures, not from God. God is just to allow its consequences, and merciful to save through Christ.
Mission 3

Irenaeus and Hick: soul-making

  • St Irenaeus (c. 130–c. 202), Against Heresies: humans are made in God's image, but must grow into his likeness (Genesis 1:26).
  • Humans are made immature on purpose, so they can grow freely into goodness.
  • John Hick (1922–2012), Evil and the God of Love (1966): the world is a "vale of soul-making" (a phrase from the poet Keats).
  • Courage, compassion and forgiveness can only grow where there is real danger and pain.
  • Epistemic distance: God keeps a distance, so that people are free to believe or not.
  • Hick: in the end all people will reach the likeness of God (universal salvation), so all suffering will prove worthwhile.
Quick check

Mission 3

Do these answers work?

  • Against Augustine: science shows death and disease long before humans existed, so natural evil cannot all come from the Fall. How could perfect beings choose evil? Why punish all humans for Adam's sin?
  • Against soul-making: the amount of suffering is too great: the Holocaust destroyed people, it did not make souls. Babies and animals suffer without growing. Is it loving to use pain as a teacher?
  • For both: free will is real only if evil is possible; virtues need challenges; God's goodness may reach beyond this life (Romans 8:18).
  • Atheists and Humanists: the answers explain away real pain; a world with less suffering would still allow free will.

Job's losses

Job, a good man, loses his children, wealth and health:

"Naked I came out of my mother's womb, and naked will I return there. The LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away. Blessed be the LORD's name." (Job 1:21, WEBBE)

  • His friends insist he must have sinned. God rejects their view. God's answer is a question: "Where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth?" (Job 38:4, WEBBE)

Mission 3

Judaism: Job, Psalms and the Shoah

  • Life as a test: "God did prove Abraham" (Genesis 22:1, JPS 1917).
  • The Shoah (Holocaust, 1933–1945; six million Jews murdered, most between 1941 and 1945) brought very different Jewish responses:
  • Eliezer Berkovits (1908–1992), Faith after the Holocaust (1973): God hides his face so humans can be free; the evil was human (paraphrase).
  • Emil Fackenheim (1916–2003): Jews must not give Hitler "posthumous victories" by abandoning Judaism (paraphrase).
  • Richard Rubenstein (1924–2021), After Auschwitz (1966): belief in a God who controls history is no longer possible (paraphrase).
  • Elie Wiesel (1928–2016): faith that argues with God, in the tradition of Job and Abraham.
Mission 3

Christians: the suffering of Christ

  • Christians believe God does not stay distant from suffering: in Jesus, God shares it.
  • "He was despised and rejected by men, a man of suffering and acquainted with disease." (Isaiah 53:3, WEBBE)
  • "By his wounds we are healed." (Isaiah 53:5, WEBBE) Christians read this as pointing to Christ's death.
  • Salvifici Doloris (John Paul II, 1984) 13 (paraphrase): the answer to "why suffering?" is found in love, shown on the Cross; suffering remains a mystery.
  • Salvifici Doloris 23 (paraphrase): those who suffer can share in Christ's sufferings, and so in his work of redemption (Colossians 1:24).
  • Catholic ambivalence: suffering is an evil to fight (medicine, Lourdes, care of the sick), yet when it comes it can be united with Christ's.
Mission 3

Practical responses

  • Prayer: comfort, strength and solidarity: God "comforts us in all our affliction, that we may be able to comfort those who are in any affliction" (2 Corinthians 1:4, WEBBE).
  • Charity: Christians through CAFOD or Christian Aid; Muslims through zakah and Islamic Relief; Jews through tzedakah; Sikhs through Khalsa Aid.
  • Hope: many believe suffering will end: God "will wipe away every tear from their eyes" (Revelation 21:4, WEBBE).
  • Humanists: use reason, science and compassion to reduce suffering now; this life is the only one, so it matters.

Mission 3Evaluate

"Religious responses solve the problem of evil."

  • Reasons for: free will explains moral evil; soul-making gives suffering a purpose; Job shows humans cannot judge God.
  • Also for: Christ's suffering shows God shares pain.
  • Also for: life as a test gives meaning.
  • Reasons against: natural evil existed before humans; the amount and pointlessness of suffering; innocent suffering of children and animals.
  • Also against: the Shoah led some Jewish thinkers (Rubenstein) to reject belief in a God who controls history.
  • Another view: responses may not solve the problem as a puzzle but help believers live with it; practical responses matter most.
  • Divergent views of success: many Catholics and Muslims say the answers, taken together, are enough for faith; others stress mystery.
  • Judge it by: Does it explain natural as well as moral evil? Does it fit the amount of suffering? Does it help people who suffer?

Mission 4

Good, evil, sin, crime

  • Morality: ideas of right and wrong that guide how people act.
  • Good: what is right, kind and beneficial. Evil: what is wrong, harmful or against God's will.
  • Sin: an act (or thought) against God's will or religious law. A religious idea.
  • Crime: an act against the law of the state, which the state can punish.
  • Some acts are both (murder, theft). Some sins are not crimes (most lies, greed). Some crimes are not seen as sins by everyone (breaking an unjust law in protest).
Mission 4

Absolute or relative?

  • Absolute morality: some acts are always right or wrong, whatever the situation or culture. "You shall not murder." (Exodus 20:13)
  • Relative morality: what is right depends on the situation, culture or consequences.
  • Absolutists value clear, fair rules; relativists value flexibility and compassion.
  • Many religious believers hold both: some absolute rules, with care for each situation.
  • Catholic teaching holds that some acts are intrinsically evil (always wrong), such as killing the innocent.

Mission 4

Conscience

  • Conscience: an inner sense of right and wrong that guides and judges our actions.
  • Christians see it as God's voice or law within: Paul says even Gentiles without the Jewish law "show the work of the law written in their hearts, their conscience testifying with them" (Romans 2:15).
  • Cardinal John Henry Newman (1801–1890) called conscience "the aboriginal Vicar of Christ" (Letter to the Duke of Norfolk, 1875).
  • Catholics must form their conscience through prayer, scripture and Church teaching, then follow it.
  • Non-religious view: conscience comes from upbringing, culture and evolved empathy.
Mission 4

Intentions and actions

  • An intention is the reason or aim behind an action.
  • The same act can have good or evil intentions: a gift given out of love, or to bribe.
  • Christianity: "man looks at the outward appearance, but the LORD looks at the heart." (1 Samuel 16:7)
  • Islam: the Prophet taught that actions are judged by their intentions (niyyah) (Sahih al-Bukhari 1, paraphrase).
  • Buddhism: actions are called skilful or unskilful, not sinful; the Buddha taught that intention (cetana) is what makes karma. "If a man speaks or acts with an evil thought, pain follows him" (Dhammapada 1, Müller).
  • Most traditions judge both: a good intention does not make every act right.

Mission 4

Can causing suffering ever be good?

  • Sometimes suffering is caused for good ends: surgery, a vaccine injection, punishment to protect society.
  • Joseph tells his brothers: "you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good" (Genesis 50:20).
  • Catholic teaching: suffering can help develop virtues such as patience, courage and compassion: "suffering produces perseverance; and perseverance, proven character" (Romans 5:3–4).
  • Natural Law and conscience as evidence of God's goodness: Aquinas: the first precept is "good is to be done and pursued, and evil is to be avoided" (Summa Theologiae I-II, q. 94, a. 2). Catholics see this law built into human nature as a sign of a good Creator.
  • But: causing suffering for pleasure, revenge or profit is evil in every tradition.
Mission 4Evaluate

"It can never be good to cause suffering."

  • Reasons for: suffering is the clearest sign of harm; Buddhists and Hindus value ahimsa (non-violence); Jesus taught love of enemies; once one hurt is justified, others may follow.
  • Reasons against: surgery and medicine cause pain to heal; just punishment protects others; Romans 5:3–4: suffering can build character; some believe God brings good out of evil (Genesis 50:20).
  • Judge it by: Does the intention matter more than the result? Is the suffering chosen by the person (as in surgery) or imposed? Is there another way to reach the good?
Mission 4

Sin and crime: types and consequences

  • Types of crime: against the person (assault), against property (theft), against the state (terrorism), hate crimes.
  • Types of sin (Catholic): mortal (grave, chosen knowingly) and venial (less serious).
  • Islam: major sins (e.g. shirk, murder) and minor sins.
  • Consequences: crime → punishment by courts; sin → separation from God, bad karma, need for repentance and forgiveness.

Mission 5

Three ways to decide

  • An ethical theory is a method for deciding what is right.
  • Rules and duties: some acts are right or wrong in themselves (e.g. Natural Law: preserve life, never kill the innocent). Often absolutist.
  • Consequences: the right act produces the best results (e.g. utilitarianism, situation ethics). Often relativist.
  • Character: become a good person, and good acts follow (virtue ethics).
  • Religious believers use all three, alongside scripture and religious authority.
Mission 5

Natural Law

  • Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274), Summa Theologiae: God built a moral order into human nature that reason can discover.
  • The first precept: "good is to be done and pursued, and evil is to be avoided" (Summa Theologiae I-II, q. 94, a. 2).
  • Primary precepts (as usually taught): preserve life; reproduce; educate the young; live in society; worship God.
  • Secondary precepts apply them: e.g. do not murder (from preserving life).
  • Evidence of God's goodness: a built-in moral law and a conscience that recognises it point to a good Creator who wants humans to flourish.
Mission 5Read the source

Aquinas on natural inclinations

"whatever is a means of preserving human life, and of warding off its obstacles, belongs to the natural law" (Summa Theologiae I-II, q. 94, a. 2)

  • Aquinas adds: humans also share with animals the inclination to "sexual intercourse, education of offspring", and, by reason, "to know the truth about God, and to live in society".

Q1 Which primary precept does the first sentence describe?

Show answer

Preserve life.

Q2 How does Aquinas find these precepts?

Show answer

Mission 5

Virtue ethics

  • Aristotle (384–322 BCE), Nicomachean Ethics: become a person of good character; aim at eudaimonia (flourishing).
  • A virtue is a habit, the mean between two extremes: courage lies between cowardice and recklessness.
  • Christian virtues: faith, hope and love: "The greatest of these is love." (1 Corinthians 13:13). Also the cardinal virtues: prudence, justice, fortitude and temperance.
  • Suffering and the virtues: Catholics believe facing suffering can build patience, courage and compassion: "suffering produces perseverance; and perseverance, proven character" (Romans 5:3–4).
  • Criticism: it does not tell you exactly what to do in a hard case.

Mission 6

The moral argument

  • Most people believe some things are really wrong, not just disliked: torturing a child for fun, for example.
  • If right and wrong are real and binding on everyone (objective morality), where does that law come from?
  • The moral argument: a moral law needs a lawgiver, a source of goodness beyond human opinion. Believers say this is God.
  • A second strand: we feel answerable to our conscience, as if to a person.
  • Both strands treat human morality as a pointer to God, not a proof by itself.
Mission 6

Conscience

  • Conscience: the inner sense of right and wrong that urges us to do good and judges what we have done.
  • John Henry Newman (1801–1890), Catholic cardinal, A Grammar of Assent (1870): when we feel shame or fear at disobeying conscience, this implies Someone we are answerable to (paraphrase).
  • Paul: people without the Jewish Law still "show the work of the law written in their hearts, their conscience testifying with them" (Romans 2:15, WEBBE).
  • Catholic teaching: conscience is a person's most secret core, where they are alone with God, whose voice echoes within (CCC 1776, paraphrase).
Mission 6

Natural Law

  • Natural Law (Thomas Aquinas, c. 1225–1274): God's plan for creation is built into human nature and can be known by reason.
  • Everyone can see some basic goods: protecting life, raising children, learning, living in society, worshipping God.
  • People of every culture share these basics. Catholics see this as evidence of God's goodness: God has made humans able to know and choose the good.
  • Conscience applies Natural Law to each choice.
  • Catholic teaching adds that suffering can help virtues grow: courage, patience, compassion. This is one way Catholics see God's goodness even in suffering (more in Mission 3).

Mission 6

Objections

  • Conscience from upbringing. Sigmund Freud (1856–1939), The Ego and the Id (1923): conscience is the superego, the rules of parents and society taken inside us.
  • Morality from evolution. Charles Darwin, The Descent of Man (1871): the moral sense grew out of social instincts that helped groups survive.
  • Morals differ. Cultures and ages disagree about right and wrong, so morality may be relative, not one law from God.
  • The Euthyphro dilemma (Plato, Euthyphro, 4th century BCE): is something good because God commands it (then goodness seems arbitrary), or does God command it because it is good (then goodness does not depend on God)?
  • Humanists: people can be good without God. Morality comes from empathy, reason and care for human wellbeing.
Mission 6

Religious replies

  • Goodness is God's nature. Aquinas and many Jewish thinkers: God commands what is good because God is good. Goodness is neither arbitrary nor outside God.
  • Shared core. Cultures differ in detail, but almost all value life, honesty, fairness and care for the weak: what Natural Law predicts.
  • Explaining how is not explaining away. Evolution or upbringing may explain how conscience develops; believers say this does not show it is not also God's voice.
  • Conscience can defy society. People have followed conscience against their upbringing and their government, as some Christians did under the Nazis. That is hard to explain if conscience is only absorbed rules.
  • Believers accept that non-religious people can be very good: the argument asks where goodness comes from, not who has it.
Mission 6Evaluate

"Conscience is the voice of God."

  • Reasons for: we feel answerable to conscience as to a person (Newman); a law "written in their hearts" (Romans 2:15); shared moral basics across cultures (Natural Law); conscience can stand against society.
  • Reasons against: conscience is the superego (Freud); evolution explains the moral sense (Darwin); consciences disagree; Humanists are good without God.
  • Another view: conscience can be God-given and still need forming by teaching and reason, so it can err (Catholic teaching).
  • Judge it by: Does explaining how conscience develops settle where it comes from? Why do consciences disagree? Does objective morality need a source beyond humans?
Mission check

?A soldier is ordered to fire on peaceful protesters. Everyone around her obeys, and refusing means punishment, yet she refuses: "I couldn't live with myself." Which comment would John Henry Newman most likely make?

The same experience of conscience can be read as God's voice, as absorbed rules, or as evolved instinct. The moral argument asks which reading explains it best.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Omnipotent, omniscient, omnipresent, omnibenevolent; transcendent and immanent; personal and impersonal; knowable and unknowable.
  • God is good, so creation is good (Genesis 1:31).

Mission 2

  • Moral evil comes from human choices; natural evil from nature. Suffering raises the inconsistent triad (omnipotent, omnibenevolent God and evil) and the evidential problem; innocent suffering is the hardest case.

Mission 3

  • Augustine: evil is a privation of good, caused by misused free will (the Fall). Irenaeus and Hick: the world is a vale of soul-making. Both face objections (evolution; the amount of suffering).
  • Job: trust God beyond understanding; practical responses: prayer and charity; Humanists act to reduce suffering.
  • Jews respond through Job, the Psalms, tzedakah and, after the Shoah, very different theologies.
  • Catholics see suffering through the Cross (Isaiah 53; Salvifici Doloris 13, 23).

Mission 4

  • Sin is against God's will; crime is against the law of the state; some acts are both.
  • Absolute morality: some acts always wrong; relative morality: depends on the situation.
  • Most traditions judge intentions as well as actions; conscience, scripture and religious law guide believers.

Mission 5

  • Situation ethics: do the most loving thing (agape). Utilitarianism: the greatest happiness of the greatest number.
  • Natural Law: reason discovers God's moral order; primary precepts: life, reproduction, education, society, worship.
  • Virtue ethics: build good character; Catholics believe suffering can build virtues.

Mission 6

  • The moral argument: objective moral law and the experience of conscience point to God as lawgiver (Newman; Romans 2:15; Kant's postulate).
  • Natural Law: God's goodness built into human reason; Catholics see conscience as God's voice within.
  • Objections: Freud (superego), Darwin (evolution), relativism, Euthyphro, Humanist ethics.
