Mission 1

Mission 1: Can a living thing ever mutate because it needs to?

Make a guess: Can a living thing ever mutate because it needs to?

Mission 1

Copying is almost perfect

  • Every cell division copies billions of bases.
  • DNA polymerase sometimes pairs the wrong base. Proofreading and repair fix almost all errors.
  • The few that escape become permanent: spontaneous mutations.
  • Errors in DNA repair add more.
  • So mutations happen even with no harmful agent present. Each person carries dozens of new mutations neither parent had.
Mission 1

Mutagens raise the rate

  • A mutagen increases the rate of mutation.
  • Chemical mutagens: e.g. nitrous acid (alters bases so they mispair); benzo[a]pyrene in tobacco smoke (binds to bases).
  • Ultraviolet light: joins neighbouring thymines, distorting the DNA.
  • Ionising radiation (X-rays, gamma rays): breaks DNA strands and damages bases.
  • Not mutagens: visible light, microwaves, radio waves. Too little energy to change DNA.
Quick check
?Cultures of cells were exposed separately to (i) ultraviolet light, (ii) visible light and (iii) nitrous acid, a chemical that alters bases in DNA. Which treatments are expected to raise the mutation rate above the background rate?
Mission 1

Body cells, germ cells and cancer

  • Germ cells make gametes. A mutation there can be inherited: it is then in every cell of the offspring.
  • Somatic (body) cells: a mutation passes only to that cell's descendants. It is not inherited.
  • Most somatic mutations do nothing noticeable.
  • A mutation in a gene that controls the cell cycle can let a cell divide without control: a step towards cancer.
  • Usually several such mutations are needed before a tumour forms.
Quick check
?A mutation occurs in a gene that controls the cell cycle in one cell lining a man's colon. Which statement about the consequences is correct?
Mission 1

Random, not directed

  • A mutation can happen anywhere in the genome.
  • Not every base is equally likely: some sites mutate far more often than others.
  • No natural mechanism makes a chosen change to a chosen base to produce a useful trait.
  • A mutagen raises the rate. It does not pick the gene.
  • The environment selects among variants that already exist. It does not create them.
  • Say: "a random mutation that happened to give resistance" — not: "the bacteria mutated to become resistant".

Mission 1

The only source of new alleles

  • A new allele can arise only by mutation.
  • Meiosis and fertilisation reshuffle existing alleles into new combinations. They make no new alleles.
  • Most mutations are harmful or neutral to the individual. A few are beneficial.
  • Over the long term, mutation is essential for evolution by natural selection.
Quick check
?Why is gene mutation described as the original source of all genetic variation?
Mission 1

SNPs, many alleles and PKU

  • A single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP): one base position that differs between individuals. It began as a base substitution.
  • Many SNPs change no amino acid (degenerate code).
  • A gene pool can hold any number of alleles of a gene. A diploid individual inherits only two.
  • Phenylketonuria (PKU): a recessive condition. Mutations in an autosomal gene for the enzyme that converts phenylalanine to tyrosine. Many different mutant alleles are known.
  • Two faulty alleles → phenylalanine builds up. Treated with a low-phenylalanine diet.
  • Commercial genetic tests report SNPs linked to disease risk: a probability, not a diagnosis. Expert interpretation is needed.
Mission 1

Why some genes barely change

  • Conserved sequence: identical or similar across a species or group of species.
  • Highly conserved: similar over long periods of evolution.
  • Example: histone H4 in peas and cattle differs at only 2 of its 102 amino acids.
  • Hypothesis 1, functional requirement: almost any change damages the product, so natural selection removes the carriers.
  • Hypothesis 2, slower mutation rate: fewer mutations arise in that sequence.
  • Say: "changes are removed by selection" — not: "the gene cannot mutate".
Quick check
?The rate at which new mutations appear per generation was measured directly in a highly conserved gene and in a gene that is not conserved. The rates were the same, but far fewer of the new mutations in the conserved gene persisted in later generations. Which hypothesis do the data support?
Mission check

?When a new insecticide was first used on a crop, it killed nearly all individuals of a hypothetical pest insect. After several years of use, most of the pest population was resistant. Which statement best explains how resistance became common?

Mutation supplies variation by chance; the environment only chooses among what is already there.

Mission 2

Mission 2: A gene has already been copied into a message. Can the cell still stop its protein being made?

Make a guess: A gene has already been copied into a message. Can the cell still stop its protein being made?

Mission 2

Control after transcription

  • Transcription is not the last switch.
  • After it, the mRNA can be edited, blocked or broken down early.
  • Splicing can give one gene several proteins.
  • How long each mRNA lasts sets how much protein it makes.
Mission 2

Splicing: introns out, exons joined

  • In eukaryotes, transcription makes primary mRNA (pre-mRNA): exons and introns.
  • Splicing removes the introns and joins the exons → mature mRNA.
  • Only mature mRNA leaves the nucleus to be translated.
  • Say: "introns are removed from the mRNA" — not: "introns are cut out of the gene".
Mission 2

Alternative splicing: one gene, several proteins

  • (1) Exons can be joined in different combinations.
  • (2) Different mature mRNAs → different polypeptides from one gene.
  • Example: one human gene gives calcitonin (a hormone) in thyroid cells and CGRP (a signalling peptide) in neurones. Different exons are kept in each.
  • So about 20 000 human genes code for many more proteins.
  • Say: "different exons are spliced together" — not: "the gene mutates to make each version".

Mission 2

How long does an mRNA last?

  • Nucleases in the cytoplasm break mRNA down.
  • In human cells an mRNA may last from minutes to days.
  • While it lasts, each mRNA is translated many times.
  • Long-lived mRNA → much protein. Short-lived mRNA → protein level can change quickly.
  • So controlling mRNA breakdown regulates translation.
Quick check
?Two genes in a human cell are transcribed at the same rate. mRNA from gene P is broken down by nucleases about 10 minutes after it is made; mRNA from gene Q persists for about two days. Assume both mRNAs are translated at the same rate while they persist. Which statement is correct?
Mission check

?A mutation inside an intron of a gene changes how the primary mRNA is spliced: exon 4 is now removed along with the introns. Exon 4 is exactly 60 bases long, and the base sequences of all the exons are unchanged. What is the effect?

The gene was untouched, yet the protein changed: what happens to the mRNA after transcription decides what is made.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Many cells in your body could still divide, yet most of the time they hold back. What keeps them in check?

Make a guess: Many cells in your body could still divide, yet most of the time they hold back. What keeps them in check?

Mission 3

Division on demand

  • Cells divide only when signals and internal checks allow it.
  • Genes that control the cycle make proteins that push it on or hold it back.
  • If control fails, a cell divides without restraint. Its descendants pile up into a tumour.
  • Uncontrolled division can lead to cancer.
Mission 3

Gates in the cycle

  • A checkpoint holds the cycle until conditions for the next stage are met.
  • G1 checkpoint: is the cell large enough? Are nutrients and signals to divide present? Is the DNA undamaged?
  • G2 checkpoint: is all the DNA replicated, and undamaged?
  • Spindle checkpoint (in metaphase): is every chromosome attached to the spindle? Only then does anaphase start.
  • Problem found → the cycle stops for repair. Damage that cannot be repaired → the cell may die by apoptosis.
  • Say: "a checkpoint holds the cell until conditions are met" — not: "a checkpoint destroys any faulty cell at once".
Quick check
?Which statement best describes the role of checkpoints in the cell cycle?
Mission 3

Cyclins: the timer

  • Cyclins are proteins whose concentration rises and falls in every cycle.
  • Different cyclins peak at different points in the cycle.
  • To pass a checkpoint, a specific cyclin must reach a threshold concentration.
  • After that checkpoint, the cyclin is broken down. Its level falls, so the cycle can repeat.
  • Say: "rising cyclin lets the cell pass the checkpoint" — not: "cyclin is a brake that must fall first".

Mission 3

When control fails

  • A mutation can disable a gene that controls the cycle.
  • A damaged cell then keeps dividing. Each division copies the damage to its descendants.
  • More mutations build up in that cell line.
  • Usually several mutations in one cell line are needed before a tumour forms.
  • The genes involved, oncogenes and tumour suppressor genes, are in the next mission.
  • Say: "cancer cells divide without the normal controls" — not: "cancer is just fast division".
Quick check
?The risk of developing most cancers rises steeply with age. Which explanation fits how tumours form?
Mission 3

Germ cells and body cells

  • Somatic (body) cell mutation: copied only into that cell's descendants.
  • It is not inherited. If it hits a cell-cycle gene, it may lead to cancer.
  • Germ cell mutation (in cells that make gametes): can be passed to offspring.
  • An inherited mutation is in every cell of the offspring.
  • Most mutations in body cells are harmless.
Quick check
?Which statement about mutations in germ cells and somatic cells is correct?
Mission check

?In a hypothetical mammal, protein Q halts the cell cycle at the G1 checkpoint when a cell's DNA is damaged and triggers apoptosis if the damage cannot be repaired. A mutation in a skin cell inactivates protein Q. Which statement best explains how this mutation can lead to cancer?

Every checkpoint is a brake. Lose one and a damaged cell keeps going; lose several and a tumour can start.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Cancer starts in the body's own genes. How does a normal gene turn into a cancer gene?

Make a guess: Cancer starts in the body's own genes. How does a normal gene turn into a cancer gene?

Mission 4

Benign or malignant

  • Both kinds of tumour are masses of cells formed by uncontrolled division.
  • Benign: stays in one place, often inside a capsule. Does not invade or spread. Does not cause cancer, but can still press on organs.
  • Malignant: invades neighbouring tissue. Cells can break away, travel in blood or lymph and form new tumours: metastasis. This is cancer.
  • Primary tumour: where it began. Secondary tumour: formed by cells that spread from the primary.
  • Malignant tumours often grow faster, but rates overlap. Invasion and spread define malignancy.
  • Say: "malignant = invades and can spread" — not just: "malignant = grows faster".
Quick check
?The mitotic index was determined for three samples (illustrative data): cells from a root-tip meristem, 0.12; cells from a tumour removed from a patient, 0.10; cells from a mature leaf, 0.00. A student concluded that the tumour must be benign because its mitotic index is lower than that of a healthy meristem. Which evaluation of this conclusion is correct?
Mission 4

Accelerators and brakes

  • Proto-oncogenes: normal genes. Their proteins stimulate division when the cell is signalled to divide.
  • A mutation can turn one into an oncogene: the protein is overactive or always on. Division is driven with no signal.
  • One mutated copy can be enough.
  • Tumour suppressor genes: their proteins slow division, repair DNA or trigger cell death.
  • A mutation that inactivates them removes the brake. Usually both copies must be lost.
  • Either change → uncontrolled division. Several such mutations together → a tumour.
  • Say: "an oncogene is a mutated version of the cell's own proto-oncogene" — not: "a foreign cancer gene".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly describes how mutations in genes that control the cell cycle lead to uncontrolled cell division?
Mission check

?In chronic myeloid leukaemia, a mutated gene in blood-forming cells makes an overactive protein that keeps the cells dividing. A drug that blocks this protein stops most of the cancer cells dividing. Which explanation is correct?

A cancer gene is the cell's own gene, stuck on (oncogene) or broken (tumour suppressor). Know which, and you know what a treatment must do.

Mission 5

Mission 5: How do scientists find out what a gene does, and how can they rewrite one?

Make a guess: How do scientists find out what a gene does, and how can they rewrite one?

Mission 5

Gene knockout

  • Gene knockout: a gene is changed so that it is inoperative. No functional product is made.
  • Compare the knockout organism's phenotype with a normal organism's.
  • Any difference is evidence of what the gene normally does.
  • The gene's DNA may still be there, just disrupted.
  • Say: "the gene is made inoperative" — not: "the gene is cut out of the chromosome".
Mission 5

Knockout libraries

  • A knockout library: a collection of strains, each with a different single gene knocked out.
  • Available for some model organisms used in research:
  • Yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae): a strain for almost every gene.
  • Mouse: an international project is knocking out every protein-coding gene and recording each phenotype.
  • Arabidopsis thaliana (a small plant): lines with most genes disrupted.
  • A researcher orders the strain for "their" gene, rather than making it.
Mission 5Read the data

What the yeast library showed

Real data from the yeast knockout collection (about 6000 genes):

Quick check
?Mice in which gene P has been knocked out show no detectable difference from normal mice under standard laboratory conditions. What is the best conclusion?
Mission 5

CRISPR–Cas9: find and cut

  • A tool adapted from a system found in bacteria.
  • (1) Cas9: an enzyme that cuts both strands of DNA.
  • (2) Guide RNA: about 20 bases, designed to be complementary to the target DNA. It binds Cas9.
  • (3) The guide RNA base-pairs with its target. Cas9 cuts there: a double-strand break.
  • To target a different gene, change only the guide RNA's sequence.
  • Say: "the guide RNA chooses the site; Cas9 cuts" — not: "Cas9 recognises the gene by its active site".
Mission 5

Repair makes the edit

  • The cell's own DNA repair reseals the break.
  • No template: repair often adds or loses a few bases. A frameshift usually makes the gene inoperative: a knockout.
  • Template supplied: DNA with the desired sequence is added. Repair copies it in: a precise change.
  • Say: "Cas9 cuts; the cell's repair makes the edit" — not: "Cas9 writes the new sequence".
Quick check
?In CRISPR–Cas9 gene editing, what determines the position in the genome at which the DNA is cut?
Mission 5

A success: sickle cell disease

  • Before birth, red blood cells make fetal haemoglobin. After birth a gene called BCL11A switches it off.
  • Fetal haemoglobin does not sickle.
  • (1) The patient's own blood-forming stem cells are collected.
  • (2) CRISPR–Cas9 cuts the switch region that turns BCL11A on in red-cell precursors. Fetal haemoglobin is made again.
  • (3) Chemotherapy clears the old stem cells. The edited cells are returned.
  • In the trial, 29 of 30 patients had no severe pain crises for at least a year.
  • Approved in the UK (November 2023) and USA (December 2023).
  • These are somatic cells: the edit is not inherited.
Quick check

Mission 5

Ethics and regulation

  • Somatic editing changes only the patient. Germ-line (embryo) editing passes changes to descendants.
  • 2018: babies were born from CRISPR-edited embryos. Scientists worldwide condemned it. The scientist was imprisoned under his country's law.
  • Issues raised: consent of future generations; cuts at the wrong site; fairness of access; treatment versus enhancement.
  • Argument made for: preventing serious inherited disease where no other option exists.
  • Regulation is national: a use banned in one country may be legal in another.
  • So there is an international effort to harmonise the regulation of genome editing.
Quick check

The work was condemned by scientists worldwide and the scientist was later imprisoned under his own country's laws. Why is there an international effort to harmonise the regulation of genome editing?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Mutations arise spontaneously from copying and repair errors, at random: the environment selects among them but does not direct them.
  • Mutagens such as UV, ionising radiation and some chemicals raise the rate but never aim it.
  • Mutation is the only source of new alleles; selection then changes their frequency.
  • Conserved sequences: selection removes changes, or fewer changes arise.

Mission 2

  • Alternative splicing joins different exons, so one gene can give several polypeptides.
  • The longer an mRNA lasts before nucleases break it down, the more protein it makes.

Mission 3

  • Cell division is controlled by genes whose proteins push the cycle on or hold it back; when mutations disable these controls, a cell line divides without restraint and can form a tumour.
  • Checkpoints (G1, G2, spindle) hold the cycle until conditions are met; irreparable damage can trigger apoptosis.
  • A specific cyclin must reach a threshold to pass each checkpoint, then falls.

Mission 4

  • A proto-oncogene mutated into an oncogene drives division; a tumour suppressor gene inactivated removes the brake. Several such changes in one cell line give a tumour.
  • Benign tumours stay put; malignant tumours invade and metastasise to form secondary tumours.

Mission 5

  • Gene knockout makes a gene inoperative; comparing phenotypes reveals its function. Libraries of knockouts exist for model organisms; no change does not prove no function.
  • In CRISPR–Cas9, a guide RNA base-pairs with the target and Cas9 cuts both strands; the cell's repair makes the edit. Uses such as embryo editing raise ethical issues, and regulation differs between countries.
