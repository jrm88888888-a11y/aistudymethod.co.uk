Mission 1

Why food, water and energy matter

  • Food: health, growth and the energy to work and learn. In 2025, 645 million people were chronically undernourished.
  • Water: drinking, sanitation, farming and industry. About 2.1 billion people lack safely managed drinking water.
  • Energy: light, cooking, industry and transport. In 2024, 655 million people had no electricity.
  • Without them, people are less healthy (social well-being) and less productive (economic well-being).
Mission 1

Unequal use

  • Energy per person, 2024: USA 76,800 kWh; India 7,813 kWh.
  • Water footprint per consumer (1996–2005): USA 2,842 m³ a year; India 1,089 m³.
  • If everyone lived like the average person in Qatar, a year's nature would be used up by 4 February 2026; for the UK, by 22 May.
  • High-income countries consume far more per person; many low-income countries lack enough.
Quick check
?The average US consumer's water footprint is 2,842 m³ a year; India's is 1,089 m³. How many times as large is the US figure?
Mission 1

When demand outstrips supply

  • Demand rises: world population 3.06 billion (1961) → 8.06 billion (2023).
  • Wealth: richer diets need more land and water; China's meat supply reached 73.5 kg per person in 2023.
  • Supply is limited: arable land per person halved, 0.359 → 0.173 ha (1961–2023).
  • Climate change has "reduced food and water security" (IPCC, high confidence).
  • Conflict and disruption can cut supply suddenly, as at the Strait of Hormuz in 2026.
Mission 1

One system: water, food and energy

  • Farming uses water: 72% of freshwater withdrawals.
  • Food uses energy: the food chain uses about 30% of the world's energy.
  • Water uses energy: to "extract, pump, lift, collect, transport and treat water".
  • So a shock to one resource can spread to the others.
Mission 1

The 2026 Hormuz shock

  • In 2025, about 20 million barrels of oil a day passed through the Strait of Hormuz.
  • In March–May 2026, flows averaged about 2.7 million a day.
  • The UN: this hit "key inputs for food production – including fertilizers, sulphur, natural gas and fuels", raising costs across food systems.
  • "Import-dependent countries are especially vulnerable".
Quick check
?Which country was most exposed to the 2026 Hormuz disruption?
Mission check

?A long drought lowers rivers, so hydroelectric stations make less electricity and its price rises. Many farmers there use electric pumps to irrigate. What is the most likely effect on food?

Food, water and energy are linked. In 2026, an energy shock at Hormuz made fertiliser, gas and fuel dearer, so farmers and import-dependent countries far away felt it.

Mission 2

UK resources: an overview

Three resources every country needs: food, water, energy.

  • Food: the UK produces about 60% of its food and imports about 40%.
  • Energy: in 2024 the UK produced about half the gas it used; the rest was imported (our calculation).
  • Water: without action, England could be up to 5,000 million litres a day short by 2055.
  • All three depend on trade, technology and how much we use.
Mission 2

How much food does the UK grow?

  • 2023: UK production = 62% of all food supplied, and 75% of foods that can be grown in the UK.
  • The UK cannot grow bananas, citrus fruit "and other products that rely on a tropical climate".
  • It grows only about 16% of its fresh fruit and 53% of its fresh vegetables (2023).
  • Defra links the low fruit and vegetable figures to "UK climate suitability, seasonality and consumer and producer choices".
Mission 2

Demand all year round

  • "Consumers in the UK demand access to fresh produce all year round, including tropical and out-of-season produce".
  • The UK's largest fresh-fruit suppliers in 2023: Spain 16%, South Africa 13%, Costa Rica 10%, Colombia 8.9%, Brazil 5.5%.
  • 58% of fresh fruit came from outside the EU.
  • In winter, southern-hemisphere countries (e.g. South Africa) are in their summer growing season.
Mission 2

High-value food from poorer countries

UK imports in 2024 (US dollars, UN Comtrade):

  • Kenya (lower-middle income): tea $146 million; beans and peas (incl. green beans) $51 million.
  • Rwanda (low income): tea $21 million.
  • Ethiopia (low income): coffee $17 million.
  • These are high-value crops: worth much more per tonne than staple grains. They earn export income and jobs, but may use land and water that could otherwise grow food for local people.
Quick check
?Why does the UK import fresh fruit from South Africa in January?
Mission 2

Organic food

  • Organic farming uses no synthetic pesticides or fertilisers and follows animal-welfare rules.
  • In 2025, 540,000 hectares of UK land were farmed organically, up 7.3% on 2024.
  • 64% of organic land is permanent pasture; there were 5,004 organic producers and processors.
  • Organic produce often costs more, so demand is linked to income and consumer choice.
Mission 2

Food miles and carbon footprints

  • Food miles: the distance food travels from farm to plate.
  • Worldwide, transporting food produces about 3.0 billion tonnes of CO₂-equivalent, about 19% of food-system emissions.
  • Fruit and vegetables cause 36% of food-miles emissions: transporting them releases almost twice the greenhouse gases of growing them.
  • More imports and more out-of-season produce = more food miles = a larger carbon footprint.
Mission 2

Local or seasonal?

  • Local sourcing: buying food grown nearby (farm shops, farmers' markets, "British" labels).
  • Between 2018 and 2021 the share of food imported from the EU fell, from 28.4% to 22.5%; it partly recovered to 24.2% in 2023. "The fall … has largely been replaced by an increase in domestically produced food".
  • But Defra's evidence review found "'Seasonal' is … a more important criteria than 'local' … except for air freighting food, as this adds considerably to its carbon footprint".
  • Growing out of season (e.g. in heated greenhouses) "can substantially increase" the footprint; importing food that is in season elsewhere "is often preferable".
  • Researchers also argue affluent countries should eat "more locally produced items", alongside more plant-based food.
Quick check
?Which choice is likely to have the smallest carbon footprint for a UK shopper in winter?
Mission 2

The trend towards agribusiness

  • Agribusiness: farming run as a large business: big, specialised, machine- and chemical-intensive, often linked to food processors and supermarkets.
  • England, farms with dairy cows: 15,115 in 2010 → 8,409 in 2025; average herd 77 → 124 cows.
  • Farms of 200 hectares or more farmed 48% of England's farmland in 2010 and 55% in 2025 (our calculation).
  • For: cheaper food, efficiency. Against: fewer family farms and rural jobs; large monocultures can harm wildlife and soils.
Quick check
?England had 15,115 farms with dairy cows in 2010 and 8,409 in 2025. What was the percentage change?
Mission check

?Country Z is a cool island with good farmland. It grows most of its own wheat, milk and potatoes, yet it still imports about a third of its food. Which pair of reasons best fits the UK pattern?

About two-fifths of UK food is imported because shoppers want tropical and out-of-season food all year, which the UK climate cannot supply. For the carbon footprint, the season food is grown in, and whether it is flown, matter more than food miles alone.

Mission 3

How much water do we use?

  • Each person uses about 137 litres a day at home, compared with 120–123 litres in Germany.
  • England's population is forecast to pass 71 million by 2055, over 8 million more than now.
  • More homes, more food and energy production, and data centres all need water.
  • Without action, England's public water supply could be up to 5,000 million litres a day (Ml/d) short by 2055.
Mission 3

Rain in one place, people in another

  • Rainfall: over 4,000 mm a year in the western Scottish mountains; around 500 mm in the driest parts of eastern England. The higher Lake District averages over 3,200 mm.
  • People: the greatest population growth is forecast in London and the south and east of England.
  • So much of the rain falls on the hills of the north and west, while demand is growing fastest in the south and east.
Mission 3

Deficit and surplus

  • Water deficit: demand is greater than the water that can be supplied sustainably. Surplus: supply is greater than demand.
  • At low river flows, "nearly all the South East and East catchments" cannot support any new water taken from rivers.
  • By 2055, deficits are forecast in the South East, East and Midlands.
  • Extra water needed 2030–2055: 2,034 Ml/d for the South East region, but 345 Ml/d for the North.
Quick check
?Why is south-east England more at risk of water shortage than north-west England?
Mission 3

Seasonal imbalance

  • Demand rises in hot spells: watering gardens, filling paddling pools.
  • But summer is when rivers run lowest. Groundwater is refilled mainly in winter (the recharge season), and "hotter, drier periods in summer months may well shorten the recharge season".
  • England had droughts in 2022, 2025 and 2026.
  • So the gap between supply and demand is widest in summer.
Mission 3

Ageing pipes and sewers

  • Water companies in England and Wales own 352,119 km of water mains.
  • About 2,967 million litres leak from them every day (2022–2025 average).
  • London's Victorian sewers were "built for four million people", but now serve "over nine million".
  • Old pipes leak and old sewers overflow, so they waste water and pollute rivers.
Quick check
?About 2,967 million litres of water leak each day. England's forecast shortfall by 2055 is up to 5,000 million litres a day. Leakage is about what percentage of that shortfall?
Mission 3

Water quality and pollution

  • Sewage: in 2024 storm overflows in England spilled an average of 31.8 times each.
  • Farming: fertiliser, slurry and pesticides can wash into rivers.
  • Without new action, the share of rivers in good ecological status could fall by 6% by 2027.
Mission 3

Managing water quality

  • Engineering: London's 25 km Thames Tideway "super sewer" now stops 95% of the spills it was built to catch.
  • Advice: Catchment Sensitive Farming gives farmers local, confidential advice on "nutrient, slurry, and manure management" and "pesticide handling".
  • Enforcement: since 2015 the Environment Agency has won 65 prosecutions against water companies, with fines over £151 million.
  • Clean water is then treated before it reaches taps.
Mission 3

Moving water: transfers

  • A water transfer moves water from an area of surplus to an area of deficit, by pipe, tunnel or river.
  • Kielder Water, Northumberland (1982): a 200 billion litre reservoir. Water is released into rivers such as the Tyne, Derwent and Tees to supply north-east England.
  • Plans to 2050 include 9 new transfer schemes, 10 new reservoirs and 2 desalination schemes.
  • But cutting use and leakage is expected to solve over 65% of England's deficit by 2049–50.
Quick check
?Why are water transfers needed in the UK at all?
Mission check

?Two imaginary regions both have leaky old mains. Region R gets 1,800 mm of rain a year and its population is steady. Region S gets 650 mm and is planning thousands of new homes. Which judgement is best?

Rain falls mostly in the north and west, but people and growth are concentrated in the south-east. Summer peaks, droughts and leaking pipes widen the gap, so the south-east runs short first.

Mission 4

What is an energy mix?

  • Energy mix: the share of a country's energy that comes from each source.
  • Primary energy: all the energy used, for transport, heating, industry and electricity.
  • Electricity is only part of it. Cars and gas boilers use fuel directly.
  • Fossil fuels: coal, oil, gas. Renewables: wind, solar, hydro, biofuels. Nuclear is low-carbon but not renewable.
Mission 4

The UK's mix today

UK primary energy, 2024:

  • Oil 38.8% · gas 32.3% · wind 10.7% · nuclear 5.2% · coal 2.5% · solar 1.9% · biofuels 1.4% · hydro 0.7%
  • Fossil fuels together: 73.5%. All renewables: 21.3%.
  • So the UK still relies on fossil fuels, mainly oil for transport and gas for heating and power.
Mission 4

  • Gas replaced most of coal's share by 2000. Renewables grew mostly after 2010 (3.0% in 2010).
Mission 4

Electricity: the fastest change

UK electricity generation:

  • Coal: 64.6% in 1990 → 0.7% in 2024. (Government figures give a higher 1990 share, about 72%: datasets count generation differently.)
  • Renewables: 1.8% in 1990 → 50.6% in 2024. Wind alone: 29.3%.
  • Gas: 30.4% in 2024; nuclear 14.3%.
  • Britain's last coal power station, Ratcliffe-on-Soar, closed in autumn 2024 (generation ended in September; the government reported coal power ending "in October").
Quick check

Mission 4

Less from our own ground

  • UK oil output peaked in 1999, gas in 2000 (North Sea).
  • By 2024, oil output was 78% below its peak and gas 73% below (our calculation).
  • 2024: the UK produced 307 TWh of gas but used 619 TWh; produced 354 TWh of oil but used 744 TWh.
  • Coal: produced 0.8 TWh, used 47 TWh. The government has said it will ban new coal mining licences.
  • The gap is filled by imports.
Quick check
?In 2024 the UK produced 354 TWh of oil and used 744 TWh. UK production was about what percentage of UK use?
Mission 4

Issues: fossil fuels

  • Economic: the oil and gas industry argues the UK will "need oil and gas for decades" and should produce it at home, "supporting British jobs".
  • Policy (2026): the government plans to end licences for new oil and gas fields but support production from existing ones.
  • Environmental: burning fossil fuels releases CO₂. UK CO₂ emissions fell from 602 Mt (1990) to 318 Mt (2024), helped by the switch away from coal.
  • Fracking (shale gas): an ML 2.9 earthquake at Preston New Road, Lancashire, in 2019; the government then stopped fracking in England.
Mission 4

Issues: renewables and nuclear

  • Wind: about 79% of the public support renewables, but only 36% would be happy with a wind farm in their local area.
  • Offshore wind farms can kill or displace birds, but their bases can also act as artificial reefs for fish.
  • Cost: the Swansea Bay tidal lagoon (£1.3 billion) was rejected; the same power from offshore wind would cost about £400 million.
  • Nuclear: Sizewell C (about 3,340 MW) was approved; the final investment decision came in July 2025.
Quick check
?Which of these is an economic issue of exploiting an energy source, rather than an environmental one?
Mission check

?A newspaper says: "Britain now runs mainly on renewable energy." In 2024, renewables made 50.6% of UK electricity, but only 21.3% of all primary energy; oil was 38.8% and gas 32.3%. Which judgement is best?

Coal's place was taken first by North Sea gas, then by wind and other renewables in electricity. But oil and gas still supply about 71% of all UK energy, and more of it is now imported.

Mission 5

Food security

  • FAO: "Food security exists when all people, at all times, have physical and economic access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food to meet their dietary needs and food preferences for an active and healthy life".
  • Food insecurity: people cannot get enough safe, nutritious food, all the time.
  • Note "all people" and "access": a country can have plenty of food while some of its people go hungry.
Mission 5

Calories per person: the world pattern

  • FAO measures the food available per person per day in kilocalories (kcal), its dietary energy supply.
  • World: 2,825 kcal in 2010; 3,016 kcal in 2023.
  • 2023: Europe 3,490 · Americas 3,438 · Asia 2,991 · Africa 2,556 kcal per person per day.
  • This is food supply, not what people actually eat (calorie intake): food later wasted in shops and homes still counts.
Mission 5

Surplus and deficit

  • Some countries have far more food per person than others. 2023, kcal per person per day: USA 3,947 · Brazil 3,423 · India 2,669 · Ethiopia 2,558.
  • A food surplus: a country produces more than it needs and can export the rest.
  • A food deficit: a country produces less than it needs, so it must import food or its people go short.
  • Richer countries can afford to import food, so a deficit in farming need not mean hunger.
Quick check
?In 2023, Europe's food supply was 3,490 kcal per person per day and Africa's was 2,556. By what percentage was Europe's supply larger than Africa's?
Mission 5

Why the world eats more

  • Rising population: 7.02 billion people in 2010; 8.09 billion in 2023.
  • Economic development: in Asia, supply per person rose from 2,694 to 2,991 kcal (2010–2023), "reflecting rising incomes and changing consumption patterns". People with more money eat more, and more meat and processed food.
  • Together: about 15% more people, each supplied with about 7% more food: about 23% more food in total.
Mission 5

Who goes hungry?

  • 2025: 7.8% of the world's population, around 645 million people, were undernourished: their calorie intake was too low.
  • Africa: about 309 million hungry people, 20.0% of its population; Asia: 292 million.
  • A wider measure, moderate or severe food insecurity (2025): Africa 56.6% · Latin America and the Caribbean 22.9% · Asia 20.3% · Northern America and Europe 8.7%.
Mission 5

Physical factors affecting food supply

  • Climate: drought and floods ruin harvests. Drought in 2010–11, then spikes in staple food prices, were causes of Somalia's famine.
  • Pests and disease: from early 2020, a massive desert locust upsurge broke out across eastern Africa, south-west Asia and around the Red Sea. One square kilometre of swarm can eat as much food in a day as 35,000 people.
  • Water stress: north-west India lost groundwater equal to a 4 cm layer of water every year (2002–2008). Rainfall was close to normal; pumping for irrigation and other uses was the likely cause.
Mission 5

Human factors affecting food supply

  • Poverty: people cannot buy food or farm inputs. A healthy diet is unaffordable for one in three people worldwide (SOFI 2026).
  • Conflict: fighting stops farming and blocks aid. Somalia's 2011 famine "predominantly affected Al-Shabaab areas".
  • Technology: seeds, fertiliser and irrigation raise yields. Asia's cereal production almost doubled from 1970 to 1995, "from 313 to 650 million tons per year". Farmers without them get far less from their land.
  • Prices: food prices rise when energy and fertiliser cost more.
Quick check
?Somalia's 2011 famine killed about 258,000 people. Which set of causes best explains it?
Mission 5

Impacts of food insecurity

  • Undernutrition: 150 million children under five are stunted, short for their age after years of too little food.
  • Famine: a widespread lack of food causing deaths. The IPC declares famine only past strict thresholds, including more than two deaths per 10,000 people per day.
  • Rising prices: a study of 1990–2011 found that rises in food prices led to more social unrest (protests and riots).
  • Soil erosion: hungry farmers overuse land (overgrazing, no fallow years), leaving soil bare to wind and rain, so future harvests fall.
Quick check
?Why can food insecurity lead to soil erosion?
Mission check

?Country X has an average food supply of 3,200 kcal per person per day, well above the world average. Yet a survey finds many children in its poorest rural district are stunted. What best explains this?

The 2023 supply of 3,016 kcal per person is an average of food available. Hunger depends on access: whether people can afford or reach food. Africa's supply is 2,556 kcal per person, and 20.0% of Africans are undernourished.

Mission 6

Two ways to grow more food

  • Farm more land: clear forest or grassland, which costs habitats and carbon.
  • Get more from each field (intensification): water, better seeds, fertiliser, new technology.
  • In Asia's Green Revolution, the area planted with cereals grew by just 0.42% a year (1967–82); the extra food came from higher yields.
Mission 6

Irrigation

  • Irrigation: supplying water to crops by canals, wells, pumps, sprinklers or drip pipes.
  • It lets farmers grow crops in dry seasons and dry places, and grow more than one crop a year.
  • Risks: irrigation can cause salt build-up and falling groundwater where more is pumped than rain replaces.
  • North-west India lost groundwater equal to a 4 cm layer of water each year (2002–2008), likely from pumping for irrigation and other uses.
Mission 6

The Green Revolution

  • From the 1960s, high-yielding varieties (HYVs) of rice and wheat were grown with fertiliser, pesticides and irrigation.
  • About 40% of Asia's cereal area grew these varieties by 1980, and about 80% by 2000.
  • Asia's cereal production almost doubled from 1970 to 1995, "from 313 to 650 million tons per year". Population grew 60%, but calories available per person rose by nearly 30%.
Quick check
?A farmer is given high-yielding rice seeds but has no irrigation and cannot afford fertiliser. What is the most likely result?
Mission 6

Green Revolution: who gained, and at what cost?

  • In Punjab, India, where land was shared fairly equally, the gains cut poverty sharply. In neighbouring Bihar, "where the land system was more feudal, poverty reduction was more limited".
  • Overuse of fertilisers and pesticides polluted waterways and killed beneficial insects and wildlife.
  • The lesson: technology raises supply, but who owns land and who can buy inputs decides who benefits.
Mission 6

Biotechnology: GM crops

  • Genetically modified (GM) crops have genes changed in a laboratory, for example to resist insects or add vitamins.
  • A 2016 US National Academies review found "no substantiated evidence of a difference in risks to human health" from GM crops then on sale, and, in US data for soybean, cotton and maize, "no evidence that GE crops had changed the rate of increase in yields".
  • Golden Rice adds beta-carotene to fight vitamin A deficiency, which affects more than 15% of Filipino children under six. The Philippines approved it in July 2021; a court revoked the permit in April 2024.
  • The new green revolution: raising yields more sustainably, using biotechnology (GM) alongside better soil and water management.
Mission 6

Hydroponics, aeroponics and vertical farms

  • Hydroponics: plants grow with roots in water containing nutrients, without soil.
  • Aeroponics: roots hang in air and are sprayed with a nutrient mist.
  • Vertical farms stack these indoors under lights. Singapore imports over 90% of its food and has only about 1% of its land for growing food.
  • But lettuce from an indoor plant factory needs 247 kWh of purchased energy per kg (dry weight), against 70 kWh in a Dutch greenhouse.
Quick check
?Using the figures earlier in this mission, how many times as much purchased energy does the plant factory use per kg of lettuce as the greenhouse?
Mission 6

Appropriate technology

  • Appropriate technology suits local people's money, skills and materials, and can be repaired locally.
  • Examples: foot-powered treadle pumps, low-cost drip irrigation kits, rainwater-harvesting tanks, and simple storage to stop crops rotting.
  • It is cheap and helps small farmers directly, but each project is small.
Quick check
?Which is the best example of appropriate technology for a small farmer in a low-income country?
Mission 6

More food is not the whole answer

  • Brazil's food supply was already above 3,300 kcal per person in 2010, and stayed there.
  • Yet undernourishment rose from below 2.5% to 3.4% in 2021, during an economic crisis and COVID-19.
  • Strategies that raise supply (irrigation, HYVs, GM, hydroponics) need strategies that give people access: incomes, cash transfers, school meals.
Mission check

?A country doubles its wheat harvest with new seeds and irrigation. Then a recession puts millions out of work and hunger rises. What would most directly cut hunger now, and keep it down?

Brazil had plenty of food; hunger tracked access. Cash for poor families and school meals bought from family farms cut undernourishment below 2.5%; recession and COVID-19 pushed it back up; sustained policy brought it down again.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Pakistan relies on the world's largest connected irrigation system. Why is salt now spoiling its fields?

Make a guess: Pakistan relies on the world's largest connected irrigation system. Why is salt now spoiling its fields?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA GCSE Geography (8035)

  • 3.2.3.2 an example of a large-scale agricultural development to show how it has both advantages and disadvantages
Mission 7

Farming on a giant scale

  • A large-scale agricultural development is a big, usually government-backed scheme to grow more food: dams, canals, irrigation or huge farms.
  • Example: Pakistan's Indus Basin Irrigation System (IBIS), "the largest contiguous surface water irrigation system in the world".
  • It "irrigates millions of hectares" in a region where rain alone is too little for many crops.
Mission 7

How it works

  • Dams store river water: more than 23 km³ in all.
  • Barrages (low dams across the river) divert water into canals: there are 23, including the Sukkur Barrage, opened in 1932.
  • About 129 km³ of Indus water a year is taken for irrigation and other uses.
Mission 7

Advantages

  • Food and water security: groundwater fed by the system is "central to the country's water and food security".
  • Extra groundwater: canal leakage refills the aquifer. Groundwater now gives over 50% of farm water and 90% of rural household water.
  • Reliable supply: groundwater is "a lifeline for farmers" when canal water varies.
Quick check
?Why is the canal system an advantage even for farmers whose canal water often runs short?
Mission 7

Disadvantage 1: waterlogging and salt

  • Water seeping from canals raised the water table: by the 1950s and 1960s fields were becoming waterlogged.
  • 35% of the canal-irrigated area is still waterlogged, mostly in Sindh province.
  • In a hot climate, water evaporates from wet soil and leaves its dissolved salt behind: salinisation.
  • Irrigation adds about 16 million tons of salt to the basin every year, threatening farming on up to 43% of the irrigated area.
Mission 7

Disadvantage 2: too much pumping

  • To lower the water table, the government encouraged pumping of groundwater.
  • Now, in parts of Punjab, unregulated pumping has depleted groundwater and wells have dried up.
  • Over-pumping affects about 18% of the irrigated area.
Mission 7

Disadvantage 3: the river and its delta

  • In 1931–54, about 107 km³ of water and 193 million tonnes of sediment reached the lower Indus each year.
  • In 1993–2003: only about 10 km³ of water and 13 million tonnes of sediment.
  • Without sediment, the sea erodes the delta: since 1944 it has lost 12.7 km² of land a year.
Quick check
?Water reaching the lower Indus fell from about 107 km³ a year to about 10 km³. By roughly what percentage did it fall?
Mission 7

Disadvantage 4: water for low-value crops

  • Four major crops (rice, wheat, sugarcane, cotton) use 80% of the water but contribute only 5% of Pakistan's GDP.
  • The World Bank says "Irrigation systems need modernizing".
Mission 7

Weighing it up

  • For: water for millions of hectares; food and water security; groundwater that supplies most rural homes.
  • Against: waterlogging (35%), salt (up to 43% threatened), over-pumping (18%), a shrinking delta, and water spent on low-value crops.
  • Ways forward: manage canal water and groundwater together ("conjunctively"); modernise irrigation; use less water and drain fields so the water table falls and salt is washed out.
  • Exam move: to assess, give advantages and disadvantages with evidence, then a judgement on which outweighs.
Mission check

?A desert country irrigates new farmland by flooding fields from a canal. After 20 years, white crusts appear on the soil and yields fall. What is the most likely cause and the best response?

The Indus Basin system waters millions of hectares and underpins Pakistan's food security, but waterlogging, salinisation, over-pumping and a shrinking delta show that large-scale schemes bring big advantages and disadvantages together.

Mission 8

Mission 8: In 1993 one Brazilian city declared that food is a right. What did it do to make that real?

Make a guess: In 1993 one Brazilian city declared that food is a right. What did it do to make that real?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA GCSE Geography (8035)

  • 3.2.3.2 sustainable food: organic, permaculture, urban farming, sustainable fish and meat, seasonal food, reduced waste and losses
  • 3.2.3.2 example of a local scheme in an LIC or NEE increasing sustainable food supplies
Mission 8

What makes food supply sustainable?

  • Sustainable food supply meets people's needs now without damaging the land, water and wildlife future food depends on.
  • It must work for the environment, the economy (affordable, farmers earn a living) and people (everyone can get enough).
Mission 8

Growing food sustainably

  • Organic farming: no synthetic fertilisers or pesticides. Yields average about 19% lower than conventional farming.
  • Permaculture: designing farms and gardens to work like natural ecosystems: mixed and perennial plants, with waste recycled as compost.
  • Urban farming: growing food in cities in gardens, rooftops and allotments, close to the people who eat it.
Mission 8

Eating and wasting sustainably

  • Sustainable fish: only 62.3% of marine fish stocks were fished within biologically sustainable levels in 2021.
  • Sustainable meat: meat from cattle and sheep has impacts about 100 times those of plant-based foods.
  • Seasonal food: Defra finds "seasonal" matters more than "local" for environmental impact, except for food flown in.
  • Less waste: 1.05 billion tonnes of food were wasted in 2022, 60% of it in homes.
Quick check
?Which choice most clearly makes a family's food more sustainable?
Mission 8

Belo Horizonte, Brazil

  • A city of about 2.5 million people in south-east Brazil.
  • Brazil is an NEE: one of the IMF's 20 emerging market economies, upper-middle income.
  • In 1993, a city law made access to food a right and set up a food secretariat.
  • Its food system has about 20 programmes and uses about 2% of the city's yearly budget.
Mission 8

Three pillars

  • 1. Food for those in need: popular restaurants (low-cost meals), school meals and a food bank.
  • 2. Fair markets: ABasteCer low-price produce markets and "straight from the farm" and organic fairs.
  • 3. Growing food: community gardens, productive backyards and school gardens using agroecology; the city also buys from family farms.
Quick check
?Which Belo Horizonte programme increases the supply of food grown locally?
Mission 8

Why is it sustainable?

  • Environmental: local, organic and agroecological food; direct trading cuts transport.
  • Economic: farmers sell straight to buyers, so producers earn more and consumers pay less.
  • Social: food is treated as a right, not charity; nutrition education runs alongside.
Mission 8

How successful has it been?

  • The World Future Council, which promotes the scheme, says its programmes "have eliminated hunger and malnutrition from the city". Elsewhere the same page says "near elimination". That is the promoter's claim, not an independent study.
  • Researchers call it "a world pioneer" in urban food policy, and describe both strengths and challenges.
  • An earlier study warned it was "premature" to call it a model.
  • Its ideas were copied nationally, in Brazil's Zero Hunger strategy.
Mission check

?A city in an emerging country wants more people to eat well while supporting nearby small farmers. Which plan follows Belo Horizonte's approach most closely?

Belo Horizonte made food a right in 1993, then backed it with low-cost restaurants, direct-from-farm markets and urban gardens, for about 2% of the city budget. Its promoters claim it ended hunger in the city; researchers praise it but warn against treating it as a ready-made model.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Food, water and energy underpin health and the economy; use is very unequal; demand can outstrip supply as population and wealth grow.
  • A shock to one resource spreads through the others.

Mission 2

  • The UK produces about 60% of its food (75% of what it can grow). Year-round demand for fruit, vegetables and tropical crops brings imports, including high-value crops from Kenya, Rwanda and Ethiopia.
  • Organic land rose 7.3% in 2025. Food miles add carbon, but seasonal beats local, except when food is flown.
  • Agribusiness: fewer, bigger farms; England's dairy farms fell 44% in 2010–2025 while average herds grew.

Mission 3

  • Demand: about 137 litres per person per day; population rising to over 71 million in England by 2055; up to 5,000 Ml/d short without action.
  • Supply–demand mismatch: over 4,000 mm of rain in the west, about 500 mm in the east; most growth in the south-east. Summer demand peaks when rivers are low. Leakage: about 2,967 Ml/d.
  • Deficit areas: South East, East, Midlands. Quality: sewage overflows and farm pollution, managed by super sewers, farm advice and enforcement. Transfers: Kielder; plans for 9 more.

Mission 4

  • UK primary energy 2024: oil 38.8%, gas 32.3%, renewables 21.3%, coal 2.5%. Coal fell from 43.8% (1970); gas replaced it, then renewables grew. Half of electricity is now renewable.
  • North Sea output peaked in 1999–2000; the UK now imports about half its gas. Every source has economic and environmental issues: emissions, earthquakes, cost, wildlife, local opposition.

Mission 5

  • Food security: all people, at all times, can get enough safe, nutritious food. Supply per person is highest in Europe and the Americas and lowest in Africa.
  • Physical factors (climate, pests, water stress) and human factors (poverty, conflict, technology, prices) affect supply and access.
  • Food consumption rises with population and incomes. Food insecurity brings undernutrition, famine, rising prices, social unrest and soil erosion.

Mission 6

  • More food: irrigation, the Green Revolution's HYVs, GM crops, hydroponics and aeroponics. Each raises supply, with costs (groundwater, pollution, energy, inequality). Appropriate technology suits local people's money, skills and materials.
  • The new green revolution aims to raise yields more sustainably, with biotechnology.
  • Ending hunger needs access as well as supply, kept up through crises.

Mission 7

  • Large-scale agricultural development example: the Indus Basin Irrigation System, Pakistan, the largest contiguous surface-water irrigation system in the world.
  • Advantages: irrigation of millions of hectares, food and water security, groundwater for farms and homes. Disadvantages: waterlogging (35%), salinisation (16 million tons of salt a year), over-pumping (18%), delta loss (12.7 km² a year), water used for low-value crops.

Mission 8

  • Sustainable food includes organic farming, permaculture, urban farming, sustainable fish and meat, seasonal food and less waste.
  • Belo Horizonte, Brazil (1993): food as a right; popular restaurants, a food bank, direct-from-farm and organic markets, and community gardens.
