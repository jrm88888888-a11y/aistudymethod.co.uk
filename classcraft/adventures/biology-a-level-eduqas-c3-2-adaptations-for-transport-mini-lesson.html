Mission 1

Mission 1: Blood leaves the heart in surges but creeps past your cells in a steady trickle. How?

Make a guess: Blood leaves the heart in surges but creeps past your cells in a steady trickle. How?

Mission 1

Why diffusion is not enough

  • Diffusion is fast only over short distances.
  • Large animals have a small SA:V and a high metabolic rate. Their surface cannot supply every cell.
  • Mass transport: the whole fluid flows, carrying everything in it. A pump (the heart) drives it.
  • It moves substances quickly over large distances, between exchange surfaces and cells.
  • Blood carries nutrients, hormones, excretory products (e.g. urea) and heat.
Mission 1

Open or closed, single or double

  • Open: blood leaves vessels and bathes the organs directly.
  • Closed: blood stays inside vessels all the way round.
  • Single (bony fish): heart → gills → body → heart. One pass through the heart per circuit.
  • Blood loses much of its pressure in the gill capillaries. It reaches the body at low pressure.
  • Double (mammals): heart → lungs → heart → body → heart. Two passes per circuit.
  • Pulmonary circuit (lungs) and systemic circuit (body). After the lungs, blood is pumped again, so the body gets high-pressure blood.
  • Say: "double = blood passes through the heart twice per circuit" — not: "two capillary beds".
Mission 1

Earthworms and insects

  • Insect: open system. A dorsal tube-shaped heart pumps blood (haemolymph) forward into the body cavity.
  • Haemolymph bathes the organs at low pressure, then re-enters the heart through pores.
  • Insect blood carries almost no oxygen: the tracheal system delivers gases to the cells.
  • Earthworm: closed system, with a vessel in every segment (vascularisation).
  • Pumps: the contracting dorsal vessel and five pairs of muscular pseudohearts (aortic arches).
  • Its blood carries respiratory gases: haemoglobin is dissolved in the plasma.
Quick check
?(Illustrative data.) In a bony fish, mean blood pressure was measured at about 5 kPa in the artery leaving the heart and about 3.5 kPa in the vessel leaving the gills. In a mammal, mean pressure in the aorta was about 13 kPa. What do these data show about the two types of circulation?
Mission 1

The mammalian circuit: named vessels

  • Heart → lungs: pulmonary artery. Lungs → heart: pulmonary vein.
  • Heart → body: aorta. Body → heart: vena cava.
  • Heart muscle: coronary arteries (branch from the base of the aorta).
  • Arteries carry blood away from the heart, whatever its oxygen content. The pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood.
Mission 1

Three layers, five vessels

  • Walls are built from up to three layers:
    • inner: endothelium (squamous: one flat cell thick, smooth);
    • middle: smooth muscle and elastic tissue;
    • outer: fibrous (collagen) tissue.

Mission 1

Arteries and arterioles

  • Each ventricular contraction forces a surge of blood into the arteries.
  • Elastic tissue stretches as the surge arrives, then recoils between beats.
  • Recoil keeps pressure up between beats and smooths the flow.
  • Thick muscle and elastic tissue let the wall withstand high pressure; fibrous tissue stops it overstretching.
  • Smooth endothelium: little friction.
  • Arterioles: smooth muscle contracts (vasoconstriction) or relaxes (vasodilation).
  • This sets how much blood enters each capillary bed.
  • Say: "the artery wall stretches and recoils" — not: "the artery wall contracts to pump blood".

Mission 1

Veins: back to the heart at low pressure

  • By the veins, blood pressure is low and there is no pulse.
  • Wide lumen: little resistance to flow.
  • Thin, flexible wall: squeezed by surrounding skeletal muscles as they contract.
  • Valves: passive flaps that close if blood starts to flow backwards.
  • So every squeeze moves blood one way: towards the heart.
  • Say: "valves prevent backflow" — not: "valves push blood along".
Mission 1

Capillaries: built for exchange

  • Wall: endothelium only, one cell thick → short diffusion distance.
  • Narrow: red cells pass in single file, close to the wall.
  • Branching: huge numbers form capillary beds → huge total surface area.
  • Every cell lies close to a capillary.
  • Flow is slowest here: more time for exchange.
  • Say: "thin walls give a short diffusion distance" — not: "thin walls let blood flow faster".
Quick check
?A muscle receives blood through a network of thousands of capillaries rather than through a single vessel of the same total volume. What is the advantage of this arrangement for exchange?
Mission 1

From surge to trickle

  • Arteries: pressure surges with each beat; elastic recoil smooths the swings.
  • Arterioles: narrow and muscular: the biggest fall in pressure. The surges die away.
  • Capillaries: one aorta branches into billions of capillaries. Their total cross-sectional area is enormous.
  • The same volume of blood per second spread over far more area → flow hundreds of times slower.
  • Veins: pressure lowest; muscles and valves return the blood.
  • So: surges in, steady trickle past the cells.
Mission 1Practical

Arteries and veins in section

  • Specified practical: low-power plan of a prepared slide of T.S. artery and vein; calculate actual size and magnification.
  • Artery: wall thick relative to the lumen; round, narrow lumen; wavy elastic layer.
  • Vein: wall thin relative to the lumen; wide lumen, often flattened or irregular.
  • Capillary: a ring of one or two flat endothelial cells, about one red cell wide.
  • Plan: outlines of tissue layers only, no cells; in proportion.
  • Eyepiece graticule calibrated: 1 unit = 25 µm. Artery diameter = 48 units = 1200 µm = 1.2 mm.
  • Drawn 60 mm wide: magnification = drawing size ÷ actual size = 60 ÷ 1.2 = × 50.

Mission check

?With age, the walls of large arteries lose elastic tissue and become stiffer. Blood pressure in an arm artery is measured at its highest (as the ventricle contracts) and its lowest (between beats). What change in these readings is expected?

Stretch and recoil in elastic arteries turn the heart's surges into steady pressure; lose the elastic tissue and the surges come back.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Your heart has no switches, yet its valves open and close at exactly the right moments. How?

Make a guess: Your heart has no switches, yet its valves open and close at exactly the right moments. How?

Mission 2

Four chambers, two pumps

  • Right side: receives deoxygenated blood from the body; pumps it to the lungs.
  • Left side: receives oxygenated blood from the lungs; pumps it to the body.
  • The septum keeps the two sides apart.
  • Route: vena cava → right atrium → right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lungs → pulmonary vein → left atrium → left ventricle → aorta.
  • Atrioventricular (AV) valves lie between each atrium and ventricle.
  • Semilunar valves lie at the base of the aorta and the pulmonary artery.
  • Coronary arteries supply the heart muscle itself.
  • In diagrams the heart faces you: its right side is on your left.
Mission 2

Built to deliver pressure

  • Cardiac muscle: contracts rhythmically, without tiring, for a lifetime.
  • Atria: thin walls. They pump only into the ventricles below.
  • Ventricles: thick walls. They pump out into the arteries.
  • Left ventricle wall about three times thicker than the right: it must drive blood round the whole body.
  • Both ventricles pump the same volume per beat.
  • Valves stop backflow. Septum stops mixing. Coronary vessels bring O2 to the hard-working muscle.
Quick check
?The wall of the left ventricle is about three times thicker than the wall of the right ventricle. Which statement correctly explains this difference?
Mission 2

Valves move with pressure

  • A valve has no muscle of its own and no nerve. Blood pressure moves it.
  • Higher pressure behind a valve pushes it open.
  • Higher pressure in front pushes it shut.
  • AV valve: open when atrial pressure > ventricular; shut when ventricular > atrial.
  • Semilunar valve: open when ventricular pressure > arterial; shut when arterial > ventricular.
  • Say: "the valve shuts because pressure in the ventricle exceeds that in the atrium" — not: "muscles close the valve".
Mission 2

The cardiac cycle

  • One cycle at 75 beats per minute lasts 60 ÷ 75 = 0.8 s.
  • (1) Atrial systole (about 0.1 s): atria contract; ventricles relaxed; AV valves open. Blood tops up the ventricles.
  • (2) Ventricular systole (about 0.3 s): ventricles contract. Their pressure rises:
    • above atrial pressure → AV valves shut;
    • above arterial pressure → semilunar valves open; blood is forced out.
  • (3) Diastole (about 0.4 s): all chambers relax. Ventricular pressure falls:
    • below arterial pressure → semilunar valves shut;
    • below atrial pressure → AV valves open; blood flows in from the veins.
Quick check
?The sinoatrial node fires. Which sequence of events then follows in the left side of the heart?
Mission 2

Reading the pressure graph

  • Three lines: left atrium, left ventricle, aorta. Find where two lines cross.
  • A: ventricle rises above atrium → AV valve shuts.
  • B: ventricle rises above aorta → semilunar valve opens. Ejection starts.
  • C: ventricle falls below aorta → semilunar valve shuts. A small notch in the aortic trace.
  • D: ventricle falls below atrium → AV valve opens. Filling starts.
  • A → B and C → D: both valves shut; the ventricle's volume stays the same.
  • Aortic pressure never falls to zero: elastic recoil (see Mission 1).
  • Ventricular volume falls from B to C and rises from D onwards.

Mission 2

Stroke volume and cardiac output

  • Stroke volume: volume pumped by one ventricle per beat.
  • Left ventricle: about 120 cm³ when full, about 50 cm³ after ejection → stroke volume ≈ 70 cm³.
  • Cardiac output = stroke volume × heart rate: the volume pumped per minute.
  • Example: 70 cm³ × 72 beats min⁻¹ = 5040 cm³ min⁻¹ ≈ 5.0 dm³ min⁻¹.
  • To find stroke volume: stroke volume = cardiac output ÷ heart rate.

Mission 2Practical

Dissecting a heart

  • Specified practical: dissection of the mammalian heart.
  • Outside: find the ventral side (coronary artery runs diagonally); identify atria, ventricles, aorta, pulmonary artery, venae cavae.
  • Cut open each ventricle. Compare wall thicknesses: measure with a ruler.
  • Find AV valves, chordae tendineae, papillary muscles, semilunar valves (pockets at the base of the aorta), septum.
  • Push a blunt probe into each vessel to see which chamber it joins.
  • Safety: sharp scalpel cutting away from you, on a board; gloves; wash hands; dispose of tissue as instructed.
Mission check

?Pressures in the right side of a heart, early in ventricular systole: right atrium 0.4 kPa; right ventricle 1.0 kPa and rising; pulmonary artery 1.3 kPa. Which statement is correct?

No switches needed: every valve opens when the pressure behind it is higher, and shuts when the pressure in front is higher.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Cut every nerve to a heart and it keeps on beating. So what are those nerves for?

Make a guess: Cut every nerve to a heart and it keeps on beating. So what are those nerves for?

Mission 3

The heart starts its own beat

  • Cardiac muscle is myogenic: it contracts without any nerve impulse.
  • The sinoatrial node (SAN), in the wall of the right atrium, is the pacemaker.
  • It sends out a wave of electrical excitation at regular intervals. Each wave starts one heartbeat.
  • Nerves and hormones only change the rate of the SAN. They do not start the beats.
  • Say: "the SAN initiates each beat" — not: "the brain sends each heartbeat".
Mission 3

Spreading the wave

  • (1) The wave from the SAN spreads across both atria → atrial systole.
  • (2) A layer of non-conducting tissue between atria and ventricles blocks it. The only way through is the atrioventricular node (AVN).
  • (3) The AVN delays the wave (about 0.1 s): the atria finish emptying first.
  • (4) The wave passes down the bundle of His in the septum to the apex, then up through the ventricle walls.
  • Ventricles contract from the apex upwards, squeezing blood up into the arteries.
  • Names: SAN, AVN, bundle of His, Purkyne fibres (also spelled Purkinje).
Quick check
?Suppose the AVN passed the wave of excitation on with no delay. What would be the main effect?
Mission 3

The ECG

  • An electrocardiogram (ECG): electrodes on the skin record the heart's electrical activity.
  • P wave: excitation spreading over the atria → atrial systole.
  • QRS complex: excitation spreading through the ventricles → ventricular systole.
  • T wave: ventricles recovering (repolarising) → diastole.
  • P to the start of QRS includes the AVN delay.
  • Heart rate (beats min⁻¹) = 60 ÷ time between two R peaks (s).
Mission 3

Worked example: heart rate from an ECG

  • ECG paper runs at 25 mm s⁻¹. One large square = 5 mm = 0.20 s.
  • R peak to R peak = 4 large squares = 4 × 0.20 = 0.80 s.
  • Heart rate = 60 ÷ 0.80 = 75 beats min⁻¹.
Quick check
?On an ECG recorded at 25 mm s⁻¹, the distance between two R peaks is 30 mm. What is the heart rate in beats per minute?
Mission check

?A mammalian heart is removed and kept supplied with warm, oxygenated salt solution through its coronary arteries. It goes on beating regularly for hours. Which statement explains this?

The beat starts inside the heart itself. Nerves from the brain only change how fast it goes.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Your blood passes every tissue all day. How does it know to drop more oxygen where muscles work hardest?

Make a guess: Your blood passes every tissue all day. How does it know to drop more oxygen where muscles work hardest?

Mission 4

Haemoglobin

  • Found in red blood cells. It carries almost all the O2 in blood.
  • A protein with quaternary structure: four polypeptide chains.
  • Each chain holds one haem group with an Fe²⁺ ion. Each haem binds one O2.
  • So one haemoglobin carries up to four O2: it becomes oxyhaemoglobin.
  • Binding is reversible: haemoglobin + 4O2 ⇌ oxyhaemoglobin.
  • On slides, erythrocytes are biconcave discs with no nucleus: more room for haemoglobin.
Mission 4

Loading and unloading

  • Partial pressure of oxygen (pO2) measures how much O2 is present, in kPa.
  • Lungs: high pO2 (about 13 kPa) → haemoglobin loads O2.
  • Respiring tissues: low pO2 → haemoglobin unloads O2.
  • Affinity: how readily haemoglobin binds O2.
  • Percentage saturation: how full the haem groups are.
Mission 4

Read the data: the dissociation curve

Human blood at 37 °C, pH 7.4 (Severinghaus, 1979):

pO2 / kPa245.3813
Saturation / %1957759198
  • Plotted, these give an S-shaped curve.
  • Low pO2: the first O2 binds with difficulty. Curve flat.
  • Binding it changes the shape of haemoglobin. The other haem groups bind O2 more easily: cooperative binding. Curve steep.
  • High pO2: almost every haem is full. Curve levels off.
  • Say: "binding of the first O2 changes haemoglobin's shape" — not: "haemoglobin molecules cooperate with each other".
Quick check
?Adult haemoglobin is about 20% saturated with oxygen at a partial pressure of oxygen of 2 kPa, 60% at 4 kPa, 90% at 8 kPa and 98% at 13 kPa. Which explanation accounts for this pattern?
Mission 4

Steep where it matters

Use the dissociation-curve table above. Blood leaves the lungs 98% saturated.

  • Resting tissue, pO2 5.3 kPa → 75%. Unloaded: 98 − 75 = 23 percentage points.
  • Hard-working muscle, pO2 2 kPa → 19%. Unloaded: 98 − 19 = 79 percentage points.
  • In the steep part, a small fall in pO2 releases a lot of O2.
  • So the tissue using O2 fastest, with the lowest pO2, gets the most.
Mission 4

The Bohr effect

  • Respiring tissue makes CO2. CO2 forms carbonic acid → H⁺ → pH falls.
  • More CO2 lowers haemoglobin's affinity for O2.
  • The curve shifts right: at the same pO2, saturation is lower. More O2 is unloaded.
  • In the lungs, CO2 diffuses out: the curve shifts left, so loading is easier.
  • The same blood at pH 7.2, calculated from the Severinghaus curve with the standard Bohr shift:
pO2 / kPa245.3813
Saturation at pH 7.4 / %1957759198
Saturation at pH 7.2 / %1343618495
  • Say: "CO2 lowers the affinity of haemoglobin for O2" — not: "CO2 pushes O2 off the haem".
Quick checkRead the data
?Blood leaves the lungs at pH 7.4, 98% saturated. In a working muscle, pO2 is 4 kPa and the extra CO2 has lowered the pH to 7.2. At 4 kPa, saturation is 57% at pH 7.4 and 43% at pH 7.2. By how many percentage points does the saturation of this blood fall in the muscle?
Mission 4

Left or right?

  • Curve to the left: higher affinity. Loads O2 at a lower pO2.
  • Curve to the right: lower affinity. Unloads O2 more readily.
  • Foetal haemoglobin: higher affinity than adult haemoglobin (different subunits).
  • In the placenta, at the same pO2, the mother's haemoglobin unloads and the foetus's haemoglobin loads.
  • Llama (high altitude, low pO2 in the air) and lugworm (burrows in mud, low-O2 water at low tide): curves to the left.
  • Say: "left = higher affinity" — not: "left means lower".
Quick check
?In the placenta, maternal blood and foetal blood are separated by a thin barrier. How is oxygen transferred from maternal blood to foetal blood?
Mission 4

Carrying CO2

  • A little CO2 dissolves in plasma. Some binds to haemoglobin. Most travels as hydrogencarbonate ions (HCO3⁻).
  • In a red blood cell: CO2 + H2O → H2CO3 (carbonic acid), catalysed by carbonic anhydrase.
  • H2CO3 → H⁺ + HCO3⁻.
  • HCO3⁻ diffuses out into the plasma. Cl⁻ diffuses in to balance the charge: the chloride shift.
  • H⁺ binds to haemoglobin → haemoglobinic acid (HHb). Oxyhaemoglobin releases its O2.
  • This buffers the blood's pH and adds to the Bohr effect.
  • In the lungs, every step reverses and CO2 diffuses out.
Quick check
?Suppose carbonic anhydrase in red blood cells were inhibited. What would happen to the release of oxygen in respiring tissues?
Mission check

?As blood flows through the lung capillaries, CO2 diffuses out of it into the alveoli. How does this help the blood load oxygen?

Haemoglobin responds to its surroundings: high CO2 makes it let go, low CO2 makes it hold on. That is how it delivers most where muscles work hardest.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Your blood vessels leak fluid all day long. So why don't your tissues flood?

Make a guess: Your blood vessels leak fluid all day long. So why don't your tissues flood?

Mission 5

Three fluids

  • Plasma: the liquid part of blood. Water carrying dissolved substances and plasma proteins (e.g. albumin, fibrinogen).
  • Tissue fluid: bathes every cell in a tissue. Like plasma, but with very little protein and no red cells.
  • Lymph: tissue fluid that has drained into lymph vessels. Contains lymphocytes.
  • Cells exchange substances with tissue fluid, not directly with blood.
Mission 5

Out and back: the big picture

  • At the arteriole end of a capillary bed, blood pressure squeezes fluid out through the capillary wall (pressure filtration).
  • That fluid is tissue fluid. It bathes the cells.
  • At the venule end, most of it returns to the blood.
  • The excess drains into lymph vessels as lymph.
  • Lymph returns to the blood in veins near the heart.
  • Out, around the cells, back: the tissues stay supplied but never flood.
Mission 5

The pressures

  • (1) Hydrostatic pressure (blood pressure) pushes fluid out. High at the arteriole end.
  • Water and small solutes (glucose, amino acids, ions, O2) are forced through gaps in the wall: pressure filtration.
  • Cells and plasma proteins are too large. They stay in the blood.
  • (2) Hydrostatic pressure falls along the capillary, as fluid leaves and blood meets resistance.
  • (3) The proteins left behind pull water in by osmosis.
  • Arteriole end: push out > pull in → net outflow. Venule end: pull in > push out → net return.
  • Say: "tissue fluid forms by pressure filtration" — not: "plasma diffuses out".
  • Newer research: in most tissues, little fluid is actually reabsorbed at the venule end; most of it returns through the lymph (Levick and Michel, 2010). Exams still use the model above.

Mission 5

Tissue fluid: the link to every cell

  • Tissue fluid carries O2, glucose, amino acids, fatty acids, ions from blood to cells.
  • Cells take them up by diffusion, facilitated diffusion or active transport.
  • Cells release CO2, urea and other wastes into tissue fluid. These enter the blood at the venule end.
  • Each cell is a short diffusion distance from tissue fluid, and tissue fluid from a capillary.
Quick check

Mission 5

Lymph: draining the excess

  • Not all tissue fluid returns to the capillaries. The excess enters lymph vessels.
  • Lymph capillaries are blind-ended, with thin walls and gaps that let fluid in.
  • Valves stop lymph flowing backwards.
  • Lymph is moved along as nearby skeletal muscles squeeze the vessels. Larger lymph vessels also contract rhythmically.
  • It passes through lymph nodes, where lymphocytes collect.
  • Lymph returns to the blood, entering veins near the heart.
  • Say: "lymph rejoins the blood" — not: "lymph circulates separately".
Quick check
?Which statement describes how excess tissue fluid is drained and what happens to it?
Mission check

?In lymphatic filariasis, parasitic worms live in lymph vessels and can block them. A person with blocked lymph vessels in one leg develops a badly swollen leg. Which explanation is correct?

Fluid leaks out at one end and returns at the other; lymph vessels carry off the rest. Block the drain and the tissues do flood.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Arteries stretch and recoil; arterioles control flow and drop pressure; capillaries' huge total area slows flow for exchange; veins return blood with valves and muscle squeeze.
  • Fish: single circulation (heart once per circuit); mammals: double circulation, so body blood is at high pressure.

Mission 2

  • Valves open and shut only because of pressure differences; atrial systole, then ventricular systole, then diastole.
  • Cardiac output = stroke volume × heart rate.

Mission 3

  • Cardiac muscle is myogenic: the SAN starts each beat; the AVN delays the wave; the bundle of His and Purkyne tissue carry it to the apex.
  • ECG: P (atria), QRS (ventricles), T (recovery); rate = 60 ÷ R–R time.

Mission 4

  • S-shaped curve from cooperative binding; the steep part lets small falls in pO2 release a lot of O2.
  • Bohr effect: more CO2 → lower affinity → curve right → more O2 unloaded in respiring tissue.
  • Foetal haemoglobin has higher affinity than adult haemoglobin: curve to the left.
  • Most CO2 travels as HCO3⁻, made by carbonic anhydrase; Cl⁻ moves in (chloride shift); H⁺ forms haemoglobinic acid.

Mission 5

  • Tissue fluid is squeezed out at the arteriole end; most returns at the venule end; the excess drains as lymph back into the blood.
  • Hydrostatic pressure falls along the capillary; the plasma proteins left behind draw water back in.
