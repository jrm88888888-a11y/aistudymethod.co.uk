Mission 1

Mission 1: When a cold pack goes cold, has the chemical inside lost energy or gained it?

Make a guess: When a cold pack goes cold, has the chemical inside lost energy or gained it?

Mission 1

System and surroundings

  • The system: the reacting chemicals, reactants and products.
  • The surroundings: everything else, water, container, thermometer, air.
  • Energy moves between them; the total is conserved.
  • This is the first law of thermodynamics.
  • A thermometer in the solution reads the surroundings, not the chemicals.
Mission 1

Heat is not temperature

  • Heat: energy transferred because of a temperature difference, in joules.
  • Temperature: a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles (in kelvin, proportional to it).
  • A bath and a cup of water at 40 °C have the same temperature.
  • The bath can give out far more heat as it cools: many more particles.
Mission 1

Heat moves by collisions

  • Particles in a warmer body have a greater average kinetic energy.
  • Where two bodies touch, their particles collide. On average, energy passes from faster to slower particles: heat transfer.
  • Collisions never stop. Net transfer stops at thermal equilibrium: same average kinetic energy, same temperature.
Quick check
?A hot piece of copper is placed in cool water in an insulated cup, and the water becomes warmer. Which statement best describes, at the particulate level, how the water is warmed?
Mission 1

Exothermic and endothermic

  • Exothermic: energy passes from system to surroundings. Surroundings warm. ΔH negative.
  • Endothermic: energy passes from surroundings to system. Surroundings cool. ΔH positive.
  • ΔH describes the system. Always write its sign.
  • At constant pressure, the enthalpy of reaction, ΔH, equals the heat q gained or lost by the system.
  • Energy can also leave as work: a gas made by the reaction pushes back the air.
  • Physical changes count too: melting and boiling are endothermic; freezing and condensing are exothermic.
  • A cold pack: the dissolving chemical gains energy from the water and your hand.
Mission 1

Heat from ΔH°: q = n × ΔH°

  • ΔH° is per mole of reaction as written (kJ/molrxn).
  • Heat transferred = (moles of reaction) × ΔH°.
  • H2(g) + ½O2(g) → H2O(l), ΔH° = −285.8 kJ/molrxn.
  • Burning 3.00 mol H2 is 3.00 mol of reaction: 3.00 × 285.8 = 857 kJ released.
  • Released: ΔH negative. Absorbed: ΔH positive.
Quick check
?Zinc metal reacts with hydrochloric acid in a flask connected to a gas syringe: Zn(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → ZnCl2(aq) + H2(g). As the reaction occurs, the solution becomes warmer and the H2(g) pushes the syringe plunger outward. Taking the reacting species as the system, which statement correctly describes the energy changes?
Mission 1

Breaking takes in, making gives out

  • Breaking bonds takes in energy: endothermic.
  • Making bonds gives out energy: exothermic.
  • Bond making releases more than bond breaking takes in → exothermic overall. Less → endothermic.
  • Every elementary reaction breaks some bonds and forms new ones.
  • The potential energy released makes the product particles move faster. They collide with the water until all reach thermal equilibrium: the temperature rise you measure.
  • The numbers come in Mission 2.
Mission 1

Energy diagrams, including dissolving

  • An energy diagram shows whether any process is endothermic or exothermic.
  • Final state higher than initial: endothermic (ice → water).
  • Dissolving: solute–solute and solvent–solvent attractions are overcome (energy in); solute–solvent attractions form (energy out).
  • New attractions stronger overall → exothermic. Weaker → endothermic: the cold pack.
Mission 1

Reaction profiles and activation energy

  • y-axis: potential energy. x-axis: reaction coordinate. Not time.
  • A hump between reactants and products: the energy barrier.
  • Activation energy, Ea: the minimum energy needed for reaction; arrow from the reactant level up to the top of the hump.
  • ΔH: from the reactant level to the product level.
  • Exothermic and endothermic reactions both have a barrier.
Quick check
?An energy diagram for a hypothetical reaction shows the reactants at 50 kJ/mol, a single maximum at 110 kJ/mol and the products at 20 kJ/mol. What is the energy change for the reaction?
Quick check
?Which sketch is a correctly labeled profile for an endothermic reaction?
Mission check

?Hydrated barium hydroxide and ammonium chloride, both solids, are mixed in a flask standing on a wet wooden block. Within a minute the water under the flask freezes, sticking the flask to the block. Which statement about the reacting chemicals is correct?

A cold pack works the same way. The chemical inside gains energy from the water and your hand, so they cool: endothermic, ΔH positive.

Mission 2

Mission 2: If breaking bonds takes in energy, how can burning a fuel give energy out?

Make a guess: If breaking bonds takes in energy, how can burning a fuel give energy out?

Mission 2

Breaking takes in, forming gives out

  • Breaking a bond is endothermic: energy is absorbed.
  • Forming the same bond releases the same amount: exothermic.
  • Bonds never release energy as they break.
  • Exothermic overall: the bonds formed are stronger in total than those broken.
  • Breaking raises the system's potential energy; forming lowers it. The net drop shows up as a temperature rise.
Mission 2

Potential energy and bond length

  • Two atoms far apart: potential energy zero.
  • As they approach, attraction lowers the potential energy.
  • Too close: nuclei repel, so it rises steeply.
  • The minimum sits at the bond length.
  • The depth of the minimum is the bond energy: energy needed to pull the atoms apart.
Mission 2

Bond enthalpy

  • Bond enthalpy: energy needed to break 1 mol of a bond, with all species gaseous. Always positive.
  • Tables list average bond enthalpies (bond energies), in kJ/mol.
  • Larger value → stronger bond.
Mission 2

Worked example: burning methane

  • ΔH = Σ(bonds broken) − Σ(bonds formed). Draw the structures; count every bond.
  • CH4(g) + 2O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2O(g)
  • Values given (kJ/mol): C–H 414, O=O 498, C=O 804, O–H 463.
  • Broken: 4 C–H + 2 O=O = 1656 + 996 = 2652 kJ.
  • Formed: 2 C=O + 4 O–H = 1608 + 1852 = 3460 kJ.
  • ΔH = 2652 − 3460 = −808 kJ/molrxn. Forming gives out more than breaking takes in.
Quick check
?Hydrogen and fluorine react exothermically: H2(g) + F2(g) → 2 HF(g). The reaction can be thought of as taking place in two stages: the bonds in the reactant molecules break, and then the bonds in the product molecules form. Which statement correctly describes the potential energy of the system during these stages?
Calculate
?Calculate ΔH for N2(g) + 3H2(g) → 2NH3(g).
  • Bond enthalpies (kJ/mol): N≡N 945, H–H 436, N–H 391. Give ΔH in kJ/molrxn.
kJ/molrxn
Mission 2

Why it is only an estimate

  • Values are averages over many compounds. The real bond in your molecule may differ.
  • They apply only to gases. If a substance is liquid or solid, the energy to change state is left out.
  • So a bond-enthalpy ΔH is an estimate.
  • Methane: −808 estimated; −890 kJ/molrxn measured, with liquid water.
Quick check
?A student uses a table of average bond enthalpies and finds that, per mole of reaction between two gases, the bond enthalpies of all the bonds in the reactants add up to 590 kJ and the bond enthalpies of all the bonds in the products add up to 750 kJ. The student will carry out the reaction in an insulated container fitted with a temperature probe. Which prediction, with its justification, is consistent with these bond enthalpy totals?
Mission 2

Working backward to a bond enthalpy

  • H2(g) + Br2(g) → 2HBr(g), ΔH° = −103.5 kJ/molrxn.
  • Given: H–H 436, Br–Br 193 kJ/mol. Find H–Br, x.
  • Broken − formed = ΔH: (436 + 193) − 2x = −103.5
  • 2x = 629 + 103.5 = 732.5
  • x = 366 kJ/mol. Two H–Br bonds form, so divide by 2.
Quick check
?For the reaction H2(g) + Cl2(g) → 2 HCl(g), ΔH° = −184.6 kJ/molrxn. The bond enthalpy of the H–H bond is 436 kJ/mol, and the bond enthalpy of the Cl–Cl bond is 243 kJ/mol. Based on these data, what is the bond enthalpy of the H–Cl bond?
Mission check

?Both these reactions are exothermic, by very different amounts: H2(g) + I2(g) → 2HI(g) and H2(g) + F2(g) → 2HF(g). Which statement explains why the fluorine reaction gives out far more energy?
  • Bond enthalpies (kJ/mol): H–H 436, I–I 151, F–F 158, H–I 299, H–F 568.

A fuel gives energy out for the same reason. Breaking its bonds takes energy in, but forming the strong bonds in CO2 and H2O gives out more.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Where does half the energy go when you burn a fuel under a can of water?

Make a guess: Where does half the energy go when you burn a fuel under a can of water?

Mission 3

Measuring an enthalpy change

  • Solutions: react in a polystyrene cup with a lid, stirrer and thermometer.
  • This is a coffee-cup calorimeter: open to the air, so at constant pressure.
  • Combustion: a spirit burner heats a known mass of water in a copper can.
  • Record the temperature before, then at regular intervals after.
  • Temperature rises: the process gave out energy, exothermic. Falls: it took energy in, endothermic.
Mission 3

q = mcΔT

  • q = mcΔT, with q in joules.
  • m: mass of the water or solution whose temperature changes, in g. Not the reactant's mass.
  • c: specific heat capacity; water 4.18 J/(g·°C).
  • ΔT: the temperature change. Same in K and °C; never add 273.
  • Dilute solutions: take density 1.00 g/mL and c of water.
Mission 3

Same heat, different temperature change

  • Specific heat capacity, c: J per gram per °C. Molar heat capacity: J per mole per °C, used as q = n × C × ΔT.
  • Same q into equal masses: larger c → smaller ΔT. Water warms slowly.
  • Heating raises the system's energy; cooling lowers it.
  • Melting and boiling take energy in; freezing and condensing give it out, all at constant temperature.
Mission 3

From q to ΔH

  • ΔH = −q ÷ n. n: amount of the limiting reactant, in mol (for a 1 : 1 equation).
  • Divide q by 1000 to get kJ.
  • The minus sign: the surroundings gain q, so the system loses it.
  • Temperature rise → ΔH negative. Fall → ΔH positive.
  • Final answer per mole of reaction, e.g. −201 kJ/molrxn.
Quick check
?When 10.0 g of solid X dissolves in 40.0 g of water at 21.0°C in an insulated calorimeter, the dissolution releases 1.67 kJ of energy. Assume that the specific heat capacity of the solution is 4.18 J/(g·°C) and that no energy is lost from the calorimeter. What is the final temperature of the solution?
Mission 3

Worked example

  • Excess zinc powder is added to copper(II) sulfate solution: Zn(s) + CuSO4(aq) → ZnSO4(aq) + Cu(s).
  • 50.0 mL of 0.200 M CuSO4(aq).
  • 1. ΔT from the graph, corrected for heat loss (how: next screen): 31.00 − 21.40 = 9.60 °C (= 9.60 K).
  • 2. q = 50.0 × 4.18 × 9.60 = 2006 J = 2.006 kJ.
  • 3. n(CuSO4) = 0.0500 × 0.200 = 0.0100 mol. Zinc is in excess, so CuSO4 is limiting.
  • 4. ΔH = −2.006 ÷ 0.0100 = −201 kJ/molrxn.
Mission 3

Finding the true ΔT

  • Background: AP questions give you ΔT, but labs correct for heat exchange like this.
  • Heat is exchanged with the air while the reaction is still going.
  • So the highest (or lowest) reading falls short of the true change.
  • Plot temperature against time. Extrapolate the line after the highest (or lowest) reading back to the time of mixing.
  • ΔT = extrapolated value − starting temperature.
Calculate
?1.00 g of iron powder is added to 50.0 g of copper(II) sulfate solution, an excess, in a polystyrene cup. Fe(s) + CuSO4(aq) → FeSO4(aq) + Cu(s). The corrected temperature rise is 12.5 °C. Calculate ΔH for the reaction.
  • Data: molar mass Fe = 55.85 g/mol; c = 4.18 J/(g·°C). The CuSO4(aq) is 50.0 mL of 1.00 M.
kJ/molrxn
Quick check
?A spirit burner containing ethanol, C2H5OH, heats 150.0 g of water in a copper can. Burning 0.920 g of ethanol raises the water's temperature from 19.0°C to 40.5°C. c(water) = 4.18 J/(g·°C); molar mass of ethanol 46.08 g/mol. What ΔH for the combustion of ethanol do these results give?
Mission 3

Why the measured value falls short

  • Accepted value for ethanol: −1367 kJ/molrxn. The can gave −675: about half.
  • The missing energy was not destroyed. It heated the air and the can, not the water.
  • Some ethanol burns incompletely (soot on the can); some evaporates from the wick.
  • Assumptions: all the heat reaches the water; the apparatus absorbs none; solutions behave like water.
  • Reduce losses: draft shield, lid, insulation, burner close to the can.
  • Heat loss makes a measured exothermic ΔH less negative.
Mission check

?A student measures the enthalpy change when potassium chloride dissolves in water. The temperature falls. The student uses a thin glass beaker with no lid instead of an insulated cup. How does the student's value of ΔH compare with the accepted value?

Exchange with the room always shrinks the measured ΔT. Under the can, the "missing half" went into the air and the can, so the value fell short.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why do some salts make water colder as they dissolve, while others warm it up?

Make a guess: Why do some salts make water colder as they dissolve, while others warm it up?

Mission 4

Dissolving in two steps

  • Overcome ion–ion attractions in the solid and some water–water hydrogen bonds: energy absorbed.
  • Form ion–dipole attractions between the ions and water: energy released.
  • Water's δ− O faces cations; its δ+ H faces anions.
  • Absorbed > released: endothermic. Released > absorbed: exothermic.
Mission 4

What the thermometer tells you

  • In an insulated cup, the water is the surroundings; the dissolving salt is the system.
  • Temperature falls: energy flowed from the water into the dissolving salt. Endothermic.
  • Temperature rises: energy flowed from the dissolving salt into the water. Exothermic.
  • Energy transferred: q = mcΔT. Water warming means q for the dissolving process is negative.
Quick check
?The particulate diagrams represent a small part of a sample of sodium chloride and water before and after some of the NaCl(s) dissolves. Which statement correctly describes the interactions shown changing as the NaCl dissolves?
Mission 4

The free energy of dissolving

  • Three contributions, each with an enthalpy part and an entropy part:
  • Breaking up the solid: ΔH > 0; the ions disperse, so ΔS > 0.
  • Reorganizing the water: some hydrogen bonds break (ΔH > 0); water molecules are held around the ions (ΔS < 0).
  • Ion–water attractions form: ΔH < 0.
  • The terms partly cancel, so ΔG° of dissolution, and solubility, are hard to predict.
Quick check
?Solid MX contains M⁺ and X⁻ ions. Solid QZ contains Q²⁺ and Z²⁻ ions that are about the same sizes as M⁺ and X⁻. A student predicts that QZ is more soluble in water than MX because, according to Coulomb's law, the ions of QZ attract water molecules more strongly. Which evaluation of the student's prediction is correct?
Mission check

?A student stirs potassium nitrate, KNO3, into water in an insulated cup. The temperature falls from 21.0 °C to 14.5 °C. Which statement explains the fall?

Every salt pays to pull its ions apart and is repaid as water surrounds them. Which is bigger decides cold (KNO3, NH4NO3) or warm (MgCl2, CaCl2).

Mission 5

Mission 5: Carbon always burns partly to CO2. So how do we know ΔH for making carbon monoxide?

Make a guess: Carbon always burns partly to CO2. So how do we know ΔH for making carbon monoxide?

Mission 5

Hess's law

  • Hess's law: ΔH for an overall process equals the sum of ΔH for its steps.
  • It follows from conservation of energy: no route can create or destroy energy.
  • Any process can be split into a series of steps, each with its own energy change.
  • So a ΔH you can't measure = the sum of ΔH values you can.
Mission 5

Three moves

  • Reverse an equation: change the sign of ΔH.
  • Multiply an equation: multiply ΔH by the same number.
  • Add equations: add their ΔH values. Species on both sides cancel.
  • Arrange the given equations so they add up to the target equation.
Mission 5

Worked example: carbon monoxide

  • Target: C(s) + ½O2(g) → CO(g).
  • (1) C(s) + O2(g) → CO2(g), ΔH° = −393.5 kJ/molrxn · (2) CO(g) + ½O2(g) → CO2(g), ΔH° = −283.0 kJ/molrxn.
  • Keep (1). Reverse (2): CO2(g) → CO(g) + ½O2(g), ΔH = +283.0.
  • Add: CO2 cancels, and ½O2 cancels from one side.
  • ΔH° = −393.5 + 283.0 = −110.5 kJ/molrxn.
Quick check
?A process occurs in two steps. Step 1, A → B, is slow and has ΔH1 = +30 kJ/molrxn. Step 2, B → C, is fast and has ΔH2 = −80 kJ/molrxn. Which of the following gives the correct ΔH for the overall process A → C, with a valid justification?
Calculate
?Nitrogen monoxide from car exhausts is oxidized in the air: 2NO(g) + O2(g) → 2NO2(g). Calculate ΔH for this reaction from:
  • (1) ½N2(g) + ½O2(g) → NO(g), ΔH° = +90.3 kJ/molrxn · (2) ½N2(g) + O2(g) → NO2(g), ΔH° = +33.2 kJ/molrxn. Answer in kJ/molrxn.
kJ/molrxn
Quick check
?The enthalpy changes of these reactions are known: S(s) + O2(g) → SO2(g), ΔH1; 2 S(s) + 3 O2(g) → 2 SO3(g), ΔH2. The enthalpy change of 2 SO2(g) + O2(g) → 2 SO3(g) can be written as ΔH = ΔH2 + nΔH1. What is the value of n?
Mission check

?Graphite turns into diamond only at enormous pressures, so ΔH for C(s, graphite) → C(s, diamond) can't be measured directly. But both forms burn.
(1) C(s, graphite) + O2(g) → CO2(g), ΔH° = −393.5 kJ/molrxn · (2) C(s, diamond) + O2(g) → CO2(g), ΔH° = −395.4 kJ/molrxn. What is ΔH° for graphite → diamond?

Same trick as for CO. Combine reactions you can measure, and Hess's law gives the one you can't.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Carbon and hydrogen don't combine cleanly to make methane. So how does the data booklet know its enthalpy of formation?

Make a guess: Carbon and hydrogen don't combine cleanly to make methane. So how does the data booklet know its enthalpy of formation?

Mission 6

Standard conditions and standard states

  • ° means standard state: gases at 1 atm (or 1 bar), solutions at 1.0 M, pure solids and liquids. Tables are usually for 25 °C (298 K).
  • Standard state of a substance: its pure, normal form under those conditions.
  • Examples: C(s, graphite), O2(g), Br2(l), H2O(l), Na(s).
Mission 6

Standard enthalpy of formation

  • ΔH°f: the enthalpy change when 1 mol of a compound forms from its elements in their standard states.
  • An element in its standard state: ΔH°f = 0.
  • Use fractions to make exactly 1 mol: 2Al(s) + 1½O2(g) → Al2O3(s).
  • Start from elements, never from gaseous atoms such as C(g) or H(g).
Quick check
?For which of the following equations is the standard enthalpy change, ΔH°, equal to the standard enthalpy of formation of CaO(s)?
Mission 6

Standard enthalpy of combustion

  • Background: not required for the AP exam, but combustion tables are common.
  • Enthalpy of combustion: the enthalpy change when 1 mol of a substance burns completely in oxygen.
  • Complete: carbon ends as CO2(g), hydrogen as H2O(l).
  • Always negative. Elements burn too: C(s) and H2(g) have values.
Mission 6

Using formation data: limestone

  • Reactants and products can both be made from the same elements.
  • Hess's law then gives ΔH° = ΣΔH°f(products) − ΣΔH°f(reactants).
  • Multiply each value by its coefficient. Elements in their standard states count as zero.
  • CaCO3(s) → CaO(s) + CO2(g)
  • ΔH°f (kJ/mol): CaCO3(s) −1207.6, CaO(s) −634.9, CO2(g) −393.5.
  • ΔH° = [−634.9 + (−393.5)] − [−1207.6] = −1028.4 + 1207.6 = +179.2 kJ/molrxn: endothermic.
  • The same pattern gives ΔG°: ΔG°rxn = ΣΔG°f(products) − ΣΔG°f(reactants).
Quick check
?Methane burns: CH4(g) + 2 O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2 H2O(l). ΔH°f (kJ/mol): CH4(g) −74.8, CO2(g) −393.5, H2O(l) −285.8. What quantity of heat is released when 0.500 mol of O2(g) is consumed by this reaction at standard conditions?
Mission check

?Propane can't be made cleanly from carbon and hydrogen. These reactions can be measured in a calorimeter:
(1) C(s) + O2(g) → CO2(g), ΔH° = −393.5 kJ/molrxn · (2) H2(g) + ½O2(g) → H2O(l), ΔH° = −285.8 kJ/molrxn · (3) C3H8(g) + 5O2(g) → 3CO2(g) + 4H2O(l), ΔH° = −2219.2 kJ/molrxn. What is ΔH°f of propane, for 3C(s) + 4H2(g) → C3H8(g)?

That's how the booklet knows the enthalpy of formation of methane too. Carbon, hydrogen and methane are each burned separately, and Hess's law combines the three values.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Exothermic: system loses energy, surroundings warm, ΔH negative. Endothermic: system gains energy, surroundings cool, ΔH positive.
  • Profiles: ΔH from reactants to products; Ea from reactants to the top of the barrier.
  • Heat flows by collisions until thermal equilibrium; q = (mol of reaction) × ΔH°.

Mission 2

  • ΔH = Σ(bonds broken) − Σ(bonds formed): exothermic when the bonds formed are stronger in total.
  • Average bond enthalpies give an estimate of ΔH; the bond energy is the depth of the potential-energy well.

Mission 3

  • q = mcΔT (m of the water or solution), then ΔH = −q ÷ n, with n for the limiting reactant.
  • Heat exchanged with the room makes measured |ΔH| too small; same q gives a smaller ΔT for larger c.

Mission 4

  • Dissolving = break the lattice (energy in) + hydrate the ions (energy out).
  • Temperature falls: endothermic. ΔG° of dissolving balances several enthalpy and entropy terms.

Mission 5

  • Hess's law: ΔH depends only on start and finish, so a hard-to-measure ΔH is the sum along another route.
  • Reverse → change the sign; multiply → multiply ΔH; add → add ΔH.

Mission 6

  • ΔH°f: 1 mol of compound from its elements in standard states (elements = 0). ΔH° = ΣΔH°f(products) − ΣΔH°f(reactants).
  • Standard state: 1 atm, 1.0 M; the same products − reactants pattern gives ΔG° from ΔG°f.
