Mission 1

Three reasons in the Qur'an

  • "Did ye then think that We had created you in jest" (Qur'an 23:115, Yusuf Ali)? The Qur'an insists human life has a purpose.
  • 1. Adam's creation and the knowledge of God: humans are made able to know God, and entrusted with the earth.
  • 2. Worship: humans are created to serve and worship God.
  • 3. Moral tribulation: life is a test of who does best.
  • The three are one purpose seen from three sides: know God, serve God, prove faithful.
Mission 1Read the source

Adam: the khalifah who knows

> "Behold, thy Lord said to the angels: 'I will create a vicegerent on earth.'" (Qur'an 2:30, Yusuf Ali)

> "And He taught Adam the names of all things" (Qur'an 2:31, Yusuf Ali)

  • The angels ask why God would place on earth one who will shed blood; God answers, "I know what ye know not" (2:30).
  • Adam names what the angels cannot (2:31–33): human knowledge is his distinction. The angels bow to him (2:34).
  • Khalifah (vicegerent, steward): humans hold the earth on trust (see Mission 2).

Mission 1Read the source

Created to worship

> "I have only created Jinns and men, that they may serve Me." (Qur'an 51:56, Yusuf Ali)

  • Pickthall: "I created the jinn and humankind only that they might worship Me." The Arabic li-ya'budun covers both serve and worship.
  • God does not need worship (51:57–58 says God asks no provision from them); worship benefits the worshipper.
  • An early gloss, often attributed to Mujahid, reads "worship Me" as "know Me": tying reasons 1 and 2 together.

Q1 Why do the two translations differ?

Show answer

The Arabic word means both serving and worshipping.

Show answer

The worshipper.

Mission 1

What counts as worship?

  • 'Ibadah (worship, from 'abd, servant) is wider than prayer.
  • Ritual worship: the Five Pillars: shahadah, salah, zakah, sawm, Hajj.
  • Everyday worship: any lawful act done for God's sake: work, study, family care, honesty, removing a harmful object from the road (Sahih Muslim 35, paraphrase).
  • Intention (niyyah) turns ordinary action into worship: the first hadith in al-Bukhari's collection says actions are judged by intentions (Sahih al-Bukhari 1, paraphrase).
  • Ihsan: worship God as though you see Him (Hadith of Jibril): sincerity, not mere routine.
  • Your spec: "the concept of worship".
Mission 1

Life as a test

  • "He Who created Death and Life, that He may try which of you is best in deed" (Qur'an 67:2, Yusuf Ali).
  • Tests by hardship: "fear and hunger, some loss in goods or lives" (2:155); tests by blessing as well: "by evil and by good" (21:35).
  • Humans are free to choose: "We showed him the Way: whether he be grateful or ungrateful" (Qur'an 76:3, Yusuf Ali).
  • Outcomes come at the Last Day (see Mission 4); how God's decree and human choice fit together.
  • Key virtues: sabr (patient endurance) and shukr (gratitude).

Mission 1Diversity

Different emphases

  • Legal and ethical emphasis (most scholars): purpose is fulfilled by obeying the Shari'ah: duties to God and to others.
  • Sufi emphasis: purpose is to know and love God. Sufis often cite a saying, "I was a hidden treasure and loved to be known, so I created creation" (paraphrase); hadith scholars say it has no sound chain, but Sufis value its meaning.
  • Modernist and reformist emphasis: khalifah and justice in this world: building a just society is itself worship.
  • Shi'a emphasis: purpose is fulfilled under the guidance of the Prophet and the Imams; justice ('adl) stressed as the frame of the test.
  • All agree: worship, knowledge of God and moral testing belong together.
Mission 1Evaluate

"For Muslims, this life is only important as a preparation for the life to come."

  • For: "the life of this world is but goods and chattels of deception" (Qur'an 3:185, Yusuf Ali); "the Hereafter is better and more enduring" (87:17); the Prophet: be in this world like a stranger or a traveller (Sahih al-Bukhari 6416, paraphrase).
  • Against: "nor forget thy portion in this world" (Qur'an 28:77, Yusuf Ali); the prayer "give us good in this world and good in the Hereafter" (2:201); khalifah gives this world value in itself; Shari'ah protects life, family, property and intellect here.
  • Middle view: this life matters because it prepares for the next: worship, justice and care for creation are the preparation.
  • Judge it by: Does "only" mean "only as preparation" or "of no value"? Can a life be both a test and a gift? Which Muslims (Sufi, modernist, legal) would accept the claim, and with what meaning?
  • 15-mark (AO2) tip: weigh the strongest view on each side and reach a reasoned judgement of your own.

Mission 2

Khalifah: trustee, not owner

  • Khalifah: steward, vicegerent, deputy. God appoints humans as khalifah on earth (2:30;).
  • The earth belongs to God: "To Him belong all things in the heavens and on earth" (4:171); humans hold it on trust (amanah).
  • A trustee may use what is entrusted, but must not waste or destroy it, and will answer for it on the Last Day.
  • The Ummah (worldwide Muslim community) shares this role collectively, not just individuals.
  • AQA term: Khalifah (stewards).
Mission 2Read the source

Stewards in the Qur'an

> "It is He Who hath made you (His) agents, inheritors of the earth: He hath raised you in ranks, some above others: that He may try you in the gifts He hath given you" (Qur'an 6:165, Yusuf Ali)

> "Mischief has appeared on land and sea because of (the meed) that the hands of men have earned" (Qur'an 30:41, Yusuf Ali)

  • 55:7–9: God has "set up the Balance" (mizan), "In order that ye may not transgress (due) balance".

Mission 2

Principles and the Prophet's example

  • Tawhid: one Creator, so creation is a unity, and every creature praises God (17:44).
  • Mizan (balance): the created order is measured; humans must not upset it.
  • No waste (israf): "waste not by excess, for Allah loveth not the wasters" (Qur'an 7:31, Yusuf Ali).
  • Planting: any tree or crop a Muslim plants that feeds a person, bird or animal counts as charity (Sahih al-Bukhari 2320, paraphrase). Even if the Last Hour comes while you hold a sapling, plant it (Musnad Ahmad, paraphrase).
  • Protected land: the Prophet set aside a hima (protected reserve) near Madinah; Islamic law later protected zones around wells and rivers (harim).

Mission 2

Animals in the Qur'an and Sunnah

  • "There is not an animal (that lives) on the earth, nor a being that flies on its wings, but (forms part of) communities like you." (Qur'an 6:38, Yusuf Ali)
  • So animals have their own worth before God.
  • A woman was punished for starving a cat she had shut up; a man was forgiven for giving water to a thirsty dog (Sahih al-Bukhari 3318 and 2363, paraphrase).
  • Slaughter with ihsan (excellence): God has prescribed excellence in all things; sharpen the blade and spare the animal suffering (Sahih Muslim 1955, paraphrase).
  • The Prophet forbade using living creatures as targets, and branding or striking animals' faces (Sahih Muslim, paraphrase).
  • Animals may be used for food, work and transport, but never with cruelty or for sport alone.
Mission 2

Animal issues today

  • Factory farming: many Muslim writers argue intensive systems break the duty of ihsan, e.g. Al-Hafiz B. A. Masri (1914–1992), a former imam of the Shah Jahan Mosque, Woking, Animals in Islam (1989).
  • Halal slaughter and stunning: some Muslim bodies accept pre-stunning that does not kill the animal; others reject any stunning, so the animal must be alive and the bleeding complete.
  • Animal experiments: many scholars permit research needed for human health, with suffering kept to a minimum; testing for cosmetics is widely opposed.
  • Wildlife: the Indonesian Council of Ulama issued a fatwa (2014) declaring the illegal killing and trade of endangered species haram.
  • Principle in each case: use is allowed; cruelty and waste are not.

Mission 2

Changing understandings

  • Classical teaching dealt with waste, cruelty and local land. Modern science shows global human impact: climate change, extinction, plastic in the oceans.
  • Seyyed Hossein Nasr (b. 1933), Man and Nature (1968): the crisis is spiritual; modern people stopped seeing nature as sacred (paraphrase).
  • Islamic Declaration on Global Climate Change (Istanbul, 2015): Muslim scholars called on the Ummah and governments to phase out greenhouse gas emissions and move to renewable energy, citing khalifah and mizan.
  • Al-Mizan: A Covenant for the Earth (2024), a scholar-led statement supported by the UN Environment Programme.
  • In practice: IFEES (UK, founded 1994 by Fazlun Khalid); "green" mosques such as Cambridge Central Mosque (opened 2019); campaigns to cut waste at Ramadan iftars and during Hajj.
  • Many argue the principles are old; the scale of the duty is new.
Mission 2Diversity

How strong a basis is khalifah?

  • Trusteeship reading (most environmental writers): khalifah means God's steward, accountable for the earth.
  • "Successor" reading: many classical commentators took khalifah to mean humans succeeding one another (or earlier beings) on earth. Ibn Taymiyya (1263–1328) rejected "God's deputy": God needs no deputy. Duties then rest on amanah and mizan rather than on the word khalifah.
  • Human-centred reading: creation is "subjected" to humans (45:13), so human needs come first; critics say this has been used to justify exploitation.
  • Practice gap: some Muslim writers note that oil-rich Muslim states and poor waste management in many countries contradict the teaching; others reply that this is a failure of practice, not of principle.
Mission 2Evaluate

"Modern science has changed Muslim beliefs about their responsibilities to the earth."

  • For: before the 20th century no scholar wrote of climate or extinction; declarations (2015, 2024) and eco-fatwas are new; scholars now apply maqasid (the aims of Shari'ah, e.g. protecting life) to the environment.
  • Against: the beliefs are unchanged: khalifah, amanah, mizan and the ban on waste are Qur'anic; the hima and the hadiths on animals are 7th-century; only the application has changed.
  • Another view: knowledge itself is a trust: knowing more about harm increases responsibility, so science changes what a faithful khalifah must do without changing belief.
  • Judge it by: Is a new application a new belief? Do Muslims now act differently, or only speak differently? Whose interpretation counts: scholars, declarations or ordinary believers?

Mission 3

Al-Qadr: what Muslims agree on

  • Al-Qadr (divine decree, "measuring out"): God knows and has recorded all that happens; for most Sunnis, God also wills and creates it.
  • For Sunnis, one of the six articles of faith. In the Hadith of Jibril, belief in the decree, "its good and its bad", is part of faith (Sahih Muslim, Book of Faith, no. 8; paraphrase).
  • Muslims also believe God is just and will judge each person by their deeds.
  • So two beliefs must be held together: God's control of all things, and human responsibility.
Mission 3Read the source

> "But ye shall not will except as Allah wills,- the Cherisher of the Worlds." (Qur'an 81:29, Yusuf Ali)

> "Let him who will believe, and let him who will, reject (it)" (Qur'an 18:29, Yusuf Ali)

> "Verily never will Allah change the condition of a people until they change it themselves (with their own souls)." (Qur'an 13:11, Yusuf Ali)

Mission 3

The Mu'tazila: justice first

  • Background: an early dispute. The Jabriyya (e.g. Jahm ibn Safwan, d. 746) said humans are compelled, like a tree moved by wind; some early Qadariyya (e.g. Ma'bad al-Juhani, d. c. 703) said God does not decree human acts at all.
  • The Mu'tazila grew from Basra in the 8th century (Wasil ibn Ata, d. 748; later Qadi Abd al-Jabbar, d. 1025). Second of their five principles: adl (God's justice).
  • A just God cannot punish people for acts He created in them. So humans originate their own acts, using a power God gives them.
  • God wills and commands only good; evil acts are the human's own (41:46: "nor is thy Lord ever unjust (in the least) to His Servants", Yusuf Ali).
  • Reason can know good and evil; God's commands match what reason sees.
  • God knows all acts in advance, but knowing is not causing.
  • AQA spelling: Mutazili.
Mission 3

The Ash'aris: God's power first

  • Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari (873/4–935/6) was a Mu'tazili until about 912, then broke with them. His school became the main Sunni theology, beside the Maturidis.
  • God is the only creator: "But Allah has created you and your handwork!" (Qur'an 37:96, Yusuf Ali). To say humans create their acts gives God partners in creating.
  • Kasb (acquisition): God creates the act, and with it a power in the person at the moment of acting; the person acquires the act and so is answerable for it.
  • Good is what God commands; God owes creatures nothing, so whatever God does is just by definition.
  • Critics, Mu'tazili and later, found kasb hard to pin down: a saying calls something obscure "subtler than al-Ash'ari's kasb".
  • AQA spelling: Ashari.

Mission 3

Shi'a Islam and bada'

  • Twelver Shi'a theology counts adl (justice) among the five roots of faith; on human acts it is close to the Mu'tazila.
  • Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq (d. 765) is reported to teach: neither compulsion nor total delegation, but "a matter between the two" (al-Kulayni, al-Kafi; paraphrase).
  • Bada' ("appearing"): God may replace one decree with another as human acts change, e.g. charity or prayer lengthening a life.

> "Allah doth blot out or confirm what He pleaseth: with Him is the Mother of the Book." (Qur'an 13:39, Yusuf Ali)

  • Shi'a scholars (e.g. al-Shaykh al-Mufid, d. 1022) explain: God's knowledge never changes; what changes is the decree as it appears to creatures.
  • Sunni critics said bada' implies God learns something new. Shi'a scholars reject that reading.
  • Sunnis too report that "nothing turns back the decree except supplication" (Tirmidhi; paraphrase).

Mission 3

Living with the decree

  • Purpose: life is a test within God's decree; humans act, are answerable, and trust God with the outcome.
  • Tawakkul (trust in God): do what you can, then rely on God.
  • Sabr (patience) in loss: "To Allah We belong, and to Him is our return" (Qur'an 2:156, Yusuf Ali).
  • Belief in Qadr is meant to prevent both pride in success and despair in failure.
Mission 3Worked answer

Explaining a school's view

Task: explain the Mu'tazili understanding of divine decree.

  • Belief: humans originate their own acts with a power God gives them.
  • Source: "nor is thy Lord ever unjust (in the least) to His Servants" (Qur'an 41:46, Yusuf Ali); 18:29 on choosing to believe.
  • Develop: because God is just (adl), He cannot punish acts He created; so reward and punishment are deserved.
  • Link: God still knows all acts in advance, so knowledge, not causation, is what the decree means for human acts.
  • Strong answers keep the school's reason (justice) attached to its belief, not just list positions.
Mission 3Evaluate

Does the Ash'ari view sacrifice God's justice?

  • Claim: The Ash'ari view protects God's power, but at the cost of God's justice.
  • For: If God creates every act, punishing the person seems to punish what God made.
  • For: Kasb is hard to define; it may be a name for the problem rather than a solution.
  • Against: Justice is not a standard above God: whatever God does is just (al-Ghazali's argument from ownership).
  • Against: The Mu'tazili view makes humans creators beside God, threatening tawhid (God's oneness); a hadith in Abu Dawud calls deniers of the decree "the Magians of this community".
  • Criteria: Which verses does each view have to reinterpret? Is "justice" a human standard or God's? Does the view keep real responsibility? Does it keep God the only creator?

Mission 4

Akhirah: one life, then the return

  • Akhirah (the hereafter, "the last"): one of the six articles of faith for Sunnis; for Twelver Shi'a, ma'ad (the return) is a root of faith.
  • Muslims believe each person lives one earthly life; there is no rebirth.
  • "Every soul shall have a taste of death: And only on the Day of Judgment shall you be paid your full recompense." (Qur'an 3:185, Yusuf Ali)
  • The stages: death → Barzakh (waiting) → the trumpet and resurrection → judgement → Jannah (paradise) or Jahannam (hell).
Mission 4

The soul and the self

  • Ruh (spirit): God "breathed into him of My spirit" when Adam was formed (Qur'an 15:29, Yusuf Ali). Its nature is known to God alone (17:85).
  • Nafs (soul, self): the person who chooses, and who will be judged.
  • The Qur'an describes the self in states: prone to evil (12:53), self-reproaching (75:2), at peace (89:27). Sufis later read these as stages of growth.
  • The soul is created: it begins with God, not eternal on its own.
  • At death the Angel of Death takes the soul (32:11).
  • Most Muslims hold that a human is soul and body: so resurrection is bodily.
Mission 4

Death and Barzakh

  • At death the person asks to return, but: "Before them is a Partition till the Day they are raised up." (Qur'an 23:100, Yusuf Ali)
  • Barzakh (barrier) is the state between death and resurrection.
  • Hadith describe two angels, Munkar and Nakir, questioning the dead: Who is your Lord? What is your religion? Who is your prophet? (Tirmidhi; paraphrase)
  • The grave becomes a foretaste: "a garden of paradise or a pit of hell" (Tirmidhi; paraphrase).
  • Martyrs are "living" with their Lord (3:169).
  • Views differ on how conscious the dead are; most Sunni and Shi'a scholars hold the dead are aware.
Mission 4Read the source

The trumpet and the resurrection

> "The Trumpet will (just) be sounded, when all that are in the heavens and on earth will swoon … Then will a second one be sounded, when, behold, they will be standing and looking on!" (Qur'an 39:68, Yusuf Ali)

> "He says, 'Who can give life to (dry) bones and decomposed ones (at that)?' Say, 'He will give them life Who created them for the first time!'" (Qur'an 36:78–79, Yusuf Ali)

  • In tradition the angel Israfil blows the trumpet (named in hadith, not in the Qur'an).

Mission 4

The Day of Judgement

  • God is "Master of the Day of Judgment" (Qur'an 1:4, Yusuf Ali).
  • Each person receives their record of deeds: in the right hand (saved) or the left (lost) (69:19–25).
  • Deeds are weighed on the balance (mizan): "if there be (no more than) the weight of a mustard seed, We will bring it (to account)" (Qur'an 21:47, Yusuf Ali).
  • Intercession (shafa'a), above all by the Prophet, only with God's permission (2:255).
  • Hadith describe a bridge (sirat) over hell to paradise (Bukhari; Muslim).
  • Mercy: "My mercy prevails over My wrath" (hadith qudsi, Bukhari; Muslim; paraphrase).

Paradise and hell

> "(Here is) a Parable of the Garden which the righteous are promised: in it are rivers of water incorruptible; rivers of milk of which the taste never changes; rivers of wine, a joy to those who drink; and rivers of honey pure and clear." (Qur'an 47:15, Yusuf Ali)

> "Every time a Group is cast therein, its Keepers will ask, 'Did no Warner come to you?' They will say: 'Yes indeed; a Warner did come to us, but we rejected him'" (Qur'an 67:8–9, Yusuf Ali)

  • 47:15 contrasts the Garden with those who drink "boiling water". 67:7–10 describe hell drawing in breath and "bursting with fury"; the damned admit, "Had we but listened or used our intelligence" (67:10).

Mission 4

Jannah and Jahannam

  • Jannah (the Garden): gardens, rivers, peace, companionship; ranks of paradise; above all, nearness to God.
  • Sunni theologians (Ash'ari, Maturidi, Hanbali) hold the righteous will see God: faces "Looking towards their Lord" (Qur'an 75:23, Yusuf Ali). Mu'tazila and Twelver Shi'a read this as spiritual vision, not sight.
  • Jahannam (hell): fire, boiling water, regret; also separation from God.
  • Most scholars teach that those who rejected faith remain in hell; believers punished for sins may be brought out by mercy and intercession (Bukhari; Muslim).
  • A minority discussed whether hell's punishment ends (e.g. Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya, 1292–1350).
Mission 4Diversity

Literal or symbolic?

  • Bodily and literal: most Sunni and Shi'a scholars. Many Sunnis (Ash'ari, Hanbali) add "without asking how" (bi-la kayf). The rivers are real, but beyond present understanding: "no person knows what delights … are kept hidden" (32:17).
  • Spiritual only: Ibn Sina (980–1037) argued the soul alone survives; the images are for ordinary believers.
  • Al-Ghazali (1058–1111), Tahafut al-Falasifa (The Incoherence of the Philosophers, 1095): denying bodily resurrection was one of three views he judged unbelief.
  • Ibn Rushd (1126–1198), Tahafut al-Tahafut: defended the philosophers; scripture may be read on more than one level.
  • Modern symbolic readings: e.g. Muhammad Asad (1900–1992), The Message of the Qur'an (1980): the images are allegories of states beyond imagining (paraphrase); 47:15 itself says "parable".

Mission 4

Why the Akhirah matters now

  • Purpose: life is a test; every act counts (99:7–8).
  • Accountability: no one escapes justice; wrongs not righted on earth will be righted.
  • Hope and fear together (khawf and raja'): fear keeps a believer from sin; hope stops despair (39:53).
  • Comfort in grief: death is a return to God (2:156).
Mission 4Evaluate

Does the Day of Judgement inspire fear or faith?

  • Claim: Belief in the Day of Judgement inspires fear more than faith.
  • Fear: vivid descriptions of hell (67:7–10; 4:56) are meant to warn.
  • Fear: a believer can never be certain of their own outcome.
  • Faith: the warnings serve mercy: a warning given is a chance to change (67:8).
  • Faith: "Despair not of the Mercy of Allah" (Qur'an 39:53, Yusuf Ali); good deeds are multiplied.
  • Criteria: Is fear itself a form of faith (taqwa, God-consciousness)? Does the effect differ between believers? Do paradise texts outnumber hell texts? What does God's mercy mean for judgement?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The Qur'an gives three reasons for human existence: Adam's creation and the knowledge of God (2:30–34), worship (51:56) and moral testing (67:2).
  • Worship ('ibadah) includes every lawful act done with the right intention.
  • Muslims debate whether this life matters only as preparation for the next; most hold it matters because it prepares.

Mission 2

  • Humans, and the Ummah together, are khalifah: trustees of God's earth, bound by mizan (balance), forbidden waste, and answerable on the Last Day (6:165; 30:41; 7:31).
  • The Qur'an and Sunnah forbid cruelty to animals (6:38; the cat and dog hadiths; ihsan in slaughter).
  • Modern science and declarations (2015, 2024) have widened how Muslims apply these duties; scholars debate whether this is new belief or new application.

Mission 3

  • Al-Qadr: God knows and records all things (and, for most Sunnis, wills and creates them); humans are still responsible and will be judged.
  • Mu'tazila: God's justice first; humans originate their acts. Ash'aris: God's power first; God creates acts, humans acquire them (kasb).
  • Shi'a bada': God may replace a decree as human acts change, without any change in God's knowledge.

Mission 4

  • Death → Barzakh → trumpet and resurrection → judgement (record, balance, intercession) → paradise or hell.
  • The Qur'an describes paradise and hell vividly (47:15; 67:7–10); Muslims read the images literally "without asking how", or as signs of realities beyond imagining.
