Mission 1

Mission 1: With air resistance ignored, which forces still act on a ball once it has left your foot?

Make a guess: With air resistance ignored, which forces still act on a ball once it has left your foot?

Mission 1

A force is an interaction

  • A force is a push or pull by one object on another. Unit: the newton, N.
  • Every force has a source: name it as "force on X by Y".
  • Force is a vector: size and direction.
  • No object exerts a net force on itself. No source, no force.
Quick check
?A book rests on a table. At the level of atoms, which statement explains why the book does not fall through the table?
Mission 1

Contact forces are electric

  • Touching surfaces never truly "touch": their atoms' charged particles repel when pushed together and attract when pulled apart.
  • Normal force, friction and tension are the total of countless tiny electric forces.
  • Adding them one by one is impossible, so we model them as single contact forces.
Mission 1

Weight

  • Weight: the gravitational force on an object by Earth (or another planet or moon).
  • Fg = mg
  • Mass (kg): how much matter. Same everywhere. Weight (N): changes with g.
  • Weight acts at the center of gravity. It needs no contact: a field force.
Quick check
?A rover weighs 1600 N at Earth's surface. Use g = 10 m/s². The gravitational field strength at the surface of Mars is 3.7 N/kg. What is the rover's weight on Mars?
Mission 1

Normal force, friction, tension

  • Normal force FN: perpendicular to the surface.
  • Friction: parallel to the surface. Opposes sliding, or the tendency to slide, between the surfaces.
  • Tension: the pull of a string, rope or cable, along it, away from the object.
  • Ideal string (light, does not stretch): the same tension all along it, and on both sides of an ideal pulley.
  • A heavy rope or chain: tension is largest at the top, where it holds the most rope below.
Mission 1

Drawing a free-body diagram

  • One object only. Show every force on it, by something else.
  • Label each arrow with what exerts it. Arrow length shows size.
  • Never draw a "force of motion" or the object's own push on something else.
  • AP rule: a dot for the object (its center of mass); each force a separate straight arrow pointing away from the dot; no components on the diagram.
Mission 1

From diagram to net force

  • Add all the forces as vectors: the net force.
  • Resolve each force along two perpendicular axes, then add components.
  • Choose one axis parallel to the acceleration, e.g. along a slope.
  • A sled is pulled by a rope: tension 50 N at 30° above the horizontal; friction 30 N.
  • Horizontal: 50 cos 30° − 30 = 43.3 − 30 = 13.3 N forward.
  • Vertical: the rope's 50 sin 30° = 25 N upward, plus the normal force, balances the gravitational force.
Quick check
?A crate is given a push and then slides down a rough ramp on its own. Which list gives all the forces acting on the crate while it slides, and no others?
Quick check
?Block A rests on top of block B, and B rests on a level floor. Both blocks are at rest. Which list gives all the forces that belong on the free-body diagram of block B?
Mission check

?A hockey puck is hit by a stick and then slides across level ice. Friction and air resistance are negligible. Which forces are exerted on the puck while it slides, after it has left the stick?

Same for the kicked ball: once the foot has gone, only Earth pulls on it (its weight). Nothing pushes it along its path.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Can an object's balance point be in empty space, where there is no material at all?

Make a guess: Can an object's balance point be in empty space, where there is no material at all?

Mission 2

Center of mass

  • Center of mass: the single point where the whole mass can be treated as concentrated.
  • A mass-weighted average position of all the bits of the object.
  • Uniform and regular (sphere, cylinder, cube, uniform beam): at the geometric center.
  • If the mass is spread symmetrically, it lies on every line of symmetry.
  • Uniform ring: at the center of the hole. No material there.
Quick check
?A cylindrical rod is made of a 0.50 m aluminum section joined end to end to a 0.50 m steel section of the same diameter. Each section is uniform, and the steel section has the greater mass. How does the location of the rod's center of mass compare with the rod's midpoint?
Mission 2

Calculating the center of mass

  • For small objects along a line: xcm = Σmᵢxᵢ / Σmᵢ.
  • 4.0 kg at x = 0, 1.0 kg at x = 5.0 m.
  • xcm = (4.0 × 0 + 1.0 × 5.0) ÷ (4.0 + 1.0) = 5.0 ÷ 5.0 = 1.0 m.
  • Always nearer the heavier object. In 2D, do the same for y.
Quick check
?A uniform rod of mass 2m and length L has a small ball of mass m fixed to one end. How far from the other end of the rod is the center of mass of the rod–ball system?
Mission 2

One object at its center of mass

  • A system can be modeled as a single object at its center of mass.
  • A spinning hammer thrown through the air: every point wobbles, but the center of mass follows a smooth projectile path.
  • Gravity acts as if at the center of mass (uniform field).
Quick check
?A diver makes two dives from the same springboard. In each dive, her center of mass leaves the board from the same height with the same velocity. In dive 1 she keeps her body straight; in dive 2 she pulls into a tight tuck soon after leaving the board. Air resistance is negligible. How do the maximum heights reached by her center of mass in the two dives compare?
Mission check

?A high jumper clears the bar by arching her back, so that her body is draped over the bar in an upside-down U shape, with her head and arms hanging on one side and her legs on the other. Which statement about her center of mass, at the instant her hips are directly above the bar, can be correct?

Yes. The center of mass is an average position of the mass. For a ring, a horseshoe or an arched jumper, that average lies in empty space.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Can something be moving fast when all the forces on it cancel out?

Make a guess: Can something be moving fast when all the forces on it cancel out?

Mission 3

Newton's first law

  • If the net force on an object is zero, its velocity is constant (possibly zero).
  • Moving things do not stop "naturally". Friction and drag stop them.
  • No net force needed to keep moving; one is needed to change velocity.
  • This holds in inertial frames: frames that are not accelerating.
Quick check
?A space probe is so far from any star or planet that the forces exerted on it are negligible. Its engines fire briefly and then shut off. Which statement describes the probe's motion after the engines have shut off?
Mission 3

Equilibrium

  • Net force: the vector sum of all the forces on an object.
  • Translational equilibrium: net force zero → at rest or at constant velocity.
  • A skydiver whose drag equals her weight falls at constant velocity: her terminal velocity. She is in equilibrium.
Quick check
?A puck slides east at constant velocity across level ice with negligible friction. Starting at time t, a constant force directed north is exerted on the puck, and no other horizontal force acts. Which statement describes the puck's motion after time t?
Mission 3

Balanced one way, not the other

  • Resolve forces along two perpendicular axes. Each axis on its own.
  • Equilibrium needs both sums zero.
  • Forces can balance along one axis and not the other: the object accelerates only along the unbalanced axis.
  • A constant force perpendicular to the initial velocity changes only that perpendicular component. The original component stays constant: projectile-like motion.
Mission 3

On a slope

  • Take axes along the slope and perpendicular to it.
  • Choose the axis parallel to the acceleration (here, along the slope).
  • Weight resolves into mg sin θ down the slope and mg cos θ into the slope.
  • A 5.00 kg block rests on a rough slope at 30.0°. Equilibrium: friction = mg sin θ, normal force = mg cos θ.
  • g = 10 m/s²: friction = 25.0 N up the slope · normal force = 43.3 N
Quick check
?A block is held at rest on a ramp by a light string that is parallel to the ramp and tied to a post at the top, as shown in the diagram. The ramp is inclined at 37° above the horizontal, friction between the block and the ramp is negligible, and the gravitational force on the block is 50 N. Use sin 37° = 0.60 and cos 37° = 0.80. What is the tension in the string?
Mission check

?Which of the following objects is in translational equilibrium?

Yes. Moving fast or slow, an object with constant velocity has zero net force on it.

A skydiver at terminal velocity falls very fast, typically around 50 m/s, with zero net force: drag balances the gravitational force. Zero net force means constant velocity, not zero velocity.

Mission 4

Mission 4: A ball on a train floor starts rolling backward with nothing pushing it. Has Newton's first law failed?

Make a guess: A ball on a train floor starts rolling backward with nothing pushing it. Has Newton's first law failed?

Mission 4

The first law needs a frame

  • Newton's first law: zero net force → constant velocity (possibly zero).
  • Velocity is measured relative to a frame, so the law is a claim about a frame.
  • Inertial frame: a frame in which Newton's first law holds.
  • For everyday motion, treat the ground as an inertial frame.
Quick check
?A train is at rest on a straight, level track. A ball rests on the smooth floor of one of its train cars; friction and air resistance are negligible. The train then starts to speed up forward, and a passenger sees the ball slide toward the back of the train car, although no horizontal force is exerted on the ball. Which statement is correct?
Mission 4

An accelerating frame is not inertial

  • A train speeds up from rest. A ball rests on its smooth floor.
  • Inside the train car, the ball slides backward, with no horizontal force on it.
  • Seen from the platform, the ball stays put; the train car speeds up beneath it.
  • The first law holds in the platform's frame and fails in the train car's frame.
  • So the speeding-up train car is not an inertial frame.
Mission 4

Which frames are inertial?

  • Any frame moving at constant velocity relative to an inertial frame is also inertial.
  • A train at steady speed on a straight track: a ball on its floor stays at rest, just as on the platform.
  • Both frames measure the same acceleration for every object, so the first law holds in both.
  • Not inertial: any frame whose velocity changes: speeding up, slowing down, or turning.
  • Turning at constant speed still changes the velocity: its direction changes.
Quick check
?Treat the ground as an inertial frame. Which of these is also an inertial frame?
Mission check

?A car rounds a level bend at a constant speed. A can rests on the car's smooth, frictionless dashboard. A passenger sees the can slide toward the outside of the bend, although no horizontal force is exerted on it. Which statement is correct?

The law has not failed on the train either. In the platform's frame the ball keeps its velocity, as the first law says; it only seems to move on its own because the speeding-up train car is not an inertial frame.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Push a car and a wheelbarrow equally hard. How much more slowly does the car speed up?

Make a guess: Push a car and a wheelbarrow equally hard. How much more slowly does the car speed up?

Mission 5

Newton's second law

  • asys = ΣF/msys: the system's acceleration equals the net external force divided by its mass.
  • The acceleration is in the direction of the net force, not necessarily the velocity.
  • 1 newton: the net force that gives 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m/s². 1 N = 1 kg·m/s².
  • Same force: a ∝ 1/m. Same mass: a ∝ F.
Quick check
?A crate slides to the left across a level floor with negligible friction. At one instant, two students push on it horizontally: one pushes to the left with a force of 20 N, and the other pushes to the right with a force of 30 N. Which statement about the crate at this instant is correct?
Mission 5

Worked example

  • A 1200 kg car: driving force 3000 N forward, resistive forces 600 N backward.
  • Net force = 3000 − 600 = 2400 N forward.
  • a = F/m = 2400 ÷ 1200 = 2.0 m/s² forward.
  • From rest, after 5.0 s: v = 0 + 2.0 × 5.0 = 10 m/s.
  • Always use the net force, never just the driving force.
Quick check
?A loaded cart of mass 8.0 kg moves along a straight, level track. The graph shows the cart's velocity v as a function of time t. What is the magnitude of the net force exerted on the cart between t = 0 and t = 2.0 s?
Mission 5

Connected objects

  • Whole system first: only external forces count. Internal pulls and pushes cancel in pairs.
  • Then one object on its own: this gives the internal force (tension, or a contact force).
  • A 0.80 kg block on a smooth table is pulled by a string over an ideal pulley to a 0.20 kg hanging mass.
  • System: net force = 0.20 × 10 = 2.0 N; total mass 1.00 kg → a = 2.0 m/s².
  • Block alone: T = 0.80 × a = 1.6 N: less than the hanging weight, because the hanging mass accelerates.
  • Atwood machine (two masses over one ideal pulley): a = (m1 − m2)g/(m1 + m2).
Quick check
?Block P of mass m and block Q of mass 2m are connected by an ideal string and rest on a level surface with negligible friction. A student pulls block P with a constant horizontal force of magnitude F, directed away from Q, and both blocks speed up together. What is the tension in the string?
Mission 5

Apparent weight

  • A scale reads the normal force on you: your apparent weight.
  • Elevator accelerating upward: FN − mg = ma → FN = m(g + a). Reads more than mg.
  • Elevator accelerating downward: FN = m(g − a). Reads less.
  • Moving at constant velocity, up or down: reads mg.
  • Only gravity acting (free fall, orbit): FN = 0. Apparently weightless, though gravity still pulls.
Quick check
?A student stands on a scale in an elevator in three situations. 1: the elevator moves upward at constant speed. 2: the elevator moves upward and is slowing down. 3: the elevator moves downward and is slowing down. The acceleration has the same magnitude in situations 2 and 3. The scale readings are R1, R2 and R3. Which ranking is correct?
Mission check

?A student exerts different constant net forces on a cart on a level track and measures the cart's acceleration each time. The graph shows the results. A block is then fixed to the cart so that the mass of the cart–block system is twice the mass of the cart, and the experiment is repeated with the same net forces. Which describes the new graph of acceleration against net force?

Same force, a ∝ 1/m. A 1200 kg car has 40 times the mass of a 30 kg wheelbarrow, so the same push (ignoring friction) gives it 1/40 of the acceleration.

Mission 6: When a truck hits a small car, which one feels the bigger force?

Make a guess: When a truck hits a small car, which one feels the bigger force?

Mission 6

Forces come in pairs

  • FA on B = −FB on A: equal magnitude, opposite direction.
  • The two forces in a pair are always:
    • the same type (both gravitational, both contact, …)
    • on different objects
    • along the same line, at the same time.
Quick check
?A hand pushes block L to the right, and L pushes a smaller block S ahead of it. Both blocks speed up to the right along a level surface with negligible friction. The diagram shows a student's representation of the forces that L and S exert on each other: FL on S is the force exerted on S by L, and FS on L is the force exerted on L by S. Which statement about the student's diagram is correct?
Mission 6

Finding the partner

  • Name the force: "on X by Y". Its partner is "on Y by X".
  • Book on a table. Weight = force on book by Earth → partner: force on Earth by book, upward.
  • Normal force on book by table → partner: push on table by book, downward.
  • Weight and normal force on the book are not a pair: both act on the book, and they are different types.
Quick check
?A lamp hangs at rest from a cord fixed to the ceiling. The cord exerts an upward force on the lamp. Which force forms a Newton's third law pair with this force?
Quick check
?A horse pulls a cart along a level road, and both speed up from rest. A student argues: "The cart pulls back on the horse with a force equal in magnitude to the horse's pull on the cart, so these forces cancel and the cart cannot speed up." Which statement correctly identifies the flaw in the argument?
Mission 6

Pairs never cancel

  • The two forces act on different objects, so they never add to zero on either one.
  • Horse pulls cart; cart pulls back on horse, equally. The cart still speeds up:
    • on the cart: horse's pull > friction on the cart.
    • on the horse: ground's forward push on its hooves > cart's pull back.
  • Equal forces, different masses → different accelerations (a = F/m).
Mission check

?Cart 1 rolls along a level track and collides with cart 2. Cart 1 has three times the mass of cart 2. Force sensors record the force exerted on cart 1 by cart 2 and the force exerted on cart 2 by cart 1; the graph shows the data, with the direction of cart 1's initial motion as positive. Friction is negligible. How does the change in velocity of cart 2 during the collision compare with that of cart 1?

Truck and car feel forces of the same size at every instant. The car and its occupants get a much larger acceleration from the same size of force, because the car's mass is much smaller. That is why the car comes off worse.

Mission 7

Mission 7: A firework bursts in mid-air. What path does the center of mass of all its pieces follow?

Make a guess: A firework bursts in mid-air. What path does the center of mass of all its pieces follow?

Mission 7

Choosing a system

  • A system: the objects you choose to study. Everything else is the environment.
  • Forces between objects inside the system are internal. Forces from the environment are external.
  • A system's properties come from the interactions between its parts: an ice cube keeps its shape because its molecules hold each other in place.
  • A system can gain or lose mass and energy across its boundary: a rocket loses mass as exhaust leaves.
Quick check
?Logs burn in an open fireplace. Smoke and hot gases rise up the chimney, and a little ash is left behind. The system is the logs and whatever remains of them in the fireplace. Which statement about the mass of this system is correct?
Mission 7

Object model or system model?

  • Object model: ignore the inside. Fine when the question is only about the motion as a whole (a ship's time to reach a dock).
  • System model: needed when the parts behave differently from the whole (two carts joined by a spring, each oscillating).
  • Or when the internal structure decides the outcome: a rubber ball bounces, a clay ball of the same size goes splat.
  • Changing what happens outside can change the inside: a gentle push moves a book and box together; a hard push makes the book slide off the box.
Quick check
?A student wants to find the time Earth takes to orbit the Sun once. The student models Earth, with its oceans, its atmosphere and its slowly churning interior, as a single object. Which evaluation of this modeling choice is correct?
Mission 7

Internal forces cannot move the center of mass

  • Internal forces come in third-law pairs: they add to zero for the system.
  • So only the net external force changes the center of mass's motion: acm = ΣFext / M.
  • Firework shell: gravity is the only external force (air resistance negligible), before and after the burst.
  • So the center of mass of all the pieces carries on along the same parabola, until pieces start hitting the ground.
Mission 7

Velocity of the center of mass

  • vcm = Σmᵢvᵢ / Σmᵢ: the mass-weighted average velocity.
  • Cart of 3.0 kg at 2.0 m/s hits a 1.0 kg cart at rest.
  • vcm = (3.0 × 2.0 + 1.0 × 0) ÷ (3.0 + 1.0) = 6.0 ÷ 4.0 = 1.5 m/s.
  • Negligible friction: vcm is 1.5 m/s before, during and after the collision, whatever each cart does.
  • A system has one vcm. It changes only if a net external force acts.
Quick check
?Cart 1, of mass 1.0 kg, moves along a level track and collides with cart 2, of mass 2.0 kg, which is initially at rest. Motion sensors record each cart's velocity, and the velocity of the center of mass of the two-cart system is calculated from the data. The graph shows the three velocities as functions of time. Which claim do the data support?
Quick check
?A projectile is launched from level ground. If it did not explode, it would land a horizontal distance R from the launch point. At the highest point of its path, it explodes into two fragments. Fragment 1, which has one-third of the projectile's mass, falls straight down and lands directly below the highest point. Both fragments land at the same time, and air resistance is negligible. How far from the launch point does fragment 2 land?
Mission check

?A person of mass m stands at one end of a uniform raft of mass 4m and length L. The raft floats at rest on still water, and the water exerts a negligible horizontal force on it. The person walks to the other end of the raft and stops. How far does the raft move relative to the water?

Same rule as the firework. Only internal forces act horizontally, so the center of mass keeps its motion: at rest for the raft, along the original parabola for the firework pieces.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Every force is exerted on one object by another; a free-body diagram shows only the forces on the chosen object, each with its source.
  • Contact forces are the large-scale result of electric forces between atoms.
  • Weight = mg (Earth's pull); normal force ⟂ surface; friction ∥ surface; tension along the string.

Mission 2

  • Center of mass: the mass-weighted average position; at the center of a uniform regular object, on every line of symmetry, and possibly outside the material.
  • xcm = Σmᵢxᵢ / Σmᵢ: nearer the heavier mass.

Mission 3

  • Zero net force ⇔ constant velocity (including at rest): translational equilibrium.
  • On a slope, resolve along and perpendicular to the surface: mg sin θ and mg cos θ.

Mission 4

  • Inertial frame: one in which Newton's first law holds. Frames at constant velocity relative to the ground are inertial; speeding up, slowing down or turning frames are not.

Mission 5

  • asys = ΣF/msys: use the net external force; internal forces cancel.
  • A scale reads the normal force: more than mg when accelerating up, less when accelerating down, zero in free fall.

Mission 6

  • Third-law pair: same type, equal size, opposite direction, on two different objects; so the pair never cancels.

Mission 7

  • Choose the system first: internal forces cancel in pairs; only the net external force changes the center of mass's motion (acm = ΣFext/M).
  • vcm = Σmᵢvᵢ/Σmᵢ, one value for the whole system, constant when the net external force is zero.
