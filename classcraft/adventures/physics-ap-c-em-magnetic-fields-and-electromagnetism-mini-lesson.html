Mission 1Topic 12.1

Mission 1: Switch on a current in a wire just above a compass. Which way does the needle turn, and why?

Make a guess: Switch on a current in a wire just above a compass. Which way does the needle turn, and why?

Mission 1

Where magnetic fields come from

  • A magnetic field is produced by moving charges (an electric current) and by permanent magnets.
  • It exerts forces on moving charges, currents and magnetic materials (iron, steel).
  • A charge at rest feels no magnetic force.
Mission 1

Field lines

  • Direction of a field line: the way the north end of a compass needle points.
  • Closer lines = stronger field. Lines never cross.
  • Field lines are closed loops: outside a magnet they run N → S; inside, S → N.
  • Bar magnet: lines leave the N pole, curve around, enter the S pole, and continue back through the magnet.
Quick check
?Point C is at the center of a bar magnet, inside it. Point D is just outside the magnet, beside its center. How does the magnitude of the magnetic field at C compare with that at D?
Mission 1

Field of a long straight wire

  • Field lines are concentric circles centered on the wire, in planes at right angles to it.
  • Right-hand rule: thumb along the current; curled fingers give the field direction.
  • Lines spread out with distance: the field gets weaker away from the wire.
  • End-on views: ⊙ current out of the page → field counterclockwise; ⊗ into the page → clockwise.
Quick check
?A long, straight, vertical wire carries a steady current upward. Point P is 5.0 cm due east of the wire, level with it. What is the direction of the magnetic field of the wire at P?
Mission 1

Flat coil and solenoid

  • Flat coil: the circles around each part of the wire combine. At the center the field is along the axis.
  • Solenoid (long coil): inside, straight, parallel, evenly spaced lines (uniform field). Outside, the pattern of a bar magnet.
  • Direction: curl the fingers of your right hand the way the current flows around the turns; the thumb points along the field inside, toward the coil's N end.
Quick check
?You look at the left end of a long solenoid. Seen from there, the current in its turns flows clockwise. Which end of the solenoid is its north end?
Mission 1

Compasses and Earth

  • A compass needle is a small dipole. It turns to align with the net field where it is.
  • Earth's field ≈ a dipole. Near the geographic North Pole is a magnetic south pole, so compass N ends point north.
  • Closed loops mean the net flux out of any closed surface is zero: ∮B·dA = 0, Gauss's law for magnetism (Maxwell's second equation).
Quick check
?The diagram shows the shape of Earth's magnetic field lines, without arrows, in a plane through Earth's geographic poles. A compass needle mounted so that it can turn in this vertical plane is placed at point P, on Earth's surface. In which direction does the north end of the needle point?
Mission check

?A flat horizontal circular coil carries a steady current that flows clockwise when viewed from above. What is the direction of the magnetic field at the center of the coil?

Same rule, new shape. Around a straight wire the field circles the current, so a compass needle just below a wire laid along it swings toward lying across the wire, tangent to a circle centered on it: fully across if the wire's field is much stronger than Earth's. Reverse the current and it swings the other way.

Mission 2Topic 12.1

Mission 2: A magnet grabs an iron nail but ignores a copper pipe. What is different inside the iron?

Make a guess: A magnet grabs an iron nail but ignores a copper pipe. What is different inside the iron?

Mission 2

Magnetism comes from moving charge

  • A charge at rest makes no magnetic field.
  • A loop of current makes a field like a tiny bar magnet: a magnetic dipole.
  • In atoms, electrons move around nuclei and spin. Each atom can act as a tiny dipole.
  • A material is magnetized when many of these dipoles point the same way.
  • A dipole's field gets weaker with distance.
  • Every magnet is a dipole. No isolated north or south pole has ever been found.
Quick check
?Which of the following produces a magnetic field? Ignore Earth's field and Earth's motion.
Mission 2

An orbiting charge is a current loop

  • Charge q moves at speed v around a circle of radius r.
  • It passes any point once per period: T = 2πr/v.
  • Average current: I = q/T = qv/(2πr).
  • That current loop is a magnetic dipole. Classical model of an atom: the orbiting electron.
Quick check
?A particle with charge q moves at constant speed v around a circle of radius r. The circulating charge acts as a small current loop, which is a magnetic dipole. Which expression gives the average current associated with the particle's motion?
Quick check
?An iron nail does not attract a steel paper clip. After the nail has been stroked several times in one direction with one pole of a strong magnet, it does attract the paper clip. Which explanation of these observations is correct?
Mission 2

Domains

  • In iron, atomic dipoles line up with their neighbors in small regions: domains.
  • Unmagnetized iron: domains point in random directions. Their fields cancel.
  • In an external field, domains along the field grow and others turn. The iron becomes a magnet: induced magnetism.
  • The near end becomes the opposite pole, so iron is attracted to either pole.
  • Field removed: in some materials many domains stay aligned: a permanent magnet.
  • Break a magnet: each piece is a complete dipole with its own N and S.
Mission 2

Three kinds of material

  • Ferromagnetic (iron, nickel, cobalt): dipoles align strongly with the field; can stay aligned. Strongly attracted; can become permanent magnets.
  • Paramagnetic (aluminum, titanium, magnesium): dipoles align weakly with the field; alignment is lost when the field goes. Weakly attracted.
  • Diamagnetic (every material; it dominates in copper, water, bismuth): a weak alignment opposite the field. Weakly repelled.
  • Iron is diamagnetic too, but its ferromagnetism swamps the effect.
Quick check
?An aluminum rod is placed in a strong external magnetic field at time t1, and the field is removed at time t2. In each graph shown, M is the net alignment of the rod's atomic dipoles along the direction of the external field (positive when along the field). Which graph best represents the aluminum rod?
Mission 2

Permeability

  • Permeability μ: how strongly a material becomes magnetized in response to an external field.
  • Free space: the vacuum permeability μ0 = 4π × 10⁻⁷ T·m/A, a constant. It appears in B = μ0I/(2πr).
  • Matter's permeability differs from μ0, depending on its composition: dipoles that align along the field raise μ above μ0; a response opposite the field lowers it.
  • Iron: far greater than μ0 (hundreds to thousands of times).
  • μ of a material is not a constant. It changes with temperature, field strength, orientation and composition.
  • Example: in a strong field iron's domains are nearly all aligned, so extra field adds little magnetization. Its μ falls.
Quick check
?At room temperature and in a weak external field, which ranking of the magnetic permeabilities of iron (μFe), aluminum (μAl), copper (μCu) and free space (μ0) is correct?
Mission check

?Rods of nickel, aluminum and copper, identical in size and shape, are each placed in turn in a strong external magnetic field and then removed from it. Which of the rods can then be a permanent magnet?

Iron, like nickel, is ferromagnetic: its domains line up strongly with a field, so the nail is pulled hard. A copper pipe is diamagnetic: its tiny response is opposite the field, far too weak to notice.

Mission 3Topic 12.3

Mission 3: A wire carrying a current lies between the poles of a magnet. Which way is it pushed?

Make a guess: A wire carrying a current lies between the poles of a magnet. Which way is it pushed?

Mission 3

The motor effect

  • A current-carrying wire in a magnetic field feels a force.
  • The force is at right angles to both the current and the field.
  • No force when the current is parallel to the field.
  • Reverse the current or the field: the force reverses.
Mission 3

Which way?

  • Right-hand rule: fingers along B, thumb along I; the force comes out of your palm.
  • As a vector: F = I ℓ⃗ × B, with ℓ⃗ along the current.
Quick check
?A straight wire in the plane of the page carries a current toward the right side of the page through a uniform magnetic field directed into the page. What is the direction of the magnetic force exerted on the wire?
Mission 3

How big?

  • FB = IℓB sin θ for a straight wire in a uniform field.
  • θ = angle between the current and the field. Largest at 90°, zero at 0°.
  • The length is the length inside the field.
  • B is in tesla (T).
Mission 3

Worked example

  • A 4.0 cm length of wire carries 2.5 A at right angles to a 0.15 T field.
  • Force = 0.15 × 2.5 × 0.040 = 0.015 N.
  • Turn the wire to 30° to the field: F = 0.015 × sin 30° = 0.0075 N.
  • Backward: 0.012 N on a 5.0 cm wire carrying 3.0 A at right angles. B = force ÷ (current × length) = 0.012 ÷ (3.0 × 0.050) = 0.080 T.
Quick check
?The figure shows a straight wire carrying a current across a region of uniform magnetic field (shaded); the field is zero outside the region. What is the magnitude of the magnetic force exerted on the wire?
Mission 3

Wires that are not straight

  • Each element feels dF = I dℓ⃗ × B, so F = ∫ I dℓ⃗ × B.
  • Uniform B: F = I L × B, with L the straight line from the wire's start to its end, whatever its shape.
  • A closed loop in a uniform field: L = 0, so no net force (there can still be a torque).
  • Non-uniform B (e.g. near another wire): integrate along the wire.
Quick check
?The diagram shows a long straight wire carrying current I1 and a straight rod, perpendicular to the wire and in the same plane, carrying current I2. The long wire produces a magnetic field of magnitude μ0I1/(2πr) at a distance r from it. The magnitude of the magnetic force on the rod can be written F = (μ0I1I2/(2π))G. What is G?
Mission check

?A long straight wire carries a current vertically upward. It is in a uniform horizontal magnetic field that points due north. In which direction is the magnetic force on the wire?

The force is at right angles to both the current and the field, and its size is B × current × length when they are perpendicular: so a wire between the poles is pushed sideways, out of the gap or into it, never toward a pole.

Mission 4Topic 12.2

Mission 4: A magnetic field can steer a beam of charged particles. Can it also make them go faster?

Make a guess: A magnetic field can steer a beam of charged particles. Can it also make them go faster?

Mission 4

Force on a moving charge

  • A current is moving charge, so a single moving charge in a field also feels a force.
  • F = qvB sin θ
  • F = q(v × B)
  • θ = angle between v and B.
  • Zero force on a charge at rest, or moving along the field.
  • Example: an electron at 3.0 × 10⁶ m/s at right angles to a 0.20 T field: F = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 3.0 × 10⁶ × 0.20 = 9.6 × 10⁻¹⁴ N.
  • Magnetic forces act between moving charges: a charge at rest neither feels one nor exerts one.
Mission 4

Which way?

  • Right-hand rule for a positive charge: fingers along B, thumb along v, force out of the palm. Negative charge: force the other way.
  • Cross product v × B, then multiply by the sign of q.
  • The force is always ⟂ to the velocity, so it does no work: the speed and kinetic energy never change.
Quick check
?An electron moves toward the right side of the page through a uniform magnetic field directed out of the page. What is the direction of the magnetic force exerted on the electron at this instant?
Mission 4

Circular paths

  • Only the direction changes. Uniform field, v ⟂ B: a circle.
  • The magnetic force is the centripetal force: magnetic force = mv²/r.
  • r = mv/(Bq)
  • Faster or heavier → bigger circle. Stronger field or more charge → tighter circle.
  • Time for one orbit: T = 2πr/v = 2πm/(Bq).
Mission 4

Worked example

  • A proton (m = 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, q = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C) at 2.0 × 10⁶ m/s, at right angles to a 0.50 T field.
  • Radius: r = mv/(Bq) = (1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ × 2.0 × 10⁶) ÷ (0.50 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹) = 0.042 m.
  • Period: T = 2πm/(Bq) = 1.3 × 10⁻⁷ s.
  • Double the field: r halves to 0.021 m, and T halves too, to 6.6 × 10⁻⁸ s.
Quick check
?A proton moves in a circular path of radius r in a uniform magnetic field, with its velocity perpendicular to the field. A second proton moves in the same field with twice the speed, its velocity also perpendicular to the field. Only magnetic forces are exerted on the protons. The radius of the second proton's circular path is kr. What is k?
Mission 4

Crossed fields

  • In both E and B fields, a charge feels two independent forces: qE (any speed) and qvB (depends on speed).
  • E and B at right angles, both ⟂ to the beam, forces opposite. Straight through only if qE = qvB: v = E/B. This is a velocity selector.
  • Faster particles: magnetic force wins. Slower: electric force wins.
  • Example: plates 1.0 cm apart, 400 V, B = 0.050 T. E = 4.0 × 10⁴ V/m; v = E/B = 8.0 × 10⁵ m/s.
Quick check
?A proton moves at 2.0 × 10⁴ m/s toward the right side of the page through a region that contains a uniform electric field of magnitude 3.0 × 10³ N/C directed toward the bottom of the page and a uniform magnetic field of magnitude 0.050 T directed into the page. The gravitational force on the proton is negligible. What is the magnitude of the net force exerted on the proton at this instant? (Use e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.)
Mission 4

The Hall effect

  • Current flows along a thin slab in a field B ⟂ to its face. The moving charge carriers are pushed to one edge.
  • Charge builds up on opposite edges: a transverse potential difference, the Hall voltage VH.
  • It stops growing when the electric force balances the magnetic force: qE = qvB.
  • With width d across which VH is measured: VH = Bvd. A constant current fixes the carriers' drift speed v, so VH ∝ B.
Quick check
?The figure shows a metal strip, in the plane of the page, carrying a current I in a uniform magnetic field B. The charge carriers in the metal are electrons. As the magnetic field pushes on the moving electrons, charge builds up on two opposite surfaces of the strip. Which surface becomes negatively charged?
Mission 4

Any angle: v × B

  • F = q(v × B), magnitude |q|vB sin θ, ⟂ to the plane of v and B.
  • Split v into v∥ (along B) and v⊥.
  • v∥ feels no force: it stays constant. v⊥ gives a circle of radius mv⊥/(|q|B).
  • Together: a helix along the field. Period 2πm/(|q|B); pitch = v∥ × period.
Quick check
?A particle of mass m and charge q > 0 enters a region of uniform magnetic field of magnitude B with speed v. Its velocity has a component v∥ parallel to the field and a component v⊥ perpendicular to the field, and it moves along a helix. What distance does the particle move along the field direction during one revolution?
Mission check

?Two protons enter two separate regions at the same speed. Region 1 contains only a uniform electric field; region 2 contains only a uniform magnetic field. Each proton enters at right angles to the field lines. Gravity is negligible. A short time later, which statement is correct?

The magnetic force is always at right angles to the velocity. It changes direction, never speed, so a magnetic field alone can bend a beam but never accelerate it to a higher energy. That is why particle accelerators use electric fields to speed particles up; magnetic fields only steer them.

Mission 5Topics 12.1–12.4

Mission 5: Two parallel wires carry currents in the same direction. Do they attract or repel, and why?

Make a guess: Two parallel wires carry currents in the same direction. Do they attract or repel, and why?

Mission 5

Field of a long straight wire

  • B = μ0I/(2πr), r = distance from the wire's axis.
  • μ0 = 4π × 10⁻⁷ T·m/A: the permeability of free space, a constant.
  • B ∝ I and B ∝ 1/distance: twice as far, half the field.
  • Direction: right-hand rule. The field is tangent to circles around the wire.
Mission 5

Worked example

  • A long wire carries 15 A. Find B 3.0 cm from it.
  • B = μ0I/(2π × 0.030) = (4π × 10⁻⁷ × 15) ÷ (2π × 0.030).
  • Shortcut: μ0/2π = 2 × 10⁻⁷ T·m/A.
  • B = 2 × 10⁻⁷ × 15 ÷ 0.030 = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ T.
  • About twice Earth's field at the surface (roughly 5 × 10⁻⁵ T): enough to swing a compass.
Quick check
?A long straight wire of diameter 0.010 m carries a current of 12 A. Point P is 0.045 m from the surface of the wire. What is the magnitude of the magnetic field produced by the wire at P? (Use μ0 = 4π × 10⁻⁷ T·m/A.)
Mission 5

Adding fields

  • Fields from several wires add as vectors (superposition).
  • Find each field's size and direction separately, then add.
  • Two wires with currents in the same direction: between them the fields are opposite, and can cancel.
  • Currents in opposite directions: between them the fields add.
  • Loop center: B = μ0I/(2R) (derived later with the Biot–Savart law).
Quick check
?Two long straight parallel wires, 1 and 2, are a distance d apart. Wire 1 carries a current I, and wire 2 carries a current 3I in the same direction. Point P is on the line joining the wires, between them, at the location where the net magnetic field produced by the two wires is zero. Ignore Earth's magnetic field. Which expression gives the distance from wire 1 to P?
Quick check
?A current is moving charge, so one moving proton makes a magnetic field too. Predict before the next screen. The figure shows a proton moving along the dashed line with velocity v, and three points P, Q and R, at the instant shown. BP, BQ and BR are the magnitudes of the magnetic field produced by the proton at these points at this instant. Which ranking is correct?
Mission 5

Field of one moving charge

  • A single moving charge produces a magnetic field. A charge at rest does not.
  • Larger speed → larger field. Greater distance → smaller field.
  • Direction: ⟂ to both the velocity and the line from the charge to the point. Right-hand rule as for a current (reverse it for a negative charge).
  • Largest beside the charge (line ⟂ to v); zero straight ahead or behind.
Mission 5

Field inside a long solenoid

  • B = μ0nI, with n = number of turns per meter (N/L).
  • Uniform inside, away from the ends. It does not depend on the solenoid's radius.
  • Unless stated otherwise: solenoids are very long and the field outside is negligible.
  • Example: 500 turns on 0.25 m, so n = 2000 turns per meter; I = 1.5 A. B = 4π × 10⁻⁷ × 2000 × 1.5 = 3.8 × 10⁻³ T.
Quick check
?A long solenoid with N turns and length L has a field of magnitude B0 inside it when the current is I. A second long solenoid has the same number of turns N spread over a length 2L and twice the radius, and it carries the same current I. What is the magnitude of the field inside the second solenoid?
Mission 5

Force between parallel wires

  • Wire 1 makes a field at wire 2: B1 = μ0I1/(2πr), ⟂ to wire 2.
  • Wire 2 carries I2 in that field, so it feels F = I2ℓB1.
  • Same direction → attract. Opposite directions → repel.
Mission 5

Worked example

  • Two long parallel wires 5.0 cm apart each carry 10 A in the same direction.
  • Field of wire 1 at wire 2: B = 2 × 10⁻⁷ × 10 ÷ 0.050 = 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ T. Force per meter: BI = 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ × 10 = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ N.
  • On 2.0 m of wire: 8.0 × 10⁻⁴ N, toward the other wire.
Quick check
?Two long straight parallel wires are 0.040 m apart. Wire 1 carries a current of 12 A, and wire 2 carries a current of 8.0 A in the opposite direction. What is the magnitude of the magnetic force exerted by wire 1 on a 1.5 m length of wire 2? (Use μ0 = 4π × 10⁻⁷ T·m/A.)
Quick check
?Two long straight parallel wires, X and Y, are 5.0 cm apart in air. X carries 2.0 A and Y carries 6.0 A, in the same direction. What is the magnetic force on a 1.0 m length of each wire?
Mission check

?A light, loosely wound coil of wire, like a soft spring, hangs vertically. A large steady current is passed through it. What happens to the length of the coil?

Parallel currents in the same direction attract: each wire sits in the other's field, and the force on it points toward the other wire. Opposite currents repel.

Mission 6Topics 12.3, 12.4

Mission 6: Why is the field at a wire loop's center about three times that at the same distance from a straight wire?

Make a guess: Why is the field at a wire loop's center about three times that at the same distance from a straight wire?

Mission 6

The Biot–Savart law

  • Split the current into small elements I dℓ⃗. Each makes a small field:
  • dB = (μ0/4π) · I dℓ⃗ × r̂ / r²
  • r = distance from the element to the point; r̂ = unit vector from the element to the point.
  • Size: dB = (μ0/4π) I dℓ sin θ / r², θ = angle between dℓ⃗ and r̂.
  • Inverse square, like Coulomb's law, but the direction comes from a cross product.
  • Total field: B = ∫ dB over the whole wire.
Quick check
?A straight piece of wire 1.0 mm long carries a current of 10 A. Point P is 5.0 cm from the piece of wire, and the line from the wire to P makes an angle of 30° with the direction of the current. Treating the piece of wire as a single current element, what is the magnitude of the magnetic field it produces at P? Use μ0 = 4π × 10⁻⁷ T·m/A.
Quick check
?Predict first. A student claims that the magnetic field produced by a short straight segment of current-carrying wire has no component parallel to the segment at any point. Which reasoning correctly supports the claim?
Mission 6

Direction and a recipe

  • dB ⟂ dℓ⃗ and ⟂ r̂: around circles centered on the line of the element. No component along it, none toward it.
  • Points on the line of an element: sin θ = 0, no field from it.
  • Recipe:
  • 1. Draw a general element; mark r, r̂ and θ.
  • 2. Find the direction of dB. Use symmetry: often all dB point the same way, or sideways parts cancel.
  • 3. Write dB in one variable (an angle or a length), then integrate.
Mission 6

Center of a loop, and arcs

  • At the center of a circle of radius R: every element has r = R and dℓ⃗ ⟂ r̂ (sin θ = 1).
  • Every dB points the same way, along the axis.
  • B = (μ0I/4πR²) ∮dℓ = (μ0I/4πR²)(2πR) = μ0I/(2R).
  • Arc subtending angle φ (radians): ∫dℓ = Rφ, so B = μ0Iφ/(4πR).
  • Straight leads pointing at the center: dℓ⃗ ∥ r̂, no contribution.
  • Combine pieces by superposition, with signs from their directions.
Quick check
?The diagram shows a closed loop made of two semicircular arcs with a common center P, joined by two straight segments that lie along a line through P. The loop carries a steady current. What is the magnitude of the magnetic field at P? Use μ0 = 4π × 10⁻⁷ T·m/A.
Mission 6

On the axis of a loop

  • Point P on the axis, distance z from the center. Every element is a distance r = √(R² + z²) away, with dℓ⃗ ⟂ r̂.
  • Each dB is tilted. The sideways parts cancel in pairs (opposite elements).
  • The axial parts add: dBz = dB · (R/r).
  • B = μ0IR² / [2(R² + z²)3/2]
  • Checks: z = 0 gives μ0I/(2R). Far away (z ≫ R): B ∝ 1/z³, the field of a dipole.
Mission 6

A straight segment

  • Segment from x = −a to x = +a; P at distance d on its perpendicular bisector.
  • Element dx: sin θ = d/r, r² = x² + d². All dB point the same way.
  • B = (μ0I/4π) ∫ d dx/(x² + d²)3/2 from −a to a.
  • B = μ0Ia / [2πd √(a² + d²)]
  • Very long segment (a ≫ d): B → μ0I/(2πd), the long-wire result.
Quick check
?A straight wire segment 0.30 m long carries a current of 4.0 A. Point P lies on the perpendicular bisector of the segment, 0.20 m from its midpoint. What is the magnitude of the magnetic field at P due to the segment? Use μ0 = 4π × 10⁻⁷ T·m/A.
Mission 6

Worked example: a square loop

  • Square of side s = 0.10 m carrying I = 5.0 A. Find B at the center.
  • Each side: a = s/2, d = s/2. Bside = μ0I(s/2) / [2π(s/2)(s/√2)] = √2 μ0I/(2πs).
  • All four sides give fields in the same direction (⟂ to the loop).
  • B = 4 × Bside = 2√2 μ0I/(πs) = 2√2 × 4π × 10⁻⁷ × 5.0 ÷ (π × 0.10) = 5.7 × 10⁻⁵ T.
  • A circle of the same perimeter gives 4.9 × 10⁻⁵ T: the square's corners sit farther out but its side midpoints sit closer.
Mission check

?A closed loop of wire is made of a circular arc of radius 0.10 m that subtends an angle of 240° at its center P, and the straight wire that joins the two ends of the arc. The loop carries a steady current of 5.0 A. Use μ0 = 4π × 10⁻⁷ T·m/A. What is the magnitude of the magnetic field at P?

At a loop's center every element is at distance R and at right angles to the line to the center, so all of them contribute fully and in the same direction: μ0I/(2R). A straight wire's distant parts contribute less and less, giving μ0I/(2πR). The ratio is π.

Mission 7Topics 12.3, 12.4

Mission 7: Why does the field inside a long solenoid not depend on the solenoid's radius at all?

Make a guess: Why does the field inside a long solenoid not depend on the solenoid's radius at all?

Mission 7

Ampère's law

  • Choose any closed path (an Amperian loop). Then:
  • ∮B·dℓ⃗ = μ0Ienc
  • Ienc = net current through the loop. Sign: curl your right-hand fingers along the direction you go around; current along your thumb counts positive.
  • Currents outside the loop add nothing to the integral, though they do change B at points on the loop.
  • True for any loop. Useful only when symmetry makes B constant along parts of the loop and parallel (or ⟂) to them.
Quick check
?The figure shows three long, straight wires, A, B and C, perpendicular to the page, with the current in each labeled; ⊙ means out of the page and ⊗ into the page. The dashed curve is an Amperian loop in the plane of the page, traversed counterclockwise as the arrow shows. What is the value of ∮B·dℓ⃗ around the loop, in the direction of traversal? Use μ0 = 4π × 10⁻⁷ T·m/A.
Mission 7

Inside and outside a wire

  • Long straight wire, radius R. By symmetry B is tangent to circles around the axis and has the same size all around one.
  • Circle of radius r: ∮B·dℓ⃗ = B(2πr).
  • Outside (r ≥ R): Ienc = I, so B = μ0I/(2πr).
  • Inside, uniform current density: Ienc = I r²/R², so B = μ0Ir/(2πR²): rises linearly from zero on the axis.
  • Example: R = 2.5 mm, I = 10 A, r = 1.0 mm. Ienc = 10 × (1.0/2.5)² = 1.6 A; B = μ0 × 1.6 ÷ (2π × 1.0 × 10⁻³) = 3.2 × 10⁻⁴ T.
Quick check
?A long, straight, solid cylindrical wire of radius 2.0 mm carries a steady current of 8.0 A spread uniformly over its cross section. What is the magnitude of the magnetic field inside the wire, 1.0 mm from its axis? Use μ0 = 4π × 10⁻⁷ T·m/A.
Mission 7

Current density that varies

  • If J depends on the distance s from the axis, add up thin rings: area dA = 2πs ds.
  • Ienc = ∫0ʳ J(s) 2πs ds
  • Example: J = J0(1 − s/R), largest on the axis.
  • Ienc = 2πJ0(r²/2 − r³/(3R)).
  • B = μ0Ienc/(2πr) = μ0J0 r (1/2 − r/(3R)) for r < R. Total current: πJ0R²/3.
Quick check
?A very long, solid cylindrical conductor of radius R carries a steady current along its length. The current density is J = J0s/R, where s is the distance from the axis and J0 is a constant. What is the magnitude of the magnetic field at a distance r < R from the axis?
Mission 7

The long solenoid

  • Assume a long solenoid: uniform B inside, along the axis; negligible B outside.
  • Rectangle with one long side (length ℓ) inside, parallel to the axis, the other outside.
  • Inside side: Bℓ. Outside side: 0. Short sides: B ⟂ dℓ⃗ inside, B = 0 outside. So ∮B·dℓ⃗ = Bℓ.
  • Turns enclosed: nℓ, each carrying I. Bℓ = μ0nℓI → B = μ0nI.
  • The inside side can be anywhere inside: B is the same everywhere inside, and the radius never appears.
Quick check
?The figure shows a cross section of a long solenoid carrying a steady current, with its data labeled, and a rectangular Amperian loop, one long side of which is inside the solenoid. The windings are drawn schematically, not to scale. What is the magnitude of the magnetic field inside the solenoid? Use μ0 = 4π × 10⁻⁷ T·m/A.
Mission 7

Superposition: cables and holes

  • Ampère's law gives each symmetric piece's field; add the pieces as vectors.
  • Coaxial cable: inner conductor +I, outer tube −I. Outside: Ienc = 0, so B = 0. Between them: μ0I/(2πr).
  • Cylinder with an off-center hole: treat it as a full cylinder carrying +J plus a cylinder filling the hole carrying −J. Add the two fields.
  • Parallel wires: add each wire's μ0I/(2πr), each tangent to its own circle.
Quick check
?A long, straight wire carrying current 2I lies along the axis of a long, thin-walled conducting cylindrical shell of radius R. The shell carries current 5I, spread uniformly around it, in the opposite direction. What is the magnitude of the magnetic field at a distance r > R from the axis?
Mission 7

A current-carrying slab

  • Very large flat slab, thickness d, uniform current density J along it.
  • Symmetry: B is parallel to the faces and ⟂ to J, has the same size at equal distances from the central plane, and points in opposite directions on the two sides.
  • Rectangle straddling the slab, long sides ℓ parallel to it on either side: only the long sides count, so ∮B·dℓ⃗ = 2Bℓ.
  • Outside: Ienc = Jdℓ, so 2Bℓ = μ0Jdℓ and B = μ0Jd/2, the same at any distance from the slab.
  • Inside: the same loop, but it encloses only the current within the loop.

Mission check

Ampère's law needs only the current enclosed and the symmetry. For a long solenoid the loop encloses nℓ turns along a length ℓ, so B = μ0nI: the radius never enters, just as R drops out for the tube.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Magnetic fields come from moving charges and magnets; field lines are closed loops, closer lines mean a stronger field.
  • Straight wire: circles, direction by the right-hand rule. Solenoid: uniform inside, bar-magnet pattern outside.
  • A compass aligns with the net field; Earth's field is roughly a dipole; there are no magnetic monopoles.

Mission 2

  • Magnetism comes from moving charges; a magnetized material has aligned atomic dipoles (domains in ferromagnets). Poles always come in pairs.
  • Ferromagnetic: strong, can stay aligned. Paramagnetic: weak, along the field, not kept. Diamagnetic: weak, opposite the field, in all materials. Permeability μ0 is constant; a material's μ is not.

Mission 3

  • Force on a current in a field: at right angles to both; zero when parallel.
  • Direction: right-hand rule.
  • FB = IℓB sin θ.

Mission 4

  • F = q(v × B).
  • The force is ⟂ to v, so speed and kinetic energy never change.
  • v ⟂ B gives a circle: r = mv/(Bq) = p/(Bq); the period does not depend on speed.
  • Crossed fields pass undeflected only particles with v = E/B.

Mission 5

  • Long straight wire: B = μ0I/(2πr); μ0 = 4π × 10⁻⁷ T·m/A.
  • Long solenoid: B = μ0nI, uniform inside, independent of radius.
  • Find B from one wire, then F = IℓB on the other.
  • Parallel wires: same direction attract, opposite repel; equal and opposite forces.

Mission 6

  • Biot–Savart: dB = (μ0/4π) I dℓ⃗ × r̂/r²; integrate, using symmetry to fix the direction.
  • Loop center μ0I/(2R); arc μ0Iφ/(4πR); loop axis μ0IR²/[2(R² + z²)3/2]; finite segment μ0Ia/[2πd√(a² + d²)].

Mission 7

  • Ampère's law: ∮B·dℓ⃗ = μ0Ienc; choose loops where B is constant and parallel, or perpendicular.
  • Wire: μ0I/(2πr) outside, μ0Ir/(2πR²) inside (uniform J); solenoid μ0nI; Ienc = ∫J dA for non-uniform J.
  • Large slab: B = μ0Jd/2 outside, independent of distance.
