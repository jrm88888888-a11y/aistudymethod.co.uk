Mission 1

Mission 1: Why does barium fizz in cold water when magnesium, in the same group, barely reacts?

Make a guess: Why does barium fizz in cold water when magnesium, in the same group, barely reacts?

Mission 1

Group II atoms

  • CCEA calls this group Group II.
  • Outer configuration ns²: Mg [Ne] 3s², Ca [Ar] 4s², Sr [Kr] 5s², Ba [Xe] 6s².
  • The outer electrons are in an s sub-shell: Group II is in the s-block.
  • The metals react by losing both s electrons: M → M²⁺ + 2e⁻.
  • Losing electrons is oxidation: oxidation state 0 → +2. The metals are reducing agents.
Mission 1

Trends down the group

  • Atomic radius increases: one extra shell per period.
  • First ionisation energy decreases: the outer electrons are further from the nucleus and more shielded. This outweighs the rising nuclear charge.
  • Second ionisation energy: M⁺(g) → M²⁺(g) + e⁻. It also decreases down the group, for the same reasons.
Mission 1

The metals react

  • Water: Ca, Sr, Ba fizz, faster down the group: M(s) + 2H2O(l) → M(OH)2(aq) + H2(g).
  • Mg with cold water: very slow. Mg(s) + 2H2O(l) → Mg(OH)2(s) + H2(g).
  • Mg with steam: burns with a bright white light. Mg(s) + H2O(g) → MgO(s) + H2(g).
  • Oxygen: 2M(s) + O2(g) → 2MO(s), white solids. Mg burns with a brilliant white flame.
  • Dilute acids: M(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MCl2(aq) + H2(g), faster down the group.
Mission 1

Why reactivity increases down the group

  • To react, each atom must lose two electrons: energy needed ≈ first IE + second IE.
  • This total falls down the group: Mg 2189 → Ca 1735 → Sr 1614 → Ba 1468 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Ba's outer electrons are in shell 6: far from the nucleus and heavily shielded. Mg's are in shell 3.
  • So barium loses its electrons far more easily: it fizzes, while Mg barely reacts with cold water.

Mission 1

Oxides and hydroxides are bases

  • Oxides with water: MO(s) + H2O(l) → M(OH)2. CaO(s) + H2O(l) → Ca(OH)2(aq).
  • Approximate pH: Mg(OH)2 ≈ 10 · Ca(OH)2 ≈ 12 · Sr(OH)2, Ba(OH)2 ≈ 13.
  • Alkalinity increases down the group: the hydroxides get more soluble, so more OH⁻ is released.
  • With dilute acids: MgO(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2O(l).
  • Ca(OH)2(aq) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + 2H2O(l).
Quick check
?Equal amounts of magnesium oxide and barium oxide are each shaken with water containing universal indicator. Which results are expected?
Mission 1

Solubility of hydroxides and sulfates

  • Hydroxides: solubility increases down the group. Mg(OH)2 is sparingly soluble.
  • Sulfates: solubility decreases down the group. MgSO4 is soluble; BaSO4 is insoluble.
  • Mg²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Mg(OH)2(s): white precipitate.
  • Ba²⁺(aq) + SO4²⁻(aq) → BaSO4(s): white precipitate.
  • Solubility curve: warm a known mass of solid in a known mass of water until it dissolves; cool and note the temperature at which crystals first appear. Repeat with more water; plot solubility (g per 100 g water) against temperature as a smooth curve.
Quick check
?Dilute sodium hydroxide is added to separate solutions of magnesium chloride and barium chloride. Dilute sulfuric acid is then added to fresh samples of each. Which observations are expected?
Mission 1

Uses of Group II compounds

  • MgO in indigestion remedies: MgO + 2HCl → MgCl2 + H2O.
  • CaCO3 in toothpaste: a mild abrasive.
  • Limestone → quicklime: CaCO3 → CaO + CO2 (strong heating).
  • Quicklime → slaked lime: CaO + H2O → Ca(OH)2.
  • Cement: limestone heated with clay. Concrete: cement, sand, gravel and water.
Mission 1

Thermal stability of carbonates and hydroxides

  • Carbonates: MCO3 → MO + CO2. Hydroxides: M(OH)2 → MO + H2O.
  • Stability increases down the group: higher temperatures are needed.
  • Cause: the cation's charge and size. All are 2+, but Mg²⁺ is smallest → highest charge density.
  • A small cation polarises the large CO3²⁻ or OH⁻ ion, distorting its electron cloud and weakening its bonds.
  • Larger Ba²⁺ polarises least, so BaCO3 is the most stable.
Mission 1

Worked example: gas volume from a metal

  • 0.180 g of Mg reacts with excess dilute HCl. Volume of H2 at 293 K and 1 atm?
  • Mg + 2HCl → MgCl2 + H2: 1 mol Mg gives 1 mol H2.
  • n(Mg) = 0.180 ÷ 24.3 = 7.41 × 10⁻³ mol = n(H2).
  • V = 7.41 × 10⁻³ × 24 dm³ mol⁻¹ = 0.178 dm³ = 178 cm³.
  • Same method for masses: mass of MgO formed = n(Mg) × M(MgO).
Quick check

Mission check

?Strontium reacts more vigorously than calcium with dilute hydrochloric acid. The sum of the first and second ionisation energies is 1735 kJ mol⁻¹ for calcium and 1614 kJ mol⁻¹ for strontium. Which explains the difference?

Same reason barium fizzes and magnesium barely reacts: down Group II the outer electrons are lost more easily.

Mission 2

Oxides and hydroxides: salt + water

  • In neutralisation, a base accepts the H⁺ from an acid.
  • Acid + metal hydroxide → salt + water: KOH + HNO3 → KNO3 + H2O.
  • Acid + metal oxide → salt + water: MgO + 2HCl → MgCl2 + H2O.
  • Ionic: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H2O(l); for a solid oxide, MgO(s) + 2H⁺(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + H2O(l).
  • A salt forms when the acid's H is replaced by a metal ion or another cation, such as NH4⁺.
Mission 2

Carbonates and hydrogencarbonates: CO2 too

  • Acid + carbonate or hydrogencarbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide.
  • CO3²⁻ accepts two H⁺; HCO3⁻ accepts one.
  • K2CO3 + 2HNO3 → 2KNO3 + H2O + CO2.
  • Ionic: CO3²⁻ + 2H⁺ → H2O + CO2. HCO3⁻ + H⁺ → H2O + CO2.
  • Observation: fizzing as CO2 escapes; a solid carbonate disappears.
Mission 2

Ammonia, amines; and metals

  • Ammonia or an amine + acid → a salt only; no water forms.
  • NH3 + HNO3 → NH4NO3. CH3CH2NH2 + HCl → CH3CH2NH3Cl.
  • The N lone pair takes the H⁺; there is no OH⁻ or O²⁻ to make water.
  • Metal + acid → salt + hydrogen: Mg + 2HCl → MgCl2 + H2. That is redox, not neutralisation.
  • Ionic: Mg(s) + 2H⁺(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + H2(g).
Quick check
?Which reaction produces a salt and water, with no other product?
Mission 2

Group II compounds as bases

  • Group II oxides are basic. With water: CaO + H2O → Ca(OH)2 (an alkaline solution).
  • With dilute acid: CaO + 2HCl → CaCl2 + H2O.
  • MgO is used in indigestion remedies. CaCO3 is in toothpaste as a mild abrasive.
Mission 2

Acids, bases and the body

  • Indigestion remedies neutralise excess stomach acid (HCl) with hydroxides or carbonates.
  • Mg(OH)2 + 2HCl → MgCl2 + 2H2O. CaCO3 + 2HCl → CaCl2 + H2O + CO2.
  • Acids and alkalis on the skin, and wart treatments, come in the lesson on chemistry in medicine.
Mission 2

The parent acid and base of a salt

  • A salt's anion comes from its parent acid; its cation from its parent base.
  • KNO3: parent acid HNO3, parent base KOH.
  • (NH4)2SO4: parent acid H2SO4, parent base NH3.
  • HCOOK (potassium methanoate): parent acid HCOOH, parent base KOH.
  • Parents are the neutral acid and base, not the ions in the salt.
Quick check

Mission 2

The pH curve: strong acid, strong base

  • A preview: pH curves get a full mission later.
  • Flask: 25.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ HCl. Burette: 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH.
  • Starts at the flask's pH, 1.00, and rises slowly.
  • Close to 25.0 cm³, pH jumps from about 3 to about 11.
  • Equivalence point: amounts match the equation; here pH 7 (at 298 K, 25 °C), mid-jump.
  • After it, excess NaOH builds up: pH rises slowly towards 13 (about 12.5 at double the volume).
Mission check

?Baking powder contains sodium hydrogencarbonate and a solid acid. When the cake batter gets wet, the two react. Why does the cake rise?

Antacid tablets with CaCO3 do the same: CaCO3 + 2HCl → CaCl2 + H2O + CO2. The CO2 gas is what you burp up.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Down Group II: radius up, IE down, so the metals lose their two s electrons more easily and reactivity rises.
  • Hydroxides get more soluble down the group; sulfates get less soluble.
  • Thermal stability increases down the group: smaller cations polarise anions more.

Mission 2

  • Oxides and hydroxides: salt + water. Carbonates: salt + water + CO2. Ammonia and amines: salt only.
  • Metal + acid gives H2, but that is redox.
