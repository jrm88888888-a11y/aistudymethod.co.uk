Mission 1

Mission 1: Christians call the Bible God's word, yet human hands wrote and chose every book. How can both be true?

Make a guess: Christians call the Bible God's word, yet human hands wrote and chose every book. How can both be true?

Mission 1

A library, not one book

  • Biblia (Greek): "the books". The Bible is a library written over many centuries by many authors.
  • Old Testament: built on the Jewish scriptures. New Testament: 27 books about Jesus and the first Christians.
  • Genres: law, history, poetry, prophecy, wisdom, gospel, letters, apocalyptic.
  • Protestant Bibles: 66 books. Catholic Bibles: 73, adding the deuterocanonical books (e.g. Tobit, Sirach, 1–2 Maccabees). Orthodox Bibles include a few more.
  • Most Protestants call those extra books the Apocrypha: useful reading, but not Scripture.
  • The canon (Greek kanon, "measuring rod"): the agreed list of books that count as Scripture.
Mission 1Read the source

Inspired, and written by people

> "Every Scripture is God-breathed and profitable for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for instruction in righteousness," (2 Timothy 3:16, WEBBE)

> "For no prophecy ever came by the will of man, but holy men of God spoke, being moved by the Holy Spirit." (2 Peter 1:21, WEBBE)

> "it seemed good to me also, having traced the course of all things accurately from the first, to write to you in order, most excellent Theophilus;" (Luke 1:3, WEBBE)

  • Inspired (Greek theopneustos, "God-breathed"): God is the source.
  • Yet Luke researched and wrote; Paul's letters show his own style, moods and plans.

Q1 What two sides of the Bible do 2 Peter 1:21 and Luke 1:3 show together?

Show answer

God's Spirit moves the writers; the writers still speak, research and write as people.

Q2 What is Scripture "profitable" for in 2 Timothy 3:16?

Show answer

Teaching, reproof, correction and instruction in righteousness: guidance for belief and life.

Mission 1

Three views of the Bible's nature

  • The literal word of God: God guided every word; the Bible is inerrant (without error) in all it says, including history and science. Used as a direct rulebook. Held by many fundamentalist and some conservative evangelical Christians.
  • Inspired by God, written by humans: God worked through writers' language, culture and limits. Inerrant (or infallible) in matters of faith and salvation; genre and context matter. Held by most Catholic, Orthodox and mainstream Protestant Christians.
  • A human record of experiences of God: written by people of faith, inspired as great writing is inspired; open to reinterpretation. Held by many liberal Christians.
  • Impact on use: the first view reads teachings as timeless commands; the others ask what a passage meant then and how its principle applies now.
  • Your spec stresses the middle view: inspired by God but written by human beings.

Mission 1Evaluate

Is the Bible the inspired word of God?

  • Claim: The Bible can be regarded as the inspired word of God.
  • For: Its own witness (2 Timothy 3:16; 2 Peter 1:21); its unity across centuries centred on Christ; the Church's long recognition; believers' experience of the Spirit speaking through it.
  • Against: Its human features (research, style, varied accounts); tensions between texts; ancient science; the canon was chosen by people over centuries.
  • Replies: The "inspired but human" view explains human features without denying inspiration.
  • Judgement criteria: What does "word of God" mean: dictated words, or witness to Christ? Does inspiration require inerrancy? Can a human selection process be guided by God?
Mission check

Christians agree the Bible is inspired, but differ on what inspiration means, how far the human writers shaped it, and which books it contains. Next: how Christians use it day to day and how they interpret it.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Where is the true Church: wherever the bishops are, or wherever the gospel is truly preached?

Make a guess: Where is the true Church: wherever the bishops are, or wherever the gospel is truly preached?

Mission 2

Two questions about the Church

  • Church translates Greek ekklesia: an assembly of people called out, not first a building.
  • In Matthew's Gospel, Jesus tells Peter: "on this rock I will build my assembly" (Matthew 16:18).
  • Christians disagree on two linked questions:
  • Who has authority? The Bible alone, or the Bible read within the Church's teaching?
  • Where is the true Church? A visible body with bishops, or wherever the gospel is truly heard?
Mission 2

Catholic: Scripture, Tradition, Magisterium

  • Council of Trent (1546): revealed truth comes in written books and in unwritten traditions handed down from the apostles.
  • Vatican II, Dei Verbum (1965), 9–10 (paraphrase): Scripture and Tradition flow from one source and form one sacred deposit of the word of God.
  • The Magisterium (the Pope and bishops in communion with him) interprets that word with authority.
  • But the Magisterium is not above the word: it serves it, teaching only what has been handed on (DV 10).
  • Argument used: the Church came first. It wrote the New Testament and recognised the canon.
  • Matthew 16:18–19: the keys given to Peter ground the bishops' authority to teach.
Mission 2

Protestant: Scripture alone

  • Martin Luther (1483–1546): popes and councils can err (Leipzig Debate, 1519); at Worms (1521) he would retract only if shown wrong by Scripture and plain reason (paraphrase).
  • Sola scriptura: Scripture is the supreme authority that judges every church teaching.
  • "Every Scripture is God-breathed and profitable for teaching" (2 Timothy 3:16).
  • Not "no tradition": most Protestants keep the creeds, because they agree with Scripture.
  • Church of England, Article VI (1571): nothing is required for salvation that cannot be read in or proved from Scripture (paraphrase).
  • Anglican writers after Richard Hooker (1554–1600) speak of Scripture first, then reason and tradition.
  • Orthodox (contrast): the Bible lives inside Holy Tradition, with the councils and the liturgy.
Quick check
?Which statement best describes the Catholic view of authority?
Mission 2Evaluate

"The Church should have more authority than the Bible."

  • For (Catholic): the Church wrote and recognised the New Testament; a living teacher is needed to settle disputed readings; Matthew 16:18–19.
  • For (Orthodox): the Bible is read within Tradition, and only Tradition shows how to read it.
  • Against (Protestant): Scripture is God-breathed (2 Timothy 3:16); popes and councils have contradicted each other; reform needs a standard outside the Church.
  • Against (Catholic, too): Dei Verbum 10 says the Magisterium serves the word, so "more authority" misstates Catholic teaching.
  • Judge it by: Can the Bible interpret itself? Who settles disputes? Does the Church create Scripture's authority or recognise it?
Mission check

?Two Christians disagree about remarriage after divorce. Marta says: "The Church's teaching office tells us how Jesus' words in the Gospels are to be read." Owen says: "Each church teaching must be tested against the Bible, which is the final authority." Which description is correct?

Both sides treat the Bible as God's word. They disagree about who has the final word on what it means.

Mission 3: The Gospels show Jesus walking on the sea and healing a blind man. Do the stories show he was God?

Make a guess: The Gospels show Jesus walking on the sea and healing a blind man. Do the stories show he was God?

Mission 3

Whose authority?

  • Authority: the right to be believed and obeyed.
  • The Gospels say people were amazed that Jesus taught "as having authority, and not as the scribes" (Mark 1:22).
  • Christians ask where that authority comes from. Two broad answers:
  • God's own authority: Jesus is God the Son, so his word is God's word.
  • Human authority: Jesus is a uniquely wise and holy human being, close to God, but not God (see Mission 4).
  • The answer changes how his teaching is followed: as a command of God, or as wise human guidance.
  • The Gospels show Jesus forgiving sins (Mark 2:5–7), walking on the sea (Mark 6:47–52) and healing a man born blind (John 9); Paul says he was declared Son of God by the resurrection (Romans 1:4).
  • Many Christians read these as signs of God's own authority: the scriptures say God alone forgives sins and treads the sea (Mark 2:7; Job 9:8).
Mission 3

"Son of God": what the title meant

  • In the Hebrew Bible, "son of God" could mean Israel, or the king God chose: "You are my son" (Psalm 2:7). A close, chosen relationship, not divinity.
  • Mark opens: "the Good News of Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (Mark 1:1); at the cross a Roman centurion says it (Mark 15:39).
  • Asked "Are you the Christ, the Son of the Blessed?", Jesus answers "I am" (Mark 14:61–62).
  • John 10:30: "I and the Father are one."
  • 1 Corinthians 8:6: one God, the Father, and one Lord, Jesus Christ, "through whom are all things": Jesus shares in God's work of creation.
  • Mainstream Christian teaching (Nicaea 325, Chalcedon 451;): Jesus is the Son, true God and true man, not a son by adoption.
Quick check
?Which belief best expresses the view that Jesus' authority is God's authority?
Mission 3

If his authority is God's: what follows

  • His teaching is binding: not advice to weigh, but God's will to obey.
  • Hard sayings (love your enemies, turn the other cheek;) cannot be set aside as unrealistic. Christians may still debate how to apply them.
  • As a role model, he is perfect: "without sin" (Hebrews 4:15). Some ask whether a sinless, divine model can be followed by ordinary humans.
  • His words carry more weight than any later Church teaching or tradition.
  • The contrast, Jesus' authority as only human, and the Sermon on the Mount.
Mission 3Evaluate

Do the miracles and the resurrection show that Jesus was divine?

  • Claim: The Gospels' miracles and resurrection show that Jesus had God's own authority.
  • For: Christians read acts such as walking on the sea and forgiving sins as things the scriptures reserve for God.
  • For: The resurrection is presented as God's verdict on Jesus (Romans 1:4).
  • Against: Prophets also worked wonders (Elijah and Elisha raise the dead in 1 Kings 17 and 2 Kings 4), so miracles alone show a link to God, not divinity.
  • Against: The texts are written from faith, decades later. Historians cannot easily reach the events behind them.
  • Against: Some Christians accept Jesus' authority on the basis of his life and teaching, not of miracles.
  • Judgement criteria: What would count as evidence of divinity? How far can the texts be read as history? Is authority shown by power, by teaching, or by both?
Mission check

?A modern Christian group reads the Gospel account of Jesus raising Lazarus. Ruth says: "This shows Jesus had God's own authority, so we must obey his hardest teachings exactly." Sam says: "It shows God worked through a holy man, so his teachings are wise guidance we apply with judgement." Which statement is correct?

Miracles can be read as signs of God's own presence or as God working through a prophet; the reading decides how binding Jesus' teaching is. Next: Jesus as teacher of wisdom and liberator.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Jesus said "love your enemies". Is that a command from God, or advice from a wise man?

Make a guess: Jesus said "love your enemies". Is that a command from God, or advice from a wise man?

Mission 4

Teacher of wisdom

  • Jesus is called "Teacher" (Rabbi) throughout the Gospels.
  • Like the Jewish wisdom tradition (Proverbs, Sirach), he teaches how to live well before God, in sayings and parables.
  • His focus: the inner life: motive, desire, the heart, not only outward acts.
  • A question for every reader: is he a teacher of God's wisdom, or only a teacher of wisdom?
Mission 4Read the source

Retaliation and enemies (Matthew 5:38–48)

> "But I tell you, don't resist him who is evil; but whoever strikes you on your right cheek, turn to him the other also." (Matthew 5:39, WEBBE)

> "But I tell you, love your enemies, bless those who curse you, do good to those who hate you, and pray for those who mistreat you and persecute you," (Matthew 5:44, WEBBE)

  • "An eye for an eye" (5:38; Exodus 21:24) limited revenge in law. Jesus asks for no revenge.
  • Love is not only for friends: "Don't even the tax collectors do the same?" (5:46).
  • The model is God, who sends sun and rain "on the just and the unjust" (5:45).
  • "Therefore you shall be perfect, just as your Father in heaven is perfect." (5:48)

Q1 What reason does Jesus give for loving enemies?

Show answer

To be children of the Father, who is good to the just and unjust alike.

Q2 How does verse 39 go beyond "an eye for an eye"?

Show answer

The old rule limited revenge; Jesus forbids revenge itself.

Mission 4

Authority as only human: what follows

  • Some Christians and others see Jesus as a uniquely wise and holy human, not God incarnate: e.g. Unitarians; some liberal Protestants; John Hick (1922–2012) read incarnation as a metaphor (The Metaphor of God Incarnate, 1993).
  • Many non-Christians respect him as a teacher too: Gandhi drew on the Sermon on the Mount for non-violence.
  • Implications for his teaching: its authority rests on its wisdom; it can be weighed by reason and conscience, like other great teaching.
  • As a role model: a fully human Jesus shows what a human being can do; his example is reachable, not superhuman.
  • Criticism: if he is only human, why should his hardest teachings bind anyone? And the Gospels present him as claiming more (see Mission 3).
Mission 4Diversity

Turning the other cheek today

  • Pacifist reading: taken as binding for all of life. Mennonites, Quakers and other historic peace churches refuse to fight. Leo Tolstoy (1828–1910) made it the heart of The Kingdom of God Is Within You (1894).
  • Non-violent resistance: Martin Luther King Jr. (1929–1968) read loving enemies as active resistance without hatred or violence.
  • Personal, not political: Augustine and Luther held that the teaching governs personal revenge, while rulers may still use force justly.
  • An ideal: "be perfect" (5:48) sets a goal of God's love; failure calls for repentance, not despair.
  • The reading often links to authority: if Jesus' words are God's, some argue they cannot be softened; if human, some argue they can be weighed. Both links are disputed.
Mission 4Evaluate

Was Jesus only a teacher of wisdom?

  • Claim: Jesus was only a human teacher of wisdom.
  • For: His teaching (antitheses, parables) fits the Jewish wisdom tradition and can be judged on its own merits.
  • For: Historians can reach his teaching more easily than claims about his divine nature.
  • Against: He teaches with personal authority ("But I tell you"), and claims to forgive sins (see Mission 3).
  • Against: C. S. Lewis (1898–1963), Mere Christianity (1952): a mere man who said what Jesus said would not be a great moral teacher; either he was what he claimed, or he was not good (paraphrase). Critics reply that the claims may come from the Gospel writers.
  • Judgement criteria: Is the Gospel picture reliable? Can teaching carry authority without divinity? Is liberation spiritual, social, or both?
Mission check

?A modern Christian peace activist refuses to take part in any war and says she follows Jesus because his life shows what humans can become. She does not believe Jesus was God incarnate. Which description fits her position?

How Christians apply "love your enemies" can follow from their view of Jesus' authority, but the link is not automatic: people on both sides of that question are pacifists.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The canon is the agreed list of books: Catholic Bibles have 73, Protestant Bibles 66.
  • Views of the Bible range from the literal, inerrant word of God, through "inspired by God but written by humans", to a human record of experiences of God.

Mission 2

  • Catholics: Scripture and Tradition form one deposit, interpreted by the Magisterium; Protestants: Scripture alone is the final authority; Orthodox: the Bible within Holy Tradition.

Mission 3

  • Christians debate whether Jesus' authority is God's own (he is God the Son) or human (a uniquely holy man close to God); the answer shapes how his teaching is followed.

Mission 4

  • Jesus teaches love even of enemies, without revenge (Matthew 5:38–48).
  • If Jesus' authority is only human, his teaching is wise guidance and his life a reachable model; if it is God's, the teaching is command. Christians apply Matthew 5:38–48 in pacifist, non-violent and personal-only ways.
