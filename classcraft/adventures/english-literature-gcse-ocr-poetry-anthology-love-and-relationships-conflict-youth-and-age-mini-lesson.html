Mission 1

A children's poem about a rose-tree. Where is the conflict?

Make a guess: A children's poem about a rose-tree. Where is the conflict?

Conflict · "Envy"Screen 1

A lesson in verse

  • Mary Lamb (1764–1847) wrote children's books with her brother Charles, including Tales from Shakespear (1807).
  • "Envy" comes from their Poetry for Children (1809), published without their names. The 1903 editor E. V. Lucas thought this poem was probably Mary's.
  • It is didactic (moral) verse: a poem written to teach a lesson.
  • Its lesson is about envy: wanting what someone else has.
Extract Conflict · "Envy"Screen 2

"Envy", stanzas 1 and 3

Conflict · "Envy"Screen 3

First reading: a fable in three steps

StanzaWhat happens
1A rose-tree can't grow violets or lilies. If it wished to change, it would "fret" for nothing.
2If it did fret, you'd think it "ne'er had seen its own red rose": it must be blind to its own flower.
3The lesson: envious people are like that "blind and senseless tree". With effort, everyone can find their own talent.
  • The rose-tree stands for a person; the other flowers are other people's gifts.

Conflict · "Envy"Screen 5

Form: a sing-song stanza with a sting

  • Six-line stanzas rhyming aabccb: bear / fair, discontent / bent, mignionet / fret.
  • Lines 3 and 6 are short (three beats against four), so each stanza ends on a clipped point: "It all in vain would fret."
  • The poem is an extended metaphor. Lamb holds back what the tree means until stanza 3: "All envious persons are". Like a fable, the moral comes last.

Mission checkScreen 9

?A children's poem about a rose-tree. Where is the conflict?

The conflict is inside the envious person. The rose-tree that is "discontent" with its "natural bent" can only "fret" in vain, blind to its own red rose. Lamb's moral is that envy is a war with yourself, and the way out is "care and culture" of your own talent.

Mission 2

A boy steals a boat for fun. Why does a mountain haunt him?

Make a guess: A boy steals a boat for fun. Why does a mountain haunt him?

Poetry · The PreludeScreen 1

A boy, a boat, a mountain

  • The Prelude is Wordsworth's long autobiographical poem in blank verse (unrhymed iambic pentameter).
  • He began it in 1798 and revised it for the rest of his life. The final version was published in 1850, three months after his death.
  • In this episode, from Book I, he remembers a childhood summer evening in the Lake District: he takes a boat that isn't his and rows out across a lake.
  • Your anthology's "Boat Stealing" is the same episode from the earlier, two-part Prelude of 1799.
Poetry · The PreludeScreen 2

Three moods in one episode

  • Pleasure and guilt: the boy pushes off proudly, but knows it is theft. Your version also mixes the thrill with guilt from the start.
  • Terror: as he rows, a huge peak seems to rise and stride after him, as if alive. He turns back.
  • Haunting: for days afterwards, strange, huge shapes fill his thoughts and dreams.
  • The turning point is the moment the peak appears: confidence becomes fear.
Poetry · The PreludeScreen 3

Your text: "Boat Stealing" (1799)

  • OCR prints the episode from the 1799 two-part Prelude, Wordsworth's first version of the poem.
  • The events and the three moods are the same as in the later 1850 text, but the wording differs in places.
  • This lesson doesn't quote your version: learn your key quotations from your own anthology.
  • Watch for how the boy describes the boat, the peak and his rowing: the methods on these screens are the ones to find.

Mission checkScreen 7

?A boy steals a boat for fun. Why does a mountain haunt him?

The theft begins as a thrill, but when the peak seems to rise and follow him, his pride turns to fear. Nature appears to judge his guilty act, and for days afterwards he senses a strange power in the world, bigger than himself. Many readers see this as a lesson in awe and conscience; others focus on the boy's own guilty imagination.

Mission 3

An army dies in its sleep. Why does Byron make the poem gallop?

Make a guess: An army dies in its sleep. Why does Byron make the poem gallop?

Conflict poetry · "The Destruction of Sennacherib"Screen 1

The story behind the poem

  • The poem retells a Bible story (2 Kings 18–19). In 701 BC the Assyrian king Sennacherib besieges Jerusalem.
  • Overnight, "the angel of the LORD" strikes the Assyrian camp (2 Kings 19:35, King James Version); the Bible gives 185,000 dead.
  • Byron wrote it in February 1815 for Hebrew Melodies, a book of songs on Old Testament subjects set to music by Isaac Nathan.
  • No battle is fought: the poem's war is between an empire and God.

Conflict poetry · "The Destruction of Sennacherib"Screen 4

The galloping beat

  • Each line has four beats in the pattern da-da-DUM: anapaestic tetrameter.
  • the as-SYR | -ian came DOWN | like the WOLF | on the FOLD
  • It sounds like horses at the charge, and it never stops, even after the army dies.
  • Rhyming couplets (fold/gold, sea/Galilee) push each pair of lines forward.
  • Stanza 2 is built on two matching similes: "summer" leaves, then "autumn" leaves. The repeated line pattern makes the reversal sudden.

Mission checkScreen 9

?An army dies in its sleep. Why does Byron make the poem gallop?

The galloping anapaests carry the Assyrian army in all its pride, and the beat doesn't stop when the army dies in one night without a battle. Byron uses the speed of the rhythm to make the empire's fall sudden and complete, a victory for God rather than for any soldier. Some readers find it unsettling that a song makes mass death sound so exciting: that is worth arguing about.

Mission 4

Winter sunlight, no battle. Why is this a Conflict poem?

Make a guess: Winter sunlight, no battle. Why is this a Conflict poem?

Conflict · "There's a certain Slant of light"Screen 1

Dickinson and the text you're reading

  • Emily Dickinson (1830–1886) wrote almost all her poems in private in Amherst, Massachusetts. Very few were printed while she lived.
  • This poem was first published in 1890, after her death, by her editors Mabel Loomis Todd and T. W. Higginson, who smoothed her dashes into commas.
  • Your anthology prints Dickinson's own handwritten version, with dashes and capitals, and lines 2, 3 and 9 are worded differently. This lesson uses the 1890 text, so learn your quotations from the anthology.
Extract Conflict · "There's a certain Slant of light"Screen 2

"There's a certain slant of light", stanzas 1–3 (1890 text)

Conflict · "There's a certain Slant of light"Screen 3

First reading: what the light does

StanzaWhat happens
1A low winter light "oppresses" the speaker, heavy as church music.
2It hurts, but leaves no visible mark: the change is inside, in how things mean.
3No one can "teach" or change it. It is despair, sent from the sky like a ruler's decree.
4"When it comes, the landscape listens, / Shadows hold their breath"; when it goes, it leaves a look like death.
  • The speaker says "us" and "we": this is a shared human experience, not one person's diary.

Conflict · "There's a certain Slant of light"Screen 5

Despair as a ruler

  • Language of power: the light "oppresses"; despair is a "seal" (a royal or legal stamp that can't be undone) and an "imperial affliction", sent down like an emperor's order.
  • So the conflict is a mind under attack: "None may teach it".
  • Synaesthesia: light (sight) is felt as a heavy weight (touch) and heard as "tunes" (sound).
  • Form: four quatrains of short lines. Lines 2 and 4 rhyme or half-rhyme (scar / are, breath / death), so the poem closes on "death".

Mission checkScreen 9

?Winter sunlight, no battle. Why is this a Conflict poem?

The conflict is fought inside the mind. The light "oppresses" and gives a "Heavenly hurt" that leaves "no scar", only "internal difference". Despair arrives like an emperor's decree, an "imperial affliction", and no one can resist it.

Mission 5

A poem against war wants songs "like a battle-cry". Why?

Make a guess: A poem against war wants songs "like a battle-cry". Why?

Conflict · "Songs for the People"Screen 1

A poet with a cause

  • Frances E. W. Harper (1825–1911) was an African American poet, speaker, teacher and novelist. She campaigned against slavery and segregation, and for women's rights.
  • Her fame grew during the American Civil War (1861–65), and her novels dealt with rebuilding the South afterwards.
  • "Songs for the People" appeared in her Poems (1895). It's a poem about what poetry is for.
Extract Conflict · "Songs for the People"Screen 2

"Songs for the People", stanzas 1, 2 and 7

Conflict · "Songs for the People"Screen 3

First reading: who the songs are for

StanzasThe songs are for…
1–2"the people", "old and young": not for war, but for a fuller life
3"the weary", until their tension relaxes
4"little children": "Sweet anthems of love and duty"
5"the poor and aged": songs of heaven's "bright and restful mansions"
6–7the whole world: "Our world, so worn and weary, / Needs music, pure and strong"
  • The words "bright and restful mansions" and stanza 2's promise of a fuller life echo the Bible: "In my Father's house are many mansions" (John 14:2) and "that they might have life" (John 10:10, both King James Version).

Conflict · "Songs for the People"Screen 5

Form: a song about songs

  • Seven quatrains; longer lines alternate with shorter ones, and lines 2 and 4 rhyme (young / sung, strife / life, cease / peace).
  • OCR's teacher guide calls this a ballad stanza, with a varying number of unstressed syllables, close to the hymns of the anti-slavery churches. The longer lines mostly have four strong beats, the shorter three.
  • Anaphora: "Let me make the songs…", "Let me sing…", "I would sing…" repeat like a refrain.
  • The structure widens: from particular people to "Our world", ending on "peace".

Mission checkScreen 9

?A poem against war wants songs "like a battle-cry". Why?

Harper borrows the power of a battle-cry, which rouses whole crowds, and points it at peace: her songs are "Not for the clashing of sabres" but for "more abundant life". The poem moves from that stirring opening to a world where "war and crime shall cease".

Mission 6

Two war poems, a century apart. How do you compare, not list?

Poetry skills · comparing poemsScreen 1

One argument, two poems

  • A comparison is one argument about both poems, not two essays joined by "on the other hand".
  • Compare what the poems say (ideas, attitudes) and how they say it (language, form, structure).
  • Find a link first, then the differences within it.
  • Keep both poems in play in each paragraph, using whereas, similarly, in contrast, both.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Two war poems, a century apart. How do you compare, not list?

Start from a link (here: both armies are destroyed by something other than battle), then let the differences drive the argument: Byron's purposeful, galloping destruction against Owen's slow, pointless waiting. Keep both poems in every paragraph, comparing ideas and methods together.

Recap

Big ideas

  • The rose-tree is an extended metaphor for a person; the moral arrives only in stanza 3: "All envious persons are".
  • Envy is presented as inner conflict: "discontent", "fret", "blind and senseless".
  • For part (a), compare how an unseen poem shows a conflict inside a person, or a conflict with others, against Lamb's quiet, teaching voice.
  • The stolen-boat episode moves through three moods: guilty pleasure, terror as the peak "rises", and a haunting that lasts for days.
  • OCR's "Boat Stealing" (1799) tells the same episode in earlier wording: learn quotations from your anthology.
  • Byron retells 2 Kings 18–19: the Assyrian army besieging Jerusalem (701 BC) dies in one night, struck by "the Angel of Death", not by soldiers. Published in Hebrew Melodies, 1815.
  • Anapaestic tetrameter in rhyming couplets gives a galloping beat that carries both the attack and the ruin; the paired leaf similes in stanza 2 put pride and fall side by side.
  • For the unseen comparison in (a), rhythm and imagery give you clear methods to compare with any poem.
  • A slant of winter light brings despair: a wound with "no scar", felt inside "Where the meanings are".
  • Language of power and oppression ("oppresses", "seal", "imperial affliction") turns a feeling into a conflict the mind can't win; the poem ends on "death".
  • The anthology prints Dickinson's manuscript version with dashes: learn quotations from that text.
  • The poem is a list of people who need songs (the old and young, the weary, children, the poor and aged), widening to the whole world.
  • Harper contrasts war's noise ("clashing of sabres", "jangle and discords") with healing music, and ends with tender hearts that "Girdle the world with peace".
  • Strong for comparing with an unseen poem about protest, hope, or the purpose of art.
  • A comparison is one argument about both poems: find a link, then explore the differences within it.
  • Compare ideas and methods together (voice, metre, rhyme, imagery), using comparative connectives in every paragraph.
  • Your exam pairs a named poem with a second poem (one you choose, or an unseen one): know which, and write about both.

Key quotations to learn

  • "This rose-tree is not made to bear / The violet blue, nor lily fair," (stanza 1, lines 1–2) the metaphor: each of us is made to be ourselves
  • "Or wish'd to change its natural bent," (stanza 1, line 5) envy as a wish to be someone else
  • "It all in vain would fret." (stanza 1, line 6) envy wears you away; clipped short line
  • "Like such a blind and senseless tree" (stanza 3, line 1) the simile that reveals the moral
  • "With care and culture all may find" (stanza 3, line 4) double meaning of "culture"; hope for everyone
  • "Some talent that is rare." (stanza 3, line 6) the cure for envy: your own gift
  • It was an act of stealth / And troubled pleasure (1850, lines 361–362) Pleasure mixed with guilt from the start (AQA, Edexcel text).
  • a huge peak, black and huge, (1850, line 378) The turning point: the threat appears.
  • Upreared its head. (1850, line 380) Personification: the mountain seems alive.
  • Strode after me. (1850, line 385) The peak seems to pursue him.
  • But huge and mighty forms, that do not live (1850, line 398) Nature's strange power stays in his mind.
  • By day, and were a trouble to my dreams. (1850, line 400) The lasting effect: the extract's last line.
  • The Assyrian came down like the wolf on the fold, (Stanza 1, line 1) The simile: a predator falling on a helpless flock.
  • And his cohorts were gleaming in purple and gold; (Stanza 1, line 2) Royal colours: the army's wealth and pride.
  • That host on the morrow lay withered and strown. (Stanza 2, line 4) The reversal in one night: summer leaves become dead leaves.
  • For the Angel of Death spread his wings on the blast, (Stanza 3, line 1) The destroyer is supernatural, not human.
  • The lances unlifted—the trumpet unblown. (Stanza 5, line 4) Negative "un-" words: a silent, motionless army.
  • And the might of the Gentile, unsmote by the sword, (Stanza 6, line 3) No human fought: God's power is the point.
  • "There's a certain slant of light," (stanza 1, line 1) an ordinary sight that triggers despair
  • "Heavenly hurt it gives us;" (stanza 2, line 1) oxymoron: holy and painful at once
  • "We can find no scar, / But internal difference" (stanza 2, lines 2–3) the wound is inside: the poem's conflict
  • "An imperial affliction / Sent us of the air." (stanza 3, lines 3–4) despair as an emperor's decree
  • "When it comes, the landscape listens, / Shadows hold their breath;" (stanza 4, lines 1–2) personification: the world waits in dread
  • "Let me make the songs for the people," (stanza 1, line 1) the poet's mission; anaphora begins
  • "Songs to stir like a battle-cry" (stanza 1, line 3) war's energy borrowed for peace
  • "Not for the clashing of sabres," (stanza 2, line 1) rejecting war; harsh sounds
  • "To hush the jangle and discords / Of sorrow, pain, and wrong." (stanza 6, lines 3–4) injustice as ugly noise music can cure
  • "And the hearts of men grown tender / Girdle the world with peace." (stanza 7, lines 3–4) closing metaphor: peace everywhere
