Mission 1

Mission 1: A sealed flask looks unchanging. Can you tell which way it will react?

Make a guess: A sealed flask looks unchanging. Can you tell which way it will react?

Mission 1

Which way will it react?

  • Beyond your spec, but it answers the video: put the concentrations right now into the Kc expression. That ratio is called Q.
  • Q < K: too little product. Net forward reaction until Q = K.
  • Q > K: too much product. Net backward reaction.
  • Q = K: at equilibrium; no net change.
Mission 1

ICE tables (worked example)

A(g) + B(g) ⇌ 2C(g). Start: [A] = [B] = 0.50, no C. At equilibrium, [C] = 0.60.

Concentration[A][B][C]
Initial0.500.500
Change−0.30−0.30+0.60
Equilibrium0.200.200.60
  • All values in mol dm⁻³.
  • Changes follow the coefficients: 0.60 of C needs 0.30 each of A and B.
  • Kc = [C]² ÷ ([A][B]) = 0.36 ÷ 0.040 = 9.0.
Mission 1

Units of Kc

  • Put units into the expression and cancel.
  • N2O4 ⇌ 2NO2: Kc = [NO2]²/[N2O4] → (mol dm⁻³)² ÷ mol dm⁻³ = mol dm⁻³.
  • N2 + 3H2 ⇌ 2NH3: (mol dm⁻³)² ÷ (mol dm⁻³)⁴ = dm⁶ mol⁻².
  • A + B ⇌ 2C: (mol dm⁻³)² ÷ (mol dm⁻³)² → no units.
  • Shortcut check: power on top minus power below gives the power of mol dm⁻³.
Mission 1

Starting from moles (worked example)

  • PCl5(g) ⇌ PCl3(g) + Cl2(g).
  • 0.500 mol PCl5 is sealed in a 2.00 dm³ flask. At equilibrium it holds 0.200 mol Cl2.
  • Moles at equilibrium: Cl2 0.200, PCl3 0.200, PCl5 0.500 − 0.200 = 0.300.
  • Divide by the volume: [PCl5] = 0.150, [PCl3] = [Cl2] = 0.100.
  • Kc = (0.100 × 0.100) ÷ 0.150 = 0.0667 mol dm⁻³.
  • Two terms on top, one below: the volume does not cancel. Moles alone give 0.133, twice too big.
Quick check
?N2O4(g) ⇌ 2NO2(g). N2O4 is placed in a sealed vessel at an initial concentration of 0.200 mol dm⁻³ and the mixture is allowed to reach equilibrium at constant temperature. At equilibrium, [NO2] = 0.080 mol dm⁻³. What is the value of Kc at this temperature?
Mission 1

Finding equilibrium amounts in the lab

  • CH3COOH(l) + C2H5OH(l) ⇌ CH3COOC2H5(l) + H2O(l): one liquid phase, so all four go in Kc.
  • Mix known amounts with a little HCl(aq) catalyst. Stopper; keep at constant temperature for about a week.
  • Titrate with standard NaOH(aq). Subtract the titre for the HCl alone → moles of ethanoic acid at equilibrium.
  • Example result: start 0.100 mol acid, 0.100 mol ethanol, 0.050 mol water (from the catalyst solution). Equilibrium: 0.040 mol acid.
  • So 0.060 mol reacted: ethanol 0.040, ester 0.060, water 0.110 mol.
  • Kc = (0.060 × 0.110) ÷ (0.040 × 0.040) = 4.1. No units: the volume cancels.
  • Count the water added with the catalyst. Leaving it out gives 2.25: too low.
Quick check
?0.400 mol SO2 and 0.200 mol O2 are sealed in a 2.00 dm³ vessel. At equilibrium the vessel contains 0.300 mol SO3. What is Kc for 2SO2(g) + O2(g) ⇌ 2SO3(g)?
Mission 1

When K is very small

  • Background: not named in your spec, but a neat shortcut.
  • Very little reactant reacts, so [reactant] at equilibrium ≈ [reactant] at the start.
  • Then solve for the product directly. No quadratic needed.
  • Z2(g) ⇌ 2Z(g), Kc = 4.0 × 10⁻⁸ mol dm⁻³, [Z2] at the start = 0.10 mol dm⁻³.
  • [Z]² = K × [Z2] = 4.0 × 10⁻⁸ × 0.10 = 4.0 × 10⁻⁹.
  • So [Z] = 6.3 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³.
  • Check: only 3.2 × 10⁻⁵ of the 0.10 of Z2 reacted. The approximation holds.
Mission 1

Background: K and Gibbs energy

  • The link: ΔG = −RT ln K (ΔG in J mol⁻¹, T in K, R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹).
  • ΔG negative ↔ K > 1: products favoured. ΔG positive ↔ K < 1.
  • K = 1000 at 298 K: ΔG = −8.31 × 298 × ln 1000 = −17.1 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Background only: you won't need this equation.
Mission check

?Back to the sealed flask: H2(g) + I2(g) ⇌ 2HI(g). It was filled with 0.500 mol H2 and 0.500 mol I2 and no HI, in a 2.00 dm³ flask. It has now reached equilibrium and holds 0.800 mol HI. What is Kc at this temperature?

Kc = 64 here. So can you tell which way a flask will react? Yes: put its concentrations into the Kc expression. Bigger than Kc: net backward; smaller: net forward; equal: at equilibrium.

Mission 2

The rate equation

  • Rate equation: rate = k[A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ.
  • Order with respect to a reactant: the power of its concentration. Here m and n.
  • Overall order = m + n.
  • Rate constant, k: the proportionality constant in the rate equation.
  • Orders are found by experiment. They need not match the coefficients in the equation.
  • Each order will be 0, 1 or 2.

  • Factor on the rate = (factor on concentration)^order.
  • Two concentrations changed together: multiply their factors. Never add.
  • The hook: ×2 gives ×4, so second order; ×2 gives ×1, so zero order. Rate = k[A]².

Worked example: orders from initial rates

X + Y → Z, at constant temperature:

  • Compare experiments where only one concentration changes.
  • 1 → 2: [X] × 3, rate × 3. First order in X.
  • 2 → 3: [Y] × 2, rate × 2. First order in Y.
  • Rate = k[X][Y]: second order overall.
Mission 2

Units of k

  • k = rate ÷ (concentration terms). Find its units by cancelling, not memorising.
  • Second order: (mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹) ÷ (mol dm⁻³)² = mol⁻¹ dm³ s⁻¹.
  • Zero: mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹. First: s⁻¹. Third: mol⁻² dm⁶ s⁻¹.
  • k changes with temperature, not with concentration.

Mission 2

Rate–concentration graphs

  • Plot initial rate against concentration of one reactant; keep everything else constant.
  • Zero order: a horizontal line. Rate does not depend on [A].
  • First order: a straight line through the origin. If A is the only reactant in the rate equation, its gradient is k.
  • Second order: an upward curve through the origin. Rate against [A]² is a straight line.
Mission 2

Finding orders in the lab

  • Initial-rate method: several runs; change one starting concentration; keep the others and the temperature the same.
  • Measure each initial rate: a tangent at t = 0, or a clock time (rate ∝ 1/t).
  • PAG10: orders from initial rates, often with a clock reaction.

Mission 3

Elementary steps

  • Most reactions happen in a series of elementary steps: single collisions or break-ups.
  • The sequence of steps is the mechanism.
  • The steps must add up to the overall equation.
  • They must also fit the rate equation found by experiment.
  • Rate-determining step (RDS), also called the rate-limiting step: the slowest step. It limits the rate of the whole reaction.
Mission 3

Intermediates and transition states

  • An intermediate forms in one step and is used up in a later one.
  • It cancels when the steps are added, so it is never in the overall equation.
  • It sits in an energy minimum, so in principle it can be detected.
  • A transition state sits at an energy maximum: bonds partly made, partly broken. It cannot be isolated.
Quick check
?Which statement correctly distinguishes a reaction intermediate from a transition state in a multistep reaction?
Mission 3

Molecularity

  • Molecularity: the number of particles that collide (or break up) in one elementary step.
  • Unimolecular: one. (CH3)3CBr → (CH3)3C⁺ + Br⁻.
  • Bimolecular: two. (CH3)3C⁺ + OH⁻ → (CH3)3COH. Also 2HI → H2 + I2: count particles, not species.
  • Termolecular: three at once. Rare: three particles seldom collide together.
  • It applies to elementary steps, never to an overall equation.
  • Background: "molecularity" is not a term in your specification.
Mission 3

How the mechanism sets the rate equation

  • The rate equation contains species in the RDS, and in any fast step before it.
  • Species that react only after the RDS are zero order.
  • If the RDS is the first step, the orders = the numbers of each particle in it.
  • Example: (CH3)3CBr + OH⁻ → (CH3)3COH + Br⁻ has rate = k[(CH3)3CBr].
  • So the RDS contains one (CH3)3CBr and no OH⁻. OH⁻ reacts in a later, fast step.
Mission 3

When the slow step is not first

  • Overall: P + 2Q → PQ2.
  • Step 1: P + Q ⇌ PQ (fast). Step 2: PQ + Q → PQ2 (slow).
  • The slow step gives rate = k[PQ][Q]. But PQ is an intermediate: replace it.
  • PQ is made from P and Q, so [PQ] is proportional to [P][Q].
  • Rate = k[P][Q]²: P and both Q particles show up.
Quick check
?The overall equation for a reaction is 2X + Y → X2Y. The experimental rate equation is rate = k[X][Y]. Which mechanism is consistent with both?
Mission 3

Energy profile of a multistep reaction

  • One maximum (transition state) per step; one minimum per intermediate.
  • Each step's Ea runs from the species entering that step up to its own maximum.
  • A later step starts from the intermediate's dip, not from the reactants.
  • The step with the largest Ea (usually also the highest peak) is the slow step.
  • Background: multistep profiles go beyond your specification.
Mission 3

Back to bromine

  • CH3COCH3 + Br2 → CH3COCH2Br + HBr (in acid).
  • Rate equation: rate = k[CH3COCH3][H⁺]. Zero order in Br2.
  • So the RDS (and any fast step before it) involves propanone and H⁺ only.
  • Accepted outline: fast protonation; slow change to an enol, CH2=C(OH)CH3; then fast reaction of the enol with Br2.
  • Br2 waits for the slow step to make the enol. More Br2 can't make that step faster.
  • H⁺ is a catalyst: used, then re-formed. It is in the rate equation.
Mission check

?Below about 500 K (225 °C), NO2(g) + CO(g) → NO(g) + CO2(g) has rate = k[NO2]². Doubling [CO] does not change the rate. Which statement explains this?

Same idea as bromine. A reactant that only joins in after the slow step can't speed the reaction up.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Two glasses of a blue sports drink look the same. How could a beam of light tell which holds more dye?

Make a guess: Two glasses of a blue sports drink look the same. How could a beam of light tell which holds more dye?

Mission 4

Why a solution looks coloured

  • White light contains every visible wavelength.
  • A coloured solution absorbs some wavelengths and transmits the rest.
  • You see the transmitted light. A blue solution absorbs mainly orange and red light.
  • More dye in the beam's path: more light absorbed.
Mission 4

Measuring absorbance

  • A colorimeter: lamp → coloured filter → sample in a cuvette → detector.
  • First, zero the instrument with a cuvette of the solvent alone (the blank).
  • The meter reads absorbance, A: how much light the sample absorbs. No units.
  • More light absorbed → less reaches the detector → higher absorbance.
Mission 4

Choosing the wavelength

  • Pick the filter whose light the solution absorbs most: the complementary colour.
  • Blue copper(II) solution: use an orange or red filter, not a blue one.
  • Here, a small change in concentration gives the biggest change in absorbance: maximum sensitivity.
Quick check
?A student uses a colorimeter to find the concentration of a blue solution of copper(II) sulfate. Which filter should the student use, and why?
Mission 4

The calibration graph

  • Make standard solutions of known concentration from one stock solution.
  • Measure each one's absorbance: same filter or wavelength, same cuvette.
  • Plot absorbance (y) against concentration (x). Here it is a straight line through the origin.
  • Measure the unknown the same way and read its concentration off the graph.
  • Example: copper(II) standards 0.020–0.100 mol dm⁻³ give A = 0.12–0.60. An unknown with A = 0.33 reads 0.055 mol dm⁻³.
  • The graph does the work: you don't need to know ε.
Quick check
?A student plots a calibration graph from copper(II) sulfate standards, then measures an unknown copper(II) solution. Which mistake would make the concentration found for the unknown too high?
Mission 4

Following a reaction rate

  • Use colorimetry when one species is coloured and its amount changes during the reaction.
  • Example: CH3COCH3 + I2 → CH3COCH2I + HI, with acid as catalyst. Only iodine is coloured (yellow-brown).
  • Put the mixture in the colorimeter (blue filter) and record absorbance every few seconds.
  • Convert absorbance to [I2] with a calibration graph. Plot [I2] against time; the gradient gives the rate.
  • Why choose it: continuous readings, no samples removed, the reaction is not disturbed.
Quick check
?Which reaction is best followed by colorimetry?
Mission check

?A green solution of nickel(II) ions is taken from a lab waste container. Its absorbance is measured under the same conditions as these nickel(II) standards.
Standards (mol dm⁻³ : A): 0.050 : 0.16 · 0.100 : 0.32 · 0.150 : 0.48 · 0.200 : 0.64. The sample gives A = 0.40. What is its nickel(II) concentration, in mol dm⁻³?

Your eyes can't tell the two glasses apart, but a detector can. The drink with more dye absorbs more orange-red light; read each absorbance off a calibration graph, and you know exactly how much more dye it holds.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Could you measure an activation energy with a cricket?

Make a guess: Could you measure an activation energy with a cricket?

Mission 5

k rises with temperature

  • k is constant only at one temperature. Raise T and k rises.
  • Hotter: a much larger fraction of collisions have E ≥ Ea.
  • Arrhenius equation: k = Ae−Ea/RT. Given in the exam.
  • e−Ea/RT is roughly the fraction of collisions with E ≥ Ea.
  • Ea in J mol⁻¹, R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ (Data Sheet), T in kelvin.
Mission 5

Larger Ea, bigger effect of temperature

  • For the same A, a larger Ea means a smaller fraction with enough energy, so a smaller k.
  • But a large Ea makes k more sensitive to T: a 10 K rise multiplies k by a bigger factor.
  • A catalyst lowers Ea, so k is larger at the same T.
  • A is the pre-exponential factor.
Quick check
?For a reaction with Ea = 50 kJ mol⁻¹, raising the temperature from 300 K to 310 K almost doubles the rate. Which statement gives the main reason for this large increase?
Mission 5

The straight-line form

  • Take natural logs: ln k = −Ea/RT + ln A. Given in the exam.
  • That is y = mx + c, with y = ln k and x = 1/T.
  • Gradient = −Ea/R, so Ea = −gradient × R.
  • y-intercept = ln A.
  • The line slopes down: higher T means smaller 1/T and larger ln k.
Quick check
?The rate constant of a reaction varies with absolute temperature according to the Arrhenius equation, k = Ae−Ea/RT. A student plots ln k (y-axis) against 1/T (x-axis) for one reaction over a range of temperatures. Which describes the graph?
Mission 5

Ea and A from a graph (worked example)

  • Two points on the line (k in s⁻¹): (3.00 × 10⁻³ K⁻¹, −6.00) and (3.40 × 10⁻³ K⁻¹, −9.60).
  • Gradient = (−9.60 − (−6.00)) ÷ (0.40 × 10⁻³) = −9.00 × 10³ K.
  • Ea = 9.00 × 10³ × 8.31 = 7.48 × 10⁴ J mol⁻¹ = 74.8 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Intercept is far off the axes, so use a point: ln A = −6.00 + 9.00 × 10³ × 3.00 × 10⁻³ = 21.0.
  • A = e²¹·⁰ = 1.3 × 10⁹ s⁻¹ (A has the units of k).
Calculate
?An Arrhenius plot has ln k on the y-axis and 1/T / 10⁻³ K⁻¹ on the x-axis. Two points on the line are (3.10, −4.20) and (3.30, −6.70). Calculate Ea, in kJ mol⁻¹. R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.

Mission 5

Two temperatures, no graph

  • Write ln k = −Ea/RT + ln A at T1 and at T2. Subtract: ln A cancels.
  • ln(k2/k1) = (Ea/R)(1/T1 − 1/T2).
  • Use natural logs and kelvin. Ea comes out in J mol⁻¹; convert at the end.
  • Two points give one line, so this is the graph method with no graph.
  • Your exams ask for the graph method. This is background.
Mission 5

What A means

  • A accounts for how often collisions happen with the right orientation.
  • A stricter orientation requirement means a smaller A.
  • A is not the fraction with enough energy: that is e−Ea/RT.
  • Find A from the intercept, or from one point: ln A = ln k + Ea/RT.
  • Background: you find A from a graph but need not explain it.
Quick check
?Reactions 1 and 2 have the same Arrhenius factor, A. Reaction 1 has the larger activation energy. Both are plotted as ln k against 1/T on the same axes. Which statement is correct?
Mission check

?A clock reaction is timed at several temperatures, with the same starting concentrations each time. Rate ∝ 1/t, so ln(1/t) stands in for ln k. On a plot of ln(1/t) against 1/T / 10⁻³ K⁻¹, two points on the line are (3.10, −2.30) and (3.40, −4.25). What is Ea? R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.

Same method for the cricket. Its chirp rate stands in for k: plot ln(chirp rate) against 1/T. The line gives an apparent Ea of roughly 50 kJ mol⁻¹ for the whole chirping process, not for one reaction.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • ICE tables: changes follow the coefficients; divide moles by volume unless the volume cancels.
  • Kc units come from cancelling mol dm⁻³; same total power top and bottom means no units.

Mission 2

  • Rate = k[A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ; orders (0, 1 or 2) come from experiment, not from the equation.
  • Factor on rate = (factor on concentration)^order; units of k by cancelling.

Mission 3

  • The slowest step sets the rate equation. Species that react after it are zero order.
  • Orders show what is in the rate-determining step (or made in fast steps before it).

Mission 4

  • Coloured species absorb light; absorbance rises in step with concentration. A calibration graph turns absorbance into concentration.
  • Colorimetry follows a rate continuously when one species is coloured.

Mission 5

  • k rises with T because a larger fraction of collisions have E ≥ Ea. Larger Ea, more sensitive to T.
  • Plot ln k against 1/T: Ea = −gradient × R; intercept = ln A.
