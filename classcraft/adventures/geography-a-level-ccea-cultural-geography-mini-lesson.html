Mission 1

Mission 1: Nearly half of the world's languages are endangered, yet 4 in 10 Londoners were born abroad. Is the world becoming more alike or more diverse?

Make a guess: Nearly half of the world's languages are endangered, yet 4 in 10 Londoners were born abroad. Is the world becoming more alike or more diverse?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • A2-2.A1.i why cultural groups exist, differences between them, and the expression of cultural nationalism
Mission 1

Culture and cultural groups

  • Culture: the shared, learned way of life of a group: language, religion, food, music, dress, customs.
  • A cultural group shares some of these traits and a sense of belonging.
  • Ethnicity: a group's shared sense of common origin and culture. Identity: how people see themselves; one person can hold several identities.
Mission 1

Why cultural groups exist

  • Isolation: mountains, forests, seas and distance let groups develop separately.
  • Shared history and ancestry: common origins, migrations and memories.
  • Religion and language: passed on through families, schools and places of worship.
  • States and borders: governments promote national languages, holidays and symbols.
  • Example: New Guinea is a hotspot of endangered languages; Bromham et al. note this pattern "may largely reflect diversity". Rugged terrain has kept many small groups apart.
Mission 1

How cultural groups differ

  • Groups differ in language, religion, customs and dress, food, and values about family and community.
  • Boundaries are fuzzy: people mix traits, marry across groups and switch languages.
  • Differences can be a source of identity and pride, or of tension when one group dominates.
Mission 1

Cultural nationalism: Wales

  • Cultural nationalism: action to protect and promote a nation's culture (especially its language and arts), which need not seek independence.
  • Census 2021: about 538,000 people in Wales aged 3 and over could speak Welsh (17.8%), down from 562,000 (19.0%) in 2011.
  • The Welsh Government's strategy, published 10 July 2017, aims for "a million Welsh speakers by 2050".
Quick check
?How many more Welsh speakers than in 2021 would Wales need to reach the 2050 target?
Mission 1

Cultural erosion: languages

  • Of about 7,000 documented languages, "nearly half are considered endangered".
  • In one database of 6,511 spoken languages, 37% were threatened or worse; at least 1,500 could stop being spoken by 2100.
  • Greater road density and more years of schooling were linked to endangerment; contact with other languages in itself was not.
Mission 1

More diverse places

  • In 2021, 40.6% of London's usual residents were born outside the UK (36.7% in 2011); in Brent, 56.1%.
  • Flows run both ways: Netflix's most-watched non-English series is South Korea's Squid Game (season 1), with 265.2 million views in its first 91 days.
Quick check
?Which evidence most challenges the idea that global culture is simply being westernised?
Mission 1

Homogenisation, heterogenisation, hybridisation

  • Homogenisation: places becoming more alike (language loss, global brands).
  • Heterogenisation: places becoming more diverse (migration to London).
  • Hybridisation: traits mixing into something new.
  • The answer depends on scale and on which indicator you choose.
Mission check

?In a fictional city: (1) a global coffee chain adds local pastries to its menu; (2) the share of residents born abroad rises; (3) fewer children speak the local language. Which processes are these, in order?

Both. Globally, between 37% and nearly half of languages are endangered, depending on the definition; locally, 40.6% of Londoners were born abroad. Western brands and films spread, but they adapt, and culture flows from South Korea to the world too.

Mission 2: In Northern Ireland, women in full-time jobs now earn slightly more than men. Has discrimination disappeared?

Specification: what this mission covers

Mission 2

Social exclusion

  • UN definition: a state in which people "are unable to participate fully in economic, social, political and cultural life", and the process that leads to it.
  • Causes include lack of income, jobs, housing, education and health care, and lack of voice or equal respect.
  • Factors named by the UN include sex, race, ethnicity, religion and socioeconomic status.
Mission 2

Discrimination

  • Discrimination: treating a person less favourably because of a characteristic such as religion, sex or race.
  • It is one process that can cause exclusion; poverty and place are others.
  • In NI, Section 75 of the Northern Ireland Act 1998 requires public bodies to "have due regard to the need to promote equality of opportunity" between people of different "religious belief, political opinion, racial group, age, marital status or sexual orientation", between men and women, by disability and by dependants.
Quick check
?Which of CCEA's six dimensions is not named in the Section 75 equality duty?
Mission 2

Gender: read the gap carefully

  • UK full-time gender pay gap, April 2026: 6.8%; all employees 12.3%.
  • Northern Ireland full-time: negative 1.6%, so women's median hourly pay was slightly higher.
  • Scotland (0.9%) and Wales (3.2%) also had smaller full-time gaps than every English region.
  • ONS: regional differences are "largely caused by differences in the composition of the employee workforce".
  • A pay gap compares averages; it is not a direct measure of unequal pay for the same job.
Mission 2

Social class: school outcomes

  • Free school meals (FSM) entitlement is a common indicator of low household income.
  • NI school leavers 2024/25 with 5+ GCSEs A*–C incl. English and maths: 48.7% of FSM-entitled leavers v 77.9% of others.
  • The gap shows that class shapes who gets the qualifications for further study and work.
Mission 2

Dimensions combine

  • Same measure, 2024/25:
  • Protestant boys on FSM: 39.9%.
  • Catholic girls on FSM: 56.8%.
  • Catholic girls not on FSM: 82.1%.
  • Gender, religion and class interact: no single dimension explains the pattern.

Mission 2

Religion and race

  • Religion: Catholic leavers now slightly outperform Protestant leavers on the benchmark (73.2% v 71.7%, 2024/25).
  • Residential division: by 2001 there were about 35 peace walls in NI, 26 in Belfast.
  • Race and ethnicity: 3.4% of NI's population belonged to minority ethnic groups in 2021.
  • Minority ethnic leavers: 65.8% met the benchmark v 72.3% of White leavers.
Mission 2

Hostility as exclusion: hate crime

  • Police in England and Wales recorded 115,990 hate crimes in 2024/25 (excluding the Metropolitan Police).
  • By strand: race 82,490; sexual orientation 18,702; disability 10,224; religion 7,164; transgender 3,809.
  • Home Office caution: recording has improved, so the figures "do not provide reliable trends", and not all hate crime is reported.

Mission 2

Exclusion has a geography

  • In NI's 2017 deprivation measure, 50 of the 100 most deprived small areas were in Belfast.
  • Many listed areas (e.g. Ardoyne, New Lodge, Shankill, Falls) are in north and west Belfast, where most peace walls stand.
  • Exclusion clusters: low income, low qualifications and segregation overlap in the same places.
Mission check

?In region T, the full-time gender pay gap is zero, mainly because many women there hold well-paid public-sector jobs. Which further evidence would best test whether women in T still face pay discrimination?

Northern Ireland's negative full-time pay gap reflects who works where, not proof that discrimination has ended. Exclusion runs through class, religion, race, sexuality and place, and each measure has limits.

Mission 3

Mission 3: A hillside with no buildings in sight looks wild. Is it natural, or did people make it what it is?

Make a guess: A hillside with no buildings in sight looks wild. Is it natural, or did people make it what it is?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • A2-2.A1.iii social constructions of nature and landscape: landscapes as human systems; natural and cultural landscapes
Mission 3

Natural and cultural landscapes

  • Natural landscape: shaped mainly by physical processes. The Giant's Causeway: some 40,000 basalt columns from volcanic activity 50–60 million years ago.
  • Cultural landscape: UNESCO's "combined works of nature and of man".
  • In 1992 the World Heritage Convention became the first international legal instrument to recognise and protect cultural landscapes.
Mission 3

A landscape as a human system

  • English Lake District: farming with local sheep such as the Herdwick, on "common fell-grazing" by "relatively independent farmers".
  • "These traditions have evolved under the influence of the physical constraints of its mountain setting".
  • System: inputs (rain, soil, people's labour and knowledge) → processes (grazing, walling, building) → outputs (walls, farms, open fells).
  • Geographer Carl Sauer (1925): a cultural landscape is fashioned from a natural landscape by a culture group.
Mission 3

UNESCO's three kinds of cultural landscape

  • Designed: "designed and created intentionally by man" (parks, gardens).
  • Organically evolved: relict, where "an evolutionary process came to an end at some time in the past", or continuing, which "retains an active social role in contemporary society".
  • Associative: justified by "religious, artistic or cultural associations of the natural element rather than material cultural evidence".
Quick check
?Terraced hillsides are still farmed today by families whose ancestors cut the terraces. Which UNESCO category fits best?
Mission 3

Ideas change what a landscape is

  • In the Lake District, Picturesque and Romantic interest stimulated efforts "to appreciate and protect scenic landscapes".
  • Romantic ideas recognised "the validity of emotional response by people to their landscapes".
  • New idea: "that landscape has a value, and that everyone has a right to appreciate and enjoy it"; the National Trust movement grew from here.
  • Social construction: the meaning and value of a landscape come from people's ideas, not only from its rocks and soils.
Mission 3

Is "wilderness" natural?

  • Historian William Cronon (1995): wilderness, "far from being the one place on earth that stands apart from humanity, … is quite profoundly a human creation".
  • NatureScot (Scotland) maps wild land using four attributes:
  • "perceived naturalness of the land cover"; "ruggedness of terrain"; "remoteness from public roads, ferries or railway stations"; "visible lack of buildings, roads, pylons and other modern artefacts".
  • Its map of wild land areas dates from 2014.
Quick check
?Which NatureScot attribute shows most clearly that "wildness" is judged by people rather than simply measured in the land?
Mission 3

A "natural" landscape carries meanings too

  • The Giant's Causeway is listed for natural values: criterion (vii), "exceptional natural beauty", and (viii), earth history.
  • But "natural beauty" is a human judgement.
  • It "has inspired legends of giants striding over the sea to Scotland": an associative layer.
  • Its study "greatly contributed to the development of the earth sciences": a scientific way of reading it.
Quick check
?On the same Lake District fell, a farmer sees grazing land, a visitor sees scenery, and a conservationist sees a habitat. What does this show?
Mission 3

Why constructions matter

  • Constructions guide decisions: what to protect, and how.
  • World Heritage status rewards the Lake District's farming traditions and scenery.
  • Scotland's wild land policy protects perceived naturalness.
  • Exam move (CCEA "explain"): define natural and cultural landscapes; name a place for each; show how a group's ideas shaped what is valued or protected there.
Mission check

?Yellowstone became "the first true national park in 1872". Visitors often call it untouched nature. Why would a geographer call this view a social construction?

Every landscape is physical, but what it means (wild, beautiful, productive, sacred) is socially constructed. Natural and cultural landscapes are ends of a spectrum: the Causeway's rocks are natural, yet its beauty and legends are cultural; the Lake District's fells are a working human system.

Mission 4: Only about one person in 27 is an international migrant. Why do some people move while most stay?

Make a guess: Only about one person in 27 is an international migrant. Why do some people move while most stay?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • A2-2.A2.i push and pull factors (economic, social, political, cultural, environmental) and barriers to migration
  • A2-2.A2.iii voluntary v forced migration; migration processes, including those used by undocumented migrants
Mission 4

Who is a migrant?

  • International migrant: "any person who changes his or her country of usual residence" (UN DESA).
  • Voluntary: chooses to move (work, family, study). Forced: compelled by conflict, persecution or disaster.
  • Economic migrant: moves mainly for work or income.
  • Refugee: outside their country "owing to well-founded fear of being persecuted".
  • Asylum seeker: has claimed protection; claim not yet decided. Undocumented (irregular): no legal permission to enter or stay.
Mission 4

Global numbers

  • 304 million international migrants in 2024, up from 275 million in 2020.
  • 3.7% of the world's population.
  • 48% were women or girls.
  • One in six was forcibly displaced across borders.
  • End of 2025: 41.6 million refugees; 9 million asylum seekers awaiting decisions.
Quick check
?International migrants rose from 275 million in 2020 to 304 million in 2024. What was the percentage increase?
Mission 4

Push, pull and barriers

  • Economic: push, unemployment or low wages; pull, jobs and higher pay.
  • Social and cultural: family already abroad, language, education, religious freedom.
  • Political: persecution and conflict push. In 2017 more than 647,000 Rohingya fled Myanmar to Bangladesh within four months.
  • Environmental: Pakistan's 2022 floods affected 33 million people.
  • Barriers (intervening obstacles): cost, distance, visas and quotas, border controls. Example: the UK's points-based system from 2021 aimed to "reduce overall levels of migration".
Mission 4

Voluntary, forced and undocumented processes

  • Regular routes: visas, points systems, family reunion, student routes.
  • Chain migration: earlier migrants provide contacts, money and housing.
  • Undocumented routes: smugglers, irregular sea crossings, overstaying visas.
  • UK: people detected crossing the Channel in small boats rose from 299 (2018) to 45,755 (2022).
  • Many forced migrants use irregular routes because regular routes are closed to them.
Quick check
?A Syrian family pays a smuggler to cross the Aegean by dinghy and then claims asylum in Greece. How is this best classified?
Mission 4

Events change the pattern

  • Most migrants are not forced: five in six are not forcibly displaced. Most move for work, family or study.
  • Political events: refugees under UNHCR's mandate rose from 16.1 million (2015) to 31.6 million (2023); Ukraine's emigrant numbers rose 124% from 2020 to 2024.
  • Mediterranean crossings: sea arrivals to Europe 363,123 (2016), 102,431 (2019), 263,048 (2023), 144,854 (2025).
  • Environmental: floods and droughts add to economic pushes (Pakistan, 2022).
Mission check

?Nepal's 2024 net migration rate was −13.5 per 1,000 people, and it had 92 males for every 100 females. Gulf destinations such as Qatar have 248 males per 100 females. What best explains Nepal's figures?

Most people stay: only 3.7% are international migrants. Those who move are selected: mostly voluntary, often young, working-age and, on labour corridors, male, pulled by jobs and helped or blocked by barriers and policy. Conflict and disaster add forced flows on top.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Within a year of August 2017, nearly 725,000 people had fled from Myanmar into Bangladesh. Why do so many people flee at once, and what happens where they arrive?

Make a guess: Within a year of August 2017, nearly 725,000 people had fled from Myanmar into Bangladesh. Why do so many people flee at once, and what happens where they arrive?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • A2-2.A2.iii voluntary versus forced migration; migration processes, including those adopted by undocumented migrants
Mission 5

Voluntary and forced migration

  • Voluntary migration: people choose to move, usually for work, study or family.
  • Forced migration: people are compelled to move by threats to life or livelihood: conflict, persecution, disaster.
  • In practice it is a continuum: many moves mix choice and compulsion.
Mission 5

Who is who

  • Refugee: outside their country "owing to well-founded fear of being persecuted for reasons of race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group or political opinion" (1951 Convention).
  • Asylum seeker: has asked for protection; the claim is not yet decided.
  • Internally displaced person (IDP): forced to flee but "not crossed an internationally recognized State border".
  • Economic migrant: moves by choice, mainly for work and higher income.
Quick check
?A man flees threats from an armed group, crosses a border and applies for protection. His claim has not yet been decided. What is he now?
Mission 5

Global numbers

  • End of 2025: 117.8 million people were forcibly displaced worldwide.
  • They include 41.6 million refugees, 9 million asylum seekers and 68.7 million people displaced inside their own country by conflict or violence.
  • Most displaced people never cross a border.
Mission 5

Conflict and persecution have raised refugee numbers

  • Refugees under UNHCR's mandate (this series leaves out Palestine refugees under UNRWA and others in need of international protection, so it is lower than the 41.6 million total): 16.1 million at the end of 2015; 31.6 million at the end of 2023, almost double (our calculation).
  • The largest one-year rise came in 2022: up 8.1 million (our calculation). Refugees from Ukraine rose from about 28,000 to 5.7 million that year, as war began.
  • Largest origins at the end of 2025: Ukraine 5.2 million, Syria 4.9 million, Sudan 2.8 million, Afghanistan 2.7 million.
  • Numbers fell to 28.5 million by the end of 2025: trends can reverse when people return.
Quick check
?Refugees under UNHCR's mandate: 16.1 million (2015), 31.6 million (2023), 28.5 million (2025); largest origins in 2025: Ukraine, Syria, Sudan, Afghanistan. Which statement is best supported?
Mission 5

Political push: the Rohingya, 2017

  • On 25 August 2017 the Arakan Rohingya Salvation Army (ARSA) attacked a military base and up to 30 security-force outposts in northern Rakhine State.
  • The authorities called their response "clearance operations".
  • A UN fact-finding mission found the response "immediate, brutal and grossly disproportionate".
  • By mid-August 2018, nearly 725,000 Rohingya had fled to Bangladesh.
Mission 5

Root causes and the trigger

  • The mission found most Rohingya "de facto stateless, arbitrarily deprived of nationality".
  • At least 392 villages, 40% of all settlements in northern Rakhine, were partly or totally destroyed.
  • Long-term causes (statelessness, restrictions) made the group vulnerable; the 2017 operations were the trigger, so people fled at once.
  • On 31 October 2026, Bangladesh hosted 1,208,018 Rohingya refugees.
Mission 5

Migration processes

  • Stages: decision to move, the journey, arrival and settlement. Moves may happen in steps (step migration) or follow earlier migrants (chain migration).
  • Documented routes: visas and work permits, family reunion, refugee resettlement, an asylum claim at a border.
  • Undocumented migrants: cross borders without permission, often paying smugglers, or enter legally and overstay a visa.
  • Undocumented journeys carry high risks: dangerous routes, debt to smugglers and no legal protection at work.
Quick check
?Which person is an undocumented migrant?
Mission 5

Environmental push

  • Sudden onset: Pakistan's 2022 floods affected 33 million people, killed more than 1,730 and displaced more than 8 million. Those displaced within Pakistan are IDPs, not refugees.
  • Slow onset: drought, salinity and sea-level rise push people gradually; such moves often look like economic migration.
Mission check

?In 2022 the number of refugees under UNHCR's mandate rose by about 8 million in one year. Which explanation and consequence fit best?

People flee at once when a trigger (here, the 2017 "clearance operations") hits a group already made vulnerable (statelessness). Where they arrive, camps grow fast: Bangladesh hosted 1,208,018 Rohingya refugees in 2026, with long-term pressure on land, jobs, services and politics.

Mission 7: In 1979 Shenzhen was what its government calls "a small backward border town". By 2024 it had 17.98 million residents. Where did they all come from?

Mission 7

Urbanisation and urban growth

  • Urbanisation: a rising share of people living in urban areas. Urban growth: a rising number of urban people.
  • Three engines: natural increase in cities, net migration into cities, and reclassification of rural places as urban.
  • Later stages of the urban cycle: suburbanisation, counter-urbanisation, then urban resurgence (re-urbanisation).
Mission 7

Global patterns since 1950

  • 1950: 20% of the world's 2.5 billion people lived in cities; 40% in towns.
  • 2025: 45% in cities, 36% in towns and 19% in rural areas, of 8.2 billion.
  • Cities overtook towns as the most common setting in 1996.
  • Megacities (10 million+): 8 in 1975, 33 in 2025 (19 in Asia), 37 projected by 2050.
  • Regions differ (2025): cities hold 59% in Northern Africa and Western Asia, 38% in sub-Saharan Africa.
Quick check
?In 2025, 45% of the world's 8.2 billion people lived in cities. About how many people is that?
Mission 7

Shenzhen

  • 17.98 million permanent residents at the end of 2024.
  • Under the UN's density-based definition, the Shenzhen city area had about 13.9 million people in 2025: a different boundary, a different number.
  • GDP in 2024: 3.68 trillion yuan, up 5.8% on the year. Exports ranked first among Chinese mainland cities for 32 consecutive years.
  • 2015–2025: Beijing, Guangzhou, Shanghai and Shenzhen each grew by more than 0.8% a year, against 0.1% for China as a whole.
Mission 7

Where China's city growth came from

  • China's urban share (national definition): 20.6% in 1982, 63.8% in 2020.
  • Urban growth, 2010–2020 censuses: 56% rural-to-urban migration, 26% administrative changes, 18% natural increase.
  • Push: low farm incomes, few rural jobs. Pull: factory and service work, higher wages.
  • Migration is selective: mostly young adults. Rural areas face "population ageing and out-migration".
Quick check
?In the UN's breakdown, 26% of China's urban growth in 2010–2020 came from "administrative changes". What does that mean?
Mission 7

In-migration: services and economy in Shenzhen

  • Economic activity: exports ranked first among mainland cities for 32 years, and GDP reached 3.68 trillion yuan in 2024. In-migrants of working age fill the jobs behind this.
  • Shenzhen calls itself "a young immigrant city"; its average age was 32.5 at the seventh national census.
  • Services: a place of millions within a lifetime needs housing, schools, clinics, water and transport built at the same pace.
  • A young population means demand for schools and maternity care now, and for elderly care later.
Mission 7

In-migration: social stability

  • Destination: rapid inflows can outpace housing, so some migrants live in crowded or informal homes; access to services can differ between long-standing residents and newcomers.
  • Source areas: selective out-migration leaves rural communities facing "population ageing".
  • Stability depends on jobs: when work is plentiful, inflows are absorbed; in downturns, pressures grow.
  • Exam move: for each implication, say who is affected: migrants, existing residents or the places left behind.
Quick check

Mission 8

Mission 8: From 1 January 2021, EU citizens needed points to get a UK work visa. By the year to March 2023, UK net migration had peaked at 944,000. How far can governments control global interactions?

Make a guess: From 1 January 2021, EU citizens needed points to get a UK work visa. By the year to March 2023, UK net migration had peaked at 944,000. How far can governments control global interactions?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • A2-2.A2.iv immigration (documented and undocumented) as a political issue and governments' responses (one case study at national or international scale: the UK)
Mission 8

Immigration as a political issue

  • Documented immigration: entry and residence with permission, such as a work or study visa.
  • Undocumented immigration: entry or stay without permission.
  • UK net migration peaked at 944,000 in the year to March 2023.
  • Year to June 2025: 49,341 detected irregular arrivals, 43,309 of them by small boat; 95% of small-boat arrivals since 2018 have claimed asylum.
  • Both kinds of flow became central issues in UK politics.
Mission 8

Response 1: the points-based system

  • After Brexit, free movement with the EU ended on 1 January 2021 and a points-based system began.
  • "EU and non-EU citizens will be treated equally".
  • Workers needed 70 points, including a job offer at the required skill level, English, and a salary threshold (generally £25,600).
  • The skill threshold for non-EU workers fell "from RQF6 to RQF3" (degree level to A-level equivalent).
  • Stated aim: to "reduce overall migration numbers".
Mission 8

What happened next

  • Net migration rose to its 944,000 peak (year to March 2023).
  • December 2023: the government announced it would raise the overseas workers' earnings threshold from £26,200 to £38,700 from spring 2024, and stop overseas care workers bringing dependants.
  • Net migration fell to 171,000 in the year to December 2025 (provisional).
  • In that year, 77% of immigrants were non-EU+ nationals: 47% came to study, 23% to work and 14% to seek asylum.
Quick check
?UK net migration fell from 944,000 (year to March 2023) to 171,000 (year to December 2025, provisional). What was the percentage fall?
Mission 8

Response 2: undocumented immigration

  • The Illegal Migration Act 2023 received Royal Assent on 20 July 2023.
  • Its stated purposes include "the removal from the United Kingdom of persons who have entered or arrived in breach of immigration control", detention, and "the maximum number of persons entering the United Kingdom annually using safe and legal routes".
  • Its central duty to remove was never brought into force; sections 1–6 were repealed on 2 December 2025.
  • Small-boat arrivals continued after the Act was passed: the year to June 2025 was only 5% below the 2022 peak.
  • Supporters present such laws as deterrence; critics raise asylum rights. These are each side's positions, not findings.
Mission 8

How far can governments control flows?

  • Who moves: the 2021 rules ended EU free movement and opened routes for non-EU workers.
  • How many: numbers rose to a peak before tighter rules followed, then fell; 2025 data are provisional.
  • Which route: irregular arrivals persisted after the 2023 Act was passed, though its removal duty never came into force.
  • Demand for workers and students, and conflict abroad, keep flows going.
  • Exam move: separate a policy's stated aim from its measured outcome, with dates.
  • CCEA "Discuss": present the government's response and the positions of those who support and oppose it, then judge with evidence.
Quick check
?Which conclusion about the UK's immigration controls is best supported?
Mission check

?Another government brings in stricter visa rules and a new law against irregular entry. Based on the UK evidence, what is the most likely result?

Ending free movement changed who could come and opened routes for non-EU workers, but UK net migration still peaked at 944,000 (year to March 2023). Tighter rules followed and it fell to 171,000 (2025, provisional); irregular arrivals persisted after the 2023 Act was passed, though its removal duty never came into force. Governments shape global flows; they rarely switch them off.

Mission 9

Mission 9: Swiss voters backed limits on immigration in 2014. What did their government actually do next?

Make a guess: Swiss voters backed limits on immigration in 2014. What did their government actually do next?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • A2-2.A2.iv immigration (documented and undocumented) as a political issue and governments' responses (one case study: Switzerland, national scale)
Mission 9

Immigration as a political issue

  • Documented immigration: people who enter with permission (visas, free movement, refugee status).
  • Undocumented (irregular) immigration: people who enter or stay without permission.
  • Anti-immigration movements campaign to reduce or limit immigration; others campaign to keep borders open. Elections and referendums turn these positions into measurable results.
  • Government responses range from quotas and visa rules to border enforcement, regularisation and integration policy.
Mission 9

Case study: Switzerland

  • International migrants were 18.7% of Switzerland's population in 1990 and 31.1% in 2024 (UN estimates); the world figure in 2024 was 3.7%.
  • Citizens can force a national vote on a change to the constitution: a popular initiative. Support for limiting immigration can therefore be measured directly.
  • Switzerland is not in the EU, but has bilateral agreements with it, including the free movement of persons.
Quick check
?Switzerland's migrant share rose from 18.7% (1990) to 31.1% (2024). Which statement is correct?
Mission 9

The 2014 vote

  • 9 February 2014, popular initiative "Against mass immigration": 50.3% yes, accepted by 12 full and 5 half cantons; turnout 56.6%.
  • The Federal Council said the initiative "calls for quotas for all foreign nationals".
  • It had recommended rejection: firms need to recruit abroad, and quotas risked the bilateral agreements with the EU.
  • The initiative's title states its supporters' framing ("mass" immigration); the result shows a narrow, not overwhelming, majority.
Mission 9

The government's response

  • In December 2017 the Federal Council adopted rules implementing the new constitutional article on immigration (Article 121a).
  • The main measure, from 1 July 2018, was a job vacancy registration requirement, intended to help place job seekers registered at regional unemployment centres.
  • Article 121a was implemented without quotas on EU citizens; free movement with the EU continued (the 2020 initiative sought to end it).
  • Exam move: compare what a proposal asked for with what the government adopted. A vote and its implementation can differ.
Quick check
?Why did the Swiss response in 2017–18 differ from the 2014 initiative's demand for quotas?
Mission 9

2020 and 2026: two more votes

  • 27 September 2020, "For moderate immigration (Limitation Initiative)", which "aims to end the free movement of persons with the EU": 38.3% yes, rejected. The Federal Council said ending free movement would threaten jobs.
  • 14 June 2026, "No to Switzerland of 10 million! (Sustainability Initiative)": population "must remain below 10 million until 2050"; 45.2% yes, rejected. The Federal Council said it threatened prosperity, security and the bilateral path with the EU.
  • Support for limits has moved between about 38% and 50%: a persistent political issue, not a settled one.
Mission 9

Undocumented immigration: a UK comparison

  • Home Office operational data: people detected crossing the English Channel in small boats numbered 299 in 2018, 45,755 in 2022 and 41,472 in 2025.
  • In 2023 Parliament passed the Illegal Migration Act; its stated purpose is "to prevent and deter unlawful migration, and in particular migration by unsafe and illegal routes".
  • Undocumented arrivals are fewer than documented ones (long-term immigration was 813,000 in 2025) but are highly visible, so they carry great political weight.
  • Responses to undocumented flows depend on other states (where boats leave from, where people can be returned to).
Quick check
?Which is a response to undocumented immigration?
Mission check

?The UK ended EU free movement in 2021; its new points-based system aimed to "reduce overall levels of migration". Net migration then peaked at 944,000 (year ending March 2023) and fell to 171,000 (year ending December 2025) after further rule changes in 2024–25. What does this show about governments' responses to immigration as a political issue?

Immigration stays a political issue because votes, labour needs and international agreements pull in different directions. Switzerland voted for limits in 2014 but adopted a job registration requirement, not quotas; the UK's numbers rose, then fell, after its rules changed.

Mission 10

Mission 10: If the internet now spans the globe, why are over two billion people still offline?

Make a guess: If the internet now spans the globe, why are over two billion people still offline?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • A2-2.A3.i cyberspace has changed socio-economic activity and helped produce international cultures
  • A2-2.A3.ii global contrasts in cyberspace persist owing to economic, social and political issues
Mission 10

What cyberspace is

  • Cyberspace: the online space created by networked computers and phones, where people communicate, trade and share information.
  • It has a physical geography: cables, data centres, masts and power supplies.
  • Internet users: 15.6% of the world's people in 2005, 73.6% in 2025 (World Bank, ITU data).
  • ITU estimates that about 6 billion people use the internet in 2025, while "2.2 billion people remain offline".
  • Data flows are uneven: cross-border traffic is concentrated "between North America and Europe, and between North America and Asia" (UNCTAD).
Mission 10

Changing economic activity

  • E-commerce: internet sales were 2.8% of UK retail sales in November 2006, peaked at 37.8% in January 2021, and averaged 27.5% in 2025 (ONS).
  • Mobile money: in Kenya, access to M-PESA "lifted 194,000 households, or 2% of Kenyan households, out of poverty".
  • The effect was "more pronounced for female-headed households"; women moved "out of agriculture and into business".
  • Services move online: banking, government forms, remote work, tutoring.
Quick check
?Why could mobile money reduce poverty in Kenya?
Mission 10

Changing social activity

  • Social networks, messaging and video calls keep migrants in touch with home and let communities organise across borders.
  • Global bandwidth "rose by 35 per cent in 2020", the first pandemic year (UNCTAD).
  • The UK's peak online share of retail (January 2021) came in the same lockdown period.
Mission 10

International cultures

  • Platforms carry the same music, films, games and memes worldwide at once.
  • Netflix: available in "more than 190 countries" and "50 languages", "entertaining over half a billion people".
  • Flows run both ways: non-English shows and music reach global audiences, creating hybrid and international cultures.
  • Infrastructure is concentrated: the USA and China "account for half the world's hyperscale data centres".
Quick check
?Which is the best example of cyberspace helping to produce an international culture?
Mission 10

Contrasts: economic

  • 2025: 94% of people in high-income countries use the internet, against 23% in low-income countries (ITU).
  • 5G: 84% coverage in high-income countries, 4% in low-income countries.
  • Affordability: access "remains unaffordable in around 60 per cent of low- and middle-income countries".
  • Poverty limits investment in cables, masts and power, and limits what households can pay for phones and data.
Mission 10

Contrasts: social

  • Rural–urban: 85% online in urban areas, 58% in rural areas (2025).
  • Gender: 77% of men online, 71% of women.
  • Skills, literacy, language and age also shape who uses the internet.
  • The digital divide: the gap between those who can use cyberspace fully and those who cannot.
Quick check
?In 2025, 94% of people in high-income countries and 23% in low-income countries used the internet. Which description is accurate?
Mission 10

Contrasts: political

  • Governments can shut down or filter the internet. Access Now recorded "at least 313 shutdowns" in 52 countries in 2025, up from 304 in 2024.
  • Myanmar had "at least 95"; conflict was "the lead trigger", with 125 conflict-related shutdowns in 14 countries.
  • Blocking: in April 2017 Turkey's internet regulator blocked "all language versions of Wikipedia".
  • Laws on data, content and ownership differ between states, so cyberspace is not one open space.
Quick check
?Which contrast in cyberspace is caused mainly by a political issue?
Mission check

?In a middle-income country, mobile signal reaches almost everyone and data is cheap. Yet women are much less likely than men to be online, and in one region use drops to zero whenever protests begin. Which issues best explain these two contrasts?

Cyberspace has changed how people trade, work and share culture, but contrasts persist: economic (cost, infrastructure), social (rural, gender, skills) and political (shutdowns, blocking).

Mission 11: In 2025 Access Now recorded at least 313 internet shutdowns. Can citizens win back the freedom to connect?

Make a guess: In 2025 Access Now recorded at least 313 internet shutdowns. Can citizens win back the freedom to connect?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • A2-2.A3.ii global contrasts in cyberspace persist owing to economic, social and political issues
Mission 11

Global contrasts in cyberspace

  • Economic: 94% of people in high-income countries are online, against 23% in low-income countries.
  • Social and spatial: 85% in urban areas, 58% in rural areas; 2.2 billion people remain offline.
  • Infrastructure: 84% of people in high-income countries have access to 5G, against 4% in low-income countries.
  • These gaps persist even as total use grows.
Mission 11

Political controls: shutting the internet down

  • Governments can block platforms, censor content or switch the internet off.
  • The #KeepItOn coalition recorded at least 313 internet shutdowns in 52 countries in 2025, up from 304 in 2024.
  • Myanmar had at least 95, the most of any country.
  • "For the third year, conflict was the lead trigger": 125 conflict-related shutdowns in 14 countries.
Quick check
?Which is a political cause of a contrast in internet access?
Mission 11

Example 1: Nigeria suspends Twitter, 2021

  • On 4 June 2021 Nigeria suspended Twitter, days after Twitter deleted a post from President Buhari's account.
  • The government argued that freedom of expression "is not absolute", and "threatened to prosecute Nigerians accessing Twitter through circumvention tools".
  • Access returned in January 2022; the authorities said Twitter had agreed to conditions, which the company "has not acknowledged".
Mission 11

Nigeria: the court

  • SERAP, a Nigerian NGO, and others took Nigeria to the ECOWAS Court of Justice, the regional court of the Economic Community of West African States.
  • On 14 July 2022 the court declared the suspension "unlawful and inconsistent with the provisions of Article 9 of the ACHPR and Article 19 of the ICCPR".
  • It ordered Nigeria to lift the suspension and "guarantee a non-repetition".
Mission 11

Example 2: Turkey blocks Wikipedia, 2017–2020

  • On 29 April 2017 Turkey's internet regulator blocked all language versions of Wikipedia.
  • The stated grounds were "maintaining national security and public order".
  • On 26 December 2019 Turkey's Constitutional Court found a violation of freedom of expression; on 15 January 2020 an Ankara court ruled to lift the ban.
Quick check
?Did the ECOWAS Court ruling end Nigeria's Twitter suspension?
Mission check

?In a fictional country a citizens' group wins a court ruling that the government's block on a social-media platform is unlawful. Based on the Nigeria and Turkey cases, what does the ruling achieve?

Sometimes, and slowly, mainly through courts. In Nigeria, SERAP won a ruling that the 2021 suspension was unlawful, although access had already returned in January 2022. In Turkey, the Constitutional Court found the Wikipedia block violated freedom of expression; access returned in January 2020, more than two and a half years after the block began.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Cultural groups exist through isolation, shared history, language and religion, and state action; cultural nationalism (Welsh: a million speakers by 2050) protects a group's culture.
  • The world is becoming more alike and more diverse at once, depending on scale and indicator.

Mission 2

  • Social exclusion = being unable to take part fully in society; discrimination is one process that causes it. Section 75's equality duty covers many groups but not social class.
  • NI data: FSM gap of 29 points in GCSE outcomes; minority ethnic gap of 6.5 points; peace walls and deprivation cluster in Belfast; hate-crime data show hostility but not trends.

Mission 3

  • Natural landscape: shaped mainly by physical processes; cultural landscape: "combined works of nature and of man" (designed, organically evolved, associative).
  • Landscapes are human systems, and their meanings are social constructions (Romantic scenery, "wilderness", "perceived naturalness") that guide protection.

Mission 4

  • 304 million international migrants (3.7%, 2024); 48% female; one in six forcibly displaced; most move voluntarily for work, family or study.
  • Push and pull factors (economic, social, political, cultural, environmental) act through barriers; processes range from visas to smuggled, undocumented crossings.

Mission 5

  • Forced migration (refugees, asylum seekers, IDPs) and voluntary economic migration have political, environmental and socio-economic causes; conflict and persecution have raised refugee numbers since 2015.
  • Know documented and undocumented processes.

Mission 6

  • Achill: 5,260 (1911) → 2,345 (2022); 65+ = 35.9%, 20–34 = 8.9% (Ireland 15.1%, 18.2%).
  • Implications: services (thresholds, long trips to hospital), economy (37% at work, 36% retired), social stability (old-age dependency 0.68, 43% holiday homes, fewer families).
  • Exam move: structure answers by the three implication headings in the specification, each with one Achill figure.

Mission 7

  • Urbanisation (share) differs from urban growth (number); three engines: natural increase, migration, reclassification.
  • In-migration brings labour and growth, and pressure on housing and services; source areas age.

Mission 8

  • The UK case: a points-based system (2021), later tightening (2023–24) and the Illegal Migration Act 2023 changed who came and how; numbers depended on more than rules.

Mission 9

  • Votes and elections measure support for limiting immigration; governments respond by balancing that support against labour needs and agreements (Switzerland 2014–2026).
  • Documented and undocumented flows need different responses: visa rules and registration versus border enforcement and removal.

Mission 10

  • Cyberspace has changed socio-economic activity (e-commerce, mobile money, online services) and spread international cultures through global platforms.
  • Global contrasts persist for economic (income, affordability, infrastructure), social (rural–urban, gender) and political (shutdowns, blocking) reasons.

Mission 11

  • Cyberspace contrasts persist for economic (income), social (urban–rural) and political (shutdowns, censorship) reasons.
  • Citizens can win back access through courts and campaigns, but slowly.
