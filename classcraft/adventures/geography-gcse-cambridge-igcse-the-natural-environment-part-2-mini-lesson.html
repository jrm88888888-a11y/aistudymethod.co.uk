Mission 1: Between 1951 and 2004 the cliffs at Aldbrough, on England's Holderness coast, retreated about 2 m a year. Why do they disappear so fast?

Make a guess: Between 1951 and 2004 the cliffs at Aldbrough, on England's Holderness coast, retreated about 2 m a year. Why do they disappear so fast?

Specification: what this mission covers

Mission 1

Where waves get their energy

  • Waves are made by wind blowing over the sea.
  • Bigger waves come from stronger winds, winds that blow for longer, and a longer fetch (the distance of open water the wind blows over).
  • When a wave breaks, water rushes up the beach (swash) and drains back (backwash).
Mission 1

Constructive and destructive waves

  • Constructive waves: low and gentle. Strong swash, weak backwash, so they add sediment and build beaches.
  • Destructive waves: high and steep, often in storms. Weak swash, strong backwash, so they remove sediment and attack cliffs.
Quick check
?A beach is getting wider and higher over a calm summer. Which waves are most likely to be doing this?
Mission 1

Four ways the sea erodes

  • Hydraulic action: the force of the water, and air forced into cracks, breaks rock apart.
  • Abrasion: waves throw sand and pebbles at the cliff, wearing it away.
  • Attrition: pebbles knock together, becoming smaller and rounder.
  • Solution: sea water dissolves some rocks, such as chalk and limestone.
  • Use Cambridge's words: abrasion is corrasion, and solution is corrosion.
Mission 1

Transport, longshore drift and deposition

  • Four ways sediment moves: traction (big pebbles roll), saltation (small pebbles and sand bounce), suspension (fine particles carried in the water), solution (dissolved).
  • Longshore drift: waves arrive at an angle, so the swash pushes sediment up the beach at that angle, and the backwash drags it straight down. Sediment zig-zags along the coast.
  • Deposition: sediment is dropped where waves and currents lose energy, such as in sheltered bays.
Quick check
?A beach faces south. The prevailing waves arrive from the south-west. Which way does longshore drift move the sand?
Mission 1

Holderness: soft clay against hard chalk

  • Holderness cliffs are glacial till (boulder clay), mostly 18,000–13,000 years old, and about 20 m high at Aldbrough.
  • At high tide, waves erode their lower and sometimes middle sections.
  • Above the waves, the wet clay slips and slumps down the cliff.
  • To the north, Flamborough Head is chalk, which "is more resistant to erosion than the overlying till".
  • Aldbrough retreated 2.16 m a year in 1951–2004: about 114 m in total (2.16 × 53, calculated).
Quick check
?Why does Holderness retreat so much faster than Flamborough Head, just to the north?
Mission check

?Two cliffs face the same North Sea storms. Cliff X is resistant chalk. Cliff Y is soft glacial clay, soaked after a wet winter. Which retreats faster, and why?

Aldbrough's cliffs are soft till. Waves attack the foot at high tide, the wet clay slumps from above, and longshore drift carries the debris south. That is why they retreated 2.16 m a year from 1951 to 2004, while chalk at Flamborough Head resists erosion.

Mission 2

Not all rock is equally tough

  • Some rocks are resistant (e.g. chalk, limestone): waves wear them away slowly.
  • Others are less resistant (e.g. clays and sands): they erode quickly.
  • Where both meet the sea, the coast erodes unevenly: this is differential erosion.
  • Resistant rock is left sticking out as a headland; weak rock is worn back into a bay.
Mission 2

A discordant coastline

  • On a discordant coastline, bands of different rock meet the coast at right angles.
  • Result: alternating headlands and bays.
  • Example: east Purbeck. "The variation in erosion resistance has caused the formation of Durlston Head, Swanage Bay, Ballard Point and Studland Bay".
  • Swanage Bay lies between two resistant points, Handfast Point (chalk) and Peveril Point.
Mission 2

A concordant coastline

  • On a concordant coastline, rock bands run parallel to the coast.
  • A resistant outer band shields the weaker rock behind it, so the coast stays fairly straight.
  • If the sea breaks through, it scoops out the weak rock behind. At Lulworth Cove, a break in the Portland and Purbeck rocks let the sea erode the softer rocks behind into "a circular eroded cove".
Quick check
?A map shows bands of granite and clay running parallel to the shoreline, with granite facing the sea. What type of coastline is this?
Mission 2

Wave refraction

  • Waves slow down when the water gets shallow.
  • Off a headland the water is shallow first, so that part of each wave slows while the rest keeps going: the wave crest bends. This is wave refraction.
  • The bending focuses wave energy on the headland and spreads it out in the bay.
Mission 2

Erosion on headlands, beaches in bays

  • Headlands get the most energy: cliffs, wave-cut platforms, caves, arches, stacks and stumps form there.
  • The next mission shows how each of these forms.
  • Bays get less energy, so sand and shingle are deposited as beaches.
Quick check
?Why do beaches usually form in bays rather than on headlands?
Mission 2

How the landforms are linked

  • Discordant geology → headlands and bays.
  • Refraction → headlands eroded (cliffs, platforms, caves, arches, stacks, stumps); bays fill with beaches.
  • Over time the headland retreats and its landforms are replaced by new ones further back.
Mission check

?On a coast, bands of hard limestone and soft clay meet the sea at right angles. Where would you expect stacks and where sandy beaches?

Where rock bands meet the coast at right angles (discordant), the resistant rock becomes headlands and the weak rock bays. Refraction focuses wave energy on headlands, carving cliffs, caves, arches and stacks, while bays fill with beaches, as at Swanage Bay between Handfast and Peveril Points.

Mission 3

Cliffs, notches and platforms

  • Waves attack the foot of a cliff between high and low tide, cutting a wave-cut notch by hydraulic action and abrasion.
  • The rock above is left overhanging; it collapses and the cliff retreats.
  • A gently sloping wave-cut platform is left, exposed at low tide.
Mission 3

Cave, arch, stack, stump: Old Harry Rocks

  • At Old Harry Rocks, Dorset, the sea exploits "a crack in the chalk joints", slowly creating a cave.
  • Erosion along joints and bedding planes turns the cave into an arch; when the arch collapses, a stack is left.
  • Old Harry's Wife "was still a stack until her eventual collapse in 1896 to render her a stump": a stump is a stack worn down until it is covered at high tide.
  • Exam move: name the process at each stage (hydraulic action, abrasion) and the weakness it exploits (joints, faults, bedding planes).
Quick check
?What is the correct order as a headland is eroded?
Mission 3

Why the sea drops sediment: beaches

  • The sea deposits sediment where it loses energy: in sheltered bays, in shallow water, behind obstacles, and where constructive waves dominate.
  • Deposition also needs a supply of sediment, from eroding cliffs, rivers and the seabed.
  • A beach builds up where more sediment arrives than is removed.
  • Sandy beaches have gentle slopes; shingle beaches (pebbles) are steeper. Slapton Sands, Devon, is shingle: "flint, chert and quartz pebbles".
Mission 3

Spits

  • Where the coast changes direction, sediment carried along the coast keeps going and is dropped in the calmer water beyond: a spit grows out from the land.
  • The end often curves round as waves bend into the calmer water behind it: a hooked (recurved) spit. Saltmarsh forms in the shelter behind.
  • Spurn formed "as a long, narrow spit from deposits moving along the coast carried by currents". It is over three miles long but as little as 50 m wide.
Mission 3

Bars

  • A bar is a ridge of sediment that joins two headlands, cutting off the water behind as a lagoon.
  • Slapton Sands, Devon, is a shingle bar linking Strete Gate with Torcross. It dammed an old estuary and formed a freshwater lagoon, Slapton Ley.
  • Its main formation probably began about 5,000 years ago, as the rising sea pushed shingle landward. Longshore drift still replenishes it.
Quick check
?What is the key difference between a spit and a bar?
Mission 3

Sand dunes

  • Onshore winds blow dry sand from a wide beach; it piles up behind obstacles at the top of the beach.
  • Embryo dunes: the first low dunes, where pioneer plants trap sand.
  • Marram grass binds the sand, so dunes grow higher (yellow or white dunes).
  • Further inland, sand gains organic matter and grassland replaces marram: grey dunes. Low, damp hollows between them are dune slacks.
Mission check

?Shingle drifts east along a straight coast. Then the coastline turns sharply north into a river estuary. What will most likely form, and why?

Spurn is a spit. Sediment carried south along the Holderness coast by longshore drift keeps going where the coast turns at the Humber mouth, and is dropped in the calmer water. Its end hooks into the estuary, with saltmarsh behind.

Mission 4

Living at the coast: opportunities and hazards

  • Opportunities: ports and trade, fishing, tourism, and flat land for farming and building.
  • Hazards: erosion (Holderness cliffs retreated 2.16 m a year in 1951–2004), flooding from storms and surges, and tsunamis.
  • At Medmerry, storms "breached this bank many times during the last 15 years".
  • Management tries to keep the opportunities while cutting the risk.
Mission 4

Four policies for each stretch of coast

  • Hold the line: keep the defences where they are.
  • Advance the line: build new defences further out to sea.
  • Managed retreat (managed realignment): let the sea in, behind a new line of defence inland.
  • No active intervention: "Let nature take its course – no work will be carried out to repair defences".
Mission 4

Hard engineering

  • Sea walls: concrete walls that reflect waves. Strong, but very costly, and waves can scour the beach in front.
  • Rock armour (rip rap): big boulders that break up waves. Cheaper than a sea wall, but can look out of place and make the beach harder to reach.
  • Gabions: wire cages filled with rocks; cheaper than walls, but the wire rusts, so they do not last as long.
  • Groynes: barriers across the beach that trap longshore drift and keep the beach wide. Down-drift, the beach can be starved: groynes at one point on the Sussex coast "have contributed to erosion" of the spit beyond them.
Mission 4

Soft engineering

  • Beach nourishment (replenishment): add sand or shingle to the beach, which absorbs wave energy. It looks natural, but must be repeated.
  • Managed retreat: give low land to the sea; it becomes saltmarsh, which absorbs wave energy.
Quick check
?Groynes keep one town's beach wide. What is their most likely cost?
Mission 4

Medmerry: the problem

  • Medmerry lies west of Selsey. A shingle bank there protected 300 properties, large caravan sites and 650 hectares of land.
  • The flood risk was 1 in 1 in any given year, and the bank had about 1 year of life left.
  • The B2145 and the utilities that serve Selsey cross the low land behind the bank.
Mission 4

The options and their costs (2007)

  • No active intervention: £8–10 million. Without winter repairs, the bank was likely to breach within a year.
  • Hold the line: £80 million; judged "not practical".
  • Managed realignment: £10 million, for benefits valued at £40 million.
  • Costs are present values: today's value of the spending over the strategy's time span.
Quick check
?Managed realignment at Medmerry was estimated to cost £10 million and to bring benefits worth £40 million. How many pounds of benefit for each £1 spent?
Mission 4

Hold the line or let the sea in?

  • For holding the line: all property and land keep some protection, and losses are delayed.
  • Against: rising flood risk, more future funding, and "increasingly out of step with natural processes".
  • For managed realignment: it works with natural processes, cuts long-term spending, manages flood risk and may create a large area of wildlife habitat.
  • Against: flooding of land and property, possible loss of beach access, and a possible cut in shingle supply to the Witterings, along the coast.
Mission 4

What happened: effects and conflicts

  • Completed in 2014, with 7 km of new embankments inland, protecting key infrastructure for Selsey.
  • The £28 million scheme was then "the largest open-coast scheme in Europe"; it created a new wetland, with the RSPB's support.
  • The government also had a duty to create new intertidal habitat, to replace losses elsewhere.
  • Conflict: realignment meant "the flooding of a relatively large area of land".
  • Evaluate: £28 million spent is not directly comparable with the 2007 present-value estimate of £10 million.
Mission 4

Is Medmerry sustainable?

  • Economic: lower long-term cost than holding a failing bank (£10 million against £80 million, 2007 estimates).
  • Social: Selsey's only road and its utilities are protected.
  • Environmental: works with natural processes and creates wetland habitat.
  • Against: farmland and land are lost to the sea, and shingle supply along the coast may change.
Quick check
?Which point best shows that Medmerry is environmentally sustainable?
Mission check

?On a low farmland coast, an old sea wall is failing. Rebuilding it would cost far more than the land behind it is worth. A town sits on higher ground nearby, but its only road crosses the low land. Which policy fits best?

At Medmerry, holding a failing bank was costed at £80 million against £10 million to realign (2007 estimates). New banks inland protect Selsey's road and services, and the flooded land became wetland. The price was a "relatively large area of land" given to the sea.

Make a guess: Hurricane Ian hit Florida with 130-knot winds in 2022. Why was water, not wind, its deadliest hazard?

Specification: what this mission covers

Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460)

  • 2.3.4 distribution and impacts of tropical storms (cyclones, hurricanes, typhoons)
Mission 5

One storm, three names

  • A tropical cyclone is "a rapidly rotating storm that begins over tropical oceans".
  • It is called a hurricane in the Atlantic and north-east Pacific, a typhoon in the north-west Pacific, and a cyclone in the Indian Ocean and South Pacific.
Mission 5

Where and when

  • They form over tropical oceans, between about 25°S and 25°N.
  • They do not form within about 480 km (300 miles) of the equator: there is too little spin there.
  • Main seasons: July–October in the Northern Hemisphere, December–March in the Southern; globally, September is the most active month.
  • The north-west Pacific has them all year round. Over cool water or land, they weaken.
Quick check
?Why do tropical storms almost never form right on the equator, even where the sea is warm?
Mission 5

Why tropical storms are hazards

  • High winds destroy buildings, trees and power lines and turn debris into missiles.
  • Intense rain causes river and flash flooding inland.
  • Storm surge: "an abnormal water level rise generated by a storm" above the normal tide, driven mainly by strong onshore winds. It is highest where the sea floor slopes gently. It causes coastal flooding.
  • Landslides: rain soaks steep slopes. Hurricane Maria (2017) triggered more than 40,000 landslides in Puerto Rico.
  • Over the past 50 years, tropical cyclones caused 1,945 disasters, killing 779,324 people.
Mission 5

Hurricane Ian, 2022: Florida

  • Ian intensified rapidly over water of 30 °C and above, with low wind shear (winds at different heights were similar).
  • 28 September: it peaked at 140 kt (category 5), then hit Cayo Costa, Florida, at 130 kt (category 4).
  • Storm surge (sea water piled up by onshore winds) flooded Fort Myers Beach to 3–4.6 m (10–15 feet) above ground.
  • In Lee County, at least 52,514 buildings were damaged, 5,369 of them destroyed.
  • At least 156 people died in the US, 66 directly from the storm: surge killed 41, freshwater flooding 12 and wind 4.
Mission 5

Hurricane Ian: Cuba

  • 27 September: Ian hit western Cuba as a category 3 hurricane (110 kt).
  • Cuba reported 5 deaths, 3 of them caused directly by the storm.
  • More than 100,000 homes were damaged or destroyed, and the whole island reportedly lost power for a few days.
  • The US damage was estimated at $112.9 billion; there is no dollar estimate for Cuba, so losses cannot be compared in money.
  • Exam move: describe impacts on people (deaths, homes, power) and on property, and say where data are missing.
Quick check
?Of Ian's 66 direct deaths in the US, 41 were caused by storm surge. What percentage is that, to the nearest whole number?
Mission check

?A category 3 hurricane is heading for a low-lying coast with a wide, gently sloping sea floor and a bay that narrows inland. Which hazard is likely to kill most people?

Ian's onshore winds drove a storm surge 3–4.6 m (10–15 feet) above ground at Fort Myers Beach. Surge caused 41 of the 66 direct deaths in the US (62%); wind caused 4.

Specification: what this mission covers

Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460)

Mission 6

Hurricane Ian, 2022

  • 27 Sept: hit western Cuba (Pinar del Río) as a category 3 hurricane, 110 kt.
  • It strengthened again over the sea, then hit south-west Florida on 28 Sept as a category 4, 130 kt.
  • Income per person (GNI per capita, 2021 PPP $): USA about $73,650; Cuba about $8,415, a UN estimate.
Mission 6

Primary effects: Florida

  • Primary effects are caused directly by the wind, rain and sea.
  • Storm surge flooded Fort Myers Beach 3–4.6 m (10–15 ft) above ground.
  • Lee County: at least 52,514 buildings damaged; 5,369 destroyed.
  • Roads and bridges to Sanibel and Pine Islands were washed away; they could be reached only by boat or helicopter.
  • 66 direct deaths in the US, all in Florida; storm surge killed 41.
Mission 6

Secondary effects: Florida

  • Secondary effects follow on from the primary ones.
  • About 4.45 million customers (about 9.62 million people) lost power in the US.
  • 90 more deaths were indirect: lack of timely medical care (18), falls and other accidents during power cuts (16), heart attacks (16).
  • Floodwater carried debris, fertiliser and pollutants into the sea; scientists then tracked a harmful algal bloom.
  • Crop and farm losses in Florida: $1.1–1.8 billion. Total US damage: $112.9 billion.
Mission 6

Effects in Cuba

  • Storm surge pushed the sea 300 m inland at La Coloma.
  • More than 100,000 homes damaged or destroyed; the whole island lost power for a few days.
  • 5 deaths, 3 of them direct.
  • Tobacco, a key export (10.4% of goods exports) and a main livelihood in Pinar del Río, suffered "disastrous" losses, putting the next harvest at risk.
  • More than 70 schools in Pinar del Río were damaged.
  • No dollar estimate of Cuba's losses exists, so the two cannot be compared in money.
Quick check
?Which of these was a secondary effect of Hurricane Ian?
Mission 6

Immediate responses

  • Warnings: the US National Hurricane Center briefed emergency managers from 23 Sept, as Ian formed.
  • Florida: a storm surge watch, then a warning, were issued for Fort Myers Beach 48 h and 30 h before tropical-storm winds arrived.
  • Cuba: Civil Defence moved 76,221 people out of danger, to government shelters or to friends and family.
  • Rescue: over 250 water rescues in Florida; the US Coast Guard received 16 live briefings from the NHC to support its rescues.
Mission 6

Long-term responses

  • USA: federal support passed $10.2 billion within two years.
  • $1.15 billion went to 387,139 households; flood insurance paid $4.67 billion to about 47,000 policyholders.
  • Cuba: the Red Cross appealed for 9 million Swiss francs to help 25,000 people, but received only 43% of it.
  • Many Cuban families rebuilt with salvaged materials; the Red Cross called for sturdier, longer-lasting shelter.
Quick check
?Which response to Ian was long term?
Mission 6

Reducing the effects: the 4 Ps

  • Monitoring: satellites, radar and aircraft that fly into storms; hurricane hunter aircraft found that Ian had strengthened.
  • Prediction: forecasts of the track and surge. The NHC's cone of uncertainty contained Ian's landfall point in every forecast.
  • Protection: strong buildings and sea defences that resist wind and water.
  • Planning: evacuation routes, shelters and rules on where to build.
  • Preparation: practice drills and emergency kits, so people act quickly when warned.
Mission 6

How well did it work for Ian?

  • Worked: at Fort Myers Beach, the surge watch and warning came 48 h and 30 h ahead; the final surge forecast, 3.7–5.5 m (12–18 ft), matched what happened, 3–4.6 m (10–15 ft).
  • Limits: the first surge forecast for Fort Myers Beach was 1.2–2.1 m (4–7 ft); it rose only as Ian's track shifted and the storm strengthened.
  • Intensity was harder to forecast than usual: Ian's second burst of strengthening, to 140 kt, was not predicted.
  • Exam move: evaluate by weighing what each P achieved against its limits, then reach a judgement.
Quick check
?Florida had far more money than Cuba, yet more people died there. Which reason is best supported by the evidence?
Mission check

?Two coasts are hit by equally strong cyclones. Coast X is wealthy, with good forecasts, but many people choose to stay in low-lying homes. Coast Y is poorer, but its civil defence moves everyone from low-lying areas to shelters before the storm. Which statement is most likely?

Cuba evacuated 76,221 people and lost 5 lives, but recovery money was short (43% of the Red Cross appeal). Florida had $10.2 billion of federal support, yet Ian hit it harder, at 130 kt, and 41 people drowned in the storm surge.

Specification: what this mission covers

Mission 7

Where coral reefs grow

  • Reef-building corals live in tropical and semi-tropical seas.
  • They cannot survive water below 18 °C.
  • The water must be clear, so sunlight reaches the algae living in the coral, and salty (32–42 parts per thousand).
  • Reefs cover only about 1% of the ocean, yet give habitat to at least 25% of marine life.
Mission 7

Where mangroves grow

  • Mangroves: salt-tolerant trees on muddy tropical and subtropical coasts, between low and high tide.
  • The world had 14.8 million hectares of mangroves in 2020.
  • Nearly 44% is in South and South-east Asia; Indonesia alone has 21%.
Mission 7

Why they matter

  • Coral reefs protect shorelines "by reducing the impact from waves, storms, and floods". They support fishing and tourism, and many medicines are being developed from reef animals and plants.
  • Mangroves store large amounts of carbon in their soils, protect coasts from storms and erosion, and support rich wildlife.
  • They also shelter young fish that later live on reefs.
Quick check
?Which is a benefit of mangroves to people on the coast?
Mission 7

Threats to reefs: heat

  • Coral bleaching: under stress, corals expel the algae (zooxanthellae) living in their tissues and turn white.
  • The leading cause is warmer water from climate change; runoff and pollution, strong sunlight and very low tides add stress.
  • From early 2023 to mid-2025, bleaching-level heat stress hit 84% of the world's coral reef area.
  • Bleached coral is not dead, but many corals die if the stress lasts.
Mission 7

Threats from people

  • Farming: fertiliser and soil wash into the sea, clouding the water and feeding algae. Poor water quality is a named threat to the Great Barrier Reef.
  • Deforestation: mangroves are cleared for fish and shrimp ponds, rice and oil palm. Forest cleared inland sends mud down rivers onto reefs.
  • Industrialisation: ports, dredging, factories and coastal building pollute water and bury habitat.
  • Tourism: hotels on the shore, sewage, boat anchors, and divers touching coral.
Quick check
?A new resort dredges a channel for boats, and its sewage is piped into the lagoon. Which threat is this?
Mission 7

Mangroves: losses and gains

  • 2000–2020: 677,000 ha of mangroves were lost, and 393,000 ha grew back, a net loss of 284,000 ha.
  • The biggest drivers of loss: aquaculture (fish and shrimp ponds) 27%, natural retreat 26%, other farming 12%, oil palm 8%, rice 8%.
  • The rate of net loss fell by 44% between 2000–10 and 2010–20.
Mission 7

Gazi Bay, Kenya: Mikoko Pamoja

  • "The world's first community-led blue carbon project": local people protect and replant mangroves.
  • The carbon the trees store is sold as carbon credits to buyers who want to balance out their own emissions.
  • 1,081 households take part.
  • The money "has enabled community development such as provision of schoolbooks, construction of school buildings and the provision of clean drinking water".
Mission 7

Zoning: the Great Barrier Reef

  • The Great Barrier Reef Marine Park, Australia, covers 344,400 km², with about 3,000 reefs.
  • Zoning divides it into areas where each activity is allowed, banned or needs a permit.
  • About 115,000 km² are "no-take" or "no-entry" zones: about a third of the park.
  • Fishing is banned in no-take zones, so fish can breed and spread to nearby areas.
Mission 7

Is the management sustainable?

  • Zoning: fishers lose some areas, but fish stocks and tourism can gain.
  • Mikoko Pamoja: local people gain, so they protect the forest. Limits: one small project, and income depends on buyers of credits.
  • Water quality must be managed on farms on land, outside the marine park.
  • Scale: no local plan can stop global ocean warming, the main threat to reefs.
  • Exam move: separate what local managers control (fishing, runoff, building) from what they cannot (ocean warming).
Quick check
?Which threat to the Great Barrier Reef can its managers not control by zoning?
Mission check

?A fishing village on another tropical coast wants to protect its mangroves but needs income. Which scheme follows the Gazi Bay model?

In Gazi Bay, buyers of carbon credits pay for the carbon that protected mangroves store. The village gains income for schools and clean water, so it has a reason to keep the trees.

Mission 8

Mission 8: A hurricane washed away Florida beaches in a day. Why do people keep paying to put the sand back?

Make a guess: A hurricane washed away Florida beaches in a day. Why do people keep paying to put the sand back?

Specification: what this mission covers

Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460)

  • 2.3.7 detailed example (named country or coastal area): causes and impacts of coastal erosion
  • 2.3.7 strategies protecting that coast from tropical storms and managing erosion, including sustainable
Mission 8

The named coast: Lee County, Florida

  • South-west Florida, on the Gulf coast: about 76 km (47.3 miles) of beaches.
  • Mostly barrier islands: Gasparilla, Cayo Costa, North Captiva, Captiva, Sanibel, Estero (Fort Myers Beach) and others, cut by eight inlets.
  • Exposed to tropical storms: Hurricane Charley (2004) and Hurricane Ian (2022) both came ashore here.
Mission 8

Causes of erosion in normal years

  • Waves carry sand offshore; longshore drift carries it along the islands. North Captiva's storm breach of 2004 later closed as drift moved sand southward.
  • Inlets trap sand that would feed the next beach, so some is dredged from inlets and moved back.
  • People: homes and roads on the islands; some shores are fixed by bulkheads (walls).
Mission 8

Causes: the storm

  • Ian struck on 28 September 2022 with winds of 130 kt (about 150 mph).
  • Storm surge raised water about 3–4.6 m (10–15 ft) above the ground at Fort Myers Beach: about a 1-in-100-year storm tide.
  • On top of the surge, waves attacked the dunes, which normal tides never reach.
Quick check
?Why did Hurricane Ian erode Lee County's beaches far more in one day than normal weather does in a year?
Mission 8

How much sand Ian took

  • Major beach and dune erosion along most of Lee County's beaches.
  • Sand lost above high-water mark (cubic yards, cy):
    • Captiva Island: 461,742 cy (17.8 cy per foot of shore)
    • Sanibel Island: 404,987 cy (6.3 cy per foot)
    • Estero Island: 301,423 cy (8.6 cy per foot)
  • Over 80 storm-surge gullies were cut across Sanibel's beach.
Mission 8

Impacts on people

  • In Lee County at least 52,514 buildings were affected and 5,369 destroyed.
  • Storm surge killed 41 people; 36 of them in Lee County.
  • The Sanibel causeway and bridges were destroyed, cutting off all road traffic to the island.
  • Lost beaches and dunes left homes and roads open to the next storm.
Mission 8

Long-term impact: critically eroded

  • Critically eroded: erosion so great that development, recreation, wildlife habitat or cultural sites are threatened or lost.
  • Lee County has 12 critically eroded beach areas, 25.4 miles in total.
  • All of Captiva Island is critically eroded.
  • In 2026 another stretch of Sanibel was added, because of storm erosion in 2022 and 2024.
Quick check
?Ian removed 461,742 cy from Captiva and 404,987 cy from Sanibel. Per foot of shoreline, Captiva lost 17.8 cy and Sanibel 6.3 cy. What does this show?
Mission 8

Managing erosion: beach nourishment

  • Beach nourishment: pumping sand onto a beach to rebuild it.
  • Captiva's beach was last nourished in 2021/22; Ian and Nicole then removed about 427,200 cy from the nourished project beach.
  • Florida's plan: nourish it again for about $13.2 million: about $31 per cubic yard.
  • State grants pay up to 75% of local beach projects.
Mission 8

Other erosion strategies

  • Hard engineering: a rock revetment and a groin protect the south shore of Redfish Pass; bulkheads line much of Gasparilla Island.
  • Recycling sand: sand dredged from Blind Pass channel (about 120,000 cy) was recommended to refill northern Sanibel.
  • Southern Sanibel had no engineered beach. For the whole island, the state recommended $23.8 million to restore beaches and dunes.
Mission 8

Protecting people from tropical storms

  • Warnings: the storm surge watch and warning for Fort Myers Beach came 48 and 30 hours before tropical-storm-force winds, so people could evacuate.
  • Planning: the Coastal Construction Control Line (CCCL) sets stricter siting and design rules for building near the beach, where storm forces are greatest.
  • Beaches and dunes are a buffer: their recovery is "vital" to protect development and infrastructure.
Mission 8

Sustainable management

  • Sustainable: protection that lasts, costs less over time and works with natural processes.
  • Dunes: CCCL permits also protect dune plants and sea turtles; plants trap sand and rebuild dunes.
  • Mangroves: a wide belt reduces wave and flood damage, and their roots stabilise the shore. Florida has about 600,000 acres of mangroves.
  • Restoring beaches and dunes also restores habitat for threatened species.
Quick check
?Which strategy for Lee County is the most sustainable in the long term?
Mission 8

Evaluation: does it work?

  • Nourishment keeps a buffer beach and tourism, but sand is lost again in storms: Captiva was nourished in 2021/22 and lost about 427,200 cy from the project beach in 2022. It must be paid for again and again.
  • Hard structures protect one spot but are fixed and costly.
  • Warnings save lives but not buildings: surge still killed 41 people.
  • Planning lines, dunes and mangroves are cheaper over time, but need space where people want to build.
  • Exam move: for a named example, give place names, dates and figures (Captiva, 2022, 461,742 cy).
Mission check

?A town on a sandy tropical island is hit by typhoons every few years. Its beach is narrowing and houses stand on the dunes. It can afford one long-term plan. Which plan best protects people from storms and manages erosion sustainably?

In Lee County, Hurricane Ian stripped 461,742 cy from Captiva alone, and nourishment bought back the beach at about $13.2 million. Planning lines, dunes and mangroves, plus warnings, protect people and the coast for longer.

Mission 9

Mission 9: Can local rules protect the Great Barrier Reef from threats that start far away?

Make a guess: Can local rules protect the Great Barrier Reef from threats that start far away?

Specification: what this mission covers

Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460)

  • 2.3.8 detailed example (named country or coastal area): why the coral reef is important
  • 2.3.8 threats to that coral reef
  • 2.3.8 strategies and techniques to protect and manage that reef, including sustainable ones
Mission 9

The Great Barrier Reef

  • Where: off the coast of Queensland, north-east Australia.
  • The Marine Park covers 344,400 km², with about 3,000 coral reefs.
  • A World Heritage site since 1981.
  • Managed by the Great Barrier Reef Marine Park Authority (GBRMPA), working with the Queensland Government.
Mission 9

Why it matters

  • Economy: worth about A$9 billion a year to Australia and 77,000 full-time jobs, mostly in tourism.
  • Coastal protection: reefs reduce the impact of waves, storms and floods on the shore.
  • Biodiversity: reefs give habitat to at least 25% of marine life while covering about 1% of the ocean.
  • People and culture: more than 70 Traditional Owner groups have connections to the reef.
  • Science and medicine: many drugs are being developed from reef animals and plants.
Quick check
?A hotel owner in Cairns says the reef matters "only to divers". Which fact best challenges this?
Mission 9

Threat 1: ocean heat and bleaching

  • Warmer water makes corals expel their algae and turn white: bleaching.
  • In 2024 the reef had its fifth mass bleaching event since 2016; it had "the largest spatial footprint ever recorded".
  • In 2025 a sixth mass bleaching event followed.
  • Hard coral cover in the northern reef fell from 39.8% (2024) to 30.0% (2025), "the largest annual decline recorded for this region since monitoring began".
  • GBRMPA: the outlook is "future deterioration due largely to climate change".
Mission 9

Threat 2: water from the land

  • 35 major river catchments drain into the reef.
  • Grazing land (cattle) is the main source of fine sediment; sugarcane is the main source of excess nutrients and pesticides.
  • Sediment blocks light. Nutrients "promote excess growth of algae, which competes with coral for space".
  • Flood plumes reach reefs far offshore after heavy rain, as in the 2019 Townsville floods.
Mission 9

Threat 3: starfish and fishing

  • Crown-of-thorns starfish eat coral. Extra nutrients from the land can make their outbreaks worse.
  • GBRMPA names "crown-of-thorns starfish outbreaks, poor water quality and some fishing activities" as key pressures.
  • "Fishing activities continue to have a high impact" on the reef's values.
  • Ports, towns, sewage works and industry also add pollutants.
Quick check
?Which threat to the Great Barrier Reef starts furthest from the reef itself?
Mission 9

Strategy 1: zoning

  • The Marine Park is divided into zones: each says which activities are allowed, banned or need a permit.
  • About 115,000 km² are "no-take" or "no-entry" zones: about a third of the Park.
  • No fishing in no-take zones, so fish can breed and spread to nearby areas.
  • Tourist operators need permits, so numbers and activities at busy sites can be controlled.
Mission 9

Strategy 2: tackling local threats

  • Starfish control: divers cull crown-of-thorns starfish on high-value reefs. "Well-targeted culling … can promote resilience".
  • Water quality: farms change how they use fertiliser and manage soil. "Meeting water quality improvement targets is a crucial step". Since 2019 water quality "has continued to improve slowly".
  • Traditional Owners: "Strengthening Traditional Owner involvement in the management of Sea Country is vital".
  • Reef 2050 Plan: a long-term plan joining up the Australian and Queensland governments' work.
Mission 9

Is it sustainable?

  • Sustainable management meets people's needs today (jobs, fishing, tourism) while keeping the reef for the future.
  • Zoning: fishers lose some areas, but fish stocks and tourism can gain.
  • Water quality: needs farmers on land to change, outside the Marine Park.
  • Culling: works reef by reef, and must keep going while outbreaks continue.
  • The limit: GBRMPA says the reef is "well-managed overall", yet the outlook is still deterioration because of climate change. Only global cuts in emissions can tackle that.
Quick check
?Why do the reef's managers say it is well managed, yet its outlook is still poor?
Mission 9

Exam move: judge a strategy

  • Name the strategy and the threat it targets.
  • Say who gains and who loses (fishers, farmers, tourism firms, Traditional Owners).
  • Say whether it is sustainable: does it keep the reef working for the future?
  • Say what it cannot fix (ocean warming).
Mission check

?A reef in another tropical country suffers farm runoff, overfishing and repeated bleaching in marine heatwaves. A new local plan adds no-take zones and fertiliser limits. Which judgement is best?

Local rules (zoning, culling, cleaner farm runoff) can manage threats that start on the reef or on nearby land, and they work best when local people gain. But no local rule can stop ocean warming, which starts with emissions around the world.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Destructive waves erode and constructive waves build. Erosion and slumping wear cliffs back; longshore drift moves the sediment along the coast.
  • Rock type sets the pace: weak till retreats fast; chalk resists.

Mission 2

  • Discordant (bands at right angles) → headlands and bays; concordant (bands parallel) → straight coast, coves if breached (Lulworth).
  • Refraction focuses energy on headlands (erosional landforms) and spreads it in bays (beaches).

Mission 3

  • Hard rock makes headlands; soft rock makes bays. Headlands are eroded along joints: cave, arch, stack, stump.
  • Where waves lose energy, sediment builds beaches; where the coast turns, it builds spits.
  • A spit that crosses a bay becomes a bar.

Mission 4

  • Hard engineering holds the line but is costly and can harm the coast down-drift; soft engineering works with natural processes.
  • Medmerry chose managed retreat: cheaper and lower-risk than holding a failing bank, with habitat gained and land lost.

Mission 5

  • Tropical storms form over warm tropical oceans between about 25°S and 25°N, but not near the equator, where there is too little spin.
  • On low coasts, storm surge is usually the deadliest hazard: in Ian, 41 of 66 direct US deaths.

Mission 6

  • Primary effects come from the storm itself (surge, wind, rain); secondary effects follow (power cuts, lost harvests, indirect deaths).
  • Immediate responses save lives (warnings, evacuation, rescue); long-term responses rebuild (aid, insurance, reconstruction). Wealth speeds recovery more than it prevents deaths.
  • Monitoring, prediction, protection and planning reduce effects, but forecasts of rapid strengthening are still uncertain.

Mission 7

  • Coral reefs (clear, warm, shallow tropical seas) and mangroves (tropical muddy coasts) protect coasts and support fish, but face warming, farming, deforestation, industry and tourism.
  • Management works best when local people gain (Mikoko Pamoja), but no local plan can stop global warming. Zoning controls where each activity is allowed.

Mission 8

  • Causes and impacts: Lee County's barrier islands erode by waves, drift and inlets; Hurricane Ian (2022) stripped beaches and dunes (Captiva 461,742 cy), destroyed 5,369 buildings and the Sanibel causeway, and left 25.4 miles critically eroded.
  • Strategies: nourishment, revetments and recycled inlet sand manage erosion; warnings, the CCCL, dunes and mangroves protect from storms; natural buffers and planning are the most sustainable.

Mission 9

  • The Great Barrier Reef: 344,400 km² Marine Park off Queensland; worth about $9 billion a year and 77,000 jobs; World Heritage since 1981.
  • Threats: ocean heat (six mass bleaching events since 2016), sediment and nutrients from farms, crown-of-thorns starfish, fishing.
  • Strategies: zoning and permits, starfish culling, water-quality targets for farms, Traditional Owner management, the Reef 2050 Plan.
  • Local management is judged sustainable when it keeps livelihoods and the reef, but it cannot stop climate change.
