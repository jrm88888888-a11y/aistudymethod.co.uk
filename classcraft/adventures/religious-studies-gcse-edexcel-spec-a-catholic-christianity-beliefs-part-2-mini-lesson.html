Mission 1

Mission 1: Christians say a dead man left his tomb alive. Why is their whole faith built on that claim?

Make a guess: Christians say a dead man left his tomb alive. Why is their whole faith built on that claim?

Mission 1

What "resurrection" means

  • Christians believe that on the third day after his death God raised Jesus from the dead.
  • Not a ghost: in the Gospels the risen Jesus can be touched and eats food (Luke 24:39–43).
  • Not a resuscitation: Lazarus came back to ordinary life and would die again. Jesus was raised to a new, transformed life that cannot die (Romans 6:9).
  • The same Jesus, still bearing the wounds of the cross, but not always recognised at first.
  • Easter Sunday, the most important Christian festival, celebrates it.
Mission 1Read the source

The empty tomb (Luke 24)

  • At dawn on the first day of the week, women bring spices to the tomb. The stone is rolled away; the body is not there (24:1–3).
  • Two men in dazzling clothes ask: "Why do you seek the living amongst the dead? He isn't here, but is risen." (24:5–6)
  • They remind the women that Jesus said he would rise on the third day; the women remember (24:6–8) and tell the eleven (24:9).
  • The apostles think it is nonsense; Peter runs, sees the linen cloths, and goes home amazed (24:11–12).

Mission 1

The appearances

  • On the road to Emmaus: two disciples walk with a stranger; they recognise Jesus when he breaks bread (Luke 24:13–35).
  • To the disciples in Jerusalem: "See my hands and my feet… a spirit doesn't have flesh and bones" (Luke 24:39). He eats fish.
  • Thomas doubts until he sees the wounds: "My Lord and my God!" (John 20:28)
  • In Galilee: Jesus sends the eleven out to the world (Matthew 28:16–20).
  • Paul lists appearances to Peter, the Twelve, more than 500 at once, James, and finally himself (1 Corinthians 15:5–8).

The ascension (Luke 24)

  • Jesus leads the disciples out as far as Bethany and lifts up his hands to bless them.
  • "While he blessed them, he withdrew from them and was carried up into heaven." (Luke 24:51)
  • They worship him and return to Jerusalem "with great joy", praising God in the Temple (24:52–53).

Show answer

Mission 1

What the ascension means

  • Jesus' earthly appearances end; he returns to the Father.
  • The Creed: he "sitteth on the right hand of God the Father Almighty": a picture of authority, not a place in the sky.
  • His human nature is taken into heaven: Christians see a pledge that humans can be with God.
  • He sends the Holy Spirit, so he is present everywhere, not in one place (John 16:7).
  • He will come again in glory (Acts 1:11).
  • Many Christians read "up" and "cloud" as signs of entering God's presence; others take the account more literally. Ascension Day is the Thursday 40 days after Easter.

Mission 1

The Paschal Mystery

  • Pascha = Passover. The Paschal Mystery is the Catholic name for Jesus' life, death, resurrection and ascension seen as one saving event.
  • Redemptive efficacy: these events do not just teach about salvation; Catholics believe they actually bring it about (redemption).
  • By dying, Christ destroyed death; by rising, he restored life (a theme of the Easter Mass, paraphrase).
  • Catholics believe the Paschal Mystery is made present in the Mass, and celebrated above all at the Easter Triduum (Holy Thursday to Easter Sunday).
  • Significance today: baptism as dying and rising with Christ (Romans 6:4); hope at funerals; Sunday Mass as a "little Easter".
Mission 1

Why it matters

  • Who Jesus is: Christians believe the resurrection shows God's approval of Jesus: he is "declared to be the Son of God with power" (Romans 1:4).
  • Victory: Christians believe sin and death are defeated.
  • Hope: Christ is the "first fruit": believers will also be raised (1 Corinthians 15:20).
  • Worship: Christians meet on Sunday, the day of resurrection; Easter is the high point of the year.
  • Life: courage, forgiveness and joy; the first disciples went from hiding to preaching.
Mission 1Evaluate

"Without the resurrection, Christianity would have no meaning."

  • Reasons for: Paul says faith would be "in vain" (1 Corinthians 15:14). The first Christian preaching centres on it (Acts 2:32). Christian hope of eternal life depends on it.
  • Reasons against: Jesus' teaching on love has value in itself; many non-Christians admire it. The Incarnation and the cross also carry meaning. Some liberal Christians, who believe Jesus is alive with God without an empty tomb, find deep meaning in a spiritual reading.
  • Another view: the resurrection gives meaning to the rest, but only together with Jesus' life and death.
  • Judge it by: What did the first Christians say mattered most? Can "Christianity" survive without a risen Jesus? Does "meaning" here mean truth, hope or ethics?
Mission check

Not a ghost and not a return to old life: Christians believe Jesus was raised bodily to a life beyond death, and then ascended to the Father.

Mission 2: Christians say Jesus' death puts people right with God. How can one death do that?

Mission 2

Saved from what?

  • Salvation: being saved from sin and its effects, to live with God for ever (eternal life).
  • Sin is anything that goes against God's will. It damages the relationship with God.
Mission 2

Law, grace and Spirit

  • Law: God's commands, such as the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20). Keeping them is part of a holy life.
  • But no one keeps the law perfectly, so most Christians believe law alone cannot save.
  • Grace: God's free, undeserved love and help. It cannot be earned.
  • "For by grace you have been saved through faith, and that not of yourselves; it is the gift of God, not of works, that no one would boast." (Ephesians 2:8–9)
  • Spirit: Christians believe the Holy Spirit lives in believers, giving grace and helping them grow more like Jesus.
Mission 2

Atonement: making "at one"

  • Atonement: sin is dealt with and the broken relationship is repaired: people are made "at one" with God.
  • Christians believe Jesus, fully God and fully human, did this by his death on the cross.
  • Forgiveness: Christians believe that through Jesus, God forgives the sins of all who repent (turn back).

Mission 2

The whole story saves

  • Christians (Catholics call it the Paschal Mystery) see Jesus' life, death, resurrection and ascension as one saving work: redemption (being bought back, set free).
  • Life: in the Incarnation God shares human life; Jesus lives in perfect obedience and shows God's love.
  • Death: Jesus gives his life for others; Christians believe it takes away sin.
  • Resurrection: Jesus defeats death; believers are promised new life (see Mission 1).
  • Ascension: Jesus returns to the Father, and the Spirit is sent to the Church.
Mission 2Read the source

Jesus and Nicodemus (John 3:10–21)

  • Context: Nicodemus, a Jewish teacher, visits Jesus by night. Jesus says he must be "born again" (from above) by the Spirit (John 3:3–8).
  • Jesus says the Son of Man must be "lifted up", like the bronze snake Moses lifted up to heal the people (John 3:14; Numbers 21:9).
  • "For God so loved the world, that he gave his only born Son, that whoever believes in him should not perish, but have eternal life." (John 3:16)
  • "For God didn't send his Son into the world to judge the world, but that the world should be saved through him." (John 3:17)
  • Those who refuse to believe have chosen darkness instead of light (John 3:18–21, paraphrase).

Q2 What does the healing show?

Show answer

The power of the risen Jesus, working through the apostles.

  • Catholic teaching reads this as: all salvation comes through Christ, even for those who have not heard of him.

Mission 2

Pictures of the cross

  • No single picture says it all. Christians use several theories of atonement, side by side.
  • Sacrifice: as animals were offered in the Temple for sin, Jesus offers himself, the Lamb of God (John 1:29), once for all.
  • Substitution: Jesus takes the punishment sinners deserve, in their place. Stressed by many evangelical Protestants.
  • Ransom: "For the Son of Man also came not to be served, but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many." (Mark 10:45)
  • Reconciliation / example: the cross shows God's love so fully that it wins people back and inspires them to love.
  • Most Christians hold several of these together.

Mission 2Diversity

Saved when? Saved how?

  • Many evangelical Protestants: saved by faith alone (Luther, 1517 onwards). A moment of conversion ("born again") brings assurance. Good works are the fruit of faith, not its cause.
  • Catholics: saved by grace, received in faith and lived out in love and good works; grace comes especially through the sacraments (baptism, Mass, reconciliation). "Faith, if it has no works, is dead in itself." (James 2:17)
  • Orthodox: salvation is a lifelong journey of becoming more like God (theosis).

Mission 2Evaluate

"Faith in Jesus is all a person needs to be saved."

  • Reasons for: "By grace you have been saved through faith … not of works" (Ephesians 2:8–9). The criminal crucified beside Jesus was promised paradise with no time for good works (Luke 23:43). The 39 Articles (XI) teach justification "by Faith, and not for our own works". Grace that had to be earned would not be grace.
  • Reasons against: "Faith, if it has no works, is dead" (James 2:17). In Matthew 25:31–46 people are judged by how they treated the hungry and the stranger. Catholics stress the sacraments, especially baptism (John 3:5). Faith that does not change how someone lives may not be real faith.
  • Another view: many Christians say the question is badly posed: real faith always shows itself in love, so faith and works cannot be separated.
  • Judge it by: What does "faith" mean: belief, or trust that changes a life? Which texts carry most weight, and why? Can a person earn what is a gift?

Mission 3

Redemption and the Paschal Mystery

  • Redemption: "buying back" or setting free. Jesus came "to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45).
  • Salvation: being saved from sin and death, for eternal life with God.
  • Grace: God's free, undeserved gift of his own life and help.
  • Pascha = Passover. The Paschal Mystery: Jesus' life, death, resurrection and ascension seen as one saving work.
  • Redemptive efficacy: Catholics believe these events actually bring about salvation, not just teach about it.
  • Catholics believe the Paschal Mystery is made present at every Mass.
Mission 3

Life: the whole of Jesus' life saves

  • Catholic teaching: Jesus' whole life is part of redemption, not only its end (CCC 517, paraphrase).
  • Adam's disobedience is undone by Christ's obedience: "as through the one man's disobedience many were made sinners, even so through the obedience of the one, many will be made righteous" (Romans 5:19).
  • His healing, forgiving and welcoming sinners show what salvation looks like.
  • His Incarnation joins God and humanity: what was broken begins to be restored.
  • Significance: Catholics follow Jesus' life as a pattern for their own (prayer, service, forgiveness).
Mission 3

Death: restoration through sacrifice

  • A sacrifice is an offering to God. Jesus offers his whole self in love and obedience.
  • "Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!" (John 1:29): like the Passover lamb, his death brings freedom.
  • At the Last Supper: "This cup is the new covenant in my blood, which is poured out for you." (Luke 22:20)
  • Significance: the Mass makes this one sacrifice present (it is not repeated); the crucifix keeps it before Catholics' eyes.
Mission 3

Resurrection: restoration through recreation

  • The Gospels say Jesus rose on the first day of the week: a new first day, as in Genesis 1.
  • Catholics believe his risen body is real but transformed: the start of a new creation.
  • "If anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation. The old things have passed away. Behold, all things have become new." (2 Corinthians 5:17)
  • Significance: in baptism Catholics die and rise with Christ (Romans 6:4); hope of resurrection at every funeral; Sunday as a weekly Easter.

Mission 3

Ascension and Pentecost: restoration of the cosmic order

  • "As they were looking, he was taken up, and a cloud received him out of their sight." (Acts 1:9) Angels promise he "will come back in the same way" (Acts 1:11).
  • Catholics believe Jesus' risen humanity enters God's presence: heaven and earth are joined; Christ reigns over all creation.
  • Pentecost (Acts 2:1–4): wind and tongues of fire; "They were all filled with the Holy Spirit" (Acts 2:4). The Spirit makes Christ's saving work available to all people.
  • Significance: Christians are sent on mission; Christ's return is awaited in hope.
Mission 3Read the source

Luke 24

  • On the road to Emmaus the risen Jesus asks: "Didn't the Christ have to suffer these things and to enter into his glory?" (Luke 24:26)
  • He is recognised when he breaks bread (24:30–35, paraphrase), a link Catholics see with the Mass.
  • His summary: the Christ must suffer and rise on the third day, and "repentance and remission of sins should be preached in his name to all the nations" (Luke 24:46–47).
  • "While he blessed them, he withdrew from them and was carried up into heaven." (Luke 24:51)

Mission 3

Salvation, grace and freedom

  • "For by grace you have been saved through faith, and that not of yourselves; it is the gift of God" (Ephesians 2:8).
  • Grace comes first: no one can earn salvation.
  • Freedom: God does not force anyone. Catholics believe grace invites a free response of faith, shown in love and good works: "faith, if it has no works, is dead in itself" (James 2:17).
  • Grace is given especially through the sacraments: baptism, Eucharist, reconciliation.
  • Other Christians: many Protestants stress salvation by grace through faith alone (sola fide), while agreeing that true faith bears fruit. In 1999 Catholics and Lutherans signed a Joint Declaration agreeing on much of this.

Mission 3Evaluate

"Jesus' death is the most important part of the Paschal Mystery."

  • Reasons for: "to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45); the cross is Christianity's central symbol; the Mass makes Christ's sacrifice present; Paul: "Christ died for our sins" (1 Corinthians 15:3).
  • Reasons against: "If Christ has not been raised, then our preaching is in vain" (1 Corinthians 15:14); the resurrection begins the new creation; the ascension and Pentecost bring salvation to all; Orthodox worship centres on Easter.
  • Another view: Catholic teaching calls it one mystery: each event makes sense only with the others.
  • Judge it by: Could any event save without the others? What does scripture stress? What do Catholic worship and the creeds stress?

Mission 4

Grace: a gift, not a wage

  • Salvation: being rescued from sin and death and brought into God's life for ever.
  • Catholics believe it comes through the whole Paschal Mystery: Jesus' life, death, resurrection and ascension (see Mission 3).
  • Grace: God's free, undeserved gift of his own life and help. No one can earn it.
  • Sanctifying grace: a lasting share in God's life, first given in baptism; it makes a person holy.
  • Actual grace: God's help at particular moments, to choose good and turn back to God.
  • Grace comes especially through the sacraments, and also through prayer, Scripture and other people.
  • So Catholics say: I have been saved (baptism), I am being saved (living by grace), I hope to be saved (at the end).

John 3 and Acts 4, read by Catholics

  • "For God so loved the world, that he gave his only born Son, that whoever believes in him should not perish, but have eternal life." (John 3:16)
  • Peter, of the risen Jesus: "There is salvation in no one else, for there is no other name under heaven that is given amongst men, by which we must be saved!" (Acts 4:12)
  • Catholic reading: salvation is God's loving gift (grace), won by Jesus' death ("lifted up", John 3:14) and resurrection (Acts 4:10).
  • Practice: "water and Spirit" is read as baptism, so Catholics baptise infants; grace is renewed at Mass and in reconciliation; Acts 4:12 drives mission.
  • All salvation comes through Christ, even for people who have never heard of him and seek God sincerely (CCC 846–847, paraphrase).

Mission 4Worked answer

Two beliefs, one source

Question: Explain two Catholic beliefs about salvation. In your answer you must refer to a source of wisdom and authority. (5)

  • Belief 1: salvation is a gift of God's love, not earned. Source: "God so loved the world, that he gave his only born Son" (John 3:16). Development: so Catholics see grace as undeserved and respond with thanks, above all at Mass.
  • Belief 2: salvation comes only through Jesus. Source: "There is salvation in no one else" (Acts 4:12). Development: so Catholics share the Gospel and baptise, because Jesus' death and resurrection are the way to God.

Mission 5

Death is not the end

  • Christians believe in eternal life: life with God that death cannot end.
  • The reason: Jesus rose from the dead (see Mission 1). Christians believe his resurrection is the first of many.
  • The creeds: "I believe in … the resurrection of the body, and the life everlasting" (Apostles' Creed).
  • Belief about the future shapes life now: hope, comfort and responsibility.
Mission 5Read the source

"I am the resurrection" (John 11)

  • Context: Jesus' friend Lazarus has been dead four days. His sister Martha meets Jesus (John 11:17–20).
  • Martha believes Lazarus will rise "in the resurrection at the last day" (John 11:24).
  • "I am the resurrection and the life. He who believes in me will still live, even if he dies." (John 11:25)
  • "Whoever lives and believes in me will never die." (John 11:26)
  • Martha replies: "I have come to believe that you are the Christ, God's Son" (John 11:27). Jesus then raises Lazarus.

Mission 5Read the source

A new kind of body

  • "The body is sown perishable; it is raised imperishable." (1 Corinthians 15:42)
  • "It is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body." (1 Corinthians 15:44)
  • Like a seed and the plant it becomes: the same person, transformed (1 Corinthians 15:36–38).
  • 2 Corinthians 5:1–10: our body is an "earthly … tent"; God gives "a building … eternal, in the heavens" (5:1). Paul would rather be "at home with the Lord" (5:8). All must appear "before the judgement seat of Christ" (5:10).
  • Q: What does "spiritual body" mean here? (A real, transformed body raised by God's Spirit, not a ghost.)
Mission 5

Judgement

  • Christians believe all people will be judged by God through Christ.
  • Catholic teaching: a particular judgement at death, and the Last (final) Judgement at the end of time.
  • Matthew 25:31–46: the sheep and the goats: people are judged by how they treated the hungry, thirsty, stranger, sick and prisoner.
  • Most Christians hold that judgement looks at faith and how it was lived; they differ on the balance (see Mission 2).

Mission 5

Heaven and hell

  • Heaven: being with God for ever in perfect happiness. Catholics call it the beatific vision: seeing God "face to face" (1 Corinthians 13:12).
  • Hell: being cut off from God for ever.
  • "These will go away into eternal punishment, but the righteous into eternal life." (Matthew 25:46)
  • Catholic teaching: hell is a state of self-exclusion: a person's own final choice to refuse God's love. God predestines no one to hell (CCC 1033, 1037).
Mission 5

Purgatory

  • Catholic teaching: purgatory is a state of purification after death for those who die in God's grace but are not yet fully holy. All in purgatory will reach heaven.
  • Catholics pray for the dead and offer Mass for them (2 Maccabees 12:46; 1 Corinthians 3:15 "saved, but as through fire").
  • Most Protestants reject purgatory: Jesus' death is enough; scripture does not teach it.
  • The 39 Articles of the Church of England (Article XXII) call it "a fond thing vainly invented, and grounded upon no warranty of Scripture".
  • Orthodox Christians pray for the dead, but do not define purgatory as Catholics do.
  • On the nature of resurrection, the 39 Articles (Article IV) stress that Christ rose with his real body, "with flesh, bones"; Catholics also teach a real, bodily resurrection of all at the end.
Mission 5Diversity

Traditional and contemporary views

  • Traditional: heaven and hell as places, often pictured as paradise above and fire below; hell is eternal punishment (Matthew 25:46).
  • Contemporary (many Catholics and Protestants): heaven and hell as states of being with or apart from God; fire is a picture, not a literal flame. Pope John Paul II taught this in 1999 talks.
  • Resurrection: most Christians expect a bodily resurrection at the end of time; some liberal Christians speak of the soul living on with God.

Mission 5

Why it matters now

  • Hope and comfort: death is not the final word; Christian funerals speak of "sure and certain hope of the resurrection" (Book of Common Prayer).
  • Responsibility: judgement means how people live matters (Matthew 25).
  • Value of life: each person is made for eternal life with God.
  • Catholic practice: prayers and Masses for the dead; All Souls' Day (2 November); the last rites prepare the dying.
  • Some Christians warn against being good only to reach heaven: love should be its own reason.
Mission 5Evaluate

"A loving God would not send anyone to hell."

  • Reasons for: "God is love" (1 John 4:8); God "desires all people to be saved" (1 Timothy 2:4). Eternal punishment for limited wrong seems unjust. Universalists and some theologians hope all will be saved.
  • Reasons against: Jesus speaks of eternal punishment (Matthew 25:46; Luke 16). Love respects free will: people may finally refuse God. Catholic teaching: God sends no one; hell is self-exclusion (CCC 1033). Justice for victims requires that evil be judged.
  • Another view: annihilation: the unsaved cease to exist, with no endless suffering.
  • Judge it by: Does "send" describe what hell is? How should Jesus' warnings be read: literally or as pictures? Can love and justice both be fully satisfied?
Mission check

Christians share hope in resurrection, judgement and eternal life with God. They differ on purgatory, on what heaven and hell are like, and on who will finally be saved.

Mission 6

The four last things

  • Catholic teaching names four last things: death, judgement, heaven, hell.
  • Death: the end of earthly life; the soul is separated from the body. It is the end of the time in which a person can choose for or against God.
  • Judgement: each life is seen in the light of Christ.
  • Heaven: eternal life with God.
  • Hell: eternal separation from God.
  • Purgatory is not one of the four: it is a stage on the way to heaven.
  • The hope behind them: Jesus' resurrection (see Mission 5). Death is real, but not the last word.
Mission 6

Two judgements

  • Particular judgement: at the moment of death, each soul is judged by Christ, and goes to heaven (perhaps through purgatory) or to hell (CCC 1021–1022, paraphrase).
  • Final (Last) judgement: at the end of time, when Christ returns (the Parousia). The dead are raised; all humanity is judged together, body and soul (CCC 1038–1041, paraphrase).
  • "We must all be revealed before the judgement seat of Christ" (2 Corinthians 5:10).
  • Why two? The particular judgement concerns the individual. The final judgement makes public the meaning of all of history, and includes the body, and every effect a life has had on others.
  • The final judgement does not overturn the first; it completes it.

Mission 6Read the source

"Saved, but as through fire"

"If any man's work is burnt, he will suffer loss, but he himself will be saved, but as through fire." (1 Corinthians 3:15)

  • Paul pictures each life as a building on the foundation of Christ (3:11). Some materials survive the "fire" of the Day; some burn (3:12–14).
  • Catholic reading: some people die in God's friendship but are not yet fully purified. Purgatory is their final cleansing before heaven (CCC 1030–1031, paraphrase).
  • Purgatory is not a second chance and not a lesser hell: all in purgatory are saved.
  • The living can help by prayer and Masses for the dead (2 Maccabees 12:45;).

Mission 6

Resurrection of the body, not just a surviving soul

  • "The body is sown perishable; it is raised imperishable. … It is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body." (1 Corinthians 15:42, 44)
  • Catholics believe the soul survives death; but the full hope is the resurrection of the body: the whole person, body and soul, reunited and transformed at the end of time, like the risen Jesus.
  • Paul: our earthly "tent" will be replaced by "a building from God, a house not made with hands, eternal, in the heavens" (2 Corinthians 5:1); he would rather be "at home with the Lord" (5:8).
  • Jesus to Martha: "I am the resurrection and the life" (John 11:25;).
  • Significance: the body matters (care for the sick, respect for the dead, burial).
Mission 6

Heaven and hell

  • Heaven: perfect, eternal union with God, seeing God "face to face" (1 Corinthians 13:12): the beatific vision.
  • Not a place in the sky, but a state of complete happiness and love with God, Mary and the saints (CCC 1023–1029, paraphrase).
  • Hell: eternal self-exclusion from communion with God, by freely and finally refusing God's love (CCC 1033, paraphrase).
  • Its chief pain is separation from God, for whom humans were made (CCC 1035, paraphrase).
  • Jesus warns: "These will go away into eternal punishment, but the righteous into eternal life" (Matthew 25:46).
Mission 6Diversity

Christians disagree

  • Catholic: particular and final judgement; purgatory; resurrection of a transformed body; prayer for the dead.
  • Orthodox: prays for the dead and believes in a state before the final judgement, but does not define purgatory as Catholics do.
  • Protestant: most reject purgatory (Church of England's 39 Articles, Article 22: a "fond thing vainly invented"); many believe the saved go straight to be "at home with the Lord" (2 Corinthians 5:8).
  • Nature of resurrection: most Christians: a real, transformed body. Some liberal Christians: a "spiritual body" (1 Corinthians 15:44) means a new, non-physical existence.
  • Hell: traditional eternal hell; some evangelicals: the lost cease to exist (annihilation); some Christians: hell as separation chosen, not torment; a few: all will finally be saved (universalism).
Mission check

?Sam says: "If my nan was judged by Christ the moment she died, a Last Judgement at the end of time is pointless. Her verdict is already known." Which reply best reflects Catholic teaching?

Death, judgement, heaven and hell frame Catholic hope: each life judged at death, all creation renewed at the end, with purgatory and prayer in between.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Christians believe God raised Jesus bodily on the third day: the empty tomb and the appearances.
  • Christians believe the resurrection shows who Jesus is and gives hope that death is defeated (1 Corinthians 15).
  • At the ascension Jesus returns to the Father, promises the Spirit and will come again.
  • The Paschal Mystery: Jesus' life, death, resurrection and ascension as one saving event.

Mission 2

  • For Christians, salvation is being saved from sin to eternal life with God; atonement is the repairing of the relationship through Jesus.
  • Christians believe law shows sin, grace is God's free, undeserved love, and the Spirit helps believers live it out.
  • Christians picture the cross as sacrifice, ransom and reconciliation; Catholics and many Protestants differ on how faith and works relate.

Mission 3

  • The Paschal Mystery is Jesus' life, death, resurrection and ascension as one saving work; Catholics believe it is made present at Mass and in the sacraments.
  • Salvation is God's gift of grace, freely accepted in faith shown in love.

Mission 4

  • Grace is God's free gift of his life and help; Catholics receive it especially through the sacraments.
  • Salvation is past (baptism), present (living by grace, with real freedom) and future (completed at the end).

Mission 5

  • Because Jesus rose, Christians believe in resurrection, judgement, and heaven or hell; 1 Corinthians 15 describes a transformed "spiritual body".
  • Catholics believe in purgatory and pray for the dead; most Protestants reject it (39 Articles, XXII).
  • Views differ: places or states; eternal punishment, annihilation or universal salvation.

Mission 6

  • Four last things: death, judgement, heaven, hell. Particular judgement at death; final judgement at Christ's return, when bodies rise.
  • Purgatory (1 Corinthians 3:15): final purification of the saved, helped by prayer.
