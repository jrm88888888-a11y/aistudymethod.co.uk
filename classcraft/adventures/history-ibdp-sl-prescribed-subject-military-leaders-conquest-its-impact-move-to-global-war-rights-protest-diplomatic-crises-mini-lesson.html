Prescribed Subject (Paper 1)

This mini-lesson covers Paper 1, the source-based paper. You study one prescribed subject chosen from five, and Paper 1 tests how well you can analyse historical sources. You will study one of these in depth, but this overview equips you for whichever your class chose.

You will meet the four question types, the skills of OPVL and cross-referencing, and a worked example built around The Move to Global War. Answer each check to collect ⭐ stars, then press Start.

Paper 1 · overview

What Paper 1 actually is

Paper 1 is the source paper, sat by both SL and HL candidates. It lasts 1 hour (plus 5 minutes reading) and is marked out of 24. You are given a small booklet of four sources — some written, usually one visual (a cartoon, photograph or map) — all on a single narrow topic.

You do not need to memorise these sources. Instead you must read, interpret, evaluate and cross-reference them, and bring in your own knowledge. Paper 1 rewards source skills, not just recall.

Paper 1 · the five options

The five prescribed subjects

You choose one prescribed subject. Each contains two case studies, and you must study both. The five are:

  • Military leaders — Genghis Khan and the Mongols, and Richard I of England.
  • Conquest and its impact — the Spanish conquest of Mexico and Peru and its consequences.
  • The Move to Global War — Japanese, and German and Italian, expansion in the 1930s.
  • Rights and protest — the US civil rights movement and apartheid South Africa.
  • Conflict and intervention — Rwanda (1990s) and Kosovo (1990s).

Note: there is no prescribed subject called "diplomatic crises" — a common distractor. The fifth option is Conflict and intervention.

Paper 1 · question types

The four questions and their marks

The four questions always follow the same shape:

  • Question 1 (5 marks) — comprehension: (a) what a source says (3), (b) the message of a visual source (2).
  • Question 2 (4 marks) — value and limitations of one source, with reference to its origin, purpose and content.
  • Question 3 (6 marks) — compare and contrast what two sources reveal.
  • Question 4 (9 marks) — a mini-essay using all four sources and your own knowledge.

Manage your time by the marks: Question 4 is worth the most, so leave it room.

Skill · OPVL

Origin, Purpose, Value, Limitations

The heart of source analysis is OPVL. For any source you ask:

  • Origin — who produced it, when, where, and in what form?
  • Purpose — why was it created, and for whom?
  • Value — what makes it useful to a historian studying this question?
  • Limitations — what can it not reliably tell us (bias, missing context, hindsight)?

Exam craft: tie value and limitations back to origin and purpose. "Because a government minister wrote this speech to reassure the public, it is valuable evidence of official messaging, but limited as a guide to what really happened."

Skill · cross-referencing

Comparing sources and the mini-essay

Question 3 asks you to compare and contrast. Do both: find where two sources agree and where they diverge, and quote or paraphrase precisely. A running comparison ("Whereas Source B argues..., Source C instead suggests...") scores better than dealing with each source separately.

Question 4 is a short essay. Group the sources into an argument, weave in your own knowledge, and reach a supported judgement. Reference sources explicitly (Source A, Source D) as evidence.

Case study · Japan

Move to Global War: Japanese expansion 1931-1941

Japan's expansion began with the Mukden (Manchurian) Incident on 18 September 1931, a staged sabotage of a railway used as a pretext to invade Manchuria. Japan set up the puppet state of Manchukuo in 1932, and — condemned by the Lytton Report — left the League of Nations in 1933.

The Marco Polo Bridge Incident of 7 July 1937 opened full-scale war with China. Expansion into South-East Asia and a US oil embargo led to the attack on Pearl Harbor on 7 December 1941, bringing the USA into the war.

Case study · Germany & Italy

Move to Global War: German and Italian expansion

Italy under Mussolini invaded Abyssinia (Ethiopia) in October 1935, again exposing the League's weakness. Nazi Germany remilitarised the Rhineland on 7 March 1936, achieved the Anschluss (union) with Austria in March 1938, and — after the Munich Agreement of September 1938 — annexed the Sudetenland.

The Nazi-Soviet Pact of 23 August 1939 cleared the way for the German invasion of Poland on 1 September 1939, which triggered war in Europe. Historian A. J. P. Taylor (The Origins of the Second World War, 1961) controversially argued Hitler was an opportunist rather than a planner — a useful interpretation to weigh.

Paper 1 · other options

The other prescribed subjects at a glance

You will only sit one, but it helps to place them:

  • Military leaders — leadership, campaigns and legacy of Genghis Khan and Richard I.
  • Conquest and its impact — the Spanish conquest of the Aztec (Cortés, 1519-1521) and Inca (Pizarro, from 1532) empires.
  • Rights and protest — US civil rights (Brown v. Board 1954; Civil Rights Act 1964) and apartheid South Africa (from 1948; Sharpeville 1960).
  • Conflict and intervention — the Rwandan genocide (1994) and the Kosovo conflict and NATO intervention (1999).
Recap

The big ideas to take away

Paper 1: a 1-hour, 24-mark source paper; choose 1 of 5 prescribed subjects, study 2 case studies

Questions: comprehension (5), OPVL value/limitations (4), compare/contrast (6), mini-essay with own knowledge (9)

OPVL: origin, purpose, value, limitations — tie value/limitations to origin and purpose

Move to global war: Japan from 1931 (Mukden); Italy 1935 (Abyssinia); Germany 1936-39; Pearl Harbor 1941

Correction: the fifth prescribed subject is Conflict and intervention, not "diplomatic crises"
