Mission 1

Mission 1: Turning one filter against another can block light, but not sound. What does that tell us about light?

Make a guess: Turning one filter against another can block light, but not sound. What does that tell us about light?

Mission 1

Plane polarised

  • Transverse wave: oscillations perpendicular to the direction of travel. They can point in many directions across it.
  • Plane polarised: the oscillations stay in one plane that contains the direction of travel.
  • For light (EM waves), the plane of oscillation is the plane of the electric field.
  • Unpolarised light (Sun, lamps): the oscillation direction changes rapidly and randomly.
  • Longitudinal wave: oscillations along the direction of travel. Only one direction is possible, so it cannot be polarised.
  • So: if a wave can be polarised, it must be transverse.
Quick check
?Predict first. A long rope passes through a narrow slot in a board. The figure shows the view along the rope towards the board, with the slot and the direction in which a student moves the rope back and forth to send a transverse wave towards the board. Which describes the wave on the far side of the board?
Mission 1

A rope through a slot

  • Shake a rope that passes through a vertical slot.
  • Shake it up and down: the wave passes through.
  • Shake it side to side: the slot stops the rope moving. No wave beyond the slot.
  • Shake it in every direction: only the up-and-down part gets through. The wave beyond is plane polarised.
Quick check
?Predict first. Unpolarised light from a lamp passes through two ideal polarising filters, P1 and P2. The lines drawn on the filters in the figure show their transmission axes. I1, I2 and I3 are the intensities of the light at points 1, 2 and 3. Which ranking of the intensities is correct?
Mission 1

Polarising filters

  • A polarising filter (Polaroid) lets through only oscillations along its transmission axis.
  • Unpolarised light in → plane polarised light out. An ideal filter passes half the intensity.
  • Add a second filter, the analyser. Axes parallel: the light passes on. Axes crossed (at 90°): no light gets through.
  • Rotate the analyser through a full turn: brightest at 0° and 180°, dark at 90° and 270°.
Mission check

?A student looks at a calculator's display through a polarising filter and slowly rotates the filter. The display is bright, goes dark after 90° of rotation, and is bright again after 180°. What does this show?

Only transverse waves can be polarised. Light can be polarised and sound cannot: light is transverse, sound is longitudinal.

Mission 2

Mission 2: You can hear someone around a corner but not see them. What decides how much a wave spreads?

Make a guess: You can hear someone around a corner but not see them. What decides how much a wave spreads?

Quick check
?Predict first. A wavefront diagram shows straight water waves of wavelength 2.0 cm, with wavefronts parallel to a barrier, passing through a gap 6.0 cm wide in the barrier; beyond the gap the wavefronts are straight in the middle and curved only at the edges. The gap is narrowed to 0.40 cm. How do the wavefronts beyond the gap change?
Mission 2

Diffraction

  • Diffraction: a wave spreads out as it passes through a gap or around the edge of an obstacle.
  • Speed, frequency and wavelength are unchanged. Only the direction of travel spreads.
  • Spreading becomes significant when the gap is comparable with λ. The narrower the gap, the more it spreads.
  • Gap about λ or smaller: nearly semicircular wavefronts, spreading through almost 180°.
  • Gap much wider than λ: very little spreading; a sharp-edged "shadow".
  • Speech: λ ≈ 0.3–3 m, like doorways. Light: λ ≈ 4–7 × 10⁻⁷ m, far smaller than any doorway.
Mission 2

Huygens and wavefront diagrams

  • Huygens' construction: every point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary wavelets. The new wavefront is the line touching all the wavelets.
  • At a gap, only part of the wavefront gets through. Wavelets from its edges spread into the "shadow".
  • Wavefront diagram: rays at 90° to wavefronts. Wavefront spacing (λ) stays the same beyond the gap.
  • Wide gap: straight in the middle, curved at the edges. Narrow gap: nearly semicircular.
Mission check

?Sea waves reach a harbour wall with a 20 m wide entrance. A boat is moored behind the wall, well to one side of the entrance. On day 1 the waves have a wavelength of 20 m; on day 2, 2.0 m, with the same amplitude. On which day is the boat rocked more, and why?

Spreading depends on λ compared with the gap or obstacle. Sound's wavelengths, about the size of a doorway, bend around it; light's, a million times smaller, barely do.

Mission 3

Mission 3: How can a slide ruled with thousands of fine lines measure the wavelength of light?

Make a guess: How can a slide ruled with thousands of fine lines measure the wavelength of light?

Mission 3

What a grating is

  • A diffraction grating: a plate with many equally spaced, parallel slits (lines).
  • Slit spacing d = 1 ÷ (lines per metre). 300 lines per mm → d = 1/300 mm = 3.33 × 10⁻⁶ m.
  • Each slit diffracts, so light from all the slits overlaps and interferes.
  • Bright beams leave only at certain angles: where light from every slit arrives in phase.
Mission 3

Deriving the grating equation

  • Light hits the grating at normal incidence (straight on).
  • Look at light leaving two adjacent slits at angle θ. Extra path for the lower slit: d sin θ.
  • In phase when this path difference is a whole number of wavelengths:
  • d sin θ = nλ, n = 0, 1, 2, … (the order).
  • If adjacent slits are in phase, all the slits are in phase.
  • n = 0: the zero order, straight through (θ = 0).
  • Highest order: sin θ cannot exceed 1, so n ≤ d/λ.
Mission 3

Worked example

  • 300 lines per mm, red laser λ = 633 nm.
  • d = 1 ÷ (300 × 10³) = 3.33 × 10⁻⁶ m.
  • First order: sin θ = λ/d = 633 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ 3.33 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.190 → θ = 10.9°.
  • Second order: sin θ = 2λ/d = 0.380 → θ = 22.3°.
  • Highest order: d/λ = 5.27 → n = 5 (at 71.7°). Total beams: 5 + 5 + 1 = 11.
  • No small-angle shortcut: angles are large. Use sin θ.
Quick check
?Light of wavelength 650 nm is incident normally on a diffraction grating that has 600 lines per millimetre. At what angle to the straight-through direction is the second-order maximum observed?
Mission 3

Why use many slits?

  • With only two slits the maxima are broad. With many slits at the same spacing d, they sit at the same angles but are far sharper.
  • But with many slits, a tiny step away from those angles puts the slits out of step and they cancel.
  • So the maxima are very sharp and very bright, with darkness between. Angles can be measured precisely.
  • Smaller d (more lines per mm) → orders farther apart.
Quick check
?Predict first. White light is incident normally on a diffraction grating. Which describes the pattern seen on a screen?
Mission 3

White light and spectra

  • Zero order is white: every wavelength has θ = 0 there.
  • Each other order is a spectrum: violet (short λ) nearest the centre, red farthest, since sin θ ∝ λ.
  • Higher orders spread wider and can overlap: second-order red (2 × 700 = 1400 nm) lies beyond third-order violet (3 × 400 = 1200 nm).
Mission 3

Measuring λ with a laser

  • Laser beam normal to the grating; screen a measured distance D away (≈ 1 m).
  • Measure the distance 2x between the two nth-order spots, either side of the centre. Halve it: x.
  • tan θ = x/D → θ → λ = d sin θ / n.
  • Use the highest order you can measure, and a large D: smaller percentage uncertainty.
  • Repeat for several orders and average (or plot sin θ against n: gradient λ/d).
  • Safety: never look into the beam or its reflections; keep reflective surfaces out of the beam path.
Mission check

?A laser beam is directed normally at a grating with 500 lines per mm. On a screen 1.00 m from the grating, the two second-order spots are 1.50 m apart. What is the wavelength of the laser light?

A grating turns a tiny, unmeasurable spacing into large, sharp angles. Measure the angle, know d, and λ follows from the grating equation.

Mission 4

Mission 4: A wave reaches the boundary between two materials. Does it carry on, bounce back, or both?

Make a guess: A wave reaches the boundary between two materials. Does it carry on, bounce back, or both?

Mission 4

Reflected and transmitted

  • Wherever the wave speed changes, a boundary splits a wave in two:
    • a reflected wave, back into the first medium;
    • a transmitted wave, on into the second.
  • The energy is shared between them (some may also be absorbed).
  • Bigger change in the medium → more reflected.
    • Sound from air into water: most is reflected.
Quick check
?Predict first. Sound from a loudspeaker in air reaches the surface of a swimming pool, and sound travels on into the water. Which property of the sound is the same in the water as in the air?
Mission 4

What changes and what doesn't

  • Frequency: unchanged. Each oscillation arriving at the boundary drives one oscillation beyond it.
  • Speed: set by the medium, so it changes.
  • Wavelength: λ = v/f, so it changes in proportion to v.
  • Reflected wave: same medium, so same speed and same wavelength as the incident wave.
Mission check

?At night, a person in a brightly lit room sees a faint reflection of the room in the window, while people outside can see into the room through the same window. What does this show?

Both. Wherever the speed changes, part of a wave is reflected and part transmitted; the frequency is kept, so the wavelength follows the speed.

Mission 5

Mission 5: How can a sound nobody can hear draw a picture of a baby before it is born?

Make a guess: How can a sound nobody can hear draw a picture of a baby before it is born?

Mission 5

What ultrasound is

  • Ultrasound: a longitudinal sound wave with frequency above 20 kHz, beyond human hearing.
  • Medical scanners use about 1–15 MHz.
  • Speed in soft tissue: about 1540 m s⁻¹ (scanners assume this value).
  • λ = c/f: at 5.0 MHz, λ = 1540 ÷ (5.0 × 10⁶) ≈ 0.31 mm.
  • Short wavelength is why it can show small structures.
Mission 5

Pulse-echo: finding a position

  • Send a short pulse. Time how long until the echo returns from a boundary.
  • The pulse travels there and back: distance to boundary d = ct/2.
  • Echoes happen where the wave is partly reflected, at boundaries between different media.
  • The same idea locates objects by sonar (sound in water) and radar (radio waves).
  • Example: an echo returns after 65 μs in soft tissue.
  • d = 1540 × 65 × 10⁻⁶ ÷ 2 = 0.050 m = 5.0 cm deep.
Quick check
?A ship sends a sound pulse straight down. The echo from the seabed returns 0.40 s later. The speed of sound in seawater is 1500 m s⁻¹. How deep is the seabed below the ship?
Mission 5

What limits the detail?

  • Wavelength: a structure much smaller than λ gives no clear echo (the wave diffracts round it). Detail finer than about λ is lost.
  • Higher frequency → shorter λ → finer detail.
  • But higher frequencies are absorbed more by tissue, so they cannot reach as deep.
  • Pulse duration τ: echoes from two boundaries closer than about cτ/2 overlap and merge into one.
  • Example: a 2-cycle pulse at 5.0 MHz lasts 0.40 μs → cτ/2 = 1540 × 0.40 × 10⁻⁶ ÷ 2 ≈ 0.3 mm.
  • So: short pulses and short wavelengths give more information.
Quick check
?A pulse-echo scanner shows a single echo where there are really two boundaries 0.2 mm apart. Which change is most likely to let it show two separate echoes?
Mission 5

A-scan

  • A-scan (A for amplitude): one beam, one direction.
  • Display: echo amplitude against time. Each spike = one boundary.
  • Spike's time → depth (d = ct/2). Gap between spikes → thickness of a layer.
  • Used to measure distances along one line, e.g. the length of the eyeball.
Mission check

?An A-scan through a soft-tissue layer shows an echo from its front surface and another from its back surface. The second echo arrives 13 μs after the first. The speed of ultrasound in the tissue is 1540 m s⁻¹. How thick is the layer?

Every echo's time gives a depth. A scanner sends thousands of pulses in different directions and plots each echo as a dot: that builds the picture of the baby.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Plane polarised: oscillations in one plane. Only transverse waves can be polarised, so polarisation is evidence that light (and all EM waves) are transverse.
  • Crossed polarising filters block all the light; rotating one varies the intensity from maximum to zero every 90°.

Mission 2

  • Diffraction is significant when the gap or obstacle is comparable with λ, and greater the narrower the gap; λ itself does not change.

Mission 3

  • Grating at normal incidence: d sin θ = nλ, d = 1/(lines per metre); n ≤ d/λ.
  • Many slits → very sharp, bright maxima at the angles given by d sin θ = nλ.

Mission 4

  • At a boundary where the wave speed changes, a wave is partly reflected and partly transmitted; frequency is unchanged, so λ changes with v.

Mission 5

  • Ultrasound: longitudinal, f > 20 kHz; pulse-echo gives depth d = ct/2.
  • Detail is limited by wavelength (higher f, finer detail, less depth) and by pulse duration (boundaries closer than about cτ/2 merge).
