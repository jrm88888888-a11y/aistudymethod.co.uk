Mission 1

What is sewa?

  • Sewa = selfless service to others, with no wish for reward, praise or payment.
  • Sikhs believe serving people is serving God, because God's light is in everyone.
  • Sewa is a mark of a gurmukh and a cure for haumai: it puts others before self.
  • It is for everyone: Sikh or not, friend or stranger.
  • Sikh teaching gives priority to service: helping others comes before one's own comfort.
Mission 1

Tan, man, dhan

  • Sikhs serve with body, mind and wealth (tan, man, dhan).
  • Tan (body): cooking and serving langar, washing dishes, cleaning the gurdwara, looking after shoes.
  • Man (mind): teaching Punjabi or gurbani, singing kirtan, giving advice, using your skills for others.
  • Dhan (wealth): giving money, food or goods; daswandh.
  • Many Sikhs see tan sewa as the humblest, and so the most powerful against ego: e.g. cleaning others' shoes.
  • All three are needed; what matters most is the attitude.

Mission 1

Where sewa comes from

  • Guru Nanak set up the first langar at Kartarpur, where everyone ate together and shared the work.
  • Mata Khivi, wife of Guru Angad, ran the langar so generously it is praised in the Guru Granth Sahib.
  • Guru Amar Das served Guru Angad for years before becoming Guru, tradition says fetching water for him daily.
  • Guru Arjan guided the building of the Harmandir Sahib by the voluntary labour of the sangat.
  • Bhai Ghanaiya (also spelled Kanhaiya; Guru Gobind Singh's time) gave water to wounded soldiers on both sides. Questioned, he said he saw only the Guru's light in each face. The Guru blessed him and gave him ointment for their wounds too.
  • So sewa built the community, its gurdwaras and its identity: it shaped how Sikhism developed.
Mission 1Read the source

Service in the world

Guru Nanak, GGS 26 (Sri Raag) (paraphrase):

  • Do sewa here, in the middle of this world; then you will be given a place of honour in God's court.

Q1 Where does GGS 26 say sewa should be done?

Show answer

In the world, in everyday life, not by withdrawing from it.

Q2 What does it promise?

Show answer

Honour in God's court: acceptance by God.

Mission 1

Daswandh: the tenth part

  • Daswandh = giving one tenth of your income for others: the gurdwara, the langar, the poor, education, disasters.
  • It is dhan sewa made regular, so giving is not left to mood.
  • Your spec links daswandh to Guru Amar Das, who organised Sikh communities to support the langar and those in need.
  • Some Sikhs give exactly a tenth; others give what they can, or give a tenth of their time.
  • It shows the priority of service: others' needs are built into a Sikh's own budget.
Mission 1

Service that protects: Khalsa and Nihangs

  • The Khalsa (1699) is a community of sant-sipahi: saint-soldiers.
  • Their sewa includes protecting the weak and standing against injustice, never for personal gain.
  • Nihangs: an order of Khalsa Sikhs, known for blue robes and traditional weapons. Historically they defended Sikh communities and gurdwaras.
  • Today, many Nihangs keep martial traditions (gatka) and serve at historic gurdwaras.
  • All three, sewa, the Khalsa and the Nihangs, grow out of the sangat: the community that trains and supports them (a later mission).

Mission 1Diversity

Sewa today

  • Gurdwara sewa: langar, cleaning, kirtan; often the first sewa a young Sikh learns.
  • Sewa in society: food for homeless people, food banks, blood drives, disaster relief (e.g. Khalsa Aid).
  • Most Sikhs do both. Some say gurdwara sewa trains the heart; others say the real test is serving strangers.
  • Family sewa: caring for elderly parents or a sick relative counts too.
  • Priority: some Sikhs say sewa matters even more than private prayer; others say nam simran and sewa cannot be separated, since prayer keeps sewa selfless.
Mission 1Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain two reasons why sewa is important to Sikhs. Refer to Sikh teaching.

  • Reason 1: Serving others is serving God, because God's light is in everyone. Development: so Sikhs serve all people equally, like Bhai Ghanaiya giving water to both sides.
  • Reason 2: Sewa removes haumai. Source: doing sewa in this world brings honour in God's court (GGS 26). Development: so humble tan sewa, like cleaning shoes, is valued as a way to become gurmukh.

Sewa is service to God through service to everyone, done with body, mind and wealth, and without wanting anything back.

Mission 2

The Khalsa

  • Khalsa: "pure", or "belonging directly to the Guru". The community of initiated Sikhs.
  • Founded by Guru Gobind Singh, the tenth Guru, at Vaisakhi 1699 in Anandpur.
  • Five Sikhs offered their lives when he asked; they became the panj pyare ("five beloved ones") and the first to receive amrit (sweetened water).
  • Then the Guru asked them to give amrit to him: the Guru and the Khalsa are equal.
  • Ideal: the sant-sipahi (saint-soldier): devoted to God, ready to defend the weak.
Mission 2

The Five Ks

  • Kesh: uncut hair, usually covered by a turban (dastar) or scarf. Acceptance of God's creation as it is.
  • Kangha: small wooden comb. Cleanliness and order in body and life.
  • Kara: steel bracelet. One God without beginning or end; a reminder to use the hands for good.
  • Kachera: cotton shorts worn as underwear. Self-control and readiness for action.
  • Kirpan: small sword. Duty to defend the weak and stand for justice; never for attack.
  • Together: a uniform of identity and commitment, worn at all times by initiated Sikhs.

Mission 2

Before the ceremony

  • Amrit Sanskar: the initiation ceremony (also Khande di Pahul). It is a choice, not a birth rite.
  • Open to anyone, male or female, of any background, old enough to understand the promises.
  • Held before the Guru Granth Sahib, often at Vaisakhi; in a gurdwara or another quiet place.
  • Five amritdhari Sikhs lead it, acting as the panj pyare; another reads from the Guru Granth Sahib.
  • Candidates bathe, wash their hair, and come wearing the Five Ks, with head covered. They wear no symbols of other faiths.
Mission 2

The ceremony

  • Water and patashas (sugar crystals) are placed in an iron bowl.
  • The panj pyare stir it with a khanda (double-edged sword) while reciting five prayers (banis), including Japji Sahib and Anand Sahib.
  • Each person receives amrit five times to drink, five times sprinkled on the eyes, five times on the hair. Each time: "Waheguru ji ka Khalsa, Waheguru ji ki Fateh" ("The Khalsa belongs to God; victory belongs to God").
  • All then drink from the same bowl: no caste, no rank.
  • The Mool Mantar is recited together.
  • The rules (rahit) are explained: daily prayers, the Five Ks, honest living; never cut hair, eat meat killed by ritual slaughter, commit adultery or use tobacco.
  • Ardas, a hukamnama, karah prashad. Anyone without Singh or Kaur takes it now.
Mission 2Read the source

One with the Guru

  • Bhai Gurdas, Var 3, pauri 11 (paraphrase): when initiated by the Guru, the disciple becomes a Sikh, and Guru and Sikh become one: like a diamond cutting a diamond, a wave merging into water, or one lamp lighting another.

Mission 2

Why take amrit?

  • Commitment: a public promise to live fully by the Guru's teaching.
  • New birth: joining the Guru's family; Anandpur counted as the initiate's spiritual birthplace.
  • Equality and identity: one family, one uniform, no caste.
  • Discipline: daily prayers (Nitnem) and the rahit shape the whole of life.
  • Service: as a sant-sipahi, to serve and protect others.
Mission 2Diversity

Amritdhari and sahajdhari

  • Amritdhari: initiated, members of the Khalsa; keep all Five Ks and the rahit.
  • Keshdhari: keep uncut hair (often a turban) but have not taken amrit.
  • Sahajdhari: "on the gradual path": believe in the Gurus and the Guru Granth Sahib but have not taken amrit and may cut their hair.
  • Amritdhari perspective: the Khalsa is the Guru's command; taking amrit completes a Sikh's commitment; the Five Ks are a uniform, not optional symbols.
  • Sahajdhari perspective: Guru Nanak taught inner devotion, Nam Japna and honest living; outward symbols without a pure heart are empty; many hope to take amrit when ready.
  • Many non-initiated Sikhs still wear a kara or keep kesh.
  • Your spec calls this khalsa and non-khalsa views of the Khalsa and the Five Ks.
Mission 2Evaluate

"A Sikh must take amrit to be a true Sikh."

  • Reasons for: Guru Gobind Singh created the Khalsa as the complete form of the Sikh path; the Rahit Maryada expects Sikhs to take amrit, and its definition of a Sikh includes belief in the tenth Guru's amrit; the Five Ks show commitment openly.
  • Reasons against: many Sikhs read that definition as asking for belief in amrit, not for having already taken it; Guru Nanak's followers were Sikhs long before 1699; amrit taken without sincerity means little.
  • Another view: Sikhism is a journey: sahajdharis are on the path, amrit is its goal.
  • Judge it by: What did the Gurus command? Does outward commitment guarantee inner devotion? Who decides who counts as a "true" Sikh?
Mission 2Worked answer

Two different views

Question: Explain two different Sikh views about the importance of the Five Ks.

  • View 1 (amritdhari): the Five Ks are essential. Development: they are part of the promise made at Amrit Sanskar, a uniform given by Guru Gobind Singh, so removing them would break that commitment.
  • View 2 (sahajdhari): what matters most is inner devotion. Development: Guru Nanak taught Nam Japna and honest living, so a Sikh may follow the Gurus sincerely before taking amrit.
Mission check

?An amritdhari Sikh nurse is told to remove her kara for hand hygiene. She asks instead to push it up her arm and tape it during patient care. Which belief best explains her request?

Amrit Sanskar turns belief into a lifelong, visible promise; Sikhs differ on whether that promise is the start or the goal of the path.

Mission 3

The sangat

  • Sangat = congregation: Sikhs gathered together to remember God, usually in the presence of the Guru Granth Sahib.
  • It usually meets at the gurdwara, but a sangat is people, not a building.
  • Anyone may join: men and women, all castes, Sikhs and non-Sikhs.
  • In the sangat Sikhs sing kirtan, hear the Guru's word, pray (Ardas), share langar and do sewa.
  • The worldwide community of Sikhs is the Panth.
Mission 3

Where the sangat came from

  • Guru Nanak gathered followers into sangats on his travels, in places later called dharamsals.
  • At Kartarpur he built a community that prayed, worked and ate together.
  • Later Gurus organised sangats across a growing area, linking them to the Guru.
  • Guru Arjan compiled the Adi Granth (1604) and installed it at the Harmandir Sahib for the sangat.
  • Before he died (1708), Guru Gobind Singh named the scripture as the Guru: Guru Granth. Many Sikhs add that authority also rests with the Guru Panth: the Khalsa community, deciding together.
Mission 3

Sat sangat: the true congregation

  • Sat Sangat = "true congregation": the company of people who remember God.
  • Guru Nanak asks how the sat sangat can be recognised; it is where the Name of the One is spoken (GGS 72, paraphrase).
  • So not every crowd is sat sangat: it is defined by what it is centred on.
  • Different views: many Sikhs say it is the gurdwara sangat before the Guru Granth Sahib; others say any gathering where God's Name is sung, even online; some also speak of the inner company of gurmukhs.
Mission 3Read the source

The Guru's school

Guru Ram Das, GGS 1316 (Kanara ki Var) (paraphrase):

  • The sat sangat is the True Guru's school: there the soul learns God's virtues.
  • Guru Arjan, GGS 1098 (Maru ki Var) (paraphrase): without the company of the holy, ego does not leave.
  • Guru Arjan, GGS 266 (Sukhmani Sahib) (paraphrase): in the company of the holy, pride dissolves; a person learns humility, sees all as friends, and bears pleasure and pain alike.

Mission 3

What the sangat does for a Sikh

  • Spiritual progress: being with others who remember God helps a person overcome haumai and stay gurmukh.
  • Training: the sangat teaches gurbani, Punjabi, kirtan and right conduct, to children and adults.
  • Support: in births, marriages, illness and death, the sangat stands with each family.
  • Sewa: the langar and every other sewa is organised and done by the sangat.
  • Khalsa and Nihangs: the Khalsa was formed from the sangat at Vaisakhi 1699; the Nihangs grew from the Khalsa to protect the community. Both serve the sangat.
  • Decisions: the sangat decides together, e.g. electing a gurdwara committee.
Mission 3

Living without a sangat

Problems for Sikhs living far from a sangat:

  • No shared kirtan, langar or Ardas with others.
  • Harder to learn gurbani, Punjabi and the Sikh way, especially for children.
  • No gurdwara for ceremonies: naming, Anand Karaj (marriage), funerals.
  • Isolation: harder to resist haumai alone, and harder to keep Sikh identity among people who do not understand it.
  • Responses: online kirtan and the daily hukamnama from Amritsar; prayer at home with a gutka (prayer book); travelling to gurdwaras; small home sangats; university Sikh societies.
Quick check
?A Sikh family moves to a remote island with no other Sikhs. Which problem would Sikh teaching most clearly warn them about?
Mission 3Diversity

How important is the sangat?

  • Essential: many Sikhs, citing the Gurus, say ego cannot be overcome without the company of the holy.
  • Very helpful, but not the only way: others stress each person's own nam simran; sangat supports, but cannot replace, it.
  • Online sangat: some welcome live-streamed kirtan as true sangat; others say being physically present, sharing langar and sewa, cannot be replaced.
  • Amritdhari / sahajdhari: both belong to the sangat equally; some Khalsa Sikhs place special weight on the Panj Pyare and gatherings of the initiated.
  • Local or global: some stress the local congregation; others the whole Panth, guided by the Akal Takht.
Mission check

?At a university far from any gurdwara, a group of Sikh students meets every week. They read from a prayer book, sing kirtan, then cook a free meal for any student who comes. In Sikh terms, which description fits the group best?

A sangat is people centred on God: it trains, supports and serves, and Sikhs feel its absence when they are alone.

Mission 4

Karma: what you sow

  • Karma = actions, and the results they bring.
  • Sikhs believe good actions bring good results; selfish actions bring suffering, in this life or a future one.
  • For Sikhs, karma from past lives helps explain the circumstances a person is born into.
  • Karma is not fate: what you choose now still matters.
  • Sikhs believe karma works within God's hukam (will and order), and God's grace is greater than karma.
Mission 4

Rebirth: coming and going

  • Sikhs believe that at death the atma (soul) leaves the body and is reborn in another body.
  • This cycle of birth, death and rebirth is called samsara; the Guru Granth Sahib also calls it "coming and going".
  • The next birth depends on karma; it may be in any life form, not only human.
  • The cycle is driven by haumai (self-centredness) and attachment.
  • The aim is to end the cycle by uniting with God.
Mission 4Read the source

Robe, field and gate

Guru Nanak, Japji Sahib, pauri 4, GGS 2 (paraphrase):

  • By karma (our actions) we receive this "robe" (a body, a birth); by God's grace (nadar) we find the gate of liberation.

GGS 78, Sri Raag (paraphrase):

  • The body is a field: whatever you sow in this life, you will harvest.
  • GGS 11, Guru Ram Das, from Rehras Sahib (paraphrase): those who meditate on God are liberated.
  • GGS 43, Guru Arjan, Sri Raag (paraphrase): life is short; liberation comes by remembering the Name and following the Guru, not by clever tricks; no one can harm the person whose hand God holds.

Mission 4

Mukti: freedom from, union with

  • Mukti = liberation: the aim of Sikh life.
  • Negative aspect (freedom from): release from rebirth, from haumai and from the five thieves.
  • Positive aspect (union with): the soul merges with God, like a drop returning to the ocean.
  • This union brings anand: bliss or deep joy (the theme of Anand Sahib).
  • Jivan mukti: many Sikhs believe a person can be liberated while still alive: a jivan mukt lives in the world, free of ego and full of love for God.
Mission 4

Grace: the gift of God

  • Grace (nadar, kirpa): God's freely given favour.
  • The Mool Mantar ends gur prasad: God is known "by the Guru's grace".
  • Sikhs cannot force mukti. Effort (remembering God, living well) and grace work together.
  • Grace often comes through the Guru: the Guru Granth Sahib and the sangat.
Mission 4

What blocks the way: maya and the five thieves

  • Maya: illusion; treating money, status and possessions as if they last, so God is forgotten. (The world is real; the illusion is thinking it is all there is.)
  • Haumai: self-centredness, the root problem (a later mission).
  • The five thieves (panj chor) steal the soul's attention from God:
  • Kaam (lust) · krodh (anger) · lobh (greed) · moh (worldly attachment) · ahankar (pride).
  • They are countered by virtues: e.g. contentment against greed, humility against pride.
Quick check

Mission 4Diversity

Different understandings today

  • Rebirth: most Sikhs take rebirth literally. Some Sikh writers read "coming and going" mainly as the restless cycle of an ego-centred life now.
  • Heaven and hell: the Guru Granth Sahib mentions them; many Sikhs see them as states of mind, not places, and not the final goal.
  • When is mukti? Some stress union after death; others stress jivan mukti now.
  • Why it matters: for some, karma motivates good actions and gives meaning to suffering. Others say Sikhs should love God for God's sake, not for reward.
Mission 4Evaluate

"Mukti depends more on God's grace than on what a person does."

  • Reasons for: Japji 4 (GGS 2): grace opens the gate of liberation. The Mool Mantar ends gur prasad. Grace can outweigh past karma.
  • Reasons against: the body is a field: what you sow, you harvest (GGS 78). The Gurus demand action: nam japna, honest work, sharing and sewa.
  • Also against: GGS 11: those who meditate on God are liberated.
  • Another view: effort and grace are not rivals: living well opens a person to grace, and grace makes the effort possible.
  • Judge it by: What do the named sources stress? Could a person be liberated with no effort? Does grace make good actions pointless, or more meaningful?
Mission check

?After a Sikh woman who spent her life in prayer and service dies, her family say at the gurdwara: "She has merged with Waheguru, like a drop of water returning to the ocean." Which idea are they describing?

For Sikhs, karma shapes each birth, but the goal is mukti: freedom from the cycle and union with God, by grace.

Mission 5

Two directions

  • Manmukh: "facing the self" (literally, the mind). Led by your own desires, comfort and image.
  • Gurmukh: "facing the Guru". God-centred: led by the Guru's teaching and remembering God.
  • These are directions, not fixed labels. Anyone can turn from manmukh to gurmukh.
  • The aim of Sikh life: become gurmukh, and so move towards mukti.
Mission 5

Haumai: "I, I"

  • Haumai = ego, pride, self-centredness. The word comes from "I, I" (hau, mai).
  • It makes a person feel separate from God and better than others.
  • It is the root of the five thieves (lust, anger, greed, attachment, pride) and keeps the soul in rebirth.
  • Guru Angad: haumai is a chronic disease, but its cure lies within it too, when God's grace and the Guru's word come (GGS 466, paraphrase).
Mission 5Read the source

Honoured or restless

GGS 125, Guru Amar Das (Raag Majh) (paraphrase):

  • The gurmukh is honoured in life and in death. The pain of ego is taken away, and the mind finds peace.

GGS 226, Guru Nanak (Raag Gauri) (paraphrase):

  • Caught in ego, people are born and die again and again.
  • Religious dress, high status and reciting scripture do not unite anyone with God while the mind stays self-centred.
  • The gurmukh overcomes ego by reflecting on the Guru's word.

Show answer

Mission 5

What makes a gurmukh?

  • Nam japna / nam simran: keeping God's Name in mind through the day.
  • Kirat karni: honest work. Vand chhakna: sharing.
  • Sewa: selfless service, with no wish for reward or praise.
  • Sangat: the company of others who remember God.
  • Accepting hukam: trusting God's will in good times and bad.
  • Virtues: truth, contentment, compassion, humility, love.
  • For many Sikhs, taking amrit (Khalsa initiation) and keeping the Rahit is part of the path.
Mission 5

Nam simran: remembering the Name

  • Nam simran = remembering God's Name, so that God is never forgotten.
  • Repeating Waheguru ("Wonderful Lord"), silently or aloud, sometimes with a mala (string of beads).
  • Singing hymns (kirtan) and reflecting on the Guru Granth Sahib.
  • Many Sikhs rise early (amrit vela) to meditate before the day begins.
  • The aim: replace "I, I" (haumai) with God at the centre. Ego shrinks as God fills the mind.
Mission 5Read the source

Will, poison and cure

  • GGS 1054–55, Guru Amar Das (Raag Maru) (paraphrase): the gurmukh recognises and accepts God's will (hukam), remembers the Name and finds lasting peace. The manmukh acts on self-will, does not grasp the Guru's word or God's will, and lives in fear.
  • GGS 538, Guru Ram Das (Raag Bihagra) (paraphrase): pride rooted in maya is a poison; the Name, received through the Guru's teaching, draws the poison out and steadies the mind.
  • GGS 466, Guru Angad (paraphrase): haumai is a chronic disease, but the cure is within it.

Q2 What is the cure for haumai in GGS 538?

Show answer

Mission 5Diversity

Why does it matter today?

  • For mukti: being gurmukh breaks the cycle of rebirth.
  • For this life: peace of mind and anand; less anxiety about status, money or image.
  • For society: haumai fuels conflict, prejudice and caste pride; gurmukhs serve and see everyone as equal.
  • Amritdhari Sikhs often see the Khalsa discipline as the surest way to be gurmukh.
  • Sahajdhari Sikhs often stress the inner attitude: humility and remembering God.
  • Many Sikhs warn that outward identity, even the Five Ks, can itself become a source of pride if the heart is not humble.
Mission 5Evaluate

"Haumai is the greatest barrier to mukti."

  • Reasons for: Guru Angad calls it a chronic disease (GGS 466). It is the root of the five thieves. GGS 226: in ego, people are born and die again. It is what separates the soul from God.
  • Reasons against: maya (illusion: treating the world as all there is) is just as strong a pull. The thieves, such as lust or greed, can each block the way. Without grace, no effort is enough: the real issue is God's gift, not one barrier.
  • Another view: the barriers are linked; maya and the thieves feed haumai, and haumai feeds them.
  • Judge it by: Which barrier do the named sources stress most? Is one barrier the root of the others? Can any barrier be overcome without grace?
Mission check

?Simran volunteers at a food bank every Saturday. Each week she posts photos of herself serving, and feels hurt if few people praise her. What would most Sikh teachers say about her service?

Gurmukh and manmukh are about direction: the same action can face towards God or towards the self.

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Sewa is selfless service to all, because God's light is in everyone; it shrinks haumai.
  • Three kinds: body, mind and wealth (tan, man, dhan, including daswandh); GGS 26: sewa in this world brings honour in God's court.
  • From Guru Nanak's langar to Bhai Ghanaiya, sewa shaped Sikhism; today it ranges from the gurdwara to disaster relief.

Mission 2

  • The Khalsa began at Vaisakhi 1699; the Five Ks are kesh, kangha, kara, kachera, kirpan.
  • Amrit Sanskar: amrit stirred with a khanda by five Khalsa Sikhs, shared from one bowl; initiates promise to keep the rahit.
  • Amritdhari Sikhs see amrit as the Guru's command; sahajdhari Sikhs stress inner devotion and a gradual path.

Mission 3

  • The sangat is the community gathered to remember God, usually before the Guru Granth Sahib; sat sangat is the company of those who remember God (GGS 72; the Guru's school, GGS 1316).
  • It helps Sikhs overcome haumai, learn, support one another and do sewa; living without one is hard.

Mission 4

  • Sikhs believe karma shapes each rebirth in samsara; the body is a field where we sow and harvest (GGS 78).
  • Mukti: freedom from rebirth (negative) and union with God (positive); by God's grace (Japji 4, GGS 2).
  • Maya, haumai and the five thieves (lust, anger, greed, attachment, pride) block the way.

Mission 5

  • Manmukh faces the self; gurmukh faces the Guru and God (GGS 125, 226).
  • Haumai ("I, I") is the chronic disease at the root of the five thieves; its cure is nam simran and the Guru's word, by grace (GGS 466).
  • A gurmukh's life shows in nam simran, honest work, sharing, selfless sewa, sangat and humility.
