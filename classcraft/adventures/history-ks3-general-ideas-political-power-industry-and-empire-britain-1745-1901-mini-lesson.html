Ideas, power, industry & empire

This mini-lesson walks you through Britain from 1745 to 1901: new ideas (the Enlightenment), the world's first Industrial Revolution, the transatlantic slave trade and its abolition, the growth of the British Empire, and the long struggle for political power and the vote.

The big picture

A timeline: 1745 → 1901

These years cover the reigns from George II to Queen Victoria (who died in 1901). Keep this timeline in your head — it holds the whole period together.

Ideas

The Enlightenment

The Enlightenment was a movement of thinkers who argued that reason, science and evidence — not just tradition or the Church — should guide how we understand the world and run society.

  • People began to question the divine right of kings and ask whether governments should answer to the people.
  • New ideas about liberty, rights and equality spread — feeding revolutions in America (1776) and France (1789).
  • A spirit of experiment and invention helped set the stage for industrial change.

Why it matters: the Enlightenment gave people the language of rights and reform that later campaigners used to argue against slavery and to demand the vote.

Industry

The first industrial nation

Britain was the first country in the world to industrialise. During the Industrial Revolution (from about the 1760s) work moved out of homes and into large factories powered by steam engines and coal.

  • New machines (like the spinning jenny and power loom) made cloth far faster than by hand.
  • Coal and iron fed the factories; the steam engine turned the wheels.
  • Railways (from the 1830s) let goods and people travel across the country at speed for the first time.

Common mistake: the Industrial Revolution was not a single sudden event. It was a gradual change over many decades — different industries and regions changed at different speeds.

Industry · everyday life

Life and work in the new towns

Industrial cities like Manchester grew so fast that housing, water and sewers could not keep up. For many workers, life was hard:

  • Long hours — often 12 or more hours a day, six days a week.
  • Child labour — young children worked in factories and mines.
  • Dangerous machines and polluted, overcrowded streets spread disease such as cholera.

But also: industry created wealth and, over time, new goods, jobs and — eventually — reforms like the Factory Acts that limited child labour.

Empire & the slave trade

The transatlantic slave trade

For much of this period Britain was heavily involved in the transatlantic slave trade. Enslaved African people were shipped across the Atlantic to work, mostly on plantations in the Caribbean and the Americas.

This is often described as a triangular trade:

  • Britain → Africa: ships carried manufactured goods (like guns and cloth).
  • Africa → the Americas: enslaved people were transported in brutal conditions (the "Middle Passage").
  • Americas → Britain: ships returned with sugar, cotton and tobacco, making huge profits.

Why it matters: the profits helped fund Britain's growth, but the trade caused immense suffering — and it became the target of one of history's biggest reform campaigns.

Reform & campaigners

Abolition — a long campaign

Campaigners called abolitionists — such as Olaudah Equiano (a formerly enslaved writer), William Wilberforce (an MP) and Thomas Clarkson — fought for years to end the trade.

1807 → 18331807: the slave TRADE was abolished · 1833: SLAVERY itself was abolished across the British Empire

Common mistake: abolition did not happen in one step. Ending the trade in 1807 came first; ending slavery itself took until 1833 — the campaign spanned decades.

Empire

Britain's growing empire

By 1901 the British Empire was the largest the world had ever seen — it was said "the sun never set" on it because it stretched right around the globe.

  • India became central to the empire; from 1858 it was ruled directly by the British government (the "British Raj"), and Victoria was later called Empress of India.
  • Africa saw rapid European expansion in the late 1800s (the "Scramble for Africa").
  • The empire brought Britain trade, raw materials and power — but was often built through conquest and control of other peoples.

Balanced view: historians study the empire's effects from many sides — wealth and connections for Britain, but loss of freedom and resources for many colonised peoples.

Political power

Who held power — and who wanted it?

In 1745 Parliament was dominated by wealthy landowners. Most people — including all women and the great majority of working men — could not vote.

As industrial towns grew, more people demanded a say. This period saw a long, step-by-step campaign for the vote and for fairer government.

Remember: the right to vote did not arrive all at once. It was widened in stages across many decades.

Political power · 1832

The Great Reform Act, 1832

The Great Reform Act of 1832 was a first big step. It:

  • Got rid of some "rotten boroughs" — tiny places with almost no voters that still elected MPs.
  • Gave seats in Parliament to growing industrial towns that had none before.
  • Gave the vote to more middle-class men — but still not to most working men or to any women.

Common mistake: 1832 did not give everyone the vote. It was an important but limited reform — the start of a long process.

Protest

Chartism — a mass movement

Many working people were disappointed by 1832. From 1838 the Chartists launched Britain's first mass political movement, setting out their demands in the People's Charter.

  • Their six points included the vote for all adult men and a secret ballot.
  • They gathered huge petitions with millions of signatures.
  • Parliament rejected the Charter at the time — but most of their demands were eventually met in later years.

Why it matters: Chartism showed ordinary people organising to demand political rights — part of the slow, staged widening of democracy.

Ireland & the Union

Ireland and the Union

In 1801 the Act of Union joined Ireland with Great Britain to form the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland, with Irish MPs now sitting in the Parliament in London.

  • Many Irish people wanted Catholic rights and, later, Home Rule (their own parliament).
  • The terrible Great Famine of the 1840s caused mass death and emigration, deepening tensions with Britain.

Ireland's relationship with Britain was one of the biggest political issues of the Victorian age.

Victorian society & reform

A reforming age

Over Victoria's reign (1837-1901), campaigners and governments slowly tried to fix some problems of industrial life:

  • Factory Acts limited working hours and child labour.
  • Public health laws improved water, sewers and housing after cholera outbreaks.
  • Education reforms began to provide schooling for more children.

Big idea: like abolition and the vote, these reforms came gradually — improvement was real but slow and hard-won.

Recap

The key points to know

Ideas: the Enlightenment valued reason, science and rights.

Industry: Britain industrialised first — factories, coal, steam, railways, crowded cities.

Slavery: trade abolished 1807; slavery abolished 1833 — a decades-long campaign.

Empire: the largest empire ever, centred on India and expanding in Africa.

Power & protest: Great Reform Act 1832 and Chartism — the vote widened in stages.

Reform: Factory Acts, public health and education slowly improved Victorian life.
