Mission 1

Mission 1: Why do Sikhs say ten different men were all the same Guru?

Make a guess: Why do Sikhs say ten different men were all the same Guru?

Mission 1

A boy who asked questions

  • Guru Nanak was born in 1469 at Talwandi, Punjab (now Nankana Sahib, Pakistan), into a Hindu family.
  • The janamsakhis (life stories written down after his death) show him questioning empty ritual from childhood.
  • The true bargain (Sacha Sauda): his father gave him money to make a good deal. He spent it feeding hungry holy men, and called that the only true bargain.
  • Sikhs see in this story the seed of langar (free shared meal) and sewa (selfless service).
Mission 1

The river experience

  • About 1499, at Sultanpur Lodhi, Nanak went to bathe in the River Bein and disappeared for three days.
  • Sikhs believe he was taken into God's presence, given the Name of God, and told to share it.
  • When he came back, his first words are remembered as: "There is no Hindu, there is no Muslim."
  • Meaning: God sees no religious labels; all people stand equal before the One God.
  • Sikh tradition says he then spoke the Mool Mantar, the opening words of the Sikh scripture.
  • Sikhs believe his teachings were divinely revealed: the hymns are God's word given through the Guru, not his own invention.
Mission 1

Travels, Kartarpur and death

  • Nanak made long journeys (udasis) across India and beyond, with Bhai Mardana, a Muslim musician who played the rabab while Nanak sang.
  • He visited Hindu and Muslim holy places, challenging ritual done without devotion.
  • About 1522 he settled at Kartarpur (now in Pakistan): a community that farmed, worshipped together and ate together in langar.
  • At Kartarpur Sikh life took shape: honest work, sharing, and remembering God's Name.
  • He died in 1539, having chosen his disciple Lehna (renamed Angad) as Guru instead of his own sons.
  • Sikhs believe the same divine jot (light) in Nanak passed to Angad, and on through all ten Gurus: ten men, one Guru.
  • A janamsakhi story: Hindus wished to cremate him, Muslims to bury him. Under the sheet only flowers were found.

Mission 1

Ten Gurus, one light

  • Sikhs believe the same divine jot (light) passed from Guru to Guru, like one flame lighting the next lamp.
  • So there are ten Gurus in human form, but one Guru's message.
  • Angad (2nd): shaped the Gurmukhi script; with his wife Mata Khivi, ran the langar.
  • Amar Das (3rd): everyone ate in langar before meeting him; appointed women as well as men to teach.
  • Ram Das (4th): founded the town that became Amritsar; wrote the wedding hymn (Lavan).
  • Arjan (5th): built the Harmandir Sahib; compiled the Adi Granth (1604); the first Sikh martyr (1606).
  • Hargobind (6th): wore two swords, miri and piri (worldly and spiritual authority); built the Akal Takht.
  • Har Rai (7th): known for healing and herbal medicine. Har Krishan (8th): Guru as a child; nursed the sick in Delhi and died aged 7.
  • Tegh Bahadur (9th): executed in Delhi (1675) for defending the right of Hindus to practise their faith.

Mission 1

Guru Gobind Singh

  • Born 1666; became the 10th Guru aged nine, after his father, Guru Tegh Bahadur, was executed (1675).
  • Vaisakhi 1699, Anandpur: he founded the Khalsa, a community of initiated Sikhs ready to give everything for their faith.
  • Khalsa Sikhs keep the Five Ks: kesh (uncut hair), kangha (comb), kara (steel bracelet), kirpan (sword), kachera (shorts).
  • Battles: he fought Mughal and hill-state armies (e.g. Anandpur, Chamkaur, 1704) to defend Sikhs' freedom. All four of his sons died in this struggle.
  • Writings: hymns such as the Jaap Sahib, said daily, and a letter to Emperor Aurangzeb, the Zafarnama. They are collected in the Dasam Granth (scholars debate the authorship of some parts).
  • 1708, Nanded: before his death he declared that the Guru Granth Sahib would be the Guru from then on.
Mission 1

Equality in the Gurus' lives

  • Guru Nanak: "no Hindu, no Muslim"; Lalo's honest bread over Bhago's feast; one langar where all sat together.
  • Guru Gobind Singh: the first five Khalsa members (the panj pyare) came from different castes and regions.
  • All drank amrit from one iron bowl: a deliberate break with caste rules about sharing food and drink.
  • Every Khalsa man took the name Singh (lion), every woman Kaur (princess), replacing caste surnames.
Mission 1Evaluate

"The Gurus in human form matter less today, now Sikhs have the Guru Granth Sahib."

  • Reasons for: the Guru for Sikhs today is the Guru Granth Sahib; Sikhs bow to it, not to pictures of the Gurus. The human Gurus ended in 1708 by Guru Gobind Singh's own command.
  • Reasons against: the scripture is the Gurus' words (Gurus 1–5 and 9). Their lives show how to live the teaching: Nanak's honest work, Tegh Bahadur's courage, Gobind Singh's Khalsa. Gurpurbs keep their stories alive.
  • Another view: most Sikhs believe the same light lives on in the Guru Granth Sahib and the Khalsa Panth, so the Gurus have not "gone". A few groups (e.g. Namdharis) accept later living gurus; mainstream Sikhs reject this.
  • Judge it by: Can teaching be separated from the teacher's example? What do Sikhs actually do: bow to the scripture, and tell the Gurus' stories?

Mission 2: Why do some Sikhs drink from one shared bowl and promise never to cut their hair?

Make a guess: Why do some Sikhs drink from one shared bowl and promise never to cut their hair?

Mission 2

The Khalsa

  • Khalsa: "pure", or "belonging directly to the Guru". The community of initiated Sikhs.
  • Founded by Guru Gobind Singh, the tenth Guru, at Vaisakhi 1699 in Anandpur.
  • Five Sikhs offered their lives when he asked; they became the panj pyare ("five beloved ones") and the first to receive amrit (sweetened water).
  • Then the Guru asked them to give amrit to him: the Guru and the Khalsa are equal.
  • Your spec names Mata Sundari, the Guru's wife, as adding sugar crystals (patashas) to the water: sweetness alongside strength. (Some accounts name Mata Sahib Kaur.)
  • Ideal: the sant-sipahi (saint-soldier): devoted to God, ready to defend the weak.
Mission 2

The Five Ks

  • Kesh: uncut hair, usually covered by a turban (dastar) or scarf. Acceptance of God's creation as it is.
  • Kangha: small wooden comb. Cleanliness and order in body and life.
  • Kara: steel bracelet. One God without beginning or end; a reminder to use the hands for good.
  • Kachera: cotton shorts worn as underwear. Self-control and readiness for action.
  • Kirpan: small sword. Duty to defend the weak and stand for justice; never for attack.
  • Together: a uniform of identity and commitment, worn at all times by initiated Sikhs.

Mission 2

Before the ceremony

  • Amrit Sanskar: the initiation ceremony (also Khande di Pahul). It is a choice, not a birth rite.
  • Open to anyone, male or female, of any background, old enough to understand the promises.
  • Held before the Guru Granth Sahib, often at Vaisakhi; in a gurdwara or another quiet place.
  • Five amritdhari Sikhs lead it, acting as the panj pyare; another reads from the Guru Granth Sahib.
  • Candidates bathe, wash their hair, and come wearing the Five Ks, with head covered. They wear no symbols of other faiths.
Mission 2

The ceremony

  • Water and patashas (sugar crystals) are placed in an iron bowl.
  • The panj pyare stir it with a khanda (double-edged sword) while reciting five prayers (banis), including Japji Sahib and Anand Sahib.
  • Each person receives amrit five times to drink, five times sprinkled on the eyes, five times on the hair. Each time: "Waheguru ji ka Khalsa, Waheguru ji ki Fateh" ("The Khalsa belongs to God; victory belongs to God").
  • All then drink from the same bowl: no caste, no rank.
  • The Mool Mantar is recited together.
  • The rules (rahit) are explained: daily prayers, the Five Ks, honest living; never cut hair, eat meat killed by ritual slaughter, commit adultery or use tobacco.
  • Ardas, a hukamnama, karah prashad. Anyone without Singh or Kaur takes it now.
Mission 2Read the source

  • The Sikh code of conduct (Rahit Maryada) says initiates become children of the Guru, born into the Khalsa family.

Mission 2

Why take amrit?

  • Commitment: a public promise to live fully by the Guru's teaching.
  • New birth: joining the Guru's family; Anandpur counted as the initiate's spiritual birthplace.
  • Equality and identity: one family, one uniform, no caste.
  • Discipline: daily prayers (Nitnem) and the rahit shape the whole of life.
  • Service: as a sant-sipahi, to serve and protect others.
Mission 2Diversity

Amritdhari and sahajdhari

  • Amritdhari: initiated, members of the Khalsa; keep all Five Ks and the rahit.
  • Keshdhari: keep uncut hair (often a turban) but have not taken amrit.
  • Sahajdhari: "on the gradual path": believe in the Gurus and the Guru Granth Sahib but have not taken amrit and may cut their hair.
  • Amritdhari perspective: the Khalsa is the Guru's command; taking amrit completes a Sikh's commitment; the Five Ks are a uniform, not optional symbols.
  • Sahajdhari perspective: Guru Nanak taught inner devotion, Nam Japna and honest living; outward symbols without a pure heart are empty; many hope to take amrit when ready.
  • Many non-initiated Sikhs still wear a kara or keep kesh.
Mission 2Evaluate

"A Sikh must take amrit to be a true Sikh."

  • Reasons for: Guru Gobind Singh created the Khalsa as the complete form of the Sikh path; the Rahit Maryada expects Sikhs to take amrit, and its definition of a Sikh includes belief in the tenth Guru's amrit; the Five Ks show commitment openly.
  • Reasons against: many Sikhs read that definition as asking for belief in amrit, not for having already taken it; Guru Nanak's followers were Sikhs long before 1699; amrit taken without sincerity means little.
  • Another view: Sikhism is a journey: sahajdharis are on the path, amrit is its goal.
  • Judge it by: What did the Gurus command? Does outward commitment guarantee inner devotion? Who decides who counts as a "true" Sikh?
Mission check

?An amritdhari Sikh nurse is told to remove her kara for hand hygiene. She asks instead to push it up her arm and tape it during patient care. Which belief best explains her request?

Amrit Sanskar turns belief into a lifelong, visible promise; Sikhs differ on whether that promise is the start or the goal of the path.

Mission 3

From Adi Granth to Guru

  • 1604: Guru Arjan gathered the Gurus' hymns into the Adi Granth ("first book") and installed it in the Harmandir Sahib, Amritsar.
  • c. 1706: Guru Gobind Singh added the hymns of his father, Guru Tegh Bahadur.
  • 1708: before he died, he declared the scripture to be the Guru from then on: the Guru Granth Sahib.
  • Sikhs call it the living Guru: the Word (Gurbani) is the Guru, so the Guru is still present and still speaks.
  • It is not an idol: Sikhs honour the divine Word in it, not paper and ink.
Mission 3

What is inside

  • 1,430 pages, called angs ("limbs", as if parts of the Guru's body).
  • Written in the Gurmukhi script; the language is mostly old Punjabi, with words from several Indian languages and Persian.
  • Opens with the Mool Mantar and Japji Sahib (Guru Nanak).
  • The main body is arranged by raag (31 musical modes), because the hymns are meant to be sung.
  • Writers: six Gurus (1–5 and 9), and Hindu and Muslim saints (bhagats or sants) such as Kabir, Ravidas, Namdev and Sheikh Farid.
  • Significance: God's word is not owned by one community or caste. Ravidas was a low-caste leatherworker; Farid a Muslim Sufi.

Mission 3

Treated as a living Guru

  • Rests on a raised throne (takht, with the manji sahib) under a canopy (chanani), wrapped in cloths (rumalas).
  • A chaur (whisk) is waved over it: an old sign of royal honour.
  • Prakash at dawn: it is opened ceremonially. Sukhasan at night: it is closed and laid to rest, often in a separate room (sach khand).
  • When carried, it goes on a Sikh's head; others stand.
  • People entering cover their heads, remove shoes and bow (matha tekna).
  • At home, the Rahit Maryada asks for a clean, separate place for it. So most families keep a full copy only exceptionally, and use a gutka (prayer book) for daily prayers.
Mission 3

How it is used

  • Worship: hymns sung as kirtan by musicians (ragis), explained in talks (katha).
  • Meditation: Sikhs repeat and reflect on its words.
  • Hukamnama (vak): it is opened at random and the first hymn on the left-hand page is read as the Guru's command for the day.
  • Ceremonies: naming a baby (first letter of the hukamnama), marriage (the couple walk round it four times), Akhand Path.
  • Daily life: a source of teaching, comfort and guidance: Sikhs turn to a hukamnama at times of decision or grief.
  • Each day's hukamnama from the Harmandir Sahib is shared worldwide, so Sikhs everywhere can hear the same words.

Mission 3

The Rahit Maryada

  • The Sikh Rahit Maryada (code of conduct) was drawn up by the SGPC (the elected body that runs historic gurdwaras in Punjab) and approved in 1945.
  • It defines a Sikh: one who believes in one God, the ten Gurus, the Guru Granth Sahib and the Gurus' teachings, and in Amrit.
  • Personal life: rise early, meditate on God's Name, say the daily prayers (Nitnem), earn honestly, share.
  • Community life: how gurdwaras, kirtan, langar and ceremonies should be done.
  • Khalsa discipline: e.g. never cut hair; no tobacco; no adultery; no meat killed in a ritual way.
  • Its role: discipline for the individual, a shared pattern for families and gurdwaras worldwide.
Mission 3Diversity

How much authority?

  • Most Sikhs and gurdwaras treat it as the agreed code, backed by the Akal Takht.
  • Some groups (e.g. the Damdami Taksal) follow their own, stricter rahit on some points.
  • Many sahajdhari Sikhs (not initiated) follow its general guidance rather than every Khalsa rule.
  • All agree it is below the Guru Granth Sahib: a human guide to living out the Guru's teaching.
  • Some Sikhs value it for keeping Sikhs united; others say it should be applied with understanding, not as a checklist.

Mission 3

Who leads? Sangat and granthi

  • Sikhism has no priests. Any Sikh, woman or man, may read from the Guru Granth Sahib and lead prayer.
  • The sangat (congregation) runs each gurdwara, usually by electing a management committee (president, secretary, treasurer).
  • The granthi reads the Guru Granth Sahib, looks after it, leads daily services, and conducts naming, marriage and funeral prayers.
  • A granthi is often paid by the sangat, and also helps the community: teaching Punjabi and Gurmukhi, giving advice.
  • The panj pyare, five respected Khalsa Sikhs, lead Amrit initiations.
  • Beyond the gurdwara: the Akal Takht in Amritsar, led by its Jathedar, issues guidance to Sikhs worldwide.
Mission 3Diversity

Different views of leaders

  • Strong central voice: many Sikhs value the Akal Takht and SGPC for keeping the Panth (Sikh community) united.
  • Local and equal: others stress that authority lies with the Guru and the whole sangat, and criticise committee politics or disputes.
  • Sants and babas: some respected teachers lead their own communities (deras). Many Sikhs welcome their teaching; others warn this can drift towards treating a human as a guru.
  • Women's leadership: the Rahit Maryada allows women to do every role; in practice, how often women serve as granthis or committee members varies.
Mission 3

One important Sikh: Bhai Kanhaiya

  • Bhai Kanhaiya (also spelled Ghanaiya; c. 1648–1718), a follower of Guru Gobind Singh.
  • In battle at Anandpur he carried water to the wounded on both sides, including enemy soldiers.
  • Sikhs complained to the Guru. Kanhaiya replied that he saw no enemy: he saw the Guru's light in every face.
  • The Guru praised him and gave him ointment to treat wounds too.
  • Teaching: sewa without discrimination; God is present in everyone.
  • Contribution: he founded the Sevapanthi order, devoted to service.
  • Relevance today: Sikh relief charities (e.g. Khalsa Aid, founded 1999) help victims of war and disaster whatever their faith.
  • Your spec lets you study any one important Sikh other than the Gurus; Bhai Kanhaiya is one strong choice.

Mission 4

The sangat

  • Sangat = congregation: Sikhs gathered together to remember God, usually in the presence of the Guru Granth Sahib.
  • It usually meets at the gurdwara, but a sangat is people, not a building.
  • Anyone may join: men and women, all castes, Sikhs and non-Sikhs.
  • In the sangat Sikhs sing kirtan, hear the Guru's word, pray (Ardas), share langar and do sewa.
  • The worldwide community of Sikhs is the Panth.
Mission 4

Sat sangat: the true congregation

  • Sat Sangat = "true congregation": the company of people who remember God.
  • Guru Nanak asks how the sat sangat can be recognised; it is where the Name of the One is spoken (GGS 72, paraphrase).
  • So not every crowd is sat sangat: it is defined by what it is centred on.
  • Different views: many Sikhs say it is the gurdwara sangat before the Guru Granth Sahib; others say any gathering where God's Name is sung, even online; some also speak of the inner company of gurmukhs.
  • The sangat helps Sikhs overcome haumai, learn gurbani and support one another. Sikhs who live far from one miss shared kirtan and langar; many use online kirtan and pray at home.
Mission 4

Who leads the gurdwara?

  • Sikhism has no priests. Any Sikh, man or woman, may lead worship.
  • Each gurdwara is run by a management committee elected from the sangat (president, secretary, treasurer).
  • The granthi reads from the Guru Granth Sahib, cares for it (opening it in the morning, closing it at night), leads prayers and often teaches.
  • Other roles: ragis (kirtan musicians), gianis (scholars who explain gurbani), sevadars (volunteers).
  • Different understandings: some Sikhs look to the Akal Takht in Amritsar and its Jathedar for guidance on the whole Panth; others stress each local sangat's own decisions. Committee elections can be hotly contested.

?At a university far from any gurdwara, a group of Sikh students meets every week. They read from a prayer book, sing kirtan, then cook a free meal for any student who comes. In Sikh terms, which description fits the group best?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Guru Nanak (1469–1539): the river experience, travels with Mardana, Kartarpur and langar; one God, and all people equal.
  • Ten Gurus in human form, one divine light; each added something (Gurmukhi, langar, Amritsar, the Adi Granth, miri and piri, martyrdom for freedom of faith).
  • Guru Gobind Singh founded the Khalsa (1699) and made the Guru Granth Sahib the Guru (1708).

Mission 2

  • The Khalsa began at Vaisakhi 1699; the Five Ks are kesh, kangha, kara, kachera, kirpan.
  • Amrit Sanskar: amrit stirred with a khanda by five Khalsa Sikhs, shared from one bowl; initiates promise to keep the rahit.
  • Amritdhari Sikhs see amrit as the Guru's command; sahajdhari Sikhs stress inner devotion and a gradual path.

Mission 3

  • The Guru Granth Sahib (1,430 angs; Gurus and bhagats; arranged by raag) is the living Guru since 1708, treated with royal honour and used for worship, guidance and ceremonies.
  • The Rahit Maryada (SGPC, 1945) is the Sikh code of conduct; most accept it, some groups follow their own rahit.
  • No priests: the sangat leads, the granthi serves; an important Sikh such as Bhai Kanhaiya shows sewa for all.

Mission 4

  • The sangat is the community gathered to remember God, usually before the Guru Granth Sahib; sat sangat is the company of those who remember God (GGS 72; the Guru's school, GGS 1316).
  • It helps Sikhs overcome haumai, learn, support one another and do sewa; living without one is hard.
  • Gurdwaras are led by elected committees and served by a granthi; there are no priests.
