Medical Ethics

This mini-lesson works through religious and ethical teachings about medical ethics: the ideas of sanctity of life and quality of life, and how believers and non-believers respond to abortion, euthanasia, fertility treatment, embryo research and organ donation.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go, and collect ⭐ stars. Throughout, a range of religious and non-religious views is shown fairly — the aim is to understand them, not to decide who is right. Press Start when you're ready.

Key idea 1

Sanctity of life

The sanctity of life is the belief that life is holy, sacred and God-given, and so has a special value that humans should not destroy. Many religious teachings on medical ethics start from this idea.

  • Christianity: humans are made "in the image of God" (Genesis 1:27), and "You shall not murder" (Exodus 20:13). Only God gives and takes life.
  • Islam: life is a trust (amanah) from Allah; the Qur'an teaches "whoever saves a life, it is as if he saved all mankind" (Qur'an 5:32).
  • Judaism: life is sacred, and pikuach nefesh (saving a life) is so important it can override most other laws.

Key term — sanctity of life: the belief that life is holy and belongs to God, so it must be respected and protected.

Key idea 2

Quality of life

The quality of life is a measure of how good or comfortable a person's life is — for example their health, freedom from pain, dignity and ability to enjoy living. Some people give this more weight when a life involves great suffering.

Sanctity of life and quality of life are two different lenses, not opposites. Many people value both: they respect life as precious and care about reducing suffering. Where the two seem to pull in different directions, people — religious and non-religious — reach different conclusions.

How people decide

Where views come from

People reach medical-ethics decisions using different authorities and reasoning. Understanding these helps explain why views differ:

  • Scripture — sacred texts such as the Bible or Qur'an.
  • Religious authority — e.g. the teaching of the Catholic Church (the Magisterium), or scholars of Islamic law (Shari'ah).
  • Conscience — an inner sense of right and wrong.
  • Situation ethics — a Christian approach that asks what the most loving action (agape) is in each situation.
  • Secular & utilitarian ethics — non-religious reasoning; utilitarianism asks which choice brings the greatest well-being and least suffering.

Balance: No single religion has just one view. Within Christianity, Islam and Judaism there is a range of opinion, and non-religious people disagree with one another too.

Issue: abortion

Abortion

Abortion is the deliberate ending of a pregnancy so that it does not result in a live birth. It is one of the most sensitive issues in medical ethics, and sincere people disagree.

A central question is: when does life begin — at conception, at some later stage, or at birth? Different answers lead to different views.

A fair range of views

Many Catholic and some other Christians: life begins at conception, so abortion is generally wrong (sanctity of life). "Before I formed you in the womb I knew you" (Jeremiah 1:5).

Some other Christians: may accept abortion in hard cases — e.g. risk to the mother's life, or rape — choosing the most loving outcome (situation ethics).

Islam: generally opposes abortion, but many scholars permit it to save the mother's life, and some allow it in early pregnancy for serious reasons.

Judaism: the mother's life takes priority (pikuach nefesh); abortion can be required if her life is at risk.

Many non-religious views: often stress the woman's rights and quality-of-life factors, while still taking the decision seriously.

Presented, not preached: these views are set out so you can explain and evaluate them. The mini-lesson does not tell you which is "right".

Issue: euthanasia

Euthanasia & assisted dying

Euthanasia means bringing about a gentle death to end suffering, usually for someone with a serious, incurable illness. You must know the types:

  • Voluntary — the person asks to die.
  • Non-voluntary — the person cannot ask (e.g. is in a coma) and others decide.
  • Active — a deliberate act causes death (e.g. giving a lethal dose).
  • Passive — death is allowed by withholding or withdrawing treatment that is keeping the person alive.

Assisted dying / assisted suicide is where someone helps a person to end their own life. In the UK, active euthanasia and assisting a suicide are against the law.

Key term — euthanasia: a "gentle" or "good" death; often called mercy killing. Note the difference between active and passive.

Issue: euthanasia

Views on euthanasia

A fair range of views

Many Christians: oppose active euthanasia (sanctity of life — only God chooses the moment of death), but many accept not using burdensome treatment that merely prolongs dying, and support hospice care to ease suffering.

Islam: generally opposes euthanasia, as life and death are Allah's decision; relieving pain is encouraged.

Judaism: generally opposes actively ending life, while allowing that death need not always be resisted at all costs.

Some non-religious & some religious people: support assisted dying for the terminally ill on quality-of-life and compassion grounds, with safeguards and consent.

Watch out

Common misconceptions

These slips lose marks and misrepresent people. Read them carefully:

  • "Religions have one fixed view." ❌ Every religion holds a range of views, and believers of the same faith often disagree.
  • "Active and passive euthanasia are the same." ❌ Active = a deliberate act causes death; passive = death is allowed by not giving treatment. Many people accept one but not the other.
  • "Sanctity of life means opposing all medical intervention." ❌ Believing life is sacred does not mean refusing treatment — most who hold it strongly support saving and caring for life.
  • "Non-religious people don't care about life." ❌ Secular and utilitarian thinkers value life and dignity too; they just reason from well-being rather than scripture.

Exam tip: use words like "many", "some" and "traditionally" rather than "all". It is more accurate and more respectful.

Issue: fertility

Fertility treatment & IVF

Some couples cannot have children naturally (childlessness / infertility). IVF (in-vitro fertilisation) fertilises an egg with sperm outside the body, then places an embryo in the womb.

A key issue is that IVF often creates spare embryos that may be frozen, donated, used in research, or allowed to perish — which raises the sanctity-of-life question again.

A fair range of views

Many Christians: welcome the desire for children. Some Catholics object to IVF because of the loss of spare embryos and because it separates conception from the natural act; other Christians accept it as a loving way to help a couple.

Islam: IVF is generally permitted within marriage, using the couple's own egg and sperm; donor eggs or sperm are usually not allowed.

Judaism: often supportive, as having children fulfils "be fruitful and multiply" (Genesis 1:28), though views on spare embryos vary.

Many non-religious views: generally support IVF as helping people to have a wanted family, weighing the well-being of all involved.

Key term — IVF: in-vitro fertilisation — joining egg and sperm in a lab, then placing an embryo in the womb.

Issue: embryo research

Embryo research, cloning & genetic engineering

An embryo is a very early stage of developing life, from fertilisation up to about eight weeks. Scientists can study spare IVF embryos to research diseases, and can alter genes (genetic engineering) or copy cells (cloning).

  • Therapeutic uses aim to treat illness (e.g. studying stem cells, gene therapy for genetic diseases).
  • Reproductive cloning (making a whole new copy of an organism) is far more controversial and is banned for humans in many countries.
A fair range of views

Sanctity-of-life view: an embryo is a human life from conception, so destroying embryos for research is wrong; "playing God" with genes is a further concern.

Supportive view (religious & non-religious): if research reduces suffering and cures disease, and uses embryos that would otherwise perish, it can be a compassionate, responsible use of knowledge.

Balance: both sides often share the goal of protecting life — they disagree about the status of the early embryo.

Issue: organ donation

Organ donation & transplants

Organ donation is giving an organ (such as a kidney or heart) to someone whose own has failed, either after death or, for some organs, while living.

A fair range of views

Many Christians: see donation as an act of love and generosity — "love your neighbour" (Mark 12:31) — that can save a life; a minority worry about treatment of the body.

Islam: scholars differ; many now permit donation to save a life (linking to Qur'an 5:32), while others are cautious about respect for the body.

Judaism: saving a life (pikuach nefesh) is a strong argument in favour; some are cautious about how death is defined.

Many non-religious views: generally support donation as maximising well-being and reducing suffering.

Notice the pattern: "saving a life" often supports donation, showing sanctity of life doesn't always oppose medical action.

Issue: animals in research

Using animals in medical research

Medicines are often tested on animals before people. This raises an ethical question about the value of animal life and human benefit.

  • In favour: humans are given a special place in creation ("stewardship"/dominion, Genesis 1:28) and testing can save many human lives.
  • Against / cautious: animals are part of God's creation and should not suffer needlessly; humans have a duty of care over them.
  • Non-religious views: weigh the benefit to people against animal suffering, often supporting strict welfare rules and alternatives where possible.

Even-handed: most people, religious or not, accept some limits — the debate is about where the line falls.

Exam skill

Writing balanced answers

Longer 4RS1 questions ask you to weigh up a statement (e.g. "Euthanasia is always wrong"). Strong answers:

  • Give arguments for and against, from more than one point of view.
  • Use religious teachings and sources accurately (e.g. sanctity of life, "love your neighbour", pikuach nefesh).
  • Include a non-religious view where relevant.
  • Reach a reasoned conclusion that responds to the arguments — not just an opinion.

Respectful language matters: present views you disagree with fairly and without mocking them. Examiners reward understanding, not point-scoring.

Recap

The essentials to know

Sanctity of life: life is holy and God-given.

Quality of life: how good/comfortable a life is (health, dignity, suffering).

Abortion: ending a pregnancy — views turn on when life begins.

Euthanasia: voluntary/non-voluntary, active/passive — a "gentle death".

IVF & embryos: helping childless couples; spare-embryo concerns.

Organ donation: "saving a life" often supports it.

Always: a range of religious and non-religious views, fairly stated.
