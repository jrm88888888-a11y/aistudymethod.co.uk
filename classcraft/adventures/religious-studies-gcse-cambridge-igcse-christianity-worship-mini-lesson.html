Worship

This mini-lesson covers the whole of Christianity Topic C1 — Worship in the Cambridge IGCSE 0490 syllabus: the church as building and as people; the key features of church buildings; the artefacts used in worship; public and private worship, including the Eucharist, Sunday services, prayer and the Lord’s Prayer; the use of the Bible; and the roles of leaders.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go and collect ⭐ stars. Christians disagree with one another about many of these things, so watch for the Different views flag — that is what part (c) of every exam question rewards. Press Start when you are ready.

C1.1 · the church

The church: a building and a people

The English word church does two jobs, and the syllabus wants both.

  • The building — a centre for worship and the centre of the Christian community: the place where people are baptised, married and buried, where the congregation gathers, and often where food banks, youth groups and choirs meet during the week.
  • The people — the community of believers itself. Paul tells the Corinthians, “you yourselves are God’s temple” (1 Corinthians 3:16), and Jesus says, “where two or three gather in my name, there am I with them” (Matthew 18:20). The very first Christians “devoted themselves to the apostles’ teaching and to fellowship, to the breaking of bread and to prayer” (Acts 2:42) — long before they had any buildings at all.

Why this matters in the exam: a question like “A church is just a building.” Discuss is asking you to weigh these two meanings against each other. Persecuted Christians and house churches worship with no building; Orthodox Christians would answer that a consecrated building is a foretaste of heaven.

C1.2 · key features

Inside a church: the key features

Different Christian communities design their buildings very differently, because they disagree about what worship is chiefly for.

  • Altar or holy table — where the bread and wine of the Eucharist are consecrated. Catholic and Orthodox churches usually call it an altar (a sacrifice is re-presented on it); many Protestant churches call it a holy table or communion table (a meal is shared at it). The word you choose is itself a theological claim.
  • Sanctuary — the holy area around the altar. In an Orthodox church it is screened off by the iconostasis, a wall of icons with royal doors.
  • Lectern — the reading stand the Bible is read from, often carved as an eagle.
  • Pulpit — the raised platform for preaching the sermon. In many Reformed, Baptist and Methodist churches the pulpit stands at the centre front, because the preached word is the heart of worship.
  • Baptistery — a pool for total immersion (Baptist and Pentecostal churches). Font — a container of holy water for pouring over an infant’s head.
  • Representations — statues, images, icons, stained glass and decoration.

C1.3 · artefacts

Artefacts used in worship

  • Copies of the Bible — often a large, decorated volume carried in procession. In an Orthodox liturgy the Gospel Book is kissed and censed.
  • Cross (empty) and crucifix (with the figure of Jesus on it). The crucifix keeps the suffering and sacrifice of Good Friday in view and is typical of Catholic churches; the empty cross proclaims the resurrection and is preferred in most Protestant churches. Both point to the same events, but they emphasise different halves of them.
  • Paten — the plate that holds the bread for Communion. Chalice — the cup that holds the wine.
  • Missal — the Catholic book of the liturgy of the Mass. Book of Common Prayer — the historic Anglican book of liturgy. Many denominations have their own service books; some free churches use none at all.
  • Lectionary — the ordered set of scriptural readings appointed for each day or feast, so that congregations across the world hear the same passages.

Different views: for Catholic and Orthodox Christians these objects are sacred and are treated with reverence (a chalice may be blessed and only handled by clergy). For many evangelical Christians the objects are simply useful tools; what matters is the faith of the worshipper, not the vessel.

C1.4a · the Eucharist

The Eucharist: one meal, many names

The central act of Christian communal worship goes by several names, and the name signals the belief:

  • Mass (Catholic) — Catholics believe in transubstantiation: the bread and wine truly become the body and blood of Christ, while the outward appearances remain. The Mass re-presents the one sacrifice of Calvary.
  • Divine Liturgy (Orthodox) — the bread and wine genuinely become Christ’s body and blood, but the Orthodox deliberately call it a mystery rather than explaining the mechanism.
  • Holy Communion / Eucharist (Anglican) — a broad range of views, from a real presence to a strongly memorial understanding.
  • The Lord’s Supper (Baptist, many free churches) — a memorial. The bread and wine (or grape juice) remain bread and wine; the service obeys Jesus’ command “do this in remembrance of me” (1 Corinthians 11:23–26).
  • Quakers and the Salvation Army celebrate no outward Eucharist at all, holding that the whole of life is sacramental.

The rite goes back to the Last Supper, where Jesus took bread and wine and said “this is my body… this is my blood of the covenant” (Mark 14:22–25).

Frequency varies too: daily Mass in a Catholic parish; a weekly Divine Liturgy; monthly or quarterly Communion in some Baptist and Presbyterian churches, precisely so that it does not become routine.

C1.4 b–d · services

Sunday, Sabbath, daily services and confession

  • Sunday worship — most Christians keep Sunday, the day of the resurrection, as the Lord’s Day. Seventh-day Adventists and some other groups keep the Saturday Sabbath instead, following the fourth of the Ten Commandments literally. The syllabus expects you to know that the Sabbath could be either day.
  • Daily services — in cathedrals, monasteries and convents, the Divine Office (Morning Prayer, Evening Prayer/Vespers, Compline) is sung every day. Many Catholics attend a short weekday Mass.
  • Acts of devotion — lighting a candle, praying the rosary, venerating an icon, the Stations of the Cross, private adoration of the Blessed Sacrament.
  • Confession and reconciliation — in Catholic and Orthodox practice, the sacrament of Reconciliation: the penitent confesses to a priest, who gives absolution and a penance. Most Protestants confess directly to God, often in a general confession said by the whole congregation, because they hold that no human mediator is needed.
C1.4e · prayer

Prayer — and the Lord’s Prayer

The syllabus names five kinds of prayer. Learn them by their purpose:

A · T · I · P · CAdoration · Thanksgiving · Intercession · Petition · Confession
  • Adoration — praising God simply for who God is.
  • Thanksgiving — thanking God for what has been given.
  • Intercession — praying for other people (the sick, the bereaved, those at war).
  • Petition — asking God for something for yourself.
  • Confession — admitting sin and asking forgiveness.

The Lord’s Prayer (Matthew 6:9–13; a shorter form at Luke 11:2–4) is the model Jesus gave, and it contains nearly all of these at once: “hallowed be your name” is adoration; “your kingdom come, your will be done” submits to God; “give us today our daily bread” is petition; “forgive us our debts, as we also have forgiven our debtors” is confession, and ties God’s forgiveness of us to our forgiveness of others; “lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from the evil one” asks for protection.

Public and private: Jesus warns against praying showily “to be seen by others” and tells his followers to pray in a room with the door shut (Matthew 6:5–8) — yet he also gives the prayer in the plural: “Our Father”. Christian prayer is both personal and corporate.

C1.4 f–i · Bible & contemplation

Contemplation, meditation and the Bible

  • Contemplation and meditation — silent, wordless attentiveness to God. Practised by monks and nuns for centuries, and in the modern Quaker meeting, where the congregation may sit in silence for an hour and speak only if moved to.
  • The Bible in public worship — set readings (from the lectionary) are heard by everyone. In many Protestant churches the sermon expounds the passage and is the longest part of the service; in an Orthodox liturgy the Gospel Book is processed, censed and kissed.
  • The Bible in private study — daily reading, often with notes; lectio divina, a slow, prayerful reading of a short passage.
  • Respectful treatment — many Christians will not place another book on top of a Bible, will stand for the Gospel reading, and will not deface or discard a Bible carelessly. Others argue that the words matter and the paper does not, so a Bible app is just as holy as a leather-bound copy.

Different views on the Bible’s role: for evangelical Christians, Scripture is the supreme authority, and worship exists largely to hear it preached. For Catholics, Scripture is read alongside the Church’s tradition and teaching authority. For Orthodox Christians it is inseparable from the liturgy in which it is proclaimed.

C1.5 · leaders

Leaders in the church

  • The Pope — Bishop of Rome and, for Catholics, the successor of St Peter and visible head of the worldwide Church.
  • Bishops — in Catholic, Orthodox and Anglican churches, bishops oversee a diocese, ordain clergy and confirm candidates. Their authority is traced through apostolic succession back to the apostles.
  • Priests (Catholic, Orthodox, many Anglicans) — consecrate the Eucharist, absolve sins, preach and pastor. Ministers and pastors (Methodist, Baptist, Pentecostal and other free churches) — preach, teach and lead the congregation, but are usually understood as set apart for a function, not given a distinct priestly power.
  • No ordained clergy at all — Quakers have none, because they believe every worshipper can be led directly by the Spirit. Some Brethren congregations are led by unpaid elders. Their scriptural argument is the priesthood of all believers: “you are… a royal priesthood” (1 Peter 2:9).

Live debate: churches disagree about who may be ordained. The Church of England has ordained women as priests since 1994, and after the law was changed in 2014 the first woman bishop was consecrated in 2015; the Catholic and Orthodox churches ordain only men. Both sides appeal to Scripture and tradition, which is why this makes such a good part (c) question.

Exam skill · part (c)

Answering the 8-mark evaluation

Every 0490 question ends with an 8-mark part (c) worth 40% of the marks. It asks you to evaluate different points of view and reach a judgement. Take this one:

“Christians do not need a church building in order to worship.” Discuss this statement.
How to build the answer

One view (agree): the church is the people (Acts 2:42; Matthew 18:20). House churches, prison chaplaincies and persecuted congregations worship with no building. Quakers need only a room and silence.

Another view (disagree): the building is the centre of the community, and for Orthodox and Catholic Christians the consecrated altar, the reserved sacrament and the icons are not decoration but the means by which God is encountered. Baptism needs a font or baptistery; the Eucharist needs an altar.

Judgement: take a side and give the reason. E.g. “Worship is possible anywhere, and the New Testament church proves it — but for the sacramental traditions the building is where the community is made, so ‘not needed’ overstates the case. It is better to say the building is valuable but not essential.”

Marks are lost by describing. Listing what is inside a church earns nothing here. You must weigh views against each other and land on a reasoned conclusion.
