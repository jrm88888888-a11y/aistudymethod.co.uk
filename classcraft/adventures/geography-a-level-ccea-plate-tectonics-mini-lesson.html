Mission 1

Mission 1: Coal in Antarctica, fossils across an ocean: what finally proved that continents move?

Make a guess: Coal in Antarctica, fossils across an ocean: what finally proved that continents move?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • A2-1.A1.i evidence for, and the theory of, plate tectonics
Mission 1

Inside the Earth

  • Diameter about 12,750 km.
  • Crust: about 5 km under oceans; about 30 km on average under continents; up to 100 km under big mountain ranges.
  • Mantle: hot, dense rock, about 2,900 km thick; solid, but able to flow very slowly.
  • Core: iron–nickel; liquid outer core (2,200 km) and solid inner core (1,250 km).
Mission 1

Lithosphere and asthenosphere

  • Lithosphere: crust plus the cooler, rigid upper mantle; averages at least 80 km thick; broken into plates.
  • Asthenosphere: below it; hot, solid rock that "can soften and flow" over geological time.
  • The rigid lithosphere is thought to move on the slowly flowing asthenosphere.
  • Not the same as crust/mantle: the boundary is about strength, not composition.
Quick check
?Which statement about the lithosphere is correct?
Mission 1

Wegener's continental drift

  • 1912: German meteorologist Alfred Wegener set out continental drift in two articles; book in 1915.
  • About 200 million years ago a supercontinent, Pangaea, began to split apart.
  • Fit: the "remarkable fit" of South America and Africa.
  • Geological fit: unusual rock structures match on the two coasts.
Mission 1

Fossils and ancient climates

  • Fossils: identical fossil species on the matching coasts of Africa and South America, now an ocean apart.
  • Glossopteris: distinctive fossil ferns in now-polar regions.
  • Coal in Antarctica: fossils of tropical plants; Antarctica must once have lain nearer the equator.
  • Ancient glaciations: glacial deposits in now-arid Africa, e.g. the Vaal River valley, South Africa.
  • Wegener: these fit if the southern continents were once joined, spanning "from the equator to the South Pole".
Mission 1

Why scientists rejected drift

  • No mechanism: what force could move continents?
  • Wegener said continents ploughed through the ocean floor; geophysicist Harold Jeffreys argued solid rock could not do that without breaking up.
  • Wegener died in 1930 on the Greenland ice cap; the debate stalled.
  • Lesson: good evidence still needs a workable explanation.
Quick check
?Coal formed from tropical plants is found in Antarctica. What does this most directly suggest?
Mission 1

The ocean floor speaks (1940s–50s)

  • Echo sounding after 1918 showed a rugged sea floor.
  • 1947: sediment on the Atlantic floor was much thinner than expected for old oceans.
  • 1950s: a mid-ocean ridge more than 50,000 km long winds around the globe.
  • New questions: why so little sediment? Why a ridge?
Mission 1

Palaeomagnetism: magnetic stripes

  • Lava contains magnetite; as it cools, grains lock in the direction of Earth's magnetic field.
  • Rocks show normal and reversed polarity: the field has flipped many times.
  • 1950s surveys: stripes of normal and reversed rock, laid out in rows on either side of the ridge.
  • 1963: Vine, Matthews and Morley proposed the stripes record field reversals; by 1966 the stripe pattern matched dated reversals.
Mission 1

Sea-floor spreading and sea-floor age

  • Sea-floor spreading (Hess; term coined by Dietz): magma rises at ridges, makes new crust and spreads outwards.
  • Rocks are youngest at the ridge crest and get older away from it.
  • Oldest dated ocean floor: about 180 million years.
  • 1968: Glomar Challenger drill cores across the Mid-Atlantic Ridge gave "the clinching evidence".
  • Old crust sinks at trenches, so Earth does not grow; this also explains thin sediment.
Quick check
?The two sides of the Mid-Atlantic Ridge move apart at about 2.5 cm per year, or 25 km per million years. Two strips of rock formed together at the ridge 4 million years ago. How far apart are they now?
Mission 1

Confirmation: earthquakes and GPS

  • Earthquakes cluster along trenches and ridges, exactly where crust is destroyed and made.
  • Deep, inclined earthquake zones under trenches (Wadati–Benioff zones) trace sinking slabs.
  • Today, satellite methods such as GPS measure plate movement directly.
  • Spreading rates range from under 2.5 cm/yr (Arctic Ridge) to over 15 cm/yr (East Pacific Rise near Easter Island).
Mission 1

The theory of plate tectonics

  • Earth's outer layer is broken into "a dozen or more large and small plates that are moving relative to one another as they ride atop hotter, more mobile material".
  • Drift (1912) described what happened; plate tectonics (1960s) explained how: spreading at ridges, destruction at trenches.

?A survey of a newly mapped ridge in a remote ocean finds four patterns. Which one most strongly supports sea-floor spreading at that ridge?

Mission 2

Earth's structure and the plates

  • Crust: oceanic about 5 km thick; continental about 30 km on average, up to 100 km under mountains.
  • Mantle about 2,900 km thick; core: liquid outer (about 2,200 km) and solid inner (about 1,250 km).
  • Plates are pieces of rigid lithosphere (crust plus the rigid uppermost mantle, at least 80 km thick over much of Earth) moving over the hot asthenosphere: solid rock that can flow very slowly.
Mission 2

What moves the plates?

  • Earth's heat comes from radioactive decay and residual heat; hot material rises and cold, dense material sinks: mantle convection.
  • Ridge push: hot, elevated new lithosphere slides "downhill" away from a mid-ocean ridge.
  • Slab pull: a cold, dense slab sinking at a subduction zone drags the plate behind it. Plates attached to slabs (Pacific, Australian, Nazca) move fastest.
  • USGS: no mechanism "can explain all the facets of plate movement".
Quick check
?Which observation is the strongest evidence that slab pull matters more than ridge push?
Mission 2

Divergent (constructive) margins

  • Plates move apart; magma rises and builds an ocean ridge. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge spreads about 2.5 cm a year; the East Pacific Rise more than 15 cm a year.
  • Iceland sits on the ridge; rifting at Krafla opened about 7 m in 1975–84.
  • On continents, stretching forms rift valleys: a new spreading centre may be developing along the East African Rift; spreading opened the Red Sea.
  • Shallow earthquakes; basaltic, mostly effusive eruptions.
Mission 2

Convergent: oceanic–continental

  • The oceanic Nazca Plate is subducted under the South American Plate, forming a deep-sea trench and lifting the Andes.
  • This convergence "sustains many of the Earth's active volcanoes"; strong earthquakes are common, some very deep (636 km under Bolivia).
  • Water released from the sinking slab helps the mantle above it melt, feeding viscous, explosive eruptions.
Mission 2

Convergent: oceanic–oceanic

  • One oceanic plate sinks beneath the other, forming a trench and a curved chain of volcanic islands: an island arc (the Marianas; the Aleutians).
  • Challenger Deep, in the Mariana Trench, is about 10,935 m deep.
  • Frequent moderate to strong earthquakes along the descending plate.
Mission 2

Convergent: continental–continental (collision)

  • When two continents meet, "neither is subducted because the continental rocks are relatively light".
  • India's collision with Asia, about 50 million years ago, crumpled the crust into the Himalaya: fold mountains.
  • CCEA calls this a collision margin.
  • Strong shallow earthquakes; little or no volcanic activity.
Quick check
?Which landform pair is matched to the correct convergent margin?
Mission 2

Conservative (transform) margins

  • Plates slide horizontally past each other; most transform faults are on the ocean floor, offsetting ridges.
  • The San Andreas fault zone is about 1,300 km long; the Pacific Plate has moved past the North American Plate at about 5 cm a year for 10 million years.
  • Shallow earthquakes; little or no vulcanicity, because crust is neither made nor destroyed.
  • Exam move: for each margin, give the process, the landform and a located example.
Mission check

?The Pacific Plate is almost ringed by subduction zones and is one of the fastest-moving plates. What does this suggest moves plates?

Internal heat (radioactive decay and residual heat) drives convection; gravity turns it into motion through slab pull and ridge push. The Atlantic widens as plates move apart at the Mid-Atlantic Ridge.

Mission 3

What makes an eruption explosive

  • Magma composition, viscosity and gas content largely decide whether an eruption is explosive.
  • Silica has the greatest effect on viscosity; most igneous rocks hold about 45–78% silica by weight.
  • Low-silica basalt is runny: at Kīlauea, lava flow fronts move up to 10 km/h. Gas escapes easily, so eruptions are effusive.
  • Silica-rich magma is sticky and traps gas, so pressure builds until it erupts explosively.
Mission 3

Eruption style follows the setting

  • Constructive (divergent) margins: basalt from the mantle; effusive fissure eruptions. Reykjanes, Iceland, is where the Mid-Atlantic Ridge rises above sea level.
  • Destructive (convergent) margins: the sinking plate feeds more silica-rich, gas-rich magma; explosive stratovolcanoes. Fuego sits where the Cocos Plate subducts beneath the Caribbean Plate.
  • Hot spots: plumes away from margins. Hawaii is more than 3,200 km from the nearest plate boundary; its eruptions are effusive.
Quick check
?A new eruption produces a column of ash many kilometres high from a steep cone above a subduction zone. Which magma best explains it?
Mission 3

Primary hazards: lava and pyroclastic flows

  • Lava flows are usually slow enough to walk away from, so they mostly destroy property: at Grindavík in January 2024, lava burned three homes.
  • Pyroclastic flows: avalanches of hot rock fragments and gas, typically faster than 80 km/h and 200–700 °C. They destroy nearly everything in their path.
  • Their speed means evacuation before they start is the only reliable protection.
Mission 3

Primary hazards: tephra, ash and gases

  • Tephra: any rock fragments blasted into the air, from fine ash to large bombs.
  • Ash is "hard, does not dissolve in water, is extremely abrasive and mildly corrosive". It can collapse roofs, and wet ash can weigh twice as much as dry. Jet engines have failed after flying through thin ash.
  • Gases: water vapour is the most abundant; carbon dioxide and sulfur dioxide (SO₂) matter most.
  • SO₂ "can cause acid rain and air pollution downwind". CO₂ is dense and collects in hollows: three ski patrol members died at Mammoth Mountain, California, after falling into a CO₂-filled snow depression.
Quick check
?Why does ash fall kill more people when heavy rain falls at the same time?
Mission 3

Secondary hazard: lahars

  • Lahars (volcanic mudflows) are slurries of debris and water that look like wet concrete.
  • Water comes from melting snow and ice, heavy rain, or crater lakes and rivers.
  • They often grow to more than 10 times their starting size, and can exceed 200 km/h on steep slopes.
  • They follow river valleys far from the volcano, and outlast the eruption: rain after Fuego's 2018 eruption produced many lahars.
Mission 3

Benefits of volcanic activity

  • Energy: about 90% of the energy used for home heating in Iceland comes from geothermal energy (2020).
  • Infrastructure worth protecting: Iceland built barriers to keep lava from the Svartsengi power plant.
  • Soils and tourism: weathered volcanic rock forms fertile soils, and volcanic landscapes draw visitors.
  • Weigh the benefits against the hazards for each place: the same plate setting brings both.
Mission 3

Armero, 1985

  • Nevado del Ruiz (Colombia) is a 5,389 m stratovolcano topped by glaciers.
  • On 13 November 1985 an explosive eruption melted ice. Lahars raced down river valleys and reached Armero, 45 km away, about two hours later.
  • Within minutes about 23,000 people, most of the town, were killed.
  • Why no escape: scientists lacked the equipment and government support to monitor the volcano, the government was focused on political unrest, and residents had been told they were safe. USGS: two hours was "plenty of time" to evacuate.
  • Impacts: the lahars were a secondary hazard. The deaths at Armero came from them, not from primary hazards near the vent.
Mission check

?A town of 20,000 lies in a river valley 50 km from an ice-capped stratovolcano. No lava could reach it. How could an eruption still kill most of its people hours later?

At Armero, melted ice made lahars that followed the valleys 45 km to the town in about two hours. Weak monitoring and governance meant no warning reached people who had time to climb to higher ground.

Mission 4

Mission 4: The same eruption that buries homes can build new land. Is a volcano a hazard or a resource?

Make a guess: The same eruption that buries homes can build new land. Is a volcano a hazard or a resource?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • A2-1.A2.ii socio-economic and environmental hazards and benefits of volcanic activity
Mission 4

Four kinds of effect

  • About 800 million people live within 100 km of an active volcano, in 86 countries.
  • Volcanic activity brings hazards and benefits.
  • Each can be:
    • socio-economic (lives, homes, jobs, money);
    • environmental (air, water, soil, ecosystems, land).
  • One eruption often does all four.
Mission 4

Socio-economic hazards

  • Kīlauea, Hawaiʻi, 2018: lava covered over 700 structures and 48 km of road; 35.5 km² buried.
  • Armero, Colombia, 1985: a lahar from Nevado del Ruiz, 45 km away, killed about 23,000 people.
  • Pinatubo, Philippines, 1991: thousands of roofs collapsed under wet ash; later lahars "caused more destruction than the eruption itself".
  • Losses: homes, roads, farmland, jobs and, where warnings fail, lives.
Mission 4

Environmental hazards

  • Gases: SO₂ "can cause acid rain and air pollution downwind".
  • CO₂ is dense and pools in hollows: three ski patrol members were killed at Mammoth Mountain, California.
  • Climate: the USGS reports that Pinatubo released "nearly 20 million tons" of SO₂ and cooled the planet by about 0.5 °C.
  • Land: lava and ash bury soils and habitats.
Quick check
?Which is an environmental hazard of volcanic activity?
Mission 4

Benefit: soils (with a catch)

  • Andisols are "soils that have formed in volcanic ash or other volcanic ejecta".
  • They have high water-holding capacity and support "some very productive forests".
  • But they can "fix" large quantities of phosphorus, making it unavailable to plants.
  • They cover only about 1% of ice-free land. Fertile, yes, but not automatically, and not widespread.
Mission 4

Benefit: geothermal energy

  • Iceland (on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge): about 90% of home heating came from geothermal energy in 2020.
  • Kenya (East African Rift): KenGen had 534 MW of geothermal capacity by June 2019, at Olkaria near Naivasha.
  • KenGen says "more than 50%" of energy consumed by Kenyans comes from the Olkaria fields.
  • Benefit and hazard meet: at Grindavík, barriers were built to protect a town and a geothermal power plant from lava.
Quick check
?Why can Iceland and Kenya both use geothermal energy on a large scale?
Mission 4

Benefits: new land, minerals and tourism

  • New land: Kīlauea 2018 added 875 acres of new land at the island's eastern edge (about 3.5 km², calculated).
  • Minerals: volcanic and hydrothermal activity concentrates useful materials such as sulfur and metal ores.
  • Tourism: eruptions, lava fields and hot springs draw visitors and jobs.
  • Each benefit carries risk: people working or walking near vents face gases; three ski patrol members died at Mammoth Mountain.
Quick check
?At Kīlauea in 2018, lava covered about 8,700 acres and created 875 acres of new land. Roughly what share of the area covered was new land?
Mission 4

Why the balance varies

  • Time: hazards are sudden; benefits (soils, heat, tourism) build over years to centuries.
  • Scale: lahars and lava are local; SO₂ cooling can be global.
  • Who gains and who loses: an energy company may gain while displaced residents lose.
  • Management changes the balance: Pinatubo forecasts saved at least 5,000 lives and $250 million of property.
Mission 4

Exam move: hazard–benefit grid

  • Use a 2 × 2 grid: socio-economic / environmental × hazard / benefit.
  • Put a named place with a fact in each cell (for example, Kīlauea lava on homes; Iceland geothermal heat).
  • Then judge: for whom, and over what time scale, do benefits outweigh hazards?
Mission check

?A village farms the rich slopes of an active stratovolcano, in a valley 30 km from the summit. Which plan keeps the benefit and best reduces the main hazard?

Volcanic activity brings socio-economic and environmental hazards (lava, lahars, gases, cooling) and benefits (soils, geothermal energy, new land, minerals, tourism). Which dominates depends on time scale, distance, who is affected and how well the hazard is monitored and managed.

Mission 5

Mission 5: On Fuego, in 2018, a golf resort evacuated safely. A village two kilometres further on was buried. Why?

Make a guess: On Fuego, in 2018, a golf resort evacuated safely. A village two kilometres further on was buried. Why?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • A2-1.A2.iii evaluate how a country prepares for and responds to volcanic activity (small-scale case study)
Mission 5

Two plate settings

  • Fuego, Guatemala: a stratovolcano in the Central American arc, where the Cocos Plate subducts beneath the Caribbean Plate (destructive margin).
  • Reykjanes Peninsula, Iceland: where the Mid-Atlantic Ridge rises above sea level; a rift zone (constructive margin).
  • Expect explosive eruptions and pyroclastic flows at Fuego; effusive fissure eruptions of runny lava at Reykjanes.
Mission 5

Fuego, 3 June 2018: the event

  • The first pyroclastic flows came at 10:00. Activity peaked at about 13:10, with a column 16–19 km high.
  • At 15:05 a large pulse began; at 15:09 it hit the Las Lajas bridge and overflowed onto the RN-14 road towards San Miguel Los Lotes.
  • About 15.1 million m³ of pyroclastic flow material was deposited in Las Lajas, more than double the average since 1999.
  • In the rainy weeks that followed, rain produced many lahars.
Mission 5

Fuego: impacts

  • Social: about two-thirds of the north-western part of San Miguel Los Lotes suffered "total inundation, destruction, and burial". An estimated 5,000 people lost their homes.
  • The death toll is uncertain: official counts of the missing are 332 (Naismith et al., 2020) and 430 (Charbonnier et al., 2023); survivors' estimates are far higher.
  • Economic: the flows overran the RN-14 road at the Las Lajas bridge.
Quick check
?Two papers give 332 and 430 people officially missing at Fuego, and survivors' estimates are far higher. What should an exam answer do?
Mission 5

Fuego: why outcomes differed

  • The private golf resort of La Reunión evacuated successfully; Los Lotes, about 2 km further south, suffered extensive loss of life.
  • Researchers found La Reunión had "a culture of preventative self-evacuation". They call residents' view that wealth decided who survived "debatable".
  • Scientists issued information from 06:30, and authorities "persistently tried to remove people from high-risk areas", but expected residents to judge the volcano and leave by themselves.
  • Locals weighed security fears and livelihoods; they clearly remember only eruptions that disrupted daily life.
Quick check
?Fuego residents clearly remember only the eruptions that disrupted daily life. What does this show about hazard perception?
Mission 5

Reykjanes, 2023–24: the event

  • From 27 October 2023 magma accumulated beneath Svartsengi; on 10 November it forced a dike beneath the Sundhnúkur crater row and Grindavík.
  • The town of 3,700 people was evacuated that day (IFRC; the Global Volcanism Program gives about 4,000). By December half the town had dropped by more than a metre.
  • Fissure eruptions followed from December 2023. On 14 January 2024 a fissure opened at the edge of town and its lava burned three homes.
Mission 5

Reykjanes: responses and impacts

  • Responses: barriers of earth and rock, built from November 2023, diverted lava away from Grindavík and the Svartsengi power plant.
  • Economic: Iceland's Natural Catastrophe Insurance (NTÍ) covers buildings damaged by eruptions, earthquakes and landslides.
  • Social: a whole town displaced from its homes and schools.
Mission 5

Evaluating Iceland's preparation and response

  • Monitoring: GPS recorded 35 cm of uplift at Svartsengi by mid-December 2023; magma volumes were compared with those before earlier eruptions.
  • Evacuation: ordered on 10 November, the day the dike formed beneath the town.
  • Mitigation: barriers diverted lava, but on 14 January 2024 a fissure opened outside the barrier and burned three homes.
  • Recovery: insurance and a state buy-out. Judgement: lives were protected; the town as a community was not.
Quick check
?Which Icelandic response builds financial resilience for households?
Mission 5

Comparing Fuego and Reykjanes

  • Hazard: Fuego's fast flows spilled out of their ravine; Reykjanes' lava was slow enough to divert.
  • Warning and action: at Fuego, evacuation was largely left to residents; Grindavík was evacuated weeks before the first eruption.
  • Recovery: Iceland had insurance and a state buy-out; at Fuego, the documented gap is in evacuation practice and communication.
  • Exam move: the plate setting explains the type of hazard; development and governance explain the impact.
Mission check

?At Pinatubo in 1991, scientists gave officials an alert system, and forecasts saved at least 5,000 lives. Which lesson from Fuego does this support?

Fast pyroclastic flows spilled from the Las Lajas ravine. La Reunión had a routine of preventative self-evacuation; Los Lotes depended on residents leaving by themselves, weighing security and livelihoods. Where warnings are agreed and acted on, as at Pinatubo and Grindavík, far fewer die.

Mission 6

Focus, epicentre and depth

  • The focus (hypocentre) is where rupture starts underground; the epicentre is the point on the surface above it.
  • Depth classes: shallow 0–70 km, intermediate 70–300 km, deep 300–700 km.
  • Shallow-focus earthquakes occur at every type of margin. Deep-focus ones occur only inside slabs sinking at subduction zones.
Mission 6

P, S and L waves

  • Rupture on a fault fractures the crust and releases energy as seismic waves.
  • P waves (primary): compressional, fastest, 5–7 km/s in typical crust.
  • S waves (secondary): shear waves, about 60% of P-wave speed; they cannot travel through liquids.
  • L waves (surface: Love and Rayleigh): slowest, but with the largest ground motions; they "cause buildings to fail".
Quick check
?Take P waves at 6.0 km/s and S waves at 3.5 km/s in crust (within the ranges earlier in this mission). The S wave arrives 20 s after the P wave. How far away is the earthquake?
Mission 6

Ground shaking and displacement

  • Ground shaking is the main primary hazard: surface waves cause buildings to fail and fall.
  • Ground displacement: the fault itself moves. Kaikōura (New Zealand), 2016: the rupture "unzipped" about 180 km in nearly 2 minutes, displacing the ground by up to 12 m.
  • More than 20 faults ruptured, a world record; parts of the coast rose by up to 6.5 m.
Mission 6

Liquefaction

  • Liquefaction: loosely packed, saturated sediments lose strength in strong shaking.
  • Buildings sink or tilt, and buried pipes can float up.
  • Christchurch (New Zealand): in March 2012 the government rezoned 251 more properties red, citing "widespread liquefaction".
Mission 6

Landslides

  • Shaking triggers landslides on steep slopes: at least 25,000 in Nepal in 2015; over 10,000 at Kaikōura in 2016.
Quick check
?Which site is most at risk of liquefaction?
Mission 6

How a tsunami forms

  • At a subduction zone the plates lock, then the fault slips in a great earthquake.
  • The sea bed is thrust up (or drops) and lifts the whole water column above it: a tsunami.
  • In deep water a tsunami travels at 800–1,000 km/h with a wavelength of 500–1,000 km; near shore it slows to 30–50 km/h, and its height and steepness grow.
Mission 6

Tsunami impacts

  • 2004, Indian Ocean (Mw 9.1, Sumatra–Andaman): the tsunami killed over 230,000 people. The USGS records deaths in 10 countries.
  • 2011, Tōhoku (Japan): the tsunami overtopped seawalls of mainly 5–10 m; more than 15,000 people died. Inundation was much wider than hazard maps predicted.
  • Near the source there is little time: at Kaikōura the first wave arrived within 10 minutes, with a 6.9 m run-up at Goose Bay.
Quick check
?A tsunami crosses 4,000 km of deep ocean at 800 km/h. How long does it take?
Mission check

?A great earthquake ruptures a subduction zone on one side of an ocean. Seven hours later a coast 6,000 km away floods, though nobody there felt any shaking. What happened?

In 2004 the sea bed moved along the Sumatra–Andaman subduction zone and lifted the whole water column. The tsunami crossed the Indian Ocean at deep-water speeds of 800–1,000 km/h and killed people in 10 countries.

Mission 7

Prediction or forecast?

  • A prediction states that an earthquake will occur in a set place, time window and magnitude range.
  • A forecast gives a probability that it will occur.
  • The USGS: "Neither the USGS nor any other scientists have ever predicted a major earthquake".
  • Two ideas have been tested as routes to prediction: seismic gap theory and dilatancy.
Mission 7

Seismic gap theory

  • Elastic rebound: strain builds on a locked fault, then is released in an earthquake.
  • Just after a large earthquake, another on that segment is unlikely; the chance then rises as strain renews.
  • Seismic gap: a segment of a plate boundary with no large earthquake for a long part of its usual recurrence interval.
  • Idea: gaps are where the next large earthquake is most likely. Set out in the 1960s; quantitative models by the early 1990s.
Quick check
?Along a plate boundary, segments X and Y ruptured in large earthquakes 10 years ago. Segment Z between them last ruptured 140 years ago. What does seismic gap theory predict?
Mission 7

A gap that filled: Maule, Chile, 2010

  • 27 February 2010: Mw 8.8 earthquake at Maule, central Chile.
  • It "appears to have been a gap-filling rupture of a locked subduction megathrust".
  • GPS had shown the plate boundary there was locked: "It has been known for 10 years that the site … was fully locked and ready to break".
  • The gap showed where. It never showed when.
Mission 7

Testing the theory

  • Kagan and Jackson tested gap forecasts against over 40 large (M ≥ 7) earthquakes in the following decade.
  • Result: the hypothesis of raised potential after a long quiet period "can be rejected with a large confidence".
  • Places with recent large earthquakes had higher hazard; long-quiet segments "remained relatively quiet".
  • So a recent earthquake does not make a plate boundary safer.
Mission 7

Parkfield: a forecast put to the test

  • Parkfield, on the San Andreas fault, California: six M≈6 earthquakes since 1857, the last in 1966.
  • Mean recurrence interval: 22 years.
  • A prediction experiment began there in 1984. The USGS estimated the next earthquake would come before January 1993, with 95% confidence.
  • It came on 28 September 2004, more than ten years beyond the window.
  • It had no obvious precursors: reliable short-term prediction "still is not achievable".
Quick check
?Using Parkfield's mean recurrence interval of 22 years, in which year was the next earthquake expected after 1966?
Mission 7

Dilatancy (dilation)

  • Proposed in 1973 as "a physical basis" for prediction.
  • Before failure, stressed rock opens microcracks and expands: dilatancy (CCEA: dilation).
  • Water then flows into the new cracks (dilatancy–diffusion).
  • Expected warning signs: a change in the ratio of P-wave to S-wave speed (Vp/Vs), and more radon gas escaping from cracked rock.
Mission 7

Did the signs appear?

  • 1970s: Vp/Vs changes of about 10% were reported before earthquakes in California and the Soviet Union.
  • Repeat measurements found only small (less than 1–2%) and "poorly reproducible" changes.
  • Radon: laboratory tests showed more radon is released as rock fractures.
  • But in the field, pre-earthquake anomalies were only about 10% of all radon anomalies recorded, and no causal link has been shown.
Quick check
?Why has dilatancy not given reliable earthquake predictions?
Mission 7

From prediction to preparation

  • Haicheng, China, 4 February 1975: people evacuated hours before the mainshock; it is credited with saving many lives, but no similar scheme has had a comparable success.
  • The USGS: "The next large earthquake in China had no precursors and thousands of people died".
  • So agencies use probabilities for long-term hazard and invest in safer buildings.
  • Chile 2010 caused "substantially less damage and loss of life relative to its size", largely because Chile "enforces high seismic safety standards".
  • Exam move (CCEA "Evaluate"): say what each method can do (gaps: where, roughly), what it cannot do (when), and judge their value against preparedness.
Mission check

?A subduction-zone segment has had no large earthquake for 150 years, while the segments on either side ruptured recently. Last month radon in local wells rose sharply. What is the best-supported advice?

Seismic gap theory can flag where strain may be stored but has failed statistical tests and cannot give a date; dilatancy signals proved small and unreliable. That is why the focus is on probabilities and preparedness.

Mission 8

Nepal, 25 April 2015: the event

  • Mw 7.8 (USGS) on Saturday 25 April at 11:56 local time. The epicentre was near Barpak, Gorkha district, about 76 km north-west of Kathmandu.
  • Thrust faulting where India converges with Eurasia at about 45 mm a year; the focus was about 8 km deep.
  • The rupture spread east, towards Kathmandu. Aftershocks included an M7.3 on 12 May.
  • Secondary hazards: at least 25,000 landslides; an avalanche killed 20 people at Everest Base Camp.
Mission 8

Nepal: social impacts

  • Over 8,790 deaths and 22,300 injuries by June 2015; the lives of about 8 million people, almost a third of the population, were affected.
  • Over half a million houses destroyed; nearly 7,000 schools completely or significantly damaged.
  • More women and girls died than men and boys, partly because of gendered roles that kept women indoors.
  • Data: USGS lists at least 8,669 killed and 384 missing, including the 12 May aftershock; EM-DAT gives 8,969. Counts differ by date and source.
Mission 8

Nepal: economic, environmental and political impacts

  • Economic: damage and losses of about US$7 billion, about one-third of GDP (2013–14). At least 700,000 more people were expected to fall into poverty.
  • Environmental: weakened slopes left the region more prone to landslides and floods in the monsoon.
  • Primary or secondary? Deaths and collapse from shaking are primary impacts; landslide damage and the poverty that followed are secondary.
Mission 8

Nepal: why vulnerability varied

  • Between communities: "poorer rural areas" were hit hardest; the six lowest-HDI districts with high losses per person included Sindhupalchowk, Gorkha and Dolakha.
  • Buildings: many houses "did not have any seismic-resistant features".
  • Isolation: road damage further isolated rural areas.
  • Timing: a Saturday, so schools were closed; a night-time quake would "certainly" have killed more.
Mission 8

Nepal: responses, short and long term

  • Short term: 134 international search-and-rescue teams from 34 countries; 4,236 helicopter flights; 7,558 people rescued by air and 4,689 by land.
  • Neighbours, especially young people, dug others out of the rubble. Transit shelters opened in Kathmandu, but tarpaulins ran short.
  • Long term: the housing programme built over 700,000 earthquake-resilient houses. Grants of NPR 300,000 came in three parts, paid only after engineers' inspections.
Quick check
?Which evidence best shows vulnerability varying within communities in Nepal?
Mission 8

Kaikōura, 14 November 2016: the event

  • Mw 7.8 at 12:02 a.m. on Monday 14 November; focus about 15 km deep.
  • Oblique-reverse faulting on the Pacific–Australia plate boundary, which moves about 40 mm a year.
  • More than 20 faults ruptured over about 180 km, a world record.
  • Secondary hazards: over 10,000 landslides, more than 200 damming rivers; coastal uplift up to 6.5 m; a tsunami with a 6.9 m run-up at Goose Bay.
Mission 8

Kaikōura: impacts and responses

  • Social: two deaths, one from a heart attack and one when a homestead collapsed. Landslides cut road and rail, stranding many visitors.
  • Response: road access to Kaikōura was restored before Christmas 2016; rail reopened in September 2017 and State Highway 1 in December 2017.
  • Economic: about 40,000 claims went to the state Earthquake Commission.
Mission 8

Evaluating New Zealand's preparation and response

  • Prepared: the Building Code requires buildings to withstand earthquake loads; homeowners with private insurance also get state hazard cover.
  • Responded: road access restored within weeks; rail in about 10 months and SH1 in about 13; about 40,000 claims handled.
  • Limits: landslides cut road and rail links and stranded visitors; full rail and SH1 links took about 10 and 13 months to restore.
  • Judgement: preparation kept deaths to two; isolation was the weak point.
Mission 8

Comparing Nepal and Kaikōura

  • Hazard: similar. Both Mw 7.8 and shallow, with thousands of landslides.
  • Buildings: many Nepali houses had no seismic-resistant features; New Zealand's Building Code requires buildings to withstand earthquake loads.
  • Exposure: Nepal's rupture spread towards the crowded Kathmandu Valley.
  • Recovery: New Zealand homeowners had state hazard insurance; Nepal relied on government grants and donor-funded programmes.
Mission check

?The same shallow Mw 7.0 earthquake strikes two towns of 50,000 people, each 20 km from the epicentre on similar ground. Town P loses 3 people; Town Q loses 900. Which difference best explains this?

The hazard was similar. Vulnerability and exposure were not: in Nepal over half a million houses were destroyed, many with no seismic-resistant features, and the poorest rural districts lost most. Kaikōura, built to a seismic code, was cut off but mostly survived, and state insurance sped recovery.

Mission 9

The hazard management cycle

  • Mitigation: reduce the hazard's effects or people's vulnerability before it strikes.
  • Preparedness: warnings, plans, drills and stockpiles.
  • Response: rescue and relief in the hours and days after.
  • Recovery: rehabilitation (weeks to months: shelter, water, services), then reconstruction (years).
  • How a place recovers sets its vulnerability for the next event, so the cycle repeats.
Mission 9

Modify the event: land-use zoning and defences

  • Land-use zoning: California's Alquist-Priolo Act bars buildings for people from active fault traces, with a set-back of 15 m (50 feet). After 2011, Christchurch's 602-hectare red zone became a river corridor.
  • Engineering defences: in 2011 Japan's tsunami overtopped seawalls of mainly 5–10 m; more than 15,000 people died.
  • Japan now plans for two levels: defences for frequent tsunamis, and evacuation-centred measures for the largest. Walls of about 12 m are planned; critics argue relocating shrinking communities may be more efficient.
Mission 9

Modify the event: hazard-resistant design

  • Braced frames and shear walls resist sideways forces; base isolation bearings change how a building moves and add damping.
  • Codes matter: in Mashiki (Kumamoto, Japan, 2016) wooden houses built before 1981 collapsed far more often than newer ones, so retrofitting older buildings is a priority.
  • New Zealand's Building Code requires buildings to withstand earthquake loads.
Mission 9

Modify the event: lava diversion

  • Earth barriers at Fagradalsfjall (2021) delayed lava by up to 16 days.
  • At Heimaey (1973), seawater sprayed on a lava flow cooled and slowed it, saving the harbour.
  • Limit: at Grindavík on 14 January 2024 a fissure opened outside the barrier, and its lava burned three homes.
  • Diversion works for slow lava, not for pyroclastic flows or lahars.
Quick check

Mission 9

Hong Kong: preventing slope failure

  • Shaking and rain both trigger landslides, so slope engineering is tectonic mitigation too.
  • Methods: drain surface and groundwater, cover slopes, build retaining walls or toe berms, remove weight from the crest.
  • Hong Kong set up a slope-safety system in 1977, after the Sau Mau Ping disaster of 1976. In 2013 the government said the risk from man-made slopes had fallen by 75%; its engineers now say "less than 20% of the 1977 level".
  • Note: the government is measuring its own programme.
Mission 9

Modify the loss: emergency aid and community action

  • Community action comes first: in Nepal in 2015, neighbours, especially young people, dug others out of the rubble.
  • Emergency aid: Nepal asked for international help within hours; 134 search-and-rescue teams from 34 countries came. Helicopters made 4,236 flights.
  • Short-term aid: transit shelters opened in Kathmandu, but tarpaulins ran short.
  • NGOs: the Red Cross used volunteer maps; in Iceland the IFRC's Disaster Response Emergency Fund (DREF) supported the response to Grindavík's evacuation.
Mission 9

Modify the loss: long-term aid and technology

  • Long-term aid: a World Bank-supported project funded over 300,000 of Nepal's more than 700,000 rebuilt homes.
  • Grants of NPR 300,000 came in three parts, paid only after engineers certified safe building; mobile masons were recruited to help.
  • Volunteer mapping: over 6,000 volunteers added data to OpenStreetMap, used by Nepal's army and the Red Cross.
  • Phones: anonymised data from 12 million phones showed an estimated 390,000 more people than normal leaving the Kathmandu Valley.

Mission 9

Evaluating management

  • Mitigation can pay: a US study found federal mitigation grants "save $6 per $1 spent", and adopting model building codes $11.
  • Pre-event measures can be exceeded by rarer events (Tōhoku's walls).
  • Rescue saves lives in hours; reconstruction decides how vulnerable people are next time.
  • Scale: local (barriers, slope drains), national (codes, insurance), global (volunteer mapping, phone alerts).
Mission check

?New Zealand kept Kaikōura's deaths to two, and Nepal's inspected rebuilding produced over 700,000 safer homes. Which judgement do these support?

Hong Kong's slope stabilisation cut the risk from man-made slopes by about 75% (2013), and US studies find mitigation repays its cost several times over. But defences can be exceeded, so rescue, rehabilitation and reconstruction still matter, and Nepal's inspected rebuilding turned recovery into lower future vulnerability.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Structure: crust, mantle, core; rigid lithosphere moves over the weak asthenosphere.
  • Wegener (1912): fit, fossils, coal in Antarctica, glacial deposits in Africa; rejected for lack of a mechanism.
  • 1950s–60s: ridges, magnetic stripes, young sea floor (oldest about 180 million years), drill cores, earthquakes → plate tectonics.

Mission 2

  • Heat drives convection; gravity drives slab pull and ridge push. Margins: divergent (ridges, rift valleys), convergent (trenches, island arcs, volcanoes, fold mountains), conservative (transform faults, shallow quakes).

Mission 3

  • Silica and gas decide eruption style; setting decides the magma: effusive at constructive margins and hot spots, explosive at destructive margins.
  • Primary hazards (lava, pyroclastic flows, tephra and ash, gases) strike near the vent; secondary hazards such as lahars can kill far away and later.
  • The same volcanoes bring benefits: geothermal heat, soils and tourism.

Mission 4

  • Hazards: Kīlauea 2018 (over 700 structures), Armero 1985 (lahar, about 23,000 deaths), Pinatubo 1991 (ash, lahars, SO₂ cooling about 0.5 °C).
  • Benefits: Andisols (water-holding, but phosphorus-fixing), geothermal heat (Iceland about 90% of home heating; Kenya's Olkaria), new land, minerals, tourism. The balance depends on time, scale, who gains and management.

Mission 5

  • Plate setting sets the hazard: explosive pyroclastic flows at Fuego, effusive lava at Reykjanes.
  • Evacuation practice, communication and capacity explain who survived; insurance and buy-outs shape recovery.

Mission 6

  • Shallow-focus earthquakes occur at all margins; deep-focus ones occur only inside slabs sinking at subduction zones.
  • P, S and L waves bring shaking; secondary hazards include liquefaction, landslides.
  • Subduction earthquakes lift the water column to make tsunamis, which give distant coasts hours of warning but nearby coasts minutes.

Mission 7

  • Seismic gap theory: segments overdue for a large earthquake are flagged (Maule 2010 filled one), but tests rejected it as a forecasting rule, and Parkfield's 2004 earthquake came over ten years late.
  • Dilatancy (dilation): cracking rock should change Vp/Vs and release radon; field signals were small, inconsistent and mostly not followed by earthquakes, so no reliable prediction method exists.

Mission 8

  • Nepal 2015 (an LEDC) and Kaikōura 2016 (New Zealand, an MEDC): same magnitude, very different impacts, because of buildings, poverty, exposure and timing.
  • Short-term responses rescue; long-term responses rebuild, and decide future vulnerability.

Mission 9

  • The management cycle runs mitigation → preparedness → response → recovery.
  • Modify the event (zoning, design, defences, diversion), the vulnerability (warnings, preparedness) and the loss (aid, insurance, community action).
  • Contrasting countries cope differently: Iceland and New Zealand before the event; Guatemala and Nepal mostly after it.
