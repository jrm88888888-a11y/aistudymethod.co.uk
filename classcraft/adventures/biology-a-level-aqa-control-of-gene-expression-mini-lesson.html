Mission 1: One early embryo cell can grow into a whole baby. Why can't a cell from your bone marrow?

Make a guess: One early embryo cell can grow into a whole baby. Why can't a cell from your bone marrow?

Mission 1

What makes a stem cell?

  • A stem cell is undifferentiated. It can do two things:
  • (1) Divide again and again, renewing itself.
  • (2) Differentiate into specialised cell types.
  • Most specialised cells cannot do both.
  • Say: "able to divide endlessly" — not: "always dividing". Stem cells can rest between divisions.
Quick check
?Which pair of properties defines a stem cell?
Mission 1

Potency: how many cell types can it make?

  • Totipotent: any cell type, including the placenta and other extra-embryonic tissues. The zygote and the cells of the first few divisions.
  • Totipotent cells exist only briefly, in early mammalian embryos.
  • Pluripotent: any body cell type, but not the placenta. Inner cell mass of the blastocyst: embryonic stem cells.
  • Pluripotent stem cells can divide in unlimited numbers.
  • Multipotent: a limited range of types. Adult tissues, e.g. bone marrow → blood cells.
  • Unipotent: one cell type, e.g. cells in heart muscle that form new cardiomyocytes.
  • As cells specialise they translate only part of their DNA: the rest stays in the nucleus, unused.
  • Say: "totipotent includes the placenta; pluripotent does not" — not: "both mean any cell".
Quick check
?A cell taken from a 4-cell human embryo can develop into a complete individual with its own placenta. A cell from the inner cell mass of a blastocyst can form any tissue of the body but not a placenta. A stem cell from adult bone marrow forms only blood cells. How should the three cells be classified?
Mission 1

Stem cells in the adult body

  • Mature mammals keep multipotent and unipotent stem cells.
  • Bone marrow: stem cells make every type of blood cell.
Mission check

?A cell is taken from adult bone marrow and grown in a dish. It divides many times. Some daughter cells stay like the parent; others become erythrocytes, neutrophils and other blood cells, but never nerve or muscle cells. Which statement is correct?

An early embryo cell can still become anything; a bone-marrow stem cell is limited to blood.

That limited range is what multipotent means.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Scientists can turn a skin cell into one that can become almost any cell. What should we use that for?

Make a guess: Scientists can turn a skin cell into one that can become almost any cell. What should we use that for?

Mission 2

Three sources of stem cells

  • Embryonic stem cells: from the inner cell mass of an early embryo, often a spare IVF embryo. Can become any body cell type. Collecting them destroys the embryo.
  • Adult stem cells: e.g. bone marrow. Make a limited range of cell types. Can come from the patient.
  • Induced pluripotent stem (iPS) cells: adult body cells reprogrammed to behave like embryonic stem cells. No embryo needed.
  • Embryonic and iPS cells are pluripotent.
Mission 2

Making iPS cells

  • Take adult somatic cells, e.g. skin fibroblasts.
  • Add the genes for a few protein transcription factors (e.g. Oct4, Sox2, Klf4, c-Myc).
  • The factors switch on the genes for pluripotency and switch off genes for specialisation.
  • The cells now divide without limit and can differentiate into many cell types.
  • First made from mouse cells (2006), then human cells (2007), by Yamanaka's team.
  • For: no embryo destroyed; cells match the patient. Against: some factors (e.g. c-Myc) can promote cancer; reprogramming is inefficient.
Quick check
?How are induced pluripotent stem (iPS) cells produced?
Mission 2

What stem cells are used for

  • In routine use: bone marrow (blood stem cell) transplants treat leukaemia and some other blood disorders.
  • Tissue repair: stem cells from the edge of the cornea can restore a burnt eye surface.
  • In clinical trials: dopamine-releasing neurons for Parkinson's; insulin-secreting cells for type 1 diabetes; retinal cells for an eye disease (macular degeneration).
  • Research: watching stem cells differentiate shows how organisms develop (developmental biology). Human cells for testing drugs.
Quick check
?Bone marrow transplants are used to treat leukaemia. Which statement explains how a transplant replaces the patient's blood cells?
Mission 2

Weighing the case

  • Possible benefits: treatments for conditions with no cure; fewer donor organs needed; human cells for research and drug testing.
  • Safety: leftover undifferentiated cells can form tumours; donor cells may be rejected unless matched; long-term effects are still being studied.
  • Ethics: embryonic stem cells come from embryos that are destroyed.
  • Views differ on the moral status of an early embryo: some hold that it has full status from fertilisation; others that status develops later.
  • Points often raised: spare IVF embryos would otherwise be discarded; iPS and adult cells avoid embryos but have their own limits.
  • Access: cost; and unregulated clinics selling unproven treatments.
  • To evaluate: weigh benefits against risks and evidence, then reach a supported judgement.
Mission 2

How society decides

  • Scientists provide evidence, checked by peer review before publication.
  • Ethics committees, regulators, governments and the public weigh evidence and values.
  • UK: the Human Fertilisation and Embryology Act lets licensed labs use human embryos for research up to 14 days after fertilisation. The HFEA issues the licences.
  • New therapies must pass clinical trials before approval.
  • Countries set different rules, because societies weigh the values differently.
  • Science shows what can be done. Society decides what should be done.
Quick check
?Which of these is a question that scientific evidence alone cannot answer?
Mission check

?In 2014, researchers in Japan treated a patient with macular degeneration, an eye disease. They made iPS cells from the patient's own skin, grew them into a sheet of retinal cells and transplanted it. Which statement about this approach is correct?

Stem cells could repair what the body cannot. Using them well means weighing benefits, risks and values, not evidence alone.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Nearly every cell in your body carries the same genes. So why doesn't a skin cell act like a nerve cell?

Make a guess: Nearly every cell in your body carries the same genes. So why doesn't a skin cell act like a nerve cell?

Mission 3

One genome, many cell types

  • Almost every cell in a body carries the same genome.
  • Each cell uses only some of its genes. Which ones decides what the cell becomes.
  • Gene expression: transcription → translation → the protein does its job.
  • Transcription is the main switch. No transcription: no mRNA, no protein.
  • As totipotent cells develop, they translate only part of their DNA: this is how cells specialise.
Quick check
?Developing red blood cells produce large amounts of the beta chain of haemoglobin, but liver cells produce none, although both cell types contain the gene. Which statement explains the difference?
Mission 3

Transcription factors: the switches

  • Transcription factors: proteins that bind specific base sequences in DNA and control transcription.
  • In eukaryotes, RNA polymerase cannot start alone. Transcription factors bind the promoter first; then RNA polymerase binds.
  • Activators stimulate transcription. Repressors inhibit it.
  • Specific transcription factors move from the cytoplasm into the nucleus, then stimulate or inhibit their target genes.
  • Each cell type has its own mix of transcription factors, so its own set of genes is on.
  • iPS cells: adult body cells given genes for a few transcription factors switch on the genes of pluripotent cells again.
  • Say: "the promoter is a DNA sequence; transcription factors are proteins that bind it" — not: "the promoter is a protein".
Quick check
?In a hypothetical species of yeast, protein P represses gene G by binding to a DNA sequence next to the promoter of gene G. A mutation changes this DNA sequence so that protein P can no longer bind to it. Which prediction about cells with this mutation is correct?
Mission 3

Oestrogen switches genes on

  • Oestrogen is a steroid hormone: lipid-soluble, so it diffuses through the phospholipid bilayer.
  • (1) Inside the cell it binds a receptor protein.
  • Binding changes the receptor's shape and releases it from an inhibitor protein; the complex then moves into the nucleus.
  • (2) In the nucleus, the hormone–receptor complex binds a specific base sequence near its target genes.
  • (3) The complex acts as a transcription factor: RNA polymerase binds; the target genes are transcribed.
  • Say: "the oestrogen–receptor complex binds the DNA" — not: "oestrogen binds the DNA".
Quick check
?Oestrogen is a steroid hormone that passes through the plasma membrane of its target cells. How does oestrogen alter the pattern of gene expression in these cells?
Mission 3

Epigenetics: switching without rewriting

  • Epigenetics: heritable changes in gene expression without any change to the base sequence of DNA.
  • Chemical tags on the DNA, and on the histone proteins it wraps around, turn genes down or up.
  • Tags can stay in place through mitosis: daughter cells keep the same genes switched off.
  • The environment can change the tags (more in the next mission).
Mission 3

Methyl groups and histones

  • (1) DNA methylation: methyl groups (–CH3) are added to cytosine bases, often in a gene's promoter.
  • Methylated promoter → transcription repressed → gene silenced.
  • Transcription factors and RNA polymerase can no longer bind the promoter.
  • (2) Loosely wound DNA can be transcribed; tightly wound DNA cannot. Acetyl groups on histones loosen the winding.
  • Decreased acetylation of histones → DNA packed tightly → transcription inhibited.
  • So transcription is inhibited by increased methylation of DNA or decreased acetylation of histones.
  • Say: "methylation silences the gene; its base sequence is unchanged" — not: "methylation mutates the gene".
Quick check
?Cytosine bases in the DNA of a gene's promoter become methylated. What is the effect on the gene?
Quick check
?In a skin cell, the promoter of gene G is methylated and the gene is not expressed. The cell divides by mitosis. What is the most likely state of gene G in the daughter cells, and why?
Mission check

?In some plants, gene F codes for a protein that stops the plant flowering. After several weeks of cold, the histones around gene F are modified and gene F stops being transcribed. When the weather warms, the new cells made by mitosis in the growing shoot also do not transcribe gene F, and the plant flowers. The base sequence of gene F is unchanged. Which statement explains these observations?

A few weeks of cold, a gene switched off, and a memory carried through every new cell. No gene was changed.

Mission 4: Identical twins start life with the same genes. Why do they become more different as they age?

Mission 4

The environment changes the tags

  • Diet, stress, chemicals and pollution can add or remove epigenetic tags.
  • Changed tags → changed gene expression → changed phenotype. The base sequence stays the same.
  • So epigenetic changes are one route by which the environment affects phenotype.
  • Say: "the environment altered gene expression" — not: "the environment mutated the genes".
Quick check
?Blood cells from people living in a city with high levels of particulate air pollution were compared with blood cells from people living in an area with clean air. The pattern of methyl tags on the DNA of several genes differed between the two groups, but the base sequences of those genes were the same. Which conclusion is best supported by these findings?
Mission 4

Twins: one genome, two lives

  • Monozygotic (identical) twins come from one zygote: the same genome.
  • So a difference between them in epigenetic tags cannot be caused by their genes.
  • Researchers compare tags in young and old pairs, and in pairs who live together or apart.
  • This lets you evaluate how much of a difference is genetic and how much is environmental.

Mission 4

Epigenetics and cancer

  • Tumour suppressor genes slow cell division. Oncogenes drive it when switched on.
  • Increased methylation of a tumour suppressor gene's promoter → gene silenced → division unchecked.
  • Decreased methylation of an oncogene → gene switched on → division driven.
  • The DNA sequence of both genes can be completely normal.
  • Treatment: tags are reversible. Drugs that reduce DNA methylation, or that stop acetyl groups being removed from histones, can switch tumour suppressor genes back on.
  • Limit: the drugs reach healthy cells too, and can switch on genes there.
  • Diagnosis: abnormal methylation can be detected early, before a tumour is large.
Quick check
?In cells from a tumour, the promoter of a tumour suppressor gene is heavily methylated and the gene is not transcribed. The base sequence of the gene is normal. Which drug is most likely to restore the gene's activity?
Mission check

?Female honeybee larvae in a hive are genetically alike. Larvae fed only on royal jelly develop into fertile queens; larvae switched to a diet of worker jelly develop into smaller, sterile workers. Which explanation is consistent with the understanding of gene expression?

Twins, bees, polluted air: the same genome can be read in different ways, and the environment helps decide which.

Mission 5

Control after transcription

  • Transcription is not the last switch.
  • After it, the mRNA can be edited, blocked or broken down early.
  • Small RNA molecules can stop an mRNA being translated.
Mission 5

RNA interference

  • RNA interference (RNAi): small RNA molecules stop a specific mRNA being translated.
  • (1) A small RNA, about 21 nucleotides long, joins a protein complex.
  • (2) Its bases are complementary to part of a target mRNA, so it base-pairs with it.
  • (3) siRNA: exact match → the mRNA is cut and broken down. miRNA: partial match → translation blocked (or the mRNA broken down); one miRNA can act on many mRNAs.
  • The gene is still transcribed. Less protein is made.
  • RNAi occurs in eukaryotes and some prokaryotes.
  • Say: "the small RNA is complementary to the mRNA" — not: "the small RNA changes the gene".

Mission check

?Apples whose cut flesh does not turn brown have been produced. Browning is caused by the enzyme polyphenol oxidase (PPO). The apples were given a gene whose RNA is cut into small RNAs complementary to the mRNA of the PPO genes. Which statement explains the result?

The gene is read, but the message is intercepted: RNA interference silences a protein without touching the DNA.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A stem cell can divide repeatedly and differentiate into specialised cells.
  • Totipotent: any cell, including placenta. Pluripotent: any body cell.
  • Multipotent: a limited range, as in adult bone marrow.

Mission 2

  • Stem cells come from embryos, adult tissues, or reprogrammed adult cells (iPS cells).
  • iPS cells are made by adding protein transcription factors to adult somatic cells.
  • Uses range from routine (bone marrow transplants) to trials (Parkinson's, type 1 diabetes); each use is weighed for benefit, safety and ethics.

Mission 3

  • Every cell carries the same genome; cell types differ in which genes are transcribed.
  • Transcription factors bind specific DNA sequences to switch genes on or off.
  • Oestrogen works through a receptor that becomes a transcription factor.
  • Epigenetic tags (DNA methylation, histone modification) change expression without changing the sequence, and pass through mitosis.

Mission 4

  • The environment can change epigenetic tags, so genetically identical cells or organisms can express different genes; the base sequence is unchanged.
  • Abnormal methylation (more on tumour suppressor genes, less on oncogenes) can drive cancer; reversible tags make drug treatment possible.

Mission 5

  • In RNA interference, a small RNA base-pairs with a complementary mRNA, which is cut or blocked: less protein, same gene.
