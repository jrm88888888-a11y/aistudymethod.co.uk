The Media

This mini-lesson covers the AQA Media option: ownership and control, the new media and the digital divide, the selection and presentation of news (news values, gatekeeping, moral panics), representations of class, gender, ethnicity, age, sexuality and disability, the audience models, and globalisation, popular culture and postmodernism.

Optional topic — you study one from each of the two option groups. The Media sits in option group 2 of Paper 2, alongside Beliefs in Society, Global Development, and Stratification and Differentiation. You take one topic from group 1 and one from group 2.

Paper 2 · ownership and control

Who owns the media, and does it matter?

  • The trends. Concentration of ownership (fewer, bigger owners); vertical integration (owning every stage from production to distribution); horizontal (lateral) integration (owning across different media); conglomeration and global conglomeration; and synergy — promoting the same product across all the platforms you own.
  • The manipulative (instrumentalist) Marxist view. Miliband argues the media are a tool wielded by the ruling class to spread ruling-class ideology and produce false consciousness. Owners intervene directly. Curran's history of the British press documents real, repeated proprietorial interference in editorial line — this is not a fantasy.
  • The hegemonic (structuralist) Marxist view. The Glasgow University Media Group (Bad News) argue bias is real but not the product of conscious conspiracy. Journalists are overwhelmingly recruited from a narrow social background, and their unconscious, taken-for-granted assumptions about what is 'normal' and 'reasonable' reproduce a middle-class, consensual worldview. Their content analyses of strike reporting found the language of 'demands' and 'offers' consistently framed workers as the disruptive party.
  • The pluralist view. Whale and other pluralists argue the media give the audience what it wants — owners chase profit, and profit comes from pleasing readers. Content is constrained by law and regulation, and by professional journalistic standards; audiences are diverse, active and quite capable of rejecting a message. Competition, not conspiracy, drives content.
  • Curran & Seaton supply the balanced verdict: ownership has real effects, but so do the market, the state, professional norms and the audience. Bias is over-determined, not dictated.

Paper 2 · new media

The digital revolution: liberation or the same old power?

  • What is 'new media'? Boyle and others point to convergence (one device does everything), interactivity, and the collapse of the distinction between producer and consumer — the audience now makes content.
  • Neophiliacs (Negroponte) are optimistic: more choice, more participation, more democracy; e-commerce and e-government; the 'long tail' of niche content; and a public sphere in which anyone can publish. Social movements can organise without a newspaper.
  • Cultural pessimists (Cornford & Robins) reply that most 'new' media are the old media in new packaging, that the same conglomerates dominate online, that interactivity is largely commercial (targeted advertising built on surveillance of the user), and that quality has been diluted by a flood of unverified content.
  • The digital divide. Access is not equal. Helsper shows the divide runs by class, age, region, gender and disability, and — crucially — that it is not only about access but about skills and confidence in using it. So new media may reproduce existing inequalities rather than dissolve them.
  • Marxists add that the online world is dominated by a small number of vast corporations, and that the user's attention and data are the product being sold. Postmodernists (Baudrillard) argue new media accelerate hyperreality — a world of signs and simulations detached from any reality they claim to represent.

Paper 2 · selection and presentation of news

News is manufactured, not found

  • News values. Galtung & Ruge identified the criteria by which events become news: extraordinariness, threshold (scale), unambiguity, reference to elite persons and nations, personalisation, negativity, frequency (does it fit the news cycle?), continuity and composition (balance of the bulletin). News is not a window on the world; it is a selection made against these criteria.
  • Gatekeeping. White's classic study of a wire editor showed one individual deciding what passed through the gate. Multiply that by every editorial decision and you have the manufacture of the news agenda.
  • Agenda setting. The media may not tell us what to think, but they are strikingly successful at telling us what to think about. What is left out is as significant as what is included.
  • Practical and organisational pressures. Deadlines, budgets, the need for pictures, reliance on official sources and press releases (which gives 'primary definers' — police, government, business — the first say), and the pull of what is cheap and easy to cover.
  • Moral panics. Cohen (Folk Devils and Moral Panics) showed how the media's exaggerated reporting of the mods and rockers created folk devils, provoked public concern and official crackdown, and thereby amplified the very deviance it denounced — the deviancy amplification spiral. Hall et al (Policing the Crisis) gave the concept a Marxist edge: the 1970s 'mugging' panic served a state in crisis by supplying a folk devil and legitimating repressive policing.

Evaluate moral panic theory: it assumes the reaction is disproportionate — but who decides what is proportionate? And in a fragmented new-media landscape, McRobbie and Thornton argue it is much harder for a single panic to take hold.

Paper 2 · audiences and media effects

From passive to active audiences

  • The hypodermic syringe model treats the audience as passive, and the message as injected directly. Bandura's Bobo doll experiments — children who watched an adult attack a doll went on to imitate the attack — are the standard supporting evidence, though they are laboratory experiments with real questions about ecological validity. Newson argued that sustained exposure to media violence desensitises the viewer. Criticisms: the audience is not a blank slate; effects studies cannot show causation in the real world; and the model licenses censorship.
  • The two-step flow (Katz & Lazarsfeld): media messages do not reach us directly but pass via opinion leaders whose social influence shapes how the message is received. The audience is embedded in social groups, not isolated.
  • Uses and gratifications (Blumler & McQuail): the question is not what the media do to people but what people do with the media. They identify diversion (escape), personal relationships (companionship), personal identity (comparing one's life with what is on screen) and surveillance (information). The audience is active — but critics say this exaggerates freedom and ignores the ideology of the content itself.
  • Reception analysis. Hall's encoding/decoding model: the producer encodes a preferred meaning, but the audience decodes it from its own social position — accepting the dominant reading, adopting a negotiated one, or mounting an oppositional reading. Morley's study of different social groups watching the same television news programme found precisely this variation by class and occupation.
  • The cultural effects model (GUMG): effects are neither immediate nor absent, but a slow drip-drip — repeated framing over years gradually shifts what the audience takes to be common sense. This is the model most sociologists now find persuasive.

Paper 2 · representations

Class, gender, ethnicity, age, sexuality and disability on screen

  • Gender. Mulvey's male gaze: mainstream film positions the viewer as male, and women appear as objects to be looked at. Ferguson's content analysis of women's magazines identified a 'cult of femininity' organised around love, the home, appearance and caring. Wolf's beauty myth argues that as women gained power, an impossible standard of beauty was raised as a new form of control. Tuchman's 'symbolic annihilation' names the process by which women are trivialised, condemned or simply left out. Later research finds more varied representations — but also the intensification of the body as a project.
  • Ethnicity. Van Dijk's analysis of news discourse found minorities recurrently framed as a problem or a threat — crime, immigration, dependency — rather than as ordinary members of society. Hall analysed the stereotypes inherited from empire. Under-representation, and confinement to a narrow band of roles, are as significant as overt hostility.
  • Class. The working class appear as either objects of ridicule or figures of pity — think of the conventions of 'poverty porn' television — while the wealthy are shown with admiration. Newman notes how little attention the media give to the lives of the poor relative to the lifestyles of the rich.
  • Age. Children are shown as victims, cute or as brats; youth as a threat (see moral panics); the elderly as forgetful, dependent and asexual — though there is a countervailing commercial interest in the affluent older consumer.
  • Disability. Barnes catalogued recurring stereotypes: the pitiable and pathetic, the sinister and evil, the object of curiosity, the burden, and the 'supercrip' whose ordinary life is presented as heroic.
  • Sexuality. Gross documented the long history of symbolic annihilation and of stereotyping of gay people; representation has widened considerably, but critics note it is often confined to comedic or tragic roles, and shaped by the commercial pull of a 'pink pound'.

Paper 2 · globalisation and postmodernism

Global media, popular culture and hyperreality

  • Cultural imperialism. A small number of Western — largely American — conglomerates dominate global media, exporting their products and, with them, their values. Critics of globalisation fear cultural homogenisation: everywhere begins to look the same. Marxists see this as ideological domination on a world scale.
  • Against homogenisation. Others argue global media are received locally and reworked — hybridity and glocalisation. Audiences are not passive; reception analysis applies globally too. And media flows are no longer only one-way.
  • Postmodernism. Baudrillard argues we now live in hyperreality: media images no longer represent a reality outside themselves but circulate as simulacra — copies without an original. The distinction between the real and its representation collapses. Strinati agrees that popular culture and the media now play the central role in forming identity, and that the boundary between high and popular culture has dissolved.
  • Evaluating postmodernism: it captures something real about a media-saturated culture, but it is hard to test, it neglects the persistent structures of ownership and inequality (the same conglomerates own the hyperreal), and it can slide into the claim that nothing is real — which is not a claim sociology can act on.
  • Popular culture and the media. The Frankfurt School saw a 'culture industry' producing standardised, passive consumers; Strinati and later cultural studies reject this elitism, insisting audiences use popular culture creatively.

Paper 2 · evaluation

Judging the debates

  • Ownership. Pluralists are right that audiences have choice and that owners chase profit; but Curran's evidence of proprietorial interference is hard to dismiss, and the GUMG's hegemonic model explains bias without requiring a conspiracy. Curran and Seaton's conclusion — that ownership matters alongside market, state, professional norms and audience — is the defensible position.
  • Effects. The hypodermic model is discredited as a general theory (though the evidence on imitation is not nothing); uses and gratifications restores agency but risks pretending content has no ideology; the cultural effects model, with its slow drip-drip of framing, is the most persuasive synthesis, and it fits the evidence from reception analysis that decoding varies systematically with social position.
  • New media. Neither the neophiliac's utopia nor the pessimist's dystopia. The technology is genuinely participatory and genuinely concentrated in corporate hands — and the digital divide means its benefits are distributed along the existing lines of class, age and disability.
  • Representation. Representations have visibly widened. The sociological question is whether the underlying framing has changed, or only the surface — and whether commercial inclusion of new audiences is the same thing as equality.

Exam craft: examiners reward candidates who ask 'compared with what?' Media effects compared with peers, family, class position? Representation better than when? Comparison is the engine of evaluation.

Recap

The big ideas to know

Ownership: Concentration, vertical and horizontal integration, conglomeration, synergy · manipulative Marxism (Miliband, Curran) · hegemonic Marxism (Glasgow University Media Group) · pluralism (Whale) · Curran and Seaton

New media: Neophiliacs (Negroponte) vs cultural pessimists (Cornford and Robins) · the digital divide is about skills as well as access (Helsper)

News: Galtung and Ruge (news values) · White (gatekeeping) · agenda setting · primary definers · Cohen (moral panics, folk devils, deviancy amplification) · Hall et al (Policing the Crisis)

Audiences: Hypodermic syringe (Bandura, Newson) · two-step flow (Katz and Lazarsfeld) · uses and gratifications (Blumler and McQuail) · reception analysis (Hall's encoding/decoding; Morley) · cultural effects (GUMG drip-drip)

Representations: Mulvey (male gaze) · Ferguson (cult of femininity) · Wolf (beauty myth) · Tuchman (symbolic annihilation) · Van Dijk and Hall (ethnicity) · Barnes (disability) · Gross (sexuality)

Globalisation: Cultural imperialism and homogenisation vs hybridity and glocalisation · Baudrillard (hyperreality, simulacra) · Strinati · the Frankfurt School's culture industry
