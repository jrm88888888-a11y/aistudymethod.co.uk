Mission 1

A photo is flat. How can you still see depth in it with one eye shut?

Make a guess: A photo is flat. How can you still see depth in it with one eye shut?

Perception · depth cues

Sensing versus perceiving

  • Sensation is your sense organs detecting physical energy, e.g. light falling on the retina at the back of each eye.
  • Perception is your brain organising and interpreting that sensory information so it means something.
  • The image on each retina is flat (2-D), yet you perceive a world with depth (3-D).
  • The brain works out distance from depth cues. Monocular cues need only one eye; binocular cues need both.
Quick check
?Mia glances at a dark shape on the floor. Light from it reaches her eyes, and a moment later she knows it is her cat. Which part is perception?
Perception · depth cues

Depth cues for one eye

CueWhat you seeWhat the brain concludes
Height in the planeAn object sits higher in the scene, nearer the horizonIt is further away
Relative sizeOf two similar objects, one looks smallerThe smaller one is further away
Occlusion (superimposition)One object partly hides anotherThe hidden one is further away
Linear perspectiveParallel lines seem to meet in the distanceWhere they meet is far away
  • Texture gradient: surface detail (grass, pebbles) looks finer and more tightly packed further away.
  • Monocular cues work with one eye and in flat pictures, which is why artists use them.
Quick check
?In a landscape painting, a range of hills is partly hidden behind a farmhouse, so the hills look further away. Which depth cue is the artist using?
Perception · depth cues

Depth cues for two eyes

  • Your eyes are a few centimetres apart, so each retina gets a slightly different image. This is retinal disparity. The closer the object, the bigger the difference.
  • The brain uses the difference to judge depth.
  • Combining the two images into one view with depth is stereopsis.
  • Binocular cues work best for objects close to you.

Mission check

?A photo is flat. How can you still see depth in it with one eye shut?

A photo is flat, but it is full of monocular depth cues: height in the plane, relative size, occlusion, linear perspective and texture gradient. They need only one eye, so your brain reads depth from them even with one eye shut. Binocular cues only help with real 3-D scenes.

Mission 2

Two equal lines; one looks longer even when you know. Why?

Make a guess: Two equal lines; one looks longer even when you know. Why?

Perception · illusions and constancies

When perception gets it wrong

  • A visual illusion is when what you perceive does not match the physical stimulus.
  • Ambiguity (ambiguous figures): one image fits two interpretations, so perception flips. The Necker cube flips which face is at the front; Rubin's vase flips between a vase and two faces.
  • Fiction: you perceive something that isn't there. In the Kanizsa triangle you see a bright white triangle with edges, but no edges are drawn.
Perception · illusions and constancies

Size constancy and the Ames room

  • Size constancy: an object looks the same size even when its retinal image shrinks with distance.
  • The brain scales up anything it judges to be far away, so a friend walking off doesn't seem to shrink.
  • The Ames room looks rectangular through a peephole, but one back corner is much further away.
  • Assuming a normal room, the brain sees both people as equally far: one looks tiny, one huge.
Perception · illusions and constancies

Depth cues misread

  • Müller-Lyer: of two equal lines, the one with fins pointing outwards looks longer.
  • Gregory: fins out look like a room's inside corner (far); arrowheads, a building's outside corner (near).
  • Size constancy then scales up the "far" line.
  • Ponzo: converging lines act as linear perspective, so the upper of two equal bars seems further and bigger.
  • These are misinterpreted depth cues.
  • Size or length illusions like these are distortions.
Perception · illusions and constancies

Shape and colour constancy

  • A visual constancy means you perceive an object as unchanged even though the image reaching your eye changes.
  • Shape constancy: a door opening towards you casts a trapezium-shaped image, but you still see a rectangular door.
  • Colour constancy: a banana still looks yellow in dim evening light.
  • With size constancy, these keep the world stable. Illusions show them applied in the wrong place.
Key study Perception · illusions and constancies

Haber and Levin (2001)

Aim
To find out whether people judge an object's size from its distance or from knowing what it is.
Method
Experiment 1: 109 male students estimated the sizes of 50 familiar objects from memory. Experiment 2: 9 male volunteers in a field judged the size and distance of 45 objects (15 fixed-size, 15 variable-size, 15 unfamiliar).
Results
Sizes were judged very accurately for fixed-size objects, less so for variable-size ones; distance did not affect size estimates. Distance was judged least accurately for unfamiliar objects.
Conclusion
Size perception depends mainly on recognising the object and recalling its usual size (prior knowledge).
Strength
Real objects outdoors are more realistic than lab drawings.
Weakness
Very small, all-male sample in Experiment 2.

Mission check

?Two equal lines; one looks longer even when you know. Why?

The fins act as depth cues: one line looks like a far corner, the other like a near one. Your brain applies size constancy and scales up the "far" line. This happens automatically, so knowing the lines are equal doesn't switch it off.

Mission 3

A pilot lands by watching the ground rush past. How is that enough?

Make a guess: A pilot lands by watching the ground rush past. How is that enough?

Perception · Gibson's direct theory

Perception without guesswork

  • Gibson argued that perception is direct: the pattern of light reaching your eyes, the optic array, holds all the information you need.
  • No inference and no past experience are needed. Processing is bottom-up: it starts from the senses.
  • Sensory input: the optic array is rich enough on its own to show depth, distance and movement.
Quick check
?Which statement would Gibson agree with?
Perception · Gibson's direct theory

Information in movement

  • Moving changes the optic array in a regular way, and the change is information.
  • Motion parallax: as you travel, near objects stream past fast and far objects slowly, so speed across your view shows distance.
  • Optic flow: moving forward, the scene streams outwards from the point you're heading for, which stays still. Faster flow, faster movement.
  • Gibson's ideas grew from his wartime work on films for training pilots.
Perception · Gibson's direct theory

Invariants and affordances

  • Invariants are features of the optic array that don't change as you move, so they give reliable information, e.g. texture gradient and the horizon.
  • Affordances are what an object offers you for action, picked up directly from its shape: a chair affords sitting, a handle affords grasping, a gap affords walking through.
  • You can see what an object affords without knowing its name.
Quick check
?From a train window, the fence beside the track flashes past while distant hills barely seem to move. What is this cue, and what does it tell you?
Quick check
?A hiker reaches a stream and at once sees that a row of flat stones can be stepped across, without working anything out. Which part of Gibson's theory does this show?
Perception · Gibson's direct theory

Born ready to perceive?

  • Gibson's theory takes the nature side: picking up information from the optic array is innate, not learned.
  • In the visual cliff studies (Gibson and Walk, 1960), crawling babies would not cross a glass floor over an apparent drop, even when their mothers called them. Young animals of many species avoided it too.
  • But crawling babies are already several months old, so some learning can't be ruled out.

Mission check

?A pilot lands by watching the ground rush past. How is that enough?

For Gibson, the moving view is enough. As she descends, near ground streams past faster than far ground (motion parallax), and the scene flows outwards from the point she is heading for (optic flow). That information is picked up directly from the light, with no inference.

Mission 4

Your eyes give your brain half a picture. What fills the gaps?

Make a guess: Your eyes give your brain half a picture. What fills the gaps?

Perception · Gregory's constructivist theory

Perception as a best guess

  • Gregory argued that sensory input is often incomplete or ambiguous.
  • The brain makes inferences: it goes beyond the input, using visual cues and past experience (prior knowledge).
  • It forms a perceptual hypothesis, a best guess about what is out there, and tests it against new input.
  • Processing is top-down: the brain constructs a model of reality from knowledge.
Perception · Gregory's constructivist theory

Illusions as evidence

  • If perception is a hypothesis, a wrong hypothesis should give an illusion.
  • Necker cube: the drawing fits two hypotheses equally well, so perception flips between them.
  • Müller-Lyer: the brain infers depth from the fins and applies size constancy, so one line looks longer.
  • Hollow face: a hollow mask is seen as a normal face sticking out, because every face you've seen does.
Quick check
?Walking home at dusk, Leo sees a dark shape on the path and jumps back, sure it's a dog. Closer up, it is a bin bag. How would Gregory explain the mistake?

Mission 5

One shape reads as H in THE and A in CAT. What decides which?

Make a guess: One shape reads as H in THE and A in CAT. What decides which?

Perception · perceptual set

Ready to see

  • Perceptual set is a readiness to perceive certain things and not others. You notice some parts of the sensory input and ignore the rest.
  • It works top-down, as in Gregory's theory: what is already in your head shapes what you see.
  • So the same stimulus can be perceived differently by different people, or by you at different times.
  • Four factors affect it: expectation, motivation, emotion and culture.
Perception · perceptual set

Four factors that set you

FactorWhat it doesExample
ExpectationYou see what context or past experience leads you to expectSkimming past a repeated "the the" in a sentence
MotivationNeeds and wants (hunger, thirst, interests) make related things stand outWhen hungry, food signs jump out at you
EmotionFeelings change what you notice, e.g. fear makes you alert to threatUpsetting words were recognised more slowly than neutral ones (McGinnies, 1949)
CultureUpbringing and surroundings shape how you interpret imagesPeople with little experience of pictures often misread depth cues in drawings (Hudson, 1960)
  • Set usually helps you perceive quickly, but it can make you miss things or see what isn't there.
Key study Perception · perceptual set

Carmichael, Hogan and Walter (1932)

Aim
To see whether a name given when a figure is first seen changes how it is later reproduced.
Method
Lab experiment, independent groups: 95 students and teachers saw 12 ambiguous line figures. Two groups heard a different name for each (e.g. "crescent moon" or "letter C"); a control group heard none. All then drew the figures from memory.
Results
Of the most changed drawings, 74% (first list) and 73% (second list) looked like the name given. In the control group, 45% resembled either name.
Conclusion
Language creates an expectation that shapes how a figure is perceived and remembered.
Strength
The control group shows the names caused the difference.
Weakness
Drawing from memory may test memory more than perception.
Quick check

Mission check

?One shape reads as H in THE and A in CAT. What decides which?

The shape is identical in both words. The letters around it set up an expectation, a perceptual set, so your brain reads it as H in THE and A in CAT. What you perceive depends on what you are ready to see, not only on the input.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Sensation is detecting energy with the sense organs; perception is the brain interpreting it.
  • Monocular cues (height in the plane, relative size, superimposition, linear perspective, texture gradient) need one eye and work in pictures.
  • Binocular cues need two eyes: retinal disparity lets the brain combine two views into depth (stereopsis).
  • Illusions happen when perception doesn't match the stimulus: fictions, ambiguous figures and distortions are the three kinds.
  • Size constancy keeps a walking-away friend the same size; misread depth cues make it misfire (Müller-Lyer, Ponzo, Ames room).
  • Shape, colour and size constancy keep objects stable; Haber and Levin (2001) found size is judged mainly from knowing the object.
  • Gibson: perception is direct and bottom-up; the optic array holds enough information, with no inference.
  • Key ideas: sensory input (the optic array), optic flow, invariants and affordances.
  • It explains fast, accurate real-world perception but not illusions or the effects of experience.
  • Gregory: perception is top-down; sensory input is incomplete, so the brain makes inferences from visual cues and past experience to construct a model of reality.
  • Key ideas: sensory input, perceptual hypothesis, inferences and prior knowledge.
  • It explains illusions and perceptual set but struggles with fast real-world perception and newborn abilities.
  • Perceptual set is a readiness to perceive some things and not others; it works top-down.
  • Expectation, motivation, emotion and culture all shape it.
  • Carmichael, Hogan and Walter (1932) showed that a word given with a figure shapes how it is reproduced.
