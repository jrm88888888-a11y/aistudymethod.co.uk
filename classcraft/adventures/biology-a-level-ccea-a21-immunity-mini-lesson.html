Mission 1

Mission 1: Most germs that land on you never get in, and most that do never get far. What stops them?

Make a guess: Most germs that land on you never get in, and most that do never get far. What stops them?

Mission 1

Skin and mucous membranes

  • Skin: an outer layer of tough, dead cells. Few pathogens can get through: a physical barrier.
  • Oily sebum keeps the skin surface acidic, inhibiting many pathogens: a chemical barrier.
  • Mucous membranes line the airways, gut, reproductive tract and eyes. No dead-cell layer here.
  • Their mucus traps pathogens. In the airways, cilia sweep the mucus up to the throat to be swallowed.
  • These primary defences are always in place, before any infection.
Mission 1

More barriers

  • Tears contain lysozyme, an enzyme that hydrolyses bacterial cell walls.
  • Stomach acid kills most pathogens swallowed in food and water.
Quick check
?Chemicals in cigarette smoke stop the cilia in the airways from beating. Why do smokers get more chest infections?
Mission 1

Sealing a cut

  • At a cut, platelets release clotting factors.
  • A cascade of reactions follows, producing the enzyme thrombin.
  • Thrombin converts soluble fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin.
  • Fibrin threads form a mesh that traps red blood cells (erythrocytes): a clot seals the wound.
  • Full mechanism: an earlier lesson.
Quick check
?Which sequence correctly describes the events that seal a cut in the skin?
Mission 1

Inflammation

  • Damaged tissue and mast cells release histamine and cytokines.
  • Arterioles dilate: more blood flows in. Heat and redness.
  • Capillary walls become more permeable: plasma, proteins and white blood cells leave. Swelling.
  • Cytokines attract phagocytes to the site.
  • Cytokine levels in blood can be measured as biomarkers of inflammation, using antibodies (see Mission 5).

Mission 1

Phagocytosis

  • Phagocytes squeeze out of capillaries (amoeboid movement) and move to the infection site.
  • A phagocyte recognises foreign molecules on the pathogen.
  • It engulfs the pathogen by endocytosis, enclosing it in a vesicle: a phagosome.
  • Lysosomes fuse with the phagosome and release hydrolytic enzymes, which digest the pathogen.
  • Soluble products are absorbed.
  • Some phagocytes then display the pathogen's antigens (see Mission 2).
  • Say: "digested inside a phagosome by lysosomal enzymes" — not: "enzymes are released onto the pathogen".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly describes how a phagocyte destroys a bacterium?
Mission 1

Two kinds of phagocyte

  • Polymorphs (lobed nucleus): the most common white blood cell; first to arrive; short-lived.
  • Monocytes leave the blood and become macrophages: larger, longer-lived.
  • Macrophages present the pathogen's antigens on their surface: antigen-presenting cells (see Mission 2).
  • Polymorphs also engulf clumps of antibody-bound antigen (see Mission 3).
Mission check

?In a phagocyte, the lysosomes cannot fuse with phagosomes, but every other step works normally. What happens to a bacterium that meets this phagocyte?

Barriers keep most pathogens out. For those that get in, a phagocyte must both engulf and digest.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Your body has never met this year's flu virus, yet it makes antibodies that fit it. How?

Make a guess: Your body has never met this year's flu virus, yet it makes antibodies that fit it. How?

Mission 2

Self, non-self and antigens

  • Every cell carries surface molecules, mostly proteins and glycoproteins, that identify it.
  • The immune system ignores self molecules and responds to non-self ones.
  • An antigen is a molecule that triggers an immune response, including antibody production.
  • CCEA: an antigen is a substance capable of stimulating the production of specific and complementary antibodies.
  • Say: "the antigen is recognised; the antibody is made against it" — not the other way round.
Quick check
?Which statement about antigens is correct?
Mission 2

One lymphocyte, one shape

  • Lymphocytes drive the adaptive (specific) response. They circulate in the blood and gather in lymph nodes.
  • Two kinds: B lymphocytes (B cells) and T lymphocytes (T cells).
  • Each lymphocyte has receptors of one shape. Each B cell can make one type of antibody.
  • You have millions of different lymphocytes, made before any infection.
  • An antigen binds only the few with a complementary receptor. It selects them: clonal selection.
  • Say: "the receptor is complementary to the antigen" — not: "the same shape as the antigen".
  • Say: "the antigen selects a cell that already fits" — not: "the cell designs an antibody to fit".
Quick check
?Which statement about B-lymphocytes and antibodies is correct?
Mission 2

Antigen presentation wakes T helper cells

  • A macrophage engulfs a pathogen and displays its antigens on its surface: an antigen-presenting cell (APC).
  • A T helper cell with a complementary receptor binds the presented antigen and is activated.
  • It divides by mitosis into a clone and releases chemical signals (cytokines).
Mission 2

T killer cells: the cell-mediated response

  • This is cell-mediated immunity.
  • Stimulated by T helper cells, T killer cells (cytotoxic T cells) divide into a clone.
  • They bind body cells that display the antigen: virus-infected cells, cancer cells, transplanted cells.
  • By direct contact, they release proteins that make pores in the target cell's membrane. The cell dies.
  • T memory cells remain after the infection.
Mission 2

B cells: the antibody response

  • This is antibody-mediated immunity.
  • A B cell's receptor binds its complementary antigen. The B cell takes it in and displays it.
  • A T helper cell activated by the same antigen binds the B cell. Only now is the B cell activated.
  • The activated B cell divides by mitosis into a clone: clonal expansion.
  • Most become plasma cells: they secrete antibody. Every cell in the clone makes the same antibody.
  • Some become memory cells: long-lived and ready for next time.
Quick check
?In an experiment, B-lymphocytes specific to an antigen were cultured with that antigen and no other cells. No antibody was produced. When helper T-lymphocytes were added to the culture, antibody production began. What does this show?
Mission 2

Why the first response takes days

  • Only a few B cells fit any one antigen.
  • They must be activated, then divide many times, before there are enough plasma cells.
  • This takes days. Meanwhile the pathogen multiplies, and you feel ill.
  • This delay is why a first infection can make you ill, or kill.
  • Memory cells survive for years. Next time they divide at once: the pathogen is usually cleared before symptoms.
Quick check
?After a person is exposed to a new pathogen for the first time, several days pass before antibodies against it can be detected in the blood. Which statement best explains this delay?
Mission 2

Switching off, and attacks on self

  • Suppressor T-cells deactivate the immune response of both B cells and T cells.
Mission 2

Transplants and immunosuppression

  • Transplanted or transfused tissue carries foreign antigens.
  • The recipient makes specific T cells that attack it and B cells whose antibodies target it: rejection.
  • Immunosuppression prevents this: X-ray irradiation or drugs that inhibit DNA replication stop lymphocytes dividing.
  • No clones form, so the foreign antigens produce no immune response.
  • The cost: the person is more susceptible to infection.
Mission check

?A child is born with no T lymphocytes. Her B lymphocytes are normal in number and type. Why does she make very little antibody against most pathogens?

The right cells already exist before infection. The antigen selects them, T helper cells switch them on, and mitosis builds the army.

Mission 3

Mission 3: A second infection with the same virus often passes without you noticing. What changed inside you?

Make a guess: A second infection with the same virus often passes without you noticing. What changed inside you?

Mission 3

What an antibody is

  • An antibody is a protein made by plasma cells.
  • It binds specifically to one antigen, because its binding site is complementary in shape.
  • Binding forms an antigen–antibody complex, which leads to the antigen's destruction.
  • CCEA: antibodies are globular proteins, specific and complementary to particular antigens, that react with antigens leading to their destruction.
Mission 3

How antibodies disable pathogens

  • Agglutination: each antibody has two binding sites, so it links pathogens together. Bacteria clump.
  • Clumped bacteria cannot spread, and phagocytes engulf many at once: phagocytosis of the complex.
  • The complex is engulfed by polymorphs; lysosomal enzymes digest it inside the cell.
  • Say: "antibodies label and clump pathogens" — not: "antibodies kill or digest bacteria".
Mission 3

Wrong blood group, clumped red cells

  • Red blood cells carry antigens that define the blood group.
  • If donor red cells carry an antigen complementary to antibodies in the recipient's plasma, those antibodies bind them.
  • The red cells agglutinate: they form antigen–antibody clumps.
  • Clumps can block small blood vessels, cutting the blood and oxygen supply to a tissue. The tissue may be damaged or die.
  • Full detail of ABO and rhesus groups: the transfusion mission.
Quick check
?Why does an antibody cause bacteria to clump together?
Mission 3

First time slow, second time fast

  • Primary response (first exposure): few specific B cells must be selected, activated and cloned. Days pass; antibody peaks low. You may become ill.
  • Memory cells form and survive for years.
  • Secondary response (same antigen again): memory cells divide at once into plasma cells. Antibody rises sooner, much higher, and stays high longer.
  • The pathogen is usually destroyed before symptoms: you are immune.
  • B memory cells and T memory cells both make the secondary response faster.
  • Say: "memory cells persist" — not: "antibodies stay in the blood for life".

Mission 3

Active and passive immunity

  • Active: your own lymphocytes make antibodies and memory cells. Takes days. Long-lasting.
  • Passive: you receive ready-made antibodies. Works at once. Short-term: the antibodies are broken down and no memory cells form.
  • Natural active: catching the infection. Artificial active: vaccination (see Mission 4).
  • Natural passive: antibodies across the placenta, or in colostrum (first breast milk).
  • Artificial passive: serum from a person recovering from a new disease with no known treatment; monoclonal antibodies from another species.
Quick check
?After a bite from a possibly rabid dog, a traveller is injected with rabies antibodies and also starts a course of rabies vaccine. Why are both given?
Mission check

?Illustrative data: a vaccine is given in two doses, one month apart. After dose 1, antibody rose slowly over two weeks to a low level. After dose 2, the same antibody rose within a few days to about ten times that level. What best explains the response to dose 2?

The second meeting is faster because memory cells are waiting, not antibodies. That is how a second infection, or a second vaccine dose, works.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Some vaccines protect for life, but the flu vaccine changes every year. Why the difference?

Make a guess: Some vaccines protect for life, but the flu vaccine changes every year. Why the difference?

Mission 4

How a vaccine works

  • A vaccine contains antigens from a pathogen: killed or weakened pathogens, purified antigen proteins, or inactivated toxins.
  • Some vaccines contain mRNA or DNA coding for an antigen. Body cells make the antigen for a short time.
  • The antigen triggers a primary response: plasma cells, antibodies and memory cells.
  • No disease develops. A later infection meets a fast secondary response.
  • Vaccination gives artificial active immunity.
  • Protection lasts only while the pathogen's antigens stay the same. Flu antigens change, so its vaccine is remade each year.
  • Say: "a vaccine contains antigens" — not: "a vaccine contains antibodies".
Quick check
?An mRNA vaccine against COVID-19 contains RNA with the sequence coding for the spike protein of SARS-CoV-2, packaged in lipid nanoparticles. Which statement correctly explains how it stimulates immunity?
Mission 4

Herd immunity

  • A pathogen spreads only when an infected person passes it to someone susceptible.
  • Vaccines protect the individual and, at high coverage, the population.
  • When a high enough percentage is immune, most contacts are immune: chains of transmission break.
  • This protects people who cannot be vaccinated: newborns, people with weakened immune systems.
  • More infectious diseases need higher coverage: about 95% for measles.
  • If coverage falls below that level, outbreaks return.
Quick check
?For measles, transmission in a population is greatly impeded once about 95% of people are immune. A community's vaccination coverage falls from 96% to 85%. Which statement correctly describes a consequence?
Mission 4

Vaccination, society and the economy

  • Vaccination protects individuals and, through herd immunity, whole populations.
  • Fewer people die or are disabled by disease.
  • Fewer hospital admissions and treatments: lower healthcare costs.
  • Fewer days lost from work and school; parents are not kept home caring for sick children.
  • Epidemics and pandemics are prevented or cut short, so trade and travel continue.
  • Eradication ends the cost of vaccinating at all: smallpox vaccination stopped worldwide.
Mission check

?Rubella is usually mild in children, but infection early in pregnancy can seriously harm the foetus. Many countries vaccinate all children, boys and girls. How does vaccinating children protect pregnant women who are not immune?

A vaccine protects the person who receives it and, at high coverage, the people around them.

Mission 5

Mission 5: How can a paper strip pick out one hormone from the thousands of chemicals in a urine sample?

Make a guess: How can a paper strip pick out one hormone from the thousands of chemicals in a urine sample?

Mission 5

One clone, one target

  • An antibody binds an antigen with a complementary shape.
  • Monoclonal antibodies: identical antibodies made by one clone of cells.
  • So every molecule binds the same antigen, and nothing else.
  • Made in the laboratory, in large amounts.
  • They can carry a dye, an enzyme or a drug to one target.
Mission 5

The pregnancy test

  • After implantation, the embryo, then the placenta, secretes hCG (human chorionic gonadotropin). Some reaches the urine.
  • (1) Urine soaks along the strip. Mobile monoclonal antibodies, complementary to hCG and carrying coloured particles, bind any hCG.
  • (2) Test line (T): fixed antibodies to hCG catch the hCG–antibody complexes. A coloured line forms.
  • (3) Control line (C): fixed antibodies that bind the mobile antibodies themselves. A line forms whether or not hCG is present.
  • C and T: pregnant. C only: not pregnant. No C line: the test has failed.
  • Say: "antibodies complementary to hCG" — not: "antibodies that detect pregnancy".
Quick check
?A home pregnancy test strip contains monoclonal antibodies. Which statement explains how the test indicates pregnancy?
Mission 5

ELISA

  • ELISA: enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. Detects, and measures, a protein in a sample.
  • (1) Capture antibody fixed to the well.
  • (2) Sample added. The target protein binds. Wash.
  • (3) Detection antibody, with an enzyme attached, binds the protein. Wash.
  • (4) Substrate added. The enzyme turns it coloured. More protein, more colour.
  • Washing removes unbound antibody. Otherwise the enzyme gives colour even with no target present.
  • Control well with no target: stays colourless.
  • Biomarker: a protein whose level signals a disease process. Cytokines (e.g. IL-6) rise in inflammation.
Quick check
?In an ELISA, the well is washed after the enzyme-linked antibody is added and before the substrate is added. Why?

Mission 5

Borrowed antibodies

  • Passive immunity: antibodies are given, not made by the person.
  • Artificial passive: e.g. monoclonal antibodies from another species, made using mouse cells.
  • Many are now "humanised": mostly human sequence, so less likely to cause a reaction.
  • E.g. palivizumab protects high-risk babies from RSV, a lung virus.
  • Short-term: the antibodies are broken down; no memory cells form.
  • Antibodies from another species are themselves foreign: the recipient may make antibodies against them.
Mission check

?A rapid test for virus X has monoclonal antibodies complementary to protein N of virus X, on mobile coloured particles and fixed at the test line. The control line works as in a pregnancy test. A person infected with virus Y, whose protein N has a different shape, uses the test correctly. What result is expected?

One clone, one shape, one target. That is how a strip finds a single hormone in a sample full of other molecules.

Mission 6

A blood group is an antigen

  • Red blood cell membranes carry antigens: glycoproteins and glycolipids.
  • Your blood group is set by which antigens your red cells carry.
  • ABO system: four groups.
  • Group A: antigen A. Group B: antigen B.
  • Group AB: both. Group O: neither.
  • Different people inherit different antigens: blood group polymorphism.

  • They are present from early childhood, without any transfusion.
  • You cannot have antibodies complementary to your own antigens: they would clump your own cells.
Quick check
?A patient of blood group A is mistakenly transfused with red blood cells from a donor of blood group B. Which statement explains why the patient's immune system responds to the transfused cells?
Mission 6

Matching donor and recipient

  • Compare the donor's antigens with the recipient's plasma antibodies.
  • Recipient has antibodies complementary to the donor's antigens: incompatible.
  • The donor's antibodies have a negligible effect: they are diluted in the recipient's much larger plasma volume.
  • Group O red cells carry no A or B antigen: they can be given to any ABO group.
  • Group AB plasma has no anti-A or anti-B: AB recipients can receive red cells of any ABO group.
  • In practice, hospitals give the same group where they can, and check rhesus too.
Quick check
?A patient has blood group B. Which donor groups' red blood cells are compatible with the patient?
Mission 6

When it goes wrong

  • Recipient's antibodies bind antigens on the donor's red cells.
  • Each antibody has two binding sites, so it links cells together: agglutination (clumping).
  • Clumps block small blood vessels: blood and O2 supply to tissues are cut off. Cells there can die.
  • Clumped cells also burst. Released haemoglobin can damage the kidneys.
  • A severe reaction can be fatal.
  • Say: "the recipient's antibodies agglutinate the donor's cells" — not: "the two bloods clot together".

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Barriers stop entry: skin (physical and chemical), mucus and cilia; clotting seals cuts.
  • Innate defences respond the same way to any pathogen; phagocytes engulf by endocytosis and digest with lysosomal enzymes in a phagosome.

Mission 2

  • Antigens select the few lymphocytes with complementary receptors; activated B cells form clones of plasma cells that all make one antibody, plus memory cells.
  • T helper cells, activated by antigen-presenting cells, switch on B cells and T killer cells, which destroy infected and abnormal cells.

Mission 3

  • Antibodies are Y-shaped proteins with two complementary binding sites; they clump pathogens for phagocytes. Active immunity makes memory cells and lasts; passive immunity is borrowed antibody and fades.
  • Memory cells make the secondary response sooner, larger and longer: that is immunity.

Mission 4

  • Vaccines (antigens, or nucleic acids coding for them) produce memory cells without disease.
  • High coverage gives herd immunity, protecting those who cannot be vaccinated.

Mission 5

  • Monoclonal antibodies are identical and bind one complementary antigen, so they can find, report or deliver to a single target.
  • ELISA: antibody captures the target; an enzyme-linked antibody and substrate make colour in proportion to the amount; washing prevents false positives.

Mission 6

  • ABO groups are set by A and B antigens on red cells; plasma has antibodies only against the antigens a person lacks.
  • Transfusion is incompatible when the recipient's antibodies match the donor's antigens: agglutination blocks vessels and cuts the O2 supply to tissues.
