Mission 1

Why does toppling one domino prove a formula for every nn?

Make a guess: Why does toppling one domino prove a formula for every nn?

Proof · mathematical induction

The structure of an induction proof

  • To prove a statement P(n)P(n) for all n∈Z+n ∈ ℤ⁺:
  • Basis: show P(1)P(1) is true.
  • Assumption: assume P(k)P(k) is true for some k∈Z+k ∈ ℤ⁺.
  • Inductive step: using that assumption, show P(k+1)P(k+1) is true.
  • Conclusion: "Since P(1)P(1) is true, and P(k)P(k) true implies P(k+1)P(k+1) true, P(n)P(n) is true for all n∈Z+n ∈ ℤ⁺ by mathematical induction." Markschemes reward a clear concluding statement.
Worked example Proof · mathematical induction

Induction for a sum

Question
Prove by induction that r=1∑n​r(r+1)=3n(n+1)(n+2)​Σ_(r=1)ⁿ r(r+1) = (n(n+1)(n+2))/3 for all n∈Z+n ∈ ℤ⁺.
  1. 1
    Basis, n=1n = 1: LHS =1×2=2= 1 × 2 = 2 and RHS =31×2×3​=2= (1 × 2 × 3)/3 = 2, so P(1)P(1) is true
  2. 2
    Assume P(k)P(k): r=1∑k​r(r+1)=3k(k+1)(k+2)​Σ_(r=1)^k r(r+1) = (k(k+1)(k+2))/3
  3. 3
    Add the next term, (k+1)(k+2)(k+1)(k+2): r=1∑k+1​r(r+1)=3k(k+1)(k+2)​+(k+1)(k+2)Σ_(r=1)^(k+1) r(r+1) = (k(k+1)(k+2))/3 + (k+1)(k+2)
  4. 4
    Take out the common factor: =3(k+1)(k+2)(k+3)​= ((k+1)(k+2)(k+3))/3, which is the formula with n=k+1n = k+1, so P(k+1)P(k+1) is true
  5. 5
    Write the conclusion sentence
Answer
True for n=1n = 1; P(k)⇒P(k+1)P(k) ⇒ P(k+1); so true for all n∈Z+n ∈ ℤ⁺ by induction.
⚠️ Watch out
factorise, do not expand. Expanding to a cubic in kk makes it hard to see that you have reached the n=k+1n = k + 1 form.

Proof · mathematical induction

Divisibility and other contexts

  • Divisibility: to prove f(n)f(n) is divisible by dd, assume f(k)=dMf(k) = dM for some integer MM. Write f(k+1)f(k+1) in terms of f(k)f(k), then show it is dd times an integer.
  • Induction also proves results for derivatives, such as dxndn​(xex)=(x+n)exdⁿ/dxⁿ(xe^x) = (x+n)e^x, and for complex numbers (De Moivre's theorem).
  • The basis need not be n=1n = 1. For example, n!>2nn! > 2ⁿ is false for n=3n = 3 (6<86 < 8) but true from n=4n = 4, so its basis is n=4n = 4.

Induction for divisibility

Question
Prove by induction that 32n−13²ⁿ − 1 is divisible by 8 for all n∈Z+n ∈ ℤ⁺.
  1. 1
    Basis, n=1n = 1: 32−1=83² − 1 = 8, which is divisible by 8
  2. 2
    Assume 32k−1=8M3^2k − 1 = 8M for some M∈ZM ∈ ℤ
  3. 3
    Write the next case using the old one: 32(k+1)−1=9×32k−1=9(32k−1)+83^(2(k+1)) − 1 = 9 × 3^2k − 1 = 9(3^2k − 1) + 8
  4. 4
    Substitute: =9(8M)+8=8(9M+1)= 9(8M) + 8 = 8(9M + 1), and 9M+19M + 1 is an integer, so 8 divides it
  5. 5
    Write the conclusion sentence
Answer
P(1)P(1) true and P(k)⇒P(k+1)P(k) ⇒ P(k+1), so 32n−13²ⁿ − 1 is divisible by 8 for all n∈Z+n ∈ ℤ⁺.
⚠️ Watch out
32(k+1)=32k+2=9×32k3^(2(k+1)) = 3^(2k+2) = 9 × 3^2k, not 32k+23^2k + 2 or 6×32k6 × 3^2k.

Mission check

?Why does toppling one domino prove a formula for every nn?

Induction needs both parts. The basis shows the first case is true, and the inductive step shows that whenever case kk is true, case k+1k + 1 is true. Together they pass the truth along the whole line: 1, then 2, then 3, and so on for every positive integer.

Mission 2

How can assuming something false prove something true?

Make a guess: How can assuming something false prove something true?

Proof · contradiction and counterexample

Proof by contradiction

  • To prove a statement, assume its negation (the opposite) is true.
  • Use valid steps to reach a contradiction: something impossible, or something that clashes with the assumption.
  • Valid steps cannot turn a true statement into an impossible one, so the assumption must be false. Hence the original statement is true.
  • Typical uses: a number is irrational (assume it equals qp​p/q in lowest terms), there are infinitely many of something (assume a finite list).
Worked example Proof · contradiction and counterexample

3​√3 is irrational

Question
Prove by contradiction that 3​√3 is irrational.
  1. 1
    Assume 3​=qp​√3 = p/q with p,q∈Z+p, q ∈ ℤ⁺ and no common factor (lowest terms)
  2. 2
    Square and rearrange: p2=3q2p² = 3q², so p2p² is a multiple of 3. Since 3 is prime, pp is a multiple of 3
  3. 3
    Write p=3mp = 3m: 9m2=3q29m² = 3q², so q2=3m2q² = 3m², and qq is a multiple of 3 too
  4. 4
    Then pp and qq share the factor 3, contradicting "lowest terms"
Answer
The assumption is false, so 3​√3 is irrational.
⚠️ Watch out
the step "p2p² is a multiple of 3, so pp is" needs 3 to be prime. Say so. The same argument fails for 4​√4, because p2p² a multiple of 4 does not force pp to be one.
Quick check
?You want to prove by contradiction: "if aa is rational and bb is irrational, then a+ba + b is irrational". What should the first line assume?
Worked example Proof · contradiction and counterexample

Euclid: there are infinitely many primes

Question
Prove by contradiction that there are infinitely many prime numbers.
  1. 1
    Assume there are finitely many primes, and list them all: p1​,p2​,…,pn​p₁, p₂, …, pₙ
  2. 2
    Form N=p1​p2​⋯pn​+1N = p₁ p₂ ⋯ pₙ + 1. Then N>1N > 1
  3. 3
    Dividing NN by any pi​pᵢ leaves remainder 1, so no prime on the list divides NN
  4. 4
    But N>1N > 1 has at least one prime factor, and it is not on the list: a contradiction
Answer
The assumption is false, so there are infinitely many primes.
⚠️ Watch out
the proof does not say NN itself is prime. It says NN has a prime factor missing from the list.

Proof · contradiction and counterexample

Disproof by counterexample

  • A statement claimed for all values is disproved by one counterexample: a value for which it is false.
  • Give the value and show why it fails. Stating the value alone does not earn the marks.
  • Example: "x2+y2=10x² + y² = 10 has no positive integer solutions" is false, since x=1x = 1, y=3y = 3 gives 1+9=101 + 9 = 10.
  • Checking many cases that work never proves an "all" statement; one failure disproves it.

Mission check

?How can assuming something false prove something true?

You assume the opposite of what you want to prove and reason with valid steps. If you reach something impossible, such as a fraction in lowest terms with a common factor, the assumption cannot be true. So its opposite, the statement you wanted, must be true.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Basis, assumption P(k)P(k), inductive step P(k)⇒P(k+1)P(k) ⇒ P(k+1), then the conclusion sentence.
  • For sums, add the (k+1)(k+1)th term to the assumed sum and factorise towards the n=k+1n = k + 1 form.
  • For divisibility, write f(k+1)f(k+1) in terms of f(k)f(k) and show it is a multiple of the divisor; the basis can be a value other than 1.
  • Contradiction: assume the negation of the conclusion, reach an impossibility, conclude the statement is true.
  • Classic results: 3​√3 (and 2​√2, 35​∛5) is irrational; there are infinitely many primes; rational + irrational is irrational.
  • One counterexample, with the reason it fails, disproves an "all" statement; examples alone never prove one.
