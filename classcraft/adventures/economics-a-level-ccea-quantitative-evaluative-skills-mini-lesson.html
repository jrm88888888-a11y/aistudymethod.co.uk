Mission 1

Mission 1: When every number in a news story is true, how can the story still mislead you?

Make a guess: When every number in a news story is true, how can the story still mislead you?

Mission 1

Read the data before you use it

  • Before you calculate anything, check four things:
  • Units: %, billions, an index, per person? Money in which currency and which year's prices?
  • Level or rate of change? "GDP" is a level; "GDP growth" is a rate of change.
  • Time period: a month, a quarter, a year? Which years are compared?
  • Source: who collected it, and how? Is it a forecast or an outturn?
  • Then ask: nominal or real? Money values not adjusted for inflation are nominal.
  • Say: "the rate of inflation fell" — not: "prices fell", when inflation is still above zero.
Mission 1

Percentage change or percentage points?

  • Percentage change = (new value − old value) ÷ old value × 100.
  • When the data are already rates (%), the plain difference is in percentage points.
  • Illustrative data: an unemployment rate rises from 4.0% to 5.0%.
  • Difference: 5.0 − 4.0 = 1 percentage point.
  • Percentage change: 1 ÷ 4 × 100 = 25%.
  • Real case: UK Bank Rate was 0.1% before December 2021 and 5.25% from August 2023.
  • That is a rise of 5.15 percentage points, or a 5,150% rise. Both true; the second sounds far more dramatic.
  • Percentage changes do not cancel: 100 falls 20% to 80; 80 rises 20% to 96, not 100.
  • Say: "rose by 1 percentage point" — not: "rose by 1%".
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A country's unemployment rate falls from 8.0% to 6.0%. Which statement is correct?
Mission 1

Ratios and fractions

  • A ratio compares two quantities: exports to imports, debt to GDP, top earners to bottom earners.
  • Illustrative data: government debt = 1,800 billion; GDP = 2,400 billion.
  • Debt-to-GDP ratio = 1,800 ÷ 2,400 = 0.75 = 3/4 = 75%.
  • Next year: debt rises 5% to 1,890; GDP rises 8% to 2,592.
  • New ratio = 1,890 ÷ 2,592 = 0.729, about 72.9%.
  • Debt rose, yet the ratio fell: the bottom of the fraction grew faster.
  • Say: "the debt-to-GDP ratio fell" — not: "debt fell".
Mission 1

Mean, median and quantiles

  • Illustrative data: weekly income of 10 households, in order:
  • 150, 200, 250, 300, 350, 400, 450, 550, 700, 2,650.
  • Mean = total ÷ number = 6,000 ÷ 10 = 600.
  • Median = middle value. With 10 values: (350 + 400) ÷ 2 = 375.
  • One very high income pulls the mean up: 8 of 10 households earn less than the mean.
  • So for skewed data, such as income and wealth, the median shows the typical household better.
  • Quantiles split ordered data into equal-sized groups: quartiles (4), quintiles (5), deciles (10).
  • Bottom quintile (2 households) gets 350 ÷ 6,000 = 5.8% of income. Top quintile gets 3,350 ÷ 6,000 = 55.8%.
  • Top household ÷ bottom household: 2,650 ÷ 150 = 17.7 times as much.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. Seven workers earn these hourly wages: 9, 10, 10, 11, 12, 14, 32. The best-paid worker gets a rise from 32 to 46. What happens to the mean and median wage?
Mission 1

Index numbers in a data extract

  • Index = (value ÷ value in base year) × 100 (method in an earlier lesson).
  • Index numbers compare growth of series in different units.
  • Illustrative data: house prices and earnings, both 100 in the base year. Ten years later: house prices 160, earnings 125.
  • 160 > 125 does not mean houses cost more than people earn. It means house prices grew faster since the base year.
  • House prices relative to earnings: 160 ÷ 125 = 1.28, so 28% higher than in the base year.
  • Rebasing: new index = old index ÷ old index in the new base year × 100.
  • Illustrative data: an index is 110 in 2020 and 132 in 2024. With 2020 = 100: 132 ÷ 110 × 100 = 120.
  • 110 → 132 is a rise of 22 points, but 20%.
  • Say: "the index rose by 22 points, a 20% rise" — not: "the index rose by 22%".
Mission 1

Worked example: nominal to real

  • Illustrative data. Average weekly pay: 600 in 2020, 690 in 2024.
  • Price index (2020 = 100): 120 in 2024.
  • Nominal pay rose: 690 ÷ 600 = 1.15, so +15%.
  • Real pay in 2024, at 2020 prices = 690 × (100 ÷ 120) = 575.
  • Real change: 575 ÷ 600 = 0.958, so −4.2%.
  • Shortcut: 15% − 20% = −5%. Close, but only an approximation; use the exact method for exam answers.
  • Say: "real pay fell by about 4%" — not: "pay fell".
Quick check
?Illustrative data. Government spending on health rises from 150 billion to 180 billion. Over the same period, a price index for health services rises from 100 to 125. What happened to real spending on health?
Mission 1

Graphs: a falling rate is not a falling level

  • On a graph of a rate of change (inflation, growth), a falling line above zero means the level is still rising, only more slowly.
  • Real case: UK CPI inflation reached 11.1% in October 2022, its highest rate in over 40 years.
  • It fell to 2.0% in May 2024.
  • Prices did not fall. They kept rising, more slowly: disinflation.
  • Also check: does the vertical axis start at zero? A cut-off axis makes small changes look large.
  • On a chart with two vertical axes, check which line belongs to which axis.
  • Say: "disinflation" — not: "deflation", unless the price level itself falls.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. An economy's quarterly real GDP growth rates are: Q1 0.8%, Q2 0.5%, Q3 0.2%, Q4 0.1%. Which statement is correct?
Mission 1

Build an evaluation chain

  • Point: answer the question in one line.
  • Analysis: a chain of reasoning, step by step: "because … which leads to … so …".
  • Evidence: quote the data with units and dates, and calculate where it helps.
  • Evaluation: "it depends on …": how big? how likely? short run or long run? who gains, who loses? how reliable is the data?
  • Judgement: weigh the factors and say which matters most, and why.
  • At A2, add a recommendation: what should be done, justified by your judgement.
  • Say: "this depends on the size of the multiplier, because …" — not just: "it depends".
Mission 1Evaluate

Did the policy work?

  • Real case: the Bank of England's target is 2% CPI inflation.
  • Bank Rate rose 14 times, from 0.1% to 5.25% (December 2021 to August 2023).
  • CPI inflation fell from 11.1% (October 2022) to 2.0% (May 2024).
  • Criteria for success: was the target met? How quickly? At what cost to growth and jobs?
  • It depends on …
  • The counterfactual: what would inflation have done anyway? Other causes, such as world energy prices, may have changed too.
  • Time lags: interest rates affect spending and prices only after a delay, so the timing of any effect is uncertain.
  • Side effects: higher mortgage and loan costs, weaker spending and output.
  • Other policies: fiscal policy was acting at the same time.
  • Judge: a target being reached shows the outcome, not on its own what caused it.
Quick check
?In an essay on whether higher interest rates brought UK inflation down after 2022, which sentence is evaluation?
Mission check

?Illustrative data. In 2022 a government began paying firms to take on apprentices aged 16–24. The youth unemployment rate fell from 15.0% in 2022 to 12.0% in 2024. Over the same years, the overall unemployment rate fell from 6.0% to 4.0%. Which conclusion is best supported by the data?

Calculate first, in the right units; then ask what else could explain the change before you credit the policy.

Mission 2

What earns the marks

Every exam graph needs:

1. Both axes labelled with the variable (Price, Quantity, Price level …), not a curve name.

2. Every curve labelled (D, S, MC, AD …).

3. Equilibrium marked: dotted lines to both axes, labelled (Pe and Qe, or P1 and Q1).

4. Every change shown: an arrow from old curve to new, with numbered labels (S1 → S2).

5. The new equilibrium labelled (P2, Q2), so the direction of each change is clear.

  • CCEA: label the diagram fully and refer to it in your answer ("as shown by the shift from S1 to S2").
  • Neat, large and simple beats artistic: straight lines are fine.

Mission 2

Build a demand and supply graph step by step

Example: a new technology cuts the cost of making solar panels.

1. Draw and label axes and curves D and S1. Mark P1 and Q1 with dotted lines.

2. Costs fall → supply increases: draw S2 to the right of S1, with an arrow from S1 to S2.

3. Mark the new equilibrium: P2 (lower) and Q2 (higher), with dotted lines.

  • Say: "an increase in supply" or "supply shifts right" — not: "supply moves down" or "the supply curve goes up".
  • Say: "an extension of demand" (a movement along D) — not: "demand increases".
Quick check
?Frost destroys part of the orange harvest. Which graph shows this correctly?
Mission 2

Firm curves: the rules examiners mark

  • MC cuts AVC and AC at their minimum points, from below.
  • For a downward-sloping linear D (= AR), MR starts at the same point on the vertical axis and is twice as steep: it hits the horizontal axis halfway to D.
  • Profit-maximising output: where MR = MC. Then go up to D to read the price.
  • Profit rectangle: (P − AC) × Q. Its top edge is at P, its bottom at AC at that output.
  • Perfect competition: the firm's D is horizontal at the market price.
  • CCEA: label it "AR = MR".
Mission 2

Worked example: areas on a graph

Illustrative data.

  • Profit rectangle: MR = MC at Q = 100. Price on D = 12. Average total cost at Q = 100 is 9.

1. Profit per unit = 12 − 9 = 3.

2. Total profit = 3 × 100 = 300.

  • Deadweight loss triangle from a tax of 4 per unit: quantity falls from 60 to 40.

1. Base = 60 − 40 = 20; height = the tax = 4.

2. DWL = ½ × 20 × 4 = 40.

  • Shade the area and label it ("profit", "DWL"). The triangle points towards the efficient quantity.

  • Takeaway: find Q where MR = MC, then go up to D for the price.

Mission 2

Macro graphs

  • AD/AS: label AD, SRAS and LRAS. Mark the price level and real output with dotted lines.
  • LRAS is vertical at full-capacity output (Yf) in the classical view. The Keynesian AS is flat at low output, then curves up and becomes vertical.
  • Output gaps: mark the distance between Y1 and Yf and label it "negative output gap" or "positive output gap".
  • Growth on a PPC: outward shift, PPC1 → PPC2. Unemployed resources: a point inside the PPC.
  • Show policy effects with an arrow: AD1 → AD2, and label Y2 and P2.
Quick check
?A student draws an AD/AS graph to show a recession. Which pair of axis labels is correct?
Mission 2

Common errors that cost marks

  • Axes labelled with curve names ("Demand", "Supply").
  • A shift drawn, but no arrow and no numbered labels.
  • Equilibrium marked by a dot only, with no dotted lines to the axes.
  • MR drawn above D, or MC not through the minimum of AC.
  • Price read at the MR = MC height instead of from D.
  • LRAS not vertical; a gap labelled but not marked between two output levels.
  • A graph that contradicts the written answer.
Mission 2

Command words and graphs

  • Draw / show: put it on the graph, with labels and arrows.
  • Explain: give the chain of reasoning in words ("costs fall → supply increases → price falls"). A graph alone is not enough.
  • Calculate: write the formula and each step. Give units.
  • Interpreting data charts: read the units and the scale; a fall in the rate of inflation is not a fall in prices; an index of 120 means 20% above the base year.
Mission check

?A student shows the effect of a successful advertising campaign for oat milk. Their graph has: the vertical axis labelled "Demand", the horizontal axis labelled "Supply"; curves D1, D2 and S; an arrow from D1 to D2 (rightward); dotted lines to P1, Q1 and P2, Q2. Which part of the graph would an examiner mark wrong?

Full marks come from labels: variables on the axes, names on the curves, arrows on the changes and dotted lines to each equilibrium.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Check units, level or rate, period, source, and nominal or real before you use any number.
  • Rates change in percentage points; a percentage change divides by the old value. A falling rate above zero is still a rise.
  • An evaluation chain runs Point → Analysis → Evidence → Evaluation → Judgement; a policy reaching its target does not on its own prove it caused the change.
  • For skewed data, the median shows the typical value better than the mean; ratios can fall while both parts rise.

Mission 2

  • Label both axes (variables), every curve, the equilibrium (dotted lines) and every change (arrow, S1 → S2, P2, Q2).
  • Use the right axes for each graph.
  • Read monopoly price from D at MR = MC; MC cuts AC and AVC at their minimum.
  • Draw, then explain the chain in words.
