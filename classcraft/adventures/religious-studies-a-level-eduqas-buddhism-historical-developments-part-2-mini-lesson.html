Mission 1

One name, many forms

  • "Buddhism" covers Theravada (Sri Lanka, Thailand, Myanmar), Mahayana (e.g. Zen, Pure Land, Nichiren in East Asia) and Vajrayana (Tibet), plus newer Western forms (e.g. Triratna, secular Buddhism).
  • They differ in scriptures, goals, practices, languages and forms of monastic life.
  • The word "Buddhism" itself was popularised by 19th-century Western scholars, who grouped these traditions under one label. Asian Buddhists spoke of the Dharma (dhamma) or the Buddha's teaching.
  • The question: are these one religion with branches, or several religions sharing a name?
Mission 1

A shared core?

  • The Three Jewels (Buddha, Dharma, Sangha): every tradition takes refuge in them.
  • The Four Noble Truths and the Eightfold Path.
  • The three marks of existence: Dukkha, Anicca (impermanence), anatta (not-self).
  • Dependent origination and karma and rebirth.
  • The five precepts for lay people.
  • 1967: the World Buddhist Sangha Council (Colombo) agreed nine basic points uniting Theravada and Mahayana, drafted by Walpola Rahula (1907–1997): e.g. the Buddha as the only master; refuge in the Triple Gem; no creator God; the Four Noble Truths; and that differing customs in different countries do not touch the essence.

Mission 1

Upaya: skilful means

  • Upaya (skilful means): the Buddha adapts his teaching to each listener's capacity, like a doctor prescribing different medicine.
  • Lotus Sutra, chapter 2 ("Skilful Means") and chapter 3: the parable of the burning house. A father's children will not leave a burning house; he promises them goat-, deer- and ox-carts. Once they are safe, he gives each the same, far finer great carriage (paraphrase).
  • The three carts are the "three vehicles"; the great carriage is the One Vehicle (ekayana) to Buddhahood.
  • So, for many Mahayana Buddhists, different teachings are provisional: true as means, not final statements.
  • This can explain diversity positively: many forms because there are many kinds of people.
  • Your spec: teachings "sometimes considered to be only provisional, different upaya (skilful means) to suit different listeners".
Mission 1Read the source

The raft

Majjhima Nikaya 22, Alagaddupama Sutta (paraphrase): A traveller reaches a great river. This bank is dangerous; the far bank is safe. With no ferry, he builds a raft from grass and branches and crosses. Should he carry the raft on his head for the rest of his journey? No: having crossed, he leaves it. In the same way, the Dhamma is for crossing over, not for holding on to.

Q1 What is the raft in the parable?

Show answer

The teaching, Dhamma: a means to cross to nirvana.

Q2 How might a Buddhist use this parable when meeting a very different Buddhist tradition?

Show answer

Forms of teaching are tools; disputes over the raft matter less than reaching the far shore.

  • Note: this is a Pali (Theravada) text: the idea that teachings are means is not only Mahayana.
Mission 1

How Buddhists respond to diversity within Buddhism

  • Inclusive / upaya view: all forms are skilful means suited to different people (common in Mahayana; Lotus Sutra).
  • Graded view: traditions are steps on one path. Tibetan lamrim ("stages of the path") places shravaka, Mahayana and Vajrayana practice in order. Older Mahayana texts called earlier schools Hinayana ("lesser vehicle"), a term now avoided.
  • Authenticity view: many Theravadins hold that the Pali Canon is the closest record of the Buddha's words and treat later Mahayana sutras with caution.
  • Exclusive view: Nichiren (1222–1282) taught that only the Lotus Sutra leads to Buddhahood in this age.
  • Co-operative view: shared bodies such as the World Buddhist Sangha Council (1966) or the UK's Network of Buddhist Organisations.
  • Blind men and the elephant (Udana 6.4, paraphrase): blind men each touch one part of an elephant and quarrel over what it is; the Buddha compares this to teachers who cling to one-sided views.

Mission 2

Test it, even the Buddha

  • Kalama Sutta (Anguttara Nikaya 3.65; see an earlier lesson): do not accept a teaching merely from tradition, scripture or a teacher's reputation; test it by whether it leads to harm or well-being.
  • Vimamsaka Sutta (Majjhima Nikaya 47): monks should investigate even the Buddha to see whether he is truly awakened (paraphrase).
  • So loyalty rests on experience, not on belonging to a group.
  • This leaves room for respect: a person's honest testing may lead them along another path.
  • Your spec: "the individual testing the teachings and staying true to experience, even if that means following a path different to Buddhism".
Mission 2Read the source

The Buddha and Upali

Majjhima Nikaya 56, Upali Sutta (paraphrase). Upali, a wealthy lay supporter of the Jain teacher Nigantha Nataputta, comes to defeat the Buddha in debate. Instead he is convinced and asks to become the Buddha's lay follower.

  • The Buddha replies (section 16): investigate carefully first; careful investigation is right for a well-known man like you.
  • Upali is even more impressed: other teachers would have paraded through the town announcing their new convert.
  • The Buddha adds (section 17): your family has long supported the Jain monks; you should still give them alms when they come.

Q1 What two things does the Buddha ask of Upali?

Show answer

Investigate before changing allegiance; keep supporting his former teachers.

Q2 What does this suggest about Buddhist attitudes to other faiths?

Show answer

Respect and generosity continue across religious lines; conversion is not a trophy.

  • Your spec: Majjhima Nikaya 56.16, the call to investigate carefully.
Mission 2

Ashoka's edicts

  • Ashoka (reigned c. 268–232 BCE), Mauryan emperor of most of India. After the bloody Kalinga war he turns to the Dharma (Rock Edict 13). His edicts are carved on rocks and pillars across his empire.
  • Rock Edict 7 (paraphrase): the king wishes that all sects may live everywhere, for all of them seek self-control and purity of heart.
  • Rock Edict 12 (paraphrase):
  • The king honours all sects, ascetics and householders, with gifts.
  • What matters most is the growth of the essentials in all of them.
  • Its root is restraint in speech: do not praise your own sect or condemn others without good reason.
  • Whoever praises his own sect and condemns others out of devotion harms his own sect.
  • Concord is good: listen to and respect one another's teachings.
  • Your spec names Rock Edicts 7 and 12.

Mission 2Diversity

Other religions and worldviews: truth claims

  • Real differences in truth claims: most Buddhists reject a creator God (the Brahmajala Sutta, Digha Nikaya 1, tells of a god who wrongly thinks he made the world); anatta contrasts with an eternal soul; liberation is by insight, not by grace (except in Pure Land's other-power).
  • Exclusivist: only the Buddha's path (or, for Nichiren, the Lotus Sutra) fully liberates.
  • Inclusivist: other religions contain real goodness and partial truth, useful as upaya, but the full path is the Dharma.
  • Pluralist: many paths can lead to the good; the 14th Dalai Lama (b. 1935): for an individual, "one truth, one religion"; for society, "several truths, several religions" (Toward a True Kinship of Faiths, 2010; short phrases).
  • Non-religious worldviews: the Buddha rejected materialism (nothing survives, actions have no fruit) as a wrong view, as well as eternalism; yet many Buddhists share humanists' reliance on reason and ethics, and secular Buddhists drop rebirth.
Mission 2

Interfaith dialogue: benefits, extent, barriers

  • Examples: the Dalai Lama with Thomas Merton (1968) and with Christian, Jewish and Muslim leaders; Thich Nhat Hanh, Living Buddha, Living Christ (1995); Buddhist and Christian monastics at the Gethsemani Encounter (Kentucky, 1996).
  • Benefits: peace and mutual understanding in multicultural societies; shared action on poverty, peace and climate; learning from one another's contemplative practice.
  • Barriers: different truth claims (God, soul, grace); Buddhism tied to national identity in some countries (Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Thailand), with fear of conversion; exclusivist schools; no single Buddhist authority to speak for all.
  • Extent varies: strong in Western convert communities and among some Mahayana groups (SGI, Rissho Kosei-kai); weaker where Buddhism is a majority faith under pressure.
Mission 2Evaluate

Does openness put Buddhist identity at risk?

  • Claim: Buddhism's openness to other paths puts its own identity at risk.
  • For: if testing may lead elsewhere, and other faiths contain good, why remain Buddhist? Blending (e.g. "dual belonging") can blur the Dharma.
  • For: in majority-Buddhist countries, fear of conversion shows openness can feel like a threat.
  • Against: openness is part of the identity: Upali, Ashoka and the Kalama Sutta are themselves Buddhist sources.
  • Against: respect is not relativism: Buddhists still hold that the Dharma ends dukkha; Nichiren and others draw firm lines.
  • Criteria: Is identity defined by doctrine, practice, community or ethics? Does the answer differ by tradition or country?
  • Issues for evaluation: openness and identity; the value of experience compared with other sources of authority.

Mission 3

How Buddhism reached Britain

  • Scholars first: the Pali Text Society (1881) translated the Pali Canon; Edwin Arnold's poem The Light of Asia (1879) made the Buddha's life widely known.
  • Early British monk: Allan Bennett, ordained in Burma as Ananda Metteyya, led a Buddhist mission to London in 1908.
  • Societies and viharas: Christmas Humphreys' Buddhist Lodge (1924, later the Buddhist Society); the London Buddhist Vihara (1926), founded by the Sri Lankan reformer Anagarika Dharmapala.
  • From the 1960s: Samye Ling, the first Tibetan centre in the West (Scotland, 1967); the Friends of the Western Buddhist Order (1967, now Triratna); Throssel Hole Soto Zen monastery (1972); Chithurst (1979) and Amaravati (1984).
  • Every major school is now present in Britain: Theravada, Zen, Tibetan, Pure Land, Nichiren and new Western forms.
Mission 3

Migration and diversity

  • Migration brought whole communities: Sri Lankans, Thais, Burmese, Chinese, Japanese; Vietnamese refugees from the late 1970s; Tibetans after 1959.
  • They founded temples as religious and community centres: language, festivals such as Wesak, food, care for elders.
  • At the 2021 Census, about 0.5% of people in England and Wales described themselves as Buddhist.
  • Diversity: many traditions, languages and ethnic groups; temples of one tradition may serve several nationalities.
Mission 3

Why Buddhism became popular

  • No creator God and no demand for "blind faith": it fits a scientific, questioning age (the Kalama Sutta's call to test teachings).
  • Meditation and mindfulness: practical help with stress and the mind; mindfulness-based therapy is now used in the NHS.
  • Ethics without commandments: compassion, non-harm, personal responsibility.
  • Counter-culture: from the 1950s–60s, writers and seekers looked East (D. T. Suzuki's books on Zen; the Beat writers).
  • Role models: the Dalai Lama and Thich Nhat Hanh.
  • Openness: people can attend a class or retreat without converting.
  • Spec issue: "The comparative significance of features which contribute to the popularity of Buddhism in Britain."
Mission 3

Heritage and convert Buddhism

  • Heritage Buddhism: the Buddhism of people whose families came from Buddhist countries and settled in Britain, including their British-born descendants. Convert Buddhism: that of people who have chosen Buddhism.
  • Your spec: heritage Buddhism is "the Buddhism of those whose relatives were born in Buddhist countries and migrated to Britain".
  • Typical heritage emphasis: devotion, offerings, merit (punya) through giving to monks, festivals, chanting in Pali or other Asian languages, family and community.
  • Typical convert emphasis: meditation, study, retreats, lay-led groups, less ritual, often in English.
  • These are tendencies: many heritage Buddhists meditate; many converts give dana to monks; second-generation British Buddhists blur the line.
  • The binary comes from scholarship: Charles Prebish wrote of "two Buddhisms" in America (1979). Others have questioned it: Jan Nattier proposed three routes in (imported by seekers, exported by missions, carried by migrants) (paraphrase).
  • Some find the labels uncomfortable when they imply that one group is more "authentic" or more "modern".

Mission 3

Amaravati and the Forest Tradition

  • The Thai Forest Tradition stresses strict monastic discipline and meditation, as taught by Ajahn Chah (1918–1992).
  • His American disciple Ajahn Sumedho (b. 1934) came to England in 1977; with lay support he founded Chithurst (1979) and Amaravati (1984, Hertfordshire), and was abbot there until 2010.
  • Monks keep the full Vinaya: celibacy, no handling of money, no food after midday, alms round (pindapata) in local streets.
  • They depend entirely on lay supporters, both Thai, Sri Lankan and other heritage Buddhists and British converts: a meeting point of the two.
  • Retreats, meditation teaching and family events serve a wide public.

Mission 3Evaluate

Is convert Buddhism as legitimate as heritage Buddhism?

  • Claim: Convert Buddhism is a less authentic form than heritage Buddhism.
  • For: it often drops rebirth, merit and devotion, which most Asian Buddhists treat as central.
  • For: it may pick meditation out of its setting of ethics, community and the monastic sangha.
  • Against: Buddhism has always adapted as it spread (India → China → Japan → Tibet); skilful means suits teaching to hearers.
  • Against: converts take refuge in the same Three Jewels; heritage practice can also be cultural rather than committed.
  • Against: many converts (e.g. among Amaravati's lay supporters) practise as traditionally as heritage Buddhists.
  • Judgement criteria: What is the essential core (refuge? Four Noble Truths? the Vinaya)? Who decides authenticity: texts, teachers or communities?

Mission 4: Can a woman become a Buddha? Why do Buddhist texts give different answers?

Make a guess: Can a woman become a Buddha? Why do Buddhist texts give different answers?

Mission 4

Two different questions

  • Q1: Can a woman reach awakening (Nirvana; become an arhat, Pali arahant)?
  • Q2: Can a woman become a fully enlightened Buddha (samma-sambuddha), who rediscovers and teaches the Dharma for a whole age?
  • Many texts answer yes to Q1 and no, not in a female body to Q2.
  • Keep two more levels apart: theoretically (what the teaching allows) and realistically (whether women get ordination, teachers, education and respect).
Mission 4

Early texts: women can awaken

  • When Ananda asks whether women who go forth can reach the fruits of the path up to arahantship, the Buddha says yes (Cullavagga X.1; see Mission 5).
  • Therigatha ("verses of the elder nuns"): about 73 poems by early nuns describing their awakening, e.g. Patacara, Kisa Gotami.
  • Soma and Mara (Samyutta Nikaya 5.2, paraphrase): Mara taunts that a woman's "two-finger wisdom" cannot reach the goal. Soma replies: what does being a woman matter, when the mind is concentrated and insight arises? Only someone who thinks "I am a woman" or "I am a man" is Mara's target.
  • So the earliest teaching treats insight, not sex, as what liberates.
Mission 4

Early and Theravada limits

  • Bahudhatuka Sutta (Majjhima Nikaya 115, paraphrase): it is impossible for a woman to be a fully enlightened Buddha, a wheel-turning king, Sakka, Mara or Brahma; possible for a man.
  • Later Theravada commentary lists eight conditions for a future Buddha to receive the prediction of Buddhahood; one is being male.
  • Traditional Buddha-images describe the 32 marks of a great man, including male physical marks.
  • A karma explanation sometimes given: female birth reflects less merit; women make merit (e.g. by supporting monks) hoping for a male rebirth.
  • Many Theravada teachers today stress the first answer: arahantship is fully open to women, and that is the goal of the path for all.

Mission 4Read the source

The Dragon King's daughter

Lotus Sutra, "Devadatta" chapter (paraphrase):

  • Manjushri says the eight-year-old daughter of the dragon king Sagara is wise and close to awakening. A bodhisattva doubts it: the Buddha himself practised for countless ages.
  • Shariputra objects: a woman's body cannot be a vessel for the Dharma; women face five obstacles (they cannot be Brahma, Indra, Mara, a wheel-turning king, or a Buddha).
  • She offers the Buddha a precious jewel, which he accepts at once. She says she will become a Buddha even faster than that.
  • Instantly her body becomes male; he completes the bodhisattva practices and becomes a Buddha in a southern world, teaching all beings.

Q1 Which of Shariputra's assumptions does the story overturn?

Show answer

That a young, female, non-human being cannot reach Buddhahood, or only after ages.

Q2 Why do some readers call the story limiting?

Show answer

She still becomes male before Buddhahood.

  • Your spec: Lotus Sutra chapter 12 (Kumarajiva's Chinese numbering, the "Devadatta" chapter).
Mission 4

Other Mahayana voices

  • Lotus Sutra overall: all beings possess the potential for Buddhahood (One Vehicle); the Buddha also predicts Buddhahood for his aunt Mahapajapati and his former wife Yasodhara (ch. 13 in Kumarajiva).
  • Vimalakirti Sutra, ch. 7: a goddess changes Shariputra into a woman's form to show that "female" and "male" have no fixed essence: all things are neither male nor female (emptiness).
  • Srimaladevi Sutra: Queen Srimala teaches the Dharma with the Buddha's approval.
  • Tara (Tibetan Buddhism): vows to reach and to teach awakening in a female body.
  • Pure Land: Amida's vows include one that women who wish it will no longer be reborn as women (often read as freeing them from social disadvantage).
  • So Mahayana is not one view: some texts affirm women fully; some still need a male body at the end.

Mission 4

Family life and the roles of men and women

  • In the dhamma: marriage is a lay, social matter; there is no Buddhist marriage sacrament. The Sigalovada Sutta (Digha Nikaya 31, paraphrase) sets mutual duties:
  • Husband to wife: respect, courtesy, faithfulness, giving her authority in the home, providing for her.
  • Wife to husband: managing the household well, hospitality to relatives, faithfulness, protecting the family's wealth, diligence.
  • In history: wealthy laywomen such as Visakha were leading patrons of the early Sangha; men dominated monastic leadership.
  • In contemporary society: in Thailand and Myanmar many young men ordain temporarily (merit often dedicated to their mothers); Western Buddhist groups usually treat men and women as equal teachers and leaders.

Mission 4

Rita Gross: Buddhism after patriarchy

  • Rita M. Gross (1943–2015), American scholar and practitioner; Buddhism After Patriarchy (1993).
  • History: Buddhist institutions have been androcentric (male-centred); women's stories were often forgotten.
  • Doctrine: core teachings (anatta, emptiness, buddha-nature) give no basis for fixed gender roles; the Dharma is "neither male nor female".
  • So: Buddhism's commitment to ending suffering for all, and its teaching that gender is empty, make it inherently feminist at its core.
  • Reconstruction: recover women's history; train and recognise women teachers; change institutions to fit the teaching.
Mission 4

Salgado and Bartholomeusz: whose feminism?

  • Tessa Bartholomeusz (d. 2001), Women under the Bo Tree (1994): history of Sri Lankan lay nuns (dasa sil mata, "ten-precept mothers") from the late 19th century; women created a respected religious life without full ordination, amid colonial and nationalist change.
  • Nirmala S. Salgado, Buddhist Nuns and Gendered Practice (2013): warns against judging nuns by Western liberal feminist ideas of rights and equality; many dasa sil matas value renunciation and do not seek higher ordination; their own understanding should come first.
  • Contrast with Gross: Gross reconstructs Buddhism using feminist values; Salgado asks whether those values fit Asian nuns' own aims.
Mission 4Evaluate

Does Buddhism align with feminism?

  • Claim: Buddhism is inherently feminist.
  • For: anatta and emptiness: no fixed male or female self; women's awakening affirmed (Therigatha, Soma); Gross.
  • For: modern movements (Sakyadhita, bhikkhuni revival) draw on the Buddha's own founding of the nuns' order.
  • Against: texts barring a female Buddha (e.g. MN 115) and the eight garudhammas (see Mission 5) place women below men.
  • Also against: the Dragon King's daughter becomes male; and "feminism" is itself a Western framework that may not fit Buddhist renunciants (Salgado).
  • Criteria: Teaching or institutions? Early texts or later ones? Whose feminism, and whose voices count: scholars, monks, nuns, laywomen?
  • Issue for evaluation: the extent to which Buddhism aligns with feminism.

Mission 5

The fourfold assembly

  • The Buddha's community has four parts: bhikkhus (monks), bhikkhunis (nuns), upasakas (laymen), upasikas (laywomen).
  • Bhikkhuni (Pali) / bhikshuni (Sanskrit): a fully ordained nun, who keeps the nuns' Vinaya (311 rules in the Theravada Vinaya; 348 in the Dharmaguptaka Vinaya used in East Asia).
  • Full ordination (upasampada) is given by a quorum of the Sangha; for nuns the Vinaya requires a dual ordination: by bhikkhunis, then by bhikkhus.
  • Below full ordination: novices (samaneri) and probationers (sikkhamana).
Mission 5Read the source

Mahapajapati Gotami asks to go forth

Cullavagga X.1 (also Anguttara Nikaya 8.51), paraphrase:

  • Mahapajapati Gotami, the Buddha's aunt and foster mother, asks him three times to let women go forth. Three times he refuses.
  • She and many Sakyan women shave their heads, put on robes and walk to Vesali. She stands outside, feet swollen, in tears.
  • Ananda asks: are women able to reach stream-entry and the further fruits, up to arahantship? The Buddha: they are.
  • Ananda reminds him she nursed him as a baby. The Buddha agrees, if she accepts eight weighty rules. She accepts them "like a garland".
  • The text adds a prediction: because women have gone forth, the true Dhamma will last 500 years instead of 1,000.

Q1 What reason does Ananda's question give for admitting women?

Show answer

They can reach the same goal as men.

Q2 Which parts of the story do critics see as later additions?

Show answer

The eight rules and the 500-year prediction.

  • Your spec: "The Buddha ordained women, after persuasion."
Mission 5

The eight garudhammas

Pali garudhamma ("weighty rules"), paraphrased:

  • 1. A nun, even of 100 years' standing, bows to a monk ordained that day.
  • 2. Nuns do not spend the rains retreat where there are no monks.
  • 3. Every fortnight nuns ask the monks for the observance day and for instruction.
  • 4. At the end of the rains, nuns invite criticism from both Sanghas.
  • 5. A nun guilty of a serious offence undergoes discipline before both Sanghas.
  • 6. A probationer trained for two years seeks full ordination from both Sanghas.
  • 7. A nun must never abuse or revile a monk.
  • 8. Nuns may not admonish monks; monks may admonish nuns.
  • Effect: the nuns' order depends on and ranks below the monks' order.
  • Some scholars think the rules were added later: rule 6 assumes a probation stage that the Vinaya only introduces afterwards.

Mission 5

How the line was broken, and where it survives

  • Ordination needs living bhikkhunis (dual ordination). Once the last nuns die, who can ordain new ones?
  • Sri Lanka: the Theravada bhikkhuni order dies out by about the 11th century, after war and invasion; Myanmar by about the 13th century. Thailand has no record of a bhikkhuni order.
  • 433 CE: Sri Lankan nuns travel to China and help give the dual ordination at Nanjing: the Chinese lineage descends partly from Sri Lanka.
  • Mahayana lineages continue unbroken today in China, Taiwan, Korea and Vietnam, under the Dharmaguptaka Vinaya.
  • Tibet: the full bhikshuni ordination of its own Vinaya (Mulasarvastivada) was never established; women ordain as novices. Some have taken full ordination from East Asian nuns. Separately, in Bhutan, 2022, over 140 women received full ordination.
  • Thailand today: a 1928 order of the Supreme Patriarch forbids Thai monks to ordain women; the Thai Sangha still applies it.
Mission 5

The late 1990s revival

  • 1987: Sakyadhita ("Daughters of the Buddha"), the International Association of Buddhist Women, founded at Bodh Gaya (Karma Lekshe Tsomo, Ayya Khema, Chatsumarn Kabilsingh and others). Aims: unite Buddhist women, education, research, and full ordination where women want it.
  • 1996, Sarnath: ten Sri Lankan women receive full ordination with Korean bhikkhunis and Sri Lankan monks.
  • 1998, Bodh Gaya: an international ordination led by the Taiwanese order Fo Guang Shan includes Theravada women.
  • From 1998, Sri Lanka: bhikkhuni ordinations held in Sri Lanka itself (Dambulla), now continuing in Sri Lanka's own Sangha.
  • 2009, Australia: Ajahn Brahm's monastery ordains four bhikkhunis; his monastery is removed from the Thai forest lineage that trained him.
Mission 5Diversity

The ordination debate

  • For revival:
  • The Buddha ordained women and affirmed their awakening.
  • Dharmaguptaka and Theravada Vinayas descend from close early schools; and Sri Lankan nuns founded the Chinese line.
  • Some (e.g. Bhikkhu Bodhi; Bhikkhu Analayo) argue monks alone may ordain nuns when no nuns exist, as at the very start.
  • Against revival:
  • The Vinaya requires dual Theravada ordination; once broken, only a future Buddha could restart it.
  • East Asian nuns follow a different Vinaya school; mixing lineages is invalid.
  • Harmony of the Sangha and respect for senior monks' rulings.
  • Another voice: some mae chi and dasa sil matas prefer their freedom from monks' authority and do not want full ordination (see Salgado).
Mission check

?A Theravada woman in Sri Lanka is fully ordained in 2020 by Sri Lankan bhikkhunis and monks. A Thai woman wishes to do the same in Thailand. Why does the Thai woman face greater difficulty?

The same Vinaya meets different histories and authorities: that is why ordination is open in Taiwan and Sri Lanka but blocked in Thailand.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Buddhist traditions differ in goal, power, practice, clergy and scripture, yet share the Three Jewels, Four Noble Truths and three marks.
  • Upaya (Lotus Sutra) and the raft (Majjhima Nikaya 22) present teachings as means, explaining diversity positively.
  • Buddhists respond to internal diversity with inclusive, graded, authenticity-based, exclusive and co-operative views.

Mission 2

  • Upali (Majjhima Nikaya 56) and the Kalama Sutta: investigate, and keep honouring other teachers; Ashoka's Rock Edicts 7 and 12: all sects seek self-control; restraint in speech; concord is good.

Mission 3

  • Buddhism reached Britain through scholars, early converts and, above all, migration; its popularity rests on meditation, a non-theistic outlook and famous teachers.
  • Heritage and convert Buddhism differ in emphasis (merit and devotion; meditation), but the line is blurred.

Mission 4

  • Early texts affirm women's awakening (arahantship); texts such as MN 115, and later Theravada, say a Buddha cannot be female.
  • Mahayana is mixed: the Dragon King's daughter attains Buddhahood instantly but becomes male first; Vimalakirti and Tara deny or transcend fixed gender.

Mission 5

  • Mahapajapati Gotami became the first bhikkhuni after Ananda's plea; the eight garudhammas placed nuns under monks, and critics question whether they are original.
  • The Theravada line died out (Sri Lanka by the 11th century); Mahayana lines survive.
