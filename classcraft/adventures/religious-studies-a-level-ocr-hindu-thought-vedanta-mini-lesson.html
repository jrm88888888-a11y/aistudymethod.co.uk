Mission 1

Vedanta: the end of the Veda

  • Vedanta means "end of the Veda": the Upanishads, the last and most philosophical part of the Vedas.
  • It became a darshana (school of philosophy), also called Uttara Mimamsa: inquiry into the later Veda.
  • Every Vedanta school comments on three texts, the prasthana-traya (triple foundation): the Upanishads, the Brahma Sutras (Badarayana; date uncertain) and the Bhagavad Gita.
  • The central question: how are Brahman, the atman (self) and the world related?
  • Three great answers: Advaita (non-dual; Shankara), Vishishtadvaita (qualified non-dual; Ramanuja), Dvaita (dual; Madhva). Ramanuja and Madhva.
Mission 1

Shankara in context

  • Shankara (also Sankara, Shankaracharya): c. 700–750 CE by many scholars; traditionally 788–820 CE. Born in Kerala.
  • Became a sannyasi (renouncer) young; disciple of Govinda, whose teacher Gaudapada had already taught non-duality (Mandukya Karika).
  • Wrote commentaries (bhashyas) on the Brahma Sutras, the main Upanishads and the Gita.
  • Rivals: Mimamsa ritualists, who held that ritual action is the heart of the Veda; and Buddhists, who denied a permanent self. Shankara argued against both: liberation comes through knowledge of the eternal self.
  • Tradition says he founded monasteries (mathas) at Sringeri, Puri, Dwarka and Jyotirmath, and organised orders of renouncers.
Mission 1

One without a second

  • For Shankara's Advaita, Brahman alone is ultimately real: "one only, without a second" (Chandogya 6:2:1, paraphrase).
  • Nirguna Brahman: without qualities; beyond thought and words. It can only be described by negation: neti, neti ("not this, not this", Brihadaranyaka 2:3:6).
  • Positively, it is described as sat-chit-ananda: being, consciousness, bliss.
  • Saguna Brahman (with qualities) or Ishvara (the Lord): Brahman as seen from within the world: creator, worshipped as Vishnu, Shiva or the Goddess. Real at the everyday level, not the final truth.
  • Shankara teaches that atman is Brahman: the true self is not a part of Brahman but Brahman, fully.
Mission 1Read the source

"You are that"

  • Chandogya Upanishad 6 (paraphrase): Uddalaka teaches his son Shvetaketu. Salt dissolved in water cannot be seen, yet every sip tastes of it (6:13). The subtle essence pervades everything; "that is the Self, and you are that" (tat tvam asi).
  • 6:11–14 add the tree that lives while its life is in it, the tiny seed of the banyan, the salt, and the man led home once his blindfold is removed by a teacher.
  • Shankara reads "you are that" as identity: the self and Brahman are one, not two.
  • The Gita on the self (Arnold):

> "Never the spirit was born; the spirit shall cease to be never; / Never was time it was not; End and Beginning are dreams!" (Bhagavad Gita 2:20, Arnold)

Mission 1

Adhyasa: superimposition

  • Adhyasa: seeing one thing as another. Shankara opens his Brahma Sutra commentary with it.
  • Rope and snake: at dusk a rope looks like a snake. The snake is superimposed on the rope. You feel real fear; but there was only ever a rope.
  • Shell and silver: mother-of-pearl glints like silver.
  • In the same way we superimpose body, mind and individuality onto the self ("I am tall", "I am sad"), and the qualities of the self onto body and mind.
  • Advaitins call the power by which the one Brahman appears as many maya.
  • Ignorance (avidya) does not make the rope vanish; knowledge removes the snake. So, for Shankara, liberation is by jnana (knowledge).

Mission 1

Maya and avidya

  • Maya (in Advaita): the power by which the one Brahman appears as many. Often translated "illusion", but the world is not "nothing".
  • Advaitins call maya indescribable: not real (it is not there in the final truth) and not unreal (it is experienced; it is not like a "son of a barren woman").
  • Avidya: ignorance in the individual; it hides Brahman and projects the world.
  • Samsara is the result: the self takes itself to be a body-mind with karma, and is reborn.
Mission 1

Three levels of reality

  • Advaitins distinguish three levels:
  • Illusory (pratibhasika): dreams, the snake in the rope. Real only while the mistake lasts.
  • Mundane, empirical (vyavaharika): the everyday world of people, gods, karma, dharma, science and worship. Fully real for us: you must still keep your promises and avoid the bus.
  • Ultimate (paramarthika): Brahman alone.
  • Each level is cancelled only by the one above: waking cancels the dream; knowing Brahman cancels the separateness of the world.
  • Shankara himself mostly contrasts two standpoints (everyday and ultimate); later Advaitins set out three.

Mission 1

Liberation, bhakti and moral action

  • Bondage (for Shankara): avidya. Liberation (moksha): direct knowledge that atman is Brahman. It is not produced by any action; it is the removal of ignorance.
  • Jivanmukti: one can be liberated while still alive.
  • Karma yoga and dharma: they do not liberate, but they purify the mind and make it ready for knowledge.
  • Bhakti is valuable but inferior for Shankara: devotion to a personal God belongs to the everyday level, where worshipper and worshipped seem two. It prepares the mind; it does not give final knowledge.
  • Moral action: required of seekers (self-control, calm, detachment, longing for liberation); the liberated act without selfish desire.
  • The Gita: Shankara reads it as teaching that knowledge is the direct means to moksha; action and devotion lead towards it.
Mission 1

Influence and scholars

  • Advaita became the most widely known school of Vedanta: carried by the Shankaracharyas of the mathas, the Smarta tradition and later Advaitins.
  • Vedanta, above all Advaita, became the best-known Hindu philosophy outside India through Neo-Vedanta and Vivekananda (see Mission 2).
  • Scholars (e.g. Gavin Flood, An Introduction to Hinduism, 1996) note that this modern prominence can hide how many Hindus follow theistic Vedanta and bhakti.
Mission 1Evaluate

Does Advaita give the best account of Vedanta?

  • Claim: Shankara's Advaita is the most convincing reading of the Upanishads.
  • For: It takes "you are that" and "one without a second" at full strength.
  • For: Adhyasa explains how one reality can appear as many, using ordinary experience.
  • Against (Ramanuja): where does avidya live? Not in Brahman, who is pure knowledge; not in the individual self, which is itself a product of avidya.
  • Against (devotional): an impersonal Brahman leaves no one to love; bhakti, central to most Hindus, is demoted.
  • Against (ethical): if separateness is not ultimately real, why care for others? Reply: because the other is your own self, harming them harms you.
  • Judgement criteria: fidelity to shruti; coherence of avidya; fit with Hindu practice; consequences for ethics.

Mission 2

Ramakrishna: a priest of the Mother

  • Sri Ramakrishna (1836–1886), born Gadadhar Chattopadhyay in a Bengal village; little schooling, no English.
  • From 1856, priest at the Dakshineswar Kali temple near Calcutta.
  • He longed to see the Goddess Kali as his Divine Mother; his disciples record that he had overwhelming visions of her.
  • He then followed many disciplines, guided by different teachers: Tantric practice; Vaishnava devotional moods; and Advaita, under the monk Totapuri, reaching nirvikalpa samadhi (absorption without any sense of difference).
  • His wife Sarada Devi (1853–1920) became his disciple and is revered as the Holy Mother.
  • His sayings were recorded by a disciple, "M" (Mahendranath Gupta), in the Kathamrita (The Gospel of Sri Ramakrishna).
Mission 2

As many faiths, so many paths

  • According to his disciples, Ramakrishna also practised Islam (1866) and Christianity (1874) for short periods, and reported experiences of God through each.
  • His conclusion (paraphrase): "As many faiths, so many paths": each religion, sincerely followed, leads to the same God.
  • His image (paraphrase): one water is called jal, pani and "water" by different people; so one God has many names.
  • God is both formless and with form; Brahman and Shakti are one, like fire and its power to burn.
Mission 2

Images, caste, and the world

  • Image worship: unlike the Brahmos, Ramakrishna worshipped the Mother in her murti; for him the image was a real meeting point with God.
  • He met Brahmo leaders, especially Keshab Chandra Sen, who made him known among educated Bengalis.
  • Caste: stories tell of him humbling his brahmin pride by cleaning the latrine of a low-caste servant.
  • A saying recorded by his disciples: serve living beings as Shiva, not out of pity (shiva jnane jiva seva). Vivekananda built on it.

Mission 2

Vivekananda

  • Swami Vivekananda (1863–1902), born Narendranath Datta in Calcutta; Western-educated; a member of the Brahmo Samaj, sceptical of visions.
  • Met Ramakrishna in 1881 and became his leading disciple; after Ramakrishna's death (1886) he became a sannyasi and travelled across India, shocked by its poverty.
  • 1893: spoke at the Parliament of the World's Religions in Chicago; became famous overnight.
  • Lectured in the USA and Britain; founded the Vedanta Society of New York (1894).
  • Returned to India a hero; founded the Ramakrishna Mission (1897). Died aged 39.
Mission 2Read the source

Chicago, 11 September 1893

Vivekananda's opening address (paraphrase):

  • He greeted the audience as "Sisters and Brothers of America".
  • He said he was proud to belong to a religion that had taught the world tolerance and universal acceptance: Hindus not only tolerate other religions but accept all religions as true.
  • He quoted a Hindu hymn: as rivers from different sources all reach the sea, so the different paths people take, straight or crooked, all lead to God.
  • He hoped the Parliament would end sectarianism, bigotry and fanaticism.

Mission 2

Neo-Vedanta

  • Neo-Vedanta (term used by Indologist Paul Hacker, 1913–1979): the modern form of Vedanta taught by Vivekananda and his successors.
  • Advaita at the centre: Brahman is the one reality; every soul is potentially divine.
  • Experience (anubhava), not only scripture, is the ground of religion: religion is "realisation".
  • Four yogas (Vivekananda's lectures, 1895–96): karma, bhakti, jnana and raja yoga, suited to different temperaments, all leading to the same goal.
  • Universal: Vedanta is presented as the eternal truth behind all religions, and as friendly to science.
  • Continued by thinkers such as Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan (1888–1975), The Hindu View of Life (1927).
Mission 2

Practical Vedanta and the Ramakrishna Mission

  • Practical Vedanta (lectures in London, 1896): if Brahman is in every being, then serving people is worshipping God.
  • Vivekananda spoke of God in the poor (daridra narayana) and attacked "don't-touch-ism" (untouchability) and neglect of the masses and of women.
  • Shankara's Advaita centred on renunciation and knowledge; Vivekananda reinterpreted it as a call to social service.
  • Ramakrishna Mission (1897), with Belur Math near Kolkata as headquarters. Motto: "for one's own liberation and for the good of the world".
  • Today: hospitals, schools, colleges, rural development, relief after disasters, in India and beyond.
  • A liberationist approach: working to lift the poor and oppressed, seeing service to them as service to God.

Mission 2

Vedanta as the religion behind all religions

  • The view: all faiths, and all traditions within Hinduism, are partial expressions of the one truth Vedanta states most fully.
  • Supporters: it grounds tolerance and interfaith respect; it unites Hindu diversity; it fits Ramakrishna's experience.
  • Hindu critics: theistic traditions (e.g. Vaishnavas, ISKCON) say a personal God, not impersonal Brahman, is the highest truth; the Arya Samaj puts the Vedas, not experience, first.
  • Scholarly critics: Paul Hacker called this inclusivism: other faiths are welcomed only as lower stages of Advaita. Anantanand Rambachan (b. 1951), The Limits of Scripture (1994), argues that Vivekananda moved authority from scripture (as in Shankara) to personal experience.
  • Other faiths: many Christians and Muslims reply that their own claims (e.g. Christ as unique saviour) are not respected by being called "partial".
Mission 2

Influence in India and the West

  • In the West: Vedanta Societies; yoga and meditation; writers drawn to Vedanta. Vedanta became the best-known Hindu philosophy outside India.
  • Popular image: Hinduism as tolerant, mystical and philosophical.
  • Critics warn this can stereotype Hinduism and hide its devotional, ritual and regional diversity.
  • Hinduism as a world religion: a universal message, not only an Indian heritage.
  • In India: pride and confidence under colonial rule; a model of service; Vivekananda's birthday is India's National Youth Day.
Mission 2Evaluate

Is Neo-Vedanta a faithful development of Hinduism?

  • Claim: Neo-Vedanta is a faithful development of Hindu tradition.
  • For: it rests on the Upanishads and Advaita; tolerance has deep Hindu roots.
  • For: Practical Vedanta draws a real ethical conclusion from "atman is Brahman".
  • Against: it centres one school (Advaita) and sidelines theistic and ritual Hinduism.
  • Against: Rambachan: it shifts authority from scripture to experience; Hacker: its inclusivism shaped Hinduism for Western audiences.
  • Judgement criteria: faithfulness to sources; fit with how most Hindus practise; fruits (service, tolerance); whether all living traditions change.

Mission 3

Ramanuja in context

  • Ramanuja: traditionally 1017–1137 CE; many scholars place him later in the 11th–12th centuries. Born near Chennai; taught at Srirangam, Tamil Nadu.
  • First studied Advaita, and disagreed with his teacher's reading of the Upanishads.
  • Became leader of the Sri Vaishnava tradition after Yamuna, devoted to Vishnu (Narayana) and Lakshmi (Sri).
  • Sources: the Upanishads, Brahma Sutras and Gita; the Pancharatra temple scriptures; and the Tamil hymns of the Alvars, poet-saints of loving devotion to Vishnu.
  • Works: Sri Bhashya (on the Brahma Sutras), a Gita commentary, Vedartha Sangraha.
Mission 3

The world as God's body

  • Vishishtadvaita ("non-duality of the qualified") is the school of Ramanuja (11th–12th century), a devotee of Vishnu. For Ramanuja there is one reality, Brahman, but it is qualified by souls and matter.
  • Brahman is Vishnu: personal, with infinite good qualities (saguna). Not beyond all qualities.
  • Souls (chit) and matter (achit) are real and eternal. They are the body of God; God is their inner soul and controller.
  • As a body belongs to a person and cannot exist apart, the world belongs to God: inseparable, yet distinct.
  • "You are that" means: you, whose inner self is Brahman, belong to that Brahman. Not strict identity.
  • The world is not illusion. Maya is God's wonderful creative power.
Mission 3

Bondage and liberation for Ramanuja

  • Bondage: karma (beginningless) contracts the soul's knowledge; it identifies with bodies and is reborn.
  • Liberation (moksha): the soul's knowledge expands; after death it enjoys the bliss of God in Vaikuntha, Vishnu's heaven.
  • The liberated soul becomes like God in bliss and purity, but never identical with God. It remains itself, in loving service.
  • Means: bhakti (continuous loving meditation on God) and prapatti (surrender), with God's grace.
  • Dharma is not abandoned: duties are done as worship of God.

Mission 3

Madhva: five eternal differences

  • Madhva (traditionally 1238–1317 CE), from Udupi, Karnataka, where he set up the Krishna temple.
  • Dvaita: dualism. For Madhva, Vishnu alone is independent; everything else is dependent on him.
  • Five eternal differences: God and souls; God and matter; soul and soul; soul and matter; matter and matter.
  • He reads Chandogya 6:8:7 as "atat tvam asi": "you are not that". A soul is a reflection of God, never God.
  • Souls are graded: some are fit for liberation; some remain in samsara; some go to darkness. A unique and much-debated teaching.
  • Liberation: by bhakti grounded in right knowledge of God, through Vishnu's grace. Liberated souls enjoy bliss, each to its own capacity.

Mission 3Evaluate

Shankara or Ramanuja?

  • Claim: Ramanuja gives a better account of Hinduism than Shankara.
  • For: Most Hindus worship a personal God; Ramanuja makes that worship the highest path.
  • For: He avoids the problem of where avidya lives; the world is simply real.
  • Against: Shankara takes the identity texts ("you are that") more literally.
  • Against: Advaita can include bhakti at the everyday level, while pointing beyond it.
  • Against (Madhva's challenge to both): only eternal difference keeps God truly supreme.
  • Judgement criteria: fidelity to shruti; coherence; fit with Hindu practice; what it means for the relationship with God.

Make a guess: "Hinduism" is a word outsiders coined. Is it one religion, many religions, or a way of life?

Mission 4

Where the word came from

  • Sindhu is the Sanskrit name of the Indus river.
  • Persians said Hindu: the land and people beyond the Indus. It was a geographical name, not a religious one.
  • Muslim rulers in India later used "Hindu" for Indians who were not Muslim.
  • "Hinduism" (early spelling "Hindooism") came into English around 1800, used by British writers and also by Indian reformers.
  • Before that, people named their own traditions: devotees of Vishnu or Shiva, followers of a teacher, the Vedic way.
  • So the word groups together traditions that did not always see themselves as one.
Mission 4

Sanatana and sadharana dharma

  • Dharma: the order that upholds the universe, and the duty and right conduct that follow from it.
  • Sanatana dharma: the eternal dharma. Many Hindus prefer it to "Hinduism": it has no founder and no beginning.
  • Sadharana dharma: universal duties for everyone: non-violence, truthfulness, not stealing, purity, self-control (e.g. Manusmriti 10.63, paraphrase).
  • These contrast with duties that depend on one's place in society and stage of life (varnashrama dharma).
Mission 4

The Hindu renaissance and neo-Hinduism

  • 19th-century reformers responded to British rule and Christian missions by presenting Hinduism as one rational, universal religion.
  • Ram Mohan Roy (1772–1833), Brahmo Samaj (1828): one formless God, from the Upanishads; against image worship and sati.
  • Dayananda Saraswati (1824–1883), Arya Samaj (1875): back to the Vedas alone.
  • Swami Vivekananda (1863–1902): Chicago Parliament of Religions (1893): Hinduism as Vedanta, tolerant, accepting all religions as true paths.
  • Neo-Hinduism (term used by Paul Hacker, 1913–1979): the modern, unified, Vedanta-centred Hinduism of these reformers.
  • Critics: it put Advaita Vedanta at the centre and shaped "Hinduism" for Western audiences. Supporters: it renewed genuine Hindu sources.

Mission 4Diversity

One religion or many?

What differs:

  • Deities: Vaishnavas (Vishnu and his avatars), Shaivas (Shiva), Shaktas (the Goddess), Smartas (several deities as forms of one Brahman).
  • Philosophy: non-dualist (Advaita), qualified non-dualist, dualist (Dvaita).
  • Practice: regional festivals, caste customs, temple or home worship, ascetic or family life.
  • No single founder, creed, scripture or authority.

What is shared (by most):

  • Respect for the Vedas; ideas of karma, samsara, moksha, dharma; puja, pilgrimage, samskaras.
  • Images used by scholars: a family of traditions; a banyan tree with many trunks (Julius Lipner).
Mission 4

Is "religion" the right word?

  • The modern idea of "religion" grew from Christian (and Jewish) history: a creed, a founder, a scripture, a membership, and a line between sacred and secular.
  • Wilfred Cantwell Smith (1916–2000), The Meaning and End of Religion (1962), paraphrase: "religion" turns living faith into a thing; "Hinduism" is an especially misleading example, since Hindus did not see themselves as one system.
  • Dharma is wider than "religion": it covers law, ethics, ritual, social duty and cosmic order.
  • Western academic divisions into religion, philosophy and ethics fit poorly: in Hindu thought a darshana (philosophy, "view") is also a path of practice and a way of life.
  • Defenders reply: every concept is imperfect; "Hinduism" still names real, shared features.
Mission 4

Different goals

Hindus describe the aim of their tradition in different ways:

  • Liberation (moksha): release from samsara, the cycle of rebirth.
  • Overcoming maya: seeing through illusion or ignorance about what is real.
  • Union with Brahman: realising the self's oneness with ultimate reality (Advaita), or loving union with a personal God (bhakti).
  • A way of life / world view: living by dharma, family duty, festivals and worship; for many Hindus, moksha is distant and dharma is the daily focus.
  • The variety of goals is one reason some say "Hinduism" is several religions.

Mission 4

Hinduism as a world religion

  • World religion model: Hinduism listed alongside Christianity, Islam and Buddhism in textbooks, censuses and interfaith events.
  • Encouraged by Vivekananda's Chicago speech (1893), migration (Hindu communities in the UK, USA, Caribbean, East Africa), and movements such as ISKCON and the Ramakrishna Mission.
  • For the model: gives Hindus a shared identity, voice in interfaith dialogue and legal recognition.
  • Against: imposes a Christian-shaped template; favours the Vedanta and Brahmin version over local and lower-caste practice.
Mission 4Evaluate

Is Hinduism one religion?

  • Claim: "Hinduism" names a single religion.
  • For: Shared ideas (karma, samsara, dharma, moksha) and respect for the Vedas link almost all Hindus.
  • For: Hindus themselves use the name; the idea of sanatana dharma gives an inner unity.
  • Against: The word came from outsiders; deities, philosophies and goals differ greatly.
  • Against: "Religion" assumes a founder, creed and canon that Hinduism lacks.
  • Judgement criteria: What makes a religion "one": a founder, a creed, shared practices, or self-identity? Who decides: scholars, reformers or ordinary Hindus?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Vedanta interprets the Upanishads, Brahma Sutras and Gita; Shankara's Advaita holds that Brahman alone is real and atman is Brahman.
  • The world appears through maya and avidya, by adhyasa (superimposition, like a rope seen as a snake); there are illusory, mundane and ultimate levels of reality.
  • Liberation is by knowledge; dharma, karma yoga and bhakti prepare the mind but are lower; critics question avidya, devotion and ethics.

Mission 2

  • Ramakrishna (1836–1886): Kali devotee and mystic; experiences through many paths led him to teach "as many faiths, so many paths"; he accepted both images and the formless.
  • Vivekananda (1863–1902): Chicago 1893; Neo-Vedanta (Advaita, experience, four yogas, all religions true); Practical Vedanta and the Ramakrishna Mission (1897): serving people as God.
  • "Vedanta as religion" grounds tolerance but is criticised as inclusivist and as sidelining theistic Hinduism.

Mission 3

  • Ramanuja (Vishishtadvaita): souls and matter are real and form God's body; liberation is loving communion with Vishnu, like him but not him, reached by bhakti, surrender and grace.
  • Madhva (Dvaita): five eternal differences; God alone independent; souls graded; "you are not that".

Mission 4

  • "Hindu" began as a Persian geographical name; "Hinduism" entered English around 1800. Many Hindus prefer sanatana dharma.
  • Hinduism has no single founder, creed or scripture; scholars speak of a family of traditions with shared ideas.
  • 19th-century reformers (Roy, Dayananda, Vivekananda) presented a unified "neo-Hinduism"; critics question whether "religion" fits.
