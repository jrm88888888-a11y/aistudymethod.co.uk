Paper 1: Communicating Information & Ideas

This mini-lesson trains the skills OCR J351 Paper 1 tests: reading two non-fiction texts from different centuries, retrieving and synthesising information, analysing language, comparing the texts, and writing your own non-fiction piece.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go (spot the device, pick the best comparison, match form to feature) and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you're ready.

Know the paper

What Paper 1 asks of you

Section A gives you two unseen non-fiction texts — one 19th century and one from the 20th or 21st century — on a linked theme, so you can compare ideas over time. The reading questions test:

  • Retrieval / information — find and select ideas and information (AO1).
  • Synthesis — draw ideas together across both texts (AO1).
  • Language analysis — how a writer uses language for effect (AO2).
  • Comparison — compare the writers' ideas and perspectives (AO3).

Section B — Writing: a non-fiction task (such as an article, letter, speech or report) for a set purpose and audience. Assessed on communication & organisation (AO5) and technical accuracy (AO6).

The whole paper is non-fiction. Both reading texts and your own writing deal with real-world information and ideas — save fiction/creative writing for Paper 2.

Reading skill

Read like a detective — annotate

Marks are lost by readers who skim. Before you write, annotate both texts:

  • Purpose — is it to inform, persuade, describe, argue?
  • Audience — who is it aimed at, and how do you know?
  • Underline striking words, phrases and key ideas.
  • Bracket the writer's viewpoint and any shift of tone.
  • Note the era — how does the 19th-c text differ in attitude from the modern (20th/21st-c) one?

PAT — Purpose, Audience, Tone. Nail these for both texts and comparison becomes straightforward.

Synthesis · AO1

Synthesising across two texts

Synthesis means drawing ideas together from both texts — combining information, not analysing style:

  • Find linked points — an idea in Text 1 that connects to one in Text 2.
  • Infer — read between the lines: what does a detail suggest?
  • Support with brief evidence from both texts.

Synthesis ≠ language analysis. Here you combine what the texts say; save "the writer uses a metaphor" for the language question.

Analysis skill · AO2

Comment on the effect, not the label

Compare two comments on the same line:

"The new rules strangle small businesses." ▸ verb "strangle" — metaphor
Weak (feature-spotting)

"The writer uses a metaphor here. This is effective and makes it interesting."

Strong (device → effect)

"The verb 'strangle' presents the rules as choking the life out of businesses, so the reader pictures something slowly killed off and feels the writer's alarm at the harm being done."

Misconception: the marks are not for spotting the metaphor — they're for explaining why 'strangle' works and what it does to the reader.

Reading skill · AO3

Perspectives over time

Because the two texts come from different centuries, they often share a topic but differ in attitude:

  • Viewpoint — the writer's opinion on the theme.
  • Perspective — where they stand (an insider? an observer? a reformer?).
  • Values of the era — a 19th-c writer may assume very different things from a 20th/21st-c one.

That gap in attitude — often shaped by the time each was written — is exactly what the comparison question rewards you for exploring.

Section B · Writing

Non-fiction writing: form, purpose, audience

Section B is a non-fiction task — informing, persuading, arguing or advising a real reader. Decode the task first:

  • Form — article? letter? speech? report? guide? Each has its own conventions.
  • Purpose — inform, persuade, argue, advise.
  • Audience — a newspaper's readers, a head teacher, the public, MPs.

Match the register to the audience. Getting the register wrong costs AO5 marks even if your ideas are good.

Forms & conventions

Know each form's features

  • Letter — greeting ("Dear…"), formal register, clear paragraphs, sign-off.
  • Article — a catchy headline, an engaging opening hook, a clear voice.
  • Speech — direct address, rhetorical questions, repetition, a rousing final line.
  • Report — a title, neutral/formal tone, findings and a recommendation.
  • Guide / leaflet — clear headings, imperatives, helpful advice.

Structure your writing: plan 4–5 paragraphs, open with a hook, group ideas logically, close memorably. Organisation is a big slice of AO5.

Persuasive toolkit

The DAFOREST toolkit

For persuasive non-fiction, DAFOREST is a checklist of moves that win readers over. Weave several in — don't just tick boxes.

Some versions use Alliteration instead of Anecdote for the "A", and drop Direct address altogether, giving the shorter AFOREST. The point is the techniques, not the acronym.

Technical accuracy · AO6

AO6 — spelling, punctuation, grammar & vocabulary

A large slice of your Section B marks is for technical accuracy and ambitious vocabulary. Easy to gain — or throw away.

  • Vary your sentences — mix short and long for control and effect.
  • Punctuate accurately — full stops, commas, and the occasional colon, semicolon or dash used correctly.
  • Reach for precise vocabulary — "unsustainable" not "bad"; "compelling" not "good".
  • Proofread — leave 3–4 minutes to catch slips.

Ambition with control beats safety with errors. Only use words you can spell and punctuate correctly.

Recap

The skills to take into the exam

Read & annotate: Purpose, Audience, Tone (PAT) for both non-fiction texts.

AO1 synthesis: draw ideas together across both texts, with evidence.

AO2 analysis: device → quotation → effect on the reader (one text).

AO3 comparison: compare ideas & perspectives in the same point, with connectives.

Non-fiction writing (AO5): match form + purpose + audience; use DAFOREST.

AO6: organise clearly, vary sentences, punctuate accurately, proofread.
