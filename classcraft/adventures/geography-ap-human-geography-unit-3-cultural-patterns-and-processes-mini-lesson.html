Mission 1: Who decides which foods, homes and customs count as normal?

Course framework: what this mission covers

  • PSO-3.A.1 culture is the shared practices, technologies, attitudes and behaviors transmitted by a society
  • PSO-3.A.2 culture traits include food preferences, architecture and land use
  • PSO-3.A.3 cultural relativism and ethnocentrism are different attitudes toward cultural difference
Mission 1

What is culture?

  • Culture: the shared practices, technologies, attitudes and behaviors of a society.
  • It is transmitted: learned from family, school and community, not inherited in our genes.
  • A culture trait is a single element of a culture, such as a food, a building style or a way of farming.
Mission 1

Culture trait: food preferences

  • UNESCO lists Washoku, traditional Japanese dietary culture, as intangible heritage (2013).
  • It is "a social practice" covering how food is produced, processed, prepared and eaten.
  • Its basic knowledge and skills are "passed down in the home at shared mealtimes": culture is transmitted.
Mission 1

Culture trait: architecture

  • Fujian tulou, southeast China: earthen houses with tall fortified mud walls around a shared courtyard.
  • Each could house up to 800 people: a whole clan in one building.
  • The design reflects shared needs and values: defense, and living together as a clan.
Mission 1

Culture trait: land use

  • The Ifugao rice terraces of the Philippine Cordilleras follow the contours of the mountains.
  • UNESCO's listing says they have followed the mountain contours "for 2,000 years", farmed with knowledge "handed down from one generation to the next".
  • How a group uses land is a culture trait, shaped by its technologies, beliefs and environment.
Quick check
?Which of these is a culture trait in the category of land use?
Mission 1

Ethnocentrism

  • Ethnocentrism: judging another culture by the standards of your own, often assuming your own way is normal or better.
  • Example: calling another group's breakfast "weird" because it differs from yours.
  • It can lead geographers to misread a landscape or a practice.
Mission 1

Cultural relativism

  • Cultural relativism: trying to understand a culture's practices in terms of its own values, history and environment.
  • Example: asking why a clan built a fortified round house, rather than calling it old-fashioned.
  • Geographers use it to describe cultures accurately, without bias from their own background.
Quick check
?A visitor says that a city's narrow streets and small shops are "less advanced" than wide roads and big-box stores back home. Which attitude is this?
Mission 1

Why it matters to geographers

  • Every geographer comes from a culture too, so attitudes shape what they notice and how they describe it.
  • Cultural relativism helps explain why a trait exists in a place: environment, history, resources.
  • Exam move: when asked to describe a culture trait, name the trait, name the place, then explain a reason it exists there.
Mission check

?A student says, "Eating with your hands is wrong. Normal people use forks." Which response best reflects cultural relativism?

Each society defines "normal" for itself, and passes it on. Judging others by your own norms is ethnocentrism; understanding them in their own context is cultural relativism.

Mission 2

What is a cultural landscape?

  • A cultural landscape is the visible mark people leave on the land.
  • It combines physical features (hills, rivers) with:
    • farming and industry (fields, factories);
    • religion and language (places of worship, street signs);
    • architecture and land-use patterns.
Mission 2

Layers: sequent occupance

  • Sequent occupance: each group that lives in a place leaves its own layer on the landscape. Geographer Derwent Whittlesey used the term in 1929.
  • Mexico City: the Aztecs built their capital, Tenochtitlan, on a small island.
  • Spanish conquerors built Mexico City "on the ruins of the prehispanic city", starting in 1521.
  • Today the Templo Mayor ruins and the colonial cathedral share the city center.
Mission 2

Land use as culture

  • Land-use patterns can last for centuries.
  • Xochimilco, south of central Mexico City, keeps chinampas: farm plots built up in shallow lake water.
  • UNESCO calls it "the only reminder of traditional Pre-Hispanic land-use in the lagoons of the Mexico City basin".
Quick check
?In a city, a mosque was built on the site of an older church, and a modern museum now stands next to both. Which concept does this show?
Mission 2

Traditional and postmodern architecture

  • Traditional architecture: building styles and materials passed down within a culture.
  • Example: the BAPS Shri Swaminarayan Mandir in Neasden, London (opened August 1995): about 5,000 metric tons of Italian and Indian marble, plus Bulgarian limestone, were hand-carved in India by 1,526 craftsmen, then assembled in London.
  • Postmodern architecture (from about the 1970s): mixes styles and historical details, often playfully, in reaction to plain glass boxes.
  • Most new skyscrapers now rise in China: in 2024, 97 of the 138 buildings 200 meters or taller completed worldwide were there.
Quick check
?An office tower has a glass body, a giant pink stone arch at its base and a top shaped like an old-fashioned clock. What style is this?
Mission 2

Gender and space: women in the workforce

  • Women's labor force participation, ages 15+, 2024: Sweden 64.5%, USA 57.5%, India 34.4%, Saudi Arabia 33.5%.
  • In Saudi Arabia the rate rose from 20.1% in 2017.
  • Laws, family norms and the types of jobs on offer all affect these rates.
  • Where more women work outside the home, this can raise demand for offices, child care and transportation.
Mission 2

Ethnicity and space: neighborhoods

  • Ethnic neighborhoods form where migrants find shared language, food, worship and support.
  • Attitudes and laws shape them. Singapore saw "signs that segregation among the races had once again started to take root", and in 1989 introduced its Ethnic Integration Policy.
  • It caps each group's share of public housing blocks and neighborhoods. In 2010, limits for Indians and other minority groups were raised to 12% (neighborhood) and 15% (block).
Mission 2

Indigenous lands

  • In the USA, about 326 Indian land areas are administered as federal Indian reservations.
  • About 56.2 million acres are held in trust by the United States for tribes and individuals.
  • The largest is the 16-million-acre Navajo Nation Reservation, in Arizona, New Mexico and Utah.
  • Reservations have their own governments, land rules and place names: a distinct cultural landscape.
Quick check
?Which example best shows attitudes toward ethnicity shaping the use of space?
Mission check

?In a town, a Spanish mission church stands on the stone base of an older Indigenous temple. Across the plaza, a 1980s bank has Greek columns made of mirrored glass. What do these features show?

Landscapes are records. Mexico City's cathedral and the Templo Mayor show sequent occupance: Aztec, then Spanish, then modern. Buildings, land use and the way space is shared also reveal beliefs about religion, gender and ethnicity.

Mission 3

Religion has a regional pattern

  • Shares of the world's population, 2020:
    • Christians 28.8%; Muslims 25.6%; religiously unaffiliated 24.2%; Hindus 14.9%; Buddhists 4.1%.
  • Some religions are spread across many regions; others are concentrated in one.
Mission 3

Concentrated or spread out

  • India is home to 95% of the world's Hindus.
  • 99% of Hindus and 98% of Buddhists live in the Asia-Pacific region.
  • Christians and Muslims live in large numbers on several continents.
  • These patterns shape the global cultural landscape: temples, mosques, churches, scripts and festivals mark each region.
Quick check
?Which statement fits the 2020 data best?
Mission 3

Sense of place and placemaking

  • Sense of place: the feelings and meanings people attach to a place.
  • Language, religion and ethnicity feed it: the sound of a language on the street, a call to prayer, a festival.
  • Placemaking: people shaping a public space so it reflects who they are: signs in their language, murals, markets, places of worship.
Mission 3

Forces that unite or divide

  • Centripetal force: anything that unites people and strengthens a state. Examples: a shared language, a shared religion, national symbols.
  • Centrifugal force: anything that divides people and weakens a state. Examples: language divides, religious conflict, ethnic separatism.
  • The same trait can do either, depending on whether it is shared across the country or marks one region off from the rest.
Mission 3

Canada: a language region

  • In 2021, French was the first official language spoken by 21.4% of Canadians.
  • In Quebec, it was 82.2%.
  • So French marks Quebec off as a distinct language region inside Canada.
Mission 3

A pull apart: the referendums

  • Quebec held referendums on sovereignty in 1980 and 1995.
  • 1980: No 59.56%.
  • 1995: No 50.58%, Yes 49.42% (calculated: 2,308,360 Yes votes ÷ 4,671,008 valid ballots); turnout 93.52%.
  • A distinct regional language can feed a separate national identity: a centrifugal force.
Mission 3

A pull together: two official languages

  • Canada's Official Languages Act gives English and French "equality of status" in federal institutions.
  • Everyone may use either language "in any debates and other proceedings of Parliament".
  • Treating both languages equally in shared institutions aims to make them a centripetal force.
Quick check
?Which is a centripetal force?
Quick check
?In 1995, Quebec's referendum on sovereignty failed by a narrow margin. What does the closeness of the result suggest?
Mission check

?Country Z has one religion shared by almost everyone. Its north speaks Language A; its south speaks Language B. Northern leaders call for a separate state. Which statement is best?

A shared language can hold a country together; a language that marks one region off can pull it apart. In Quebec, a French-speaking region, sovereignty lost by 50.58% to 49.42% in 1995, and Canada uses two official languages to bind both together.

Mission 4: By 1980, new high-yielding seeds grew on about 40% of Asia's cereal land; by 2000, about 80%. Why did they spread fast in some places and slowly in others?

Make a guess: By 1980, new high-yielding seeds grew on about 40% of Asia's cereal land; by 2000, about 80%. Why did they spread fast in some places and slowly in others?

Course framework: what this mission covers

AP Human Geography

  • IMP-3.A define the types of diffusion
  • IMP-3.A.1 relocation and expansion (contagious, hierarchical, stimulus) diffusion
Mission 4

Diffusion

  • Diffusion: the spread of an idea, innovation or trait from its place of origin, the hearth, to other places over time.
  • Two main types:
    • Relocation diffusion: people move and take the trait with them.
    • Expansion diffusion: the trait spreads outward while staying strong in the hearth.
Mission 4

Relocation: a gene's journey

  • Japanese breeders developed Norin 10, a short-stemmed wheat.
  • A US Department of Agriculture breeder took samples to the United States, where they were used in breeding in the late 1940s.
  • From 1953, Norman Borlaug crossed these semi-dwarf wheats with Mexican varieties in Mexico.
  • Each step: people carried the seed to a new place.
Mission 4

From the top down

  • In 1967, Pakistan imported about 42,000 tons of semi-dwarf wheat seed from Mexico; India imported 18,000 tons.
  • CIMMYT: "At the time this was the largest seed purchase in the history of agriculture".
  • Governments bought the seed and passed it down to farmers: hierarchical diffusion, from the top of the system downward.
  • No one migrated with this seed, so it is not relocation diffusion.
Mission 4

Expansion: contagious and hierarchical

  • Contagious diffusion: spreads to nearby people and places, like a ripple. For example: farmers see a neighbor's bigger harvest and plant the same seed.
  • Hierarchical diffusion: spreads from the most important or connected people and places downward. For example: a new seed goes first to research stations and larger, better-funded farms, then to smaller farms.
  • In Asia, governments built input and credit systems to spread the new seeds.
Quick check
?A new phone app is used first by people in large cities, then in mid-sized towns, and only later in small villages. Which type of diffusion is this?
Mission 4

Stimulus diffusion

  • Stimulus diffusion: the underlying idea spreads, but the original trait is rejected or reworked to fit the new place.
  • KFC opened its first restaurant in China in Beijing in 1987.
  • The fast-food chain idea spread, but much of the menu was reworked around local foods: "rice dishes, congees" (rice porridge), "fresh vegetables" and tea.
Quick check
?Which is the best example of stimulus diffusion?
Mission 4

Asia's Green Revolution

  • High-yielding modern varieties (MVs) of wheat and rice spread across Asia from the late 1960s.
  • About 40% of Asia's cereal area grew MVs by 1980, and about 80% by 2000.
  • Asia's cereal production rose from 313 to 650 million metric tons a year between 1970 and 1995.
Mission 4

Why it spread fast in some places

  • The new varieties "worked best for wheat and rice in the best-irrigated areas".
  • Governments subsidized "fertilizer, power, and water" and built credit systems: a push from the top of the hierarchy.
  • Where water, credit and support were in place, seeds spread fast from farm to farm (contagious).
Mission 4

Why it spread slowly in others

  • Physical barriers: rain-fed and drought-prone areas gained less; varieties suited to poorer areas came only "within 10 to 15 years".
  • Economic barriers: seed, fertilizer and water cost money, so credit decided who could adopt.
  • Political barriers: governments chose where to build input and credit systems.
  • Exam move: when explaining a diffusion pattern, name the type of diffusion and the barrier that slowed it.
Mission check

?A new drought-tolerant maize seed is released in a country. Five years later, it is common in irrigated areas near the capital with good bank credit, but rare in remote dry areas. What best explains the pattern?

Diffusion is rarely even. The Green Revolution seeds spread fastest where irrigation, credit and government support existed, and slowest where physical and economic barriers stood in the way.

Mission 5

Three historical forces

  • Colonialism: a state settles and rules territory overseas.
  • Imperialism: a state extends its power and control over other places, by rule, trade or influence.
  • Trade: long-distance exchange that brings peoples into regular contact.
  • All three moved languages, religions and customs across the world. Their traces are today's cultural patterns.
Mission 5

Colonial languages became official

  • Brazil, a former Portuguese colony: "Portuguese is the official language of the Federative Republic of Brazil."
  • Haiti, a former French colony: Creole and French are official.
  • Philippines: "under Spanish rule for 333 years", then under US control; it has English as an official language.
Mission 5

Religion followed rule

  • Philippines: Spain colonized the islands in the 16th century and ruled them for 333 years.
  • Today it is "one of only two predominantly Roman Catholic countries in Asia".
  • Pattern: colonizers often spread their religion and language along with their rule.
Quick check
?Why is Portuguese the official language of Brazil, far from Portugal?
Mission 5

Lingua franca

  • Lingua franca: a language "used as a means of communication between populations speaking vernaculars that are not mutually intelligible".
  • Trade origin: the original Lingua Franca was a jargon "developed by Crusaders and traders in the eastern Mediterranean".
  • Colonial use: "both the Dutch and the British used Malay for communication" with peoples of Southeast Asia.
  • Today: "international air traffic control uses English as a common language".
Mission 5

Swahili: a trade language

  • A Bantu language of the East African coast, with "an enormous number of Arabic loanwords".
  • It "dates from the contacts of Arabian traders with the inhabitants of the east coast of Africa".
  • Now a lingua franca in Tanzania, Kenya, the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Uganda.
  • Global force (trade) + local culture = a new form of expression.
Quick check
?A Kenyan trader, whose first language is Luo, sells goods to a Ugandan buyer, whose first language is Luganda. They bargain in Swahili. What is Swahili doing here?
Mission 5

From pidgin to creole

  • Pidgin: a simple contact language that grew from "sporadic and limited contacts between Europeans and non-Europeans", often through trade, plantations or mining. It has no native speakers.
  • Creole: one view is that creoles "are erstwhile pidgins that were nativized and expanded by children".
  • Creoles "developed in colonial European plantation settlements in the 17th and 18th centuries".
  • Creolization: the process that blends languages (or other culture traits) into a new one.
Mission 5

Haitian Creole

  • A French-based language that developed "in the late 17th and early 18th centuries".
  • "It developed primarily on the sugarcane plantations of Haiti from contacts between French colonists and African slaves."
  • It shows strong influence from African languages.
  • Official since 1987; first language of "about 95 percent of Haitians".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly separates a pidgin from a creole?
Mission 5

The pattern today

  • Official languages of many former colonies are the colonizer's language.
  • Lingua francas grew where traders and rulers needed a shared language.
  • Creoles grew where colonialism forced peoples together, as on plantations.
  • Tok Pisin in Papua New Guinea, mostly English vocabulary, is now spoken as an everyday language.
  • Exam move: to "explain", name the process (colonialism, trade), then the result (official language, lingua franca, creole) and link them with "because".
Mission check

?On a hypothetical island, a colonial power set up sugar estates and brought in workers who spoke many different languages. They communicated in a simple mix based on the colonizers' words. Their children grew up speaking that mix as their first language, with new grammar of its own. Which term best describes what happened?

Colonialism and trade forced peoples into contact. Shared contact languages served as lingua francas, and where children made them native, creoles formed, as in Haiti.

Mission 6

Culture is socially constructed

  • Socially constructed: cultural ideas and practices are made and remade by people; they are not fixed or natural.
  • They change through small-scale processes (a family moving to a city) and large-scale ones (urbanization, globalization).
  • These work through media, technology, politics, economics and social relationships.
Mission 6

Globalization and urbanization reshape culture

  • Globalization: growing links between places through trade, migration, media and the internet.
  • Urbanization: people moving into cities, where they meet new languages, foods and ideas.
  • Example: in 2021, 40.6% of London's residents were born outside the UK, up from 36.7% in 2011.
Mission 6

Time-space convergence

  • Time-space convergence: places become "closer" in time because travel and communication get faster.
  • In 2024, 5.5 billion people, 68% of the world's population, were online.
  • But 2.6 billion were still offline; 93% of people in high-income countries used the internet, against 27% in low-income countries.
Quick check
?Which is the best example of time-space convergence?
Mission 6

More English

  • Among websites whose content language is known, 49.5% use English; the next, Spanish, is 6.0%.
  • English spreads through the internet, business, science and entertainment: a driver of cultural convergence.
  • Cultural convergence: cultures becoming more alike as they interact.
Mission 6

Fewer languages

  • Of about 7,000 documented languages, "nearly half are considered endangered".
  • In one database of 6,511 spoken languages, 37% were threatened or worse.
  • Without action, "at least 1,500 languages" could stop being spoken by 2100.
Mission 6

Why indigenous languages are lost

  • The same study found that "greater road density, which may encourage population movement, is associated with increased endangerment".
  • Contact with other languages was not a driver of loss in the study.
  • More years of schooling was also linked to endangerment: "formal education can contribute to loss of language diversity".
  • UNESCO: 40% of the world's people lack education in a language they speak or understand.
Quick check
?Which finding links language loss to faster links between places?
Mission 6

Convergence: one culture spreading

  • US films took 63% of cinema admissions in Europe in 2024 (69% in 2023).
  • Global media and platforms carry the same songs, shows and brands to many countries.
  • Convergence is not only American: Netflix's most-watched non-English series, South Korea's Squid Game (season 1), had 265.2 million views in its first 91 days. One show, watched worldwide.
Mission 6

Divergence: cultures staying or becoming distinct

  • Cultural divergence: cultures becoming more different, or holding on to what makes them distinct.
  • In Europe, national films' share of admissions rose in 22 of 32 markets with data in 2024.
Quick check
?Which evidence best shows cultural divergence rather than convergence?
Mission check

?In a made-up country, teenagers stream the same global hits and post online in English, a local film industry booms, and two village languages lose their last young speakers. Which labeling is correct?

The country is becoming both more alike and more diverse. Globally, between 37% and nearly half of languages are endangered, and English dominates online; locally, places such as London (40.6% born abroad) are more diverse than ever, and national films gained ground in Europe in 2024. Time-space convergence speeds up both processes.

Mission 7

Cultural hearths

  • A cultural hearth: the place where a cultural trait begins.
  • From hearths, traits diffuse (spread): language families, languages, dialects, religions, ethnic cultures and gender roles.
  • Relocation diffusion: people move and take the trait with them.
  • Expansion diffusion: the trait spreads outward while staying strong at the hearth.
Mission 7

From family to dialect

  • Language family: languages descended from one common ancestor.
  • Branch: a large group within a family (e.g., Germanic, Romance).
  • Language: a system of communication with its own words and grammar (e.g., English, Spanish).
  • Dialect: a regional version of one language, with its own words, accent or grammar.
Mission 7

The Indo-European family

  • Languages of the Indo-European family "are spoken by almost half of the world's population".
  • Branches include:
    • Germanic: English, German, Dutch
    • Romance: Spanish, Portuguese, French, Italian
    • Indo-Iranian: Hindi, Bengali, Persian
    • Balto-Slavic: Russian, Polish
    • Celtic: Irish, Welsh
  • All descend from one ancestral language, Proto-Indo-European.
Mission 7

Where was the hearth?

Two main hypotheses:

  • Anatolian (farming): spread with agriculture out of the Fertile Crescent area, beginning about 9,500–8,500 years ago.
  • Steppe: spread from the grasslands north of the Black and Caspian Seas, no earlier than 6,500 years ago, mostly with horse-based herding from about 5,000 years ago.
Mission 7

What new evidence shows

  • Ancient DNA (2015): about 4,500 years ago, Corded Ware people in Germany traced about 75% of their ancestry to steppe herders: "a massive migration" into Europe. This supports a steppe source for at least some European branches.
  • Language trees (2023): the family's root is about 8,120 years old.
  • Proposed hybrid: a homeland south of the Caucasus, then a branch onto the steppe and into Europe.
  • Either way: relocation diffusion by migrating farmers or herders.
Quick check
?Which hypothesis says Indo-European languages spread with farming, out of the Fertile Crescent area?
Mission 7

Later spread: colonialism

  • From about 1500 CE, European empires carried Spanish, English, Portuguese and French across oceans: relocation diffusion.
  • Today these languages are spoken widely in the Americas, Africa and Oceania, far from their hearths.
  • Example: Spanish missionaries built missions in what is now San Antonio, Texas, in the 18th century, as part of Spain's "efforts to colonize, evangelize and defend the northern frontier of New Spain".
Mission 7

Dialects

  • One language, many regional versions.
  • Examples: British English "lift", American English "elevator"; regional US words for a soft drink ("soda", "pop", "coke").
  • Dialects form where groups are separated by distance, physical barriers or borders, so the language changes differently in each place.
  • A line on a map marking where a word or sound changes is an isogloss.
Quick check
?Speakers in two valleys separated by a high mountain range use different words for the same objects but understand each other easily. What do they speak?
Mission 7

Toponyms: names on the map

  • A toponym: a place name.
  • Toponyms record who named a place, so they show past diffusion even where a language is no longer spoken.
  • Examples: Spanish names across the US Southwest: San Antonio, Los Angeles, Santa Fe, Sierra Nevada.
  • Names such as New England record settlers naming a place after their homeland.
Quick check
?A region where almost nobody speaks Spanish today has many towns named "San..." and "Santa...". What do these toponyms most likely show?
Mission check

?Language family Q (hypothetical) is spoken across one large connected region and also on several distant continents. Its oldest written records come from one valley in the middle of the connected region. Which explanation fits best?

Indo-European spread from one ancient hearth as people migrated, then overseas with colonial settlement. Its languages are now spoken by almost half the world's people.

Mission 8: Why is one major religion found mostly in one country, while another has spread across every continent?

Make a guess: Why is one major religion found mostly in one country, while another has spread across every continent?

Course framework: what this mission covers

AP Human Geography

  • IMP-3.B explain what factors lead to the diffusion of universalizing and ethnic religions
  • IMP-3.B.1 religions (with languages, ethnic cultures, gender roles) diffuse from cultural hearths
  • IMP-3.B.3 religions have distinct places of origin and diffused by different processes; practices and belief systems affected how widely they spread
  • IMP-3.B.4 universalizing religions (Christianity, Islam, Buddhism, Sikhism) spread by expansion and relocation diffusion
  • IMP-3.B.5 ethnic religions (Hinduism, Judaism) are generally found near the hearth or spread by relocation diffusion
Mission 8

Two kinds of religion

  • Universalizing religion: tries to appeal to all people, anywhere; welcomes and often seeks converts. AP examples: Christianity, Islam, Buddhism, Sikhism.
  • Ethnic religion: closely tied to one people and often one place; rarely seeks converts; people are usually born into it. AP examples: Hinduism, Judaism.
  • These beliefs and practices affect how far a religion spreads.
Mission 8

Hearths of universalizing religions

  • Christianity: from the life and teachings of Jesus of Nazareth, 1st century CE, in the eastern Mediterranean.
  • Islam: Muhammad, born c. 570 in Mecca, died 632 in Medina, Arabian Peninsula.
  • Buddhism: Prince Siddhartha "attained Enlightenment and became Buddha" at Bodh Gaya, India.
  • Sikhism: founded by Guru Nanak (1469–1539) in the Punjab region, late 15th century.
Mission 8

Hearths of ethnic religions

  • Hinduism: South Asia (the Indian subcontinent); no single founder or date of origin.
  • Judaism: the eastern Mediterranean (the Levant).
  • Both have stayed strongly linked to their hearths: their holy places are there.
Quick check
?Which pair matches the AP categories?
Mission 8

Expansion diffusion

  • Hierarchical: spreads from powerful people downward. Example: Emperor Asoka built the first temple at Bodh Gaya in the 3rd century BCE; rulers who adopted Buddhism helped spread it.
  • Contagious: spreads person to person, through trade routes, missionaries and preaching.
  • Christianity is now "the most widely diffused of all faiths".
Mission 8

Relocation diffusion of universalizing religions

  • People move and take their religion with them.
  • Christianity: Spanish Franciscan missionaries built missions in what is now Texas in the 18th century.
  • Sikhism: with migration, the share of Canadians who are Sikh rose from 0.9% in 2001 to 2.1% in 2021.
Quick check
?A king adopts a new religion; his officials follow, and then it spreads to ordinary people across his kingdom. What type of diffusion is this?
Mission 8

Ethnic religions stay close to home

  • Hinduism: India is home to 95% of the world's Hindus; 99% of Hindus live in the Asia-Pacific region.
  • It rarely seeks converts, so it has grown mainly where it began.
  • Hindu temples, festivals and rivers sacred to Hindus are concentrated in South Asia.
Mission 8

Ethnic religions by relocation

  • Judaism: about 0.2% of the world's people are Jewish.
  • In 2020, 45.9% of Jews lived in the Middle East–North Africa region and 41.2% in North America; Israel is home to 46% of all Jews.
  • This pattern comes from relocation: Jewish communities moved over many centuries, and many settled in North America.
  • Hinduism too: the Hindu share of Canada rose from 1.0% (2001) to 2.3% (2021).
Quick check
?Most of the world's Jews live in two regions: around the hearth and in North America. Which process best explains the North American share?
Mission 8

The result today

  • Universalizing religions are now spread far from their hearths: 30.7% of Christians live in sub-Saharan Africa and 22.3% in Europe.
  • 59% of Muslims live in the Asia-Pacific region, far east of Mecca.
  • Ethnic religions remain concentrated near their hearths, or in places their people moved to.
Mission check

?Religion Z (hypothetical) began 300 years ago in one mountain valley. Its followers send teachers to every continent and welcome anyone who wishes to join. Religion Y began in the same valley; one is born into it, and it does not seek converts. Which pattern is most likely 300 years later?

Universalizing religions seek converts, so they spread by expansion and relocation far from their hearths. Ethnic religions stay near their hearths, as Hinduism does in India, or spread when their people move, as Judaism did.

Make a guess: There are more than three times as many KFC restaurants in China as in the USA. Why does KFC in China sell rice porridge?

Course framework: what this mission covers

AP Human Geography

  • SPS-3.B explain how diffusion results in changes to the cultural landscape
  • SPS-3.B.1 acculturation, assimilation, syncretism and multiculturalism are effects of the diffusion of culture
Mission 9

Diffusion changes cultures

  • Cultural diffusion: the spread of cultural traits (foods, languages, beliefs, building styles) from place to place.
  • When cultures meet, four main effects can follow: acculturation, assimilation, syncretism and multiculturalism.
  • Each leaves marks on the cultural landscape: the visible imprint of culture on a place.
Mission 9

Acculturation and assimilation

  • Acculturation: a group adopts some traits of another culture but keeps its own. Example: eating at a fried-chicken chain while still cooking family dishes at home.
  • Assimilation: a group takes on another culture so fully that its original traits fade. Example: grandchildren of immigrants who speak only the new country's language.
  • Acculturation is partial; assimilation is near-complete.
Quick check
?A family moves to a new country. The parents learn the new language at work but speak their first language at home and cook its dishes. Which effect is this?
Mission 9

KFC in China

  • KFC opened its first restaurant in China in Beijing in 1987.
  • By December 31, 2025 there were 12,997 KFC restaurants in over 2,500 cities across China.
  • The menu includes "beef burgers, pork, seafood, rice dishes, congees, fresh vegetables, desserts, coffee, tea" alongside chicken.
Mission 9

Who adapts to whom?

  • KFC in mainland China is run by Yum China, a separate company with its principal offices in Shanghai and Plano, Texas. It pays Yum! Brands 3% of system sales.
  • The brand adapts to local tastes (congee, tea), and Chinese customers adopt an American fast-food trait.
  • Customers adopting fried chicken while keeping their own food is acculturation; where a menu blends the two cuisines into new items, that is syncretism (next screen).
  • Earlier in this lesson the same menu was an example of stimulus diffusion. That term names how the idea spread; these terms name its effect on culture.
Mission 9

Syncretism

  • Syncretism: traits from different cultures blend into something new.
  • Mexico's Day of the Dead, on UNESCO's heritage list: "The fusion of pre-Hispanic religious rites and Catholic feasts brings together two universes".
  • The blend is visible in the landscape: home altars and decorated graves.
Quick check
?Which is the clearest example of syncretism?
Mission 9

Multiculturalism

  • Multiculturalism: many cultures coexist in one society, each keeping its traits.
  • Some states make it official. Canada's Multiculturalism Act (1988) declares a policy to "recognize and promote the understanding that multiculturalism reflects the cultural and racial diversity of Canadian society".
  • It acknowledges "the freedom of all members of Canadian society to preserve, enhance and share their cultural heritage".
Mission 9

A multicultural landscape: Neasden, London

  • The BAPS Shri Swaminarayan Mandir in Neasden, London, was inaugurated in August 1995.
  • About 5,000 metric tons of Italian and Indian marble and Bulgarian limestone were "hand-carved in India" by 1,526 craftsmen, then assembled in London.
  • Diffusion of people and skills put a traditional Hindu temple into a London suburb: the landscape now shows multiculturalism.
Quick check
?A city has Hindu temples, mosques, churches and synagogues, each serving its own community. Which effect does this landscape show?
Mission check

?A made-up global coffee chain opens in Japan and sells matcha (green tea) lattes beside its espresso. Why, and what effect does this show?

KFC in China sells congee because a global brand adapts to local tastes to win customers. Its menu mixes American and Chinese food, and where the two blend into something new that is syncretism, while customers adopt some American traits but keep their own (acculturation).

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Culture = shared practices, technologies, attitudes and behaviors, transmitted by a society; culture traits include food preferences (Washoku), architecture (Fujian tulou) and land use (Ifugao terraces).
  • Ethnocentrism judges others by your own culture's standards; cultural relativism seeks to understand a culture on its own terms.

Mission 2

  • A cultural landscape combines physical features, farming, industry, religion, language, architecture and land use; sequent occupance adds layers over time.
  • Attitudes to gender (women's work), ethnicity (neighborhoods, Singapore's quotas) and indigenous lands (reservations) shape how space is used.

Mission 3

  • Language, religion and ethnicity form regional patterns that give places a sense of place and drive placemaking.
  • They are centripetal when shared and centrifugal when they mark one region off from the rest.

Mission 4

  • Relocation diffusion: people carry a trait to a new place (Norin 10, Japan → USA). Government seed imports to South Asia then spread it hierarchically. Expansion diffusion: contagious (nearby), hierarchical (from important places or people down), stimulus (idea spreads, trait changes, as with KFC's menu in China).
  • Barriers (physical, economic, political) explain why diffusion is fast in some places and slow in others.

Mission 5

  • Colonialism, imperialism and trade spread languages and religions: Portuguese in Brazil, Catholicism in the Philippines.
  • New forms of expression arose: lingua francas (Swahili, English) and creoles (Haitian Creole), through creolization.

Mission 6

  • Culture is socially constructed and changes through urbanization, globalization and communication technology.
  • Time-space convergence spreads English and threatens indigenous languages (convergence), while local cultures also push back (divergence); cultural flows now run in many directions, not only from the USA.

Mission 7

  • Languages diffuse from cultural hearths: family → branch → language → dialect. Indo-European spread by migration (Anatolian vs Steppe hypotheses; possibly a hybrid) and later by colonialism.
  • Maps, charts (family trees) and toponyms show this diffusion.

Mission 8

  • Universalizing (Christianity, Islam, Buddhism, Sikhism): seek converts; spread by expansion (hierarchical, contagious) and relocation.
  • Ethnic (Hinduism, Judaism): tied to one people and place; found near the hearth or spread by relocation.

Mission 9

  • Diffusion can lead to acculturation (adopt some, keep own), assimilation (original traits fade), syncretism (blend into something new) or multiculturalism (cultures coexist).
  • Each effect leaves visible marks on the cultural landscape.
