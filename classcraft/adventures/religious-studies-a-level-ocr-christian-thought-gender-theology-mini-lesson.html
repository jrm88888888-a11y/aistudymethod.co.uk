Mission 1

Human words for God

  • Anthropomorphic language: describing God in human terms (Father, King, Judge, "the hand of God").
  • Christians believe God is spirit (John 4:24) and beyond all creatures, so no human word fits God exactly.
  • Classical theology treats such words as analogy (Thomas Aquinas) or metaphor: true in one way, not in another.
  • Mainstream teaching: God has no sex. Catholic teaching: God "is neither man nor woman: he is God" (Catechism of the Catholic Church 239).
  • Yet Christian worship has used mostly male words: Father, King, Lord, "he".
  • The question: if the words are not literal, do they still shape how people see God, and men and women?
Mission 1

Daly: "if God is male, then the male is God"

  • Mary Daly (1928–2010), American feminist philosopher and theologian; Beyond God the Father (1973).
  • Her claim: "if God is male, then the male is God" (Beyond God the Father, ch. 1).
  • A male image of God, she argues, makes men seem god-like and legitimates patriarchy: male rule in Church, family and society.
  • The symbol of "God the Father" is not harmless picture-language: it shapes power.
  • The implications Daly draws (the "unholy trinity", spirituality through nature) and the comparison with Ruether.

Mission 2

What is feminist theology?

  • Patriarchy: a social system in which men hold power and male experience is treated as the norm.
  • Feminist theology asks how patriarchy has shaped Christian scripture, doctrine, worship and leadership, and what should follow.
  • Context: first-wave feminism (votes for women; Elizabeth Cady Stanton, The Woman's Bible, 1895–98); second-wave feminism of the 1960s–70s.
  • Key themes: women's experience as a source for theology; a hermeneutic of suspicion (reading texts alert to male bias: Elisabeth Schüssler Fiorenza, In Memory of Her, 1983); language for God (McFague).
  • Two paths: reform Christianity from within (Ruether, McFague) or leave it as beyond repair.
Mission 2

Rosemary Radford Ruether

  • Rosemary Radford Ruether (1936–2022), American Catholic theologian; Sexism and God-Talk (1983).
  • Her critical principle: whatever denies or diminishes "the full humanity of women" is not redemptive; whatever promotes it reflects the divine (paraphrase, ch. 1).
  • She is a reformist: she stays within Christianity and recovers its prophetic-liberating tradition, in which God sides with the oppressed.
  • Chapter 5 asks the question of this mission: "Can a Male Saviour Save Women?"
  • Your spec lists chapter 9 ("The New Earth") as its reading; the male-saviour question itself is in chapter 5.
Mission 2

The problem of a male Christ

  • Over centuries, Christ's maleness was used to rank men above women.
  • Aquinas, following Aristotle, wrote that as an individual, "woman is defective and misbegotten" (Summa Theologica I q.92 a.1 ad 1), while insisting that woman is part of God's plan for human nature.
  • Christ's maleness has been used to argue that only men can represent Christ as priests.
  • An early Christian principle: what Christ did not take on, he did not heal (Gregory of Nazianzus, 4th century). If Christ's maleness is what saves, women seem left out.
  • Ruether: Christ's maleness has no ultimate significance; it matters only as a social symbol in patriarchal societies.
Mission 2

Jesus against the warrior-messiah

  • Many Jews of Jesus' time hoped for a Messiah like King David: a warrior who would defeat Israel's enemies and rule.
  • Ruether: Jesus rejected that model of power. He came as a servant, not a ruler:

> "whoever wants to become great amongst you shall be your servant." (Mark 10:43, WEBBE)

  • He sided with the marginalised: the poor, outcasts, women. Women followed him and were first witnesses of the resurrection (Luke 24:9–10).
  • So Jesus' life announces the "kenosis of patriarchy" (Ruether's phrase): the self-emptying of male power.
  • His maleness, read this way, is a critique of patriarchy, not a support for it.
Mission 2Read the source

Wisdom, a female figure

> "Doesn't wisdom cry out? Doesn't understanding raise her voice?" (Proverbs 8:1, WEBBE)

> "For she is a reflection of everlasting light, an unspotted mirror of the working of God, and an image of his goodness." (Wisdom 7:26, WEBBE)

  • In Hebrew, wisdom is hokmah; in Greek, sophia. Both are feminine, and the texts personify Wisdom as a woman who is with God at creation (Proverbs 8:22–31).
  • Ruether: Wisdom is a female principle in God; early Christians called Christ "the wisdom of God" (1 Corinthians 1:24). John's Logos (Word) took over her role.

Show answer

She reflects God's light and goodness; she is present at creation.

Mission 2

The androgynous Christ and the Christlike female

  • Androgynous Christ: Christ unites "masculine" and "feminine" qualities (e.g. Julian of Norwich, Revelations of Divine Love, c. 1395, calls Jesus "our Mother").
  • Ruether values the aim but warns that many androgynous pictures still treat the male as the norm and the "feminine" as a mere side of him.
  • Her own answer: Christ is not confined to the historical Jesus. The Christian community continues Christ's identity, so Christ can be met "in the form of our sister" (Ruether's phrase).
  • The Christlike female: Jesus came to the last and least. Women, among the most marginalised, can show the liberated humanity Christ brings in a fuller way than men who keep power: the last become first (Matthew 20:16).
Mission 2Diversity

Responses to Ruether

  • Catholic and Orthodox teaching: Christ saves all humans because he took on human nature; maleness is not what saves. But his maleness still matters as a sign, e.g. for priesthood.
  • Many evangelicals: Christ is defined by scripture and the creeds; the historical Jesus cannot be replaced by a community's Christ.
  • Other feminists: Elizabeth Johnson, She Who Is (1992), develops a fuller Sophia (Wisdom) Christology.
  • Daphne Hampson: Ruether picks only the parts she likes; a Christianity tied to a male past cannot be made feminist (see Mission 3).

Mission 2Evaluate

Can a male saviour save women?

  • Claim: A male saviour can save women.
  • For: The creeds stress that Christ became human (incarnation), not male; Galatians 3:28: in Christ "neither male nor female".
  • For: Ruether: Jesus' life overturns patriarchal power.
  • For: Ruether: Christ as Wisdom is not only male.
  • Against: Christ's maleness has been used for centuries to exclude women; symbols shape power (Daly).
  • Against: Hampson: the uniqueness of one historical man makes the male normative for ever.
  • Judgement criteria: What saves: Christ's humanity or his maleness? Can tradition be reinterpreted this far? What effects do the symbols have on real women?
  • Spec issues: "whether or not a male saviour can save women"; "whether or not Christianity is essentially sexist".

Mission 3

Reform or leave?

  • All feminist theologians agree that Christianity has been shaped by patriarchy.
  • Reformists (Ruether, McFague, Johnson): the core of Christianity is liberating; remove the sexism and stay.
  • Post-Christian feminists (Daly, Hampson): the sexism is built in; Christianity cannot be repaired, so they left.
  • Post-Christian does not mean atheist: both kept a spirituality; Hampson still believes in God.
  • The question: is patriarchy a stain on Christianity, or part of its structure?
Mission 3

Mary Daly

  • Mary Daly (1928–2010), American Catholic philosopher and theologian; taught at Boston College (a Jesuit university).
  • The Church and the Second Sex (1968): a reformist call for change.
  • Beyond God the Father (1973): now post-Christian: "if God is male, then the male is God" (see Mission 1).
  • The male symbol of God sanctions male rule in church, family and society.
  • God is not a noun but a verb: "Be-ing", the power of becoming in which women share.
  • Later: Gyn/Ecology (1978) and Pure Lust (1984).
Mission 3

The "unholy trinity"

  • Beyond God the Father, ch. 4: patriarchy produces a "phallic morality" of domination.
  • Its fruits Daly calls an unholy trinity: rape, genocide and war.
  • She sets it against the Christian Trinity: Father, Son and a Spirit often imagined as male, a sacred image of male power.
  • Her argument (paraphrase): a religion whose highest symbols are male trains societies to accept male violence and to blame women (the scapegoating of Eve).
  • So the problem is not a few sexist verses: it runs through the symbols themselves.
Mission 3

Spirituality through nature

  • Daly calls women to leave patriarchal religion and find a spirituality of "Be-ing".
  • Beyond God the Father, ch. 6: sisterhood as a "cosmic covenant": harmony with all life and the natural world.
  • Gyn/Ecology: patriarchy's domination of women and its domination of nature are linked; liberation of both belongs together (a root of ecofeminism).
  • Spirituality is found in women's experience, community and nature, not in church, creed or male priesthood.
  • Spec issue: "whether or not only women can develop a genuine spirituality".

Mission 3

Daphne Hampson

  • Daphne Hampson (b. 1944), British theologian (St Andrews); Theology and Feminism (1990); After Christianity (1996). Once active in the campaign for women priests.
  • Christianity is "irredeemably sexist" because it is a historical religion: it claims a unique revelation of God in a particular past.
  • That past was patriarchal: a male Jesus, God as Father and Son, male apostles. Christians must always look back to it, so the bias can never be removed.
  • Not moral: feminism needs full equality now. Not true: after the Enlightenment, she argues, a unique divine act in one time and place is not credible.
  • She is a post-Christian theist: she believes in God, known through human experience, but not in Christ's uniqueness.
Mission 3Read the source

"Neither male nor female"

> "There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free man, there is neither male nor female; for you are all one in Christ Jesus." (Galatians 3:28, WEBBE)

  • Reformists (Ruether): this is the liberating core of the Gospel; later sexism betrays it.
  • Hampson: one verse, written by a man in a patriarchal world, cannot outweigh the structure of a religion centred on a male saviour and a Father God.

Mission 3Diversity

Ruether, Daly and Hampson compared

  • Is Christianity patriarchal? All three: yes, deeply.
  • Can it be reformed? Ruether: yes, from its prophetic core. Daly and Hampson: no.
  • What about Jesus? Ruether: he empties male power. Daly: Christ-worship sanctifies maleness. Hampson: his uniqueness makes the male normative.
  • God? Ruether: God/ess, the primal Matrix (her terms). Daly: God as Verb, Be-ing. Hampson: God known in experience, beyond any tradition.
  • Where do they go? Ruether stays in the Catholic Church and helps shape the Women-Church movement. Daly and Hampson leave.
  • Your spec: compare Ruether's and Daly's feminist theologies.
Mission 3Evaluate

Can Christianity be changed, or should it be abandoned?

  • Claim: Christianity is essentially sexist and should be abandoned.
  • For: Hampson: a historical religion is tied to a patriarchal past; Daly: Christ-worship sanctifies maleness.
  • For: Centuries of reform have left many churches still male-led.
  • Against: Ruether: the prophetic tradition and Jesus' practice contain the critique of patriarchy; Galatians 3:28.
  • Against: Real change has happened: women priests and bishops; inclusive language; female images of God (see Mission 1).
  • Against (traditional Christians): God has no sex; equal dignity with different roles is not sexism.
  • Judgement criteria: Is sexism the structure or a distortion? Is a religion defined by its origins or by how it develops? Does reform answer Hampson's point about the past?
  • Spec issues: "whether Christianity can be changed or should be abandoned"; "whether or not Christianity is essentially sexist".
Mission 3

Impact on believers and communities

  • Language: inclusive-language Bibles, hymns and liturgies (e.g. "humankind" for "mankind").
  • Ministry: feminist theology fed the case for ordaining women.
  • Communities: Women-Church groups and women's liturgies; feminist biblical scholarship taught in many seminaries.
  • Limits: the Catholic and Orthodox Churches keep an all-male priesthood; many Christians reject feminist theology as shaped by secular ideology.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Words like Father and King are anthropomorphic: analogies or metaphors. Mainstream Christian teaching holds that God has no sex.
  • Daly (Beyond God the Father, 1973): "if God is male, then the male is God".

Mission 2

  • Feminist theology examines patriarchy in Christianity; reformists stay to change it, post-Christians leave.
  • Ruether (Sexism and God-Talk, 1983): Jesus rejected the warrior-messiah; his maleness has no ultimate significance; Christ can be met in women.
  • Ruether: Christ is Wisdom (Sophia), a female figure in the Bible.

Mission 3

  • Daly: worshipping Christ sanctifies maleness, so she left Christianity.
  • Daly (Beyond God the Father, 1973): if God is male, the male is God; God as Be-ing.
  • Daly: patriarchy's unholy trinity of rape, genocide and war; spirituality in harmony with nature.
  • Hampson (Theology and Feminism, 1990): Christianity is irredeemably sexist because it is tied to a unique, patriarchal past; she remains a theist.
  • Ruether reforms from within; Daly and Hampson leave.
