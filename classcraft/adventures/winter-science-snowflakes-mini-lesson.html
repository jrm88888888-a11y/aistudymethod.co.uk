Why Every Snowflake Has Six Sides

3 missions and a finale. Six sides, floating ice and the hunt for two matching snowflakes. Guess, learn, then check.

Mission 1

Mission 1: Why does every snowflake have six sides?

Make a guess: Why does every snowflake have six sides?

Mission 1

One molecule of water

  • A water molecule is H₂O: one oxygen atom joined to two hydrogen atoms.
  • Each join is a covalent bond: a shared pair of electrons. Covalent bonds are strong.
  • The molecule is bent. The two bonds meet at about 104.5°.
  • It is not a hexagon. So where does six come from?
Mission 1

Two kinds of attraction

  • Inside a molecule: strong covalent bonds hold H to O.
  • Between molecules: much weaker attractions, called intermolecular forces.
  • Water's are strong for intermolecular forces, but still far weaker than a covalent bond. A-level students will call them hydrogen bonds.
  • When ice melts, it is these forces between molecules that are overcome. The covalent bonds stay put.
Quick check
?An ice cube melts. What is overcome?
Mission 1

Rings of six

  • In ice, each water molecule is held by four neighbours.
  • The molecules join into rings of six: hexagons.
  • The rings stack into a hexagonal lattice: a repeating, ordered pattern. Scientists call this form of ice ice Ih.
  • William Barnes confirmed this lattice with X-rays in 1929.
Mission 1

From ring to crystal

  • A snow crystal is not a frozen raindrop.
  • Water vapour in a cloud turns straight to ice on a tiny frozen droplet.
  • It first grows as a tiny six-sided prism: a flat plate or a short column, copying the lattice.
  • On a plate, the six corners stick out into the moist air, so they grow fastest and sprout branches.
Mission 1

Kepler asked first

  • In 1611 the astronomer Johannes Kepler wrote a short book, The Six-Cornered Snowflake, as a New Year gift for his emperor.
  • He guessed that the six sides came from the way tiny units stack, like cannonballs.
  • He admitted the science of his day could not answer it.
  • Barnes's X-ray work of 1929 confirmed Kepler was on the right track: stacking water molecules gives the six-fold pattern.
Quick check
?How does a snow crystal form?
Mission check

?Why does every snowflake have six sides?

Molecules in ice form six-sided rings; the crystal copies them.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why does ice float?

Make a guess: Why does ice float?

Mission 2

Density

  • Density = mass ÷ volume. How much stuff is packed into a space.
  • Liquid water: about 1.00 g/cm³. Ice: about 0.92 g/cm³.
  • Freeze 100 g of water and you still have 100 g. But it now takes up about 9% more space.
  • Less dense than the liquid, so it floats.
Mission 2

Lattice versus liquid

  • In the liquid, molecules jostle and slide past each other, packed fairly close.
  • In ice, the forces between molecules lock them into the open hexagonal lattice, with more space between them.
  • Same molecules, more space between them: lower density.
  • The basic particle model misses this: it ignores the forces and the spaces between particles.
Quick check
?100 g of water freezes solid. Which is correct?
Mission 2

The 4 °C twist

  • Cool water and it gets denser, as you would expect, down to about 4 °C.
  • Below 4 °C it gets less dense again as the molecules begin to arrange themselves more openly.
  • At 0 °C it freezes to ice, less dense still.
  • Water is densest at about 4 °C, not at freezing. In most substances the solid is the densest form.
Mission 2

Why lakes freeze from the top

  • In autumn, surface water cools to about 4 °C and sinks. The lake fills with 4 °C water from the bottom up.
  • Colder water is less dense, so it stays on top, and that is where ice forms.
  • Ice floats, so it builds a lid on the surface. Below the lid, the lake stays liquid.
  • If the coldest water and ice sank, lakes could freeze from the bottom up, and some might never fully thaw in summer.
Quick check
?A lake cools in autumn. Which water ends up at the bottom?
Mission check

?Why does ice float?

Ice's open lattice leaves more space between molecules.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Are two snowflakes ever the same?

Make a guess: Are two snowflakes ever the same?

Mission 3

Shape depends on the weather

  • In the 1930s physicist Ukichiro Nakaya grew the first artificial snow crystals, in a cold lab at Hokkaido University.
  • He found that the shape depends on temperature and humidity in the cloud.
  • Near −2 °C: thin plates. Near −5 °C: columns and needles. Near −15 °C: large branched stars. Around −30 °C: plates and columns again.
  • More humidity: more branching. Low humidity: simple prisms.
Mission 3

Reading a snowflake

  • A slender needle says: I grew near −5 °C in high humidity.
  • A big branched star says: I grew near −15 °C.
  • A capped column (a column with a plate on each end) says: I started near −5 °C, then moved into air near −15 °C.
  • Nakaya compared snowflakes to messages sent down from the clouds.
Quick check
?You find a capped column: a short column with a flat plate on each end. What does it tell you?
Mission 3

A crystal's life story

  • As a crystal falls, it meets ever-changing temperature and humidity.
  • Each change makes the arms grow a little differently. The final shape records the exact path it took.
  • All six arms took the same path at the same times, so they grow alike: symmetry.
  • No two crystals follow the same path, so no two complex crystals look alike.
Mission 3

Bentley's claim, and how to test it

  • Wilson Bentley, a Vermont farmer, photographed his first snow crystal in 1885, then more than 5,000 others. He never found two the same.
  • "No two alike" is a claim about every snowflake ever. 5,000 photos can support it but never prove it.
  • One matching pair would disprove it. Tiny, simple plates can look identical under a microscope.
  • Bentley also chose the most photogenic crystals, so his album is not a fair sample of falling snow.
Quick check
?Bentley photographed more than 5,000 snow crystals and found no two alike. What does that show?
Mission 3

Twins in the lab

  • Libbrecht (Caltech) grew two crystals side by side in the same changing conditions.
  • They came out nearly identical: same conditions, same shape.
  • So the shape is set by growth conditions, not by chance or magic.
  • Nature almost never gives two crystals exactly the same journey. That is the real reason.
Mission check

?Are two snowflakes ever the same?

Complex ones almost never; tiny simple ones can look alike.

Finale

Snow crystal or snowflake?

  • A snow crystal is a single crystal of ice, with the six-fold pattern.
  • A snowflake can be one crystal, or a clump of hundreds that collided in the air.
  • Those big fluffy flakes in a mild snowfall are clumps.
  • Perfect stars are rare. Most snow crystals are small and simple.
Quick check
?Ice melts at 0 °C. Diamond, a giant covalent structure, needs a far higher temperature. Why?
Recap

The big ideas

  • A water molecule is bent, held together by strong covalent bonds.
  • Between molecules are much weaker forces. Melting ice overcomes those, not the covalent bonds.
  • In ice the molecules form rings of six: a hexagonal lattice. The crystal copies that pattern.
  • The open lattice leaves more space between molecules, so ice is less dense than water and floats. Most solids sink.
  • Water is densest at about 4 °C, so lakes freeze from the top.
  • Shape depends on temperature and humidity along the crystal's path. Complex crystals are never alike; simple ones can be.
