Mission 1: Why will a shop swap a meal for a printed slip of paper but not for a bag of apples?

Mission 1

Life without money: barter

  • Barter: swapping goods and services directly. Apples for bread, a haircut for a meal.
  • Problem 1: the double coincidence of wants. You need someone who has what you want and wants what you have.
  • Problem 2: no common measure. How many apples is a haircut worth? Every pair of goods needs its own price.
  • Problem 3: hard to save. Apples rot.
  • Problem 4: hard to split. Half a cow is not much use.
  • Money solves all four. It makes specialisation and trade far easier.
Mission 1

The functions of money

  • Money: anything generally accepted as a means of payment.
  • Its functions:
    • Medium of exchange: accepted in payment for goods and services.
    • Unit of account (or measure of value): prices and incomes are stated in it, so values can be compared.
    • Store of value: holds purchasing power over time.
    • Standard of deferred payment: debts and future payments are stated in it.
  • Say: "money is a medium of exchange" — not: "money is something you buy things with because it is valuable".
Mission 1

When money stops working: Zimbabwe

  • 2007–2008: prices in Zimbabwe rose faster and faster: hyperinflation.
  • Official annual inflation reached 231 million per cent (July 2008).
  • In early 2009 the central bank issued a 100 trillion Zimbabwe dollar note.
  • What failed first: the store of value. Money lost value in days, so people spent it at once.
  • Then the unit of account: shops priced goods in US dollars or changed prices several times a day.
  • Then the medium of exchange: sellers refused Zimbabwe dollars.
  • In 2009 Zimbabwe allowed the US dollar and other currencies to be used instead.
Quick check
?In a country with very high inflation, a family is paid in the local currency. They change their wages into a foreign currency on payday and keep their savings in it. Which function of the local currency has broken down most clearly?
Mission 1

What makes good money?

  • Acceptable: people trust that others will take it.
  • Scarce / limited in supply: if anyone can make more, it loses value.
  • Durable: does not rot or wear out quickly.
  • Portable: easy to carry.
  • Divisible: can be split into small amounts.
  • Uniform: every unit is the same.
  • Hard to forge.
  • Gold scores well on most. Apples score badly on durability and uniformity.
Mission 1

Forms of money

  • Commodity money: has value itself. Gold, silver, salt.
  • Representative money: paper that could be swapped for gold (a gold standard).
  • Fiat money: notes and coins with little value as objects. Accepted because the law makes them legal tender and people trust they will be accepted.
  • Bank deposits: numbers in accounts, spent by card, transfer or cheque.
    • In the UK, bank deposits are about 97% of broad money; notes and coins only about 3% (Bank of England, 2014).
  • Cryptocurrencies (e.g. Bitcoin): a debated case.
    • Their prices swing a lot. Few shops price goods in them.
    • Most central banks say they are not money in the full sense.
Quick check
?A café accepts payment in a cryptocurrency. Its price in US dollars often moves by 10% or more in a week, so the café must rewrite its menu prices in the cryptocurrency every day. Which function of money is failing most directly here?
Mission 1

Liquidity

  • Liquidity: how quickly and cheaply an asset can be turned into spending power without losing value.
  • Most liquid to least: notes and coins → current account deposits → savings deposits → fixed-term deposits → bonds and shares → houses.
  • Money is the most liquid asset. Narrow and broad measures of money are drawn along this ladder.
Mission 1

Narrow money and broad money

  • Narrow money: money that can be spent at once.
    • Notes and coins, plus banks' reserve balances at the central bank.
    • Some measures of narrow money also count current account deposits.
  • Broad money: narrow money plus deposits held in banks and building societies, including savings deposits.
    • UK measure: M4.
  • Most broad money is created by commercial banks when they lend (see Mission 3).
  • Say: "broad money includes bank deposits" — not: "money means notes and coins".

Mission 2

What financial markets do

  • Financial markets link savers (who have spare money) with borrowers (who need it).
  • Their roles:
    • Help people save: a safe place and a return.
    • Lend to households and firms: mortgages, business loans.
    • Make exchange easy: payments for goods and services.
    • Provide forward markets: fix a price today for a deal in the future.
    • Provide a market for equities (shares), so firms can raise money.
  • Benefit to the real economy: savings flow to investment, which raises productive capacity and growth.
Mission 2

Money, capital and foreign exchange markets

  • Money market: short-term lending and borrowing (under a year), e.g. bills between banks and firms.
  • Capital market: long-term finance through shares and bonds.
    • Primary market: new shares or bonds are sold, raising money for the issuer.
    • Secondary market: existing ones are traded between investors (e.g. the stock exchange).
  • Foreign exchange (forex) market: currencies are bought and sold.
  • Forward market example (illustrative): an exporter expects to receive 100,000 euros in 3 months. It agrees a fixed exchange rate today, so a fall in the euro cannot hurt it.
Mission 2

Debt versus equity

  • Debt: borrowed money that must be repaid with interest.
    • Loans from banks.
    • Bonds: a borrower (government or firm) sells an IOU that pays fixed yearly interest and repays the face value at maturity.
  • Equity: shares give part-ownership of a firm.
    • Shareholders may get dividends if the firm makes a profit; nothing is guaranteed.
    • If a firm fails, lenders are paid before shareholders.
  • So: shares usually carry more risk but a higher expected return.
  • Say: "a firm raises equity finance by selling shares" — not: "a firm borrows by selling shares".
Quick check
?A clothing firm will receive 500,000 US dollars from a customer in six months. It wants to know today exactly how much that will be worth in its own currency. Which part of the financial system helps most?
Mission 2

Liquidity, return and risk

  • Every asset can be judged on three things:
    • Liquidity: how easily it turns into spending power.
    • Rate of return: what it earns (interest, dividends, price gains).
    • Risk: how far that return could vary, or be lost.
  • Cash and current account deposits: most liquid, little or no return, low risk.
  • Bonds: less liquid, fixed interest, prices can change.
  • Shares: higher expected return, more risk.
  • Opportunity cost of holding money = the interest forgone by not holding bonds or savings instead.
    • When interest rates rise, holding money costs more, so people hold less of it.
Mission 2

How bonds work

  • A bond has:
    • Face value: what is repaid at the end, e.g. 1,000.
    • Coupon: the fixed yearly interest, e.g. 5% of 1,000 = 50 a year.
    • Maturity: the date it is repaid, e.g. in 10 years.
  • After it is sold, a bond can be traded. Its market price can change; its coupon cannot.
  • Suppose new bonds now pay more interest.
    • The old bond still pays only 50 a year.
    • No one will pay 1,000 for it when a new 1,000 bond pays more.
    • Its price must fall until it offers a similar return.
  • Bond prices and market interest rates move in opposite directions.
Mission 2

Calculating the yield

  • Yield = coupon ÷ market price × 100.
  • Worked example (illustrative data): a bond pays a fixed 50 a year.
    • Bought at 1,000: yield = 50 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = 5%.
    • New bonds pay 6.25%. The old bond's price falls until its yield matches.
    • 50 ÷ price = 0.0625, so price = 800.
    • Check: 50 ÷ 800 × 100 = 6.25%.
  • Simplified: this ignores the repayment at maturity, which would hold the price a little higher.
  • Price falls → yield rises. Price rises → yield falls.
  • Say: "the coupon is fixed; the yield changes with the price" — not: "the interest on the bond went up".

Mission 3

What banks do

  • Commercial (retail) banks:
    • Accept deposits and keep them safe.
    • Make loans to households and firms.
    • Run payments: cards, transfers.
  • They channel savings into investment: firms borrow to build and expand.
  • Borrowers pay interest: the price of credit. Banks charge more for riskier loans.
  • Collateral: an asset the bank can take if a loan is not repaid (e.g. a house for a mortgage).
  • Investment banks: help firms raise money by selling shares and bonds; trade in financial markets; advise on takeovers.
  • Many large banks do both. Since 2019, the UK's biggest banks must ring-fence retail banking from riskier investment banking.
Mission 3

A bank's balance sheet

  • Liabilities: what the bank owes.
    • Customers' deposits (the bank owes them on demand or at a set date).
    • Borrowing from other banks.
  • Assets: what the bank owns or is owed.
    • Reserves: cash and deposits at the central bank. Most liquid, earn least.
    • Liquid assets: e.g. short-term government bills.
    • Loans to customers. Least liquid, earn most.
    • Investments such as bonds.
  • Capital (equity) = assets − liabilities. It absorbs losses.
Mission 3

Liquidity, profitability and security

  • Banks want three things that pull against each other:
    • Profitability: lend long-term at high interest.
    • Liquidity: enough cash and liquid assets to pay depositors who withdraw.
    • Security: avoid losses that would wipe out capital.
  • More lending → more profit, but less liquid and more risk.
  • Rules limit the risk:
    • Reserve ratio (liquidity ratio) = liquid assets ÷ deposits.
    • Capital ratio = capital ÷ risk-weighted assets.
  • Real world: international (Basel III) rules require capital of at least 8% of risk-weighted assets, plus extra buffers.
Quick check
?A bank sells some of its short-term government bills and uses the money to make more 25-year mortgages. What is the most likely effect?
Mission 3

Loans create deposits

  • When a bank makes a loan, it credits the borrower's account with a new deposit.
  • That deposit is new money: no one else's balance went down.
  • Example (illustrative): a bank lends 10,000 to a café owner.
    • Assets: loans +10,000.
    • Liabilities: deposits +10,000.
    • Broad money rises by 10,000 at once.
  • When loans are repaid, deposits fall: money is destroyed.
  • What limits lending? Demand for loans, the risk of non-payment, the interest rate set by the central bank, and capital and liquidity rules.
  • Say: "banks create deposits when they lend" — not: "banks just lend out savers' money".
Mission 3

The money multiplier model

  • A textbook model of how deposits spread through the banking system.
  • Fractional reserve banking: banks keep only a fraction of deposits as reserves and lend the rest.
  • Worked example (illustrative): reserve ratio 10%; a new cash deposit of 1,000.
    • Round 1: keep 100; lend 900. The 900 is spent and deposited in another bank.
    • Round 2: keep 90; lend 810.
    • Round 3: keep 81; lend 729 … and so on.
  • Maximum total deposits = 1,000 × (1 ÷ 0.10) = 10,000.
  • Bank credit multiplier = 1 ÷ reserve ratio = 10.

Mission 3

Why the multiplier overstates

  • The simple multiplier assumes banks lend every penny of excess reserves and every loan comes back as a deposit.
  • In reality:
    • Banks may keep excess reserves, e.g. when they fear losses.
    • People and firms hold some cash instead of deposits.
    • Lending depends on demand for loans and on capital rules.
  • Real world:
    • The Fed's required reserve ratio has been 0% since March 2020.
    • Central banks today mostly set interest rates and supply the reserves banks need.
  • So the multiplier is a maximum, not a forecast.
  • Link to prices (quantity theory): if money grows faster than real output, inflation may follow.
Mission 3

Systemic risk

  • Systemic risk: the risk that one bank's failure spreads to the whole financial system.
  • Banks lend to each other and hold similar assets. A loss in one can cause losses in many.
  • If depositors fear a bank may fail, they rush to withdraw: a bank run.
  • Real world:
    • Northern Rock (UK, September 2007): queues of depositors; it was later nationalised.
    • Lehman Brothers (US, September 2008): its collapse froze lending between banks worldwide.
  • Governments then rescued banks to stop the crisis spreading.

Mission 4

The bank behind the banks

  • A central bank is the bank at the centre of a country's money system. Examples:
  • Bank of England (founded 1694); US Federal Reserve (1913); European Central Bank (1998), for the countries that use the euro.
  • Its customers are the government and commercial banks, not households or firms.
  • Commercial banks hold accounts there called reserves.
  • When customers of one bank pay customers of another, the two banks settle by moving reserves between their accounts at the central bank.
  • It is usually not run for profit: its aim is a stable economy.
Mission 4

What a central bank does

  • Monetary policy: sets the policy interest rate to keep inflation low and stable.
  • Issues banknotes.
  • Banker to the government: holds its main account and helps manage its borrowing.
  • Banker to the banks: holds their reserves and runs the system they use to pay each other.
  • Lender of last resort: lends to banks that run short of cash.
  • Financial stability: watches for risks to the whole system and, in many countries, supervises banks.
Mission 4

The goal: stable prices

  • Most central banks aim for low, stable inflation, often a numerical target.
  • UK: the government sets an inflation target of 2% (CPI). The Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee (MPC), nine members, sets Bank Rate to hit it.
  • The UK target is symmetric: inflation below 2% is treated as seriously as inflation above it.
  • Stable prices help firms and households plan, borrow and invest.
Mission 4

The tools

  • Policy interest rate: the rate at which the central bank lends to, or pays on reserves of, commercial banks. Other rates tend to follow.
  • UK: Bank Rate (often called the base rate), set by the MPC; it feeds through to loan and savings rates (the transmission mechanism).
  • Open market operations: buying or selling government bonds to change bank reserves and the money supply.
  • Quantitative easing (QE): buying bonds on a large scale with newly created money, to push down long-term rates.
  • The Bank of England sets no minimum reserve ratio for monetary policy.
Quick check

Mission 4

Lender of last resort

  • A bank can be solvent (assets worth more than it owes) but illiquid: it cannot turn its loans into cash fast enough.
  • If depositors panic and withdraw at once (a bank run), even a sound bank can fail.
  • The central bank can lend it cash against good collateral: it is the lender of last resort.
  • Classic rule (Walter Bagehot, 1873): lend freely, against good collateral, at a high rate.
  • Real-world: on 14 September 2007 the Bank of England gave emergency support to Northern Rock. Queues of savers still formed outside its branches.
  • Risk: moral hazard. If banks expect rescue, they may take bigger risks (see Mission 5).
Quick check
?A bank's loans are worth more than it owes, but depositors suddenly withdraw more cash than it holds. What can the central bank do as lender of last resort?
Mission 4

Central banks, banks and the money supply

  • Most money is bank deposits, created when commercial banks lend (see Mission 3).
  • The central bank shapes that lending:
  • Policy rate: a higher rate makes loans dearer, so banks lend less and deposits grow more slowly.
  • QE: buying bonds creates reserves for banks and deposits for the sellers, so broad money rises directly.
  • Regulation: rules on capital and liquidity limit how far banks can expand lending.
  • So the central bank influences the money supply and the banking sector, but does not fix the quantity of money directly.
Mission 4

Keeping the system safe

  • Banks fail in ways that can spread to the whole economy. Central banks often help regulate them.
  • UK (since 2013): the Bank of England's Financial Policy Committee (FPC) looks at risks to the whole system.
  • The Prudential Regulation Authority (PRA), part of the Bank of England, supervises individual banks and insurers.
  • The Financial Conduct Authority (FCA), separate from the Bank, regulates how firms treat customers.
  • More on regulation and crises in Mission 5.
Mission 4Evaluate

Should central banks be independent?

  • UK: the Bank of England has set interest rates independently since 1997. The ECB's independence is written into EU treaties.
  • For: rate-setters cannot be pushed to cut rates before an election, so promises of low inflation are more credible. Inflation expectations may stay anchored.
  • Against: unelected officials make decisions that affect jobs, house prices and savers; accountability is weaker.
  • Against: monetary and fiscal policy may pull in opposite directions without coordination.
  • It depends on: how the target is set and reviewed, how open the central bank is, and how far it strays into areas such as QE that change who gains and loses.

Mission 5

What finance does for the real economy

  • The financial sector: banks, insurers, pension funds, markets.
  • It channels savings to firms and households that want to borrow and invest.
  • It runs the payments system: wages, card payments, bills.
  • It spreads and prices risk: insurance, diversified funds.
  • It turns short-term deposits into long-term loans (maturity transformation).
  • UK: financial services were about 9% of economic output in 2023, a large share for a rich economy.
Mission 5

Two ways a bank fails

  • A bank's assets: loans, bonds, reserves. Its liabilities: deposits and other borrowing.
  • Capital (equity) = assets − liabilities: the owners' stake that absorbs losses.
  • Insolvency: loans go bad, assets fall below liabilities, capital is wiped out.
  • Illiquidity: the bank is solvent but cannot turn loans into cash fast enough when depositors withdraw (a run).
  • The two feed each other: rumours of losses start a run; selling assets in a hurry causes losses.
  • Say: "capital is the owners' funding that absorbs losses" — not: "capital is cash the bank keeps in the vault".
Mission 5Worked example

Reading a bank's ratios

Illustrative data, Bank A (billions):

AssetsLiabilities and capital
Cash and reserves5Deposits85
Government bonds15Other borrowing9
Loans80Capital6
Total100Total100
  • Leverage ratio = capital ÷ total assets = 6 ÷ 100 = 6%.
  • Capital ratio = capital ÷ risk-weighted assets. Safe assets count for less; say risk-weighted assets are 50. Ratio = 6 ÷ 50 = 12%.
  • Liquidity: liquid assets ÷ deposits = (5 + 15) ÷ 85 = 23.5%.
  • Stress: 8% of loans go bad. Loss = 0.08 × 80 = 6.4, more than capital of 6. Bank A is insolvent.
  • More capital = a bigger cushion. More liquid assets = more time in a run.

Mission 5

Systemic risk

  • Systemic risk: the risk that trouble at one bank spreads to the whole financial system.
  • Banks lend to each other, so one failure means losses for others (contagion).
  • Fire sales: a bank dumps assets, prices fall, other banks' assets lose value.
  • Panic: depositors run from banks that look similar.
  • Lending shrinks (a credit crunch), so firms cannot borrow; output and jobs fall.
  • Credit creation makes it worse: when banks lend, deposits grow (see Mission 3); when they stop lending, deposits and spending shrink.
  • These costs fall on people outside the bank: a negative externality, a reason to regulate.
Mission 5

Moral hazard and too big to fail

  • Moral hazard: being protected from the cost of a risk makes people take more risk.
  • If a bank is too big to fail, its owners and lenders expect a rescue.
  • So profits from risky lending stay private, while big losses fall on taxpayers.
  • Lenders charge such banks less, so they can grow even bigger and riskier.
  • Deposit insurance and the lender of last resort (see Mission 4) help to prevent runs, but can add to moral hazard.
  • Say: "moral hazard: behaviour changes after protection is given" — not: "adverse selection", which is hidden information before a deal.
Quick check
?In a crisis, a government rescues a large bank but lets a large clothing retailer go bust. Which is the strongest economic reason for treating them differently?
Mission 5

The crisis of 2007–08

  • (1) 2000s: US house prices boom. Lenders sell sub-prime mortgages to borrowers with weak credit.
  • (2) Mortgages are bundled into securities and sold worldwide; banks borrow heavily (high leverage) to buy them.
  • (3) From 2006, US house prices fall. Defaults rise; nobody knows which banks hold the losses.
  • (4) August 2007: banks stop lending to each other. September 2007: run on Northern Rock (UK). 15 September 2008: Lehman Brothers goes bankrupt.
  • (5) Governments rescue banks: in 2008–09 the UK government took large stakes in RBS and Lloyds Banking Group.
  • An asset bubble in housing, fed by cheap credit, burst.
Mission 5

From bank losses to the real economy

  • Banks with less capital cut lending to rebuild their ratios: the lending collapse.
  • Firms cancel investment; households cut spending; AD falls.
  • UK real GDP fell by just over 6% from early 2008 to mid-2009: at the time, the deepest UK recession since the Second World War.
  • The cost of rescues added to government borrowing; the crisis was not only a banking problem.
Mission 5

Who regulates UK finance

  • Before 2013 the FSA, the Bank of England and the Treasury shared the job; the gaps were blamed for missing the risks.
  • Since April 2013:
  • Financial Policy Committee (FPC), at the Bank of England: watches risks to the whole system (macroprudential), e.g. sets extra capital buffers when credit grows fast.
  • Prudential Regulation Authority (PRA), part of the Bank of England: supervises the safety of individual banks and insurers (microprudential).
  • Financial Conduct Authority (FCA), separate: regulates how firms treat customers and keeps markets honest.
  • The Bank of England is still lender of last resort: it lends to solvent banks short of cash (see Mission 4).
Mission 5

The rules after the crisis

  • More capital (Basel III): banks must hold core capital of at least 4.5% of risk-weighted assets, plus a 2.5% buffer, and more for the biggest banks.
  • Leverage ratio: capital of at least 3% of total assets, with no risk weights (UK: 3.25% for large banks).
  • Liquidity coverage ratio: enough liquid assets to survive 30 days of stressed outflows.
  • UK ring-fencing (from 2019): big banks separate everyday deposits from riskier investment banking.
  • Stress tests: regulators test whether banks could survive a severe recession.
  • Bail-in: losses fall on a failing bank's owners and lenders first, not taxpayers.
Quick check
?The Bank of England notices that mortgage lending across the whole UK is rising very fast and house prices look stretched. Which body's job is it to respond to this risk to the whole system?
Mission 5

Why regulating finance is hard

  • Global finance: banks and money move across borders; a crisis abroad can hit home banks.
  • Regulatory arbitrage: activity moves to where rules are lighter, or to shadow banks (funds and lenders outside bank rules).
  • Information: regulators know less than banks; risks look small until they are not.
  • Regulatory capture: regulators may come to see things the industry's way.
Mission 5Evaluate

Do tighter rules make us safer?

  • For: more capital and liquidity mean banks absorb losses and survive runs; fewer rescues; less moral hazard.
  • Against: capital costs banks more than deposits, so loans may be dearer and scarcer, slowing investment and growth.
  • Against: risk can move to shadow banks, where it is harder to see.
  • It depends on: how high the requirements are; whether rules are international; whether regulators can spot risks in time; and whether a failing bank can really be wound down without a rescue.
Mission check

?A country's government announces that it will cover every loss of its three largest banks, with no new rules on how much capital they hold. What does economic theory predict?

Protection from losses changes behaviour. Because bank failures spread to the whole economy, governments protect banks, and then regulate them to limit the extra risk this invites.

Mission 6

What monetary policy is

  • Monetary policy: the central bank's use of interest rates and the money supply to influence demand and inflation.
  • UK: the Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee (MPC), 9 members, sets Bank Rate.
  • The Bank has had operational independence since 1997: the government sets the target; the MPC chooses the rate.
  • Expansionary policy: lower rates, more money. Contractionary: higher rates, less money.
Mission 6

Goals: an inflation target

  • Main goal: price stability, which means low and stable inflation.
  • UK: 2% CPI inflation, set by the government. The target is symmetric: undershooting is treated as seriously as overshooting.
  • If inflation is more than 1 percentage point away from 2%, the Governor writes an open letter to the Chancellor explaining why.
  • Why a target: it anchors expectations. If people expect 2%, wages and prices are set to match.
Mission 6

The central bank's tools

  • Policy rate (base rate, Bank Rate, discount rate): the rate the central bank pays or charges banks.
  • Open market operations: buying or selling government bonds. Buying adds money to the banking system; selling drains it.
  • Minimum reserve requirements: the share of deposits banks must hold as reserves.
  • Quantitative easing (QE): large-scale bond buying when rates are near zero (see Mission 7).
Mission 6

The transmission mechanism

  • How a rise in the policy rate reaches inflation:
    • Market rates rise: mortgages, loans, savings. Borrowing costs more; saving pays more. Consumption (C) and investment (I) fall.
    • Asset prices fall (houses, shares, bonds). Households feel less wealthy and spend less.
    • Confidence and expectations: a signal that the central bank is serious about inflation.
    • Exchange rate rises: exports more expensive abroad, imports cheaper. Net exports fall; import prices fall.
  • Result: AD grows more slowly. Less pressure on firms' capacity, wages and prices. Inflation slows.
  • A cut works the other way.
  • Say: "Bank Rate" (UK) — not: "the interest rate", when you mean the Bank of England's policy rate.
Mission 6

Monetary policy on AD/AS

  • Contractionary (rate rise): AD shifts left, AD1 → AD2.
    • Real output falls. The price level is lower than it would have been.
    • Closes an inflationary (positive) gap.
  • Expansionary (rate cut): AD shifts right.
    • Real output and the price level rise. Unemployment falls.
    • Closes a deflationary or recessionary (negative) gap.
  • Say: "inflation slows" (disinflation) — not: "prices fall", unless the price level actually falls (deflation).

Mission 6

What the committee weighs

  • The MPC forecasts inflation about two years ahead, because rate changes take time to work.
  • It looks at: spare capacity and unemployment, wage growth, inflation expectations, the exchange rate, house prices and credit, and world energy and commodity prices.
Mission 6

Real case: the rate rises after the pandemic

  • UK: Bank Rate was 0.1% (March 2020). The MPC raised it from December 2021 to 5.25% (August 2023).
  • CPI inflation peaked at 11.1% (October 2022) and was back at 2.0% in May 2024 (ONS).
  • Inflation then fell, but economists debate how much was due to higher rates and how much to falling energy prices.
Mission 6

Strengths and limits

  • Strengths:
    • Flexible: rates can change in small steps, several times a year.
    • Reversible, and quick to decide: no new law needed.
    • An independent central bank is credible: a promise to hit the target shapes expectations.
  • Limits:
    • Time lags: up to about two years for the full effect on inflation (Bank of England).
    • Supply shocks: rate rises can lower inflation, but at the cost of lower output and jobs.
    • Low confidence: firms and households may not borrow, however cheap credit is.
    • One rate for everyone: regions, and borrowers and savers, are hit differently.
Mission 6

The zero lower bound

  • Rates cannot be cut far below zero: people could hold cash instead.
  • Zero lower bound: once rates are near zero, conventional policy has little room left.
  • Real cases: Bank Rate 0.1% (March 2020). The European Central Bank's deposit rate reached −0.5% (2019); the Bank of Japan's short-term rate was −0.1% from 2016 to March 2024.
  • Liquidity trap (Keynes): rates are so low that more money does not raise spending; people just hold it.
  • Central banks then turn to QE and forward guidance (see Mission 7).
Quick check
?A recession has cut confidence. The central bank's policy rate is already 0.25%, and banks are unwilling to lend. Why might a further rate cut do little to raise AD?
Mission 6Evaluate

How well can interest rates steer the economy?

It depends on:

  • The cause of inflation: demand-pull responds well; a supply shock forces a choice between inflation and output.
  • Time lags: a rate change made now acts on demand up to two years later, when conditions may have changed.
  • Confidence and debt: with many variable-rate borrowers, rates bite fast; when confidence is low, cuts may not work.
  • How low rates already are: near the zero lower bound, other tools are needed.
Mission check

?A sharp rise in world oil prices pushes a country's inflation well above target, while real output falls. The central bank raises its policy rate. What is the most likely result in the short run?

Interest rates work through demand. After a supply shock, slowing inflation comes at the cost of lower output and jobs in the short run.

Mission 7: When interest rates can go no lower, how can a central bank still get people spending?

Make a guess: When interest rates can go no lower, how can a central bank still get people spending?

Mission 7

When the usual tool runs out

  • The usual tool: cut the policy interest rate to make borrowing cheaper.
  • In the UK: the MPC cut Bank Rate from 5.75% (2007) to 0.5% (March 2009).
  • Rates cannot go far below zero: people could hold cash at 0% instead. This floor is the zero (or effective) lower bound.
  • With the economy still weak, central banks turned to unconventional tools.
  • Main one: quantitative easing (QE).
Mission 7

What QE is

  • QE: the central bank creates new central bank reserves (electronic money) and uses them to buy assets, mainly government bonds.
  • UK government bonds are called gilts.
  • It buys from investors such as pension funds and insurers, not straight from the government.
  • The seller's bank account is credited: broad money (bank deposits) rises; banks' reserves rise.
  • So QE increases the money supply while the policy rate is stuck near zero.
  • Say: "the central bank creates reserves to buy bonds" — not: "prints banknotes and gives them away".
Quick check
?Which statement best describes quantitative easing?
Mission 7

How QE reaches spending

  • 1. Bond prices up, yields down. Big purchases raise bond prices. A bond's yield (its return) moves the opposite way to its price.
  • 2. Lower long-term interest rates. Government bond yields are a benchmark, so mortgage and business loan rates tend to fall.
  • 3. Portfolio rebalancing. Sellers use their new money to buy other assets (shares, company bonds): their prices rise, firms can borrow more cheaply.
  • 4. Wealth effect. Higher asset prices make owners feel richer: more consumption.
  • 5. Exchange rate. Lower yields can make the currency less attractive: it tends to fall, exports become cheaper.
  • 6. Confidence. It signals the central bank will act.
  • Result: AD shifts right, raising real output and the price level towards the inflation target.
Mission 7

Worked example: price up, yield down

  • Illustrative data. A bond pays a fixed 3 a year, for ever.
  • Yield = annual payment ÷ price × 100.
  • Price 100: 3 ÷ 100 × 100 = 3%.
  • QE buying pushes the price to 120: 3 ÷ 120 × 100 = 2.5%.
  • Price 150: 3 ÷ 150 × 100 = 2%.
  • The payment is fixed, so paying more for it means a lower return.
  • Say: "higher bond prices mean lower yields" — not: "bond prices and interest rates rise together".

Mission 7

Forward guidance

  • Forward guidance: the central bank tells people how it expects to set interest rates in future.
  • If people expect Bank Rate to stay low for longer, long-term interest rates fall now, and firms and households can plan with less uncertainty.
  • Real example: in August 2013 the MPC said it would not consider raising Bank Rate from 0.5% at least until the unemployment rate fell to 7%.
  • Unemployment fell faster than the Bank forecast; in February 2014 the guidance was broadened.
  • It works only if it is credible. Guidance is conditional, not a promise.
Mission 7

Funding for Lending

  • Funding for Lending Scheme (FLS): launched in July 2012 by the Bank of England and HM Treasury.
  • Banks could borrow cheaply from the Bank for several years.
  • The more a bank lent to households and firms, the more cheap funding it could get.
  • Aim: cut banks' funding costs so loans became cheaper and easier to get.
  • From 2014 it was focused on lending to small and medium-sized businesses.
  • Difference from QE: FLS targets bank lending directly; QE works through asset prices and yields.
Quick check
?How did the Funding for Lending Scheme differ from quantitative easing?
Mission 7

Reversing QE

  • Quantitative tightening (QT): the central bank shrinks its bond holdings.
  • Two ways: stop reinvesting as bonds mature, or sell bonds back to investors.
  • Reserves and deposits shrink: the money supply contracts.
  • The chain runs in reverse: bond prices fall, yields rise, borrowing costs rise: AD tends to shift left.
  • UK: the MPC decided to begin reducing its stock of bonds in February 2022.
  • Central banks try to reverse QE gradually and predictably, so markets are not shocked.
Mission 7

Real world: QE in practice

  • The Bank of England began QE in March 2009, with Bank Rate at 0.5%.
  • In total it bought £895 billion of bonds: £875 billion of gilts and £20 billion of company bonds.
  • The last increase was announced in November 2020, in the pandemic.
  • Question to weigh: why did central banks buy on this scale, and what happens when they stop?
Mission 7Evaluate

Does QE work?

It depends on:

  • Confidence. If households and firms want to save and banks will not lend, new reserves sit idle: "pushing on a string".
  • Who gains. Higher bond, share and house prices help people who own them, which may widen wealth inequality. Supporters reply that QE protected jobs and incomes.
  • Timing and exit. Too much for too long risks inflation; unwinding risks bond-market disruption and losses for the central bank.
  • In the UK, those losses are covered by HM Treasury, so they fall on taxpayers.
  • Evidence. Most studies find early QE lowered long-term yields. How much it raised output and inflation is still debated.
Mission check

?A central bank bought large amounts of government bonds during a slump. Years later, with inflation above target, it starts selling some of the bonds back to investors. Other things equal, what is the most likely effect?

Buying bonds pushes long-term rates down; selling them pushes rates up. The same chain works in both directions.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Money is anything generally accepted as payment. It removes the double coincidence of wants.
  • Functions: medium of exchange, unit of account, store of value.
  • A fourth: standard of deferred payment.
  • Narrow money is notes, coins and reserves; broad money adds bank deposits.

Mission 2

  • Financial markets link savers and borrowers; money markets are short-term, capital markets long-term.
  • Debt (loans, bonds) must be repaid with interest; equity (shares) gives ownership and uncertain dividends.
  • Bond prices and market interest rates move in opposite directions.
  • Yield = coupon ÷ market price × 100.

Mission 3

  • Banks create money when they lend: a loan creates a matching deposit.
  • Banks balance profitability against liquidity and security; reserve and capital ratios limit risk.
  • The multiplier model: maximum deposits = new reserves × 1 ÷ reserve ratio. It overstates because of idle reserves and cash leakages.

Mission 4

  • A central bank is banker to the government and to the banks; it issues notes and runs monetary policy.
  • Its main goal is low, stable inflation; its tools include the policy rate, open market operations and QE.
  • It is lender of last resort, helps keep the financial system safe, and is often independent of government.

Mission 5

  • Banks fail through insolvency (losses wipe out capital) or illiquidity (a run); capital and liquidity ratios measure the cushions.
  • Bank failures spread (systemic risk), and expected rescues encourage risk (moral hazard, too big to fail): two reasons to regulate.
  • After 2007–08: FPC, PRA and FCA, Basel III capital and liquidity rules, ring-fencing and stress tests; regulation has costs and limits.

Mission 6

  • The central bank changes the policy rate to steer AD and keep inflation near its target.
  • Higher rates: borrowing costs more, asset prices lower, currency stronger. AD shifts left; inflation slows.
  • Lags, supply shocks and low confidence limit what interest rates can do.
  • Near the zero lower bound, rate cuts lose their power.

Mission 7

  • At the zero lower bound, QE creates reserves to buy bonds: bond prices rise, yields fall, borrowing gets cheaper, AD shifts right.
  • Forward guidance works through expectations about future interest rates.
  • Funding for Lending tied cheap bank funding to lending.
  • Reversing QE (QT) contracts the money supply and pushes yields up.
