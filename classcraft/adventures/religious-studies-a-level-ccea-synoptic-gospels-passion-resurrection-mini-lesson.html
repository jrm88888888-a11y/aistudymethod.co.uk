Mission 1

One event, three accounts

  • The Passion narrative tells Jesus' suffering and death (Latin passio, suffering).
  • Your passages: Mark 15:21–47, Matthew 27:27–61, Luke 23:26–56.
  • Shared outline: Simon of Cyrene carries the cross → crucifixion at "the Skull" (Golgotha) between two criminals → the charge "King of the Jews" → mockery → darkness from the sixth to the ninth hour → the Temple veil torn → death → a centurion's words → women watching → burial by Joseph of Arimathea.
  • Most scholars hold that Mark wrote first and Matthew and Luke used Mark. So differences often show deliberate emphasis.
  • Redaction criticism asks: what did each evangelist change, and why?
Mission 1

Mark: abandoned and revealed

  • Stark and brief. Jesus says only one thing from the cross:
  • "Eloi, Eloi, lama sabachthani?" … "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Mark 15:34, quoting Psalm 22:1)
  • He is mocked by passers-by "wagging their heads" (15:29; compare Psalm 22:7) and by the chief priests: "He saved others. He can't save himself." (15:31): ironically true.
  • He dies with "a loud voice" (15:37). The Temple veil is torn "from the top to the bottom" (15:38): access to God opened, or judgement on the Temple.
  • A Gentile centurion: "Truly this man was the Son of God!" (15:39). The first human in Mark to say it, at the cross.
  • Theme: the suffering Messiah: who Jesus is can be seen only in his death.
Mission 1

Matthew: fulfilment and a new age

  • Follows Mark closely, with additions.
  • Wine "mixed with gall" (27:34; compare Psalm 69:21).
  • The mockery stresses Son of God: "If you are the Son of God, come down from the cross!" (27:40); "He trusts in God. Let God deliver him now" (27:43; compare Psalm 22:8 and Wisdom 2:18). It echoes the temptations (4:3, 6).
  • After his death: "The earth quaked and the rocks were split" (27:51); tombs open and saints are raised (27:52–53): signs of the new age and of resurrection.
  • The centurion "and those who were with him" say, "Truly this was the Son of God!" (27:54)
  • Joseph is "a rich man" and a disciple (27:57; compare Isaiah 53:9 "with a rich man in his death"), with his own new tomb (27:60).
  • Theme: fulfilment and the obedient Son whose death shakes the world.
Mission 1

Luke: innocent, forgiving, faithful

  • On the way, Jesus speaks to the daughters of Jerusalem (23:27–31): weep for yourselves.
  • "Father, forgive them, for they don't know what they are doing." (23:34; missing from some early manuscripts)
  • One criminal mocks; the other says Jesus "has done nothing wrong" (23:41) and asks, "remember me when you come into your Kingdom." (23:42)
  • "Assuredly I tell you, today you will be with me in Paradise." (23:43): salvation now, for an outcast.
  • Last words: "Father, into your hands I commit my spirit!" (23:46; Psalm 31:5): trust, not abandonment.
  • The centurion "glorified God, saying, 'Certainly this was a righteous man.'" (23:47): Greek dikaios, righteous or innocent.
  • The crowds go home "beating their chests" (23:48): repentance begins.
  • Theme: the innocent, righteous Son who forgives and saves to the end.

Q1 Both last words quote a psalm. What different picture of Jesus' death does each create?

Show answer

Mark: real anguish and abandonment, Psalm 22:1; Luke: calm trust in the Father, Psalm 31:5.

Q2 Why might Luke have the centurion say "righteous" rather than "Son of God"?

Show answer

Luke stresses Jesus' innocence, already declared by Pilate (23:4) and the criminal (23:41); "righteous" also fits Wisdom 2:18, where the righteous man is God's son.

Mission 1

The Old Testament in the Passion

  • The evangelists tell the story in the words of scripture, so the death is seen as part of God's purpose, not a defeat.
  • Psalm 22 (the righteous sufferer): "My God, my God …" (22:1, Mark and Matthew); mockers who "shake their heads" (22:7); "They divide my garments amongst them. They cast lots for my clothing." (22:18; all three).
  • Psalm 69:21 "they gave me vinegar to drink": the soldiers' sour wine (Luke 23:36; Mark 15:36; Matthew 27:34, 48).
  • "Into your hand I commend my spirit" (Psalm 31:5): Jesus' last words in Luke (23:46).
  • Isaiah 53:12 "was counted with the transgressors": crucified between criminals; Luke has Jesus quote it at the supper (22:37).
  • Amos 8:9 "I will cause the sun to go down at noon": darkness as the day of the Lord's judgement.
  • The Temple veil (Exodus 26:31–33) hid the Most Holy Place: torn, it signals a new access to God or the end of the old Temple.
Mission 1

God's saving plan in Luke

  • Luke stresses that the Christ must suffer (Greek dei): "Didn't the Christ have to suffer these things and to enter into his glory?" (24:26)
  • "all things which are written in the law of Moses, the prophets, and the psalms concerning me must be fulfilled" (24:44).
  • In Acts, Peter says Jesus was "delivered up by the determined counsel and foreknowledge of God" (Acts 2:23), though people bear responsibility.
  • Salvation: the mockers say "He saved others. Let him save himself" (23:35); the irony is that by not saving himself he saves the criminal (23:43).
  • Forgiveness (23:34) and innocence (23:4, 14–15, 22, 41, 47) run through the account: a righteous man dies for others.
Mission 1Diversity

Matera and Marshall

  • Frank J. Matera (b. 1942), Passion Narratives and Gospel Theologies (1986), paraphrase: each Passion narrative condenses its Gospel's theology:
  • Mark: the crucified Messiah and Son of God, recognised only at the cross.
  • Matthew: the obedient Son of God, whose death fulfils scripture and begins a new age.
  • Luke: the innocent, righteous prophet and Saviour, who forgives and brings salvation to the lost.
  • I. Howard Marshall (1934–2015), Luke: Historian and Theologian (1970), paraphrase:
  • Salvation is the central theme of Luke's theology.
  • Luke is both a careful historian and a theologian: his changes serve meaning, not invention.
  • Against critics who say Luke has no theology of the cross, Marshall points to "my body which is given for you" (Luke 22:19) and the "blood" by which God bought the Church (Acts 20:28): Luke does see Jesus' death as saving.
  • Your spec asks for "contemporary scholarship": these two are widely used for the Synoptic Passion narratives.

Mission 1

Death and burial compared

  • Women witnesses: all three have women watching from a distance (Mark 15:40–41; Matthew 27:55–56; Luke 23:49), the link to the empty tomb.
  • Joseph of Arimathea: Mark: "a prominent council member … looking for God's Kingdom" (15:43); Matthew: "a rich man", "Jesus' disciple" (27:57); Luke: "a good and righteous man" who "had not consented to their counsel" (23:50–51).
  • Proof of death: in Mark Pilate checks with the centurion (15:44–45): Jesus really died.
  • The tomb: cut in rock; a stone rolled against the door (Mark 15:46; Matthew 27:60). Women see where he is laid.
  • Sabbath: Luke's women "rested according to the commandment" (23:56): Jesus' followers keep the Law.
  • Significance: the burial confirms a real death and a known tomb, preparing for the resurrection (see Mission 2).
Mission 1Evaluate

Contradiction or complementary portraits?

  • Claim: The differences between the Passion narratives undermine their reliability.
  • For: Different last words (Mark/Matthew: Psalm 22:1; Luke: Psalm 31:5); one criminal penitent or both mocking; "Son of God" or "righteous man".
  • For: Matthew's earthquake and raised saints (27:51–53) appear in no other source.
  • Against: The core is shared by all three (much of it also by John): crucified under Pilate as "King of the Jews", darkness, death, a centurion, women witnesses, burial by Joseph.
  • Against: Redaction critics (Matera) show the differences are theological emphases, typical of ancient biography; differing detail is common in honest testimony.
  • Against: Some harmonise: Jesus may have said more than one thing on the cross; Christian devotion has long joined them as the "Seven Last Words".
  • Judgement criteria: What kind of writing are the Gospels? Is a difference in emphasis a contradiction? Which details are multiply attested?

Mission 2

The oldest summary

  • The earliest written account of the resurrection is not a Gospel: it is Paul's list, about AD 53–55.
  • "…that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day according to the Scriptures, and that he appeared to Cephas, then to the twelve." (1 Corinthians 15:4–5, WEBBE)
  • Paul says he received this: a tradition older than his letter.
  • The Gospels (Mark c. 65–70; Matthew and Luke c. 80–90, most scholars) add narratives: the women, the empty tomb, the appearances.
  • Two strands run through the accounts: the tomb was empty and Jesus was seen (Mark 16:1–8 has the tomb and a promise of meeting).
Mission 2Read the source

Mark's ending

> "He has risen! He is not here. See the place where they laid him!" (Mark 16:6, WEBBE)

> "They went out, and fled from the tomb, for trembling and astonishment had come on them. They said nothing to anyone; for they were afraid." (Mark 16:8, WEBBE)

  • Three women (Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James, Salome) come with spices; a young man in a white robe speaks.
  • Jesus himself does not appear in 16:1–8: a promise points to Galilee (16:7).

Q1 How does Mark's account end?

Show answer

With fear and silence, not with an appearance.

Q2 Why might a writer end there?

Show answer

Readers are left to respond: will they tell the news the women did not?

Mission 2

Mark's longer ending

  • The oldest complete manuscripts (Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus, 4th century) end at 16:8.
  • Mark 16:9–20 is widely judged a later addition (2nd century), drawing on Luke, John and Acts: appearances to Mary Magdalene, to two walking, to the eleven; a commission; the ascension.
  • Catholic and Orthodox Christians, and many Protestants, still read 16:9–20 as canonical (inspired scripture), whoever wrote it.
  • Significance: the earliest Mark has an empty tomb with no appearance; the Church's tradition completed the story.
Mission 2

Matthew 28: power and commission

  • A great earthquake; an angel rolls back the stone and sits on it; the guards "became like dead men" (28:4).
  • The women (Mary Magdalene, "the other Mary") meet Jesus, take hold of his feet and worship (28:9).
  • The chief priests bribe the guards to say: "his disciples came by night and stole him away while we slept" (Matthew 28:13, WEBBE). Matthew answers a rumour still current in his day (28:15).
  • On a mountain in Galilee the eleven worship, "but some doubted" (28:17).
  • The Great Commission: "Go and make disciples of all nations…" and the promise "I am with you always" (28:19–20).
  • Significance: the risen Jesus has all authority (28:18); the Church's mission flows from it.
Mission 2

Luke 24 at a glance

  • Tomb (24:1–12): the women find no body; two men in dazzling clothes: "Why do you seek the living amongst the dead?" (24:5). The apostles think it "nonsense" (24:11); Peter runs to look.
  • Emmaus (24:13–35): two disciples walk with an unrecognised stranger; he explains the Scriptures; they know him "in the breaking of the bread".
  • Jerusalem (24:36–49): Jesus stands among the eleven, shows hands and feet, eats fish, opens their minds, commissions them.
  • Bethany (24:50–53): he blesses them and is carried up into heaven; they return to the Temple with joy.
  • All of it happens in or near Jerusalem, in what reads as one day (Acts 1:3 later gives forty days). Galilee is only remembered (24:6).
Mission 2Diversity

Critical comparison

  • Women named: Mark three; Matthew two; Luke Mary Magdalene, Joanna, Mary mother of James "and the other women" (24:10).
  • Messenger(s): Mark a young man inside; Matthew an angel on the stone outside; Luke two men.
  • Appearances: Mark (16:1–8) none; Matthew to the women, then Galilee; Luke Jerusalem only.
  • Response: Mark fear and silence; Matthew joy and worship, some doubt; Luke disbelief turning to joy.
  • Explaining the differences: each evangelist's redaction (Matthew answers the theft rumour; Luke's Gospel ends where Acts begins: Jerusalem); independent memories; oral transmission.
  • Agreement: first day of the week, women first at the tomb, Mary Magdalene named in all three, tomb empty.

Mission 2

What it meant to the first believers

  • Power of God: God raised the one people crucified; the promise of power is coming (24:49).
  • Salvation as God's plan: "it was necessary for the Christ to suffer and to rise" (24:46): the cross was not a defeat.
  • Forgiveness of sins: "repentance and remission of sins should be preached in his name to all the nations, beginning at Jerusalem." (Luke 24:47, WEBBE)
  • Relationship with God: minds opened, a blessing, worship and great joy (24:45, 50–53).
  • Future of the Church: "You are witnesses of these things." (24:48): the story continues in Acts.
  • Empty tomb: God's vindication of Jesus and a new creation begun; appearances: personal encounter, commission, faith.
Mission 2Evaluate

Do the differences undermine the narratives?

  • Claim: The differences between the resurrection narratives show they are historically unreliable.
  • For: Number of women, messengers and place of appearances cannot all be harmonised easily; Mark's earliest form has no appearance.
  • For: Each writer shapes the story to his purpose (redaction), so details may be theological, not factual.
  • Against: Independent witnesses usually differ in detail but agree on the core: empty tomb, women first, Jesus seen.
  • Against: Women as first witnesses would be an odd invention, since women's testimony was often given less weight then.
  • Judgement criteria: What counts as the "core"? How much variation do eyewitness accounts normally show? Is theological shaping the same as invention?

Mission 3

What is at stake

  • Paul makes the resurrection the hinge of Christian faith:
  • "If Christ has not been raised, then our preaching is in vain and your faith also is in vain." (1 Corinthians 15:14, WEBBE)
  • Three separate questions:
  • Did it happen? (history)
  • Can a historian show it? (method)
  • What kind of event was it? (theology: a body revived, a body transformed, or a change in the disciples)
  • Christians answer these differently. This mission maps the answers.
Mission 3

Fiction: fraud or a swoon

  • Hermann Samuel Reimarus (1694–1768): fragments published after his death by Lessing (1774–78). The disciples stole the body and invented the story to keep their movement going: a fiction (fraud).
  • Matthew already knew this charge (Matthew 28:13).
  • H. E. G. Paulus (1761–1851), Life of Jesus (1828): a rationalist swoon theory: Jesus did not die, revived in the cool tomb and was seen alive.
  • That would be resuscitation of a man who never died, not resurrection.
  • Standard criticisms: the disciples suffered for their claim (fraud gives no motive); a half-dead man could not have inspired worship of a conqueror of death (D. F. Strauss' own reply to swoon theories).
Mission 3Read the source

What kind of body?

> "See my hands and my feet, that it is truly me. Touch me and see, for a spirit doesn't have flesh and bones, as you see that I have." (Luke 24:39, WEBBE)

> "It is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body." (1 Corinthians 15:44, WEBBE)

  • Luke also says the risen Jesus vanished (24:31) and stood among them suddenly (24:36).

Q1 Which features in Luke stress continuity with the body that died?

Show answer

Hands and feet, touch, flesh and bones, eating: 24:39–43.

Q2 What does Paul's contrast suggest?

Show answer

The raised body is changed: imperishable, "spiritual", not simply the old body back.

Mission 3

Resuscitation or resurrection

  • Resuscitation: a dead body returns to the same mortal life. The person will die again: the widow's son at Nain (Luke 7:15), Jairus' daughter, Lazarus (John 11:44).
  • Resurrection: raised to a new, transformed, imperishable life beyond death.
  • "Christ, being raised from the dead, dies no more. Death no longer has dominion over him!" (Romans 6:9, WEBBE)
  • Catholic teaching (Catechism of the Catholic Church, 646): Christ's resurrection was not a return to earthly life like the raisings he worked (paraphrase).
  • Contemporary critique: some read Luke's fish and flesh as a crude "resuscitation"; others (Rudolf Bultmann, 1884–1976, who read Easter as the rise of the disciples' faith; John Shelby Spong, 1931–2021, Resurrection: Myth or Reality?, 1994) reject a bodily event altogether and read resurrection as transformed faith.
  • Your spec's phrase: "resuscitation or resurrection".

Mission 3Evaluate

Which reading fits the narratives?

  • Claim: The Gospels describe a resuscitation, not a resurrection.
  • For: Luke and John stress touch, wounds and eating (Luke 24:39–43; John 20:27): the same body back.
  • For: a physical reading answers the charge that the disciples saw a ghost.
  • Against: the same accounts show a body that passes locked doors (John 20:19), vanishes and is not recognised at first (Luke 24:16, 31).
  • Against: Paul's "spiritual body" (1 Corinthians 15:44) and "dies no more" (Romans 6:9) describe transformation.
  • Against (from another side): Bultmann and Spong say both readings miss the point: the texts express faith, not biology.
  • Judgement criteria: continuity and change: which do the texts keep together? Does the word "body" need to be physical?

Mission 4

Why interpretation cannot be avoided

  • Every reader interprets: translation, genre, historical setting and the reader's own questions all shape meaning.
  • The Synoptic Gospels show this inside the Bible: Matthew and Luke reuse and edit Mark (the Synoptic Problem). The evangelists were already interpreters.
  • So the issue is not whether to interpret but how, and who has the right to decide.
  • Synoptic task: link this theme to at least one other unit (e.g. AS 1 Luke, A2 3 Old Testament, AS 8 Philosophy of Religion) and to human experience.
Mission 4Read the source

One saying, two versions

> "Whoever divorces his wife and marries another commits adultery against her." (Mark 10:11, WEBBE)

> "I tell you that whoever divorces his wife, except for sexual immorality, and marries another, commits adultery…" (Matthew 19:9, WEBBE)

  • Catholic teaching: a valid sacramental marriage cannot be dissolved; an annulment declares that a valid marriage never existed.
  • Orthodox churches may allow a second marriage, by oikonomia (pastoral discretion), with a penitential rite.
  • Many Protestant churches allow remarriage after divorce, citing Matthew's exception (and 1 Corinthians 7:15).

Q1 What does Matthew add to Mark's saying?

Show answer

An exception: "except for sexual immorality".

Q2 Why does this matter for authority?

Show answer

Churches who all accept scripture reach different practices, depending on which text they treat as governing and how.

Mission 4Diversity

Who has the right to interpret?

  • Individual reader with the Spirit: Protestant sola scriptura and the clarity (perspicuity) of scripture on what is needed for salvation.
  • Magisterium: Catholic teaching gives authentic interpretation to the Pope and bishops, serving the Word (Dei Verbum 10).
  • Tradition of the Fathers and councils: Orthodox reading of scripture within the Church's worship and tradition.
  • Scholarly community: historical-critical methods test readings by evidence.
  • Spectrum of authority: from fundamentalist (inerrant, often literal) to liberal (a human witness, reinterpreted for each age).
Mission 4

Readings from the margins

  • Liberation theology: Gustavo Gutiérrez (1928–2024), A Theology of Liberation (1971): read scripture from the side of the poor; God's preferential option for the poor (e.g. Luke 4:18, the Magnificat).
  • Feminist: Elisabeth Schüssler Fiorenza (b. 1938), In Memory of Her (1983): a "hermeneutic of suspicion" towards male-centred texts, recovering women's leadership in the early church. Phyllis Trible (b. 1932), Texts of Terror (1984): reading stories of abused women in memory of the victims.
  • Postcolonial: how texts were used in empire and mission, and how colonised peoples read them back.
  • Critics: these readings may bring a programme to the text; supporters reply that every reading has a standpoint, and these make it visible.

Mission 4Evaluate

Can a disputed text still have authority?

  • Claim: Because the Bible can be interpreted in so many ways, it cannot be a real authority.
  • For: the same texts have supported opposite causes (slavery and its abolition; apartheid and its opponents).
  • For: if a church or a reader decides the meaning, the authority seems to lie with the interpreter.
  • Against: national constitutions are interpreted too, yet remain authoritative; interpretation within a community and tradition sets limits.
  • Against: much of scripture's core (love of God and neighbour, the creeds' summary) is widely agreed; disputes are at the edges.
  • Judgement criteria: authority in theory or in practice? Is a shared method (tradition, Magisterium, scholarship) enough to limit disagreement? Does disagreement show a weak text or a rich one?
Mission 4

The problem texts must face

  • The problem of evil: if God is all-powerful and all-loving, why is there evil and suffering?
  • J. L. Mackie (1917–1981), "Evil and Omnipotence" (Mind, 1955): God omnipotent, God wholly good, and evil exists cannot all be true together (an inconsistent triad).
  • Moral evil (caused by human choice) and natural evil (disease, earthquake): AS 8.
  • Texts play two roles: they sharpen the problem (the Bible insists on God's power and goodness) and they respond to it.
  • Link to AS 8: Dostoyevsky (The Brothers Karamazov, 1880) and Camus (The Plague, 1947) criticise Christian answers to suffering.
Mission 4

Many biblical voices on suffering

  • Retribution: obedience brings blessing, disobedience brings curse (Deuteronomy 28:15).
  • Testing: Job, "blameless and upright" (Job 1:1), suffers by permission; his friends' retribution theory is rebuked (Job 42:7).
  • Mystery: God's answer from the whirlwind: "Where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth?" (Job 38:4).
  • Lament: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Psalm 22:1; spoken by Jesus on the cross, Mark 15:34).
  • Redemptive suffering: the Servant "pierced for our transgressions" (Isaiah 53:5); Joseph: "you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good" (Genesis 50:20).
  • Fall and hope: sin entered through one man (Romans 5:12); God "will wipe away every tear" (Revelation 21:4).

Mission 4

From texts to theodicies

  • Augustine (354–430): reading Genesis 3 with Romans 5:12, evil is a privation of good entering through the free fall of angels and humans; suffering is just punishment or its consequence.
  • Irenaeus (c. 130–c. 202) and John Hick (1922–2012), Evil and the God of Love (1966): humans made in God's image grow into his likeness (Genesis 1:26) through a world of challenge: soul-making.
  • Jürgen Moltmann (1926–2024), The Crucified God (1972): in Jesus' cry of abandonment, God shares suffering; the cross is God's answer, not an explanation.
  • Process theology (AS 8): God's power is persuasive, not coercive.
  • Bart Ehrman (b. 1955), God's Problem (2008), argues the Bible's answers conflict and none satisfies him.

Do the texts help with suffering?

  • Claim: Religious texts make the problem of suffering harder, not easier, to understand.
  • For: the Bible insists that God is all-powerful and good, creating the problem; its answers conflict (Ehrman); retribution texts can add guilt to pain.
  • For: Ivan Karamazov (Dostoyevsky) rejects any harmony bought with a child's suffering.
  • Against: the texts give many responses because suffering takes many forms; Job and the lament psalms allow protest within faith.
  • Against: for believers the cross and resurrection give hope and solidarity rather than theory (Moltmann; Revelation 21:4).
  • Judgement criteria: explanation or consolation? Is diversity of answers a weakness or realism? Do the texts answer the philosopher's question, or the sufferer's?
Mission 4

Leadership in the texts and in churches

  • Servant leadership: "whoever wants to become great amongst you shall be your servant" (Mark 10:43); Jesus washes the disciples' feet (John 13:14).
  • Peter as rock (Matthew 16:18): read by Catholics as the basis of the papacy; by most Protestants as Peter's confession or Peter's role in the first church.
  • Shepherds "not as lording it over those entrusted to you" (1 Peter 5:3).
  • Church models: episcopal, presbyterian, congregational, charismatic.
  • In society: 26 Church of England bishops sit in the House of Lords; church leaders spoke in Northern Ireland's peace process; Martin Luther King Jr led the US civil rights movement from the pulpit.
Mission 4Evaluate

The continuing debate about leadership

  • Claim: Religious leaders have lost the authority to lead in faith communities and in society.
  • For: abuse scandals and cover-ups (in Ireland, e.g. the Ryan and Murphy reports, 2009) badly damaged trust.
  • For: in a secular, plural society, many reject any public role for unelected religious leaders.
  • Against: leaders who serve, protest injustice or build peace still win respect across divides.
  • Debate on women: 1 Timothy 2:12 against Galatians 3:28. The Presbyterian Church in Ireland has had women elders since 1926 and ministers since 1973; the Church of Ireland ordained women priests from 1990; Catholic teaching (Ordinatio Sacerdotalis, 1994) reserves priestly ordination to men.
  • Judgement criteria: authority from office, character or service? Faith community or wider society? Whose voices are counted?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Mark: abandonment and revelation (Psalm 22:1; centurion: Son of God). Matthew: fulfilment and cosmic signs (earthquake, raised saints). Luke: innocence, forgiveness and trust (Psalm 31:5; "righteous man").
  • Matera: each Passion condenses its Gospel's theology; Marshall: salvation is Luke's central theme, and Luke sees Jesus' death as saving.

Mission 2

  • The accounts hold two strands: the empty tomb and the appearances; the details (women, messengers, places) differ by writer.
  • Mark ends in silence (16:8; 16:9–20 a later addition); Matthew adds the guard, Galilee and the Great Commission; Luke keeps everything in Jerusalem.

Mission 3

  • Resuscitation returns a person to mortal life; resurrection, as Christians mean it, is a transformed life that cannot die.

Mission 4

  • Interpretation is unavoidable: who interprets (individual, Magisterium, tradition, scholars) and how (fundamentalist to liberal; liberation, feminist) decides what a text requires.
  • On suffering, scripture gives many voices (retribution, testing, mystery, lament, redemption, hope), developed into theodicies by Augustine, Irenaeus/Hick and Moltmann, and criticised (Mackie, Ehrman, Dostoyevsky).
  • Leadership texts stress service; debates continue over women's leadership, trust after abuse, and religion's public voice.
