Mission 1: Did Jesus think God's Kingdom had already arrived, or was still to come?

Make a guess: Did Jesus think God's Kingdom had already arrived, or was still to come?

Mission 1

What the Kingdom of God is

  • Greek basileia tou theou: God's reign or rule, more than a place.
  • Jewish background: God is king of all (e.g. Psalm 145:13); prophets hoped God would one day reign openly, putting the world right.
  • Luke uses the phrase more than 30 times: it is the heart of Jesus' preaching (4:43; 8:1; 9:2).
  • In Luke the Kingdom brings reversal: the poor, the sick, sinners and outsiders are welcomed (6:20; 14:21).
  • The key question: when does this reign arrive?
Mission 1

Parables in Luke

  • A parable (Greek parabolē, comparison): a short story or image from everyday life that points to the Kingdom.
  • Characteristics: ordinary settings (farming, feasts, money, family); a twist that surprises; an open ending that demands a response.
  • Purpose: to teach, to provoke decision, and to reveal or conceal: "To you it is given to know the mysteries of God's Kingdom, but to the rest it is given in parables" (8:10).
  • Interpretation has changed over time:
  • Early and medieval Church: allegory: every detail has a hidden meaning.
  • Adolf Jülicher (1857–1938), Die Gleichnisreden Jesu (1888–99): one main point per parable.
  • C. H. Dodd (1884–1973), The Parables of the Kingdom (1935): parables announce a crisis: the Kingdom is here now.
Mission 1

The Sower (8:4–15)

  • Seed falls on four soils: path (eaten by birds), rock (no root), thorns (choked), good soil (fruit "a hundred times").
  • Luke gives the explanation: "The seed is the word of God." (8:11)
  • Path: the devil takes the word away. Rock: faith fails in testing. Thorns: "choked with cares, riches, and pleasures of life" (8:14). Good soil: those who "hold it tightly, and produce fruit with perseverance" (8:15).
  • Luke's emphasis: perseverance, and the danger of riches (a Lukan theme).
  • Kingdom meaning: the Kingdom is already being sown in Jesus' preaching, with a great harvest to come. Small beginnings, sure end.

The Great Banquet (14:12–24)

> "Blessed is he who will feast in God's Kingdom!" (Luke 14:15, WEBBE)

> "Go out quickly into the streets and lanes of the city, and bring in the poor, maimed, blind, and lame." (Luke 14:21, WEBBE)

  • A guest at a Pharisee's meal speaks of the future feast of the Kingdom (Isaiah 25:6). Jesus replies with a story.
  • The invited guests make excuses (field, oxen, marriage). The host fills his house with the poor, then with people from "the highways and hedges" (14:23).
  • The servant was first sent to say "Come, for everything is ready now" (14:17); after the refusals the host is angry (14:21) and the servant reports "there is still room" (14:22).
  • Just before (14:12–14): invite "the poor, the maimed, the lame, or the blind" to your own feasts.

Mission 1

Already here

  • "If I by God's finger cast out demons, then God's Kingdom has come to you." (11:20)
  • "God's Kingdom is within you." (17:21) The Greek entos hymōn can mean "within you" or "among you", in Jesus himself.
  • "The law and the prophets were until John. From that time the Good News of God's Kingdom is preached" (16:16): a new era has begun.
  • The disciples are sent to say "God's Kingdom has come near to you" (10:9).
  • Present-tense texts point to the Kingdom arriving in Jesus' ministry: healings, exorcisms, welcome of sinners.
Mission 1

Not yet

  • "May your Kingdom come." (11:2) The disciples still pray for it.
  • "There are some of those who stand here who will in no way taste of death until they see God's Kingdom." (9:27)
  • "This generation will not pass away until all things are accomplished." (21:32)
  • At the Last Supper Jesus will not drink wine again "until God's Kingdom comes" (22:18).
  • People "from the east, west, north, and south" will feast in it (13:29): a future gathering.
  • Future-tense texts point to a final coming, with judgement and the Son of Man (17:24).
Mission 1

The Kingdom in the past

  • God has always reigned: the Kingdom is not new to Israel's faith (Psalm 145:13).
  • Luke 13:28 pictures Abraham, Isaac, Jacob and the prophets "in God's Kingdom": Israel's past belongs to God's reign.
  • Luke 16:16 divides time: the law and prophets "until John"; then the Kingdom proclaimed.
  • For Luke's first readers, Jesus' ministry was itself past: the Kingdom had come in a history they could tell (Luke 1:1–4).
  • So "past, present, future" are three angles on one reign: promised, inaugurated, completed.
Mission 1

Schweitzer: thoroughgoing eschatology

  • Albert Schweitzer (1875–1965), The Quest of the Historical Jesus (1906; English 1910).
  • Jesus was an apocalyptic prophet: he expected the Kingdom to break in very soon, within his hearers' lifetime (cf. 9:27; 21:32).
  • When it did not come, Jesus went to his death to force its arrival (paraphrase of Schweitzer's famous image of Jesus throwing himself on the "wheel of the world").
  • His ethics were an interim ethic: demands for a short time before the end.
  • Conclusion: Jesus was mistaken about the timing; liberal "lives of Jesus" had remade him in their own image.
  • Critics: Schweitzer relied on Matthew and Mark and played down present-tense texts such as Luke 11:20 and 17:21.
Mission 1

Marshall: present and future held together

  • I. Howard Marshall (1934–2015), Luke: Historian and Theologian (1970); The Gospel of Luke (1978).
  • The Kingdom is already present in Jesus' person and works, and still future in its completion (often called inaugurated eschatology).
  • Luke presents salvation as the core of the Kingdom: offered now, completed later.
  • Luke does not give up the future hope; he reckons with a delay and stresses faithful waiting (12:35–40; 19:11).
  • Compared with Schweitzer: no "mistaken prophet"; the tension of "already" and "not yet" is intended.
  • Critics: some say inaugurated eschatology smooths over texts with a short time-frame (9:27; 21:32).

Mission 1

Significance for early believers

  • Luke wrote probably c. 80–90 CE: after the fall of Jerusalem (70 CE), with the end not yet come.
  • Delay: Luke notes that people "supposed that God's Kingdom would be revealed immediately" (19:11), then tells a parable of servants trading while the master is away.
  • Not "when?" but "how to live?": Acts 1:6–8 turns the question about the Kingdom's timing into a call to witness by the Spirit.
  • Hope in persecution: the future feast and judgement sustain faith.
  • Identity: Gentiles from "east, west, north, and south" (13:29) belong; the Church sees itself as the people of the Kingdom.
  • Ethics: the Kingdom's reversal shapes community life: sharing with the poor (Acts 2:44–45).
Mission 1Evaluate

Is Luke's Kingdom best understood as future?

  • Claim: The Kingdom of God in Luke is best understood as future.
  • For: Clear future sayings (9:27; 21:31–32; 22:16–18); the Lord's Prayer; feast imagery.
  • For: Schweitzer: the apocalyptic setting of first-century Judaism makes imminent expectation the natural reading.
  • Against: Strong present sayings (11:20; 16:16; 17:21); the Nazareth "Today" (4:21).
  • Against: Marshall: present and future are held together; salvation is offered now.
  • Against: Luke's own editing (19:11; Acts 1:7) suggests he corrects those who expected an immediate end.
  • Judgement criteria: Which sayings are most likely original? How should 17:21 be translated? Does Luke's editing reflect Jesus or the later Church?

Mission 2: In John's Gospel, who wanted Jesus dead, and why did a religious quarrel end on a Roman cross?

Make a guess: In John's Gospel, who wanted Jesus dead, and why did a religious quarrel end on a Roman cross?

Mission 2

The people with power

  • Pontius Pilate: Roman prefect of Judaea (AD 26–36). Only Rome could order crucifixion.
  • Caiaphas: high priest (c. AD 18–36), appointed by Rome; he kept his post by keeping order. Annas: his father-in-law, a former high priest, still powerful (18:13).
  • Chief priests: the Temple aristocracy, mostly Sadducees.
  • Pharisees: teachers of the Law, respected by the people. In John they act alongside the chief priests (7:32; 11:47).
  • The council (Sanhedrin): called by the high priest (11:47).
  • "The Jews" (hoi Ioudaioi): John's term, often meaning the Jerusalem authorities, sometimes the people of Judaea, sometimes Jesus' unbelieving opponents generally. Jesus and his disciples are Jews too.
Mission 2

The Temple cleansing

  • In John it comes at the start of the ministry (2:13–22), at Passover; in the Synoptics it is in the last week.
  • Jesus drives out traders and money-changers: "Don't make my Father's house a marketplace!" (2:16)
  • The disciples recall Psalm 69:9: "Zeal for your house will eat me up." (John 2:17)
  • "Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up." (2:19); "he spoke of the temple of his body" (2:21).
  • Significance: Jesus claims authority over the Temple, the centre of the chief priests' power and income, and presents himself as the place where God is met.
  • Some scholars think John moved the event to the start for theological reasons; others that there were two cleansings, or that John's date is right.
Mission 2

Sabbath and the Law of Moses

  • Healing at the pool on the Sabbath (5:1–18): the authorities "persecuted Jesus … because he did these things on the Sabbath" (5:16).
  • Jesus argues from the Law: if a boy is circumcised on the Sabbath "that the law of Moses may not be broken", why object to making a man "completely healthy"? (7:23)
  • The man born blind (ch. 9), also healed on the Sabbath: "This man is not from God, because he doesn't keep the Sabbath" (9:16). The man is expelled (9:34).
  • Nicodemus defends due process: "Does our law judge a man unless it first hears from him personally …?" (7:51)
  • Many hold that Jesus is accused of leading the people astray (7:12, 47), a serious charge under the Law (Deuteronomy 13).
  • Conflict over the interpretation of the Law, not about whether the Law matters: both sides appeal to Moses (5:45–46).
Mission 2

Christology and blasphemy

  • The deepest charge in John is about who Jesus claims to be (Christology).
  • He "called God his own Father, making himself equal with God" (5:18).
  • "before Abraham came into existence, I AM." They pick up stones (8:58–59).
  • "I and the Father are one." (10:30) "We don't stone you for a good work, but for blasphemy, because you, being a man, make yourself God." (10:33)
  • Before Pilate: "We have a law, and by our law he ought to die, because he made himself the Son of God." (19:7)
  • The Law: "He who blasphemes the LORD's name, he shall surely be put to death." (Leviticus 24:16)
  • For John's readers, the charge is the truth about Jesus; for the authorities in the story, it is blasphemy. John's irony depends on both.

Political expediency

After the raising of Lazarus, the chief priests and Pharisees meet in council (anthology extract 6, John 11:45–57):

> "If we leave him alone like this, everyone will believe in him, and the Romans will come and take away both our place and our nation." (John 11:48, WEBBE)

> "it is advantageous for us that one man should die for the people, and that the whole nation not perish." (John 11:50, WEBBE)

  • "Our place" may mean the Temple, or the leaders' own position.
  • John adds that Caiaphas "prophesied that Jesus would die for the nation, and not for the nation only" (11:51–52): his political calculation says more than he knows.

Show answer

Q2 How is Caiaphas' advice both political and, for John, theological?

Show answer

Politically, sacrifice one man to keep order; theologically, Jesus' death will save and gather God's children.

Mission 2

Arrest and trials

  • Arrest (18:1–12): in a garden across the Kidron, by "a detachment of soldiers and officers from the chief priests and the Pharisees" (18:3). Jesus says "I am he" and they fall to the ground (18:5–6): he is in control.
  • Before Annas (18:13, 19–24): questioned about his disciples and teaching; struck by an officer.
  • To Caiaphas (18:24, 28): John records no night trial before the full council (unlike Mark 14:53–65). The decision was already taken in 11:53.
  • Before Pilate (18:28–19:16): the authorities stay outside the Praetorium "that they might not be defiled, but might eat the Passover" (18:28).
  • "It is illegal for us to put anyone to death" (18:31): only Rome could execute.
  • Pilate goes in and out seven times between Jesus inside and the accusers outside.
Mission 2

The charges before Pilate

  • First: "If this man weren't an evildoer, we wouldn't have delivered him up to you." (18:30)
  • Political charge: "Are you the King of the Jews?" (18:33). Jesus: "My Kingdom is not of this world." (18:36)
  • Pilate: "I find no basis for a charge against him." (18:38) He offers Barabbas; has Jesus flogged; "Behold, the man!" (19:5)
  • Religious charge: "he made himself the Son of God" (19:7). Pilate is more afraid.
  • Political pressure: "If you release this man, you aren't Caesar's friend!" (19:12)
  • "We have no king but Caesar!" (19:15): the chief priests, guardians of God's kingship, declare loyalty to Caesar.
  • The sign on the cross: "JESUS OF NAZARETH, THE KING OF THE JEWS" (19:19): a political execution.

Mission 2Diversity

Rivkin and Culpepper

  • Ellis Rivkin (1918–2010), Jewish historian, What Crucified Jesus? (1984), paraphrase:
  • Ask what, not who, crucified Jesus: the Roman imperial system.
  • The high priest was a Roman appointee; his council was a political body for keeping order, not the Jewish people's religious court.
  • Any charismatic leader who drew crowds was a danger in an occupied land; Jesus was executed to prevent unrest, as others were.
  • The Pharisees and the Jewish people were not responsible.
  • R. Alan Culpepper (b. 1946), Anatomy of the Fourth Gospel (1983), and later essays on anti-Judaism in John, paraphrase:
  • Read John as a narrative: "the Jews" are a character group representing the world's unbelief, not a historical verdict on a people.
  • John's irony makes the trial a trial of the judges: Jesus is the true king and judge.
  • Because of its later use against Jews, Christian interpreters must face the Gospel's language honestly.

Mission 2Evaluate

Who was responsible for the death of Jesus?

  • The Temple authorities: they plotted (11:53), arrested (18:3), handed him over (18:30) and pressed Pilate (19:12, 15). "He who delivered me to you has greater sin." (19:11)
  • Pilate and Rome: only Rome could crucify; Pilate gave the order (19:16); the charge was kingship (19:19). Rivkin: the system.
  • "The Jews": John's wording, but Culpepper and most scholars read it as a character group or the authorities, not a people; Jesus and his disciples were Jews.
  • Jesus himself: "No one takes it away from me, but I lay it down by myself" (10:18).
  • God's plan: Caiaphas' words are prophecy (11:51); Jesus' hour has come (12:23).
  • Judgement criteria: historical responsibility (who acted) vs theological meaning (why it happened)? Does John's irony change how we read his accusations? Responsibility of individuals vs groups?
Mission 2

"The Jews", then and now

  • John was probably written when Jewish followers of Jesus were being put out of synagogues (9:22; 16:2): a bitter family quarrel within Judaism.
  • Harsh lines (e.g. "You are of your father the devil", 8:44) were later torn from this context.
  • For centuries some Christians accused Jews of deicide (killing God). This fed persecution.
  • The Catholic Church's Nostra Aetate (1965) taught that what happened in the Passion cannot be blamed on all the Jews then alive, nor on Jews today (paraphrase). Many Protestant churches have made similar statements.
  • Today many readers use Culpepper's approach: read John's characters in their story, and refuse to apply its polemic to Jewish people.
  • Historians widely agree that crucifixion was a Roman punishment for sedition, carried out on Pilate's authority.

Mission 3: Jesus' last words are not the same in every Gospel. Do the accounts clash, or tell one story three ways?

Make a guess: Jesus' last words are not the same in every Gospel. Do the accounts clash, or tell one story three ways?

Mission 3

One event, three accounts

  • The Passion narrative tells Jesus' suffering and death (Latin passio, suffering).
  • Your passage: Luke 23:26–56, part of anthology extract 7 (Luke 23:26–24:53).
  • Shared outline: Simon of Cyrene carries the cross → crucifixion at "the Skull" (Golgotha) between two criminals → the charge "King of the Jews" → mockery → darkness from the sixth to the ninth hour → the Temple veil torn → death → a centurion's words → women watching → burial by Joseph of Arimathea.
  • Most scholars hold that Mark wrote first and Matthew and Luke used Mark. So differences often show deliberate emphasis.
  • Redaction criticism asks: what did each evangelist change, and why?
Mission 3

Mark: abandoned and revealed

  • Stark and brief. Jesus says only one thing from the cross:
  • "Eloi, Eloi, lama sabachthani?" … "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Mark 15:34, quoting Psalm 22:1)
  • He is mocked by passers-by "wagging their heads" (15:29; compare Psalm 22:7) and by the chief priests: "He saved others. He can't save himself." (15:31): ironically true.
  • He dies with "a loud voice" (15:37). The Temple veil is torn "from the top to the bottom" (15:38): access to God opened, or judgement on the Temple.
  • A Gentile centurion: "Truly this man was the Son of God!" (15:39). The first human in Mark to say it, at the cross.
  • Theme: the suffering Messiah: who Jesus is can be seen only in his death.
Mission 3

Luke: innocent, forgiving, faithful

  • On the way, Jesus speaks to the daughters of Jerusalem (23:27–31): weep for yourselves.
  • "Father, forgive them, for they don't know what they are doing." (23:34; missing from some early manuscripts)
  • One criminal mocks; the other says Jesus "has done nothing wrong" (23:41) and asks, "remember me when you come into your Kingdom." (23:42)
  • "Assuredly I tell you, today you will be with me in Paradise." (23:43): salvation now, for an outcast.
  • Last words: "Father, into your hands I commit my spirit!" (23:46; Psalm 31:5): trust, not abandonment.
  • The centurion "glorified God, saying, 'Certainly this was a righteous man.'" (23:47): Greek dikaios, righteous or innocent.
  • The crowds go home "beating their chests" (23:48): repentance begins.
  • Theme: the innocent, righteous Son who forgives and saves to the end.

Mission 3

The Old Testament in the Passion

  • The evangelists tell the story in the words of scripture, so the death is seen as part of God's purpose, not a defeat.
  • Psalm 22 (the righteous sufferer): "My God, my God …" (22:1, Mark and Matthew); mockers who "shake their heads" (22:7); "They divide my garments amongst them. They cast lots for my clothing." (22:18; all three).
  • Psalm 69:21 "they gave me vinegar to drink": the soldiers' sour wine (Luke 23:36; Mark 15:36; Matthew 27:34, 48).
  • "Into your hand I commend my spirit" (Psalm 31:5): Jesus' last words in Luke (23:46).
  • Isaiah 53:12 "was counted with the transgressors": crucified between criminals; Luke has Jesus quote it at the supper (22:37).
  • Amos 8:9 "I will cause the sun to go down at noon": darkness as the day of the Lord's judgement.
  • The Temple veil (Exodus 26:31–33) hid the Most Holy Place: torn, it signals a new access to God or the end of the old Temple.
Mission 3

God's saving plan in Luke

  • Luke stresses that the Christ must suffer (Greek dei): "Didn't the Christ have to suffer these things and to enter into his glory?" (24:26)
  • "all things which are written in the law of Moses, the prophets, and the psalms concerning me must be fulfilled" (24:44).
  • In Acts, Peter says Jesus was "delivered up by the determined counsel and foreknowledge of God" (Acts 2:23), though people bear responsibility.
  • Salvation: the mockers say "He saved others. Let him save himself" (23:35); the irony is that by not saving himself he saves the criminal (23:43).
  • Forgiveness (23:34) and innocence (23:4, 14–15, 22, 41, 47) run through the account: a righteous man dies for others.
  • Links to the spec's terms (with): sacrifice, salvation, atonement, forgiveness of sins, relationship with God.
Mission 3Diversity

Matera and Marshall

  • Frank J. Matera (b. 1942), Passion Narratives and Gospel Theologies (1986), paraphrase: each Passion narrative condenses its Gospel's theology:
  • Mark: the crucified Messiah and Son of God, recognised only at the cross.
  • Matthew: the obedient Son of God, whose death fulfils scripture and begins a new age.
  • Luke: the innocent, righteous prophet and Saviour, who forgives and brings salvation to the lost.
  • I. Howard Marshall (1934–2015), Luke: Historian and Theologian (1970), paraphrase:
  • Salvation is the central theme of Luke's theology.
  • Luke is both a careful historian and a theologian: his changes serve meaning, not invention.
  • Against critics who say Luke has no theology of the cross, Marshall points to "my body which is given for you" (Luke 22:19) and the "blood" by which God bought the Church (Acts 20:28): Luke does see Jesus' death as saving.

Mission 3Evaluate

Contradiction or complementary portraits?

  • Claim: The differences between the Passion narratives undermine their reliability.
  • For: Different last words (Mark/Matthew: Psalm 22:1; Luke: Psalm 31:5); one criminal penitent or both mocking; "Son of God" or "righteous man".
  • For: Matthew's earthquake and raised saints (27:51–53) appear in no other source.
  • Against: The core is shared by all three (much of it also by John): crucified under Pilate as "King of the Jews", darkness, death, a centurion, women witnesses, burial by Joseph.
  • Against: Redaction critics (Matera) show the differences are theological emphases, typical of ancient biography; differing detail is common in honest testimony.
  • Against: Some harmonise: Jesus may have said more than one thing on the cross; Christian devotion has long joined them as the "Seven Last Words".
  • Judgement criteria: What kind of writing are the Gospels? Is a difference in emphasis a contradiction? Which details are multiply attested?

Mission 4

The oldest summary

  • The earliest written account of the resurrection is not a Gospel: it is Paul's list, about AD 53–55.
  • "…that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day according to the Scriptures, and that he appeared to Cephas, then to the twelve." (1 Corinthians 15:4–5, WEBBE)
  • Paul says he received this: a tradition older than his letter.
  • The Gospels (Mark c. 65–70; Matthew and Luke c. 80–90, most scholars) add narratives: the women, the empty tomb, the appearances.
  • Two strands run through the accounts: the tomb was empty and Jesus was seen (Mark 16:1–8 has the tomb and a promise of meeting).
Mission 4

Luke 24 at a glance

  • Tomb (24:1–12): the women find no body; two men in dazzling clothes: "Why do you seek the living amongst the dead?" (24:5). The apostles think it "nonsense" (24:11); Peter runs to look.
  • Emmaus (24:13–35): two disciples walk with an unrecognised stranger; he explains the Scriptures; they know him "in the breaking of the bread".
  • Jerusalem (24:36–49): Jesus stands among the eleven, shows hands and feet, eats fish, opens their minds, commissions them.
  • Bethany (24:50–53): he blesses them and is carried up into heaven; they return to the Temple with joy.
  • All of it happens in or near Jerusalem, in what reads as one day (Acts 1:3 later gives forty days). Galilee is only remembered (24:6).
  • Extract (7): Luke 23:26–24:53.
Mission 4Read the source

The road to Emmaus

> "Beginning from Moses and from all the prophets, he explained to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning himself." (Luke 24:27, WEBBE)

> "Their eyes were opened and they recognised him; then he vanished out of their sight." (Luke 24:31, WEBBE)

  • Two ways the risen Jesus is known: Scripture explained and bread broken (24:30, 35).
  • Early believers met him in the same two ways: the reading of scripture and the Eucharist.

Mission 4

What it meant to the first believers

  • Power of God: God raised the one people crucified; the promise of power is coming (24:49).
  • Salvation as God's plan: "it was necessary for the Christ to suffer and to rise" (24:46): the cross was not a defeat.
  • Forgiveness of sins: "repentance and remission of sins should be preached in his name to all the nations, beginning at Jerusalem." (Luke 24:47, WEBBE)
  • Relationship with God: minds opened, a blessing, worship and great joy (24:45, 50–53).
  • Future of the Church: "You are witnesses of these things." (24:48): the story continues in Acts.
Mission 4

Sacrifice and atonement in Luke?

  • Atonement: being made "at one" with God through Christ's death.
  • Luke omits Mark's "ransom for many" (Mark 10:45); the Acts speeches rarely say "died for our sins". Hans Conzelmann (1915–1989; The Theology of St Luke, 1960) concluded Luke has no direct theology of the cross as saving.
  • I. Howard Marshall (1934–2015), Luke: Historian and Theologian (1970): salvation is the central theme of Luke–Acts. Luke does give the cross saving meaning, though lightly: the body "given for you" and the cup "poured out for you" (Luke 22:19–20); the Church bought "with his own blood" (Acts 20:28). The resurrection shows Jesus is Lord and Saviour. (paraphrase)
  • Frank J. Matera (b. 1942), Passion Narratives and Gospel Theologies (1986): Luke tells Jesus' death as that of the innocent, righteous one (23:47), whom God vindicates; salvation is offered by the risen, exalted Lord. Luke 24:26, the Messiah must suffer and enter glory, is the key. (paraphrase)
  • Sacrifice: the Emmaus meal echoes the Last Supper: the one who gave his body now breaks bread again.

Mission 4Evaluate

Do the differences undermine the narratives?

  • Claim: The differences between the resurrection narratives show they are historically unreliable.
  • For: Number of women, messengers and place of appearances cannot all be harmonised easily; Mark's earliest form has no appearance.
  • For: Each writer shapes the story to his purpose (redaction), so details may be theological, not factual.
  • Against: Independent witnesses usually differ in detail but agree on the core: empty tomb, women first, Jesus seen.
  • Against: Women as first witnesses would be an odd invention, since women's testimony was often given less weight then.
  • Judgement criteria: What counts as the "core"? How much variation do eyewitness accounts normally show? Is theological shaping the same as invention?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The Kingdom of God is God's reign; in Luke it brings reversal, welcoming the poor and outsiders.
  • Parables such as the Sower and the Great Banquet show it sown now, harvested and feasted later.
  • Schweitzer: Jesus expected an imminent end and was mistaken; Marshall: the Kingdom is inaugurated in Jesus and completed in the future. Luke's readers, facing delay, were called to faithful witness.

Mission 2

  • Religious conflict in John: Temple cleansing, Sabbath healings and the Law, and above all Jesus' claim to be equal with God (blasphemy).
  • Political conflict: fear of Rome (11:48), Caiaphas' expediency (11:50), the charge of kingship before Pilate (18:33; 19:12–19).
  • Rivkin: the Roman imperial system crucified Jesus, not the Jewish people; Culpepper: "the Jews" are a narrative character group; John's polemic must not be turned against Jews today.

Mission 3

  • Luke 23:26–56: Old Testament fulfilment (Psalms 22, 31, 69; Isaiah 53:12; Amos 8:9), God's plan ("must"), salvation for the criminal, forgiveness and innocence.
  • Matera: each Passion condenses its Gospel's theology; Marshall: salvation is Luke's central theme, and Luke sees Jesus' death as saving.

Mission 4

  • The accounts hold two strands: the empty tomb and the appearances; the details (women, messengers, places) differ by writer.
  • In Luke the risen Lord is known in Scripture and the breaking of bread and sends witnesses with forgiveness; Marshall finds salvation central and a real, light theology of the cross; Matera stresses the vindicated righteous one.
