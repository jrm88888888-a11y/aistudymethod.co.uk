Mission 1

Mission 1: A plant takes in carbon dioxide and water and gives out oxygen. Which of the two did the oxygen come from?

Make a guess: A plant takes in carbon dioxide and water and gives out oxygen. Which of the two did the oxygen come from?

Mission 1

Photosynthesis in one line

  • carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen
  • 6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2 (light energy, absorbed by chlorophyll)
  • Light energy becomes chemical energy in the carbon compounds made.
  • Energy is transformed, not created.
  • Chloroplasts act as transducers: they convert the energy of light photons into the chemical energy of ATP, then sugar.
  • Say: "photosynthesis transforms light energy" — not: "plants make energy".
Mission 1

Water is split for its hydrogen

  • CO2 has no hydrogen. Glucose has 12 H atoms. They come from water.
  • Light energy is used to split water.
  • Its hydrogen (as protons and electrons) reduces CO2 to glucose.
  • The oxygen left over is a by-product: it is released.
  • CO2's carbon and oxygen end up in glucose and in some new water, not in the O2.
  • Plants, algae and cyanobacteria (prokaryotes) all split water and release O2.
  • Say: "the oxygen released comes from water" — not: "the plant keeps the carbon of CO2 and releases its oxygen".

Mission 1

Inside a chloroplast

  • Envelope: outer and inner membrane.
  • Thylakoids: flattened membrane sacs. A stack is a granum (plural grana).
  • Lamellae (intergranal thylakoids) link the grana.
  • Stroma: the fluid around the thylakoids, inside the inner membrane.
  • Thylakoid membranes hold chlorophyll and electron carriers.
  • The chlorophyll is organised into two photosystems.
  • Light-dependent stage: thylakoid membranes. Light-independent stage: stroma.
  • Stroma also holds circular DNA, 70S ribosomes, starch grains and lipid droplets.
Mission 1

Built for the job

  • Huge thylakoid membrane area: room for many photosystems, carriers and ATP synthase.
  • Small volume inside each thylakoid: protons pumped in build a steep gradient fast.
  • Stroma: Calvin cycle enzymes and substrates concentrated together, right next to the thylakoids that supply ATP and reduced NADP.
  • DNA and ribosomes: the chloroplast makes some of its own proteins.
  • Starch grains: sugar stored where it is made.
Quick check
?Which statement correctly matches an adaptation of the chloroplast to its function in photosynthesis?
Mission 1

The leaf: built to trap light

  • Upper epidermis with waxy cuticle: transparent; cuts water loss.
  • Palisade mesophyll: tall, tightly packed cells near the top surface. Most chloroplasts are here, for maximum light absorption.
  • Spongy mesophyll: loosely packed cells, air spaces for gas diffusion.
  • Stomata (mostly in the lower epidermis): CO2 in, O2 and water vapour out.
  • Xylem (in veins) brings water and inorganic ions; phloem carries sugars away.
  • Broad, thin leaf: large surface area, short diffusion path.
  • Chloroplasts move inside palisade cells: in dim light they gather on the walls facing the light; in very bright light they line the side walls.
Mission check

?Chloroplasts from a hypothetical alga are broken open and separated into two fractions: the thylakoid membranes, and the stroma with no thylakoids. Each fraction is placed in the light with water, ¹⁴C-labelled CO2, ADP, inorganic phosphate, and a dye that accepts electrons in place of NADP. No ATP or reduced NADP (NADPH) is added. The O2 comes from water split in the thylakoid membranes; the carbon from CO2 is built into sugar in the stroma.

Mission 2

Mission 2: A green leaf also hides orange and yellow pigments. How can you reveal them, and what are they for?

Make a guess: A green leaf also hides orange and yellow pigments. How can you reveal them, and what are they for?

Mission 2

One leaf, several pigments

  • Chloroplasts hold several photosynthetic pigments:
  • Chlorophyll a: blue-green.
  • Chlorophyll b: yellow-green.
  • Carotenoids: carotene (orange) and xanthophylls (yellow).
  • Chlorophyll a is the primary pigment. The others are accessory pigments.
  • Each pigment absorbs a different set of wavelengths, so together they use more of the spectrum.
  • Chlorophyll has a magnesium ion at its centre and four nitrogen atoms in its ring. Nitrogen is also needed for amino acids, proteins (including enzymes) and nucleotides.
  • Lack of either → less chlorophyll → yellowing leaves (chlorosis), less growth. Specified practical: role of nitrogen and magnesium in plant growth.
Mission 2

Absorbing light

  • A pigment absorbs a photon only if its energy matches the gap to an excited state.
  • Then an electron is raised to a higher energy level. This energy is later turned into chemical energy.
  • Chlorophyll absorbs blue (about 430–450 nm) and red (about 640–680 nm) strongly.
  • It absorbs little green (about 500–600 nm): green is reflected or transmitted, so leaves look green.
  • Absorption spectrum: absorbance against wavelength (nm), for one pigment.
  • Say: "leaves look green because green light is reflected" — not: "chlorophyll absorbs green light".
Mission 2

Light harvesting

  • Pigments are grouped in light-harvesting systems (antenna complexes) in the thylakoid membrane.
  • Absorbed energy passes from pigment to pigment, inwards.
  • It reaches one reaction centre chlorophyll a.
  • Light-harvesting system + reaction centre = a photosystem.
Quick check
?The absorption spectrum of chlorophyll a has peaks at about 430 nm (blue) and 660 nm (red) but a trough at about 550 nm (green). Which statement best explains this pattern?
Mission 2

Practical: separating the pigments

  • Specified practical: separation of chloroplast pigments by chromatography.
  • Grind leaf with sand and solvent (e.g. propanone). Spot the extract on a pencil origin line; dry; repeat to concentrate.
  • Stand in solvent below the origin, in a sealed container. Remove before the front reaches the top; mark the front at once.
  • Pigments separate by solubility in the solvent versus attraction to the stationary phase.
  • Rf = distance from origin to spot centre ÷ distance from origin to solvent front.
  • Identify by Rf and colour against reference values for the same solvent and stationary phase.
Mission 2

Worked example: Rf

Illustrative data. Distances from the origin line: solvent front 82 mm.

1. Orange spot: 77 mm. Rf = 77 ÷ 82 = 0.94.

2. Blue-green spot: 49 mm. Rf = 49 ÷ 82 = 0.60.

3. Yellow-green spot: 33 mm. Rf = 33 ÷ 82 = 0.40.

  • Rf has no units and is always less than 1.
  • Measure from the origin, not the paper edge; to the centre of the spot.
Quick check

Mission check

Mission 3

Mission 3: Switch off the light and a leaf stops making sugar within seconds, yet that stage needs no light. Why?

Make a guess: Switch off the light and a leaf stops making sugar within seconds, yet that stage needs no light. Why?

Mission 3

The light-independent stage

  • Happens in the stroma.
  • Uses ATP and reduced NADP from the light-dependent stage.
  • Builds CO2 into sugar in a cycle: the Calvin cycle.
  • Three steps: fixation, reduction, regeneration.
  • It needs no light directly, but stops soon after the light does.
  • Say: "light-independent reactions" — not: "dark reactions that happen at night".
Quick check
?A student is drawing a model of how the two stages of photosynthesis in a chloroplast are connected. Which set of arrows between the light-dependent stage and the Calvin cycle should the model show?
Mission 3

Fixation: rubisco captures CO2

  • CO2 combines with ribulose bisphosphate (RuBP, 5C).
  • Enzyme: rubisco.
  • The 6C product is unstable: it splits at once into two glycerate 3-phosphate (GP, 3C).
  • No ATP is used in this step.
  • Written Rubisco; GP is glycerate-3-phosphate.
Mission 3

Reduction: GP to triose phosphate

  • Each GP is converted to triose phosphate (TP, 3C).
  • ATP supplies energy (and phosphate).
  • Reduced NADP supplies hydrogen: it reduces GP.
  • ADP, Pᵢ and NADP go back to the thylakoids.
  • Say: "reduced NADP reduces GP" — not: "rubisco reduces CO2".
Quick check
?What is required to convert glycerate 3-phosphate into triose phosphate in the Calvin cycle?
Mission 3

Regeneration: keeping the cycle turning

  • Most TP is used to regenerate RuBP, using ATP (no reduced NADP).
  • 5 TP (15 C) → 3 RuBP (15 C).
  • Worked count for one glucose (6 C):

1. Fix 6 CO2 → 12 GP → 12 TP (36 C).

2. 10 TP (30 C) → 6 RuBP (30 C): the cycle is ready again.

3. 2 TP (6 C) leave → one glucose.

  • So five-sixths of TP is recycled; one-sixth is the product.
  • Cost per glucose: 18 ATP (12 for reduction, 6 for regeneration) and 12 reduced NADP.
Quick check
?In a Calvin cycle that produces one molecule of glucose, how many molecules of triose phosphate are made in total, and how many of them are used to regenerate RuBP?
Mission 3

What TP becomes

  • Carbohydrates: glucose → sucrose (transport), starch (store), cellulose (cell walls).
  • Lipids: glycerol and fatty acids.
  • Amino acids: carbon skeletons from the cycle, plus nitrogen from nitrate or ammonium ions taken up by roots.

Each compound piles up where the cycle is blocked: GP when light stops (no ATP or reduced NADP), RuBP when CO2 stops.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Brighter lamps stop making a greenhouse crop grow any faster. What should the grower change instead?

Make a guess: Brighter lamps stop making a greenhouse crop grow any faster. What should the grower change instead?

Mission 4

The limiting factor

  • Rate of photosynthesis depends on several factors at once: light intensity, CO2 concentration, temperature.
  • The one in shortest supply sets the rate: the limiting factor.
  • Raise the limiting factor → rate rises.
  • Raise any other factor → no change.
  • Light: powers the light-dependent stage. CO2: substrate for rubisco. Temperature: speed of enzyme-catalysed steps.
Mission 4

Reading the curves

1. Rate rises as light rises: light is limiting.

2. The curve flattens: light is no longer limiting. Another factor is.

3. A curve at higher CO2 reaches a higher plateau: CO2 was limiting the first plateau.

  • On the rising part, curves at different CO2 overlap: there, only light matters.
  • Say: "the plateau shows another factor is limiting" — not: "the plant has reached its maximum rate".
Quick check
?Pondweed submerged in water at 25 °C with the carbon dioxide concentration of ordinary air was exposed to increasing light intensity. The rate of photosynthesis rose at first and then levelled off, staying constant however much the light intensity was increased. Which change is most likely to raise the rate at this plateau?
Mission 4

Temperature and water

  • Light-independent reactions are enzyme-catalysed: warmer → faster, up to an optimum.
  • Above the optimum: enzymes such as rubisco denature; rate falls.
  • Low temperature can limit the rate even in bright light and high CO2.
  • Say: "water stress limits photosynthesis by closing stomata" — not: "water is short as a reactant".
Mission 4

Gross, net and the compensation point

  • Leaves respire all the time, in light and dark.
  • Gross photosynthesis: total rate.
  • Net photosynthesis = gross − respiration. This is what gas exchange measures.
  • Rate measured as CO2 uptake or O2 production per unit time.
  • Compensation point: light intensity where photosynthesis = respiration. Net gas exchange is zero.
Mission 4

Worked example: finding the compensation point

Illustrative data: net O2 release by a leaf (arbitrary units).

1. Straight line. Gradient = (2.2 − (−1.0)) ÷ (40 − 0) = 0.08 per light unit.

2. y-intercept = −1.0: in the dark the leaf only respires, so respiration = 1.0.

3. Compensation point: net = 0. 0 = −1.0 + 0.08x → x = 1.0 ÷ 0.08 = 12.5 light units (the x-intercept).

4. Gross at 30 units = net + respiration = 1.4 + 1.0 = 2.4.

  • Assumes respiration stays the same in light and dark.
Mission 4

Practical: measuring the rate

  • Specified practical: investigation into factors affecting the rate of photosynthesis.
  • Pondweed (e.g. Elodea or Cabomba) in sodium hydrogencarbonate solution (CO2 source).
  • Measure O2: volume collected in a photosynthometer capillary per minute (better than counting bubbles).
  • Vary light: lamp distance; intensity falls about as 1 ÷ d². Use an LED or a water heat shield.
  • Vary CO2: hydrogencarbonate concentration. Vary temperature: water bath.
  • Let the plant acclimatise at each setting; repeat; keep other factors constant.
  • Alternatives: algal beads in hydrogencarbonate indicator; floating leaf discs.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Photosynthesis transforms light energy into chemical energy: water is split, its hydrogen reduces CO2 to sugar, and its oxygen is released.
  • Light-dependent stage (light reactions): thylakoid membranes. Light-independent stage (Calvin cycle): stroma.

Mission 2

  • Chromatography separates leaf pigments; identify each by Rf (origin to spot ÷ origin to front) and colour, against references for the same solvent.
  • Pigments absorb only wavelengths whose energy matches an electron jump: mainly blue and red. Accessory pigments widen the range used.

Mission 3

  • Rubisco fixes CO2 with RuBP → 2 GP; ATP and reduced NADP reduce GP → TP; five-sixths of TP regenerates RuBP (using ATP); one-sixth makes sugars, lipids and amino acids.
  • Light off: GP rises, RuBP falls. CO2 down: RuBP rises, GP falls.

Mission 4

  • The rate is set by the factor in shortest supply (light, CO2 or temperature). A plateau means another factor now limits.
  • Net photosynthesis = gross − respiration; at the compensation point, net gas exchange is zero.
