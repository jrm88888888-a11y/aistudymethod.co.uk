Mission 1

Four possible answers

  • "Who is a Jew?" has more than one Jewish answer. Four factors keep coming up:
  • Birth: being born into the Jewish people.
  • Faith: believing in God and the teachings of Judaism.
  • Works: keeping the mitzvot (commandments) and living a Jewish life.
  • Ritual: ceremonies that mark belonging, such as brit milah and bar/bat mitzvah.
  • Jews are a people as well as a faith: someone can be Jewish by birth and not religious at all.
  • Groups weigh these factors differently; that is where the disagreements lie.
Mission 1

Born of a Jewish mother

  • Halakhah (Jewish law) defines a Jew as someone born of a Jewish mother, or someone who has converted according to halakhah (see Mission 2).
  • The father's status does not decide it: the Mishnah rules that the child of a non-Jewish mother takes the mother's status (Kiddushin 3:12, paraphrase); the Talmud derives the converse, that the child of a Jewish mother is Jewish (next screen).
  • This matrilineal rule is held by Orthodox and Masorti (Conservative) Judaism, and is the basis of Jewish status in the Chief Rabbinate of Israel.
  • Reasons given: the Talmud derives it from the Torah (next screen); some modern writers add that a child's mother is always known, or that mothers were the main teachers of faith at home. Those are later explanations, not the legal source.
Mission 1Read the source

Deuteronomy 7:3–4

> "neither shalt thou make marriages with them: thy daughter thou shalt not give unto his son, nor his daughter shalt thou take unto thy son." (Deuteronomy 7:3, JPS 1917)

> "For he will turn away thy son from following Me, that they may serve other gods" (Deuteronomy 7:4, JPS 1917)

  • The Talmud (Kiddushin 68b, paraphrase) notes "he" in verse 4 is the non-Jewish father, who would turn away "thy son": that is, the grandson born to your daughter is still called "thy son". So the child of a Jewish mother is Jewish.
  • The child of a Jewish father and a non-Jewish mother is not called "thy son"; halakhically, that child is not Jewish without conversion.

Q1 In verse 3, which marriage leads to a child the Talmud still calls "thy son"?

Show answer

Your daughter marrying a non-Jewish man: her child stays Jewish.

Q2 Is this rule written plainly in the verse, or derived by interpretation?

Show answer

Derived: the Talmud reads it out of the wording, as part of the oral Torah.

Mission 1

Once a Jew, always a Jew?

  • In halakhah, Jewish status by birth cannot be lost. "An Israelite, even though he has sinned, remains an Israelite" (Talmud Sanhedrin 44a, paraphrase).
  • So a Jew who never practises, or who joins another religion, is still halakhically Jewish (though regarded as a sinner).
  • Israeli law is different. The Law of Return (1950; amended 1970) gives the right to settle to Jews, their children and grandchildren. In 1962 Israel's Supreme Court ruled that Brother Daniel (Oswald Rufeisen), born Jewish but a Catholic monk, did not count as a Jew under that law.
  • The UK JFS case (Supreme Court, 2009): a Jewish school's admission test based on matrilineal descent was ruled ethnic discrimination. Jewish schools now use tests of practice instead.

Mission 1

Brit milah: the covenant of circumcision

  • "every male among you shall be circumcised" (Genesis 17:10, JPS 1917): the sign of the covenant with Abraham.
  • On the eighth day (Genesis 17:12), even on Shabbat or Yom Kippur; delayed if the baby is unwell (pikuach nephesh: saving life comes first).
  • Performed by a mohel (trained circumciser); the sandek holds the baby; an empty chair for Elijah; blessings and the baby's Hebrew name.
  • It does not make the boy Jewish: he is Jewish from birth. It brings him into the covenant and fulfils a mitzvah.
  • For girls: naming ceremonies (e.g. simchat bat, "joy of a daughter") in synagogue or at home.
Mission 1Diversity

Attitudes to circumcision

  • Orthodox and Masorti: a binding mitzvah from the Torah; a father's duty; neglecting it is serious.
  • Reform and Liberal: almost all practise and encourage it as the sign of the covenant; some trained Reform doctors act as mohalim.
  • Many secular Jews also circumcise their sons as a mark of belonging to the people.
  • A small minority question it on grounds of bodily autonomy or pain; some hold a non-surgical welcoming ceremony instead (sometimes called brit shalom). Most Jewish bodies reject this as replacing the covenant.
  • Outside pressure: a German court ruled against infant circumcision in 2012; Germany then passed a law protecting it. Jewish and Muslim groups defended it together.
Mission 1

Bar and bat mitzvah

  • Bar mitzvah (son of the commandment): at 13, a boy becomes responsible for keeping the mitzvot. It happens automatically by age.
  • The celebration: he is called up to read from the Torah (an aliyah), may lead prayers, and now counts in a minyan; he starts wearing tefillin.
  • Bat mitzvah (daughter of the commandment): halakhically at 12 for girls.
  • Orthodox: girls' celebrations often include a talk on Torah, study or a group ceremony (e.g. bat chayil in some UK communities); usually no Torah reading in a mixed service.
  • Reform and Liberal: girls at 13 do exactly what boys do, reading from the Torah. The first public American bat mitzvah was Judith Kaplan's in 1922.
  • Some Progressive communities also hold Kabbalat Torah or confirmation at 15–16.

Mission 1

Faith and works: a people as well as a religion

  • Faith: Maimonides' Thirteen Principles set out beliefs; some Jews see belief as central to being a "good" Jew, but not to being a Jew.
  • Works: for many Orthodox Jews, Jewishness is lived through the mitzvot: Shabbat, kashrut, prayer, study (see Mission 3).
  • Secular and cultural Jews: identity through family, history, language, food, Israel, memory of the Holocaust. Humanistic Judaism (founded 1963) celebrates Jewish culture without belief in God.
Mission 1Evaluate

Is birth the most important factor in Jewish identity?

  • Claim: Birth matters more than faith, works or ritual in making someone a Jew.
  • For: Halakhah defines Jewish status by birth (or conversion) alone; status cannot be lost (Sanhedrin 44a).
  • For: God's covenant is with a people and its descendants (Genesis 17:7); identity is inherited.
  • Against: Without practice and belief, identity may fade; Jewish life is lived through mitzvot and rituals.
  • Against: Many Progressive Jews accept children of a Jewish father raised as Jews (see Mission 2), and the JFS ruling challenged birth-based tests.
  • Judgement criteria: Status ("is a Jew") or quality ("lives as a Jew")? Whose authority decides: halakhah, a movement, the State of Israel?

Mission 2

A non-missionising religion

  • Judaism does not send missionaries or seek converts.
  • Reason: non-Jews do not need to become Jewish to be righteous. The rabbis taught that the righteous of all nations have a share in the world to come (Tosefta Sanhedrin 13:2, paraphrase), if they keep the seven Noahide laws.
  • So a convert is not "saved" by converting; they take on the covenant and its 613 mitzvot.
  • Yet sincere converts are welcomed: a convert (ger, or ger tzedek, "righteous convert") becomes fully Jewish.
Mission 2Read the source

Ruth, the model convert

> "for whither thou goest, I will go; and where thou lodgest, I will lodge; thy people shall be my people, and thy God my God" (Ruth 1:16, JPS 1917)

  • Ruth, a Moabite widow, refuses to leave her Israelite mother-in-law Naomi.
  • Naomi urges her to go back several times (Ruth 1:8–15). The rabbis drew from this the custom of discouraging a candidate at first.
  • Ruth becomes the great-grandmother of King David (Ruth 4:17).

Q1 Which two things does Ruth join?

Show answer

A people, "thy people", and a faith, "thy God": both together.

Q2 Why might rabbis turn a candidate away at first?

Show answer

To test sincerity, as Naomi tested Ruth.

Mission 2

The Shulchan Arukh: questions to the convert

  • Shulchan Arukh ("set table"), the law code of Joseph Karo (1488–1575), printed 1565; conversion in Yoreh De'ah 268, drawing on the Talmud (Yevamot 47a–b).
  • The candidate is asked (paraphrase): Why do you want to convert? Do you not know that Jews in this time are oppressed and suffer?
  • If they answer, "I know, and I am not worthy", they are accepted.
  • They are taught the basics of faith (God's unity; the ban on idolatry) and some lighter and stricter mitzvot, with their rewards and punishments, but not burdened with too much at once.
  • The aim: an informed, free and sincere commitment.
Mission 2

Three requirements, before a beth din

  • Acceptance of the mitzvot (kabbalat ol mitzvot): commitment to live by halakhah. In Orthodox law, this is essential.
  • Circumcision (milah) for men. If already circumcised, a drop of blood is drawn as a sign of the covenant (hatafat dam brit).
  • Immersion in a mikveh (ritual pool of natural water) for men and women: the person emerges as a Jew.
  • All before a beth din (rabbinical court) of three judges.
  • In Temple times an offering was also brought; since the Temple's destruction this is not possible.
  • The convert takes a Hebrew name as a child of Abraham and Sarah.

Mission 2Diversity

Orthodox, Masorti and Progressive conversion

  • Orthodox: full commitment to halakhah (Shabbat, kashrut, family purity), usually after 1–3 years of study and living near an Orthodox community; milah and mikveh required. Orthodox batei din accept only Orthodox conversions.
  • Masorti (Conservative): halakhic process with milah, mikveh and a beth din; commitment to Jewish practice, interpreted less strictly. Not accepted by Orthodox authorities.
  • Reform and Liberal: study (often about a year), commitment to Jewish life, and a beth din. Rules on milah and mikveh vary: British Reform generally asks for both; American Reform has not required them since the 1890s, though many rabbis encourage them.
  • Consequence: a convert may be Jewish in one movement and not in another. In Israel, Progressive conversions count for citizenship under the Law of Return (court rulings, latest 2021), but the Chief Rabbinate does not accept them for marriage.
Mission 2

Patrilineal descent

  • Patrilineal descent: the child of a Jewish father (and a non-Jewish mother) can be Jewish without conversion.
  • US Reform (Central Conference of American Rabbis, 1983): a child of either Jewish parent is presumed Jewish if raised as a Jew, shown by "timely" public acts of Jewish identity (e.g. naming, Jewish education, bar/bat mitzvah). Reconstructionist Judaism adopted a similar policy in 1968.
  • UK Liberal Judaism also accepts a child of either Jewish parent raised as a Jew.
  • Orthodox and Masorti reject it: status passes through the mother (Kiddushin 68b). Such a person must convert to be Jewish in their eyes.
  • Note: the Reform rule asks more of some children (a Jewish upbringing), not only less: a child of a Jewish mother raised with no Jewish identity may not be presumed Jewish.
  • "Jewishness": the debate is over whether identity is a legal status (halakhah) or a lived, chosen belonging.

Mission 2Evaluate

Should halakhah be the only test of who is a Jew?

  • Claim: Halakhah (the mother's line or halakhic conversion) should be the only test of who is a Jew.
  • For: One clear standard keeps the Jewish people united; every movement accepts Orthodox conversions, but Orthodox authorities accept no others.
  • For: It rests on the Torah as interpreted by the oral law (Deuteronomy 7:4 in Kiddushin 68b; Yevamot 47a–b).
  • Against: It can exclude people who live fully Jewish lives (a child of a Jewish father raised in a synagogue).
  • Against: Progressive Jews see halakhah as evolving; equal treatment of fathers and mothers reflects modern values and many mixed families.
  • Judgement criteria: Unity or inclusion? Status or lived identity? Who holds authority: the beth din, a movement, the State of Israel?

Mission 3

Shabbat: creation and freedom

  • Shabbat (rest): from just before sunset on Friday until nightfall on Saturday, about 25 hours.
  • Creation: God "rested on the seventh day from all His work which He had made" (Genesis 2:2, JPS 1917). Resting imitates God.
  • Freedom: Deuteronomy's version of the commandment links rest to the Exodus: "thou was a servant in the land of Egypt" (Deuteronomy 5:15, JPS 1917). Slaves cannot rest; free people can.
  • Covenant: Shabbat is "a perpetual covenant" between God and Israel (Exodus 31:16, JPS 1917).
  • The rabbis read "Remember" (Exodus 20:8) and "Observe" (Deuteronomy 5:12) as two sides: positive celebration and refraining from work.

> "and call the sabbath a delight, and the holy of the LORD honourable" (Isaiah 58:13, JPS 1917)

Q1 Who must rest, according to Exodus 20:10?

Show answer

Q2 What does Isaiah add to "not working"?

Show answer

Mission 3

Shabbat at home

  • Friday evening: candles lit before sunset, traditionally by the woman of the house, with a blessing.
  • Kiddush ("sanctification") over wine: recalls creation and the Exodus.
  • Two challah loaves: a reminder of the double portion of manna before the Sabbath in the wilderness (Exodus 16:22).
  • Family meals, songs, guests; synagogue services Friday night and Saturday morning, with the weekly Torah portion.
  • Havdalah ("separation") on Saturday night: wine, sweet spices, a plaited candle; Shabbat is divided from the week.
Mission 3

What counts as "work"?

  • The Hebrew word is melachah: creative work, not effort.
  • The Mishnah lists 39 categories (Shabbat 7:2, paraphrase), based on the work of building the Mishkan (Tabernacle), because the Torah places the Shabbat command beside it (Exodus 31, 35).
  • Examples: sowing, cooking, writing, kindling a fire, building, carrying between private and public space.
  • Applied today: Orthodox authorities treat switching on lights and driving as forbidden; timers and pre-cooked food are used. An eruv (boundary) lets people carry within an area.
  • Pikuach nephesh (saving a life) overrides Shabbat completely (Talmud Yoma 85b): a doctor treats a patient; a sick person is driven to hospital.

Mission 3Diversity

Keeping Shabbat differently

  • Orthodox (shomer Shabbat, "Shabbat-keeping"): the 39 melachot are binding halakhah; no driving, phones, cooking or writing; walk to synagogue.
  • Masorti (Conservative): halakhic in principle; in 1950 American Conservative rabbis allowed driving to synagogue and using electricity on Shabbat. Practice among members varies.
  • Reform and Liberal: Shabbat as rest and renewal; individuals decide which practices make it holy (e.g. candles and family meal, but driving to synagogue).
  • Secular Jews: many keep a Friday-night family dinner as a cultural tradition.
  • All see Shabbat as a sign of Jewish identity; they differ on whether the rabbinic definitions of work are binding.
Mission 3

Dress and identity

  • Kippah (skullcap): a sign of respect before God; a custom, not a Torah law. Orthodox men wear one at all times; in Progressive synagogues it is optional for men and women.
  • Tallit (prayer shawl) with tzitzit (fringes): "that ye may look upon it, and remember all the commandments of the LORD" (Numbers 15:39, JPS 1917). Orthodox men also wear a small tallit under their shirt all day.
  • Tefillin: small leather boxes with Torah passages, bound on arm and head at weekday morning prayer (Deuteronomy 6:8).
  • Modesty (tzniut): Orthodox women usually wear skirts and cover elbows and knees; many married Orthodox women cover their hair (scarf, hat or wig).
  • Haredi men often wear black suits and hats; Hasidic groups keep distinctive dress.
  • Progressive Jews: everyday dress like their neighbours; some women choose kippah, tallit and tefillin.
Mission 3

The daily rhythm

  • On waking, Modeh Ani ("I thank you"); blessings (brachot) before and after food and for many daily events.
  • Three daily services: Shacharit (morning), Minchah (afternoon), Ma'ariv (evening); the Shema morning and evening.
  • Mezuzah on the doorposts (Deuteronomy 6:9): touched on entering and leaving.
  • Daily Torah study, alone or with a partner.
  • The Temple: the rabbis linked the three prayer times to the daily Temple offerings (Talmud Berakhot 26b); after 70 CE prayer replaced sacrifice, "the offering of our lips" (Hosea 14:3, paraphrase).
Mission 3

The kosher home

  • Kashrut (dietary law; full detail in Mission 4): kosher animals, shechitah, no blood, no mixing meat and milk.
  • In the home: separate sets of dishes, cutlery, pans and often sinks for meat and dairy; parev (neutral) foods go with either.
  • Food bought with a hechsher (certification mark).
  • Diversity: Orthodox Jews keep full kashrut at home and outside; some Jews keep a kosher home but eat out more freely; many Reform and secular Jews keep some practices (e.g. no pork) as identity; others none.
  • Some Progressive Jews add ethical concerns ("eco-kashrut").

Mission 3Evaluate

Is strict observance essential to Jewish identity?

  • Claim: Strict keeping of Shabbat, kashrut and dress is essential to Jewish identity today.
  • For: These are mitzvot from the Torah, interpreted by the oral law; for Orthodox Jews they are God's will, not options.
  • For: Visible practice marks Jews as a distinct people and has helped them survive assimilation.
  • Against: Halakhic status comes from birth or conversion, not observance (see Mission 1); a non-observant Jew is still a Jew.
  • Against: Progressive Jews keep the meaning (rest, holiness, ethics) while adapting the form; secular Jews express identity through culture.
  • Judgement criteria: Identity as status or as a way of life? Does adaptation keep or lose the purpose of a mitzvah?

Mission 4

Kosher means "fit"

  • Kasher (kosher) means fit or proper; the system of food laws is kashrut.
  • It covers three things: which animals may be eaten; how they are killed and prepared; keeping meat and milk apart.
  • The laws come from the Torah (mainly Leviticus 11, Deuteronomy 14) and are developed in the Oral Torah (Talmud, tractate Chullin) and later codes (Shulchan Arukh, Yoreh De'ah).
  • Food that is not kosher is often called treif.
  • Plant foods are all kosher in themselves; they must be checked for insects.

Q2 What reason does Leviticus 11:44 give for the food laws?

Show answer

  • Birds: the Torah lists forbidden species (mostly birds of prey); Jews eat only birds with a tradition of being kosher (e.g. chicken, turkey, duck).
Mission 4

Why keep kashrut?

  • Holiness: eating becomes a way of being set apart for God (Leviticus 11:44; 20:26).
  • Self-discipline: Maimonides (1138–1204), Guide for the Perplexed III.35 and III.48: the food laws train people to control desire; he also suggests some forbidden foods are unhealthy.
  • Obedience: many laws are chukkim, statutes with no reason given. The Sifra on Leviticus 20:26 (paraphrase): do not say "I dislike pork", but "I would like it, yet my Father in heaven has forbidden it".
  • Respect for life: "the blood is the life" (Deuteronomy 12:23, JPS 1917), so blood is never eaten; slaughter must cause as little suffering as possible.
  • Identity: kashrut marks Jewish homes and community.

Mission 4

Meat, dairy, parve

  • The Torah says three times: "Thou shalt not seethe a kid in its mother's milk" (Exodus 23:19, JPS 1917; also Exodus 34:26; Deuteronomy 14:21).
  • The Oral Torah reads the three verses as three bans: no cooking, eating or benefiting from meat and milk together (Chullin 115b).
  • The rabbis extend it: all meat and poultry (a rabbinic extension) with all dairy.
  • Parev (neutral): neither meat nor dairy, e.g. fish, eggs, fruit, vegetables, grains.
  • Waiting after meat before dairy: customs of six hours (most), three (many German Jews) or one (Dutch custom); based on Chullin 105a.
Mission 4

The kosher kitchen

  • Two sets of crockery, cutlery, pans and cloths: meat (fleishig) and dairy (milchig), often colour-coded.
  • Two sinks or separate washing-up bowls; sometimes two dishwashers or ovens.
  • Packaged food carries a hechsher (supervision mark) from a rabbinic authority, e.g. the London Beth Din (KLBD).
  • Kashering: making a utensil kosher again (e.g. boiling or heating), when a rabbi allows it.
  • A third set for Pesach, when no leaven (chametz) is used.
  • Children learn the system at home: the kitchen becomes a place of holiness.
Mission 4Diversity

Orthodox and progressive

  • Orthodox: kashrut is God's law, binding in every detail; strict supervision; many eat only in kosher restaurants.
  • Masorti: binding, with some leniencies (e.g. some eat vegetarian food in non-kosher restaurants).
  • Reform (1885): the Pittsburgh Platform said laws on diet "originated in ages and under the influence of ideas entirely foreign to our present mental and spiritual state".
  • Progressive today: informed choice: many avoid pork and shellfish, or keep meat and milk apart at home; some are vegetarian; some keep "eco-kashrut" (ethical, sustainable food).
  • Your spec: "different attitudes to … keeping a kosher home".

Mission 5

Origins in the Torah

  • Rosh Hashanah ("head of the year"): 1–2 Tishri (autumn). The Torah calls it "a memorial proclaimed with the blast of horns" (Leviticus 23:24, JPS 1917) and "a day of blowing the horn" (Numbers 29:1, JPS 1917).
  • The rabbis name it the New Year, the anniversary of creation, and the Day of Judgement (Yom ha-Din).
  • Yom Kippur ("Day of Atonement"): 10 Tishri. "ye shall afflict your souls" (Leviticus 23:27, JPS 1917).
  • In Temple times, the High Priest made atonement in the Holy of Holies (Leviticus 16). Since 70 CE, prayer, repentance and fasting take its place.
  • From Rosh Hashanah to Yom Kippur: the Ten Days of Repentance (Aseret Yemei Teshuvah, 1–10 Tishri), the Days of Awe.
Mission 5Read the source

Leviticus 16 and 23

> "And it shall be a statute for ever unto you: in the seventh month, on the tenth day of the month, ye shall afflict your souls, and shall do no manner of work" (Leviticus 16:29, JPS 1917)

> "For on this day shall atonement be made for you, to cleanse you; from all your sins shall ye be clean before the LORD." (Leviticus 16:30, JPS 1917)

> "from even unto even, shall ye keep your sabbath." (Leviticus 23:32, JPS 1917)

  • The rabbis read "afflict your souls" as fasting and other self-denial.

Q1 What two things does Leviticus 16:30 say the day does?

Show answer

Atonement and cleansing: being made clean from sins before God.

Q2 Which phrase gives the day its evening-to-evening length?

Show answer

"from even unto even": a full day, extended to about 25 hours.

Mission 5

The shofar in the Hebrew scriptures

  • A shofar is a ram's horn (or another kosher animal's horn, not a cow's).
  • Sinai: "the voice of a horn exceeding loud" as God gives the Torah (Exodus 19:16, JPS 1917).
  • Abraham and Isaac: a ram caught by its horns is offered instead of Isaac (Genesis 22:13). The Talmud links the ram's horn to God's remembering the binding of Isaac (Rosh Hashanah 16a).
  • Kings: Solomon is anointed: "blow ye with the horn, and say: Long live king Solomon" (1 Kings 1:34, JPS 1917). On Rosh Hashanah God is proclaimed King.
  • Alarm and repentance: "Blow the horn in Zion, sanctify a fast" (Joel 2:15, JPS 1917).
  • On Rosh Hashanah afternoon many Jews also say tashlikh ("you will cast") prayers by flowing water, a custom that symbolically casts sins away (Micah 7:19).
  • Freedom and future: the Jubilee is proclaimed with the horn on Yom Kippur (Leviticus 25:9); a "great horn" will gather the exiles (Isaiah 27:13).
  • Maimonides (Laws of Repentance 3:4, paraphrase): its call says "wake up, sleepers; examine your deeds and return".

Mission 5

The machzor and the changed Amidah

  • Machzor ("cycle"): the special prayer book for the festivals, especially the High Holy Days, with long poems and extra services.
  • Key prayers: Unetaneh Tokef (God as judge deciding "who shall live and who shall die"; repentance, prayer and charity change the decree's severity); Avinu Malkeinu ("Our Father, our King").
  • Amidah (standing prayer) amended for the Ten Days: "Remember us for life"; "inscribe us in the book of life"; God called "the holy King" instead of "the holy God".
  • The confession (Vidui: Ashamnu, Al Chet) is said in the plural: "we have sinned", even by those who did not do a given sin.
  • So the whole people stands together before God: unity and identity.
Mission 5

Kol Nidre

  • Kol Nidre ("all vows"): an Aramaic formula sung three times as Yom Kippur begins, with the Torah scrolls held.
  • It cancels vows made to God (rash personal vows), not promises or contracts made to other people.
  • Many Ashkenazi texts refer to vows of the coming year (a wording linked to Rabbeinu Tam, 12th century).
  • Its haunting melody makes it the best-known moment of the year, drawing even Jews who rarely attend.
  • History: some rabbis opposed it; some anti-Jewish writers claimed it made Jews' oaths worthless; 19th-century Reform removed it, but most Progressive communities later restored it.
  • Its meaning for many: the community stands together, free of empty words, ready for honest repentance.
Mission 5

The 25-hour fast

  • Five afflictions (Mishnah Yoma 8:1): no eating or drinking, no washing, no anointing (oils, creams), no leather shoes, no marital relations.
  • About 25 hours: the fast begins before sunset and ends after nightfall, adding time to the holy day.
  • Exempt: children under bar/bat mitzvah age; anyone whose health would be endangered (pikuach nephesh overrides).
  • Many wear white (some men a kittel): purity and simplicity.
  • Five services, ending with Ne'ilah ("closing of the gates") and a final shofar blast; the book of Jonah is read in the afternoon.
  • Mishnah Yoma 8:9 (paraphrase): Yom Kippur atones for sins against God; for sins against another person it does not atone until you have made peace with that person.

Mission 5Diversity

How far are they kept?

  • Yom Kippur has the highest synagogue attendance of the year; many Jews who keep little else fast.
  • Orthodox: the full day in synagogue, every service, the full fast; two days of Rosh Hashanah, including in Israel.
  • Progressive: shorter services, much English, egalitarian participation; most keep the fast; Kol Nidre kept; Reform often keeps one day of Rosh Hashanah.
  • Secular Jews: some fast as a mark of identity; in Israel the roads fall quiet on Yom Kippur.
  • Value of the fast: focus on the soul instead of the body; empathy with the hungry (Isaiah 58:6–7 is read in the morning: the fast God wants is freeing the oppressed). Challenge: hunger and tiredness may distract from prayer; health exemptions apply.
Mission 5Evaluate

Is yearly confession an admission of failure?

  • Claim: Regularly confessing sins every year shows a failure to grow spiritually.
  • For: If the same sins are confessed every year, it might seem little has changed.
  • For: Plural confession may let individuals avoid facing their own wrongs.
  • Against: Judaism expects humans to sin (yetzer hara); teshuvah is a lifelong process, not one event.
  • Against: Admitting fault is itself growth; the Mishnah demands real change towards others (Yoma 8:9).
  • Judgement criteria: What counts as "spiritual development"? Does regular confession lead to changed behaviour? Is honesty about failure a weakness or a strength?

Mission 6

The story: Exodus 12–15

  • Pesach (Passover): spring festival, from 15 Nisan, for 7 days (8 for many Jews outside Israel).
  • Exodus 12: before the tenth plague, each family kills a lamb and marks the doorposts with its blood; God "will pass over" those houses (Exodus 12:13). Israel leaves in haste: the dough has no time to rise (Exodus 12:39).
  • Exodus 13: keep the festival; eat unleavened bread; tell your children.
  • Exodus 14: the sea divides; Israel crosses on dry land; the Egyptian army is drowned.
  • Exodus 15: the Song of the Sea: "I will sing unto the LORD, for He is highly exalted" (Exodus 15:1, JPS 1917).
  • The core theme: redemption, God freeing a people from slavery to serve God.
Mission 6Read the source

"Because of what the LORD did for me"

> "And this day shall be unto you for a memorial, and ye shall keep it a feast to the LORD; throughout your generations ye shall keep it a feast by an ordinance for ever." (Exodus 12:14, JPS 1917)

> "And thou shalt tell thy son in that day, saying: It is because of that which the LORD did for me when I came forth out of Egypt." (Exodus 13:8, JPS 1917)

  • The Mishnah draws the lesson: in every generation, each person must see themselves as if they personally came out of Egypt (Pesachim 10:5, paraphrase).

Mission 6

The seder plate

  • Seder means "order": a home meal with a fixed order of 15 steps.
  • On the plate:
  • Zeroa (roasted shank bone): the Pesach lamb offered in the Temple. Vegetarians often use a roasted beetroot, an option the Talmud already mentions (Pesachim 114b).
  • Beitzah (roasted egg): the festival offering; also read as mourning for the Temple.
  • Maror (bitter herbs, often horseradish): "they made their lives bitter with hard service" (Exodus 1:14, JPS 1917).
  • Charoset (apple, nuts, wine): the mortar the slaves used for bricks.
  • Karpas (green vegetable) dipped in salt water: spring and new life; tears.
  • Chazeret (a second bitter herb, often lettuce).
  • Beside it: three matzot and wine for four cups.

Mission 6Diversity

Community, family and identity

  • Pesach is one of the most widely kept Jewish practices, including among Jews who rarely go to synagogue.
  • It is a home festival: parents teach children; the whole family takes part; guests are invited.
  • Orthodox: full traditional Haggadah (the book that guides the seder) in Hebrew; two seders outside Israel; strict chametz (leavened food) rules.
  • Progressive: one seder (Reform follows the Israeli 7 days); new Haggadot with English, discussion, and additions such as Miriam's cup (water, honouring Miriam and women in the story).
  • Secular and communal seders: freedom as a cultural and ethical theme.
  • Pesach expresses identity in worship: a people that remembers its origin together.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Halakhah: a Jew is born of a Jewish mother or converted; birth status cannot be lost.
  • Faith, works and ritual shape how Jews live their identity; secular Jews show identity can be cultural as well as religious.
  • Brit milah on the eighth day and bar/bat mitzvah at 13 (12 for Orthodox girls) mark covenant and adult duty, not membership.

Mission 2

  • Judaism welcomes sincere converts but does not seek them; Ruth is the model.
  • Shulchan Arukh (Yoreh De'ah 268): questions, teaching, acceptance of the mitzvot, circumcision (men) and mikveh (all), before a beth din of three.
  • Orthodox, Masorti and Progressive movements differ on conversion and on patrilineal descent, so "Jewishness" depends on who is asked.

Mission 3

  • Shabbat recalls creation (Genesis 2:2) and freedom from slavery (Deuteronomy 5:15): from Friday sunset to Saturday nightfall.
  • Candles, Kiddush, challah and Havdalah frame the day; Orthodox Jews keep the 39 melachot, Progressive Jews adapt.
  • Dress (kippah, tallit, tefillin, modesty) and the kosher home express Jewish identity, with wide diversity.

Mission 4

  • Kashrut (fitness) comes from Leviticus 11 and Deuteronomy 14 and is developed in the Oral Torah; reasons include holiness, self-discipline and obedience.
  • Meat and milk are kept apart (from Exodus 23:19 as the rabbis read it); parve foods are neutral; a kosher kitchen keeps two sets of utensils.

Mission 5

  • Rosh Hashanah (1–2 Tishri): New Year and Day of Judgement; the shofar wakes people to repentance; tashlikh casts sins away symbolically.
  • Yom Kippur (10 Tishri): the 25-hour fast, Kol Nidre, plural confession and Ne'ilah; it atones for sins against God, and against people only once peace is made.
  • The machzor and the amended Amidah show the people united before God as King and Judge.

Mission 6

  • Pesach recalls the Exodus (Exodus 12–15): redemption from slavery; "because of that which the LORD did for me" (Exodus 13:8).
  • The seder plate and the Haggadah teach the story through taste, questions and song; the family and community pass on identity.
