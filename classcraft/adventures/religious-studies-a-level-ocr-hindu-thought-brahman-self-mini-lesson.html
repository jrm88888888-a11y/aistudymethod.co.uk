Mission 1

Brahman

  • Brahman: the one ultimate reality, the source and ground of everything. From a root meaning "to grow, expand": the "great" or "vast".
  • Not a god among gods: all gods, people and things depend on Brahman.
  • Both transcendent (beyond the world) and immanent (within it).
  • Isha Upanishad 1 (Paramananda translation, 1919): "All this, whatsoever exists in the universe, should be covered by the Lord."
  • Hindus hold two ways of seeing Brahman, often together: nirguna and saguna.
  • Not to be confused with Brahma (the creator god) or a Brahmin (priestly varna).
Mission 1

Nirguna and saguna

  • Guna: quality. Nir: without. Sa: with.
  • Nirguna Brahman: Brahman without qualities: beyond form, name, description and thought. Impersonal.
  • Saguna Brahman: Brahman with qualities: a personal God who loves, creates, protects and can be worshipped, e.g. as Vishnu, Shiva or the Goddess.
  • Many Hindus say these are not two Brahmans but one reality seen in two ways.
  • Schools differ: Advaita (non-dualist Vedanta, following Shankara) treats nirguna Brahman as the highest truth; Ramanuja, Madhva and many Vaishnavas hold that Brahman is always a personal God with qualities (for them "nirguna" means free of any bad quality).
  • An image often used: water and ice. The same substance, formless or in form.

Kena Upanishad 1:3–8

Kena Upanishad (Swami Paramananda's translation, 1919):

> "There the eye does not go, nor speech, nor mind. We do not know That; we do not understand how It can be taught." (1:3)

> "That which cannot be thought by mind, but by which, they say, mind is able to think: know that alone to be the Brahman, not this which people worship here." (1:5; 1:6 in most editions)

  • In this translation, verses 1:4–8 repeat the pattern for speech, mind, eye, ear and breath (1:5–9 in most editions).

Mission 1

Neti neti: not this, not this

  • The Brihadaranyaka Upanishad (2.3.6, paraphrase) describes Brahman as "not this, not this" (neti neti); the sage Yajnavalkya repeats it (e.g. 4.4.22).
  • The via negativa: say what Brahman is not, since any positive word would limit it.
  • Not a body, not the senses, not the mind, not any object: every description falls short.
  • The same idea as Kena 1:3: "There the eye does not go, nor speech, nor mind."
  • How it helps: it guards against mistaking any form or idea for the whole of Brahman, and turns the seeker inward to the self that knows.
  • Criticism: if nothing can be said, how can anyone know or love Brahman? Saguna answers this.
Mission 1

Sat-chit-ananda and sat-chit-achit

  • Sat-chit-ananda: being (sat), consciousness (chit), bliss (ananda).
  • Not three parts: three pointers to one reality. The phrase is later Vedanta; its roots are in the Upanishads, e.g. Taittiriya 2.1 (paraphrase): Brahman is reality, knowledge and the infinite.
  • Advaita (Shankara) uses it as the nearest words for nirguna Brahman, still only pointers.
  • Sat-chit-achit (OCR's phrase): for Ramanuja (Vishishtadvaita), reality has three aspects: God, chit (conscious selves) and achit (non-conscious matter).
  • Selves and matter are the body of God: real, distinct, yet wholly dependent on God, who is saguna, full of good qualities.
  • So OCR's two phrases show two pictures of Brahman: pure being-consciousness-bliss, or a personal God with the world as his body.
Mission 1

Tat tvam asi: that you are

  • Chandogya Upanishad 6: the teacher Uddalaka Aruni teaches his son Shvetaketu: "tat tvam asi", "that you are" (first at 6.8.7, then repeated).
  • Examples (6.11–6.14, paraphrase): the one sap of life in a tree; the invisible essence in a tiny banyan seed; salt dissolved in water, tasted everywhere but unseen; a man led home blindfolded who finds the way with a guide.
  • Advaita: the self (atman) is Brahman; the difference is an illusion.
  • Vishishtadvaita: the self is part of Brahman's body: one with God, yet distinct.
  • Dvaita (Madhva): self and God are always different; Madhva's school reads the phrase differently.
  • Also Chandogya 3.14.1 (Shandilya Vidya, paraphrase): "all this is Brahman".

Mission 1

Why both matter: Brahman in deities, world and scripture

  • Deities: for most Hindus, Vishnu, Shiva, the Goddess and others are forms of saguna Brahman. A worshipper may treat one as supreme (an ishta-devata, chosen deity).
  • Images: in a murti, many Hindus believe God is truly present, so it is bathed, dressed and offered food. Some Hindus (e.g. the Arya Samaj) reject image worship.
  • World: Brahman pervades all things (Isha 1), so rivers, cows and people can be honoured as sacred.
  • Scripture: the Vedas are seen as Brahman in sound (shabda-brahman); the syllable Om stands for Brahman: "This Word is indeed Brahman" (Katha 2:16, Paramananda).
  • Importance: saguna Brahman makes bhakti possible; nirguna Brahman is the goal of jnana and meditation. Many Hindus worship forms while believing Brahman is beyond them.
Mission 1Diversity

What kind of belief in God?

  • Monism: only one reality; the world and selves are not separate from Brahman (Advaita).
  • Monotheism: one personal God, supreme over all (many Vaishnavas, Shaivas, Shaktas).
  • Henotheism: worshipping one god as supreme without denying others (a term made popular by Max Müller, applied to Vedic hymns).
  • Polytheism: many gods; how some outsiders describe Hindu worship; many Hindus reject this label.
  • Panentheism: all is in God, but God is more than all (often used of Ramanuja).
  • Hindus disagree among themselves; different labels fit different schools and practices.
Mission 1Evaluate

Is Hinduism monotheistic?

  • Claim: Hinduism is best understood as monotheistic.
  • For: All deities are forms of one Brahman (Rig Veda 1.164.46, paraphrase: the wise call the one by many names).
  • For: Many devotees treat one God as supreme (Lipner's polycentrism).
  • Against: Advaita's nirguna Brahman is not a personal God: that is monism, not monotheism.
  • Against: In village and family practice, many deities are worshipped side by side for different needs, close to polytheism in practice.
  • Judgement criteria: Which school? Belief or practice? Do Western categories fit Hinduism at all?
  • Also debated: whether it is possible to worship an impersonal God.

Mission 2

Atman: the self that is not the body

  • Atman: in Hindu belief, the real, eternal self in every living being.
  • Hindus hold that it is not the body, the mind or the personality: these change; the atman does not.
  • The body is like clothing:

> "Nay, but as when one layeth / His worn-out robes away, / And taking new ones, sayeth, / 'These will I wear to-day!' / So putteth by the spirit / Lightly its garb of flesh" (Gita 2:22, Arnold)

> "Never the spirit was born; the spirit shall cease to be never" (Gita 2:20, Arnold)

  • The atman wearing a body and caught in rebirth is called the jiva or jivatman (embodied self).
  • Katha II teaches the same: "This Self is never born, nor does It die." "It is not slain even though the body is slain." (Katha 2:18, Paramananda)
  • Gita II.13–28 is Krishna's teaching to Arjuna on the battlefield: the self cannot kill or be killed.

The chariot (Katha Upanishad 3)

Yama, the god of death, teaches the boy Nachiketas (Paramananda translation, 1919):

> "Know the Atman (Self) as the lord of the chariot, and the body as the chariot. Know also the intellect to be the driver and mind the reins." (Katha 3:3)

  • Katha 3:4 (paraphrase): the senses are the horses; the objects of the senses are the roads.

> "He who does not possess discrimination, whose mind is uncontrolled and always impure, he does not reach that goal, but falls again into Samsara" (Katha 3:7)

Q1 Who drives the chariot, and for whom?

Show answer

The intellect drives, holding the reins of the mind; the atman is the lord of the chariot, who rides.

Show answer

The journey fails: the person is reborn in samsara, 3:7.

Mission 2

Brahman: the reality behind everything

  • Brahman: ultimate reality, the ground of all that exists (see Mission 1).
  • Sat-chit-ananda: being, consciousness, bliss. Not three parts: three ways of naming one reality.
  • Neti neti, "not this, not this" (Brihadaranyaka Upanishad): nothing we can point to is Brahman; every description falls short.
  • Sat-chit-achit in Ramanuja's thought: Brahman (God) with conscious souls (chit) and non-conscious matter (achit) as his "body". Your spec glosses it "God-consciousness-no consciousness".
  • Shandilya Vidya (Chandogya Upanishad III.14, paraphrase): all this is Brahman; this self within my heart, smaller than a grain, greater than the worlds, is that Brahman.
Mission 2Read the source

"You are that" (Chandogya VI)

Uddalaka teaches his son Shvetaketu (Chandogya Upanishad VI.11–14, paraphrase):

  • VI.11 A great tree lives as long as the life (jiva) runs through it; when life leaves, the tree dies, but the life itself does not die.
  • VI.12 Break open a tiny fig seed: inside, nothing can be seen. From that unseen subtle essence the great tree grows.
  • VI.13 Salt dissolved in water is unseen, yet every sip tastes salty. So the Real is here though you do not see it.
  • VI.14 A man led blindfold far from home finds his way back by asking; one who has a teacher comes home to the Real.
  • Each teaching ends: that subtle essence is the self of all; that is the Real; that is the atman; you are that (tat tvam asi), Shvetaketu.

Q1 What do the salt and the seed have in common?

Show answer

Each is present but unseen, as Brahman is present in all things.

Q2 What does VI.14 add?

Show answer

You need a teacher, a guru, to realise the truth.

Two selves in one heart?

  • Jivatman: the individual self, embodied, acting, reaping the results of its deeds.
  • Paramatman: the supreme self, God, dwelling in every heart as inner controller.

> "There are two who enjoy the fruits of their good deeds in the world, having entered into the cave of the heart ... The knowers of Brahman call them shadow and light." (Katha 3:1, Paramananda)

  • Mundaka Upanishad 3.1.1 (paraphrase): two birds on one tree; one eats the fruit, the other watches without eating.
  • The big question: are the two really one, or really two?

Mission 2Diversity

Three Vedanta answers

  • Advaita (non-dual), Shankara (c. 788–820 by tradition): atman is Brahman. The sense of being a separate jiva is due to avidya (ignorance) and maya. Tat tvam asi means strict identity.
  • Vishishtadvaita (qualified non-dual), Ramanuja (trad. 1017–1137): souls are real and distinct, but are the body of Brahman, as a part to its whole. Tat tvam asi: Brahman is the inner self of "you", as a soul is of its body.
  • Dvaita (dual), Madhva (1238–1317): God (Vishnu) and souls are eternally different. Souls depend wholly on God. Madhva reads the text as atat tvam asi, "you are not that".
  • All three are Vedanta: they read the same Upanishads, Gita and Brahma Sutras.
Mission 2

Monist or monotheist?

  • Monist: ultimately only one reality; God and the soul are one (Advaita).
  • Monotheist: one God, and souls are different from God (Dvaita; also, in different ways, many Vaishnava bhakti traditions).
  • Vishishtadvaita sits between: one reality, with real internal distinctions.
  • Not "monism good, monotheism bad": each claims the Upanishads, and each shapes worship differently.
  • Your spec also asks whether Hinduism is polytheist or henotheist: see Mission 1.

Mission 2Evaluate

Is the atman identical with Brahman?

  • Claim: Atman is Brahman: the self is God, not a separate soul.
  • For: "You are that" and "this self ... is Brahman" (Shandilya Vidya) read most naturally as identity.
  • For: If Brahman is infinite and non-dual, nothing can stand outside it, so no second self.
  • Against: Experience: I suffer and do not know everything; Brahman does not suffer. Ramanuja: identity cannot explain this.
  • Against: Bhakti needs two: love and worship require a lover and a beloved (Madhva; much popular practice).
  • Judgement criteria: Which reading fits the most texts? Which explains experience of ignorance and suffering? Which supports the way Hindus actually live and worship?

Linking belief, source, development

Task: explain how tat tvam asi helps Hindus understand the self.

Mission 3

Bhakti marga: the way of loving devotion

  • Bhakti (from bhaj, "to share in", "to worship"): loving, personal devotion to God.
  • Bhakti marga: the way of devotion, one of the paths to moksha.
  • God is approached as saguna, with qualities: Vishnu (often as Krishna or Rama), Shiva, or the Goddess.
  • The devotee relates to God as servant, friend, parent, child or beloved.
  • The heart of it: love and surrender; liberation through God's grace.
  • Liberation is often seen as eternal loving closeness to God, not loss of self.
Mission 3Read the source

Offered with love, open to all

> "Whate'er thou doest, Prince! / Eating or sacrificing, giving gifts, / Praying or fasting, let it all be done / For Me, as Mine. So shalt thou free thyself / From Karmabandh, the chain which holdeth men / To good and evil issue" (Bhagavad Gita 9:27–28, Arnold)

> "… whoso will turn to Me, / Though they be born from the very womb of Sin, / Woman or man; sprung of the Vaisya caste / Or lowly disregarded Sudra,--all / Plant foot upon the highest path" (Bhagavad Gita 9:32, Arnold)

Mission 3

Origins: the Tamil saints

  • The movement began in South India, in Tamil, c. 6th–9th centuries CE.
  • Alvars: 12 Vaishnava poet-saints devoted to Vishnu (e.g. Nammalvar; Andal, a woman). Their hymns were gathered as the Four Thousand Divine Hymns.
  • Nayanars: 63 Shaiva saints devoted to Shiva (e.g. Appar, Sambandar, Sundarar; Karaikkal Ammaiyar, a woman). Their hymns form the Tevaram.
  • They sang in Tamil, not Sanskrit, travelling from temple to temple.
  • Background: the Gita's teaching on devotion; temple worship growing; competition with Jain and Buddhist communities in South India.
Mission 3

Vaishnava bhakti

  • Loving devotion to Vishnu and his avatars, especially Krishna and Rama.
  • Krishna as child, cowherd and lover; the love of the gopis (cowherd women) in the Bhagavata Purana, and of Radha in later poetry such as Jayadeva's Gita Govinda (12th century), as the model of the soul's longing for God.
  • Rama as the ideal king and the object of devotion (Tulsidas; Hanuman as the perfect devotee).
  • Key ideas: prapatti (surrender) and God's grace.
  • After Ramanuja, Sri Vaishnavas divided over grace: the monkey analogy (the baby monkey clings to its mother: the soul must make some effort; Vadakalai) and the cat analogy (the mother cat carries the kitten: grace alone; Tenkalai). (see Mission 5)
  • Today: ISKCON, Swaminarayan and many other movements.
Mission 3

Shaiva bhakti

  • Loving devotion to Shiva, often worshipped as the lingam.
  • Shiva is both fearsome ascetic and loving lord who chooses his devotees and saves them by grace.
  • The Nayanars' hymns (Tevaram) are still sung in Tamil Shiva temples; they shaped Shaiva Siddhanta theology.
  • Lingayats (Virashaivas), from Basava: each devotee wears a small lingam on the body, rejects temple priests and caste ranking, and worships Shiva directly.
  • Shaiva bhakti shows the same pattern as Vaishnava: local language, personal love, grace.
Mission 3

Yatra: pilgrimage as devotion

  • Yatra (pilgrimage): a journey to a holy place to receive darshan (sight) of the deity.
  • The saints themselves were pilgrims, singing at temple after temple.
  • Pandharpur wari: each year Varkari devotees walk about 250 km from Alandi and Dehu to Vithoba's temple, singing the hymns of Jnaneshvar and Tukaram, arriving for Ashadhi Ekadashi.
  • Vaishnava sites: Vrindavan and Mathura (Krishna), Ayodhya (Rama). Shaiva sites: Varanasi, the twelve jyotirlingas.
  • For the devotee, the hardship of the journey is itself an offering of love.

Mission 4

Karma: action and its results

  • Karma means action, and the results that action brings.
  • Every intentional act (in deed, word or thought) leaves an impression that will bear fruit, now or in a later life.
  • Brihadaranyaka Upanishad IV.4.5 (paraphrase): as a person acts, so they become; doing good, one becomes good; doing evil, one becomes evil. What a person desires shapes what they do.
  • Karma is not fate: today's choices make tomorrow's karma.
  • Karma is not a judge's sentence: most Hindus see it as a moral law of cause and effect, like a seed growing into a plant.
Mission 4Read the source

Samsara: the cycle of lives

  • Samsara: the cycle of birth, death and rebirth, driven by karma and by desire and ignorance.

> "To man's frame / As there come infancy and youth and age, / So come there raisings-up and layings-down / Of other and of other life-abodes, / Which the wise know, and fear not." (Gita 2:13, Arnold)

> "Some Jivas (individual Souls) enter wombs to be embodied; others go into immovable forms, according to their deeds and knowledge." (Katha 5:7, Paramananda)

  • What travels? The atman, with a subtle body (sukshma sharira) that carries the karmic impressions.

Mission 4

Three kinds of karma

  • Sanchita (accumulated): the whole store of karma from past lives, not yet bearing fruit. Arrows in the quiver.
  • Prarabdha (fruit-bearing): the part of that store that has begun to work out in this life: body, family, lifespan. An arrow already shot: it cannot be called back.
  • Kriyamana or agami (karma in the making): karma made now by present acts. The arrow being fitted to the bow: still in your hands.
  • Liberation, on the common Vedanta account: knowledge burns sanchita, stops new kriyamana sticking; prarabdha runs its course until this body dies.
  • Your spec's terms: accumulated karma, fruit-bearing karma, karma in the making.

Mission 4

Realms and types of rebirth

  • Rebirth can be as a god (deva), a human, an animal, or an immovable form such as a plant (Katha 5:7). Tradition speaks of 8,400,000 forms of life.
  • Realms (lokas): heavenly worlds (svarga), the earth, and hell-worlds (naraka). Time in heaven or hell is temporary: when the karma is used up, the soul is born again.
  • Chandogya Upanishad V.10.7 (paraphrase): those of good conduct soon enter a good birth; those of bad conduct a low birth, even as an animal.
  • So neither heaven nor hell is the final goal. The goal is moksha: release from the whole cycle (see Mission 5).
Mission 4

Why a human birth matters

  • Animals and other beings mostly live out their karma; they do not choose with full moral awareness.
  • Humans can reflect, choose and act deliberately: they make new karma and can seek moksha.
  • So a human birth is precious and rare: an opportunity, not a reward to waste.
  • Gods also live out good karma in heaven, and must be reborn when it is spent.
  • Your spec: "the significance of human rebirth".
Mission 4

Good karma still binds

  • Good deeds bring good results, but a good result is still a result: it ties the soul to more births, even in heaven. A golden chain still binds.
  • Sakam karma: action done with desire for its fruit (reward, praise, heaven). It binds.
  • Nishkam karma: action done without desire for its fruit, as duty or as offering to God. It does not bind.
  • Karma yoga: the path of selfless action; purifies the mind and leads towards liberation.
  • Your spec prints "nikam karma"; the usual form is nishkam (or nishkama) karma.

Mission 4Evaluate

Is karma fair?

  • Claim: Karma is a just way of explaining why lives turn out differently.
  • For: Everyone receives what their own acts produce: no favourites, no randomness.
  • For: It gives a reason to act well, and hope: the future is not fixed.
  • Against: No one remembers past lives; can it be just to suffer for what you cannot recall?
  • Against: It can be misused to blame the poor, the sick or the disabled for their condition; many Hindus reject that use.
  • Against: If my present is shaped by the past, how free am I? Response: prarabdha sets the situation, not the choice.
  • Judgement criteria: Does it fit experience? Does it leave room for free will? Does it lead to compassion or to blame?
  • Spec issues: karma and justice, free will and ethical behaviour; karma over multiple lives.

Mission 5

Moksha: release from the cycle

  • Moksha means release or liberation: freedom from samsara, the cycle of birth, death and rebirth.
  • In Hindu teaching, what binds the self is karma, desire and avidya (ignorance of the true self).
  • Moksha is believed to end all three: no more rebirth, even in heaven.
  • It is the highest of the four aims of life, and for many Hindus the purpose of life itself.
Mission 5

Free while still alive?

  • Jivanmukti: liberation while still in a body. Many traditions, especially Advaita, hold that the liberated live on until their fruit-bearing karma (prarabdha) runs out (see Mission 4).
  • The jivanmukta acts without selfish desire, is calm in pleasure and pain, and makes no new binding karma.
  • Videhamukti: liberation at death, when the body falls away.
  • Many theistic traditions see full liberation as coming after death, in God's presence.
Mission 5Diversity

What is moksha like?

  • Advaita (Shankara): realising that atman is Brahman. Nothing new is gained: ignorance ends. Like rivers entering the sea, losing name and form (Mundaka Upanishad 3.2.8, paraphrase).
  • Vishishtadvaita (Ramanuja): the soul keeps its identity and enjoys God's bliss for ever, serving him in his realm (Vaikuntha).
  • Dvaita (Madhva): the soul is eternally distinct and dependent; liberated souls enjoy bliss in God's presence.
  • Gaudiya Vaishnavism (Chaitanya; ISKCON today): loving service of Krishna (prema) is the highest goal, valued even above moksha.
  • Classical Samkhya and Yoga: kaivalya, the self's "aloneness", freed from matter.

Mission 5

Many paths to one goal

  • Jnana yoga (knowledge): study, reflection and meditation that remove ignorance. Suits the reflective. Central for Advaita.
  • Karma yoga (action): duty done without desire for its fruit (Gita 2:47). Suits the active.
  • Bhakti yoga (devotion): loving worship of a personal God. Suits the emotional; open to all, whatever their birth.
  • Raja yoga (meditation, after Patanjali) is often counted as a fourth path.
  • The Gita weaves the paths together; most Hindus follow more than one (detail in another lesson).
  • Spec issue: "the diversity of paths to achieving moksha".
Mission 5Read the source

Chosen by the Self

> "This Self cannot be attained by study of the Scriptures, nor by intellectual perception, nor by frequent hearing (of It); He whom the Self chooses, by him alone is It attained." (Katha 2:23, Paramananda)

> "Make Me thy single refuge! I will free / Thy soul from all its sins! Be of good cheer!" (Gita 18:66, Arnold)

Show answer

Q2 What does Krishna promise in Gita 18:66, and what does he ask?

Show answer

  • Theistic Vedantins read both verses as teaching grace: liberation is finally God's gift.
Mission 5

Grace and surrender

  • Anugraha: grace, God's free favour towards souls.
  • Prapatti: surrender, taking refuge (sharanagati) in God, giving up any claim to save oneself.
  • In the Sri Vaishnava tradition after Ramanuja (c. 1017–1137), prapatti is a direct way to liberation, open to all, including those who cannot follow the demanding path of bhakti yoga.
  • Bhakti and liberation: love of God both prepares the soul and is itself the goal.
  • Other traditions stress grace too: Shaiva Siddhanta teaches that Shiva's grace (arul) frees the soul.
Mission 5Diversity

The monkey and the cat

  • Sri Vaishnavas divided over how grace and surrender relate. Two images:
  • Monkey (markata-nyaya): the baby monkey clings to its mother as she leaps. God carries the soul, but the soul must hold on. Surrender is an act the soul must make. Vadakalai ("northern") school; Vedanta Desika (1268–1369).
  • Cat (marjara-nyaya): the mother cat picks up the kitten by the scruff; the kitten does nothing. Grace alone saves; even surrender is not a "means" but recognition of God's care. Tenkalai ("southern") school; Pillai Lokacharya (trad. 1205–1311) and Manavala Mamunigal (1370–1443).
  • Both schools need grace and surrender; they disagree on whether the soul's act contributes.
  • Compare Christian debates about grace and free will: a useful parallel, not an identity.

Mission 5Evaluate

Effort or grace?

  • Claim: Moksha is achieved by human effort rather than by grace.
  • For: Karma is a law of one's own actions; liberation must be earned by knowledge or selfless action.
  • For: Advaita: what binds is ignorance, so knowledge, not a gift from outside, frees.
  • Against: Katha 2:23 and Gita 18:66 point to grace and refuge.
  • Against: If liberation needs heroic effort, it is out of reach for most people; prapatti opens it to all.
  • Middle ground: the monkey image: grace carries, the soul clings.
  • Judgement criteria: Which fits the scriptures best? Which makes liberation fair and open to all? Does grace undermine moral effort?
  • Spec issue: moksha as the ultimate aim, in relation to samsara and karma.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Brahman is the one ultimate reality: nirguna (without qualities, beyond words) and saguna (with qualities, a personal God).
  • Kena 1:3–8: Brahman is what makes speech, mind and senses work, "not this which people worship here"; neti neti: not this, not this.
  • Sat-chit-ananda (being, consciousness, bliss), sat-chit-achit (God with selves and matter as his body), tat tvam asi (that you are).

Mission 2

  • In Hindu belief, atman is the eternal self; the jivatman is that self embodied and reborn (Gita 2:22; the Katha chariot shows the self as lord, the intellect as driver).
  • Tat tvam asi (Chandogya VI) is read as identity (Shankara), part and whole (Ramanuja) or difference (Madhva).
  • Brahman as sat-chit-ananda, beyond description (neti neti); monist and monotheist readings of the self.

Mission 3

  • Bhakti is loving devotion to a personal God; liberation comes by grace and surrender.
  • It began with the Tamil Alvars (Vishnu) and Nayanars (Shiva), c. 6th–9th centuries, and spread north through saints of every caste, singing in local languages.

Mission 4

  • Karma: action and its fitting results, a law within the universal order; samsara: rebirth driven by karma, desire and ignorance (Gita 2:13; Katha 5:7).
  • Three kinds: accumulated (sanchita), fruit-bearing (prarabdha), karma in the making (kriyamana/agami).
  • Human birth is the chance to act freely and seek moksha; selfless action (nishkam karma, karma yoga) does not bind (Gita 2:47).

Mission 5

  • Moksha: release from samsara by ending karma, desire and ignorance; jivanmukti is liberation while alive.
  • Advaita: realising identity with Brahman; theistic traditions: eternal loving relationship with God.
  • Grace (anugraha) and surrender (prapatti): the monkey (Vadakalai, the soul clings) and the cat (Tenkalai, the soul is carried).
