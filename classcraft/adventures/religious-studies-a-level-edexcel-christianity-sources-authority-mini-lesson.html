Mission 1

A library, not one book

  • Biblia (Greek): "the books". The Bible is a library written over many centuries by many authors.
  • Old Testament: built on the Jewish scriptures. New Testament: 27 books about Jesus and the first Christians.
  • Genres: law, history, poetry, prophecy, wisdom, gospel, letters, apocalyptic.
  • Protestant Bibles: 66 books. Catholic Bibles: 73, adding the deuterocanonical books (e.g. Tobit, Sirach, 1–2 Maccabees). Orthodox Bibles include a few more.
  • Most Protestants call those extra books the Apocrypha: useful reading, but not Scripture.
  • The canon (Greek kanon, "measuring rod"): the agreed list of books that count as Scripture.
Mission 1

How the Old Testament canon formed

  • The Hebrew Bible has three parts: Torah (Law), Prophets, Writings. Jewish communities accepted them in stages; the Writings were settled by about the 2nd century CE.
  • The older idea of a single "Council of Jamnia" (c. 90 CE) fixing the list is now widely doubted.
  • The Septuagint (Greek translation, 3rd–1st centuries BCE) included extra books. The early Church mostly read the Old Testament in Greek.
  • Jerome (c. 347–420) preferred the Hebrew list, but his Latin Vulgate came to include the extra books.
  • Reformation: Luther's Bible (1534) placed them in a separate section. Catholic Council of Trent (1546) declared them fully canonical.
Mission 1

How the New Testament canon formed

  • Earliest texts: Paul's letters (c. 50s–60s CE); the Gospels followed (c. 65–100 CE, in most scholars' dating).
  • Marcion (c. 140) accepted only an edited Luke and ten of Paul's letters; the Church's rejection of him pushed it to define its list.
  • Irenaeus (c. 180) defended exactly four Gospels.
  • Tests Christians used: apostolic origin (by or close to an apostle); orthodoxy (agreeing with the rule of faith); catholicity (widely used); use in worship.
  • Some books were disputed for a long time (e.g. Hebrews, James, 2 Peter, Revelation).
  • Athanasius, Festal Letter 39 (367), is the first known list of exactly our 27 books; North African councils (Hippo 393, Carthage 397) confirmed it for the West.
  • So the canon was recognised over time, not decreed in one moment.

Mission 1

Three views of the Bible's nature

  • The literal word of God: God guided every word; the Bible is inerrant (without error) in all it says, including history and science. Used as a direct rulebook. Held by many fundamentalist and some conservative evangelical Christians.
  • Inspired by God, written by humans: God worked through writers' language, culture and limits. Inerrant (or infallible) in matters of faith and salvation; genre and context matter. Held by most Catholic, Orthodox and mainstream Protestant Christians.
  • A human record of experiences of God: written by people of faith, inspired as great writing is inspired; open to reinterpretation. Held by many liberal Christians.
  • Impact on use: the first view reads teachings as timeless commands; the others ask what a passage meant then and how its principle applies now.
Mission 1

Objective inspiration

  • Objective view: inspiration lies in the text itself. God is its true author, so the words carry God's truth whether or not a reader feels it.
  • Strong forms: verbal, plenary inspiration (every word, in every part); at the extreme, a dictation model (rarely held by scholars).
  • Linked to inerrancy, e.g. the Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy (1978).
  • Catholic teaching (Dei Verbum 11, 1965): God is the author, and the human writers are true authors too; Scripture teaches without error the truth God willed for our salvation (paraphrase).
  • Strength: a fixed, public authority. Challenge: tensions between texts, and the obvious human features of the writing.
Mission 1

Subjective inspiration

  • Subjective view: inspiration lies in people: the writers' experience of God, and the Spirit at work in the reader.
  • The writers were inspired as people of faith; the text is their human response, shaped by their culture.
  • The Bible becomes God's word when God speaks through it to the reader. Karl Barth (1886–1968, Church Dogmatics I) called Scripture a witness to the Word of God, Jesus Christ (paraphrase).
  • Friedrich Schleiermacher (1768–1834) located inspiration in the writers' religious consciousness.
  • Strength: fits the human features of the text and believers' experience of being addressed by it. Challenge: who decides which parts "speak"? Authority may become private.

Mission 1Evaluate

Is the Bible the inspired word of God?

  • Claim: The Bible can be regarded as the inspired word of God.
  • For: Its own witness (2 Timothy 3:16; 2 Peter 1:21); its unity across centuries centred on Christ; the Church's long recognition; believers' experience of the Spirit speaking through it.
  • Against: Its human features (research, style, varied accounts); tensions between texts; ancient science; the canon was chosen by people over centuries.
  • Replies: The "inspired but human" view explains human features without denying inspiration.
  • The subjective view moves authority to the reader's encounter with God.
  • Judgement criteria: What does "word of God" mean: dictated words, or witness to Christ? Does inspiration require inerrancy? Can a human selection process be guided by God?

Mission 2

The Bible in Christian life

  • Worship: readings at most services; many churches follow a lectionary, a set cycle of readings (often over three years). Preaching explains them.
  • Sacraments and prayer: the words of the Eucharist and the Lord's Prayer come from Scripture; the Psalms are sung and prayed daily in many traditions.
  • Personal devotion: daily reading; Bible-study groups; lectio divina ("divine reading": read, meditate, pray, contemplate).
  • Decisions and ethics: Christians look to the Bible for moral advice and guidance.
  • Meaning and comfort: in illness, grief or fear, many turn to passages they know by heart.
Mission 2

From Scripture to the Church's ethical teaching

  • Churches build moral teaching on the Bible, but rarely by quoting one verse.
  • They draw out principles: love of God and neighbour, the image of God, justice for the poor, forgiveness.
  • They read Scripture with tradition (earlier teaching, creeds) and reason; Catholics add the Magisterium, e.g. the Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992).
  • Some issues the Bible never names (IVF, nuclear weapons, climate change): principles are applied to new cases.
  • Disagreements (e.g. on divorce or sexuality) often come from different models of interpretation, not different Bibles.
Mission 2

Revealed and natural theology

  • Revealed theology: knowledge of God that God gives: in Christ, through Scripture (and, for Catholics, Tradition).
  • Natural theology: knowledge of God by reason from the world: "the invisible things of him … are clearly seen, being perceived through the things that are made" (Romans 1:20, WEBBE).
  • Aquinas (c. 1225–1274): reason can show that God exists; revelation is needed for truths such as the Trinity. They complement each other.
  • Barth (1886–1968) rejected natural theology ("Nein!", 1934, against Emil Brunner): God is known only where God reveals himself, in Christ.
  • The Spirit in revelation: the Spirit inspired the writers (2 Peter 1:21) and guides readers "into all truth" (John 16:13, WEBBE). Mystics (e.g. Julian of Norwich, Revelations of Divine Love) and Quakers (the "inner light") stress direct experience of God. More in Mission 3.
Mission 2

Models of interpretation

  • Literal / fundamentalist: the text means what it plainly says; every statement is true as written.
  • Conservative (evangelical): inerrant or infallible, but read by genre and context (poetry as poetry, history as history).
  • Catholic: the literal sense (what the human author meant) and spiritual senses (allegorical, moral, anagogical), read in Tradition and guided by the Magisterium.
  • Liberal / historical-critical: a human document; study sources, authors, editing and context; apply its principles, not every rule.
  • Existential: what matters is how the text addresses me now (e.g. Bultmann, 1884–1976, "demythologising").

Mission 2Evaluate

Strengths and weaknesses of the models

  • Literal: clear and confident; takes the text seriously. But ignores genre; conflicts with science and history; hard to apply to texts that seem to clash.
  • Conservative: keeps high authority and uses scholarship on genre. But who decides what is poetry? Critics say it is selective.
  • Catholic / Church-guided: unity and continuity; guards against private readings. But can make the text depend on an institution; Protestants say it can override Scripture.
  • Liberal / critical: honest about human origins; frees principles from ancient culture. But risks reducing authority to the reader's preferences.
  • Judgement criteria: faithfulness to the text's genre and context; continuity with the Church; usefulness today; coherence with what Christians believe inspiration is.

Mission 3

Two routes to knowing God

  • Natural knowledge: what anyone can know of God through reason, conscience and the created world.
  • Revealed knowledge: what God makes known of himself, received by faith: in Jesus Christ, the Bible, the Church, the Spirit.
  • The study of each: natural theology and revealed theology.
  • The debate is not "reason or faith?" but: how much can reason reach, and what needs revelation?
  • And: can natural knowledge alone save anyone, or lead them to trust God?
Mission 3

An innate sense of the divine

  • Christians believe humans are made "in God's image" (Genesis 1:27), with an inbuilt capacity and desire to know God.
  • Openness to beauty and goodness as glimpses of God; intellect able to reason towards God.
  • Augustine: "our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee" (Confessions I.1).
  • John Calvin (1509–1564), Institutes of the Christian Religion (final edition 1559):
  • I.i: almost all wisdom is knowing God and knowing ourselves, and the two are bound together (paraphrase).
  • I.ii: true knowledge of God is piety: reverence and love, not mere information (paraphrase).
  • I.iii: every human has a sensus divinitatis, a natural sense of the divine (paraphrase).

Mission 3

The order of creation

  • "The heavens declare the glory of God. The expanse shows his handiwork." (Psalm 19:1)
  • Many Christians hold that something of God can be seen in the apparent design and purpose of nature.
  • Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274): from God's effects we can know "whether He exists", but not God's essence (Summa Theologiae I, q. 12, a. 12).
  • Aquinas: "faith presupposes natural knowledge, even as grace presupposes nature" (ST I, q. 2, a. 2).
  • Catholic teaching (First Vatican Council, Dei Filius, 1870): God can be known with certainty by the natural light of reason from created things (paraphrase).

Mission 3

Why natural knowledge is not enough

  • Sin: the "senseless heart was darkened" (Romans 1:21); humans suppress or twist what they know.
  • Finite minds: God's essence exceeds any creature's grasp.
  • Aquinas: "It is impossible to attain to the knowledge of the Trinity by natural reason." (ST I, q. 32, a. 1)
  • Calvin: the sense of the divine is real but corrupted into idolatry and superstition; Scripture is like spectacles that bring the blurred knowledge into focus (Institutes I.vi.1, paraphrase).
  • So: natural knowledge can leave people "without excuse", but cannot bring full knowledge or trust.
Mission 3

Faith, grace and the Spirit

  • Faith: trusting response to God's self-revelation.
  • Grace: God's free gift of knowledge of himself: "by grace you have been saved through faith … it is the gift of God" (Ephesians 2:8).
  • Through the Holy Spirit: "God revealed them through the Spirit. For the Spirit searches all things" (1 Corinthians 2:10).
  • The Spirit "will guide you into all truth" (John 16:13): inspiring Scripture, illuminating its readers, guiding the Church.
  • Mysticism: direct experience of God given by the Spirit, e.g. Julian of Norwich (c. 1343–after 1416), Revelations of Divine Love; Teresa of Ávila (1515–1582), The Interior Castle (1577). Churches test such experience against Scripture and tradition.
Mission 3

Revealed in Christ, the Church and the Bible

  • Jesus Christ: for Christians, the full and perfect revelation of God. "He who has seen me has seen the Father." (John 14:9)
  • God "has at the end of these days spoken to us by his Son" (Hebrews 1:2).
  • The Bible: the written witness to God's revelation.
  • The Bible as revealed theology: natural theology cannot tell the story of Israel, Christ and the Spirit; it can at most prepare for it.
  • The Church: the community where Scripture is read, the sacraments given and the faith handed on; in Catholic and Orthodox teaching it also interprets revelation with authority.
  • Revelation does not cancel reason: it tells reason what it could not find alone.
Mission 3Diversity

Barth versus Brunner, 1934

  • Emil Brunner (1889–1966), Nature and Grace (1934): sin damaged but did not destroy the image of God; a point of contact remains for revelation to address.
  • Karl Barth (1886–1968), No! (Nein!, 1934): there is no point of contact; God is known only where God reveals himself, in Jesus Christ.
  • Context: some "German Christians" used natural theology (nation, race, history) to back Nazism; Barth's Barmen Declaration (1934) rejected any source of revelation besides Jesus Christ.
  • Catholic: natural knowledge is real and grace perfects it (Aquinas; Vatican I).
  • Reformed (Calvin): natural knowledge is real but fruitless without Scripture and the Spirit.

"God can be known through reason alone."

  • Against: reason reaches that God exists, not who God is (Trinity, Incarnation): Aquinas himself.

Mission 4

The puzzle

  • Christians inherited strict monotheism: "The LORD is our God. The LORD is one." (Deuteronomy 6:4)
  • Yet the New Testament shows early Christians worshipping Jesus: in John's Gospel, Thomas calls him "My Lord and my God!" (John 20:28).
  • So: is Jesus God, or something less than God?
  • In the 4th century this became a crisis that split the Church and reached the emperor.
Mission 4

Arius: the Son is created

  • Arius (c. 256–336), a priest in Alexandria, Egypt; dispute from c. 318.
  • God alone is unbegotten, unchanging, without beginning.
  • So the Son (the Word, Logos) must have a beginning: created by God before time, the first and highest creature, through whom God made everything else.
  • Slogan of his party, condemned at Nicaea and attacked by Athanasius: "there was when he was not".
  • Texts he used: "the firstborn of all creation" (Colossians 1:15); "the Father is greater than I" (John 14:28); Proverbs 8:22 (Wisdom "created" in the Greek version).
  • His aim: to protect the oneness and changelessness of God.
Mission 4

Athanasius: only God can save

  • Athanasius (c. 296–373), deacon at Nicaea, then bishop of Alexandria from 328; exiled five times.
  • On the Incarnation (c. 318 or c. 335; date debated).
  • Humans, made in God's image, were sliding back into death and corruption through sin.
  • Only the Creator can re-create: a creature cannot restore the image of God.
  • "For He was made man that we might be made God" (On the Incarnation 54): sharing God's life (theosis, deification).
  • So if the Son is a creature, we are not saved. The Son must be fully God.

Mission 4

Nicaea, 325

  • Emperor Constantine called the first ecumenical (worldwide) council to end the dispute: roughly 250–300 bishops at Nicaea (ancient counts vary; tradition says 318).
  • The creed of Nicaea: the Son is "begotten, not made", "of one substance with the Father".
  • Key word homoousios: of the same substance (being) as the Father; not homoiousios, "of similar substance".
  • So the Son is co-equal with the Father and has no beginning.
  • Arius was condemned and exiled. Arian views stayed strong for decades; the Council of Constantinople (381) confirmed Nicaea.

Mission 4

From Nicaea to Chalcedon

  • Next question: if the Son is fully God, how is Jesus also human?
  • Apollinarius (d. c. 390): the divine Word replaced a human mind in Jesus. Reply, Gregory of Nazianzus: "For that which He has not assumed He has not healed" (Letter 101).
  • Nestorius (d. c. 451): stressed two natures so strongly he seemed to make two persons; rejected calling Mary Theotokos ("God-bearer"). Condemned at Ephesus (431).
  • Eutyches (d. c. 456): after the union, only one nature, the human absorbed into the divine. Condemned at Chalcedon (451).
  • Chalcedon set the boundaries between these views.
Mission 4Read the source

The Chalcedonian Definition (451)

> "one and the same Christ, Son, Lord, Only-begotten, to be acknowledged in two natures, inconfusedly, immutably, indivisibly, inseparably"

  • Two natures: fully divine and fully human, "truly God and truly man".
  • One person (hypostasis): not two sons, not a mixture.
  • Four "without"s: no confusion (against Eutyches), no change, no division, no separation (against Nestorius).

Mission 4

Two natures and the work of salvation

  • Fully God: only God can forgive sin, conquer death and restore God's image (Athanasius).
  • Fully human: only a human can stand in our place and heal every part of human nature (Gregory: "not assumed … not healed").
  • One person: so the one who dies on the cross is God the Son in his humanity; Mary is Theotokos.
  • Mediator: "one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus" (1 Timothy 2:5).
  • Significance for Christians: Jesus is worshipped; prayer is to the Father through him; his life is God's life shown in human form.
Mission 4Diversity

Who accepts Chalcedon?

  • Accept: Catholic, Eastern Orthodox and most Protestant churches.
  • Oriental Orthodox (Coptic, Armenian, Ethiopian, Eritrean, Syriac, Malankara): did not accept Chalcedon. They teach one united nature of the Word incarnate (miaphysite), fully divine and fully human, and reject the label "monophysite" (the view of Eutyches). Joint statements with the Eastern Orthodox (1989, 1990) found the faith largely shared.
  • Church of the East (Assyrian): did not accept Ephesus (431, the council that condemned Nestorius); its 1994 Common Christological Declaration with Rome affirmed a shared faith in Christ.
  • Non-Trinitarian groups today: e.g. Jehovah's Witnesses hold that Jesus is God's first creation, a view close to Arius'.

Mission 4Evaluate

"The Chalcedonian Definition explains how Jesus is both God and human."

  • For: it keeps both biblical truths (John 1:1, 1:14) and rules out every known error; it has united most churches for over 1,500 years.
  • For: it serves salvation: fully God to save, fully human to heal.
  • Against: it says what must be affirmed, not how: critics call it a set of boundaries, not an explanation.
  • Against: its Greek terms (nature, substance, hypostasis) depend on ancient philosophy; some modern theologians (e.g. John Hick, The Metaphor of God Incarnate, 1993) call two natures incoherent or a metaphor.
  • Against: it divided the Church: Oriental Orthodox churches rejected it.
  • Judge it by: Is a "boundary" enough? Does the language still make sense? Does it fit the Jesus of the Gospels?

Mission 5

The question: how is a sinner put right with God?

  • Justification: being put right (made or declared righteous) before God. From Greek dikaioō, "to justify, acquit".
  • Christians agree: all have sinned (Romans 3:23), and God's grace saves through Christ.
  • They have disagreed on how: by faith alone? By faith and works? Who decides who is saved?
  • 1517: Martin Luther (1483–1546), an Augustinian friar and professor at Wittenberg, posts the 95 Theses against the sale of indulgences.
  • The quarrel over indulgences became a quarrel over justification: the heart of the Reformation.
Mission 5

Luther's discovery

  • Luther tried to earn peace with God through monastic discipline, fasting and confession. It failed.
  • He came to hate the phrase "God's righteousness" (Romans 1:17; Latin iustitia Dei), reading it as the standard by which God punishes sinners.
  • Breakthrough (he recalls it in his 1545 Preface to his Latin works, paraphrase): it means the righteousness God gives as a gift, by which the righteous "live by faith".
  • He said it was as if he had been born again and entered paradise through open gates (paraphrase).
  • So righteousness is not achieved by us (active) but received (passive).
  • Salvation is justification by grace: God's free, undeserved favour, not a reward.

Q2 Which verse links justification with peace with God?

Show answer

Romans 5:1.

Mission 5

Sola fide: faith alone

  • Sola fide: we are justified by faith alone, not by faith plus works.
  • Faith here is trust (fiducia) in Christ's promise, not only agreeing to doctrines.
  • Imputed (credited) righteousness: Christ's righteousness is counted as ours, like a debt paid into our account. Luther called it an alien righteousness: it comes from outside us.
  • So the believer is simul iustus et peccator: at the same time righteous (in God's verdict) and a sinner (in himself).
  • Luther's German New Testament (1522) translated Romans 3:28 "by faith alone" (allein); he defended the word in his Open Letter on Translating (1530).
  • Sin: Luther, following Augustine, held that the fallen will is in bondage (The Bondage of the Will, 1525). It cannot turn to God unaided.

Mission 5

Calvin: grace and predestination

  • John Calvin (1509–1564), French Reformer in Geneva; Institutes of the Christian Religion (1536; final edition 1559).
  • Agreed with Luther: justification is God's acceptance of us, by imputation of Christ's righteousness, received by faith (Institutes III.11.2).
  • Fallen humans cannot choose God by themselves, so salvation depends wholly on God's choice: election.
  • > "By predestination we mean the eternal decree of God, by which he determined with himself whatever he wished to happen with regard to every man" (Institutes III.21.5, trans. Beveridge, 1845)
  • Double predestination: some are elected to eternal life; others are left to condemnation (the reprobate). He called the decree decretum horribile, an "awe-inspiring" or "dreadful" decree (III.23.7).
  • Sources: Romans 8:29–30; Romans 9:16; Ephesians 1:4–5.
  • Calvin urged believers to find assurance by looking to Christ, "the mirror" of election, not by speculating about the decree (III.24.5, paraphrase).
Mission 5

Christ the Mediator: Prophet, Priest and King

  • Calvin: because of sin, humans cannot reach God; only one who is both God and human can be the Mediator (Institutes II.12).
  • His threefold office (munus triplex, II.15):
  • Prophet: Christ is the full revelation of God; he brings God's word.
  • Priest: he offers himself as the sacrifice for sin, reconciling us to God, and still intercedes for us (Hebrews 7:25).
  • King: he rules and protects his Church, now spiritually and finally in glory.
  • Luther shared the core (Christ the one Mediator, 1 Timothy 2:5) but did not organise it this way.
  • Notions the spec lists, in one chain: sin → Christ the Mediator → his sacrifice → justification by grace → revelation of God in Christ.
Mission 5Diversity

Who is chosen?

  • Augustine (354–430) had already taught that God's grace chooses the elect; Calvin built on him.
  • Reformed (Synod of Dort, 1618–19): unconditional election, limited atonement, irresistible grace.
  • Arminians (after Jacobus Arminius, 1560–1609) and Methodists (John Wesley, 1703–1791): God elects those he foresees will believe; grace can be resisted; Christ died for all.
  • Lutherans: predestination to life by grace; no decree of damnation.
  • Catholic teaching: God predestines no one to hell (Catechism 1037, paraphrase); grace and free will cooperate. The Council of Orange (529) had already rejected predestination to evil.
  • Key text for the wider view: God "desires all people to be saved" (1 Timothy 2:4).

Mission 5

So why be good?

  • Reformers' answer: good works do not make us righteous; they follow from being made right, as fruit follows from a good tree (Matthew 7:17).
  • Luther, The Freedom of a Christian (1520): a Christian is free from the need to earn salvation, and so free to serve the neighbour in love (paraphrase).
  • "We are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works" (Ephesians 2:10).
  • Calvin: faith alone justifies, yet the faith that justifies is never alone; it always brings a new life (sanctification) (Antidote to Trent, 1547, paraphrase).
  • The elect are chosen "that we would be holy" (Ephesians 1:4): predestination is to holiness, not to licence.

For the Reformers, good conduct is the result of being put right with God, not the route to it. Part 2: the Catholic answer at Trent, and Sanders.

Mission 6: When Jesus died on the cross, did God suffer too, or can God not suffer at all?

Make a guess: When Jesus died on the cross, did God suffer too, or can God not suffer at all?

Mission 6

What "impassible" means

  • Impassibility: God cannot be acted upon or changed by anything outside God, and so does not undergo suffering or changing emotions (Latin pati, to suffer or undergo).
  • Linked to immutability: God does not change.
  • It does not mean God is cold or indifferent: classical theologians held that God loves perfectly, but without passion in the sense of being moved from outside.
  • Passibility: God can be affected and can suffer.
  • The modern question: can a God who cannot suffer really love a suffering world?
Mission 6Read the source

Texts on both sides

> "For I, the LORD, don't change" (Malachi 3:6, WEBBE)

> "In all their affliction he was afflicted, and the angel of his presence saved them." (Isaiah 63:9, WEBBE)

  • Other texts for unchanging: "God is not a man… that he should repent" (Numbers 23:19); "the Father of lights, with whom can be no variation" (James 1:17).
  • Other texts for feeling: God "was sorry that he had made man… and it grieved him in his heart" (Genesis 6:6); "My heart is turned within me, my compassion is aroused" (Hosea 11:8).

Q1 What does each quoted verse claim about God?

Show answer

Malachi: God does not change; Isaiah: God shares his people's affliction.

Q2 Why must a reader decide how to relate the two sets of texts?

Show answer

Read literally, they seem to conflict; one set has to interpret the other.

Mission 6

Why classical theology held impassibility

  • From the early Church to the Reformation, most theologians read the feeling texts as anthropomorphic: true of God's acts, not of changes in God.
  • Perfection: a perfect being lacks nothing; change would be gain or loss.
  • Reliability: an unchanging God is faithful (Malachi 3:6).
  • Thomas Aquinas: love and joy in God are acts of will, not passions; God "loves without passion" (Summa Theologica I, q. 20, a. 1).
  • The early Church rejected patripassianism (the view that the Father himself suffered on the cross).
  • Cyril of Alexandria (d. 444): the divine Word "suffered impassibly": he suffered in his own flesh, while remaining unchanged as God (paraphrase).

Mission 6

Moltmann: the context

  • Jürgen Moltmann (1926–2024), German Reformed theologian; Theology of Hope (1964), The Crucified God (1972; English 1974).
  • As a teenage soldier he saw Hamburg destroyed by bombing (1943); a prisoner of war 1945–48, he came to faith reading the Bible in the camp, and felt understood by the Christ who cried out on the cross.
  • After Auschwitz, he argued, Christians cannot speak of an untouched God watching from heaven.
  • He recalls Elie Wiesel's account of a boy hanged in Auschwitz: a prisoner asks "Where is God?", and Wiesel hears an inner answer: here, on the gallows (paraphrase of Night, 1958).
  • Moltmann's reply: God is in the suffering, not above it.
Mission 6

Moltmann: the crucified God

  • Your anthology extract (Moltmann on the suffering of God, McGrath's Christian Theology Reader): paraphrased here, never quoted.
  • The cross is an event within the Trinity: between the Father and the Son.
  • The Son suffers dying and abandonment by the Father. The Father suffers the grief of the Son's death. The suffering differs, so this is not patripassianism.
  • From this event the Spirit of love goes out to the godforsaken.
  • A God who cannot suffer, he argues, also cannot love: love means being open to being hurt (paraphrase).
  • So God chooses to suffer, out of love: suffering is not forced on God from outside.
  • Result: God is in solidarity with all victims; no one is outside God's reach.
Mission 6Read the source

The cry from the cross

> "At the ninth hour Jesus cried with a loud voice, saying, 'Eloi, Eloi, lama sabachthani?' which is, being interpreted, 'My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?'" (Mark 15:34, WEBBE)

  • Jesus quotes Psalm 22:1.
  • Moltmann: the heart of the cross; real godforsakenness, endured by God the Son.
  • Classical reading: the Son, in his human nature, takes on human abandonment; Psalm 22 ends in trust and praise.

Q1 Why is this verse central for Moltmann?

Show answer

It shows the Son abandoned by the Father: suffering inside God's own life.

Q2 How might an impassibilist read the same cry?

Show answer

As the Son suffering in his human nature and identifying with sinners, while the divine nature is unchanged; the psalm ends in trust.

Mission 6

Weinandy: defending impassibility

  • Thomas G. Weinandy (b. 1946), Capuchin Franciscan; Does God Suffer? (2000).
  • His context: by the late 20th century passibility had become, he argues, close to a new orthodoxy; he defends the older view.
  • Impassibility does not make God cold: God is love fully in act (pure act); God's love never needs to grow, so it cannot be increased or lessened by suffering.
  • Suffering involves losing some good. A God who suffered would lack something, and so could not be the perfect source of healing.
  • A suffering God, he argues, is no help to sufferers: what they need is a God able to free them from suffering, not one trapped in it.
Mission 6

Weinandy: how God suffers in Jesus

  • The answer is the incarnation: the Son of God truly lives a human life.
  • So "the Son of God suffered and died" is true: the one who suffers is God the Son, as man, in his human nature.
  • This is the communication of idioms (communicatio idiomatum): what is true of either nature is said of the one person.
  • God does not suffer in the divine nature, but God really experiences suffering as a human being in Jesus.
  • Contrast: Moltmann places suffering in the relations of the Trinity itself; Weinandy places it in the Son's humanity.

Mission 6Evaluate

Does God suffer in or through Jesus?

  • Claim: God suffers, not only in Jesus' humanity, but in God's own life.
  • For (Moltmann): It makes sense of texts where God grieves (Genesis 6:6; Hosea 11:8; Isaiah 63:9).
  • For: It answers Auschwitz with God's solidarity; love that cannot suffer may not be love.
  • Against (classical): a suffering God changes and lacks some good; the feeling texts are anthropomorphic.
  • Against (Weinandy): a suffering God could not rescue sufferers; God suffers in Jesus as man, not in the divine nature.
  • Against: Moltmann may come close to patripassianism, or make suffering part of God's nature.
  • Implications of a suffering God: comfort and solidarity for sufferers; but questions about God's power, perfection and ultimate victory over evil.
  • Judgement criteria: Which set of biblical texts interprets the other? Can love be perfect without being affected? Does a suffering God help or hinder hope?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The canon is the agreed list of books: Catholic Bibles have 73, Protestant Bibles 66.
  • It was recognised over centuries: the Old Testament from Jewish and Greek traditions, the New Testament by tests of apostolic origin, orthodoxy and use (Athanasius, 367).
  • Views of the Bible range from the literal, inerrant word of God, through "inspired by God but written by humans", to a human record of experiences of God.
  • Objective inspiration places inspiration in the text; subjective inspiration in the writers' and readers' experience.

Mission 2

  • Christians use the Bible in worship, devotion, ethics and times of need.
  • Churches turn Scripture into ethical teaching through principles, tradition and reason; models of interpretation (literal, conservative, Catholic, liberal, existential) lead to different guidance.
  • The Bible is revealed theology; natural theology is knowledge of God through reason and nature; the Spirit inspires writers and illuminates readers.

Mission 3

  • Natural knowledge: an innate sense of the divine (image of God; Calvin's sensus divinitatis) and the order of creation (Psalm 19; Romans 1; Acts 17; Aquinas).
  • Revealed knowledge: for Christians, it comes by faith and grace through the Spirit, fully in Jesus Christ, through the Bible and the Church.
  • Catholic, Reformed and Barthian views differ on how much natural knowledge survives sin.

Mission 4

  • Arius: the Son is the first, highest creature ("there was when he was not"). Athanasius: only God can re-create and save, so the Son is fully God.
  • Nicaea (325): the Son is homoousios, "of one substance with the Father", "begotten, not made".
  • Chalcedon (451): one person in two natures, without confusion, change, division or separation; accepted by most churches, not by the Oriental Orthodox.

Mission 5

  • Luther: justification by grace through faith alone; Christ's righteousness imputed; the believer is righteous and sinner at once (Romans 1:17; 5:1; Ephesians 2:8–9; Galatians 2:16).
  • Calvin: salvation rests on God's eternal election (double predestination); others (Arminians, Methodists, Catholics) reject unconditional reprobation.
  • Good works matter as the fruit and evidence of faith, not as a means of earning salvation.

Mission 6

  • Impassibility: God is not changed or made to suffer by anything outside God; classical theology read the Bible's "feeling" texts as anthropomorphic.
  • Moltmann (The Crucified God, 1972): after Auschwitz, God suffers in the cross: the Son dies forsaken, the Father grieves; a God who cannot suffer cannot love.
  • Weinandy (Does God Suffer?, 2000): God is impassible, but the Son of God truly suffers as man in Jesus.
