Mission 1

Line after line of 'If'. Advice, or a test no one could pass?

Make a guess: Line after line of 'If'. Advice, or a test no one could pass?

Poetry · "If—"Screen 1

"If—" in brief

  • Rudyard Kipling (1865–1936) first published "If—" in Rewards and Fairies (1910), a book of stories and poems for children.
  • It is written as a father's advice to his son.
  • Kipling said it was inspired by Leander Starr Jameson, who led the failed Jameson Raid in southern Africa (1895–96).
  • In a nationwide BBC poll in the mid-1990s it was voted the UK's favourite poem.

Poetry · "If—"Screen 3

One sentence, thirteen tests

  • The whole poem is one sentence: 32 lines of conditions, each starting "If". The main clause waits until line 31.
  • Anaphora (repeating "If you can") stacks up the tests, so the reader waits for the reward.
  • Antithesis sets opposites side by side: you trust yourself / "all men doubt you"; "Triumph and Disaster".
  • Four eight-line stanzas, rhymed ababcdcd, in iambic pentameter. Lines of 11 syllables (ending "about you") alternate with lines of 10.

Poetry · "If—"Screen 6

The reward, and two ways to read it

  • Self-control and endurance: lose everything and "never breathe a word"; push on when there's "nothing in you" but the Will.
  • "the unforgiving minute" is a metaphor: time won't forgive a wasted second.
  • The reward comes last: "Yours is the Earth", and, "which is more", manhood. Only now do we learn the speaker is a father.
  • Readings: many find it inspiring; others see a narrow, demanding ideal. Olivier Rey (2006) read it as a father imposing impossible conditions.

Anthology poetry · PianoScreen 1

The poem in context

  • D. H. Lawrence (1885–1930) grew up in Eastwood, Nottinghamshire, the son of a miner.
  • He was very close to his mother, Lydia, who died in 1910.
  • "Piano" was published in New Poems (1918). An earlier draft of the poem was written before his mother's death.
  • Context isn't assessed in Section B (AO2 and AO3), but it helps you understand the speaker's grief.

Mission 3

True love never changes, says Shakespeare. So why end on 'If'?

Make a guess: True love never changes, says Shakespeare. So why end on 'If'?

Poetry · Sonnet 116Screen 1

Sonnet 116 in brief

  • First published in 1609, in the collection Shakespeare's Sonnets (154 sonnets).
  • Many sonnets in the collection are addressed to a young man, but 116 names no one: it defines love in general.
  • The speaker argues that true love never changes, whatever happens to the lovers.
  • The opening echoes the marriage service in the Book of Common Prayer, which asks if anyone knows an impediment (a reason the couple can't marry).

Poetry · Sonnet 116Screen 4

Form: an argument in fourteen lines

  • A Shakespearean sonnet: three quatrains and a rhyming couplet, abab cdcd efef gg.
  • Iambic pentameter: which AL | ters WHEN | it AL | ter A | tion FINDS.
  • The quatrains build the case like an argument: what love isn't, what it is, what it defeats.
  • The volta comes at the couplet: from defining love to staking his own word on it.
  • Lines 6 and 8 end on an extra soft syllable ("shaken", "taken"): a feminine ending.

Poetry · Sonnet 116Screen 6

Language: love against Time

  • Negatives define love by what it isn't: "love is not love", "O no", "Love’s not Time’s fool", "alters not".
  • Polyptoton (one root, different forms): "alters" / "alteration", "remover" / "remove". Change is named, then refused.
  • Time is personified as a reaper whose "bending sickle" cuts down "rosy lips and cheeks". Beauty falls; love doesn't.
  • "the marriage of true minds": love of minds, not just bodies.

Mission checkScreen 9

?True love never changes, says Shakespeare. So why end on 'If'?

The couplet is a wager. If his definition is wrong, he "never writ" and "no man ever loved", and we know both are false, so the "If" works as proof. Strong answers show how the negatives, the fixed images and the couplet build certainty, and may weigh whether that certainty sounds calm or insistent.

She said 'I love thee true'. So why is the knight left dying?

Make a guess: She said 'I love thee true'. So why is the knight left dying?

Keats · La Belle Dame sans MerciScreen 1

The poem in brief

  • John Keats (1795–1821) wrote it in April 1819, in a letter to his brother George in America. It was first printed, without Keats's name, in Leigh Hunt's The Indicator on 10 May 1820.
  • The title means the beautiful lady without pity. It comes from a 15th-century French poem by Alain Chartier.
  • It is a ballad: an old, song-like form that tells a story, here a medieval tale of a knight and a faery.
  • Your anthology prints Keats's letter version, which begins "knight-at-arms".

10–11He dreamed of pale kings and warriors who cried that she had him "in thrall"; he woke on the hillside.

Keats · La Belle Dame sans MerciScreen 4

Form: a ballad that goes in a circle

  • Three four-beat lines (iambic tetrameter), then a two-beat line: "And no birds sing." It stops short, like the knight's life.
  • Rhyme: ABCB, as in many folk ballads.
  • Frame: a speaker questions the knight (1–3); he answers (4–12).
  • Circular structure: stanza 12 echoes the words of stanza 1. He ends where he began, trapped.
  • The dream (10–11) is the turn: "Pale warriors, death-pale were they all;". Her earlier victims?

Keats · La Belle Dame sans MerciScreen 6

Language and the lady

  • Illness: "I see a lily on thy brow" (deathly white) and a "fading rose" on his cheek.
  • Pathetic fallacy: withered sedge and silent birds mirror him; the full "granary" shows the world ready for winter, but not him.
  • We see the lady only through him: wild eyes, sweet food, tears.
  • Readings: an enchantress; a love that can't last; grief for Keats's brother Tom, who died in December 1818. Amy Lowell argued it isn't autobiographical.

Mission 5

A poem made almost entirely of questions. Does Blake answer them?

Make a guess: A poem made almost entirely of questions. Does Blake answer them?

Poetry · "The Tyger"Screen 1

"The Tyger" in brief

  • William Blake (1757–1827) published "The Tyger" in Songs of Experience (1794).
  • Its partner poem is "The Lamb" in Songs of Innocence (1789): a gentle creature beside a fierce one.
  • The speaker talks directly to the tiger (apostrophe) and asks who, or what, could have made it.
  • "Tyger" is Blake's own spelling; your anthology keeps it.
Extract Poetry · "The Tyger"Screen 2

Stanzas 1–3

Poetry · "The Tyger"Screen 3

Form: a hammering beat

  • Six quatrains in rhyming couplets (aabb).
  • Mostly trochaic tetrameter with the last weak beat dropped: TY-ger | TY-ger | BURN-ing | BRIGHT. Seven syllables, four heavy beats: they fall like hammer blows.
  • Line 4 shifts to an iambic rhythm: could FRAME thy FEAR-ful SYM-me-TRY.
  • Circular structure: stanza 6 repeats stanza 1, but "Could frame" becomes "Dare frame".

Poetry · "The Tyger"Screen 6

Language: the maker at the forge

  • An extended metaphor of a blacksmith's forge: "hammer", "chain", "furnace", "anvil". Making the tiger is fierce, dangerous work.
  • Many readers hear allusions: "On what wings dare he aspire?" to Icarus, "What the hand dare sieze the fire?" to Prometheus, who stole fire from the gods.
  • "When the stars threw down their spears" is often read as the fall of the rebel angels.
  • The central question: "Did he who made the Lamb make thee?" Could one creator make gentleness and terror?

Mission 6

A duke shows a visitor a portrait. What is he really saying?

Make a guess: A duke shows a visitor a portrait. What is he really saying?

Poetry · "My Last Duchess"Screen 1

A duke, a portrait, a visitor

  • Browning published the poem in 1842. It is headed "Ferrara", a city in Italy.
  • The Duke is usually identified as Alfonso II d'Este, Duke of Ferrara. His first wife died in 1561, aged 16; poisoning was rumoured.
  • He then sought a new bride, negotiating through the Count of Tyrol.
  • In the poem the Duke shows the Count's envoy (messenger) a portrait of his "last Duchess" while they arrange his next marriage.
Extract Poetry · "My Last Duchess"Screen 2

Poetry · "My Last Duchess"Screen 3

A dramatic monologue in disguised couplets

  • A dramatic monologue: one character speaks to a silent listener (the envoy), and gives away more than he means.
  • We only hear the Duke, so we judge him from his own words.
  • Rhyming couplets in iambic pentameter: that's MY | last DUCH | ess PAINT | ed ON | the WALL.
  • Heavy enjambment hides the rhymes ("I call / That piece a wonder"): the speech sounds casual, but the strict form shows control.

On the surface the Duke is showing off a painting. Underneath, he reveals his pride, his jealousy and his need to control, and hints that he had his wife silenced. Because he says this to the envoy arranging his next marriage, many readers hear a warning about how the next Duchess must behave.

Mission 7

She begs to be remembered, then says forget her. Why?

Make a guess: She begs to be remembered, then says forget her. Why?

Relationships · "Remember"Screen 1

A sonnet about being remembered

  • Christina Rossetti (1830–1894) wrote "Remember" in 1849, aged 18.
  • It was published in 1862 in her first collection, Goblin Market and Other Poems.
  • Her Christian faith was central to her life. Belief in an afterlife helps explain why death is pictured as going away, not as an ending.
  • The speaker addresses a loved one, perhaps a fiancé ("our future that you planned"), about how to remember her after she dies.
Extract Relationships · "Remember"Screen 2

"Remember" (the whole sonnet)

Relationships · "Remember"Screen 3

Two halves, two requests

  • "Remember" is a Petrarchan sonnet: an octave (8 lines) and a sestet (6 lines).
  • Rhyme scheme: ABBAABBA CDDECE.
  • The octave repeats one request: remember me.
  • The volta (turn) comes with "Yet" in line 9. The sestet changes the request: if you forget for a while, "do not grieve".
  • The last two lines weigh being forgotten against being remembered with sadness.
Relationships · "Remember"Screen 4

Gentle words for death

  • Euphemism: death is "gone away" into "the silent land", a quiet journey rather than an ending.
  • Physical closeness is lost: "When you can no more hold me by the hand".
  • "Nor I half turn to go yet turning stay" catches the hesitation of a parting.
  • Repetition: "remember" appears five times, a plea that grows softer.
  • "the darkness and corruption" is the one harsh image: the body's decay, which some readers also link to sin.

?She begs to be remembered, then says forget her. Why?

The octave asks to be remembered again and again. At the volta, love turns selfless: "Better by far you should forget and smile / Than that you should remember and be sad." Readers argue about how calm she really is, but the change itself is deliberate.

Recap

Big ideas

  • "If—" (1910) is one 32-line sentence: a father's list of conditions, with the reward held back to the final lines.
  • Anaphora, antithesis and personification ("Triumph and Disaster", "impostors") present an ideal of calm self-control and endurance.
  • Compare it with other Part 3 poems about advice, ideals or parents and children; Section B rewards methods and links, not context.
  • "Piano" (New Poems, 1918) moves from a present-day song to a childhood memory, three times, until the past takes over.
  • The speaker resists ("In spite of myself", "insidious", "Betrays") but is overwhelmed; the simile "like a child" undoes his adult self.
  • Long, flowing lines in rhyming couplets and sound words (boom, tingling, tinkling, clamour) make the memory physical.
  • In Section B, compare methods (AO2) and make links (AO3); context is background only.
  • Sonnet 116 (1609) defines true love as unchanging: a fixed mark, a guiding star, stronger than Time.
  • Three quatrains build an argument, and the couplet stakes the poet's own word on it.
  • Compare it with other Part 3 poems about love, constancy and argument; Section B rewards methods and links, not context.
  • Keats wrote the ballad in April 1819 (printed 1820); a speaker questions a sick knight, who tells of a faery lady, a dream of pale kings and waking alone.
  • The short last lines, the ABCB rhyme and the circular structure make the knight's state feel stuck and final.
  • We see the lady only through the knight, so her love and her cruelty are open to interpretation.
  • In Section B you compare two poems with clean copies: plan the link before you write.
  • "The Tyger" (Songs of Experience, 1794) asks who could make a creature so beautiful and terrifying; its partner is "The Lamb".
  • Rhyming couplets, a heavy trochaic beat and the forge metaphor make creation sound powerful and dangerous; the circular ending swaps "Could" for "Dare".
  • Compare it with other Part 3 poems about awe, danger or questioning; Section B rewards methods and links, not context.
  • "My Last Duchess" (1842) is a dramatic monologue: the Duke speaks to a silent envoy and reveals more than he means.
  • Disguised couplets, enjambment and euphemism ("I gave commands") show a man whose calm speech hides total control.
  • In your exam, focus on comparing methods and ideas; context isn't assessed in Section B.
  • "Remember" (1849, published 1862) is a Petrarchan sonnet in which a speaker imagines her death and asks her lover to remember her.
  • The volta at "Yet" turns the request around: his happiness matters more than her memory.
  • In Section B, compare language, form and structure with another anthology poem.

Key quotations to learn

  • If you can keep your head when all about you (line 1) The first test: calm when others panic.
  • If you can meet with Triumph and Disaster / And treat those two impostors just the same; (lines 11–12) Personification: success and failure are both fakes.
  • And never breathe a word about your loss; (line 20) Self-control: loss borne in silence.
  • Except the Will which says to them: ‘Hold on!’ (line 24) Endurance beyond physical strength.
  • If you can fill the unforgiving minute (line 29) Metaphor: time doesn't forgive waste.
  • And—which is more—you’ll be a Man, my son! (line 32) The delayed reward; the speaker revealed as a father.
  • Softly, in the dusk, a woman is singing to me; (stanza 1, line 1) The gentle present that triggers memory.
  • A child sitting under the piano, in the boom of the tingling strings (stanza 1, line 3) Vivid, physical memory.
  • In spite of myself, the insidious mastery of song (stanza 2, line 1) Memory as a sly power he resists.
  • And hymns in the cosy parlour, the tinkling piano our guide. (stanza 2, line 4) Warm, safe childhood home.
  • my manhood is cast / Down in the flood of remembrance (stanza 3, lines 3–4) The adult self overwhelmed.
  • I weep like a child for the past. (stanza 3, line 4) The simile that ends the poem.
  • Let me not to the marriage of true minds / Admit impediments (lines 1–2) Echo of the marriage service; love of minds.
  • Which alters when it alteration finds, (line 3) Polyptoton: change named, then refused.
  • O no, it is an ever-fixed mark (line 5) Metaphor: love as an unmoving sea-mark.
  • It is the star to every wandering bark, (line 7) Metaphor: love guides the lost.
  • Love’s not Time’s fool, though rosy lips and cheeks (line 9) Time personified; beauty fades, love doesn't.
  • If this be error and upon me proved, / I never writ, nor no man ever loved. (lines 13–14) The couplet's wager.
  • O what can ail thee, knight-at-arms, (Stanza 1 (questioner)) The frame: the poem begins with a question.
  • Alone and palely loitering? (Stanzas 1 and 12) Repeated at the end: the circular structure.
  • And no birds sing. (Stanzas 1 and 12) Short line and bleak, silent nature.
  • And sure in language strange she said, (Stanza 7 (knight)) He reports words he may not have understood.
  • Pale warriors, death-pale were they all; (Stanza 10 (knight's dream)) Her earlier victims mirror the pale knight.
  • Thee hath in thrall (Stanza 10 (dream warriors)) The dream's verdict: he is enslaved by her.
  • Tyger! Tyger! burning bright (lines 1 and 21) Apostrophe and fire imagery: dazzling and dangerous.
  • Could frame thy fearful symmetry? (line 4) Beauty and terror joined; "Could" becomes "Dare" in line 24.
  • What the hammer? what the chain? (line 13) The forge metaphor: violent, powerful creation.
  • When the stars threw down their spears (line 17) Often read as the fall of the rebel angels.
  • Did he who made the Lamb make thee? (line 20) The central question: one maker for both?
  • Dare frame thy fearful symmetry? (line 24) The circular ending, more fearful than before.
  • That's my last Duchess painted on the wall, (line 1) "last": one of a series; she is now just a possession.
  • The curtain I have drawn for you, but I) (line 10) He controls who sees her.
  • She looked on, and her looks went everywhere. (line 24) His jealousy at her friendliness.
  • My gift of a nine-hundred-years-old name (line 33) Pride in rank and family.
  • This grew; I gave commands; / Then all smiles stopped together. (lines 45–46) Euphemism hiding what he ordered.
  • Taming a sea-horse, thought a rarity, (line 55) The final image of control.
  • Remember me when I am gone away, (line 1) The first request; death as going away.
  • Gone far away into the silent land; (line 2) Euphemism: death as a quiet place.
  • Nor I half turn to go yet turning stay. (line 4) Hesitation at parting.
  • You tell me of our future that you planned: (line 6) The future the couple has lost.
  • the darkness and corruption (line 11) The one harsh image of death.
  • Better by far you should forget and smile (line 13) The selfless reversal.
