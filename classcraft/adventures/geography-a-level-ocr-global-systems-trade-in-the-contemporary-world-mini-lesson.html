Mission 1: If goods still dominate world trade, why have services and trade between developing countries grown faster?

Make a guess: If goods still dominate world trade, why have services and trade between developing countries grown faster?

Specification: what this mission covers

Mission 1

Three components of trade

  • Merchandise: physical goods: raw materials, food, fuels, manufactures. World exports, 2024: $24.62 trillion.
  • Services: work done for others across borders: finance, transport, tourism, software. World exports, 2024: $8.88 trillion.
  • Capital: investment that crosses borders, such as foreign direct investment (FDI): $1.6 trillion in 2025.
Quick check
?A software firm in Bengaluru, India, runs IT support for a London bank. Which component of trade is this?
Mission 1

What the world trades

  • 2024 merchandise by value: manufactures 68.8%, fuels 11.2%, food 9.1%, ores and metals 4.0%, agricultural raw materials 1.2%.
  • Manufactures dominate world trade, but many developing countries export mainly primary goods.
  • 95 of 142 developing countries depended on commodities for more than 60% of merchandise exports in 2019–21.
Mission 1

Capital: where FDI goes

  • 2025: developed economies received about $723 billion; developing economies $901 billion, of which developing Asia $644 billion.
  • The least developed countries received $43 billion: 2.7% of the world total.
  • The top 20 host economies took more than 80%. Capital is far more concentrated than population.
Mission 1

Intra-regional trade: the EU

  • Intra-regional trade: trade between countries in the same region.
  • The EU's single market allows "free movement of goods, services, capital and persons".
  • 2025: trade with other EU members made up 83% of Luxembourg's goods trade, 78.8% of Czechia's and 78.3% of Slovakia's.
  • Germany 55.9% and France 53.6% (our calculation); Ireland 33.3% (our calculation) and Cyprus 28% trade more outside the EU.
Mission 1

Inter-regional trade

  • Inter-regional trade: trade between world regions.
  • China's share of world goods exports: 3.8% (2000), 14.5% (2024). Much of it goes to other regions.
  • Apple's products are assembled mainly by partners in Asia; its largest sales segment is the Americas: $178.4 billion of $416.2 billion in 2025.
  • Patterns shift: since 2016 the share of US flows with China has fallen by 42%.
Quick check
?Which is an example of intra-regional trade?
Mission 1

South-South trade

  • South-South trade: trade between developing countries (UNCTAD's group; in OCR's terms, mostly EDCs and LIDCs).
  • Its share of world trade rose from about 17% in 2010 to 21% in 2022.
  • Value: US$5.3 trillion in 2022, against US$3.9 trillion in 2019.
  • It now equals "more than 50 per cent of the trade between developed countries (North-North)".
Quick check
?South-South trade rose from US$3.9 trillion (2019) to US$5.3 trillion (2022). By what percentage did it grow?
Mission 1

Why South-South trade has grown

  • Rising economies: China's share of world goods exports rose from 3.8% (2000) to 14.5% (2024); developing Asia drew $644 billion of FDI in 2025.
  • Regional links: South-South trade is a large share of developing regions' own trade: about 40% in Latin America, about 60% in South and East Asia.
  • New agreements: the African Continental Free Trade Area aims "to eliminate trade barriers and boost intra-Africa trade".
  • Not one-way: compared with 2021, the South-South share "slightly decreased for most regions", mainly because of lower trade with China.
Mission 1

The growth of services

  • World service exports: $0.88 trillion (1990), $8.88 trillion (2024): about 10 times as large. Goods: about 7 times (our calculation).
  • ICT services rose from 5.3% of service exports in 2000 to 14.8% in 2024.
  • Many services can be delivered digitally, so distance matters less.
  • Since 2020, though, services trade has recovered more slowly than goods trade.
  • Exam move (OCR "Explain"): link each growth trend to a named cause and a dated figure.
Mission check

?A firm in Kenya sells accounting software, delivered online, to small businesses in Nigeria and Ghana. Which factors from this mission best explain why trade like this is growing?

Goods (merchandise) are still the largest part of trade, but services have grown faster, helped by digital delivery, and trade between developing countries has risen from about 17% to 21% of world trade as Asian economies grew. Capital flows remain concentrated in a few hosts.

Mission 2

Two ways to measure

  • Value of exports: goods and services sold abroad, in US$. Compare countries per person as well as in total.
  • Human Development Index (HDI): combines life expectancy at birth, expected and mean years of schooling, and gross national income per person.
  • HDI runs from 0 to 1: Norway 0.970, Vietnam 0.766, Sierra Leone 0.467 (2023).

Mission 2

Read the pattern with care

  • Total exports tell a weaker story: China exported the most (US$3,545 billion) but ranks 78th on the HDI. Total exports vs HDI: rank correlation 0.71 (our calculation).
  • Correlation is not causation: trade can raise incomes, but richer, healthier, better-educated countries also produce more to sell.
  • Unequal shares: the least developed countries (LDCs) held 14.4% of world population but sold only 1.0% of world exports in 2023 (our calculation).
  • 95 of 142 developing countries were commodity-dependent in 2019–2021.

Mission 2

How trade can promote development

  • Money: foreign direct investment into developing economies reached US$901 billion in 2025: factories, jobs, tax revenue.
  • People: migrants abroad sent US$665 billion in remittances to low- and middle-income countries in 2024.
  • Ideas and technology: foreign firms bring machinery, skills and management methods that local firms can copy.
  • Stability: in 1950 France proposed pooling coal and steel with Germany so that war would be "not merely unthinkable, but materially impossible"; that trade link began today's EU.
Mission 2

Vietnam: trade-led development

  • Exports: US$2.3 billion (1990) → US$376 billion (2023), in current dollars: about 161 times (our calculation).
  • HDI: 0.499 (1990) → 0.766 (2023).
  • People living on under $3.00 a day: 57.5% (1992) → 1.6% (2022).
  • Flows at work: inward investment (money), foreign factories and know-how (technology and ideas), workers moving from farms to export jobs (people).
Quick check
?What can you safely conclude from Vietnam's data?
Mission 2

How trade can deepen inequality

  • Money: commodity exporters depend on prices they do not control; when prices fall, export income falls.
  • Gains can stay with a few: in Sierra Leone in 2005, "a disproportionate share of income from diamond mining still found its way into private hands".
  • People: low-cost export jobs can mean unsafe work. The Rana Plaza collapse in Bangladesh's ready-made garment sector, 24 April 2013, "killed 1,138 workers and left more than 2,500 injured".
  • Technology: countries that sell mainly raw materials capture less of the value than those that make the finished goods.
Mission 2

How trade can fuel conflict and injustice

  • Conflict: "conflict diamonds" are rough diamonds "used by rebel movements or their allies to finance conflict aimed at undermining legitimate governments".
  • Governance response: the Kimberley Process (2003) bans rough-diamond trade with non-participants.
  • Injustice: workers far down the supply chain bear the risks (Rana Plaza), while most of the value is captured by brands and buyers.
  • The same flows that build growth (money, people, ideas, technology) can carry these harms.

Mission check

?Country Y earns most of its exports from one mineral. During a price boom its export value rises five-fold, but its HDI barely changes and most mining income goes to a few firms. Then the price falls. Which conclusion is best supported?

Across countries, more exports per person goes with higher HDI, and Vietnam shows trade can drive development. But when trade depends on one commodity, or gains stay with a few, flows of money, people and technology can widen inequality or fund conflict instead.

Mission 3: In 2023 the Panama Canal cut the number of ships it let through. In 2026 conflict choked oil shipping through the Strait of Hormuz. Has technology really made the world "shrink"?

Make a guess: In 2023 the Panama Canal cut the number of ships it let through. In 2026 conflict choked oil shipping through the Strait of Hormuz. Has technology really made the world "shrink"?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 2.2.1-2.a technology, transport and communications increasing connectivity of global supply chains
Mission 3

A "shrinking world"

  • Time–space convergence: places get closer in travel or communication time as technology improves.
  • Cost–space convergence: moving goods and information gets cheaper.
  • Time–space compression (David Harvey): faster flows speed up economic and social life.
  • The world is not smaller; the friction of distance falls.
Mission 3

The 20th century: jets and containers

  • Jet aircraft cut intercontinental journeys from days by sea to hours by air.
  • Air: by 1980 the real cost of airfreight had fallen to about a quarter of its level at the start of the Second World War. Air passengers rose from 310 million (1970) to 4,456 million (2019).
  • Containerisation: standard steel boxes move ship to train to truck without unpacking.
  • A study of trade flows, 1962–1990, found "economically large" effects, supporting "containerization being a driver of 20th century economic globalization".
  • Container port traffic: 547 million TEU (2010) to 913 million (2024).
Quick check
?Air passengers rose from 310.4 million (1970) to 4,455.7 million (2019). How many times the 1970 figure is the 2019 figure?
Mission 3

The 21st century: ICT

  • "Thanks to fibre optic cables, satellites and digital technology, the cost of overseas telecommunications is approaching zero".
  • More than 600 active and planned submarine cables; over 1.5 million km in service in early 2026.
  • Internet users: 15.6% of people (2005), 73.6% (2025, estimate).
  • Mobile phones, social networking and electronic banking move information and money instantly: time–space compression.
Mission 3

Management systems and trade agreements

  • Management and information systems: barcodes, tracking and supply-chain software let firms run just-in-time production across many countries.
  • Trade agreements lower tariffs and set shared rules. As of 1 October 2026, 388 regional trade agreements were in force.
  • Together with containers and cables, these systems make global supply chains more connected: parts, products and data move between factories in different countries.
Mission 3

Connected supply chains are exposed

  • A 2023 drought cut Panama Canal transits: daily crossings fell to 27 in January 2024, against a long-term average of 38.
  • Carriers had to reroute "through the Suez Canal and the Strait of Magellan and around the Cape of Good Hope".
  • About 34% of global seaborne exports of oil pass through the Strait of Hormuz.
Quick check
?What does the Panama Canal drought show about global supply chains?
Mission 3

The world shrinks unevenly

  • Internet use: 94% in high-income countries, 23% in low-income countries; 85% in urban areas, 58% in rural areas.
  • Exporting a container cost $3,204 from a landlocked developing country and $1,268 from a transit country (World Bank figures for 2014).
  • Technology shrinks the world most for rich, urban, coastal places.
Mission check

?A firm in a landlocked low-income country can now email buyers in Europe and be paid in seconds. Its goods still travel by road to a foreign port. Has the world "shrunk" for this firm?

The world has shrunk in time and cost: 14 times as many air passengers in 2019 as in 1970, and about three-quarters of people online. But it has shrunk unevenly (94% online in rich countries, 23% in poor ones), and physical geography and conflict still bite: drought at Panama, security concerns at sea.

Mission 4: Apple employs about 166,000 people. Toyota and its affiliates run more than 50 manufacturing operations in 27 countries and regions outside Japan. Why do these two giants make their products in such different ways?

Make a guess: Apple employs about 166,000 people. Toyota and its affiliates run more than 50 manufacturing operations in 27 countries and regions outside Japan. Why do these two giants make their products in such different ways?

Specification: what this mission covers

Mission 4

How TNCs organise production

  • A transnational corporation (TNC) operates in more than one country.
  • OCR calls these multinational corporations (MNCs).
  • Foreign direct investment (FDI): a firm builds or buys a business abroad and keeps a lasting say in running it.
  • Outsourcing: contracting another firm to do part of the work. Offshoring: moving work to another country, owned or contracted.
Mission 4

Apple: outsourcing at global scale

  • Headquarters: Cupertino, California; about 166,000 full-time equivalent employees (September 2025).
  • "A significant majority" of manufacturing is done by outsourcing partners located mainly in China mainland, India, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and Vietnam.
  • Partners "primarily located in Asia" carry out final assembly of "substantially all" its hardware.
  • Apple keeps design, software, services and marketing.
Mission 4

Apple: markets, gains and risks

  • Net sales, year to September 2025: $416.2 billion; Greater China $64.4 billion, a large new market.
  • Gain: these arrangements "can lower operating costs".
  • Risk: they "reduce the Company's direct control over production and distribution".
  • Tariffs and trade controls "can materially adversely affect the Company's business and supply chain".
Quick check
?Which is an example of FDI rather than outsourcing?
Mission 4

Toyota: spatial organisation

  • Headquarters in Japan. Toyota and its affiliates run more than 50 overseas manufacturing organisations in 27 countries and regions.
  • Vehicle production, year to March 2026 (thousands): Japan 4,149; North America 2,050; Asia 1,802; Europe 815; other 477; total 9,293.
  • Employees, 31 March 2026: 390,927, of whom 207,137 in Japan, 66,331 in Asia, 65,411 in North America and 24,741 in Europe.
Mission 4

Toyota: production and linkages

  • The Toyota Production System: Just in Time, where "each process produces only what is needed for the next process in a continuous flow", and Jidoka, stopping work when a problem occurs.
  • Low stocks need reliable suppliers close to the plant: strong local linkages.
  • In Asia, Toyota has been "developing relationships with local suppliers".
  • Building near customers lets it match local costs to local sales, reducing currency risk.
Quick check
?Why does Just in Time production encourage suppliers to locate near Toyota's plants?
Mission 4

Toyota: trading and marketing

  • Sells in about 200 countries and regions; 9.6 million vehicles in the year to March 2026.
  • Glocalisation: Toyota designs for local "road conditions, climate, and values", an approach it calls "Best in town".
  • Share of vehicles sold that were made in the same region: North America 74.6%, Europe 69.0%, Asia 96.4%.
  • Bulky goods such as cars cost a lot to ship, so building near buyers pays; small, light goods such as phones can be assembled in a few places and shipped worldwide.
  • Building inside a market reduces exposure to tariffs: Toyota notes that "in 2025, a significant increase in tariffs on exports to the United States" was announced.
Mission 4

The global shift of manufacturing: China

  • China's share of world manufacturing value added: 8.5% in 2004 (first year in the data), 28.0% in 2024 (our calculation).
  • In 2010 China (18.0%) passed the USA (16.7%) (our calculation).
  • TNCs drove much of this, through FDI and outsourcing: Apple's partners, including those in China mainland, do its final assembly mainly in Asia.
  • This shows the growing influence of MNCs in EDCs such as China.
Mission 4

Outsourcing services: India

  • TNCs in developed countries outsource services to India: IT support, software, call centres, accounting.
  • Pull factors: many English-speaking graduates, lower wages, and time zones that allow round-the-clock work.
  • India is now also on Apple's list of main manufacturing partner locations.
  • An example of MNC influence in an EDC through outsourcing.
Mission 4

The new international division of labour

  • New international division of labour (NIDL): headquarters, design and research stay mainly in ACs; routine production moves to EDCs with lower labour costs.
  • Apple: design in California; assembly by partners in Asia.
  • Labour mobility has grown too: workers move to where jobs are. India received $137.7 billion in remittances in 2024, the most of any low- or middle-income country.
Mission check

?A bicycle maker (bulky products, sold mainly in Europe) and a headphone brand (small, light products, sold worldwide) are choosing production strategies. Which pairing is best supported by this mission?

Apple keeps design and outsources assembly to partners mainly in Asia: lower costs, less control. Toyota uses FDI, building where it sells, with nearby suppliers for Just in Time. Product size, control and trade policy explain the difference, and both strategies network places and markets.

Mission 5

What is a regional trading bloc?

  • A regional trading bloc is a group of countries, usually neighbours, that agree to lower barriers to trade between themselves.
  • It is one kind of multi-governmental organisation (MGO): governments pool some decisions.
  • The WTO calls these regional trade agreements (RTAs): "reciprocal preferential trade agreements between two or more partners".
  • As of 1 October 2026, 388 RTAs were in force.
Mission 5

How deep does a bloc go?

  • Free trade area: no tariffs between members; each keeps its own tariffs on outsiders.
  • Customs union: free trade plus a common external tariff.
  • Single market: plus free movement of services, capital and people.
  • Economic union: plus shared economic policies, sometimes a single currency.
  • The EU is a single market: "free movement of goods, services, capital and persons".
Quick check
?Two countries remove tariffs on each other's goods and agree to charge the same tariff on imports from everyone else. They do not allow free movement of workers. What have they formed?
Mission 5

The EU: trade inside the bloc

  • Eurostat, 2025: trade with other EU countries made up between 28% (Cyprus) and 83% (Luxembourg) of each member's goods trade.
  • "Intra-EU trade dominates for most countries".
  • Larger economies are more balanced: 44.1% of Germany's goods trade and 46.4% of France's was with non-EU countries.
  • Intra-EU exports rose from €1,500 billion in 2002 to peaks above €4,000 billion in recent years.
Quick check
?In 2025, 44.1% of Germany's goods trade was with non-EU countries. What share was with other EU countries?
Mission 5

What a bloc does for trade

  • Trade creation: removing barriers lets members buy from each other where it is cheaper, so trade between them grows.
  • Trade diversion: a common external tariff can make members buy from a partner even when an outsider is cheaper.
  • Common rules: shared standards and paperwork cut the cost of selling across borders.
  • Bargaining power: the bloc negotiates trade deals as one, with more weight than any one member.
Mission 5

The costs of membership

  • In a customs union, members give up setting their own tariffs.
  • Outsiders face the common external tariff, so some lose sales to members.
  • Firms in members gain a bigger market but also face more competition.
  • Exam move: weigh gains for members against losses for outsiders and lost national control.
Mission 5

A newer bloc: AfCFTA

  • In March 2018, 44 of the 55 African Union states signed the African Continental Free Trade Area agreement in Kigali.
  • Trading under it began on 1 January 2021.
  • It covers about 1.3 billion people and a combined GDP of about US$3.4 trillion.
  • Its aim is "to eliminate trade barriers and boost intra-Africa trade". It is a free trade area, lower on the ladder than the EU.
Mission 5

Free trade zones are not free trade areas

  • A free trade zone is a place inside one country where goods can be stored, processed and re-exported without customs duties: a type of special economic zone.
  • UNCTAD counted nearly 5,400 special economic zones of all kinds in 147 economies in 2019; free zones are the most basic kind.
  • A free trade area is an agreement between countries. Do not mix them up in an answer.
Quick check
?Which statement best explains why intra-EU trade dominates for most EU countries?
Mission check

?AfCFTA aims to remove trade barriers between African states. If it works as blocs are designed to, what would you expect?

Most EU countries trade more with each other because the single market removes tariffs and many other barriers between members, and sets common rules, while nearby partners are cheap to reach. The bloc also shapes trade with outsiders, through the common external tariff and joint trade deals.

Mission 6

India: an emerging and developing country

  • EDC (OCR's term): an economy growing fast and integrating into world trade, but still with low average incomes.
  • GDP per person, 2024: US$2,592.
  • Trade (exports + imports) = 45.9% of GDP in 2024, up from 15.5% in 1990.
  • So trade now shapes India's economy far more than a generation ago.
Mission 6

Components: what India sells

  • Goods exports 2024, top groups by value:
    • mineral fuels and oils 17.1%; electrical machinery 9.1%; machinery 7.4%;
    • gems and jewellery 6.7%; pharmaceuticals 5.3%; vehicles 5.0%.
  • Services were 45.6% of exports in 2024 (calculated from US$374.9bn services and US$447.3bn goods).
  • ICT services were 47.4% of service exports: software and IT support sold abroad.
Mission 6

Direction: who India trades with

  • Goods exports 2024: USA 18.3%, UAE 8.5%, Netherlands 5.6%, Singapore 3.6%, China 3.4%, UK 3.2%.
  • Goods imports 2024: China 18.2%, Russia 9.6%, UAE 8.0%, USA 5.6%, Saudi Arabia 4.4%, Iraq 4.3%.
  • Goods imports (US$697.7bn) exceeded goods exports (US$434.4bn).
Quick check

Mission 6

Change over time: opening up

  • Exports of goods and services: US$22.6bn (1990) → US$829.8bn (2024), current prices.
  • Average tariff on imports (weighted): 56.4% in 1990, 27.0% in 1992, 4.6% in 2022.
  • Services' share of exports: 27.8% (2000) → 45.6% (2024) (our calculation).
Mission 6

Change over time: new partners

  • 2014 → 2024 share of India's goods trade:
    • exports to the USA: 13.4% → 18.3%;
    • imports from China: 12.7% → 18.2%;
    • imports from Russia: 0.9% → 9.6%.
  • In 2024, 88.7% of India's imports from Russia were mineral fuels (our calculation).
Quick check

Mission 6

Interdependence: economic and environmental

  • Economic: India needs fuel; mineral fuels were 31.2% of goods imports in 2024.
  • It needs electronics; electrical machinery was 12.0% of imports. China supplied US$127.0bn of goods but bought US$14.9bn, a gap of US$112.1bn.
  • The USA needs India's goods and IT services; India needs the US market.
  • Environmental: fuel imports tie growth to fossil fuels; India's CO2 emissions reached 3,154 Mt in 2024, 8.0% of the world total.
Mission 6

Interdependence: political and social

  • Political: trade deals bind partners to agreed rules. The UK–India deal was signed on 24 July 2025 and came into force on 15 July 2026.
  • Buying more fuel from Russia since 2014 links India's energy costs to one supplier's politics and to the sanctions others impose.
  • Social: IT exports depend on skilled graduates serving clients abroad, so education and English-language skills shape who benefits.
Mission 6

Impacts: economic development

  • GDP per person: US$443 (2000) → US$2,592 (2024), current US$.
  • Share living below US$3.00 a day (2021 prices): 46.4% (2004) → 5.3% (2022).
  • Trade is one cause among several (reforms, investment, services growth); the data show association, not proof.
Mission 6

Impacts: social equality and political stability

  • Agriculture still employed 42.4% of workers in 2024, yet farm goods are not among the top export groups.
  • So export gains flow mainly to manufacturing and IT services workers; many rural workers gain less.
  • Political stability: dependence on imported fuel (31.2% of imports) exposes India to oil price shocks and to tension among partners.
Mission 6

Weighing it up

  • Strengths: diverse partners; a strong ICT services sector; falling poverty.
  • Risks: a large goods deficit, especially with China; fuel dependence; uneven gains between sectors.
  • Exam move (OCR "Assess"): judge each impact (development, stability, equality) separately, with a figure for each, then decide overall.
Mission check

?An EDC earns most from IT services sold to one large rich market, imports most of its fuel from one supplier, and employs most of its workers in farming. Which judgement is best supported?

For an EDC like India, trade brings growth and falling poverty but also interdependence (markets, fuel, components) and uneven gains. Judge each impact with evidence.

Mission 7

Mission 7: The UK has bought more than it sold almost every year since 1998. So how can it still shape world trade?

Make a guess: The UK has bought more than it sold almost every year since 1998. So how can it still shape world trade?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 2.2.1-3.a case study of one AC showing how a core economy drives change in the global trade system to its own advantage (economic, political, social factors): advantages for trade incl. patterns, partners, negotiations and agreements; opportunities such as sustained growth; challenges such as trade deficit
Mission 7

The UK as a core economy

  • An AC (advanced country) at the core of the world economy has the market size, money and rule-making power to steer trade.
  • In 2025 the UK ranked fourth in the world for exports of goods and services, and second for services exports.
  • But only 16th for goods exports (down from 14th in 2024).
  • Trade (exports + imports) equalled 61.3% of UK GDP in 2025: the economy depends on it.
Mission 7

Patterns: what the UK sells

  • Services were 57.3% of UK exports in 2025, up from 47.6% in 2015.
  • Top service exports (2025): other business services £179.2bn, financial services £98.7bn, travel £67.0bn.
  • Top goods exports (2025): mechanical power generators £45.7bn, pharmaceuticals £44.1bn, cars £28.9bn.
  • In 2023, 68.3% of service exports were delivered remotely, with no goods crossing a border.
Quick check
?Which statement best describes the pattern of UK exports?
Mission 7

Partners

  • Largest export market (2025): United States 21.8% of UK exports, then Germany 6.7%, Ireland 6.1%, Netherlands 5.7%.
  • The EU took 40.7% of UK exports in 2025 (42.2% in 2015) but supplied 49.1% of UK imports.
  • Investment ties match trade ties: the US is the largest investor in the UK (inward FDI stock £640.3bn, end 2024).
  • Interdependence: partners need UK services; the UK needs their goods.
Mission 7

Negotiations and agreements

  • UK–EU Trade and Cooperation Agreement: text agreed December 2020; it covers all UK trade with the EU.
  • CPTPP (Indo-Pacific bloc): in force for the UK with Japan, Singapore, Chile, New Zealand, Vietnam, Peru, Malaysia and Brunei on 15 December 2024.
  • UK–India: negotiations launched 13 January 2022; signed 24 July 2025; in force 15 July 2026.
  • UK total trade in 2025: £152.4bn with CPTPP members; £48.0bn with India.
Mission 7

Shaping the rules to its advantage

  • Economic: a core economy offers a large, rich market. Partners negotiate for access, so it can bargain for terms that suit its strengths, such as services.
  • Political: the UK sets its own preference scheme. The Developing Countries Trading Scheme (DCTS) cuts tariffs for 65 developing countries; it began on 19 June 2023.
  • The UK government says the DCTS also "benefits UK businesses and consumers by reducing the import cost" of thousands of products.
  • The UK decides who qualifies (UN least developed countries; World Bank low and lower-middle income). Beneficiaries do not set the terms.
Quick check
?Which is the best evidence that the UK shapes the trade system rather than only responding to it?
Mission 7

Opportunity: sustained growth

  • UK exports reached £913.2bn in 2025, up 3.5% on 2024; services exports rose 7.3%.
  • In the 12 months to August 2026, exports were £949.4bn, up 5.4%.
  • The IMF forecast UK export volumes to grow 1.9% in 2026 and 1.3% in 2027 (a forecast, not a result).
  • Openness also attracts investment: inward FDI stock £2.1 trillion at end 2024.
Mission 7

Challenge: the trade deficit

  • Trade deficit: imports worth more than exports.
  • 2025: deficit £39.4bn.
  • It hides two opposite balances: a goods deficit of £234.3bn and a services surplus of £194.8bn (2025).
  • Long-run: a goods deficit every year since 1983; a services surplus every year since 1966; an overall deficit every year since 1998 except 2020 and 2021.
Quick check
?In 2024 the UK had a goods deficit of £198.0bn and a services surplus of £176.9bn. What overall balance do these give?
Mission 7

Social and regional factors

  • Gains are uneven: at end 2024 London held 49.7% of UK inward FDI stock; Northern Ireland held 0.9%.
  • Weaker goods exports matter for regions that depend on manufacturing jobs.
  • The EU share of UK exports fell from 42.2% (2015) to 40.7% (2025); part of the challenge is trading with the nearest market under a new agreement.
  • Exam move (OCR "Assess"): weigh influence (services strength, agreements, DCTS) against limits (deficit, goods weakness), then reach a judgement.
Mission check

?A student argues: "A country that runs a trade deficit every year cannot be a core economy that drives the trade system." Which response is best supported by the UK case?

A core economy's power comes from what it specialises in and the terms it can set. The UK's services surplus, market size and agreements give it influence, while its trade deficit in goods remains its main challenge.

Mission 8

Mission 8: Ethiopia imports almost six times the value of goods it exports. Why can't it simply sell more goods?

Make a guess: Ethiopia imports almost six times the value of goods it exports. Why can't it simply sell more goods?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 2.2.1-3.a case study of one LIDC (Ethiopia) showing how peripheral economies exert limited influence and can only respond to change in the global trade system (economic, political, social factors): trade components incl. patterns, partners, negotiations and agreements; why it has limited access to global markets; opportunities such as diversification; challenges such as political instability
Mission 8

Ethiopia: a peripheral economy

  • LIDC: eligible for the IMF's Poverty Reduction and Growth Trust (OCR's definition).
  • A UN least developed country and landlocked.
  • 2023 goods: exports US$2,859 million; imports US$17,050 million.
  • A goods trade deficit of US$14,191 million (our calculation).
  • Goods only: services exports (US$7.4 billion in 2023) were larger than goods exports.
Mission 8

Components: what flows in and out

  • Exports, 2023: raw materials 70.5%, consumer goods 14.3%, intermediate 12.7%, capital 2.5%.
  • Top exports: coffee (US$1,219 million), sesame seeds, cut flowers, vegetables, soya beans.
  • Imports: consumer goods 41.7%, intermediate goods 32.4%, capital goods 22.4%.
  • Pattern: primary goods out, manufactured goods in.
Quick check
?In 2023 Ethiopia exported US$1,219 million of unroasted coffee out of US$2,859 million of exports. What share of exports was coffee?
Mission 8

Partners

  • Export partners, 2023: Saudi Arabia 10.4%, USA 9.7%, Netherlands 7.7%, UAE 5.7%, Somalia 5.0%.
  • Import partners: China 32.7%, India 9.6%, Kuwait 7.8%, Saudi Arabia 6.6%, USA 6.3%.
  • Ethiopia buys from large manufacturing economies; it sells crops to many, each taking a small share.
Mission 8

Why access to markets is limited

  • Geography: landlocked; exports must cross another country to reach a port.
  • Tariff escalation: importers set "higher import duties on semi-processed products than on raw materials, and higher still on finished products". This discourages processing in the countries where raw materials come from.
  • Rules: Ethiopia's WTO accession working party was set up in 2003; it negotiates to join rules others already wrote.
Mission 8

Negotiations and agreements: who decides?

  • Many of Ethiopia's best routes into rich markets are one-way preference schemes, granted by the importer.
  • US AGOA: a country must not "engage in gross violations of internationally recognized human rights".
  • UK DCTS: cuts tariffs for 65 developing countries; the UK sets who qualifies.
  • The importer can grant or withdraw access. The exporter can only meet the conditions.
Quick check
?Which fact best shows that Ethiopia has limited influence over its trade agreements?
Mission 8

Opportunity: diversification

  • Diversification: widening the range of exports to reduce dependence on a few crops.
  • Cut flowers: US$200 million (2019) → US$231 million (2023).
  • But exports became less diverse overall: raw materials rose from 63.8% (2019) to 70.5% (2023) of exports; consumer goods fell from 17.5% to 14.3%.
  • Coffee rose from 29.6% to 42.6% of exports (our calculation).
Mission 8

Challenge: political instability

  • Conflict in northern Ethiopia began in November 2020.
  • The USA terminated Ethiopia's AGOA eligibility, effective 1 January 2022.
  • USTR cited "gross violations of internationally recognised human rights" (as reported).
  • The decision threatened the textile industry and hopes of becoming a light-manufacturing hub.
Mission 8

Reading the trade data

  • Exports to the USA: US$294 million (2019), US$330 million (2021), US$276 million (2023).
  • The USA's share of exports: 11.0% → 10.8% → 9.7%.
  • Careful: prices, harvests and conflict also changed. The data fit a loss of access; they do not prove its effect alone.
Quick check
?Exports to the USA fell from US$330 million (2021) to US$276 million (2023). Which conclusion is best supported?
Mission 8

Exam move: limited influence

  • Economic: a narrow base of primary exports; prices and tariffs are set elsewhere.
  • Political: access depends on importers' preference schemes, which can be withdrawn; instability at home triggered withdrawal.
  • Social: jobs in export textiles and flowers depend on that access.
  • Judgement: Ethiopia responds to the trade system (diversifying, seeking WTO entry); it does not drive it.
Mission check

?A landlocked LIDC earns most of its export income from one mineral ore. The main importing country raises its tariff on the refined metal but not on the ore. What is the most likely response?

Ethiopia cannot "simply sell more": it is landlocked, its exports are mostly raw crops, and the rules on tariffs and preferences are set by importers. When conflict broke out, the USA withdrew duty-free access. That is a peripheral economy responding, not driving.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Trade has three components: merchandise ($24.62 trillion in 2024), services ($8.88 trillion) and capital (FDI $1.6 trillion in 2025); patterns are both intra-regional (the EU) and inter-regional (Asia to the Americas).
  • Over the long run, South-South trade and services have grown faster than trade overall, though both slowed after 2020–21.

Mission 2

  • Pattern: exports per person and HDI are strongly linked (rank correlation 0.92 across 164 countries, 2023); total exports less so (0.71); LDCs: 14.4% of people, 1.0% of exports.
  • Both sides: flows of money, people, ideas and technology can build growth and stability (Vietnam; the EU's origins) or deepen inequality, conflict and injustice (commodity dependence, Rana Plaza, conflict diamonds).

Mission 3

  • Technology, transport and communications make global supply chains more connected, and more exposed to shocks.

Mission 4

  • TNCs organise production through FDI (Toyota: plants in 27 countries and regions, 47% of staff outside Japan) or outsourcing (Apple: partners mainly in Asia); both create linkages, new markets and risks.
  • MNCs shape EDCs through outsourcing; the new international division of labour separates design in ACs from production in EDCs.

Mission 5

  • A regional trading bloc lowers barriers between members; blocs range from free trade areas to customs unions, single markets and economic unions.
  • Blocs cause trade creation and trade diversion, give members bargaining power, and cost them some control over trade policy. In 2025 intra-EU trade dominated for most EU members.

Mission 6

  • India's trade: goods led by fuels, electrical goods, gems and pharmaceuticals, plus a growing share of IT services; top export market the USA, top import source China.
  • Since 1990 trade has grown from 15.5% to 45.9% of GDP as tariffs fell; since 2014 the USA, China and Russia have grown in importance, bringing interdependence and uneven impacts.

Mission 7

  • The UK is a core AC: second for services exports, with the US and EU as key partners; it shapes trade through agreements (TCA, CPTPP, UK–India) and its own DCTS.
  • Opportunities: growing exports and large inward investment. Challenge: a persistent trade deficit, as a goods deficit outweighs a services surplus; gains concentrate in London.

Mission 8

  • Ethiopia (LIDC, landlocked, UN LDC) exports mainly raw crops (70.5% raw materials; coffee 42.6%) and imports manufactured goods (2023), with a goods trade deficit of US$14.2 billion.
  • Its access depends on importers' rules (tariff escalation, AGOA, DCTS); diversification has stalled, and instability cost it AGOA access in 2022.
