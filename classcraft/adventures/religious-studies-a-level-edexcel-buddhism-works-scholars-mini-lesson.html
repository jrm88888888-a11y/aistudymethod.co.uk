Mission 1

Mission 1: If a father lies to get his children out of a burning house, has he done wrong?

Make a guess: If a father lies to get his children out of a burning house, has he done wrong?

Mission 1

The Lotus Sutra

  • Full title: Saddharmapundarika Sutra, "the Lotus of the True Dharma". A Mahayana text.
  • Composed in stages, roughly 1st century BCE to 2nd century CE, in India.
  • Kumarajiva's Chinese translation (406 CE) made it one of the most loved texts in East Asia: Tiantai (China), Tendai and Nichiren Buddhism (Japan).
  • Its big claims: there is really one vehicle (ekayana) to Buddhahood; all beings can become Buddhas; the Buddha is far greater than his one human life.
  • It teaches mainly through parables: stories that carry the doctrine.
Mission 1

Upaya: skilful means

  • Upaya (full form upaya-kausalya): skilful means, skill in adapting the teaching to the hearer.
  • A good teacher meets people where they are: a child, a scholar and a grieving parent need different words.
  • Lotus Sutra chapter 2 ("Skilful Means"): the Buddha's wisdom is too deep to be grasped at once, so he taught in many ways, to lead beings step by step (paraphrase).
  • So some teachings are provisional: true enough to help, but not the final word.
Mission 1Read the source

The burning house

Lotus Sutra, chapter 3 (paraphrase):

  • A rich old man owns a huge, decaying house with one narrow door. Fire breaks out.
  • His children are inside, absorbed in their games. They do not know what fire is, or fear it.
  • He calls them; they ignore him. So he says: the goat-carts, deer-carts and ox-carts you wanted are waiting outside the gate!
  • They rush out and are safe. Then he gives every child the same great carriage, drawn by white oxen: better than anything promised.
  • The Buddha asks Sariputra: did the father lie? Sariputra: no; he saved their lives, and that was his aim from the start.

Q1 Why did the father not simply tell the truth about the fire?

Show answer

The children did not understand fire, so the warning meant nothing to them; the promise of carts did.

Q2 Why does Sariputra say no falsehood was told?

Show answer

The father's purpose was their rescue, and he gave them more than he promised.

Mission 1

Decoding the parable

  • House on fire = samsara, burning with birth, old age, sickness, death and suffering.
  • Children = all beings, absorbed in pleasures, unaware of danger.
  • Father = the Buddha, who cares for all beings as his children.
  • Three carts = the three vehicles taught earlier: the way of disciples (sravaka), of solitary Buddhas (pratyekabuddha), and of bodhisattvas.
  • Great carriage = the one Buddha vehicle: all are finally led to full Buddhahood.

Mission 1

The magic city and the hidden gem

  • Magic city (chapter 7, paraphrase): a guide leads travellers along a long, dangerous road to a place of treasure. Exhausted, they want to turn back. He conjures a city for them to rest in. Refreshed, they are told: this city is not real; the treasure is near.
    • Meaning: the arhat's nirvana is a resting place on the way, not the final goal.
  • Hidden gem (chapter 8, paraphrase): a poor man falls asleep drunk at a rich friend's house. The friend, called away, sews a priceless jewel into his robe. The man wanders in poverty, content with a little; years later the friend shows him the jewel.
    • Meaning: told by arhats about themselves. They already had what was needed for Buddhahood but settled for less.
    • Later East Asian Buddhism read the gem as Buddha nature within every being.
Mission 1

The Buddha's life as skilful means

  • Lotus Sutra "Lifespan" chapter (ch. 16 in Kumarajiva's version; ch. 15 in Kern's): the Buddha says he became awakened countless ages ago (paraphrase).
  • So his birth, renunciation, awakening under the tree and death were a display for beings' sake.
  • The physician (paraphrase): a doctor's sons have taken poison; some refuse the cure. He goes away and sends word he has died. Shocked, they take the medicine. He returns.
  • Meaning: if the Buddha always seemed present, people would grow careless. His parinirvana was skilful means.
Mission 1

Wisdom, liberation, loving-kindness

  • Wisdom: only Buddhas fully understand the true nature of things; all teaching flows from that wisdom (chapter 2).
  • Liberation: the sutra predicts Buddhahood for Sariputra (as the Buddha Padmaprabha) and for many others, even, in a later chapter, for Devadatta, the Buddha's enemy, and for a dragon king's young daughter. No one is excluded.
  • Loving-kindness: the Buddha is father to all beings. The bodhisattva Sadaparibhuta ("Never Disparaging") bowed to everyone he met, saying they would become Buddhas, even when they beat him (paraphrase).
  • Skilful means: compassion choosing the right words for each hearer.
  • Paraphrase only: your anthology does not set Lotus extracts for this topic; cite chapters.

Mission 1Diversity

Theravada and Mahayana readings

  • Mahayana: the Lotus Sutra is the Buddha's word (buddhavacana), revealing what earlier teaching held back. Nichiren Buddhists chant its title, Namu Myoho Renge Kyo.
  • Theravada: does not accept the Mahayana sutras as the Buddha's word. Arahantship is the real goal, not a resting place.
  • Common ground: Pali texts already show the Buddha adapting his teaching to hearers, and the raft simile (Majjhima Nikaya 22) says the Dhamma is for crossing over, not for clinging to.
  • The difference: adapting how truth is taught (shared) vs treating earlier doctrines as provisional (Mahayana).
  • Scholar: Michael Pye, Skilful Means: A Concept in Mahayana Buddhism (1978), traces how upaya became a key Mahayana idea.
Mission 1Evaluate

Is skilful means the key to Buddhism's diversity?

  • Claim: Skilful means explains why Buddhism has so many forms.
  • For: Different schools can be seen as teachings fitted to different people and cultures, all leading to one goal.
  • For: It lets Buddhists respect other traditions without calling them simply false.
  • Against: It is a Mahayana idea: Theravadins reject the claim that their goal is provisional, so it cannot unite all Buddhists on its own terms.
  • Against: If any teaching may be provisional, how does anyone tell which is final? The risk is that "skilful" excuses anything.
  • Judgement criteria: Does the idea fit what the Pali texts already say? Does it respect each school's own view of itself? Does it keep a test for what counts as helpful?

Mission 2

Bodhisattva: a being set on awakening

  • Bodhisattva: bodhi (awakening) + sattva (being): a being bound for full Buddhahood.
  • In the Pali texts the word names Gautama before his awakening, through many lives (the Jataka stories).
  • Tradition tells how, ages ago, the ascetic Sumedha met the Buddha Dipankara and resolved to become a Buddha himself.
  • In Mahayana Buddhism the bodhisattva becomes the ideal for everyone: not only one future Buddha, but all who make the vow.
Mission 2

The arhat: the worthy one

  • Arhat ("worthy one"): one who has fully ended greed, hatred and delusion, and so ends rebirth at death.
  • Edexcel uses the Pali form arahant, and nibbana.
  • Four stages of awakening:
  • Stream-enterer: has seen the Dhamma; at most seven more lives.
  • Once-returner: one more human birth.
  • Non-returner: no return to the human world.
  • Arhat: free.
Mission 2

Bodhicitta and the vow

  • Bodhicitta, "the awakening mind": the wish to become a Buddha for the sake of all beings.
  • It is made formal in the bodhisattva vow, often taken in a ceremony before a teacher.
  • A common East Asian form, the Four Great Vows (paraphrase): beings are countless, I vow to free them; delusions are endless, I vow to end them; teachings are boundless, I vow to learn them; the Buddha way is unsurpassed, I vow to reach it.
  • Not "I postpone nirvana forever". Many Mahayana texts teach that a Buddha reaches a non-abiding nirvana: free, but never withdrawn from beings.
Mission 2Read the source

Shantideva's aspiration

Shantideva (8th century, India), Bodhicaryavatara ("Entering the Way of Awakening"), chapter 10, verse 55 (paraphrase):

  • For as long as space remains, and as long as there are living beings, may I too remain, to drive away the suffering of the world.
  • The 14th Dalai Lama often names this verse as a lifelong inspiration.

Q1 What two things set the length of the bodhisattva's commitment?

Show answer

The existence of space and of suffering beings.

Q2 Does the verse say the bodhisattva never reaches awakening?

Show answer

No: it is about staying to help; on the non-abiding view, a Buddha does both.

Mission 2

The six perfections

  • Paramita, "perfection" or "gone to the far shore": a quality practised to its fullest.
  • Dana: generosity, giving even one's life.
  • Sila: ethical conduct.
  • Ksanti: patience, enduring harm without anger.
  • Virya: energy, joyful effort.
  • Dhyana: meditation, a stable mind.
  • Prajna: wisdom, seeing the emptiness of all things.
  • A list of ten adds skilful means, vow, power and knowledge, one for each of the ten stages.
  • Theravada has its own list of ten paramis (e.g. renunciation, truthfulness, loving-kindness, equanimity), practised by Gautama in past lives.
Mission 2

Wisdom and compassion: two wings

  • Compassion (karuna) without wisdom becomes attachment: "I am the helper; you are the helped."
  • Wisdom (prajna) without compassion becomes cold: seeing emptiness, but doing nothing.
  • So the perfection of wisdom guides the other five, like a sighted person leading others on a road (an image from the Perfection of Wisdom texts, paraphrase).
  • True giving sees no fixed giver, gift or receiver: all are empty. It gives freely, without pride.
  • The Lotus Sutra's loving-kindness and skilful means (see Mission 1) are this compassion in action.
Mission 2

The ten bhumis

  • Bhumi, "ground" or stage. The classic list is in the Dasabhumika Sutra.
  • 1 Joyful · 2 Stainless · 3 Luminous · 4 Radiant · 5 Hard to conquer · 6 Facing (wisdom) · 7 Far-going · 8 Immovable · 9 Good intelligence · 10 Cloud of Dharma.
  • Stage 1 begins with the first direct insight into emptiness; each stage perfects one paramita (1 generosity … 6 wisdom … 10 knowledge).
  • From stage 8 the bodhisattva cannot fall back.
  • Traditionally the whole path takes three countless ages.
  • Their place in traditions: central in Indian and Tibetan teaching; East Asian schools such as Zen and Pure Land stress other routes (sudden awakening; Amitabha's vow).

Mission 2Diversity

Arhat and bodhisattva compared

  • Goal: arhat — nirvana, the end of rebirth; bodhisattva — full Buddhahood.
  • Motive: arhat — freedom from suffering, then teaching others; bodhisattva — freedom of all beings from the start.
  • Length: arhat — possible in this life; bodhisattva — countless lives.
  • Who: Theravada — the arhat is the goal for most; a few aim at Buddhahood. Mahayana — the bodhisattva path is for all.
  • Fairness check: calling arhats "selfish" is a Mahayana polemic. In the Pali texts the Buddha sent his first arhat disciples out to teach "for the welfare of the many" (Vinaya, Mahavagga I.11, paraphrase).
Mission 2Evaluate

Is the bodhisattva ideal higher than the arhat ideal?

  • Claim: The bodhisattva ideal is a higher ideal than that of the arhat.
  • For: It joins wisdom to universal compassion; no one is left behind.
  • For: It follows the Buddha's own example: he chose Buddhahood and taught for 45 years.
  • Against: Arhats also teach and serve; the contrast is overdrawn.
  • Against: A goal that is reachable in this life may motivate more than one that is countless lives away.
  • Judgement criteria: Which ideal fits the Buddha's teaching as recorded? Which is reachable? Does a higher aim make a better path?

Mission 3

The great bodhisattvas

  • Mahayana texts describe bodhisattvas at the highest stages of the path (see Mission 2): of vast wisdom and power, active in many worlds.
  • Buddhists turn to them in devotion: they recite their names and mantras, and make offerings before their images.
  • Each embodies a quality of awakening, so devotion also trains that quality in the devotee.
  • They are often called "celestial" or "transcendent" bodhisattvas.
  • Three that your spec names: compassion, wisdom, and the Buddha to come.
Mission 3

Avalokitesvara: compassion

  • The bodhisattva of compassion, "the lord who looks down" on the world's suffering.
  • Lotus Sutra chapter 25 (Kumarajiva): those who call his name in danger are saved; he takes whatever form will help each being (paraphrase).
  • Often shown with a thousand arms (to help everywhere) and eleven heads.
  • Mantra: Om mani padme hum.
  • Names: Guanyin in China (often in female form), Kannon in Japan, Chenrezig in Tibet.
  • Tibetan Buddhists regard the Dalai Lama as an emanation of Avalokitesvara.
Mission 3

Manjusri: wisdom

  • The bodhisattva of wisdom (prajna).
  • Holds a flaming sword that cuts through ignorance, and a book of the Perfection of Wisdom on a lotus.
  • Usually shown as a youth: wisdom that is fresh and sharp.
  • In the Vimalakirti Sutra he alone dares to visit the wise layman Vimalakirti and debate with him.
  • Mount Wutai in China is honoured as his special place.
  • What he represents: wisdom that sees emptiness and frees beings.
Mission 3

Maitreya: the Buddha to come

  • Maitreya (Pali Metteyya), from maitri, loving-kindness.
  • Now in the Tushita heaven; he will be the next Buddha, when the Dhamma has been forgotten.
  • Accepted in Theravada too: the Pali canon foretells him (Digha Nikaya 26).
  • Often shown seated with feet on the ground, ready to rise and come.
  • In China the jolly "laughing Buddha" (Budai) is popularly linked with him.
  • What he represents: hope; the Dhamma will be renewed.

Mission 3

Basham: the bodhisattva of the Mahayana

  • A. L. Basham (1914–1986), historian of India, The Wonder That Was India (1954); your anthology extract (2) is his chapter "The Bodhisattva" in de Bary (ed.), The Buddhist Tradition.
  • Paraphrase of his main points:
  • The bodhisattva ideal grew in Mahayana as a new ideal, beside and above the older one of the arhat.
  • The bodhisattva takes on the suffering of others and gives up his own good for theirs.
  • His merit can be transferred to others: a striking new idea set against strict karma.
  • The doctrine grew alongside rising devotion in Indian religion: bodhisattvas became saviours to whom people pray.
  • Read the extract itself: paraphrase only.
Mission 3

Rahula: an ideal for all Buddhists

  • Walpola Rahula (1907–1997), Sri Lankan Theravada monk and scholar, What the Buddha Taught (1959); extract (3) is his "The Bodhisattva Ideal in Buddhism" in Gems of Buddhist Wisdom.
  • Paraphrase of his main points:
  • The bodhisattva ideal is not only Mahayana: Theravada accepts it too.
  • There are three ways: disciple (arahant), solitary Buddha, and bodhisattva. Anyone may choose the bodhisattva path; few do, because it is so hard.
  • The bodhisattva's long practice of the perfections is praised in Theravada texts.
  • Rahula rejects sharp Mahayana-vs-Theravada contrasts, and the label Hinayana ("lesser vehicle").
Mission 3Diversity

Basham and Rahula compared

  • Agree: the bodhisattva is marked by compassion and the perfections; the ideal grew out of the stories of the Buddha's past lives.
  • Basham: a historian outside the tradition; stresses development and difference: a new ideal, new saviour figures, transfer of merit.
  • Rahula: a Theravada monk inside the tradition; stresses continuity and common ground: one ideal shared by all Buddhists.
  • Why they differ: different questions (how did the idea develop? what do Buddhists hold in common?) and different standpoints.
  • Use both: one explains change, the other explains unity.

Mission 3

Transfer of merit

  • Merit (Sanskrit punya, Pali puñña): the good karmic result of wholesome actions.
  • Theravada: after a death, families give food and robes to monks and dedicate the merit to the dead relative (pattidana). The dead rejoice (anumodana), and rejoicing is itself a wholesome act that makes merit of their own.
  • Pali source: the Tirokudda Sutta (Khuddakapatha 7) describes gifts given on behalf of departed relatives (paraphrase).
  • Mahayana: bodhisattvas dedicate all merit to the awakening of all beings (parinamana), often at the end of every practice. In Pure Land Buddhism, Amitabha's merit brings devotees to his Pure Land.
  • Your spec calls this "transfer of karmic merit".
Mission 3Read the source

Who purifies whom?

> "By oneself the evil is done, by oneself one suffers; by oneself evil is left undone, by oneself one is purified. Purity and impurity belong to oneself, no one can purify another." (Dhammapada 165, Müller 1881)

Q1 What problem does this verse raise for transfer of merit?

Show answer

If each person alone makes and reaps karma, how can another's merit help them?

Q2 How does the Theravada idea of rejoicing (anumodana) answer it?

Show answer

The receiver's own act of rejoicing is wholesome, so the merit they gain is theirs.

Mission 3Evaluate

Does transfer of merit contradict karma?

  • Claim: Transferring merit contradicts the Buddha's teaching that each person reaps their own karma.
  • For: Dhammapada 165: no one can purify another.
  • For: Basham's point: transfer of merit is a new idea, alongside the rise of saviour figures.
  • Against: In Theravada, what helps is the receiver's own rejoicing: karma stays personal.
  • Against: Giving away merit is itself generosity (dana), so it increases merit; and wisdom sees no fixed owner of merit.
  • Judgement criteria: Is karma a strict law of individual payment, or about intention? Does practice (funerals, dedications) show what Buddhists really believe?
  • Your spec: debates about this belief in its Theravada context.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Upaya (skilful means): the Buddha adapts his teaching to his hearers, so some teachings are provisional.
  • The burning house: the father lures his children out with promised carts, then gives all one great carriage; the three vehicles lead to the one Buddha vehicle.
  • The magic city (a resting place) and the hidden gem (what we already have) make the same point; even the Buddha's death was skilful means.

Mission 2

  • A bodhisattva vows to reach Buddhahood for the sake of all beings (bodhicitta); the arhat reaches nirvana through four stages.
  • Six perfections: generosity, ethics, patience, energy, meditation, wisdom; wisdom guides the rest, and compassion moves them.
  • The ten bhumis map the path stage by stage, from Joyful to Cloud of Dharma.

Mission 3

  • Avalokitesvara (compassion), Manjusri (wisdom, sword and book) and Maitreya (loving-kindness, the Buddha to come) embody qualities of awakening and receive devotion.
  • Basham stresses the bodhisattva as a new, developing ideal; Rahula stresses that it is shared by Theravada too.
  • Transfer of merit: Theravada dedication and rejoicing (pattidana, anumodana); Mahayana dedication to all beings (parinamana); debated against strict personal karma.
