Mission 1

Mission 1: If a country's yearly deficit keeps shrinking, how can its debt still be growing?

Make a guess: If a country's yearly deficit keeps shrinking, how can its debt still be growing?

Mission 1

Three kinds of spending

  • Current spending: day-to-day running costs that are used up within the year. Salaries of teachers and nurses, medicines, office supplies.
  • Capital spending: investment in assets that last for years. New roads, rail lines, hospitals, school buildings.
  • Transfer payments: money paid to people with no good or service in return. Pensions, unemployment benefits, child benefit.
  • Transfers move income from taxpayers to recipients. They are not part of government spending (G) in AD, because no output is bought.
  • G is a component of AD. Capital spending can also raise LRAS over time (better infrastructure, more productive workers).
  • Say: "capital spending" for long-lasting assets — not: "spending on capital cities" or "big spending".
Mission 1

Where the money comes from, and what for

  • Direct taxes: on income and wealth, paid straight to the government. Income tax, corporation tax.
  • Indirect taxes: on spending, collected by sellers. VAT (sales tax), excise duties on fuel, alcohol and tobacco.
  • Tax types, rates and progressive vs regressive taxes are covered in a later lesson.
Mission 1

How big is the state?

  • Size of the public sector: government spending as a % of GDP.
  • Rough figures for total government spending, 2023: France about 57% · Germany about 48% · UK about 44% · US about 37%.
  • Higher spending can mean: more public services · more redistribution (benefits and free services reduce inequality) · more infrastructure for growth.
  • But it needs higher taxes, which may weaken incentives to work and invest. It may also crowd out private spending and may be less efficient (weaker profit incentive).
  • The evidence on size and growth is mixed. What the money is spent on matters as much as how much.

Mission 1

The budget balance

  • Budget balance = tax revenue − government spending (incl. transfers), over one year.
  • Deficit: spending > revenue. Surplus: revenue > spending. Balanced budget: equal.
  • Illustrative data (billion): tax revenue 900 · current spending 650 · capital spending 100 · transfers 250.
  • Spending = 650 + 100 + 250 = 1,000.
  • Budget balance = 900 − 1,000 = −100: a deficit of 100.
  • A deficit means the government injects more into the economy than it takes out in tax: other things equal, it adds to AD.
  • UK term: public sector net borrowing (PSNB) is the deficit.
Mission 1

Deficit vs debt

  • Deficit is a flow: how much more the government spends than it raises in one year.
  • National debt is a stock: the total owed, built up from all past borrowing.
  • Each year's deficit is borrowed, so it is added to the debt. A surplus can be used to pay debt down.
  • Picture a bath: the deficit is the water flowing in this year; the debt is the water in the tub.
  • So a smaller deficit still adds to the debt, just more slowly. Paying the debt down needs a surplus.
  • Say: "the deficit fell, so the debt grew more slowly" — not: "the deficit fell, so the debt fell".
Quick check
?A government's deficit falls from 80 billion last year to 50 billion this year. Nothing else changes. What happens to the national debt this year?
Mission 1

Why deficits change: cyclical vs structural

  • In a recession: incomes, profits and spending fall, so tax revenue falls. Unemployment rises, so benefit spending rises. The deficit grows without any new decision.
  • In a boom, the reverse: the deficit shrinks.
  • Cyclical deficit: the part caused by the economy being below its potential output. It disappears when the economy recovers.
  • Structural deficit: the part that remains even when the economy is at potential output (no output gap). It reflects long-run choices about tax rates and spending.
  • Actual deficit = cyclical part + structural part.
  • So government spending (including transfers) tends to move against the trade cycle.

Mission 1

Measuring the burden: debt as a % of GDP

  • Debt is compared with nominal GDP, because GDP is the income from which taxes are paid.
  • Debt-to-GDP ratio = (national debt ÷ nominal GDP) × 100.
  • Rough figures for gross government debt, 2023: Japan over 200% · Italy about 135% · US about 120% · UK about 100% · Germany about 62%.
  • Worked example (illustrative data, billion): debt 800 · nominal GDP 1,000 → ratio 80%.
  • Next year nominal GDP grows 5% to 1,050.

Quick check
?Illustrative data: a country's national debt is 600 billion and its nominal GDP is 1,200 billion. Next year it runs a deficit of 36 billion and nominal GDP grows by 4%. What is the new debt-to-GDP ratio, to one decimal place?
Mission 1

Why high debt can limit a government

Costs of a high debt:

  • Debt interest: money spent on interest cannot be spent on schools or health, or returned in lower taxes.
  • Credit ratings: agencies grade the risk that a government will not repay. A downgrade can raise the interest rate it must pay.
  • Example: in April 2010 Greece's rating was cut to "junk" by S&P. Its 10-year bond yields rose above 10%, and it needed EU and IMF loans.
  • Future taxes and spending: higher taxes or spending cuts may be needed later.
  • Less room to borrow in the next recession.

Possible benefits of a deficit:

  • It supports AD and jobs in a recession.
  • Borrowing for capital spending can raise future output, which helps pay the interest.
Mission 1Evaluate

Is a deficit a problem?

It depends on:

  • Why it exists: a cyclical deficit falls as the economy recovers; a structural deficit does not.
  • What it pays for: capital spending adds assets that can raise productive capacity; most current spending pays for today's services.
  • Interest rate vs growth: if the interest rate on the debt is below the growth rate of nominal GDP, the debt ratio is easier to keep stable.
  • Who lends: domestic lenders, long-dated bonds and borrowing in the country's own currency usually reduce the risk of a sudden crisis.
  • Starting level and confidence: the same deficit worries lenders more when debt is already high.
  • Economists disagree on how high is too high. There is no single safe debt ratio.
Mission check

?Illustrative data: a government's national debt is 300 billion at the start of Year 1. Its budget balances are: Year 1, surplus of 20 billion · Year 2, surplus of 5 billion · Year 3, deficit of 15 billion. What is the national debt at the end of Year 3?

The deficit is the yearly flow; the debt is the stock. Every deficit adds to the debt, however small.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why does an economy grow in bursts and slumps instead of at a steady pace?

Make a guess: Why does an economy grow in bursts and slumps instead of at a steady pace?

Mission 2

The trend and the cycle

  • Plot real GDP against time.
  • The trend line shows potential output: it rises steadily as productive capacity grows (long-term growth).
  • Actual real GDP wobbles above and below the trend: short-term fluctuations.
  • These ups and downs are the business cycle.
  • Also called the economic cycle or trade cycle: the same thing.
  • Say: "actual output fell below potential" — not: "potential output fell", when the economy has spare capacity.
Mission 2

The phases of the cycle

  • Boom: fast growth, actual output above trend. Peak: the top.
  • Downturn (slowdown): growth falls. Recession: real GDP falls.
  • Trough: the bottom. Recovery (upturn): growth returns.
  • Common definition of a recession: real GDP falls for two consecutive quarters.
  • Say: "a slowdown: growth falls but stays positive" — not: "a recession".

Mission 2

Output gaps

  • Output gap = actual output − potential output.
  • Negative output gap: actual below potential.
  • Spare capacity, idle machines, cyclical unemployment. Downward pressure on inflation.
  • Positive output gap: actual above potential.
  • Overtime, shortages of workers, capacity stretched. Upward pressure on wages and prices. It cannot last.
Mission 2

Output gaps on AD/AS and the PPC

  • AD/AS: LRAS is vertical at full-employment output (Yf).
  • AD1 meets SRAS left of Yf: output Y1 < Yf.
  • That is a negative output gap.
  • AD2 meets SRAS right of Yf: output Y2 > Yf.
  • That is a positive output gap.
  • On a PPC, a negative gap is a point inside the curve: resources are unemployed.
  • A positive gap is output beyond its sustainable level. Resources are over-used (overtime, machines run without maintenance).
Mission 2

Worked example: the size of the gap

  • Illustrative data.
  • Potential output = 2,000 bn. Actual real GDP = 1,950 bn.
  • Gap = 1,950 − 2,000 = −50 bn.
  • As % of potential: −50 / 2,000 × 100 = −2.5%.
  • A negative output gap of 2.5% of potential output.
  • Two years later, actual = 2,030 bn and potential = 2,000 bn: gap = +30 bn = +1.5% of potential.

Mission 2

Why gaps are hard to measure

  • Actual output is measured; potential output is not. It has to be estimated.
  • Estimates use past trends, business surveys of spare capacity, and unemployment data.
  • Different institutions get different answers, and revise them later.
  • GDP itself is revised. UK example: the fall in real GDP in the 2008–09 recession was estimated at 5.8% in earlier data, then revised to 6.3% (OBR, 2012).
  • So policymakers may not know in real time whether the gap is positive or negative.
Mission 2

Why output swings: shocks and instability

  • Demand shocks shift AD: a collapse in confidence, a financial crisis, falling export demand, a big change in government spending.
  • Supply shocks shift SRAS: a sharp rise in oil prices, a pandemic, a harvest failure.
  • Real case: UK real GDP fell for five quarters in a row and shrank by more than 6% between early 2008 and mid-2009. It took five years to regain its pre-recession size (ONS, 2018).
Mission 2

Smoothing the cycle

  • Automatic stabilisers: changes in tax revenue and spending that happen without any new decision.
    • Recession: incomes fall → tax revenue falls; unemployment rises → benefit spending rises. AD falls by less.
    • Boom: tax revenue rises; benefit spending falls. AD rises by less.
  • Discretionary fiscal policy: the government chooses to raise spending or cut taxes in a recession, and the reverse in a boom.
Quick check
?Illustrative data: potential output is estimated at 800 bn. Actual real GDP is 776 bn. Which statement is correct?
Mission 2Evaluate

Should governments try to close every output gap?

It depends on:

  • Measurement: potential output is estimated, so the size and even the sign of the gap may be wrong.
  • Time lags: policy can take a year or more to work, so it may arrive when the gap has closed.
  • The cause: a demand shock suits demand-side policy; after a supply shock, boosting AD can add to inflation.
  • Size and length: a small, short gap may close on its own; a deep recession can leave lasting damage.
Mission check

?A surge in confidence and borrowing has kept a country's actual output above its potential output for two years. Unemployment is below its natural rate. Which is most likely to happen next?

When demand pushes output above what the economy can sustain, inflation tends to rise. When it falls below, workers and machines sit idle.

Mission 3

Mission 3: When a country's money loses value abroad, who wins and who loses at home?

Make a guess: When a country's money loses value abroad, who wins and who loses at home?

Mission 3

Prices across the border

  • A firm sets its price in its own currency. The exchange rate decides what that price is abroad.
  • Illustrative data: the pound falls from 1.50 to 1.20 dollars: a 20% depreciation.
  • UK export priced £10: in the US, it was 10 × 1.50 = $15; now 10 × 1.20 = $12. Cheaper abroad.
  • US import priced $30: in the UK, it was 30 ÷ 1.50 = £20; now 30 ÷ 1.20 = £25. Dearer at home.
  • Memory aid: SPICED — Strong Pound, Imports Cheap, Exports Dear. A weak pound does the reverse.
Quick check
?A country's currency depreciates from 2.00 to 1.50 units of foreign currency. A firm keeps the price of its export at 50 in its own currency. What does a foreign buyer now pay?
Mission 3

Net exports and AD

  • Depreciation: exports cheaper abroad → export volumes rise. Imports cost more at home → import volumes fall.
  • So net exports (X − M) tend to rise, and AD shifts right (AD1 → AD2).
  • Step 1: AD1 meets SRAS below full capacity.
  • Step 2: AD1 → AD2: real GDP rises, the price level rises, unemployment tends to fall.
  • Step 3: imported raw materials and parts cost more, so SRAS shifts left (SRAS1 → SRAS2): more inflation, less of the output gain.
  • An appreciation does the reverse: net exports fall, AD shifts left.
  • A central bank interest rate cut tends to weaken the currency: a second channel from monetary policy to AD.
  • Say: "a depreciation shifts AD to the right" — not: "a movement along AD".

  • Terms of trade worsen: import prices rise in home currency; export prices don't.
  • Winners: exporters, firms competing with imports, the tourism industry.
  • Losers: firms buying imported inputs, consumers, people travelling abroad, borrowers with debts in foreign currency.
  • Real example: the pound fell sharply after the UK's EU referendum in June 2016. UK CPI inflation rose to 3.1% by November 2017, above the 2% target; the Bank of England put most of the overshoot down to the weaker pound raising import prices.
  • Say: "the current account may improve" — not: "a depreciation always cures a deficit".
Quick check

Mission 3

The Marshall-Lerner condition

  • A depreciation makes each import dearer. Whether spending on imports falls depends on how much the import volume falls.
  • Marshall-Lerner condition: starting from balanced trade, a depreciation improves the trade balance if PEDx + PEDm > 1 (absolute values: the price elasticities of demand for exports and for imports).
  • Illustrative data: exports = imports = 100 (home currency). The currency depreciates by 10%.
  • Short run: PEDx = 0.3, PEDm = 0.4. Sum = 0.7 < 1.
    • Exports: 100 × (1 + 0.1 × 0.3) = 103.
    • Imports: 100 × 1.1 × (1 − 0.1 × 0.4) = 105.6.
    • Trade balance: 103 − 105.6 = −2.6. It worsens.
  • Long run: PEDx = 0.8, PEDm = 0.7. Sum = 1.5 > 1.
    • Exports 108, imports 110 × 0.93 = 102.3, balance +5.7. It improves.
  • Say: "the sum of the elasticities must exceed 1" — not: "each elasticity must exceed 1".
Mission 3

The J-curve

  • Right after a depreciation, demand is inelastic: contracts are fixed, buyers take time to switch suppliers, and exporters need time to expand.
  • So the current account worsens first: the same imports cost more.
  • Over time, elasticities rise. Once the Marshall-Lerner condition holds, the balance improves.
  • Plotted against time, the path looks like a J.
  • Real example: in November 1967 the UK devalued the pound from 2.80 to 2.40 dollars (a 14.3% cut). The current account stayed in deficit in 1968 and moved into surplus in 1969.
Mission 3Graph lab

Trace the J-curve

Illustrative data: the currency depreciates by 10% in quarter 0. Elasticities rise from a sum of 0.7 to 1.5 over two years.

  • Takeaway: the balance worsens while elasticities are low, and improves only once their sum passes 1.
Quick check

Mission 3Evaluate

Does a weaker currency help?

It depends on:

  • Elasticities and time: buyers are slow to switch, so volumes change little at first; the gain may take years.
  • This is the J-curve.
  • Spare capacity: with output below its full employment level, more AD raises output and jobs; near full capacity, mostly inflation.
  • Import content of exports: in global supply chains, dearer imported parts eat into the price advantage.
  • Non-price competitiveness: quality, design and reliability may matter more than price.
  • Why the currency fell, and others' response: a fall caused by lost confidence may also deter investment; rivals may devalue too (competitive devaluation).
Mission check

?Investors abroad move funds into a country, and its currency appreciates sharply. Other things equal, which effect of the appreciation is most likely in the short run?

An appreciation is the mirror image of a depreciation: net exports and AD fall, and imported inflation eases.

Mission 4

Mission 4: What makes one country's goods cheaper to buy than another's, even when wages are higher?

Make a guess: What makes one country's goods cheaper to buy than another's, even when wages are higher?

Mission 4

What competitiveness means

  • International competitiveness: the ability of a country's firms to sell goods and services, at home and abroad, against foreign rivals.
  • Price competitiveness: selling at lower prices than rivals for similar quality.
  • Non-price competitiveness: quality, design, reliability, innovation, brand, after-sales service, delivery times.
  • Two price measures: relative unit labour costs and relative export prices.
  • Net trade (X − M) also depends on real incomes at home and abroad, exchange rates, the state of the world economy, protectionism and non-price factors.
Mission 4

Unit labour costs

  • Unit labour cost (ULC) = total labour costs ÷ output.
  • Or: labour cost per worker ÷ output per worker (productivity).
  • Illustrative data, same currency:
Labour cost per workerOutput per workerULC
Country A50,0002,000 units50,000 ÷ 2,000 = 25
Country B20,000500 units20,000 ÷ 500 = 40
  • A pays 2.5 times the wage, but its ULC is lower: each A worker produces 4 times as much.
  • Productivity is the key: it lets firms pay higher wages without raising ULC.
  • Say: "unit labour costs rose" — not: "wages rose", unless productivity stood still.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. In a year, labour cost per worker rises by 4% and output per worker rises by 4%. What happens to unit labour costs?
Mission 4

Worked example: relative unit labour costs

  • Relative ULC = home ULC ÷ trading partners' ULC (in a common currency) × 100.
  • Rising: less competitive than in the base year. Falling: more competitive.
  • Illustrative data. Year 1: home ULC = partners' ULC = 40. Index = 100.
  • Year 2: home pay per worker +5%, productivity +1%.
    • Home ULC = 40 × 1.05 ÷ 1.01 = 41.58 (about +4%: 5% − 1%).
    • Partners' ULC +2% = 40.80.
    • Relative ULC = 41.58 ÷ 40.80 × 100 = 101.9: less competitive.
  • Now the home currency depreciates 5% against partners' currencies.
    • Home ULC in partners' currency falls by 5%: 101.9 × 0.95 = 96.8: more competitive.
  • So the exchange rate can outweigh wage and productivity changes.
Mission 4

Relative export prices

  • Relative export prices = index of home export prices ÷ index of rivals' export prices (in a common currency) × 100.
  • Illustrative data: home export price index 104, rivals' 102. Relative export prices = 104 ÷ 102 × 100 = 102.0: less price competitive.
  • Home currency depreciates 3%: 104 × 0.97 ÷ 102 × 100 = 98.9: more price competitive.
  • Export prices depend on ULC, but also on other costs (energy, materials), profit margins and the exchange rate.
  • Say: "relative export prices" compare our export prices with rivals' export prices — not: with our import prices (that is the terms of trade).
Quick check
?Which change would make a country's relative export price index fall, so it becomes more price competitive?
Mission 4

What drives competitiveness

  • Price factors:
    • wage costs relative to productivity (investment, skills, technology, management)
    • the exchange rate
    • inflation relative to trading partners
    • taxes, regulation, energy costs, infrastructure
  • Non-price factors: quality, design, innovation (research and development), reliability, branding.

Mission 4

Benefits and problems

  • Benefits of being competitive:
    • export-led growth and more jobs
    • a stronger current account
    • more inward FDI
    • economies of scale from bigger markets
  • Problems of being uncompetitive:
    • current account deficits and falling shares of world markets
    • deindustrialisation and structural unemployment in exposed industries
    • slower growth; pressure on the currency, so dearer imports and inflation
    • less inward investment
  • Competing on low wages keeps costs down but also keeps living standards down. Competing on productivity raises both.
Mission 4Evaluate

How useful are the measures?

It depends on:

  • Non-price factors: a country with high relative ULC can still win on quality and brand.
  • Sector mix: ULC averages over the whole economy; exporters may differ from the average.
  • The exchange rate: it can swing relative costs fast, and reverse just as fast.
  • The route: lower relative ULC from productivity growth is lasting; from wage cuts it lowers living standards.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. In a year, Country K's labour cost per worker rises 6% and its output per worker rises 6%. Its trading partners' unit labour costs, in their own currencies, do not change. K's currency appreciates 10% against theirs. What happens to K's relative unit labour costs?

Competitiveness is relative and measured in a common currency. Pay, productivity and the exchange rate all count.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Current, capital and transfer spending. Transfers are not part of G.
  • Deficit = flow (one year); debt = stock (all past borrowing). A smaller deficit still adds to the debt.
  • Actual deficit = cyclical + structural; recovery removes only the cyclical part.
  • The burden is measured as debt ÷ nominal GDP; the ratio can fall even when the debt rises.

Mission 2

  • Actual real GDP fluctuates around a rising trend (potential output): the business cycle.
  • Phases: boom, peak, downturn, recession, trough, recovery.
  • Output gap = actual − potential. Below potential: spare capacity. Above potential: inflationary pressure.
  • Demand and supply shocks cause the swings.

Mission 3

  • Depreciation: exports cheaper abroad, imports more expensive at home, net exports and AD rise. Appreciation: the reverse.
  • The price level rises (AD and dearer inputs); importers, consumers and travellers lose, exporters gain.
  • Marshall-Lerner: a depreciation improves the trade balance only if PEDx + PEDm > 1.
  • J-curve: worse first, better later.

Mission 4

  • ULC = labour cost per worker ÷ output per worker. High wages with high productivity can still mean low ULC.
  • Relative ULC and relative export prices compare a country with its rivals in a common currency. Rising = less competitive.
  • Productivity, inflation, the exchange rate and non-price factors drive competitiveness.
