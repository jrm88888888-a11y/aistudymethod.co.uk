Shakespeare: Tragedy or Comedy

Component 1 is the Drama paper. Section A is your Shakespeare play, studied alongside a prescribed critical anthology; Section B is your other drama text. This lesson teaches the genre skills — tragedy and comedy — and how to use criticism, so it works whichever play your centre has chosen.

  • Section A (Shakespeare): a passage-and-whole-text essay that must engage with the critical anthology of extracts Edexcel supplies.
  • Genre, not gossip: the examiner rewards how you read a play as tragedy or comedy, using method and criticism — not plot retelling.
  • The five AOs: AO1 argument and terminology, AO2 dramatic method, AO3 context, AO4 connections, AO5 different interpretations.

Named plays appear only as swappable exemplars — Edexcel revises its set-text list, so the transferable genre skills are what matter. Press Start when you are ready.

Component 1 · the paper

How Section A actually works

Edexcel pairs your Shakespeare study with a critical anthology: a booklet of short extracts from critics and theatre practitioners. In the exam you are expected to use that critical material to build and complicate an argument.

  • You write one essay that ranges across the whole play but is triggered by a printed extract or theme.
  • Weaving in critical opinion — agreeing, qualifying, disagreeing — is how you access AO5 (different interpretations).
  • The play is set as a tragedy or a comedy, and the genre gives you your analytical vocabulary.

Golden rule: a critic is a sparring partner, not an authority to quote and obey. Marks come from doing something with the critical idea against the text.

Genre · the tragic toolkit

The vocabulary of tragedy

The tragic vocabulary the exam expects still comes from Aristotle’s Poetics (4th century BC), written about Greek drama and adapted by critics to Shakespeare.

  • Hamartia — the error or fatal flaw that begins the fall; argue whether it is a moral defect or a mistake of judgement.
  • Hubris — overreaching pride that ignores human or divine limits.
  • Peripeteia — the reversal, where the action swings against the protagonist.
  • Anagnorisis — recognition: the hero finally sees what the audience saw long before.
  • Catharsis — the purging or clarification of pity and fear in the audience.
  • Nemesis — the retribution that completes the pattern and restores a diminished order.

Use terms as tools. Naming peripeteia earns nothing; showing where the reversal falls and what the dramatist gains by placing it there is AO2.

AO5 · tragic criticism

Bradley, Kott and the critical quarrel

The anthology exists because critics disagree about how to read Shakespearean tragedy. Three touchstones:

  • A.C. Bradley, Shakespearean Tragedy (1904) — a character-centred tradition. Bradley reads the tragedies as studies of great individuals brought down by a flaw, treating the heroes almost as real psychologies.
  • Jan Kott, Shakespeare Our Contemporary (1964) — a political and absurdist reading: history as a brutal “Grand Mechanism” of power, the plays speaking to modern violence.
  • Psychoanalytic critics relocate the flaw inward, into repression and desire.

Later critics attacked Bradley for ignoring the play as a staged, structured artwork (the reaction associated with L.C. Knights). That quarrel — character versus dramatic construction — is itself excellent AO5 material.

Genre · the comic toolkit

How comedy is built

If your play is a comedy, a different set of conventions applies. Northrop Frye, in Anatomy of Criticism (1957), described comedy as an archetypal movement: a repressive law or blocking figure is overcome, the action passes through a “green world” of confusion and disguise, and a renewed society is sealed by marriage and reconciliation.

  • The blocking figure (the senex) — a father, ruler or law that obstructs young love.
  • Disguise, mistaken identity, cross-dressing — engines of comic confusion and of gender play.
  • The fool or clown — licensed truth-telling.
  • Festive inversion — Mikhail Bakhtin’s carnival: hierarchy is temporarily turned upside down before order returns.
  • The multiple marriage ending — comedy resolves outward into community, where tragedy narrows to death.

Dark comedy alert: the strongest essays notice what the happy ending excludes or silences — the figure left unmarried, unforgiven or unheard.

AO2 · dramatic method

Reading the play as a play

AO2 is dramatic method, not decoration. For any Shakespeare passage, look for:

  • Soliloquy and aside — privileged access to a mind that may be self-deceiving; the aside makes the audience a co-conspirator.
  • Blank verse and its collapse — when a verse-speaking figure drops into broken prose, the form enacts disorder or madness.
  • Dramatic irony — the audience knows what a character does not: the engine of both pity and comic pleasure.
  • Structure — the five-act shape, the placing of the reversal, the comic volta where confusion turns to resolution.
  • Stagecraft — entrances, exits, off-stage violence, props and the playhouse itself.

Convert feature into effect. Do not write “this is a soliloquy”; write what the soliloquy lets the dramatist do to the audience at this point in the arc.

AO3 · context

Production and reception

AO3 has two halves, and most candidates write only one.

  • Production — the world the play came from: Elizabethan and Jacobean beliefs about the ordered cosmos (the “Great Chain of Being”), kingship and succession, damnation, gender and marriage, and the material conditions of the open-air playhouse with boy actors playing women.
  • Reception — the world the play is read into. A comedy’s treatment of a humiliated outsider, or a tragedy of a racial outsider, lands very differently for a modern audience than for its first one.

Weld it. If deleting the context sentence leaves the analysis unchanged, it was decoration. Context is a lens, not a hat.

AO5 · using criticism

Deploying a critic so it earns marks

The anthology only pays off if a critical idea produces a textual claim.

  • Weak: “Bradley says the hero has a tragic flaw.” (report)
  • Strong: “A Bradleian focus on flaw makes the hero legible as a psychology, but Kott’s Grand Mechanism suggests the play cares less about his soul than about the machine of power that grinds it — and the text’s cold final couplet arguably sides with Kott.”

Set two critics against each other, then let the text arbitrate. That is AO5 and AO1 working together.

Attribution honesty: if you are unsure a critic said something, describe the position without a false quotation. Examiners reward accurate handling of ideas, not invented citations.
