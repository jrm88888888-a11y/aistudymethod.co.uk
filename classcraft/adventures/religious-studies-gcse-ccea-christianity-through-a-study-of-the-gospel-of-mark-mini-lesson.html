Christianity: the Gospel of Mark

Unit 5 studies five themes in Mark's Gospel: the identity of Jesus, Jesus the miracle worker, the teaching of Jesus (the Kingdom and the Sabbath), the death and resurrection, and the role and nature of Christian discipleship. Mark is the shortest, fastest and bluntest Gospel — and every screen here is anchored in CCEA's own set passages.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go and collect ⭐ stars. Watch for the Set passage and Evaluation (AO2) flags — CCEA awards marks for knowledge and understanding (AO1) and for analysis and evaluation (AO2). Press Start when you are ready.

The identity of Jesus · set passages: Mark 1: 1–13 · 4: 35–41 · 6: 30–44

John the Baptist, the storm, the feeding of the 5000

Mark opens at a sprint: "The beginning of the good news of Jesus Christ, the Son of God." The reader is told who Jesus is at once — and then watches the characters struggle to work it out. CCEA studies the titles Son of God, Messiah, Son of Man, Son of David and Saviour through these events.

  • John the Baptist (1: 1–13). John appears in the wilderness preaching "a baptism of repentance for the forgiveness of sins", clothed in camel's hair, eating locusts and wild honey. He points beyond himself: "The one who is more powerful than I is coming... I have baptised you with water; he will baptise you with the Holy Spirit." Jesus is baptised; the heavens are torn apart; the Spirit descends like a dove; a voice says, "You are my Son, the Beloved." At once the Spirit drives him into the wilderness for forty days, tempted by Satan, with the wild beasts, and angels wait on him. Mark tells the temptation in two verses — he is in a hurry to reach the action.
  • Calming the storm (4: 35–41). A great windstorm; the boat is swamped; Jesus is asleep on a cushion. "Teacher, do you not care that we are perishing?" He rebukes the wind and says to the sea, "Peace! Be still!" There is a dead calm. "Why are you afraid? Have you still no faith?" And the disciples ask the question the whole Gospel is driving at: "Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?"
  • Jesus feeds the 5000 (6: 30–44). He sees the crowd and has compassion, "because they were like sheep without a shepherd". With five loaves and two fish he feeds five thousand men, seated in groups of hundreds and fifties; twelve baskets of scraps are collected. The echoes are deliberate: a shepherd for Israel, and bread in the wilderness.
Set passages: Mark 8: 27–33 · 9: 2–13 · 10: 35–45 · 10: 46–52 · 11: 1–11

Peter, the Transfiguration, James and John, Bartimaeus, the entry

  • Peter's declaration (8: 27–33). On the road to Caesarea Philippi: "Who do you say that I am?" Peter: "You are the Messiah." Jesus sternly orders them to tell no one — and immediately begins to teach that the Son of Man must undergo great suffering, be rejected, be killed, and after three days rise again. Peter takes him aside and rebukes him. Jesus turns and says, "Get behind me, Satan! For you are setting your mind not on divine things but on human things."
  • The Transfiguration (9: 2–13). Six days later, up a high mountain with Peter, James and John: his clothes become dazzling white, and Elijah with Moses appear. Peter babbles about building three dwellings — "he did not know what to say, for they were terrified". The cloud and the voice: "This is my Son, the Beloved; listen to him!" Coming down, Jesus orders them to tell no one until the Son of Man has risen from the dead.
  • The request of James and John (10: 35–45). "Grant us to sit, one at your right hand and one at your left, in your glory." Jesus: "You do not know what you are asking." The other ten are angry. Then the key saying: "whoever wishes to become great among you must be your servant... for the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life a ransom for many."
  • Blind Bartimaeus (10: 46–52). A blind beggar shouts, "Jesus, Son of David, have mercy on me!" The crowd tells him to be quiet; he shouts louder. Jesus calls him; he throws off his cloak and springs up. "What do you want me to do for you?" — "My teacher, let me see again." — "Go; your faith has made you well." And he follows him on the way. The last person healed before Jerusalem is a beggar who sees exactly who Jesus is.
  • The entry into Jerusalem (11: 1–11). Jesus rides in on a colt; people spread cloaks and leafy branches and shout, "Hosanna! Blessed is the one who comes in the name of the Lord!" Then the anticlimax that only Mark records: he goes into the Temple, looks round at everything, and — since it is late — goes back out to Bethany. The reckoning is coming tomorrow.

The "messianic secret". Notice how often Jesus commands silence in Mark (1: 44; 8: 30; 9: 9). Scholars call this the messianic secret. The most persuasive explanation is theological: until the cross, "Messiah" will be misunderstood as a promise of conquest. Only a crucified Messiah can be safely proclaimed — which is why the first human being to confess Jesus as God's Son in Mark does so at the cross (15: 39).

Jesus the miracle worker · set passages: Mark 1: 21–28 · 1: 29–34 · 1: 40–45 · 2: 1–12 · 3: 1–6

Authority — and the trouble it causes

  • A man with an evil spirit (1: 21–28). In the Capernaum synagogue on the Sabbath. The crowd is astounded because he teaches "as one having authority, and not as the scribes"; the unclean spirit shouts, "I know who you are, the Holy One of God" — and Jesus silences it and drives it out.
  • Jesus heals many (1: 29–34). He raises Simon's mother-in-law by taking her hand, and the fever leaves her; that evening the whole city gathers at the door and he heals many, not permitting the demons to speak, because they knew him.
  • A man with a dreaded skin disease (1: 40–45). "If you choose, you can make me clean." Moved with pity, Jesus stretches out his hand and touches him — the untouchable — and says, "I do choose. Be made clean." He sternly warns him to say nothing, but the man proclaims it freely, so that Jesus can no longer enter a town openly.
  • A paralysed man (2: 1–12). Four friends dig through the roof and lower him down. "Son, your sins are forgiven." The scribes think: "It is blasphemy! Who can forgive sins but God alone?" Jesus answers, "so that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins" — and tells him to stand up, take his mat and walk.
  • A man with a paralysed hand (3: 1–6). Sabbath, synagogue, watchers waiting to accuse him. "Is it lawful to do good or to do harm on the sabbath, to save life or to kill?" They are silent. He looks round at them "with anger", grieved at their hardness of heart, and heals the man. The Pharisees go out and conspire with the Herodians — their political enemies — about how to destroy him. By chapter three, the plot to kill Jesus has begun.
Set passages: Mark 5: 21–24, 35–43 · 5: 25–34 · 7: 24–30 · 9: 14–29

Faith, fear and an argument Jesus loses

  • Jairus' daughter (5: 21–24, 35–43). A synagogue leader begs him to come; on the way, news arrives that the girl has died. "Do not fear, only believe." He puts the mourners outside, takes her hand and says, "Talitha koum" — "Little girl, get up." She does. He orders them to tell no one, and to give her something to eat: a small, tender, entirely practical detail.
  • A woman with a haemorrhage (5: 25–34). Twelve years of bleeding, twelve years of doctors, all her money gone, and — under purity law — twelve years of being untouchable. She touches his cloak from behind. "Who touched my clothes?" She comes in fear and trembling and tells him the whole truth. "Daughter, your faith has made you well; go in peace." He gives her back not only her health but her place among people.
  • The Syro-Phoenician woman's daughter (7: 24–30). A Gentile woman begs him to heal her daughter. His reply is hard: "Let the children be fed first, for it is not fair to take the children's food and throw it to the dogs." She answers, "Sir, even the dogs under the table eat the children's crumbs." — "For saying that, you may go; the demon has left your daughter." It is the only place in the Gospels where someone wins an argument with Jesus, and Mark places it just before the gospel starts spreading beyond Israel.
  • A boy with an evil spirit (9: 14–29). The disciples have failed. The father says, "if you are able to do anything, have pity on us." — "If you are able! All things can be done for the one who believes." The father cries out the most honest sentence in the New Testament: "I believe; help my unbelief!" Jesus heals the boy, and tells the disciples privately, "This kind can come out only through prayer."

Contemporary meaning (AO2): Christians take from these accounts that faith may be imperfect and still be enough; that Jesus consistently restores the excluded; and that healing in Mark is never merely medical — it is restoration to community. Sceptics argue the accounts are later legend, or explain them psychosomatically. A strong answer states the objection accurately before responding to it.

The teaching of Jesus · set passages: Mark 1: 14–15 · 2: 23–28 · 4: 1–20 · 4: 21–23 · 4: 26–29 · 4: 30–32

The kingdom and the Sabbath

  • The coming of the Kingdom (1: 14–15). Jesus' first words in Mark, and the summary of everything else: "The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God has come near; repent, and believe in the good news."
  • The Sabbath (2: 23–28). The disciples pluck grain as they walk. The Pharisees object. Jesus cites David eating the bread of the Presence and concludes: "The sabbath was made for humankind, and not humankind for the sabbath; so the Son of Man is lord even of the sabbath." He does not abolish the Sabbath — he insists it was given as a gift, not a trap.
  • The Sower (4: 1–20). Path, rocky ground, thorns, good soil. The word is snatched away, withers in persecution, is choked by "the cares of the world and the lure of wealth", or bears fruit thirty, sixty, a hundredfold. A parable about hearing.
  • The Lamp on a Stand (4: 21–23). Nobody brings a lamp to put it under a basket or a bed. "For there is nothing hidden, except to be disclosed." The kingdom is secret for now, not for ever.
  • The Growing Seed (4: 26–29). A man scatters seed, sleeps and rises night and day, "and the seed would sprout and grow, he does not know how". The earth produces of itself. Then the harvest. Growth is God's work, not the sower's cleverness — a rebuke to anxious activism.
  • The Mustard Seed (4: 30–32). The smallest of seeds becomes the greatest of shrubs, so that the birds can nest in its shade. Twelve unimpressive Galileans are the mustard seed of the kingdom.

Set passages: Mark 10: 13–16 · 10: 17–31 · 12: 28–34

Children, wealth and the greatest command

  • Jesus and the children (10: 13–16). The disciples turn the children away; Jesus is indignant: "Let the little children come to me; do not stop them; for it is to such as these that the kingdom of God belongs. Whoever does not receive the kingdom of God as a little child will never enter it." He takes them in his arms and blesses them. The point is not childish innocence but dependence: children in that society had no status and nothing to bargain with — and that, says Jesus, is the way in.
  • Entry into the Kingdom (10: 17–31). A man runs up: "Good Teacher, what must I do to inherit eternal life?" — "Why do you call me good? No one is good but God alone." He has kept the commandments since his youth. Jesus, looking at him, loved him, and said, "You lack one thing; go, sell what you own, and give to the poor... then come, follow me." He goes away grieving, "for he had many possessions." "It is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle..." The disciples are astounded — "Then who can be saved?" — "For mortals it is impossible, but not for God; for God all things are possible."
  • The Greatest Command (12: 28–34). A scribe asks which commandment is first. Jesus answers with the Shema (Deut. 6: 4–5): "Hear, O Israel: the Lord our God, the Lord is one; you shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, and with all your soul, and with all your mind, and with all your strength." And the second: "You shall love your neighbour as yourself" (Lev. 19: 18). The scribe agrees warmly, adding that this is "much more important than all whole burnt offerings". Jesus tells him, "You are not far from the kingdom of God" — a rare and generous word to a religious expert.

The death and resurrection of Jesus · set passages: Mark 11: 15–19 · 14: 1–2, 10–11, 12–26, 27–31, 32–50, 53–65

Temple, plot, supper, garden, trial

  • Jesus goes to the Temple (11: 15–19). He drives out those buying and selling, overturns the tables of the money changers and the seats of the dove sellers, and will not let anyone carry anything through. "Is it not written, 'My house shall be called a house of prayer for all the nations'? But you have made it a den of robbers." The chief priests hear it and look for a way to kill him. This is the act that seals his death.
  • The plot (14: 1–2) — two days before Passover, "not during the festival, or there may be a riot". Judas (14: 10–11) goes to the chief priests to betray him, and they promise him money.
  • The Last Supper (14: 12–26). "One of you will betray me — one who is dipping bread into the bowl with me." He takes bread: "Take; this is my body." And the cup: "This is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many." He will not drink of the fruit of the vine again until he drinks it new in the kingdom of God.
  • Peter's denial predicted (14: 27–31). "You will all become deserters." Peter: "Even though all become deserters, I will not." Jesus: "before the cock crows twice, you will deny me three times."
  • Gethsemane and the arrest (14: 32–50). "Abba, Father, for you all things are possible; remove this cup from me; yet, not what I want, but what you want." Three times he returns to find them sleeping. Judas kisses him. A bystander cuts off an ear. "All of them deserted him and fled" — Mark even records the young man who runs away naked, leaving his linen cloth behind.
  • Before the Jewish Council (14: 53–65). The testimony does not agree. The high priest: "Are you the Messiah, the Son of the Blessed One?" Jesus: "I am" — the only place in Mark where he says it outright, and he says it when it will cost him his life. The high priest tears his clothes: blasphemy. They spit at him, blindfold and strike him.
Set passages: Mark 15: 1–20 · 15: 21–41 · 15: 42–47 · 16: 1–8

Crucifixion, burial and the empty tomb

  • The Roman trial and mocking (15: 1–20). Pilate asks, "Are you the King of the Jews?" — "You say so." Then silence, "so that Pilate was amazed". The crowd asks for Barabbas. The soldiers clothe him in purple, twist a crown of thorns, salute him — "Hail, King of the Jews!" — and strike him. Every mockery in Mark is unintentionally true.
  • Crucifixion and death (15: 21–41). Simon of Cyrene is compelled to carry the cross to Golgotha. Jesus is crucified between two bandits under the inscription "The King of the Jews". Passers-by jeer: "He saved others; he cannot save himself." Darkness covers the land from noon until three. He cries, "Eloi, Eloi, lema sabachthani?" — "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" — and breathes his last. The curtain of the Temple is torn in two, from top to bottom. And the centurion, the man who killed him, says: "Truly this man was God's Son!" The women — Mary Magdalene, Mary and Salome — watch from a distance.
  • The burial (15: 42–47). Joseph of Arimathea, a respected council member, boldly asks Pilate for the body, wraps it in linen and lays it in a tomb hewn from rock, rolling a stone against the door. The women see where he is laid.
  • The resurrection (16: 1–8). Very early on the first day of the week the women bring spices, worrying who will roll away the stone — and find it already rolled back. A young man in a white robe says: "Do not be alarmed; you are looking for Jesus of Nazareth, who was crucified. He has been raised; he is not here.... he is going ahead of you to Galilee." And they flee, "for terror and amazement had seized them; and they said nothing to anyone, for they were afraid."

The ending. Mark's earliest and best manuscripts stop at 16: 8 — on fear and silence. The longer endings printed in many Bibles (16: 9–20) do not appear in those manuscripts. Scholars debate whether the original ending was lost or whether Mark meant it: a Gospel that breaks off with frightened disciples hands the story to the reader. Knowing this — and citing it accurately — is a mark of real GCSE quality.

Christian discipleship · set passages: Mark 1: 16–20 · 2: 13–17 · 3: 13–19 · 6: 1–6 · 6: 7–13

Called, chosen, rejected, sent

  • Jesus calls his disciples (1: 16–20; 2: 13–17). "Follow me and I will make you fish for people." Immediately Simon and Andrew leave their nets; James and John leave the boat and their father Zebedee — the cost is family as well as work. Then he calls Levi from the tax booth and eats in his house with "tax collectors and sinners". Criticised, he replies: "Those who are well have no need of a physician... I have come to call not the righteous but sinners."
  • Jesus chooses the Twelve (3: 13–19). He appoints twelve "to be with him, and to be sent out to proclaim the message, and to have authority to cast out demons." Discipleship in Mark is both — company and commission. The list ends, without comment, "and Judas Iscariot, who betrayed him."
  • The challenge of witnessing (6: 1–6). In his hometown they take offence: "Is not this the carpenter, the son of Mary?" — "Prophets are not without honour, except in their hometown." Mark says bluntly that he could do no deed of power there, except cure a few sick people, and he was amazed at their unbelief. Rejection is not a sign of failure; Jesus met it too.
  • The mission of the Twelve (6: 7–13). Sent out two by two with authority over unclean spirits, ordered to take nothing for the journey except a staff — no bread, no bag, no money in their belts; sandals, but not two tunics. If a place will not welcome them, "shake off the dust that is on your feet". They proclaim repentance, cast out demons, anoint the sick with oil and cure them. Deliberate vulnerability: they must depend on hospitality, and their message must stand on its own.
Set passages: Mark 8: 34–38 · 9: 42–50 · 12: 13–17 · 12: 41–44 · 14: 66–72

The cost, the coin, the widow, and Peter

  • The cost of being a disciple (8: 34–38). "If any want to become my followers, let them deny themselves and take up their cross and follow me. For those who want to save their life will lose it, and those who lose their life for my sake, and for the sake of the gospel, will save it. For what will it profit them to gain the whole world and forfeit their life?" To his first hearers, "take up your cross" was not a metaphor about minor inconveniences — they had watched people carry crossbeams to their execution.
  • Coping with temptation (9: 42–50). Fierce, deliberately shocking language: better a millstone round your neck than to cause one of these little ones to stumble; if your hand causes you to stumble, cut it off. It is hyperbole — exaggeration for effect, a standard rabbinic device — but the seriousness is real: deal ruthlessly with what destroys you. "Have salt in yourselves, and be at peace with one another."
  • Paying taxes (12: 13–17). A trap set by Pharisees and Herodians. "Whose head is this, and whose title?" — "The emperor's." — "Give to the emperor the things that are the emperor's, and to God the things that are God's." And they were utterly amazed. The state has a claim; it does not have an unlimited claim.
  • The widow's offering (12: 41–44). Rich people put in large sums; a poor widow puts in two small copper coins. Jesus calls the disciples over: "this poor widow has put in more than all those who are contributing... they contributed out of their abundance; but she out of her poverty has put in everything she had, all she had to live on." Generosity is measured by what it costs, not by what it totals.
  • The pressure of discipleship (14: 66–72). In the courtyard below, Peter denies him three times, the last with curses and an oath. The cock crows the second time. He remembers — and breaks down and weeps. Mark, traditionally linked to Peter's own preaching, does not spare him.

Discipleship today (AO2): CCEA links these to identity, belonging, meaning, purpose, truth, values and community cohesion. Mark gives you a rare gift for evaluation: his disciples are consistently useless — they misunderstand, argue about rank, fall asleep, run away. Any argument that "real Christians would never fail" collapses against the Gospel's own evidence.
