Mission 1

Mission 1: If a storm stops or a crowd is fed, does it matter whether it really happened?

Make a guess: If a storm stops or a crowd is fed, does it matter whether it really happened?

Mission 1

What is a nature miracle?

  • A miracle in the Gospels is an act of power. Mark calls them "mighty works" and presents them as showing God at work.
  • Nature miracles: Jesus shows power over the natural world (wind, sea, food), not only over illness or evil spirits.
  • In the Jewish scriptures, only God rules the sea and feeds his people in the wilderness.
  • So the question each story raises is: who is this?
  • Many Christians see Jesus' miracles as signs that God's Kingdom has come near (1:15); in Mark they call for faith.
Mission 1

Calming the storm

  • Evening on the Sea of Galilee; Jesus says, "Let's go over to the other side." (Mark 4:35)
  • A great storm fills the boat with water; Jesus is asleep on a cushion (4:37–38).
  • The disciples wake him: "Teacher, don't you care that we are dying?" (Mark 4:38)
  • He rebukes the wind, "Peace! Be still!", and there is a great calm (4:39).
  • He asks: "Why are you so afraid? How is it that you have no faith?" (Mark 4:40)
Mission 1Read the source

"Who then is this?"

"They were greatly afraid and said to one another, 'Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?'" (Mark 4:41)

"He makes the storm a calm, so that its waves are still." (Psalm 107:29)

  • In the Psalm, it is God who stills the storm.

Q1 What are the disciples afraid of at the end of the story?

Show answer

Not the storm now, but the power of the one in the boat.

Q2 What answer does the Psalm suggest to their question?

Show answer

Jesus does what God does.

Mission 1

Feeding the five thousand

  • The Twelve return from their mission; Jesus takes them to a quiet place to rest (6:30–31).
  • A crowd follows. Jesus "had compassion on them because they were like sheep without a shepherd" (Mark 6:34) and teaches them.
  • Disciples: send them away to buy food. Jesus: "You give them something to eat." (Mark 6:37)
  • Five loaves and two fish. "He took the five loaves and the two fish; and looking up to heaven, he blessed and broke the loaves, and he gave to his disciples to set before them" (Mark 6:41).
  • "They all ate and were filled." Twelve baskets left over; five thousand men ate (6:42–44).
  • Took, blessed, broke, gave: Mark uses the same four actions with bread at the Last Supper (14:22).
  • Echoes: God feeding Israel with manna (Exodus 16); the prophet Elisha feeding a hundred, with some left over (2 Kings 4:42–44); the shepherd of Psalm 23 and Ezekiel 34.
Quick check
?In the feeding of the five thousand, Jesus took the loaves, "blessed and broke" them and gave them. Why do many Christians link this with the Last Supper?
Mission 1Diversity

What do the nature miracles show?

  • Identity: for Christians, Jesus has God's power over nature, so they see him as the Son of God.
  • Compassion: he feeds the hungry and cares for the frightened.
  • The Messiah's banquet: Jews hoped for a great feast in God's Kingdom; the feeding is a foretaste.
  • Interpretations among Christians:
  • Literal: real events; God, who made nature, can act in it (Catholic, Orthodox and many evangelical Christians).
  • Sharing: some liberal Christians suggest the crowd shared food they had hidden; the "miracle" was generosity.
  • Symbolic: stories that teach trust in Christ in hard times, and point to the Eucharist.
  • Significance today: trust in God in crisis; the Eucharist; Christian charities such as CAFOD, Christian Aid and Tearfund "give them something to eat".
Mission 1

Non-religious arguments

  • Science: storms on the Sea of Galilee rise and drop suddenly; calm can follow by chance.
  • Natural explanations: the crowd may simply have shared food they had brought.
  • David Hume (1711–1776): testimony to miracles is never strong enough to outweigh the uniform experience of nature; and miracle stories "are observed chiefly to abound among ignorant and barbarous nations" (An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding, 1748, Section X).
  • Late and biased witnesses: the stories were written decades later by believers.
  • No proof of divinity: in the Bible, prophets such as Elisha also work wonders; Mark warns that false messiahs will show "signs and wonders" (13:22).
  • Humanists usually see the miracles as legend or symbolic stories.
Quick check
?Which statement is a non-religious argument against the nature miracles, rather than a Christian interpretation of them?
Mission 1Evaluate

"The nature miracles must have really happened for them to matter to Christians."

  • Reasons for: Christians claim Jesus is divine; if he did not have God's power, the stories mislead. Mark presents them as events, with places and numbers. Paul says faith is empty if Christ is not raised (1 Corinthians 15:14, paraphrase); many apply the same logic to miracles.
  • Reasons against: Jesus refuses to give a "sign" to prove himself (8:11–12); the stories' message (trust, compassion, the Eucharist) holds even if read symbolically; many Christians' faith rests on the resurrection and on experience of God, not on these stories.
  • Christian responses to science: a natural cause and God's action are not rivals (God can act through nature and its timing); laws describe what usually happens, not what God must do.
  • Judge it by: Can a story be true in meaning without being literal? What does "proof" mean in religion? Is Hume's standard of evidence fair to unique events?
Mission 1Worked answer

Two contrasting beliefs

Question: Explain two contrasting beliefs about the feeding of the five thousand.

  • Belief 1: It really happened as Mark says. Development: God's Son has power over creation, so Jesus multiplied the bread (Mark 6:41–44); Catholic and many evangelical Christians hold this.
  • Belief 2: It is a story of sharing, or a symbol. Development: the crowd shared their food, or the story points to the Eucharist; some liberal Christians and many non-religious people take this view.
Mission check

?A firefighter rescues a child moments before a bridge collapses. The child's mother says, "That was God." A scientist says, "That was training and luck." Which response is most like the view of many Christians about Mark's nature miracles?

Christians differ on how literally to read the nature miracles, but agree that they point to who Jesus is.

Mission 2: Jesus' own disciples failed to free a suffering boy. What went wrong?

Make a guess: Jesus' own disciples failed to free a suffering boy. What went wrong?

Mission 2

Exorcism in Mark

  • Exorcism: driving out an evil (Mark says "unclean") spirit or demon from a person.
  • In the first century, many Jews and Gentiles believed spirits could seize people and cause illness, fits, silence or violence.
  • Mark presents Jesus' exorcisms as a battle: God's Kingdom against Satan.
  • Jesus: no one can plunder a strong man's house "unless he first binds the strong man" (Mark 3:27).
  • Jesus commands with a word. Other exorcists of the time used spells, names or objects.
  • The spirits recognise who Jesus is, even when people do not.
Mission 2

The man in the synagogue

  • Capernaum, the Sabbath. People are amazed: Jesus teaches "as having authority, and not as the scribes" (1:22).
  • A man with an unclean spirit cries out: "I know who you are: the Holy One of God!" (Mark 1:24)
  • Jesus: "Be quiet, and come out of him!" (Mark 1:25) The spirit shakes him and leaves.
  • The crowd: "For with authority he commands even the unclean spirits, and they obey him!" (Mark 1:27)
  • Mark's first miracle: teaching and power go together. Jesus' fame spreads across Galilee (1:28).
  • Silencing the spirit is part of what scholars call the Messianic secret (in Mark, Jesus often orders silence about who he is): he does not want to be named this way yet.
Quick check
?In Mark's exorcism stories, the spirits call Jesus "the Holy One of God" or "Son of the Most High God". What point do many Christians think Mark is making?
Mission 2

The boy: a scene of failure

  • Jesus comes down from the Transfiguration with Peter, James and John (9:2–13).
  • Below, a crowd, arguing scribes, and the other disciples, who have failed.
  • The father: his son has a spirit that makes him mute, throws him down, makes him foam at the mouth (9:17–18).
  • "I asked your disciples to cast it out, and they weren't able." (Mark 9:18)
  • Jesus: "Unbelieving generation, how long shall I be with you?" (Mark 9:19)
  • Ill "from childhood"; thrown into fire and water. The father: "If you can do anything, have compassion on us and help us." (9:21–22)
Mission 2Read the source

"Help my unbelief!"

"Jesus said to him, 'If you can believe, all things are possible to him who believes.' Immediately the father of the child cried out with tears, 'I believe. Help my unbelief!'" (Mark 9:23–24)

  • Jesus turns the father's "if you can" back on him: the question is not Jesus' power but the father's trust.

Q1 What two things does the father say at once?

Show answer

He believes, and he admits his belief is weak.

Q2 Does Jesus refuse to help because the faith is weak?

Show answer

No: he heals the boy, and takes him by the hand, 9:25–27.

Mission 2

"Why couldn't we?"

  • The boy seems dead; "Jesus took him by the hand and raised him up" (9:26–27).
  • Privately the disciples ask: "Why couldn't we cast it out?" (Mark 9:28)
  • Jesus: "This kind can come out by nothing but by prayer and fasting." (Mark 9:29)
  • Problems of discipleship shown here:
  • Weak faith: the "unbelieving generation" includes the disciples (9:19).
  • Relying on themselves: earlier they had been given authority and had cast out demons (6:7, 13); perhaps they treated it as their own power, not God's.
  • Lack of prayer: Jesus has just been on the mountain, where God's voice spoke (9:7); below, they have been arguing (9:14).
  • Pride: straight after, they argue about who is the greatest (9:33–34).
Quick check
?According to Mark 9:28–29, why could the disciples not cast out the spirit?
Mission 2Diversity

Exorcism today

  • Spiritual evil is real: Catholic, Orthodox and many evangelical Christians believe evil spirits exist.
  • Pentecostal and charismatic churches practise deliverance ministry: prayer to free people from evil.
  • Medical reading: many Christians think Mark describes conditions we now treat as epilepsy or mental illness; Jesus' care still matters.
  • Symbolic reading: the stories show Jesus freeing people from whatever enslaves them; some read "Legion" as a picture of Roman occupation.
  • Non-religious view: Humanists see possession as a pre-scientific explanation; they warn that exorcism can harm vulnerable people who need medical help.
Mission 2Evaluate

"Mark's exorcism stories have little meaning for Christians today."

  • Reasons for: modern medicine explains fits and mental illness; belief in possession can lead to harm; many Christians now read these stories as about illness, not demons.
  • Reasons against: many Christians still believe in spiritual evil and pray for deliverance; the stories show Jesus' authority over all evil; the boy's story teaches prayer and honest faith ("Help my unbelief!", 9:24); they model compassion for people others fear.
  • Humanist view: the stories have historical interest and show compassion, but no supernatural truth.
  • Judge it by: Does the meaning depend on believing in demons? Who decides what a story means today? Does a medical explanation remove a spiritual one?
Mission 2Worked answer

Problems of discipleship

Question: Explain two problems of discipleship shown in the healing of the boy (Mark 9:14–29).

  • Problem 1: weak faith. Source: Jesus calls them an "unbelieving generation" (9:19). Development: Christians today may also doubt, and need to say, like the father, "Help my unbelief!"
  • Problem 2: relying on their own strength. Source: "This kind can come out by nothing but by prayer" (9:29). Development: Christians believe service depends on God, so prayer comes before action.
Mission check

?A new youth leader runs a successful first term, then a crisis comes and nothing she tries works. An older Christian says: "You stopped praying because things were going well." Which part of Mark 9:14–29 is this advice most like?

In Mark, even trusted disciples fail when they rely on themselves; Christians take the boy's story as a call to honest faith and prayer.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why did Jesus describe God's Kingdom as a seed, not a palace?

Make a guess: Why did Jesus describe God's Kingdom as a seed, not a palace?

Mission 3

The Kingdom and the parables

  • Jesus' first words in Mark: "The time is fulfilled, and God's Kingdom is at hand! Repent, and believe in the Good News." (Mark 1:15)
  • Kingdom of God: God's reign or rule, not a place on a map; wherever God's will is done.
  • Jesus teaches about it in parables: short stories from everyday life with a deeper meaning.
  • Farming images: his hearers knew sowing, soil, weeds and harvest.
  • Parables invite hearers to think and decide: "Whoever has ears to hear, let him hear." (Mark 4:9)
Mission 3

The parable of the sower

  • A farmer scatters seed widely by hand (4:3).
  • Seed on the path: birds eat it (4:4).
  • On rocky ground: springs up fast, but has no root; the sun scorches it (4:5–6).
  • Among thorns: choked, no fruit (4:7).
  • On good soil: a huge harvest, "thirty times, … sixty times, … one hundred times" (4:8).
  • Much seed seems wasted; yet the good soil gives a rich harvest.
Mission 3

Four kinds of hearers

  • "The farmer sows the word." (Mark 4:14) The seed is God's message.
  • Path: they hear, but Satan takes the word away at once (4:15).
  • Rocky ground: receive it with joy, but fall away under "oppression or persecution" (4:16–17).
  • Thorns: "the cares of this age, and the deceitfulness of riches" choke it (4:19).
  • Good soil: hear, accept and bear fruit (4:20).
Quick check
?A new Christian is keen at first, but stops going to church after being mocked at work for her faith. Which soil in the parable of the sower does this most match?
Mission 3

The lamp on a stand

  • "Is a lamp brought to be put under a basket or under a bed? Isn't it put on a stand?" (Mark 4:21)
  • A lamp exists to give light; the Kingdom is not meant to stay hidden.
  • "There is nothing hidden except that it should be made known." (4:22)
  • For now Jesus' teaching is partly hidden in parables (4:11); one day it will be clear to all.
  • Christian values: faith shown openly in action and honesty, not kept private.
Mission 3

The growing seed

  • Found only in Mark (4:26–29).
  • A man scatters seed, then sleeps and rises; the seed grows "though he doesn't know how" (4:27).
  • "The earth bears fruit by itself: first the blade, then the ear, then the full grain in the ear." (Mark 4:28)
  • When the grain is ripe, the harvest comes (4:29): a common biblical image of God's final judgement.
  • Meaning: the Kingdom grows by God's power, quietly and in stages; humans sow but cannot force it.
  • Significance: patience and trust for Christians who see little result.
Mission 3Read the source

The mustard seed

"It's like a grain of mustard seed, which, when it is sown in the earth, though it is less than all the seeds that are on the earth, yet when it is sown, grows up and becomes greater than all the herbs, and puts out great branches, so that the birds of the sky can lodge under its shadow." (Mark 4:31–32)

  • Birds nesting in a great tree was a prophets' image of nations gathering under God's rule (Ezekiel 17:23).

Q1 What contrast does the parable draw?

Show answer

A tiny beginning, a huge result.

Q2 What might the birds stand for?

Show answer

People of every nation finding shelter in the Kingdom.

Quick check
?The growing seed and the mustard seed are both about the Kingdom. What do they have in common?
Mission 3

Four ways to understand the Kingdom

  • Present reality: the Kingdom is "at hand" (1:15); seen now in healings, forgiveness and changed lives.
  • Future hope: the full Kingdom comes at the end, at the harvest (4:29), when the Son of Man comes (13:26).
  • Inner state: God reigns in a person's heart: the good soil that hears and accepts (4:20).
  • Community: the Kingdom as a people gathered under God, like birds in the branches (4:32); many see this in the Church.
  • Most Christians hold several together: "already, but not yet".
Mission 3Diversity

Christians and the Kingdom today

  • Catholic teaching: the Church is the seed and beginning of the Kingdom on earth; it grows towards completion at the end of time.
  • Many evangelicals: the Kingdom begins in personal conversion, the inner response to the word; it shows in a changed life.
  • Social action and liberation theology: the Kingdom is built where the hungry are fed and the oppressed set free.
  • Future-focused Christians (e.g. some Adventists and Pentecostals) stress Christ's Second Coming to establish the Kingdom fully.
  • Values today: generous sharing of the faith (the sower), patience (the seed), openness (the lamp), courage under rejection (the tenants).
  • Non-religious view: Humanists may value the call to build a better society, without belief in God's reign.
Quick check
?A Christian group sets up a food bank and says, "This is the Kingdom of God breaking in right now." Which understanding of the Kingdom are they stressing?
Mission 3Evaluate

"The Kingdom of God is more about the future than the present."

  • Reasons for: the harvest is still to come (4:29); the mustard seed has not yet grown; Jesus speaks of the Son of Man "coming in clouds" (13:26); suffering and injustice show God's reign is not yet complete.
  • Reasons against: "the time is fulfilled, and God's Kingdom is at hand" (1:15); healings and exorcisms show it already here (3:27); the good soil bears fruit now (4:20); the scribe is "not far from God's Kingdom" (12:34).
  • Another view: "already, but not yet": present in seed, future in harvest.
  • Judge it by: What do the seed parables suggest about time? What does "at hand" mean? Does stressing the future make Christians less active now, or more hopeful?
Mission check

?A teacher starts a lunchtime club for pupils who struggle to read. In the first weeks only two come, but she keeps going; a year later it has thirty members and runs in other schools. Which parable does this most resemble?

Mark's Kingdom parables say God's reign begins small and hidden, meets mixed responses, grows by God's power and will be complete at the harvest.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Two copper coins and a jar of perfume: why did Jesus praise these two women above everyone else?

Make a guess: Two copper coins and a jar of perfume: why did Jesus praise these two women above everyone else?

Mission 4

The widow at the treasury

  • In the Temple courts, Jesus warns against scribes who "devour widows' houses" yet make long prayers (12:38–40).
  • He sits opposite the treasury. Many rich people give large sums (12:41).
  • A poor widow puts in two small coins, together a quadrans, a tiny amount (12:42).
  • Widows often had no income and no male protector: among the poorest people.
  • Jesus calls his disciples to notice her (12:43).
  • Context, beyond your set text: two days later, at Bethany, a woman pours a jar of costly perfume on Jesus' head. Guests complain it could have been sold for more than 300 denarii; Jesus defends her: "She has done a good work for me" (14:3–9).
Mission 4Read the source

"All that she had"

"Most certainly I tell you, this poor widow gave more than all those who are giving into the treasury, for they all gave out of their abundance, but she, out of her poverty, gave all that she had to live on." (Mark 12:43–44)

Q1 How can two tiny coins be "more"?

Show answer

They were all she had; the rich gave what they could spare.

Q2 What does Jesus measure a gift by?

Show answer

Its cost to the giver, not its size.

Quick check
?Some Christians read the widow's story as praise; others also read it as a warning about the Temple system. Which detail supports the second reading?
Mission 4Diversity

Giving like the widow today

  • Sacrificial giving: many Christians give a fixed share of income; some give a tithe (one tenth).
  • Cost, not size: the poorest gifts count fully with God; churches value time and care as well as money.
  • Simple living: some, like religious orders, give up possessions to serve.
  • Justice: the widow's story also warns religious leaders not to exploit the poor (12:40).
  • Pluralist society: Christians give alongside people of other faiths and none; for Christians, giving expresses trust in God, not only kindness.
  • Non-religious view: Humanists also value generosity, but would not urge the poor to give all they have.
Mission 4Evaluate

"The widow's example is unrealistic for Christians today."

  • Reasons for: giving away all you live on could leave a family in need; Christians also have duties to dependants (1 Timothy 5:8).
  • Reasons against: Jesus praises total trust in God; the point is the attitude of the heart (Matthew 6:21); many Christians live simply so others can live; small gifts by the poor still matter.
  • Another view: the story may be a lament at a system that takes from widows (12:40), not a command to copy her.
  • Judge it by: Is the story an instruction or an example of attitude? What does "all" mean in different lives? Who should give the most?
Mission check

?Two pupils donate to a hospice appeal. One, from a wealthy family, gives £50 from a large allowance. The other gives £5, all she had saved for a school trip. Applying Mark 12:41–44, how would many Christians compare the gifts?

Jesus measures a gift by its cost to the giver; many Christians see in the widow, and in other women in Mark, the self-giving that disciples are meant to have.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • In Mark, nature miracles show Jesus doing what, in the Jewish scriptures, only God does: stilling the sea, feeding his people in the wilderness.
  • Calming the storm asks "Who then is this?" and calls for faith.
  • Christians read them literally, as acts of sharing, or symbolically; non-religious people (Hume, Humanists) doubt the evidence and say they prove nothing about divinity.

Mission 2

  • Mark's exorcisms show Jesus' authority over evil and the coming of God's Kingdom; the spirits know who he is.
  • The disciples' failure with the boy shows problems of discipleship: weak faith, self-reliance, lack of prayer, pride.
  • Christians differ on possession today (spiritual, medical, symbolic); Humanists see a pre-scientific explanation.

Mission 3

  • The Kingdom of God is God's reign, "at hand" in Jesus; parables reveal it to those with ears to hear.
  • The sower shows four responses to the word.
  • The growing seed shows growth by God's power, quietly and in stages; the mustard seed, growth from tiny to great.
  • Christians understand the Kingdom as present and future, inner and communal: "already, but not yet".

Mission 4

  • The widow gave "all that she had to live on": Jesus values a gift by its cost; some also read a warning against exploiting the poor.
