Mission 1

Mission 1: Protons, electrons and neutrinos: what decides which family a particle belongs to?

Make a guess: Protons, electrons and neutrinos: what decides which family a particle belongs to?

Mission 1

Fundamental or composite?

  • Fundamental particle: no internal structure, as far as any experiment shows.
  • Fundamental: quarks and leptons.
  • Also fundamental: the gauge bosons (the exchange particles).
  • Composite: made of smaller particles. Protons and neutrons are made of quarks.
  • Quarks are never found alone: they are always bound inside composite particles (quark confinement).
Mission 1

The particle families

  • Hadrons: made of quarks; feel the strong interaction.
  • Baryons: three quarks (proton, neutron). Antibaryons: three antiquarks (antiproton, antineutron).
  • Mesons: a quark and an antiquark, e.g. pions (π).
  • Leptons: fundamental; do not feel the strong interaction. Electron, neutrino.
  • Gauge bosons: photon, W⁺, W⁻, Z⁰, gluon.
Quick check
?A particle is made of an up quark and a down antiquark. How is it classified?
Mission 1

Leptons

  • Leptons are fundamental and feel no strong interaction.
  • Examples: electron and neutrino, and their antiparticles.
Mission check

?A beam from an accelerator contains antineutrons, negative pions (π⁻), positrons and protons. Which of these particles never feel the strong interaction?

Family is decided by what a particle is made of, not by mass, charge or lifetime, and an antiparticle stays in its partner's family. Quarks or antiquarks inside: a hadron, which feels the strong interaction. Fundamental: a lepton, which does not.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Protons and neutrons are built from the same two kinds of quark. Why is only one of them charged?

Make a guess: Protons and neutrons are built from the same two kinds of quark. Why is only one of them charged?

Mission 2

The quarks

  • Up (u): charge +⅔e, baryon number +⅓.
  • Down (d): charge −⅓e, baryon number +⅓.
  • Strange (s): charge −⅓e, baryon number +⅓.
  • Antiquarks (ū, d̄, s̄): every number reversed. ū: −⅔e, d̄: +⅓e, baryon number −⅓.
Mission 2

Building baryons

  • Three quarks. Add the charges.
  • Proton uud: ⅔ + ⅔ − ⅓ = +1.
  • Neutron udd: ⅔ − ⅓ − ⅓ = 0.
  • Antiproton ūūd̄: −1. Antineutron ūd̄d̄: 0.
  • Baryon number: 3 × ⅓ = +1 (antibaryons: −1).
Quick check
?An antibaryon is made only of up and down antiquarks and has a charge of −2e. What is its quark content?
Mission 2

Building mesons

  • A quark and an antiquark.
  • Baryon number: ⅓ − ⅓ = 0.
  • π⁺ = ud̄: ⅔ + ⅓ = +1.
  • π⁻ = dū: −⅓ − ⅔ = −1. The π⁻ is the antiparticle of the π⁺.
Quick check
?A meson is built from up and down quarks and antiquarks only. Which charge can it not have?
Mission check

?Quarks combine only in certain ways. Which of these combinations can not exist as a particle?

Quark charges are fractions: up +⅔, down −⅓. A proton (uud) totals +1; a neutron (udd) totals 0.

Mission 3

Mission 3: An electron meets its antimatter twin and both vanish. What comes out, and why never just one thing?

Make a guess: An electron meets its antimatter twin and both vanish. What comes out, and why never just one thing?

Mission 3

Annihilation

  • Every particle has an antiparticle: same mass, opposite charge.
  • When a particle meets its antiparticle, they annihilate.
  • Their rest energy (mass) becomes the energy of photons: usually two gamma-ray photons.
  • e⁻ + e⁺ → γ + γ
  • Charge: −1 + 1 = 0 ✓ (photons are neutral).
  • Momentum: almost at rest, total momentum ≈ 0. One photon would carry momentum away, so there must be two, travelling in opposite directions.
  • Energy: total energy before = total photon energy after.
Quick check
?An electron and a positron annihilate, producing two photons. Which quantity is different after the annihilation from before it?
Mission 3

How much energy?

  • Slow electron and positron: each photon carries the rest energy of one electron: 0.511 MeV.
  • From ΔE = Δmc²: 2 × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × (3.00 × 10⁸)² = 1.64 × 10⁻¹³ J, shared between two photons.
  • Each photon: 8.2 × 10⁻¹⁴ J ≈ 0.51 MeV. Wavelength λ = hc/E ≈ 2.4 × 10⁻¹² m: a gamma ray.
  • If the particles were moving, their kinetic energy adds to the photon energy.
Quick check
?A muon and an antimuon, both almost at rest, annihilate to produce two photons. The rest energy of a muon is 105.7 MeV. What is the wavelength of each photon?
Mission 3

Pair production

  • The reverse: a photon's energy becomes a particle and its antiparticle.
  • γ → e⁻ + e⁺
  • Minimum photon energy = rest energy of the pair = 2 × rest energy of one particle.
  • Electron–positron: at least 2 × 0.511 = 1.022 MeV.
  • Extra photon energy becomes the particles' kinetic energy.
  • It happens near a nucleus, which takes some momentum: a lone photon cannot conserve both energy and momentum.
Mission 3

Making antimatter in accelerators

  • High-energy particles from accelerators collide; some of their kinetic energy becomes mass.
  • p + p → p + p + p + p̄: an antiproton is created along with an extra proton.
  • At least 2 × 938 MeV must become new rest energy. With a stationary target, more is needed: the products must also carry the incoming momentum. Head-on collisions avoid this waste.
  • Antiparticles are created in pairs with particles: charge and baryon number stay balanced.
  • Antimatter is stored in vacuum, held by magnetic and electric fields, so it never touches matter.
Mission check

?An electron and a positron, each with 0.30 MeV of kinetic energy, collide head-on and annihilate into two photons. The rest energy of an electron is 0.511 MeV. What is the energy of each photon?

An electron and a positron vanish into two gamma photons that fly apart in opposite directions. One photon alone could not conserve momentum. Any kinetic energy the pair had goes into the photons too.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Every particle can be pushed and pulled by four kinds of force. How do you know which ones it feels?

Make a guess: Every particle can be pushed and pulled by four kinds of force. How do you know which ones it feels?

Mission 4

Four fundamental interactions

  • Strong: acts on quarks, so on all hadrons. Range about 10⁻¹⁵ m, roughly a nucleon's diameter. Strongest.
  • Electromagnetic: acts on charged particles. Infinite range.
  • Weak: acts on all quarks and all leptons. Range about 10⁻¹⁸ m.
  • Gravitational: acts on all mass. Infinite range. By far the weakest.
  • Strength order: strong > electromagnetic > weak > gravitational.
Mission 4

Who feels what

  • Ask two questions: made of quarks? Charged?
  • Hadrons (made of quarks): strong and weak, plus gravity. Electromagnetic too if charged (neutral hadrons only slightly, through their charged quarks).
  • Leptons: weak, never strong, plus gravity. Electromagnetic too if charged.
  • Electron: gravity, electromagnetic, weak.
  • Neutrino: weak and gravity only. That is why neutrinos pass through the Earth.
Quick check
?Which of these particles never feels the strong interaction?
Mission 4

Forces by exchange

  • Particles exert forces by swapping exchange particles.
  • The exchange particles of the four forces are the gauge bosons.
  • Picture two skaters throwing a heavy ball back and forth: they push apart. (The picture cannot show attraction.)
  • Electromagnetic: the virtual photon.
  • Weak: W⁺ and W⁻.
  • Weak also: Z⁰. Strong: the gluon, between quarks. Gravity: the graviton (predicted, not yet detected).
Quick check
?In a hydrogen atom, the electron is attracted to the proton. Which particle is exchanged between them to produce this force?
Mission check

?Which interactions does a negative pion, π⁻ (quark content dū), experience?

Two questions decide it. Made of quarks? Then it feels the strong interaction. Charged? Then it feels the electromagnetic one. All quarks and leptons feel the weak interaction and gravity.

Mission 5

Mission 5: A neutron left on its own soon decays. What changes inside it when it does?

Make a guess: A neutron left on its own soon decays. What changes inside it when it does?

Mission 5

The weak interaction changes quarks

  • Quarks come in types, called flavours, such as up and down.
  • Only the weak interaction can change a quark's flavour.
  • Only the weak interaction involves neutrinos.
  • So β decay is a weak interaction: a quark changes flavour and a neutrino or antineutrino is emitted.
Mission 5

β⁻ decay at quark level

  • In a nucleus, or on its own: a neutron becomes a proton.
  • n → p + e⁻ + ν̄
  • udd → uud: one down quark becomes an up quark.
  • d → u + e⁻ + ν̄
  • Charge check: −⅓ = +⅔ + (−1) + 0 ✓
Mission 5

β⁺ decay at quark level

  • Only inside a nucleus: a proton becomes a neutron.
  • p → n + e⁺ + ν
  • uud → udd: one up quark becomes a down quark.
  • u → d + e⁺ + ν
  • Charge check: +⅔ = −⅓ + (+1) + 0 ✓
  • A free proton cannot do this: the products have more mass. The nucleus supplies the energy.
Quick check
?A carbon-11 nucleus (6 protons, 5 neutrons) holds 17 up quarks and 16 down quarks. It decays by β⁺ emission to boron-11. How many up and down quarks does the boron-11 nucleus hold?
Mission check

?In the Sun, the first step of fusion joins two protons into a deuterium nucleus (one proton and one neutron), and a positron and a neutrino are emitted. What happens at quark level, and which interaction is responsible?

One proton becomes a neutron (uud → udd) by the weak interaction, the same change as in β⁺ decay. The neutrino is the giveaway. This slow weak step is why the Sun burns for billions of years.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Charge and energy would both balance if a proton turned into a positron and light. So what forbids it?

Make a guess: Charge and energy would both balance if a proton turned into a positron and light. So what forbids it?

Mission 6

The bookkeeping rules

  • Some quantities are the same before and after every interaction and decay.
  • Charge (in units of e): always conserved.
  • Also baryon number and lepton number.
  • Check each quantity separately: add up the left side, add up the right side.
Quick check
?Two protons collide at high energy: p + p → p + n + X, where X stands for all the other particles produced. What must the total charge of X be?
Mission 6

Baryon number, B

  • Quark +⅓, antiquark −⅓.
  • Baryons (qqq), e.g. p, n: B = +1. Antibaryons: B = −1.
  • Mesons (q q̄): B = 0. Leptons and photons: B = 0.
  • Total B is the same before and after.
Mission 6

Lepton number, L

  • Leptons (e⁻, νₑ …): L = +1. Antileptons (e⁺, ν̄ₑ …): L = −1. Everything else: L = 0.
  • Total L is the same before and after.
Quick check
?A student proposes that a free neutron decays into a proton and an electron and nothing else: n → p + e⁻. Which conservation law that holds in all nuclear decays would this proposal break?
Mission 6

Worked example: allowed or forbidden?

  • Free neutron decay: n → p + e⁻ + ν̄ₑ
  • Q: 0 = (+1) + (−1) + 0 ✓
  • B: 1 = 1 + 0 + 0 ✓
  • L: 0 = 0 + 1 + (−1) ✓ → allowed.
  • Proposed: p → e⁺ + γ
  • Q: +1 = +1 + 0 ✓
  • B: 1 → 0 ✗ · L: 0 → −1 ✗ → forbidden.
  • One broken law is enough to rule an interaction out.
Mission check

?Which of these proposed interactions is forbidden by a conservation law?

A proton turning into a positron and light keeps charge and energy balanced, but breaks baryon number (1 → 0) and lepton number (0 → −1). No one has ever seen it.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Hadrons (made of quarks; feel the strong interaction): baryons (qqq), antibaryons (q̄q̄q̄), mesons (qq̄). Leptons: fundamental, no strong interaction.
  • Three classes: gauge bosons, leptons, hadrons (mesons and baryons).

Mission 2

  • u: +⅔e; d: −⅓e; each quark has baryon number +⅓; antiquarks have everything reversed.
  • Proton uud (+1), neutron udd (0); antiproton ūūd̄, antineutron ūd̄d̄; π⁺ ud̄, π⁻ dū.
  • s: −⅓e, baryon number +⅓.

Mission 3

  • Annihilation: particle + antiparticle → two photons; charge, energy and momentum are conserved.
  • Pair production needs a photon of at least 2 × the rest energy of one particle; extra energy becomes kinetic energy.

Mission 4

  • Four interactions: strong (quarks, hadrons; ~10⁻¹⁵ m), electromagnetic (charges; infinite), weak (all quarks and leptons; ~10⁻¹⁸ m), gravitational (all mass; infinite).
  • Hadrons feel strong and weak; leptons feel weak but never strong.
  • Forces come from exchange particles: virtual photon (electromagnetic), W± (weak).
  • Gluons (strong), Z⁰ (weak), graviton (gravity, predicted).

Mission 5

  • Only the weak interaction changes quark flavour or involves neutrinos.
  • β⁻: d → u + e⁻ + ν̄ (a neutron becomes a proton). β⁺: u → d + e⁺ + ν (a proton becomes a neutron, only in a nucleus).

Mission 6

  • Check each conserved quantity separately; one failure forbids the interaction.
  • Charge, baryon number and lepton number are conserved in every interaction.
