Mission 1

Mission 1: Why do some nerve signals race faster than a car while others creep along at walking pace?

Make a guess: Why do some nerve signals race faster than a car while others creep along at walking pace?

Mission 1

An action potential

  • A nerve impulse is an action potential: a brief reversal of the membrane potential, lasting about 1–2 ms.
  • Resting: −70 mV.
  • Depolarisation: a stimulus raises the potential to threshold (about −55 mV), then it shoots up to about +30 to +40 mV.
  • Repolarisation: it falls back towards −70 mV.
  • Hyperpolarisation: a brief dip below −70 mV, then back to rest.
Mission 1

Ions and gates

  • A stimulus opens some Na⁺ channels: Na⁺ diffuses in → depolarisation.
  • At threshold, voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open. More Na⁺ in → more depolarisation → more channels open: positive feedback.
  • At the peak, Na⁺ channels close. Voltage-gated K⁺ channels open: K⁺ diffuses out → repolarisation.
  • K⁺ channels are slow to close → too much K⁺ leaves → hyperpolarisation.
  • The Na⁺/K⁺ pump and leak channels then hold the resting potential.
  • Say: "K⁺ diffusing out repolarises the membrane" — not: "the pump repolarises it".
Quick check
?Which sequence of events produces depolarisation and then repolarisation during an action potential?
Mission 1

All or nothing

  • Below threshold: no action potential.
  • At or above threshold: a full-size action potential, the same size every time.
  • This is the all-or-nothing principle.
  • So a stronger stimulus cannot make a bigger action potential.
  • It makes more action potentials per second: a higher frequency.
Quick check
?Two oscilloscope traces (illustrative data) were recorded from the same sensory neurone. With a weak stimulus there were 5 action potentials in 100 ms; with a strong stimulus there were 20 action potentials in 100 ms. In both traces every action potential peaked at +30 mV. What do the traces show?
Mission 1

The refractory period

  • Straight after an action potential, that region cannot fire again: the refractory period.
  • Voltage-gated Na⁺ channels are closed and cannot reopen yet.
  • Effects: impulses are discrete (separate); they travel one way; their frequency is limited.
  • Worked example: refractory period = 2 ms.
  • Step 1: 2 ms = 0.002 s.
  • Step 2: maximum frequency = 1 ÷ 0.002 = 500 impulses per second.
Mission 1

Local currents carry it along

  • Na⁺ that entered at the active region diffuses sideways inside the axon.
  • Outside, Na⁺ moves towards the active region.
  • The next region is depolarised to threshold → its own voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open → a new action potential.
  • The region behind is refractory (it cannot fire again yet), so the impulse moves forwards only.
  • The ions only move a short way: the impulse travels, not the ions.
Mission 1

Myelin speeds things up

  • Myelinated fibres conduct much faster than non-myelinated fibres of similar diameter.
  • Myelin insulates: no ions cross the membrane under it.
  • So action potentials occur only at the nodes. Local currents spread to the next node.
  • The impulse jumps node to node: saltatory conduction. Fewer regions to depolarise → faster.
  • Say: "myelin insulates" — not: "myelin conducts the impulse".
Mission 1

What sets the speed?

  • Myelination: the biggest effect.
  • Axon diameter: wider axon → less resistance to current flow inside it → faster.
  • Temperature: warmer → ions diffuse faster → faster. Too hot → channel and pump proteins denature.

Mission 2

Mission 2: A nerve signal reaches a gap it cannot jump. So how does the message get across?

Make a guess: A nerve signal reaches a gap it cannot jump. So how does the message get across?

Mission 2

Inside a synapse

  • A synapse is a junction between a neurone and another cell.
  • Presynaptic side: the axon terminal, the synaptic knob.
  • It holds vesicles of neurotransmitter and many mitochondria.
  • The synaptic cleft: a gap of about 20 nm.
  • Postsynaptic membrane: carries specific receptor proteins.
  • At a cholinergic synapse the neurotransmitter is acetylcholine (ACh).
Mission 2

Transmission, step by step

  • (1) An action potential depolarises the presynaptic membrane.
  • (2) Calcium ion channels open; Ca²⁺ diffuses in.
  • (3) Ca²⁺ is a signal inside the neurone: vesicles move to and fuse with the presynaptic membrane. ACh is released by exocytosis.
  • (4) ACh diffuses across the cleft and binds to its complementary receptors.
  • (5) The receptors open Na⁺ channels; Na⁺ diffuses in → depolarisation: an excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP).
  • If the depolarisation reaches threshold, an action potential starts in the postsynaptic neurone.
  • Say: "ACh binds to receptors with a complementary shape" — not: "ACh enters the next neurone".
Quick check
?What is the role of calcium ions in the release of neurotransmitter from a presynaptic neurone?
Mission 2

Clearing the cleft

  • The signal must stop, or the postsynaptic cell would keep firing.
  • Neurotransmitter is removed: broken down, or taken back into the presynaptic neurone (reuptake).
  • Acetylcholinesterase (in the cleft) hydrolyses ACh into choline and ethanoic acid.
  • These diffuse back into the synaptic knob and are recombined into ACh, using ATP from the mitochondria.
  • ACh is then repackaged into vesicles.
Mission 2

One-way traffic

  • Signals cross a synapse in one direction only: presynaptic → postsynaptic.
  • Why: vesicles of neurotransmitter are only on the presynaptic side.
  • And receptors are only on the postsynaptic membrane.
  • This is unidirectionality.
Quick check
?Why can a signal pass in only one direction across a synapse?
Mission 2

Neurone to muscle

  • A synapse can link a neurone to an effector cell, such as a muscle fibre.
  • Motor neurone → skeletal muscle fibre: the neuromuscular junction.
  • Its neurotransmitter is acetylcholine: the same one used at many synapses between neurones.
  • ACh binds to receptors on the muscle fibre membrane → depolarisation → the fibre contracts.
  • The postsynaptic membrane here is folded: the motor end plate.
Quick check
?A motor neurone forms a neuromuscular junction with a skeletal muscle fibre. Which statement about acetylcholine at this junction is correct?
Mission check

?Calcium ions were removed from the fluid around a synapse. When the presynaptic neurone was stimulated, action potentials still reached the presynaptic terminal, but no response was recorded in the postsynaptic neurone. What does this result show?

The gap is crossed by a chemical, and the chemical is released only when calcium tells the vesicles to go.

Mission 3

Mission 3: One nerve cell can receive thousands of messages at once. How does it decide whether to fire?

Make a guess: One nerve cell can receive thousands of messages at once. How does it decide whether to fire?

Mission 3

Adding up: summation

  • One EPSP is usually too small to reach threshold.
  • Spatial summation: several presynaptic neurones release neurotransmitter at the same time → their EPSPs add.
  • Temporal summation: one presynaptic neurone fires several times in quick succession → its EPSPs add before they fade.
  • Total reaches threshold → action potential. Below threshold → none (all-or-nothing).
Mission 3

Brakes: inhibitory synapses

  • An inhibitory neurotransmitter (e.g. GABA) opens channels that let Cl⁻ in or K⁺ out.
  • The inside becomes more negative than at rest: hyperpolarised.
  • This is an inhibitory postsynaptic potential (IPSP).
  • The membrane is now further from threshold: an action potential is much less likely.
  • EPSPs and IPSPs act on the same membrane, so they sum: excitation minus inhibition.
  • Say: "an IPSP hyperpolarises the membrane" — not: "an inhibitory synapse is just a weaker excitatory one".
Quick check
?A postsynaptic neurone has a resting potential of −70 mV and a threshold of −55 mV. At the same moment it receives two excitatory inputs, each producing a +8 mV change, and one inhibitory input producing a −6 mV change. What is the result?
Quick check
?One presynaptic neurone synapses with a postsynaptic neurone. A single impulse gives an EPSP below threshold. When the presynaptic neurone fires several impulses in rapid succession, the postsynaptic neurone fires an action potential. Which explanation is correct?

Mission 3

Drugs at synapses: find the step

  • Ask: which step does the drug change? Release, receptor, breakdown or reuptake.
  • Same shape as the neurotransmitter → binds and activates receptors → more stimulation.
  • Binds and blocks receptors → less stimulation.
  • Inhibits the breakdown enzyme or blocks reuptake → neurotransmitter stays in the cleft → stimulation lasts longer.
  • You will be told what a drug does: apply it to these steps.
Quick check
?Organophosphate insecticides inhibit acetylcholinesterase. Predict the effect at the neuromuscular junctions of a poisoned animal.
Mission check

?A drug binds to GABA receptors on a postsynaptic neurone. When GABA is also bound, the drug makes the receptor's chloride ion channel open more often. What is the effect on the postsynaptic neurone?

A neurone fires only when its accelerators outweigh its brakes; a drug that strengthens the brakes quietens it.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Not one filament in a contracting muscle gets shorter. So how does the whole muscle shorten?

Make a guess: Not one filament in a contracting muscle gets shorter. So how does the whole muscle shorten?

Mission 4

From muscle to myofibril

  • Muscle → bundles → muscle fibres → myofibrils → sarcomeres.
  • A muscle fibre is very long and has many nuclei. It forms when many cells fuse.
  • Fibre membrane: sarcolemma. Fibre cytoplasm: sarcoplasm.
  • Inside each fibre:
    • Many myofibrils, packed side by side.
    • Many mitochondria: supply ATP.
    • Sarcoplasmic reticulum: stores calcium ions (Ca²⁺).
    • T-tubules: inward folds of the sarcolemma that carry impulses deep into the fibre.
Mission 4

The sarcomere

  • A sarcomere runs from one Z line to the next.
  • Thin actin filaments: attached to the Z lines.
  • Thick myosin filaments: in the middle, held at the M line.
  • Bands:
    • A band (dark): the full length of the myosin filaments, including overlap.
    • I band (light): actin only. Z line in its centre.
    • H zone: myosin only, in the centre of the A band.
  • Alternating dark and light bands: skeletal muscle is striated.
Mission 4

Sliding filaments

  • Frame 1 — Relaxed: little overlap; wide I band; wide H zone.
  • Frame 2 — Contracted: myosin heads pull actin towards the M line.
  • The filaments slide past each other. Neither gets shorter.
  • Z lines move closer → the sarcomere shortens → the whole muscle shortens.
  • I band narrows. H zone narrows, and may disappear. A band stays the same.
  • Say: "the filaments slide past each other" — not: "the filaments contract".
Quick check
?Electron micrographs of a sarcomere are taken before and after it contracts. Which set of changes is observed?
Mission 4

Read the data: overlap and force

  • Gordon, Huxley and Julian (1966) held single frog muscle fibres at fixed sarcomere lengths, stimulated them, and measured the force.
  • Maximum force: sarcomere length 2.0–2.25 µm.
  • Longer than 2.25 µm: force falls in a straight line, to zero at 3.65 µm.
  • Shorter than 2.0 µm: force also falls, as the filaments start to collide.
  • Why zero at 3.65 µm?
    • Myosin filament: 1.6 µm. Each actin filament (to the Z line): about 1.025 µm.
    • 1.6 + 1.025 + 1.025 = 3.65 µm: actin and myosin no longer overlap.
  • More overlap → more myosin heads reach actin → more force. Strong evidence for sliding filaments.
Quick check
?In the data on the earlier screen, a sarcomere held at 3.0 µm produces about 46% of the maximum force. Why is the force lower than at 2.2 µm?
Mission 4

The cross-bridge cycle

  • Frame 1 — An impulse spreads along the sarcolemma and down the T-tubules → the sarcoplasmic reticulum releases Ca²⁺ into the sarcoplasm.
  • Frame 2 — Ca²⁺ causes tropomyosin to move, exposing binding sites on actin.
  • Frame 3 — A myosin head (carrying ADP + Pi) binds actin: a cross-bridge. The head pivots: the power stroke pulls actin towards the M line. ADP and Pi are released.
  • AQA name for the cross-bridge: actinomyosin bridge.
  • Frame 4 — A new ATP binds the head → the head detaches. ATPase on the head hydrolyses ATP → ADP + Pi; this re-cocks the head.
  • The cycle repeats while Ca²⁺ and ATP are present.
  • Relaxation: Ca²⁺ is actively transported back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum (uses ATP); binding sites are blocked again.
  • Say: "Ca²⁺ uncovers the binding sites; ATP detaches the head" — not: "Ca²⁺ powers the stroke".
Quick check
?A drug blocks the release of calcium ions from the sarcoplasmic reticulum of a skeletal muscle fibre. The motor neuron still stimulates the fibre normally. What is the effect on the fibre?
Mission 4

Muscles only pull

  • Muscle exerts force only by contracting. It can pull; it cannot push.
  • So muscles work in antagonistic pairs: one contracts, the other relaxes and is stretched.
  • Elbow: biceps contracts → arm bends. Triceps contracts → arm straightens.
  • Tendons: tough, inelastic cords joining muscle to bone. They pass the pull to the bone.
  • The skeleton is incompressible: bones do not squash, so the pull moves them as levers.
  • Say: "the triceps contracts to straighten the arm" — not: "the biceps pushes the arm straight".
Quick check
?The biceps contracts and shortens to flex the elbow. When the arm is then straightened, how is the biceps returned to its longer, resting length?
Mission check

?After death, cells stop making ATP. A few hours later the skeletal muscles become rigid and cannot be stretched (rigor mortis). Which explanation fits the sliding filament model?

The filaments slide; the heads must let go to slide again. No ATP, no letting go.

Mission 5

The neuromuscular junction

  • Where a motor neurone meets a muscle fibre.
  • Impulse arrives → Ca²⁺ enters the axon terminal → vesicles release acetylcholine (ACh) by exocytosis.
  • ACh diffuses across the cleft → binds receptors on the sarcolemma → Na⁺ enters → depolarisation.
  • An action potential spreads along the sarcolemma and down the T-tubules → Ca²⁺ released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum (see Mission 4).
  • Acetylcholinesterase in the cleft hydrolyses ACh, so each impulse gives one response.
  • The sarcolemma at the junction is folded: more area, more ACh receptors.

Quick check
?The drug curare binds to the ACh receptors on the sarcolemma without opening them. What happens when a motor neurone carries impulses to a muscle treated with curare?
Mission 5

Keeping ATP topped up

  • Myosin heads and Ca²⁺ pumps use ATP fast. A fibre holds only enough ATP for a few seconds of hard work.
  • Phosphocreatine (creatine phosphate) is the first back-up:
    • PCr + ADP → creatine + ATP. One step: fast; no oxygen needed.
    • Lasts a few more seconds of maximum effort.
    • Rebuilt from creatine when the muscle rests, using ATP from respiration.
  • Then respiration supplies ATP: aerobic if O2 is enough; anaerobic if not.
  • Say: "ATP is used as soon as it is made" — not: "muscles store ATP for later".
Quick check
?In the first few seconds of a sprint, muscle fibres use ATP faster than respiration can make it. What keeps ATP available?

Mission 6

Mission 6: Your body temperature wobbles all day. Why is that a sign that control is working, not failing?

Make a guess: Your body temperature wobbles all day. Why is that a sign that control is working, not failing?

Mission 6

A steady inside

  • Homeostasis: keeping the internal environment within limits, whatever happens outside.
  • Internal environment = blood and tissue fluid around the cells.
  • Regulated variables include core temperature, blood pH, blood glucose and the water potential of the blood.
  • Each variable fluctuates around a set point. It is never perfectly constant.
Mission 6

Why the limits matter

  • Temperature too high: enzymes denature. Too low: too few successful collisions, so reactions slow.
  • pH away from the optimum: ionic and hydrogen bonds in the enzyme alter, so the active site changes shape. Fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form.
  • Blood glucose too low: cells lack respiratory substrate. The brain is hit first.
  • Blood glucose too high: blood water potential falls, so water leaves cells by osmosis.
  • Say: "lower water potential" — not: "the blood is more concentrated with water".
Mission 6

The control loop

  • Stimulus: a change in a variable.
  • Receptor detects it.
  • Coordinator (e.g. hypothalamus, pancreas) compares it with the set point.
  • Effector (a muscle or a gland) makes the response.
  • The parts are linked by nerves (fast) or hormones in the blood (slower, longer-lasting).
  • Say: "receptors detect; effectors respond" — never swap them.
Quick check
?Which statement about homeostasis in humans is correct?
Mission 6

Negative feedback

  • Negative feedback: the response reverses the change.
  • Variable rises above the set point → response lowers it.
  • Variable falls below the set point → response raises it.
  • So it works from both sides, and the variable oscillates around the set point.
  • The response starts only after a change, so small swings are normal.
  • Say: "negative feedback opposes the change" — not: "negative feedback lowers the level".
Mission 6

Two directions, two mechanisms

  • Most variables have separate negative feedback mechanisms for a rise and for a fall.
  • Blood glucose: insulin lowers it; glucagon raises it.
  • Temperature: sweating and vasodilation cool; shivering and vasoconstriction warm.
  • Each can be switched on actively, so a departure in either direction is corrected faster.
  • That gives a greater degree of control than one mechanism switching on and off.
Quick check
?During a long run, a person's blood glucose concentration falls to 3.5 mmol dm⁻³. Glucagon secretion increases and the concentration returns to 5 mmol dm⁻³. Which description of this sequence is correct?
Mission 6

Positive feedback

  • Positive feedback: the response increases the original change.
  • The loop runs until an end point removes the stimulus.
  • It cannot hold a set point, so it is not used to keep variables steady. That is why homeostasis uses negative feedback.
  • Childbirth: the baby stretches the cervix → oxytocin → stronger uterine contractions → more stretching … until birth.
  • Say: "positive feedback amplifies the change" — not: "positive feedback is the helpful kind".
Quick check
?Why are homeostatic variables controlled by negative feedback rather than by positive feedback?
Mission check

?Two hormones help regulate blood calcium in mammals. When blood calcium falls below normal, the parathyroid glands secrete parathyroid hormone (PTH), which raises it. When blood calcium rises above normal, the thyroid secretes calcitonin, which lowers it. Which statement is correct?

New variable, same logic: a response that reverses the change is negative feedback, whichever way it pushes.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • At threshold, voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open (depolarisation); then K⁺ channels open (repolarisation). Action potentials are all-or-nothing; strength is coded by frequency.
  • Myelinated and wider fibres conduct faster.
  • Myelin restricts action potentials to the nodes (saltatory conduction).

Mission 2

  • Action potential → Ca²⁺ enters the presynaptic terminal → exocytosis of neurotransmitter → diffusion across the cleft → binding to complementary receptors → Na⁺ in → EPSP → action potential if threshold is reached.
  • One-way, because vesicles are presynaptic and receptors postsynaptic.
  • Acetylcholinesterase breaks ACh down so the signal stops.

Mission 3

  • EPSPs and IPSPs sum on one membrane; an action potential fires only if the total reaches threshold. IPSPs hyperpolarise.
  • Predict a drug's effect from the step it changes: release, receptor, breakdown or reuptake.

Mission 4

  • Sarcomeres shorten because actin slides over myosin: I band and H zone narrow, A band stays the same. More overlap, more force.
  • Ca²⁺ uncovers binding sites on actin; myosin heads bind, pull, and use ATP to detach and reset.
  • Muscles only pull, so they work in antagonistic pairs; tendons pass the pull to bone.

Mission 5

  • Slow fibres (mitochondria, myoglobin, capillaries) respire aerobically and resist fatigue; fast fibres rely on anaerobic respiration and phosphocreatine and tire quickly.
  • Phosphocreatine regenerates ATP from ADP in the first seconds of hard work.
  • At the neuromuscular junction, ACh depolarises the sarcolemma.

Mission 6

  • Homeostasis keeps internal variables within limits around a set point; negative feedback reverses a change from either side, so small oscillations are normal.
  • Positive feedback amplifies a change until an end point; it drives processes such as birth or ripening, not steady states.
