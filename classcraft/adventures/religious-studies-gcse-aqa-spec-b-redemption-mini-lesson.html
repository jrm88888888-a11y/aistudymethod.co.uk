Mission 1

Mission 1: Why is the font by the door and the altar at the far end of a Catholic church?

Make a guess: Why is the font by the door and the altar at the far end of a Catholic church?

Mission 1Read the source

Worship in spirit and truth

"God is spirit, and those who worship him must worship in spirit and truth." (John 4:24, WEBBE)

  • The Catechism starts here: worship is not tied to one place; the whole earth is holy, and Christians are the "living stones" of God's house (CCC 1179, 1 Peter 2:5; paraphrase).
  • Yet where they are free to, Catholics build churches. A church is more than a meeting hall: it is a visible sign of the Church living in that place, God dwelling with his people (CCC 1180, paraphrase).
  • It is a house of prayer where the Eucharist is celebrated and kept, and it should be beautiful and fit for prayer (CCC 1181, paraphrase).

Q1 If God can be worshipped anywhere, why build a church at all?

Show answer

It gathers the community, houses the Eucharist, and is a sign of God among his people.

Q2 What does "living stones" suggest the real Church is?

Show answer

The people, not only the building.

Mission 1

A building shaped like the faith

  • Many churches have a cruciform (cross-shaped) plan: the building itself recalls Christ's death.
  • The nave (from the Latin for "ship") holds the people: the Church as a ship carrying believers to God.
  • The sanctuary, raised and at the head of the church, holds the altar and ambo.
  • Walking from the door to the altar pictures the Christian life: entering by baptism, fed by the Eucharist, travelling towards heaven.
  • The church as the Father's house that believers journey towards: CCC 1186 (paraphrase).
Mission 1

Facing east

  • Traditionally a church is oriented: the altar is at the east end.
  • The sun rises in the east, a sign of Christ's resurrection and of his promised return; the prophet speaks of the "sun of righteousness" rising "with healing in its wings" (Malachi 4:2).
  • So the whole assembly faces one way, towards the coming Christ, in reflection and hope.
  • Not every church can face east (the site may not allow it); St Peter's in Rome has its entrance at the east.
  • Orientation still shapes worship: everything leads the eye to the altar.
Mission 1

Common and divergent styles

  • Common to all: an altar, an ambo, a tabernacle, a font, a crucifix, a chair for the priest.
  • Gothic and Gothic Revival (e.g. St Chad's Cathedral, Birmingham, by A. W. N. Pugin, 1841): height, pointed arches and stained glass lift the eye and mind to heaven: God's transcendence.
  • Byzantine style (e.g. Westminster Cathedral, opened 1903): domes and mosaics: heaven brought close.
  • Modern, "in the round" (e.g. Liverpool Metropolitan Cathedral, 1967): the altar in the centre with people gathered around it.
  • After the Second Vatican Council (1962–65), Catholic teaching asks that churches help the people take a full and active part in the liturgy (Sacrosanctum Concilium 14, 124, paraphrase).
  • Decoration (statues, Stations of the Cross, windows) teaches the faith and supports prayer.

Mission 1

The altar

  • The altar is the centre of the church. The church is built around it.
  • It is both an altar of sacrifice and the table of the Lord (CCC 1182, paraphrase).
  • Sacrifice: at Mass, Catholics believe Christ's one sacrifice on the cross is made present, so the altar stands for the cross itself.
  • Meal: Jesus "took bread … saying, 'This is my body which is given for you. Do this in memory of me.'" (Luke 22:19)
  • So the altar expresses redemption: Christ's death and resurrection bring salvation, and believers share in it.
  • A fixed altar is usually of stone (Christ the "cornerstone", Ephesians 2:20), often with relics of saints placed beneath it.
Mission 1

The lectern (ambo)

  • The ambo (CCEA's term; AQA and Edexcel say lectern) is the fixed reading desk from which the Word of God is proclaimed.
  • The Catechism says the dignity of God's Word needs a fitting place, where everyone's attention is easily drawn (CCC 1184, paraphrase).
  • Used for the readings, the psalm and usually the homily.
  • Redemption: the readings tell the story of salvation, from creation to Christ. "So faith comes by hearing, and hearing by the word of God." (Romans 10:17)
  • Catholics speak of two tables: the table of the Word (ambo) and the table of the Eucharist (altar). Both feed the people.
Mission 1

The tabernacle

  • The tabernacle is a locked, richly made box where the consecrated hosts are reserved (kept) after Mass.
  • Catholics believe Christ is really present in the consecrated bread (the Real Presence).
  • A sanctuary lamp (often red) burns beside it: Christ is here.
  • Catholics genuflect (bend the knee) towards it, and come to pray before it.
  • The Catechism asks that it stand in a most worthy place, so that its dignity and position help people adore the Lord present there (CCC 1183, paraphrase).
  • Redemption: the risen Christ stays with his people; Holy Communion is taken from it to the sick and dying.
Mission 1

The crucifix

  • A crucifix is a cross bearing the figure of the crucified Christ.
  • Catholic liturgical rules ask for one on or near the altar at Mass.
  • It shows the cost of redemption: "we preach Christ crucified" (1 Corinthians 1:23).
  • It joins the altar and the cross: what happened on Calvary is what the Mass makes present.
  • It invites personal reflection on Christ's love and on carrying one's own cross.
Mission 1

The font, by the door

  • The font holds the water for baptism. In many churches it stands near the entrance; some have a baptistery (CCC 1185).
  • Its place says: you enter the Church through baptism.
  • Baptism unites a person with Christ's death and resurrection: "buried … with him through baptism into death, that just as Christ was raised … so we also might walk in newness of life." (Romans 6:4)
  • Many fonts are eight-sided: Christ rose on the "eighth day", the start of the new creation.
  • Holy water stoups at the door let Catholics bless themselves and remember their baptism as they come in.

Mission 1Diversity

Table or altar? Cross, crucifix or risen Christ?

  • Altar: stresses the sacrifice of the Mass. Table: stresses the shared meal. Many modern Catholic altars look like a table: both meanings at once. (Many Protestant churches have a communion table, stressing the shared meal.)
  • Crucifix: Christ's suffering and self-giving love.
  • Plain (empty) cross: Christ's victory; the cross is empty because he is risen. Common in Protestant churches; found in Catholic churches too.
  • "Risen Christ": a figure of Christ risen or reigning, often robed, on or before the cross: resurrection and new life.
  • For the individual: each artefact invites a different prayer (sorrow, thanks, hope). For the community: together they tell the whole Paschal Mystery: death and resurrection.
Mission 1Evaluate

"Catholics do not need a special building to worship God."

  • For: God is worshipped "in spirit and truth" (John 4:24); the whole earth is holy (CCC 1179). Mass can be said in a field, hospital or prison. The Church is the people, "living stones" (1 Peter 2:5).
  • Against: the sacraments need an altar and a font; the tabernacle keeps Christ present for prayer and for the sick. A church is a visible sign of God among his people (CCC 1180). Beauty, orientation and layout teach redemption and lift the mind to God.
  • Another view: the building is not essential, but it is valuable: worship is possible anywhere, and still richer in a place made for it.
  • Judge it by: Is worship mainly personal or communal? What does a building do that a field cannot? Does persecution (Christians without churches) show what is essential?

Mission 2

Saved from what?

  • Salvation: being saved from sin and its effects, to live with God for ever (eternal life).
  • Sin is anything that goes against God's will. It damages the relationship with God.
Mission 2

Atonement: making "at one"

  • Atonement: sin is dealt with and the broken relationship is repaired: people are made "at one" with God.
  • Christians believe Jesus, fully God and fully human, did this by his death on the cross.
  • Grace: God's free, undeserved love and help. Christians believe it cannot be earned (Ephesians 2:8–9).
  • Forgiveness: Christians believe that through Jesus, God forgives the sins of all who repent (turn back).

Mission 2

The whole story saves

  • Christians (Catholics call it the Paschal Mystery) see Jesus' life, death, resurrection and ascension as one saving work: redemption (being bought back, set free).
  • Life: in the Incarnation God shares human life; Jesus lives in perfect obedience and shows God's love.
  • Death: Jesus gives his life for others; Christians believe it takes away sin.
  • Resurrection: Jesus defeats death; believers are promised new life.
  • Ascension: Jesus returns to the Father, and the Spirit is sent to the Church.
Mission 2Read the source

Jesus and Nicodemus (John 3:10–21)

  • Context: Nicodemus, a Jewish teacher, visits Jesus by night. Jesus says he must be "born again" (from above) by the Spirit (John 3:3–8).
  • Jesus says the Son of Man must be "lifted up", like the bronze snake Moses lifted up to heal the people (John 3:14; Numbers 21:9).
  • "For God so loved the world, that he gave his only born Son, that whoever believes in him should not perish, but have eternal life." (John 3:16)
  • "For God didn't send his Son into the world to judge the world, but that the world should be saved through him." (John 3:17)
  • Those who refuse to believe have chosen darkness instead of light (John 3:18–21, paraphrase).

Q1 According to John 3:16, what must a person do to receive eternal life?

Show answer

Believe in God's Son.

Q2 According to John 3:17, why did God send his Son?

Show answer

To save the world, not to condemn it.

Mission 2

Pictures of the cross

  • No single picture says it all. Christians use several theories of atonement, side by side.
  • Sacrifice: as animals were offered in the Temple for sin, Jesus offers himself, the Lamb of God (John 1:29), once for all.
  • Substitution: Jesus takes the punishment sinners deserve, in their place. Stressed by many evangelical Protestants.
  • Ransom: "For the Son of Man also came not to be served, but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many." (Mark 10:45)
  • Reconciliation / example: the cross shows God's love so fully that it wins people back and inspires them to love.
  • Most Christians hold several of these together.

Mission 2Diversity

Saved when? Saved how?

  • Past: "I have been saved": Jesus' death has already won salvation (Ephesians 2:8).
  • Present: "I am being saved": grace changes a person day by day (1 Corinthians 1:18).
  • Future: "I will be saved": salvation is completed at the final judgement (Romans 5:9).
  • Many evangelical Protestants: saved by faith alone (Luther, 1517 onwards). A moment of conversion ("born again") brings assurance. Good works are the fruit of faith, not its cause.
  • Catholics: saved by grace, received in faith and lived out in love and good works; grace comes especially through the sacraments (baptism, Mass, reconciliation). "Faith, if it has no works, is dead in itself." (James 2:17)
  • Orthodox: salvation is a lifelong journey of becoming more like God (theosis).

Mission 2Evaluate

"Faith in Jesus is all a person needs to be saved."

  • Reasons for: "By grace you have been saved through faith … not of works" (Ephesians 2:8–9). The criminal crucified beside Jesus was promised paradise with no time for good works (Luke 23:43). The 39 Articles (XI) teach justification "by Faith, and not for our own works". Grace that had to be earned would not be grace.
  • Reasons against: "Faith, if it has no works, is dead" (James 2:17). In Matthew 25:31–46 people are judged by how they treated the hungry and the stranger. Catholics stress the sacraments, especially baptism (John 3:5). Faith that does not change how someone lives may not be real faith.
  • Another view: many Christians say the question is badly posed: real faith always shows itself in love, so faith and works cannot be separated.
  • Judge it by: What does "faith" mean: belief, or trust that changes a life? Which texts carry most weight, and why? Can a person earn what is a gift?

Atonement means the one who was wronged pays the price and opens the way back. Christians believe God did this in Jesus.

Mission 3

Redemption and the Paschal Mystery

  • Redemption: "buying back" or setting free. Jesus came "to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45).
  • Salvation: being saved from sin and death, for eternal life with God.
  • Grace: God's free, undeserved gift of his own life and help.
  • Pascha = Passover. The Paschal Mystery: Jesus' life, death, resurrection and ascension seen as one saving work.
  • Catholics believe the Paschal Mystery is made present at every Mass.
Mission 3

Life: the whole of Jesus' life saves

  • Catholic teaching: Jesus' whole life is part of redemption, not only its end (CCC 517, paraphrase).
  • Adam's disobedience is undone by Christ's obedience: "as through the one man's disobedience many were made sinners, even so through the obedience of the one, many will be made righteous" (Romans 5:19).
  • His healing, forgiving and welcoming sinners show what salvation looks like.
  • His Incarnation joins God and humanity: what was broken begins to be restored.
  • Significance: Catholics follow Jesus' life as a pattern for their own (prayer, service, forgiveness).
Mission 3

Death: restoration through sacrifice

  • A sacrifice is an offering to God. Jesus offers his whole self in love and obedience.
  • "Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!" (John 1:29): like the Passover lamb, his death brings freedom.
  • At the Last Supper: "This cup is the new covenant in my blood, which is poured out for you." (Luke 22:20)
  • Restoration through sacrifice: the broken relationship between God and humanity is mended.
  • Significance: the Mass makes this one sacrifice present (it is not repeated); the crucifix keeps it before Catholics' eyes.
Mission 3Read the source

The death of Jesus (Mark 15:21–39)

  • Simon of Cyrene is forced to carry the cross (15:21). Above Jesus: "THE KING OF THE JEWS" (15:26).
  • Mockers: "He saved others. He can't save himself." (15:31)
  • Darkness; Jesus cries, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (15:34, quoting Psalm 22:1)
  • "The veil of the temple was torn in two from the top to the bottom." (15:38)
  • The centurion: "Truly this man was the Son of God!" (15:39)

Q2 Why does it matter that a Roman soldier speaks the last words?

  • Christians read the mockers' words as ironic: by not saving himself, he saves others.
Mission 3

Resurrection: restoration through recreation

  • The Gospels say Jesus rose on the first day of the week: a new first day, as in Genesis 1.
  • Catholics believe his risen body is real but transformed: the start of a new creation.
  • "If anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation. The old things have passed away. Behold, all things have become new." (2 Corinthians 5:17)
  • Restoration through recreation: death is defeated and creation itself begins to be made new.
  • Significance: in baptism Catholics die and rise with Christ (Romans 6:4); hope of resurrection at every funeral; Sunday as a weekly Easter.

Mission 3

Ascension and Pentecost: restoration of the cosmic order

  • The disciples ask, "Lord, are you now restoring the kingdom to Israel?" (Acts 1:6). Jesus' answer points wider: the Spirit will send them to "the uttermost parts of the earth" (Acts 1:8, paraphrase).
  • "As they were looking, he was taken up, and a cloud received him out of their sight." (Acts 1:9) Angels promise he "will come back in the same way" (Acts 1:11).
  • Catholics believe Jesus' risen humanity enters God's presence: heaven and earth are joined; Christ reigns over all creation.
  • Restoration of the cosmic order: everything is brought back into right order under Christ.
  • Pentecost (Acts 2:1–4): wind and tongues of fire; "They were all filled with the Holy Spirit" (Acts 2:4). The Spirit makes Christ's saving work available to all people.
  • Significance: Christians are sent on mission; Christ's return is awaited in hope.

Mission 3Evaluate

"Jesus' death is the most important part of the Paschal Mystery."

  • Reasons for: "to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45); the cross is Christianity's central symbol; the Mass makes Christ's sacrifice present; Paul: "Christ died for our sins" (1 Corinthians 15:3).
  • Reasons against: "If Christ has not been raised, then our preaching is in vain" (1 Corinthians 15:14); the resurrection begins the new creation; the ascension and Pentecost bring salvation to all; Orthodox worship centres on Easter.
  • Another view: Catholic teaching calls it one mystery: each event makes sense only with the others.
  • Judge it by: Could any event save without the others? What does scripture stress? What do Catholic worship and the creeds stress?
Mission 3Worked answer

Explaining redemption

Question: Explain two Catholic beliefs about redemption. Refer to sacred writings or another source of Catholic belief and teaching in your answer.

  • Belief 1: Jesus' death is a sacrifice that restores humanity's relationship with God.
  • Source: at his death "the veil of the temple was torn in two" (Mark 15:38). Development: the way to God is open, so Catholics take part in that one sacrifice at Mass.
  • Belief 2: the resurrection begins a new creation. Development: death is defeated, so in baptism Catholics die and rise with Christ and hope for eternal life.
  • 5-mark tip: two beliefs, each developed, plus one source of wisdom and authority.

Mission 4

Grace: a gift, not a wage

  • Salvation: being rescued from sin and death and brought into God's life for ever.
  • Catholics believe it comes through the whole Paschal Mystery: Jesus' life, death, resurrection and ascension (see Mission 3).
  • Grace: God's free, undeserved gift of his own life and help. No one can earn it.
  • Sanctifying grace: a lasting share in God's life, first given in baptism; it makes a person holy.
  • Actual grace: God's help at particular moments, to choose good and turn back to God.
  • Grace comes especially through the sacraments, and also through prayer, Scripture and other people.
  • So Catholics say: I have been saved (baptism), I am being saved (living by grace), I hope to be saved (at the end).
Quick check

Mission 4

Saved, being saved, will be saved

  • Past: "by grace you have been saved through faith" (Ephesians 2:8). Christ's death and resurrection won salvation; a Catholic receives it in baptism.
  • Present: "work out your own salvation with fear and trembling" (Philippians 2:12). Grace grows through the sacraments, prayer and love.
  • Future: "he who endures to the end will be saved" (Matthew 24:13). Salvation is completed after death and at the final judgement.
  • Contrasting views:
  • Many evangelical Protestants stress the past: a moment of conversion by faith alone, with assurance of salvation now.
  • Some Reformed Christians hold that the truly saved will always persevere ("once saved, always saved").
  • Catholics stress all three: salvation can be lost by grave (mortal) sin and restored through reconciliation, so they live in hope.
  • Orthodox: salvation as theosis, a lifelong growth into God's likeness.
Mission 4

Grace and freedom

  • Grace comes first. No one can turn to God without God's help.
  • Freedom remains. God never forces love: a person can accept grace or refuse it.
  • Council of Trent (Decree on Justification, 1547): free will, moved by grace, cooperates with it, and can also reject it (paraphrase).
  • Martin Luther (On the Bondage of the Will, 1525): fallen humans contribute nothing; God alone saves.
  • Later Calvinists (Synod of Dort, 1618–19): the grace that saves cannot finally be resisted.
  • Catholic image: grace is like light; a person can open or close the shutters.

Mission 4

Irenaeus: Christ re-lives our story

  • Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 130–c. 202), Against Heresies (Adversus Haereses, c. 180), Books III and V.
  • Key idea: recapitulation (Greek anakephalaiōsis, "summing up again"), from Ephesians 1:10: God's plan is "to sum up all things in Christ".
  • Christ goes through every stage of human life and does right where Adam went wrong: obedience in place of disobedience.
  • "As in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive." (1 Corinthians 15:22)
  • Mary's "yes" undoes Eve's "no": the knot tied by Eve is untied by Mary (Against Heresies III.22.4, paraphrase).
  • Christ became what we are, so that we might become what he is (Book V, preface, paraphrase).
  • Metaphors: a new head for humanity; healing; victory over sin, death and the devil; humanity growing up into God's likeness.
Mission 4

Anselm: a debt only the God-man can pay

  • Anselm of Canterbury (1033–1109), Cur Deus Homo ("Why God Became Man", 1098).
  • Sin refuses God the honour owed to him and breaks the order of creation.
  • That order can be restored only by satisfaction: giving back more than was taken. Otherwise, punishment follows.
  • Humans ought to pay, but cannot: they already owe God everything.
  • Only God can pay; only a human ought to pay. So the payer must be both: the God-man.
  • Jesus, sinless, did not owe death. He gave his life freely, a gift of infinite worth that makes satisfaction for all.
  • Metaphors: debt, honour and satisfaction, drawn from law and the society of Anselm's day.
  • Not the same as penal substitution (later Reformers): for Anselm, Christ's satisfaction is offered instead of punishment.
Mission 4

Irenaeus and Anselm compared

  • Problem: Irenaeus: death and corruption; humanity lost God's likeness. Anselm: dishonour and debt owed to God.
  • Focus: Irenaeus: the whole life (Incarnation to resurrection). Anselm: the death on the cross.
  • Picture: Irenaeus: healing and victory. Anselm: payment and restored order.
  • Similarities: both say only one who is truly God and truly human can save; both start from Adam's sin; both stress God's initiative.
  • Criticisms: Anselm can make God seem to need payment, like a medieval lord; Irenaeus explains less clearly why Jesus had to die.
  • Catholic teaching uses both: the Catechism speaks of Christ recapitulating human life (CCC 518) and of his death making satisfaction for sins (CCC 615), paraphrased.

Mission 4

Redemption in the liturgy

  • Liturgy: the Church's public worship. Catholics believe it celebrates and makes present the Paschal Mystery (Sacrosanctum Concilium 5–6, paraphrase).
  • The Mass: the one sacrifice of the cross made present; after the consecration all proclaim the Mystery of Faith: the Lord's death and resurrection.
  • Lamb of God (Agnus Dei): "Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!" (John 1:29)
  • The year: Easter is the centre; every Sunday is a weekly Easter; Lent prepares for it.
  • The Triduum: Holy Thursday (Last Supper), Good Friday (Veneration of the Cross; no Mass), the Easter Vigil (new fire, the Exsultet, baptisms).
  • The Exsultet calls Adam's sin a "happy fault" (felix culpa), because it won so great a Redeemer.
  • Other influences on the liturgy: praise of the Creator (Gloria, Sanctus), the Trinity, Jewish roots (Passover, psalms), culture and the reforms of Vatican II.
Mission 4Evaluate

"The story of redemption is the main influence on Catholic liturgy."

  • Claim: the liturgy is shaped above all by Christ's death and resurrection.
  • For: every Mass makes present the sacrifice of Calvary; the Mystery of Faith and Agnus Dei proclaim it; the year centres on Easter; Sunday is a weekly Easter.
  • Against: much of the Mass praises God as Creator and Trinity (Gloria, Sanctus, Creed); the Liturgy of the Word covers the whole Bible; Jewish worship, culture and Vatican II's reforms also shaped it.
  • Another view: these are not rivals: the Creator and the Trinity are praised because of what God has done in redemption.
  • Judge it by: What is at the centre of the Eucharistic Prayer? Which feasts matter most? Could the liturgy exist without the Paschal Mystery?

Mission 5

One meal, many names

  • Christians share bread and wine, as Jesus did at his Last Supper. Most churches call it a sacrament (Baptists: an ordinance).
  • Eucharist: Greek for "thanksgiving".
  • Mass: Catholic name (from the Latin dismissal, "Go, it is the sending").
  • Holy Communion: sharing with Christ and each other (Anglican, Methodist and many others).
  • Lord's Supper or Breaking of Bread: many Protestant churches.
  • Divine Liturgy: the Orthodox service in which the Eucharist is celebrated.

Mission 5

The Catholic Mass

  • Liturgy of the Word: readings from the Bible and a homily (sermon).
  • Liturgy of the Eucharist: the priest says Jesus' words over unleavened bread (hosts) and wine (the consecration).
  • Catholics believe the bread and wine become the body and blood of Christ: the Real Presence.
  • Transubstantiation: the substance (what it really is) changes; the appearances (taste, look) of bread and wine remain (Council of Trent, 1551).
  • The Mass makes present Christ's one sacrifice on the cross; it does not repeat it.
  • Catholics must attend Mass on Sundays; those prepared and free from serious sin receive Communion.
  • Vatican II calls the Eucharist "the source and summit of the Christian life" (Lumen Gentium 11, 1964).

Mission 5

Anglican and Lutheran Holy Communion

  • Church of England: Holy Communion is the main Sunday service in many parishes; people kneel or stand at the altar rail and receive both bread and wine.
  • Anglican beliefs vary: some (Anglo-Catholics) believe in a real, objective presence; many evangelical Anglicans stress a spiritual presence received by faith.
  • Lutherans: Christ's body and blood are truly present "in, with and under" the bread and wine; the bread is not changed in substance.
Mission 5

Memorial and spiritual presence

  • Memorial view (Huldrych Zwingli, 1484–1531): "this is my body" means "this represents my body". The bread and wine are symbols that help believers remember Christ's death.
  • Baptist practice: the Lord's Supper, often monthly; bread and small individual cups of (often non-alcoholic) wine are passed along the rows; usually all believers are welcome.
  • Spiritual presence (John Calvin, 1509–1564; Presbyterian and Reformed): the bread stays bread, but by the Holy Spirit believers truly feed on Christ through faith.
  • All these churches agree: Jesus commanded it, it proclaims his death "until he comes" (1 Corinthians 11:26), and it unites believers.

Mission 5Diversity

A spectrum of belief

  • Catholic: transubstantiation; Real Presence remains, so consecrated hosts are kept in the tabernacle and adored.
  • Orthodox: a real change, called a mystery, not defined in philosophical terms; leavened bread and wine given together on a spoon; babies receive too.
  • Lutheran: real presence in, with and under the bread and wine.
  • Anglican: a range, from real presence to spiritual presence.
  • Presbyterian (Reformed): spiritual presence received by faith.
  • Baptist and many evangelicals: memorial.
  • Quakers and The Salvation Army: no outward Communion; inward communion with Christ.
Mission 5

Why it matters

  • Unity with Christ: Catholics and Orthodox believe they receive Christ himself; others meet him spiritually or remember him.
  • Unity with each other: "we, who are many, are one body" (1 Corinthians 10:17).
  • Forgiveness and strength: grace to live as Christians in the week ahead.
  • Remembering and hoping: looking back to the cross and forward to Christ's return.
  • Sent out: the Mass ends by sending people to serve others.
  • "Source and summit": all of Catholic life flows from the Eucharist and leads back to it.
  • A sadness: Christians disagree about who may receive, so many cannot share Communion together.
Mission 5Evaluate

"The Eucharist is the most important act of Christian worship."

  • Reasons for: Jesus commanded it (1 Corinthians 11:24). Catholics call it "source and summit"; Catholics and Orthodox believe Christ is truly present. It unites the Church across the world.
  • Reasons against: many Protestants put preaching the Bible at the centre, with Communion monthly. Quakers and The Salvation Army worship without it. Jesus also stressed love in action (Matthew 25:31–46).
  • Judge it by: What did Jesus command? Is Christ present in it in a special way? Which practice most changes how Christians live?
Mission 5Worked answer

Catholic and Protestant interpretations

Question: Explain Catholic and Protestant interpretations of the Eucharist.

  • Catholic: at the consecration, bread and wine become Christ's body and blood (transubstantiation). Source: "this is my body" (Matthew 26:26), taken literally. Develop: so hosts are reserved in the tabernacle and adored.
  • Protestant (Baptist): the Lord's Supper is a memorial. Source: "Do this in memory of me" (1 Corinthians 11:24). Develop: so the bread is shared as a symbol, and the focus is remembering and obeying.
  • Common ground: both obey Jesus' command and proclaim his death.

Mission 6

What popular piety is

  • Popular piety: forms of prayer and devotion that grow from ordinary believers' faith and culture.
  • Examples: the Rosary, the Stations of the Cross, Eucharistic adoration, pilgrimages, processions, candles, medals.
  • They sit alongside the liturgy (the Mass and sacraments), not instead of it.
  • They engage the whole person: hands (beads), feet (walking), eyes (images), voice (repeated prayer).
  • They can be prayed anywhere: at home, in hospital, on a bus, in a group.
Mission 6

The Rosary

  • A string of beads with a crucifix, used to count prayers.
  • Start: Apostles' Creed, Our Father, three Hail Marys, Glory Be.
  • Then five decades. Each: one Our Father, ten Hail Marys, one Glory Be.
  • The Hail Mary begins with Gabriel's and Elizabeth's words: "Rejoice, you highly favoured one! The Lord is with you." (Luke 1:28); "Blessed are you amongst women, and blessed is the fruit of your womb!" (Luke 1:42)
  • During each decade the person thinks about one mystery: an event in the life of Jesus and Mary.
  • Four sets: Joyful, Luminous (added by Pope John Paul II in 2002), Sorrowful, Glorious.
  • Christ-centred: Pope John Paul II called it a summary of the whole Gospel, prayed "with Mary". The repetition calms the mind for meditation.
Mission 6

The Sorrowful Mysteries

  • Prayed especially on Tuesdays and Fridays, and in Lent.
  • 1. The Agony in the Garden: "My soul is exceedingly sorrowful, even to death." (Matthew 26:38)
  • 2. The Scourging at the Pillar (John 19:1). 3. The Crowning with Thorns (Matthew 27:29).
  • 4. The Carrying of the Cross (John 19:17). 5. The Crucifixion and Death (Luke 23:46).

Mission 6

The Stations of the Cross

  • Fourteen images around the walls of most Catholic churches, showing Jesus' last journey.
  • 1 condemned to death · 2 takes up his cross · 3 falls the first time · 4 meets his mother · 5 Simon of Cyrene helps · 6 Veronica wipes his face · 7 falls the second time
  • 8 meets the women of Jerusalem · 9 falls the third time · 10 stripped of his garments · 11 nailed to the cross · 12 dies · 13 taken down · 14 laid in the tomb
  • Many churches add a 15th: the Resurrection.
  • At each station: "We adore you, O Christ, and we praise you, because by your holy cross you have redeemed the world." Then a reading, a reflection and a prayer.
  • Origin: pilgrims in Jerusalem walked the Via Dolorosa; Franciscans spread the practice to Europe, so others could make the journey at home.
  • Prayed especially on Fridays in Lent and on Good Friday.
Mission 6

Dramatised prayer: a Church on a journey

  • Dramatised prayer: prayer that is acted out with the body: walking, stopping, kneeling, carrying.
  • Walking the Stations shows the Church as the pilgrim People of God, travelling with Christ towards the resurrection.
  • It is a journey of service: Simon helps carry the cross; Veronica shows compassion; Jesus speaks to the women even while suffering.
  • Each station asks: where do people carry crosses today, and how can I help?
  • Other dramatised prayer: live outdoor Stations; the Good Friday Walk of Witness in many towns; Passion plays (e.g. Oberammergau, Germany, since 1634).
  • Influence: these dramas make the Passion vivid for believers and outsiders alike.

Mission 6

Eucharistic adoration

  • Catholics believe the consecrated host remains the Body of Christ after Mass.
  • So they adore (worship) Christ present in it, outside Mass too.
  • Exposition: the host is placed in a monstrance (a gold or silver stand with a glass centre) on the altar; people pray in silence.
  • Benediction: hymns (e.g. Tantum Ergo, by Thomas Aquinas), incense, and the priest blesses the people with the monstrance.
  • Holy Hour: one hour of prayer, recalling Jesus in Gethsemane: "What, couldn't you watch with me for one hour?" (Matthew 26:40)
  • Some parishes have perpetual adoration: someone is always praying, day and night. The feast of Corpus Christi often has a procession.

Mission 6Evaluate

"Popular piety is as important for Catholics as the Mass."

  • Claim: devotions like the Rosary matter as much as the Mass.
  • For: they can be prayed daily, anywhere, by anyone, including the sick. The Rosary summarises the Gospel. They make faith personal and part of everyday life. The Church encourages them (CCC 1674).
  • Against: they extend the liturgy but do not replace it (CCC 1675). The Eucharist is the source and summit of Christian life. Devotions can drift into superstition and need guiding (CCC 1676). Many Protestants see some of them as unbiblical.
  • Judge it by: Does "as important" mean "the same kind of thing"? Which leads to which? What does the person praying actually gain?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A church is a sign of God dwelling with his people and a house of prayer; its layout traces the journey from baptism (font) to Eucharist (altar).
  • Altar (sacrifice and meal), ambo/lectern (Word), tabernacle (Real Presence), crucifix (cost of redemption) and font (new birth) each express redemption.
  • Contrasting artefacts (table/altar, crucifix, plain cross, risen Christ) stress different sides of the Paschal Mystery.

Mission 2

  • For Christians, salvation is being saved from sin to eternal life with God; atonement is the repairing of the relationship through Jesus.
  • Christians believe law shows sin, grace is God's free, undeserved love, and the Spirit helps believers live it out.
  • Christians picture the cross as sacrifice, ransom and reconciliation; Catholics and many Protestants differ on how faith and works relate.

Mission 3

  • The Paschal Mystery is Jesus' life, death, resurrection and ascension as one saving work; Catholics believe it is made present at Mass and in the sacraments.
  • Death restores through sacrifice, resurrection through recreation, ascension restores the cosmic order (Mark 15, John 20, Acts 1–2).

Mission 4

  • Grace is God's free gift of his life and help; Catholics receive it especially through the sacraments.
  • Salvation is past (baptism), present (living by grace, with real freedom) and future (completed at the end).
  • Irenaeus: Christ re-lives and heals the human story (recapitulation); Anselm: Christ's freely given death makes satisfaction for the debt of sin.

Mission 5

  • The Eucharist (Mass, Holy Communion, Lord's Supper) goes back to Jesus' Last Supper: "this is my body… my blood of the new covenant".
  • Catholics: transubstantiation and the Real Presence, the "source and summit"; Orthodox: a real change, a mystery; Lutherans: real presence; Anglicans: a range; Reformed: spiritual presence; Baptists: memorial.

Mission 6

  • Catholic teaching is that popular piety (Rosary, Stations, adoration) extends the liturgy but never replaces it (CCC 1674–1676).
  • The Rosary: five decades, each a meditation on one mystery.
  • The Stations retrace Jesus' way to the cross.
  • Eucharistic adoration follows from belief in the Real Presence.
