Mission 1

Mission 1: Rub a balloon and it becomes charged. Where did that charge come from?

Make a guess: Rub a balloon and it becomes charged. Where did that charge come from?

Mission 1

Two kinds of charge

  • Charge is positive or negative. Like charges repel; unlike charges attract.
  • Protons: +e. Electrons: −e. Neutrons: no charge.
  • e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, the elementary charge.
  • Neutral object: equal numbers of protons and electrons. Charged object: it has gained or lost electrons.
  • Charge comes only in whole-number multiples of e: Q = ±ne. Charge is quantized.
  • Point charge: a model for a charged object whose size is negligible in the situation.
Quick check
?A small plastic bead has a net charge of −4.8 × 10⁻¹⁷ C. Which statement correctly describes the bead? Use e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.
Mission 1

Evidence: Millikan's oil drops

  • Tiny charged oil drops between two horizontal charged plates. Between them is a uniform electric field E: a charge q there feels a force qE.
  • Adjust the plates' charge until a drop hovers: qE = mg, so q = mg/E. (The drop's weight comes from its falling speed with the plates uncharged.)
  • Example: weight 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁴ N, E = 5.0 × 10⁴ N/C → q = 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁴ ÷ 5.0 × 10⁴ = 6.4 × 10⁻¹⁹ C = 4e.
  • Over many drops: every charge a whole-number multiple of 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. Never 1.5e, never 2.3e.
  • Evidence that charge is quantized.
Quick check
?In an experiment, the net charges on five small oil drops are measured as 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, 4.8 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, 6.4 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, 9.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, and 11.2 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. Which conclusion is best supported by these data?
Quick check
?Predict first. A polyethylene rod is rubbed with a dry wool cloth and gains a charge of −8.0 nC. Which statement about the cloth after the rubbing is correct?
Mission 1

Charging by friction: charge is conserved

  • Charge is conserved: it is never created or destroyed, only moved.
  • Rubbing moves electrons from one surface to the other. Protons stay put, locked in nuclei.
  • Polyethylene rubbed with wool: electrons move wool → polyethylene. Polyethylene −, wool +.
  • The two charges are equal and opposite: the total stays zero.
  • So the balloon's charge came from the material it was rubbed on.
Mission 1

Conductors, insulators, contact

  • Conductors (metals): electrons free to move. Added charge spreads over the surface.
  • Insulators: electrons bound to atoms or molecules. Added charge stays where it was put.
  • Ideal conductor: its electrons move with no hindrance.
  • Charging by contact: touch a charged conductor to a neutral one. Charge flows until both share it; the total is unchanged.
  • Two identical conducting spheres share the total equally.
Mission 1

Induction: why neutral things are attracted

  • Charged rod near a neutral conductor: its free electrons move. Near side: opposite charge. Far side: same charge. This is induced charge separation.
  • The near side is closer, so the attraction wins: the neutral object is pulled toward the rod.
  • The free electrons keep moving until the field inside the conductor is zero.
  • Neutral insulator (a wall): electrons cannot flow, but each molecule polarizes slightly. The near surface gains a slight opposite charge: weaker attraction.
  • So a charged object attracts any neutral object. Only repulsion proves both are charged.
Mission 1

Charging by induction

  • 1. Hold a negative rod near a neutral metal sphere on an insulating stand.
  • 2. Ground the sphere with a wire: repelled electrons flow to the ground.
  • 3. Remove the ground wire, rod still in place.
  • 4. Remove the rod. The sphere is left positive, charge spread over its surface.
  • The rod never touches; it loses none of its charge.
  • The ground (Earth) is so large it can give or take electrons and stay effectively neutral.
  • Connecting to Earth: grounding.
Quick check
?A negatively charged rod is held near a neutral metal sphere on an insulating stand, and the sphere is connected to ground by a wire. This time the rod is taken away first, and only then is the wire removed. What is the final charge on the sphere?
Mission 1

Permittivity: how easily a material polarizes

  • In an electric field, a material's electron arrangement shifts: it polarizes.
  • Conductor: free electrons move until the field inside it is zero.
  • Insulator (dielectric): charges cannot move freely; each atom or molecule polarizes a little.
  • Permittivity measures how strongly a material polarizes in a field.
  • Free space: the constant ε0 = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² C²/(N·m²). Any material: a different value, greater than ε0.
  • Loosely held electrons → polarizes more easily → larger permittivity.
Mission check

?A rod on an insulating handle has a charge of −6.0 nC. It touches a neutral metal sphere on an insulating stand and is then pulled away. Nothing else touches either object. Afterward, the rod's charge is −2.0 nC. What is the charge on the sphere?

Charge is never created: it is moved, as electrons, from one object to another, and the total is conserved. The balloon's charge came from the material it was rubbed on.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Halve the distance between two charges. How many times bigger does the force between them get?

Make a guess: Halve the distance between two charges. How many times bigger does the force between them get?

Mission 2

Coulomb's law

  • The force between two point charges is proportional to the product of the charges and to 1/r²: an inverse-square law.
  • It acts along the line joining them: like charges repel, unlike attract. Each exerts an equal and opposite force on the other.
  • |FE| = k|q1q2|/r², with k = 1/(4πε0) = 9.0 × 10⁹ N·m²/C².
  • ε0 = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² C²/(N·m²), the permittivity of free space.
Mission 2

Spheres act as points

  • A uniformly charged sphere acts, outside itself, like a point charge at its center.
  • So r is measured center to center.
  • Two spheres 2.0 cm across with a 3.0 cm gap: r = 1.0 + 3.0 + 1.0 = 5.0 cm.
  • Any charged objects can be modeled as point charges when they are small compared with their separation.
Mission 2

Worked example

  • +3.0 nC and +5.0 nC, centers 4.0 cm apart, in air.
  • |FE| = 9.0 × 10⁹ × (3.0 × 10⁻⁹)(5.0 × 10⁻⁹) ÷ 0.040² = 8.4 × 10⁻⁵ N.
  • Both positive: repulsion.
  • Halve r: force × 2² = 4 times: 3.4 × 10⁻⁴ N.
  • Double one charge: force × 2.
Quick check
?Two small charged spheres whose centers are 0.60 m apart exert electric forces of magnitude 1.0 N on each other. Their charges do not change. At what center-to-center separation would the forces have a magnitude of 9.0 N?
Mission 2

Charges inside a material

  • Charges immersed in an insulating material: it polarizes and weakens the force.
  • Replace ε0 by the material's permittivity ε: F = q1q2/(4πεr²).
  • Larger ε, smaller force. Force = (ε0/ε) × its value in a vacuum.
  • Air: ε ≈ ε0. Water: ε ≈ 80ε0: forces between charges in water are about 1/80 as strong.
Quick check
?Two small charged spheres are a distance r apart in a vacuum, and the electric force between them has magnitude F. The spheres, with unchanged charges, are then placed a distance r/2 apart in a large tank of an insulating liquid whose permittivity is ε = 5.0ε0. In the liquid, Coulomb's law applies with ε in place of ε0. What is now the magnitude of the force between the spheres?
Mission 2

More than two charges

  • Find each force separately, then add them as vectors.
  • A (+2.0 nC) at x = 0; B (+1.0 nC) at x = 0.10 m; C (−3.0 nC) at x = 0.30 m. Force on B:
  • From A: repulsion, pushes B in +x: 1.8 × 10⁻⁶ N.
  • From C (0.20 m away): attraction, pulls B toward C, also +x: 6.8 × 10⁻⁷ N.
  • Resultant: 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ N in +x.
  • Not in a line? Resolve into components, add, then use Pythagoras.
Quick check
?Three small charged spheres are fixed on the x-axis: a sphere with charge +q at x = 0, a sphere with charge −4q at x = d, and a sphere with charge +2q at x = 2d. Each can be modeled as a point charge. Which expression gives the magnitude of the net electric force exerted on the +2q sphere by the other two spheres? (k = 1/(4πε0).)
Mission 2

Electric force vs gravity

  • Both are inverse-square laws: Newton's law of gravitation and Coulomb's law.
  • Gravity only attracts. Electric forces attract or repel.
  • Electron and proton in a hydrogen atom: FE/FG = e²/(4πε0Gme mp) ≈ 2.3 × 10³⁹. Between particles, gravity is negligible.
  • Large objects (planets, stars) are almost exactly neutral: their electric forces cancel, while gravity always adds. So gravity dominates at large scales.
  • Contact forces (normal force, friction, tension) are electric forces between the atoms of the surfaces, too many to add one by one.
Quick check
?Two protons are a distance r apart. What is the ratio FE/Fg of the magnitude of the electric force between them to the magnitude of the gravitational force between them? Use k = 9.0 × 10⁹ N·m²/C², ε0 = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² C²/(N·m²), G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg², e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, mp = 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, and me = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg.
Quick check
?For two small charged spheres a distance r apart, the magnitude of the electric force between them divided by the magnitude of the gravitational force between them is R. The charge on each sphere is then halved, the mass of each sphere is doubled, and the separation is increased to 3r. What is the new value of this ratio?
Mission check

?Two small charged spheres, 1 and 2, exert electric forces of magnitude F on each other. The charge on sphere 2 is doubled, and the distance between the spheres is tripled. What is the magnitude of the force now exerted on sphere 2 by sphere 1?

Force ∝ 1/r². Halving the distance makes the force 4 times bigger; tripling it makes the force 1/9.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Field lines are just drawn on paper. How can their spacing tell you how strong a force is?

Make a guess: Field lines are just drawn on paper. How can their spacing tell you how strong a force is?

Mission 3

The electric field

  • Electric field: a region where a charge feels a force. Charges create it; it is there even when nothing is placed in it.
  • E = FE/q, measured with a test charge small enough not to disturb the charges making the field.
  • A vector. Unit: N/C.
  • Direction: the push on a positive charge: away from +, toward −.
  • A negative charge is pushed opposite to E.
Quick check
?At point P, a charge of +2.0 µC experiences an electric force of 5.0 × 10⁻³ N directed east. This charge is removed and a charge of −6.0 µC is placed at P; the charges that produce the field do not move. Taking east as positive, what is the electric force on the −6.0 µC charge?
Quick check
?Predict first. A student sketches the electric field lines around two equal positive point charges a short distance apart. Which description of a correct sketch is right?
Mission 3

Field lines

  • A field line shows the direction of the force on a positive charge.
  • Lines start on + charges and end on − charges (or run to or from far away).
  • Lines never cross: the field has one direction at each point.
  • Closer lines = stronger field. Lines are drawn so their density matches E.
  • Patterns: single + (radially out) · single − (radially in) · + and − pair · two + (a neutral point between) · parallel plates.
  • Vector field map: an arrow at each point, its length showing the field's strength.
  • A field line is not the path a charge follows.
Mission 3

The field of a point charge

  • |E| = k|q|/r²
  • Inverse square: double r, E falls to ¼.
  • Field lines agree: the same lines pass through every sphere round the charge, area 4πr². Line density ∝ 1/r².
  • A charged sphere: outside, its field is that of a point charge at its center.
  • +4.0 nC, at 0.20 m: E = 9.0 × 10⁹ × 4.0 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ 0.20² = 9.0 × 10² N/C, away from the charge.
Mission 3

Adding fields

  • Several charges: add their fields as vectors.
  • +4.0 nC and +1.0 nC, 0.40 m apart. Where between them is E = 0?
  • There the two fields point opposite ways. Equal when 4.0/x² = 1.0/(0.40 − x)², x measured from the 4.0 nC charge.
  • Square roots: 2/x = 1/(0.40 − x), so 2(0.40 − x) = x and x = 0.27 m.
  • The neutral point is nearer the smaller charge.
Quick check
?A particle with charge +q is fixed a distance 2d to the left of point P, and a particle with charge +3q is fixed a distance 3d to the right of P. Which expression gives the magnitude of the net electric field at P?
Mission check

?The field lines of a long, straight charged wire spread out radially, in planes at right angles to the wire: they spread in two dimensions, not three. Point P is 2.0 cm from the wire and point Q is 6.0 cm from it, both far from the wire's ends. How does the electric field strength at P compare with that at Q?

Lines are drawn so that their density matches E. Closer lines, stronger field. A point charge's lines spread over spheres (area ∝ r²), so E ∝ 1/r²; a long wire's spread over cylinders (area ∝ r), so E ∝ 1/r.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Charge a metal ball. Where does the extra charge end up, and what is the field inside the metal?

Make a guess: Charge a metal ball. Where does the extra charge end up, and what is the field inside the metal?

Mission 4

Why the field inside is zero

  • A metal has free electrons that move at the slightest force.
  • Electrostatic equilibrium: no charge is moving.
  • Any field inside the metal would push the free electrons, so they would move.
  • So in equilibrium: E = 0 everywhere inside the metal.
  • Electrons rearrange to make this happen in a tiny fraction of a second.
Quick check
?Predict first. A solid metal sphere on an insulating stand is touched at one point by a negatively charged rod, which leaves excess electrons on it. Once the sphere has reached electrostatic equilibrium, where is its excess charge?
Mission 4

Where the charge goes

  • E = 0 inside → no net charge inside. All excess charge sits on the outer surface.
  • Positive excess charge = a shortage of electrons on the surface.
  • Just outside, the field is perpendicular to the surface. A sideways part would push surface charges along.
  • Sphere: charge spreads evenly; field lines are radial outside, as if from a point charge at the center.
  • Hollow sphere: the same outside. No field lines in the metal or in the empty cavity.
Mission 4

Insulators are different

  • In an insulator, charge cannot move freely. It stays where it was put.
  • Excess charge can be spread through the volume, not just on the surface.
  • So the field inside an insulator can be nonzero.
  • Uniformly charged plastic sphere: inside, E points away from the center (positive charge) and grows from zero at the center.
  • Outside a uniformly charged sphere, metal or plastic: E = kQ/r².
Quick check
?Two spheres of the same size, one metal and one plastic, stand far apart on insulating stands. Each is touched at one point by a negatively charged rod and gains the same small excess charge there. Point P is just outside each sphere, on the side directly opposite the point that was touched. Once each sphere has settled, how does the magnitude of the electric field at P for the metal sphere compare with that for the plastic sphere?
Mission check

?A solid lump of metal with a sharp point at one end carries excess charge and has settled. Which statement is correct?

Free electrons move until the field inside the metal is zero, whatever its shape; that puts all the excess charge on the outer surface, with the field ⟂ to it.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Like charges repel, unlike attract; e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C; charge is quantized in multiples of e.
  • Charge is conserved: friction, contact and induction move electrons; they never create charge.
  • Grounding lets electrons flow to or from Earth: charging by induction leaves the opposite charge to the rod.
  • Permittivity measures how strongly a material polarizes; ε0 for free space.

Mission 2

  • |FE| = k|q1q2|/r²; add forces from several charges as vectors.
  • Gravity only attracts and is negligible between particles; electric forces attract or repel.

Mission 3

  • E = FE/q (test charge); vector field maps and field lines; add fields as vectors.
  • Point charge: |E| = k|q|/r²; parallel plates: uniform field, the same force anywhere between them.

Mission 4

  • In a conductor in equilibrium: E = 0 inside the metal, excess charge on the outer surface, field ⟂ to the surface; a charged sphere acts outside like a point charge.
  • Insulators keep charge where it is put, so the field inside them can be nonzero.
