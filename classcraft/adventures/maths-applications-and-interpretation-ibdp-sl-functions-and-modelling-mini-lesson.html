Gradient and the forms of a line

  • Gradient m=x2​−x1​y2​−y1​​m = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁): the rate of change of yy per unit of xx.
  • Gradient-intercept form: y=mx+cy = mx + c, where cc is the yy-intercept.
  • Point-gradient form: y−y1​=m(x−x1​)y − y₁ = m(x − x₁) through the point (x1​,y1​)(x₁, y₁).
  • General form: ax+by+d=0ax + by + d = 0.
  • A road climbing 1.2 m over 15 m horizontally has gradient 151.2​=0.081.2/15 = 0.08, an 8% incline.

Parallel and perpendicular lines

  • Parallel lines have equal gradients: m1​=m2​m₁ = m₂.
  • Perpendicular lines: m1​×m2​=−1m₁ × m₂ = −1, so m2​=−m1​1​m₂ = −1/m₁ (the negative reciprocal).
  • To read a gradient from general form, rearrange to y=mx+cy = mx + c. For 2x+5y−7=02x + 5y − 7 = 0: y=−52​x+57​y = −2/5x + 7/5, so m=−52​m = −2/5.
  • Any line perpendicular to it has gradient 25​5/2.

Functions · lines and graphs

Functions, domain, range and inverses

  • A function gives each input exactly one output. Notation: f(x)f(x), v(t)v(t), C(n)C(n).
  • The domain is the set of allowed inputs; the range is the set of outputs. f(x)=2−x​f(x) = √(2 − x) has domain x≤2x ≤ 2 and range f(x)≥0f(x) ≥ 0.
  • The inverse function f−1f⁻¹ undoes ff: solving f(x)=10f(x) = 10 is the same as finding f−1(10)f⁻¹(10).
  • The graph of f−1f⁻¹ is the reflection of ff in y=xy = x. Only one-to-one functions have inverses; the domain of f−1f⁻¹ is the range of ff.

Key features of graphs, with technology

  • Sketch: the shape, with axes and key features labelled. Draw: accurate, to scale.
  • Key features: intercepts, zeros (roots of f(x)=0f(x) = 0), maximum and minimum points, vertex, symmetry, vertical and horizontal asymptotes.
  • To solve f(x)=g(x)f(x) = g(x), graph both on your GDC and find the intersection points. A GDC also graphs sums and differences such as y=f(x)−g(x)y = f(x) − g(x), whose zeros are the same xx-values.

Mission check

?Taxi A charges £3.50 plus £1.20 per km. Taxi B charges £6.00 plus £0.95 per km. When does the cheaper one switch?

Each cost is a linear function of distance: the fixed fee is the yy-intercept and the price per km is the gradient. The cheaper taxi switches where the lines intersect: 3.50+1.20d=6.00+0.95d3.50 + 1.20d = 6.00 + 0.95d gives d=10d = 10 km, where both cost £15.50. Below 10 km A's lower fee wins; beyond it B's smaller gradient wins.

Functions · modelling

Linear and piecewise linear models

  • A linear model f(x)=mx+cf(x) = mx + c fits a quantity that changes by the same amount per unit of xx: mm is the rate, cc the start value.
  • A piecewise model uses different rules on different parts of the domain, like a phone plan with a data allowance, then a charge per GB.
  • Always state a reasonable domain: data used cannot be negative.
Calculate
?A phone plan costs €12 a month, which includes 5 GB of data. Each extra GB costs €2.50. Find the cost, in euros, of a month in which 8.4 GB is used.
Functions · modelling

Exponential growth and decay models

  • An exponential model fits a quantity that changes by the same percentage each step: f(x)=kax+cf(x) = ka^x + c or f(x)=ka−x+cf(x) = ka^(−x) + c (with a>0a > 0), or f(x)=kerx+cf(x) = ke^rx + c.
  • In kaxka^x: a>1a > 1 is growth, 0<a<10 < a < 1 is decay. A 20% fall each year means a=0.8a = 0.8; a 5% rise means a=1.05a = 1.05.
  • The starting value is f(0)=k+cf(0) = k + c.
  • The graph has a horizontal asymptote y=cy = c: decay models get ever closer to cc without reaching it.

A cooling drink

Question
The temperature of a cup of tea, in °C, tt minutes after it is made, is modelled by T(t)=20+70(0.92)tT(t) = 20 + 70(0.92)^t. Find (a) the initial temperature, (b) the temperature after 10 minutes, (c) the equation of the horizontal asymptote and what it represents, (d) when the tea reaches 40 °C.
  1. 1
    (a) Substitute t=0t = 0: T(0)=20+70=90T(0) = 20 + 70 = 90 °C
  2. 2
    (b) Substitute t=10t = 10: T(10)=20+70(0.92)10=50.4T(10) = 20 + 70(0.92)¹⁰ = 50.4 °C (3 sf)
  3. 3
    (c) As tt grows, 70(0.92)t→070(0.92)^t → 0, so T=20T = 20: the room temperature
  4. 4
    (d) Use your GDC to solve 20+70(0.92)t=4020 + 70(0.92)^t = 40 (intersect with y=40y = 40): t=15.0t = 15.0 minutes

(a) 90 °C (b) 50.4 °C (c) T=20T = 20, room temperature (d) after 15.0 minutes

the initial value is k+c=90k + c = 90, not k=70k = 70.

Functions · modelling

Direct and inverse variation

  • Variation models have the form f(x)=axnf(x) = axⁿ, n∈Zn ∈ ℤ.
  • n>0n > 0: direct variation, such as y=ax2y = ax² (yy varies as the square of xx).
  • n<0n < 0: inverse variation, such as y=x2a​=ax−2y = a/x² = ax⁻². The yy-axis is a vertical asymptote.
  • Find aa from one data point: if y=ax−2y = ax⁻² and y=45y = 45 when x=2x = 2, then a=45×22=180a = 45 × 2² = 180, and y=20y = 20 when x=3x = 3.
  • Choose a model from the context and the shape of the data, and beware of extrapolating far beyond the data.

Mission check

?A phone bought for €900 loses 20% of its value every year. Is it ever worth nothing?

Losing 20% each year multiplies the value by 0.8, so V=900(0.8)tV = 900(0.8)^t: an exponential decay model with horizontal asymptote V=0V = 0. In the model the phone is never worth nothing. In reality a buyer may pay only scrap value, so a model V=k(0.8)t+cV = k(0.8)^t + c with c>0c > 0 could fit better: that is reflecting on the model.

Functions · modelling

Quadratic models

  • f(x)=ax2+bx+cf(x) = ax² + bx + c, a=0a ≠ 0, fits data that rise then fall (or fall then rise): a thrown ball, an arch, the area of a fenced plot.
  • The vertex gives the maximum (a<0a < 0) or minimum (a>0a > 0); the axis of symmetry is x=−2ab​x = −b/2a.
  • The zeros (use your GDC) often mark where the model starts or ends, such as when a ball lands.
  • Choose a reasonable domain: a height cannot be negative, so the model stops at the zero.
Worked example Functions · modelling

A thrown ball

Question
A ball is thrown so that its height, in metres, tt seconds later is h(t)=−4.9t2+14t+1.5h(t) = −4.9t² + 14t + 1.5. Find (a) the maximum height, (b) when the ball lands, (c) a reasonable domain for the model.
  1. 1
    (a) Axis of symmetry: t=−2(−4.9)14​=1.43t = −14/(2(−4.9)) = 1.43 s (3 sf)
  2. 2
    Substitute: h(1.43)=11.5h(1.43) = 11.5 m (using the exact value t=710​t = 10/7)
  3. 3
    (b) Use your GDC to solve h(t)=0h(t) = 0: t=−0.103t = −0.103 or t=2.96t = 2.96; reject the negative root
  4. 4
    (c) From the throw to the landing: 0≤t≤2.960 ≤ t ≤ 2.96
Answer
(a) 11.5 m (b) after 2.96 s (c) 0≤t≤2.960 ≤ t ≤ 2.96
⚠️ Watch out
the tt-value of the vertex, 1.43, is a time. The question asks for the height.
Functions · modelling

Cubic models and finding parameters

  • f(x)=ax3+bx2+cx+df(x) = ax³ + bx² + cx + d fits data with up to two turning points, such as the volume of an open box cut from a sheet.
  • A box from a 30 cm by 20 cm sheet with squares of side xx cut out has V=x(30−2x)(20−2x)V = x(30 − 2x)(20 − 2x), domain 0<x<100 < x < 10. A GDC gives the maximum: V≈1,056V ≈ 1,056 cm³ at x≈3.92x ≈ 3.92.
  • Finding parameters: substitute known points into the model. Three points give three linear equations in aa, bb, cc: solve them with your GDC.

Functions · modelling

Sinusoidal models

f(x)=asin(bx)+dorf(x)=acos(bx)+df(x) = asin (bx) + d or f(x) = acos (bx) + d
  • Amplitude ∣a∣=2max−min​|a| = (max − min)/2; principal axis y=d=2max+min​y = d = (max + min)/2.
  • Period =b360∘​= (360°)/b, with xx in degrees: the time for one full cycle.
  • acos(bx)+dacos (bx) + d with a>0a > 0 starts at a maximum; with a<0a < 0 it starts at a minimum.

  4. 4

Recap

Big ideas

  • A line has gradient m=x2​−x1​y2​−y1​​m = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁) and can be written y=mx+cy = mx + c, y−y1​=m(x−x1​)y − y₁ = m(x − x₁) or ax+by+d=0ax + by + d = 0; parallel means m1​=m2​m₁ = m₂, perpendicular means m1​m2​=−1m₁ m₂ = −1.
  • A function has a domain (inputs) and a range (outputs); f−1f⁻¹ undoes ff, exists for one-to-one functions and is the reflection of ff in y=xy = x.
  • Key features (intercepts, zeros, maximum and minimum, asymptotes) and intersections come from a GDC or from solving f(x)=g(x)f(x) = g(x).
  • Same amount each step: linear f(x)=mx+cf(x) = mx + c (piecewise if the rule changes). Same percentage each step: exponential kax+cka^x + c.
  • An exponential model kax+cka^x + c has horizontal asymptote y=cy = c; decay approaches it but never reaches it.
  • Variation axnaxⁿ: find aa from one point. Choose models from context and data shape; beware extrapolation.
  • Quadratic models: vertex for the maximum or minimum, zeros for start and end, and a domain that makes sense.
  • Find a model's parameters by substituting points and solving the linear equations with your GDC.
  • Sinusoidal models: amplitude 2max−min​(max − min)/2, principal axis y=2max+min​y = (max + min)/2, period b360∘​(360°)/b.
