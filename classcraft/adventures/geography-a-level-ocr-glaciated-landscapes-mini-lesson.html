Mission 1

A glacier is a system

  • Inputs: snow, mainly in the accumulation zone, high on the glacier.
  • Store: ice, which flows downhill under its own weight.
  • Outputs: meltwater, vapour and icebergs, mainly from the ablation zone lower down.
  • Equilibrium line: the boundary between the two zones, where a year's gain equals a year's loss.
  • Energy flows too: solar energy melts ice and drives freeze–thaw; gravity moves ice and debris downslope; heat from the bed can melt the base.
Mission 1

From snow to ice sheet

  • Buried snow compacts and recrystallises. After about a year it becomes firn, about two-thirds as dense as water; over time, firn becomes glacier ice.
  • An ice sheet is glacial ice covering more than 50,000 km². Only two exist today: Greenland and Antarctica.
  • In the last ice age, ice sheets also covered much of North America and Scandinavia.
Mission 1

How ice is gained and lost

  • Accumulation: direct snowfall; avalanches from slopes above; snow blown in by wind.
  • Ablation: melting; sublimation, ice turning straight to vapour; evaporation of meltwater; calving of icebergs where ice meets water; avalanches from the glacier's own front.
  • Ablation dominates at low altitude and in summer; accumulation at high altitude and in winter.
Mission 1

Mass balance

  • Mass balance = accumulation − ablation, over a year.
  • Positive: the glacier thickens and its snout may advance. Negative: it thins and its snout may retreat. Glaciers grow "when winter snowfall exceeds summer melting" (USGS).
  • Measured in water equivalent (w.e.): the depth of water if the ice gained or lost were spread evenly over the glacier.
  • Ice still flows downhill whatever the balance.
Quick check
?In one year a glacier gains 2.1 m w.e. and loses 1.4 m w.e. What follows?
Mission 1

Why rates vary over time

  • Daily: melt peaks in the warm afternoon and slows overnight.
  • Seasonal: winter is mostly accumulation; summer is mostly ablation.
  • Decades: reference glaciers lost on average 214 mm w.e. a year in the 1980s and 896 in the 2010s.
  • Millennia: glaciers covered about 32% of land at the last glacial maximum; about 10% today.
  • Wetter climates feed more accumulation, so glaciers there can reach lower, warmer ground before ablation balances the gain.
Mission 1

What controls a glacier?

  • Precipitation: totals and timing set accumulation; winter snow adds most.
  • Latitude and altitude: both lower the temperature, so ice can survive at sea level near the poles but only high up in the tropics.
  • Relief and aspect: in the Northern Hemisphere, north- and north-east-facing slopes get less sun, so snow lasts longer there. Steeper slopes make ice flow faster.
  • Geology: well-jointed rock is easier for ice to pluck; a soft or wet bed lets ice slide.
Mission 1

How ice moves

  • Internal deformation (creep): the weight of overlying snow, firn and ice deforms the ice.
  • Basal sliding: ice slides on a thin film of water at its base.
  • Warm-based glaciers sit at the pressure melting point, so they slide and erode strongly.
  • Cold-based glaciers are frozen to the bed: "the movement at the bottom can be a tiny fraction of the speed of flow at the surface".
Mission 1

Weathering, nivation, mass movement

  • Frost action: water freezes in cracks, expands and prises rock apart; repeated cycles shatter it.
  • Nivation: freeze–thaw, meltwater and slow mass movement under and around a snow patch deepen a hollow (a nivation hollow), which may later grow into a corrie.
  • Mass movement: rockfalls and avalanches drop debris onto the ice, which carries it away.
  • Solar energy drives the freeze–thaw cycles; gravity moves the debris. Energy in, material out.
Quick check
?A snow patch lies all summer on a shaded, north-facing hillside. Which process is most likely to deepen the ground beneath it?
Mission 1

Glacial erosion

  • Plucking: meltwater freezes onto jointed rock, and moving ice tears blocks away. "As the ice moves, it plucks rock from the sides and bottom of the valleys" (NPS).
  • Abrasion: debris frozen into the base scrapes the bed like sandpaper, smoothing it and leaving scratches (striations).
  • Warm-based ice erodes most, because it slides.
Mission 1

Transport and deposition

  • Debris travels on the ice (supraglacial), within it (englacial) and beneath it (subglacial).
  • Ice carries debris "like a conveyor belt" and drops it where the ice melts (NPS).
  • Till is dropped directly by ice: unsorted and angular.
Quick check
?A cutting shows a deposit of angular stones of all sizes, mixed with clay and with no layers. What laid it down?
Mission check

?Stakes show a glacier's ice moving 80 m down-valley a year near its snout. Yet the snout ends 20 m further up-valley than a year ago. What explains this?

Retreat describes only where the snout is. NSIDC: a retreating glacier "simply melts faster than it flows". That is how Muir Glacier retreated 12 km while its ice kept moving towards the sea: its mass balance was negative.

Mission 2

Snow to ice; valley glacier to ice sheet

  • Buried snow compacts and recrystallises. "After about a year" it becomes firn, "about two-thirds as dense as water"; more burial turns it to glacier ice.
  • Valley glaciers "flow down valleys"; ice sheets "flow outward in all directions".
  • An ice sheet covers more than 50,000 km². Only two exist today: Greenland and Antarctica.
Mission 2

The glacial budget

  • Mass balance (glacial budget): "whether a glacier loses or gains mass over the course of a year".
  • Budget = accumulation − ablation.
  • Positive: the glacier thickens; the snout may advance. Negative: it thins; the snout may retreat.
  • The snout responds late: it takes years for extra ice to flow down to the front.
Quick check
?In one year a glacier gains 3.2 million tonnes of ice and loses 4.0 million tonnes. What is its budget, and what is the likely result?
Mission 2

Warm-based and cold-based ice

  • Pressure melting point: the weight of ice above lowers the temperature at which ice melts.
  • Warm-based: the base reaches it, so meltwater forms at the bed. Meltwater "encourages basal sliding, and rapid ice velocities".
  • Cold-based: the base stays below it, frozen to the rock; flow is "very slow or no flow".
  • Dry Valleys, Antarctica: mean annual air temperature about −19.8 °C; glaciers' basal temperatures about −17 °C, "no free running water".
Mission 2

What sets a glacier's base temperature?

  • Air and ground temperature: colder climates give colder ice.
  • Geothermal heat warms some glaciers from below.
  • Ice thickness: thick ice presses harder, so the base reaches melting point more easily.
  • Polythermal glaciers are "partly frozen to their bed": thick ice may be warm-based while thin margins stay frozen.
Quick check
?Glacier P sits in a mild, wet mountain range; its bed is at the melting point. Glacier Q sits in a polar desert; its base is −15 °C. Which statement is best supported?
Mission 2

How ice moves

  • Internal deformation (creep): "All glaciers flow by creep". Ice is plastic: under its own weight, ice crystals deform and slip.
  • Basal sliding: ice "melts under pressure, resulting in a film of water at the ice-bed interface", and slides on it.
  • Bed deformation: some glaciers sit on soft, wet sediment that itself deforms.
  • Speeds vary hugely: in 1986, Hubbard Glacier, Alaska, moved forward 10 m a day during a surge, a short burst of fast flow.
Mission check

?Two glaciers sit side by side in the same mountain range. Glacier X is 300 m thick; Glacier Y is 40 m thick. Surveys show X moves several times faster than Y. Which explanation fits best?

Thickness and climate decide whether the bed thaws. A warm-based glacier slides on meltwater; a cold-based one moves only by slow internal deformation. That is why some glaciers move metres a day while others barely move.

Mission 3

What glaciers do to rock

  • Erosion: ice and its debris wear away the bed and valley sides.
  • Entrainment: loosened rock is picked up, often by freezing onto the base of the ice.
  • Transport: debris rides on, in and under the ice.
  • Deposition: debris is dropped where the ice melts.
Mission 3

How ice erodes

  • Plucking (quarrying): meltwater freezes onto jointed rock, and moving ice tears blocks away. "As the ice moves, it plucks rock from the sides and bottom of the valleys" (NPS).
  • Abrasion: rock fragments in the base scrape the bed like sandpaper, polishing it and cutting striations.
Quick check
?Which process needs rock debris already frozen into the base of the ice?
Mission 3

Corries

  • A corrie (cirque) is a steep-sided, armchair-shaped hollow at a glacier's head, "a rounded amphitheater" (NPS).
  • Ice turns in rotational flow. Plucking steepens the back wall; abrasion deepens the floor; a rock lip is left at the front.
  • After the ice melts, a small lake, a tarn, may fill the hollow. Piegan Glacier still sits in a cirque in Glacier National Park, Montana.
Mission 3

Arêtes and pyramidal peaks

  • Arête: a knife-edged ridge left where two glaciers carved either side, such as the Garden Wall, Glacier National Park.
  • Corries cut backwards (headward erosion) until only a ridge separates them.
  • Pyramidal peak (horn): a peak "scraped nearly vertical by glaciers on three or more sides", such as Reynolds Mountain.
Quick check
?Two corries erode backwards towards each other from opposite sides of a ridge. What do they leave between them?
Mission 3

Glacial troughs

  • Rivers cut V-shaped valleys; glaciers scour them into U-shaped troughs, wide-floored and steep-sided.
  • A trough glacier fills the whole valley, so it erodes the sides as well as the floor.
Mission 3

Roches moutonnées

  • A roche moutonnée is an "asymmetrical bedrock dome sculpted by glaciers" (NPS).
  • Stoss (up-ice) side: abrasion grinds it into a gentle, smoothed slope.
  • Lee (down-ice) side: plucking tears out joint blocks, leaving a steep, jagged face.
  • So the shape shows which way the ice flowed: from the gentle side towards the steep side.
  • Lembert Dome, in Tuolumne Meadows, Yosemite, is a roche moutonnée: its "steep western face and gradual eastern slope indicate the direction of glacial flow" (NPS).
Mission 3

Striations

  • Striations are scratches cut into bedrock by stones in the base of the ice.
  • They run parallel to ice flow, so they record its direction.
  • Near Tuolumne Meadows, "Glacial polish, striations, and erratics on Fairview Dome" show the domes were overtopped by ice.
  • In the UK, striations on Edinburgh's Castle Rock run roughly east–west, matching the ice that moved in from the west (BGS).
Mission check

?A rock knob in a glen has a smooth, gently sloping, scratched western side and a steep, broken eastern face. What happened?

Lembert Dome is a roche moutonnée. Ice up to more than 500 m thick filled Tuolumne Meadows. Abrasion smoothed the up-ice side; plucking broke away the down-ice side. NPS: its steep western face and gradual eastern slope show the direction of flow.

Mission 4

Till: what ice leaves behind

  • Ice carries debris "like a conveyor belt" and drops it where the ice melts (NPS).
  • Material dropped directly by ice is till (boulder clay).
  • Till is unsorted (clay to boulders, mixed) and angular, because ice does not sort or round what it carries.
Mission 4

Moraines along the glacier

  • Lateral moraine: a ridge of debris along the valley side, much of it from frost-shattered walls above.
  • All moraines are till, so they are unsorted and angular.
Mission 4

Moraines across the valley

  • Terminal moraine: a ridge at the ice's furthest point. "The longer a glacier stays in one spot... the larger this moraine will be" (NPS).
  • Recessional moraines: smaller ridges behind it, where retreat paused. A glacier may stay "in a steady state for decades, or even longer" (NPS).
  • Moraines mark old ice limits: Glacier National Park's Little Ice Age maximum was reconstructed from them (USGS).
Quick check
?Three ridges of unsorted debris cross a valley floor, one behind another, below a glacier. The ridge furthest down-valley is the largest. What are they?
Mission 4

Drumlins

  • Drumlins: smooth, oval hills of till, shaped beneath moving ice.
  • The steeper, blunt end faces the way the ice came from; the long, tapering end points down-ice (RGS).
  • They often occur in swarms, a "basket of eggs". In Strangford Lough, County Down, the sea has flooded a drumlin swarm, leaving "drowned drumlins" as islands.
  • How they form is still debated. RGS: the most widely accepted idea is that ice overloaded with till moulded it into streamlined hills.
Quick check
?A field of drumlins has steep, blunt ends facing north-west and long, tapering tails pointing south-east. Which way did the ice flow?
Mission 4

Erratics: Big Rock, Alberta

  • An erratic is a rock carried by ice and dropped far from its source, often on different bedrock.
  • Okotoks "Big Rock": a 16,500-tonne quartzite boulder, about 41 m long, 18 m wide and 9 m tall.
  • It was part of a mountain formation in what is now Jasper National Park. A rockslide dropped debris onto a glacier, which carried it out of the mountains; it arrived between 10,000 and 30,000 years ago.
Mission 4

Till plains and till sheets

  • A till plain (till sheet) is a broad, low, gently rolling surface of till laid down as an ice sheet melted.
  • Holderness, East Yorkshire, is a UK example: its glacial tills are "mostly late Devensian (18 000 to 13 000 years old)" (BGS).
  • Drumlins and moraines often stand out of the plain, giving its gentle relief.
Mission check

?On limestone moorland lies a large granite boulder. The nearest granite outcrop is 30 km to the north-west, and drumlins nearby point north-west to south-east. What best explains the boulder?

Big Rock is an erratic. A rockslide in what is now Jasper National Park dropped it onto a glacier, which carried it out onto the plains and left it as the ice melted, 10,000–30,000 years ago.

Mission 5

The case: Lötschental, Swiss Alps

  • A glaciated valley in the canton of Valais, on the edge of the Jungfrau–Aletsch–Bietschhorn UNESCO World Heritage Site.
  • The site's heights run from 809 m to 4,274 m.
  • Its landforms include "U-shaped glacial valleys, cirques, horn peaks, valley glaciers and moraines".
  • It holds the Aletsch Glacier, "the largest and longest in Europe".
Mission 5

Physical factors

  • Geology: "400 million-year-old crystalline rocks thrust over younger carbonate rocks". Uplift that built the High Alps began 20–40 million years ago.
  • Altitude: high peaks keep ice through the summer; valley floors are warm enough for farms and villages.
  • Aspect: the site has "dry southern/wet northern exposures", so snow and ice survive differently on each side.
  • Relief: steep walls feed rock and ice down onto glaciers and valley floors.
Mission 5

Landforms that link up

  • Cirques cut back into a peak; where three or more meet, a horn forms; between two, an arête.
  • Glaciers leave the cirques and join in a U-shaped trough. Smaller tributary glaciers leave hanging valleys above it.
  • Moraines mark where ice dropped debris; screes and rockfall cones build at the foot of steep walls.
  • Each landform supplies or shapes the next: walls → debris → glacier → moraine.
Quick check
?In a glaciated valley, a stream drops as a waterfall from a high side valley into the main trough. Why?
Mission 5

Change over millions and thousands of years

  • Tens of millions of years: plate collision raises the Alps.
  • Hundreds of thousands of years: repeated glacials carve troughs and cirques. At the last glacial maximum, glaciers covered about 32% of Earth's land, against about 10% today.
  • Thousands of years: since the ice shrank, plants colonise bare ground. The site holds "superb examples of plant succession".
Mission 5

Change in years, minutes and seconds

  • Years: from 2019 the lower Birch Glacier front advanced about 50 m; its tongue thickened by up to 15 m in 2017–2023.
  • Why? Rockfalls from the Kleines Nesthorn piled debris on it. Authorities estimate over 10 million tonnes.
  • Seconds to minutes: on 28 May 2025 the glacier collapsed under that load. A mass of ice and rock "about two kilometres long" and "several dozen metres thick" buried "a considerable part of the village".
Quick check
?Why did the lower Birch Glacier advance in the years before it collapsed, while most Alpine glaciers were retreating?
Mission 5

Why the toll was low

  • The glacier had been monitored for years by authorities and researchers.
  • When rockfall increased, observation was stepped up.
  • Blatten was evacuated as a precaution before the collapse. ETH/WSL: monitoring and evacuation "largely avoided" a tragedy.
  • One man, aged 64, was missing; his body was found in June.
  • Contrast: in 2002, a glacier collapse at Kolka–Karmadon, Russia, buried a village and killed 125 people.
Mission 5

Is climate change to blame?

  • ETH/WSL: climate change "probably" helped thaw the ground, increase rockfalls and drive the glacier's unusual advance.
  • A direct link to permafrost thaw "has not yet been conclusively proven".
  • Exam move: write "likely contributed", not "caused", unless a source proves cause.
Mission check

?In another Alpine valley, a cliff above a glacier starts shedding rock. Radar shows the glacier tongue speeding up. A village lies 2 km below. Which response gives the village the best chance of no deaths?

At Blatten, years of monitoring and a precautionary evacuation meant the village was empty when the glacier fell. Resilience came from knowing the landscape and acting early.

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 1.1.2-2.b case study of an ice-sheet landscape (with the valley-glacier case): physical factors, inter-relationships between landforms, change from seconds to millennia
Mission 6

What is an ice-sheet landscape?

  • An ice sheet is land ice covering more than 50,000 km²; it spreads "under their own weight in broad domes".
  • Only two today: Greenland and Antarctica.
  • Greenland Ice Sheet: about 80% of the island; 1.7 million km²; over 3 km thick; about 2.9 million km³ of ice.
  • Melted, it would raise sea level about 7.4 m.
  • Case focus: Sermeq Kujalleq (Jakobshavn Isbræ) and Ilulissat Icefjord, West Greenland: beyond the UK.
Mission 6

The physical factors

  • Climate: snowfall builds the dome; summer melt removes ice at the margin.
  • Relief of the bed: troughs channel ice into fast outlet glaciers; Jakobshavn Isbræ, "Greenland's largest outlet glacier", drains about 6.5% of the ice-sheet area.
  • Ocean: at marine margins, ocean temperature affects the ice front; at Jakobshavn its "dominant influence" is through the rigidity of the winter mélange (the ice-choked fjord water in front of the glacier).
  • Meltwater: water reaching the bed changes speed.
Quick check
?Jakobshavn Isbræ drains about 6.5% of the Greenland Ice Sheet's area through one fjord. Which factor best explains why so much ice leaves through one outlet?
Mission 6

Landform system 1: dome to fjord

  • Dome (accumulation) → outlet glacier / ice stream (fast flow) → calving front → icebergs and mélange (ice-choked fjord).
  • Ilulissat Icefjord: the glacier is "still actively eroding the fjord bed" (UNESCO): fast ice deepens a glacial trough into a fjord below sea level.
  • On ice-free ground beside the margin, sliding ice leaves striated, ice-moulded bedrock (roches moutonnées) and moraines where the front once stood.
  • Feedbacks: a rigid winter mélange held the front ~6 km farther forward in 2016–18, slowing the glacier and thickening it.
  • Each landform depends on the one up-flow and controls the one down-flow.
Mission 6

Landform system 2: meltwater

  • Supraglacial lakes form on the surface in summer.
  • One large lake drained in under 2 hours, down 980 m to the bed, by water-driven fracture becoming a moulin.
  • The ice sheet lifted and sped up, then slowed over 24 hours.
  • Many such drainages "could explain the observed net regional summer ice speedup".
  • Water leaves the bed in proglacial rivers that build outwash plains beyond the margin; sediment-filled channels under the ice can be left as eskers.
Quick check
?After the lake drained to the bed, the ice sheet sped up and lifted. What is the best explanation?
Mission 6

Change: seconds to hours

  • Glacial earthquakes: in a best-recorded example (Alaska), ice slid in a stick-slip event lasting 30–60 seconds.
  • In Greenland such events are seasonal and their rate doubled over the five years before 2006.
  • Lake drainage: under 2 hours.
  • Calving: large surface depressions become "the detachment points for major calving events".
Mission 6

Change: seasons to decades

  • Seasonal: summer speed-up and thinning, winter slowdown and thickening.
  • Decadal: Jakobshavn speed 6,700 m/yr (1985) → 5,700 (1992) → 9,400 (2000) → 12,600 (2003).
  • Fast outlets "can significantly alter ice discharge at sub-decadal timescales".
  • Ice sheet: lost 3,902 ± 342 billion tonnes 1992–2018 → 10.8 mm of sea-level rise; losses peaked at 345 Gt per year in 2011.
Quick check
?Jakobshavn Isbræ flowed at 5,700 m/yr in 1992 and 12,600 m/yr in 2003. What was the percentage increase?
Mission 6

Change: centuries to millennia

  • Memory: a North Greenland ice core reaches back 123,000 years, into the last interglacial.
  • Glacial cycles: ice sheets "once covered much of the Northern Hemisphere" in Pleistocene ice ages.
  • Land rebound: as ice thins, the land rises and the ice sheet's gravitational pull on the ocean weakens; local sea level around Greenland is projected to fall by 2100 (median −0.9 m, RCP2.6; −2.5 m, RCP8.5).
  • Falling relative sea level leaves raised shorelines: a landform of the future coast.
Mission 6

Exam move: inter-relationships across timescales

  • Link: climate and ocean → speed → calving → fjord erosion; meltwater → bed → speed → outwash.
  • Nest timescales: seconds (quakes) inside seasons (speed cycle) inside decades (acceleration) inside millennia (ice-age cycles, rebound).
  • Judge: at marine margins, ocean and bed shape can outweigh air temperature over short timescales.
Mission check

?The Northeast Greenland Ice Stream had retreated from the continental shelf edge by 21,600 years ago: this early retreat was driven by warm Atlantic water and amplified by a seabed sloping down inland. A grounding line then held for a time behind grounding-zone wedges. After 15,200 years ago, ice-shelf break-up, caused by warm Atlantic water melting the shelf from below together with surface thinning from atmospheric warming, triggered rapid retreat. What does this suggest controls an ice-sheet landscape over millennia?

The same factors work at Jakobshavn over years: ocean conditions through the mélange, and bed relief through the trough. An ice-sheet landscape is a system: climate, ocean and bed control flow, and flow builds the landforms, over seconds to millennia.

Mission 7

Mission 7: How can a ridge of river gravel wind uphill and over hills, when rivers only flow downhill?

Make a guess: How can a ridge of river gravel wind uphill and over hills, when rivers only flow downhill?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 1.1.2-3.a post-glacial climate change and glacio-fluvial processes forming kames, eskers, outwash plains; modification by present and future climate
Mission 7

Where meltwater flows

  • Meltwater flows supraglacially (on top of the ice), englacially (within the ice) and subglacially (below the ice).
  • Surface water reaches the bed "through the bases of crevasses and moulins".
  • Discharge peaks in summer and in the afternoon of each melt day, and is lowest in winter, so deposits record pulses of high and low flow.
Mission 7

Meltwater erodes and carries

  • Meltwater "is usually very rich in sediment, which increases its erosive power": it erodes by abrasion with its load, as rivers do.
  • Under the ice, water is under pressure. Its path is "controlled mostly by the slope of the ice surface, rather than the slope of the bed".
  • So subglacial water can flow uphill over obstacles for short distances, cutting channels a normal river could not.
  • It carries huge loads of sand and gravel when discharge is high, then drops them when it slows.
  • Melting peaks as the climate warms: deglaciation releases huge volumes of meltwater and sediment.
Mission 7

Fluvioglacial deposits are different

  • Till (ice-laid): unsorted, unlayered, angular to sub-angular clasts in a fine-grained matrix.
  • Fluvioglacial (water-laid): sorted by size, stratified (in layers), with rounded clasts.
  • Why: water drops the largest particles first as it slows, and each flood pulse lays a new bed.
Quick check
?A pit face shows rounded pebbles in distinct layers, coarse gravel beds alternating with sand beds. Which agent laid it down, and why?
Mission 7

Eskers

  • An esker is a long ridge of sand and gravel, "deposited by glacial meltwater flowing through tunnels within and underneath glaciers".
  • The tunnel fills with sediment; "As the ice retreats, the sediments are left behind as a ridge".
  • Size: "usually metres to tens of metres high"; length "from hundreds of metres to hundreds of kilometres".
  • Eskers "tend to be oriented parallel to ice flow", and can cross hills because the water was under pressure.
  • UK: near Carlisle, "Several parallel eskers were created by meltwaters that flowed subglacially towards an ice margin" (BGS). Ireland: an esker exposed at Kinnitty, Co. Offaly.
  • Eskers form mainly during deglaciation, when warming delivers the most meltwater.
Mission 7

Kames and kame terraces

  • Kames: mounds or flat-topped hills "made up of stratified sand and gravel", laid down in hollows in or on stagnant ice, then let down as the ice melted.
  • Kame terraces: "marginal deposition between the glacier and sidewalls results in kame terraces": benches along a valley side.
  • Kames often sit among kettle holes, as kame and kettle topography; Charlesbreen, Svalbard, is an example.
  • UK: glaciofluvial sand and gravel in the Tyne Gap and Vale of Eden form "ice-marginal fans, flat-topped plateaux, mounds, ridge systems and terraces" (BGS).
Mission 7

Outwash plains (sandur)

  • Outwash plains, also called "'Sandur' (after the Icelandic term for sand)", are built by "braided, sediment-rich streams" flowing away from the ice.
  • Beyond the ice, meltwater velocity falls: "The largest sediment (usually gravel) is deposited first", with finer sand "carried further downstream".
  • So grain size fines with distance from the snout, and channels braid around gravel bars.
  • Iceland's Skeiðarársandur is a classic sandur; in 1996 an eruption beneath the ice "collected in a subglacial lake at a tremendous rate", then burst out as a jökulhlaup (glacial outburst flood) across it.
Quick check
?Samples are taken along an outwash plain, from the ice front outwards. Which pattern is most likely?
Mission 7

Post-glacial climate change

  • At the last glacial maximum, glaciers covered about 32% of land; today about 10% (NSIDC).
  • The last British–Irish Ice Sheet "was at its maximum around 27,000 years ago".
  • As climate warmed, ablation exceeded accumulation: ice thinned, stagnated and retreated.
  • Effect on processes: huge seasonal meltwater floods, abundant debris and dead ice → rapid glacio-fluvial deposition.
  • Result: eskers in tunnels, kames against dead ice, outwash plains in front, and kettle holes where buried ice melted.
Mission 7

Modification now and in future

  • Once ice has gone, the landforms are relict and start to change.
  • Rivers incise outwash into terraces; kettle lakes infill with sediment and peat; steep kame and esker slopes slump.
  • Warmer, wetter conditions can speed weathering, mass movement and river erosion of loose sand and gravel.
  • Where glaciers are still retreating, new landforms appear: young eskers at Breiðamerkurjökull, Iceland, and "the number, area, and volume of glacial lakes around the world has grown as glaciers melt and retreat".
  • Bigger proglacial lakes raise the risk of outburst floods that rework outwash plains.
Quick check
?Which change is most likely to modify a relict outwash plain in lowland Britain as the climate gets warmer and wetter?
Mission check

?A dry channel is cut across a col between two valleys. Its floor rises, then falls, along its length, and no stream flows in it today. How did it most likely form?

Beneath ice, meltwater flows under pressure, guided by the ice surface, not the land. That is why subglacial channels and eskers can run uphill.

Mission 8

Mission 8: Near Tuktoyaktuk in Arctic Canada, a 49 m hill rises out of flat tundra. Its core is solid ice. How did it form?

Make a guess: Near Tuktoyaktuk in Arctic Canada, a 49 m hill rises out of flat tundra. Its core is solid ice. How did it form?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 1.1.2-3.b patterned ground and pingos formed by past climate; modified by present and future climate
Mission 8

Permafrost and the active layer

  • Periglacial areas are cold, unglaciated areas shaped by frost.
  • Permafrost: ground at or below 0 °C for at least two years. It is "defined solely by temperature".
  • Active layer: the top layer, which thaws each summer. Taliks: unfrozen ground within permafrost.
  • About 85% of Alaska has some form of permafrost.
Mission 8

Frost processes

  • Freeze–thaw: water in cracks expands as it freezes and prises rock apart.
  • Frost heave: water drawn to the freezing front builds ice lenses, which lift the ground and push stones up. Under roads, "The expanding ice pushes up the road and creates a hump".
Mission 8

Patterned ground

  • Circles, polygons and stripes that form "when water and ice in the ground move the rocky soil around". In polar deserts they can be up to 5 m across.
  • Frost heave pushes stones up and outwards, so they gather at the edges (sorted patterned ground).
  • On slopes, circles are drawn out into stripes.
Mission 8

Ice wedges

  • In winter, frozen ground contracts and cracks; in spring, meltwater enters, freezes and widens the crack.
  • Repeated each year, the crack grows into a vertical ice wedge.
  • Wedges join into ice-wedge polygons across the tundra.
Quick check
?Why do stones collect at the edges of the circles in sorted patterned ground?
Mission 8

Pingos

  • Pingos are ice-cored hills 3–70 m high and 30–1,000 m across, found only in permafrost.
  • Closed-system: under a lake lies unfrozen ground (a talik) within permafrost. When the lake drains, the bed freezes from the surface and from the sides, squeezing water to the centre under pressure; it lifts the ground and freezes into an ice core.
  • Ibyuk Pingo is 49 m high, the world's second-tallest. It is one of 1,350 pingos in the region, most of which grew in drained lake beds.
  • Open-system: groundwater under pressure rises through gaps in discontinuous permafrost and freezes near the surface.
Quick check
?On a valley floor in discontinuous permafrost, groundwater flowing down from the hills rises under pressure and freezes into a mound. What has formed?
Mission 8

Thermokarst

  • Thermokarst: thawing ground ice "can gradually cave in", leaving "irregular surfaces of marshy bowls".
  • Permafrost can contain up to 80% ice; when it melts, the ground can collapse.
  • Signs of thaw include thaw slumps, degraded ice-wedge polygons and thermokarst lakes.
Mission 8

Landforms from past climates

  • Pingos and patterned ground form only under permafrost, so finding them shows the climate when they formed.
  • When permafrost thaws, a pingo's ice core melts and it collapses. Mackay links such collapse features to "paleo pingos, in areas now without permafrost".
Mission 8

Modification now and in future

  • From 2007 to 2016, permafrost temperature rose by 0.29 ± 0.12 °C globally. From 1979 to 2021, the Arctic warmed nearly four times as fast as the globe.
  • By 2100, near-surface permafrost area is projected to fall by 2–66% under a low-emissions pathway (RCP2.6) and 30–99% under a high one (RCP8.5) (IPCC).
  • Thaw deepens the active layer, degrades ice wedges into thermokarst troughs and can make pingos collapse.
Quick check
?In a lowland with no permafrost today, a circular hollow is ringed by a low rampart. What does it most likely show?
Mission check

?In continuous permafrost in Siberia, a lake drained 30 years ago. Now a round mound with an ice core is rising from the middle of the old lake bed. What is driving its growth?

Ibyuk is a pingo. Under a drained lake, unfrozen ground (a talik) was surrounded by permafrost. As it froze inward, water was forced to the centre under pressure, lifted the ground and froze into an ice core.

Mission 9

Mission 9: About half of the 800-mile Trans-Alaska Pipeline runs above the ground. Why not bury all of it?

Make a guess: About half of the 800-mile Trans-Alaska Pipeline runs above the ground. Why not bury all of it?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 1.1.2-4.a case study of a periglacial landscape used by people: activity and reasons; impacts on flows of energy and material; permafrost thaw; thermokarst
Mission 9

Case study: Alaska's North Slope

  • Oil was discovered at Prudhoe Bay, on Alaska's Arctic coast, in 1968.
  • The Trans-Alaska Pipeline System (TAPS) runs about 1,290 km (800 miles) from Deadhorse to the port of Valdez. It was completed in 1977 and has carried more than 18 billion barrels.
  • It is raised on supports for 420 miles and buried for 380.
Mission 9

Warm oil, frozen ground

  • About 85% of Alaska has some form of permafrost; in places it is up to 80% ice.
  • Oil was expected to flow at about 70–80 °C (158–176 °F); buried, it "would thaw a cylindrical area 20 to 30 feet in diameter within a decade".
  • Thawed ice-rich ground loses volume, so the pipe could sink and break.
  • So the pipe is raised where permafrost is unstable, and buried in thawed soils, bedrock or thaw-stable ground.
Quick check
?Why is TAPS buried along some stretches but raised along others?
Mission 9

The oil field's footprint

  • Oilfields need roads, gravel pads, pipelines and gravel mines across the tundra.
  • Raynolds and colleagues mapped 62 years of change (1949–2011) at Prudhoe Bay.
  • By 2010, over 34% of the intensively mapped area was affected by oil development.
  • Impacts on the landscape: dust, flooding beside roads, and changed vegetation and drainage.
Mission 9

Flows, thaw and thermokarst

  • Energy: road dust darkens snow and tundra, so they absorb more sunlight and thaw earlier.
  • Water: roads and pads block drainage; ponded water stores heat and warms the ground beneath.
  • Landform: ice in the permafrost melts and the ground caves in, forming thermokarst: "irregular surfaces of marshy bowls".
  • Raynolds et al. found thermokarst expanded across 19% of the remaining natural landscape between 1990 and 2001, with a sudden increase soon after 1990; road dust and roadside flooding contribute.
Quick check
?Which sequence best shows how a road on ice-rich permafrost can lead to thermokarst?
Mission 9

A warming climate adds risk

  • The Arctic warmed nearly four times as fast as the globe in 1979–2021.
  • Hjort et al. project that 550 km of TAPS lies where near-surface permafrost may thaw by 2050 (a projection).
  • Across the Russian Arctic, 45% of hydrocarbon extraction fields are in regions where thaw-related ground instability could cause severe damage (projection).
Mission check

?Engineers plan a pipeline to carry warm oil across ice-rich permafrost in Siberia. They propose raising long stretches on supports. Why?

Buried warm oil would thaw ice-rich permafrost; the ground would collapse into thermokarst ("irregular surfaces of marshy bowls") and take the pipe with it. So pipelines are raised over unstable permafrost and buried only where thaw does no harm.

Mission 10

Mission 10: For a century, two dams stood on a river draining glaciated mountains. Why did the beach at its mouth shrink?

Make a guess: For a century, two dams stood on a river draining glaciated mountains. Why did the beach at its mouth shrink?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 1.1.2-4.b case study of a glaciated landscape used by people (e.g. dam building): sediment trapping, channel scour, valley-floor change
Mission 10

A glaciated landscape

  • The Elwha flows north from the Olympic Mountains to the Strait of Juan de Fuca.
  • Valley glaciers "carved out deep U-shaped troughs"; they peaked about 19,000 years ago.
  • About 14,000 years ago a continental ice sheet reached about 11 km inland of today's coast.
  • Ice dammed the lower valley, leaving lake and outwash deposits now exposed in river bluffs.
  • Small glaciers remain around Mount Olympus.
Mission 10

Glacial sediment feeds the river

  • A glaciated valley is full of loose debris: till, outwash, lake silts.
  • River bars in the lower Elwha contain "large proportions of reworked glacial materials", from silt and clay to cobbles.
  • So the natural river carries a heavy load of sand, gravel and cobbles to the sea.
  • Hold that load back, and the whole system downstream must adjust.
Mission 10

The dams

  • Elwha Dam: 32 m high, built 1913, 7.9 km from the mouth; reservoir Lake Aldwell.
  • Glines Canyon Dam: 64 m high, built 1927, 21.7 km upstream; reservoir Lake Mills.
  • Both were hydroelectric projects.
  • They also "blocked the migration of salmon upstream".
Mission 10

Sediment trapping

  • A reservoir is still water. The river slows, loses energy and drops its load.
  • Coarse gravel and boulders settle first, building a delta at the reservoir head; fine silt and clay drift further towards the dam.
  • About 19 million m³ of sediment had built up in the two reservoirs by 2010.
  • Lake Mills sediment: 48% silt and clay, 37% sand, 15% gravel, cobbles and boulders.
Quick check
?Where in a reservoir would you expect the coarsest sediment to settle?
Mission 10

Channel scour below the dams

  • Water leaving a dam carries almost no sediment, so it has spare energy.
  • It scours: picks up fine bed material and leaves the coarse behind.
  • On the Elwha this caused "bed coarsening and armoring", with an "armored, cobble substrate".
  • Other changes: more channelisation, less lateral migration of the channel, older floodplain forests.
  • Dams can cause "downstream incision, bed armoring, channel simplification, and coastal shoreline retreat".
Mission 10

The valley floor and the coast

  • The dams blocked salmon from the upper river, and spawning gravel below them dwindled. Salmon below Elwha Dam fell to about 1% of pre-dam numbers.
  • Shorelines need river sand and gravel to replace what waves remove.
  • The Elwha delta shoreline retreated up to 160 m between 1939 and 2006.
  • Erosion "averaged 0.6 m/yr during the latter 20th century". A cobble low-tide terrace was left behind.
Quick check
?Why does a river's bed often coarsen below a large dam?
Mission 10

Removing the dams: a natural experiment

  • The dams came down between 2011 and 2014.
  • About 30 Mt of trapped sediment was exposed. In the old Lake Mills, the river cut down more than 10 m into its own deposits.
  • In the second year the reservoirs supplied "~70 times more sediment" than the river's natural load.
  • A wave of sand and gravel raised much of the channel by about 1 m and made its bed sediment about 16 times finer.
Mission 10

Where the sediment went

  • In five years about 65% of the stored sediment was eroded.
  • Of what moved, only about 10% stayed in the river and floodplain; about 90% reached the coast.
  • The delta grew by about 60 ha: "a fundamental regime shift from coastal erosion to deposition".
  • Beaches that had been eroding began to build out.
Quick check
?Before removal, about 19 million m³ of sediment sat in the reservoirs. If about 65% was eroded within five years, roughly how much moved?
Mission check

?A new 80 m dam is built in a glacial trough in the Alps. Its river carries sand and gravel to a lake 30 km downstream. What change is most likely over the next few decades?

On the Elwha, two dams trapped sediment from a glaciated valley. Below them the river scoured and armoured its bed, and the coast, starved of sand and gravel, retreated by up to 160 m. Removing the dams reversed much of this within five years.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Mass balance = accumulation − ablation. A negative balance makes the snout retreat, even though the ice keeps flowing forward.
  • Ice moves by deformation and basal sliding.
  • Ice erodes by plucking and abrasion, and deposits unsorted till.

Mission 2

  • Budget = accumulation − ablation; it sets whether a glacier grows or shrinks, and the snout follows after a lag.
  • Warm-based ice slides on meltwater and moves faster; cold-based ice is frozen to its bed and creeps slowly.

Mission 3

  • Plucking makes steep, broken faces; abrasion makes smooth, striated slopes.
  • Corries, arêtes and troughs on high ground; roches moutonnées where ice sheets scoured lower ground.

Mission 4

  • Ice drops unsorted till: moraines at its edges, drumlins and till plains beneath it, erratics far from their source.

Mission 5

  • The Lötschental shows a valley-glacier system at work: rock, ice and debris link the landforms, and change runs from millions of years to seconds.
  • Monitoring and early evacuation turned a disaster that destroyed a village into one with a single death.

Mission 6

  • Greenland's ice sheet (1.7 million km², over 3 km thick) drains through outlet glaciers such as Jakobshavn Isbræ (6.5% of its area), shaped by climate, bed relief, ocean and meltwater.
  • Landforms link as systems (dome → outlet → calving front → icefjord; lake → moulin → bed → outwash) and change over seconds (quakes), hours (lake drainage), seasons, decades (speed doubled 1992–2003) and millennia (ice-core record, rebound).

Mission 7

  • Meltwater flows on, in and under ice; it erodes, carries and sorts sediment, so fluvioglacial deposits are sorted, stratified and rounded, unlike till.
  • Most formed during post-glacial warming; today rivers, mass movement and lake infilling modify them, and glacier retreat creates new ones.

Mission 8

  • Permafrost is defined by temperature; the active layer thaws each summer.
  • Frost heave sorts patterned ground; contraction cracking builds ice wedges; freezing of a drained lake bed builds a closed-system pingo.
  • Thaw degrades these landforms into thermokarst; collapsed pingos record past permafrost.

Mission 9

  • TAPS is raised for 420 of its 800 miles because warm oil would thaw ice-rich permafrost.
  • Infrastructure alters energy and water flows; ground ice melts; thermokarst spreads (19% of remaining natural landscape at Prudhoe Bay).

Mission 10

  • Dams in glaciated valleys trap the heavy glacial sediment load: deltas build in reservoirs.
  • Below the dam, sediment-free water scours and coarsens the bed; floodplains simplify; coasts retreat.
  • The Elwha's dam removal shows these effects can reverse quickly when sediment supply returns.
