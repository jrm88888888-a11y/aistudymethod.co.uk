Mission 1

What food security means

  • FAO (World Food Summit, 1996): "Food security exists when all people, at all times, have physical and economic access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food that meets their dietary needs and food preferences for an active and healthy life".
  • OCR's three pillars: availability (enough food, from farms or imports), access (the money or rights to obtain it) and use (a diet, clean water and health care that turn food into nutrition).
  • FAO adds a fourth: stability, access "at all times".

Mission 1

How much food is available per person

  • Dietary energy supply (DES): food available per person per day, in kilocalories (kcal).
  • World: 2,825 kcal (2010) → 3,016 kcal (2023).
  • 2023: Europe 3,490 · Americas 3,438 · Oceania 3,137 · Asia 2,991 · Africa 2,556 kcal.
  • Diets change as incomes rise: in Asia, DES rose from 2,694 to 2,991 kcal, and animal foods' share of protein from 34% to 37%, "reflecting rising incomes and changing consumption patterns".
Quick check
?In 2023, Europe's food supply was 3,490 kcal per person per day and Africa's 2,556. By what percentage did Europe's supply exceed Africa's?
Mission 1

Production is not consumption

  • Trade moves food from surplus to deficit regions: Singapore imports over 90% of its food, with about 1% of its land available for food production.
  • Much production feeds animals or fuel, not people directly.
  • DES measures supply, not intake: food later wasted in shops and homes still counts.
  • So high production or supply does not mean everyone eats enough.
Mission 1

Hunger: a shifting pattern

  • Prevalence of undernourishment (PoU): the share of people whose calorie intake is insufficient.
  • 2025: 7.8% of the world, about 645 million people, down from 8.1% in 2024.
  • Africa: 20.0%, about 309 million people, now more than Asia's 292 million.
  • Asia's rate fell from 7.9% (2022) to 6.7% (2024). The centre of world hunger is moving to Africa.
Mission 1

The Global Hunger Index (GHI)

  • Combines four indicators: undernourishment and child mortality (one-third each); child stunting and child wasting (one-sixth each).
  • 0 = no hunger; 100 = worst. Bands: low (≤9.9), moderate (10.0–19.9), serious (20.0–34.9), alarming (35.0–49.9), extremely alarming (≥50.0).
  • 2026: world 18.1 (moderate), higher than in 2018, "interrupting the decline since 2000".
  • Hunger is serious in Africa south of the Sahara and South Asia. Somalia scores 47.0, last of 122 ranked.
Mission 1

The Global Food Security Index (GFSI)

  • Economist Impact scores 113 countries on 68 drivers in four pillars: affordability, availability, quality and safety, and sustainability and adaptation.
  • 2022 (latest edition): Finland 83.7, Ireland 81.7, Norway 80.5 at the top.
  • Global food security "continued to deteriorate after hitting its peak in 2019".
  • It was "supported by Corteva Agriscience", an agribusiness: ask who funds an index.
Quick check
?Which statement best distinguishes the GHI from the GFSI?
Mission 1

Reading hunger data critically

  • PoU is a modelled estimate, not a head count.
  • The GHI cannot score countries with missing data: Sudan, Burundi, South Sudan and Yemen are rated alarming only provisionally.
  • The GFSI dates from 2022.
  • Exam move: name the indicator, say what it measures, and give one limitation before using its numbers.
Mission 1

Within countries: the USA

  • The USA had the highest food supply per person of 176 countries in 2023: about 3,950 kcal a day.
  • Even so, 13.7% of US households (18.3 million) were food insecure at some time in 2024.
  • By state (2022–24 average): from 9.0% in North Dakota to 19.4% in Arkansas.
  • In Africa, more than half of people (56.6%) faced moderate or severe food insecurity in 2025.
  • Food security varies between places at every scale, and changes over time.
Mission 1

Exam move: describing patterns

  • Contrast between (Africa 20.0% undernourished) and within (US states 9.0% to 19.4%).
  • Explain with access as well as supply: income, prices, trade and conflict decide who eats.
Mission check

?In 2024, 13.7% of US households were food insecure at some time, although the USA had the world's highest food supply per person, about 3,950 kcal a day in 2023. What best explains this?

The same logic answers the hook. The world's 3,016 kcal per person is an average of supply; around 645 million people still went hungry in 2025 because access is unequal, and 20.0% of Africans were undernourished.

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 3.4-1.b physical conditions for growing food: air, climate, soil, water
  • 3.4-1.b feeding the world as a system: growing, processing, transporting, disposing of waste

Mission 2

What crops and animals need

  • Climate and air: warmth for a long enough growing season, no damaging frost, enough sunlight for photosynthesis, and carbon dioxide from the air.
  • Soil: depth, texture, drainage, nutrients and pH control roots, water and machinery access.
  • Water: rain or irrigation must supply crops when they grow.
  • Relief: slope and altitude shape temperature, soil depth and what machines can do.
  • England's land grading uses "a combination of climate, topography and soil characteristics".
Mission 2

Water is the tightest limit

  • Agriculture takes 69% of the world's freshwater withdrawals; municipal 12%, industry 19% (FAO AQUASTAT).
  • Irrigation lifts yields but can drain stores: in north-west India, satellites showed groundwater falling by 4.0 cm a year (2002–2008), likely from "unsustainable consumption of groundwater for irrigation".
  • Climate change "has slowed this growth" in agricultural productivity "over the past 50 years globally" (IPCC).
Quick check
?A farm region has deep, fertile soil and a long, warm growing season, but crops wilt every summer. Which physical condition most limits it?
Mission 2

Feeding the world is a system

  • Inputs: land, water, seed, fertiliser, feed, energy, labour, capital.
  • Growing → processing (milling, packing, cooking) → transporting and trading → retail → consumption → waste disposal.
  • Each stage uses energy and can lose food. Food systems produce about a third of global human greenhouse gas emissions.
  • Not all crops become food: 36% of the calories grown by the world's crops go to animal feed, and only 12% of those feed calories reach human diets.
Mission 2

Losses and waste

  • Food loss: food lost between harvest and the shop. "Over 13 percent of food is lost globally in the supply chain after harvest on farms and before the retail stages" (FAO).
  • Food waste: food thrown away by shops, food service and households. In 2022: 1.05 billion tonnes, "almost one-fifth of all food available to consumers"; 132 kg a person (UNEP).
  • Where waste happens: households 60%, food service 28%, retail 12%.
  • Causes differ: poor storage, pests, heat and slow transport cause loss; over-buying, date labels and plate waste cause waste.
Quick check
?Food waste was 1.05 billion tonnes in 2022, and households produced 60% of it. About how much did households waste?
Mission 2

Ways of producing food

  • Intensive: high inputs of labour or capital per hectare, high output per hectare (glasshouses, paddy rice, feedlots).
  • Extensive: low inputs spread over large areas, low output per hectare (ranching, rangeland grazing).
  • Subsistence: grown mainly to feed the farm household. Commercial: grown to sell for profit.
  • The two scales cross: subsistence farming can be intensive (terraced paddies); commercial farming can be extensive (cattle stations).
Mission 2

Who farms the land?

  • Farms under 2 hectares are 84% of all farms, but operate only about 12% of farmland and produce roughly 35% of the world's food.
  • Grazing uses 25% of ice-free land; crops use 12%.
  • High-tech intensive systems swap land for energy: lettuce in a plant factory needed 247 kWh of electricity per kg (dry weight), against 70 kWh in a Netherlands greenhouse.
Quick check
?A family farms 1 hectare of terraced rice by hand, uses much labour per hectare, and eats most of the harvest. How is it best classified?
Mission 2

Methods shape the system

  • Commercial, intensive systems depend on long chains: processing, refrigerated transport, retailers; losses shift towards retail and households.
  • Subsistence systems have short chains, but little storage, cooling or roads, so more is lost after harvest.
  • So reducing losses means different things in different places: storage and roads in one, shopping and labelling in another.
  • Exam move (OCR "Assess"): judge which stage of the system matters most for a named place, and say why it differs elsewhere.
Mission check

?In a hot country, most farmers are smallholders who sell surplus mangoes at a market a day's drive away on poor roads, with no cold storage. Where in the system is food most likely to go missing?

Food goes missing at different stages in different systems: mostly after harvest where storage and roads are poor, and mostly in homes and shops where chains are long and food is cheap.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Strawberries in December, and food prices that spike worldwide at once. Is global food a gift or a risk?

Make a guess: Strawberries in December, and food prices that spike worldwide at once. Is global food a gift or a risk?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 3.4-1.c influence of globalisation on the food industry: increased demand, global tastes
  • 3.4-1.c issues: food miles, inequality between TNCs and small suppliers, obesity, price crisis
  • 3.4-1.c opportunities: technological innovation, short-term food relief, consumer choice
Mission 3

A global food system

  • Food is now grown, processed and sold through global supply chains.
  • TNCs (agribusiness, food processors, supermarkets, fast-food chains) link farms in one country to shoppers in another.
  • Trade rules, container shipping and cold chains make it possible.
  • OCR asks for three things: how globalisation changes demand, the issues it creates and the opportunities it brings.
Mission 3

Increased demand

  • As incomes rise, diets shift towards more meat and dairy.
  • China's meat supply per person: 3.32 kg a year (1961) → 44.00 kg (2000) → 73.54 kg (2023).
  • Livestock need feed: China's soybean imports rose from 10,425 thousand tonnes (2000) to 103,387 thousand tonnes (2023), almost 10 times (our calculation).
  • The soy is crushed into meal for livestock feed, so a diet change in one country drives farming in another.
Mission 3

Global tastes

  • Shoppers expect year-round fruit and vegetables and foods from other cuisines.
  • In 2024 Kenya exported US$281.3 million of vegetables; US$81.8 million (29%) went to the UK.
  • Global brands (fast food, soft drinks, snacks) spread similar tastes across very different countries.
Quick check
?Why did China's soybean imports rise almost tenfold between 2000 and 2023?
Mission 3

Issue 1: food miles

  • Food miles: the distance food travels from producer to consumer.
  • One analysis: transport is about 6% of food's emissions; what we eat (meat, dairy) matters far more.
  • A 2022 study counting full supply chains: about 19%. The evidence is still debated.
  • Air freight carries only 0.16% of food miles but emits about 50 times more CO₂e than a boat per tonne-kilometre.
  • Exam move (OCR "Assess"): food miles matter most for air-freighted, perishable food.
Mission 3

Issue 2: TNCs and small suppliers

  • A few large buyers (supermarkets, processors) deal with many small suppliers.
  • Big buyers can set prices, change orders late or pass on costs; small suppliers have little choice.
  • The UK set up the Groceries Code Adjudicator, which "regulates the relationships between the UK's largest grocery retailers and their direct suppliers".
  • Farmers in poorer exporting countries, further down the chain, often have even less bargaining power.
Mission 3

Issue 3: obesity

  • In 2022, 2.5 billion adults were overweight, including 890 million living with obesity.
  • Adult obesity rose from 12.1% (2012) to 16.2% (2024) of adults worldwide.
  • Globalised food spreads cheap, energy-dense processed food and drinks, often alongside undernutrition in the same country.
Mission 3

Issue 4: price crises

  • The FAO Food Price Index tracks world prices for a basket of foods (2014–2016 = 100).
  • 2007–08: 78.5 (Jan 2007) → 132.7 (Jun 2008).
  • 2020–22: 91.2 (May 2020) → 160.2 (Mar 2022), its peak.
  • Connected markets pass a shock in one region (drought, war, high fuel prices) to importers everywhere; poor households, who spend most on food, are hit hardest.
Quick check
?The FAO Food Price Index rose from 91.2 (May 2020) to 160.2 (March 2022). By about what percentage did it rise?
Mission 3

Opportunities

  • Technological innovation: global investment spreads new methods, e.g. cell-based food, which FAO says may "provide more sustainable ways of producing animal protein".
  • Short-term food relief: global trade and shipping let food be bought where it is plentiful and moved quickly to an emergency.
  • Consumer choice: year-round, varied, often cheaper food; exporters such as Kenya's vegetable growers gain markets.
Quick check
?Which is an opportunity of a globalised food industry, as OCR uses the term?
Mission check

?A drought cuts the wheat harvest in one major exporting country. Which outcome best shows globalised food as both a risk and an opportunity?

Globalisation raises demand (meat, feed) and choice, but also brings food miles, unequal power, obesity and shared price shocks. Whether it is a gift or a risk depends on who you are: shopper, small supplier or poor importer.

Mission 4

Malthus: the pessimistic view

  • Malthus (1798): "Population, when unchecked, increases in a geometrical ratio. Subsistence increases only in an arithmetical ratio".
  • Population outgrows food until checks act:
    • preventive checks lower births (for example, later marriage)
    • positive checks raise deaths (for example, famine and disease).
  • A Malthusian position on food security: food supply sets a ceiling, and growing population pushes against it.
Mission 4

Boserup: the optimistic view

  • Ester Boserup (The Conditions of Agricultural Growth, 1965): in Turner and Ali's summary, "population growth is the central source of demand driving the intensification of cultivation".
  • Pressure on land drives new methods, such as irrigation for double and triple cropping and "green revolution" inputs.
  • In short: necessity is the mother of invention.
  • A Boserupian position on food security: population pressure raises food output.
Quick check
?As population density rose, farmers shortened fallow periods and built terraces to grow more crops on the same land. Which view does this support?
Mission 4

Testing the views with data

  • World population: 3.06 billion (1961), 8.06 billion (2023).
  • World cereal yield: 1,422 kg per hectare (1961), 4,229 kg per hectare (2023).
  • World food supply: 2,196 kcal per person per day (1961), 2,884 (2013), on FAO's older series.
  • Yields grew about 3.0 times while population grew about 2.6 times (our calculation).
Quick check
?World food supply was 2,196 kcal per person per day in 1961 and 2,884 in 2013. By what percentage did it rise?
Mission 4

Physical factors in food security

  • Geology gives the parent material for soils and affects drainage and minerals.
  • Soil: depth, fertility, texture and drainage set what can be grown and how much.
  • Length of growing season: set by temperature (frost-free days) and rainfall; short or dry seasons limit harvests.
  • Land is limited: world arable land per person fell from 0.359 ha (1961) to 0.173 ha (2023).
Mission 4

Land ownership, competition and land grabbing

  • Land ownership systems: who holds land, and how securely, affects investment. In Bangladeshi villages (1950–1986) farmers kept pace with demand, but the share of landless households rose.
  • Competition for land: crops for export and feed (South America's soy area more than doubled 2000–2019), bioenergy (which the IPCC links to "risks … to water supply, food security and biodiversity" at large scale) and cities.
  • Land grabbing: large-scale acquisitions of land, often by foreign investors. The Land Matrix tracks "concluded deals, intended and failed attempts to acquire land through purchase, lease or concession".
Quick check
?Which is an example of land grabbing?
Mission 4

Evaluating the views

  • Against Malthus: food per person rose 31% (1961–2013), as yields tripled; he did not foresee the Green Revolution or large-scale trade in food.
  • For a Malthusian caution: 645 million people were still undernourished in 2025; six of nine planetary boundaries (Earth-system limits that mark the "safe operating space for humanity") were transgressed in 2023, seven by 2025.
  • Limits of Boserup: she assumed gradual growth and time to adapt; rapid growth or degraded soils can outpace intensification.
  • Exam move: say which view the evidence supports, at what scale, and for whom.
Mission check

?A study of Bangladeshi villages (1950–1986) found that small farmers "kept pace with the demands on production", but the share of landless households increased. Which conclusion is best supported?

Globally, food per person rose, from 2,196 to 2,884 kcal a day (1961–2013), because cereal yields roughly tripled while population grew 2.6 times. That fits Boserup better than Malthus, but hunger and planetary limits remain, and the answer depends on scale.

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 3.4-2.a case study of one place where human and physical factors combine to cause food insecurity (Somalia, 2010–12; 2022 comparison)
Mission 5

From food insecurity to famine

  • Food insecurity: people lack reliable access to enough safe, nutritious food.
  • The IPC scale runs to Famine (Phase 5), declared only when one area passes three thresholds:
    • at least 20% of households face an extreme lack of food;
    • at least 30% of children under five are acutely malnourished;
    • more than 2 non-trauma deaths per 10,000 people a day (or 4 per 10,000 children under five).
Mission 5

Famine is about access (Sen)

  • Amartya Sen showed famines have struck when food supply was not much lower than in earlier years, and famine areas sometimes exported food.
  • Bangladesh, 1974: floods raised prices and destroyed a crop; farm workers' real incomes "declined so much that this group was disproportionately stricken by starvation".
  • The key idea is entitlement: whether people can command food through work, wages, trade or aid.
Quick check
?A region's harvest is only slightly below normal, yet food prices double and day labourers lose their work. What does Sen's approach predict?
Mission 5

Somalia 2010–11: physical and human causes

  • A joint FAO (FSNAU) and FEWS NET study looked at the areas "most affected by the 2010-11 drought, subsequent spikes in staple food prices, and constraints on humanitarian access".
  • Physical: drought (failed rains) cut crops and pasture.
  • Human, economic: staple food prices spiked.
  • Human, political: armed conflict restricted access for aid agencies.
  • The UN declared famine on 20 July 2011.
Mission 5

How severe?

  • An estimated 258,000 people died between October 2010 and April 2012, including an estimated 133,000 children under five.
  • In southern and central Somalia, an estimated 4.6% of the population and about 10% of children under five died.
  • Lower Shabelle, Mogadishu and Bay were hardest hit; deaths peaked at about 30,000 a month from May to August 2011.
  • Hunger weakens resistance to disease, so many famine deaths come from disease.
Mission 5

Governance and access to aid

  • Much of the famine area was controlled by al-Shabaab, an armed group. It banned WFP in January 2010 and later 16 international organisations.
  • An ODI study found restrictions by both al-Shabaab and donors "hindered attempts to address the famine".
  • Donor counter-terrorism rules restricted aid in al-Shabaab areas; USAID humanitarian funding fell by 88% between 2008 and 2010.
  • The famine "predominantly affected Al-Shabaab areas".
Mission 5

Warning without action

  • "Sophisticated early warning systems first forecast a likely emergency as early as August 2010".
  • The full-scale response "was not launched until July 2011".
  • Oxfam and Save the Children: "a culture of risk aversion caused a six month delay"; donors wanted proof first.
  • High-profile media coverage came only as the response was launched. The UN's coordinator: "we could have done more before famine was declared".
Quick check
?Deaths peaked at about 30,000 a month in mid-2011. Roughly how many people a day is that?
Mission 5

How the factors combined

  • Drought alone did not cause the famine. It hit a population already exposed by conflict and poverty.
  • Drought → crop and livestock losses → higher prices and lost income → falling entitlements.
  • Conflict and aid bans → aid blocked; donor rules and caution → help delayed.
  • Exam move: OCR wants how human and physical factors combine: show each link in the chain and which factor turned a drought into a famine.
Mission 5

Testing a theory: Sen on democracy

  • Sen: "no substantial famine has ever occurred in any independent and democratic country with a relatively free press".
  • Reasoning: elected governments face pressure to act, and a free press raises the alarm.
  • In 2011 much of the famine area was run by an armed group, not an elected government, and international attention came late.
Mission 5

Somalia 2022: lessons learned?

  • A study for Somalia's health ministry, WHO and UNICEF estimated 43,000 excess deaths "might have occurred in 2022" in the drought, half among children under five.
  • Famine was "averted for now"; UNICEF judged "there could have been many more deaths had humanitarian assistance not been scaled up".
  • Both 2011 and 2022 figures are modelled estimates: compare them with care.
Mission check

?In 2022 Somalia again faced a severe drought. An estimated 43,000 people died, but famine was averted. Which difference from 2011 best explains why?

In 2011 the warning came in August 2010, but drought, price spikes, an armed group's aid bans, donor rules, risk aversion and late media attention let food insecurity become famine: about 258,000 deaths. Physical and human factors combined; the human ones decided the outcome.

Mission 6

Energy pathways and chokepoints

  • An energy pathway is the route energy takes from producer to consumer: a pipeline, shipping lane or power line.
  • A chokepoint is a narrow point on a pathway with few alternatives.
  • OCR calls these pinchpoints: points where storage or distribution can fail and put food security at risk.
  • In 2025, an average of 20 million barrels a day of crude oil and oil products passed through the Strait of Hormuz.
  • Asia is "normally the destination for the vast majority of the oil and gas that flows through the Strait".
Mission 6

Suez and the Red Sea

  • The Suez Canal "normally passes about 15 percent of global maritime trade volume".
  • After attacks on shipping in the Red Sea, Suez trade volume "dropped by 50 percent year-over-year in the first two months of 2024".
  • Ships diverted around the Cape of Good Hope, adding "10 days or more on average" to deliveries.
  • A chokepoint does not have to close to cause harm: delay and extra cost spread along supply chains.
Mission 6

The 2026 Hormuz disruption

  • "The conflict in the Middle East that began on 28 February 2026 has impeded oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz".
  • Flows averaged 2.7 million barrels a day in March, April and May.
  • The North Sea Dated crude price "reached an all-time high of $144 per barrel – more than double its pre-war levels".
  • Responses: IEA members released 400 million barrels of emergency stocks; Saudi Arabia and the UAE used pipelines that bypass the strait.
Quick check
?Oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz fell from about 20 million barrels a day (2025) to 2.7 million (March to May 2026). What was the percentage fall?
Mission 6

From energy shock to food shock

  • The UN's SDG Report 2026: disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz "affected the movement of key inputs for food production – including fertilizers, sulphur, natural gas and fuels".
  • This drove up "production and transportation costs across food systems".
  • "Import-dependent countries are especially vulnerable".
  • The link is the water–food–energy nexus: farming runs on fuel and fertiliser.
Mission 6

Who is most at risk?

  • Risk depends on import dependence, income and stocks, across the development spectrum:
    • Singapore (high income) imports more than 90% of its food, but diversifies suppliers and stockpiles.
    • Jordan (upper-middle income) produced 80 thousand tonnes of cereals in 2023 against a domestic supply of 3,539 thousand tonnes.
    • Sub-Saharan Africa holds 71% of the world's extreme poor.
  • In 2025, 645 million people were chronically undernourished; undernourishment "continued to increase in Western Asia".
  • The people most at risk are the poorest households in import-dependent places, who have least money to absorb price rises.
Quick check
?A chokepoint closure raises world prices of fuel, fertiliser and grain. Which country is most at risk?
Mission check

?A country imports most of its diesel and fertiliser by sea through one strait. The strait is blocked for three months. What is the most likely first effect on its farmers?

In 2026 Hormuz flows fell about 86.5% (our calculation from IEA figures). The UN found that fertilisers, sulfur, natural gas and fuels were disrupted, raising production and transport costs across food systems. Import-dependent countries were most exposed: an energy shock became a food shock.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Between 11,000 and 5,000 years ago, the Sahara had permanent lakes, plants and people. Today it is desert. Did people cause the change?

Make a guess: Between 11,000 and 5,000 years ago, the Sahara had permanent lakes, plants and people. Today it is desert. Did people cause the change?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 3.4-3.a physical and human causes of desertification and its effect on ecosystems and food risk
  • 3.4-3.a case study of one dryland area (ecosystem, climate, hydrology); worsening factors (population, land grabs, climate change)
Mission 7

What desertification is

  • IPCC: "Desertification is land degradation in arid, semi-arid, and dry sub-humid areas, collectively known as drylands, resulting from many factors, including human activities and climatic variations".
  • It means drylands losing productivity (soil, water, vegetation), not simply sand dunes advancing.
  • Its "range and intensity … have increased in some dryland areas over the past several decades (high confidence)".
Mission 7

The Green Sahara and its end

  • From 11,000 to 5,000 years ago, the Sahara had high rainfall, varied vegetation, permanent lakes and people; wet conditions reached 31° N. This is the African Humid Period.
  • It was paced by slow changes in Earth's orbit (precession), which strengthened the summer monsoon and pushed its rains north.
  • As that forcing faded, the monsoon retreated. The high rainfall is "best explained by strong vegetation and dust feedbacks"; the IPCC adds that a cooling of the north Atlantic played a role.
  • Feedback: fewer plants → brighter, dustier land → less rain → fewer plants.
Mission 7

Case study: the Sahel

  • Where: the semi-arid belt along the southern edge of the Sahara, from Senegal to Sudan.
  • Ecosystem: grassland and dry savanna, with scattered trees; used for pastoralism and rain-fed crops (millet, sorghum).
  • Climate: a short summer rainy season brought by the West African monsoon. Summer rainfall was high in the 1950s and 1960s, fell sharply in the 1970s and 1980s (the driest year in the index was 1984) and has partly recovered since.
  • Hydrology: Lake Chad shrank to less than 2,000 km² in the 1980s, a fall of more than 90%. Since 2001 its southern pool has slightly increased, and the authors conclude that "for now Lake Chad is not disappearing".
Mission 7

Human causes

  • Croplands in the West African Sahel "have doubled since 1975, with settlement area increasing by about 150%".
  • Overgrazing reduced the seed bank of annual plants, leaving them more vulnerable to climate change.
  • IPCC: the main human drivers interacting with climate change are "expansion of croplands, unsustainable land management practices and increased pressure on land from population and income growth".
  • Bare soil loses more water to runoff and more soil to wind, so plants struggle to return: positive feedback.
Mission 7

Worsening factors

  • Population: Niger's population rose from about 3.5 million in 1960 to about 27.9 million in 2025, roughly eightfold; its fertility rate was 5.9 births per woman in 2024.
  • Land grabs: large-scale acquisitions of land for commercial farms can push farmers and herders onto marginal land. The IPCC finds that secure tenure, "especially for communally managed lands, helps reduce arbitrary appropriations of land for large-scale commercial farms".
  • Climate change: "Climate change will exacerbate several desertification processes (medium confidence)".
  • These factors interact: more people on less land, in a more variable climate.
Mission 7

Is the Sahel still desertifying?

  • IPCC: "wetting and greening conditions have been observed in this region over the last three decades".
  • One analysis found climate the dominant cause of greening in 1982–2003, with pockets of desertification in Nigeria and Sudan.
  • Studies disagree because they use different indicators, places and periods. The relative roles of climate and people "are location-specific and evolve over time (high confidence)".
Quick check
?Which conclusion best fits the IPCC's assessment of desertification in the Sahel?
Mission 7

Impacts on ecosystems, landscapes and people

  • Ecosystems: desertification has "contributed to the loss of biodiversity in some dryland regions (medium confidence)", and invasive plants have reduced ecosystem services.
  • Landscapes: soil erosion, more dust storms, and sand and dune movement that damages roads and infrastructure.
  • Food and people: the 1970s–80s Sahel droughts contributed to "hunger, out-migration and suffering for millions of pastoralists". Desertification "has already reduced agricultural productivity and incomes (high confidence)".
  • Food risk: the IPCC projects that desertification and changing climate will reduce crop and livestock productivity (high confidence).
Quick check
?Why does desertification raise food risk for households in the Sahel?
Mission 7

Areas at risk

  • Desertification hotspots (falling vegetation productivity, 1980s–2000s) covered about 9.2% of drylands and affected about 500 million people in 2015.
  • Most people affected are in South and East Asia, the circum-Sahara region including North Africa, and the Middle East including the Arabian Peninsula (low confidence).
  • Risk is highest where dryland populations are poor: poverty limits both adaptation and investment in sustainable land management.
Mission check

?About 18,000 years ago, sand seas covered almost 50% of the land between 30° N and 30° S; today they cover about 10%. Did people cause that change?

Deserts grow and shrink with natural climate change: the ice-age sand seas shrank, and the Green Sahara ended about 5,000 years ago through orbital change in the monsoon, amplified by feedbacks. Today, in the Sahel, climate and people both drive change, in proportions that vary by place.

Mission 8

Mission 8: How can warm water in the Pacific leave families in southern Africa short of food?

Make a guess: How can warm water in the Pacific leave families in southern Africa short of food?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 3.4-3.b climate change increasing extreme events (wildfire, El Niño, floods, drought) affecting production
  • 3.4-3.b water scarcity exacerbating food production issues
  • 3.4-3.b tectonic hazards affecting food production and distribution
Mission 8

Shocks to the food system

  • A shock is a sudden event that cuts production (growing) or distribution (moving and selling food).
  • IPCC: "Increasing weather and climate extreme events have exposed millions of people to acute food insecurity".
  • Extremes cause "sudden losses of food production and access to food".
  • Wildfire: "Observed increases in areas burned by wildfires have been attributed to human-induced climate change in some regions"; fires destroy crops, pasture, livestock and stores.
  • Future heat and drought "will increase food prices".
Mission 8

El Niño reaches across the world

  • El Niño: warmer-than-usual surface water in the central and eastern tropical Pacific; "During El Niño, upwelling weakens or stops altogether".
  • Shifting ocean heat shifts rising air and rain belts, so rainfall changes far away (teleconnections). Episodes "typically last nine to 12 months".
  • Southern Africa, 2023–24: El Niño was "the primary driver of the significant increase in food insecurity"; 71 million people were food insecure, 17.9% of the population (SADC).
  • SADC stresses that El Niño was "not the sole driver" of food insecurity: other pressures were already there.
Quick check
?Why can El Niño cut maize harvests thousands of kilometres from the Pacific?
Mission 8

Drought: the Horn of Africa, 2020–23

  • Southern Ethiopia, southern Somalia and eastern Kenya had "the fifth consecutive failed season since OND 2020".
  • World Weather Attribution: climate change made such droughts "about 100 times more likely", mainly through "the strong increase in evaporative demand caused by higher temperatures".
  • "Households engaged in rain-fed agriculture, agropastoralism, and pastoralism are vulnerable to drought": crops fail and livestock die.
Mission 8

Floods: too much water at the wrong time

  • Floods drown crops, kill livestock, wash away soil and seed stores, and cut roads to markets.
  • Somalia, 2026: FAO reports "severe drought" in the main maize areas, and rains "heightening the risk of floods" along the Juba and Shabelle rivers.
  • FAO also lists "conflict‑related trade disruptions" and "high costs of pump irrigation due to high fuel prices": shocks hit together.
Mission 8

Water scarcity makes every shock worse

  • Agriculture already takes 69% of world freshwater withdrawals.
  • "Roughly half of the world's population currently experience severe water scarcity for at least some part of the year" (IPCC).
  • Where farmers pump groundwater faster than it refills, the buffer against drought shrinks: north-west India lost groundwater at 4.0 cm a year, August 2002 to October 2008, "Annual rainfall was close to normal".
  • So scarcity turns a dry year into a crop failure, and limits how far irrigation can expand.
Quick check
?North-west India's groundwater fell by about 4.0 cm a year from August 2002 to October 2008. About how much did it fall over the whole period?
Mission 8

Earthquakes: Nepal, 2015

  • 25 April 2015, magnitude 7.6, centred in Gorkha district.
  • Production: the quake struck "only a few weeks prior to the start of the paddy planting season"; it destroyed "the stockpile of stored grains"; over 17,000 cattle and about 40,000 smaller animals died.
  • Distribution: "Rural areas... were particularly devastated and further isolated due to road damage and obstructions"; the PDNA stresses "physical connectivity of the affected areas to market centres".
  • PDNA: "The widespread loss of food stocks, potential loss in crop productivity and loss of livestock" could raise food insecurity.
Mission 8

Volcanoes: ash on farmland

  • "Ashfall can have serious detrimental effects on agricultural crops and livestock" (USGS).
  • Crop survival is "severely limited when ash thickness is greater than 100-150 mm".
  • "Fluorine poisoning and death can occur in livestock that graze on ash-covered grass"; ash also blocks animals' guts.
  • "The abrasiveness of ash can damage farm machinery and equipment".
Quick check
?Which effect of the Nepal 2015 earthquake hit food distribution rather than production?
Mission 8

Shocks combine

  • Climate change raises the frequency of extremes; water scarcity removes buffers; tectonic hazards strike without warning.
  • The poorest farmers, with no insurance, savings or irrigation, lose most and recover slowest.
  • Exam move (OCR "Evaluate"): compare shocks by speed, scale, warning time and whether they hit production, distribution or both.
Quick check
?A strong earthquake strikes a mountain farming region just before the main planting season. Which outcome is most likely to cut food supply in the following year?
Mission check

?In an El Niño year, the rains fail over two maize regions far from the Pacific. Region X irrigates from a deep, full aquifer. Region Y's aquifer has been falling for years. Which outcome is most likely?

El Niño cuts food far away by shifting rainfall through the atmosphere; water scarcity decides how hard that shock hits. Earthquakes add a local shock to production and distribution.

Mission 9

Mission 9: Shishmaref, an Iñupiaq village on an Alaskan island, has voted three times to move. Why, and why is it still there?

Make a guess: Shishmaref, an Iñupiaq village on an Alaskan island, has voted three times to move. Why, and why is it still there?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 3.4-3.b case study of an indigenous farming technique in an extreme environment: conditions, methods, threats to food security
Mission 9

Cold environments are warming fast

  • From 1979 to 2021, the Arctic warmed nearly four times as fast as the globe.
  • From 2007 to 2016, permafrost temperature rose by 0.29 ± 0.12 °C globally.
  • Since 2000, glaciers have lost about 5% of their ice globally, and between 2% and 39% regionally.
Mission 9

Shishmaref, Alaska

  • About 500 people live on a barrier island near the Bering Strait, only about a quarter of a mile (about 400 m) wide.
  • NSIDC: since the 1980s summer sea ice has become less predictable, so storm waves batter the thawing permafrost shore; about 7 m of land washes away each year.
  • Residents moved buildings back, laid rocks and sandbags and built seawalls. They voted to relocate in 1973, 2002 and 2016.
  • Actions like these are adaptation: adjusting to the impacts of change. Mitigation means tackling the cause of the change instead.
  • A US Army Corps of Engineers report, cited by NSIDC, put the cost of moving at about $179 million.
Quick check
?Which is mitigation rather than adaptation?
Mission 9

Sámi reindeer herding: the environment

  • Where: Sápmi, northern Fennoscandia. In Sweden, the herding area covers nearly 40% of the country.
  • Terrain and ecosystems: mountain tundra used in summer; lowland boreal forest in winter. "Lichen rangelands are key to sustaining reindeer carrying capacity" (IPCC).
  • Climate: long, cold, snowy winters and a short growing season. Reindeer dig through snow to reach lichen.
  • Northern Fennoscandia has about 600,000 semi-domesticated reindeer.
Mission 9

How the herding works

  • Sweden has 51 Sámi reindeer-herding villages (samebyar): 33 mountain, 10 forest and 8 concession villages.
  • Mountain herding: "long migratory routes between summer and winter pastures". Forest herding is more static. Winter pastures may be used only from 1 October to 30 April.
  • About 4,600 people own reindeer and herding employs about 2,500 (undated figures from the Reindeer Portal, which cites Sámediggi). Reindeer give meat, and historically also milk and transport.
  • In bad winters, some herders give supplementary feed.
Mission 9

Threats to food security

  • Icing: rain on snow freezes into an ice crust over the lichen. This "is associated with... cases of catastrophic mass starvation", though no trend in such events has yet been found (IPCC).
  • Thaw: Sámi herders have seen thawing ground in summer grazing areas since the mid-1990s, leading to "soggy falls and rotten spring vegetation".
  • Land loss: pastures are encroached on by "mining, hydro power development, wind power development and industrial scale logging".
  • Predators: 40,000–45,000 reindeer are killed by predators each year in Sweden (SLU figures).
  • Restricted movement combined with climate change is expected to threaten herding's future (IPCC, high confidence).
Quick check
?After a mild spell in January, heavy rain falls on deep snow and then freezes. Why might herders' reindeer starve?
Mission check

?An Arctic village stands on a river bank of frozen silt. Each summer more of the bank slumps into the river. Residents have voted to relocate, yet the village is still there. Which explanation fits best?

Less sea ice and a thawing permafrost coast mean about 7 m of Shishmaref is lost each year, NSIDC reports. Residents adapt with seawalls and by moving buildings, and have voted three times to relocate, but one estimate put the cost of moving at about $179 million.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Asia produces about half the world's cereals; Europe and the Americas have the highest supply per person; Africa the lowest, with the fastest-growing hunger.
  • Food security rests on availability, access and use; the GHI and GFSI measure different things, and patterns change between and within countries.

Mission 2

  • Food production needs suitable climate and air, soil and water; water is often the tightest limit (agriculture takes 69% of withdrawals).
  • Feeding the world is a system from growing to waste; methods range from intensive to extensive and subsistence to commercial, and each shapes where food is lost.

Mission 3

  • Rising incomes and global tastes drive demand (China's meat 3.32 → 73.54 kg per person; soybean imports ≈10×); Kenya's vegetables fill UK shelves.
  • Issues: food miles (6–19% of food emissions, debated), TNC–supplier power, obesity (16.2% of adults), price crises (FFPI peak 160.2, Mar 2022). Opportunities: innovation, short-term relief, consumer choice.

Mission 4

  • Malthus: population outruns food until checks act.
  • Boserup: population pressure drives intensification.
  • Since 1961 food per person has risen globally, but hunger, inequality and environmental limits keep the debate open.
  • Food security also depends on geology, soil and growing season, and on land ownership, competition and land grabbing.

Mission 5

  • Famine is about access (entitlements), not only supply.
  • Somalia 2011: drought (physical) plus price spikes, conflict, aid bans and a delayed response (human) caused famine.
  • 2022: a severe drought, but scaled-up aid averted famine.

Mission 6

  • Energy pathways (pipelines, shipping lanes, power lines) pass through chokepoints such as Hormuz and Suez; disruption raises costs far away.
  • Risk is highest where import dependence meets low income; pinchpoints threaten food as well as fuel.

Mission 7

  • Desertification is land degradation in drylands, caused by climatic variation and human activity together.
  • The Sahara greened and dried naturally; in today's Sahel, cropland expansion, overgrazing and population pressure interact with rainfall change, and land grabs and climate change worsen it.

Mission 8

  • Climate change is increasing extremes (wildfire, drought, floods, strong El Niño effects) that cut food production; El Niño drove southern Africa's 2023–24 food crisis.
  • Water scarcity removes the buffer against drought; tectonic hazards destroy stores, animals and roads, hitting both production and distribution.

Mission 9

  • Cold environments are warming fastest; permafrost and ice are retreating, with impacts on land, ecosystems and people.
  • Sámi reindeer herding: migration between mountain and forest pastures; threatened by icing, thaw, land loss and predators.
