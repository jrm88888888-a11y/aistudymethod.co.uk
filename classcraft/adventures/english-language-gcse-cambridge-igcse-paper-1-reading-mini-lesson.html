Mission 1

The text never says she's afraid. How do you know she is?

Make a guess: The text never says she's afraid. How do you know she is?

Reading skills · explicit and implicit informationScreen 1

Stated or suggested

  • Explicit information is stated on the page: you can point to it. She arrived at ten past nine.
  • Implicit information is suggested: you work it out from clues. She kept her coat on suggests she isn't planning to stay.
  • Working out what is suggested is inference. Every inference must be backed by evidence from the text.
  • Both skills are tested early in your paper, usually on a set group of lines.

Reading skills · explicit and implicit informationScreen 5

Making an inference that earns credit

  • Use a chain: detail → what it suggests → why. She did not take her coat off → she isn't settling in → she may need to leave fast.
  • Don't just repeat the text: she keeps her coat on is retrieval, not inference.
  • Don't overreach: nothing in the text says she has been burgled before.
  • Stay in the lines the question names. Evidence from elsewhere earns nothing.
  • When a question says in your own words, explain the idea; don't copy the phrase.

Mission checkScreen 9

?The text never says she's afraid. How do you know she is?

You know because the details imply it. She keeps her coat on, grips a key like a weapon, loses count and creeps away: each is evidence, and inference means saying what the evidence suggests and why.

Mission 2

Two texts, a few lines. How do you sum them up without copying?

Make a guess: Two texts, a few lines. How do you sum them up without copying?

Language · summary and synthesisScreen 1

Summary and synthesis

  • Summary: pick out the main points that answer the question, from one text.
  • Synthesis: bring two texts together on one focus, showing what they share or how they differ.
  • Both reward selection (only what fits the focus) and inference (what the details suggest).
  • Use your own words, with brief quotations as evidence. This is not language analysis.
  • Your Q2(a) summary uses one text (Text B): at most 120 words, entirely in your own words.

Language · summary and synthesisScreen 4

Select, infer, reword

Task: What do both writers find at the lake?

  • Select: underline only details about what they find: beauty, quiet, air.
  • Infer: what do the details suggest? a dream of beauty → Bird is overwhelmed.
  • Reword: quiet could feel physical → the stillness is almost a shock.
  • Link: Both…, whereas…, with one or two brief quotations.

  • Paper 1, Q2(a) (15 marks: 10 reading, 5 writing): a selective summary of Text B in continuous writing, no more than 120 words, in your own words. Q2(b) (5 marks) asks about attitudes and opinions.
Write itScreen 8
?Synthesise in a few lines.

Using both extracts, write 2–4 sentences on how the two writers' experiences of the lake differ. Use your own words and at most two short quotations.

Model answer

The writers differ most in how they share the lake. Bird seems to have it to herself: the only sign of other people is the distant ring of a woodcutter's axe, which adds to the peace. Hale's quiet is broken by a hundred tourists, and he has to walk away from the car park to feel the lake is yours again. Bird also delights in the facts, while Hale thinks they miss what matters.

Check your own answer

Mission checkScreen 9

?Two texts, a few lines. How do you sum them up without copying?

Select, infer, reword. Pick only the details that answer the focus, say what they suggest, and write them in your own words, linking the two texts with words like both and whereas. Brief quotations are evidence; long copied lines are not a summary.

Mission 3

Slim, skinny, scrawny: same body. Why three different verdicts?

Make a guess: Slim, skinny, scrawny: same body. Why three different verdicts?

Reading skills · word choiceScreen 1

Meaning and associations

  • Denotation: a word's dictionary meaning. Slim, skinny and scrawny all denote "thin".
  • Connotation: the associations and feelings a word carries. Slim sounds approving; skinny is more casual and can criticise; scrawny suggests weakness or poor health.
  • Writers choose words for their connotations, so a single word can steer your judgement.
Reading skills · word choiceScreen 2

Where the judgement hides

  • Verbs show how something acts: a waiter who slaps down a cup is ruder than one who puts it down.
  • Adjectives and adverbs describe; nouns name, and can judge too (a shack vs a cottage).
  • A semantic field is a group of words linked by one topic, such as decay or war.
  • Zoom in: name the word or its class, give its connotations, then say how it shapes the reader's view.

Mission 4

The fog 'crept'. Fog can't creep. Why does the writer say it did?

Make a guess: The fog 'crept'. Fog can't creep. Why does the writer say it did?

Reading skills · imageryScreen 1

Saying one thing in terms of another

  • Simile: compares two things using like or as. The lake was like a sheet of tin.
  • Metaphor: says one thing is another. The lake was a sheet of tin.
  • Personification: gives something non-human human actions or feelings. The wind howled at the door.
  • Extended metaphor: one comparison developed across several phrases or sentences.
  • Each one invites you to see a thing in a new light.
Reading skills · imageryScreen 2

The senses, and how to read an image

  • Sensory imagery appeals to sight, sound, touch, taste or smell.
  • Pathetic fallacy: giving weather or nature human feelings, often to reflect a character's mood.
  • Read any image in three steps: what is compared to what → what they share → what that makes you feel or think.
  • The streetlights were tired eyes → lights and eyes both glow and droop → the street seems weary and watchful.

Mission checkScreen 9

?The fog 'crept'. Fog can't creep. Why does the writer say it did?

"Crept" is personification: the fog is given a living creature's sneaking movement. It makes the fog seem sly, deliberate and threatening, so the reader feels uneasy before anything happens.

Mission 5

One city, two writers, no feelings stated. How do you spot attitude?

Make a guess: One city, two writers, no feelings stated. How do you spot attitude?

Language · viewpoint, attitude and toneScreen 1

Viewpoint, attitude, tone

  • Viewpoint: the writer's position or opinion on the subject.
  • Attitude: how the writer feels about it (admiring, uneasy, scornful…).
  • Tone: how the writing sounds as a result (warm, weary, mocking…).
  • Perspective: where the writer stands: their time, place and experience.
  • Writers rarely state an attitude. You infer it from evidence, then name it precisely: uneasy beats negative.

Mission checkScreen 9

?One city, two writers, no feelings stated. How do you spot attitude?

You spot it in the writer's choices. Dickens notices furtive strangers and endless streets, so his tone is uneasy and weary; the modern writer notices friendly faces and glittering bridges, so hers is warm. Name the attitude precisely, then prove it with the words and patterns that create it.

Mission 6

Same photo, charity advert and news report. Why such different words?

Make a guess: Same photo, charity advert and news report. Why such different words?

Language · purpose, audience and formScreen 1

Purpose, audience, form

  • Purpose: what the text wants to do: inform, explain, argue, persuade, advise, entertain. Many texts have more than one.
  • Audience: who it's for: age, knowledge, interests, attitude, and how the text speaks to them.
  • Form: the type of text (article, advert, leaflet, letter, speech, blog, report, review) and its conventions.
  • Ask: who is this for, what does it want them to think, feel or do, and how does its form shape it?

Mission checkScreen 9

?Same photo, charity advert and news report. Why such different words?

Because they want different things. The news report's purpose is to inform a general reader, so it uses facts, figures, the past tense and an official quotation. The charity advert's purpose is to persuade readers to give, so it tells one dog's story, speaks to you directly and ends with a clear way to act.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Explicit information is stated; implicit information is suggested and must be inferred.
  • A creditworthy inference links a detail to what it suggests and why, using only the lines the question names.
  • When told to use your own words, explain the idea rather than copying it.
  • Summary selects the main points from one text; synthesis brings two texts together on one focus.
  • Select only what fits, infer what the details suggest, reword, then link with both, whereas, unlike.
  • Q2(a): one text, no more than 120 words, all in your own words.
  • Denotation is a word's dictionary meaning; connotation is the associations it carries.
  • Verbs, nouns, adjectives and adverbs can all carry a writer's judgement; groups of linked words form a semantic field.
  • Strong answers zoom in on a word, give its connotations and link them to the effect on the reader.
  • Simile (like/as), metaphor (is), personification (human actions or feelings) and extended metaphor all describe one thing in terms of another.
  • Sensory imagery appeals to the five senses; pathetic fallacy gives weather or nature human feelings.
  • Read an image in three steps: what is compared, what they share, what it makes the reader feel or think.
  • Viewpoint is the writer's position; attitude is how they feel; tone is how the writing sounds. Perspective is where they stand.
  • Infer attitude from what the writer notices, word choice, imagery and patterns such as repetition, then name it precisely.
  • Q2(b) asks you to explain attitudes and opinions from evidence in Text B.
  • Purpose (what a text wants to do), audience (who it's for) and form (its type and conventions) shape every choice a writer makes.
  • Link each choice to its effect on the reader and to the purpose: avoid lists of features.
  • For media texts, comment on presentational features too: image, layout, colour, font and caption.
  • The same skills work in reverse when you write for a set audience, purpose and form.
