Mission 1

What is the Synoptic Problem?

  • Matthew, Mark and Luke are the Synoptic Gospels: they can be "seen together" (Greek synopsis) in parallel columns.
  • They share much of the same content, often in the same order, sometimes in the same wording in Greek.
  • Yet each also differs: material found in only one, changes of wording, order and emphasis.
  • The Synoptic Problem: what literary relationship explains both the agreements and the differences?
  • Source criticism studies the written sources behind a text.
  • John is not counted: it has a different order, style and much different content.
Mission 1Read the source

An aside in three Gospels

> "But that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins"—he said to the paralytic— (Mark 2:10, WEBBE)

> "But that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins—" (then he said to the paralytic) (Matthew 9:6, WEBBE)

> "But that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins," he said to the paralysed man (Luke 5:24, WEBBE)

  • All three break off Jesus' sentence at the same point with the same narrator's aside.

Q1 Why is the shared aside hard to explain by shared memory alone?

Show answer

An aside is the writer's own construction; independent writers would hardly break the sentence identically.

Q2 What does it suggest?

Show answer

A literary link: one writer copied another, or all used a common written source.

Mission 1

Triple, double, special

  • Triple tradition: in all three (e.g. the healing of the paralysed man, the feeding of the five thousand). Streeter: about 90% of Mark's content is in Matthew, over half in Luke.
  • Double tradition: in Matthew and Luke but not Mark: about 220–235 verses, mostly sayings (the Lord's Prayer, the Beatitudes, John the Baptist's preaching).
  • Special material: only in one:
  • M (Matthew): the magi, parable of the sheep and goats.
  • L (Luke): the shepherds, the Good Samaritan, the Lost Son.
  • Mark has very little of his own (e.g. the blind man of Bethsaida, 8:22–26).

Mission 1

Why Mark first?

  • Content: almost all of Mark is in Matthew or Luke. Why would a later writer shorten Matthew and leave out the birth, the Sermon on the Mount and the Lord's Prayer?
  • Order: Matthew and Luke follow Mark's order; where one departs, the other usually keeps it. They rarely agree together against Mark.
  • Wording: Matthew and Luke often agree with Mark, seldom with each other against him.
  • Style: Mark's rougher Greek and Aramaic words are smoothed or dropped by the others.
  • Harder readings: "He could do no mighty work there" (Mark 6:5) becomes "He didn't do many mighty works there because of their unbelief" (Matthew 13:58). A writer is more likely to soften a difficult phrase than to create one.
  • Markan priority is held by most scholars today.
Mission 1

Q: the lost sayings source

  • If Matthew and Luke did not copy each other, the double tradition needs a source: Q (German Quelle, "source").
  • Mostly sayings, little narrative; no passion story.
  • Read the source:

> "You offspring of vipers, who warned you to flee from the wrath to come?" (Matthew 3:7, WEBBE)

> "You offspring of vipers, who warned you to flee from the wrath to come?" (Luke 3:7, WEBBE)

  • Identical words, absent from Mark.
  • No manuscript of Q survives. But the Gospel of Thomas (found at Nag Hammadi, 1945) shows that sayings collections existed.
  • Matthew and Luke place Q sayings differently (Matthew gathers them into sermons; Luke scatters them), which suggests independent use.
Mission 1

Two sources, then four

  • Two-source hypothesis: Matthew and Luke each used Mark and Q, independently. Proposed by C. H. Weisse (1838) and H. J. Holtzmann (1863).
  • Four-source hypothesis: B. H. Streeter (1874–1937), The Four Gospels: A Study of Origins (1924): Mark, Q, M and L.
  • Streeter linked each to a church centre: Mark with Rome, Q with Antioch, M with Jerusalem, L with Caesarea.
  • He treated M and L as sources, not just leftover material; many scholars now see some of M and L as the evangelists' own writing or oral tradition.
Mission 1

Other solutions

  • Augustinian: Matthew, then Mark, then Luke (Augustine, Harmony of the Gospels, c. 400): the traditional order.
  • Griesbach / Two-Gospel hypothesis: Matthew first, Luke used Matthew, Mark combined both (J. J. Griesbach, 1789; revived by W. R. Farmer, The Synoptic Problem, 1964).
  • Farrer hypothesis: Mark first, Matthew used Mark, Luke used both: no Q needed (Austin Farrer, "On Dispensing with Q", 1955; Mark Goodacre, The Case Against Q, 2002).
  • The Farrer case rests on the minor agreements: places where Matthew and Luke agree against Mark in triple-tradition passages, e.g. both add "Who … struck you?" to the mocking (Matthew 26:68; Luke 22:64; not in Mark 14:65).

Mission 1Evaluate

Is the two-source hypothesis the best solution?

  • Claim: The two-source hypothesis is the best explanation of the Synoptic Problem.
  • For: Explains Markan order and wording, Mark's harder readings, and the double tradition.
  • For: Matthew and Luke use Q sayings in different places and forms, which fits independent use.
  • Against: Q is hypothetical: no manuscript and no ancient mention.
  • Against: The minor agreements suggest Luke knew Matthew (Farrer, Goodacre).
  • Against: Literary models may underrate oral tradition: memory and performance could explain some agreements and differences (e.g. James Dunn, Jesus Remembered, 2003).
  • Judgement criteria: Simplicity (fewer lost sources) or fit with all the data? How much weight do the minor agreements bear? Does the answer change how we read each Gospel?

Mission 2

Form criticism

  • German Formgeschichte, "history of forms".
  • K. L. Schmidt (1919): Mark's framework of times and places is mostly editorial; the Gospel is made of separate units (pericopae) strung together.
  • Martin Dibelius, Die Formgeschichte des Evangeliums (1919), and Rudolf Bultmann (1884–1976), The History of the Synoptic Tradition (1921), classified the units by form:
  • Pronouncement stories (Bultmann: apophthegms; Dibelius: paradigms): a short scene leading to a saying.
  • Miracle stories: problem → Jesus acts → proof → reaction.
  • Sayings and parables; legends and historical stories.
  • Each form had a Sitz im Leben, a "setting in life" in the early Church: preaching, worship, controversy, teaching.
  • Bultmann judged that many units were shaped or created by the communities. Vincent Taylor (1933) and others thought eyewitnesses limited this.
Mission 2

Redaction criticism

  • German Redaktionsgeschichte: the evangelists as editors and theologians, not just collectors.
  • Method: compare a Gospel with its source (usually Mark), and ask why each change was made.
  • Günther Bornkamm, "The Stilling of the Storm in Matthew" (1948): Matthew puts the storm after sayings on following Jesus; "his disciples followed him" into the boat (8:23); Mark's "Teacher, don't you care that we are dying?" (Mark 4:38) becomes a prayer: "Save us, Lord!" (Matthew 8:25). The boat becomes a picture of the Church.
  • Hans Conzelmann, The Theology of St Luke (1954): Luke's Salvation History.
  • Willi Marxsen, Mark the Evangelist (1956), named the method.
  • Limits: depends on knowing the sources (see Mission 1); may read too much into small changes.
  • A third approach, narrative criticism, reads a Gospel in its final form as one story, with plot, characters and setting.
Mission 2Read the source

Peter's confession, three ways

  • Jesus asks, "But who do you say that I am?" (Mark 8:29, WEBBE). Peter answers:
  • Mark 8:29: "You are the Christ."
  • Luke 9:20: "The Christ of God."

> "You are the Christ, the Son of the living God." (Matthew 16:16, WEBBE)

  • Matthew adds "Son of the living God" and Jesus' blessing: "on this rock I will build my assembly" (Matthew 16:18). Focus: Jesus' divine sonship and the Church.
  • Mark follows with Peter rebuking Jesus and being called "Satan" (8:32–33); Luke leaves the rebuke out.

Q1 What does Matthew's addition suggest about his concerns?

Show answer

Jesus' identity as Son of God, and Peter's role in the Church.

Q2 What might Luke's omission of the rebuke show?

Show answer

A gentler portrait of the apostles, who lead the Church in Acts.

Mission 2

Narrative criticism

  • Reads each Gospel as a unified story in its final form, not as layers of sources.
  • Tools from literary study: narrator and point of view; plot (conflict, turning points, climax); characters (Jesus, disciples, authorities, crowds); setting (places, times); the implied reader whom the story shapes.
  • David Rhoads and Donald Michie, Mark as Story (1982); R. Alan Culpepper, Anatomy of the Fourth Gospel (1983); Robert Tannehill, The Narrative Unity of Luke–Acts (1986, 1990).
  • Question it asks: what does this story do to its reader?
  • It sets aside the questions "Which source?" and "Did it happen like this?": it can be used by sceptics and believers alike.
Mission 2

Reading Luke as a story

  • Plot: "he intently set his face to go to Jerusalem" (Luke 9:51): a ten-chapter journey drives the plot to the cross.
  • Frame: the Gospel begins and ends in the Temple (1:9; 24:53).
  • Characters: the Pharisees often oppose Jesus, yet some warn him (13:31) and dine with him (7:36); outsiders (a Samaritan, a tax collector) become models.
  • The reader knows more: from chapters 1–2 the reader knows Jesus is Son of God; characters in the story are still finding out. This dramatic irony draws the reader in.
  • Tannehill: Luke and Acts form one plot: God's purpose, announced in the infancy narrative, unfolds to Rome.
Quick check

Mission 2Diversity

How Christians respond to criticism

  • Catholic: Pius XII, Divino Afflante Spiritu (1943), encouraged study of literary forms; Vatican II, Dei Verbum 12 (1965), asks readers to attend to the writers' "literary forms"; the Pontifical Biblical Commission (1993) called the historical-critical method indispensable, while warning against reducing faith to it.
  • Mainline and liberal Protestants: largely accept source, form and redaction criticism.
  • Conservative evangelicals: use the methods with caution; stress eyewitness memory (e.g. Richard Bauckham, Jesus and the Eyewitnesses, 2006; Birger Gerhardsson, Memory and Manuscript, 1961).
  • Fundamentalists: often reject critical methods as threatening the Bible's inerrancy.
  • Narrative criticism is widely welcomed across these groups because it reads the text as given.
Mission 2Evaluate

How much do these methods help?

  • Claim: Biblical criticism is essential for understanding the Synoptic Gospels.
  • For: Shows each evangelist's theology (redaction); the life of the early Church (form); the text's history (textual criticism).
  • For: Narrative criticism helps readers see each Gospel's story and its effect, as first hearers would.
  • Against: Form criticism's assumption of creative communities ignores eyewitnesses and the short time span; its "laws" of tradition have been questioned (E. P. Sanders, The Tendencies of the Synoptic Tradition, 1969).
  • Against: Methods can fragment the text and seem to leave little for faith; narrative criticism may ignore history altogether.
  • Judgement criteria: Understanding for whom: historian, preacher, believer? Do the methods complement each other? Can critical study and faith in inspiration coexist?
  • Spec: "the importance of biblical criticism … with reference to narrative criticism".

Mission 3

"Who do you say that I am?"

  • At the centre of Mark's Gospel Jesus asks his disciples who he is (Mark 8:27–30).
  • Peter: "You are the Christ." (Mark 8:29). Matthew adds "the Son of the living God" (Matthew 16:16); Luke has "The Christ of God" (Luke 9:20).
  • Jesus tells them to tell no one, then speaks of himself as the Son of Man who "must suffer" (Mark 8:31).
  • Three titles meet in one scene: Messiah (Christ), Son of God, Son of Man.
  • A Christological title is a name that says who Jesus is and what he came to do.
  • Each title had a meaning before Jesus. Each evangelist uses it in his own way.
  • Your spec names two: Son of Man and Son of God.
Mission 3Read the source

"My son" in the Old Testament

"Son of God" did not mean "divine" to most Jews of Jesus' day. It named someone in a close, chosen relationship with God.

> "Israel is my son, my firstborn" (Exodus 4:22, WEBBE)

> "I will be his father, and he will be my son." (2 Samuel 7:14, WEBBE)

> "You are my son. Today I have become your father." (Psalm 2:7, WEBBE)

  • Angels are "sons of God" too (Job 1:6).

Q1 Who is God's "son" in each of the three quotations?

Show answer

The people Israel; David's royal heir; the king on the day of his enthronement.

Q2 Mark 1:11 has the voice at Jesus' baptism say, "You are my beloved Son". Which verse above does it echo, and what does that suggest?

Show answer

Psalm 2:7: Jesus is presented as the anointed king, God's chosen Son.

Mission 3

Son of God in each evangelist

  • Mark: "the Good News of Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (1:1; "Son of God" is missing from some early manuscripts).
  • In Mark only God (baptism 1:11; transfiguration 9:7) and demons (3:11) know it, until a Gentile centurion at the cross: "Truly this man was the Son of God!" (15:39). The title is revealed in his death.
  • Matthew: the disciples worship him as "truly the Son of God" after he walks on water (14:33); Peter's confession adds the title (16:16).
  • Luke: the title is linked to his conception by the Holy Spirit (1:35) and to the human race: the genealogy runs back to "Adam, the son of God" (3:38).
  • Jesus calls God "Abba, Father" in prayer (Mark 14:36): a relationship of trust and obedience.

Mission 3

Son of Man: the background

  • Hebrew ben adam, Aramaic bar enash: literally "son of a human", that is, a human being.
  • Psalm 8:4: "What is the son of man, that you care for him?": a frail human.
  • Ezekiel: God addresses the prophet as "Son of man" over 90 times (e.g. Ezekiel 2:1).
  • Daniel 7:13–14: in a vision, "one like a son of man" comes "with the clouds of the sky" to the Ancient of Days and is given "dominion, and glory, and a kingdom" for ever.
  • In Daniel the kingdom is then given to "the people of the saints of the Most High" (7:27): many scholars read the figure as representing God's faithful people, persecuted and then vindicated; others see an individual heavenly or messianic figure.
  • So the phrase can mean ordinary humanity or a heavenly figure of glory. Jesus' use could carry either, or both.
Mission 3

Three groups of sayings

  • In the Synoptics "Son of Man" is almost always on Jesus' own lips, and no one ever confesses him by it.
  • A common grouping (from Rudolf Bultmann, 1884–1976):
  • Earthly ministry: authority "to forgive sins" (Mark 2:10); "lord even of the Sabbath" (Mark 2:28); "no place to lay his head" (Luke 9:58).
  • Suffering: "must suffer many things … and be killed, and after three days rise again" (Mark 8:31; also 9:31; 10:33–34); "came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many" (10:45).
  • Future glory: "coming in clouds with great power and glory" (13:26); at his trial, "sitting at the right hand of Power" (14:62, joining Daniel 7:13 and Psalm 110:1).
  • Luke adds "came to seek and to save that which was lost" (19:10): the title serves his theme of salvation for outsiders.
  • Matthew has the Son of Man as judge of the nations (25:31).
Mission 3Diversity

What did "Son of Man" mean?

  • Geza Vermes (1924–2013), Jesus the Jew (1973): in Aramaic it could be a modest way of saying "I"; no title was intended.
  • Bultmann, Theology of the New Testament (1948–53): only the future sayings go back to Jesus, and in them he spoke of another figure (Mark 8:38 can be read that way); the Church later identified him with Jesus.
  • Morna Hooker (b. 1931), The Son of Man in Mark (1967): drawn from Daniel 7: the Son of Man has authority from God, is rejected and suffers, and is vindicated. All three groups belong together.
  • Many scholars add that the phrase was ambiguous enough to let Jesus speak about his mission without the political weight of "Messiah".
Quick check
?Which saying belongs to the group in which the Son of Man suffers?
Mission 3Evaluate

Which title best expresses who Jesus is in the Synoptics?

  • Claim: "Son of Man" says more about Jesus in the Synoptic Gospels than "Son of God".
  • For: It is Jesus' own preferred self-designation; it holds together his authority, his suffering and his future glory.
  • For: It drew on Daniel 7 without the political danger of "Messiah" or the misunderstanding of "Son of God".
  • Against: Its meaning is disputed (Vermes: just "I"); it was never used in the early Church's confessions of faith.
  • Against: "Son of God" frames Mark (1:1; 15:39) and marks the turning points (baptism, transfiguration, trial, cross).
  • Against: Each evangelist has his own emphasis: Matthew stresses Jesus as Son of David and Son of God; Luke stresses Saviour and Lord.
  • Judgement criteria: Which title is closest to Jesus' own words? Which one shapes each Gospel's structure? Should the titles be read in their first-century or later Christian meaning?
  • Your spec asks how each evangelist presents the titles: compare at least two Gospels.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The Synoptic Problem asks what literary relationship explains the agreements and differences of Matthew, Mark and Luke; source criticism studies their sources.
  • Most scholars hold Markan priority; the two-source hypothesis adds Q, and Streeter's four-source hypothesis adds M and L (with proto-Luke).
  • Alternatives: Augustinian, Griesbach (Two-Gospel) and Farrer (no Q, based on the minor agreements).

Mission 2

  • Redaction criticism (Bornkamm, Conzelmann, Marxsen) treats the evangelists as theologians, shown by how they change their sources.
  • Narrative criticism (Rhoads and Michie, Culpepper, Tannehill) reads each Gospel as a unified story: plot, characters, setting, reader.

Mission 3

  • Messiah (anointed), Son of God (in a chosen relationship with God) and Son of Man (a human being; Daniel's figure of glory) all had Old Testament meanings before Jesus.
  • Mark reveals Jesus as Son of God at the cross; Matthew stresses fulfilment; Luke links the titles to salvation for all.
  • Son of Man sayings fall into earthly, suffering and future groups; scholars (Vermes, Bultmann, Hooker, Dodd, Brown) disagree about what Jesus meant by them.
