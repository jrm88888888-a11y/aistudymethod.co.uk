Macbeth · story and tragic shapeScreen 1

The play in five acts

Macbeth was written c.1606 and first printed in the First Folio of 1623. Shakespeare took the story from Holinshed's Chronicles (1587).

ActWhat happens
1Macbeth wins a battle for King Duncan. Three witches greet him as future king. Lady Macbeth pushes him to murder Duncan, a guest in their castle.
2Macbeth kills Duncan in his sleep. Duncan's sons flee. Macbeth is crowned at Scone.
3Macbeth has his friend Banquo murdered; Banquo's son Fleance escapes. Banquo's ghost appears at a feast.
4The witches show Macbeth apparitions. He has Macduff's wife and children killed. Malcolm and Macduff gather an army in England.
5Lady Macbeth sleepwalks, then dies. The army reaches Dunsinane. Macduff kills Macbeth; Malcolm will be crowned.

Macbeth · story and tragic shapeScreen 5

The shape of a tragedy

  • A tragedy follows a great figure from high status to death, through his own choices.
  • Tragic hero: admired at the start (brave, loyal, "valiant").
  • Fatal flaw (hamartia): the weakness that drives the fall. Many readers name Macbeth's "Vaulting ambition" (Act 1 Scene 7).
  • Turning point: the choice that can't be undone, Duncan's murder (Act 2 Scene 2).
  • Downfall and restored order: Macbeth dies; Malcolm, the rightful heir, takes the throne.
  • Readers disagree on how far the witches and Lady Macbeth share the blame.

Macbeth · story and tragic shapeScreen 7

How the play ends

  • Macduff kills Macbeth and enters with his head (Act 5 Scene 8). The hero who fixed a rebel's head "upon our battlements" ends the same way.
  • Malcolm sums up the reign as the work of "this dead butcher, and his fiend-like queen,".
  • This circular structure (a traitor beheaded in Act 1 and Act 5) frames the play.
  • But Malcolm's verdict isn't the whole play: the audience has heard Macbeth's soliloquies and seen his guilt, so many feel pity as well as horror.

Mission 2

Macbeth argues himself out of murder. Why does he do it anyway?

Make a guess: Macbeth argues himself out of murder. Why does he do it anyway?

Macbeth · ambition and declineScreen 1

Ambition before anyone pushes

  • Macbeth thinks of murder within moments of the prophecy, in an aside (words the other characters don't hear): "My thought, whose murder yet is but fantastical," (Act 1 Scene 3).
  • He also hopes "chance may crown me / Without my stir.": he wants the crown without the crime.
  • When Duncan names Malcolm heir, Macbeth asks the stars to hide "my black and deep desires." (Act 1 Scene 4).
  • So the desire is his before Lady Macbeth reads his letter.

Macbeth · ambition and declineScreen 4

What tips the balance

  • He tells his wife: "We will proceed no further in this business:".
  • Lady Macbeth attacks his courage and his manhood: "When you durst do it, then you were a man;".
  • She answers his doubt "If we should fail?" with a plan to blame the grooms.
  • By the end of the scene he is "settled": "False face must hide what the false heart doth know."
  • One reading: she persuades him. Another: she gives him permission for what he already wants.

Macbeth · Lady Macbeth and genderScreen 1

What the play expects of men and women

  • Jacobean wives were expected to obey their husbands and to be gentle and nurturing.
  • In the play, being "a man" means courage in battle; Lady Macbeth stretches it to daring to kill.
  • Boy actors played the women: women didn't act on the public stage.
  • The witches blur gender too: "You should be women, / And yet your beards forbid me to interpret / That you are so." (Banquo)

Macbeth · Lady Macbeth and genderScreen 5

"Then you were a man": manhood as a weapon

  • Macbeth sets a limit: "I dare do all that may become a man; / Who dares do more is none." (Act 1 Scene 7)

Mission 4

The witches never tell Macbeth to kill. So are they to blame?

Make a guess: The witches never tell Macbeth to kill. So are they to blame?

Macbeth · the supernaturalScreen 1

Witches and a Jacobean audience

  • James VI of Scotland, who became James I of England in 1603, took part in the North Berwick witch trials (from 1590).
  • In Daemonologie (1597, reprinted 1603) he argued that witches were real servants of the Devil.
  • Not everyone agreed: Reginald Scot's The Discoverie of Witchcraft (1584) was sceptical, and James's book argues against such doubters.
  • So the witches could terrify; whether they cause anything is the play's question.

Macbeth · the supernaturalScreen 4

Equivocation

  • Equivocation: wording a statement with a double meaning, so that it misleads without being a plain lie.
  • After the Gunpowder Plot (November 1605), the Jesuit Henry Garnet was condemned and executed; he was mocked for defending equivocation.
  • Macbeth was probably written in 1606. The Porter jokes about "an equivocator, that could swear in both the scales against either scale" (Act 2 Scene 3).
  • Many critics read this as a nod to Garnet. The witches equivocate too.

?The witches never tell Macbeth to kill. So are they to blame?

Partly. The witches tell truths that win trust and promises that mislead (equivocation), and a Jacobean audience would see them as dangerous. But they never tell Macbeth to kill: Banquo hears the same prophecies and stays loyal, so the choices are Macbeth's.

Macbeth · guiltScreen 3

Visions only Macbeth sees

  • His rhetorical questions show a mind unsettled.
  • The dagger leads him on: "Thou marshall’st me the way that I was going;". A sign, or his own desire?

  • Productions differ: some show the ghost, some an empty stool.

Macbeth · kingship and orderScreen 1

A Scottish king on the English throne

  • James VI of Scotland became James I of England in 1603; Shakespeare's company became the King's Men.
  • James argued for the divine right of kings (1598): a king is chosen by God and answers only to God.
  • So regicide (killing a king) was a crime against God.
  • James was said to descend from Banquo. Holinshed's Banquo helps kill Duncan; Shakespeare's is loyal.

Macbeth · kingship and orderScreen 5

A sacrilege, and a tyrant

  • Macduff calls the murder "Most sacrilegious murder hath broke ope / The Lord’s anointed temple," (Act 2 Scene 3): the king's body is holy.
  • The Gunpowder Plot (1605) had tried to kill James; the play was probably written in 1606.
  • From Act 3 Scene 6 on, Macbeth is called "tyrant" again and again: an illegitimate, cruel ruler.
  • Angus: Macbeth's title hangs "like a giant’s robe / Upon a dwarfish thief." (Act 5 Scene 2).

Mission checkScreen 8

?Why does killing a king make horses eat each other?

Many in the audience believed a king was chosen by God (divine right), so killing one was an attack on God's whole order. Shakespeare shows that order breaking: day turns dark, an owl kills a falcon, horses eat each other. Order returns only when Malcolm, the rightful heir, is crowned.

Mission 7

Verse, prose, rhyme: why does Shakespeare change how people speak?

Make a guess: Verse, prose, rhyme: why does Shakespeare change how people speak?

Macbeth · verse and key linesScreen 1

Blank verse

  • Most of the play is blank verse: unrhymed lines of iambic pentameter.
  • An iamb is an unstressed then a stressed syllable (da-DUM); pentameter means five of them, about ten syllables a line.
  • Macbeth's first line is regular: so FOUL / and FAIR / a DAY / i HAVE / not SEEN.
  • Nobles and serious moments usually use verse. Breaks in the rhythm (short lines, pauses) are worth noticing.
Macbeth · verse and key linesScreen 2

  • A soliloquy is a speech spoken alone, revealing thoughts; an aside is spoken so that others on stage don't hear.

Macbeth · verse and key linesScreen 6

When the form breaks

  • After the murder, the Macbeths' verse splinters into tiny shared lines: "When?" / "Now." / "As I descended?" / "Ay." (Act 2 Scene 2). Panic is in the rhythm.
  • Lady Macbeth, a confident verse speaker, sleepwalks in prose (Act 5 Scene 1).
  • Couplets seal decisions: "It is concluded. Banquo, thy soul’s flight, / If it find heaven, must find it out tonight." (Act 3 Scene 1).
  • Short lines leave silence: "Signifying nothing." ends the soliloquy well short of ten syllables.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Verse, prose, rhyme: why does Shakespeare change how people speak?

Each form is a signal. Blank verse is the play's normal voice for nobles; the witches' short chants sound like spells; couplets seal decisions; and broken lines or prose show minds coming apart, as in Lady Macbeth's sleepwalking.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Macbeth (c.1606, first printed 1623) follows a tragic shape: an admired hero, a fatal choice (Duncan's murder in Act 2), a chain of crimes and a downfall, ending with order restored under Malcolm.
  • The play starts and ends with a traitor's head cut off; Macbeth is praised for violence that he later turns on his king.
  • Malcolm's verdict, "this dead butcher", is one view; the soliloquies let the audience feel more than horror.
  • Macbeth's ambition shows in asides before Lady Macbeth speaks to him; in Act 1 Scene 7 his conscience argues against the murder.
  • The riding metaphor ("Vaulting ambition, which o’erleaps itself") presents ambition as his only motive and a self-defeating one.
  • After the murder, thought shrinks and action speeds up: from a whole soliloquy of doubt to "firstlings of my heart" becoming "firstlings of my hand".
  • The play links manhood to daring and violence; Lady Macbeth uses it as a weapon ("then you were a man"), while Macduff offers a gentler idea of being a man.
  • Her invocation of the spirits (Act 1 Scene 5) shows a cruelty that has to be summoned, and a fear of remorse.
  • She is shut out from Macbeth's later crimes and breaks under guilt; Malcolm's "fiend-like queen" is one verdict, not the only one.
  • James I's Daemonologie (1597) treated witches as the Devil's servants, but sceptics like Reginald Scot (1584) doubted them: audiences weren't all believers.
  • Equivocation (double meaning meant to mislead) was linked with treason after the Gunpowder Plot (1605); the Porter jokes about it and the apparitions use it.
  • The witches tempt and mislead; Macbeth chooses. Banquo, who hears the same prophecies, is the contrast.
  • The dagger (Act 2 Scene 1) and Banquo's ghost (Act 3 Scene 4) are seen only by Macbeth; the play leaves their reality open, but both show his guilty mind.
  • Blood, water and sleep are motifs: "A little water clears us of this deed" in Act 2 is reversed by "Out, damned spot!" in Act 5.
  • Lady Macbeth's guilt grows and breaks her (verse to prose); Macbeth's fear numbs ("I have supp’d full with horrors").
  • James I (King of England from 1603, patron of Shakespeare's company) argued for the divine right of kings (1598): regicide was a crime against God.
  • After Duncan's murder, nature turns "unnatural": the falcon and owl symbolise a subject killing his king.
  • The play contrasts true kings (Duncan, Edward the Confessor, Malcolm) with the "tyrant" Macbeth. Context earns credit only when it is linked to the play.
  • Blank verse is unrhymed iambic pentameter (five da-DUMs); the witches chant in short, stress-first rhymed lines; prose is for comedy, low status or a breaking mind; couplets close scenes and seal decisions.
  • Soliloquies and asides let the audience hear what Macbeth hides; the "Tomorrow" soliloquy uses repetition and a theatre metaphor to show despair.
  • Closed book: learn the key lines on screen 8.

  • So foul and fair a day I have not seen. (Act 1 Scene 3 (Macbeth)) Regular iambic pentameter; echoes the witches.
  • Double, double, toil and trouble; (Act 4 Scene 1 (Witches)) Trochaic chant: the sound of a spell.
  • Hear it not, Duncan, for it is a knell / That summons thee to heaven or to hell. (Act 2 Scene 1 (Macbeth)) A couplet sealing the decision to kill.
  • Tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow, (Act 5 Scene 5 (Macbeth)) Repetition: time without meaning.
  • Life’s but a walking shadow; a poor player, (Act 5 Scene 5 (Macbeth)) Life as an empty performance.
  • Signifying nothing. (Act 5 Scene 5 (Macbeth)) A short final line that trails into silence.
