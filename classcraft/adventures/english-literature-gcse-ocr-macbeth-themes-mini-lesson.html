Macbeth

The play in brief

A brave soldier who becomes a tyrant

Macbeth is one of Shakespeare's shortest and darkest tragedies. Its hero is a Scottish general whose ambition, once awakened, drives him to murder and destroys him:

  • Returning from battle, Macbeth and Banquo meet three Witches who prophesy that Macbeth will become Thane of Cawdor and then king, and that Banquo's descendants will be kings.
  • Spurred by the prophecy and by Lady Macbeth, Macbeth murders King Duncan, a guest in his castle, and seizes the throne — an act of regicide.
  • Fearing the prophecy about Banquo, he has him killed, but is haunted by Banquo's ghost. Growing tyrannical, he slaughters Macduff's family.
  • Consumed by guilt, Lady Macbeth sleepwalks and dies. Macduff — "not of woman born" — kills Macbeth, and Malcolm, Duncan's son, becomes king.

Genre: a tragedy — a great man with a fatal flaw (here, "vaulting ambition") falls from greatness. Order is broken by his crime and only restored at the end.

Theme · Ambition

"Vaulting ambition"

The engine of the tragedy is ambition. Macbeth desires the crown so fiercely that he over-reaches and destroys himself. He names the flaw himself:

"Vaulting ambition, which o'erleaps itself
And falls on th' other —"
  • Method: the metaphor of a rider vaulting onto a horse and leaping too far, falling on the other side.
  • Effect: it presents ambition as self-defeating — it over-reaches and causes its own ruin, foreshadowing Macbeth's fall.

Context link: unchecked ambition that topples the natural order would alarm a Jacobean audience who believed a subject's place was fixed by God — more on that shortly.

Worked model · annotation

Annotating a quotation

Here is a famous line from Lady Macbeth, annotated the way you'd mark up a quotation in the exam:

"…look like th' innocent flower,
But be the serpent under't."
  • "innocent flower" — imagery of purity and beauty: a disguise, the harmless face to show the world.
  • "serpent under't" — the snake: hidden danger and evil, with echoes of the serpent in Eden and betrayal.
  • The contrast (flower vs serpent) dramatises appearance vs reality — a mask over murderous intent.
  • Effect: the reader sees how the Macbeths weaponise deceit, hiding a "false heart" behind a "false face".

Notice the pattern: each note is quote → method → effect. That is exactly the sentence shape your essay paragraphs need.

Theme · Guilt

Blood that will not wash away

After the murder, guilt torments both Macbeths. Shakespeare makes it physical, using the recurring motif of blood and water:

  • Macbeth: "Will all great Neptune's ocean wash this blood clean from my hand?" — hyperbole: even an entire ocean could not cleanse his guilt.
  • Lady Macbeth (sleepwalking, later): "Out, damned spot! out, I say!" — guilt made physical; the once-ruthless woman cannot rub away an imagined bloodstain.

Notice the reversal: Lady Macbeth once said "A little water clears us of this deed," but guilt returns to overwhelm her, driving her to madness and death.

Effect: the blood that "will not out" shows guilt as inescapable — a moral stain that no action can remove. Conscience destroys them both.

Theme · The supernatural

Witches, daggers and ghosts

The supernatural saturates the play, creating dread and disorder:

"Is this a dagger which I see before me…" — in this soliloquy, Macbeth hallucinates a dagger leading him to Duncan. The supernatural and his guilty conscience blur together.

Theme · Kingship

Good king vs tyrant

Shakespeare contrasts two models of rule to explore kingship and the divine right of kings:

Duncan vs Macbeth

Duncan — gracious, virtuous, generous; his rule is natural and God-given. His murder is regicide: a crime against God and the natural order.

Macbeth — a tyrant who takes the throne by blood, ruling through fear and murder. Scotland "bleeds" under him until Malcolm restores order.

Why it matters: after Duncan's death, unnatural events erupt — darkness at noon, horses eating each other — signalling that killing the rightful king has overturned the natural order itself.

Jacobean context · AO3

Written for King James I (c.1606)

Understanding the play's original moment sharpens your analysis. Shakespeare wrote Macbeth around 1606 for King James I (James VI of Scotland), patron of Shakespeare's company, the King's Men:

  • Descent from Banquo: James believed he was descended from Banquo, so Banquo is shown as noble and his line as future kings — flattering the king.
  • Divine right of kings: James held that a king is God's deputy on earth, so murdering a king is a sin against God and nature — making Duncan's death especially horrifying.
  • Witchcraft: James was fascinated by it and wrote Daemonologie; the Witches tap real contemporary fears.
  • The Gunpowder Plot (1605): a Catholic plot to blow up James and Parliament made the horror of killing a king intensely topical. The Porter's talk of an "equivocator" alludes to the trial of the Jesuit Henry Garnet.
Evidence done well

Embed quotes — keep them short

Weave short quotations into your own sentence rather than dropping in a whole line. Short + embedded = fluent and precise:

Clumsy vs embedded

❌ "Macbeth feels guilty. The quote is: 'Will all great Neptune's ocean wash this blood clean from my hand?'"

✔ "Macbeth's fear that not even 'Neptune's ocean' could 'wash this blood clean' shows a guilt so vast it can never be undone."

Misconception: long quotations don't earn more marks — they waste time and bury your analysis. Quote the few words you'll actually analyse.

Context & AO3

Context serves the analysis

Context can enrich your points — but only if it explains a choice in the text. It must be woven in, not bolted on as a history lecture.

Bolted-on vs woven-in

❌ "The play was written for James I. He believed in the divine right of kings. Now back to Macbeth."

✔ "Because a Jacobean audience saw the king as God's deputy, Duncan's murder feels not just criminal but sacrilegious, which is why the natural world itself convulses after it."

Misconception: context is not a paragraph you paste in. One relevant clause that illuminates a method or theme beats a page of dates.

The ending

"Sound and fury, signifying nothing"

By the final act, Macbeth is isolated and hollow. Hearing of Lady Macbeth's death, he reflects on the emptiness of life:

"…it is a tale
Told by an idiot, full of sound and fury,
Signifying nothing."
  • Method: a metaphor for life as a meaningless, badly-told story — bleak nihilism.
  • Effect: ambition has cost Macbeth everything; his crimes have left him despairing and empty.

Restoration: Macduff kills Macbeth and Malcolm, the rightful heir, becomes king — order is restored, the tragic pattern complete.

Worked model · a paragraph

A Macbeth paragraph in action

Watch how one paragraph makes a point, embeds a short quote, analyses method & effect, and weaves in context:

Model paragraph

"Shakespeare presents guilt as a stain that cannot be removed. When Macbeth despairs that not even 'Neptune's ocean' could 'wash this blood clean', the hyperbole of an entire ocean conveys a conscience so overwhelmed that no act could ever cleanse it. This blood motif returns in Lady Macbeth's 'Out, damned spot!', so that the couple who thought 'a little water' would clear them are both destroyed by the very guilt they dismissed. For a Jacobean audience, who saw the murder of God's anointed king as a sacrilege, this inescapable guilt would feel like divine justice."

Spot the moves: point first, embedded short quotes, method → effect, a linked second quotation, and context woven in at the end.

Avoid these traps

The five marks-killers

  • Feature-spotting: naming techniques without explaining their effect.
  • Retelling the plot: narrating events instead of arguing an answer to the question.
  • Bolted-on context: a history block on James I that doesn't serve a point.
  • Fat quotes: long copied lines instead of short, embedded ones.
  • Vague terms: "it makes it interesting" — always say how and why it affects the audience.

Fix them all with one habit: every sentence should make a point, prove it with a short quote, or explain a method's effect — and, where it fits, weave in context.
