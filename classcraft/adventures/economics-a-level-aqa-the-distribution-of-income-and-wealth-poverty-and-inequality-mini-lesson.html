Mission 1

Mission 1: If buyers and sellers both gain from a trade, how can a free market still produce the wrong amount?

Make a guess: If buyers and sellers both gain from a trade, how can a free market still produce the wrong amount?

Mission 1

What a free market gets right

  • Each point on the demand curve shows the marginal benefit (MB) of one more unit to buyers.
  • Each point on the supply curve shows the marginal cost (MC) of one more unit to sellers.
  • At competitive equilibrium, P = MB = MC.
  • Consumer surplus: what buyers would pay minus what they do pay.
  • Producer surplus: what sellers receive minus the least they would accept.
  • Consumer + producer surplus = community surplus.
  • If there is no market failure, equilibrium makes this total as large as possible: allocative efficiency.
Mission 1

Market equilibrium vs the social optimum

  • Buyers and sellers weigh only their private benefits and costs: market equilibrium is where MPB = MPC.
  • Society counts all benefits and costs: the social optimum is where MSB = MSC.
  • Allocative efficiency: resources make the goods society values most; the value of the last unit equals its full cost, MSB = MSC (for a single firm: P = MC).
  • No outside effects: MPB = MSB and MPC = MSC, so the market reaches the social optimum.
  • Outside effects: the market equilibrium and the social optimum are at different quantities.
  • Say: "the market equilibrium is not the socially optimum position" — not: "the market is not in equilibrium". A market can be in equilibrium and still be inefficient.

Mission 1Graph lab

Too much, too little

Illustrative data: MSB = 120 − Q and MSC = 20 + Q. The social optimum is Q = 50.

  • Takeaway: any quantity other than MSB = MSC creates a welfare loss, whether output is too high or too low.
Mission 1

Worked example: measuring the welfare loss

  • Illustrative data: MSB = 120 − Q; MSC = 20 + Q (price in $, quantity in units per week).
  • Optimum: 120 − Q = 20 + Q, so Q = 50, value 70.
  • Output is 65 instead. At Q = 65: MSC = 20 + 65 = 85; MSB = 120 − 65 = 55.
  • Each extra unit from 50 to 65 costs society more than it is worth.
  • Welfare loss = triangle between MSC and MSB, from Q = 50 to 65.
  • Area = ½ × base × height = ½ × (65 − 50) × (85 − 55) = ½ × 15 × 30 = 225.
  • Total surplus at the optimum = ½ × 50 × (120 − 20) = 2,500. At 65 it falls to 2,500 − 225 = 2,275.

Mission 1

What market failure means

  • Market failure: the price mechanism fails to allocate resources efficiently, so the market outcome is not the social optimum.
  • The result is too much or too little of a good, and a welfare loss.
  • Complete market failure: no market exists at all: a missing market (e.g. street lighting).
  • Partial market failure: a market exists, but at the wrong price or quantity (e.g. too much tobacco, too little vaccination).
Mission 1

Sources of market failure

SourceWhat goes wrongMission
Negative externalitiesCost to third parties: overproduction
Positive externalitiesBenefit to third parties: underproduction
Public goodsFree riders: often no market at all
  • Information gaps (asymmetric information): buyers or sellers misjudge value.
  • Market power (monopoly): output restricted, price above MC.
  • Inequality: the market may share income and wealth in a way society judges unfair.
Mission 1

Private, external, social

  • Externality: a cost or benefit to a third party, not paid for or received through the market.
  • Social cost = private cost + external cost. At the margin: MSC = MPC + MEC.
  • Social benefit = private benefit + external benefit. At the margin: MSB = MPB + MEB.
  • External cost from production (e.g. pollution): MSC above MPC, so the market overproduces.
  • External benefit from consumption (e.g. vaccination): MSB above MPB, so the market underproduces.
  • Either way: a welfare (deadweight) loss triangle that points to the social optimum.
Quick check
?In a region, a factory's smoke harms nearby farms' crops, and there is no way to charge the factory for this. Which statement is correct?
Mission 1Evaluate

Are markets good at allocating resources?

  • Is inequality a market failure? It depends on what "failure" means.
  • For: markets reward people by what they own and earn, so an unequal outcome can leave many unable to buy necessities; on this view the market has failed.
  • Against: some economists keep "market failure" for inefficiency; how much inequality is fair is a normative (value) judgement.
  • It depends on: how big the failure is, whether better information or property rights could fix it, and whether intervention would do better.
Mission check

?Illustrative data: a competitive market for bread has no externalities and settles at 1,000 loaves a day. The government pays bakers to raise output to 1,200 loaves a day. What happens to total surplus from bread?

With no market failure, equilibrium is already the efficient quantity. Moving away from it in either direction loses surplus.

Mission 2

Income is a flow, wealth is a stock

  • Income: a flow of money over a period, e.g. per year.
    • wages, rent, interest, dividends, profit, government benefits
  • Wealth: a stock of assets owned at a point in time, minus debts.
    • property, savings, shares, pension funds
  • They feed each other: wealth earns income (rent, interest, dividends); saved income builds wealth.
  • So inequality in wealth tends to create inequality in income, and the reverse.
  • Say: "wealth is a stock; income is a flow" — not: "wealthy" means "high income". A retired homeowner can be wealthy on a low income.
Quick check
?Which of these is a change in a household's wealth rather than its income?
Mission 2

Equality or equity?

  • Equality: everyone has the same: equal incomes, wealth or opportunities.
  • Equity: fairness. What is fair is a value judgement: a normative question.
  • Equality of outcome: the same result for all. Equality of opportunity: the same chances, results may differ.
  • Horizontal equity: people in the same position are treated the same.
  • Vertical equity: people in different positions are treated differently, e.g. higher incomes pay a higher share in tax.
  • Say: "equity means fairness" — not: "equity means equality". An equitable distribution need not be equal.
Mission 2

The Lorenz curve

  • Line up everyone from poorest to richest.
  • Horizontal axis: cumulative % of population. Vertical axis: cumulative % of income (or wealth).
  • Line of equality: the 45° line. The bottom 20% get 20%, the bottom 40% get 40%, and so on.
  • The Lorenz curve shows the actual shares. It sags below the line of equality.
  • Read it: a point at (40, 15) means the poorest 40% receive 15% of income.
  • The further the curve from the line of equality, the more unequal the distribution.
Mission 2

The Gini coefficient

  • Gini coefficient = A ÷ (A + B).
    • A: area between the line of equality and the Lorenz curve.
    • B: area under the Lorenz curve.
  • 0 = perfect equality. 1 = perfect inequality (one person has everything). Often shown as 0–100.
  • You need to interpret the Gini coefficient, not calculate it.
  • UK disposable household income: Gini 32.9 on the 0–100 scale (financial year ending 2024, ONS).
  • Great Britain household wealth: Gini about 0.6, or 60 on the 0–100 scale (2018–2020, ONS). The richest 10% of households held 43% of all wealth; the poorest half held 9%.
  • World Bank Gini estimates (0–100 scale): South Africa 63.0 (2014); Brazil 48.9 (2020); Slovak Republic 23.2 (2019).
  • Countries survey in different years, so compare with care.
  • Limits: two different Lorenz curves can give the same Gini; a Gini does not show where the inequality lies, or how rich the country is.

  • Takeaway: shifting income from richer to poorer groups pulls the Lorenz curve towards the line of equality; here the Gini falls from 0.38 to 0.30.

Mission 2

Why is income unequal?

  • Human capital: education and skills raise productivity and pay.
  • Ownership of wealth: land, property and shares earn rent, interest and dividends; inheritance passes them on.
  • Inequality of opportunity: family background affects schooling, contacts and health.
  • Discrimination by gender, ethnicity and others.
  • Power: bargaining power of unions, employers with monopsony power, top executives.
  • Tax and benefit policy: progressive taxes and benefits narrow gaps; regressive taxes widen them.
  • Globalisation and technological change: raise the pay of scarce skills; some routine jobs move abroad or are automated.
Mission 2

Inequality as a market failure, and its effects

  • Markets allocate goods to those willing and able to pay. A market can be efficient and still leave some with very little.
  • So an unequal distribution can be seen as a market failure: the market fails to produce an equitable outcome. Whether it does is a value judgement.
  • Effects:
    • individuals: worse health, education and housing for those on low incomes
    • growth: big rewards can create incentives to work and invest; but very unequal societies may waste talent, and an OECD study (2014) found rising inequality lowered growth in OECD countries
    • living standards and social stability: high inequality is linked to lower trust and more political tension
  • The evidence on growth points both ways, so economists disagree on how much inequality harms it.
Mission 2Evaluate

How much inequality is a problem?

It depends on:

  • Values. Fairness is a normative judgement; people disagree on how equal outcomes should be.
  • Causes. Inequality from effort and skill is viewed differently from inequality from inheritance, luck or discrimination.
  • Mobility. Wide gaps matter more if children of poor families stay poor.
  • Living standards of the poorest. Rising inequality with rising absolute incomes differs from falling incomes at the bottom.
  • Measurement. Income or wealth? Before or after taxes and benefits? Wealth is distributed far more unequally than income.
Mission check

?In country P, the Gini coefficient for household income is 0.30 and for household wealth is 0.65. (Illustrative data.) What can you conclude?

A Gini coefficient measures how evenly something is shared, not how much there is: here wealth is shared less evenly than income.

Make a guess: If almost everyone in a country gets richer, can more people end up counted as poor?

Mission 3

Two meanings of poverty

  • Absolute poverty: income too low to pay for basic needs: food, clean water, shelter, clothing.
    • Measured against a line that is fixed in real terms (it rises only with prices).
  • Relative poverty: income far below the typical income of the same society.
    • Measured against a line that moves with the median income, e.g. 60% of median household income.
  • Median: the middle income when all households are lined up from lowest to highest.
  • Absolute poverty asks: can you survive? Relative poverty asks: can you take part in normal life where you live?
  • Say: "relative poverty is low income compared with the median" — not: "a milder form of absolute poverty".
Mission 3

Worked example: the line moves

  • Illustrative data. Nine households, weekly income:
    • 150 · 220 · 280 · 340 · 400 · 460 · 520 · 650 · 900
  • Median = 400. Relative line = 0.6 × 400 = 240.
  • Below 240: two households (150, 220). Relative poverty rate = 2/9 = 22%.
  • Ten years later, in real terms: the bottom three gain 2%; everyone else gains 20%.
    • New median = 400 × 1.2 = 480. New line = 0.6 × 480 = 288.
    • Bottom three now: 153 · 224.4 · 285.6. All below 288.
    • Relative poverty rate = 3/9 = 33%.
  • Absolute line fixed at 200 (real terms): one household below it before (150), one after (153).
  • Everyone got richer. Relative poverty rose; absolute poverty did not.
Quick check
?Over 20 years, the real income of every household in a country doubles, including the median household's. The poverty lines are 60% of the median (relative) and a fixed basket of basic needs (absolute). What happens?
Mission 3

Poverty lines in practice

  • International poverty line (World Bank): $3.00 a day per person, at 2021 prices, adjusted for purchasing power parity (PPP).
    • PPP: adjusts for what money buys in each country, so $3.00 means the same basket everywhere.
    • It is an absolute line. It replaced $2.15 a day (2017 prices) in June 2025.
    • Higher lines for richer countries: $4.20 and $8.30 a day.
  • UK: relative low income = below 60% of median household income, before or after housing costs (BHC/AHC).
  • The UK also uses an "absolute low income" line: 60% of the 2010/11 median, held fixed in real terms.
Mission 3

Case: the numbers

  • World, below the international line:
    • 1990: around 2.3 billion people.
    • 2025: around 831 million: about 1 in 10 people.
  • UK, 2023/24, median income £650 a week (BHC). Line: 0.6 × 650 = £390 a week.
    • Relative low income: 17% of people BHC; 21% AHC.
    • Children: 31% AHC. Pensioners: 16% AHC.
  • These figures are for a couple with no children. So the UK relative line is about £28 a day each (390 ÷ 7 ÷ 2): far above the World Bank's $3.00 a day.
  • One country can cut absolute poverty fast while relative poverty barely moves.
Mission 3

Causes of poverty

  • Individuals and households:
    • unemployment, low pay, part-time or insecure work
    • low human capital: few skills or qualifications
    • sickness, disability, caring duties, old age
    • large families or one earner (e.g. lone parents)
    • discrimination (gender, ethnicity, disability) and unequal opportunity
    • owning little wealth or property: no income from rent, interest or profit
  • Economy and government:
    • recession, rising prices of essentials (food, energy, rents)
    • changes to taxes and benefits
    • conflict, weak institutions, climate shocks (low-income countries)
Mission 3

What changes the poverty count?

  • Absolute poverty falls when the real incomes of the poor rise.
  • Relative poverty falls only when the poor's incomes rise faster than the median.
  • So a recession can cut relative poverty: the median falls while benefits and pensions hold steady.
  • And fast growth can raise relative poverty, if gains go mostly to middle and high earners.
  • Say: "relative poverty fell because the median fell" — not: "the poor became better off".
Mission 3

Effects of poverty and inequality

  • Individuals: worse health and shorter lives, poorer diet, stress, lower school results, debt.
  • Firms: lower demand for most goods; workers with less training and lower productivity.
  • Economy:
    • low human capital → lower productivity and growth
    • higher spending on benefits and health care; less tax paid
  • Counter-argument: some inequality gives incentives to work, train and take risks.
Mission 3Evaluate

Can growth alone end poverty?

  • Growth helps: much of the fall in world absolute poverty since 1990 came in fast-growing economies such as China and India.
  • It depends on:
    • the measure: growth cuts absolute poverty more reliably than relative poverty
    • who gains: "inclusive" growth raises low incomes; growth in a few high-pay sectors may not
    • prices of essentials: rising rents or food prices can absorb income gains
    • public services: health, schooling and clean water reach people that higher average incomes may not
Quick check
?During a recession, median household income falls by 5% in real terms. State pensions are held constant in real terms, and most pensioners' income comes from the state pension. What is most likely to happen to measured relative poverty among pensioners?
Mission check

?Over ten years, the real incomes of the poorest fifth of a country's households rise by 15%. Median real household income rises by 40%. Which outcome is most likely?

Absolute poverty tracks the real incomes of the poor. Relative poverty tracks the gap between the poor and the median, so it can rise while almost everyone gets richer.

Mission 4

Equity is not equality

  • Equality: everyone gets the same (income, wealth or opportunity).
  • Equity: a fair distribution. What is fair is a value judgement (normative).
  • Horizontal equity: people in the same position are treated the same (same income → same tax).
  • Vertical equity: people in different positions are treated differently (higher income → pay more).
  • Equality of outcome vs equality of opportunity: many argue fairness needs the second, not the first.
  • Say: "an equitable distribution" (fair) — not: "an equal distribution", unless you mean identical shares.
Mission 4

Why redistribute?

  • In a free market, income depends on the factors you own and what they earn: wages, rent, interest, profit.
  • Some own little: low skills, no property, unable to work. Their market income may be very low.
  • Many economists call a very unequal outcome a market failure: markets can be efficient yet produce an inequitable result.
  • Redistribution: government moves income or wealth from richer to poorer households, using:
    • taxes, transfers and benefits, state-provided services, and laws on pay.
Mission 4

Progressive, proportional, regressive

  • Compare the average tax rate (tax ÷ income) at different incomes:
    • Progressive: average rate rises as income rises.
    • Proportional: average rate stays the same.
    • Regressive: average rate falls as income rises.
  • Marginal tax rate: the rate on the next unit of income.
  • Illustrative data. Tax bands: 0% on the first 12,000; 20% from 12,000 to 50,000; 40% above 50,000.
    • Income 30,000: tax = 20% × 18,000 = 3,600. Average rate = 3,600 ÷ 30,000 = 12%. Marginal rate 20%.
    • Income 80,000: tax = 20% × 38,000 + 40% × 30,000 = 7,600 + 12,000 = 19,600. Average rate = 24.5%. Marginal rate 40%.
  • Average rate rises with income: progressive.
  • Taxes on spending (e.g. VAT) are usually regressive relative to income: poorer households spend a bigger share of income.
Quick check
?Using the bands in the previous screen (0% on the first 12,000; 20% from 12,000 to 50,000; 40% above 50,000), what is the average tax rate on an income of 60,000?
Mission 4

Transfers: universal or means-tested?

  • Transfer payments: money paid by government with nothing produced in return: pensions, unemployment and disability benefits, child benefit.
  • Universal benefit: paid to everyone in a group, whatever their income (e.g. a state pension for all over an age).
  • Means-tested benefit: paid only to those with low income or wealth (e.g. the UK's Universal Credit).

Mission 4

The poverty trap

  • Poverty trap: as a low earner earns more, benefits are withdrawn and taxes start, so they keep little of the rise.
  • Effective marginal tax rate (EMTR): share of an extra unit of earnings lost to tax and benefit withdrawal.
  • Case: UK, Universal Credit. For each extra £1 of net earnings, benefit falls by 55p (the taper rate, cut from 63% in November 2021).
  • Worked example: a basic-rate taxpayer on Universal Credit earns £100 more.
    • Income tax 20% + National Insurance 8% = £28. Net earnings rise by £72.
    • Benefit withdrawn: 55% × £72 = £39.60.
    • Kept: £72 − £39.60 = £32.40.
    • EMTR = 1 − 0.72 × 0.45 = 67.6%.
  • Say: combine the rates by multiplying what is kept (0.72 × 0.45) — not: by adding them (28% + 55% = 83%), since the taper applies to net earnings.
Mission 4Graph lab

Change the taper

Line: net income (after tax and benefits) against gross earnings. Flat line = nothing kept; 45° line = everything kept.

  • Takeaway: a higher taper flattens the line (stronger trap) but ends benefit sooner (cheaper). A lower taper helps work incentives but stretches benefit to higher earners.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A worker pays income tax at 20% on extra earnings. A means-tested benefit is withdrawn at 60% of each extra unit of net (after-tax) earnings. What is the worker's effective marginal tax rate?
Mission 4

Universal basic income

  • Universal basic income (UBI): a regular cash payment to every adult, with no means test and no work condition.
  • For:
    • no withdrawal as earnings rise, so no poverty trap from the benefit itself
    • simple; full take-up; no stigma
    • security when work is irregular
  • Against:
    • very costly: paid to all, so higher taxes, which raise marginal tax rates for earners
    • may reduce work for some (an income effect)
    • a flat payment ignores different needs (disability, housing costs)
  • Case: Finland, 2017–2018. 2,000 unemployed people received €560 a month, tax-free, with no conditions.
    • Employment: recipients worked 78 days on average in the second year, about 6 more than a control group.
    • Recipients reported better wellbeing and less financial stress.
    • Limits: not universal (unemployed only), two years, small sample; other benefit rules changed in 2018, which blurs the result.
Mission 4

Minimum wages

  • A legal minimum wage: a price floor in the labour market.
  • Competitive labour market: a minimum above the equilibrium wage raises pay for those in work but may cut jobs (excess supply of labour).
  • Monopsony employer: a minimum wage can raise pay and employment.
  • Evidence is mixed:
    • Card and Krueger (1994), New Jersey fast food: no fall in employment after a rise.
    • Other studies find job losses, especially for young and low-skilled workers or after large rises.
  • Targeting: helps the low-paid, but many poor households have no one in work, and some low-paid workers live in better-off households.
Mission 4

Services, skills and fair treatment

  • State provision of merit goods: free or subsidised education and health care benefit poorer households most, relative to income.
  • Human capital: training and schooling raise future earnings: tackles a cause of poverty, not just the symptom.
  • Targeted spending: free school meals, housing support, help with energy bills.
  • Anti-discrimination laws: equal pay for equal work; open hiring and promotion.
  • Trade-off: slow to work (years for education to raise pay), but can raise efficiency as well as equity.
Quick check
?A government replaces a means-tested benefit with a universal payment of the same maximum amount, paid to everyone whatever their income. What is the most likely effect?
Mission 4Evaluate

Equity or efficiency?

  • Redistribution often involves a trade-off: more equity, but weaker incentives and some lost output.
  • It depends on:
    • rates: high tax rates and high withdrawal rates weaken incentives more
    • how people respond: if labour supply hardly changes with pay, little output is lost
    • design: universal vs targeted benefits
    • use of the money: spending on education and health can raise productivity, so equity and efficiency go together
  • Moral and political judgement: how much inequality is fair, and how much output to give up for it, is normative. Economics measures the trade-off; it cannot settle it.
Mission check

?A government wants to weaken the poverty trap for low-income working families on a means-tested benefit. Which change would do this most directly?

The poverty trap is about the share of each extra unit that is lost, not the level of support. Lower the tax and withdrawal rates on the next unit, and work pays more, though usually at a higher cost to government.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • At the social optimum MSB = MSC and total surplus is as large as possible: allocative efficiency.
  • Market failure (for example externalities and public goods) leaves output above or below that point, causing a welfare loss.

Mission 2

  • Income is a flow; wealth is a stock. Wealth is usually distributed more unequally than income.
  • The Lorenz curve shows cumulative shares; the further it sags from the line of equality, the higher the Gini coefficient (0 = perfect equality, 1 = perfect inequality).
  • Causes include human capital, wealth ownership, discrimination, power, tax and benefits, and technology.

Mission 3

  • Absolute poverty: below a fixed real line for basic needs. Relative poverty: below a share (often 60%) of the median.
  • Growth cuts absolute poverty more reliably than relative poverty; a recession can even cut relative poverty.

Mission 4

  • Equity is fairness, a value judgement; equality means identical shares.
  • Progressive taxes: the average tax rate rises with income.
  • Means-tested benefits target the poor but create a poverty trap: a high effective marginal tax rate.
  • UBI, minimum wages and human capital policies trade off cost, targeting and incentives.
