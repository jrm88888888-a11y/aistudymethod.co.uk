Mission 1

Mission 1: Why can an amoeba survive without lungs or a heart, but you can't?

Make a guess: Why can an amoeba survive without lungs or a heart, but you can't?

Mission 1

Supply against demand

  • Every living cell needs oxygen in and carbon dioxide out, plus nutrients in and wastes out.
  • Heat is exchanged with the surroundings across the surface too.
  • Supply depends on surface area: the area materials cross.
  • Demand depends on volume: the amount of living, respiring tissue.
  • So the key number is the surface area to volume ratio = surface area ÷ volume. Written SA:V or SA/V.
Mission 1

Worked example: bigger means less surface per volume

  • Total surface area rises with size. SA:V falls.
  • Say: "a smaller surface area to volume ratio" — not: "a smaller surface area".

Mission 1

Small organisms: diffusion is enough

  • A single-celled organism, e.g. Amoeba, exchanges gases across its cell-surface membrane.
  • O2 diffuses in and CO2 diffuses out, each down its concentration gradient.
  • High SA:V, and no part of the cell is more than a few micrometres from the surface.
  • Diffusion is fast only over short distances. Ten times the distance takes about a hundred times as long.
  • Approximate times for O2 to diffuse through water: 0.01 mm (about one cell) 0.02 s · 1 mm 4 minutes · 1 cm 7 hours.
Mission 1

Bigger bodies: change shape

Two shapes with the same volume, 8 mm³:

  • Cube, 2 × 2 × 2 mm: surface area 24 mm², SA:V = 3 mm⁻¹.
  • Flat sheet, 4 × 4 × 0.5 mm: surface area 40 mm², SA:V = 5 mm⁻¹.
  • Flattened or thin shapes add surface with little extra volume.
  • They also keep every cell close to the surface.
  • Examples: the flat body of a flatworm; thin, flat leaves; root hairs.
Mission 1

Bigger bodies: specialised systems

  • In a large, active animal the body surface is too small, and most cells are too far from it.
  • Specialised exchange surfaces pack a huge area inside the body: lungs, gills.
  • Ventilation brings fresh air or water to that surface.
  • Mass transport: a fluid flows in bulk, carrying substances long distances quickly. Diffusion covers only the last micrometres.
  • Mammals: blood pumped round by the heart (circulation); air moved in and out by ventilation.
  • Flowering plants: water and mineral ions in xylem; sugars in phloem (translocation).
  • Plants need transport despite a lower metabolic rate: they are large, and roots and leaves are far apart.
Quick check
?Why does gas exchange become a greater challenge as organisms increase in size?
Mission 1

Size, heat loss and metabolic rate

  • Mammals and birds keep a nearly constant body temperature. They lose heat across their surface.
  • Small body → high SA:V → loses heat faster per gram.
  • To replace it, its cells respire faster: a higher metabolic rate per unit body mass.
  • So small mammals and birds need more O2 per gram, delivered quickly.
  • Say: "higher metabolic rate per unit body mass" — not: "higher metabolic rate". A large animal still uses more energy in total.

Mission check

?An amoeba gets all its oxygen by diffusion across its cell-surface membrane. Why could a mouse not get enough oxygen by diffusion across its body surface alone?

Small size helps the amoeba: its high SA:V lets diffusion supply every part. A larger body has too little surface for its volume, so it needs lungs to exchange gases and a heart to carry them.

Mission 2

What makes an exchange surface efficient

  • Large surface area: more molecules cross at once.
  • Thin: a short diffusion distance, often one cell or less.
  • Steep concentration gradient, kept up by supply on one side and removal on the other.
  • Examples: root hair cells (large area); alveoli (thin); gills and alveoli (blood supply and ventilation keep the gradient).
Mission 2

Fick's law

  • Rate of diffusion ∝ (surface area × difference in concentration) ÷ thickness of surface.
  • Each feature on the last screen is one term in this relationship.
  • Area × 2 → rate × 2. Difference in concentration × 2 → rate × 2. Thickness × 2 → rate ÷ 2.
  • Area ÷ 2 and difference × 2 together → rate unchanged.
  • Use it to compare rates as ratios, not to find an absolute rate.
Quick check
?A model exchange surface is changed. Its surface area is doubled and its thickness is tripled. The difference in concentration does not change. By what factor does the rate of diffusion change?
Mission 2

Keeping the gradient steep

  • Diffusion evens out concentrations. If nothing moves, the gradient disappears and net diffusion stops.
  • Outside: ventilation replaces used air (lungs) or water (gills) with fresh.
  • Inside: continuous blood flow carries O2 away and brings CO2.
  • A dense network of capillaries puts blood next to every part of the surface.
  • Say: "blood flow carries oxygenated blood away, keeping the gradient" — not: "blood slows down to collect more oxygen".
Quick check
?Suppose that blood flow through the gill capillaries of an anaesthetised fish is stopped while water continues to flow over the gills, and that oxygen uptake across the gills falls almost to zero within a minute. Which explanation is best supported?

?Lungless salamanders have no lungs and, as adults, no gills. They exchange gases across their skin and the lining of the mouth. Which set of features would best suit this way of life?

Mission 3: How do your lungs pull air in when they have no muscle of their own?

Mission 3

The airway tree

  • Air path: trachea → two bronchi → many bronchioles → alveoli (air sacs).
  • Repeated branching ends in hundreds of millions of alveoli: a surface of tens of square metres inside the chest.
  • Gas exchange happens only across the alveoli. The airways conduct air to them.
  • The lungs sit in the thorax, enclosed by the ribs and intercostal muscles, with the diaphragm below.

Mission 3

The alveolus: built for diffusion

  • Alveolar wall: one layer of flattened (squamous) epithelial cells.
  • Capillary wall: one layer of endothelial cells. Over much of the surface, air to blood is under 1 µm.
  • A dense capillary network wraps each alveolus. Continuous blood flow keeps the gradient.
  • Ventilation keeps alveolar air high in O2 and low in CO2.
  • A moist lining lets gases dissolve. Surfactant in it lowers surface tension, so alveoli do not collapse.
  • Say: "thin alveolar epithelium, one cell thick" — not: "thin cell walls". Animal cells have no cell walls.
Quick check
?Mammalian lungs contain a branched network of bronchioles ending in millions of alveoli. How does this branching contribute to gas exchange?
Mission 3

Breathing in

  • (1) The diaphragm contracts and flattens, moving down.
  • (1) The external intercostal muscles contract, moving the ribs up and out. The internal intercostals relax.
  • (2) Thorax volume rises, so pressure in the lungs falls below atmospheric.
  • (3) Air flows in, down the pressure gradient. The lungs are pulled open; they do no work themselves.
Mission 3

Breathing out

  • At rest, breathing out is mostly passive. The diaphragm and external intercostals relax.
  • The diaphragm domes upward; the ribs move down and in; stretched elastic fibres in the lungs recoil.
  • Thorax volume falls, pressure rises above atmospheric, and air flows out.
  • Forced breathing out: the internal intercostal muscles contract, pulling the ribs down and in.
  • External and internal intercostals are antagonistic: they move the ribs in opposite directions.
  • Say: "the diaphragm relaxes and domes upward" — not: "the diaphragm contracts and pushes up".
Quick check
?Which sequence of events draws air into the lungs during inspiration?
Mission check

?During a rugby match, a player is tackled hard. A small tear in the surface of one lung lets air leak into the space around that lung. The diaphragm and intercostal muscles on that side still contract normally, but that lung does not inflate. Which explanation is best?

The muscles change the volume of the thorax; the lungs simply follow. Break that link and the lung stays collapsed.

Mission 4

Mission 4: How can you tell from the outside how well a person's lungs are working?

Make a guess: How can you tell from the outside how well a person's lungs are working?

Mission 4

Lung volumes on a spirometer trace

  • A spirometer records the volume of air breathed in and out against time.
  • Tidal volume: volume in or out in one normal breath (about 0.5 dm³ at rest).
  • Inspiratory reserve volume: the extra you can breathe in after a normal breath in.
  • Expiratory reserve volume: the extra you can breathe out after a normal breath out.
  • Vital capacity = tidal volume + inspiratory reserve + expiratory reserve: the most you can breathe out after the deepest breath in.
  • Residual volume: air left after the hardest breath out. A spirometer cannot measure it.
Quick check
?A student's spirometer readings are: tidal volume 0.5 dm³, inspiratory reserve volume 3.0 dm³, expiratory reserve volume 1.2 dm³. What is the student's vital capacity?
Mission 4

Worked example: ventilation and oxygen uptake

  • Breathing rate: breaths per minute (peaks per minute on a trace).
  • Ventilation rate = tidal volume × breathing rate.
  • At rest: 0.50 dm³ × 12 min⁻¹ = 6.0 dm³ min⁻¹. Exercise: 1.6 dm³ × 25 min⁻¹ = 40 dm³ min⁻¹.
  • Oxygen uptake: soda lime absorbs the CO2 breathed out, so the trace drifts down as O2 is used.
  • Example (illustrative data): the trace falls 0.75 dm³ in 3.0 min, so O2 uptake = 0.75 ÷ 3.0 = 0.25 dm³ min⁻¹.
Quick check
?During gentle exercise, a student breathes 15 dm³ of air per minute at a breathing rate of 20 breaths per minute. What is her tidal volume?
Mission 4Practical

Spirometer and exercise

  • PAG10: breathing data from a spirometer or data logger.
  • Method: chamber filled with medical-grade oxygen; soda lime in the circuit; nose clip; record for 1–2 minutes at rest.
  • Exercise for a set time and intensity (e.g. 3 minutes of step-ups), then record again at once. Compare with the same person's resting trace.
  • Safety: disinfect the mouthpiece; soda lime is corrosive; stop if dizzy; no one with asthma or a heart or lung condition.
  • Calculate: tidal volume, breathing rate, ventilation, O2 consumption (slope).
Mission check

?At rest, an athlete's tidal volume is 0.6 dm³, inspiratory reserve volume 2.8 dm³ and expiratory reserve volume 1.4 dm³. During hard exercise her tidal volume rises to 2.2 dm³. Which statement is correct?

Lung volumes, breathing rate and disease data all answer one question: how much air reaches a large, thin, well-supplied surface.

Mission 5

Mission 5: How do fish and insects get oxygen to their cells without lungs?

Make a guess: How do fish and insects get oxygen to their cells without lungs?

Mission 5

Gills: a huge surface in water

  • Each side of a bony fish's head has gills under a bony flap, the operculum.
  • Each gill arch carries two rows of gill filaments.
  • Each filament is covered in gill lamellae: thin plates at right angles to it.
  • Lamellae are also called gill plates.
  • Lamellae give a large surface area, a thin barrier and a dense network of capillaries.
  • Water flows one way: in through the mouth, over the gills, out under the operculum.
Mission 5

Ventilating the gills

  • (1) Mouth opens; floor of the buccal cavity (mouth cavity) lowers. Volume rises, pressure falls: water flows in.
  • (1) The operculum is closed, so water cannot enter from behind.
  • (2) Mouth closes; buccal floor rises. Pressure rises: water is forced over the gills.
  • (2) The operculum opens and water leaves.
  • Pressure in the buccal cavity stays above that in the opercular cavity behind the gills, so water flows over the gills almost continuously.
Mission 5

Counter-current flow

  • In each lamella, blood flows in the opposite direction to the water.
  • Blood always meets water with more O2 than itself.
  • So a diffusion gradient is maintained along the whole length of the lamella.
  • Blood can leave with an O2 concentration close to that of the incoming water.
  • Parallel flow (same direction), for comparison: the gradient falls to zero part-way along. Blood can reach at most the value where the two meet, about half.
  • Say: "a diffusion gradient is maintained along the whole lamella" — not just: "blood and water flow in opposite directions".
Quick check
?In a model of one gill lamella, water enters with a relative O2 concentration of 100 and blood enters with 0. Water and blood flow at equal rates and carry O2 equally well. What is the highest value the blood could reach with each type of flow?
Mission 5

Insects: air piped to the cells

  • Air enters through spiracles: pores along the thorax and abdomen.
  • Tracheae: tubes kept open by rings of chitin.
  • They branch into tracheoles: very fine tubes that reach in among respiring cells.
  • O2 diffuses in air all the way to the cells: fast. Blood is not needed to carry it.
  • The tracheole walls are the exchange surface: huge total area, very thin.
  • The tracheole ends hold tracheal fluid. In active muscle, lactate lowers the cells' water potential, water leaves the tracheoles by osmosis, and air reaches closer to the cells.
Mission 5

Ventilation and the water problem

  • Active insects ventilate: muscles squeeze and expand the abdomen (and thorax), pumping air in and out of the tracheae.
  • In some insects (e.g. locusts), front spiracles open as the body expands and rear ones as it contracts: air flows one way.
  • On land, any moist exchange surface also loses water.
  • Adaptations that cut water loss: spiracles close with valves when the insect is inactive; hairs around spiracles trap moist air; a waxy cuticle covers the body.
  • The trade-off: spiracles open → more O2 in, but more water vapour out.
Quick check
?In very dry air, a resting insect keeps its spiracles closed most of the time and opens them only briefly. Why?
Mission 5Practical

Dissecting gills and tracheae

  • PAG2: dissect, examine and draw the gas exchange system of a bony fish and/or insect tracheae.
  • Fish (head from a fishmonger): lift the operculum; cut out one gill arch; float it in water under a hand lens to see filaments and lamellae spread out.
  • Insect (e.g. a locust): open the abdomen under saline; tracheae look silvery because they hold air. Mount a piece: chitin rings show under the microscope.
  • Safety: sharp tools away from you; gloves; wash hands. Use animals already dead. Record with labelled drawings.
Mission check

?In the legs of many wading birds, warm blood flowing down to the feet in arteries passes very close to cold blood flowing back up in veins. The feet stand in cold water. How does this arrangement reduce heat loss from the feet?

Counter-current exchange works for heat as well as for O2: opposite flows keep a gradient along the whole length.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • As size increases, SA:V falls; demand (volume) outgrows supply (surface).
  • Diffusion alone is enough only over short distances; large organisms need flattened shapes, specialised exchange surfaces and ventilation.
  • Mass transport (circulation, xylem, phloem) carries substances over long distances.
  • Small endotherms: high SA:V → fast heat loss → high metabolic rate per unit body mass.

Mission 2

  • Efficient exchange surfaces: large area, thin, steep gradient kept up by ventilation and blood flow.
  • Fick's law: rate ∝ (surface area × difference in concentration) ÷ thickness.

Mission 3

  • Trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli; thin, moist, ventilated alveoli wrapped in capillaries give a huge, short diffusion path.
  • Breathing in: diaphragm and external intercostals contract → thorax volume up → pressure down → air in. Breathing out reverses this.

Mission 4

  • Vital capacity = tidal volume + inspiratory reserve + expiratory reserve; measured with a spirometer.
  • Ventilation per minute = tidal volume × breathing rate.

Mission 5

  • Fish: water flows one way over gill lamellae; counter-current blood flow keeps a diffusion gradient along the whole lamella.
  • Insects: spiracles → tracheae → tracheoles carry air straight to respiring cells; closable spiracles, hairs and a waxy cuticle limit water loss.
