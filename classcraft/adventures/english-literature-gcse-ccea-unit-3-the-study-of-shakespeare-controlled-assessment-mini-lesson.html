Unit 3: The Study of Shakespeare (Controlled Assessment)

Unit 3 is controlled assessment: one extended writing task, a maximum of 2 hours, worth 20% of the qualification. Your centre chooses a task from the CCEA task list, based on a theme, and your response must engage with the whole play. Your teachers mark it and CCEA moderates.

The unit

What the Shakespeare task is

Unit 3 is assessed by controlled assessment: a single extended writing question based on a theme, completed in a maximum of 2 hours, worth 20%.

  • CCEA sets the tasks — the level of control for task setting is high, and your centre must choose from the list CCEA provides.
  • The question must enable a response to the whole play. This is not a passage-based task: you are expected to range across the text.
  • The tasks are replaced each year. Always check the current Task List for your moderation date on the CCEA English Literature microsite.
  • Research, planning and preparation happen under limited supervision; the timed assessment is under high control.

Themes: CCEA task themes have included Conflict and Love, with tasks asking candidates to examine how Shakespeare presents the theme — or the conflict or love between characters — in their chosen play.

The task

The three things every task asks for

The CCEA tasks spell out exactly what a response must do. Whatever the theme, you should:

  • communicate a response to and understanding of meanings and features identified in the play;
  • identify dramatic methods, show understanding of them and comment on them;
  • provide contextual information to enhance understanding of the play.

The four CCEA assessment objectives: AO1 respond to texts critically and imaginatively, and select and evaluate relevant textual detail to illustrate and support interpretations; AO2 explain how language, structure and form contribute to writers presentation of ideas, themes, characters and settings; AO3 make comparisons and explain links between texts, evaluating writers differing ways of expressing meaning and achieving effects; AO4 relate texts to their social, cultural and historical contexts, and explain how texts have been influential and significant to readers in different contexts and at different times.

Read the three bullets as a mark scheme. If a paragraph of your essay does not do at least two of them, it is not earning its place.

Method

Tracing a theme across the whole play

Because the question demands a response to the whole play, structure your essay as a journey, not a heap.

  • Early: how is the theme introduced? What does the play let us assume?
  • Development: where does it complicate, escalate or turn?
  • Crisis: the scene where the theme costs someone something irreversible.
  • Late: what does the play finally conclude — and does the ending endorse it or refuse to?

Choose four or five key moments spread across the acts. An essay that never leaves Act One cannot answer a whole-play question, however brilliant its close reading.

Dramatic methods

Shakespeare methods worth naming

  • Soliloquy — a character alone with the audience. It creates intimacy and complicity: we are made confidants of a plan we may find appalling.
  • Verse and prose — a shift between them is meaningful. Watch who speaks in verse, when, and what it means when they stop.
  • Imagery patterns — a run of images (blood, disease, light and dark, clothing, gardens) that saturates the play and becomes its way of thinking.
  • Dramatic irony — the audience knows what a character does not, so every ordinary line becomes double-edged.
  • Staging — who is on stage and silent, who enters at the worst moment, what is done in public and what in private.
  • Structure — where the climax sits, what is placed immediately after a horror (Shakespeare often follows terror with comedy, and the whiplash is the point).

Context

Context that enhances understanding

The CCEA task asks for contextual information to enhance understanding of the play. That final phrase is the whole rule: if the context does not make the play clearer, it does not belong.

  • Belief and world-view — what an audience of the time took for granted about order, fate, kingship, the supernatural, gender or honour.
  • Theatrical context — an outdoor daylight playhouse, no lighting effects, boys playing women, an audience that talked back. This changes how a scene works.
  • Reception context — how the play has been staged and read differently since, which is also AO4 territory.
  • Integrate, do not append. One woven sentence per paragraph, tied to a specific choice in the text.

The test: delete your context sentence. If the paragraph means exactly the same without it, the context was decoration.

The controlled assessment rules

Knowing the rules protects your mark.

  • CCEA sets the task. Your centre chooses from the published list; the tasks are replaced each year, so use the current list for your moderation date.
  • You may research, plan and prepare under limited supervision — teachers must not provide model answers or writing frames.
  • The writing itself is under high control, with a maximum of 2 hours.
  • Teachers mark, CCEA moderates.

The practical consequence: what you take into the timed session — your plan, your quotations, your argument — is what you will have. Prepare a genuine argument, not a bag of quotations.

Writing

Two hours, spent well

  • 0–10 min: plan. Your argument in one sentence, then four or five claims in the order that builds best, each with its moment.
  • 10–105 min: write. Introduction that takes a position; five or six developed paragraphs; a conclusion that turns the argument once more.
  • 105–120 min: check. Quotation accuracy, character names, the sentences that do not say what you meant.
  • Every paragraph: claim · quotation · dramatic method · effect on the audience · a sentence of context where it genuinely illuminates.

Never invent a quotation. If the exact words will not come, refer precisely to the moment instead. An accurate reference is evidence; a misquotation is a hole in your credibility.

Evidence

Quoting Shakespeare well

Quotation is where Shakespeare essays are won and lost. The rules are simple and unforgiving.

  • Short and exact. Four or five words with one image in them beats six lines you cannot analyse.
  • Analyse the words you chose. If your comment would be identical without the quotation, the quotation is decoration.
  • Spread the evidence across the acts — a whole-play question needs whole-play proof.
  • Do not paraphrase into modern English and call it a quotation. Either quote exactly, or refer to the moment.

Learn quotations in clusters: three or four short lines per theme, per key character. That way any task the CCEA list throws at you is already covered.
