Mission 1

Mission 1: Joining nuclei releases energy, and so does splitting them. How can both be true?

Make a guess: Joining nuclei releases energy, and so does splitting them. How can both be true?

Mission 1

Nuclear fusion

  • Fusion: two light nuclei join to form a heavier nucleus, often with a spare particle.
  • ²1H + ³1H → ⁴2He + ¹0n + energy
  • Light nuclei sit on the steep left side of the binding energy per nucleon curve. Heavy nuclei sit on the gentle right side.
  • Fusion (from the left) and fission (from the right) both move nuclei towards the peak near iron: more tightly bound.
  • More binding → less total mass → energy released (E = Δmc²).
Quick check
?In the fusion reaction ²1H + ³1H → ⁴2He + ¹0n, the binding energies per nucleon are 1.11 MeV for ²1H, 2.83 MeV for ³1H and 7.07 MeV for ⁴2He. A free neutron has no binding energy. How much energy is released in one reaction?
Mission 1

Worked example: deuterium–deuterium fusion

  • Per nucleon, D–T fusion gives more: 17.6 MeV ÷ 5 = 3.5 MeV, against about 0.85 MeV for uranium fission.
  • ²1H + ²1H → ³2He + ¹0n. Atomic masses (u): ²H 2.014102; ³He 3.016029; n 1.008665.
  • Before: 2 × 2.014102 = 4.028204 u.
  • After: 3.016029 + 1.008665 = 4.024694 u.
  • Δm = 0.003510 u → × 931.5 = 3.27 MeV.
  • Electrons: 2 on each side, so atomic masses are fine.
Quick check
?One reaction in the Sun's core is ³He + ³He → ⁴He + 2 ¹H. The atomic masses are 3.016029 u for helium-3, 4.002603 u for helium-4 and 1.007825 u for hydrogen-1 (the electron masses balance). Take 1 u = 1.661 × 10⁻²⁷ kg and c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. How much energy is released in one reaction?
Quick check
?Predict first. Hydrogen fusion in a star needs a core temperature of the order of 10⁷ K. Why is such a high temperature needed?
Mission 1

The electric barrier

  • Nuclei are positive: they repel.
  • The strong force attracts only within about 10⁻¹⁵ m. The nuclei must get that close.
  • Two protons 2 × 10⁻¹⁵ m apart: electric potential energy about 0.7 MeV.
  • So the nuclei need very high kinetic energy: a very high temperature (about 10⁷ K in the Sun's core).
  • Only the fastest nuclei fuse, helped by a quantum effect (tunnelling). The rest just bounce off.
Quick check
?Predict first. Hydrogen makes up most of the Sun's outer layers as well as its core, yet hydrogen fusion happens only in the core. Why?
Mission 1

Fusion in stars

  • A cloud of gas and dust collapses under its own gravity.
  • Gravitational potential energy → thermal energy: the core gets hotter and denser.
  • Hot and dense enough: hydrogen fuses into helium. Overall: 4 ¹H → ⁴He + 2e⁺ + 2ν. Including the positrons' annihilation, about 26.7 MeV in total.
  • High density → frequent collisions → enough fusions per second to keep the star shining.
  • Outward gas pressure and radiation pressure balance inward gravity: a stable star.
Mission check

?Approximate binding energies per nucleon are: ²1H 1.1 MeV; ⁴2He 7.1 MeV; ²⁰10Ne 8.0 MeV; ²⁸13Al 8.3 MeV; ⁴⁰20Ca 8.6 MeV; ⁵⁶26Fe 8.8 MeV; ⁹²36Kr 8.5 MeV; ¹⁴⁴56Ba 8.3 MeV; ²³⁶92U 7.6 MeV. Which pair of processes would both release energy?

In both fusion and fission the products are more tightly bound, so their total mass is less: E = Δmc². Both processes move nuclei towards the most tightly bound size, near iron. Light nuclei fuse up to it; heavy nuclei split down to it. Either way the total mass falls, and that mass leaves as energy.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why will the Sun end as a small, fading ember, while far heavier stars blow themselves apart?

Make a guess: Why will the Sun end as a small, fading ember, while far heavier stars blow themselves apart?

Mission 2

A star in balance

  • Gravity pulls every layer of a star inwards.
  • Pressure pushes outwards: hot gas (gas pressure) and radiation (radiation pressure).
  • Fusion in the core keeps the core hot, so the pressure is maintained.
  • Balanced: the star is stable, neither collapsing nor expanding. Huge forces, zero resultant.
Quick check
?Suppose the rate of fusion in a main-sequence star's core suddenly fell. What would happen to the core at first?
Mission 2

The HR diagram

  • Luminosity (vertical, log scale, in units of the Sun's) against surface temperature (horizontal, log scale).
  • Temperature decreases to the right: hot blue stars on the left, cool red stars on the right.
  • Main sequence: diagonal band, upper left to lower right. About 90% of stars.
  • Red giants: upper right. Red supergiants: across the top.
  • White dwarfs: lower left.
  • Instability strip: a near-vertical band above the main sequence; stars there pulsate, so their luminosity varies.
  • Lines of constant radius run diagonally, from upper left to lower right: L ∝ R²T⁴.
Quick check
?The HR diagram shows the main sequence, the Sun and a star X. Which statement about star X is correct?
Mission 2

What position tells you

  • Same temperature, more luminous → bigger (more surface area).
  • Upper right: cool, yet very luminous → huge.
  • Lower left: hot, yet dim → tiny.
  • On the main sequence, mass sets the position: more massive stars are higher, hotter and more luminous.
  • More massive → burns fuel much faster → shorter main-sequence life.
Mission 2

Reading a star's spectrum

  • A star's spectrum: a continuous spectrum crossed by dark absorption lines.
  • Peak wavelength → surface temperature (Wien's law).
  • Dark lines → elements in the star's outer layers: match them with lines measured in the laboratory.
  • Temperature fixes the horizontal position on the HR diagram.
Quick check
?The spectrum of star P peaks at 480 nm; the spectrum of star Q peaks at 960 nm. Both spectra show dark lines that match the laboratory lines of calcium. What can be deduced?
Mission 2

Mass decides the path

  • Core hydrogen runs out → core contracts and heats up; outer layers expand and cool.
  • Low mass (like the Sun): main sequence → red giant → outer layers drift off as a planetary nebula → core left as a white dwarf.
  • High mass (more than roughly 8 times the Sun's mass): main sequence → red supergiant → supernova → neutron star, or a black hole for the most massive.
  • More mass → stronger gravity → hotter core → heavier elements fused.
  • A white dwarf has no fusion: it slowly cools and fades.
Mission 2

Paths on the HR diagram

  • A star does not slide along the main sequence as it ages.
  • Sun-like star: up and to the right (red giant) → leftwards as the core is exposed → down to the white-dwarf region → fades downwards and to the right as it cools.
  • Massive star: from the upper main sequence, across to the right (red supergiant). After the supernova, the remnant (a neutron star or black hole) is not plotted on the HR diagram.
Quick check
?Astronomers observe a glowing shell of gas expanding gently away from a very hot, very small star at its centre: a planetary nebula. Which describes the central star?
Mission check

?Astronomers find a neutron star at the centre of the expanding remains of a supernova. What can be concluded about the star that produced it, compared with the Sun?

The Sun has too little mass. Its core will never get hot enough to fuse heavy elements, and its leftover core is too small to collapse further: a white dwarf that fades.

Mission 3

Mission 3: How can a tiny yearly shift in a star's position tell us its distance, and then its size?

Make a guess: How can a tiny yearly shift in a star's position tell us its distance, and then its size?

Mission 3

Distance units

  • Astronomical unit (AU): mean Earth–Sun distance, 1.50 × 10¹¹ m.
  • Light-year (ly): distance light travels in one year, 9.46 × 10¹⁵ m.
  • Parsec (pc): distance at which 1 AU subtends an angle of 1 arcsecond.
  • 1 pc = 3.26 ly (data booklet), about 3.09 × 10¹⁶ m.
  • 1 arcsecond = 1/3600 of a degree.
Mission 3

Stellar parallax

  • Observe a nearby star twice, six months apart: from opposite sides of Earth's orbit.
  • It shifts against the very distant background stars.
  • Parallax angle p: half the total shift. It is the angle the Earth–Sun distance (1 AU) subtends at the star.
  • Closer star → bigger p.
  • d (pc) = 1/p (arcsec)
  • Only works for nearby stars: further away, p is too small to measure.
Mission 3

Worked example

  • A star has a parallax angle of 0.20 arcseconds.
  • d = 1/0.20 = 5.0 pc.
  • In light-years: 5.0 × 3.26 = 16 ly. In metres: 16.3 × 9.46 × 10¹⁵ = 1.5 × 10¹⁷ m.
Quick check
?Two measurements of the position of a nearby star against very distant background stars, made six months apart, differ by an angle of 0.0700 arc-second. Take 1 pc = 3.09 × 10¹⁶ m, 1 ly = 9.46 × 10¹⁵ m and 1 AU = 1.50 × 10¹¹ m. What is the distance to the star in AU?
Mission 3

How big is a star?

  • Temperature from the spectrum's peak: Wien's law, λmax T = 2.9 × 10⁻³ m K.
  • Luminosity from brightness and distance: L = 4πd²b, with d from parallax.
  • Then Stefan–Boltzmann, L = σAT⁴ with A = 4πR²: R = √(L/(4πσT⁴)).
  • Example: λmax = 290 nm → T = 2.9 × 10⁻³ ÷ 290 × 10⁻⁹ = 1.0 × 10⁴ K.
  • L = 1.0 × 10²⁸ W → R = √(1.0 × 10²⁸ ÷ (4π × 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ × (1.0 × 10⁴)⁴)) = 1.2 × 10⁹ m: about 1.7 Sun radii.
Quick check
?An HR diagram is drawn with luminosity on the vertical axis (increasing upwards) and surface temperature on the horizontal axis (decreasing to the right). A star in the upper right of the diagram has a luminosity of 5.0 × 10⁴ L⊙. Its spectrum peaks at a wavelength of 725 nm. Take L⊙ = 3.83 × 10²⁶ W, σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴ and Wien's displacement law λmax T = 2.9 × 10⁻³ m K. The star is modelled as a black body. What is its radius?
Mission check

?Stars X and Y have the same surface temperature and the same apparent brightness. The parallax angle of X is half that of Y. What is (radius of X) ÷ (radius of Y)?

Parallax gives the distance; with the brightness, that gives the luminosity; with the temperature from Wien's law, that gives the size.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Fusion: light nuclei join; the products are more tightly bound, so their total mass is less and energy is released.
  • Fusion (light nuclei) and fission (heavy nuclei) both move towards the peak of the binding energy per nucleon curve near iron.
  • Fusion needs very high temperature (to overcome electric repulsion) and high density (for frequent collisions): conditions in a star's core.

Mission 2

  • Stable star: gravity in, gas and radiation pressure out, kept up by fusion.
  • HR diagram: luminosity up, temperature decreasing to the right; main sequence, giants upper right, white dwarfs lower left.
  • Mass decides the end: low mass → planetary nebula + white dwarf; high mass → supernova + neutron star or black hole.

Mission 3

  • Parallax: p is half the yearly shift; d (pc) = 1/p (arcsec); 1 pc = 3.26 ly.
  • A star's radius: T from Wien's law, L = 4πd²b, then R = √(L/(4πσT⁴)).
