Mission 1

Mission 1: At the center of a charged ring the field is zero. Why not a little way along its axis?

Make a guess: At the center of a charged ring the field is zero. Why not a little way along its axis?

Quick check
?Predict first. A student uses E = (1/(4πε0))∫(dq/r²)r̂ to find the electric field at a point P on the perpendicular bisector of a uniformly charged, straight rod of length L, a short distance from the rod. Which step of the method is correct?
Mission 1

The method

  • Split the object into tiny pieces of charge dq. Each acts as a point charge.
  • dE = k dq r̂/r², pointing away from dq (for dq > 0).
  • Resolve dE into components. Integrate each component separately.
  • Use symmetry first: components that cancel in pairs need no integral.
  • Uniform line of charge: λ = Q/L, so dq = λ dx (or λR dθ along an arc).
  • Non-uniform λ(x): Q = ∫λ(x) dx.
Mission 1

Finite rod, point on its axis

  • Rod of length L, charge Q; P on the axis at distance d beyond the end. Let x = distance of a charge element dq = λ dx from P.
  • Every dE points the same way, so magnitudes add directly.
  • E = ∫ kλ dx/x², from d to d + L = kλ[1/d − 1/(d + L)] = kQ/(d(d + L)).
  • Far away (d ≫ L): → kQ/d², a point charge. ✓
  • Q = 4.0 nC, L = 0.20 m, d = 0.10 m: E = (9.0 × 10⁹)(4.0 × 10⁻⁹)/(0.10 × 0.30) = 1.2 × 10³ N/C.
  • A point charge at the rod's center (0.20 m away) would give only 9.0 × 10² N/C.
Quick check
?A thin rod of length L, with charge +Q spread uniformly along it, lies on the x-axis from x = 0 to x = L. The magnitude of the electric field at point P, at x = −L, is E1. The rod is replaced by a rod of length 2L, with the same charge +Q spread uniformly, lying from x = 0 to x = 2L. The magnitude of the field at P is now E2. What is the ratio E2/E1?
Mission 1

Ring, point on its axis

  • Ring radius R, charge Q; P on the axis, distance x from the center.
  • Every dq is the same distance √(x² + R²) from P.
  • Pieces on opposite sides: perpendicular components cancel; axial components add.
  • Axial part of each: k dq/(x² + R²) × x/√(x² + R²).
  • E = kQx/(x² + R²)3/2, along the axis.
  • At the center, x = 0 → E = 0: every piece is canceled by the opposite piece.
  • Small x: E ≈ kQx/R³, growing ∝ x. Far away (x ≫ R): → kQ/x². So E rises from zero, peaks, then falls.
Mission 1

Arc, point at its center

  • Every piece is distance R from the center. dq = λR dθ.
  • Measure θ from the arc's line of symmetry. Components along that line add; the others cancel in pairs.
  • E = ∫ (kλR dθ/R²) cos θ = (kλ/R)∫cos θ dθ.
  • Semicircle (θ from −π/2 to π/2): E = 2kλ/R = 2kQ/(πR²).
  • Full ring: E = 0 (∫cos θ dθ over 2π is zero).
  • Charge is not the whole story: the same Q on a full ring gives zero, on a semicircle 2kQ/(πR²).
Quick check
?A thin, insulating rod is bent into a semicircle of radius 0.15 m and carries charge +4.0 nC spread uniformly along its length. What is the magnitude of the electric field at the center of the semicircle? Use k = 9.0 × 10⁹ N·m²/C².
Mission 1

Rod's bisector and the infinite line

  • Rod length L, charge Q; P on the perpendicular bisector at distance y.
  • Components along the rod cancel in pairs; components away from the rod add.
  • E = ∫ kλy dx/(x² + y²)3/2, from −L/2 to L/2 = kQ/(y√(y² + L²/4)).
  • Infinite line (L → ∞, λ fixed): E = 2kλ/y = λ/(2πε0y). Falls as 1/y, not 1/y².
  • Same answer as Gauss's law, which needs no integral.
Quick check
?A thin rod of length L carries charge Q spread uniformly. Point P is on the rod's perpendicular bisector, a distance y = L/2 from the rod. What is the magnitude of the electric field at P, as a multiple of kQ/L²?
Mission check

?The figure shows three thin, insulating arcs of the same radius, each with charge spread uniformly over the parts labeled. E1, E2 and E3 are the magnitudes of the electric field at the centers of Arcs 1, 2 and 3. Which ranking is correct?

Fields of the pieces add as vectors. At a ring's center every piece is canceled by the opposite one. Along the axis the sideways parts still cancel, but the axial parts all point the same way and add.

Mission 2

Mission 2: A charge sits inside a closed box of any shape. Why is the total flux out of the box always the same?

Make a guess: A charge sits inside a closed box of any shape. Why is the total flux out of the box always the same?

Mission 2

Electric flux

  • Electric flux ΦE: a measure of how much field passes through a surface. Unit N·m²/C.
  • Flat surface, uniform field: ΦE = E·A = EA cos θ.
  • Area vector A: size = area, direction ⟂ to the surface.
  • θ = angle between E and A. Field straight through: θ = 0, Φ = EA. Field along the surface: θ = 90°, Φ = 0.
  • Example: E = 200 N/C, A = 0.050 m², θ = 60° → ΦE = 200 × 0.050 × 0.50 = 5.0 N·m²/C.
  • Closed surface: A points outward. Field leaving → positive flux; field entering → negative.
Quick check
?The figure shows edge views of three flat surfaces, S1, S2 and S3, in a uniform electric field; each surface extends the same distance into the page. Φ1, Φ2 and Φ3 are the magnitudes of the electric flux through the three surfaces. Which ranking is correct?
Mission 2

Flux as a surface integral

  • Curved surface or non-uniform field: split into small patches dA, then add.
  • ΦE = ∫E·dA. Closed surface: ∮E·dA.
  • Point charge q at the center of a sphere of radius r:
  • E is radial, so E ∥ dA everywhere; |E| = kq/r² is the same over the whole sphere.
  • ΦE = (kq/r²)(4πr²) = 4πkq = q/ε0.
  • r has canceled. Any sphere around q gives the same flux.
Quick check
?An open hemispherical surface of radius R, shaped like a bowl with no flat base, is in a uniform electric field of magnitude E. The field is parallel to the hemisphere's axis of symmetry. What is the magnitude of the electric flux through the hemispherical surface?
Mission 2

Gauss's law

  • ∮E·dA = qenc/ε0
  • Gaussian surface: any closed, imaginary 3D surface. It can pass through empty space.
  • qenc = net charge inside. Charges outside contribute zero net flux: every line that enters also leaves.
  • E in the integral is the total field, from charges inside and outside.
  • It holds for every closed surface. Symmetry is needed only to solve it for E.
  • Gauss's law is Maxwell's first equation.
Quick check
?A closed surface encloses two small beads with charges +2.0 nC and −6.0 nC. A third bead, with charge −3.0 nC, is outside the surface. What is the net electric flux through the closed surface? Use ε0 = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² C²/(N·m²).
Mission 2

Size and shape don't matter

  • Same qenc → same total flux, for a sphere, a cube or a lumpy bag.
  • Field-line picture: every line from q crosses any enclosing surface once (net). A bigger surface meets weaker field over more area.
  • +5.0 nC inside any closed surface: ΦE = 5.0 × 10⁻⁹ / 8.85 × 10⁻¹² = 565 N·m²/C.
  • Flux through part of a closed surface does depend on size and shape.
  • Long line with charge per unit length λ: a length ℓ inside the surface encloses q = λℓ.
Mission 2

Using Gauss's law: spherical symmetry

  • Choose a Gaussian surface where E is ∥ to dA with constant |E|, or ⟂ to dA (zero flux).
  • Spherical charge: choose a concentric sphere of radius r. Then ∮E·dA = E(4πr²).
  • E(4πr²) = qenc/ε0, so E = qenc/(4πε0r²).
  • Thin spherical shell, charge Q, radius R:
  • Outside (r > R): qenc = Q → E = kQ/r², as if all the charge were at the center.
  • Inside (r < R): qenc = 0 → E = 0.
  • Shell R = 0.10 m, Q = +5.0 nC: at r = 0.20 m, E = (9.0 × 10⁹)(5.0 × 10⁻⁹)/0.20² = 1.1 × 10³ N/C. At r = 0.05 m: 0.
Quick check
?A point charge +q is at the center of a thin, insulating spherical shell of radius R. The shell carries charge −3q spread uniformly over its surface. What are the magnitude and direction of the electric field at a distance r > R from the center?
Mission check

?An infinitely long line of charge with uniform linear charge density λ lies along the axis of a closed cylindrical Gaussian surface of radius r and length ℓ. The net electric flux through this surface is Φ1. The surface is then replaced by a closed coaxial cylinder of radius 3r and length 2ℓ, and the net electric flux through the new surface is Φ2. What is the ratio Φ2/Φ1?

Total flux depends only on qenc. The radius does not matter; doubling the length doubles the enclosed charge, λℓ → 2λℓ. A box around a point charge always gives q/ε0.

Mission 3: Inside a uniformly charged ball, does the field get stronger or weaker toward the center?

Make a guess: Inside a uniformly charged ball, does the field get stronger or weaker toward the center?

Mission 3

Choosing the Gaussian surface

  • Gauss's law always holds, but gives E only with symmetry.
  • Pick a surface where, on each part, E is either ∥ to dA with constant |E| or ⟂ to dA.
  • Spherical charge → concentric sphere.
  • Long line or cylinder → coaxial cylinder (curved side ∥, flat ends ⟂).
  • Large flat sheet → pillbox crossing the sheet (flat faces ∥, side ⟂).
  • No such symmetry (a cube, a short rod): Gauss's law is true but cannot give E alone.
Quick check
?For which of the following can Gauss's law, with a suitably chosen Gaussian surface, be used to calculate the magnitude of the electric field at the stated point?
Mission 3

Long line of charge

  • Charge per unit length λ. Gaussian cylinder: radius r, length ℓ.
  • Ends: E ⟂ dA → no flux. Curved side: flux E(2πrℓ).
  • E(2πrℓ) = λℓ/ε0 → E = λ/(2πε0r) = 2kλ/r. Falls as 1/r.
  • λ = 3.0 nC/m, r = 0.050 m: E = 2(9.0 × 10⁹)(3.0 × 10⁻⁹)/0.050 = 1.1 × 10³ N/C.
Mission 3

Sheets and parallel plates

  • Large sheet, charge per unit area σ. Pillbox, face area A, crossing the sheet.
  • Flux out of both faces: 2EA = σA/ε0 → E = σ/(2ε0), the same at any distance.
  • Two plates, +σ and −σ (σ = Q/A): fields add between them, cancel outside.
  • Between: E = σ/ε0 = Q/(ε0A). Outside: 0. E ∝ σ.
  • Q = 2.0 nC on plates of area 0.010 m²: σ = 2.0 × 10⁻⁷ C/m², E = 2.3 × 10⁴ N/C.
Quick check
?Two large, parallel metal plates, each of area A, are far from other objects, and their separation is much smaller than their dimensions. One plate carries a net charge +4Q and the other carries a net charge −Q. Which expression gives the magnitude of the electric field between the plates, away from their edges?
Mission 3

Charge from a charge density

  • λ (C/m) → dq = λ dx. σ (C/m²) → dq = σ dA. ρ (C/m³) → dq = ρ dV.
  • Uniform: Q = λL, σA or ρV. Non-uniform: integrate.
  • Use thin pieces matching the symmetry:
  • Disk: ring of radius r, dA = 2πr dr. Sphere: shell, dV = 4πr² dr. Cylinder: shell, dV = 2πrℓ dr.
  • Example: sphere of radius R, ρ = ρ0r/R.
  • Q = ∫0ᴿ (ρ0r/R)(4πr²) dr = (4πρ0/R)(R⁴/4) = πρ0R³.
Quick check
?A thin, flat, insulating disk of radius R carries a surface charge density that varies with distance r from its center as σ(r) = σ0(1 − r²/R²), where σ0 is a positive constant. What is the total charge on the disk?
Mission 3

Uniformly charged insulating sphere

  • Insulator: charge stays where it is put, spread through the volume.
  • Charge Q, radius R. Gaussian sphere radius r.
  • Inside (r < R): qenc = Q(r³/R³). E(4πr²) = qenc/ε0 → E = kQr/R³. Grows ∝ r.
  • Outside (r ≥ R): E = kQ/r².
  • E is zero at the center and greatest at the surface.
  • Moving in: you are surrounded by more charge, but the charge outside your Gaussian sphere gives no net field (shell result). Only qenc counts.
  • Metal sphere with the same Q: charge on the surface, E = 0 everywhere inside.
Quick check
?A plastic sphere of radius 0.10 m carries a charge of 4.0 nC spread uniformly through its volume. What is the magnitude of the electric field 0.050 m from its center? (k = 9.0 × 10⁹ N·m²/C²)
Mission 3

Non-uniform ρ(r)

  • qenc(r) = ∫0ʳ ρ(s) 4πs² ds, then E = qenc/(4πε0r²).
  • Example: ρ = ρ0r/R (r < R).
  • qenc = (4πρ0/R)(r⁴/4) = πρ0r⁴/R.
  • E = πρ0r⁴/(R · 4πε0r²) = ρ0r²/(4ε0R). Grows ∝ r².
  • Check at r = R: E = ρ0R/(4ε0). Outside formula kQ/R² with Q = πρ0R³ gives the same. ✓
  • Cylinder: use qenc = ∫0ʳ ρ(s) 2πsℓ ds and E(2πrℓ) = qenc/ε0.
Mission check

?A very long, solid, insulating cylinder of radius R has a uniform volume charge density. The magnitude of the electric field is E1 at a distance R/2 from the axis and E2 at a distance 2R from the axis. What is the ratio E2/E1?

Inside a uniform insulator only qenc counts, so E grows from zero at the center (∝ r in a sphere or cylinder) and is largest at the surface. For the ball: weaker toward the center, zero at it.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • E = k∫dq r̂/r²: resolve, use symmetry to cancel components, integrate what is left (dq = λ dx or λR dθ).
  • Results: rod axis kQ/(d(d + L)); ring axis kQx/(x² + R²)3/2; semicircle center 2kQ/(πR²); infinite line 2kλ/y.

Mission 2

  • ΦE = ∫E·dA (outward area vector on closed surfaces); Gauss's law ∮E·dA = qenc/ε0 holds for every closed surface.
  • Spherical symmetry: E(4πr²) = qenc/ε0; a charged shell has E = 0 inside and kQ/r² outside.

Mission 3

  • Line: E = λ/(2πε0r); sheet: σ/(2ε0); parallel plates: Q/(ε0A) between, 0 outside.
  • Non-uniform charge: Q = ∫ρ dV with shells; inside an insulator only qenc counts, so E grows from zero at the center.
