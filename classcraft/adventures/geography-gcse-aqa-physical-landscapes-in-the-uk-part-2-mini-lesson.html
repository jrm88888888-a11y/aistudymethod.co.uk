Mission 1

Britain under ice

  • The last British–Irish Ice Sheet was at its maximum around 27,000 years ago. The timing varied from place to place, between about 30,000 and 22,000 years ago.
  • It stretched from the edge of the continental shelf in the north to South Wales, north Norfolk and the Vale of York.
  • Scotland, Northern Ireland, most of Wales and northern England lay under the ice. Most of southern England lay beyond it.
  • The ice melted away, but it left the UK's uplands carved and its lowlands covered in its deposits.
Quick check
?At the last glacial maximum, which of these places lay beyond the edge of the ice sheet?
Mission 1

Freeze-thaw weathering

  • Water seeps into cracks in rock.
  • When the water freezes it expands, pushing the crack wider. When the ice thaws, water seeps deeper into the crack.
  • Repeated freezing and thawing widens the crack until angular pieces break off.
  • On cold, bare upland slopes this supplies much of the rock that glaciers carry.
Mission 1

Glacial erosion: plucking and abrasion

  • Plucking: meltwater freezes onto rock at the bed; as the ice moves on, it tears blocks away. "As the ice moves, it plucks rock from the sides and bottom of the valleys".
  • Abrasion: rocks held in the base of the ice scrape the bedrock like sandpaper, leaving scratches called striations.
  • Plucking leaves rough, jagged rock; abrasion leaves smooth, polished rock.
Quick check
?A rock surface is smooth and polished, with long parallel scratches. Which process formed it?
Mission 1

How ice moves

  • Ice flows downhill under its own weight.
  • "Glaciers move by internal deformation of the ice, and by sliding over the rocks and sediments at the base".
  • "A glacier may slide on a thin layer of water at its base". This basal sliding makes erosion strong.
Mission 1

Rotational slip and bulldozing

  • Rotational slip: in a hollow high on a mountain, ice moves in a curved, rotating path, like a spoon scooping.
  • It deepens the hollow's floor by abrasion and steepens its back wall by plucking.
  • Bulldozing: at the front (the snout), an advancing glacier pushes loose rock and soil ahead of it, like a bulldozer.
Mission 1

Moving forward, melting back

  • Snow falls high up, builds into ice, and the ice flows downhill.
  • At the snout, ice melts. If more ice melts than arrives, the snout retreats up the valley.
  • NSIDC: "Even as it retreats, the glacier still deforms and moves down slope, like a conveyor belt".
  • Retreat describes where the snout is, not which way the ice flows.
Mission 1

Transport

  • Glaciers carry rock on the ice (from freeze-thaw and rockfall on the valley sides), within it, and beneath it.
  • The ice carries everything from clay to huge boulders, then drops it "at the far edge of the glacier where the ice is melting".
Mission 1

Deposition: till and outwash

  • Glaciers deposit when the ice melts and can no longer carry its load.
  • Till: dropped directly by the ice. Unsorted: angular rocks of all sizes mixed together. In west Cumbria the tills are "extremely compact and stony" and "up to 10 m thick".
  • Outwash: carried away by meltwater streams, then deposited as the water slows. Sorted into layers, with rounded stones.
  • Example: "Extensive deposits of glaciofluvial sand and gravel were laid down in the Tyne Gap and Vale of Eden".
Quick check
?A pit in a lowland shows layers of rounded gravel and sand, each layer a similar grain size. How was it most likely deposited?
Mission check

?Scientists drill a line of stakes into a glacier. Every year the stakes move several metres downhill, yet every year the snout is further up the valley. How can both be true?

A glacier is a moving store of ice. "A retreating glacier does not flow uphill; it simply melts faster than it flows". Muir Glacier's ice kept flowing towards the sea while its snout retreated 12 km.

Mission 2

Reading a glaciated upland

  • In glaciated mountains: "Massive U-shaped valleys, cirque lakes or tarns, horns, moraines, and arêtes".
  • Erosional landforms dominate the high ground, where the ice formed and was most powerful.
  • Depositional landforms lie lower down, where the ice melted and dropped its load.
  • Each landform is evidence of a process: read it like a clue.
Mission 2

Corries and tarns

  • Corrie (cirque): a deep, armchair-shaped hollow at the head of a glacier, "like an ice cream scoop carving out a chunk of mountain".
  • Ice turns in a rotational movement: plucking steepens the back wall, abrasion deepens the floor, and a rock lip is left at the front.
  • After the ice melts, a small lake, a tarn, may fill the hollow. Red Tarn, below Helvellyn in the Lake District, is 25.6 m deep.
Mission 2

Arêtes and pyramidal peaks

  • Arête: a knife-edged ridge between two corries cut back towards each other. At Helvellyn, Striding Edge and Swirral Edge separate the corries.
  • Pyramidal peak: a peak "scraped nearly vertical by glaciers on three or more sides".
  • Both form as corries erode backwards into the mountain.
Quick check
?Three corries erode back into the same mountain from different sides. Which landform is left at the top?
Mission 2

Glacial troughs, truncated spurs and hanging valleys

  • Glacial trough: a glacier scours a V-shaped river valley "into a U-shape": steep sides, wide flat floor.
  • Truncated spurs: the ice cuts off the ends of the river's interlocking spurs, leaving cliffs along the trough sides.
  • Hanging valley: a smaller tributary glacier cuts less deeply, so its valley ends high above the trough, often with a waterfall. Cwm Idwal in Snowdonia is "a striking example of a glacial hanging valley".
Mission 2

Ribbon lakes

  • A ribbon lake is a long, narrow lake in the floor of a glacial trough.
  • Ice eroded weaker rock more deeply, leaving basins behind bars of harder rock.
  • Windermere, "the largest natural lake in England", covers 14.8 km². A shallow sill divides it into two basins, 64 m and 42 m deep.
Mission 2

Moraines

  • Moraine: till (unsorted rock debris) deposited by a glacier.
  • Terminal moraine: a ridge across the valley at the snout's furthest point. "The longer a glacier stays in one spot … the larger this moraine will be".
  • Ground moraine: an uneven sheet of till spread across the valley floor as the ice melts back.
  • Lateral moraine: a ridge along the valley side, from debris falling onto the glacier's edge. Medial moraine: a ridge down the middle, where two lateral moraines join as glaciers meet.
  • Recessional moraines: smaller ridges across the valley, marking pauses as the ice retreated.
Quick check
?A curved ridge of unsorted boulders, sand and clay crosses a valley floor. It is the lowest of several such ridges down the valley. What is it most likely to be?
Mission 2

Drumlins

  • Drumlin: a smooth, oval hill of till, shaped beneath moving ice; often found in groups, a "basket of eggs".
  • Moving ice deposits till, then moulds and streamlines it as it keeps moving: deposition and erosion working together.
  • The blunt, steep end faces up-ice; the long, tapered end points the way the ice flowed.
  • The Vale of Eden, Cumbria, has elongate drumlins; their shape shows that ice "flowed down the Vale of Eden".
Quick check
?In a field of drumlins, every hill has its steep, blunt end facing north-west and its tapered end facing south-east. Which way did the ice flow?
Mission 2

Erratics

  • An erratic is a rock carried by ice and dropped far from its source, often on a different type of rock.
  • Big Rock, Okotoks, Alberta: a 16,500-tonne quartzite boulder, about 9 m tall.
  • It came from a mountain formation in what is now Jasper National Park. A rockslide dropped it onto a glacier, which carried it to the plains between 10,000 and 30,000 years ago.
  • Erratics show which way the ice moved, and how far.
Mission check

?At Norber, in the Yorkshire Dales, hundreds of large, angular sandstone and siltstone boulders sit on limestone, more than 1 km from their source rock and more than 120 m above it. What is the best explanation?

The Norber boulders are erratics. Ice moved them "for more than 1 km and more than 120 m above their source outcrop". Big Rock is the same: a glacier carried it out of the mountains and left it on the prairie as the ice melted.

Mission 3

The Lake District: where and why it matters

  • North-west England, in Cumbria. England's largest National Park and a UNESCO World Heritage Site; 2,362 km².
  • Scafell Pike (978 m), England's highest mountain, is here; Wastwater (74 m) is England's deepest lake.
  • The fells and lakes "radiate outwards" from Dunmail Raise, like the spokes of a wheel.
Mission 3

The rocks

  • Skiddaw Group (north, about 500 million years old): sea muds, "crumpled and squeezed"; mostly smooth mountains.
  • Borrowdale Volcanics (centre, about 450 million years old): "very hard lavas and ashes" from eruptions. They form the "highest and craggiest mountains: Scafell, Helvellyn and the Langdale Pikes".
  • These peaks are not old volcanoes: they are "the harder volcanic rocks that have withstood erosion".
  • Windermere Group (south): softer sedimentary rocks, gentler scenery.
Mission 3

How the ice came

  • About 2 million years ago Earth's climate cooled; glaciers and ice sheets "covered most of mainland Britain".
  • Up to 10 cold glacials alternated with warmer interglacials.
  • The last British–Irish ice sheet was at its biggest about 27,000 years ago. The last Ice Age ended about 10,000 years ago.
  • After the ice sheet melted, small glaciers returned to Helvellyn's hollows in a short cold spell, the Loch Lomond Stadial.
Mission 3

Erosional landforms at Helvellyn

The Lake District National Park's list of features around Helvellyn:

  • Corrie (local name "cove"): the east face of Helvellyn. There are nine corries on the eastern slopes; Nethermost Cove has a headwall 280 m high.
  • Tarn: Red Tarn, 9.8 ha and 25.6 m deep, fills a corrie.
  • Arête: Striding Edge and Swirral Edge, the ridges between corries.
  • Glacial trough: Grisedale. Ribbon lake: Ullswater.
  • U-shaped valley: Honister, "carved out of volcanic rock by glacial action".
Mission 3

How a corrie forms

  • Snow collects in a north- or east-facing hollow and turns to ice.
  • Plucking: ice freezes onto rock and tears it out as it moves.
  • Abrasion: rocks in the ice scrape the floor deeper.
  • The ice turns in a rotational slide, deepening the back and leaving a lip at the front.
  • When two corries cut back towards each other, the ridge between them becomes an arête.
Quick check
?At Helvellyn, which landform is Striding Edge?
Mission 3

Depositional landforms

  • Moraine: the lip of Red Tarn, debris dumped at the front of the corrie glacier.
  • Till: red, "extremely compact and stony" glacial deposits, up to 10 m thick, in north-west England.
  • Drumlins: elongated hills of till in the Vale of Eden and around the north of the Lake District.
  • Glaciofluvial sand and gravel, laid down by meltwater in the Vale of Eden.
Quick check
?Which pair gives one erosional and one depositional landform from the Lake District?
Mission check

?A walker in Snowdonia finds a deep, armchair-shaped hollow facing north-east, with a steep back wall, a small lake, and a low ridge of jumbled boulders holding the lake in. What best explains it?

The hollows around Helvellyn are corries. Small glaciers plucked and scraped them out of hard volcanic rock, left knife-edge arêtes between them, and dumped moraines at their lips.

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA GCSE Geography (8035)

  • 3.1.3.4 overview of economic activities in glaciated uplands: tourism, farming, forestry, quarrying
  • 3.1.3.4 conflicts between land uses, and between development and conservation
Mission 4

A glaciated upland that people use

  • The Lake District National Park in Cumbria covers 2,362 km². Ice carved its corries, troughs and ribbon lakes.
  • 38,993 people live there (Census 2021).
  • 17.45 million tourists visited in 2025.
  • It is a UNESCO World Heritage Site: "an unrivalled example of a northern European upland agro-pastoral system" (sheep and cattle farming).
Mission 4

Four ways to earn a living

  • Tourism: visitors spent £1.784 billion directly in 2025.
  • Farming: centuries of upland sheep farming, including the Herdwick breed. Thin soils, steep slopes and heavy rain suit grazing, not crops.
  • Forestry: woodland covers 13% of the park; 95 km² is owned or managed by the Forestry Commission.
  • Quarrying: slate has long been dug here. Honister Slate Mine sells slate and now also mine tours, a via ferrata and a zip wire.
Quick check
?Honister Slate Mine now runs mine tours, a via ferrata and a zip wire. What does this show?
Mission 4

When land uses clash

  • Tourism v. local people: demand for holiday homes pushes up house prices. In 2011, 24% of homes were holiday or second homes.
  • Tourism v. farming: walkers, dogs and cars share narrow lanes and grazing land with sheep and farm traffic.
  • Tourism v. jobs: tourism jobs are often seasonal and low-paid.
  • Traffic: visitors bring congestion, parking problems and litter.
Mission 4

Development v. conservation

  • UNESCO lists the risks: "development pressures, changing agricultural practices and diseases, and tourism".
  • Windermere: fast powerboats and water-skiing clashed with quiet enjoyment and wildlife. A 10 mph speed limit came into force in 2005.
  • Honister: new adventure attractions bring jobs, but add structures and visitors high on the fells.
  • The question is always the same: does new development (jobs, homes, attractions) harm what makes the place worth conserving?
Quick check
?Which is a conflict between development and conservation?
Mission 4

Managing visitors

  • Rules: the Windermere 10 mph speed limit (2005) cut noise and wash from fast boats.
  • Protecting habitats: some lakeshore has been cordoned off so plants can regrow.
  • Spreading visitors out: car parks, buses and lake boats steer people away from overloaded spots.
  • Education: signs and rangers explain why sheep, walls and paths need care.
Mission 4

Development: pros and cons

  • Water storage: Haweswater is a reservoir. It drowned the village of Mardale, which reappeared when the reservoir was 89% empty in the 1995 drought.
  • Renewable energy: high rainfall and steep streams suit hydro-electric power; pipes, buildings and power lines change a protected view.
  • Tourism: £1.784 billion of spending, but erosion and congestion.
  • Conservation: woodland planting and path repair change the landscape too, slowly and on purpose.
Mission check

?A company wants to reopen a closed slate quarry inside a national park. It would create jobs, but lorries would use narrow valley roads used by walkers and farm traffic. Which statement best describes the issue?

The Lake District has 38,993 residents but 17.45 million tourists a year. Every use (farming, forestry, quarrying, tourism, water) competes for the same land, so the answer is managed sharing: rules, repair and planning decisions that weigh development against conservation.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Over 17 million tourists visited the Lake District in 2025. What do all those visitors do to it?

Make a guess: Over 17 million tourists visited the Lake District in 2025. What do all those visitors do to it?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA GCSE Geography (8035)

  • 3.1.3.4 an example of a glaciated upland area in the UK used for tourism: attractions, social/economic/environmental impacts, management strategies
Mission 5

A glaciated upland that people visit

  • The Lake District in Cumbria is England's largest National Park and a UNESCO World Heritage Site (since 2017).
  • Ice shaped it: corries, U-shaped troughs and ribbon lakes sit among over 350 fells.
  • It holds England's highest mountain (Scafell Pike, 978 m), deepest lake (Wastwater, 74 m) and longest lake (Windermere, 17 km).
Mission 5

Why people come: the attractions

  • Scenery and quiet: most visitors come for the scenery, peace and quiet, and walking.
  • Activities: walking, climbing, sailing, lake cruises; exploring towns and villages.
  • Heritage: the landscape "inspired writers and artists", including the poet William Wordsworth.
  • Access: the railway reached Windermere in 1847; car ownership from the 1960s brought "ever increasing numbers".
Quick check
?Which of these is an attraction that comes directly from glaciation?
Mission 5

Economic impacts

  • 17.45 million tourists came in 2025.
  • They spent £1.784 billion directly; the total economic impact was £2.396 billion.
  • Tourism is "the main source of income" for the Lake District economy.
  • Spending also supports other businesses, such as wholesalers and the building trade: a multiplier effect.
  • Downside: shops "cater for the requirements of tourists not local people".
Mission 5

Social impacts

  • Only 38,993 people live in the National Park: about 450 tourists per resident each year (our calculation).
  • Holiday homes: 24% of homes were holiday or second homes in 2011; demand "pushes up house prices for local people".
  • Pressure on services: sewage systems, roads and facilities.
  • Benefit: services built for tourists, such as public transport and roads, also serve local people.
Mission 5

Environmental impacts

  • Footpath erosion: "millions of pairs of walking boots, the weather and gradient" wear scars into the fells.
  • Lakeshore erosion at Windermere: trampling destroys plants, soil washes away, and "only a stony beach remains".
  • Traffic congestion and litter.
Quick check
?Which impact is social?
Mission 5

Honeypot sites

  • A honeypot site draws very large numbers of visitors to one small area, like bees to honey.
  • Windermere is one: in 2022 Windermere Lake Cruises carried over 1.3 million people.
  • It was the 4th most popular paid-for attraction in England and the most popular in the North West.
  • Honeypots concentrate both the money and the damage.
Mission 5

Managing the impacts

  • Repair paths: Fix the Fells looks after 344 upland paths (661 km); in 2025, 147 volunteers gave 3,206 days.
  • Protect lakeshores: some Windermere shore has been cordoned off to let habitats regrow.
  • Rules on the water: a 10 mph speed limit on Windermere since 2005 lets sailing boats and kayaks use it safely. It was controversial: many businesses "had to diversify".
  • Routes for all: Miles without Stiles are graded, stile-free paths for wheelchairs, pushchairs and anyone wanting an easier walk.
  • Better services: the Park Authority aims for better information, public transport and toilets.
Quick check
?Which strategy is aimed most directly at footpath erosion on the high fells?
Mission check

?In another UK glaciated upland, a valley village fills with cars on summer weekends. Verges are churned up and residents cannot get their own cars out. Which response best tackles the cause while keeping the visitors' spending?

Tourism brings the Lake District £1.784 billion of direct spending but also erosion, traffic and housing pressure. Good management keeps the benefits and spreads or reduces the pressure: repaired paths, protected shores, rules, accessible routes and public transport.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The last ice sheet reached South Wales, north Norfolk and the Vale of York around 27,000 years ago. Ice erodes by plucking and abrasion, helped by freeze-thaw, and deposits till (unsorted) and outwash (sorted by meltwater).
  • Ice moves by rotational slip in hollows and bulldozes debris at the snout.

Mission 2

  • Erosion landforms: corries, tarns, arêtes, pyramidal peaks, troughs, truncated spurs, hanging valleys, ribbon lakes. Deposition: moraines (lateral, medial, terminal, ground), drumlins and erratics.

Mission 3

  • Lake District: hard Borrowdale volcanic rocks make the highest, craggiest fells; Skiddaw Group rocks make smoother hills.
  • Erosional landforms at Helvellyn: corries (east face), Red Tarn, Striding and Swirral Edge (arêtes), Grisedale (trough), Ullswater (ribbon lake). Depositional: moraine at Red Tarn's lip; drumlins and till in the Vale of Eden.

Mission 4

  • Glaciated uplands support tourism, farming, forestry and quarrying; these uses conflict with each other and with conservation.

Mission 5

  • The Lake District attracts 17.45 million tourists with glacial scenery, activities and heritage.
  • Impacts are economic (spending, jobs), social (holiday homes, services) and environmental (path and lakeshore erosion, traffic).
  • Exam move: in "Evaluate" or "To what extent" answers, judge each strategy against the impact it targets (e.g. Fix the Fells v. footpath erosion) and name a drawback (the 10 mph limit forced some businesses to diversify).
