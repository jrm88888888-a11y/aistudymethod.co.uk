Mission 1

Measuring how strongly buyers react

  • A price rises 10%. Do buyers cut back by 1%? Or by 30%?
  • Price elasticity of demand (PED) measures how responsive quantity demanded is to a change in the good's own price.
  • PED = %ΔQd ÷ %ΔP
  • Percentages, not units, so we can compare goods measured in litres, tickets or tonnes.
  • Price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions, so PED is negative.
  • Your course quotes PED with its minus sign (e.g. −0.4). Judge "elastic" or "inelastic" by its size, ignoring the sign.
Mission 1

Worked example

  • Illustrative data. A café raises the price of a coffee from £2.50 to £3.00. Sales fall from 400 to 340 cups a day.
  • %ΔP = (3.00 − 2.50) ÷ 2.50 × 100 = +20%
  • %ΔQd = (340 − 400) ÷ 400 × 100 = −15%
  • PED = −15 ÷ 20 = −0.75. Size below 1: demand is price inelastic.
  • Use PED to predict: a further 8% rise → %ΔQd = −0.75 × 8 = −6%.
  • Or work back: to cut sales by 9%, %ΔP = −9 ÷ −0.75 = +12%.
Quick check

Mission 1

Slope is not elasticity

  • Illustrative data. Straight-line demand: P = 10 − Q. Every 1 off the price adds 1 to quantity. The slope never changes.
  • Price 8 → 7 (−12.5%): Q 2 → 3 (+50%). PED = −4. Elastic.
  • Price 3 → 2 (−33.3%): Q 7 → 8 (+14.3%). PED = −0.43. Inelastic.
  • Same 1-unit changes, very different percentages.
  • Point PED = (ΔQ ÷ ΔP) × (P ÷ Q). At P = 5, Q = 5: PED = −1 × 5 ÷ 5 = −1.
  • Upper half: elastic. Midpoint: unitary. Lower half: inelastic.
  • Say: "demand is relatively inelastic over this price range" — not: "the curve is steep, so demand is inelastic".

Mission 1

What makes demand elastic or inelastic?

  • Substitutes: more and closer substitutes → more elastic. Buyers can switch.
  • Necessity or luxury: necessities and habit-forming goods → less elastic.
  • Share of income: a small share of the budget (salt, matches) → less elastic.
  • Time: buyers find substitutes over time, so demand is more elastic in the long run.
  • How widely the market is defined: one brand of bread has many substitutes; "food" has none. Narrow → more elastic.
  • Say: "demand for petrol is price inelastic" — not: "petrol is inelastic".
Mission 1

Real evidence: US gasoline

  • Real data (Hughes, Knittel and Sperling, 2008). Short-run PED for gasoline (petrol) in the US:
  • 1975–1980: −0.21 to −0.34
  • 2001–2006: −0.034 to −0.077
  • In 2001–2006, a 10% price rise cut gasoline use by less than 1% in the short run.
  • Both periods: inelastic. But demand became less elastic.
  • Think: which determinants on the previous screen might explain it? (How easy is it to switch away from driving? How big a share of income is fuel?)
  • Long run: buyers can change cars, homes and jobs, so demand is more elastic than in the short run.

Mission 2

Revenue is the buyers' spending

  • Total revenue (TR) = price × quantity sold.
  • What sellers receive, buyers spend: TR = total expenditure.
  • On a demand curve, TR is the rectangle under the chosen point: height P, width Q.
  • Example (illustrative data): 1,000 tickets at £4 → TR = £4,000.
Mission 2

Two effects pull in opposite directions

  • Raise the price and two things happen:
  • Price effect: each unit still sold earns more → TR up.
  • Quantity effect: fewer units are sold → TR down.
  • Which effect is bigger? Compare the percentage changes.
  • That comparison is exactly what PED measures.
Mission 2

The revenue rule

Unitary (unit) elasticequal to 1TR unchangedTR unchanged
Price inelasticless than 1TR risesTR falls
  • Inelastic: price and TR move the same way.
  • Elastic: price and TR move opposite ways.
  • PED is negative (e.g. −0.4). Judge elastic or inelastic by its size, ignoring the sign.
  • Say: "demand is price inelastic, so a price rise increases total revenue" — not: "a price rise always increases revenue".
Mission 2

Worked example: from PED to revenue

  • Illustrative data. A bus company sells 1,000 tickets a day at £4. PED = −0.4.
  • It raises the fare to £5.
  • %ΔP = (5 − 4) ÷ 4 × 100 = +25%.
  • %ΔQd = PED × %ΔP = −0.4 × 25 = −10% → 1,000 → 900 tickets.
  • TR before: £4 × 1,000 = £4,000. TR after: £5 × 900 = £4,500.
  • TR rises by £500 (+12.5%). Demand is inelastic, so price and TR move the same way.
  • Check: if PED were −2, Qd would fall 50% to 500, and TR would fall to £2,500.

Mission 2

Revenue diagrams: gain and loss areas

  • Price rises from P1 to P2. Compare two rectangles:
  • Gain: (P2 − P1) × Q2, the extra paid on units still sold.
  • Loss: P1 × (Q1 − Q2), revenue on the units no longer sold.
  • Inelastic demand (steep over this range): small fall in Q → gain > loss → TR rises.
  • Elastic demand (flat over this range): big fall in Q → loss > gain → TR falls.
  • From an earlier screen (illustrative data): gain £1 × 900 = £900; loss £4 × 100 = £400; TR up £500.
  • Say: "demand is relatively inelastic over this price range" — steepness alone is not elasticity.

Mission 2

Along a straight-line demand curve

  • On a straight-line demand curve, PED changes along the line (from Mission 1).
  • Top half: elastic. Cutting price raises TR.
  • Bottom half: inelastic. Cutting price lowers TR.
  • Midpoint: unit elastic. TR is at its maximum.
  • Illustrative data, P = 10 − Q: TR = 16 at P = 8, 25 at P = 5, 16 at P = 2.

Mission 2

Why firms care

  • Inelastic demand (few close substitutes, a necessity, brand loyalty): a price rise raises TR.
  • Elastic demand (many substitutes): price cuts and discounts can raise TR.
  • Firms try to make demand less elastic: branding, advertising, loyalty schemes.
  • Firms with several groups of buyers charge more where demand is inelastic (e.g. peak travel) and less where it is elastic (e.g. student tickets).
  • Revenue is not profit. Selling fewer units also cuts total costs, so with inelastic demand a price rise usually raises profit too.
  • Say: "total revenue" — not: "profit", when you apply the revenue rule.
Mission 2

Why governments care

  • Indirect taxes raise most revenue on goods with inelastic demand: the quantity bought falls little, so the tax base stays large.
  • Examples: tobacco, alcohol, fuel.
  • Real data: the average PED for tobacco products in high-income countries is about −0.4 (IARC, 2011). So a 10% price rise cuts consumption by about 4%.
  • So a tax on an inelastic good raises revenue but cuts consumption only a little.
  • For a given supply curve, the more inelastic demand is, the larger the share of the tax consumers bear.
  • Subsidies raise consumption most where demand is elastic (e.g. cheaper public transport fares win more passengers).
Quick check
?A government wants a new indirect tax on a product to do two things: raise a large amount of tax revenue and cut consumption sharply. Demand for the product has a PED of −0.3. What should its advisers conclude?
Mission 2Evaluate

How useful is PED for decisions?

It depends on …

  • Accuracy of the estimate: PED comes from past data, assuming other things stay the same. Incomes, tastes and rivals' prices change.
  • Time: demand is usually more elastic in the long run, as buyers find substitutes. A tax that raises revenue now may raise less later.
  • Size of the change and where on the curve: PED differs along a demand curve. A small-change estimate may not hold for a big price rise.
  • Firm vs market: one firm's demand is more price elastic than the whole market's if rivals do not follow its price rise.
  • Unintended consequences: high taxes on inelastic goods can encourage smuggling and illegal markets.
  • Judge: PED is a useful guide to the direction of the revenue change. Firms and governments should treat its exact size with caution.

A price cut raises revenue only when the quantity bought rises by a bigger percentage than the price falls.

Mission 3

Elasticity for any cause of demand

  • PED measures how quantity demanded responds to a good's own price.
  • The same idea works for any determinant of demand.
  • Income elasticity of demand (YED) = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in income.
  • Cross elasticity of demand (XED) = % change in quantity demanded of good A ÷ % change in the price of good B.
  • A change in a determinant other than the good's own price shifts the demand curve.
Mission 3

Normal and inferior goods

  • The sign of YED tells you the type of good.
  • YED > 0: normal good. Income rises → demand rises (shifts right). E.g. clothes, holidays, eating out.
  • YED < 0: inferior good. Income rises → demand falls (shifts left). E.g. supermarket value ranges, instant noodles.
  • Why? With more income, people switch to alternatives they prefer.
  • The same good can be normal for one group and inferior for another.
  • Say: "inferior" means demand falls as income rises — not: poor quality.
Mission 3Graph lab

Average incomes rise 10%. Demand for a value-range pasta has a YED of −0.5.

  • Takeaway: a rise in income shifts demand for a normal good right and for an inferior good left.
Mission 3

Necessities and luxuries

  • The size of YED shows how strongly demand responds.
  • 0 < YED < 1: income inelastic. A necessity: demand rises, but by a smaller % than income. E.g. bread, electricity.
  • YED > 1: income elastic. A luxury: demand rises by a bigger % than income. E.g. foreign holidays, restaurant meals.
  • Necessities and luxuries are both normal goods.
  • Say: "income inelastic" — not: "inferior". YED of 0.3 is a normal good.
Mission 3

Worked example: calculating YED

  • Illustrative data: weekly household income rises from 500 to 520.
  • Instant noodles bought each week fall from 20 packs to 18.
  • % change in income = (520 − 500) ÷ 500 × 100 = +4%.
  • % change in quantity demanded = (18 − 20) ÷ 20 × 100 = −10%.
  • YED = −10 ÷ +4 = −2.5.
  • Negative: an inferior good. Strongly responsive (size 2.5).
  • Second good: income +4%, restaurant meals +10%. YED = 10 ÷ 4 = +2.5: a luxury.
  • Always put quantity on top.

Mission 3

Cross elasticity: related goods

  • XED = % change in Qd of good A ÷ % change in price of good B.
  • XED > 0: substitutes. Price of coffee rises → demand for tea rises.
  • XED < 0: complements. Price of games consoles falls → demand for games rises.
  • XED = 0 (or close): unrelated. E.g. pens and bus fares.
  • Size shows how close the link is: +3 means close substitutes; +0.2 means weak substitutes.
  • Say: "a rise in the price of B shifts the demand curve for A" — not: "a movement along A's demand curve".

Mission 3

Worked example: calculating XED

  • Illustrative data.
  • Price of coffee rises 10%. Quantity of tea demanded rises 5%.
  • XED = +5 ÷ +10 = +0.5. Positive: substitutes (weak ones).
  • Price of consoles falls 20%. Quantity of games demanded rises 30%.
  • XED = +30 ÷ −20 = −1.5. Negative: complements (strong ones).
  • Signs matter: write the % change in price with its sign.
Quick check

Mission 3

What makes the values large or small?

  • YED:
    • Necessity or luxury? Essentials have low YED.
    • Income level: a car may be a luxury in a low-income country and a necessity in a rich one.
    • Better alternatives: if a preferred option exists, the good may be inferior.
  • XED: how close are the goods in use? Close substitutes or tight complements give large values.
  • XED: brand loyalty weakens the link between substitutes.
  • Time: responses grow larger as buyers have longer to adjust their habits.
Mission 3

Why these numbers matter

  • Firms use YED to forecast sales as incomes grow or fall.
    • Luxury sellers expect big swings over the economic cycle; necessity sellers are steadier.
    • In a recession, sales of inferior goods may rise: shops push their value ranges.
    • A mix of high- and low-YED products spreads the risk.
  • Firms use XED to predict the effect of a rival's price cut (substitutes) and to price complements: cheap printer, dear ink.
  • Governments: YED helps forecast tax revenue as incomes grow. XED shows how a tax on one good shifts demand to its substitutes.
Mission 3Evaluate

How far can firms rely on elasticity estimates?

  • It depends on how the estimate was made: values come from past data; tastes and incomes change.
  • It depends on other things being equal: in reality prices, incomes and tastes change together.
  • It depends on the size of the change: values are reliable only for small changes.
  • It depends on who the buyers are: the same good has different YED for poorer and richer households.
  • Time lags: buyers take time to switch, so short-run values understate the long-run effect.

Mission 4

What PES measures

  • Price elasticity of supply (PES): how strongly quantity supplied responds to a change in price.
  • PES = %ΔQs ÷ %ΔP
  • Price up → quantity supplied up. So PES is normally positive.
  • A bigger number = a stronger response.
  • Say: "a rise in price causes an extension of supply" — not: "supply increases" (that is a shift).
Mission 4

Mission 4

Straight-line supply curves

  • A straight supply line through the origin: PES = 1 at every point, whatever its slope.
  • A straight line that starts on the price axis: PES > 1 (elastic) all along it.
  • A straight line that starts on the quantity axis: PES < 1 (inelastic) all along it.
  • So slope alone does not tell you PES. Check where the line meets the axes.
  • Say: "relatively elastic supply" for a line cutting the price axis — not: "elastic because it is flat".

Mission 4

Worked example

  • Illustrative data. A furniture maker supplies 400 tables a month at a price of £20.
  • The price rises to £25. Quantity supplied rises to 450.
  • %ΔQs = (450 − 400) ÷ 400 × 100 = +12.5%.
  • %ΔP = (25 − 20) ÷ 20 × 100 = +25%.
  • PES = 12.5 ÷ 25 = 0.5 → inelastic.
  • Working backwards (illustrative data): PES = 1.6 and price rises 5% → %ΔQs = 1.6 × 5 = +8%.
Quick check

Mission 4

What makes supply elastic?

  • Time: the longer firms have to respond, the more elastic supply is.
  • Spare capacity: idle machines and staff → output can rise fast.
  • Stocks and storage: goods held in stock, or that can be stored, can be released quickly.
  • Mobility of factors: workers, machines and land that can switch from other uses → more elastic.
Mission 4

Short run vs long run

  • Momentary (very short) run: output is fixed. Supply is vertical (PES = 0). E.g. fish already landed today.
  • Short run: firms use spare capacity, overtime and stocks. Supply is inelastic.
  • Long run: firms build capacity, and new firms enter. Supply becomes more elastic.
  • The same rise in demand → in the short run mostly a price rise; in the long run more of a quantity rise.

Mission 4

Real world: cocoa, 2024

  • Cocoa beans come mainly from West Africa.
  • Poor West African harvests in the 2023–24 season cut supply.
  • In March 2024, cocoa futures in New York passed $10,000 a metric ton for the first time. Prices had roughly doubled since January.
  • Why could other growers not fill the gap? Newly planted cocoa trees take a few years to bear beans. In the short run, PES is very low.
  • Long-run response: growers elsewhere (e.g. Brazil, Ecuador) are working to expand production.
Mission 4Evaluate

How useful is PES for decisions?

  • Firms: knowing PES shows whether they can profit from a demand surge. Keeping stocks and spare capacity raises PES.
  • Government: the more inelastic supply is, the larger the share of a tax producers bear. A subsidy to raise output works only if supply can respond.
  • Policy example: if housing supply is inelastic, measures that raise demand mostly push up prices. Measures that make supply more elastic (e.g. faster planning) raise quantity.

It depends on …

  • Time horizon: short-run and long-run PES can be very different.
  • Spare capacity in the economy: near full capacity, supply is less elastic than in a recession.
  • Accuracy: PES estimates come from past data. Costs, technology and regulation change.

Mission 5

A tax on spending

  • An indirect tax is a tax on spending on goods and services.
  • The seller hands the tax to the government. Buyers pay it only through the price.
  • Examples: VAT and sales taxes; duties on fuel, alcohol and tobacco.
  • To the seller, the tax is like an extra cost of each unit sold.
Mission 5

Specific or ad valorem?

  • Specific tax: a fixed amount per unit (e.g. per litre of fuel).
    • S shifts up by the same amount at every quantity: parallel.
  • Ad valorem tax: a percentage of the price (e.g. VAT).
    • The tax per unit grows as the price rises: the new curve pivots, diverging from S.
  • Example (illustrative data), a 20% tax on the pre-tax price:
    • pre-tax price £5 → tax £1
    • pre-tax price £10 → tax £2
Quick check
?A government charges a tax of 15% on the pre-tax price of every phone sold. How does it change the supply curve?
Mission 5

The tax diagram

  • Build it in steps (a tax of t per unit):
  • 1. Start at equilibrium: Pe, Qe.
  • 2. Supply shifts up by the tax: S → S + tax. Vertical gap = t.
  • 3. New equilibrium: price buyers pay rises to P1; quantity falls to Q1.
  • Sellers keep P1 − t per unit.
  • The price rises by less than the tax (unless demand is perfectly inelastic or supply perfectly elastic).
  • Say: "supply decreases; there is a contraction of demand" — not: "demand falls".
Mission 5

Who bears the tax?

  • The incidence of a tax: how its burden is shared between consumers and producers.
  • Consumers' share per unit = P1 − Pe (the rise in the price they pay).
  • Producers' share per unit = Pe − (P1 − t) (the fall in the price they keep).
  • The two shares add up to the tax, t.
  • Government revenue = t × Q1: the rectangle between P1 and P1 − t, from 0 to Q1.
  • Who hands the money over (the formal incidence or impact) does not decide who bears it.

Mission 5

Worked example

  • Illustrative data. Before the tax: Pe = £6, Qe = 60 units.
  • Specific tax: £1.50 per unit. After the tax: consumers pay £7, Q1 = 50.
  • Producers keep £7 − £1.50 = £5.50.
  • Consumers' burden: (£7 − £6) × 50 = £50.
  • Producers' burden: (£6 − £5.50) × 50 = £25.
  • Government revenue: £1.50 × 50 = £75 (= £50 + £25 ✓).
  • Consumers bear 2/3 of the tax: demand here is less elastic than supply (PED −1, PES 2 at the start).

Mission 5

Elasticity decides the split

  • The side of the market that is less price elastic bears more of the tax.
  • Inelastic demand (e.g. cigarettes, fuel): sellers raise the price and buyers keep buying, so consumers bear most.
  • Elastic demand: buyers switch away, so sellers must absorb the tax: producers bear most.
  • Extreme cases:
    • perfectly inelastic demand: consumers bear it all
    • perfectly elastic demand: producers bear it all
  • Approximate consumers' share = PES ÷ (PES + |PED|).
  • Say: "demand is price inelastic" — not: "demand is low".

Mission 5

Stakeholders

  • Consumers: pay a higher price, buy less; consumer surplus falls.
  • Producers: keep less per unit, sell less; producer surplus falls. Revenue falls.
  • Workers: fewer units made can mean fewer jobs in the industry.
  • Government: gains revenue (t × Q1) to spend; can target goods it wants used less.
  • Society:
    • where the good has a negative externality, the tax can move output toward the social optimum
  • Indirect taxes are often regressive: lower-income households spend a larger share of income on them.
Mission 5

Case: the UK sugar levy

  • Soft Drinks Industry Levy, from April 2018, charged on producers and importers. Rates at the start:
    • 18p per litre: drinks with 5 g to 8 g of sugar per 100 ml
    • 24p per litre: 8 g or more
  • (Both rates have risen each year since April 2025.)
  • Below 5 g: no levy. So firms could avoid it by cutting sugar.
  • 2015 to 2018, average sugar in UK soft drinks fell from 4.4 g to 2.9 g per 100 ml.
  • Most of the fall came from reformulation, not from buyers switching.
  • Caution: the study shows a trend; it cannot prove the levy alone caused it.
Mission 5Evaluate

Does the tax work?

  • It depends on PED:
    • inelastic demand → high revenue, small fall in Q
    • elastic demand → bigger cut in consumption, less revenue
    • one tax rarely maximises both
  • It depends on information: to correct an externality, the tax should match the external cost. That is hard to measure.
  • It depends on time and side effects: PED grows in the long run; very high taxes can encourage smuggling and illegal markets.
  • Governments use elasticity estimates to predict both the revenue and the change in behaviour.
Mission check

?A city has a fixed number of parking spaces and no space to add more. The city now charges the owners a fixed tax for each space used per day. Demand for parking is unchanged. Who bears the tax?

The less elastic side bears more. When one side cannot respond at all, it bears the whole tax, whoever is legally charged.

Mission 6

A payment per unit

  • A subsidy is a payment from the government to producers that lowers their costs.
  • A specific subsidy pays a fixed amount per unit.
  • An ad valorem subsidy pays a percentage of the price.
  • Why governments use them:
    • lower prices of goods they want used more, e.g. public transport
    • support producers' incomes, e.g. farmers
    • raise output where there are external benefits
Mission 6

The subsidy diagram

  • Build it in steps (a subsidy of s per unit):
  • 1. Start at equilibrium: Pe, Qe.
  • 2. Supply shifts down by the subsidy: S → S − subsidy. Vertical gap = s.
  • 3. New equilibrium: the price buyers pay falls to P1; quantity rises to Q1.
  • Producers receive P1 + s per unit.
  • The price falls by less than the subsidy (unless demand is perfectly inelastic or supply perfectly elastic).
  • Say: "supply increases; there is an extension of demand" — not: "demand increases".
Mission 6

Who gains, and what it costs

  • Cost to the government = s × Q1: the rectangle between P1 + s and P1, from 0 to Q1.
  • That rectangle splits in two:
    • consumers' gain per unit = Pe − P1
    • producers' gain per unit = (P1 + s) − Pe
  • The two gains per unit add up to the subsidy, s.
  • This split is the incidence of the subsidy.

Mission 6

Worked example

  • Illustrative data. Before: Pe = £8, Qe = 60 units.
  • Specific subsidy: £3 per unit. After: consumers pay £6, Q1 = 70.
  • Producers receive £6 + £3 = £9.
  • Cost to government: £3 × 70 = £210.
  • Consumers' share: (£8 − £6) × 70 = £140.
  • Producers' share: (£9 − £8) × 70 = £70.
  • Consumers get 2/3: here demand is less elastic than supply (PED −0.67, PES 1.33 at the start).

Mission 6

Elasticity decides the split

  • The side of the market that is less price elastic gets more of the subsidy.
  • Inelastic demand: the price falls a lot → consumers gain most.
  • Elastic demand: the price barely falls → producers keep most.
  • Extreme cases:
    • perfectly inelastic demand: consumers get it all; Q does not change
    • perfectly elastic demand: price unchanged; producers get it all
  • More elastic demand and supply → a bigger rise in Q (and a bigger bill).
  • Approximate consumers' share = PES ÷ (PES + |PED|).

Start: a subsidy of 2 per unit; PED −0.5, PES 1. Consumers get 2/3 of each unit's subsidy.

  • Takeaway: more elastic demand → the price falls less, so producers keep more (consumers' share 1/3) and Q rises more.

Mission 6

Stakeholders

  • Consumers: pay less, buy more; consumer surplus rises.
  • Producers: receive more per unit, sell more; producer surplus rises.
  • Workers: more output can mean more jobs in the industry.
  • Government: pays s × Q1. Opportunity cost: that money cannot fund schools or hospitals, or must come from taxes or borrowing.
  • Producers may come to depend on the subsidy, with less pressure to cut costs.
Mission 6

Case: paying the buyer instead

  • From 2023 to 30 September 2025, US buyers of qualifying new electric cars could claim a federal tax credit of up to $7,500.
  • This subsidy went to buyers, so it shifted demand up, not supply.
  • The split is the same: it depends on elasticities, not on who receives the payment.
  • If car supply is inelastic, sellers can raise prices and keep much of the credit.
Mission 6Evaluate

Is the subsidy worth it?

  • It depends on elasticities: with elastic demand, the price barely falls and most of the money goes to producers.
  • It depends on information: to correct an external benefit, the subsidy should match its size. That is hard to measure.
  • It depends on the opportunity cost: the same money could fund other spending or lower taxes.
  • It depends on time and side effects: subsidies are hard to remove once firms and voters rely on them; firms may stay inefficient.
Mission check

?A small country's wheat farmers sell all their wheat at the world price, which they are too small to change. The government pays farmers a subsidy for every tonne they grow. Who gains the subsidy?

Farmers can sell any amount at the world price, so the price does not fall. They receive the world price plus the whole subsidy.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • PED = %ΔQd ÷ %ΔP: it measures how strongly quantity demanded responds to price. Size above 1: elastic; below 1: inelastic.
  • Substitutes, necessity, share of income, time and market definition decide how elastic demand is.
  • Slope is not elasticity: along a straight-line demand curve PED changes from elastic to inelastic.

Mission 2

  • When demand is price inelastic, price and total revenue move the same way; when it is elastic, they move in opposite ways; at unit elasticity TR is unchanged.
  • PED guides firms' pricing and governments' choice of what to tax, but estimates change over time and along the curve.

Mission 3

  • YED = % change in Qd ÷ % change in income. Positive: normal. Negative: inferior.
  • Size: below 1 is income inelastic (necessity); above 1 is income elastic (luxury).
  • XED = % change in Qd of A ÷ % change in price of B. Positive: substitutes. Negative: complements. Zero: unrelated.

Mission 4

  • PES = %ΔQs ÷ %ΔP: below 1 is inelastic, above 1 is elastic.
  • Supply is more elastic with spare capacity, stocks, mobile factors and, above all, more time.

Mission 5

  • An indirect tax shifts supply up by the tax; the price rises by less than the tax and quantity falls.
  • The burden falls more on whichever side is less price elastic, whoever hands the money over.
  • Specific taxes shift S in parallel; ad valorem taxes pivot it.

Mission 6

  • A subsidy shifts supply down by the subsidy; the price falls by less than the subsidy and quantity rises.
  • The government pays s × Q1; the less elastic side of the market gets more of it.
