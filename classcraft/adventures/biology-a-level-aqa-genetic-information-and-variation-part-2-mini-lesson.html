Mission 1

Mission 1: Each parent passes on chromosomes, yet the number never doubles. How is it halved so exactly?

Make a guess: Each parent passes on chromosomes, yet the number never doubles. How is it halved so exactly?

Mission 1

Why halve?

  • Body cells are diploid (2n): two sets of chromosomes, one set from each parent. Human 2n = 46.
  • Gametes are haploid (n): one set. Human n = 23.
  • Fertilisation fuses two gametes: n + n = 2n.
  • Without halving, the number would double every generation.
  • Meiosis is the reduction division: one diploid cell → four haploid cells.
  • Say: "a haploid cell has one of every chromosome" — not: "half of every chromosome".
Mission 1

Homologous pairs

  • Homologous chromosomes: a matching pair, one maternal, one paternal.
  • Same size and shape; the same genes at the same loci.
  • They may carry different alleles of those genes.
  • After replication, each homologue has two identical sister chromatids.
  • Sister chromatids are copies. Homologues are not.
  • Say: "the two chromatids of one chromosome are identical copies" — not: "one chromatid came from each parent".
Quick check
?A diploid nucleus of an organism with 2n = 14 undergoes meiosis. What is produced?
Mission 1

Meiosis I: the pairs separate

  • DNA is replicated once, before meiosis I. Then come two divisions.
  • Prophase I: chromosomes condense. Homologues pair up, forming a bivalent.
  • Non-sister chromatids cross over at points called chiasmata. The nuclear envelope breaks down; the spindle forms.
  • Metaphase I: pairs line up on the equator. Each pair faces the poles independently of the others.
  • Anaphase I: homologues are pulled to opposite poles. Sister chromatids stay joined.
  • Telophase I: two cells, each haploid. Each chromosome still has two chromatids.
Mission 1

Meiosis II: the chromatids separate

  • No DNA replication between the two divisions.
  • Prophase II: a new spindle forms in each cell.
  • Metaphase II: chromosomes line up singly on the equator.
  • Anaphase II: centromeres divide; sister chromatids move to opposite poles.
  • Telophase II: nuclear envelopes form. Four haploid cells, each chromosome a single chromatid.

Mission 1

Find meiosis in any life cycle

  • Find where the chromosome number goes 2n → n. That is meiosis.
  • Where n + n → 2n, that is fertilisation.
  • Mitosis can happen in haploid or diploid cells. It keeps the number.
  • In animals, meiosis makes gametes.
  • In many plants and fungi, meiosis makes spores. Haploid organisms then make gametes by mitosis.
Mission check

?In a moss, the leafy green plant is haploid. It makes sperm and eggs by mitosis. After fertilisation, the zygote grows into a small diploid stalk with a capsule on top. The capsule releases haploid spores, each of which grows into a new leafy plant. Where in this life cycle does meiosis happen?

Find where 2n becomes n, and you have found meiosis, in a moss, a fern or a human.

Mission 2

Mission 2: One extra chromosome can change a whole body. So how do some plants thrive with doubled sets?

Make a guess: One extra chromosome can change a whole body. So how do some plants thrive with doubled sets?

Mission 2

Gene or chromosome mutation?

  • Gene mutation: a change in the bases of one gene.
  • Chromosome mutation: a change in the number or structure of whole chromosomes.
  • One chromosome carries hundreds of genes. Gain or lose one, and hundreds of genes change dose at once.
  • Development depends on genes being in the right balance.
  • A whole extra set keeps every gene in proportion. Many plants live well with one.
Mission 2

Non-disjunction in meiosis

  • Normal meiosis: each pair of homologous chromosomes separates (meiosis I); then each pair of chromatids separates (meiosis II).
  • Non-disjunction: a pair fails to separate. Both go to the same pole.
  • (1) In meiosis I: all four gametes are abnormal. Two have one extra chromosome (n + 1); two have one missing (n − 1).
  • (2) In meiosis II: two gametes are normal, one is n + 1, one is n − 1.
  • Fertilisation by a normal gamete gives a zygote with 2n + 1 or 2n − 1. Every cell inherits it by mitosis.
Mission 2

Worked example: counting chromosomes

  • Human body cells: 2n = 46. Normal gametes: n = 23.
  • Non-disjunction of one pair gives gametes with 24 or 22.
  • Zygote: 24 + 23 = 47 (one chromosome present three times).
  • Zygote: 22 + 23 = 45 (one chromosome present once).
  • Error in meiosis I: gametes 24, 24, 22, 22. Error in meiosis II: 24, 22, 23, 23.
Mission 2

Down syndrome

  • Down syndrome: three copies of chromosome 21 (trisomy 21). 47 chromosomes in each cell.
  • Most cases: non-disjunction of chromosome 21 in meiosis, usually in the egg-forming cells of the mother.
  • The chance rises with maternal age. One explanation: eggs pause part-way through meiosis I for decades, and the links holding chromosomes together weaken.
  • Effects vary widely between people; they include some degree of learning disability and a raised chance of heart defects.
  • The genes on chromosome 21 are normal. The problem is their dose.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Brothers and sisters share both parents, yet each gets a different genetic mix. Where does it come from?

Make a guess: Brothers and sisters share both parents, yet each gets a different genetic mix. Where does it come from?

Mission 3

Three shuffles

  • Every gamete a parent makes carries a new combination of alleles.
  • Shuffle 1: how each homologous pair lines up at metaphase I.
  • Shuffle 2: crossing over between homologues in prophase I.
  • Shuffle 3: random fertilisation: which gamete meets which.
  • Say: "meiosis makes new combinations of alleles" — not: "meiosis makes new alleles".
Mission 3

Shuffle 1: independent assortment

  • At metaphase I, each homologous pair faces the poles at random.
  • Each pair's orientation is independent of every other pair's.
  • So each gamete gets a random mix of maternal and paternal chromosomes.
  • The term is independent segregation of homologous chromosomes.
  • Say: "each pair lines up independently" — not: "all the maternal chromosomes go to one pole".
Mission 3

Worked example: how many combinations?

  • Let n = number of homologous pairs.
  • 1 pair: 2 orientations → 2 kinds of gamete.
  • 2 pairs: 2 × 2 = 4 kinds. 3 pairs: 2 × 2 × 2 = 8.
  • Rule: combinations = 2ⁿ.
  • Human, n = 23: 2²³ = 8 388 608 kinds of gamete from each parent.
  • Random fertilisation: 2²³ × 2²³ = 2⁴⁶ ≈ 7.0 × 10¹³ possible combinations in a zygote. Before any crossing over.
  • Say: "multiply the orientations: 2ⁿ" — not: "add them: 2 × n".
Quick check
?Garden peas have a diploid number of 14. Ignoring crossing over, how many different combinations of maternal and paternal chromosomes can random orientation of bivalents produce in the gametes?
Mission 3

Shuffle 2: crossing over

  • In prophase I, homologues pair up (a bivalent).
  • Non-sister chromatids (one from each homologue) break and rejoin at chiasmata.
  • They exchange equivalent sections, so alleles swap between homologues.
  • The result: recombinant chromatids, carrying new combinations of alleles.
  • So even the four cells from one meiosis can all differ.
  • Say: "non-sister chromatids exchange sections" — not: "sister chromatids swap sections".
Quick check
?Which statement describes crossing over during meiosis?
Mission 3

Shuffle 3, and why sex pays

  • Any sperm can fertilise any egg: random fertilisation. Each zygote is a new combination from two parents.
  • Say: "variation arises at random; selection acts on it afterwards" — not: "offspring get the variation they need".
Mission 3

When separation fails: non-disjunction

  • Non-disjunction: a homologous pair (anaphase I) or two sister chromatids (anaphase II) fail to separate.
  • One gamete gets an extra chromosome (n + 1); another lacks it (n − 1).
  • Fertilised by a normal gamete → a zygote with 2n + 1 (three copies: trisomy) or 2n − 1.
  • Mitosis copies this into every body cell.
  • A change in chromosome number is a chromosome mutation.
  • Down syndrome: usually three copies of chromosome 21, so 47 chromosomes.
  • The chance of non-disjunction in eggs rises with the mother's age.
  • Say: "the extra chromosome comes from non-disjunction in meiosis, before fertilisation" — not: "it is made at fertilisation".
Quick check
?In one meiosis in a parent, the first division is normal. In the second division, the two chromatids of chromosome 21 fail to separate in one of the two cells; the other cell divides normally. How many chromosomes are in each of the four gametes from this meiosis?
Mission check

?A mutation in a hypothetical species of animal prevents crossing over during meiosis, but meiosis is otherwise normal. Compared with the gametes of a normal individual, which prediction about the gametes of an individual with this mutation is correct?

Take one shuffle away and the others still work. Siblings differ because meiosis shuffles twice and fertilisation deals once more.

Mission 4

Three levels of biodiversity

  • Biodiversity: the variety of life, at three levels.
  • Ecosystem (habitat) diversity: the range of habitats or ecosystems, e.g. sand dune, woodland, meadow, stream.
  • Species diversity: the number of species and how evenly the individuals are spread among them.
  • Genetic diversity: the variety of alleles within a species.
  • Biodiversity can be studied at any scale, from a small local habitat to the whole Earth.
Quick check
?A survey of an island records three findings: the island contains six distinct ecosystems; 420 species have been identified; and its population of one lizard species carries 18 alleles at a studied gene, compared with 4 alleles in the same species on a neighbouring island.   • Species richness: the number of species. Both meadows: 4.
  • Species evenness: how evenly individuals are spread among the species. A is even; B is dominated by buttercup.
  • Species diversity needs both. Meadow A is more diverse.
  • Say: "diversity depends on richness and evenness" — not: "diversity is the number of species".
Mission 4

The index of diversity

  • An index of diversity combines the number of species with the number of individuals of each.
  • d = N(N − 1) ÷ Σn(n − 1)
  • N = total number of organisms of all species; n = number of organisms of each species.
  • Lowest value 1 (only one species). Higher value = more diverse.
  • Meadow A: 4.33. Meadow B: 1.65.
Mission 4

Worked example

Illustrative data: pitfall traps in a wood. Species P 12, Q 6, R 2.

  • N = 12 + 6 + 2 = 20. N(N − 1) = 20 × 19 = 380.
  • Σn(n − 1) = (12 × 11) + (6 × 5) + (2 × 1) = 132 + 30 + 2 = 164.
  • d = 380 ÷ 164 = 2.32.
  • Compare it only with a site sampled by the same method and effort.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A rock pool holds limpets 8, barnacles 6, periwinkles 4 and sea anemones 2. Using d = N(N − 1) ÷ Σn(n − 1), what is the index of diversity?
Mission 4

Sampling for an index

  • You cannot count every organism. Take samples that represent the habitat.
  • Random sampling: random-number coordinates on a grid; quadrats of equal size. Avoids bias.
  • Take many samples. One quadrat misses species and shows nothing about variation.
  • Compare two sites only with the same method and effort.
Quick check
?Ecologists want to compare the species diversity of the plant communities in two meadows by calculating an index of diversity, N(N − 1) ÷ Σn(n − 1), for each. Which procedure is most appropriate?
Mission check

?Illustrative data. Two hedgerows were sampled for insects with the same method and effort. Each had the same 6 species and 120 insects in total. In hedgerow X, one species made up 100 of the 120 insects. In hedgerow Y, each species had 20 insects. Which statement is correct?

Counting species is only half the story. How evenly the individuals are shared out decides which habitat is more diverse.

Mission 5

Genetic diversity

  • Genetic diversity: the number of different alleles of genes in a population.
  • Every member of a species has the same genes. They differ in which alleles they carry.
  • Small, isolated or captive populations often have low genetic diversity.
  • Say: "different alleles of the same genes" — not: "different genes".
Quick check
?Cheetahs (Acinonyx jubatus) show extremely little genetic variation: unrelated cheetahs are so alike that skin grafts between them are not rejected. In a reserve, cheetah numbers have been stable for 20 years, and the species present and their relative abundances have not changed. Which statement about biodiversity in the reserve is correct?
Mission 5

From features to sequences

  • Genetic diversity can be compared by:
  • the frequency of observable or measurable characteristics;
  • the base sequence of DNA or of mRNA;
  • the amino-acid sequence of the protein coded for.
  • Fewer differences = more closely related.
  • Gene technology changed the method: inferring DNA differences from characteristics has been replaced by sequencing DNA directly.
  • Why: characteristics are also affected by the environment, and one characteristic may be controlled by many genes.
  • Say: "compare base sequences" — a base change may not change the amino acid (the code is degenerate), so proteins can hide DNA differences.

Mission 5

Why genetic diversity matters

  • Environments change: new diseases, new climates, new chemicals.
  • High genetic diversity: some individuals are more likely to carry alleles that let them survive. The population persists.
  • Low genetic diversity: if one is susceptible, nearly all are. The population can crash.
  • Example: California condors fell to 22 birds in 1982. All living condors descend from the 27 taken into captivity by 1987.
  • Say: "the population is more likely to contain resistant individuals" — not: "each individual is more resistant".
Quick checkRead the data
?Real data (Zhu et al. 2000). In Yunnan, China, farmers planted rows of disease-susceptible rice varieties among disease-resistant varieties in every rice field of 5 townships (1998) and 10 townships (1999). Plots of the susceptible varieties grown alone (monoculture) were the comparison.
Which conclusion is best supported?
  • Rice blast (a fungal disease) on the susceptible varieties: 94% less severe in mixtures.
  • Yield of the susceptible varieties: 89% greater in mixtures.
Mission check

?Almost all bananas in international trade are one variety, Cavendish. Every plant is grown from a piece of another plant, so all are genetically identical. A fungal wilt disease that kills Cavendish plants is spreading between plantations. Why are biologists concerned for the whole crop?

Numbers do not protect a population; variety does. Genetic diversity is a population's insurance against change.

Mission 6

Mission 6: If no animal can change its own genes, how can a whole population change over time?

Make a guess: If no animal can change its own genes, how can a whole population change over time?

Mission 6

Where variation comes from

  • Individuals in a population differ in phenotype.
  • Causes: genes, environment, and both together.
  • Mutation is the original source of all new alleles. It is random: it does not happen because an allele is needed.
  • Meiosis and random fertilisation make new combinations of existing alleles. They make no new alleles.
  • Genetic diversity = the number of different alleles of genes in a population. More diversity gives selection more to act on.
  • Say: "a mutation arose by chance" — not: "the organism mutated to survive".
Quick check
?A population of a plant species has two alleles of a flower-colour gene. A third allele, never seen before, appears in one seedling. Which process could have produced it?
Mission 6

More young than the habitat can support

  • Populations overproduce: far more offspring than can survive.
  • Resources are limited: food, water, nest sites, territory, light.
  • So members of the same species compete (intraspecific competition).
  • Selection pressures decide who survives and breeds:
    • biotic: predation, disease, competition;
    • abiotic: e.g. extreme cold, drought.
Mission 6

Natural selection, step by step

  • (1) Variation: individuals differ in a heritable trait.
  • (2) Selection pressure: e.g. a predator that finds pale beetles more easily on dark bark.
  • (3) Differential survival and reproduction: individuals with the advantageous phenotype are more likely to survive and leave more offspring.
  • (4) Inheritance: they pass on the advantageous allele. Over many generations its frequency rises.
  • No individual changed. The proportions in the population changed.
  • Result: an increased proportion of the population with the advantageous characteristic.
Mission 6

Fitness

  • Fitness is about reproductive success: surviving offspring that go on to breed.
  • Not size, strength or speed. Those count only if they lead to more offspring.
  • A phenotype that raises fitness in one environment may lower it in another.
  • Say: "leaves more surviving offspring" — not: "is the fittest because strongest".
Quick check
?In a population of red deer, stag X is the largest and strongest male but leaves two offspring that survive to breed. Stag Y is smaller and weaker but leaves six offspring that survive to breed. Which stag has the higher fitness, and why?
Mission 6

Only heritable traits evolve

  • Selection changes a population only if the trait is heritable: coded in the base sequence of DNA and passed on in gametes.
  • Acquired characteristics are not inherited: trained muscles, an injury, a learned skill.
Quick check
?A woman develops large, strong arm muscles through years of rowing training. Will her children inherit larger arm muscles as a result of her training?
Mission 6

Evolution: a change in allele frequency

  • Evolution = a change in the allele frequencies of a population over time.
  • Gene pool: all the genes, and all their alleles, in a population.
  • Adaptation: an inherited feature that improves survival or reproduction in a particular environment.
  • Adaptations may be anatomical, physiological or behavioural.
  • Over long periods, adaptation and selection (with isolation) produce new species and the diversity of life.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Meiosis is a reduction division: one DNA replication, two divisions, four haploid cells, so fertilisation restores the diploid number.
  • Meiosis I separates homologous pairs (the halving); meiosis II separates sister chromatids.

Mission 2

  • Non-disjunction in meiosis gives gametes with n + 1 or n − 1; fertilisation then gives 2n + 1 or 2n − 1 in every cell.
  • Down syndrome is trisomy 21, usually from non-disjunction in the mother's meiosis; its chance rises with maternal age.

Mission 3

  • Independent assortment (orientation of pairs at metaphase I) and crossing over (prophase I) give every gamete a new combination of alleles.
  • Random fertilisation multiplies the combinations again.
  • Independent assortment alone gives 2ⁿ kinds of gamete per parent (n = number of homologous pairs).
  • Non-disjunction gives gametes n + 1 or n − 1; Down syndrome is trisomy 21.

Mission 4

  • Species diversity = richness (number of species) + evenness (how individuals are spread).
  • d = N(N − 1) ÷ Σn(n − 1): higher = more diverse.

Mission 5

  • Genetic diversity is the variety of alleles in a population; high diversity makes it more likely that some individuals survive a change.
  • Fewer base differences = more closely related; sequencing has replaced inference from observable characteristics.

Mission 6

  • Random mutation supplies new alleles; selection pressures make individuals with advantageous heritable phenotypes leave more offspring, so those alleles rise in frequency.
  • Evolution is a change in allele frequencies; fitness is reproductive success.
