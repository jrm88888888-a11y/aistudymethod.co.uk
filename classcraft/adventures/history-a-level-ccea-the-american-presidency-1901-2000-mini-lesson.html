The American Presidency 1901–2000

This mini-lesson covers CCEA Unit A2 1, Option 4 — The American Presidency 1901–2000: how the office grew from apparent weakness in 1901, through the two Roosevelts and Wilson, the New Deal, the 'Imperial Presidency', the shocks of Vietnam and Watergate, and the Reagan revival.

Emerging international presence 1901–20

Theodore Roosevelt and Woodrow Wilson

  • Theodore Roosevelt became president in 1901 after the assassination of McKinley — at 42, the youngest ever. He expanded presidential activism at home and abroad.
  • Woodrow Wilson (1913–21) led the USA into the First World War in 1917 and set out his Fourteen Points (1918).
  • But the Senate rejected the Treaty of Versailles and the League of Nations (1919–20), showing the limits of presidential power.

The passive presidents 1921–32

'Return to normalcy'

After Wilson, the presidents Harding, Coolidge and Hoover favoured a more limited, business-friendly role — Harding's promise of a 'return to normalcy'. Prosperity in the 1920s gave way to disaster with the Wall Street Crash of 1929 and the Great Depression, which Hoover struggled to contain.

The New Deal 1933–45

Franklin D. Roosevelt

Franklin D. Roosevelt (1933–45) transformed the presidency. His New Deal from 1933 used federal power to fight the Depression through relief, recovery and reform. FDR was elected four times and led the USA through most of the Second World War, dying in office in April 1945. The office was now central to national life.

The 'Imperial Presidency' 1945–63

Truman, Eisenhower and Kennedy

As the USA became leader of the 'Free World' in the nuclear age, presidential power grew further — what the historian Arthur M. Schlesinger Jr. later called the 'Imperial Presidency' (in his 1973 book of that title). Truman authorised the atomic bombs (1945) and the Truman Doctrine (1947); Eisenhower managed Cold War tensions; Kennedy embodied a vigorous presidency until his assassination in 1963.

Limits and Watergate 1963–80

Vietnam and Watergate

  • Lyndon Johnson's 'Great Society' reforms (and the Civil Rights Act of 1964) were overshadowed by the Vietnam War, which drained public trust.
  • Richard Nixon resigned on 9 August 1974 over the Watergate scandal (the break-in was in 1972) — a low point for the office.
  • Ford and Carter then governed a weakened, more closely scrutinised presidency.

'Morning in America' 1981–2000

Reagan and after

Ronald Reagan (1981–89) revived presidential and national prestige — his 1984 campaign spoke of 'Morning in America'. His successors George H. W. Bush (1989–93) and Bill Clinton (1993–2001) benefited to some extent from this restoration, presiding over the end of the Cold War and the 1990s.

Recap

The big ideas to know

1901–20: T. Roosevelt (from 1901) and Wilson; WWI 1917; Senate rejects the League.

1921–32: passive presidents Harding, Coolidge, Hoover; Crash 1929.

1933–63: FDR's New Deal; the 'Imperial Presidency' (Schlesinger) — Truman, Eisenhower, Kennedy.

1963–80: Johnson & Vietnam; Nixon and Watergate (resigned 1974); Ford, Carter.

1981–2000: Reagan's 'Morning in America'; Bush and Clinton.
