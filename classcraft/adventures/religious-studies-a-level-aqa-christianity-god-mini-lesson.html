Mission 1

Mission 1: If God never changes, how can God listen to your prayers and love you back?

Make a guess: If God never changes, how can God listen to your prayers and love you back?

Mission 1

One God, maker of all

  • "In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth." (Genesis 1:1)
  • "there is one God, the Father, of whom are all things, and we for him; and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things" (1 Corinthians 8:6)
  • Creator: Christians believe everything that exists depends on God for its existence, at every moment.
  • Omnipotent creator and controller: God sustains and governs all things (providence).
  • Transcendent: God is not part of the universe and is beyond it.
  • Unknowable: God's being exceeds human understanding: "my thoughts are not your thoughts" (Isaiah 55:8).
Mission 1

From nothing, not from God

  • Creatio ex nihilo: God created out of nothing, without any pre-existing material.
  • Sources: 2 Maccabees 7:28 (God made them "not of things that were"); Hebrews 11:3.
  • Emanation (contrast): in Plotinus (c. 204–270), all things flow out of the One by necessity, like light from the sun.
  • Mainstream Christian theology rejects emanation: it makes the world part of God's own substance, and it makes creation necessary, not free.
  • Augustine (354–430) on creatures: "they were made of nothing, by Thee, not of Thee" (Confessions XIII.33).
  • So the world is distinct from God, and freely given.

Mission 1

All-powerful and unchanging

  • Omnipotence: Aquinas: God "can do all things that are possible absolutely" (Summa Theologiae I, q. 25, a. 3): not the logically contradictory.
  • Immutability: God does not change. "For I, the LORD, don't change" (Malachi 3:6); "no variation nor turning shadow" (James 1:17).
  • Why it matters: change means gaining or losing something; a perfect God lacks nothing to gain.
  • Augustine: "For that truly is which remains unchangeably." (Confessions VII.11)
  • Significance for believers: God's promises and love can be trusted absolutely.
Mission check

?A student says: "The universe is part of God's own being. It flowed out of God the way warmth flows out of a fire, and God could not have done otherwise." Which Christian belief does this most directly contradict?

Christians hold that God made the world freely, from nothing, and remains unchanging in love: the ground of a relationship the believer can trust.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Was Jesus fully God, or the first and greatest thing God made? Why did it matter so much?

Make a guess: Was Jesus fully God, or the first and greatest thing God made? Why did it matter so much?

Mission 2

The puzzle

  • Christians inherited strict monotheism: "The LORD is our God. The LORD is one." (Deuteronomy 6:4)
  • Yet the New Testament shows early Christians worshipping Jesus: in John's Gospel, Thomas calls him "My Lord and my God!" (John 20:28).
  • In John's Gospel, Jesus says: "I and the Father are one." (John 10:30)
  • So: is Jesus God, or something less than God?
  • In the 4th century this became a crisis that split the Church and reached the emperor.
Mission 2

Arius: the Son is created

  • Arius (c. 256–336), a priest in Alexandria, Egypt; dispute from c. 318.
  • God alone is unbegotten, unchanging, without beginning.
  • So the Son (the Word, Logos) must have a beginning: created by God before time, the first and highest creature, through whom God made everything else.
  • Slogan of his party, condemned at Nicaea and attacked by Athanasius: "there was when he was not".
  • Texts he used: "the firstborn of all creation" (Colossians 1:15); "the Father is greater than I" (John 14:28); Proverbs 8:22 (Wisdom "created" in the Greek version).
  • His aim: to protect the oneness and changelessness of God.
Mission 2

Athanasius: only God can save

  • Athanasius (c. 296–373), deacon at Nicaea, then bishop of Alexandria from 328; exiled five times.
  • On the Incarnation (c. 318 or c. 335; date debated).
  • Humans, made in God's image, were sliding back into death and corruption through sin.
  • Only the Creator can re-create: a creature cannot restore the image of God.
  • "For He was made man that we might be made God" (On the Incarnation 54): sharing God's life (theosis, deification).
  • So if the Son is a creature, we are not saved. The Son must be fully God.
Mission 2Read the source

The Word before the world

> "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God." (John 1:1, WEBBE)

> "The Word became flesh and lived amongst us." (John 1:14, WEBBE)

> "who, existing in the form of God, didn't consider equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, taking the form of a servant" (Philippians 2:6–7, WEBBE)

Q1 Which phrase in John 1:1 did Athanasius' side stress?

Show answer

"The Word was God."

Q2 What does Philippians 2 say the Son did to become human?

Show answer

He "emptied himself", taking the form of a servant.

Mission 2

Nicaea, 325

  • Emperor Constantine called the first ecumenical (worldwide) council to end the dispute: roughly 250–300 bishops at Nicaea (ancient counts vary; tradition says 318).
  • The creed of Nicaea: the Son is "begotten, not made", "of one substance with the Father".
  • Key word homoousios: of the same substance (being) as the Father; not homoiousios, "of similar substance".
  • So the Son is co-equal with the Father and has no beginning.
  • Arius was condemned and exiled. Arian views stayed strong for decades; the Council of Constantinople (381) confirmed Nicaea (see Mission 3).
  • Significance: "Son of God" means sharing the Father's divine nature, not being a created or adopted son.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Christians pray to Father, Son and Holy Spirit. How can that still be one God?

Make a guess: Christians pray to Father, Son and Holy Spirit. How can that still be one God?

Mission 3

One God, three persons

  • The Trinity: one God in three persons: Father, Son and Holy Spirit.
  • Christians believe each person is fully God, yet there is one God, not three.
  • "Hear, Israel: The LORD is our God. The LORD is one." (Deuteronomy 6:4)
  • Three views the Church rejected:
  • Tritheism: three separate gods.
  • Modalism: one person appearing in three modes (associated with Sabellius, 3rd century).
  • Subordinationism: the Son and Spirit as lesser than the Father.
Mission 3Read the source

Biblical basis

The word "Trinity" is not in the Bible. Christians find the pattern in it:

> "baptising them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 28:19, WEBBE)

> "The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ, God's love, and the fellowship of the Holy Spirit be with you all." (2 Corinthians 13:14, WEBBE)

  • At Jesus' baptism, the Spirit descends "as a dove" and the Father's voice speaks (Matthew 3:16–17).
  • "yet to us there is one God, the Father, of whom are all things … and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things" (1 Corinthians 8:6): Paul places Jesus inside the Jewish confession of one God.
  • "I and the Father are one." (John 10:30)

Q1 In Matthew 28:19, how many "names" are there?

Show answer

One "name", shared by three.

Mission 3

The Son and the Spirit as God

  • Son: "the Word was God" (John 1:1); "My Lord and my God!" (John 20:28).
  • Spirit: lying to the Holy Spirit is lying "to God" (Acts 5:3–4); "Where could I go from your Spirit?" (Psalm 139:7).
  • Christians read the Spirit as personal: teaches and reminds (John 14:26); not just a force.
  • Early Christians worshipped and baptised in the threefold name before the doctrine was defined.
  • The doctrine explains what Christians already did in prayer.

Mission 3

From Nicaea to Constantinople

  • Nicaea (325): the Son is homoousios (of one substance) with the Father (see Mission 2).
  • Cappadocian Fathers: Basil of Caesarea (c. 330–379), Gregory of Nazianzus (c. 329–390), Gregory of Nyssa (c. 335–c. 395).
  • Their formula: one ousia (being, substance), three hypostaseis (persons).
  • Constantinople (381) completed the creed: the Spirit is "the Lord and Giver of life", who "proceeds from the Father", and "with the Father and the Son together is worshipped and glorified".
  • This Nicene Creed is shared by Catholic, Orthodox and most Protestant churches.
Mission 3

Co-equal and co-eternal

  • Co-equal: no person is greater or lesser in being; all share the one divine nature.
  • Co-eternal: no person had a beginning; there was never a time when the Father was without the Son and the Spirit.
  • The Athanasian Creed (Latin, 5th–6th century): none of the three is before or after another, none greater or less (paraphrase).
  • But a relation of origin: the Father begets, the Son is begotten, the Spirit proceeds.
  • "The Father is greater than I" (John 14:28): read as Jesus speaking in his humanity, or of the Father as source, not of a greater being.
  • Importance: Christians pray to the Father, through the Son, in the Spirit; many say the Trinity shows God is love within himself (1 John 4:8).
Mission 3Evaluate

"The doctrine of the Trinity is consistent with monotheism."

  • For: there is one divine being (ousia); the persons are not three beings but three relations within one God.
  • For: the New Testament writers were Jewish monotheists who placed Jesus inside the one God (1 Corinthians 8:6).
  • Against: Jewish and Muslim critics: worshipping three persons compromises God's oneness.
  • Against: popular explanations slide into tritheism or modalism; perhaps no coherent middle exists.
  • Against: "person" today means a separate centre of consciousness, making three persons sound like three gods.
  • Judge it by: What does "one" mean (number, being, unity)? Does it fit the texts? Can a mystery be consistent without being explained?
Mission check

?A youth leader says: "God is like one actor who plays three parts: first the Father in the Old Testament, then the Son in the Gospels, then the Spirit at Pentecost." Which assessment would most Christian theologians (Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant) give?

For mainstream Christian teaching, the doctrine keeps two truths together: one God, and three distinct, co-equal, co-eternal persons; losing either is seen as the error.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Christians pray "Our Father". Does that mean they believe God is male?

Make a guess: Christians pray "Our Father". Does that mean they believe God is male?

Mission 4

Human words for God

  • Anthropomorphic language: describing God in human terms (Father, King, Judge, "the hand of God").
  • Christians believe God is spirit (John 4:24) and beyond all creatures, so no human word fits God exactly.
  • Classical theology treats such words as analogy (Thomas Aquinas) or metaphor: true in one way, not in another.
  • Mainstream teaching: God has no sex. Catholic teaching: God "is neither man nor woman: he is God" (Catechism of the Catholic Church 239).
  • Yet Christian worship has used mostly male words: Father, King, Lord, "he".
  • The question: if the words are not literal, do they still shape how people see God, and men and women?
Mission 4

God as personal, God as Father

  • Personal: God can be known, loved and spoken to; God acts with will and love, not as an impersonal force.
  • Jesus taught his followers to pray "Our Father in heaven" (Matthew 6:9).
  • Christians receive "the Spirit of adoption, by whom we cry, 'Abba! Father!'" (Romans 8:15).
  • In the Trinity, "Father" names a relationship: the Father of the Son (see Mission 3). For many Christians it is a revealed name, not just one image among many.
Mission 4

"Father": pastoral benefits and challenges

  • Benefits: intimacy and trust (Jesus' Abba); belonging as God's children; care, protection and provision; a model of good fatherhood.
  • Challenges: for people hurt by an absent or abusive father, the image may bring fear, not comfort.
  • It may suggest God is distant or stern, or that God is male.
  • Feminists argue it has been used to justify male authority in the Church and family.
  • Pastoral responses: stress the father of Luke 15 (who runs to forgive), use other biblical images alongside it, or teach clearly that "Father" is not about sex.
Mission 4

God as King, God as Love

  • King: "God is the King of all the earth" (Psalm 47:7). Meaning: God's sovereignty; Jesus preaches God's Kingdom.
  • Challenge: the image comes from male, often violent, monarchy; it can make God seem distant and controlling.
  • Love: "God is love" (1 John 4:8). Not anthropomorphic in the same way, and not gendered.
  • Many Christians use Love to interpret Father and King: God's power and authority are those of love.
Mission 4Read the source

Female images of God in the Bible

> "As one whom his mother comforts, so I will comfort you." (Isaiah 66:13, WEBBE)

> "Can a woman forget her nursing child, that she should not have compassion on the son of her womb? Yes, these may forget, yet I will not forget you!" (Isaiah 49:15, WEBBE)

  • Jesus compares himself to "a hen [who] gathers her chicks" (Matthew 23:37).
  • "God created man in his own image… male and female he created them." (Genesis 1:27)
  • The medieval mystic Julian of Norwich (c. 1343–after 1416) wrote that God is truly our Mother as well as our Father (Revelations of Divine Love, ch. 59, paraphrase).

Q1 What quality of God do the Isaiah verses express through a mother's love?

Show answer

Comfort and faithful, unforgetting compassion.

Q2 How might Genesis 1:27 support using female images for God?

Show answer

Both male and female are in God's image, so both can reflect God.

Mission 4

McFague: metaphors and models

  • Sallie McFague (1933–2019), American theologian; Metaphorical Theology (1982), Models of God (1987).
  • All talk of God is metaphor: a model of God both "is and is not" what it says (paraphrase).
  • Trouble comes when one metaphor is taken literally and becomes an idol: e.g. God as King.
  • The monarchical model (God as king and lord over the world): distant, controlling, and male. She argues it encourages domination and passivity, and fits badly with an age of nuclear and ecological threat.
  • So she proposes new models: God as Mother, Lover and Friend, and the world as God's body.
Mission 4

McFague: God as Mother

  • Mother as creator: God brings the world to birth, from God's own being, rather than making it as an object from outside (paraphrase).
  • Mother's love (agape in her sense): love that says "it is good that you exist" and wants all its children to live and flourish.
  • Mother as judge: justice as care for all she has borne; judgement falls on what harms the whole family, including the earth.
  • Ethic: care for others and for the planet; a mother does not dominate her children.
  • McFague does not say God is female: Mother is one partial model, used with others.
Mission 4

Daly: "if God is male, then the male is God"

  • Mary Daly (1928–2010), American feminist philosopher and theologian; Beyond God the Father (1973).
  • Her claim: "if God is male, then the male is God" (Beyond God the Father, ch. 1).
  • A male image of God, she argues, makes men seem god-like and legitimates patriarchy: male rule in Church, family and society.
  • The symbol of "God the Father" is not harmless picture-language: it shapes power.
  • Daly came to see Christianity as beyond reform and left it ("post-Christian").
Mission 4Diversity

Christian feminist and other responses

  • Reform from within: e.g. Rosemary Radford Ruether (1936–2022) and Elizabeth Johnson (She Who Is, 1992): recover female and wisdom (Sophia) images; keep Christianity.
  • Leave: Daly, and Daphne Hampson (b. 1944): Christianity is irredeemably patriarchal.
  • Inclusive language: some Protestant churches allow inclusive or female language for God in some prayers and hymns, alongside "Father".
  • Traditional: Catholic and Orthodox teaching, and many evangelicals, keep "Father" as the name Jesus revealed, while teaching God has no sex.
  • Some feminists also criticise "Mother": it may fix women's role as nurturing and motherhood.

Mission 4Evaluate

Is it valid to refer to God as Mother?

  • Claim: It is valid for Christians to refer to God as Mother.
  • For: The Bible itself uses maternal images (Isaiah 49:15, 66:13; Matthew 23:37), and Julian of Norwich did so.
  • For: If God has no sex, female words are as fitting (and as limited) as male ones.
  • For: McFague: male-only language has harmful effects; Mother expresses care for all.
  • Against: Jesus taught "Father", and the name expresses the Father–Son relation in the Trinity.
  • Against: McFague's "world as God's body" may blur God and creation (a risk of pantheism).
  • Against: "Mother" may itself fix a narrow idea of women.
  • Judgement criteria: Is God-language revealed or chosen? Does any image bring a sex into God? Which images help people to know God and treat each other justly?
Mission check

?A hymn-writer writes "God our Mother". A critic replies: "That's wrong, because calling God Father shows that God is literally male." On what point do mainstream Christian teaching and feminist theologians agree, against the critic?

Almost all sides agree God is not male; the live question is which words best speak of God, and what those words do to people.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Some Christians believe God cannot do everything and did not create from nothing. What God is that?

Make a guess: Some Christians believe God cannot do everything and did not create from nothing. What God is that?

Mission 5

The classical picture

  • Mainstream Christian theism (see Mission 1): God is omnipotent (all-powerful), creator of everything from nothing (ex nihilo), unchanging and impassible.
  • God's will is never finally defeated: nothing happens that God does not at least permit.
  • This picture faces the problem of evil: why does an all-powerful, all-loving God allow suffering?
  • Process theology gives a radically different account of God's power and of creation.
Mission 5

Whitehead: reality as process

  • Alfred North Whitehead (1861–1947), English mathematician and philosopher; Process and Reality (1929).
  • Reality is not made of fixed things but of events: moments of experience he calls actual occasions.
  • Each occasion takes in its past, then decides how to become: there is a degree of freedom and creativity in everything, not only in humans.
  • Then it perishes and becomes part of the past for new occasions.
  • God is not the exception to this system but its chief example.
Mission 5

God with two natures

  • Primordial nature: God's unchanging vision of every possibility and value; it offers each occasion its best possibility.
  • Consequent nature: God receives everything that happens. God is affected, and changes, as the world changes.
  • Charles Hartshorne (1897–2000), The Divine Relativity (1948): God is dipolar: unchanging in character, changing in experience.
  • Panentheism: the world is in God (God includes it and is more than it), unlike pantheism (the world is God).
Mission 5

Not omnipotent: persuasion, not force

  • God offers each occasion an initial aim: a lure towards its best outcome.
  • The occasion may follow or resist it. God cannot override it.
  • David Ray Griffin (1939–2022), God, Power, and Evil: A Process Theodicy (1976): no being can wholly determine another's choices; God's power is persuasive, not coercive.
  • So God is not omnipotent in the classical sense: not because God is weak, but because power over free beings can only persuade.
  • Whitehead, following Plato, called the creation of the world "the victory of persuasion over force" (Adventures of Ideas, 1933).
Mission 5Read the source

Whitehead on God

> "He does not create the world, he saves it: or, more accurately, he is the poet of the world, with tender patience leading it by his vision of truth, beauty, and goodness." (Whitehead, Process and Reality, Part V)

> "God is the great companion—the fellow-sufferer who understands." (Whitehead, Process and Reality, Part V)

Q1 What kind of power does the image "poet of the world" suggest?

Show answer

Persuasive, creative power that leads by a vision, not power that forces.

Q2 Which of God's two natures does "fellow-sufferer" express?

Show answer

The consequent nature: God receives and feels what happens in the world.

Mission 5

Not creator from nothing

  • Process thinkers reject creation ex nihilo. There has always been some world: God and the world exist together.
  • God creates by bringing order out of chaos, and goes on creating: creation is ongoing.
  • Some read Genesis 1:2 ("The earth was formless and empty") as God ordering a chaos already there.
  • Whitehead even wrote that it is as true to say that the World creates God as that God creates the World (Process and Reality, Part V, paraphrase): each affects the other.
  • So God is not creator in the classical sense, but the world's continuing guide and source of order and novelty.
Mission 5

Evil and a God who suffers with us

  • Since God cannot coerce, God is not to blame for failing to prevent evil (Griffin).
  • God suffers with every victim (the consequent nature) and works within every situation for the best possible outcome.
  • God took a risk in luring the world towards complexity: richer beings can enjoy more, but can also suffer and cause more suffering.
  • For process Christians, Jesus shows God's way: love that persuades and suffers, not force.
Mission 5

Objective immortality

  • Each occasion perishes, but it is not lost: it is taken up for ever into God's consequent nature.
  • Objective immortality: we live on as objects in God's memory and experience, not as continuing subjects who go on experiencing.
  • Whitehead speaks of God's "tender care that nothing be lost" (Process and Reality, Part V).
  • Hartshorne: the meaning of a life is its permanent contribution to God's experience.
  • Judgement becomes God's evaluation of what each life contributes; heaven and hell are not places.
  • Some process theologians (e.g. Griffin; Marjorie Suchocki, b. 1933, The End of Evil, 1988) also allow subjective immortality: personal life after death.

Mission 5Evaluate

Is the process God the Christian God?

  • Claim: Process theology offers an adequate Christian concept of God.
  • For: It solves the problem of evil without blaming God.
  • For: It fits biblical texts where God is affected and changes course (e.g. Exodus 32:14; Hosea 11:8) and where God is love (1 John 4:8).
  • For: It fits a scientific picture of an evolving universe.
  • Against: It conflicts with core biblical and creedal claims: God creates all things (Genesis 1:1; "maker of heaven and earth") and is Almighty.
  • Against: A God who cannot guarantee the defeat of evil may not be worthy of worship or able to ground hope (a common criticism of classical and evangelical theologians).
  • Against: Objective immortality offers no personal life after death: is being remembered enough?
  • Judgement criteria: Is God's power best shown by control or by love? How far must a Christian concept of God fit the creeds? What does hope need?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Christians believe one omnipotent, transcendent God created everything ex nihilo, freely, and called it very good; emanation and construction are rejected models.

Mission 2

  • Arius: the Son is the first, highest creature ("there was when he was not"). Athanasius: only God can re-create and save, so the Son is fully God.
  • Nicaea (325): the Son is homoousios, "of one substance with the Father", "begotten, not made".

Mission 3

  • Christians believe in one God in three co-equal, co-eternal persons; not three gods, not three modes, not ranked.
  • Biblical basis: the threefold name (Matthew 28:19; 2 Corinthians 13:14), the divinity of Son and Spirit, and Jewish monotheism (Deuteronomy 6:4).
  • Paul places Jesus inside the one God (1 Corinthians 8:6).

Mission 4

  • Words like Father and King are anthropomorphic: analogies or metaphors. Mainstream Christian teaching holds that God has no sex.
  • "Father" brings intimacy, trust and belonging, but can hurt those with painful experiences of fathers and has been used to support male authority.
  • King expresses sovereignty; Love interprets both.
  • McFague (Models of God, 1987): God as Mother, Lover, Friend; the world as God's body.
  • Daly (Beyond God the Father, 1973): "if God is male, then the male is God".

Mission 5

  • Process theology (Whitehead 1929; Hartshorne; Griffin): reality is events; God has a primordial nature (vision of possibilities) and a consequent nature (feels and is changed by the world).
  • God is neither omnipotent (power is persuasive, not coercive) nor creator from nothing (God orders and guides an ever-existing world).
  • Objective immortality: every life is preserved for ever in God's experience, not as a continuing subject.
