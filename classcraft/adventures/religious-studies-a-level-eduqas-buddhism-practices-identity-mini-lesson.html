Mission 1: Buddhists "take refuge" every day. What are they sheltering from, and where do they go?

Make a guess: Buddhists "take refuge" every day. What are they sheltering from, and where do they go?

Mission 1

Three Jewels, three refuges

  • The Three Jewels (Pali Tiratana, Sanskrit Triratna): the Buddha, the Dhamma (teaching, truth) and the Sangha (community).
  • A refuge (sarana) is a shelter. Here: shelter from dukkha and the round of rebirth (samsara).
  • Going for refuge is the basic act that marks a person as a Buddhist, in every school.
  • A common picture: the Buddha is the doctor, the Dhamma the medicine, the Sangha the nurses and fellow patients who help one recover.
  • They are called jewels: rare, precious, and worth more than anything else one could rely on.
Mission 1Read the source

The refuge formula

In Pali, from the Khuddakapatha (Khp 1), chanted by Buddhists across the world:

  • Buddham saranam gacchami · Dhammam saranam gacchami · Sangham saranam gacchami
  • Literal rendering: "To the Buddha for refuge I go. To the Dhamma for refuge I go. To the Sangha for refuge I go."
  • Then repeated with dutiyam pi ("for a second time…") and tatiyam pi ("for a third time…").

Q1 Why might the formula be said three times?

Show answer

To make the commitment firm and deliberate, not a passing thought; repetition also marks it as a formal act.

Q2 Who is "I" here?

Show answer

The person committing: the formula is a personal choice, said aloud.

Mission 1

Refuge in the Buddha

  • Historical: Siddhattha Gotama, the teacher who found the path and showed it to others. Not a creator god; for most schools, a guide rather than a saviour who acts for us (Pure Land differs: A later screen).
  • Ideal: awakening itself, the goal anyone can reach. Refuge is in the qualities of a Buddha: wisdom, compassion, purity.
  • "Whoever sees the Dhamma sees me" (Samyutta Nikaya 22.87, paraphrase): the Buddha is met in his teaching.
  • Theravada: stresses the historical Buddha as teacher and model.
  • Mahayana: many Buddhas (e.g. Amitabha) and the Buddha-nature in all beings; the Buddha is also a reality beyond one human life.
Mission 1

Refuge in the Dhamma

  • Dhamma as teaching: the words of the Buddha: the Four Noble Truths, the path.
  • Dhamma as truth: the way things really are; ultimately nibbana, unconditioned reality, which the teaching points to.
  • Traditional qualities: well proclaimed; seen here and now; timeless; ehipassiko ("come and see"); leading onward; known by the wise for themselves.
  • The raft (Majjhima Nikaya 22): the teaching is like a raft for crossing a river, to be used, not carried on one's head after crossing.
  • Your spec writes dharma.

Mission 1

Refuge in the Sangha

  • Noble Sangha (ariya-sangha): those who have reached at least the first stage of awakening (stream-entry). In Theravada the strict object of refuge.
  • Monastic Sangha: monks (bhikkhus) and nuns (bhikkhunis). They keep and teach the Dhamma; lay people support them with food and gifts; the monks give teaching and blessings in return.
  • Fourfold assembly: monks, nuns, laymen, laywomen.
  • All Buddhists past, present and future.
  • Spiritual friends: one's close companions in practice. The Buddha told Ananda that good friendship is "the whole of the holy life" (Samyutta Nikaya 45.2, paraphrase).
Quick check
?A Theravada teacher says: "Strictly, the Sangha we take refuge in is not the people in this temple: it is those who have truly seen the Dhamma." Which meaning is she using?
Mission 1

How the three fit together

  • Each refuge needs the others: the Buddha discovered the Dhamma; the Dhamma is what he taught; the Sangha preserves it and lives it.
  • Refuge in one without the others is incomplete: a teacher without teaching, a teaching without anyone practising it.
Mission 1

Going for refuge in practice

  • Daily puja: homage to the Buddha (Namo tassa…, three times), then the three refuges, often the Five Precepts. In Sri Lanka this is called pansil.
  • Bowing three times before a shrine: one bow for each jewel.
  • Ceremony: a new Buddhist may take the refuges and precepts formally before a monk or teacher.
  • Tibetan: refuge also in the lama (teacher); many make 100,000 refuge prostrations as a preliminary practice.
  • Pure Land: the nembutsu is a calling on, and refuge in, Amida (Amitabha) Buddha.
  • Triratna (founded by Sangharakshita): Going for Refuge is the central act; ordination is "effective going for refuge".
Mission 1Diversity

Heritage and convert Buddhists

  • Heritage Buddhists: born into Buddhist families and cultures (e.g. Sri Lanka, Thailand, Tibet, and their communities in Britain).
  • Refuges chanted in Pali or another traditional language from childhood; temple-centred; the monastic Sangha central; making merit by giving.
  • Convert Buddhists: adults who choose Buddhism, often in the West.
  • A formal, often emotional decision; refuge ceremony as a turning point; often meditation-centred; "sangha" may mean one's practice group or friends.
  • Both chant the same formula. These are tendencies, not rules: many heritage Buddhists meditate; many converts join traditional temples.
  • Your spec asks for the meaning of each refuge for both groups.
Mission 1Read the source

Which refuge is safe?

> "Men, driven by fear, go to many a refuge, to mountains and forests, to groves and sacred trees." (Dhammapada 188, Müller 1881)

> "He who takes refuge with Buddha, the Law, and the Church; he who, with clear understanding, sees the four holy truths" (Dhammapada 190, Müller 1881)

  • Müller's "the Law" is Dhamma; "the Church" is Sangha. Verse 192: that refuge is "the best refuge".
  • Modern alternative refuges: materialism (possessions), relationships, secular values (career, success, science), political beliefs (nation, ideology).
  • Buddhist analysis: worldly refuges, ancient (sacred trees) or modern (money, status), are impermanent (anicca), so they cannot give final security. They may still be wholesome supports.

Q1 What drives people to the first kind of refuge?

Show answer

Fear.

Q2 Why might a Buddhist say a bank account is a refuge like the "sacred trees"?

Show answer

It is relied on for safety, but it changes and cannot end dukkha.

Mission 1Evaluate

Which refuge matters most?

  • Claim: The Dhamma is the most important of the three refuges.
  • For: before his death the Buddha said the Dhamma and discipline would be the teacher after him (Digha Nikaya 16).
  • For: the Buddha and the Sangha both point to the Dhamma; "whoever sees the Dhamma sees me".
  • Against: without the Buddha, the Dhamma would not have been found and taught.
  • Against: without the Sangha, the Dhamma would not have been kept or passed on; for many converts, the Sangha is what makes practice possible.
  • Against: refuge is taken in all three together; the jewels may not be meant to be ranked.
  • Judgement criteria: Which refuge is most needed now that the Buddha has died? Do heritage and convert Buddhists answer differently? Is ranking the jewels a Buddhist question?
Mission check

?A student says: "I only need the Dhamma. The Buddha and the Sangha are optional extras." Which reply best fits the traditional Buddhist practice of going for refuge?

Buddhists take refuge from dukkha in three linked jewels: the one who found the way, the way itself, and those who walk it.

Mission 2

Meditation on the path

  • Pali bhavana: "cultivation" or "bringing into being". Meditation is mind-training, not just sitting.
  • The Eightfold Path falls into three trainings: sila (ethics), samadhi (meditation, concentration), panna (wisdom).
  • The samadhi group: right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration (the 6th, 7th and 8th factors).
  • The trainings support each other: ethics steadies the mind; a steady mind sees clearly; clear seeing deepens ethics.
Mission 2

Two kinds of meditation

  • Samatha: calm, tranquillity. The mind settles on one object and becomes still.
  • Vipassana: insight, clear seeing. The mind looks into how things really are (see Mission 3).
  • An early text pairs them (AN 2.30, paraphrase): calm develops the mind and lets go of passion; insight develops discernment and lets go of ignorance.
  • Many Theravada teachers, following Buddhaghosa: calm suppresses the defilements for a while; insight uproots them.
  • Jhana (Sanskrit dhyana): a state of deep absorption reached through samatha.
Mission 2

How samatha is practised

  • Choose one object: the breath (anapanasati, mindfulness of breathing), a coloured disc (kasina), a recollection of the Buddha, or loving kindness.
  • Sit upright and still; return the mind each time it wanders.
  • Counting breaths (e.g. one to ten) is a common start.
  • Five hindrances block calm: sensual desire, ill will, sloth and torpor, restlessness and worry, doubt.
  • As they fade, a steady mental image or sign (nimitta) may arise and absorption becomes possible.

Taming the mind

"As a fletcher makes straight his arrow, a wise man makes straight his trembling and unsteady thought, which is difficult to guard, difficult to hold back." (Dhammapada 33, Müller)

"It is good to tame the mind, which is difficult to hold in and flighty, rushing wherever it listeth; a tamed mind brings happiness." (Dhammapada 35, Müller)

  • ("listeth" = wishes)

Q1 What does the image of the arrow-maker suggest meditation does?

Show answer

It straightens and steadies the mind, as skilled work shapes an arrow.

Q2 What result does verse 35 promise?

Show answer

Happiness: a tamed mind brings happiness.

Mission 2

Metta bhavana: loving kindness

  • Metta: loving kindness, a warm wish for the welfare of beings. Bhavana: cultivation.
  • Metta is one of the four brahmaviharas ("divine abodes"): loving kindness, compassion (karuna), sympathetic joy (mudita), equanimity (upekkha).
  • The Karaniya Metta Sutta (Sutta Nipata 1.8, paraphrase): as a mother protects her only child with her life, cultivate a boundless heart towards all beings.
  • Method (Buddhaghosa, Visuddhimagga IX): begin with oneself, then a dear person, a neutral person, a hostile person, and break down the barriers between them.
  • Many Western groups (e.g. Triratna) teach five stages: self, good friend, neutral, difficult, all beings.
  • Metta is a samatha practice that can lead to jhana; it is also the antidote to ill will.
  • Eduqas wording: metta bhavana, "loving kindness".
Quick check
?In Buddhaghosa's method of metta bhavana, why does the meditator begin by wishing themselves well?
Mission 2Diversity

Personalised practice

  • Buddhaghosa lists forty meditation subjects and matches them to temperaments (Visuddhimagga III):
  • Greedy temperament: reflection on the body's unattractiveness.
  • Hating temperament: the four brahmaviharas (e.g. metta).
  • Deluded or speculative temperament: mindfulness of breathing.
  • Faithful temperament: recollection of the Buddha, Dhamma and Sangha.
  • Traditionally a teacher (kalyanamitta, "good friend") chooses the subject for the student.
  • Traditions differ: Theravada stresses breath and the brahmaviharas; Tibetan schools add visualisation; Zen stresses sitting itself (see Mission 3).
Mission 2Evaluate

"Calm is only a preparation; insight is what frees the mind."

  • For: the Buddha learned deep formless states from his teachers Alara Kalama and Uddaka Ramaputta, then left them: calm alone did not end suffering (MN 26).
  • For: AN 2.30: only insight removes ignorance, the root of samsara.
  • For: many Theravada commentators say calm only suppresses defilements; insight uproots them.
  • Against: the Buddha awakened from the fourth jhana (the deepest of four absorptions, beyond pleasure and pain) and recalled childhood jhana as "the path" (MN 36).
  • Against: right concentration is the four jhanas: an essential factor, not an extra (SN 45.8).
  • Against: the American monk Thanissaro Bhikkhu argues that calm and insight develop together; metta also shapes moral character.
  • Judgement criteria: What do the earliest texts say? Can insight be stable without calm? Is ethics changed by calm as well as by insight?
  • Spec issue: "the relative importance of different traditions of meditation".
Mission check

?Priya has practised breath meditation for years. She can reach deep, joyful stillness, but her old irritation with her sister comes back as soon as she gets up. Most Theravada teachers, following Buddhaghosa, would explain this how?

Calm steadies the mind; it is a real part of the path, but it works with ethics and insight. A later mission: insight, mindfulness and zazen.

Mission 3

Vipassana: clear seeing

  • Vipassana (Pali; Sanskrit vipashyana): insight, "seeing clearly".
  • The meditator watches experience closely: breath, sensations, feelings, thoughts.
  • Aim: to see directly the three marks of existence:
  • Anicca: everything changes; nothing stays.
  • Dukkha: nothing conditioned can fully satisfy.
  • Anatta: no fixed, lasting self is found anywhere.
  • Seeing this weakens craving and ends ignorance: insight leads towards Nirvana (nibbana).
  • Samatha steadies the mind; vipassana uses that steadiness to see (see Mission 2).
Mission 3Read the source

Foundations of mindfulness

The Satipatthana Sutta (Majjhima Nikaya 10; also Digha Nikaya 22), paraphrase: a meditator is mindful of four things, seeing each arise and pass away:

  • Body (breathing, posture, parts of the body).
  • Feelings (pleasant, unpleasant, neutral).
  • Mind (e.g. a mind with or without anger).
  • Dhammas (mental qualities and teachings: the hindrances, the Four Noble Truths).

"'All created things perish,' he who knows and sees this becomes passive in pain; this is the way to purity." (Dhammapada 277, Müller)

"Without knowledge there is no meditation, without meditation there is no knowledge: he who has knowledge and meditation is near unto Nirvana." (Dhammapada 372, Müller)

Q1 Which mark of existence does verse 277 ask the meditator to see?

Show answer

Anicca: all created things perish.

Q2 What does verse 372 say about meditation and wisdom?

Show answer

Each needs the other; together they bring one near Nirvana.

Mission 3

Right mindfulness

  • Sati: mindfulness; the word also carries the sense of remembering (keeping the Dhamma in mind).
  • Right mindfulness is the 7th factor of the Eightfold Path.
  • It runs through all practice:
  • In ethics: noticing anger before it becomes harsh speech; remembering the precepts.
  • In samatha: noticing when the mind wanders and bringing it back.
  • In vipassana: watching things arise and pass.
  • In daily life: walking meditation, eating, working with full attention.
  • "Right" matters: mindfulness joined to wisdom and good intention, not attention for any purpose.

Mission 3

Ways of practising insight

  • Mahasi Sayadaw (1904–1982), Burma: mentally noting each experience ("rising", "falling" of the abdomen; "hearing", "thinking").
  • S. N. Goenka (1924–2013), in the tradition of Sayagyi U Ba Khin (1899–1971): ten-day silent courses; three days of breath awareness, then scanning bodily sensations with equanimity; metta on the last day.
Mission 3

Zazen: just sitting

  • Zazen: Japanese, "seated meditation"; central to Zen.
  • Posture: lotus or half-lotus, back upright, eyes half-open, hands in an oval mudra.
  • Soto Zen (Dogen, 1200–1253): shikantaza, "just sitting": no object, no goal-seeking. Sitting itself expresses the Buddha nature already present.
  • Rinzai Zen: zazen often with a koan (a paradoxical question) to break through ordinary thinking towards satori or kensho (awakening, seeing one's nature).
  • Eduqas wording: zazen, "just sitting".
  • Unlike samatha it has no chosen object; unlike vipassana it does not analyse experience into parts.
Quick check
?A practitioner sits facing a wall, upright, with no object to focus on and no state to reach, simply sitting with full awareness. Which practice is this?
Mission 3

Meditation in lived Buddhism

  • Purpose: Enlightenment (awakening); on the way, calm, kindness and wisdom grow.
  • For centuries, mainly monks, especially forest monks, meditated intensively.
  • Many lay Buddhists in Asia practise mainly through chanting (refuges, precepts, protective paritta), giving (dana) and study, and keeping precepts.
  • From c. 1900, Burmese teachers (e.g. Ledi Sayadaw, then Mahasi and U Ba Khin) taught insight meditation to lay people; today it is central for many lay and Western Buddhists.
  • Value for all Buddhists today: debated in the later screen.
Mission 3Evaluate

"Meditation is the most important element of Buddhist practice."

  • For: the Buddha awakened in meditation; only insight removes ignorance.
  • For: Dhammapada 372: knowledge needs meditation; together they bring one near Nirvana.
  • For: many modern lay and Western Buddhists put meditation at the centre of practice.
  • Against: ethics (sila) is the first training; meditation built on bad conduct goes wrong.
  • Against: many lay Buddhists practise mainly through giving, chanting and precepts; Pure Land Buddhists rely on Amida's vow.
  • Against: the trainings depend on each other, so none is "most" important; wisdom is the goal and meditation a means.
  • Judgement criteria: Important for whom (monastic, lay, Theravada, Pure Land)? Measured by the goal or by daily life? Can one training stand without the others?
  • Spec issue: "the value and relevance of meditation for all Buddhists today".
Mission check

?On day six of a ten-day silent course, Tom feels an itch. He does not scratch. He watches it grow, change and fade, and notices that it is not "his" in any lasting way. What practice is he doing, and with what Buddhist aim?

Insight turns calm attention into understanding: change, unsatisfactoriness and not-self, seen in one itch.

Mission 4

Dana: giving

  • Dana: generosity, giving. The first of the ten perfections (Theravada) and of the six perfections (Mahayana).
  • Three kinds of gift: material things; fearlessness (protection, safety); the Dhamma (teaching), counted highest.
  • Lay people give food to monks on the morning alms round, and robes at Kathina after the rains retreat.
  • Intention matters: giving with a glad mind before, during and after the gift.
  • Giving of time, money and possessions; the community supports those inside and outside it: e.g. temples feeding the poor; the Tzu Chi Foundation (Taiwan, 1966) in disaster relief.
Mission 4Read the source

Giving in the Dhammapada

"The uncharitable do not go to the world of the gods; fools only do not praise liberality; a wise man rejoices in liberality, and through it becomes blessed in the other world." (Dhammapada 177, Müller)

"The gift of the law exceeds all gifts; the sweetness of the law exceeds all sweetness; the delight in the law exceeds all delights; the extinction of thirst overcomes all pain." (Dhammapada 354, Müller)

  • ("liberality" = generosity; "the law" = the Dhamma; "thirst" = craving)

Q1 What result of generosity does verse 177 describe?

Show answer

Being "blessed in the other world": a good rebirth.

Q2 Which gift does verse 354 rank highest?

Show answer

The gift of the Dhamma, the teaching.

Mission 4

Punya: merit

  • Punya (Sanskrit; Pali puñña): merit, the good kamma produced by wholesome actions.
  • Three bases of making merit: giving (dana), ethics (sila), meditation (bhavana).
  • Merit brings happiness, a better rebirth and good conditions for practice; it carries over into future lives.
  • Eduqas: merit is "gained and accumulated", "carries over" and "may be transferred".
  • Beyond merit: the awakened one is "above good and evil, above the bondage of both" (Dhammapada 412, Müller): no longer making kamma at all.
Mission 4

Sharing merit

  • Theravada: after giving, people share (dedicate) the merit, often to dead relatives, while monks chant; water is poured from a small jug into a bowl.
  • Source: the Tirokudda Sutta (Khuddakapatha 7, paraphrase): departed relatives wait outside the walls; gifts to the Sangha dedicated to them help them.
  • Kamma is personal: "by oneself one is purified … no one can purify another" (Dhammapada 165, Müller).
  • So many Theravada teachers explain: the dead (or living) person rejoices in the good deed (anumodana) and so makes merit of their own. Sharing merit is itself generous, so the giver loses nothing.
  • Mahayana: parinamana: bodhisattvas turn over their vast merit to all beings; practice ends with a dedication of merit.
Quick check
?Theravada teaching holds that kamma is personal. How do many Theravada teachers explain the sharing of merit with a dead relative?
Mission 4Evaluate

"Giving in order to gain merit is not truly generous."

  • For: seeking a better rebirth is still a kind of craving; the goal is to go beyond merit (Dhammapada 412).
  • For: some reformist Buddhists criticise merit-making as calculating; the anthropologist Melford Spiro (Buddhism and Society, 1970) shows the aims can come apart, distinguishing merit-focused ("kammatic") from Nibbana-focused ("nibbanic") Buddhism.
  • For: Mahayana stresses giving merit away, not storing it.
  • Against: the wish to do good is wholesome desire, not craving; the Buddha praised the giver (Dhammapada 177).
  • Against: dana is training in letting go: the first perfection, the start of the path.
  • Against: merit-making binds lay and monastic communities together and supports the Sangha.
  • Judgement criteria: Does motive cancel the good of the gift? Is merit an end, or a stage? Are lay and monastic goals rightly different?
  • Spec issue: "the relative importance of dana and punya compared with other Buddhist teachings".
Mission check

?On her late father's birthday, Mrs Lee, a Thai Buddhist in Leeds, gives food to the monks at a local vihara. While they chant, she slowly pours water from a small jug into a bowl. Which explanation would most Theravada Buddhists give?

Bowing and giving work on the mind of the one who gives, and merit is shared by rejoicing. Mahayana Buddhists add that buddhas and bodhisattvas respond.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Going for refuge to the Buddha, Dhamma and Sangha, chanted three times in Pali, marks a person as a Buddhist.
  • Buddha: historical teacher and ideal of awakening; Dhamma: teaching and ultimate truth; Sangha: noble, monastic, fourfold, all Buddhists, spiritual friends.
  • Heritage and convert Buddhists share the formula but often live it differently; Buddhists see other "refuges" today as impermanent.

Mission 2

  • Meditation (bhavana) is the samadhi training of the Eightfold Path: right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration.
  • Samatha calms the mind (breath, kasinas, metta) and can lead to the jhanas; vipassana gives insight (see Mission 3).
  • Metta bhavana, personalised subjects and debate about how calm relates to insight show the variety of Buddhist practice.

Mission 3

  • Vipassana is insight into the three marks (anicca, dukkha, anatta); right mindfulness (sati) runs through all practice.
  • Styles differ: Mahasi noting, Goenka body-scanning, and Zen zazen (just sitting, or with koans).

Mission 4

  • Puja, images and stupas focus devotion on the Buddha. Theravada: he does not respond; devotion trains the mind and makes merit. Mahayana: buddhas and bodhisattvas can respond.
  • Dana makes punya (merit), which can be shared.
