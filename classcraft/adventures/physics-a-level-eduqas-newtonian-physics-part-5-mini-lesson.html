Mission 1

Mission 1: Keep cooling a gas in a sealed box. At what temperature would its pressure reach zero?

Make a guess: Keep cooling a gas in a sealed box. At what temperature would its pressure reach zero?

Mission 1

Pressure and the mole

  • p = F/A, F the force perpendicular to the surface. 1 Pa = 1 N m⁻².
  • A gas pushes perpendicular to every surface it touches.
  • 1 mole: NA = 6.02 × 10²³ particles (atoms or molecules).
  • Amount of substance n = N/NA.
  • Molar mass M: the mass of one mole. n = total mass ÷ M.
  • M in kg mol⁻¹ = Mr/1000. Helium: Mr = 4.0, M = 4.0 × 10⁻³ kg mol⁻¹.
Quick check
?A cylinder contains 0.64 kg of oxygen gas, which consists of O2 molecules. The molar mass of oxygen atoms is 16 g mol⁻¹, and NA = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹. How many oxygen molecules are in the cylinder?
Quick check
?Predict first. Students measure the pressure of three samples of gas, each sealed in a rigid container, at several temperatures between 0 °C and 100 °C. The samples are of different gases and different amounts, and each behaves as an ideal gas. For each sample they plot pressure against temperature in °C and get a straight line. They then extend each best-fit line to zero pressure. What do they find?
Mission 1

Absolute zero by extrapolation

  • Fixed volume of gas: measure pressure at several temperatures, 0–100 °C.
  • Plot pressure against θ in °C: a straight line.
  • Extend the line to zero pressure: it meets the axis at about −273 °C.
  • Every gas, any amount: the same intercept.
  • This is absolute zero: 0 K = −273.15 °C.
  • T(K) = θ(°C) + 273.15 (often + 273).
Mission 1

The ideal gas equation

  • pV = nRT (n moles) and pV = NkT (N molecules)
  • R = 8.31 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ (molar gas constant).
  • k = R/NA = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹ (Boltzmann constant).
  • SI units only: pressure in Pa, V in m³, T in K.
Mission 1

Worked example: a gas cylinder

  • Rigid cylinder of helium: V = 0.020 m³, pressure 2.0 × 10⁵ Pa, θ = 20 °C → T = 293 K.
  • Moles: n = pV/RT = (2.0 × 10⁵ × 0.020)/(8.31 × 293) = 1.6 mol.
  • Molecules: N = pV/kT = (2.0 × 10⁵ × 0.020)/(1.38 × 10⁻²³ × 293) = 9.9 × 10²³.
  • Mass: n × M = 1.64 × 4.0 × 10⁻³ = 6.6 × 10⁻³ kg.
  • Warmed to 60 °C (333 K), V fixed: pressure ∝ T, so the pressure = 2.0 × 10⁵ × 333/293 = 2.3 × 10⁵ Pa.
Quick check
?A bubble of gas of volume 2.0 cm³ is released at the bottom of a lake, where the pressure is 3.0 × 10⁵ Pa and the temperature is 7 °C. It rises to the surface, where the pressure is 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa and the temperature is 17 °C. Treat the gas as ideal, with no gas gained or lost. What is its volume at the surface?
Mission 1

Reading gas graphs

  • p against V, constant T: a curve (pV = constant). Hotter gas: curve further from the origin. Each curve is an isotherm.
  • pV against p, constant T: horizontal line.
  • V against T (constant p) and p against T (constant V): straight lines through 0 K.
  • On p–V axes, the product pV at a point is proportional to T (fixed amount of gas).
Quick check
?The pressure–volume diagram shows three states, A, B and C, of a fixed amount of ideal gas. Which ranks the temperatures of the gas in the three states, from highest to lowest?
Mission check

?A sealed, rigid container holds an ideal gas at 7 °C. The gas is heated to 287 °C. How does its new pressure compare with its starting pressure?

Pressure ∝ T in kelvin. Every gas line points to the same zero: 0 K = −273 °C, absolute zero.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How fast are the air molecules around you moving right now?

Make a guess: How fast are the air molecules around you moving right now?

Mission 2

The ideal gas model

  • A very large number of particles (atoms or molecules) in rapid, random motion.
  • Particle volume is negligible compared with the volume of the container.
  • Collisions with each other and the walls are perfectly elastic: no kinetic energy lost.
  • Collision time is negligible compared with the time between collisions.
  • No forces between particles except during collisions.
  • Energy is randomly distributed among the molecules: at any instant they have a wide range of speeds.
Quick check
?Careful measurements show that at very high pressures a real gas no longer obeys the ideal gas equation closely. Which reasoning, based on the assumptions of the ideal-gas model, best supports this observation?
Mission 2

Pressure from collisions

  • A molecule of mass m hits a wall with velocity component u perpendicular to it and rebounds elastically.
  • Its momentum changes by 2mu. So the wall pushes on it, and it pushes back on the wall (Newton's third law).
  • Many hits every second → an average force on the wall. Pressure = total perpendicular force ÷ area.
  • Smaller volume (T fixed): same speeds, but hits on each square metre are more frequent → pressure rises.
Quick check
?A sealed, rigid container of ideal gas is heated. What happens to the pressure of the gas, and why?
Mission 2

Temperature measures mean kinetic energy

  • ½mc̄² = (3/2)kT, where c̄² is the mean square speed
  • Mean translational kinetic energy per molecule ∝ absolute temperature.
  • Translational: moving from place to place. Molecules such as N2 also spin; that energy is extra.
  • Same temperature → same mean translational kinetic energy, whatever the gas.
  • In this model, as T → 0 K this mean kinetic energy → 0: absolute zero.
  • k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹, the Boltzmann constant.
Mission 2

Worked example: root-mean-square speed

  • rms speed: square every speed, take the mean, then the square root.
  • Speeds 300, 400, 500 m s⁻¹: mean = 400; rms = √((300² + 400² + 500²)/3) = 408 m s⁻¹. Not the same.
  • Nitrogen, N2, at 300 K.
  • m = 0.028 kg mol⁻¹ ÷ 6.02 × 10²³ = 4.65 × 10⁻²⁶ kg.
  • Mean translational kinetic energy = 1.5 × 1.38 × 10⁻²³ × 300 = 6.21 × 10⁻²¹ J.
  • rms speed = √(3kT/m) = √(3 × 1.38 × 10⁻²³ × 300 ÷ 4.65 × 10⁻²⁶) = 517 m s⁻¹.
Quick check
?A sealed container of gas is heated from 27 °C to 177 °C. By what factor does the mean kinetic energy of the gas molecules increase?
Mission 2

Internal energy of an ideal gas

  • Internal energy: total random kinetic energy of the particles + total potential energy from the forces between them.
  • Ideal gas: no forces between particles → no potential energy. U is all kinetic.
  • Monatomic: U = (3/2)NkT = (3/2)nRT
  • U depends only on T (for a fixed amount). Same T → same U, whatever the volume.
Mission check

?A container holds a mixture of hydrogen (H2) and oxygen (O2) at one temperature. An oxygen molecule has 16 times the mass of a hydrogen molecule. What is (rms speed of the hydrogen molecules)/(rms speed of the oxygen molecules)?

Temperature fixes the mean translational kinetic energy. For air (mostly N2) at room temperature that means rms speeds near 500 m s⁻¹, faster than a passenger jet.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Molecules hit the walls at random. How can that add up to a pressure you can calculate exactly?

Make a guess: Molecules hit the walls at random. How can that add up to a pressure you can calculate exactly?

Mission 3

Using the kinetic theory equation

  • p = ⅓ρc̄² = ⅓(N/V)mc̄²
  • Nm is the total mass of gas, so Nm/V = ρ and p = ⅓ρ × (mean square speed).
  • Air: p = 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa, ρ = 1.2 kg m⁻³.
  • Mean square speed = 3p/ρ = 2.5 × 10⁵ m² s⁻². rms speed = 500 m s⁻¹.
Quick check
?Nitrogen in a cylinder has a density of 3.40 kg m⁻³. The root-mean-square speed of its molecules is 517 m s⁻¹. Treat it as an ideal gas. What is its pressure?
Mission 3

Linking speed to temperature

  • Theory: p = ⅓(N/V)mc̄², so pV = ⅓Nmc̄². Experiment: pV = NkT.
  • Equate: ⅓Nmc̄² = NkT, so mc̄² = 3kT.
  • Halve both sides: ½mc̄² = (3/2)kT. Mean translational KE ∝ T.
  • One mole of monatomic gas has NA molecules: total translational KE = NA × (3/2)kT = (3/2)RT, using k = R/NA.
Quick check
?An ideal gas is heated from 27 °C to 927 °C. By what factor does the rms speed of its molecules increase?
Mission check

?Two sealed containers of equal volume are at the same temperature. Each holds the same number N of molecules of an ideal gas. Each molecule of gas Y has 4 times the mass of a molecule of gas X. What is (pressure of Y)/(pressure of X)?

About 10²⁷ collisions on each square metre every second average out. Momentum change per hit × hits per second gives a steady force: pV = ⅓Nm × (mean square speed), which agrees with pV = NkT.

Mission 4

Mission 4: In a cold room, metal feels colder than wood. Is it really at a lower temperature?

Make a guess: In a cold room, metal feels colder than wood. Is it really at a lower temperature?

Quick check
?A 0.20 kg copper block at 80 °C is lowered into a large tank containing 200 kg of water at 20 °C. The water has far more internal energy than the block. Which statement describes the resultant thermal energy transfer?
Mission 4

Which way does energy flow?

  • Temperature decides the direction: net energy transfer is always from higher to lower temperature.
  • Not "more energy to less": a hot cup can lose energy to a huge cold bath.
  • Heat is energy in transit because of a temperature difference. A body does not "contain heat".
  • Thermal equilibrium: no net energy transfer between bodies in contact. They are at the same temperature.
  • If A and B are each in thermal equilibrium with C (a thermometer), A and B are at the same temperature. This is why thermometers work.
Quick check
?Sample P is warmed from 10 °C to 30 °C. Sample Q is warmed from 290 K to 310 K. Which statement about the two temperature changes is correct?
Mission 4

Internal energy

  • Internal energy = total random kinetic energy + total potential energy of the particles.
  • KE: random motion (vibration in solids; moving about in liquids and gases).
  • PE: from the forces between particles. It depends on their separation.
  • The energies are randomly distributed: particles have a range of energies.
  • Not included: the KE of the whole object moving, or its gravitational PE.
  • Heat it → internal energy rises, and (unless it is changing phase) its temperature rises.
  • Temperature ≠ internal energy. A bath at 40 °C has far more internal energy than a cup at 40 °C: many more particles.
Quick check
?You have 1.0 kg of ice at 0 °C and 1.0 kg of liquid water at 0 °C. Which statement about their internal energies is correct?
Mission check

?A thermometer at room temperature, 20 °C, is placed in a cup of water at 70 °C. Its reading rises for a while and then stays steady. Why does the steady reading give the temperature of the water?

Metal and wood left in the same room reach thermal equilibrium with it: same temperature.

Metal feels colder because it conducts energy away from your warmer hand much faster.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Squash a gas and it warms up, though nothing hot touched it. Where did the energy come from?

Make a guess: Squash a gas and it warms up, though nothing hot touched it. Where did the energy come from?

Mission 5

Two ways to change internal energy

  • Internal energy U: random kinetic + potential energy of the particles. A property of the gas's state.
  • Heating: energy crosses the boundary because of a temperature difference.
  • Work: energy crosses the boundary because a force moves it (a piston).
  • Heat and work are energy in transit. A gas does not "contain" heat or work; it contains internal energy.
Mission 5

The first law

  • Conservation of energy for a gas: change in U = energy in by heating + work done on it.
  • ΔU = Q − W. Q: heat supplied to the gas. W: work done by the gas.
Quick check
?A gas in a cylinder is supplied with 500 J of heat. At the same time it expands, doing 200 J of work on the piston. Using ΔU = Q − W, where W is the work done by the gas, what is the change in internal energy ΔU of the gas?
Mission 5

Work done by a gas

  • Gas at constant pressure pushes a piston of area A a distance Δx.
  • Work = force × distance = pA × Δx = pΔV (p = pressure).
  • Work done by the gas = pΔV. Expansion: positive. Compression: negative (work is done on the gas).
  • Units: Pa × m³ = N m⁻² × m³ = J.
Mission 5

Work as an area

  • On a pressure–volume graph, the work done = area under the graph, between the start and end volumes.
  • Constant pressure: a rectangle, pressure × ΔV.
  • Changing pressure: count squares, or use a trapezium for a straight line.
  • Expansion (moving right): work done by the gas. Compression (moving left): work done on the gas.
Mission 5

Worked example: heating at constant pressure

  • A monatomic ideal gas at 2.0 × 10⁵ Pa expands from 1.0 × 10⁻³ m³ to 2.5 × 10⁻³ m³. Pressure stays constant.
  • pΔV = 2.0 × 10⁵ × 1.5 × 10⁻³ = 300 J of work done by the gas.
  • nRΔT = Δ(pV) = pΔV = 300 J, so ΔU = (3/2)nRΔT = 450 J.
  • ΔU = Q − W: 450 = Q − 300 → Q = 750 J.
Quick check
?A fixed mass of an ideal gas is taken from state X to state Y. On a p–V graph the process is a straight line from X (1.0 × 10⁻³ m³, 4.0 × 10⁵ Pa) to Y (4.0 × 10⁻³ m³, 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa), where each state is given as (volume, pressure). What is the work W, as it appears in ΔU = Q − W, for this process?
Mission 5

Reading the signs

  • Negative Q: the gas loses energy by heating its surroundings.
  • Negative ΔU: internal energy falls, so the temperature of an ideal gas falls.
  • Negative W: work is done on the gas (it is compressed).
  • Constant volume: no work, so all the heat supplied goes into U.
  • Solids and liquids barely change volume: W is negligible, so Q = ΔU.
Quick check
?A sample of 8.0 g of helium, a monatomic ideal gas of molar mass 4.0 g mol⁻¹, is heated at constant pressure from 20 °C to 80 °C. The thermal energy supplied is 2.49 × 10³ J. R = 8.31 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. What is the change in internal energy ΔU of the helium?
Mission check

?An ideal gas is in a cylinder whose walls and piston are perfectly insulated. The gas expands, pushing the piston outwards. What happens to the temperature of the gas?

Energy in = energy out + change stored. Push a piston in and you do work on the gas: its internal energy, and so its temperature, rises. Let it push out with no heating, and it cools.

Mission 6

Mission 6: A gas is taken round a loop and ends exactly where it started. Where did the energy it took in go?

Make a guess: A gas is taken round a loop and ends exactly where it started. Where did the energy it took in go?

Mission 6

A path in steps

  • Split a path on the p–V graph into simple steps.
  • Constant volume step: W = 0, so ΔU = Q.
  • Constant pressure step: W = pΔV.
  • Every step: ΔU = (3/2)nRΔT = (3/2)Δ(pV) for a monatomic ideal gas.
  • Then Q = ΔU + W (from ΔU = Q − W).
Mission 6

Worked example: two steps

  • Monatomic ideal gas. A (1.0 × 10⁻³ m³, 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa) → B (1.0 × 10⁻³ m³, 2.0 × 10⁵ Pa) → C (3.0 × 10⁻³ m³, 2.0 × 10⁵ Pa).
  • A → B (constant V): W = 0. ΔU = 1.5 × (200 − 100) = 150 J. Q = 150 J.
  • B → C (constant p): W = 2.0 × 10⁵ × 2.0 × 10⁻³ = 400 J. ΔU = 1.5 × (600 − 200) = 600 J. Q = 600 + 400 = 1000 J.
  • Whole path: Q = 1150 J, W = 400 J, ΔU = 750 J. Check: 1150 − 400 = 750. ✓
  • (pV values in J: A 100, B 200, C 600.)
Quick check
?A sample of ideal monatomic gas in state A has pressure p0 and volume V0. It is heated at constant pressure until its volume is 2V0 (state B), then heated at constant volume until its pressure is 3p0 (state C). Using ΔU = Q − W, what is the total energy transferred to the gas by heating from A to C?
Mission 6

Negative values

  • Continue: C → D cools at constant V to 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa; D → A compresses at 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa back to 1.0 × 10⁻³ m³.
  • C → D: W = 0, ΔU = 1.5 × (300 − 600) = −450 J, so Q = −450 J.
  • D → A: W = 1.0 × 10⁵ × (1.0 − 3.0) × 10⁻³ = −200 J. Negative: work is done on the gas.
  • ΔU = 1.5 × (100 − 300) = −300 J. Negative: U falls, so T falls.
  • Q = ΔU + W = −300 + (−200) = −500 J. Negative: the gas gives out 500 J by heating.
Quick check
?In one process, a gas has Q = −120 J and W = −200 J, using ΔU = Q − W with W the work done by the gas. Which description is correct?
Mission 6

Round a cycle

  • A → B → C → D → A ends at the starting state: same p, V, T, so ΔU = 0 for the cycle.
  • So net Q = net W.
  • Net W = 0 + 400 + 0 − 200 = 200 J = the area enclosed by the loop (1.0 × 10⁵ × 2.0 × 10⁻³).
  • Net Q = 150 + 1000 − 450 − 500 = 200 J. ✓
  • Clockwise on a p–V graph: the gas does net work on its surroundings. This is how a heat engine works.
Quick check
?A gas is taken anticlockwise round a loop on a p–V graph. The loop encloses an area equivalent to 150 J. Using ΔU = Q − W, which is correct for one cycle?
Mission check

?A heat pump warms a house. Its working gas goes round a cycle. In each cycle, 300 J of work is done on the gas while it is compressed, the gas does 100 J of work while it expands, and it takes in 600 J by heating from the cold air outside. How much energy does it give out by heating to the house in each cycle?

Over a full loop ΔU = 0, so net Q = net W. In an engine the gas does net work; in a heat pump net work is done on it, so it gives out more by heating than it takes in. Nothing is stored or destroyed.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Extrapolating pressure against θ (°C) to zero pressure gives absolute zero, −273 °C.
  • pV = nRT = NkT; k = R/NA.

Mission 2

  • Ideal gas: many particles in random motion, negligible volume, elastic collisions, no forces except in collisions.
  • Pressure comes from momentum changes as particles hit a surface.
  • Mean translational KE per particle ∝ absolute temperature: (3/2)kT. Same T, same mean translational KE; lighter particles move faster.
  • Ideal gas internal energy is all kinetic: U = (3/2)nRT for a monatomic gas.

Mission 3

  • p = ⅓ρc̄².
  • Combine with pV = NkT: ½mc̄² = (3/2)kT.

Mission 4

  • Net energy transfer goes from higher to lower temperature until thermal equilibrium (same temperature, no net transfer).
  • Internal energy = random KE + intermolecular PE of the particles; absolute temperature measures the average random KE. T (K) ≈ temperature in °C + 273; temperature changes are the same in K and °C.

Mission 5

  • Heat and work are energy in transit; the first law is conservation of energy.
  • ΔU = Q − W, W done by the gas; W = pΔV or the area under the p–V graph.

Mission 6

  • Split a path into constant-volume and constant-pressure steps; ΔU = (3/2)Δ(pV); Q = ΔU + W.
  • Negative W: compression. Negative Q: heat given out. Negative ΔU: temperature falls.
  • Round a cycle ΔU = 0, so net Q = net W = area enclosed by the loop.
