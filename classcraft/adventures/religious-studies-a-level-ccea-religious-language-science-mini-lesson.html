Mission 1

Why talking about God is hard

  • We learn words from finite things we can see and share: "wise" from wise people, "father" from fathers.
  • Classical theists believe God is infinite, timeless, not a physical object, and not one thing among others: this is God's transcendence.
  • So the words may not fit: can a word learned in time describe a God outside time?
  • The question for this unit: can talk about God be meaningful, and if so, how?
Mission 1

Cognitive and non-cognitive

  • Cognitive language makes factual claims: it says how things are, so it is true or false. "God exists." "Jesus rose from the dead."
  • Non-cognitive language does something else: it expresses feelings or commitments, commands, praises or shapes a way of life. It is not true or false as a report of fact. "Lord, have mercy." "Hallelujah."
  • Most believers take central claims ("God created the world") as cognitive: they mean them as true.
  • Hare's bliks, and some readings of Wittgenstein, treat religious language as mainly non-cognitive (–f).
  • The difference matters: a challenge such as verification (see Mission 3) is aimed at cognitive claims.

Mission 1

Univocal and equivocal

  • Univocal: a word means exactly the same in both uses. "The cat is black"; "the car is black".
  • If "wise" is univocal in "Sophie is wise" and "God is wise", God's wisdom is just like ours, only bigger. The danger: anthropomorphism, making God in human form (a God who learns, forgets, changes mind).
  • Equivocal: the same word with completely different meanings. A cricket bat; a bat in a cave.
  • If "wise" is equivocal, it tells us nothing about God. Why then call God wise rather than foolish? The danger: we can know nothing about God at all.
  • Medieval thinkers asked: is there a path between these two?
  • A third route: the via negativa ("negative way") speaks of God only by saying what God is not (not finite, not in time, not a body). It avoids anthropomorphism, but may say nothing positive.
Mission 1Read the source

Aquinas: neither the same nor different

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274), Summa Theologiae I, question 13, article 5 (c. 1268):

> "Univocal predication is impossible between God and creatures."

> "Neither, on the other hand, are names applied to God and creatures in a purely equivocal sense, as some have said."

  • His reason against equivocal names: then "from creatures nothing could be known or demonstrated about God at all".

Q1 Why does Aquinas reject univocal language?

Show answer

God is not wise in the limited, separate way a human is wise; the word cannot mean the same.

Q2 What would follow if all words about God were equivocal?

Show answer

We could know or prove nothing about God from the world.

Mission 2

Analogy: the middle path

  • Recap (see Mission 1): univocal language makes God human-like; equivocal language tells us nothing.
  • Analogy: a word used of God and of creatures with a meaning that is related but not identical: partly the same, partly different.
  • Aquinas: analogy is "a mean between pure equivocation and simple univocation" (Summa Theologiae I.13.5).
  • Why it is possible: God is the cause of creatures, so creatures carry some likeness of God, however faint.
Mission 2Read the source

Aquinas on "healthy"

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274), Summa Theologiae I, question 13, article 5:

> "Therefore it must be said that these names are said of God and creatures in an analogous sense, i.e. according to proportion."

  • His example (paraphrase): "healthy" is said first of an animal. Medicine is called healthy because it causes health; urine is called healthy because it is a sign of health. One word, related meanings, all pointing to one thing.
  • He adds (I.13.6): goodness and wisdom are "applied primarily to God rather than to creatures", because they flow from God; but we learn the words from creatures first.

Q1 Why is "healthy" neither univocal nor equivocal here?

Show answer

The meanings differ but all relate to the health of the animal.

Q2 According to I.13.6, where does goodness exist most fully?

Show answer

In God; creatures have it by sharing in it.

Mission 2

Analogy of attribution

  • A quality is attributed to something because of its relation to the thing that has it properly, usually as cause or effect.
  • Aquinas' case: medicine is "healthy" because it causes health in the animal.
  • A common textbook case: we call bread "good", meaning the baker is skilled; the goodness of the bread comes from the baker.
  • Applied to God: God is the cause of all goodness and wisdom in the world, so the words can be used of God.
  • But Aquinas insists on more than "God causes good things": God is good, in a higher way (I.13.2, I.13.6).
Mission 2

Analogy of proper proportion

  • A quality belongs to each thing in proportion to its nature.
  • A good dog: loyal, obedient, healthy. A good person: honest, kind, wise. Same word, fitted to each kind of being.
  • So God's goodness is goodness in proportion to God's infinite nature: real goodness, but far beyond ours.
  • Like a scale: dog's goodness : dog's nature = human goodness : human nature = God's goodness : God's nature.
Quick check
?"A good knife cuts well; a good doctor heals; so God's goodness is goodness fitting God's own perfect nature." Which approach is this?
Mission 2

Cognitive or non-cognitive?

  • For Aquinas, "God is good" is true: it says something real about God, though we cannot fully grasp how. A cognitive, realist view.
  • Analogy gives real but limited knowledge: we know that God is good, not fully what God's goodness is like.
Mission 2Diversity

Who uses analogy today?

  • Catholic teaching: the Fourth Lateran Council (1215): between Creator and creature no likeness can be noted without a greater unlikeness (paraphrase). Erich Przywara (1889–1972), Analogia Entis (1932), defended the analogy of being.
  • Reformed Protestant: Karl Barth (1886–1968) called the analogy of being "the invention of Antichrist" (Church Dogmatics I/1, 1932, preface). For Barth, likeness to God is known only through God's revelation in Christ (analogy of faith).
  • Medieval critic: John Duns Scotus (c. 1266–1308): some words, such as "being", must be univocal, or we could not reason about God at all.
  • Modern philosophy: Richard Swinburne (b. 1934), The Coherence of Theism (1977), argues many words about God are used univocally, with care.
Mission 2Evaluate

Does analogy work?

  • Claim: Analogy lets believers speak meaningfully about God.
  • For: It avoids both errors: anthropomorphism (univocal) and emptiness (equivocal).
  • For: It fits how believers speak: "Father", "shepherd", "king" are meant as true, yet not literal.
  • For: It is grounded in creation: "the invisible things of him … are clearly seen, being perceived through the things that are made" (Romans 1:20, WEBBE), a verse Aquinas cites in I.13.5.
  • Against: It assumes God is the cause of creatures; a sceptic who rejects that has no reason to accept the analogy.
  • Against: Proportion with an unknown: if we do not know God's nature, we do not know what God's goodness amounts to.
  • Against: Suffering strains the analogy: what kind of goodness allows what we see?
  • Against: Attribution alone says only that God causes good, not that God is good, as Aquinas himself admits.
  • Judgement criteria: Does it tell us anything? Does it rest on contested premises? Does it fit worship and scripture?
Mission 2Worked answer

Comparing usefulness

Task: evaluate whether analogy is more useful than the via negativa.

  • Point: Analogy says more: "God is good" gives real, if limited, content; denials alone may say nothing.
  • Counter-point: The via negativa runs less risk: analogy may smuggle in human ideas of goodness.
  • Link to a scholar: Aquinas uses both: he begins with what God is not (ST I.3) and then speaks by analogy (ST I.13).
  • Judgement step: decide which criterion matters most (content, or safety from error), and argue from it.

Mission 3

Logical positivism

  • 1920s–30s: the Vienna Circle, a group of philosophers and scientists led by Moritz Schlick (1882–1936), with Rudolf Carnap and others.
  • Inspired by the success of science and by early Wittgenstein.
  • Their aim: a test for which statements are meaningful (tell us something about the world) and which are nonsense.
  • A. J. Ayer (1910–1989) attended the Circle's meetings in 1932–33 and brought its ideas to Britain in Language, Truth and Logic (1936).
Mission 3Read the source

Hume's fork

David Hume (1711–1776), An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding (1748), Section XII, Part III, on any book of theology or metaphysics:

> "Does it contain any abstract reasoning concerning quantity or number? No. Does it contain any experimental reasoning concerning matter of fact and existence? No. Commit it then to the flames: for it can contain nothing but sophistry and illusion."

  • The positivists took Hume's two prongs as the only two kinds of meaningful statement.

Q1 What two kinds of reasoning does Hume accept?

Show answer

Abstract reasoning about quantity and number, such as maths; and experimental reasoning about matters of fact.

Q2 Why would a positivist say a sermon on "God's love" fails Hume's test?

Show answer

It is not maths, and no experiment or observation could settle it.

Mission 3

Analytic and synthetic

  • Analytic statements are true (or false) by definition: "All bachelors are unmarried"; "2 + 2 = 4". Checking the meaning is enough. They tell us nothing new about the world.
  • Synthetic statements are true or false because of how the world is: "It is raining in Leeds"; "Water boils at 100 °C at sea level". You check them by experience.
  • Logical positivists: every meaningful statement is either analytic or empirically verifiable.
  • "God exists" is not true by definition (for them), and no observation could verify it.
Mission 3

Ayer's verification principle

  • Language, Truth and Logic (1936), ch. 1 (paraphrase): a statement is factually meaningful only if it is analytic, or if we know what observations would lead us to accept it as true or reject it as false.
  • Strong verification: the statement can be conclusively proved by experience. Ayer thought this too strict.
  • Weak verification (Ayer's choice): experience can make it probable.
  • In principle, not just in practice: Ayer's example, "there are mountains on the far side of the moon", was meaningful in 1936 because we knew what would verify it.
  • Result (ch. 6): talk about a transcendent God is literally meaningless: not false, but saying nothing. So "there is no God" is meaningless too; agnosticism, which treats the question as open, is also ruled out.

Mission 3

Problems for the principle

  • Self-defeating: the principle is neither analytic nor verifiable by observation, so by its own test it is meaningless. (Ayer's later reply: it is a proposal about how to use "meaningful", not a fact.)
  • Universal laws: "All water boils at 100 °C at sea level" can never be conclusively verified: we cannot test every case. Strong verification rules out science itself.
  • History: statements about the past ("Julius Caesar crossed the Rubicon") cannot be observed now.
  • Too generous: Ayer's revised version (2nd edition, 1946: "direct" and "indirect" verification) was shown by critics to let in almost any statement.
  • Ayer later said, in a BBC interview with Bryan Magee (1978), that much of the book's view was mistaken (paraphrase).
Mission 3

Hick: eschatological verification

  • John Hick (1922–2012), Faith and Knowledge (1957); "Theology and Verification" (1960).
  • Parable of the Celestial City (paraphrase): two travellers walk one road. One believes it leads to the Celestial City; the other believes it leads nowhere. They meet the same good and bad things on the way. Only at the final corner will one of them be shown to be right.
  • Christian claims are verifiable in principle, after death (eschaton, "the end"): if they are true, believers will experience it.
  • Asymmetry: verifiable if true, but not falsifiable if false (no one would be there to find out).
  • So, even on Ayer's weak principle, "God exists" is meaningful: we know what experience would verify it.
Mission 3Read the source

Face to face

> "For now we see in a mirror, dimly, but then face to face. Now I know in part, but then I will know fully, even as I was also fully known." (1 Corinthians 13:12, WEBBE)

  • Paul expects knowledge of God that is partial now and full later.
  • Hick's travellers live in the "now": the road is ambiguous. The "then" is the final corner.

Q1 How does this verse fit Hick's argument?

Show answer

It expects a future experience in which what believers hope for will be known directly.

Q2 Why would Ayer not be satisfied?

Show answer

The verifying experience depends on life after death, which is itself unverifiable now and contested.

Quick check
?Hick says Christian belief is verifiable but not falsifiable. Why not falsifiable?
Mission 3Evaluate

Does verification make religious language meaningless?

  • Claim: The verification principle shows that religious language is meaningless.
  • For: It demands what science demands: a clear link between words and experience.
  • For: Much God-talk is compatible with any experience; it is hard to see what it claims.
  • Against: The principle fails its own test; strong verification also rules out universal laws and history.
  • Against: Hick: religious claims are verifiable in principle, after death.
  • Against: Much religious language is not meant as empirical report; a test for science may not fit worship.
  • Against: Some claims have empirical content: e.g. that Jesus lived and died under Pontius Pilate.
  • Judgement criteria: Is the principle itself justified? Does any version avoid its problems? Does it fit what believers mean?
Mission 3Worked answer

Building a judgement

Task: evaluate the claim that Hick's eschatological verification answers Ayer.

  • Point: It meets Ayer's weak, in-principle test: we know what experience would verify the claim.
  • Counter-point: It assumes personal survival after death, which is just as unverifiable now.
  • Response: Hick says the test asks only what would verify a claim, not whether it can be done today, as with mountains on the moon in 1936.
  • Judgement: Weigh which matters more: the logic of "in principle", or the fact that the verifying experience depends on another disputed claim.

Mission 4

Early Wittgenstein: the picture theory

  • Ludwig Wittgenstein (1889–1951), Austrian-British philosopher, Cambridge.
  • Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus (1921): a meaningful sentence is a picture of a possible fact: its parts match things in the world.
  • What cannot be pictured (ethics, God, the meaning of life) cannot be said; it can only show itself. The book ends by telling us to be silent about it (paraphrase).
  • The Vienna Circle read it as support for verification (see Mission 3). Wittgenstein later rejected the picture theory.
Mission 4

Meaning is use

  • Philosophical Investigations (published 1953, after his death): words are like tools in a toolbox: they do many different jobs (§11).
  • His builders (§2, paraphrase): one calls "Slab!" and the other brings a slab. The word is not a picture; it is part of an activity.
  • "The meaning of a word is its use in the language" (§43).
  • To find what a word means, ask how it is used, not what object it names.
Mission 4

Language games and forms of life

  • A language game: an activity with its own rules for how words are used. Wittgenstein's list (§23, paraphrase) includes giving orders, describing, telling a joke, thanking, greeting and praying.
  • "Love" means one thing in a tennis score, another in a wedding vow.
  • A form of life: the shared practices and way of living within which a language game makes sense.
  • Religious language belongs to a religious form of life: worship, prayer, confession, care for others.
  • So believers' words are meaningful to participants who know the rules, even if not cognitive in the scientific sense.
Quick check
?A doctor says a patient has "faith" in the treatment; a priest says a person has "faith" in God. What would Wittgenstein say about the word "faith" here?
Mission 4Read the source

Believing in a Last Judgement

From Wittgenstein's 1938 lectures on religious belief, published from students' notes as Lectures and Conversations (1966) (paraphrase):

  • Suppose someone believes in a Last Judgement, and I do not. Am I contradicting him? Wittgenstein says no.
  • The believer has a picture that guides his whole life: it is in his thoughts in every decision.
  • It is not like a weather forecast that evidence could make more or less likely. It is not reasonable in that sense, but not unreasonable either.
  • The unbeliever is not denying the same thing; he does not use that picture at all.

Q1 Why does Wittgenstein say he is not contradicting the believer?

Show answer

They are not making rival claims in one game; the believer's words play a different role in his life.

Q2 How does this differ from Hick's view of belief in life after death (see Mission 3)?

Show answer

Hick treats it as a factual claim verifiable in future; Wittgenstein treats it as a picture that regulates life.

Quick check

Mission 4Evaluate

Strengths and weaknesses of language games

  • Strengths: answers Ayer and Flew: religious language is meaningful within its game; respects how believers actually use words; explains why believers and atheists often talk past each other.
  • Weakness: most believers mean realist claims. "If Christ has not been raised, then our preaching is in vain and your faith also is in vain." (1 Corinthians 15:14, WEBBE)
  • Weakness: it rejects religious claims that could be empirically checked (e.g. historical claims about Jesus).
  • Weakness: it may allow no conversation between groups: outsiders cannot criticise, and believers cannot persuade.
  • Weakness: any coherent practice could be shielded from criticism, however odd (a worry raised by Kai Nielsen).
  • Weakness: it may not give adequate meaning to the word "God", as a real being who acts.
  • Judgement criteria: Does it fit what believers mean? Does it allow dialogue and criticism? Does it answer the verification challenge?
Mission 4Worked answer

Judging a non-cognitive view

Task: evaluate whether language games show that religious language is meaningful.

  • Point: Meaning is use (Wittgenstein, Philosophical Investigations, §43); prayer and worship have clear rules for those who take part.
  • Counter-point: Paul treats the resurrection as a fact (1 Corinthians 15:14); a game-only meaning loses that claim.
  • Develop: A Wittgensteinian such as D. Z. Phillips would reply that "fact" means something different in faith; critics answer that believers mean the ordinary sense.
  • Judgement: decide whether "meaningful to participants" is enough, or whether meaning must include the claim to be true.

Mission 5

How science reaches beliefs

  • Observation: notice a pattern in nature.
  • Hypothesis: propose an explanation that makes predictions.
  • Experiment: test the predictions under controlled conditions; others repeat the test.
  • Revise: if predictions fail, change or drop the hypothesis.
  • Reasoning is inductive: from many cases to a general law. So conclusions are provisional, open to new evidence.
  • Science explains natural events by natural causes (methodological naturalism). That is a rule of method, not itself a claim that nothing else exists.
  • The scientific world view goes further: the claim that only what science can study is real.
Mission 5

How religions reach beliefs

  • Revelation: God makes truth known, through scripture, prophets and (for Christians) Jesus.
  • Tradition and authority: communities interpret scripture over centuries (e.g. creeds; Catholic magisterium).
  • Religious experience: encounters taken as contact with God.
  • Reason: natural theology argues from the world to God (–).
  • Faith: trust and commitment, not only belief that a claim is true.
  • Religious belief develops too: doctrines are interpreted afresh, and readings of scripture change.
Mission 5

Connections and differences

  • Connections: both seek truth; both interpret evidence through reasoning; both work in communities that test and correct ideas.
  • Many pioneers of modern science (e.g. Kepler, Boyle, Newton) saw science as studying God's ordered creation.
  • Some philosophers treat God as the best explanation of the evidence, much like a scientific hypothesis.
  • Differences: science needs repeatable tests; revelation and experience are often unrepeatable and personal.
  • Science asks how natural processes work; religion also asks why there is anything, and what life is for.
  • Science holds conclusions provisionally; faith involves commitment that is not given up at the first difficulty.

Mission 5Read the source

Flew's invisible gardener

Antony Flew (1923–2010), "Theology and Falsification" (1950), adapting a parable by John Wisdom (paraphrase):

  • Two explorers find a clearing with flowers and weeds. One says: "A gardener tends this plot." The other disagrees.
  • They watch. No gardener appears. They put up an electric fence and patrol with bloodhounds. Nothing.
  • The believer says: "The gardener is invisible, intangible, silent."
  • The sceptic asks: how does such a gardener differ from an imaginary one, or no gardener at all?
  • Flew's point: a claim that nothing could ever count against dies "the death by a thousand qualifications".

Q1 What scientific step do the explorers carry out?

Show answer

They test a hypothesis by observation and experiment.

Q2 Why does Flew say the believer's claim loses its meaning?

Show answer

It is qualified until nothing could count against it, so it no longer asserts anything.

Mission 5

Kinds of truth

  • Empirical truth: matches what can be observed and tested.
  • Literal truth: a text means exactly what it says (e.g. six 24-hour days in Genesis 1).
  • Symbolic or mythological truth: a story teaches a truth that is not historical description (e.g. Genesis 1 teaches that the world depends on God and is good).
  • Existential truth: a belief is true in being lived by, shaping meaning and purpose.
  • Christian thinkers long warned against making scripture a science textbook: e.g. Augustine, The Literal Meaning of Genesis (c. 401–415), urged Christians not to contradict what can be known of nature by reason (paraphrase).
  • Many believers hold that religious statements make real truth-claims (e.g. "God exists") as well as symbolic ones.
Mission 5Diversity

Four ways to relate science and religion

Ian Barbour (1923–2013), Religion in an Age of Science (1990), sorted views into four models:

  • Conflict: one must defeat the other. Richard Dawkins (b. 1941), The God Delusion (2006): God is a scientific hypothesis, and a very improbable one. Some young-earth creationists also see conflict, from the other side.
  • Independence: separate domains that cannot clash. Stephen Jay Gould (1941–2002), Rocks of Ages (1999): "non-overlapping magisteria", science for facts, religion for meaning and value.
  • Dialogue: each raises questions for the other (e.g. why is nature orderly and understandable at all?).
  • Integration: both describe one reality. John Polkinghorne (1930–2021), physicist and Anglican priest: science and theology both seek truth through evidence and reflection.
Quick check
?"Science tells us how the universe works; religion tells us why it exists and how to live. They cannot clash." Which of Barbour's models does this express?
Mission 5Evaluate

Can science and religion conflict?

  • Claim: Science and religion answer different questions, so they cannot conflict.
  • For: Science studies natural causes; it cannot measure meaning, value or purpose.
  • For: Many scientists have held religious faith without contradiction.
  • Against: Many religious claims are about the world (creation, miracles, the soul), where science also speaks.
  • Against: Dawkins: the existence of God is itself a factual claim, open to evidence.
  • Against (from belief): some believers reject independence too, because faith claims real truth about the world.
  • Judgement criteria: Are religious claims factual, symbolic or both? Can a claim be meaningful if untestable (Flew)? Does the scientific method answer every question worth asking?
Mission 5Worked answer

Exploring a difference

  • Scholar: Flew's gardener: the believer's claim is qualified until nothing could count against it.
  • Develop: For Flew, an untestable claim may assert nothing. For a believer, God is not one more object in the world to be tested.
  • Link: So the difference is in what each counts as evidence, which shapes whether they can conflict.

Mission 6

Darwin: evolution by natural selection

  • Charles Darwin (1809–1882), On the Origin of Species (1859); humans included in The Descent of Man (1871).
  • Variation: individuals of a species differ.
  • Inheritance: many differences are passed on.
  • Struggle: more offspring are born than can survive.
  • Selection: those better suited to their environment survive and reproduce more.
  • Over vast time, small changes add up to new species: all life shares common descent.
  • This explains the appearance of design (as in Paley's watch) without a designer.
  • It challenges a literal reading of Genesis 1–2 and the idea of humans as a separate creation.
Mission 6Read the source

Darwin's closing sentence

On the Origin of Species, 1st edition (1859), final sentence (opening):

> "There is grandeur in this view of life, with its several powers, having been originally breathed into a few forms or into one;"

2nd edition (1860) onwards:

> "having been originally breathed by the Creator into a few forms or into one;"

  • Darwin later called himself an agnostic (letter, 1879).

Q1 What did Darwin add in 1860?

Show answer

The words "by the Creator".

Q2 Does natural selection, as Darwin states it here, rule out a creator?

Show answer

Not by itself: he describes life's origin as breathed by the Creator, and evolution as what followed.

Mission 6

Dawkins: the blind watchmaker

  • Richard Dawkins (b. 1941): The Blind Watchmaker (1986); The God Delusion (2006).
  • Natural selection is a blind watchmaker: it produces the appearance of design with no foresight or purpose.
  • It is not chance: tiny variations are selected cumulatively, step by step, so complex organs build up gradually.
  • A designer would be even more complex than what it explains: so "who designed the designer?"
  • Darwin, Dawkins says, made it possible to be an intellectually fulfilled atheist (paraphrase, 1986).
  • Responses: many theists reply that evolution explains how life develops, not why a universe exists whose laws allow it.
Quick check
?What does Dawkins mean by calling natural selection a "blind watchmaker"?
Mission 6Diversity

Religious responses to evolution

  • Young-earth creationism: Genesis 1 read literally: six days, a world thousands of years old; species created as separate kinds (e.g. Answers in Genesis).
  • Old-earth creationism: accepts an ancient universe ("days" as long ages), but holds that God created kinds, and humans, directly.
  • Theistic evolution (evolutionary creation): God creates through evolution.
  • Catholic teaching: Pius XII, Humani Generis (1950), allowed study of the evolution of the human body, with each soul created directly by God; John Paul II (1996) called evolution "more than a hypothesis".
  • Many Orthodox, Anglican and other Protestant Christians also accept evolution; many evangelicals are creationists.
  • Judaism, Islam and other faiths show a similar range of views.
Mission 6

Intelligent design and irreducible complexity

  • Intelligent design (ID): some features of life are best explained by an intelligent cause. ID claims to be science and does not name the designer.
  • Michael Behe (b. 1952), Darwin's Black Box (1996): irreducible complexity: a system that fails if any one part is removed (his model: a mousetrap; his case: the bacterial flagellum).
  • So, Behe argues, it could not be built step by step by natural selection.
  • Critiques: parts can evolve for other functions first, then be co-opted (some flagellum proteins work in a cell's secretion system); "not yet explained" is not "unexplainable" (God of the gaps).
  • Kitzmiller v. Dover (US federal court, 2005): ID is not science, so cannot be taught as such in public-school science classes.
  • Dawkins: ID is creationism renamed. Some theologians also object: God becomes a tinkerer inside the world, not its creator.
Quick check
?Which statement correctly distinguishes intelligent design from young-earth creationism?
Mission 6Evaluate

Do scientific origins make God unnecessary?

  • Claim: Scientific explanations of origins make belief in a creator unnecessary.
  • For: The Big Bang and evolution explain the universe's and life's development by natural causes.
  • For: Ockham's razor: do not add a cause that explains nothing extra. Dawkins: a designer is more complex than what it explains.
  • Against: Science starts from laws and an early state; it does not explain why anything exists (Aquinas).
  • Against: Many scientists see evolution as the way God creates; fine-tuning raises further questions.
  • Judgement criteria: Is "creator" a cause inside the chain of events, or the reason the chain exists? Is the scientific or the philosophical explanation more complete?
Mission check

?A biologist who is a practising Catholic says: "Life evolved over billions of years by natural selection, and God is the creator who holds the whole process in being." Which position is this?

For theistic evolution, the creator's role is not to fill gaps in the science but to give the whole process its existence.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Words learned from finite things struggle to describe an infinite, timeless God; cognitive language makes factual claims, non-cognitive language expresses, praises or guides.
  • Univocal language risks anthropomorphism; equivocal language risks emptiness; Aquinas rejects both (ST I.13.5).

Mission 2

  • Analogy is a middle path between univocal and equivocal language (Aquinas, ST I.13.5): words used of God are related but not identical.
  • Attribution names God through cause and effect; proper proportion fits each quality to each nature; both give real but limited knowledge.

Mission 3

  • Logical positivists (Vienna Circle; Ayer, Language, Truth and Logic, 1936): a statement is meaningful only if analytic or empirically verifiable; God-talk, atheism and ethics are meaningless.
  • Criticisms: the principle fails its own test and excludes universal laws and history; Hick replies that religious claims are verifiable (not falsifiable) after death, so meaningful.

Mission 4

  • Later Wittgenstein (Philosophical Investigations, 1953): meaning is use; language games within forms of life; religious language is meaningful to participants, though often read as not cognitive.
  • Strengths: answers verification and falsification; weaknesses: believers mean realist claims, no dialogue between games, thin meaning for "God".

Mission 5

  • Science reaches provisional conclusions by observation, hypothesis and repeatable experiment; religion by revelation, tradition, experience, reason and faith.
  • Flew's gardener: a claim that nothing could count against may assert nothing. Kinds of truth include empirical, literal, symbolic and existential.
  • Barbour's models: conflict (Dawkins), independence (Gould), dialogue, integration (Polkinghorne).

Mission 6

  • Darwin's natural selection explains apparent design; Dawkins' blind watchmaker has no foresight.
  • Responses: young-earth and old-earth creationism, intelligent design (Behe's irreducible complexity), theistic evolution.
