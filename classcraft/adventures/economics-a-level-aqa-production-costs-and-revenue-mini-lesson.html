Mission 1

Mission 1: If a kitchen hires twice as many cooks, why might it not make twice as many meals?

Make a guess: If a kitchen hires twice as many cooks, why might it not make twice as many meals?

Mission 1

Short run and long run

  • Fixed factor: an input whose quantity cannot be changed quickly, e.g. a kitchen, ovens, a factory building.
  • Variable factor: an input that can be changed quickly, e.g. hours of work, ingredients, energy.
  • Short run: at least one factor of production is fixed.
  • Long run: all factors are variable. The firm can build a bigger kitchen.
  • The split is not a set number of months: a market stall can grow in weeks; a power station takes years.
Mission 1

Total, average and marginal product

  • Total product (TP): total output from a given number of workers.
  • Average product (AP): output per worker. AP = TP ÷ number of workers.
  • Marginal product (MP): the extra output from one more worker. MP = change in TP ÷ change in workers.
  • Marginal product is also called marginal returns; AP is average returns.
  • The fixed factor stays the same throughout.

Mission 1

The law of diminishing returns

  • Law of diminishing marginal returns: in the short run, as more of a variable factor is added to a fixed factor, the marginal product of the variable factor eventually falls.
  • Why: each extra cook has less of the fixed factor to work with: fewer ovens and less space per cook.
  • It is not because later cooks are worse: all cooks are assumed identical.
  • It applies only in the short run. In the long run nothing is fixed.
  • Say: "diminishing marginal returns: MP falls" — not: "total output falls".

Mission 1

How MP and AP fit together

  • When MP is above AP, AP rises: a cook who adds more than the average pulls the average up.
  • When MP is below AP, AP falls.
  • So MP cuts AP at AP's maximum (in the café kitchen: the 3rd cook, MP = AP = 12).
  • TP is at its maximum when MP = 0 (the 6th cook).
  • Same logic as test scores: a new score above your average raises it.
Mission 1

From product to cost

  • Suppose each cook is paid the same wage per hour, and labour is the only variable cost.
  • Marginal cost (MC) = wage ÷ MP: the cost of one more meal.
  • Illustrative data: wage 60 per hour, same café kitchen.
    • 2nd cook: MP 14 → MC = 60 ÷ 14 ≈ 4.29.
    • 4th cook: MP 8 → MC = 60 ÷ 8 = 7.50.
    • 5th cook: MP 4 → MC = 60 ÷ 4 = 15.
  • Rising MP → falling MC. Falling MP → rising MC.
  • So diminishing marginal returns give the upward-sloping part of the MC curve (cost curves in Mission 3).
Quick check
?A workshop pays each worker the same wage. As it adds workers, the marginal product of labour falls. What happens to its marginal cost?
Mission 1

Labour productivity

  • Labour productivity = output ÷ labour input: output per worker or per hour worked.
  • What raises it:
    • more and better capital per worker (machines, computers)
    • technology: better methods and equipment
    • human capital: education, training, skills, health
    • better management and organisation; motivation
    • infrastructure (transport, broadband)
  • More workers sharing the same equipment means less capital per worker, so output per worker can fall.
  • Productivity is not production: a firm can produce more by hiring more workers while output per worker stays the same.
Mission 1

Worked example: why productivity matters

  • Illustrative data. A worker paid 600 a week makes 300 units.
  • Unit labour cost = 600 ÷ 300 = 2 per unit.
  • Training raises output to 360 units: productivity up (360 − 300) ÷ 300 × 100 = 20%.
  • Same wage: unit labour cost = 600 ÷ 360 ≈ 1.67.
  • Even if the wage rises 10% to 660: 660 ÷ 360 ≈ 1.83, still below 2.
  • Significance:
    • Competitiveness: lower costs per unit let firms cut prices or raise profits, at home and in export markets.
    • Wages: higher productivity lets firms pay higher real wages without raising costs per unit.
    • Growth: higher output per worker raises an economy's productive potential.

Mission 2

What is specialisation?

  • Specialisation: concentrating on a limited range of tasks or products.
  • It happens at every level:
    • Individual: a dentist, an electrician, a coder.
    • Firm: a company that only makes bicycle brakes.
    • Region: a city known for finance or for films.
    • Country: one economy exports coffee, another aircraft parts.
  • Division of labour: splitting the making of one product into separate tasks, each done by a different worker.
  • Division of labour is specialisation inside a firm.
Mission 2

Adam Smith's pin factory

  • Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations (1776), described a small pin workshop.
  • Pin-making was split into about 18 distinct operations: one draws the wire, one straightens it, one cuts it, one sharpens the point…
  • 10 workers made upwards of 48,000 pins a day: about 4,800 each.
  • Working alone and untrained, Smith wrote, each "certainly could not" have made twenty pins a day.
  • Smith's three reasons output soared:
    • Dexterity: repeating one task builds skill and speed.
    • Time saved: no switching between tasks and tools.
    • Machinery: a narrow task is easier to mechanise, so machines get invented.
  • Real case, 1913: Ford's moving assembly line cut the time to assemble a car from about 12.5 hours to 93 minutes by early 1914.
Mission 2

Worked example: output and cost per unit

A sandwich bar has 4 workers, each paid 12 per hour (illustrative data).

  • Before: each worker makes whole sandwiches: 30 per hour each, so 120 per hour in total.
  • After: one slices, one fills, one wraps, one serves: 200 per hour in total.
  • Step 1: output per worker after = 200 ÷ 4 = 50 per hour.
  • Step 2: labour productivity rises from 30 to 50: (50 − 30) ÷ 30 × 100 = +66.7%.
  • Step 3: labour cost per sandwich before = 12 ÷ 30 = 0.40.
  • Step 4: labour cost per sandwich after = 12 ÷ 50 = 0.24: down 40%.
  • Same workers, same wage, same hours: more output, lower cost per unit.

?A bakery has 6 workers, each paid 90 per day. When each worker bakes whole loaves, the bakery makes 120 loaves a day. After it divides the work into separate tasks, the same 6 workers make 180 loaves a day (illustrative data). What is the labour cost per loaf after the change?
Mission 2

Who gains?

  • Firms: higher labour productivity, lower unit costs, more output from the same inputs.
  • Workers: can build expert skills; easier to train for one task; possibly higher pay as productivity rises.
  • Consumers: lower prices, more output, often better quality from experts.
  • The economy: more output from scarce resources: higher living standards.
  • Countries: specialising and trading lets each focus on what it does best.
Mission 2

The downsides

  • Workers: repetitive work → boredom, lower motivation, more mistakes, absence and staff turnover.
  • Smith himself warned (Book V) that a lifetime of "a few simple operations" can leave a worker's mind dulled.
  • Narrow skills: if a task is automated or demand changes, workers face structural unemployment.
  • Interdependence: if one stage stops, the whole line stops.
  • Countries and regions: over-reliance on a few products; exposed to a shock in one market or supply chain.
  • Real case, 2021: a global shortage of computer chips forced car makers around the world to cut production.
  • Loss of variety: standardised products may cut consumer choice.
Quick check
?A car plant buys a key electronic part from a single specialist supplier abroad. A fire at that supplier's factory halts its output, and within a week the car plant stops its own production line. Which disadvantage of specialisation does this show best?
Mission 2

Evaluate: how far should division of labour go?

It depends on…

  • The size of the market. Smith: division of labour is "limited by the extent of the market". A village baker selling 50 loaves a day cannot keep 6 specialists busy.
  • The type of task. Routine, repeated tasks gain most. Creative or varied work (design, care work) gains less, and repetition can harm quality.
  • How jobs are designed. Job rotation and teamwork can keep productivity gains while cutting boredom and absence.
  • Time period. Gains appear quickly; the risks (deskilling, dependence on one supplier or one export) show up later.
  • Say: "labour productivity rose" (output per worker) — not: "production rose", which could just mean more workers.
Mission 2

Specialise, then exchange

  • A specialist makes far more of one thing than they need, and none of everything else.
  • So specialisation only works with exchange: selling what you make to buy what you need.
  • Individual: a nurse sells her labour for a wage, then buys food, rent and transport.
  • National: a country exports what it specialises in and imports the rest.
  • The more people specialise, the more they depend on trade, and on a cheap, trusted way to trade.
Mission 2

Barter and its problem

  • Barter: swapping goods or services directly, with no money.
  • Problem 1: it needs a double coincidence of wants: each side must want exactly what the other offers, at the same time.
  • Problem 2: no common measure: with 100 goods, every good needs a price in terms of the other 99 (4,950 exchange rates).
  • Problem 3: indivisibility: a cow cannot be split to buy one pair of shoes.
  • Money fixes this as a medium of exchange: anything widely accepted in payment. Sell to anyone for money, then buy from anyone.
  • So money makes specialisation and exchange far cheaper and quicker.
Quick check
?In a village with no money, a farmer has eggs and wants shoes. The shoemaker has shoes but wants a haircut, not eggs. What problem does this show, and how does money solve it?
Mission check

?A car-repair garage keeps the same 8 mechanics on the same hours. Instead of each mechanic doing whole jobs, each now does one type of job: tyres, brakes or servicing. Repairs completed per mechanic rise by a third. Which explanation fits the analysis of the division of labour?

Same workers, same hours, more output: the division of labour raises productivity.

Mission 3

Fixed and variable costs

  • Fixed costs (FC, or total fixed cost TFC): do not change with output in the short run. Rent, insurance, loan interest, salaried staff.
  • Fixed costs must be paid even at zero output.
  • Variable costs (VC, or total variable cost TVC): change with output. Ingredients, energy, packaging, hourly wages.
  • Total cost (TC) = fixed cost + variable cost.
  • In the long run all costs are variable: the bakery can move to a bigger site (see Mission 4).
Mission 3

Average and marginal cost

  • Average fixed cost: AFC = TFC ÷ Q.
  • Average variable cost: AVC = TVC ÷ Q.
  • Average total cost (often just average cost, AC): ATC = TC ÷ Q = AFC + AVC.
  • Marginal cost: the extra cost of producing one more unit. MC = change in TC ÷ change in Q.
  • Fixed costs do not change, so MC is also the change in variable cost.
  • Say: "marginal cost is the cost of the next unit" — not: "the average cost of all units".

?A firm's fixed costs are 200 a week. When it produces 40 units, its total variable cost is 600. Illustrative data. What is its average total cost at 40 units?
Mission 3

Why the curves have their shapes

  • MC falls at first, then rises. Why: with the ovens fixed, diminishing marginal returns set in. Each extra worker adds less output, so each extra batch needs more labour (MC = wage ÷ MP).
  • AFC falls all the time: the same fixed cost spread over more units.
  • AVC and ATC are U-shaped:
    • below its minimum, MC pulls the average down
    • above it, MC pulls the average up
  • So MC cuts AVC and ATC at their lowest points (bakery: AVC lowest at 3 batches, ATC at 4).
  • The gap between ATC and AVC is AFC, so it narrows as output rises.

Mission 3

What shifts the cost curves

  • Higher input prices (wages, energy, raw materials): AVC, ATC and MC shift up. AFC unchanged.
  • Higher fixed costs (rent, insurance, a licence fee): AFC and ATC shift up. AVC and MC unchanged.
  • Higher productivity (better technology, training, organisation): each worker makes more, so MC, AVC and ATC shift down.
  • Say: "a rise in fixed cost shifts ATC" — not: "it shifts MC".

Mission 4: Why do some industries have thousands of small firms while others have only two or three?

Mission 4

Short run and long run

  • Short run: at least one factor of production is fixed, usually capital (the size of the plant).
  • Long run: all factors can be changed. The firm can build a bigger plant or a smaller one.
  • So in the long run there are no fixed costs: all costs are variable.
  • The length of each period differs by industry: a café can add a coffee machine in a week; a steelworks takes years to build.
  • Diminishing returns (see Mission 1) are a short-run idea: they need a fixed factor.
  • Say: "the short run is the period when at least one factor is fixed" — not: "the short run is less than a year".
Mission 4

Returns to scale

  • Returns to scale: what happens to output when the firm changes all its inputs by the same proportion.
  • Double every input, then compare output:
  • Increasing returns to scale: output more than doubles.
  • Constant returns to scale: output exactly doubles.
  • Decreasing returns to scale: output less than doubles.
  • Returns to scale are long run: nothing is fixed.
  • Say: "decreasing returns to scale" for the long run — not: "diminishing returns", which is short run with one factor fixed.
Quick check
?A furniture maker increases its workers, machines and floor space by 50%. Its weekly output rises from 400 chairs to 560 chairs. What does this show?

  • Average cost = 1,000 ÷ 100 = 10 per unit.
  • It doubles all inputs: 20 workers, 4 machines. Input prices stay the same, so inputs cost 2,000.

  • With input prices constant: increasing returns → LRAC falls; decreasing returns → LRAC rises.
  • Factor prices up (e.g. higher wages or energy prices) → cost curves shift up. Productivity up → each unit needs fewer inputs → cost curves shift down.
Mission 4

Many short runs make one long run

  • Each plant size has its own U-shaped short-run average cost (SRAC) curve.
  • In the long run the firm picks the plant with the lowest average cost for the output it plans.
  • The long-run average cost (LRAC) curve traces the lowest average cost at each output. It is the envelope of the SRAC curves.
  • Each SRAC touches the long-run curve at one output and lies above it everywhere else.
  • So short-run average cost can never be below long-run average cost at the same output.
  • Where LRAC is falling, each SRAC touches it to the left of the SRAC's own minimum. Only at the LRAC minimum do the two minimums meet.
  • Say: "the LRAC is the envelope of the SRAC curves" — not: "the LRAC joins the minimum points of the SRAC curves".

Mission 4

The shape of the long-run curve

  • LRAC falls: economies of scale. LRAC flat: constant returns to scale (output and total cost grow in step). LRAC rises: diseconomies of scale.
  • Textbook shape: U-shaped. The reasons for each part are in Mission 5.
  • Many studies find an L-shaped LRAC: costs fall steeply, then stay roughly flat over a wide range. Diseconomies, if any, appear only at outputs firms rarely reach.
  • Say: "economies of scale lower average cost" — not: "economies of scale lower total cost". Total cost still rises with output.
Mission 4

Minimum efficient scale

  • Minimum efficient scale (MES): the lowest output at which long-run average cost reaches its minimum.
  • On a U-shaped curve: the bottom of the U (the start of the flat part, if there is one).
  • On an L-shaped curve: where the curve first flattens.
  • A firm producing below MES has higher average cost than a rival at or above MES.
  • The steeper the curve below MES, the bigger that cost penalty, and the harder it is for small firms to survive.
  • Say: "MES is the smallest output at the lowest average cost" — not: "MES is the biggest a firm can grow".
Mission 4

How many firms can a market hold?

  • Compare MES with market demand at a price equal to the minimum average cost.
  • Room for firms at MES ≈ market quantity ÷ MES.
  • Illustrative data. Market demand: 1,000,000 units a year.
  • MES of 10,000 → room for up to 100 firms: many small firms can compete (low concentration).
  • MES of 400,000 → room for only 2 firms at lowest cost: a few big firms (high concentration, oligopoly).
  • MES at or above total market demand → room for one firm at lowest cost: a natural monopoly.
Mission 4

Real world: large passenger jets

  • Designing a new airliner costs billions before the first plane is sold. Spreading that cost needs huge output.
  • Two firms make most of the world's large passenger jets.
  • Real data (company figures): in 2023 Airbus delivered 735 commercial aircraft and Boeing 528.
  • Compare hairdressers: low MES, small local markets, thousands of firms.
  • High MES relative to market demand is one reason a market ends up with few firms.
Quick check
?At a price equal to the lowest possible average cost, buyers want 600,000 units a year. The minimum efficient scale is 150,000 units a year (illustrative data). How many firms can the market hold, each producing at the lowest average cost?
Mission 4Evaluate

Does MES decide how many firms there are?

It depends on …

  • How big the market really is: trade can turn a national market into a world market, leaving room for more firms at MES.
  • How steep the cost curve is below MES: if the cost penalty is small, small firms survive by offering variety, local service or a niche.
  • Technology: new methods can lower MES (small-scale brewing, online retail) or raise it (huge research costs).
  • Firms' behaviour and barriers: mergers, branding and patents can concentrate a market even when MES is low.
  • Judge: MES sets a limit on how many firms can produce at lowest cost. It does not, on its own, fix the number.
Mission check

?A country buys 1.2 million washing machines a year. The minimum efficient scale is 0.8 million machines a year (illustrative data). The country then joins a free-trade area, and its producers can now sell in a market of 6 million machines a year. Which statement is correct?

The number of firms a market can hold at lowest cost depends on MES compared with the size of the market, so a bigger market can hold more of them.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Why can a giant firm make things more cheaply than a small one, and when does size start to hurt?

Make a guess: Why can a giant firm make things more cheaply than a small one, and when does size start to hurt?

Mission 5

Cost per unit as a firm grows

  • Economies of scale: long-run average cost falls as the scale of output rises.
  • Diseconomies of scale: long-run average cost rises as the scale of output rises.
  • They are long run: the firm grows by changing all its inputs, including plant size.
  • A firm's own growth moves it along its LRAC curve.
  • Say: "economies of scale lower average cost" — not: "lower costs". Total cost still rises with output.
Mission 5

Internal economies of scale

  • Internal economies come from the firm's own growth.
TypeWhy average cost falls
TechnicalBigger, specialised machines; mass production; larger containers cost less per unit of capacity
ManagerialSpecialist managers (finance, marketing, engineering) each run a bigger output
FinancialLenders see big firms as less risky: lower interest rates, more ways to raise money
PurchasingBulk buying earns discounts on inputs
MarketingOne advert or brand spread over many more sales
Risk-bearingMany products and markets: a fall in one is offset by others
  • Many are about indivisibilities: some inputs (a machine, an advert, a specialist) cannot be bought in small slices.
Mission 5

Worked example: why bigger ships carry more cheaply

  • Think of a cargo hold as a cube (simplified model).
  • Side 1 → 2: surface area (steel to build) 6 → 24, ×4. Volume (cargo it holds) 1 → 8, ×8.
  • Steel per unit of cargo: 6 ÷ 1 = 6 → 24 ÷ 8 = 3. It halves.
  • This is the law of increased dimensions, a technical economy of scale.
  • A bigger ship also needs only a slightly larger crew and engine per container carried.
  • Real data: MSC Irina, delivered in 2023, carries 24,346 twenty-foot containers.
Quick check
?Which is an example of a financial economy of scale?
Mission 5

External economies and diseconomies

  • External economies come from the growth of the industry (often in one area), not of the firm.
  • Examples: a pool of skilled workers, specialist suppliers nearby, shared transport links, local colleges and research.
  • Real clusters: technology firms around Silicon Valley, financial firms in the City of London.
  • Effect: every firm's LRAC curve shifts down, whatever its own output.
  • External diseconomies: the industry grows so much that congestion, higher rents and competition for skilled workers push every firm's LRAC curve up.
  • Say: "internal economies: a movement along the LRAC; external economies: a shift of the LRAC" — not the other way round.

Mission 5

Diseconomies of scale

  • As a firm grows very large, average cost can rise. Main causes:
  • Communication: more layers of managers; messages are slow and distorted.
  • Coordination and control: hard to keep many sites and departments working together; harder to check that staff do their jobs well.
  • Motivation: workers feel like a small part of a huge machine; absence and staff turnover rise.
  • These are internal diseconomies: they come from the firm's own size.
Quick check
?A firm has grown to 40,000 employees in 30 countries. Decisions take months to pass through layers of managers, and staff surveys show falling motivation. Its cost per unit is now rising as output grows. What is the best explanation?
Mission 5Evaluate

Is bigger better for consumers?

It depends on …

  • Whether cost savings are passed on: with strong competition, lower average cost means lower prices; a dominant firm may keep the savings as profit.
  • Diseconomies: past a point, more size raises average cost.
  • Barriers to entry: economies of scale protect established firms, which can weaken the pressure to keep costs and prices down.
  • Judge: size can lower costs, but whether consumers gain depends on competition and how the firm is run.
Mission check

?A food firm grows over several years (illustrative data). At 10,000 meals a week its total cost is 200,000. At 50,000 meals it is 600,000. At 120,000 meals it is 1,800,000. What do these figures show?

Growing can lower cost per unit, but only up to a point; judge it by average cost, not total cost.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Short run: at least one factor fixed. Long run: all factors variable.
  • Law of diminishing marginal returns: adding a variable factor to a fixed factor eventually makes MP fall.
  • MC = wage ÷ MP, so falling MP means rising MC.
  • Labour productivity is output per worker or per hour; it rises with capital per worker, technology and human capital.

Mission 2

  • Specialisation and the division of labour raise labour productivity (Smith: dexterity, time saved, machinery) and cut unit costs.
  • Costs: monotony, narrow skills, interdependence and over-reliance; how far to go depends on market size and the task.
  • Specialists must exchange; money as a medium of exchange removes barter's need for a double coincidence of wants.

Mission 3

  • TC = FC + VC; fixed costs are paid even at zero output.
  • AFC, AVC, ATC are costs per unit; MC is the cost of the next unit.
  • Diminishing returns make MC rise; MC cuts AVC and ATC at their lowest points.
  • Input prices move AVC, ATC and MC; fixed costs move AFC and ATC; productivity gains shift costs down.

Mission 4

  • In the long run all factors and costs are variable; the LRAC is the envelope of the SRAC curves, and SRAC is never below it.
  • Increasing returns to scale (with constant input prices) make long-run average cost fall; decreasing returns make it rise.
  • Minimum efficient scale compared with market demand limits how many firms can produce at the lowest average cost.

Mission 5

  • Economies of scale: long-run average cost falls as output grows; diseconomies: it rises.
  • Internal economies (technical, managerial, financial, purchasing, marketing, risk-bearing) come from the firm's own growth.
  • External economies and diseconomies come from the industry's growth and shift every firm's LRAC.
