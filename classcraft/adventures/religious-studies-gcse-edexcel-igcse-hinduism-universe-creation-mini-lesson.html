Mission 1

A universe that breathes

  • Most Hindus see time as cyclical: universes come into being, last, and dissolve, again and again, without beginning or end.
  • Each cycle: creation (Brahma) → preservation (Vishnu) → dissolution (Shiva). Then rest, then a new creation.
  • One "day of Brahma" (a kalpa) lasts 1,000 cycles of the four ages:
  • "If ye know Brahma's Day / Which is a thousand Yugas; if ye know / The thousand Yugas making Brahma's Night" (Bhagavad Gita 8.17, Arnold).
  • Each cycle has four ages (yugas); many Hindus believe we now live in the last and darkest, the Kali Yuga.
  • Purpose: the universe gives every atman the chance to act, learn and in the end reach moksha.
Mission 1Read the source

The Hymn of Creation

Rig Veda 10.129, the Nasadiya Sukta (paraphrase):

  • In the beginning there was neither existence nor non-existence; no air, no sky beyond (v. 1).
  • There was no death, no immortality, no day or night: only That One, breathing without breath (v. 2).
  • Darkness was hidden in darkness; from heat (tapas) That One was born; then came desire, the first seed of mind (vv. 3–4).
  • Who really knows? Where did creation come from? The gods came after the world was made, so who knows where it came from? (v. 6)
  • Whether it was made or not, the one who watches over it in the highest heaven surely knows, or perhaps he does not know (v. 7).

Mission 1

Creation stories and Aum

  • A widely told story (Puranas): between cycles, Vishnu lies on the serpent Shesha on the cosmic ocean. A lotus grows from his navel; in it Brahma is born and creates the worlds.
  • Aum (Om): many Hindus say the universe begins as sacred sound; Aum is that first vibration and stands for Brahman. It is chanted at the start and end of prayers.
  • Different stories, one message: the universe comes from the divine and depends on it.
Mission 1

Prakriti and purusha

  • Prakriti: matter, nature: everything physical, including the body, the senses and even the mind.
  • Purusha: spirit, pure consciousness, which is not made of matter.
  • Bondage = spirit identifying itself with matter.
Mission 1

The three gunas

  • All of prakriti is made of three gunas (qualities, or "strands"), mixed in different amounts:
  • Sattva: goodness, light, purity, calm, wisdom.
  • Rajas: passion, activity, desire, restlessness.
  • Tamas: darkness, laziness, ignorance, decay.
  • "Sattwan, Rajas, and Tamas, so are named / The qualities of Nature" (Bhagavad Gita 14.5, Arnold).
  • Foods, actions and people show them: fresh fruit (sattva), spicy food (rajas), stale food or alcohol (tamas).
  • Human nature: a person's character is shaped by the gunas. Hindus try to grow sattva, then rise beyond all three.

Mission 1Evaluate

"Hindu cosmology fits well with modern science."

  • Reasons for: an immense, ancient universe (billions of years) rather than a young one; endless cycles echo some cyclic models in physics. Rig Veda 10.129 asks open questions, like a scientist. Many Hindus see no conflict, reading yugas (ages) and lokas (worlds) spiritually.
  • Reasons against: current mainstream cosmology describes one universe, about 13.8 billion years old, with no evidence that it repeats. Texts speak of lokas, devas and asuras (heavenly beings and their rivals), which science cannot test. Some Hindus read the yuga figures literally and some do not, so "fit" depends on the reading.
  • Judge it by: Are religion and science answering the same question (how or why)? Should ancient numbers be read literally? Does a "fit" make a belief more true, or only easier to hold?

Mission 2

The atman: the real you

  • Atman = the eternal self, or soul, in every living being: humans, animals, plants.
  • It is not the body and not the mind: both are part of prakriti (matter) and change; the atman does not.
  • "Never the spirit was born; the spirit shall cease to be never" (Bhagavad Gita 2.20, Arnold).
Mission 2Read the source

Mission 2

Samsara: the wheel of rebirth

  • Samsara: the endless cycle of birth, death and rebirth (reincarnation).
  • At death the atman leaves the body and takes a new one: human, animal or other, in this world or another loka.
  • What decides the next birth? Karma: the results of past actions (see Mission 3).
  • Samsara is seen as full of suffering and impermanence. The goal is to escape it.

Mission 2

Why we stay stuck: avidya, maya, attachment

  • Avidya = ignorance: not knowing who we really are; thinking "I am this body".
  • Maya = the power that makes the changing world seem to be all there is (see Mission 1).
  • From ignorance come selfishness, greed and anger: we grab for things that cannot satisfy the atman.
  • In Hindu thought, sin is action against dharma that comes from this ignorance; it creates bad karma and binds the atman.
  • Detachment (vairagya): acting and enjoying life without clinging. "The soul which is not moved ... / Takes sorrow and takes joy indifferently, / Lives in the life undying!" (Bhagavad Gita 2.15, Arnold)
Mission 2

Moksha: liberation

  • Moksha: release from samsara; the atman is free and is never reborn.
  • Reached through knowledge, devotion, selfless action or meditation.
  • Jivanmukti: being liberated while still alive. "When all desires dwelling in the heart cease, then the mortal becomes immortal and attains Brahman here." (Katha Upanishad 6.14, Paramananda)
Mission 2Diversity

Atman and Brahman: one or two?

  • Advaita Vedanta ("non-dual"; Shankara, c. 8th century CE): atman is Brahman. The sense of being separate is avidya. Moksha = realising this identity. A monist view.
  • Vishishtadvaita ("qualified non-dual"; Ramanuja, traditionally 1017–1137): atman is a real part of God, as a body belongs to a soul: one with God, yet distinct.
  • Dvaita ("dual"; Madhva, 1238–1317): atman and God (Vishnu) are eternally distinct. Moksha = eternal, blissful service to God. A monotheist view.
  • The sage Uddalaka teaches his son Svetaketu: as salt dissolved in water is everywhere in it though unseen, so is the Self; "tat tvam asi": "you are that" (Chandogya Upanishad 6.13, paraphrase). Advaitins read this as identity; Ramanuja and Madhva read it differently.

Mission 2

Why it matters today

  • Respect for life: the same kind of atman lives in every being, so many Hindus are vegetarian and practise ahimsa.
  • Facing death: funerals (antyesti) include cremation, which many Hindus believe helps release the atman; ashes are often scattered in the Ganges or another river.
  • Living well: every action shapes the next life; the long goal is moksha.
  • Equality: many Hindus stress that the atman has no caste, gender or race.
Mission 2Evaluate

"The atman and Brahman are identical."

  • Reasons for: "tat tvam asi" (Chandogya 6.8.7 and following) read literally; the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad (4.4.6) says one free of desire, being Brahman, goes to Brahman (paraphrase). Advaita explains why the self feels limited: avidya. Mystics describe experiences of unity.
  • Reasons against: if the atman were already Brahman, how could it be ignorant? (Ramanuja's objection). The Gita speaks of loving devotion to Krishna, which needs two. Dvaita: identity would leave no one to enjoy God's love.
  • Judge it by: Which reading fits the most scriptures? What happens to devotion (bhakti) on each view? Does the view explain suffering and ignorance?
Mission 2Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain Hindu teachings about samsara and moksha.

  • Teaching 1: the atman is reborn in body after body (samsara). Source: "So putteth by the spirit / Lightly its garb of flesh" (Gita 2.22). Development: so Hindus see death as a change, not an end, and try to live well because their deeds shape their next life.
  • Teaching 2: the goal is moksha, release from samsara. Development: Advaitins see it as realising that atman is Brahman; Dvaita Hindus as eternal loving service to God; either way, it is the final end of suffering.

Mission 3

Karma means action

  • Karma = "action", and the results that follow from it.
  • The law of karma: every intentional action (thought, word, deed) has a consequence for the one who does it.
  • Good actions bring good results; harmful actions bring suffering, in this life or a future one.
  • Karma is about intention: an accident is not the same as a cruel act.
Mission 3

Karma and samsara

  • Karma decides the next birth: what kind of body, family and circumstances the atman is born into.
  • Good karma → a better rebirth, perhaps in a higher loka. Bad karma → a harder rebirth.
  • But all karma, good or bad, keeps the atman in samsara: some Hindus say good karma is a golden chain, but still a chain.
  • So the goal is not just good karma: it is to be free of binding karma, which leads to moksha.
Mission 3

Three kinds of karma

  • Sanchita karma: the whole store of karma from all past lives.
  • Prarabdha karma: the part that has begun to ripen in this life: your body, family, many circumstances.
  • Kriyamana karma: karma being created now by today's choices; it will ripen later.
  • Classic picture: an archer. Arrows in the quiver = sanchita. An arrow already shot = prarabdha (it cannot be called back). The arrow now on the bow = kriyamana (still your choice).
Quick check
?In the archer picture, which kind of karma is the arrow already flying through the air?
Mission 3

Karma is not fate

  • Fate says: everything is fixed and you can do nothing. Karma says: your past shaped where you start, but you choose what you do now.
  • Prarabdha sets some limits: you cannot choose your birth, your body or some events.
  • Kriyamana is free: how you respond to those limits creates new karma.
  • Freedom and its limits: the gunas (see Mission 1) and old habits pull on us, but humans can choose; that is why humans, more than animals, can work towards moksha.
  • So karma makes people responsible: blaming "fate" is not an excuse.
Mission 3

Sakam and nishkam karma

  • Sakam karma: action done with desire for its results (reward, praise, profit). It creates binding karma and keeps the atman in samsara.
  • Nishkam karma: action done without selfish desire, as duty or as an offering to God. It does not bind.
  • "Let right deeds be / Thy motive, not the fruit which comes from them." (Bhagavad Gita 2.47, Arnold)
  • This is the heart of karma yoga, the path of action (see Mission 7).
  • Example: helping at a food bank to look good on social media (sakam) or simply because it is right and an offering to God (nishkam).
Mission 3

Karma, suffering and freedom

  • Why do people suffer? Many Hindus answer: partly because of karma from past actions, in this life or earlier ones.
  • But karma does not mean "they deserve it, so don't help": compassion (daya) and service (seva) are duties, and they create good karma.
  • Suffering can be a chance to use up old karma and grow spiritually.
  • Purpose of life: to learn through experience, act rightly and in the end reach moksha.
Mission 3Diversity

Who runs the law of karma?

  • An impersonal law: some Hindus see karma as a natural law, like gravity: no judge is needed.
  • God as overseer: many Vaishnava and Shaiva Hindus believe God gives the fruits of karma fairly, and can also forgive and show grace.
  • Grace beyond karma: in bhakti traditions, surrender to God can free a devotee from the bonds of karma (Ramanuja's prapatti, "surrender").
Mission 3Evaluate

"Karma is a fair explanation of suffering."

  • Reasons for: it makes the universe just: nothing happens without a cause. It makes people responsible for their actions. It explains why good people may suffer (karma from earlier lives).
  • Reasons against: no one remembers past lives, so it is hard to learn from them. It could be misused to blame victims of illness, poverty or caste. Many Hindus add God's grace or compassion, suggesting karma alone is not the whole answer.
  • Judge it by: Is it fair to be affected by deeds you cannot remember? Does belief in karma make people kinder or harsher in practice? Does adding grace weaken or strengthen the idea?
Mission 3Worked answer

Influence on Hindus today

Question: Explain two ways in which belief in karma influences Hindus today.

  • Way 1: Hindus try to act ethically, e.g. honestly and without violence. Development: every action creates kriyamana karma, which shapes the next birth.
  • Way 2: many Hindus try to act without selfish desire. Source: "Let right deeds be / Thy motive, not the fruit which comes from them" (Gita 2.47). Development: nishkam karma does not bind, so it leads towards moksha.

Mission 4

Brahman: ultimate reality

  • Most Hindus believe in one ultimate reality behind everything: Brahman.
  • Brahman is eternal and unchanging; the source, support and end of all that exists.
  • Many Hindus simply call Brahman God.
  • Do not confuse: Brahman (ultimate reality) · Brahma (one deity, the creator in the Trimurti) · Brahmin (the priestly class).
  • Hindus understand Brahman in two main ways: nirguna (without qualities) and saguna (with qualities).
Mission 4

Nirguna Brahman

  • Nirguna: "without qualities" (attributes).
  • Your spec says "without form".
  • Brahman is beyond every name, shape and description; no human idea can contain it.
  • The Upanishads describe it by negation: neti, neti, "not this, not this".
  • Impersonal: not a "someone" to pray to, but pure being, consciousness and bliss.
  • "There the eye does not go, nor speech, nor mind." (Kena Upanishad 1.3, Paramananda)
Mission 4

Saguna Brahman

  • Saguna: "with qualities".
  • Your spec says "with form".
  • Brahman known as a personal God with qualities such as love, power, beauty and mercy, who can be known, worshipped and loved.
  • Many Hindus know saguna Brahman as Vishnu (and his avatars Rama and Krishna), Shiva, or the Goddess (Devi, Shakti).
Quick check
?Which statement describes saguna Brahman?
Mission 4

One reality, two ways

  • Most Hindus see nirguna and saguna as one Brahman, understood in two ways, not two Gods.
  • An image often used: water is one substance, whether as formless vapour or as ice with a shape.
  • Humans need form to relate to the infinite: saguna Brahman lets people love and serve God.
  • In daily life (saguna): worship (puja) before a murti, singing devotional songs (bhajans), festivals, prayer to a chosen deity.
  • In daily life (nirguna): meditation, study of the Upanishads, seeking knowledge (jnana) that sees Brahman in everything.
Mission 4Evaluate

"Brahman is best understood as nirguna."

  • Reasons for: the Upanishads say Brahman is beyond eye, speech and mind (Kena 1.3). Any quality would limit the infinite. Advaita teachers (the school that holds only Brahman is ultimately real) hold nirguna as the highest truth. It avoids picturing God as a human.
  • Reasons against: in the Gita, Krishna reveals himself as the Supreme, and says the path to the unmanifest is harder for embodied beings (Bhagavad Gita 12.5, paraphrase). Love (bhakti) needs a personal God. Most Hindus worship Brahman in saguna form.
  • Another view: both are true: one reality seen from two sides, as the Gita and many teachers hold.
  • Judge it by: Which scriptures carry most weight? Can the infinite have qualities? Which view makes sense of Hindu worship?
Mission 4Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain two Hindu beliefs about the nature of Brahman.

  • Belief 1: Brahman is nirguna, beyond all qualities. Source: "There the eye does not go, nor speech, nor mind" (Kena Upanishad 1.3). Development: so some Hindus seek Brahman through meditation and knowledge rather than images.
  • Belief 2: Brahman is also saguna, with qualities. Development: Brahman can be known as a personal God such as Vishnu or Shiva, so many Hindus worship murtis with love (bhakti).

Mission 5

Four aims of life

  • Hindus speak of four purusharthas (aims of human life):
  • Dharma: duty, right living, righteousness.
  • Artha: wealth, work, success, security, gained honestly.
  • Kama: pleasure, love, enjoyment of the arts and family life.
  • Moksha: liberation from samsara (see Mission 2).
  • Artha and kama are good, not sins, as long as they are pursued within dharma.
  • Moksha is the final aim; the other three prepare for it.
Mission 5

What is dharma?

  • From a root meaning "to hold" or "support": dharma is what holds the world and society together.
  • It means cosmic order, right conduct and personal duty.
  • Some duties are for everyone (sadharana dharma): truthfulness, non-violence (ahimsa), self-control, generosity.

Mission 5

Four ashramas: stages of life

  • Brahmacharya: student. Learn, show self-discipline, respect the teacher.
  • Grihastha: householder. Marry, work, raise a family, support others; artha and kama matter most here.
  • Vanaprastha: retirement ("forest-dweller"). Hand over to the next generation; turn to spiritual life.
  • Sannyasa: renunciation. Give up possessions and ties; focus on moksha. Few Hindus take this stage today.
  • Your spec prints "asharma"; the usual spelling is ashrama.
  • Hindus also speak of varnashrama dharma: duty according to your varna (one of four social groups: priests, rulers, merchants, workers) and your ashrama. Some see varna as set by birth; others, reading Gita 4.13, by qualities and work.
  • Dharma changes with each stage: the same person's duty at 15, 35, 60 and 80 is different.
Quick check
?Mr Patel, 64, has handed his business to his daughter. He now spends more time reading the Gita and helping at the mandir, though he still lives at home. Which ashrama best fits him?
Mission 5Evaluate

"Varnashrama dharma is still relevant today."

  • Reasons for: the ashramas give a wise shape to life: learning, family, handing on, spiritual focus. Varna, read as qualities and work (Gita 4.13), says everyone has a role and a duty. Svadharma (your own duty, Gita 3.35) helps people find their own path rather than copy others.
  • Reasons against: varna by birth has been used to justify caste discrimination, which many Hindus and the Indian Constitution reject. Modern careers, mobility and long lives do not fit four neat stages. Sannyasa is rare today.
  • Another view: keep the ashramas and the idea of duty; reject birth-based varna.
  • Judge it by: Which version of varna is meant? Can an old ideal guide without being followed exactly? What do Hindus in Britain actually do?
Mission 5Worked answer

Mission 6

Why is there suffering?

  • Hindu teachings point to several linked causes, not one.
  • Karma: actions have results. Harmful actions bring suffering later, in this life or a future one (samsara, the cycle of birth and death).
  • Avidya (ignorance): not knowing the true self. People think "I am this body", so loss, illness and death seem to destroy them.
  • Maya (illusion): taking the changing world as if it were the lasting reality.
  • Desire and attachment: grasping at things that cannot last leads to anger, fear and grief.
  • Karma and ignorance keep the atman (eternal self) bound in samsara, and so suffering goes on, until it gains moksha (liberation).
Mission 6

Free, but not unlimited

  • Most Hindus believe humans have free will: we choose how to act.
  • But some things are already set by past karma: the family, body and situation you are born into.
  • A common picture: you cannot choose the cards you are dealt, but you choose how you play them.
  • Teachers name karmas (kinds of karma): prarabdha karma is already bearing fruit now; kriyamana karma is being made by today's choices (more in Mission 3).
  • A human birth is seen as precious: humans, unlike animals, can choose to act on dharma and seek moksha.
  • So karma is not fate: the past shapes the situation, the present choice is still yours.
Mission 6

Ignorance and knowledge

  • Avidya is not lack of information. It is not knowing what you really are: the atman, not the body.
  • From that ignorance come selfishness ("I and mine"), greed (lobha) and anger (krodha).
  • The Gita names three "gates" to ruin: lust, anger and greed (Bhagavad Gita 16.21, paraphrase).
  • Sin (papa) here means wrong action that produces bad karma and binds the self; many theistic Hindus also see it as turning away from God.
  • Knowledge (vidya, jnana) means spiritual insight, gained from scripture, a guru and practice.
  • Knowledge removes ignorance as light removes darkness; with it, fear and selfishness lose their grip.

Mission 6Diversity

Responding to suffering

  • Advaita (non-dual) Hindus: the atman is one with Brahman and never really suffers. Suffering belongs to the world of maya; knowledge of the true self ends it.
  • Vaishnava and other devotional Hindus: suffering comes from forgetting God. Devotion and God's grace free the soul; hardship can turn the heart towards God.
  • Practical response, shared by most: accept what cannot be changed as karma, without blaming the sufferer; act now to relieve pain through seva (selfless service) and compassion.
  • Purpose of life: suffering can be a spur to seek moksha, the release that ends suffering for good.
Mission 6Evaluate

"Suffering is always the result of a person's own karma."

  • For: the law of karma is that actions bring results; nothing happens without a cause (Gita, Upanishads). It explains why the innocent seem to suffer: past lives.
  • For: it gives suffering meaning and hope: acting well now changes the future.
  • Against: many Hindus say suffering also comes from avidya and maya, not only from one's own actions; others' free choices can harm the innocent.
  • Against: it risks blaming victims; most Hindu teachers say the right response is compassion (daya, seva), never judgement.
  • Against: devotional Hindus say God's grace can lift karma, so karma is not the whole story.
  • Judge it by: Does it fit what scripture says? Does it lead people to act more kindly? Can it explain the suffering of a newborn child?

Karma explains how situations arise; it never excuses cruelty. Hindu virtues turn the question "why do they suffer?" into "how can I help?"

Mission 7

Yoga means union

  • Yoga comes from a root meaning "to join": union of the self with the divine.
  • It is a whole way of life, not just exercise. (The postures many people do are one small part of one path.)
  • The goal is moksha: liberation from samsara, the cycle of birth and death.
  • Hindus speak of four paths (margas) or yogas, suited to different people:
  • karma (action) · jnana (knowledge) · astanga / raja (meditation) · bhakti (devotion).
Mission 7

Karma yoga: the path of action

  • Doing your duty (dharma) selflessly, without craving its rewards.
  • "Let right deeds be / Thy motive, not the fruit which comes from them." (Bhagavad Gita 2.47, Arnold)
  • Work is offered to God; so it does not create binding karma.
  • Suits active people. Examples: a nurse who works for patients, not praise; seva (service) at a mandir or food bank.
Mission 7

Jnana yoga: the path of knowledge

  • Gaining wisdom about the true self (atman) and ultimate reality (Brahman).
  • Practice: study of scripture with a guru; hearing, reflecting and meditating on the teaching.
  • Viveka (discrimination): telling the lasting from the passing.
  • Linked especially with Advaita Vedanta: knowing that the atman is one with Brahman.
  • Suits thoughtful, reflective people; demanding and often slow.
Quick check
?Meena spends an hour each day studying the Upanishads with her teacher, then reflects on the question "What is my true self?" Which path is she mainly following?
Mission 7

Bhakti yoga: the path of devotion

  • Loving devotion to a personal God: e.g. Krishna, Rama, Shiva or the Goddess.
  • Practice: puja, singing (bhajan, kirtan), repeating God's names (japa), pilgrimage, surrender.
  • "A leaf, a flower, a fruit, water poured forth, / That offering I accept, lovingly made" (Bhagavad Gita 9.26, Arnold).
  • Open to everyone, whatever their learning or caste; God's grace helps the devotee.
  • Often described as the most widely followed path in Hinduism today.
Mission 7Diversity

Which path is best?

  • Advaita (Shankara): jnana is the direct path; knowledge alone ends ignorance. Other paths purify the mind for it.
  • Vaishnavas (Ramanuja; ISKCON): bhakti is highest; God's grace saves.
  • Gandhi and others: karma yoga in the world, serving others as service of God.
  • Swami Vivekananda (1863–1902): all four lead to the same goal; each suits a different temperament (active, reflective, emotional, meditative).
  • Many Hindus read the Gita as weaving the paths together rather than ranking them.
Mission 7

Paths, karma and moksha

  • Karma: selfless action makes no binding karma; selfish action keeps the self in samsara.
  • Jnana: knowledge burns up karma "As the kindled flame / Feeds on the fuel till it sinks to ash" (Bhagavad Gita 4.37, Arnold).
  • Bhakti: God's grace releases the devotee from karma.
  • Moksha means different things: for Advaita, realising oneness with Brahman; for devotional Hindus, eternal loving relationship with God.
  • So the marga a Hindu follows shapes how they hope to reach moksha, and what they think moksha is.
Mission 7

One life, many paths

  • Most Hindus combine paths rather than choose one.
  • Morning: meditation and breathing (raja).
  • Work: doing the job well, without selfishness (karma).
  • Evening: arti at the home shrine; singing bhajans (bhakti).
  • Weekend: a Gita study class (jnana).
  • Why different types matter: people differ, and so do the stages of life (ashramas): a student, a parent and a retired person have different time and strengths.
Mission 7Evaluate

"Bhakti is the best path to moksha."

  • For: open to everyone, whatever their learning; the Gita calls the devoted yogi "truest and best" (6.47).
  • For: relies on God's grace, not on human strength alone; the most widely practised path.
  • Against: Advaita says only knowledge removes ignorance; bhakti prepares for it.
  • Against: without selfless action (karma yoga), devotion can become private and ignore others' needs.
  • Against: many Hindus say no path is "best": each suits a different person.
  • Judge it by: What is moksha (union or relationship)? Does the answer depend on the person? Do the paths really separate?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Most Hindus see the universe as cyclical: creation, preservation, dissolution, repeated; a day of Brahma is 1,000 cycles of four yugas, and many Hindus believe we live in the Kali Yuga.
  • In Hindu teaching, prakriti (matter) is woven from three gunas: sattva, rajas, tamas; maya is the power that hides the divine behind the world.

Mission 2

  • Hindus believe the atman is the eternal self, distinct from body and mind; it moves from body to body in samsara, shaped by karma.
  • In Hindu teaching, avidya (ignorance), maya and attachment keep the atman bound; moksha is release, possible even while alive (jivanmukti) for some schools.
  • Advaita: atman is Brahman. Vishishtadvaita: atman is part of God, yet distinct. Dvaita: atman is eternally distinct from God.

Mission 3

  • In Hindu teaching, karma is action and its results; it decides rebirth in samsara, and only action without binding desire leads towards moksha.
  • Sanchita (store), prarabdha (ripening now) and kriyamana (being made now): many Hindus say the past sets limits, but present choices are free, so karma is not fate.
  • The Gita teaches that sakam karma (with desire for results) binds; nishkam karma (without selfish desire, offered to God) does not (Gita 2.47).

Mission 4

  • Most Hindus believe Brahman is the one ultimate reality: spirit, absolute truth, the source and support of all.
  • For Hindus, nirguna Brahman is beyond all qualities; saguna Brahman is Brahman with qualities, known as a personal God.

Mission 5

  • The four aims: dharma (duty), artha (wealth), kama (pleasure), moksha (liberation); Hindus see artha and kama as good within dharma.
  • Sanatana dharma ("eternal dharma") is what many Hindus call their religion; varnashrama dharma links duty to varna and ashrama (student, householder, retired, renunciate).

Mission 6

  • Hindus link suffering to karma, avidya (ignorance of the true self), maya (illusion) and attachment; humans have free will within limits set by past karma.
  • Different Hindus stress knowledge, devotion or service as the answer to suffering.

Mission 7

  • Yoga means union with the divine; the four paths are karma (action), jnana (knowledge), astanga/raja (meditation) and bhakti (devotion), all aiming at moksha.
  • Hindus disagree about which path is highest but most combine paths to suit temperament and stage of life.
