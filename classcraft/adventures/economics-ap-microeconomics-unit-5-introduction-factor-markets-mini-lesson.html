Mission 1

Mission 1: Nobody buys workers for their own sake, so what decides how many a business wants to hire?

Make a guess: Nobody buys workers for their own sake, so what decides how many a business wants to hire?

Mission 1

Derived demand

  • Firms do not want workers for their own sake. They want the goods and services workers produce.
  • So the demand for labor is a derived demand: derived from the demand for the product.
  • More people order home deliveries → firms want more delivery drivers.
  • Factor markets: firms buy the services of labor, capital and land; households sell them. Their prices are wages, interest and rent.
  • How much labor a firm hires depends on workers' productivity, the price of the output and the wage.
Mission 1

Why labor demand slopes down

  • A higher wage → firms want fewer workers: a movement along the demand curve.
  • Call it a decrease in the quantity of labor demanded.
  • Substitution effect: labor is now more expensive relative to machines, so firms switch to capital where they can.
  • Output (scale) effect: higher costs → higher prices → fewer products sold → fewer workers needed.
  • And diminishing returns: each extra worker adds less output, so is worth hiring only at a lower wage.
  • Labor demand slopes down; labor supply slopes up as the wage rises.
  • Axes: "Wage (W)" and "Quantity of labor (L)".
Mission 1

Worked example: marginal revenue product

  • Illustrative data. A bakery sells loaves in a competitive market at 5 each.
  • Marginal product (MP): the extra output from one more worker.
  • Marginal revenue product (MRP): the extra revenue from one more worker. MRP = MP × MR.
  • Here P = MR = 5, so MRP = MP × 5.

Mission 1

How many to hire: MRP = wage

  • In a competitive labor market, the firm is a wage taker: each extra worker costs the market wage.
  • So the marginal resource cost of labor equals the wage: MRC = W.
  • Rule: hire while MRP ≥ W; stop where MRP = W.
  • Bakery at W = 30: workers 1–4 have MRP of 60, 50, 40, 30. The 5th adds only 20. Hire 4.
  • So the firm's MRP curve is its demand curve for labor: at each wage, it shows how many workers it hires.
  • Label: "D = MRP". Hire where MRP = MRC.
  • If the firm has market power in its output market, MR < P, so MRP < VMP: it hires fewer workers.
Mission 1

Shifts and movements

  • A change in the wage → movement along the curve.
  • These shift labor demand (D1 → D2):
    • Demand for the product, and so its price (derived demand).
    • Productivity of workers: training, better equipment, better management.
    • Price of capital: cheaper machines that replace workers shift demand left; machines that workers use can shift it right.
    • Non-wage costs of employing workers, e.g. employer payroll taxes.
    • The number of firms hiring this kind of labor.
  • In MRP terms: a higher output price or higher MP shifts the MRP curve right.
  • Say: "a rise in the wage decreases the quantity of labor demanded" — not: "the demand for labor falls".

Mission 1

The least-cost input rule

  • Firms can use labor (L) or capital (K). Cost is lowest when the last dollar spent on each gives the same extra output:
  • MP_L ÷ W = MP_K ÷ r (r = price of capital).
  • Illustrative data: MP_L = 20, W = 10 → 2 units per dollar. MP_K = 60, r = 20 → 3 units per dollar.
  • Capital gives more per dollar, so use more capital, less labor until the ratios are equal (diminishing returns closes the gap).
  • Profit-maximizing amounts also need MRP_L = W and MRP_K = r.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A firm hires labor at 15 per hour and rents machines at 40 per hour. The marginal product of the last worker is 30 units; the marginal product of the last machine is 60 units. To produce the same output at the lowest cost, what should the firm do?
Mission 1

Shifts in labor demand: summary

  • Output price rises → MRP rises → labor demand shifts right.
  • Productivity (MP) rises → MRP rises → labor demand shifts right.
  • Wage rises → movement along D = MRP: fewer workers hired.
  • Show on a graph: W on the vertical axis, L horizontal; D1 = MRP1 → D2 = MRP2; the MRC = W line fixed.
Mission check

?Illustrative case: cheaper imports cut the price that farmers get for strawberries. The wage that farms must pay strawberry pickers does not change. What happens to the number of pickers that farms want to employ?

Workers are hired for what they produce, so a change in the product's price moves the whole demand for labor.

Make a guess: What makes people willing to do a job, and why isn't the pay the whole story?

Mission 2

Who supplies labor, and why it slopes up

  • Households supply labor; firms demand it.
  • Each hour of work is an hour less of leisure. The wage is the reward for giving it up.
  • Supply to one occupation slopes up: a higher wage draws workers in from other jobs, and from outside the labor force.
  • A change in the wage → movement along the supply curve.
  • Call it a change in the quantity of labor supplied.
  • Three levels: an individual's hours; supply to an occupation or industry; supply to the whole economy.
  • Axes: "Wage (W)" and "Quantity of labor (L)"; curve "S". Labor supply slopes up; labor demand slopes down.
Mission 2

Monetary and non-monetary factors

  • Monetary (wage) factors: basic pay, overtime, bonuses, commission, pensions, perks.
  • Non-monetary (non-wage) factors:
    • working conditions: safety, hours, stress, flexibility, holidays;
    • job security and promotion and training chances;
    • status, satisfaction, location, commuting time.
  • Workers weigh the net advantages of a job: pay plus everything else.
  • Say: "better working conditions increase labor supply (a shift right)" — not: "they increase the quantity of labor supplied".
  • Only the wage moves along the curve.
Mission 2

What shifts the supply of labor

  • These shift supply to an occupation (S1 → S2):
    • Wages in other jobs (alternatives): higher pay elsewhere → supply here shifts left.
    • Working conditions and other non-wage factors.
    • Qualifications and training needed, and licensing rules.
    • Education: more people with the right skills → shifts right.
    • Immigration: more workers arriving → shifts right.
    • Demographics: the size and age of the population.
    • Leisure preferences and culture, e.g. attitudes to working parents.
Quick check
?Which change would shift the supply curve of delivery drivers to the right?
Mission 2

The whole economy: population, migration, participation

  • Labor supply to the whole economy depends on:
    • population size and age structure: more people of working age → more labor;
    • net migration: workers moving in or out;
    • participation: the share of working-age people in the labor force (working or looking for work).
  • Labor force participation rate = labor force ÷ civilian non-institutional population aged 16+ × 100.
  • Participation depends on childcare, retirement age, health, education (students), and culture.
  • Example: in many high-income countries, far more women joined the labor force from the 1970s onward.
  • Example: the US labor force participation rate peaked at 67.3% in 2000 (BLS); the retirement of the large baby-boom generation is one reason it has been lower since.
Quick check
?In a country, a large generation of workers reaches retirement age and leaves the labor force. Few young people are entering work, and wages have not changed. What happens to the economy's labor supply?
Mission check

?Illustrative case: in past years, a hospital found that each pay rise attracted more nurses. This year it raises nurses' pay by 10%, and at the same time brings in longer, less flexible shifts. The number of nurses willing to work there barely changes. What is the best explanation?

Pay is only part of a job's reward. Working conditions shift the whole supply curve.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why do some of the most useful jobs pay less than jobs that seem far less useful?

Make a guess: What mostly decides how much a job pays?

Mission 3

Wages are a price

  • The wage rate is the price of labor.
  • Demand for labor comes from firms. It is a derived demand: firms want workers for the goods and services they make (see Mission 1).
  • Supply of labor comes from workers. More people offer to work in a job at a higher wage (see Mission 2).
  • In a competitive labor market (many firms, many workers, none able to set the wage), the wage settles where demand meets supply: We, Le.
  • Above We: more workers want the job than firms want to hire, so wages are bid down. Below We: firms cannot fill jobs, so wages are bid up.
  • The demand curve for labor is the MRP curve: D = MRP.
Mission 3

What shifts the curves

  • Demand for labor shifts right when: demand for the product rises · its price rises · workers become more productive (training, better machines) · machines that replace workers become more expensive.
  • Supply of labor shifts right when: more people have the skills or qualifications · the job becomes more attractive (safer, flexible hours, status) · the working population grows (including migration) · wages in other jobs fall.
  • A change in the wage itself moves along the curves; it does not shift them.
  • Say: "the quantity of labor supplied increases" for a move along S — not: "labor supply increases".
Quick check
?A new rule means it takes two more years to qualify as an electrician. Other things equal, what happens in the market for electricians?
Mission 3

One firm: hire until MRP equals the wage

  • A firm in a competitive labor market is a wage taker: it can hire as many workers as it wants at the market wage.
  • So the supply of labor to the firm is horizontal at We. Each extra worker adds exactly We to costs.
  • Label it S = MRC (marginal resource cost = the wage).
  • MRP (marginal revenue product): the extra revenue from one more worker. MRP = MP × MR.
  • In a perfectly competitive product market MR = P, so MRP = MP × P.
  • Rule: hire while MRP > wage; stop where MRP = wage.
  • Profit-maximizing rule: MRP = MRC, and here MRC = wage.

When demand for labor rises

  • Takeaway: when supply of labor is inelastic, a rise in demand mostly raises the wage; when it is elastic, it mostly raises employment.
Mission 3

Worked example: demand for labor rises

  • Illustrative data. Labor demand: Ld = 1000 − 40W. Labor supply: Ls = 60W − 200. (W = wage per hour, L = workers.)
  • Equilibrium: 1000 − 40W = 60W − 200 → 100W = 1200 → W = 12, L = 520.
  • Demand for the product rises: Ld = 1200 − 40W.
  • New equilibrium: 1200 − 40W = 60W − 200 → W = 14, L = 640.
  • Wage: +2 ÷ 12 = +16.7%. Employment: +120 ÷ 520 = +23.1%.
  • Say: "demand for labor increased, causing an extension of supply" — not: "supply increased".
Mission 3

Why wages differ

  • Wage differentials: gaps in pay between jobs, people, regions or sectors.
  • Demand side: high productivity or a valuable product means high demand for the worker.
  • Supply side: long training, rare skills or talent keep supply small and inelastic.
  • Compensating differentials: dangerous, dirty or night-shift jobs must pay more to attract workers.
  • Non-money rewards: jobs people love (a sense of vocation, status, long holidays) attract more workers, so supply is larger and pay lower.
  • Immobility: workers who cannot move or retrain let gaps last.
  • Market power and discrimination also matter.
  • Like water and diamonds: water is vital but cheap; a diamond is not vital but costs far more.
  • How useful a thing is does not set its price. Scarcity relative to demand does.
Mission 3

Real data: a pay ladder

  • US, May 2024, mean annual wage (BLS):
  • All occupations: $67,920.
  • Registered nurses: $98,430.
  • Fast food and counter workers: $31,350.
  • Both kinds of work are useful. Nurses need years of training (small supply) and are in high demand. Fast food jobs need little training, so many people can do them (large supply).

Mission 3

What differentials do

  • Signals: high pay tells workers where to train and move. Over time supply shifts right and the gap can narrow.
  • Skill shortages: if training takes years, a gap can last and posts stay unfilled.
  • Inequality: wider differentials mean a more unequal spread of income.
  • Technology: machines that replace routine tasks cut demand for some workers and raise it for workers whose skills work with the machines.
  • In a competitive market each worker earns the value of their marginal product. Different productivity, skills and training mean different pay, which can add to income inequality.
Mission check

?Care workers are widely seen as vital, yet in many countries they earn less than many office workers. Other things equal, which change would most likely raise care workers' wage?

Pay follows demand and supply in the job market, not how useful a job feels.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why would a firm refuse to hire a worker who would add more to its sales than they are paid?

Make a guess: A firm is the only big employer in town. One more worker would add more to its sales than the wage it pays. Why might it still not hire them?

Mission 4

One buyer

  • Monopsony: a market with a single buyer (or one dominant buyer).
  • In a labor market: one employer hires most of the workers with a particular skill or in a particular place.
  • Examples: a mine or factory in a remote town · a state health service that employs most of a country's nurses.
  • In a product market: a few large retailers buying from many small farmers.
  • A monopsonist is a wage setter, not a wage taker.
  • Say: "monopsony" (one buyer) — not: "monopoly" (one seller).
Mission 4

Why one more worker costs more than the wage

  • A monopsonist faces the whole upward-sloping supply curve of labor.
  • To attract one more worker it must raise the wage.
  • It must pay that higher wage to all its workers, not just the new one.
  • So the marginal cost of labor is above the wage.
  • Labels: S (labor supply = the wage) and MFC (marginal factor cost, also called MRC), drawn above S.
Mission 4

  • Illustrative data. A firm is the only employer in its town. Wage needed to attract L workers: W = 4 + 2L (per hour).

Mission 4

The monopsony diagram

  • Step 1: the supply of labor and the MRP curve. If the employer acted as a wage taker, it would hire where they cross: Wc, Lc.
  • Step 2: draw MFC above S. Profit is greatest where MRP = MFC: employment Lm.
  • Step 3: the firm pays the lowest wage that attracts Lm workers. Read down to the supply curve: wage Wm.
  • Result: Wm < Wc and Lm < Lc. Fewer workers, lower pay.
  • The last worker hired at Lm adds more revenue (MRP) than they are paid (Wm).
  • Say: "read the wage from the supply curve" — not: from the MRP or the marginal cost curve.

Mission 4

Monopsony without one employer

  • Firms can set wages even when there are several employers, if workers cannot easily switch.
  • Imperfect information: workers do not know what other firms pay or which jobs are open.
  • Immobility and switching costs: long commutes, housing costs, family ties, firm-specific skills.
  • Each firm then faces an upward-sloping supply of labor, so it has some monopsony power.
  • Employers acting together can do the same. Real case: in 2015 a US court approved a $415 million settlement of a lawsuit claiming that Apple, Google, Intel and Adobe agreed not to recruit each other's staff, holding pay down.
Mission check

?A region has many apple farmers but only one buyer, a juice company. To buy more apples it must offer a higher price, and it pays that price on all the apples it buys. Compared with a market where many buyers compete (same supply, same value of apples to buyers), what would we expect?

A single buyer that must pay more on every unit to buy one more unit chooses to buy less, and pays less.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Labor demand is derived demand: it depends on demand for the product.
  • MRP = MP × MR; a firm hires until MRP = wage, so the MRP curve is its labor demand curve.
  • A wage change moves along the curve; product price, productivity, capital prices and non-wage costs shift it.

Mission 2

  • Labor supply to a job slopes up: a higher wage draws workers from other jobs and from outside the labor force.
  • The wage moves along the curve; non-wage factors, alternatives, education, migration and demographics shift it.

Mission 3

  • In a competitive labor market the wage is set where demand for labor meets supply of labor.
  • Wage differentials come from differences in demand (productivity) and supply (skills, training, how pleasant the job is).
  • A wage-taking firm hires until MRP = wage.

Mission 4

  • A monopsonist must raise the wage for all workers to hire one more, so the marginal cost of labor is above the wage.
  • It hires where MRP = MFC and pays the wage on the labor supply curve.
