Mission 1

The Opening

  • Surah al-Fatiha ("The Opening") is the first surah of the Qur'an: seven verses.
  • It is recited in every rak'ah (cycle) of salah, though some schools allow a short phrase of praise in its place in the third and fourth rak'ahs. With 17 obligatory rak'ahs a day, most Muslims say it at least 17 times a day.
  • A hadith teaches that there is no valid prayer without it (Sahih al-Bukhari, paraphrase).
  • It is also called Umm al-Kitab, "Mother of the Book": the whole Qur'an in summary.
  • Muslims also recite it at weddings, at funerals, for the sick and to begin important tasks.
Mission 1Read the source

Surah al-Fatiha

In the name of Allah, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful (1:1). Praise belongs to Allah, Lord of all the worlds (1:2). The Merciful; Master of the Day of Judgement (1:3–4) (paraphrase).

"Thee do we worship, and Thine aid we seek." (Surah 1:5, Yusuf Ali)

"Show us the straight way," (Surah 1:6, Yusuf Ali) the way of those Allah has blessed, not of those who earn anger or go astray (1:7, paraphrase).

Q1 Which verses praise Allah, and which ask him for something?

Show answer

1:1–4 praise; 1:5–7 turn to worship and request.

Q2 What do believers ask for?

Show answer

Guidance: to be shown the straight path.

Mission 1

How al-Fatiha describes Allah

  • Lord of the worlds (Rabb al-'alamin): creator, owner and sustainer of everything.
  • Most Gracious, Most Merciful (Ar-Rahman, Ar-Rahim): mercy is named twice in four verses.
  • Master of the Day of Judgement: Allah is just; every person will answer to him.
  • The only one worshipped, the only true helper: "Thee do we worship" (1:5). This is tawhid in a single line.
  • Guide: only Allah shows the straight path (as-sirat al-mustaqim).
  • Mercy and judgement belong together: Allah forgives, and also holds people responsible.
Mission 1

Submission

  • Islam means submission (to the will of Allah); a Muslim is one who submits. Both words share one Arabic root with salam, peace.
  • Al-Fatiha is spoken as a servant to a Lord: "we worship… we seek… show us".
  • Submission is chosen, not forced: the believer asks to be guided.
  • Significance today:
  • Each prayer begins again with praise and a request for guidance.
  • Daily choices (honesty, food, money, time) are measured against the "straight path".
  • Belief in judgement gives a reason for responsibility; belief in mercy gives a reason to repent and not despair.

Mission 1

Oneness in Surah al-Anaam

Surah 6:13–24 argues for tawhid (the oneness of Allah):

  • All that exists in night and day belongs to him; he hears and knows all (6:13).
  • He is the Originator of the heavens and earth; he feeds and is not fed: he needs nothing (6:14).
  • Only he can remove harm or give good; he has power over all things (6:17–18).
  • He is witness between the Prophet and his hearers (6:19).
  • On the Day of Judgement, those who set up partners will find them gone, and deny they ever did it (6:22–24).
  • The message: no rival, no partner, no helper needed. Associating partners with Allah (shirk) is the opposite of tawhid (more in Mission 2).
Mission 1Read the source

"He is the one Allah"

"But in truth He is the one Allah, and I truly am innocent of (your blasphemy of) joining others with Him." (Surah 6:19, Yusuf Ali)

"Say: He is Allah, the One and Only;" (Surah 112:1, Yusuf Ali)

  • Surah al-Ikhlas (112) is the shortest statement of tawhid: Allah is One, eternal, neither begets nor is begotten, with none like him.

Mission 1

Tawhid in worship

  • Shahadah: "There is no god but Allah, and Muhammad is the Messenger of Allah": the first words of faith.
  • Salah is directed to Allah alone, facing one qiblah (direction), Makkah.
  • No images of Allah or the Prophet in mosques: geometric patterns and Arabic calligraphy instead.
  • Every Muslim prays directly to Allah, with no go-between.
  • Tawhid is the first of the Sunni six articles of faith and the first of the Shi'a Usul ad-Din (roots of religion). Both traditions put it first.
  • Significance today: trust in one God for all needs; equality of all worshippers before one Lord.
Mission 1

The 99 beautiful names

  • Al-Asma' al-Husna: the beautiful names. "The most beautiful names belong to Allah: so call on him by them" (Surah 7:180, Yusuf Ali).
  • A hadith speaks of 99 names (Sahih al-Bukhari; Sahih Muslim). Lists of the names differ, and scholars find more than 99 in the Qur'an and Hadith together.
  • No single name can sum up Allah; together they show what Allah is like and how he relates to people.
  • Uses today:
  • Du'a (personal prayer): Muslims call on the name that fits the need ("Ya Ghaffar" when asking forgiveness).
  • Dhikr (remembrance): repeating names, often on 99 or 33 prayer beads (subha / tasbih).
  • Names for people: Abd ("servant of") + a name, e.g. Abdur-Rahman.
Mission 1

Six names, six verses

  • Ar-Rahman (the Most Gracious): "(Allah) Most Gracious!" (Surah 55:1, Yusuf Ali). Mercy to all creation.
  • Ar-Rahim (the Most Merciful): "Allah will love you and forgive you your sins: For Allah is Oft-Forgiving, Most Merciful." (Surah 3:31, Yusuf Ali)
  • As-Salam (the Source of Peace): "the Source of Peace (and Perfection)" (Surah 59:23, Yusuf Ali).
  • Al-Khaliq (the Creator): "the Creator of all things: then worship ye Him" (Surah 6:102, Yusuf Ali).
  • Al-Ghaffar (the All-Forgiving): 2:173 sets food laws, then excuses those forced by necessity: "For Allah is Oft-forgiving Most Merciful." (Surah 2:173, Yusuf Ali)
  • Ar-Razzaq (the Provider, Sustainer): "For Allah is He Who gives (all) Sustenance" (Surah 51:58, Yusuf Ali).
  • Relationship to humanity: Allah creates, provides, forgives, gives peace, and is merciful to all and especially to those who turn to him.

Mission 1Diversity

Understanding Allah today

  • Sunni and Shi'a Muslims share al-Fatiha, tawhid and the names; Shi'a theology also stresses Allah's justice ('adl) as a root of religion.
  • Sufi Muslims make dhikr of the names central, seeking nearness to Allah through constant remembrance.
  • Scholars have long discussed how to read names that sound human ("hearing", "seeing"): most say they are real attributes, but not like any creature's ("there is none like unto Him", 112:4).
  • Non-religious views: many atheists and Humanists ask how an all-merciful, all-powerful God fits a world of suffering, and see the names as human ideas about God.
  • Muslim responses: suffering can be a test (2:155); justice is completed on the Day of Judgement; human understanding of Allah's wisdom is limited (2:30).
Mission 1Evaluate

"Al-Fatiha is the most important surah for Muslims today."

  • Reasons for: recited in every prayer, usually in every rak'ah; called Mother of the Book; contains praise, tawhid, judgement and guidance in seven verses; known by every Muslim, whatever language they speak.
  • Reasons against: Surah al-Ikhlas (112) states tawhid even more directly, and a hadith says it equals a third of the Qur'an. Surah al-Baqarah holds much of the guidance for daily life. Many Muslims say all of the Qur'an is equally the word of Allah, so ranking surahs is unwise.
  • A non-religious view: for a Humanist, no text has special authority; its value lies in what it inspires people to do.
  • Judge it by: Important for what: worship, belief, or law? Does frequency of use equal importance? Can parts of a revealed book be ranked?
  • (d) 12-mark tip: support each view with a source, include a non-religious view where asked, and reach a justified conclusion.

Mission 2

Three kinds of people

  • Surah al-Baqarah ("The Cow") is the longest surah: 286 verses, mostly revealed in Madinah.
  • Its opening sorts people into three groups:
  • Believers (mu'minun): 2:2–5.
  • Those who reject faith (kafirun): 2:6–7.
  • Hypocrites (munafiqun): they say they believe but do not: 2:8–20.
  • The surah then gives guidance for the new Muslim community: prayer, fasting, charity, family life, justice.
Mission 2Read the source

Who the believers are

"This is the Book; in it is guidance sure, without doubt, to those who fear Allah;" (Surah 2:2, Yusuf Ali)

"Who believe in the Unseen, are steadfast in prayer, and spend out of what We have provided for them;" (Surah 2:3, Yusuf Ali)

They also believe in what was revealed to the Prophet and before him, and are sure of the Hereafter (2:4, paraphrase).

Q1 Name three things believers do, according to 2:3.

Show answer

Believe in the unseen; pray steadfastly; give from what Allah provides.

Q2 Which earlier revelations do believers accept?

Show answer

Those sent before the Prophet, e.g. to Musa and Isa.

Mission 2

Blessings of belief

  • Guidance: "They are on (true) guidance, from their Lord" (Surah 2:5, Yusuf Ali).
  • Success: "it is these who will prosper" (2:5): in this life and the next.
  • Peace: those who follow Allah's guidance will have "no fear, nor shall they grieve" (2:38).
  • Righteousness is more than ritual: 2:177 says it is belief in Allah, the Last Day, angels, the Book and the messengers, and giving to family, orphans, the needy and travellers, praying, paying zakah, keeping promises and patience in hardship.
  • Belief, worship and action belong together. Faith (iman) shows in deeds.
Mission 2

Those who reject, those who pretend

  • Kafir comes from a root meaning "to cover": one who covers over the truth after it is clear.
  • 2:6: for such people, warning makes no difference. 2:7: Allah "hath set a seal on their hearts".
  • Readings of the seal: many commentators explain it as the result of a person's own repeated rejection, not a reason for it. Others link it to Allah's decree (al-qadr), while holding humans still responsible.
  • Hypocrites: "In their hearts is a disease" (2:10). They claim to make peace but cause harm (2:11–12).
  • Many Muslims today apply this to themselves first: is my faith real, or only words?

Mission 2

Shirk

  • Shirk: associating partners with Allah: treating anything as divine, or as sharing Allah's power, alongside him.
  • It is the opposite of tawhid (oneness).
  • "Allah forgiveth not that partners should be set up with Him; but He forgiveth anything else, to whom He pleaseth" (Surah 4:48, Yusuf Ali). Muslims understand this of someone who dies without repenting; whoever turns back can be forgiven.
  • Luqman to his son: "false worship is indeed the highest wrong-doing" (Surah 31:13, Yusuf Ali).
  • Why so serious? It denies who Allah is, and puts the created in the place of the Creator.
  • Major shirk: worshipping other gods or idols. Minor shirk: a hadith calls showing off in worship (riya') "minor shirk" (Musnad Ahmad, paraphrase).
Mission 2Read the source

The golden calf

While Musa is away, his people make a calf from their ornaments:

"it seemed to low: did they not see that it could neither speak to them, nor show them the way? They took it for worship and they did wrong." (Surah 7:148, Yusuf Ali)

  • 7:149: they realise their error and beg for mercy and forgiveness.
  • 7:150: Musa returns "angry and grieved", throws down the tablets and seizes his brother Harun, who explains the people overpowered him.
  • In the Qur'an, Harun had warned the people (20:90); he is not the maker of the calf.

Q1 What does the Qur'an say the idol could not do?

Show answer

Speak to them or guide them.

Q2 What do the people do once they realise their wrong?

Show answer

Repent and ask for mercy, 7:149.

Mission 2

Shirk today

  • Idols are not only statues. The Qur'an warns of one who "takes as his god his own vain desire" (Surah 45:23, Yusuf Ali).
  • Many Muslim teachers apply this to modern "partners": wealth, fame, career, a person, the self, when put before Allah.
  • Effects on daily life: no images in worship; prayer only to Allah; trust in Allah over luck charms; checking one's motives (no showing off).
  • Divergent views:
  • Some Muslims (often Salafi) treat amulets, praying at saints' tombs or asking saints to intercede as shirk.
  • Many Sufi, Shi'a and other Sunni Muslims see visiting shrines and asking the righteous to pray for them as honouring Allah's friends, not worshipping them.
  • All agree that worship belongs to Allah alone. They differ on where the line falls.

Mission 2Diversity

Shirk and other religions

  • People of the Book (Jews and Christians) received earlier revelations: "Our Allah and your Allah is one" (Surah 29:46, Yusuf Ali).
  • 2:62: Jews, Christians and Sabians who believe in Allah and the Last Day and do good "shall have their reward". Scholars differ: some read it as open to them today; others say it refers to those before the Prophet or who accept him.
  • The Qur'an criticises belief in Allah as "one of three" (5:73). Christians reply that the Trinity is one God, not three. Many Muslims still see it as compromising tawhid.
  • "Let there be no compulsion in religion" (Surah 2:256, Yusuf Ali); "To you be your Way, and to me mine." (Surah 109:6, Yusuf Ali)
  • Today: some Muslims keep distance from others' worship; many join interfaith work, e.g. A Common Word (2007), an open letter from Muslim scholars to Christian leaders on love of God and neighbour.

Mission 2Evaluate

"Believing in the right things matters more than doing good deeds."

  • Reasons for: shirk is the gravest wrong (4:48): the Qur'an puts right belief first. Al-Baqarah begins with belief in the unseen (2:3). Deeds without iman (faith) are not acts of worship.
  • Reasons against: 2:177 says ritual alone is not righteousness; faith shows in giving and keeping promises. Hypocrites (2:8–12) claim belief, but their deeds expose them. 2:62 links reward to belief and doing right.
  • A non-religious view: Humanists hold that goodness is judged by how we treat others, and needs no belief in God.
  • Muslim responses: belief and action cannot be separated; good deeds done for Allah have their full meaning; Allah judges intentions as well as actions.
  • Judge it by: Can belief and action be ranked, or does one express the other? What does "matters" mean: to Allah, to society, or to the person?
  • (d) 12-mark tip: evaluate both sides, refer to the Qur'an, include the non-religious view, and justify a conclusion.

Mission 3

Creation as signs

  • Al-Khaliq, the Creator, made everything from nothing, by his will.
  • The Qur'an has no single creation story. Its verses are spread through many surahs.
  • They present the natural world as ayat: signs pointing to Allah. (The same word is used for the verses of the Qur'an.)
  • Creation does not run itself: Allah keeps it in being and governs it.
Mission 3Read the source

Signs for those who believe

"Verily in the heavens and the earth, are Signs for those who believe." (Surah 45:3, Yusuf Ali)

The creation of humans and scattered animals are signs for those of firm faith (45:4); night and day, rain that revives dead earth, and the changing winds are signs "for those that are wise" (45:5, paraphrase).

Q1 Name two signs in 45:5.

Show answer

Night and day alternating; rain bringing life to dead earth; changing winds.

Q2 Who does 45:3–5 say will recognise the signs?

Show answer

Believers, those of firm faith, those who are wise.

Mission 3

The events of creation

  • One mass, split apart: the heavens and earth "were joined together… before we clove them asunder" (Surah 21:30, Yusuf Ali).
  • Life from water: "We made from water every living thing" (21:30); every animal is made from water (24:45).
  • Smoke: the sky was "smoke"; heaven and earth answer Allah's command, "We do come (together), in willing obedience" (Surah 41:11, Yusuf Ali).
  • Six days (periods): "in six days" (7:54; 57:4), then Allah "established on the throne": ruling all. Many Muslims read "days" (ayyam) as long ages (compare 22:47).
  • No weariness: "nor did any sense of weariness touch Us" (Surah 50:38, Yusuf Ali). Allah does not need rest.
  • Order: sun, moon, night and day, each "in its rounded course" (21:33); the heaven built with might, and its vast extent (51:47).
  • Humans in stages: created "in diverse stages", made to grow from the earth "growing (gradually)" (71:14, 71:17).

Mission 3

Creation and science today

  • Many Muslims see the Qur'an and science as in harmony: 21:30 is read as fitting the Big Bang; 51:47 as fitting an expanding universe; 21:33 as orbits.
  • Other Muslim scholars warn against reading modern science into the text: the Qur'an is a book of guidance, not a science textbook, and science changes.
  • Evolution: views vary. Some Muslims accept it fully as Allah's method; many accept it for plants and animals but hold that Adam was specially created; some reject it.
  • Non-religious view: many atheists and Humanists say the universe and life are explained by natural processes, with no need for a creator.
  • Muslim responses: science explains how; it cannot explain why there is anything at all. The order of nature is itself a sign (45:3).
Mission 3

"I will create a vicegerent"

  • Allah tells the angels he will place a khalifah on earth: a vicegerent, successor or steward who acts on another's behalf.
  • The angels ask: will you place there one who will cause mischief and shed blood, while we praise you?
  • Allah: "I know what ye know not." (Surah 2:30, Yusuf Ali)
  • Allah teaches Adam the names of all things (2:31). The angels cannot name them; Adam can (2:32–33).
  • Humans have knowledge and free will: they can do great harm, or great good.

Mission 3

Above the angels

  • Allah commands the angels to bow down to Adam (2:34). They all do, except Iblis, who refuses out of pride (Iblis was of the jinn, 18:50).
  • Humans are honoured "above a great part of our creation" (17:70). Angels always obey; humans can choose to obey, which gives their obedience its worth.
  • Humans accepted the Trust (amanah) that heavens, earth and mountains refused (33:72).
  • Adam and his wife eat from the forbidden tree, prompted by Satan, and leave the Garden (2:35–36).
  • Adam repents and is forgiven: "his Lord Turned towards him" (2:37). Muslims do not believe in inherited original sin.
  • Guidance will come; those who follow it need not fear (2:38); those who reject it face the Fire (2:39).

Mission 3

Khalifah today

  • Stewardship: the earth belongs to Allah; humans hold it in trust. A hadith: the world is green and beautiful, and Allah has made you stewards over it to see how you act (Sahih Muslim, paraphrase).
  • Accountability: every person will answer on the Day of Judgement for how they used the earth, animals and other people.
  • Environment: many Muslims link khalifah to caring for the planet, e.g. the Islamic Declaration on Global Climate Change (Istanbul, 2015).
  • Animals: they are "communities like you" (6:38): to be treated with care.
  • Knowledge: Adam's naming of things encourages Muslims to learn and study the world.
  • Different emphases: some Muslims stress khalifah as responsibility for the environment; others stress it mainly as obeying Allah's law in every area of life.
Mission 3Evaluate

"Humans are the most important part of Allah's creation."

  • Reasons for: humans are khalifah (2:30), taught the names (2:31), honoured by the angels' bowing (2:34) and "above a great part" of creation (17:70).
  • Reasons against: creation as a whole is a sign of Allah (45:3–5); animals are "communities like you" (6:38). The Qur'an says humans proved "unjust and foolish" in bearing the Trust (33:72). Khalifah means responsibility, not ownership.
  • A non-religious view: Humanists see humans as one species among many, produced by evolution; any special status comes from our abilities and our duties, not from a creator.
  • Judge it by: Does "most important" mean most honoured, or most responsible? Is it Allah, rather than any creature, who is at the centre?
  • (d) 12-mark tip: weigh both sides with Qur'an references, include the non-religious view, and justify your conclusion.

Mission 4

Justice at the heart of Islam

  • Justice ('adl): giving each person their due; fairness without favour.
  • Allah is Al-'Adl, the Just, one of the beautiful names.
  • "Allah commands justice, the doing of good, and liberality to kith and kin" (Surah 16:90, Yusuf Ali).
  • When judging between people, "judge with justice" (Surah 4:58, Yusuf Ali).
  • Justice is owed to everyone, including enemies: do not let hatred make you unjust (5:8).
  • On the Day of Judgement, Allah judges every person with perfect justice.
Mission 4

Surah An-Nisa 4:105–112

  • Allah sent the Book "that thou mightest judge between men, as guided by Allah" (4:105). The Prophet must not plead for the treacherous.
  • People can hide their plots from others, but not from Allah (4:108).
  • Whoever does wrong and then seeks forgiveness will find Allah forgiving (4:110).
  • Whoever commits a wrong and throws the blame on an innocent person carries "a falsehood and a flagrant sin" (4:112).
  • Context: classical commentators link these verses to a case in Madinah: a Muslim stole, then blamed an innocent Jewish man. The verses cleared the innocent man.
  • The lesson: justice does not take sides by religion, tribe or friendship.
Mission 4Read the source

Even against yourselves

"O ye who believe! stand out firmly for justice, as witnesses to Allah, even as against yourselves, or your parents, or your kin, and whether it be (against) rich or poor:" (Surah 4:135, Yusuf Ali)

  • It continues: do not follow your desires and swerve; Allah knows all that you do (4:135, paraphrase).

Q1 Against whom must a Muslim be ready to give honest witness?

Show answer

Themselves, their parents, their relatives.

Q2 Why mention "rich or poor"?

Show answer

Neither wealth nor pity should bend the truth.

Mission 4

Justice in 4:124–134

  • Equal reward: whoever does good, "be they male or female" and has faith, will enter Paradise, with "not the least injustice" (4:124).
  • The weak: "stand firm for justice to orphans" (4:127): orphans' property and rights must be protected.
  • Marriage: wives' rights are protected; settling disputes fairly and peacefully is "best" (4:128).
  • Honesty about limits: perfect fairness is hard (4:129), but believers must not abandon those in their care.
  • All things belong to Allah, who sees everything (4:126, 4:131–134): justice is done before Allah, not just before people.

Mission 4

Justice in the Prophet's life

  • No favours for the powerful: when people asked him to excuse a high-status woman from punishment for theft, the Prophet refused: earlier nations were ruined, he said, because they punished the weak and let the powerful go; the law would apply even to his own daughter Fatimah (Sahih al-Bukhari, paraphrase).
  • Stop oppression: "Help your brother, whether oppressor or oppressed." Asked how to help an oppressor, he said: by stopping him (Sahih al-Bukhari, paraphrase).
  • Equality: in his Farewell Sermon he taught that no Arab is superior to a non-Arab, nor the reverse, except by righteousness (Musnad Ahmad, paraphrase).
  • Trust: even before prophethood he was known as al-Amin, the trustworthy.
  • "Ye have indeed in the Messenger of Allah a beautiful pattern (of conduct)" (Surah 33:21, Yusuf Ali): his example shows how to apply justice.
  • Applying justice today: Muslims differ in emphasis. Some stress personal honesty and fair dealing; others stress campaigning (e.g. against poverty or racism) and zakah and charity work.
  • Muslims also differ on how far state law should enforce Shari'ah rulings (more on the later screen).
Mission 4

Shari'ah and its sources

  • Shari'ah: "the way" or "path" (originally a path to water): Allah's guidance for the whole of life, not only crime and punishment.
  • It covers worship (salah, zakah, sawm, hajj), family, food, business, and how people treat each other.
  • Fiqh: the human work of understanding and applying Shari'ah.
  • "Obey Allah, and obey the Messenger, and those charged with authority among you. If ye differ in anything among yourselves, refer it to Allah and His Messenger" (Surah 4:59, Yusuf Ali).
  • 4:60 warns against seeking judgement from false authorities (taghut) instead.
  • Sunni sources: Qur'an · Sunnah (Hadith) · ijma' (scholarly consensus) · qiyas (reasoning by analogy).
  • Shi'a (Twelver) sources: Qur'an · Sunnah, including the teachings of the Imams · consensus · 'aql (reason).
Mission 4Read the source

Authority of the Prophet

"It is not fitting for a Believer, man or woman, when a matter has been decided by Allah and His Messenger to have any option about their decision:" (Surah 33:36, Yusuf Ali)

  • 33:21: the Prophet is the "beautiful pattern" for those who hope in Allah and the Last Day.
  • 33:35 names men and women alike, in faith, prayer, charity, fasting and remembrance, promising both forgiveness and great reward.

Q1 Why do Muslims follow the Sunnah as well as the Qur'an?

Show answer

Allah and his Messenger together decide; the Prophet's life is the pattern, 33:21, 33:36.

Q2 What does 33:35 suggest about men and women before Allah?

Show answer

Both are promised the same reward for the same faith and deeds.

Mission 4

Shari'ah in Muslim life today

  • Worship: times and manner of salah; fasting in Ramadan; paying zakah.
  • Food: halal and haram (e.g. no pork or alcohol; animals slaughtered in Allah's name).
  • Money: no riba (interest): "Allah hath permitted trade and forbidden usury" (Surah 2:275, Yusuf Ali). Islamic banks use profit-sharing, leasing or agreed-profit sales instead.
  • Family: marriage contracts, inheritance, religious divorce.
  • In the UK: Muslims are bound by UK law; many scholars say keeping such agreements is itself a Shari'ah duty ("fulfil (all) obligations", 5:1). Shari'ah councils can advise on religious matters such as Islamic divorce, but they are not courts of law.

Mission 4Diversity

Divergent understandings

  • Sunni schools (madhhabs): Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, Hanbali. All accepted as valid; they differ on details (e.g. some prayer positions, some food rules).
  • Shi'a (Ja'fari) school: living senior scholars (mujtahids, e.g. a marja') continue to give rulings through ijtihad (independent reasoning).
  • Traditional: follow the long-established rulings of a school.
  • Reformist: return to the Qur'an's principles (justice, mercy, public good) to apply them in new situations.
  • States: some Muslim-majority countries base national law partly on Shari'ah; many others use it for family and personal matters only; in non-Muslim countries it is personal and community practice.
Mission 4Evaluate

"Following Shari'ah is unnecessary for living a good life today."

  • Non-religious arguments (for): Humanists and atheists argue that reason, empathy and human rights are enough to live well; laws should be made democratically, not from ancient texts; religious rules can conflict with modern equality.
  • Muslim responses (against): Shari'ah is guidance from Allah, who knows human nature best (4:105); it covers the whole of life, including worship, which secular laws do not; it builds justice, protects the weak (4:127, 4:135) and gives a shared path.
  • Further Muslim views: much of Shari'ah agrees with secular ethics (honesty, care for the poor); differences in application show it can adapt (qiyas, ijtihad).
  • Judge it by: What is a "good life": moral behaviour alone, or a relationship with God? Who decides what is just? Can religious and secular law work side by side?
  • (d) 12-mark tip: set out the non-religious argument fairly, give Muslim responses with references, and reach a justified conclusion.
Mission check

?A Muslim head teacher must choose between two pupils for a scholarship awarded on academic merit. One is her niece; the other has higher marks from a poorer family. Which reasoning best fits the Qur'an's teaching on justice?

Justice in the Qur'an means fairness without favourites: towards family, rich and poor alike. Shari'ah aims to put that justice into practice in every part of life.

Mission 5: If you believe your religion is true, can you also believe other religions are true?

Make a guess: If you believe your religion is true, can you also believe other religions are true?

Mission 5

Three answers

  • Exclusivism: only one religion is true and leads to salvation. Many evangelical Christians cite "I am the way, the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father, except through me." (John 14:6)
  • Inclusivism: one religion holds the fullest truth, but people of other faiths may share in it. The Catholic Church "rejects nothing that is true and holy" in other religions (Nostra Aetate 2, 1965).
  • Pluralism: many religions are valid paths to the same ultimate reality. Philosopher John Hick (1922–2012) argued this in God and the Universe of Faiths (1973).
  • Each religion contains people who hold each view.
  • For Muslims the line that cannot be crossed is tawhid (God's oneness): this shapes how they see other faiths.
Mission 5

Proselytisation

  • Proselytisation: trying to convert others to your religion.
  • Christianity: "Go and make disciples of all nations" (Matthew 28:19). Evangelism shares the Gospel freely (e.g. the Alpha course); most Churches reject pressure or bribes.
  • Islam: da'wah (invitation to Islam): "Call unto the way of thy Lord with wisdom and fair exhortation" (Surah 16:125, Pickthall), never by force: "There is no compulsion in religion." (Surah 2:256, Pickthall)
  • Criticism: Humanists and others say pressing beliefs on people disrespects them. UK law protects the freedom to change religion (Human Rights Act 1998, Article 9).
Mission 5

Shirk and Muhammad's call

  • Shirk: associating partners with Allah, the one sin the Qur'an says Allah will not forgive if unrepented.
  • Surah 7:148–150: Moses' people "chose a calf (for worship)" (7:148, Pickthall), and he returned in anger.
  • So some Muslims will not join worship that, in their view, gives Allah partners; others join inter-faith events but not the prayers.
  • Muhammad's call: "And warn thy tribe of near kindred" (Surah 26:214, Pickthall). After first preaching privately, he called his own clan publicly to worship one God.
  • Muslims today interpret this command in divergent ways: by example and good character, by open preaching, or by inter-faith dialogue.
Quick check
?Which teaching would a Muslim give against forcing someone to convert?
Mission 5Evaluate

"Religious people should not try to convert others."

  • Reasons for: pressure disrespects others' freedom and beliefs; it can damage community relations; pluralists see no need; dialogue builds understanding better.
  • Reasons against: if you believe something is true and saving, sharing it is love; Matthew 28:19 and da'wah are commands; freedom of speech includes religious speech; people remain free to say no.
  • Other views: share by example, not argument; answer questions honestly when asked; never use force, bribes or deception.
  • Judge it by: Is there a difference between inviting and pressuring? Does respect mean silence?
Mission check

?At an inter-faith week event, a Hindu speaker says: "God is one, and the different religions are like rivers flowing into the same sea." Which view does this express?

Believers answer the question in different ways: exclusivist, inclusivist or pluralist, and all three views are found in most religions.

Mission 6: Can a believer and an atheist agree on how to live, and where do they part ways?

Make a guess: Can a believer and an atheist agree on how to live, and where do they part ways?

Mission 6

Four non-religious positions

  • Atheism: the belief that there is no God.
  • Agnosticism: the view that we do not, or cannot, know whether God exists.
  • Humanism: a non-religious way of life built on reason, evidence, empathy and human flourishing. In Britain, Humanists UK (called the British Humanist Association until 2017) speaks for it.
  • Secularism: the view that the state and public institutions should be neutral between religions and beliefs. The National Secular Society (founded 1866) campaigns for it.
  • Say: secularism is about the state, not about personal belief. Many religious people are secularists.
Mission 6Read the source

Obeying God and the Prophet

"O ye who believe! Obey Allah, and obey the messenger and those of you who are in authority; …" (Qur'an 4:59, Pickthall)

"Verily in the messenger of Allah ye have a good example for him who looketh unto Allah and the Last Day, and remembereth Allah much." (Qur'an 33:21, Pickthall)

  • Shari'ah (the "path" or way): God's guidance for the whole of life, drawn from the Qur'an and the Prophet's example (Sunnah).

Q1 Who must Muslims obey, according to 4:59?

Show answer

Allah, the messenger, and those in authority.

Q2 Why does 33:21 matter for Shari'ah?

Show answer

The Prophet's life is the model, so his example shows how to live the Qur'an.

Mission 6

"You don't need religious rules to be good"

  • The non-religious argument (atheist, Humanist): people can be good using reason and empathy; rituals such as prayer and fasting change nothing; rules from the 7th century do not fit modern life; state law already protects people.
  • Muslim responses:
  • Shari'ah is not only about being good to others. Ibadah (worship) is the purpose of life: "I created the jinn and humankind only that they might worship Me." (Qur'an 51:56, Pickthall)
  • The Creator knows what is best for people, so God's guidance is wiser than any human system.
  • Prayer and fasting train self-discipline and God-consciousness (taqwa), and build the ummah (community).
  • Many British Muslims see no clash: following the law of the land is itself part of keeping agreements, and much of Shari'ah is personal (prayer, food, charity, finance).
  • Shared ground: many Muslims and Humanists agree on honesty, justice and care for others; they part ways over whether worship of God is needed to live well.
  • Muslims differ: the Sunni schools of law and Shi'a (Ja'fari) law reach different rulings; some modern Muslims stress the purposes (maqasid) of Shari'ah over its detailed rules.

?A hospital in Britain is planning its chaplaincy team. A Humanist proposes that a non-religious pastoral carer should join the Christian, Muslim, Jewish and Hindu chaplains. Which statement best describes the shared value and the disagreement here?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Al-Fatiha praises Allah as Lord of the worlds, Most Gracious, Most Merciful and Master of the Day of Judgement, and asks for guidance: submission in prayer.
  • Surah 6:13–24 teaches tawhid: Allah is one, needs nothing, has no partner; shirk is its opposite.
  • The 99 names (e.g. Ar-Rahman, Ar-Rahim, As-Salam, Al-Khaliq, Al-Ghaffar, Ar-Razzaq) are used in du'a, dhikr and personal names.

Mission 2

  • Al-Baqarah opens by describing believers (belief in the unseen, prayer, giving), those who reject faith, and hypocrites; belief brings guidance and success.
  • Shirk, ascribing partners to Allah, is the gravest wrong (4:48; 31:13); the golden calf (7:148–150) is the Qur'an's example of idol worship.
  • Muslims differ on what counts as shirk today and on how to relate to other faiths; 2:256 forbids compulsion.

Mission 3

  • Allah created everything by his will: heavens and earth split from one, the sky from smoke, life from water, in six "days", without weariness; nature is full of signs (45:3–5).
  • Humans are khalifah (2:30): given knowledge, honoured above the angels, free to choose; Adam repents and is forgiven.
  • Muslims today see khalifah as stewardship and accountability; views on science and evolution vary.

Mission 4

  • Justice is a quality of Allah (Al-'Adl) and a command to all believers; 4:105–135 forbids defending the guilty, blaming the innocent, or favouring kin, rich or poor.
  • The Prophet's life (Hadith) shows justice applied equally; 33:21 makes him the pattern to follow.
  • Shari'ah rests on the Qur'an (4:59; 33:36) and Sunnah, with Sunni and Shi'a methods; it is applied in many ways today, and Muslims respond to non-religious claims that it is unnecessary.

Mission 5

  • Exclusivism: one true religion. Inclusivism: one fullest truth others can share. Pluralism: many paths to one reality.
  • Proselytisation divides opinion: sharing truth, or disrespecting freedom?

Mission 6

  • Atheism, agnosticism and Humanism are views about God and life; secularism is a view about the state.
  • Many Muslims answer "you don't need religious rules" by saying observance is worship of God, not only good behaviour.
