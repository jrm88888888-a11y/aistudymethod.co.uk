Sociocultural approach to behaviour

The sociocultural approach studies how other people and culture shape behaviour. This mini-lesson covers social identity theory, social cognitive theory, stereotypes, cultural dimensions, and enculturation and acculturation — anchored to real studies.

Approach · assumptions

How the social world shapes behaviour

  • Behaviour is shaped by groups, social norms and culture.
  • We learn much behaviour by observing others and by belonging to social groups.
  • Culture — the shared values and practices of a group — influences cognition and behaviour, and can be studied using dimensions.
Social identity theory

Social identity theory (Tajfel and Turner)

Social identity theory (Tajfel and Turner, 1979) explains group behaviour through three processes:

  • Social categorisation — we sort people into in-groups and out-groups.
  • Social identification — we adopt the identity of our group.
  • Social comparison — we compare our group favourably to others to boost self-esteem.

Tajfel et al. (1971) — the minimal group paradigm — randomly assigned boys to meaningless groups; even with nothing at stake they allocated more points to their own in-group. In-group favouritism arises from mere categorisation.

Social cognitive theory

Social cognitive theory (Bandura)

Social cognitive theory (Bandura) says we learn by observing models and imitating them. Four conditions are needed:

Attention → Retention → Reproduction → Motivationwe must notice, remember, be able to copy, and have a reason to imitate

Bandura, Ross and Ross (1961) — the Bobo doll study — found children who watched an adult behave aggressively toward a Bobo doll imitated that aggression, including novel acts. Vicarious reinforcement (seeing a model rewarded) makes imitation more likely.

Stereotypes

The formation and effect of stereotypes

A stereotype is a generalised belief about a social group.

  • Formation: social categorisation and illusory correlation — Hamilton and Gifford (1976) showed people over-perceive a link between a minority group and rare (negative) behaviours.
  • Effect: stereotype threat — Steele and Aronson (1995) found African American students performed worse on a test when it was framed as measuring intelligence (activating a negative stereotype) than when it was not.

Culture & dimensions

Culture and cultural dimensions

Hofstede (1980) analysed IBM employees across dozens of countries and identified cultural dimensions that describe values, including:

  • Individualism vs collectivism — loose ties and personal goals vs tight groups and shared goals.
  • Power distance — acceptance of unequal power.
  • Uncertainty avoidance and masculinity–femininity.

Etic vs emic: dimensions are an etic (cross-culture comparison) tool; some argue they oversimplify or stereotype whole nations.

Enculturation & acculturation

Enculturation and acculturation

Enculturation is learning the norms of your own culture (often through observation and modelling, e.g. Bandura).

Acculturation is cultural change from prolonged contact with a different culture. Berry's model identifies four strategies:

  • Integration, Assimilation, Separation and Marginalisation.

Lueck and Wilson (2010) studied acculturative stress in Asian immigrants, finding language ability and discrimination affected stress levels.

Research & ethics

Research methods and ethics

Sociocultural psychology uses lab experiments (Bandura, Tajfel), field and cross-cultural surveys (Hofstede) and interviews.

  • Watch for cultural bias and ethnocentrism when comparing cultures.
  • Ethics matter: the Bobo doll study raises concerns about exposing children to aggression.
Recap

The big ideas to know

Social identity theory: Tajfel & Turner; Tajfel et al. (1971) minimal groups

Social cognitive theory: Bandura, Ross & Ross (1961) Bobo doll; modelling

Stereotypes: Hamilton & Gifford (illusory correlation); Steele & Aronson (stereotype threat)

Culture: Hofstede (cultural dimensions; individualism–collectivism)

Enculturation & acculturation: Berry (four strategies); Lueck & Wilson (acculturative stress)
