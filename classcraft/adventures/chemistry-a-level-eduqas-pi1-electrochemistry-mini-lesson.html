Mission 1: When iron rusts, does it gain or lose electrons?

Mission 1

  • Oxidation and reduction always happen together: a redox reaction.
  • The electron and oxidation-number definitions work for every redox reaction.
  • Memory aid: OIL RIG, Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain (of electrons).
Mission 1

Oxidation numbers: the rules

  • An uncombined element is 0: Fe, O2, Cl2.
  • A simple ion's oxidation number is its charge: Fe³⁺ is +3.
  • Usually O is −2 and H is +1. F is −1 in all its compounds.
  • Exceptions: O is −1 in peroxides (H2O2); H is −1 in metal hydrides (NaH).
  • In compounds, group 1 metals are +1; group 2 metals, +2.
  • The numbers add up to the charge on the species: 0 for a compound.
Mission 1

Worked examples

  • SO4²⁻: x + 4(−2) = −2, so S is +6.
  • H2S: 2(+1) + x = 0, so S is −2.
  • Cr2O7²⁻: 2x + 7(−2) = −2, so 2x = +12 and each Cr is +6.
  • Rust, Fe2O3: 2x + 3(−2) = 0, so each Fe is +3.
  • Write the sign first: +3, not 3+.
Mission 1

Rusting: where the electrons go

  • Simplified: 4Fe(s) + 3O2(g) → 2Fe2O3(s).
  • Fe: 0 → +3. Each Fe atom loses 3 electrons: oxidised.
  • O: 0 → −2. Each O atom gains 2 electrons: reduced.
  • Electrons move from the species oxidised to the species reduced.
  • Metals generally lose electrons to form positive ions; non-metals gain them to form negative ions.
  • The same ideas hold across the s-, p- and d-blocks: Mg → Mg²⁺, Cl2 → 2Cl⁻, Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺.
Mission 1

Oxidising and reducing agents

  • The oxidising agent takes electrons, so it is itself reduced.
  • The reducing agent gives electrons, so it is itself oxidised.
  • Agents are reactants, never products.
  • Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu. Zn: 0 → +2, oxidised: the reducing agent. Cu²⁺: +2 → 0, reduced: the oxidising agent.
Quick check
?Chlorine reacts with aqueous iron(II) ions: 2Fe²⁺(aq) + Cl2(aq) → 2Fe³⁺(aq) + 2Cl⁻(aq). Which species is the oxidising agent in this reaction?
Mission 1

Half-equations

  • A half-equation shows the oxidation or the reduction alone, with the electrons written in.
  • Oxidation: electrons are products. Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻.
  • Reduction: electrons are reactants. Cl2 + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻.
  • Both atoms and charge must balance.
  • Also learn: 2S2O3²⁻ → S4O6²⁻ + 2e⁻ (thiosulfate to tetrathionate).
Mission 1

Balancing in acid: worked example

Cr2O7²⁻ → Cr³⁺ in acidic solution:

  • Balance Cr: Cr2O7²⁻ → 2Cr³⁺.
  • Balance O with H2O: add 7H2O on the right.
  • Balance H with H⁺: add 14H⁺ on the left.
  • Balance charge with e⁻: left +12, right +6, so add 6e⁻ on the left.
  • Cr2O7²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6e⁻ → 2Cr³⁺ + 7H2O. Check: 2 Cr each fall by 3 (+6 → +3), so 6e⁻.
  • Same steps: MnO4⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H2O (Mn +7 → +2).
Mission 1

Combining half-equations

  • Scale so the electrons lost equal the electrons gained, then add and cancel them.
  • Oxidation: Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + e⁻. Multiply by 6 to give 6e⁻.
  • Add to the dichromate(VI) half-equation from the earlier screen; cancel 6e⁻:
  • Cr2O7²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6Fe²⁺ → 2Cr³⁺ + 7H2O + 6Fe³⁺
  • Check charge: left −2 + 14 + 12 = +24; right +6 + 18 = +24.
  • Electrons never appear in the overall equation.
Quick check
?In acidic solution, MnO4⁻(aq) is reduced to Mn²⁺(aq) and Fe²⁺(aq) is oxidised to Fe³⁺(aq). In the balanced overall redox equation, what amount of Fe²⁺ reacts with 1 mol of MnO4⁻?
Mission check

?In the thermite reaction, aluminium reacts with iron(III) oxide: Fe2O3(s) + 2Al(s) → Al2O3(s) + 2Fe(l). What happens to the iron?

Rusting: Fe 0 → +3, so iron loses electrons. In thermite, iron gains them back. Oxidation numbers tell you which way.

Mission 2: How can you measure the voltage of half a battery, when a voltmeter needs two connections?

Make a guess: How can you measure the voltage of half a battery, when a voltmeter needs two connections?

Mission 2

Only differences can be measured

  • A voltmeter needs two electrodes, so it measures a potential difference between two half-cells.
  • No single half-cell's potential can be measured on its own.
  • So one half-cell is chosen as the reference and given the value 0 V.
  • Every other half-cell is measured against it.
Mission 2

The standard hydrogen electrode

  • Standard hydrogen electrode (SHE): H2 gas bubbling over platinum in a solution of H⁺(aq).
  • Conditions: H2 at 100 kPa, 1.00 mol dm⁻³ H⁺(aq), 298 K.
  • Half-equation: 2H⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ H2(g). E⦵ = 0.00 V by definition.
  • Platinum is inert: it carries electrons and gives a surface for the reaction. It is often coated with fine platinum black.
  • Zero is an agreed value, not "no reaction".
Mission 2

Building a cell to measure E⦵

  • Electrodes: carry electrons between the half-cell and the wire.
  • Solutions: hold the ions that react in each half-cell.
  • Salt bridge (e.g. filter paper soaked in KNO3(aq)): ions move through it to keep each solution neutral. No electrons pass through it.
  • High-resistance voltmeter: draws almost no current, so it reads the full potential difference.
  • In the lab: clean the metal with emery paper; use KNO3, not KCl, if Ag⁺ is present (AgCl would precipitate).
Quick check
?Why is the standard electrode potential of the hydrogen half-cell, 2H⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ H2(g), given as 0 V?
Mission 2

Measuring E⦵ for nickel

  • Connect a standard Ni²⁺/Ni half-cell to the SHE.
  • The voltmeter reads 0.25 V, and nickel is the negative electrode: electrons leave it.
  • So E⦵(Ni²⁺/Ni) = −0.25 V. Negative electrode against the SHE → negative E⦵.
  • The whole difference is assigned to nickel, because the SHE is 0 V.
Mission 2

Why conditions matter

  • E changes with concentration, temperature and gas pressure.
  • Standard: 298 K, 100 kPa for gases, 1.00 mol dm⁻³ for ions. Only then is it E⦵.
  • More Ni²⁺(aq): the equilibrium Ni²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Ni shifts right, so E becomes less negative.
Mission 2

Half-cells with no metal: platinum electrodes

  • Some couples have no solid metal: Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺, Cl2/Cl⁻.
  • Dip platinum into a solution of both forms: Fe³⁺(aq) and Fe²⁺(aq), 1.00 mol dm⁻³ each.
  • For a gas (Cl2), bubble it over platinum in Cl⁻(aq), like the SHE.
  • Measured against the SHE: E⦵(Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺) = +0.77 V.
Mission 2

Writing it down: cell diagrams

  • Half-equations are written as reductions: oxidised form + ne⁻ ⇌ reduced form (IUPAC convention).
  • Pt(s) | H2(g) | H⁺(aq) ‖ Ni²⁺(aq) | Ni(s)
  • | = phase boundary; ‖ = salt bridge. The SHE goes on the left.
  • Ion/ion couple: Pt(s) | H2(g) | H⁺(aq) ‖ Fe³⁺(aq), Fe²⁺(aq) | Pt(s); a comma separates species in the same phase.
  • E(cell) = E(right) − E(left). With the SHE on the left, the cell EMF equals E⦵ of the right half-cell.
Mission 2

Reading a table of E⦵ values

  • Listed in order, the values form the electrochemical series.
  • More positive E⦵: the species on the left is more easily reduced.
  • More negative E⦵: the species on the right is more easily oxidised.
  • So the strongest oxidising agent is on the left at the most positive end; the strongest reducing agent is on the right at the most negative end.
  • Compare signed values: −2.37 V is the lowest value, not the "largest".
Quick check
?Standard electrode potentials: Mg²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ Mg(s), E⦵ = −2.37 V · Zn²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ Zn(s), E⦵ = −0.76 V · Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ Cu(s), E⦵ = +0.34 V · Ag⁺(aq) + e⁻ ⇌ Ag(s), E⦵ = +0.80 V. Which species is the strongest oxidising agent?
Mission check

?A standard Pb²⁺/Pb half-cell is connected to a standard hydrogen electrode. The voltmeter reads 0.13 V.
The lead electrode is the negative electrode. Which statement is correct?

You never measure half a cell alone. You measure it against the SHE, agreed to be 0 V, and the whole reading belongs to the other half-cell.

Mission 3: Why does a lithium coin cell give 3 V when an alkaline AA cell gives 1.5 V?

Make a guess: Why does a lithium coin cell give 3 V when an alkaline AA cell gives 1.5 V?

Mission 3

Cell potential from two E⦵ values

  • E⦵cell = E⦵(positive electrode) − E⦵(negative electrode). In a cell diagram: E⦵(right) − E⦵(left).
  • Reduction happens at the positive electrode (right-hand side of the diagram): the couple with the more positive E⦵.
  • Boards call E⦵cell the EMF, the standard cell potential or the standard emf.
  • Worked example: zinc and silver half-cells.
  • Ag⁺(aq) + e⁻ ⇌ Ag(s), E⦵ = +0.80 V. Zn²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ Zn(s), E⦵ = −0.76 V.
  • Ag⁺ is reduced; Zn is oxidised. E⦵cell = +0.80 − (−0.76) = +1.56 V.
  • Cell diagram: Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(aq) ‖ Ag⁺(aq) | Ag(s).
  • Never multiply E⦵ by a coefficient: 2Ag⁺ or Ag⁺, the value is +0.80 V.
Quick check
?Standard electrode potentials:
Fe³⁺(aq) + e⁻ ⇌ Fe²⁺(aq) E⦵ = +0.77 V
Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ Cu(s) E⦵ = +0.34 V
Which statement about the reaction Cu(s) + 2Fe³⁺(aq) → Cu²⁺(aq) + 2Fe²⁺(aq) is correct?
Mission 3

Will it react?

  • For an equation, find the species reduced: its E⦵ goes first.
  • E⦵cell positive: the reaction is feasible as written. Negative: the reverse is feasible.
  • Cl2 + 2Fe²⁺ → 2Cl⁻ + 2Fe³⁺: +1.36 − (+0.77) = +0.59 V. Chlorine oxidises Fe²⁺.
  • I2 + 2Fe²⁺ → 2I⁻ + 2Fe³⁺: +0.54 − (+0.77) = −0.23 V. No reaction; instead Fe³⁺ oxidises I⁻.
  • Shortcut, using the electrochemical series: the oxidised form of the more positive couple reacts with the reduced form of the less positive couple.
Quick check

Fe²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ Fe(s) E⦵ = −0.44 V

Mission 3

Why lithium gives a big voltage

  • Li⁺(aq) + e⁻ ⇌ Li(s): E⦵ = −3.04 V, the most negative common value.
  • So Li is the strongest reducing agent: it gives up electrons most readily.
  • E⦵cell is the gap between two E⦵ values. A very negative E⦵ makes a big gap with almost any partner.
  • Zinc (−0.76 V) is far less negative. An alkaline cell, with a zinc negative electrode, gives about 1.5 V.
  • A lithium coin cell reaches about 3 V with one cell, not two stacked.
  • Lithium reacts with water, so lithium cells use a non-aqueous electrolyte.
Mission 3

Background: E⦵cell and Gibbs energy

  • Beyond your course, but it explains the sign rule: ΔG⦵ = −nFE⦵cell (F = charge on 1 mol of electrons).
  • n = electrons transferred in the balanced equation: Zn + 2Ag⁺ → Zn²⁺ + 2Ag, n = 2.
  • Positive E⦵cell → negative ΔG → feasible. Your exams use E⦵cell only.
Mission 3

Measuring a cell's voltage

  • Clean each metal strip with emery paper to remove the oxide layer.
  • Dip each into a solution of its ions, 1.00 mol dm⁻³, at 298 K.
  • Salt bridge: filter paper soaked in saturated KNO3(aq). (KCl would precipitate AgCl in a silver half-cell.)
  • Use a high-resistance voltmeter: almost no current flows, so it reads the maximum voltage.
  • Ions of one element in two oxidation states (e.g. Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺): platinum electrode, equimolar solution.
  • Compare with the prediction. Small differences come from non-standard concentrations or temperature and oxide films.
Mission check

?A cell uses a chlorine half-cell: Cl2(g) + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2Cl⁻(aq), E⦵ = +1.36 V. Which metal half-cell, joined to it, gives the largest E⦵cell?
Mg²⁺/Mg −2.37 V · Zn²⁺/Zn −0.76 V · Cu²⁺/Cu +0.34 V · Ag⁺/Ag +0.80 V

Same reason as lithium. The more negative the E⦵ of the metal, the bigger the gap, and the bigger the voltage.

Mission 4: Can a car run on hydrogen without burning it?

Mission 4

What a fuel cell does

  • A fuel cell turns the chemical energy of a fuel and oxygen directly into electrical energy. Nothing burns.
  • Fuel and oxygen are fed continuously to separate electrodes.
  • Negative electrode (anode): the fuel is oxidised, releasing electrons.
  • Positive electrode (cathode): oxygen is reduced, gaining electrons.
  • Electrons flow through the external circuit (e.g. a car's motor) from the negative to the positive electrode.
Mission 4

The hydrogen fuel cell: acidic electrolyte

  • Negative electrode: H2 → 2H⁺ + 2e⁻.
  • Positive electrode: O2 + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2H2O.
  • H⁺ ions move through the electrolyte to the positive electrode.
  • Double the first and add: 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O. The only product is water.
  • EMF = 1.23 − 0.00 = +1.23 V (from E⦵ of the two couples).
Quick check
?In a hydrogen fuel cell with an acidic electrolyte, hydrogen gas is supplied to the anode. Which half-equation represents the reaction at the anode?
Quick check
?In a hydrogen fuel cell with an acidic electrolyte, which equation represents the reaction that occurs at the cathode?
Mission 4

The same cell with an alkaline electrolyte

  • Some fuel cells use an alkaline electrolyte (e.g. KOH(aq)); OH⁻ ions carry the charge.
  • Negative electrode: H2 + 2OH⁻ → 2H2O + 2e⁻.
  • Positive electrode: O2 + 2H2O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻.
  • Overall, still 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O, and still 1.23 V.
Mission 4

Background: carbon fuels

  • Beyond your specification: other fuels such as methanol and ethanol can be used too.
  • Their cathode reaction is the same; at the anode the fuel becomes CO2, so they do release CO2.
  • To balance the anode half-equation: C atoms first; then O with H2O; H with H⁺; charge with e⁻.
  • Methanol: CH3OH + H2O → CO2 + 6H⁺ + 6e⁻.
  • Ethanol: C2H5OH + 3H2O → 2CO2 + 12H⁺ + 12e⁻ (4 O, 12 H, zero charge on each side).
Mission 4

Benefits and drawbacks

  • Benefits: the only product is water (no CO2, CO or soot at the point of use); more of the fuel's energy becomes electrical energy than in an engine; it runs as long as fuel is supplied; it is refuelled, not recharged.
  • Drawbacks: most hydrogen is made from fossil fuels (or by electrolysis, which needs electricity); hydrogen is flammable and hard to store (high-pressure tanks); few refuelling stations; costly platinum catalysts.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Oxidation = electron loss, oxidation number rises; reduction = electron gain, oxidation number falls.
  • Half-equations: balance atoms (H2O, H⁺ in acid), then charge with e⁻; scale so electrons cancel.
  • Oxidising agents gain electrons and are reduced; reducing agents lose electrons and are oxidised.

Mission 2

  • Only potential differences can be measured; the SHE is the 0 V reference.
  • E⦵ of a half-cell = the cell reading against the SHE under standard conditions, signed by direction of electron flow.
  • More positive E⦵: stronger oxidising agent on the left; more negative: stronger reducing agent on the right.

Mission 3

  • E⦵cell = E⦵(positive) − E⦵(negative) = E⦵(right) − E⦵(left). Never multiply E⦵ by coefficients.
  • Positive E⦵cell: feasible. But kinetics and non-standard conditions can change what you see.
  • Lithium's very negative E⦵ makes a large gap, so lithium cells give a large voltage.

Mission 4

  • Fuel cell: fuel oxidised at the negative electrode, O2 reduced at the positive electrode; electrons through the external circuit; nothing burns.
  • Acidic: H2 → 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ and O2 + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2H2O; overall 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O, 1.23 V.
  • Benefits: water the only product, efficient, refuelled. Drawbacks: hydrogen production, storage and cost.
